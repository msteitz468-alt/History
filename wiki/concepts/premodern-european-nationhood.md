# Premodern European Nationhood

**Premodern European nationhood** is the analytical claim, advanced by Azar Gat in *Nations* (2013, ch. 5), that the [[national-state|national state]] was the *typical* form of political consolidation in medieval and early-modern Europe — that Europe's so-called "dynastic monarchies" were in substance **national monarchies**, and that most modern European peoples trace continuous descent from medieval ethnopolitical formations. It is Gat's core empirical case against the modernist position on [[nationalism]] and a pillar of his wider thesis on the [[antiquity-of-nations]]. The argument is contested: it directly challenges the dominant "modernist" school (Gellner, Anderson, Breuilly), and Gat concedes that premodern and modern nationhood differ substantially in form.

## The thesis: the "mushrooming" of national states

For Gat, "the barbarian invasions which put an end to the Western Roman Empire were the starting point of both a new, European civilization . . . and Europe's history of national states." North and west of the old Roman frontier, the national state "was the most typical form of political consolidation in Europe . . . soon after the beginning of state consolidation." He attributes this to geopolitics: "Alone of all the great civilizations of Eurasia, Europe was never united by force from within, nor was it conquered from outside," so "the middle-size state, rather than the smaller or larger templates" became the norm. He surveys the continent "in a clockwise direction" and finds the same pattern of "ethnogenesis" and durable "ethnopolitical consolidations" everywhere: "From the British Isles to Russia ethnic realities formed the basis of the emergent states."

**England is the paradigm case** — early and precocious. An Anglo-Saxon nation formed "by the tenth century," disrupted by the Norman Conquest of 1066, then re-emerged as English. Gat quotes Patrick Wormald — "In the tenth century England was permanently united, politically and administratively: much earlier than France, let alone Spain, Italy or Germany" — and James Campbell — "It may seem extravagant to describe early England as a 'nation-state'. Nevertheless it is unavoidable." Bede's *gens Anglorum* took for granted "that the people of all the Anglo-Saxon petty-states comprised one people," and "ethnonational affiliation trumped religion in the allegedly primarily religious and non-national Middle Ages." Britain, Gat stresses, "is famously the story of not one but four nations, all of which go back to medieval times" — Scotland's case sealed by the Declaration of Arbroath (1320), which asserted the right of the "*Scottorum nacio*" to independence on grounds of "separate descent" and "self-government."

The same recurs in **Scandinavia** (Denmark, Norway, Sweden crystallized "from around the year 1000"; Saxo Grammaticus equates *natio* and *patria* "as a matter of course"); the **medieval German Empire**, "the closest there would be to a German national state until the nineteenth century"; the **Czech lands**, where the Hussite movement was "both Czech and popular, which is the very definition of national"; and **Poland**, **Lithuania**, and **Russia**, where (Gat argues) "national identity was often stronger in medieval Eastern Europe" than under later imperial expansion and enserfment.

## Southern versus northern Europe

Gat draws a sharp geographical contrast. North of the old Roman frontier — sparsely populated tribal lands of Germanic and Slavic settlement — the national state was "almost as old as the state itself." South of it, two different patterns held. In **Italy**, "Europe's most rugged peninsula cum archipelago," the rugged landscape "proved most conducive to the rise of smaller polities," so the city-state recurred and Italy "remained politically divided until the nineteenth century" — though a "supraregional Italian identity and sentiment did exist." In **southeast Europe**, medieval national states (Serbia, Croatia, Bulgaria, Hungary) did form, but here "imperial expansion ultimately came from the outside and eliminated rather than built on the area's national states" — the Ottomans destroying them and, by reaction, calling forth the **Habsburg Empire**, "a very different animal" that "scarcely possessed a truly dominant imperial people." In **Iberia**, Reconquista forged Castilian-dominated Spain, but Portugal and Catalonia retained distinct national identities; and **France** became "the exception rather than the rule," uniquely successful in assimilating Occitan, Breton, Alsatian, and Basque populations into one nation.

## The objections rebutted (section C)

Section C is the analytical heart: Gat addresses five modernist objections claiming the premodern nation was *impossible*.

**(i) Universal religion / the Church.** The objection: universal faiths like Christianity competed with and impeded particular national identities. Gat's counter: "religion, even an ostensibly universal one, was as much if not more of a force for the nation." He quotes Colette Beaune: "Nations are particularistic; Christianity is universal. One might have expected that Christianity itself would have tempered the growth of nationalism. But this did not happen. Most European nations in the Middle Ages had little difficulty projecting their sense of nationhood in religious terms." In the Orthodox east, "national churches . . . — Bulgarian, Serb, Greek, Russian — became a reality of the utmost . . . national significance," and after Ottoman conquest "it was the national churches above all that . . . came to embody the national identity." Crucially, the village parish network was "the most powerful propaganda organ of the times . . . the framework of the premodern 'imagined community' which Anderson has missed completely."

**(ii) The imperial idea / Holy Roman Empire.** The objection: the Roman-model ideal of universal empire dominated the Middle Ages. Gat's counter: the ideal "lost any real potency" already with Charlemagne's empire's disintegration in the ninth century; "universal papal authority was a mirage." He quotes Susan Reynolds: "Forget the old textbook idea of universal empire . . . [it] was never a serious threat to the supremacy of kings." Real empires, moreover, were national at their core — "there was an imperial people or ethnos at the center of almost every empire" — and "the great majority of empires were both national and multinational, and there was no necessary contradiction between the two."

**(iii) Dynastic (rather than popular) rule.** The objection: if realms switched hands by inheritance, what national substance could they have? Gat's counter: "many if not the majority of Europe's dynastic monarchies were national monarchies . . . political boundaries were roughly congruent with kin–culture identities." A rigid inheritance rule was simply the political community's defence against "the specter of anarchy and civil war," yet "the monarchs in the majority of kingdoms and times were native, and they derived legitimacy from this fact" — foreign rulers "sat on bayonets," or quickly acculturated (the Hanoverians "became thoroughly English"). He cites Reynolds: a kingdom "comprised and corresponded to a 'people' (*gens, natio, populus*)."

**(iv) Social inequality / the elite–peasant gulf.** The objection: neither aristocracy nor peasantry felt part of a common collective. Gat's counter: societies varied greatly in polarization (Macedon, Scotland c.1300, medieval Norway/Sweden, Catalonia, and the Hussite lands all had powerful popular elements), and the silent masses can be read through their *actions*: "even in the most despotic and/or oppressive of state societies the masses of peasantry identified with their native country and kinsmen, most notably in time of national crisis." In emergencies even regimes that "normally disenfranchised and ignored the masses" appealed to "common brotherhood, culture, and motherland." His summation: "The peasants of old, barely less so than the European proletarians of 1914, did have a motherland after all."

**(v) Dialect fragmentation / lack of a standard vernacular.** The objection: deep dialect differences prevented any common culture. Gat's counter: this is "very selective in its choice of cases." Germany, Italy, and France were one pole, but "in some countries dialect diversity was very mild." Open plains and unified state control "decrease linguistic variation": in Poland "the dialect variation . . . was weak," even the nobility's pronunciation "no different from that of the peasants"; "plainly put, Russian was Russian." Such variations "never came close to mutual unintelligibility. Nor did they hinder a sense of common peoplehood." Gellner's image of landlords and peasants speaking different languages "has been wholly unrepresentative."

Gat reinforces all five with philological evidence that *natio* and *gens* "have been almost identical in meaning" since Roman times (Isidore of Seville, Reynolds, Althoen), and with "the most amazing medieval document" — the Council of Constance (1415–16), where delegations "voting in national blocs of interest" debated "the meaning of the national concept," the English delegation even insisting a *natio* transcends "whether a nation obeys one prince only or several."

## Relation to the modernist debate

This chapter is Gat's central empirical case for [[antiquity-of-nations|the deep-roots position]] in the nation/nationalism debate. He argues "the overwhelming majority of historians of the societies concerned" support medieval and early-modern nationhood, and that the modernists (Gellner, Anderson, Breuilly) "have scarcely tested their abstract schema . . . against the actual experience of premodern times," skewing their case selection. The thesis is **contested**: the editors Gat cites concede the field "often resembled a dialogue of the deaf," and Gat himself insists this is "not an 'essentialist' proposition" — identities undergo "constant historical transformations," and modernity (popular sovereignty, citizenship, mass literacy) genuinely "turned [the nation] from a significant to a dominant political factor." The disagreement is narrow but real: whether "a sense of common identity and solidarity on a country-state scale existed in the premodern world."
