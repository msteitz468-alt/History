## Definition and Scope

The **Murji'a**, **Qadariyya**, and **Jahmiyya** were three early Islamic theological currents that flourished between roughly 650 and 800 CE, predating the mature schools of [[mutazilism|Mu'tazilism]], [[asharism|Ash'arism]], and [[al-maturidi|Maturidism]]. None was a unified sect with formal membership; all were fluid coalitions characterised by shared positions on particular doctrinal questions. All three names were subsequently used as polemical labels by opponents (especially Hanbalites), which complicates recovery of their actual positions.

Together they represent the first wave of distinctively Islamic theological reflection: responses to the civil wars, to the problem of sin in a community that defined itself by moral excellence, and to the encounter with pre-Islamic Arab fatalism.

## The Murji'a ("The Deferrers")

### Origins and Political Context

The Murji'a emerged from the trauma of the First and Second Civil Wars (656–661; 680–692). The core question was whether the controversial caliphs Uthman and Ali — one assassinated, the other killed in battle — had been sinners or victims. The [[kharijites]] condemned both; the [[shia-islam|Shi'a]] condemned Uthman while venerating Ali; the Umayyads' spokesmen condemned Ali. Many Muslims recoiled from all of these partisan positions.

The term *murji'a* derives from *irja'* ("deferral" or "postponement") — a reference to Qur'an 9:106. The first known declaration of this principle is attributed to al-Hasan ibn Muhammad ibn Ali (d. c. 718), son of a failed Shi'ite candidate in the Second Civil War, who issued a statement proclaiming "deferment of judgement" (*irja'*) on Uthman and Ali — leaving their status to God. Blankinship describes this as a "pietistic solution" to the sectarian impasse (Winter, ch. 2).

### Core Doctrines

**1. Suspension of judgement on contested figures.** The morally compromised caliphs of the civil wars could not be judged in absentia; their status is deferred to God. Present-day wrongdoers, however, can be condemned — not as unbelievers, but as "misguided believers" (*mu'minun dullal*).

**2. Faith is separable from works.** The Murji'ites developed a doctrine, grounded partly in their political need to avoid condemning fellow Muslims, in which interior faith (*iman*) was held to be analytically distinct from external actions. The bare confession of belief (*shahada*) suffices to constitute a person as a Muslim.

**3. Faith is indivisible and cannot decrease.** Mainstream Murji'ite teaching held that faith, as perfect belief, could neither increase nor decrease (later modified under pressure from Qur'anic verses suggesting otherwise).

**4. Predestination, mostly.** Murji'ites generally upheld a predestinarian cosmology, consistent with their Umayyad-era environment.

### Political Development

The Murji'a were initially a quietist movement counselling non-participation in civil war. Their popularity spread in cities exhausted by sectarian conflict. Paradoxically, their insistence on justice pushed them into greater political activity: they championed the rights of non-Arab Muslims (*mawali*), insisting that mere confession of belief — regardless of ethnic origin — sufficed for full Muslim status. This made them a vehicle for the grievances of the large Persian and Iraqi convert population. A Murji'ite revolt occurred between 728 and 746.

### Abu Hanifa and the Mainstream

The most prominent figure associated with mainstream Murji'ism was **Abu Hanifa** (c. 699–767), the Kufan eponym of the Hanafi legal school. Abu Hanifa's pacific doctrine distinguished itself from the radical fringe. His *al-Fiqh al-Akbar I* — arguably the earliest surviving Muslim creed — represents Murji'ite doctrine in its most organised form, opposing the Kharijites, Qadariyya, Shi'ites, and Jahmiyya, while affirming predestinarian belief and suspension of judgement on Ali and Uthman. The document shows how close mainstream Murji'ism already was to what would become Sunni orthodoxy.

### Legacy

Most Murji'ite positions were eventually absorbed into the Sunni synthesis. The name *Murji'a* became a Hanbali term of abuse, but the Ash'arites, Maturidis, and Hanafis all incorporated Murji'ite conclusions — particularly the separability of faith from works, and the principle that grave sinners remain believers. The name disappeared; the substance survived.

## The Qadariyya ("The Free-Willers")

### Origins

The term *qadar* refers to God's decree or measure over creation; the *Qadariyya* were those who denied, in whole or part, that God predetermined human actions. The name was applied by their opponents, who accused them of "denying the *qadar*" — i.e., divine predestination. This makes the label ironic: the Qadariyya claimed *humans* possessed genuine *qadar* (capacity to act); their opponents used the same word to mean God's predetermination.

The dispute was existentially urgent. The pre-Islamic Arab worldview assumed a predetermined fate (*dahr*). Early caliphs endorsed determinism, which served dynastic legitimacy by implying that God had ordained their rule and that opposition to it was futile. Pietists, concerned about their own moral accountability, found this theologically intolerable.

### Al-Hasan al-Basri and the Basran School

The foundational figure is **al-Hasan al-Basri** (646–728), a non-Arab Muslim (*mawla*) born in Medina, who moved to Basra after 663. Al-Hasan criticised the Umayyad governors of Iraq and was forced into hiding between 705 and 714. His theological position, formulated in an epistle to the caliph Abd al-Malik, cited Qur'anic verses indicating that humans are responsible for their actions. His key formulation: God creates only good; evil originates with humans or the devil; God has foreknowledge of human choices but this foreknowledge does not predetermine them.

Al-Hasan's position was *moderate* Qadarism — later Sunni traditionists reclaimed him as an exemplar of piety, distinguishing his restrained position from the more radical subsequent developments.

His leading disciple **Qatada ibn Di'ama** (d. 735) shared this position.

### Radical Qadarism and Political Revolt

A more thoroughgoing free-will doctrine — in which God neither foreknows nor decrees human actions — emerged shortly after al-Hasan's death, associated with a group of Basran Kharijites led by Shabib al-Najrani. This "strong" Qadarism attracted Umayyad hostility; the caliph Umar II attacked it while accepting al-Hasan's moderate version.

**Ghaylan al-Dimashqi** (d. between 731 and 735), a government secretary of Coptic origin, gave Qadarism its first revolutionary political expression, launching a campaign against the caliph Hisham (r. 724–43). The movement culminated in the coup of Yazid III against al-Walid II in 744, briefly implementing a Qadarite political programme. The caliph agreed to step down if he failed to uphold it — logically consistent with the Qadarite belief in human moral accountability. Political failure sent Qadarism into eclipse.

**Ma'bad al-Juhani** (d. 699) was the first Qadarite to be executed for his beliefs.

### Legacy

The Qadariyya subsequently split into two streams:
1. A pietistic strand re-absorbed by the proto-Sunni hadith scholars, who claimed al-Hasan al-Basri as their predecessor
2. A doctrinally defined alignment that fed into [[mutazilism|Mu'tazilism]]

The Mu'tazilites took up free will as one of their Five Principles (God's justice, *adl*, requiring genuine human agency) and elaborated it philosophically. The Qadariyya themselves disappeared as a distinct label.

The [[kharijites|Kharijites]]' parallel insistence on human responsibility for sin (Qur'an 4:79) made them natural precursors to the Qadarite position, and Blankinship notes that the Ibadi branch of Kharijism both paralleled and may have anticipated Qadarite and Mu'tazilite free-will doctrines (Winter, ch. 2).

## The Jahmiyya

### Origins and the Figure of Jahm ibn Safwan

The **Jahmiyya** take their name from **Jahm ibn Safwan** (d. 746), secretary to the rebel al-Harith ibn Surayj (d. 746) who called for a return to the Qur'an and Sunna against the worldly Umayyads. Jahm is the most enigmatic of the early theological figures: his actual teachings survive only in hostile later sources, making reconstruction difficult.

What is attributed to him:
- **Faith as internalised knowledge only.** Faith is merely knowledge in the heart, requiring no outward expression. This radicalised the Murji'ite separation of faith from works to its logical extreme.
- **Absolute predestination.** All human acts are determined by God.
- **The non-eternity of Paradise and Hell.** Both may be temporary rather than everlasting — an extreme position condemned universally.
- **The createdness of the Qur'an.** Jahm is sometimes credited with originating this doctrine, but Blankinship judges this "probable that they were not" actual positions of Jahm, since the question was not actively debated until after 800 CE (Winter, ch. 2).
- **Negation of the divine attributes.** A strongly negative theology that denied real existence to all divine attributes.

### The Problem of the Sources

Blankinship is explicit that Jahm's positions are "obscure, being mentioned only in much later, hostile sources" and that "no alleged followers of him are heard of for seventy years after his death" (Winter, ch. 2). The term *Jahmiyya* was subsequently deployed mainly by Hanbalites as a polemical label for Mu'tazilite-sympathetic positions, regardless of whether the target had any connection to Jahm himself.

This makes the Jahmiyya less a coherent sect than a retrospective category — a Hanbali polemical construct used to delegitimise rational theology by association with an early radical. The historical Jahm may have held none of the positions attributed to him by ninth-century Hanbali sources.

### Legacy

Despite the uncertainty, the *label* Jahmiyya had real effects: it was the primary term Hanbalites used to condemn anyone they regarded as denying the divine attributes or affirming the createdness of the Qur'an. Ahmad ibn Hanbal's resistance to the *mihna* was explicitly framed as resistance to *Jahmiyya* doctrine. The label thus structured the Sunni-Mu'tazilite confrontation without the historical Jahm having to have been a significant figure.

## Comparative Analysis

| Feature | Murji'a | Qadariyya | Jahmiyya |
|---|---|---|---|
| Core concern | Political: who to condemn among civil-war actors | Moral: is human action genuinely free? | Theological: what is faith; what are God's attributes? |
| Position on predestination | Mostly predestinarian | Denied full predestination | Absolute predestination (Jahm's own view) |
| Position on sin | Sin does not expel from Islam; judgment deferred | Sin is genuinely human and condemnable | Faith is interior; sin is not directly relevant to belief |
| Position on faith | Faith = belief; separable from works; indivisible | Varied | Faith = inward knowledge only; no external expression required |
| Sunni legacy | Absorbed into Hanafi/Maturidi synthesis | Absorbed into Mu'tazilism, then Shi'ite theology | Name used as polemical weapon; actual teachings mostly condemned |
| Primary figures | Abu Hanifa; al-Hasan ibn Muhammad | Al-Hasan al-Basri; Ghaylan al-Dimashqi | Jahm ibn Safwan |

## Historiography

The three labels are primarily known through hostile sources: Hanbali heresiographies, Ash'arite refutations, and later biographical dictionaries that had their own reasons to distort. Modern scholarship has substantially revised the picture.

Josef van Ess, *Theologie und Gesellschaft* (Berlin, 1991–5) is the definitive study of this period. W. Montgomery Watt, *The Formative Period of Islamic Thought* (Edinburgh, 1973) provides an accessible English synthesis. Michael Cook, *Early Muslim Dogma* (Cambridge, 1981) is essential on the Qadariyya specifically.

Khalid Blankinship's chapter in Winter's *Cambridge Companion* (2008) emphasises the priority of political over doctrinal factors in generating these sects: "the earliest Muslim schisms emerged over what were in the first instance political matters" and their doctrinal fixity was a secondary development.

The collection's coverage of this period is moderate through the Winter volume; no dedicated primary-source texts on these sects are in the collection.
