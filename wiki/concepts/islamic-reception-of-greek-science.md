# Islamic Reception of Greek Science

The **Islamic reception of Greek science** refers to the multi-century process by which Muslim scholars encountered the inherited Greek scientific corpus — above all the astronomical and philosophical works of Ptolemy and Aristotle — and engaged it not as a passive or reverential preservation but as a living body of knowledge subject to correction, reconstruction, and ultimately replacement. The concept is central to George Saliba's revisionist historiography in [[saliba-islamic-science-renaissance-2007]].

## The Classical Narrative vs. Saliba's Alternative

The dominant ("classical") narrative holds that Islamic civilization acted as a conduit, preserving Greek texts during European late antiquity and the early medieval period before transmitting them to Latin Europe in the 12th–13th centuries. In this account, Muslim scholars added little of their own and entered a period of decline after roughly the 12th century.

Saliba argues that this narrative is factually wrong on almost every point. He demonstrates that:

1. The translation of Greek texts was itself driven by specific **social and political competition** within Abbasid society, not by an abstract love of learning.
2. Translators corrected, not merely copied, the texts they were rendering — al-Ḥajjāj b. Maṭar corrected a numerical error in Ptolemy's Almagest IV.2 during the translation itself (see [[al-hajjaj-ibn-matar]]).
3. Islamic astronomers developed a sustained, internally coherent tradition of **critical objections** (the [[shukuk-tradition|*shukūk* tradition]]) to Ptolemaic astronomy beginning in the 9th century and continuing through the 16th.
4. The tradition did not decline after the 12th century but produced increasingly sophisticated alternative models that anticipated Copernicus.

## Social Context of the Reception

The encounter with Greek science did not occur in an intellectual vacuum. Saliba argues it was structurally conditioned by the **'Abd al-Malik reforms** (late 7th c.), which made Arabic the language of bureaucratic administration. This created a competitive market for different kinds of expertise:

- **"Foreign sciences"** (Greek, Persian, Sanskrit) were associated with non-Arab specialists who occupied the highest levels of government service — physicians, astronomers, advisors.
- **"Islamic sciences"** (Quranic studies, Arabic grammar, jurisprudence, *ḥadīth*) were the domain of Arab scholars and lower-ranking bureaucrats whose authority rested on mastery of Arabic.

This polarization, expressed in the *shu'ūbiyya* movement of the early 9th c. (Arab-vs.-non-Arab polemics), meant that the **importers of Greek science had to defend their enterprise constantly**. Any flaw in the imported knowledge could be used by opponents to discredit the whole project. This competitive pressure generated the very rigor and critical sophistication that is the subject of Saliba's book:

> "It was in such an environment that the newly imported Greek sciences were cast." (*Islamic Science and the Making of the European Renaissance*, ch. 3)

Practically, this meant that a translator like al-Ḥajjāj could not afford to let what appeared to be a mathematical error stand in the Almagest. And a generation later, astronomers were driven to verify, correct, and ultimately surpass every major parameter in Ptolemaic astronomy.

## The Epistemological Division: "Foreign" vs. "Islamic" Sciences

One of Saliba's structural contributions is the analysis of how the early division between *'ulūm al-awa'il* (sciences of the ancients, i.e. foreign sciences) and *'ulūm al-shar'iyya* (religious sciences) shaped Islamic intellectual life throughout the medieval period. This division:

- Was not primarily theological in origin but arose from bureaucratic competition.
- Persisted and "could very quickly gain ground in this early period and could persist throughout Islamic intellectual history."
- Explains why astronomers strategically separated *'ilm al-hay'a* from astrology — to align their discipline with the religious establishment rather than with suspect divinatory arts (see [[ilm-al-hayya]]).

## Phases of Critical Engagement

The critical reception of Greek science unfolded in several overlapping registers, all documented from the 9th century onward:

### 1. Correction of Factual Errors and Parameters
Islamic astronomers quickly discovered that virtually every major Ptolemaic astronomical parameter was incorrect. Between roughly 820–870 CE alone, they revised:

- **Precession rate**: Ptolemy's value of 1°/100 years corrected to ~1°/66 years; modern value ~0°50'/year.
- **Inclination of the ecliptic**: Ptolemy's 23°51'20" corrected to ~23°33°, a value "still in use today."
- **Solar apogee position**: found to have moved ~11° since Ptolemy's time, now understood as correlating with precession.
- **Lunar month length**: al-Ḥajjāj corrected Ptolemy's report of 29;31,50,8,20d to the arithmetically correct 29;31,50,8,9,20d in his very translation of the Almagest.

These corrections were made possible by new observational methods, especially the *fuṣūl* (seasons) method developed in the early 9th century, which was superior to Ptolemy's equinox/solstice method for determining the solar apogee.

### 2. Mathematical Updating
Greek astronomy used chord functions (Menelaos theorem). By the 9th century, Islamic astronomers possessed a fully developed trigonometric system of sines, cosines, and tangents — developed partly through Sanskrit inheritance — that was demonstrably more powerful. Astronomers immediately began updating Ptolemaic mathematics. Ḥabash al-Ḥāsib (fl. c. 850) used these functions freely in his *zīj*. Naṣīr al-Dīn al-Ṭūsī's *Taḥrīr* (1247) explicitly added spherical sine and tangent theorems to replace Ptolemy's chord table, noting that "the method of the moderns, which uses the sines at this point instead of the chords, is easier to use."

### 3. Cosmological Critique
The most fundamental critique arose from reading the *Almagest* together with Ptolemy's *Planetary Hypotheses*. The *Almagest* treated planetary models mathematically; the *Hypotheses* insisted those models referred to real physical spheres. But Ptolemy's mathematical devices — above all the **equant** — required a physical sphere to move uniformly around an axis that did not pass through its center. This was physically impossible in Aristotelian cosmology. The contradiction was noted as early as Muḥammad b. Mūsā b. Shākir (d. 873), barely two decades after the first Arabic translation of the Almagest. See [[shukuk-tradition]] for the full tradition of critiques.

### 4. Theoretical Objections: The Philosophy of Science
By the time of Mu'ayyad al-Dīn al-'Urḍī (d. 1266), the critique had matured into explicit philosophy of science. 'Urḍī distinguished between what astronomical theory was licensed to do by observations alone and what was Ptolemy's mere conjecture (*ḥads*):

> "As for the method of conjecture (*ḥads*), he (i.e. Ptolemy) has no priority in it, (especially) after his mistakes have been clearly exposed. If anyone else ever finds something, which agrees with the principles, as well as the particular motions of the planet which were found by observation, then that person would have a greater claim to the truth."

This is a sophisticated epistemological claim: Ptolemy's authority extends only as far as his observations and proofs; his theorizing is no more privileged than anyone else's, particularly once he has been shown to contradict his own stated principles.

### 5. Alternatives and the New Astronomy
Critique was not an end in itself. From the 9th century onward, Muslim astronomers developed **alternative models** to replace the defective Ptolemaic ones. These included the *mumtaḥan* (Verified) astronomical tables of the Ma'mūn period and — most consequentially — the new mathematical devices of the [[maragha-school|Maragha school]] (13th c.) and [[ibn-al-shatir|Ibn al-Shāṭir]] (14th c.) that eliminated the equant problem while preserving predictive accuracy. See [[islamic-astronomy]].

## The "Decline" Myth
A recurrent Saliba argument throughout chapter 3 is that the supposed "decline" of Islamic science after the 12th century is an artifact of the classical narrative's assumptions, not a historical reality. The [[shukuk-tradition]] continued to generate new texts through the 16th and 17th centuries; Shams al-Dīn al-Khafrī (d. 1550) and Bahā' al-Dīn al-'Āmilī (d. 1622) and their commentators continued to engage the Ptolemaic problems actively. By the early 16th century, "no self-respecting astronomer would have continued to uphold the long-discarded and obsolete astronomy of Ptolemy."

## Historiographical Significance
Saliba's account of the Islamic reception of Greek science is itself a historiographical intervention against:

- **Eurocentric narratives** of scientific progress that treat Islamic civilization as passive custodians.
- **"Decline thesis"** interpretations (e.g., the view that the closure of the "gates of ijtihad" stifled Islamic science after 1200).
- **Internalist history of science** that ignores social and political conditions of knowledge production.

The chapter's key methodological move is to show that the social conditions of reception — the competitive Abbasid bureaucratic environment — directly generated the intellectual rigor that produced original science. Structure and content cannot be separated.
