# Christianity in Japan (the *Kirishitan* Century, 1549–1639)

The roughly ninety-year career of Catholic Christianity in [[japan|Japan]] — from **Francis
Xavier's** landing at Kagoshima in 1549 to the **expulsion of the Portuguese in 1639** — a
mission that won perhaps 300,000 converts and then was annihilated by the most thorough
religious persecution in Japanese history. The "Christian Century" (C. R. Boxer's phrase) is
treated by **Jurgis Elisonas** in [[cambridge-history-japan-v4-early-modern|CHJ Vol. 4]]
(ch. 7, "Christianity and the Daimyo"). It is the foreign-religious half of the inward turn
that produced **[[sakoku]]**.

## Definition and Scope

Christianity reached Japan through the **Portuguese trade**: the same ships that carried the
Macao [[global-silver-trade|silk-for-silver]] commerce carried the **Jesuit** missionaries,
and the religion advanced where its **commercial and political utility** did. It was
concentrated in **western Japan**, above all northern **Kyūshū** — the trading ports of
Hirado and, after 1571, **Nagasaki**, which by the early seventeenth century was a city of
~25,000 almost entirely Christian.

## The mission and its method

- **Deus or Dainichi?** Elisonas dwells on the **translation problem** at the mission's root.
  Xavier, a poor linguist, was misled by his guide **Yajirō** into preaching the Christian God
  as ***Dainichi*** — Mahāvairocana, the central Buddha of [[japanese-buddhism|Shingon]] —
  so that he "began his mission in Japan by preaching Dainichi." The Buddhist vocabulary
  eased entry by creating a (deceptive) common ground, and Xavier's coming from "Tenjiku"
  (India, the homeland of Buddhism) lent him charisma; the Jesuits later corrected course and
  rendered God by the Latin *Deus*.
- **The symbiosis of daimyo, missionaries, and merchants.** Conversion was bound to commerce
  and power. Kyūshū daimyo — **Ōmura Sumitada** (the first Christian daimyo), **Ōtomo
  Sōrin**, **Arima** — sought the Portuguese trade that followed the priests, and some
  granted the Jesuits **Nagasaki** outright (a Jesuit-administered "colony"). Mass conversions
  of whole domains followed the lord's decision; the faith took deepest root where it had been
  "nourished for three generations."

## Suppression

- **Hideyoshi's edicts.** [[toyotomi-hideyoshi|Hideyoshi]] issued the first **expulsion edict
  in 1587** during his Kyūshū campaign, opening "Although Japan is the **land of the gods**
  (*shinkoku*) … wicked teachings … must not be allowed" — though he did not enforce it
  rigorously. After the **San Felipe** affair he had **26 Christians crucified at Nagasaki in
  1597** (the "Twenty-Six Martyrs").
- **The Tokugawa anti-Christian system.** [[tokugawa-ieyasu|Ieyasu]] and especially his
  successors made eradication systematic. A general **ban (1614)** ordered the missionaries
  expelled; many defied it and went underground (the Jesuits claimed 17,000 baptisms under
  persecution, 1614–26). Persecution intensified under Hidetada (the **Great Martyrdom of
  Nagasaki, 1622**) and was "perfected" under **Iemitsu**. Elisonas describes its **two
  faces**: inward, the hunting-down of all believers to apostatize or die; outward, the
  conviction that the "Christian peril" could be eliminated only by **total isolation** from
  its sources. The five **closed-country directives (*sakoku-rei*, 1633–39)** fused three
  interlocking aims — banning Japanese travel abroad (a capital crime after 1635), reducing
  the European presence, and the merciless persecution of Christianity — culminating in the
  **expulsion of the Portuguese (1639)** and the beheading of the 61-man Macao embassy that
  came in 1640 to plead the ruling's reversal.
- **The machinery.** A central **Office of the Inquisition** (*shūmon aratame yaku*,
  est. 1640, lasting to 1792) coordinated the purge; daimyo appointed religious commissioners;
  suspects were made to trample on holy images (*fumie*) and every household was registered at
  a Buddhist temple (the *terauke* system — turning [[japanese-buddhism|Buddhism]] into an
  organ of state surveillance). The **Shimabara Rebellion (1637)** — a rising of Christian
  peasants and rōnin in Kyūshū, crushed with great slaughter — was the trigger that hardened
  seclusion into final form.

## Aftermath: the kakure Kirishitan

By the 1660s "there were practically no Christians left in Japan": some **3,000 had died for
the faith** and the mass had been driven to apostasy. A few isolated groups of
**"crypto-Christians" (*kakure Kirishitan*)** survived in remote Kyūshū, but deprived of
priests and texts they "imperceptibly drifted … into a syncretic folk creed tinctured with
Buddhism and Shintō," only to be rediscovered after Japan reopened in the nineteenth century.

## The wider religious settlement (New Cambridge History of Japan)

The [[howell-early-modern-japan-asia-world|New Cambridge History of Japan Vol. II]] (Mark
Teeuwen, ch. 13) sets the anti-Christian system within the **whole religious order** of
[[edo-period|Tokugawa]] Japan. The compulsory **temple-registration (*terauke*)** system that
began as anti-Christian surveillance became the backbone of a state-managed religious
settlement: every household affiliated with a [[japanese-buddhism|Buddhist]] temple, while
[[shinto|Shintō]] shrines, popular cults, and pilgrimage flourished alongside in a broadly
**syncretic** religious life. On this reading the extirpation of Christianity was one element
of a larger Tokugawa **domestication of religion** — turning institutional religion into an
instrument of social registration and order rather than a rival to the state.
