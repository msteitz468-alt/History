# The Peace and Truce of God (Pax Dei, Treuga Dei)

## Definition

The **Peace of God** (*Pax Dei*) and the later **Truce of God** (*Treuga Dei*) were a series of church-led movements, beginning in southern Gaul (Aquitaine, Burgundy) in the late tenth century, that sought to limit private warfare and protect non-combatants by ecclesiastical sanction. The **Peace** protected categories of persons and property — clergy, the poor, peasants, merchants, churches, livestock, agricultural tools — from the violence of armed men (the *armati*), under threat of excommunication, with the protections proclaimed at great open-air councils where relics were assembled and oaths sworn. The **Truce**, developed from c. 1027, prohibited fighting during specified holy seasons and days (eventually from Wednesday evening to Monday morning, and in Advent and Lent).

[[reuter-ncmh-v3-1999|NCMH III]] treats the Peace as one of the most striking features of the long tenth century's religious history — the contemporaries **Adhémar of Chabannes** and **Radulf (Rodulfus) Glaber** stress it heavily as a mass movement, though Reuter notes other observers say less, leaving its scale debated.

## Context and Interpretation

The Peace movement is bound up with the [[feudal-revolution-debate|"feudal revolution"]] thesis. In the mutationist reading, it is a **symptom and response** to the collapse of public order around 1000: as royal and comital justice failed and castle-based banal lords waged private *werra*, the **bishops and abbots** stepped into the vacuum, mobilising the populace and the cult of relics to impose a peace the state could no longer guarantee. Fossier in NCMH III lists "justice and the Peace of God" as the (as-yet imperfect) barriers erected against aristocratic violence, which he sees declining after c. 1000.

In a broader religious reading (Reuter, after Tellenbach and Head/Landes), the Peace is one expression of the long tenth century's **intensified lay participation in Christianity** — alongside mass pilgrimage, the veneration of relics, and the millennial religiosity around the dates 1000 and 1033 — rather than evidence of simple ecclesiastical decadence and worldliness.

## Significance

The Peace and Truce of God:

- placed the **church at the centre of public order** in regions where lay public authority had dissolved, prefiguring the assertive papal-reform church of the eleventh century;
- helped articulate an emerging **ideology of legitimate vs. illegitimate violence**, channelling the warrior class toward sanctioned targets — a current that scholars connect to the later ideology of **knighthood** and, ultimately, to the **Crusade** (Urban II proclaimed both the Truce and the First Crusade);
- and contributed to the fixing of the village around its church and relics (see [[encellulement]]).

## Contested Points

- The **scale and novelty** of the movement (whether it was a genuine mass phenomenon or is over-weighted by a few vivid chroniclers).
- Its **causal relation** to the breakdown of public order — circular in the mutationist account, since the same scarcity of sources is read both as evidence of new violence and as evidence of new recording.
- Its **geography**: a southern- and western-French / Burgundian / Catalan phenomenon that, like the *mutation* itself, fits poorly onto Germany and England, where royal authority persisted.
