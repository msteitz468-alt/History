## Definition

*Barzakh* (Arabic: "barrier," "isthmus," "partition") designates the intermediary state or realm that the human soul inhabits between individual death and the collective resurrection at the end of time. The term appears in the Quran at 23:100 — "Behind them is a barzakh until the day they are resurrected" — and was developed by kalam theologians and Sufi philosophers into a fully elaborated doctrine of post-mortem existence.

The barzakh answers the practical question of what happens to individual souls in the period between personal death (which occurs continuously throughout history) and the final resurrection (which is a single collective event). It is part of the broader structure of [[islamic-eschatology]].

Sources draw on Marcia Hermansen's chapter in [[winter-cambridge-companion-islamic-theology-2008]].

## Theological Content

### The Kalam View

[[asharism]] and [[mutazilism|Maturidism]] made belief in the barzakh a mandatory article of faith. Prophetic traditions describe:
- Immediate examination by angels after death, asking the soul about its religious affiliation
- An anticipatory experience in the grave (*adhab al-qabr*, "punishment of the grave") that foreshadows eternal destiny
- The continuation of soul-life in a state comparable to deep sleep, from which the trumpet blast of resurrection will awaken it

Medieval theologians debated the mode of grave punishment: is it physical (requiring some reconstitution of the body) or psychological (occurring in the imaginative faculty through dream-like forms and images)? Al-Ghazali's *Remembrance of Death and the Afterlife* (Book XL of the *Ihya' 'ulum al-din*) provides the most extensive Asharite treatment, discussing both physical and psychological dimensions.

**Mutazilite rejection.** Most Mutazilis, and perhaps the Kharijites, rejected the barzakh as an article of faith, disputing the scriptural basis for a conscious intermediary state. The debate hinged on interpretation of relevant hadith about the grave.

### The Philosophical-Sufi View

Avicenna (d. 1037) and the falsafa tradition took a psychological reading: the soul after death exists in an imaginal or spiritual mode, not dependent on the body. The "return" in Quran 89:27–28 ("O contented soul, return to your Lord") was interpreted as reunion with an archetypal pre-existing source of the person's essential reality.

Ibn Arabi (d. 1240) elaborated the *barzakh* into a fundamental cosmological concept: the imaginal world (*alam al-mithal* or *alam al-khayal*) is itself a barzakh between the material world and pure spirit. The soul after death exists as an "imaginal form" in this realm — existentially real, but not identical to either gross matter or pure intellect. This makes the barzakh the arena where the soul continues to develop and where the eschatological symbols (garden, fire, resurrection, judgement) are experienced as archetypal realities. See [[philosophical-sufism]].

## The Barzakh as Sleep

A recurring analogy: "This intermediary stage is comparable to a sleep from which one will be awakened at the resurrection, just as the present life also resembles a dream in comparison to the subsequent stages." The comparison frames human time-experience as nested levels of wakefulness, with the final resurrection as the fullest awakening into unmediated reality.
