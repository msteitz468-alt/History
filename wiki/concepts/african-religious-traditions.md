# African Religious Traditions

## Definition and Scope

**African religious traditions** refers to the indigenous belief systems of sub-Saharan Africa — systems characterised by belief in a High God, a layered spirit world, ancestor veneration, divination, and the integration of religious life with social governance. The term is necessarily a scholarly construct (**etic**) grouping an enormous diversity of local traditions. Davidson (1966) summarises them using anthropologist William Howells' phrase: "a godly religion of a most general kind" — meaning a religion with recognisably theistic attributes, not the fetish-worship or "hellish delusions" that 17th-century missionaries described.

These traditions coexisted with — and persistently resisted displacement by — [[islam-in-africa|Islam]] and [[african-christianity|Ethiopian Christianity]] over more than a millennium. They remain structurally active in many communities today.

## Emic vs. Etic

The emic level is highly local: each community developed what Davidson calls its own "spiritual charter" — a particular form of spiritual identity and reassurance linking the living to specific founding ancestors and to a local set of lesser gods. There is no single pan-African religious system; the shared structural features (High God, intermediary spirits, ancestor veneration, specialist priests) are the product of scholarly comparison, not African self-description.

At the emic level Africans distinguished: appeals to the High God (indirect, through lesser gods); appeals to tutelary and nature gods (spirits of storm, mountains, rivers, iron — e.g. the Yoruba Olodumare and Orisha-nla); the ancestor cult (the specific dead of one's authorised lineage, distinct from the undifferentiated dead); and sorcery/witchcraft as a third level of supernatural intervention.

## Structural Features

### The High God and Cosmology

Davidson reports a near-universal belief across sub-Saharan Africa in a single High God conceived not in human form but as a "Life-Force" — the energy differentiating life from matter. The High God seldom intervenes directly in human affairs: Yoruba mythology has Olodumare make the earth in four days but delegate the actual work to Orisha-nla. A recurring myth explains the god's withdrawal from direct contact with humanity — in Dinka tradition a greedy woman hit God with her hoe; in Ashanti an old woman jolted him with her pounding stick while making fufu. Both variants share the pattern of human transgression ending divine immanence.

### Ancestor Veneration

Ancestor worship was, in Davidson's analysis, "one of the central organising factors in African society." It regulated individuals, villages, and polities. The dead do not die utterly — they rejoin the Life-Force but retain spiritual identity and remain members of the living community. An African chief explained to a British commission that land belonged to "a vast family of which many are dead, few are living, and countless members are unborn."

The relationship with ancestors was contractual: the living owed ceremonial duty; the ancestors owed protection and prosperity. Not all ancestors were worshipped indiscriminately — only those in the direct authorised succession from a community founder could act as intermediaries. Davidson compares these "appointed ancestors" to canonised saints.

Puberty initiation introduced young people to the ancestors, their founding narratives, and their cult objects — formally incorporating the initiate into the community's "secret society" (open to all members, secret only in ceremony).

### Secret Societies and Governance

Institutions such as the Poro society of the Guinea coast wielded both religious and political authority. Davidson notes that 15th-century European mariners could neither buy nor sell goods along the Guinea coast without the elders of the Poro society's approval. These societies "interpreted the will of the ancestors" but also arranged public festivals, settled secular disputes, and set market prices — demonstrating the inseparability of religious and political authority in pre-colonial African governance.

### Divination and Witchcraft

At a third level, the supernatural manifested through sorcery and witchcraft. Africans distinguished between deliberate sorcery (conscious evil magic), unpremeditated witchcraft (malicious spirits leaving human hosts at night), and good magic. Specialist witch doctors (Davidson notes the term is a European label) served diagnostic, therapeutic, and dispute-resolution functions — combining herbal medicine, psychological insight, and knowledge of community relations. The Zande of the southern Sudan, described by Evans-Pritchard and cited by Davidson, used a poison oracle (administered to chickens) to identify the supernatural agent behind misfortune — while maintaining a clear empirical distinction between the snake's poison and the witch's will that sent the snake.

Davidson stresses that African witchcraft practices were comparable in structure to those of 16th–17th century Christian Europe; David Livingstone noted that Scottish witch-trial ordeals operated on a logically parallel (if inverted) mechanism.

### Specialist Priests

Shrine priests (e.g. the Ashanti *okomfo*) underwent multi-year training: herbs for ritual dance and spiritual possession, laws and taboos of the god, arts of divination and incantation. Their authority derived from knowledge of herbal medicine, psychological skill, and mediation with the spirit world. Davidson's Priestess of the Python exemplifies this: through questioning, she identified not supernatural cause but social breach (neglect of kin duty), resolving the client's anxiety through moral reintegration.

## Islam and Christianity in Relation to Indigenous Traditions

Both Islam and Christianity coexisted with indigenous African religion for centuries without displacing it. Davidson attributes the persistence to two factors: (1) indigenous religion's therapeutic and psychological efficacy — its practical "physical and mental healing" function that competing faiths could not fully replicate; and (2) its function as social identity, extending life beyond death through communal continuity. "For Africans the goal in life was to live as one's fathers had lived. It might not be progress, but it was stability." The "backsliding" that Christian missionaries repeatedly complained of reflects indigenous religion's structural depth rather than shallow conversion.

## Ethiopian Christianity as a Distinctive Case

Ethiopian Christianity, established in 333 CE, represents a distinctive African-Christian synthesis. When Egypt fell to Islam in 640 CE, Ethiopia was cut off from Mediterranean Christianity for over 800 years. The result was a tradition deeply intertwined with Old Testament narrative and with Solomonid dynastic mythology: Ethiopian Christian rulers claimed descent from Menelik I, son of Solomon and the Queen of Sheba; the Zagwe dynasty (who usurped the throne c. 1100 CE) traced ancestry to Moses. King Lalibela commissioned ten rock-hewn churches at his capital in the 13th century — a project with both political purpose (to legitimise Zagwe rule against the Solomonids and win clerical support) and genuine piety. The churches at Lalibela absorb Greek, Roman, Byzantine, Muslim (pointed arches), Persian, and possibly Central Asian and Chinese motifs through Ethiopia's commercial and cultural ties with the Middle East — making them a monument to Christian cosmopolitanism under isolation. Ethiopia remained architecturally capable of what Davidson notes the great European architects "were scarcely achieving better results" in contemporaneous stone construction. The Solomonid dynasty was restored in 1270 and ruled continuously thereafter, representing "one of the longest uninterrupted lines of royal succession in history."

## Limitations and Critiques

- **Homogenisation**: The category "African religious traditions" flattens enormous diversity. The Zande, Ashanti, Yoruba, Dinka, and Tallensi traditions differ substantially; Davidson's synthesis risks an ethnographic essentialism.
- **Temporal presentism**: Davidson and his anthropological sources treat mid-20th-century practices as transparent windows onto timeless ancient traditions — a methodologically problematic move challenged by later scholars who emphasise historical change within indigenous systems.
- **Evolutionary framework**: Davidson's comparison of "pagan Africans" to "Christians of the Middle Ages" (both living in an "age of faith") is sympathetic but still frames African religion on a European developmental ladder.
- **Popular bias**: *African Kingdoms* was a 1966 corrective polemic; its characterisations of indigenous religion should be cross-checked against specialist anthropological and historical literature.
