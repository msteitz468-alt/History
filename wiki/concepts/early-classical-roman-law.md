# Early Classical Roman Law and Jurisprudence

The beginning of the **"classical" period of Roman private law** under Augustus and the
Julio-Claudians — when Rome's professional **jurists** articulated "the most conspicuous and
influential Roman contribution to Western civilization." Treated in [[bowman-cah-v10-1996|CAH X]]
Ch. 21 (**Bruce W. Frier**); for the *application* of law see [[roman-imperial-justice]].

## Formulary procedure and the rise of the jurists

Classical law rested on the **formulary procedure** — a two-stage trial dividing **jurisdiction**
(the praetor, *in iure*, deciding whether the plaintiff stated an acceptable cause of action and
framing the ***formula***) from **adjudication** (a lay ***iudex***, *apud iudicem*, hearing the
advocates and returning a verdict that, as a rule, "could be neither reviewed nor appealed"). The
key participants — praetor, *iudex*, advocates — "normally had no special competence in law." The
**juristic movement** therefore began *outside* the judicial system: self-styled experts
(*iurisconsulti*) assisting laymen, who in the first century BCE (Q. Mucius Scaevola, Servius
Sulpicius Rufus) began to study legal norms on an intensive, **"scientific" basis** — "a true
legal science under the control of professionals" that, by the late Republic, already determined
large areas of law.

## Augustus' procedural reforms

Frier's central argument is that the Principate did **not** at first diminish juristic
independence — on the contrary, it consolidated it. The jurists, drawn from the **Italian and
equestrian stock** of Augustus' new oligarchy, were well placed to "interpret the aspirations of
the new regime within the limited but important domain of private law," and emperors saw value in
**preserving private law's independence as a symbol of legitimacy and continuity**. Augustus
three times **refused a general grant of power over law and morals** (*cura legum et morum*),
concentrating instead on procedure:

- The ***lex Iulia de iudiciis privatis*** (probably **17 BCE**) eliminated the archaic *legis
  actio* and made **formulary procedure** the norm for private suits at Rome; it regulated the
  *album* of judges and introduced the distinction between **statutory trials** (*iudicia
  legitima*, between citizens at Rome before a single judge, valid for 18 months) and trials
  dependent on magisterial office — giving the **urban praetor's court a special standing**.
- The ***ius respondendi*** — Augustus began granting **selected jurists the right to issue
  opinions (*responsa*) backed by his own authority**, which carried "very great, if not
  determinative, weight" in trials. This "increased the authority of law" while letting the
  emperor **avoid deciding questions of private law himself**, isolated a privileged expert
  group, and created a **new instrument of patronage** binding the jurists (granted the right
  only on entering the Senate, with Tiberius extending it to the equestrian Masurius Sabinus) to
  the governing elite.

The result, Frier stresses, was an "astonishing situation, seldom replicated in Western law": the
jurists came to **command the judicial system from outside it** — the *lex Iulia* giving
procedure a new coherence, the *ius respondendi* giving the best juristic thought "direct and
immediate effect."

## Labeo and Capito

Early classical jurisprudence is known mainly through **M. Antistius Labeo**. Augustus' reign was
marked by the rivalry of Labeo and **C. Ateius Capito** — "personal and political." Capito (a
Sullan centurion's grandson) was the **sycophantic courtier**; Labeo (son of one of Caesar's
assassins) the man of "prickly insistence on constitutional details," driven by an "excessive,
foolhardy passion for ***libertas***" — refusing the consulate Augustus offered, who then
advanced Capito's date in revenge. But the jurists judged the rivalry differently: where Capito
"clung narrowly to received views," **Labeo "undertook numerous innovations"** on the basis of
his command of language and other learning. The consequence is dramatic — Capito is **all but
ignored** by later jurists, while **Labeo is cited more than any jurist before the high classical
period**, "the first indisputably 'classical' jurist," remembered for a more conscious
**methodological** approach (solving problems through the direct interpretation of fixed texts —
statutes, edicts, contracts, wills). Frier notes the paradox that the *politically*
traditionalist Labeo was the greater *legal* innovator.

## The long erosion

Frier sets the Augustan settlement against its slow undoing: "almost at once" began the process
by which the balanced system would be **eroded and supplanted** — the gradual replacement of
formulary procedure by **extraordinary *cognitio*** under imperial officials, and the rise of
**imperial rescripts** as a source of law. But "neither change was complete until after the end
of the classical period" in the mid-third century — and in the interval the jurists "introduced
changes which were permanently to affect Western understanding of what law is."

## Significance

The Augustan reform of procedure and the creation of the *ius respondendi* launched the
**classical age of Roman jurisprudence** — a centuries-long professional discussion that produced
the conceptual architecture preserved in **Justinian's Digest** (533 CE) and inherited by the
entire Western legal tradition. That this flowered under an autocracy, by the emperor's choice to
**leave private law to independent experts** as a guarantee of continuity, is Frier's central
irony.
