# Fundamentalism

Originally an emic term coined within early twentieth-century American Protestantism to describe a movement defending core scriptural doctrines (biblical inerrancy chief among them) against theological modernism and liberal biblical criticism. Historians and sociologists of religion later extended "fundamentalism" as an etic comparative category to describe structurally similar movements across other religious traditions — most prominently in the study of modern political Islam, but also applied to Jewish, Hindu, and other religious revivalist and traditionalist movements that combine strict scriptural literalism, opposition to secularizing modernity, and often explicit political mobilization.

The comparative use of "fundamentalism" beyond its Protestant origin is contested: critics argue the term imports Christian theological assumptions (particularly around scriptural inerrancy) that map imperfectly onto other traditions' different relationships to text, law, and authority, and that its association with the American religious right can carry misleading connotations when applied to, for instance, Islamist political movements with very different social bases and goals. Scholars of [[concepts/political-islam|political Islam]] often prefer more specific terms (Islamism, Salafism) precisely to avoid these conflations, while acknowledging "religious fundamentalism" retains value as a broad comparative frame for movements sharing anti-modernist, scripturally literalist, and often politically mobilized characteristics across traditions.
