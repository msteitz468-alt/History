# The Carolingian Aristocracy

## Definition

The **Carolingian aristocracy** was the transregional noble elite — the families from whom counts,
bishops, abbots, and royal officers were drawn — whose collaboration was the indispensable basis of
the [[carolingian-empire]] and whose competing interests were also its chief solvent. In
[[mckitterick-ncmh-v2-1995|NCMH II]] (Airlie, Ch. 16; Nelson, Ch. 15) the nobility is the pivotal
class through which Carolingian power was exercised and contested.

## Wealth Without Taxation

A structural feature distinguished the Carolingian elite from its Roman predecessor: the Carolingian
state **did not tax its aristocracy**. NCMH II frames this with a memorable image — the Carolingian
elite "took a very much bigger slice of the available cake than their predecessors in the Roman empire
had of theirs" (though the whole cake was far smaller), precisely because there was no Roman-style
fiscal levy on elite wealth. Aristocrats grew rich through the **spoils of war**, through more aggressive
lordship (**Grundherrschaft** — heavier burdens on the peasantry), and through **office** (*honores*).
Kings extracted a return not by taxation but through institutionalised **"annual gifts,"** the sale of
high office (**simony**, in the ecclesiastical case), and a share of the **profits of justice**.

## Honores and the King–Noble Bargain

The relationship of king and noble was a negotiated **sharing of resources and power**. The king granted
*honores* — counties, abbeys, benefices, a cut of judicial fines — and the noble owed loyalty, military
service, and counsel. Both shares could grow together when conquest and economic activity expanded the
"cake"; but when expansion stopped, the same magnates who had built the empire by fighting together
**turned to fighting each other**, the dynamic visible in the civil wars after 840
([[battle-of-fontenoy-841]]). Nithard's account of the diplomacy of 840–843 exposes a distinct class of
**lesser aristocrats**, dependent on great magnates yet making claims on them, whose shifting allegiances
kings had to court.

## Significance

The Carolingian nobility is the key to the [[nature-of-carolingian-government|debate over Carolingian
government]]: an empire ruled *through* a self-interested, untaxed, land-based aristocracy was powerful so
long as royal patronage and conquest aligned noble and royal interests, and fragile once they diverged. The
honor-and-benefice nexus is also a principal root of later **[[feudalism|feudal]]** relations (see
[[vassalage]]).

## Historiography

NCMH II reflects the major German and Anglophone scholarship on the *Adel* (Tellenbach, Reuter, Werner): the
nobility as a transregional, intermarried, office-holding class whose "imperial aristocracy" bound the empire
together. Debates concern how far noble power was autonomous versus royally derived, and whether the rise of
aristocratic landlordship necessarily drained royal resources (Nelson argues it need not have, if the economy
was growing).
