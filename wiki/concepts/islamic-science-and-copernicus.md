## Definition and Scope

The "Maragha Connection" — or the Islamic-Copernican connection — is the scholarly argument that
the core mathematical tools used by Nicolaus Copernicus (1473–1543) in *De Revolutionibus* (1543)
and the *Commentariolus* (before 1514) were not independently invented but were derived from the
planetary-theory tradition of the Islamic world, specifically from astronomers of the Maragha school
(13th–15th centuries). The two key theorems at issue are the [[tusi-couple|Ṭūsī Couple]] and
[[urdi-lemma|ʿUrḍī's Lemma]], and the key models are the lunar, upper-planet, and Mercury models
of [[ibn-al-shatir|Ibn al-Shāṭir]] (d. 1375).

Saliba's book provides the most comprehensive synthesis of this argument, framing it as the
argumentative climax of his broader thesis: that Islamic astronomical science was not a passive
"preservation" of Greek knowledge but an active, critical reform tradition — one whose highest
achievements reached Renaissance Europe and supplied the mathematical scaffolding for the
Copernican revolution.

## The Mathematical Evidence

### The Ṭūsī Couple

[[nasir-al-din-tusi|Naṣīr al-Dīn al-Ṭūsī]] (d. 1274) formalized a theorem in 1260–61 (first
articulated in 1247 in his *Taḥrīr al-majisṭī*) that allows linear motion to be generated from
two circular motions: a small sphere rotating inside a large sphere of twice its radius at twice
the speed in the opposite direction causes a point on the small sphere to oscillate along a
diameter of the large sphere. This theorem solved a structural problem in Ptolemaic latitude theory.

Copernicus uses an identical theorem in *De Revolutionibus* III, 4, and provides a proof
strikingly similar to Ṭūsī's. In 1973, Willy Hartner demonstrated that Copernicus's proof assigns
the same alphabetic labels to geometric points as Ṭūsī's Arabic proof — Arabic *alif* = Latin A,
Arabic *bāʾ* = Latin B, and so on — with a single exception: Ṭūsī's *zayn* appears as Copernicus's
F. Saliba adds manuscript evidence: the Arabic letters *zayn* and *fāʾ* are visually similar in
medieval manuscript hands, suggesting Copernicus (or an intermediary) misread the letter — a
"misreading" that itself confirms direct contact with an Arabic exemplar:

> "Not only did Copernicus apparently seek to solve the same problem of the Greek astronomical
> tradition by adopting the same approach... he also used a theorem that had been invented by Ṭūsī
> about 300 years earlier, and supplied a proof that was very similar to the one supplied by Ṭūsī,
> with a slight modification in protocol, but still adhered to the same geometric points that were
> used by Ṭūsī in the original proof. All of this cannot be mere coincidence." — Saliba, ch. 6

### ʿUrḍī's Lemma and the Upper-Planet Model

Muʾayyad al-Dīn al-ʿUrḍī (d. 1266), a colleague of Ṭūsī and engineer of the [[maragha-observatory]]
instruments, devised a lemma stipulating that two equal lines forming equal angles with a base line
have their free endpoints connected by a line parallel to that base. This lemma allowed him to
eliminate the Ptolemaic equant by transferring half the eccentricity to an epicyclet on the
deferent circumference. The model was adopted by [[qutb-al-din-al-shirazi|Quṭb al-Dīn al-Shīrāzī]]
(d. 1311) and then by Ibn al-Shāṭir — who incorporated it into his unified geocentric system
alongside an Apollonius-type epicycle.

Copernicus's model for the upper planets is identical to Ibn al-Shāṭir's except for the
transposition of the center from the Earth to the Sun. Critically, Copernicus used ʿUrḍī's Lemma
implicitly — without providing its proof — whereas ʿUrḍī had proved it explicitly. It was Kepler's
teacher Michael Maestlin who, responding to Kepler's question about this "omission," supplied the
proof independently, suggesting Copernicus received the model ready-made without full understanding
of its component theorems.

### The Mercury Model and the Swerdlow-Neugebauer Argument

The Mercury model provides Saliba's strongest evidence. When Noel Swerdlow studied Copernicus's
*Commentariolus*, he found that Copernicus misunderstood the model he was describing: Copernicus
claimed Mercury's epicycle would appear largest when the Earth was at 90° from the apsidal line,
whereas the model itself — and Ibn al-Shāṭir's original — predicts the largest apparent orbit at
±120°. Swerdlow concluded:

> "This misunderstanding must mean that Copernicus did not know the relation of the model to
> Mercury's apparent motion. Thus it could hardly be his own invention for, if it were, he would
> certainly have described its fundamental purpose rather than write the absurd statement that
> Mercury 'appears' to move in a larger orbit when the Earth is 90° from the apsidal line. The
> only alternative, therefore, is that he copied it without fully understanding what it was really
> about." — Swerdlow, cited in Saliba, ch. 6

Swerdlow and Neugebauer in their joint study of Copernican mathematical astronomy concluded: "it
is no longer the problem of 'if' but 'when, where and in what form' did Copernicus learn of those
earlier works."

## Channels of Transmission

No Arabic text used by Copernicus has been identified as a Latin translation. Copernicus is not
known to have read Arabic. Saliba argues the transmission happened through:

### Byzantine Route

Gregory Chioniades (14th c.) and other Byzantine astronomers traveled to Islamic lands to study
Islamic astronomy, producing Byzantine Greek manuscripts of Maragha-school results. Vatican
manuscript Gr. 211 contains a Greek version of the Ṭūsī Couple, though without the full proof.
Byzantine scholars fleeing Constantinople after 1453 brought books already incorporating Islamic
mathematical astronomy into Italian humanist circles, where they were read in Greek — not
translated into Latin but directly absorbed.

### Renaissance Arabists

Guillaume Postel (1510–1581) owned Vatican manuscript Arabo 319, a copy of Ṭūsī's *Tadhkira*
with marginal annotations in Latin demonstrating his ability to read the technical text. He
acquired another technical Arabic astronomical manuscript (al-Kharaqī's *Muntahā al-idrāk*) in
Constantinople in 1536 — the same year as the French diplomatic mission to Suleiman the
Magnificent. Postel was subsequently appointed professor of mathematics and oriental languages at
the Collège Royal, dying 1581, the year Copernicus died — meaning Postel was Copernicus's
contemporary. Leo Africanus (d. ca. 1550), who taught Arabic at Bologna, and Jean-Albert
Widmanstadt (1506–c.1559), a student of Leo Africanus, represent a network of Arabists in northern
Italy where Copernicus studied.

### Scientific Instrument Contacts

Antonio de Sangallo the Younger (1484–1546) copied in detail a 9th-century Baghdad astrolabe,
including its Arabic maker's inscription, suggesting high Renaissance regard for Islamic
instruments. The Arsenius family of astrolabists in the Flemish area fitted new retes to existing
Arabic astrolabe maters — implying direct bilingual competence. These instrument contacts confirm
the broader pattern of Islamic-to-European scientific transfer during the 15th–16th centuries.

### The Medici Oriental Press

Cardinal Ferdinand de Medici established an Arabic press after acquiring manuscripts brought by
the Syriac Patriarch Niʿmatallāh. The Press published, among other works, 3,000 copies of a
reworking of Euclid's *Elements* — not the original Greek translation but Ṭūsī's own 13th-century
reworking. The press's commercial rationale reveals that the European market for Arabic scientific
texts was substantial, confirming active demand for Islamic science in the late 16th century.

## Saliba's Central Historiographic Claim

The evidence forces, Saliba argues, a redescription of the intellectual nature of the European
Renaissance. If the only two mathematical theorems in Copernican astronomy that go beyond Euclid
and Ptolemy — the Ṭūsī Couple and ʿUrḍī's Lemma — were both invented by Maragha-school
astronomers, then:

1. Renaissance science was not a self-contained "rebirth" from Greek sources; it absorbed the
   critical products of Islamic astronomy.
2. Islamic astronomy in the 13th–16th centuries was not "preserving" Greek science but actively
   reforming and replacing it — works that were "expressly to object and reformulate the Greek
   classical scientific tradition."
3. The period conventionally labeled the "decline" of Islamic science was, precisely when viewed
   through the Copernican connection, the period of its greatest contribution to world science.

## Scholarly Controversy

The argument remains contested. Saliba acknowledges "the exact route by which Copernicus learned
of the earlier astronomical findings of the Muslim astronomers" has not been traced. Critics who
favor independent discovery must explain the identical alphabetical labeling of geometric proofs
and the misunderstanding of the Mercury model that implies copying without comprehension. The
"if" question, in Swerdlow and Neugebauer's formulation, is settled; the "when, where, and how"
remains open as of Saliba's writing.
