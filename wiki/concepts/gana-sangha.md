# The Gana-Sangha (Early Indian Republics)

The **gana-sanghas** ("rule by assembly / by the many") were the **non-monarchical tribal
polities** of ancient North India — the "republics" that flourished alongside, and in tension with,
the Gangetic monarchies from c. 600 BCE. Documented for the wiki through Romila Thapar's
[[thapar-history-of-india-v1-1966|*A History of India*, Vol. I]] (1966), which treats the
**republic/monarchy duality** as a defining feature of the age.

## Distribution and character

The republics clustered on the **northern and north-western periphery** of the Gangetic kingdoms —
the Himalayan foothills and the Punjab — generally in the **less fertile hill country**. Some were
single tribes (the **Shakyas**, Koliyas, Mallas), others confederacies (the **Vrijis/Licchavis**,
Yadavas). They had **emerged from the Vedic tribes** and retained far more tribal tradition than the
monarchies: government ran through an **assembly** of tribal/family representatives meeting in a Moot
Hall under an elected **raja** (a chief, *not* a hereditary king), with matters debated and, failing
consensus, **put to the vote**. Thapar reads their frequent origin-legends (founders of royal but
exiled lineage; founding families of brother–sister union) as signs that they had **parted company
with Vedic orthodoxy** — brahman sources indeed disparage some as "degenerate kshatriyas" for
ceasing to honour the brahmans and Vedic ritual.

## Heterodoxy and the social-contract theory

Because they lacked a divinely-sanctioned monarchy, the republics were **less bound to brahman
political theory and more tolerant of unorthodox opinion** — and it is no accident, Thapar stresses,
that the two great founders of the [[buddhism|heterodox]] sects came from them: the **Buddha** was a
**Shakya**, and **Mahavira** (founder of [[jainism|Jainism]]) a **Jnatrika**. The republics also
produced the striking **Buddhist account of the origin of the state** — "possibly the earliest
expression of the theory of social contract": after a primal harmony decayed into desire, family,
and private property, men **elected** one of their number, the **Mahasammata** ("Great Elect"), to
rule and keep justice in return for a fixed share of the produce. This **elective, contractual**
conception of kingship — the polar opposite of the brahmanical divine king — suited the republican
order.

## Significance and decline

The gana-sanghas are the wiki's evidence that ancient India contained a **genuine non-monarchical,
proto-republican tradition** with corporate, debate-and-vote government — a counterweight to the
"Oriental despotism" stereotype Thapar criticises (see [[caste-varna]]). The Buddha so admired their
procedure that he used it as the model for governing the monastic **Sangha**. Yet the long-run
current ran against them: the **decline of tribal culture and the spread of an agrarian, revenue-based
economy favoured the monarchies**, above all [[magadha|Magadha]], whose expansion absorbed them.
Isolated republics survived in the periphery until c. 400 CE — and remained zones where Buddhism was
strong and where foreign invaders (Greeks, Shakas, Kushanas, Huns) were assimilated.

## Limitations and critique (contested)

"Republic" is an imperfect translation: the gana-sanghas were **oligarchic**, their assemblies
dominated by the **kshatriya** landowning families, not democracies of the whole population (and
resting, like the monarchies, on shudra cultivators and the emerging untouchables). How
representative and how durable they were is debated, and the romantic modern reading of them as
"ancient Indian democracy" (encouraged by nationalist historiography) is treated with caution.
