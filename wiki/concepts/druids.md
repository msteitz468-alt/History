# Druids and Celtic Religion

## Definition and Scope

Druids and associated religious systems formed a key ideological and ritual component of Iron Age Celtic societies. Classical sources describe druids as a learned class with roles in religion, law, philosophy, and possibly sacrifice/astronomy. Archaeology reveals sanctuaries (enclosed ritual sites with votive deposits, weapon trophies, shafts), sacred springs, and deposition practices. The category distinguishes emic (Celtic practitioners and beliefs) from etic (classical and modern scholarly constructs). Contested: extent of pan-Celtic uniformity vs. regional variation; reliability of classical ethnography (often filtered through Mediterranean lenses); degree of continuity into Roman and early medieval periods.

## Religious Systems (sanctuaries, votives, druids) — Cunliffe 2018 range 05 grounding

Elements of Celtic belief systems persisted under Roman rule in Gaul and Britain: "a reality demonstrated by the maintenance of many Celtic religious sites of great antiquity, temples such as Gournay and Hayling Island and sacred springs like those of Sulis and Coventina in Britain and Sequana in Gaul."

Druidic continuity hinted: "A chance remark by Ausonius, writing in the fourth century ad, that Attius Patera, a professor in Burdigala (Bordeaux), came from a Druidic family might reflect the continuation of a tradition of scholarship going back into pre-Conquest times."

Ethnic/ritual unity supported by institutions like the Consilium Galliarum (deployed in 3rd-c Gallic Empire). Possible symbolic recall via "the refortification of the old Iron Age oppida of Alet and Le Yaudet".

Language evidence in ritual: Celtic curse tablets and personal names at Bath (Sulis Minerva spring) into 3rd/4th centuries AD; parallels in Gaul (Amélie-les-Bains, Chamalières).

Pre-Roman sanctuaries and practices are detailed in the source's Ch. 11 treatment (see further reading for excavation reports on Gournay by Brunaux et al., Sequana temple wooden figurines at source of the Seine, headhunting/sanctuaries of southern France, nemeton/temenos/bothros terminology).

## Historiography and Critiques

Classical sources (Caesar on druids in Gaul; Posidonius-derived ethnography) provide the main written evidence but are biased by conquest context and philosophical agendas. Archaeology (sanctuary excavations, votive hoards, weapon deposits at Gournay) supplies independent data on ritual practice, deposition, and cosmology. Debates center on druidic hierarchy, human sacrifice claims, and whether druids formed a trans-regional "priesthood" or localized elites. Post-Roman: limited survival of terminology/tradition (e.g. Ausonius); Insular vernacular literature offers later, Christian-filtered echoes.

Cunliffe (2018) integrates classical texts critically with material evidence, emphasizing regional diversity alongside shared Atlantic/Iron Age frameworks. Collection strong on sanctuaries and persistence via this source and related (e.g. Gournay, Hayling, Bath).
