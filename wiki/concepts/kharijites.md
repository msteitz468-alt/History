## Definition and Scope

The Kharijites (Arabic: *Khawārij*, 'those who go out') were the earliest sectarian movement within Islam, distinguished by their insistence that the moral quality of a ruler — and of any professing Muslim — was a matter of decisive theological and political consequence. Their core position: a man who sinned gravely was no longer a true Muslim, forfeited the right to rule, and could be fought or killed. This was the most radical possible answer to the early Islamic debate over the imamate, the question of who legitimately commanded the Muslim community.

Hodgson identifies the Kharijites as the first group to pose the problem of the imamate in its essentials, doing so already in the days of Mu'awiyah: "it was up to each believer to decide who was the most pious, or at least an adequately pious commander; to join with others who acknowledged that man's command; and to separate from any self-styled Muslims who irresponsibly accepted as imam an unworthy man" ([[hodgson-venture-of-islam-v1-1974]]).

## Origins

The movement originated in the [[first-fitna-656|First Fitna]]. When 'Ali accepted arbitration with Mu'awiyah at Siffin (657), a faction broke away on the grounds that 'Ali had compromised God's judgment — *la hukm illa li-llah* ('judgment belongs to God alone'). For these 'secedants', the imamate could not be settled by human negotiation; it had to be earned and demonstrated by righteous conduct. The first Kharijite revolt was crushed by 'Ali at Nahrawan (658), but the movement proved unkillable.

## Theology of the Imamate

The Kharijite solution to the imamate problem was both egalitarian and totalizing:

- **Election by the righteous remnant**: The imam need not belong to any particular family or tribe. Any sufficiently pious Muslim could be chosen — and equally, any imam who proved unworthy could be deposed or killed.
- **Faith as observable conduct**: *Islam* and *iman* (faith) must carry a visible moral consequence. Inner profession meant nothing if outward behavior was corrupt. This placed them in direct opposition to the [[piety-minded-opposition|Piety-minded]] Murji'is (especially Abu-Hanifah's Kufan school), who held that judgment on a sinner's soul belonged to God alone and that outward profession of faith sufficed for the purposes of community membership.
- **Isti'rad**: The most extreme wing, the Azraqis (named for Nafi' b. Azraq), accepted the practice of killing all Muslims outside their own righteous community, including women and children, as apostates. This represented the logical extreme of the principle that sin nullified Muslim status.

## Major Factions

Hodgson's text identifies a spectrum of Kharijite groupings, ranging from the insurrectionary to the quietist:

| Faction | Characteristics | Location |
|---|---|---|
| **Azraqis** | Most extreme; accepted *isti'rad* (killing of all outside their community, including families); named for Nafi' b. Azraq | Iraq/Iran frontier |
| **Najadis** | Followers of Najdah b. 'Amir; active in Arabia during the [[second-fitna-680|Second Fitna]] | Arabia |
| **Shaybanis** | Several raids along the Tigris under al-Hajjaj and after; major rising in the third fitna under Dahhak b. Qays | Iraq and Jazirah |
| **Ibadiyyah** | Least activist; centered in Basra; spread doctrine widely without demanding immediate rebellion | Basra, 'Uman, Maghrib |

The Ibadis remain the only surviving Kharijite tradition, now centered in Oman and parts of North Africa. Their moderation — refusing the *isti'rad* principle — allowed them to persist where the more violent factions burned out.

## Political Role Under the Umayyads

Under the [[umayyad-caliphate|Umayyad Caliphate]], Kharijite revolts were a persistent, destabilizing feature of the caliphate's eastern provinces. Al-Hajjaj b. Yusuf, 'Abd al-Malik's viceroy in the east (694–714), crushed major Kharijite insurrections in Iraq and Iran within his first six years — a major achievement of Marwani centralization. Yet Kharijite revolts continued throughout Umayyad rule, particularly in the Jazirah and eastern Arabia.

During the third fitna (744–750), two major Kharijite movements — one in southern Arabia, one in the upper Mesopotamian plain — nearly succeeded in seizing the caliphate. Marwan II, whose military reforms had concentrated his forces in disciplined drill units rather than tribal contingents, managed to suppress both before being overwhelmed by the [[abbasid-revolution-750|Abbasid Revolution]].

Hodgson observes that the Kharijite pattern may have attracted the *dhimmi* non-Muslim peasant population precisely because it seemed to promise a return to the clarity and non-interference of 'Umar's original policy of Arab separation from subject peoples — but that in the long run, the ideal of benevolent absolutism proved more satisfying to that population than Kharijite egalitarianism.

## Theological Legacy

The Kharijite debates generated or sharpened several major questions in early Islamic theology:

1. **What constitutes a Muslim?** The Kharijite insistence that grave sin expelled a person from the community was one pole in the debate; the Murji'i suspension of judgment was the other; the eventual Sunni synthesis held a middle ground.
2. **Free will vs. predestination (*qadar*)**: The Kharijite emphasis on moral consequence and human responsibility contributed to the Qadari school's position that human free will must be real for moral accountability to make sense. The opposing determinist position (favored in Medina and later in Khurasan) held that God decreed all acts.
3. **The 'ilm of the imam**: Kharijite attacks on the moral failures of rulers pushed all parties to articulate more precisely what kind of knowledge (*'ilm*) the imam needed, and how that knowledge could be recognized.

## Relation to the Sunni-Shi'a Crystallization

The Abbasid settlement of 750 gradually polarized Muslim allegiance into what later became labelled Sunni and Shi'i. The Kharijites refused both poles. They accepted neither the 'Alid doctrine of a special divinely-guided imamate nor the Sunni acceptance of the historical community's choices. As Hodgson notes, their doctrine that the righteous remnant must actively separate from — and if necessary fight — all corrupt Muslims made any durable accommodation with a caliphal state nearly impossible. Over time, most Kharijite communities either perished in revolt or retreated (as the Ibadis did) to peripheral regions where they could maintain autonomy.

## Historiography

Scholarly treatment of the Kharijites long focused on their violence and 'fanaticism', treating them as a pathological deviation. More recent scholarship (Watt, *Kharijite Thought in the Umayyad Period*, 1961; *Islamic Philosophy and Theology*, 1962) has reconstructed their internal logic more sympathetically, showing the Kharijite-Murji'i polarity as a structuring axis of early Islamic theology rather than a fringe phenomenon. Hodgson treats them as one of the three primary answers to the imamate problem — alongside the Shi'a and the eventual Sunni consensus — and credits them with generating the theological debates that shaped all subsequent Islamic thought on the relation between faith and works, moral conduct and political legitimacy.
