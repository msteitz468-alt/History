# Liber Iudiciorum

The ***Liber Iudiciorum*** (also known as the *Lex Visigothorum*, *Book of Judges*, or *Forum Iudicum*) was a unified, territorial law code promulgated by the Visigothic King **Recceswinth** in **654 CE**. It represents one of the most important legislative achievements of the Early Middle Ages, systematically replacing the traditional dual system of law (separate Roman and Gothic codes) with a single, territorial code applicable to all subjects within the Visigothic Kingdom.

## Historical Context and Development

Since the settlement of the Visigoths in southwestern Gaul and Iberia in the fifth century, the kingdom operated under a system of **legal personality**: Goths were subject to Germanic-influenced codes (such as the *Code of Euric* and the revised *Codex Revisus* of Leovigild), while the Romano-Hispanic majority was subject to Roman-influenced codes (primarily the *Breviary of Alaric* or *Lex Romana Visigothorum*).

King Chindasuinth (r. 642–653) initiated the legal unification process, which was completed by his son and co-ruler, Recceswinth. In 654, Recceswinth formally abolished the personality of laws, declaring:
> *"We forbid the laws of foreign peoples [i.e., Roman law] to be used in judicial proceedings... Let the laws of this book be observed by all."*

## Key Features and Structure

The code consists of twelve books, sub-divided into titles, containing over 500 individual laws. It was heavily influenced by Roman law structure and vulgar Roman law concepts, blended with Germanic legal traditions and Catholic moral theology:
- **Territorial Jurisdiction**: It applied equally to Goths and Romans, removing ethnic distinctions from the law. This coincided with the removal of the ban on intermarriage, fostering a unified Hispano-Gothic identity.
- **Royal Supremacy**: The code established the king as the sole source of law and justice. However, it also declared that the king was bound by the law: *"The law must be obeyed by the king, as it is by the people."*
- **Influence of the Church**: The code was revised and expanded at various Councils of Toledo (especially under King Ervig in 681 and King Egica in 693), reflecting the tight alliance between the Catholic episcopate and the crown. This collaboration introduced severe anti-Jewish legislation into the code, including forced baptisms, property confiscation, and bans on Jewish rituals.
- **Compensations and Punishments**: Unlike pure Germanic law, which favored monetary compensations (*wergild*), the *Liber Iudiciorum* made extensive use of physical and capital punishments, including execution, mutilation, flogging, and enslavement, reflecting Late Roman penal practices.

## Historical Significance and Legacy

The *Liber Iudiciorum* survived the collapse of the Visigothic Kingdom in 711. Under Islamic rule, Christian Mozarabs were permitted to use the code to govern their internal disputes. In the Christian kingdoms of the Reconquista, it remained the foundational legal text, translated into Castilian in the 13th century as the *Fuero Juzgo*, influencing Spanish law into the modern era.
