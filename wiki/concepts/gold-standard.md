# International Gold Standard (pre-1914)

## Definition and Scope

The **international gold standard** was the monetary arrangement, dominant from roughly 1870 to 1914, under which participating governments promised to exchange their national currencies freely for gold at a fixed rate. This made it straightforward to settle commercial accounts for goods sold in one currency but paid for in another, and underpinned a genuinely global, London-centered trading and investment system in the decades before the [[first-world-war-1914|First World War]].

London functioned as the de facto capital of this system for two reasons: the British pound had been freely convertible into gold since 1821 (decades before most other advanced economies followed after 1870), and British clearinghouses, insurance brokers, and shipping agents were the largest, cheapest, and most experienced in the world. By 1914, London handled the clearing of roughly 70 percent of American companies' foreign accounts, and British firms owned about 70 percent of the world's shipping tonnage (Paxton and Hessler, *Europe in the Twentieth Century*, 5th ed., ch. 1).

## Causal Drivers

- British financial and maritime primacy, itself downstream of early industrialization and naval dominance.
- The classical-liberal commitment to minimal state interference in trade and finance.
- A brief but decisive period (1860–1879) in which the world's major trading nations imposed almost no tariffs and few other restrictions on the movement of goods, capital, or people — before 1914 one could travel internationally without a passport, sell products on any continent, or collect foreign investment income without government involvement.

## Major Phases

- **1821**: Britain adopts gold convertibility for the pound sterling.
- **1860–1879**: Near-total continent-wide free trade among the major powers.
- **Post-1870**: Most other advanced economies adopt the gold standard, cementing London's centrality.
- **1914**: Europe supplied roughly 83 percent of the world's foreign investment (in developed and underdeveloped regions alike); on the eve of the war, European investment in the United States totaled nearly $7 billion, roughly ten times US investment in Europe. The outbreak of general war effectively ended the classical gold-standard/free-trade order.

## Geographic Spread

Centered on Britain/London but embracing all major industrial economies of Western and Central Europe and, more loosely, North America; underdeveloped regions (Latin America, colonial Africa and Asia) were drawn in as recipients of European capital (e.g., Canadian mines, Argentine railroads, Egyptian cotton farms, South African gold mines) rather than as co-equal participants.

## Interaction

Closely bound up with [[new-imperialism|the New Imperialism]] — European capital export both followed and reinforced formal and informal empire — and with the [[second-industrial-revolution|Second Industrial Revolution]]'s productive surplus, which required overseas outlets for investment.

## End Conditions

The classical gold standard did not formally end in 1914, but the assumptions underpinning it — passport-free travel, unregulated capital flows, presumptive peace among the Great Powers — were shattered by the outbreak of the [[first-world-war-1914|First World War]] and the belligerents' subsequent departure from gold convertibility to finance the war. Interwar attempts to restore it (1920s) proved unstable and collapsed in the Great Depression.
