# Western Greek (Sicilian) Tyranny

**Western Greek tyranny** is the distinctive variety of one-man rule that flourished
in the colonial cities of Sicily and southern Italy — Gela, Acragas, Rhegium and above
all **[[syracuse|Syracuse]]** under the **Deinomenids**. [[boardman-cah-v4-1988|CAH
Vol. IV]] (Ch. 16, David Asheri) treats it as **structurally different** from the
tyranny of the Greek mainland, and ancient writers (Timaeus, and Aristotle's pupil
**Phaenias of Eresus**, author of a monograph *On the Tyrants of Sicily*) already saw
Sicily as peculiarly prone to it.

## A different chronology and character

The Sicilian "Old Tyranny" came **late and was short**: setting aside the obscure
Panaetius of Leontini and the unique cruelty of **Phalaris of Acragas** (c. 570–554),
the true era of Sicilian tyranny **began when mainland tyranny was already ending**,
and was itself over before 461. More important is its **socio-political character**.
Where many mainland tyrants rode a *demos* against the aristocracy, Sicilian tyranny:

- **aimed at restoration, not revolution** — it typically **re-established and
  strengthened the old landed oligarchies** rather than overthrowing them (Gelon
  restored the exiled *gamoroi* to Syracuse and treated the *demos* as "a most
  unthankful fellow resident");
- **reaffirmed agriculture** and the horse-breeding elite even while encouraging trade
  and manufacture, and so **did not pave the way for Athenian-style maritime
  democracy**;
- wielded tools drawn from **Near-Eastern statecraft** — **forced population transfer**
  (Gelon's deportations of Camarina, Megara Hyblaea and Euboea), **mercenary armies**,
  and **dynastic intermarriage** (the Deinomenid–Emmenid alliance).

## The roots: colonial *stasis*

Asheri locates the cause in the **social structure of the colonies**. Most were ruled
by **narrow oligarchies** of first-settler descendants who monopolized the best land
and the magistracies — the **gamoroi** ("land-sharers") at Syracuse, the *pacheis*
("fat cats") at Megara, the self-styled *hippeis* ("horsemen") elsewhere; their
Olympic chariot-victories and lavish Delphic dedications advertise their wealth.
**Mass immigration** then produced a large, **unenfranchised *demos*** of mixed origin,
shut out of land and office, alongside Sicel serfs and free natives with their own
grievances. This layered antagonism — "landowners and villeins, oligarchs and demos,
settlers and natives, and ethnic cliques among the settlers" — bred the *stasis* on
which the tyrants rose.

## The Deinomenid apogee

The phenomenon peaked with **Hippocrates of Gela** and then **[[gelon|Gelon]]**, who
fused most of Greek Sicily under [[syracuse|Syracuse]], and his brother **Hieron** and
ally **Theron of Acragas**. Their courts produced the **panegyric culture** — Pindar's
Olympian and Pythian odes, Simonides, Bacchylides, Aeschylus at Syracuse — that
transmuted conquest and deportation into a memory of the tyrant as **"benefactor,
saviour, liberator,"** crowned by the victory of **[[battle-of-himera-480bce|Himera]]
(480)** and Gelon's heroic cult.

## Limitations and debates

The concept depends on a **hostile and panegyric source-base** simultaneously — the
critical Herodotus, the "super-patriotic" Sicilian historians (Timaeus), and the court
poets — making the tyrants' real policies hard to recover. The neat contrast of
**restorative western** versus **revolutionary mainland** tyranny is a useful model,
not a law (mainland tyranny was itself varied), and the very categories (*gamoroi*,
*demos*, "empire") are partly imposed by later, mainland-derived vocabulary. Asheri
also cautions against reading **ethnic or "panhellenic" motives** into what were
local, political and commercial conflicts.
