# Kokinshū (Kokin Wakashū — Collection of Early and Modern Poems)

The **first imperial anthology of Japanese poetry** (*waka*), compiled ca. 905 under
Emperor Daigo by **Ki no Tsurayuki** (873?–945?) and three other minor-noble poets.
The Kokinshū established the 31-syllable **tanka** as the canonical waka form, fixed the
vocabulary and style of Japanese court poetry for centuries, and — through its famous
preface — gave waka its theoretical justification as a serious art. Source:
[[cambridge-history-japan-v2-heian|Cambridge History of Japan, Vol. 2]].

## Context and compilation

Waka had undergone a "dark age" in the early ninth century, displaced from public life
by Chinese poetry (kanshi) under emperors like Saga, who compiled three imperial
kanshi anthologies (814–827). By the mid-ninth century waka began to reappear in
public, driven by:
- The **perfection of [[kana-script|kana]]** (the native syllabary), which made casual
  waka correspondence easy and natural.
- The rise of **poetry contests** (*uta-awase*) sponsored by the rear palace consorts —
  competitive occasions that elevated the status of waka composition.
- The **screen poem** (*byōbu uta*): waka inscribed on folding screens decorated with
  Japanese paintings, spreading from consorts' apartments to the public palace (ca. 850–900).
- The activity of "quasi-professional" waka poets from the lesser nobility, above all
  Tsurayuki, who worked to elevate waka's prestige.

The Kokinshū contains **1,111 poems** organized by season, love, travel, elegies,
and other categories, drawn from named poets of the recent past and the present,
plus anonymous pieces (some of which preserve archaic verse from the Man'yōshū era).

## Tsurayuki's Preface

Tsurayuki supplied both a **Japanese preface** (kana preface — the first work of
literary criticism in Japanese) and a **Chinese preface**. The Japanese preface compared
waka to "a plant with the human heart as its seed and innumerable leaves of words,"
and identified the **six poetic modes** (the *Kokinshū roppō*) from the Chinese **Greater
Preface to the Book of Odes**. This preface justified waka as a vehicle of serious human
feeling — not mere erotic pastime — and is the founding document of Japanese literary
theory.

## The Kokinshū style

The anthology's imperial patronage — "exalted sponsorship of Kokinshu, together with
its precedent-setting status, made the waka the nation's supreme literary form and
established the anthology as the supreme authority in matters of poetic taste." The
**Kokinshū style** became the orthodox style in a society where orthodoxy was heavily
enforced by social pressure and peer surveillance.

Characteristics of the style:
- **Six Dynasties techniques**: wit, verbal play, pivotword (*kakekotoba*), associative
  words (*engo*), and conceits adapted from continental models but applied to Japanese subjects.
- **Screen-poem romanticism**: images of nature conventionally associated with the four
  seasons (haze = spring; cuckoo = summer; deer = autumn; snow = winter), often
  composed for specific social occasions.
- **Elegance and facility**: the poems excel in graceful expression and social aptness;
  they are less known for depth or originality than for refined execution.
- A **restricted vocabulary** of about 2,000 items — a canon of "poetic words" (*utakotoba*)
  that defined the universe of permissible images.

Two representative poems from the anthology illustrate the style:

> (Tsurayuki, on spring):  
> *yo no naka wa / yume ka utsutsu ka / utsutsu to mo / yume to mo shirazu / arite nakute ka*  
> (Is this world a dream or reality? I do not know whether I am awake or asleep,
> whether I am alive or gone.)

> (Mibu no Tadamine, on love, with pivot on "matsu" = "pine" / "wait"):  
> *aki no no ni / oku shiratsuyu wa / tama ni nuka / nugeba kiezenu / mono ni shi araneba*  

## Later imperial anthologies and evolution of the tradition

The Kokinshū inaugurated a series of **Twenty-One Imperial Anthologies** (*nijūichidaishū*).
The immediately following ones — **Gosenshū** (Later Selection, ca. 951) and **Shūishū**
(Collection of Gleanings, ca. 1005–1007) — closely resemble the Kokinshū in style and
were compiled during the high Fujiwara period. 

Later anthologies reflect the strain within the tradition:
- **Goshūishū** (ca. 1086): the first anthology to depart from the Kokinshū style,
  appearing "at just the moment when the salons were fading from the scene" — those
  of Michinaga's daughters Shoshi, Kenshi, and Ishi, who had "helped to shape almost
  every aspect of the culture."
- **Kin'yoshū** and **Shikashū** (early 12th century): reflect the "progressive" debate
  between Minamoto no Tsunenobu (who sought to broaden waka themes to rural life)
  and Minamoto no Shunrai (who advocated colloquialisms and Man'yōshū vocabulary,
  including a notorious "love" tanka with the crude images of dog and crow).
- **Senzaishū** (compiled by Fujiwara no Shunzei/Toshinari, 1114–1204): represents
  the last great synthesis — "old words and new feeling," rejecting Kokinshū preciousness
  while retaining its canonical diction, achieving a "melancholy, lonely, austere tone"
  that expressed *mujōkan* (awareness of mutability). Shunzei's own poem on Fukakusa
  at evening is given as exemplary.

## Significance

The Kokinshū is the canonical text of Japanese literary culture in the same sense that
the Confucian classics are canonical in Chinese learning: it provided the vocabulary,
the forms, the examples, and the critical framework that all subsequent waka poets
worked within, contested, or reacted against. Its compilation marks the moment when
Japanese poetry definitively replaced Chinese as the supreme art form of the aristocratic
court, and when the [[kana-script|kana syllabary]] was publicly consecrated as a vehicle
for serious literature alongside kanbun.
