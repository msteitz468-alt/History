# Ancient Siege Warfare (Greek and Roman)

Siege and fortress warfare across Greek and Roman antiquity — often **more decisive than pitched battle** for the outcome of wars (especially classical Greek and late Roman campaigns). [[sabin-van-wees-whitby-cambridge-history-greek-roman-warfare-2007|CHGRW]] treats sieges inside the "Battle" chapters' naval-and-sieges halves (Strauss; de Souza; Gilliver; Rance) and in campaign chapters (Krentz, Roth, Whitby). **Whitby** notes sieges are often more archaeologically productive than battles when sites were abandoned: Persian ramps and tunnels at Old Paphos (498 BCE); Dura-Europus (~AD 257, with Roman counter-tunnels and underground dead); Masada (AD 70–3) circumvallation, forts, and siege mound. Alesia (52 BCE) shows text–archaeology interplay: Napoleon III’s excavations confirmed the Burgundy site, but reconstructions were over-shaped by Caesar’s homogenized description (*BG* 7.72–4), which misplaced some outer obstacles and generalized dispositions — “the text then influenced the interpretation of the material remains for over a century.”

## Scope

- Assault, blockade, circumvallation, betrayal, and negotiation.
- Artillery (torsion and tension), rams, towers, mines, and countermines.
- Fortification design and urban defence (Aeneas Tacticus onward).
- Naval dimensions of coastal and island sieges.

Distinct from purely field-battle tactics ([[hoplite-warfare]], [[roman-legionary-tactics]]).

## Archaic and classical Greek sieges

**Krentz / related CHGRW Greek chapters** stress that early mainland siege warfare was limited by poverty: simple walls, often acropolis only; raids and set-piece battles predominated; Homer’s Troy is not a true siege. Colonies facing Carthaginians, Lydians, and Persians built better fortifications (two-storey towers, ditches; Samos aqueduct). The Persian Wars were a turning point: Athens abandoned the city rather than risk siege in 480, then rapidly fortified city and Piraeus; imperial tribute funded the Long Walls (450s) — the Athens–Piraeus circuit >21 miles withstood land siege while supplied by sea.

Before the artillery revolution, **defence kept the advantage**, making sieges long and costly. In the Archidamian War, attempts to storm a city **failed twice as often as they succeeded**; surprise attacks were ~3× more successful than failed; democracies were reluctant to sacrifice citizen lives on storming, while Carthage, Syracuse, and Macedon (non-democratic, mercenary-rich) were the most successful stormers in the fourth century. Innovative but incomplete machines — Spartan mound + rams/fire at Plataea; Theban flamethrower at Delium 424; Brasidas at Torone 423 — **none took a city by these machines alone**.

**Circumvallation** is first attested at Samos 440; once the wall was complete, defenders were usually doomed (typical survival ~8 months on prior harvest; Phlius held 20 months = twice expected). Costs were huge: Athens 1,200 talents for Samos (*IG* i³ 363), **2,000 talents for Potidaea** (Thuc. 2.70.2) — perhaps ~25% of Athens’ financial reserves, prompting special taxes. Finance explains the rarity of long archaic sieges (Samos c. 523 only 40 days); Thucydides saw that if similar sieges did not take place earlier, the reason was lack of money — “the growth of the Athenian empire marks a significant change in Greek siege warfare.” Besiegers lived exposed to weather and disease (Syracuse 413; Potidaea 430: 1,500 of 4,000 hoplites lost in 40 days, mainly plague). Alternatives: treason, intimidation, trickery, rare successful assault.

***Epiteichismos*** (permanent forts in enemy land) worked only under certain conditions, especially naval support: Pylos 425–409 success; Decelea 413 devastating (≥20,000 slave deserters, livestock loss, Euboea supply shift); most other forts were quickly retaken — Decelea the great exception.

**Aeneas Tacticus** (*How to Survive under Siege*, perhaps 350s; maybe Aeneas of Stymphalos) stresses fifth columns, nighttime trickery, and total internal security — ban private meetings, monitor weddings/funerals, register arms, identity tokens, rotate sentries — “disturbingly modern.” Under siege, women (and slaves/children) sometimes fought from rooftops with tiles and stones (Plataea 431; Corcyra 428; Selinus 411); Sinope c.370 disguised women as men on walls; women were less often massacred than men but faced enslavement and rape (historians reticent; Homer and tragedy more open).

## Fourth-century and Hellenistic revolution

**Fourth-century transformation** ran through the Sicilian Carthaginian–Greek wars (Near Eastern rams/towers, mercenary specialists) then eastward transfer. Non-torsion arrow-shooters were invented by Dionysius I’s engineers at Motya 399; true torsion catapult within ~half a century — cities fallable in weeks not years. Fortress response: thicker/higher walls, 30-ft towers with shuttered catapult ports, full stone construction — Messene 369–368 first all-stone Greek town walls; still few walls withstood Philip/Alexander siegecraft. Philip used cash to attract scientists/engineers for a siege train (relatively new after Dionysius); specialists rose with technical complexity and military monarchy (Polyidus for Philip; Diades/Charias for Alexander; Archimedes at Syracuse; Ctesibius at Alexandria).

**De Souza** (via Demosthenes *Philippic* 3) marks the 340s as the start of an era in which, over the next ~200 years, sieges became commonplace and perhaps **more typical combat experience than pitched battles**. Despite sophisticated siege trains, **nearly every city Philip II took was through treachery** — Aeneas’s manual is largely about preventing politically motivated betrayal. Assault methods: ladders, rams, mining/counter-mining, secondary walls (Perinthus, Lilybaeum, Abydus). By ~300 torsion was widespread; bolt-to-stone thrower ratios ~6:1 (Philip V; New Carthage captures). *Helepolis* towers (Demetrius) and ship platforms (Tyre, Rhodes, *sambucae* at Syracuse) integrated artillery with assault. Ships rated above ten may have been siege/harbour platforms analogous to Demetrius’s great towers (Rhodes 305). Specialist logistics: best torsion ropes from women’s hair (Rhodes once shipped 680 kg); Rhodes 305 expended 2,300 missiles in a single night; by 149 Carthage held some 2,000 artillery pieces.

Advantage generally remained with **resource-rich attackers**. Blockade via circumvallation (Lilybaeum, Capua) was hard vs good harbours. Psychological impact of engines rivalled tactical effect. Defeat could mean massacre, enslavement, or destruction (Thebes; Tyre’s 2,000 crucifixions; Iliturgi; Carthage and Corinth 146). Offensive siege technology became an expensive industry (Demetrius at Rhodes 305–304); Archimedes’ defensive engines delayed Roman capture of Syracuse.

**Strategic shift by the second century:** increasing Hellenistic reluctance to besiege (funds; fewer engineers) while **Romans kept siege as key strategy**, perfecting craft in the late Republic; *epiteichismos* refined via colonies and roads — validated in the [[second-punic-war-218-201bce|Second Punic War]].

## Imperial Roman sieges

**Gilliver** (CHGRW II.4): successful siege warfare was essential for empire-building but expensive; Rome could maintain winter armies, field engineers/artillery, and logistical support for static forces — aided by late-Republican professionalization. Outside Parthia, enemies rarely saw complex siege machines; their arrival could terrorize “barbarians.” Once begun, a Roman siege was **virtually never abandoned** until capture or surrender — needed as imperial example; Masada was assaulted after the “official” end of the Jewish revolt and the Flavian triumph, with little booty, as symbol of Rome’s and the new emperor’s authority.

Unusual for Romans to begin blockade without first attempting assault; entirely passive sieges like Numantia or Alesia were rare and considered bad for morale (idleness → poor discipline). Titus circumvallated Jerusalem only after assaults failed — **7 km of works in three days**. Mining in the imperial period was extremely rare until the fourth century (Caesar’s Marseilles mine was flooded by defenders) — absence may indicate extraordinary success of blockade and heavyweight assault. Artillery (*scorpiones* and *ballistae*) was essentially anti-personnel; *ballistae* could terrorize civilians over walls. **Control is conspicuously absent** in most Roman sacks — soldiers given free rein to destroy, murder, rape, and pillage as siege reward; no evidence of systematic urban-clearance training or recruit training for siege/urban fighting. Polybius’s “organized mayhem” is idealized.

## Late antique sieges

**Rance / Whitby** (CHGRW II.9–10): siege significance rose in late antiquity. Sieges form **over half** of late antique military engagements; Romans were usually defenders (often *limitanei*) before sixth-century offensives. Only Persians regularly matched Roman logistical/technological capacity for long sieges (Amida 502/3 three months; Dara 573 six months); Germanic peoples preferred surprise, treachery, deception (Fritigern: “no quarrel with stone walls”); Huns and Avars overran Balkan cities using machines and human waves. Italian reconquest was structured as siege and countersiege.

Artillery terminology shifted: bolt-projecting *catapulta* became called *ballista*; the *onager* was the only late Roman stone-projector (cruder, simpler); Procopius’ 530s *ballista* appears tension-powered — utility of easier maintenance over pure power. Avars introduced oriental traction *petroboloi* (Thessalonica 586), distant ancestors of the medieval counterweight trebuchet. Late Roman “blockade” often meant incomplete investment — circumvallation rare after the early third century; religious leadership (e.g. bishop Eunomius’ “Thomas the Apostle” stone-thrower at Theodosiopolis; monks on Mardin walls c.608) bolstered defender morale; sacks rewarded troops and reinforced commander–army bonds when politics allowed.
