# The Mecelle (Mecelle-i Aḥkām-i ʿAdliyye)

The *Mecelle-i Aḥkām-i ʿAdliyye* (Arabic: *Majallat al-Aḥkām al-ʿAdliyya*) was the [[ottoman-empire|Ottoman Empire]]'s codification of [[fiqh|Ḥanafī]] civil law, published in sixteen books between 1870 and 1877, containing 1,851 articles in the Turkish language. It was the single most important Ottoman legal document of the nineteenth century and, as Wael Hallaq argues in [[hallaq-sharia-2009|*Sharīʿa*]] (ch. 15), both a product of and a monument to the structural contradictions of the [[colonial-transformation-of-islamic-law|colonial transformation of Islamic law]].

## Origins and Political Context

The Mecelle emerged from the broader context of the [[tanzimat-reforms|Tanzimat reforms]] and the increasing pressure — economic, military, and legal — of European hegemony on the Ottoman state. By the late 1860s, two competing visions of Ottoman legal reform had crystallized:

- **The Westernizing faction**, led by the grand vizier Ali Pasha, called for the adoption of the French *Code Civil* of 1804 (the *Code Napoléon*) as the basis for Ottoman civil law.
- **The Islamic jurist faction**, led by the Sharīʿa scholar **Aḥmet Çevdet Pasha**, argued that the law had to remain "faithful to the cultural constitution of the Empire" and "suit the present conditions."

Çevdet Pasha won the debate, and the committee he chaired produced the Mecelle. Its source was the corpus juris of the Ḥanafī [[madhhab|school]], "especially those opinions within it that seemed to the drafters to offer — especially in their reconstituted form — a modernized version of Islamic law." The opinions chosen were not necessarily the most authoritative Ḥanafī doctrines; some were imported from other schools when generally approved by later Ḥanafīs.

## Content and Scope

The Mecelle covered civil law and civil procedure. Conspicuously, it **excluded marriage and divorce** — the most sensitive domain of personal status law — leaving these in the hands of the Sharīʿa courts. Its sixteen books addressed contracts, sales, hire, guarantee, assignment of debts, agency, gift, wrongful appropriation, interdiction, constraint and pre-emption, and joint ownership. It also addressed civil procedure.

After its complete publication, the Mecelle was promulgated as a **sultanic code** — a legally binding act of the sovereign state. This was, Hallaq argues, a "momentous act sanctioning, once and for all, the supreme authority of the state" over the [[fiqh|fiqh]]: law was no longer the province of the jurists but of the state.

## The Mecelle as Transitional Artifact

Hallaq's analytical assessment of the Mecelle is double-edged:

**Structurally, it was code-like but did not function as a code.** "While in structure and appearance the Mecelle was code-like, it did not really function as codes do, enjoying unrivaled, exclusive authority. In practice, it continued to coexist with the books of the jurists." It was immediately rivaled by the 1880 Code of Civil Procedure, modeled on the French example.

**Politically, it was a state-capture instrument.** By promulgating the Mecelle as sultanic law, the Ottoman state asserted that [[fiqh]] "from now on was not the province of the jurists but rather that of the state." The Mecelle said, in effect: the sovereign owns the law.

**Symbolically, it was a last-ditch effort.** "It was obvious to the reformers and even to their opponents that the venture of the Mecelle was a last-ditch effort to salvage the Sharīʿa as a law in force, but it was also an attempted remedy applied to a problem that had originated as a remedy." The problem was the structural incompatibility between the Sharīʿa and the nation-state; the Mecelle tried to solve it by making the Sharīʿa legible in state terms — and in doing so, confirmed the state's superiority.

**Linguistically, it was a nationalist move.** The systematic substitution of Turkish for Arabic as the language of legal instruction was "integral to the intentional spreading of nationalist feelings." The Mecelle, written in Turkish, thus weakened the Arabicate hermeneutical tradition that was the living substrate of Islamic jurisprudence: "knowledge of the Arabicate tradition — central to the law — was weakened."

## Institutional Context: The Niẓāmiyye Courts and the Nāʾib's College

The Mecelle was intended for use in the Niẓāmiyye (secular state) courts, whose staff were being trained in non-*fiqh* law. The Nāʾib's College (est. 1854–55), originally training Sharīʿa judges, introduced the codified Mecelle into its curriculum in 1876 alongside new law of procedure and evidence. But by the 1880s the College's graduates were increasingly uncompetitive with the graduates of the new secular law schools: "the training of these judges was not commensurate with the sort of skills needed to operate the Niẓāmiyye courts." The number of College graduates fell from c.100 in 1914 to twelve in 1916. In 1924, the College was abolished by the Turkish Republic as part of the general abolition of Sharīʿa institutions.

## Aftermath and Legacy

The Mecelle remained in force in Ottoman successor states long after the Ottoman collapse. It was applied in Syria and Lebanon under the French Mandate, in Palestine under British Mandate, and in Jordan until 1977. Its longevity attests to its structural utility as a codified civil law that Muslim populations could relate to as Islamic while the state retained legislative sovereignty over it.

In Hallaq's analysis, the Mecelle is not a success story of Islamic legal adaptation but rather evidence of the logic of colonial transformation: even the most determined attempt to preserve the Sharīʿa within modernity ended by confirming the state's monopoly over the law.
