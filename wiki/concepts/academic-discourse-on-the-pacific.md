## Definition and Scope

This concept describes the body of European and Euro-American academic, literary, artistic, and popular discourse that has constructed the Pacific as a distinctive cultural space — specifically as "Europe's Other" — since the era of exploration. Johnston's chapter (ch. 34 of [[hattori-samson-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v2-2023]]) surveys how the Pacific entered European intellectual traditions, how scholars analyzed that entry through colonial discourse theory, how the field has shifted toward Indigenous agency and spatial approaches, and how Pacific peoples have contested and reclaimed the archive of encounter. The argument has two axes: (1) the Pacific was systematically constructed as the site of European alterity — exotic, erotic, savage, prelapsarian — producing a discourse Johnston names "Pacificism" by analogy with Said's Orientalism; and (2) that archive cannot be simply dismissed as imperial prejudice because it continues to generate creative, critical, and scholarly meaning, including for Pacific peoples themselves.

## The Orientalism Analogue: "Pacificism"

Johnston proposes "Pacificism" as the (admittedly awkward) Pacific corollary of Orientalism: "a way that Europeans came to terms with the region based on its special place in European experience and imagination; the Pacific is the source of a rich and long tradition of images of the other; and expertise in the Pacific produced discursive forms typified by particular vocabulary, imagery, and scholarship, supported by institutions and colonial bureaucracies." The key tenets of Said's *Orientalism* (1979) "replay fairly neatly in the Pacific context." Academic traditions "upheld ontological and epistemological distinctions between the Pacific and Europe," and popular imagination shaped these into "a truism of cultural difference, if not alterity."

The analogy is imperfect: formal annexation of Pacific colonies emerged "only in the mid to late nineteenth century — considerably later than Said's case studies" — yet the attitude and discourse were "indubitably imperial from the exploration voyages onwards."

## European Visions of the Pacific: The Archive of Encounter

European voyages to the Pacific were motivated primarily by territorial ambition and trade but generated a surplus of cultural knowledge. Three areas of European encounter shaped the archive:

**1. Social organization.** European travelers interpreted Pacific societies through the lens of their own hierarchical assumptions. Island cultures with recognizable dynastic hierarchies (Hawai'i, Tahiti/Polynesia) were found intelligible, making them "amenable to European ideas of diplomacy, exchange, and trade at the level of elite governmentality." Societies whose organization was less legible — Melanesia, Australia, Tierra del Fuego — "fared poorly in the European recognition of sovereignty and statehood" and were characterized as "chaotic and unpredictable, lacking in (recognizable) order and social structure, and inherently savage." Dumont d'Urville's classification (from the late 1820s) linked Polynesians and Micronesians as superior Pacific "races" to the detriment of Melanesians. Micronesia remained "less prominent in the scholarship on Pacific encounters, despite a long tradition of European engagement with the region beginning in the 1500s, in part because the Spanish saw voyage accounts as critical territorial intelligence and kept them secret."

**2. The natural world.** The Pacific's unique flora and fauna challenged European taxonomic systems. Islander relationships with the environment — navigation by stars and currents, accommodative small-scale agriculture, species mobility across archipelagoes — were "intriguing, challenging, and at times incomprehensible to European observers." The Enlightenment read Pacific environments as potentially Edenic, pre-lapsarian: "the Pacific seemed to reveal to Europeans an Edenic environment that pre-dated the Fall, and showed what a society might be like that was liberated from the Christian regulation of morality, mores, and identity."

**3. Subjectivity, gender, and sexuality.** Pacific forms of identity appealed selectively: hierarchical relations were comprehensible to Romantic and Enlightenment thinkers; freedom from European norms was favourably assessed. The 'arioi of Tahiti — mobile religious elites with esoteric ritual practices — troubled accounts. Bligh's 1789 encounter with Tahitian māhū (men in same-sex relations) inaugurated a tradition in which male–male relations in the Pacific "caught the imperial attention, and troubled their assumptions about gender and sexuality." Purea ("Queen of Tahiti") was satirized for her sexuality and body, entering "the English imagination not as the Other but as a setting for an argument about morality and corruption" (Dening). Johnston reads these representations within a comparative imperial tradition that positioned Indigenous women's bodies (Pocahontas, Sarah Baartman, Truganini) as sites of racial and sexual fantasy.

## Print and Visual Culture

The Pacific generated an exponentially growing print and visual culture from the exploration period. Cook's first voyage journals — controlled by the Admiralty ("Captain Elliot himself came on board, and I delivered to him a letter for the Admiralty, with a box, containing the common log books of the ship, and the journals of some of the officers") — were edited into John Hawkesworth's *An Account of the Voyages* (1773): "Voyage accounts were widely read, highly influential, and disseminated throughout British culture; the knowledge they produced about exotic cultures and climates was absorbed into domestic British culture and spread throughout Europe." Engravings, panoramas, portraits, broadsides, and ballads all participated. The 1802 French wallpaper "Les Sauvages de la Mer Pacifique" (designed by Jean Gabriel Charvet, printed by Joseph Dufour) — a twenty-panel panorama 2 metres high and over 10 metres long featuring scenes from Cook's journals — exemplifies this dissemination; Nicholas Thomas describes it as "a luminous evocation of voluptuous foliage, human variety, refined performance, remarkable ritual garb and regalia" that is "evidently fantastic, an overt romanticisation reducing the power, presence and complexity of Pacific life to enticing decoration."

Missionary literature from 1796 (London Missionary Society) onwards added a "moral corrective" to licentious visions. Missionary writing deployed the Pacific as "an exemplary mission field, the prototype for global evangelical reform" while simultaneously producing "extraordinarily detailed accounts of Pacific peoples, cultures, and environments." By the mid-to-late nineteenth century, missionary ethnography merged into anthropology. Johnston notes the paradox Samson identifies as "othering and brothering": missionaries worked simultaneously with fixed ideas of savagery and with the assumption that Pacific "others" were "mutable, complex, and commensurate with Europeans as part of a shared, religiously inspired, human-ness."

## Colonial Discourse Analysis and Its Limits

Colonial discourse theory — above all Said's Orientalism — gave twentieth-century scholars tools to analyze imperial tropes of race, gender, and sexuality. Greg Dening's work on Captain Wallis's 1767 encounter at Tahiti is Johnston's paradigm case: Europeans "made some assumptions about hierarchy and social order, and missed many other signs, to tragic ends." Superior weaponry and excessive force meant that "The realpolitick of discovery and possession meant the Native was not owed the ordinary etiquettes of war." Acts of violence regularly occurred when tabu or protocol were transgressed, but "imperial ways of seeing blinded the visitors to rules of engagement other than their own." Europeans "regularly misidentified leaders and misread their influence."

Yet Johnston argues these texts "have always exceeded that theoretical paradigm": "There are forms of encounter, kinds of evidence, and complex, unsettled accounts that cannot be neatly accounted for simply by identifying imperial hegemony." Positioning missionaries as "unquestioning agents of imperial power and their Pacific converts as misled dupes is neither intellectually convincing nor respectful of human motivations and complexity."

## The "Sea of Islands" Shift and Spatial Approaches

The key late twentieth-century shift was Epeli Hau'ofa's 1993 "Sea of Islands" essay, which explicitly rejected the imperial geography of "continental men" who saw "small land masses in large (if not empty) oceans" and who confined "ocean peoples to tiny spaces." Hau'ofa proposed "Oceania" over "Pacific" as a descriptor: "'Oceania' connotes a sea of islands with their inhabitants. The world of our ancestors was a large sea full of places to explore, to make their homes in, to breed generations of seafarers like themselves… Theirs was a large world in which peoples and cultures moved and mingled unhindered by boundaries of the kind erected much later by imperial powers." This "trans-Pacific currency" (Teresia Teaiwa) resonated immediately with postcolonial scholarship and has influenced historians who "compellingly reread the Pacific past along these lines."

The spatial turn in history, anthropology, and geography reinforced this shift. Space becomes "an object of analysis and an interpretative grid for reinterpreting the colonial and imperial pasts," enabling analysis of "dynamic flows and counterflows" rather than center-periphery models. Ships become analytical objects: Foucault's "heterotopia par excellence," a "floating piece of space" that is both self-contained and a "physical portal for contact with other worlds." Dening: Europeans "came in islands of their own ships or they made islands of their own in mission stations and forts."

Mobility approaches reinstated Pacific agency: Islanders "engaged energetically and deliberately with the British (and broader) world." Individuals like Mai and Tupaia "joined European ships to further their own political and intellectual interests, to travel, and to gain new experiences to enrich them personally and to bring those experiences and knowledges back to their communities." Tracey Banivanua-Mar located the beginnings of decolonization movements in the nineteenth century as a "dialogue that Indigenous people maintained with colonial power, in which they asserted their right to choose the best and reject the worst of colonisation."

## Case: Tupaia's Map

Johnston closes with Tupaia — Raiatean navigator and 'arioi priest who engaged with Cook's Endeavour at Tahiti in 1769 — as exemplar of the ongoing potency of Pacific encounter. Tupaia produced eight watercolour drawings and a map of most Polynesian island groups. Banks praised his "experience in the navigation of these people and knowledge of the Islands in these seas; he has told us the names of above 70, the most of which he has been at." Cook declared Tupaia "the likeliest person to answer our purpose." The map slipped into obscurity until J.C. Beaglehole located a copy in Banks's papers in the British Library (published 1955). It then gained steadily in scholarly significance: first as evidence in debates about ancient Polynesian navigation; then as evidence of ethnohistorical approaches; now as part of "Oceania's political and cultural renaissance." Eckstein and Schwarz's 2019 book-length reconstruction in the *Journal of Pacific History* characterizes the map as a collaborative, cross-cultural knowledge assemblage, positioning Tupaia "at the heart of knowledge production about the South Seas" and as "a cultural intermediary whose ability to translate one highly complex system of wayfinding, of representational world-making and ultimately of cosmology into a very different order of knowledge far exceeded the abilities of any of his European interlocutors."

Johnston frames the map as "a living document of the enlarged world of Oceania" — both deeply historic and profoundly agentic in the present for scholars and Pacific communities alike.

## Lisa Reihana and the Contemporary Archive

The chapter opens and closes with Lisa Reihana's immersive digital video work *Pursuit of Venus (Infected)* (2015–17), which re-stages and re-peoples Dufour's 1802 wallpaper with Māori, Islander, and Aboriginal performers who "activate the stories of the past with their bodies, their music, and their dance." The "infection" of the title refers simultaneously to the venereal disease Cook's ships brought to the Pacific and to "an infected point of view" — a new and challenging perspective that "Pacifc performers put their own vision into the European vision of the Pacific, to make the familiar strange again." Reihana's work embodies Johnston's argument that the imperial archive of Pacific encounter "cannot simply be reduced or dismissed for these European prejudices" but remains "available for Pacific peoples — as well as scholars of diverse ethnicities and locations — to interpret, display, and contest."

## Historiography

Johnston's chapter is itself a work of intellectual history and historiography — a meta-survey of how the Pacific has been known and represented. Key interlocutors include Greg Dening (*Performances*, 1996; *Islands and Beaches*, 1980), Nicholas Thomas (*Islanders*, 2010; *Entangled Objects*, 1991), Epeli Hau'ofa (collected essays in *We Are the Ocean*, 2008), Jane Samson (*Race and Redemption*, 2017), Tracey Banivanua-Mar (*Decolonisation and the Pacific*, 2016), and most recently the Eckstein–Schwarz reconstruction of Tupaia's map (2019). The chapter notably addresses how the concept of "Pacificism" as an analogue to Said's Orientalism is useful but imperfect given the later timing of formal Pacific colonization, and how scholarship has progressively decentred European agency while remaining embedded in European-language archives.
