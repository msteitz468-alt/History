# Revolution

Revolution denotes a rapid, fundamental transformation of a state's political order, ruling coalition, and often its underlying social and economic structures, distinguished from ordinary political change by its scale, speed, and the illegitimacy (in the eyes of the revolutionaries) of the prior order it overthrows. Historians and social scientists have long debated what distinguishes a "true" revolution from a coup, rebellion, or civil war: Crane Brinton's comparative "anatomy" identified common stages (old-regime fiscal crisis, moderate phase, radicalization, terror, Thermidorian reaction) across the English, American, French, and Russian revolutions, while Theda Skocpol's structural approach emphasized state breakdown under international and class pressures over revolutionary ideology or leadership as the decisive causal factor.

A further distinction, elaborated by Hannah Arendt, separates revolutions aimed at founding new, freer political institutions (her preferred sense, exemplified by the American Revolution) from those primarily driven by social/economic grievance and prone to descend into terror (her reading of the French and Russian cases). Historians also distinguish revolution from temporally adjacent but conceptually separate categories: mere sequence of unrest is not revolution without a decisive break in ruling authority, and not every successful revolt produces lasting structural transformation.

The concept remains actively contested at the margins — whether certain 20th-century decolonization movements, military coups with populist backing, or "color revolutions" qualify as genuine revolutions in the classical sense, or represent a distinct phenomenon, is a live debate rather than a settled classification.
