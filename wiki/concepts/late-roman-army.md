# The Late Roman Army

The reshaping of the Roman army between the Severans and Constantine — the slow emergence, out of
the static legionary frontier army of the [[high-empire|High Empire]], of the **late-Roman dual
system** of mobile field army (*comitatenses*) and frontier troops (*limitanei*). Treated in
[[bowman-cah-v12-2005|CAH XII]] Ch. 5 (**Brian Campbell**).

## Continuity through the crisis

Campbell stresses **gradual change, not revolution**. The army that entered the
[[third-century-crisis|crisis]] in 235 was still the legion-based force of the Principate (33 legions
by the Severan era), and it **held the empire together** despite plague, civil war, and the loss of
only the Agri Decumates, Dacia, and Mesopotamia. But strains drove adaptation: the rising importance
of **cavalry** and of ethnic units (Moors, Palmyrenes), the heavier use of **detachments**
(*vexillationes*) brigaded for mobility, and the new strategic reality of a **two-front threat**
(Sassanid Persia in the east, Germanic confederations on the Danube/Rhine). Campbell rejects Luttwak's
"grand strategy" model: Rome lacked the high command and intelligence for empire-wide doctrine, and
military decisions were largely **ad hoc**.

## Gallienus and the cavalry

[[gallienus|Gallienus]] (253–268) was the pivotal innovator. He concentrated an independent
**cavalry force** at Milan under a separate commander (Aureolus — the next two emperors, Claudius and
Aurelian, rose as cavalry commanders), fortified strongpoints (Milan, Verona, Aquileia) garrisoned by
legionary detachments, and — by edict (c. 262) — **excluded senators from legionary command**,
replacing the *legatus legionis* with equestrian *praefecti*. Campbell cautions this was driven by
the **need for professional, experienced commanders**, not anti-senatorial animus, and that the Milan
cavalry was a tactical response, not yet a permanent field army. The title ***protector*** also
emerged as a mark of imperial favour and a route of officer promotion.

## Diocletian: holding the frontiers

[[diocletian|Diocletian]] roughly **doubled the army** (from 33 to at least 67 legions, with
auxiliaries in proportion), though probably with **smaller units**, and bound it to the new
[[late-roman-administration|fiscal system]] (conscription as the *praebitio tironum*, levies in kind,
the settlement of barbarian recruits). His emphasis was on a **strong, permanent frontier presence**:
densely garrisoned provinces, more easily defensible forts, and fortified frontier roads such as the
**Strata Diocletiana** (Arabia–Palmyra–Euphrates). A **field army** (*comitatus*) existed but was not
yet central — Campbell sees it as the emperor's enlarged escort, drawing on frontier troops when
campaigning. His policy "looked back to Hadrian and the Antonines": hold the limits, repel
incursions, counter-attack where possible.

## Constantine: the field army

**Constantine** decisively shifted the balance, enlarging and privileging the mobile **comitatenses**
(infantry legions ~1,000 strong, new *auxilia*, cavalry *vexillationes*) and clearly distinguishing
them from the frontier **ripenses/limitanei** under regional *duces*. He created the new commands of
**magister peditum** and **magister equitum**, **abolished the praetorian guard** (312, for siding
with Maxentius), and developed the **scholae palatinae** and *protectores domestici* as the imperial
bodyguard and an officer cadre. Campbell rebuts **Zosimus'** charge that Constantine thereby caused
the fall of the West by "softening" frontier troops in the cities: the field army was a sensible
response to the impossibility of a purely static defense, and Constantine **did not "barbarize"** the
army.

## Significance and debate

The result was the characteristic late-Roman army of the *Notitia Dignitatum*: a **mobile field
force** backing a **graded frontier defense**, professional and equestrian-officered, increasingly
the **guarantor of the emperor's power**. Whether this was "defense-in-depth" (Luttwak) or simply
pragmatic adaptation (Isaac, Campbell), and whether Diocletian or Constantine deserves the credit,
remain debated — but the through-line is the **reorientation from a frontier-static to a
mobile-reserve army** forged in the crisis.
