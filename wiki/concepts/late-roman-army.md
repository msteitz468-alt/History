# The Late Roman Army

The reshaping of the Roman army between the Severans and Constantine — the slow emergence, out of
the static legionary frontier army of the High Empire, of the **late-Roman dual
system** of mobile field army (*comitatenses*) and frontier troops (*limitanei*). Treated in
[[bowman-cah-v12-2005|CAH XII]] Ch. 5 (**Brian Campbell**).

## Continuity through the crisis

Campbell stresses **gradual change, not revolution**. The army that entered the
[[third-century-crisis|crisis]] in 235 was still the legion-based force of the Principate (33 legions
by the Severan era), and it **held the empire together** despite plague, civil war, and the loss of
only the Agri Decumates, Dacia, and Mesopotamia. But strains drove adaptation: the rising importance
of **cavalry** and of ethnic units (Moors, Palmyrenes), the heavier use of **detachments**
(*vexillationes*) brigaded for mobility, and the new strategic reality of a **two-front threat**
(Sassanid Persia in the east, Germanic confederations on the Danube/Rhine). Campbell rejects Luttwak's
"grand strategy" model: Rome lacked the high command and intelligence for empire-wide doctrine, and
military decisions were largely **ad hoc**.

## Gallienus and the cavalry

[[gallienus|Gallienus]] (253–268) was the pivotal innovator. He concentrated an independent
**cavalry force** at Milan under a separate commander (Aureolus — the next two emperors, Claudius and
Aurelian, rose as cavalry commanders), fortified strongpoints (Milan, Verona, Aquileia) garrisoned by
legionary detachments, and — by edict (c. 262) — **excluded senators from legionary command**,
replacing the *legatus legionis* with equestrian *praefecti*. Campbell cautions this was driven by
the **need for professional, experienced commanders**, not anti-senatorial animus, and that the Milan
cavalry was a tactical response, not yet a permanent field army. The title ***protector*** also
emerged as a mark of imperial favour and a route of officer promotion.

## Diocletian: holding the frontiers

[[diocletian|Diocletian]] roughly **doubled the army** (from 33 to at least 67 legions, with
auxiliaries in proportion), though probably with **smaller units**, and bound it to the new
[[late-roman-administration|fiscal system]] (conscription as the *praebitio tironum*, levies in kind,
the settlement of barbarian recruits). His emphasis was on a **strong, permanent frontier presence**:
densely garrisoned provinces, more easily defensible forts, and fortified frontier roads such as the
**Strata Diocletiana** (Arabia–Palmyra–Euphrates). A **field army** (*comitatus*) existed but was not
yet central — Campbell sees it as the emperor's enlarged escort, drawing on frontier troops when
campaigning. His policy "looked back to Hadrian and the Antonines": hold the limits, repel
incursions, counter-attack where possible.

## Constantine: the field army

**Constantine** decisively shifted the balance, enlarging and privileging the mobile **comitatenses**
(infantry legions ~1,000 strong, new *auxilia*, cavalry *vexillationes*) and clearly distinguishing
them from the frontier **ripenses/limitanei** under regional *duces*. He created the new commands of
**magister peditum** and **magister equitum**, **abolished the praetorian guard** (312, for siding
with Maxentius), and developed the **scholae palatinae** and *protectores domestici* as the imperial
bodyguard and an officer cadre. Campbell rebuts **Zosimus'** charge that Constantine thereby caused
the fall of the West by "softening" frontier troops in the cities: the field army was a sensible
response to the impossibility of a purely static defense, and Constantine **did not "barbarize"** the
army.

## Significance and debate

The result was the characteristic late-Roman army of the *Notitia Dignitatum*: a **mobile field
force** backing a **graded frontier defense**, professional and equestrian-officered, increasingly
the **guarantor of the emperor's power**. Whether this was "defense-in-depth" (Luttwak) or simply
pragmatic adaptation (Isaac, Campbell), and whether Diocletian or Constantine deserves the credit,
remain debated — but the through-line is the **reorientation from a frontier-static to a
mobile-reserve army** forged in the crisis.

## The fourth-century army (CAH XIII)

[[cameron-cah-v13-1998|CAH XIII]] Ch. 7 (**A. D. Lee**) carries the army through the fourth century, read
chiefly through the ***Notitia Dignitatum*** (the army "on paper") and **Ammianus Marcellinus** (the army
in action). Key points:

- **The two-tier system matured**: the mobile **comitatenses** (field army) versus the **ripenses /
  limitanei** (frontier troops). Lee stresses Constantine **expanded** an existing idea (Gallienus,
  Severus) rather than inventing it, and — against Zosimus' polemic — **did not strip the frontiers**; he
  also valued the *ripenses* and built frontier works, completing Diocletian's **separation of civil and
  military command** (the *dux*).
- **Proliferation of unit-types**: smaller-than-legionary units — cavalry *vexillationes*, infantry
  *legiones*, and the new **auxilia** (the elite Germanic-recruited backbone of the field army) — plus the
  *scholae* (the new bodyguard replacing the abolished **praetorians**), under the *magister officiorum*.
- **Regionalization**: after Constantine the single field army fragmented into **multiple regional armies**
  (a practical response to the empire's size); by 400 the *Notitia* shows **five eastern field armies**
  (two *praesental*, near Constantinople, plus East/Thrace/Illyricum) under *magistri militum*, and a more
  centralized but more numerous set of small western armies (Gaul, Africa, Spain, Britain, Illyricum —
  largely [[stilicho|Stilicho's]] formalization). This effectively **reformulated, even partly abandoned,
  the original Constantinian model**.
- **The strategic context** was harder: the [[sasanian-empire|Sassanids]] east and the
  [[germanic-peoples|Frankish, Alamannic and Gothic confederacies]] north made the army's role more vital,
  its enlarged size a strain on manpower and budget, and its loyalty (after the third-century mutinies) a
  standing imperial anxiety.

## Goldsworthy: regime security, and the continuity-vs-decline question

[[sources/goldsworthy-roman-warfare-2000|Adrian Goldsworthy, *Roman Warfare* (2000)]] frames the late
army through an explicit **continuity-vs-decline** question — did it *evolve* to meet a changed
situation, or simply decay? — and answers that it retained the **potential** to be as effective as any
earlier Roman army (professionalism, discipline, training, logistics still gave real advantages, and it
usually won when properly led and supplied), but that a **fragmented command structure** (multiple
*duces* and *comites*, the civil–military split, regionalized *limitanei*, splintering field armies)
increasingly kept it from *realizing* that potential at scale.

His most distinctive causal claim concerns the **comitatenses**: against the traditional reading that
interior troop-concentration was a strategic reserve answering frontier incursions the cordon could no
longer stop, Goldsworthy argues the driver was **regime security against internal rivals** — Severus
needed loyal troops after a civil war, Gallienus because he had lost most of the empire, and Constantine
(an insecure civil-war victor who "spent half of his reign as a usurper") built the large field armies
above all as "the emperor's ultimate guarantee of power." He calls them a **two-edged sword**: the same
proximity that secured the emperor made the field armies "responsible for the murder of many emperors."
On this reading **civil war**, not foreign war, is the structural driver of late-army reorganization —
it accounted for the greater number of the period's pitched battles and repeatedly drained frontier
garrisons. Goldsworthy also insists the *limitanei* were **not a farmer-militia** but regular units doing
internal-security, policing, and anti-banditry work; notes the equipment shift (rectangular *scutum* and
heavy *pilum* giving way to oval shields, the lighter *lancea*, and lead-weighted *plumbatae* of roughly
double a pilum's range); and finds **no good evidence for a significant proportional increase in
cavalry**, only more heavy *cataphractoi/clibanarii* in the east against Persia. See the
[[roman-grand-strategy|grand-strategy controversy]].

## OHCW (Purcell; Chadwick)

**Purcell** (Ch. 23): Principate military system to the 3rd c. — ~30 citizen legions (~165,000) under senatorial command + more numerous auxiliaries (citizenship on discharge from Claudius); high command deliberately non-professional to keep army integrated; soldiers omnipresent in building, tax, customs, policing — “military rule”; *frumentarii* secret service in 2nd c. When scribes were soldiers, “the end of the ancient world was in sight.” **Chadwick**: third-century near-collapse averted by Diocletian/Constantine army-state reforms; long barbarian incorporation into the force structure before 476 (see [[barbarization-of-the-roman-army]]). Source: [[boardman-oxford-history-classical-world-1986]].

## Update — divergent fates, c. 420–602 (CAH XIV)

[[cameron-cah-v14-2000|CAH XIV]] (Lee, Ch. 11) frames the fifth–sixth-century army around its **two opposite
outcomes**: in the West the Roman army **ceased to exist as a state institution**, replaced by the forces of
the successor kingdoms (none of which kept a standing army); in the **East it survived in recognizable form
to the early seventh century** — and with it the empire itself.

- **Structure** continued the Diocletianic/Constantinian model: **limitanei** (frontier garrisons under
  *duces*, partly self-supporting from assigned lands — a deliberately maintained "imperial presence" that
  controlled all cross-border movement) and mobile **comitatenses** (palatine *praesental* armies near the
  capitals, plus regional commands under *magistri militum*). Recruitment mixed **hereditary service**
  (veterans' sons), **conscription** on the tax registers (commutable for *aurum tironicum*), and
  ever-heavier reliance on **non-Roman troops**, increasingly organized as **federate** ethnic war-bands
  under their own chiefs (the two Theoderics each led ~10,000 in the Balkans).
- **Why the West's army dissolved** is, for Lee, a **fiscal** story (echoing Heather's "vicious circle").
  Valentinian III's Novel of 444 states the problem nakedly: the empire desperately needed an army but could
  no longer **squeeze the supplies** from a shrinking economy. As tribes learned that **occupying land**
  (not merely drawing pay) was the key to security, the state lost control of the **tax base** region by
  region; by the 450s only Italy and southern Gaul remained, and even there armies could be raised only by
  **coalition with local élites** (Aetius against Attila). It was "not that all western Romans had lost the
  ability or will to fight, but that the state could not deploy this potential" — the machinery for raising,
  paying and moving armies had broken down. The army of Italy, by the end largely **federate**, was the
  kingmaker (Ricimer) until [[fall-of-the-western-roman-empire-476|476]].
- **Why the East's survived**: its richer, defensible **revenues** still funded a standing army; it
  domesticated its federates (and the *optimates*) into the imperial structure rather than being supplanted
  by them, as in the West. This is the military face of the [[eastern-roman-empire-425-518|East–West
  divergence]] — the army that [[justinian|Justinian]] would send to reconquer Africa and Italy.

Source: [[cameron-cah-v14-2000]] (Ch. 11, A. D. Lee). The great late-Roman treatise on military matters
is now ingested as **[[vegetius-epitome-military-science|Vegetius, *Epitome of Military Science*]]** —
an idealizing reform-tract that holds up the army of the early Empire as a model, to be used with care
as evidence for the late-Roman army's actual practice.

## Harris, *Roman Power* (2016)

[[harris-roman-power-2016|Harris]] tracks army change from the high empire through both late-antique contractions. Pre-235: auxilia became quasi-permanent (often outnumbering legionaries); recruitment provincialized (from Hadrian, normal to recruit locally from Romanized/Hellenized elements except Britain); many soldiers never experienced battle — the “deplorable failure” to protect the Danube in 170–1 suggests change for the worse. Major conquest still required new legions (Trajan’s II Traiana and XXX Ulpia; Marcus’s II–III Italica; Septimius’s three Parthicae). Aspiring senators who once did ≥10 competitive years of service now held brief tribunates “often a single year… quite unembarrassing not to have seen active service” (Pliny). On size under the tetrarchs, Harris finds it hard to believe the regular establishment exceeded Severus’s ~450–475,000: John Lydus’s 389,704 under Diocletian has some plausibility; quality is another matter; vast new conquests would have required unwanted mobilization.

After 337 Harris is sharply anti-pollyanna. The *De rebus bellicis* observer noted vast army expenditure throwing the tax system into difficulties — “But for all the money… these armies failed to defend the empire’s western half.” Against Lee (1998) and Elton (1996): “if the Roman army was not ‘an unmitigated disaster’… let us at least admit… that it was a disaster.” High modern western-army estimates (Elton 300–600k for 350–425) are “bad guesswork”; 600,000 is incredible because the richer second-century empire strained to support ~350,000; the *Notitia* lacks unit strengths and mixes dates; Stilicho raised only ~5,000 against Gildo (397); Honorius’s slave-recruit offers and desperate taxes signal too-small establishment. “Neither the number nor the quality of the troops… from Valens and Valentinian… to the final years of the western empire, were sufficient.” Conscription worked poorly (self-mutilation; western commutation of service for money in crisis years as evidence that “the population’s will to resist was inadequate”); when Roman at all, soldiers were “irregularly paid, undertrained, insubordinate, inclined to desert, and often engaged in non-military occupations.” Unitary “Roman army” is anachronistic after Hadrianople: harm of German federates was mercenary loyalty slip, not ethnicity.

For the Justinianic–Heraclian east: Narses assembled “scarcely” 18,000 vs Franks in 555; Agathias claims “scarcely” 150,000 soldiers Spain to Georgia late in Justinian’s reign — Heraclius cannot have afforded nearly as many; never again a Byzantine force even of 555’s modest dimensions. Small Heraclian forces were more the result of “financial and political-social problems than of demographic ones.” Seventh-century technical factors (stirrup, infantry/cavalry balance) end inconclusively (Trombley); multi-ethnic defensive armies intensified negative effects; “in practice the Roman army increasingly resembled a large band of brigands.”

## From Gibbon, Decline and Fall (1776–1788)

[[actors/gibbon-edward|Gibbon]]’s late army is enervated Palatines, shrunken legions, self-mutilating recruits, and barbarian iron. **Gibbon’s views.**

- **German arms weak until Rome decayed:** poverty of iron, deep infantry columns; “A repulse was a sure defeat; and a defeat was most commonly total destruction” — barbarian challenge surprising only after luxury and sedition hollowed Roman superiority.
- **Claudius Gothicus at Naissus:** claimed Gothic armada of ~320,000; ~50,000 slain; survivors winter-killed by famine/pestilence; “Gothic Claudius” as restorer title.
- **Palatines vs Borderers:** Constantine censured for higher pay to idle interior garrisons; frontier chain of Diocletian neglected — “mortal wound” under which “the empire, till the last moment of its dissolution, continued to languish.”
- **Scale:** legions of 6,000 shrunk to ~1,000–1,500; 132 legions; 583 frontier stations; “complete force… computed at six hundred and forty-five thousand soldiers” — beyond ancient wants and later faculties.
- **Self-mutilation:** “many of the youth of Italy and the provinces chose to cut off the fingers of their right hand, to escape from being pressed into the service.”
- **Veteran land grants** as “the first rudiments of the feudal tenures.”
- **Theodosian/Gothic armies and Frigidus:** Goths and Alani in imperial service; idolatrous symbols of Jupiter/Hercules vs “the invincible standard of the cross”; Honorius’s post-Stilicho purge disqualified brave pagan/Arian officers (measures Alaric would have approved).

## CHGRW synthesis (Elton, Rance, Lee, Whitby, Fear)

[[sabin-van-wees-whitby-cambridge-history-greek-roman-warfare-2007|CHGRW]] Vol. II Chs. 8–12 (Elton, Whitby, Rance, Lee, Fear) supplies the densest recent multi-author treatment of late Roman forces. Distinctive contributions that do not merely restate CAH/Goldsworthy:

### Elton: structure, flexibility, numbers (Ch. 8)

**Structural flexibility** over rigid org charts: almost any imperial official could command regardless of “correct” rank (Iovius 361; Bonus at Luca 553). Core narrative sources (Ammianus, Procopius) are strong on systems, weak on numbers/technical terms; *Notitia Dignitatum*, laws, papyri, inscriptions, and Maurice’s *Strategicon* fill technical gaps.

**Three organizational phases:** (1) Gallienus–Constantine (~260–320): standing *comitatenses* around emperor; *duces* on borders; (2) Constantine–Maurice (~320–580): permanent *comitatenses* vs *limitanei*, *magistri*, regional field armies; (3) Maurician (~580+): exarchs, Opsikion, then themes after territorial loss. Constantine made permanent the field/border distinction — administratively they differed mainly in physical standards, service length, and retirement tax benefits. After Constantine’s sons divided the empire, regional field armies crystallized (Gaul, Illyricum, East); under sole Constantius II a central praesental “great army” plus regional armies at Trier, Sirmium, Antioch; 364 division created western and eastern praesental armies; most field regiments likely split *seniores/iuniores* then. Justinianic changes: *magister militum per Armeniam* (528); reconquest field armies in Africa/Italy/Spain; *limitanei* re-established in Africa; Procopius’s claim Justinian stopped paying eastern frontier troops is exaggerated. Maurice: Illyrian army disappears after Sirmium 582; Italy/Africa *magistri* upgraded to exarchs; praesental forces become Opsikion; seventh-century territorial themes from field armies in Asia Minor.

**Against progressive limitanei decline:** the field + border system lasted more than three centuries; post-Maurice changes were reactive/logistical; when territory was reconquered (Justinian Africa) the field/border system was restored — not structural failure of an army that “fell.”

**Unit sizes (Elton):** field-army legions c. 1,200 by mid-fourth century; *auxilia palatina* c. 1,200 (or 600); cavalry *vexillationes* c. 600; *scholae* 500; early cohorts/*alae* still ~500 (*Numidae Justiniani* 508 in 533). *Foederati* evolved: late-fourth-century permanently established cavalry units (e.g. *Honoriaci*); also short-term allied contingents under own leaders but Roman strategic command; *excubitores* (Leo I, 300); generals’ *bucellarii*; sixth-century larger regional infantry (Isaurians, Armenians, Tzanni).

**Army size:** John Lydus (via prefect’s office) Diocletian army 389,704 + navy 45,562; Agathias “previously” 645,000 vs “now” 150,000 (hostile to Justinian). Jones from *Notitia* ~600,000 late fourth; Elton argues **~half a million fourth century** (little different from early imperial establishment), **~300,000 sixth** for reduced empire. Campaign armies typically 10–20,000 regional; Julian 363 combined ~83,000 (or 65,000); Joshua Stylite 52,000 eastern+praesental 502. (Harris’s scepticism of high counts is recorded above; keep both attributed.)

**Equipment continuity:** spear, sword, shield, helmet, metal armour (mostly mail) third–seventh centuries; Vegetius’s claim infantry stopped wearing armour under Gratian is a misreading of exceptional Gothic-war orders. Cataphracts/*clibanarii* concentrated in east; from sixth century many cavalry with both bow and lance. Infantry always numerical core (~2:1 unit ratio *Notitia*; Strasbourg 10k foot/3k horse; Maurice ideal 24k/10k). Navy was part of the army, not a separate service. *Scholae palatinae* replaced praetorians after Constantine dissolved them (312/324); still militarily used 559 vs Huns; lower worth 626 when attempt to divert rations to regulars during Avar-Persian siege. Careers: 20 years field / 24 border for full discharge bonus; long service common; after 395 emperors rarely led in person until Maurice/Heraclius revival.

### Rance: battle, equipment, treatises (Ch. 10)

Change from Constantine to Heraclius was **gradual role-shift**, not a medieval cavalry revolution; [[battle-of-adrianople-378|Adrianople 378]] heads traditional chronology but has been reinterpreted; “Byzantine” as a label for fifth–sixth-century eastern armies falsely implies discontinuity. Late antique *tactica* show a markedly more cautious attitude to pitched battle — still strategically decisive but ordinarily avoided without advantages of numbers, position, or surprise; “risk” pejorative, “opportunity” the watchword.

**Treatises.** Vegetius’ *Epitoma* (preferably Theodosius I, range 383–450) is a selective reform blueprint, not a full description of the contemporary army — expressly neglects cavalry because “present practice suffices.” Maurice’s *Strategicon* (ascribed 582–602) is the key late sixth-century handbook: codifies existing practice more than invents a new system; stresses infantry’s importance against readings of cavalry monopoly.

**Tactical roles.** Sample ratios: Strasbourg 357 — 10,000 infantry / 3,000 cavalry; 478 eastern force — 30,000 / 8,000; Belisarius Africa 533 — 10,000 / 5,000. Tactical emphasis shifted from infantry as main arm (to late fourth century) toward cavalry as main offensive arm by the early–late sixth under Hunnic/Avar influence — but Rance rejects dismissing sixth-century infantry as “unreliable.” At Taginae, Mons Lactarius (552) and Casilinum (554) the main Roman battle line was infantry or dismounted cavalry; sieges still required infantry. Low-intensity warfare forced cavalry to dismount; comparison of Ammianus on Rhine Alamanni/Franks (350s–60s) with Theophylact on Danube Slavs (590s) suggests long-term continuity in irregular operational capabilities.

**Equipment and “despecialization.”** Vegetius 1.20 armour claim rejected: artifactual, monumental, and historical evidence show continued mail/scale; by the sixth century front ranks got extra equipment while standard infantry panoply lightened for non-pitched-battle combat. Battlefield infantry shifted from earlier “volley and charge” toward a compact “legionary phalanx” (standard from the third century, option since early second) that often stayed stationary under missile barrage. Diverse missiles: *spiculum*/ango-type heavy javelins, *verutum*, crossbows/*manuballistae–arcuballistae*, *solenarion*, lead-weighted *plumbatae/mattiobarbuli* (Vegetius claims archer-like range en masse). Close-order infantry absorbed light-infantry capabilities (Vegetius wants a quarter–third of recruits trained as archers; Maurice forms ad hoc light units from a third–half of the best archers in close-order ranks). Cataphracti/*clibanarii* were never numerous and should not define unilinear progress toward heavier cavalry — shock/spectacle forces aiming to break morale, often cooperating with horse-archers. Procopius’ “composite archer-lancer” is an ideal (officers/*bucellarii*) more than universal type. By Maurice, cavalry train as both *cursores* and *defensores*; Avar models influence equipment; stirrups first appear in the *Strategicon* — not revolutionary as once thought (high saddles), but aiding quicker training of horse-archers.

**Battle deployment and *fulcum*.** Standard remained Principate-style: close-order infantry centre, cavalry wings, archers often rear firing overhead. Maurice’s *fulcum* is a shield-wall of front ranks boss-to-boss; Germanic etymology but **not pure “barbarization”** — narratives describe comparable walls/*testudo*-like linkage earlier. Late Roman infantry routinely deterred cavalry charges; dismounting cavalry to fight as infantry in crisis is frequent. Mounted combat as “pursuits and counter-pursuits”; feigned flight becomes frequent after Aurelian at Immae (272) under steppe influence. Casualty asymmetries in Roman victories: Strasbourg ~243 Roman dead vs Alamanni 6,000–8,000; Adrianople’s two-thirds Roman losses were **exceptional massacre**. Closing judgment: despite disasters like Adrianople and Yarmuk, late Roman forces **won far more actions than they lost**; adaptability (*harmozesthai*) to enemies and terrain — not simplistic “defeat” — explains combat performance; broader strategic/political contexts explain territorial loss better than battlefield inferiority.

### Lee, Whitby, Fear: state, logistics, social presence

**Lee** (Ch. 11): fifth–sixth-century *limitanei* given land to farm does **not** make them worthless “soldier-farmers” — they still received salary (into Justinian’s reign), need not have farmed personally, and land could incentivize frontier defence. For fiscal burden, logistics scale, and army share of late expenditure see [[roman-military-finance]]. **Whitby** challenges the paper-tiger caricature: late sixth-century victories (Monocarton 582, Solachon 586; Avar series 599) show continuing capability; capacity for recovery shown by third-century crisis vs early-fourth ascendancy. **Fear**: *limitanei* often live in villages not barracks (Abinnaeus; Dura detachments); Nessana Theodosians own fields/olive presses and marry locals while retaining complex rank structure; military markets spur trade (Diocletian price edict aims to stop merchants gouging soldiers). Training continuity: Maurice preserves fitness, multi-weapon, and *campidoctor* close-order drills distinguishing Roman from barbarian; *armatura* becomes festive specialty by Vegetius’ day; large-scale *ambulatio/decursio* and Avar-influenced ring hunt train envelopment.

## Woolf, *Rome: An Empire's Story* (2012)

Source: [[woolf-rome-empires-story-2012]].

- Early-imperial infantry army at the edge was slow against interior disasters; mobile enemies who broke through could outrun Roman armies; third-century raiders reached Athens, Ephesus, Tarragona finding rich cities often without functioning walls.
- Civic response: late third/fourth-century city defensive circuits; Gallienus began a more mobile cavalry-based rapid-response force, enlarged under Constantine; eastern Romans and Persians both developed heavy cavalry—landscape becoming more “medieval” (knights/castles among peasant villages).

## From Mitchell, Later Roman Empire (2015)

Source: [[mitchell-later-roman-empire-2015]].

- **Notitia Dignitatum overstates strength.** Eastern section ~401, western ~425: even if accurate as compilations, commanders overstated active men for supply claims and bureaucrats exaggerated ranks—official lists cannot be read as real military strength.
- **Threefold aims of fourth-century warfare.** Maintain frontier integrity against barbarians; civil war among rivals on Roman soil; provide elite squadrons with fighting as occupation and spoils/booty (Ammianus 353–78). Civil-war loyalty strengthened by rich Roman pickings and fiscal privileges (Licinius’s 311 Brigetio letter: up to five *capita* exemptions).
- **Force structure.** *Limitanei* (frontier garrisons), *comitatenses* (mobile field armies), *palatini* (capital forces); fifth–sixth-century elite retinues as *bucellarii*. Boundaries fluid; field armies drew from garrisons and increasingly from barbarians. Main commands with *magistri militum*, *comites*, and *duces*. Jones-style rejection of huge paper totals (Lactantius’s tetrarchic “quadrupling”; Lydus 435,266; *Notitia* readings to ~600,000) vs campaign figures.
- **Western collapse of capacity.** Heather (following Jones): ~half western regiments vanished 395–425; later “replenishment” largely regrading *limitanei* as *comitatenses*; by mid-fifth century “the army was a shadow of its former self” (cf. Liebeschuetz). Microcosm: Synesius’s Libya—forty Huns petitioning for proper pay; native *limitanei* demoralized, protection by imported barbarians.
- **Numerical decline.** Agathias’s ~150,000 at Justinian’s death (565) vs alleged earlier peak 645,000—none trustworthy, but “very steep numerical decline since the fourth century.” Largest reliable post-350 field army = Julian’s 65,000 (363); Edessa 503 = 52,000 never subsequently matched; Belisarius-era Persian campaigns 20–30k; Africa 15k / Italy initially 7k regulars; Heraclius’s mobile Zagros campaigns perhaps no more than 10–25k.
- **Pay and mutiny.** Recurrent pay reductions/withholding: Procopius on *limitanei* arrears; African mutiny under Stotzas; Italian arrears; Priscus quarter-pay cut 588; Maurice’s Balkan pay cuts and 602 winter-order produced mutiny elevating Phocas. Anastasius’ Long Wall of Thrace (~60 km west of Constantinople) as Constantinopolitan defense against Bulgar danger. Strasburg 357 re-established Rhine control for fifty years (Julian’s 13,000 vs Chnodomar’s 35,000 Alemanni).
