# The Languages of Pre-Roman Italy

Pre-Roman Italy held an **astonishing diversity of languages**, attested by
inscriptions from nearly every region over more than 700 years — from the Greek
colonists' introduction of the **alphabet** in the eighth century BCE to the
general adoption of **Latin** that extinguished the local tongues by the first.
[[boardman-cah-v4-1988|CAH Vol. IV]] (Ch. 15, J. H. W. Penney, *The Languages of
Italy*) treats them as the best evidence for identifying the **peoples** of ancient
Italy, since "whatever literature there may have been besides that of Rome has been
lost." The inscriptions are far more reliable than the glosses in ancient authors or
the etymologies of modern speculation.

## The alphabet and its diffusion

The history of writing is itself "an important part of the cultural history of early
Italy." The far south and Sicily borrowed the alphabet directly from the **Greek
colonies**; the **[[etruscans|Etruscans]]** borrowed it very early (from the Euboean
settlers of [[pithecusae|Pithecusa]] and Cyme) and **transmitted it to most of the
rest of Italy**. Borrowing was never mechanical: each people adjusted the model to
its own sound-system — dropping the Greek O and the signs for voiced stops (useless
in Etruscan), inventing a digraph and later the letter **8 for [f]** (perhaps a
Sabine invention passed back to the Etruscans), splitting V to mark *o* versus *u*.
These divergences let scholars **trace each alphabet's pedigree**.

## The non-Indo-European languages

- **Etruscan** — c. 10,000 inscriptions (earliest c. 700, latest 1st century),
  mostly short epitaphs of personal names; the longer texts are the *Cippus
  Perusinus*, the Capua tile, the bilingual **Pyrgi gold tablets** (Etruscan and
  Phoenician), and the **liturgical linen book** reused as Egyptian mummy-wrapping
  (over 1,200 words). Etruscan **belongs to no known language family**, so the
  etymological method fails; its only clear relative is the **pre-Greek language of
  Lemnos** (the fifth-century stela), a genetic link that seems likely. Modern
  progress is **grammatical and "bi-linguistic"** (deducing content from context),
  not decipherment — the script is read, the meaning often is not.
- **Raetic** (the Alpine inscriptions) — probably non-Indo-European, with possible
  affinities to Etruscan, fitting the ancient tradition that the Raeti were
  Etruscans driven into the mountains by the Gauls.
- **North Picene** (the Novilara stela, c. 500) — remains "in isolation," possibly
  not even Indo-European.

## The Indo-European languages

Most of Italy's languages were Indo-European, falling into the **Italic** group and
some outliers:

- **Latin and Faliscan** — a closely-related sub-group of Italic; archaic Faliscan
  is "remarkably like the language of the earliest Latin inscriptions," the two
  diverging substantially only from the fifth century.
- **Osco-Umbrian** (the other Italic sub-group) — **Oscan** (the language of the
  Samnites/Sabelli, remarkably uniform across four centuries), **Umbrian** (of the
  Iguvine Tables), and the central "minor dialects" (Marsian, Vestinian,
  Marrucinian, Paelignian). The early **South Picene** inscriptions — including the
  Penna Sant'Andrea texts naming the *Safineis* — are now read as a forerunner of
  Osco-Umbrian, documenting the [[italic-peoples|Italic peoples]] back into the
  Archaic period.
- **Venetic** (north-east; ~250–300 inscriptions) — Indo-European, probably linked
  to Italic/Latin; written in a north-Etruscan alphabet with syllabic punctuation.
- **Messapic** (Apulia/the Iapyges) — a distinct language of the south-east, of
  possible Balkan ("Illyrian") origin but unproven.
- **Lepontic and Gaulish** (the lakes and the Po) — **Celtic**; Lepontic perhaps an
  early wave of Celtic immigrants preceding the main Gallic incursions of the late
  fifth and fourth centuries.
- **Ligurian** — only vestigially attested in place-names; the Lunigiana
  statue-menhir inscriptions are assigned to it on geography alone.

## Why it matters

The linguistic map is the **skeleton of the ethnic map**: it confirms the in-situ
distinctiveness of the [[etruscans|Etruscans]], traces the **expansion of the
Osco-Umbrian peoples** from the central Apennines, and shows the **spread of literacy**
radiating from Greek colonies through Etruria to the whole peninsula. The
inscriptions also preserve native **institutions** — the Oscan *touto* (citizen
body), the *meddix tuticus*, the Umbrian *maro* and *uhtur* — otherwise lost.

## Limitations and debates

The greatest limitation is **brevity and obscurity**: most texts are short
name-lists, Etruscan is grammatically read but semantically opaque, and South Picene,
Messapic, Raetic and North Picene resist confident interpretation. The central
**theoretical dispute** is whether the Italic languages arose by **divergence** from
a unified Proto-Italic or by **convergence** of originally distinct tongues in
prolonged contact — unresolved. The very term *Italic* is contested, used narrowly
(Osco-Umbrian only) or broadly (the whole Indo-European group of Italy).
