# The Imperial Finances (Augustus to Vitellius)

The fiscal system of the early **[[principate|Principate]]** — how the emperors raised and
spent the money of empire, and how they came to **monopolize control of state finance** without
ever formally abolishing the Republican treasury. Treated in [[bowman-cah-v10-1996|CAH X]]
Ch. 8 (**D. W. Rathbone**).

## Expenditure: the army was not the whole story

The largest single regular cost was the **standing army** (a legionary's pay was 900 sesterces
a year; a discharge bounty 12,000): modern estimates put the wage bill, with bounties, at **400
million sesterces** or more. But Rathbone corrects the textbook picture: much of this was
**notional** (deductions at source; supplies in kind rather than cash), and the army "in this
period accounted on average for **less than half** of all imperial spending." The other half
was **munificence** — public buildings, roads, the corn supply, games and festivals, and the
*ad hoc* **liberality** expected of the emperor as the richest man in the world (Nero: "there
is nothing that might not be hoped for from my magnanimity"). Claudius probably spent as much
in cash in and around Rome each year as the army cost. The later claim that taxation existed
"to pay for the armies that guaranteed peace" has, Rathbone notes, "a propagandist whiff."

## Revenue: pragmatism, not system

- **No direct tax on citizens or Italian soil.** As heir to the city-state ideology, Rome did
  not tax Roman citizens on the person or "citizen land" (*ius Italicum*); the *tributum* levied
  on Italy in the triumviral emergency was discontinued.
- **Provincial direct taxes** (*tributum soli* on land, *tributum capitis* on persons) were
  levied on subject lands. Augustus introduced a **regular cash poll-tax** (first in Egypt) and
  spread Roman-style **census** assessment, brusquely monetizing the northern provinces. But
  rates and bases differed province to province — "not a programmatic scheme." Collection was
  devolved to the **theoretically autonomous cities and tribes**, each owing a fixed lump sum;
  the *publicani* were largely **eliminated** from direct-tax collection in the Greek East
  (the work of Gabinius and Caesar).
- **Indirect taxes (*vectigalia*)** — customs (*portoria*; 25% on the eastern frontier, 2–5%
  between provinces), the 1% auction tax, the 4% slave-sales tax, the **5% inheritance tax**
  (*vicesima hereditatum*, which *did* reach Roman citizens), and the 5% manumission tax — were
  still farmed to *publicani* and were "probably crucial," being politically easier to raise and
  yielding immediate cash.
- **State assets and the *patrimonium*.** Beyond *ager publicus* and mines, the emperor's own
  **patrimonium** (the estates of the Julii, Octavii, and Claudii) grew uniquely — by legacy
  (his "friends" embraced much of the nobility and the client kings), by absorbing the mines,
  and by usurping from the *aerarium* the *bona vacantia*, *caduca*, and *bona damnatorum* (a
  Hellenistic royal prerogative). Its political value: it let emperors claim they **subsidized**
  rather than exploited the state.

## Coinage

Augustus stabilized a **trimetallic** system (gold *aureus* = 25 silver *denarii*; *as* and
fractions; the *sestertius* the unit of account), minted chiefly at **Lyons** and **Rome**.
Minting was effectively **controlled by the emperor** (the "S.C." on bronze advertised official
coinage, not senatorial control). Rathbone argues there was **little forward planning**: heavy
issues of small denominations were one-off responses to shortages (cf. the "liquidity crisis"
of AD 33), and **Nero's reform of AD 64** (45 aurei and 96 denarii to the pound; silver reduced
to 93.5%) is best explained not as a money-saving "devaluation" but as an administrative and
**aesthetic** overhaul (it accompanied an attempt at an all-orichalcum bronze coinage).

## The emperor's control: a "red herring" of administration

Rathbone's central argument cuts through the long dispute over whether Claudius created a new
imperial treasury (*fiscus*) separate from the Republican ***aerarium Saturni***. The evidence
"tells against any neat division between 'imperial' and 'senatorial' finances": the *aerarium*
was credited with the revenues of imperial Egypt; the imperial accountants (*a rationibus*)
existed under Augustus, not just Claudius. **The administrative question is a red herring** —
Augustus and his successors controlled state finance not by diverting revenue into a new
treasury but by **monopolizing the decision-making**. State finance was **de-politicized by the
death of Republican politics**: no ambitious individual could any longer propose controversial
wars, buildings, or fiscal changes; the army was paid automatically, the plebs fed, the
provinces taxed at a level that "for over two hundred years underwent only minor adjustments."

## A structural weakness

Rathbone stresses a paradox that would haunt the Principate: imperial wealth was **enormous but
hard to mobilize**. Because Rome depended on local elite **co-operation** to collect taxes, it
"rarely dared to increase provincial taxation"; because emperors faced criticism for taxation
but credit for spending, they **preferred to spend rather than save** (Tiberius' large reserve
was exceptional, and Gaius spent it at once). The result was a chronic **lack of central
reserves** — "a weakness embedded in the system from its inception" — leaving needy emperors to
turn to the Senate and the rich, via the inheritance tax or, in the villainous version,
confiscation and judicial murder.

## Contested

- Whether a separate *fiscus* was created (and when) — Rathbone (no neat division) vs. the older
  Millar/Jones debate.
- The rationale of minting and of Nero's reform — fiscal expedient vs. administrative/aesthetic.
- Whether provincial taxation was light (and thus a reason for elite acceptance of Rome) or
  whether the dependence on local co-operation **constrained** Rome from raising it.
