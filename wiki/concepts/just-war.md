# Just War (Bellum Iustum) and the Sanctification of Violence

## Definition

**Just war** (*bellum iustum*) is the Christian doctrine that some wars are morally
legitimate — even meritorious — when waged by proper authority, for a just cause,
and with right intention. [[luscombe-ncmh-v4-2004|NCMH IV]] (Ernst-Dieter Hehl,
Ch. 7, "War, peace and the Christian order") traces how, in the eleventh and twelfth
centuries, the older patristic framework was transformed: war ceased to be merely a
regrettable necessity tolerated for the sake of peace and became, for the warrior
who fought rightly, **a positively Christian act** — the conceptual road that led to
the [[crusades|Crusade]].

## The Dual Evaluation of War

Hehl frames the period around a long-standing **dual evaluation**: fighting was both
condemned (as sin and worldly violence) and required (as the duty of the ruler to
maintain order). The Carolingian and Ottonian ideal of kingship made the **first
duty of the ruler the maintenance of peace and justice** — Conrad II went to
Friesland in 1038 *pacem firmando*, "to strengthen the peace," and the coronation
*ordines* charged the king to protect church, people, and kingdom and to fight the
heathen. War, in this vision, was a means to maintain world order — to keep the
peace.

## The Eleventh-Century Transformation

What was new, Hehl argues, was twofold:

1. **The Church itself took up the direction of violence.** Building on the
   [[peace-of-god|Peace and Truce of God]], reforming churchmen developed ideas about
   how the church could fulfil the protective tasks once reserved to kings —
   recruiting "knights of St Peter" (*milites sancti Petri*), and, from
   Gregory VII's planned 1074 eastern expedition onward, sanctifying war in defence
   of Christians and the holy places.
2. **War became a path to salvation.** Fighting non-Christians in defence of
   Christian belief and believers came to be seen as "a means to salvation for the
   warrior, indeed a duty that fell upon the church." The crusade fused, for the
   first time, a **worldly activity (war) with the Christian life**, no longer
   opposed to one another. As Hehl puts it, "to a certain extent, western Christendom
   constituted itself as such by going jointly to war": the pope summoned the whole
   of Latin Christendom, and the warrior's ideal justification shifted from the old
   aristocratic pursuit of fame to **love of neighbour**.

## Canon Law and Systematisation

The doctrine was given juristic shape in the twelfth century, above all in
**[[ius-commune|Gratian's *Decretum*]]** (Causa 23), which gathered the Augustinian
texts on war into a coherent body and asked systematically when violence was lawful —
providing the framework later refined by the Decretists and by Thomas Aquinas. Just
war thus sits at the intersection of the period's military, religious, and legal
transformations.

## Significance

The just-war idea underwrote the **militarisation of medieval Christianity**: the
military-religious orders (Templars, Hospitallers), the ideology of the
[[reconquista|Reconquista]] and the Baltic crusades, and the crusade movement
itself. It is the doctrinal bridge between the warrior ethic of [[chivalry]] and the
institutional Church, and a foundational strand in the longer Western tradition of
the ethics of war.
