## Definition and Scope

Islamic epistemology in theology (kalam) addresses two interlocking questions: (1) how human minds can attain knowledge, and (2) what it means to understand divine speech — the Quran — given that God's discourse differs categorically from human language. The field was not a separate philosophical discipline; it was embedded in legal theory (usul al-fiqh) and dogmatic theology, and the central debates arose from the practical need to interpret revelation correctly.

The core tension runs between **literal meaning** and **figural or allusive meaning** (haqiqa vs. majaz), and between **essentialist semantics** — words anchor to universal essences behind them — and **pragmatic or performative semantics** — words mean what speakers in contexts intend them to mean. Both poles had theological stakes: the first risked intellectualising God in an Avicennan direction; the second risked reducing divine speech to the conventions of seventh-century Arabia.

Sources for this page draw on Paul-A. Hardy's chapter in [[winter-cambridge-companion-islamic-theology-2008]].

## The Kalam Doctrine of Divine Speech

Classical kalam held by consensus that "the Creator of the world is a speaker (mutakallim)" (Hardy, citing al-Ghazali's *al-Mustasfa*). Divine discourse comprises several illocutionary types: statement (khabar), command (amr), prohibition (nahy), question (istikhbar), and others. Recognising this range was crucial: if God's speech includes commands and prohibitions, the simple equation of meaning with truth-conditions (applicable only to statements) breaks down.

[[asharism]] resolved this by distinguishing **kalam nafsi** — eternal "interior speech" devoid of sound and letters, which al-Ashari called the real divine discourse — from the expressed Arabic text of the Quran, which enters time as a sign of that eternal speech. This distinction enabled Asharites to affirm the Quran's divine authority while acknowledging its linguistic-temporal mediation.

## Al-Ghazali's Verbal Epistemology

Al-Ghazali developed a two-tier epistemology of divine discourse, most fully articulated in *al-Mustasfa min 'ilm al-usul* and the esoteric *Mishkat al-anwar* (Niche of Lights).

**First tier — logical-conventional semantics.** For the Muslim community (umma) and its scholars, understanding the Quran proceeds through prior cognition of *wad' al-lugha* — the conventional assignments of Arabic speakers. Faith (iman) is *tasdiq*, assent to truth: "its essential nature (haqiqa) is recognition (al-i'tiraf) of the existence of what the Messenger has reported about its existence." Al-Ghazali, following Aristotle and Avicenna, held that logical norms govern quranic expression: words signify by correspondence (dalalat al-mutabaqa), inclusion (dalalat al-tadamun), or implication (dalalat al-iltizam). Figurative meaning (majaz) is asymmetrically dependent on literal meaning: "every majaz has a haqiqa but it is not necessary that every haqiqa have a majaz."

**Second tier — mystical-allusive semantics.** In the *Niche of Lights*, written after the *Revival of the Religious Sciences*, al-Ghazali presents a higher register accessible only to the *arifun* (knowers, saints). At this level, the Quran ceases to be a static container of meaning. "Nothing possesses existence (huwiya) other than He [God] except in a figural sense (bi'l-majaz)" — meaning that for the saint, all language ultimately refers to God. The knowers "ascend from majaz to haqiqa": figural speech becomes, paradoxically, more literal than what ordinary speakers call literal. God's proclamation "never leaves their hearing" without the mediation of a reported khabar.

The mechanism is **ishara** (allusion, pointing): a speech-act performed with a quranic sentence that already has a literal meaning, which simultaneously extends beyond it without cancelling it. Al-Ghazali explicitly rejects the Ismaili Batiniyya's erasure of the zahir (surface meaning): "only those who bring the two together achieve perfection." The Quran thus functions like a Möbius strip — inner and outer are one surface.

**The dilemma.** If God's intentions are linguistically formulated, they will be indeterminate, as the historian al-Shahrastani (d. 1153) observed: "texts are limited; facts are infinite." If God's intentions are non-linguistic (the kalam nafsi view), they are complete but risk a God whose mind resembles Avicenna's Neoplatonic Intellect more than the personal Quran-speaker. Al-Ghazali navigates between these poles by bifurcating his theory along the two tiers above.

## Ibn Taymiyya's Critique

Ibn Taymiyya (d. 1328) attacked the entire apparatus of logical semantics as a fiction. His position, developed in *al-Radd 'ala al-mantiqiyyin* and *Kitab al-Iman*, holds that meaning is constituted purely by **discourse in use** (al-kalam al-musta'mal) — what speakers actually do with words in specific communicative contexts.

Key theses:
- The fundamental unit of analysis is the **sentence**, not the word: "meaningful discourse is only conveyed by a complete sentence." Individual words stripped of context carry no fixed meaning.
- God's habit of address (*'adat al-mutakallim*) is the sole key to interpretation. "The mere hearing of the expression without knowledge of the speaker and his habit signifies nothing."
- No essences lurk behind words. "Meaning is nothing deeper than the use of ordinary words in particular contexts."
- The distinction between literal and figural meaning is unstable and ultimately erases itself: many "literal" words are dead metaphors whose original figural life has been forgotten. "'Literal' [reflects] those words we are able to handle based on our present and past knowledge."
- **Hermeneutics, not epistemology** is the foundation: "The soundest method of commentary on the Quran is to comment on it with the Quran itself."
- Faith (iman) is not tasdiq (assent to truth): "it may very easily turn out that someone may say, 'I know very well that what you say is true; nevertheless, I will not follow you but fight against you.'" God speaks to command obedience, not to convey propositional truth.

Ibn al-Qayyim (d. 1350), Ibn Taymiyya's student, extended this by arguing that language is learned through **tawqif** (divine inspiration) operative in particular communicative acts — not through a prior convention (istilah) established by agreement. Children learn language from exposure and pointing, never from a prior assignment of names.

## Comparison and Theological Stakes

| Dimension | Al-Ghazali | Ibn Taymiyya |
|---|---|---|
| Semantic unit | Word (and its essence) | Sentence-in-context |
| Meaning-ground | Wad' al-lugha + logical essence | 'Adat al-mutakallim (speaker's habit) |
| Literal vs. figural | Figural asymmetrically depends on literal | Distinction ultimately erases; literal = familiar usage |
| Faith (iman) | Tasdiq (assent to truth) | Obedient response to command |
| Higher meaning | Ishara (allusion), multi-tiered | No; one level, empirical world |
| Quran as | Arena of encounter, point of ascent | Self-interpreting text; hermeneutic circle |

Both positions, Hardy argues, ironically share the view of the Quran as a "static container of meaning" in their first-order registers — al-Ghazali's esoteric tier transcends this, but only for saints. Both remain bound to the perceptible world: "a world means perceptible objects continuous in space and time." Al-Ghazali breaks free by positing a *malakut* (immaterial world of divine royalty) for which earthly language is only a representation.

## Connection to Kalam Schools

- [[kalam]]: the disciplinary home of these debates; the consensus that God is a "speaker" is its starting point
- [[asharism]]: contributed the kalam nafsi doctrine (eternal interior speech) that al-Ghazali presupposes; affirmed that prophets and saints hear divine speech differently from ordinary believers
- [[mutazilism]]: would reduce divine speech to created, conventional language; rejected by Asharites
- [[occasionalism-islamic]]: the Asharite metaphysical framework (God creates all events including cognitive acts) underlies al-Ghazali's claim that God "creates in the hearer a necessary knowledge"
- [[philosophical-sufism]]: the doctrine of ishara and the arifun derives from Sufi gnoseology; al-Ghazali's *Niche of Lights* occupies the interface of kalam and Sufism
- [[quran]]: the primary object of all interpretation; its status as uncreated or created (the key Mutazilite-Sunni controversy) frames the entire debate
- [[tahafut-al-falasifa]]: al-Ghazali's critique of the philosophers shapes his appropriation and critique of Avicennan epistemology here
- [[islamicate]]: the broader civilisational context in which these debates mattered for law, politics, and spiritual authority

## Historiography

The chapter by Paul-A. Hardy in the Cambridge Companion applies contemporary philosophy of language (Austin's speech-act theory, Gricean pragmatics) to classical Islamic debates, which risks anachronism but illuminates structural parallels productively. Hardy is explicit that al-Ghazali and Ibn Taymiyya anticipate disputes between modern Muslim traditionalists (often Ghazalian) and Salafi-fundamentalists (Taymiyyan).

Collection coverage: the Bibliotheca Alexandrina is strong on al-Ghazali and medieval kalam; thinner on Ibn Taymiyya's own texts and on post-medieval legal-epistemological debates. The [[kalam]] page covers the broader theological discipline.

## Key Figures

- [[al-ghazali]] (d. 1111): Asharite synthesiser; two-tier verbal epistemology; *Mishkat al-anwar*, *al-Mustasfa*
- [[ibn-taymiyya]] (d. 1328): Hanbali critic; discourse-in-use; hermeneutic over epistemological approach
- Al-Shahrastani (d. 1153): historian of religions; noted the finitude-of-text problem
- Ibn al-Qayyim (d. 1350): Ibn Taymiyya's student; extended the tawqif theory of language origins
- Abu Hayyan al-Tawhidi (d. 1023): Sufi theorist of ishara; *al-Isharat al-Ilahiya*
