# Mass Extinction

A **mass extinction** is the sudden, worldwide disappearance of a large
proportion (conventionally ≳40%) of biological groups across a range of
ecological niches over a brief interval of geological time. It is distinguished
from **background extinction** — the continuous, ordinary loss of species that is
"the price paid for evolution" — by its scale, speed, and breadth. More than
99.9% of all species that ever lived are extinct; fossil species average only a
few million years of existence (marine invertebrates ~5–10 million; Cenozoic
mammals ~1–2 million).

## The Establishment of Extinction

That species could vanish at all was long denied. Carl Linnaeus held the number
of species fixed since creation; Jean-Baptiste de Lamarck doubted extinction
(though more subtly than usually credited); Thomas Jefferson thought the American
mammoth might still live in the West. **Georges Cuvier** (1769–1832), founder of
comparative anatomy and vertebrate paleontology, first *established* extinction
as real: in 1806 he distinguished living elephants from the extinct mammoth and
mastodon and argued that conspicuous Pleistocene forms (woolly rhinoceros, Irish
elk, cave bear) and a Mesozoic mosasaur were too large to survive undiscovered
and must be extinct. Cuvier explained extinction by **catastrophism** — a series
of violent revolutions, the Noachian flood the most recent — which later gave way
to gradualist understanding, but his insight that extinction is real endured.

## The "Big Five"

Quantitative work by **Raup and Sepkoski** (plotting extinctions of marine
invertebrate families through time) revealed five peaks rising above background:

| Event | Age (approx.) | Severity |
|---|---|---|
| End-Ordovician | ~443 Ma | ~12% of families; glaciation, sea-level fall |
| Late Devonian | ~365 Ma | ~75% of species; reefs and shallow marine faunas |
| End-Permian ("Great Dying") | ~252 Ma | >90–95% of marine species — the largest of all; Siberian Traps volcanism, Pangaea |
| End-Triassic | ~201 Ma | many land vertebrates and ~90% of bivalve species; volcanism |
| End-Cretaceous (K–Pg) | ~66 Ma | the dinosaurs, pterosaurs, ammonites; best-studied (see below) |

(Ages reflect the 2016 figures given by Rhodes; specialist values differ
slightly.) Each "event" typically spans hundreds of thousands to several million
years and often comprises multiple pulses, complicating any single-cause story.

## Causes and the K–Pg Debate

Suggested drivers include glaciation and sea-level change, continental
reconfiguration, massive volcanism (flood basalts), bolide impact, ocean anoxia,
and disease. The **end-Cretaceous (K–Pg)** extinction is the touchstone case: the
Alvarez team's 1980 discovery of an iridium anomaly, shocked quartz, and
spherules — plus the Chicxulub crater — argues for an asteroid impact, while the
contemporaneous Deccan Traps volcanism argues for (or compounds) a volcanic
cause. The dispute is treated in full at
[[controversies/k-pg-extinction-cause|What Caused the End-Cretaceous (K–Pg) Mass
Extinction?]]. A recurring puzzle for *any* single cause is **differential
survival**: birds, mammals, many insects, fish, and corals passed through the
K–Pg while dinosaurs and ammonites did not, despite sharing environments.

## The "Sixth Extinction"

Human predation, habitat destruction, and introduced species have driven historic
extinctions (the dodo, 1662; the passenger pigeon, last individual 1914) and are
raising extinction rates far above background. E. O. Wilson and others argue a
sixth mass extinction is under way — "we are the giant meteorite of our time" —
making the deep-time record of past extinctions a guide to a possible future. In
this framing the [[controversies/pleistocene-megafaunal-extinction|late-Pleistocene
megafaunal losses]] may mark the human contribution's early onset.
