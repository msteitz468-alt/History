# The Anthropocene

## Definition

The **Anthropocene** is the proposed geological epoch in which humankind has
exerted a significant — even dominant — impact on the Earth's environment, such
that human activity rivals or exceeds the great natural forces shaping the planet's
systems. Popularized by Paul Crutzen from 2000, it is offered as the successor to
the Holocene, the ~11,700-year interglacial in which agriculture and civilization
arose.

J. R. McNeill, in [[mcneill-cwh-v7-2015|CWH VII]], adopts the term as the master
frame for the post-1750 world while treating it as a *historian's* concept rather
than (yet) a ratified geological one. For McNeill the Anthropocene is, above all, a
way of underscoring "the scale, scope, pace, and centrality of environmental change
in human affairs."

## Emic / Etic

Purely **etic** — an analytical and stratigraphic category coined by late-twentieth
and twenty-first-century scientists. No historical actor before c. 1980 conceived
of their age in these terms; the concept is a product of the very environmental
crisis it names.

## What drove it: the great shifts

McNeill identifies three thresholds in the human relationship with the Earth, each
increasing the complexity of society and of its environmental footprint: the
acquisition of fire and language (which made us human); the adoption of agriculture
([[domestication]], which laid the basis for cities and states); and **the adoption
of fossil fuels, which made us modern.** The Anthropocene's chief motor is the
[[fossil-fuel-energy-transition|fossil-fuel energy transition]]: total human energy
use multiplied roughly **90–100-fold between 1750 and 2015**, and by c. 1870 humans
were already burning more fossil energy each year than all annual photosynthesis
produced. Coupled to this was unprecedented [[demographic-transition|population
growth]] and [[industrialization]].

## McNeill's two-period scheme

Following historians' conventions (no need for the geologists' "golden spike" or
synchronicity), McNeill dates the Anthropocene **diachronously** — Venice and Mexico
City had entered their local Anthropocenes before 1750; remote peaks and ocean
trenches perhaps not yet. Within the 1750–present epoch he distinguishes two phases:

- **c. 1750–1950 — the age of coal**, with ~1% annual population growth. "Turbulent."
- **c. 1950–present — the age of oil**, with ~2% annual population growth. "Tumultuous."
  This second phase is the **[[great-acceleration|Great Acceleration]]**, and it
  provoked its own antithesis: modern popular **[[environmentalism]]** after c. 1965.

## The "golden spike" debate

Geologists, who claim authority over Earth's periodization, require a synchronous,
datable stratigraphic marker. Leading candidates: the layer of **radioactive fallout**
from atmospheric nuclear testing (first test, Alamogordo, July 1945; atmospheric ban,
1963) — favoring a 1945/1950 start tied to the [[world-wars-era|World Wars Era]] and
Great Acceleration — and the global layer of **concrete** (now ~3 tons per square
meter of land). Earlier proposed starts include late-Pleistocene megafaunal
extinctions (~15,000 BP; see [[pleistocene-megafaunal-extinction]]) and the
Neolithic; some argue the epoch has not begun at all.

## Limitations and critiques

- **Start date is genuinely unresolved** — estimates span ~15,000 BP to 1945 to
  "not yet," reflecting disagreement over *which* human impacts should count.
- **"Anthropos" obscures inequality:** for a century after 1850 high fossil-energy
  use was confined to ~one-fifth of humanity (Europe, North America, later Japan) —
  the single most important source of those regions' global dominance. Critics
  (e.g. proponents of the "Capitalocene") argue the responsibility is far from
  evenly human.
- **Risk of teleology and determinism** in reading all of modern history as a march
  toward environmental crisis.
