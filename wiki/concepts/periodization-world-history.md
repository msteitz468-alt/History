## Definition and Scope

Periodization is the division of historical time into named segments with
identifiable beginnings, endings, and dominant characteristics. It is
fundamental to all historical writing — history cannot be written without
implicit or explicit periodizations — but it takes distinctive and especially
contested forms in world history.

Using the language of Kracauer and Adorno, Lang describes the historiographical
period as a "force field" pulled between poles of chronology and immanence,
perpetually in tension and without resolution. The period implies universality
(time shared by humanity as a whole) while every actual period is particular
(shaped by specific regions, classes, and cultures).

## The Core Tension

Two poles of the problem:

**Universality**: McNeill offered the world as a whole and its evolution as an
evolutionary process as a periodizing framework. Toynbee offered civilizations.
These attempt to find universals that can anchor a genuinely global
periodization. But the danger, as Lang notes, is that such attempts merely
"shift the spiritual unity of the nation onto the world as a whole" —
replacing nationalism with a subtler universalism that still imposes one
community's categories on everyone else.

**Particularity**: Postmodern and postcolonial historians (Heidegger, Foucault,
Derrida) insisted on the selectiveness of universalist histories and their
roles as ideologies and forms of power. Moving toward the particular means
privileging lived experience, local context, and contingency over grand
periodizing schemes. But pure particularity collapses world history into a
random collection of local histories.

## Historical Development of the Problem

- **19th century**: Nation states shaped both periodization and the historians
  who worked within them. Ranke saw the nation as a partial resolution:
  the state as a universal integrating multiple particulars. But the
  universality of the nation state was always incomplete.
- **20th century**: National conflicts undermined national periodization.
  Toynbee, McNeill, and world-systems historians sought new universals.
- **Late 20th century**: Postcolonial and postmodern turns challenged all
  universalizing periodizations. Roland Barthes (1956): either posit a
  reality permeable by history or a reality impenetrable and particular.
- **World history today**: No consensus periodization. Each volume of the
  *Cambridge World History* uses a different organizational schema,
  deliberately chosen to challenge standard periodization such as
  "classical" or "early modern."

## Implications for This Wiki

The temporal framework in `CLAUDE.md` (24 named periods) is one solution to
the periodization problem — a working scaffold, not a claim to have resolved
the theoretical tensions. The framework blends:
- Archaeological/geological periodization (Paleolithic, Neolithic, Bronze Age)
  for prehistory, which avoids European-centred categories
- Broadly western-conventional periodization (Classical Antiquity, Middle Ages)
  for ancient and medieval periods, where the collection is strongest
- Neutral chronological labels (Long 19th Century, World Wars Era) for modern
  periods

Users of this wiki should understand that period labels are analytical tools,
not ontological claims about the structure of the past.
