## Definition

The *repartimiento de mercancias* (also *reparto de mercancias*) was the forced distribution of goods — typically overvalued merchandise — by colonial corregidores to indigenous communities at prices set by the corregidor himself. Communities were compelled to accept the goods (mules, cloth, wine, iron tools, and other items) and to repay in cash or in labor at the assigned valuation, regardless of whether they wanted or needed the goods.

Emic usage: the term was used by colonial administrators and eventually appeared in indigenous petitions as a named grievance. Etic usage: historians treat the *reparto* as a parasitic extraction mechanism grafted onto the tribute system that systematically transferred wealth from indigenous households to corregidores and their merchant backers.

The *repartimiento de mercancias* is closely linked to but distinct from [[mita|mita]] (labor draft) and [[colonial-andean-tribute|tribute]] (the *tasa*): it operated outside the legally mandated tribute schedule and was technically illegal for most of the colonial period, though widely practiced.

## Origins and Mechanics

Corregidores were royal officials appointed to govern indigenous "districts" (*corregimientos*) and to supervise [[reduccion|reduccion]] communities. Beginning in 1680 a law of "saleable offices" required corregidores to purchase their posts, which they then recouped through intensified exactions on the indigenous population. The *reparto* was their primary instrument. As Thierry Saignes notes: "Of course they responded [to the purchase requirement] by increasing their exactions on Indians, and they did so with relative impunity because they could shift the onus of collection onto the [[curaca|caciques]]."

The mechanism worked through the [[curaca|caciques]], who were required to distribute the goods within their communities and collect the resulting debt. An intercepted note copied by Father José de Arriaga illustrates the relationship: a lieutenant corregidor wrote to a cacique: "Brother X, I am sending you today a certain number of bottles of wine. See to it that they are sold in so many days and at such and such a price. Since I look after your business, look after mine." This collusion simultaneously implicated the caciques and made them absorb popular resentment for extracting repayment.

The [[reduccion|reductions]] "constituted the new framework for enforcing the tribute quota (*tasa*), the rotating labor service ([[mita]]), and later the forced purchase of merchandise (*repartos de mercancias*)."

## Relationship to Tribute and the Colonial Fiscal System

Saignes distinguishes three channels of forced commerce: "transactions that went to the state, those that went to the market, and those that went to the Church." The *reparto* occupied the market channel but operated through state agents, blurring the distinction between taxation and commercial extortion. Andean communities did not in principle object to monetization as such; they "in principle did not challenge the tributes, the mining corvée, the forced sale of merchandise, or even the ecclesiastical tithe, only the manner in which they were applied." Anger arose when corregidores "arbitrarily decided who had to pay tribute or serve as a mitayo, or when they altered the price of foodstuffs the Indians had to sell."

## Geographic and Chronological Scope

The *reparto* existed in some form across the viceroyalty of Peru throughout the colonial period but became most damaging after 1680 as the saleable-offices policy spread. The Bourbon [[bourbon-reforms]] of the mid-eighteenth century initially attempted to legalize and regulate the *reparto* (under the 1756 *reglamento*), creating officially sanctioned amounts by province and commodity — a reform that backfired by giving corregidores legal cover for abuses they had previously had to conduct clandestinely.

The *reparto* was one of the chief immediate grievances cited by participants in the [[tupac-amaru-rebellion-1780|Tupac Amaru II uprising]] (1780). Its abolition was one of the first concessions the crown made during and after suppressing the revolt.

## Limitations and Critiques

The concept is sometimes confused in the literature with the earlier *repartimiento de indios* (labor allocation), which is related to but distinct from the *mita*. "Repartimiento" thus carries two distinct colonial meanings depending on context. The forced *reparto de mercancias* should not be conflated with voluntary market participation by indigenous communities, which Saignes treats as a separate and more complex phenomenon. Not all goods distributed through the *reparto* were resented equally; communities sometimes used reparto goods as inputs for local production or onward trade, making the line between coerced and voluntary commerce difficult to draw.
