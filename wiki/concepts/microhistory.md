# Microhistory (and Its Relation to World History)

## Definition

**Microhistory** is the analytical approach, developed in Italy in the 1970s–80s
(Carlo Ginzburg, Giovanni Levi, Edoardo Grendi), of studying the past at a
drastically **reduced scale of observation** — a single village, trial, person, or
document — read intensively to reveal structures and connections invisible at the
macro scale. Ginzburg's chapter in CWH VI makes a deliberately counter-intuitive
claim: microhistory, **far from being opposed to world history, is an
indispensable tool of it.**

## The Argument: Scale, Evidence, and Comparison

Ginzburg frames the methodological dilemma of writing history in a globalized,
endangered world. Grand narratives risk being **ethnocentric**; but
"analytic approaches based on first-hand evidence are incompatible with the breadth
and scope" of a global inquiry. The obvious escape — **comparison** — is, he warns,
no panacea: Marc Bloch's 1928 call for comparative history remained "an unfulfilled
promise," and Evans-Pritchard quipped that "there is only one method... the
comparative method — and that is impossible." Microhistory offers a third way: the
close reading of a **fragment** can both ground world history in real evidence and
illuminate large-scale connections, because the small case is chosen precisely
where it opens onto the general.

## The Demonstration (an Enacted Method)

Rather than argue abstractly, Ginzburg *performs* microhistory through a case
study: tracing, from tiny textual clues (shared chronological "fingerprints",
borrowed metaphors, a translation on Marx's desk), the transmission of a single
idea — that **"the world of civil society has certainly been made by men"** — from
Giambattista **Vico** (*Scienza nuova*, via the principle *verum et factum
convertuntur*, what is true and what is made converge) back to **Hobbes**, forward
through **Michelet's** translation to **Marx** (the famous Darwin–Vico footnote in
*Capital*: "human history differs from natural history in this, that we have made
the former") and on to Labriola, Sorel, and Trotsky. The minute philological
detective work reconstructs a vast, genuinely transnational intellectual web — the
modern conviction that humans *make their own history* — thereby showing the
micro-scale as a route *into*, not away from, world-historical questions.

## Significance and Contestation

Placed as a closing "questions of method" chapter in [[bentley-cwh-v6-2015|CWH VI]]
(alongside [[global-historiography|Subrahmanyam on early modern historiography]]),
the essay defends the **complementarity of scales** against a global history that
would dissolve into thin generalization. It is contested: critics ask whether the
exceptional, intensively documented case can be representative, and whether
microhistory "scales up" without losing its evidentiary rigor (a debate
Francesca Trivellato has framed as "Is there a future for Italian microhistory in
the age of global history?"). Ginzburg's answer is that the tension between the
fragment and the whole is itself productive — the discipline's strength, not its
weakness.
