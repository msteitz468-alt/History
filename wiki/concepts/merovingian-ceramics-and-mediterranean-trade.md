# Merovingian Ceramics and Mediterranean Trade

**Pottery as a measuring stick for the slow death of the Mediterranean trade system — a "real but earlier"
Pirenne horizon.** Michel Bonifay and Dominique Pieri's chapter (Oxford Handbook, Ch. 37) tracks two
commodity-bearing ceramic classes — African Red Slip (ARS) tableware and transport amphorae — to map the
chronological arc of Mediterranean commerce into Frankish Gaul, providing the quantitative backbone of the
Pirenne debate.

## Definition and Scope

ARS (Tunisian/Tripolitanian fine tableware) and late-antique amphorae (carrying wine from Gaza and the
Aegean, North African oil, and *garum*) are closely datable, so their presence and absence proxy trade
volumes. **Marseille** was "the primary Mediterranean gateway for imports into Frankish Gaul" — Gregory of
Tours notes Syrian merchants there (see [[migrants-and-minorities-in-merovingian-gaul]]).

## A Three-Phase Chronology

- **c. 450–550 (peak):** substantial ARS and amphora imports reach southern Gaul (Marseille, Arles,
  Narbonne, Toulouse), extending the late-Roman system.
- **c. 550–650 (decline):** imports continue but fall sharply; local imitations of ARS appear; regional
  divergence sharpens — Provence and Septimania keep contacts, northern Gaul sees "near-total absence."
- **c. 650–700 (near-cessation):** Mediterranean imports effectively cease; the disappearance of amphorae
  implies the collapse of "an entire food-commodity trade system."

## The Pirenne Assessment

Bonifay and Pieri engage Pirenne directly: the *thesis* that Islamic expansion disrupted Mediterranean
trade "has ceramic support, but Pirenne's date is slightly early and his geography too general." The
disruption is "visible primarily in southern Gaul," while the northern economy was "largely already running
on internal exchange circuits" (see [[merovingian-economy]]). Decline was gradual, not catastrophic.
Importantly, local production (Argonne, Mayen, and cooking wares) "vastly outnumber[s] fine imports even at
peak import periods" — Mediterranean fine ware was always a small fraction of ceramic use.

## Historiography

The chapter is "a landmark synthesis of archaeometric and typological evidence" (the Bonifay–Pieri ARS
sequence is the standard reference). The ceramic data substantially resolves the Pirenne question:
Mediterranean contacts persisted into the seventh century for the south but not the north, and ebbed
gradually — a finding that complements Esders's case for sustained *diplomatic* connectivity (see
[[merovingian-byzantine-relations]]) and Strothmann's gold-to-silver shift (see [[merovingian-coinage]]).
