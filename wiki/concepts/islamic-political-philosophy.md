Islamic political philosophy (*falsafa al-madaniyya*) is the tradition of systematic reflection on the best city, the virtuous regime, and the nature of political rulership developed by the Arabic-speaking Hellenizing philosophers (*falasifa*) from the 9th through the 13th centuries CE. The tradition is founded by [[al-farabi]] and extended by [[ibn-sina]] (Avicenna) and [[ibn-rushd]] (Averroes). Its defining feature — which separates it from both [[kalam]] (Islamic theological politics) and from pure jurisprudence — is the integration of ethical formation (*soulcraft*) with political organization (*statecraft*): the virtuous city is the city whose inhabitants' souls are ordered toward true human happiness.

## Definition and Scope

As Ibn Khaldun summarized the philosophers' conception of the political regime, its purpose is "what is incumbent upon each of the inhabitants of the social organization with respect to his soul and moral habits so that they may entirely dispense with judges. They call the social organization that obtains what is required 'the virtuous city' and the rules observed with respect to that 'the political regime.'" This is explicitly distinguished from the jurists' and theologians' view of politics as the governance of a community through religious statutes. The philosophers were interested in the underlying philosophical conditions of human flourishing within community.

The tradition is self-consciously Platonic and Aristotelian but adapts Greek sources to an Islamic environment in which the prophet-legislator, rather than the philosopher-king, is the paradigm of highest human authority.

## Al-Farabi: Founder and Framework

Al-Farabi (870–950) is universally recognized as the founder of political philosophy within the medieval Islamic tradition. His central texts are the *Book of the Political Regime* (*Kitab al-siyasa al-madaniyya*), the *Selected Aphorisms* (*Fusul muntaza'a*), the *Attainment of Happiness* (*Tahsil al-sa'ada*), and his commentaries on Plato's *Republic* and *Laws*. In the *Selected Aphorisms*, each moral discussion is embedded within political context: discussions of the soul precede and follow analyses of the body politic, the domestic economy, the king-in-truth, and the virtuous city. Al-Farabi's governing insight is that ethical teaching is first and foremost a political undertaking.

Al-Farabi's central political-philosophical move is to reconcile Plato and Aristotle: "the distinctions scholars habitually draw between Plato and Aristotle are precisely the ones al-Farabi seems to delight in collapsing, overlooking, or simply obfuscating." Both, in his reading, see citizen virtue as the primary concern of the lawgiver. The virtuous city is one whose ruler — morally and intellectually perfected — orders the souls of citizens toward genuine happiness (*sa'ada*), which ultimately consists in intellectual perfection and proximity to the separate intelligences.

**The prophet-philosopher synthesis.** Al-Farabi's most distinctive and influential move is the identification of the philosophical ruler with the prophet-legislator. The philosopher, possessing both theoretical knowledge of the truth and a perfected imagination, can represent those truths to ordinary people in symbolic form. "In everything of which philosophy gives an account based on intellectual perception, religion gives an account based on imagination." Philosophy is prior to religion in time; religion is an imitation of philosophy. This implies that the true lawgiver is the philosopher who founds a religion by translating philosophical truth into symbolic-imaginative form accessible to non-philosophers. The prophet's imaginative faculty receives an "overflow" of intelligibles from the agent intellect and communicates them as revelation.

**Virtuous and defective cities.** Al-Farabi distinguishes the virtuous city from several types of defective city: the ignorant city (which pursues wealth, pleasure, or honor rather than true happiness); the immoral city (which knows the good but does not pursue it); and the erring city (whose ruler falsely claims to know the good). His treatment draws directly on Plato's classification of regime types in the *Republic*.

## Avicenna's Contribution

[[ibn-sina]] follows Al-Farabi's framework in connecting the philosopher-prophet to the political community but concentrates more on the metaphysical grounding of prophethood — specifically, the role of the agent intellect in transmitting intelligibles to the prophet's powerful imagination. Avicenna's political philosophy is embedded within his broader metaphysics and psychology rather than treated as a distinct discipline. He subordinates the practical to the theoretical more than Al-Farabi does, limiting his ethical teaching more to the individual.

## Averroes: Platonic Politics

[[ibn-rushd]] (Averroes, d. 1198) wrote a *Commentary on Plato's Republic* — the only Arabic philosophical commentary on the text — because Aristotle's Politics had not been translated into Arabic. Averroes endorses Platonic politics more directly than Al-Farabi, criticizing democracy for "the emphasis it places on the private and its inability to order the desires of the citizens." He identifies the best regime as "the city whose opinions and actions are in accordance with what the theoretical sciences prescribe," and argues that sound practice requires sound theory: the natural hierarchy of the virtues — practical arts serving moral virtues serving theoretical virtues — must be reflected in the regime's organization. Character formation takes precedence over institutional form; what matters is not the type of regime but whether the ruler possesses the moral and intellectual qualities for rulership.

## Al-Kindi and al-Razi: Individual-Focused Ethics

Al-Kindi and al-Razi offer ethical philosophy that is more individualistic and less politically engaged than the Al-Farabian tradition. Al-Kindi's ethical treatise (*On Dispelling Sorrows*) focuses on Stoic-Socratic detachment from the passions. Al-Razi's *Book of the Philosophic Life* takes Socrates as a moral model but measures the value of the philosophic life by its results for human well-being generally — a quantitative, consequentialist measure that avoids the Al-Farabian subordination of the individual to the political community. Both al-Kindi and al-Razi "subordinate the practical to the theoretical" in a way that limits their ethical teaching to the individual.

## Ibn Miskawayh and the Ethical Tradition

Ibn Miskawayh (d. 1030) and Yahya ibn 'Adi (d. 974), both students in the Al-Farabian orbit, wrote works entitled *Tahdhib al-akhlaq* (Refinement of Character), which apply Aristotelian virtue ethics to the individual. Miskawayh's work is the most important Arabic text in the Aristotelian ethical tradition proper, preceding the sustained political context of Al-Farabi's mature works.

## Relation to Islamic Law

The philosophers' political philosophy stands in deliberate tension with jurisprudential (*fiqh*) approaches to governance. The jurists legislate for a community through revealed law (*sharia*); the philosophers seek to understand the conditions under which any community can achieve human happiness — conditions that, for Al-Farabi, the prophet-legislator uniquely fulfills. This produces an uneasy relation: the philosophers respect Islamic law as the symbolic embodiment of philosophical truth, while implying that philosophy provides a deeper understanding of why that law is good. This position was deeply suspicious to orthodox religious scholars.

## Contested Questions

The most debated issue in secondary scholarship is whether Al-Farabi's identification of philosophy with prophecy and the philosopher with the lawgiver represents his sincere position or a rhetorical camouflage for a more radical, purely philosophical teaching. Muhsin Mahdi and Leo Strauss argued that Al-Farabi conceals an esoteric philosophical teaching behind exoteric accommodation to Islam; others (including the chapter's author, Charles Butterworth) read the identification of philosophy and prophecy as genuine rather than merely strategic. This bears on whether Islamic political philosophy is fundamentally a "philosophy of religion" or a "political science" that subsumes religion.

## Historiography

The chapter in Adamson and Taylor's *Cambridge Companion to Arabic Philosophy* (2005), written by Charles Butterworth, provides the most accessible English-language overview. Muhsin Mahdi's *Al-Farabi and the Foundation of Islamic Political Philosophy* (2001) is the major monograph. The political philosophy tradition is well-attested in surviving texts: Al-Farabi's political works are available in translation, Averroes' *Commentary on the Republic* has been edited and translated. Avicenna's political philosophy must be reconstructed from scattered passages across his encyclopedic works. The tradition is weak for post-Averroes developments in the Eastern Islamic world, where political philosophy was largely absorbed into [[philosophical-sufism]] and [[post-avicennan-philosophy]].
