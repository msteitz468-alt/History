## Definition and Scope

"Sufi metaphysics" or "theosophical Sufism" designates that strand of Islamic mysticism which moved beyond the earlier Sufi concern with stages, states, and the purgation of the soul toward a systematic philosophical account of the structure of reality: the nature of God, the relation between divine essence and divine attributes, the mechanism of creation, the ontological status of human beings and the cosmos, and the logic of the soul's return to its source. The term "theosophy" (*ḥikma ilāhiyya*) is preferred in the Islamic context to "philosophy" (*falsafa*) precisely because it insists on the union of intellectual knowledge with mystical illumination — the combination of *ḥikmat-i yūnānī* (Greek rational philosophy) with *ḥikmat-i yamanī* (Yemenite/intuitive wisdom).

This tradition must be distinguished from:
- **Classical kalam** (speculative theology), which addressed similar questions but from a commitment to scriptural categories and rational argument without mystical experience as an epistemological foundation
- **Earlier Sufi ethics and psychology** (represented by figures like Qushayrī and Ghazzālī), which systematized the inner life without constructing a full ontology
- **Falsafa** (Islamic Aristotelianism), which shared the Neoplatonic vocabulary but rejected the primacy of mystical experience as a path to knowledge

## Origins and Intellectual Sources

The theosophical tradition drew on multiple streams:

**Neoplatonism**: The emanationist metaphysics of Plotinus, transmitted through Arabic pseudo-Aristotelian texts (Theology of Aristotle, Liber de Causis), provided the conceptual scaffolding of hierarchical emanation from a single divine source and the soul's itinerary of return.

**Avicennan philosophy**: Ibn Sīnā's (Avicenna's) synthesis of Aristotelianism and Neoplatonism — particularly his "floating man" thought experiment and his concept of the active intellect — provided key building blocks. Suhrawardī both engaged with and critiqued peripatetic philosophy, while Ibn ʿArabī inherited Avicennan prophetology and cosmology.

**Iranian light mysticism**: Zoroastrian angelology, the Iranian cosmological tradition of light and darkness, and the Persian concept of divine *xvarnah* (luminous glory) fed into Suhrawardī's philosophy of illumination. Suhrawardī explicitly traced his intellectual lineage through pre-Islamic Persian priest-kings alongside Hermetic and Platonic traditions.

**Quranic and Prophetic sources**: The *ḥadīth* of the "hidden treasure" ("I was a hidden treasure and wanted to be known, so I created the world") is the foundational Quranic-adjacent text for Ibn ʿArabī's account of creation. The *nafas al-Raḥmān* (breath of the Merciful), the divine names of the Quran, and the prophetic traditions about Muḥammad as light all enter the theosophical synthesis.

**Hermetic tradition**: Suhrawardī derived his claimed spiritual authority from Hermes (identified with Quranic Idrīs), through whom a stream of wisdom passed to the Greek philosophers and thence to the Sufis.

## Major Figures and Their Contributions

### Suhrawardī al-Maqtūl (1153–1191): The Philosophy of Illumination

[[suhrawardi-yahya|Shihābuddīn Suhrawardī]], the *shaykh al-ishrāq* ("master of illumination"), executed in Aleppo at age thirty-eight by order of Saladin's son after pressure from orthodox jurists, founded what he called *ḥikmat al-ishrāq* — the philosophy of illumination. His key move was the identification of existence (*wujūd*) with light (*nūr*): "What is conceived metaphysically as existence (*wujūd*) coincides with what is grasped in terms of the root experience as Light (*nūr*). In this context existence is light."

The Absolute is pure light; beings are ordered according to the degree to which they are illuminated or veiled. A detailed angelology mediates the descent of light through longitudinal and latitudinal orders; Gabriel is the archetype of humanity (*rabb an-nawʿ al-insānī*), while each soul also has a personal guardian angel — its "twin" or heavenly double, from which it was separated upon entering the body. Life in the material world is thus existence in the *ghurbat al-gharbiyya* ("western exile"), the realm of darkness and matter, while the goal is return to the *sharq* (Orient of pure light).

Suhrawardī's system incorporated Zoroastrian divine names for angels, Platonic forms reinterpreted as luminous archetypes, and an eschatology in which the soul's degree of illumination at death determines its post-mortem condition. His symbolic narratives — *ʿAql-i Surkh* ("The Red Intellect"), *Awāz-i Par-i Jibrīl* ("The Sound of Gabriel's Wing"), *Ṣafīr-i Sīmurgh* ("The Whistling of the Sīmurgh") — present this angelology and soul-journey in mythic-narrative form.

### Ibn ʿArabī (1165–1240): The Unity of Being

[[ibn-arabi|Muḥyuʾddīn Ibn ʿArabī]], *ash-shaykh al-akbar* ("the greatest master"), born in Murcia, Spain, educated partly by women saints, traveled through the Islamic world and settled in Damascus where he died. He produced an enormous corpus — *Al-Futūḥāt al-Makkiyya* (Meccan Revelations, 560 chapters) and *Fuṣūṣ al-Ḥikam* (Bezels of Wisdom, 29 chapters) being the most celebrated — which constitutes the most comprehensive theosophical system in Islamic history.

Ibn ʿArabī's doctrine of [[wahdat-al-wujud|waḥdat al-wujūd]] (Unity of Being) holds that God alone truly "is"; creation consists of the self-manifestation (*tajallī*) of the divine names in the "fixed archetypes" (*al-aʿyān al-thābita*). The divine essence remains unknowable; what is knowable and experienced is the names and attributes. The Perfect Man ([[al-insan-al-kamil]]) is both the microcosm of all divine names and the necessary medium through which God is known and manifested — "the pupil in the eye of humanity." Muḥammad as the *Ḥaqīqa Muḥammadiyya* (Muḥammadan Reality) is the total theophany of all names and the prototype of the universe.

### Ṣadruddīn Qōnawī (d. 1274)

Ibn ʿArabī's foremost interpreter, encountered in Konya (Anatolia) and the primary transmitter of his ideas to the Persian-Turkish mystical milieu. His work ensured that Ibn ʿArabī's terminology became standard in post-thirteenth century Sufism even among those who did not fully accept his metaphysics.

### Maḥmūd Shabistarī (d. after 1320): *Gulshan-i Rāz*

[[shabistari-mahmud|Shabistarī]], writing near Tabriz in 1311, composed the *Gulshan-i Rāz* ("Rose Garden of Mystery"), a Persian poem in reply to eighteen questions on Sufi terminology and metaphysics. Schimmel calls it "the handiest introduction to the thought of post-Ibn ʿArabī Sufism." Shabistarī describes the Perfect Man as he who completes the twofold arc — twenty steps of descent from the First Intelligence to earth, then twenty steps of the arc of ascent back to divine unity — a Neoplatonic schema adapted through the theosophical tradition and subsequently embedded in the teachings of the Bektashi and other orders. Lāhījī's fifteenth-century commentary on the *Gulshan-i Rāz* became the standard study text for several Iranian orders. Muḥammad Iqbal responded to Shabistarī's questions with his *Gulshan-i Rāz-i Jadīd* (1927).

### ʿAbd al-Karīm Jīlī (d. after 1408): *Al-Insān al-Kāmil*

A descendant of the founder of the Qādiriyya order, Jīlī systematized Ibn ʿArabī's thought while developing it in some new directions. His *Al-Insān al-Kāmil* ("The Perfect Man") analyzed the levels of divine manifestation (*tajallī*): from essence to attributes, actions, similitude, and sense-color vision. In his formulation, *tawḥīd* at its highest is not a unity of will but "the extinction of the ignorance of our essential and unmovable identity with the only Real" — a decisive shift toward gnosis over moral striving. He nonetheless maintained that "the Lord remains Lord and the slave remains slave," preserving a formal distinction even within the framework of ontological unity.

## Core Doctrines of the School

**Emanation and return**: Following Neoplatonic schemas, existence flows from the One through descending degrees of being and must return to its source. The twenty-step arc of descent and the twenty-step arc of ascent model this double movement.

**The divine names as creative archetypes**: The Quranic divine names are not merely epithets but ontological principles — each is the *rabb* (Lord) of a class of beings, while those beings are its *marbūb*. The structure of creation reflects the structure of the divine names.

**The imaginal world** (*ʿālam al-mithāl*): Between the purely spiritual and the purely material planes lies a world of images — not illusory but genuinely real, the site where spiritual forms take shape. This doctrine, elaborated by Ibn ʿArabī and later the Safavid philosopher Mullā Ṣadrā, became central to the philosophy of [[illuminationism]] in its later development.

**The Perfect Man** ([[al-insan-al-kamil]]): The human being is potentially the microcosm of all divine names; the Prophet, the saints, and ultimately every individual who realizes their full human potential stand at the axis of cosmic manifestation.

**Theophany over pantheism**: The school insists — against both orthodox critics and some Western interpreters — that creatures are not identical with God but are theophanies (*tajalliyāt*): God manifested through, not equated with, the created order.

## Contestation and Orthodox Critique

The theosophical school generated sustained opposition within Islam from its beginnings. Ibn Taymiyya (d. 1328) produced the most important medieval Hanbalite critique, arguing that waḥdat al-wujūd dissolved the distinction between Creator and creature and undermined moral accountability. The popular condensation *hama ūst* ("everything is He") — which circulated widely in Persian and Turkish poetry — seemed to many orthodox observers to license antinomianism and confuse the worshipper with the worshipped.

In South Asia, the Naqshbandī reformer Aḥmad Sirhindī (d. 1624) developed the counter-doctrine of *waḥdat al-shuhūd* (unity of witnessing), arguing that the mystic's experience of unity is a subjective epistemic state rather than an objective metaphysical fact. Even Sirhindī, however, acknowledged his debt to Ibn ʿArabī: "We latecomers have also benefited from the blessings of that great man."

## Legacy and Diffusion

After the Mongol conquests of the thirteenth century — which paradoxically catalyzed rather than suppressed mystical production — theosophical Sufism became the dominant intellectual framework of Islamic mysticism. By the fourteenth century, even mystics who rejected Ibn ʿArabī's metaphysics employed his terminology. The school's influence spread:
- **Iran**: Through the Safavid philosopher [[heirs-of-mulla-sadra|Mullā Ṣadrā]] (d. 1640), who incorporated Suhrawardian and Ibn ʿArabian elements into a new synthesis (*al-ḥikmat al-mutaʿāliya*, "transcendent wisdom")
- **Anatolia/Turkey**: Through Qōnawī's school; Yūnus Emre's first Turkish mystical poetry reflects this milieu
- **India**: Through emigrants from Iran and through the Chishti, Suhrawardi, and Qadiri orders; the *Gulshan-i Rāz* served as a standard text for Iranian dervish orders into the modern period

## Historiography

Western scholarship on theosophical Sufism has been dominated by two figures: Henry Corbin, whose phenomenological approach privileged the "imaginal world" and "creative imagination" as keys to the tradition, and Seyyed H. Nasr, who interpreted it within a perennialist framework emphasizing the tradition's non-pantheistic character. A. A. Affifi's 1936 Cambridge dissertation offered the first sustained academic analysis of Ibn ʿArabī and argued for pantheism — a reading that later scholarship has largely revised without fully abandoning. William Chittick's philological work has provided the most detailed lexical mapping of Ibn ʿArabī's actual terminology.
