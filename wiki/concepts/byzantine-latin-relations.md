# Byzantine-Latin Relations and Perceptions

## Definition and Scope

How Byzantine writers understood, named, and related to the peoples of the Latin West — from rough indifference before 1000, through the mixed experience of the First Crusade, to the permanent transformation brought by the Fourth Crusade (1204). Alexander Kazhdan's essay challenges the standard narrative of unrelieved Byzantine-Latin hostility, showing that the relationship was far more complex: economic integration, inter-marriage, military service, and individual admiration coexisted with cultural disdain and eventual catastrophe.

This page covers **Byzantine literary perception** and **the social reality of Latins in Byzantine society** (11th–12th centuries). Byzantine religious polemic against the Latin church is a separate tradition not covered here. For Islamic and Armenian perceptions of the same period see [[concepts/byzantine-perception-of-islam]] and [[concepts/armenian-sources-on-crusades]].

## Key Sources

### The Primary Byzantine Sources

**Anna Komnene, *Alexiad*** (written c. 1140s): The most important Byzantine account of the First Crusade and of Byzantine-Latin relations under her father Alexios I. Anna used the noun *Latinos* 97 times, *Keltos* 176 times (used interchangeably with *Latinos*), and *Frankos* 20 times. She distinguished the generic "Latins" (dangerous aliens) from the more specific "Franks" (predominantly Normans, whom she regarded as partially "ours"). Her account is the primary source for Byzantine perceptions of the Crusade leadership, especially Bohemond.

**Niketas Choniates, *Chronike diegesis*** (covering events to after 1204): Used *Latinos* as noun and adjective 127 times. His stereotype of the Latin closely mirrors his stereotype of the "barbarian" — cruel, arrogant, greedy, treacherous, stupid, unbridled in speech. Yet Choniates is not simply anti-Latin: he praises Frederick Barbarossa in panegyrical terms, admires Conrad of Montferrat's handsomeness and reason, and explicitly condemns Byzantine undiscriminating animosity toward Westerners as "foolishness."

**John Kinnamos**: Prefers specific (sometimes confusing) ethnonyms over the generic "Latin"; more unambiguously anti-Crusader than Choniates, entirely blaming Crusaders for conflicts during the Second Crusade while justifying every Byzantine position.

**Michael Attaleiates** (late 11th century): Occasionally uses *Latinos*, but with confused ethnic identity; more commonly speaks of "Franks" or "Normans."

### Byzantine Chrysobulls
Imperial edicts exempting monasteries from billeting foreign soldiers — itemizing specific ethnonyms (Varangians, Rus', Franks, Germans, English, Alans) — provide evidence for which groups were present in significant numbers at various dates.

## Major Themes

### The Development of the Term "Latinoi"

Before the 11th century, Byzantine writers did not use *Latinoi* as a generic appellation for Westerners. Theophanes (c. 800), Patriarch Nikephoros, pseudo-Genesios, the Continuators of Theophanes, and Constantine Porphyrogennetos all knew numerous specific western peoples (Franks, Italians, Germans, etc.) but not a generic "Latin" unity. The saint's lives of South Italy (10th–11th centuries) use "Latins" only to denote the Latin *language* in contrast to Greek.

The term *Latinos* as a generic appellation for Westerners first appears in a patriarchal decision of 1054. After the First Crusade the adoption was rapid: Anna Komnene used it 97 times. By the 12th century, Byzantine intellectuals firmly conceived the West as a unified entity — "Latin peoples" and "Latin habits" — for the first time.

This shift parallels the emergence of a unified "Frank" category: originally applied to Germanic tribes of ancient Gallia, then to the population of Gallia generally, and eventually (by Skylitzes and Anna Komnene) primarily to the Normans of South Italy — reflecting the historical fact of their expansion southward.

### Stages of Perception

Kazhdan identifies an evolving trajectory (though he resists a rigid schematization):

1. **Before the 11th century**: West perceived as disaggregated — separate peoples without unity. No concept of "Latins." Normans and other Westerners hired as mercenary commanders, some achieving very high rank (Herve Frankopoulos reached *magistros*; Robert Crepin commanded the Byzantine right wing against rebels; Roussel de Bailleul became powerful enough to proclaim a rival emperor in 1074).

2. **Alexios I's reign (1081–1118)**: Normans still dominant as mercenaries, but Alexios systematically tamed them — marrying them into the Komnenian clan, granting them high titles, but reducing their independent military power. The First Crusade introduced a new mass of Latins; Anna Komnene's *Alexiad* crystallizes the first systematic Byzantine literary typology of "the Latin." The relationship is ambivalent: Alexios extracted oaths and used the Crusaders to recover western Anatolia, but his refusal to support the siege of Antioch and Bohemond's seizure of the city sowed permanent distrust.

3. **Manuel I's reign (1143–1180)**: Described by Western sources as a "Latinophile" (William of Tyre: Manuel "scorned the effeminate Greculi and trusted only the Latins"). Prosopographical evidence is more ambiguous: the highest military commands went to the Komnenian clan, not to Westerners; Latins under Manuel were primarily merchants (up to 60,000 Latins in Constantinople per Eustathios of Thessalonike), diplomats, and interpreters rather than military commanders. The Norman spirit of independence had been suppressed; later in Manuel's reign some Westerners returned to high positions.

4. **The Angeloi dynasty (1185–1203)**: Partial reversion to the earlier pattern, with mercenaries recovering some positions lost under the Komnenoi.

5. **The Fourth Crusade (1204) and aftermath**: Permanent transformation. Choniates: "Between us and them lies an open gap, and we are separated in our views and diametrically opposed." The sack of Constantinople ended any possibility of restoring the pre-1204 ambivalence.

### Anna Komnene's *Alexiad* as Historical Source

Written in the 1140s by Alexios I's daughter, the *Alexiad* is both a biography of her father and the most detailed Byzantine narrative of the First Crusade. As a source it is:
- **Indispensable**: no other contemporary Byzantine account of the First Crusade survives at comparable length and detail.
- **Deeply partial**: Anna was defending her father's legacy (she had supported a failed coup attempt to give him the throne over his younger brother John II). She presents Alexios as consistently wise, the Crusaders as at best useful and at worst threatening.
- **Technically sophisticated**: Anna was a genuine scholar in the classical tradition; her Attic prose is among the finest Byzantine historical writing.
- **Distinctive in its Latin terminology**: Her massive use of *Keltos/Keltikos* (176 instances) as a synonym for *Latinos* reflects her literary archaism — she preferred Greek classical ethnonyms. This makes her usage analytically complex.

Kazhdan notes that in Anna's terminology, "Franks" (predominantly Normans) are treated as partially "ours" — some Frankish commanders followed Alexios loyally — while "Latins" generically are dangerous aliens.

### Latins in Byzantine Society

The reality of Western presence in Byzantium complicates any simple hostility narrative:

**Military commanders** (mid-11th to early 12th centuries):
- Herve Frankopoulos: reached *magistros*, commanded Frankish contingents, held an estate in the Armeniakon theme.
- Robert Crepin (*Krispinos*): commanded Byzantine right wing under Romanos IV; later revolted, was exiled, his soldiers pillaged Mesopotamia in revenge.
- Roussel de Bailleul: commanded a 400-man Frankish contingent; became independent, backing the proclamation of a rival emperor in 1074.
- Roger (son of Dagobert): Norman commander elevated by Alexios I; married into the Dalassenoi (Komnenoi-related) family; founder of the Norman-Byzantine Rogerioi lineage.
- Peter Aliphas: signed the 1108 treaty of Devol; founder of the Petraliphai family (which produced commanders and sebastokratores into the 13th century).

**Intermarriage and Hellenization**: Several Norman families became fully integrated Byzantine dynasties — the Rogerioi, Petraliphai, Raoul lineages — preserving "barbaric" surnames while operating as Byzantine aristocrats.

**Merchants and residents**: By Manuel I's reign the Latin presence was primarily commercial. Eustathios of Thessalonike estimated 60,000 Latins in Constantinople; Bar Hebraeus counted 30,000 "Frankish" merchants (though he added they were not conspicuous given the city's size); more than 10,000 Venetians were arrested in 1172. The Pisans and Genoese maintained smaller resident communities.

**The 1182 massacre of Latins**: The attack on the Latin quarter of Constantinople under Andronikos I — described from the Arab side in Ibn Jubayr's (confused) account — marks the violent rupture of the commercial integration that had built up under the Komnenoi.

### The Fourth Crusade as Watershed

Kazhdan and the volume as a whole treat 1204 as the moment that permanently restructured the perception of the relationship. Choniates, writing with the sack fresh in memory, hardened the literary stereotype into something closer to outright hostility. Yet even Choniates preserved individual portraits of admirable Latins (Frederick Barbarossa, Conrad of Montferrat, the Venetian merchant Dominicus who saved Choniates' family), and explicitly criticized Byzantine undiscriminating hatred of all Westerners.

## Scholarly Significance

Kazhdan's contribution is methodological: against the traditional narrative of Byzantine-Latin contrast and hostility (rooted partly in 19th-century confessional historiography), he insists on prosopographical evidence alongside literary stereotyping. The literary sources say "hostile"; the chrysobulls, seals, and biographical data say "complicated integration." The "schism" of 1054 was not, he argues, an actual separation of churches and cultures at the time — that narrative was retrospectively constructed.

The contested element: whether the traditional binary (Catholicism vs. Orthodoxy; feudalism vs. Byzantine "totalitarian" regime; oral vs. literate culture) reflects medieval reality or is a confessional-historiographical artifact. Kazhdan leans toward the latter.
