# Phalanx

A closed tactical formation of heavily armed infantry (hoplites) whose power derives not primarily from the weapons of the front rank but from the physical and moral forward pressure exerted by the rearward ranks. The formation presupposes the most completely armed warriors possible throughout all ranks.

## Definition and Mechanics (per Delbrück range)

Rearmost ranks almost never arrive at the point of using their weapons. Nevertheless, complete protective armament is required for all. An unarmored person cannot really fight an armored one. Forming several ranks of unarmored men behind would be little more than pretense: the foremost ranks, realizing they could expect no true support, would lose drive. If the armored enemy penetrated at any point, the unarmored rear would give ground at once and the flight would pull the entire army back.

Lightly armed or partially armed men can perform secondary services (helping friendly wounded, killing or taking prisoner enemy wounded, harassing with stones and javelins on the flanks), but the phalanx as such requires fully armed warriors throughout.

The first rank is of utmost importance. Tyrtaeus repeatedly praises the men "among those fighting in front." Later theorists recommend placing the most reliable men in the first and last ranks to hold the whole together. In Sparta the Spartiates (professional warriors) predominantly formed the first ranks over the Perioeci.

## Flank Vulnerability

The relatively few warriors of the outside columns could hardly hold off an attack on the flank. While they stopped and wheeled, they would either force the entire phalanx to stop (preventing rear ranks from pressing forward) or the phalanx would break apart and be rolled up. Cavalry, even in small numbers, is particularly dangerous: the strength of the phalanx is immediately broken because it can no longer move forward without falling into disorder.

Historical illustration: battle at Corcyra 373 BCE (Xenophon, *Hellenica* 6.2.21). Spartans attempted to wheel the last files into a new front with refused flanks; the enemy drove in more strongly, the wheeling movement could not be carried out, and column after column took to flight.

## Origin

The Dorians who subjugated part of the Peloponnese were the first not only to recognize the value of the closely knit grouping but to bring it into logical and effective practice. Legendary accounts:

- Pausanias (4.8.11) on the Messenian wars: Lacedaemonians did not pursue because it was more important to hold their orderly formation than to kill one or another of the fleeing enemy. "It was also an ancient custom with them to pursue somewhat slowly, keeping it in mind to preserve their formation rather than killing those who fled before them."

- Polyaenus (1.10): When the Heraclidae were surprised by enemies in the middle of sacrifices, they ordered pipers to march forward. The hoplites strode forward in time with the melody and rhythm, formed their battle ranks solidly, and were victorious. This taught the Lacedaemonians always to have pipers lead them into battle; their god promised they would conquer as long as they fought accompanied by pipers.

The piper symbolizes tactical formation itself: a group of heroes engaged in individual combat does not march in step.

## Distinction from Missile Combat

Missile weapons (bow, sling, javelin) played only a very small role with the hoplite phalanx. When deployed forward they had to disappear before the clash; pushing back through the phalanx caused disorder. Firing from behind in an arching trajectory without real aiming was ineffective, especially when the phalanx itself was advancing. Archers, slingers, and javelin-throwers formed an auxiliary arm only. The real combat force of the Greeks in the Persian Wars consisted of hoplites.

## Classical mechanics (Wheeler / Hunt, CHGRW)

[[sabin-van-wees-whitby-cambridge-history-greek-roman-warfare-2007|CHGRW]] (Wheeler I.7;
Hunt I.5) defines the classical phalanx for tactical discussion as a **deep heavy-infantry
formation** (usually square/rectangular) whose character derives from **mass cohesion**,
not mere linear deployment. Late technical vocabulary betrays developmental character:
“hoplite” is not securely attested until late sixth/early fifth century though armour
appears c.725; Thucydides does not use “phalanx” as a technical formation term (prefers
*taxis*/*parataxis*). Core literary bases for mechanics are Thucydides’ Mantinea (418)
and Polybius’ phalanx–legion comparison (Wheeler flags Polybius as slanted to discourage
Greek resistance to Rome).

**Geographic and chronological unevenness.** The heavy-infantry phalanx did not develop
uniformly: not in Thessaly/Thrace (cavalry/light infantry), not in Macedonia before the
early fourth century, little use among Aetolians/Acarnanians; Arcadia exported
heavy-infantry mercenaries. Mountainous Greece made heavy infantry its national tactic
despite scarce unbroken plains — Epaminondas called Boeotian plains the “dance floor of
Ares.” Creation of **depth** likely belongs to the sixth century (Persian-War armies
already customarily deep); Chigi vase (c.640) and Tyrtaeus show organized linear clash /
mutual support before proven deep phalanx. Evolution from fluid small-band combat to
closely ordered mass progressed over the seventh–sixth centuries. See
[[hoplite-revolution-debate]].

**Organization.** Continuity of front (shields/spears) plus depth for weight/momentum
(*syntaxis*, Aristotle). Loss of *aspis* brought shame (*rhipsaspia* = *lipotaxia*
prosecutable at Athens) because the whole line’s safety depended on the shield. Sub-units:
Spartan *morai*/*lochoi*; Athenian *taxeis*/*lochoi*. File (*stichos*) is the building
block: *protostatēs* leads, *ouragos* at rear (already classical, not only Macedonian).
Favoured classical depth **eight** (also 4, 10, 12, 16; Thebans 25 and 50); Greek *dory*
~8 ft means only ranks 1–2 (possibly 1–3) actually fought; depth beyond eight yields
diminishing shock, beyond sixteen none. Hellenistic tacticians give combat intervals of
three feet (*pyknōsis*); *aspis* breadth ~3 ft. Allied battle lines (Mantinea 418; Nemea
394) were not continuous masses — small gaps between *polis* contingents made whole-line
coordination impossible.

**Charge and contact.** Concave shield design encouraged **rightward drift** as each man
sought his neighbour’s shield (Thuc. 5.71); shield mainly protected the left when
advancing. Wheeler reads the drift as the phalanx charging with a slight veer right
almost in echelon, not individuals independently edging mid-charge. Two phalanxes could
not smash together in a catastrophic crash of the first two ranks; Spartan slow advance
aimed at intimidation via discipline; armies at a run likely slowed before contact;
Coronea’s literal crash was judged unique. Best troops/general on the right + rightward
tendency produced mutual right-wing victories; success permitted wheeling left across the
enemy rear (Agis at Mantinea set a Spartan tactical precedent). Theban deep formations
(Delium 25; Nemea >16; Leuctra ≥50) are misread if explained only as more push — depth
beyond 16 yields no push gain; Epaminondas combined Theban mass with Spartan manoeuvre,
preferred the left, and targeted enemy command. By the 420s **reserves** were well known
(Solygea, Delium, Amphipolis); *orthioi lochoi* columns with gaps suited rough/uphill and
light-infantry fighting (Ten Thousand).

**Othismos.** Metaphorical open-formation school (Fraser, Cawkwell, Krentz, Goldsworthy,
van Wees) vs traditional literal collective push (Woodhouse, Grundy, Cartledge, Hanson,
et al.). Wheeler: many references metaphorical, but some shoving literal; defining battle
as pure shoving match *or* pure individual fighting both go too far. Hellenistic ranks
6–16 pressured the front by body weight (Polybius/tacticians); classical combat is
imagined as front-rank close bunching with constant rear pressure, possibly evolving into
local shoving matches. Probable Polybian maniple depth six is only two less than the most
frequent classical Greek depth of eight — cautioning simple linear-vs-phalanx dichotomies.

**Limits.** The phalanx was chiefly designed for forward movement; flanks and rear
vulnerable if unprotected; it conceals limited combat skills of militia; can deter cavalry
if men hold; effectiveness depends on file cohesion against similar heavy infantry.

## Macedonian and Hellenistic phalanx (Sekunda / Sabin, CHGRW)

Sekunda (CHGRW I.11A) argues Philip II’s 359/8 emergency winter program — helmets,
*peltai*, greaves, and *sarissas* — was inspired by **Iphicratean** re-equipment of
mercenaries (rimless *peltai* “of the same size” as hoplite shields per Aristotle fr. 498;
spears lengthened by half to ~12 ft; longer swords): few modern historians credit
Iphicrates enough as inspiration. Later *sarissas* reached enormous lengths (Polyaenus
16-cubit / 24 ft; Aelian recommends no shorter than 8 cubits / 12 ft). Alexander’s force
differed from Philip’s 359/8 creation: as resources grew, part of the phalanx received
heavier equipment (Alexander Sarcophagus shows infantry with hoplite shields and
cuirasses); **Hypaspists** lighter, mobile link between phalanx and right-wing cavalry.
Infantry architecture: *dekas* expanded from 10 to 16; *lochos* of 512 = 32 files; 9,000
Foot Companions in six territorial *taxeis*; 3,000 Hypaspists in three *chiliarchiai*.
Sekunda against small-shield-only orthodoxy: at least two Macedonian shield sizes (larger
~80 cm deeply concave rimless for heavy “hoplites”; smaller *pelte* for peltasts). Color-
named regiments (Antigonid Chalcaspides / Leucaspides; Seleucid Argyraspides, etc.).

Sabin (CHGRW II.13A) softens Polybius’ harshness on pike-phalanx inflexibility: Eumenes
at Gabiene and Seleucid pikemen at Magnesia formed hollow squares and tried orderly
withdrawal — but Roman multi-line flexibility remained superior. Heavy infantry remained
“queen of battle”; pikes of first five ranks projected, usual depth 16 (24–32 common).
Defensive phalanx with secure flanks almost indefinite; attacking phalanx pushes Romans
back (Cynoscephalae, Pydna) but rarely alone forces flight until javelins, rough terrain,
or flank/rear attack break the pike hedge. Lendon notes many Greeks after early-second-
century Roman victories still held the Macedonian-style phalanx superior — Polybius had
to argue the reverse, attributing contrary views to fortune-explanations of Roman success.

## Hellenistic pike phalanx (Price, OHCW)

**Simon Price** (*Oxford History of the Classical World*, 1986) situates the
post-classical formation in Successor armies: Philip’s reorganization created an improved
heavy-armed infantry (the phalanx) that differed from the old primarily by replacing the
short thrusting spear with a **long pike** — about **5.5 m** in Alexander’s time, about
**6.5 m** a century later — plus important cavalry. Seleucids used military colonies with
service obligation; Ptolemies granted revocable scattered landholdings (dead cavalry
holdings reclaimed for the crown). This is the world of **professional, not citizen,
soldiers**; mercenaries (especially light troops) increased greatly and “rarely betrayed
for gold.” The tactical mass of the pike phalanx is the formation Goldsworthy contrasts
with Rome’s manipular system below.

## Goldsworthy: rigidity against the Roman manipular system, and the limits of Hellenistic "limited war"

[[sources/goldsworthy-roman-warfare-2000|Adrian Goldsworthy]]'s *Roman Warfare* (2000) contrasts the
single-line Hellenistic phalanx described above with the flexible Roman **manipular legion** and its
**triplex acies** (three-line system, with over half the infantry held in reserve): "the manipular
legion was flexible while the phalanx was not, and this proved the decisive factor in a clash between
the two." Where the rigid phalanx had neither reserves nor the command structure to exploit a break in
an enemy line, Roman armies could feed fresh troops into a local gap, restoring breaks in their own line
through the initiative of junior officers rather than central direction. Goldsworthy treats this as
decisive at **Cynoscephalae** (197 BCE) and **Pydna** (168 BCE) — at Cynoscephalae, an accidental,
disorganized encounter battle in which a break in the Roman line was restored by a junior tribune's own
initiative; at Pydna, the Macedonian phalanx outran its own cohesion advancing over rough terrain, and
Roman centurions exploited the resulting gaps by leading small parties to attack the pikemen from the
flanks (the battle nonetheless lasted only about an hour, exceptionally brief for the period). He gives
a similar account of **Magnesia** (190 BCE), where a deep Seleucid phalanx held its center only until
elephant panic and Roman cavalry combined with infantry pressure to dissolve it.

Goldsworthy sets this tactical contrast inside a broader interpretive thesis: Hellenistic
(post-Alexander Successor) warfare was a distinct, **limited-war military culture** — professional,
technically skilled, expensive-to-replace armies that avoided decisive, annihilating battle by design,
and preferred a negotiated settlement (ransom, indemnity, territorial concession) once one side gained
a clear advantage. This made it fundamentally incompatible with Rome's total-victory-only approach,
which never accepted any outcome short of the enemy's destruction or permanent subordination. Goldsworthy
takes this to be the deeper reason Hannibal's tactical triumphs could not translate into strategic victory:
"by his own understanding of war Hannibal won the Second Punic War at Cannae, but the Romans were
following a different set of rules." A phalanx-based army, built to win a Hellenistic-style limited war,
was structurally unsuited to defeating an opponent that refused to recognize a battlefield "win" as
ending the conflict.
