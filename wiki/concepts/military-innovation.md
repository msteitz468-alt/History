# Military Innovation

**Military innovation** is the process by which armed forces develop and adopt new doctrines,
technologies, and organizational forms that change how — and how well — they fight. It is the
central analytical concept of Williamson Murray and Allan Millett's
*[[sources/murray-millett-military-innovation-interwar-1996|Military Innovation in the Interwar
Period]]* (1996), whose comparative method — hold the era (1918–1939) and the conditions
(austerity + rapid technological change) fixed, vary the national institution — produced the
wiki's case studies of [[armored-warfare]], [[amphibious-warfare]], [[carrier-aviation]],
[[close-air-support]], [[radar-and-air-defense]], [[submarine-warfare]], and interwar
[[strategic-bombardment]]. It is the institutional complement to the
[[military-effectiveness|military-effectiveness]] framework and to the
[[revolution-in-military-affairs]] debate.

## The peculiar difficulty of peacetime innovation

Innovation is uniquely hard for a military because, unlike any other organization, it "must
innovate and prepare for a war" that "will occur at some indeterminate point in the future,"
"against an opponent who may not yet be identified," in unpredictable political conditions, and
"in an arena of brutality and violence which one cannot replicate." Michael Howard's image: the
officer's profession "is almost unique in that he may only have to exercise it once in a
lifetime… It is as if a surgeon had to practice throughout his life on dummies for one real
operation." Worse, "disciplined organizations rarely place a high value on new and untried
ideas," and war's fog and friction make it "difficult for military organizations to determine
what has actually happened on the battlefield." The editors stress that innovation is
**nonlinear** — "changes in inputs… do not necessarily yield changes of proportionate magnitude
in outputs" — so the study of it "is more of an art than a science." They deliberately offer "no
grand theory of innovation," following Stephen Rosen's finding that the literature contains no
agreed causal model.

## Changing the *context* of war

Murray's most useful schematic ranks innovations by what they change:

| Level | Change | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Tactics | technical change (equipment) | local |
| Operations | operational change (procedures) | theatre |
| **Strategy/Context** | **contextual change** | **decisive** |

"Military innovations that have the greatest influence are those that **change the context
within which war takes place**." The exemplar is the British air-defense system: by fusing
radar, fighters, and control, Dowding "built an effective air defense system that altered the
entire context within which air forces operated" — making Stanley Baldwin's dictum that "the
bomber will always get through" "no longer valid." Crucially, "while the Germans may have
possessed better equipment and even tactics, the British operated within a broader framework of
contextual change" and so "created a new logic within which the Luftwaffe was incapable of
winning." Most innovation, however, "is evolutionary rather than revolutionary"; revolutionary
innovation "is the exception."

## Two modes: revolutionary vs. evolutionary

- **Revolutionary innovation** is "largely… a phenomenon of top-down leadership… well-informed
  about the technical as well as conceptual aspects." Its one clear case in the volume is
  Dowding. But top-down direction is double-edged — French generals used the same tight control
  to enforce the "methodical battle," and Bomber Command to enforce an unworkable bombing
  doctrine: "if you are right, top-down leadership will allow you to get it very, very right. If
  you get it wrong… you will get it very, very wrong."
- **Evolutionary innovation** "depends on organizational focus over a sustained period… rather
  than on one particular individual." The German–British armor contrast is the model: the gulf
  between the two armies in May 1940 was the cumulative result of "incremental" changes "over
  the previous two decades." "Military leadership has the most influence on innovation by
  long-term cultural changes rather than immediate short-term decisions" — Seeckt's remaking of
  the Reichswehr officer corps versus Milne's merely "personal" and quickly dissipated influence
  in Britain.

## What makes innovation succeed

The book distils recurring factors (chs. 8–9):

1. **A concrete strategic problem.** Present "in virtually every case": the U.S. and Japanese
   navies' problem of projecting power "in the vastness of the Pacific" pulled
   [[carrier-aviation]] and [[amphibious-warfare]]; Germany's two-front continental problem
   pulled [[armored-warfare]] and [[close-air-support]]. Where no concrete problem existed (the
   European navies and the carrier), innovation languished. A problem alone is insufficient —
   there must also be "clear institutional conceptions and interest": the Germans aimed "to
   overthrow the results of World War I," the French "merely aimed to hold on."
2. **Military culture.** "The sum of the intellectual, professional, and traditional values of
   an officer corps." The decisive enabler in this volume — an honest, study-minded culture
   that (a) drew **realistic lessons from the last war** (the Germans, vs. Montgomery-Massingberd
   suppressing the unfavourable 1932 report), (b) treated **mistakes as learning, not as
   career-ending** ("part of the learning experience, not… items deserving punishment"), and
   (c) took **doctrine seriously** as an integrating framework (the *Truppenführung* let armor
   and close air support slot into combined-arms mobile war).
3. **Honest experimentation and education.** Realistic **war games and exercises** used "to
   illuminate possibilities," *not* to "justify current, 'revealed' doctrine" — the U.S. Navy's
   Newport fleet-problem cycle (which worked out massed carrier strikes as early as 1923) and
   the German *Kriegsspiel*. And **professional military education** that cultivated imagination
   rather than dogma (contrast the British regimental anti-intellectualism and the French/Air
   Corps Tactical School's "absolute doctrine").
4. **Geography and the strategic framework** set the stage — island powers leaned to
   [[strategic-bombardment|strategic bombing]] and carriers, continental Germany to ground
   mobility — and political/strategic guidance, especially in democracies, often decided which
   innovations a service was even allowed to pursue.

## Failure to innovate

The mirror image. The volume's premier cautionary tale is "innovation **ignored**":
[[submarine-warfare|anti-submarine warfare]], where the clear lesson of 1917 was set aside by
all three navies against the grain of service culture and treaty politics. The Italians are the
study's worst case — failure lodged "on the backs of an officer corps that failed its nation."
And brilliance at one level cannot redeem neglect at another: German tactical/operational
innovation was undermined by "logistic and intelligence systems that failed to function," which
their own culture held in "contempt."

## Limitations and Critiques

The authors disclaim universality — they decline a predictive "grand theory," and concede that
a "concrete problem" is shown to be a *common* but not provably *necessary* condition. The deeper
caution is the nonlinearity and role of **chance**: Dowding reached Fighter Command only "as a
sop for losing out in the race to become chief of air staff." The framework is descriptive and
comparative rather than law-like — useful, the editors insist, precisely because "thinking about
the past is indeed the only path to the future," not because it yields guarantees. Compare the
more techno-centric [[revolution-in-military-affairs]] thesis, which this institutional-culture
account implicitly rebukes.
