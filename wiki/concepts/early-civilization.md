# Early Civilization

Trigger (2003) defines early civilizations as the first societies in any region to develop states, cities, and complex economies and belief systems on a scale significantly larger and more hierarchical than preceding Neolithic or chiefdom societies. They are characterized by:

- The presence of states (either city-states or territorial states)
- Urban centers
- Marked social stratification (class systems)
- Specialized economies with long-distance trade and craft production
- Elaborate religious and symbolic systems, often including divine or semi-divine kingship
- Literacy or other specialized knowledge systems in at least some cases
- Significant differences from both smaller-scale societies and later industrial civilizations

Trigger emphasizes that "early civilization" is an analytical category based on structural similarities rather than a single evolutionary stage or historical tradition. His comparative study of seven cases (Egypt, Mesopotamia, Shang China, Aztecs, Maya, Inka, Yoruba) shows both striking cross-cultural regularities and significant idiosyncratic differences rooted in local cultural traditions.

**Key distinction (Trigger 2003):** Only two basic viable forms of political organization appear in early civilizations — **city-states** and **territorial states** — and these influence economic behaviour, settlement patterns, administration, and elite/commoner relations at least as much as environmental differences do.

From the source: “Two sorts of state appear to have been associated with early civilizations: city-states and territorial states… Each of the civilizations being studied consisted of city-states or territorial states but not both.”

City-states: smaller scale, higher urban density (farmers often resident in cities for protection), more heterarchical power-sharing and market-oriented exchange. Territorial states: larger scale, dispersed rural populations, more hierarchical administration, stronger central control of exotics and elite production.

**Major cross-cultural regularities (Trigger):** Tiny upper class appropriating most agricultural surpluses via multiple mechanisms; kingship as universal embodiment of sovereignty (divinity claims stronger where rulers remote); class systems protected by law and professional forces; religion with undifferentiated natural/supernatural/social realms, deities as personified forces, mutual human-god dependence via sacrifice (projecting taxation/class relations into the cosmos and imposing moral limits on exploitation); shared cosmological structure (multi-level vertical cosmos, center privilege, axis mundi); only limited functional alternatives for administration, land tenure, and military organization.

**Greater diversity than expected:** Agricultural regimes, tools, and intensity highly variable; values and personal aspirations strongly idiosyncratic and enduring; art styles unique; writing full in only four cases. Cultural traditions actively shaped specific forms alongside functional constraints.

## Historiography Note

The concept builds on V. Gordon Childe's "Urban Revolution" but moves beyond trait-list approaches to performance, information, power, and meaning. It challenges both extreme relativists (who deny meaningful cross-cultural comparison) and simplistic materialist evolutionists (who underplay the role of specific cultural traditions and human cognitive/psychological factors).
