# Banditry (*Latrocinium*) in the Roman Empire

**Brigandage** (*latrocinium*) treated not as random crime but as a **structural and permanent
feature** of the Roman imperial order — the characteristic form of resistance and alienation in a
world of cities. The analysis is **Brent D. Shaw's**, in [[bowman-cah-v11-2000|CAH XI]] Ch. 11; it
extends the argument of his influential article "Bandits in the Roman Empire," and stands beside
the broader account of [[roman-citizenship-and-belonging|belonging and exclusion]].

## Where bandits came from

Banditry was bred at the **margins of state power** — both geographical and temporal:

- **Ecological frontiers**: the great mountain walls (Alps, Taurus, Atlas, Isauria), forests and
  marshlands "beyond the practical limits of the armed power of the central state" and beyond the
  reach of the cities on which government depended (e.g. the *Boukoloi*, the wild cattle-herders of
  the Nile delta marshes).
- **Temporal "fault lines"**: the breakdowns of central authority — the civil wars of AD 68–69 and
  193–197 — when "whole armies could be classified as bandits" and *latro* became a label to paste
  on a hated political enemy.

Its recruits were the **socially dislodged**: army deserters and soldiers who would not retire, the
defeated of civil wars, **transhumant shepherds** whose movements took them beyond lowland
civilization, **fugitive slaves** forming maroon communities, and fishermen drifting between
mercenary service and piracy. All were straightforwardly classed by the state and its law as
*latrones*.

## A natural disaster of everyday life

Bandit raids were frequent enough to be feared **like a flood or an earthquake**. Travel beyond the
town walls carried real apprehension; people of rank "simply disappeared" from the roads (Pliny's
*eques* Robustus and Metilius Crispus, vanished with their armed escorts); tombstones across the
empire record death *a latronibus*. Governors might recruit professional **bandit-hunters** for
their staff, and the army's *beneficiarii* doubled as a rough rural police. Around this reality
clustered a lurid **xenophobic folklore** linking brigands to the dead, blood sacrifice, ritual
cannibalism, and malign cults.

## The bandit as a mirror of empire

For the literate urban elite, the bandit was a **dual figure** (like the barbarian and the
*rusticus*): an evil, unacceptable form of power in a "just and civilized" world — **but**, against
a "bad" emperor whose rule was felt to be oppressive, the "pristine and pure" brigand could be
turned into a **moral warning** about the better order the empire ought to be. So the historians
gave great named brigands set-piece roles in their narratives: **Iulius Maternus**, the army
deserter who challenged Commodus in Gaul and Spain (Herodian); and **Bulla Felix** ("the Lucky"),
whose bold raids in Italy embarrassed Septimius Severus (Dio). Behind each celebrated bandit, Shaw
insists, stood "dozens, if not hundreds, of anonymous and much less successful challenges to Roman
order" — banditry as a **permanent process of alienation** intrinsic to the empire, not an
aberration.

## Significance and contest

Shaw's reading makes *latrocinium* a **lens on the limits of the Roman state** — its incomplete
monopoly of violence, the city–country fault line, and the criminalization of those outside the
urban order. The framework engages (and partly qualifies) **Eric Hobsbawm's "social bandit"** model
of the rebel-as-popular-hero: Shaw shows the elite *construction* of the bandit was as important as
the social fact, and that the label *latro* was itself a flexible instrument of power. As avowedly
sociological history built largely on literary, juristic, and anecdotal evidence (banditry being
"so remote from literacy" that bandits rarely speak for themselves), it remains an interpretive
rather than settled account.
