## Definition and Scope

"Orthodoxy as a social phenomenon is not a 'thing' but rather a process." — Ahmed El Shamsy, in [[winter-cambridge-companion-islamic-theology-2008]]

Islamic orthodoxy, in the analytical sense used here, refers to the historically contingent process by which certain theological doctrines became established as normative within Muslim communities. It is the product of the interaction among three societal arenas: the community of scholars (*'ulama'*), ordinary believers, and the government. This concept page focuses on the mechanisms of this construction and its historical trajectory from the ninth through the nineteenth centuries, as analyzed in Chapter 5 of [[winter-cambridge-companion-islamic-theology-2008]].

The concept is distinct from "Islam" or "Sunni Islam" as substantive theological categories. It is an *etic* analytical framework for studying how doctrinal authority is produced, maintained, and enforced — a process that involves contestation, exclusion, selective remembering, and social coercion, not just the triumph of correct doctrine.

## The Mechanisms of Orthodoxy Construction

### 1. Scholarly Transmission and the Isnad System

From the eighth century onward, the primary mechanism for establishing doctrinal authority was the face-to-face transmission of knowledge through chains of authoritative teachers (*isnad*). Students received texts through *sama'* (hearing a teacher read a text) or *qira'a* (reading a text aloud to a qualified teacher), who then certified the transmission. This system created:

- A cosmolopitan class of scholars united by shared methodological norms
- A mechanism of exclusion: scholars whose doctrinal views were too heterodox could be discredited as unreliable transmitters
- A portable form of authority that did not depend on any single institution

The *ijaza* (permission to teach a text) gradually supplemented and then largely replaced the more demanding *sama'* and *qira'a* methods. Western scholarship has interpreted this as degeneration, but El Shamsy argues it reflects the maturation of a more literate scholarly culture, not its decline.

### 2. The Role of Institutions: Mosque, Madrasa, Sufi Lodge

No single institution monopolised Islamic education in the pre-Ottoman period. The mosque was the original site of public teaching; the private home provided space for advanced or controversial instruction; the *madrasa* (funded by waqf endowments) emerged from the tenth–eleventh centuries as a semi-public venue for sustained scholarship.

A critical development was the Ottoman formalisation of the madrasa system under Mehmed II (d. 1481), who created a standardised hierarchy of madrasas with fixed career paths and a standardised curriculum. The texts of al-Iji, al-Taftazani, and al-Sharif al-Jurjani — all late Ash'ari or Maturidi kalam authorities — underpinned this standardised theological syllabus. The position of the *seyhulislam* (shaykh al-islam) as the supreme state-appointed religious authority completed the Ottoman centralisation of orthodoxy definition.

### 3. The Mechanisms of Exclusion

Scholars enforced orthodoxy through a graduated toolkit of exclusion:
- Denial of social courtesies (the greeting, smiling, participation in funeral prayers)
- Discrediting individuals as unreliable hadith transmitters
- Excluding heterodox positions from the *ijma'* (scholarly consensus)
- Posthumous recantation: attributing deathbed repentances to controversial figures (e.g., [[al-juwayni|al-Juwayni]] and [[fakhr-al-din-al-razi|Fakhr al-Din al-Razi]] were posthumously alleged to have recanted their kalam, while later Shafi'i scholars explained away al-Shafi'i's hostility to kalam)
- Judicial authority: judges could deny the status of court witness, which implied social and religious disqualification
- Extreme case: judges could sentence declared heretics to death

### 4. Government and Orthodoxy

Government never controlled the *content* of Islamic orthodoxy in the pre-modern period — this was the prerogative of the scholars — but it exercised decisive influence over its *propagation* and *enforcement*. Key mechanisms included:

- **Appointment of judges and teachers**: By selecting individuals of approved doctrinal orientation, rulers shaped which theological positions were amplified. Nizam al-Mulk's network of Nizamiyya madrasas, for example, was explicitly staffed to promote Ash'arism.
- **The mihna (833–848)**: The Abbasid caliph al-Ma'mun's attempt to impose the Mu'tazilite doctrine of the createdness of the Qur'an through the judicial apparatus. It failed due to sustained resistance from the scholarly establishment, demonstrating that government could not override scholarly consensus by fiat.
- **Safavid conversion of Iran to Shi'ism** (early 16th century): Shah Isma'il's successful forced conversion of an overwhelmingly Sunni population by importing Twelver scholars from Lebanon, deploying military force, and declaring adherence to Sunnism a capital offence. Succeess was partly due to the weakness of the Sunni scholarly establishment, still recovering from the Mongol invasion.
- **Wahhabi-Sa'udi alliance** (18th–19th centuries): The alliance of Muhammad ibn 'Abd al-Wahhab's theological challenge to Ottoman legitimacy with Ibn Sa'ud's territorial ambitions, drawing on Ibn Taymiyya's anti-rationalist framework and resulting in the sacking of Karbala (1801) and occupation of Mecca (1803–1812).

### 5. Popular Religion

Ordinary believers were not passive recipients of scholarly orthodoxy. They actively constructed, propagated, and defended their own religious practices, which at times deviated significantly from 'ulama' norms. Key examples:

- **Grave visitation**: The Cairene tradition of visiting saints' graves (*al-Qarafa*) persisted despite widespread scholarly discomfort. The majority of 'ulama' sought to regulate the practice rather than abolish it; a vocal minority condemned it. It continues to the present day.
- **Sufi popular practice**: The Qalandars and other marginal Sufi groups occupied an ambiguous position, tolerated through a kind of "suspended judgment" even when their behaviour appeared scandalous.
- **Civic conflict over doctrine**: When doctrinal disputes became publicly mobilised — as in the Baghdad riots of 1067 sparked by the Ash'arite Abu Nasr al-Qushayri's lectures against Hanbali theology — popular identity aligned with scholarly factions, producing street violence that had to be suppressed by the vizier Nizam al-Mulk.

## Historical Trajectory

### 9th–10th Centuries: Competing Discourses

The earliest period was marked by sharp competition between:
- **The traditionists** (*muhaddithun*): cosmopolitan, consensus-oriented, transmission-focused
- **The dialectical theologians** (*mutakallimun*, primarily [[mutazilism|Mu'tazilites]]): formal and rationalist, prone to sectarianism and mutual denunciation of heresy

The stark distinction disappeared with the Ash'ari and Maturidi syntheses in the tenth century. Both schools adopted the traditionist practice of producing memorisable credos while defending the use of rational argument.

### Late 10th Century: The Sunni Consensus Crystallised

"By the end of the tenth century, the broad outlines of the developed Sunni orthodoxy had taken shape. This orthodoxy was structured around several established schools of law, which defined right action, and the three main 'schools' of theology (Ash'aris, Maturidis and traditionists) that defined right belief." ([[winter-cambridge-companion-islamic-theology-2008]])

See [[asharism]], [[maturidism]], and [[developed-kalam-tradition]] for the constituent schools.

### 950–1450: The Middle Period

Small, often ephemeral states with rulers of slave or foreign background were compelled to present themselves as guardians of the theological status quo, leaving actual orthodoxy definition to the scholars. This produced a period of relative scholarly autonomy and the flourishing of kalam supercommentary.

### Ottoman Period: Centralisation

The Ottoman Empire centralised and formalised the madrasa system and the seyhulislam's authority, producing the closest approximation to an "official" Sunni orthodoxy that pre-modern Islam achieved. The standardised curriculum was a product of government intervention and produced uniformly trained administrators-cum-scholars.

### Shi'i Orthodoxy: A Parallel Development

Twelver Shi'i scholarly culture developed roughly a century later than Sunni orthodoxy, partly because the living Imams had served as the community's supreme authority until 874 (the start of the occultation). The khums (one-fifth tax) gave Twelver scholars financial independence from government patronage that their Sunni counterparts often lacked. Twelver orthodoxy converged on the Usuli model — emphasising the hierarchical scholarly class headed by the *marji' al-taqlid* (source of emulation) — after the defeat of the rival Akhbari movement in the late eighteenth century.

## Contested Nature of the Concept

The analytical concept of "the social construction of orthodoxy" is contested at several levels:

1. **Normative vs. descriptive**: Muslim scholars who work within a tradition generally prefer to describe orthodoxy as the recovery of correct doctrine rather than its social construction.
2. **Continuity vs. discontinuity**: Whether the "Sunni consensus" of the tenth century represents genuine continuity with the Prophet's community or a significant innovation is a live debate.
3. **Roots of popular religion**: Whether civic conflicts over orthodoxy (like the 1067 Baghdad riots) were primarily driven by genuine doctrinal commitment or by social and ethnic tensions mobilised through religious language remains "a heated but as yet inconclusive debate among historians."
