# Roman Social Status and the Augustan Social Legislation

The structure of **legal and social status** in the early empire — citizen, freedman, slave;
senator, *eques*, decurion — and the **legislation by which Augustus tried to reshape the
behaviour of the citizen body** (marriage, adultery, manumission). Treated in
[[bowman-cah-v10-1996|CAH X]] Ch. 18 (**Susan Treggiari**).

## The primacy of legal status

Treggiari opens from the jurist Gaius' classification: "all men are either **free or slaves**;
free men are either ***ingenui* (freeborn) or *libertini* (freedmen)**; of freedmen there are
three classes — Roman citizens, Latins, or *dediticii*." To the Roman lawyer **legal status is
the essential distinction**: Roman civil law applied only to citizens, and the consequences of
status were concrete (a man who unwittingly married a non-citizen or slave begot illegitimate or
slave children; bequests to a non-citizen were void). The category was, however, **fluid** — a
citizen could fall into slavery by capture, a slave be freed into full citizenship, and many
lived in disputed or intermediate states (*statuliberi*, *bona fide servientes*, the informally
manumitted). The privilege of citizenship is captured in the figure of **Paul of Tarsus**, who
could halt official proceedings by claiming his citizen rights — the counterpart of the humble
trader Cicero said should be able to trust his status anywhere.

## The orders (*ordines*)

Roman society was articulated into **orders** defined by constitutional function:

- the **senatorial order** (membership by election to the quaestorship, now a defined hereditary
  class with a **one-million-sesterce** census from 18 BCE — see [[senate-under-the-principate]]);
- the **equestrian order** — a "second order" between Senate and *plebs*, marked by the gold ring
  and the HS **400,000** census, which had "evolved gradually over a couple of centuries" (jurors
  since C. Gracchus). Augustus, by holding the census efficiently and **multiplying
  administrative posts**, "defined the second order more formally and greatly increased its
  prestige" (5,000 *equites* in the parade; Tiberius uniting jurors and *equites equo publico*);
- the **decurions** of the towns (the local councils, "an order just as the Senate did at Rome,"
  a channel upward to the equestrian and senatorial orders);
- and registered groups — *libertini*, the *scribae*, the *apparitores* — bridging upper class
  and freed slave.

## Wealth and birth cut across the orders

Treggiari stresses that *ordines* alone do not describe society: **economic** distinction (the
gulf between rich and poor) and **birth** modify it. Senatorial and equestrian fortunes could be
vast (Lentulus the augur reputedly HS 400 million; Seneca 300 million; the imperial freedmen
**Narcissus** 400 million and **Pallas** 300 million under Claudius — the moralists' favourite
scandal), against a labourer's HS 3 a day and a soldier's HS 900 a year. **Birth** (*nobilitas*,
descent from consuls, patrician or mythical lineage) carried weight independent of wealth, but
"absence of distinctions of birth was not an insuperable barrier": the Senate was "always
recruited from below," and the orders were knit together by **patronage, intermarriage, and the
army** as a channel of mobility (Velleius Paterculus' own rise via service under Tiberius).

## The Augustan moral legislation

Against this background Treggiari sets Augustus' famous attempt to **legislate morality and
reproduction** — among the most intrusive social programmes of any ancient state:

- The **marriage laws** — the *lex Iulia de maritandis ordinibus* (**18 BCE**) and the *lex Papia
  Poppaea* (**9 CE**) — penalized the **unmarried and childless** (the *caelibes* and *orbi*) in
  their capacity to inherit, while **rewarding fertility** (the *ius trium liberorum* — privileges
  for parents of three children) — aimed above all at the **propertied classes** and the
  reproduction of the elite.
- The ***lex Iulia de adulteriis*** made **adultery a public crime** (a *quaestio*), removing it
  from the family's private jurisdiction and exposing the conduct of the elite (and Augustus' own
  daughter and granddaughter, the two Julias) to the state.
- The **manumission laws** — the *lex Fufia Caninia* (2 BCE, capping testamentary manumissions)
  and the *lex Aelia Sentia* (4 CE, restricting manumission by age and creating the inferior
  status of the *Latini Iuniani* for the informally freed) — **regulated the flow of freed slaves
  into the citizen body**, reflecting anxiety about its composition.

## Significance and contest

The legislation expresses the Augustan concern to **define and grade the citizen body**,
reinforce the hierarchy of orders, and secure the reproduction of the propertied classes — part
of the same "restoration" impulse as the [[augustan-religion|religious restructuring]]. Its
**effectiveness is debated**: the marriage laws were resented, repeatedly evaded, and amended
(the *lex Papia Poppaea* a partial retreat); Tacitus records their unpopularity and the swarm of
*delatores* they bred. How far they altered actual behaviour — versus serving as ideological
self-definition of the regime — remains contested.
