# Roman Private Law (the late Republic)

The **civil law** of persons, property, obligations, and procedure as it stood by the
death of Cicero — the most enduring of Rome's creations, and (Crook stresses) a
**"jurists' law"** built mainly through advances in **procedure**. Treated in
[[crook-cah-v9-1992|CAH IX]] Ch. 14 (J. A. Crook).

## How the law grew: the praetor and the jurists

Rome had **no code** (the revered **Twelve Tables** were never a full one) and few
private-law statutes. The substantive law developed instead through the **praetors**,
who could not make *ius civile* but, controlling the courts and holding *imperium*,
created new remedies — the ***ius honorarium*** ("praetorian law"), which grew "to
assist, supplement, or correct" the civil law and became a distinct corpus. The
novelties were largely invented by the ***iurisprudentes*** — learned amateurs of the
governing class whose role (*agere*, *cavere*, *respondere*: pleading, drafting,
giving opinions) had passed from the *pontifices* to secular experts. Crook: Roman
law, like English, was "**jurists' law**," and as the Republic ended, jurisprudence
and **advocacy** (taken over by orators like Cicero under Greek rhetorical influence)
split into two professions.

## The law of persons (*libertas, civitas, familia*)

- ***Libertas***: free or slave. The slave was a **paradox** — a *res* (chattel,
  property) yet also a person; killable, yet (since Sulla's *Lex Cornelia*, 81)
  killing another's slave was murder. Slaves were freed by *manumissio* (becoming
  citizens, but *libertini* owing duties to their patron).
- ***Civitas***: only *cives Romani* enjoyed full Roman law; *peregrini* (most free
  people, until Italy was enfranchised in 90/89 and 49) were excluded from *dominium*,
  *potestas*, and inheritance from Romans, though commercial law was open to them.
- ***Familia*** and ***patria potestas***: the **agnatic** patriline, in which the
  eldest male held lifelong power (including the right of death) over all descendants
  in his *potestas* — who could **own nothing** (the *peculium* a discretionary
  exception). Marriage might or might not involve ***manus*** (the wife passing into
  the husband's line); by the late Republic *manus*-free marriage and a degree of
  **female independence** (the weakening of *tutela mulierum*) were common, though
  Crook cautions against overstating women's "emancipation." **Testation** — total
  freedom to dispose by will — was the *paterfamilias*' greatest power, eroded by the
  praetorian *bonorum possessio* and the ***querela inofficiosi testamenti*** (suit
  against unfair disinheritance), "a great inroad into *patria potestas*."

## Property and obligations

- **Property**: an abstract, total concept of ownership (*dominium ex iure
  Quiritium*), divorced from mere *possessio*; the ancient *res mancipi*/*nec mancipi*
  distinction (formal conveyance by *mancipatio* for land, slaves, etc.), mitigated by
  ***usucapio*** (title by uninterrupted possession). Plus servitudes, usufruct, and
  real security (*fiducia*, *pignus*).
- **Obligations** divided into **contract** and **delict**. The great contractual
  advance was the rise of the **consensual contracts** (sale, hire, partnership,
  mandate) — *bilateral* and *bonae fidei*, created by mere agreement — beside the old
  unilateral, strict *stipulatio*. The three **delicts** were *furtum* (theft, widely
  defined, penalties now pecuniary), *iniuria* (assault and insult — a major
  praetorian creation, edging toward libel/slander), and *damnum iniuria datum*
  (property damage, under the **Lex Aquilia**, developed by juristic interpretation).

## Procedure (the law of actions)

Civil procedure was **two-stage**: before the **praetor** (*in iure*, who framed the
issue but never tried it) and then *apud iudicem* before a lay **judge**, *arbiter*,
*recuperatores*, or the **centumviral court** (inheritance cases). The decisive
development was the ***formulary system*** (the **Lex Aebutia**) replacing the rigid
old *legis actiones* with flexible written **formulae** — adaptable to both sides'
claims (*exceptiones*) and to praetorian innovation. The system was **adversarial**
(no professional judge, no public force to compel attendance or enforce judgment — only
*infamia* and the seizure of assets); all condemnations were for **money**, never
direct recovery. Hence **advocacy** was as vital as jurisprudence.

## Italy and the provinces

Local **municipal courts** kept civil jurisdiction (capped in value); the **governor**
ran assizes with the standard formulary procedure (the *tabula Contrebiensis* shows it
applied to a Spanish boundary dispute, 87), left local jurisdiction to the provincials
where he chose, or (like Verres) intervened — provincial judgments being as final as
those at Rome.
