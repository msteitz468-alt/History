# Dark Matter Historiography (Middle Eastern Religious History)

**Dark matter historiography** is Tannous’s metaphor for reconstructing the history of the late antique and medieval Middle East around the mass of [[simple-believers|simple believers]] and non-elite majorities who do not write the surviving elite texts but whose presence shaped leaders, institutions, and confessional outcomes — as physical dark matter shapes galaxies without emitting light.

## Emic vs. etic

- **Emic:** Not an actors’ category; modern analytical metaphor.
- **Etic:** Challenges the habit of reading community size and historical importance from the volume of surviving high literature (especially Arabic Islamic texts under Muslim political power).

## Core claims (Tannous)

1. **No necessary link** between a community’s demographic size and the volume of literature it produces or that survives — especially when that community holds political power.
2. **Pirenne’s clean break** (“The Roman … became Arabized as soon as he was conquered by Islam. … There was a clean cut: a complete break with the past”) underweights long Christian majorities and continuities.
3. **Impressionistic demography** (library and classroom impressions of Muslim numerical hegemony) is not a substitute for careful inference; Umayyad/Abbasid/Mamluk censuses do not exist.
4. Comparative analogy: fourth-century Roman pagans almost certainly outnumbered Christians, yet surviving literature reverses the ratio.

> "there is no direct or necessary connection between the size of a religious community and the volume of literature that its adherents produce, especially when those adherents hold political power."
> — Tannous, Conclusion

## Programmatic stakes

If simple Christians remained a large share of the population deep into the medieval period (archaeologically and in Western witnesses such as Burchard of Mt. Zion, read critically), both Christian and Muslim religious histories of the Middle East must be rewritten: [[islamization-of-the-middle-east|Islamization]] is slower and more social; Islamic culture absorbs late antique “bloodstream” materials through co-residence; and medieval Middle Eastern history cannot be equated with Islamic history alone ([[transconfessional-history]]).

## Limitations

- Metaphor can be over-applied; not all silences are equal.
- Positive reconstruction of non-elites still depends on elite mediation and material culture.
