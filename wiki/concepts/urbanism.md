# Urbanism (The City as Analytical Category)

## Definition

**Urbanism** is the analytical study of what cities are, how they differ from
villages and towns, and how urban life reorganizes society. It is distinct from
[[urbanization]] (the historical *process* by which cities form) and from the
individual city place pages. This page records the conceptual
apparatus historians and archaeologists use to define and compare cities —
drawn from [[yoffee-cwh-v3-2015|Yoffee's introduction to CWH Vol. III]].

There is no single agreed definition of "the city." Three families of
definition recur:

- **Demographic / trait-list** — a city is large, dense, and permanent
  (Wirth's "a relatively large, dense, and permanent settlement of socially
  heterogeneous individuals," 1938), identifiable by a checklist of features.
- **Functional** — a city is defined by what it *does* for its region: a nodal
  point of administration, exchange, storage and redistribution, defense,
  pilgrimage, and ceremony, in a structured relation to a restructured
  hinterland (Weber; central-place theory).
- **Relational / experiential** — a city is a qualitatively new *kind of social
  space*: heterogeneous, anonymous, a place of strangers and of new identities
  as citizens that overlie but do not erase kin and ethnic identities
  (Simmel's "mental life"; Wirth's "urbanism as a way of life").

Yoffee adds, half-seriously, a final criterion in the volume's conclusion:
**cities must be awesome** — monumentally and experientially overwhelming to
those who entered them.

## Childe's Ten Criteria (the "urban revolution")

V. Gordon Childe (1950) gave archaeology its first operational definition,
"ten rather abstract criteria, all deducible from archaeological data," to
"distinguish even the earliest cities from any older or contemporary village."
Grouped: (1) large, dense population; (2) full-time specialists not tied to
subsistence; (3) concentration of surplus / taxation; (4) monumental public
buildings; (5) a ruling class; (6) [[writing-and-record-keeping|writing and
record-keeping]]; (7) exact and predictive sciences (arithmetic, geometry,
astronomy); (8) naturalistic and symbolic art; (9) long-distance trade in
luxuries and vital materials; (10) state organization based on residence rather
than kinship. See [[urban-revolution]] for the concept and its critique.

## The comparative method

CWH Vol. III is an explicit experiment in **controlled comparison** — comparing
cities by *feature* (performance, information, landscape, power, creation, empire)
rather than treating each civilization in isolation. This descends from Robert
McC. Adams' *Evolution of Urban Society* (1966), which compared Mesopotamian and
Mesoamerican cities directly, and from the city-state comparative projects of
Nichols & Charlton (1997) and Hansen's Copenhagen Polis Centre (2000, 2002). The
juxtapositions are deliberately provocative — Rome with [[places/tenochtitlan|Tenochtitlan]],
Athens with [[places/jenne-jeno|Jenne-jeno]].

## Key thinkers (genealogy)

- **Numa Denis Fustel de Coulanges** (*La cité antique*, 1864) — the city as
  built from kin and ancestor cult; religion as the "cement" of urban aggregation.
  Long marginal in archaeology, seminal in sociology (Durkheim, Weber). Newly
  relevant given the centrality of ritual at early and pre-urban sites
  (Göbekli Tepe, Poverty Point).
- **Max Weber** (*Die Stadt*, 1921) — cities as arenas of political struggle
  among economic, ethnic, occupational, and religious groups; the consumer-city
  vs producer-city contrast (now largely superseded).
- **The Chicago school** (Park, Burgess, Wirth) via Simmel — urbanism as a
  social form; concentric zonation; migration-fed heterogeneity.
- **V. Gordon Childe** and **Robert McC. Adams** — the archaeological turn:
  trait-lists, then settlement survey and [[urbanization|central-place]] analysis.
- **Paul Wheatley** (*Pivot of the Four Quarters*, 1971) — the ritual centrality
  of the earliest cities; the city as cosmogram.

## Limitations and critiques

- Trait-lists essentialize: they risk denying early cities their distinctive
  qualities and histories, and they smuggle in the Mesopotamian/Western city as
  the template (the "first city" problem; Emberling).
- The hierarchical, palace-controlled "oriental city" model has been overturned
  for many cases — see [[heterarchy]] and the [[hierarchy-vs-heterarchy-early-cities]]
  controversy. Cities such as [[places/jenne-jeno|Jenne-jeno]] were large and
  complex without centralized kingship.
- Modern urban studies are an underused resource for the ancient world; the
  differences (industrialization, globalization) are real but the similarities
  (numbers, heterogeneity, hinterland dependence) license comparison.

## Wickham, Framing (2005) — early medieval urban criteria and regional patterns

[[wickham-framing-the-early-middle-ages-2005|Wickham, *Framing the Early Middle Ages* (2005)]] supplies a
working definition and comparative hierarchy for **400–800**, holding economic and
political/institutional frames together so that Rome, Recópolis, Pella, Dijon, York, and Ribe can sit in
one argument.

### Definitions: double vision, Biddle, “town” vs “city”

Early medieval urban continuity debates are archaeologically fruitful but often lack shared criteria:
historians and archaeologists view “the city” with a **double vision**—economic function versus
politico-administrative status (late Roman *municipia*, episcopal seats, post-Roman secular centres).
Wickham adopts Martin **Biddle’s** twelve-element ideal type (*Kriteriabündel*: defences, street planning,
markets, mint, legal autonomy, central-place role, dense population, economic diversification, urban
house-types, social differentiation, complex religious organization, judicial functions—any three or four
as minimum) as exportable beyond England. A cultural split matters: northern scholarship treats medieval
urbanism as predominantly **economic** (English “town”, French *ville*, German *Stadt*), while
Mediterranean languages (*città*, *ciudad*) stress institutional and monumental Roman inheritance. Wickham’s
own working definition privileges **economic** criteria as ultimately most significant: relative
demographic concentration, a market, and economic activities structurally different from the countryside
(Biddle 3, 7, 8). He restricts “**city**” to economically urban centres that contemporaries would also have
called *civitates* (or equivalents); by the latest Roman/post-Roman West the residual monumental kit was
mainly walls, cathedral, and royal/comital palace.

### Curiae, notables, bishops; hierarchy of indicators

Across East and West in the fifth–sixth centuries the *curia*/*boulē* ceased nearly everywhere—**not**
itself failure of city government. Curiae became outdated under greater central tax control and expanded
senatorial paths out of curial duty; by the late sixth century tax-raising was largely central-government
business. In the East, informal *proteuontes*/*ktētores* (leading men/landowners) plus provincial governor
chose city officials; in the West, bishops play a more central role beside *honorati* and surviving (ill-
documented outside Italy) curiae. Evidentiary ladder: tax-raising *curia* ⇒ firm economic foundation;
clearly characterised informal notables ⇒ richest owners choosing city life and artisanal demand; **only
bishop plus count/duke/pagarch** ⇒ need other indicators (archaeology, churches, street plan, Biddle
elements) before treating the place as economically urban ([[tax-based-vs-land-based-states]]).

### Aristocratic residence and regional urban hierarchy c.700–50

Roman aristocrats lived in cities because each was the locus of tax-raising and political power.
Post-Roman, those imperatives weakened: in **northern Francia, Britain, much of Spain, and much of the
Byzantine empire** aristocrats tended to ruralise; where they stayed urban (**Italy, Andalucía,
Syria-Palestine, Egypt**) cities remained important secular administrative centres—so institutional factors
retained a causal role in economic urban survival. Document-based rank c.700–50: most urbanised
aristocracies in Egypt, Italy, Syria-Palestine; next southern Spain and southern Gaul (perhaps Marmara near
Constantinople, maybe Africa); most rural Greece and Anatolia, northern Spain, and above all **northern
Gaul**. Britain can be excluded until c.700 because urbanism as an economic phenomenon had effectively
vanished ([[merovingian-cities]]).

### Material change: forums, houses, polyvalent “decline”

Physical decay of forum/agora complexes could coincide with rich private houses and privately founded
churches. Encroachment on public space can be read as private vitality amid decaying municipal zoning **or**
as planning crisis; local balance mattered more than either principle alone. After forum/curial decline, the
West shows two stable models: (1) islands of church-focused monumental activity (*città ad isole*), commoner
in Gaul and Africa; (2) more focused clustering near old fora, commoner in Spain and Italy—neither inferior
to early imperial urbanism; forum weakening ≠ economic urban recession. Eight material change types (forum
demonumentalisation, less monumental building, spatial fragmentation, house division, simpler techniques,
amenity collapse, intramural burial, abandonment) are **polyvalent**—only full abandonment clearly equals
urban weakness alone; accumulation of several indicators means fabric weakening and can cross minima for
urbanism. **No reliable** early medieval urban population figures exist between late imperial
Rome/Constantinople estimates and Domesday 1086.

### Regional sketches (West and East)

- **Italy:** two new urban housing models—one-room wood/reused-stone houses without antique parallel;
  two-storeyed houses (kitchen/stores below, living above) from Byzantine Rimini, Ravenna, Rome (Forum
  Nervae c.800) and Lombard Pisa. Clearest documentary urban aristocrats and generalised urban survival in
  the West—but Italian aristocrats after the sixth-century crisis were **not rich**.
- **Spain:** extreme sub-regional heterogeneity—NE (Barcelona, Zaragoza) more continuous; SE
  (Cartagena hinterland/Tudmīr) broke down c.600–800; Andalucía/Mérida stronger continuities; northern Meseta
  largely rural by the seventh century. **Mérida** is the best Spanish parallel to Italian low-key
  continuity (street plan retained; sixth-century focus on cathedral/ducal palace not forum; temporary low
  around 700 with ceramic improvement already in the eighth under Arabs). When usable, Spanish cities show
  greater maintenance of monumental centrality than Italy and much greater than Africa.
- **Africa (Vandal fiscal exit):** abandonment of curial tax-raising strengthened episcopal centrality;
  removal from the fiscally based Mediterranean exchange system disrupted local production and wealth,
  helping explain forum demonumentalisation that weakened spatial urban coherence more than in the East
  ([[roman-africa]]).
- **Syria-Palestine / Egypt:** strongest urban continuities, **independent of Roman fiscal survival**.
  Kennedy’s 1985 polis-to-*madīna* model (infill, colonnades→shops, enclosed mosques, respect for merchants)
  is accepted with refinements (Umayyad/Abbasid monumental ambitions; major centres largely kept classical
  street plans; spatial change cultural and independent of economic rise/fall and of urban political autonomy,
  largely complete by 500). No other former Roman region matches this pair’s pronounced urban continuities
  ([[roman-syria]], [[roman-egypt]]).

### Emporia, barometer towns, and the typical city

Urban life lost momentum nearly everywhere as states weakened, exchange shrank, and aristocracies grew
poorer—important exceptions only **Egypt and Syria-Palestine**; low points vary (eighth-century western
Mediterranean; early sixth northern Gaul). **Emporia** meet commercial/artisanal urban criteria but were
not political or ecclesiastical residences of the wealthy; kings controlled tolls but left them monumentally
unimpressive and politically marginal—hence fragility when Viking-era routes shifted. Eighth-century
**Dorestad** looks more commercially active than most west Mediterranean towns, but was merchant/artisan-
dominated and more fragile than landowning political centres such as Verona or Gerasa; Cologne’s hybrid role
was better equipped for survival. ‘Commercial barometer’ towns (Marseille, Dorestad, Venice) were
**atypical**; the typical city 400–800 (and until the Industrial Revolution) focused local landowning
surplus, aristocratic demand, production, markets, and administration—solid future growth lay in
city–hinterland relations, not long-distance commerce alone ([[aristocratic-demand]],
[[scandinavian-urbanisation]], [[merovingian-cities]], [[carolingian-economy]]).
