# Fayyum Mummy Portraits

The **"Fayyum" portraits** are naturalistic painted panel portraits of the deceased, set over the
face of the mummy, made in [[roman-egypt|Roman Egypt]] from the **first to third centuries CE**.
Though named for the [[the-fayyum|Fayyum]] cemeteries (Hawara, Philadelphia, Fagg el-Gamus) where
they were first found in quantity, they were produced elsewhere in Egypt too.

They are, in Bagnall and Rathbone's description, "a unique and striking survival from the Roman
world" — almost the only substantial body of **classical-style panel painting** to survive from
antiquity, the technique (encaustic or tempera on wood) otherwise known mostly from literary
description. As such they are the single best evidence for what Greco-Roman portrait painting,
celebrated in ancient sources but everywhere else lost, actually looked like.

## Significance

- **A hybrid funerary culture.** The portraits embody the fusion at the heart of
  [[roman-egypt|Roman Egypt]]: a Greco-Roman naturalistic likeness, with contemporary Roman
  dress, hairstyles, and jewellery, attached to a thoroughly Egyptian practice — mummification,
  which "well into the Roman period… was practised by Greeks and Romans as well as Egyptians"
  because the soul needed a body to dwell in.
- **A portrait of provincial society.** The sitters — their dress, ornament, and apparent age —
  give a vivid demographic and social cross-section of the propertied population of Roman Egypt,
  complementing the documentary picture from the [[papyrology|papyri]].
- **Art-historical value.** They preserve a lost classical medium and anticipate, in their frontal
  gaze and luminous eyes, the conventions of later Byzantine and Coptic icon painting.
