# Roman Republican Religion

## Definition and Scope

**Roman Republican religion** refers to the complex of practices, institutions,
and ideas through which the Romans of the Republic (traditionally 509–27 BCE)
maintained their relationship with the gods and embedded that relationship in
every aspect of public life. It is **not** a separate domain of Roman culture:
as North argues in his pivotal CAH chapter, Roman religion had "no autonomous
existence." It was woven into the structure of the magistracy, the Senate,
military command, agriculture, family law, and the entire calendar.

This page follows the **revisionist framework of J.A. North (CAH VII.2, Ch. 12)**,
which explicitly rejects the developmental and decline narratives dominant in
earlier scholarship (Warde Fowler, Latte, Dumézil). North's working hypothesis:
treat religious phenomena as **broadly constant** across the Republican period
rather than as evolving from primitive animism toward Greek anthropomorphism
toward philosophical skepticism. This does not mean there was no change — there
clearly was — but the change was of the system, not a deterioration of it.

## Source Problems

North's first and most important contribution is his critique of the sources.
**Every significant account of Roman Republican religion was written in the late
Republic or Principate** — by Cicero, Varro, Livy, Dionysius of Halicarnassus,
Ovid, Plutarch. These authors had their own agendas; moreover, some of our best
evidence reaches us through **Christian polemicists** (Tertullian, Arnobius,
Lactantius) who excerpted Varro to refute paganism.

This creates a layered problem:
- The detailed evidence is largely from the first centuries BCE/CE
- It was written by people with strong reasons to simplify or distort
- The Christian transmission filtered further
- The underlying "old" religion — the actual cult forms of the fifth–third centuries
  — is almost entirely invisible directly

Consequence: "a narrative history of early Roman religion, giving the 'facts' and
placing them in an evolutionary or explanatory sequence, was impossible." North
does not claim we know nothing; he claims the older frameworks (animism → polytheism
→ decline) are constructs imposed on inadequate evidence. What we can recover is
the **structure** of the system as it operated throughout the Republic, treating
later evidence as cautiously reflecting earlier conditions.

## Priestly Colleges: Not a Separate Caste

The Roman priestly system was organized into several **colleges** — permanent
bodies with distinct ritual competencies. The key feature is what these colleges
were **not**: an independent priestly caste insulated from politics. Roman priests
were the same men who dominated politics, law, and military command.

**The four major colleges:**

**Pontifices** (*pontifical college*): the widest competence of any college.
The pontifices advised on religious law, maintained the state calendar (deciding
which days were *fas* or *nefas*), supervised the major cult acts, controlled
the family law (wills, adoptions — insofar as these had religious dimensions),
and were available for citizen consultation on religious questions. Their
*pontifex maximus* emerged, probably gradually through the Republic, as the
most powerful priestly figure. He had special authority over the **Vestal Virgins**
and conducted their induction ceremony.

**Augures** (*augural college*): responsible for the *auspices* — the reading of
divine signs (flight of birds, behavior of animals, lightning) that preceded
all official action. The augurs also defined the *templum* (the ceremonially
delimited space within which auspices were valid) and the *pomerium* (the
sacred boundary of the city). Crucially, the *auspicia* were not kept with
the priests but transferred wholesale to the magistrates at the founding of
the Republic — so augurs advised on procedure, but the actual taking of
auspices belonged to the magistrates themselves.

**Fetiales**: specialists in the rituals of warfare and diplomacy — the just-war
declarations (*rerum repetitio*, demand for reparations; *indictio belli*,
formal declaration), the conclusion of treaties by oath and sacrifice, the
surrender of guarantors (*deditio*). Their procedures gave Roman warfare its
formal religious legitimation.

**Duoviri / Decemviri sacris faciundis**: the keepers of the **Sibylline Books**
(a collection of oracles in Greek verse purchased, according to tradition, by
Tarquin the Proud from a sibyl). They were consulted after prodigies to find the
appropriate *remedia* (ritual remedies). The Books legitimated new cults and
rituals — it was under their authority that most foreign gods entered the Roman
system. They were originally two, expanded to ten (decemviri) in 367 BCE as
part of the Licinian-Sextian settlement admitting plebeians to the college.

**Key structural point**: priestly authority in Rome resided not in the colleges
alone, but in the **interaction between magistrates, Senate, and priests**. The
Senate accepted or rejected prodigies, referred them to the appropriate college
for *remedia*, and ultimately authorized the introduction of new cults. No single
institution could construct a monopoly of religious authority — a structural
parallel to the diffusion of political power under the Republic.

## The Nature of Roman Gods

Roman gods ranged from the great personalized deities of the Capitoline triad
(Jupiter, Juno, Minerva) to single-function *numina* with no mythology and no
personality — entities invoked precisely for their function in a specific ritual
context.

The operative relationship was **contractual**, not devotional. Roman vows (*vota*)
were precise formulae: if the god delivered the specified benefit, the worshipper
would deliver the specified offering. The gods were understood as *bound* to
accept exactly what was offered — no more, no less. This is not cynicism but a
feature of the system: the gods were **negotiating partners**, reliable parties
to agreements, not all-powerful rulers whose favor was uncertain.

**Prodigies** (*prodigia*): departures from the normal order (animals giving birth
to monsters, rains of blood, lightning striking temples) were signs that the
divine-human relationship had been disrupted. The correct response was to
*accept* the prodigy (the Senate decided whether to accept it as divinely
significant), refer it to the appropriate college for *remedia*, and perform the
prescribed ritual to restore the relationship. The system was reactive, not
prophetic: the goal was to restore a disturbed pax deorum (peace with the gods),
not to predict the future from omens.

**Sacrifice** (*sacrificium*): the standard act of religious communication. A
successful sacrifice was one where the victim's entrails showed no defect
(*litatio*). Repeated sacrifice until *litatio* was normal procedure — not a sign
of anxiety but of precision.

## Religion and Action: No Autonomous Religious Sphere

The most fundamental principle of Roman Republican religion is that **no public
action took place outside a religious framework**. There were no autonomous
secular spheres:

- **Warfare**: March and October framed the military year with festivals
  (the Salii, the armed processions, the October Horse sacrifice). A general
  took the *auspices* before battle; triumph ceremonies ended with offerings
  at Jupiter Capitolinus. The fetial rites gave war its moral and divine
  sanction.

- **Agriculture**: The agricultural calendar was dense with religious
  observance — the Robigalia (grain blight, April 25), the Parilia (purification
  of flocks, April 21), the Saturnalia (December), the *ambarvalia* (field
  purification). These were not separate from farming; they constituted the
  proper practice of it.

- **Governance**: The election of magistrates, the passing of laws, the
  declaration of war, the ratification of treaties — all embedded in auspicial
  procedure, vows, and sacrifices.

This integration means, as North stresses, that "there cannot be a separate
history of Roman religion in the way there can be a separate history of
Christianity." The religion was not a layer added to Roman society; it was
the medium through which Roman society organized itself.

## The Vestal Virgins

The **six Vestal Virgins** (*Vestales*) were the most liminal figures in Roman
religious life — they crossed the categories that normally separated religious
from social roles. They were:

- **Priests** (the only female priests in state cult; they required a quasi-male
  legal status, exempting them from normal *tutela*)
- **Virgins** (sexually inviolate — the survival of Rome was believed to depend
  on their chastity; a Vestal found guilty of unchastity was buried alive)
- **Fire-keepers** (the sacred fire on Vesta's hearth in the Forum; its extinction
  was a catastrophic omen requiring the *pontifex maximus*'s intervention)
- **Grain processors** (*mola salsa*, the sacred spelt-salt mixture used in all
  public sacrifice, was prepared by the Vestals)
- **Symbol of Rome's continuity** (the *pignora imperii* — pledge-objects on which
  Rome's survival depended — were in the Vestal's charge)

The special relationship between the *pontifex maximus* and the Vestals — he
inducted new Vestals, exercised disciplinary authority, acted with them in
certain rituals — is probably not a transfer of a royal prerogative but an
original feature of the pontifical college.

## Transition from Monarchy: The Religious Settlement of the Republic

The establishment of the Republic in the late sixth century BCE required a
solution to the religious dimension of kingship. North's analysis:

**The rex sacrorum**: The title *rex* was retained in Rome but stripped of
all political power. A patrician priest, chosen by the pontifical college for
life, bore the title *rex sacrorum* ("king for sacred rites") and performed
ritual duties on certain fixed dates. He was **excluded from all political office
and from the Senate** — the opposite of the major flamines, who participated in
political life. This was a deliberate prophylactic: whoever bore the title *rex*
would never again threaten the city with tyranny.

**The pontifex maximus vs. the rex**: Scholarly debate exists about whether
the *pontifex maximus* was made head of the college from the Republic's founding
or only gradually emerged as the dominant figure. North's conclusion: given how
the republican system actually worked — religious authority disseminated through
the senate and magistrates, the *pontifex* having access to all these areas while
the *rex* was excluded from politics — it was structurally *inevitable* that the
*pontifex maximus* would emerge as the more powerful figure over time, regardless
of anyone's original intentions.

**Jupiter Capitolinus and Etruscan continuity**: The most striking feature of
the religious transition is **what did not change**. The temple of Jupiter
Capitolinus — built by the last Tarquin, finished as the Republic was founded,
dedicated by the first Republican magistrates — became the *center* of
Republican religious life. Victorious generals returned there to lay their
laurels; the annual magistrates began their year there; the triumph ceremonial
involved the victor dressed and made up **in the guise of the king** and of
Jupiter's cult statue. This was not unconscious survival but the retention of
consciously regal ceremonial by the new regime.

The reason: the Etruscan cultural world had permeated Roman religion so deeply
that there was no alternative vocabulary. Jupiter was an ancient Latin deity.
The Romans were part of an Etruscan cultural milieu and could not simply reject
its religious forms without a vocabulary in which to replace them.

## Innovation and Change

North explicitly rejects the narrative that new cults represent confession of
failure — the desperate priests shoring up a collapsing edifice. **Innovation
was a normal mode of the system's functioning**, not a deviation from it. The
system was structurally flexible: it could accept foreign gods through *evocatio*
(summoning), through generals' vows, and through Sibylline Book recommendations,
integrating them into existing frameworks without threatening the whole.

Key examples of religious innovation in the period 400–220 BCE:

**Aesculapius (290s BCE)**: The Greek healing deity Asclepius was introduced,
his temple on the Tiber island dedicated c. 291 BCE. But healing cults with
female presiding deities were already widespread in Central Italy; what was
new may not have been the concept of a healing sanctuary but the specific Greek
male deity.

**Victoria (294 BCE)**: A temple to Victory (*Victoria*) was dedicated in 294
BCE — evidently derived from awareness of Greek Victory cults and the
invincibility of Alexander the Great. Roman war gods began attracting the titles
*Victor* and *Invictus* in the same period. The new goddess appeared immediately
on Roman coinage.

**Lectisternium (399 BCE)**: A ritual in which the statues of six paired deities
(three pairs: Apollo/Latona, Hercules/Diana, Mercury/Neptune) were brought out
and offered a feast — often interpreted as a great "turning point" toward
anthropomorphism. North is skeptical: Greek influence goes back more than a
century before 399 BCE; and even the meal offered follows the model of the
*epulum Iovis* (feast of Jupiter) at the Ludi Romani.

**Greek rites of Ceres** (third century BCE): A new set of observances for Ceres
(*Graeca sacra*) introduced before the Hannibalic Wars, centered on the
relationship of Ceres and Proserpina (Demeter and Persephone), with Greek
priestesses who were granted Roman citizenship. Unlike the traditional Ceres
cult, this new set focused on the women of the community — a significant
innovation in giving women a more prominent place in public religious
ceremonial.

**The Sibylline Books as innovation machine**: The Books legitimated new cults
by the authority of ancient oracular text and foreign (Greek) origin. They
offered *remedia* — not predictions but prescriptions for averting harm
signaled by prodigies. The senate's other foreign advisor, the **haruspices**
summoned from Etruscan cities, similarly offered interpretations of prodigies
— though the scope of Etruscan prophetic tradition was broader than what
appears in Roman public records.

## Social Conflict and Religion

North argues that given the total integration of religion into public life,
any sustained social conflict must eventually find religious expression. The
**Struggle of the Orders** had religious dimensions:

**The patrician monopoly**: Through the co-optation system (existing college
members chose new ones), the patricians dominated the major priestly colleges.
The Lex Ogulnia (300 BCE) broke this by reserving places in the augural and
pontifical colleges for plebeians — the colleges were each expanded to 8–9
members with guaranteed plebeian positions. The *pontifex maximus* election
reform (the first plebeian *pontifex maximus* was **Ti. Coruncanius**, c. 253
BCE) transferred selection from the college to a popular procedure.

**Plebeian religious initiatives**: The temple of Ceres, Liber, and Libera
(probably early fifth century, Sibylline recommendation) became the center
of the plebeian movement — the aediles took their title from it; its records
were stored there. The temple of Mercury, of the Dioscuri (Castores) — these
may reflect South Italian Greek contacts channeled through the plebeians.

**Cn. Flavius and priestly secrets** (304 BCE): In the censorship of Appius
Claudius Caecus, the freedman **Cn. Flavius** published the legal calendar
and the forms of actions (*legis actiones*) — previously a pontifical secret
allowing patricians to control legal procedure. There were also disputes about
temple dedication procedure. Together these suggest a late-fourth-century
moment of conflict over priestly authority that was eventually resolved through
the Lex Ogulnia and the election of the *pontifex maximus*.

**The *sacer* sanction**: Anyone who attempted tyranny could be declared *sacer*
— dedicated to the gods — meaning he could be killed without the killer incurring
any blood guilt. This was the religious equivalent of the political prohibition
on monarchy.

## The End of the Republican System: First Signs

North identifies the **Bacchanalian affair** (senatus consultum de Bacchanalibus,
186 BCE — just beyond this volume's scope) as the **first unmistakable sign of
religious transformation**. The senate's suppression of Bacchic worship across
Italy revealed, for the first time, **autonomous religious groups** — people
choosing religious affiliation outside the civic structures, capable of secret
organization across wide areas. This was entirely alien to the native tradition.

But the phenomenon was not sudden. Plautus (late third century) shows casual
awareness of Stoic and Epicurean ideas; there is archaeological evidence for
Bacchic cult in the second half of the third century. North's interpretation:
the second half of the third century — during and after the Hannibalic War —
saw profound changes of religious attitude in Italy that our historical sources
barely register. The transformation of the system (not its "decline") was under
way before Polybius wrote.

## Limitations and Critiques of North's Framework

**What North's approach achieves**: escapes the trap of retrojecting late-republican
"decline" onto the middle Republic; gives an accurate account of the structural
features of the system across the full period.

**What it risks**: by treating the system as broadly constant, it may underestimate
real change in the fifth–third centuries that simply leaves no trace. North is
aware of this ("it is not quite enough to say we have plenty of evidence of change
within the system, but none of deep change"). His answer is to remain agnostic
about specific early events rather than invent a narrative.

**The Dumézilian alternative**: Dumézil's structural-anthropological approach
(tripartite Indo-European ideology: sovereignty / warfare / fertility) found
expression in the Capitoline triad and the major flamines. North rejects it as
an "a priori scheme" imposed on the evidence. The debate is not resolved; North's
empiricism is now more influential in Anglo-American scholarship but Dumézil
retains adherents in French classical studies.

## Religion in the Ciceronian age: continuity and change (CAH IX, Ch. 19, Beard)

Mary Beard's chapter on the **late Republic (146–44)** restates and sharpens the
"embedded" thesis and resists the old narrative of religious **decline**:

- **Religion was embedded in politics, not a separate "establishment religion."**
  Its centre was "politics, military activity, and public life" — the gods actively
  ensured Rome's safety and victory in return for correct ritual (the reversible axiom:
  Rome's *failures* implied a lapse in cult). The **Senate** was the principal religious
  authority; the priestly colleges were repositories of **knowledge**, not power;
  priests were the **same men** who held the magistracies. Political action was
  physically located in **religious space** (the Senate met in a *templum*; the rostra
  was a *templum*). Beard stresses the **alienness** of this undifferentiated system to
  the modern mind.
- **Public, not private, belief.** Roman religion was "ideologically committed to the
  public sphere"; private devotion (votives, *lararia*, the cult of Ceres among the
  poor) existed but was drawn into civic display. **Personal "faith" is the wrong
  category** — for nearly all Romans the gods "simply and self-evidently existed."
- **Against the "decline" myth.** The image of late-Republican religion as chaotic and
  neglected is, Beard argues, an artefact of the **sources**: Cicero reports only
  *anomaly and crisis* (undigested, contemporary), where Livy gives ordered hindsight
  and the Augustan poets construct a "convenient foil" of late-Republican impiety to
  flatter the **Augustan restoration**. In fact temples (Pompey's Venus Victrix,
  Hercules, Minerva; the rebuilt Capitol) continued to be founded and repaired.
- **Disruption as the failure of old rules in unprecedented situations.** The real
  late-Republican strain was not impiety but the **impossibility of resolving new cases
  by traditional rules** — e.g. **Bibulus** (59) "watching the sky" by message against
  [[julius-caesar|Caesar's]] laws (was *obnuntiatio* valid if not announced in person?),
  and the **Bona Dea** sacrilege of [[catiline|Clodius]] (62/61). Both show the system
  **still being applied**, not abandoned — its conventions simply had no answer for the
  violence and one-man power of the age (culminating in the **deification of Caesar**).

Beard's governing theme is the **active interrelationship of continuity and change**:
superficially unchanging rituals (the Parilia) acquired wholly new meanings (the
birthday of Rome; Caesar's Munda victory), while continuity of attitude was upheld
precisely *through* adaptation of form.

## OHCW (Stockton; Lyne — bridge to Augustus)

**Stockton** presents Augustan temple rebuilds, Secular Games, and moral legislation as claiming to **restore** the high seriousness of old Republican cult and *mos maiorum* after civil-war neglect — the “restoration” that Price (CAH X) re-reads as restructuring. **Lyne** notes that poets of the first Augustan period negotiated that claim (Horace public Odes; Propertian irony; Ovid embodying the tide Augustus tried to stem). OHCW thus supplies the late-Republican/Augustan hinge rather than new Republican-period evidence. Source: [[boardman-oxford-history-classical-world-1986]] (Stockton Ch. 22; Lyne Ch. 24).
