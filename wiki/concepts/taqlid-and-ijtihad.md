## Definition

- **Taqlīd**: Literally "imitation" or "following." In Islamic legal thought, acceptance of the rulings of earlier legal scholars without independently verifying their grounds. In the broader epistemological sense, adopting beliefs on the basis of authority rather than personal rational verification.
- **Ijtihād**: Independent legal or rational judgment. Deriving rulings directly from the Koran, ḥadīth, and other primary sources through one's own effort of understanding.

The classical claim that the "gate of ijtihād had closed" (*insidād bāb al-ijtihād*) — that sufficiently qualified independent legal reasoning was no longer possible after the formation of the main Sunni legal schools — was one of the most contested ideas in later Islamic intellectual history. Many later thinkers regarded this claim as spurious, an excuse for intellectual ossification.

Sources: [[adamson-philosophy-islamic-world-2016]], Ch. 58.

## The Epistemological Dimension

Beyond the legal sphere, the taqlīd/ijtihād debate had a broader epistemological dimension: on what grounds can a religious believer claim certainty in their beliefs?

**"Externalist" defense of taqlīd**: Beliefs can be certain even if adopted by imitation, provided the teachers being imitated had unimpeachable access to the truth (as did Muḥammad through prophetic revelation). The grounds of certainty are *external* to the individual believer. Analogously: your conviction that Madagascar exists, based on the testimony of reliable sources, can count as knowledge even without personal verification.

**"Internalist" critique of taqlīd**: Certainty requires that you reflect on your beliefs and establish for yourself that they must be true. Al-Sanūsī's formulation was extreme: "lowly taqlīd is at the root of the unbelief of the idolaters." For him, *kalām* (theological rational discourse) was not an optional refinement for scholars but an obligation for every Muslim.

This debate mirrors the internalist/externalist dispute in modern epistemology, which asks whether the conditions for justified belief must be internally accessible to the believer or may be external (e.g., the reliability of the belief-forming process).

## Key Positions

### Al-Sanūsī (Moroccan, fl. 15th c.)

The Moroccan theologian Muḥammad al-Sanūsī argued the maximally rationalist position: every Muslim must engage in rational reflection (*naẓar*) to confer certainty on their religious beliefs. Taqlīd, however humble, undermines genuine faith. His influence spread from the Maghreb to Cairo and into the Ottoman empire, where even thinkers associated with the ostensibly anti-rationalist Ḳāḍīzādelī movement quoted him approvingly.

### The Ḳāḍīzādelī Movement (Ottoman, 17th c.)

Despite their reputation as anti-rationalists, the Ḳāḍīzādelīs (see [[ottoman-philosophy]]) endorsed mathematics, astronomy, medicine, and the philosophical ideas already incorporated into Islamic theology. Their hostility was directed at perceived innovations in practice and Sufi excesses, not at rational inquiry as such.

### Kātib Çelebī (Ottoman, 17th c.)

In *Mīzān al-ḥaqq* (The Balance of Truth), Kātib Çelebī insisted on the value of traditional scholarly disciplines — logic, mathematics, astronomy — as indispensable for any Muslim jurist. He expressed concern that philosophy had retreated in the madrasas but blamed intellectual complacency rather than any principled anti-rationalism.

### [[muhammad-abduh]] (Egyptian, 19th c.)

Abduh condemned taqlīd as the fundamental obstacle to Islamic renewal. He saw the fatalism and passivity of contemporary Muslim societies as a consequence of blind following of precedent, and argued that Islam requires individual rational action and reform.

### [[muhammad-iqbal]] (Indian, early 20th c.)

Iqbāl connected the taqlīd problem to his broader political philosophy: Islam's flexibility and adaptability to historical circumstances requires each generation to engage in genuine *ijtihād*, not slavish adherence to precedent. He referenced Shāh Walī Allāh's related arguments (*Reconstruction*, pp. 92, 115, 163).

## Historical Trajectory

The debate intensified in the colonial period, when the perceived weakness of Muslim societies relative to European powers was often attributed — by reformers — to intellectual stagnation caused by taqlīd. The reformist solution was usually some form of renewed ijtihād, though reformers disagreed sharply about what this would look like in practice. [[islamic-modernism]] thinkers like Abduh argued for returning to the original sources of Islam rather than following the medieval schools; more traditionalist scholars defended taqlīd as preserving the accumulated wisdom of generations of jurists.
