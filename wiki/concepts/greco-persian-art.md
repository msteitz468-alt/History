# Greco-Persian Art

**"Greco-Persian art"** is the modern label for the **hybrid artistic idiom of the
western [[achaemenid-empire|Achaemenid]] satrapies** — chiefly Anatolia — in which
**Persian, Greek (Ionian) and local Anatolian** elements fused, above all in **funerary
sculpture, wall-painting and seal-engraving** of the fifth and fourth centuries BCE.
[[boardman-cah-v4-1988|CAH Vol. IV]] (Ch. 3e, Mellink) documents it as the visible trace
of Persian rule on a society it otherwise barely reorganized.

## What it is

An **etic** (art-historian's) category, not an ancient term. It names the art produced
when **local Anatolian noblemen who collaborated with the Persian regime** commissioned
monuments that mixed:

- **Persian content and manners** — banqueting, processions of chariots and horsemen,
  the hunt, figures in trousers, *kandys* and *bashlyk*, the topknotted "Persian" horse
  of the Persepolis reliefs, the occasional fire-cult rite;
- **Greek (Ionian) technique and style** — executed by **Ionian and Lydian artists** (with
  some Attic help), often in flat, paint-dependent relief;
- **Anatolian burial tradition** — the funerary banquet and procession, set before
  **chamber-tombs and tumuli** in the native manner, the **burial customs themselves
  remaining un-Persianized**.

## Where it appears

- **Dascylium** ([[dascylium]]) — the type-site: painted **tomb-stelae** of officials and
  noblemen, and the **bullae** of the satrapal archive (the "royal hero" and lion-griffin).
- **The Karaburun tomb** near Elmali (northern Lycia, the Milyad, c. 475 BCE) — the
  richest example: wall-paintings of a banquet, a chariot procession, and a **battle in
  which the local lord spears a Greek hoplite from horseback** — advertising his role as
  a Persian ally; he wears purple Persian dress, his wife "looks entirely Greek."
- **The Lydian–Phrygian tumuli** (Güre/Ikiztepe, Çectepe near Celaenae) — looted
  chamber-tombs with painted *klinai*, chariot reliefs, and hoards of Achaemenid **silver
  vessels** (omphalos bowls, incense-burners) beside Lydian and Phrygian graffiti.
- **[[sardis]]** and the wider arc "**from Thrace to inner Lycia**," including the
  fire-cult altar from Bünyan in Cappadocia and the Paphlagonian rock-cut tomb façades.

## Significance

Greco-Persian art is the **measure of how Persia actually ruled western Anatolia**: not
by colonization or cultural imposition, but through a **native nobility** that "compromised
with Persian fashions" while keeping its art tied to Greece. It is the **provincial
counterpart**, on a humbler level, to the grand synthesis achieved under royal auspices at
**Persepolis** — itself built in part by the Ionian, Lydian and Carian craftsmen the Susa
foundation-charters name. Mellink makes Sardis's earlier **Ionian–Lydian–Achaemenid
*koine*** the seedbed from which "the art of the Persian empire owed a basic debt."

## Limitations

The category lumps together work of varying date, quality and ethnic authorship, and the
line between a Persian official's tomb and a Persianizing local lord's is often
unclear (the Aramaic-inscribed stelae may mark the former). It privileges **funerary**
evidence because that is what survives; the satrapal palaces, archives and furnishings
that would show the full picture are mostly undug or looted.
