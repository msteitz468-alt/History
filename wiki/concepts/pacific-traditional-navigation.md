# Pacific Traditional Navigation

## Definition and Scope

**Pacific traditional navigation** — also called **wayfinding** — is the body of Indigenous knowledge systems by which Pacific Island peoples traversed the world's largest ocean across thousands of years without metal instruments, charts, or fixed external reference systems. It encompasses: celestial navigation (star compass, rising and setting points of stars and star-groups); swell and wave-piloting; wind-position systems; environmental reading (clouds, birds, fish, phosphorescence, currents); vessel design adapted to these methods; and the social, ritual, and pedagogical structures through which the knowledge was held, transmitted, and legitimized. These systems enabled the settlement of every habitable island across an ocean spanning more than a third of the earth's surface — "an exploratory and migratory feat unmatched in human history" ([[jones-matsuda-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v1-2022]], ch. 14, Nuttall et al.).

The concept is **emic**: the navigational traditions described here were developed and named by Pacific peoples. Western scholars have given them analytical labels ("deictic," "star compass," "etak"), but the underlying systems predate Western contact by millennia.

## The Deictic Frame: The Navigator as Fixed Point

Pacific navigational systems are fundamentally different in epistemological structure from Western positional navigation (latitude/longitude grids). They are **deictic**: "the navigator is the (only) fixed point in the world, and the world flows past — a fitting conceptualization for a maritime system" ([[jones-matsuda-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v1-2022]], ch. 14, citing Diaz 2011). Because there are no landmarks at sea, fixed-grid mapping is functionally useless; instead, the navigator learns a specific **seaway** to a specific destination — the winds, the swell pattern, the starpath — and the reciprocal signs for return. Accumulated experience of individual routes builds up into a "rhizomatic network" of interconnected seaways; at the edge of a navigator's range, local knowledge is exchanged with navigators of neighbouring routes.

## The Star Compass

Micronesian and Polynesian traditions both organize celestial navigation around a **star compass** — a set of reference points defined by the rising and setting positions of named stars around the horizon. In the Caroline Islands tradition described by master navigator Hilary Larry Raigetal, "the star compass is comprised of sixteen star reference points that rise in the east and set in the west, in addition to the stationary Polaris, or North Star" ([[jones-matsuda-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v1-2022]], ch. 15). At the equator, the rising of **mailap** (part of the Altair constellation) divides the northern and southern hemispheres; Polaris and the Southern Cross at its zenith divide east from west. On each island, a **star chart** specifying where all thirty-two compass stars point to is memorized by navigators: "These charts allow one to know from a position on any given island where all thirty-two stars on his star compass are pointing to."

A complementary tradition, Te Nohoanga Te Matangi (TNTM), used on Taumako in the Solomon Islands, names **thirty-two wind positions** around the navigator's horizon and inter-relates them with "calendrics (seasons, solstices, and equinoxes), the rises and sets of asterisms (stars, star groups, celestial bodies, dark shapes in the sky), swell patterns, appearances of light patterns that show the way to land routes between islands, special winds and weather, animal and plant behaviours" ([[jones-matsuda-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v1-2022]], ch. 14). The system is not merely mnemonic: "the navigator's relationship with those phenomena is that of a descendant to ancestors and so the inter-relationships between the navigator and the ancestors is dynamic in the system. The voyagers are responsible to be respectful of all phenomena, and the phenomena are willing and proactive in guiding the voyagers." Sustainability is structurally embedded in the ontology.

## Swell and Wave Piloting

Stars are unavailable in cloud or daylight; swell patterns supply the complementary system. In Caroline Islands navigation, "eight certain wave patterns along with two general ocean currents whose positions are learned relative to that of the moon" are mastered ([[jones-matsuda-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v1-2022]], ch. 15). Swells are generated by distant weather systems and persist even when winds change; a skilled navigator reads their direction against the vessel's motion to maintain a consistent bearing even in overcast conditions. Environmental indicators also include "twenty stars that are associated with weather," and specific birds and fish (**epar**) that serve as positional markers: "Over many centuries, certain fish and birds have been identified at certain locations and become known to navigators as epar. They are given names and are used to guide navigators to their destinations."

## The Deep-Water Sailing Canoe

Pacific navigational systems were inseparable from the vessel designs that made them operational. Across the ocean a diverse range of craft evolved, all adapted to shallow coasts and coral reefs (requiring minimal draught), long blue-water passages (requiring stability and speed), and construction from locally available organic materials. Nuttall et al. characterize these designs: "Sails are to the Pacific Islands as the horse was to continents; the sailing vessel is as the wheel" ([[jones-matsuda-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v1-2022]], ch. 14).

Two primary design lineages existed, and a hybrid:

**The Proa / Flying Proa** — The epitome of Pacific design efficiency. Asymmetrical, double-ended, with a single main hull and an outrigger or smaller second hull **always to windward**. To change direction relative to wind, the Proa **shunts**: the sail is moved from one end (old bow) to the other (new bow). "Shunting is a mode of sailing that is unique to Pacific and Indonesian craft." The windward side of the hull is differently shaped from the leeward side: "flat and the latter curved. This has the effect of hydrodynamically balancing the drag of the outrigger and of 'crabbing' the hull to windward, increasing windward efficiency well beyond that capable in a catamaran design." The flying proa could sail closer to the wind than any European vessel — 50 degrees off the wind vs. the catamaran's 70 degrees — and substantially closer than the 32.5 degrees of a sophisticated modern racing yacht.

Key variants: central Carolines **Wa** (Micronesia); Chamorro **Sakman** (Marianas); Marshall Islands **Walap**; Tongan **Kalia**; Fijian **Drua** (see also below).

**The Drua/Kalia/'Alia complex** — A hybrid double-hull form that emerged in central Oceania (Fiji/Tonga/Samoa), probably between 1600 and 1750 CE, combining "the naval architecture excellence of the Proa form, the maritime mechanics of Fiji, the navigation and seafaring expertise of Tonga, and the seamless planking skills of Samoan shipwrights" ([[jones-matsuda-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v1-2022]], ch. 14). Kalia in Tonga were built up to **36 metres long**, drew less than a metre, and were capable of more than **15 knots**; capable of carrying up to 50 tonnes and well over 100–300 warriors. Construction depended on **vesi loa** (Intsia bijuga) timber from the southern Lau Islands — "the titanium of the Pacific." Construction could take up to seven years; only a chief of means could assemble the community-wide workforce. "The sailing craft was the greatest asset a community could possess. It was a central aspect of all culture, imbued with sacredness."

**TePuke of Taumako** — the Vaka o Lata (voyaging canoe of Lata) from the Santa Cruz Islands, Solomon Islands. Double-ended with a Proa rig; a main hull of hardwood tamanu (Callophylum inophylum) run to submarine trim; outrigged with variable floats for load conditions. "TePuke carry heavy loads, like trucks." Measured cruising speed of 10 knots in 15–20 knot winds; TeAlo Lili (the offshore variant) clocked at over **15 knots** in 30-knot side-wind. The TePuke represents an "unparalleled window into ancient voyaging practice": the design, materials (timber, sennit, woven pandanus sail), and thirty-step construction process link directly to prehistoric voyaging technology.

All designs shared core features: **shallow draught** (accessible over reefs and beaches; no deep harbour required); **organic materials** (locally sourced timber, coconut-fibre cordage, woven leaf sails — entirely renewable); **flex-lashing construction** rather than rigid joinery, allowing hulls to move with waves; and the capacity to be hauled ashore in storms or flooded and allowed to sit semi-submerged until seas calmed.

## The Pedagogical System: Knowledge Transmission

Pacific traditional navigation was not a discrete technical skill but a nested system of knowledge requiring mastery of interconnected domains. In the Caroline Islands, the knowledge of the voyaging crew was organized around **ten temairong** (sources of all knowledge), each holding a distinct specialization: the chief (Tamol), master navigator (Palulap), weatherman (Tauheman), spirit man (Tauyalus), herbal medicine man (Tautafey), master carver (Senap), master builder (Taupwang), masseuse (Tausheo), diviner (Taubwe), and thinker (Tauyan) ([[jones-matsuda-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v1-2022]], ch. 15, Raigetal). These roles directly mapped onto crew positions on the canoe — the chief and the medicine/spirit specialists on the highest platform (epep); the navigator, weatherman and diviner on the main hull (buunel waa); the carver, thinker, and builder on the outrigger platform (taam).

A candidate for the **pwo** (master navigator initiation ceremony) was expected to master **all ten** domains, not just navigation. The process spanned decades: Raigetal himself required more than twenty years of study before receiving pwo in 2016 under the Weriyeng school. The canoe house (not only a storage facility but the classroom) was the foundational institution; learning began for boys "at the early age of six."

Knowledge was proprietary: held within clans and families, transmitted only with explicit consent and customary payment. Exceptions required compelling justification — which is precisely what made Mau Piailug's decision to teach Hawaiians in 1975 (see [[processes/pacific-navigation-revival]]) so fraught and culturally significant.

## Chronological Scope and Settlement Capacity

Pacific navigational traditions enabled the settlement of the ocean in successive phases. Nuttall et al. reconstruct the timeline from archaeological and genetic evidence: First colonists arrived in the northwest Pacific (now Micronesia) and Solomons/Vanuatu/New Caledonia chains "some 3,000–4,000 years ago"; central Oceania archipelagoes (Fiji, Sāmoa, Tonga) "over 3,000 years ago"; and "from there throughout eastern Polynesia beginning some 2,000 years ago, with the final outliers of Hawai'i, Rapa Nui, and Aotearoa reached between 700 and 1200 CE" ([[jones-matsuda-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v1-2022]], ch. 14). By 800 BCE Pacific ancestors had arrived in the Marshall Islands. By about 1000 CE Polynesians were in Hawai'i (over 2,000 nm from the Marquesas); by 1200 CE in Aotearoa (1,600 nm from Rarotonga). "Five hundred years ago, when Columbus sailed across the Atlantic, the Moriori had reached Rēkohu (Chatham Island), over 300 nm to the east of Aotearoa New Zealand, and the colonisation of the entire Pacific Ocean was complete."

Evidence suggests some groups reached South America, carrying back the sweet potato and the bottle gourd, now staples across the Pacific.

## Limitations and Critiques

- The terms "canoe" and "wayfinding" carry diminutive connotations that systematically understate the technology. Nuttall et al. note that "the signifier 'canoe' has been used generically since European contact to describe Indigenous craft, sailed or paddled, and is now so ingrained in the literature as to defy rebranding — even though the term implies insubstantial craft rather than the often massive, blue-water, long-range, planked-hull sailing vessels." The authors prefer "vessel" and "sailcraft."
- Western fascination with long-range Polynesian migrations (Hawai'i, Rapa Nui, Aotearoa) has overshadowed the broader Pacific maritime culture, particularly in Micronesia and Melanesia, where different but equally sophisticated traditions survived longer.
- The "out of Taiwan 3,000 years ago" origin narrative for Austronesian settlement is under pressure from contemporary research; Nuttall et al. note that "the authors remain agnostic on this subject."
- Oral and performative transmission means that much navigational knowledge has been irreversibly lost during the colonial period; what survives is a fragment of what existed.
