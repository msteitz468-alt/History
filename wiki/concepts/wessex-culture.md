# Wessex Culture

The **Wessex culture** (c. 1900–1550 B.C.) refers to the rich individual burials of the Early Bronze Age in Wessex (southern Britain, especially around Stonehenge and the chalk lands), characterized by inhumations accompanied by collared urns, personal ornaments of gold, amber, shale, copper, jet, and faience, bronze weapons (daggers, axes), and distinctive goldwork with linear geometric patterns incised into hammered sheets. It marks a phase of conspicuous elite display linked to control of prestige goods and metals at the junction of metal-rich western Britain and consumers in central eastern Britain and Europe.

## Definition and Scope

" This information formed the basis of studies by Stuart Piggott and others that helped define the Wessex culture of the Early Bronze Age, which lasted from c. 1900 to 1550 B.C. Corpses were inhumed in burial pits accompanied by collared urns, a variety of small vessels used for offerings and incense, and personal ornaments, which sometimes were made of valuable amber, shale, copper, gold, and jet. Many of the finest objects were fashioned from exotic materials, some of which have electrostatic properties (materials that can take an electrical charge and spark, such as amber and coal shale). Bronze weapons and tools, including daggers and axes, were buried with the dead and provide a means of relative dating and sequencing. The goldwork of the Wessex tombs is especially distinctive, with linear geometric patterns incised into sheets of hammered gold. Particularly rich burials are known from Bush Barrow and Upton Lovell as well as farther afield." (verbatim from range 02, Bogucki & Crabtree 2004, lines ~545–570).

The culture is closely tied to the Stonehenge landscape, where ~500 round barrows were built in the Early Bronze Age in groups at prominent locations (e.g. King Barrow Ridge linear cemeteries, Normanton Down with >25 barrows including Bush Barrow, Winterbourne Stoke).

## Key Developments

- Individual rather than collective burial, with personal status display via exotic materials and gold.
- Part of broader European increase in social stratification signaled by rich burials in the Early Bronze Age.
- Linked to metalworking development, trade/exchange networks, and control of prestige goods distribution.
- Rich burials disappear by end of Early Bronze Age, but high-quality metalwork production and deposition (rivers, lakes, bogs) continues as conspicuous consumption by high-status persons in Middle/Late Bronze Age.

## Historiography and Links

Originally defined from 19th-century excavations of barrows (Piggott 1935). In this encyclopedia framed within the transition from collective Neolithic monuments to hierarchical Early Bronze Age societies, with Wessex at the intersection of western metal sources and eastern/continental consumers. Cross-references broader Bronze Age Britain and Ireland entry (field systems, hoards, settlement hierarchy at sites like Runnymede).

As of this ingest, treated as regional expression of Bronze Age social differentiation (see related process pages). May contrast or overlap with beaker-phenomenon and corded-ware-culture in earlier phases; part of the same trajectory toward individual prestige burial.
