# The Coming of the Greeks and the Prehistory of the Greek Language

When, how, and from where did **Greek-speakers** arrive in Greece — and how did the
historical Greek dialects (Doric, Ionic, Aeolic, Arcado-Cypriot) come to be
distributed as they were? This is the linguistic counterpart of the Aegean
archaeological record, and it bears directly on the [[mycenaean-civilization|
Mycenaean]] world, the [[dorian-invasion-1120bce|Dorian invasion]], and the
[[indo-european-migrations|Indo-European migrations]]. [[edwards-cah-v2p2-1975|CAH
Vol. II.2]] (Ch. XXXIX(a), John Chadwick — co-decipherer of Linear B) sets out the
modern reconstruction, which substantially **revised the older "three waves" model**.

## Three sources of evidence

Greek before the eighth century BCE can be reconstructed only by combining:

1. the **classical dialects** worked backward from early inscriptions;
2. the **[[aegean-scripts|Linear B]]** tablets (Mycenaean Greek, c. 1400–1200 BCE),
   shown by Ventris and Chadwick (1952) to be Greek;
3. the **comparison with related Indo-European languages**, back to reconstructed
   Proto-Indo-European.

"Satisfactory deductions about the prehistoric period are impossible without at least
two sources."

## The classification of the dialects

The ancient triad (Doric, Ionic, Aeolic) is refined by modern scholarship into:

- **West Greek** — **Doric** (the Peloponnese, Crete, Melos, Thera, Rhodes, Cos, the
  Carian coast) and **Northwest-Greek** (Phocis, Locris, Aetolia, Elis). The dialect
  of the [[dorians|Dorian]] and allied invaders.
- **East Greek** — **Arcado-Cypriot** (the conservative remnant of Mycenaean,
  surviving in landlocked Arcadia and in distant Cyprus, "out of touch for at least
  five centuries" yet astonishingly alike) and **Attic-Ionic** (Athens and the
  [[ionian-migration-1050bce|Ionian]] coast).
- **Aeolic** — Lesbian, Thessalian, Boeotian: a looser group, probably a distinct
  Mycenaean-era unit (located perhaps in Thessaly).

The decipherment confirmed that **Mycenaean Greek** belongs with East Greek
(Arcado-Cypriot): it already shows the East-Greek change of *-ti* to *-si* (e.g.
*e-ko-si* = *echonsi*) by 1400 BCE, while West Greek kept the original *-ti*
(*echonti*). So **two dialects (East and West) demonstrably existed by 1400 BCE**.

## The revision of the "three waves" theory

The older model imagined the Greek language formed *outside* Greece and brought in by
**three successive invasions** — Ionians, then Achaeans (Arcado-Cypriot + Aeolic),
then Dorians. Chadwick (following Porzig and Risch) argues this is "unnecessary":

- The most characteristic features of **Ionic** (e.g. the shift of *ā* to *ē*) are
  demonstrably **post-Mycenaean** — so Ionic was *not* a separate dialect in Mycenaean
  times, but **diverged from the common Mycenaean (East-Greek) dialect** only during
  and after the Dark Age migrations.
- There is **no archaeological intrusion** of a new culture between the start of the
  Middle Helladic and the end of the Late Helladic — except the *destruction* of
  Mycenaean civilization, which had nothing to put in its place.
- Many of Greek's borrowed words (place-names in **-ssos / -nthos**, plant- and
  animal-names like *kyparissos*, *terebinthos*) come from a **pre-Greek Mediterranean
  language** and were taken up *in Greece* — so "to ask when the Greeks reached Greece
  may well be a meaningless question."

**The economical hypothesis:** a **single intrusion of Indo-European speakers** around
the **Early Helladic III / Middle Helladic transition (c. 2100–1900 BCE**, possibly
raised to ~2300), who — fused with the earlier **pre-Greek Mediterranean population**
— produced *Greek itself inside the southern Balkan area*. The dialects then
differentiated **within Greece**, the great divergence coming with the breakdown of
the Mycenaean koine at the [[dorian-invasion-1120bce|Dorian invasion]] and the
attendant migrations.

## The Blegen–Haley place-name argument

The non-Greek place-names (those in -ssos/-nthos — Knossos, Halicarnassos, Corinthos,
Mount Hymettos) correlate with **Early Helladic** sites and with **no later**
prehistoric horizon (Blegen & Haley, 1928). This locates the **pre-Greek population**
in EH I–II and dates the arrival of Greek-speakers to the violent break at the end of
the Early Helladic period — the linguistic anchor for "the coming of the Greeks."

## Significance

The page underwrites three things across the wiki: (1) the **[[mycenaean-civilization|
Mycenaeans]] were Greeks** speaking an East-Greek dialect, read directly in
[[aegean-scripts|Linear B]]; (2) the **[[dorian-invasion-1120bce|Dorian invasion]]**
is independently demanded by the West-Greek/East-Greek divide, even apart from the
legends; and (3) the **dialect map** of classical Greece is the residue of Dark Age
movements, not of separate invasions from outside. It is also a model case of
historical method — combining philology, archaeology, and a deciphered script — and
connects to the [[indo-european-migrations|Indo-European]] question and the standing
controversy on the [[origins-of-the-indo-europeans|origins of the Indo-Europeans]].

## Limitations and critique

The "single intrusion ~2100 BCE" view is the volume's preferred hypothesis, not a
certainty: the date of the Greek arrival, the precise EH III vs MH break, and the
relation of the Aeolic group remain debated, and "no reliable estimate can be made on
linguistic grounds" of how long dialect divergence took. Later scholarship continues
to argue the absolute date (and some still defend a later, second-millennium arrival).
The reconstruction is also only as good as the meagre, ambiguous Linear B evidence for
non-Attic dialects.
