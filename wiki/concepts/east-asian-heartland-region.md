# The East Asian Heartland Region

An **analytical-geographic frame** introduced by [[major-cook-ancient-china-2017|Major & Cook
(2017)]] to describe the stage of early Chinese history *before* there was a "China." Because
the name **"China" derives from the state of [[qin-dynasty|Qin]]** (pronounced "chin") and only
becomes meaningful with the [[qin-unification-221-bce|unification of 221 BCE]], the authors
avoid projecting it back onto the diverse prehistoric and Bronze Age cultures of the region.
The Heartland Region is their substitute unit of analysis.

## Definition (emic to the source / etic to history)

- The **East Asian Heartland Region** proper is the grain-growing zone of the **basins of the
  eastward-flowing [[yellow-river-valley|Yellow]] and Yangzi Rivers and the Sichuan Basin** —
  divided by the [[qinling-mountains|Qinling]] climate barrier into a **northern zone** (loess,
  continental, millet/wheat/soybeans, compact walled settlements, road travel) and a **southern
  zone** (monsoon, rice, boat travel).
- The **Extended East Asian Heartland Region** is the surrounding ring of forests, grasslands,
  deserts, mountains, high plateaus, and subtropical jungles — the [[steppe|steppe]], the
  northeast, the northwest, and the south — through which technologies and peoples reached the
  core.

The frame is deliberately **not** the modern PRC: at least 55 languages are still spoken within
China's borders today, and ancient ethno-linguistic diversity was almost certainly greater.

## The Two Myths It Rebuts

Major & Cook deploy the Heartland concept against two named folk-historiographical myths:

- The **"myth of isolated China"** — that the region evolved walled off from outside influence.
  Against it they marshal the early arrival, from western Asia via [[silk-roads|Central Asian and
  steppe]] intermediaries, of **bronze metallurgy** (c. 2200 BCE), the **chariot and horsemanship
  complex** (c. 1300 BCE), and **wheat** — all *before* the formal Silk Road. See
  [[shang-dynasty|Shang]] contacts and [[sanxingdui|Sanxingdui]].
- The **"myth of unchanging China"** — which "confuses continuity with stasis." Cultural and
  graphic continuity (e.g. the [[oracle-bone-divination|oracle-bone]]-descended script) is real,
  but the region's political and social forms changed continually.

Both myths, they argue, are products of 19th–20th-century European and American historiography.

## Relation to Other Frames

The Heartland frame aligns with the **multi-regional** reading of Chinese origins — the
[[quxi-leixing|quxi leixing]] model and [[liu-chinese-neolithic-2004|Liu (2004)]] — against the
old [[central-plains-china|Central Plains]]-centric, monocentric nationalist narrative (see
[[nationalism-and-archaeology]] and the
[[origins-chinese-civilization-monocentric-vs-multiregional|origins debate]]). It complements
[[china|the China anchor page]], which carries the post-unification dynastic story the term
"China" properly names.

## Limitations

As a teaching device the term risks substituting one essentialized container ("Heartland") for
another ("China"); critics could note that the grain-basin definition still privileges the
agrarian core over the steppe and southern peoples it brackets as "Extended." Major & Cook use
it heuristically, not as a bounded culture.
