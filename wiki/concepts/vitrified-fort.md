# Vitrified Fort

A **vitrified fort** is a type of hillfort whose exterior wall face has been smoothed and fused together by intense heat and wind, producing a glassy or slag-like surface.

## Definition and Scope

The glossary entry provides the precise definition used by the encyclopedia:

> "vitriﬁed fort: A hillfort with an exterior wall face that is smoothed and fused together by heat and wind." (range_08:70–71)

The fusion is generally interpreted as the result of deliberate or accidental firing of the timber-laced rampart, in which the stone facing and rubble core were subjected to temperatures high enough to melt and vitrify the siliceous components. The resulting wall surface is extremely hard and resistant to erosion.

## Construction and Interpretation

Vitrified forts are a subset of the broader class of timber-laced or "murus gallicus"-style fortifications common in Iron Age Europe. The vitrification process:
- Requires substantial timber framing within the wall.
- Produces a fused outer skin that may have had both structural and symbolic/display functions.
- Is most famously documented in Scotland (e.g. Tap o' Noth, Craig Phadrig) but occurs more widely in temperate Europe.

Debate exists over whether the firing was intentional (to strengthen the wall or as a ritual act) or the result of destruction by fire during attack. The glossary definition remains neutral on causation.

## Relation to Other Concepts

- Part of the spectrum of Iron Age defensive architecture alongside ordinary hillforts, oppida, and Viereckschanzen.
- Linked to [[concepts/hallstatt-culture]] and [[concepts/la-tene-culture]] in regions where timber-laced construction was practiced.
- Contrasts with Mediterranean-style stone or mud-brick fortifications (cf. Heuneburg mud-brick phase).
- Related to glossary terms "wattle and daub" (for internal construction) and "tuyere" (metalworking parallels in high-temperature processes).

## Geographic and Chronological Scope

Primarily Early Iron Age (Hallstatt/La Tène equivalent), with some possible Late Bronze Age antecedents. Strongest concentration in the British Isles (especially Scotland) and parts of central and western Europe. The phenomenon is absent or rare in the Mediterranean and steppe zones covered by the encyclopedia.

## Historiography

Vitrified forts attracted antiquarian attention in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries because of their striking appearance. Modern excavation and experimental archaeology (firing of reconstructed timber-laced walls) have clarified the physical processes. The 2004 glossary offers a concise, descriptive definition without committing to a single explanatory model.

## Collection Coverage Note

The encyclopedia's strong coverage of northern and western European Iron Age fortifications includes vitrified forts as a regionally distinctive variant. Complements discussion of hillforts (Maiden Castle) and oppida (Heuneburg, Bibracte, Manching) elsewhere in the work.
