## Definition and Emic/Etic Distinction

**Colonial Andean tribute** refers to the mandatory fiscal extraction imposed by the Spanish colonial state on the indigenous population of the Andean viceroyalty (broadly the former [[inca-empire|Inca Empire]]). It is both an emic category — indigenous people experienced, resisted, and sometimes accepted the tribute as part of a negotiated "pact" with the crown — and an etic analytical concept used by historians to describe the primary mechanism of colonial surplus extraction.

The tribute system had several components that must be distinguished:

- **The *tasa* (quota):** The scheduled levy specifying what each [[ayllu]] or [[reduccion|reduccion]] owed collectively and individually, expressed in cash, goods, or labor.
- **The *originario/forastero* distinction:** The fundamental census category dividing tribute payers. *Originarios* ("natives") were registered members of a community who held land rights in the [[ayllu]]; they owed full tribute. *Forasteros* ("outsiders," "strangers") were migrants who had settled outside their natal community. Their tribute obligations were initially lower, which made *forastero* status an attractive evasion strategy.
- **The *cédula de cobranza*:** The official collection warrant issued to [[curaca|caciques]] authorizing and requiring tribute collection from specified individuals or households.
- **Yanacona and mitayo categories:** *Yanacona* (servitors detached from communities, living on estates or in towns) and *mitayos* (draft laborers at mines) had distinct tributary statuses, complicating both collection and census.

## The Toledan Formula and Its Internal Contradiction

Viceroy [[toledo-francisco-de|Francisco de Toledo]] designed the tribute as a deliberate hybrid: "The tribute was determined on the basis of the amount each *repartimiento* (fiscal district) had to pay ... and then it was determined what each individual Indian had to pay according to his name and his ayllu ... and he was taxed in the form of labor services in order to oblige him to work and to give him a sense of his affairs" (Luis Capoche, *Relación general de la villa imperial de Potosí*).

The core contradiction was identified immediately by the audiencia officers: "The tribute should be levied either on the collectivity, or on the actual number of taxable Indians, but not under a rule that includes both these criteria; for if this is done, it is collected as a lump sum and the living are made to pay for the dead." The Jesuit José de Acosta added that forcing Indians to sell labor for cash tribute meant they were "obliged to forsake their towns in order to find someone who will hire them" — which "permanently sabotaged all efforts at separating the Indian and Spanish 'republics.'"

## The Forastero Problem and Demographic Evasion

The tribute system's most consequential unintended consequence was mass migration. Beginning with the epidemic decade of 1587–1597, households facing tribute quotas they could not meet fled their home ayllus. Because *forasteros* paid reduced tribute and were initially harder for the state to track, migrant status became a fiscal strategy. By the late seventeenth century "outsiders" made up 80 percent of some Andean towns. The census of 1683–1684 in the south-central Andes confirmed aggregate population levels but documented radical internal redistribution: in Paucarcolla (north of Lake Titicaca), 60 percent of the population consisted of *forasteros*; in Carangas, only 15 percent.

**Viceroy La Palata's reform (1680s–1730s):** In response, the Viceroy Duque de La Palata "reduced the 'outsiders' to the places where they had settled," making land access — not birth or ayllu membership — the basis of tributary status. "These reforms placed 'outsiders' who owned land into the same tribute category as 'natives' of the community. Implicitly, tribute paying was now decided by land access and political inclusion in the community, which meant that the ancient criteria of birth or membership in a kinship group had at last been abrogated."

## Tribute as Reciprocity: The Indigenous Interpretation

Indigenous actors did not uniformly experience tribute as simple extraction. Saignes argues that "the indigenous vision did not challenge the tributes, the mining corvée, the forced sale of merchandise, or even the ecclesiastical tithe, only the manner in which they were applied." The *mita* at Potosí was conceptualized in some quarters as a "battle" with underground forces, fulfilling the Andean model of reciprocal labor owed to the supreme lord (now the Spanish king, successor to the Inka). Andean people perceived the political contract as entailing "protection and guaranteed ownership of their land in exchange for their consent to the extraction of colonial wealth." The caciques of three great Charcas confederations wrote to the king in 1608: "All things well considered, Your Majesty does not have in his kingdoms more humble nor more useful vassals than us, as can be seen by the riches sent to Spain and those enjoyed by the Spaniards living in this land."

Resistance arose not from rejection of tribute per se, but when local authorities "arbitrarily decided who had to pay tribute or serve as a mitayo" or when prices and goods were manipulated by the [[repartimiento-de-mercancias|repartimiento de mercancias]].

## The Cacique's Burden and the Cédula de Cobranza

The [[curaca|caciques]] bore operational responsibility for tribute collection and were personally liable for shortfalls. The *cédula de cobranza* empowered and exposed them simultaneously: they could demand payment from community members, but they were also held accountable by corregidores for the full quota regardless of deaths, absences, and flight. This double bind drove caciques into the fraudulent schemes — under-reporting tribute payers, sheltering forasteros, falsifying census data — that eroded their legitimacy and eventually destroyed their dynasties.

The 1690 Potosí investigation of cacique abuses showed that one-third of them "laid heavy economic burdens on their subjects not just to satisfy legal colonial levies but also to enrich themselves," using devices including "falsifying numbers of tribute payers and forasteros or faking land records."

## Limitations

The analytical concept of "tribute" flattens a complex and regionally differentiated set of obligations. In practice the tribute system overlapped with: Church tithes (*diezmos*) and fees for sacraments; the *mita* labor draft; the *repartimiento de mercancias*; and various local impositions. Distinguishing what "tribute" meant to any given community at any given moment requires attention to local context, census status, and the specific demands of the resident corregidor and priest.

The originario/forastero binary, while analytically useful, was fluid in practice. Sons of *originarios* might adopt *forastero* status; *forasteros* could "naturalize" after years of residence; caciques manipulated both categories for fiscal advantage.
