# Medieval Infantry

## Definition and Scope

Medieval infantry in the period 1000–1300 comprised the foot soldiers—sergeants (*servientes*), archers, crossbowmen, and supporting followers—who formed the bulk of any army but rarely held the tactical initiative. They were essential yet socially subordinate to the mounted knightly elite. France emphasises that "infantry could provide simple mass, which was at times useful, although they tended to play a more passive role in combat, where the initiative usually lay with the cavalry."

This was not a standing force or universal militia but an ad hoc collection drawn from the followings of lords, urban levies, and mercenaries. The development of sergeancies and mercenaries from the late tenth century argues against any widespread universal obligation to military service.

## Nature and Recruitment

### No Universal Obligation

Despite occasional royal or princely appeals (e.g., John in 1213 or Henry II's experiments), rulers could not reliably raise troops by invoking ancient tradition:

> "There is a remarkable passage in the Annals of St Bertin under the year 859: 'Some of the common people living between the Seine and the Loire formed a sworn association amongst themselves, and fought bravely against the Danes on the Seine. But because their association had been made without due consideration, they were easily slain by our more powerful people.' This 'association', it must be noted, had to be made ad hoc: this was not the dying struggle of a militia system against a new seigniorial military system, but a specific reaction to particular circumstances which the wealthy bearers of arms saw as dangerous to their interests." (Ch. 6, France)

The Assizes of Arms in England (1181, 1252, Statute of Winchester) and French city levies systematised the arming of free men and produced large infantry forces by the late thirteenth century (Edward I's Commissions of Array), but quality was variable and selection involved "a mixture of coercion and cash."

In France, "the bulk of the sergeant-service was from the cities." Communal movements were armed; cities supplied vital men and money in return for privileges. Similar patterns held in Flanders, northern Italy, and the Latin Kingdom of Jerusalem (where 2,000 of 5,025 sergeants came from cities).

### Support for Cavalry Retinues

A knight required servants and supporters. Even lesser men needed attendants for horses, cooking, tents, and baggage.

> "A knight needed servants and supporters. If he was well-to-do, an armiger, perhaps a man close to knighthood, was also desirable, but lesser men could only manage servants. Henry I’s treaty of 1101 with Flanders specified that each knight must have three horses. Looking after horses was labour-intensive, and then food had to be cooked, tents erected and clothes stored. Undoubtedly, there was much doubling-up but, even so, if we allow for the demands of status, it is likely that there were two or three servants for every cavalryman. They could not have been allowed to twiddle their thumbs in the course of campaigns and must have formed part of the infantry of many armies, or at least an element that reinforced the sergeants and the more specialist troops, notably the archers and crossbowmen." (Ch. 6, France)

### Archers and Specialists

Archers were "a formidable instrument of war." They feature in accounts from Hastings (breaking the shieldwall) to Brémule (1119), Bourgthéroulde (1124), Northallerton (1138), and numerous sieges (Crema, Lodi). Baldwin I of Jerusalem had a corps under a *magister sagittariorum*. Richard I used large numbers on the Third Crusade.

Bowmen were raised from lords' followings; the skill was diffused (sculptures often show them as hunters). National specialities were limited in the West compared to the Middle East. English Assizes increasingly required bows among the poorest.

### Mercenaries and Social Control

Mercenaries (Brabanters, Routiers, Cotereaux, Aragonese, etc.) were a fact of life from the late tenth century onward and were used by all major rulers (Conqueror, Henry II, Barbarossa, Richard, Philip Augustus, Jerusalem, Italian cities). They were often employed for ravaging.

The Church excoriated them (Lateran Council 1179), associating them with heretics and promising crusader privileges against them, but this reflected the difficulty of controlling the upper class directly. Ravaging was widespread among all forces:

> "The march begins. Out in front are the scouts and incendiaries. After them come the foragers whose job it is to collect the spoils and carry them in the great baggage train. Soon all is tumult . . . The incendiaries set the villages on fire and the foragers visit and sack them. The terrified inhabitants are either burned or led away with their hands tied behind their backs to be held for ransom." (Chanson des Lorrains, quoted Ch. 6, France)

Mercenaries were frequently "the surrogate for the wrath of the Church." When they operated independently (e.g., bands in the Limousin 1180s), they were crushed by a combination of peasant confraternities ("Capuchins") and lords using other mercenaries. Peasant militias that threatened social order were suppressed (Peace of God movements, Children's Crusade, Pastoureaux, Pseudo-Baldwin affair).

The elite feared arming the "poor" except under controlled circumstances. "Underlying feudal society and its celebration of noble chivalry was the blunt fact that armies needed large numbers of lesser men to perform necessary tasks."

## Role and Limitations

Infantry provided mass and specialist skills (archery, siege work) but lacked cohesion because of the episodic, ad hoc nature of medieval armies. Training and discipline were rare except in unusual circumstances (William's 1066 army at Dives; the First Crusade's growing cohesion).

> "This was a general problem of all armies and a consequence of their ad hoc nature. The strength of an army is training and coherence, based on a uniform discipline... Overall, any group of soldiers, cavalry or infantry, lacked cohesion and were likely to be of uneven quality. This is why commanders used mercenaries to such a considerable extent." (Ch. 6, France)

Even mercenaries were usually recruited as individuals or small groups and retained only as long as needed. By the thirteenth century, large infantry armies appeared (England under Edward I; Italian cities), but quality remained generally poor until more continuous service developed later.

The ruling elite maintained a claimed monopoly of war, legitimised by the Church, yet "the official doctrine of a monopoly was a myth. War demands excellence, and this is rarely confined to a convenient social group." By the end of the period the catch-all "man-at-arms" was emerging.

## Historiography

France's treatment is institutional and social rather than narrowly tactical. It counters romantic or "national speciality" views (e.g., over-emphasis on Welsh archers as hired specialists) and stresses the ad hoc, retinue-derived reality against older assumptions of universal fyrd or militia continuity. Source criticism is essential: numbers and roles in chronicles are conventional.

Archaeology and administrative records (Assizes, pay accounts) supplement narrative sources. The model explains both the presence of large foot elements and the persistent social contempt for them.

Collection coverage is moderate-to-strong for High Medieval military institutions via France, Verbruggen, and Cambridge series.
