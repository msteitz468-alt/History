# The Meiji Constitution (1889)

The **Constitution of the Empire of Japan**, promulgated as a "gift" of the emperor on
11 February 1889 — **Asia's first modern written constitution** and the legal capstone of the
[[meiji-period|Meiji]] state. Drafted in secret under [[ito-hirobumi|Itō Hirobumi]] on the
**Prussian model**, it created a parliament (the Imperial Diet) while keeping sovereignty and
real power with the emperor and the oligarchy around him. From the
[[cambridge-history-japan-v5-nineteenth-century|Cambridge History of Japan Vol. 5]]
(W. G. Beasley, ch. 10).

## Origins and motives

Beasley identifies three convergent pressures behind the constitutional movement: lesser
members of the political elite wishing to **restrain the oligarchs' arbitrary authority**; the
government's wish to marshal "public opinion" behind the state; and the need to project an
image of "**enlightenment**" abroad to help **revise the unequal treaties** of 1858. The
movement ran from the **Charter Oath (1868)** and the short-lived Kōgisho assembly (1869),
through the [[samurai|popular-rights]] agitation for an elected parliament, to an
**imperial decree of 1881** promising a constitution by 1890.

## The Prussian choice

A decisive split within the oligarchy shaped the outcome. **Ōkuma Shigenobu** pressed
(1881) for a rapid **British-model** constitution — "constitutional government is party
government" — implicitly threatening Satsuma–Chōshū dominance; **Itō Hirobumi** took up the
challenge, forced Ōkuma out, and committed Japan to the **Prussian model**, in which
parliament held only legislative power while "the organization of the chief officers of the
administration remained the prerogative of the ruler." Iwakura (and Itō) fixed three
principles: the emperor would **appoint and dismiss ministers**; the **cabinet would not be
subject to parliamentary control** (ministers responsible to the emperor, not the Diet); and
the government would be **freed from budgetary dependence** (the previous year's budget
continuing if the Diet rejected a new one). Itō then studied constitutional government in
Germany and Austria (1882) and drafted the document **in seclusion with German and
German-influenced advisers**, while opposition was muzzled by press controls and the **Peace
Preservation Law (1887)**.

## Structure

- **Sovereignty in the emperor**, "sacred and inviolable," head of an imperial line
  "unbroken for ages eternal"; he commanded the armed forces, conducted foreign policy,
  appointed ministers, and could issue emergency ordinances.
- A bicameral **Imperial Diet**: an elected **House of Representatives** (on a narrow
  tax-based franchise — about 1% of the population) and a **House of Peers** drawn from the
  new nobility, designed as a conservative counterweight.
- The Diet's chief leverage was the **budget**, but even a rejected budget defaulted to the
  prior year's, blunting the power of the purse.
- Ministers responsible to the emperor, not the Diet; a **Privy Council** (and, outside the
  constitution, the *genrō* elder statesmen and the **independence of the military command**)
  held the substance of power.
- Grounded ideologically in ***kokutai*** (the "national essence"), reinforced by the
  **Imperial Rescript on Education (1890)**.

## Significance

The Meiji Constitution made Japan the **first non-Western constitutional state** and secured
the "enlightened" image that helped win **treaty revision (1894)**. But it was, by design, a
framework for **controlled, oligarch-led politics**: it conceded the *forms* of representative
government while reserving sovereignty and executive power to the emperor and those who ruled
in his name. Its ambiguities — especially the **independence of the military command** and the
absence of cabinet responsibility to the Diet — would prove fateful in the twentieth century,
opening the path to military domination in the [[world-wars-era|1930s]]. It endured unamended
until the post-1945 Occupation constitution replaced it.
