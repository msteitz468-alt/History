# Revolution in Military Affairs

The **Revolution in Military Affairs** (RMA) is the claim that precision-guided munitions, sensors, computing, and networked information had so transformed warfare as to "fundamentally alter the character and conduct of conflict." As framed by Lawrence Freedman in [[sources/freedman-strategy-a-history-2013|Strategy: A History]], the RMA promised dominance through superior knowledge — the prospect, hovering "on the edge of pronouncing the 'fog of war' lifted and the problem of friction answered." It is the late-twentieth-century inheritor of the operational/maneuverist tradition, repackaged once "Soviet communism imploded and the Warsaw Pact soon evaporated" and the operational approach "was never tested in the circumstances for which it was designed."

## Origins and Definition (emic)

The term arose by analogy. Andrew Marshall, head of the Pentagon's Office of Net Assessment, was "aware that during its last years there had been talk in the Soviet Union of a 'military technical revolution' that might bring conventional forces up to new levels of effectiveness." After the 1991 Gulf War, Marshall encouraged the phrase **"revolution in military affairs"** over "military-technical revolution" specifically "to stress the importance of operational and organizational changes as well as technological ones." His analyst Andrew Krepinevich defined the RMA in 1994 as

> what occurs when the application of new technologies into a significant number of military systems combines with innovative operational concepts and organizational adaptation in a way that fundamentally alters the character and conduct of conflict … by producing a dramatic increase—often an order of magnitude or greater—in the combat potential and military effectiveness of armed forces.

Although "the origins of the RMA lay in doctrine, the driver appeared technological" — the interaction of systems that "collected, processed, and communicated information with those that applied military force," a "so-called system of systems." Long-range precision strike would let "time and space … decline as serious constraints"; agile forces could call in "non-organic firepower" rather than carry their own. The completion of this move was explicitly Boydian: enemies could be defeated by "following the Boyd line of acting more quickly and moving more deftly," denied any chance to complete "Boyd's now-famous OODA loop" (see [[ooda-loop|the OODA loop]], [[boyd-john|John Boyd]]).

## Doctrinal Apparatus

The doctrine acquired its vocabulary in the 1990s. A 1996 National Defense University publication introduced **"shock and awe"** — "all efforts should be focused on overwhelming the enemy physically and mentally as quickly as possible," so that "the enemy's perceptions and grasp of events would be overloaded, leaving him paralyzed." *Joint Vision 2010* (1997) defined **information superiority** as "the capability to collect, process, and disseminate an uninterrupted flow of information while exploiting or denying an adversary's ability to do the same." Arthur Cebrowski and John Garstka argued a "form of 'network-centered warfare' could make battles more efficient in the same way that the application of information technology by businesses was making economies more efficient," recognizing a **cognitive domain** where "many battles and wars are won or lost."

## The Gulf War as Apparent Proof

The 1991 Gulf War "vindicated this vision." In Desert Storm "the equipment worked well: cruise missiles fired from a distance of some one thousand kilometers navigated their way through the streets of Baghdad, entered their target by the front door, and then exploded." But this was "a most flattering light": the Iraqis were "poorly armed and trained conscripts" who "had conceded command of the air" (see [[command-of-the-air|command of the air]], [[strategic-bombardment|strategic bombardment]]). Freedman's verdict is double-edged — "the vindication carried its own refutation," since the win was "helped by Saddam Hussein's ignorance of the real military balance," and future opponents would "take more care."

## Limitations and Critiques (etic)

Freedman treats the RMA as etic illusion shaped by "political preferences about the sort of war the Americans would like to fight" — a vision, in Bacevich's phrase, "of the Persian Gulf War replayed over and over again." It assumed an enemy who "inhabit the same moral and political universe," and was "unlikely to be followed by opponents who would seek to exploit the presumed American weaknesses of impatience and casualty intolerance." The model "betrayed a detached attitude to the well-springs of conflict," envisaging "a war without tears" — and it was unclear "who would fight such a war," given the absence of "peer competitors."

The weak chose **asymmetric war** instead: "imposing pain rather than winning battles," drawing "the civilian sphere into the conflict while avoiding open battle." 9/11 "took the notion of asymmetry to the extreme." Afghanistan and Iraq toppled regimes quickly but "did not settle the matter": Rumsfeld's bet on far fewer forces "soon appeared imprudent as U.S. forces struggled to cope with an insurgency." Having "ignored counterinsurgency for over three decades," American forces, trained for "large-scale combat operations" rather than "operations other than war," "coped poorly with irregular warfare" (see [[counterinsurgency|counterinsurgency]], [[guerrilla-warfare|guerrilla warfare]]). The "old classics of guerrilla warfare—from T. E. Lawrence to David Galula—began to be rediscovered"; Petraeus's "surge" worked less through numbers than by "working with the grain of Iraqi politics."

### The Myth of the Master Strategist

Chapter 17 extends the critique to the very idea of mastery. Colin Gray's seventeen factors and Harry Yarger's "holistic" strategist amounted to "a counsel of perfection" — Gray himself conceded Yarger "appeared to encourage, even demand, an impossibility." Hitting an enemy "in exactly the right place" to make it "crumble quickly" failed because "societies could adapt to shocks." Pure control "was always an illusion." The dream of "a military strategy separate from a political strategy was not only misleading but also dangerous." Hence "the idea of a master strategist was therefore a myth": it demands "an impossible omniscience" and ignores the "real and immediate demands of strategy-making." Only political leaders can be master strategists, and even they "could not begin to comprehend all the relevant factors." This is the structural counterpoint to the RMA's faith that war can be controlled "from above."
