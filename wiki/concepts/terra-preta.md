# Terra Preta and the Built Amazon

One of the most radical claims marshalled in Charles Mann's [[mann-1491-2005|*1491*]] (Chs. 9–10) is that
**Amazonia — the supposed archetype of pristine wilderness — is in large part a human artifact**, a
"built environment" engineered by large, settled societies over millennia. The keystone evidence is
**terra preta do Índio** ("Indian dark earth"): patches of extraordinarily **fertile, anthropogenic
soil** scattered through a basin otherwise notorious for thin, acidic, aluminum-poisoned ground.

## Indian dark earth

Where most Amazonian soil is so poor it forced shifting **slash-and-burn** agriculture, *terra preta* is
deep, black, and rich — and **made by people**, apparently by working charcoal, pottery, bone, and
organic waste into the ground (modern science calls the charcoal-based version **"biochar"**). Mann
reports its astonishing properties: farmers prize it, work it for decades with minimal fertiliser, and it
has **retained its nutrients for up to a millennium** under punishing tropical conditions — "if you read
the textbooks, it shouldn't be there" (Bruno Glaser). Its very existence implies **large, sedentary
populations** generating waste and deliberately building soil over long periods. Estimates of its extent
range wildly — from a conservative ~0.1–0.3% of the basin to William Woods's guess of up to **10%** (an
area the size of France) — but, as Mann notes, even a few thousand square miles of such farmland "was
enough to feed the millions in the Maya heartland." Locals now dig it up to sell as **potting soil**,
destroying the archaeology in the process.

## The orchard-forest

Beyond the soil, researchers (Charles Clement, William Balée, Clark Erickson) argue the **forest itself**
was shaped by people, who concentrated and propagated useful species — above all the **peach palm**
(*Bactris gasipaes*), a self-sustaining tree, more productive per acre than rice or maize, with edible
oily fruit, heart-of-palm, wood, and sap. Much of what looks like "pristine high forest," Balée argues,
is actually **old fallow** — the regrown orchards of vanished farming peoples; Amazonians themselves often
**do not distinguish "wild" from "cultivated"** land. The early eyewitness is the **Orellana expedition
(1542)**, the first Europeans down the Amazon, whose chronicler Carvajal described **dense riverbank
populations** — settlements lining the bank "for more than a hundred miles," war-canoe fleets of
thousands, "very large cities" inland — a populous river world that had **vanished** by the time later
Europeans arrived, swept away by [[virgin-soil-epidemics|disease]] (the "Stone Age tribespeople" of later
imagination were in part **refugees** who, hiding from disease and slave-raids, underwent Balée's
"agricultural regression").

## The debate

This is among the most contested claims in the [[pre-columbian-americas|revisionist]] case. The leading
skeptic, **Betty Meggers** of the Smithsonian, holds that Amazonian soils and the recurring **"mega-Niño"**
droughts imposed a hard ceiling: any society pushing past slash-and-burn to permanent cultivation would be
"knocked back" by drought, so slash-and-burn "avoids the risks... it's the smart thing to do," and the
large-population claims are "wishful thinking." Against her, Erickson and Clement argue the lowland forest
is "among the finest works of art on the planet" and "basically... all human created." Mann presents the
revisionist case sympathetically while airing Meggers's objections — the magnitudes (how much *terra
preta*, how many people) remain genuinely open. See the [[bolivian-beni|Beni]] earthworks
([[holmbergs-mistake]]) for the savanna counterpart.

## Significance

If correct, the built-Amazon thesis is the strongest single case for the [[pristine-myth|managed-landscape
argument]]: even the planet's iconic "untouched" wilderness was a **cultural landscape**, its apparent
wildness the product of catastrophic depopulation. It carries sharp **present-day political stakes** —
over who controls Amazonia, and whether "wilderness" preservation or Indigenous land-management is the
authentic baseline.
