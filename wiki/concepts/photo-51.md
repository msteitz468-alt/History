# Photo 51

Photo 51 is the nickname given to an X-ray diffraction image of the **B form of DNA** taken by Raymond Gosling under the supervision of Rosalind Franklin in May 1952 at King's College London. It proved to be the decisive piece of experimental evidence in identifying the double-helical structure of DNA.

The image was shown to James Watson by Maurice Wilkins in January 1953 without Franklin's knowledge. Its distinct "X" pattern immediately identified a helical structure, while the spacing of the reflections allowed Watson and Crick to calculate the pitch (34.4 Å) and rise (3.4 Å) of the helix.

## Scientific Analysis

### Experimental Conditions
Photo 51 was obtained using a highly purified gel of DNA (the "Signer DNA") prepared by Swiss chemist Rudolf Signer. Franklin and Gosling controlled the humidity inside the microcamera by bubbling hydrogen through salt solutions, keeping the DNA fiber hydrated at approximately 92% relative humidity. This high hydration allowed the fibers to adopt the paracrystalline B-form rather than the shorter, drier A-form.

### Structural Features revealed by the image
The photograph displays several key structural coordinates:
- **The "X" Pattern:** The heavy black stripes radiating from the center form an "X", which is the mathematical signature of a helical diffraction pattern.
- **The Blank Spaces:** The empty regions along the axis indicate that the helix has regular periodic repeats, allowing Stokes and Crick to calculate the helical coordinates.
- **Repeat and Stacking:** The pitch (one full turn of the helix) was measured at 34.4 Å, with the individual bases stacked 3.4 Å apart, suggesting ten base pairs per turn.

## Historiographical Significance

Photo 51 is at the center of the controversy surrounding the discovery of the double helix. Because Franklin had put the image aside to focus on the Patterson calculations of the A-form, Watson and Crick were able to utilize her B-form parameters to build their model before she published her own analysis. In his 1999 memoir retrospective, Watson admitted that seeing Photo 51 was the "pivotal moment" that psychologically mobilized them to build the model.
