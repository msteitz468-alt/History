# Merovingian Christology and Devotion to Jesus

**Not a remote, militaristic Christ but a "hide-and-seek theology" of the embodied savior.** Lynda Coon's
chapter (Oxford Handbook, Ch. 46) recovers Merovingian meditations on Jesus from sculpture, hymn, and
hagiography, arguing [REVISION] that Merovingian Christianity rested on "a triangulation among godhead,
embodied savior, and human actor," in which "Jesus' humanity had to be fished out of what at first glance
might seem to be an ocean of divinity." The cross and the human body were interchangeable: "in seamless
fashion, the Merovingian cross metamorphosed into the human body, and human actors took on the attributes of
the wood of salvation."

## Three Sculpted Meditations: Absence, Torture, Light

Coon organizes the chapter around three Rhineland/Poitevin monuments and three meditational themes.

- **Moselkern Stele (Absence, 7th c.):** a basalt-lava stele where "body and cross collapse into one,"
  Jesus's physicality preserved through anthropomorphic crosses; the standard reading of a cosmic, remote
  Christ is revised — "the human Jesus is a savior of absence, whose embodiment in the rock must be puzzled
  out by the meditative viewer." (Ristow theorizes a "little Christ" in low relief, yielding a Trinity of
  images.)
- **Hypogée des Dunes (Torture, 8th c., Poitiers):** two thieves bound to crosses flank a scourging pillar,
  with Jesus's body now lost; flagellation was "a very rare subject for art before the Carolingian period."
  Abbot Mellobaudes may have built the crypt as a Merovingian Holy Sepulcher. Gregory of Tours and
  [[venantius-fortunatus|Venantius Fortunatus]] both meditate on the passion relics — the cords on the
  pillar, "the holes in Jesus' hands, the bloody nails, the penetrating spear."
- **Niederdollendorf Stele (Light, late 7th c.):** a **solar Christ** with the attributes of Sol Invictus
  and a bi-fold mandorla — "a signature Merovingian artistic style" preserved in Carolingian copies of
  Fortunatus's figural cross-poems (*Carmen* 2.4–2.5; St. Gallen, Cod. Sang. 196) — possibly depicting the
  Last Judgment, "extremely rare iconography in Western art before the High Middle Ages."

## Radegund as Christological Actor

**[[radegund-of-poitiers|Radegund]]** (d. 587) bridges all three themes. Two hagiographies balance body and
mind — Fortunatus (c. 587) foregrounding embodiment ("Jesus inhabits her bowels… the food filling up her
belly withered by fasting"), Baudonivia (c. 600) the *mens intenta*. Her **branding ritual** (a heated brass
plate pressed *in signo Christi*, leaving two bleeding *foramina*) makes her wounds "objects of meditation…
singled out for Christians to contemplate," and Fortunatus coined *tortrix* for her self-mortification (see
[[early-medieval-penance]]). Her acquisition of a **True Cross relic** from Byzantium for Poitiers (see
[[merovingian-byzantine-relations]]) made her feel "that Christ had come to dwell within her"; the Good
Friday light-miracle in her dark oratory, and her transmutation into the paschal candle of the *Exultet*
(with its bee imagery for female purity), complete the light theme.

## Significance and Historiography

Against the dominant scholarly reading of a "remote, militaristic, cosmic" Merovingian Jesus, Coon argues for
a devotion "vacillat[ing] between heights of divinity and depths of tortured humanity" — one more rebuttal of
the "theological void" image and a key piece of the volume's recovery of Merovingian religion (see
[[merovingian-hagiography]], [[merovingian-liturgy-and-lay-religion]], [[visions-and-the-afterlife-merovingian]]).
