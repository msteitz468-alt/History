# The Great Synthesis — Rationalism and Traditionalism in Islamic Law

Source: [[hallaq-origins-evolution-islamic-law-2005]], chapters 7–8 and Conclusion. Part of [[formative-period-islamic-law]].

## Definition and Scope

The "Great Synthesis" is Hallaq's term for the methodological-doctrinal achievement that completed the formative period of Islamic law: the synthesis between two opposed approaches to legal reasoning — **[[ahl-al-ray]]** (the rationalists, who derived law through human reason guided by social experience) and **[[ahl-al-hadith]]** (the traditionalists, who insisted law must rest on the Quran and authenticated Prophetic hadith alone). Neither approach, taken in isolation, could sustain a viable doctrinal school; the synthesis was the precondition for the emergence and survival of the four Sunni *madhhabs*.

Hallaq identifies the Great Synthesis as a distinct stage in the overall development of Islamic law, logically and historically prior to (though overlapping with) the completion of [[usul-al-fiqh]] (legal theory) and the crystallization of the [[madhhab-formation|doctrinal schools]]. "Nor could this methodology itself have arisen without the creation of a synthesis between the opposing forces of rationalism and traditionalism, a synthesis that gave Sunnite Islam its defining features."

## The Terms of the Opposition

**Rationalism** (*ra'y*, *qiyas*, *istihsan*): Law may be derived through trained human judgment operating on the principles implicit in the revealed texts. The jurist's reasoned opinion (*ra'y*) is a legitimate and necessary instrument for extending the law to novel cases not directly addressed by the Quran or Sunna. Analogical reasoning (*qiyas*) systematized this extension; *istihsan* (juristic preference) allowed departure from strict analogy where the equitable outcome required it.

**Traditionalism** (*hadith*, *sunna*): Law may only rest on what God and the Prophet directly commanded or exemplified. Human reason, operating independently, is suspect — it substitutes the jurist's fallible judgment for divine command. The authority of authenticated hadith chains constitutes the ceiling of legal derivation; anything above that ceiling is speculation (*zann* at best, innovation at worst).

These are not static positions. The great 3rd/9th-century jurists of each school occupied positions along a spectrum, and the synthesis was precisely the development of methodological frameworks that gave both poles their proper place: traditionalism anchored the law in revelation; rationalism equipped it to function as a living system addressing novel realities.

## The Role of Usul al-Fiqh

The synthesis was institutionalized in [[usul-al-fiqh]] — the science of legal theory — which codified the sources of law, their hierarchical relationships, and the methods for deriving rulings from them. Usul al-fiqh was itself the synthesis's technical expression: it specified precisely *when* and *how* rationalist tools (*qiyas*, *ijma'*, *istihsan*, *maslaha*) could operate, and what constraints the traditionalist commitment to hadith placed on them. Without usul al-fiqh, the synthesis would have remained an implicit methodological posture rather than a teachable, transmissible, and enforceable discipline. With it, the doctrinal school had a reproducible methodology that could generate authoritative rulings while maintaining its claim to textual grounding.

## Evidence: Surviving vs. Defunct Schools

Hallaq uses the Great Synthesis explicitly as the diagnostic criterion for distinguishing surviving from defunct personal schools:

**Failure due to incomplete synthesis:**
- **[[zahirism|Zahirites]]** (Dawud al-Zahiri, d. 270/884): Insisted on pure literalism, rejected *qiyas* entirely. Could not develop the doctrinal flexibility needed to address the full range of legal problems, and could not attract the broad juristic talent necessary for authority construction. "The failure to bring the doctrine of a personal school to the paradigm of what we have called the Great Synthesis... was obviously the main cause of the demise of the Zahirite school, and possibly that of Abu Thawr."
- **Abu Thawr** (d. 240/854): similarly unable to complete the synthesis and build a self-sustaining doctrinal tradition.

**Recovery through synthesis:**
- **Hanafis**: Originally the most explicitly rationalist school, associated with *ra'y* and *qiyas* in Kufa. After the [[mihna-833]] damaged the Mu'tazilite-rationalist alliance, the Hanafis rehabilitated their position (Thalji's contributions), incorporated hadith more systematically, and aligned with Maturidite theology — a mainstream synthesis that allowed them to dominate in Iraq and the eastern provinces.

**Success through synthesis:**
- **Malikis**: Malik's school was grounded in Medinese practice (*'amal ahl al-Madina*) — itself a traditionalist claim, but one that incorporated a large body of established custom alongside hadith. The synthesis was embedded in the school's foundational practice-based authority.
- **Shafi'is**: Shafi'i's career was precisely the intellectual effort to forge a synthesis: his *Risala* (the foundational text of usul al-fiqh) systematically reconciled the claims of reason and tradition by subordinating both to a hierarchy of sources with Prophetic hadith at the apex. The Shafi'i school then allied with Ash'arite theology — a moderate rationalist-traditionalist synthesis in theology that mirrored their jurisprudential position.
- **Hanbalis**: Most traditionalist of the four surviving schools, yet even Hanbalism developed *qiyas* as a subsidiary tool (though with far greater reluctance than the Hanafis). The school's survival required at least minimal rationalist accommodation.

## The Synthesis and Authority Construction

The Great Synthesis was not only a methodological achievement — it was the precondition for the [[madhhab-formation|authority construction]] that turned personal schools into doctrinal schools. The founding eponym of each school had to be constructed as an "absolute *mujtahid*" — a jurist who could engage directly with the revealed texts through a complete, self-sufficient methodology. That methodology was necessarily synthetic: it needed both the rationalist tools to generate a living body of law and the traditionalist anchoring to claim divine authority.

A purely rationalist founding image would lack the grounding in revelation that gave the school its authority over the masses. A purely traditionalist founding image could not explain how the law addressed novel cases — it would reduce the school to mere transmission. The absolute *mujtahid* figure combined both: he derived law directly from revelation (traditionalist legitimacy) *through* a systematized methodology (rationalist structure). This combination was what made the doctrinal school's authority plausible.

## The Synthesis and Sunni Identity

The Great Synthesis is, in Hallaq's account, the event that produced **Sunni Islam** as a distinctive configuration. The [[sunni-orthodoxy-formation]] was not simply the triumph of traditionalism over rationalism but the integration of both within a framework that:
- Privileged Prophetic Sunna as the highest normative source above the Companions and all human reason
- Permitted rationalist legal tools (*qiyas*, *ijma'*, etc.) within strictly bounded methodological channels
- Delegitimized both hyper-rationalism (substituting human reason for divine command) and hyper-literalism (rejecting all rational extension of the texts)

The alignment of the surviving doctrinal schools with mainstream theological positions (Maturidism for Hanafis, Ash'arism for Shafi'is) mirrored this jurisprudential synthesis in the domain of theology.

## Historiography

Hallaq's "Great Synthesis" concept is his own analytical coinage, though the underlying tension between *ra'y* and hadith traditions is well established in the field (Schacht, *The Origins of Muhammadan Jurisprudence*, 1950; and subsequent scholarship). The concept is most useful as a diagnostic tool for explaining differential survival of legal schools. It is less useful as a precise historical periodization, since the synthesis was a gradual achievement across the full 2nd–4th/8th–10th century span, not a single event.

The claim that the synthesis was the *cause* of the doctrinal schools' success (rather than a symptom or co-product of other factors like political patronage and doctrinal elaboration) remains open to debate. Hallaq treats it as a necessary but not sufficient condition, alongside political support, theological alignment, and distinctive juristic identity.
