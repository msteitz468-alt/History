## Definition

The *quṭb* (Arabic: "pole" or "axis"; pl. *aqṭâb*) is the concept in Sufi cosmology of a single living saint who stands as the cosmic pivot around which the universe revolves — its teleological "final cause" in Aristotelian terms. The quṭb is the "perfect man" (*al-insân al-kâmil*) in whom human nature is fully realized: he knows all things and above all knows God totally. For the sake of producing this perfection, the entire created order — all nature, and indeed all other humans — is understood to exist. He is the culminating point of the [[ibn-arabi|myth of the microcosmic return]]: creation proceeds from God to find its return path through the consciousness of the perfected saint.

The quṭb's identity in any given generation is not publicly known; his authority is total but secret. Muhammad was the quṭb of his time; subsequent quṭbs continue the function without prophetic mission.

## Development

The concept developed out of several converging streams:
- **Piri-muridi hierarchy:** The practical experience of Sufi master-disciple chains generated a natural sense of hierarchy in spiritual attainment. The quṭb is the logical apex of that hierarchy — the master without a master.
- **Shi'i imâm doctrine:** Hodgson notes that the quṭb concept "was deeply influenced by Shi'i conceptions of the sinless imâm, ever present (even if hidden) as guarantor of the faith." The Sufi innovation was to relocate this cosmic guarantee from a specific genealogical lineage (Alid) to a spiritually attained rank accessible (in principle) to any perfected saint.
- **Neo-Platonic cosmology:** The concept of a single pivot around which the cosmos revolves draws on the neo-Platonic hierarchy of being, adapted through the Falsafa tradition and available to Sufi thinkers from the 10th century onward.

The concept "was being worked out during several generations of Sufis, beginning with later High Caliphal times, but it took full form only in the Earlier Middle Period," reaching its definitive formulation in the work of [[ibn-arabi]].

## The Saintly Hierarchy

Below the quṭb, Sufi cosmology posited a pyramidal hierarchy of lesser saints, each bearing the relation of murîd to the one above and pîr to those below:
- At the apex: the quṭb (pole)
- Below: the *abdâl* ("substitutes" or "replacements") — a fixed number of saints whose spiritual function is to maintain cosmic order in their respective regions
- Below: further ranks of spiritually advanced men and women
- At the base: ordinary murîd disciples, tied through their pîrs to the hierarchy above

This invisible hierarchy was conceived as actively maintaining the world: "The highest saints were said to move perpetually—at least in spirit—through the world, seeing that it was kept in good order" (Hodgson). The preacher 'Abdulqâdir Gîlânî was widely held to have been the quṭb of his time.

## Political Implications

[[ibn-arabi]] developed the quṭb concept into a direct challenge (or supplement) to the political-legal authority of the caliph and [[ulama]]. The quṭb, he argued, is a *mujtahid* by direct divine acquaintance rather than by scholarly training — "the true caliph (khalîfah) of God directly." His authority in matters of [[sharia]] is thus more fundamental than any formally appointed caliph's. This provided a cosmological legitimation for the Sufi hierarchy's practical authority in Muslim society — an authority that operated "through his invisible hierarchy, the abdâl saints, and finally through his more visible representatives, the pîrs."

As Hodgson notes, this was also "a far more basic sway than any outward caliph" — the quṭb replaced the caliph as the true spiritual center of the Ummah precisely when the actual caliphate had been politically marginalized.

## Social Function

The quṭb concept served several social functions:
1. **Spiritual legitimation of pîr authority.** Since every pîr stands somewhere in the saintly hierarchy, pîr-murîd obedience is not merely personal loyalty but participation in a cosmic order.
2. **Integration of popular saint veneration.** The same logic that made the quṭb's blessing efficacious made every pîr's blessing potentially efficacious for laypeople — providing cosmological grounding for the vast popular saint-cult associated with [[tariqa-orders]].
3. **Political critique without confrontation.** The invisible quṭb's authority was a standing implicit critique of any visible caliph or sultan who fell short of the ideal — but since the critique operated in an invisible register, it did not require direct confrontation.

## Limitations and Critique

The concept was contested within the Sufi tradition itself: some Shari'ah-minded Sufis were uncomfortable with the near-prophetic authority it ascribed to saints, and with the implication that the quṭb's spiritual authority could override the [[sharia]]. The 'ulama who accepted saint-veneration generally maintained a distinction between the "evidentiary miracles" (mu'jizât) of prophets and the "merely incidental wonders" (karâmât) of saints — a distinction the more intense Sufis regarded as secondary.
