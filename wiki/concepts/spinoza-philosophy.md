## Definition

Spinoza's philosophical system identifies God with Nature (*Deus sive Natura*) as a single, infinite, deterministic substance of which all things — including human minds and bodies — are necessary modes, rejecting a personal, providential deity who intervenes in or judges human affairs. The system is rigorously deterministic: everything follows from the necessity of the divine nature, leaving no room for a freely willing, anthropomorphic God or for miracles in the traditional sense.

## Emic vs. Etic

Emic to seventeenth-century rationalist philosophy: Spinoza articulated the system himself, in his own technical vocabulary, though later labels such as "pantheism" are subsequent scholarly characterizations of his position.

## Historical Significance and Contestation

Spinoza's rationalism provoked his 1656 excommunication (*herem*) from Amsterdam's Sephardic Jewish community and was widely condemned across confessional lines in his own century as atheistic, yet it became a major current within Enlightenment thought and, later, a touchstone for scientifically minded thinkers seeking a religious sensibility compatible with a lawful, deterministic universe — most famously articulated by [[actors/einstein-albert|Albert Einstein]], who described his own religious feeling as belief in "Spinoza's God, who reveals himself in the lawful harmony of all that exists, but not in a God who concerns himself with the fate and doings of mankind." Whether Spinoza's system is best read as a sophisticated theism, a disguised atheism, or something genuinely distinct from both remains debated among historians of philosophy.
