# The Reichskirche (Ottonian-Salian Imperial Church System)

## Definition

The **Reichskirche** ("imperial church") is the term for the dense integration of the **bishoprics and royal abbeys** of the German Reich into the apparatus of royal government under the [[ottonian-dynasty|Ottonian]] and Salian emperors (c. 950–1100). In the classic German constitutional formulation (*Reichskirchensystem*), the crown used the **higher clergy as its principal agents of rule**: bishops and imperial abbots held regalian rights, supplied the bulk of the army and the itinerant court's hospitality, and governed counties, while the king controlled their **appointment, investiture, and (on a vacancy) their revenues**. [[reuter-ncmh-v3-1999|NCMH III]] (Müller-Mertens) traces its development as the institutional backbone of Ottonian monarchy.

## Mechanisms

- **The royal chapel as a seminary of bishops.** Clerics — drawn from the high nobility — served as **royal chaplains** (*capellani*) and cathedral canons, then were promoted to bishoprics, remaining royal counsellors thereafter. The practice began under **Henry I** and was systematised by **Brun**, Otto I's brother (archbishop of Cologne and duke of Lotharingia). Under **[[henry-ii-holy-roman-emperor|Henry II]]** roughly two-thirds of episcopal vacancies were filled from the chapel.
- **Investiture by the king.** The ruler invested bishops and abbots, sometimes overriding canonical free election and deleting free-election clauses from privileges (Henry II).
- **Regalian endowment.** Emperors granted bishoprics and royal monasteries **immunities, the *bannus*** (right to command and hold courts), markets, mints, tolls, and **whole counties** — concentrated in the late tenth and early eleventh centuries — building up ecclesiastical lordships that were also organs of the state.
- **A theology of episcopal service.** Ruotger's *Life of Brun* articulated a **new episcopal ideal** legitimating bishops' "political affairs and dangerous wars" as service to the priest-king who was Christ's representative — squarely against the ninth-century monastic-ascetic ideal of the bishop.

## Why It Worked in the Reich

NCMH III stresses the **continuity** that made the Reichskirche possible: unlike west Francia, where royal control over the church dissolved with Carolingian power, the **east Frankish kings retained lordship over all bishoprics and many monasteries**, so the Ottonians inherited an intact instrument. Bishops also offered the crown a **non-hereditary** resource: their offices reverted to the king on death and could not be passed to heirs, unlike comital lands — making prelates more reliable agents than the entrenched ducal and comital dynasties.

## Significance and Contested Status

The Reichskirche made the German monarchy the strongest in Latin Europe and underwrote the **[[ottonian-renaissance|Ottonian Renaissance]]** (cathedral schools, manuscript and metalwork patronage). But the king's **investiture of bishops and his treatment of the papacy as an imperial bishopric** (most baldly under [[otto-iii|Otto III]]) set the Reich on a collision course with the eleventh-century reform movement: the **Investiture Contest** (from 1075) was in large part a struggle over the Reichskirche.

The very concept of a **"system"** is debated: revisionist scholarship (Timothy Reuter himself, in influential articles) questioned whether the Ottonians operated a deliberate, planned *Reichskirchensystem* at all, or whether historians have over-systematised a set of pragmatic, ad hoc, and mutually beneficial arrangements between rulers and aristocratic prelates. NCMH III accordingly speaks of Ottonian "characteristics" and tendencies rather than a fixed institutional machine.
