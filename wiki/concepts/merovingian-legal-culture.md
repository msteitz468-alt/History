# Merovingian Legal Cultures

**Law as cultural capital, not a coherent system: a flexible repertoire of legitimating scripts in "a
small state with the memory of a big one."** Alice Rio's chapter (Oxford Handbook, Ch. 23) reconstructs
Merovingian legal practice from codes, formularies, charters, royal *placita*, and miracle stories,
arguing that contemporaries drew on "a multitude of different legal reference points" to justify their
actions rather than applying a single body of rules.

## Definition and Scope

Roman legal hallmarks were absent: "no known professional lawyer; venues… that coexisted more than they
coordinated; an emphasis on self-help; reliance on local consensus." Rio rejects the old binaries —
"Roman versus Germanic; literacy versus orality; rationality versus irrationality… coercion versus
consensus" — yet stresses that "legal matter… makes up a very substantial proportion of the written
evidence," revealing "a pervasive concern with the legal realm."

## Salic Law and the Codes

The 65-title *Pactus Legis Salicae* (see [[salic-law]]), "probably issued late in the reign of
[[clovis|Clovis]]," is read through Patrick Wormald's argument that northern codes were "custom, policy
and judgment" whose written form was "an ideologically driven afterthought, designed to project royal
authority." The ethnic distinctions in Salic Law are "now seen as contributing to the *engineering* of
ethnic identity rather than merely reflecting it: a way of inventing tradition" — a key link to
[[ethnogenesis]]. Continued use of Roman legal instruments, Rio warns, "should not necessarily be
counted as evidence for continuity."

## Formularies and Documentary Culture

The chapter's distinctive material is the **formulary**. The Angers collection preserves a man's
declaration that a break-in destroyed his "documents of sale, loan securities, transfers of property,
donations, marriage gifts… too many to list" — evidence of a dense documentary habit, even though only
**27 original private documents** survive (14 via St. Gall). Formularies circulated as "a widely diffused
pool of available models that scribes shared," driven "by demand from end users… rather than by any sort
of imposition from above." Their function was legitimation, not regulation: "having a written document
made did not tie down the scope of 'legal' action to any particular rules; it simply allowed users to
express what they were doing as something that involved legitimate, recognizable rights."

## Proof, Feud, and the Plurality of Fora

Cases could go before royal tribunals, bishops', abbots', or counts' courts, or local assemblies of
*boni homines* and *rachymburgii*, with **no privileged mode of proof**. Oath-helping "needed to
mobilize, as well as to reflect, public opinion"; ordeal and torture were tools for cases where authority
"did not want to leave things to chance" (slaves were tortured automatically, PLS 40.4). On **feud**, the
Sichar–Chramnesind case is the test: Rio's synthesis is that feud "was one available model of behavior
among many others, and a way of ensuring legal legibility for one's actions" — Clothild's revenge invokes
"a decades-old blood debt… as a transparent excuse" that "only makes sense in a context in which feud was
both commonly understood and taken seriously."

## Conclusion

"The Merovingian kingdoms were, in essence, small states with the memory of a big one. This expanded
their range of available, justifiable forms of state action, but also meant that they could not deny
alternatives." By this point "law was less a function of government than a form of cultural capital,
manipulated and lent meaning by its end users."
