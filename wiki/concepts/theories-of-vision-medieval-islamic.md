## Definition and Scope

The medieval Islamic tradition on the physiology and physics of vision represents the most sustained ancient and medieval engagement with the question of how sight works. It inherited three competing ancient Greek frameworks, mediated by Galen's anatomical synthesis, and transmitted them through Arabic translations into a new intellectual context. The outcome was Ibn al-Haytham's *Book of Optics* (Arabic: *Kitāb al-Manāẓir*, c. 1011 CE) — the first formulation of an intromissionist theory combined with geometrical optics, anticipating the modern understanding of light bouncing off objects and entering the eye. This concept page covers the theoretical landscape from which Ibn al-Haytham emerged (particularly the contributions of [[al-kindi|al-Kindī]] and [[al-farabi|al-Fārābī]]), rather than Ibn al-Haytham's synthesis itself.

The topic illustrates how the Islamic philosophical tradition absorbed, adjudicated, and extended ancient science in a domain far from theology — and how empirical observation, mathematical modelling, and philosophical argument interacted in medieval natural science.

## The Three Ancient Frameworks

### 1. Extramissionism (Plato, Galen)

**Plato** (*Timaeus*, 45b–46c): Vision occurs when an invisible stream of pure fire emitted from the eyes encounters external light. The resulting connection causes motion in the soul — seeing. This explains: (a) directionality of sight (we see only what the stream reaches, i.e., what is in front of us with eyes open); (b) necessity of illumination (the external fire must be present to connect with the visual stream); (c) mirror reflections (the visual stream meets external illumination on the reflective surface). Plato also discusses how curved mirrors produce different reflections and why mirror images are reversed.

**Galen**: Modified Plato using anatomy and Stoic pneumatic physiology. The brain distils a very fine *pneuma* (breath) from the blood; this pneuma travels via the optic nerves to the eyes. So refined is this pneuma that it has the nature of light itself. The pneuma does not leave the eye as a stream, but transforms the air in front of the eyes into an instrument of vision — a "chain reaction" extending as far as the visual field reaches. This avoids the objection that a human body could never generate a stream large enough to see a distant horizon. Vision is still, in effect, a special kind of reaching out to touch distant objects — the Stoic metaphor compared the visual stream to a walking-stick with which the viewer "taps" whatever is seen.

### 2. Intromissionism — Atomist Version (Epicureans)

Thin atomic films (*eidola*, "images") are constantly shed by all visible objects. When such a film reaches the eye, it collides with soul-atoms through the portal of the eye. Sight is reduced to touch from a distance, with the object sending something to the viewer rather than the viewer reaching out. The theory explained visual illusions: a square tower appearing round from afar is explained by the atomic film's sharp corners being knocked off by air resistance in transit. Weakness: the films should entangle with one another in mid-air, and it is unclear how they shrink to fit the eye.

### 3. Intromissionism — Aristotelian Version

For Aristotle, vision occurs when an illuminated transparent medium (air filled with sunlight) between viewer and object transmits the visual *form* (not matter) of the object to the eye, activating the potential of the visual faculty. Darkness prevents sight because unilluminated air cannot carry forms. Problem identified by John Philoponus: this theory cannot explain *directionality* — why can't we see what is behind us? The illuminated air behind us is also in contact with our eyes.

### 4. Geometrical Optics (Euclid, Ptolemy)

Euclid applied geometry to model vision: only objects lying on an unobstructed straight line to the eye are visible. The visual field is a cone whose vertex is at the eye. Mirrors are handled by geometry — the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection. The model is mathematically powerful but does not specify the physical process. Ptolemy added physical content: the cone represents visual *rays* emitted from the eyes (extramissionist), and this allows him to explain distance perception (the ray reaches the object) and refraction (rays are slowed in denser media, explaining why a stick in water looks bent).

## Arabic Engagement: Al-Kindī

[[al-kindi|Al-Kindī]] wrote extensively on optics, including numerous works on mirrors. He adopted a **geometrical extramissionism** — combining Ptolemy's visual ray theory with Euclid's geometrical framework — and used this to criticise Aristotle's intromissionism.

His key argument against Aristotle (originally made by Theon of Alexandria): if objects transmit visual forms through the air as Aristotle claimed, they would look the same from every angle. But a circle viewed obliquely appears oval, not circular. This angular distortion is what the visual ray theory predicts — the geometry of the rays changes with viewing angle — but what the Aristotelian form-transmission theory cannot easily explain.

Al-Kindī's positive theory:
- **Transparency**: air is transparent not because it actively carries forms (Aristotle) but simply because it does not intercept visual rays. Transparency is negative — the absence of interception — not an active Aristotelian potentiality.
- **Colour and visibility**: it is the element *earth* that intercepts visual rays and makes things visible. Air, fire, and water are too rarified. Different elemental proportions produce different colours; darker colours belong to things with more earth in them.
- **Blue sky**: explained by earthy particles (*exhalations*) suspended in the atmosphere intercepting and colouring the rays.
- **Illumination requirement**: vision occurs only when the visual ray and a ray of light fall on the same surface simultaneously. It is not the intervening medium (as in Aristotle) that must be illuminated, but the surface of the visible object itself. In principle, one could see through a void — though al-Kindī denied that voids exist.

## Al-Fārābī and the Prestige of Geometrical Optics

[[al-farabi|Al-Fārābī]] accepted the geometrical extramissionist framework even though he was a committed Aristotelian in other domains. In his *Enumeration of the Sciences*, he identified optics' main purpose as accounting for optical illusions and also alludes to its use in measuring heights of mountains — a practical application already discussed by al-Kindī. The fact that a rigorous Aristotelian could adopt extramissionism for vision illustrates how powerfully the geometrical tradition dominated the field in this period.

## The Problem that Opened the Way to Ibn al-Haytham

The deepest objection to extramissionist theories: if the action is all at the far end where rays contact the object, how does information return to the eye so that the viewer can register what the ray has touched? Every extramission theory implicitly requires *some* intromission (from object to eye). If something must come from the object anyway, the visual ray is redundant. This logical pressure pointed toward a purely intromissionist-geometrical synthesis — which is what Ibn al-Haytham achieved in his *Book of Optics* by positing that it is *point sources of light reflected from the object's surface* that enter the eye, along straight lines, and that the eye's cone structure selects and organises these rays.

## Significance

The debate over vision in the medieval Islamic tradition illustrates several features of the broader philosophical culture:

1. **Empirical pressure on theoretical frameworks**: the angular appearance of circles, the colour of the sky, the geometry of mirror reflections — specific observable phenomena drove the debate and constrained theory-choice.
2. **Mathematical modelling as a philosophical tool**: geometry provided precision unavailable to purely qualitative Aristotelian analysis.
3. **The translation-commentary cycle**: theories were not simply transmitted but actively adjudicated and modified, with Arabic-writing thinkers taking genuine positions rather than merely preserving Greek ones.
4. **Cross-disciplinary integration**: vision theory drew on Aristotelian natural philosophy, Galenic anatomy, Euclidean geometry, Ptolemaic astronomy, and Islamic theology's concern with divine providence in creation.
