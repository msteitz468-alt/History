## Definition

The "Persianate zone" is Hodgson's term for that portion of Islamdom in which Persian served as the primary language of polite culture—as contrasted to the "Arabic zone" comprising the Arabic-speaking lands west of Iraq and south of the Mediterranean coast. The term extends to cover "all cultural traditions, carried in Persian or reflecting Persian inspiration," including Turkish-language literature built on Persian models and the Persian-influenced literatures of Muslim India ([[hodgson-venture-of-islam-v2-1974]]).

The concept distinguishes cultural orientation from ethnicity or political affiliation. One need not be Iranian to participate in Persianate culture; the Seljuk Turks, the Ghaznavids, and later the Ottomans and Mughals all operated primarily within the Persianate cultural sphere despite their Turkic or mixed ethnic identities.

## Geographic Extent

At its peak in the High and Late Middle Ages, the Persianate zone encompassed:
- The Iranian plateau (core)
- Transoxiana and Central Asia
- Khurasan and Afghanistan
- Anatolia (under Seljuk-successor rule; where Rumi composed in Persian)
- Northern India and eventually the subcontinent under Muslim rule
- The steppe lands of the Mongol world, where Persian was the language of Ilkhanid and successor court culture

The "Arabic zone" to the west—Syria, Egypt, the Maghrib, Arabia—shared the same Islamic religion and Shari'ah but was effectively cut off from the most creative cultural currents of the Middle Periods. As Hodgson notes, "this ignorance [of Persian] only helped to cut off that portion of Islamdom from the most creative currents that were inspiring the majority of Muslim peoples."

## Turkish as a Third Classical Language

Within the Persianate zone, Turkish eventually emerged as a third classical literary language, but always in subordinate relation to Persian. Turkish literature "was based on the Persian tradition; it was almost as widespread in use geographically as was Persian, but in most places it was used in more limited social circles, and it never reached the level of Persian as a major cultural vehicle." Ottoman court poetry, for example, worked within Persian conventions even when composed in Turkish.

## Analytical Significance

The Persianate/Arabic zone distinction is Hodgson's revision of Toynbee's "Iranic" vs. "Arabic" civilization distinction. Hodgson accepts the underlying observation—there is a genuine cultural boundary—but criticizes Toynbee for "much exaggerating the isolation of Syria and Egypt as core of the 'Arabic' civilization." The Arabic zone was not culturally self-contained; it received and produced much within the shared Islamic framework, but was distinctively cut off from the Persianate literary tradition.

The Persianate zone concept has been widely adopted in subsequent Islamic studies scholarship, though sometimes with modifications. It is a fundamentally useful tool for avoiding the error of treating "Islamic civilization" as a single undifferentiated cultural entity.
