## Definition and Scope

In *Ḥayy ibn Yaqẓān*, Ibn Ṭufayl articulates a tripartite framework of spiritual practice organized around the concept of **mimesis** (imitation, *tashabbuh*): the soul's ascent toward God is accomplished by progressively imitating three ordered models — the animal, the celestial body, and the Necessarily Existent — corresponding to the three aspects of human nature. This framework provides the practical structure for the spiritual discipline Hayy develops after completing his philosophical investigation of the divine, bridging theory and contemplative practice.

The framework is not merely metaphorical. Ibn Ṭufayl describes Hayy's actual daily practices in each mode with considerable specificity, grounding an abstract metaphysical hierarchy in concrete bodily and contemplative regimens.

## The Three Modes

### First Mode: Animal Mimesis

Hayy recognizes that his body belongs to "the world of generation and decay" and makes demands — food, shelter, warmth — that cannot be entirely refused without destroying the instrument of contemplation. He therefore devises a strict minimalist ethic of bodily care:

**Diet**: He distinguishes three types of food (green vegetables; ripe fruit; animals and their products) and develops an ethical hierarchy for each, preferring ripe fruits whose seeds he can preserve and replant, then vegetables, and only as a last resort meat — always taking the most abundant species and never threatening the survival of a kind. "He chose the lesser of evils. He was forced to condone in himself the slighter form of opposition to His work."

**Shelter**: Minimal housing against weather, already in place.

**Quantity**: Only enough to prevent weakness from interfering with the second mode of imitation. "He felt it should be enough to stave off hunger, but no more."

This mode is explicitly described as a **necessary evil** — it handles sensory things that are "veils blocking out" contemplative experience. The goal is to minimize its demands, not to cultivate or refine them.

### Second Mode: Celestial Mimesis

The heavenly bodies provide Hayy's primary model of sustained consciousness directed toward the divine. He identifies three sets of celestial properties to imitate:

1. **Their action toward the sublunary world**: The stars warm, illuminate, and sustain life below without themselves being diminished. Hayy translates this into active compassion — attending to injured plants, clearing obstacles from water sources, helping trapped animals, tending the sick and encumbered. "He imitated their action by never allowing himself to see any plant or animal hurt, sick, encumbered, or in need without helping it if he could."

2. **Their intrinsic properties**: Transparency, luminescence, purity, and circular motion. Hayy translates this into **bodily cleanliness** (frequent washing, cleaning teeth and nails, scenting himself with plant oils, keeping clothes clean and fragrant) and **circular motion** (orbiting the island's shoreline, circumambulating his house or certain rocks, spinning in place until dizzy). "He did begin to sparkle with vitality, cleanliness, and beauty."

3. **Their relation to the Necessarily Existent**: Unbroken contemplative attention to the divine, total submission to His will, continuous awareness. Hayy translates this into sustained mental prayer — fixing the mind on the Necessarily Existent, cutting sensory bonds, restraining imagination, "trying with all his might to think only of Him, without idolatrously mixing any other thought with the thought of Him." The spinning practice serves this mode as well: fast enough rotation suppresses sensory and imaginative faculties, allowing the non-corporeal self to act more powerfully.

The second mode brings substantial contemplative continuity but is still incomplete: "one remains self-conscious and self-regarding" even in deep concentration on the divine — a residual subjecthood that itself becomes an obstacle to full union.

### Third Mode: Divine Mimesis

This is the summit — imitating the Necessarily Existent in His positive attributes (knowledge, power, wisdom, all reducing to His identity) and His negative attributes (absolute transcendence of physicality). For the positive attributes: to know the divine through a non-physical faculty is to participate in His self-knowledge, which is identical with Himself. For the negative: to eliminate every vestige of the physical from the self.

Hayy eventually recognizes that even the second mode retains physical elements — the circular motion is still motion, the care for animals still uses bodily faculties. He therefore abandons active practices and retreats entirely inward: "He would stay in his cave, sitting on the stone floor, head bent, eyes shut, oblivious to all objects of the senses and urges of the body, his thoughts and all his devotion focused on the Being Whose Existence is Necessity, alone and without rival."

The achievement of this mode is described as: "The third sort of imitation is attainment of the pure beatific experience, submersion, concentration on Him alone Whose existence is necessary. In this experience the self vanishes; it is extinguished, obliterated — and so are all other subjectivities. All that remains is the One, True identity, the Necessarily Existent."

## The Nested Logic

The three modes form a nested dependency:

- Mode 1 is required to sustain Mode 2.
- Mode 2 is required to enable and sustain Mode 3.
- Mode 3 is the goal that gives Mode 2 its purpose.
- Mode 1 is a necessary means, not an end, and should be minimized rather than cultivated.

This creates a practical philosophy of spiritual discipline that is simultaneously **ascetic** (minimizing bodily demands), **active-compassionate** (imitating celestial beneficence toward the world), and **contemplative** (suppressing all faculties short of the non-corporeal self).

## Relation to Classical Sources

The concept of *homoiōsis theō* (assimilation to God) originates in Plato (*Theaetetus* 176b) and runs through Middle Platonism and Neoplatonism. In the Arabic tradition it appears in the pseudo-Aristotelian *Theology of Aristotle* (an Arabic paraphrase of Plotinus), the Epistles of the Brethren of Purity (*Rasāʾil Ikhwān al-Ṣafāʾ*), and in Ibn Sīnā's ethical writings. Ibn Ṭufayl's originality lies in integrating this abstract concept with the specific Ptolemaic cosmology of celestial spheres as intermediate models, and in embodying the entire framework in a single character's developmental narrative.

## Significance

The framework provides a structure for understanding how the philosophical-mystical life is possible for a being who is simultaneously animal, rational, and potentially divine. It refuses both a purely contemplative asceticism that ignores the body's legitimate claims and a purely active compassion that never rises to contemplation. The three modes are not phases to be successively discarded but permanent, ongoing dimensions of the enlightened life — with Mode 1 perpetually managed, Mode 2 actively practiced, and Mode 3 pursued as the ultimate goal.
