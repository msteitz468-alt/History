# Augustan Imperial Expansion and Ideology

The territorial growth of the Roman empire under **[[augustus|Augustus]]** and the
**ideology of world conquest** that accompanied it. Treated in [[bowman-cah-v10-1996|CAH X]]
Ch. 4 (**Erich S. Gruen**), whose governing thesis frames the whole question: Augustus
was **"less concerned with a systematic plan for world dominion than with a systematic
construct of his image as world conqueror."**

## The central thesis: image over blueprint

Gruen rejects both poles of the older debate — that Augustus had a **grand strategy** of
defensible scientific frontiers (Luttwak, Syme on the Elbe–Danube line) and that he was a
**relentless imperialist** of world conquest checked only by the Varian disaster (Brunt,
Wells). Neither catches the **diversity and improvisation** of his foreign ventures:
*"Location, circumstances and contingencies determined decisions."* There was no uniform
plan. But running through every region is one constant — the **manufacture of a reputation
for martial supremacy**, often outrunning the reality of control.

The precedents were Republican: *Victoria*, not *Pax*, was the dominant numismatic and
oratorical theme of the Republic, triumphs its most coveted prize. Augustus inherited that
militarist culture and **monopolized** it.

## Diplomacy dressed as conquest

The clearest case is the **East**, where Augustus *"affected war but practised diplomacy."*

- **The Parthian standards (20 BCE).** The trophies and captives lost at
  [[battle-of-carrhae-53bce|Carrhae]] (53) and in Antony's campaign were recovered **by
  negotiation**, not battle — Phraates IV yielding them in a bargain that tacitly fixed the
  **Euphrates as the boundary** of spheres of influence. Yet Rome proclaimed it as Parthian
  **submission**: the Senate voted a triumph and triumphal arch, the coinage blared *signa
  recepta*, and the cuirass of the **Prima Porta statue** put the transfer at its centre.
- **Armenia.** The installation of Roman client kings (Tigranes, 20 BCE; later Ariobarzanes,
  Vonones) was advertised on coins as ***Armenia capta*** / ***Armenia recepta*** —
  "capture" of a kingdom secured without a battle. Real Roman control there was intermittent
  and, by Augustus' death, slipping.
- **Britain.** Augustus three times let it be known he was about to invade (34, 27, 26 BCE)
  — feeding the poets' expectation of conquest — then quietly **abandoned** the idea,
  reframing the submission-embassies of British princes as "good as a conquest, and much
  cheaper." Aggressiveness itself was *"a matter of policy."*

## Real conquest and the ad hoc client system

Elsewhere there *was* hard fighting, but rarely from a master plan:

- **Egypt, Ethiopia, Arabia.** Egypt annexed (30) and held as a special domain under an
  equestrian prefect; expansion beyond the First Cataract (Cornelius Gallus), the abortive
  **Arabian expedition of Aelius Gallus** (26/25), and the **Ethiopian campaigns of
  Petronius** (to Napata) — extending suzerainty over the Dodecaschoenus.
- **Asia Minor & the Near East.** A web of **client princes** (Polemo of Pontus, Archelaus
  of Cappadocia, Amyntas of Galatia, [[herod-the-great|Herod]] of Judaea), generally kept in
  place regardless of past loyalty to Antony — but **provincialized ad hoc** when a king died
  awkwardly (**Galatia**, 25, on Amyntas' death) or misruled (**Judaea**, AD 6, on
  Archelaus' deposition). Gruen denies any settled policy of converting clients into provinces.
- **Spain.** The long, bloody **Cantabrian–Asturian War** (26–19) — Augustus' last personal
  command (and illness) — was driven less by strategy or economics than by the affront that a
  corner of Iberia still defied Rome; its close, marked by the **shutting of Janus' gates**
  (25), capped his *Autobiography*.
- **The North.** The **Alpine** conquest (Raetia, Noricum, 15 BCE, by Tiberius and Drusus),
  and the great **Illyrian/Pannonian** campaigns culminating in the brutal **Pannonian–
  Dalmatian revolt** (AD 6–9), carried Roman power to the **middle Danube** — the one
  enduring strategic gain, the new provinces of Pannonia and Dalmatia.

## Germany and the limits of advertisement

Gruen reads the crossing of the **Rhine** not as a drive for an Elbe frontier but as an
extension of the **policing of Gaul**: the river was "an artificial and largely ineffectual
barrier," Germanic tribes living on both banks and repeatedly stirring Gallic unrest. The
campaigns of **Drusus** (12–9 BCE, to the Elbe, where he died) and **Tiberius** were
*"invasions rather than conquests, the Germans intimidated rather than subdued"* — garrisons,
deportations, and altars to Augustus, but never a settled province. The
**[[battle-of-teutoburg-forest-9ce|Varian disaster]]** (AD 9) Gruen treats as **less of a
caesura** than is usually claimed: Augustus made no public surrender of Germany, rushed
Tiberius and then Germanicus back with eight legions, and continued offensive demonstrations
— to *"controvert any suggestion of Roman weakness."*

## The monuments of the image

The ideology is legible in the regime's self-presentation, which juxtaposed *pax* with the
**victory that produced it**: the *Res Gestae* (preamble: *"the achievements whereby he
subjected the world to the power of the Roman people"*); the triple triumph of 29 and the two
cities named **Nicopolis**; the temple of **Mars Ultor** and the **Forum Augustum** lined
with Republican *triumphatores*; the **Prima Porta cuirass**; and even the **Ara Pacis**,
which Gruen reads not as a counterpoint but as the same balance — *"the accomplishment of
peace is inseparable from success in war,"* its shadow-obelisk (the Solarium) pointing at the
altar on Augustus' birthday and itself a memorial of the **subjection of Egypt**.

## Contested

- **Grand strategy vs. improvisation.** Luttwak's "hegemonic" system and the Syme/Kraft
  Elbe–Danube design vs. Gruen's contingency. The "scientific frontier" reading is now widely
  doubted.
- **Expansionist vs. defensive Augustus.** Brunt/Wells (a world-conqueror checked by Varus)
  vs. those who see deliberate limits. Gruen rejects the "limits" evidence (the deathbed
  *consilium coercendi imperii* in Tacitus) as probably **Tiberian**, serving Tiberius' own
  caution.
- **Varus as turning point** — major caesura (the conventional view) vs. Gruen's stress on
  continuity.
