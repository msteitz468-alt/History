# Bondage and Manpower in Southeast Asia

A central organising insight of Anthony Reid's
[[reid-southeast-asia-age-of-commerce-v1-1988|*Southeast Asia in the Age of Commerce*]] (Vol. 1, Ch.
IV) — and of the wider historiography of the region — is that the decisive scarce resource in
pre-colonial [[southeast-asia|Southeast Asia]] was **not land but people**. In a thinly peopled world
(see [[southeast-asia|the ~5.5 persons/km² demography]]) of abundant forest, impermanent buildings, and
insecure property, **"it was in followers that power and wealth were primarily expressed."** Wealth and
status were measured in the number of dependants a "lord" could attract and hold, and the whole social
and political order — descent groups, warfare, the state — turned on the **vertical bond of obligation
between two individuals**, the master providing protection, patronage, and the means of ritual life, the
bondsman providing labour, military service, and presence at the feasts that displayed the master's
power.

## Debt as the master-bond

Bondsmen were acquired by inheritance, as perquisites of office, as marriage gifts, by capture in war,
or by seeking protection — but the **most characteristic source of obligation was debt**. An inability
to pay debts or judicial fines could lead to outright sale as a slave; short of that, the advance of
capital, or the need to meet the unavoidable expenses of **marriage and death rituals**, or the
difficulty of finding **bride-wealth**, bound the debtor (or his labour, via bride-service) to a
patron. The result was the great **household/compound**: an establishment gathering close family,
dependent relatives, concubine-slaves and their children, adoptees, and free and unfree followers, in
which — Reid stresses — the society itself cared less whether a given person was "slave,"
"debt-bondsman," or "poor relation" than that all **belonged to a certain man or compound**. It was
these compounds, not individual houses or whole cities, that were typically walled. The system might be
called "patriarchal" except that **women, too, headed descent groups** and held important places within
them (see [[women-in-precolonial-southeast-asia]]).

## Manpower, warfare, and the state

The manpower logic reshaped **warfare** (Ch. IV). Because the object of war was to **seize people, not
territory**, fighting aimed at intimidation, trickery, and the capture or displacement of populations
rather than at slaughter; battle casualties were low, but famine, disease, and deportation following
campaigns were demographically devastating. Nobles brought their own men at their own expense and were
**reluctant to risk in battle the followers who were the basis of their own standing** ("each feared to
lose his slaves, which are their only wealth"). The weaker party characteristically **fled into the
forest** rather than defend a city, which is why fortification was rare and capitals were impermanent.
Reid argues that European **ruthlessness** — a willingness to accept heavy casualties and fight to the
death — was a major reason small European forces could win crucial victories over far larger Southeast
Asian armies. The state itself was a structure for mobilising and counting manpower (Java, Siam, Burma,
and Vietnam all kept household registers for tax and corvée), producing the characteristic combination
of **sharply stratified hierarchy with a loose, fluid political structure** that so baffled European
observers — kings of great personal power resting on flourishing ports and webs of personal obligation
rather than on fixed territorial bureaucracy.

## Significance

This frame distinguishes Southeast Asian states from the land-and-tax-based agrarian empires of India
and China and explains much that puzzled outsiders: the kinglessness Legazpi found in the Philippines,
the portability of cities, the prestige of slaves-as-wealth, and the bloodless-but-depopulating
character of regional war. It links directly to the [[southeast-asian-age-of-commerce|age of commerce]]
(trade and bullion enlarged the followers a ruler could command) and to the demographic pattern
(warfare over manpower kept populations low). It is the social substrate beneath the
[[indianization-of-southeast-asia|Indianized]] and Islamic-era polities alike.

## Limitations and critiques

The "manpower state" model can be overdrawn: the densely settled wet-rice cores (Red River delta,
Upper Burma, Central Java) and the more bureaucratic, Sinicised **Vietnamese** state fit it less well,
and **Victor Lieberman** emphasises long-run administrative integration and territorial consolidation
on the mainland that the pure manpower-and-followers picture understates. The category "slavery" also
covers a wide spectrum — from chattel war-captives to honoured debt-clients — and translating it by the
Atlantic-world term risks distortion. As a heuristic for why power in the region was reckoned in people
rather than acres, however, it remains foundational.

## From Miksic & Goh, *Ancient Southeast Asia* (2017)

[[miksic-goh-ancient-southeast-asia-2017|Miksic & Goh]] document **Classic-period** legal/institutional detail that anchors Reid's early-modern manpower model in earlier Javanese, Balinese, and Khmer practice.

**Java and Bali: debt and sima sanctuary.** Late 9th-c. Java: villagers unable to repay debts fled to *sima* (tax-free religious zones); debtors apparently could not pursue them there — sanctuary function of religious estates. *Sima* dwellers free to leave; status desirable especially for wage workers; inscriptions limit occupational numbers to prevent tax escape. Balinese debtors could likewise find refuge from creditors in *sima* and pay debts in installments, as in Java. Concurrent_with [[mataram-kingdom]].

**Angkor contrast.** In other regions servitude seems consequence of particular misfortune (unpaid debt or war capture); at Angkor, people listed as property/deeds of donation — serfdom-like **inherited unfreedom**, "the fate of normal human beings" rather than misfortune-based servitude. Contrasts_with Angkorian serfdom vs Javanese/Balinese debt-bondage spectrum — important internal differentiation within the manpower-state model.
