## Definition and Scope

Illuminationism (ishrāq, lit. "illumination" or "oriental dawn") is the philosophical tradition inaugurated by [[suhrawardi-yahya]] and continued by a lineage of commentators, primarily in Iran and the eastern Islamic world. It presents itself as a revival of ancient wisdom shared by sages of Greece, Persia, and India, while in practice constituting a systematic re-imagining and critique of [[ibn-sina|Avicenna's]] Peripatetic philosophy expressed in the metaphorical language of light and darkness.

Adamson notes that the label was shrewdly chosen: "ishrāqī" is cognate with Avicenna's own experimental self-designation mashriqī ("eastern"), but where that term caused confusion, Suhrawardī's exercise in branding was "a great success." The tradition should be understood as one of four interlocking branches of [[post-avicennan-philosophy]], alongside Ashʿarite kalām, Shiite philosophical theology, and philosophical Sufism ([[adamson-philosophy-islamic-world-2016]]).

## Core Doctrines

### Metaphysics of Light

The central innovation is the replacement of Avicenna's metaphysics of *existence* with a metaphysics of *light*. God is the "Light of lights" (*Nūr al-anwār*), from which radiate a hierarchy of immaterial "pure lights." Physical bodies are described as "dark barriers" — not mere absences but active obstructions to luminous being. Suhrawardī insists this is not metaphor: "He is not using the word 'light' metaphorically … God is the light of heaven and earth [Koran 24:35]" ([[adamson-philosophy-islamic-world-2016]]).

The key distinction from Avicennan emanationism: whereas Avicenna equated God's primacy with necessary existence, Suhrawardī identifies the highest principle with self-evident, self-luminous self-awareness. Light is "that which is immediately manifest to whoever or whatever beholds it." Self-awareness thus becomes the criterion of immateriality, and immaterial self-aware beings are lights of varying degrees of purity.

### Knowledge by Presence (*ʿilm al-ḥuḍūrī*)

Suhrawardī rejects the Aristotelian-Avicennan theory that knowledge consists in the acquisition of universal concepts abstracted from particulars. Instead, he proposes that all genuine knowledge is "knowledge by presence" — direct, unmediated apprehension. The paradigm is vision: sight occurs simply by a visible, illuminated object being present to the eye with no obstacle between them. Presence is defined as "the non-existence of absence."

This epistemology allows individual sense-perceptions to count as fully genuine knowledge, and permits God to know particulars directly rather than only "in a universal way" as Avicenna had argued — a major theological advantage.

### Critique of Definition and Peripatetic Logic

Suhrawardī argues that definitions are useless as instruments for acquiring knowledge. If one already knows the essential features that make up a definition, one already knows the defined thing. If not, there is no guarantee the list of features is complete. Knowledge by presence renders definitions superfluous. He also simplified Avicenna's logical apparatus, reducing the ten Aristotelian categories to five and arguing that negative propositions can be rephrased as affirmations with negative predicates.

### Essence–Existence Distinction Rejected

Against Avicenna (and in contrast to Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī's defense of the real distinction), Suhrawardī argues that existence is not a real feature of things out in the world but only a mental judgment. Real essences existing independently of existence would themselves need to exist — an absurd regress. "Existence, contingency, and relations are things applied only by the mind (*maḥmūlāt ʿaqliyya*)."

### Platonic Forms as Dominating Lights

Suhrawardī revives the Platonic theory of Forms, which had been rejected by all Islamic Aristotelians. He calls physical individuals "talismans" and their Forms "dominating lights" or "archetypes" — not universals in the mind, but self-subsistent lights that emanate form into many bodily individuals.

### World of Images (*ʿālam al-mithāl*)

A distinctive Illuminationist metaphysical innovation: a fourth realm of being interposed between the intellective and the physical worlds, populated by immaterial but imaginable entities — jinn, demonic beings (*shayāṭīn*), and the objects of prophetic vision and dreams. This realm resolves a longstanding problem in Neoplatonism (no room for intermediate supernatural beings) and provides a metaphysical basis for Prophetic and mystical experience.

### Transmigration

Suhrawardī considered the transmigration of human souls into animal bodies at least possible, invoking "Buddha and the eastern sages" alongside Pythagorean-Platonic precedent. Later Illuminationists divided on the question: Shahrazūrī affirmed it confidently; Ibn Kammūna suspended judgment; Quṭb al-Dīn al-Shīrāzī stressed Suhrawardī's own tentativeness.

## The Illuminationist Lineage

Illuminationism after Suhrawardī was not a school with direct teacher-student transmission. The three principal early commentators — [[ibn-kammuna]] (Jewish, likely remained so throughout life), Shahrazūrī, and Quṭb al-Dīn al-Shīrāzī (student of [[nasir-al-din-tusi]]) — engaged critically with Suhrawardī, sometimes departing significantly from his positions:

- **Ibn Kammūna** rejected Suhrawardī's denial of the soul's pre-eternity, argued for the soul's eternal pre-existence on Avicennan grounds, and was instrumental in transmitting Illuminationist ideas from Syria eastward.
- **Shahrazūrī** wrote a full commentary on the *Philosophy of Illumination* and affirmed transmigration on the authority of mystical sages.
- **Quṭb al-Dīn al-Shīrāzī** wrote both a commentary on Suhrawardī (drawing heavily on Shahrazūrī) and contributed to astronomy and medicine at Marāgha. He sometimes reverted to Avicennan positions (e.g., restoring fire as a fourth element) where he found Suhrawardī's revisions unconvincing.

Illuminationism was subsequently absorbed into the Safavid synthesis, most notably by Mullā Ṣadrā, who stood at the intersection of Avicennism, Illuminationism, and philosophical Sufism.

## Relation to Other Currents

Illuminationism participates in what Adamson terms "Avicennan scholasticism" — the dominant mode of later Islamic philosophy in which originality is expressed through critical engagement with Avicenna, not rejection of him. Though Suhrawardī positions himself as Avicenna's opponent, his system presupposes Avicennan categories at nearly every step. Illuminationism also overlaps substantially with [[sufism|philosophical Sufism]], sharing the ideal of direct mystical vision over discursive proof, while maintaining a rigorous critical edge toward Peripatetic demonstrations that sets it apart from purely devotional Sufism.

## Historiography

Research on post-formative Islamic philosophy, including Illuminationism, was until recently neglected by European scholars. Adamson notes that "research on him [Suhrawardī] and his followers is in its early stages," with most surviving material in unstudied manuscripts in Istanbul, Iran, Egypt, and India. The field has advanced significantly since c. 2010. The standard treatment of the Illuminationist tradition as a unified "school" is an oversimplification; the critical independence of figures like Ibn Kammūna complicates any linear account.
