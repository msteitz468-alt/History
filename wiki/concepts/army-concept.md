# Army Concept

The **Army Concept** (term coined/used by Andrew F. Krepinevich Jr. in *The Army and Vietnam*, 1986) refers to the U.S. Army's deeply institutionalized paradigm for how wars should be fought. It emerged from the service's dominant experience in World War II (particularly the European Theater breakout and advance 1944–45) and was only marginally modified by Korea.

## Core Characteristics

- **Mid-intensity / conventional war focus**: Preparation for clashes between large, modern field armies using combined arms, rather than insurgencies, colonial policing, or "low-intensity conflict."
- **Firepower substitution for blood**: Heavy reliance on artillery, air support, and material to minimize U.S. casualties. The preferred solution to tactical problems is "more firepower."
- **Big-unit operations and attrition**: Preference for division- and corps-level maneuvers aimed at destroying enemy main forces and seizing terrain, measured by body counts, sorties flown, and tonnage of ordnance.
- **Organizational and career incentives aligned to the paradigm**: Training, promotion, unit design (ROAD divisions), and command emphasis all reinforced conventional norms. Short one-year tours, "ticket punching," and the prestige of command in large formations over advisory or pacification roles.

## Origins and Reinforcement

The Army Concept was codified in field manuals, service school curricula (Command and General Staff College, etc.), and force structure decisions in the late 1940s and 1950s. The "worst-case" Soviet threat in Europe was also the "preferred" threat because it matched existing doctrine, training, and equipment. Brushfire wars and insurgencies in the decolonizing world were treated as special cases that did not require permanent changes to the core paradigm.

From the advisory years evidence (1954–1965), the Concept manifested concretely in ARVN force structuring and training: initial JCS proposals for a 234,000-man conventional army were scaled but still produced "seven 'standard' divisions" modeled as "a very close parallel on a considerably lighter scale of the division as we knew [it] in World War II," with light divisions disbanded because "they were so light that they were practically worthless." Training programs were "almost exact copies of corresponding U.S. Army training courses," including irrelevant large-unit maneuvers and 20-mile marches unsuited to Vietnamese physique and the actual threat. MAAG leadership (e.g., Gen. Samuel "Hanging Sam" Williams) was instructed "to organize a military establishment in Vietnam that would be capable of fending off attacks from the North" and conducted corps maneuvers accordingly, while paramilitary forces (Civil Guard, Self Defense Corps) were "generally ignored" by the Army. "It was easier for the Army to envision a Korea-type threat in Vietnam... than the insurgency threat."

**Daddis challenge to the attrition reduction (Ch. 3, Range 02 from [[sources/daddis-westmorelands-war-2014]])**: The reduction of Westmoreland/MACV to "pure attrition" / body-count focus is a "myth." Actual concept integrated attrition as *means* (for "long pull" + shield) with explicit pacification/RD and RVNAF pillars. "Westmoreland’s strategy rested on the three pillars of attrition, pacification, and ARVN training." "defeating the enemy’s ground forces served only as a means to the larger end of securing the south’s population." "the main purpose of defeating the enemy through offensive operations... must be to provide the opportunity through RD to get at the heart of the matter, which is the population of South Vietnam." Mission holistic ("assist and support the RVNAF in their efforts to defeat communist subversive insurgency, and to accelerate effective GVN control"). "one war" integration of "all political, military, economic, and security (police) programs." "catchphrases like 'attrition strategy'... fail to convey the true nature of Westmoreland’s approach." "The claim that 'several years were to pass before the American command began to accept the crucial role of population security' simply does not hold up..." Metrics: Honolulu goals (secure pop +10% etc.); HES "graded hamlets in both security and development areas"; "body counts... only one of many statistical indicators." PROVN incorporated substantially. Three-phase sustained campaign. `contrasts_with:` narrower Krepinevich/Nagl readings of the Concept's exclusivity in Vietnam application. See also hub [[hubs/warfare/cold-war/vietnam-war/vietnam-war]] and [[events/vietnam-war]].

Even when the Kennedy administration pushed counterinsurgency and "flexible response," the Army largely absorbed the new rhetoric while preserving its conventional core (e.g., airmobility developed as an enhancement to conventional mobility rather than a tool for population control or small-unit COIN). Army leadership responses included Gen. George Decker's "Any good soldier can handle guerrillas," Gen. Earle Wheeler's assertion that "the essence of the problem in Vietnam is military," and Gen. Maxwell Taylor's view that counterinsurgency was "just a form of small war" with "not much heart" in the effort. Doctrinal output remained thin and framed conventionally: the 1962 FM 100-5 stated operations against irregular forces "are primarily offensive in nature. Thus, the conventional force must plan and seize the initiative at the outset." No dedicated Army-level counterinsurgency manual existed by early 1965; training hours were often padded by relabeling conventional subjects.

**Willbanks Ch. 1 evidence (advisory implementation, exclusive range):** "In the early years of the US training mission in Vietnam, there was some effort to train the South Vietnamese forces for civic action and pacification. However, over time as the insurgency gained momentum, less and less emphasis was given to the special aspects of counter-guerrilla warfare; the focus shifted to preparing the force for the worst threat scenario—guerrilla main force units and an external invasion from North Vietnam." "the ARVN was structured and trained to meet a possible external attack by the PAVN, not the actual struggle against the National Liberation Front." US imposed doctrine/tactics; collective training starved by ops and politicization; advisors ill-suited (no lang/COIN exp); post-1965 ARVN "relegated to 'a secondary endeavor.'" "conscious decision to form the South Vietnamese forces in the image of US forces" locked in dependence. See [[sources/willbanks-vietnam-the-course-of-a-conflict-2018]] Range 01. `contributed_to:` [[events/vietnam-war]] advisory phase; contrasts Daddis integration nuance.

## Consequences in Vietnam

According to Krepinevich, the Army Concept produced a force and a strategy (Westmoreland's attrition / search-and-destroy approach) that was structurally unsuited to defeating a nationalist insurgency that drew strength from the rural population. Pacification and the "other war" were consistently subordinated. Technological fixes (herbicides, sensors, helicopter gunships) were applied within the old paradigm rather than prompting a paradigm shift. The result was a war of tactics without strategy, high body counts that did not translate into control of the population, and eventual political collapse of support in the United States.

**Intervention years (Ch. 6–7 evidence):** Westmoreland's explicit concept sent U.S. forces "against the hardcore DRV/VC forces in reaction and search and destroy operations" while ARVN handled populated areas. Force calculations fudged (ARVN battalions = VC; U.S. =2x) and "phase 3" declared to justify 3:1 ratios. Ia Drang "validated" big-unit firepower. 95% combat battalions in search-and-destroy late 1966; 86% in mid-1967. PROVN (Army Staff study) called for pacification priority and unified planning; suppressed as "conceptual document." McNamara: "the large-unit operations war... is largely irrelevant to pacification as long as we do not lose it." Body count as metric produced "if the commander before you had killed X number of vc, you had best kill X + 1." Artillery/air evaluated on expenditures. 9th Division (Ewell) institutionalized kill ratios; "We really blew a lot of civilians away." "the worst personnel policy in history" (12-mo tours, 6-mo commands) incentivized quick "box scores" over sustained security. Defoliation (RANCH HAND) destroyed >1.5M acres in 1967 peak; RAND found "over 500 civilians experience crop loss for every ton of rice denied the vc." "Army operations in South Vietnam were oriented overwhelmingly toward the Army Concept... Pacification was the 'other' war... It was far easier to fall back on the logistical and technological elements of strategy—sensors, ground radars, firepower on call, helicopter gunships, herbicides and defoliants—all of which gave the Army the opportunity to wage counterinsurgency American-style." (Krepinevich).

In the advisory years this rigidity was visible in the preference for offensive sweeps and "find, fix, fight, and finish the enemy" over population security. MAAG chief Lt. Gen. Lionel McGarr exhorted advisers to "'out conventional' the unconventionalists!" despite acknowledging that "We cannot hope to match vc foot mobility with mechanical mobility alone." The Strategic Hamlets program (adopted 1962) suffered from MAAG's emphasis on military sweeps rather than sustained local security; Operation SUNRISE involved forced resettlement that alienated peasants, and data on "fortified hamlets" was grossly falsified (8,000 claimed but most "shams"). MACV under Harkins (chosen for lack of COIN experience and close Taylor ties) continued to prioritize ARVN offensive operations. "Since we did not know where the enemy was, ten times we launched a military operation, nine times we missed the Viet Cong, and the tenth time we struck right on the head of the population." (Ngo Dinh Nhu).

Nagl (Learning to Eat Soup..., Ch. 6) deepens the cultural mechanism: Harkins "exemplified the traditional army 'can-do' spirit of professional optimism, even at the expense of reality" ("I am optimistic, and I am not going to allow my staff to be pessimistic"; napalm "puts the fear of God into the Viet Cong. And that is what counts"). WWII/Korea generation "had become so accustomed to winning... that they could no longer imagine they could lose"; "Harkins’ strategy was a fantasy of the past, but the fantasy was real because it had been institutionalized." "chilling effect" on reporting. ARVN design "in direct insubordination" to guidance for internal threat; "The ARVN, as did the U.S. Army, sent a bullet (or a shell, bomb) rather than a man wherever possible." ARMY magazine 1962: guerrilla work "the burial place for the future of any officer"; general-purpose soldier need not learn language or "subtleties" of training forces "without a military tradition." Army "more inhibited. Its memory is long and its problem-solving techniques emphasize caution, thoroughness, and a highly developed, ingrained degree of skepticism." "If the Army has been slow... because the Army’s battle for survival required it to concentrate on other things." Hutton (Nagl): "The organizational culture of the US Army is very big and hard to change... It’s much easier to change one regiment [in the British Army] than the U.S. Army." Even Kennedy push met resistance; "the essence of the problem is military" solvable as WWII/Korea. Doctrine lagged and was US-developed, not in-theater. 

## Post-Vietnam Fate

After Vietnam the Army largely sought to avoid low-intensity missions again, designing forces (e.g., later Light Infantry Divisions) still optimized for conventional contingencies and attempting to convert future irregular threats into problems it knew how to solve with firepower and maneuver. The Concept proved highly resilient.

**Tet 1968 as non-transformative shock:** Despite MACV viewing Tet as a military success ("Battle of the Bulge"; 37k+ body count, VCI mauled), the offensive discredited the strategy with civilian leadership (Wheeler: enemy retained "will and the capability to continue"; "RD program had suffered a severe setback"; no victory plan beyond indefinite attrition). Troop ceiling imposed, Westmoreland recalled, Vietnamization accelerated. Within the Army, however, "the Army had convinced itself that it had won the Tet Offensive; therefore, no soul-searching was necessary." "enormous casualties proved it: the Army was winning." Post-Tet "one war" guidance under Abrams (population security criterion) produced minimal field change; Ewell continued extreme kill-ratio operations (158:1 peaks); SPEEDY EXPRESS and Hamburger Hill pursued exchange ratios in populated or remote areas with limited security payoff. Komer: "There was no change in strategy whatsoever... myth of a change in strategy is a figment of media imagination."

**Paths untaken evaluation:** The attrition strategy, invasion of North, and Laotian incursion (EL PASO/Summers barrier) were "all heavily weighted toward employing the Army’s men and resources to win the mid-intensity war characterized in phase 3 insurgency operations." Enclave and demographic-frontier alternatives (Taylor; Warnke/ISA) shifted emphasis to securing the populated coastal plain and denying insurgent access via strike teams and spoiling actions; these "offered advantages... primarily through buying time for the South Vietnamese at a reduced cost in U.S. casualties and resources" and "placed greater emphasis on defeating the insurgent threat." Adoption would have required reallocating resources away from the Concept's preferred big-unit model.

**Lessons unlearned and resilience:** Post-war SOF rebuilding accompanied "No More Vietnams" conditions (vital interests, sufficient force, "clear intention of winning," public support) that focused blame outward rather than on doctrine and force structure. FM 100-20 (1981) largely restated 1967 counterguerrilla doctrine, prioritizing "strike campaigns" and main-force destruction; "winning the hearts and minds of the people finishes a poor second." LIDs retained heavy mid-intensity missions; intelligence and personnel systems continued to favor conventional expertise. "As in the Kennedy years... we are turning back again in the hope that the next war will be a conventional war." (Yarborough). "Low-intensity warfare represents the most likely arena of future conflict for the Army, and counterinsurgency the most demanding contingency." The Army "is trying, through the six tests enunciated by Secretary Weinberger, to transform it into something it can handle." "America’s enemies are not going to play to its military strong suits; rather, they will exploit its weak points." "in spite of its anguish in Vietnam, the Army has learned little of value" (Conclusions).

## Nagl's Engagement and Cross-Cultural Extension (Ch. 1)

Nagl explicitly builds on and tests Krepinevich's thesis: the U.S. Army's failure to learn COIN in Vietnam is "best explained by the differing organizational cultures of the two armies" and "the American army was not a learning institution... organizationally disposed against learning how to fight and win counterinsurgency warfare." The Army Concept is the concrete manifestation of the American pre-campaign culture (continental, conventional, essence = "ground combat by organized regular divisional units"; preference for "mid-intensity, or conventional, war and a reliance on high volumes of firepower"). This rigidity prevented adaptation even when presented with British Malaya lessons via the British Advisory Mission. "It was as a result of this failure to become a 'learning institution' that the American army did not learn how to fight a counterinsurgency war in Vietnam." Nagl contrasts it directly with the British Army's colonial/regimental culture that enabled learning in Malaya (see [[nagl-learning-to-eat-soup-with-a-knife-2005]] and [[learning-institution]] Question Set #1).

## Application of the Army Concept in the Fighting Years, 1965-1972 (Nagl Ch. 7)

Nagl's Chapter 7 applies the Army Concept thesis directly to operations and organizational behavior during the main U.S. combat period. The pre-existing conventional orientation blocked effective counterinsurgency even as the war's realities became clear.

**MACOV and doctrinal mismatch (Ch. 7):** The Mechanized/Armor Combat Operations in Vietnam (MACOV) study recognized the need for “Area Type Warfare” given “the elusive nature [of the] enemy and insufficient friendly intelligence regarding the location and activities of the enemy” requiring units to “expect contact with the enemy at any time and from any direction.” Yet doctrine and practice remained “oriented in relation to its past rather than to the realities of the situation faced: ‘Current U.S. doctrine concerning combat tactics and techniques employed by armor and mechanized infantry units is based principally on experience gained during World War II and the Korean War.’” 

**Force structure and support realities:** The Army “remained organized in corps and divisions despite the success of such experiments as CIDG and CAP, which incorporated small numbers of American soldiers into local fighting units to provide population security.” ARVN “remained dependent on large infusions of American artillery and close air support, logistics, and helicopters; the Regional Forces and Popular Forces were a poor stepchild until the aftermath of the Tet Offensive made ‘Vietnamization’ a priority.” Both U.S. and emulated ARVN forces “used air power and artillery indiscriminately to destroy enemy forces — and, too often, the population of SVN.”

**Figure 7-3 verdict on COIN doctrine (Ch. 7):** In the fighting years 1965-1972 the Army Concept produced failure on every criterion for successful counterinsurgency doctrine: Victory: No; Objective: No; Unity of command: No; Minimum necessary force: No; Mass/appropriate structure for threat: No. “In short, the U.S. Army’s concept of how to fight and win precluded the development of a successful counterinsurgency doctrine in South Vietnam.”

**ARCOV study limited scope (Ch. 7):** The 1966 Army Combat Operations in Vietnam (ARCOV) study, prompted by the need to adapt structure “still configured to fight a mechanized war in Germany,” analyzed engagements and recommended light, mobile TO&E changes (four line companies, added LAWs and machine guns, helicopter mobility for battalion/brigade elements). “Virtually all of the changes that we recommended to the TO & E of the maneuver battalion were adopted eventually.” But per Colonel (Ret.) Powell Hutton: “The changes were all in line with a conventional combat orientation; ‘We were not into pacification at all.’”

**Institutional barriers to recognizing Concept failure (Ch. 7):** Huge MACV size and structure “made not only change but also learning extremely difficult.” Westmoreland refused an intermediate Army Component Headquarters. Staffs screened seniors from bad news (John Paul Vann: even best subordinates “continually screen him [Westmoreland] from the realities of the situation in Vietnam”). Robert Komer: “Viet Nam was a tragedy of bureaucratic inability to adapt to unconventional situations.” Institutional “can-do” and career protection prevented candid assessment; civilians in DoD assessed more dispassionately because “not fettered with a rigorous belief that firepower yielded results” and did not fear careers hinged on proving bullets and bombs would succeed.

**Post-Tet limits (Ch. 7):** Only Tet’s obvious failure prompted political masters to impose CORDS and replace Westmoreland with Abrams (who had earlier recognized problems via PROVN and pushed “One War”). Yet “the organizational culture of the United States Army precluded organizational learning on counterinsurgency during the Vietnam War.” Abrams “confronted a culture that by then was so entrenched in its attitude that even the MACV commander could not change it.” (See Figure 7-4 for Question Set #1 results showing theoretical thinking and local doctrine development remained blocked.)

This Ch. 7 evidence (cross-referenced heavily by Nagl to [[krepinevich-the-army-and-vietnam-1986]]) shows the Army Concept not merely as pre-war inheritance but as active operational and institutional constraint throughout the fighting years. ## Daddis nuance on doctrinal and intellectual engagement (Range 01 grounding)

While the Army Concept emphasized conventional firepower and big-unit operations (per Krepinevich/Nagl), Daddis (*Westmoreland's War*, Range 01) documents that US Army officers in the 1950s–early 1960s "were not seduced entirely by the prospects of conventional war in Europe" and "found the topic of unconventional warfare worthy of intellectual energy." Doctrine and education engaged Maoist theory, decolonization, limited war lessons from Korea, and broad definitions. Counterinsurgency was defined comprehensively as "those military, paramilitary, political, economic, psychological, and civic actions taken by a government to defeat subversive insurgency" with emphasis that "military action... 'only a part of the overall internal defense and development effort.'" "Prevailing doctrine increasingly viewed insurgency and counterinsurgency as 'political, social, and economic rather than military problems.'" Westmoreland personally advanced this as West Point superintendent (Counterinsurgency Committee; 54 hours integrated instruction). "A review of the army’s doctrine, education system, and professional journals on the eve of the Vietnam War seems crucial for understanding how both the US Army and William Westmoreland approached the development of strategy..." "to claim that a conventional mindset caused defeat in Vietnam is at very least incomplete." `contrasts_with:` rigid "inability to adapt" portrayals; `contributed_to:` more precise debate on what was attempted vs. executed. ## Historiography

Krepinevich's thesis is widely accepted in U.S. military history circles as the leading explanation for Army performance in Vietnam on the operational/doctrinal level. Critics note it downplays civilian political constraints and the actual performance of some units, and that "better COIN" might still not have produced a sustainable South Vietnamese state given GVN weaknesses. The work remains the starting point for any serious analysis of U.S. Army adaptation (or non-adaptation) in Vietnam.

Tet Offensive exposed the political bankruptcy of the Concept without prompting internal change: Army measured success by body counts and exchange ratios while civilians saw the failure of strategy to produce lasting security or a victory plan. Post-Tet reorientation rhetoric (Abrams "one war") did not alter field incentives or resource allocation; attrition practices continued. Evaluation of untaken paths (Ch. 10) demonstrates that enclave/demographic strategies aligned better with counterinsurgency requirements than the Concept-congruent alternatives (invasion, Laotian barrier) that the Army favored. Post-war resilience of the Concept—manifest in doctrine (FM 100-20 restating prior conventional counterguerrilla manuals), force design (LIDs with mid-intensity primacy), and "lessons" focused on civilian will rather than organizational change—confirms Krepinevich's core argument that the Army Concept is deeply embedded and resistant to low-intensity environments. "the Army has learned little of value" from Vietnam while policymakers endorsed the misperceptions, risking repetition against adaptive adversaries.
