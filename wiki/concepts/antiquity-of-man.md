# Antiquity of Man (the Deep-Time Revolution)

The **antiquity of man** refers to the mid-nineteenth-century scientific establishment — and the surrounding cultural controversy — that the human species is vastly older than the roughly six thousand years allowed by literal biblical chronology. It was one of the foundational intellectual shifts that made "prehistory" thinkable at all: only once humans were granted a deep past stretching back into the geological record could there be a history before written records.

## Definition and Scope

The concept is **etic and historiographical**: it names a transformation in how the past was conceived, not a period or event in the past itself. Its core claim is that human beings coexisted with extinct Pleistocene animals (mammoth, woolly rhinoceros, cave bear) during a remote "Stone Age," and that the span of human existence must therefore be measured in tens or hundreds of thousands of years rather than millennia.

This claim is the precondition for the whole prehistoric framework of this wiki: the [[three-age-system|Three Age System]], the Palaeolithic/Neolithic division, and the reading of [[periods/deep-prehistory|deep prehistory]] from stratigraphy and fauna rather than texts.

## Origin and Key Steps

- **Boucher de Perthes** at Abbeville and Amiens (from the 1840s) reported chipped flint tools in the gravels of the Somme alongside extinct animals; long dismissed, his finds were vindicated when British geologists (Joseph Prestwich and John Evans) visited in **1859** and confirmed the association.
- **Charles Lyell's** uniformitarian geology (*Principles of Geology*, 1830–33) supplied the necessary deep timescale, and his *The Geological Evidences of the Antiquity of Man* (**1863**) gave the new consensus its canonical statement.
- **Charles Darwin's** *On the Origin of Species* (1859) and *The Descent of Man* (1871) provided the evolutionary frame within which a long, "rude" human ancestry made sense.
- **John Lubbock's** *Prehistoric Times* (1865) popularized the synthesis and coined "Palaeolithic" and "Neolithic," subdividing the Stone Age.
- **Daniel Wilson** had already given currency to the very word "prehistoric" (*The Archaeology and Prehistoric Annals of Scotland*, 1851).

By the 1860s the deep antiquity of humankind was professional orthodoxy in Britain and France; the popular reception in the English-speaking world ran a generation behind.

## Contestation

The concept is marked `contested: yes` not because deep human antiquity is now in scientific doubt — it is not — but because its *establishment* was a genuine controversy, and because specific early claims for extreme antiquity were false. Nineteenth-century enthusiasm produced several spurious "Tertiary man" claims (the Calaveras skull, California "Table Mountain" finds) that were later exposed as hoaxes or intrusive burials. The general framework was sound; particular American claims for Pliocene humans were not.

The deeper contest was theological and cultural: the new chronology directly contradicted Ussher-style biblical literalism, and its acceptance was often framed — by its own advocates — as a reprise of the earlier struggle over heliocentric astronomy.

## A Popular Witness: Allen (1885)

[[allen-prehistoric-world-1885|Emory Adams Allen's *The Prehistoric World; or, Vanished Races* (1885)]] is a clear specimen of how this revolution reached a mass American readership a generation after the European breakthrough. Allen explicitly dates the shift — "Hardly fifty years have passed since scientific men began to attribute to the human race an antiquity more remote than that assigned them by history and tradition" — and explicitly compares its reception to that of heliocentrism: "At first these views met with general opposition, much as did the theory of the present system of astronomy when it was first proclaimed." He performs the standard reconciliation with scripture (that the Bible offers "no data... on which to base an estimate as to the antiquity of man") and leans on Lyell-style geology and Laplace's nebular hypothesis to supply the deep frame. He also reproduces the era's credulity, accepting the bogus Calaveras/Tertiary-man evidence.

## Limitations and Critique

As an analytic category the concept must be handled carefully: (1) "antiquity of man" was entangled with unilineal, racially hierarchical models of cultural "progress" from savagery to civilization, and those evolutionary-stadial assumptions do not survive; (2) early absolute estimates were crude and often wildly off, since reliable dating waited on the twentieth-century [[radiocarbon-revolution|radiocarbon revolution]]; (3) the framework was developed almost entirely from European (and a little North American) evidence and projected outward.
