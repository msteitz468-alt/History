## Definition and Scope

Ibn Khaldun's classification of the sciences in Chapter VI of the [[ibn-khaldun-muqaddimah]] constitutes one of the most systematic medieval Islamic taxonomies of knowledge. It is distinctive not merely as a catalog but as a sociological theory: Ibn Khaldun argues that sciences are crafts, that crafts are products of civilization (*'umran*), and that civilizational complexity determines both the number and quality of sciences present in any given society.

The classification divides all sciences into two fundamental types:

1. **Philosophical (intellectual) sciences** (*al-'ulum al-hikmiyya al-falsafiyya*): those that are "natural to man" and to which "he is guided by his own ability to think." These are not restricted to any religious group. They include logic, physics, mathematics (geometry, arithmetic, music, astronomy), and metaphysics.

2. **Traditional (transmitted) sciences** (*al-'ulum al-naqliyya al-wad'iyya*): those that depend on "information based on the authority of the given religious law." Intellect has only an auxiliary role within them. These include Qur'anic science, hadith, jurisprudence, speculative theology (*kalam*), Sufism, and dream interpretation.

## The Sciences as Crafts: The Social Theory

Ibn Khaldun's most original contribution is not the classification itself (which largely follows earlier Islamic encyclopedic traditions) but the sociological framework within which it is embedded. The argument runs as follows:

> "It should be known that the sciences with which people concern themselves in cities and which they acquire and pass on through instruction, are of two kinds."

Scientific instruction is a *craft* (*sina'a*). All crafts require sedentary culture (*'umran hadari*), specialized labor above subsistence, and a tradition of teachers passing knowledge to students. Therefore:

- Sciences flourish only where civilization is large and densely settled.
- The decline of a city (Baghdad, al-Qayrawan, Córdoba) causes the collapse of its scientific tradition.
- The East (Egypt, Khurasan, Transoxania) surpasses the Maghrib in scientific cultivation not because Easterners are more intelligent by nature but because their sedentary culture is more continuous and their institutional support (waqf endowments, colleges) more robust.

Ibn Khaldun applies this sociological lens explicitly to the Maghrib of his own day, lamenting that the collapse of Córdoba and al-Qayrawan has left North African scientific instruction thin, with students studying for sixteen years in Morocco achieving what Tunisian students accomplish in five — "not for any other reason" than the poor quality of instruction caused by civilizational contraction.

## The Intellectual Sciences in Detail

Ibn Khaldun surveys the philosophical sciences systematically. His account largely follows the Aristotelian-Avicennan tradition transmitted through [[al-farabi]] and [[ibn-sina]], though he maintains critical distance:

### Logic
Logic is the "norm enabling a person to distinguish between right and wrong" in both definitions and arguments. Ibn Khaldun traces its development from "sententious, disconnected" early Greek discussions to Aristotle's systematization (the Organon, eight books). He notes that recent Islamic scholars (al-Khunaji, Fakhr al-Din Ibn al-Khatib) have reorganized logic extensively, discarding the Categories and neglecting the "matter" books (Apodeictica, Topics, Rhetoric, Poetics, Sophistici Elenchi) in favor of purely formal syllogistic — a reorganization Ibn Khaldun regards as an impoverishment.

Early Muslim theologians "greatly disapproved" of logic because it rests on the existence of natural universals outside the mind, which conflicts with the Ash'arite atomist theology. Since al-Ghazzali and Ibn al-Khatib, scholars have been "somewhat more lenient," arguing that wrong logical arguments do not necessarily invalidate the articles of faith they were used to support.

### Mathematics (*ta'alim*)
The four mathematical sciences:
- **Arithmetic**: properties of numbers; subdivided into practical calculation, algebra, business arithmetic, and inheritance law calculation
- **Geometry**: from Euclid; "enlightens the intellect" and disciplines the mind as "soap does to a garment"
- **Astronomy**: studies celestial motions geometrically; the *zij* (astronomical tables) are its applied subdivision; Ibn Khaldun notes that al-Ma'mun's observatory project was abandoned incomplete
- **Music**: knowledge of proportional sound and modes

### Physics
The study of bodies from the standpoint of motion and rest. Avicenna's *Shifa'* is the master text. Ibn Khaldun notes that Avicenna often opposed Aristotle, while Averroes abridged and followed Aristotle without opposition.

### Medicine
A subdivision of physics. Ibn Khaldun includes a notable methodological note: traditions attributed to the Prophet about medicine are Bedouin practical knowledge, not revealed religious law. "Muhammad was sent to teach us the religious law. He was not sent to teach us medicine or any other ordinary matter."

### Metaphysics
The study of "existence as such." Ibn Khaldun describes how recent scholars (al-Baydawi, Ibn al-Khatib) have merged the problems of theology (*kalam*) with those of philosophy, confusing the two disciplines. Theology's subject is defending articles of faith against heretics; philosophy's subject is investigating existence as it is. These are fundamentally different enterprises with different starting points.

## The Traditional Sciences in Detail

Ibn Khaldun surveys the religious sciences at considerable length as well: Qur'anic recitation and orthography, tafsir, hadith criticism (with discussion of the six canonical collections), jurisprudence and its four schools, the principles of jurisprudence (*usul al-fiqh*), speculative theology, Sufism, and dream interpretation.

On **speculative theology** (*kalam*), Ibn Khaldun provides a detailed history: the Mu'tazila innovations, the Ash'arite response under Abu al-Hasan al-Ash'ari and al-Baqillani, the subsequent mixing of theological and philosophical problems, and his conclusion that "this science — the science of speculative theology — is not something that is necessary to the contemporary student," since the heretics have been refuted.

On **Sufism**, he distinguishes four legitimate topics (pious exertion, the removal of the veil of sense perception, acts of divine grace, and technical Sufi vocabulary) from the doctrine of "absolute oneness" (*wahdat al-wujud*) associated with Ibn al-Arabi and Ibn Sab'in, which he regards as derived illicitly from extremist Shi'a incarnationism. See also [[asharism]], [[kalam]], [[mutazilism]].

## Historiography

Ibn Khaldun's classification synthesizes and critiques several earlier Islamic encyclopedic traditions:
- The Aristotelian tradition as transmitted by [[al-farabi]] and [[ibn-sina]]
- Al-Ghazzali's reconciliation of philosophy and theology (*Ihya'*)
- The Ash'arite theological tradition (see [[asharism]])

His most original contribution is the sociological embedding of the sciences in civilizational theory. He explicitly rejects the premise that Eastern peoples are inherently more intelligent than Maghribis; the difference is entirely institutional and cultural. This anticipates modern sociology of knowledge.

His critique of philosophy is addressed separately in [[ibn-khaldun-critique-of-philosophy]].

The Muqaddimah's classification influenced later Ottoman encyclopedists and was read by early European orientalists, but its reception in the Islamic world was limited until the 19th-century rediscovery. See [[the-translation-movement]] for the earlier Islamic appropriation of Greek science that Ibn Khaldun describes.
