# La Tène Culture

## Definition and Scope

La Tène culture is the second phase of the European Iron Age (c. 450 BCE onward, with sub-phases Ia–III or A–D), best known for its distinctive curvilinear art style, weapon and ornament types, and association with historically attested Celts. It follows Hallstatt and is characterized by expansion of material culture styles, shifts in burial, craft standardization, and the development of oppida and early state institutions in temperate Europe before Roman conquest.

## Key Features (from range)

- Art and material culture: elaborate ornamental style "developed from that initiated in fifth century Germany" (Ch. 10, ~p. 299, cache ~1560s); flagons, swords, scabbards, brooches with curvilinear decoration. "Early La Tène burial groups... rich locally-made items, often decorated in an elaborate ornamental style known as early Celtic" (Ch. 9 end/10, ~p. 276, cache ~160s).

- Burial: diversity regionally, but weapons with men, jewellery with women common; vehicles shift to two-wheeled chariots in early phases in some areas (Champagne, Rhineland); rich graves continue Hallstatt traditions into 5th/4th c before decline. By La Tène III (1st c BCE) burials largely disappear in many areas; cremation adopted in NW Europe (Rhineland, N France, later SE England).

- Expansion: "During the fourth century BC this culture group expanded eastwards from its earlier location in France, Switzerland and Germany, to include most of Austria, Czechoslovakia and Hungary" (Ch. 10, ~p. 299, cache ~1580s). Styles adopted/copied more widely (Britain/Ireland art; brooches throughout N Europe to Russia/Scandinavia).

- Chronology anchored by changing brooch forms: "the constant change in brooch styles makes this type an important chronological marker throughout the Iron Age" (Ch. 10 fig caption, ~p. 303, cache ~1730s).

## Settlement and Economy Shift

From Ch. 10: new larger fortified centres (oppida), wheel-made pottery, kilns (e.g. 12 at Sissach), centralized iron and other production near ores (Manching, Kelheim, Noricum/Magdalensberg), standardization, coinage (initially high value copying Philip/Alexander, later small denom. for local use), weights systems.

Imports (wine amphorae, serving vessels) now concentrated on major settlements rather than solely elite graves (except fringes like SE England Welwyn-type).

## Relation to Hallstatt and End

Succeeds Hallstatt; early La Tène rich graves "continue some of the traditions of the sixth century" (Ch. 9/10, ~p. 276, cache ~150s). Represents "a different pattern of social organization" after early Iron hierarchies collapse ~500 BCE or so, with competition for status persisting but expressed differently, less dependent on restricted prestige exchange.

Culminates in early states and oppida; "by the first century early forms of the state had emerged in many areas of Europe" (Ch. 10, ~p. 315, cache ~2530s). Roman conquest marks end of independent development.

**Retrospect context from Cunliffe range 05 (Ch. 12–13 / developed and retreat):** La Tène marks "the later emergence, expansion, and subsequent evolution of one cultural complex for a brief period of three or four centuries." After systems-collapse ~450 BCE of prior Hallstatt order, "an elite which can be characterized by the distinctive La Tène material culture" led to migrations (mainly S and E) until ~200 BCE, followed by internal transformation 200–50 BCE into "urban-based states" with coinage before "the Celtic communities of Continental Europe were completely overrun" by Rome. "The two most commonly used methods of defining Celts—language and La Tène material culture and art—both present problems in that they represent very different cultural attributes... by no means coterminous." (retrospect). Updates prior range claims with retreat/ developed phase outcomes.

**Grounded claims from Champion et al. 1984 range lines 18501-22500 (Ch. 9-10):** "Most of this phase will be the subject of the following chapter, but its initial stages in Champagne... are marked by a series of rich burials which continue some of the traditions of the sixth century" (transition, ~p. 276, cache ~140s). "The burials show considerable diversity from region to region... there is, nevertheless, a considerable degree of homogeneity" (Ch. 10, ~p. 299, cache ~1550s). Brooch and pottery standardization: "by the first century BC there was a marked pattern of standardization in certain products" (Ch. 10, ~p. 306, cache ~1970s). "Imported classical goods are seldom found in central and western Europe after 400 BC" (Ch. 9, ~p. 294, cache ~1460s).

**Cunliffe 2018 contribution (R01 range, Ch. 2)**: La Tène site discovered 1857 at Lake Neuchâtel (piles, ~40 iron weapons initially); interpreted variously as village, bridge, or ritual platform. 1871 Bologna congress linked identical material at Marzabotto (Etruscan town) to historical Celtic settlers in Po valley (per Livy/Polybius), establishing La Tène material culture as the archaeological expression of the Celts. Hildebrand (1872) proposed the Hallstatt/La Tène division of the Iron Age. Tischler (1885, Duchcov hoard) and Reinecke (La Tène A for formative 5th c. BCE phase pre-migration) refined chronologies (fibulae, swords, chapes). Jacobsthal (1944) defined the art styles (Early, Waldalgesheim, Plastic, Sword) as an original European achievement. The correlation was powerful but later critiqued for circularity with linguistic models; La Tène material is scarce/absent in some areas of documented Celtic activity (Greece, Asia Minor). La Tène represents a later phase of cumulative Celticity, not its origin point. Grounded in [[sources/cunliffe-ancient-celts-2018|Cunliffe 2018]] (Ch. 2, pp. 40–44, 49–51, cache ~1520–1560, 1780–1810).

## Additional Grounded Claims from Cunliffe, The Ancient Celts (2018), range_04 (Ch. 8–12 contexts)

- Post-migration social transformation and material culture shift: "The migratory movements which began towards the end of the fifth century bc were largely, but not entirely, over by the beginning of the second century, by which time the immense energy previously spent on conquest and raiding was being turned to more productive activities." Result: "throughout the Celtic world intensification in production and exchange becomes evident on an unprecedented scale." Societies "very different from those existing at the height of the migration period." (the developed celtic world 311–12)
- Eastern fringes and Dacian impact on La Tène communities: "In eastern Europe the situation was different. The mountains of the Balkans formed an effective barrier between the Adriatic and the Middle Danube regions, sheltering the Celtic communities from Roman interference..." "To the east of the Celts of the Middle Danube lay the Thracians and the Dacians..." After end of second century "it is unlikely that any recognizable Celtic community existed east of the Middle Danube valley, except for the Galatians..." Dacian king Burebista (80s–60s bc) "began a policy of expansion westwards, moving first against the Scordisci and then against the Boii and Taurisci... The defeat of the Celtic tribes was so devastating that Strabo was able to write of the ‘desert of the Boii’." Displaced Boii moved into Noricum and Helvetian areas. (the developed celtic world 316, 327–9)
- Late La Tène elite material culture (warrior/ prestige items): Warriors’ tombs "from the late second century bc... Most contain fittings appropriate to the feast, such as amphorae containing wine, jugs, basins, and paterae, frequently imported from the Roman world, as well as fire-dogs and other hearth furniture. A few were provided with parts of chariots or chariot fittings." "In Gaul... the richer burials were often accompanied by short swords with anthropoid hilts. Widely distributed from Hungary to Britain, such swords may have been an emblem of a particular social class." Rich female: "decorated mirrors and sometimes bronze bowls." (the developed celtic world 343–5)
- Social zones and oppida development: Evidence suggests "three broad social zones: a core zone of tribes or early states which were adopting governments of elected magistrates; an intermediate zone of polities ruled by kings where large oppida... had developed; and a periphery where elite status was still being expressed in the ancient manner with sumptuous burial rites." "The development of oppida controlling route nodes, and thus the movement of goods, is one of the more visible results..." (the developed celtic world 319, 345)
- Clientage and warfare transition: Aspiring warriors served "as auxiliaries in the Roman army, much as his ancestors might have been engaged in a less formal way as mercenaries by the Mediterranean powers." Noricum example: Celtic kingdom in eastern Alps achieving unity under Nori, with "rich supplies of iron... worked locally into a fine steel, well known in the Roman world as ferrum Noricum." Trade and coinage developed. (the developed celtic world 319, 321–2)

## Additional Evidence from Cunliffe 2018 (Ch. 5 range)

Early La Tène (c.450/440–370 BCE) origins as reorientation from Hallstatt core: "In the middle of the fifth century the political geography of barbarian Europe changed dramatically... over much of the west central Hallstatt elite burial zone rich burials ceased and the hillforts occupied by the aristocracy came to an end, in the case of Heuneburg with a catastrophic fire. Meanwhile, in a broad zone to the north stretching from the Loire to Bohemia there developed, in the course of the La Tène A period, a warrior aristocracy which frequently buried its dead with their spears and swords. It was in this zone that the highly distinctive Celtic art style emerged to serve the aristocratic market." "Early La Tène culture differed in many significant ways from that of the old Hallstatt chiefdoms; the geographical focus of the two barely overlapped. In the La Tène burials fighting weapons were frequent, in contrast to the Late Hallstatt elite, whose only weapons were for hunting or display; moreover, the La Tène aristocracy favoured the two-wheeled vehicle as a funerary cart in contrast to the heavier traditional four-wheeled vehicle. The one important similarity was that both had a system of elite burial employing a funerary vehicle together with sets of wine-drinking equipment derived from, or inspired by, the accoutrements of the Mediterranean symposion." (124–125; range ~910–970).

Power centres shifted northward: continuity e.g. in Middle Rhineland (Hohenasperg/Neckar to Moselle focus), with Marne, Loire/Bourges, Bohemia/Dürrnberg as key zones. "two zones of power and innovation: a Loire–Marne–Moselle zone in the west with trading links to the Po valley via the central Alpine passes and the Golasecca culture, and a Bohemian zone in the east with separate links to the Adriatic via the eastern Alpine routes and the Venetic culture." Etruscan beaked flagons and other bronzes concentrated in Middle Rhine elite graves in La Tène A. "the scene was set for a new act in the story of Europe—the Celtic migrations." (126–129; range ~980–1110).

## Additional Detail from Bogucki & Crabtree 2004 (Range 03)

"During the fourth century BC this culture group expanded eastwards from its earlier location in France, Switzerland and Germany, to include most of Austria, Czechoslovakia and Hungary." "by the first century BC there was a marked pattern of standardization in certain products." Oppida as culmination: large fortified nucleated settlements with craft specialization (iron implements, pottery), coin minting, planned streets, elite enclosures. Manching example of 380 ha site with murus Gallicus (unusual this far east), extensive excavation showing production/exchange functions. Imports now at major settlements. "Most of this phase will be the subject of the following chapter, but its initial stages in Champagne... are marked by a series of rich burials which continue some of the traditions of the sixth century." Transition to states: "by the first century early forms of the state had emerged in many areas of Europe."
