# La Tène Culture

## Definition and Scope

La Tène culture is the second phase of the European Iron Age (c. 450 BCE onward, with sub-phases Ia–III or A–D), best known for its distinctive curvilinear art style, weapon and ornament types, and association with historically attested Celts. It follows Hallstatt and is characterized by expansion of material culture styles, shifts in burial, craft standardization, and the development of oppida and early state institutions in temperate Europe before Roman conquest.

## Key Features (from range)

- Art and material culture: elaborate ornamental style "developed from that initiated in fifth century Germany" (Ch. 10, ~p. 299, cache ~1560s); flagons, swords, scabbards, brooches with curvilinear decoration. "Early La Tène burial groups... rich locally-made items, often decorated in an elaborate ornamental style known as early Celtic" (Ch. 9 end/10, ~p. 276, cache ~160s).

- Burial: diversity regionally, but weapons with men, jewellery with women common; vehicles shift to two-wheeled chariots in early phases in some areas (Champagne, Rhineland); rich graves continue Hallstatt traditions into 5th/4th c before decline. By La Tène III (1st c BCE) burials largely disappear in many areas; cremation adopted in NW Europe (Rhineland, N France, later SE England).

- Expansion: "During the fourth century BC this culture group expanded eastwards from its earlier location in France, Switzerland and Germany, to include most of Austria, Czechoslovakia and Hungary" (Ch. 10, ~p. 299, cache ~1580s). Styles adopted/copied more widely (Britain/Ireland art; brooches throughout N Europe to Russia/Scandinavia).

- Chronology anchored by changing brooch forms: "the constant change in brooch styles makes this type an important chronological marker throughout the Iron Age" (Ch. 10 fig caption, ~p. 303, cache ~1730s).

## Settlement and Economy Shift

From Ch. 10: new larger fortified centres (oppida), wheel-made pottery, kilns (e.g. 12 at Sissach), centralized iron and other production near ores (Manching, Kelheim, Noricum/Magdalensberg), standardization, coinage (initially high value copying Philip/Alexander, later small denom. for local use), weights systems.

Imports (wine amphorae, serving vessels) now concentrated on major settlements rather than solely elite graves (except fringes like SE England Welwyn-type).

## Relation to Hallstatt and End

Succeeds Hallstatt; early La Tène rich graves "continue some of the traditions of the sixth century" (Ch. 9/10, ~p. 276, cache ~150s). Represents "a different pattern of social organization" after early Iron hierarchies collapse ~500 BCE or so, with competition for status persisting but expressed differently, less dependent on restricted prestige exchange.

Culminates in early states and oppida; "by the first century early forms of the state had emerged in many areas of Europe" (Ch. 10, ~p. 315, cache ~2530s). Roman conquest marks end of independent development.

**Grounded claims from Champion et al. 1984 range lines 18501-22500 (Ch. 9-10):** "Most of this phase will be the subject of the following chapter, but its initial stages in Champagne... are marked by a series of rich burials which continue some of the traditions of the sixth century" (transition, ~p. 276, cache ~140s). "The burials show considerable diversity from region to region... there is, nevertheless, a considerable degree of homogeneity" (Ch. 10, ~p. 299, cache ~1550s). Brooch and pottery standardization: "by the first century BC there was a marked pattern of standardization in certain products" (Ch. 10, ~p. 306, cache ~1970s). "Imported classical goods are seldom found in central and western Europe after 400 BC" (Ch. 9, ~p. 294, cache ~1460s).
