# The Gortyn Code

The **Gortyn Code** is the great law inscription of the Cretan city of Gortyn — twelve
columns of *boustrophedon* writing (some 600 lines) cut into a curved wall, the
**longest and most complete legal text to survive from early Greece** and the fullest
single window we have into the law and society of an Archaic Greek community.
[[boardman-cah-v3p3-1982|CAH Vol. III, Part 3]] (Ch. 39c, R. F. Willetts) treats it
and the wider Cretan world it reflects.

## The "Queen of Inscriptions"

The Code (often called the "first European law-code," though it is "a codification not
of law but of laws") is written in an **archaic 18-letter Cretan alphabet**, in
**boustrophedon** (alternating left-right lines) — a conservative retention long after
other Greeks had moved on. On letter-forms and style it is dated to the **fifth century
(c. 480–450)**, but it codifies and amends **much older written law**, so it preserves
practices reaching back into the Archaic period. Each clause is framed as a conditional
sentence — *if* such-and-such, *then* the legal consequence. It is "the only document
which gives an authentic record of the processes of early Greek law."

## Cretan codification and its setting

Crete belongs to the seventh-century **wave of Greek legal codification** that (Willetts
stresses) began **not on the mainland but in the colonies** — the western lawgivers
**Zaleucus of Locri** and **Charondas of Catane** producing the oldest written Greek
laws, partly to give a single code to colonists of mixed origin. Significantly the
Gortyn Code is **associated with no lawgiver**: Crete "was not a colony but a centre of
pilgrimage for legal inspiration," its prestige resting on the ancient tradition of
Minos as Zeus-inspired lawgiver (the law on stone at **Drerus**, c. 650–600 — limiting
re-tenure of the *kosmos* office — is perhaps the earliest surviving complete Greek law).
Crete also exported cultural forms: the musician **Thaletas** and the seer **Epimenides**
carried Cretan ritual arts to Sparta and Athens.

## Cretan society in the Code

The Code reveals a conservative **Dorian agrarian aristocracy**, structurally akin to
Sparta (Aristotle thought the Spartan constitution derived from the Cretan):

- **Magistrates** — the *kosmoi* (collectively the *kosmos*), drawn from privileged
  clans, who had taken over the war-leadership of the abolished early kings; a council
  of elders; a weak citizen assembly; specialist judges (*dikastai*) and recorders.
- **A sharply graded social hierarchy** of **(1) free citizens, (2) *apetairoi*** (free
  persons without political rights), **(3) serfs (*woikeis*)** and **(4) chattel slaves
  (*doloi*)** — the relative standing legible in the **scale of fines**: rape of a free
  person cost 1,200 obols, of a household slave as little as 1–2.
- **Serfdom, not chattel slavery, as the base.** The original populations had become
  **tribute-paying serfs tied to the *klēros* (the ancestral estate)** — but the Cretan
  *woikeus* had unusual rights: he could marry and divorce, hold a house and cattle,
  possess money, and use the courts. This patriarchal serfdom endured for centuries
  alongside the **syssitia** (communal meals funded, Aristotle notes, from public lands
  and serf tributes so that all citizens — men, women, children — were maintained at
  public expense), and the **agelai** ("herds") in which youths were raised and married
  off collectively.
- **Property, women, and inheritance.** The Code protects the patriarchal *oikos* and
  its *klēros*, regulating the marriage of the **heiress (*patroiokos*)** to the next of
  kin — yet it preserves striking **older matrilineal survivals**: women hold and inherit
  property in their own right (sons take two parts, daughters one), a mother's property
  passes separately, and a free woman could marry a serf. Adultery and rape are treated
  as **civil wrongs settled by fines**, not crimes.

## Significance

- The **single richest source for Archaic-to-Classical Greek law and society** outside
  Athens — and the prime evidence that early Greek legal codification was driven by
  alphabetic literacy, commercial change, and the need to fix custom in a time of
  social transformation.
- A model of a **conservative Dorian society** preserving tribal institutions (serfdom,
  syssitia, age-classes) into the historical period — the Cretan counterpart, and
  claimed ancestor, of the Spartan system.
