# Migrants and Minorities in Merovingian Gaul

**Who counted as a minority in a kingdom with no settled majority identity.** Wolfram Drews's chapter
(Oxford Handbook, Ch. 6) surveys the migrant and minority populations of Frankish Gaul — Jews,
eastern Christians ("Syrians"), Irish monks, Arian Goths, and the unfree — and finds the very
categories unstable: Gregory of Tours has "no precise term" for the majority population, defining a
person instead by their *civitas* of origin. The Franks themselves "probably did not regard
themselves as migrants" after settling, converting, and inheriting Roman administration.

## Definition and Scope

The chapter contrasts two kinds of minority: those continuing a **late-Roman provincial tradition**
(Jews above all) and those who were genuine **incomers** (Irish *peregrini*). Religion, language,
legal status, and accent — not biology — drew the lines.

- **Irish monks (religious migrants).** [[columbanus|Columbanus]], Gallus, and others practiced
  *peregrinatio religiosa*, deliberately "maintaining links to their insular roots" and refusing
  assimilation, "tantamount to losing their status as pilgrims." See
  [[merovingian-monasticism]]. The *Schottenkirchen* of Vienna, Würzburg, and Regensburg preserve
  their memory; but unlike the Jews, their identity "did not persist into the high medieval period."
- **Eastern Christians ("Syrians" and Greeks).** Stable groups are "attested only in Arles,
  Narbonne, and Orléans, and possibly… Marseille"; psalms were sung alternately in Greek and Latin
  at Arles. "Syrian" was an accent-and-origin label, "not… a separate Christian denomination." After
  610 the references cease.
- **Arians.** Arian Christianity "was one of the main markers of a separate Gothic identity," a means
  for a ruling minority to "maintain its cohesion." There was generally "no religious conflict
  between Arians and Catholics in the Gothic kingdom of Toulouse"; after [[battle-of-vouille-507|
  Vouillé]] (507) only Septimania remained Arian, and the faith declined quietly.

## Jews — The Key Minority

Drews treats the Jews as "the most important minority… perceived as such, but not as outsiders."
Communities clustered south of the Loire (Marseille, Arles, Orléans, Bourges, Clermont, Uzès,
Visigothic Narbonne). Free Jewish males had held **Roman citizenship since 212**, and — unlike
Visigothic Spain, where the *Liber Iudiciorum* abolished it in 654 — "no such official replacement
ever took place in Merovingian Gaul." Jews spoke vulgar Latin, held public offices (criticised by
the councils of Clermont 535, Paris 614, Clichy 626/7, and Chlothar II's edict of 614), and worked
as doctors, butchers, merchants, and tax-farmers.

Crucially, "Merovingian kings never pursued any coherent policy regarding the Jews." Episodes of
coercion were local and exceptional: at **Clermont in 576** Bishop Avitus baptised "more than 500
Jews" after mob violence, though Bachrach argued those who fled were too well-connected "to have
been victimized successfully," and those who stayed "chose rather to remain citizens of Clermont";
Chilperic's attempt to convert Jews near his court in 582 failed, with some "revert[ing] to their
ancestral religion." Nine of the 60-plus Merovingian councils legislated on Jews (last:
Chalon-sur-Saône, 647/653), chiefly on slaves, mixed marriages, and Holy-Week conduct. At Guntram's
royal entry to Orléans, Jews sang in their own language — "a living testimony to the vastness of
Guntram's royal — possibly imperial — rule."

## Slaves and the Unfree

The unfree are a structural minority throughout: [[salic-law|Salic Law]] mandated torture in serious
cases against slaves (PLS 40.4) precisely because, as another's property, "community support was not
a relevant consideration." Manumission "for the redemption of the soul" is attested epigraphically
(see [[merovingian-epigraphy]]).

## Historiography

Drews's chapter dismantles the assumption of a homogeneous Frankish Gaul, showing minority status as
legal and cultural rather than ethnic — consistent with [[ethnogenesis]] and with the
bioarchaeological evidence for flexible identity (see [[merovingian-bioarchaeology]]). Key references
include Toch's *Economic History of European Jews* (2013) and Bachrach's work on early medieval
Jewish policy.
