Islamic property law (the property-related dimensions of muʿāmalāt in [[fiqh]]) governs the acquisition, transfer, use, and disposition of wealth (māl), distinguishing between ownership of substance (ʿayn / milk) and ownership of usufruct (manfaʿa). Property doctrine permeates virtually every branch of [[sharia]] — it underpins contract law, the ribā prohibition, zakāt eligibility, inheritance, the [[waqf]] institution, and family maintenance. Hallaq's chapter 9 of [[hallaq-sharia-2009]] is explicitly titled "Property and Ownership"; what follows draws on property-law principles embedded throughout chapters 6–8 of the same work alongside the opening of chapter 9.

## Definition of Māl (Wealth/Property)

Property in [[fiqh]] is not simply any object but an object capable of having a monetary value (māl) and of lawful use. Ritually impure substances — wine, pork, insects, carcasses — cannot be the locus of a valid contract regardless of their economic utility. Property eligible for lawful transactions must be:
- In existence (or potentially in existence, for specified exceptions such as salam)
- Owned by the disposing party
- Capable of delivery
- Possessed by the owner (misappropriated property and stray animals cannot be sold or rented)

## Substance (ʿAyn) vs. Usufruct (Manfaʿa)

A fundamental distinction in all property-related fiqh is between:
- **ʿAyn** (substance/corpus): the physical object itself — can be owned, sold, pledged, bequeathed
- **Manfaʿa** (usufruct): the right to use and enjoy the object — can also be independently owned, sold (through ijāra), bequeathed, and encumbered

This distinction generates the entire edifice of lease/hire law (ijāra), the waqf institution (where the corpus is inalienably endowed but the usufruct continues to be distributed), and the nafaqa rules of family law (the wife owns the substance of her food but only the usufruct of clothing and housing). A slave's usufruct may be bequeathed to a legatee while the slave is sold to another owner; the sale does not affect the legatee's right to the usufruct.

## Full Ownership (Milk Tāmm) and Its Conditions

Full ownership — the combination of ʿayn and manfaʿa — is the standard assumed in most contractual transactions. Contracts of sale transfer full ownership. Conditions for valid exercise of ownership rights:
- Legal competence (majority, sanity, absence of interdiction for insolvency or foolhardiness — sufh)
- Property must be "fully owned" at the time of contractual disposition: an agent appointed to divorce a wife whom the principal has not yet married has no valid subject-matter
- Property must be free of encumbrances for purposes of sale (pledged, misappropriated, or stray property cannot be sold)

## Possession (Qabd / Yad) and Unlawful Appropriation (Ghaṣb)

Possession (yad, literally "hand") is distinct from ownership and has independent legal significance:
- For zakāt: property not currently in the owner's possession (e.g., an unlawfully appropriated herd) is exempt from the tax obligation until recovered
- For sale: delivery capability requires that the property be in the seller's possession and free of encumbrances at time of contract
- For muḍāraba: the sleeping partner must "deliver" (part company with) the capital to the agent for the fiduciary relationship to arise; if the "sleeping" partner continues to use the capital, the essential contractual element is not realized

**Ghaṣb** (unlawful appropriation) generates liability for the appropriator (ghāṣib) for any rent value of the period during which the rightful owner was deprived of enjoyment. Growth or profit accruing on unlawfully appropriated property while in the hands of the appropriator belongs to the owner, not the appropriator — this principle is applied by analogy in muḍāraba when an agent acts outside his mandate.

## Fungibles (Mithliiyyāt) and Non-Fungibles (Mutaqawwimāt)

- **Fungibles** (mithliiyyāt): objects replaceable by equivalent kind and measure — grain, cash, standard commodities. In loans (qarḍ), an equivalent must be returned, not the same object. Fungibles are always regarded as "the price" when exchanged for non-fungibles.
- **Non-fungibles** (mutaqawwimāt, also called ʿayniyyāt): unique objects whose value cannot be exactly replicated — real estate, specific animals, hand-crafted goods. In free loans (ʿāriya), the specific object must be returned.

The ribā prohibition applies specifically to exchanges of the same fungible genus in unequal amounts (e.g., gold for gold at different weights), because such exchanges constitute an illicit "growth" on the property of one party.

## Capital Eligible for Partnership

Contractual partnership (sharikat ʿaqd) historically required that the capital invested be naqd — gold and silver coin — because goods (ʿurūḍ) have no fixed value and therefore the profit cannot be precisely determined at inception. The majority of later Ḥanafite, Mālikite, and Ḥanbalite jurists required naqd; the Shāfiʿites accepted goods as valid capital. This requirement reflects the broader fiqh principle that anything capable of producing discord and dispute through vagueness (gharar fāḥish or jahāla) is legally prohibited.

## Property, Zakāt, and the Concept of Nominal Ownership

The zakāt doctrine embeds a theological-legal understanding of property rights: the Quran teaches that "all things ultimately belong to God and that Muslims are the trustees of earthly wealth, accountable for the ways in which they dispose of it." Zakāt obligation attaches to property that is (a) fully owned, (b) capable of growth, (c) in excess of subsistence, (d) held for a full lunar year, and (e) free of debt. The rate of 2.5% on the net growth of wealth above the niṣāb enacts in legal form the principle that surplus wealth generates a religious and social obligation. Hoarding is a "cause for divine condemnation."

## The Waqf (Pious Endowment) as Property Institution

The [[waqf]] is the premier property institution of classical Islamic civilization: the owner permanently alienates the corpus (ʿayn) of an asset — real estate, agricultural land, commercial property — which is frozen as inalienable, while the usufruct (manfaʿa) is directed in perpetuity to a designated beneficiary (usually a pious or charitable purpose). Discerning minors (mumayyiz) may be beneficiaries of a waqf even though they cannot conclude contracts. The legal separability of ʿayn and manfaʿa is the formal condition that makes the waqf institution coherent.

## Inheritance and the Estate (Tarika)

Upon death, the estate (tarika) is subject to three prior claims before distribution: funeral expenses, outstanding debts, and bequests. Property rights over the estate do not extinguish at death: creditors' rights survive against the estate and — through kafāla — against the guarantors' estates and their heirs in turn. The rule "there is in principle no statute of limitations in the Sharīʿa" (regarding property claims) is stated explicitly by Hallaq in the context of guaranty law.

Inheritance rules distribute the estate through fixed Quranic shares and agnatic residue — see [[islamic-family-law]] for the full treatment. The key property-law aspect is that slaves cannot inherit (whatever they inherit would belong to their master, the propositus himself), and property transferred through inheritance is not contractual (making the rules about bequest to non-Muslims different from those about inheritance by non-Muslims).

## Historiography

Property law in classical Islam has attracted substantial attention from economic historians studying the endowment institution ([[waqf]]), credit practices, and the ribā prohibition's effects on capital formation. Debates center on whether the waqf system inhibited capital accumulation and economic development (Kuran, The Long Divergence, 2010) or instead provided a robust extra-state infrastructure for public goods (Çizakça). The relationship between Islamic property law and the common law of trust has also generated comparative legal scholarship. Hallaq's [[hallaq-sharia-2009]] is distinctive in treating property not as an isolated doctrinal field but as embedded in a total moral-legal order.
