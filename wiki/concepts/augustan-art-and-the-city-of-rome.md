# Augustan Art and the Transformation of Rome

The **Classicizing artistic programme** of Augustus and the physical **rebuilding of the city of
Rome** as the marble capital of empire — the visual expression of the [[principate|Principate's]]
ideology. Treated in [[bowman-cah-v10-1996|CAH X]] Ch. 20 (**Mario Torelli**; with the urban
development of Ch. 15).

## Augustan Classicism

Torelli identifies one of the most "dramatically apparent" changes in ancient art: in the first
two decades of Augustus' reign, official art turned decisively to a **Classicism inspired by the
great Attic models of the fifth and fourth centuries BCE**. Late-Republican art had mixed the
**baroque Hellenistic** *luxuria Asiatica* with a spare, "realistic" **Italic** portrait
tradition (the two coexisting, as in the realistic faces of the Arringatore and Caesar versus the
pathos-laden Pompey). The decade after the **constitutional settlement of 27 BCE** marks the
transition: the **"Prima Porta"-type portrait of Augustus** embodies "the propaganda message of
the new convictions of the Principate"; in painting the **Third Style** replaced the open
perspectives of the Second; in architecture Attic models prevailed. The new style was "not
entirely new" — it drew on 150 years of neoclassical experience — but what was new was its
**pervasive, official, all-embracing** character, radiating from the **capital** to the
municipalities and "the lowest levels of society," and its grafting onto a **nostalgic Roman
and Italian national past**.

## The politics of style

Torelli reads the stylistic shift politically. The **ban on baroque** "effrontery" accompanied
the **censure of anything not conforming to the moral restoration**: the baroque/Hellenistic and
the Italic-realist modes were pushed to the margins of **private consumption** (silverware, genre
painting, the "trifles" and erotic themes of the villa gardens — "pseudo-messages void of
content"). Above all, with **triumphs reserved to the emperor and his family**, the great
aristocratic building financed *ex manubiis* (from war-spoils) **came to an end**: Cornelius
Balbus (19 BCE) was "the last triumphing general able to erect an edifice from his spoils." Only
the *princeps* now had "the right to mix or to juxtapose the public with the private." The
diffusion was carried by the **neo-Attic workshops** (the school of Pasiteles), increasingly
concentrated around the palace, creating a stable artistic tradition through a "capillary network
of workshops" and the circulation of moulds and casts.

## The Augustan model and the city

Augustus resumed Caesar's plans to imprint the ***nomen Iulium*** on the capital, beginning (like
Pompey and Caesar) from a **dynastic conception** — his **Mausoleum** (echoing Alexander's tomb),
Agrippa's Alexandrian-inspired works in the **Campus Martius** (the Pantheon, baths, *stagnum*,
*saepta*). The keystones were the *opera triumphalia*: the **Temple of Palatine Apollo** (36–28,
beside his house, "evoking the palaces of Hellenistic kings"); the **Forum of Augustus** with the
**Temple of Mars Ultor** (vowed 42, dedicated 2 BCE), where Venus Genetrix and Mars Ultor stood
in a dynastic "sacred marriage" flanked by Augustus' ancestors facing the *summi viri* of Roman
history — "the gens of the new Aeneas and the new Romulus" recapitulating Rome's fortunes (a
theme also of the **Ara Pacis**); and the **Solarium** with its Egyptian obelisk.

Beyond new building, Augustus imposed **"a new coherence on buildings which already existed"** —
the restored Forum Romanum (the temple of Divus Julius between his Actian and Parthian arches;
the Portico of Gaius and Lucius) "re-employing" venerable monuments within his personal
propaganda and excluding "undesirable" aristocratic ones. He also **reorganized the city
administratively**: the restoration of the walls and gates (AD 2–10) reaffirming the symbolic
city boundary, the *cohortes vigilum* (fire-watch), the division into **fourteen regions**, and
the **compital cult** of his Genius (12–7 BCE). His *Res Gestae* boast — restoring **eighty
temples**, leaving "a city of marble which he had received made of brick" (Suetonius) — captures
the "intensely urban character of his efforts," the grandest in the city's history before
Domitian.

## Alexandria, Egypt, and the private

Torelli stresses the recurrent **Alexandrian/Egyptian model** — in Agrippa's Campus Martius (the
confusion of public parks, pools, and dwellings), in the obelisks, and in the private
architecture and the Egyptianizing imagery of the late Second and Third Styles. This was "not
merely mannerist exoticism" but a recognition of the deep affinity (and diversity) between life
at Rome and Alexandria — a model that, banished from official austerity, was **privatized** into
the villa and the garden, and would resurface monumentally only in **Nero's Golden House**.

## Significance

Augustan art is the **visual counterpart of the political settlement**: a Classicizing idiom that
monopolized public self-representation for the emperor, suppressed the dynastic display of the
old nobility, and fused Greek "inimitable models" with a revived Romano-Italian past to propagate
the religious, moral, and political ideology of the regime "into the most remote municipalities
of Italy and among the lowest levels of society." The marble Rome it created became the model for
the cities of the Western empire.
