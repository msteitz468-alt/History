# Renaissance Humanism (the *studia humanitatis*)

Renaissance humanism as analysed by **Robert Black** in [[allmand-ncmh-v7-1998|NCMH VII]] (Ch. 12).
Black's chapter is a sustained historiographical argument: humanism was **not** a new "philosophy of
man" but a **literary and educational movement** — the revival and imitation of classical (chiefly
Latin) Antiquity — that triumphed because it redefined what it meant to be a gentleman fit to rule.

## Definition (Emic vs. Etic)

Following **Kristeller**, humanism is defined by contemporary usage: a **humanista** was a teacher of
the **studia humanitatis** — the five disciplines of **grammar, rhetoric, poetry, history and moral
philosophy** (the canon set out in the late 1430s in Tommaso Parentucelli's library plan for Cosimo
de' Medici; the phrase revived by Salutati in 1369 from Cicero's *Pro Archia*, in the sense of
*paideia*, education, not philanthropy). Humanism was thus a **self-conscious movement** with a
genuine *esprit de corps* ("nostra studia humanitatis"), detectable above all in a writer's
deliberate imitation of **classical rather than medieval Latin**. It reached back to Petrarch's
discovery of *Pro Archia* (1333) and the Paduan/Aretine/Bolognese "pre-humanists" (Lovato Lovati,
Mussato).

## The Burckhardt–Kristeller Debate (the chapter's spine)

- **Burckhardt (1860).** *The Civilization of the Renaissance in Italy* made the Renaissance about a
  new **individualism** — "man became a spiritual individual" — born of the amoral politics of the
  Italian city-states; the classical revival was secondary. This Hegelian "spirit of the age"
  reading dominated for a century (Symonds, Cassirer, Gentile, Garin; even **Hans Baron's** "civic
  humanism" sits within it).
- **Kristeller's repudiation.** In "the single most influential article on Renaissance humanism
  written this century," Kristeller argued the humanists "were neither good nor bad philosophers, but
  no philosophers at all." Humanism was a **literary movement** rooted in grammar and rhetoric; it
  could not have replaced scholasticism as "the new philosophy" because **Aristotelian scholasticism
  thrived in Italy until the mid-seventeenth century**. Its essence was precisely what Burckhardt
  dismissed — the **revival of Antiquity** — and it owed deep debts to medieval traditions
  (the *ars dictaminis*, French grammatical study, the Byzantine Greek heritage).

Black sides with Kristeller and presses the **continuity** case hard: there was no consensus on the
"dignity of man" (Manetti and Facio exalted it; Poggio, Alberti and Petrarch dwelt on human
*misery*); the schoolroom curriculum (the *Ianua*, verse grammars like the *Doctrinale*, *themata*)
showed marked **continuity** with the Middle Ages; and "no one will ever identify the 'characteristic
stamp' of the Renaissance, because it exists only in the minds of Hegel, Burckhardt and their
followers."

## Genuine Achievements (where humanism *did* break with the past)

- **The Latin classics recovered.** The humanists "virtually completed the corpus of the Latin
  classics" — Tacitus, Lucretius, Catullus, Propertius, Cicero's letters and orations — through
  manuscript hunting (Poggio).
- **Textual criticism and antiquarianism.** Conjectural emendation; Poliziano's ranking of
  manuscripts by age (anticipating *stemmatics*); the public library (Florence, Rome, Venice,
  Urbino, Cesena); Poggio and Biondo's critical study of ancient remains (proto-archaeology).
- **The revival of Greek.** With **no** medieval western tradition of Greek, **Manuel Chrysoloras**
  (Florence, c. 1397) inaugurated western Greek scholarship; almost all classical Greek literature
  became available in the original and in Latin. **Lorenzo Valla's** philological critique of the
  Vulgate New Testament (using the Greek) was a genuine innovation — though it lay unpublished and
  without impact until **Erasmus's** edition of 1505. (On balance, Antiquity in the fifteenth century
  still meant largely **Roman** Antiquity.)
- **Script reform.** Humanist bookhand (modelled, wrongly, on what was actually **Caroline minuscule**
  — see [[caroline-minuscule]]) and Italic cursive: lasting reforms still with us.

## Civic Humanism, Reconsidered

Black qualifies **Baron's "civic humanism"**: a developed republican ideology existed in the Italian
communes long before Bruni (Ptolemy of Lucca, the assimilation of Aristotle's *Politics*); Bruni's
real contribution was to restore its **Ciceronian** foundations. Salutati's and Bruni's apparent
contradictions (praising both equality and oligarchy; monarchy and republic) track **Florentine
political and diplomatic realities** after 1382, not a clean medieval-vs-Renaissance break.

## Why Humanism Succeeded (Black's thesis)

If humanism shared so much with medieval learning, why did it conquer Europe? Not for practical
political lessons (its moral platitudes "could hardly have helped any Italian politician"). Black's
answer: humanism redefined **gentility**. It persuaded Italian — then European — elite society that
"without its lessons no one was fit to rule or lead" (Vergerio's *De ingenuis moribus*, c. 1402;
Guarino; Alberti: letters, not the hawk, make the gentleman). In a fluid society endlessly seeking
"better definitions of social and political acceptability," a classical education — **endorsed by
the prestigious ancients** — became the indispensable badge of the ruling class. Hard-headed
Florentine businessmen paid large sums for it precisely because it was **useful to a ruling class**,
even though "better Latinity could never mean superior morality."

## Spread North

Humanism was overwhelmingly Italian but crossed the Alps before 1500, **imported** rather than
indigenous (the Dutch *devotio moderna* was devotional, not humanist). Its greatest inroads were in
**Germany** (Rudolf Agricola; the Deventer school under Hegius, teacher of **Erasmus**; Reuchlin and
Hebrew; Celtis and German patriotic Tacitism), with thinner progress in France (Lefèvre d'Étaples,
Gaguin), Spain (Nebrija's grammar) and England (Grocyn, Linacre; Erasmus's 1499 visit).
