## Definition and Scope

Pancasila ("Five Principles" in Sanskrit-derived Indonesian) is the state ideology of the Republic of Indonesia, articulated by Sukarno in June 1945 during the debates preceding independence. The five principles are: (1) belief in one God (*Ketuhanan Yang Maha Esa*); (2) just and civilized humanity; (3) the unity of Indonesia; (4) democracy guided by inner wisdom through deliberation/representation; and (5) social justice for all Indonesians.

Pancasila's political function has evolved considerably since 1945, from a founding nationalist synthesis to a state-enforced ideological test under Suharto's New Order.

## Historical Development

**Founding context (1945)**: Pancasila emerged from a critical compromise during the constitutional debates of mid-1945. Muslim groups initially sought explicit recognition of Islam's role in the state — the "Jakarta Charter" would have obliged Muslims to follow Islamic law. The inclusion of "belief in one God" rather than "Islam" was itself a compromise: it acknowledged the centrality of religion while avoiding a confessional Islamic state. The *Cambridge History of Southeast Asia* (Tarling, ed., Vol. 2) notes that "the Muslims made concessions in the constitutional discussions just before the Japanese surrendered, and agreed not to include the specific mention of Islam in the state ideology of Pancasila. Instead, they settled for the Jakarta Charter, a draft prologue to be tagged on to but not part of the constitution."

**Under Sukarno**: Pancasila was invoked as a legitimizing framework throughout the revolutionary period and parliamentary democracy, but without systematic enforcement. During Guided Democracy, Sukarno used deviation from Pancasila's principles as a rhetorical weapon against political opponents.

**Under Suharto — Pancasila as sole guiding principle (1987)**: In 1987, the New Order required all societies — including political parties and mass organizations — to declare Pancasila as their "sole guiding principle" (*asas tunggal*). The *Cambridge History* characterizes this as "the de-ideologization of the Indonesian political structure": by forcing all groups to subscribe to one ideology, Suharto effectively divorced political parties from their "primordial and traditional sources of power." Muslim parties (forced into the Development Unity Party) and nationalist-Christian coalitions (into the Indonesian Democratic Party) were thus severed from their grassroots religious and cultural identities.

## Emic vs. Etic

**Emic**: Pancasila represents the authentic expression of Indonesian national identity — a synthesis that transcends the divisions between Muslim, Christian, Javanese, Sumatran, and other communities. In this reading, it is the genius of the founding generation's achievement.

**Etic**: Critical scholarship has identified Pancasila as an elastic ideological container that has been filled with different political content at different moments — a founding nationalist compromise, a revolutionary rallying point under Sukarno, and an instrument of authoritarian depoliticization under Suharto. Its fifth principle ("social justice") was invoked by the PKI; its first ("belief in one God") by Muslim groups; its third ("unity of Indonesia") by the military to justify suppression of ethnic separatism. This elasticity is both its political strength (it provides a common language) and its analytical weakness (it can justify almost any policy).

## Contested Dimensions

The principal contested dimensions are:
1. **Islam and the secular state**: Whether the *Ketuhanan* principle adequately accommodates Muslim political aspirations or whether it permanently disadvantages Muslim parties relative to a notionally secular state ideology
2. **Authenticity**: Whether Pancasila represents a genuinely indigenous Indonesian political philosophy or a post-hoc rationalization of Sukarno's personal preferences
3. **Political use**: Whether the *asas tunggal* requirement under Suharto was a legitimate protection of national unity or an abuse of state ideology to suppress political pluralism
