# World-Systems Theory

A macro-historical framework holding that the basic unit of historical analysis above
the local scale is not the individual civilization but the **world-system** — a
self-contained, usually unequal, set of relationships binding two or more societies
through trade, war, and cultural exchange. The approach reverses the older
"civilizationist" emphasis (Spengler, Toynbee), which treated civilizations as discrete
comparable entities and had little to say about the connections *between* them.

## Definition and Scope

**Emic vs etic.** World-systems theory is wholly *etic* — an analytical tool imposed by
modern historians and sociologists, not a category used by historical actors. Its claim
is that connections between societies are so pervasive that "a world system should
replace the civilization as the basic unit for historical analysis on the macro scale"
([[benjamin-empires-ancient-eurasia-2018|Benjamin 2018]]).

Immanuel Wallerstein distinguished three types:
- **World economy** — two or more states that trade with and periodically fight one
  another, none permanently dominant. Benjamin reads the [[roman-empire|Roman]]–[[parthian-empire|Parthian]]
  relationship of the [[silk-roads|First Silk Roads Era]] as an ancient world economy.
- **World empire** — arises when one state in a world economy comes to dominate the
  others under its hegemony. Benjamin judges this *not* to apply to First-Silk-Roads-Era
  Afro-Eurasia, because none of the four great empires (Han, Kushan, Parthian, Roman)
  ever dominated the rest.
- **Minisystem** — a group of interacting societies that are not states; the relations
  among pastoral-nomadic confederations ([[xiongnu|Xiongnu]], [[yuezhi|Yuezhi]]) might
  qualify.

## What It Is Not

It is not a claim that the premodern world was globally integrated. Wallerstein himself
restricted the *modern* capitalist world-system to the period after c. 1500 CE. The
controversy (below) is over how far back, and how loosely, the concept can legitimately
be stretched.

## Major Debates

- **When did the first world-system appear?** Wallerstein dated it to the sixteenth
  century. **Janet Abu-Lughod** (*Before European Hegemony*, 1989) located a thirteenth–
  fourteenth-century Afro-Eurasian system pre-dating European dominance. **Andre Gunder
  Frank and Barry Gills** (*The World System: Five Hundred Years or Five Thousand?*, 1992)
  pushed the origin all the way back to the first Mesopotamian agrarian civilization
  (c. 3200 BCE), arguing for a single continuous Afro-Eurasian system thickened by
  exchange networks like the Silk Roads. [CONTROVERSY]
- **Is "world economy" even the right model for antiquity?** Benjamin concedes it is
  "arguable whether the Silk Roads ever constituted a world system the way Wallerstein
  conceived of it," but holds the *central argument* — connections over discreteness —
  to be highly relevant.

## Relationship to Other Frameworks

`analogous_to: [[human-web]]` — J.R. & W.H. McNeill's reconceptualization of history as
thickening webs of connection covers similar ground with less rigid structure.
`contributed_to: [[big-history]]` — together with the Annales historians (Bloch, Le Roy
Ladurie, Braudel), world-systems theorists helped produce the transdisciplinary field of
big history. `contrasts_with:` the civilizationist tradition of Spengler and Toynbee.
The framework also underpins analyses of the [[atlantic-slave-trade]] and
[[global-silver-trade|early-modern global economy]].

## Limitations and Critiques

Critics note the difficulty of specifying core/periphery for premodern systems lacking a
single dominant capitalist core; the risk of anachronistically projecting modern economic
integration onto loosely connected ancient exchanges; and the under-specification of how
"unequal" exchange operated before bulk commodity trade. Benjamin's own use is
deliberately eclectic — he borrows the *connections-first* premise while declining to
force the First Silk Roads Era into a formal core–periphery structure.
