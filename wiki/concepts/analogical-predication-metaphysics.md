## Definition and Scope

**Analogical predication** (*per prius et posterius*, Arabic: *bi'l-tashkik* or predication "by priority and posteriority") is a mode of predication intermediate between **univocal predication** (same term, same definition for all instances) and **equivocal predication** (same term, wholly different meanings). In analogical predication, a term applies to a primary instance first and most fully, and to secondary instances derivatively, with the primary instance as the cause or ground of the attribute in the secondary instances.

The canonical Aristotelian examples:
- **"Healthy"**: said primarily of the organism (health as a state of the body), derivatively of medicine (healthy = productive of health), of complexion (healthy = sign of health), etc.
- **"Warm"**: said primarily of fire (warmth in its highest degree), derivatively of all warm things, with fire as the cause of warmth in the others.
- **"Being"** (existent): said primarily of substance (which exists *by itself*), derivatively of accidents (which exist only *in* a substance).

---

## The Logical Structure

In univocal predication, all instances share the same nature and the same definition. In a univocal genus, no member is the cause of the generic attribute in the others — all are equally "animal," no one animal being more the cause of animality in the others.

In analogical predication (*per prius et posterius*):
1. One instance is **primary** — it possesses the attribute most fully and is its own ground.
2. Other instances are **derivative** — they possess the attribute in a weaker or dependent mode, *through* the primary instance (which is typically their cause).
3. The term is neither univocal (they do not all have the same definition) nor merely equivocal (the meanings are not wholly unrelated — they all trace back to the same primary reference).

This means: things predicated analogically of a first term do **not** form a strict Aristotelian genus. A genus requires a definition that applies univocally to all members. If "intellect" is predicated *per prius et posterius* of the First Principle and the separated intellects, they do not share a genus in the strict sense.

## Averroes' Metaphysical Application

In Discussion 7 of the *Incoherence of the Incoherence*, Averroes mobilizes analogical predication as his primary defense of divine simplicity and uniqueness against al-Ghazali's objection that the philosophers are committed to an embarrassing consequence: if God shares "intellectuality" with the angels, He shares a genus with them, and must therefore be differentiated by a specific difference — making Him composite.

Averroes' response:

> "The term 'intellect' is predicated analogically of the separate intellects according to the philosophers, and there is among them a first intellect which is the cause of all the other intellects … the proof that they have not one nature in common is that some of them are the causes of others and the cause of a thing is prior to the effect, and the nature of cause and effect cannot be one in genus except in the individual causes."

The point is that when a term applies *per prius et posterius*, the primary instance (God, or the First Intellect) is the **cause** of the attribute's existence in all derivative instances. This causal priority is precisely what rules out genuine generic community: members of a genus are all of the same rank; no member is the cause of the generic attribute in the others. But God is not a member of the same rank as the angels — He is the cause of their being intellects at all.

Applied broadly to the metaphysical terms:
- **"Existent" (being)**: predicated primarily of God/the First Substance, derivatively of all other existents. God is not in a genus of "existents" with creatures — He is the cause of their existence.
- **"Substance"**: predicated primarily of what exists by itself in the fullest sense (God), derivatively of created substances.
- **"Intellect"**: predicated primarily of the First Intellect (God), derivatively of all other intellects.

This dissolves the genus-specific-difference objection to divine simplicity: the First Principle admits no univocal genus, and therefore the composition-argument (every genus-member has genus + specific difference) does not apply to it.

---

## The Uniqueness Corollary

Averroes further argues that the analogical structure of existence entails uniqueness of the First:

> "That which is in the extreme perfection of existence must be unique, for if it were not unique, it could not be of the extreme perfection of existence, for that which is in the extreme degree cannot participate with anything else, for in the same way as one single line cannot have two extreme points at the same end, things extended in existence and differentiated through increase and decrease have not two extremes at the same side."

The analogy is to a spectrum: if existence comes in degrees (with the First at the maximum), there can be only one maximum — just as a line has only one endpoint at each end. Two supposed "gods" of equal rank would have to share a univocal nature, which would both require composition and prevent either from being the maximum.

---

## Al-Farabi's Role

Averroes explicitly credits al-Farabi (rather than Avicenna) with the correct treatment of "existence" as analogically predicated:

> "Farabi proved this in his book about demonstration … And Farabi explains this in his Book of Letters and he shows that one of the reasons for the occurrence of this mistake [treating 'existence' as an accident] is that the term 'existent' in Arabic is a derivative in form."

Al-Farabi argued that the translators used "existent" (*mawjud*) to render the Greek *on* (being), not intending to signal an accident but the very quiddity of things. Avicenna misread the term as indicating an accident (derivative form implies accidental predication in Arabic grammar), leading to his erroneous doctrine that existence is additional to essence. Averroes follows al-Farabi against Avicenna on this point.

---

## Critique of Avicenna

Averroes argues that Avicenna's failure to distinguish univocal from analogical predication of existence led to:
1. Treating "existence" as a necessary attribute (common to all things) — but a univocal necessary attribute of things of radically different essences is impossible, because a single necessary attribute must flow from a single nature.
2. Treating existence as an "accident" additional to essence outside the soul — generating the infinite regress problem.
3. Being vulnerable to al-Ghazali's objections about God sharing genus with other existents.

Averroes' corrective: "existence" as a broad ontological term is predicated *per prius et posterius*, with God as the primary term and all other existents as derivative. This makes existence both a *real* and a *non-generic* predicate of God — preserving the doctrine of divine simplicity while maintaining genuine predication.

## Historical Significance

The doctrine of analogical predication entered Latin scholasticism through the translation of Averroes' commentaries on Aristotle (Michael Scot's translations, early 13th century). Thomas Aquinas developed a related doctrine of *analogia entis* (analogy of being) that is partly indebted to this Averroean tradition, though Aquinas ultimately modified it. The dispute over whether "being" (existence) is univocal (Duns Scotus) or analogical (Thomists) or equivocal (some nominalists) became a central dispute in 13th–14th century scholasticism.

In the Islamic tradition, the question remained less explicitly developed after Averroes; in the Eastern lands (Persia and later Mughal India), Mulla Sadra's doctrine of the "gradations of being" (*tashkik al-wujud*) represents a later and substantially different engagement with the same underlying problem.
