# Early Roman Source Criticism

## Definition and Scope

The methodology governing how historians reconstruct the early history of
[[rome|Rome]] — from the regal period (~753–509 BCE traditional) through the early
Republic — given a fundamental **source problem**: all narrative accounts of this
era were written centuries later, drawing on fragmentary and often fabricated
evidence, and the authentic archival record before the mid-fourth century was
sparse at best. [[walbank-cah-v7-2-1989|CAH VII.2 Ch. 1]] (Ogilvie & Drummond) is
the most systematic recent treatment, and explicitly warns that no single
methodology can be considered definitive for this period.

## The Sources and Their Problems

### Greek sources

**Timaeus of Tauromenium** (c. 315–264 BCE), a Sicilian historian working in
Athens, gave the first organized Greek account of early Rome in the introductions
to his history of the Western Greeks and of the Rome-Carthage rivalry. He was
reliant on local traditions and his account of the regal period was inevitably
reconstructed rather than documented.

**Polybius** (born c. 210–200 BCE) — the most critical of ancient historians and
second only to Thucydides as a "scientific investigator" — used Fabius Pictor as
his main Roman source and wrote an analytical account of Rome's rise. Only six of
his forty books survive substantially, and Book VI (treating early Roman
institutions) is fragmentary. His Platonic-derived cyclical philosophy colors the
account.

**Dionysius of Halicarnassus** (c. 60 BCE–after 7 BCE) spent twenty-two years
researching Roman history, producing twenty books of *Roman Antiquities* (eleven
survive). He used the same annalistic sources as Livy but had access to additional
recondite versions; his approach is moralistic, rhetorically elaborate, and
structurally influenced by Greek political theory.

**Diodorus Siculus**, **Plutarch**, and (in fragmentary form) **Cassius Dio** offer
supplementary but derivative coverage.

### Roman sources

The first Roman history was by **Q. Fabius Pictor** (writing in Greek, c. 200 BCE),
who focused on the foundation legends and his own day while giving little to the
fifth and fourth centuries. His chauvinistic purpose makes his reliability suspect,
though his clear fabrications cannot be firmly proven from the fragments.

The first-century BCE "big annalists" — **Q. Claudius Quadrigarius**, **C.
Licinius Macer**, **Valerius Antias**, **Q. Aelius Tubero** — produced large works
(seventy-five books in Antias's case) that suddenly expanded the historical
narrative. The expansion was largely inventive. Antias's reckless figures and Macer's
*popularis* political coloring are notorious. Quadrigarius **began at 390 BCE**,
implying he considered everything before that date largely legendary — a telling
admission.

**T. Livius (Livy)** (c. 59 BCE–17 CE): books I–X survive (753–293 BCE); through
literary genius, not scholarly method, Livy became the only early annalist whose
work is substantially preserved. He had minimal research interest and drew
heavily on the annalists, incorporating their fabrications. His approach is
fundamentally moral: what matters is the conduct, not the institutional mechanics.

**Antiquarians** — **M. Terentius Varro** (116–27 BCE), **Verrius Flaccus** — also
transmitted much by systematic collection rather than critical history.

### The pontifical record

The *pontifex maximus* kept an annual whiteboard record (*tabulae pontificum*,
eventually published in eighty books as the *Annales Maximi* c. 130 BCE by
P. Mucius Scaevola). Its scope was limited: eclipses, high corn prices, religious
events, elections, portents. A detailed narrative history of fifth-century politics
was **not** in these records; the annalists' rich fifth-century detail cannot derive
from them. Moreover, whether the pontifex maintained continuous records from the
early Republic (or whether a break at the Gallic Sack of 390 BCE was genuine or
pretextual) is itself contested.

### The consular fasti

A consecutive list of annual magistrates survives with high uniformity across all
ancient witnesses, including the late Republic's inscribed *Capitoline Fasti*. This
list derives ultimately from a single common tradition (possibly a very early
document) and provides the backbone of Roman chronology. It was not, however, a
narrative source — it could indicate family names, the consular/tribunate form,
and admissions of plebeians to office, but no more.

### Inscriptions and archaeology

Genuine inscriptions from before c. 250 BCE are meager and often spurious. What
does exist — the **Lapis Niger** (archaic sanctuary inscription at the Comitium),
the **Satricum inscription** (dedication by the *suodales* of P. Valerius to Mars,
c. 500 BCE), the **Pyrgi bilingual dedication** (Etruscan and Phoenician), the
**Capitoline Fasti** (published c. 30/17 BCE) — is valuable precisely because it is
primary, but most alleged archaic documents contain anachronisms (*cognomina*,
literary dating formulae) that mark them as later fabrications or reconstructions.

Archaeology is the most trustworthy source for structural conditions but cannot
supply narrative history. No archaeological trace of the Gallic Sack of 390 has
been found.

## Techniques of Fabrication

The annalists "invented" early Roman history by:

1. **Retrojecting contemporary experience** — the agrarian conflicts of the Gracchan
   era provided the template for supposed fifth-century land disputes; tribunician
   prosecutions before the centuriate assembly are modeled on mid-Republican practice.
2. **Greek parallels** — the five classes of Servius echoed Solon; the Decemvirate
   was made to parallel the Athenian lawgivers.
3. **Family aggrandizement** — the Valerii were credited with the foundations of
   liberty; the Claudii were stereotyped as patrician tyrants; the Fabii received
   heroic tragedy at the Cremera (477 BCE).
4. **Repetition** — entire episodes were duplicated (with modest variation) across
   different years, filling the narrative structure created by the annalistic year-
   by-year format.
5. **Literary elaboration** — battle narratives, speeches, and moral tableaux were
   composed on Hellenistic models, producing credible and readable history without
   authentic sources.

## Methodological Implications

Ogilvie and Drummond conclude:

- Before the mid-fourth century, **detailed political narrative is almost always
  unverifiable** and usually fictional.
- The proper focus should be **structural trends** and **general developments** 
  (land tenure, social differentiation, constitutional evolution, trade networks)
  that can be supported by institutional survivals, comparative analysis, and
  archaeology.
- Even apparently uncontroversial material may be the historians' own work; the
  onus is on demonstrating **how a given datum could have been reliably preserved**
  before accepting it as potentially authentic.
- The volume's editors **deliberately embraced a plurality of approaches** to the
  contested origins chapters (Torelli, Momigliano, Drummond, Cornell) rather than
  seeking a single authoritative account.

The Ogilvie-Drummond methodology is more skeptical than Cornell's (Chs. 6–8),
which is more willing to extract structural information from the annalistic
tradition. Both are more skeptical than the older positivist tradition.
