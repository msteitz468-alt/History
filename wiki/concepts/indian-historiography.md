# Indian Historiography (Ancient India)

## Definition and Scope

This page covers the methods, sources, biases, and intellectual history of the scholarly study of ancient India — from its initial neglect, through British colonial-era Indology, Indian nationalist reaction, and the post-independence historiography that shapes current scholarship.

This is primarily an **etic** framework: the problems of ancient Indian historiography are partly problems of source survival and type, and partly problems of the interpretive frameworks imposed by successive generations of scholars with their own political and cultural investments.

## The Problem of Ancient Indian History: Sources

Avari identifies three valid forms of primary historical evidence for ancient India:

### 1. Literary Texts

The literary corpus divides into religious and secular sections:

**Religious texts:** The Vedas and Vedic literature, the Mahabharata and Ramayana epics, Buddhist works (Tripitakas, Nikayas, Dipavamsa), Jain Agam texts, legal manuals (Dharmashastras, Dharmasutras), the devotional Puranas, and devotional hymns of Vishnu and Shiva worshippers.

**Secular texts:** The Arthashastra (political economy), mathematical and astronomical treatises, medical and surgical writings, grammatical works and lexicons, works on architecture, and works on music, dance and drama.

A fundamental problem: "No systematic historical works by ancient Indians are available, in the way historians such as Herodotus (485–425 bc), Livy (59 bc to ad 17), Ssu-Ma-Chien (c. 145–87 bc) or the Venerable Bede (c. ad 673–735) composed histories of early Greece, Rome, China and England." The closest approximations are Banabhatta's biography of King Harsha (seventh century CE) and Kalhana's Rajatarangini (history of Kashmir). Foreign accounts — Greek, Chinese, Tibetan, Roman, Islamic (including Al-Biruni) — are often more systematically historical than Indian texts.

Avari identifies a critical bias: "The early Indians paid scant attention to precise documentation and systematic recording of events in their country." The Indians were "slower in developing intelligible scripts than the ancient Egyptians and Mesopotamians." All the religious and secular texts, however, do contain "a huge amount of material" from which historians can extract historical information — "the challenge for the historians is to be able to distinguish the historical from the non-historical evidence."

**The brahmanical bias:** Because the orientalists of the first phase of Indology were tutored mainly by brahman Sanskritists, they interpreted the Indian past "mostly through the Sanskrit texts, which narrowed their horizons." The brahmanical texts are not a neutral record: they reflect an upper-caste, ritual-centred worldview and systematically marginalise non-brahmanical traditions, women, lower castes, and regional dissenting movements.

### 2. Archaeological Evidence

Three strands of archaeological work in India:
1. Discovery, recording, restoration and cataloguing of above-ground remains, especially inscriptions on rocks, stones, pillars, temple walls and copper plates. The inscriptions include royal declarations (like Ashoka's edicts), commemorative records, land grants, and commercial inscriptions from guilds.
2. Recovery of materials below ground: ancient coins, fossils, tools, and from the 1920s onwards, the Harappan culture and civilisation.
3. Selected recent diggings at famous religious, urban or commercial sites to corroborate textual and epigraphic evidence.

Archaeology is particularly crucial for prehistoric periods (before c. 1500 BCE) where no readable texts exist, and for the Harappan Civilisation whose script remains undeciphered.

### 3. Multi-Disciplinary Research

Since the mid-twentieth century, contributions from anthropology, ethnography, geology, meteorology, genetics, biology and botany have greatly enhanced historical understanding. Genetics provides clues to the origins of South Asian peoples; anthropology guides historians toward oral history from marginalised subaltern groups who exist in large numbers in India.

## Chronological Problems

"All dates of ancient Indian history are somewhat fluid, and in the dating of some events one has to agree a certain 'give and take' of a few years." Ancient India lacked a uniform dating system. Different dynasties and religious communities used different calendars with different start-years. Chronology has been established largely through synchronisation with Greek, Islamic, and Chinese sources (for example, Alexander's invasion of 327 BCE), astronomical calculations from Indian sources (Aryabhatta's 499 CE work), and Islamic chronology (which began from 622 CE and is more systematic than the ancient Indian).

## The Intellectual History of Ancient Indian Historiography

### Phase 1: The Orientalists (late 18th century)

Systematic historiography of ancient India began only in the mid-eighteenth century. The first phase was characterised by a "uniquely fruitful interaction between power and scholarship" under the Governor-General Warren Hastings. Orientalists including Sir William Jones (1746–94), Nathaniel Halhead, Charles Wilkins (1749–1836) and Henry Colebrook (1765–1837) studied Sanskrit, translated major texts (Bhagavadgita, Manusmriti, various Dharmashastras, Shakuntala), and provided the first systematic interpretations of Indian culture to European audiences. Their major weakness: because tutored by brahman Sanskritists, they interpreted the Indian past through Sanskrit texts alone, narrowing their horizons.

### Phase 2: Colonial Denigration and Utilitarianism (early 19th century)

The orientalists' project came under challenge as British power over India consolidated after 1799. Social movements in Britain — utilitarianism, evangelism, free trade — exercised profound impact in India. The iconic figure of this phase is James Mill (1773–1836), whose multi-volume History of British India divided Indian history into three periods (Hindu, Muslim, British) and argued that India had been gripped by "oriental despotism" in the first two periods.

Lord Macaulay (1800–59) "displayed a patronising attitude towards the country and her culture." His *Minutes on Indian Education* (1835) castigated both Hindu and Islamic education and proposed replacing Sanskrit and Urdu with English in state schools. Avari cites Macaulay's own words: "We must at present do our best to form a class who may be interpreters between us and the millions whom we govern; a class of persons, Indian in blood and colour, but English in taste, in opinions, in morals, and in intellect." This colonial-era denigration is what the concept of **orientalism** (Edward Said's analysis, 1994) captures — though Avari notes Said's analysis does not apply to all British scholars of India, citing the substantial contributions of Cunningham, Prinsep, Fergusson, Vincent Smith, and John Marshall.

### Phase 3: Indian Nationalist Response (19th–early 20th century)

The Indian response to British historiographical dominance came in two forms:

**Uncritical Hindu nationalism:** Figures like B.G. Tilak (1856–1920), Dayanand Saraswati (1824–83), Bankim Chandra Chatterjee (1838–94), and V.D. Savarkar (1883–1966) adopted nationalistic fervour and "uncritically examined and glorified Hinduism and the Hindu past." This tendency, Avari argues, is "a defensive reaction in the face of the progress that has been made in the West during the last 200 years." He explicitly criticises it as a distortion comparable to colonial denigration.

**Measured academic nationalism:** Other Indians adopted "a more measured approach" — including R.G. Bhandarkar (1837–1925), H.C. Raychaudhuri (1892–1957), and R.C. Majumdar. These academics followed and improved the high standards set by Vincent Smith.

### Phase 4: Post-Independence Scholarship (from 1947)

British and European historians have adopted a "more humble" approach. The most significant new development is the Marxist historiography pioneered by D.D. Kosambi, whose An Introduction to the Study of Indian History (1956) and The Culture and Civilisation of Ancient India in Historical Outline (1965) introduced new concepts and methodologies. Avari credits Kosambi with applying "mathematician's logic, ethnologist's acute observation and scholar's incisive interpretation." The Marxist method "of studying the present by asking relevant social and economic questions from the past is one of the soundest ways of studying history."

Contemporary eminent historians in this tradition include professors Romila Thapar and Irfan Habib. A.L. Basham's The Wonder that was India is cited as "perhaps the most magnificent" of post-independence works from European scholars.

## Key Distortions to Guard Against

Avari identifies several recurring distortions in the study of ancient India:

1. **Western stereotyping:** The "imbalance" caused by Western obsession with Indian religion — particularly Hinduism and Buddhism — has produced stereotypes of Indians as "a highly spiritual people who do not care for material goods" or having a "fatalistic approach." This misses India's intellectual, mathematical, artistic, and commercial heritage.

2. **Nationalist glorification:** The "unfortunate tendency" among some modern Indians to study ancient India "for the purpose of glorifying India and proclaiming the quite unproven achievements of ancient Indians." Avari gives the example of a widely circulated quote falsely attributed to Macaulay that praises India — no proof of which exists in any of Macaulay's actual writings.

3. **The "Hindu period" fallacy:** It would be "a mistake to regard the ancient period as the 'Hindu period'." There is very little archaeological evidence of Hindu culture during the first 4,000 years of ancient Indian history (7000–3000 BCE). Vedic Hindu culture began around 2000 BCE; pervasive Buddhist and Jain influences then dominated for a thousand years or more; resurgent "Puranic and devotional Hinduism" emerged only from around CE 500.

4. **Failure to acknowledge India's shortcomings:** The ancient Indians were "slower in developing intelligible scripts" than Egyptians and Mesopotamians; "showed abysmal disregard for issues of inequality and poverty"; and lagged behind China in technology. The caste system "remains perhaps the greatest moral blot on the record of ancient India."

5. **The false dichotomy of Hindu vs. Muslim periods:** Mill's three-fold periodisation (Hindu/Muslim/British) is analytically invalid and politically tendentious.

## Limitations and Critiques

The brahmanical dominance of the textual record means that Indian history written primarily from literary texts is necessarily a history of elites, written by an elite, in an elite language. The subaltern groups — lower castes, women, tribal peoples, non-Sanskritic communities — are systematically under-represented. Archaeology and anthropology are crucial correctives, but even archaeological evidence tends to favour urban and elite material culture. Oral history and anthropological methods remain underexploited.

The colonial-era argument that India had a "timeless" or "cyclical" historical consciousness and thus no real historiography is itself a product of the distortions Avari analyses. The absence of systematic historical writing in the Greek/Roman mold does not mean the absence of historical consciousness.
