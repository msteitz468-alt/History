# Post-Processual Archaeology

Post-processual archaeology emerged in the 1980s as a broad critique of the positivist, processual (often called "New Archaeology") paradigm that had dominated Anglo-American archaeology since the 1960s. It rejects the idea that archaeology can or should aspire to a unified, objective, law-like science modeled on the natural sciences. Instead, it emphasizes the socially constructed, interpretive, and political nature of archaeological knowledge.

Core positions include:
- The past is not directly accessible; all interpretations are shaped by the present social, political, and personal context of the interpreter (hermeneutics).
- Material culture is actively meaningful and symbolic; it is not merely a passive reflection of social organization or adaptation.
- Power, ideology, agency, gender, and individual or small-group action must be central concerns.
- There is no single "correct" interpretation; multiple readings are possible and debate is essential.

In the European context (as surveyed in Hodder ed. 1991), post-processual ideas interacted with longer-standing Marxist, historical, and critical traditions rather than simply replacing processual ones. Many European countries never fully adopted the American processual package, making the "post-processual" turn less of a sharp break and more of an intensification of existing critical and historical emphases.

The movement is closely associated with Ian Hodder's work on contextual archaeology and the broader "interpretive" turn.

### Insights from Eastern Europe under Soviet Influence (Hodder ed. 1991, Range 05)
Czechoslovak and Hungarian chapters show why the interpretive/critical turn resonated differently in Eastern Europe. Under Soviet influence, explicit theory was risky: "theory became dangerous, whether Marxian or not." Official "vulgar Marxism" (reduced to class struggle and base/superstructure) was often imposed dogmatically or used as superficial garnish, while genuine theoretical debate risked ideological conflict because "ideology and philosophy were part and parcel of the institutionalised section of the political system." 

Local responses included strong empirical traditions ("archaeological ethnography," "historicity") that emphasized reanimating the makers of artefacts and synthetic historical processes over abstract models or schematic evolution — approaches that prefigured or paralleled post-processual concerns with meaning, context, agency, and the limits of categories. "Creative uncertainty" (László) acknowledged that "our methods and tools are restricted, our conclusions cannot be absolute." New Archaeology arrived late (often via personal contacts or foreign projects rather than texts) and was met with defenses of tradition rather than wholesale adoption.

These cases confirm that European post-processualism built upon (and sometimes intensified) pre-existing critical, historical, and Marxist-inflected skepticism toward positivist "New Archaeology," rather than representing a clean break imported from the Anglosphere. The 1989–1990 political openings (explicitly reflected in 1990 comments) are presented as enabling freer engagement with international interpretive debates.

## Historiography and Critiques

Critics (including some processual archaeologists) charge post-processualism with relativism, lack of methodological rigour, and over-emphasis on the present at the expense of the past. Defenders argue that explicit acknowledgement of subjectivity and politics leads to more responsible and reflexive practice.

European variants often retained stronger commitments to historical particularism and Marxism than some Anglo-American expressions.
