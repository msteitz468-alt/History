## Definition and Scope

**Aqida** (Arabic: "creed", lit. "that which is bound or tied fast") refers to the core articles of Islamic belief — those doctrines whose affirmation is obligatory and whose denial is held to exclude a person from the community of believers. In Islamic theological tradition, a credal article of this standing must be grounded in categorical proof-texts (Qur'an verses unambiguous in both transmission and meaning, *qat'i al-dalala*) rather than probabilistic or speculative arguments.

The five foundational beliefs, derived from [[quran|Qur'an]] 2:285 and elaborated in [[hadith]] literature, became the standard skeleton of the early creed:
1. Belief in one God (*tawhid*)
2. Belief in God's angels
3. Belief in God's scriptures
4. Belief in God's messengers (prophets)
5. Belief in the Day of Judgement (and divine destiny/qadar)

This skeleton is notably minimal. The Qur'an did not supply a single consolidated credal text; al-Razi later observed that doctrines are scattered through the Qur'an as "the armature of other, practical teachings" rather than as a systematic treatise (Winter, ch. 1). The elaboration of creed beyond this kernel was driven by political crisis, not abstract theology.

## Historical Development

### The Political Foundation (632–661)

Formal creed-writing was not native to the first Islamic generation. The Prophet's death in 632 triggered succession disputes whose moral dimension rapidly acquired theological weight. The First Civil War (*fitna*, 656–661), fought among Abu Bakr's supporters, the party of Ali, and the Kharijites, forced early Muslims to address questions the Qur'an had not settled explicitly: What disqualifies a ruler? Does grave sin expel a Muslim from the community of believers? Is faith separable from works?

Blankinship (Winter, ch. 2) is emphatic that "the earliest Muslim schisms emerged over what were in the first instance political matters" and that their doctrinal fixity came only later. The credal tradition therefore crystallised around political fault lines, not independent philosophical reflection.

### The Five Doctrinal Fault Lines

The early creed took shape in response to five interrelated disputes:

**1. The caliphate and political legitimacy.** Who has the right to rule the Muslim community? The [[kharijites|Kharijites]] demanded a morally exemplary leader elected by the community; the [[shia-islam|Shi'a]] insisted on a descendant of the Prophet designated by divine appointment (*nass*); the proto-Sunnis favoured membership of the Quraysh tribe as the minimal qualification; the Murji'ites counselled suspension of judgement on controversial past caliphs.

**2. Sin and its consequences.** Does grave sin (*kabira*) expel a Muslim from the community of believers? The Kharijites said yes — the unrepentant mortal sinner is an apostate. The Murji'ites said judgment should be deferred to God. The early [[mutazilism|Mu'tazilites]], following Wasil ibn Ata (d. 748), identified an "intermediate state" (*manzila bayn al-manzilatayn*) — the grave sinner is neither believer nor unbeliever. The proto-Sunni position, eventually dominant, held that the grave sinner remains a believer but may face punishment.

**3. Free will versus predestination (qadar).** The pre-Islamic Arab worldview assumed a predetermined fate (*dahr*). Early caliphs, especially Mu'awiya (661–80) and Abd al-Malik (685–705), endorsed determinism, which served dynastic legitimacy by implying that God had ordained their rule. Pietist dissidents — first the Qadariyya, later the Mu'tazilites — insisted on genuine human agency as a precondition for moral accountability.

**4. The divine attributes.** Do Qur'anic descriptions of God's "hands", "face", and "throne" indicate real attributes coeternal with God, or must they be allegorised? The Mu'tazilites denied that attributes had real existence separate from God's essence, to preserve divine unity. The Sunni traditionists, especially the Hanbalites, insisted the attributes were real and coeternal, accepted "without specifying how" (*bi-la kayf*). The Ash'arites ultimately synthesised these positions.

**5. The createdness of the Qur'an.** The Mu'tazilites held the Qur'an to be God's created speech; the Sunni traditionists, led by Ahmad ibn Hanbal (780–855), insisted it was uncreated and coeternal with God. This became the flashpoint for the Abbasid *mihna* (inquisition, 833–851), in which Mu'tazilism was briefly enforced as state doctrine.

### Abu Hanifa's Al-Fiqh al-Akbar I

The earliest surviving formal creed text is plausibly attributed to Abu Hanifa (c. 699–767), the Kufan jurist associated with the [[early-theological-sects|Murji'ite]] tradition. The *al-Fiqh al-Akbar I* contains ten points:
- Opposition to the Kharijites, Qadariyya, Shi'ites, and Jahmiyya
- Suspension of judgement (*irja'*) regarding Uthman and Ali
- Equal respect for all Companions of the Prophet
- A form of predestinarian belief
- Reference to God as established on His throne

Blankinship notes this document "shows how close the Murji'a were to later Sunnism" (Winter, ch. 2). It is the prototype for the systematic creed (*aqida*) literature that would be codified by al-Ash'ari (874–936) and [[al-maturidi]] (d. 944).

### Ash'arite Synthesis (mid-10th century)

By the mid-tenth century the Muslim world had settled on several enduring doctrinal alignments: the Ash'ari, Maturidi, and Hanbali Sunnis; Mu'tazilism among the Twelver and Zaydi Shi'a; Neoplatonism among the Ismaili Shi'a; and the Ibadi doctrines among residual Kharijite communities. The [[asharism|Ash'arite]] creed represented the decisive [[kalam]] formulation of Sunni belief, defending Hanbali substance with Mu'tazilite argumentative method.

## Key Credal Disputes and Their Resolution

| Dispute | Kharijite position | Shi'a position | Mu'tazilite position | Ash'arite resolution |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Grave sin | Expels from Islam | Depends on imam's guidance | "Intermediate state" | Sinner remains believer; may be punished temporarily |
| Free will | Human agency (to ground moral responsibility) | Varies; Twelvers adopted Mu'tazilism | Full human agency | God creates human acts; humans "acquire" them (*kasb*) |
| Divine attributes | Allegorical | Varies | No real existence; denial of attributes (*ta'til*) | Real but coeternal; accepted "without specifying how" |
| Qur'an | Created | Varies | Created | Uncreated (the eternal speech of God) |
| Caliphate | Elected; moral excellence required | Designated descendant of Ali | Just ruler required | Qurayshi; moral fitness preferred but not constitutive |

## Analytical Notes

The credal tradition is unusual in world religious history for the speed with which it politicised. Within thirty years of the Prophet's death, the entire framework of later Islamic theology — free will, sin, the divine attributes, the nature of the caliphate — had its structural outline, driven by civil war rather than philosophical disputation. The Greek philosophical heritage entered only in the second Islamic century and was absorbed into an already-structured debate, not its origin.

The minimal doctrinal kernel (the five beliefs) remained stable throughout these controversies, which is why Ash'arite, Maturidi, and Hanbali Sunnis, though disagreeing on significant points of method and detail, all remained within the fold of Sunni Islam. The creed distinguished intra-community disagreement from apostasy.

## Historiography

Blankinship's chapter in Winter's *Cambridge Companion* (2008) is the most accessible English-language guide to the formative period. W. Montgomery Watt, *The Formative Period of Islamic Thought* (Edinburgh, 1973) remains the standard reference for the political-theological nexus. Josef van Ess, *Theologie und Gesellschaft im 2. und 3. Jahrhundert Hidschra* (Berlin, 1991–5), 6 vols., is the most thorough primary-source study but in German only.

The collection's coverage of this period is moderate through the Winter volume; deeper primary sources (the kalam texts themselves) are largely absent.
