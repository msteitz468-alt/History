# Diplomacy of Violence

The **diplomacy of violence** is Schelling's term for the use of military force (or the threat of it) as an instrument of bargaining and influence rather than as a means of direct physical conquest or disablement. It is "the uglier, more negative, less civilized part of diplomacy — nevertheless, diplomacy."

## Core Distinction

The traditional Western image of military strategy is the application of brute force: seizing, occupying, disarming, expelling, or destroying the enemy's capacity to resist. In this mode the adversary's interests and will are largely irrelevant except as obstacles measured by opposing strength.

The diplomacy of violence instead exploits the adversary's interests, fears, and capacity to suffer. Its currency is the power to *hurt* — pain, grief, privation, destruction of valued things — held in reserve or applied contingently so as to structure the adversary's choices. "The power to hurt is bargaining power. To exploit it is diplomacy — vicious diplomacy, but diplomacy."

Key contrasts (Schelling):

- Brute force overcomes strength; the power to hurt exploits wants and fears.
- Brute force is measured relative to enemy strength; the power to hurt is not directly cancelled by the enemy's ability to hurt in return.
- Brute force succeeds when used; the power to hurt succeeds most when its use can be withheld.
- Brute force is unilateral; coercion requires a common interest (at minimum, the avoidance of mutual damage) and some communication of contingency.

## Requirements for Effective Coercive Diplomacy

- The victim must know what behavior will trigger the violence and what behavior will cause it to be withheld.
- There must be an "assurance" (explicit or implicit) that compliance avoids the threatened pain.
- Interests cannot be absolutely opposed; there must be a bargain in which the target is better off complying (taking the threat into account) than not.
- The threat must be credible.

The violence, when used, is often communicative or demonstrative: not the final blow but evidence that more is available and contingent.

## Scope and Limits

Schelling notes that with enough brute force a state may not need to bargain at all. But in the nuclear age — and in many conventional settings where decisive military victory is too costly or risky — the diplomacy of violence becomes the central mode. The book is an exploration of its principles, difficulties, and techniques (commitment, risk manipulation, idiom of action, etc.).

The concept is not limited to the nuclear era. It describes siege warfare aimed at surrender rather than storm, economic strangulation, terror bombing intended to break will, sanctions, and limited uses of force for political signaling.
