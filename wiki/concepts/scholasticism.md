# Scholasticism

## Definition and Scope

**Scholasticism** is the method of learning, teaching, and disputation that developed in the
[[twelfth-century-renaissance|cathedral schools and emerging universities]] of high-medieval
Europe and dominated Western intellectual life into the later Middle Ages. Less a body of
doctrine than a **method**, it applied the tools of **logic and dialectic** to the authoritative
texts of Scripture, the Church Fathers, and (increasingly) the rediscovered works of Aristotle,
seeking to reconcile apparent contradictions and to systematise knowledge through reasoned
argument. Its characteristic form was the *quaestio* — posing a question, marshalling authorities
*for* and *against*, and resolving the conflict by distinction.

## Origins and Major Figures (NCMH IV)

The [[luscombe-ncmh-v4-2004|New Cambridge Medieval History, Vol. IV]] places the rise of
scholastic thought at the heart of what made the eleventh and twelfth centuries "perhaps the most
dynamic period in the European middle ages":

- **Anselm of Canterbury** (d. 1109) — "faith seeking understanding" (*fides quaerens
  intellectum*) and the ontological argument; the application of reason to faith.
- **Peter Abelard** (d. 1142) — the boldest dialectician; his ***Sic et Non*** ("Yes and No")
  juxtaposed conflicting patristic authorities to train students in resolving them by logic.
- **The school of Chartres** — humanist study of the *artes* and the Platonic natural philosophy
  of the *Timaeus*.
- **Gratian** (*Decretum*, c. 1140) — the same method applied to **canon law**, harmonising
  discordant canons; the NCMH notes his theology was shaped by early scholastic thought.
- **Peter Lombard** (*Sentences*, c. 1150) — the systematic theological textbook whose
  *quaestio* structure became the standard framework of university theology for centuries.

This intellectual ferment was institutionally rooted in the shift from monastic to **urban
cathedral schools** and the birth of the **university** (Bologna for law, Paris for theology) —
part of the broader [[twelfth-century-renaissance]].

## Emic vs. Etic

The medieval masters did not call themselves "scholastics"; the term (from *schola*, school) is a
later — and originally often pejorative (Renaissance humanist, then Enlightenment) — label for the
method of the schoolmen. The *practice* of dialectical disputation, however, was thoroughly emic
and self-conscious.

## Limitations and Critiques

- Renaissance humanists and later critics mocked scholasticism as arid hair-splitting (the
  caricature of "how many angels can dance on a pinhead") — a polemic more than a fair description.
- Its reliance on authority and syllogism is often contrasted with later **empirical** science,
  though recent scholarship stresses the rationalising, questioning impulse scholasticism shared
  with — and bequeathed to — the scientific tradition.
