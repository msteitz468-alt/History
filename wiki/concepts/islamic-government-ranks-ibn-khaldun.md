## Overview

In Chapter III of the [[ibn-khaldun-muqaddimah|Muqaddimah]], [[ibn-khaldun|Ibn Khaldun]] provides the most systematic comparative analysis of Islamic governmental offices produced in the medieval period. His account is unusual in that it traces the *evolution* of each office across multiple dynasties — Rashidun, Umayyad, 'Abbasid, Fatimid, Almoravid, Almohad, Hafsid, Merinid, Mamluk (Turkish) — rather than simply defining what the offices ought to be. The result is an institutional history governed by a single analytical principle: offices change in response to the needs of [[asabiyyah|asabiyyah]] and the stage of the [[the-dynastic-cycle-ibn-khaldun|dynastic cycle]].

## The Fourfold Structure of Royal Functions

Ibn Khaldun reduces all royal and governmental functions to four types, each requiring a different kind of helper:

1. **Military protection and war operations** — "the sword" — led by the wazir in early Umayyad usage, later by the amir
2. **Correspondence with those far away** — "the pen" — led by the secretary (*katib*)
3. **Tax collection and expenditure management** — "the finances" — led by the chief of the *diwan*
4. **Access control to the ruler** — led by the doorkeeper (*hajib*)

All other governmental ranks are subdivisions of these four. The relative importance of "the sword" versus "the pen" shifts predictably across the dynastic cycle: the sword predominates at foundation and at senility; the pen predominates at the secure middle period when the ruler's main task is administering and taxing a stable realm.

## The Wazirate (*Wizarah*)

The wazirate is "the mother of governmental functions and royal ranks." The word derives either from *mu'azarah* (help/assistance) or *wizr* (burden), both pointing to its core function: helping the ruler carry his load.

**Pre-Islamic origins**: The institution existed in Persian and Byzantine forms; Arab familiarity with it came through contact with those empires. The word *wazir* was not originally Islamic.

**Rashidun period**: No formal wazirate existed. The Prophet consulted Abu Bakr closely, leading some observers to call Abu Bakr Muhammad's wazir — but as a description, not a title. The simplicity of early Islam and the illiteracy of Arabs made specialized secretarial functions unnecessary.

**Umayyad period**: The wazirate emerged as a formal position with general supervisory authority over military and advisory matters. It was the highest rank, outranking the secretariat and doorkeeper. The wazir had broad discretion delegated by the caliph.

**'Abbasid period**: The wazirate reached its institutional apex. Under Harun al-Rashid, Ja'far b. Yahya was effectively called "sultan" — his power extended across all governmental functions except the doorkeeper's, which he disdained. The wazirate bifurcated when non-Arab rulers took control of the caliphate:
- **Executive wazirate**: the wazir executes the ruler's decisions but does not initiate them
- **Delegated wazirate**: the wazir controls the ruler and exercises all caliphal functions; this is equivalent in legal theory to the caliph delegating his full authority, raising jurisprudential questions about whether two delegated wazirs could be appointed simultaneously

**Post-'Abbasid**: Non-Arab amirs and sultans found the title "wazir" beneath them (they were "sultans") and reserved it for subordinate financial officials. In the Mamluk (Turkish) dynasty of Egypt, the wazir became specifically the head of tax collection, while military and political authority vested in the na'ib (deputy).

**Umayyad Spain**: The wazirate was subdivided into specialized functions — wazir for finances, wazir for correspondence, wazir for border regions, wazir for petitions — each sitting in a dedicated "house" (*dar*). The liaison wazir between these specialists and the caliph became the most important, effectively functioning as a doorkeeper.

**Almohad/Hafsid/Merinid dynasties**: The wazirate was used in its original general sense at first, then progressively narrowed or elevated according to dynastic custom.

## The Doorkeeper (*Hijabah*)

The doorkeeper (*hajib*, literally "one who screens") began as a security measure and rose, in some dynasties, to become the highest office in the state.

**Origin**: After early Islamic leaders (including 'Umar, 'Ali, Mu'awiyah) were attacked, rulers needed someone to screen access. 'Abd al-Malik's doorkeeper instructions survive: admit only the muezzin, the postal official (in case of important news), and the person in charge of food (lest it spoil) — all others to be turned away.

**Umayyad East**: A relatively minor position, subordinate to the wazir.

**Umayyad Spain**: Rose to great prominence. Great personalities of the dynasty held it. Under the Umayyad decline, the person who controlled the caliph — effectively the regent — was called *hajib*: al-Mansur b. Abi 'Amir and his sons held this title while exercising royal power. The reyes de taifas competed to use the title as honorific of sovereignty. The combined title *dhu l-wizaratayn* (holder of both wazirates, of the sword and the pen) appeared.

**'Abbasid**: A second, more restrictive entrance system developed (for the inner circle, not merely common people), and a third still more restrictive one appeared in later senility, used by factions seeking to isolate the young caliph from external influences — a mechanism of dynastic control. Ibn Khaldun identifies the emergence of this third entrance restriction as a symptom of advanced senility.

**Hafsid dynasty**: The doorkeeper's office expanded to encompass all executive functions — finance, correspondence, military leadership, petitions — as the ruler entered seclusion. The hajib became the effective ruler. The last strong Hafsid ruler, Sultan Abul-'Abbas, abolished the office entirely to reclaim personal control.

**Mamluk (Turkish) dynasty of Egypt**: The doorkeeper functions as a local magistrate enforcing law among the population; the top executive is the na'ib, not the hajib.

## The Financial Administration (*Diwan*)

The *diwan* (ministry of taxation and military allowances) is the third pillar of royal authority alongside soldiers and correspondence: "Royal authority requires soldiers, money, and the means to communicate with those who are absent."

**Origin of the word**: Ibn Khaldun records two etymologies: (1) from the Persian *dewaneh* (crazy), because the secretaries in Khosraw's court appeared to be talking to themselves while calculating; (2) from the Persian word for devils (*diwaneh*), applied to secretaries because of their quick comprehension. The word then transferred to the book of accounts, and finally to the office itself.

**Foundation under 'Umar**: The diwan was first established in Islam under 'Umar I, prompted by the arrival of large sums from al-Bahrayn that proved difficult to distribute equitably without written records. Military muster rolls were the initial concern: knowing which soldiers were present and eligible for allowances.

**Arabization**: Under 'Abd al-Malik, Arabic replaced Greek in the Syrian diwan (completed in one year by Sulayman b. Sa'd) and Persian in the Iraqi diwan (completed by Salih b. 'Abd al-Rahman under al-Hajjaj's order). This administrative arabization was part of the broader Marwanid program of asserting Arab dynastic identity.

**Structural evolution**: The diwan expanded from military muster rolls to cover land tax, customs duties, expenditures, and salary administration. In some dynasties it was subdivided: inspector of the army (*nazir al-jaysh*) for military allowances; wazir for general finance; inspector of the privy purse (*nazir al-khass*) for the ruler's personal estates.

## The Secretariat (*Diwan al-Insha'*)

Official correspondence is, Ibn Khaldun argues, not strictly required by the nature of royal authority — desert dynasties dispensed with it entirely. It became important in Islam because of the Arabic literary tradition and the need to convey decisions in appropriately elevated style.

The secretary (*katib*) must be "selected from among the upper classes and be a refined gentleman of great knowledge." Ibn Khaldun reproduces at length the Epistle of 'Abd al-Hamid b. Yahya (Umayyad secretary under Marwan II, d. 750) — a document he treats as the classic exposition of secretarial virtues. The epistle calls for secretaries who combine modesty, sound judgment, command of Arabic literature and poetry, knowledge of accounting, discretion with secrets, and loyalty to their masters.

The secretariat's importance varies inversely with "the sword's" importance in the dynastic cycle. In the middle period of a secure dynasty, skilled correspondence allows the ruler to exercise authority over distant territories; in the early and late periods, military capacity matters more and the secretariat is subordinate.

## The Police (*Shurta*)

The chief of police combines investigative authority (determining guilt through pressure and circumstantial evidence — functions the religious law cannot perform) with executory authority (carrying out legal punishments after the judge has ruled). This dual function explains the office's structural position: between the juridical system and the military apparatus.

In Umayyad Spain the police was divided into "great police" (jurisdiction over all classes including notables) and "small police" (common people only). The chief of the great police sat at the palace gate and could be a stepping stone to the wazirate or doorkeeper's office.

## The Admiralty (*Almirante*)

Ibn Khaldun's treatment of the admiralty is distinctive in its explicitly Maghribi focus: the office survives, he notes, only in the western Mediterranean. The word itself (*almirand*) he derives from the Romance languages.

He provides a substantial narrative of Muslim naval power in the Mediterranean: from initial Arab reluctance (under 'Umar's ban on seafaring) to Mu'awiyah's authorization, through the Aghlabid conquest of Sicily, the height of Fatimid and Umayyad Spanish naval power (200 vessels each under 'Abd al-Rahman III), the Christian reconquest of the eastern Mediterranean during Fatimid and 'Abbasid weakness, and the Almohad naval recovery under Admiral Ahmad as-Siqilli. Ibn Khaldun's own time saw Muslim naval weakness in the East and ongoing Maghribi naval capacity — he expresses hope for Muslim naval revival.

## The Dynamics of Office Evolution

Running through all these specific accounts is a single analytical principle: offices are not static institutions but adaptive responses to the needs of the ruling group at each stage of the dynastic cycle.

At foundation: almost no formal offices; the ruler is personally accessible; "the sword" is supreme; simplicity and desert customs prevail.

At consolidation: formal offices emerge as governance complexity requires specialization; the ruler begins to withdraw; the wazir and doorkeeper appear.

At prosperity: offices multiply and specialize; "the pen" rises in relative importance; elaborate court ritual develops; titles proliferate.

At senility: offices are captured by factions seeking to control the secluded ruler; the doorkeeper or wazir becomes the effective ruler; the ruler's formal titles become empty; the dynasty approaches its end.

This is why the same office can signify entirely different things in different dynasties and at different stages: the hajib in early 'Abbasid times was a minor court functionary; the hajib in late Umayyad Spain was the most powerful man in the state.
