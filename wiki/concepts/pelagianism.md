# Pelagianism

**Pelagianism** is the name given to a fifth-century movement, and to the body of doctrine
Augustine constructed as its refutation, centered on the goodness and moral capacity of human
nature. Brown's key claim is that "Pelagianism as we know it... had come into existence, but in
the mind of **[[augustine-of-hippo|Augustine]]**, not of **[[pelagius|Pelagius]]**": Augustine
welded the mild ascetic Pelagius, the combative **Caelestius**, and the philosopher
**[[julian-of-eclanum|Julian of Eclanum]]** into a single coherent heresy.

## Doctrine

The Pelagian tendency held that human nature, created good, retains full freedom and capacity for
perfection; sin is imitation and habit, not an inherited defect ("rust" on sound metal, not a
wound in the metal itself); and baptism confers a fresh start for a heroic active life rather than
beginning a lifelong convalescence. Against the perfectionist demand — "since perfection is
possible for man, it is obligatory" — Augustine set his doctrine of
**[[original-sin-and-grace|original sin and grace]]**: humanity is "tied into" Adam's guilt by
physical descent, freedom is not a starting condition but something healed and achieved, and God
gives not merely commands but the *love* that alone fulfills them. The movement had a genuine
reformist edge — denunciations of judicial torture, cruelty, and aristocratic hypocrisy — and
appealed especially to Roman Christians whose faith had become conventional, and to provinces
dislocated by barbarian invasion (Britain, southern Italy, Gaul).

## The Controversy

The crisis broke in Africa around 411, when Caelestius, applying for the priesthood at Carthage,
was denounced for denying the transmission of Adam's sin and the rationale of infant baptism; the
African bishops invoked a letter of Cyprian affirming infant baptism against him. The councils of
Carthage and Milevis (416) condemned Pelagian ideas and pressed Pope Innocent I; after Pelagius's
acquittal at the **Synod of Diospolis (415)** and Pope Zosimus's initial reversal, an imperial
edict of Honorius (30 April 418) and Zosimus's *Epistula tractatoria* condemned the movement
empire-wide. Augustine preached "Causa finita est." The cause was then carried by Julian of
Eclanum into a decade-long polemic with Augustine, and revived among the monasteries of Africa and
southern Gaul (Hadrumetum, Marseilles, Lérins) as a "revolt of the monasteries" over
predestination that occupied Augustine's last years.

## Contested Significance

An older historiography read the Pelagian controversy as "the last phase of the long debate
between Christianity and classical culture" — Augustine's darker anthropology defeating a sunnier,
Stoic, classical view of human freedom, and so marking the turn from antiquity to a medieval world
of original sin and inscrutable grace. Brown records this reading but does not fully endorse it,
and in 2000 explicitly retracted his own earlier dismissal of the Julian controversy as "an
unintelligent slogging match." Post-1967 critical editions of Pelagius's own commentary on Paul
now allow him to be known "on his own terms," as one Christian teacher among others rather than
only through Augustine's polemics.
