# Kesselschlacht

**Kesselschlacht** ("cauldron battle") is the German term for a battle of encirclement, in
which a mobile force envelops an enemy formation from multiple directions, cutting off its
escape and supply routes and forcing its surrender or destruction within the resulting
pocket (*Kessel*). The technique was a signature feature of German operational practice on
the Eastern Front in the Second World War — massive early *Kesselschlachten* around Kiev,
Smolensk, and elsewhere destroyed whole Soviet army groups in 1941 — and was itself later
turned against the Wehrmacht by the Red Army as Soviet operational art matured, most
famously at Stalingrad (1942–43) and in subsequent encirclement operations across 1943–45.

## Doctrinal Context

The *Kesselschlacht* is closely associated with the broader German concept of
*Bewegungskrieg* (war of movement/maneuver warfare), which
prized rapid, decisive maneuver to destroy enemy forces outright rather than grinding
attritional advance. Its double-edged character — devastatingly effective against a
surprised or poorly coordinated enemy, but reliant on the encircling force's own mobility,
fuel, and logistics holding up — made it a recurring feature of Eastern Front campaigns
analyzed on both sides (see [[events/second-battle-of-kharkov-1942]],
[[events/kerch-campaign-1942]]).
