# Mesoamerican Periodization

## Definition

The conventional **stage chronology** used to organize prehistoric [[mesoamerica-culture-area|Mesoamerica]].
Adams (CHNPA II.1, Ch. 1) renames the old "Lithic" as **Paleoindian**, adopts **Archaic**,
and treats **Formative** as interchangeable with **Preclassic**. Absolute dates are regional
and often contested; the stages are best read as large chronological blocks rather than
uniform evolutionary grades.

## Stages (Adams framework, approximate)

| Stage | Approx. range (Adams) | Character |
|---|---|---|
| Paleoindian | ~40,000?/10,000–7000 BCE | First foragers; entry debates |
| Archaic | ~7000–2000 BCE | Broad-spectrum foraging; plant domestication |
| Preclassic / Formative | ~2000 BCE–150/300 CE | Villages, ranking, early states, Olmec/Maya/Oaxaca florescences |
| Classic | ~150/300–650/900 CE | Teotihuacan; Classic Maya; Monte Albán peak |
| Early Postclassic | ~650/900–1250 CE | After Classic collapses; Tula/Toltec and regional reorganizations |
| Late Postclassic | ~1250–1519 CE | Aztec Triple Alliance and contemporaries to Spanish invasion |

## From CHNPA II.1 — Stage Content and Boundary Debates

### Paleoindian and Archaic (Adams; Zeitlin & Zeitlin)

Adams: Paleoindian ~40,000?/10,000–7000 B.C.; earliest *certain* settlers in Mesoamerica about
10,000 B.C., possibly as early as 40,000 years ago; New World entry not later than 15,000 B.C.
via Beringia. Archaic 8/7000–1500 B.C.: plant domestication by at least 7000 B.C. (perhaps
8000); nonagricultural villages ~5000 B.C.; agricultural villages established all over
Mesoamerica by 1500 B.C. (Adams 2000).

Zeitlin & Zeitlin: Lithic/Preceramic era = Paleoindian + Archaic. Paleoindian ends / Archaic
begins somewhere between **9000 and 7000 b.c.** during global warming; North American Archaic
often set at 7000 b.c. with horse extinction. Strictly, earliest pottery at **2300 b.c.** ends
the Preceramic; some extend Archaic to **1500 b.c.** when fully sedentary agriculture is
essentially complete. Archaic subdivided: Early (9/7000–5000 b.c.), Middle (5000–3000), Late
(3000–2000). Radiocarbon from two stratified Oaxaca Valley sites sets the beginning of the
Mesoamerican Archaic at **8750 b.c.**—though many still use traditional 7000 b.c. (Zeitlin &
Zeitlin 2000).

**Process, not event:** inability to agree on precise Paleoindian/Archaic cutoffs reflects
dealing with processes of cultural change rather than discrete events—there is no single date
when hunting/gathering was replaced by agriculture or nomadism by sedentary villages (Zeitlin
& Zeitlin 2000).

### Preclassic / Formative (Adams)

Preclassic 1500 B.C.–A.D. 150: cultural elaboration toward complex cultures over ~1,650 years;
[[olmec|Olmec]] earliest to reach “civilization” about 1350 B.C., with possible Pacific Coast
predecessors; from ~600 B.C. large regional centers with major buildings; by A.D. 150 most
defining Mesoamerican features distinguishing it from North and Central American cultural areas
were in place (Adams 2000). Interchangeable labels: Formative ≈ Preclassic.

### Classic (Adams; Hammond)

Adams: Classic A.D. 150/300–650/900 depending on area; second florescence built on Preclassic
successes; first large-scale economic and political systems binding several regions.

Hammond: the tripartite Preclassic–Classic–Postclassic scheme was **developed in the Maya Area
and extended to the rest of Mesoamerica**. Classic defined as the period of lowland monumental
stone inscriptions using hieroglyphic script and [[mesoamerican-calendar|Long Count]]; with the
most widely accepted correlation, Classic ~A.D. 250–900 (8.10.10.0.0–10.4.0.0.0); correlation
places 11.16.0.0.0 on 12 Nov A.D. 1539. Until radiocarbon, the Long Count was the only absolute
chronology in the prehispanic New World; trade items and style extended it across Mesoamerica.
Archaic–Preclassic transition traditionally ~2000–1500 B.C.; Postclassic A.D. 900 to Spanish
Conquest (Hammond 2000).

### Evans & Webster 2001 — Encyclopedia default stages

The encyclopedia Introduction (Webster & Evans) uses a compact consensus skeleton that
aligns with Adams/Hammond but with slightly different Formative bounds:

| Stage | Evans & Webster (Intro) | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Paleoindian | pre–end of Pleistocene; secure widespread occupation by Clovis horizon ~11,500–11,000 BP | Pre-13,500 BP claims in Middle America remain equivocal (Valsequillo, Tlapacoya, El Bosque) |
| Archaic | **8000–2000 B.C.** | Agricultural origins; dry-cave sequences in Tehuacán, Oaxaca, Tamaulipas |
| Formative | **2500 B.C.–A.D. 300** (alt. entry uses c. 1600 B.C.–A.D. 250) | Ranking by 1200–800 B.C.; first urban state ~500–300 B.C.; writing/calendars by Late Formative |
| Classic | **A.D. 300–900** | Second great horizon (Teotihuacan + Classic Maya + Gulf/highland peers) |
| Postclassic | **A.D. 900–1519** | After Teotihuacan decline (~700) and Maya center declines (700–900); Aztec expansion early 15th c. |
| Spanish Conquest | 1521 Tenochtitlan; most of Mesoamerica within ~30 years; Itza to 1697 | Ends independent native political evolution |

Maize: domesticated from **teosinte** in west-central Mexican uplands; directly dated samples
no older than ~4,700 years, but editors judge first domestication **likely 4000–3000 B.C.**;
wide highland/lowland spread 4000–3500 years ago; settled pottery-using agriculturalists
common after **2000 B.C.** Source: [[evans-webster-archaeology-ancient-mexico-central-america-2001]].

### Entry-level refinements (EW2001)

**Classic subdivisions (Robert S. Santley):** traditionally A.D. 200 to 800–950 (earlier
start/end in some areas); Early Classic (200–600) and Late Classic (600–950); **“Middle
Classic”** (c. 400–600) marks wide distribution of Teotihuacan objects/imitations;
**“Epiclassic”** follows withdrawal of Teotihuacan influence; no single center dominated all
Mesoamerica. Complex societies developed or increased dramatically relative to Formative
antecedents—most were states based on economic/social stratification with
administrative-military-judicial apparatus.

**Postclassic as time-block (Michael E. Smith):** “Postclassic” designates roughly A.D.
900–1530 (fall of Classic cultures to Spanish conquest). Originally a developmental stage
(warlike/secular vs peaceful/theocratic Classic); labels are now applied to **blocks of time,
not stages or culture types**—earlier subjective judgments proved misleading. Redefinition led
to defining the **Epiclassic** (c. A.D. 700–900) between the fall of Teotihuacán (c. 700) and
Lowland Classic Maya collapse (c. 900); for Maya Lowlands this interval is the **Terminal
Classic**.

**Maya chronological scheme (David L. Webster):** Late Postclassic A.D. 1250–1500; Early
Postclassic 1000–1250; Terminal Classic 800–1000; Late Classic 600–800; Early Classic 250–600;
Protoclassic 100–250; Late Formative 400 B.C.–A.D. 100; Middle Formative 1000–400 B.C.; Early
Formative 2500–1000 B.C.; Archaic before 2500 B.C. Long-count dates anchor the Classic; earlier
periods rely on ceramics, style, stratigraphy, radiocarbon, and obsidian hydration.

**Formative terminology (John E. Clark):** developmental labels originally tied “Classic” to
civilizational climax; modern practice prefers Formative/Classic/Postclassic as chronological
labels only. With improved dating, Mesoamericanists hope eventually to describe time in
Western calendar terms rather than arbitrary phases. Formative dates in Clark’s entry: **1600
B.C. to A.D. 250** (radiocarbon years)—start marks first ceramic technology and sedentary
village life; terminal date approximates Maya emphasis on Long Count.

**Sedentism marker (Gordon R. Willey):** simple sedentary agricultural villages dominant in
many southern and central regions by at least **1500 BCE**; best evidence for first settled
sedentary communities where subsistence farming was established by about **2000 BCE**, with
earlier sedentism possible on shellfish/fish/game/wild-plant bases. Key crops domesticated
~3000–3300 B.C. but widespread sedentary villages after ~2000 B.C.; by 1500 B.C. sedentary
farming and maize staple widespread (Feinman; Reed; Gonlin & Evans).

**Regional schemes:** Formative/Classic/Postclassic applied with local absolute dates (southern
Maya Classic A.D. 250–900; Monte Albán Period I from ~500 B.C.; Michoacán Formative villages
by Early Formative; Southwest ceramic sedentism not before A.D. 200–300)—period labels are
conventional and do not always match settlement shifts. Civic-ceremonial monumental planning
probably dates from the **Middle Formative**, setting patterns for urban planning carried into
Classic regional styles (Lorraine Williams).

**Protoclassic marginalized:** traits once thought Classic-first appear within Late Preclassic;
population-replacement/elite-invasion theories lack support. Yet Early Classic c. A.D. 250 is
still justified by the transition to historicity on hundreds of monumental inscriptions
(Hammond 2000). Early Classic ceramic chronology problem: Uaxactun Tzakol triad wrongly assumed
lowland-wide—absence of Tzakol misread as depopulation (Hammond 2000).

### Postclassic (Adams; Charlton)

Adams: Early Postclassic A.D. 650/900–1250—collapse/transformation of Classic civilizations;
hybrid cultures; tribute-seeking predatory military states. Late Postclassic A.D. 1250–1519—
climax of reformulated regional cultures; Aztec, Tarascan, Maya, Zapotec, Mixtec relatively
well known from documents and eyewitness accounts.

Charlton (CMSR after Tula): four-period model A.D. 1150/1200–1521—Periods 1–3 (to 1428)
migrations and city-state consolidation; Period 4 (1428–1519) Triple Alliance expansion. Fall
of Tula was a **process more than an event**; historical and archaeological chronologies are
alternative portrayals of the past (Charlton 2000). See [[altepetl]].

## Limitations

- Dates for "Classic" and "collapse" differ sharply between central Mexico (Teotihuacan
  ends ~550–650 CE) and the Maya lowlands (monument collapse ~800–900 CE).
- Radiocarbon conventions (calibrated vs uncalibrated) vary by author; Zeitlin & Zeitlin
  (Ch. 2) mark uncalibrated dates with lowercase b.c./b.p.
- "Classic" once implied a peak aesthetic/political stage; modern use is mainly chronological.
- Wiki framework periods (Classical Antiquity, Late Antiquity, etc.) are Old-World anchors —
  map Mesoamerican stages onto them carefully and prefer indigenous stage names in body text.
- Ceramic–political correlations (e.g. Aztec I/II/III vs documentary periods) are contested
  and radiocarbon-sensitive (Charlton 2000).
