The Arabic philosophical psychology of soul (*al-nafs*) and intellect (*al-aql*) is the tradition of systematic reflection on the nature, powers, and operations of the human soul developed by the *falasifa* (Hellenizing Islamic philosophers) from the 9th through the 13th centuries. Rooted in Aristotle's *De Anima* and supplemented by Neoplatonist sources, the tradition produced some of its most original results in the theory of the internal senses, the analysis of intentionality, and above all in competing accounts of the intellect's relation to a single, separate Agent Intellect. This page covers the broad psychological tradition; the specific controversy over whether there is one or many material intellects is covered separately in [[unity-of-intellect]].

## Nature of the Soul

All Arabic philosophers — in contrast to the Islamic theologians (*mutakallimun*) — accepted some conception of the soul derived from Greek tradition. The mutakallimun, committed to an atomistic metaphysics in which all created beings are aggregates of atoms and accidents held together by divine power, had no room for an immaterial soul. The philosophers rejected this bundle theory of personal identity vigorously, partly because it threatened the possibility of genuine cognition and moral responsibility.

For most *falasifa*, Aristotle's definition held: the soul is the first "form" or "actuality" of a body potentially alive (*De Anima* II.1). Soul is inseparable from body as its organizing principle, and follows Aristotle's three-tier division into nutritive (common to plants, animals, humans), sensitive (animals and humans), and intellective (humans alone).

**Al-Razi's Platonism.** Abu Bakr al-Razi (d. ca. 925) was an exception, rejecting Aristotle in favor of Plato's *Timaeus*. He accepted the tripartite soul (desiderative, spirited, rational), believed in transmigration, and posited a World Soul from which all animal souls have fallen. His metaphysics of the five eternal beings (God, Soul, time, space, matter) gives soul a co-eternal status alien to most Islamic philosophical psychology.

**Avicenna's Dualism.** [[ibn-sina]] (Avicenna, d. 1037) accepted the Aristotelian framework but introduced a form of soul-body dualism foreign to Aristotle. For Avicenna, the individual human soul is not merely the body's organizing form but a subsistent being in its own right — a complete substance independent of any relation to the body. The soul requires the body for its initial creation and individuation (since matter individuates members of a species), but not for its continued existence. After the death of the body, the individual soul retains its individuality in virtue of its own intrinsic substantiality and the accumulation of uniquely individual embodied experiences. Avicenna is thus the only *faylasuf* who upholds the personal immortality of the individual human soul.

## The Flying Man Argument

Avicenna's most celebrated psychological argument is the "flying man" thought experiment (*al-rajul al-ta'ir*), a precursor of Descartes' cogito. The reader is asked to imagine herself newly created as a mature adult but suspended in a void, with all sensory input — both from external objects and from her own body — excluded. Even under these conditions, Avicenna argues, she would undoubtedly affirm her own existence. But that affirmation cannot depend on bodily experience, which has been hypothetically eliminated. Therefore self-awareness is entirely non-sensory, demonstrating that the soul is a self-subsistent reality independent of the body. The argument also grounds Avicenna's claim that the intellect can attain genuine philosophical self-knowledge that is irreducible to sensation.

## Intentionality and Cognition

A general theory of cognition applicable to both sense and intellect was developed from Aristotle's claim that cognition involves the reception of the form of a perceived object without its matter. Arabic philosophers interpreted this as a theory of intentionality: every cognitive faculty grasps its object by receiving an "intention" (*ma'na*, literally "meaning" or "idea") — a form insofar as it is an object for a cognitive faculty. There are sensible intentions (color, sound), imagined intentions (mental images), estimative intentions (non-sensible properties conveyed through sense), and intelligible intentions (universal concepts).

For Avicenna, intentionality is closely connected to his metaphysical distinction between essence and existence: the intention is the mental existence (*al-wujud al-dhihni*) of the form or quiddity as received in the soul of the perceiver. Averroes rejected the metaphysical basis for this while upholding the general thesis that cognized forms have two "subjects": a *subject of truth* (the extramental thing itself) and a *subject of existence* (the faculty in which the form exists as an intention).

All cognition, on this account, is a hierarchy of abstraction from senses (grasping particulars through material organs) to intellect (grasping universals without material organs). Avicenna identified four grades: sensation, internal senses at two levels, and intellection.

## The Internal Senses

The doctrine of the internal senses (*al-hawass al-batina*) is one of the most original and influential contributions of Arabic philosophy to the psychology of cognition. It attempts to systematize Aristotle's account of pre-intellectual cognitive capacities — common sense, imagination, memory — supplemented by Galenic physiology (localization in the brain's ventricles).

**Al-Farabi's sparse scheme.** [[al-farabi]] posits a common sense (collating information from the five external senses) and an imagination with two initial functions — retaining sensible impressions when the external object is absent, and composing or dividing retained impressions. He later adds a third function of "imitation" (*muhakat*): the imagination can depict an object through an image other than its own sensible appearance. This becomes foundational for al-Farabi's theory of the prophet: the prophet's powerful imagination receives an "overflow" of intelligibles from the Agent Intellect and represents them symbolically to ordinary people as revelation.

**Avicenna's five-faculty system.** Avicenna posits five internal faculties, each assigned a specific location in the brain's ventricles, governed by two principles: (1) reception and retention must be functions of distinct faculties (like water, what receives an impression easily does not retain it well); and (2) diversity of objects diversifies faculties.

The five faculties are:
1. **Common sense** (*hiss mushtarak*): receives and distinguishes sensible forms from the external senses.
2. **Retentive imagination** (*al-khayal / al-musawwira*): stores sensible forms after the external object is absent.
3. **Estimation** (*wahm*): grasps non-sensible "intentions" accompanying sensible forms — e.g., the sheep's instinctive perception of *hostility* in the wolf. This is Avicenna's most innovative and controversial faculty.
4. **Memorative faculty**: retains estimative intentions.
5. **Compositive imagination** (*al-mutakhayyila*): actively manipulates images and intentions; in humans, when under rational direction, becomes the **cogitative faculty** (*al-mufakkira*) responsible for much of what we ordinarily call "thinking."

**Averroes' revision.** [[ibn-rushd]] eliminates the estimative faculty in animals entirely, arguing that sensation and imagination already perceive pleasant and painful qualities without a separate estimation faculty. He reduces the internal senses to four: common sense, imagination, cogitation (reserved for humans), and memory. For Averroes, "intentions" are restricted to the cogitative faculty's grasp of the individual as such — its function is explaining incidental perception (perceiving the white thing as *Socrates*), not the broad range of affective properties Avicenna assigned to estimation.

## Theories of the Intellect

The framework for all Arabic theories of the intellect was Aristotle's distinction in *De Anima* III between the agent and potential intellects, supplemented by Greek commentators who multiplied the intellect's stages. The standard Arabic scheme, from al-Kindi through Averroes, identifies four types:

1. **Agent Intellect** (*al-aql al-fa'al*): A separate, immaterial substance — not a faculty in each individual soul, but a cosmological entity (the last of the emanated Intelligences in al-Farabi's system). Its function is to actualize the potential intellect by rendering objects intelligible, functioning as an efficient cause of human understanding.

2. **Potential (Material) Intellect** (*al-aql al-hayulani*): An innate capacity within the human soul for receiving intelligibles, following Aristotle's *De Anima* III.4. For most philosophers, this is a faculty of the individual soul. Averroes uniquely came to believe that the material intellect, like the Agent Intellect, must be a single separate substance shared by all humans — the position at the center of the [[unity-of-intellect]] controversy.

3. **Habitual (Speculative) Intellect** (*al-aql bi-al-malaka*): The potential intellect once it has acquired some intelligibles and developed a disposition for thinking at will.

4. **Acquired Intellect** (*al-aql al-mustafad*): The habitual intellect fully actualized, having acquired all possible intelligibles. At this stage the intellect becomes akin to the separate intelligences and can know the Agent Intellect itself — the highest human achievement, constituting intellectual felicity.

**Al-Farabi** held that the potential intellect is a faculty of the individual soul; its immortality depends on the degree to which it actualizes itself by acquiring immaterial intelligibles. **Avicenna** agreed that each human has an individual potential intellect (he argued for individual soul immortality), but held that its operation always requires "illumination" from the Agent Intellect at the moment of actual understanding, not just initially. **Averroes** in his mature Long Commentary argued that the material intellect must be a single numerically united substance shared across all humans, because otherwise individual intellects would corrupt with the body and universal knowledge would be impossible — this is the unity-of-intellect doctrine.

## The Prophet's Psychology

The prophet's psychological constitution is a central topic in Arabic philosophical psychology, connecting the theory of imagination to political philosophy. For al-Farabi, the prophet uniquely combines theoretical intellectual perfection (acquired through philosophy) with a perfected imaginative faculty that can receive intelligibles and represent them symbolically. Avicenna's account is more metaphysically specific: the prophet's "intuition" (*hads*) — the capacity to move through the middle terms of a syllogism instantly and without instruction — is extraordinarily powerful, enabling direct contact with the Agent Intellect that others can only approach after long effort.

## Historiography

Deborah Black's chapter in Adamson and Taylor's *Cambridge Companion to Arabic Philosophy* (2005) provides the systematic account. Key primary texts include: al-Farabi's *Treatise on the Intellect* (partial translation in Hyman and Walsh); Avicenna's *De Anima* (the psychological book of the *Shifa'*), available in partial English translation; and Averroes' Long Commentary on *De Anima* (available in Latin; partial English translation in Hyman and Walsh). The scholarship on Avicenna's psychology has advanced significantly since 1980, with key contributions from Goichon, Druart, Black, Hasse, and Gutas. The flying man argument has attracted particular attention in contemporary philosophy of mind as a precursor of Cartesian-style introspection arguments.
