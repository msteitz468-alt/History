# Proprietorial Warfare

Analytical framework developed by John France to characterize the dominant mode of warfare in Western Europe 1000–1300. The term is etic (an analytical construct applied by the modern historian); contemporaries spoke of "private war", "feud", "guerre", or simply the pursuit of rights and claims by force, without a single overarching label distinguishing proprietorial considerations from other motives.

## Definition and Core Thesis

Warfare in this period was shaped by four interlocking factors, which together produced a distinctive "common stamp" on armies and the conduct of war across 1000–1300, different from what had gone before and from what came after:

> "In this period, warfare was shaped by four main factors:
> a. the dominance of land as a form of wealth;
> b. the limited competence of government;
> c. the state of technology which, broadly, favoured defence over attack;
> d. the geography and climate of the West." (Ch. 1)

Landownership was "the distinguishing characteristic of the ruling elites of western Europe." Kings claimed overarching sovereignty but "in practice they were not sharply distinguished from other landowners, who shared their authority rather than acted as its agents." Estates were "gathered into families by random means – the accidents of birth, death, marriage and political patronage" and were typically "packets of land, often very small individually, scattered amongst the similar holdings of others." In the absence or relative weakness of superior authority, "war was essential to defending and expanding lands and rights because... it was, if not the only, then at least the ultimate, means of settling disputes."

> "Warfare in this period was, therefore, nearly always proprietorial, or at the very least influenced by proprietorial considerations. This is not to say that warfare was always and only about landed possession; but merely that it was a powerful influence that was always taken into account." (Ch. 1)

The outstanding characteristics of medieval warfare — "the castle and the armoured knight" — "arose from the dominance of proprietorial power." Because "land transport was poor and expensive, it was cheaper for landowners to travel around their estates eating up the renders of scattered lands. This meant that they needed collecting points with comfortable accommodation, which were strong enough to resist a degree of attack – in short, castles." The armoured mounted knight was likewise "a response to the needs of proprietors."

## Major Characteristics

**Castles as proprietorial instruments.** Castles functioned first as homes, estate centres, and symbols of control over dispersed holdings. Their siting followed patterns of landownership rather than purely abstract "strategic" considerations. They enabled both defence and offensive action: garrisons could raid to extort renders, deny resources to rivals, or enforce claims. The multiplication of strongholds intensified the fundamental nature of proprietorial war by providing secure bases from which to ravage.

**Small, ad hoc armies and the absence of infrastructure.** Even kings "could not afford to maintain standing armies or large arsenals." "War is an expensive business." Soldiers equipped themselves "from the proceeds of their landed holdings – often provided for them by the lord for this very purpose – and from the fruits of pillage." As liquidity increased in the twelfth century the "cash nexus" grew important, but armies "remained ad hoc bodies, kept together only for as long as necessity demanded." Professional soldiers were dismissed as soon as possible "because of the enormous drain on resources." "The gathering of anything that we might call a large army was a relatively rare act."

**Ravaging, Vegetian warfare, and the limits of battle.** Because armies had to live off the land and because plunder formed part of soldiers' pay, "ravaging was an integral part of the business of managing an army." This served a military purpose: "destroying the countryside of your enemy would impoverish him, shake the loyalty of his vassals, who it was his duty to protect, and make him and them question the point of fighting on if a settlement was possible." Commanders admired Vegetius:

> "A great strategy is to press the enemy more with famine than with the sword." (Ch. 1, quoting Vegetius)

Modern historians therefore emphasise "what might be called 'Vegetian warfare', centred on raids and destruction, rather than battle or even siege." Battle was not the normal objective:

> "It is preferable to subdue an enemy by famine, raids and terror, than in battle where fortune tends to have more influence than bravery." (Ch. 1, quoting Vegetius)

Succession disputes or extreme threats could produce battle (e.g., references to Brissac 1068, Cassel 1071, Tinchebrai 1106, Lincoln 1141), but commanders were generally cautious; "fortune tends to have more influence than bravery" (Vegetius, cited Ch. 1). Major sieges were likewise not lightly undertaken.

**Mouvances and the confusion of sovereignty and ownership.** Power rested on land. "Every kingdom was a lordship and every king had the preoccupations of a landowner: every lordship resembled a kingdom..." Circles of influence "based on landownership which rarely coincided with the geographical area of settlement of any nation" were termed *mouvances*. These overlapped heavily, creating scope for vassals to change sides and for constant petty war. "Private war" was not a contradiction but "simply an extension of legitimate self-interest which was not wholly differentiated from sovereign power."

**Social order, chivalry, and restraints.** The apparatus of war "was also intended to enforce the social order. The castle and the armed band were not merely a defence against other mouvances but also the means by which the lord exploited the peasantry." Kings and magnates "claimed a monopoly of war and in time generated a military ethos, chivalry, which glorified their role." Brutality was mitigated among elites by kinship, neighbourly relations, the economic incentive of ransom, and the fact that "nobody wanted to rule over a desert." Yet "the economic motivation for such clemency is indicated by the brute fact that infantry on the losing side in a conflict were generally massacred." The ethics of war "arose from and were limited by the central concerns of a landowning group – they were an aspect of proprietorial warfare." (Ch. 1)

**Continuity and subtle change.** "Although there was much open land, hedges, thickets and woodland were common and could shelter infantry, especially archers." "Even in armies of modest size, therefore, the knight was never alone." Western Europeans proved "singularly reluctant to change their style of warfare when they came into contact with other civilizations." Improvements in fortifications and siege machinery in the later period often arose from "civilian innovation and arose from the hiring of specialists from outside the military sphere." There was never "a great leap forward" in the killing-ground, which remained "very largely limited by the range of the bow and the length of sword or spear."

## Relation to Cavalry, Authority and Technology (from Ch. 2–5)

The proprietorial structure directly shaped military participation and equipment. Knights (*milites*) were the armed expression of the landed elite; their equipment reflected wealth and status. "All who came to war brought their own equipment: the rich, the proprietors and their immediate supporters, could afford the best weapons and defensive equipment." The evolution of mail hauberks, great helms, horse armour ("great horse"), and heavier protection was driven by the ability of proprietors to invest in their own and their retainers' protection. By the late twelfth century a clearer social distinction emerged between landed knights (forming a gentry/noble layer) and sergeants, even when the latter were mounted and sometimes comparably equipped.

Fragmented authority flowed from the same root: "kings claimed a sovereignty, but in practice they had, to a greater or lesser degree, to share power with others whose allegiances were uncertain..." Kings were "essentially aristocratic landowners writ large, masters of vast and often uncertainly defined mouvances." This produced both limited wars over adjustments (common in Angevin–Capetian conflicts under Henry II and Louis VII) and savage, sustained conflict when survival of a mouvance or social order was perceived to be at stake (e.g., Frederick I's wars in Lombardy against cities).

Technological development was constrained because "Without an infrastructure of war there could be no systematic development." "The only repository of military memory, beyond that of individuals, lay in the households of major rulers, but these were very few and far between." Provision of weapons was "the obligation of the individual"; economic expansion therefore "was diverted into the weapons and protection of the wealthy." The most visible technical progress occurred in fortifications and siegecraft, often through civilian specialists.

## Historiography

Older historiography, influenced by Clausewitz and popularised through Delbrück and Oman, treated war as battle and emphasised decisive engagement. "To an older generation of historians, war was battle." Recent scholarship, re-examining sources, has stressed the infrequency of battle, the strength of fortifications, the small size of armies, and the centrality of the raid:

> "the staple of war was the raid – a cheap and expedient way of undermining the economy and willpower of your enemy." (Ch. 1)

France's proprietorial model supplies the structural explanation for this pattern: the same landholding realities that made large standing forces impossible and ravaging economically rational also explain elite conservatism and the slow pace of change.

## Limitations and Scope

The framework is explicitly focused on Western Europe (with comparative remarks on the Latin East and Italy). It does not claim to be a universal model. Ideological factors (especially crusading), personality, and contingency could override or modulate proprietorial logic in particular campaigns. The model privileges social, economic, and institutional causation over purely technological or "great man" explanations, while acknowledging that "when great issues were at stake horrific behaviour was not uncommon." It notes that the pattern involved "a tremendous commitment of resources" and could have "terrible consequences" even if the common experience was episodic ravaging rather than annihilation.

**Emic/etic note**: No contemporary source uses the modern analytical term "proprietorial warfare." The concept is an etic tool for explaining why certain institutions (castle, knight), practices (ravaging, ransom among elites), and limits on scale persisted. Contemporaries experienced these as the normal conduct of claims, feuds, and lordship.

## Evidence from Siege, Armies and Command (Ch. 9–11)

Later chapters supply concrete illustration of the proprietorial constraints. Siege was "a test of political will and resources on both sides"; many city sieges failed because "a military solution in the Lombard plain was impossible, for the cities were too numerous and their political connections were too ramified" (Barbarossa at Milan). "In the end it was the garrison, not the walls, that mattered." No engine "actually altered the balance of advantage between attack and defence"; the decisive requirement was "to isolate it with overwhelming numbers" plus "good leadership, skill, determination, organization and plentiful supplies – difficult enough to achieve when armies were transient." Engineers existed but "were rewarded much more modestly than the traditional military caste." (Ch. 9)

Armies "remained ad hoc bodies... It was always difficult to sustain them in the field and even the greatest of forces could melt away." "the persistent association of rank, political power and military authority meant that there could be no clear chain of command." "armies changed only slowly." Sizes fluctuated wildly even under the same leader; core was always the commander's own household troops. (Ch. 10)

Command required front-rank bravery and a personal troop core because "persuasion, not command, was the only way to get things done." Even kings consulted under fire. Professionals (e.g. Guérin at Bouvines) played subordinate roles. "The medieval commander was first and foremost a man of rank." "The commander’s essential skill was persuasion." Conquest required conciliation because "sustaining armies was difficult." (Ch. 11)

These details confirm that the "common stamp" of proprietorial warfare persisted through the period; technical and organisational increments did not produce structural transformation.

## Campaign Style, Battle Rarity, and Tactical Limits (Ch. 12–13)

The proprietorial framework produced a characteristic campaign style in which battle was exceptional and not the normal object of operations.

> "Across the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, war did not change radically. Its staple was destructive raiding punctuated with sieges and, more occasionally, by battle." (Ch. 12)

Commanders preferred to avoid pitched battle because "running away was fairly easy" and "it needed both sides to resolve upon battle." Battles most often arose incidentally from sieges or raiding (e.g., Hattin provoked by siege; Cortenova 1237 as classic ambush after Frederick II's ravaging of Mantua/Brescia lands and castle captures like Montichiari). "A very notable battle that arose from the circumstances of raiding and siege was Frederick II’s victory at Cortenova..." The Milanese relief army, disorganized after a rapid winter march and river crossings, was struck while its vanguard was separated from the carroccio and baggage. (Ch. 12)

The decisive factor remained command cohesion rather than technology or formation alone:

> "The decisive factor in the battles that have been analysed was the ability of the commander and the control that he exerted. This continued to be the case throughout the thirteenth century, although there are indications of a new coherence in both infantry and cavalry, but these appear unevenly and there is little sense of conscious development." (Ch. 12)

Cavalry held the initiative through mobility and training, but mass shock charges were rare and difficult to execute because of the "individualistic ethos" of knights; control was achieved through small conroi units, banners, and personal leadership. The Templar Rule (13th c.) — the closest to a contemporary manual — prescribed formation in small units around banners with reserves (squires with spare mounts) for repeated charges or mêlée support, not a single all-out shock. At Bouvines this repeated-unit technique was employed successfully under Guérin; at Muret Simon de Montfort's cohesive small force exploited enemy disunity. (Ch. 12–13)

Infantry remained essential in solid mass ("Thick and tight!") for resisting cavalry and providing army solidity, as at Worringen, Bouvines, Cortenova (carroccio stand), and earlier battles (Hastings, Tinchebrai, Brémule, Northallerton, Legnano). City communal infantry showed specialization (crossbowmen, pikemen, shield-bearers in homogeneous sections); Flemish forces used guild-based differentiation. (Ch. 12)

There was no linear tactical evolution. The 13th century saw greater prominence for disciplined cavalry (Muret, Bouvines) and hints of professionalism, yet "the thirteenth century was truly the age of cavalry" only in the sense that cavalry actions "dictated events" — not uncontested supremacy. Infantry was "now better armed than ever before." Victories such as Courtrai (1302) showed dense foot could defeat knights on suitable ground. "All medieval armies were the products of particular conditions... each victory was sui generis... no mechanism to bring ideas together in a systematic way." Edward I's balanced methods died with him (Bannockburn). "Victory in battle depended... on the personality of the commander and his ability to adapt forces... without a staff, without a logistics service and, above all, without an institutional memory." (Ch. 13)

This confirms and extends the Ch. 1 thesis: proprietorial social structure (ad hoc retinues, limited infrastructure, elite ethos) produced conservative, personality-dependent, raid/siege-dominant war with battle as rare, high-risk decision point rather than routine climax.

## Evidence from Peripheries, Ideology, and Synthesis (Ch. 14–16)

France's later chapters supply further grounding for the proprietorial model while showing how ideology and environmental pressures modulated (but did not overturn) it.

**Baltic frontier (Ch. 14: "Europe, Ideology and the Outsider")**: Pure proprietorial expansion (Saxon landowners on the Elbe, tribute-taking) often accommodated pagan neighbours and was limited by local dynastic feuds and reluctance to impose conformity. The crusading turn (1147 onward) supplied "an iron will to expand" through the Church's appeal and the creation of permanent military cores (Sword-Brothers, later Teutonic Order). "Fundamental to this victory was the will of the Church... without the appeal of the crusade few would have ventured to the harsh and dangerous north: only very slowly did individual knights appear... They could certainly never have formed themselves into corporate bodies — the religious orders — without that appeal." Castles + crossbows + cogs (large merchant ships) gave technical edge, but "without the industrial base which generated such weapons... there were grave limits." The result was "as near to total war as medieval men could get — a war of atrocity and counter-atrocity" precisely because it pitted "the other" (pagans) against a religiously sanctioned cause, in contrast to the moderation usual among western elites. Proprietorial ambitions and ideology were initially "in opposition," but the Church supplied the missing "common will" and "vested interest in conquest."

**Crusade in the Middle East (Ch. 15)**: The crusade was presented in terms that appealed directly to proprietorial instincts: recovery of "God's land," Jerusalem as dispossessed lord's fief (Innocent III), "The points at issue are Jerusalem, the Cross and the Land" (Richard to Saladin). Yet the Latin states remained chronically short of "dependable people" and "the military religious orders compensated" by providing a "regular force" with western assets. "Everything depended on good leadership and sound tactics." The Franks adapted western close-order methods to horse-archer conditions ("tight formation," infantry-cavalry coordination at Arsuf) but "stuck to tried and tested methods" and "the European style of war remained part of the identity of the Franks in the East, and was adapted rather than transformed" (Ch. 16). Manpower limits made the field army and cities decisive; after Hattin "the ruling class had been wiped out" and the kingdom collapsed rapidly. Ransom conventions largely held for elites on both sides.

**Perspectives synthesis (Ch. 16)**: The core thesis is restated and extended: "War was the instrument of landed proprietors because land was the primary source of wealth... It was the needs of a landowning elite which gave rise to the characteristic institutions of medieval warfare, the castle and the knight." Armies remained "collections of personal retinues"; "the social exclusivity of the aristocracy dominated war." "This style of warfare was adaptable, precisely because it was so centred on close-quarter fighting... Even so, the European style of war remained part of the identity... and was adapted rather than transformed." "There was no linear development but, rather, a series of impulses produced changes that were not always sustained, and in the end much depended on powerful personalities." The only true rival (Mongols) exposed the limits; the West had "no real response," while Egypt developed the Mamluk regular army in response. "the only apparent pattern is diversity."

These chapters confirm that even when ideology or distance created exceptional conditions, the underlying proprietorial logic (retinue structure, elite control of command, adaptation without institutional revolution, ransom among peers) persisted. Brutality toward "the other" or civilians in existential theatres (Baltic, Holy Land) was the exception that proved the rule of moderation among western landholding elites.

## The Eighteenth-Century Analogue (Whitman)

`analogous_to:` the monarchical property-war of the eighteenth century as analyzed
by Whitman ([[whitman-verdict-of-battle-2012|The Verdict of Battle]], 2012). The
concepts are distinct — France's is medieval and includes private/feudal war,
Whitman's era is defined precisely by the *monopolization* of war by sovereigns —
but the underlying logic is the same: war as the enforcement of property and
inheritance claims by a landed (now dynastic) proprietor class. Whitman: "War was
at base a form of the pursuit of profit, a way of sorting out conflicting property
claims" — as late as the 1804 French Civil Code, booty remained among the "natural
modes of acquiring property." The Abbé de Fleury (1715) made the litigation frame
explicit: "The causes of war are the same as those for which private persons file
lawsuits... [war is] private law applied to the interest of sovereigns." Dynastic
succession wars (Spanish, Austrian successions) were argued as inheritance cases —
sovereigns as claimants "In re Austrian Succession." See [[jus-victoriae]] and
[[verdict-of-battle]] for the settlement machinery, and
[[war-of-the-austrian-succession-1740]] for the paradigm case.
