# Pact of ʿUmar (al-Shurūṭ al-ʿUmariyya)

The **Pact of ʿUmar** is the foundational document of the [[dhimmi|dhimma]] — "a kind
of bilateral contract in which the non-Muslims agree to a host of discriminatory
regulations in return for protection" ([[cohen-under-crescent-cross-1994|Cohen]]). Cast
as a letter of submission from conquered Christians to Caliph ʿUmar, who "confirms" its
terms, it enumerates the disabilities and obligations of [[dhimmi|dhimmīs]] in the
Islamic polity. Though addressed to Christians (the majority population at the conquest,
especially in former Byzantine lands), its stipulations "extended in practice to the
Jews as well."

## Attribution and Dating (Contested)

The Pact is traditionally ascribed to the caliph **ʿUmar I** (ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb,
r. 634–644), but **no text can be dated earlier than the tenth or eleventh century**.
Cohen presents it as a composite crystallizing over time from:

- pre-Islamic tribal custom and the Prophet's own precedents in Arabia;
- the circumstances and treaties of the great conquests;
- Byzantine (eastern Roman) Christian-Roman Jewry law absorbed as the conquests took
  over much of the eastern Roman Empire.

The scholarly dispute is a live one: **A. S. Tritton** doubted the ʿUmar I attribution;
**Albrecht Noth** reopened the question, arguing that many *shurūṭ* derive from actual
conquest-era arrangements and that several stipulations originally protected the
**fragile identity of the conquering Arab minority** amid a vast non-Muslim majority
rather than being designed to demean. On this reading the *zunnār* (girdle) and
distinctive dress were pre-existing markers (documented in the sixth-century *Chronicle
of Seert* and John of Ephesus) that conquerors required subject populations to *keep*
so they would remain distinguishable.

## Core Stipulations

As catalogued by Cohen (Ch. 4):

- **Houses of worship:** no new construction; no repair of ruined buildings — but
  enforcement was inconsistent for centuries and varied by legal school and a town's
  conquest status (treaty vs. force). First clear prohibition possibly under ʿUmar II
  (r. 717–720).
- **Public display of religion:** no crosses or scriptures shown in streets/markets, no
  loud clappers, chanting, or funeral/festival processions. Cohen notes this targeted
  *Christian* outdoor ritual far more than largely-indoor Jewish practice, and was
  motivated by **hierarchy/humility, not blasphemy** (contrast the anti-blasphemy logic
  of Christian Eastertide restrictions).
- **Symbols of subordination:** rising when Muslims sit; no riding saddled mounts; no
  bearing arms; houses built no higher than Muslims'; no honorific *kunyas* (Abū-names);
  no Arabic seal inscriptions; **distinctive dress** (the *ghiyār* / *zunnār*).
- **Religion and conversion:** no proselytizing; no obstruction of a co-religionist who
  wishes to convert to Islam.

The first extant edict specifying dhimmī dress in detail is that of the Abbasid caliph
**al-Mutawakkil, 850 CE**. Geniza evidence shows the dress rules were sporadically
enforced at best — little visible distinction between Jews and Muslims in
Fatimid/early-Ayyubid times.

## Enforcement, Amendment, and Comparison

Cohen's structural point: unlike the charters of Latin Christendom (which each new ruler
and pope had to renew — a recurring source of insecurity), the Pact was treated by
jurists such as **Ibn Taymiyya** as **permanent**, not requiring re-promulgation and not
subject to unilateral relaxation. This lent dhimmī status "greater predictability" and "a
greater feeling of security." When Muslim clerics pressed rulers on the dhimma, the aim
was usually to *enforce* existing (often neglected) restrictions, not invent new ones —
and Malikite doctrine discouraged collective punishment of a whole community for one
member's violation, making community-wide mob attacks "extremely rare" (the 1066 Granada
pogrom being a cited exception). The most notorious *amendment* was the Fatimid caliph
[[al-hakim|al-Ḥakim]]'s humiliating requirement (during his persecution) that Jews wear
a wooden calf and Christians a cross in the public baths — recognized by Muslim
chroniclers themselves as exceeding the original ʿUmariyyan terms.

Cohen identifies the Pact's dress clause as the likely **source of the Fourth Lateran
Council's (1215) distinctive-dress requirement** for Jews — but with an inverted
purpose: in Christendom the badge served *segregation and exclusion* (rooted in fear of
sexual/spiritual pollution), whereas the Islamic marker preserved a settled *hierarchy*
of coexistence. See [[dhimmi]] and [[managing-religious-pluralism-compared]].

## Historiography

The authenticity/dating question (Tritton vs. Noth) is the central scholarly debate and
is why `contested: yes`. The document's late attestation means it functions less as a
seventh-century decree than as a *retrospective idealization* of dhimma norms by later
jurists — most systematically the fourteenth-century Ibn Qayyim al-Jawziyya's *Aḥkām
ahl al-dhimma*, the first comprehensive Islamic treatise on dhimmī law.
