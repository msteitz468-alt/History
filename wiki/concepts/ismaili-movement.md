# Ismaili Movement

Branch of Shi'i Islam that recognizes a line of Imams descending from Isma'il ibn Ja'far al-Sadiq (d. 760 CE). Developed distinctive esoteric (batini) interpretation of the Quran and Sharia, hierarchical da'wa (missionary) organization, and at times revolutionary political expression (e.g., Fatimid Caliphate, Nizari Assassins).

## Definition and Scope

Ismailis believe the Imamate continues in a living or hidden Imam who provides authoritative esoteric guidance. Key features:
- Ta'wil (allegorical/esoteric exegesis) alongside zahir (literal).
- Cyclical view of history with speaking and silent Imams/prophets.
- Emphasis on reason, philosophy (falsafa), and gnosis alongside revelation.
- Political: Fatimids established a caliphate in North Africa/Egypt (909-1171); Nizaris in Alamut period used targeted violence.

Distinguished from Twelver Shi'ism (which awaits the 12th Imam) and Sunni orthodoxy.

## Interaction and Key Processes

Closely tied to Abbasid/Seljuk Sunni revival (countered by Nizamiyyah madrasas and Ghazali's critiques). Al-Ghazali wrote against them. Influenced by and contributed to Islamic philosophy and Sufism. Ghulat (extremist) tendencies in early period.

## Historiography

Often viewed through hostile Sunni or Western "Assassin" legends (medieval European accounts exaggerated). Modern scholarship (from Hodgson, Daftary, etc.) emphasizes intellectual sophistication, pluralism in some periods, and role in transmission of Greek philosophy. Collection sources (al-Ghazali biographies, Islamic societies surveys like Lapidus) provide context on their challenge to Sunni political and intellectual hegemony in the 10th-12th centuries.
