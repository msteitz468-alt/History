## Definition and Scope

Big History is an approach to historical inquiry that traces the past from the
Big Bang (~13.8 billion years ago) to the present as a single coherent
narrative, integrating cosmology, geology, evolutionary biology, and human
history within one framework. It treats archaeology and written history as
complementary rather than as distinct disciplines, and extends the temporal
scope of "history" far beyond the appearance of writing or civilization.

The organizing framework is energy flows, increasing complexity, and the
emergence of new forms of collective learning. Christian identifies a series
of "thresholds" — moments of rapid increase in complexity — as the structural
backbone of the entire narrative. The appearance of human language is one such
threshold; the Neolithic Revolution another; industrialization a third.

The *Cambridge World History* adopts a modified version of this approach. It
does not go back to the Big Bang, but it does treat all of human history —
not just the post-writing record — as its subject, explicitly extending the
scope of historical inquiry to cover the ~95 percent of human history that
occurred during the Paleolithic era.

## Emic vs. Etic

Big History is purely etic — no historical actor called their situation "big
history." It is a contemporary scholarly framework, applied retrospectively
across all periods.

## Methodological Implications for This Wiki

The CWH's adoption of a big-history scope justifies the wiki's temporal
framework, which begins with Deep Prehistory (before 3.3 million BP) rather
than with the appearance of writing (~3,300 BCE). It also explains the
collection's inclusion of archaeological, genetic, and linguistic sources
alongside textual ones as equally valid historical evidence.

The framework's emphasis on collective learning — the accumulation and sharing
of information across generations as the driver of human distinctiveness — is
a recurring analytical frame in period and process pages covering prehistory
and early history.

## Limitations and Critiques

- **Loss of human specificity**: Critics argue that framing human history
  within cosmic time dilutes the specifically human character of historical
  inquiry. At planetary scales, individual agency and cultural contingency
  disappear.
- **Teleological risk**: The "thresholds of complexity" narrative can imply
  an arc of progress, even when Christian explicitly disavows this.
- **Biological determinism**: The emphasis on energy capture and complexity
  risks reducing history to ecological or physical processes.
- **Eurocentrism unresolved**: Christian's framework avoids the traditional
  European periodization, but the structural features it privileges (energy
  capture, complexity, scale) reflect particular Western and scientific values.
- **Institutional marginality**: As of 2015, Big History remains largely
  outside mainstream historical scholarship, associated more with general
  education than specialist research.

## Relation to World History

Big History is distinct from, but related to, world history. World history
typically begins with the appearance of settled societies or writing and
focuses on connections, comparisons, and global processes within human
history. Big History extends the time frame and the disciplinary scope but
shares world history's anti-nationalist, anti-Eurocentric orientation.

## Applied: the Silk Roads as a "Goldilocks" Gear-Shift (Benjamin 2018)

[[benjamin-empires-ancient-eurasia-2018|Craig Benjamin]] — co-author with David Christian of
the standard big-history textbook — uses the framework to interpret the
[[silk-roads|First Silk Roads Era]]. He reads the Silk Roads as a revolutionary
**"goldilocks moment"** that magnified **collective learning** (humanity's unique capacity
to store and exchange information), placing the network among the great gear-shifts —
alongside the appearance of agriculture and of cities — that "helped facilitate a gear shift
that led eventually toward modernity." Echoing William McNeill's claim that intercultural
contact was "the main drive wheel of history," Benjamin treats the era's biological and
geological context (the [[eurasian-steppe]], horse evolution and domestication, monsoon
winds) as integral to the historical analysis — a hallmark big-history move. He pairs it
with [[world-systems-theory]], the [[human-web]], and ancient-[[proto-globalization|
globalization]] theory.
