## Definition

*Todesraum* ("death space") is an analytical category describing the qualitative transformation in Germans' experience of, relationship to, and cultural processing of mass death between 1914 and 1945. The concept encompasses not only the scale of killing (Germany lost approximately 2 million soldiers in World War I and a far greater number in World War II) but the cultural, psychological, and political work done to make mass death meaningful, acceptable, and eventually — in the Nazi period — active instruments of social policy and genocide.

Thomas Kühne's chapter "Todesraum: War, Peace, and the Experience of Mass Death, 1914–1945" in the Oxford Handbook of Modern German History (2011) is the primary source for this conceptualization. Kühne is Strassler Family Professor in the Study of Holocaust History at Clark University.

Source: [[smith-oxford-modern-german-history-2011]], Chapter 23.

## Historical Trajectory

### World War I: The First Todesraum (1914–1918)

The First World War produced a rupture in German (and European) experience of death. Rüdiger Overmans's figures show approximately 2 million German military dead. The soldier Willy Peter Reese's memoir *A Stranger to Myself* (Russia, 1941–1944) — one of the key texts Kühne uses — reaches back to a tradition of representing military experience that began in 1914.

Erich Maria Remarque's *All Quiet on the Western Front* (1928) crystallized a pacifist counter-memory: war as mechanized slaughter stripping death of heroic meaning. The novel's political impact — reviled by the Nazi right, celebrated by the left — shows the cultural battleground that mass death became in the Weimar period.

The wartime "spirit of 1914" generated what contemporaries experienced as a sacralization of national sacrifice. "Soldiers of the nationalist-socialist war and the 20th century" (Kühne's formulation) carried this inheritance forward.

### Weimar: Contested memory (1918–1933)

The war's outcome generated two competing cultures of memory around its dead:

**Nationalist-martial culture**: Veterans' organizations like the Stahlhelm and the kyffhäuser framed the war dead as bearers of a still-unrealized national mission. The concept that Germany had not been defeated in the field but "stabbed in the back" (Dolchstoßlegende) by internal enemies transformed military defeat into martyrdom requiring revenge. The German Fatherland Party (1917) and its successors claimed the dead demanded a "victorious peace" (*Siegfriede*) that the civilian government had betrayed.

**Democratic/pacifist culture**: The Reichsbanner (SPD-affiliated veterans' organization) and liberals invoked the dead as an argument against future war. The scale of mass death was a reason to pursue international reconciliation (as in Locarno, 1925, and League of Nations membership).

Carl Schmitt's *The Concept of the Political* (1932) — defining politics as the friend-enemy distinction — provided theoretical support for the view that political community requires the willingness to kill and die. This framework authorized the nationalist-militarist culture of death rather than the democratic-pacifist one.

### Nazi Todesraum (1933–1945)

Hitler's regime systematically mobilized the cultural resources of wartime sacrifice:

**Memorialization and mobilization**: Nazi propaganda staged elaborate rituals of commemoration that linked the World War I dead to the ongoing Nazi movement. Hitler's January 1939 Reichstag speech — in which he "prophesied" that another world war would mean "the annihilation of the Jewish race in Europe" — cast the anticipated conflict as the culmination of sacrificial national struggle.

**Normalization of mass killing**: The T-4 euthanasia program (begun 1939) killed approximately 70,000 German psychiatric patients before popular and clerical protest forced its official end. The program represented a transfer of wartime attitudes toward death — its instrumentalization in the service of national fitness — into peacetime domestic policy. It also provided organizational, personnel, and technical precursors (including gas chambers) for the Holocaust.

**Eastern Front**: The invasion of the Soviet Union (June 1941) expanded the todesraum in all dimensions. Kühne emphasizes the "Commissar Order" (mandating execution of captured Soviet political officers), the deliberate starvation of 3.3 million of 5.7 million Soviet POWs in German captivity (57 percent), and the Generalplan Ost vision of reducing the Slavic population by 30–50 million over two decades. Jochen Klepper's diary (September 1941) records both excitement and horror at what soldiers were witnessing.

**Soldiers' solidarity (*Kameradschaft*)**: Kühne's monograph *Kameradschaft* (2006) argues that the primary bonding mechanism of German soldiers in both wars — comradeship (*Kameradschaft*) — functioned not despite but partly through participation in atrocity. Solidarity was maintained across moral divides by shared complicity. This is not an argument about innate German "eliminationism" but about the social psychology of group violence.

**Scale of World War II German losses**: Rüdiger Overmans' demographic study counts German military losses in World War II at approximately 5.3 million. Civilian losses in bombing, expulsion, and famine added further millions. The war's final years (1943–1945) saw Germany itself enter the todesraum it had imposed on others.

## Key Analytical Dimensions

**Death as political resource**: The mass dead of World War I were politically mobilized to authorize both extreme nationalism (they demand revenge) and extreme war-readiness (their sacrifice must be redeemed by victory). Nazi ideology was unusual in making future death — deliberate mass killing of enemies defined in racial terms — an explicit program rather than an unfortunate necessity.

**Continuity and rupture**: The chapter sits in the debate about continuity between World War I and the Holocaust. Kühne's framework suggests the World War I experience of mass death — and especially the cultural work done to process it in Weimar — created conditions of possibility for the Holocaust, without arguing for simple causal determinism.

**Soldiers' moral experience**: Using diaries, memoirs, and letters, Kühne reconstructs how soldiers experienced and rationalized their participation in mass violence. Willy Peter Reese's memoir (cited by Kühne) records a soldier who finds himself becoming "a stranger to himself" — neither fully embracing nor fully resisting the violence that surrounded him. This moral ambiguity is central to Kühne's account.

## Contested Aspects

- Was the Nazi mass killing the outcome of a distinctly German "culture of death" rooted in World War I, or was it primarily driven by ideological and structural factors common to other fascist and colonial regimes?
- What was the relationship between "ordinary" soldiers' comradeship and participation in the Holocaust? Kühne differs from Daniel Goldhagen's thesis of "willing executioners" without denying German complicity.
- How should we weigh the consensual and coercive dimensions of German wartime society's relationship to mass killing?
