# The Fossil-Fuel Energy Transition

## Definition

The **fossil-fuel energy transition** is the epochal shift, beginning in a few small
regions of northwestern Europe by the 1750s, **from plant fuels to fossil fuels and
from animate prime movers (human and animal muscle) to machines powered by
combustion**. Vaclav Smil, in [[mcneill-cwh-v7-2015|CWH VII]], calls it the change
that "created the modern world"; J. R. McNeill ranks the adoption of fossil fuels
alongside the mastery of fire and the adoption of [[domestication|agriculture]] as
one of three thresholds that transformed the human condition — the one that "made us
modern." It is the energy basis of [[industrialization]], the
[[demographic-transition|population explosion]], and the
[[anthropocene|Anthropocene]].

## From organic flows to fossil stocks

For nearly all of human history, societies lived within a **solar energy regime** —
tapping the near-instant flow of recent photosynthesis (food, wood, charcoal, plus
modest wind and water power). Smil's image: living "on a steady income." The
transition meant beginning to draw down a **finite store of accumulated energy
capital** — coal, oil, and gas, the fossilized product of ~500 million years of
photosynthesis. By the 1890s the energy content of mined coal surpassed all the
phytomass fuel burned worldwide; by c. 1870 humans already burned more fossil energy
each year than all annual photosynthesis produced. Total human energy use multiplied
roughly **90–100-fold between 1750 and 2015**.

## Energy density and quality

Smil stresses that the transition was as much about **quality** as quantity. Air-dry
wood holds ~15 MJ/kg; charcoal and the best anthracite coal ~29–30 MJ/kg; refined
oil products ~42 MJ/kg — nearly three times wood. Higher energy density brought
decisive advantages in extraction, transport, storage, and flexibility: economies
running on liquid fuels are "far more efficient and flexible than those dependent on
coal or wood." His six "universal measures" for tracking the transition: energy
density, power density, the maximum power of prime movers, the efficiency of energy
conversions, per-capita useful-energy consumption, and the maximum energy of weapons.

## Coal → oil → the world

McNeill and Smil trace a sequence of "kings":

- **Coal** (king c. 1750–1900): the [[long-19th-century|nineteenth century]]'s fuel.
  Britain sat at the northwestern end of a "carboniferous crescent" from Silesia to
  the Scottish Lowlands. The local cheapness of coal — Wrigley's point that early,
  fuel-wasteful steam engines made sense only where coal was very cheap at the
  pithead — was, in Sugihara's words, "largely a matter of chance."
- **Oil** (king from c. 1960): twice coal's energy density, far cheaper to transport
  by pipeline and tanker, and uniquely able to power aircraft. The fuel of the
  [[great-acceleration|Great Acceleration]] and the [[cold-war|Cold War]] "age of
  oil."

## Uneven diffusion

For roughly a century after 1850 high fossil-energy use was confined to Europe,
North America, and (from the 1870s) Japan — "the single most important reason" for
those regions' global dominance (see [[great-divergence]]). Smil argues that in
"fundamental energetic terms the path of all late modernizers was preordained": each
had to follow the Euro-American pattern. Soviet Russia's transition began in the
1920s, China's in the 1950s, India's only in the 1990s; since 1965 most of the
growth in energy use has occurred outside the old industrial cores, above all in
East Asia. China overtook the United States as the world's largest energy user in
2010.

## Limitations and critiques

- Smil flags the transition's possibly **unrepeatable** character — and its
  open-ended environmental costs (see [[anthropocene]], [[great-acceleration]]).
- "Transition" language can imply an orderly, completed process; in reality fossil
  use is still *rising*, and renewables remain a small share.
- The framing can understate the role of institutions, wages, and politics relative
  to physical energy (cf. the [[great-divergence|Great Divergence]] debate).
