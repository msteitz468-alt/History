## Definition and Scope

Islamic eschatology (Arabic: *akhira*, "the last things") is the body of theological doctrine concerning death, the intermediary state, resurrection, judgement, paradise and hell, and the ultimate destiny of souls and the created order. In kalam classification it belongs to **sam'iyyat** — "matters heard", or received in faith from revelation — because unlike tawḥid (divine unity) or nubuwwa (prophecy), eschatological events lie outside the reach of rational proof. The theologian's task is defensive: to protect scriptural predictions from denial or allegorisation.

Alongside tawḥid and nubuwwa, the "ultimate return" (ma'ad) is one of the three most fundamental doctrines of Islam. Eschatology was therefore never a peripheral topic: its correct understanding was a condition of orthodox faith.

Sources for this page draw on Marcia Hermansen's chapter in [[winter-cambridge-companion-islamic-theology-2008]].

## Quranic Foundations

The Quran is the primary eschatological source. Meccan chapters, composed in urgent rhyming prose, insist on the reality of judgement as a test of faith for those who deny resurrection. Sura 81 (Al-Takwir, "The Overthrowing") provides the archetypal vision of cosmic reversal: the sun darkened, stars falling, mountains set in motion, seas boiling, and the sky "rolled up like scrolls of parchment." Nature — the stable reference point of pre-Islamic Arabian poetry — is completely subverted.

**The Primordial Covenant (Quran 7:172).** Before creation, all souls implicit in Adam's loins were asked "Am I not your Lord?" and testified "Yes." Human life is thus a process of remembering and fulfilling an already-undertaken covenant.

**Creation and return.** The Quran pairs creation with resurrection: "As We originated the first creation so We will bring it back again" (21:104). The creation of Adam from dust and the divine spirit (15:29) establishes the human composite as uniquely capable of bearing the *amana* (Trust) that the heavens, earth, and mountains refused (33:72). Eschatological judgement is the reckoning for whether that Trust was honoured.

**The divine command "Be!" (kun fa-yakun).** Creation is both instantaneous response to the divine will and continuous divine management (tadbir). The new creation (khalq jadid) at resurrection mirrors the original.

## The Structure of the Afterlife

### Death and the Intermediary State (Barzakh)

[[barzakh]] (literally "barrier" or "isthmus") is the state between individual death and collective resurrection. Prophetic traditions describe examination by angels immediately after death, asking about religious affiliation; the result is an anticipatory experience in the grave that foreshadows eternal destiny. Medieval debates disputed whether "punishments of the grave" are physical or occur in the imaginative faculty through dream-like images.

Asharism and Maturidism made belief in the barzakh an article of faith. Most Mutazilis and perhaps the Kharijites rejected it, reading the relevant scriptural passages differently.

Every creature has a *ajal musamman* (divinely determined term, 6:2). Once death comes, the soul exists in the barzakh "until the time of collective resurrection" (23:100). Philosophers influenced by Avicenna (d. 1037) took a psychological view: the soul's "return" is to its archetypal source, not to a reconstituted body.

### The Resurrection (Qiyama, Ba'th)

A trumpet sounds twice (39:68). The first blast ends all life; the second signals the resurrection of the dead (qiyama, "the rising up"). All humans assemble (hashr) on a vast and featureless plain. The Hajj pilgrimage contains echoes of this final assembly: the crowd, the uniform white garments resembling burial shrouds.

**The physical vs. spiritual debate.** The great majority of kalam theologians insisted on bodily resurrection, since "nothing is impossible for God" (cf. 36:81). A complete denial of resurrection is heretical. However, a denial of *physical* resurrection was maintained by certain Mutazilis and by falsafa practitioners including al-Farabi and Avicenna, who argued that the soul's resurrection is psychological/spiritual. The Asharite-Maturidi consensus held the physical view against this.

There is no original sin in Islamic anthropology: "every child is born with the sound original disposition (fitra)." Humans have been given a divine spirit; eschatological discipline restores rather than inaugurates their felicity.

### Judgement (Yawm al-Hisab)

All human actions were recorded (36:12). At judgement, deeds testify for or against their agents; limbs themselves will speak (41:19–24). Deeds are weighed in scales (mizan). The judged must traverse a narrow bridge (sirat) stretched over hellfire. A heavenly pool (hawd) of the Prophet awaits believers. Avarice will be embodied as a snake coiled around the miser's neck: punishment is embodied in forms commensurable with sin.

Nations too are judged in history: divine blessing or punishment is meted out to communities that fulfil or reject God's messengers.

### Promise and Threat (al-Wa'd wa'l-Wa'id)

A largely Mutazilite doctrine asserting that individuals' eternal fates can be rationally ascertained: God has promised to reward the good and punish the evildoer, so obedience guarantees heaven and grave sin guarantees hell. Asharites and Hanbalites rejected this as privileging human reason over divine sovereignty. The Islamic piety that emerges instead holds a **balance of hope and fear**: God's mercy is said to outweigh His wrath, yet even the most pious may sin at the last moment. Al-Ghazali: "It is not proper that the bondman's quest for Heaven should be for anything other than meeting with his Lord. As for the rest of Heaven's delights, man's participation in them is no more than a beast let loose in a pasture."

### Paradise (Janna) and Hell (Jahannam)

**Paradise** is a garden (janna) arranged in levels, verdant and abundant, where all wishes are fulfilled. Celestial food, drink, and companions (*hur*, perpetually virginal maidens) are described literally in the Quran and hadith. Some scholars held these pleasures indicate the transformation of human nature rather than simple sensory repetition. At the summit: the **beatific vision** (ru'ya, the vision of God) — a spiritual reward explicitly higher than material delights. The Quran states "the satisfaction (ridwan) from God is greater than such delights of the Garden" (9:72). Muhammad Iqbal (d. 1938) argued heaven and hell were representations of inner states of character.

**Hell (jahannam, al-nar, "the Fire")** is a place of just chastisement. Whether any punishment there would be truly eternal was disputed: for sinning believers, hell may serve as a temporary purgatory. Multiple Quranic terms for hell were interpreted by commentators as indicating ranks and levels.

## The Beatific Vision (Ru'ya)

The vision of God is the apex of eschatological reward and the site of a defining Asharite-Mutazilite controversy.

**Asharite-Hanbali-Maturidi position:** The hadith literature clearly stated "a veil shall be lifted, and the believers shall gaze upon the face of God." God is to be seen, in an ocular way that is nonetheless *amodal* (bi-la kayf — "without asking how"). The verse "vision (absar) cannot attain Him" (6:104) applies only to complete comprehension, not to all seeing. There is no logical reason why corporeal sight (basar) cannot apprehend a non-corporeal reality. Moses did not see God in his lifetime because God chose to veil Himself — this does not preclude vision in the next life.

**Mutazilite position:** Sight is a corporeal sense. God is neither substance nor accident. Therefore sight of God is axiomatically impossible. God Himself told Moses "you shall not see Me" (7:143).

This controversy was among the most significant in kalam. Asharism's insistence on the beatific vision served to maintain God's relational character — a God who can be encountered, not merely deduced.

## Intercession (Shafa'a)

The doctrine of intercession was contested partly because it seemed to compromise divine unity and justice.

**Quranic tension.** The Quran states "no soul shall bear the burden of another" (6:164; 17:15) and rejects redemptionist theology (2:48). Yet it opens a door: "no one shall intercede with Him except by His permission" (2:255). Intercession by angels (53:26), true witnesses (43:86), or those with a covenant with God (19:87) may avail.

**Prophetic intercession.** A set of hadith regarded as sound by traditional canons presented the Prophet as interceding for sinning members of his community both at the judgement day and following their condemnation to hell. Sunni Islam — especially Asharism and Sufi circles — affirmed this.

**Mutazilite objection:** Any form of intercession compromises God's unity and justice. God's moral arithmetic cannot be overridden.

**Asharite-Sufi response:** Without prophetic intercession, God becomes "a calculating, merciless automaton, unresponsive to human prayer." The concept of *wasila* (mediating spiritual aid) includes the Prophet and the *awliya'* (friends of God, saints). Sufi devotion to the Prophet as *al-insan al-kamil* (the Perfect Human Being) intensified intercessory belief.

**Later Hanbali and Wahhabi rejection:** Building on Ibn Taymiyya's hostility to intermediaries, Wahhabism (18th century) and certain strands of Islamic modernism sought to eliminate all forms of intercession. This represents a radical break with the Asharite synthesis. See [[salafiyya]] and [[islamicate-conservatism]].

## Messianism and Apocalyptic

### The Mahdi

A messianic figure, the *Mahdi* ("guided one"), appears in hadith chapters on *fitan* (crises and civil wars). In Sunni tradition, the concept has not been particularised as a strong millenarian expectation under ordinary conditions, though it has been invoked in crises.

**Twelver Shia:** The Mahdi is identified as the Twelfth Imam, who disappeared as a child in 939 CE and entered *ghayba* (occultation). He remains an inspiring spiritual presence. He is known as al-Qa'im ("the one who will rise up") and Sahib al-Zaman ("ruler of the times"). Most Shii political theory before the modern period held that no political order could be legitimate in the Imam's absence, producing political quietism.

**Historical Mahdist movements:** The Abbasid revolution (8th century), Ibn Tumart of the Almohads (d. 1130), various South Asian movements, and the Sudanese Mahdist revolt of Muhammad Ahmad (d. 1885) illustrate how eschatological expectation can fuse with political mobilisation.

**The Mujaddid (Renewer).** A milder form: a prophetic tradition holds that a scholarly Renewer appears at the beginning of each Islamic century. Unlike Mahdism, this has not generally been used for political mobilisation.

### Signs of the Hour

Specific sequences of cosmic signs precede the end: the sun rising from the west, the Dajjal (Deceiver, "the false Messiah," al-masih al-dajjal), the Beast, lunar eclipses, the return of Jesus, the appearance of Ya'juj and Ma'juj (Gog and Magog), and a final fire. The Dajjal figure — a one-eyed deceiver — features in apocalyptic literature that reflected historical anxieties rather than authoritative kalam doctrine; the Quran itself does not name him.

### The Second Coming of Jesus

Jesus (Isa) will return before the day of judgement, assisting the Mahdi against the forces of evil (inferred from hadith and Quran 43:61). The returned Jesus is identified as a Muslim following the law of the Quran — no vindication of earlier religious claims is entailed. Since there is no original sin and therefore no need for redemption, the Islamic Jesus did not die for humanity's sins (Quran 4:157). He will die of natural causes before the judgement day, for "every soul shall taste death."

## The Salvation of Non-Muslims

A troubling issue for Muslim theologians: if God's mercy and justice are supreme, what of virtuous monotheists who never encountered Islam?

The Quran's critique of earlier monotheisms was radical: Christians had distorted tawḥid through Trinitarian doctrine; Jews had rejected multiple prophets. Yet it also praised Christian clergy (5:82). The underlying Islamic premise was an *ur-Monotheismus*: an ancient shared tawḥid delivered to all peoples but progressively lost through distortion (taḥrif). Salvation was therefore available at many points in history.

Al-Ghazali allowed salvation to non-Muslims of his day "provided always that Islam had not been accurately presented to them, and that they had not wilfully refused it." This represents the generous end of a spectrum that also includes exclusivist positions deriving from Quranic verses such as "the religion of God is Islam" (3:19).

| Issue | Asharism / Maturidism | Mutazilism | Falsafa |
|---|---|---|---|
| Barzakh (grave) | Mandatory belief | Rejected | Psychological re-reading |
| Physical resurrection | Affirmed | Some deny physical form | Spiritual/psychological |
| Beatific vision | Affirmed (bi-la kayf) | Denied (God not visible) | Varies |
| Intercession | Affirmed | Denied or minimised | Not applicable |
| Promise and threat | Divine sovereignty limits rational prediction | Rationally determinable | Not applicable |

## Relation to Other Concepts

- [[kalam]]: the theological discipline that classified and defended eschatological doctrines under sam'iyyat
- [[asharism]]: dominated Sunni eschatology; affirmed beatific vision, intercession, physical resurrection, barzakh
- [[mutazilism]]: contested beatific vision, intercession, barzakh, and physical resurrection
- [[quran]]: the primary eschatological source; apocalyptic suras especially prominent
- [[hadith]]: essential for eschatological detail (signs of the Hour, intercession, punishments of the grave)
- [[barzakh]]: the intermediary state between death and resurrection
- [[imamate]]: Shii theology transforms Mahdism into a constitutive political-eschatological doctrine
- [[philosophical-sufism]]: Ibn Arabi and others developed an esoteric eschatology in which the afterlife is experienced as states of the soul and imagination; the *barzakh* is an imaginal realm
- [[occasionalism-islamic]]: the Asharite metaphysics of divine sovereignty underpins the rejection of necessary causal connection between deeds and destinies; God judges by will, not mechanical causation
- [[sunni-islam]]: the Sunni consensus (Asharite-Maturidi) on eschatological issues defined orthodoxy

## Historiography

Marcia Hermansen (in [[winter-cambridge-companion-islamic-theology-2008]]) emphasises interpretive diversity as the defining feature of Islamic eschatology: "it would be futile to present Muslim theological positions on eschatology as if there were a consensus regarding each detail." The absence of a central magisterium means debates have remained open across fourteen centuries.

Modern scholars of Islamic apocalyptic (David Cook, Haddad and Smith) have noted the connection of eschatology to theodicy: the end of things vindicates divine justice. Hermansen also notes sociological dimensions: millenarian movements reflect crises of political legitimacy, not authoritative kalam.

**Collection coverage note:** The Bibliotheca Alexandrina has strong coverage of Asharite-Maturidi kalam texts and al-Ghazali's *Remembrance of Death and the Afterlife*. Shii eschatology and post-medieval millenarian movements are less well covered.
