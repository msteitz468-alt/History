# Palermo Stone

The **Palermo Stone** is the largest surviving fragment of a 5th-Dynasty
(c.2400 BCE) basalt royal **annal-stele** — one of the most important contemporary
sources for the chronology and government of the
[[actors/old-kingdom-egypt|Old Kingdom]] and a key witness behind
[[concepts/egyptian-dynastic-chronology|Egyptian dynastic chronology]].

## Description

The stele was inscribed on both sides with royal annals reaching back to mythical
prehistoric rulers and the time of the god Horus, then human kings down to the 5th
Dynasty. It was originally about 2.1 m long and 0.6 m wide; "most of it is now
missing, and there is no surviving information about its provenance." The main
fragment, in the Palermo Archaeological Museum, has been known since 1866; further
pieces are in Cairo and the Petrie Museum, London. Each regnal year is partitioned
by vertical lines imitating the *renpet* (regnal-year) hieroglyph.

## What it records

The annals register, year by year: cult ceremonies, taxation, sculpture and
building, warfare, and — distinctively — **Nile inundation levels**. Donald Redford
argued that these Old Kingdom *genut* "were concerned with hydraulic/climatic
change, which, with its crucial agricultural and economic consequences, was
potentially the most important aspect of change as far as the reputation of each
individual king was concerned" — i.e. the flood height was itself a measure of royal
efficacy under [[concepts/divine-kingship|divine kingship]].

A caution for chronology: the Old Kingdom year-entries may count **biennial
cattle-censuses** rather than calendar years, so the apparent number of "years" for a
reign may need doubling.
