# The Aegean Bronze Age Scripts

The writing systems of Bronze Age Crete and Mycenaean Greece — **Cretan
Pictographic ("Hieroglyphic"), Linear A, and Linear B** (plus the freak **Phaestus
Disk**). They matter to history doubly: as the earliest writing of the Aegean, and
because **Linear B's decipherment proved the [[mycenaean-civilization|Mycenaeans]]
were Greeks**. Treated in [[edwards-cah-v2p1-1973|CAH Vol. II, Part 1]] (Ch. XIII(a),
Sterling Dow; XIII(b), John Chadwick).

## The isolation of Minoan literacy

Dow's central theme is that **Minoan literacy was strangely isolated and limited**.
Though in contact with literate Egypt, the Levant and Anatolia for centuries, the
Minoans borrowed almost nothing from them, set up **no public inscriptions**, and —
remarkably — produced **no surviving written literature** at all. Writing grew at
home, out of the seal-using habit, and was used overwhelmingly for **administration**.
Its end was as total as its isolation: outside Cyprus, no trace of Aegean writing
survived into Archaic Greece — the Dark Age was **completely illiterate** (the
"baneful signs" Bellerophon carries in the *Iliad* are the only echo).

## The sequence of scripts

- **Cretan Pictographic / "Hieroglyphic" (MM I–III, ~2000–1600):** phonetic signs
  evolved from seal-pictures, surviving mainly on seal-stones; the earliest Aegean
  writing. (The term "Hieroglyphic" has the wrong overtones — these owe little to
  Egypt.)
- **Linear A (MM III–LM I, ~1700–1450):** a syllabary developed out of the
  Pictographic, used for **palace accounts** (the Hagia Triada tablet-archive). Its
  language is **not Greek** and remains **undeciphered**; guesses (West Semitic;
  Luwian) are unproven. It is the parent of Linear B.
- **Linear B (LM II–LH IIIB, ~1450–1200):** the syllabary (~90 signs) adapted from
  Linear A to write **Greek**. Found at **[[knossos|Knossos]]** (the only Cretan
  site) and on the mainland at **Pylos, Mycenae, Thebes, Tiryns**.

The Minoans used a **decimal** numeral system (units, tens, hundreds, thousands)
with no separate sign for five, and weights/measures showing **Babylonian**
influence — almost their only borrowing.

## The Phaestus Disk

A unique baked clay disk (~1600 BCE, MM IIIb) found at Phaestus, stamped on both
sides in a spiral with **45 different signs** impressed from **movable type** — an
isolated anticipation of **printing**, "immense in potentiality but null in effect."
Its clay and signs are **un-Cretan** (probably an import, perhaps from **Lycia**),
its language unknown; it stands utterly alone, and is accepted as genuine only
because it was found in a controlled excavation.

## Linear B as a historical document

Ventris's **1952 decipherment** (with Chadwick) made Linear B the earliest readable
European language. The tablets are **terse administrative records of a single year**,
baked by the fires that destroyed the palaces. They reveal a **bureaucratic palace
economy** under a king (*wanax*), with a *lawagetas*, *telestai*, and *hequetai*
(followers/charioteers); vast **flocks** (~100,000 sheep at Knossos) and a centrally
organized **textile** industry; **bronzesmiths** (~400 at Pylos), flax, spices, and
named deities (including Dionysus and a "Phoenician spice"). The Pylos **"coast-guard"
tablets** and a hasty offering-list seem to register the **emergency** just before
that palace fell to the Sea Peoples. What the tablets do *not* give is political
history: no king's name, no foreign relations, no narrative.

## Limitations and Contestation

The scripts illustrate the **limits of administrative archives as history** — they
quantify an economy but are silent on events and persons. Linear A and the Phaestus
Disk remain **undeciphered**, and the "Minoan" language unknown. Linear B's
implications are themselves contested: its presence at Knossos ~1400 destroyed
Evans's belief that Minoans conquered Mycenae, and the **dating of the Knossos
tablets** (Evans's ~1400 vs. **L. R. Palmer's** down-dating to the twelfth century)
is a live dispute — see **[[fall-of-knossos]]**.

## The Literacy Gap and the Alphabet

The collapse of the [[mycenaean-civilization|Mycenaean]] palaces around 1200 BCE
**extinguished Linear B entirely** — the syllabary was a specialist palace-accounting
tool, useless once the palace economy vanished, and Greece became **illiterate for
some four centuries** (only [[cyprus|Cyprus]] preserved a syllabary). Literacy returned
only in the eighth century, and through a wholly different channel: not the revival of
the lost syllabary but the borrowing of the [[phoenicians|Phoenician]] consonantal
script and its transformation into the vowel-bearing Greek **[[alphabet|alphabet]]**
(~750 BCE). The contrast is fundamental — a restricted scribal syllabary that died with
its bureaucracy, versus an easily-learned alphabet that spread through trade and
underwrote the literate culture of the polis. See [[alphabet|the alphabet]].
