# Childe's Culture Concept

**Childe's culture concept** is the analytical cornerstone of culture-historical archaeology. Childe defined an archaeological "culture" as a constantly recurring assemblage of types (houses, pottery, burial rites, tools, etc.) and treated it as the material expression of a particular social or ethnic group.

## Origin and Formulation

The concept was taken over from German anthropology and applied systematically by Childe in his pan-European syntheses. In Champion et al. (1984) the definition is quoted directly: a culture is "a constantly recurring assemblage of types such as houses, pottery and burial rites, and regarded it as the physical manifestation of a particular social or ethnic group."

When traits of one culture appeared in another, this was explained as "interaction" (diffusion or influence); replacement of one culture by another was read as migration or conquest. The goal was to reconstruct a kind of prehistoric "political and military history" of peoples.

"Childe defined a culture as a constantly recurring assemblage of types such as houses, pottery and burial rites, and regarded it as the physical manifestation of a particular social or ethnic group." (Champion et al. 1984, Ch.1 / range lines ~320-340)

## Role in Interpretation

The culture concept provided the basic unit for ordering material and writing narratives. It was tightly linked to the Three Age System and to diffusionist explanations that traced most major innovations (farming, metallurgy, architecture) to the Near East. "In their place, it became important to ask where a feature observed in a particular culture had come from. A concern with tracing connections of this sort between cultures largely precluded the possibility of the independent invention of such features..." (Champion et al. 1984, Ch.1 / range lines ~350-365)

## Critiques and Supersession

The radiocarbon revolution demonstrated that many supposed diffusions were chronologically impossible or reversed. Processual archaeology rejected the equation of material assemblages with ethnic groups and shifted attention to adaptive behaviour, subsistence, settlement systems, and internal social dynamics. "The nature of the fundamental relationship between the material culture studied by the archaeologist and the social groups inferred by him was not seriously considered, although it can now be seen to be much less simple than was supposed..." (Champion et al. 1984, Ch.1 / range lines ~345-355)

Modern usage treats "cultures" (e.g. Linearbandkeramik, Corded Ware, Urnfield) as etic analytical constructs useful for description and comparison, not as direct proxies for self-conscious peoples.
