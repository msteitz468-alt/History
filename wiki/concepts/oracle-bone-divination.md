# Oracle-Bone Divination (Pyro-osteomancy)

The [[shang-dynasty|Shang]] practice of **divining by heat-cracking animal bones and turtle
shells** and — the Shang innovation — **inscribing the divination on the bone itself**. The
inscribed oracle bones are the **earliest substantial corpus of Chinese writing** and the
documentary threshold between proto-history and history in the
[[east-asian-heartland-region|East Asian Heartland]]. From [[major-cook-ancient-china-2017|Major
& Cook (2017)]]; complements [[anyang|Anyang]] and [[shang-dynasty|the Shang]].

## Technique

- **Materials.** Primarily the **plastrons (bottom shells) of turtles** and the **scapulae of
  cattle or water buffalo**; occasionally deer, sheep, tiger, even human bone. The practice
  descends from un-inscribed Neolithic and [[erlitou|Erlitou]]-period scapulimancy (which used
  pig and sheep bones and did **not** inscribe the charge).
- **The charge.** Diviners posed **paired "charges"** — questions phrased as complementary
  statements ("should the king make a burnt offering of one penned ox?" / "should the king
  perhaps *not*…?") — covering war, weather, harvest, childbirth, the fortunes of the royal
  consorts, even the king's toothache.
- **The crack.** A **hot bronze poker** was applied to prepared hollows on the back of the bone;
  the resulting **cracks** (Chinese *bu* 卜, an oracle-crack pictograph) were read by the diviner,
  and the king pronounced the prognostication.
- **The archive.** The charge, sometimes the outcome ("verification"), was then **engraved and the
  bone filed** — occasionally polished and filled with cinnabar — in pits holding hundreds of
  pieces. This is, in the phrase used for [[anyang|Anyang]], a "distinctly bureaucratic archive."

## King Wu Ding and the "New School"

The greatest corpus belongs to **King [[shang-dynasty|Wu Ding]]** (c. 1250–1192 BCE), who divined
obsessively on every matter of state and person. Under **Zu Jia** and successors a **"New School"**
ritual reform imposed a more rigid calendar of ancestral sacrifice, **suppressed negative
responses**, and **narrowed the range of topics** put to the bones — a contraction of the divinatory
field that is itself legible in the inscriptions.

## Discovery and the Authentication of the Shang

The corpus re-entered history c. **1899–1900 CE**, when scholars recognized archaic characters on
**"dragon bones"** sold as medicine in Chinese pharmacies. Traced to **Xiaotun** village near
[[anyang|Anyang]], they prompted the **1928** excavations that matched inscribed king-names to
[[sima-qian|Sima Qian's]] Shang genealogy and **confirmed the Shang dynasty's historical reality**
(while, conversely, the absence of the name [[xia-dynasty|"Xia"]] on any oracle bone fuels the
[[xia-dynasty-historicity|Xia question]]).

## Significance

Oracle-bone script is already a **mature, standardized logographic system** in the late Shang —
the direct ancestor of the modern Chinese script, the basis of the
[[east-asian-heartland-region|Heartland's]] claim to graphic continuity. Major & Cook stress that
writing here was **not invented to talk to spirits**: most divination bones are *uninscribed*, and
the inscriptions are after-the-fact records — administration and display, not communion (compare
the parallel argument for [[anyang|Anyang]] and [[uruk|Uruk]] under
[[writing-and-record-keeping]]).
