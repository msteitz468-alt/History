## Definition

The founding governing document of the United States, drafted at the 1787 Philadelphia Convention to replace the weak Articles of Confederation with a stronger federal structure combining separated executive, legislative, and judicial branches, a system of checks and balances, and a division of powers between federal and state governments. Ratification, hard-fought in several key states, was secured partly through the advocacy of the *Federalist Papers* — written chiefly by [[actors/hamilton-alexander|Alexander Hamilton]], James Madison, and John Jay — and was conditioned in several states on the promise of a Bill of Rights, added as the first ten amendments in 1791.

## Emic vs. Etic

Wholly emic: the document and the debates surrounding it (the Convention, the Federalist/Anti-Federalist pamphlet war, state ratifying conventions) are contemporary political acts, not later historiographical constructs.

## Historical Significance

The Constitution established the first durable written federal constitutional framework of the modern era, resolving the 1780s crisis of confederation-era weakness (inability to tax, regulate commerce, or enforce treaties) while embedding compromises — notably over slavery and small-state/large-state representation — whose consequences shaped subsequent American political development, including the sectional crisis that produced the Civil War. It has been amended twenty-seven times but never wholly replaced, making it the world's oldest continuously operating written national constitution.
