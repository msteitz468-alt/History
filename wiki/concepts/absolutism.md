# Absolutism (Absolute Monarchy)

**Absolutism** is the form of European monarchy, characteristic of the seventeenth and
eighteenth centuries, in which the ruler claimed **supreme and undivided sovereign authority**
— ideally unconstrained by representative estates, subject only to God and (in theory) natural
and divine law. Its paradigm is the France of **Louis XIV** (r. 1643–1715), the subject of
[[new-cambridge-modern-history-v5|*The New Cambridge Modern History*, Vol. V]]. It is a central
— and contested — category of early-modern political history.

## Emic and etic

The **etic** term "absolutism" is a later (nineteenth-century, often pejorative) coinage. The
**emic** language of the age spoke of *monarchie absolue* or *pouvoir absolu* — "absolute" in
the root sense of *absolutus*, "freed from" (legal restraint), not "total." Contemporaries
theorized it as **absolute but not arbitrary**: the king's will was law, but he was bound by
divine and fundamental law and by his duty to justice. The classic statements are Bossuet's
*Politics Drawn from Holy Scripture* (divine-right kingship) and Louis XIV's own *Mémoires*
("the concept of absolute monarchy," as NCMH V puts it), encapsulated in the apocryphal
*"L'état, c'est moi."*

## The practice

In its French paradigm, absolutism combined:

- **Personal rule** — after the Fronde, Louis XIV governed without a chief minister, "the
  king's part in the work of government" (NCMH V) made central.
- **Court and image** — the palace of **Versailles** as a theatre that domesticated the
  nobility and projected royal glory (*le style Louis XIV*; the Academies under Colbert).
- **Bureaucratic reach** — the **intendants** as royal agents in the provinces, bypassing
  older corporate bodies.
- **A standing army** and the leading military machine of the age.
- **Mercantilism** — Colbert's direction of the economy (tariffs, state industry, the navy)
  to maximize royal revenue and power — "Colbert as the embodiment of mercantilist policy."
- The subordination of the **Church** ("regarded as a department of State"; Gallicanism).

The model was widely emulated — "the widespread adoption of the methods of Louis XIV's
monarchy" — by the Habsburgs, Brandenburg-Prussia, and (in a distinctive form) Romanov Russia.

## A contested concept

Recent scholarship heavily **qualifies** "absolutism." Far from wielding total power, even
Louis XIV governed by **negotiation and compromise** with provincial elites, venal
officeholders, and corporate bodies whose cooperation he needed (the "absolute" state rested
on bargains, not omnipotence). Some historians reject the term as a myth manufactured by royal
propaganda and later historiography, preferring "the early-modern state" or treating absolutism
as **aspiration more than achievement**; others retain it for the genuine seventeenth-century
concentration of sovereign claims. The wiki uses it as a useful ideal-type while flagging its
gap from practice.

## Significance

Absolutism is the dominant model of the early-modern European state outside the
"constitutional" exceptions (England after 1688, the Dutch Republic), and the foil against
which the [[enlightenment|Enlightenment]] and the [[french-revolution-1789|French Revolution]]
defined themselves. It shaped state-building, the fiscal-military state, and the
[[balance-of-power|balance of power]] of the age of Louis XIV.
