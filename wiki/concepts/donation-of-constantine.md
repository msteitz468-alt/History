# The Donation of Constantine

## Definition

The **Donation of Constantine** (*Constitutum Constantini*) was a **forged document**, produced in
the eighth century, purporting to record the emperor Constantine I's grant to Pope Sylvester I (and
his successors) of primacy over the other patriarchates and of **temporal dominion over Rome, Italy,
and the western provinces**. In [[mckitterick-ncmh-v2-1995|NCMH II]] it is named (McKitterick, Ch. 1;
Noble, Ch. 21) as one of the three most audacious forgeries of the period, alongside the
**Pseudo-Isidorian Decretals** and the **Le Mans forgeries**.

## Context and Purpose

The Donation belongs to the era in which the [[the-papacy-eighth-ninth-centuries|papacy]] was breaking
free of Byzantium and constructing the territorial **papal state** under Frankish protection (the
[[pippinid-coup-751|coup of 751]], the Donation of Pippin, 756). NCMH II places it among the products of a
society — that of the Franks above all — that attached enormous importance to the **written word** and to
**documentary proof**, and which therefore also produced its most determined forgeries: "the greater the
emphasis on written records, the more determined could be the efforts to circumvent or manipulate them"
(McKitterick, Ch. 1). The forgery supplied an ancient, imperial pedigree for papal claims that were in fact
novel.

## Afterlife

Though a fabrication, the Donation was treated as genuine for centuries and became a cornerstone of papal
claims to temporal authority in the High and Late Middle Ages. It was definitively exposed as a forgery by
the Renaissance humanist **Lorenzo Valla** (1440) on philological grounds — a landmark in the development of
historical source criticism.

## Significance

The Donation of Constantine is both a key instrument of the eighth-century construction of papal authority
and, in its later exposure, a milestone in the history of **historical method** — the demonstration that
documents can be tested and dated by internal evidence.

## Historiography

NCMH II treats the Donation within the larger problem of **forgery and the cult of the written record** in
the early medieval West. The exact date and place of composition (Rome vs. Francia; 750s–760s vs. later) and
its precise intended use remain debated among specialists.

The text itself is now ingested (as "The Edict of Constantine to Pope Silvester") in
**[[edwards-constantine-and-christendom|Constantine and Christendom]]** (TTH 39), alongside the related
Constantinian legends — the *Oration to the Saints* and the *Discovery of the Cross*.

## From Logan, History of the Church in the Middle Ages (2013)

Logan: a forgery drawn up at the **Lateran**, quite possibly in the **early 770s**, purporting to be a fourth-century grant by Constantine to Pope Sylvester I of authority over Rome, Italy, and the western regions; likely aimed more at **Byzantine** claims than Germanic kings; **no evidence of eighth-century deployment**; later treated as prima facie papal supremacy (popes unaware of forgery); Lorenzo Valla proved forgery in the fifteenth century. **Pseudo-Isidore** later transmitted it. Situated within the [[franco-papal-alliance]] and [[the-papacy-eighth-ninth-centuries]] temporal-claims story rather than as a working 750s diplomatic tool.
