## Definition and Scope

Pacific cultural identity refers to the multiple, contested, and historically constituted ways in which Pacific Islanders understand and express who they are — as individuals, communities, nations, and as a region. The concept, developed most fully in [[denoon-cambridge-pacific-islanders-1997]] by Rotuman scholar Vilsoni Hereniko, insists that "cultural identity is process, not product." It is "always in a state of becoming, a journey in which we never arrive; who we are is not a rock that is passed on from generation to generation, fixed and unchanging."

The section analyses Pacific cultural identities through oral history, literature, visual and performing arts, and political expression.

## Distinction: Emic vs. Etic

- **Emic (Rotuman framework):** History is divided into *ao maksul ta* (time of darkness / pre-colonial), *ao taf ta* (time of light / colonial), and *ao fo'ou ta* (new time / post-colonial). The "new time" is marked by negotiability of identity — "in the ao fo'ou ta, Pacific cultural identities are indeed negotiable."
- **Etic (scholarly debate):** Western scholars invoking "invented traditions" and "inauthenticity" are contested by Islander scholars who argue the question of "authenticity is far less important than understanding the cultural, historical and political reasons for the essentialist stance adopted by some present-day political movements."

## The Essentialising Problem

A central tension is between:
1. **Political essentialism** — the strategic assertion of a fixed, bounded, authentic "Pacific" or indigenous identity for purposes of sovereignty claims, land rights, and cultural survival. Such essentialism is politically necessary even when scholars know it is historically simplified.
2. **Scholarly deconstruction** — the recognition that "Pacific cultures are anything but unitary" and that "seeming inconsistencies or contradictions in the way Islanders symbolically express their identities are symptoms of the multiplicity of competing cultures and ideologies in contemporary society."

Hereniko does not resolve this tension but argues it is the wrong focus: "The question of 'authenticity' is far less important than understanding the cultural, historical and political reasons for the essentialist stance." Islander scholars increasingly insist that "outsiders refrain from pontificating about indigenous identities."

## Historical Phases of Identity Formation

**Time of darkness (ao maksul ta):** Pre-colonial identities were contested and dynamic — "marked by exploration and trade." The oral history of Pohnpei reveals "conquest by successive outsiders, with accompanying changes in social practice and new skills and knowledge." Darkness in Polynesian cosmology was "fluid and ambiguous" — Hine Nui te Po (Lady of Darkness) was originally the Lady of Light. The pre-contact period produced "some of the most dynamic and imaginative art forms."

**Time of light (ao taf ta):** Missionisation and colonialism suppressed many cultural practices and "transformed cultural identities by converting Islanders to a new religious order while suppressing certain cultural practices." A linear view of history ("darkness" to "enlightenment") replaced circular/cyclical views. Yet "Islanders recognised the malleability of Christianity and the need to modify Christian practices to suit their cultures" — satirical forms, code-switching, syncretic Christianity all emerged.

**New time (ao fo'ou ta):** Since World War II, "Islanders have sought ways to reconcile indigenous culture, Christianity, and Euro-American values of materialism and progress. A wide range of fusions ensued, creating different identities from which to choose and multiple ways of symbolising them."

## Symbols and National Identity

Pacific elites have attempted to crystallise national identities through cultural symbols: "Whether these symbols are slit-gongs, pigs' teeth, and decorative leaves as in Vanuatu, the bird of paradise of Papua New Guinea, or the flag of the Federated States of Micronesia, the intention is to create a feeling of identification and pride in one's nation."

Key examples: the 1987 Fiji coups prompted rushed attempts to establish a "distinctly Fijian presence in the civil service and commerce" including a new parliament building modelled on a Fijian bure; Hawaiian sovereignty activists wore gourd helmets before the Honolulu Federal Court (1976); Maori tangi (funeral ceremonies) assert indigenous practices against pakeha hegemony. Language revival is "perhaps the most potent symbol": Maori language nests (*punana leo*) adopted as a model by Hawaiians.

## Regional Identity and the University of the South Pacific

Regional identity is "largely confined to the educated elite." The University of the South Pacific has been both a site of pan-Pacific identity formation and a site of failure to live up to its promise: its Literature and Language Department had "yet to teach any Pacific language" a generation after its founding, and "those who benefit most academically are those who choose not to participate in cultural activities."

Regional institutions — the South Pacific Commission, Forum Secretariat, Pacific Conference of Churches, USP — produce a cohort of "pan-Pacific" or international persons. The South Pacific Festival of Arts (now Festival of Pacific Arts), begun 1972 in Suva, has been a crucial site of cultural encounter and exchange: "a turning point in restoring dignity and pride to all Pacific countries."

## Gender Dimension

Pacific women's identities are doubly contested — by indigenous patriarchal structures and by Western colonial and post-colonial frameworks. "Many Pacific women have embraced Western feminism in their search for a cultural and gender identity which is free of both foreign colonialism and indigenous male oppression." Some Maori women challenge male elders who claim "women have no right to speak publicly on the marae." In Tonga and Samoa there is "a strong anti-feminist movement among women intellectuals, or at least a tendency to reject established feminism because of its primary association with white, Western, middle-class women." Island women are "increasingly forging regional organisations based on gender, circumventing the hierarchies of race, nation, and patriarchy."

## Multiple and Contextual Identity

Islander identity is inherently contextual: a Rotuman may identify as "Fijian" in Hawai'i (since Rotuma is constitutionally part of Fiji) or as "Pacific Islander" in England. "The answer, in other words, depends on the context and the respondent's motives." The Chamorro case is presented as the extreme example of "essentialising notions of identity, mixed heritage, and international mobility" — a people deeply hybridised by Spanish, Mexican, Filipino, and American contact who are challenged to prove the authenticity of their "Pacific" identity.

## Limitations

The concept as deployed draws primarily on Polynesian and Melanesian cases; Micronesian voices are less prominent. The source's analysis is predominantly that of Western-educated Islander elites, as Hereniko himself acknowledges: "Most of the sources used here, then, are views of the elite."

## Historiography

The key scholarly debate is the "invention of tradition" debate (Hobsbawm, Keesing) versus an insider critique defending essentialist political strategy (Trask, Hereniko). The source positions itself between these poles, treating essentialism as politically understandable while analytically problematising fixed identity claims.
