## Definition and Scope

The Islamic theology of revelation (*wahy*) addresses three interrelated questions:

1. **The nature and modalities of revelation**: How does God communicate with humans? What is the status of prophetic reception?
2. **The nature of the Qur'an**: Is it created or uncreated? Human or divine in its formulation? What does it mean to say that God speaks Arabic?
3. **Inimitability (*i'jaz*)**: What makes the Qur'an inimitable, and how does this inimitability serve as proof of its divine origin?

The concept is distinct from the parallel question of whether revelation is *necessary* (a question answered primarily by prophetology and ethics — see [[islamic-theological-ethics]]) and from the question of whether God exists, which the fact of revelation presupposes. The theology of revelation asks about revelation's structure, not its warrant.

## Terminology

Arabic uses two root-clusters for revelation: **WHY** (*wahy*, *awha*: the act of solemn, awe-inspiring communication, sometimes linked etymologically to sound or noise) and **NZL** (*nazala*, *nuzzila*, *anzala*: coming-down, descent, a place-related physical connotation). In the Qur'an, NZL-forms are far more common than WHY-forms, conveying the image of something sent down from on High rather than "unveiled." This linguistic point shapes the Islamic understanding of revelation as fundamentally a divine descent into history, not an interior illumination.

Qur'an 59:21 establishes the weight of the revelation: "If We had sent down this Qur'an upon a mountain, you would have seen it humble itself and split apart by the fear of God."

## God as Revelator: Beyond Ontology

A key theological move in classical Islamic theology (articulated by Ibn Taymiyya, d. 1328, and summarised by Michot): God's *lordship* (*rububiyya*) — his creative-ontological power — does not define his *godhead* (*ilahiyya*). The word *al-ilah* ("the God") must be understood in the passive sense (*al-ma'luh*): He who has the exclusive right to be worshipped and loved. It is revelation, not creation, that inaugurates this relation. "It is as if creation were nothing but an occasion for revelation, which alone will lead to its completion." ([[winter-cambridge-companion-islamic-theology-2008]], ch. 9)

A God who only created but never communicated would be "closer to the prime mover of Aristotle's metaphysics than to the God of the Qur'an." Revelation is thus not a supplement to creation but its theological completion.

## Modalities of Prophetic Revelation

Classical Islamic theology, drawing on Qur'an 42:51, identified three modes by which God speaks to humans:

1. **Inspiration (*ilham*)** in the awakened state or in dreams — "the true vision of a prophet is indeed a kind of revelation."
2. **Words from behind a veil** — as with Moses at Sinai (Qur'an 7:143; 19:52).
3. **Angelic mediation** — God sends Gabriel as messenger; Gabriel recites to the Prophet, who then speaks on God's behalf.

The third mode governs the Qur'an itself. Classical scholars (including Ibn 'Abbas) understood Gabriel as the active reciter; the Prophet as the recipient and transmitter. The physical phenomenon of receiving revelation was intense and well-attested: Muhammad shook, sweated even in cold weather, fell into trance, and reported different aural qualities — "the ringing of a bell" being "the most painful" form. ([[winter-cambridge-companion-islamic-theology-2008]], ch. 9, citing Bukhari, *Iman*)

The crucial distinction between prophetic revelation and possession by jinn (a charge made by the Meccans: Qur'an 15:6, 26:27, 37:36) was maintained by insisting that God does not dwell within humans or speak *through* them as a jinn possesses an epileptic; rather, God speaks *through his messenger* in the sense that the prophet speaks on His behalf. Between possession and incarnation, classical theology carves a space for genuine prophethood that leaves the prophet's humanity intact.

## The Uncreated Qur'an and the Mihna

The most theologically significant internal controversy in early Islam concerned whether the Qur'an is created or uncreated. The Mu'tazilite position — that the Qur'an is created (*makhluq*) — was imposed as official state doctrine by the Abbasid caliph al-Ma'mun and his successors during the *mihna* (Inquisition, 833–848 CE). Scholars who refused to affirm the created Qur'an, most famously Ahmad ibn Hanbal, were persecuted.

The *mihna* failed. Mainstream Sunni orthodoxy consolidated around the view that the Qur'an is **uncreated and eternal** — the speech of God (*kalam Allah*), not a product of time or human agency. The consequence is radical: Muhammad loses all authorship of the Qur'an. Unlike the Gospels, the Islamic scripture is never named after its prophetic vehicle. The Prophet's traditional designation as *ummi* (originally meaning "Gentile," later reinterpreted as "unlettered") was increasingly deployed to underline that he could not possibly have authored it.

The doctrine of the uncreated Qur'an is structurally related to Islamic occasionalism (see [[occasionalism-islamic]]): just as God creates all accidents directly without natural intermediaries, God authored the Qur'an without any human co-authorship. The Prophet is, in the words of the chapter, "a *causa serva* only" — a servant-cause.

## Inimitability (I'jaz)

From the ninth century onwards, increasing emphasis was placed on the **linguistic and stylistic inimitability** (*i'jaz*) of the Qur'an. The Qur'an itself contains explicit *tahhaddi* (challenge) verses: "Say: If all humans and jinn gathered together to produce the like of this Qur'an, they could not produce the like of it" (17:88). The doctrine of *i'jaz* was developed partly as apologetic confirmation of the exclusively divine nature of the text, and partly as theological aesthetics.

Several dimensions of inimitability were identified:

- **Linguistic superiority**: al-Shafi'i regarded Arabic as "the richest and most extensive in vocabulary" of all tongues; the Qur'an represents its apex. Ibn Taymiyya argued that Arabic's precision in "expressing detailed meanings and distinguishing between the subtle ones and the main ones by special terms" surpasses even Greek.
- **The Arabic-Islam bond**: The contemporary Arab poet Adonis (cautiously cited) argued that the first Muslims adhered to the Qur'an "because they saw in it a model of eloquence and a hitherto unknown and unanticipated form of writing." The chapter endorses the more restricted claim that the bond between revelation and the Arabic language is textually established in the Qur'an itself: "We have sent it down as an Arabic Qur'an" (12:2).
- **Untranslatability**: A translation of the Qur'an is not the Qur'an — at best "an essay to render its meanings." The situation is compared to the Jewish Bible rather than the Christian one.

The *i'jaz* doctrine had far-reaching consequences for Islamic civilisation: the Arabic sciences (grammar, lexicography, rhetoric, science of readings) became central sciences of the religion. Ghazali's formulation: "in themselves, linguistic science and syntax are not of the sacred sciences, but it has become necessary to engage in their study because of the law since this law has come in the language of the Arabs."

## The 'Uthman Vulgate and Textual Permanence

The collection of the Qur'an into a written form proceeded with notable rapidity following the Prophet's death. The vulgate produced under the third caliph 'Uthman (d. 656) has remained invariable in its organisation and content for fourteen centuries — "without a single word of the Qur'an being deleted, added or changed." This permanence was theologically grounded in Qur'an 15:9: "It was We who sent down the Reminder, and We will be preserving it." Multiple canonical *qira'at* (readings) were accepted for some passages, but the content of the vulgate itself was fixed.

## Avicennan Prophetology and its Limits

Al-Farabi (d. 950) and [[ibn-sina]] (d. 1037) approached prophethood from within Aristotelian-Neoplatonic psychology: after an immaterial contact between the Prophet's soul and the Active Intellect (the angelic intelligence governing the sublunar world), the Prophet's imaginative and rational faculties translate the divine message into human discourse. The Prophet's psychological powers differ from those of ordinary dreamers not in kind but in degree and perfection.

This had a significant implication: Avicenna treated revelation as primarily *practical* — its purpose is the establishment of a just society, not the disclosure of metaphysical truth. For those capable of philosophical reasoning, revelation is "of no immediate interest" as a source of knowledge; the philosopher recognises in scripture only images of truths already attained by reason. Mainstream Sunni orthodoxy (represented by the Ash'arite-influenced majority) firmly rejected this instrumentalisation of revelation, insisting that the manifest meaning of the Qur'an corresponds genuinely to accessible truth — "truth and ethics are not incompatible."

## Post-Prophetic Revelation: Dream-Vision, Inspiration, and Sainthood

With the sealing of prophethood (*khatm al-nubuwwa*) at Muhammad's death in 632, formal revelation (*wahy*) ceased. The question then arose whether divine communication continues in any form. Classical sources affirm it does:

- **Hadith qudsi** (holy traditions): approximately ninety sayings where the Prophet transmits first-person divine speech not included in the Qur'an — e.g., "O My servants, I have forbidden injustice to Myself."
- **True dream-visions (*ru'ya sadiqah*)**: "The veridical dream-vision of the believer is one forty-sixth part of prophecy" (Bukhari, *Ta'bir*, 2).
- **Sufi inspiration**: Shi'ite imams and later Sufi masters claimed divine inspiration (*ilham*) as a form of post-Muhammadan divine communication.

Mainstream Sunnism channelled this possibility through *ijma'* (community consensus): God's guidance is mediated by the whole *umma* and its scholars, not entrusted to any particular charismatic class. Ibn Taymiyya: "The consensus of the community dispenses with the necessity of the infallibility of imams."

The boundary between inspiration and new prophecy was carefully policed. When late-classical movements — Baha'ism, Qadianism — claimed their founders received genuine new revelation, they "inevitably excluded themselves from Islam."

## Historiography

The chapter by Yahya Michot in Winter's *Cambridge Companion* draws heavily on the work of Ibn Taymiyya as the organising theological voice — a methodological choice that foregrounds a Hanbali-Salafi synthesis over the Ash'arite mainstream. The Avicennan strand is an important counterweight. Key reference entries: EI2 articles on *i'djaz*, *al-Kur'an*, *lawh*, *mihna*, *nubuwwa*, *ummi*, *wahy*.

The question of *i'jaz* has generated a substantial secondary literature intersecting with Arabic literary theory; the theological and aesthetic dimensions are not always kept separate. The relationship between the uncreated Qur'an doctrine and the *mihna* is a major topic in the historiography of Abbasid intellectual politics.
