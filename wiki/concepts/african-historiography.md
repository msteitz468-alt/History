# African Historiography (the Recovery of the African Past)

## Definition and Scope

**African historiography** is the study of how the African past has been written — and, above all,
the twentieth-century **recovery** of that past from the colonial-era denial that Africa had any
history at all. As [[davidson-the-african-past|Basil Davidson]] documented, an authoritative
mid-century view (he quotes Sir Reginald Coupland) held that "the main body of the Africans" had
"no history" but had "stayed, for untold centuries, sunk in barbarism" — so that African history
"could be no fit subject for scholarly investigation because no such history existed." This was the
**"myth of the Negro past"** (Melville Herskovits) that rationalised racial subordination by
casting Africans as "children who had failed to grow up." The rehabilitation of African history
was therefore inseparable from **decolonisation** and the assertion of African dignity.

## The "no history" myth and its roots

The denial had deep intellectual roots — Hegel's exclusion of sub-Saharan Africa from world
history, the conflation of "history" with **written records** (which much of Africa lacked in
European-recognised forms), and the political utility of the myth in justifying conquest and the
[[atlantic-slave-trade|slave trade]]. Davidson's polemical purpose in assembling
[[davidson-the-african-past|*The African Past*]] was to refute it by exhibiting the documentary
record that already existed.

## The four kinds of evidence

The recovery rests on combining sources, each partial, that together reconstruct the African past:

1. **Arabic documentary sources** — the geographers and travellers who described Africa from the
   ninth century on: al-Mas'udi, **al-Bakri** (on [[ghana-empire|Ghana]]), **Ibn Battuta** (on
   [[mali-empire|Mali]] and the [[swahili-coast|Swahili coast]]), Ibn Khaldun, and **Leo
   Africanus** — by far the richest written record for the medieval Western Sudan and East Coast.
2. **European accounts** — the Greco-Roman *Periplus of the Erythraean Sea* for the early Indian
   Ocean coast, and from the fifteenth century the **Portuguese** and later European travellers,
   traders, and missionaries (invaluable but freighted with the bias Davidson is at pains to flag).
3. **Oral tradition** — the dynastic and migration traditions preserved by specialists such as the
   West African [[griot-oral-tradition|griot]], a genuine historical source when critically handled
   (the Vansina method).
4. **Archaeology** — the material record (Great Zimbabwe, the Sudanic cities of Kumbi Saleh and
   Jenne-jeno, Aksum, Igbo-Ukwu, the Swahili stone towns) that independently corroborates and
   extends the texts — decisive for periods and regions the written sources do not reach.

## Emic vs. Etic

The concept of a written "history" of Africa is largely **etic** — an external scholarly category.
But indigenous traditions of recording the past (royal genealogies, the griot's repertoire,
Ethiopian and Swahili chronicles, the *Tarikh* histories of Timbuktu) are **emic** and ancient;
part of the recovery has been recognising these African modes of historical memory as history, not
mere "myth."

## Limitations and Critiques

- The dependence on **outsider (Arabic and European) texts** risks seeing Africa through foreign
  eyes and privileging the states and coasts those outsiders visited over stateless and interior
  societies.
- The nationalist-era recovery sometimes **over-corrected** — emphasising great states, kings, and
  empires (a "drums and trumpets" African history) in mirror-image of the colonial canon, at the
  expense of social and economic history; later social, gender, and environmental historians have
  rebalanced this.
- **Oral tradition** raises genuine reliability problems (telescoping, legitimation, feedback from
  written sources) that require critical method.
- Davidson himself, a committed popular and anti-colonial historian, wrote with an evident
  **purpose** that shapes selection and emphasis.
