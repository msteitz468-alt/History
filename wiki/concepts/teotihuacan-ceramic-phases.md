# Teotihuacan Ceramic Phases

## Definition and Purpose

The **Teotihuacan ceramic phase sequence** is the primary relative chronological framework
for the history of [[teotihuacan|Teotihuacan]] and the broader [[basin-of-mexico|Basin of
Mexico]] from c. 500 BCE to c. 650 CE. Because Teotihuacan lacked a full writing system
and had no calendric inscriptions equivalent to the Maya Long Count, "local chronologies
depend mainly on sequences of stylistic and technological change in ceramics (broken
pottery fragments, 'sherds,' survive in great numbers), architecture, and other durable
materials" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 1). Absolute chronological estimates are based primarily on
calibrated radiocarbon dates, sometimes supplemented by archaeomagnetism, obsidian
hydration, and ceramic cross-ties with the dated Maya Long Count chronology.

Cowgill uses the term **phase** to mean "a stretch of time identifiable by prevalence of a
specific complex of cultural objects, especially ceramics," emphasizing "the time dimension
within a culturally fairly homogeneous region." A **period** is "a more inclusive interval
comprising more than one phase, and often a broader region." The **Teotihuacan Period**
comprises the interval from the Patlachique phase through the Metepec phase (Cowgill 2015,
Ch. 1).

## The Phase Sequence

Cowgill stresses that all absolute dates "are only my current guesses, and are open to
significant changes… all dates much before the 1519–1521 Spanish Conquest should be
understood as having at least a 50 to 100 year 95 percent 'confidence interval'"
(Cowgill 2015, Ch. 1).

### Pre-Teotihuacan Phases

**Cuanalán** (c. 500–200 BCE)
The local Teotihuacan Valley variant of the broader Cuicuilco/Ticomán ceramic tradition
(also called the Ticomán I–III tradition). Serving vessels tend toward simple red-slip
decoration in a red slip over the natural surface, "often remarkably graceful in form"
(Cowgill 2015, Ch. 3). Bowls with medial or shoulder breaks in profile, often with large
hollow supports, are characteristic. Two nucleated villages in the Teotihuacan Valley
(Cuanalán and TF-35) are associated with this phase. This phase is contemporaneous with
Cuicuilco's growth into the Basin's dominant polity.

**Tezoyuca** (c. 200–100 BCE)
A ceramic complex that "strongly influenced the Cuicuilco tradition in the Basin of Mexico"
around 200 BCE. Notable for: a distinctive style of white-on-red painting; slant-eyed
figurines; and various vessel forms with possible links to the Chupícuaro tradition, some
180 km to the west. Tezoyuca ceramics are especially characteristic at 13 small sites atop
isolated steep-sided hills — locations with wide viewsheds and defensive possibilities —
ringing the Teotihuacan Valley and adjacent regions. Bennyhoff (1967) firmly argued this
represents a phase prior to (not overlapping with) Patlachique. Most hilltop sites appear
abandoned in the subsequent Patlachique phase. The complex may represent an intrusion from
the west or simply local adoption of western ceramic styles; Cowgill is skeptical of
migration hypotheses (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 3).

### Teotihuacan Period Phases

**Patlachique** (c. 100–1 BCE)
The phase during which Teotihuacan grew from "almost nothing to become a large city"
(Cowgill 2015, Ch. 4). Area of fairly dense Patlachique sherd cover extends over 6–8 km².
Ceramics return to the Cuicuilco/Cuanalán tradition after the Tezoyuca interlude, though
with some influences possibly from the Cholula region. Key forms: composite silhouette
vessels with shoulder breaks; ollas with "wedge" rims (less angular than Tzacualli);
hollow supports (less common than before); bichrome and polychrome decoration including
combinations of red, white, and resist; angular broad red geometric shapes with white
borders. The clearest evidence of Patlachique civic construction is **Stage One of the Moon
Pyramid** — a square platform 23.5 m on a side, faced with small pink cut stones in mud
mortar, oriented about 11–12° east of north (different from the later canonical 15.5°).

**Tzacualli** (c. 1–100 CE)
The phase of Teotihuacan's supremacy in the Basin. The city expanded to cover roughly
20 km² with a population estimated at 60,000–80,000. The ceramics are a local development
from Patlachique, not a new intrusion. Supports disappear. Serving wares are sometimes
monochrome red, often polychrome with resist decoration in "soft glowing colors"; reds
are less brilliant than later; specular red is rare or absent. Motifs are abstract but
more curvilinear than Patlachique. Flat-bottomed outcurving bowls may begin to appear.
Pastes are "a distinctive reddish brown with yellowish or whitish inclusions" (Hopkins
1995, cited in Cowgill 2015, Ch. 5), a characteristic extending into Miccaotli. Storm God
jars appear as an early type. The **Sun Pyramid** — basal dimensions 216 m × 216 m
(enlarged to 222.7 m), height ~63 m, volume ~1,175,000 m³ — was built in a single
prodigious construction effort during this phase.

**Miccaotli** (c. 100–170 CE)
The phase following Tzacualli, with the city and polity continuing to grow. Cowgill argues
against the older estimate of a very brief (half-century) Miccaotli phase, noting "the
quantity of Miccaotli sherds collected by the Teotihuacan Mapping Project" suggests at
least 70 years. A Bayesian statistical analysis of 33 calibrated radiocarbon dates from
the Teopancazco compound (Beramendi-Orosco et al. 2009) suggests an earlier start for the
subsequent Tlamimilolpa phase, implying an earlier endpoint for Miccaotli.

## Chronological Methods and Uncertainties

Pan-Mesoamerican stage schemes (Paleoindian, Archaic, Preclassic/Formative, Classic,
Postclassic) are poorly suited to Teotihuacan because "major breaks in both systems do not
correspond well to major changes at Teotihuacan" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 1). Cowgill prefers
the local ceramic phase sequence.

Absolute dates depend chiefly on calibrated radiocarbon dating. Phase boundaries "come in
chunks of two or three centuries, or even longer" in many Mesoamerican chronologies; being
able to resolve periods of a century or less "would transform our understanding of the past
in the same way that the resolving power of microscopes and telescopes transformed biology
and astronomy" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 1). Bayesian statistical analysis of multiple calibrated
dates in stratigraphic context offers the best prospect for improvement.

Cross-ties with the Maya Long Count chronology (available from c. 250–1000 CE in Maya
inscriptions, and from as early as 36 BCE in some dated inscriptions elsewhere in Eastern
Mesoamerica) can supplement radiocarbon dates but are limited at Teotihuacan.

## Historiography

Phase names derive from Náhuatl terms. Pedro Armillas (1950) introduced named phases
(Tzacualli, Miccaotli, Xolalpan, Tlamimilolpa) in place of numbers, also documenting the
city at over 750 ha. James Bennyhoff (1960s, largely unpublished) refined the sequence
substantially as part of the Teotihuacan Mapping Project, and his work underpins Evelyn
Rattray's (2001) published synthesis, currently "the best available study of Teotihuacan
ceramic chronology, in spite of some ambiguities and inconsistencies" (Cowgill 2015,
Ch. 1). Smith (1987) and Sanders (1986, 1995) also contribute major ceramic studies.
Cowgill notes his own absolute dates "differ somewhat from those in earlier publications,
including my own," primarily because of the Beramendi-Orosco et al. (2009) Bayesian
analysis. The TMP ceramic analysis (1960s) and a 1970s reanalysis by Rattray disagree on
Patlachique sherd counts by 61%; only a new analysis of the (carefully preserved) TMP
collections can resolve this.

## Evans & Webster 2001 (phase usage, not a redefinition)

[[evans-webster-archaeology-ancient-mexico-central-america-2001|Evans & Webster (eds.) 2001]]
does not replace the Rattray/Cowgill absolute chronology, but signed entries use the local
phase names as working labels across the Basin and Tula region (**EW2001**):

- **Patlachique:** Parsons — Cuicuilco and Teotihuacan each ~40,000 as competing multitiered
  capitals; major Basin modification only in Tzacualli after earlier Formative shoreline
  pattern (Basin of Mexico entry).
- **Tzacualli / Miccaotli:** Pendergast parallels Altun Ha green-obsidian offering c. A.D.
  250–300 with Miccaotli Sun Pyramid Adosado and Feathered Serpent Temple Ofrenda 1.
- **Late Tlamimilolpa–Metepec (c. A.D. 300–750):** Mastache & Cobean / Healan — Teotihuacan-
  related colonization of the Tula region peaking Late Tlamimilolpa; Chingú as provincial
  center; many sites abandoned ~750 with Metepec-era demise of the Teotihuacan system.
- **Post-Teotihuacan sequence:** Armillas succession Teotihuacán–Coyotlatelco–Mazapan–Aztec
  (Cobean & Mastache); Coyotlatelco traditionally c. A.D. 700–900, some settlements as early
  as 600–650, coinciding with first occupations after Teotihuacan-related abandonment.

Use these as **cross-regional usage notes**; absolute dates remain contested (see Chronological
Methods above and [[teotihuacan-collapse-550ce]]).
