# Reducción (Colonial Resettlement / Mission Town)

A *reducción* (Portuguese *redução*; cf. [[jesuit-aldeias|aldeia]]) was the forced or
negotiated **concentration of dispersed indigenous populations into nucleated,
grid-planned towns** under colonial and ecclesiastical supervision. The term names both a
secular-administrative program and a missionary institution, and the distinction matters.

## Two Faces of the Reducción

- **Toledan (administrative) reducciones (emic/etic).** As part of the Toledan
  reorganization of Peru in the 1570s, the Spanish crown resettled Andean communities into
  new towns to make tribute collection, the [[mita|mita labor draft]], and religious
  instruction legible and enforceable. This is the institutional backbone of the mature
  colonial Andes treated by Saignes in
  [[cambridge-history-native-peoples-americas-v3-2|CHNPA III Part 2]].
- **Mission reductions.** Religious orders — above all the Jesuits among the
  [[guarani|Guaraní]], and in western [[amazonia|Amazonia]] and the
  [[southern-cone|Chaco]] — gathered frontier peoples into self-governing mission towns
  that combined evangelization, communal production, and (in the Guaraní case) armed
  self-defense against [[bandeirantes|bandeirante]] slaving.

## Limitations and Critique

The reducción is contested both as policy and as category. It disrupted Andean
[[vertical-archipelago|verticality]] and dispersed settlement patterns, concentrated
populations in ways that worsened epidemic mortality, and never fully "reduced" indigenous
life: communities retained outlying lands, evaded residence rules, and reworked the
institution to their own ends. Historians debate whether reducciones were primarily
instruments of extraction, of evangelization, or of an indigenous strategy of survival
within the colonial order — an instance of the broader [[ethnogenesis]] theme.
