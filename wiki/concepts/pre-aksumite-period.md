# Pre-Aksumite Period (Historiographical Concept)

## Definition

"Pre-Aksumite" is a retrospective periodisation label applied since the mid-twentieth century to archaeological and epigraphic materials from the northern Horn of Africa (primarily the Tigray highlands and southern Eritrea) that pre-date the emergence of the [[aksum|Aksumite kingdom]] (broadly, before the first century AD). The term groups communities showing South Arabian architectural, inscriptional, and religious affinities together with simpler, apparently indigenous farming settlements under a single chronological umbrella.

Phillipson (*Foundations of an African Civilisation*, 2012) criticises the label on two grounds: it names a period *retrospectively* — by what it eventually became — and it imposes a false homogeneity on communities that were in fact culturally diverse and geographically distributed. As he writes, the term has led to "emphasis on élite elements of ancient society and culture, with concomitant disregard for signs of underlying continuity."

A related sub-label — **Ethio-Sabaean** — was applied specifically to communities showing strong South Arabian affinities (monumental temples, Sabaean-script inscriptions, Almaqah worship). "Ethio-Sabaean" and "Pre-Aksumite" were sometimes treated as successive phases, sometimes as synonyms. Phillipson argues the former term perpetuates the misapprehension that South Arabian colonisation drove cultural change in the Horn; its continued use is deprecated in his framework.

A third sub-label — **Proto-Aksumite** — was coined by Fattovich and Bard for materials from Beta Giyorgis hill near Aksum dating to the last four centuries BC, on the grounds that they represent a "significant break with previous trends and greater continuity with the succeeding Aksumite civilisation." Phillipson accepts the term's local validity for Beta Giyorgis but argues it should not be extended to the wider region, where equivalent dating evidence is sparse and no general hiatus in settlement is attested.

## Examples

The following site-types were typically subsumed under "Pre-Aksumite":
- **Elite/monumental sites**: [[yeha|Yeha]] (Great Temple and Grat Be'al Gebri), Maqaber Ga'ewa, [[hawelti-melazo|Hawelti-Melazo]], Gobochela, Kaskase — all characterised by South Arabian architectural forms, Almaqah dedications, and South Arabian-script inscriptions.
- **Non-élite / indigenous farming sites**: Ancient Ona sites (Sembel, Mai Temenai, Mai Chiot, etc.) near Asmara, and Kidane Mehret/Beta Giyorgis in the Aksum area — stone-built compounds, hand-made pottery, flaked-stone tools, no South Arabian connections.

The contrast between these two categories is one of the principal empirical arguments against treating the period as a cultural unity.

## Limitations and Critiques

1. **Retrospective naming**: labelling something by what it preceded distorts analysis; it presupposes the telos of Aksumite civilisation rather than letting earlier communities be understood on their own terms.
2. **False homogeneity**: the Ethio-Sabaean monumental sites and the Ancient Ona peasant settlements were contemporary but culturally distinct; lumping them together masks the diversity of northern Horn society in the first millennium BC.
3. **Southern Arabian bias**: the predominant attention given to élite, Sabaean-affiliated sites produced a skewed record. Recent survey has shown these sites were "very few in number when viewed in the context of overall contemporary settlement."
4. **Chronometric imprecision**: the "Pre-Aksumite" period was originally dated by correlation with southern Arabian inscriptional chronologies, themselves contested. Radiocarbon dating for the first millennium BC is "unusually imprecise" due to atmospheric fluctuations; many proposed dates remain disputed.
5. **Political over-reading**: the assumption that a unitary kingdom called [[dmt-kingdom|D'MT]] governed the northern Horn during this period rests on only seven inscriptions, none in primary archaeological context, and the term *mkrb* (formerly translated "overlord of a federation") may simply mean "blessed."

## Phillipson's Alternative Framework

Rather than employing the "Pre-Aksumite / Aksumite" binary, Phillipson advocates situating all materials in an **absolute chronology** (years BC/AD) while recognising continuous indigenous development. He proposes recognising, at minimum:
- A phase of farming communities with stone-built domestic architecture and local material culture extending from at least the eighth century BC (and probably earlier), with continuity from still earlier traditions.
- An overlapping phase of elite/religious sites with selective South Arabian borrowings, concentrated in the ninth to fourth centuries BC.
- A highly localised "Proto-Aksumite" phase at Beta Giyorgis (last four centuries BC), whose inhabitants were "directly ancestral to those who were subsequently responsible for the establishment and expansion of the Aksumite kingdom."
