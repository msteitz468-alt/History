# Belonging and Exclusion in the Roman Empire (Citizenship, Barbarism, *Infamia*)

How the Roman Empire defined who **belonged** and who did not — the spectrum running from the
Roman citizen at the centre, through the *peregrine* provincial, the rural *rusticus* and ethnic
"internal barbarian," down to the legally **infamous** outcast and the chattel slave. The view
"from the margins" of the social order described "from above" in [[roman-city-government]].
Treated in [[bowman-cah-v11-2000|CAH XI]] Ch. 11 (**Brent D. Shaw**), a sociological essay
companion to the banditry analysis in [[roman-banditry]].

## Massive unity, massive diversity

Shaw's premise: the empire of c. 80 million was a **militarily created hegemony** whose boundaries
"were not coterminous with anything that could reasonably be called a 'Roman society'." It
harboured thousands of distinct communities — in Spain c. 700 communities (only ~100 with Roman
status), in eastern North Africa some 500 ethnic *gentes*. The "load-bearing unit" that held it
together was the **mosaic of cities**: membership in the empire was measured first as membership in
a **city**, reinforced by **worship of the emperor** and the universal test of **paying tribute**.
Aelius Aristides could praise Rome's "citizenship and its grand conception" as the badge of a
common *patria* — while having to deny, in the same breath, the recalcitrant highlands and
unintegrated enclaves that gave him the lie.

## Citizenship as a tool of integration

The later first century was the **watershed** in the spread of **Roman citizenship** to
provincials — but never (Shaw stresses) to build a "national state." Its aim was to **bind local
men of power to the central state**: citizenship was a *beneficium*, a reward for loyalty given by
the emperor alone. The mechanisms:

- The **decline of new colonial foundations** (the last real *coloniae* under Trajan) in favour of
  **promoting existing communities** to *municipium* or *colonia* status (esp. under Hadrian), and
  of **en bloc** grants replacing first-century *ad hominem* ones.
- The **Latin right** (*ius Latii*) — a collective, flexible instrument: holders of municipal
  office (or, under *Latium maius*, all decurions) became Roman citizens. Vespasian gave it to
  **all Spain**; the *Lex Irnitana* and *Lex Salpensana* show its detailed working.
- The **auxiliary diploma** — citizenship after 25 years' service, the chief route in the cityless
  frontier provinces (until the c. 140 change, when soldiers' sons increasingly already held it).

But there was **no bureaucracy to police status**. Citizenship was transmitted through the
**family**; proof depended on a kept diploma or (in record-keeping regions like Egypt) a
birth-registration obtained only "on request." Frauds went undetected for generations (the Alpine
Anauni who served as praetorians while not actually citizens). And the **practical value** of
citizenship "declined in inverse proportion to the numbers who acquired it": the **right of
appeal** worked for local aristocrats (the Athenian elite before Marcus) but failed ordinary
citizens — Paul and an anonymous appellant of AD 70 both appealed, and both were executed. The
real benefit was the **omnipresence of Roman legal norms** (the Babatha archive's "self-
romanization") more than any bare right.

## The uneven map of integration

The spread was **geographically uneven**: dense, uniform municipal development in the Mediterranean
plains (Narbonensis, Baetica, eastern Africa, Italy) with rapid acculturation to a common **Latin
culture**; partial, status-by-personal-patronage integration in the **Greek East** (where old
poleis resisted the Roman municipal model and Latin written culture could not displace Hellenism —
though gladiators and the arena transplanted readily); and slow, individual grants in the
**cityless Celtic/Germanic fringe**, retarded by Mediterranean prejudice against "northern types."
Tellingly, the Germanies/Raetia/Noricum produced **no senators**, Egypt only two (Alexandrian),
Britain none; even Africa's and Spain's senators were mostly **Italian settler stock**.

## Barbarians and "internal barbarians"

Beyond and within the cities lay those who lacked the *civitas* — by definition "uncivilized,"
hence **barbari**. Shaw analyses the *barbaros* as a **dichotomous ideological construct**, not an
empirical report: the barbarian as **anarchic, animal, less-than-human** (justifying conquest,
enslavement, and extermination — Domitian's "I have forbidden the Nasamones to exist"; the
genocidal scenes on the Columns of Trajan and Marcus), *and* as the **noble savage** (Tacitus'
*Germania* as a mirror of Rome's own vices). The same logic applied to **internal barbarians** —
despised *ethnê* (Mysians, "lying" Arabs, Egyptians and Jews "born for slavery"), and the
contemptible **rural population**: the *rusticus* marked by his body and dress "like a barbarian,"
and above all the mobile, marginal **shepherd**, simultaneously idealized and feared.

## The machinery of dishonour: *infamia*

Within the city, exclusion was graded by **honour and shame** — a "physics of morality" enacted in
**public**: the theatre seating that mapped the social order (senators, equites/decurions, citizens
by age, then the *pullati* poor, women, gladiators in the rafters), and **theatrical punishment**
finely graded by whip and status. Formal **infamia** (slow to crystallize into a unified concept
before the late second century) struck both for **acts** (theft, breach of trust, insolvency,
false accusation) and for **occupations**: undertakers (*libitinarii*, ritually banished beyond the
walls), criers, **gladiators** (free men crossing the status frontier by *auctoramentum*, "selling"
their bodies under compulsion of poverty), **actors/mime-players** (the *stupidus* as scapegoat;
infames barred from marrying the elite), and **prostitutes and *lenones*** (pimps), defined by the
public loss of *pudor*. Beneath all, **slaves** — and the **exposed children** (*expositio*,
"if it's a boy keep it, if a girl discard it") who fed back into the slave system — were the
"quintessential outcasts," natally alienated, "counted nowhere."

## Significance and contest

Shaw reframes "being Roman" as a **spectrum of belonging and not-belonging**, governed less by
formal law than by the interplay of imperial categories with thousands of local communities'
values — and insists that the empire's celebrated integration was, for the **mass** of its
people, a structure for "the more stable and efficient exploitation of inferiors." The
interpretation is avowedly **sociological and ideological** (drawing on Auerbach, Finley, Ste
Croix), and as such contests the more benign Gibbon–Mommsen image of the golden-age empire from
below.

- **Always-already inclusive tradition.** Claudius’s AD 48 Lyon speech (ILS 212; also Tacitus) proposing Gallic nobles for the Senate appealed to outsider kings (Numa Sabine; Tarquin Corinthian/Etruscan). “Claudius … was correct that Roman identity was in flux right from the very beginning.”
- **Multiple simultaneous identities.** Acts’ Paul anecdote models provincial identity politics (Jew, Roman citizen by birth vs purchased, Tarsian, Pharisee, resident alien)—citizenship privilege revealed at the point of flogging. Treating all provincials as “in some sense Romans” obscures distinctions that mattered (legionary citizens vs auxiliary *peregrini*; informal soldier marriages and non-citizen children).
- **Local identities under empire.** Unprecedented Mediterranean mobility (slave trade, military redeployment, urban immigration); diasporas tracked via cults; little host-society celebration of hybridity. Greeks and Jews reformulated identities for imperial contexts—Jewish life more portable via Greek and scripture-based worship; local communities east and west built parallel Trojan/Greek foundation myth-histories.
