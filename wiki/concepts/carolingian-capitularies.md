# Carolingian Capitularies

## Definition

**Capitularies** (*capitularia*, from the division of the texts into *capitula*, "chapters")
were the administrative and legislative edicts issued by the Carolingian rulers from
[[pippin-iii|Pippin III]] onward, and in growing volume under [[charlemagne]] and his successors.
They are the central documentary evidence for how the [[carolingian-empire]] was — or was meant to
be — governed, and a primary battleground in the [[nature-of-carolingian-government|debate over the
reality of Carolingian administration]].

## A Heterogeneous Genre

NCMH II (Fouracre, Ch. 3) stresses that capitularies are **not a single kind of document**. They
"include a wide range of documents produced in different conditions and for different purposes": some
expressed idealistic or moral-reforming intentions; some conveyed real government orders; some were
drawn up in response to requests from the localities. This heterogeneity is the key to their highly
variegated content — and the reason they cannot be read straightforwardly as either a law code or an
administrative archive. They must be distinguished, too, from the **leges** (the so-called Germanic
law-codes of the various peoples), with which they are easily and wrongly conflated.

## Capitularies and Reform

The capitularies embody the Carolingian conviction that **"revival, reform and expansion went hand in
hand"**: the cleansing of the Christian community, the moral welfare of the king's subjects, and the
strengthening of royal authority were a single enterprise. Charlemagne's mandate *De Litteris Colendis*
("On Cultivating Letters") and the reforming legislation of 779, 789, and the great capitulary of 802
show a ruler "seriously interested in these ideas, even if they were not put forward very systematically,
coherently or practically." By directing the reform of the church at the moral life of the subject, the
capitularies **widened the brief for the state's intervention** in everyday life — a theocratic conception
of government. The **Edict of Pîtres (864)** under [[charles-the-bald]] is a notable later example,
addressing coinage, defence, and fortification.

## Significance and Controversy

The capitularies sustain both sides of the [[nature-of-carolingian-government]] debate. Read as
innovations, they suggest a thorough reform of Frankish rule and a partial reconstitution of Roman public
authority. Read sceptically (Reuter), they reveal more about royal *intention* and ideological ambition
than about administrative *achievement*, since evidence of their implementation is thin. Either way they
mark the early medieval West's most ambitious experiment in **government by the written word** — itself
bound up with the literacy and book-culture of the [[carolingian-renaissance]].
