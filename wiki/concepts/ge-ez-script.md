# Ge'ez Script (Ethiopic)

## Definition and Scope

The term **Ge'ez** denotes the Semitic language of the Aksumite kingdom; the term **Ethiopic** denotes the script used to write it (in the Aksumite context — Phillipson applies this distinction consistently). Ethiopic script was one of two scripts used to write Ge'ez in the Aksumite period; the other was the **ASAM-derived** script (also called "pseudo-Sabaean" or "ESA-derived" in older literature, terms Phillipson rejects as implying inferiority or foreign origin).

The Ethiopic script, written left to right, is the ancestor of the modern Tigrinya and Amharic scripts; Ge'ez itself survives as the liturgical language of the Ethiopian and Eritrean Orthodox churches.

## Origins

Ge'ez belongs to the South Semitic branch of the Semitic languages. It was formerly believed that Ge'ez was introduced to the Horn of Africa from southern Arabia during the first millennium BCE. Current linguistic consensus (Goldenberg 1977; Hudson 1977, 2000; Appleyard 1996) holds that Semitic speech began in the Horn at a significantly earlier date — the language was indigenous rather than transplanted. South Arabian scripts and influences were grafted onto an indigenous Semitic-language substrate.

The **ASAM-derived** script closely replicated the formal script used in pre-Aksumite inscriptions of the D'MT horizon and earlier periods. It could be written from left to right or right to left (boustrophedon writing had already gone out of use). Like all Semitic scripts of the period, it was unvocalised — indicating consonants only, providing only an approximation of pronunciation, and thus difficult for readers unfamiliar with the text.

## The Two Aksumite Scripts

In the Aksumite period Ge'ez was written in two distinct scripts, which also correlated with certain linguistic distinctions — so they were not simply stylistic alternatives:

| Script | Direction | Vocalisation | Used for |
|---|---|---|---|
| ASAM-derived | Right-to-left (or left-to-right) | Never vocalised | Formal, archaising stone inscriptions; often deliberately archaic |
| Ethiopic | Left to right | Initially unvocalised; incipient vocalisation from 4th c. CE | Less formal graffiti, everyday use, coins (from 6th c.), manuscripts |

An example of the linguistic distinction between the scripts: the word for "king" was written *mlk* in ASAM-derived script but *ngs / negusa* in Ethiopic script; "son of" was *bn* vs. *wld / walda* respectively — the Ethiopic forms being those that survived in later Ge'ez, Tigrinya, and Amharic.

## Vocalisation

A major development within the Aksumite period was the addition of **vocalisation** to the Ethiopic script: small appendages to the consonantal symbols indicated vowels, creating what became the modern Ge'ez/Tigrinya/Amharic abugida. The earliest attested vocalisation appears in fourth-century inscriptions (RIE 188, 189). Adoption was slow and irregular: coin inscriptions — used from the early sixth century onward in Ge'ez — "sometimes" showed partial but inconsistent vocalisation. Phillipson notes that resistance to vocalisation may have reflected conservatism, a desire to restrict literacy, or a desire for simplicity.

Controversies about possible Indian influence on the form taken by Ge'ez vocalisation (Kobishchanov 1979) and its relationship to broadly contemporaneous Armenian practice (Olderogge 1974) are unresolved; Phillipson does not pursue them.

## Ge'ez as a Language

Ge'ez was the everyday vernacular for most inhabitants of the Aksumite kingdom's core area during the first seven centuries CE, with Cushitic languages (notably Agau dialects) used in recently subsumed peripheral regions. Cushitic loanwords entered Ge'ez (evaluated by Appleyard 1978, Ehret 1979), most numerous in Amharic. Basic literacy in Ge'ez was not restricted to elites or professional scribes — graffiti on rocks and pottery (often recording personal names of passers-by, owners, or users) suggest wider diffusion, becoming progressively more common in later centuries.

Modern Tigrinya descends from Ge'ez; Amharic is a more distant relative. Ge'ez has evolved considerably since the Aksumite period, so modern liturgical Ge'ez should not be assumed to be identical to the ancient everyday vernacular.

## Greek and Trilingual Inscriptions

Greek was the primary language of international trade in the Aksumite world. Zoscales, the coastal ruler mentioned in the mid-first century CE *Periplus of the Erythraean Sea*, had "a good knowledge of Greek." Aksumite coinage inscriptions were initially exclusively in Greek (from c. AD 270); by the sixth century, silver and copper coin inscriptions shifted to Ge'ez while gold — internationally circulating — retained Greek.

By the mid-fourth century under Ezana, **trilingual inscriptions** in ASAM-derived Ge'ez, Ethiopic Ge'ez, and Greek were erected at the main road-junctions of Aksum. Phillipson's analysis shows the texts were drafted first in Ge'ez and translated into Greek — sometimes with additions amplifying the Christian content for a foreign readership. By the late fifth century, Greek coin inscriptions became garbled (letters omitted, confused, or mis-ordered), indicating that die-engravers were no longer fluent in Greek; the problem was not general illiteracy but specifically linguistic — Ge'ez die-inscriptions from the same period contain far fewer errors.

## Numerical Notation

The Ge'ez system of numerical notation was derived from its Greek counterpart. The earliest known use of Ge'ez numerals is on a third-century stela at Anza (inscription RIE 218); the system is also used in Ezana's fourth-century inscriptions (RIE 185/185bis, 188, 189). Earlier Ge'ez inscriptions (such as the Safra inscription, RIE 183, tentatively dated to the late third century) spell numbers out as words rather than using numerical notation.

## The Ethiopian Calendar

The name of at least one Ethiopian month — **Magabit** — appears in Ezana's inscriptions (RIE 190 and 271), recording that his campaign against the Noba departed on the 8th day of Magabit. The Greek version notes that Magabit was "an Aksumite month" (no attempt to relate it to any other calendar), while the Ge'ez version needs no such explanation — further evidence that the Greek texts were for foreign readers.

## Historiography

The linguistic history of the Aksumite period is complicated by the rarity of extended texts: fewer than twenty stone inscriptions have significant length; coins and pottery graffiti provide additional but limited data. The sole surviving Aksumite manuscript of any substance is the **Abba Garima Gospels** (radiocarbon-dated to the sixth or seventh century CE), kept at a monastery near Adwa. Other Ge'ez texts (Bible translations, etc.) survive only in much later copies.

Whether early Ge'ez was introduced from southern Arabia or was indigenous has been resolved in favour of indigenous origin by modern linguists, but the South Arabian script tradition clearly influenced the ASAM-derived script used in formal inscriptions. Phillipson consistently employs "ASAM-derived" rather than "pseudo-Sabaean" to avoid the implication of inferiority.
