# Caesar's Dictatorship (49–44 BCE)

The autocratic regime through which **[[julius-caesar|Caesar]]**, after his
[[roman-civil-war-49-45bce|civil-war victory]], governed the Roman world — a
patchwork of reform, patronage, and escalating honours that ended in the
**perpetual dictatorship** and his murder. Treated in [[crook-cah-v9-1992|CAH IX]]
Ch. 11 (Rawson), who stresses how **uncertain and disputed** the evidence is.

## The office and its escalation

Caesar held the **dictatorship** in escalating forms — eleven days in 49, then annual,
then (after Thapsus) for ten years, and finally, by February 44, **dictator
*perpetuo*** ("for life"). He also took repeated consulships, the powers of a
tribune, the censorship (*praefectus morum*) for life, and the right to nominate
magistrates — concentrating the Republic's offices in one person. Rawson follows
**Dio** (defending his reliability) on the flood of **honours**: the title
*imperator* and *parens patriae*, statues among the kings and gods, his image on the
coinage (the first living Roman so honoured), a temple to himself and his Clemency,
and Antony as his *flamen*.

## The reforms

Beneath the autocracy lay a genuine, wide-ranging programme:

- **The empire and citizenship.** Latin and Roman rights extended in the West
  (Transpadane Gaul, Gades), generous treatment of Greek cities (tax-collection
  returned to the cities from the *publicani*), and **colonies** for veterans and the
  urban poor across the Mediterranean — including the revival of **Corinth and
  Carthage** — settling perhaps 80,000 abroad.
- **Italy.** Veteran settlement in small, dispersed groups (avoiding Sulla's
  brutality), reorganization of local government (the municipal charters), a much
  **enlarged Senate (900)** drawing in Italian and equestrian men.
- **Rome.** Grand building (the **Forum Iulium** and temple of **Venus Genetrix**, the
  Saepta, the Basilica Iulia, a planned library), the cut of the **grain dole** from
  320,000 to 150,000, the abolition of most **collegia**, and statesmanlike **debt
  legislation** (valuation at pre-war prices; no wholesale cancellation) that
  satisfied neither creditors nor debtors.
- **The calendar.** With the Alexandrian astronomer **Sosigenes**, the reform of the
  Roman calendar to the **Julian** solar year of 365¼ days — his most lasting legacy.

Rawson notes these reforms were mostly **not revolutionary** in content (Cicero asked
for much the same in the *Pro Marcello*) — what was intolerable to the Roman elite was
that they were imposed **autocratically, by edict**.

## The kingship question

The sources and modern scholars divide on whether Caesar **sought the title of king**.
He accepted honours **evoking kingship** (triumphal/regal dress, the golden chair, the
Alban kings' boots), but at the **Lupercalia (15 Feb. 44)** publicly **refused the
diadem** Antony offered, ordering it recorded that he had declined — perhaps coveting
"the glory of refusing." Rawson concludes Cicero did not believe Caesar aimed at the
*name* of king; the deeper offence was ***regnum*** — overweening one-man power
itself — and the prospect of **divinization** (Antony already designated his *flamen*).

## Contested

The **interpretation of Caesar** is itself a historiographical battleground: Mommsen's
visionary democratic monarch; Meyer's would-be Hellenistic god-king (converted by
Cleopatra); the British "brilliant opportunist with no long-term plan"; and the view
of megalomania and mental decay. Rawson's verdict, with the ancients (Seneca, Cicero):
Caesar was driven above all by **glory and the refusal to set bounds to his
pre-eminence** — he "did not put the safety and honour of his country above his own
advantage."
