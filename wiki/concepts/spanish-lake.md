## Definition and Scope

The "Spanish Lake" denotes the claim, more imaginary than legally enforced, that Castile exercised hegemonic sovereignty over the Pacific Ocean from the sixteenth century onward. As Buschmann and Cosano write, this was "a hegemony, more imaginary than real, that Castile exercised over the Pacific" — the phrase itself was a twentieth-century scholarly retrospective, not an Iberian self-description. The concept has been used by historians such as Arif Dirlik to frame the Pacific as passing through successive "lakes" of hegemony: Spanish (16th–17th c.), British (18th–19th c.), and American (19th–20th c.), following O.H.K. Spate's framing of the Pacific as "a European artifact."

The concept captures a real but limited Iberian legal and diplomatic claim, not an operational maritime empire. The Pacific remained, in Spanish scholarly eyes, "a sea of islands that were deemed not urgent to explore."

## Historical Origins

**Balboa and the naming of the ocean.** Vasco Núñez de Balboa crossed the Isthmus of Panama in 1513, becoming "the first European to glimpse the Pacific," which he named *El Mar del Sur* (the South Sea). Ferdinand Magellan's circumnavigation (1519–22) produced the name *El Oceano Pacífico* — "one of the greatest geographic misnomers" since the ocean is far from tranquil — and immediately necessitated a renegotiation of the Treaty of Tordesillas.

**Treaty of Zaragoza (1529).** To settle competing Portuguese and Spanish claims after Magellan's circumnavigation, the Treaty of Zaragoza (Saragossa) extended Tordesillas-style hemispheric division to the Pacific. Most importantly it "ensured that the spice islands of Maluku remained under Portuguese control." The treaty was the first extension of European territorial conceptions into the Pacific, though its line was imprecisely drawn across a largely unexplored ocean.

**The Iberian century of exploration (1511–1606).** Spanish and Portuguese expeditions charted numerous Pacific islands and "established Spanish footholds in the Philippines and a Portuguese presence in East, South, and Southeast Asia." Spanish mariners charted the northern Pacific return route, giving rise to the Manila Galleon exchange — "a route that was to last for 250 years." The galleon connected Manila with Acapulco: "American bullion to the Philippines where it was exchanged for Chinese goods, especially silk and porcelain."

**Philip II's union of crowns (1580).** When Philip II assumed the Portuguese crown after a dynastic crisis, he tried but largely failed to merge the two oceanic empires. Spanish authorities proposed administering Southeast Asian islands from Manila; Portuguese authorities insisted on the Indian Ocean orientation of Melaka and Maluku. The conflict highlighted island Southeast Asia "as a borderland between the Indian Ocean and the Pacific."

## The Concept in Iberian Thought

Iberian cartographers and scholars never used the term "Spanish Lake," but they did construct a distinctive Pacific vision: the ocean was understood not as an entity in itself but as "a sea of islands" whose importance derived entirely from their connection to the colonial edifice of the Americas and the Philippines. "Information on the Pacific is dispersed in the subdivisions of Lima, the Philippines, and New Spain, illustrating once again that this region's principal significance for Spain was through its colonial connections."

This contrasted sharply with the Franco-British Enlightenment vision, which obsessed over imaginary geographic features (Terra Australis Incognita, the Northwest Passage) and framed the Pacific as a zone of scientific discovery. Spanish intellectuals rejected this framing: naval historian Martín Fernández de Navarrete questioned "the historical value of the northern European expeditions to the Pacific since the link to colonial enterprises was missing." Duke Almodóvar mocked the Franco-British expeditions as "ludicrous undertakings," and literary critic Pedro Estala attributed their exotic descriptions to sensory deprivation after long voyages.

## Limits of the Claim

The Spanish Lake was structurally anchored to the Americas. When Latin American independence movements severed that anchor in the 1810s–1820s, the coherence of the concept dissolved. The last Manila galleon sailed in 1815, "signalling a yielding of economic interests in the Pacific to northern European and American players." Spain's Philippine and Micronesian possessions were thereafter conceptualized as remnants devoid of their terrestrial anchor — low on the cognitive pyramid of empire, well below Atlantic colonies Cuba and Puerto Rico.

## Reconceptualization as "Spanish Oceania"

By the second half of the nineteenth century the term *Oceania Hispana* (Spanish Oceania) arose to describe the Philippines and Spanish Micronesia (Marianas, Carolines, Palau). This represented a fundamental shift: the Pacific was no longer conceptualized as a sea subordinate to the Americas but as an island domain in its own right, using the French-derived Oceania/Micronesia/Polynesia geographic vocabulary developed by Dumont d'Urville and others.

The Caroline Islands crisis of 1885 crystallized this new vision. Spanish geographers deployed the argument that effective control of the Marianas implied administration of all Micronesia under an "umbrella designation." Pope Leo XIII adjudicated the Caroline and Palau island chains to Spain. Street demonstrations against Germany erupted in Spain on 4 September 1885, generating a wave of cultural production — plays, musical compositions, books — celebrating Spanish Oceania.

## End of the Spanish Pacific

The Spanish-American War (1898) ended the Spanish Pacific irrevocably. The remaining Marianas and Carolines not claimed by the USA were sold to Germany. "At the end of this conflict, the Spanish vision of the Pacific effectively ended."

## Historiographical Debates

The "Spanish Lake" concept has been critiqued on multiple levels:

- **Dirlik's challenge:** Arif Dirlik argued the successive-lakes framework is a "historical shorthand" that encodes a "Euro-American conceptualization" of the Pacific, marginalizing Asian and Pacific Islander agency.
- **Spate's framework:** O.H.K. Spate, who borrowed and popularized the term from Schurz, argued the Pacific was "integrated into human consciousness through European economic and cartographic imagination" — a framing that critics find Eurocentric.
- **Revisionist "Pacific Worlds" approach:** Ricardo Padrón argues that Spanish cartographers "initiated the concept of a Pacific Rim" and that this projection originated in conceptualizing the Americas — shifting agency somewhat toward Iberian actors. Birgit Tremml-Werner links the Manila Galleon not merely to New Spain but to the "fortunes of China and Japan," foregrounding Asian economic agency. Buschmann and Cosano argue the Iberian vision is "more accommodating to the emerging concept of 'Pacific Worlds'" than the Franco-British framework.
