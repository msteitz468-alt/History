# The Principate (the Augustan settlement)

The system of government by which **[[augustus|Augustus]]** refounded the Roman state
after Actium — a **monarchy clothed in Republican forms**, holding supreme power
through an accumulation of traditional offices and, above all, *auctoritas*. The
foundation of the Roman Empire. Treated in [[bowman-cah-v10-1996|CAH X]] Chs. 2–3
(J. A. Crook).

## Not a single "settlement"

Crook stresses that the Principate was **not the product of a drawing-board** but
arose "incomplete and tentative, out of the vicissitudes of a continuing political
story." There was no single "constitutional settlement of 27 BCE": Augustus himself
speaks of *"my sixth (28) and seventh (27) consulships."* The process was conceived as
a **"steady return to normality"** (*res publica restituta*) after the abnormality of
the triumvirate — "business as usual after alterations."

## The settlement of 28–27 BCE

After the **purge of the Senate** (the *lectio* of 28) and the abolition of the
triumviral acts, on **13 January 27** Augustus, in a staged consular session,
**"transferred the *res publica* from his power into the arbitrament of the Senate and
people."** The Senate's prepared response was to grant him a vast ***provincia*** —
Spain, Gaul, and Syria (plus Egypt), where most of the legions lay — on a ten-year
tenure, while he continued to hold the **consulship** by annual election. His formal
authority was thus "vast, and in its totality un-republican," yet expressed in
**familiar concepts** (sovereignty vested in Senate and people; nothing incompatible
with *mos maiorum*). Three days later he was given the unprecedented name
**"Augustus"** ("of august augury," with a hint of "Romulus"), the **civic crown**, and
the **golden shield** proclaiming his *virtus, clementia, iustitia,* and *pietas*.

## The settlement of 23 BCE

The grave **illness of 23** (Augustus nearly died; his heir **Marcellus** did) forced
a redefinition. Laying down the **consulship** (to free the aristocracy's highest
prize), Augustus instead took two powers that became the **stable formula of imperial
authority**:

- ***tribunicia potestas*** — the powers of a tribune (annual, for life): the right to
  summon the Senate, propose legislation, veto, and the symbolic role of **protector
  of the plebs**; it became the **chronological marker of the reign** and the badge of
  a designated colleague/successor;
- ***imperium proconsulare maius*** — proconsular power **superior to any other
  governor's**, exercised over his *provincia* without lapsing on entry to the
  *pomerium*.

Crook insists this was **not a retreat in a struggle over power** — Augustus' *factual*
power (resting on the armies, the plebs, and the governing class) was never in question
— but the search for the **most acceptable *modus vivendi***: a way to express
overwhelming power within tolerable, traditional limits.

## *Auctoritas* and the paradox

The governing principle, in Augustus' own words (*Res Gestae* 34): *"I excelled all in
**auctoritas** [influence/prestige], but had no more **potestas** than my colleagues in
each magistracy."* The Principate's deep **paradox**, recurring throughout Crook's
account, is the simultaneous effort to **restore the Rome of past glories** and the
rapid growth of **new monarchical concepts and structures** (the temple of Palatine
Apollo beside his house, the imperial cult in the East, the dynastic succession). It
was a regime "founded on the ostensible principle of election to office," yet every
ruler "thought in exclusively dynastic terms about the succession" — Augustus marrying
his blood relations to secure it.

## Contested

The "settlement" and the strength of **senatorial opposition** are debated. Crook
**rejects** the "crisis" reading (championed by Schmitthenner and others) that makes
mounting opposition the key to events down to 23 — that the trials of Primus and the
Caepio–Murena conspiracy nearly toppled the regime and forced a constitutional retreat.
He argues opposition "had no sufficient base of power" to compel Augustus; the driver
was rather **Augustus' own precarious health** (*timor mortis*) and the need to leave
something stable behind. The reality of the Republican consular *imperium maius*, and
the date at which formal autocracy "began," remain disputed.

## OHCW (Stockton; Purcell; Lintott)

**Stockton** (Ch. 22): 28/7 “restoration” + name Augustus; 23 formula (tribunician power for life, *imperium maius*); Agrippa and Maecenas shared the burden; “made haste slowly.” Rejects empty dyarchy: Senate rubber-stamped, yet remained the pool of administrators and the focus of opposition dreams — “freedom under the law” as an ideal transmitted to Europe. Core social judgment: the mass welcomed peace, prosperity, and efficiency more than a few aristocrats’ *libertas*.

**Purcell** (Ch. 23): High Principate (Flavian–Antonine) stability from multi-year revenue planning, provincialization of last client kingdoms, and incorporation of municipal/provincial elites — Rome completing the shift from conqueror to capital of a homogeneous empire. Second century as an “age without history”; crisis of 193 resembles 69 and signals *stability*, not automatic third-century chaos. Government reduces to **jurisdiction and exaction**; only ~100 men held *imperium* at once; *familia Caesaris* and procuratorial posts mark **proximity and elite incorporation**, not modern bureaucracy (“the search for bureaucracy… is vain”; what was required was *litterae*). “Ability to incorporate, not administrative excellence, was Rome’s greatest Art of Government.”

**Lintott** (Ch. 26): under the Principate, People and Senate gradually lost effective decision power to emperor and intimates *in camera*; secrecy bred ignorance and rumour; traditional war/conflict material dried up while aristocratic rivalries centred on **treason trials** — the structural precondition of Tacitean court history.

## Flower, *Roman Republics* (2010)

Flower’s multi-republic map ends with the principate established by Augustus (as Octavian now called himself) in **January 27**, after the second triumvirate (43–33) and a short transition including Actium (31). Within that frame, **Octavian is the next constitution-designer** after the failed Sullan lawgiver path: once Sulla’s system was seen as flawed and unworkable, Romans needed either a new lawgiver or the political will to debate solutions through compromise; “in the event, they did neither, until Octavian seized power by force and designed his own constitution.” Sulla’s example as lawgiver and city-founder set a precedent even for Augustus (Flower cites Suetonius *Aug.* 28: the wish to be *optimi status auctor*). She also notes that Italian countryside disorder and high general violence persisted **until the principate**, despite intermittent “restoration of constitutional government” after the Social and Civil Wars. Source: [[flower-roman-republics-2010]].

## Harris, *Roman Power* (2016)

[[harris-roman-power-2016|Harris]] periodizes Roman power with **AD 16** as a major discontinuity: Tiberius “slowed down the expansion of the Roman Empire” and “more than anyone, finished off the old aristocracy and made the monarchy absolute (not that I would want to minimize the tyrannous nature of the regime of Augustus).” Absolutism “came out into the open with Tiberius”: war and peace, military command, effective choice of consuls, initiation of legislation, connivance in an explosion of treason trials, rule from Capri; he stopped the formality of the Senate renewing powers (Augustus last did so AD 13); he brought a larger part of the praetorian guard into a permanent city camp — Gibbon’s “for ever riveted the fetters of his country”; the *domus Augusta* concept gained wide currency under him. Emperors were “chronically insecure: the life-expectation of a Roman ruler was the shortest of any known monarchy.” Relatively successful generals (Corbulo; Agricola) made emperors nervous, so large wars of conquest preferably had to be led by the emperor in person — “who might like that idea but usually did not.” Corbulo reportedly said in 47 that “in former times Roman generals had been lucky men”; Roman generals would never again be allowed autonomous foreign conquest. Claudius’s claim of conquest “without any losses” showed both imperial lying and that “the Roman people was losing its historic appetite for warfare.”

Harris also stresses ideology and succession: the principate rested on military foundation plus military prestige, quasi-divine status, family continuity, and advertised virtues — yet imperial-cult effect on security is disputed (“They would have done much better… to have imposed sacrifices, like mediaeval cathedral builders”); virtue propaganda from AD 69 may have mostly impressed officials seeking favour. Of 72 Augusti (not minor usurpers) between 14 and 337, **50 died from Roman military actions**; life-expectation was especially low 211–284. ~40 men claimed Augustus in the half-century from 235; only Claudius Gothicus died in bed. Julio-Claudian monarchy plus armed force were vital before assimilation of provincial elites was complete; Tacitus said one-man rule was necessary “in the interest of peace” (*Histories* 1.1); Augustus, Tiberius, and Claudius put Rome on the path to massive incorporation.

## From Gibbon, Decline and Fall (1776–1788)

[[actors/gibbon-edward|Gibbon]]’s definition of the Principate is the opening constitutional thesis of the *Decline and Fall*. **Gibbon’s views.**

- **Absolute monarchy disguised:** the system of Augustus “may be defined an absolute monarchy disguised by the forms of a commonwealth”; masters surround the throne with darkness and profess themselves accountable ministers of the senate.
- **Free constitution lost:** Augustus restored dignity but destroyed independence of the senate — “The principles of a free constitution are irrecoverably lost, when the legislative power is nominated by the executive.”
- **Augustus as subtle tyrant:** “A cool head, an unfeeling heart, and a cowardly disposition”; from age nineteen the mask of hypocrisy never laid aside; virtues and vices artificial.
- **220 years of suspended military danger:** to Commodus’s death, dangers inherent in military government were largely suspended; “the two centuries from Augustus to Commodus passed away unstained with civil blood” (except the short eruption after Nero).
- **World-empire as prison:** when the empire filled the world and fell to one person, “the world became a safe and dreary prison for his enemies” — resist fatal, flight impossible (contrast: divided Europe and liberty).

## Woolf, *Rome: An Empire's Story* (2012)

Woolf’s Chs. IX and XI on how empire produced emperors. Source: [[woolf-rome-empires-story-2012]].

- **Empire before emperors.** “Rome had an empire before it had emperors.” Super-commanders (Sulla, Pompey, Caesar) coordinated the resources and policy empire needed; emperors did so better and imposed peace. The emperor role emerges first in the provinces—“the need for coordination of military power and revenues over great regions was most obvious. Only once it was established there was the controlling power of emperors applied to … the capital. The empire … had saved (and captured) the city.” From Republican chaos Rome “sleepwalked into military autocracy, and it worked.”
- **Monarchy without the name.** Essentials of monarchy present from the start: inner circle of favourites/advisers/secretaries; palace intrigues; central pooling of information/resources; court patronage nexus; hereditary succession (long unacknowledged). Early empire still called Principate (*princeps*); Greeks used *basileus*, Egyptians treated them as pharaohs.
- **Dynastic institutionalization.** Tiberius’ planned accession (AD 14) first stage institutionalizing Augustan charisma. AD 41: dynasty survived Caligula’s assassination; Senate discussion of Republic while Praetorians installed Claudius—“issue never seriously raised again.” After Nero (68) and Year of Four Emperors (69), institutions snapped back; bronze tablet (Dec 69) cites Augustus/Tiberius/Claudius precedents—“as if Senate, equites, people, army, and provinces all felt a need for one man to hold the centre.”
- **Moral empire and Actium.** Civil wars from Gracchi to Actium explained as collective moral failure and corrupting luxury of imperial success; Augustus’ moral rearmament and 27 BCE shield (*virtus*, justice, mercy, *pietas*) plus title Augustus framed Actium as restoring Golden Age track.
- **Theodicy of good fortune.** Weber: powerful powerful by heaven’s will—reassurance that society is just and history meaningful. Octavian as son of deified Caesar; cults of Roma/Roman People; provincial/client-king cults after Actium. “What would have been really innovative was a secular concept of empire.”
