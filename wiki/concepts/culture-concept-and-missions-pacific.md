## Definition and Scope

"Culture" — the concept that human beings are born into, shaped by, and understand the world according to a bounded, plural, relative system of shared meaning — is now so paradigmatic that "it is easy to forget that it has a history" (Gardner, ch. 46, [[hattori-samson-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v2-2023]]). That history runs directly through the Pacific: missionary encounters with Islander societies generated the raw data from which anthropologists assembled the culture concept; and the concept then returned to the Pacific, reshaping both mission practice and decolonizing political movements.

This page traces (a) the pre-cultural missionary vocabulary and its assumptions; (b) the emergence of the anthropological culture concept largely via Pacific fieldwork; (c) the entry of the concept into missiology and its uptake by Pacific Christian communities; and (d) the role of the concept in decolonizing churches and nations.

Three key precepts of the mature culture concept (as operative in the 20th century):
1. **Relativism**: no culture holds absolute truth; truth is culturally determined.
2. **Pluralism**: all cultures are equally valid and should coexist.
3. **Holism and boundedness**: people identify as members of a single defined culture.

## Pre-Cultural Missionary Vocabulary (1688–1870s)

From the Spanish Jesuits who arrived on Guam in 1688 through the Protestant and Catholic missions sweeping Oceania in the nineteenth century, missionaries described Islander lives using terms like "tradition," "custom," "practice," often prefaced with "primitive" or "savage." For English-speaking missionaries at least, "culture" referred to refinement and civilization, "reserved for the higher classes and almost exclusively to Europeans."

Missionaries practiced simultaneous "brothering" and "othering" (Samson's formulation): brothering asserted the essential unity of the human species and justified the missionary project; othering recoiled from specific practices (cannibalism, dancing, sexual customs) while building fundraising narratives for home congregations. These missionaries quickly discovered that conversion did not produce Europeanization: "those who learned to read Bible passages in Islander languages . . . remained steadfastly Tahitian, Fijian, Samoan, or Tongan in their social practices."

## Tylor and the First Definition

E.B. Tylor opened *Primitive Culture* (1871) with a definition that folded customs and practices into a single term:

> "Culture, or civilization, taken in its broad, ethnographic sense, is that complex whole which includes knowledge, belief, art, morals, law, custom, and any other capabilities and habits acquired by man as a member of society."

As Stocking points out, Tylor's "culture" still lacked relativism or pluralism and remained synonymous with civilization. It was another forty years before it began to take on its twentieth-century meaning.

## Boas, Malinowski, Mead: Anthropological Revolution (1880s–1930s)

Franz Boas, after a year among the Inuit of Baffin Island (late 1880s), developed a relativist critique of social evolutionism. He challenged "the obsession with comparing one society to another" and proposed a branching tree of human development in place of a single ladder. His student Ruth Benedict (*Patterns of Culture*, 1934) "untethered culture from history," arguing cultures were of "such infinite variety that their origins and trajectory could not be explained" — described approvingly by Kroeber as "largely 'propaganda for the anthropological attitude.'"

The decisive popularization came via Pacific fieldwork:

- **Malinowski's *Argonauts of the Western Paciﬁc* (1922)**, from fieldwork in the Trobriand Islands (Papua), became a standard anthropological text. His functionalist view held that culture was like an anatomy — an organic whole where each part depended on the other. Any mission-induced change threatened the whole: "missionaries were, by default, a danger to their converts, for change invariably led to decay and/or death." He expressed visceral contempt for missions in his diary: "These people destroy the natives' joy in life; they destroy their psychological raison d'être. And what they give in return is completely beyond the savages."

- **Mead's *Coming of Age in Samoa* (1928)** spread the concept to a mass readership (over a million copies in sixteen languages). Her Samoan case was explicitly polemical — the culture concept deployed against conservative Christian America. She argued that Samoan culture, even after conversion, demonstrated that "culture was sovereign" over biology in shaping adolescent experience.

## Entry into Missiology (1910s–1950s)

The Edinburgh 1910 World Missionary Conference was a turning point. Chinese theologian Cheng Jingyi demanded ecumenism beyond national identity; Asian delegates called for "national" churches reflecting their peoples' characters. Though these demands were rebuffed ("European dominance was paradoxically both the root of the problem and yet also indispensable to the solution," as Stanley observed), the pressure built.

Roland Allen's *Missionary Methods: St Paul's or Ours* (1912) attacked the model of the well-equipped European mission station as a "bunker" that maintained distance from local communities and institutionalized racial paternalism: "the arrogance of the imperial European convinced their race and their methods were essential to success."

From the late 1920s, anthropology entered mission training. Radcliffe-Brown at Sydney admitted missionaries to courses originally designed for colonial officers in Papua and New Guinea (1928). In France, Protestant missionary Maurice Leenhardt returned from New Caledonia to hold key posts in French anthropology, bridging mission and anthropological fieldwork.

The concept entered mainstream theology with H. Richard Niebuhr's *Christ and Culture* (1951), drawing directly on Malinowski and Benedict. Niebuhr's framework — especially the positions "Christ is in culture" and "Christ transforms culture" — gave missionaries theological grounds to accept rather than destroy local practices.

## Pacific Uptake and Decolonizing Churches (1960s–1980s)

The first meeting of Pacific Island churches at the LMS college at Malua, Sāmoa (c. 1961), declared "The Coconut curtain has lifted." Organized by Fijian Setereki Tuilovoni and Tongan Sione Havea (both trained in the USA in networks of third-world Christian decolonization activists), and facilitated by the World Council of Churches, the conference bypassed European mission boards. Solomon Islander Bishop Leonard Alafurai articulated the synthesis: "Christ came and fulfilled all cultures and all people" — cultures were made by God and fulfilled by Christ, not opposed by him.

From this conference grew the Pacific Theological College in Suva (1966), with sociology and anthropology embedded in the curriculum. Divinity theses from 1968–73 tracked the "indigenization" of Christianity: Samoan congregational traditions, Gilbertese worship, Anglican localization in the Solomons.

The conference also produced the Pacific Conference of Churches — "a staunchly political organization which supported decolonizing churches and nations."

## Culture as the Nucleus of New Nations

In the years of decolonization, Paciﬁc clergy and devout Christian politicians moved between pulpit and parliament carrying theologies of enculturation/contextualization:

- **Vanuatu**: Prime Minister Walter Lini was an Anglican priest; five ordained Presbyterian pastors sat in cabinet. The constitution's preamble acknowledged both Christian faith and traditional culture. The Vanuatu Cultural Centre became the umbrella for the National Archives, National Museum, and a fieldworkers unit for protecting culture and heritage.
- **Papua New Guinea**: Catholic priest John Momis was central to the PNG constitution; layman Bernard Narokobi wrote *The Melanesian Way* (1980); lay preacher Peter Kenilorea became first prime minister of the Solomon Islands (1978).
- Virtually all Pacific constitutions begin "with a preamble that acknowledges both the Christian faith of the citizens and their traditions and cultures."

## Critique and Tensions

1. **Emic vs. etic mismatch**: The European culture concept understood Fijian or Samoan life as a distinct, unified system. Pacific Islanders understood custom (*kastom*) as "a more fragmented and dynamic set of practices deployed and developed in response to the challenges of colonialism and everyday life" (Gardner on Tippett's experience in Fiji).

2. **Reification risk**: Malinowski and others were "in danger of fetishizing the purity, fragility, and value of 'pure' Indigenous cultures" — a form of salvage anthropology that could freeze culture as static.

3. **Gender exclusion**: Missions and colonial indirect rule "almost invariably ignored the traditional place of women in the power structures of the Pacific." Pacific churches were "unreflexively paternalistic." Historian Anne Dickson-Waiko argued women in PNG "were only truly colonized following independence" as Indigenous men reified pre-colonial culture in ways that returned women to the village. Pacific women retained influence through Christian fellowship groups in Melanesian villages, where their "everyday modernity" (Douglas's phrase) counterpoints formal male political domination.

4. **Kastom as politics**: The Maasina Rule movement in Malaita (Solomon Islands, late 1940s) — with five of nine leaders from the South Seas Evangelical Mission — deployed custom not as the anthropological concept's "rigid structure of unchanging laws" but as a political tool: "codify customs — keeping those deemed worthy and rejecting those that were not — and to form an Indigenous government structure outside of British colonial control."
