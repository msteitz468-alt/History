## Definition and Scope

Zahirism (Arabic *al-Zahiriyya*, from *zahir* = "evident," "manifest") is a minor school of Sunni Islamic legal theory (*usul al-fiqh*) distinguished by its insistence that legal obligation can derive only from the surface or manifest meaning of the Quran and authenticated hadith. It rejects all forms of independent legal reasoning, including analogical extension (*qiyas*), legal consensus (*ijma'*) beyond the immediate Companions of the Prophet, and interpretation of scripture at any level beyond its evident literal sense.

Zahirism is one of several historical responses to the foundational problem of Islamic jurisprudence: how to extend a religious legal system beyond the explicit provisions of its founding texts. The four dominant Sunni legal schools — Maliki, Shafi'i, Hanafi, and Hanbali — all permitted some degree of analogical reasoning, disagreeing only about its scope and conditions. The Zahiris rejected this consensus.

## Origins and Development

The school takes its name from the approach (not a personal name) of Dawud ibn Khalaf (d. 884 CE), a student of [[shafii-muhammad-ibn-idris|al-Shafi'i]]. Al-Shafi'i had himself sought to systematize and restrict the use of independent judgment (*ra'y*), allowing only constrained reasoning (*qiyas*) and insisting on the primacy of sound hadith. Dawud pushed further, rejecting even this moderate use of analogy in favor of strict literal reading.

The school attracted adherents in the eastern Islamic world but never achieved the political patronage that sustained the four major schools. It survived primarily through the writings of its greatest champion, the Andalusi scholar [[ibn-hazm|Ibn Hazm]] (d. 1064), who produced the most elaborate defense of Zahiri principles and applied them across jurisprudence, theology, and logic.

## Core Principles

**1. Scripture as the sole source of law.** All legal obligations and prohibitions must be grounded in an explicit command or prohibition in the Quran or in an authenticated hadith. Ibn Hazm supported this with Quranic verses asserting the book's comprehensiveness (6:38, 16:89).

**2. Rejection of analogical reasoning (*qiyas*).** To extend a prohibition or obligation by analogy from a stated case to an unstated one introduces human judgment where only divine authority should speak. Ibn Hazm argued that analogy requires identifying the operative "reason" (*illa*) behind a ruling — but this identification is itself an act of independent interpretation inadmissible under Zahiri principles.

**3. Rejection of post-Companion consensus (*ijma'*).** Legal consensus is authoritative only for the immediate Companions of the Prophet; later consensus cannot generate new obligations.

**4. Permissibility by default.** "If God did want us to do something in particular, or want us to avoid doing it, He would have set it down in revelation" (Adamson, ch. 23, paraphrasing Ibn Hazm). Anything not explicitly prohibited or required is therefore permitted but not obligatory.

**5. Epistemological certainty.** Ibn Hazm argued that allowing extended or metaphorical interpretation of scriptural language leads inexorably to skepticism. Literal reading is the only guarantee of juridical certainty. This connects Zahirism to a broader Islamic epistemological anxiety about the conditions of genuine knowledge.

## Paradoxical Effects

Zahirism's extreme textualism could produce results more liberal than the mainstream schools on specific questions, because it drastically narrows the range of actionable prohibitions. Ibn Hazm's analysis of homosexuality is the most cited example: the texts disapprove but do not prescribe capital punishment, and analogical extension from adultery is inadmissible, so the death penalty is ruled out. The mainstream Maliki and Shafi'i schools reached the opposite conclusion through analogy (Adamson, ch. 23).

## Relation to Philosophy and Logic

Ibn Hazm studied logic under teachers connected to the Baghdad Peripatetic school. His legal epistemology reflects this training: just as demonstrative argument in Aristotelian logic must proceed from self-evident premises by valid inference, Zahiri legal reasoning must proceed from explicit scriptural premises by direct application. Both share a commitment to certainty and a suspicion of judgment that falls short of demonstration.

## Historical Fate

Zahirism did not survive as a living legal tradition after the medieval period. The four major Sunni schools consolidated their positions, and Zahiri texts became primarily objects of scholarly interest. Ibn Hazm's works were burned by political enemies in his own lifetime — an act he met with remarkable equanimity, noting in verse that ideas cannot be consumed by fire.

In contemporary Islamic legal discourse, Zahirist arguments occasionally resurface in reformist contexts, because the school's insistence that anything not explicitly prohibited is permitted can serve as a basis for challenging traditional legal restrictions.
