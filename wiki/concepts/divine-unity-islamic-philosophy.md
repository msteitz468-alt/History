## Definition and Scope

*Tawhid* (Arabic: تَوْحِيد) — divine unity or oneness — is the central doctrinal commitment of Islam. In the context of Islamic philosophy, the question shifts from bare assertion to rigorous demonstration: *can the unity of God be proved, and what does "unity" mean in a philosophically precise sense?* This concept page focuses on the philosophical (rather than purely theological) problem of divine unity as elaborated in the tradition running from the Mu'tazilites through Avicenna to Averroes.

The problem has two dimensions: (a) the *numerical* question — why can there not be two or more gods? — and (b) the *internal* question — in what sense is God's essence free from any inner plurality (of attributes, genus/specific difference, matter/form, essence/existence)?

---

## The Positions

### Mu'tazilite Account
The Mu'tazilites held that God has no attributes (sifat) additional to his essence. To say God is "knowing" and "powerful" using the same terms we use for creatures, as if knowledge and power were real additions to his essence, would compromise unity by introducing a plurality of eternal entities (the essence, plus the attribute of knowledge, plus the attribute of power, etc.). They resolved divine epithets by treating them as purely negative: "Knowing" means "not ignorant"; "Powerful" means "not powerless." This move purchases unity at the cost of positive content in theological language.

### Avicenna's Account
Avicenna grounded unity in the concept of the Necessary Existent (*wajib al-wujud*). The key argument: if there were two necessary existents, the species "necessary existence" would be attributed to both. But what makes a thing a necessary existent must be either (i) something peculiar to it (in which case the other cannot share it) or (ii) something it has in common with the other (in which case both are composite — they have a common nature plus something peculiar — and composites are not truly necessary existents). Therefore the necessary existent is unique.

Averroes endorses the logic but criticizes Avicenna's choice of terms: the possible/necessary dichotomy as Avicenna states it ("what has a cause" vs. "what has no cause") is equivocal and generates paradoxes. The correct proof should proceed through genuine possibility (what could be otherwise) rather than the broad Avicennan dichotomy.

### Averroes' Account
Averroes' most distinctive contribution (Discussions 5 and 7 of the *Incoherence of the Incoherence*) is the use of **analogical predication** (*per prius et posterius*) to dissolve apparent generic community between the First and derivative beings. Terms like "existent," "substance," and "intellect" are not univocal across the First and its effects; they are predicated primarily of the First and derivatively of everything else, with the First as their cause. This means no shared univocal genus licenses a composition-argument against the First, while still allowing that the First is genuinely and most fully whatever "existent" or "intellect" signifies.

He reconstructs the proof of unity through a tripartite disjunction: if two supposed necessary existents differed, they would differ (i) numerically — impossible without matter, (ii) specifically — impossible without shared genus hence composition, or (iii) in rank — in which case one is cause of the other, and the First is unique. The first two horns lead to contradiction; the third entails uniqueness.

> "In the third case … the necessary existent will have to be one, and will be the cause of all the separate existents. And this is the truth."

### Ash'arite Account
The Ash'arites accept real attributes as eternal and additional to the essence, but distinct from it. This means God has a kind of internal plurality (essence plus the attributes of knowledge, power, will, life). They deny this implies genuine compositeness or dependency. The philosophical tradition, especially Averroes, argues this position is internally incoherent: an essence that requires attributes for its perfection, where the attributes require the essence as their substratum, is a compound — and compounds are not necessary existents.

## The Five Kinds of Plurality (Philosophical Framework)

Averroes (following the Peripatetic tradition as filtered through Avicenna) distinguishes five kinds of plurality, all of which must be denied of the First:

1. Quantitative division (the First is not a body)
2. Matter/form composition (the First is pure act, no potency)
3. Attribute plurality (knowledge, power, will must reduce to the one essence)
4. Genus/specific-difference composition (no definition in the strict sense applies to the First)
5. Essence/existence duality (the First's quiddity just *is* its existence)

Each denial is defended through arguments about what "necessary existence" entails, and each connects to the broader proof of unity.

---

## The Paradox of the Attributes

The most contested problem within this framework: if God is genuinely knowing, powerful, willing, and living, how are these not additional to his essence? The Peripatetic answer — that these terms are **relational or negative descriptions of a single simple essence**, not names of distinct real attributes — preserves unity but appears to empty theology of positive content.

Averroes' resolution: these terms are *genuinely* predicable of the First, but they describe one and the same essence from different relational or negative vantages. The essence that is pure act, free from all matter and potency, is already (by its very nature) whatever "intellect," "knowing," "living," "powerful," and "willing" refer to — because all those terms, when their imperfect creaturely connotations are stripped away, converge on the same reality: self-subsistent pure actuality that is the cause of order in all things.

## Relation to Divine Simplicity

Divine unity (uniqueness: there is only one God) and divine simplicity (no internal parts or composition) are analytically connected in the philosophical tradition. A genuinely simple first cause cannot be multiplied, because individuation and plurality require matter or compositional differentiators. The proof of unity thus presupposes the proof of simplicity, and vice versa.

See [[divine-simplicity-philosophical]] for the simplicity arguments in detail.

## Historiography

The problem of tawhid in Islamic philosophy has been addressed by: A.J. Arberry, Henry Corbin, Oliver Leaman (*An Introduction to Medieval Islamic Philosophy*, 1985), and more recently Frank Griffel on al-Ghazali. The relationship between the theological tradition (Ash'arites, Mu'tazilites) and the philosophical tradition (Farabi, Avicenna, Averroes) on unity is the subject of ongoing scholarly debate about whether Islamic philosophy is genuinely a philosophical project or a rationalizing theology. Averroes' insistence on returning to Aristotle — against Avicenna's innovations — is a recurrent theme in Dimitri Gutas' work on the Baghdad Aristotelians.

**Collection coverage**: The van den Bergh translation of *Tahafut al-Tahafut* is the main source available. The collection does not currently hold al-Farabi's *Book of Letters*, Avicenna's *Metaphysics of the Shifa*, or the key Ash'arite kalām texts (al-Ghazali's *Iqtisad* or *Ihya*) that would round out this debate.
