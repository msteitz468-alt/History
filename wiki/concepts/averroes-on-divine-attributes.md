## Definition and Scope

Averroes' account of divine attributes is his positive reconstruction — embedded within his point-by-point refutation of al-Ghazali's *Tahafut al-Falasifa* — of how the Islamic Peripatetic philosophers can coherently maintain that God (the First Principle, the Necessary Existent) is genuinely one, genuinely simple, genuinely incorporeal, and genuinely knowing, without those claims implying internal plurality or composition. The account runs across Discussions 4–8 of the *Incoherence of the Incoherence*, where Averroes defends the philosophers' proofs of God's existence, unity, simplicity, and self-knowledge against al-Ghazali's dialectical attacks.

This concept page covers: (1) the two-agent-type argument for God's existence; (2) the proof of unity through the impossibility of two necessary existents; (3) the five-fold denial of plurality; (4) the reductive account of divine attributes as negations and relations; (5) the intellect-identity doctrine (God as pure intellect knowing itself); and (6) Averroes' critique of Avicenna's divergences and his preferred Peripatetic alternatives.

---

## The Agent-Type Argument for God's Existence (Discussion 4)

Averroes opens his response to al-Ghazali's charge that the philosophers cannot prove a creator by distinguishing two kinds of agent:

> "There are two kinds of agent: (1) the agent to which the object which proceeds from it is only attached during the process of its becoming; once this process is finished, the object is not any more in need of it … (2) the agent from which nothing proceeds but an act which has no other existence than its dependence on it."

The second kind — the agent whose object exists only through continuous dependence on the act — is, Averroes insists, the superior kind. The philosophers' God is precisely this second type: an eternal mover whose eternal act produces and conserves the world at every moment. The syllogism is: the world is an act (or depends on an act); every act implies an agent; therefore the world has an agent. Whether that act is temporal or eternal is a further question; what is established is the dependency relation. Against al-Ghazali's charge of self-contradiction (how can an eternal creator create?), Averroes argues that an eternal act issuing from an eternal agent is a *more* perfect form of causation, not a contradiction.

Averroes is also critical of al-Ghazali's (and Avicenna's) use of the possible/necessary distinction as the primary route to the First Principle. He argues that Avicenna's dichotomy — existence into "what has a cause" (the possible) and "what has no cause" (the necessary) — is equivocal and does not by itself prove the impossibility of an infinite series of necessary beings with causes. The demonstrative form should instead show: the series of genuinely *possible* existents cannot regress infinitely (because that would leave the possible without a cause), so it must terminate in a *necessary* cause; and that necessary cause likewise cannot regress in a straight line, so it terminates in what is necessary *by itself*, i.e., the First. Averroes reconstructs this proof, but attributes its best version to the ancient Peripatetics rather than to Avicenna.

## The Proof of God's Unity (Discussion 5)

Al-Ghazali presents two philosophical proofs for God's unity, then tries to refute both. Averroes defends reconstructed versions of each.

**First proof (the species of necessary existence is exclusive).** If there were two necessary existents, both would possess necessary existence. Either they possess it through their own individual nature — in which case it cannot be shared and the two cannot both have it — or through a common nature — in which case each is composed of a common element and a peculiar element, i.e., each is a compound, which contradicts necessity. Averroes endorses the logic of this proof but notes that Avicenna's formulation is loose: the proof really rests on whether the difference between two supposed necessary existents is numerical (placing them in matter), specific (implying a shared genus), or a difference of rank (in which case one is prior to the other, hence its cause, and the two are not co-equal). Only the third option is coherent, and it entails that the necessarily existent is unique as the first term of the ranked series of separated intellects.

> "If there were two necessary existents, the difference between them must consist either in a numerical difference, or in a specific difference, or in rank. In the first case they would agree in species; in the second case in genus, and in both cases the necessary existent would have to be composite. In the third case, however, the necessary existent will have to be one."

**Second proof (duality requires differentiation, differentiation requires composition).** Two things that are the same in every way cannot be two. Two things that differ must differ in something while agreeing in something, which implies composition. Composition in the First Principle is impossible. Averroes accepts the logical skeleton but identifies the error in al-Ghazali's rebuttal: al-Ghazali assumes that two things which are differentiated must share a *univocal* genus, but Averroes insists that many terms ("existent," "substance," "intellect") are predicated *per prius et posterius* (analogically, with one term as first and the others derivative). When a term is analogical rather than univocal, things can be differentiated without implying a common genus in the strict sense, so two supposed "gods" of the same rank would indeed require a shared genus and specific difference — making them composite and temporal — while two intellects of different rank stand in a causal relationship, leading back to a single first.

## The Five-Fold Denial of Plurality (Discussion 5, continued)

Averroes rehearses the five kinds of plurality that the philosophers deny to the First Principle:

1. **Quantitative division** — The First is not a body, so cannot be divided spatially or imaginatively into extended parts.
2. **Qualitative division (matter/form)** — The First is not composed of matter and form. Averroes notes that even the heavenly bodies, contrary to Avicenna, are not composed of matter and form in the ordinary sense; incorruptibility proves absence of ordinary matter. A fortiori the First, which is pure act, admits no such division.
3. **Plurality of attributes** — If knowledge, power, and will were attributes additional to the divine essence, each would be either itself a necessary existent (plurality of necessary existents) or an effect of the essence (introducing composition and conditionality). The Peripatetic solution is that these "attributes" are in reality *the very same essence* described from different relational or negative perspectives. Averroes marks this as "a proof which comes very near to being an absolute truth" for the case of immaterial substance.
4. **Genus/specific-difference plurality** — Definitions require genus plus specific difference; what is simple has no genus-specific difference structure; the First is simple and indefinable except analogically.
5. **Essence/existence plurality** — Averroes sharply criticizes Avicenna for treating existence as an accident additional to quiddity outside the soul. For Averroes (following al-Farabi and what he takes to be Aristotle's genuine view), "existence" is predicated analogically of the categories; in simple substances it is identical with quiddity. The notion that God has a quiddity to which existence is "added" is rejected as generating an infinite regress (if existence is itself an existent, what is its existence?).

## The Reductive Account of Divine Names (Discussion 5)

At the heart of Averroes' account is the claim that all apparent pluralizing attributes of God reduce to three logical categories: **negations**, **relations**, and what is properly the divine **essence itself**. Averroes defends the philosophical reduction that al-Ghazali summarizes and then attacks:

- "First" = relation to all subsequent existents.
- "Principle" = relation to effects.
- "Eternal" = denial of past non-existence; "Everlasting" = denial of future non-existence.
- "Substance" = denial of inherence in a substratum (negation).
- "Necessary existent" = denial of a cause for existence, plus being the cause of everything else (negation combined with relation).
- "Intellect," "Knowing," "Known" = the First is free from matter; everything free from matter *is* intellect, i.e., it thinks its own substance. Knower, knowing, and known are one in the First because the First's self-knowledge is its very essence.
- "Agent," "Creator," "Power" = the First's existence is the source from which the existence of the universe emanates necessarily. Power just *is* the knowledge, and the knowledge just *is* the essence.
- "Will" = the First is not inattentive to what emanates from it and is not opposed to it; it is not compelled from without. "His will is nothing but His very power and His power is nothing but His very knowledge and His knowledge nothing but His very essence."
- "Life" = consciousness of self and of one's acts; in the First, living is again identical with essence.
- "Generosity" = existence emanating from the First for no end referring to itself — a pure negation of interested purpose combined with the relation of emanation.
- "Absolute Good" = negation of imperfection and of the possibility of non-existence; or: being the principle of order in things (relational).
- "Lover and Beloved," "Enjoyer and Enjoyed" = the First possesses the most perfect splendour and perceives it; love and enjoyment indicate self-relation and self-perception, not additional attributes.

Averroes endorses this scheme with one important exception: he disputes al-Ghazali's claim that "intellect" is a *negation* for the philosophers. On the contrary, Averroes insists that intellect (being free from matter and self-thinking) is the *most positive* and most specific appellation for the First's essence in Peripatetic theology, in contrast to Plato who refused to identify the Good with intellect.

## The Intellect-Identity Doctrine and Divine Knowledge (Discussion 6)

The most philosophically dense section of Averroes' defense concerns divine knowledge. He argues the following chain:

1. Forms that are in matter are, as such, not known. When forms are abstracted from matter by intellect, they *become* intellect (known in act). This is why Aristotle says that "intellect is nothing but the forms abstracted from matter."
2. Things that by their nature are already free from matter are therefore, *a fortiori*, always in act as knowledge.
3. The First Principle is entirely free from matter; its existence is pure act with no potency. Therefore it *is* intellect in the highest and most complete sense.
4. The separate intellect's knowing does not follow from the existents it knows (as ours does); rather, the existents follow from it. Its knowledge is the *cause* of the order and arrangement of things, not the effect.
5. Therefore, the First conceives existents by conceiving the harmony and order that exist in them through its own essence. Knowing its essence, it knows everything — because its essence *just is* the order and harmony that all beings participate in.
6. Self-knowledge and knowledge of other things are therefore *not* two different knowings in the First. The First's "knowledge of other things in second intention" (knowing them as effects of its self-knowledge as principle) is not an addition to its self-knowledge; it is the same act described relationally.

Averroes criticizes al-Ghazali's argument that self-knowledge and knowledge-of-others must be different (because one can be imagined without the other). This argument, Averroes says, assimilates the First's intellect to human intellect, which acquires its knowledge from existing things and is therefore always imperfect and in part potential. The First's intellect is categorically different:

> "It is weak thinkers who, not having understood this, ask whether the First Principle thinks its own essence or if it thinks something outside its essence … nothing is more truly free from all imperfection than His intellect."

On Avicenna specifically: Averroes defends Avicenna's position that the First knows universals but not individuals against al-Ghazali's charge of contradiction, but also clarifies that what Avicenna really means is that the First knows all things through a mode of knowledge that is *not* the universal (which is knowledge in potency and imperfect) — it knows in a mode that is more like individual knowledge in its actuality, but that cannot be identified with either human universal or human individual cognition. Human understanding cannot represent this divine unity of knowledge; it stands at the limit of our capacity.

## The Incorporeality of the First and the Genus-Differentia Argument (Discussion 7)

Averroes defends the philosophical thesis that nothing can share the First's genus and be differentiated from it through a specific difference. His key move, repeated several times, is the distinction between:

- **Univocal predication** (same term, same definition): things univocally sharing a genus *do* require compositional differentiation via specific difference; two such supposed gods would be compound and therefore neither necessary nor first.
- **Analogical predication (*per prius et posterius*)**: terms like "existent," "substance," "intellect," "principle" are predicated of a first term and then of derivative terms in order of priority and posteriority. The first term in the analogical series is the *cause* of the attribute's existence in all subsequent terms.

Applied to God: "intellect" is predicated of the First and of the separated intellects not univocally but analogically. The First intellect is the cause of all other intellects' being intellects. There is therefore no univocal genus shared between God and the angels that would require God to be composed of genus plus specific difference. This dissolves al-Ghazali's argument that the philosophers are committed to a composition of generic "intellectuality" shared between God and the first effect.

Averroes also argues that any attempt to posit two "gods" of equal rank faces a fatal dilemma: if they differ individually, they are in matter (impossible for a necessary being); if they differ specifically, the term "god" is predicated of them equivocally — not as genuine gods at all; if one is superior to the other, the superior is the cause of the inferior, and there is only one First Principle. The Qur'anic verse "Were there in both heaven and earth Gods beside God, both surely would have been corrupted" is cited as scriptural confirmation.

## Divine Simplicity and Pure Existence (Discussion 8, opening)

Averroes defends the philosophers' claim that the First is "pure existence" — that its existence stands in relation to no quiddity in the way that existence in other things relates to a separate quiddity. For all other things, quiddity and existence are distinguishable (one can know what a triangle is without knowing whether it exists). For the First, existence is not something added to a prior quiddity; existence *is* its quiddity and constitutes it essentially.

The argument against Avicenna's treatment of existence-as-accident (Discussion 5, fifth kind of plurality) is maintained here: if existence were an accident in the existent, then asking whether this existence itself exists would generate an infinite regress. Existence in the sense of "true" (as the copula or second-intention term) need not be separated from essence in all cases; in simple substances it is identical with the quiddity. Averroes follows al-Farabi here against Avicenna.

## Averroes' Critique of Avicenna

Throughout Discussions 4–8, Averroes distinguishes his position from Avicenna's in several respects:

1. **Possible/Necessary distinction**: Avicenna's division of existence into "what has a cause" and "what has none" is equivocal and does not by itself secure the proof of a First Principle. The correct proof proceeds through genuine possibility, not through this broad dichotomy.
2. **Composition of heavenly bodies**: Avicenna alone among philosophers attributed matter and form to the heavenly bodies; Averroes (with Themistius) insists the heavens are simple and incorruptible, perhaps forms without ordinary matter or possessing "matter" only in an equivocal sense.
3. **Existence as accident**: Avicenna's claim that existence is an accident additional to essence is rejected as a "most erroneous theory." Averroes follows al-Farabi: "existence" as the broad term for entity is predicated analogically, and in the simplest substances it is identical with quiddity.
4. **Divine knowledge of universals**: Avicenna's formula that God knows all things in a universal and timeless way is defended against al-Ghazali as not implying the contradiction Ghazali supposes — but Averroes adds that Avicenna does not fully explain *why* this is not a contradiction, and his own exposition (the intellect-identity doctrine) is more adequate.

## Relation to al-Ghazali and the Theological Tradition

Averroes' final rhetorical point in Discussion 6 is sharp: al-Ghazali's entire philosophical education came from the philosophers he attacks. Al-Ghazali championed logic, used syllogism, wrote treatises modeled on the philosophers, and even claimed to extract logic from the Qur'an. To attack the philosophers' theology while being entirely indebted to their epistemology is, for Averroes, intellectually inconsistent.

At the same time, Averroes acknowledges that the deepest questions about divine knowledge and divine unity are not suitable for general public discourse. He explicitly invokes a doctrine of graduated disclosure: these truths are "poison" for those without philosophical preparation, "nourishment" only for the trained. The *Tahafut al-Tahafut* itself is justified as a corrective medication required because al-Ghazali had already brought the poison into the open.

Averroes also distinguishes his position from the Ash'arite theologians, who accept that God has attributes additional to his essence (knowledge, power, will as real attributes distinct from essence) and from the Mu'tazilites, who deny attributes altogether but whose proof of simplicity Averroes regards as more rigorous than Avicenna's. The correct philosophical position — the genuine Peripatetic one — denies real plurality of attributes because it insists on divine simplicity and identity of essence with its "attributes" understood as relational or negative descriptions.

## Historiography

The *Incoherence of the Incoherence* has been foundational for the question of whether Averroes represents a coherent philosophical theology or merely a dialectical defense. Scholars including Simon van den Bergh (translator), Oliver Leaman, and Deborah Black have argued that Averroes' positive theology — the intellect-identity doctrine, analogical predication, the refusal of existence-as-accident — is philosophically substantive and represents a genuine alternative to both Avicenna and the Ash'arites. Barry Kogan and Alfred Ivry have examined the relationship between Averroes' various treatments of divine knowledge (the *Incoherence*, the long commentaries on Aristotle, and the shorter treatises), finding tensions on the question of whether the First knows particulars. The Latin reception of these arguments — through translations by Michael Scot and others — fed directly into the 13th-century debates at Paris over the unity of the intellect and the eternity of the world.

**Collection coverage**: The van den Bergh translation is the primary source available in the collection. The Bibliotheca Alexandrina holds no separate Averroes commentary volumes; coverage of his positive philosophical theology depends on the *Tahafut al-Tahafut* alone. Avicenna's *Metaphysics of the Shifa* (not in collection) would fill the contrast cases; al-Farabi's *Book of Letters* (referenced by Averroes on the "existent" question) is also not currently held.
