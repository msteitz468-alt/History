# Remarkable Trinity (Wunderliche Dreifaltigkeit)

## Definition — The Verbatim Passage

The trinity appears in the final section (§28) of Book One Chapter One, titled "The
Consequences for Theory." Having traced the logic by which absolute war is modified in
practice, [[clausewitz-carl-von|Clausewitz]] arrives at his most compressed statement of
what war actually is:

> War is more than a true chameleon that slightly adapts its characteristics to the given
> case. As a total phenomenon its dominant tendencies always make war a **paradoxical
> trinity** — composed of primordial violence, hatred, and enmity, which are to be
> regarded as a blind natural force; of the play of chance and probability within which
> the creative spirit is free to roam; and of its element of subordination, as an
> instrument of policy, which makes it subject to reason alone.

The three tendencies are immediately given social correlates:

> The first of these three aspects mainly concerns **the people**; the second **the
> commander and his army**; the third **the government**. The passions that are to be
> kindled in war must already be inherent in the people; the scope which the play of
> courage and talent will enjoy in the realm of probability and chance depends on the
> particular character of the commander and the army; but the political aims are the
> business of government alone.

## The Three Elements

### 1. Primordial Violence, Hatred, and Enmity — the People

The first element is the pre-rational, affective force that war draws upon from the
population. It is a "blind natural force" — it cannot be legislated away or logically
bounded. In Chapter One §3, Clausewitz explains why even sophisticated states cannot
escape it: "If war is an act of force, the emotions cannot fail to be involved. War may
not spring from them, but they will still affect it to some degree, and the extent to
which they do so will depend not on the level of civilization but on how important the
conflicting interests are and on how long their conflict lasts." Primitive passion is the
energetic substrate that makes wars more than board games.

### 2. Chance and Probability — the Commander and Army

The second element is the domain of the military professional: the space where skill,
courage, *coup d'oeil*, and genius operate. War "always lasts long enough for influence to
be exerted on the goal and for its own course to be changed in one way or another" (§23).
The creative spirit is "free to roam" within this space precisely because chance — not
rational calculation alone — governs individual military outcomes. Clausewitz calls war
"a gamble": "In the whole range of human activities, war most closely resembles a game of
cards" (§21). Yet the competent commander does not merely submit to chance; he exploits
it through coup d'oeil — the "quick recognition of a truth that the mind would ordinarily
miss or would perceive only after long study and reflection" (Chapter Three).

### 3. Subordination as Instrument of Policy — the Government

The third element is rational purpose: the political object that "was the original motive"
and that "will reassert itself" as the logic of extremes loses force (§11). This is the
element associated with governments and statesmen. The subordination of war to reason is
what prevents war from becoming an autonomous force that "usurp[s] the place of policy"
(§23). It is also, as Clausewitz stresses, the *most precarious* of the three — easiest
to neglect in the heat of operations.

## Why "Wunderliche"?

The German *wunderlich* means remarkable, strange, or wonderful — carrying a sense of
genuine wonder at something paradoxical. The trinity is "wunderlich" because the three
forces are simultaneously interdependent and potentially in tension with one another.
Clausewitz writes: "These three tendencies are like three different codes of law,
deep-rooted in their subject and yet variable in their relationship to one another." The
strangeness lies in this variability: no fixed hierarchy governs them. In a dynastic
cabinet war the governmental (rational) element dominates; in a people's war like the
Napoleonic conflicts the first element (passion) threatens to overwhelm the third.

## Theory's Task: Maintain the Balance

The implications for theory are explicit. A theory "that ignores any one of them or seeks
to fix an arbitrary relationship between them would conflict with reality to such an
extent that for this reason alone it would be totally useless." The task of theory is
therefore demanding: "to develop a theory that maintains a balance between these three
tendencies, like an object suspended between three magnets."

This image — three magnets exerting pulls in different directions — is crucial. There is
no resting equilibrium; any particular war will be pulled toward one pole or another
depending on its character. The analytical job is to identify *which* tendencies dominate
in each case.

## Relation to Absolute vs. Real War

The trinity is the conceptual resolution of the tension between [[absolute-vs-real-war]].
Absolute war — war driven to the logical extreme by reciprocal escalation — would exist
only if the third element (rational policy) perfectly dominated the other two, dictating
maximum effort, *and* if the second element (chance and friction) were absent. In practice,
the first element (popular passion) is never a pure rational instrument, and the second
element (friction, chance) constantly disrupts the efficient translation of political
intent into military outcome. The trinity explains *why* real war always falls short of
the absolute: the three forces are never in perfect alignment.

## Relation to War as Continuation of Policy

The famous dictum — "war is not merely an act of policy but a true political instrument,
a continuation of political intercourse, carried on with other means" (§24) — is often
read as asserting that policy is the master, violence merely its servant. The trinity
complicates this reading. Policy (the government's rational aim) is *one* of three forces,
not the master of the other two. The political aim "must adapt itself to its chosen means,
a process which can radically change it." Clausewitz adds: "the political aim is a tyrant.
It must adapt itself to its chosen means" — the phrase makes clear that political purpose
is constrained by the "grammar of war," not unconstrained over it. See
[[war-as-continuation-of-policy]] for the full argument.

## Contested Interpretations

The trinity has generated sharply divergent readings:

**Policy-primacy reading.** Many readers — including Moltke's critics, Cold War nuclear
strategists, and some political scientists — read the trinity as confirming civilian
supremacy: the government sets the aim, the military executes. Michael Howard notes
(introduction) that Clausewitz intended, had he lived to complete the revision, to give
this element "the most emphasis." On this reading the trinity is ultimately hierarchical:
reason supervises passion and chance.

**Genuine three-way tension reading.** A competing view, supported by the "three magnets"
image and by Clausewitz's warning against fixing any "arbitrary relationship," holds that
the trinity describes an irreducible tension. No element permanently dominates. Popular
passion can outrun governmental intention (revolutionary and total war); military chance
can nullify political planning (Clausewitz's own friction argument); and governments can
misread both. On this reading the trinity is inherently destabilising — a warning as much
as a model.

**Marxist and Leninist appropriations.** The 1984 editors' note mentions the Marxist
interpretation, which tended to read the first element (popular passion) as the primary
driver, making war an expression of class antagonism that governments merely organise.
This inverts the policy-primacy reading.

**Liddell Hart's misreading.** Howard (introduction) notes that critics such as Liddell
Hart read the unrevised Books as endorsing unlimited battle and annihilation as ends in
themselves, ignoring the trinity's insistence that even "absolute" drives are checked by
the other two elements.
