# Childhood in Merovingian Gaul

**How a post-Roman society marked the stages of growing up — and dying young.** Émilie Perez's
chapter (Oxford Handbook, Ch. 9) reconstructs Merovingian childhood from the convergence of texts,
grave goods, and bone, anchored in her study of **315 children from four northern-Gallic cemeteries**
(Cutry, Saint-Sauveur, Goudelancourt-lès-Pierrepont, Saint-Martin-de-Fontenay, 6th–8th c.). Age and
gender, she argues, "appear to have been essential to the social construction of identity in
childhood."

## Definition and Scope

Children are "poorly represented in contemporary texts and pictures, and… underrepresented in
archaeological remains." A known phenomenon of **underrepresentation** — too few infants in
collective cemeteries given expected preindustrial mortality — is explained partly by selective
burial: baptism at birth "does not seem to have become a widespread ritual habit in Gaul" before the
seventh century, and rules governing access to cemeteries developed only by the tenth.

## Social Age Groups and Legal Thresholds

Perez adapts a four-group scheme: **0–2** (high mortality), **3–7** (age 7 the first moral threshold;
children "irresponsible, fragile, and dependent"), **8–12** (approaching puberty), and **13–17**
(legal adulthood, with adult rights and risks — marriage, inheritance, childbirth, fighting). The
law fixed these stages: under [[salic-law|Salic Law]] a newborn's wergeld rose at the **ninth day**
when it received a name (PLS 24.6); the age of majority was **twelve** in the *Pactus Legis Salicae*,
fourteen in the *Lex Visigothorum*, and fifteen in the *Lex Ribuaria* and *Lex Burgundionum*.

## Grave Goods, Gender, and Weapons

Children were furnished nearly as often as adults (52% vs. 58%), peaking at ages 8–12. Jewellery —
mostly glass beads — formed a third of children's deposits; weapons appeared in only 6% of children's
graves. Gendering tracked the adult pattern (see [[gender-in-merovingian-gaul]]): juvenile burials
were 77% neutral, 23% feminine, with feminine goods peaking (42%) among 13–17-year-old girls.
Boys' masculine display was strongest at **8–12**: at Cutry, axes, spears, and arrows appear from
age 4, and **child-sized weapons** (scramasaxes under 25 cm) "may refer to their family's aspirations
for their future identity… the warriors they would have become had they lived." Written sources
confirm an aristocratic military apprenticeship — hunting, horsemanship, weapons — with boys sent to
uncles or the royal court to be "nourished."

## Amber, Amulets, and the Marginal Infant

Amber (prized as protective and against infertility) and amulets — shells, fossils, animal teeth,
rock crystal, bells — were **more common in children's graves than adults'**, growing more varied
from age eight; adolescents averaged seven amber beads. Bishops condemned amber's apotropaic use:
Eligius of Noyon warned "let no woman suspend amber on her neck… invoking Minerva." Infants under one
were "marginal beings": dying before being named and integrated, they "may have received a distinct
funerary treatment like burial outside of the collective cemetery" — in "wells, pits, abandoned
houses, or along roads."

## Historiography

Perez's quantitative, osteologically-grounded method exemplifies the post-1980 turn of
[[merovingian-archaeology]] toward bioarchaeology and the social sciences (see
[[merovingian-bioarchaeology]]). The work cross-checks artifact-based sexing against osteological
sex and reinforces Halsall's life-cycle model, while emphasizing that in the countryside
"physiological development and growth… played a more important role than chronological age in the
medieval conception of childhood."
