# Oikos (the Greek Household)

The **oikos** was the basic unit of classical Greek society: the household understood
as the combination of people (kin, dependants, and slaves), property, and the physical
dwelling. It was simultaneously the fundamental economic unit of production and
consumption and the primary site of private life, standing in a defining tension with
the public sphere of the [[polis]]. Material here draws on the "Private life" chapter
of [[osborne-classical-greece-2000|Osborne (ed.), Classical Greece 500–323 BC]].

## Emic vs Etic

Emic: *oikos* is a Greek actors' category central to law (inheritance, the epikleros),
economy, and religion. Etic: modern historians use it to reconstruct gender, sexuality,
childhood, and domestic economy from fragmentary and male-authored evidence.

## Core Features

**The household economy.** The oikos was the primary locus of wealth acquisition:
Aristotle's *Politics* Book One builds the oikos — with its family, land, property,
and slaves — as the basic unit of the polis economy (see [[classical-greek-economy]]).
Wealthy Athenian estates combined diverse income-earning assets — scattered farms and
houses, some owned and some rented out, income-earning and domestic slaves,
furniture, and money lent at interest — an "opportunistic" rather than systematically
planned portfolio, reflecting piecemeal acquisition over time (through debt security,
inheritance, and the like) rather than rational estate management. Some wealthy
households integrated slave manufacturing directly into the family's accounts (the
elder Demosthenes' sword and couch workshops, each employing several dozen slaves),
while other owners had slaves "living apart," paying a fixed daily rent to the owner
while working for a third-party manager. Household demand for purchased goods was
structurally limited by the ideal of self-sufficiency (*autarkeia*) and by modest
housing even among the wealthy; "consumer durables" as a category were largely
absent, their place taken by slaves as both status symbols and labor-saving devices.
Autarkeia was nonetheless bound up with the market rather than opposed to it: all
cultivators aimed at self-sufficiency, which paradoxically required producing a
marketable surplus for cash needed for taxes, liturgies, and goods that could not be
made at home. Conspicuous consumption was ideologically fraught in democratic
Athens: litigants in court routinely downplayed their own wealth, while Theophrastus's
comic *Characters* caricatures figures who flaunt petty luxury as socially deviant.
Credit within the oikos and its citizen network operated on a reciprocity ideology
distinct from interest-bearing commercial loans: *eranos* loans were interest-free
contributions pooled from friends and neighbors for someone in need (ransom, funeral,
dowry, a liturgy payment), and failure to reciprocate such help was treated in court
rhetoric as a serious character flaw.

**The house and the polis/oikos boundary.** Excavated Greek houses were typically
small, unimpressive mud-brick structures on stone footings with beaten-earth floors,
in visual contrast to the monumental temples of the polis — producing, in James
Davidson's reading, a graphic public image of civic magnificence set against private
modesty. From the mid-fifth century BCE the house or palace became the standard
backdrop of Attic tragedy, used to dramatize the tension between the loyalties of
polis and oikos and between public façade and private (often female) character —
Euripides' Medea is reasonable in public but reveals her true ferocity indoors.
Athenian houses afforded privacy through few street-facing windows and an internal
courtyard for light; the *andron* ("men's room"), a small room with an off-centre
doorway and a raised platform for around seven couches, hosted the symposium and was
often more richly decorated than other rooms, while positioned close enough to the
street that passersby could hear the party.

**Marriage and children.** Marriages were arranged by parents, with betrothal
(*enguê*) sometimes agreed years before the wedding while the girl was still a
child; brides — especially heiresses — might marry as young as twelve or thirteen,
while husbands were typically in their late twenties or older, so a new bride
commonly entered a household headed by an older, often widowed mother-in-law. At
betrothal the bride's father spoke a formula giving his daughter "for the ploughing
of legitimate children," reflecting that marriage was conceived primarily as a
reproductive alliance rather than a romantic union. The wedding itself centred on an
evening procession leading the bride to her new home, and on the *anakalypteria*
(unveiling of the bride at the banquet, followed by "unveiling gifts"), a moment of
erotic and symbolic charge marking the groom's first sight of her face; a Boeotian
custom burned the bridal cart's axle to mark the finality of the move. Divorce could
in theory be initiated by husband, wife, or the wife's father. A dowry had to be
returned if a marriage ended without issue, including when a husband divorced an
adulterous wife — creating a perverse incentive under which wronged husbands
sometimes accepted a bribe ("lucrative silence") rather than prosecute; Draconian law
permitted killing an adulterer caught in the act, though such a killer could still
face a murder charge from the victim's family. Athenian law required an heiress
(*epiklêros*), on her father's death, to marry his closest available male relative to
keep property intact, even at the cost of dissolving a current marriage. Child
mortality was high (roughly a third of children at Olynthus died before puberty)
despite high fertility from early female marriage age; Greek demographic anxiety
centred less on overpopulation than on *oliganthropia* (population shortage) —
Aristotle attributed Sparta's failure to recover from its defeat at Leuctra (371 BCE)
partly to this. Sparta enacted pro-natalist measures (confirmed bachelors stripped of
citizen rights; fathers of three or more sons exempted from military and financial
obligations), and oracle consultations widely reflect anxiety about childlessness
rather than about excess births. Children were needed to continue the oikos, to
support aging parents, and to honor them in death through tomb libations and
ribbon-tying — Electra's offerings at Agamemnon's tomb in Aeschylus's *Choephori*
exemplify the practice, and women predominate as grave-visitors in vase painting.
Athenian sons were legally obliged to feed, house, and tend the graves of parents,
enforceable by lawsuit, and dishonoring parents carried disqualification from
citizenship and public office — though no actual prosecutions for such abuse are
attested; conversely a father with surviving children could not legally disinherit
them by will.

**Women, the body, and dress.** Respectable Athenian women were idealized as staying
indoors and cultivating pale skin, sometimes with a toxic lead-based white powder;
women's public appearances, at festivals or funerals, were regarded as morally risky
"moments of danger," and some scholars argue that full female seclusion was in
practice a luxury only wealthy households could afford, since poorer families needed
women's labor outside the home — a point Aristotle makes explicitly in linking the
supervisory magistracy of *gynaikonomoi* to aristocratic rather than democratic
government. Citizen women caught indecently exposed in public (*akosmousai*) were
liable to a fine. The body itself carried ideological weight: outdoor nakedness and a
sun-tanned physique from gymnasium exercise or farm labor marked free Greek
masculinity, while a pale, sedentary physique was coded as barbarian, artisan, or
womanish, a contrast visually reproduced in black-figure vase painting (men dark,
women pale). Clothing carried similar signals: the simple, unshaped drapery of Greek
dress (*chiton*, *himation*) was set ideologically against the tailored trousers and
turbans of "barbarians," so that wearing Persian dress (the Spartan regent Pausanias;
later Alexander the Great) signified oriental despotism and luxury; the modest,
poverty-coded *tribon* cloak contrasted with the wealth-signaling *chlanis*, and
wealthy Athenians were sometimes suspected of disguising taxable wealth by wearing a
shabby cloak over a fine chiton. Spinning and weaving were coded women's work with
deep symbolic resonance — a wool fillet was hung on the door at a daughter's birth,
and women wove the annual robe (*peplos*) for the cult statue of Athena Polias.

**Slaves within the oikos.** Household slaves were incorporated into the oikos
through the same welcoming ritual used for a new bride, and often participated in
household sacrifices; some maidservants formed close, intimate relationships with
their mistresses. Athenian household slaves were notorious for a relative lack of
deference, which the "Old Oligarch" attributes to economic logic — slaves working in
businesses outside the household were a revenue source that would suffer if they were
too fearful to press for payment — though real brutality toward slaves persisted even
in democratic Athens; the master–slave bond is described in the source as an unequal
but sometimes affectionate alliance. Mass enslavement typically followed the fall of a
Greek city, meaning Greeks themselves (not only Persians or other non-Greeks) could be
enslaved, though the bulk of Athens's servile population came from Thrace, Anatolia,
and Syria rather than from Greece itself. Ancient population estimates for slaves are
unreliable and likely inflated (a late-fourth-century census attributed to Demetrius
of Phalerum reported a very large slave population at Athens, a figure modern
scholars regard with considerable skepticism even at a fraction of its stated size);
slaves were not visually distinguished by skin color, though escaped slaves might be
tattooed on the forehead. Chattel slavery at Athens — individually owned and
saleable — is distinguished from more "national"/serf-like systems such as the
Spartan [[slavery|helots]] (see [[sparta]]), in which an entire conquered population
lived as hereditary agricultural serfs bound to the land, releasable only by the
state, and paying a fixed produce-share to an individual master under threat of a
curse if the master demanded more than his due.

**Symposium, friendship, and sexuality.** Shared eating and drinking created and
marked social bonds across register: sacrifice sealed marriage and incorporated wives
and children into the citizen phratries at the Apatouria; Spartan mess membership
(paid in barley, wine, cheese, and figs) was a requirement of citizenship; and
interstate truces were themselves called "libations" (*spondai*). At the symposium,
held in the *andron*, hired women (*mistharnousai*) performed music and dance and
were assumed sexually available, while *hetaeras* — distinct from brothel prostitutes
(*pornai*) — lived in ongoing, sometimes monogamous mistress-relationships with
citizen men and received "gifts" rather than a fixed fee, a socially important but
"slippery" distinction. Greek friendship (*philia*) was itself constituted through
reciprocal favors and gifts rather than pure sentiment, functioning as an economic,
social, political, and judicial resource. Greek male–male sexuality is presented in
the source as a genuinely contested subject among modern scholars — theories range
from a role/power-based "pseudo-sexuality" distinct from gender-object choice, to a
reaction to female seclusion, to a descendant of archaic initiation ritual — with the
evidentiary caveat that no sexual acts were illegal and no inquisitorial apparatus
investigated private sexual conduct in the Greek world (unlike later Christian
states). The *erastes*/*eromenos* (admirer and admired youth) relationship between
citizen males could range from distant courtship to sexual relationship; Xenophon's
*Constitution of the Spartans* claims the Spartan version was as innocent as a
parent–child bond — while acknowledging contemporaries found this hard to believe.

**Household religion.** The oikos was a locus of cult as well as economy: household
sacrifice incorporated slaves and new brides alike, and the family maintained its own
ritual obligations (tomb cult for the dead, the rites tying children into the
phratry) alongside the polis's public religious calendar (see [[polis-religion]]).

## Limitations and Critiques

The evidence is overwhelmingly Athenian, elite, and male-authored; generalizing from
it to "the Greek household" is contested. Much of it derives from law-court oratory
composed to persuade a jury, from comedy and moralizing philosophy, and from
normative sources (laws, rituals, moral precepts) that describe an ideal rather than
straightforward behavior — a methodological caution the source itself stresses:
generalizations about other cities or peoples (Spartan drinking customs, Milesian
women's modesty, Persian "luxury") are often shaped by ideological motive and must be
checked against structural regularities (geography, technology, education) and
against language's non-neutral classifying role. Claims about full female seclusion,
in particular, are debated as possibly describing an elite ideal rather than a
universal practice, since poorer households needed women's labor outside the home.
Several numerical figures reported in the source (slave population totals, specific
fines) are flagged as OCR-damaged in extraction and are given here only in hedged,
approximate form.
