# Late Antique Literary Culture

The literary and educational world of the fourth and early fifth centuries — the **"golden age" of
Christian literature** built on, and in tension with, the classical **rhetorical education**, and the
emergence of new **eastern Christian literatures** (Syriac, Coptic). Treated in [[cameron-cah-v13-1998|CAH
XIII]] Chs. 22 (**Averil Cameron**, education & literary culture), 23a (**Sebastian Brock**, Syriac), and
23b (**M. Smith**, Coptic).

## Christianity and the classical inheritance

Cameron's organizing tension: state-supported Christianity turned a "minor element" of Roman culture into
a "highly disturbing factor," yet the **only education generally available remained the traditional
rhetorical curriculum**, exclusively classical in content. So **every great Father was trained in pagan
rhetoric** and had to "come to terms somehow with classical rhetoric" — Jerome's famous dream of being
accused of being "a Ciceronian, not a Christian"; Basil's treatise on the **judicious reading** of the
classics by young Christians; Augustine the ex-rhetor whose *City of God* is steeped in Sallust, Cicero
and Virgil; John Chrysostom the pupil of the pagan **Libanius**. Against this stood the counter-ideal of
the ***sermo piscatorius*** — the "language of fishermen," the "true simplicity" of scripture against the
"tricks" of rhetoric — and the ascetic rejection of learning dramatized in the **Life of Antony**. The
result was a "golden age": in Greek, **Athanasius, the Cappadocians (Basil, Gregory of Nyssa, Gregory of
Nazianzus), John Chrysostom**; in Latin, **Ambrose, Jerome, Augustine** — figures Cameron insists cannot be
quarantined as "patristics" apart from cultural history.

## "Hellene" = "pagan": culture and the construction of the other

A momentous **semantic shift**: in Christian Greek, **"Hellene"/"Hellenic" came to mean simply
"pagan"/"paganism"** — fusing "Greek culture" with "the old religion," with deep consequences "for those
Christians busy writing and praying in Greek." Cameron stresses (with her *Rhetoric of Empire*) that
Christian writers deployed the **constructed images of "pagans," heretics and Jews** as a "culture of the
other," and that — far from a settled triumph — **"Christianization proceeded as much by ambiguity and
cultural appropriation as by direct confrontation"** (aligning with [[christianization-of-the-roman-empire|Brown's
"wavering century"]]). [[julian|Julian's]] law barring Christians from teaching the classics provoked
exactly this anxiety (Gregory of Nazianzus' passionate defence of Christian access to the classical
heritage).

## The new eastern literatures

The period's other great development is the **rise of Christian literatures in eastern languages** — a
shift of cultural gravity eastward accompanying Constantinople and the Holy Land pilgrimage:

- **Syriac** (Brock): a "third culture" alongside Greek and Latin, heir to a "threefold inheritance," with
  its own genres and a vigorous two-way **translation traffic with Greek**; its towering figure
  **Ephrem the Syrian** (the great hymnographer), centred on **Edessa** and **Nisibis**.
- **Coptic** (Smith): the Christian literature of Egypt — biblical and apocryphal texts, **monastic texts
  and martyrologies** (the desert tradition), the **Nag Hammadi library** of Gnostic tractates, and
  **Manichaean writings** ([[manichaeism|Manichaeism]] having spread into Egypt).

## Significance

This chapter set supplies the wiki's account of the **cultural transformation** that accompanied the
political and religious changes of CAH XIII: the **fusion and friction of classical and Christian
learning** (every Father a rhetor, yet the *sermo piscatorius* exalted), the freighting of "Hellenism"
with "paganism," and the **diversification of Christian culture into new languages** that would outlast the
empire (and, in Syriac and Coptic, survive the Arab conquest). Cameron's thesis — that Christianization
worked through **appropriation and ambiguity**, not just confrontation — makes literary culture a central
exhibit in the volume's anti-triumphalist reading of late antiquity.
