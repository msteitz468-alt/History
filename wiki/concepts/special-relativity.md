# Special Relativity

**1905**. Albert Einstein’s theory that the laws of physics (including the constancy of the speed of light) are the same for all observers in inertial (constant-velocity) reference frames; there is no absolute rest or absolute time; simultaneity, duration, and length are relative to the observer’s motion; the speed of light *c* is invariant.

## Definition and Scope

The theory applies only to the “special case” of uniform rectilinear motion (inertial frames), not accelerated motion or gravity (see general relativity). Core postulates:

1. **Principle of relativity**: The laws of mechanics and electrodynamics are identical in all inertial reference systems. “The phenomena of electrodynamics as well as of mechanics possess no properties corresponding to the idea of absolute rest.”

2. **Constancy of the speed of light**: Light in vacuum propagates at a constant velocity *c* (~186,000 miles/sec) independent of the motion of the emitting body or the observer. “Light always propagates in empty space with a definite velocity V that is independent of the state of motion of the emitting body.”

Consequences include relativity of simultaneity (two events simultaneous for one observer need not be for another moving relative to him), time dilation, length contraction, relativity of mass, and the mass-energy equivalence E = mc² (derived in a follow-up paper).

## Intellectual Genesis (from Isaacson range)

Einstein’s path began at age 16 with the thought experiment of riding alongside a light beam: a frozen electromagnetic wave seemed impossible (“There seems to be no such thing...”). The asymmetry in magnet-and-coil induction (different theoretical accounts depending on which moves) troubled him; Föppl’s book posed the same question. The Michelson-Morley null results (no detectable ether) were taken for granted rather than being the starting point; Einstein sought a universal formal principle.

He rejected an emission theory (light speed relative to source) in favor of the light postulate after struggling with Maxwell’s equations. The key insight (“An analysis of the concept of time was my solution”) came in a May 1905 conversation with Michele Besso: simultaneity is not absolute. “Two events which, viewed from a system of coordinates, are simultaneous, can no longer be looked upon as simultaneous events when envisaged from a system which is in motion relative to that system.”

The paper uses vivid operational definitions (train/embankment lightning strikes; rod measurements; light clocks) and declares the ether “superfluous.” No citations; presented deductively from postulates. “Let me note that my friend and colleague M. Besso steadfastly stood by me...”

## Key Distinctions

- Not “everything is relative”: spacetime intervals and *c* are invariants. Einstein briefly considered “Invariance Theory.”
- Distinguishes uniform motion (special) from acceleration/gravity (general).
- Preserves causality and the maximum speed of information (no faster-than-light signaling).

## Interaction with Other Concepts

Enabled by Maxwell’s field equations (wave speed *c*) and Galileo’s ship principle (extended to electromagnetism). Contrasted with Newtonian absolute time/space (“Absolute, true, and mathematical time... flows equably without relation to anything external”). Contributed to quantum debates indirectly by undermining classical determinism in combination with light-quanta work. Later reconciled with gravity via general relativity (1915).

## Historiography

Einstein stressed the deductive, postulate-driven method over induction from specific experiments. Historians (Holton, Miller, Norton) trace influences of Hume (no absolute concepts without observation), Mach (critique of Newton’s absolutes), Poincaré (relativity principle), Lorentz-FitzGerald contraction, and technological context (Bern clock synchronization patents, electrical signals). The paper’s accessibility (high-school math) aided rapid spread once Planck and others engaged it. Isaacson notes the personal backdrop: patent office, trains past Bern clock tower, Besso collaboration.

**Grounding quotes (Isaacson)**: “If I pursue a beam of light with the velocity c... I should observe such a beam of light as an electromagnetic field at rest though spatially oscillating.” “The idea that these two cases [magnet moving vs. coil moving] should essentially be different was unbearable to me.” “Time cannot be absolutely defined, and there is an inseparable relation between time and signal velocity.” Train/embankment simultaneity example; “Our judgments in which time plays a part are always judgments of simultaneous events.”
