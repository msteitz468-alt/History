# Japanese Buddhism

The form taken in [[japan|Japan]] by the [[buddhism|Buddhist]] religion — imported from
the Asian mainland in the sixth century, adopted as an instrument of state and high
civilization, and developed over the centuries into a series of distinctively Japanese
schools. Alongside indigenous [[shinto|Shintō]], Buddhism is one of the two pillars of
Japanese religious life. This page covers its arrival through the [[heian-period|Heian]]
era; the [[heian-period|Kamakura]]-era popular sects (Pure Land, Nichiren, Zen) follow in
Cycle 2.

## Arrival and the Soga victory

Buddhism reached the [[yamato-japan|Yamato]] court as a **diplomatic gift from the Korean
kingdom of Paekche** (traditionally **538 or 552 CE**) — an image of the Buddha, sutras,
and a letter commending "amongst all doctrines the most excellent." It split the court:
the **Soga** clan championed it; the **Mononobe** and **Nakatomi** defended the native
*kami*. The Soga's military victory (**587**) settled the matter — and, Mason and Caiger
argue, marked the triumph of **bureaucratic "new men" over clans of divine descent**, so
that Buddhism "aided the centralization of political authority." [[shotoku-taishi|Prince
Shōtoku]] then became its great early patron, founding temples (the **Hōryūji**, the
world's oldest surviving wooden temple complex).

## Doctrine

Imported already highly developed (through China, from Indian roots), Buddhism taught that
all beings are trapped in an endless cycle of rebirth governed by ***karma***; that
existence is inherently **suffering**; and that release (*nirvana*) comes by extinguishing
selfish craving through moral conduct, meditation, and wisdom. In Shōtoku's day this was
pursued as **individual monastic effort**. Japanese Buddhism became overwhelmingly
**Mahāyāna** ("Great Vehicle"), eclipsing the earlier Nara "Hīnayāna" schools.

## Nara state Buddhism

In the [[nara-period|Nara period]] Buddhism became a **department of state-building**.
**Emperor Shōmu** made the **Tōdaiji** and its colossal bronze Buddha (dedicated 752) the
center of a nationwide system of **provincial temples** meant to focus the realm on the
welfare of the state and the imperial house — the priest **Gyōgi** mobilizing popular
support, and the priesthood serving as the era's best-educated technical class (and the
one channel of upward mobility for commoners). The danger of politically ambitious monks
(the **Dōkyō** scandal, 770) helped drive the court to abandon Nara.

## Heian Buddhism: Tendai and Shingon

Heian religion was dominated by two **Mahāyāna** sects brought from [[tang-dynasty|Tang]]
China c. 800:

- **Tendai** — founded by **Saichō** (Dengyō Daishi, 767–822) on **Mt. Hiei** (Hieizan)
  outside Kyoto, which became the greatest center of Japanese Buddhist learning (thousands
  of buildings, ~20,000 monks at its height). Saichō stressed broad scholarship and
  **partnership with the state**, and Tendai became the seedbed from which most later
  Japanese sects sprang.
- **Shingon** — the **esoteric** ("True Word") school founded by **Kūkai** (Kōbō Daishi),
  centered on Mt. Kōya, with elaborate ritual, mandalas, and mantras.

Both spread into the countryside, became great **shōen** landholders, inspired much art
and architecture (e.g. the Byōdōin Phoenix Hall), and **absorbed Shintō** through the
*honji suijaku* identification of kami with buddhas.

## The Kamakura revolution: Buddhism becomes a mass religion

The [[kamakura-period|Kamakura period]] (1185–1333) saw Buddhism, hitherto monastic and
elite, become a **genuinely popular religion**, stripped of esoteric trappings — a response
to the era's turmoil and the widespread belief that the world had entered ***mappō***, the
degenerate "latter age" when the old disciplines no longer availed:

- **Pure Land (Amidism).** **Hōnen** (1133–1212) taught that salvation came not by arduous
  self-cultivation but by faith in the **Amida** Buddha, expressed in the ***nembutsu*** —
  reciting *Namu Amida Butsu* ("Homage to Amida") — to be reborn in Amida's **Pure Land**;
  his **Jōdo** sect made religion accessible to all. His radical disciple **Shinran**
  (1173–1262) — who broke his monastic vows, married, and founded the **Shinshū** —
  taught that even **one** heartfelt invocation sufficed, since faith itself was Amida's
  gift; he shifted Japanese Buddhism "from monks to the laity." (Amidism had earlier
  inspired the Byōdōin Phoenix Hall.) Jōdo and Shinshū became the largest Buddhist sects.
- **Nichiren** (1222–82) — a fisherman's son turned fiery prophet — preached exclusive
  devotion to the **Lotus Sutra** (the chant *Namu Myōhō Renge Kyō*), denounced all rival
  sects, and fused religion with **nationalism**, prophesying the [[mongol-invasions-of-japan-1274-1281|Mongol
  invasions]] as divine punishment; his intolerant, this-worldly sect still endures.
- **[[zen-buddhism|Zen]]** — the meditation school (Eisai's Rinzai, Dōgen's Sōtō), treated
  on its own page; it shaped the warrior ethos and the classic Japanese aesthetic.

## Significance

Buddhism was the vehicle of high continental civilization into Japan — writing, art,
architecture, learning, and statecraft all arrived in its train — yet, once absorbed, it
"continued to flourish as an element of a distinctively Japanese civilization," fused with
Shintō rather than replacing it. Its Kamakura-era popularization (Pure Land, Nichiren, Zen)
made it, for the first time, the faith of the whole people.
