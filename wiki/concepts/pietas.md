# Pietas

Roman virtue of dutiful respect and piety toward gods, family (especially parents and ancestors), country, and allies. Embodied in the adjective *pius* (as in "pius Aeneas" in Virgil). Central to Roman self-image, state religion, and family ethics. Distinct from but overlapping with Greek eusebeia (piety to gods) and broader notions of loyalty.

## Definition and Scope

Pietas encompassed:
- Religious: Proper performance of sacrifices, maintenance of cults, respect for omens and divine order.
- Familial: Obedience and care for parents, ancestors, children; burial duties.
- Civic: Loyalty to the res publica, treaties, and allies.
- Personal: The "pious" hero balances these without conflict (or resolves them heroically).

It was both an emic Roman category (celebrated in coinage, literature, state cult — e.g., Temple of Pietas) and an etic analytical tool for understanding Roman behavior and ideology.

## Key Examples from Sources

- Virgil's *Aeneid*: Aeneas as *pius Aeneas* — carries father Anchises from burning Troy, founds Rome through dutiful wandering and fighting.
- Homeric background (via Roman reception): Warrior bonds and guest-friendship (xenia) transformed into Roman pietas (Nisus/Euryalus in Aeneid echoes Patroclus/Achilles).
- Ovid and other poets: Transformations often punish impietas (disrespect to gods or family).
- Historical: Roman generals and statesmen invoked pietas in justifications; the virtue was personified and culted.

## Historiography and Limitations

Roman authors (Cicero, Virgil, Livy) present pietas as uniquely Roman and foundational to their success. Modern scholars note it was selectively applied (e.g., toward citizens vs. others) and evolved under Empire (emperor as pater patriae). It contrasts with Greek emphasis on arete or dike. Collection sources (Homer, Virgil, Ovid, general Classical surveys) show its transmission from Greek epic to Roman national epic and its use in propaganda.
