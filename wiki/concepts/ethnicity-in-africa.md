# Ethnicity in Africa

## Definition and Scope

Ethnicity in Africa is a contested analytical and historical category describing the formation, maintenance, and mobilisation of collective identities based on perceived shared language, descent, territory, and custom. It is simultaneously traditional and modern, fixed and fluid, inclusive and exclusive. As Richard Waller writes, it is "at once 'traditional' and thoroughly modern; apparently fixed and coherent, yet conflicted and constantly redefined" ([[parker-reid-oxford-modern-african-history-2013]], ch. 6).

The concept must be sharply distinguished from **tribe** — the colonial-era term that treated African identities as primordial, static, bounded, and timeless. Historians now recognise that "Africans had identities, but not tribes; it would take much effort before the two came into even approximate alignment." Ethnicity as a social fact is the outcome of historical processes, not a primordial inheritance.

Ethnicity is one of many possible identities — alongside class, religion, gender, and urban affiliation — held simultaneously and deployed situationally.

## The Colonial Construction of Tribe

The first systematic application of tribal categories came with colonial rule. Tribes were assumed to be primordial, fixed, and mutually hostile. Colonial officials used Caesar and Tacitus as models; they believed Africans were at an earlier stage of social evolution that Europeans had already traversed.

**Tribe as governance**: tribal categories made sense of complex social terrain, placed people, determined where they "belonged," and organised who could rule whom. British Africa elaborated this most extensively: under indirect rule, by the 1930s, native authorities controlled local treasuries and courts within a system of tribal government. Membership of a tribe determined residence and access to resources.

**The transformation under colonial rule**: half a century of colonial practice changed tribe from administrative fiction to social reality. Tribes were built out by ethnographic research, codified in customary law, and given real resources to control. As John Iliffe summarised: "Europeans believed Africans belonged to tribes; Africans built tribes to belong to."

## Processes of Ethnogenesis

**Instrumentalism (Barth)**: Frederic Barth's *Ethnic Groups and Boundaries* (1969) showed how ethnic identities could be fluid and situational — primarily concerned with the management of difference to facilitate access to resources. This inspired precolonial historians, especially in East Africa, to examine how identities based on complementary modes of subsistence (pastoral/agricultural) structured competition and facilitated interaction.

**Invention of tradition**: Terence Ranger's 1983 essay on colonial Africa showed how colonial states invented traditions to structure their rule, incorporating Africans through "models of subservience." Ethnogenesis was dialectical: African collaborators were "as much partners as subordinates in the business of creating legitimacy." Karen Fields showed how a colonial search for "hegemony on a shoestring" led rulers to support what they believed to be legitimate tribal authorities and to engage in discourses about tradition they could not fully comprehend or control.

**Constructivism**: the dominant approach from the 1980s. Unlike instrumentalism, constructivism concerned itself with how ethnic identities were made as well as the purposes they served. "Ethnic entrepreneurs and culture-brokers — embattled conservatives, 'progressive traditionalists', mission-educated young men, and simple opportunists — struggled to assert and propagate their own versions of ethnicity, using the newly created languages of Christian literacy to write themselves collectively into existence." Written history, drawing inspiration from the Old Testament as a tribal and providential narrative, played a central role.

**Moral economy**: John Lonsdale's analysis of Kikuyu moral economy argued that communities are essentially "communities of argument." Ethnicity is better understood as continuing discourse about power, accountability, and civic virtue than as a discrete cultural package.

## Gender and Ethnicity

Women were largely ignored in early studies of ethnicity except as objects of male concern. Leroy Vail thought ethnicity's appeal strongest for men, even though "the 'place' of women — and proper gender relations — was a 'central issue' in the formation of ethnic ideologies during the colonial period." Sandra Greene showed how Anlo women both accepted and contested their roles as guardians of lineage identity. Women played the crucial role in determining how ethnic identities were domesticated and transmitted: "without them ethnicity was not sustainable."

## Performance and Memory

Identity is **performative** as well as declarative. "Being Asante" was something you did, not just something you were (Jean Allman). The Asante Odwira festival reconstituted power and dramatised the complex relations giving meaning to Asante identity. Colonial beni dance troupes in eastern Africa gave young men opportunities to demonstrate modernity through competitive performance.

**Social memory** undergirds ethnic identity. Memory is not fixed; it is "performative and embodied, and constantly being restated, re-enacted, and reinterpreted" (Rosalind Shaw on Sierra Leone). Landscape is a key carrier of memory: Shambaa thought of themselves as "people who live in a particular botanical environment"; Asante's being was rooted in the struggle to hack civilisation out of the forest. Colonial land dispossession therefore posed an existential threat to community identity.

**Healing and spirituality**: communities of healing — cults of affliction like Lemba in West Central Africa, shrine complexes, and healing shrines in Buganda — created networks of identity and trust that predate and persist alongside colonial ethnicity.

## Long-Term Roots

Jan Vansina identified an equatorial African tradition of governance that endlessly renewed and reinvented itself. David Schoenbrun's linguistic study of community formation in the Great Lakes region and Ronald Atkinson's work on Acoli identity in northern Uganda trace ethnic identities through the longue durée. Communities were not timeless; they were in constant motion — breaking apart and reforming as followers left to seek their fortunes elsewhere.

Colonial conquest constrained but did not destroy this older flexibility. It "closed down some options, hardened identities, and forced people to re-evaluate belonging in a new and often defensive way."

## Historiography

**Primordialist phase** (pre-1960s): tribe treated as natural and ancient; ethnic identity taken at face value as part of precolonial baseline.

**Nationalist phase** (1960s–1970s): historians emphasised commonalties and resisted the tribal frame; ethnicity seen as obstacle to nation-building. Amilcar Cabral: "For the nation to live, tribe must die."

**Instrumentalist phase** (1970s): Barth and followers focused on boundaries and access to resources; ethnicity as situational and fluid.

**Constructivist phase** (1980s–present): emphasis on how identities were made in the colonial crucible through dialectical processes involving rulers, ruled, and African intermediaries. Mahmood Mamdani's argument that colonial "decentralised tribal despotisms" consolidated tribe at independence remains influential but contested.

**Current directions**: the intersection of the local and global (transnational diaspora communities, "strategic essentialism" for NGO and tourism markets); the resurgence of "neo-traditionalism" that denies historical process; the distinction between John Lonsdale's "moral ethnicity" (internal community discourse on accountability) and "political tribalism" (inter-ethnic violence and rival constituencies in national politics).

## Limitations and Critiques

- "Strong" constructions of ethnicity are too static and determinist; "weaker" versions too diffuse.
- The creole/instrumentalist model overlooked how identities were constructed, not just mobilised.
- Marxist scholars argued ethnicity was false consciousness masking class relations.
- Post-independence attempts to capture community for the state through official ethnicities largely failed — "identity at the local level was simply too fluid and self-referential to be a reliable prop for an imposed nation."
