# Egyptian Ethnicity and Identity

What it meant to be "Egyptian" — and the sharp distinction, central to Barry Kemp's
[[sources/kemp-ancient-egypt-anatomy-2018|*Anatomy of a Civilization*]] (Ch. 1, "Who
were the ancient Egyptians?"), between Egyptian **cultural** identity and Egyptian
**biological** make-up. Kemp's thesis: the Egyptians most scholars study are *the bearers
of a culture*, not a genetic population — "To be an Egyptian one simply acted the part.
It was all a matter of style, of cultural statement."

## Identity as an Imagined Community

Kemp applies Benedict Anderson's **"imagined community"**: Egyptians, "speaking and
writing a common language, occupying a territory with a well-catalogued geography centred
on the Nile valley and subscribing to a distinctive culture, imagined themselves as a
single community." The chief difference from a modern nation was [[concepts/divine-kingship|divine
kingship]] — the community was "presided over by a line of divine kings" — yet "Egypt had
an existence separate from Pharaoh," who was obligated to maintain its integrity.

- The land was **Kmt**, "the black land" of fertile Nile silt, set against the desert
  "red land" of Seth; Egyptians called themselves generically "mankind," the norm of
  humanity.
- The **Story of Sinuhe** (c. 1950 BCE, copied for seven centuries) is the earliest
  articulation of national consciousness: "It is all there: geographical frontiers,
  language, dress, bodily cleanliness, even sleeping on proper beds." Identity was marked
  by **language** (the near-qualifying test), **dress** (white linen), and burial in
  Egyptian soil — not by birth or a concept of citizenship.

## Permeable Barriers: Identity in Practice

Despite an official ideology of ethnic separateness and demeaning rhetoric about
foreigners, "the barriers which the Egyptians thought kept them separate from foreigners
were, in practice, very permeable." There were **no exclusion laws**, and foreigners who
adopted the language and style were absorbed:

- **Military recruitment** of foreigners began at least in the [[actors/old-kingdom-egypt|Old
  Kingdom]]: the official **Weni** (reign of Pepi I, c. 2265–2219 BCE) levied soldiers
  from five named Nubian homelands and Libya; a royal decree of the period protected
  temple property against "friendly Nubians."
- In the [[actors/new-kingdom-egypt|New Kingdom]], **Sherden** mercenaries (from across
  the Mediterranean), **Medjay** (eastern-desert people), Nubians and Libyans were
  settled on farmland (Papyrus Wilbour, land register of Ramesses V, c. 1142 BCE) and
  appear as legal witnesses; Ramesside hymns acknowledge them "stretched out asleep" in
  the fields they love. Ramesses III's **Papyrus Harris I** records over 5,800 foreign
  persons donated to three temples plus 971 Libyans assigned to herding.
- The **Pan-grave culture** (c. 1780–1540 BCE) marks immigration of eastern-desert people
  (probably Medjay from the Sudanese Red Sea hills) who settled in the Upper Egyptian
  valley and whose distinctive culture vanished as they were Egyptianized in the early
  New Kingdom.
- **Libyan** groups (Libu, Mashwash), repeatedly defeated in New Kingdom war texts,
  nonetheless migrated into the valley and supplied the rulers of the Third Intermediate
  Period; the **Pasenhor stela** traces 15 generations back to the Libyan chief Buyuwawa,
  who probably entered just after the New Kingdom. (See [[actors/libyan-dynasties-egypt]].)

## The "Race" Question and the Biological Evidence

Kemp endorses the modern position that biological racial categories are practically
meaningless for ancient populations — **"There are no races, only populations."** He notes
the conventional "Caucasoid" classification of Egyptians but stresses that the relevant
biological boundary "runs through the northern Sudan, not across the Mediterranean": "the
Sahara Desert has been a more significant barrier to human groups than the Mediterranean
Sea." The **"dynastic race"** hypothesis (a forceful eastern people imposing the First
Dynasty on a Negroid population) is dismissed as "troubled waters which most people who
write about ancient Egypt from within the mainstream of scholarship avoid."

The skeletal evidence is too thin and biased for a grand population history: tiny samples,
better preservation in the south, archaeologists' wholesale discarding of bones, even
male/female skull metrics that read "as if of different origins." A real biological
**cline** ran along the valley — southern Egypt merging with northern Nubia (with a
darkening of skin colour), the north-eastern delta merging with southern Palestine. The
**Amarna South Tombs Cemetery** (319 skulls, c. 1350 BCE) shows "approximately equal
representation" of southern (possibly Nubian), Mediterranean and north-eastern peripheral
ancestral forms — a cosmopolitan population whose **uniform "Egyptian" material culture
masked its diverse origins** ([[places/amarna]]). Deep-time discontinuity is also visible:
the Late Stone Age cemetery at **Gebel Sahaba** (northern Sudan, c. 14,000–12,000 BP; 58
burials, nearly half dead by violence) holds people physically distinct from pharaonic
Egyptians, implying genetic discontinuity in the valley between the Palaeolithic and the
Predynastic.

## Limitations and Critiques

Kemp's culture-over-biology framing is itself a position in a charged, politicized debate
(Afrocentrist and "Black Athena" arguments on one side; older racial-typological
scholarship on the other) — the standing controversy
[[controversies/peopling-of-ancient-egypt]], where Cheikh Anta Diop's argument for a Black
African Egypt is recorded. His own evidence base — the skeletal record — he concedes is too
fragmentary to settle origins; the claim of "permeability" rests on textual and material
absorption rather than systematic demographic data.
