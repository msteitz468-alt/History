# Devotio Moderna (New Devotion)

## Definition

The **Devotio Moderna** (“New Devotion”) was a late-medieval spiritual movement that swept across much of northern Europe in the fifteenth century, offering laity and secular clergy a model of perfection **in the world** without monastic vows—equal, not second-best, to the monastic ideal that had defined Christian perfection since at least the late eleventh century ([[logan-history-of-the-church-in-the-middle-ages-2013|Logan]], ch. 17).

Three components form the movement: the **Brethren of the Common Life**, the **Sisters of the Common Life**, and the **Canons Regular** (Windesheim). It was urban, bourgeois, and literate—and **determinedly within the church**, unlike Hussites or Lollards.

## Emic vs Etic

- **Emic:** a practical path of interior conversion, common life without vows, manuscript copying, and vernacular/devotional reading; perfection available to householders and schoolboys as well as canons.
- **Etic:** historians treat it as a major strand of late-medieval lay spirituality, often linked (cautiously) to later Reformation and humanist educational culture without making it a mere “prelude.”

## Gerard Groote and Founding

**Gerard Groote** of Deventer (d. 1384)—Paris-trained, not a great theologian—after 1374 conversion gave his house to women who became **Sisters of the Common Life**; spent three years near Arnhem Carthusians; as deacon became popular preacher against evils of the day (the bishop of Utrecht forbade all deacon preaching to silence him); magnet for laity and secular clergy seeking spiritual life; died of plague 1384. The movement spread from Deventer to Zwolle, Kampen, and beyond.

## Brethren Structure

The Brethren took **no vows**, could leave freely, formed **no order** (Fourth Lateran had banned new orders). Mixed laymen and clerics; housed schoolboys (e.g. Erasmus 1484–87) with spiritual direction more than formal teaching; copied devotional manuscripts; funded by rents, sales, and copying; houses across Netherlands, Flanders, Germany (Münster 1400, Cologne 1417, etc.).

## *The Imitation of Christ*

**Thomas à Kempis** (canon of Mount St Agnes near Zwolle) produced *De imitatione Christi*—four booklets united 1418; 1441 autograph MS at Brussels Royal Library. Intended for canons and pious souls in the world; accessible, aphoristic; prioritizes virtue over learned discourse, love of God over self, *memento mori*. Not wholly anti-intellectual (Van Engen’s caution: learning is good if conscience and holy life come first). ~900 MSS survive; Dutch (1420) and German (1434) translations; 50+ languages. Logan: “Without doubt, no other book of the fifteenth century has had such a profound influence on the spirituality of ordinary people.”

## Limitations and Contests

The movement’s relationship to later Protestantism is often overstated in older narratives; Logan stresses its **orthodox, intra-ecclesial** character. Regional intensity varied; Italian confraternity piety and English parish calendar piety were parallel but not identical forms of vibrant fifteenth-century devotion.

## Historiography

Logan points to **John Van Engen**, *Sisters and Brothers of the Common Life*, as the classic treatment, plus Post and Scheepsma; for English parish piety as reappraisal of “decay and decline,” **Eamon Duffy**, *The Stripping of the Altars*.
