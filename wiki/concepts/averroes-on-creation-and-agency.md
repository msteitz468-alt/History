## Definition and Scope

Averroes' account of creation and agency — developed across Discussions 2, 3, and the lead-in to Discussion 4 of [[tahafut-al-tahafut]] — defends the philosophically coherent claim that [[ibn-rushd|God]] is the genuine agent and maker of the world even though the world is eternal (without temporal beginning or end). The central thesis is that *agency does not require temporal production*: an eternal first cause can be a real maker in the fullest sense, and the world can be God's genuine act and work despite having no first moment of creation. This distinguishes the philosophical position both from the Ash'arite theologians (who insist on temporal origination as the mark of divine creation) and from the Neoplatonic emanationist scheme of [[ibn-sina|Avicenna]] and al-Fārābī (which Averroes considers a distortion of genuine Aristotelianism).

The concept belongs to the broader [[philosophy-versus-theology-in-islam]] debate and is Averroes' main contribution to Islamic discussions of the [[eternity-of-the-world]] and [[arguments-for-gods-existence-islam]]. It draws on and departs from the Avicennan framework treated in [[emanationism-avicennan]].

## The Second Discussion: Incorruptibility and Eternal Agency

Al-Ghazālī (in [[tahafut-al-falasifa]]) argues that the philosophers' four proofs for the eternity of the world in the past apply equally to its eternity in the future, so the world's incorruptibility is as problematic as its beginninglessness. Averroes accepts this symmetry and uses it to strengthen the philosophical position: if the eternal cause does not change, neither beginning nor ending can be attributed to its effects.

A critical argument concerns the relationship between the eternal First Existent and the acts flowing from it:

> "When one imagines an eternal entity whose acts are not delayed after its existence — as indeed must be the case with any entity whose existence is perfect — then, if it is eternal and does not enter past time, it follows necessarily that its acts also cannot enter past time, for if they did they would be finite and this eternal existent would be eternally inactive and what is eternally inactive is necessarily impossible."

Averroes thus holds that an eternal agent *necessarily* produces eternal effects: not because of compulsion but because a perfect being does not defer its acts. The theologians' error is to regard God's existence as eternal while treating His act (the world) as temporally bounded — this implies God was for an infinite duration "eternally inactive," which is impossible.

On the charge that calling the world "eternal" and "God's product" involves a contradiction, Averroes replies that applying the expression "production" to an eternal making is *more* appropriate, not less: "To apply the expression 'production' for the world's creation as the Divine Law does is more appropriate than to use it of temporal production, as the Ash'arites did, for the act, in so far as it is an act, is a product, and eternity is only represented in this act because this production and the act produced have neither beginning nor end."

### Non-existence and agency

A subordinate argument concerns the philosophical analysis of creation-from-nothing. The Ash'arite position (that God can annihilate the world absolutely) requires that the agent's act attach primarily and essentially to non-existence — which is incoherent. For the philosophers, the agent always acts *on something*: production converts potential being into actual being; destruction converts actual being into potential being. Neither act attaches to pure non-existence as such.

> "The theologians perceived the agent in the way the weak-sighted perceive the shadow of a thing instead of the thing itself and then mistake the shadow for it."

Non-existence follows secondarily and accidentally when a form is replaced, but it is never the direct object of any genuine causal act. This means creation-from-nothing (absolute *ex nihilo* production) is not a coherent category for the philosophers — not because God lacks power, but because pure non-existence is not an object that any act can address. For Averroes, "eternal becoming" is the correct description of the world's status, not "eternity" in the static sense nor "temporal production."

## The Third Discussion: God as Agent and Maker — a Real, Not Metaphorical, Relation

[[al-ghazali]] argues in three steps that the philosophers cannot coherently call God the "agent" and "maker" of the world: (1) an agent must act by will and choice, and God on their account acts by necessity; (2) "act" implies temporal production, but the world is eternal; (3) from a simple unity only one thing can proceed, yet the world is diverse.

Averroes contests all three:

### (1) The nature of divine agency

Averroes rejects the claim that "agent" by definition means a voluntary deliberator:

> "There are in brief two kinds of agents ... The agent is what causes some other thing to pass from potency to actuality and from nonexistence to existence; this actualization occurs sometimes from deliberation and choice, sometimes by nature."

God is neither a natural agent (whose act is necessary in its substance) nor a voluntary agent in the ordinary human sense (who lacks what he wills and changes upon obtaining it). God is the agent of *both* voluntary and natural causes — He is the agent of the causes themselves, "drawing forth the Universe from non-existence to existence and conserving it." This is a *more perfect* form of agency than any empirical variant.

Averroes quotes what he attributes to Aristotle: "The Agent must be such that His capacity must be proportionate to His power and His power proportionate to His will and His will proportionate to His wisdom ... He exists, making what He wants out of nothing." The philosophers do not deny divine will; they deny that divine will resembles human will in its insufficiency and changeability. "There is no difference between one who says that God wills with a will which does not resemble the human will, and one who says that God knows through a knowledge which does not resemble human knowledge."

Critically, Averroes insists that divine agency proceeds *from knowledge*, not from brute necessity: "they believe that God's act proceeds from Him through knowledge, not through any necessity which calls for it, either in His essence or outside His essence, but through His grace and His bounty."

### (2) Eternal act is still genuine act

On the charge that "act" implies temporal production, Averroes distinguishes two senses of the world being God's "act":
- In the Ash'arite sense: God produced the world at a moment, before which it did not exist.
- In the philosophical sense: God is the eternal efficient cause upon which the world perpetually depends. The relation of the world to God is that of an effect to its cause, sustained continuously, not that of a product to a one-time making event.

The world is "eternal becoming" — constituted by motion, and motion is always coming-to-be. The philosophers "do not mean by the expression 'eternal' that the world is eternal through eternal constituents, for the world consists of movement. And since the Ash'arites did not understand this, it was difficult for them to attribute eternity at the same time to God and to the world."

God is the agent of the unity that binds the world's parts together: "He is the giver of the unity through which the world is united, and the giver of the unity which is the condition of the existence of the composite." As giver of that constitutive unity, He is the agent of everything. "And it is evident that the First Principle is the principle of all these principles, and that He is an agent, a form, and an end."

### (3) The problem of plurality from unity — Averroes against Avicenna

Al-Ghazālī presses a third objection drawn from the Avicennan school: if the First Principle is absolutely simple, only one thing can proceed from it directly; yet the world is irreducibly diverse. The Avicennan solution — that the first emanated intellect's self-knowledge plus knowledge of the First generates a triad of sphere, soul, and intellect — multiplies indefinitely, generating the spheres by a cascade.

Averroes explicitly breaks from [[emanationism-avicennan]] here. He regards the Avicennan schema as philosophically unjustified — "extraneous to philosophy, and not congruous with its principles" — and as generating worse difficulties than the theologians' position:

> "The fundamental mistake of Avicenna and Farabi was that they made the statement that from the one only one can proceed, and then assumed a plurality in the one which proceeds. Therefore they were forced to regard this plurality as uncaused."

The genuine Aristotelian (Peripatetic) position, as Averroes reconstructs it, is that plurality arises from three factors: *intermediates*, *dispositions* (of the recipients), and *instruments*. The unity emanating from the First Principle is received differently by different natures, producing plurality through the plurality of recipients rather than through hidden plurality in the source itself:

> "My opinion is that according to the principles of the Peripatetics the cause of plurality is a combination of three factors, the intermediates, the dispositions, and the instruments; and we have already explained how all these depend on the Monad and refer to it."

Crucially, "the cause of the plurality of the separate intellects is the difference in their natures, by which they receive the knowledge they gain of the First Principle and which acquire from the First Principle a unity which by itself is one single act, but which becomes many through the plurality of the recipients." This avoids both the Avicennan ad hoc multiplication of principles and the Ash'arite dissolution of secondary causation.

## The Proof of a Creator's Existence (Discussion 3 / Lead-in to Discussion 4)

Averroes outlines the philosophical proof for the existence of a creative First Principle. The proof proceeds through the impossibility of an infinite regress of temporal causes:

> "The manner in which they established this creator was by assuming that every temporal product must have a cause, but that this cannot go on infinitely, and that therefore of necessity the series must end in an eternal cause."

This eternal cause cannot be a body, since bodies are subject to the accidents and circular movements that mark them as produced. The proof thus requires establishing: (a) that the world's temporal-causal series terminates in an eternal principle; (b) that this principle is incorporeal; and (c) that it is genuinely an agent and not merely a formal or final condition.

Averroes distinguishes the Aristotelian proof from the theologians' proof: the philosophers do not infer an eternal, incorporeal First Cause by pointing to temporal creation-from-nothing. Rather, they observe that the celestial bodies subsist through their motion, that every motion requires a mover, and that the series of movers must terminate in an unmoved mover — immaterial because material movers are finite in power (a body can move for only a finite time and must eventually be exhausted). This unmoved first mover is identified with the First Principle that gives the world its unity, its form, and its end.

The world is described as analogous to "a single state: a state is upheld through one ruler and many deputies subordinate to him; all the deputies in the state are connected with the first ruler." The First Principle is the source of the unity permeating all its parts: "in this way it is true that God is the creator, supporter, and preserver of everything, and to this the Divine Words apply: 'Verily, God supports the heavens and the earth lest they should decline.'"

Revelation and philosophy here converge, each providing what the other lacks. Revelation is "a mercy bestowed on all these classes of men" — it communicates to those without philosophical training, and to those whose intellects reach their limit, knowledge that is "necessary in the life and existence of man."

## Relationship to Ash'arite Theology

Averroes holds that the Ash'arite alternative — temporal creation from nothing by a God who exists without acting for an infinite "before" — generates insoluble problems:

1. It requires God to have been "eternally inactive," then to have begun acting, which is impossible for a perfect being.
2. It makes the agent's act attach to non-existence essentially, which is philosophically incoherent.
3. It denies secondary causation: the Ash'arites treat God as the only real agent, which contradicts sensory evidence of things acting on other things and makes knowledge impossible.
4. The Ash'arite account of annihilation (pure destruction into nothing) is equally incoherent, for the same reasons as creation-from-nothing.

The Mu'tazilite position (treating divine attributes as relative to, not additional to, God's essence) is closer to the philosophical position than the Ash'arite one, but still falls short.

## Historiography

The Third Discussion is the most substantial section of [[tahafut-al-tahafut]] devoted to systematic philosophical theology. Averroes' reconstruction of the "genuine" Aristotelian position against both Avicenna and al-Ghazālī has been the subject of considerable modern scholarship. Key debates include:

- Whether Averroes' critique of Avicennan [[emanationism-avicennan]] reflects a genuine recovery of Aristotle or his own philosophical innovation.
- The degree to which Averroes' language of God acting "through grace and bounty" is Islamic apologetics layered onto Aristotelian metaphysics or reflects a genuinely distinct metaphysical commitment.
- The question of Averroes' own positive account of how plurality arises from the One — he explicitly admits he "cannot give in this book an answer to this question supported by a demonstrative proof."

The van den Bergh translation (1954) remains the standard English edition. [[ibn-rushd-incoherence-of-incoherence]] is the source page.
