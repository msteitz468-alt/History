## Definition

The **environmental history of the Middle Millennium** (500–1500 CE) studies the
reciprocal, **co-evolutionary** relationship between human societies and the
natural world across this period: the ways people shaped nature (through
agriculture, irrigation, deforestation, herding, and mining) and nature
simultaneously shaped people (through climate, disease, soil, and the constraints
of terrain). Joachim Radkau's synthesis ([[kedar-cwh-v5-2015|CWH V]], Ch 2) treats
the period not as a single linear trajectory but as a set of **overlapping cycles**,
and frames its central, distinctive feature: human alteration of the environment
**accelerated** over the millennium, but remained **local, mostly reversible, and
self-affecting** — those who caused environmental damage generally belonged to the
same community that suffered it, "a major and fundamental difference from today."

## Radkau's Three-Realms Model

Modifying Braudel's wheat/rice/maize division, Radkau classifies the era's
human–environment relations into three "realms," each producing a distinctive
"second nature" (a culturally shaped way of life):

1. **The great irrigation systems** (wet-rice and canal agriculture). The most
   transformative single development of the age was the spread of rice paddies in
   monsoon Asia (McNeill: it "affected more persons than any other single
   transformation"). Highly productive but ecologically fragile — vulnerable to
   salinization (Mesopotamia under the Abbasids), siltation, and the collapse of the
   labor regimes that maintained terraces. Critiques **Wittfogel's "hydraulic
   society"** thesis (irrigation → centralized despotism) as ecological determinism;
   Ostrom's work shows communal management was common.
2. **The nomadic realms** (steppe, desert, semi-desert). [[pastoral-nomadism|Pastoral
   nomadism]] reached its historical climax in this period. Whether nomads degraded
   or regenerated arid environments is "one of the greatest mysteries of
   environmental history" — Radkau rejects both the colonial "children of the desert
   ruin the land" stereotype and its romantic inverse.
3. **The European combination of rainfed agriculture and animal husbandry.** The
   three-field system, the heavy plough (Marc Bloch, Lynn White Jr.), and forest
   pasture; an "ecological-reserve"-dependent system that, unlike Asian wet-rice,
   was checked from pushing population to the limit — and which Radkau links, only
   "from modern hindsight," to a later European "invention of invention."

## The Cyclical Structure

Radkau identifies several long cycles rather than one master narrative:
- **The nomadic cycle** — the ebb and flow of [[pastoral-nomadism|steppe
  conquests]] from Late Antiquity to the gunpowder age, with an ecological dimension
  (nomads disrupting irrigation systems).
- **The forest cycle** — lordship first expressed through clearance, later through
  forest protection (Charlemagne's *Capitulare de villis*, Norman England, Alpine
  anti-avalanche rules); the origins of "sustainable forestry" (von Carlowitz 1713,
  pre-modern Japan) read critically as elite resource control, not innocent
  conservation.
- **The epidemiological–demographic cycle** — the [[justinianic-plague-541|Plague of
  Justinian]] (541–750) marking, for some, "the definitive end of antiquity" and
  weakening Byzantium before the Arab conquests; the [[black-death|Black Death]]
  (climax c. 1350); and the gradual build-up of immunities that let population settle
  onto a permanent growth trend — inverted catastrophically in the post-1492
  Americas.

## The Climate Question

The millennium opens in the cold trough of the **Late Antique Little Ice Age** (LALIA,
c. 536–660) — the volcanic-and-solar cooling that, in Kyle Harper's reading, combined with
the [[justinianic-plague-541|Justinianic Plague]] to close antiquity; for the full Roman-era
climate sequence (Roman Climate Optimum → Late Roman Transition → LALIA) see the dedicated
[[roman-climate-optimum|Roman climate and disease regime]] page. The period then spans the
**Medieval Climate Optimum** (warm) and the onset of the **Little Ice Age** (cooling from
c. 1300, sharply visible only from the sixteenth century). Radkau is cautious: pre-modern
sources record *weather*, not *climate*;
regional variation was huge (Ellenblum: the European warm period coincided with
cold, drought, and famine in the eastern Mediterranean). He warns against
"how-climate-made-history" determinism while granting climate a real role.

## Significance and Limitations

The chapter's deepest methodological claim is that **the Middle Millennium is the
age of "small worlds"** — local, semi-autonomous human–nature relationships — and
that a single global "master story" (whether of decline or of harmony) is
"misplaced" given fragmentary evidence (Horden & Purcell's *Corrupting Sea* is the
exemplar of this anti-generalizing stance). It is the wiki's anchor for the
[[societal-collapse-and-environmental-determinism|collapse-vs-resilience debate]],
and it connects to [[afro-eurasian-world-system|the world system]] (disease and
goods traveling the webs) and to [[technology-and-innovation|technology]] (the water
mill, the heavy plough).
