# Maragha School

## Definition and Scope

The "Maragha school" is the historiographical designation for the tradition of theoretical astronomy inaugurated at the [[maragha-observatory|Maragha Observatory]] (founded 1259 CE, northwest Iran) under the direction of [[nasir-al-din-tusi|Naṣīr al-Dīn al-Ṭūsī]] (d. 1274), and continued through several generations of astronomers whose primary intellectual project was the systematic reform of Ptolemaic planetary theory. The school is characterized not by continuous institutional affiliation — the observatory's peak period ended with al-Ṭūsī's death — but by a shared programme: to construct astronomical models that were consistent with the Aristotelian requirement of uniform circular motion while preserving the observational predictions of Ptolemy.

The Maragha school is the institutional and intellectual cradle of the two new mathematical theorems — the [[tusi-couple|Ṭūsī couple]] and [[urdi-lemma|ʿUrḍī's Lemma]] — that made physically consistent planetary models possible, and which subsequently appear in the work of Copernicus.

As Saliba writes, the school's "main mission, as was enunciated later by Muʾayyad al-Dīn al-ʿUrḍī (d. 1266) of Damascus, one of the most distinguished astronomers of that tradition, was to create an astronomy that did not suffer from the cosmological shortcomings of Ptolemaic astronomy, that could account for the observations just as well as Ptolemaic astronomy could do if not better" ([[saliba-islamic-science-renaissance-2007]]).

## Institutional History

The [[maragha-observatory|Maragha Observatory]] was founded in 1259 under the patronage of the Mongol Ilkhan Hülegü, who had conquered Baghdad the previous year and destroyed the ʿAbbāsid caliphate. Al-Ṭūsī, who had accompanied Hülegü's campaigns, negotiated the establishment of the observatory and directed it until his death. The facility housed a large library and specialized instruments for observational astronomy, the construction of which was overseen by [[al-urdi|al-ʿUrḍī]] himself.

The observatory assembled the leading astronomers of the Islamic world in one place for roughly two decades (ca. 1259–1274), a concentration of talent unique in the history of medieval Islamic science. After al-Ṭūsī's death the observatory declined, but the intellectual tradition it had generated continued — carried by former members and their students — into the following centuries at Damascus, Tabriz, Samarqand, and eventually Istanbul.

## Core Members and Contributions

### Muʾayyad al-Dīn al-ʿUrḍī (d. 1266)
Originally from Damascus; chief instrument-maker at Maragha; author of the *Kitāb al-Hayʾa*. Introduced [[urdi-lemma|ʿUrḍī's Lemma]], which allowed a physically permissible replacement for the equant in the model for the upper planets. Also reformed the lunar and Mercury models by adjusting directions and magnitudes of motions without introducing new theorems. Saliba rates his role as "pivotal" and "comparable only to that of Naṣīr al-Dīn al-Ṭūsī" ([[saliba-islamic-science-renaissance-2007]]).

### Naṣīr al-Dīn al-Ṭūsī (d. 1274)
Director of the observatory; author of the *Taḥrīr al-majisṭī* (1247) and the *al-Tadhkira fī al-Hayʾa* (ca. 1260/61). Introduced the [[tusi-couple|Ṭūsī couple]]. Applied it to the Moon and upper planets; acknowledged failure for Mercury. The Ṭūsī couple, as transmitted to Copernicus, became the most cited mathematical link between the Maragha school and the European Renaissance.

### Quṭb al-Dīn al-Shīrāzī (d. 1311)
Al-Ṭūsī's student and colleague. Applied both ʿUrḍī's Lemma (lunar model, upper planets) and a double application of the Ṭūsī couple (Mercury model) — the latter solving what al-Ṭūsī had acknowledged as unsolved. Author of the *Nihāyat al-idrāk* and the *Tuḥfa Shāhīya*. Saliba describes his attempt to produce nine different models for Mercury as marking "the beginning of a tradition that began to seek alternative mathematical solutions to the same physical problem" ([[saliba-islamic-science-renaissance-2007]]).

## Extended Maragha Tradition

The reform programme continued beyond the observatory's institutional life through a chain of astronomers:

- **[[ibn-al-shatir|Ibn al-Shāṭir]]** (d. 1375, Damascus): Unified the Maragha reforms into a single geocentric system, eliminating all eccentrics, using only epicycles, and applying ʿUrḍī's Lemma and the Ṭūsī couple systematically. His lunar model is mathematically identical to Copernicus's; his Mercury model uses the same double Ṭūsī couple.
- **ʿAlāʾ al-Dīn al-Qushjī** (d. 1474, Samarqand and Istanbul): Used ʿUrḍī's Lemma for Mercury.
- **Shams al-Dīn al-Khafrī** (d. 1550): Produced four mathematically equivalent Mercury models — a step toward the recognition that mathematical representation need not be unique, anticipating a modern philosophy of science.

## The School's Intellectual Programme

The Maragha school represents the culmination of the [[critique-of-ptolemy|critique of Ptolemy]] that had developed over four centuries:

1. **Phase 1 (9th–10th c.)**: Observational and parametric correction.
2. **Phase 2 (11th c.)**: Theoretical critique (Ibn al-Haytham's *Shukūk*).
3. **Phase 3 (Maragha, 13th c.)**: New mathematical theorems enabling reformed models.
4. **Phase 4 (14th–16th c.)**: Synthesis and extension (Ibn al-Shāṭir; Khafrī).

The school's characteristic shift was from *critique* to *construction*: from demonstrating that Ptolemy's models were physically impossible to inventing the mathematics that made physically consistent alternatives achievable. As Saliba summarizes: "The works of ʿUrḍī and Ṭūsī ushered in the second, more important phase, when they spoke directly to that need of creating new mathematics. And each of them had a new theorem to add" ([[saliba-islamic-science-renaissance-2007]]).

## Significance for the European Renaissance

Saliba's overarching argument in *Islamic Science and the Making of the European Renaissance* is that the Maragha school's tools — above all the Ṭūsī couple and ʿUrḍī's Lemma — were transmitted to Renaissance Europe, where they reappear in Copernicus's *Commentariolus* and *De Revolutionibus*. The mathematical identity of models (Ibn al-Shāṭir's lunar model = Copernicus's; Ibn al-Shāṭir's and Shīrāzī's Mercury model = Copernicus's) is the evidentiary core. The mechanism of transmission — whether via manuscripts known in Italy or via an intermediate tradition — remains an open question in the history of science.

## Historiography

The Maragha school as a coherent historiographical category was effectively constituted by E. S. Kennedy's work in the 1960s–70s, extended by Victor Roberts, Noel Swerdlow, Otto Neugebauer, and George Saliba. The term itself is anachronistic — medieval astronomers did not self-identify as a "school" — but it captures the genuine intellectual continuity across institutions and generations.

The school is sometimes described as a "scientific revolution before the Renaissance" (Peter Adamson, cited in existing wiki pages on the Maragha observatory). Saliba's framing is more specific: not a revolution in the heliocentric sense, but the creation of the mathematical tools without which the Copernican revolution could not have taken the form it did.
