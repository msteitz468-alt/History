# Literature and the Second Sophistic

The literary culture of the High Empire — above all the brilliant **Greek revival** of the late
first and second centuries known as the **Second Sophistic**. Treated in [[bowman-cah-v11-2000|CAH
XI]] Ch. 31 (**Ewen Bowie**), centred on the Greek-speaking East.

## Paideia and the imagined community of Hellenes

Across the flourishing cities of the empire spread an **elite that defined itself by culture as
much as wealth**. In the West that culture was **Latin** (the canon of late-Republican and early
imperial Rome); in the East, **Greek**, with the two largely separate (only easterners in Roman
service learned Latin). Greek culture rested on the immense prestige of a **canon of classical
texts** — Homer, Euripides, Plato, Demosthenes, Menander — diffused since Alexander. A
**text-oriented education** in classical Greek (alongside the spoken *koine*) was the "almost
indispensable passport" enrolling a man from Borysthenes or Tyre into the **worldwide imagined
community of Hellenes**, guaranteeing common bonds with educated Greeks anywhere.

## The sophists

The chief distinguishing mark of this culture was the **sophist** — the eminent public teacher and
performer of **rhetoric**, whose **declamations (*meletai*) and lectures (*dialexeis*)** are
chronicled in **Philostratus' *Lives of the Sophists*** (c. 230). The typical career: a gifted
youth moved to a great centre (**Ephesus, Smyrna, Athens**), studied under a *maestro*, then taught
and declaimed himself, travelling for **honours, fees, and civic offices**. The greatest commanded
huge fees (Polemo charging Herodes Atticus over six talents for a performance), imperial chairs of
rhetoric, statues, *prohedria*, tax immunities, and imperial favour (Hadrian's gifts to Dionysius
and Polemo). Sophists wielded real influence — as **peacemakers** in civic strife and **envoys** —
though Bowie cautions this fell to them more as **local aristocrats** than as sophists.

## Archaism and the classical past

The repertoire of declamation was drawn from **Greek history between the Persian Wars and
Alexander**: for an hour the sophist *was* Themistocles or Demosthenes, his audience fifth-century
Athenians. This **archaism** — also the **Atticist** dialect, classicizing sculpture, the revived
beard — Bowie reads partly as the shared educational language of Hellenism, partly as a contrast
between the **glorious independence of the classical city** and the **limits on civic action under
Roman rule** — a retreat into a magnified past.

## Sophists, philosophers, and the boundary genres

Sophists and **philosophers** were rivals in tertiary education (the Athenian schools continued;
Epictetus at Nicopolis, Plutarch at Chaeronea). The boundary was porous: **Favorinus** of Arles and
**Dio Chrysostom** mixed philosophical argument with sophistic display (Dio's *Kingship* orations,
his Euboean and Borysthenite discourses; and his serious speeches urging the Bithynian cities to
**moderate their ruinous status-rivalry**). **Lucian** of Samosata pushed declamation toward
**satire and fantasy** (the *True Histories*, the dialogues, the spoof biographies), the most
original literary voice of the age.

## New and old genres

- The **Greek novel** (romance) — the extant five (Chariton, Xenophon of Ephesus, Achilles Tatius,
  Longus, Heliodorus) — a new form (boy meets girl, separation, perils, reunion), a **sibling of
  the Sophistic**, bringing fantasy to the reader's *personal and sexual* life as the sophist
  brought it to public life. Its readership (elite? women and children?) is debated, though its
  constant classical allusion implies an educated audience.
- The persistence of the **old genres**: prolific philosophy, sophistic oratory, and
  **historiography** — much of it reworking earlier writers in Atticist idiom, exemplified by
  **Arrian** of Nicomedia (the *Anabasis of Alexander* modelling Xenophon), the polymathic compendia
  of **Plutarch**, **Gellius**, **Galen**, and **Athenaeus**, and the geography of Pausanias.

## Significance

The Second Sophistic was the **cultural self-assertion of the Greek elite** within the Roman order —
a virtuoso revival of classical Hellenism, performed in the theatres and *bouleuteria* of the
eastern cities, that made **paideia the passport of an empire-wide Greek aristocracy** and produced
the era's most characteristic literature (Philostratus, Dio, Aristides, Lucian, the novelists). It
is the literary face of the prosperity and civic competition described for the
[[roman-greek-east|Greek East]].
