## Definition and Scope

[[ibn-rushd|Averroes']] most sustained methodological contribution in the
[[tahafut-al-tahafut|Tahāfut al-Tahāfut]] is the distinction between **demonstrative proof**
(*burhān*, Greek *apodeixis*) and **dialectical argument** (*jadal*, Greek *dialektikē*). This
distinction runs throughout the First Discussion on the eternity of the world and underlies
Averroes' charge that [[al-ghazali|al-Ghazālī's]] refutations in the
[[tahafut-al-falasifa|Tahāfut al-Falāsifa]] never rise to the level of genuine demonstrations
and therefore cannot constitute knowledge (*ʿilm*) of their conclusions.

The distinction is not original to Averroes — it derives from Aristotle's division of his logical
works, with the *Posterior Analytics* (*Burhān*) governing demonstration and the *Topics*
(*Jadal*) governing dialectic — but Averroes deploys it with polemical precision as a diagnostic
tool to expose the structural weaknesses of [[kalam|kalām]] argumentation.

---

## The Three Tiers of Argument

Averroes consistently distinguishes three levels of argument in the First Discussion:

### 1. Demonstration (*Burhān*)

A demonstrative proof proceeds from premisses that are:
- **Necessary**: they cannot be otherwise.
- **Proper to the genus**: they concern the specific subject-matter of the inquiry, not borrowed
  from another domain.
- **Primary**: not themselves requiring prior demonstration for the inquiry in question.

Demonstrative proof produces *necessity* in the mind — not merely persuasion. Averroes uses the
terms **apodictic** and **stringent** for this level. He holds that the Aristotelian analysis of
motion and eternity, properly conducted, belongs to this level.

### 2. Dialectical Argument (*Jadal*)

A dialectical argument proceeds from **common notions** (*mashhūrāt*, Greek *endoxa*) — opinions
held by all, or by the many, or by the learned. Such premisses are not necessarily true but are
widely accepted. Dialectical argument produces *persuasion* and may be useful in disputation, but
it cannot establish knowledge. Common notions approach the **equivocal**: the same term carries
different meanings in different domains, and an argument that conflates these meanings commits
what Averroes calls "one of the well known seven sophisms":

> "This argument is in the highest degree dialectical and does not reach the pitch of demonstrative
> proof. For its premisses are common notions, and common notions approach the equivocal, whereas
> demonstrative premisses are concerned with things proper to the same genus."

### 3. Sophistical Argument

Below dialectic lies sophistry: argument designed to produce **perplexity and doubt** in the
reader without genuinely engaging the truth. Averroes explicitly accuses al-Ghazālī of this on
multiple occasions:

> "And since Ghazali knew that all these arguments have no other effect than to bring doubts and
> perplexity to those who cannot solve them — which is an act of wicked sophists..."

Averroes is not accusing al-Ghazālī of conscious deception throughout; rather, he distinguishes
between al-Ghazālī's better arguments (which reach the level of dialectic) and his weaker ones
(which are sophistical in effect, whether or not intentionally so).

## Applied to al-Ghazālī's First Proof

The most detailed application of the demonstration/dialectic distinction appears in Averroes'
critique of al-Ghazālī's First Proof for temporal creation.

Al-Ghazālī's argument turns on the concept of "possibility" (*imkān*): before the world exists,
its existence is "purely possible," and a new determining principle (*murājjiḥ*) is needed to
actualize it. Averroes replies that "possible" is equivocal:

> "For the term 'possible' is used in an equivocal way of the possible that happens more often than
> not, of the possible that happens less often than not, and of the possible with equal chances of
> happening, and these three types of the possible do not seem to have the same need for a new
> determining principle."

Furthermore, "possible" applies in different senses to (a) the agent (capacity to act) and (b) the
patient (capacity to receive), and these have different logical relationships to external
determination. Al-Ghazālī's argument treats "possible" as a single, unambiguous term and builds
a syllogism on it — but a demonstrative syllogism requires premisses proper to its genus, free of
equivocation.

The same argument also conflates multiple distinct sub-questions — the nature of voluntary versus
natural agents, whether God resembles either, the difference between the eternal and the
eternal-through-another — into a single problem. Averroes identifies this as the sophistic error
of treating a plurality of questions as one.

## The Criterion of Truth: Sound Understanding vs. Consensus

A crucial corollary: Averroes rejects the claim that rational necessity (*ḍarūra*) can be
established by **universal assent**. Al-Ghazālī argues that if something were truly self-evident,
all rational minds would recognize it. Averroes replies:

> "It is not a condition of objective truth that it should be known to all. That anything should be
> held by all does not imply anything more than its being a common notion, just as the existence of
> a common notion does not imply objective truth."

The criterion is not majority agreement but **sound understanding** (*al-fahm al-salīm*) — the
disciplined intellect that applies the rules of demonstration and can distinguish genuine necessity
from apparent necessity:

> "The final criterion rests with the sound understanding which does not base itself on prejudice
> and passion, when it probes according to the signs and rules by which truth and mere opinion are
> logically distinguished. Likewise, if two people dispute about a sentence and one says that it is
> poetry, the other that it is prose, the final judgment rests with the 'sound understanding' which
> can distinguish poetry from prose, and with the science of prosody."

This appeal to the disciplined expert over popular consensus is characteristically Averroist and
links to his broader doctrine of the three classes of men (demonstrative, dialectical, rhetorical)
appropriate to different modes of address.

## The Charge Against al-Ghazālī's Destructive Method

Al-Ghazālī explicitly states in the First Discussion that the *Tahāfut al-Falāsifa* is a purely
destructive project — it aims to show that the philosophers lack demonstrative knowledge of the
world's eternity, without itself affirming temporal creation. He promises a future constructive work.

Averroes treats this as a structural failure:

> "To oppose difficulty with difficulty does not bring about destruction, but only perplexity and
> doubts in him who acts in this way, for why should he think one of the two conflicting theories
> reasonable and the opposite one vain? A perfect refutation would be one that succeeded in showing
> the futility of their system according to the facts themselves."

Purely destructive dialectic leaves the reader unable to orient toward the truth. Averroes also
notes the practical danger: al-Ghazālī promises a later constructive work (*The Golden Mean in
Dogmatic Beliefs*), but it may never appear — "perhaps he never composed it" — and readers would
be left permanently in perplexity. "It would have been necessary for him to begin by establishing
the truth, before starting to perplex and confuse his readers."

## Relation to Averroes' Broader Epistemology

The demonstration/dialectic distinction is not merely polemical in the *Tahāfut al-Tahāfut*. It
connects to Averroes' larger epistemological project, developed in his Aristotle commentaries:

- Demonstrative knowledge (*burhān*) is the province of **philosophers** trained in the *Posterior
  Analytics*; it produces genuine science (*ʿilm*).
- Dialectical argument is the province of **theologians** (*mutakallimūn*); it produces probable
  belief, not knowledge.
- Rhetorical argument is appropriate for the **masses**; it produces assent through imagery,
  emotion, and example.

The kalām tradition — [[kalam]] — practices dialectic and therefore occupies a middle position:
more rigorous than popular preaching, less rigorous than philosophy. Averroes respects dialectic
as having a legitimate function in disputation but denies it the epistemic status its practitioners
(and al-Ghazālī in particular) claim for it.

This epistemological hierarchy shapes Averroes' reading of [[philosophy-versus-theology-in-islam]]
as a conflict not between equally valid methods but between a higher and a lower form of inquiry —
a conflict the *Tahāfut al-Tahāfut* is designed to resolve in philosophy's favor.

## Historical Influence

The distinction as deployed in the *Tahāfut al-Tahāfut* became central to the **Latin Averroist**
tradition (see [[arabic-philosophy-latin-reception]]). Siger of Brabant, Boethius of Dacia, and
others in the thirteenth-century Arts faculty at Paris invoked the demonstrative/dialectical
distinction to separate the domains of philosophy and theology — philosophy proceeding by
demonstration, theology by faith and dialectical authority. This separation was condemned in the
1277 Paris condemnations, which listed among its propositions the claim that what is true in
philosophy may be false in theology.
