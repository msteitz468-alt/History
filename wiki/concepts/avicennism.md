# Avicennism

The reception and adaptation of [[ibn-sina|Avicenna's]] philosophical
system — particularly his metaphysics of essence and existence and his
proof of a Necessary Existent — by Latin scholastic thinkers following the
twelfth-century translation of his works into Latin, chiefly through the
[[dominicus-gundisalvi|Toledo School of Translators]].

## Definition and Scope

Avicennism in the Latin context denotes the substantial, often
insufficiently credited influence of Avicennan metaphysics on early
scholastic philosophy, distinct from the separate later tradition of
[[post-avicennan-philosophy|post-Avicennan philosophy]] that developed
within the Islamic East itself. Latin Avicennism shaped debates over the
distinction between essence and existence, the nature of universals, and
proofs for the existence of God among figures including William of
Auvergne and, more critically, Thomas Aquinas.

## Origin (Emic/Etic)

The term is a modern historiographical (etic) category used to trace a
specific line of textual and doctrinal influence; medieval thinkers
themselves engaged directly with Avicenna's texts rather than identifying
themselves as members of an "Avicennist" school in the way the term is used
by modern scholars.

## Applications and Examples

Latin Avicennism's central legacy is the essence-existence distinction,
which Aquinas both adopted and substantially modified, and Avicenna's
proof of a Necessary Being, influential on later scholastic natural theology
even as Latin philosophers increasingly synthesized it with Aristotelian and
Augustinian elements distinct from Avicenna's own system.

## Limitations and Critiques

Scholars debate how much of "Avicennism" in Latin scholasticism represents
direct, accurate transmission of Avicenna's actual positions versus
selective, sometimes distorting adaptation by translators and readers
working through an unfamiliar philosophical idiom and their own doctrinal
commitments.
