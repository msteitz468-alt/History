# Nuclear Pacific

A term capturing the Pacific Ocean and its island peoples as the primary geographic site for
nuclear weapons testing, nuclear diplomacy, popular resistance, and continuing environmental
and human rights struggles from 1945 to the present. As Barbara Rose Johnston writes, it is
"a term born from a uniquely resonant pairing of a science and technology that made its
historical appearance as an expression of military power in an Oceanian and archipelagic
world — and has legacies that extend thousands of years into the future." The concept spans
the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki (1945), decades of US, British, and French
nuclear testing across Pacific Island territories, the anti-nuclear movement, and the
Fukushima reactor crisis (2011).

Source: [[hattori-samson-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v2-2023]], ch. 56 (Barbara Rose Johnston).

## Definition and Scope

"The Nuclear Pacific" frames the entire arc from Hiroshima through Fukushima as a unified
phenomenon: "the Pacific has been a figurative and literal ground zero for nuclear diplomacy,
popular resistance, and continuing debates about human rights, international law, economic
and political power, and the environment." It encompasses:

- **Military violence**: the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki, August 1945
- **Testing as display**: 67+ nuclear bombs detonated in the Marshall Islands (1946–58);
  210 French tests in French Polynesia (1960–96); British tests at Christmas Island/Maralinga
- **Nuclear colonialism**: the structural use of colonially subordinated Pacific peoples and
  territories as sacrifice zones — "critically a story of militarism, colonialism, and the
  disproportionate burden borne by Indigenous peoples and ethnic minorities"
- **Anti-nuclear resistance**: the South Pacific Nuclear Free Zone movement, Treaty of
  Rarotonga (1985, fully established 1996), the Rainbow Warrior affair
- **Civilian nuclear disaster**: Fukushima Daiichi (2011) as a structural continuation of
  the same logic — expendable communities, government suppression, and health denial

## The Atomic Bombings: Origins of the Nuclear Pacific

The concept begins with the August 1945 destruction of Hiroshima and Nagasaki — "cautionary
tales of the destructive and radiological warfare that world powers have claimed to try to
avoid since 1945." Within months, Operation Crossroads (July 1946) detonated two atomic
bombs in Bikini Lagoon "before an audience of tens of thousands, including representatives
of the world's nations," inaugurating the Cold War as a demonstration of power in Oceania.
"Thus, with the Pacific War concluded, the Cold War began with military activities shifting
from the battlefield to the proving grounds."

## Nuclear Colonialism: Structure and Mechanisms

Johnston's central analytical contribution is framing Pacific testing not as a series of
discrete events but as "nuclear colonialism" — a systematically colonial structure with
identifiable mechanisms:

**Selection of sacrifice zones by political marginality.** "The vast and relatively
underpopulated expanses of Oceania proved ideal for nuclear testing by the Americans, British,
and French, especially as Islanders were politically marginal in essentially colonial
relationships in American Micronesia, the British Gilbert Islands, and French Polynesia."
Populations were given "minimalist information and false promises of a healthy return" before
detonations, or not notified at all.

**Research on non-consenting human subjects.** "At the dawn of the nuclear age much was
unknown about the nature and behaviour of unstable isotopes." Indigenous and ethnic communities
were studied precisely because "traditional lifeways reflected dependence upon and deep
immersion in the local environment," making their bodies ideal radiation indicators. The 1954
Bravo hydrogen bomb exposed Rongelap and Utrik Atoll communities; after evacuation, Rongelap
people were returned to their contaminated home in 1957 where they "began an unwitting
servitude as subjects in a 'living laboratory'." US medical teams visited regularly "to
collect information on radiogenic health — human subjects for radiation exposure from weapons
testing … the cycles of data collection continued for decades."

**Ecological destruction.** Between 1946 and 1958 the US conducted 23 tests around Bikini
Atoll and 44 around Enewetak, "vaporizing reefs, atomizing islands, and producing both
regional and global fallout with heaviest deposition in the Marshall Islands." The equivalent
of "more than 7,000 Hiroshima bombs" of radioactive fallout blanketed the Marshallese nation
from 20 tests, releasing 150 times more Iodine-131 than Chernobyl. These changes "heralded
the possibility of fundamental alteration to local and planetary systems themselves — as well
as to all of the living organisms they supported."

**Suppression of findings.** Research was "framed to adhere to official government narratives
of strategic necessity." The "assumption that radiogenic health effects must be demonstrated
through direct causality (one isotope, one outcome) meant that science on cumulative and
synergistic effects was not pursued." Contradictory findings were "censored and silenced."

## The Human Testimony: Lijon Eknilang and the Marshallese Voice

Johnston foregrounds the testimony of Lijon Eknilang, a Marshallese nuclear survivor, as
both historical evidence and political advocacy. On the Bravo fallout:

> "The fall-out that our bodies were exposed to caused the blisters and other sores we
> experienced over the weeks that followed. Many of us lost our hair, too. The fall-out was
> in the air we breathed, in the fresh water we drank, and in the food we ate during the
> days after Bravo. This caused internal exposure and sickness."

On intergenerational effects:

> "Many of my friends keep quiet about the strange births they had. In privacy, they give
> birth, not to children as we like to think of them, but to things we could only describe as
> 'octopuses', 'apples', 'turtles', and other things in our experience. We do not have
> Marshallese words for these kinds of babies because they were never born before the radiation
> came."

Eknilang testified before the World Court, asking it to provide "the safety of the environment
upon which their survival depends." Her testimonies "unified the claims of Indigenous peoples
around the world who have been dispossessed, have been experimented upon, and have suffered
from nuclear colonialism and militarism."

## French Nuclear Colonialism and Economic Dependency

France conducted 193 tests at Mururoa and Fangataufa atolls in the Tuamotu Archipelago
(1960–96), with a further 17 elsewhere. Declassified French Ministry of Defence papers
(published 2013 in *Le Parisien*) revealed that French authorities knew "plutonium fallout
hit the whole of French Polynesia, a much broader area than France had previously admitted."
Tahiti was exposed to 500 times the maximum accepted radiation levels over a two-day period
in July 1974. Around 150,000 military and civilian personnel were potentially exposed; at
the time of publication only eleven had received compensation.

The French programme created structural economic dependency: the Centre d'Expérimentation
du Pacifique (CEP) employed ~5,400 local workers at its peak (1967) and depopulated farming
and fishing sectors in favour of government wages. Food self-sufficiency in the 1950s
evolved into import dependence (three-quarters of food imported by the 1990s). When testing
scaled back, "the territorial government was in debt, producing only one-quarter of its own
revenues, the other 75 per cent issuing from France and almost half of that from military
expenditures."

## British Testing and the Terra Nullius Redux

Britain's Operation Grapple (Christmas and Malden Islands, 1957–58) and Australian
Maralinga tests replicated a familiar colonial logic. In "a remarkable perpetuation of
the terra nullius doctrine," government scientists declared the arid western lands of
South Australia empty and "therefore suitable for nuclear weapons testing." The
Yankunytjatjara and Pitjantjatjara were "constantly forming and re-forming, splitting and
recombining" across the Prohibited Zone. Fijian naval ratings were invited to Christmas
Island to "show the Navy how to live on a small Pacific Island" while atomic tests were
carried out — Ratu Penaia Ganilau attending without rubber boots because "the Navy couldn't
find a pair large enough for Ratu Penaia's feet" and later dying of leukemia, his family
attributing this to fallout exposure.

## Anti-Nuclear Resistance and the Treaty of Rarotonga

Protests mounted across Oceania from the 1970s. The South Pacific Forum appealed for an
end to tests. A People's Charter for a Nuclear Free and Independent Pacific (1983) "linked
opposition to testing with sovereignty questions." In 1985 the Rainbow Warrior, a Greenpeace
ship harassing French tests, was mined and sunk by French military saboteurs in Auckland
harbour. A South Pacific Nuclear Free Zone (SPNFZ) was declared in 1985 — the largest
contiguous nuclear-free zone in the world, covering more than half the southern hemisphere —
though "none of the nuclear powers — the United States, Great Britain, or France — supported
the agreement at all." Full formalisation with nuclear-power signatories came only in 1996.
Related: [[nuclear-free-pacific-movement]].

## The Runit Dome and Continuing Legacy

"Cleanup" of Enewetak (1977–80) scraped radioactive soil and dumped highly radioactive
debris into an unlined pit created by an earlier detonation on Runit Island, then capped
with 4 inches of cement. Radioactive bulldozers, cranes, boats were dumped into the lagoon.
The resulting dome "encases radioactive material that will remain hazardous for thousands
of years." A 2012 UN Human Rights Council report declared the Marshall Islands had suffered
"violations of humanitarian law resulting from the development, testing, and use of weapons
of mass destruction." Legal claims were processed through a framework of Western individual
property rights incompatible with Marshallese communal tenure — "culturally and historically,
land is not bought and sold in the Marshalls. Land and resources are held in a common rights
system, with use rights inherited through female descendants."

Social stigma compounded physical harm: "People didn't want to shake our hands for fear we
would contaminate them" (Alimira Matayoshi, 2001). Bias in education, courtship, and
employment "developed — and could persist across generations."

## Fukushima and Structural Continuity (2011)

The 2011 tsunami-induced breach of Fukushima Daiichi demonstrated that "nuclear colonialism
is not a history that exists only in a defined period." The plant's irregular workforce —
"by the early 1980s, irregular workers came to compromise nearly 90 per cent of all nuclear
workers" — was recruited from depopulated farming and fishing villages. Workers were "sworn
to secrecy, after a superficial safety education drill," then "sent into highly contaminated,
hot and wet labyrinthine areas." A Fukushima Health Management Survey estimated paediatric
thyroid cancers "several tens of times larger" than expected. Social suppression of questions
mirrored test-site patterns: bloggers were hacked, journalists died under mysterious
circumstances, and doctors advised against disclosing cancer diagnoses "to enhance employment
or marriage prospects."

By late 2011, cesium-134 and cesium-137 from Fukushima was present in Pacific bluefin
tuna taken off San Diego, linking Fukushima into a continuous Pacific radioecological system.

## Global Ecology Framework

Johnston advances a "global ecologies" analytical framework: the Nuclear Pacific is not
only a political or human rights history but a transformation of planetary ecologies —
"the alteration of entire environments." The rise of atomic science "not only grew hand in
hand [with militarism], but quickly established themselves as progenitors of completely
unique and novel ecologies — which themselves artificially created new elements with
radiogenic and mutagenic properties." This makes nuclear history "in many ways, a never-
ending story given the nature of radioactive half-life and related environmental health
threats."

## Historiography

Johnston identifies most Nuclear Pacific histories as either "anthropological studies of
communities displaced by nuclear testing" or analyses focused on "the region-wide political
machinations and implications of the nuclear Cold War." Environmental histories are only
beginning to emerge — using ethnographic data on pre-testing subsistence and scientific
data on contamination to trace the full ecological transformation of test atolls.

Key methodological challenge: security restrictions and military-industrial bias in research
design have suppressed and distorted the scientific record. The "assumption that radiogenic
health effects must be demonstrated through direct causality" excluded research on cumulative
and synergistic effects. Declassified materials (US 1994, France 2013) confirm systematic
concealment.
