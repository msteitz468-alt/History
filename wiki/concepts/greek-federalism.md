# Greek Federalism (the Koinon and the Alternatives to the Polis)

## The polis was not the only form

The independent **city-state** (*polis*) is so identified with Greek civilization that its
**alternatives** are easily overlooked. [[lewis-cah-v6-1994|CAH Vol. VI]] (Ch. 11, P. J.
Rhodes, "The Polis and the Alternatives") makes the fourth century the moment to take them
seriously: alongside the polis stood the **tribal/ethnos state**, the **federal league
(*koinon*)**, and the **territorial monarchy** — and it was these, not the polis, that
pointed toward the future. The chapter's closing question, "**the failure of the polis?**,"
asks whether the city-state's inability to combine into larger units doomed it before
Macedon.

## The ethnos and the koinon

In the less urbanized regions of Greece, peoples were organized **not by cities but by
tribes** (*ethne*) living in villages (*komai*) with some central organ for foreign policy:
- **Aetolia and Acarnania** in the late fifth century were communities of tribes with only
  loose central organization; over the fourth century the **Aetolian *koinon*** developed
  an **assembly**, a smaller deliberative **council (*synedrion*)** with cities represented
  in proportion to size, an inner board (*apokletoi*), and a single **general (*strategos*)**
  — becoming, by the Hellenistic age, a true **federation of cities** that could attach
  distant states by *isopoliteia* ("equal citizenship").
- **Thessaly** was organized in four **tetrads** under tetrarchs, who could unite to elect a
  single leader, the **tagos** — an office often left vacant, and fought over by the tyrants
  of **Pherae** (Jason, Alexander) and their opponents the Aleuadae of Larissa, before
  **Philip of Macedon** reunited Thessaly and had himself elected *archon*.
- The **Boeotian Confederacy** (revived by Thebes after 379; see
  [[thebes-hegemony-371bce]]) and the **Arcadian League** (with its federal capital
  **Megalopolis**, founded after the [[liberation-of-messenia-369bce|liberation of
  Messenia]]) were the most developed federal experiments of the period — though the
  Arcadian League's internal split led directly to **[[battle-of-mantinea-362bce|
  Mantinea]]**.

## Monarchy as an alternative

Rhodes treats **Macedon** as another alternative to the polis: a kingdom on the Greek
fringe that "retained its archaic monarchy" with "rudimentary" institutions — a hereditary
but army-acclaimed kingship, the king ruling through chosen **companions (*hetairoi*)**
rather than a constitution, with the assembly (the army) consulted chiefly for royal
succession and capital trials. This is the structure that, under **Philip** and
**Alexander**, would overwhelm the city-states. **[[dionysius-i|Dionysius I]]'s** Syracuse
was the other great monarchical alternative (see [[western-greek-tyranny]]).

## The "failure" of the polis?

The analytical core is whether the polis **failed**. Its strength — intense citizen
participation, the small face-to-face community — was also its weakness: city-states could
not durably **combine**, and the leagues that tried (Boeotian, Arcadian) tended to be
dominated by their leading city or torn by faction. Rhodes resists a simple verdict: the
polis remained the vital cell of Greek life (and would flourish, depoliticized, under the
Hellenistic kingdoms), but its inability to scale up left the field to **federation and
monarchy** — and ultimately to Macedon.
