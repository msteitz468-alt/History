# Greek Geometric Culture and Art

**Geometric** is the name — taken from its rectilinear, compass-and-ruler pottery
decoration — for the material culture of Greece in roughly the **ninth and eighth
centuries BCE**, the world in which historical Greece was born. [[boardman-cah-v3p1-1982|CAH
Vol. III, Part 1]] (Ch. 19, John Boardman) calls it the "**threshold of history**":
almost everything of Archaic and Classical Greece — its cities and their rivalries,
its established ruling families and defined lands, its representational art and its
writing — "can be seen to take its origin in the behaviour, culture, art of Geometric
Greece."

## A World Known Almost Wholly from Archaeology

Geometric Greece left no contemporary written history; it is reconstructed from
**pottery, graves, and sanctuaries**, supplemented by the indirect testimony of
[[homer|Homer]] and Hesiod. The evidence is "tangled and lacunose" — most Geometric
sites stayed inhabited into Classical times, so settlement and cemetery sizes are hard
to fix. Yet the signs of **recovery** are unmistakable: the number of identified
settlements **more than doubles** between the eleventh and eighth centuries (though
still barely a third of Late Bronze Age levels), and population rises steeply through
the ninth and eighth. Boardman links the slow pace of recovery partly to agriculture —
**olive groves**, needing decades of secure tending to restore, are a barometer of
returning stability and the confidence to "make provision for sons and grandsons."

## The Geometric Style and the Return of the Image

The **pottery** gives the period its name: a disciplined decoration of meanders,
concentric circles, zigzags, and banding, perfected above all at **Athens** (the
Dipylon workshops), where it reached monumental scale on grave-markers. Its most
historically charged development is the **return of the human figure**: after centuries
of pure abstraction, **figured scenes** reappear in the eighth century — funerals
(*prothesis* and *ekphora*), processions, battles on land and sea — the first Greek
**narrative art** since the Bronze Age, and the visual counterpart of the crystallizing
[[homer|Homeric]] tradition. Alongside the pottery, **bronze-working** revived
(tripod-cauldrons, figurines dedicated at the new sanctuaries), and the eighth century
saw the rise of the great **pan-Hellenic sanctuaries** (Olympia, Delphi) as arenas of
display.

## The Threshold of the Orientalizing and Historical Age

Geometric culture is the **launch-point** of historical Greece: it is the era of the
emergence of the [[city-state|polis]], of the adoption of the [[alphabet|alphabet]]
(~750), of the first overseas trade and colonization led by [[euboea|Euboea]], and of
the **orientalizing** absorption of eastern motifs that would dissolve the strict
Geometric style into the Archaic. By 700 BCE the elements were in place — writing,
representational art, the polis, overseas horizons, the Homeric inheritance — out of
which Archaic and Classical Greek civilization grew.

## Significance

- `part_of:` the [[greek-dark-age|Dark Age]] recovery and its transition to the
  [[archaic-period|Archaic Period]].
- `produced:` Greek **narrative figured art** (the Dipylon scenes) and the visual
  culture of the early polis.
- `concurrent_with:` the adoption of the [[alphabet|alphabet]], the rise of the
  [[city-state|polis]], and the [[euboea|Euboean]] orientalizing trade.

## Historiography

Boardman's chapter is a frankly **archaeological** synthesis, candid about the limits
of the evidence (undefinable settlement sizes, reliance on graves and sanctuaries) and
about the risk of reading Homer's "allusions to contemporary life" directly onto the
material record. The social and economic structure of Geometric and Archaic Greece is
deferred to CAH III, Part 3. The Aegean Geometric world is among the collection's
strengths.
