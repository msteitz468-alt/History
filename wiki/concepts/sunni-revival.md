# Sunni Revival

A historians' term for the resurgence of Sunni religious and political authority across the central Islamic world during the eleventh and twelfth centuries CE, following nearly a century in which major Shi'a-aligned dynasties — the [[buwayhid-dynasty|Buyids]] in Baghdad and Iran and the Fatimid Caliphate in Egypt — had dominated much of the region's political and religious life.

## Key Features

The revival is associated above all with the rise of the Sunni [[seljuk-empire|Seljuk]] Turks, whose vizier Nizam al-Mulk sponsored a network of state-funded Sunni colleges (the Nizamiyya madrasas, most famously in Baghdad) that institutionalized Sunni legal and theological education and produced a trained class of jurists and officials loyal to Sunni orthodoxy. The theologian al-Ghazali, associated with the Baghdad Nizamiyya, is often treated as the movement's leading intellectual figure, reconciling Sunni orthodoxy with Sufi mysticism and philosophical method in a synthesis that shaped subsequent mainstream Sunni thought.

## Emic vs. Etic

The "Sunni Revival" is an etic historiographical construct rather than a term contemporaries used of themselves; medieval Sunni scholars and patrons understood their activity as restoring and defending correct practice rather than participating in a labeled historical "movement."

## Contested Points

Historians debate how sharply to draw the line between genuine religious/doctrinal revival and the more straightforwardly political motives of Seljuk patronage (using madrasas to build a loyal administrative and juridical class); some scholars also question whether "revival" implies a prior decline of Sunnism that overstates Buyid- and Fatimid-era Shi'a dominance of everyday religious life, which for most ordinary Sunni populations may have continued relatively unbroken.
