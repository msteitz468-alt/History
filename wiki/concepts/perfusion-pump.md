# Perfusion Pump

## Overview
The Carrel-Lindbergh perfusion pump (often called the "glass heart" or perfusion apparatus) is a sterile device developed by surgeon Alexis Carrel and aviator Charles Lindbergh between 1930 and 1935. It was designed to keep human and animal organs functioning outside the body by circulating a synthetic nutrient fluid under sterile, pulsating gas pressure.

## Mechanical Design and Impact
The perfusion pump solved the problem of bacterial contamination—which had ruined previous organ-maintenance experiments—by using a three-chambered pyrex glass structure without rubber joints or seals. The pump utilized pulsating gas pressure (air and nitrogen) to simulate blood pressure and allow the organ to breathe. This research, published in *The Culture of Organs* (1938), proved that organs could be kept alive for weeks and laid the mechanical and conceptual foundation for the heart-lung machines used in modern surgery.
