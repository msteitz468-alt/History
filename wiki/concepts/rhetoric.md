# Rhetoric

The art of persuasive speech — taught, theorized, and contested across the Greco-Roman world for a thousand years, and, in Charles Freeman's account, "one of the supreme arts" of ancient civic culture. Rhetoric was the practical technology of politics and law in societies where public assemblies and courts decided everything by spoken persuasion, and it became the backbone of elite education from Classical Athens through the Roman Empire and into Christian Late Antiquity.

## Origins and the Sophists

Systematic rhetoric emerged in fifth-century Sicily and reached Athens as a "media event" with **Gorgias'** embassy of 427 BCE. Gorgias' *Encomium of Helen* likened speech to a *drug* acting on the soul — persuasion as a force that could produce conviction independent of truth. The itinerant [[sophists]] (Gorgias, Protagoras, Prodicus, Isocrates) taught rhetoric as a teachable skill, and their claim that the "weaker argument" could be made "stronger" made rhetoric morally suspect from the start.

## The Classical Debate

Rhetoric became the site of a foundational argument about knowledge and ethics:
- **Isocrates** insisted that genuinely persuasive power required the speaker's real moral character.
- **[[plato]]** (in the *Gorgias*) denounced rhetoric as a mere manipulative "knack" (*empeiria*), the counterfeit of true philosophical knowledge — flattery, not art.
- **[[aristotle]]** rehabilitated it analytically in the *Rhetoric*, defining persuasion through three modes: **ethos** (the speaker's character), **pathos** (the audience's disposition/emotion), and **logos** (the argument itself). This tripartite scheme remains the standard analytic vocabulary.

## Roman and Christian Continuity

Rhetoric passed into Rome as the core of aristocratic formation, its supreme practitioner and theorist being [[cicero]], and its educational codification owing much to Quintilian. It flourished again in the **Second Sophistic** of the Greek-speaking Empire (a revival of virtuoso public declamation, 1st–3rd centuries CE). It then shaped **Christian preaching**: Ambrose, John Chrysostom ("golden-mouthed"), and above all **Augustine** — a professional teacher of rhetoric before his conversion — carried classical persuasive technique into the sermon and the theological treatise.

## Contested Status

The ancient quarrel — rhetoric as ethical art (Isocrates, Aristotle, Quintilian's "good man skilled in speaking") versus rhetoric as amoral manipulation (Plato) — never resolved, and recurs whenever persuasion is separated from truth. Freeman explicitly links the ancient debate to modern political oratory. Rhetoric's institutional centrality also shaped what survives: an education built on declamation privileged certain genres and authors and helped fix the classical canon.
