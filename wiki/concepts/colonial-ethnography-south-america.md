## Definition and Scope

Colonial ethnography of South America designates the body of European writing — chronicles, relaciones, missionary accounts, grammar compilations, geographical questionnaires, legal depositions — that attempted to describe the peoples, societies, religions, and histories of native South Americans during the first two centuries of contact (c.1500–1700). This literature is the primary external record for the early colonial period and the major source of indirect evidence about pre-Columbian societies outside the Andes.

Sabine MacCormack's analysis emphasizes that this literature was produced from a "handful of heavily guarded sites, most of which were on the coast." European knowledge of the interior was radically limited: "all the early maps of South America, which provide much information about coastal regions and much less about the interior." The cognitive geography of the continent as Europeans understood it was therefore fundamentally distorted from how its inhabitants experienced it. When the Quirandíes Indians on the Paraná River in 1527 "were able to inform a Spaniard that they had heard about the ebb and flow of the Pacific Ocean," they demonstrated a continental awareness that Europeans mapping from coasts inward could not yet share.

Writing was, as MacCormack notes, "a tool of the invaders, an instrument to organize and control subject populations, preserving, for the most part, only those aspects of their cultures, religions, and historical memories that were meaningful in the new colonial context." Yet because "a written text, once it leaves the writer's hands, acquires a certain autonomy," colonial ethnographic writing also preserved — sometimes unintentionally — information whose value was recognized only later.

## The Conceptual Frameworks Imposed by Europeans

European observers brought a set of pre-formed cognitive structures that shaped what they could and could not perceive:

**Natural law and behetría**: The Infante Don Juan Manuel's fourteenth-century distinction between Christian and gentile societies — the latter living "without ceremonies or worship" but "with much justice" according to natural law — recurred constantly. Pascual de Andagoya's description of the province of Cueva (Panama, 1514) found people who "lived in much justice, in the law of nature, without any ceremony or worship" — a framing that simultaneously acknowledged their social order and denied their full religious status. For decentralized societies lacking visible hierarchical authority, the medieval Castilian term *behetría* (a settlement free to choose its own lord or omit doing so) was pressed into service; it was applied to Colombian and North Andean societies by Pedro Cieza de León.

**Paradise and the golden age**: Pedro Vaz de Caminha's 1500 letter describing the Tupinambá on first contact framed them as the paradisal innocent — "as close as might be imagined to humankind's paradisal origins." This was partly genuine millennial expectation (Jerusalem's reconquest, the coming of Antichrist, paradisal society) and partly a projection screen for European critics of their own society. Jean de Lery, writing about [[tupinamba]] nudity, explicitly used it as an occasion to compare unfavorably the "elaborate attire, paint, wigs, curled hair, great ruffs and infinity of trifles" of French women.

**Classical antiquity as interpretive grid**: European ceremony, law, and governance provided constant analogues. Tupinambá chiefs who led by persuasion and consensus reminded observers of Spartan elders who made collective decisions about war and peace. The Tupinambá funerary eulogy for a dead warrior recalled "Roman funerary eulogies as described in a famous passage by the Greek historian Polybius." Lery found the Tupinambá to practice the Justinianic precepts *honeste vivere, alterum non laedere, suum cuique tribuere* — "to live honorably, to avoid harming one's neighbor, and to give to each his own."

**Gender inversion**: The "women warriors" spoken of by Guaraní translators gave European resonance to the ancient Amazonian myth, because "to look for Amazons in an actual social and historical reality...meant thinking that the human world was somehow capable of attaining a completeness in which even an imaginary social order could come to fruition." Cabeza de Vaca's observation that Guaycurú women could exempt captives from death led him to declare: "For sure, these women enjoy more freedom than our Lady Queen Isabel gave to the women of Spain."

**The "darkness of paganism"**: European theological assumptions dictated that even peoples who appeared to have "no religion" must have something that could be analyzed within the framework of salvation history. When Lery found that the Tupinambá concept of Toupan (associated with thunder) did not map onto the Christian God — because when he suggested it did, the Tupinambá declared such a frightening being "was good for nothing" — the gap between indigenous and Christian categories became an occasion for theological elaboration rather than anthropological curiosity.

## The Tupinambá and Cannibalism

The [[tupinamba]] of coastal Brazil, who occupied the Atlantic coast until mid-seventeenth century, provided the most extensive early ethnographic corpus: Hans Staden (captive 1553, published 1557), André Thevet (1555), Jean de Lery (1557), Claude d'Abbeville and Yves d'Evreux (early seventeenth century). Theodore de Bry's Frankfurt compilations (from 1592) disseminated this material widely across Europe in sensationalized illustrated form that, MacCormack notes, "read in and reproduced...perspectives on the lives and manners of savages" — stripping out the "deep ceremoniousness of village life" and "complex courtesies" that the original authors had actually recorded.

Ritual cannibalism drove European fascination and theoretical debate: "all the early observers noted, cannibalism was driven not by hunger but by the desire for vengeance." The Tupinambá sought to capture enemies, glorify them, then kill and eat them so as to gain new enemies in turn — "it is vengeance which alone connects those who have lived (and died) and those who will live (to die)" in what Carneiro da Cunha and Viveiros de Castro would later analyze as a distinctive mode of historical consciousness. Yves d'Evreux was struck by Tupinambá funerary customs: dead warriors were interred with "flour, water, and meat, as well as his axes, his fishing hooks, his bow and arrows, and a fire in a small pit," and followers asked him to "remember them to their fathers and grandfathers, their kinsfolk and friends who dance beyond the mountains of the Andes." D'Evreux's Christian counterpart was to insist on burial without grave goods in a Christian cemetery — achievable only by coercion.

Montaigne, who knew the accounts and "observed and abhorred the calculated atrocities of confessional warfare, found Indian man-eating less inhuman than the deeds of his own countrymen." Lery, who survived the siege of Sancerre in 1573 where human flesh was eaten under starvation, brought exceptional moral authority to the same judgment.

The Tupinambá also posed the question of political authority in its starkest form. They had "no public domain of any kind." No legal system. No coercive chief. Multiple chiefs who had "no authority other than giving advice...in their assembly or carbet which they hold every evening." Leaders persuaded rather than commanded; they explained "the traditions of the past as they applied to the present" and created consensus for collective action. "They believed that there existed sufficient territory to meet everyone's needs. The only grounds for war was vengeance, which did not produce property or power but was an end in itself." On the currency question: when a Tupinambá who had acquired a blackened base-metal coin tried to purchase oysters with it, was told he needed a "white metal" coin, whitened it with chalk, and obtained his oysters amidst laughter — the episode exposed the full gulf between exchange economies.

## The Guaraní and Rio de la Plata

German gunner Ulrich Schmidel (1534–1554) and Spanish adelantado Alvar Núñez Cabeza de Vaca (1541–1544) produced the main accounts of the Guaraní and neighboring peoples of the Paraguay/Paraná basin. The Guaraní — closely related linguistically to the [[tupinamba]] and using Guaraní as a regional lingua franca — were "widely feared" but on friendly terms with the Spanish. They practiced settled agriculture (maize, manioc, potatoes) supplemented by honey and domestic fowl, lived in wooden palisaded villages, and produced cotton textiles of exceptional quality (Schmidel noted that "their women weave large cotton shawls, very choice, like fine cloth from Arles, into which they work many kinds of figures, such as deer, ostriches, Indian sheep"). The king of the Xarayes hosted a dignified court "surrounded by hunters, dancers, and musicians, while attendants cleaned his path and scattered flowers and herbs where he was to walk" — described by Schmidel as simply holding court "like the greatest lord in the land."

The interplay of Guaraní and Spanish mythic imaginations produced the idea of "Camire/Candire" — a land without evil and without mortality somewhere to the west. This was simultaneously a Guaraní eschatological concept (connected to the migrations of c.1522 and 1549 toward a "land of immortality and perpetual rest") and the Spanish legend of El Dorado, refracted through the Amazonian contact zone.

## The Inka Empire and the Andean Shift in Ethnographic Method

The encounter with the [[inca-empire]] transformed European ethnographic method. The sheer institutional complexity of Tawantinsuyu — stone cities that "impressed many Europeans as being superior even to the admired architectural creations of the Romans"; a Sun temple; chosen women weaving state cloth and brewing maize beer; khipu record-keepers; a hierarchy from Sapa Inka through regional kuraka to village head — forced observers to abandon the natural-law framework and develop something more like political history.

Pedro Cieza de León (traveling the Andes 1541–1550) was the first to understand the contrast: where the Colombian behetría could sustain itself ecologically because fertile land and forest mobility made flight from any coercive power feasible, Andean valleys were "desolate, full of mountain highlands and snowy expanses," so populations had no alternative to state subjection. Ecology thus explained political structure — a strikingly proto-determinist argument. "Because they cannot live alone, they must be subject and not give up their lands."

At the [[collision-at-cajamarca-1532]], Spaniards who had been trained in elaborate courtly ceremony recognized and described the Inka court's ceremonial in its own terms: Atawallpa's silver-poled litter, the dancers and musicians in the vanguard, attendants sweeping the sacred path, great lords approaching barefoot and bearing burdens, the parasol shading the sacred person. Pedro Pizarro "still remembered how the gold and silver ornaments of Atawallpa's followers had sparkled in the afternoon sunlight" forty years later. When the last independent Inka Tupac Amaru I was executed in Cusco in 1572, "over a hundred thousand Indian men and women" fell into silence at his raised hands, demonstrating Inka authority even at its end — an authority so deep that the Spanish "ordered [his head] to be removed...because a large multitude of Indians were worshiping and adoring it without even stopping to eat."

The linguistic work of Fray Domingo de Santo Tomás (Quechua grammar and lexicon, 1560) went beyond ethnographic description into political argument: declaring Quechua "as elegant, ordered, and articulate a language as Latin and Spanish" was implicitly arguing for Andean self-government with minimal Spanish interference. His questionnaire for tribute assessors highlighted the crucial Inka distinction between labor dues and tribute from personal possessions — a distinction lawyers like Juan Polo de Ondegardo believed essential for administering the Andean population without destroying it.

After 1572 (Tupac Amaru I's execution), the character of Andean ethnographic inquiry shifted: the prospect of a negotiated Inka-Spanish co-governance closed, and Spanish enquiry became more administrative and extractive.

## Limitations and Critiques

- **Coastal bias**: The entire corpus is heavily skewed toward Atlantic and Pacific coastal zones and the Inka heartland. The Amazon interior, the Orinoco basin, the vast grasslands of the Southern Cone, and the Paraguayan mission frontier are represented fragmentarily and late.
- **Framework imposition**: Every major European category — natural law, behetría, paradise, the golden age, Amazons, the "darkness of paganism," Roman parallels — deformed the observer's vision before the pen touched paper. MacCormack: European cognitive structures "conditioned and guided their perceptions of the indigenous world."
- **Translation and intermediary distortion**: "Without guides and interpreters, the European penetration of the continent would have been inconceivable." Yet the chain from indigenous speaker through translator (often Guaraní-speaking prisoners of war) to Spanish reporter to European publisher involved multiple transformations at each step.
- **The De Bry effect**: Theodore de Bry's Frankfurt illustrated compilations (1592 onward) spread sensationalized images of "savages" that overrode the more careful original accounts. European audiences "read in and reproduced...perspectives on the lives and manners of savages" from De Bry rather than from Lery or Staden.
- **Selective preservation**: Writing "supplanted, and as a result destroyed, alternative, indigenous methods of handling and preserving information." What survived was what colonial institutions found administratively useful or theologically significant.
