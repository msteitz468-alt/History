## Definition

An analytical framework for examining women's participation in the sciences across history, encompassing both the institutional and cultural barriers that long excluded or marginalized women from formal scientific training, employment, and recognition (exclusion from universities and academies, restricted career paths, unequal credit for collaborative work) and the substantial contributions women nonetheless made, often against considerable obstacles.

## Emic vs. Etic

Largely an etic historiographical lens, developed mainly from the later twentieth century onward, though the barriers themselves were often explicitly named and contested by contemporaries — including the women scientists who faced them.

## Significance

[[actors/curie-marie|Marie Curie]] stands as one of the most prominent case studies: the first woman to win a Nobel Prize (Physics, 1903, shared with her husband Pierre and Henri Becquerel), the first person to win a second Nobel in a different science (Chemistry, 1911), and the first woman to hold a professorship at the Sorbonne — achievements won despite exclusion from the French Academy of Sciences and public controversy over her personal life that nearly cost her the second prize. Her career illustrates both the exceptional talent required for women to gain recognition in this period and the persistent institutional resistance they faced even after achieving it.
