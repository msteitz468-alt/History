# Shinto

The indigenous religious tradition of **Japan** — the "**way of the *kami***" — centred on the veneration
of *kami* (deities, spirits, and sacred powers of nature, place and ancestry), purity and pollution,
shrine ritual, and a mythology binding the islands, the gods, and the **imperial line** into one sacred
order.

## Core Features (emic)

- ***Kami*** — the innumerable sacred presences: gods of the myths (the sun goddess **Amaterasu**, the
  storm god Susanoo), but also spirits of mountains, rivers, trees, ancestors and extraordinary things.
- **Purity and ritual.** The tradition centres on **purification** (*harae*), the avoidance of pollution
  (death, blood), offerings and festivals (*matsuri*) at **shrines** (*jinja*), rather than on doctrine
  or scripture.
- **Sacred kingship.** Its founding mythology — recorded in the **[[kojiki-yasumaro|Kojiki]]** (712) and
  the *Nihon Shoki* (720) — descends the **imperial house from Amaterasu**, making the emperor the
  ritual link between the *kami* and the realm.

## History

Originally a diffuse complex of local cult and myth, the tradition was given **literary and political
form** in the eighth century, when the **Yamato** court commissioned the Kojiki and Nihon Shoki to
**legitimate imperial rule**. From the sixth century it coexisted and fused with imported **[[buddhism|
Buddhism]]** (and Confucian and Daoist ideas) in a long syncretism (*shinbutsu-shūgō*); "**Shinto**"
emerged as a name precisely to mark off the native *kami*-way from Buddhism. In the modern era the state
forcibly **separated Shinto from Buddhism** (1868) and fashioned **State Shinto** around the emperor and
nation — an ideology central to imperial Japan until 1945, after which Shinto was disestablished.

## Significance and Debate

Shinto is fundamental to **Japanese identity, ritual life and the imperial institution**, and its myths
(via the Kojiki) pervade Japanese culture. Scholars debate how far a unified "Shinto" existed before it
was constructed as a category in opposition to Buddhism and, later, weaponized as State Shinto — so the
term is partly a modern, retrospective label for a long and varied tradition.
