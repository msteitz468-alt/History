# Tribal Self-Determination

Post-[[termination-policy]] framework of federal (and comparative North American) policy and Native politics emphasizing inherent tribal governmental authority, jurisdiction, resource control, and government-to-government relations with settler states. Closely intertwined with the process page [[native-american-renaissance-1960-1995]] (Washburn’s CHNPA I.2 ch. 15 is the principal source for this ingest).

## Emic and etic

- **Etic (policy label):** “Self-determination without termination” names the post-1970 U.S. executive doctrine (Nixon 1970; reaffirmed Reagan 24 Jan. 1983) rejecting 1950s termination while promoting tribal self-government. Statutes (esp. Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act, P.L. 93-638, 4 Jan. 1975) operationalize contracting of federal services to tribes.
- **Emic (Native politics):** Tribal leaders seek restoration or expansion of jurisdiction, recognition, and economic control; pan-Indian radicals sometimes challenged existing tribal governments as unrepresentative (see [[american-indian-movement]], [[wounded-knee-occupation-1973]]). Canadian Aboriginal peoples use parallel language of aboriginal title, treaty rights, and self-government under Constitution Act 1982.

## Policy genealogy (United States)

1. **IRA inheritance.** [[indian-reorganization-act-1934]] established representative tribal constitutions for accepting tribes; Navajo and others refused. Collier’s tribal-unity model subsumed village/band/clan units (Hopi, Sioux critiques of “boilerplate” governments, energy-company control, corruption). IRA structures later underwrote Reagan-era government-to-government practice.
2. **Termination and repudiation.** 1950s Eisenhower-era termination (Menominee, Klamath, HCR 108) identified with “freedom from federal supervision”; results widely judged disastrous; Menominee Restoration 1973 signaled official end. LBJ-era “gradual termination” talk (Udall) coexisted with Great Society funding surge (OEO → ANA).
3. **Self-determination statutes and IHS.** Transfer Act 1954 moved Indian Health Service to Public Health Service; P.L. 93-638 let tribes manage IHS programs—by end of 1980s more than one-third of service units tribally operated; Indian Health Care Improvement Act 1976 (amended 1980). Everett R. Rhoades, M.D. (Kiowa), directed IHS 1982–91.
4. **Government-to-government rhetoric.** From 1981 Reagan administration declared federal–tribal relation specifically government-to-government; cross-deputizing of officers; tribal auto plates (Red Lake Chippewa 1974); *Oklahoma Tax Commission v. Sac and Fox Nation* (17 May 1993) barred state income/vehicle taxes on members in Indian Country—Court recognized sovereign self-governance “within the family of governments in the federal constitutional system.”
5. **Indian preference and BIA.** *Morton v. Mancari* (1974) upheld expanded Indian preference; BIA workforce ~87% Indian; dual narrative of Washington vs tribal authority persisted.
6. **Reagan Commission critique.** Presidential Commission on Indian Reservation Economies (report Nov. 1984) charged BIA consumed >⅔ of budget on itself, overregulation deterred investment, trust status and sovereign immunity discouraged business—used to justify market-oriented self-determination.

**AUTHOR POSITION (Washburn):** Dependency on outside assistance is the Achilles heel of tribal governments (quoting NCAI president Joseph B. DeLaCruz); 1990s leaders increasingly looked to market and tribal initiative rather than federal solutions alone. Contested among Native and non-Native critics who stress underfunding, trust obligations, and structural barriers.

## Jurisdiction and courts

- ~150 tribal courts / Courts of Indian Offenses largely under IRA constitutions; National American Indian Court Judges Association (1968); NARF (1970 California → Boulder 1971).
- *Williams v. Lee* (1959): non-Indian storekeeper could not sue Navajo debtor in Arizona state court—protects tribal-court authority.
- *Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez* (1978): individual U.S. citizen rights (enrollment discrimination claim by Julia Martinez) must yield to tribal regulations; Indian Civil Rights Act 1968 remedies against tribes only in tribal courts.
- *Oliphant v. Suquamish* (1978): tribes lack criminal jurisdiction over non-members on reservation; power to tax/regulate outsider businesses retained.
- 1991 Bush meeting: leaders sought bills to “restore” jurisdiction over non-Indians and non-member Indians; Congress later authorized criminal prosecution of non-member Indians, not non-Indians.
- **AUTHOR POSITION (Washburn):** Continued petitions for federal recognition of remnant “tribes” are best evidence that tribal existence enjoys a renaissance; U.S. nearly unique in recognizing permanent self-governing aboriginal tribes whose authority is inherent, not state-derived.

## Treaty rights and resources

Treaty fishing, hunting, water, and land-claim litigation expanded after 1960. *Washington et al. v. Washington State Commercial Passenger Fishing Vessel Assn.* (1979): 1854–55 treaty phrase “right of taking fish … in common with all citizens of the Territory” means equal share of take between present-day Indians and non-Indians. Gaming under Indian Gaming Regulatory Act 1988 became a major late-period economic instrument of self-determination (see process page).

## Canadian and Greenland analogues

Not identical legal forms, but Washburn treats them within the same comparative renaissance:

- Canada never comfortable with “aboriginal title” phrasing; Indian Act 1876; post-WWII assimilation assumption; 1969 White Paper termination analogue withdrawn 1971; *Calder* 1973; James Bay Agreement 1975; Constitution Act 1982 (Indians, Inuit, Métis); Penner Committee 1983 recommended Indian governments as another order of government; Nunavut 1993.
- Greenland Home Rule 1979 as nativist political autonomy within Danish realm.
- Fear of losing rights under BIA-abolition or Canadian responsibility-shifting proposals (Robert Kennedy episode; 1969 Canadian analogue): “The BIA may be a son-of-a-bitch … but it is our son-of-a-bitch.”

## Critiques and limits

- Subtribal legitimacy disputes (Hopi villages; Sioux); corruption cases (Navajo MacDonald).
- Supreme Court “anguish” cases (*Oliphant*; *Lyng* 1988 sacred sites).
- Federal funding scale (~$3B/year 1980s) leads some non-Indians to dismiss tribal governments as non-serious—Washburn reports the perception.
- Alaska’s corporate model under [[alaska-native-claims-settlement-1971]] deliberately avoids IRA-style permanently racial institutions—status still contested.
- Radical 1960s–70s challenge vs later conventional politics: **AUTHOR POSITION (Washburn)** that radicals ceased attacking tribal governments as entities and sought to influence them.
