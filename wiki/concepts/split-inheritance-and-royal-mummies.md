# Split Inheritance and the Royal Mummies

One of the most distinctive — and consequential — institutions of the [[inca-empire|Inca empire]] was
**split inheritance**, bound up with the cult of the **royal mummies**. Terence D'Altroy
([[daltroy-incas-2004|*The Incas*]]) presents it as a central key to why Tawantinsuyu expanded so
explosively: it built **perpetual conquest into the very rules of succession**.

## The institution

When an Inca ruler died, his **office and political authority** passed to his most able son — but
**not his property**. Instead, the dead king's lands, palaces, servants, and wealth were retained, in
perpetuity, by a corporate descent group called the **panaqa**, formed from his other descendants, whose
task was to **maintain his estate and venerate his mummy** forever. The mummy (*mallki*) was not
"dead" in the European sense: it was kept, fed, clothed, consulted through **mediums**, carried out to
the great plazas of [[cuzco|Cuzco]] for ceremonies of state, and "spoke" in political affairs.
**Queens (*qoya*)** had their own panaqa and mummies and likewise continued to participate. By 1532
there were some **ten royal panaqa** in Cuzco, a society in which the **honored dead remained active
players**.

## The engine of expansion

The political logic is what makes the institution famous (an argument associated with **Geoffrey Conrad
and Arthur Demarest**, *Religion and Empire*). Because a new ruler inherited the **throne but not the
treasury** — the previous king's wealth was locked up forever in his cult — each emperor came to power
**land-poor** and had to **acquire his own estates and revenue from scratch**. The only way to do that
was **fresh conquest**. Split inheritance thus created a structural, self-perpetuating **imperative to
expand**: every reign needed new territory to fund the living court *and* the ruler's own future mummy
cult. D'Altroy ties the spectacular [[inca-empire|royal estates]] of the Sacred Valley
(**Machu Picchu**, Ollantaytambo, Pisac — all Pachacuti's) directly to this system: they were private
domains held first by the ruler and then "in trust for his reverence."

## Significance and debate

The model elegantly links **religion, succession, and military expansion** into a single mechanism, and
helps explain both the pace of Inca conquest and its potential fragility (an ever-growing burden of
dead kings' estates competing with the living state for land and labour). It also illuminates the
**Waskhar–Atawallpa civil war**: the panaqa of the royal mummies were powerful factional players in the
succession crisis that the Spanish exploited. The institution is reconstructed from the Spanish
*crónicas* (Betanzos, Cobo) and is therefore subject to the usual cautions about that **filtered,
victors'-and-conquerors' record**; the strong "split inheritance drove all expansion" thesis is debated,
with critics noting that ideology, security, and opportunity also propelled conquest. But the mummy cult
itself is well attested — the Spanish were sufficiently disturbed by the veneration of the royal mallki
that they **hunted down and confiscated the mummies** in the 1550s–60s to break the old order.
