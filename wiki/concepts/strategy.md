# Strategy (the Practice of Strategy)

**Strategy** is the linking of **political ends to means** — including the threat or use
of armed force — in a contest of opposing wills. The standard short definition from
[[cambridge-history-of-strategy-v1|Duyvesteyn & Heuser]] is "**a comprehensive way to try
to pursue political ends, including the threat or actual use of force, in a dialectic of
wills**." This page treats strategy as a *practice* (what rulers and polities actually did)
rather than as a body of *theory*, following the comparative, global approach of the
**Cambridge History of Strategy** (2024).

## Emic and etic: the word vs. the thing

The term is etically applied. **"Strategy"** derives from the Greek *strategos* ("army
leader, general"); the abstract noun *strategía* in something like its modern sense was
coined in the **Byzantine** Empire (Emperor Leo VI, c. 900) and entered Western European
languages only around **1800** (the translation of Leo's *Tactica*, and the era of the
French Revolution). One school therefore holds that **strategy did not exist before the
word** — that it is a product of the "military Enlightenment," Napoleonic mass war, and the
modern territorial state.

Duyvesteyn and Heuser **reject** this. To detect strategy before the term, they adopt
Kimberly Kagan's working definition: evidence of strategy exists wherever leaders "**set
objectives, establish priorities among them, and allocate resources to them**, whether or
not they develop or keep to long-range systematic plans." By looking at the *choices* made
about raising, equipping, and deploying force (cavalry vs. infantry, army vs. navy, fortify
vs. attack), strategic decision-making can be inferred across "any political unit, polity or
grouping" — not only the modern state. On this view strategy is a **global, eternal
phenomenon**, traceable from ancient Mesopotamia and China onward.

## The tools of strategy

Strategy is not only war-fighting. Its instruments, the editors stress, are far wider than
the kinetic: **armed force** (the most powerful instrument); **economic leverage** (trade
and its denial, tribute, subsidies, *Danegeld*, bribes); the **surrender of territory or
resources** to protect something more valuable; **alliances** (short-term coalitions, and
kinship real or manufactured — above all the **dynastic marriage**); and **propaganda and
narrative** (the Assyrian king boasting on stone of sacked cities; passing off draws as
triumphs) to rally support, deter rivals, and shore up the ruler's own position. Strategy is
thus **instrumental and relational** — an ends–means link plus assumptions about an
adversary's plans.

## The central finding: strategy is messy

The Cambridge History's most striking conclusion is **deflationary**. Against the "highly
stylised representation" of strategy in the strategic-studies literature — clean
prioritisation, ends rationally matched to means — the case studies reveal a practice that
is "**messy, frustrating and often guided by luck rather than anything else**." Objectives
are **unstable**; the alignment of objectives and resources is frequently **faulty**;
strategy is often a one-sided exercise that ignores the adversary's agency. In case after
case (Napoleon, the 19th-c. guerrilla, the American Civil War, the Russian Empire, even the
Second World War) the contributors find strategic practice "**haphazard and incoherent if
not lacking altogether**." The editors ask whether strategy is better explained by a model
of "**muddling through and ad hockery**" than by far-sighted prioritisation — while still
insisting that deliberate ends–means reasoning is genuinely and widely attested.

## The universality question

The series' organizing question is whether strategic practice has **universal features** or
is **culture-specific** — testing strategic studies' assumption of an "immutable underlying
unity of all strategic calculations." The comparative answer is mixed and is developed on
the [[strategic-practice-across-civilizations]] page: some features recur strikingly across
unconnected civilizations (the aspiration to **universal monarchy**, **peacetime
deterrence**, **dynastic-marriage diplomacy**), while much is contingent on culture,
technology, and material circumstance — Clausewitz's dictum that "every age has its own kind
of war."

## Limitations

As an etic category, "strategy" risks **anachronism** (reading modern instrumental
rationality into pre-modern actors) and **over-attribution** (inferring deliberate plans
from outcomes that were really luck or muddle — the very thing the editors warn against). The
evidence is also radically uneven: the *reasoning* behind decisions survives mainly for
resource-rich states, and almost never for non-state actors (guerrillas, insurgents),
biasing the record toward states and the West.
