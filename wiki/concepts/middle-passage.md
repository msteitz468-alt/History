# Middle Passage

The forced transatlantic voyage of enslaved Africans from West and Central African ports to the Americas — the "middle" leg of the Atlantic triangular trade, between the outbound voyage of European ships carrying manufactured goods to Africa and the return voyage carrying American commodities (sugar, tobacco, cotton) to Europe. Conducted over roughly four centuries (mid-15th to mid-19th century), the Middle Passage transported an estimated 12.5 million enslaved Africans, of whom roughly 1.8 million died during the crossing itself from disease, malnutrition, suffocation, and violence under conditions of extreme overcrowding.

Ships packed captives below deck in configurations designed to maximize the number transported relative to profit margins, producing horrific mortality and morbidity; surviving accounts (including former captives such as Olaudah Equiano) describe the voyage's physical and psychological brutality in detail. The Middle Passage functioned as both a demographic and cultural rupture, severing enslaved Africans from their natal communities while producing new, syncretic Afro-American cultural forms in the process of forced transport and subsequent creolization in the Americas.

## Significance

The Middle Passage is the central atrocity of the Atlantic slave trade and a foundational historical experience underlying the African diaspora in the Americas, its demographic scale and human cost central to modern reckonings with the trade's legacy.
