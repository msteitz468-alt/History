## Definition

A **cacicazgo** is a polity headed by a hereditary chief (cacique) whose authority rested on ranked lineage, redistribution of prestige goods, ceremonial functions, and control of exchange networks. The term derives from the Arawak word for "chief" (recorded by Spanish colonisers in the late 15th–early 16th century) and was subsequently applied by anthropologists as the Antillean instance of the broader cross-cultural concept of "chiefdom" — a politically centralised, rank-based society above the tribal level but below the state.

In the pre-Columbian Caribbean context, cacicazgos are most clearly attested in Puerto Rico and Hispaniola at the time of Spanish contact (c. AD 1492–1520), but their archaeological roots extend to the post-Saladoid period (c. AD 900–1200) visible in settlement clusters, ceremonial plazas and stone-lined courts (batey), and shifts in burial practice from communal clan cemeteries to household lineage interments.

## Evidence for Ranked Societies in the Caribbean

### Spanish documentary sources (used cautiously)

Spanish chroniclers identified seventeen regional chiefs (caciques) in Puerto Rico at contact, each associated with a major river drainage; their villages were supported by village headmen (see Coll y Toste 1907; Rouse 1952). In Hispaniola, five paramount chiefs (caciques principales) each controlled multiple subordinate chiefs. The cacique presided over redistribution of food and prestige goods, organised labour, adjudicated disputes, and directed communal ritual — functions consistent with ethnographic definitions of chiefdom-level leadership.

### Archaeological evidence

**Settlement hierarchy:** The post-AD 900 landscape in Puerto Rico shows a two-tier structure of small hamlets and nuclear-family villages allied in "settlement clusters," with larger central nodes at ceremonial centres. This is consistent with multi-level political integration, though Keegan and Hofman (2017) and Torres (2012) emphasise the limits of purely hierarchical readings.

**Tibes Ceremonial Centre:** Located on the Río Portugués north of modern Ponce, active c. AD 700–1200. Seven parallel-sided stone-lined courts, a large quadrangular plaza, and a unique circular "Plaza de Estrella" cover 16 hectares. No evidence of permanent large-scale habitation or "elite" foodways at the site; labour estimates for court construction do not approach monumental scale (Torres et al. 2014). Keegan and Hofman interpret the site as a venue for formalised inter-community ceremonial exchange ("star formation exchange" following Rubel and Rosman 1978) rather than a seat of hierarchical political authority.

**Caguana Ceremonial Centre (Capá):** Active c. AD 1200–1500, near Utuado in northwest Puerto Rico. Fifteen precincts; 22 freestanding monoliths with petroglyphs narrating indigenous mythology facing each other across a central plaza. The spatial layout replicates Tibes at larger scale and with more elaborate artistic investment, suggesting a shift toward more concentrated symbolic authority — consistent with more developed cacicazgo organisation — under Hispaniolan cultural influence (Chicoid/Meillacoid).

**Batey (ball court/plaza):** Stone-lined courts occur at virtually every scale of settlement from small hamlets to major ceremonial centres. Sizes vary enormously. In residential contexts, courts appear to have formalised community exchange and passage, providing a neutral zone (Keegan and Hofman 2017). In larger ceremonial contexts, courts structured multi-community exchange events at intervals of years or decades. The term *batey* (the Arawak word for the ball game played in these courts) is often used interchangeably with the courts themselves.

**Burial evidence:** In Saladoid times (c. 400 BCE–AD 500), burials concentrated in central communal plazas, interpreted as clan cemeteries and/or ancestor veneration. After AD 500, burials shift to beneath domestic structures, reflecting growing household-level lineage identity and what may be the materialisation of inherited status attached to household "Houses" (Morsink 2011). This shift is a symptom of emerging ranked social differentiation at the household scale.

**Cranial modification:** Tabular-oblique fronto-occipital flattening — a permanent identity marker initiated in infancy — is observed in Cuevas and Ostiones burial populations (Crespo-Torres 2000), consistent with the marking of social rank or group membership.

## The Egalitarian / Hierarchical / Heterarchical Debate

### Egalitarian model (Siegel 1992, 1996)

Peter Siegel argued that Saladoid communities were egalitarian, with achieved rather than inherited leadership, drawing on his excavations at Maisabel (Puerto Rico) and ethnographic analogy with the Wai Wai of lowland South America. The emergence of social hierarchies with inherited status ranked the post-Saladoid as a new evolutionary stage.

### Hierarchical model (Rouse 1992; Steward and Faron 1959)

Rouse treated Caribbean culture history as an evolutionary progression toward Taíno, with cacicazgo as the apex of indigenous development, closely parallel to Steward's "Circum-Caribbean Chiefdom" type in the comparative ethnological literature.

### Rejection of the egalitarian premise (Keegan and Hofman 2017; Heckenberger 2002)

Keegan and Hofman argue that Saladoid communities already possessed ranked lineages and inherited status when they colonised the Caribbean, inheriting the Arawak cultural predisposition (habitus) for institutional social ranking based on bloodline and birth order. The distinction between "egalitarian" and "autonomous" is crucial: Saladoid villages were autonomous (capable of independent action) without being egalitarian (equal in social standing). Conflating these two concepts has distorted the interpretation of Caribbean social evolution. On this view, the post-Saladoid does not represent the emergence of ranking ex nihilo but its increasing materialisation and formalisation as communities grew and exchange relations were restructured.

### Heterarchical critique (Torres 2013; Curet 2003; Pauketat 2007)

Torres (2013), following Pauketat's critique of "chiefdom" as a classifying device that discourages explanation, advocates returning attention to local historical contingency and community-level diversity. Puerto Rican societies, Curet (2003:19–20) concludes, had "unique ideological foundations, political structure, and organizations that developed from their distinct ancestral societies through different and divergent historical processes." The ceremonial centres at Tibes and Caguana, rather than expressing vertical hierarchy, may reflect a more heterarchical mosaic of competing and collaborating community clusters.

## The Role of Plazas and Ball Courts (Batey)

The batey — both the physical court and the ball game played in it — was a central institution of Caribbean political economy. Courts appear beginning in the 8th century AD (Curet and Stringer 2010) and may have been influenced by contacts with the Isthmo-Colombian region (Rodríguez Ramos 2013:166–167), but their use was specific to the islands.

**At the residential scale:** Small courts in individual villages created liminal neutral zones within communities, formalising public space for marriage ceremonies, puberty rituals, death rites, and the passage of strangers. The open-ended linear court functioned partly as a "road" signalling hospitality to travellers. Construction and maintenance of a court was itself a community act that materialised social obligations and capacity.

**At the ceremonial centre scale:** Large multi-court complexes (Tibes, Caguana, Jácanas) served as inter-community exchange venues at multi-year intervals. The Plaza de Estrella at Tibes, with its unique star-shaped layout of triangular stone projections radiating from a circular court, is interpreted by Keegan and Hofman as physically encoding "star formation exchange" — a structure in which a host clan at the centre conducted dyadic ceremonial exchanges with surrounding partner communities (modelled on Rubel and Rosman's [1978] comparative ethnological work from highland New Guinea). The parallel-sided courts at the same sites encoded smaller, bilateral two-partner exchanges.

**The ball game itself:** The rubber-ball game (batey) described by Spanish observers involved two teams in the stone-lined courts and carried deep cosmological significance. The courts' alignment, dimensions, and embellishment with petroglyphs were not incidental.

## Redistribution and Political Economy

Cacicazgo authority rested partly on redistribution: the cacique accumulated agricultural surplus, prestige goods, and labour obligations, then redistributed these at ceremonial occasions. Spanish sources describe caciques presiding over areyto ceremonies (communal dances and feasts) and controlling access to exotic materials. Archaeologically, this system is visible in the increasing scale of exchange networks, the accumulation of Chicoid-style prestige pottery (Boca Chica, Capá, Esperanza), and the labour investment in ceremonial architecture.

Keegan et al. (1998) argue that the primary economic goal of chiefdom-level organisation in the Caribbean was population growth — actively encouraging fertility and attracting followers — rather than extracting surplus from a stable population.

## Tibes as Archaeological Evidence

The **Centro Ceremonial Indígena de Tibes** (10 km north of Ponce on the Río Portugués) is the key archaeological case for cacicazgo emergence. Its sequence:

1. **Saladoid village phase** (earliest occupation): Semicircular mound arrangement around an unmarked central plaza used as a burial ground.
2. **Ceremonial centre phase** (c. AD 700–1200): Residential activities ceased; major spatial reorganisation produced seven parallel-sided courts, the large central quadrangular plaza (constructed over the Saladoid burial ground), and the Plaza de Estrella. Peak construction AD 1000–1200.
3. **Abandonment** (c. AD 1200): The centre ceased to function as a major ceremonial venue; limited subsequent visitation only.

The abandonment of Tibes coincides with the emergence of **Caguana** (~AD 1200–1500), suggesting a political restructuring: the centre of gravity of Puerto Rican ceremonial exchange shifted from the Río Portugués drainage toward the Tanamá valley in the northwest interior, possibly reflecting shifting demographic and political configurations under Hispaniolan influence.

The absence of habitation debris and "elite" food remains at Tibes (deFrance 2013) argues against the site as a chief's permanent residence; instead it functioned as a neutral inter-community ceremonial venue, consistent with heterarchical rather than strictly hierarchical political organisation.

## Relationship to Taíno Identity

The concept of "Taíno" as a pan-Caribbean identity label applied to all late pre-Columbian Greater Antillean peoples is itself contested. Keegan and Hofman (2017) prefer to emphasise the "mosaic" of regional identities, arguing that the post-Saladoid produced diverse cultural trajectories that were partially homogenised by Hispaniolan influence after AD 1200 but never fully unified. The "Taíno" label, derived from a word meaning "good people" or "noble" in Arawak, was applied by Spanish observers without the nuance the archaeological record reveals. See `[[taino]]`.

## Historiography

The cacicazgo concept entered Caribbean archaeology via Steward and Faron (1959) and was formalised by Rouse (1992). Its original formulation was in fact derived partly from early European accounts of Antillean caciques — making Caribbean archaeology unusual in that the comparative concept was partly built on the very societies it is now used to analyse (Redmond and Spencer 1994).

The concept has been critiqued from multiple angles: as an evolutionary stage-label that obscures local diversity (Torres 2013; Curet 2003); as inappropriately hierarchical when the evidence supports heterarchy (Torres 2012); and as teleologically projecting backward from Spanish-contact descriptions onto earlier archaeological periods (Keegan and Hofman 2017). The current consensus favours using the term descriptively with explicit acknowledgement of its limits, while emphasising community-level analysis and local historical contingency.
