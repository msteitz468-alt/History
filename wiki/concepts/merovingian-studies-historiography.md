# Merovingian Studies (Historiography)

**How the Merovingian past has been written, and why it has been so politically charged.** From the
eighteenth century the field has been organized by a single axis — were the Franks Germanic
conquerors who founded a new civilization (the **Germanist** view) or did the Merovingian centuries
belong to a continuous Roman-Christian world (the **Romanist** view)? Paul Fouracre (Ch. 2) and
Agnès Graceffa (Ch. 3) trace how that debate, and the Carolingian denigration that preceded it,
made the Merovingians at once central to and distorted within European historiography.

## Definition and Scope

The field covers the interpretation of a thin but continuous written record — above all
[[gregory-of-tours|Gregory of Tours]] — alongside law, charters, hagiography, and (increasingly)
[[merovingian-archaeology|archaeology]]. Its central problem is that nearly every later account
inherited a bias built into its sources.

- **Francocentric dominance.** Because the unbroken chain of narrative sources begins with Gregory,
  French scholarship had a unique entry point, and its interpretations became "models for Europe as
  a whole." "Such a long-established Francocentric view of early medieval history has had the effect
  of throwing the spotlight onto the Merovingians and making the fact that Gaul turned into Francia
  a salient point in European development."
- **The Carolingian denigration problem.** "Much aided by the Carolingian denigration of their
  predecessors, Merovingian history has often been written in terms of the progressive decline of
  the dynasty… to the point that one wonders how the dynasty possibly could have survived longer
  than any other in Europe." The Carolingians simultaneously promoted Frankish-Trojan origin myths
  (via Pseudo-Fredegar; see [[fredegar]]) and subjected Merovingian kings to *damnatio memoriae* —
  see [[rois-faineants]].

## Major Phases of the Debate

- **The foundational quarrel (early eighteenth century).** Henri de Boulainvilliers (1727, 1732):
  the Franks were Germanic conquerors who enslaved the Gallo-Romans, the nobility descend from
  Franks, the Third Estate from Gallo-Romans. Against him, Jean-Baptiste Dubos (1734): the Franks
  entered Gaul lawfully at Roman invitation, Clovis gained power legally from Rome, and early
  Frankish society had no aristocracy. These positions fixed the **Germanist vs. Romanist** divide.
- **Enlightenment negativism.** Voltaire denied the Merovingians "city, law, literature, or police"
  (1764); Gibbon's *Decline and Fall* (1776) converged with this.
- **Revolution and the race-war narrative.** Sieyès (1789) told the nobility to "go back to the
  Germanic forests from whence they came." Liberal historians (Guizot, Michelet, Augustin Thierry,
  1820s–50s) wrote national history for all; Thierry's *Récits des temps mérovingiens* "reinvented
  the names of the Frankish kings so that they seemed more 'Germanic'" and cast the period as a race
  war; Michelet called the Frankish period the "childhood of the nation."
- **Nationalist and racial science (1870–1950).** After 1871, Mommsen and Fustel de Coulanges
  clashed over Alsace-Moselle; Gobineau (1853) recast race as biological hierarchy; Gustav Kossinna
  mapped "Germanic" territory by material culture. German *Siedlungsgeschichte* and place-name
  research were ultimately put to National Socialist use.
- **Post-war denationalization and "Late Antiquity."** The trauma of the Holocaust pushed the field
  from nation/tribe toward cultural-exchange models. The invention of **Late Antiquity** as a
  period (Peter Brown 1971; Marrou 1975) and the ESF "Transformation of the Roman World" project
  (1990s) institutionalized continuity; Walter Pohl's verdict: "a single Germanic people probably
  never existed."

## The Ethnogenesis Turn and the Fiscal School

Two post-war research programs dominate. The **ethnogenesis** model (Reinhard Wenskus 1961) holds
that peoples formed culturally around a *Traditionskern* preserved by elites — "the principal source
of cohesion in early medieval peoples was thus not biological but cultural" — developed by Herwig
Wolfram (1979) and Walter Pohl (1998); see [[ethnogenesis]]. The French **fiscal school** (Goffart
1980; Durliat 1990; Werner 1998) argued the Merovingians preserved late-Roman direct taxation and
that the "invasions" were an appropriation of fiscal property, not a destruction of Roman structure;
Chris Wickham (1993) and others judged it "too systematic."

## Recent Revisionism

- **Fouracre's inversion.** The Merovingians were remarkable for **stability, not weakness**: "The
  survival of Francia is the strongest testimony to the stability of its political and religious
  culture, and it was this stability that was the bedrock of Carolingian power." Where else could
  one find repeated rule "by children and queen regents" without the polity collapsing?
- **The Pirenne thesis, superseded.** Henri Pirenne (1937): "Without Mahomet… there could have been
  no Charlemagne" — the Roman world ended when Arab power cut Mediterranean trade. Now rejected:
  exchange had declined *before* the Arab rise, and the key driver was the collapse of taxation
  (Wickham 2005), within a multicausal transition (trade recession, warfare, fifth-century climatic
  cooling, sixth-century plague).
- **Thematic expansion after 1980.** Poverty and power, childhood (see [[merovingian-childhood]]),
  gender (see [[gender-in-merovingian-gaul]]), the history of emotions (Rosenwein), and the
  plurality of religious forms opened the field; Martin Heinzelmann (1994) showed Gregory's
  eschatology structured his whole *History*.

## Historiography

This page is itself a historiographical map. Its key lesson, shared with the editors' introduction,
is that the "Dark Age" label is an artefact of source paucity and Carolingian propaganda rather than
a description of the period. The decisive modern correctives are the recognition of Gregory's
constructed agenda, the dismantling of racial archaeology (see [[merovingian-archaeology]]), and the
reframing of the era within [[late-antiquity|Late Antiquity]].
