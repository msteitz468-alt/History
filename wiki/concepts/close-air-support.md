# Close Air Support

## Definition

Close air support (CAS) in its modern usage refers to "air action against hostile targets which are in close proximity to friendly forces and which require detailed integration of each air mission with the fire and movement of these forces" in order to reduce the danger from friendly fire. It is the air mission tied most tightly to the ground scheme of maneuver, and is therefore conceptually distinct from independent [[strategic-bombardment|strategic bombing]] and from the contest for [[command-of-the-air|command of the air]]. Richard Muller frames the interwar story of CAS as one of "successful military innovation" only in the qualified German case: "the ability of the major air powers to carry out the task of close air support remained minimal until the final two years of World War II, with the qualified exception of Germany."

A symptom of the mission's low status was that "the air forces of the interwar era even experienced difficulty creating an accepted vocabulary for this form of air attack." In the US Army Air Corps it was one component of "attack aviation"; the British spoke of "trench strafing," "ground strafing," and "close support"; the Germans of the "battle aviation mission" (*Schlachtfliegereinsatz*) and later "direct army support." German doctrine did not even "recognize CAS as a distinct mission," dividing army support instead into "direct" and "indirect."

## The Central Tension: Independence vs. the Army-Auxiliary Role

The decisive variable was institutional, not technical. Each air arm faced "real threats to their institutional survival during the interwar period," and CAS "seemingly promised to return air power to its World War I status as an auxiliary to the ground forces." Air leaders therefore favored missions — "strategic bombing," attaining "command of the air," deep interdiction, or *operativer Luftkrieg* — that "not only maximized the theoretical contributions of air power to a future war, but furthered institutional imperatives as well. Close air support, so it seemed, did neither." In the final analysis "close support aviation suffered because of a conscious neglect on the part of the respective air force leaderships." This is the interpretive crux: who innovated in CAS was shaped less by funding or aircraft than by whether the mission threatened or served the air force's bid for independence.

## Germany: Why the Luftwaffe Built Effective CAS

The Imperial German air service (*Luftstreitkräfte*) had already "developed army support aviation into a viable military instrument," driven by "the German emphasis on mastering the operational level of warfare, coupled with realistic training and a productive tradition of inter-arm cooperation." By the March 1918 "Michael" offensive, thirty-eight *Schlachtstaffeln* attacked en masse "in conjunction with assault troops thoroughly grounded in the principles of air-ground cooperation" — innovation that "bubbled up from below" and was then "selecting out tactical innovators and giving them authority to apply their ideas further afield."

Crucially, the path from 1918 to 1939 was not smooth: "it would be wrong to assume an uninterrupted evolutionary path from the Schlachtflieger of 1918 to the Stukas of 1939–1941." German postwar analysis "far surpassed those of the Allied powers," and because "close air support was first and foremost an operational matter, it was the kind of problem for which they were able to devise effective solutions." The Spanish Civil War served as a laboratory: the Condor Legion under Wolfram von Richthofen made "modifications in principles of employment and training" that "corrected many of the deficiencies," yielding "practical and valuable experience with air liaison, signals, dive bombing." Yet "little took place in Spain that had not already been delineated in German manuals dating back to 1918."

Notably, the Junkers 87 Stuka "was a far from ideal close support weapon: although its bombing accuracy was excellent, it was slow and vulnerable to ground fire," and in August 1939 the Stuka units appeared "as part of the strategic bombardment force." German CAS effectiveness rested instead on integration into [[armored-warfare|combined-arms]] mobile war — air liaison officers (*Flivos*), a "versatile and flexible force structure," and close army-Luftwaffe cooperation — which "brought dramatic results" and "was just sufficient to give the Germans a decisive edge in the campaigns of 1939–1941."

## Britain and the United States: Institutional Resistance

For the RAF, "the raison d'être ... through much of the interwar period was not to prepare for a future war ... It was, rather, to insure its own survival as a separate service." Missions like CAS, "which as a rule did little to promote the need for an independent RAF ... would naturally go begging." The official history, *The War in the Air*, distilled the CAS lesson as: "Except in certain and rare circumstances, the results were disappointing when compared with the losses sustained" — and "this brief statement was essentially what passed for 'close air support doctrine' at the highest levels of the air staff until well into World War II." A senior RAF officer later admitted that "the RAF forgot how to support the army," and General Wavell's 1939 verdict found "the RAF had given little or no thought to the problem of close support of ground operations." The one bright spot, "air control" of the Empire, was treated as irrelevant to a "civilized" war. The price came in France and Flanders in 1940, where the Advanced Air Striking Force "exhibited an inability to cooperate effectively with the ground forces."

In the United States, the early lead under Mitchell — whose 1918 "Provisional Manual" echoed the German *Schlachtflieger* directives — "atrophied" as "strategic bombardment" and the heavy bomber took precedence. Colonel Horace Hickam's 1934 article "Why Attack Aviation?" shows "thinking about attack aviation had retreated rather far from the original battlefield support concept." The sole dedicated unit, the Third Attack Group, was "undoubtedly a stepchild of a service that was itself a stepchild," and by 1941 attack aviation "as originally conceived ... was moribund," producing "a near disaster on the battlefield, retrieved only by the common sense of tactical commanders on the spot." (The US Marine Corps, with its "uncluttered intellectual and doctrinal focus," was the American exception.)

## Why This Is an Instructive Military Innovation Case

CAS demonstrates that doctrine and inter-service relations, not technology, decide [[military-innovation|innovation]]. "Successful close air support in the first instance depended upon organization, doctrine, and training, rather than upon the existence of specialized weapons systems." The Luftwaffe "dominated the field of close support for the first three years of the war" with aircraft "approaching obsolescence and ... not designed for close air support in the first place," while the Air Corps "received ever more efficient attack types in the 1930s, but its ability to perform the mission hardly increased at all." Purpose-built ground-attack aircraft were generally "bitter disappointments." Innovation came from "small, virtually self-contained flying units" — the USMC, the Condor Legion, the RAF's air-control detachments — and proceeded by "trickle-up" from junior commanders, whereas "'top-down' initiatives, such as McNair's attempts to develop more effective close support procedures in 1942, frequently met with failure."

## Limitations and Critiques

The concept is contested. Effectiveness was "notoriously difficult to assess (much of the evidence being anecdotal in nature)," with much rest on "moral" rather than material effect; "recent studies have indicated that the effect on morale greatly exceeded the material damage inflicted." Sources are partisan: official histories were sometimes "a cleverly constructed plea for an independent air force," and US air- and ground-force factions read the same manuals (FM 100–20) as either a charter for success or the air force's "Declaration of Independence." The German "laboratory" cases (Spain, the Palestine campaign of 1918) "took place under exceptional conditions" — established air dominance, demoralized opponents, favorable terrain — of limited relevance to war "against a continental opponent." The unresolved command question — centralized air-force control versus an organic "army air force," fought over "well past World War II" — remains the live dispute the concept inherits.
