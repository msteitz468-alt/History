# Partial Offensive

**Partial offensive** (French: *offensive partielle*; also "local offensives," Joffre's public term "nibbling" or *grignoter*) was the French High Command policy, instituted by Joffre in September–October 1914 and continued through early 1915, of conducting limited, localized attacks along the front rather than a general offensive. The explicit purposes were to maintain the "spirit of the offensive" among troops, prevent the enemy from disengaging or shifting forces laterally, demonstrate continued allied pressure (especially to Russia), and seize key local terrain while awaiting sufficient "weapons of siege warfare" (heavy artillery and ammunition) for larger efforts.

## Definition and Scope

Partial offensives were deliberately smaller in scale and objective than prewar decisive-battle doctrine or the later 1915 "rupture" attempts. They typically involved one or a few corps/divisions on narrow fronts, often without full artillery preparation or heavy guns, aimed at "fixing" the enemy or gaining minor advantages (e.g., a hill dominating a railway). They were distinguished from "general" or "principal" attacks (Artois/Champagne main efforts).

Joffre publicly framed them as "nibbling" at the enemy. Orders emphasized "aggressive attitude" and "vigor" and "will" to fight even after recognizing the end of mobile war.

## Origin and Drivers

- **September 17, 1914** (race to the sea phase): Joffre ordered army commanders on the already-static front to "maintain an aggressive attitude" to "prevent the enemy from disengaging and making lateral moves from one part of the front to another."
- Mid-October 1914 (after mobile phase ended): "demonstrate the 'vigor' and 'will' of the French to fight" and "maintain the spirit of the offensive among our troops and not let them lapse into inaction."
- Strategic context: multifront grand strategy required keeping Germans occupied in the west to aid Russia (Grand Duke Nicholas complaints about insufficient Western pressure; German shifts east confirmed by intel). Joffre reassured Russia that French halt was due to "continuous barrier of entrenchments"; as soon as siege weapons arrived, offensive would resume.
- Practical: shortage of 75-mm rounds forced halt after Marne (Joffre telegram to minister of war); only "partial" success possible "until the Russians could 'finish the business.'"

`caused_by:` [[events/first-world-war-1914|end of mobile warfare after First Ypres]], ammunition crisis, Russian alliance pressure; `enabled:` retention of initiative at high human cost while rebuilding forces.

## Examples from 1914–1915 (Doughty Ch. 3)

- **November–early December 1914**: Numerous local actions. Third Army (Sarrail) attacks heights west of Verdun/Aubréville to protect double-track railway (nearly 10,000 casualties in Nov alone). Second Army (Castelnau) attacks by XI, IV, XVI corps north/south of Somme: one gained 300–400 m, others 300–1,000 m or failed outright; "fixed" enemy but "attacking infantry had paid a high price for only small gains."
- **December 1914 Artois (Tenth Army)**: Part of larger coordinated effort but effectively partial in execution due to weather and means. XXI and X corps supporting; XXXIII (Pétain) focused on Carency after main failed. Gains minimal; suspended Jan 13 after shifting units elsewhere.
- **Supporting attacks Dec 1914–Mar 1915**: Many "to fix the enemy, prevent him from shifting forces laterally, and confuse him about the location of the main attack." Often launched with little or no artillery prep (e.g., XI Corps Dec 27 "without artillery preparation").
- **St-Mihiel region (First/Third Army)**: Repeated attempts to reduce salient and secure heights/rail access; small gains, heavy cost (16,000 in one period May–June 1915 secondary).

These produced thousands of casualties with "usually by the seizure of a piece of key terrain" as best outcome. French lacked machine guns, artillery, and wire-cutters in adequate numbers.

## Shift Away (mid-1915 onward)

By late 1914–early 1915, Joffre planned and executed larger "principal" attacks (Artois by Tenth Army toward Cambrai/Vimy; Champagne by Fourth toward Mézières) while still using partials as supporting "general action." Partial offensives never fully ceased (Joffre later ordered local actions during 1915–16 pauses), but the term and policy of relying on them as primary activity gave way to attempts at "breaking through" once more artillery/ammo arrived. Joffre cautioned politicians not to expect "very great results" from small offensives "since 'weapons of siege warfare' were 'not yet ready.'"

In 1915 critiques, partials were sometimes contrasted unfavorably with needed larger, better-prepared actions. Joffre himself later condemned "uninterrupted succession of partial attacks" in favor of artillery concentrations separated by delays for counterbattery.

`preceded_by:` mobile war offensives; `followed_by:` larger Artois/Champagne offensives and attrition strategy; `contributed_to:` [[processes/attrition-warfare-wwi]] and recognition of need for heavy artillery.

## Historiography and Assessment

Doughty presents partial offensives as a necessary interim measure born of material reality and alliance politics, not senselessness. They kept French forces "active" and tied down Germans (French view), but at terrible cost (268,000 dead Oct 1914–Mar 1915 period) while "the French were less prepared for the grinding siege warfare ... than the war of movement that had just ended." German responses (counterattacks, shifts east) showed limits. Contemporary Russian complaints and later French political attacks (Poincaré etc.) highlighted the human price without strategic payoff. Strong collection coverage via Doughty on French decision-making; notes Joffre's tight central control even over local actions (detailed trench studies, specific instructions to army commanders).
