# The Assyrian King List and the Eponym (Limmu) Canon

The twin chronological instruments by which the **Assyrians recorded time** — a
king-list and an annual **eponym (limmu) canon** — and the backbone of
[[mesopotamian-chronology|Mesopotamian chronology]]. CAH singles them out as
evidence that the Assyrians "did themselves much of the work" the modern historian
must otherwise do, in pointed contrast to the schematizing
[[sumerian-king-list|Sumerian King List]]. Treated in
[[edwards-cah-v1p2-1971|CAH Vol. I, Part 2]] (Ch. XXV, §III).

## The eponym (limmu) system

Each year was named after an annual official, the **limmu** ("cycle"), appointed at
[[ashur|Ashur]] — in early periods a hereditary prince of a leading city, later a
rotating provincial governor. Because the king customarily served as limmu early in
his reign, the **eponym canon could be cross-coordinated with the king-list**,
yielding an unbroken, *sequential* count of years. The names were preserved both on
a **row of inscribed stelae** at Ashur (an ordinary citizen could literally walk the
row and count) and on tablets noting each year's chief event. The ~84 eponyms
attested in the [[kultepe|Kültepe]] trade tablets let the
[[old-assyrian-trade|Old Assyrian commerce]] be reconstructed almost year by year.

## The king-list and the "tent-dwellers"

The standard king-list survives in several Neo-Assyrian copies (the best, from
Khorsabad, written 738 BCE). Its **first section — "17 kings who lived in tents"** —
is not a literal royal sequence but a list of the **nomadic tribal ancestors** of
the Assyrian people (several names, e.g. **Didanu/Dedan** and **Hanu/Hana**, are
known as tribal ancestors from the Bible and the Mari texts) — closely parallel to
the twelve sons of Jacob as ancestors of Israel's tribes. **Ushpia**, placed at the
end of this section as first builder of the Ashur temple, marks the **end of the
nomadic period** and the founding of the city. The later sections, copied from royal
inscriptions, give the historical dynasties.

## Why it matters: contrast with the Sumerian King List

The two great Mesopotamian king-lists embody **opposite historiographical methods**:

- The [[sumerian-king-list|Sumerian King List]] *uncritically stacks* the dynasties
  of different city-states **end-to-end as if successive**, even when they
  **overlapped or ruled simultaneously**, and inflates reigns to mythic lengths —
  a theological scheme of single, divinely-rotated "kingship," not a chronology.
- The Assyrian apparatus, by tying a **sequential annual eponym count** to the
  king-list, produced a **genuinely usable relative chronology** — the reason
  Assyrian dates are far more securely reconstructible than Babylonian ones.

This contrast reflects a real cultural trait: a distinctively **Assyrian interest in
history and chronology**, itself rooted in a belief in the *periodic recurrence* of
events (the 350-year "*šūru*-period" used by [[shamshi-adad-i|Shamshi-Adad I]] to
time temple rebuildings).

## Limitations and critiques

- The recurrence-belief that motivated the record-keeping also **distorted** it:
  reigns and intervals could be adjusted to fit 350-year cycles and astral schemes.
- The early sections (tent-dwellers; the gap where eponym tablets were "eaten up")
  are reconstructions, and the **absolute** anchoring of the whole system still
  depends on the unresolved [[mesopotamian-chronology|high/middle/low chronology]]
  debate (tied to the Venus tablets of Ammisaduqa).
- It remains a **royal/official** source, silent on much and shaped by dynastic
  legitimation.
