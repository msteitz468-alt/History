# Roman Demography

The reconstruction of the **population structure** of the early Roman Empire — its mortality,
fertility, migration, and size — from desperately thin evidence. Treated in
[[bowman-cah-v11-2000|CAH XI]] Ch. 27 (**Bruce W. Frier**), who builds a picture from **model life
tables** anchored by the rare hard data, concluding that Rome "broadly resembles most populations
before the modern demographic transition," at the harsh end of the pre-modern range.

## A high-mortality regime

Frier's foundational figure: **life expectancy at birth of about 25 years** (best modelled by
**Coale–Demeny Model West, level 3**), with a plausible range of 20–30. This rests less on ancient
data than on the conviction that Roman conditions resembled the lowest pre-modern levels (early
twentieth-century India and rural China). The evidence that exists supports it:

- **"Ulpian's life table"** (a third-century annuity schedule) implies adult life expectancy
  consistent with the model.
- The **Egyptian census returns** (~300 documents, 1,100+ persons, first–third centuries) — "by far
  the best surviving demographic source" — give an age distribution implying life expectancy in the
  low twenties.
- **North African epitaphs** (which almost always record age at death) yield credible mortality
  rates, though distorted by **age-rounding and age-exaggeration** (Africans living "nineteen
  psychological years for every ten calendar years," taking pride in centenarians); **European
  epitaphs** are "incurably biased" and unusable.

Death rates ran **40–44 per thousand**, with **over half of all deaths children under ten** and
only one newborn in eight surviving to age 60. Death was **seasonal** (worst in late summer), and
the major causes — "fevers" (malaria, typhoid), pulmonary disease (TB, pneumonia), dysentery — were
aggravated by **poor nutrition, bad sanitation, dense urbanism, and a state that could not
quarantine pestilence**. The empire's vast size insulated its *total* population from
catastrophe, so famines and plagues were **regional** — until the **[[antonine-plague-165|Antonine
Plague]]** (from 165, probably smallpox) became the first true **pandemic**, raging a quarter-
century ("the Malthusian checks had begun").

## High fertility and "natural fertility"

Such mortality demanded heavy fertility. Frier shows the literary impression of small, childless
Roman families is **untrustworthy**: to remain stationary, the average woman reaching menopause had
to bear **at least five or six children** (a gross reproduction rate near 3). The key findings,
drawn from the Egyptian census:

- **Early, near-universal female marriage** (women marrying at 15–20, almost all married by their
  late twenties; men later, in their mid-twenties) — Rome "had no spinster class."
- **"Natural fertility"**: marital fertility followed the universal pre-transition shape, declining
  with age, with **no sign of conscious family limitation** — couples did not stop after a target
  number of children. Yet peak rates were **moderate** (a birth roughly one year in three),
  sustainable because the burden was spread across all fertile women.
- **Fertility control was indirect and non-marital**: contraception and abortion (known but
  associated with non-marital fertility), prolonged **breastfeeding and post-partum abstinence**
  (the real brake within marriage, spacing births to protect mothers), and **infant exposure**
  (not used to limit family size, and not immoral or illegal before the late empire). The
  randomizing effect of infant mortality meant huge variance — nine-tenths of couples declaring
  three or fewer surviving children.

## A youngish, slowly growing population

High mortality produced a **young population** (mean age ~27; ~35% under 15, only ~6% over 60),
matching the Egyptian returns. Differential mortality is hard to detect: **senators** were slightly
advantaged (the Senate maintained by ~20 quaestors a year implies male life expectancy near 30),
yet their **urban residence** exposed them to city disease; **slaves** probably fared worse than
free, though perhaps better than subsistence peasants. The empire's long-term **intrinsic growth
rate** was low — the Egyptian data fit ~**0.15% a year** (doubling every ~350 years) — leaving the
population "delicately poised" between under- and overpopulation in the classic **Malthusian**
manner, very possibly already (like *ancien régime* France) near its ceiling.

## Significance and contest

Frier's demography is the **deep structure beneath** the economic, social, and political life of
the empire: a regime of high mortality and high fertility, early marriage, and slow growth, whose
 subjects lived short lives in a perennially young society. The framework is built on **model life
tables and the unique Egyptian census** — and therefore remains **contested** (the exact life
expectancy, the reality of family limitation, sex differentials, the gross population of the empire
— commonly estimated around 50–60 million — all turn on slender and skewed evidence).
