# Holmberg's Mistake (the Myth of the Timeless Indian)

**"Holmberg's Mistake"** is Charles C. Mann's name ([[mann-1491-2005|*1491*]], 2005) for the long-standing
Western assumption that **Native Americans lived in an eternal, unchanging, ahistorical state** until
1492 — passive inhabitants of a "pristine wilderness" who lacked, in social-science terms, **agency**.
It is the historiographical error the revisionist scholarship of the late twentieth century overturned,
and the organising target of Mann's whole book. This page records the myth and its undoing; the
substantive new picture is at [[pre-columbian-americas]].

## The Sirionó and the Beni

The eponym is the anthropologist **Allan R. Holmberg**, who lived among the **Sirionó** of the Bolivian
**Beni** in 1940–42 and, in *Nomads of the Longbow* (1950), described them as "among the most culturally
backward peoples of the world" — people with almost no material culture, who could not count past three
or make fire, "the quintessence of man in the raw state of nature," seemingly unchanged for millennia.
Holmberg was a careful, compassionate observer, but he was **wrong**: the Sirionó were not Stone-Age
holdovers but the **shattered survivors of a recent catastrophe** — smallpox and influenza had killed
over **95% of them in the 1920s** (driving the population through a genetic bottleneck), and the
remnant were then hunted and enslaved by cattle ranchers. Mann's analogy: Holmberg "had come across
refugees from a Nazi concentration camp, and concluded that they belonged to a culture that had always
been barefoot and starving." Worse, Holmberg did not see that the Sirionó were **walking through a
landscape shaped by someone else** — the Beni's tens of thousands of square miles of mounds, causeways,
canals, raised fields, and fire-managed savanna, built (per Clark Erickson and William Denevan) by a
populous Arawak society a thousand years before (see [[pristine-myth]]).

## A myth held by friend and foe alike

The Mistake "held sway for almost five centuries" and, Mann stresses, was embraced **both by those who
despised Indians and those who admired them**:
- The **Noble Savage** pole — from Las Casas's "gentle as cows" innocents in a "terrestrial paradise"
  (1530s) to Thoreau's eco-sage to the weeping "Crying Indian" of the 1971 anti-litter campaign — cast
  Indians as pure beings who *never altered* their environment.
- The **feeble-barbarian** pole — George Bancroft's "unproductive waste…feeble barbarians" (1834),
  Kroeber's "insane, unending" warfare, Morison's "pagans expecting short and brutish lives," and the
  1987 textbook that called the pre-Columbian hemisphere a land "empty of mankind and its works" — cast
  them as lazy, static, and historyless.

Both poles denied Indians **agency** and **history** ("because history is change, they were people
without history"). Mann links the error to its era: a time of ascendant nationalism and apparent
white global dominance, when historians identified history with (European) nations.

## The overturning

A convergence of **new disciplines and techniques** — demography, epidemiology, palynology,
radiocarbon and ice-core dating, satellite imagery, soil science, genetics — made the timeless-Indian
picture untenable from the 1960s onward, just as decolonisation and the lesson of Japan's rapid change
taught the West that non-Western societies were dynamic. The result was the three revisions synthesised
in [[pre-columbian-americas]]: **more** people, **earlier** settlement, and far more
**environmental engineering** than supposed.

## Critique and limits

Mann is careful to register the **counter-attack**: an older generation of scholars (Betty Meggers of
the Smithsonian on the Beni; the geographer Thomas Vale) charges that the revisionists have "simply
replaced the old myth with a new one — the myth of the humanized landscape," reading too much into
"meager evidence" to fit a politically congenial story (inflating native numbers magnifies the scale of
the European-caused loss). The revisionist case is now mainstream but its **magnitudes remain genuinely
contested** — see [[pre-columbian-american-population]] and [[clovis-first-vs-pre-clovis]]. The value of
"Holmberg's Mistake" as a concept is that it names a **default frame**, not merely a factual error — one
still visible in textbooks, film, and environmental rhetoric.
