# Leidang (Naval Levy)

The Scandinavian naval levy — ON *leiðangr*, Danish *leding*, Swedish *ledung* — the
obligation on the coastal population to provide ships, crews, provisions, and weapons
for royal defence. Per [[helle-cambridge-history-scandinavia-v1-2003|the Cambridge
History]] it was a central institution of [[unification-of-the-scandinavian-kingdoms|state
formation]], military organisation, and ultimately royal **taxation**.

## Structure and Transformation

In Denmark the country was divided into *skipæn* (each supplying one ship) and
*havner* (each supplying one man and his provisions); the system worked best under
the Valdemars in the late 12th and early 13th centuries. Saga tradition credits
[[norway|Norway]]'s [[harald-fairhair|Hákon the Good]] with organising the levy.
Across the 12th–13th centuries the levy was progressively **commuted into permanent
land taxation** — a process completed in all three kingdoms in the second half of the
13th century, and a principal foundation of Scandinavian royal finance.

## Social Consequences

The commutation underpinned the emergence of a **privileged noble class** (exempt
from the new land tax in return for mounted military service) toward the end of the
13th century. Crucially, **Norwegian peasants retained the military function of the
naval levy through the 13th century**, which is given as a key reason they kept more
political weight than their Danish and Swedish counterparts, who were reduced to a
fiscal base earlier. See [[thing-assembly]], [[medieval-scandinavia]].

## Historiography

Origins and early character are debated (the saga attribution to Hákon the Good is
not contemporary). The institution links military, fiscal, and constitutional
history, making it central to interpreting the divergent social structures of the
three kingdoms.
