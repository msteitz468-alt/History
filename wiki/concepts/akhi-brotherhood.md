# Akhi Brotherhood

## Definition and Scope
Organized associations of youths (“the Youths”) in the Turkomān towns of Anatolia during the 14th century. Each cell is presided over by a “Brother” (Akhi) who maintains a dedicated cell/hospice equipped with a horse, saddle, and gear. Membership is typically drawn from a single occupation or trade (e.g., silk merchants) but also welcomes destitute foreign youths.

## Causal Drivers / Structural Features
Daily contributions from members fund communal meals, fruit, and entertainment. The president uses these resources to host and provision travelers and strangers with exceptional courtesy and liberality. Cells function as both mutual-aid societies and hospices that oppose oppressors. No traveler is permitted to leave until he has been properly entertained.

## Geographic Spread and Interaction
Present “in all the Turkomān towns.” Documented examples include a 200-member silk-merchant cell and groups in Burdūr and other centers. The institution facilitates long-distance travel and mobility across Anatolia at a time when small beghs and fragmented polities made safe passage valuable. Parallels broader Indian Ocean hospitality customs that supported merchant and scholarly movement.

## Historiography / Collection Note
Primary eyewitness description comes from Ibn Battuta’s Anatolian leg (Lee 1829 translation of the *Rihla*). The account distinguishes the Akhis from other groups and emphasizes their practical hospitality rather than purely mystical or military character. Cross-references to earlier futuwwa traditions exist in other sources; this is the clearest 14th-c. snapshot in the collection.
