## Definition and Scope

Wilāya (also transliterated *walāya*) is the concept of Sufi sainthood — the spiritual state, the institution, and the hierarchical structure through which God's grace is mediated to the Muslim community. The word *walī* (pl. *auliyāʾ*), usually translated "saint," literally means "someone who is under special protection, friend." Qushayrī noted it is grammatically both active and passive: a walī is both one whose affairs are led (*tuwulliya*) by God, and one who performs (*tawallā*) worship and obedience.

The *auliyāʾ Allāh*, "friends of God," are explicitly mentioned in the Koran: "Verily, the friends of God, no fear is upon them nor are they sad" (Sūra 10:63). This Koranic grounding made the concept non-negotiable for mainstream Sufism even as its elaboration became increasingly controversial.

Hujwīrī (11th c.) states the centrality plainly: "You must know that the principle and foundation of Sufism and knowledge of God rest on saintship" (H 210). Jāmī similarly opened his history of Sufism with a treatment of sainthood.

## Two Types of Sainthood

Al-Ḥakīm at-Tirmidhī distinguished two foundational types:

1. **Wilāyat ʿāmma** — "general sainthood" common to all sincere faithful: those recognizable by "loveliness of their speech, and fine manners, and submission, and generosity, showing little opposition, and accepting the excuse of everyone" (N 121).
2. **Wilāya khāṣṣa** — "special sainthood" of advanced mystics "who have become annihilated in God and remain through Him, and the walī is he who has been annihilated in Him and lives in Him" (N 5).

Tirmidhī further distinguished between the **walī ṣidq Allāh** (reaching sainthood through faithful adherence to sharīʿa and ṭarīqa) and the **walī minnat Allāh** (reaching sainthood by divine grace through loving surrender). Both paths require divine grace for the final illumination.

## The Saintly Hierarchy

From at least the time of Tirmidhī, a detailed cosmic hierarchy of saints evolved. In its fullest form as described by Schimmel:

- **Quṭb** ("axis, pole") or **ghauth** ("help"): the supreme spiritual authority; the virtual center of spiritual energy upon whom the well-being of the world depends
- **Three nuqabāʾ** ("substitutes")
- **Four autād** ("pillars")
- **Seven abrār** ("pious"): spiritual guardians of sacred places; some towns claim to be protected by the Seven
- **Forty abdāl** ("substitutes"): the group that gained the most popular prominence
- **Three hundred akhyār** ("good")
- **Four thousand hidden saints**

Ibn ʿArabī claimed seven abdāl, one for each of the seven climates. Rūzbihān Baqlī developed a variant system of 360 saints (the cosmic pleroma): 300 with hearts like Adam's, 40 like Moses's, 7 like Abraham's, 5 like Gabriel's, 3 like Michael's, and one quṭb like Isrāfīl — plus four prophets taken to heaven alive (Idrīs, Khiḍr, Ilyas, Jesus).

The **Forty abdāl** held special cultural prominence across the Muslim world. Place names (e.g., Kĭrklareli — "the county of the Forty" — in European Turkey) attest to this. The abdāl were understood to "substitute" for each other at death, maintaining the cosmic number.

## The Quṭb

The quṭb is the conceptual center of the hierarchy. He "rests in perfect tranquillity, grounded in God — that is why all the 'minor stars' revolve around him." Henry Corbin analyzed the Sufi orientation toward the Pole as a place where "the celestial revelation, 'the sun at midnight,' will appear to the pilgrim on the mystical Path."

The quṭb's cosmic governance is tangible: "Every night the autād must go round the whole universe, and if there should be any place on which their eyes have not fallen, next day some imperfection will appear in that place, and they must then inform the Quṭb, in order that he may fix his attention on the weak spot and that by his blessing the imperfection may be removed." (H 228)

Schimmel notes "a close structural relationship between the concept of the quṭb as the highest spiritual guide of the faithful and that of the hidden imām of Shia Islam." Rūmī, despite being relatively moderate, said: "He who does not know the true sheikh — i.e., the Perfect Man and quṭb of his time — is a kāfir, an infidel" (M 2:3325).

Later elaborations: Aḥmad Sirhindī (d. 1624), the great Naqshbandī reformer, claimed for himself and three of his descendants the rank of **qayyūm**, superior even to the quṭb.

## The Khatm al-Wilāya (Seal of Sainthood)

Tirmidhī introduced the concept of the **khatm al-wilāya** — the "seal of sainthood" — which became a major subject of Sufi debate. Just as the prophets have their seal and most perfect manifestation in Muhammad, the saints have a final and most perfect saint. The problem of whether this "seal" had already appeared or was yet to come remained open.

The **relationship between prophet and saint** was a corollary controversy: Which rank is higher? Traditional Sufi schools agreed on the superiority of the prophet. "The end of saintship is only the beginning of prophethood. Every prophet possesses the aspect of saintship in himself." But "in the last stage of saintship the spirit of the saint becomes similar to the body of the Prophet" (N 443, citing Simnānī). Intoxicated mystics sometimes claimed the saint's rapture surpassed the prophet's sobriety.

## Miracles (Karāmāt)

The capacity to work miracles (*karāmāt*, "charismata") was a key marker of genuine sainthood — but also a contested one. The saint's miracles are carefully distinguished from the prophet's (*muʿjizāt*, "what renders others incapable to do the same"). Types of saintly miracles documented by Schimmel:

- **Firāsa** (cardiognosia/soul-reading): knowing disciples' inner states, seeing destinies
- **Ṭayy al-makān**: being present in multiple places simultaneously; appearing at a disciple's bedside
- **Vicarious suffering**: bearing a disciple's illness or injury in one's own body
- Nature miracles: taming lions, causing rain, healing the sick
- Conversion miracles: convincing materialists by walking through fire

The greatest Sufi masters were skeptical of miracle-mongering. Abū Saʿīd ibn Abīʾl-Khayr dismissed walking on water and flying through the air: "Frogs and waterfowl do that as well!" (NS 67). A ḥadīth popular in Indian and eastern Sufi circles declared: "Miracles are the menstruation of men" — meaning they come between man and God, polluting the purely theocentric orientation. "It is better to restore one dead heart to eternal life than to restore to life a thousand dead bodies."

## Popular Veneration and Its Dangers

Wilāya moved from elite mystical theory to mass popular practice through the cult of saints' tombs. Sanctuaries (often built over pre-Islamic sacred places) became sites for seeking the saint's **baraka** (blessing power): circumambulation, vow-taking, hanging cloth, prayers for fertility or healing. The **ʿurs** ("wedding") — anniversary of the saint's death — became the major festival of veneration, understood as the saint's "wedding" with the divine.

Schimmel notes the darker side: theories like *ḥasanāt al-abrār sayyiʾāt al-muqarrabīn* ("the good deeds of the pious are the bad deeds of those who are brought near") could license antinomian behavior. Sheikhs claiming to be beyond the law, fabricating amulets, and exploiting followers' credulity were persistent problems. "Many of those who preached poverty as their pride became wealthy landlords" through waqf endowments and tribute from poor followers.

Modern Muslim reformers (Atatürk in 1925 abolishing dervish orders in Turkey; Iqbal decrying "Pirism") saw the wilāya institution in its degenerate form as a principal obstacle to Islamic revival.

## Historiography

Wilāya scholarship has been shaped by tension between sympathetic insider accounts (classical Sufi biographies, *ṭabaqāt* literature) and critical external perspectives (Ibn al-Jauzī's medieval criticism; 19th–20th century reformist denunciation). Émile Dermenghem's *Le culte des saints dans l'Islam Maghrebin* (1954) remains foundational for North Africa. J. Spencer Trimingham's *The Sufi Orders in Islam* (1971) provides institutional context. Corbin's phenomenological approach illuminates the cosmological dimension.

The collection (Bibliotheca Alexandrina) is strong for classical Sufi hagiography and theoretical treatments; weaker for popular saint veneration in sub-Saharan Africa and Southeast Asia.
