# The Han State Economy (the "Balanced Standard")

The system of **state economic intervention** developed under [[han-wudi|Emperor Wu of Han]] to
finance his wars — above all the **state monopolies on salt and iron** and the
price-stabilization schemes that Sima Qian anatomizes in his **"Treatise on the Balanced
Standard"** (*pingzhun*, *Shi ji* 30). One of the earliest and most influential experiments in
state-managed political economy. From [[sima-qian-records-han-ii|Sima Qian's *Records of the
Grand Historian: Han Dynasty II*]].

## The fiscal crisis of Wu's wars

The early [[han-dynasty|Han]] had practised frugality, rebuilding from "the evils left by the
[[qin-dynasty|Qin]]" and filling the treasury. [[han-wudi|Emperor Wu's]] ceaseless campaigns —
against the [[xiongnu|Xiongnu]], into Central Asia, and on every frontier — together with vast
construction and relief for flood and famine, **drained that wealth**. The state turned to
extraordinary measures to raise revenue without (in theory) burdening the peasant cultivator —
whom Confucian and Legalist orthodoxy alike held to be the "basic" occupation, against the
"secondary" and distrusted pursuit of **commerce**.

## The instruments

Sima Qian records the suite of interventions, devised by finance officials such as the
merchant's son **Sang Hongyang** and **Kong Jin**:

- **State monopolies on salt and iron.** The government took over the production and sale of
  these universal necessities, establishing bureaus across the empire and (tellingly)
  appointing former salt-and-iron magnates to run them — so that "many merchants actually got to
  be government officials."
- **The "equable marketing" and "balanced standard" systems.** The state used its
  transport network to **buy goods cheap where abundant and sell where dear**, both stabilizing
  prices and capturing the merchants' speculative profit for the treasury.
- **Currency manipulation** (repeated re-coinages and a "white metal" and deerskin "treasury
  notes"), the **sale of ranks and offices**, and **heavy taxes on merchants' carts, boats, and
  hoarded wealth** (the *suanmin* property levies), with rewards for informers.
- Sumptuary and legal pressure on merchants (barred from silk and carriages, debarred — with
  their descendants — from office), reviving the old policy of "making life difficult for the
  merchants" to keep men at the plough.

## Significance and the historian's view

The "Balanced Standard" made the Han state a **direct economic actor** — financing imperial
expansion through monopoly and market intervention rather than the land tax alone, and setting a
precedent for state economic management invoked across later Chinese history (and debated in the
famous Han *Discourses on Salt and Iron*). Sima Qian, hostile to both [[han-wudi|Wu's]]
militarism and to commercial greed, frames the treatise as a study of how currency and profit
corrode the agrarian order — and, implicitly, of how the costs of empire fall on the people. (His
companion **"Biographies of the Money-makers"** treats the great merchants the monopolies
targeted, a rare ancient appreciation of commercial wealth.)
