# Roman Virtues

Roman virtues were the cluster of ethical and social qualities Roman elite culture prized as constitutive of proper civic and personal character, transmitted through the [[concepts/mos-maiorum|mos maiorum]] ("the way of the ancestors") rather than through abstract philosophical codification. Core virtues included [[concepts/pietas|pietas]] (dutiful respect toward gods, family, and state), [[concepts/roman-virtus|virtus]] (martial courage and manly excellence), *gravitas* (dignified seriousness), *dignitas* (personal standing and honor), *fides* (good faith and reliability), *constantia* (steadfastness), and *frugalitas* (disciplined simplicity, opposed to luxury).

These virtues functioned as a social currency as much as an ethical ideal: reputation for embodying them underwrote a Roman aristocrat's claim to office, military command, and political influence, and their public performance — in oratory, funeral eulogies (*laudationes*), monuments, and historiography — was as important as private conviction. Roman moralists and historians, notably Cato the Elder, Cicero, and later Livy and Sallust, repeatedly framed Rome's political crises and civil wars as symptoms of virtue's decay under the corrupting pressures of wealth, luxury, and factional ambition imported from conquered Greek and eastern territories.

The rhetoric of virtue and its decline became a recurring device in Roman political self-understanding, invoked by figures from the Gracchi to Augustus (whose regime explicitly claimed to restore the traditional virtues) as a way of diagnosing and prescribing remedies for perceived political dysfunction.
