# Seventeenth-Century Kikongo

**Seventeenth-Century Kikongo (SCK)** denotes the variety of the [[kikongo-language-cluster]] documented in three surviving missionary linguistic works produced in the [[kingdom-of-kongo]] between 1624 and 1659. These are the oldest extant book-length sources written in any Bantu language (Doke 1935) and are of exceptional value both for historical linguistics and for the history of the Kongo kingdom.

Bostoen & de Schryver (ch. 3 in Bostoen & Brinkman 2018) establish through Bayesian phylogenetics and diachronic phonological analysis that SCK represents the **variety of South Kikongo spoken at Mbanza Kongo**, the kingdom's inland capital — not the coastal Soyo variety as earlier scholarship assumed. This finding has significant implications for understanding the kingdom's linguistic landscape and the stability of its dialectal divisions.

## The Three Primary Sources

### 1. *Doutrina Christã* (1624) — Mateus Cardoso
An interlinear Portuguese–Kikongo catechism produced under the leadership of Mateus Cardoso, a Portuguese Jesuit. It is the earliest surviving substantial text in any Bantu language. Cardoso worked with Kongo Christians who had access to the court variety of Kikongo. The catechism consistently uses *\<d\>* where Van Gheel and Brusciotto use *\<r\>* or *\<bh\>* — reflecting orthographic conventions rather than significant phonemic differences. The *Doutrina* was republished and translated in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries (Bontinck & Ndembe Nsasi 1978).

### 2. *Vocabularium Latinum, Hispanicum, e Congense* (1652) — Joris Van Gheel
A trilingual Latin–Spanish–Kikongo wordlist surviving thanks to a manuscript copied by the Flemish Capuchin Joris Van Gheel (the Capuchin mission began in 1645). It provides the fullest lexical coverage of the three sources — ninety-two basic-vocabulary items were recoverable for phylogenetic analysis. Van Gheel uses the grapheme *\<bh\>* for the voiced bilabial fricative /β/ and often *\<r\>* where Cardoso uses *\<d\>*. The published reworking by Van Wing & Penders (1928) was so heavily altered as to be unreliable; the KongoKing project produced a full digitization of the original manuscript (Guy Ndouli, TLex software).

### 3. *Regulae quaedam pro difficillimi Congensium idiomatis faciliori captu* (1659) — Hyacintho Brusciotto a Vetralla
A grammar of Kikongo published under the authorship of the Italian Capuchin Hyacintho Brusciotto a Vetralla. It is the earliest surviving grammar of a Bantu language. Brusciotto describes noun classes in terms borrowed from European grammatical categories, calling augments and noun prefixes "articles" — a misapprehension that nonetheless preserves important morphological data. The grammar was republished by Guinness (1882). Only thirty-seven basic-vocabulary items were recoverable for phylogenetic analysis.

### Corpus Status
These three sources cluster together as immediate relatives in the Bayesian consensus tree of the KLC (Figure 3.3 in Bostoen & de Schryver 2018), confirming they document the same variety. An additional eighteenth-century corpus — French–Kikongo dictionaries produced by French missionaries near Kinguele (capital of the Kakongo kingdom, present-day Cabinda) in the 1770s, digitized by Sharah Drieghe — documents a distinct West Kikongo variety and provides crucial comparative data.

## Genealogical Position within the KLC

### Placement in the Bayesian Tree
The three seventeenth-century sources cluster within the **central subunit of South Kikongo**, together with:
- Early-nineteenth-century Kisikongo (de Cannecattim 1805)
- Late-nineteenth-century Kisikongo (Bentley 1887, 1895) — documented at Mbanza Kongo / San Salvador
- Mid-twentieth-century South Kikongo (da Silva Maia 1961)
- Present-day Kisikongo (Ndonga Mfuwa 1995; José T. Kumenda, interviewed 2013)

They do **not** cluster with Kisolongo (coastal, west of Congo delta) or Kizombo (eastern). The SCK variety is therefore the **direct ancestor of Kisikongo**, spoken today in the area of the former capital, and only a collateral ancestor ("grandnephew or grandniece" in Bostoen & de Schryver's formulation) of other South Kikongo varieties.

This contradicts Bentley (1887), Van Wing & Penders (1928), and Bontinck (1976), who argued — based on the *\<r\>* vs. *\<d\>* orthographic variation — that the seventeenth-century sources represented the Soyo coastal variety (ancestral to Kisolongo).

## Phonological Analysis: Four Key Features

Bostoen & de Schryver (ch. 3) analyze four phonological features of SCK, demonstrating why earlier classifications were wrong and pinpointing SCK's exact genealogical position.

### 1. Retroflexion of *\*d* before high front vowels (*\*d > r / V__\*ɪ*)
Van Gheel (1652) and Brusciotto (1659) sometimes write *\<r\>* where Cardoso (1624) writes *\<d\>* before *i*. De Cannecattim (1805) interpreted this as a dialect difference — coastal Soyo speakers using *r*, court speakers using *d* — and Bentley and Bontinck accepted this as evidence for a Soyo affiliation of the Van Gheel/Brusciotto documents.

Bostoen & de Schryver show this interpretation is incorrect for two reasons: (a) the *\<r\>* notation is not systematic in either Van Gheel or Brusciotto; both documents testify to both orthographic conventions. (b) The *\<r\>* notation likely reflects the retroflex [ɽ], an archaism still attested in present-day North Kikongo varieties (Kibembe, Kihangala) but lost in all South Kikongo varieties since the nineteenth century. It is therefore irrelevant for internal classification within South Kikongo and provides **no evidence** for a Soyo affiliation.

### 2. Intervocalic lenition of *\*p* (*\*p > β*)
SCK attests the voiced bilabial fricative /β/ as the reflex of Proto-Bantu intervocalic *\*p* (noted *\<bh\>* in Van Gheel and Brusciotto). Earlier scholars took this as evidence of closer affiliation with Kisolongo, which also preserves /β/. Bostoen & de Schryver show this is a **shared retention** (not innovation): the evolution path is *\*p > °ɸ > β > v*, with /v/ as the most innovative reflex within the KLC. Several other subclades, including northern West Kikongo varieties (Yingubi, Yishira, Yisangu, Yipunu), also preserve /β/ or /β/-like sounds. Eastern South Kikongo varieties (Kitsootso, Dihungu, Kipombo) independently underwent *°ɸ > h*, a distinct innovation shared with East Kikongo — indicating that eastern South Kikongo is genealogically closer to East Kikongo than to central South Kikongo. The shared retention of /β/ between SCK, Kisolongo, and Kizombo therefore cannot establish closer kinship.

### 3. Intervocalic loss of *\*b* (*\*b > Ø / V__V[-closed]*)
In the vast majority of words, SCK has lost Proto-Bantu *\*b* between vowels where the second vowel is not a closed vowel of the first aperture degree. This is a **shared innovation** that is regular only within the South Kikongo subclade (and in Kindibu, which genealogically belongs to South Kikongo despite appearing in the Central Kikongo convergence zone). All other KLC subclades regularly maintain *\*b* in this context.

Crucially, the analysis also distinguishes within South Kikongo: eastern South Kikongo varieties Kizombo and Kindamba show *\*b > g* (velarization) rather than simple loss, while Kitsootso, Kipombo, and Dihungu show an intermediate stage with phonologized /w/. This innovation pathway (*\*b > Ø > w > ɰ > ɣ > g*) marks eastern South Kikongo as a discrete subunit and confirms that SCK cannot be ancestral to all South Kikongo varieties — eastern South Kikongo had already diverged by the seventeenth century.

### 4. Phonological augment merger
The Bantu augment (a pre-prefix marking definiteness, specificity, or syntactic function) underwent distinctive evolution within each KLC subclade. Proto-Bantu had augments identical in form to pronominal prefixes (CV-shape); in the KLC these reduced to simple vowels in most subclades, or disappeared entirely (in Kikongoid, East Kikongo, North Kikongo, and Central Kikongo).

SCK, as documented by Brusciotto (1659), has a **two-vowel augment pattern** (*e-* before noun prefixes with front vowels or nasals; *o-* elsewhere), yielding an *e-o-o* pattern. This is derived from an earlier three-vowel *e-a-o* pattern (itself via vowel lowering from the Proto-Kikongo *i-a-u* pattern). West Kikongo maintained the *i-a-u* or *i-u-u* pattern; eighteenth-century Kakongo (West Kikongo area) shows *i-u-u*. The *e-o-o* pattern is thus a **shared innovation** corroborating South Kikongo as a discrete genealogical subclade distinct from West Kikongo.

Within South Kikongo, the augment pattern further distinguishes three subunits:
- **Central South Kikongo** (Kisikongo, Mbanza Kongo): *e-o-o* — same as SCK, confirming direct descent
- **Western South Kikongo** (Kisolongo, Atlantic coast): *e-e-o* — levelling toward front vowel *e-* for classes 2 and 6
- **Eastern South Kikongo** (Kizombo, Kitsootso, Dihungu, Kindamba): *e-e-e* — complete levelling to front vowel *e-*

These three South Kikongo augment patterns were already divergent in the seventeenth century. SCK's *e-o-o* pattern matches only the Kisikongo trajectory. The messy augment systems visible in western and eastern South Kikongo today reflect the lateral influence of the socially prominent central Kisikongo variety obscuring inherited patterns.

## Main Conclusion: SCK is Not the Ancestor of Present-Day Kikongo

Bostoen & de Schryver's (ch. 3) central finding, stated as a summary in Table 3.5 of the chapter: "The Kikongo found in the *Doutrina Christã* (1624), the *Vocabularium Congense* (1652) and the *Regulae quaedam* (1659) is thus not ancestral to the entire KLC and not even ancestral to the entire South Kikongo subclade to which it belongs. Only Kisikongo from the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries as found in Bentley (1887, 1895), da Silva Maia (1961) and Ndonga Mfuwa (1995) can be considered as its direct descendants."

Western and eastern South Kikongo varieties are "at best grandnephews or grandnieces of seventeenth-century Kikongo."

## Historical Implications

**1. The kingdom was linguistically diverse.** The SCK documents record only the variety of the court and capital. The kingdom's northern provinces (Mpangu, Nsundi, northern Mbata) were predominantly East and Central Kikongo-speaking — languages distinct enough from SCK to be considered separate languages by lexical-similarity standards. The assumption of a monolingual Kongo kingdom is historically incorrect.

**2. Linguistic divergence predates the kingdom.** The three South Kikongo subunits (western/Kisolongo, central/Kisikongo, eastern/Kizombo) had already diverged before the seventeenth century. The [[kongo-decentralization]] following [[battle-of-mbwila-1665]] deepened rather than created this variation.

**3. The court language had lateral influence.** Despite not being the ancestor of all KLC varieties, the prestigious Mbanza Kongo court variety exerted strong contact-induced lateral influence on neighboring South Kikongo varieties — visible in the messy augment patterns and phonological irregularities in present-day northern Angolan varieties.

**4. Missionary documentation was court-centered.** From the seventeenth century through the early nineteenth, all major Kikongo linguistic works focused on the court variety at Mbanza Kongo. Only after European colonialism did missionaries begin documenting other KLC varieties (Kisolongo: Craven & Barfield 1883, Visseq 1889; East Kikongo/Kintandu: Butaye 1909, 1910; Central Kikongo/Kimanyanga: Laman 1912, 1936). This documentary bias has distorted understanding of the kingdom's actual linguistic diversity.

## Historiography

Earlier scholarly assessment of the seventeenth-century Kikongo sources focused on identifying the geographic origin of the missionaries who produced them — an approach that confused place of missionary activity with linguistic variety documented. De Cannecattim (1805) proposed that Van Gheel and Brusciotto described the Soyo coastal variety while Cardoso described the court variety; Bentley (1887), Van Wing & Penders (1928), and Bontinck (1976) accepted and elaborated this view. Bostoen & de Schryver (2018) demonstrate it is wrong: all three sources document the same variety (they cluster together in the phylogenetic tree) and that variety is the court/capital South Kikongo of Mbanza Kongo, not Soyo.

The major linguistic works of the twentieth century on these sources (Doke 1935; Van Bulck 1954; Mukuna 1984; Nsondé 1995; Bonvini 1996) provided valuable contextual study but rarely applied systematic historical-comparative phonology. The dissertations of De Kind (2012) and Bleyenberg (2012) — part of the KongoKing project — were the first to do so rigorously.

The key methodological contribution is the integration of **diachronic phonology** with **Bayesian lexical phylogenetics**: neither alone is sufficient. Phonological evidence can identify shared retentions vs. shared innovations; lexical phylogenetics provides the overall tree topology. Together, they allow precise placement of historical varieties that no longer have living speakers.
