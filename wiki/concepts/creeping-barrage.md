# Creeping (or Rolling) Barrage

Artillery fire plan in which the barrage lifts and advances in front of the assaulting infantry on a precise timetable, providing continuous suppression of the enemy’s forward positions and wire while the attackers move just behind the shell line.

## Western Front Context (Griffith emphasis)
- Early war and 1916 Somme bombardments were largely static and prolonged; often failed to cut wire or suppress deep dug-outs.
- The creeping barrage became central to the 1917–18 BEF method: short, intense, accurately predicted or registered preparation followed by a moving curtain of fire.
- Required close infantry–artillery coordination, reliable watches, and disciplined movement by the assault troops (“lean on the barrage”).
- Griffith notes its superiority, in British practice, to over-reliance on the hand grenade for the final assault phase.

## Relation to Other Developments
Paired with neutralising (rather than purely destructive) fire, improved counter-battery work, and the platoon all-arms tactics of SS 143. Enabled the more flexible, lower-density infantry formations of 1917–18.

Key element in any analysis of successful set-piece attacks (e.g. later phases of the Somme, Messines, Cambrai, and the Hundred Days).
