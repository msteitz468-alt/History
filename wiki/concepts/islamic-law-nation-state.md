## Definition

"Islamic law and the nation-state" designates the structural antagonism between the pre-modern [[sharia]] — a community-based, jurist-generated, non-state legal order — and the modern nation-state's claim to exclusive sovereignty over law. Hallaq treats the collision between these two formations as the central explanatory axis of Islamic legal history from the 19th century to the present.

The modern state's defining attributes relevant to law are: (1) the exclusive right to make and enforce law; (2) centralization and bureaucratization of adjudication; (3) codification as a tool of homogenization; (4) the production of a uniform legal subject (the citizen); and (5) subordination of all competing legal authorities to the state apparatus.

## Structural Incompatibility

The Sharīa and the modern state were structurally incompatible at every institutional level:

**Sovereignty.** Pre-modern Islamic governance involved "a thousand-year-old situation in which the typical Islamic proto-state administered a law neither of its own making nor subject to the ruler's will to power." The nation-state reversed this: law became an expression of sovereign will, not an independent epistemic order constraining it.

**The legal profession.** The [[ulama]] class derived authority from mastery of [[usul-al-fiqh]] and [[fiqh]] — epistemic authority, not political appointment. The modern state replaced this class with salaried judges and lawyers trained in secular law faculties, appointed and dismissed by state authority. "This dismantling … finally led to the extinction of this group as a species."

**Institutional finance.** The [[waqf]] endowment system had provided the financial independence of [[madrasa]] education and the juristic profession. State seizure of waqf properties (Ottoman Ministry of Endowments from 1826; Egyptian nationalization 1952; Iranian Pahlavi confiscation) removed the material base of independent legal reproduction.

**Adjudication.** The pre-modern [[qadi-judiciary]] court operated within and drew from the local moral community; testimony and credibility were embedded in social networks. The modern state court is a bureaucratic institution with salaried officials, documentary evidence requirements, and centralized hierarchy.

**Plurality vs. uniformity.** The Sharīa tolerated and indeed cultivated legal pluralism ([[taqlid-and-ijtihad]], school divergence, contextual adjudication). The modern state requires homogeneous law: "unifying and homogenizing the law — and all that which constitutes the world of the Subject — is one of the primary concerns of the modern state."

## Post-Colonial Dynamics: Islamization Without Restoration

Paradoxically, post-independence Muslim states that pursued "Islamization" of law found themselves using the identical structural apparatus as their colonial predecessors:

**Egypt** amended Article 2 of its constitution in 1980 to make the Sharīa "the principal source of legislation," but the Supreme Constitutional Court's 1993 definition reduced Sharīa to broad Quranic principles applicable by any judge trained in modern law — effectively evacuating the fiqh tradition while preserving the constitutional symbol.

**Pakistan** created the Federal Shariat Court (1980) to review laws for repugnancy to the Sharīa, but the FSC was systematically constrained: family law, the constitution, fiscal law, and procedural law were excluded from its jurisdiction; its decisions were subject to Supreme Court reversal; and its early bench was drawn entirely from the national courts, not the ulama. "Where true legal power lay" was unmistakable.

**Iran (1979)** presented the starkest case. Khomeini's doctrine of **Wilāyat al-Faqīh** (guardianship of the jurist) claimed to subordinate the state to a Sharīa-expert ruler. But Khomeini's own later formulation inverted this: the Faqīh-Ruler could abrogate even the pillars of Islam if "the interests of the Islamic country" required. Law became "a tool … for the establishment of justice in society, a means for man's intellectual and moral reform" — a distinctly modernist, instrumentalist conception utterly foreign to classical doctrine. "The state does not operate within the framework of the law; rather, it is the law that operates within the state." The ulama who opposed this recognized that "in the guise of upholding Islam the state might make it subservient to its own goals and ultimately absorb it within itself."

**Indonesia** followed a different trajectory but reached the same structural conclusion: the 1991 Compilation of Islamic Law unified and modernized sharī family law under state authority, while the Dutch-era principle that sharī courts required ratification from secular courts was only reversed in 1989 — demonstrating how deeply the colonial state structure had persisted.

**Turkey** (1926) abolished the Sharīa entirely and replaced it with European codes. Yet Islamic practice persisted at the social level, with large proportions of marriages conducted according to religious custom despite legal non-recognition.

## Hallaq's Theoretical Frame

Hallaq draws on Talal Asad's formulation: "In the modern state, law is an element in political strategies — especially strategies for destroying old options and creating new ones." The state's appropriation of the law is not a neutral administrative act; it is a constitutive restructuring of social order.

The family becomes the paradigmatic arena: "inasmuch as the law is a manifestation of the state and its will to power, the family, as a prototype of the nation, is the reconstituted invention of the state." Marriage, divorce, custody, and inheritance law are the state's tools for producing the normative citizen and the nuclear family that loyalty to the nation-state requires.

## Irreversibility

Hallaq's central claim is that the nation-state did not merely restrict the Sharīa: it destroyed the social ecology that made the Sharīa's operation possible. "If the way to the law is through the state, then neither Sharīa nor fiqh can ever be restored, reenacted, or refashioned … without the agency of the state. More importantly, none of these restorative options can be realized without the contaminating influence of the state, rendering extinct the distinctiveness of pre-modern Sharīa as a non-state, community-based, bottom-up jural system."
