## Definition and Scope

"Philosophy in Islamic India" denotes the tradition of rational inquiry — in the fields of metaphysics, logic, kalām, and epistemology — pursued by Muslim scholars in the Indian subcontinent from the early Islamic contact period through the colonial era. It is usefully divided into two partially overlapping phases: (1) an **importation phase** in which the philosophical tradition of Safavid Iran and the earlier Shīrāzī schools was transplanted to Mughal India, and (2) a **distinctive Indian phase** in which a recognizably different curriculum (*dars-i niẓāmī*) developed and Indian scholars began producing original work.

Sources: [[adamson-philosophy-islamic-world-2016]], Chs. 56, 58, 61.

## Historical Background

Islam reached the Indian subcontinent early in ways that influenced science (astronomical and astrological texts; the *Kalīla wa-Dimna* fables) without producing sustained philosophical engagement. The great Central Asian scientist [[al-biruni]] (fl. early 11th c.) wrote the *Taḥqīq mā li-l-Hind* (A Truthful Account of India) after learning Sanskrit in the entourage of the Ghaznavid warlord Maḥmūd; he was struck by parallels between Indian philosophy and Greek thought known in Arabic. But systematic Islamic philosophical activity in India awaited the establishment of the Mughal dynasty.

The **Mughals** were founded by Babur (d. 1530), a descendant of Tamerlane's line, in the early sixteenth century. His grandson Akbar (r. 1556–1605) extended Mughal authority over most of India through gunpowder-based military conquest. The Mughals rose to prominence at almost the same time as the [[safavid-empire]] in Iran, and the two empires were in significant intellectual contact: "it might instead be better to think of a single tradition with two branches" (Adamson, Ch. 56).

## Phase 1: Importation from Safavid Iran (16th–17th century)

### Fatḥallāh Shīrāzī and the Dashtakī Tradition

The principal conduit for Persian philosophical ideas into Mughal India was **Fatḥallāh Shīrāzī** (d. 1589), who had studied under the younger Dashtakī (Ghiyāth al-Dīn) at Shīrāz and then joined Akbar's court. He proved himself an all-around intellectual — astronomical researcher and military engineer as well as philosopher — and disseminated the Shīrāzī philosophical tradition. The Dashtakīs were read in Mughal India "probably to a greater degree than philosophers whose names are more famous today, like Mullā Ṣadrā and even Avicenna himself" (Adamson, Ch. 56).

### ʿAbd al-Ḥakīm Siyālkotī

A scholar of the Punjab region in favor at the court of Shāh Jahān, Siyālkotī was invited by the Shah to pass judgment on the classic disputes between al-Ghazālī and Avicenna (eternity of the universe, God's knowledge of particulars, bodily resurrection). He represents the Mughal court's sustained engagement with the inherited Avicennan tradition.

### Maḥmūd Jawnpūrī and the *Rising Sun*

The "first really major Muslim thinker in India" (Adamson) was **Maḥmūd Jawnpūrī** (fl. first half of 17th c.), active in northern India and tutor to one of Shāh Jahān's sons in Bengal. His philosophical commentary on his own work, *al-Shams al-Bāzigha* (The Rising Sun), became a standard philosophical text for subsequent Indian students.

Jawnpūrī engaged critically with [[mir-damad]]'s "perpetual creation" (*ḥudūth dahrī*) doctrine, arguing that it was incoherent: it requires the same thing (say, a giraffe) to be created twice — once "perpetually" at the level of the divine and once in time — making the thing "prior to itself." He concluded it was better to return to the emanationist framework of al-Fārābī and Avicenna.

## Phase 2: The Dars-i Niẓāmī and Indigenous Development (18th–19th century)

### The Faranghī Maḥall Family and the Curriculum

Over time a distinctively Indian Islamic curriculum emerged — the *dars-i niẓāmī*, named in honor of **Niẓām al-Dīn Sihālavī**, an eighteenth-century member of the prominent Faranghī Maḥall scholarly family of Lucknow. The dars-i niẓāmī was not primarily intended to produce philosophers: it trained students in logic and related subjects as preparation for careers as jurists and government officials. But this pedagogical activity generated a large volume of commentaries and glosses on the curricular texts, and the commentary form, as elsewhere in Islamic intellectual history, proved compatible with genuine philosophical innovation.

The Faranghī Maḥall family received patronage from Mughal princes and "carried forward the rational sciences in eighteenth-century India" (Adamson, Ch. 58). A work by Jawnpūrī was included in the dars-i niẓāmī curriculum; Niẓām al-Dīn was a fifth-generation student of Fatḥallāh Shīrāzī.

### Al-Bihārī and the *Ladder of the Sciences*

**Muḥibballāh al-Bihārī** (d. 1707) produced the *Sullam al-ʿulūm* (Ladder of the Sciences), a dense survey of logic, philosophy of language, and epistemology that was included in the dars-i niẓāmī curriculum. More than ninety commentaries and glosses on this work were composed in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries — a striking illustration of the productivity of the commentary tradition.

A philosophically significant issue emerged in this textual tradition: whether God can be defined. Drawing on Avicenna's distinction between mental existence (*wujūd dhihnī*) and concrete existence (*wujūd khārijī*), commentators debated whether God might be conceptually complex (admitting parts in a definition) while remaining metaphysically simple in reality. This led to a more general skeptical worry, parallel to problems raised in early modern European philosophy by Descartes and Hume: if mental representations may differ in structure from the things they represent, how can we be certain our ideas match external reality? Indian commentators generally did not embrace radical skepticism, holding that experience yields universal knowledge that "reveals" external things — but they acknowledged an unresolvable residual worry.

**Faḍl al-Ḥaqq al-Khayrabādī** (19th c.) went furthest, doubting that our ideas ever fully capture the essences of external things.

### The Khayrābādī School

The Khayrābādī school (19th c.) continued the rational sciences in India under British colonial rule. Its scholars wrote on logic and philosophy more independently than their predecessors, focusing on specific problems rather than working primarily as glossators. Remarkably, they engaged directly with Avicenna (d. 1037), demonstrating that the Avicennan tradition remained philosophically alive more than eight centuries after his death.

**Faḍl al-Ḥaqq al-Khayrābādī** was also a political figure: he supported a fatwā approving of jihād against the British, was tried for treason, and deported to a penal colony where he died in 1861. Yet his son ʿAbd al-Ḥaqq continued the school's activities with royal patronage, and the school survived into the early twentieth century.

## Cross-Cultural Encounters

### Akbar's Policy

Emperor Akbar decreed that religious scholars should study medicine, logic, physics, mathematics, metaphysics, history, and the Hindu traditions, including Sanskrit and the Nyaya and Vedanta philosophical schools. This was partly political (Akbar married Hindu women and maintained the loyalty of a Hindu majority population) but generated genuine philosophical cross-fertilization.

### Dārā Shikūh and the *Confluence of the Oceans*

The most striking instance of Islamic-Hindu philosophical encounter was the work of the seventeenth-century Mughal prince **Dārā Shikūh** (1615–59), son and intended heir of Shāh Jahān. A passionate student of Sufism and Sanskrit literature, Dārā Shikūh produced translations of the Upanishads into Persian and pronounced them "the oldest of the revelations sent by God to humankind." His treatise *Majmaʿ al-baḥrayn* (Confluence of the Oceans) systematically compares Islamic philosophical Sufi terminology with Sanskrit concepts:

- Sanskrit *māyā* matched to the Sufi concept of passionate love (*ʿishq*)
- Koranic divine names matched to Sanskrit equivalents
- Avicenna's theory of the five internal senses identified in Sanskrit sources

He argued that both traditions share the same cosmological theories and similar accounts of the soul, differing only in their outer garb. World-cycles repeating infinitely, he maintained, could be reconciled with the Koran's resurrection verses. Dārā was later executed on charges of irreligion (clearly politically motivated — his death paved the way for his brother's accession), but his work represents a philosophically sophisticated attempt at religious syncretism grounded in both Islamic Sufism and Sanskrit scholarship.

## Shāh Walī Allāh

An eighteenth-century Delhi thinker, **Shāh Walī Allāh** (d. 1762), combined Sufi-inflected metaphysics with a "renewal" (*tajdīd*) program for Islam. Drawing on the Illuminationist-Sufi concept of the "world of images" (archetypes like Platonic Forms accessible through the imagination), he argued that the various religions of the world are specific versions of a single paradigm religion. Yet he maintained Islam's privileged status: Muḥammad's message was uniquely universal and therefore "most final." His thinking influenced Muhammad Iqbal in the twentieth century. See [[muhammad-iqbal]].

## Historiography

Philosophy in Islamic India has received far less attention in European scholarship than the parallel Safavid or Ottoman traditions. Adamson's Ch. 56 and Ch. 58 provide the principal modern English-language survey for this period in a general history of Islamic philosophy. The *dars-i niẓāmī* curriculum and the al-Bihārī commentary tradition in particular remain understudied.

**Collection coverage**: Weak to moderate. No specialist monographs on Mughal-era philosophy are confirmed present. The Bibliotheca Alexandrina's coverage of South Asia is thin overall. The Adamson survey is the primary source.
