# Merovingian Religious Architecture

**Nearly three centuries of church-building — basilicas, octagonal baptisteries, and the spolia of Rome —
that remade the Gallic landscape as Christian.** Pascale Chevalier's chapter (Oxford Handbook, Ch. 30)
surveys the ecclesiastical buildings of Merovingian Gaul, the material expression of the religious
construction boom that left over 800 establishments across Francia (see [[merovingian-cities]]).

## Definition and Scope

The overwhelming majority of Merovingian churches were **timber-roofed basilicas** with a single or
three-aisled nave and a semicircular (apsidal) east end — "both the inherited Late Roman church type and
the locally reproduced norm" through the fifth–seventh centuries. Rarer cruciform plans exist (Saint-
Laurent at Grenoble, rebuilt in the sixth century). The period (c. 481–751) represents roughly **270 years
of intensive ecclesiastical construction**.

## Baptisteries and Key Buildings

The most architecturally elaborate surviving Merovingian buildings are **baptisteries**, "commonly built
on an octagonal plan," with high-quality masonry and decorative programs — Aix-en-Provence, Fréjus,
Marseille, and Riez. The benchmark case study is the **baptistery of Saint-Jean at Poitiers**, "the most
thoroughly studied Merovingian ecclesiastical building," with multiple construction phases. Chevalier
re-dates the **Le Puy** baptistery to the late fifth or sixth century, confirming "the active construction
of major baptisteries throughout the Merovingian period proper."

## Furnishings, Decoration, and Spolia

Churches were equipped with complex liturgical furniture — stone altars, chancel screens (*cancellum*), a
raised processional *solea*, and an *ambo* (pulpit) — supporting the liturgy described in
[[merovingian-liturgy-and-lay-religion]]. Stained-glass windows are archaeologically attested at many
sites, and stucco decoration was common. The reuse of antique material — **spolia** of columns, capitals,
marble, and brick — was "ubiquitous," reflecting both the availability of Roman material and "a deliberate
aesthetic/ideological link to the Roman past," a built parallel to the Romanizing claims of
[[merovingian-coinage]] and [[merovingian-epigraphy]].

## Historiography

Chevalier's "new reflections" revise the chronology of major buildings (notably Le Puy), pushing
significant construction into the Merovingian period proper rather than treating it as Carolingian, and
join the volume's larger argument against a culturally inert "Dark Age." Inscriptions and graffiti from
monastic sites (Hamage, Luxeuil) reinforce how widespread building and Christian material culture had
become (see [[merovingian-epigraphy]], [[merovingian-monasticism]]).
