# Holy War (Jihad) 1914 — German Global Strategy and Ottoman Proclamation

The 1914 invocation of Islamic holy war (jihad) was a German-initiated peripheral strategy to widen the European war by inciting revolt among the approximately 270 million Muslims under British, Russian, and French rule, using the Ottoman Sultan-Caliph and the entry of Turkey as the instrument. It exemplified the Central Powers’ effort to convert naval and colonial inferiority into global reach.

## Definition and Scope

Unlike classical juristic *jihād* (spiritual striving or regulated military campaigns against the Abode of War), the 1914 version was a modern, instrumental, pan-Islamic and proto-nationalist appeal against European imperialism. The fatwas of November 1914 took “the form of general mobilization” and summoned “the ulema, professors and teachers, and all students of the religious and profane sciences ... together with most officials, and the young men of the motherland” alongside universal conscription for Ottoman subjects aged 20–45.

The Sultan claimed the Caliphate (inherited through the Abbasids, custodianship of Mecca and Medina), carrying implicit temporal authority over Muslims worldwide. The appeal was deliberately extended to Shi’ites as well as Sunnis and translated into Arabic, Persian, Urdu, and Tatar. It named Britain, France, Russia, Serbia, and Montenegro as enemies enslaving Muslims and called on the Crimea, Kazan, Turkestan, Bokhara, Khiva, India, China, Afghanistan, Iran, and Africa to rise.

## Origin and German Strategic Concept

German global strategy pre-dated and shaped the Ottoman alliance. Colmar von der Goltz (1890s) envisioned a strong Turkish army diverting Russians from East Prussia via the Caucasus or seizing the Suez Canal from southern Syria to cut Britain’s route to India.

Max von Oppenheim (archaeologist, Arabic expert) told Bülow in 1908 that a Turco-German war against England would produce “an overall revolt of the Muslims in the British colonies.” On 30 July 1914 the Kaiser wrote: “Our consuls in Turkey and India, our agents, etc., must rouse the whole Moslem world into wild rebellion against this hateful, mendacious, unprincipled nation of shopkeepers; if we are going to shed our blood, then England must at least lose India.”

On 2 August Moltke demanded revolution in India and Egypt and pressure on Persia; he added the Caucasus (5 Aug), Morocco/Tunisia/Algeria/Afghanistan (20 Aug), and Zionism. “In just over two weeks Moltke had drawn up a shopping list that embraced, as the Kaiser told Enver on 15 August, Asia, India, Egypt, and Africa.” But the army had no men or munitions to spare; overland links were rudimentary; expertise lacking. OHL relied on the foreign office (Zimmermann, Oppenheim, Jäckh) to conjure revolutionary armies while insisting on control of objectives.

The Ottoman alliance provided legitimacy; yet German projects often ignored Turkish national sensibilities. Turkey entered for its own reasons (sovereignty, security against Russia) and had no intention of becoming Germany’s proxy. In North Africa, Central Asia, and the Near East, Turkish and German objectives overlapped but were not congruent.

## Implementation and the Ottoman Proclamation

Enver’s 22 October Turkish war plan listed six options, one of which was “a holy war... against the enemies of the Central Powers.” The most specific was the attack on Egypt (VIII corps, possible XII corps support). “Enver’s plan was, in truth, not a plan... Enver gave no attention to either of the major areas of vulnerability, the Dardanelles and Mesopotamia.”

The confirmation came on 14 November 1914 with the Sheikh-ul-Islam’s declaration before the Sultan. Up to 270 million Muslims (170m Asia, 50m Africa) were potentially targeted; fewer than 2 million lived under German rule. Muslims opposing the Caliphate were threatened with “the fire of hell”; those fighting Germany/Austria-Hungary with “painful torment.”

Within Turkey the religious-secular relationship was complex: the Young Turks were modernizing and secularizing (emancipation of women, civil law) yet grafted their agenda onto the Caliphate appeal for legitimacy and unity. Enver had used the Caliphate successfully in Libya. The Kaiser appealed to Enver specifically in these terms.

## Key Theatres and Outcomes (1914–1915)

- **Caucasus**: 3rd Army offensive; Sarikamish disaster (Dec 1914–Jan 1915) — Turks lost ~75,000 (including 30,000 to frostbite); Russians ~20,000. Confirmed image of Ottoman decline; limited war expansion.
- **Suez, Egypt, Libya**: Turkish 4th Army (19,000 men) attacked across Sinai; British forces in Egypt ~70,000. Logistical impossibility (railhead distance, desert); Bedouin volunteers used for agitation. Egypt declared British protectorate 18 Dec 1914; Khedive Abbas Hilmi deposed. Attack failed; educated Egyptian nationalists remained passive or pro-German in sentiment only.
- **Persia and Afghanistan**: German mission (Niedermayer, Wassmuss et al., later Indian revolutionaries under Hentig/Pratap) sent via Persia to Kabul to secure Afghan invasion of India and internal revolt. Persia divided and weak (Russian north, British south spheres post-1907); “without patriotic energy.” Missions plagued by command disputes (army vs foreign office). Limited results.
- **India**: Primary British imperial concern. Indian army financed by India, garrisoned empire; 1914 dispatch of five Indian Expeditionary Forces left subcontinent exposed (British troops down to 15,000). Bengal terrorism intensified (36 incidents 1915); Defence of India Act enabled mass internments. Overseas revolutionaries (Paris, US) sought German aid; provisional government in Kabul had minimal local impact.

## Causal Drivers and Limitations

`driven_by:` German recognition of naval/colonial weakness and desire to strike Entente at imperial periphery; Ottoman desire to recover lost provinces (Egypt, Libya) and assert Caliphal leadership; pre-existing pan-Islamic and pan-Turanian ideas (Gokalp, Young Turks).

`produces:` attempted widening of war; propaganda apparatus (Oppenheim); some agitation (Bedouin, frontier tribes) but no mass uprisings.

Limitations: shallow staff work; logistical overstretch; fractured local nationalisms (Egyptian Watanists vs Umma; Persian tribalism); Turkish priorities not identical to German; Entente pre-emptive measures (protectorate, internment, martial law); exemption of Italy from jihad call for diplomatic reasons.

The strategy was “an unguided missile, its purpose to turn Germany’s encirclement and its naval and colonial inferiority to good account. Political objectives were subordinated to military necessity.”

## Historiography and Critiques

Grounded exclusively in Hew Strachan, *The First World War, Volume I: To Arms* (2001), Ch. 9. Strachan presents the holy war as emblematic of German “peripheral strategy” and the *Weltkrieg* conception, but also of wishful thinking, internal German friction (OHL vs Auswärtiges Amt), and the limits of imposing revolution from outside. Emphasizes that Turkey entered for rational and emotional domestic reasons, not as pawn. The 14 November proclamation is the symbolic high point; actual military outcomes (Sarikamish, Suez failure) demonstrated overreach.

Contested elements: degree of genuine pan-Islamic fervour vs instrumental use; effectiveness of German missions (Niedermayer credited personally despite failures); whether strategy prolonged or merely complicated the war.
