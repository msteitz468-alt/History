## Definition and Scope

This page documents Ronald C. Po's critique, in chapter 24 of the *Cambridge History of the Pacific Ocean* (2022), of three dominant misperceptions about China's relationship with the sea during the Qing period (1644–1839): (1) that Qing expansion was purely territorial-continental, with no maritime dimension; (2) that the Qing lacked maritime consciousness; and (3) that maritime China can be understood primarily through the lens of southeast coastal China. Po argues these misperceptions derive largely from reading Qing history through the lens of its nineteenth-century maritime failures rather than its active eighteenth-century maritime engagement.

The chapter builds on Po's own book *The Blue Frontier: Maritime Vision and Power in the Qing Empire* (Cambridge University Press, 2018).

## Misperception 1: Qing Expansion as Purely Territorial

Standard historiography, following John King Fairbank's "Confucian society unprepared for the crisis presented by the West," cast the Qing as a passive continental regime hostile or indifferent to the sea. Georg Hegel theorized China as "by its very nature averse to the sea." A naval historian wrote: "during the Qing dynasty, the Chinese did not understand the developing maritime dimension of their national security and prosperity."

Po's counter-argument: studies of the Qing as a "high Qing imperialist" land power — particularly Peter Perdue's *China Marches West* — have shown the Qing was an active imperial actor in Central Eurasia during the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. But this literature focused on inland frontiers. Po insists that "the maritime frontier and oceanic governance were very much discounted in academic discussion" and that once inland borders were secured, "the Qing did not overlook the importance of managing its sea border."

The Qing's primary military concern was always with Inner Asian threats from Mongolians and Dzungars; the maritime frontier seemed relatively calm before Western gunboats arrived. But this asymmetry of threat perception should not be read as maritime indifference.

### The Qing Navy and Its Sea Zones

During the Kangxi–Yongzheng–Qianlong period, the Qing established a naval force "responsible for guarding and policing four discrete yet interconnected sea zones off the China coast": the Guangdong coast, the Taiwan Strait, the Jiangsu-Zhejiang spaces, and the Bohai Sea. Functions included:
- Suppressing piracy (the south China piracy crisis tested Qing maritime sovereignty)
- Protecting commodity shipping
- Rescuing and repatriating shipwreck survivors — including those from Siam, Luzon, Korea, and the Ryukyus — "irrespective of race and origin, than were its Asian neighbours"
- Establishing customs institutions to regulate sea trade

"Prior to the nineteenth century, its supremacy across the western Pacific fostered domestic and Sino-nanyang trade (trade between China and Southeast Asia)."

## Misperception 2: Lack of Maritime Consciousness

Po adopts the IMO definition of maritime consciousness as "maritime domain awareness (MDA)" — a state's effective understanding of matters associated with the maritime domain affecting its "security, safety, economy, or environment." He argues China's maritime consciousness has been "long discounted or overlooked" through a set of stereotypes: (1) China is a purely agrarian society; (2) Confucian hierarchy demeans commerce; (3) imperial officials suppressed merchants to extract taxes.

Po's counter-evidence:

**Regional differentiation**: "At least during the Qing Dynasty, some coastal provinces, such as Fujian and Guangdong, were not very ideal for crop cultivation." Qing scholar-official Lan Tingzhen (1664–1730) noted "Fujian has very limited arable farmland and more than half of the population turned to maritime trade." Since the Song and Yuan, Fujian and Guangdong were established economic hubs in the Nanyang trade.

**Qing statecraft writers**: Lan Dingyuan (1680–1733), Kong Yuxun, Mao Wenquan, Chen Lunjiong (1703–30) urged Qing authorities to allow merchants to move freely by land or sea to maximize trading opportunity. "The classification of merchants as the lowest group in the Qing's four-class status system… was not as straightforward as one might assume, since the state's attitudes towards commerce… was actually quite flexible and enlightened."

**Gang Zhao** (*The Qing Opening to the Ocean*, 2013) argues Qing maritime policy was "the most open in Chinese history in terms of encouraging private traders and opening ports."

**Customs institutionalization**: After Kangxi annexed Taiwan in 1683, a maritime customs office emerged in 1684 as "an institutional innovation that provided a more systematic and centralized approach to managing private sea trade and maritime security." The customs system covered eight major sea lanes.

### The Inner–Outer Sea Framework

Qing sea charts such as the *Qisheng yanhai tu* applied an "inner–outer binary" model: "the inner sea constituted the empire's domestic seawater, where the monarch could claim ownership of maritime resources within the empire's readily accessible oceanic realm. By contrast, the outer sea was a capricious domain that lay beyond the purview of administrative governance and economic extraction." This binary led the Qing to know a great deal about coastal Southeast Asia but "relatively little about countries farther away" — a structural difference from European maritime consciousness, which extended to global scale from the sixteenth century. The inner-outer framework was not a fixed boundary: "time and space, coupled with relationality, malleability, mutuality, and contrariety, were the foundations of the Qing's justifications for its sovereignty across the western end to the Pacific Ocean."

The concept of Chinese sea power is not reducible to Mahan's model. Zhang Xie (1574–1640) used the notion of "extending military control onto the sea" in Chinese terms. Joseph Needham observed that "naval armed might meant something very different indeed in the Chinese and the Portuguese interpretations." Po uses "sea power" to denote "the set of maritime practices and policies through which the Qing court fashioned and maintained its maritime borders."

## Misperception 3: Southeast China Centrism

Existing maritime China scholarship concentrates almost exclusively on the coast south of Shanghai: Fujian, Guangdong, the Pearl River Delta, Nanyang trade. Po calls this "southeast China centrism." He argues the Bohai Sea — the innermost gulf of the Yellow Sea, bounded by Liaoning Peninsula to the east and Shandong Peninsula to the south — constitutes an equally important "sub-maritime middle ground" that has been systematically neglected.

### The Bohai Sea as a Maritime Middle Ground

The Bohai is the smallest of China's four seas (78,000 km² surface area, 823,000 km² total with basins draining into it). It is closest to Beijing and has been closely guarded since the eleventh century. Three Qing navies were established to police it. The PRC declared it China's "definite internal sea" in 1958.

**Historical significance:**
- Qin Shi Huang sent expeditions into the Bohai in search of immortality elixirs; Emperor Wu of Han built replicas of fairy islands to attract immortals — making it a site of cosmological significance in Chinese literary and political imagination.
- From the Ming onwards, famous for sea salt production and fisheries (documented by Jiang Chenying and Gu Zuyu in the seventeenth century).
- From the eighteenth century: soybean paste (*dou bing*) shipped from the Bohai region to Fujian and Guangdong, where Fujian and Guangdong merchants discovered it enhanced deteriorating southern soil quality — "a catalyst that helped foster Bohai's commercial network as well as the trading connection between north and south China."

**Port Cities as Standard and Central Markets** (applying Skinner's marketing model to maritime space):
- Standard markets: Longkou, Dongying, Yantai (northern Shandong), Zhili, Fengtian, Huludao, Panjin, Yinghou
- Central markets: Dengzhou (connected traders from Shandong to northeast and southeast China, Korea, Japan — also a strategic naval station since the Yongzheng era), Jinzhou, Qinhuangdao (pearl oysters, grapes, walnuts; wholesaling hub for Bohai merchants and south Chinese buyers), Dalian

By the early nineteenth century, Qing experiments with coastal grain transport routes from south to north via the northeast coast, using Shanghai merchant shippers, further integrated Bohai commercial networks into official governance — "a notable expansion and validation of the collaborative role of extra-bureaucratic commercial groups in coastal governance."

## Braudel's Influence and the Pacific Reorientation

Po opens the chapter by tracing the intellectual genealogy of oceanic history to Fernand Braudel's 1949 *The Mediterranean and the Mediterranean World in the Age of Philip II*. After Braudel, "the Atlantic, Pacific, and Indian oceans" were reframed as "arenas that facilitated trans-regional exchanges and inter-cultural interactions" and the histories of countries attached to them were "significantly rewritten throughout the second half of the twentieth century." Wang Gungwu is credited as the chief promoter of a maritime perspective on Chinese history from within the field of Chinese studies.

Po's chapter is positioned as a corrective to this literature — not questioning its validity but expanding its geographic and chronological scope: the long eighteenth century (1644–1839) was "relatively under-researched from a China-centred maritime angle" and was also "the century when China began to interact more proactively with the Pacific world."

## Implications for Contemporary Claims

Po draws an explicit, careful comparison between the Qing inner-outer binary and the PRC's contemporary maritime sovereignty claims in the South China Sea (nine-dash line): "we need to be very careful when comparing the inner-outer model with that of the European maritime ruling principle." The Qing never drew exact boundary lines; "no exact boundary such as the nine-dash line had been established for the maritime space the Qing court claimed."

## Historiography

Po's chapter is positioned within the "New Qing History" that reframes the Qing as an active imperial and maritime power, drawing on Laura Hostetler, Peter Perdue, and Yuanchong Wang. It challenges both Fairbank's "China unprepared" thesis and the reduction of Qing maritime history to the story of its late decline. The chapter supplements the quantitative political-economy analysis of Deng (ch. 23) with cultural-intellectual history: literary perceptions, sea charts, statecraft writing, and the construction of maritime consciousness in Qing texts.
