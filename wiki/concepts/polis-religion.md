# Polis Religion (Religion and the State in Archaic Greece)

**Polis religion** names the distinctive Archaic Greek relationship between **cult and
state**: a society wholly **embedded in religion** that nonetheless kept its religious
practices under **political control** — never ruled by priests or prophets.
[[boardman-cah-v4-1988|CAH Vol. IV]] (Ch. 7a, J. K. Davies) builds the analysis around a
single Herodotean vignette (the Samians rescuing the Corcyraean boys by inventing a
festival) to show "the vocabulary and syntax of Greek ritual" and the **two ways of
defining a community** — as a descent-group bound by cult, and as an autonomous political
actor.

## The double movement toward secularization

Davies' thesis is that, far from Greece becoming a culture of **temple-states and priestly
leaderships** (as some Near Eastern societies did), a **double movement** tipped the
balance toward the polis:
1. a "gradual and partial **emancipation of 'the state'** from the rhythms and institutions
   of cult"; and
2. a "**reorientation and redefinition of cult** activities in ways more convenient for
   civic organization."

These were "not foregone conclusions" — *polis* did not have to prevail over shrine — so
the chapter sets out to identify **why** the direction of change was toward secularization.

## Six forms of the movement

Davies traces it through six channels:
- **Freedom to innovate in cult**, especially hero-cults — the politically motivated
  **transfer of heroes' bones** (Orestes to Sparta c. 560, the Aeacidae lent to Thebes and
  returned as useless, Theseus to Athens 476), close to Roman *evocatio*.
- **Public officials taking over cult** — at Athens the archon, *basileus* and polemarch
  superintend festivals; new offices (*hieropoioi*, *hieromnemones*) are created **rather
  than enlarging the priesthoods**, a deliberate choice to keep cult administration in the
  community's hands.
- **Written sacred law** (*leges sacrae*) — taboo and sacrifice-calendars fixed and codified
  on stone, becoming **alterable by the people** (the Olympia decree: changes valid "with
  the approval of the Council and the people in full assembly").
- **Civic gods** — the patron deity (Athena Polias for Athens), the ancestral god (Apollo
  Patrous), extending the lineage-descent idea to the whole citizen body.
- **"Public" festivals** (*demosios*/*demoteles*) — financed from state revenue (the
  Panathenaea 566, the Dionysia 502/1), meeting needs the lineage-cults could not, and
  expressing "self-conscious corporate identity."
- **Cult defining the space of the polis** — processions, branch-shrines, synoecism-cults
  (the Athenian Synoikia) linking centre and periphery.

## Two driving forces

The chapter explains the pattern by two long-term phenomena: the **partial emancipation of
the moral order from theology** (oaths, divine witnesses, curses, and divine punishments
slowly giving way as the state's own coercive power grew), and the **impact of increased
wealth** — for ritual diverts resources, and the sixth-century turn to **stone temples**
(the 300 talents for the Alcmaeonid temple at Delphi; tax-surpluses bespoken for the
Ephesian Artemisium) drew on city and dynastic funds, making patronage "not just piety but
an investment in civic or personal prestige" that **subordinated the god** to city or
tyrant. The growing temple treasuries also helped create the matrix of fifth-century
**public finance**.

## Fifth-century Athenian civic religion (CAH V)

[[lewis-cah-v5-1992|CAH Vol. V]] (Chs. 8e–8f, Burkert and Knox) shows the mature form of
polis religion at Classical Athens. Its defining absences are structural: **no priestly
hierarchy** (priests were "technicians of ritual sacrifice" over local and family cult),
**no holy book, no fixed sacred history, no revealed ethical code**. Consequently
**theology was the business of the poets** — Homer and Hesiod, and then the
[[athenian-drama|tragedians]], who "assumed the same prerogative and burden" of relating the
gods to human justice. Worship was organized as **civic festival**: the cult calendar
defined the community's identity through the year (Burkert), and the great
**[[athenian-drama|Dionysia]]** was at once an act of worship of Dionysus and "a demonstration
to the Greek world of Athenian imperial power" — the [[delian-league|tribute]] displayed, the
war-orphans paraded, financed (like the warships) through the **liturgy** system. The
**[[parthenon|Parthenon]]**, a shrine of Athena Polias, stored the imperial reserves in its
treasury — the fusion of the political and the religious that Davies' Archaic analysis
predicts, now at full civic scale.

## Critique

Davies explicitly rejects the over-simplifications that "State, people and religion were
indissolubly united" or that religion became "a dependent appendage of national sentiment":
much everyday piety (phratry, *genos*, household, immigrant and private cults) lay **outside**
polis boundaries, and major shrines (Olympia, Delphi, Dodona) predated any cohesive polis
nearby. The relationship "was not one of identity," but turned on the **different social
purposes** the same group or institution could serve.
