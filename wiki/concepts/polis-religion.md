# Polis Religion (Religion and the State in Archaic Greece)

**Polis religion** names the distinctive Archaic Greek relationship between **cult and
state**: a society wholly **embedded in religion** that nonetheless kept its religious
practices under **political control** — never ruled by priests or prophets.
[[boardman-cah-v4-1988|CAH Vol. IV]] (Ch. 7a, J. K. Davies) builds the analysis around a
single Herodotean vignette (the Samians rescuing the Corcyraean boys by inventing a
festival) to show "the vocabulary and syntax of Greek ritual" and the **two ways of
defining a community** — as a descent-group bound by cult, and as an autonomous political
actor.

## The double movement toward secularization

Davies' thesis is that, far from Greece becoming a culture of **temple-states and priestly
leaderships** (as some Near Eastern societies did), a **double movement** tipped the
balance toward the polis:
1. a "gradual and partial **emancipation of 'the state'** from the rhythms and institutions
   of cult"; and
2. a "**reorientation and redefinition of cult** activities in ways more convenient for
   civic organization."

These were "not foregone conclusions" — *polis* did not have to prevail over shrine — so
the chapter sets out to identify **why** the direction of change was toward secularization.

## Six forms of the movement

Davies traces it through six channels:
- **Freedom to innovate in cult**, especially hero-cults — the politically motivated
  **transfer of heroes' bones** (Orestes to Sparta c. 560, the Aeacidae lent to Thebes and
  returned as useless, Theseus to Athens 476), close to Roman *evocatio*.
- **Public officials taking over cult** — at Athens the archon, *basileus* and polemarch
  superintend festivals; new offices (*hieropoioi*, *hieromnemones*) are created **rather
  than enlarging the priesthoods**, a deliberate choice to keep cult administration in the
  community's hands.
- **Written sacred law** (*leges sacrae*) — taboo and sacrifice-calendars fixed and codified
  on stone, becoming **alterable by the people** (the Olympia decree: changes valid "with
  the approval of the Council and the people in full assembly").
- **Civic gods** — the patron deity (Athena Polias for Athens), the ancestral god (Apollo
  Patrous), extending the lineage-descent idea to the whole citizen body.
- **"Public" festivals** (*demosios*/*demoteles*) — financed from state revenue (the
  Panathenaea 566, the Dionysia 502/1), meeting needs the lineage-cults could not, and
  expressing "self-conscious corporate identity."
- **Cult defining the space of the polis** — processions, branch-shrines, synoecism-cults
  (the Athenian Synoikia) linking centre and periphery.

## Two driving forces

The chapter explains the pattern by two long-term phenomena: the **partial emancipation of
the moral order from theology** (oaths, divine witnesses, curses, and divine punishments
slowly giving way as the state's own coercive power grew), and the **impact of increased
wealth** — for ritual diverts resources, and the sixth-century turn to **stone temples**
(the 300 talents for the Alcmaeonid temple at Delphi; tax-surpluses bespoken for the
Ephesian Artemisium) drew on city and dynastic funds, making patronage "not just piety but
an investment in civic or personal prestige" that **subordinated the god** to city or
tyrant. The growing temple treasuries also helped create the matrix of fifth-century
**public finance**.

## Fifth-century Athenian civic religion (CAH V)

[[lewis-cah-v5-1992|CAH Vol. V]] (Chs. 8e–8f, Burkert and Knox) shows the mature form of
polis religion at Classical Athens. Its defining absences are structural: **no priestly
hierarchy** (priests were "technicians of ritual sacrifice" over local and family cult),
**no holy book, no fixed sacred history, no revealed ethical code**. Consequently
**theology was the business of the poets** — Homer and Hesiod, and then the
[[athenian-drama|tragedians]], who "assumed the same prerogative and burden" of relating the
gods to human justice. Worship was organized as **civic festival**: the cult calendar
defined the community's identity through the year (Burkert), and the great
**[[athenian-drama|Dionysia]]** was at once an act of worship of Dionysus and "a demonstration
to the Greek world of Athenian imperial power" — the [[delian-league|tribute]] displayed, the
war-orphans paraded, financed (like the warships) through the **liturgy** system. The
**[[parthenon|Parthenon]]**, a shrine of Athena Polias, stored the imperial reserves in its
treasury — the fusion of the political and the religious that Davies' Archaic analysis
predicts, now at full civic scale.

## Critique

Davies explicitly rejects the over-simplifications that "State, people and religion were
indissolubly united" or that religion became "a dependent appendage of national sentiment":
much everyday piety (phratry, *genos*, household, immigrant and private cults) lay **outside**
polis boundaries, and major shrines (Olympia, Delphi, Dodona) predated any cohesive polis
nearby. The relationship "was not one of identity," but turned on the **different social
purposes** the same group or institution could serve.

## From Hall, *Archaic Greek World* (2014)

[[sources/hall-history-archaic-greek-world-2014|Hall (2014)]] accepts that each *polis* developed a cult system, calendar, patron deities (Zeus Polieus, Athena Polias/Polioukhos), civic priests, and mediation of participation at panhellenic shrines — but calls the pure “polis religion” picture **misleading even for the Classical period**.

**Cross-cutting cultic communities.** The Delphic Amphictyony is organized by *ethnē*, not *poleis*. Exclusion from sanctuaries is sometimes **ethnic** (Dorian *xenoi* barred at Paros; Cleomenes barred as Dorian from Athena Polias at Athens) or **gender-based** (Thesmophoria), not simply civic. Extra-urban sanctuaries (Perachora, Isthmia, Argive Heraion, Samian Heraion, Didyma) fail de Polignac-style tests as automatic boundary-markers of a single *polis*: many predate urbanism, receive large shares of non-Greek metal offerings, and function as arenas of competitive elite display shared across settlements. Eighth-century temple and dedication surges are **not secure *polis* birth certificates** — Dark Age ritual continuity (e.g. Kalapodhi before 1200 in a non-*polis* landscape) and early temples in regions where *poleis* appear late (Achaea, Thermon/Aetolia) show cult can express individual or multi-settlement power (including later tyrants’ building) without neonate citizen collectivity.

**Relation to Davies.** Hall’s caveats align with Davies’s own rejection of total identity between state, people and religion, while hardening the chronological point: cult evidence does not tell a “fundamentally different story” from gradual urbanization. See [[panhellenic-games]] for amphictyonic Delphi and the First Sacred War problem.

**Source:** [[sources/hall-history-archaic-greek-world-2014|Hall (2014)]], Ch. 4 (cult and the rise of the *polis*).

## Cult, Civic Status, and Citizenship (Osborne 2000, Ch. 3, Thomas)

[[sources/osborne-classical-greece-2000|Osborne (ed.), *Classical Greece 500–323 BC*]]
(Ch. 3, Rosalind Thomas) argues that ancient political theorists — notably Aristotle, and
historians like Thucydides — largely neglected the religious dimension of polis life in
their analyses, an omission that created a lasting modern scholarly gap around the
inseparability of cult and citizenship. Each polis had its own patron deity and a
localized version of shared Panhellenic cults — Hera at Samos and Argos, Athena Polias at
Athens with her cult statue in the Parthenon.

Cult participation directly reinforced civic status: the phratries (brotherhoods) held the
**Apatouria** festival, at which children were formally introduced and vetted against
fraudulent citizenship claims (directly relevant to the Neaera citizenship-fraud case).
Standing for Athenian office required proving citizenship by specifying the location of
family altars to Zeus Herkeios and Apollo Patroos, and of family graves ([Aristotle],
*Constitution of the Athenians* 55.3). Some cults were celebrated exclusively by citizen
women — notably the **Thesmophoria**, a Demeter fertility festival — showing gendered
civic-religious participation. The Great Panathenaea, celebrated every four years,
displayed the full civic hierarchy in procession (citizens, women, metics, and, more
subordinately, slaves); once Athens developed her maritime empire, she required subject
allies to bring a cow and hoplite panoply as tribute-cult offerings to the Panathenaea,
symbolizing imperial subordination through religious participation.

New cults could be formally admitted into a polis's religious life by state decision:
Athens sanctioned metic cults (Bendis the Thracian goddess, Isis) on petition, and adopted
Pan and Boreas after apparitions/aid during the Persian Wars (490, 480 BC); Asclepius
arrived in Athens in 420 BC, initially housed by the tragedian Sophocles. Some cities used
formal communal curses inscribed on stone to enforce political loyalty and deter treason —
Teos in Ionia (c. 470 BC) invoked destruction on wrongdoers and their descendants. By
contrast, "elective" mystery cults — the Eleusinian Mysteries of Demeter, and the Cabiri
("Great Gods") of Samothrace — transcended normal polis, tribal, and even ethnic
boundaries, admitting initiates by individual choice regardless of civic status, gender, or
free/slave status.

## OHCW (Parker) — orthopraxy, embedded cult, secular control

[[boardman-oxford-history-classical-world-1986|OHCW (1986)]], Ch. 11 (Robert Parker), supplies the classical counterpart to Davies’s Archaic analysis. **No Church:** power in religious matters lay with secular power-holders; priests part-time specialists without clerical class; seers know, rulers decide (Themistocles vs wooden-walls experts, 480). There was therefore “no religious organization that could spread moral teaching, develop doctrine, or impose an orthodoxy.” **Orthopraxy:** recognizing the gods meant observing cult; piety was acts of respect, not private spiritual diaries; household heads and magistrates sacrificed for dependents; religion reinforced communal belonging over intention-ethics.

**Embedded religion.** Parker urges a concept parallel to embedded economy: every formal social grouping was also a religious grouping, from household Zeus of the Courtyard to Greeks as nation at Panhellenic sanctuaries; belonging meant sharing lustral water before sacrifice. At Athens, democracy transformed cult (aristocratic cults absorbed, deme importance rose); slaves lacked distinctive group religion. Sacrifice calendars listed gods/heroes, offerings, and days; dominant tone festive; Panhellenic games and dramatic festivals remained religious ceremonies. *Do ut des* prayer-economy created ordered two-sided relation with the god, not simple bribery.

**Crisis and survival.** Late-fifth philosophical critique and Socrates’ charge of not recognizing the city’s gods mark a religious crisis that was “surmounted”; explicit atheism rare; philosophers kept the divine as wise/good while still using traditional cult forms. Ruler-cult (Lysander; Alexander; Hellenistic kings) signals loss of political freedom more than loss of faith. Aligns with Davies’s double movement toward secular control of cult and Hall’s caveats that cult ≠ pure *polis* birth certificate — see full formation at [[greek-religion-and-mythology]].
