# The Scientific Revolution

## Definition and Scope

The **Scientific Revolution** is the transformation of European natural knowledge between roughly
**1543** (Copernicus's *De revolutionibus* and Vesalius's *De humani corporis fabrica*) and **1700**
(Newton's *Principia*, 1687) — the emergence of a mathematised, experimental, mechanical
understanding of nature and of new institutions and methods for producing knowledge. As treated in
the [[oxford-handbook-early-modern-europe-v2|Oxford Handbook of Early Modern European History]]
(Kathleen Crowther), it is a defining strand of early-modern intellectual history, though the term
itself is now heavily qualified.

## Major developments

- **Astronomy and cosmology**: the **Copernican** heliocentric model; **Kepler's** elliptical
  orbits; **Galileo's** telescopic observations and his trial (1633); culminating in **Newton's**
  universal gravitation and laws of motion.
- **The mechanical philosophy**: the conception of nature as matter in motion, governed by law
  (Descartes, Boyle, Hobbes, Gassendi), displacing the Aristotelian-scholastic cosmos of
  qualities and final causes.
- **Anatomy and physiology**: Vesalius's anatomy; Harvey's demonstration of the circulation of the
  blood (1628).
- **Method**: programmatic statements of a new way of knowing — **Bacon's** inductive,
  experimental, useful science and **Descartes's** mathematical rationalism.
- **Instruments and institutions**: the telescope, microscope, barometer, and air-pump; and the new
  **scientific societies** (the Royal Society, 1660; the Académie des Sciences, 1666) and journals
  that made knowledge collective and public.

## Emic vs. Etic

"The Scientific Revolution" is an **etic, twentieth-century** coinage (Koyré, Butterfield). Early
modern practitioners called themselves **natural philosophers**, not "scientists" (a nineteenth-
century word), and pursued what they saw as the reform or recovery of natural philosophy, often
within deeply **religious** and even magical/alchemical frameworks (Newton's alchemy and theology;
the Hermetic and Neoplatonic currents). The clean separation of "science" from religion and magic
is a retrospective projection.

## Limitations and Critiques

- **"Revolution" overstated**: historians (Shapin's *The Scientific Revolution* opens "There was no
  such thing as the Scientific Revolution, and this is a book about it") stress **continuity** with
  medieval and Renaissance natural philosophy, the slow and uneven uptake of the new ideas, and the
  survival of magic, alchemy, and astrology alongside them.
- **Eurocentrism and the connected world**: the older story of an autonomous European genius is
  qualified by attention to the **non-European inheritance** (Islamic astronomy and optics, Indian
  and Chinese knowledge) and to how **global exploration, commerce, and empire** supplied data,
  specimens, and instruments (the "global" history of science).
- **Social dimension**: the field now studies science as **social practice** — patronage, courts,
  instruments, trust, and the making of "matter of fact" (Shapin & Schaffer) — rather than as
  disembodied ideas.
- Its relation to the [[industrial-revolution|Industrial Revolution]] and to the
  [[enlightenment|Enlightenment]] (which popularised and extended its authority) is debated.
