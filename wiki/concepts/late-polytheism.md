# Late Polytheism

The religious world of the non-Christian Roman empire in the third and early fourth centuries — what is
conventionally (and pejoratively) called **"paganism."** Treated in [[bowman-cah-v12-2005|CAH XII]] Ch. 17
(in three parts: **17a The World-View**, **17b The Individual and the Gods**, **17c The Cult of the Gods**),
the first part by **Garth Fowden**. Fowden's framing is itself a historiographical intervention.

## "Paganism" as a Christian invention

Fowden's opening argument: **"'Paganism' was first invented towards the end of its existence,"** during the
very period of this volume. Previously there was simply "religion" or "piety" — the cult of the many gods of
many peoples — and the *monotheists* (Jews, then Christians) were "the aberrations." Only as Christianity's
fortunes rose did it need to define "the other," coining ***paganus*** (rustic/civilian, "not one of ours")
in the Latin west from the mid-fourth century, while in the Greek east adherents became **"Hellenes."** The
single, undifferentiated category "paganism" was a polemical convenience — it could be "credited with
whatever infamy" any cult had ever shown. Fowden therefore prefers **"polytheism,"** while conceding it too
is "a clumsy and implicitly monotheist category."

## Polytheism as a religion of the *longue durée*

Unlike Christianity or Islam, polytheism had **no founding moment, no dogma, no revelation** — "it
accumulated out of people's experience of life and nature," comprehensible only *en la plus longue durée*.
It was so diverse across ethnic traditions "that it is tendentious to regard it as a single religion at
all." Crucially, Fowden argues the **henotheist and even monotheist tendencies** within it (any god with a
large clientele could claim sovereignty by *assimilating* others — Jupiter Dolichenus taking Isis, Serapis,
Mithras under his wing) were **"additives, a superstructure that demoted but never denied the many gods."**
Even Plotinus' exalted One was shown "in the multitude of the gods": **"there was no such thing as 'pagan
monotheism.'"** Divinity had become "heavily impregnated with a somewhat impersonal notion of 'power'"
(*dynamis*) — gods as energies radiating from sun, planets and stars, bound to all creation by a network of
**universal "sympathy."**

## The philosophers and the holy man

Fowden's central figures are the **Platonist philosophers**, in whom holiness and learning were fused:

- **Plotinus** (studied under Ammonius Saccas at Alexandria from 232; taught at Rome 244–270; the *Enneads*
  edited by **Porphyry**): the sage treads "the overwhelmingly greater part of the road to the divine by his
  own unaided effort," aspiring to self-forgetting union with the **transcendent One** (achieved four times
  in Porphyry's presence). He was revered as a **"holy man,"** a focus of holiness — the figure "more
  characteristic of late polytheism than of any other period since the pre-Socratics."
- **Theurgy and Hermetism**: where **Porphyry** doubted ritual could touch the intellectual soul,
  **Iamblichus** (d. c. 320–5, at Apamea) made theurgy — "the perfective operation of ineffable acts" and
  "unutterable symbols" — central, depending on a "glittering celestial hierarchy" of gods, angels and
  daemons. The **Hermetica** (attributed to Hermes Trismegistus = the Egyptian Thoth) and the **Chaldaean
  Oracles** likewise taught the soul's purification through ritual *and* intellection.
- **Magic and astrology**: Fowden insists there is "no absolute distinction between philosophy and magic"
  any more than between religion and magic. Astrology (Septimius Severus and Julia Domna's palace ceilings
  painted with the imperial horoscope), curse-tablets, amulets, oracles and the **magical papyri** (the
  "Thebes cache") all rested on the same cosmology of universal sympathy — occult techniques valid
  regardless of the practitioner ("It is not the science that is to be blamed, but... the man").

## The contest with Christianity

The structural weakness Fowden identifies: polytheism **separated conventional cult from ethics and
spirituality** — virtue and the spiritual life were "primarily the domain of the philosophers," who were
often indifferent to public cult, while the ordinary worshipper sought not to *imitate* the gods but to
adjust to their power. Against the **regular communal instruction** the Christian received from his bishop,
the polytheist had only the street-corner Cynic or a florilegium. Some (Iamblichus) tried to make polytheism
"more coherent and better able to resist Christian attack," but theurgy and Hermetism "remained the
preoccupation of an élite." In the philosophers' "distaste for the social and even political involvement"
that Plato had recommended, Fowden sees "one of the reasons why public polytheism seemed at the last to
lack the will to adjust, to fight and to live."

## Significance

Fowden's chapter is both a description and a **methodological corrective**: it strips away the Christian
polemical category "paganism," recovers polytheism as a non-dogmatic, infinitely diverse religion of the
*longue durée* with real henotheist depth, and locates its intellectual vitality (Neoplatonism, theurgy,
Hermetism, the occult sciences — all of which "deeply influenced both Christian and Muslim civilization")
precisely where older scholarship saw only decadence. Yet it also explains the *structural* disadvantage
that, more than any "crisis"-driven anxiety, left public polytheism unable to compete with the organized,
ethical, communal church.
