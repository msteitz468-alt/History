## Definition and Scope

The myth/history debate concerns how far native South American — especially Andean and Amazonian — testimony about the past constitutes historical record in a recognizable sense, versus a structural or cosmological encoding that uses a past idiom but is not primarily about temporal sequence, cause, and contingency. The debate has practical consequences for the use of oral tradition and native chronicle in writing pre-Columbian and early-colonial history, and theoretical consequences for understanding whether "historicity" is universal or culturally specific.

The concept "myth" in this debate was typically taken to mean "a class of narratives that explain the categorical makeup of the world as it is by referring to a time when the laws of nature did not operate as they do now and categories were labile." The concept "history" was taken to mean discourse that "gives greater weighting to agency and social action in the present, which is informed by knowledge of past times that are qualitatively the same as the present" (Hill, in *Rethinking History and Myth*, 1988). The debate was whether these were genuinely distinct phenomena, or whether the antithesis itself was ethnocentric.

## Main Positions

### 1. Positivist Chronologism (Rowe and predecessors)
John H. Rowe's 1945 "Absolute Chronology in the Andean Area" treated Inka dynastic lore as preserved in the chronicles as essentially chronicle-historical in the European sense — a king-list with datable reigns. "Until the 1950s, scholarly readings of 'Inka history' treated it in parallel to Mediterranean ancient history." This approach could produce concrete results (the conventional sequence of twelve Sapa Inka rulers) but required trusting sources whose modes of transmission were poorly understood.

### 2. Lévi-Straussian Structuralism
Claude Lévi-Strauss's *The Savage Mind* (1962) "persuaded many that Amazonian narratives, irrespective of narrative tense, used the past only as an idiom and were meaningful rather as repositories of logical relationships among elementary categories. Like the steps in solving an algebraic equation, structuralists suggested, narratives are sequential in form but express a total set of relationships that is simultaneous or timeless." On this view, Amazonian societies were "cold" to history — not because they lacked intelligence, but because their meaning-structures were organized by categorical logic rather than temporal sequence.

The North American baseline shared this skepticism for different reasons. Steward and Faron's 1959 summary of the *Handbook of South American Indians* declared that Amazonian peoples "lacked a sense of history and took no interest in genealogies. Even those societies whose principal religious activity centered in a cult of the dead were concerned with ancestors in a general sense and not as identifiable individuals. There is no group whose oral history extended back more than one hundred years."

### 3. Zuidema's Andean Structuralism
R. T. Zuidema applied structuralism specifically to Inka dynastic tradition. His argument: "a roughly standardized list of Inka names, which the Spanish understood as a royal succession list," was not a chronological series at all. It was "a finite set of titles in fixed hierarchical relation, each of which was occupied at successive times by different individuals." The deeds attributed to each name were not unique historical events but archetypes associated with structural positions. "'History' was an elaborate temporal metaphor for the synchronic array of rank in the Inka capital of Cusco." Zuidema's conclusion: "I would consider the whole of Inka history up to the time of the Spanish conquest...as mythological...An historical chronology up to the Spanish conquest will have to be established independently by archaeology."

Structuralism also implied that modern Andean village structures preserved pre-Inka "basics" in essentially unchanged form (*lo andino*), an interpretation later attacked as reinstating the old indigenist fallacy of "unchanging tradition."

### 4. Heirs of Structuralism: Ritual as History-Making
From c.1980 onward, critiques from Marxists and non-Marxist historicists — accelerated by the Shining Path insurgency convulsing Ayacucho, exactly the zone where Andean structuralism had been forged — "cast a deep shadow over any...rendering" of unchanging Andean tradition. The second generation sought to reframe structure as intelligible change. "Ritual came to be reinterpreted as history-making, not history-defying, action."

Gary Urton's *The History of a Myth* (1990) showed that the reason Pacariqtambo is today considered the Inka origin shrine was traceable to a specific legal hearing in 1569 at which an Andean noble "concretized" a preexisting myth in local geographic terms — a datable act of mythopolitics. The ceque system of Cusco, previously read as a purely ritual schema, could be partly understood through "practical projects — historical facts in the usual sense — such as the administration of Cusco's irrigation infrastructure."

### 5. The 1988 Synthesis: Myth and History as Complementary
The volume *Rethinking History and Myth* (Hill, ed., 1988) articulated the emerging consensus: "regardless of genre variation and the binding of memory into idealized social patterns, changing contingency elicits and informs every performance of tradition. None is in principle ahistoric." The myth/history antithesis was rejected as an "ethnocentric" analytical artifact — not a distinction between two types of real phenomenon. Instead, myth and history were redefined as complementary modes: myth "gives priority to structure" and "guarantees that relations of contrast and difference of major social importance will not be forgotten," while history "gives greater weighting to agency and social action." The two "inform each other reciprocally." "In nonliterate societies, the telling of the past is radically elliptical; history is understood in relation to a few crucial moments of rapid change, with little attention to the idea of constantly chronicled time."

### 6. Tupinamba Cannibalism as Structuring of History
Manuela Carneiro da Cunha and Eduardo Viveiros de Castro's work on the [[tupinamba]] (published 1985) showed that a non-state Amazonian society had a sophisticated mode of historical consciousness organized not around king-lists or chronology but around the logic of reciprocal vengeance. The [[tupinamba]] "ordered action over time through the 'eternal perpetuation' of combat." Their society lacked lineages, hereditary ranks, and prescriptive marriage rules, and instead built temporal continuity through the capture, incorporation, ritual killing, and eating of enemies. "It is vengeance which alone connects those who have lived (and died) and those who will live (to die)....The memory of each group, its very future is achieved through its enemies." This was not "ahistoric" — it was a different, robust mode of historical consciousness.

## The Chimor and Inka King-List Problem

The debate bears directly on concrete historical questions. The king-lists of both [[chimor]] (Chan Chan's ruler sequence) and the [[inca-empire]] (the twelve-Sapa-Inka sequence) are the backbone of Andean pre-Columbian chronology. If Zuidema is right that the Inka sequence represents titles and structural positions rather than sequential individuals, the conventional chronology collapses. If Rowe is right, it provides a framework that can be calibrated against archaeology and astronomical data. Current scholarship sits between these poles: most scholars treat the later Inka rulers (from Pachacuti onward, c.1438) as historical individuals while remaining agnostic or skeptical about earlier figures.

The problem is compounded by the fact that different Inka kin-corporations (*panakas*) held different and irreconcilable dynastic traditions: "historical truth is felt to be relative to, and the property of, the group 'owning' a tradition." What looks like a contradiction in the record may reflect the normal functioning of Andean historical epistemology rather than error.

## Broader Significance

The debate shaped the fundamental methodological question for the volume [[cambridge-history-native-peoples-americas-v3-1]] and indeed for all South American historical scholarship: "How different and how separate are mythic and historical 'past-discourses'?" Salomon's assessment (1999): "The main casualty of debate was not the idea of 'Indian history.' It was the myth/history antithesis itself." Researchers must now "take local categories of genre and the internal properties of narratives — not imported dichotomies like myth/history — as the points of departure."

The debate also connects to the broader "literacy thesis" (Goody/Ong): the claim that historical consciousness in the Western sense requires writing. The South American evidence — rich oral traditions showing genuine historical concern, complex structural memory, and politically motivated mythohistory — constitutes one of the strongest challenges to this thesis.

## Resolution Status

Open. The framing of the debate as myth *versus* history has been substantially dissolved, but its underlying empirical questions — can Inka king-lists be used for chronology? How far do Amazonian oral traditions encode historical events? — remain genuinely contested and dependent on further archaeological and linguistic work.
