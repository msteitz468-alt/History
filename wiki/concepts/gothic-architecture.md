# Gothic Architecture

## Definition

**Gothic** is the architecture of the pointed arch, rib vault, and flying buttress that
emerged in the Île-de-France around the middle of the twelfth century and became the
dominant style of later medieval northern Europe. [[luscombe-ncmh-v4-2004|NCMH IV]]
(Peter Kidson, Ch. 19) places its birth at the close of this volume's period: "the
twelfth is the century when the Gothic style began to flourish in the north," and the
"transition from rounded to pointed forms is the most noticeable feature of the turn
from [[romanesque|Romanesque]] to Gothic."

## Character and Origin

Gothic combined three elements — the **pointed arch**, the **rib vault**, and the
**flying buttress** — to channel structural loads more efficiently than the massive
walls of the Romanesque, permitting taller, lighter buildings pierced by large windows
filled with stained glass. The conventional birthplace is the choir of the abbey church
of **Saint-Denis**, rebuilt under Abbot **Suger** from c. 1140, followed by the great
cathedrals of the Île-de-France and its environs — **Chartres, Paris (Notre-Dame),
Laon, Amiens, Reims** — whose construction NCMH IV's plates illustrate. (The name
"Gothic" is an anachronistic, originally contemptuous Renaissance coinage; medieval
builders called it simply the "modern" or "French" work, *opus francigenum*.)

## Significance

Gothic is the architectural signature of the thirteenth-century apogee of medieval
Europe; its birth in royal-Capetian France in the 1140s coincides with, and visually
expresses, the rise of the [[capetian-dynasty|Capetian monarchy]] and the wealth of the
[[commercial-revolution|towns]] and the Church. NCMH IV treats it as just emerging here;
its full flowering belongs to [[abulafia-ncmh-v5-2000|NCMH V]] and the
[[high-middle-ages|thirteenth century]].

## The Thirteenth-Century Apogee and the Shift of Patronage

[[abulafia-ncmh-v5-2000|NCMH V]] (Paul Binski, Ch. 1e) treats the thirteenth century
as the period in which Gothic was **refined and diffused** across north-west Europe — and
in which the locus of creative initiative shifted decisively. Where twelfth-century
patronage had run through **reformed monasticism** (Suger's Saint-Denis), by 1200 the
driving patrons were increasingly the **cathedral chapters, the cities, the lay
aristocracy, and above all the royal court** — Binski reads the developed Gothic style as
in this sense both **royal and urban**, even as episcopal patronage stayed central. The
Gothic cathedral becomes "a sign of the triumphalist mood of a newly militant
universalising Church."

- **The "classic" phase.** A small group of cathedrals — Chartres (begun 1194), Reims
  (1211), Amiens (1220) — established the heroic high-Gothic of unprecedented interior
  height, achieved by the mature rib vault, flying buttress, and expanded window. This
  phase reached its **nemesis with the collapse of the vaults of Beauvais** (1284), "the
  greatest interior space conceived in western Europe since the Pantheon."
- **Paris as engine.** By 1200 Paris had become arguably the most dynamic artistic centre
  in northern Europe, precisely while older centres — **Rome and Constantinople** —
  stagnated; Binski cautions that administrative power and cultural flourishing did not
  coincide (Rome's art patronage collapsed until the century's last decades).
- **Rayonnant and regional diversification.** After c. 1230, with the key period of
  structural experiment over and the economy slowing, emphasis shifted from sheer scale
  to **refinement** — the Parisian **Rayonnant** style of extreme thinness (the
  Sainte-Chapelle) — and to a proliferation of regional Gothics: the richly finished
  English cathedrals (Lincoln; the Decorated Style from c. 1290), German *opus
  Francigenum* (Cologne), the austere **Cistercian** variant that had earlier carried the
  style into Germany, Spain, and Italy, and the influential **Mendicant** church type.
- **Standardization.** Alongside this diversity ran growing uniformity: the integration
  of cathedral functions under one roof, more uniform liturgical practice (the Use of
  Sarum), and the centralization of canonization at Rome.

**Historiographical note.** Binski registers the modern debate over **Chartres** as a
"site of tension": once read (Mâle, Panofsky, von Simson) as the serene canonical
embodiment of scholastic or Neoplatonic order, it has been "debunked" as a brilliant but
incoherent building raised by anonymous contractors, and re-read through neo-Marxist and
ideological lenses as a vehicle of clerical social control. The revisionist turn toward
**regional and non-French Gothic** (Bourges's alternative design tradition; the still
under-studied Spanish Gothic) makes "a single Gothic idiom representing an 'essential'
thirteenth century" now seem "both improbable and unnecessary."

## The Fourteenth-Century Diversification (NCMH VI)

[[jones-ncmh-v6-2000|NCMH VI]] (Paul Binski, Ch. 8; and the architecture chapter) carries Gothic
into the fourteenth century, whose "hallmark... is not internationalism... but rather extraordinary
diversity." New **urban and court patronage** (Cologne, Prague, Siena, Bruges, and the ephemeral
brilliance of papal **Avignon**) multiplied regional idioms, while the **Italian painters** (Giotto,
Simone Martini, the Lorenzetti) created a new pictorial naturalism. In architecture the century
produced its showiest regional manners — the English **Decorated Style** (summarised at Ely) and
then the **Perpendicular**, and the spacious **mendicant** churches of southern France (Toulouse)
whose influence spread into Germany. The courtly synthesis of these strands is the
**[[international-gothic|International Gothic]]** of c. 1370–1430. The episode confirms the
revisionist stress on **regional diversity** over a single Gothic idiom.
