# Escalation Dominance

**Escalation dominance** is the condition in which one side possesses the ability to escalate a conflict to a higher level of violence while denying the opponent any attractive response at that level. If one side can credibly threaten to move up the escalation ladder (from conventional to tactical nuclear to strategic nuclear, or from limited strategic strikes to larger ones) in a way that leaves the other side worse off, it is said to hold escalation dominance.

## Role in Nuclear Strategy

The concept was central to efforts to restore credibility to nuclear threats after massive retaliation lost plausibility. If the West could dominate the escalation process at every rung — or at least convince the opponent that it might irrationally or unpredictably do so — then even a conventional Soviet attack in Europe could be deterred by the risk of uncontrolled escalation.

Schelling's "threat that leaves something to chance" and Kahn's detailed escalation ladders both fed into this line of thinking. The idea influenced NATO's flexible response doctrine and later US selective options.

## Kahn's Formulation (Grounded in Freedman 1981 Range 05)

Herman Kahn’s main contribution was in *On Escalation*, which described an "escalation ladder of 44 rungs, in which nuclear weapons were first used at rung 15." To many the fact that anybody can find some 30 different ways to use nuclear weapons "was quite perverse (though not every rung above number 15 does involve nuclear weapons)."

More important than the individual rungs were six "fire-breaks" or "thresholds" at which very sharp changes in the character of the escalation took place. These were: "(1) ‘don’t rock the boat; (2) nuclear war is unthinkable; (3) no nuclear use; (4) central sanctuary; (5) central war; and (6) city targeting’." The fire-breaks reflected "the importance of saliencies in thoughts as well as deed."

Kahn provided a Soviet escalation ladder in which conventional options loomed as large as nuclear options do in the Western ladder. Different strengths made escalation easier. "The stronger may ... feel that once it has shattered its opponent’s nuclear incredulity, the escalation dominance available to it may be so much strengthened that it will have its own way relatively easily."

## Late 1970s Application: ICBM Vulnerability and Selective Options (Range 08)

By the late 1970s the scenario that dominated debate was a Soviet surprise ICBM attack on US Minuteman silos that would eliminate that leg of the triad while leaving the attacker with a large reserve force. Colin Gray articulated the resulting problem of escalation dominance: "On current trends in the strategic balance, an American President should, prudently, be deterred from initiating strategic nuclear employment; should he proceed nonetheless, the war would very likely terminate after an almost wholly counter-military exchange (which the Soviet Union should win unequivocally) because the United States could not possibly secure an improved war outcome by initiating attacks against Soviet industry and (through relocation) population." (Freedman 1981, ICBM VULNERABILITY, p.389, citing Gray).

Only ICBMs offered realistic hard-target counter-force options against enemy ICBMs. Their loss would leave the President with "one option: to annihilate Russian cities, ‘knowing full well, however, that the Russians could strike back at our populated centers far more effectively’" (Senator Jackson). The burden of initiating (or escalating to) city attacks would fall on the US, handing the USSR escalation dominance. (Freedman 1981, p.388-389).

Schlesinger's doctrine sought to restore options precisely to address such asymmetries and to provide limited pre-planned responses tied to European contingencies. Schlesinger quoted on the implications of Soviet counter-force growth: "could bring into question our ability to respond to attacks in a controlled, selective, and deliberate fashion. It could also give the Soviets a capability that we ourselves would lack... Worst of all, it could arouse precisely the fears and suspicions that our arms control efforts are designed to dispel." (Freedman 1981, p.392).

The concept also shaped debates over theatre nuclear modernization in Europe. Renewed emphasis on longer-range systems for "rear-echelon" attacks and the 1979 decision to deploy 572 US medium-range missiles was partly an attempt to restore a regional escalation balance without decoupling from the central US deterrent. (Freedman 1981, SELECTIVE OPTIONS / Options for a European War).

## Limits and Critiques

Freedman notes that Soviet doctrine showed little interest in the fine-grained limitation and intra-war bargaining presupposed by Western escalation-dominance thinking. Soviet pronouncements on the Schlesinger doctrine were "unremittingly hostile". Colonel A. A. Sidorenko stressed that nuclear weapons would become "the main means for destroying the enemy in battle" upon first employment. (Freedman 1981, p.381).

By the book's Conclusion, Freedman observes that the search for escalation dominance and selective options ultimately moved strategic thought toward "second- and third-order issues" while the fundamental problem of uncontrollable escalation remained. Stability rested more on the fear that "things will get out of hand" than on any demonstrated ability to dominate or control escalation ladders.
