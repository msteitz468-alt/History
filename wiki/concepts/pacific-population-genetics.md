## Definition and Scope

Pacific population genetics is the body of molecular and genomic methods applied to reconstructing the settlement history, population movements, and biological relationships of [[oceania|Pacific]] peoples. It encompasses four successive methodological generations: (1) serological (blood-group) studies; (2) uniparental markers — mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) tracking maternal lines and Y-chromosome analysis tracking paternal lines; (3) genome-wide single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) and short tandem repeat (STR) surveys; and (4) ancient DNA (aDNA) extraction from archaeological skeletal remains. The field also includes the *commensal approach*, using the genetics of transported animals and plants as proxies for human mobility.

Source: [[jones-matsuda-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v1-2022]], ch. 10 (Matisoo-Smith).

## Historical Development

### Early Racial Typology (Pre-1960)

European descriptions of Pacific peoples, beginning with Magellan (1520) and Medaña (1590s), first classified islanders by phenotype — skin colour, body size — and embedded those descriptions in hierarchical racial schemes. Blumenbach (1775, 1781) placed Pacific peoples within a "Malay" category; Dumont d'Urville (1832) gave the tripartite Melanesia/Polynesia/Micronesia division, encoding the prejudice that Polynesians and Micronesians were "physically, mentally, and culturally superior to the 'black races' of Melanesia" — a framework that structured biological research for over a century.

The Yale Bayard Dominick Expeditions (from c. 1920) collected anthropometric data across Polynesia to determine "the physical characters and of the racial affinities of Polynesians." Osteological analyses consistently noted "the relative homogeneity of Polynesians, their overall robusticity, and the fact that, while they tend to cluster with Micronesian populations in analyses, they are clearly phenotypically different from populations of the western Pacific."

### Blood-Group Studies (1919–1980s)

The first blood-genetic study of Pacific peoples (Armstrong and Matheson, 1924) analysed ABO variation in Samoa. Fifty years of blood-group surveys produced an awkward result: "The general lack of blood group B in Polynesia, but high levels of blood group A was at odds with the ABO data for the rest of the Pacific," and as late as 1962 Simmons wrote, "If the comparisons are valid, then American Indians and Polynesians shared a common gene pool, more so than Polynesians and other races to the west and northwest." Thor Heyerdahl's Kon-Tiki hypothesis (1952) thus could not be definitively refuted by genetics alone in that era.

The 1985 discovery of an alpha-thalassaemia mutation shared between Polynesians and Melanesians provided "the first unequivocal evidence of genetic connections of Polynesians to populations in the western Pacific." Because malaria has never been present east of Vanuatu, "for Polynesian populations to have frequencies above 10 per cent for haemoglobinopathy (abnormal haemoglobin) means that they shared recent ancestry with populations who had lived in an area where malaria was endemic" — connecting [[austronesian-expansion|Austronesian expansion]] firmly to the west.

### mtDNA Studies (1987–2008)

The 1987 "Mitochondrial Eve" paper (Cann, Stoneking, Wilson) established mtDNA as a population-history tool. By 1989 the "9 base-pair deletion" defining mtDNA haplogroup B was found at high frequency in Polynesians, identifying an East Asian origin. Additional mutations accompanying this deletion were labeled the **Polynesian motif** (now formally haplogroup B4a1a1). The motif's frequency increases eastward across the Pacific to near-fixation in East Polynesians and was taken as a marker of the [[austronesian-expansion|Austronesian/Lapita spread]].

The ancient Near Oceanic haplogroups P, Q, M27/M28/M29 — "their origins dating to between 40,000 and 60,000 years BP" — correspond with the deep archaeological record of [[near-and-remote-oceania|Near Oceania]] (earliest New Guinea highlands ~50,000 BP; northern Australia ~65,000 BP). The frequency of Near Oceanic lineages decreases steadily eastward, reaching near-zero in East Polynesia.

The mtDNA pattern was widely read as supporting the "Express Train to Polynesia" model: a rapid, low-admixture Austronesian expansion from Taiwan through Near Oceania to Remote Oceania. This reading proved premature when Y-chromosome data were added.

### Y-Chromosome Studies (1990s)

Y-chromosome haplogroups K, M, S, and C are largely of Near Oceanic or Wallacean origin; haplogroup O derives from Southeast Asia. Critically, "where the mtDNA of populations in Remote Oceania indicated primarily Asian-derived maternal lineages, the Y chromosomes of the region showed primarily Near Oceanic lineages with a smaller contribution of the Asian derived Hg O lineages." This asymmetry — more Asian ancestry through the maternal line — "could be the result of the Pacific tradition of matrilineal and matrilocal societies," and implies substantial admixture within Near Oceania, supporting a "Slow Boat" rather than an Express Train model.

A "Polynesian motif" Y-chromosome equivalent — haplogroup C2a1 — is found exclusively in Polynesian populations.

### Genome-Wide SNP Studies (2008–present)

Genome-wide SNP analyses confirmed gradual increase of Southeast Asian-derived markers from eastern Indonesia through New Guinea into Remote Oceania, beginning ~4,000 BP. In West Polynesian populations, approximately 87 per cent of ancestry appears Asian-derived, with significant sex bias: "significantly more Asian-derived mutations seen on the X chromosome than across the rest of the genome, suggesting that there was a larger component of Asian-derived maternal ancestry in the remote Pacific."

A Society Islands (East Polynesian) genome study estimated Asian-derived admixture at ~70 per cent, tracing the most likely source to lowland Philippine populations. The estimated admixture date with "northern Melanesian" populations was **1700–1200 BP** — substantially later than the Lapita migration itself (~3,300–2,800 BP). The closest source for the Society Island samples was the Polynesian outlier populations in the northern Solomon Islands, which also fits recent linguistic reassessments.

### Ancient DNA and Population Turnover

Next-generation sequencing applied to the Teouma Lapita cemetery (Efate, Vanuatu) in 2016 recovered genome-wide data from four Lapita-associated female skulls. All four showed "little to no 'Papuan' ancestry, but clustered most closely to East Asian populations" and possessed mtDNA haplogroup B4a1a1. This challenged the Slow Boat and Triple-I models and implied the first inhabitants of Remote Oceania were genetically unmixed Asians.

Two 2018 follow-on papers proposed "near complete 'population turnover'" in Vanuatu within a few hundred years of initial Lapita occupation: the original Asian Lapita population was "nearly totally replaced by peoples from Near Oceania, specifically from New Britain" — yet the newcomers continued speaking Austronesian languages. This decoupling of language from biological ancestry is among the most contested findings in the field.

Matisoo-Smith cautions that the Teouma skulls are ceremonially secondary, all female, and too few to represent the full founding population: "the osteological analyses of the post-cranial remains from the cemetery indicated that there was a significant level of phenotypic variation, particularly between male and female burials."

## Models of Lapita Origins

Four main models have structured debate; they are distinguished by the degree of genetic admixture proposed during the Bismarck Archipelago phase (~3,800–3,300 BP):

| Model | Admixture? | Key Proponents | Genetic Implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| Express Train | None or minimal | Bellwood, Diamond | Lapita = phenotypically "Asian"; Near Oceanic component entered later |
| Indigenous Origins | None (Lapita formed locally) | Clark, Terrell | Lapita = local Near Oceanian; phenotypic heterogeneity from bottlenecks |
| Slow Boat | Significant | Kayser et al. | Lapita colonists admixed in Near Oceania before moving east; "Melanesian"-looking |
| Triple-I (Intrusion–Integration–Innovation) | Significant | Roger Green | New people from SE Asia integrated with Near Oceanic populations, then innovated |

The 2016–2018 aDNA results from Teouma revived the Express Train in modified form but remain contested due to sample size and the female-only skull assemblage.

## The Commensal Approach

Developed by Matisoo-Smith from 1994 in response to political pushback from Indigenous communities against human DNA research (following the 1993 Aotearoa conference on Indigenous intellectual property rights), the commensal approach uses genetic analysis of species transported by Pacific colonists — principally:

- **Pacific rat (*Rattus exulans*)**: chosen because, unlike dogs/pigs/chickens, it belongs to a different species from post-European introductions and has been shown to be the direct descendant of founding-canoe rats. Bones appear in the earliest archaeological sites across Remote Oceania.
- **Sweet potato (*Ipomoea batatas*)**, **bottle gourd (*Lagenaria siceraria*)**, **paper mulberry (*Broussonetia papyrifera*)**.

The first rat mtDNA study identified two "interaction spheres" in the Polynesian triangle: one linking New Zealand (sourced from Cook Islands and Society Islands via the Kermadecs) and one linking the Society, Marquesas, and Hawaiian islands — "fully consistent with linguistic and cultural evidence of contacts within Polynesia." Commensal genetics now demonstrate "evidence of return voyaging from Polynesia to the Americas" via DNA of both plants and animals.

The commensal approach "opened doors to Pacific communities and provided an opportunity to discuss genetic research and to demonstrate both the values and the many issues involved in a less threatening context."

## Health, Diet, and Isotope Studies

Analysis of Teouma skeletal remains using isotopes (dietary reconstruction) and dental calculus (plant remains) indicates that earliest Lapita peoples "were eating more native in-shore marine and reef species and terrestrial plant resources than later Lapita and post-Lapita populations, who had a greater dependence on carbohydrates and domesticated animals." Sex-differentiated diets are also attested: "males apparently having a more varied diet and greater access to higher trophic-level protein resources." Skeletal lesions indicate scurvy, gout, and other metabolic conditions.

The Wairau Bar site (Aotearoa New Zealand, ~1300 CE) — the last major landmass settled by humans — has been analysed via aDNA, isotopes, and osteology of over forty burials, supporting "a mass migration and strategic settlement model for Aotearoa."

## Contested Issues and Limits

1. **Female-only aDNA assemblage at Teouma**: results may not represent total founding-population diversity.
2. **Ascertainment bias**: genome-wide SNP datasets designed primarily for other populations may under-represent Pacific-specific variation.
3. **Decoupling of language, biology, and culture**: "it is increasingly clear that language, biology, and culture do not necessarily travel hand in hand."
4. **Population turnover vs. continuity**: the claim of near-total replacement within centuries remains debated; critics note the limited sample and unusual secondary burial context.
5. **Ethics and Indigenous data sovereignty**: much of the field's methodological innovation has been driven by community resistance to extractive research; the commensal approach itself was a response to political constraints.

## Historiography

Biological anthropology in the Pacific has been "at the forefront of both theory and approaches to understanding human history and human variation" while simultaneously reproducing the racialized assumptions of its era. Nineteenth-century phenotypic classifications encoded by d'Urville in 1832 structured blood-group research until the 1980s. The "Polynesian motif" label — applied to haplogroup B4a1a1 — is itself identified as a misnomer and "quite offensive to many of the Indigenous peoples in the region who may carry this genetic marker but do not consider themselves to be Polynesian."

The field has increasingly moved toward community collaboration: "true collaborative and community-engaged research is the only way that anthropological and health research involving Indigenous communities should happen in the future."
