# Merovingian Bioarchaeology

**Reading status, diet, mobility, and identity directly from Merovingian bones and teeth.** Andrea
Czermak's chapter (Oxford Handbook, Ch. 7) surveys how skeletal science — stable-isotope analysis,
strontium provenancing, and ancient DNA — now lets the human remains in furnished cemeteries
*answer* questions that grave goods and texts cannot. It is the natural scientific complement to the
post-1980 turn of [[merovingian-archaeology]] away from ethnic grave-typology.

## Methods

- **Bone vs. teeth.** Bone collagen records "an isotope blend of dietary intake of roughly ten years
  preceding death"; teeth "have almost no material turnover and do not remodel during lifetime" — "a
  lifelong storage for isotopes" capturing childhood. Czermak's **serial microsampling** of tooth
  dentine yields an age-mapped dietary chronology for the first ~20 years of life.
- **Carbon (δ¹³C)** distinguishes C3 plants (wheat, most European grain) from C4 plants (millet,
  "the only relevant C4 plant" in temperate Europe — see [[farming-and-diet-in-merovingian-gaul]]).
- **Nitrogen (δ¹⁵N)** indexes trophic level: higher values mean more animal protein.
- **Strontium (⁸⁷Sr/⁸⁶Sr)** fixes geographic origin: values outside the local range flag
  non-locals.

## Case Study: Niedernai (Alsace, Upper Rhine)

A small cemetery used for about **two generations** (mid-5th to early 6th century) on the former
Roman frontier — its earliest dead "likely born in the last decades of the Roman Empire," its last
witnessing the start of Merovingian rule. Of 21 analysed individuals, **7 of 17** strontium results
fell outside the local range, marking non-locals; two women had millet-rich (C4) childhood diets
pointing to origins in southern/eastern Europe. Crucially, the non-locals "had the same isotope
composition at the end of their lives as those identified as locals" — **newcomers were fully
integrated** by death. Czermak reads this as evidence that "definitions of kinship and social
identity may have been flexible": "people sharing their lives, their food, and their final resting
place might have also shared their identity."

This bears directly on the migration-vs-cultural-realignment debate: Brather and Fehr argue that
furnished Alsatian burials reflect "a cultural realignment of the societies in the former Roman
frontier area," not direct barbarian migration — consistent with the [[ethnogenesis]] model.

## Status, Diet, and Identity

Childhood diet was **sex-differentiated**: young males consistently had more animal protein than
young females, perhaps reflecting "a special appreciation for women delivering sons." Because
"access to animal protein might have been determined by gender or status," diet itself functioned
as a social marker, and immigrants "might change their diet habits to that of the indigenous
population in order to assimilate." Diet thus becomes a second register of identity alongside the
grave-good register studied in [[gender-in-merovingian-gaul]] and [[merovingian-childhood]].

## Historiography

Czermak notes that DNA and isotope techniques, "still costly," are used more rarely on early
medieval remains in France than in England and Germany — a methodological gap also flagged for
[[merovingian-archaeology]]. The chapter's significance is to convert ethnicity from a label read
off artifacts into a hypothesis testable against the body, generally supporting the constructivist
(ethnogenesis) reading of Merovingian identity over the older migrationist one.
