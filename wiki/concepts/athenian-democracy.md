# Athenian Democracy

## Definition

**Athenian democracy** was the system of **direct, participatory self-government** by the body
of adult male citizens (the *dēmos*) that reached its classical form in the mid-fifth century
BCE. [[lewis-cah-v5-1992|CAH Vol. V]] (Ch. 4, P. J. Rhodes) describes it as government **by,
and for the benefit of, the dēmos**, in which "all citizens were involved together" in
deciding and "all could be involved in turn" in executing. By Aristotle's later typology its
combination of pay for office and sovereign popular power made it an **"extreme" democracy**.

## The institutions

- **The Assembly (*ekklesia*)** — the sovereign body, in theory all adult male citizens; it
  enacted laws, levied taxes, made war and peace, and elected military officers. Its one major
  restraint was *probouleusis*: it could take positive decisions only on matters prepared by
  the Council.
- **The Council of Five Hundred (*boule*)** — chosen by lot, 50 from each of Cleisthenes' ten
  tribes; it prepared the Assembly's business and supervised the administration. Its standing
  committee, the **prytaneis** (probably instituted by [[ephialtes]]), chaired both bodies, a
  new president chosen by lot each day.
- **The lawcourts (*dikasteria*)** — large popular juries (hundreds to thousands), drawn from
  a panel of **6,000** sworn jurors over thirty, which took over the political jurisdiction
  stripped from the [[areopagus|Areopagus]]. No professional judges, prosecutors (the
  volunteer *ho boulomenos*), or lawyers; litigants pleaded their own cases.
- **The magistracies** — some 700 officials, almost all chosen **by lot**, holding office for
  **one year only** and never twice, deliberately treated as routine posts "any loyal citizen
  could perform." The great exception was the **board of ten generals (*strategoi*)**, who —
  because military command required ability — were **elected and re-electable**, and so became
  the real political leaders (see [[pericles]]).

## Pay and participation

The radical innovation was **state pay for civic duty** — [[pericles|Pericles']] **jury pay**,
then pay for other offices, and (in the 390s) for attendance at the Assembly itself. Pay made
the participatory machine workable for the poor and was the feature Aristotle thought defined
extreme democracy; ancient writers and modern alike linked Athens' ability to afford it to the
revenue of the **[[delian-league|empire]]**, though the city sustained the payments after the
empire was gone. Safeguards against hasty decision (the *graphe paranomon* against illegal
proposals, two-day debates, quorums) were normally respected but could fail at the worst
moments — the Assembly that installed the oligarchy of 411, or the illegal mass trial of the
generals after Arginusae.

## The making of the democracy

Rhodes presents it as the cumulative work of generations, not a single founding:
**Draco** (written law, 621), **Solon** (the ban on debt-bondage, the volunteer prosecutor,
the appellate *heliaia*), **[[cleisthenic-reforms-508bce|Cleisthenes]]** (the territorial
tribes and Council, which gave citizens a "taste for political activity"), the confidence bred
by **[[battle-of-marathon-490bce|Marathon]]** and **[[battle-of-salamis-480bce|Salamis]]** (a
"thetes' revolution" in the fleet), and finally the deliberate work of **[[ephialtes]]** and
**Pericles**. Rhodes stresses that the impulse "came from above": the aristocratic democratic
leaders offered the dēmos control, and "when invited to take control of its own affairs, the
dēmos accepted."

## Limits and significance

The democracy was **radical for its citizens and narrow in its citizenry**: women, resident
foreigners (*metics*), and a large enslaved population had no political rights, and the
poorest citizens (*thetes*) were barred from office. Of perhaps 300,000 people in Attica at
431, only ~45,000 (15%) were adult male citizens. Yet within those limits Athens came, as
Rhodes concludes, "to see political freedom as the right of all, not only of the rich or well
born" — a discovery "whose influence has not been limited to Classical Greece." Athens and
[[sparta]] became the recognized paradigms of **democracy** and **oligarchy**, a polarity the
[[peloponnesian-war]] sharpened into an ideological civil war across the Greek world.

## Radical Democracy: Institutions in Detail (Osborne 2000, Ch. 3, Thomas)

[[sources/osborne-classical-greece-2000|Osborne (ed.), *Classical Greece 500–323 BC*]]
(Ch. 3, Rosalind Thomas) supplies further institutional texture. Athens became a "radical"
democracy in the 460s BC once the judicial powers of the [[areopagus|Areopagus]] were
transferred to the popular courts and Assembly. Direct democracy meant citizens voted in
person rather than through representatives; most officials were chosen by lot, with the
generalship (*strategia*) the key elected, indefinitely-renewable exception. The
Assembly's sovereignty could produce whiplash: in 427 BC it voted to massacre all
Mytilenean men and enslave the women and children, then reconvened the next day and
narrowly reversed the decision after the Cleon–Diodotus debate (Thucydides 3.36–49); in
406 BC, after Arginusae, it illegally tried and condemned to death all eight generals
together for failing to recover the dead (Xenophon, *Hellenica* 1.7) — cited by some
ancients as proof the *demos* should not be prevented from doing what it wants. The Pnyx
could not seat the full citizen body; modern estimates put its capacity at roughly 6,000
in the fifth century, and quorums of 6,000 are attested as the functional equivalent of a
full assembly — only about a third of citizens lived within six miles of the city centre,
skewing habitual attendance toward those nearby.

The Council (*Boulê*) of 500, chosen annually by lot from the ten tribes (fifty per tribe,
apportioned by deme population — the largest deme, Acharnae, sent twenty-two members),
prepared the Assembly's agenda and had executive functions; Council service was paid, and
demographic calculations suggest a very high proportion of qualifying Athenian citizens
must have served on the Council at least once in a lifetime, against a much smaller
(~1,200) class wealthy enough to fund liturgies. Jury courts (*dikastai*, "judges") were
considered the most characteristically Athenian institution, embodying accountability to
the people, with jurors drawn from the panel of 6,000 citizens over thirty and no
presiding professional judges; there was no public prosecutor, so the system depended on
volunteer/semi-professional prosecutors and increasingly on paid professional speechwriters
(*logographoi*, e.g. Antiphon) who wrote speeches litigants then delivered as their own —
a practice defendants often concealed as undemocratic. The whole apparatus institutionalized
deep distrust of individual power accumulation: annual, accountable, sortition-based office
holding, and **ostracism** (exile of a single individual by popular vote for ten years) as a
safeguard against tyranny.

**Sparta by contrast**: Isocrates called Sparta a military camp, and Aristotle termed its
kings "hereditary generals for life." The Spartan Assembly (of the roughly 9,000
*Homoioi*, "Equals") voted on major questions — including the 432/431 declaration of war on
Athens — but there is little evidence citizens could debate or amend from the floor. Sparta
had no popular courts and no assembly accountability over officials; judicial power over
life-and-death penalties rested with a small oligarchic body, illustrated by the acquittal
of Sphodrias (for an unauthorized 378 BC attack on Piraeus) despite his failure to appear,
engineered by King Agesilaus — what Xenophon called "the most unjust verdict given in a
Spartan court." The ephors, elected annually by the whole citizenry, were Sparta's
"democratic" check on royal power but were also accused of being open to bribery, since they
were poor. The Gerousia (28 life-tenured elders, drawn in practice from a narrow set of
elite families) was the purest oligarchic element, unaccountable to the Assembly. Sparta's
real distinctiveness lay less in its formal constitutional structure — which resembled
other poleis more than Athens' did — than in its subjugation of the enslaved Messenian
helot population, which necessitated a highly developed warrior society (see
[[polis|Polis]] for the archaic origins of Messenian helotage and the "long Spartan
stasis").

## The Politics of the Democracy: Cimon to Pericles (Osborne 2000, Ch. 5, Ober)

The two generations after the popular revolution of 508/7 BC ([[cleisthenic-reforms-508bce|
Cleisthenes]]) saw the extension of democratic institutions and the growth of popular
political consciousness. Cimon's policy of alliance with oligarchic Sparta collapsed after
Sparta rebuffed Athenian aid against the helot revolt at Messenia, damaging his political
standing. In 462/1 BC **Ephialtes** persuaded the Assembly to strip the Areopagus of its
powers of constitutional oversight and increase the power of the popular courts; he was
subsequently murdered. **Pericles**, rising after Ephialtes, publicly rejected old-style
aristocratic backroom politics organized around small networks of trusted friends
(*hetaireiai*, political clubs), building instead a loyal mass constituency through
Assembly speechmaking. An opposing bloc of wealthy anti-democratic citizens was organized
in the Assembly by **Thucydides son of Melesias** — a distinct historical figure from the
historian Thucydides of Oloros, though probably a relative — who was ostracized in 443 BC;
after his ostracism the oligarchic movement went underground, and some political clubs
became centres of revolutionary agitation. Pericles was reelected as general annually from
443 BC until his death in 429 BC; Thucydides the historian describes his ascendancy as
"in fact rule by the first citizen" (2.65).

The anonymous **"Old Oligarch"** (a critical pamphlet of the 430s–420s) argued from an
explicitly anti-democratic viewpoint that the self-interested rule of the Athenian *demos*
nonetheless made Athens strong — an early instance of the *physis*/*nomos* debates over
democracy's legitimacy explored further at [[polis|Polis]] (Protagoras, the sophists).

## Civil War and Restoration, 404/3 BC

After Sparta's victory in the [[peloponnesian-war|Peloponnesian War]], the Athenian
Assembly was forced, under duress, to pass legislation turning over effective control of
the city to **the Thirty**, led by Critias, operating under Spartan military occupation.
The Thirty murdered democratic leaders, exiled thousands of ordinary citizens, and
confiscated property. A democratic resistance based at Phyle, aided by Thebans, retook
Piraeus; a pitched battle ended in victory for the democrats, and Critias was killed in
the fighting. The restored democracy of 403 BC passed a **general amnesty** for past
revolutionary misdeeds and repaid the Thirty's Spartan debts from public funds; the
reconciliation policy worked, ending open oligarchic activism at Athens and inaugurating a
long period of relative social harmony and renewed prosperity ([Aristotle], *Constitution
of the Athenians*). Critias — an associate of Socrates and Plato's uncle — had his grave
monument depict Oligarchy setting fire to Democracy, with an epitaph proclaiming pride in
having "restrained the arrogance of the cursed Athenian demos." **Socrates** was tried and
executed in 399 BC on charges of impiety and corrupting the youth, in part because
Athenians believed Critias had learned his "evil ways" from him — though Socrates had been
tolerated as a critic of democracy for a generation and was not prosecuted immediately
after the Thirty's fall.

Later fourth-century politics show the amnesty's legacy: in 346 BC **Isocrates** wrote an
open letter urging Philip II of Macedon to unite the factious Greek cities and lead an
invasion of Persia, seeing this as both a cure for endemic Greek civil conflict and a way
to export impoverished, stateless Greeks via colonization. After the catastrophic defeat
at Chaeronea (338 BC), **Lycurgus'** prosecution speech *Against Leocrates* (331/330 BC)
argued that fleeing the city during crisis amounted to voting for the polis's destruction,
articulating an austere civic-duty vision of citizenship at a moment when Athens — though
facing severe post-Chaeronea uncertainty — nonetheless averted a slide into *stasis* (see
[[polis|Polis]]). Demosthenes, architect of the anti-Macedonian policy that led to the
defeat, was nevertheless given the honour of delivering the annual oration over the war
dead; Lycurgus became a major post-Chaeronea Athenian politician, driving public building
and moral reform, and was reputedly a student of Plato. Athenian lawcourt oratory more
broadly served an important civic function of publicly testing and revising democratic
values before large audiences — contrary to Plato's view (see [[polis|Polis]] on the
*Republic*) that political dispute was simply destructive of the polis.

## OHCW (Boardman/Griffin/Murray 1986) — Forrest, Hornblower, Murray

[[sources/boardman-oxford-history-classical-world-1986|OHCW (1986)]] layers three chapter authors onto the institutional story:

**Forrest (Ch.1) — archaic scaffolding.** Solon (594) cancelled debts, banned debt-slavery, strengthened the assembly with a new council as “second anchor,” and replaced birth (Eupatridae) with four land-wealth classes for office access. Cleisthenes after 510 “added the people to his faction,” creating demes with local self-government grouped into ten tribes each mixing Plain/Coast/City blocks—structure lasting ~200 years. In 487 election-plus-lot for archons eventually elevated the ten elected tribal generals over archons as chief officers (efficiency over pure lot-principle). Marathon victory, Aristotle notes shrewdly, gave the people political confidence.

**Hornblower (Ch.6) — empire-funded radical democracy and its limits.** Imperial revenue (boosted by [[cimon|Cimon]]’s 460s operations) made possible the 462 changes of Ephialtes and [[pericles|Pericles]]; jury pay enabled large *dikasteria*; further pay for *boule* and festivals meant democracy was “paid for by the allies.” ~40,000 adult male citizens eligible; perhaps 6,000 attending important Pnyx debates. Formal popular sovereignty limited by deme life, the *boule* of 500 (not a pure cross-section), the generals, demagogues, and *ekklesia* procedure/psychology (infrequent meetings; show-of-hands; aristocratic deference). Democracy was more immediate than modern systems yet less inclusive (slaves, women, subject allies excluded). Closing thesis after Chaeronea: imperialism proved incompatible with democracy.

**Murray (Ch.9) — family, associations, public culture.** The developed city was a network of associations (gennetai, drinking groups, gymnasia, burial clubs, schools) creating *polis* belonging; multiple memberships produced freedom to choose among demands. Citizenship law required two citizen parents—democratic imposition of peasant norms on aristocracy that had married abroad. Women’s seclusion and male public groupings shifted balance from family to community (magnificent festivals and public buildings; mean private houses): “erosion of the family was the price” of escaping tribal/kinship ties into a new civic organization.

## Freeman (2014) — closing assessment

[[sources/freeman-egypt-greece-rome-2014|Freeman (2014)]] offers a summary historiographic
framing: "It is easy to point to the shortcomings and contradictions of Athenian
democracy. However, it remains unique as the world's only example of a successfully
functioning and sustained direct democracy. It lasted for nearly 140 years."
