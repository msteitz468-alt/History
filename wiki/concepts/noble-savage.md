# Noble Savage

## Definition

A European image of Native Americans (and other non-Europeans) as living in natural virtue uncorrupted by civilization — the positive pole of a binary that also produced denigrating “ignoble” stereotypes. CHNPA historiography (Washburn & Trigger, ch. 2) traces it from early contact literature through Lahontan/Rousseau to later image scholarship (Berkhofer, Fairchild, Pearce). It is a **European category imposed on Native peoples**, not an indigenous self-description. Both noble and ignoble images distorted actual Native histories. See [[invention-of-the-native]], [[native-north-american-historiography]].

## First Contact and the Fashioning of the Image

Per Washburn & Trigger, the image of the “noble savage” began to be fashioned at first contact, emphasizing initial innocence and generosity as much as nakedness—traits constantly noted by Columbus and the explorers who followed him. Friendly receptions often rooted in Native cultures that used propitiatory gestures toward superior power and expected reciprocal sharing; such group-solidarity ethics morally impressed Europeans whose daily life was the antithesis. Columbus’s *Diario* gifts/trade scene is cited as the idyllic type repeated by Vespucci, Cabral, Magellan, Verrazzano, Drake, and others (including reported offers of women).

Columbus used a New World Garden of Eden image; innocence soon met European avarice, cruelty, and lust once Native “weakness” and lack of iron/arms were noted. Concurrent early commentators also denigrated Indians as thralls of the devil, lustful cannibals, or people living in terror of nature—the innocence-versus-degeneration debate continued into the nineteenth century. Portuguese Brazil first-contact reports paralleled Spanish themes of innocence and generosity; French–Tupinamba alliance created respectful reciprocal relations that later French writers and allies treated as a model for French–Indian relations in North America (Léry, Thevet → Montaigne, Ronsard, Rabelais). Hans Staden documented both pleasing traits and torture/cannibalism.

## Seventeenth–Eighteenth-Century French Development

Seventeenth-century French writings usually admired some aspects of Native life. Marc Lescarbot (*Histoire de la Nouvelle France*, 1609), influenced by Montaigne and classical Tacitus/Strabo, portrayed Micmacs (Mi’kmaqs) as noble savages free of many European vices. Champlain described eastern Canadian Natives as rude and undisciplined; Sagard took a middle path (courage/generosity admired; religious ignorance condemned). Jesuits saw Huron country as Satan’s stronghold yet noted Hurons more generous/less quarrelsome than Europeans; opinions swung between admiration and lurid accounts of religious “depravity” to win mission support ([[huron-wendat]]).

Fur traders’ accounts (Boucher, Radisson, Denys, Perrot, La Potherie) were less dominated by literary stereotypes than clerical works. Exception: baron de Lahontan’s 1703 memoirs and dialogues with “Adario” (apparently Kondiaronk, d. 1701) ridiculed French religious dogma and social injustice—message that Indians were free, Frenchmen slaves. **Adario is the direct prototype of Rousseau’s noble savage.** Authenticity of ascribed Native views is partly corroborated by Lafitau and Jesuits on facts if not attitudes; modern Native scholar Georges Sioui argues Lahontan understood Native culture more deeply than any other seventeenth-century European in Canada. Thus the so-called myth of the Noble Savage was not simply a product of the salons of Paris, as is often claimed, but a concept that many individuals who had experienced Native life at first hand helped to develop.

English captivity literature and “white Indians” also fed Rousseauian conclusions: many captives preferred Indian life; Crevecoeur, Colden, Franklin claimed thousands of Europeans became Indians with no reverse converts (Vaughan & Richter revise this for New England convert towns but concede Indian culture incorporated strangers more thoroughly than Puritan New England). Colonial husbandmen’s writings systematically denied the positive character of Indian life in the interest of land appropriation—an English land-focused counterpart to the noble-savage celebration.

Gilbert Chinard is cited on the continuing contradiction between brute-beast and noble-savage schools: nearly all missionaries of Canada and the Caribbean assert Indians are the most barbarous and inhuman of peoples, then in their accounts show the opposite—“what commences as an indictment finishes with a dithyramb.”

## Enlightenment and Romantic Reworkings

Later French writers with little Native contact used Charlevoix but altered interpretations. Raynal’s *Histoire philosophique* (1770) portrayed North American Indians as noble savages (with Rousseau and Voltaire) exemplifying uncorrupted human nature—hardy, brave, magnanimous, free of monetary greed; principal failing lack of science/superstition. Chateaubriand similarly praised morals while condemning ignorance of Christianity. Dainville/Bossange *Beautés de l’histoire du Canada* (1821) introduced romantic style: conflict of savagery vs civilization; fiery passions and gory tortures; cruelty as innate human nature needing state curb (post-Revolutionary pessimism); contemporary Indians unchanging—romantic belief in innate, unalterable behavior rather than historical responsiveness.

The noble-savage frame thus slides easily into its opposite: static “nature” that can be either idealized virtue or immutable racial deficiency. Nineteenth-century biological racism, polygenism, and Darwinian rationales for anticipated extinction (see [[invention-of-the-native]]) inverted Enlightenment optimism about improvable nature into a progressive tragedy of the vanishing Indian.

## Image Scholarship

Canonical treatments mapped in CHNPA’s bibliographic essay include H. N. Fairchild, *The Noble Savage* (1928); R. H. Pearce, *Savagism and Civilization*; R. F. Berkhofer, Jr., *The White Man’s Indian* (1978); Honour, *New Golden Land*; Chiappelli, *First Images of America*. New France–focused work includes Dickason, *Myth of the Savage*. Stereotypes also tracked in literature and film.

## Limitations

- European category, not indigenous self-description.
- Binary with ignoble stereotypes: both poles erase historical specificity and Native agency.
- Can romanticize stasis and deny political calculation, agriculture, state formation, and change—feeding the same past-bound frame as denigration ([[invention-of-the-native]]).
- Missionary and traveler accounts that generate the image often contradict themselves within a single text (Chinard).
- Modern idealization of an unchanging Native “core,” though intending to honor Native values, risks minimizing rationality in ways ethnohistorians (Washburn & Trigger via Bailey) warn against; see [[ethnohistory]], [[indigenous-historical-consciousness]].
