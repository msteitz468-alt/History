# The Administration of Justice under the Principate

How Roman law was *applied* — the courts, judges, and procedure of the late Republic, and the
way **imperial jurisdiction** progressively overtook them under [[augustus|Augustus]] and the
[[julio-claudian-dynasty|Julio-Claudians]]. Treated in [[bowman-cah-v10-1996|CAH X]] Ch. 12
(**H. Galsterer**); concerned with the application of law, not its substance (for which see the
volume's chapter on Roman legal science).

## Continuity from the Republic

Galsterer stresses that in justice the volume's dates "are irrelevant": the system inherited
from **Sulla's reforms** ran on with only gradual change. Its pillars at Rome were:

- **Civil jurisdiction** by the two senior praetors — the *praetor urbanus* (citizens) and
  *praetor peregrinus* (foreigners) — using the **formulary system**: a bipartite procedure in
  which the magistrate, *in iure*, framed a *formula* (a scenario) and referred the factual
  decision to a private ***iudex***. The praetor's annual **edict** accumulated the *formulae*
  that met new needs — the engine of Republican legal development.
- **Judges** drawn from the *album iudicum*, organized since the *lex Aurelia* (70 BCE) into
  *decuriae* of senators, knights, and *tribuni aerarii*; Augustus added a fourth (lower-census)
  *decuria* and Caligula a fifth. Service was a serious obligation — even curators of streets
  and aqueducts sat as judges.
- **Criminal jurisdiction** by the standing jury-courts (**quaestiones perpetuae**), each for a
  statutory crime (*repetundae*, *peculatus*, *ambitus*, *de sicariis et veneficis*), prosecuted
  by **popular accusation** (*nominis delatio*) — with the *delatores* and *indices* an
  "ineradicable defect" of the system, like the Athenian sycophants. There was no public
  prosecutor, police, or enforcement: the winner himself had to bring the *actio iudicati*
  against a recalcitrant loser.

In **Italy**, after the Social War, the municipal *IIviri/IIIIviri iure dicundo* ran local
justice on the Roman model, but under **upper limits of competence** (the *lex Rubria* fixed
~15,000 sesterces) and the spreading right of *revocatio Romam* — restricting municipal courts
to petty cases. In the **provinces** the governor used both the formulary process and,
increasingly, ***cognitio*** — inquiry into fact and law in person with his *consilium*, based
"entirely upon his *imperium*" — touring the assize-centres (*conventus*) of his province.

## The rise of imperial jurisdiction

The introduction of one-man rule transformed the system "in different ways." The Republican
courts survived — Augustus reorganized them in the detailed **leges Iuliae iudiciorum** (17
BCE), foundational for centuries — but were "hemmed in" from two directions:

1. **The Senate as a court.** The consuls, with the Senate as jury, became a peers' court for
   delinquent senators, taking especially *maiestas* and *repetundae* cases (see
   [[senate-under-the-principate]]).
2. **The emperor.** Combining the **tribunician *ius auxilii*** (reshaped in 30 BCE as the right
   to *appellatum iudicare*), his **proconsular *imperium*** (allowing *cognitio* in his own
   right), and his overriding ***auctoritas***, the emperor "very soon became the most important
   institution in law." He took some cases at first instance (where his decision would serve as
   an *exemplum*), and above all heard **appeals** — a thing that had not existed in the Republic
   (where *provocatio* was a political check on *imperium*). Appeals multiplied so fast that
   Augustus delegated them (urban cases to the *praetor urbanus*, provincial to chosen
   *consulares*).

Galsterer's emblematic case: when **Piso** was accused of poisoning Germanicus (AD 20), three
courts were potentially competent — the *quaestio*, the consuls-and-Senate, and the emperor —
and "the decision which court to choose lay with the accusers," who in *maiestas* cases "for
evident reasons" preferred the emperor's. **Nero's** attempt (Tac. *Ann.* 13.4) to route all
appeals from Italy and the public provinces to the **Senate** "failed blatantly": litigants
preferred the emperor's court.

## The drift to *cognitio* and the new officials

The trend was from the old *ordo iudiciorum* (praetor + private judge, bound by procedural
limits) toward **extraordinary *cognitio*** — flexible, unbound inquiry. The praetors' role
shrank (their edict was now near-standardized, ossifying legal development), and the aediles'
jurisdiction passed to imperial officials: the **praefectus urbi**, **praefectus vigilum**, and
**praefectus annonae**, who exercised first-instance jurisdiction on the emperor's *imperium*,
and the imperial ***consilium***, which "by and by became the most important body for the
development of law." Augustus and **Claudius** were both famed (and, in Claudius' case,
suspected) as zealous judges; Claudius' surviving speech against the lawlessness of the
*delatores* — "Let us stop the lawless tyranny of the accusers" — "at least sounds good," though
its effect was slight.

## Significance

The story of justice is the quiet counterpart to the political narrative: without abolishing the
Republican courts, the emperor became, through appeal and *cognitio*, the **apex and last resort
of the entire legal order** — the idea that "a citizen might appeal to him" reaching "even the
last and least of the provincials." This concentration of the legal order in the *princeps* and
his *consilium* and prefects is, in Crook's phrase elsewhere, the deepest formal difference
between Republic and Empire.
