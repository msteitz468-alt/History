# X-ray Crystallography

X-ray crystallography is an analytical technique used to determine the three-dimensional atomic structure of a crystal. By directing a beam of X-rays at a crystal, the electrons in the atoms diffract the beam into a specific pattern of spots. Historians of science track how this method transitioned from inorganic mineral studies to complex organic molecules, creating the foundation for molecular biology.

## Principles of Diffraction

Crystallography relies on several core physical principles:
- **Bragg's Law:** Formulated by William Lawrence Bragg in 1912, the law relates the spacing of atomic planes ($d$) in a crystal to the angle of diffraction ($\theta$) and the wavelength ($\lambda$) of the X-rays:
  \[n\lambda = 2d \sin\theta\]
- **Fourier Transforms:** The spot patterns on the film represent the reciprocal space of the crystal's electron density. Reconstructing the real space coordinates requires a mathematical Fourier transform.
- **The Phase Problem:** Because photographic film only records the intensity of the diffracted spots and not their phase, crystallographers must use techniques like Patterson function analysis or heavy-atom isomorphous replacement to determine the phases and solve the structure.

## Application to Organic Structures

During the mid-twentieth century, researchers like J.D. Bernal, Dorothy Hodgkin, and Max Perutz pioneered the application of crystallography to biological molecules. Rosalind Franklin's work represents a critical transition point:
- **Disordered Carbons:** In Paris under Jacques Mering, Franklin mastered the diffraction of amorphous, non-crystalline carbons, describing their structures mathematically.
- **DNA Hydration:** At King's, Franklin discovered that DNA adopts different space groups based on water content (crystalline A-form and paracrystalline B-form), applying Patterson functions to locate the phosphorus backbone.
- **Virology:** At Birkbeck, Franklin and Aaron Klug solved the helical structure of Tobacco Mosaic Virus (TMV) and initiated work on polio, demonstrating that crystallography could solve complex macromolecular assemblies.
