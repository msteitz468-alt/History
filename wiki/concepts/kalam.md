## Definition and Scope

**Kalam** (Arabic: "discussion", "speech", "discourse") designates the tradition of Islamic speculative theology — reasoned argument about the nature of God, the cosmos, human responsibility, and the basis of religious knowledge. Hodgson defines it as "discussion of points of religious belief on the basis of rational criteria" (*Venture of Islam*, Vol. 1, p. 2010). A practitioner was called a *mutakallim* (pl. *mutakallimun*).

Kalam is to be distinguished from:
- **Fiqh** (jurisprudence): the derivation of legal norms from Qur'an and hadith — the core discipline of the [[ulama]].
- **[[islamic-philosophy|Falsafah]]**: the Philosophic tradition inherited from the Greeks, which took rational demonstration rather than revealed authority as its starting point and operated with an explicitly cosmological rather than juridical framework.
- **[[mutazilism]]**: the first major and most rationalist school of kalam, which flourished c. 750–900 CE; Mu'tazilism is a school within kalam, not synonymous with it.

Kalam grew from within the Islamic prophetic tradition and never surrendered the authority of Qur'an and [[hadith]] as its primary reference points — even when, as with the Mu'tazilis, it gave rational argument wide scope in interpreting that authority. The more conservative strand of the [[ulama]] (the *Hadith folk*) regarded kalam as bid'a (illicit innovation) and refused to engage with it at all, insisting that whatever was given by revelation needed no rational proof and ought not to be subjected to one.

**Emic usage**: The term was used by practitioners and critics alike as a description of the discipline; critics sometimes used it pejoratively to suggest mere verbal disputation without genuine religious content.

**Etic usage**: Modern scholars use kalam to denote the entire tradition of Islamic speculative theology across its many schools.

## Origins and Initial Agenda

The initial occasion for kalam was apologetic: defending Islamic doctrines against challenges from other monotheists (Christians, Jews, Zoroastrians, Manichaeans) and from internal anthropomorphist interpretations of Qur'anic language about God.

The first task was therefore negative: to show that "there was nothing in the Qur'an which was repugnant to careful reasoning" (*ibid.*, p. 1998). But this apparently modest project quickly demanded something more ambitious: the establishment of positive cosmological positions. To show that the Qur'an was rational required some account of what rationality meant, which meant some framework for understanding God's relation to creation, the structure of the cosmos, the nature of human freedom, and the criteria of valid theological argument.

This logic drove kalam from piecemeal apologia toward systematic theology.

## The Mu'tazili Foundation

The school that first gave kalam a comprehensive systematic form was [[mutazilism]]. The first great systematic Mu'tazili thinker, Abu-l-Hudhayl (d. 840), organised doctrines under five heads:

1. **Unity of God** (*tawhid*): The absolute unity and simplicity of God — condemning anthropomorphism, Manichaean dualism, and the popular literalism of Qur'anic imagery. The Mu'tazilis extended this to a controversial claim: God cannot possess distinct attributes (knowledge, power) that are "other than" His essence, since this would introduce a multiplicity of eternal entities alongside God.

2. **Justice of God** (*'adl*): God is perfectly just; He cannot inflict undeserved punishment. This entailed that **human beings possess genuine freedom of will and are therefore genuinely responsible for their acts.** God could not punish humans for actions they did not freely choose.

3. **The Promise and the Threat** (*wa'd wa-wa'id*): Emphasis on the reality of the coming Judgment and the importance of righteousness over mere community membership; the Qur'an was elevated as the standard at the expense of hadith.

4. **The Intermediate Position** (*al-manzila bayn al-manzilatayn*): The Muslim grave sinner occupies a position between full believer and unbeliever — he is neither saved nor fully damned by allegiance alone.

5. **Commanding Right and Forbidding Wrong** (*al-amr bil-ma'ruf wa-l-nahy 'an al-munkar*): The obligation to take active initiative toward godly social order.

## The Ash'ari Counter-System

By the end of the ninth century, the Mu'tazili positions had come under sustained attack from scholars who identified more closely with the Hadith folk but were unwilling simply to dismiss kalam entirely. The two most significant figures were **Abu-l-Hasan al-Ash'ari** (d. 935) and **Abu-Mansur al-Maturidi** (d. 944).

These thinkers sought to "limit kalam and its cosmology more closely to a defence of the doctrines given in the accumulating hadith" (*ibid.*, p. 2061), while retaining kalam's philosophical vocabulary and argumentative forms. They were "sceptical of the powers of abstract reason" and attempted "to deduce as little as possible from the supposed requirements of reason as such" (*ibid.*, p. 2065).

### The Ash'ari Doctrinal Response

**On divine attributes**: Where the Mu'tazilis had denied that God possessed distinct attributes (knowledge, power) lest these be "other than" His essence and thus co-eternal with Him, al-Ash'ari affirmed that God does possess real attributes, but that they are "not other than His essence." The Mu'tazili dilemma (attributes as separate eternal entities) was answered by a definitional move that preserved the full effectiveness of a God who knows and acts, without conceding a plurality of co-eternal realities.

**On faith** (*iman*): Against the Mu'tazili intellectualisation of faith as sheer propositional assent, al-Ash'ari drew on legal definitions that included "assent and affirmation and action as well, though the assent was primary" (*ibid.*, p. 2113). Faith was not mere intellectual conviction but personal commitment expressed in act.

**On divine omnipotence vs. human responsibility**: This was the central dilemma. The Mu'tazili solution — humans genuinely choose their acts, so God's justice in rewarding and punishing them is vindicated — seemed too neat. Al-Ash'ari reportedly posed the problem of three brothers to his Mu'tazili teacher (al-Jubba'i): one in Paradise for many good works, one with a lower post having died young, one in Hell — where God had allowed the middle brother to die young to forestall future sin but had not done the same for the third. No simple system of merit could resolve this. The Ash'ari solution was the doctrine of **kasb** (acquisition): God alone creates all actions directly, but superadds to some actions a quality of voluntary acquisition by which God's will makes the individual a voluntary agent and morally responsible — without the individual being the genuine originator of the action.

**On cosmology: the atomist solution**: The Ash'ari school developed a systematic cosmological counter to the Philosophic world view. Against the Faylasufs' world of enduring substances with essential natures and inherent causal powers, the Ash'aris posited a world of atomic points in space and time, with "no continuity except the will of God, which created every atomic point anew every moment" (*ibid.*, p. 2255). Natural sequences — fire burning cotton, the sun rising — are not necessary causal connections but God's "customary ways" (*'ada*), which He could at any moment alter. This framework allowed for any amount of miraculous events while denying the philosophical necessity of miracles (since every event is equally an act of divine will), and it grounded the validity of historical knowledge over abstract universal knowledge.

Hodgson summarises the contrast with the Philosophic tradition: "For the Faylasufs, it was the wise and knowing elite... that were the only persons really fully human. For the Ash'aris... the Divine blessing was upon the average individual whose overt acts conformed with the pattern laid down for the community of Muhammad" (*ibid.*, p. 2205–8).

## Kalam's Social and Institutional Position

Despite its intellectual sophistication, kalam remained "at best secondary to that of the Shari'ah in the eyes of most of the 'ulama'" throughout the High Caliphal period and well beyond (*ibid.*, p. 2280). The majority of Hadith-minded scholars were hostile to kalam disputation altogether, regarding any attempt to "prove" revelation through rational argument as presumptuous and potentially corrosive of genuine faith. The Ash'ari and Maturidi schools, though they aligned kalam with Hadith folk positions, remained suspect in the eyes of the most consistent traditionalists.

Kalam's institutional base was therefore not the mosque or the madrasa (in the early period) but the court and the circles of scholars who had direct Abbasid patronage. The Mu'tazili school in particular flourished under al-Ma'mun's active promotion, including his controversial Inquisition (*mihna*, 833–848 CE) in which scholars were tested on the doctrine of the createdness of the Qur'an. The failure of the *mihna* marked a turning point after which Mu'tazilism lost court backing and increasingly ceded ground to the Hadith-aligned alternatives.

## Relationship to Falsafah

Kalam and Falsafah stood in a structurally ambiguous relationship: competitors for the domain of rational discourse about God and the cosmos, but mutually borrowing from each other's conceptual vocabulary. The Mu'tazilis absorbed Greek philosophical categories while insisting on the priority of revelation. The Ash'aris developed a sophisticated counter-cosmology that was, as Hodgson notes, "philosophically anti-Philosophical" — using the tools of philosophical argument to undermine the Philosophic world view.

The deeper divergence lay in their foundational epistemological commitments. Falsafah started from the axiom of the rational order of the cosmos and deduced theological conclusions from there. Kalam started from the authority of revelation and used rational argument to clarify, defend, and systematise doctrines given by that authority. The Faylasufs saw revelation as a useful socio-political necessity for the masses; the mutakallimun saw rational argument as a useful apologetic tool for revelation.

## Historiography

J. Schacht's "New Sources for the History of Muhammadan Theology" (*Studia Islamica*, 1953) is cited by Hodgson as providing useful observations on kalam's relationship to other Islamic currents. The field has since been substantially reshaped by the recovery and publication of early Mu'tazili texts (many preserved only in later compilations), allowing a more precise reconstruction of the school's development. The question of how much Greek philosophical material entered kalam through direct contact with Falsafah, as opposed to through the mediation of Christian theological disputations, remains open. Hodgson notes that the Mu'tazili vocabulary "seems to echo, though imprecisely, the Greek philosophical tradition; perhaps it reflects Christian theological discussions with which the Mu'tazilis and other Muslims took issue" (*ibid.*, p. 2037).
