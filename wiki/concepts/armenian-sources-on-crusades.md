# Armenian Sources on the Crusades

## Definition and Scope

The Armenian historiographical tradition provides a distinctive perspective on the Crusades — neither Byzantine nor Muslim, but that of a Christian people politically fragmented between Byzantine, Seljuk Turkish, and later Mongol powers, and partially integrated with the Frankish newcomers in Cilicia. Robert W. Thomson's essay analyzes how Armenian writers integrated the Crusade phenomenon into their existing intellectual and prophetic frameworks from 1098 through the 13th century.

This page covers the Armenian written tradition (manuscripts, chronicles, colophons) rather than the military or political history of Cilician Armenia or Armenian participation in Crusading expeditions.

## Key Sources

### Manuscript Colophons
Armenian scribes regularly added colophons to copied manuscripts — the earliest firsthand comments on the Crusaders. The first Armenian description of the Crusaders dates to 1098, from a scribe named John on the Black Mountain near Antioch, who describes the arrival of "the western nation of heroes" bearing "the sign of Christ's Cross." The scribe Aaron (1099, Alexandria) equates the Franks with "the valiant race of Romans, who had come at God's instigation." A colophon of 1130 refers to Frankish rule as a "tyranny" (br'nakalut'iwn) — the same word used of Saladin elsewhere. The Armenian term for "Crusader" (xac'enkalk', "those who had taken up the cross") appears only once in a 1270 colophon.

### Matthew of Edessa (chronicle to 1136)
The first Armenian historian to offer an interpretation of the Crusades. He frames the arrival of the Franks as fulfillment of the prophecy of the Armenian patriarch Nerses the Great (4th century) — a prophecy that had been elaborated in a 10th-century *Life of Nerses* far beyond its original fifth-century context. Matthew does not interpret the Crusaders as harbingers of the last things. His chronicle is the principal source for 11th–12th century Armenian history and was continued beyond his death.

### Samuel of Ani
Relying on the lost Chronicle of John the Deacon, Samuel briefly describes the "perfidious attitude of Alexios II, 'the son of Satan,'" toward the Crusaders — reflecting Armenian hostility to Byzantium. Writing when Ani was under Georgian control, he did not view the Crusades as central to Armenian history.

### Vardan Arewelc'i (fl. mid-13th century)
Notes the fulfillment of Nerses' prophecy and gives the famous legend of the First Crusade's origin — a French count's eye knocked out by Muslim guards at Jerusalem's holy tomb; he carried the eye to Rome, inflaming Western opinion. This story (not found in the original Syriac of Michael the Syrian or in Bar Hebraeus) appears to be of Armenian origin. Vardan also helped translate the Chronicle of Michael the Syrian into Armenian with a Syrian named Ishox, adapting and abbreviating the original.

### Smbat (Smpad) the Constable's Chronicle (completed c. 1272)
Brother of king Het'um I of Cilicia; well-informed about Crusade events. Notes that fuller details on the Frankish princes may be found in Frankish historians. Incorporates westernizing practices into Cilician law (his adaptation of Mxit'ar Gosh's code). Offers no prophetic interpretation of the Crusades. Completed shortly before Smbat's death in 1276.

### Kirakos of Gandzak
Repeats the Nerses prophecy (connecting the disasters to the Mongols). Notes that the Crusaders came to Asia Minor via Thrace to avenge Christian sufferings at the hands of "Scythians" (Turks) and Persians, but were "much vexed by the son of Belial called Alexios" (Alexios I Komnenos). Does not link the new Romans (Crusaders) with the old Romans (Constantine's era).

### Step'annos Orbelean (History of Siunik', 1299)
Metropolitan of Siunik' in northeastern Armenia; concerned with local history, largely ignores the Crusades. Contains the first substantial quotation from the Pseudo-Methodian Apocalypse in Armenian historical writing, but does not integrate this into contemporary commentary.

### The Letter of Concord (Pseudo-historical)
A strongly pro-Western document (probably a translation of a French original), purporting to be the pact between Constantine the Great and Trdat (Tiridates) of Armenia, their patriarchs Silvester and Gregory the Illuminator. Grants Bethlehem to Trdat, consecrates Gregory as patriarch of all Armenians, and predicts future salvation from Roman (= Frankish) descendants. Representative of the extreme pro-Roman party in Cilician Armenia.

## Major Themes

### The Prophetic Framework: Nerses' Prophecy
The dominant Armenian intellectual response to the Crusades was to fit them into an existing prophetic tradition. The Armenian patriarch Nerses I (died c. 373) became the anchor of this tradition, his prophecy progressively elaborated in later recensions of his *Life*. By Matthew of Edessa's time, the prophecy predicted: the Ishmaelites (Arabs/Turks) would subject the Greeks to tribute; then "the valiant race of the Romans, who are called Franks" would arrive, capture Jerusalem, and remove the "subjection of the Greeks." Eventually salvation would come from the Romans for Armenia.

This tradition made the Crusades feel predicted, contained, and purposeful — a consolation for a people experiencing repeated catastrophe. However, historians diverged: Matthew of Edessa used it; Kirakos connected the prophecy to the Mongols rather than the Crusaders; Smbat mentioned it only in relation to the fall of Ani (1044); Step'annos largely ignored it. There was no single Armenian interpretation.

### The Constantine-Trdat Tradition
A perennial feature of Armenian historical writing from the 5th century: the legendary meeting of the newly converted kings Constantine (Roman emperor) and Trdat (Armenian king), accompanied by their patriarchs Silvester and Gregory the Illuminator. In various versions, this encounter included the division of the holy places in Jerusalem between the two parties. The arrival of the Crusaders ("Romans") made this tradition immediately topical. In Cilicia it was elaborated with apocalyptic themes from Pseudo-Methodius: predictions of a new Constantine and new Trdat who would liberate Jerusalem, overcome Anti-Christ, and usher in the end-times.

### Ambivalence: Relief and Disruption
Armenian responses to the Crusaders were mixed. The initial colophons are generally favorable — relief at a Christian military force against Seljuk oppression. But ambivalence quickly appeared:
- A colophon of 1130 calls Frankish rule a "tyranny."
- Samuel of Ani calls Alexios I "the son of Satan" for his treatment of the Crusaders — a reflection of Byzantine-Armenian antagonism more than pro-Crusader enthusiasm.
- Greater Armenia (away from Cilicia) showed relatively little interest in the Crusades, which did not reach most Armenians directly.
- In Cilicia, Crusader influence was pervasive but not unanimously welcomed; even writers who discussed westernizing habits often condemned them.

### Cilicia vs. Greater Armenia
A structural divide in Armenian responses:
- **Cilician Armenia**: in direct contact with Crusader states; social institutions, law codes, religious practices, art, and language were transformed. Smbat the Constable adapted Mxit'ar Gosh's law code to incorporate Western practices. The extreme pro-Roman (Latinophile) party produced texts like the Letter of Concord. Prophetic elaboration (the Pseudo-Epiphanius homily on Antichrist) was Cilician in origin.
- **Greater Armenia** (under Georgian, then Mongol control): writers like Samuel of Ani, Kirakos, Vardan, Step'annos Orbelean paid far less attention to the Crusades. Their concerns were local — Mongol invasions, liturgical disputes (Greek calendar controversy), provincial church affairs. When they mentioned Crusaders at all, they were peripheral actors.

### Armenian Perception of Franks vs. Byzantines vs. Muslims
Armenian writers generally called the Crusaders "Franks," "Romans," or "Latins" — almost never "Crusaders" (the Armenian equivalent appears once). The "Romans" label connected Crusaders to a long Armenian tradition of Roman alliance and aid. Byzantines (the Greek Romans) were viewed with suspicion and hostility — "the son of Satan/Belial" for Alexios I. Muslims remained the primary oppressors from whom Armenians hoped the Romans/Franks would deliver them, though this hope was repeatedly disappointed (the Crusaders did not liberate Greater Armenia from Turks or Mongols).

### The Fourth Crusade and Its Echo
The scribe Gregory at the patriarchal see of Hr'omkla noted the fall of Constantinople to the Crusaders in 1204: "At first we were saddened; then we remembered the prophecy of saint Nerses, and we await the liberation of Jerusalem and all this land." This encapsulates the Armenian interpretive reflex: catastrophe becomes evidence of prophetic progression toward eventual deliverance.

William of Rubruck (1253–55), returning from the Mongol khan, was told by an Armenian bishop that Armenians awaited Western deliverance: "Just as the souls in Limbo were waiting for the coming of Christ so as to be set free, so are we waiting for your coming in order to be delivered from this slavery we have been in for so long."

## Scholarly Significance

Thomson demonstrates that there was never one "Armenian attitude" to the Crusades, any more than a unified Armenian attitude to Byzantium or any other great power. What is distinctive is the *intellectual* response — the attempt by some Armenian historians to integrate the Crusades into the broader arc of Armenian history by linking them to traditions from 5th-century authors. The Franks were not merely new actors; they became raw material for elaborating the oldest Armenian aspiration: the return of the golden age of a new Trdat and a new Gregory, with help from the heirs of Constantine. This hope proved tenacious long after the Crusading period: it persisted in responses to the fall of Constantinople in 1453 and even into modern times.
