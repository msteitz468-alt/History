# Hunter-Gatherer Warfare

**Hunter-gatherer (forager) warfare** refers to organized lethal violence between bands or groups in pre-agricultural, non-state societies. Evidence from ethnography, archaeology (skeletal trauma, rock art, fortifications), and comparative anthropology demonstrates that war was present and often frequent among many forager groups, not a late invention of "civilization."

## Definition and Scope

- Distinguishes small-scale raiding, ambush, and revenge killings from large-scale battles (rare in pure forager contexts).
- "Primitive warfare" (older term) often carried pejorative or romantic connotations; modern analysis treats it as one end of a spectrum of human collective violence.
- Key data points: elevated rates of violent death in many documented forager societies (often 10-30%+ of adult male deaths attributable to violence, comparable to or exceeding many state societies when normalized).

## Major Evidence Clusters

- Australian Aboriginals (Tiwi, Murngin/Warner, Arunta, etc.): ritualized and lethal raiding, "revenge expeditions," club fights over women and insults.
- Yanomamo (Venezuela/Brazil): "fierce people"; chronic raiding for women and revenge; club fights and archery ambushes.
- New Guinea highlanders and lowland groups: ritual warfare, ambushes, watchtowers, gardens as targets.
- North American foragers (Plains, Northwest Coast, woodland groups pre-agriculture intensification).
- African foragers (!Kung, Hadza, Pygmies) – lower rates in some cases, higher in others; variation important.
- Mesolithic/Upper Paleolithic skeletal and rock-art evidence (Spanish Levant paintings, etc.).

## Causal Drivers (per evolutionary synthesis)

- Resources (game, water, plants, territory).
- Women and reproductive success (direct abduction or as outcome of status/ revenge cycles).
- Status, prestige, and "web of desire" (revenge, insult, glory).
- Kin selection and coalitional psychology.

## Relation to Later Warfare

Forager patterns (small groups, surprise, limited logistics, high per-capita risk) provide the baseline that agriculture, sedentism, and the state scaled up and transformed — producing larger forces, fortifications, specialized warriors, and the "Leviathan" bargain (centralized coercion to suppress internal feuding while directing violence outward).

## Historiography and Debates

- "Peaceful forager" romanticism (some 1960s-70s anthropology) vs. evidence of significant lethal violence.
- Chagnon, Gat, Keeley, LeBlanc, and others vs. critics emphasizing cultural variation or definitional issues ("what counts as war").
- Archaeological under-detection: many forager deaths leave little trace; taphonomic bias.
