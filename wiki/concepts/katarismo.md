## Definition

Katarismo is an Aymara political movement that fused indigenous ethnic identity with peasant-class struggle. It arose in Bolivia in the late 1960s as a critique of the "peasantist" ideology of the 1952 MNR Revolution, which had officially replaced the category "Indian" with "peasant" and subordinated ethnic consciousness to a unifying class identity. Katarismo rejected that erasure, insisting that to be Aymara was a distinct and prior form of political being — one demanding recognition alongside, not dissolved into, class solidarity.

As the Bolivian sociologist Silvia Rivera Cusicanqui argued, Katarismo is the recovery of a "long-term memory" obscured by the "short-term memory" of the agrarian reform and the peasant union struggle. Long memory reached back through centuries of [[tupac-amaru-jose-gabriel|colonial resistance]] to an Andean civilisation predating the Bolivian state — a civilisation whose most resonant martyrs included [[aymara-kingdoms|Túpac Katari]] himself.

**Emic/etic distinction.** Katarismo is simultaneously an emic political identity (adopted and named by Aymara activists themselves) and a scholarly analytical category used by political scientists and historians. Inside the movement the label carried pride and precision; outside it was sometimes treated as a synonym for indigenous ethnic politics in general, which the movement's own practitioners resisted as an oversimplification.

## Core Claims and Symbols

- **"Two eyes" analysis.** The CSUTCB (Central Union Confederation of Bolivian Peasant Workers, founded 1979) stressed the necessity to analyse Bolivian society simultaneously "as peasants, together with other exploited classes, and as Aymaras, Quechuas, and others, together with the other oppressed nations of the country."
- **Invocation of Túpaj Katari.** The movement named itself after the eighteenth-century Aymara rebel who besieged La Paz in 1781 before being drawn and quartered at Peñas. Oral tradition records his last words: "I will return as millions." The slogan became Katarismo's rallying cry.
- **The wiphala.** The multicoloured flag (representing Qullasuyu, Tawantinsuyu, or indigenous identity in general) became the movement's emblem and ultimately a symbol of Andean indigenous mobilisation across the continent.
- **Aymara-language radio.** Kataristas broadcast cultural and political programmes in Aymara, maintaining organisational coherence through the altiplano during military repression.
- **Plurinational state.** Beginning with CSUTCB position papers in 1979, Katarismo called for Bolivia to be redesigned as a "plurinational" state — a concept eventually inserted into Article 1 of the constitution in 1994 as "multiethnic and pluricultural," though "nation" remained a state monopoly.

## Origin and Early Development

The immediate precondition was the collapse of the Military–Peasant Pact between the Bolivian army and indigenous peasants. In 1969 General Barrientos sought to levy a land tax on titles distributed by the 1953 agrarian reform. This created a fiscal crisis for Aymara altiplano communities whose economies were based on subsistence self-sufficiency, and broke the loyalty of peasants who had been incorporated as MNR/army clients. After Barrientos's death (1969), a brief leftist military opening created political space in which Katarismo crystallised.

The August 1971 congress in Potosí placed Katarista leader Jenaro Flores atop the CNTCB. Hugo Banzer's military coup, just days later, suppressed the movement, but Kataristas maintained presence through "cultural radio programs in Aymara" and community-level organising, eventually announcing a clandestine press conference to launch the Túpaj Katari Peasant Confederation at the end of 1977.

## Internal Tendencies

Katarismo was never monolithic. Two principal tendencies emerged:

| Tendency | Party | Emphasis |
|---|---|---|
| "Two eyes" (class-ethnic synthesis) | MRTK (Túpaj Katari Revolutionary Movement) | Articulated with wider labour movement via CSUTCB |
| Indianist | MITKA (Túpaj Katari Indian Movement) | Urban Aymara militancy; influenced by Fausto Reinaga's *La revolución india* (1969) |

Neither formed a stable mass electoral party. Both subdivided repeatedly. Nevertheless Katarista proposals gained a recognised place in national politics and eventually produced the historic alliance between the MRTKL (Túpaj Katari Revolutionary Liberation Movement) and the MNR that elected Victor Hugo Cárdenas as Bolivia's first Aymara vice-president in 1993.

## Institutional Expression: CSUTCB

The Central Union Confederation of Bolivian Peasant Workers (CSUTCB), formed in 1979, was the movement's main institutional vehicle. It dissolved the Military–Peasant Pact and became the primary representative of virtually the entire Bolivian peasantry. Its 1983–1984 draft of a New Basic Agrarian Law — delivered to President Siles "wrapped in Aymara ritual weavings" amid a large demonstration — went far beyond land redistribution to propose a new community-state relationship grounded in the principle that "communities have 'native' rights because they predate the Bolivian state."

## Limitations and Criticisms

- Electoral parties fragmented; none built stable, broad representation.
- The 1982 return to democracy released an "avalanche of demands" but government concessions came as "decrees on wet paper" — unimplemented.
- After 1985, neoliberal economic policies combined with Jaime Paz and Banzer alliances weakened CSUTCB's Katarista leadership.
- Critics on the left charged that the Cárdenas–MNR alliance of 1993 was an "audaz alianza" (bold, surprising alliance) with neoliberals that compromised ethnic autonomy for electoral access.
- The Indianist wing (MITKA) was critiqued as excessively top-down and urban by highland communities more embedded in the union tradition.

## Geographic Reach

Centred in the Bolivian Aymara altiplano, Katarismo extended influence to:

- Southern Peru (Aymara-speaking communities around Lake Titicaca)
- Northern Chile (Aymara communities reactivated after the restoration of democracy in 1990)

A multi-state Aymara political unity never materialised, partly because times were not ripe and partly because of excessively personalised leadership in coordinating bodies like CISA (Coordination of Indians of South America).

## Historiography

The movement was primarily recovered by Bolivian scholars of Aymara origin, including the Taller de Historia Oral Andina (THOA), which documented the "cacique movement" of the early twentieth century as the deep prehistory of Katarismo. Silvia Rivera Cusicanqui's *Oprimidos pero no vencidos* (1984) remains the foundational academic text. Albó's essay "De MNRistas a Kataristas" (Historia Boliviana, 1985) and its English counterpart in Stern (ed.), *Resistance, Rebellion and Consciousness* trace the full political evolution. The chapter in *Cambridge History of Native Peoples of the Americas* Vol. III Part 2 (1999) reflects an explicitly pro-indigenous standpoint; readers should note the advocacy dimension when evaluating its claims about internal dynamics.
