# The Mixed Constitution and Anacyclosis (Polybius, Book 6)

The theory — given its most influential form in **Book 6** of [[polybius-histories|Polybius's
*Histories*]] — that the stability and success of a state depend on **mixing** the three "simple"
forms of government, so as to arrest the natural **cycle of constitutional decay** (*anacyclosis*).
It is, McGing notes, "the most famous part" of Polybius, and the foundation Polybius offered for his
central question: under what kind of constitution did Rome conquer the world?

## Anacyclosis: the Cycle of Constitutions

Polybius held that each of the three good (*simple*) forms of government carries within it the seed of
its own corruption, so that constitutions revolve in a natural cycle:

- **kingship** (monarchy ruling by reason) → degenerates into **tyranny**
- → overthrown into **aristocracy** → degenerates into **oligarchy**
- → overthrown into **democracy** → degenerates into **mob-rule / ochlocracy**
- → collapse into chaos, from which a "primitive monarch" emerges and the cycle begins again.

Each form "is like a living organism that has its own natural birth, rise, fall, and death"; the
cycle itself is "the product of nature." This is the political analogue of Polybius's wider sense of
organic rise and decline (and of his use of **Tyche**).

## The Mixed Constitution

The escape from the cycle is **mixture**. The Spartan lawgiver **Lycurgus**, foreseeing the
instability of the simple forms, deliberately combined the best features of monarchy, aristocracy and
democracy so that "the balance between [them] would fend off the natural tendency to corruption,"
preserving Spartan liberty longer than any other state. **Rome** achieved the same balance — but "by
means of a natural set of developments rather than as a conscious decision": its **consuls**
(monarchic), **Senate** (aristocratic) and **people / assemblies** (democratic) each held real power
and **checked** the others. This system of mutual check is, for Polybius, the institutional secret of
Roman steadiness under the shock of Cannae and of her rise to world empire. Polybius compares Rome to
the other "mixed" states (Sparta, Carthage, Crete), judging Roman institutions superior at fostering
courage (the famous account of the inspiring Roman aristocratic funeral, 6.53–4).

## Limitations and Critiques

- The theory has Greek roots (Plato's *Laws*, Aristotle's *Politics*, the Spartan "mixed" tradition);
  Polybius's originality was to **apply it to Rome** and to explain Roman success by it.
- His description of the Roman system is **schematic and incomplete** (the surviving text is
  fragmentary): he omits the different voting assemblies and most magistracies, treating only the
  consuls among office-holders — "a schematic plan of the main power structures, not a complete
  inventory."
- The cyclical *anacyclosis* is a stylised, deterministic model that does not fit the messier reality
  of constitutional change; and Polybius's own appeal to organic decline implied (pessimistically)
  that even Rome's mixed constitution must eventually decay.

## Afterlife

Book 6 gave the concept its long career in Western political thought: **[[cicero-republic-and-laws|
Cicero]]** (*De re publica*, now ingested — Rome as the real-world embodiment of the balanced
constitution and the *res publica* as a partnership in justice), then — after the Renaissance recovery
of Polybius — **Machiavelli** (*Discourses*), **Montesquieu**
(*The Spirit of the Laws*, and the separation of powers), and **John Adams** (*Defence of the
Constitutions of… the United States*, 1787), making the mixed/balanced constitution "a central element
in all modern discussion of the nature of republics" and a direct ancestor of the American system of
**checks and balances**.
