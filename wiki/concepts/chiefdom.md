# Chiefdom

A **chiefdom** is a middle-range political form between egalitarian village society and the
state: in **Earle's** definition (adopted by [[liu-chinese-neolithic-2004|Li Liu]]), "a
polity that organizes centrally a regional population in the thousands," with which "some
degree of heritable social ranking and economic stratification is characteristically
associated." Chiefdoms are the analytic category Liu applies to the
[[longshan-culture|Longshan]] polities of the [[yellow-river-valley|Yellow River]] on the eve
of the state.

## Diagnostic Features and Limits

Archaeologically, chiefdoms typically show a **two-to-three-tier settlement hierarchy** with
populations from the low thousands to tens of thousands, the ability to **mobilise tribute
labour** for monumental construction (the rammed-earth walls of [[taosi|Taosi]],
Guchengzhai, Wangchenggang), and **hereditary ranking** expressed in burials. Their defining
**limit** is logistical: elite control over resources and people operates within roughly a
**20 km radius / half-day journey**, because decision-making is **not internally
specialised**. This ceiling is what distinguishes chiefdoms from states, which break it
through **delegated decision making** (Spencer 1998) — administrative specialisation that
plants secondary centers at transport nodes (as [[erlitou-culture|Erlitou]] did).

Liu stresses that Longshan chiefdoms were **small and cyclical** — rising and collapsing
without producing a durable ruling apparatus (see [[early-china-state-formation]]).

## Network vs. Corporate Strategies

Drawing on **Blanton et al. (1996)**, Liu distinguishes two political orientations among
Longshan chiefdoms:

- **Network / individualising** (Shandong, southern Shanxi): power built through
  individual-centered exchange beyond the local group, **prestige goods**, rich burials, and
  individual ancestral cults. Vulnerable to disruption of exchange networks — hence cycles of
  expansion and collapse.
- **Corporate / group-oriented** (central and northern Henan): group interests over elite
  individuals, **corporate labour** in rammed-earth enclosures, communal ritual, minimal
  intra-group differentiation.

Liu's key claim: it was the **corporate, group-oriented** chiefdoms — maintaining internal
solidarity through military conflict over local resources — that gave rise to the early state,
not the flashier network chiefdoms. This is also why the most complex *network*-style
chiefdom, Taosi, collapsed while the less integrated corporate systems of central Henan and
the [[yiluo-basin|Yiluo basin]] produced [[erlitou-culture|Erlitou]].

## Critique

The chiefdom-to-state typology is contested in anthropology (some reject "chiefdom" as a
unitary stage). Liu uses it as a graded analytic measure tied to **[[settlement-archaeology|
settlement and mortuary data]]**, not as a rung on a unilineal ladder — consistent with her
multi-causal, non-sequential model. Compare the chiefdom-to-state transitions in
[[mesoamerican-state-formation]] and [[andean-state-formation]].
