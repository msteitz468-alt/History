## Definition and Scope

Chapter VI of the [[ibn-khaldun-muqaddimah]] includes a systematic survey of what Ibn Khaldun calls the *transmitted* or *traditional* sciences (*al-'ulum al-naqliyya*) — those that depend on "information based on the authority of the given religious law" and whose validity is grounded in revelation and transmission rather than independent rational inquiry. This page covers his treatment of those sciences in detail: Qur'anic readings and exegesis, hadith, jurisprudence and its principles, speculative theology, Sufism, dream interpretation, and the Arabic language sciences that undergird them all.

For the broader classification of sciences (both transmitted and philosophical), see [[ibn-khaldun-on-the-sciences]]. For his historiographical method, see [[ibn-khaldun-historiography]].

---

## The Qur'anic Sciences (Section 10)

Ibn Khaldun begins the transmitted sciences with Qur'anic reading (*qira'at*) and Qur'anic interpretation (*tafsir*). The two are related but distinct disciplines.

### Qur'anic Readings (*qira'at*)

The men around Muhammad transmitted the Qur'an in slightly varying ways — differences of pronunciation and, occasionally, of certain words. Seven specific reading traditions (the "Seven Readings") became established and continuously transmitted. Ibn Khaldun notes a historiographical dispute: some scholars denied that the *qira'at* were transmitted with true *mutawatir* (mass-corroborated) continuity, arguing that pronunciation cannot be fixed with absolute certainty; the majority maintained continuous transmission. He sides with the qualified majority position: the readings are continuously transmitted except for fine points of phonetic detail (length of long vowels, weakening of the *alif*) "because the ear is not able to determine how it must be done."

The discipline was particularly cultivated in al-Andalus. Ibn Khaldun traces its western florescence through Mujahid (ruler of Denia, patron of Qur'anic studies), Abu 'Amr ad-Dani (whose *Kitab at-Taysir* became the authoritative reference), and Abul-Qasim b. Firruh ash-Shatibi (who versified the whole subject for memorization). Alongside readings, Qur'anic orthography (*rasm*) — how letters are written in the Qur'anic text, which departs from standard usage in numerous places — forms an ancillary discipline.

### Qur'anic Interpretation (*tafsir*)

Ibn Khaldun distinguishes two modes of Qur'anic exegesis:

1. **Traditional exegesis** (*tafsir bi-l-ma'thur*): based on information from the early Muslims about why particular verses were revealed, which verses are abrogating and which abrogated, and what the Prophet explained. This is the essential mode; it can only be known through chains of transmission from the Companions and their successors.

   Ibn Khaldun's most historically interesting observation here concerns contamination: the early Muslim commentators, lacking books and scholarly tradition, turned to the People of the Book (Jews and Christians) for information about the beginnings of creation and cosmological matters. Much of this material came from Himyarite converts to Islam — figures such as Ka'b al-Ahbar, Wahb b. Munabbih, 'Abdallah b. Salim — who were "not capable of verifying the information they transmitted." As a result, "the Qur'an commentaries were filled with material of such tendencies transmitted on their authority" — a body of *isra'iliyyat* (Israelite lore) that cannot be verified or acted upon. Later scholars like Abu Muhammad b. 'Atiyah (Maghribi) and al-Qurtubi applied critical selection, retaining what was "most likely" and discarding unverifiable material.

2. **Linguistic-rhetorical exegesis** (*tafsir bi-l-diraya*): using lexicography and the discipline of *bayan* (rhetoric and style) to illuminate the Qur'anic text's literary dimensions. This mode became possible only after the linguistic sciences had been systematized. Its supreme representative is the *Kashshaf* of az-Zamakhshari — Ibn Khaldun praises it unreservedly for its rhetorical brilliance while warning against its Mu'tazilite theological commitments: "Competent orthodox scholars have, therefore, come to disregard his work and to warn everyone against its pitfalls. However, they admit that he is on firm ground in everything relating to language and style." A scholar with sound orthodox formation can use it safely for its linguistic content.

## The Science of Hadith (Section 11)

Ibn Khaldun describes the hadith sciences as those that govern the critical evaluation and transmission of Prophetic traditions. He is thorough and technically precise:

**Abrogating and abrogated traditions**: When two traditions conflict and one is demonstrably earlier, the later abrogates the earlier. Az-Zuhri called this "a baffling and impossible task"; ash-Shafi'i mastered it.

**Chains of transmission and transmitter criticism** (*rijal* and *jarh wa-ta'dil*): The validity of traditions depends on probability of truth, which depends on scrutinizing transmitters for reliability, memory, probity, and the mode of transmission (direct hearing, reading, written permission). Ibn Khaldun catalogues the classification system: *sahih* (sound), *hasan* (good), *da'if* (weak), and the subcategories — *mursal* (skipping the first transmitter), *munqati'* (omitting one link), *mu'dal* (omitting two links), *mu'allal* (affected by infirmity), *shadhdh* (singular), *gharib* (unusual), *munkar* (singular and suspect).

**The canonical collections**: Al-Bukhari's *Musnad as-Sahih* is the highest-ranked; Ibn Khaldun discusses its notorious puzzle of chapter headings that appear disconnected from the traditions beneath them, offering an explanation (transmitted via al-Ballafiqi from Ibn Bakkar) that resolves several apparent contradictions. Muslim b. al-Hajjaj's *Sahih* is preferred by Maghribi scholars because it avoids the repetitions and organizational peculiarities of al-Bukhari. Abu Dawud, at-Tirmidhi, and an-Nasa'i completed the five main collections by including "good" as well as "sound" traditions.

**Critical closure**: Ibn Khaldun makes an important methodological statement — at his time (late 14th century), the tradition sciences have effectively closed. The canonical scholars were "too capable and too firmly possessed of independent judgment to have neglected or omitted any tradition, so that it is impossible that some later scholar might discover one." What remains is textual correction and verification of chains back to the canonical authors, not new discoveries.

## Jurisprudence and Inheritance Laws (Section 12)

[[fiqh]] (jurisprudence) is "the knowledge of the rules of God which concern the actions of all responsible Muslims." Ibn Khaldun emphasizes the diversity of scholarly opinion that necessarily arises from interpreting the Qur'an and Sunnah on particular cases. The four law schools (Malikite, Hanafite, Shafi'ite, Hanbalite) represent the surviving traditions of what was once a much wider range of independent judgment (*ijtihad*).

He traces the historical evolution: early Muslims exercised direct, oral transmission and broad individual judgment. As Islam spread and the need for written systematization grew, the science crystallized. Ash-Shafi'i was "the first" to systematize the principles governing legal derivation. The Malikite tradition of the Maghrib and its canonical texts (the *Muwatta'*, the *Mudawwanah*) receive specific attention; Ibn Khaldun notes that Ibn Abi Zayd improved the *Mudawwanah* by correcting misplaced material.

Inheritance law (*fara'id*) is treated as a subdivision of jurisprudence with its own technical methods.

## The Principles of Jurisprudence (*usul al-fiqh*, Section 13)

The science of jurisprudential principles concerns the *sources* and *methods* of legal derivation: how to extract legal rulings from the Qur'an and Sunnah through analogy (*qiyas*), scholarly consensus (*ijma'*), and interpretive techniques such as abrogation. Ibn Khaldun follows the standard Ash'arite framing: the four sources are the Qur'an, Sunnah, scholarly consensus, and analogical reasoning.

He observes that recent scholars have extensively mixed the problems of *usul al-fiqh* with those of kalam (speculative theology) and logic, producing hybrid works of great technical complexity. This he regards as a regrettable tendency toward auxiliary science inflation — the methods of derivation should remain instrumental, not become ends in themselves. See [[ibn-khaldun-on-the-sciences]] for his general argument against prolonged study of auxiliary sciences.

## Speculative Theology (*kalam*, Section 14)

[[kalam]] is "a science that involves arguing with logical proofs in defense of the articles of faith and refuting innovators who deviate in their dogmas from the early Muslims." Ibn Khaldun provides a historical survey:

- **Origins**: The Mu'tazila introduced the use of rational proof and philosophical categories into theological discourse, generating heresies (on the createdness of the Qur'an, the divine attributes, and free will) that required orthodox response.
- **Ash'arite response**: Abu al-Hasan al-Ash'ari and al-Baqillani developed systematic orthodox *kalam* to refute the Mu'tazila with their own rational methods. This became the dominant school.
- **Later scholasticism**: More recent scholars (al-Baydawi, Fakhr al-Din Ibn al-Khatib) merged the problems of *kalam* with those of philosophy (Avicennan physics, metaphysics), producing works that are methodologically confused — theology's subject is defending articles of faith against heretics, while philosophy's subject is investigating existence as it is. These are different enterprises.

Ibn Khaldun's evaluative conclusion is pointed: **"This science is not something that is necessary to the contemporary student."** The heresies it was designed to refute have been defeated; the books suffice to preserve the arguments. The merging of *kalam* with philosophy has produced confusion without corresponding benefit. See [[kalam]] and [[developed-kalam-tradition]] for further context.

## Sufism (Section 16)

Ibn Khaldun's treatment of [[sufism]] is the most nuanced and analytically important section on the religious sciences. He distinguishes four legitimate topics of Sufi science:

1. **Pious exertion and self-mortification** (*mujahada*): the discipline of the soul through fasting, prayer, and withdrawal from the world, enabling direct perception of truth. Ibn Khaldun regards this as legitimate and grounded in the practice of early Muslims.

2. **The removal of the veil of sense perception**: the Sufi claim that through sustained practice the adept achieves direct perception of spiritual realities. Ibn Khaldun accepts this as real but warns against premature or unguided pursuit.

3. **Acts of divine grace** (*karamat*): miraculous gifts granted to saints. Ibn Khaldun accepts their reality within the framework of Islamic orthodoxy.

4. **Sufi technical vocabulary** (*istilahat*): the specialized language Sufis use for their experiences, which may appear heterodox to outsiders but is internally coherent.

**What Ibn Khaldun rejects** is the doctrine of "absolute oneness" (*wahdat al-wujud*) associated with Ibn al-Arabi, Ibn Sab'in, and the tradition of "philosophical Sufism." He regards this as an illicit importation of Neoplatonic and extremist Shi'a incarnationist ideas into Sufism — a confusion between the mystical path toward union with the divine (legitimate) and metaphysical monism (a heresy). He writes that these later Sufis "have gone beyond the path of the early Sufis" and entered territory that is "contrary to the religious law."

He also examines Sufi *malama* ("blame") practices and the sociological relationship between Sufism and [[tariqa-orders]] (the organized brotherhoods), though he does not use the term *tariqa* systematically — the institutionalization of orders was still in progress in his lifetime. See [[early-sufism]] and [[philosophical-sufism]] for pre-Ibn Khaldun context.

## Dream Interpretation (Section 17)

Ibn Khaldun places dream interpretation (*ta'bir al-ru'ya*) among the transmitted sciences because its principles are grounded in Prophetic statements about true dreams. True dreams are direct perceptions from the spiritual world — the soul, temporarily freed from the constraints of sense during sleep, receives impressions from the "world of truth." Confused dreams (*adghat ahlam*) are merely the products of imagination replaying stored sensory impressions.

The science of interpretation converts the symbolic language of dream imagery into its referents in the waking world. Ibn Khaldun regards it as a genuine, if minor, science — its validity is established by Prophetic tradition, its method is essentially one of learned symbolic decoding.

## The Arabic Language Sciences (Sections 44–51, in the broader Ch. VI survey)

Ibn Khaldun treats the linguistic sciences as the essential auxiliary foundation for all the religious sciences. Because the Qur'an and Sunnah are in Arabic, anyone who would master the religious sciences must first command the linguistic sciences. He identifies four pillars:

### Grammar (*nahw*)
The most important of the four, because grammatical case endings (*i'rab*) determine meaning in ways that cannot be inferred from context alone. The science originated as a rescue operation: as non-Arabs entered Islam and linguistic habits eroded, Abul-Aswad ad-Du'ali (advised by 'Ali) first fixed the rules; al-Khalil b. Ahmad al-Farahidi perfected them; Sibawayh codified them in *The Book* (*al-Kitab*), the definitive text of the science. Ibn Khaldun traces the bifurcation into Basrian and Kufian schools, and notes Ibn Hisham's *Mughni fi l-i'rab* as the most sophisticated recent work.

### Lexicography (*lugha*)
Explains the conventional meanings of Arabic words, also a rescue science prompted by semantic corruption. Al-Khalil's *Kitab al-'Ayn* — organized by articulation point rather than standard alphabetical order, covering all possible letter combinations — was the foundational work. Ibn Khaldun provides an extended account of its combinatorial method (calculating all two-, three-, four-, and five-consonant root combinations). Al-Jawhari's *Sihah* organized by final letter became the standard reference; Ibn Sidah's *Muhkam* (Spain) was a comprehensive alternative. Ath-Tha'alibi's *Fiqh al-Lughah* addressed the subtler problem of usage-specific vocabulary that cannot be inferred from general meanings.

### Rhetoric and Style (*bayan*)
The most sophisticated of the language sciences, concerned with the conditions of speech that make expression appropriate to its situation (*balaghah*), with metaphor and metonymy (*bayan* proper), and with rhetorical figures (*badi'*). Ibn Khaldun notes that the East cultivated *balaghah* and *bayan* more thoroughly than the Maghrib, which tended to focus on the easier science of *badi'* — a tendency he attributes to the greater civilizational density of the East creating demand for luxury sciences. The key text is as-Sakkaki's *Miftah*, abridged and transmitted by al-Qazwini's *Talkhis*.

The *fruit* of this science — Ibn Khaldun's phrase — is understanding the **inimitability of the Qur'an** (*i'jaz*): the demonstration, through rhetorical analysis, that the Qur'an's language surpasses human capacity. "Something of it may be understood by those who have a taste for it as the result of their contact with the (Arabic) language and their possession of the habit of it."

### Literature (*adab*)
"Expert knowledge of the poetry and history of the Arabs as well as the possession of some knowledge regarding every science." Literature is distinctive in having no formal object of study — its purpose is identical with its fruit, namely, the acquisition of a good habit in Arabic prose and poetry. See [[adab-arabic-literary-culture]] for the broader adab tradition. The four canonical foundation texts of adab are: Ibn Qutaybah's *Adab al-Katib*, al-Mubarrad's *Kamil*, al-Jahiz's *Bayan wa-Tabyin*, and Abu 'Ali al-Qali's *Nawadir*.

## Ibn Khaldun's Critical Principles in the Religious Sciences

Across all these sciences, several critical principles recur:

1. **Contamination awareness**: The most important is recognition that early tafsir was contaminated by unverifiable Israelite material. Scholars must actively filter this out.

2. **Transmission skepticism**: The elaborate hadith classification system exists precisely because not all traditions are equally reliable. Chain verification (*isnad* criticism) and text plausibility (*matn* evaluation) together determine validity.

3. **Auxiliary/core distinction**: The linguistic sciences are valuable but must remain instrumental. When grammar, lexicography, or *usul al-fiqh* grow so elaborate that they consume the student's energies before reaching the core religious sciences, they have defeated their own purpose. See Section 37 of Ch. VI.

4. **Civilizational embedding**: All sciences, including religious ones, are crafts (*sana'i'*). They require sedentary culture, institutional support, and chains of teachers. When civilizations contract — as with al-Qayrawan and Córdoba — their religious scientific traditions also decay. This sociological point is Ibn Khaldun's most original contribution to the philosophy of science.

## Historiography

Ibn Khaldun's survey of the religious sciences is the most comprehensive medieval Islamic meta-analysis of these fields as a system. It synthesizes the encyclopedic tradition (going back to al-Farabi's *Ihsa' al-'Ulum*) with a sociological framework unique to the Muqaddimah. His critical evaluation of specific works — praising the *Kashshaf* while warning against its theology, noting the closure of hadith scholarship, dismissing kalam as no longer necessary — reflects both command of the tradition and remarkable intellectual independence.
