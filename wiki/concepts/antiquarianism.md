# Antiquarianism

## Definition and Scope

**Antiquarianism** was the pre-professional study of the material and documentary remains of the past — monuments, coins, inscriptions, manuscripts, customs, and topography — pursued by educated amateurs ("antiquaries") chiefly between the sixteenth and early nineteenth centuries. Distinct from narrative history (which organized the past into stories of states and great men), antiquarianism privileged the collection, description, and classification of physical survivals. It was a principal ancestor of modern **archaeology** and of local and material history.

## The 16th–17th Century British Tradition

British antiquarianism coalesced around the project of recovering the nation's pre-Roman and Romano-British past — a project shaped by Renaissance humanism, Protestant historiography, and dynastic competition between the peoples of the British Isles. Its founding moment is conventionally placed with **John Leland**, appointed by Henry VIII as the king's antiquary and tasked with surveying the nation's monuments and manuscripts. Leland placed the Druids at the head of a tradition of British learning, but his work remained unpublished when he went permanently insane around 1545, leaving his manuscript to circulate privately among contemporaries ([[sources/hutton-blood-and-mistletoe-2009|Hutton, Blood and Mistletoe (2009)]]).

Leland's successor as England's leading antiquary, **John Bale**, set the ambivalent pattern that would characterise the British engagement with ancient monuments for the next century: he first condemned the Druids as evidence of pagan-superstition analogous to Catholic corruption, then reversed himself after reading continental scholarship, praising them as contributors to early British learning. The **Holinshed Chronicles** (1577), assembled under the co-ordination of Raphael Holinshed with sections on ancient religion written by William Harrison, presented two incompatible mythologies about the Druids to a vernacular English audience — their pious founding under the legendary King Druiyus and their later degeneration into human sacrifice — without reconciling them. These tensions characterised British antiquarianism throughout the period: the same monument or institution could serve Protestant apologetics, royal patronage, or nationalist rivalry depending on how it was framed.

The most consequential single antiquarian project of the period was **William Camden**'s *Britannia*, first published in Latin in 1586 at the urging of the Flemish geographer Abraham Ortelius. Camden's original work confined itself strictly to the classical record on Druids; as successive editions appeared and the book was translated into English in 1610, Camden's treatment expanded and became more favourable, culminating in the (erroneous) claim — based on a mistranslation of Origen's Greek — that the Druids had been monotheists who prepared the British for Christianity. Other scholars spotted the mistake within decades, but it was embedded in a work of very high prestige and repeated for over a century.

A complementary current ran through early Stuart England, where scholars associated with the authority of Parliament connected Druids to the origins of common law and representative assemblies. Sir Edward Coke declared that the common law had been nurtured by Druids; John Selden, in his notes to Drayton's *Poly-Olbion* (1612) and in a history of English law, praised the Druids as guardians of customary law who met in regular conventions — in effect the ancient British ancestors of Parliament. This politicised reading coexisted with purely hostile accounts; the period saw no consensus, only a proliferation of contradictory appraisals.

Field antiquarianism — the physical investigation of monuments in the landscape — gathered pace in the mid-seventeenth century. It was given institutional form by the **Royal Society** (founded 1660), which formally directed [[actors/aubrey-john|John Aubrey]] and his friend James Long in July 1663 to consider excavations at [[places/avebury|Avebury]]. The first edition of **Aylett Sammes**'s history of ancient Britain (1676) introduced the first modern visual image of a Druid to English readers — drawn ultimately from Conrad Celtis's 1490s account of stone statues at a Bavarian monastery — as well as the first printed image of Caesar's wicker man, thereby giving visual form to both the benevolent and the terrifying stereotypes simultaneously. Edmund Gibson's revised Camden *Britannia* (1695), assembled using a network of some thirty contributors from across Britain and coordinated from Oxford, represents the culmination of this generation's effort: it incorporated Aubrey's unpublished manuscript, the Welsh and Scottish sections by Edward Lhuyd and others, and a first systematic comparative approach to British monuments.

## Relevance to the Druid Revival

In Britain, antiquaries were central to the [[processes/druid-revival|Druid Revival]]: it was field antiquaries — above all [[actors/aubrey-john|John Aubrey]] and then [[actors/stukeley-william|William Stukeley]] — who detached [[places/stonehenge|Stonehenge]], [[places/avebury|Avebury]], and the megaliths from Roman or Danish authorship and attributed them to native British Druids. Antiquarian field survey, speculative reconstruction, and a taste for the picturesque and the patriotic together produced the Druid-megalith association that dominated British imaginations for over a century. The eventual displacement of antiquarianism by professional, evolutionary archaeology and the [[concepts/three-age-system|three-age system]] in the mid-nineteenth century also stripped the Druids of their scholarly standing.

## Limitations and Critique

Antiquarianism mixed genuine empirical observation (accurate plans, the recording of monuments since destroyed) with credulous speculation, forgery, and nationalist projection. Its lack of stratigraphic method and chronological control left it unable to date monuments, allowing imaginative attributions to flourish. Later scholarship has both criticized its speculative excesses and rehabilitated its descriptive records as invaluable evidence for vanished sites.
