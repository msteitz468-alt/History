# Taxpayer Identity

The political construction of "the taxpayer" as a distinct, virtuous, and often exclusive category of citizen whose consent is required for legitimate government action, in contrast to non-taxpaying or dependent populations ("tax eaters"). In American history the category has frequently been racialized and used to justify limits on democracy.

## Definition and Scope

"Taxpayer" functions simultaneously as:
- A fiscal descriptor (one who pays taxes).
- A moral/political identity (responsible, productive, entitled to voice).
- A gatekeeping device (only "taxpayers" should decide on public spending or enjoy full rights).

The concept is analytically useful for tracking how fiscal categories are mobilized to draw boundaries around "the people."

## Historical Uses

- **Redemption and Jim Crow**: White "taxpayer leagues" and paramilitary organizations framed Black political participation and Reconstruction tax policies (higher property taxes on plantations to fund universal schooling) as illegitimate expropriation by non-taxpayers. State constitutional conventions and poll taxes were justified as protecting taxpayers from being outvoted by those who "paid no taxes."
- **20th century**: The identity is invoked against welfare recipients, public employees, and later against immigrants or the poor. "Taxpayers' money" becomes a rhetorical weapon against social programs.
- **Contemporary**: Explicit and implicit use in debates over voting rights, felon disenfranchisement, school funding, and any expansion of the welfare state.

## Relation to Broader Arguments

Williamson ties taxpayer identity politics directly to the contraction of democracy. Where the colonial and Reconstruction moments expanded the set of people entitled to tax themselves for collective purposes, the "taxpayer" frame contracts it — often along lines of race and class — and delegitimizes the fiscal demands of the broader citizenry.

## Contestation

Proponents treat taxpayer status as a neutral, objective qualification for voice on fiscal matters. Critics argue that the category is historically constructed, selectively applied (large corporations and the wealthy have often paid low effective rates while claiming taxpayer standing), and functions as a successor to older property or racial qualifications for citizenship.
