# Medieval Siegecraft (1000–1300)

Analytical concept for the theory and practice of attacking and defending fortifications in Western Europe and the Latin East, 1000–1300. Siege was the dominant form of large-scale military action; no single technological development altered the fundamental balance between attack and defence; success hinged on political will, resources, numbers, isolation of the target, and the quality of the garrison rather than any decisive engine. Grounded exclusively in France Ch. 9.

## Definition and Scope

Siegecraft in this period encompassed the full range of techniques for reducing castles and cities: blockade, assault, mining, artillery, and engineering works (towers, penthouses, camps). It was "simply a very specialized form of battle" that "did what battle in other societies was designed to do – to destroy the basic strength of the enemy and acquire it for your own use."

**Emic perspective**: Sieges were described in terms of camps, engines (*mangonels*, *petrariae*, *trebuchets*), mining, and storming. Contemporary terminology for engines was inconsistent and often confusing.

**Etic perspective**: France stresses continuity and limits. "No machine in this period actually altered the balance of advantage between attack and defence." The best method was always "to isolate it with overwhelming numbers".

## Fortifications: Castles and Cities

"The fortifications of cities were not, in principle, very different from those of castles." Milan (1158) incorporated a Roman arch; Alessandria earth-and-timber; many Palestinian coastal cities had double walls inherited from Roman past. Advanced features (machicolations, angled arrow-slits, sally-gates, barbicans, steep talus, stone-lined ditches) appeared at Carcassonne, Oxford, Aigues-Mortes, Conway, Caernarfon, rebuilt Acre, and Belvoir-model sites.

Citadels often formed distinct inner strongpoints (Tower of David at Jerusalem; Château de Narbonne at Toulouse; Templar castle at Acre suburb Montmusard). "Citadels on the perimeter sometimes strengthened the defences of a city, although Antioch was an exception." At Toulouse 1217 the citadel was cut off from the city proper.

"In the end it was the garrison, not the walls, that mattered."

## Methods of Attack

- **Surprise and dissension**: Best means. Frederick II seized Vicenza 1236 via internal sympathizers and absent troops. Moissac 1212 fell when citizens lost will despite willing garrison. Parma 1247 defected via exiles' sally.

- **Assault**: "If surprise was impossible, a determined assault well pressed home was often enough" (Tonbridge 1088 stormed day 2; Winchcombe 1144 under "cloud of arrows"; Jerusalem 1099 attempted with single ladder).

- **Psychological warfare**: Impaling heads (Nicaea, Antioch, Tiberias 1187); hanging bodies (Ascalon 1153); mutilation (Corno Vecchio garrison hands cut off).

- **Rams, penthouses ("cats", "sows")**: Covered approach and battering. At Jerusalem 1099 ram broke outer wall then burned for tower; Acre ram burned by garrison. "cats" of boiled leather at Toulouse 1211.

- **Mining**: "the most consistently successful tactic". Deep saps at Rochester (1215, southeast keep corner undermined then props burned with fat of 40 pigs), Bedford (1224), Crac des Chevaliers (1271), Marqab (1285). Countermeasures: heavy batters collapsed shallow mines; cisterns for flooding. At Nicaea breach filled overnight; at Dover/Acre improvised defences. Success depended on soil (easy at Dover; useless in marshy Caerlaverock 1300). Countermining at Acre blocked French efforts.

- **Siege towers**: Built to overawe and provide fire base or assault platform. Mobile examples at Jerusalem 1099 (Godfrey's brought close; bridge improvised); Lisbon (28m then 25m high, Pisan engineer); Bothwell 1301 (transported in sections, covered in hides). Limitations: terrain (steep slopes at 'Arqa, Jerusalem Zion Gate); fire (soaked in vinegar at Jerusalem; wet hides at Lisbon); weight/clumsiness; cost. "Siege-towers were remarkable structures, but they were not a certain solution... Above all, they were costly and justified only for major objectives."

- **Fortified camps**: Provided shelter, protection from sallies, logistic base, and coercion (Victoria only four bow-shots from Parma 1247). Essential for major operations; First Crusade built none at Jerusalem but elaborate works at Acre.

## Artillery

"The traction-trebuchet was the dominant form of artillery in our period. It was a device that originated in China and was transmitted to Europe by about the ninth century via the Arab world." Beam pivoted between uprights; short pulling end (ratio ~1:5); sling on throwing end. "petraria and mangana indicate sizeable examples and mangonella and tormenta lesser ones." Range ~120m for 15kg at Caerphilly replica; shorter (50–60m) vs upper works. "machines were always very vulnerable to a sudden sally".

Counterweight trebuchet ("trebuchet", trabuchetum, tribok) appears suddenly ~1199 (Codagnellus at Castelnuovo Bocca d'Adda). Load end wound down against massive pivoted counterweight; greater accuracy and power (Danish replica 47kg at 100m). French evidence at Toulouse 1217 ("ran to the ropes and wound the trebuchets"; "released the ropes"; "set dressed stone in the slings"); 1211 Castelnaudary demolished tower and hall with single stones. "the counterweight-trebuchet did not radically alter the balance of advantage between attack and defence." Walls built stronger (ponds at Kenilworth/Caerphilly); specialists needed; ponderous; limited range meant operators needed protection; missiles had to be selected carefully.

Defenders used artillery effectively from towers (Jerusalem 1099 14 machines vs Toulouse tower; Milan 1158 countered Barbarossa's machine on Roman arch).

"No machine in this period actually altered the balance of advantage between attack and defence."

## Engineers and Infrastructure

"Western armies were occasional bodies, lacking a continuous existence which could nurture the special skills needed to build, operate and develop siege-equipment." Rare early specialists (Robert of Bellême, Gaston of Béarn, Geoffrey of Anjou). Italian cities (wealth, maritime skills) produced engineers (William Ricau at Jerusalem 1099; Guintelmus of Milan; Marchesius of Crema).

Thirteenth century: "engineers could enjoy great importance". Elias of Oxford, Urricus (knighted by John), Masters Osbert and Bertram (Henry III), Master Robert of Ulm (Caerlaverock 1300), Master James of St George (Stirling 1304), Jean de Mézos (knighted by St Louis), Nicoloso (James I of Aragon). Frederick II kept Calamandrinus in shackles. "By the end of the twelfth century, such people had become so important that the writer Guiot de Provins was dismayed at the prospect of their dominating war." Yet "they were rewarded much more modestly than the traditional military caste: in war, status and conformity to the canons of chivalrous behaviour counted for more."

## Political and Resource Dimension

"Successful siege could achieve all that victory in battle offered and perhaps more, but at the price of much the same risks." Required "good leadership, skill, determination, organization and plentiful supplies – difficult enough to achieve when armies were transient."

"It was pre-eminently kings who could achieve the conditions for success in siegewarfare." Strong central authority (England, France) absorbed castles into political system and reduced need for sieges. Fragmented Germany saw frequent but small-scale, unsuccessful sieges. Italy dominated by cities made siege common. Crusader East reduced by epic Islamic sieges (Acre 1291 with ~100 machines).

"Sieges were simply a very specialized form of battle." No development "altered the balance between defence and attack."

## Historiography and Limitations

Older views sometimes over-emphasised decisive engines or linear progress. France's analysis shows technical sophistication (counterweight trebuchet, concentric designs) but persistent stalemate: "the best way to seize a fortification was to isolate it with overwhelming numbers". Change was incremental and civilian-derived (engineers from outside military sphere).

**Emic/etic note**: No single contemporary term equates to "siegecraft" as systematic doctrine; practices were pragmatic responses to the proprietorial landscape of castles and cities. The etic model highlights why poliorcetics remained the area of greatest technical effort without producing revolutionary shifts.

Limitations: Strongest for western Europe and Latin East. Muslim East showed heavier use of counterweight trebuchets and enormous towers by 13th c. (Baybars' Tower at Crac). The model does not cover post-1300 gunpowder developments.
