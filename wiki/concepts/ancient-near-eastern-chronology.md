# Ancient Near Eastern Chronology (Method)

How absolute dates are constructed for the Bronze Age Near East and Egypt — the
scaffolding on which every event date in the period depends. Set out
systematically in [[edwards-cah-v1p1-1970|CAH Vol. I, Part 1]] (Ch. VI), where the
editors commissioned three specialists to fix a uniform scheme: **W. C. Hayes**
(Egypt), **M. B. Rowton** (Western Asia), **F. H. Stubbings** (Aegean). The
chapter is one of the most durable parts of the volume — its *methods* remain
standard even where its *dates* have shifted. See
[[cambridge-ancient-history-methodology]] for how the house chronology functions.

## The Egyptian Anchor: Sothic Dating

Egyptian chronology is anchored by the **Sothic cycle**: the Egyptian civil
calendar of 365 days (no leap year) drifted against the solar year by one day
every four years, so the heliacal rising of **Sothis (Sirius)** returned to the
same calendar date only every ~1,460 years. Recorded Sothic risings (e.g. in
year 7 of Senusret III; in the reign noted for 1526/25 BCE) give fixed points
from which reign-lengths are counted backward and forward. **Lunar dates** in
dated texts provide secondary checks. Egypt's relatively continuous king-lists
make it the **best-anchored** chronology of the ancient world and the reference
against which Western Asian and Aegean dates are cross-tied.

## The Western Asian Apparatus (Rowton)

Mesopotamia lacks an astronomical anchor as clean as Egypt's, so its chronology is
assembled from interlocking documentary series:

- **Assyrian eponym-lists (līmu)**: each year named after an official; a single
  error displaces the count by at most one year, making these the **backbone of
  Assyrian chronology**. Rowton shows the Assyrian king-lists are effectively
  lists of eponym-periods.
- **King-lists** (Assyrian; Babylonian King-Lists A and B; the **Sumerian
  king-list**): reliable in their later, near-contemporary portions, unreliable
  and inflated for the earliest dynasties.
- **Year-names (date-formulae)**: Babylonian years named for a king's notable act;
  for the First Dynasty of Babylon the year-name lists are exceptionally full and
  compensate for gaps in the king-lists.
- **Synchronisms**: cross-references between rulers (e.g. Mari–Babylon–Eshnunna
  links in the Mari archives; the capture of Larsa by Hammurabi in his 30th year)
  knit the regional sequences together.

## Radiocarbon's Limited Role (in 1970)

The chapter treats **radiocarbon** as a check of last resort for the historical
periods: useful for prehistory but, before calibration, too imprecise to compete
with the documentary scheme for the 2nd–3rd millennia. (Tree-ring calibration and
high-precision dendro-anchoring later transformed this balance — a key way modern
chronology has advanced beyond CAH I.1.)

## The Central Problem: The Date of the First Dynasty of Babylon

The single greatest uncertainty is the absolute date of the **First Dynasty of
Babylon** (Hammurabi's dynasty), fixed from **observations of Venus recorded under
Ammiṣaduqa** (its tenth king). The Venus data admit *several* solutions ~56/64
years apart, generating the **high / middle / low chronology** problem. Rowton
adopts the **middle chronology** (end of the dynasty = **1595 BCE**, Hammurabi
~1792–1750), naming **1651** (high) as a serious alternative. This single choice
ripples through every Bronze Age date in the volume — and is the subject of its own
[[mesopotamian-chronology|controversy page]].

## Liverani: two chronologies, and a verdict on the short chronology

[[liverani-ancient-near-east-2014|Liverani (2014)]] frames the field's two dating systems
as complementary but not to be conflated: an **archaeological chronology** of round
approximations (relative stratigraphy and radiocarbon, expressed BP) that prevails for
prehistory, and a **historical chronology** of precise regnal numbers (reconstructing
ancient dating systems and transposing them into ours) that prevails for the literate
periods. He notes radiocarbon must be **dendro-calibrated** because ¹⁴C production is not
uniform (uncalibrated dates run too young for ancient periods, especially 2000–7000 BCE),
and that Mesopotamian king-lists contain *deliberate* distortions — kings omitted for
political reasons, contemporary dynasties listed as sequential, mythological reign-lengths.
On the [[mesopotamian-chronology|First-Dynasty-of-Babylon problem]] he adopts the **middle
chronology** ([[hammurabi|Hammurabi]] 1792–1750 BCE) and explicitly rejects the **short
chronology** (1728–1696) as a "hopefully passing trend" because it compresses the Hittite
and Syrian sequences unacceptably — a useful datapoint on where current opinion sits.

## Why This Page Is Marked `contested: yes`

The *methods* are settled, but the *absolute anchoring* of the 2nd-millennium and
earlier is not: the high/middle/low choice, the reliability of the Sumerian
king-list's early dynasties, and the integration of radiocarbon all remain live.
Treat CAH's specific BCE dates as the **middle-chronology** convention, not as
fact, and flag conflicts with later sources to [[mesopotamian-chronology]].
