# Economic Aspects of the Cold War, 1962–1975

US objectives during the Cold War were "to prevent Soviet attacks on the United States and its allies and to prevent the spread of Communism as a political and economic system to other countries, whether by force or by threat, subversion, persuasion, or bribery." The "principal instruments for preventing the spread of Communism by nonmilitary means involved building an international economic system conducive to economic prosperity; engaging in persuasion, providing incentives, and occasionally imposing economic sanctions; and, not least, promoting a robust US economy that could serve as a stimulant to others and as a beacon for the benefits of a free, enterprise-based, market-oriented economy." (Cooper, Ch. 3)

## Economic Policy as Complement to Deterrence

"Economic policy, broadly interpreted, was an essential complement to the policy of deterrence. In the end, the Soviet Union was not defeated in military combat, but rather it collapsed because of internal economic weaknesses that were increasingly evident to the Soviet people and, more gradually, to their leaders." This marked "a sharp contrast to the robust performance of economies in the orbit of the United States and its allies – first in Western Europe and in Japan, followed soon by southern Europe and the four Asian tigers (South Korea, Taiwan, Hong Kong, and Singapore)." "It was increasingly evident that Communism did not work well economically – it did not deliver significantly higher standards of living to ordinary people." "After some initial successes, the Communist countries became mired in inflexible systems of resource allocation and low levels of innovation in a world increasingly dominated by rapid technological advances." "Gradually those countries in the non-Communist world that experimented with central planning and control drew away from it – including eventually even China and Vietnam." (Cooper, Ch. 3 "Economic aspects of the Cold War, 1962–1975")

In the 1960s "Communism was still seen as an aggressive, vigorous ideology," with "nearly every year" bringing "some perceived new Communist threat" (Cuba 1960, Congo/Lumumba, Berlin Wall 1961, Cuba missiles 1962, China-India 1962, Dominican Republic 1964, Indonesia 1965, Syria/ Six Days War 1967, Czechoslovakia 1968, Allende Chile 1971, Portugal/Angola 1975, Vietnam throughout). "President Richard M. Nixon could say to his senior officials in 1971 'the impressive thing about the Communist leaders is their total absolute conviction that they're going to win, and their determination to do everything to win.'"

## World Economic Performance

"The 1960s was a decade of high global economic growth, perhaps the highest decadal growth in history." US growth interrupted by recessions 1960–61, 1970–71, 1975; global output declined after 1974 oil price shock. "Continental Western Europe and Japan, in particular, experienced extraordinary growth." Imports into the US "grew by 170 percent during the 1960s, from $15 billion in 1960 to $40 billion in 1970."

The USSR and Warsaw Pact "were also growing rapidly." Official Soviet figures showed growth of "10.1 percent a year during the 1950s, declining to a still high 7.0 percent during the 1960s and to 5.3 percent during the 1970s." CIA estimates: 6.0, 5.1, and 3.7 percent. "On either measure, the USSR in the early 1960s was on a roll, reflected in the frequent exuberant boasts of the Soviet premier, Khrushchev." Growth declined; "inability to innovate, or even to absorb foreign innovations, loss of discipline among Soviet workers, and failure to maintain installed equipment and to scrap obsolete equipment" cited as causes.

Foreign trade "did not fit comfortably into national central planning." Comecon formed 1949 with "socialist division of labor" concept, but "never received enthusiastically by many of Comecon's members" and "trade within Comecon was limited." "Trade with non-Communist countries... was even more limited." During détente, "Exports by industrialized countries to the Comecon countries... grew from $2.8 billion in 1960 to $8.7 billion in 1970 to $34 billion in 1975." Share of market-oriented countries in Comecon imports rose from 27% (1960) to 46% (1975). Soviet hard currency from oil exports, whose price rose sharply 1974.

"the USSR grew faster than the United States to 1970, raising Soviet GDP on one estimate from 35 percent of the US level in 1950 to 44 percent in 1975, before declining to 34 percent in 1990." "In the Soviet Union, 15 to 17 percent of GDP was devoted to equipping and supporting the military, and an even larger and growing portion was devoted to investment, which by all accounts was used very inefficiently." "Soviet standards of living increased respectably during the 1960s." Khrushchev after 1956 Hungarian revolt "tried to cut down on military spending and redirected resources into consumer goods"; Brezhnev "increased military spending in the mid-1960s."

## US Economic and Security Policies

President Kennedy "was convinced that US policy toward the USSR, and toward the world, must be based on a robust US economy." He proposed "significant trade liberalization," investment tax credit, and "a significant reduction in income taxes" (enacted 1964). Defense expenditures: from $45 billion (8.8% GDP) 1960 to $49 billion (7.1%) 1965; Vietnam buildup to $77 billion (8.8%) 1968, then down to 5% by 1976. International affairs spending expanded (foreign assistance, Peace Corps, USIA). NASA rose to peak $5.1 billion in 1965; moon landing 1969 "to reestablish the United States as being on the frontiers of technology in the eyes of Americans and of those around the world – not least in the Soviet Union."

## Trade and Financial Policy Toward Communist Countries

US trade policy toward Communists (absent specific sanctions): high Smoot-Hawley tariffs (1930) without MFN treatment (except Yugoslavia, later Poland). "A combination of central planning in the USSR... and high import duties into the United States assured little trade." Export controls on "military goods and of 'strategic' goods" via Export Control Act 1949; COCOM with West Europe and Japan. Generally "Communist countries were denied access to these credits" (CCC, Exim Bank, etc.).

Specific sanctions (26 times 1960–75, plus UN): against North Korea, China (from 1950, relaxed 1972), Cuba (1960 onward, persists), GDR (considered 1961), UAR/Egypt (1963–65 credits cut), North Vietnam (1964 full embargo), Kampuchea (1975). Rewards: MFN to Poland 1960, Laos aid resumed 1962 (suspended 1975), Romania MFN 1975.

Détente trade: 1972 grain sale $750 million to USSR; October 1972 trade agreement for MFN (would cut average tariffs ~64%). Jackson-Vanik amendment tied MFN to emigration freedom; Soviets "backed out of the 1972 trade agreement, and Soviet goods never received MFN treatment." "The Jackson–Vanik amendment is an example of the ability of a determined Congress to thwart an American president's foreign policy."

## Trade and Financial Policies Toward Allies

Kennedy proposed "a bold new round of trade negotiations" (Kennedy Round, concluded 1967). "The first three [benefits] concerned benefits to the US economy, including enhancing its capacity to bear burdens of defense." Remaining reasons "promoting the strength and unity of the West, proving the superiority of free choice, aiding developing nations, and maintaining US leadership of the free world – were suffused with references to competition with the 'Sino-Soviet world'."

## Historiography / Significance (from source)

The chapter highlights that "the battle of ideas was not decisive; but the cumulative experience was increasingly difficult to ignore. In the end, this cumulative comparative experience was at least as important in preventing the spread of Communism as was deterrence." "Economic prosperity in the West made it easier to carry the financial burdens of defense, deterrence, and containment."
