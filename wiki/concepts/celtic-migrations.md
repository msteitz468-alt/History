# Celtic Migrations

## Definition and Scope

The Celtic (or Galatian/Gallic) migrations of c. 450–50 BCE refer to the movements of La Tène-associated peoples from central European homelands outward into northern Italy (Cisalpine Gaul/Po valley), the Balkans and Greece, Asia Minor (Galatia), and with mercenary service extending to Egypt and beyond. These are distinguished from earlier Urnfield/Hallstatt dynamics and from the later developed oppida world. The phenomenon is attested in classical texts (Polybius, Livy, Pausanias, Caesar) and by La Tène material culture spreads, with ongoing debate on scale (mass folk movement vs. warrior elites + cultural diffusion) and drivers.

## Key Claims Grounded in Cunliffe 2018 Range (Ch. 6–8 context, eastern fringes, Po Valley art links, classic culture of migration period)

- **Italy / Po valley settlement**: "the continuous threat created by the Celtic settlement in the Po valley were instrumental in the honing of Rome’s military competence." (warfare and society 211–212). The initial invasion in the early fourth century and ongoing presence shaped Roman military development. Polybius presents Celts as volatile contrast to Roman steadiness.

- **Greece and Delphi**: References to the horde that entered Asia Minor in 278 BC having camped "in its shadow waiting to pillage Delphi" (celts and others on the eastern fringes 209). Brennos is noted as "Brennos fleeing from Delphi" among failed leaders who committed suicide (warfare and society 230).

- **Asia Minor / Galatians**: In 189 BC "in Galatia in 189 bc, when the Tolistobogii and Trocmi took off their clothes before battle with the Romans, exposing their podgy white bodies" (warfare and society 223). Celts retained identity; specific tribes named. Earlier "the horde that entered Asia Minor in 278".

- **Egypt (aftermath of Asian movements)**: Celts serving "as early as 274 bc serving in the army of Ptolemy II Philadelphos. They were most likely recruited from the horde that entered Asia Minor in 278" (celts and others on the eastern fringes 208). Rebelled, "driving some 4,000 of the mercenaries onto an island in the Sebennytic branch of the Nile, where, Pausanias tells us, ‘they perished by hunger and each other’s swords’." Later settled as cleruchs in Fayum; "Among the 50,000 or so troops ... 6,000 ‘Gauls and Thracians’" at Raphia 217 BC (warfare and society / eastern fringes). "Gauls still retained their identity throughout the second century bc" with service at Abydos and Hermopolis Magna; funerary stelae in Alexandria naming Gauls.

- **Mechanics and social drivers (from classic Celtic warfare/society of the period)**: Conflict endemic and embedded in social system; "the feast ... provided the opportunity for an aspiring young man to make his claim ... The feast may well have been the moment when a warrior would propose to lead a raid" (warfare and society 232–233). Potlatch, status competition, clientage, hostage-taking, and warrior ethos (single combat, noise, nakedness for ritual, torcs) facilitated mobile warrior bands. "The Celts have in their company, even in war, companions whom they call parasites. These men pronounce their praises..." (warfare and society 226–227, quoting Athenaeus/Poseidonius).

- **Art and continued links post-migration (Po Valley transmission)**: "It is now widely believed that the Celtic groups in the Cisalpine region, notably the Boii and the Senones, were responsible for integrating Graeco-Italic ideas with Celtic ones and transmitting the result back to the innovating centres north of the Alps. This interpretation would imply that close social links were maintained between the Celts who had emigrated to the south and those who remained in their northern homelands." (the arts of the migration period 252–253, Vegetal Style section). "Celtic Art in the Fourth Century: Influences from the Po Valley" — "the relationship of the last with the broad northern zone" and "close relationship between the craftsmen of the Po valley and Marne in the fourth century" (evidenced by jockey-cap helmets, Vegetal Style motifs shared).

## Relation to Other Concepts and Periods

Preceded_by / part_of broader La Tène expansion from Hallstatt base. Contributed to pressure on classical Mediterranean polities (Rome, Macedon, Hellenistic kingdoms). Enabled by and contributed_to mercenary service networks and elite competition. Contrasts_with later Roman conquest and oppida development.

Atlantic façade communities participated in or paralleled these dynamics (e.g., chariot persistence in Britain; shared La Tène art and warrior equipment), though specific migration waves documented primarily in Mediterranean-contact zones.

## Historiography and Limitations (from range)

Classical sources biased: "The writing of Greek and Roman history was formulaic." Pausanias structures Celtic attack like Persian Wars; Polybius uses Celts to highlight Roman virtues (volatile vs. steadfast); Caesar plays on prejudices "providing a justification for his actions amounting in some cases to genocide" (warfare and society 211–213). Poseidonius more anthropological but Stoic lens of Noble Savage. Regional and chronological variation: "To imply that any generalized description has universal application is evident nonsense." Most sources concern 4th–2nd c groups near Mediterranean; remote areas (e.g. northern Scotland under Calgacus) may reflect archaic behaviour or literary stereotypes. "A further possibility, that primitive warfare exhibited many similarities irrespective of time, place, and ethnicity, has also to be borne in mind."

Cunliffe integrates texts with archaeology (Po Valley art transmission as evidence of maintained links; specific artifacts and sites grounding identity retention).
