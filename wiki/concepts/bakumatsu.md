# Bakumatsu

**Bakumatsu** (幕末, literally "end of the shogunate") is the conventional Japanese periodization for the final phase of Tokugawa rule, generally dated from Commodore Perry's arrival in 1853 to the [[meiji-restoration-1868]] of 1868. It designates the overlapping crises — foreign intrusion, domestic radicalization, shogunal delegitimization, and civil conflict — that destroyed a political order that had endured for over 250 years.

## Definition and Scope

Bakumatsu is a retrospective periodization, not an emic category used by contemporaries who instead spoke of *kaikoku* (opening the country), *jōi* (expelling the barbarians), and *kōbu gattai* (union of court and military). As a scholarly frame it captures the structural interdependence of three crises:

1. **The foreign crisis**: the forced [[opening-of-japan-1853-1858|opening of Japan]] by Western naval pressure and the resulting treaty-port system, which exposed the shogunate's inability to protect the "Divine Land."
2. **The legitimacy crisis**: the shogunate's practice of signing treaties without imperial sanction, which made the court's formal approval constitutionally visible and contested for the first time in centuries.
3. **The security crisis**: the proliferation of armed *shishi* (men of high purpose), political assassinations, domain-level military activism, and ultimately civil war that made ordinary governance impossible.

These crises were causally linked: foreign pressure → shogunal capitulation → imperial outrage → domestic radicalization → civil conflict → collapse.

## Causal Drivers

### Foreign Pressure

Perry's fleet of "black ships" entered Edo Bay on July 8, 1853, and his demand for a treaty provoked immediate crisis. The shogunate, unable to resist militarily, consulted the daimyos and the imperial court for the first time in over two centuries — a sign of weakness that broke the fiction of bakufu omnipotence. As daimyo Kuroda Nagahiro's memorial put it, "as the result of the long years of peace, people high and low alike had come to crave comfort. Their spirits had fallen into a decline." The [[opening-of-japan-1853-1858|Treaty of Kanagawa]] (1854) and the Ansei commercial treaties (1858) followed, each signed without imperial sanction.

### Imperial Court Activation

The [[emperor-komei|Emperor Kōmei]]'s refusal to sanction the Harris commercial treaty (1858) converted a shogunal foreign-policy crisis into a constitutional one. For the first time since the early seventeenth century, imperial will became a variable in political outcomes. The sonnō jōi slogan — "revere the emperor, expel the barbarians" — fused anti-foreign xenophobia with imperial legitimism, giving radical samurai a nationalist idiom that bypassed the shogunate's authority.

### The Ansei Purge and Radicalization

Tairō Ii Naosuke's [[ansei-purge-1858|Ansei Purge]] (1858–1860) — executing *shishi* leaders including Yoshida Shōin and Hashimoto Sanai, arresting court nobles sympathetic to the anti-treaty movement — temporarily silenced opposition but permanently radicalized it. Ii's assassination at the Sakuradamon Gate (1860) demonstrated that the shogunate could no longer protect its own senior officials. The following years saw escalating political violence in Kyōto: assassinations of suspected "traitors" (often with notes left at the scene justifying "divine punishment"), Chōshū domain forces attacking the palace at the [[hamaguri-gate-battle-1864|Battle of the Hamaguri Gate]] (1864), and the near-destruction of the capital by fire.

## Major Phases

| Phase | Dates | Characteristics |
|---|---|---|
| Opening crisis | 1853–1854 | Perry's arrival; Treaty of Kanagawa; court consultation begins |
| Treaty crisis | 1855–1858 | Harris negotiations; Harris Treaty signed without imperial sanction; Kōmei's abdication threats |
| Purge and terrorism | 1858–1862 | Ansei Purge; Ii assassination; political violence in Kyōto |
| Court radicalization | 1862–1863 | *Sonnō jōi* faction dominates Kyōto; Chōshū ascendant at court |
| Counter-coup | 1863–1864 | Coup of the Eighth Month (Sept. 1863); Battle of Hamaguri Gate; First Chōshū Expedition |
| Domain wars | 1864–1868 | Chōshū-Satsuma alliance; Second Chōshū Expedition; Boshin War and Restoration |

## Key Actors

- **[[emperor-komei]]**: the pivotal imperial actor; his refusal to sanction the treaties made imperial approval constitutionally visible; his death in 1867 opened the path for the Restoration
- **[[tokugawa-shogunate]]** (especially tairō Ii Naosuke, senior councillors Abe Masahiro and Hotta Masayoshi): the bakufu navigating between foreign demands and domestic opposition
- **Tokugawa Yoshinobu (Keiki)**: the last shogun, who attempted to reform the bakufu from within before surrendering power in the Restoration
- **Sonnō jōi *shishi***: lower samurai activists, most prominently from Chōshū and Satsuma, who radicalized the anti-foreign movement into anti-shogunate revolution
- **[[sonno-joi|Sonnō jōi movement]]**: the ideological driver of radical activism

## Interaction with Other Processes

Bakumatsu intersected with broader mid-nineteenth-century dynamics: the aftermath of the [[opening-of-japan-1853-1858|Opium Wars]] and the demonstration of Western military-industrial superiority; the worldwide crisis of agrarian empires under industrial capitalism; and Japanese intellectual traditions (Mito school nativism, rangaku or Dutch learning) that provided competing frameworks for responding to Western pressure.

The term is sometimes contrasted with *sonnō jōi* (the ideological slogan) and *kōbu gattai* (the moderate reformist program), which were competing political responses within the Bakumatsu crisis rather than the crisis itself.

## End Conditions

Bakumatsu ended with the [[meiji-restoration-1868]]: shogun Tokugawa Yoshinobu's restoration of governmental power to the emperor in October 1867, followed by the [[boshin-war-1868|Boshin War]] (1868–1869) in which forces claiming to act in the emperor's name defeated pro-shogunate domains. The new Meiji government issued the [[charter-oath-1868]] and relocated the imperial seat to Edo (renamed Tōkyō).

## Historiography

Japanese historiography long treated Bakumatsu through the lens of its outcome: an inevitable march toward Meiji modernization driven by the "enlightened" anti-shogunate domains. Post-1945 Marxist scholarship emphasized class conflict — the dissolution of the *han* system and the lower samurai's frustrated social ambitions. Recent English-language scholarship (Conrad Totman, Marius Jansen, Andrew Gordon) has emphasized the contingency of the Restoration and the genuine viability of alternative outcomes, especially kōbu gattai reform. Keene's biography of Meiji frames the entire Bakumatsu period through the lens of Emperor Kōmei's political personality, recovering the court as a genuine locus of power rather than a passive backdrop.
