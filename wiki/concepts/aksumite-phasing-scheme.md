# Aksumite Phasing Scheme (Phillipson)

## Definition

David Phillipson (*Foundations*, 2012) deliberately **rejects** the use of named phases or period-labels for the history of the northern Horn and [[aksum|Aksumite civilisation]], preferring instead an "absolute chronology expressed in terms of years BC or AD." This is itself a methodological position worth recording as a concept, since it stands in explicit contrast to several competing phase-schemes that have shaped the literature.

### Competing schemes in the literature

Earlier scholars proposed various named-phase systems; Phillipson surveys and critiques their deficiencies:

| Label | Proponent(s) | Date range (approximate) | Phillipson's critique |
|---|---|---|---|
| "Pre-Aksumite" | General scholarly usage; Ullendorff 1960 | All materials pre-dating Aksumite c. 1st c. AD | Retrospective; homogenises culturally diverse communities; see [[pre-aksumite-period]] |
| "Ethio-Sabaean" | Conti Rossini 1928; mid-20th c. consensus | 9th–4th c. BC (elite sites) | Perpetuates the colonisation myth; see [[south-arabian-influence-horn]] |
| "Transitional" / "Intermediate" | Various | Last four centuries BC (broadly) | Ill-defined; the same label used with "varied or even contrary definitions" by different scholars |
| "Proto-Aksumite" | Fattovich & Bard 2001 | c. 400–1st c. BC (Beta Giyorgis) | Locally valid for Beta Giyorgis; misleading if applied over a wider area |
| "Classic Aksumite" / "Late Aksumite" | Various | 1st–7th c. AD subdivisions | Phillipson prefers absolute chronology rather than these compartments |

### Phillipson's positive framework

Rather than a phase scheme, Phillipson proposes anchoring all materials in absolute dates while recognising four broadly distinguishable developmental horizons (inferred from his narrative structure):

1. **Pre-complex farming communities** (before c. 900 BC; possibly earlier): stone-built domestic architecture, hand-made pottery, flaked-stone tools, no metals or writing. Represented at rock-shelter sites in the Aksum region and Tembien; at Ancient Ona sites (Asmara area) and Kidane Mehret. Continuity of population from much earlier periods is assumed.

2. **First-millennium-BC highland communities with selective South Arabian contact** (c. 900/800–400 BC): two parallel streams —
   - *Elite/monumental* sites (Yeha, Maqaber Ga'ewa, Hawelti-Melazo, Kaskase): South Arabian-script inscriptions, Almaqah temples, monumental stone architecture; numerically very few.
   - *Non-élite farming* sites (Ancient Ona, Kidane Mehret lower levels): continuity of domestic material culture with no South Arabian connections; the majority of the population.

3. **Proto-Aksumite horizon at Beta Giyorgis** (c. 400–1st c. BC): highly localised; elite funerary platform with stelae and shaft graves; pottery homogeneous and showing "limited continuity with that produced during earlier periods"; imports predominantly of Nile Valley rather than South Arabian affinity. Phillipson accepts Fattovich and Bard's term for this specific site but declines to extend it regionally.

4. **Aksumite kingdom** (c. 1st c. AD onward): major new settlement established in the valley between Beta Giyorgis and Mai Qoho hills; gradually expanded. Christianity adopted in the second quarter of the fourth century. Kingdom declined c. 7th c. AD. Cultural influence extended much later.

## Examples

The practical implication of Phillipson's approach is visible in his chapter structure: rather than "Pre-Aksumite" and "Aksumite" parts, he divides *Foundations* into "Before Aksum" (Part One, Chs 2–3) and "The Kingdom of Aksum" (Part Two, Chs 4–16), with thematic chapters (epigraphy, religion, economy, etc.) cutting across chronological boundaries rather than being confined to a phase.

## Limitations and Critiques

- **Practical utility**: named phases, however imperfect, provide convenient shorthand for scholarly communication. Phillipson's absolute-chronology approach is rigorous but increases the terminological burden.
- **The "Proto-Aksumite" compromise**: Phillipson retains this term for Beta Giyorgis while rejecting it regionally, creating a potential inconsistency.
- **Applicability**: the scheme is derived primarily from Tigray and southern Eritrea, where archaeological coverage is best. It may not capture equivalent developments in areas with less research.
- **Radiocarbon precision**: the very imprecision of radiocarbon dating for the first millennium BC (due to atmospheric fluctuations) undermines the absolute-chronology approach in practice, leaving many assignments as approximate as the named phases they replace.
