## Definition and Scope

To cultural anthropologists, "ethnohistory meant the reconstruction of past life-ways by analysing documentary materials" ([[denoon-cambridge-pacific-islanders-1997]]). It occupied the disciplinary boundary between history (document-oriented, event-focused) and anthropology (culture-oriented, structure-focused): "A long-standing convention in Western scholarship was that historians studied events while anthropologists studied cultures."

In Pacific contexts, ethnohistorians typically began "with the era where archaeologists left off," reconstructing pre-contact societies from the earliest European contact records. The method "embodies an obvious paradox: the aim is to formulate inferences—informed guesses—about a society that is no longer extant, using materials from the period of European contact."

## Source Base and Its Limits

Ethnohistorical sources are primarily European: "published explorers' accounts, unpublished correspondence, ships' logs, and early missionary reports." Supplementary archival records — "census reports, land deeds, court testimony, wills, tax rolls, petitions" — can add quantitative depth. Marshall Sahlins's work on early Hawai'i and Fiji "set a standard for the comprehensive and innovative use of archival sources."

Source-criticism is methodologically essential. Early visitors "were following their own agendas — exploration, trade and missionisation, usually in that order." Key evaluative questions include: Was the writer present for a day or months? Did they disembark? Are claims based on personal observation or on secondhand informants? Is corroboration available, and is apparent corroboration in fact plagiarism?

"Gender relations and women's status are particularly difficult to reconstruct. European explorers and traders — all men — came from a highly gender-stratified, male-dominant society." The result is that "men's activities are relatively well documented while detailed information on women's lives scarcely exists."

## The 1980s Expansion

In the 1980s, some anthropologists advocated "reorienting ethnohistory to include the study of culturally variable models and genres of history." The move drew on the insight that "'There are no culture-free historical facts'": indigenous concepts of time, event, and the past are not transparent windows but their own historical constructions. This expanded definition made Western historical categories themselves objects of inquiry.

The expansion coincided with historians' own "extension beyond particular sequences of events," producing "productive collaborations" between disciplines that had previously divided the past between them.

## Critique from Post-Colonial Historiography

Anti-colonial writers have questioned whether ethnohistory as traditionally practised is either possible or politically defensible. Critics charged that "ethnohistory tended to portray pre-European societies as timeless, unchanging isolates, neglecting the patent fact that they had their own dynamics of development before Westerners arrived. Too often, anthropologists and historians implicitly and perhaps unwittingly conveyed the impression that culture change and 'history' begin with foreign contact."

Edward Said's *Orientalism* (1978) sharpened this critique: "representing the culture or the past of an indigenous people is implicitly a matter of political power." These challenges, while epistemologically radical, "do not imply that nothing certain can be known about past events; the recognition that knowledge is contingent does mean that we must evaluate carefully all forms of historical discourse."

## Relation to Oral History

David Henige's 1982 *Oral Historiography* marked the broadening of the field: historians' acceptance that "oral traditions are seen as valid sources" required confronting the distinctive properties of oral transmission. "Anthropologists have long known that revision is common in oral accounts, and is intensified when political fortunes are at stake." Hawaiian chiefly genealogies were routinely adjusted to legitimate current political arrangements; David Malo reported that "genealogists were called the wash-basins of the *ali'i* [chiefs], in which to cleanse them."

Oral traditions, like travellers' accounts, "are produced in a contemporary context, reflecting particular points of view" and performing "identity work" in the terms of Geoffrey White's Solomon Islands research.

## North American Ethnohistory (CHNPA I.1)

### Pioneering role and forty-year record

In North America, per Trigger & Washburn’s CHNPA Preface, ethnohistory “played an important pioneering role in the development of the study of minority histories,” complementing mainline studies. Over the forty years before 1996, ethnohistorians mainly of European descent documented Native peoples’ important ongoing roles in North American society and realized indigenous societies endure and sometimes flourish rather than disappear—discovering what Native peoples had long known and transforming nonaboriginal understanding of their own history. This record contrasts with the extinction premise of the older [[invention-of-the-native]] frame.

Washburn & Trigger define ethnohistory not as a discipline but as a corpus of analytical techniques that scholars from different disciplines can use to study the history of nonliterate peoples. Native American scholars have joined non-Native ones in interpreting the past of both groups. From first contact, each group shaped the other’s history; anthropological attempts to isolate Native lifestyle to achieve a pure “ethnographic present” free of outside influence and change have been abandoned. Historians and anthropologists recognize Indians and non-Indians as caught in rapid, endless historical change without isolation of one group from the other.

### Genealogy: Boas → acculturation → Spicer

Nineteenth-century evolutionist and Boasian anthropology made little provision for studying post-contact cultural change (seen as disintegration; Boas deleted sewing machines but not HBC blankets from potlatch descriptions). Exception: James Mooney’s Ghost Dance study detailed trauma of change/loss of power. Late 1920s: Boasian relativism + expanded history ground ethnohistory. Precursors include W. C. MacLeod’s *American Indian Frontier* (1928); Alfred G. Bailey’s *Conflict of European and Eastern Algonkian Cultures* (1937)—first major North American Indian-history study—combining Innis influence with cultural relativism; Felix Keesing’s Menomini study (1939) suggesting many “aboriginal” features were products of European interaction. Neither Bailey nor Keesing attracted deserved contemporary attention—showing how alien “history” still was to American anthropologists.

From the 1930s, as it became evident Natives would not die out or easily assimilate, U.S. anthropologists studied how lifeways changed under European domination—initially “acculturation” for useful generalizations to advise government humanely. Postwar land-claims documentary research hastened the shift. Acculturation slowly evolved into ethnohistory, birth marked by Edward Spicer’s *Cycles of Conquest* (1962) on Southwest peoples under Spanish, Mexican, and American administrations. Focus shifted from only forces of acculturation to resistance and preservation of traditional cultures/identities; later diversity of reactions within groups and non-monolithic European nationalities (Trigger *Children of Aataentsic*). North American ethnohistorians were slower than Australian/NZ counterparts to use archaeology for contact-period processes.

Franz Boas’s prior transformation of anthropology underwrote the method’s anti-racist and holistic strengths while leaving a shortcoming: interest in reconstructing pre-contact “pristine” cultures more than contact consequences, and continued underestimation of pre-contact change. See [[native-north-american-historiography]] for the longer Boas–Parkman–Turner arc.

### Anthropology–history convergence and subjectivism

In the decades before 1996, anthropology reshaped Indian history writing and vice versa: history’s shift from elites to communities/individuals, classes/genders/races; Indian history placed alongside immigrants, workers, African-Americans, women; fascination with epistemology of studying “the Other.” French Annales (mentalities, structures) reinforced structural anthropology; Sahlins’s historical anthropology and Wolf’s *Europe and the People without History* (1982) were influential though leading theorists worked mostly outside North America. Hermeneutics, semiotics, deconstruction, and phenomenology led many to deny data can be considered independently of observers’ beliefs/values. Because European reports were greatly influenced by preconceptions, European intellectual history became an essential prerequisite for ethnohistory.

### Theory wars: rationalist-materialist vs idealist-cultural relativist

Heated theoretical controversies confront rationalist-materialist and idealist-cultural relativist interpretations:

- **Rationalists** (Trelease; Trigger *Children of Aataentsic*; Jennings on political calculation) argue universal rationality minimizing effort/risk: Natives soon valued European metalware utilitarianism, became dependent, ignored traditional game-limiting sanctions, fought over trade routes/hunting territories; dependence undermined self-confidence and traditional beliefs.
- **Cultural-anthropology influence** (Sahlins; Hamell): Europeans’ goods first valued for traditional religious significance explaining two centuries of relations; others claim until ~1760 Midwest Natives determined own destinies amid colonial rivalries; early warfare as continuation of long-standing conflicts scaled up by firearms and mourning-war replacement of disease losses (Richter).
- **Extreme subjectivism** (Calvin Martin): historical consciousness alien to North American Indians “not conceived in history”—Washburn & Trigger reject the claim that ethnohistorians cannot understand alien cultures or write histories of peoples uninterested in history.

Many Native and Euro-American scholars argue a traditional pattern of Native cultures irreconcilably different from Europeans, with a basic core of beliefs/values surviving massive lifeway change (Martin; Sioui). Modern idealism, though intending to honor Native values, risks sharing with Parkman a minimization of rationality that Natives may find denigrating. Ethnohistorians need a middle ground between extreme rationalism and extreme idealism (Bailey identified the problem decades earlier). See [[indigenous-historical-consciousness]].

### Native voices, Deloria, and politicization

Ideology’s role rose with Native voices; non-Indian scholars’ sympathy led to addressing injustices, coercion, hypocrisy, and Native resourcefulness. Trigger argues ethnohistory’s major accomplishment so far is revealing Euro-American attitudes more than Native self-perceptions over time. Post-WWII Indian anthropologists/historians amplified Native writers, lawyers, novelists, artists. Vine Deloria Jr.’s *Custer Died for Your Sins* (1969), especially the “anthros” chapter, forced self-justification; 1960s radical campus climate and Vietnam anti-establishment feelings facilitated “reinventing” anthropology (Hymes) as action/applied work. 1989 AAA retrospective still engaged Deloria; he attacked resistance to repatriation/rebury of skeletal remains.

Some Natives deny non-Natives understanding or moral right to study Native history and demand permission and royalty-sharing. Washburn & Trigger reject exclusivity: North American history is intercultural; European documentation requires bias analysis needing both cultures; modern Natives may not more easily understand seventeenth–nineteenth-century ancestors than Euro-Americans understand theirs; cooperation and more Native recruitment needed—ecumenical not balkanized research. Axtell: “The Indian presence precipitated the formation of an American identity”; searching for “pure” Indian postcontact history remains a fantasy.

### Beyond ethnohistory — braiding Native historical philosophy

Nabokov (CHNPA I.1 ch. 1) argues even enlightened ethnohistory that depicts Indians as active agents (Sandos & Burgess; Calvin Martin’s “bifurcated Indian”) is only half the solution; the next step is braiding Native American historical philosophy and metahistorical frameworks into many-voiced accountings for Indian pasts before, during, and after Euro-American relations, while Cohn insists colonial structures must not be ignored. Fogelson’s ethno-ethnohistory is a central methodological banner. Salisbury’s bibliographic essay maps late-20th-c. transformation of colonial eastern Native scholarship via interdisciplinary ethnohistory (*Handbook* vols. 4 and 15; Jennings; Axtell; Trigger; Richter; White *Middle Ground*). Method literature includes Washburn “Ethnohistory: History ‘in the Round’”; Trigger “Problems and Prospects” and *Natives and Newcomers*; Axtell *After Columbus*; Simmons *Spirit of the New England Tribes* on oral tradition.

## CHNPA I.2 — ethnohistory’s demythologizing record and Subarctic method debates

Part 2 preface (Trigger & Washburn) restates that ethnohistorians of European descent over ~40 years before 1996 documented enduring Native roles and disproved evolutionary/romantic stereotypes that cast Indians as doomed or as noble savages belonging only to the past—discovering what Native peoples had long known. Co-editors value ethnohistory’s pioneering role in minority histories while rejecting extreme relativism (interpretations judged by coherence and documentary correspondence). See [[native-north-american-historiography]] for the full anti-relativism claim and I.2 chapter architecture.

Ray’s Northern Interior bibliographic essay (I.2 ch. 13) maps ethnohistorical practice onto fur-trade archives: HBC records as primary spine; Speck vs post-Speck consensus on family hunting territories as products of long fur-trade participation rather than purely aboriginal tenure; Martin *Keepers of the Game* vs Krech’s critics as a flashpoint of worldview/disease/hunting theses; women and company-family studies (Van Kirk; Brown) as central to Métis/mixed-blood ethnogenesis. These debates show ethnohistory operating as a multi-author method cluster, not a single school.
