# Natural Law

The doctrine that there exists a **moral law inherent in nature and accessible to human reason**, prior
and superior to the positive (man-made) law of any state — and that human enactments are just only so
far as they conform to it. One of the most consequential ideas in the history of Western law, ethics
and politics.

## Emic origins and the Ciceronian formulation

The idea is **Stoic** in origin: a rational order (*logos*) pervading the cosmos, in which human reason
shares, so that to "live according to nature" is to live by reason. Its classic and most influential
**Latin statement** is **[[cicero|Cicero's]]**, in *[[cicero-republic-and-laws|De legibus and De re
publica]]*:

> "True law is right reason in agreement with nature; it is of universal application, unchanging and
> everlasting… It is a sin to try to alter this law, nor is it allowable to attempt to repeal any part
> of it… There will not be different laws at Rome and at Athens, or different laws now and in the
> future, but one eternal and unchangeable law will be valid for all nations and all times."

On this view **justice is natural, not merely conventional**; an unjust statute is not truly law; and
all persons share, by reason, in a single moral community.

## Afterlife (etic and emic)

Cicero's formulation became the channel by which Stoic natural law entered the Western mainstream:
- the **Roman jurists** distinguished *ius naturale* within the framework of Roman law;
- **Christian thought** absorbed it — Paul's "law written on their hearts," then **Aquinas's**
  systematic account of natural law as the rational creature's participation in the eternal law;
- the **early-modern** theorists of natural law and natural rights — **Grotius**, Pufendorf, **Locke**
  — secularized and reworked it into the foundation of international law and of rights-based politics,
  feeding the **American** and **French** revolutionary declarations.

## Limitations and critiques

Natural-law thinking is **contested**. Its premises (a purposive natural/cosmic order, a universally
legible reason) are open to **sceptical attack** — a tension Cicero himself acknowledged. Legal
**positivists** (Bentham — "nonsense upon stilts"; later Austin, Kelvin, Hart) deny any necessary
connection between law and morality and treat "natural law" as the smuggling of preference into
metaphysics. Historicists and relativists note that supposedly "universal" natural law has in practice
encoded particular cultural assumptions. The debate between natural-law and positivist accounts of the
foundation of law remains open.
