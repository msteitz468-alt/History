## Definition and Scope

Maynes and Waltner (Ch. 9) propose the family and household as central themes of
world history, linked to global history through two concepts: **domestication**
and **biopolitics**.

**Domestication** refers not only to the Neolithic domestication of plants and
animals, but also to the *self-domestication* of humans and the emergence of new
forms of family with "the cultural invention of *human* domestic life." Clive
Gamble argues the earliest Neolithic communities may have been the first "to
develop fully modern minds and a fully symbolic culture" — pointing to a powerful
dialectic between family, household, and the emergence of the state.

**Biopolitics** (after Foucault) refers to political control over human bodies —
attempts to count, measure, and regulate fertility and family structures. Foucault
located spectacular examples in the modern world, but biopolitics, like
domestication, can be traced to at least the early Neolithic.

## The Family-State Dialectic

A recurring argument: families provided idealized *templates* for states and
political structures.
- Male rulers represented themselves as heads of ancient descent lineages
  through the male line.
- Chinese philosophers described the well-ordered state on the analogy of the
  well-ordered family.
- The metaphor "likening the power of the king to that of the father" naturalized
  both masculine political power and masculine authority in the household.
- This naturalized political power, making its rules and authority seem
  self-evident — which is why, from the earliest states, rulers tried to regulate
  family structures. Hammurabi's law code (18th c. BCE) was deeply concerned with
  the family.

The metaphors cut both ways and changed over time. During the French Revolution,
Lynn Hunt showed, the idea of *fraternity* challenged older metaphors of *paternal*
authority.

## Households as Agents of Socialization

Drawing on Ian Hodder's work at Çatalhöyük (Neolithic Anatolia), the chapter
argues that households were critical agents of socialization. Çatalhöyük's
cheek-by-jowl residences created "childscapes" that taught children how society
and social networks were organized — cooperative production, division of labor,
settlement building, kinship rules — "all emerged without any form of the state."
Internal burials connected past to future across generations, creating a powerful
sense of the movement of time, and even carried cosmological information.

## Reframing Paleolithic Families

The chapter participates in the feminist revision of Paleolithic social models
(see [[gender-history]]). The "Man the Hunter" model (dominant until the 1970s)
imagined male acquisition/production and biologically-determined female
reproduction. Revisions highlight:
- The central role of women as gatherers and producers
- The constructed (cultural, not merely biological) nature of Paleolithic families
- The importance of households in demographic behavior and child socialization
- Genetic evidence that marriages between communities of farmers and foragers
  may have helped spread agricultural technologies and lifeways

## Cosmology and Religion

Family structures shaped religious cosmologies and vice versa:
- Egyptian cosmologies linked gods by family ties (e.g., "Isis discovered seeds
  and Osiris taught the Egyptian people how to plant them").
- Monotheistic religions offered cosmologies implying clear gender hierarchies,
  tempting an association with the emergence of new forms of patriarchy.
- But religious cosmologies could also be *at odds* with family structures —
  e.g., when they idealized celibacy, as some forms of Buddhism and Christianity did.

## Modern Biopolitics

As modern states extended their administrative reach, they regulated domestic
life with increasing detail and success. By the 20th century, biopolitics — "the
routine state surveillance, measurement, and management of human life" — became
a normal function of governments worldwide. The most brutal forms were associated
with colonial regimes and with the racial politics of fascism and eugenics; but
in modest forms (who may marry whom, the ideal family size), family regulation
became routine. The two historiographies of politics and the family have moved
"ever closer together."

## Emic vs. Etic

"Domestication" (of humans) and "biopolitics" are etic analytical frameworks.
Families and households are emic, lived realities in every society. The chapter's
move is to use the etic concepts to connect the deep prehistoric record (Neolithic
households) to modern state formation.

## Limitations and Critiques

- The family-state analogy can be over-extended; not all states modeled themselves
  on families, and the direction of causation is often unclear.
- Projecting "biopolitics" back to the Neolithic risks anachronism.
- Generalizing about "the family" across all cultures obscures enormous variation
  in kinship, marriage, and household forms.
