# The Grammar of Warfare

A pair of linked analytical ideas through which [[sources/halsall-warfare-society-barbarian-west-2003|Guy
Halsall]] reframes the study of war in the [[early-middle-ages|Barbarian West]]: (1) the
**substantivist** method, that warfare can be understood only within the norms, values and
mentalities of the society practising it; and (2) the **"grammar of warfare,"** the idea that
much early medieval war was a **form of communication** governed by shared codes, so that
combatants could read each other's "statements" and keep violence from spiralling out of control.

## Substantivist vs. normalist (etic frame)

Halsall opposes two ways of writing about early medieval war:

- **Normalist** — the assumption that there are timeless principles of strategy and tactics, an
  "inherent military probability," and a "rational man model" valid across all periods; gaps in
  the thin early medieval evidence are filled with better-documented analogies from other eras.
  Halsall allows that early medieval commanders did seek advantage (surprise, terrain, the sun at
  the enemy's back) but holds that the normalist method "cannot be entirely adequate."
- **Substantivist** — the view, linked to the social-constructionist history of emotions, that
  warfare and even battlefield fear are shaped by contemporary values (battle as God's judgement;
  culturally specific notions of honour and shame, anger and death). Its weakness is the opposite
  one: structuralism "copes badly with change through time."

Halsall resolves the tension with **structuration**: norms (the "structure") are a society's
cumulative "memory bank" of past interactions; they govern action but are also constituted *by*
action, so people can play *with* the rules as well as *within* them, and warfare changes — slowly,
because its deliberate, ritualised character partly shields it, but it changes (he dates major
transformations to ~600 and the ninth century). This is why the whole 450-year period must **not**
be treated as a static "Germanic" or "heroic" whole.

## The grammar of warfare (the codes)

Because "warfare … is a form of communication, and in some instances a type of diplomacy," it
needs a **grammar**: codes of conduct that make a belligerent's actions legible to the enemy. When
war was about **maintaining a status quo** — as much endemic raiding was — knowing the appropriate
response kept escalation in check; conversely, **breaking the codes** (calling in "peoples from
beyond the Rhine," killing a reigning king) was itself a legible statement that this was a more
serious war for higher stakes. Dramatic change could come from the violent meeting of cultures with
**different** rules — Halsall's leading case being the [[viking-age|Viking]] wars, where pagans not
bound by the shared sacral codes (oaths sworn on Christian objects) appeared as treacherous
oath-breakers.

This connects to the early medieval **spectrum of violence** — from compensable brawls, through the
violence of armed bands (*contubernia*), local and aristocratic feud, cross-border raiding, up to
full "public war" (*bellum*) waged on the king's orders — and to the mechanisms of **feud** and the
**peacemaking king**, who made internal peace yet won external "peace" through war (the Roman
*domitor gentium*). Contemporaries rarely theorised these levels (Isidore of Seville's typology is
exceptional) but they governed conduct.

## Why it matters / what it explains

The grammar frame underlies Halsall's substantive conclusions about how war was actually waged in
the [[medieval-warfare|early medieval West]] — see the hub overview
[[early-medieval-warfare|Early Medieval Warfare in the Barbarian West]]:
- that **ravaging/harrying** was a deliberate *challenge to battle* and a communicative act
  (attacking the enemy king's claim to protect his people), not merely economic destruction;
- that battle was hedged with **ritual** (ordeal, fasting, relics, single combat) because its
  outcome was a divine verdict;
- that much warfare was limited, recurrent and stylised, while a few wars deliberately tore up the
  rules.

## Limitations and critiques

- The substantivist/structuration apparatus is **abstract and hard to falsify** against the very
  thin evidence Halsall himself stresses; sceptics may judge the "grammar" partly imposed.
- Halsall is by his own admission **Frankocentric**; how far the codes he reconstructs from
  Merovingian/Carolingian Gaul and Anglo-Saxon England held in Ireland, the Celtic west, Lombard
  Italy or Visigothic Spain is uncertain.
- His rejection of the **normalist** tradition is contested by historians of war (e.g. Bernard
  Bachrach) who defend a stronger continuity of Roman military rationality and larger, more
  "professional" Carolingian armies — a live disagreement, not a settled one.
