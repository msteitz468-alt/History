# Native North American Historiography

## Definition

The changing body of written (and later archaeological and ethnohistorical) interpretation of Native North American peoples produced largely by non-Native observers and scholars — and the critique of that tradition. Source biases have shifted across centuries: Iberian moral theology, French and English colonial reportage, Enlightenment [[noble-savage]] imagery, nineteenth-century evolutionary anthropology, and twentieth-century [[ethnohistory]] each rewrote the Native past for different questions.

Washburn & Trigger open CHNPA I.1 ch. 2 by stating the greatest research problem: written sources derive largely from the non-Native side and are subject to distortions, misconceptions, biases, and ignorance associated with an external cultural perspective; those biases have also varied over the centuries. Citing Christopher Hill, they note every generation rewrites history by asking different questions, so historical writing often reveals as much about historians and their social milieu as about their objects of study. Knowledge does not depend only on written sources: oral history, archaeology (e.g. illuminating the [[battle-of-little-bighorn-1876]]), ethnography, and linguistics can complement one another when studied separately and compared (Sapir; Murdock).

The co-editors’ Preface frames Chapters 1–2 as examining changing Native views of their history ([[indigenous-historical-consciousness]]) and the views of non-Native historians before archaeological and contact narratives proceed. They value alternative (e.g. feminist, minority) histories and note ethnohistory’s pioneering role in minority histories, but reject extreme relativism: interpretations can be judged by internal coherence and correspondence with factual evidence. The volume “probably marks the end of an era” in which Native American history was studied almost entirely by people of European descent, grounded in evolutionary and romantic stereotypes that cast Natives as earlier European stages and as doomed to extinction.

## Major Phases (CHNPA frame)

### 1. Fifteenth–sixteenth-century preconceptions and Iberian conscience

Before mainland contact, Europeans already anticipated unknown peoples via travelers’ tales, classical stereotypes of alien groups, and accounts of Africa and Asia (Marco Polo; Canary Islanders; Portuguese West Africa; Norse “skraelings”; Mandeville monsters). The [[noble-savage]] image began at first contact, emphasizing innocence, generosity, and nakedness; concurrent commentators also denigrated Indians as thralls of the devil, lustful cannibals, or people living in terror of nature.

Spanish accounts are most conflicting because material interest and moral conscience intertwined. The Valladolid debate (1550–51) between Bartolomé de las Casas and Juan Ginés de Sepúlveda asked whether Natives shared Spanish humanity and inviolable rights to lands and persons (except just wars). Las Casas answered yes; Sepúlveda, following Aristotle, held some were natural slaves. Dominican Bernardino de Minaya helped obtain Pope Paul III’s bull *Sublimis Deus* (9 June 1537) affirming Indians are “truly men.” Despite Francisco de Vitoria and the crown “New Laws,” settler practice rarely matched royal norms. Tomás Ortiz’s 1525 denunciation catalogued vice; Vitoria’s *On the Indians Lately Discovered* (1526) denied Indians were of unsound mind, attributing seeming stupidity largely to “bad and barbarous upbringing,” analogous to European peasants. Las Casas’s “destruction of the Indies” is glossed as destruction of the Indies’ wealth—its people. Black Legend controversy (intensified at the Columbus quincentenary) still turns on whether Las Casas exaggerated atrocity.

Portuguese Brazil first-contact reports paralleled Spanish innocence themes; French–Tupinamba alliance created respectful reciprocal relations seen as a model for later French–Indian relations in North America (Léry, Thevet → Montaigne). French North American accounts (Cartier; Ribault/Laudonnière; Gourgues) developed a policy of *douceur* that became the cornerstone of New France colonization. English contact was slight; Frobisher’s Inuit encounters were brief and hostile; John White’s Roanoke drawings are called the earliest and best pictorial evidence of North American Natives; Thomas Hariot’s *A briefe and true report* (1588) is praised as detailed ethnography without travelers’ marvels.

### 2. Seventeenth–eighteenth-century colonial reportage and Enlightenment frames

Seventeenth-century French writings usually admired some aspects of Native life: Lescarbot (*Histoire de la Nouvelle France*, 1609) portrayed Mi’kmaqs as noble savages free of European vices; Champlain described eastern Canadian Natives as rude and undisciplined; Sagard took a middle path; Jesuits saw Huron country as Satan’s stronghold yet noted Hurons more generous/less quarrelsome than Europeans ([[huron-wendat]]). Fur traders’ accounts were less dominated by literary stereotypes; exception: Lahontan’s Adario dialogues (see [[noble-savage]]).

Joseph-François Lafitau (*Moeurs des sauvages amériquains*, 1724), often lauded as father of comparative ethnology, was in fact the most influential eighteenth-century exponent of medieval cultural degeneration. Pierre Charlevoix’s *Histoire* (1744) marked a historiographic advance (systematic narrative, source evaluation, footnotes) and proto-Enlightenment ethnology: Natives as rational as Europeans; differences of knowledge/education; improvable by association; Iroquois balancing French and English for independence.

English settlement’s land focus conditioned interpretations of Natives. Captivity literature and “white Indians” showed many captives preferred Indian life; Crevecoeur, Colden, Franklin claimed thousands of Europeans became Indians with no reverse converts (Vaughan & Richter revise this for New England convert towns). English clerical justifications for dispossession ranged from Purchas’s “Virginias Verger” and Gorges’s epidemic providentialism to Puritan New Israel/Canaanite typology (Ezra Stiles as late as 1783; Cotton Mather’s excoriations). Roger Williams denied the king’s patent right. The “virgin land” myth (Henry Nash Smith) was later attacked as Jennings’s “widowed land” emptied by disease and warfare ([[virgin-soil-epidemics]]; [[columbian-exchange-1492]]).

Later French writers with little Native contact (Raynal 1770; Chateaubriand; Dainville/Bossange 1821) reworked Charlevoix into romantic noble-savage or unchanging-innate-behavior frames. Seventeenth–eighteenth-century “racism” must be qualified: “red men” distinction was absent in the colonial period (Vaughan); physical differences were attributed to environment before the nineteenth century. After the Revolution (most Indians sided with British), “bloodthirsty monster” language and terms like “redskins” increased. Late eighteenth–nineteenth century: biological explanations; immutable racial characteristics; polygenism; then Darwinian evolutionism as more broadly acceptable racist rationale despite common origin—anticipating total extinction as a tragic but minor episode in progress.

### 3. Nineteenth-century evolutionism, romanticism, and national histories

Early nineteenth-century U.S. literature swung toward removal as “preservation” (Barbour, McKenney, Cass under Jackson); Tocqueville noted it was “impossible to destroy men with more respect for the laws of humanity.” Professional history developed mainly in the second half of the century. George Bancroft celebrated Jackson for planning removal so land east of the Mississippi passed to “exclusive possession of cultivated man.” Hildreth’s more “scientific” history left Indians outside his moral circle. Francis Parkman cast Indians as treacherous wilderness tenants—“thorough savages” unlikely to evolve civilization alone; ignored agricultural economies; held Indians less sensitive to pain than “higher races.” Jennings and others later attacked his insensitivity; Morison praised him.

Prescott’s *Conquest of Mexico* (1843) framed civilization vs semi-civilization and triggered debate on Native American character; elaborate North American archaeology was often attributed to Mesoamericans or Old World intruders. Lewis H. Morgan, reading Prescott against his Iroquois model (*League of the Ho-de-no-sau-nee*, 1851), denied indigenous Western Hemisphere civilization (“Montezuma’s Dinner,” 1876; *Ancient Society*, 1877)—an intellectualized racism many Euro-Americans were happy to accept ([[band-tribe-chiefdom-state]]; [[chiefdom]]; [[north-american-states-debate]]). John Gorham Palfrey reduced King Philip to a “squalid savage”; by contrast John William De Forest’s *History of the Indians of Connecticut* (1851) denied English superior virtue, appalled by Pequot “indiscriminate butchery” 1637, and read King Philip’s War as reaction to humiliation and land loss rather than general conspiracy ([[king-philips-war-1675]]; [[pequot-war-1637]]). Justin Winsor’s critical, geography-heavy history retained racial language yet morally condemned Columbus as the New World’s “despoiler.”

Nineteenth-century French Canadian historiography (Garneau; priest-historians Ferland, Faillon, Casgrain; later Suite and Groulx) darkened traditional cultures and stressed Iroquois cruelty; intellectual racism entered largely via Parkman. Early English Canadian historians cast allies Joseph Brant and Tecumseh as Canadian patriots, claimed generous Canadian treatment versus U.S. removal, yet thanked Providence for epidemics sweeping southern Ontario/Quebec clean for settlers (Hopkins).

Anthropology professionalized in museums then universities: Enlightenment evolutionary synthesis (Gallatin; Robertson 1777) without early racist capacity claims; mid-century linkage of cultural and biological evolution; Lubbock’s natural selection explaining “primitive” condition; ethnography valued mainly for reconstructing early European stages. The Moundbuilder controversy cast advanced earthwork cultures as non-Indian until Cyrus Thomas (1894) demolished that idea—but by also equating Moundbuilders with disrupted eighteenth-century Midwest peoples, preserving assumption of little pre-contact change ([[north-american-mound-building]]; [[mississippian-culture]]; [[adena-culture]]; [[hopewell-culture]]). Historians increasingly relied on anthropologists; Parkman especially expressed contemporary scientific as well as popular views. Negative views of Parkman and other North American historians cannot be understood independently of nineteenth-century anthropology.

### 4. Twentieth-century professionalization — Boas, marginalization, ethnohistory

Franz Boas transformed anthropology too late for full nineteenth-century effect: study total cultures of specific peoples; oppose biological explanations of behavior; equal biological capacity of races; reject civilized/primitive dichotomy; Boasian relativism denied Europeans could objectively judge others’ morality/religion by their own. Shortcomings: interest in reconstructing pre-contact “pristine” cultures more than contact consequences; continued underestimating pre-contact change. Strengths: anti-racism, holism, dignity of pre-European Native ways of life.

Late nineteenth–early twentieth century, historians’ attention to Natives declined further; Natives became exclusive anthropological concern, confined to introductory chapters, still doomed-to-disappear. Frederick Jackson Turner’s “Significance of the Frontier” (1893) framed frontier as “meeting point between savagery and civilization”; democracy as product of free/vacant land. Schlesinger’s *Age of Jackson* (1945) celebrated democratic spirit without discussing Jackson’s Indian dealings.

Renewed interest accompanied 1920s economic history: Harold Innis’s *The Fur Trade in Canada* (1930) assigned Natives a significant prolonged post-discovery Canadian role; George T. Hunt’s *The Wars of the Iroquois* (1940) tied fur-trade economics to new alliances and warfare ([[eastern-north-american-fur-trade]]; [[beaver-wars-17th-century]]). Precursors of ethnohistory: Mooney’s Ghost Dance study; MacLeod’s *American Indian Frontier* (1928); Alfred G. Bailey’s *Conflict of European and Eastern Algonkian Cultures* (1937)—first major North American Indian-history study; Keesing’s Menomini study (1939). Acculturation studies evolved into ethnohistory, birth marked by Edward Spicer’s *Cycles of Conquest* (1962). See [[ethnohistory]] for method, theory wars, and Native-voice politics.

### 5. Late twentieth-century revision — agency, moral historiography, Native authorship

Historians turning to Indians found stereotypes and antiquated frames. Revisionist Western historians (Limerick, White, Friesen) emphasized conflict/exploitation. Source myths were dismantled (Chief Seattle oration as Euro-American composition; exaggerated Cherokee removal death tolls—Prucha; scalping as European invention; U.S. Constitution consciously patterned on Iroquois Confederacy—Grinde/Johansen vs Tooker). Moral disputes pitted Francis Jennings (*Invasion of America*) against Alden Vaughan’s more favorable early Puritan portrait; Axtell and Washburn hold historical actors are moral as well as economic/political; Sheehan prefers impersonal “process”; Handlin attacked Jennings, Nash, and Dee Brown as 1960s victim-celebration. Sarah Carter’s *Lost Harvests* (1990) showed Canadian Plains Indians after bison disappearance hoped to become successful farmers; failure mainly due to government policies, not Indian character—dispelling a myth long accepted by Euro-Canadian historians and anthropologists.

Nabokov’s ch. 1 tracks Native-authored and collaborative historical production: mission-educated pioneers (Samson Occom; Pablo Tac); William Apes on “Indian” as degrading imported word; William W. Warren’s Ojibwa history forced into European chronology; Copway, Peter Jones, David Cusick; George Sword’s Oglala event history; Emmett Starr’s Cherokee genealogies after *Cherokee Phoenix* (1828) launched Indian journalism; Sioui’s “autohistory”; Doris Duke Oral History Project (~$5 million, 1967–1972) at seven regional centers; tribal museums and NMAI’s first-person mandate. The 1992 Columbian quincentenary emphasized coming to terms with lasting suffering from European diseases and colonization rather than celebrating European achievements as in 1892.

Salisbury’s bibliographic essay (ch. 7 context) maps late-20th-c. transformation of colonial eastern Native scholarship via interdisciplinary ethnohistory: *Handbook of North American Indians* vols. 4 (Washburn) and 15 (Trigger); Jennings; Axtell; Calvin Martin *Keepers of the Game* vs Krech; Trigger *Children of Aataentsic* and *Natives and Newcomers*; Richter *Ordeal of the Longhouse*; White *Middle Ground*. Mainstream assessments (Higham, Wise, Kammen *The Past Before Us* 1980) long ignored specialized Indian/ethnohistorical work—Indian history remained peripheral while historians and anthropologists formed an ethnohistory alliance.

## Volume conventions and authorship (CHNPA I.1)

The co-editors acknowledge the history was written by Euro-Americans and Euro-Canadians despite recruitment efforts; professional study remained largely the domain of historians and anthropologists of European descent. Naming policy retains *Handbook* ethnonyms with self-designations in brackets; restricts “Iroquois” to Five/Six Nations members; eschews “prehistory” as segmenting Native historical continuum; avoids “tribe” except as U.S. administrative term; follows Jennings in avoiding racial expressions except historical or statistical usage.

## CHNPA I.2 Preface — synthesis purpose and anti-relativism (Trigger & Washburn)

Part 2’s shared editorial frame (claims from range 01 preface slice) restates and extends the historiographic stance:

- The North America volume **synthesizes existing knowledge** of indigenous peoples north of the Rio Grande from earliest appearance into the 1990s rather than presenting original research or pioneering methods; co-editors (American historian and Canadian anthropologist) deliberately retained divergent political viewpoints.
- Editors **rejected Handbook-style tribal histories** because ethnic identities shifted over time and poorly frame ecology, disease/settlement responses, or pan-Indian identity; thematic and regional chapters were preferred. Architecture: chs. 7–11 cover ~1600–1880s settled regions; ch. 12 reservation experience 1880–1960 and pan-Indian identity; Northern Interior and Arctic run through modern times; final chapter (Washburn) evaluates post-1960 trends ([[native-american-renaissance-1960-1995]]).
- While valuing alternative (feminist/minority) histories and [[ethnohistory]]’s role in minority history, co-editors **reject extreme relativism** that treats all pasts as incommensurate; interpretations can be judged by internal coherence and correspondence with growing documentary evidence.
- Naming conventions match Part 1: *Handbook* ethnonyms with self-designations in brackets; Canadian Eskimos regularly Inuit, Alaskan as Eskimos; “Iroquois” restricted to Five/Six Nations vs Iroquoian family; avoids “prehistory” and “tribe” except as U.S. administrative term; avoids racial color terms except historical/statistical usage.

### Subarctic fur-trade historiography (Ray bibliographic essay, I.2 ch. 13)

Most important Northern Interior records: HBC archives (Provincial Archives of Manitoba); E.E. Rich on mercantile HBC to 1867; Ray *Canadian Fur Trade in the Industrial Age*; classic general study Innis *Fur Trade in Canada*. Land-tenure debate: Speck’s aboriginal family territories vs Bailey and later consensus that family hunting territories are fur-trade products (*Who Owns the Beaver?*, Bishop & Morantz 1986). Calvin Martin *Keepers of the Game* challenged by Krech (ed.) *Indians, Animals, and the Fur Trade*. Monographic spine: Francis & Morantz; Bishop; Ray *Indians in the Fur Trade*; Yerbury and Abel on Athapaskans/Dene; Ray & Freeman *“Give Us Good Measure”*; Van Kirk and Brown on women/fur-trade families. Modern mega-project literature: Richardson and Salisbury on James Bay; Berger/O’Malley on pipeline inquiry; Watkins *Dene Nation*; Asch on aboriginal rights and NWT constitutional arrangements.

## Contested Positions (attributed)

| Position | Proponents / locus | Core claim |
|---|---|---|
| Extreme relativism: alternative histories incommensurate and equally authentic | Postmodern currents CHNPA co-editors address | Each view must be accepted on its own terms |
| Interpretations judged by coherence and evidence | Trigger & Washburn (Preface) | Professionals must subject all interpretations to scholarly analysis |
| Oral tradition historically worthless | Lowie (1915–17); later Ewers | No reliable substitute for the written word |
| Oral tradition can complement written sources | Ethnohistorians; Vansina methods; Day, Wiget case studies | Corroborative methods recover accurate content |
| “Invasion” as dominant frame | Jennings | Puritan hypocrisy/perfidy; Parkman critique |
| Invasion *and* invited settlement/commerce/migration | Vaughan | Multifaceted European presence |
| Historical consciousness alien to North American Indians | Calvin Martin (extreme subjectivism) | Ethnohistorians cannot write histories of peoples “not conceived in history” |
| Rejected by Washburn & Trigger | Co-editors | Extreme subjectivism denied |
| Non-Natives lack moral right to study Native history | Some Native permission/royalty claims (Martin) | Exclusivity of research rights |
| Ecumenical cooperation needed | Washburn & Trigger | Intercultural history; more Native recruitment; not balkanized research |
