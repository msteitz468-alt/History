## Definition

**Proto-globalization** is the analytical concept with which [[kedar-cwh-v5-2015|CWH V]]
closes (Diego Olstein, Ch 25, developing an outline by S. N. Eisenstadt): the idea
that the [[middle-millennium|Middle Millennium]] (500–1500) was a **prelude to
globalization** — an era in which the connective processes that would later span the
globe were already at work across Afro-Eurasia, "falling short" of true globalization
in two precise senses. It is the volume's synoptic answer to its own organizing
question, recasting "expanding webs of exchange and conflict" as the first, partial
movement toward an integrated world.

## The Mechanism

Olstein/Eisenstadt define proto-globalization as the action of **three pervading
forces** promoting regional integration — **religion, commerce, and conquest** (the
same triad of "regular trans-civilizational interactions" the volume opened with) —
furthered by **four types of movement**: of **people, animals and plants, goods, and
ideas**. These operated at **three levels**: (1) commercial, political, military, and
cultural relations *between* the realms of Islam, China, India, and Europe; (2)
**hybridization** of traits drawn from two such realms; and (3) the construction of
**hegemonies** within each realm and the reactions they provoked.

## "Falling Short" in Two Senses

Olstein argues proto-globalization fell short of full globalization in two ways:

- **Geographically** — its scope was **only Afro-Eurasia** (plus the northern half of
  Africa). The Middle-Millennium world was one of **"worlds together"** for Eurasia and
  North Africa but **"worlds apart"** for the Americas, sub-Saharan Africa, Australasia,
  and Oceania, which lay outside the system (and, in the American case, were
  themselves internally integrating — see [[aztec-empire]], [[inca-empire]]).
- **In depth** — global trends did **not yet prevail over local ones**. Local
  conditions of social life remained fundamental everywhere, even in the connected
  Afro-Eurasian core. This local–global tension Olstein captures with the companion
  term **"proto-glocalization"** (from *glocalization*, the blending of global and
  local): the period's integration was always refracted through, and resisted by,
  local adaptation.

## Relation to the Volume's Argument

Proto-globalization situates the Middle Millennium within Eisenstadt's longer scheme —
a "parenthesis opened by the [[axial-age|Axial Age]] and closed by modernization,
multiple modernities, and globalization" — in which the Axial civilizations "enjoyed a
new lease on life," the [[empire|system of empire]] "thrived as never before"
(Chinese, Mongol, Muslim, Byzantine, Carolingian, Inca), and "the foundations for
globalization and modernization were laid." It thus connects directly to
[[afro-eurasian-world-system|the Afro-Eurasian world system]] (its empirical content)
and to [[cultural-crystallization|cultural crystallization]] (Eisenstadt's "second
Axial Age of sorts").

## Contestation

As a periodizing and interpretive device, proto-globalization shares the contested
status of [[middle-millennium|the "Middle Millennium"]] itself and of the
Jaspers–Eisenstadt civilizational framework (see [[axial-age-controversy]]). Critics
may question whether retrojecting "globalization" onto 500–1500 illuminates or
distorts — whether the medieval webs are best read as an early phase of a single long
process culminating in modern globalization, or as a distinct formation that the
teleological label flattens. Olstein's own "worlds together / worlds apart" and
"proto-glocalization" qualifications are, in part, hedges against that teleology.

## The Early Modern Realization (CWH VI)

If proto-globalization names the *prelude*, [[early-modern|the Early Modern
period]] (1500–1800) is where the process is consummated. [[bentley-cwh-v6-2015|CWH
VI]], titled *The Construction of a Global World*, narrates exactly the moment when
Olstein's two "shortfalls" are overcome: the **geographical** shortfall ends when
the Iberian voyages join the American "worlds apart" to the Afro-Eurasian system
(see [[columbian-exchange-1492]]), creating a single planetary arena of biological,
commercial, and cultural exchange; and the **depth** shortfall begins to close as
global trends — silver flows, the Atlantic slave and plantation complex, worldwide
missionary religion — start to bear on local conditions everywhere. The continuity
is deliberate: CWH VI is the empirical sequel that turns "proto-" into the thing
itself. The wiki accordingly treats early modern globalization through
[[early-modern-migrations]], [[intercultural-relations-early-modern]],
[[interpolity-law]], and the trade and production processes of CWH VI's later
cycles, while preserving Olstein's caution that "glocal" local refraction never
disappears.

## The First Silk Roads Era as "Ancient Globalization" (Benjamin 2018)

[[benjamin-empires-ancient-eurasia-2018|Craig Benjamin]] argues the
[[silk-roads|First Silk Roads Era]] (c. 100 BCE–250 CE) is "as clear an example of ancient
globalization as one can find anywhere," applying **Justin Jennings's** test (a surge in
long-distance connections producing a shared global culture) and John Tomlinson's "complex
connectivity." Of Jennings's eight trends, Benjamin finds several plainly present:
**time–space compression**, **standardization** (shared languages, tariffs, the value of
Roman gold/silver coin), **homogenization** (religions and technologies adopted across
regions), and — decisively — **vulnerability**: the interdependence that made the
near-simultaneous third-century imperial collapses cascade into the
[[collapse-of-first-silk-roads-era-250|disintegration of the network]]. He pairs this with
[[world-systems-theory]], the [[human-web]], and [[big-history]] as complementary lenses on
the same connectivity. This pushes the "proto-globalization" idea back **well before** the
medieval and early-modern horizons this page otherwise centers on.
