# The Discovery of Religion (Origins of Comparative Religion)

## Definition

This concept covers the **early modern intellectual revolution** that created the
modern, comparative, non-theological study of religion — what Guy Stroumsa (CWH
VI) calls the **"discovery of religion."** His central claim is chronological and
revisionist: comparative religion was **not** born with the university chairs of
the late nineteenth century (under the influence of Cuvier's comparative anatomy
and Darwin), but with a **paradigm shift in the seventeenth century**, when a
small number of scholars — Catholic and Protestant, usually working alone —
learned to recognize the world's many religions, past and present, as
**variant expressions of a single universal human phenomenon** rather than simply
as *true religion* versus *idolatry*.

## The Paradigm Shift

The older, binary, Augustinian frame opposed *vera religio* to *falsa religio*, with
religion understood as an interior, personal "right belief." The early modern shift
made religion an **objective**, comparable, social phenomenon — "a central aspect
of any society, endowed with a different function in each one." Drawing on Wilfred
Cantwell Smith, Stroumsa notes that after the Reformation religion came
increasingly to be identified with a **system of beliefs and practices** (the
seventeenth century favoring *dromena*, acts; the eighteenth, *legomena*, the
underlying words). Stroumsa resists the fashionable language of the **"invention"
of religion**: religion had always been there (Herodotus, the Hebrew Bible, and
Cicero all recognized it); what was new were the **categories** for analyzing it
synoptically.

## Three Causes

Stroumsa identifies three historical preconditions:

1. **The Great Discoveries.** The dramatic change of scale in cross-cultural
   contact (see [[columbian-exchange-1492]], [[intercultural-relations-early-modern]])
   confronted Europeans with the religions of the Americas and of literate Asia —
   the Americas and South/East Asia becoming the "first laboratories" where Spanish
   and Italian missionaries (Acosta, Las Casas) invented new categories. The
   problem of **idolatry** and of "political religion" (ritual serving the state)
   was a central preoccupation.
2. **The Renaissance and philology.** Renewed interest in antiquity and the growth
   of modern philology enabled the study of classical and oriental languages and
   the publication of texts from many cultures.
3. **The Wars of Religion.** The violent fracture of Christendom (the Reformation
   and its wars; see [[protestant-reformation-1517]]) shook the claim of any one
   faith to express perennial divine truth — Ottoman tolerance furnishing an
   uncomfortable contrast — and pushed the best minds toward seeing religions as
   reflecting the values of particular societies.

## Significance and the "Crisis of European Consciousness"

Stroumsa locates the breakthrough in the early-to-mid seventeenth century,
connected to Paul Hazard's "crisis of the European consciousness" (c.1680–1715;
Jonathan Israel pushes it back to the 1650s) — the same conjuncture that produced
Newtonian science, Cartesian and Spinozan philosophy, and the "Republic of
Letters." The comparative study of religion thus stands among the **human
sciences born of that crisis**, a phenomenological approach to religion three
centuries before the term existed.

## Contestation

The thesis is **revisionist** against the standard nineteenth-century origin story
and against the "invention of religion" school (which holds that "religion" as a
universal category is itself a modern, Western — even colonial — construct).
Stroumsa's middle position — that the *category* was newly forged but the
*phenomenon* was always recognized — is itself debated.
