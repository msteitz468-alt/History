# Neo-Babylonian Civilization (Society, Economy, and Learning)

The institutional and intellectual world of the [[neo-babylonian-empire|Neo-Babylonian
Empire]] (626–539 BCE) — its society, law, temple-and-trade economy, and the
extraordinary flowering of **mathematics, astronomy, and literature** that was
Babylonia's most lasting bequest to the world. Where [[assyrian-civilization|Assyrian
civilization]] is reconstructed from royal annals and palace reliefs, Neo-Babylonian
society is known from **tens of thousands of everyday legal and administrative
tablets** — making it, paradoxically, one of the best-documented societies of
antiquity at the level of ordinary economic life. [[boardman-cah-v3p2-1991|CAH Vol.
III, Part 2]] treats it in three chapters: **28a** (M. A. Dandamaev, society and
economy), **28b** (Asger Aaboe, mathematics and astronomy), and **28c** (Erica
Reiner, literature).

## The documentary base

Dandamaev opens by stressing the **richness of the sources**: the under-ninety years
of the dynasty are documented by "**tens of thousands of texts**," over ten thousand
published — promissory notes, sale/lease/gift contracts, hire of slaves and freemen,
trade documents, lawsuits, official and family correspondence. Crucially, almost all
come from **temple and private archives**: the **state archives are lost**, probably
because the chancellery increasingly used **Aramaic on perishable leather and
papyrus**, while cuneiform clay survived. This shapes everything we can and cannot
know.

## Society: citizens, aliens, and the assembly

Neo-Babylonian society was layered by **civic status**, not the bureaucratic rank
that ordered Assyria:

- **Citizens (*mār banî*)** of the privileged cult-cities (Babylon, Borsippa, Sippar,
  Nippur, Uruk) were free, legally equal, exempt from military conscription and
  corvée (*kidinnūtu*), and organized in a **popular assembly (*puhru*)** around the
  city's chief temple. The assembly acted as an organ of **local self-government and
  justice** — handling private disputes, theft of temple property, inheritance,
  family suits — usually presided over by the royal city-governor and temple
  administrator. The status was **inheritable**; only the wealthy played an active
  role, and decisions were often taken by the **elders (*šībūtu*)** alone. Over the
  dynasty the long struggle between crown and assembly ended in the **assembly's
  defeat**, its jurisdiction shrinking to local matters.
- **Freemen without civic rights** — manumitted slaves, and **aliens**. Babylonia was
  **ethnically mixed**: Chaldaeans and Aramaeans alongside the old population, plus
  large alien communities (Egyptians, Persians, Cilicians, Jews, "Ionians,"
  "fugitives from Media"). Dandamaev stresses an absence of "racial hostility";
  aliens owned property, married locally, gradually **assimilated** (keeping the
  ethnic term as a family name), and where densely settled — like the **Jewish
  exiles near Nippur** — kept their own elders and self-government. **Egyptians**
  alone among aliens often appear as **cuneiform scribes**.
- **Dependent populations** — the agricultural serf-like **\*ikkaru** (tied to the
  land, not sold, but unable to leave without permission) and **slaves**.

## Law

Justice ran through both the **people's assembly** and the **king's tribunal** (a
council of five or six professional judges, in Sippar headed by the high priest of
Ebabbar). The king was the highest judicial authority but **not absolute** — he could
not arbitrarily seize property or life. **Hammurabi's laws** were still copied and
studied (the thirty-fold restitution for temple theft remained in force), and a
fragmentary set of genuinely Neo-Babylonian laws survives, mostly on **marriage and
property**. Contracts were written by professional scribes before 3–10 witnesses, in
duplicate, sealed (debtors pressing a thumbnail). Loans normally ran at **20% annual
interest**, no longer legally capped. **Debt-bondage had declined** sharply: a creditor
could jail but not sell an insolvent debtor, self-sale had vanished, and Hammurabi's
time-limit on debt-slavery was obsolete — signs, Dandamaev argues, of a relatively
high standard of living. Pledges were commonly **antichretic** (the creditor exploiting
the labour of the pledged slave or person against the interest).

## The temple-and-private economy

In Neo-Babylonian times the **royal economy weighed less** than the **temples and
great private households**:

- **The crown** held only a small land-fund managed like a private estate; revenue
  came from **taxes (mostly in kind), tolls, and dues**, supporting the administration
  and army. The realm was divided into districts under governors (*bēl pīhāti*) and
  cities under *bēl ṭēmi*.
- **The temples** (Esagila in Babylon, **Eanna in Uruk**, Ebabbar in Sippar, Ezida in
  Borsippa, etc.) were huge economic engines — Eanna owned **5,000–7,000 cattle and
  100,000–150,000 sheep**, advanced loans, and ran commercial business. They drew the
  **tithe** from the whole free population, employed scores of **scribe-accountants**,
  and farmed vast tracts through **rent-collectors** who sub-leased to free
  leaseholders and *ikkaru* (one contract leases Eanna 7,410 hectares with 400
  *ikkaru* and 400 oxen). Under [[nabonidus|Nabonidus]] the crown tightened its grip,
  installing **royal commissioners** and a **"king's chest"** to siphon temple revenue.
- **Agriculture** rested on **irrigation** (state, temple, and private canals), with
  **barley and dates** the staples; land was costly, favouring date-palm gardens over
  cereal. **Craft** passed father-to-son but without guilds; craftsmen (free or
  privileged slaves) worked for temples and clients, and slaves were apprenticed
  (15 months to 6–8 years) because a skilled slave was far more profitable.

## Slavery and free labour — the limits of slave economy

Dandamaev's analytically central argument: although slavery was legal and prisoners
abundant, **the Babylonian economy could not absorb mass slavery**. Slave labour was
"less effective and needed constant supervision"; with no large workshops or
*latifundia* (except temple estates), **free hired labour and small leaseholders**
formed the backbone of production. Hence the **Jewish exiles of 597/587 were not
enslaved** but settled on state land near Nippur as taxpaying farmers — a point that
qualifies the Biblical image of "bondage." Privileged slaves could own property,
seals, even other slaves, and pay quit-rent (*peculium*); but only their masters
could free them, and temple slaves never. Free hired workers — boatmen, brick-makers,
canal-diggers — were comparatively well paid (averaging 12 shekels/year) and even
**struck for unpaid wages**, knowing they could not be replaced by slaves.

## Trade and money

Under the Chaldaeans Babylonia prospered as the **hub between the Mediterranean and
the Gulf/Iran**. Great **business houses** — above all the **Egibi** of Babylon
(active c. 700–500 BCE) — bought and sold land, houses, and slaves and ran **banking
operations** (deposits, promissory notes, financing ventures). International commerce
ran in **partnership companies** with proportional profit-sharing: alum and linen
from Egypt, dye, wine, honey, cedar from Syria/Phoenicia, iron and copper from
Cilicia and "Ionia," Greek (Athenian) pottery, all flowing to Babylon. **No coinage
was used**: payment was in **weighed silver ingots** of stamped, standardized purity,
with gold treated as a commodity (ratio ~13.6:1 to silver). Dandamaev gives a full
price-table (a *kur* of barley = 1 shekel; an ox ~20 shekels; a slave ~1 mina).

## Mathematics

Aaboe (28b) makes the large claim that opens his chapter: "**In no domain has the
influence of ancient Mesopotamia on Western civilization been more profound and
decisive than in theoretical astronomy and, principally through it, mathematics.**"
The backbone was the **sexagesimal (base-60) place-value system** — the most efficient
number system of antiquity, reducing arithmetic to routine via multiplication and
reciprocal tables, and surviving today in our division of **hours and degrees**.
Babylonian mathematics ran strongly to **algebra and number theory**: solutions of
first- and second-degree equations (by the still-current quadratic "formula"), correct
areas and volumes, free use of the **"Pythagorean theorem" over a millennium before
Pythagoras** (the tablet **Plimpton 322** implies fifteen Pythagorean triples). But it
**never stated a theorem in general terms or anything like a proof**.

## Astronomy — the ancestor of the exact sciences

The astronomical texts come chiefly from **three archives**: [[nineveh|Ashurbanipal's
library]] (the celestial-omen series ***Enūma Anu Enlil***), the great **astronomical
archive at [[babylon|Babylon]]**, and Uruk. Aaboe distinguishes three classes:

- **Omen astrology** — the ~70 tablets of *Enūma Anu Enlil* (judicial astrology
  concerning king and country), plus *Mul Apin* and the Royal Reports (c. 700 BCE).
  **Personal astrology / horoscopes** are attested in cuneiform from **410 BCE**,
  establishing **Babylonian priority** over the Hellenistic world.
- **Astronomical Diaries** — running from c. **750 BCE to 75 CE** (the latest datable
  cuneiform text), recording, month by month, the Moon's progress past the **Normal
  Stars**, planetary phases, eclipses, weather, **commodity prices**, river levels,
  and terse **historical notes** (e.g. Babylon greeting Alexander's troops, and
  Alexander's death).
- **Mathematical astronomy (ACT type)** — the **fourth–first centuries BCE** zenith:
  purely **arithmetical** lunar and planetary theories (Systems **A** and **B**) using
  **step functions** and **zigzag functions** to predict phenomena, with no geometric
  models or "orbits." Aaboe sees in it "the **first successful attempt** at giving a
  mathematical description of a class of natural phenomena capable of yielding
  numerical predictions testable against observation" — the defining program of the
  exact sciences ever since.

**Transmission:** via **Hipparchus** (c. 150 BCE) — who took Babylonian parameters,
observations, and the very *idea* of numerically predictive theory — and **Ptolemy**
(whose king-list runs from **Nabonassar, 747 BCE**, and who used the lunar System B
month-value), Babylonian astronomy became "the common ancestor of modern efforts in
the exact sciences." Neugebauer's 1988 reading of a Greek papyrus giving a column of
lunar System B confirmed how deeply Hellenism absorbed it.

## Literature

Reiner (28c) stresses that we know not what was **composed** in first-millennium
Babylonia but what was **kept** in libraries — the "**stream of tradition**"
(Oppenheim) of [[nineveh|Ashurbanipal's library]], Sultantepe, Babylon, and Seleucid
Uruk, much of it copied from second-millennium originals. "Literature" here is broad,
including the standardized works of **divination, magic, ritual, and scholarship**;
true *belles-lettres* were a small fraction (~one-third of ~1,500 tablets). The major
**narrative poems**:

- **Gilgamesh** — "still the most appealing work of ancient Near Eastern literature,"
  a twelve-tablet *éducation sentimentale* on mortality and friendship, compared by
  Reiner to the *Iliad*; it contains the **Flood story** whose 1872 discovery made
  Mesopotamian literature famous.
- ***Enūma Eliš*** (Poem of Creation) — the seven-tablet theomachy in which **Marduk**
  defeats Tiamat and is exalted with fifty names; a "theological justification of
  Marduk's rise" with a remarkably brief account of creation but detailed attention
  to the **calendar** (the Babylonian preoccupation).
- **Atrahasis** — the older flood-and-creation epic; **Anzu**, **Descent of Ishtar**,
  **Nergal and Ereshkigal**, **Adapa**, **Etana**.
- ***Erra (Irra) Epic*** — uniquely tied to a **historical event** (the plague and
  troubles at Babylon, 765 BCE), its fourth tablet a dramatic "**Destruction of
  Babylon**"; its named poet claims it was revealed to him whole in a dream.
- **"Pseudo-autobiographies" / *narû* literature** — first-person royal poems (Birth
  Legend of Sargon, Marduk Prophecy) and the prose **autobiography of Nabonidus's
  mother Adad-guppi**, ending in a lesson to a future king; **propaganda** works like
  *Advice to a Prince* and the *Verse Account of Nabonidus*.

Akkadian **hymns and prayers** grew long and learned (the 200-line hymn to Shamash,
god of justice, with its ethical vignettes); **rhyme was unknown** but **acrostics
and telestichs** (often spelling the author's name) were used; the organizing
principle of the verse was **parallelism**, as in Hebrew and Ugaritic poetry. By this
period **Aramaic** had replaced Akkadian as the spoken and everyday written language,
yet the cuneiform "stream of tradition" was copied for a thousand years more for
religious, scholarly, and display purposes.

## Significance and limitations

The chapters together correct the view of Neo-Babylonia as a mere political afterglow
of Assyria: it was a society of **privileged self-governing cities, a temple-and-trade
economy with limited slavery and a real role for free labour**, and the **intellectual
summit of Mesopotamian civilization** — the source of the exact sciences. The
**limitations** are candidly stated: Dandamaev notes the documentary record, though
vast, is "insufficiently investigated," with no state archives; Aaboe stresses that
the **theories behind the astronomy must be reconstructed** by modern scholars (no
ancient text states them) and that the creators are **anonymous**; Reiner emphasizes
the **near-impossibility of dating or periodizing** compositions known only from late
library copies. Source bias toward [[nabonidus|Nabonidus]]-era propaganda
([[fall-of-babylon-539bce|Cyrus's accounts]]) demands critical handling.
