# Genocide

## Definition

**Genocide** — coined by the Polish-Jewish jurist **Raphael Lemkin** in *Axis Rule in
Occupied Europe* (1944) and codified in the **UN Genocide Convention (1948)** as "acts
committed with intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial
or religious group" — is, Mark Levene argues in [[mcneill-cwh-v7-2015|CWH VII]], one
of the few scholarly terms to have achieved "ubiquitous and general usage in a matter
of decades," recognized by a global public even as scholars vigorously dispute its
precise definition.

## Emic / Etic

**Etic**, and very recent — a mid-twentieth-century legal-analytical coinage. Lemkin
built it (from Greek *genos*, "people/race," + Latin *-cide*) precisely to name a
phenomenon for which no prior word existed, and campaigned almost single-handedly for
its codification in international law (see [[international-law]]).

## Lemkin's insight: the homogenizing nation state

Levene situates the concept in what he calls "arguably the major, long-lasting
world-historical change of the Great War": **the drive toward culturally homogeneous
national states in place of historically multi-ethnic empires.** The ascendancy of
[[nationalism|national states]] over the Ottoman, Russian, Austrian, and German
imperial orders threw into relief the diverse minorities who did not "fit" the
national self-image. Lemkin — himself a member of a "supranational Jewish group" the
1919 Minorities Treaties had failed to protect — saw the **potential of the nation
state to murder or "suffocate out of existence" national cultures lacking the
security or means to form their own states.** This is why Levene treats genocide not
as an aberration but as a **"systemic by-product of a modern, world-historical
trajectory."**

## Perpetrator-defined victims

Levene stresses (against any primordialist reading) that the victim "group" is often
a **construction of the perpetrators**, not a coherent community: by the mid-twentieth
century European Jews shared no single binding cultural, religious, or biological
trait, yet the Nazis "knew" them as one racial entity; the destruction of "kulaks" or
Vendéens shows genocide may have "little or nothing to do with the victims' actual
communal attributes." Zygmunt Bauman's two state strategies toward its "strangers"
frame the dynamic: the **anthropophagic** (forcible assimilation into "clones of the
dominant self") and the **anthropoemic** (expulsion/deportation) — with the distance
from these to "wholesale annihilation" narrower than usually assumed.

## The Holocaust and its hinterland

The destruction of close to **six million European Jews** (~72% of those under Nazi
hegemony) is, Levene notes, "the one case universally recognised" as genocide:
transnational, sustained across four years of total war, sparing neither men, women,
nor children. The "Final Solution" emerged from the Nazi failure to expel Jews beyond
Europe and the stalling of Operation Barbarossa, beginning with the mass shooting of
~2.5 million Soviet Jews and escalating to the death camps (Auschwitz-Birkenau).
Levene resists treating it in isolation, placing it within a **"geographically
interconnected sequence of mid-century genocides"**: the Ustaša assault on Serbs,
Chetnik killings of Muslims, the UPA's killing of Poles in Volhynia — and, crucially,
the **Stalinist** assaults of the late 1920s–30s, above all the **Holodomor** (the
1932–33 terror-famine, ~4 million Ukrainian deaths within ~7.7 million Soviet deaths).
All represent surges toward the homogeneous state begun half a century earlier with
the empires' collapse.

## Roots in the long nineteenth century

Following the volume's introduction, Levene links twentieth-century genocide to
nineteenth-century legacies: the rise of the homogenizing national state; the extreme
violence of "high [[imperialism]]" (the Herero genocide, and the
**[[congo-free-state|Belgian Congo]]** — Leopold II's rubber regime, documented by
**[[hochschild-king-leopolds-ghost|Hochschild]]**, whose ~10-million demographic
collapse is a standard contested borderline case, turning on the intent threshold); and the
**social-evolutionary and racial thought** that rationalized the elimination of
"inferior" groups — a "toxic brew" especially likely when rulers felt "behind" in a
merciless inter-state competition.

## Historiography and contested questions

- **Definition:** scholars dispute the Convention's restriction to national/ethnic/
  racial/religious groups (excluding political and social groups), and the centrality
  of "intent."
- **Singularity vs. comparability of the Holocaust:** an argument for its
  uniqueness must be weighed against synchronous mid-century genocides; this is a
  genuine, unresolved interpretive dispute (one the wiki records without adjudicating,
  per the Contradiction Protocol).
- **Enforcement gap:** universal acclaim for the 1948 Convention did not translate
  into willingness to invoke it — Levene roots this in "the raison d'être of the
  nation state qua modern sovereign polity," whose drive for cohesion makes it
  reluctant to constrain itself.
