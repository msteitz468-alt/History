# Plan XVII

Plan XVII was the French army's final pre-war concentration and deployment plan for a war against Germany. Though officially a concentration plan rather than a detailed war plan, its main body stated: "The intention of the commander-in-chief is to deliver, with all forces assembled, an attack against the German armies." It set the framework for French operations in the opening of the Great War.

## Definition and Scope

Completed under Joffre, Plan XVII organized five armies in the northeast for an offensive posture. It envisaged French forces occupying a central strategic position from which they could respond to German moves through Lorraine, Luxembourg, or Belgium. The plan provided options for attacks north or south of the Metz-Thionville fortifications or northward into Belgium (toward Arlon and Neufchâteau). It was not a rigid blueprint for maneuvers; Joffre retained flexibility to decide final strategy and operations after mobilization when more political, diplomatic, and military information became available.

## Development and Key Changes from Prior Plans

Earlier Plans XI–XVI (1891–1909) rested primarily on a defensive-offensive strategy: initial defense behind fortifications (Verdun-Toul-Épinal-Belfort lines built under Séré de Rivières), followed by counterattack. Attention to a possible German advance through Belgium or Luxembourg grew after intelligence (the "Avenger" 1904 revelations) and de Lacroix's 1908 analysis of German envelopment traditions. Plan XVI (1909) increased forces north/northwest of Verdun and created a Sixth Army reserve near Châlons for counterattack, but still emphasized a relatively shallow advance.

After Michel's ouster (July 1911), Joffre immediately revised Plan XVI (published September 6, 1911): shifted forces from the Italian frontier and second-line/reserve units into the front line (increasing immediately available divisions from 38 to 58 claimed); moved Fifth Army farther left (to Mézières); pushed Sixth Army closer to Verdun/Belgian frontier west of Luxembourg. This placed two armies (seven corps) nearer the Belgian border and placed greater emphasis on an *immediate offensive* rather than defensive-offensive.

Over the following 18+ months, Joffre and staff refined the concept amid analysis of German alternatives (respecting neutrals; limited advance via Luxembourg/eastern Belgium; or main drive through Belgium). Joffre became convinced the Germans intended a deep advance through Belgium (fortifications between Metz-Thionville reinforced). Political authorities (Caillaux, then Poincaré) refused permission for French preemptive entry into Belgium absent a "positive menace," forcing the plan to accommodate this constraint while preparing for an offensive.

The April 1913 presentation to the Superior Council of War received unanimous support. After ministerial approval, the plan was published in stages.

## Deployment under Plan XVII

- **Armies** (right to left): First, Second, Third, and Fifth along the German/Luxembourg/Belgian frontier. Fourth Army in reserve behind Second/Third (to shift right or left as needed).
- Fifth Army (five infantry corps + cavalry corps) held a broad front from east of Luxembourg's border with France west beyond Mézières to Hirson.
- Four "Reserve Division groups" (three divisions each): positioned to support flanks and central armies.
- **Missions**:
  - First and Second: prepare to attack south of Metz-Thionville.
  - Third: focus on Metz-Thionville (invest or repel forces from the fortifications).
  - Fifth: prepare to attack north of Metz-Thionville or into Belgium (Neufchâteau if Germans entered; retain forces on Belgian frontier if not).
  - Fourth (if Germans in Belgium): advance toward Arlon on right of Fifth and left of Third.
- Alternatives preserved flexibility for Lorraine thrust or Belgian maneuver.

A secret annex addressed British "Army W" (six infantry + one cavalry divisions expected ~16 days after mobilization, to act on left of French army).

## Relation to Allies and Intelligence Assumptions

The plan operated within the 1892–94 Franco-Russian military convention requiring simultaneous offensives ("engaged to the utmost, as quickly as possible") to force Germany to fight on two fronts. Staff talks (1910–1913) committed France to a "vigorous and determined" offensive with nearly all forces on the northeastern frontier beginning ~day 11; Russia offensive on day 14 into the "heart" of Germany. "Defeat of the German armies remains... the first and principal objective."

Joffre and planners expected a German advance through Luxembourg and Belgium but underestimated the depth and scale: they calculated Germany lacked sufficient active corps for a deep drive west of the Meuse-Sambre (Liège-Namur-Charleroi-Maubeuge) without heavily integrating reserves as "shock" troops (French intel knew German plans called for using reserves like active but assessed they remained lighter and less capable for this). German war games and handbooks were acquired but did not alter the core underestimation of the right-wing envelopment.

## Flaws and Command Philosophy

Plan XVII placed three-quarters of the French army south of Verdun, setting up an advance into Alsace-Lorraine despite Joffre's own arguments to politicians about the difficulties there. It reflected confidence in an immediate offensive and the short-war assumption. Joffre concealed full intentions from political superiors and even some commanders to avoid "meddling" and premature commitment (e.g., response to Dubail in early August 1914: "That's your plan, not mine").

The plan reflected the new High Command powers: Joffre had authority over training, doctrine, regulations, mobilization, and concentration. Per 1913 regulations on large units, the government fixed the political objective and distributed means; the commander-in-chief conducted strategy and operations.

## Historiography and Limitations

Doughty emphasizes Plan XVII as a concentration plan granting Joffre latitude rather than a fixed operational script. It enabled the "miracle of the Marne" recovery but exposed misjudgments about German reserve employment and Belgian depth that contributed to the Frontiers disasters. Later criticism focused on force maldistribution and underestimation of the German right wing (Gallieni and Lanrezac, commanders of Fifth Army, raised pre-war doubts about Fifth Army's strength and mission for a Belgian advance). The plan's offensive intent aligned with but was distinct from the broader "offensive à outrance" doctrinal emphasis.
