# Ancient Mediterranean Ecology

The physical stage on which Greco-Roman economic life played out: a narrow, low-productivity
inland sea ringed by young, tectonically active mountains, whose short-run volatility and
long-run climatic drift shaped agriculture, disease, and settlement. Developed here from
**Robert Sallares's** ecological chapter in [[scheidel-cambridge-economic-history-greco-roman-world-2007|The Cambridge Economic History of the Greco-Roman World]] (2007).

## Physical Geography

The Mediterranean Sea runs roughly 3,800 km end to end. Evaporation exceeds river inflow, so the
basin depends on a constant Atlantic inflow current; the sea is consequently low in nutrients and
poor in fish relative to its size. The African–Eurasian plate collision (closing at roughly 2 cm a
year) produced the mountainous, seismically active terrain surrounding it — limestone geology,
uneven topography, and a scarcity of large flat plains, which fragmented agricultural land into
small, dispersed holdings.

## Climate History

The now-classic "Mediterranean climate" — hot dry summers, mild wet winters — has existed only
since roughly 3000 BCE. Neolithic rainfall was higher and more evenly distributed, supporting
deciduous oak and lime forest where evergreen scrub now dominates; Mediterranean staple crops like
the vine are consequently poorly adapted to their own namesake climate (growing through summer
drought, dormant in winter), since too little time has passed for genetic adaptation. Dendrochronology
from the Parthenon's timbers, together with Theophrastus's fourth-century BCE botanical observations
(he is effectively the founder of plant biogeography), confirm the modern climatic pattern was
already in place in classical Greece.

Interannual rainfall variability was severe even within this pattern: droughts around 360 and 330
BCE in Greece bracketed a wet spell that flooded Lake Copais before the Battle of Chaeronea (338
BCE); a mild spell visible in tree-ring records coincides with Hannibal's crossing of the Alps
(late third century BCE).

### The Roman Warm Period

Multiple independent proxies — Alpine glacier advance/retreat records, Iceland Basin sediment
grain-size, mercury deposition in Galician peat bogs — indicate a "Roman Warm Period" peaking
around 150 CE, possibly some 2°C warmer than the present and more sustained than the later
Medieval Warm Period. Corroborating evidence includes the spread of viticulture to Roman Britain
(Wollaston, the Nene Valley) and olive cultivation near Sagalassos in southwest Anatolia, both
beyond the crops' present-day tolerance zones. Sallares argues the warming's economic effects were
regionally uneven: likely benefiting arable farming in northern Europe while potentially harming
agriculture in the semi-arid Near East.

## Catastrophes

The record includes Vesuvius's destruction of Pompeii and Herculaneum (79 CE); Etna eruptions in
44–42 BCE with probable short-term climatic effects; earthquakes destroying Helike (373 BCE) and
Olympia (426 CE); and locust plagues recorded by Livy in North Africa (125 BCE) and
Campania/the Pontine Marshes/Apulia (173 BCE, severe enough to require Roman army intervention).
Tiber flooding deposited sediment that raised the Roman Forum's ground level several meters above
its archaic strata; Tacitus records a Senate debate on flood control in 15 CE (*Annals* 1.76, 1.79).
Expanding coastal floodplains, such as near Ostia, created fertile new land but also expanded
malarial breeding grounds.

## The Deforestation Debate

Whether the ancient Mediterranean suffered a large-scale, basin-wide "ruined landscape" through
deforestation is a genuinely contested question among specialists, with no consensus on scale or
chronology. Sallares, weighing the minimalist position of Grove and Rackham against the more
maximalist reading of Hughes and McNeill, concludes that human impact varied greatly by locality
and that no single generalization validly covers the whole basin.

## Pollution and Metallurgy

Sharp rises in lead and copper deposition — visible in Greenland ice cores, Swedish lake
sediments, and Swiss and Spanish peat bogs from around 600 BCE onward — mark the onset of
large-scale ancient mining and metallurgy. The Laurium silver-lead mines that funded the Athenian
navy and empire were, on this evidence, "the first major source of anthropogenic pollution in
world history." Cinnabar mining at Almadén in Spain is attested from the fifth century BCE.

## Agriculture and Polyculture

The characteristic Mediterranean agricultural regime — cereals (wheat and barley), olives, and
vines grown in combination — existed in the Near East from around 3000 BCE but reached the
western Mediterranean only in the first millennium BCE, carried by Greek and Phoenician trade and
colonization from roughly 800 BCE. Barley, more drought- and poverty-tolerant, was favored in arid
or poor regions (Attica, North Africa) and among the poor generally; wheat was the preferred grain
of the wealthy, a class distinction attested in Xenophon, Theophrastus, Josephus, and the Spartan
common messes. Grafting — possibly transmitted from Far Eastern citrus cultivation — enabled the
first-millennium-BCE domestication of the apple, sweet and sour cherry, pear, and pistachio.

## Disease Ecology

Mean life expectancy at birth is conventionally put at 20–30 years across antiquity (developed
further at [[ancient-demography]]). Skeletal evidence attests brucellosis (goat-milk-borne) in
about a fifth of Herculaneum's 79 CE victims, and thalassaemia (a malaria-resistance trait) and
treponematosis at Metapontum, linked there to the site's marshy Hellenistic-period decline.

Epidemic disease left a documented but contested record: the "Plague of Athens" (430–426 BCE,
described by Thucydides) killed roughly a third of the city's population and, on modern
retrodiagnosis, was more plausibly smallpox or typhus than bubonic plague; the "Antonine Plague"
(latter half of the second century CE, roughly two decades, probably smallpox) may have killed
about a third of the population in some regions; the mid-third-century "Plague of Cyprian" remains
unidentifiable. True bubonic plague (*Yersinia pestis*) only becomes epidemiologically significant
with the Plague of Justinian in the sixth century CE, though the black rat (*Rattus rattus*)
vector had been spreading silently through the Roman world for centuries beforehand.

Malaria (*Plasmodium falciparum*, temperature-dependent) was endemic across western-central Italy
and Rome, southern Italy, Sicily, Sardinia, North Africa, and the eastern Mediterranean, likely
carried to Italy from Greece and the Near East by ship-borne mosquitoes as trade intensified; Galen
in the second century CE regarded it as especially common at Rome. Sallares argues that Italy's
north/south economic divide, still visible today, has an ancient origin in falciparum malaria's
spread from North Africa into southern Italy.

## Significance

Sallares's chapter reframes Greco-Roman economic performance as bounded by an unusually
unforgiving physical environment — fragmented arable land, unpredictable rainfall, endemic
disease, and periodic catastrophe — against which the [[economic-efflorescence|economic growth]]
documented elsewhere in the volume looks more, not less, remarkable. It supplies the environmental
baseline for [[ancient-demography]] and for regional pages on Mediterranean agriculture.
