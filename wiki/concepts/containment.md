# Containment

**Containment** was the organizing grand strategy of the United States in the
[[cold-war|Cold War]]: the doctrine that, rather than rolling back or appeasing Soviet
power, the US should **prevent the further expansion of Soviet/Communist influence** by
sustained political, economic, and military counter-pressure until the Soviet system
mellowed or collapsed from within. Articulated by the diplomat **George F. Kennan** and
enacted under **Harry Truman**, it framed American policy — with variations — for four
decades. From [[leffler-westad-cold-war-v1|*The Cambridge History of the Cold War*, Vol. I]].

## Origins: Kennan and the security dilemma

In 1945 American grand strategy was, as Leffler shows, "a mystery to Truman and his
advisers." Truman alternated between hoping to "get along with" Stalin and frustration with
Soviet behavior in Eastern Europe, Iran, and Turkey ("I'm tired of babying the Soviets,"
1946). The intellectual breakthrough came from **George Kennan**, whose **"Long Telegram"**
(February 1946) and pseudonymous **"X" article** ("The Sources of Soviet Conduct,"

## From doctrine to policy

- **The Truman Doctrine (March 1947)** — aid to Greece and Turkey, and the open-ended
  pledge "to support free peoples who are resisting attempted subjugation," universalized
  the logic of containment.
- **The [[marshall-plan-1948|Marshall Plan]] (1947–52)** — the economic arm: reviving
  Western Europe so that Communist parties could not win power amid postwar misery.
- **[[nato|NATO]] (1949)** — the military arm: a transatlantic alliance to deter Soviet
  attack on Western Europe.
- **NSC-68 (1950)** — the document (drafted by Paul Nitze) that **militarized** containment,
  calling for a massive arms build-up; the [[korean-war-1950|Korean War]] (1950) provided the
  political shock that funded it, roughly tripling US defense spending.

## Variants and the debate

Containment was contested from the start, including by Kennan himself, who insisted he had
meant a **selective, political** containment of a few vital industrial regions, not the
**global, militarized** version that NSC-68 and later administrations adopted (and that
drew the US into [[korean-war-1950|Korea]] and later Vietnam). Critics on the right
(advocates of "rollback" / liberation) thought it too passive; critics on the left thought
it self-fulfilling, provoking the very hostility it claimed to contain. The wiki's
historiography of Cold War origins (traditionalist / revisionist / post-revisionist) turns
in part on how one reads containment — as prudent defense or as expansion.

## Significance

Containment supplied the **conceptual through-line** of US Cold War policy from 1947 to
1989, generating its institutions (NATO, the national-security state), its economic
statecraft (the Marshall Plan), and its proxy wars. Its eventual "success" — the internal
collapse of Soviet communism (1989–91) — was claimed by its defenders as vindication of
Kennan's original prediction that a contained USSR would ultimately mellow or break.

## Soviet Perspective on Containment (from Range 02, Pechatnov)

Kremlin viewed US policy, especially Marshall Plan, as deciding "to rebuild Western Europe (including the western part of Germany) as a junior partner in a transatlantic bloc against the USSR. Instead of a multipolar system... it now had to reckon with an American-led anti-Soviet global coalition." Marshall Plan seen as "Trojan horse" undermining Soviet security zone. Response: Cominform "two camps" discipline, full Sovietization acceleration, Berlin blockade gamble.

Stalin shifted to "policy of tenacity and steadfastness": "To get anything from such partners, we must arm ourselves with the policy of tenacity and steadfastness."
