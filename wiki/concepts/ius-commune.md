# The Revival of Law (Ius Commune — Roman and Canon Law, c. 1070–1200)

## Definition

The ***ius commune*** ("common law" in the continental sense) is the body of learned
**Roman and canon law** that was recovered, taught, and systematised in the eleventh
and twelfth centuries and became the shared juristic framework of medieval and
early-modern continental Europe. [[luscombe-ncmh-v4-2004|NCMH IV]] (Peter Landau,
Ch. 5, "The development of law") treats this legal revival as mirroring "the changes
that took place in every sphere of government" — the broad shift from oral custom and
the [[ordeal|ordeal]] toward written, reasoned, professionally administered law.

## The Two Laws

- **Roman law.** The rediscovery and study of Justinian's *Corpus Iuris Civilis*
  centred on **Bologna**, where **Irnerius** (fl. early 12th c.) turned the city into
  the centre of Roman-law studies, glossing the texts. Bologna can "justly claim to
  have been teaching and studying the law for longer than any other European
  university." Frederick Barbarossa's *Authentica Habita* (1155) granted its law
  teachers and (foreign) students legal privileges and protection — evidence that an
  organised community of scholars already existed; the student corporations
  (*universitates*) of *Citramontani* and *Ultramontani* governed by elected rectors
  made Bologna the prototype of the student-run [[medieval-university|university]].
- **Canon law.** The reform papacy's need for an ordered, authoritative law of the
  Church produced a succession of collections culminating in the ***Decretum*** of
  **Gratian** (c. 1140, the *Concordia discordantium canonum*) — a vast, dialectically
  arranged harmonisation of conflicting authorities that founded canon law as a
  scholarly discipline, taught alongside Roman law at Bologna and later supplemented
  by the papal *decretals* (the *Liber Extra* of 1234).

Together the *leges* (Roman law) and *canones* (canon law) formed the *utrumque ius*
("both laws") of the medieval jurist, and their fusion in teaching and practice is
the *ius commune*.

## Effects

- **The decline of the ordeal.** Across the period, irrational proofs (trial by
  ordeal, judicial combat) were slowly phased out in favour of "rational procedures
  for evaluating evidence" — a shift completed when the Fourth Lateran Council (1215)
  forbade clergy to participate in ordeals.
- **Professionalisation of government.** The making, imposition and interpretation of
  new **written law** — statutes, assizes, constitutions — required trained officials,
  driving the growth of literate administration in church and state alike.
- **A distinct English path.** Landau and the editors note that English **[[common-law|
  common law]]** descends from "the king's law" applied throughout the realm from the
  time of **[[henry-ii-of-england|Henry II]] (1154–89)** — a national, case-based,
  centrally administered system that diverged from the Roman-canonical *ius commune*
  of the continent, even as it shared the era's general move toward written,
  professional law.
- **The "feudal" law.** In Italian cities customary feudal practice was systematised
  in the written ***Libri Feudorum*** — the very text from which later jurists
  abstracted the misleading category of "[[feudalism|feudalism]]."

## Significance

The legal revival gave Latin Europe a **common juristic language and a professional
class of jurists**, underpinned the centralising claims of both papacy and monarchies
(canon law for the [[gregorian-reform|reform papacy]]; Roman law for imperial and
royal authority), and made law one of the three higher faculties of the new
universities. It is, with theology, the intellectual core of the
[[twelfth-century-renaissance]].

## From Logan, *A History of the Church in the Middle Ages* (2013)

[[logan-history-of-the-church-in-the-middle-ages-2013|Logan]] stresses that eleventh-century reform invoked **tradition and pristine practice**, not new decretals: **Burchard of Worms** (before 1020), a Roman collection in **74 titles**, **Ivo of Chartres** (1090s) — “much of the credit for establishing canon law as a scholarly discipline” — and **Anselm of Lucca** (mid-1080s) with canons strongly supporting papal power to reform. [[leo-ix|Leo IX]] used provincial synods, intimidation, and deposition against resistance. This pre-Gratian collecting tradition is the reform-era root of the learned canon law later fused into the *ius commune*.
