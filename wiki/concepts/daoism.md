# Daoism (Taoism)

One of the great currents of Chinese thought and religion, centred on harmony with the **Dao** (the
"Way") — the nameless, spontaneous source and pattern of all things — and on living by **non-action** and
**naturalness** rather than striving, convention or force. The contemplative counter-pole to
Confucianism's social engagement.

## Philosophical Daoism (*daojia*) — emic

The classical philosophy rests on two foundational texts:

- the **Laozi** (*Daodejing*, "Classic of the Way and Virtue"), attributed to the semi-legendary
  **Laozi**, aphoristic and political-mystical;
- the **[[zhuangzi-watson|Zhuangzi]]**, the work of **[[zhuangzi|Zhuang Zhou]]** and his school, literary
  and individualist.

Core ideas:
- the **Dao** — the ineffable Way that generates and orders the cosmos, beyond words and distinctions;
- ***wu wei*** — "non-action," acting **in accord with the natural course** rather than against it,
  without forcing or contriving;
- ***ziran*** — spontaneity, naturalness, "self-so";
- the **relativity of distinctions** and skepticism toward language, fixed values and conventional
  knowledge;
- the ideal of the **sage** who returns to simplicity, and (in the Laozi) the ruler who governs best by
  governing least.

## Religious Daoism (*daojiao*)

From the second century CE, Daoism also became an **organized religion** — with scriptures, priesthoods,
ritual, alchemy and the **quest for immortality** and longevity, a pantheon of deities, and movements
such as the Celestial Masters. It absorbed popular cults and interacted deeply with **Buddhism**.

## Significance and Critique

Daoism shaped Chinese **medicine, science, art, poetry and landscape sensibility**, the reception of
**Chan (Zen) Buddhism**, and a perennial **counter-current** to Confucian officialdom — many a scholar
was "Confucian in office, Daoist in retirement." Its political dimension (rule by *wu wei*) influenced
statecraft, and its naturalism appealed to modern ecological and anti-authoritarian readings. Scholars
note that the unified category "Daoism" loosely binds the **philosophical** texts and the later
**religious** movement, whose relationship is debated; "Laozi" and the dating of the *Daodejing* remain
contested.
