# Roman Imperial Cult

The organized veneration of Roman emperors (living and, especially, deceased) as divine or semi-divine, which spread across the empire from the reign of [[augustus]] onward and became a standard civic marker of loyalty to Rome.

## Definition and Origins

The imperial cult grew directly out of **Hellenistic ruler-cult** precedents — the divine honours accorded to Alexander's successors — and out of the older Greek habit of honouring benefactors as more-than-human. Julius Caesar was deified after death; Augustus was styled *divi filius* ("son of the deified") and, in the Greek East where ruler-worship was familiar, received cult in his lifetime, while in Rome he was cautious, accepting worship of his *genius* and full deification only posthumously. Successive emperors were voted *divus* status by the Senate upon death (Vespasian's dying quip, "I think I am becoming a god," captures the routine).

## Emic vs. Etic Readings

The cult can be read two ways, and Charles Freeman (following Simon Price) stresses both:
- **Etic** (the historian's political reading): a mechanism of loyalty and integration — a shared ritual language binding a vast, diverse empire to its centre. The Emperor Decius's edict of 249 requiring universal sacrifice, and the army's ritual calendar (the third-century Dura-Europos *Feriale* is dominated by imperial anniversaries), show the cult functioning as a test of allegiance — which is precisely why it put **Christians** in an impossible position.
- **Emic** (genuine provincial devotion): in the Greek cities the cult was often locally initiated, an idiom of civic prestige and real religious feeling, not mere flattery. Simon Price's thesis is that provincials worshipped the emperor because he was "the one individual who could actually get things done" — a genuine locus of power in their cosmos. The [[aphrodisias]] Sebasteion, built by local families, is the outstanding material witness to this.

## Spread, Syncretism, and Limits

The cult fused readily with local deities (Tertullian mocked the empire's patchwork of regional gods — Astarte in Syria, Dusares in Arabia, Caelestis in Africa), and shaded into the emergent third-century **henotheism** of a supreme "Highest God" (*Theos Hypsistos*). Provincial temples, priesthoods (often the summit of a local notable's career), games, and imperial anniversaries carried it into everyday civic life. Its central limitation was religious: monotheists — Jews (generally exempted) and Christians (not) — could not participate, making the cult a recurring flashpoint of persecution until Constantine.

## Critiques and Contested Points

Scholars debate how far provincials "believed" in imperial divinity versus performing political conformity — a false dichotomy in Price's influential account, which argues ancient categories did not separate religion from power as modern ones do. The degree of central direction versus local initiative is likewise contested; the evidence (Aphrodisias, the eleven Asian cities competing to host Tiberius's temple, with Smyrna winning) points strongly to provincial enthusiasm rather than imposition.
