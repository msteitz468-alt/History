# Limited Nuclear War

**Limited nuclear war** refers to the theoretical possibility of using nuclear weapons in a controlled, constrained fashion — with limits on numbers, yields, targets, geography, or duration — so that a conflict does not automatically escalate to all-out strategic exchange and societal destruction.

## Intellectual Origins and Rationale

The concept emerged in the 1950s as a reaction to the perceived bankruptcy of Massive Retaliation. If the choice was always between doing nothing and risking Armageddon, deterrence would fail against limited probes. Theorists argued for restoring usable options through discrimination in targeting (counterforce rather than countervalue), restrictions on yield and area, and explicit bargaining during conflict to establish "rules of the game."

Henry Kissinger's *Nuclear Weapons and Foreign Policy* (1957) was a prominent early statement. The idea fed into "graduated deterrence" proposals (British school led by Rear-Admiral Sir Anthony Buzzard) and influenced later exploration of city-avoidance and multiple options.

Graduated deterrence held that punishment should fit the crime and that "We should not cause, or threaten to cause, more destruction than is necessary." The threat of tactical atomic war was presented as "a terrible and tremendous deterrent in itself, without the additional threat of total, global war." Focus was on military rather than civilian targets. Colonel Richard Leghorn (US) proposed carefully graded responses: attacks on reinforcements and logistics, nuclear forces through attacks on stockpiles and delivery vehicles, with unilateral renunciation of city attacks unless the Free World cities were first hit. (Freedman 1981, LIMITED MEANS, pp.112-114).

Glenn Snyder elaborated the distinction between deterrence through denial (influencing the aggressor's estimate of probability of gaining the objective) and through punishment (influencing estimate of possible costs). Limited nuclear capabilities were argued to strengthen both. (Freedman 1981, LIMITED MEANS, p.112).

## Practical and Exercise Evidence

Military exercises cast grave doubts on the feasibility of limitation. In Operation Sage Brush (Louisiana, size of Greece and Portugal), after some seventy bombs each of not more than 40 kilotons yield dropped on military targets, umpires ruled that all life in the state had "ceased to exist". In Carte Blanche (West Germany), tactical weapons "used" only by NATO Allies; over two days 355 devices (mostly over West German territory) would have left up to 1.7 million West Germans dead and 3.5 million wounded — more than five times the number of German civilian casualties in World War II — even without residual radiation effects. West German public opinion was alarmed. (Freedman 1981, LIMITED MEANS, pp.109-110).

## 1970s Revival: Selective Options and Schlesinger Doctrine (Grounded in Freedman 1981 Range 08)

The concept was revived in the 1970s as the pure assured-destruction consensus eroded under Soviet build-up, parity anxieties, and moral/technical critiques. James Schlesinger articulated requirements for a force able to "implement a variety of limited pre-planned options and react rapidly to retargeting orders so as to deter any range of further attacks". Emphasis shifted to precise, low-collateral strikes against military targets (airfields, rail yards, oil facilities) in support of conventional operations, especially in Europe. (Freedman 1981, SELECTIVE OPTIONS, p.359, 383-384).

Schlesinger stressed "coupling" to Europe and distinguished alliance first-use (to honour commitments) from surprise strategic first strike. New technologies (improved accuracy to CEP under 1/6 mile, MIRVs, enhanced-radiation "neutron bomb") were promoted to make limited use appear more feasible and less catastrophic to defended populations. (Freedman 1981, p.352-353, 384-385).

The neutron bomb controversy (1976-77) illustrated limits: despite design to disable tank crews with reduced blast/heat, public characterization as "kills people, leaves property intact" and European fears of lowered nuclear threshold produced major political resistance. "The special fears aroused by any nuclear weapon... could not be resolved simply by greater tailoring." (Freedman 1981, p.385-386).

## European Theatre Specifics

NATO's existing tactical nuclear stockpile was large but obsolete — high yields, "dirty", disproportionate for battlefield use. Conceptual framework for theatre employment remained "inadequate and far less elaborate" than for central strategic systems. Schlesinger encouraged modernization for precision and control. The 1979 decision to station 572 US ground-based medium-range ballistic/cruise missiles in Europe aimed at a distinctive response to Soviet Euro-strategic modernization while preserving the link to central US forces. (Freedman 1981, p.386-387, 2095-2099 range).

Soviet doctrine offered little encouragement for limitation. Pronouncements were hostile; Sidorenko's *The Offensive* (1970) treated first nuclear use as a dramatic shift in which "nuclear weapons will become the main means for destroying the enemy in battle." Emphasis remained on victory and war-making capacity, not collateral control or negotiation pauses. (Freedman 1981, p.381, 1939-1945).

## PD59 and the Limits of Limitation (1980)

President Carter approved PD59 (July 1980) seeking capacity for "prolonged but limited nuclear war". Targets prioritized Soviet leadership, military forces, and control mechanisms; a "secure strategic reserve" would be withheld; retargeting based on real-time surveillance. Critics (including within Administration) noted difficulties: "what objectives an enemy would seek... how these campaigns would end, or how any resulting symmetries could be made meaningful." (Freedman 1981, THE POLITICAL SCIENCE OF NUCLEAR STRATEGY, p.393, Brown quote p.394).

Colin Gray/Keith Payne proposals for attacks aimed at political disintegration (crippling KGB, eliminating Moscow bureaucracy) echoed early airpower theorists (Douhet, Fuller) hoping strategic bombardment would detach elites from masses. Freedman observes this returned to "the crude political science of early airpower theorists" and that "when we even try to imagine how soldiers and statesmen will cope with the stress of the opening shots of a nuclear war... we can see how difficult it is to imagine the political and psychological state of the societies they represent". (Freedman 1981, p.394-395, 2554-2570).

## Verdict

Freedman concludes in the overall book verdict that "No operational nuclear strategy has yet to be devised that does not carry an enormous risk of degenerating into a bloody contest of resolve or a furious exchange of devastating and crippling blows against the political and economic centres of the industrialized world." The popular association of any nuclear use with "utter catastrophe" proved resistant to doctrinal refinement. (Freedman 1981, CONCLUSION, p.396, 2582-2586).
