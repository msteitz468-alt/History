# Populism

Populism is a contested political-analytical category describing movements and rhetoric that frame politics as a fundamental struggle between "the people" (conceived as a virtuous, homogeneous whole) and a corrupt, self-serving "elite" — political, economic, cultural, or media — claimed to have subverted popular sovereignty for narrow interests. Political scientist Cas Mudde influentially characterized it as a "thin-centered ideology" that can attach to a wide range of substantive left- or right-wing programs (economic redistribution, nationalism, anti-immigration policy, anti-corruption), rather than a fixed ideological content of its own.

Historically, the label originates with movements explicitly claiming it — the American People's (Populist) Party of the 1890s and Russian narodnichestvo — but has since been applied comparatively across an enormous range of cases: Latin American movements (Peronism), European interwar and postwar radical parties, and 21st-century figures across the political spectrum (Trump, various European nationalist parties, and left-populist movements alike), which scholars group by shared rhetorical structure (people-versus-elite framing, appeals to a unified popular will, suspicion of intermediary institutions) despite otherwise sharply divergent policy content.

The concept remains genuinely contested: critics note that "populism" is often applied asymmetrically as a pejorative by establishment commentators against movements outside the political mainstream, while defenders of the analytical category argue that its structural (rather than purely policy-content) definition allows meaningful comparison across otherwise very different left- and right-wing movements.
