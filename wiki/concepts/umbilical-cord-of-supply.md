# Umbilical Cord of Supply

van Creveld's vivid metaphor for the lines of communication that connect an army in the field to its base. Like an umbilical cord, these lines sustain the army but also restrict its movement and expose it to mortal danger if severed.

The longer and more complex the cord (especially in the railway and motorized eras), the greater the "friction" and the more strategy becomes subordinate to logistics.

Key implications:
- Armies cannot operate indefinitely far from their sources of supply without risking paralysis or collapse.
- Advances that look brilliant on a map often become impossible once the growing length and vulnerability of the umbilical cord are taken into account (classic example: German 1941 drive into Russia; Rommel in North Africa).
- Cutting or over-stretching the enemy's umbilical cord can be more decisive than winning tactical battles.
- In modern warfare the cord includes not just food/fodder/ammunition but fuel, spare parts, and the enormous maintenance tail required by mechanized forces.

The concept is central to understanding why "the products of the machine had finally superseded those of the field" and why mobility gains from technology have often been offset by increased logistic demands.

See also [[line-of-communication]], [[van-creveld-supplying-war-1977|Supplying War (van Creveld)]].

In the final synthesis of the work, van Creveld concludes that detailed logistic planning often proves conservative or inadequate in practice, and that "in the final account, it was the willingness - or lack of it - to override the plans, to improvise and take risks, that determined the outcome." The study ultimately affirms Napoleon's dictum "that, in war, the moral is to the physical as three to one." This underscores why the "umbilical cord" constraint frequently overrides apparent command genius or technological mobility gains.
