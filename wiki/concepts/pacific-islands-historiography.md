## Definition and Scope

Pacific Islands historiography encompasses the methods, debates, and perspectives applied to constructing histories of the peoples who inhabit Melanesia, Polynesia, and Micronesia. The field is unusually self-conscious about its own politics because, as Samoan historian Albert Wendt wrote in 1965, "what we think about the past of Samoa (and its future) is determined by what we are" ([[denoon-cambridge-pacific-islanders-1997]]). The recognition that history is an interested and situated practice arrived in Pacific studies early, driven partly by the scale of colonialism's distortions and partly by the richness of indigenous oral and genealogical traditions that resist assimilation into Western narrative formats.

A defining structural problem is the absence of pre-contact written sources: "Most obviously, we focus on the experiences of peoples who did not produce written accounts before the coming of Europeans." This makes ethnohistorical reconstruction from European-contact-era documents unavoidable and fraught, since "nearly all the sources are written by foreigners."

## The Tripartite Regional Division and Its Critique

The foundational geographical categories — Melanesia, Polynesia, Micronesia — were codified by the French explorer Dumont d'Urville in an address to the Geographical Society of Paris in the early 19th century. The categories had a basis in "linguistic and cultural affinities" but were also racially encoded from the start: "Melanesia means 'black islands', and nineteenth-century writers deterministically equated culture and behaviour with perceived racial categories." D'Urville and his contemporaries "saw the three regions as coterminous with human 'races'," producing stereotypes in which "Polynesians were sensual and hospitable; Melanesians were typically described as savage."

The conceptual separation of race and culture was a major project of early 20th-century anthropology, particularly through Franz Boas, who articulated "cultural relativism, the doctrine that all cultures are equally valid as human life-ways, and are not to be judged from the vantage point of Western values." Boasian "historical particularism" had "far-reaching influence on Pacific studies generally, particularly in the vehement rejection of racial determinism."

The tripartite scheme has come under sustained critique. Archaeologists and prehistorians find "a distinction between Near Oceania and Remote Oceania more useful for describing the stepwise settlement of the Pacific Islands." Scholars have "increasingly asserted that the 'regions' never were discrete entities, and if the labels suggest social, cultural, or racial isolation then they may be more misleading than illuminating." The Cambridge History itself retains the labels as "a useful shorthand" while "emphatically" rejecting "any equation of 'races' with the geographic labels."

## Colonial Historiography

Colonial history is characterised not as a period but as a style: it "situates agency, causality and effective power in the actions of the imperial nations and their citizens. Europeans are generally portrayed as actors, and Islanders as the acted upon." Three persistent colonial-historiographical tropes are identified: (1) Islander naivety and susceptibility to materialist appeals; (2) foreign introductions as the primary agents of change; (3) European first-contact events as the most significant moments in Island history.

The missionary John Williams exemplifies the first trope: his elaborated version of Fauea's speech promoting Christian conversion foregrounded Islander desire for Western technology. "Published histories seldom challenged the fundamental premises of Islander simple-mindedness and the irresistible appeal of Western technology." Colonial historians also frequently characterised Islanders as lazy, a charge that "typically sustained an argument for importing foreign labour" and whose origins can usually be traced to the interests of settler or plantation capital rather than to Islander behaviour.

Sylvia Masterman's 1934 *Origins of International Rivalry in Samoa* illustrates a colonial historiography that could be explicitly sympathetic while still invoking racial theory: she described the Samoans as passing "from a state of primitive but happy disorder, to a condition of semi-civilised but unhappy confusion" requiring external intervention.

A related variant is what Kerry Howe called "Fatal Impact" interpretations: histories that commiserated with Islanders while "portraying them as hapless victims of Western political and technical superiority." The label derives from Alan Moorehead's book.

## The 'Island-Oriented' Revision

The revisionist turn crystallised around James Davidson, "whose appointment in 1954 to the world's first Chair of Pacific History at the Australian National University is a benchmark date for the new historiography." Davidson and his students "valorised indigenous societies, recontextualised events in native cultural terms, and asserted 'the image of an active, initiative-taking savage whose way of life was not necessarily ravaged by European contact'." Importantly, Davidson began his histories of Samoa not with the arrival of explorers but "with descriptions of Samoan social structure and political institutions."

The post-colonial revisionists were "for the most part, Australian and New Zealand historians" whose position at the "British colonial periphery" informed their "critical" stance towards Great Power actions. Their challenge to colonial canards was genuine: Davidson called the Samoans "a 'politically capable people' who were unjustly treated as wards by the New Zealand administration."

However, the revisionist programme faced internal tensions. Kerry Howe's *Where the Waves Fall* was simultaneously charged by Caroline Ralston with smuggling in a "fatal impact" perspective (the very title defined Islands as places where "waves" land) and by Malama Meleisea with "underplaying the destructive effects of Western interference" by giving too much weight to native agency. "Howe was simultaneously charged with being too Eurocentric and too optimistic about Islanders' ability to withstand Western incursion."

## Indigenous Post-Colonial Historians

Western-trained Island scholars — including Samoan historians Eteuati, Meleisea, and Wendt; Tongan historian Epeli Hau'ofa; and Papua New Guinean John Waiko — have sought to produce "decolonised" histories. They use "the narrative format, adopt the objective authoritative voice of academic writing, and publish in English" while "attempting to assert a native cultural and political point of view."

Albert Wendt's 1965 thesis on the Mau rebellion exemplifies this position. He departs "radically from island-oriented historiography in recognising that history is an interested and subjective product." His thesis concludes with a self-reflexive dialogue that acknowledges the constructedness of all history: "A lot of history is written as wish-fulfilment, deliberately or otherwise . . . objectivity is for astronauts and moon-flights."

Meleisea's edited volume *Lagaga* articulates the Samoan view of historical knowledge as intrinsically proprietary: "For Samoans, knowledge is power, and the most powerful knowledge is historical knowledge: treasured and guarded in people's heads, in notebooks locked in boxes and matai's briefcases or with their precious mats under mattresses."

The limits of indigenous post-colonial historiography are sharp. Roger Keesing was attacked in the Solomons for publishing accounts of the 1927 'massacre' of a colonial officer because he had articulated "the representations of the wrong indigenes" — the Kwaio pagans rather than the post-colonial nationalist elite. Keesing argued that "the cultural nationalist discourse of a post-colonial elite, counterposing the indigenous and authentic to the foreign, alien, and exploitative, lays down ideological smokescreens that cover and hide the realities of class interest."

## Subaltern Historiography and the Decolonisation Question

The "subaltern historiography" associated with South Asian scholars (Ranajit Guha and the Subaltern Studies group) represents the most radical alternative yet to colonial frameworks. "Subaltern" denotes people "not in power and who do not belong to the dominant group: the difference may be gender, ethnicity, political standing, or economic class." A truly decolonised history must be "anti-foundational," breaking from the perspective of dominant classes.

The concept sits "uneasily with many Pacific intellectuals and foreign scholars" because it requires addressing "stratification and other forms of inequality within Island societies" — territory that nationalist anti-colonialism has resisted. Kerry Keesing noted that "the more sophisticated approaches of the best Third World scholarship have yet to wash up on Pacific shores."

As the volume itself concedes: "Even though it may be decolonized, Pacific history is still essentially a product of a Western intellectual tradition and world view." The tension between nationalist history (which shares colonial history's premise of bounded cultural essences) and a genuinely subaltern historiography remains unresolved.

## Indigenous Historical Genres

Indigenous Pacific historical genres are diverse and do not map onto Western "history." In Kiribati, genealogical traditions were called *mythistoire* ("myth-history") because, for Islanders, "the two are inseparable in indigenous conceptualisation." Hawaiian myth-history explains that "the taro plant is the older brother of humankind." Samoan oral genres catalogued by the Reverend Samuel Ella (~1847) included eleven distinct types: war songs, genealogies (*gafa*), popular songs on current events, and *fagogo* (narrative-songs with culture heroes). Genealogies were politically charged: "Hawaiian chiefly genealogies could be told differently according to immediate purpose; genealogical specialists would highlight particular lines of relationship to legitimise an alliance between chiefs or to make peace possible."

In modern Pacific oral contexts, "the 'mornings of the country' (*taeao o le atunu'u*)" — notable events cited in formal oratory — have shifted over time: "Nowadays most of the *taeao* cited are 'gospel mornings' marking the arrival of the first missionaries." This illustrates that "tradition" is "a model of the past that is symbolically constructed in the present and reflects contemporary agendas."

## Ethnohistory as Method

See also [[ethnohistory]].

Ethnohistory in Pacific studies meant "the reconstruction of past life-ways by analysing documentary materials," beginning from the era of European contact and working backwards. Its sources are overwhelmingly European: "published explorers' accounts, unpublished correspondence, ships' logs, and early missionary reports." Marshall Sahlins's researches on early Hawai'i and Fiji "set a standard for the comprehensive and innovative use of archival sources."

Source-criticism is methodologically central: "Travellers' reports are, at best, highly selective. Early visitors to the Pacific were following their own agendas — exploration, trade and missionisation, usually in that order." Gender is a particularly acute blind spot: "European explorers and traders — all men — came from a highly gender-stratified, male-dominant society" and "local women were often seen merely as potential sexual release for the crew."

Bernard Smith's *European Vision and the South Pacific, 1768–1850* (1960) was path-breaking: Smith analysed 18th–19th century graphic representations to reveal European constructs like the "noble savage" and characterised European attitudes as "soft primitivism" (the Tahitians as pre-lapsarian innocents) versus "hard primitivism" (Calvinist-evangelical view of Islanders as "depraved and benighted savages"). Edward Said's *Orientalism* (1978) extended this critique: "representing the culture or the past of an indigenous people is implicitly a matter of political power."

## Institutions and Key Works

The field is institutionally anchored in: the *Journal of the Polynesian Society* (JPS, from late 19th century); the Bernice P. Bishop Museum in Hawai'i; the Research School of Pacific Studies at the Australian National University (ANU) and its *Journal of Pacific History* (JPH); and the University of Hawai'i Press (UHP, which replaced ANU Press as leading publisher after the 1980s). The Institute of Pacific Studies (IPS) at the University of the South Pacific in Fiji publishes Islander scholars. Key historiographical landmarks: Davidson's "Problems of Pacific History"; Greg Dening's "Ethnohistory in Polynesia"; Kerry Howe's "The Fate of the 'Savage' in Pacific Historiography"; and Epeli Hau'ofa's "Our Sea of Islands."

## Limitations and Critiques

1. **Gender**: Women's history is systematically under-documented because "male visitors interacted primarily with Island men." Marxist-feminist scholars (following Engels) attribute gender asymmetry to capitalism and state formation, but most Pacific scholars believe "a male bias in public authority was not solely a European introduction, but was and is endogenous to many cultures."
2. **Connectivity bias**: Colonial administration shrank maritime trade networks, so that "when serious academic research began in the region, inter-island connections were markedly less important than they had been." Emphasising "connectedness rather than isolation has sweeping implications for the way we represent such issues as ethnicity, identity, mobility, and 'Western impact'."
3. **Insider/outsider tension**: "Some indigenous Pacific leaders and intellectuals argue that foreign scholars have neither the right nor the competence to write objectively about cultural 'others'." The post-colonial Pacific is "marginal to the economic and strategic concerns of the major industrial nations," making the question of whose version of history carries authority acutely political.
