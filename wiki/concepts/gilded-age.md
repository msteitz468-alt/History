# The Gilded Age

The conventional American periodization term for roughly the 1870s through the 1890s (bridging Reconstruction and the Progressive Era), characterized by rapid industrialization, massive corporate consolidation, extravagant wealth accumulation among industrialists and financiers, mass immigration, urbanization, and — the term's original satirical point — a surface of prosperity and progress masking rampant political corruption, labor exploitation, and social inequality. The term derives from [[actors/twain-mark|Mark Twain]] and Charles Dudley Warner's 1873 novel of the same name, whose title played on "golden age" — gilding being a thin layer of gold over a base material, implying the era's glittering wealth concealed rot beneath rather than reflecting genuine, broadly-shared prosperity.

Historians generally treat the Gilded Age as the social and cultural counterpart to the specifically economic process of [[processes/corporate-consolidation-gilded-age|corporate consolidation]] underway in the same decades — the trusts, monopolies, and industrial combinations (Standard Oil, the railroads, U.S. Steel's antecedents) that concentrated enormous wealth and market power among a small number of businessmen. The period's labor conflicts (the Great Railroad Strike of 1877, the Homestead Strike of 1892, the Pullman Strike of 1894) and its political corruption (patronage machines, corporate influence over legislatures) are usually read as direct products of this unregulated concentration of industrial capital.
