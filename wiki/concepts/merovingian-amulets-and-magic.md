# Amulets, Magic, and Divination in Merovingian Gaul

**A world "awash in amulets," where magic and divination were strategies for managing risk, not mere pagan
survivals.** Two chapters of the Oxford Handbook — Genevra Kornbluth on amulets (Ch. 40) and William
Klingshirn on magic and divination (Ch. 41) — recover the practical supernaturalism of Merovingian Gaul and
argue, in a key revision, that the orthodox/magical boundary was a clerical construct rather than a lived
reality.

## Amulets and Protective Jewelry (Kornbluth)

"The Merovingian world was awash in amulets, from bear claws to pendants of gemstone and gold." The
bound-pendant corpus alone has **211 specimens** — 57% rock crystal, 24% iron ore, the rest amber, glass,
tooth, and shell. Every one of the 53 anthropologically sexed contexts was **female** (clustering at ages
18–40), and Kornbluth reads bound pendants as "markers of feminine gender, adult identity, high social
status, and locational/community membership." Two methodological points stand out: **cowrie shells**
imported from the Red Sea and Indo-Pacific confirm the long-distance connections of
[[garnet-and-bead-trade]]; and pierced **coins** show functional redefinition — "the act of suspension
lifted them out of the monetary context and placed them among amulets." Distribution is local, not ethnic:
"regional difference is not the same as ethnic difference." Clerical condemnation is the best evidence of
practice — Gregory of Tours lists "bear claws and fat" among a false holy man's stock-in-trade.

## Magic and Divination (Klingshirn)

Klingshirn reframes the whole category [REVISION]: magical and divinatory practices were "strategies for
accessing divine power and knowledge in a world of deep uncertainty and risk," not "paganism." Conciliar and
homiletical sources name a whole **taxonomy of practitioners** — *harioli* (diviners), *sortilegi* and
*caragii* (lot-casters), *haruspices* (entrail-readers), *praecantatores* (spell-casters), *herbariae*
(female herbalist-healers), and *filacterarii* (amulet-makers). The richest evidence is **lot-divination**:
three families of texts — the *Sortes Sangallenses* (the only securely datable Merovingian collection,
covering love, illness, enemies, office, lawsuits, captivity, poisoning), the *Sortes Sanctorum*, and the
*Sortes Sangermanenses*. Clergy themselves practiced it: the Council of Vannes (462/468) condemned clerics
"devoted to auguries… which they call Lots of the Saints." Penitentials tariffed it (the Bobbio Penitential:
10 years for death-causing *maleficium*, 3 for erotic *veneficium*).

## The Porous Boundary

The boundary between sanctioned religion and condemned magic was genuinely porous. Gregory's own father
carried relics in "a gold medallion" and credited them with rescue — illustrating how relic use shaded into
amulet function (see [[liturgical-commemoration-and-memoria]]). On the Charnay fibula, scholars dispute
whether the runic text is "a Christian amulet versus a purely runic formula" — "the line between spiritual
advice and Christian divination may be difficult to draw" (see [[merovingian-epigraphy]]). Eighth-century
catalogues like the *Indiculus superstitionum* and the *Homilia de sacrilegiis* show the sheer breadth of
what was practiced — a vitality the Church sought to police, not a void.
