# Marxist Archaeology

Marxist archaeology applies the historical materialist framework of Marx and Engels to the study of the past, emphasizing class struggle, base and superstructure, modes of production, and the social relations underlying material culture. It treats archaeological evidence as a means to understand social and economic structures rather than solely chronological or typological sequences.

## Varieties and Historical Development

Distinct European traditions emerged, shaped heavily by political contexts:
- In the Soviet bloc (Eastern Europe), official state ideology imposed a dogmatic or "vulgar" Marxism from the late 1940s/1950s, reducing it often to class struggle and base/superstructure tenets, frequently as non sequitur garnish on traditional culture-historical work.
- Many archaeologists accepted Marxist philosophical theses as the primary available social theory even while opposing communism politically.
- Dogmatic versions included 19th-century ethnographic survivals (e.g., "matriarchy" as a universal stage) that were difficult to discard under party control.
- Post-Stalinist shifts (e.g., after 1956 in Hungary, after 1968 in Czechoslovakia) relaxed some pressures but ideology remained intertwined with the political system.

## Eastern European Experience Under Soviet Influence (from Hodder ed. 1991)

In Czechoslovakia, ideological training in the 1950s-60s had limited effect on specific theory: "the training was on a rather general, often dogmatic, level which made it difficult to use in specific instances. Moreover, the official 'Marxism' of that time included many theses based on nineteenth-century ethnography (e.g. 'matriarchy' as a stage in the development of humankind) which were hardly acceptable to most archaeologists but could not be discarded while party ideology was in control." Surprisingly little direct political influence on theory development, as "very few archaeologists accepted Marxism as a political theory. Most of them, however, adopted it as the one social theory to which they knew no alternative." The 1968 Russian invasion shifted state ideology away from Marxism toward a purely political doctrine supporting the party bureaucracy; "Marxism was still taught in schools but nobody believed it any more, so ideological pressure was relaxed." This allowed New Archaeology to begin creeping in, though "theory became dangerous, whether Marxian or not."

In Hungary, "A strong Soviet influence began to dominate the scene from the late 1940s and particularly from the 1950s, and this had little, if any, intellectual ancestry in Hungary." "Marxism basically meant vulgar Marxism in the dogmatic Stalinistic period of the 1950s, when the entire theory was reduced simply to the tenet of class struggle and the relationship between base and superstructure." Most publications retained traditional frameworks "with some kind of ideological garnish." "It was a general practice in archaeological articles written according to the traditional framework that Marxism only appeared in the form of periodic and mostly non sequitur quotes from Marx and Engels." No dedicated theoretical books appeared for decades; research remained largely atheoretical and descriptive. "In the Marxist framework 'class' was the only accepted form for the description of social categories for a long time."

Local adaptations included "archaeological ethnography" (Gyula László) and "historicity" traditions (András Mócsy, István Bóna) that operated within or alongside Marxist constraints, emphasizing empirical reanimation of past lives and historical processes over schematic evolution.

Theoretical debate itself was often avoided because "a review of theoretical questions would have involved a rethinking of ideological issues as well. During most of the period surveyed... these issues remained taboo because ideology and philosophy were part and parcel of the institutionalised section of the political system."

## Relation to Broader Traditions and Post-Processualism

European Marxist archaeology often blended with historicism, hermeneutics, and critical theory rather than the positivist processual package. It contributed to the socio-political reflexivity later amplified in post-processual archaeology. Access to Western literature, personal contacts, and post-1989 political changes enabled re-evaluation and freer engagement with international debates.

## Historiography and Critiques

Critics note the dogmatic imposition under Soviet influence often stifled genuine theoretical innovation, producing "ideological garnish" rather than substantive analysis. Defenders highlight the value of materialist attention to production, social relations, and long-term processes, and the role of critical Marxism in countering nationalist abuses of the past. Post-1989 Eastern European perspectives (e.g., "now that the country has literally lost its chains") stress the need to preserve empirical strengths while loosening ideological constraints.
