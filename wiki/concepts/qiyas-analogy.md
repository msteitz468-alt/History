## Definition and Scope

Qiyās is the fourth and most complex formal source of Islamic law ([[usul-al-fiqh]]). The term is commonly translated as "analogy," but Hallaq insists this translation is reductive: "modern scholarship has systematically managed to reduce it to its analogical form, thereby neglecting its other components" (Hallaq, ch. 2, §7). In its full theoretical articulation, qiyās encompasses a family of inferential methods — analogy proper, a fortiori argument (in both a minore ad maius and a maiore ad minus forms), reductio ad absurdum, and indicative inference. What unifies them is that all operate by identifying a ratio legis (ʿilla, the attribute or cause that grounds a legal norm) shared between an original textual case and a new case requiring a ruling.

Qiyās is not a *material* source (it provides no substantive content of law). It is a *formal* or *methodological* source: a set of interpretive tools for extracting norms from the Qurʾān and Sunna and transposing them to novel cases.

## Historical Development

### Origins in raʾy (considered opinion)

Before qiyās was theorized as a distinct category, the dominant mode of juristic reasoning was *raʾy* — "considered opinion" or personal judgment. Throughout the first/seventh and most of the second/eighth century, raʾy encompassed everything from loose reasoning to strict analogy and a fortiori argument. Qiyās emerged as a terminological and conceptual refinement when it became necessary to defend systematic reasoning against the traditionalist charge that raʾy was arbitrary human legislation: "ray became the umbrella term for a wide variety of legal arguments … ijtihād al-raʾy … meant the exertion of mental energy for the sake of arriving, through reasoning, at a considered opinion" (Hallaq, ch. 1, §4).

By the middle of the second/eighth century (c. 770 CE), the term qiyās was being used to designate disciplined, text-based reasoning as distinguished from loose personal opinion: "qiyās, signifying disciplined and systematic reasoning on the basis of the revealed texts, the Qurʾān and hadith. In addition to analogy, its archetypal form, qiyās encompassed the a fortiori argument in both of its forms" (Hallaq, ch. 1, §4).

### Al-Shāfiʿī's intervention

The jurist al-Shāfiʿī (d. 204/820) played a pivotal role in the conceptual history of qiyās. He rejected raʾy and *istiḥsān* as arbitrary, and collapsed the distinction between qiyās and ijtihād, using the terms interchangeably for text-based inferential reasoning. For him, qiyās was the *only* legitimate method of going beyond the letter of revealed texts: "Shāfiʿī appears to have been the first jurist consciously to articulate the notion that Islamic revelation provides a full and comprehensive evaluation of human acts" (Hallaq, ch. 1, §4). Crucially, qiyās for al-Shāfiʿī does not generate independent rules; it *discovers* what God intended but left unstated: "Qiyās does not itself generate rules or legal norms; it merely discovers them from, or brings them out of, the language of revealed texts" (Hallaq, ch. 1, §4).

This theory, however, was contested in al-Shāfiʿī's own time and not widely accepted until more than half a century after his death, when the Great Synthesis of rationalism and traditionalism consolidated qiyās as the fourth root of law.

### Theorization in the fourth/tenth–fifth/eleventh centuries

The mature articulation of qiyās as a formal methodological category — with its four elements, its typology by ratio legis, and its relationship to other forms of inference — was the work of the post-formative period. It was embedded in the larger project of [[usul-al-fiqh]] and presupposed the epistemological framework distinguishing certain (qaṭʿī) from probable (ẓannī) knowledge: all qiyās conclusions are probable, never certain, except when subsequently ratified by [[ijma-consensus|ijmāʿ]].

## The Four Elements of Analogy

In its archetypal analogical form, qiyās consists of four components (Hallaq, ch. 2, §7):

1. **The new case** (*farʿ*): the case requiring a legal ruling.
2. **The original case** (*aṣl*): a case whose ruling is stated in the Qurʾān, Sunna, or sanctioned by consensus.
3. **The ratio legis** (*ʿilla*): the attribute shared by both cases that "causes" the legal norm to arise.
4. **The legal norm** (*ḥukm*): the ruling transposed from the original to the new case.

The canonical example: grape-wine is prohibited by the Qurʾān. A jurist faces date-wine. The shared ratio legis is *intoxication*. Therefore date-wine is also prohibited. The ratio legis may be (a) explicitly stated in the text, (b) intimated by its semantic structure, (c) established by sequence of events, or (d) known through consensus.

## The Ratio Legis Problem

Identifying and confirming the ratio legis is the most theoretically contested aspect of qiyās. The ratio must be: (1) the *complete* set of attributes giving rise to the norm in the original case (not a partial subset); (2) *relevant* (mulāʾim) to the law's aims; and (3) not merely conjectural when the law requires more. Where the ratio is explicit, analogy is straightforward. Where it must be inferred, the method of *suitability* (munāsaba) applies: even absent textual statement of a reason, the jurist may recognize that a prohibition serves an evident human good (protecting life, mind, religion, property, or offspring) and reason on that basis.

The complexity of the ratio produced the distinction between **causative qiyās** (ratio and rationale both identifiable) and **indicative qiyās** (rationale merely inferred or unknown). Hallaq notes this distinction "is often one of form, not substance" (ch. 2, §7).

## Types of Qiyāsic Argument

### 1. Analogy proper (qiyās al-ʿilla)
Extension of a known ruling to a new case on the basis of a shared ratio legis. The wine/date-wine example above is the textbook case.

### 2. A fortiori (qiyās al-awlā)
*A minore ad maius*: if a lesser wrong is prohibited, a greater one is a fortiori prohibited. Uttering "Fie" before one's parents is prohibited by Qurʾān 17:23; striking them is therefore a fortiori prohibited.
*A maiore ad minus*: if a more serious act is permitted, a lesser one is permitted a fortiori. Killing enemy combatants is lawful; confiscating their property is therefore also lawful.

### 3. Reductio ad absurdum (qiyās al-ʿaks)
A ruling is established by demonstrating that its converse is false or unwarranted. If the non-existence of a ratio leads to the absence of the associated rule, then the presence of the ratio must bring the rule into existence.

## Istiḥsān and Maṣlaḥa as Extensions

Two important secondary methods stand in a dialectical relationship with qiyās:

**Istiḥsān** ("juristic preference"): a form of "preferred qiyās" that creates an exception to the standard analogical rule on the basis of a more suitable text, necessity, or consensus. Developed primarily by the Ḥanafite school, it was attacked by al-Shāfiʿī as arbitrary but survived as a valid subsidiary method in Ḥanafite and Ḥanbalite jurisprudence. In mature theory it is treated as another species of qiyās, not an independent source.

**Maṣlaḥa/istiṣlāḥ** ("public interest"): the Mālikite tradition's parallel to istiḥsān, reasoning on the basis of the five universal aims of the law (protection of life, mind, religion, property, and offspring). Theorists treat it as an extension of the suitability (*munāsaba*) method within qiyās, not an independent category: "most theorists do not devote to it an independent section or chapter but treat it under the category of suitability" (Hallaq, ch. 2, §7).

## Twelver-Shīʿite Rejection and Covert Use

Shīʿite jurists formally rejected qiyās — especially on the authority of Imamic condemnations of it — but in hermeneutical practice its substance was unavoidable and "subsumed under a different guise" (Hallaq, ch. 2, §9). The Akhbārist-Uṣūlist dispute within Shīʿism turned in part on this: Uṣūlists admitted inferential reasoning while avoiding the term qiyās; Akhbārists rejected any reasoning not grounded in infallible Imamic reports.

## Historiography

The reduction of qiyās to simple analogy has been the dominant — and misleading — framing in Western scholarship. Hallaq's corrective (already advanced in publications of the 1980s and summarized here) emphasizes the full typology: "corrections of this misconception were made in the 1980s" (Hallaq, ch. 2, §7). A further historiographical issue is the relationship between qiyās and raʾy: Schacht's influential account treated raʾy as secular and qiyās as an Islamicizing overlay, a view Hallaq rejects. The continuity between raʾy, ijtihād al-raʾy, and qiyās is terminological and conceptual evolution within a single juristic tradition, not a transition from secular to religious law.
