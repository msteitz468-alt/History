# Nomos and Thesis (Law of Liberty vs. Law of Legislation)

**Etic** analytical distinction, coined by F. A. Hayek in *Law, Legislation and Liberty* I: *Rules and Order* (1973): **nomos** is the law of liberty — end-independent rules of just conduct, grown through custom and judicial precedent, corresponding to the *ius/droit/Recht/diritto* family; **thesis** is the law of legislation — enacted rules of organization directing government toward particular purposes, corresponding to *loi/Gesetz/legge*. The legal expression of the [[concepts/spontaneous-order|cosmos/taxis]] distinction, and the analytic core of Hayek's claim that the confusion of the two under the single word "law" is what let modern legislatures claim unlimited "sovereign" power. For Hayek, "Law, liberty, and property are an inseparable trinity."

## Law is older than legislation

The historical anchor of Vol. 1: "Legislation, the deliberate making of law, has justly been described as among all inventions of man the one fraught with the gravest consequences, more far-reaching in its effects even than fire and gun-powder … Law in the sense of enforced rules of conduct is undoubtedly coeval with society." The idea that law could be *made* "appeared hardly earlier than in classical Greece and even then only to be submerged again and to reappear and gradually gain wider acceptance in the later Middle Ages."

Hayek's historical genealogy (attributed — a partisan but heavily documented reconstruction):

- **Pre-articulate rules.** Ethology and cultural anthropology show individuals "learned to observe (and enforce) rules of conduct long before such rules could be expressed in words"; a rule is first "a propensity or disposition to act or not to act in a certain manner." Even animal territoriality functions as property — he cites the "elaborate system of property tenure" of crayfish and the robin rivalries in which "victory does not go to the strong but to the righteous — the righteous of course being the owners of property."
- **Articulation.** Early chiefs exercised authority in two distinct modes — enforcing rules regarded as already established vs. commanding joint enterprises (hunt, war) — and it was the need to *justify* the former that drove customary rules into words. Articulation "will thus sometimes in effect, though not in intention, produce new rules."
- **Early law-givers restated, did not create.** "The historians of law are agreed that in this respect all the famous early 'law-givers', from Ur-Nammu and Hammurabi to Solon, Lykurgus and the authors of the Roman Twelve Tables, did not intend to create new law but merely to state what law was and had always been."
- **Growth outside rulers' control.** The universal rules that made an open society possible evolved chiefly in relations rulers could not command: "It is in the ius gentium, the law merchant, and the practices of the ports and fairs that we must chiefly seek the steps in the evolution of law" — perhaps beginning "with the first instance of silent barter" at tribal boundaries.
- **Athens.** Even at the height of the democracy the *nomoi* could not be altered by simple assembly decree; change required the specially chosen *nomothetae*, and the mover of an illegal decree faced the "indictment for illegal proceedings" (*graphē paranomōn*). Aristotle "even denied the right to be called a constitution" to assembly rule unbound by law — the first recorded collision of popular sovereignty with the rule of law. See [[concepts/athenian-democracy]].
- **Rome.** Classical Roman civil law "is almost entirely the product of law-finding by jurists and only to a very small extent the product of legislation"; Justinian's Byzantine codification was "later falsely regarded as the model of a law created by a ruler and expressing his 'will'."
- **The medieval doctrine.** Quoting Fritz Kern: "According to medieval ideas … the enactment of new law is not possible at all; and all legislation and legal reform is conceived of as the restoration of the good old law which has been violated."
- **England's exception.** England alone "preserved the tradition of the Middle Ages and built on the medieval 'liberties' the modern conception of liberty under the law" — Coke against James I and Bacon, Hale against Hobbes. Hayek reinterprets Montesquieu's famous attribution of British liberty to separation of powers: the real basis was "that the law that governed the decisions of the courts was the common law, a law existing independently of anyone's will."

## Nomos: the law of liberty

The judge in a spontaneous order serves "an ongoing order of actions": his concern is the reasonable expectations parties formed from prevailing practice, "never … whether the action in fact taken was expedient from some higher point of view"; he must ignore "any wishes of a ruler or any 'reasons of state'." Only "actions towards other persons" (*operationes quae sunt ad alterum*) can generate rules of just conduct — hence even perfectly general rules of religious conformity would infringe liberty, since they delimit no protected domain. Not all expectations can be protected: "the law does not prohibit the setting up of a new business even if this is done in the expectation that it will lead to the failure of another." The maximal coincidence of expectations is achieved by delimiting protected domains — "good fences make good neighbours" — and Hayek adds that anthropology has "completely refuted" the myth of primitive communism preceding property. Law in the ordinary sense has no purpose: it "is not a means to any purpose, but merely a condition for the successful pursuit of most purposes … of all multi-purpose instruments it is probably the one after language which assists the greatest variety of human purposes" — grounded in Hume's dictum that the benefit of justice "arises from the whole scheme or system," not from particular applications.

Common law, Hayek argues (quoting Lord Mansfield), is *more* abstract than statute: it "does not consist of particular cases, but of general principles, which are illustrated and explained by those cases." He records his own change of mind on codification: decades in the common-law world did not shake his civilian prejudice for codes, but he came to hold that predictability may be *greater* where the judge is also bound by generally held, non-statutory views of justice.

**Grown law still needs correction.** Case-law development is "in some respects a sort of one-way street" that cannot retrace its steps without unjustly disappointing expectations; worse, it historically tilted toward the class that supplied the judges (master-and-servant, landlord-and-tenant, creditor-and-debtor law). Deliberate legislation is then the only remedy — Hayek's own concession that thesis has a legitimate corrective role within nomos.

## Thesis: the law of legislation

Legislatures did not originate as makers of nomos: "The chief concern of what we call legislatures has always been the control and regulation of government," and parliamentary institutions arose "from the need to obtain consent to taxes." Maitland: "The further back we trace our history the more impossible it is for us to draw strict lines of demarcation between the various functions of the state: the same institution is a legislative assembly, a governmental council, and a court of law." As late as the seventeenth century it could be questioned whether Parliament could make law inconsistent with the common law. A rule of conduct "cannot be 'carried out' or 'executed' … what it prescribes is never accomplished but remains a standing obligation"; a thesis is executed like an administrative measure. Conflating the two under "law" is what allowed governmental assemblies to inherit the unlimited authority properly conceded only to statements of nomos.

Hayek maps the distinction onto **private vs. public law** (with criminal law, Anglo-fashion, under private law), warning that "to regard only the public law as serving general welfare and the private law as protecting only the selfish interests of the individuals would be a complete inversion of the truth." Constitutional law is not the source but the superstructure of law — H. L. A. Hart's "rule of recognition," not itself a rule of just conduct; "public law passes but private law persists." Sovereignty doctrine (Bacon, Hobbes, Austin, the German positivists Laband to Kelsen) rests on the false premise that all law is willed; in fact "all power rests on, and is limited by, opinion, as was most clearly seen by David Hume … even the most powerful dictatorship crumbles if the support of opinion is withdrawn."

## The transformation thesis and its politics

Hayek's account of modern legal change is openly polemical. Lawyers, he argues, transmit "false economics" — "the wholly untrue legend" that early capitalism impoverished workers — teacher to student, making the profession a channel for socialist ideas; his exemplar of philosophy remaking law is **Carl Schmitt**, "who, long before Hitler came to power, directed all his formidable intellectual energies to a fight against liberalism in all its forms" and whose "concrete order formation" superseded normative liberal law under the Third Reich. Of three senses of "social" legislation (removing class bias in existing law; tax-financed services for the unfortunate; directing private activity to particular groups' benefit), the third — "inspired by the will-o-the-wisp of 'social justice'" — has driven "the gradual transformation of the purpose-independent rules of just conduct … into purpose-dependent rules of organization" (Trade Disputes Act 1906; New Deal-era decisions; German *Sozialpolitik*). An assembly habituated to "running the country" comes to feel responsible for removing every grievance, though "most of the grievances of particular individuals or groups can be removed only by measures which create new grievances elsewhere." A "socialist judge," on this account, "would really be a contradiction in terms." The separation-of-powers lineage he endorses runs from the First Agreement of the People (1647) — "in all laws made or to be made every person may be bound alike" — through Locke's "promulgated established laws, not to be varied in particular cases," to Montesquieu; its constitutional consequence is drawn in [[concepts/demarchy]].

## Limitations and critiques

- The nomos/thesis line is sharper than legal history: statute has shaped "private law" since antiquity, and Hayek's own concessions (legislative correction of class-biased case law) blur the normative asymmetry.
- Legal historians dispute the strong "law-found-not-made" reading of medieval jurisprudence (Kern's thesis has itself been revised) and the idealized portrait of common-law neutrality.
- The claim that the profession's leftward drift rests on "false economics" is an attributed polemic, not wiki-voice fact; the underlying labor-history debate (living standards under early industrialization) is genuinely contested among economic historians.
