## Definition and Scope

The question of God's knowledge of particulars asks: does the divine intellect know individual, temporally indexed, contingent events — "Zayd arriving at sunrise tomorrow," "this fire burning this piece of cotton" — or only universal truths and species? The dispute is one of the most consequential in medieval Islamic philosophy, because the answer bears on prophecy, providence, miracles, and the coherence of divine omniscience alongside divine immutability.

This page records [[ghazali-incoherence-of-philosophers|al-Ghazālī's]] Discussion 13 of the *Tahāfut al-Falāsifa*, which constitutes the **second of the three doctrines declared unbelief** (*kufr*) in that work. The first is the eternity of the world; the third is the denial of bodily resurrection. The middle charge — that [[ibn-sina|Avicenna]] and the Peripatetic tradition effectively deny God's knowledge of particulars — is treated here as the gravest epistemological heresy because it severs divine providence from the created order.

## The Avicennan Position (as reconstructed by al-Ghazālī)

Avicenna's doctrine, as al-Ghazālī renders it in Discussion 13, holds that:

1. God knows things through a single, eternal, unchanging act of self-knowledge.
2. This knowledge encompasses universals — species, genera, essential properties — but not numerically individual, temporally particular things as such.
3. God knows "man" universally but does not know *this* man at *this* moment in the way an observer knows him.
4. The reason given is immutability: if God knew a particular as "arriving now," that knowledge would have to change when the moment passes into "has arrived." But change in the knower follows change in the object of knowledge. Since God cannot change, he cannot know particulars as such.

The Avicennan school qualified this: God knows particulars "in a universal way" (*bi-wajhin kullī*) — meaning he knows the causal chain that generates any particular outcome without knowing it in its temporal particularity.

## Al-Ghazālī's Refutation

Al-Ghazālī attacks this position through several interlocking arguments, all extracted directly from his opponents' own commitments:

### Argument 1 — The permanent knowledge counter-example

"If God creates for us knowledge of the arrival of Zayd tomorrow at sunrise and perpetuates this knowledge, neither creating another knowledge nor inattention to this knowledge, we would then, at the time of the sunrise, know of his arriving *now* purely by the previous knowledge; and afterwards that he had arrived earlier. This one permanent knowledge would be sufficient to encompass these three states." (Discussion 13, §14)

The force of this: change in the *temporal index* of the known object does not *logically entail* change in the knower, because a single permanent cognitive act can encompass past, present, and future states of that object. The philosophers' claim that change in the object necessitates change in the knower is, for al-Ghazālī, an undemonstrated assumption.

### Argument 2 — Tu quoque: universals are already diverse

Even if the philosophers' principle were granted — that difference in the object of knowledge entails difference in the cognitive relation, hence change — this would be *equally* devastating to their own account of God's knowledge of universals. For God would know "man," "animal," and "inanimate object" — these are different things. The relations to them are different. Therefore on their own principle, God's one knowledge could not be knowledge of all these different genera without multiplying and differentiating. If they evade this by saying one knowledge can encompass different universals, al-Ghazālī replies: the differences between genera and species are *greater* than the differences between the temporal states of one particular thing. If the former difference does not compel multiplicity in divine knowledge, neither does the latter. (Discussion 13, §§16–17)

### Argument 3 — Use the philosophers' own eternal-temporal framework

The philosophers hold that temporal events proceed from the eternal celestial soul via perpetual, uniform emanation. If temporal events can proceed from an eternal, unchanging source, then temporal cognitions can, by parallel reasoning, occur in the divine without constituting change in the divine essence. The philosophers themselves admit a version of the eternal producing the temporal; they cannot then cite "eternal/temporal" asymmetry to block divine knowledge of particulars. (Discussion 13, §§23–27)

### Argument 4 — Divine perfection requires particular knowledge

Al-Ghazālī turns the perfection argument against the Avicennans: "His perfection consists in His knowledge of all things. If there would occur to us knowledge corresponding to every temporal event, this would be a perfection for us, neither a deficiency nor an enforcement on us. So let this be the case with respect to Him." (Discussion 13, §29) Limiting divine knowledge to universals is, on this reading, not a conclusion that follows from divine perfection, but a *restriction* on it.

## The Heresy Charge

Al-Ghazālī's labeling of the Avicennan position as *kufr* (unbelief) rather than mere error (*bid'a* or mere philosophical mistake) reflects the stakes: if God does not know particular events, prophets cannot receive particular revelations, prayer becomes meaningless (God does not know *this* supplicant's need), and divine providence dissolves into an impersonal metaphysical mechanism indistinguishable from the Neoplatonist One. The charge is not primarily about epistemology; it is about the preservation of a personal God who acts in history.

## Subsequent Reception

The dispute did not end with al-Ghazālī. [[ibn-rushd|Averroës]] (Ibn Rushd) in the *Tahāfut al-Tahāfut* defended a version of the Avicennan position, arguing that al-Ghazālī had misread it. Later [[asharism|Ash'arite]] theology generally followed al-Ghazālī in insisting on divine knowledge of particulars, grounding this in God's will and creative act rather than any analogy with human cognitive change. The problem reappears in the Latin West through Aquinas's doctrine of divine eternity and the *nunc stans* (eternal present), which attempts a similar reconciliation of immutability with particular knowledge.

## Historiography

The primary source is Marmura's English translation of the *Tahāfut al-Falāsifa* (Discussion 13). The question of whether al-Ghazālī faithfully represents Avicenna is actively debated: Michael Marmura's own notes, and Dimitri Gutas's work on Avicenna, suggest al-Ghazālī's reconstruction is polemically sharpened but not wholly unfair. The debate about divine knowledge of particulars is often treated as a sub-case of the larger medieval problem of God's foreknowledge and human freedom, though the Islamic framing differs importantly from the Latin scholastic one in its emphasis on divine *will* rather than divine *intellect* as the primary attribute at stake.
