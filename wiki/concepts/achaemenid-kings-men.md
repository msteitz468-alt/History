# The King's Men — the Achaemenid Gift Economy

## Definition

The web of **personal bonds** between the [[achaemenid-empire|Achaemenid]] Great King and his
"Faithful" — the Persian aristocracy and favoured collaborators — cemented by an **unequal gift
economy** (*polydoria*). [[sources/briant-from-cyrus-to-alexander-2002|Briant]] identifies this,
alongside the [[achaemenid-tribute-economy|tribute economy]], as the real social mechanism of
empire: not bureaucracy but a system of reciprocal favour and obligation binding every noble
personally to the king.

## How It Worked

- **Bandaka.** The core relationship: a *bandaka* was "simultaneously subject and loyal to the
  king" (the Akkadian renders it *qallu*, "slave"), ratified by a formal right-hand pledge; to
  break the bond was to grasp a man's girdle.
- **Unequal exchange.** Only the king gives, and only royal gifts oblige the recipient — robes,
  gold-bridled horses, the *akinakes* dagger, all worn constantly as public marks of favour.
  Receiving placed obligation on the recipient without binding the king in return.
- **Earning favour.** Deeds done "under the king's eyes" won recognition; secretaries recorded
  the names, city, and parentage of those who distinguished themselves, kept in a register of
  Benefactors (*orosangae*) consulted by later kings.
- **The hierarchy of proximity.** Court ranks — Friends (*philoi*), Tablemates (*homotrapezoi*),
  Royal Kinsmen (*syggeneis*), and personal-service titles (cupbearer, quiver-bearer, lance-bearer)
  — measured favour by physical closeness to the royal person. Marriage to a king's daughter was a
  supreme honour but conferred **no succession rights**.
- **Precarity.** Favour, lands, and titles were revocable at any time; the disgraced (*atimia*)
  were forced to use clay cups instead of gold.

## The Dynastic Pact

The Persian nobility accepted a "dynastic pact" — "our interests are the same and our enemies are
the same": their wealth depended on continuing Persian imperial dominance, so they shared the king's
interest in suppressing subject revolts. Clan nobility (birth) and court nobility (royal favour)
coexisted; favour could relativize birth but the great families remained the king's primary men.
Struggles for the throne were always fought *within* the royal House — no outsider ever imposed
himself.

## Persians, Greeks, and Others

Foreign elites (Greeks, Egyptians) could receive titles and gifts but were structurally excluded
from the highest commands — [[achaemenid-satrapy|satrap]], *strategos*, garrison commander — which
remained reserved for the Persian aristocracy. Themistocles, honoured "as an equal to Persians of
the first rank," was the conspicuous exception that proves the rule. The whole system was replicated
at satrapal courts, extending the gift-and-service culture across the Persian diaspora.

## Why It Mattered — and Its Fragility

The gift economy explains both the empire's cohesion and its collapse. It bound the ruling class
tightly to the king's person — but, as Briant argues for [[darius-iii|Darius III]]'s fall, it created
an "ideology of the King" without an "ideology of the Empire." Once the king was defeated in battle,
the same elite calculus that had made nobles Faithful made them rational defectors, transferring
loyalty to [[alexander-the-great|Alexander]], who deliberately reproduced the gift-for-service
relationship to win them.
