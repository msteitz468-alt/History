## Definition and Scope

This page documents Ibn Khaldun's analytical treatment of Arab tribal character — the structural features he attributes to Arab Bedouins as a specific type within the broader [[bedouin-and-sedentary-society]] framework — as developed in Chapter II of [[the-muqaddimah]]. It is an **etic** concept: a social-scientific analysis written by Ibn Khaldun from the outside, not a description of how Arab tribal peoples understood themselves.

Ibn Khaldun treats the Arabs as the most extreme instantiation of desert (badawa) civilization because of their exclusive reliance on camels, which forces deeper desert penetration than any other pastoral economy. All his structural claims about Bedouin character in general apply to Arabs with maximum intensity.

This analysis is distinct from [[pre-islamic-arabia]] (which covers historical conditions) and from [[asabiyyah]] (which is the solidarity concept applicable to all tribal peoples, not only Arabs).

## Camel Nomadism as the Root Condition

Ibn Khaldun subdivides pastoral peoples by animal:
- Sheep and cattle herders (shawiyah): travel to find pasture in hilly terrain; do not penetrate deep desert; include Berbers, Turks, Turkomans, Slavs
- **Camel nomads**: forced into the deep desert because camels require desert shrubs, salty water, and warm sandy terrain for calving; "the hilly pastures with their plants and shrubs do not furnish enough subsistence for camels"

The Arabs rely exclusively on camels. This ecological constraint makes them the deepest desert dwellers of all pastoral peoples. From this ecological base, all the distinctive features of Arab tribal character follow in Ibn Khaldun's model:

> "As a result, they are the most savage human beings that exist. Compared with sedentary people, they are on a level with wild, untamable (animals) and dumb beasts of prey."

This is a structural characterization, not a racial one: the same character traits would emerge in any people who lived exclusively by camel nomadism in deep desert. The Berber Zanatah in the west are their "counterparts" in the Maghrib.

## The Structural Paradox of Arab Leadership

Chapter II develops a central paradox about Arabs and royal authority:

**Arabs are simultaneously (a) the most capable of conquest and (b) the least capable of governance.**

*On conquest*: Their deep desert roots make Arab groups the most savage and the least subordinated to government discipline. Savagery — understood as willingness to use force without restraint — is the quality that enables conquest:

> "The Arabs are a savage nation, fully accustomed to savagery and the things that cause it. Savagery has become their character and nature. They enjoy it, because it means freedom from authority and no subservience to leadership... Savage nations are better able to achieve superiority than others."

*On governance*: The same qualities that enable conquest undermine stable rule. Arab Bedouins are so individualistic that each aspires to leadership and will not subordinate himself to another. They plunder subject populations without providing the law-and-order that makes civilization work:

> "Every Arab is eager to be the leader. Scarcely a one of them would cede his power to another, even to his father, his brother, or the eldest (most important) member of his family."
> 
> "Under the rule of (the Arabs), the subjects live as in a state of anarchy, without law. Anarchy destroys mankind and ruins civilization."

## Arabs and the Destruction of Sedentary Civilization

Sections 24-25 contain Ibn Khaldun's most severe assessment of Arab rule. He argues that flat territories conquered by Arabs are "quickly ruined" for structural reasons:

1. **Need for building materials**: Arabs tear down buildings to get stones for cooking-pot supports and wood for tent poles. "The very nature of their existence is the negation of building, which is the basis of civilization."
2. **Plunder without investment**: Arabs regard all property as potential spoil; they do not value craftwork and do not pay craftsmen, destroying the economic basis of specialized labor
3. **No law beyond extraction**: Arab rulers level fines on property to extract wealth, not to deter crime; this increases rather than reduces disorder
4. **No fixed center**: Arab groups have no homeland they wish to defend or develop; all territory is equally exploitable pasture

Ibn Khaldun supports this with historical observation: the Yemen "is in ruins, except for a few cities"; Persian civilization in the Arab Iraq "is likewise completely ruined"; in contemporary Syria the same pattern holds. Most dramatically, the Banu Hilal and Banu Sulaym migrations into Ifriqiya and the Maghrib in the 11th century ruined "the whole region between the Sudan and the Mediterranean" that had previously been settled — three and a half centuries of Arab presence turned it from cultivated land to waste.

> "It is noteworthy how civilization always collapsed in places the Arabs took over and conquered, and how such settlements were depopulated and the (very) earth there turned into something that was no (longer) earth."

## The Religious Solution

Sections 26-27 argue that the structural vices of Arab tribal character — inability to subordinate, mutual jealousy, destructive individualism — can be overcome only by religion. Religion performs several functions:

1. **Suppresses haughtiness and jealousy**: "The qualities of haughtiness and jealousy leave them. It is, then, easy for them to subordinate themselves and to unite (as a social organization). This is achieved by the common religion they now have."
2. **Provides internal restraint**: Religious law internalized through conviction (rather than imposed through punishment) produces the fortitude-preserving self-discipline that external law destroys; see [[bedouin-and-sedentary-society]] for the law-and-fortitude problem
3. **Accelerates good-quality acquisition**: Arabs are quick to accept religious truth precisely because their desert character has not been corrupted by urban habits; they are in their "first natural state"

The Islamic conquests are Ibn Khaldun's paradigm case: under Muhammad, Arab group jealousy was suppressed, religious law substituted for external legal discipline, and the result was the greatest expansion of royal authority any nation had ever achieved. When the Arabs later abandoned religion and returned to desert habits, they lost royal authority to non-Arabs and reverted to being purely Bedouin. "When the Arabs forgot the religion, they no longer had any connection with political leadership, and they returned to their desert origins."

## Flat Territory and the Terrain Constraint

Section 24 adds a geographic constraint on Arab power: they can only dominate flat territory. Mountainous regions are safe from Arab predation because Arabs will not cross difficult terrain to reach them. Flat plains, however, offer no natural defense, and Arab groups can raid them repeatedly.

This terrain-sensitivity explains the geographic distribution of Arab settlement and devastation in Ibn Khaldun's historical examples: the flatlands of Iraq, Syria, and the Maghrib plains were ruined; the mountains of the Maghrib and the elevated terrain of Persia were less affected.

## Contested Reception

Several features of Ibn Khaldun's Arab character analysis have been contested:

- **Ethno-geographic essentialism**: Later readers have criticized the tendency to derive character from ecology in ways that approach determinism; Ibn Khaldun himself acknowledges elsewhere that customs, not nature, produce character — but the Arab analysis partially naturalizes character through the camel-ecology argument
- **Selective evidence**: The Banu Hilal example (11th-century Maghrib devastation) was Ibn Khaldun's most vivid case; recent scholarship has questioned whether the Banu Hilal migrations actually caused the scale of civilizational destruction he describes, or whether he overstated the devastation
- **Internal contradiction**: Ibn Khaldun simultaneously argues that Arabs are "closest to being good" (section 4, citing moral freshness) and "most remote from royal leadership" (section 27, citing structural incapacity for governance); the tension is never fully resolved
