# The Enlightenment

The **Enlightenment** was the European intellectual movement of the late seventeenth and
eighteenth centuries that exalted **reason, science, criticism, and human progress** as the
means to improve society — challenging inherited authority in religion, politics, and
knowledge. Centered on France's *philosophes* but spanning Britain, the German lands,
Scotland, Italy, and the American colonies, it is the intellectual signature of the *ancien
régime* (NCMH VII's "Old Regime") and the seedbed of modern liberalism, secularism, and
revolution. From [[new-cambridge-modern-history-v7|*The New Cambridge Modern History*, Vol.
VII]] (Alfred Cobban).

## Emic and etic

The movement named itself: *les Lumières*, *Aufklärung*, *illuminismo*, "enlightenment" — light
against the "darkness" of superstition and prejudice. Kant's *What Is Enlightenment?* (1784)
gave the classic emic definition — "humanity's emergence from its self-imposed immaturity,"
*sapere aude* ("dare to know"). "The Enlightenment" as a unified historical period is an etic
construct historians both use and debate.

## Core themes

- **Reason and criticism** — the application of critical reason to all authority and tradition.
- **Science as the model** — above all **Newton** (the prestige of natural science as "the new
  evangel") and **Locke's** empiricist psychology; NCMH VII stresses the era was one of
  **diffusion** of the scientific revolution's ideas more than fresh advance.
- **Criticism of religion** — from deism and toleration to outright anticlericalism and
  materialism (the "early and later criticism of religion in France and England").
- **Progress and the social sciences** — optimism about human improvement; the beginnings of
  political economy, sociology, and history as critical disciplines.
- **Vehicles** — the *Encyclopédie*, salons, academies, journals, and the "republic of
  letters"; figures from **Voltaire, Montesquieu, Diderot, Rousseau** to **Hume, Smith, and
  Kant**.

## A contested concept

The Enlightenment is heavily debated. Cobban (NCMH VII) already noted the "superficiality" of
much eighteenth-century philosophy and that its direct influence touched only a "small area" of
society. Later scholarship multiplies it into **many Enlightenments** (national, religious,
radical vs. moderate — Jonathan Israel's "Radical Enlightenment"), questions its coherence,
and contests its legacy: celebrated as the root of human rights and science, it has also been
charged (by Adorno/Horkheimer, postcolonial critics) with instrumental reason, Eurocentrism,
and complicity in racial and imperial hierarchies. Its relationship to the
[[french-revolution-1789|French Revolution]] — cause, or merely backdrop — is a classic dispute.

## Significance

The Enlightenment reframed the authority of **reason over tradition** and supplied the
vocabulary of rights, progress, toleration, and reform that drove the
[[american-revolution|American]] and [[french-revolution-1789|French]] revolutions, the
[[abolition-of-slavery|antislavery]] movement, and modern liberalism — while provoking enduring
counter-currents (Romanticism, conservatism, religious revival).

## Exemplar: Gibbon’s philosophical history

Attributed notes from the ingest of [[sources/gibbon-decline-and-fall-1776]]; not a full intellectual biography.

- **[[gibbon-edward|Edward Gibbon]]** is a principal English expression of Enlightenment historical method applied to Rome: candid rational inquiry, secondary causes “with becoming submission,” irony toward clerical power, and a progressive-history coda (modern Europe as a republic of competing powers with gunpowder and military science as barriers against a second total submersion — see [[gibbonian-decline-thesis]]).
- His self-positioning as proud of birth in a “free and enlightened country” and his refusal to transcribe martyr-torture catalogues until he knows “how much I ought to believe” illustrate the movement’s source-critical and anticlerical temper even when the subject is late antiquity.
