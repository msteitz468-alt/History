# Dharma

## Overview

**Dharma** is one of the most pervasive and multi-layered concepts in the religious and
social thought of South Asia. It carries at minimum three overlapping meanings: cosmic
law or universal order; religious duty and right conduct; and the specific obligations of
one's social position within the [[caste-varna|varna]] system. In practice these senses
were rarely separated, and it was this conflation that gave dharma its extraordinary power
as both a religious sanction and a social instrument.

## Vedic Origins

Avari does not trace dharma to a single Vedic hymn, but the concept is implicit in the
entire structure of [[vedic-age|Vedic]] religion. The yajna (sacrifice) was itself an act
of maintaining cosmic order — feeding the gods ensured that the gods maintained the
world. The priests (brahmans) who performed the yajna were thus performing a cosmic
duty. The Brahmanas (commentaries on the Vedas, composed c. 900–600 BCE) systematized
the ritual obligations of each class, laying the groundwork for the concept of
varna-specific dharma: the idea that each of the four varnas had its own prescribed duties
from which deviation was both a social and a cosmic offense.

The Vedic texts also contain an early cosmic sense of dharma: the Rig-Veda's hymn of
creation (Book X, 129) opens with the question "Who verily knows and who can here
declare it, whence it was born and whence comes this creation?" — suggesting that the
early Vedic thinkers were concerned with the underlying order of the universe, even while
remaining uncertain about it. The gods Varuna and Mitra in the Rig-Veda are explicitly
associated with the maintenance of cosmic and moral order.

## Varnashrama Dharma — Social Function

The most socially consequential elaboration of dharma was the doctrine of
**Varnashrama Dharma**: the obligation of each person to fulfill the duties of their varna
and life-stage (ashrama). Avari describes the varna system as developing through the
Vedic period into "a religiously inculcated system of colour bar, known as Varnashrama
Dharma." This was not merely an abstract theological concept; it operated as the ideological
underpinning of social stratification:

> "Within a relatively small population, the groups came to be rigidly compartmentalised
> on the basis of a religiously inculcated system of colour bar, known as Varnashrama
> Dharma."

The priests and warriors (brahmans and kshatriyas) consolidated their positions partly
by controlling the textual definition of dharma. Since "all Vedic texts were revised by the
learned brahmans, who were in close alliance with the kshatriyas, there is a danger that
the inviolate caste system as explained in the texts is a particular form of ideological
construct."

The four varnas had their textual scriptural foundation in the Purusha hymn (Rig-Veda
Book X, 90.11–12), where the brahman emerges from the mouth of the Primordial Man, the
kshatriya from his arms, the vaishya from his thighs, and the shudra from his feet — making
the hierarchical order appear divinely ordained rather than historically constructed.

## Dharma as Duty in the Epics

By the period when the Mahabharata and Ramayana took their present form (post-600 BCE,
with oral origins much earlier), dharma had become the central ethical concept of the
narrative. Avari describes the epics as conveying "the call to duty and Dharma, as
interpreted in Vedic Brahmanism" as their "core message." The Mahabharata in particular
raised questions of dharma as conscientious dilemma: the conscience of a king, the morality
of warfare, succession rights.

Dharma was also explicitly invoked as a duty to resist foreign intrusion. When Alexander the
Great invaded the northwest in 327–325 BCE, Avari notes that "brahman clans who considered
it a part of their dharma or religious duty to fight him" resisted him, even after Ambhi of
Taxila had submitted. This reveals dharma operating not merely as ritual compliance but as
active, even martial, obligation.

## Dharma in the Heterodox Challenge

The great Shramanist heterodoxies that arose after 600 BCE — Buddhism and Jainism — did
not reject the concept of dharma as such, but reinterpreted it profoundly. The Buddhist
**Dhamma** (the Pali form of dharma) referred to the Buddha's teaching itself: a path of
right conduct, non-violence, and liberation from the cycle of reincarnation that was
available to any person regardless of birth rank, not just to those fulfilling their varna
duty. This was a fundamental challenge to Varnashrama Dharma. The Buddhist Sutta Nipata
quoted by Avari makes the challenge explicit: "No Brahman is such by birth. No outcaste is
such by birth. An outcaste is such by his deeds. A Brahman is such by his deeds." — making
conduct, not birth-duty, the measure of righteousness.

Jainism similarly recognized dharma as a universal ethical commitment to non-violence
(ahimsa) and renunciation rather than as a set of caste-specific ritual obligations. Mahavira
(599–527 BCE), the 24th Tirthankara, and the [[buddha|Buddha]] "championed reform of Vedic
Brahmanism by their insistence that people should learn to give up their vices and follies,
to adopt right conduct in their daily lives and not be too concerned with rituals and
sacrifices."

## Significance

Dharma served simultaneously as cosmic principle, social cement, and political legitimation
in ancient India. Kings derived their authority partly from their adherence to dharma —
"the king's power was to be matched by his obligations to his subjects." The concept thus
linked the metaphysical, social, and political dimensions of Indian civilization in a way
that made it extraordinarily durable across the transitions from Vedic to post-Vedic,
Brahmanical to Buddhist to Hindu political culture.

## Historiography

The multi-vocality of dharma has made it a contested concept in both ancient and modern
times. Avari notes the ideological dimension: Vedic texts that codified dharma were
"revised by the learned brahmans, who were in close alliance with the kshatriyas," raising
the question of whether the scripturally sanctioned dharma represents divine order or
brahmanical self-interest. The epics were subject to "successive revisions over many
centuries," accumulating "Hindu religious imageries" that made their treatment of dharma
progressively more orthodox. Modern Hindu nationalists (Hindutva writers) have used the
Vedic sanction of dharma as part of their argument for the indigenous origins of all Indian
civilization, an argument Avari characterizes as revisionist and unsupported by the
linguistic and archaeological evidence.
