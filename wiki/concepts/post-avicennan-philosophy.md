## Definition and Scope

"Post-Avicennan philosophy" or "Avicennan scholasticism" denotes the dominant mode of philosophical and theological production in the eastern Islamic world from approximately the late 12th century to the 17th century (Safavid period). Its defining characteristic: **philosophical originality is expressed through critical engagement with [[ibn-sina|Avicenna]]'s system**, not through a return to Aristotle or departure into entirely new frameworks.

The term challenges the European historiographical narrative in which Islamic philosophy "ends" with Averroes and al-Ghazālī, after which the Islamic world lapses into decline while Latin scholasticism surges forward. Adamson argues this narrative "makes for bad history, the kind that involves thinking something never existed simply because you haven't looked for it" ([[adamson-philosophy-islamic-world-2016]]). The scholar Dimitri Gutas proposed placing the end of the Islamic "golden age" at c. 1350 — a full century after the peak of Mongol destruction.

## The Parallel to Latin Scholasticism

The eastern Islamic tradition of the 12th–17th centuries is structurally parallel to Latin scholasticism:

| Feature | Latin Scholasticism | Avicennan Scholasticism |
|---|---|---|
| Institutional base | Universities (rising from 11th–12th c.) | Madrasas (expanding under Seljūqs and successors) |
| Canonical authority | Aristotle | Avicenna |
| Primary genre | Commentary | Commentary, gloss, super-gloss |
| Dominant mode | Syllogistic disputation | Dialectical enumeration of arguments |
| Key dispute format | *quaestio* | Arguments for and against |

Adamson: "Like their Latin-writing, Christian contemporaries, Muslim philosopher-theologians in the East were producing enormously sophisticated treatises full of finely drawn distinctions, always depending on their mastery of logic."

## Four Branches

From the 12th century onwards, post-Avicennan philosophy develops four intertwining branches, all responding to Avicenna:

1. **Ashʿarite kalām enriched by Avicenna**: exemplified by [[fakhr-al-din-al-razi]] and the Shīrāz theologians. Avicennan metaphysics is selectively adopted and selectively criticized to reinforce Ashʿarite positions.

2. **Shiite philosophical theology** (Ismāʿīlī and Twelver): exemplified by [[nasir-al-din-tusi]] in his Ismāʿīlī phase, and continuing through the Safavid period with Mullā Ṣadrā. Avicennan epistemology and cosmology are deployed to prove Shiite doctrines (the Imamate, the Imam as perfect teacher, etc.).

3. **Philosophical Sufism**: the integration of Avicennan metaphysical language with the mysticism of Ibn ʿArabī. [[rumi]] and Ṣadr al-Dīn al-Qūnawī are the central 13th-century figures. The "oneness of existence" (*waḥdat al-wujūd*) doctrine uses Avicennan vocabulary.

4. **[[illuminationism|Illuminationism]] (ishrāq)**: inaugurated by [[suhrawardi-yahya]], presenting itself as a revival of ancient Platonic-Persian-Indian wisdom while in practice constituting a thoroughgoing internal critique of Avicennism expressed in the language of light.

## Central Debates

The following philosophical controversies recur across all four branches and span the entire post-Avicennan period:

- **Essence vs. existence**: Is Avicenna's distinction between the essence and existence of contingent things a real distinction (al-Rāzī, al-Ṭūsī) or only a mental distinction (Suhrawardī)? If real, is existence univocal across God and creatures (al-Rāzī, Dashtakī) or analogical (al-Ṭūsī, Dawānī)?
- **Divine knowledge of particulars**: Does God know individual things directly, or only "in a universal way" as Avicenna claimed?
- **Certainty and demonstration**: Do Avicenna's arguments reach genuine demonstration (*burhān*) or merely persuasion? This becomes "something like an obsession for theologians like al-Rāzī and al-Ṭūsī."
- **The soul and self-awareness**: Does Avicenna's "flying man" argument succeed? Is the soul a bundle of faculties or a unified seat of self-awareness ([[abu-l-barakat-al-baghdadi]])?
- **Logic and its limits**: Is Avicennian logic self-sufficient? The Liar Paradox, relational syllogisms, and the nature of definitions are contested across Marāgha, Shiraz, and the Ottoman-Safavid-Mughal courts.

## The Mongol Disruption and Continuity

The Mongol invasions (Baghdad 1258) disrupted but did not terminate this tradition. Adamson, citing Wilferd Madelung, notes that in the eastern Islamic territories under Mongol (later Īl-Khān) rule, "the study and teaching of philosophy was most certainly allowed and was practiced openly" — arguably freer than in Mamluk Egypt, where [[ibn-taymiyya]]'s cultural environment was more restrictive. The Marāgha observatory (founded 1259 by [[nasir-al-din-tusi]] under Mongol patronage) constituted "a scientific revolution before the Renaissance" according to some historians.

## The Decline Narrative Critiqued

The standard European account that Islamic philosophy "ended" with Averroes and al-Ghazālī rests on the observation that later thinkers in the Islamic world had "little or no influence on European philosophy." Adamson identifies this as a methodological error — absence from European libraries is not absence of existence. The vast surviving manuscript tradition in Istanbul, Cairo, Tehran, and Lucknow attests to a rich and continuous tradition. Scholarship on the Ottoman and Mughal branches "remains almost entirely untouched by secondary literature in European languages."

## Historiography

The category "Avicennan scholasticism" is analytical rather than emic — contemporaries would have described themselves as theologians, philosophers, jurists, or Sufis. The concept is useful for perceiving structural similarities across these self-described different groups. Adamson acknowledges it is "a bit of a simplification" even as he introduces it. The four-branch model is also schematic: most major thinkers — particularly [[nasir-al-din-tusi]] and Mullā Ṣadrā — cross the boundaries between branches.
