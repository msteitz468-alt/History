# The Eastern Question

The name given by nineteenth-century European diplomacy to the **problem of the Ottoman
Empire's weakening and the disposition of its territories** — the recurrent crises over who
would inherit the lands of "the Sick Man of Europe," and how a general war among the Great
Powers might be avoided (or won) in the process. From Caroline Finkel's
[[finkel-osmans-dream|*Osman's Dream*]].

## The problem

After the [[siege-of-vienna-1683|Treaty of Karlowitz (1699)]] the Ottoman Empire was on the
defensive, and from the late eighteenth century its retreat became the central preoccupation of
European diplomacy. The "Eastern Question" was the tangle of issues this raised: **Russian
expansion** toward the Straits and the Balkans (and its claim to protect Ottoman Orthodox
Christians); the rise of **Balkan Christian nationalisms** (Serbia, Greece, Romania, Bulgaria);
the determination of **Britain, France, and Austria** to prevent any single power (especially
Russia) from seizing the Straits and dominating the eastern Mediterranean; and the fate of the
Holy Places and the Arab lands. The Powers oscillated between **propping the empire up** (to
preserve the balance of power) and **carving it up**.

## The course of the question

- **Greek independence (1821–32)** — the first successful Balkan nationalist secession, backed
  by the Powers (Navarino, 1827).
- **The Egyptian crises (1831–41)** — Mehmed Ali of Egypt's near-overthrow of the sultan,
  resolved by Great-Power intervention.
- **The Crimean War (1853–56)** — Britain and France fighting *alongside* the Ottomans to
  check Russia: the empire's preservation as a Great-Power interest, and its formal admission
  to the "Concert of Europe."
- **The Great Eastern Crisis (1875–78)** — Balkan revolts, the Russo-Turkish War, and the
  **Congress of Berlin (1878)**, which stripped away much of Ottoman Europe (independent
  Serbia, Montenegro, Romania; an autonomous Bulgaria; Austria occupying Bosnia; Britain taking
  Cyprus).
- **The Balkan Wars (1912–13)** — the near-total expulsion of the Ottomans from Europe.

Financial dependence sharpened the question: after the **1875 default**, the European-run
**Ottoman Public Debt Administration (1881)** controlled a large share of state revenues —
"aggressive exploitation and tutelage on the part of industrializing European states," in
Finkel's phrase.

## Significance

The Eastern Question made the Ottoman Empire's fate **a permanent item of European Great-Power
politics** for over a century, and its crises repeatedly brought Europe to the brink of general
war — a tension finally discharged in **1914**, when a Balkan assassination (the Eastern
Question's heartland) triggered the [[first-world-war-1914|First World War]], in which the
empire's choice of the German side led to its [[fall-of-the-ottoman-empire-1922|dissolution]].
Finkel cautions that the "Sick Man" framing was itself a polemical European construct (see
[[ottoman-decline-thesis]]): the empire's last century was as much one of
[[tanzimat-reforms|reform and resilience]] as of retreat.
