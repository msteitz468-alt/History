# The Three Orders (Those Who Pray, Those Who Fight, Those Who Work)

## Definition

The **scheme of the three orders** divides Christian society into three
functional estates: ***oratores*** ("those who pray" — the clergy and monks),
***bellatores*** ("those who fight" — kings, lords, and knights), and
***laboratores*** ("those who work" — chiefly the peasantry). The orders are held
to be mutually dependent and divinely ordained: each labours for the others, so that
the social hierarchy is presented as a harmonious order willed by God. As
[[luscombe-ncmh-v4-2004|NCMH IV]] notes (editors' Introduction; Flori, Ch. 6), the
formulation was given classic expression "at the beginning of the eleventh century"
by **Adalbero, bishop of Laon**, who "expressed his vision of the three orders in
the house of God on earth: those who work, those who fight and those who pray."

## Origin and Function

The tripartite scheme has deep roots (an Indo-European trifunctional pattern, per
Georges Dumézil; ninth-century English and Carolingian antecedents), but its
canonical Latin statements come from **Adalbero of Laon** and **Gerard of Cambrai**
around 1025–1030 — exactly the opening of this period. Georges Duby (*The Three
Orders: Feudal Society Imagined*, 1978) read these texts as **ideology**: a model
articulated by bishops at a moment of political stress to legitimise a hierarchical
order, sanctify the warrior aristocracy's monopoly of violence, and bind the
labouring majority to its subordinate place — an "imaginary of feudalism" rather
than a neutral description.

Within NCMH IV the scheme frames several themes:
- the emergence of the **[[chivalry|bellatores]]** as a self-conscious order whose
  status rested on the ability and duty to bear arms;
- the differentiation of the **oratores** through monastic reform and the new orders
  ([[citeaux|Cistercians]], [[gregorian-reform|reform clergy]]);
- and the consolidation of the seigneurie over the **laboratores** of the
  countryside (see [[feudalism]], [[bipartite-manor]]).

## Contested Points

- **Description vs. prescription.** Following Duby, most historians read the three
  orders as an interested ideological construction, not an accurate map of an
  increasingly complex society — one that conspicuously omitted **townsmen,
  merchants, and artisans**, whose rise the commercial revolution was making
  impossible to ignore.
- **Coherence over time.** The neat tripartition coexisted uneasily with the messier
  realities of lordship, free and unfree gradations, and urban estates, and was
  always more a literary topos than a working constitution.
