# Genroku Culture (the Tokugawa Townsman Renaissance)

The flowering of **urban commoner (*chōnin*) culture** in the [[edo-period|Tokugawa]] peace,
named for the **Genroku era (1688–1703)** at its peak — the "**Floating World**" (*ukiyo*) of
the merchant cities, and the first great Japanese culture made **by and for townspeople**
rather than the court or the warriors. From George Sansom's
[[sansom-short-cultural-history-japan|cultural history]].

## The rise of the chōnin

By 1700 the [[edo-period|commercial revolution]] had given the **merchants (*chōnin*)** "most
of the money and most of the fun," even as the [[samurai]] kept rank and swords. Sansom
stresses the resulting **two cultures in tension**: the old samurai/court arts, "perpetuating
classical standards," and a "new, popular and unrestrained" townsman culture. The bakufu's
**sumptuary edicts** ("townsmen should not wear silk… should not live extravagantly") failed
utterly — the rich merely hid luxury (a sober robe with a silk lining), bequeathing a lasting
aesthetic of concealed richness. The Osaka rice magnate Yodoya, with his fifty gold screens,
was the emblem of a wealthy, sophisticated, demanding bourgeois patronage — "not the men to
be satisfied with a feeble pattern or a badly acted play."

## The Floating World (ukiyo)

Genroku arts "centred round what was called *Ukiyo*, the Floating World" — the realm of
"fugitive pleasures," of theatres, restaurants, wrestling, and the licensed quarters, peopled
by actors, courtesans, jesters, and the dissolute sons of merchants. Its characteristic forms:

- **Ukiyo-zōshi** — realistic "books of the floating world": the witty, money-and-love
  stories of **Ihara Saikaku**.
- **Drama** — the puppet theatre (**Bunraku**) and **Kabuki**, raised to art by
  **Chikamatsu Monzaemon** ("the Japanese Shakespeare"), dramatizing the conflict of duty
  (*giri*) and feeling (*ninjō*).
- **Ukiyo-e** — "pictures of the floating world": genre painting and (later) the woodblock
  colour **print**, alongside the great decorative designers **Kōetsu**, **Sōtatsu**, and
  **Ogata Kōrin** — works Sansom defends as a true, vital **native** art, not "proletarian
  rubbish," freed at last from the perfume of China.

## The decline of the samurai arts

Conversely, Sansom charts the **fossilization** of the old patrician arts under Tokugawa
formalism: classical poetry "dead," the **tea ceremony** degenerating into "empty ritual,"
**Noh** preserved as an upper-class amusement (briefly attacked by the Confucian moralist
Arai Hakuseki as "a danger to the State"), and **Buddhism** vanishing as a cultural force
after Nobunaga, Hideyoshi, and Ieyasu broke its power. The old forms kept vitality "only in
so far as they were enlivened by contact with the commoners' activities." Even Edo's growth
failed to inspire architecture (the gaudy Shōgunal **mausolea at Nikkō** the chief monument).

## Significance

Genroku culture marks the **democratization of Japanese art** — the shift of cultural
leadership from court and cloister to the city merchant — and produced enduring genres
(Kabuki, Bunraku, the ukiyo-e print, the popular novel) that became, for the world, among the
most recognizable faces of Japanese culture. It is the social mirror of the
[[edo-period|baku-han]] order's deep tension between a ruling class losing economic power and
a commoner class gaining it.
