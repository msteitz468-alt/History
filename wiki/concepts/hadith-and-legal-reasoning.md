# Ḥadīth and Legal Reasoning

The relationship between [[hadith|ḥadīth]] (formal Prophetic narrative equipped with chains of transmission) and legal reasoning designates one of the central conflicts in the formation of Islamic law: the confrontation between the growing corpus of Prophetic traditions and the older, practice-based modes of legal argument collectively known as *raʾy* (personal opinion and discretionary reasoning). The resolution of this conflict — through the eventual subordination of raʾy to text-controlled methods — was the proximate cause of mature [[usul-al-fiqh|uṣūl al-fiqh]] (legal theory).

## Raʾy and Its Spectrum

During the first two centuries H, *raʾy* was the dominant mode of legal reasoning. The term covered a wide spectrum:

- **Discretionary and loose reasoning** — personal judgment drawing on local practice, custom, and equity
- **Juristic preference (istiḥsān)** — reaching reasonable results rather than applying strict logical inference when qiyās would produce hardship or inequity
- **Strictly logical arguments** — analogical reasoning (*qiyās*), *a fortiori* arguments, and similar structured inferences

All three categories operated under the umbrella of raʾy. The Medinese, Iraqians, and Syrians made extensive use of all of them. The Iraqians (Kufans) were especially identified with qiyās-style reasoning and with istiḥsān, to the point that al-Shāfiʿī later called them "the Folk of Qiyās."

The critical feature of second-century raʾy is that it was not purely independent of Prophetic and Qurānic authority — Kufan jurists already recognized that qiyās "had to rest on the texts and that it could not be used in the presence of established sunnaic and textual rules." What was absent was a systematic theory requiring all reasoning to be grounded in and controlled by Prophetic texts.

## The Traditionalist Challenge

With the proliferation of formal Prophetic ḥadīth during the second half of the second/eighth century, raʾy came under structural pressure:

> "The more pronounced this [Prophetic] authority became, the less freedom the jurists had in expounding discretionary opinion. For, after all, the raison d'être of Prophetic authority was its ability to induce conformity of conduct to the Prophetic model. Insofar as it included discretionary and personal opinion, raʾy stood as antithetical to this notion of authority."

The traditionalist (*ahl al-ḥadīth*) movement — a mobile, geographically unattached class of scholars devoted to collecting and authenticating Prophetic narratives — drove this pressure. By the middle of the third/ninth century, ḥadīth had "won the war against raʾy," and six canonical ḥadīth collections had been compiled and arranged specifically to service legal reasoning.

## The Terminological Dissolution of Raʾy

As ḥadīth asserted itself, the broad term *raʾy* underwent terminological disaggregation. Hallaq identifies three categories that crystallized out of the old rubric:

### 1. Ijtihād
Originally, *ijtihād* appeared in combination as *ijtihād al-raʾy* — the exertion of mental energy to reach a considered opinion. By the third/ninth century it shed the *raʾy* component and came to designate the full juristic effort of a qualified mujtahid exercising his interpretive faculties across all applicable methods (including qiyās and hermeneutical analysis of texts). In [[usul-al-fiqh|uṣūl al-fiqh]], ijtihād became the overarching concept of which qiyās was a subset — the inverse of al-Shāfiʿī's usage, where the two had been near-synonyms. See [[taqlid-and-ijtihad|Taqlīd and Ijtihād]].

### 2. Qiyās
*Qiyās* designated the formally structured analogical and logical arguments that had always been present within raʾy but that now emerged as the sole *legitimate* method of inference once ḥadīth had displaced sunnaic practice. Al-Shāfiʿī's crucial theoretical move was to define qiyās as purely text-controlled: it does not generate norms independently but "discovers" them from the language of the Qurān and Prophetic ḥadīth. Qiyās "cannot repeal or supersede" the sources on which it rests. See [[qiyas-analogy|Qiyās (Analogy)]].

Al-Shāfiʿī's formal articulation of qiyās encompassed:
- **Analogy proper**: four-element structure (new case, original case, ratio legis/ʿilla, transposed norm)
- **A fortiori** (*a maiore ad minus*, *a minore ad maius*)
- **Reductio ad absurdum**: establishing a rule by demonstrating the invalidity of its converse
- **Causative vs. indicative inference**: depending on whether the ratio and its rationale are explicitly known or only conjectured

Qiyās is also qualified by abrogation: a jurist must know which texts have been abrogated before employing any as the basis for inference.

### 3. Istiḥsān
*Istiḥsān* ("juristic preference") was the category that fared worst in the terminological redistribution. Al-Shāfiʿī attacked it as "human legislation" — reasoning that departed from text without sufficient warrant, effectively arbitrary raʾy under a polite name. His attack was polemically effective: in later legal theory, istiḥsān was formally redefined by Ḥanafī theorists as a *species* of qiyās — a "preferred" analogical inference grounded ultimately in a revealed text, consensus, or necessity. The Ḥanbalī school eventually adopted it as well, despite Ibn Ḥanbal's own hostility to all human reasoning. Only the literalist Ẓāhirite school (see [[zahirism|Ẓāhirism]]) refused this accommodation and consequently expired.

In mature Ḥanafī uṣūl, istiḥsān covers three situations:
1. A Prophetic ḥadīth overrides what strict qiyās would dictate (e.g., eating accidentally while fasting invalidates the fast by qiyās, but is pardoned by an explicit ḥadīth)
2. Consensus sanctions a practice that strict qiyās would void (e.g., hire contracts without immediate payment)
3. Necessity requires departure from qiyās (e.g., praying after ritual purification with impure water when pure water is unavailable)

### Maṣlaḥa (Public Interest)
Related to istiḥsān, the Mālikī concept of *istiṣlāḥ/maṣlaḥa* ("public interest") was the Medinan equivalent of Kufan istiḥsān: reasoning not obviously grounded in a specific text but oriented toward the five universal legal principles (protection of life, mind, religion, private property, and offspring). Later Mālikī theory retrospectively argued that all such reasoning ultimately derived from a universal textual principle, thus bringing maṣlaḥa under the same text-dependence required by the emerging mainstream.

## The Subordination of Raʾy: Significance

The displacement of raʾy by text-controlled methods had several systemic consequences:

1. **Epistemic hierarchy**: Qurān and ḥadīth became "material" sources of certain or probable knowledge; [[ijma-consensus|ijmāʿ]] and qiyās became "procedural" sources drawing on the former. Human reasoning operates only in the absence of a relevant text and must remain anchored in that text.

2. **Plurality of opinion preserved**: The shift did not produce legal monism. Because most Prophetic ḥadīth yielded only probable (not certain) knowledge (being transmitted through less than *mutawātir* chains), and because the ratio legis (*ʿilla*) in qiyās is often disputed, qualified jurists (mujtahids) can legitimately reach different conclusions from the same sources. The maxim "all qualified jurists are correct" preserved doctrinal diversity within the new text-controlled framework.

3. **Taqlīd as the structural consequence**: Once the mujtahid class was defined by its mastery of texts and hermeneutical method, all others — jurist-muqallids and laymen — were relegated to following (taqlīd) the mujtahid's opinion. See [[taqlid-and-ijtihad|Taqlīd and Ijtihād]].

4. **Hermeneutics as the core of legal theory**: Because rules must be derived from texts, the theory of legal language became central to [[usul-al-fiqh|uṣūl al-fiqh]]: the distinction between univocal (*naṣṣ*), ambiguous, general, and metaphorical expressions; the rules of abrogation; the transmission-credibility requirements for ḥadīth (chain continuity, transmitter justice/ʿadāla, precision, absence of sectarian bias).

## Historiography

The narrative of raʾy's defeat by ḥadīth is subject to several qualifications in Hallaq's account:

- The defeat was *incomplete*: Ḥanafī istiḥsān survived in modified form; the later Ḥanbalī school adopted qiyās despite Ibn Ḥanbal's hostility; only the Ẓāhirites held the traditionalist line to the point of extinction.
- Al-Shāfiʿī's role was theoretically important but *not decisive*: his theory lacked acceptance in his own lifetime and his followers remained few until Ibn Surayj's circle a century later. The standard attribution of the raʾy/ḥadīth synthesis to al-Shāfiʿī was a retrospective construction.
- The Mihna (833–848 CE) — the caliphal Inquisition over the createdness of the Qurān — was ultimately a conflict over hermeneutics: the Muʿtazilite rationalists vs. the traditionalists headed by [[ibn-hanbal-ahmad|Aḥmad b. Ḥanbal]]. The traditionalists' victory signaled not that they had won intellectually, but that extreme rationalism had lost political support. The synthesis that followed was a centrist position between the two extremes.
