# Ancient Near Eastern Modes of Production

The economic-anthropological framework Mario Liverani uses to read three millennia of
Near Eastern history through production and exchange rather than narrative events
([[liverani-ancient-near-east-2014|Liverani 2014]]). It is the analytic engine that
lets him correct the documentary bias toward palaces and luxury (see
[[ancient-near-east-historiography]]) by foregrounding how surplus was produced,
who owned the means of production, and how goods moved.

## Definition and Scope

Liverani treats "mode of production" not as Marxist orthodoxy but as a heuristic. The
nineteenth-century **"Asiatic mode of production"** debate, he argues, belongs to the
history of Marxist studies rather than to the Near East itself: it was built on
indirect, largely irrelevant evidence. What remains useful is the underlying analytic —
identifying (a) the type of ownership of the means of production (above all land), (b)
the relationship between means of production and workforce, (c) the size of production
units, and (d) the accumulation of surplus. The variable combination of these factors
defines a society's economic structure.

He is equally wary of the **modernist–primitivist** dichotomy: Near Eastern economies
were neither modern market economies nor "elementary" primitive ones. Concepts like
market and currency are anachronistic here, yet the systems were genuinely sophisticated
and demand purpose-built models.

## Causal Drivers

The decisive driver is the **Urban Revolution** (see [[urban-revolution]]), which
created the **"great organisations"** — temple and palace — as centres of production.
These institutions concentrated surplus, drove labour specialisation, and subordinated
food-producers to those holding political and administrative control. Before and at the
periphery of urbanisation, simpler arrangements persisted.

## Major Phases — The Two Models

Liverani's core schema is a pair of interacting modes:

| | **Palace (palatial) mode** | **Household (domestic) mode** |
|---|---|---|
| Origin | Product of the Urban Revolution | Residual from the Neolithic |
| Means/workforce | Producers in a servile relation to the great organisations | Owners of means of production = the workforce |
| Labour | Strong, dynamic full-time specialisation | No structured full-time specialisation |
| Surplus | Centripetal, redistributive influx; hierarchical | Roughly egalitarian among members |
| Exchange logic | **Redistribution** (Polanyi) | **Reciprocity** (Polanyi) |

The two are locked in a **hegemony–subordination** dynamic: the palace mode cannot
exist without the household mode it absorbs and restructures, stripping the latter of its
former autonomy. This oscillation — palatial-urban concentration vs. the reassertion of
village/tribal household organisation — recurs across the whole sweep of ANE history and
is, for Liverani, the deep rhythm beneath the dynastic narrative (e.g. the "crises" of
urbanisation between the Early, Middle, and Late Bronze Ages).

## Geographic Spread

The palatial-redistributive current dominated the **fertile irrigated plains**
(Lower Mesopotamia above all): irrigation, cereal cultivation, sheep/goat herding,
organised trade, palace workshops, and the religious character of political authority.
Alternative strategies prevailed in **resource-extraction and marginal zones** — metals,
wood, semiprecious stones; transhumance; rain-fed agriculture — typically managed by
communal groups under an elite, where **reciprocity** persisted more strongly. The
notable exception to redistributive dominance is **inter-state commercial exchange**,
best documented in the [[old-assyrian-trade|Old Assyrian trade]] networks.

## Interaction (Systems of Exchange)

Liverani folds Polanyi's three integrative models into the scheme:

- **Reciprocity** — household mode and marginal/inter-personal exchange
- **Redistribution** — the dominant system, tied to palace and temple administration
- **Market** — largely unsuitable for ANE economies, though certain market-like
  elements emerge early in some areas

He stresses these are **interpretive, not descriptive**: the same act of exchange can be
read as reciprocal or redistributive depending on purpose, so the models belong as much
to the study of ideology as of economy. Quantification — the only way to grasp the real
weight of exchange — is defeated by limited and selective sources (Old Assyrian trade
again the exception).

## End Conditions

The framework describes a structure that persists, with oscillations, until the
period's close; it does not "end" so much as scale up. The rise of territorial empires
(Neo-Assyrian, then Persian) folds local palatial economies into supra-regional systems
of tribute and provincial extraction, transforming the redistributive logic onto an
imperial scale.

## Limitations and Critiques

- The two-model schema is admittedly idealised; real economies blended modes, and the
  "servile" characterisation of palace-dependent labour is debated.
- Importing Polanyi and Marx risks imposing external categories on data that resist them
  — a tension Liverani concedes by calling the models interpretive constructs.
- The persistent scarcity of quantitative data means the relative weight of palace vs.
  household vs. market remains, for most periods, an argument rather than a measurement.
