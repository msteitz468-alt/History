## Definition and Scope

Kongo cosmopolitanism denotes the historically documented capacity of the [[kingdom-of-kongo|Kingdom of Kongo]] to engage systematically with foreign objects, practices, and symbolic systems — not as passive recipients of external influence, but as active agents who appropriated foreign material and transformed it into specifically Kongo markers of prestige, authority, and identity. The related concept of **extraversion** (a term Clist ch. 8 borrows from Vos ch. 10 of the same volume) captures the structural dimension: Kongo elites deliberately cultivated relationships with the Atlantic world in order to channel foreign goods into domestic power hierarchies. The two concepts are complementary — extraversion describes the *strategy*; cosmopolitanism describes the *character of the resulting culture*.

Together, chapters 6 (Fromont), 7 (Cranshof et al.), and 8 (Clist) of *The Kongo Kingdom* (Bostoen & Brinkman 2018) provide a convergent multi-material demonstration of this phenomenon across three distinct object classes: Christian visual objects, woven-motif ceramics, and tobacco pipes.

## Three Material Domains

### 1. Christian Visual Culture (Fromont, ch. 6)

The introduction of Christianity in the late fifteenth and early sixteenth centuries under [[afonso-i-kongo|Afonso I]] is the paradigm case of Kongo extraversion. European Christian imagery — crucifixes, pendants depicting the Virgin Mary and Saint Anthony, clerical vestments, swords — entered the kingdom not as colonial impositions but as objects the Kongo elite sought out and remade. Fromont (ch. 6) demonstrates that:

- Copper alloy pendants of saints were worn in ways that created visual echoes between the saint's garb and the wearer's own prestige clothing. Saint Anthony's Franciscan habit mirrored the draped cloth of elite Kongo men; the tonsure approximated the *mpu* cap. The saints effectively became Kongo nobles in the act of being worn.
- Swords, among the most emblematic objects of Kongo kingship, were actively reworked: ivory handles, recomposed metalwork, angel-face pommels that also participated in the logic of power figures (*minkisi*). The sword from Tomb 4 at Kindoki is a foreign object that has been thoroughly Kongoized.
- Christian insignia (crucifixes, medals, swords) formed a unified set of political regalia founding the kingdom's legitimacy on Christianity. The *extraversion* lay in making this foreign religion the cornerstone of domestic authority.
- The gendered dimensions of this appropriation were structured by local categories: men, who held the most prominent public leadership roles, were the primary bearers of Christian political insignia; women's prestige derived partly from commercial wealth and initiatory associations — alternative sources that were locally grounded.

### 2. Woven-Motif Ceramics (Cranshof et al., ch. 7)

The second domain involves the transfer of the kingdom's most prestigious domestic production — elaborately woven raffia cloth — into a foreign medium (ceramics) in a process of skeuomorphism. Cranshof et al. (ch. 7) argue that:

- The geometric interlaced patterns characteristic of luxury raffia cloth and *mpu* caps were deliberately transposed onto ceramics. This was not a spontaneous folk tradition but a calculated prestige move: the potter invoking the authority of luxury textile culture.
- This transfer occurred only in the Kongo cultural zone; neighboring regions with equally strong weaving traditions (the Kuba, various Malebo Pool traditions) did not make the same move at the same scale — suggesting the transfer was specific to the particular combination of political centralization and textile prestige that characterized the Kongo kingdom.
- The ceramic tradition's geographic distribution and chronological profile mirror the kingdom's political rise and fall, confirming that this material homogeneity was produced *by* centralized political integration and ended *with* political fragmentation.

In this domain, cosmopolitanism operates differently: the Kongo did not appropriate an external prestige system but projected their own most valued material (luxury cloth) onto a new medium, creating a distinctively Kongo material culture that marked territorial and political identity across multiple object classes simultaneously.

### 3. Tobacco Pipes (Clist, ch. 8)

The most striking example of extraversion — because it involves an object with no antecedent whatsoever in Kongo material culture — is the adoption of tobacco and the smoking pipe, analysed in detail in the [[introduction-of-tobacco-kongo]] entry. Key cosmopolitan dimensions:

- Tobacco arrived from the Americas via the Atlantic trade, almost certainly through English and Dutch contacts at the Congo mouth, between c. 1583 and c. 1612.
- Within one to two generations, Kongo craftsmen had created an entirely new pipe typology — maintaining the long-stemmed form from English/Dutch models but covering it with local geometric decoration, thickening the walls, enlarging the furnaces, and producing some examples in serpentinite stone transported over 100 km.
- The few imported pipes found in Kongo excavations confirm that the adoption was not a matter of trading for foreign objects, but of learning the form and immediately producing local versions. At El Mina on the Gold Coast, imported English and Dutch pipes dominate; at Kongo sites, local production dominates almost immediately.
- Smoking was initially associated with noble status — first tried by nobles (the high price of Brazilian tobacco limited access), then diffusing down to commoners and eventually possibly slaves as local tobacco cultivation developed.
- Stone pipes, produced exclusively in Mbata province in the seventeenth century, functioned as *intra-elite* status markers — the Mwene Mbata using them to differentiate himself within the kingdom's nobility. Even within the prestige category, cosmopolitan foreign objects were actively hierarchized by Kongo social actors.
- Clist (ch. 8) explicitly invokes "extraversion" to describe this process: "engravings, watercolours, written sources and archaeological objects all reveal the extent and the speed of the material cultural transformation within a generation of contact under the leadership of the first Christianized Kongo kings."

## The Homogenization Argument

A cross-cutting argument connecting all three chapters is that political centralization produced **material-culture homogenization** across the kingdom's provinces. This is demonstrated by:

- The simultaneous presence of standardized clay pipe types (Af1, Af2, Af3) at [[mbanza-kongo|Mbanza Kongo]], Ngongo Mbata, and Kindoki during the sixteenth to eighteenth centuries — the same types at the capital and in the northern provinces.
- The distribution of D-pot ceramics (ch. 7) at precisely the kingdom's three major political-commercial centres, not at intermediate sites.
- Bostoen and de Schryver's linguistic evidence (referenced in ch. 7 and ch. 8) showing contact-induced convergence toward the high-prestige South Kikongo variety spoken near Mbanza Kongo, producing a "central convergence zone" across the [[kikongo-language-cluster]].
- The parallel disappearance of elaborate ceramics, luxury weaving traditions, and complex stone pipe production after the political fragmentation following the Battle of Mbwila (1665) and the civil wars (1641–1718).

Material culture, linguistic convergence, and political integration thus moved together as a single system. The kingdom's cosmopolitanism was not peripheral decoration but integral to its political economy: the flow of foreign prestige goods sustained the internal hierarchy that made centralization possible.

## Extraversion and Its Limits

The concept of extraversion — turning outward to generate inward power — illuminates Kongo political history but also has limits. The chapters reveal at least two complications:

1. **Saturation and fragmentation.** The typological divide in pipes after c. 1718, the collapse of woven-motif ceramics, and the shift in glass-bead types all confirm that when the Atlantic connections sustaining the prestige economy were disrupted by political collapse, the material culture they had generated collapsed too. Extraversion was a strategy that produced path-dependent vulnerability: the more the kingdom's internal legitimacy depended on the visible command of foreign goods, the more it was exposed to disruptions in Atlantic supply chains.

2. **Local transformation as a bulwark.** Precisely because Kongo craftsmen consistently *made* prestige objects rather than merely *trading* for them, the tradition proved more durable than a purely import-dependent system would have been. The rapid localization of pipe production — and the centuries-long ceramic tradition — show that extraversion was always paired with active local creativity. The kingdom was cosmopolitan in the deepest sense: it remade the world in Kongo terms.

## Historiography

The older historiography on the Kongo and Atlantic contact was structured by an implicit metropolitan/periphery model in which African societies were portrayed as recipients of European religious and material culture. The revisionist turn — associated above all with John Thornton (1984, 1998b), Cécile Fromont (2014), and the KongoKing project — established that the Kongo elite were sophisticated and deliberate agents whose engagement with Christianity and European material goods served internally legible goals. Chapters 6, 7, and 8 of Bostoen & Brinkman (2018) collectively provide the most extensive multi-material demonstration of this agency available for any single African kingdom.

The concept of extraversion (Bayart 2000, applied to Kongo by Vos) imports a framework from political science that is fruitful but requires caution: it captures the strategic dimension but can underplay the genuine cultural transformations that occurred when foreign objects became deeply embedded in local cosmological and social systems. The angel-face sword pommel that participates in *minkisi* logic was no longer simply a foreign object being instrumentalized; it had become part of a local practice with its own coherent logic.
