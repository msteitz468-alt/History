# The Cypriot Syllabary

The **Cypriot syllabary** is the remarkable script with which the Greeks of
[[cyprus|Cyprus]] wrote their dialect for most of a millennium — a **syllabic** system
(each sign a consonant-plus-vowel or a bare vowel) at a time when the rest of the Greek
world had adopted the [[alphabet|alphabet]]. Its survival is the clearest single sign
of Cyprus's cultural conservatism and its unbroken link to the Aegean Bronze Age.
[[boardman-cah-v3p3-1982|CAH Vol. III, Part 3]] (Ch. 36d, T. B. Mitford & Olivier
Masson) treats it.

## A Bronze Age survival

Unlike mainland Greece, which lost its Bronze Age [[aegean-scripts|Linear B]] in the
Dark Age and re-acquired writing as the new Semitic-derived alphabet, **Cyprus never
became illiterate**. Its Classical syllabary descends directly from a Late Bronze Age
script Evans called **"Cypro-Minoan"** (three varieties: C.M.1 island-wide, C.M.2 on
Enkomi tablets, C.M.3 at Ugarit), itself derived ultimately from **Aegean Linear A**.
The discovery (1979) of a bronze spit in a c. eleventh-century tomb at **Skales near
Old Paphos**, incised *o-pe-le-ta-u* — already a recognizable Greek (Arcado-Cypriot)
genitive, *Opheltau* — proved the syllabary existed by c. 1050 BCE. The apparent gap
in texts until the eighth century reflects not illiteracy but the loss of perishable
materials (wood, leather): "the old problem of a supposed illiterate Dark Age is now
satisfactorily solved." The island's **strong local tradition prevailed against both
the Phoenician letters and the new Greek alphabet**.

## Form and use

- **The signary.** The "Common" syllabary current in central Cyprus has about
  **55–56 signs** — strikingly tidier than Linear B's 87, having shed the homophones,
  the ideograms, and the complex *dwe/nwa/pte* signs. It notes the five vowels and
  forms consonant-plus-vowel groups (*ka, ke, ki, ko, ku*…), but is **predominantly
  written right-to-left** (sinistroverse), unlike the left-to-right Linear scripts.
- **Imperfections for Greek.** The script was "ill suited to writing a Greek dialect":
  it could not distinguish voiced/voiceless/aspirated stops (one sign serves *pa/ba/
  pha*), ignored aspiration and double consonants, and dropped nasals before
  consonants — yet it solved final consonants elegantly with "dead-vowel" syllables
  (*po-to-li-se* = *ptolis*).
- **Two repertories.** A **"Common"** signary (typified by Idalium) and a divergent
  **South-Western or "Paphian"** one (with ~20 distinctive forms, often dextroverse),
  closely linked to the Archaic script of Curium. It also wrote the still-undeciphered
  non-Greek **"Eteo-Cypriot"** language, especially at Amathus.

## The key documents

The cornerstone is the **Bronze Tablet of Idalium** (ICS no. 217, c. 480–470 BCE) — over
1,000 signs, complete and legible, recording King Stasicyprus and the city of Idalium
contracting with the physician Onasilus and his brothers to treat warriors wounded in
the Persian-and-Citian siege, paying them in land "instead of a fee." Its decipherment
(begun 1871 by George Smith, using the **Phoenician–Cypriot bilingual of Idalium**)
unlocked both the script and the dialect. Masson's 1961 corpus assembled ~380
inscriptions from Cyprus and ~80 mercenary graffiti from Egypt; nearly 1,000 are now
known. The script's last datable use is at the **Nymphaeum of Kafizin, 220/219 BCE**,
after which the *koine* and the alphabet finally displaced it.

## Significance

- A living demonstration that **Cyprus preserved Mycenaean-Greek cultural elements**
  (the Arcado-Cypriot dialect, religion, the script) across the Dark Age that
  obliterated them on the mainland.
- A control case for the history of writing: the syllabary's persistence shows the
  [[alphabet|alphabet's]] triumph was not inevitable, and that **conservatism and
  local political identity** (Paphos exaggerated its peculiar signary as a mark of its
  theocratic uniqueness) could keep an "inferior" script alive for centuries.
