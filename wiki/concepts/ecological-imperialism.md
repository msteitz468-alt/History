# Ecological Imperialism

**Ecological imperialism** names the process by which European humans, together with their accompanying plants, animals, and pathogens (the "portmanteau biota"), achieved demographic and ecological dominance in temperate zones outside Europe through biological competition and transformation rather than (or in addition to) military conquest.

The term and the analysis are most closely associated with Alfred W. Crosby’s 1986 book, which argues that the creation of "Neo-Europes" in North America, southern South America, Australia, and New Zealand was fundamentally a story of Old World biota out-competing and displacing indigenous biota in climates similar to those of Europe.

## Core Elements

- **Portmanteau biota**: The bundled suite of European weeds (e.g., plantain/"Englishman’s foot," dandelion), livestock (horses, cattle, pigs, sheep, rabbits), crops, and pathogens that traveled with settlers.
- **Pioneer advantage**: Weeds often arrived or spread first, preparing disturbed ground for crops and livestock.
- **Explosive multiplication**: In the absence of Old World competitors, predators, and diseases, European animals reproduced at rates that rapidly altered landscapes and provided food, transport, and power.
- **Immunological and disease component**: Old World crowd diseases (smallpox, measles, etc.) continued to play roles, but the longer-term pattern emphasized competitive displacement of native species and humans.
- **Temperate-zone specificity**: Success was greatest where climates matched Europe’s; outcomes differed sharply in tropical environments (e.g., sub-Saharan Africa, much of South and Southeast Asia).

## Distinctions

Ecological imperialism is distinct from (but intertwined with):
- Political/military imperialism (guns, administration, capital).
- The initial Columbian Exchange (1492+), which Crosby had already analyzed; this work extends the story into the mechanisms of sustained replacement and the 19th-century "civilian wave" of mass migration.
- Pure geographic determinism (see related controversy pages and Diamond critiques).

## Historiographic Significance

Crosby’s framing shifted attention from purely human-centered narratives of conquest and "discovery" toward the biological and ecological preconditions that made certain forms of European expansion demographically transformative. It influenced environmental history, global history, and world-systems scholarship by insisting that "man is a biological entity first."

The concept is foundational for understanding why the temperate "Neo-Europes" became the world’s primary agricultural exporters and demographic extensions of Europe while producing very different long-term population structures in the tropics.
