# Consumer Capitalism and the End of the Cold War

Mass consumer goods and the system of mass production/marketing of abundance became “symbols and the substance” of the end of the Cold War in 1989–90. Consumerism involved not only goods but “purchasing, desire, glamour, and flexible, consumption-driven identities.”

Pre-Cold War US goods (Gillette, Singer, automobiles, etc.) were equated with “America” = modernity, leisure, and the New Woman. Soviet leaders admired Fordist techniques but critiqued exploitation; conservatives decried materialism and feminization.

Early Cold War: Marshall Plan, “people’s capitalism,” USIA exhibitions (Brussels 1958, Moscow 1959 kitchen debate) presented American consumer abundance as proof of superiority. Gender (appliances “liberating” women) and race/class claims were central. Both systems promised abundance as a legitimacy test; the consumption gap was politically explosive (Berlin Wall).

Late Cold War: multilocal adaptations rather than pure “Americanization.” European and Japanese variants drew on local traditions plus “transnational America.” Advertising and media deregulation spread glamour and desire.

China (post-1978 Deng “modernization” and open door): advertising, tourism, rock concerts (Jan & Dean 1986), McDonald’s, Mastercard; per capita income doubled 1978–90; urban consumerism and “commodity self” challenged state monopoly.

Soviet bloc: rock music (1980s golden age) functioned as generational and political resistance; borrowing for consumer subsidies (Poland); elites with Western access drove “new thinking.” Gorbachev looked to West European social-democratic models.

Third World: almost all nationalist programs promised consumer modernity and prosperity; elites embraced it (especially oil states); critics included Islamic fundamentalists and the marginalized poor.

Consumerism “adapted to and transcended” national (specifically US) identification. In differentiated, multilocal forms it became “the ‘ism’ that ‘won’.”
