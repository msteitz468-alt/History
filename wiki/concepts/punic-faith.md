# Punic Faith (*fides Punica*) and the Memory of Carthage

## Definition

***Fides Punica*** — "Carthaginian faith" — was a sardonic Latin byword meaning gross
treachery and faithlessness. It is the linguistic core of a wider phenomenon: the
**construction of Carthage as a moral antitype** by the very people who destroyed it.
The adjective itself, *Punicus* (from *Poenus*), was, in one scholar's words, "nearly
always defamatory and pejorative" — not a self-designation but a Roman ethnic moniker.
For Richard Miles ([[miles-carthage-must-be-destroyed-2010]]) the entire received image
of the treacherous, cruel, decadent, child-sacrificing Carthaginian is an ideological
artefact, because Rome destroyed not only the city but its archive: in 146 BCE it
dispersed Carthage's libraries to the Numidian princes, leaving the Greco-Roman version
of events unchallenged. Studying Carthage is therefore "like reading a transcript of a
conversation in which one participant's contribution has been deleted."

## Genealogy of the Stereotype

- **Greek origins.** The hostile profile began with the Sicilian Greeks, Carthage's
  pre-Roman rivals. **Timaeus of Tauromenium** (c. 345–250 BCE) — a "ghost historian"
  surviving via Diodorus — was pivotal: he cast Carthage as "the agent of the barbarous
  Orient in the West," fabricated a common 813 BCE founding date for Rome and Carthage,
  and lavished attention on child sacrifice.
- **Roman adoption.** Romans began writing their own history *during* the wars with
  Carthage (Fabius Pictor, c. 210 BCE; Cato's Latin *Origines*, to 149 BCE), so the
  defeat of Carthage cemented Roman historical orthodoxy. Early epic — Naevius' *Bellum
  Poenicum*, Ennius' *Annales* — recast the wars as divinely ordained, with Venus for
  Rome and Juno for Carthage.
- **The cult of *fides*.** The first temple to *Fides* was built in 249 BCE; Miles notes
  the irony that the cult of "good faith" grew precisely as Rome's overseas realpolitik
  made good faith its first victim (e.g. the seizure of Sardinia in 238–237, condemned
  even by Polybius as "contrary to all justice"). The **Regulus legend** (self-sacrifice
  on the altar of good faith) crystallised by the 120s BCE and was burnished under
  Augustus.
- **Livy and Augustus.** [[livy|Livy]] did not coin *fides Punica* but entrenched it,
  contrasting Roman virtue with Carthaginian vice (Hannibal's perfidy "worse than
  Punic"). [[augustus|Augustan]] ideology revived *fides* and *pietas* as the foundations
  of Roman greatness, with Carthage as the ultimate moral foil.

## Miles's Counter-Argument

Carthaginians were no more faithless than Romans in the [[second-punic-war-218-201bce|Second
Punic War]]; the charge served to deflect attention from Rome's own breaches (the
abandonment of Saguntum, the seizure of Sardinia). The stereotype was an instrument of
**power over the past**: with destruction, Rome gained "the power to justify the
unjustifiable" and became "the makers of history." That Corinth was levelled in the same
year (146 BCE) exposes the hypocrisy of the "fear of Carthage" justification — destruction
as a deliberate symbolic statement that dissent against Rome would not be tolerated.

## The Afterlife of Carthage

- **Appropriating Heracles.** Augustus claimed the [[heracles-melqart-syncretism|Heraclean
  legacy]] Hannibal had used — a triple triumph timed to the festival of Hercules
  (29 BCE), the Via Augusta from Gades — marking Carthage's "final defeat" on the
  ideological plane.
- **Refoundation.** Julius Caesar conceived, and Augustus built (from 29 BCE), *Colonia
  Iulia Concordia Carthago* — a monument to *concordia* on the site of Rome's bitterest
  enemy. Miles notes the irony that this Roman rebuilding destroyed more of Punic Carthage
  than Scipio's army had.
- **Virgil's Dido.** The *Aeneid* gave the enmity a mythic prehistory in the
  [[dido-elissa|Dido]]–Aeneas affair. Miles reads Virgil's Dido as industrious, honest,
  and pious — "more Roman than Aeneas" — subverting the stereotype and pointing to
  Carthaginians' capacity to become "good Romans," while her dying curse foretells the
  wars and Hannibal the avenger.
- **Modern reuse.** The stereotype was revived by 18th–19th c. Europe and America: France
  cast Britain ("*perfide Albion*") as the modern Carthage; Thomas Jefferson echoed "the
  *Punica fides* of modern Carthage"; Flaubert's *Salammbô* (1862) projected Oriental
  decadence onto a colonial-era Carthage. Carthage also served the oppressed as a metaphor
  (Irish antiquarians; Seamus Heaney; "Iraq is Washington's Carthage"). Miles's point: the
  continued "relevance" of Carthage has always been parasitic on the abiding obsession
  with Rome.

## Limitations

The argument is itself an interpretation built by reading hostile sources against the
grain; where independent Carthaginian testimony is absent, the corrective rests on
material culture and on inference. The claim that specific Roman cults and texts were
direct *responses* to Hannibalic propaganda is part of Miles's broader
[[heracles-melqart-syncretism|Heracles thesis]] and shares its contestability.
