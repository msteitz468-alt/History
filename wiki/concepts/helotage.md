# Helotage

**Helotage** denotes the system of dependent agrarian populations (*heilôtai*) bound to Spartan masters in Laconia and Messenia, and by extension comparable Greek serf-like statuses. [[sources/hall-history-archaic-greek-world-2014|Hall (2014)]] treats helotage within Archaic conquest, inequality, and peasant economy (Chs.7, 10), stressing complexity over simple “enslavement on the land” models.

## Definition and scope

Hall argues helots of Laconia/Messenia and Thessalian Penestai fit peasantry/serf definitions: family units, marriage rights, fixed or proportional dues to masters, no political rights. Pollux places them between free and slave. Pylos survey suggests village residence in western Messenia.

Helotage is **not** identical to chattel slavery (*dmôes* in Homer/Hesiod are permanently household-resident unfree; *thêtes* are hired casual/seasonal wage laborers). Sharecroppers and dependent communities owed regular quotas; larger elite holdings needed labor beyond household capacity.

## Origin and the Messenian Wars

Modern consensus links helotage to the [[events/messenian-wars-archaic|Messenian Wars]]: Messenians enslaved on their territory after the First War or after a failed Second War rebellion; forced to deliver a substantial share of produce (like southern Laconian helots); surplus enables Spartan full-time military training needed to police dependents.

Hall complicates annexation-and-enslavement:

- Tyrtaeus fr. 5 speaks of capturing **Messene**, not all Messenia.
- Evidence suggests a **spectrum** of free and unfree labor; sanctuary dedications imply perioikoi in parts of Messenia; some Messenians may have abandoned plots and fled.
- Pausanias’ post-First conditions (oath, half produce, funeral mourning — also required of Laconian perioikoi per Hdt. 6.58.3) stress social-political as well as economic exploitation.
- Aelian: some farm, some sold, some killed.
- Parallel dependent labor at Sicyon, Argos, Syracuse, Byzantium, Heraclea Pontica, Thessaly, West Locris, Crete, Megara under Bacchiads.

Primary poetic witness: Tyrtaeus frs. 6–7 — Messenians “just like asses oppressed by heavy burdens, bringing to their masters out of hateful necessity half of all the fruits that the earth bears,” and ritual mourning obligations at masters’ deaths.

## Relations of production and Archaic unrest

Partible inheritance and variegated microzones produced subsistence risk; loans on personal security and inadequate *thês* wages widened the gap between wealthy and dispossessed, fomenting Archaic unrest that climaxed at Athens in the early sixth century ([[concepts/seisachtheia]]). Helotage is Sparta’s extreme institutionalization of dependent labor within that wider Archaic pattern.

## End conditions / transformation

Third Messenian War (460s); liberation of Messenia 369 by Epaminondas after Leuctra; new Messene on Ithome. What was unique, Hall argues, was less dependency itself than a vocal Messenian diaspora that realized an independent Peloponnesian state with Theban help ([[places/messenia]]).

## Emic / etic

- **Emic:** *heilôtai*, *perioikoi*, ritual and legal disabilities.
- **Etic:** “helotage,” “serfdom,” “peasantry” as comparative labels; Pollux’s intermediate status is ancient but classificatory schemes vary.

## Limitations

Source poverty for Archaic Messenia; Classical and fourth-century ideology; [[concepts/spartan-mirage]] filters; archaeological difficulty of identifying status from survey alone.

## From the Oxford History of the Classical World (1986)

**Forrest (Ch.1, [[sources/boardman-oxford-history-classical-world-1986|OHCW 1986]]).** Early-seventh-century tradition credits Lycurgus with military training that made Sparta Greece’s most efficient power. Helots were **state-owned** quasi-slaves on allotted land; the individual Spartiate took half the produce; notional ~9,000 adult male Spartiates were outnumbered on the order of ~7:1. “The numbers of the breed and its real or imagined racial coherence established helot discontent, the danger of helot revolt, as the key to much of Spartan behaviour for centuries.” Homoioi “equals” were defined by survival of training, land allotment, and rule-obedience—groping toward citizenship as automatic rights amid shared helot fear/exploitation.

**Murray (Ch.9).** Theoretical elegance—“soldiers make slaves, slaves make soldiers, slaves need soldiers to suppress them”—impressed political thinkers as a **counter-ideal** to Athenian democracy. Syssitia contributions came from allotment land farmed by state-owned helot slaves, descendants of neighbouring communities constantly rebelling. Evidence bias note: Xenophon’s Sparta booklet can forget to mention helots because slavery was universal—silence where no Athens/Sparta contrast is needed.

**Hornblower (Ch.6).** Helots approximated more closely to medieval serfs than to ordinary Greek chattel slaves and were of **one nationality (Messenians)**—a core domestic reason for Sparta’s “reluctant” imperialism after 478. Leuctra (371) shattered the invincibility myth, but “its most important consequence for her was the Theban refoundation of Messenia as an independent state after many centuries of helotage (369),” after which Sparta sank to second-class rank (**`produced:`** loss of the economic base of the *agoge*; **`followed_by:`** [[events/liberation-of-messenia-369bce]]).
