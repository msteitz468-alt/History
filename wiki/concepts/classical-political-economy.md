# Classical Political Economy

The tradition of economic thought, founded by **[[adam-smith|Adam Smith]]** and developed across the late
eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, that established **economics as a systematic science** and supplied
the intellectual framework of **market capitalism** and **economic liberalism**.

## Core Ideas (emic)

- **The division of labour** as the source of rising productivity and the wealth of nations (Smith's
  pin-factory).
- **The self-regulating market** — prices, profit and competition coordinating production through the
  "**invisible hand**," so that the pursuit of self-interest can serve the common good.
- **Free trade and the critique of mercantilism** — wealth as production and exchange, not hoarded
  bullion; the case against protection and monopoly.
- **The labour theory of value** and the analysis of **distribution** among rent, profit and wages —
  developed by **Ricardo**; **Malthus's** population principle and the spectre of subsistence wages;
  **Say's** law of markets.
- A general faith in **economic laws** and, in most of the school, in limited government ("laissez-faire").

## Significance and Critique

Classical political economy shaped the **economic policy of the industrializing world** (free trade, the
gold standard, the repeal of the Corn Laws) and framed nineteenth-century debate about growth,
distribution and poverty. It was also the platform that its critics built upon and against: **Karl Marx**
turned the labour theory of value into a critique of capitalism; the **marginalist revolution** of the
1870s (Jevons, Menger, Walras) recast value around utility and superseded the classical framework with
neoclassical economics. Its assumptions — rational self-interest, the benevolence of markets, the
universality of "economic law" — remain contested in the history of economic thought.
