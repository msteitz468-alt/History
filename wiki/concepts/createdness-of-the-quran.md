## Definition and Scope

The doctrine of the createdness of the Qur'an (khalq al-Qur'an) holds that God's scripture — as His speech — was created in time rather than being coeternal with God. It is the most politically explosive application of the Mutazilite attributes doctrine (see [[divine-attributes-islam]]). The controversy culminated in the [[mihna-833]], the Abbasid inquisition in which the Caliph al-Ma'mun imposed the createdness doctrine and imprisoned or tortured scholars who refused it, most notably Ahmad ibn Hanbal.

Source: [[winter-cambridge-companion-islamic-theology-2008]], ch. 6 (Nader El-Bizri).

---

## The Mutazilite Argument

The Mutazilite case for the Qur'an's createdness proceeds from their core commitment to divine unity (tawhid). The argument may be reconstructed as follows:

1. The Qur'an is God's speech.
2. God's speech is either coeternal with God (uncreated) or not coeternal with God (created).
3. To maintain pure monotheism, one must avoid a "plurality of eternals" — anything coeternal with God would constitute a second eternal alongside God, compromising tawhid.
4. Therefore, the Qur'an must be created (makhluq).

This argument was "seconded by qur'anic proof-texts that point to the descent of revelation in the Arabic tongue that is constrained by place and time, as to its accessibility to finite human apprehension."

The Mutazilites further held that the divine attributes — including speech — are not real properties distinct from the essence (see [[mutazilism]]). If attributes are reduced to the essence, then "God's speech" names nothing distinct from God's essence; the scripture, as an artifact accessible to human ears and eyes in a specific language at a specific time, must therefore be temporal and created.

---

## The Problem the Argument Creates

The Mutazilite thesis was "ill founded on the same grounds that it presupposes." By asserting that whatever is sensory and temporal is created, the Mutazilites appeared to:

- Deny that the Qur'an is genuinely God's Word (if created, is it still divine?)
- Reduce divine transcendence to the measure of human reason ("the reality of divinity seems to be determinable by the judgements of human reason")
- Undermine the personal God of religious experience by emptying divine names of real content

As El-Bizri notes, "transcendence became paradoxically delimited by a negation of the attributes."

## Sunni Responses

### Hanbalite Position

The strictest Sunni response: the Qur'an is uncreated in every sense — not only its meaning but its voiced enunciation (lafziyya) and even its letters (harf) and sounds (sawt). Ibn Hanbal held that "God speaks with an uncreated voice (sawt) or letter (harf)." Accordingly, Hanbalites rejected the Asharite accommodation that the spoken recitation is created while the divine word itself is not.

Ibn Badran: "whosoever believes that any of these aforementioned matters are created, should be charged with infidelity, and whoever does not declare that person infidel, shall himself or herself be an infidel." Ibn Hanbal refused to pray behind or associate with anyone who held the lafziyya to be created.

### Asharite Resolution

[[al-ashari]] staked out a median position. His key distinction: "whatever is sensory is created." Therefore:

- The **sensible pronunciation** (lafziyya) in the recitation (qira'a) — the sounds produced by the human voice, the ink marks in manuscripts — **is created**.
- **Divine speech as such** (kalam Allah), as what is recited (maqru'), **is uncreated** and coeternal with God's essence.
- The **createdness of utterances** (huduth al-alfaz) is distinct from the **beginninglessness of their meanings** (qidam al-ma'ani).

This preserved God's eternal speech without affirming that human sounds and letters are eternal. Ash'ari added a regress argument: if the Qur'an were created, it was commanded into being by God's word "Be!" (kun). But then "Be!" would itself need to be commanded by another "Be!", ad infinitum — a purposeless infinite regress. Therefore God's words must be coeternal with Him.

In his Exposition of the Fundamentals of Faith (al-Ibana), Ash'ari affirmed: "God's words are not created." In his Kitab al-Luma', he specified: "God's speech is uncreated and is coeternal with His essence" — but sounds and letters are "created expressive traces of the uncreated divine word."

### Maturidi Position

[[al-maturidi]] and his school similarly affirmed the uncreatedness of divine speech while permitting greater rational elaboration. Abu Hanifa's tradition was invoked to ground this position in the earliest Sunni legal-theological synthesis.

### Late Kalam Synthesis

Al-Taftazani (d. 1390) affirmed: "the divine words were uncreated, and they resided in the divine essence, even though they are written in the volumes, preserved in the hearts, heard by the ears and recited by the tongues. The Qur'an as God's speech is also uncreated (ghayr makhluq), while its enunciation (lafziyya) is not eternal." He maintained that divine speech is "not of the genus of letters and sounds" and counted it as one of eight divine attributes alongside 'ilm, qudra, hayat, sam', basar, irada, and khalq.

## Connections to the Attributes Debate

The createdness controversy is strictly derivative from the divine attributes problem ([[divine-attributes-islam]]). The Mutazilite insistence that attributes (including speech) cannot be real distinct eternals drives the createdness conclusion. The Asharite position that attributes are real and coeternal without being separable from the essence drives the uncreated-speech conclusion.

The controversy also connects to:

- **Hermeneutics**: If divine speech is eternal, what is the status of its expression in a specific human language (Arabic)? The Qur'an is constrained by grammar, syntax, and idiom — yet these cannot be constraints on the eternal divine Word. The resolution (Asharite and Maturidi) is that the temporal Arabic expression is a created medium conveying an uncreated meaning.
- **Occasionalism**: The same Asharite insistence that God is the sole genuine agent connects to the view that God alone truly "speaks" — human recitation is a created act that conveys but does not constitute the divine word.

---

## Political Dimension

The [[mihna-833]] under al-Ma'mun, continued under al-Mu'tasim and al-Wathiq, made the createdness doctrine state policy and subjected scholars to interrogation and imprisonment. Ahmad ibn Hanbal's refusal to affirm the createdness became the defining act of Sunni resistance. The collapse of the Mihna under al-Mutawakkil (848) ended the political enforced of Mutazilism and marked the permanent defeat of the createdness doctrine as a Sunni option.

## Historiography

The khalq al-Qur'an controversy is well documented in primary sources (Ibn Abi Ya'la's Tabaqat al-Hanabila; Ash'ari's Ibana and Luma'; later kalam compendia) and extensively treated in modern scholarship on kalam (Van Ess's Theologie und Gesellschaft; Gimaret's La doctrine d'al-Ash'ari). The Bibliotheca Alexandrina collection is strong on this controversy through the kalam primary-source translations and specialist monographs. The political history of the Mihna is covered in standard Abbasid historiography.
