## Definition and Scope

This concept covers the argument that **shamanism was the original religion of
fully modern humans** — the religious system shared by our common ancestors before
the great post-Ice-Age cultural divergence. Ehret (Ch. 14) advances this on the
basis of comparative ethnography combined with the worldwide evidence of rock art.

## The Argument

In the shamanic belief system, existence is **bimodal**: it has two spheres —
the concrete world of everyday experience and a parallel realm of Spirit. The
surface of the earth forms the interface between them. The **shaman** (female or
male) is the religious and medical practitioner who taps into the world of Spirit
for the benefit (or sometimes the harm) of human beings, entering a state of
**trance** that transports them to the Spirit realm to engage Spirit power and
bring it back into the everyday world.

Ehret argues that the wide ethnographic distribution of shamanism in recent eras,
combined with rock art's apparent depiction of shamanic trance experience, points
to shamanism as the inherited religious system of all modern humans.

## The Rock Art Evidence

- Rock art in southern Africa (dating to before ~29,000 BCE at Apollo Cave,
  Namibia) and European cave art of similar age "seem to have had the trance
  experiences of shamans as their principal subject."
- Specific spirit figures and animals *differed* between regions (because humans
  everywhere are historical beings who developed different myths over time), but
  the art shares deep **structural features**:
  - Painted rock surfaces held special "numinous significance" as a *boundary*
    between the everyday world and the realm of Spirit.
  - In widely separated regions, painters depict animals *emerging from* or
    *entering* a crack in the rock — i.e., a spirit animal passing between the two
    worlds.
  - These widely shared motifs "draw on a more ancient heritage of ideas and
    imagery going back to our common ancestors in Africa."
- The use of sheltered rock surfaces as canvases for shamanist art occurs across
  the world, from southern Africa to the most distant reaches of human settlement.

This interpretation is tied to [[paleolithic-art-symbolic-culture]] — the
shamanic reading is the dominant explanation of *why* the art was made.

## Significance

If correct, shamanism represents a deep cultural universal — a shared religious
inheritance carried by modern humans out of Africa during the
[[paleolithic-global-dispersal]], surviving in recognizable form across tens of
thousands of years and every inhabited continent. It would be one of the clearest
cases of a Paleolithic cultural trait with continuous global descent, illustrating
the deep-time roots of cultural convergence/shared heritage (see
[[divergence-convergence]]).

## Emic vs. Etic

"Shamanism" is an etic category (the word derives from the Tungusic *šaman* of
Siberia, generalized by anthropologists to a worldwide type). Applying it to
Paleolithic peoples is an inference from later ethnography and from art, not from
any emic Paleolithic self-description.

## Limitations and Critiques

- The shamanistic interpretation of rock art (Lewis-Williams's "neuropsychological
  model") is influential but **contested**: critics argue it over-reads trance
  imagery into art that may have had other functions (hunting magic, totemism,
  narrative, decoration).
- "Shamanism" is a broad, possibly over-extended category lumping diverse
  religious practices; projecting a single "original religion" onto all Paleolithic
  humans risks homogenizing genuine diversity.
- Ethnographic analogy from recent foragers to Pleistocene peoples is methodolog-
  ically fraught.
- Direct evidence of Paleolithic belief is, by nature, absent; all reconstruction
  is inferential.
