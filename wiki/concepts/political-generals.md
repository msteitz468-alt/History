# Political Generals

"Political generals" refers to the practice, especially pronounced in the Union army of the American Civil War, of commissioning officers — often with little or no military training — because their political standing, ethnic-community influence, or capacity to recruit volunteers served the Lincoln administration's need for broad political and popular support for the war effort. Prominent examples include Benjamin Butler, Nathaniel Banks, Daniel Sickles, Franz Sigel (valued for his draw among German-American recruits), and John A. Logan.

The practice reflected the structural reality that the pre-war Regular Army was tiny and that raising a mass volunteer force required state governors and the federal government to co-opt prominent political figures as a condition of mobilizing manpower and sustaining Republican Party and War Democrat support for the war. Some political generals proved militarily capable despite their lack of formal training; others, most notoriously Butler and Banks, presided over costly defeats and were retained in command longer than their battlefield record would otherwise justify, generating persistent friction with West Point-trained professionals such as [[actors/mcclellan-george-b|George B. McClellan]], who viewed their promotions as corrosive to military effectiveness and discipline.

Historians treat the phenomenon as a case study in the tension, in a democratic war effort, between military meritocracy and the political coalition-building necessary to sustain a long, costly conflict — a tension Lincoln managed pragmatically rather than resolved.
