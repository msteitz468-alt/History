## The Thesis

Wael Hallaq's central argument in [[hallaq-sharia-2009]] is that modernity did not *reform* the [[sharia]] but structurally **destroyed** it — dismantled the social ecology, institutional infrastructure, hermeneutical tradition, and moral community that together constituted the Sharīa as a living system. The surviving nominal forms (constitutional articles, family law codes, Islamic courts, "Islamization" programs) are not a reformed or diminished version of the Sharīa; they are a fundamentally different phenomenon that happens to use the same name.

This is not a descriptive claim about legal decline but a structural and epistemological argument: modernity produced an **epistemic rupture** from which there is no return.

## What Was Destroyed

Hallaq opens his concluding chapter with an extended characterization of what the Sharīa *was* before its destruction:

> "It cannot be overstated that, for over a millennium, the Sharīa represented a complex set of social, economic, moral and cultural relations that permeated the epistemic structures of the social and political orders."

The Sharīa was not merely a body of rules but a **discursive practice** that:

- Embedded legal authority in the moral community and local social fabric (witness testimony presupposed knowledge of local honor and custom)
- Operated through epistemic rather than political authority (the [[qadi-judiciary]], the mufti, the professor — their authority was learned and moral, not derived from state appointment)
- Was financially independent through [[waqf]] endowments and [[madrasa]] structures
- Was characterized by productive pluralism ([[taqlid-and-ijtihad]], school divergence) rather than homogenizing uniformity
- Distinguished itself from modern law by being "an ijtihādic process, a continuously renewed exercise in hermeneutic … not 'a body of rules of action or conduct prescribed by [a] controlling authority'"

The fiqh was not "law" in the modern sense: "Individual opinions, strictly speaking, did not constitute law in the same sense in which we now understand the modern code, regulation or 'case law', nor was it the 'legal effect' of stating the will of a sovereign that the Muslim jurists intended to accomplish in any way."

## The Mechanism of Destruction

Hallaq identifies the **nation-state** as the agent of destruction, and [[colonial-transformation-of-islamic-law]] as the historical vehicle. The forces of destruction operated in tandem: "centralization, codification (in the widest sense of the word), bureaucratization, jural homogenization and — to ensure totalistic compliance — ubiquitous militarization, all of which are in fact the props of the modern state project."

The destruction operated through a two-step process:

### Step 1: Entexting
The first move was the reduction of living fiqh to fixed texts — initiated in **British India**, where colonial administration required predictable, written Islamic rules for use by English-trained judges. This "entexting" severed the Sharīa from its "anthropological and sociological legal past": once the living practice was fixed in texts, the entire supporting ecology of oral transmission, contextual adjudication, and community-embedded authority was made superfluous. "Entexting the Sharīa therefore had the effect of severing nearly all its ties with the anthropological and sociological legal past."

### Step 2: Transplantation and Politicization
The entexted fiqh residue was then transplanted into the structures of the modern state — constitutional articles, family law codes, sharia courts operating under state authority. This transplantation politicized the Sharīa in ways the pre-modern system had never known: "Whereas the pre-modern Sharīa and its fiqh operated largely outside dynastic rule, the entexted and transplanted fiqh had now come to be lodged within the structures of the state."

The result is what Hallaq calls an **aberrancy**: "when the Sharīa (however imagined) is reasserted in any Muslim country, as happened, for instance, in Iran in 1979 and thereafter, the entexted conception combines with another conception of state-appropriated law to produce an aberrancy, one whose domestic advocates (seeking legitimacy) and external foes (seeking condemnation of Islamic revolutionary regimes) are equally happy, though for entirely different reasons, to call what ensues 'Sharīa.'"

## Irreversibility

The most radical dimension of the thesis is its claim of irreversibility. The conditions that made the Sharīa possible — a moral community, a financially independent juristic profession, non-state sources of legal authority, an Arabicate hermeneutical tradition transmitted through living scholars — "no longer exist" and cannot be reconstructed within the modern state:

> "If the way to the law is through the state, then neither Sharīa nor fiqh can ever be restored, reenacted, or refashioned (by Islamists or ulama of any type or brand) without the agency of the state. More importantly, none of these restorative options can be realized without the contaminating influence of the state, rendering extinct the distinctiveness of pre-modern Sharīa as a non-state, community-based, bottom-up jural system. This distinctiveness would be impossible to replicate."

This is not a counsel of despair about Islamic law but a theoretical claim about the structural dependency of legal systems on their social ecologies. The Sharīa presupposed and was produced by "a moral community which law, in its operation, took as granted" — that community has been dissolved by the same modernizing forces that dismantled the law.

## The Identity Paradox

Hallaq acknowledges a further paradox: the destroyed Sharīa has become more important as an **identity marker** precisely because it was destroyed. The surviving residue — family law codes, constitutional references, calls for Islamization — functions in a "politics that the pre-modern counterpart did not know." The Sharīa is now primarily a political symbol, not a functioning legal order: "The Sharīa has become a marker of modern identity, engulfed by modern notions of culture and politics (but, ironically, much less by law)."

This politicization is structurally inherent. Islamist movements calling for the restoration of the Sharīa are themselves products of the modern state system they oppose; they have no access to the "anthropological past" of the pre-modern Sharīa and would inevitably reproduce state-law structures if they achieved power (as demonstrated by Iran, Pakistan, and Sudan).

## The Failure of Modern Islamic Legal Thought

Chapter 17 provides the evidentiary foundation for the irreversibility claim: Hallaq surveys modern attempts to construct a replacement methodology — neo-ijtihad, [[maqasid-al-sharia]], the Double Movement — and finds them all insufficient. The Būṭī–Marzūqī dialogue (2006) demonstrates that even committed scholars cannot agree on a common framework, exhibiting "the persistent paralysis created by" the 19th-century epistemic break. "The differences as well as the intersecting similarities between and among all these thinkers not only defy neat classification but at once also affirm their failure to provide indigenous solutions to the epistemic havoc wrought by modernity."

## Scholarly Context and Reception

The thesis is a sharp intervention against:

1. **Reform narratives** — Western and Muslim liberal scholarship that interprets post-colonial legal changes as "reform" of Islamic law toward more rational or equitable forms.
2. **Orientalist decline narratives** — the traditional view that Islamic law declined because of the "closure of the gate of ijtihad" (a myth Hallaq had dismantled in earlier work).
3. **Islamist restoration projects** — the belief that the Sharīa can be restored through political will and legislative effort.

Hallaq's argument is controversial because it implies that *all* modern Islamic states — secular and Islamic alike — operate the same structural transformation, and that calls for "implementation of the Sharīa" are, at the level of institutional reality, incoherent.
