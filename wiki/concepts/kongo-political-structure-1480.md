## Definition and Scope

MacGaffey's analysis (Chapter 2 of [[bostoen-brinkman-kongo-kingdom-2018|Bostoen & Brinkman 2018]]) offers a structural reconstruction of the [[kingdom-of-kongo|Kingdom of Kongo]] as it existed around 1480 — three years before Portuguese contact — by reading backwards from modern Central African ethnography and early-modern documentary sources simultaneously. His core argument is that Kongo scholarship has been distorted by four persistent bad intellectual habits imported from European social science, and that correcting these habits reveals a polity fundamentally different from how it has usually been represented.

This concept page synthesizes MacGaffey's structural model of Kongo political culture, which he argues was not an exceptional creation but an emergent instance of a region-wide Central African political tradition. The model stands in productive tension with [[kongo-origins-c1390|Thornton's origins narrative]] (Chapter 1 of the same volume): while Thornton argues Kongo was already a relatively centralized polity in the early documentary record, MacGaffey emphasizes the cosmological and decentralized foundations that persisted beneath whatever administrative superstructure existed.

## The Four Bad Habits MacGaffey Critiques

MacGaffey opens by identifying four analytical distortions that have shaped Kongo historiography:

1. **Tribal isolationism**: Treating societies as singular, bounded units rather than as versions of a shared regional system. Vansina (1990) and Ekholm (1977) are exceptions, analyzing Congo-basin societies as a single interconnected system.

2. **The state/stateless dichotomy**: The colonial-era assumption that states required a special causative factor (the "Hamite" theory) has been replaced by an overcorrection that celebrates "empires" as predecessors of modern states while neglecting other forms of political organization.

3. **Rational vs. irrational action**: Enlightenment-derived assumption that "religion" and "ritual" are separate from and subordinate to rational political action. MacGaffey argues that in Central African thought, no such divide existed — occult forces were "objects of positive knowledge" just as material forces were. Assimilating African political cosmology into European categories of "the supernatural" distorts the record.

4. **Descent-rule determinism**: The assumption that social organization was governed by fixed rules of descent and succession rather than being continuously produced by political struggle. MacGaffey argues rules are made by the winners, not handed down.

## The Central African Cultural Tradition

MacGaffey draws on Vansina's model of a hegemonic tradition originating 4,000 years ago among Western Bantu speakers, diffused across a zone including Zambia but not east of the Great Lakes. This tradition began with the cognitive assumption that life should be regular; exceptional success or misfortune was attributed to *occult* forces (using *occult* in its original sense of "not ordinarily visible," not the later European sense of "supernatural"). The source of these forces was the land of the dead (*nsi a bafwa*), a place physically accessible to those who knew the way.

### The Four Expert Roles

Throughout this tradition, four categories of expert in the occult operated as a contrast set, defining each other by mutual opposition:

| Role | Sphere | Public/Private | Orientation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Headman** (*n'kuluntu*) | Social reproduction | Public | Hierarchical |
| **Witch** (*kindoki*) | Social reproduction | Private | Individual gain |
| **Earth-chief** (*nganga nsi*) | Production | Public | Community prosperity |
| **Healer** (*nganga*) | Production | Private | Individual remedy |

MacGaffey argues this is "not just a collection of superstitions, but a matrix of legitimate governance, an indigenous guide to political practice." The headman and earth-chief competed and cooperated across the divide of time (social order, ancestry) and space (land, nature's productivity). This explains why, across Central Africa, the story of political centralization typically involves a "stranger-chief" conqueror from elsewhere who relegates the prior earth-chief to responsibility for agricultural fertility — a structural narrative of the merger of the two spheres.

## The House as Basic Social Unit

The basic unit of production and social reproduction in Kongo c. 1480 was the **House**: a bilateral descending kindred several generations deep, consisting of a headman (*n'kuluntu*), his wives and resident children and grandchildren, together with affines, clients, and slaves. Wealth was measured not in land but in people (*mbongo bantu*). "[Not land, but] slaves were the only form of private, revenue producing property recognized in African law" (Thornton 1998, cited by MacGaffey).

Kinship terminology was non-genealogical in European terms: relationships were labeled contextually, allowing the same person to stand in multiple relations. Slaves could be called *mwana* (child) of their owner; so could apprentices.

## Matrilineality: A Later Development

One of MacGaffey's most consequential arguments is that **Kongo in 1480 did not have matrilineal descent groups**. The assumption that it did rested on:

- The lingering evolutionary assumption that matrilineality was a universal "stage" of social development.
- The linguistic error of supposing that the word *kanda* in early Kongo texts denoted a matriclan, when in fact *kanda* could refer to any group or category.
- Anne Hilton's (1985) error, which MacGaffey argues was based on misreading Van Gheel's 1652 dictionary.

MacGaffey contends that matrilineality in Kongo was a product of the [[atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic slave trade]] from the late seventeenth century onward. As the Atlantic market preferred male slaves, coastal regions accumulated a surplus of residual women who could be acquired without reciprocal wife-exchange. Women and their offspring were reckoned as "matrilineage" members of their male owner — but these groups were lineally heterogeneous. "Matrilineal descent, from the eighteenth century to the twentieth century, was the product of patriarchal ambition, not of rules of descent and succession." Maps in Vansina (1990) show that the geographic limits of the Atlantic slave trade correlate closely with the distribution of matrilineal descent in the Congo basin.

## The Nature of Kingship

MacGaffey argues that African and specifically Central African political leadership depended not on "imperative control backed by armed force" (as in European models) but on **manifesting occult power** (*kindoki*). Warfare between chiefs was typically limited and ceremonial — an ordeal concluding after the first casualty, with payment in slaves — rather than territorial conquest. Clients and adherents were *attracted* rather than *compelled*; they sought to share in the chief's power by imitating court etiquette, copying fashions, and acquiring *min'kisi* (titles, trade privileges, association memberships) in exchange for ritual payments that European observers called "tribute."

Once initiated, a chief became a sort of human *n'kisi* (*corps-fétiche* in de Heusch's phrase): subject to behavioral restrictions, but also empowered to impose restrictions on others and collect fines for infractions. His real power depended on local conditions and political skill. All chiefs were at least partly controlled by those who had managed their initiation.

**Centralization is thus a secondary and contingent feature** of the Central African political system, not its defining characteristic. MacGaffey argues that "why a system of petty states should have given rise at [[mbanza-kongo|Mbanza Kongo]] to a large, slave-based center is still unknown" (citing Thornton 1982). The Mwene Kongo in 1480 was *primus inter pares* among a network of principalities, not an absolute sovereign.

## The Provincial System and Its Cosmological Basis

The provincial structure of Kongo — with its elaborate hierarchy of governor-titles, the special status of [[mbata-province-kongo|Mbata]] as co-regent, and the roles of Mwene Vunda and Mwene Kabunga as electors and earth-priests — is, in MacGaffey's model, best understood as a scaled-up version of the village-level contest between headman and earth-chief. The investiture ceremony, in which a priest (*kitomi*, or on the royal level the Mwene Kabunga/Kavunga or Mwene Vunda) initiated the political chief by connecting him to the forces of the earth, united the spheres of production and reproduction.

"Demonstrations of such power by pomp and circumstance and, on occasion, by spectacular executions, *attracted* rather than *compelled* clients and adherents, who sought to share in it by imitating court etiquette, copying its fashions, and acquiring parts of it in the form of *min'kisi* (titles, trade privileges, membership in closed associations) in exchange for ritual payments that European chroniclers later called 'tribute.'"

## Kongo and Christianity

MacGaffey argues that when Portuguese missionaries arrived in 1483 and 1491, the Kongo court interpreted baptism within its existing cosmological categories. The Mwene Kongo and Mwene Soyo initially sought to monopolize the new power; when pressed by the nobility for access, Kongo became a Christian kingdom in which a Catholic missionary performed the role of earth-priest (*kitomi*) at royal investiture. European priests performed all the public and private roles expected of *banganga*: baptism as initiation ceremony, religious medals as protective charms, agricultural blessings appealing to earth-spirits (*bisimbi*), and royal consecration.

MacGaffey observes: "The missionary and the African understood each other better than most of us can understand either" (quoting Hastings 1994). The resulting syncretic Christianity ignored central European doctrines — Original Sin, Immaculate Conception, *ex nihilo* creation — and understood salvation pragmatically as a source of solutions to this-worldly problems. Missionaries could find no Kikongo word for "virgin" and had to adopt a word meaning "to renew a *n'kisi*" for "to create."

## Decline and Reversion

Broadhead (1979), cited approvingly by MacGaffey, argued that the late-seventeenth-century "decline" of Kongo following the civil wars and the [[battle-of-mbwila-1665|Battle of Mbwila (1665)]] was not so much a collapse as a *reversion*: the post-civil-war structure, in which the central authority presided over "groupings of regional powers, each being merely the center of a group of independent potentates, and all being tied together by a system of alliances and marriages" (Thornton 1983: 114), closely resembled the structure of 1480, when the Mwene Kongo was *primus inter pares* and government was more a matter of ritual affiliation than administration.

The [[kongo-decentralization|process of decentralization]] thus, in MacGaffey's framing, was not a departure from Kongo's essential political character but a return to it after an exceptional interlude of centralization driven by Portuguese alliance, Atlantic trade revenues, and Christian institutional frameworks.

## Limitations and Critique

MacGaffey acknowledges his reconstruction is "necessarily speculative, since archaeological and other 'facts' are almost entirely lacking." His method — reading backwards from modern ethnography to reconstruct 1480 — risks conflating different time periods, a criticism that applies even when checking against nineteenth- and seventeenth-century materials.

Thornton explicitly contests MacGaffey's characterization of Kongo in 1480 as primarily a ritual/symbolic polity. Thornton argues the pre-1550 documentary record shows nobles removed by royal decree, incomes derived from royally-assigned appointments, state-distributed money, and substantial royal military resources. The debate remains open. It can be framed as a question of which Kongo sources are taken as primary: the earliest European visitor accounts (which support Thornton's more centralized reading) or the structural patterns visible across the broader Central African ethnographic record (which support MacGaffey's reading).
