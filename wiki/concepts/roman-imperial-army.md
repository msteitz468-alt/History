# The Roman Imperial Army and Navy (Augustus to Nero)

The **standing professional army** created by **[[augustus|Augustus]]** out of the legions of
the civil wars — the institution that defended, paid for, and ultimately could *make* the
[[principate|Principate]]. Treated in [[bowman-cah-v10-1996|CAH X]] Ch. 11 (**Lawrence
Keppie**).

## From civil-war host to standing army

The late-Republican army was a fluctuating force (15+ legions of citizen conscripts, raised and
disbanded by the year, with no permanent army list, commanded by the provincial governor
through rotating tribunes). The **civil wars of 49–30 BCE** multiplied it — Caesar fielded some
36–38 legions; the triumvirs revived disbanded units for **Philippi** — and lengthened service.
After Actium, Octavian could "pose as reuniting the old Caesarian army under himself," settling
his veterans in **28 Italian colonies** (and Antony's in the provinces). The legions that
emerged became **permanent, self-perpetuating formations** lasting three centuries.

## The Augustan settlement of the legions

By mid-reign there were **28 legions** (the sequence I–XXII with duplicated numerals fixed by
25 BCE; later theories of a steady wartime build-up "can be set aside"), stationed in the
**frontier provinces**, mostly the emperor's. After the **[[battle-of-teutoburg-forest-9ce|loss
of XVII, XVIII, XIX]]** (AD 9), the total settled at **25** — *not* raised to match the losses
or the wider frontiers, "the financial burden was simply too great."

Augustus regularized the soldier's life to remove the uncertainties that had driven civil-war
agitation:
- **Fixed terms of service** — 16 years (set 13 BCE, the old Republican maximum), raised to
  **20 years plus 5 in reserve** in AD 5.
- **Fixed cash discharge bounty** (the *praemia*) — replacing the politically toxic land grants
  in Italy (3,000 denarii by AD 5). Annual legionary pay was 900 sesterces.
- A new **military treasury (*aerarium militare*, AD 6)** to fund the bounties, primed by
  Augustus himself and fed by the unpopular **5% inheritance tax** and the 1% auction tax.
- A **ban on soldiers' marriage** during service.
- A reformed **command structure** for now-permanent units: a *legatus legionis* appointed
  directly by the emperor for several years, an equestrian *praefectus castrorum*, and (in
  senator-free Egypt) legions commanded by the prefect of the camp.

Keppie stresses that these terms were a perennial **grievance**: the legionaries' demands at the
**mutinies of AD 14** (release at 16 years, cash on discharge, against long service and harsh
centurions) show that "Augustus and his successors did not always live up to their
responsibilities."

## Auxilia

Defence was "shared between Rome and her subject peoples." The **auxilia** — non-citizen
infantry **cohortes** and cavalry **alae** (and mixed *cohortes equitatae*), c. 500 strong,
named for their tribe of origin (e.g. *cohors VI Nerviorum*) — were recruited from the newly
won provinces, often stationed near home, equipped in their native fashion, and commanded by
prefects (frequently tribal nobles like **Arminius**, made citizen and *eques* for his
service — before he turned it against Rome). By AD 23 Tacitus reckoned them roughly **equal in
number to the legions (~150,000)**. Their conditions were less regularized (no fixed term or
automatic bounty); from Claudius, **citizenship** became the standard cheap reward. Heavy
reliance on recently subjected peoples carried risk — the **Pannonian revolt of AD 6** was
fuelled by a concentration of Dalmatian auxiliaries who "saw a chance to throw off the Roman
yoke."

## The navy

The civil wars proved the value of a permanent fleet. Octavian, who had built his navy "almost
from scratch" to beat Sextus Pompeius (Mylae, Naulochus) and Antony (Actium), established two
standing fleets — at **Misenum** (western Mediterranean) and **Ravenna** (Adriatic/Aegean) —
totalling perhaps **15,000–20,000 men** and 75–100 ships (chiefly triremes). The strong
**Greek** maritime tradition is reflected in the ships' names and the officer titles
(trierarch, navarch, *praefectus classis*).

## The High Empire: organization and the turn to frontier defence (CAH XI, Hassall)

[[bowman-cah-v11-2000|CAH XI]] Ch. 9 (**M. Hassall**) describes the mature army of AD 70–192 — by
Trajan's reign **30 legions** (c. 165,000 men), with auxiliaries and the fleets, *numeri*, and
Rome garrison bringing the total close to **half a million**: "the largest standing army the
world had yet known," and the best trained and equipped.

- **The legion.** c. 5,500 men: nine cohorts of six 80-man centuries, plus (from the late first
  century, as the Inchtuthil fortress shows) a **double-strength first cohort** of five 160-man
  centuries, plus 120 cavalry. Commanded by a senatorial *legatus* (combined with the governorship
  in single-legion provinces), with the experienced equestrian *praefectus castrorum*, a young
  *tribunus laticlavius*, five equestrian *tribuni angusticlavii*, and **59 long-service
  centurions** (25+ years) capped by the *primus pilus* — the *primi ordines* the professional
  backbone, paid ~16× the ranker.
- **The auxilia.** Now **over 380 regiments**, outnumbering the legions in manpower: *cohortes*
  (quingenary ~480 / milliary ~800), cavalry *alae*, and the versatile part-mounted **cohortes
  equitatae** — flexibly brigaded and split (the Antonine Wall forts, the Vindolanda tablets). Pay
  ran one and a half to two *aurei* below the legionary in graded steps (cohort foot < part-mounted
  trooper < *ala* trooper). Domitian raised army pay (legionary 9→12 *aurei*) c. AD 84.
- **Citizenship and the diploma.** The auxiliary's reward for 25 years was **Roman citizenship**
  and *conubium*, certified on a bronze **diploma** — "the only mechanism by which citizenship was
  regularly extended in the frontier provinces." A change of AD 140 withdrew the retrospective
  grant to children, probably because their sons now received citizenship on enlistment, like
  legionaries' sons. Recruitment shifted from the Italian/Narbonese colonies toward **provincial
  and local** sources (and soldiers' own sons), the ethnic character of units fading as they
  settled.
- **From field army to frontier garrison.** Hadrian's turn to **consolidation behind fixed
  frontiers** redefined the army's primary role as **frontier defence**: auxiliaries and *numeri*
  manning the *limites*, watchtowers, and rear policing, legions as local backup. The system's
  **weakness was the absence of a strategic reserve** — met only by stripping one frontier for
  another or raising new legions (Marcus' **II and III Italica**, AD 165) — a problem not solved
  until Constantine's mobile field army. Discipline tracked the emperor (rigorous under the
  camp-sharing Hadrian, decayed under [[commodus]]).
- **Army and society.** In the frontier provinces the army's pay and demand reshaped local
  economies (Samian ware, grain *paid for*), soldiers undertook civil works, census-taking, and
  district administration (*centuriones regionarii*, *beneficiarii*), and intermarriage and local
  recruitment steadily dissolved the line between garrison and province — "converting barbarians
  into loyal citizens." See [[roman-frontiers]].

## Significance

Augustus' army was the structural foundation of the Principate: a **de-politicized,
professional force** loyal to the emperor who paid and discharged it (the soldiers "thought of
both their pay and gratuities as coming direct from the emperor"). But its cost dominated the
[[imperial-finances|imperial budget]], its conditions bred recurrent mutiny, and its latent
power to choose emperors — masked while the Julio-Claudian succession held — was revealed in the
[[year-of-the-four-emperors-69ce|civil war of 68–69]].
