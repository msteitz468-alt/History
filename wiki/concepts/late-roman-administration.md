# The Late Roman State and Administration

The transformation of Roman imperial government from the [[severan-dynasty|Severans]] to Constantine —
the **"new state"** of the [[roman-tetrarchy|tetrarchy]] and its aftermath: a reorganized provincial
hierarchy, an enlarged bureaucracy, a reformed fiscal system, and a new ideology of imperial power.
Treated in [[bowman-cah-v12-2005|CAH XII]] Ch. 6 (**Elio Lo Cascio**, 6a–6d).

## "Fracture and continuity": the revisionist frame

Lo Cascio's governing argument (6a) challenges the old "principate → dominate" watershed model. The
late-antique state was, he argues, **far less bureaucratized, dirigiste, and totalitarian** than the
catastrophist tradition holds; much of that image is an artefact of the **sources** (the abundant
fourth-century legal codes — *Codex Theodosianus* — versus the meagre third-century narrative).
Bureaucracy did grow and the ruling class turned over (the equestrian order eventually vanishing as a
distinct *ordo*), but **social mobility remained high**, the "caste system" of late antiquity was
never fully realized, and the state's "oppressive" economic presence was limited to a few spheres
(provisioning Rome, Constantinople, and the armies). The age from Severus to Constantine was "an age
of both **fracture and continuity**."

## Severan legitimation and the army (6b)

The Principate had **no constitutional solution to succession** (a "duplication of legal systems" —
the *res publica* of the *populus* beside the personal administration of the *princeps*). The
Severans relied on **dynastic ideology** ([[septimius-severus|Severus']] self-adoption into the
Antonine house; the elevation of the *domus* to *sacra*/*divina*; the extraordinary prominence of the
**Severan women** — Julia Domna *mater castrorum*, Julia Mamaea *mater senatus, patriae, generis
humani*). The army was the decisive power: Severus dissolved the old praetorians, stationed a legion
(II Parthica) at **Albano** near Rome, and gave **equestrian prefects** command of his new legions —
not (Lo Cascio insists) from anti-senatorial policy but from circumstance.

## The crisis decades (6c)

During the "anarchy," **dynastic continuity broke down** and legitimation increasingly sought a
**sacred/religious** basis (from Elagabalus' sun-god to Aurelian's Sol Invictus — anticipating
Constantine's use of Christianity). Power was **multiplied and distributed** (co-emperors holding
different fronts), prefiguring the tetrarchy. [[gallienus|Gallienus']] reforms accelerated the
**professionalization of command** and the replacement of senatorial by equestrian governors in armed
provinces (datable to c. 262) — again, Lo Cascio stresses, a pursuit of **efficiency**, not a
princeps-vs-senate struggle. Other developments: large inter-provincial "**provinces of war**," the
*annona militaris*, attempts at fairer taxation (Philip), the **end of monetary pluralism** (local
coinages ceasing; mints multiplying near the frontiers), and the relentless **debasement** of the
silver coinage to its nadir under Claudius II. Rome itself, its *annona* reorganized by the Severans,
**declined as the centre of power** once emperors resided on the frontiers — while Italy moved, via
the *correctores*, toward **provincialization**.

## The new state of Diocletian and Constantine (6d)

The [[roman-tetrarchy|tetrarchy]] gave **official form to a territorial partition** of imperial duties
and a **new legitimation** (Iovius/Herculius; the *adoratio*; the *consilium* becoming the
*consistorium*) — drawing on eastern ceremonial yet, Lo Cascio argues, **essentially conservative**.
The administrative reforms (much more innovative at the **periphery** than the centre) were a
coherent response to the threat of disintegration:

- **Provinces** more than doubled (from ~48 to over 100; *Laterculus Veronensis*), now mostly under
  equestrian *praesides*, with **military command separated** and given to *duces*. Italy and Egypt
  lost their special status; Italy lost its **tax immunity**.
- Provinces grouped into **twelve dioceses** under *vicarii* (the great fiscal districts), themselves
  later grouped under the **praetorian prefects** — transformed by Constantine (after disbanding the
  praetorians in 312) into **purely civilian "viceroys."**
- A reorganized **fiscal system**: the *iugatio–capitatio* assessment (land-units *iuga* and
  head-units *capita*), regularized **levies in kind** (*annona*) to pay an army insulated from
  currency depreciation, and the first imperial **"budget."** (The coinage reforms and Price Edict are
  treated more fully under [[roman-money-and-finance|coinage and the economy]].)
- At the **centre**, bureaucratization was completed under Constantine: four "ministers" in the
  *consistorium* — the **quaestor sacri palatii** (legislation), the **comes sacrarum largitionum**
  (money/finance), the **comes rerum privatarum** (imperial estates), and the **magister officiorum**
  (the bureaucracy) — with the **praepositus sacri cubiculi** over the palace, and the system recorded
  in the *Notitia Dignitatum*.

Lo Cascio repeatedly notes the **continuity** between tetrarchic and Constantinian administration
(the sharp contrast lies in religion, not government), and that even the enlarged bureaucracy remained
**thin** by modern standards (roughly one official per 5,000–10,000 inhabitants).

## Significance

The "new state" was the **administrative architecture of late antiquity** — provinces, dioceses,
prefectures, a civilian-military split, a salaried bureaucracy, and a fiscal system geared to the
army — that endured, with modification, to Justinian. Lo Cascio's revisionism reframes it not as a
totalitarian "dominate" rupturing the Principate but as a **radicalization of pre-existing
tendencies** under crisis pressure.

## Update — government in operation, 425–600 (CAH XIV)

[[cameron-cah-v14-2000|CAH XIV]] (Whitby, Ch. 7) stresses that the late empire developed, **for the first
time in Roman history, genuinely complex bureaucratic structures** — the very thing that let immobile,
palace-bound emperors retain authority without campaigning. Administration and the **decline of the
[[late-roman-cities|curial class]]** stood in a **cyclical relationship**: as city councils surrendered their
traditional tasks (revenue-raising, transmitting imperial orders, keeping local order), the central
bureaucracy grew more intrusive to compensate — which further weakened the curiae. **Active** reforming
emperors (Anastasius, Justinian) could dominate and overhaul the machine but "raised hackles"; **passive**
ones were despised for neglect. The codes themselves measure government's reach: a quarter of the
[[codification-of-roman-law|Theodosian Code]] (and of Justinian's) concerns administrative duties, precedence
and the *fisc*. Whitby's overall verdict is of **gradual development, not crisis** — the eastern system
worked well enough to underwrite Justinian's wars and survive into the seventh century. Source:
[[cameron-cah-v14-2000]] (Ch. 7, Michael Whitby).

## Harris, *Roman Power* (2016)

Harris traces a long-term shift from somewhat haphazard republican methods to **more systematic, routine-based procedures** operated by expert officials—at least until mid-third-century military crises—then a tetrarchic–Constantinian intensification. Diocletian revived provincial census-taking; radically reorganized personal/property taxation (privileged communities lost immunity); split ~50 provincial units into ~100; organized dioceses of 8–9 provinces (attested 314) to facilitate tax-gathering; reformed currency; commissioned the *Codex Gregorianus* and *Hermogenianus*; reorganized recruitment (landowner recruit obligation; sons of veterans; settle non-Romans for service). *Frumentarii* were replaced by *agentes in rebus* (establishment later limited, e.g. 1,174 in *CTh* 6.27.23 of 430). Bureaucracy debate: amateurism at the top coexisted with expertise lower down; census complexity crescendo under the Severans (Ulpian *Digest* 50.15.4 detailed property declarations). After Caracalla, governors held *ius gladii* over all citizens (*Digest* 1.18.6.9); short terms satisfied many ambitions.

Post-337, Harris rejects both “huge fatal corruption” and minimizer positions; rapid high-office turnover was a serious obstacle (e.g. 32 praetorian prefects of Italy/Africa 407–455 on incomplete lists). Jones’s ~30,000 regular officials was “not vast” relative to empire size; under Stilicho and Fl. Constantius the empire was **less not more centralized** than under earlier emperors. Curials/decurions under fourth-century pressure for tax revenue and community duties (*CTh* 12.1 longest chapter); local amenities tended to deteriorate. Justin II’s cession of governor nomination to bishops and principal inhabitants signals diminished imperial power. Source: [[harris-roman-power-2016]].

## From Gibbon, Decline and Fall (1776–1788)

[[actors/gibbon-edward|Gibbon]] reads Diocletian–Constantine administration as the internal machinery of decay. **Gibbon’s views.**

- **Twin principles:** “Ostentation was the first principle of the new system instituted by Diocletian. The second was division” — empire, provinces, every civil and military branch; more wheels, less rapid, more secure.
- **Illustrates causes of decay:** policy introduced by Diocletian, improved by Constantine, completed by successors “will tend to illustrate the secret and internal causes of its rapid decay” (to Theodosian Code / *Notitia*).
- **Civil/military separation:** “The divided administration which had been formed by Constantine, relaxed the vigor of the state, while it secured the tranquillity of the monarch.”
- **116 provinces:** “the whole empire was distributed into one hundred and sixteen provinces, each of which supported an expensive and splendid establishment.”
- **Four civil prefects:** after Praetorians suppressed and military command removed, “the captains of the guards were transformed into the civil magistrates of the provinces” (East, Illyricum, Italy, Gauls).
- **Torture under treason maxim:** in treason “all privileges were suspended, and all conditions were reduced to the same ignominious level”; magic-treason inquisitions under Valentinian/Valens.
- **Latin oblivion after Justinian:** “from his reign we may date the gradual oblivion of the Latin tongue” — easterners reject the name “Greeks” yet cling to succession from Augustus.

## Woolf, *Rome: An Empire's Story* (2012)

Source: [[woolf-rome-empires-story-2012]].

- From “government without bureaucracy” (tiny senatorial/equestrian cadre over city elites held by shared aristocratic culture) to much larger fourth-century bureaucracy recruited from educated non-aristocrats; complex ranks focused on praetorian prefects and frontier-zone courts.
- Diocletian increased province numbers and grouped them under prefects; eventually four great prefectures each managing roughly a quarter of empire and a wing of tax/government bureaucracy, with less involvement from senators or local elites. Helped extract more revenue for slightly larger armies; **unintentionally** made some regions effectively self-sufficient (local taxes for local defence), easing later fragmentation.
- **Notitia Dignitatum** (late fourth, revised early fifth): illustrated hierarchy—data a centralized empire would need, but expensive illustration and rapid obsolescence show idealized panorama of power; includes west and east when empire effectively divided.
- **Theodosian Code** (435–38): capacity to plan/execute shows bureaucracy could produce results; need to request edict copies from provincial governors shows “astonishing lack of record keeping”; still treats empire as one in the 430s despite post-Adrianople disasters.

## From Mitchell, Later Roman Empire (2015)

Source: [[mitchell-later-roman-empire-2015]].

- **Petition-and-response, not modern legislature.** The Theodosian Code (~2,500 rescripts/edicts since Constantine, 16 books, published 437 to stress east–west constitutional unity) creates a false impression of pro-active imperial legislation; late emperors largely continued the petition-and-response pattern of the principate. Laws made in one half were not regularly disseminated to the other—the Code makes legislation look more universal than practice was.
- **Justinianic Corpus.** Code 529/534, Digest and Institutes 533 (Tribonian’s commission) subsumed pre-Constantinian rulings and superseded previous legislation; Mitchell calls it among the most astonishing monuments of Justinian’s age—though imperial rhetoric often reveals ideology more than applied case practice.
- **Diocletianic foreshadowing.** Gregorian (~292) and Hermogenian (295) codes first systematic codification attempts; Latin for all imperial edicts even in the East (Prices Edict known from eastern copies few locals could understand).
- **Praetorian prefecture.** Evolved from guard command to senior civil deputy: tetrarchic one-per-ruler; Constantine end-reign five prefects; after 337 regional prefectures (Oriens; Galliae; Italiae/Africae/Illyricum). Central administration = emperor + chief ministers under strict protocol, replicated by provincial *iudices* and diocesan vicars; decisions largely reactive.
- **Julian’s civic reforms.** Restored cities’ revenue rights, enlarged councils, tried to reduce tax demands and curial burdens—shoring up *polis* government eroded by state intrusion, rising taxes, and enlarged bureaucracy; “putting the clock back” was not limited to religion.
- **Practical rule (Edessa ~497–505).** Anastasius remits *chrysargyron*; bishop and governor as sole effective petitioners for aid; governors use violence to extract tax; tax remissions reserved to the emperor personally (CJust. 10.16.13). Zeno’s 479 law required officials to remain accessible 50 days after office for civil complaints—even amid eastern anarchy, showing internalized ideology of good governance.
- **Civic evolution 300–600.** *Curiales* still define fourth-century cities mainly as liturgy/tax/troop-supply agents while governors run justice and public works. After ~450 power shifts to *possessores/ktetores*, *patēr tēs poleōs* (centrally appointed), and bishops—control by powerful individuals and religious leaders rather than elected constitutional structures. Military mobility rested on roads, state transport liturgies, and increasingly maritime logistics centered on Constantinople; major-road maintenance and official-transport requisition continued into Justinian’s reign. Church-council acts (Ephesus, Chalcedon, Carthage 411) are a real-time window on Theodosian governance and church–state networks.
