# The Biblical Hittites

The term **"Hittite"** as used by modern scholars for the Late Bronze Age kingdom of
[[hittites|Hatti]] is borrowed from the Hebrew Bible, where a people called *Hittites*
(singular *hittî*, plural *hittîm*; the "sons of Heth," *bĕnê Het*) appear centuries
after that kingdom's fall. The German *Hethiter* — literally "sons of Heth" — preserves
the borrowing transparently. [[sources/bryce-world-neo-hittite-kingdoms-2012|Bryce
(2012)]] devotes a chapter to the resulting **identification problem**: what, if any,
connection links the Bible's Hittites to the Bronze Age empire and to its Iron Age
[[neo-hittite-states|Neo-Hittite successors]]?

## The biblical material (emic terms)

Two distinct biblical "Hittite" groups must be separated:

- **The Palestinian "Hittites"** of the patriarchal narratives and the Table of Nations
  — the "sons of Heth," with Heth a son of Canaan (Gen. 10:15). They live in the Judaean
  hill-country, sell Abraham the cave of Machpelah (Ephron the Hittite, Gen. 23), and are
  listed among the peoples Israel is to destroy (Deut. 20:16–17). Crucially, **all their
  attested personal names are Semitic** (Ephron, Zohar, Beeri, Elon) — with the possible
  exception of **Uriah the Hittite**, David's officer (2 Sam. 11). These look nothing like
  Anatolians.
- **The northern "land of the Hittites"** — a geographic usage (e.g. the kings "of the
  Hittites" hired by Israel, the territory "from the wilderness... to the great river"),
  reflecting the Iron Age survival of the name **Hatti** for north Syria, the zone of the
  Syro-Hittite states.

## The historiographical question (etic problem)

Bryce lays out four logical possibilities for the Palestinian Hittites:

1. **Pure coincidence** — *Het*/*hittî* and the historical *Hatti* are unconnected, and the
   modern label rests on a false assumption.
2. **Genuine migration** — the biblical name reflects real population movement from Anatolia
   and north Syria into Palestine at the end of the Bronze Age.
3. **Etymological link, no ethnic/cultural connection** (the view Bryce attributes to
   **Itamar Singer**) — by the 7th–6th c. BCE, when the biblical texts were composed,
   "Hatti" had become a vague geographic term for the whole region from south-east Anatolia
   to the Egyptian border, stripped of ethnic meaning; biblical writers applied it
   **anachronistically** to a Canaanite hill-country group.
4. Variants combining the above.

Bryce inclines toward the view that the **Palestinian "Hittites" were not Anatolians** — the
Semitic onomastics weigh decisively against direct descent — while the **northern "land of
the Hittites" does** preserve a real geographic memory of Hatti. The famous proposed
equation of **Tid'al king of Goiim** ("Nations," Gen. 14) with a Hittite **Tudhaliya** he
treats as unprovable.

## Significance

- A cautionary case in **how a modern discipline inherits its core term from an unrelated
  ancient text** — "Hittite" is a scholarly convenience, not the self-designation of either
  the Bronze Age people (who called their official language *Nesite*) or the biblical group.
- `contrasts_with:` the [[neo-hittite-states|Neo-Hittite states]], whose claim to the
  "Hittite" label rests on demonstrable [[luwians|Luwian]]-Anatolian continuity, not on
  the biblical usage.
