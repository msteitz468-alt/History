# Trade Diaspora

## Definition

A **trade diaspora** is a geographically dispersed network of merchants linked by
mutual solidarity — common profession, religion, language, or place of origin —
who specialize in trade across cultural lines and great distances. The concept
was given its influential form by **Philip D. Curtin** (*Cross-Cultural Trade in
World History*, 1984), who argued that such "special institutional arrangements"
were ubiquitous from the Neolithic to the dawn of industrialization. Their two
salient features are **geographical dispersion** (an asset when communication and
transport were fragile, easing the circulation of people, capital, and
information) and the **lack of an autonomous sovereign state** (forcing them to
negotiate status with host political authorities, primarily through economic
leverage). Curtin provocatively included the European **chartered companies**
under the same rubric, to deflate claims of Western exceptionalism.

## Examples

Across the early modern world (Trivellato and Parker, CWH VI): the **Armenians**
of New Julfa (Isfahan), the **Sephardic Jews** of the Mediterranean and Atlantic,
the **Hokkien/Fujian Chinese** of maritime Southeast Asia, the **Hausa** and
**Wangara** of West Africa, and a cluster of Gujarati-based communities (Parsis,
Jains, Sindhis), as well as Multanis, Punjabis, and Afghans across Central Asia.

## Monocentric vs. Polycentric Networks

Sebouh Aslanian's distinction, adopted in CWH VI:

- **Monocentric** — organized around a single regulating hub. The clearest case
  is the **Armenian** network centered on **New Julfa**, where a corporate
  Assembly of Merchants and a *kalantar*-led court enforced agreements binding
  sedentary investors to traveling agents who reached from Sweden to the Pacific.
  Armenian fortunes rose under Safavid patronage and fell with the dynasty's
  eighteenth-century collapse.
- **Polycentric** — multiple independent nodes without a single home base, best
  exemplified by the **Sephardim**, scattered by the 1492 expulsion across North
  Africa, Italy, the Ottoman lands, Amsterdam, Hamburg, London, and the Iberian
  empires, positioned at the junctions of the silver and sugar trades and
  building credit relations with non-Jews while keeping community conformity.

## Critique and Status

The concept is **contested**. Recent scholarship (Subrahmanyam, Aslanian) stresses
what Curtin's apolitical, cohesive "enclave" model underplays: the **political
negotiation** between foreign merchants and territorial powers, the **competitive
tensions within** merchant communities, and the **porous boundaries** between all
those engaged in commerce in a given place. The most nuanced reframing depicts
these networks as **"circulating commercial societies"** — circuits of "merchants
and things" around nodal points, reinforced by endogamous marriage and shared
religion. Trivellato further notes that Curtin's analogy between stateless
diasporas and the chartered companies remains "little more than an academic
provocation" without sustained comparison.
