## Definition and Scope

The unified theory proposed by Matthew Spriggs in the 2022 Cambridge History of the Pacific Ocean is an attempt to construct a single explanatory framework — drawing on political economy theory, anarchist theory, collective action theory, and the heterarchy literature — for the entire sequence of Austronesian maritime migration from Taiwan across island Southeast Asia and into the remote Pacific, spanning roughly 5,000 years. As Spriggs puts it: "The aim here is perhaps immodest in that it seeks to develop a unitary explanation for human migration episodes across a third of the planet."

The theory argues that colonization, exchange-system change, and shifts in social complexity are not three separate phenomena but outputs of a single underlying dynamic: the structural contradiction between elite hierarchy formation and commoner resistance to that hierarchy, resolved through maritime fission and re-founding.

## Intellectual History: Political Economy in Pacific Scholarship

Spriggs traces the theory's genealogy through sixty years of Pacific social science:

- **Marshall Sahlins (1958)** – *Social Stratification in Polynesia*: agricultural productivity → population → stratification; small atolls produce minimal chiefdoms; large islands produce Hawaiian-style hierarchies.
- **Irving Goldman (1970)** – tripartite classification: Traditional (small-island, ascribed rank), Open (secular power, militaristic, unstable), Stratified (Hawai'i, Society Islands, Tonga). Goldman's "Open" societies overlap with cases Friedman later calls "devolved."
- **Jonathan Friedman (1981)** – "Notes on structure and history in Oceania": prestige-good systems underpin chieftaincy; where trade density increases, systems devolve toward big-man forms; where isolation intensifies, chiefs shift to "theocratic feudalism" controlling land and agricultural intensification. Friedman extended the framework beyond Polynesia to include Melanesia and Micronesia.
- **Geoff Irwin (1992)** – brought in island accessibility and isolation as variables; small, isolated islands tend to be abandoned ("Polynesian mystery islands").
- **Earle and Spriggs (2015)** – explicitly Marxist political economy approach to Pacific archaeology.
- **Furholt, Grier, Spriggs, and Earle (2020)** – synthesis of top-down (Marxist) and bottom-up (anarchist) approaches. Six analytical elements: "aspiring power, property rights, collaboration and balancing, resistance, overlapping layers of action, and embeddedness."

A key structural distinction the framework insists on: Austronesian-speaking groups (Micronesia, Polynesia, coastal Melanesia) carried ideologies of ascribed genealogical ranking; Papuan-speaking groups of New Guinea and the central Solomons did not. As Bellwood observed, Papuan systems show "the almost total lack of commitment to principles of ascribed ranking" — a deep ideological difference that shapes where chiefly hierarchies develop and persist.

## Core Causal Mechanism: Structural Contradiction and Fission

The theory's central proposition: "the trigger for maritime colonization in Neolithic societies in this region was to escape from growing structural inequalities." Population growth on agriculturally productive but spatially limited islands generates elite bottlenecks; elite control of surplus intensifies; sections of the community fission and migrate. But the mechanics of fission embed a contradiction:

**Leaders** (typically younger sons of chiefly families, never heirs in their own right) had access to resources sufficient to organize a voyage. They sought to re-establish hierarchy with themselves at the apex in a new place. "The aim for a really ambitious young man of a junior line might perhaps have been to remove himself and his followers as far as possible from his home settlement in order to convert himself and his descendants into a senior line without interference."

**Commoners** who joined sought to escape "increasing demands on their time and resources by emergent and potentially oppressive elites." Their interests were "by no means necessarily the same as those of the putative leaders."

This contradiction was temporarily suppressed during the early colonization phase because the leader's control of boat technology, return links to the homeland (for further migrants, cultigens, specialist knowledge), and ritual authority created real bottlenecks. But "after a period of perhaps only a few generations, after which demographic viability was assured and food security established, the need for fixed hierarchies would probably be questioned and resistance set in."

## Migration Sequence and Key Episodes

### Taiwan and the Island Southeast Asian Neolithic (c. 6500–4000 BP)

The Taiwanese Neolithic (Tapengkeng / Dabenkeng culture) emerged from mainland Chinese coastal groups, possibly responding to "the not-too-distant emergence of densely populated villages, walled settlements, ceremonial complexes, extravagantly furnished elite-class burials and overt signs of social inequalities." The Peinan site (c. 3500 BP, possibly 80–160 ha) shows jade-rich burials indicating social differentiation, strictly standardized housing, and granary complexes — conditions generating "thresholds of stress" for fission. By c. 4200–4000 BP a push south reached the Batanes Islands; by 4000–3800 BP the Cagayan Valley of Luzon was settled as a "significant Taiwanese colony." By 3500 BP much of island Southeast Asia was settled.

### Lapita Expansion (c. 3300–2800 BP)

Lapita populations — aDNA evidence shows them to have been "essentially unadmixed East Asians, whose closest living relatives today are indeed those of farming populations in northern Luzon and the Taiwanese aborigines" — moved through the Bismarck Archipelago (facilitated by the volcanic "sanitizing" of West New Britain c. 3300 BP), leapfrogging the main Solomons into Remote Oceania (Reefs–Santa Cruz, Vanuatu, New Caledonia, Fiji, Tonga) with "no significant pause." In Remote Oceania they encountered no prior human populations.

### End of Lapita and Post-Lapita Transformation (c. 2800–2400 BP)

In Vanuatu a secondary migration of Australo-Papuan populations from the New Britain area arrived during late Lapita times, genetically swamping the East Asian Lapita founders (by c. 2500 BP, some Vanuatu individuals show 96–99% Papuan ancestry). Culturally: burial rites shifted, Lapita iconography simplified and disappeared, long-distance exchange ceased, settlements moved from coastal to dispersed inland patterns. Earle and Spriggs call this an "egalitarian turn" driven by secondary migrants who "almost certainly had memories of more egalitarian social systems prior to the irruption of Asian migrant populations." As links to homelands broke, the chiefly basis of Lapita power collapsed.

### Eastern Polynesian Expansion (c. 1200 BP – 1300 CE)

A 1,600-year pause in Samoa and Tonga was partly technological (navigating against prevailing winds into eastern Polynesia was "extremely difficult... except in the strongest El Niño conditions") but also social: "Nascent hierarchies were starting to establish archipelago-wide chiefdoms in Tonga and Samoa... Conquest warfare and raids on adjacent archipelagoes were soon to become a feature of expanding predatory chiefdoms." The safety valve was renewed voyaging — initially northwest to Polynesian outlier islands in Melanesia, from whose northern groups (e.g. Ontong Java) westerly winds enabled movement east. Probable staging route: outliers → Marquesas → Society Islands → Hawaii, Easter Island, New Zealand.

## Social Complexity Outcomes and Island Variables

Island size, resource base, connectivity, and isolation together determined which sociopolitical form survived and consolidated:

| Island Context | Outcome |
|---|---|
| Large, isolated, rich (Hawai'i) | Staple finance systems; maximum hierarchy; eventually "internal" wealth finance |
| Small, isolated (Easter Island) | Devolution; Goldman's "Open" society; oral traditions record hierarchy-overthrow |
| Large, temperate, continental-remnant (New Zealand) | Tribal organization; Sahlins: "tribal group populations were only in the low thousands… control of scattered resources was difficult to enforce and opportunities for agricultural intensification were limited in most areas" |
| Vanuatu (Papuanized) | "Elite power was broken post-Lapita and never comprehensively re-established" — modern migration from Vanuatu is negligible |
| Hierarchical archipelagoes (Tonga, Samoa) | Mass migration in contemporary era: "All of the above Polynesian nations and territories have a majority of their populations living outside the country of their birth or recent descent" — the last bottleneck (control of travel technology) now broken by affordable air travel |

## Theoretical Framework

Six elements from Furholt et al. (2020) are embedded:
1. **Aspiring power** – junior elite lines unable to inherit, leading expeditions
2. **Property rights** – in boats, which structured who could lead
3. **Collaboration and balancing** – temporary alliances between dissatisfied commoners and elite organizers
4. **Resistance** – commoner rejection of re-imposed hierarchy once viability was secured
5. **Overlapping layers of action** – "multiple agency, discrete interests, and sociopolitical action to effect hegemony, resistance and negotiation"
6. **Embeddedness** – each migration episode is historically specific, not just a demographic response

Wengrow and Graeber's critique of evolutionary stage models informs the framework: the theory explicitly rejects "too-neat categorizations of societies into evolutionary stages or directions" and foregrounds "the breadth of political creativity that can be found across space and time."

## Limitations and Criticisms

Spriggs acknowledges the theory is "admittedly underdetermined by the data at hand, which are spotty at best" — a "big-picture view of processes that at the local level may have seemed to play out rather differently." The theory applies specifically to Austronesian-speaking populations and does not account for Papuan-speaking groups, whose social logics differ fundamentally. The Austronesian-Papuan boundary (running through the Bismarck Archipelago and central Solomons) is a structural constraint on the theory's domain of application.

## Historiography

The chapter builds on sixty years of Pacific political-economy scholarship but represents the first attempt to synthesize colonization motivation, exchange-system evolution, and social-complexity trajectories into a single causal framework. Key precedents (Sahlins 1958; Goldman 1970; Friedman 1981; Irwin 1992) were all anthropological and ethnohistoric; the explicit archaeological framing through Lapita aDNA evidence (Skoglund et al. 2016; Lipson et al. 2018; Posth et al. 2018) is recent. The aDNA revolution substantially supports the model's claim of eastward East Asian population movement followed by secondary Australo-Papuan admixture.

The theory is contested in its unified ambition: Solheim's "Nusantao" counter-theory (ch. 25, Andaya, same volume) argues for a multidirectional maritime trade network rather than a unidirectional Bellwood-Blust expansion. Andaya notes that "the unidirectional pattern of the Bellwood–Blust thesis has also been questioned because a study of the expansion of Austronesian languages to Melanesia shows 'little or no genetic input from Taiwan'."
