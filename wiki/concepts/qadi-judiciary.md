## Definition and Scope

The **qāḍī** (judge) is the central institutional figure of the Islamic legal system, administering justice within the framework of [[sharia]] and [[fiqh]]. Almost every comprehensive fiqh work dedicated three chapters to what modern observers would recognize as procedural law: (1) the qāḍī's qualifications, conduct, and court procedure; (2) the obligations and rights of plaintiffs and defendants; (3) evidence — oral testimony, oaths, written instruments, and confession.

The qāḍī's institution was elaborated not only within the major fiqh works but also in a specialized independent genre: the **adab al-qāḍī** (or adab al-qaḍāʾ) treatises, dedicated entirely to the conditions of judgeship and court conduct. The significant decline of dedicated jihād treatises after the 3rd/9th century coincides with a proliferation of adab al-qāḍī works, indicating where juristic attention increasingly concentrated.

This page covers the classical theory of the qāḍī's court. For individual judges as historical actors, see actor pages. For the evidentiary rules the qāḍī applies, see [[islamic-law-of-evidence]].

## Appointment and Personal Qualifications

The majority of jurists, including Twelver Shīʿites, held that only a man can be a qāḍī. Abū Ḥanīfa permitted women to serve as judges in all areas except ḥudūd offenses and qiṣāṣ (where their testimony is not accepted). Ibn Jarīr al-Ṭabarī (d. 310/923), eponym of the defunct Jarīrite school, reasoned that women may exercise ijtihād like men and therefore can serve in the full range of judicial matters.

The qāḍī's appointment derives from the sovereign; he serves as the sovereign's representative (delegated judge). His conduct is governed by an elaborate ethics: he must be serious yet polite, firm yet gentle, economic in speech, and must give litigants his undivided attention. He must not accept gifts or bribes; if he does not receive income from the Public Treasury, he may charge litigants and court users appropriate fees.

## The Court Session (Majlis al-Ḥukm)

The qāḍī was "under the legal and moral obligation to hear and adjudge all disputes (except for those which involved conflict of interest), and was forbidden to turn away claimants or disputants, even if they appeared before him outside his schedule." The duty to "raze injustice" admitted no exception: "protracted adjudication and postponements in the judicial process were thus abhorred, and deemed to aggravate injustice."

Procedural equity governed physical access to the court session: queues were maintained; when simultaneous arrival disrupted queue order, lots were drawn. The qāḍī was commanded to greet and invite all litigants to sit, and to treat parties with equal respect — "equity was of the essence, beginning with the litigants' physical approach to the majlis al-ḥukm."

Most later jurists strongly advised against a qāḍī relying on prior personal knowledge of litigants when formulating decisions; those who permitted such reliance categorically excluded ḥudūd matters. The qāḍī was required to have a group of legal scholars (fuqahāʾ) attend his court sessions, ensuring collegial oversight. He could not adjudicate cases involving relatives or friends.

Importantly, "the greater bulk of the matters handled by the Muslim court did not involve litigation." The court served primarily as an administrative and certifying institution for contracts, endowments (waqf), marriages, inheritances, and guardianship — non-litigious social functions that constituted the everyday business of the court.

## The Structure of Lawsuits (Daʿwā)

A valid lawsuit (daʿwā) must meet several conditions to be "sound" (ṣaḥīḥa): it must issue from a party with standing, must not be internally contradictory, and must state with specificity the nature, characteristics, size, value, and location of the disputed object or matter. Defective suits (nāqiṣa) that can be rectified are not dismissed; incurable defects void the suit.

The fundamental distinction between **plaintiff** (muddaʿī) and **defendant** (muddaʿā ʿalayhi) determines the burden of proof. The Mālikite and Shāfiʿite position: the plaintiff is the party whose claim to change the presumed status quo is weaker. The Ḥanafite position: the plaintiff is the party who, if he abandons the claim, cannot be compelled to persist; the defendant has no such option. The Ḥanbalites add initiative as a criterion. In disputes where no presumption favors either side, both parties simultaneously occupy plaintiff and defendant positions and must both adduce evidence (bayyina).

The **presumption of innocence** (barāʾa) and the **presumption of continuity** are fundamental to allocating burdens. A claim of outstanding debt against a defendant who denies it requires the plaintiff to produce evidence; the defendant need only swear an oath of non-indebtedness. Similarly, a "natural state of affairs" is presumed to continue until the contrary is demonstrated: prolonged, undisputed possession of property signals ownership.

The majority of jurists hold that the plaintiff determines jurisdiction; the Ḥanafites confer this on the defendant, consistent with the presumption of innocence.

## Conflict of Interest and the Integrity of Judgment

The qāḍī may not adjudge disputes involving relatives or friends — "this appears to be the unanimous position of all Sunnite schools." Twelver Shīʿites, however, permitted a qāḍī to adjudicate disputes involving his own descendants and ascendants. Gift-taking and bribery are categorically forbidden. Upon leaving office, the qāḍī remains accountable: his successor may review his decisions, and he "could be called upon by his successor to account for any misconduct during his tenure, and had to face justice like any other defendant."

Judgments may be reviewed and, in some circumstances, reversed by successors. But if a judgment has already been implemented, reversal is suspended in matters involving the slightest suspicion — particularly in ḥudūd and qiṣāṣ cases.

## Arbitration (Taḥkīm) and the Court's Enforcement Role

Parties may opt for arbitration (taḥkīm) as an alternative to the qāḍī's court. Once an arbiter (ḥakam) is appointed and proceedings begin, no party may withdraw. The arbiter's decision is binding. Jurists disagree on whether withdrawal is permitted during proceedings. The qāḍī enforces the arbiter's decision if it is legally sound and accords with his school's doctrine; otherwise he may revoke it. Ḥanafites exclude ḥudūd and qiṣāṣ from arbitration; Ḥanbalites and Shāfiʿites add family law.

When defendants refuse to appear voluntarily, the qāḍī may send assistants (aʿwān), and, for serious cases, may request police (shurṭa) or the governor's soldiers to compel appearance.

## Collective Rights and Public Prosecution

The fiqh distinguishes individual from collective rights. Ḥudūd offenses (except qadhf) belong to the collective category (ḥaqq Allāh) and may be prosecuted by any member of the community. The **muḥtasib** (market inspector/moral enforcer) and the qāḍī together "fulfilled some of the functions associated in modern law with public prosecution" in matters of public interest — in the absence of a state prosecution apparatus. The daʿwā al-ḥisba allows any private individual to bring a public-interest claim.

## Inter-Court Communication (Kitāb al-Qāḍī ilā al-Qāḍī)

Mālikites, Shāfiʿites, and Ḥanbalites generally allowed written communication between judges for coordinating evidence and decisions across jurisdictions. The Ḥanafites and Twelver Shīʿites were more restrictive; the latter "appear to have rejected this type of communication categorically." This instrument was inadmissible as evidence in ḥudūd cases across all schools. Hallaq treated this judicial communication in a separate study ("Qāḍīs Communicating").

## Historiography

The adab al-qāḍī genre is among the richest primary sources for the social history of Islamic law. Hallaq's survey of qāḍī court records (sijillāt) and the adab literature has established that the court's function was primarily administrative and certifying rather than litigious — a finding that substantially revises earlier scholarship that modeled Islamic courts on adversarial Western judicial systems.

The gender bar on female qāḍīs has been a contested topic in both classical fiqh (where three distinct positions exist: majority exclusion, Ḥanafite partial inclusion, and Ṭabarī's full inclusion) and in modern Islamic legal reform movements.

The collection's coverage is primarily through Hallaq's synthetic treatment; primary sources cross-referenced include Māwardī's *Ḥāwī*, Nawawī's *Rawḍa*, and various adab al-qāḍī treatises.
