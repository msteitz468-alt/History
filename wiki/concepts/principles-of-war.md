# Principles of War

A codified set of enduring maxims of military planning and conduct — in
current US Army doctrine (FM 3-0): Objective, Offensive, Mass, Economy of
Force, Maneuver, Unity of Command, Security, Surprise, and Simplicity —
intended to distill recurring lessons of successful and unsuccessful
campaigns across military history into a compact analytical checklist.

## Definition and Scope

Each principle addresses a distinct dimension of campaign and battle
planning: clarity of purpose (Objective), seizing initiative (Offensive),
concentrating decisive combat power (Mass and Economy of Force),
flexibility of positioning (Maneuver), coherent command (Unity of Command),
protection from enemy action (Security), achieving the unexpected
(Surprise), and clarity of plans (Simplicity). The framework is used both
as a planning tool and as a retrospective analytical lens for evaluating
historical campaigns.

## Origin (Emic/Etic)

The principles trace to British theorist J.F.C. Fuller's early
twentieth-century attempts to systematize the "principles of war" drawn from
Clausewitz, Jomini, and Napoleonic-era practice; they were subsequently
adapted into US and other national military doctrines, becoming formal,
codified (emic, institutionally endorsed) doctrine rather than a purely
academic analytical construct.

## Applications and Examples

[[summers-harry-g|Harry G. Summers]]'s influential *On Strategy* (1982)
applied the principles of war, alongside Clausewitzian theory, to analyze
American strategic failures in the Vietnam War, arguing that US
policymakers had systematically violated principles such as Objective and
Mass by pursuing limited-war aims without clearly defined, achievable
military objectives.

## Limitations and Critiques

Critics argue the principles are so general as to be compatible with almost
any outcome in retrospective analysis, risk oversimplifying the genuinely
contingent and context-dependent nature of strategic success, and can
encourage mechanical checklist thinking in place of situational judgment —
even as they remain durable teaching tools in professional military
education.
