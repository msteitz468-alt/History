# Para-fascism

Griffin's category for regimes that wear fascism's clothes while being its
enemy: counter-revolutionary (anti-communist **and** anti-fascist) regimes of
traditional elites or the military that "masquerade as revolutionary
(pro-fascist)" while their real dynamics are "essentially reactionary and
conservative." He coins "para-" for its OED connotations of "alteration,
perversion, simulation" of the real thing, explicitly preferring it to
"fascistized," "semi-fascist," "pseudo-fascist," etc.

The behavioral signature: "A para-fascist regime, however ritualistic its
style of politics, well orchestrated its leader cult, palingenetic its
rhetoric, ruthless its terror apparatus... will react to genuine fascism as
a threat" — cooperating with a fascist movement at most tactically before
neutralizing it, either by force (Salazar crushing the National Syndicalists,
1935) or by absorption (Franco's 1937 creation of FET y de las JONS over the
[[falange|Falange]]: "Its success in neutralizing fascism's revolutionary
impetus by absorption has nevertheless deceived numerous historians into
mistaking Franco's regime for the real thing").

## Regimes classified para-fascist by Griffin

Piłsudski's Poland · Primo de Rivera's and [[franco-francisco|Franco]]'s
Spain · Salazar's Portugal · Metaxas's Greece · Smetona's Lithuania ·
Ulmanis's Latvia · Dollfuss/Schuschnigg's Austria (which "stood firm against
fascism" in 1934) · Horthy's Hungary · King Carol's and then Antonescu's
Romania — where the genuinely fascist [[iron-guard|Iron Guard]] "had in
quick succession been crushed, wooed, coopted, and liquidated by two types
of para-fascist regime, one of them with the full backing of the Third
Reich" · [[vichy-france|Vichy France]] ("a dynamic form of home-grown
neo-conservatism"; Pétain compared to Hindenburg, "not... Hitler").

## The Spanish evidence in monographic depth (Payne 1987)

Payne's regime history ([[sources/payne-franco-regime-1987]]) supplies the fullest documentation of para-fascist mechanics for the founding case. Franco's July 1937 "totalitarian state" was explicitly modeled on the Catholic Monarchs, not functional totalitarianism — "a unitary and authoritarian state that permitted varying degrees of traditional pluralism." The FET was a top-down state creation whose National Council held at most 20 genuine Falangists of 50; the Fuero del Trabajo (1938) copied the Carta del Lavoro's form while infusing social-Catholic content; the syndical system in practice ran separate employer/worker sections with employers dominant — "the opposite of fascist corporatist theory." Franco forbade Nazi stylistic imitation by circular (October 1939), purged the Naziphile syndical chief Salvador Merino (1941 — Payne's parallel to Mussolini's 1928 breaking of Rossoni), and let regime theorists reinterpret "totalitarian" out of existence (García Valdecasas 1942; Arrese 1944), while the Falangist delegation at the Nazi-organized 1942 Vienna youth congress defended Catholic positions against Nazi racism. Systematic defascistization ran 1943–45 ("movement" not "party"; foreign-comparison press ban; fascist salute abolished September 1945; "organic democracy" rhetoric). Payne's formula — "from 1937 to 1945 the Franco regime was doctrinally at least a semifascist state" — and his verdict that a Hitler victory would have made Francoism "more radical and overtly fascist in form" mark both the kinship with and the distance from Griffin's category; his closest comparative parallel is Antonescu's Romania, which allied with and then crushed the Iron Guard within six months.

## Significance and critiques

The concept underwrites the field's fascism/authoritarianism boundary from
the fascist-movement side: Griffin's general law is that "authoritarian
regimes based on military, monarchical, personal or presidential
dictatorship have all proved equally lethal to fascism's micro-climate," and
"there is no precedent for fascism prevailing over non-fascist authoritarian
forces." It converges with
[[payne-typology-of-authoritarian-nationalism|Payne's]] three-sector
typology (fascism / radical right / conservative right) and with
[[paxton-anatomy-of-fascism-2004|Paxton's]] observation that every
authoritarian regime able to rule alone crushed its fascists — three
independently built frameworks agreeing that Franco, Salazar, Vichy, and
Antonescu were not fascist regimes. Critics note the category can make
"genuine" fascism unfalsifiably rare (only two regimes ever qualify) and
that co-opted fascists (Falangists, Legionaries) did shape para-fascist
regimes from inside.

## Griffin (2007): Abortive Fascisms and the Modernist Test

*Modernism and Fascism* confirms the boundary from the modernist side: Franco's Spain and Salazar's Portugal are explicitly excluded from the set of [[modernist-state|modernist states]]. In Portugal's "parafascist" Estado Novo, José Zúquete documents António Ferro's *failed* attempt to inject the cultural modernism of *Orpheu* into Salazar's regime; in Franco's Spain "fascism was again marginalized, the [[falange|Falange]] reduced to playing a largely propagandistic role despite the official hero cult" of José Antonio — though the futural thrust of *El Escorial*'s aesthetics is flagged as worth revisiting. The counterfactual cuts the other way too: had Mussolini and Hitler aged in power, their regimes "may have gone the way of Salazar's Portugal and Franco's Spain, charismatic power draining away" — but "such an atrophy of modernist energies would have been the ultimate betrayal of the core fascist world-view." Genuine abortive fascisms with full modernist dynamics: the BUF (technocratic rebirth; Mosley's mazeway of "bowdlerized Nietzsche, Spengler, a secularized Christianity, and Keynesian economics"), Brazil's Integralists (180,000 members in 1934; Salgado's "fourth era of humanity"; the Sigma symbol; his role in the 1922 Week of Modern Art), and the [[iron-guard|Iron Guard]]. (Source: [[griffin-modernism-and-fascism-2007]].)

## Payne (1980): fascistization, the "2 percent rule," and the satellite framework

*Fascism: Comparison and Definition* ([[payne-fascism-comparison-definition-1980]], ch. 5) is the fullest early survey of the para-fascist field. Parliamentary breakdown swept Europe (Italy 1922–25, Spain 1923, Portugal 1926, Poland 1926, Greece 1926–36, Lithuania 1926, Yugoslavia 1929, Germany/Austria 1933), initially producing "syncretistic, semipluralist forms of right-wing dictatorship" *without* fascist components; only after Hitler's 1933 triumph did right-authoritarian regimes begin **"fascistization"** — adopting outward trappings for mobilization and a modern image — intensifying into "Nazification" by the late 1930s. Payne's structural findings:

- **The "2 percent rule":** in north-European democracies fascist-type parties almost never exceeded ~2 percent of the vote (Quisling's Nasjonal Samling peaked at 2.16 percent in 1933); exceptions — Finland's IKL 8.3 percent (1936, after a real civil war plus Soviet threat and Karelian irredentism), the Dutch NSB 7.9 percent (1935, "a very Dutch kind of national socialism," corporatist, non-antisemitic, accepting Jewish members), Belgium's Rex 11.49 percent + VNV 7.12 percent (1936 — neither yet "categorically fascist"; Degrelle's full fascist turn after 1936 collapsed his vote to 4.43 percent by 1939). Where nationhood, prosperity, and parliamentary tradition were secure there was "no space nor 'need' for revolutionary nationalism" — France, despite generating the ideas first, illustrates the same law ("after February 6, 1934, antifascism became the dominant political fact in France, a thousand times more important than fascism").
- **Country survey of the fascistized right:** Austria — Heimwehren (200,000 claimed members by 1929, Mussolini-financed, Korneuburg Oath 1930, only 6 percent of the 1930 vote) absorbed into Dollfuss's Fatherland Front under the May 1934 "conservative, Catholic, and virtually theocratic" corporative constitution; "neither the old Heimwehren nor the Dollfuss-Schuschnigg regime... ever became fascist in the fully typical sense." Hungary — interwar Europe's densest assortment of right-radical groups (Trianon losses, the Kun episode, a swollen jobless bureaucratic middle class); Gömbös, premier 1932, forced to moderate before taking office, told Göring in 1935 Hungary would be national-socialist within three years, died 1936. Poland — Piłsudski's "conservative and moderate semipluralist" regime; the Colonels' OZN (1936–39) as "bureaucratic state protofascism," briefly leaning on Piasecki's Falanga. Baltic — Smetona's Lithuania; Päts and Ulmanis as "authoritarian democracy" (von Rauch), preventive coups *against* local fascist movements (Veterans' League, Thunder Cross). Balkans — royal dictatorships (Yugoslavia 1929, Bulgaria 1935, Greece 1936, Romania 1938); Stojadinović's failed pseudofascistization; Metaxas "more a figure of the radical authoritarian right than a fascist revolutionary... obviously resembled Franco much more than Hitler or Mussolini." Czechoslovakia — the region's one functioning democracy; its significant case was the fascistized Slovak People's Party, beneficiary of the state's destruction.
- **Wartime satellites:** collaboration was "primarily a circumstantial affair"; Quisling, who assisted foreign conquest of his own country, became "a new name for national ignominy"; only Polish fascists kept resisting Germany after conquest. Hitler preferred conservative right-authoritarians as satellite rulers — "more compliant and less challenging" — and was reluctant to empower even Quisling or Mussert; favored foreign fascists (Degrelle) ended in the Waffen-SS rather than in power. The Ustasha state was "by far the most radical satellite regime" and "the most Nazified": its 1941–44 genocide against Orthodox Serbs was "a program of truly Hitlerian proportions, given the size of Croatia and the means at their disposal" — though even Pavelić compromised with the Catholic Church rather than impose a one-party totalitarian system. Payne's verdict: except possibly Slovakia, the satellites were "primarily if not exclusively creatures of Nazi expansion... a phenomenon of Hitler's Europe rather than of the genuine political evolution of their own lands," analogous to the postwar People's Democracies as "products of imperial conquest and hegemony."
