# The Twelfth-Century Renaissance

## Definition

The **Twelfth-Century Renaissance** is the intellectual and cultural flowering of
Latin Europe between roughly 1070 and 1200 — the revival of learning, law, philosophy,
science, and Latin and vernacular literature that [[luscombe-ncmh-v4-2004|NCMH IV]]
(David Luscombe, Ch. 12, "Thought and learning") treats as the mental dimension of the
period's general "take-off." The term is Charles Homer Haskins's (1927); like all such
"renaissance" labels it is etic and contested (critics note continuities with the
Carolingian and Ottonian revivals, and that "renaissance" overstates the novelty), but
it remains the standard name for a genuine and many-sided expansion of European
intellectual life.

## Major Features

- **The shift from monastery to school.** The principal centres of learning moved from
  the rural monasteries to the **cathedral and urban schools** (Chartres, Laon, Paris,
  and the law schools of Bologna), and from there toward the new
  **[[medieval-university|universities]]**. Luscombe stresses that literacy was not a
  clerical monopoly: lay nobles were often literate, and education "formed character as
  well as the mind," teaching manners and the liberal arts together.
- **Scholasticism.** A new method of teaching and inquiry — the dialectical
  reconciliation of authorities through question, objection, and resolution — took
  shape in theology and law. Its emblematic figure is **Peter Abelard** (1079–1142,
  *Sic et Non*), alongside **John of Salisbury**, the school of **Chartres**, and the
  systematising of theology by Peter Lombard (the *Sentences*) and of canon law by
  Gratian (see [[ius-commune]]).
- **The translation movement.** Greek and especially **Arabic** learning entered Latin
  Europe in a great wave of translation — in Spain (Toledo), Sicily, and the
  Mediterranean — recovering the bulk of **Aristotle**, Greek and Arabic science,
  mathematics, astronomy, and medicine. **Adelard of Bath** (c. 1080–c. 1152) and
  others brought this "new logic" and natural philosophy north; the Cordoban
  philosopher **Ibn Rushd (Averroes)** commented on Aristotle in works "soon to be
  widely known in the Latin universities."
- **Latin and vernacular literature.** A "golden age" of Latin coexisted with the rise
  of vernacular writing — the *[[chanson-de-geste|chansons de geste]]*, the troubadour
  lyric, and the courtly romance of Chrétien de Troyes (see NCMH IV, Ziolkowski, Ch. 18).

## Significance and Critique

The twelfth-century revival created the institutional and intellectual foundations of
the later medieval and modern West: the university, the scholastic method, a
professional class of jurists and theologians, and the assimilation of the Greco-Arabic
scientific and philosophical inheritance. The chief historiographical debate is over
the **"renaissance"** label itself: how far it marks a true rebirth versus a continuous
growth from the earlier medieval revivals, and whether the emphasis on a "renaissance"
obscures the period's own originality.
