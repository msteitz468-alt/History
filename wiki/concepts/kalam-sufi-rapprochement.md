## Definition and Scope

This page documents the *historical process* by which Islamic mysticism ([[sufism]]) and speculative theology ([[kalam]]) moved from uneasy coexistence toward institutional and doctrinal integration — a movement that Toby Mayer calls "arguably the central dynamic of Islamic intellectual history" ([[winter-cambridge-companion-islamic-theology-2008]]). The rapprochement was not linear and was never total: tensions, trials of mystics, and persistent anti-kalam Sufi currents characterized the entire arc.

This page is distinct from:
- [[philosophical-sufism]]: the specific Akbarian (Ibn 'Arabi–Qunawi) tradition of merging Sufi metaphysics with Avicennan philosophy — a later and more specialized development
- [[early-sufism]]: the formative period of Islamic mysticism, c. 750–945 CE
- [[kalam]]: Islamic speculative theology as a systematic discipline
- [[sufism]]: the Sufi movement as a whole, including its social and institutional history

## The Initial Unity and Its Disruption

Mysticism, theology, jurisprudence, and Qur'anic exegesis "formed a seamless unity in the apostolic period of Islam" (Mayer). The disruption came with the crystallization of the Islamic sciences in the 9th century, each developing its own authorities, principles (*usul*), and schools (*madhahib*). This "fixation" threatened to restrict the role of spirituality, which had been central to early Islamic religious culture.

**Al-Hasan al-Basri (d. 728)** is the pivotal originating figure for *both* disciplines. From his circle emerged proto-Sufis (Ibn Wasi', Farqad, Yaz id al-Raqqashi, Habib al-'Ajami) and the two founders of Mu'tazilite theology (Wasil ibn 'Ata', d. 748; 'Amr ibn 'Ubayd, d. 769). Both Mu'tazilite founders were also noted ascetics. The proto-Sufi concept of *hal* (transitional spiritual state) may originate with Basri; the Sufi practice of systematic self-examination (*muhasaba*) appears first in his circle.

## The Mu'tazilite-Sufi Early Connection (and Its Rupture)

The early link between Mu'tazilism and Sufism has been almost completely eclipsed in later memory. There were indeed figures categorized as "Sufi Mu'tazilites" (*sufiyyat al-mu'tazila*): the Baghdad school's founder Abu Sahl Bishr ibn al-Mu'tamir (d. 825) had Sufi followers; the major Mu'tazilite al-Nazzam (d. 845) taught Sufi students; the ecstatics Abu Yazid al-Bistami and Abu Hafs al-Haddadi were members of the Mu'tazila.

The rupture accelerated during the **Inquisition (*mihna*, 833–851 CE)**, when the Abbasid state enforced the Mu'tazilite doctrine of the created Qur'an. Prominent Sufis resisted in varying degrees (Bishr al-Hafi passively; Dhu'l-Nun al-Misri actively, suffering imprisonment). The *mihna*'s failure confirmed Sufism's already strong links with the "orthodox" Sunni party (*ahl al-hadith*), effectively foreclosing a Mu'tazilite Sufism. After al-Mutawakkil's caliphate and the discrediting of Mu'tazilism, the Sufi Mu'tazilite became "an anomalous figure."

## The Bakriyya, Salimiyya, and Karramiyya: Proto-Asharite Sufi Schools

Three major early Sufi schools were *simultaneously theological* in character, and their theologies were shaped in opposition to Mu'tazilism:

### The Bakriyya

Derived from 'Abd al-Wahid ibn Zayd (d. 793), a student in Basri's circle. An ascetic community established at 'Abbadan (island at the confluence of the Qar un and Tigris rivers) using distinctive conical cells for contemplative exercises. One of its main disciples, Abu Sulayman al-Darani (d. 830), first systematized the key Sufi concepts of the spiritual *state* (*hal*) and *station* (*maqam*) on the path to God. Notable figures at 'Abbadan included Bishr al-Hafi, Sari al-Saqati, and **Sahl al-Tustari (d. 896)**.

The Bakriyya was "as much a theological school as a spiritual movement," with a theology moulded in opposition to Mu'tazilism and in certain respects *foreshadowing Ash'arism*. Louis Massignon argued that its theologies were "explored and vindicated through mystical experimentation."

### The Salimiyya

Engendered by Tustari through his disciple Muhammad ibn Salim and the latter's son Ahmad ibn Salim. A major Sufi movement of the late 9th century, sometimes listed in doxographical works as a *kalam school*: Baghdadi's *al-Farq bayn al-firaq* calls it "a band of kalam scholars in Basra." The Salimiyya's doctrines were opposed to Mu'tazilism and linked with the anti-Mu'tazilite *hashwiyya* (outspoken literalists).

A key Salimiyya proposition: "when one recites the Qur'an, God Himself recites it by one's tongue" — closely analogous to Hanbalite positions on the uncreated Qur'an. Tustari's upholding of the divine attributes (characteristically: "attributes of the attributes" transcending human comprehension — "God is a fire ablaze. There is no way to Him and no escape from plunging into Him") represents the amodal affirmation of divine attributes that became standard in [[asharism]] and Hanbalism — but with a characteristically *experiential* tone that marks the Sufi difference from purely scholastic kalam.

### The Karramiyya

Founded by Muhammad ibn Karram (d. 870), who spent time at Balkh where remnants of Manichean or Buddhist religious institutions may have influenced his development of the *khanqah* (convent). The khanqah became the standard term in the Persianate world for a Sufi convent. Ibn Karram's theology veered toward corporealism (*tajsim*) — a reaction against Mu'tazilite abstraction — and was eventually anathematized. Yet the movement was widely influential in eastern Islam, drawing Hanafite scholars who rejected Mu'tazilism.

The Karramiyya's violent asceticism produced an *epochal reaction*: the **Malamatiyya** (People of Blame), led by Hamdun al-Qassar and Abu Hafs al-Haddadi. Their teaching rejected all spiritual ostentation (*riya'*) in reaction to Karramite histrionic otherworldliness. Spiritual striving was for God alone, or worthless. The Malamati ethic was later formalized by 'Abd al-Rahman al-Sulami (d. 1021) in his *Risalat al-Malamatiyya*, and in Ibn 'Arabi's hierarchy the highest saints are in the Malamati ranks.

## The Crisis of Esoterism: al-Hallaj and the Limits of Integration

From the 9th century, certain Sufis suggested a radical *incommensurability* of the mystical path with exoteric norms — key figures: al-Hallaj (d. 922), Niffari (d. after 977), Abu Yazid al-Bistami (d. c. 875), Hamdun al-Qassar (d. 884).

The problem had multiple dimensions:
- **Saintly thaumaturgy** seemingly rivaled prophetic thaumaturgy — accepted by the masses, denied by Mu'tazilites
- **God-realization (*ittisal*)** appeared to assert a union or identification with God, threatening the creator–creature distinction on which worship and moral accountability rested
- **The theopathic locution (*shath*)**: ecstatic utterances (most famous: Hallaj's "Ana'l-Haqq" — "I am the Real/Truth") that amounted, in Carl Ernst's words, to "a virtual supplementary canon, formed by the uninterrupted contact which God maintains with the elect"

**The Hallaj trials (913 and 922 CE)** under the Abbasid caliph al-Muqtadir represent the moment when the tension most momentously broke surface. The central theological issue was *ittisaf* — the claim that saints "see by God and hear by God," becoming manifestations of God in creation. Hallaj himself rejected *hulul* (divine infusion into a material body), explaining the saint's status as a "thinning of existential veils" through self-annihilation — but this distinction eluded his persecutors.

## The Rescue of Integrality: Junayd and Sober Sufism

**Junayd (d. 910)** heralds the decisive reaction. His "sobriety" (*sahw*) Sufism reasserted what Mayer calls "the Islamic genius for integrality": expressing Muslim esoterism in a way that contributed to, rather than undermined, the wider religion.

Junayd's key move: insisting on the *subjectivity* of the experience of annihilation (*fana'*) — ecstatics like Bistami had "passed away only 'in their imagination' (*'ala al-tawahhum*)." After annihilation, the mystic returns (*baqa'*) — "present in himself and in God after having been present in God and absent in himself." This doctrine of sobriety, later systematized as *wahdat al-shuhud* (unity of witnessing, subjective theomonism), became the standard of "orthodox" Sufism against the more immanentist readings.

Junayd's legacy was particularly strong in the **Hanbalite Sufi tradition**: the strict rejection of any superimposition on Qur'an and hadith produced a Sufism in "impeccable conformity with the consensual foundations of the tradition." Key Hanbalite Sufis: Ruwaym, 'Amr al-Makki, Khwaja 'Abdallah Ansari, 'Abd al-Qadir al-Jilani; later, Ibn Qayyim al-Jawziyya documented this tradition systematically.

## The Compilatory Manuals: Institutionalizing the Rapprochement

The period c. 966–1072 saw the production of five key compilatory works that imposed unity on regional Sufi traditions, defined technical terms, and above all **demonstrated Sufism's conformity with orthodox Sunni creeds**:

1. Abu Talib al-Makki (d. 966): *Qut al-qulub* (Food of Hearts)
2. Abu Nasr al-Sarraj (d. 988): *Kitab al-Luma'* (Book of Gleams)
3. Abu Bakr al-Kalabadhi (d. c. 990): *al-Ta'arruf li-madhhab ahl al-tasawwuf* (Disclosure of the Way of the Sufis)
4. 'Abd al-Rahman al-Sulami (d. 1021): *Tabaqat al-Sufiyya* (Generations of Sufis)
5. 'Ali al-Hujwiri (d. 1071/72): *Kashf al-mahjub* (Unveiling the Veiled, in Persian)

**Kalabadhi's *Disclosure*** is especially significant for the kalam-Sufi rapprochement. Its lengthy preliminary section (chapters 5–30) amounts to a detailed Sufi *creed* (*'aqa'id*) conforming to conventional Islamic catechisms: divine attributes, the Beatific Vision, theodicy. The positions are essentially Ash'arite (though the Hanafi-Maturidite background is also evident): divine attributes are "neither He nor other than He"; the Qur'an is God's eternal, uncreated Word; believers will have a true vision of God in the hereafter, but "without any modality or circumscription"; created acts are God's creation but humans possess a capacity (*istita'a*) in acting.

Kalabadhi also contains a crucial epistemological distinction — between *ta'rif* (God's self-disclosure *to* creation, cosmological knowledge of God) and *ta'arruf* (God's self-disclosure *through Himself*, the superior mystical knowledge). Mayer argues this distinction was taken up by Avicenna and became the basis for his famous *Burhan al-Siddiqin* (Proof of the Truthful) — a remarkable case of Sufi epistemology influencing Islamic philosophy.

Such creeds in Sufi literature were "not artifices to win acceptance from the Shari'a-minded" but expressions of "the Sufis' own conviction that Sufism lies at the very heart of the religion, and is *sine qua non* for its spiritual vitality" (Mayer, [[winter-cambridge-companion-islamic-theology-2008]]).

## Al-Ghazali and the Seljuk Synthesis

The decisive historical integration of [[kalam]] and [[sufism]] was achieved by **[[al-ghazali]] (d. 1111)** under the patronage of the Seljuks, the major Sunni Turkish power in Iran, Iraq, and Anatolia from the mid-11th to late 12th centuries.

Ghazali's synthesis was preceded by **al-Qushayri (d. 1072)**, whose widely influential *Treatise* (*Risala*) and esoteric Qur'anic commentary assumed an Ash'arite dogmatic framework. Ghazali's project — the *Ihya' 'ulum al-din* (Revival of the Religious Sciences) — was more ambitious: to revive the exoteric sciences (law, theology, jurisprudence) *through* Sufism, making Sufism the spiritual animating force for the entire Islamic civilizational structure.

In one of his last works, *O Youth*, Ghazali proposed that Sufism (euphemized as "the science of the states of the heart") was an *individual duty* (*fard 'ayn*) on Muslims, not merely a collective duty of sufficiency (*fard kifaya*). This was, in Mayer's words, "breathtakingly radical" — matched, however, by the *de facto* pervasion of Muslim society by organized Sufism through the expansion of the great orders (*turuq*) from the 12th century onward.

**Ash'arism and Sufism under the Seljuks**: The Seljuks promoted Ash'arism through the Nizamiyya colleges (Baghdad, Nishapur, Merv, etc.) while simultaneously endowing *khanqahs* for Sufi practice. Ash'arism had earlier been persecuted by the Mu'tazilite vizier Kunduri (until his death in 1063). After Kunduri, "Ash'arite Sufism was undoubtedly the main intellectual bequest of the Seljuks to Islam."

The continuity of this synthesis was embodied in figures like **Ruzbihan Baqli (d. 1209)**, whose theology was strongly Ash'arite (as seen in his credal work *Maslak al-tawhid*) even as he claimed personal visions of God, applying the Hanbalite-Ash'arite formula "without how" (*bila kayf*) to mystical experience; and **Abu Hafs al-Suhrawardi (d. 1234)**, whose work became the basic textbook of institutional Persianate Sufism while also defending Ash'arism against Hanbalism.

## The Transcendentalisation of Ash'arism in Sufism

Mayer traces a distinctive transformation: Ash'arite **occasionalism** (the world is made of atoms with no inherent power of duration, requiring God's direct creative act at every moment) was taken by Sufi thinkers from its original context (defending divine omnipotence against Mu'tazilite delegation theory) into a fully *ontological* register.

Ghazali, in his exegesis of Qur'an 28:88 ("Everything is perishing except His Face"), argues that created things are "perishing unceasingly and at every moment" — not at some particular moment, but *structurally*. The Ash'arite atomic substance, stripped of all inherent power by the kalam argument, is now stripped of all *existence* — leaving only God as the real ground of the cosmos. Ghazali: "the only existent is the Face of God" (*fa-yakunu'l-mawjudu wajha'llahi ta'ala faqat*).

**[[ibn-arabi]]** makes the same transition explicitly: the Ash'arites "are on the right lines in their doctrine. But they fall short in maintaining the theoretical distinction between accidents and substances within the cosmos. In fact, the whole cosmos is a 'sum of accidents' (*majmu' al-a'rad*), involving nothing substantial." God, not atoms, is the real ground of the cosmos — the Ash'arite occasionalism dissolves into what Ibn 'Arabi systematizes as *wahdat al-wujud* (Unity of Existence). This speculative development is treated more fully under [[philosophical-sufism]].

## Debates and Limits of the Rapprochement

The synthesis was never uncontested within Sufism itself. Major figures like 'Ala' al-Dawla Simnani (d. 1336) and Ahmad al-Sirhindi (d. 1624) believed that Ibn 'Arabi's *wahdat al-wujud* promoted antinomian spirituality by demolishing the creator–creature distinction on which worship and moral accountability depended. Their response was *wahdat al-shuhud* (subjective theomonism) — but even this was itself a speculative system, demonstrating that by the 13th century "there was no reverting to the pre-speculative Sufism of the classical period."

Ash'arism's early critics within Sufism included groups like the Karramiyya and the Salimiyya, who maintained their own alternative theological syntheses. And always, a significant current of Sufi sentiment — represented by the Malamatiyya and, in a different register, by Ibn 'Arabi's own apophatic tendencies — resisted systematic kalam formulation altogether, preferring allusion (*ishara*), paradox, and poetry to the more kataphatic approach of orthodox creeds.

## Historiography

- Louis Massignon's reconstruction of Hallaj and early Sufi-Mu'tazilite connections was foundational, though some specific claims have been revised.
- The "older generation of historians" who supposed that rigorous Shari'a observance was antithetical to Sufi spirituality (and vice versa) is explicitly corrected in chapter 12 of [[winter-cambridge-companion-islamic-theology-2008]] — mainstream Sufism was built *on* the law's foundations.
- Alexander D. Knysh's *Islamic Mysticism: A Short History* provides the broader narrative framework; studies by Hermann Landolt and William Chittick cover the speculative tradition.
- The question of Kalabadhi's influence on Avicenna's *Burhan al-Siddiqin* (raised by Mayer) remains a live scholarly question with significant implications for the history of Islamic philosophy.
