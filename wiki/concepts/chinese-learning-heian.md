# Chinese Learning in Heian Japan (Kanbun Culture)

The systematic assimilation of Chinese language, literature, history, and law by
the [[heian-period|Heian]] aristocracy — the intellectual infrastructure on which both
official government and the brilliant [[heian-literature|vernacular culture]] of the
period rested. Documented in detail in the [[cambridge-history-japan-v2-heian|Cambridge
History of Japan, Vol. 2]].

## The Medium: Kanbun

All official writing — government documents, legal codes, formal dedications, dynastic
histories — was conducted in **kanbun** (literary Chinese). Proficiency in Chinese
composition was the mark of a cultivated gentleman and a prerequisite for government
service. The ability to compose Chinese **shih** verse (kanshi) in correct tone patterns and
rhyme categories was expected at formal banquets and ceremonial occasions, though
high-ranking nobles often commissioned specialist literati to write on their behalf.

The gap between formal Chinese writing and everyday life was managed through
compendia. **Kuchizusami** ("Fun with Recitation," written 970 by Minamoto no
Tamenori for a seven-year-old Fujiwara heir) exemplifies the genre: a primer organized
under nineteen categories covering the three luminaries, the palace gates, government
offices, Chinese and Japanese history, ommyōdō (Taoist divination), Buddhism,
measurement tables, and a multiplication table. Tamenori's purpose was memorization
through song: "if he will sing these words out he will commit them to memory." The
work draws on Chinese encyclopedias (especially those of Po Chü-i) and on the
**Wamyo ruijū sho** (935, a Japanese-word dictionary-encyclopedia compiled at the
order of a princess by Minamoto no Shitago, 911–983).

For advanced learners, Tamenori's **Sezoku genbun** (a collection of maxims from
fifty-three Chinese books) and Fujiwara no Akihira's (989–1066) **Honchō monzui**
(a prestigious anthology of kanbun belles-lettres modeled on the T'ang-wen-ts'ui)
provided models of style. Honchō monzui became the canonical guide for later writers
of elegant Chinese prose and verse.

## The State Academy (Daigaku-ryō)

The institutional center of Chinese learning was the **Daigaku-ryō** ("Bureau of the
Great Learning"), commonly called the Academy, attached to the Ministry of Ceremonial.
Its formal subjects included:
- **Literature** (monjō): composition in Chinese; the most prestigious and most popular track
- **Law** (myōbō): study of the legal codes (ritsuryō)
- **Arithmetic** (san): mathematics
- **Calligraphy** (sho): Chinese script styles

The Academy flourished under **Emperor Saga** (r. 809–823) and reached its peak
under **Sugawara no Michizane** (845–903), whose private school reportedly enrolled
hundreds of students, of whom nearly a hundred gained Academy admission. The
hereditary scholarly families — above all the **Sugawara**, **Ōe**, and **Kiyohara** — served
as professors (hakase), transmitting their traditions in near-monopoly. A junior-level
institution called the **Kangakuin**, founded by Fujiwara no Fuyutsugu (775–826), and
analogous besso (student halls) attached to specific aristocratic houses, gradually
supplemented and in the late Heian period effectively eclipsed the Academy itself.

The Academy's decline was structural: the rise of the hereditary Fujiwara regency
diminished the utility of a Chinese education for political advancement, since birth
outweighed scholarly achievement in the allocation of high offices. After fires and
neglect in the late Heian, the Academy's main functions were absorbed by private
literati academies. One of its units, the **Shōgakuin** (attached to the southern part of
the grounds), saw its superintendency become hereditary in a Minamoto branch and
ultimately in the **Tokugawa** house — persisting as a title until the Meiji Restoration.

Kukai's **Shugei Shūchiin** ("Academy of Arts and Sciences"), founded shortly after
the Heian court's establishment, represents an idealistic outlier: a school open to poor
as well as wealthy students, combining Confucian and Buddhist instruction. It did not
survive long, but it illustrates the breadth of early Heian educational ambition.

## The Six National Histories (Rikkokushi)

Official dynastic historiography in the Chinese mode produced the **Six National
Histories** (Rikkokushi), compiled by imperial order between 720 and 901:

| Title | Coverage | Compiled |
|---|---|---|
| Nihon shoki | to 697 | 720 |
| Shoku Nihongi | 697–791 | 797 |
| Nihon kōki | 792–833 | 840 |
| Shoku Nihon kōki | 834–850 | 869 |
| Nihon Montoku tennō jitsuroku | 850–858 | 879 |
| Nihon sandai jitsuroku | 858–887 | 901 |

Compilation required the highest officials as nominal superintendents, with specialist
literati as the actual compilers. The histories are annals only — unlike Chinese dynastic
histories, they lack the biographical and thematic sections that make the Chinese model
so comprehensive. The **Nihon sandai jitsuroku** contains an extraordinary range of
material, including a report of a giant seen strolling in front of the Shishinden (the
"strangling ghost") — evidence that the compilers incorporated supernatural lore from
the daily records on which the histories were based.

A seventh National History was attempted under Emperor Suzaku (r. 930–946): the
**History Compilation Bureau** (Senkokushishō) was staffed from 936 through the 950s,
but the project produced only a draft or less, its elderly directors lacking competent
assistants. The **cessation of the National Histories after 901** is symptomatic of
the broader cultural shift in which Chinese-style official endeavors gave way to the
flourishing of native literary culture.

The material in the National Histories was later rearranged thematically by
**Sugawara no Michizane** in the **Ruiju kokushi** (892), which gathered entries on the
same topic from all six histories — a reference tool that made the material accessible
to later scholars and poets. Michizane's achievement illustrates the mature phase of
Chinese learning: the received tradition was not merely imitated but reorganized and
used creatively.

## The Legal Codes (Ritsuryō)

Alongside the histories, the [[ritsuryo-state|ritsuryō]] legal codes were canonical texts
in Chinese. The **administrative code** (ryō) and **criminal code** (ritsu) were organized
following T'ang Chinese models, including the "eight kinds of oppression" and the six
"deliberations" that required amelioration of punishment based on personal status.
Tamenori's Kuchizusami includes a brief history of imperial interest in legal compilation
beginning with the Seventeen Articles of Shōtoku Taishi, and enumerates the major
divisions of both codes — evidence that the law was treated as a branch of learning
alongside literature and history.

## Historiography

The Cambridge History of Japan, Vol. 2, approaches this material through close reading
of the educational compendia themselves (especially Kuchizusami and Sezoku genbun),
using them as windows into what Heian aristocrats considered essential knowledge. The
chapter emphasizes that the Heian mind was "quite incurious about the natural world"
— science for its own sake was absent; knowledge was useful, social, magical, or
literary. The collection in the Bibliotheca Alexandrina is strong for this period through
the CHJ series and Sansom's cultural history; primary source translations (Nihon shoki,
etc.) are available for the earlier histories.
