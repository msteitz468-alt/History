# The Griot and Oral Tradition

The **griot** (Mande *jeli*, pl. *jeliw*) is the West African hereditary bard, genealogist, praise-singer,
and **keeper of history** — and, more broadly, the human institution through which much of sub-Saharan
Africa's pre-literate past was preserved. Oral tradition is therefore a **first-class historical source**
for regions and periods with few written records, and one of the defining methodological problems of
African history. Documented for the wiki through **[[niane-sundiata|*Sundiata*]]**, itself a transcription
of a griot's performance.

## The institution

Among the Mande, the *jeliw* form an endogamous occupational caste bound to noble lineages. They preserve
and perform **genealogies, praise-poems, and epics** (above all the [[sundiata-keita|Sundiata]] epic) in
*kuma koro*, "ancient speech," accompanying themselves on instruments (the *kora*, the *balafon*). They are
also **political actors** — counsellors, spokesmen, diplomats, and the social memory that legitimizes
rulers by tying them to the founding ancestors. As the griot Mamadou Kouyaté declares in *Sundiata*: "we
are vessels of speech, the memory of mankind."

## As historical evidence (emic and etic)

- **Emic:** within Mande society the griot's word *is* history — authoritative, sacred, and the living
  charter of identity. To the Mande, to dismiss the Sundiata tradition as fiction is to deny the reality of
  their own culture (Conrad's argument on [[mali-empire]]).
- **Etic:** for the modern historian, oral tradition (theorized by **Jan Vansina**) carries genuine
  information about the past but is **formulaic, telescoped, genealogically structured, and shaped by the
  performer's caste and purpose** — reliable, linguists caution, only two or three centuries deep, and
  unreliable for literal chronology. It is strongest as evidence of **social structure, values, memory and
  identity**, and must be triangulated with archaeology and external (Arabic, European) texts.

## The asymmetry: states vs. stateless societies (Horton)

[[ajayi-crowder-history-west-africa-v1-1971|*History of West Africa* Vol. I]] (Robin
Horton, Ch. 3) draws out a consequence that shapes the whole archive of African history:
**oral tradition is far richer for states than for [[stateless-societies|stateless
societies]].** The great states keep **specialist court historians** (the griots), so
their traditions can run 200, 500, even 1,000 years deep; stateless societies, lacking
that office, have traditions "nothing like as deep, as rich, or as continuous." Horton is
also sharply sceptical of a common method: collecting *all* variant versions of a
tradition and extracting a "common denominator" gets one no nearer historical truth — his
Niger Delta village whose variants all reduce to "seven men who came down from the sky"
makes the point. His conclusion is not to discard oral tradition (he collected it himself)
but to insist it be **triangulated** with linguistic maps, culture-trait maps, and
archaeology. This asymmetry is itself a **source bias**: because chroniclers and griots
alike gravitated to the commercial states, the historical record over-represents states
and under-represents the millions who lived statelessly.

## Significance and critique (contested)

Oral tradition made possible the **decolonization of African history** — recovering an indigenous past
against the colonial claim that Africa "had no history." It anchors the UNESCO *General History of Africa*
project. The **contest** is over its evidentiary limits: how far episodes like Sundiata's life can be
treated as fact, how much later concerns reshape the "remembered" past, and how to weigh oral against
documentary sources where they conflict.
