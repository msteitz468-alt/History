# Logistics and Supply (Clausewitz)

In Book Five of *On War*, Carl von Clausewitz treats **maintenance and supply** (*Unterhalt und Verpflegung*) not merely as an administrative or bureaucratic task, but as a critical strategic constraint that directly dictates the movement, speed, and operational freedom of armies. 

Clausewitz’s core principle is that **supply is a condition of war, and must never be treated as its object** (Book Five, Chapter Fourteen).

---

## The Four Methods of Provisioning

Clausewitz outlines four methods for provisioning troops in the field, which are usually used in combination:

1. **Supplies furnished by local households (Billeting):** The most natural method for short periods. A force of 150,000 men can live off local households in a populated area for a day or two without depots.
2. **Requisition by the troops themselves:** Individual units seize what they need directly. Highly flexible but leads to disorder if sustained.
3. **General Requisition:** The army demands supplies through local civil authorities, who distribute the burden equitably. This is the most effective way to live off the land for weeks or months.
4. **Depots (Magazines):** Storing massive amounts of grain and forage in fortified or secure locations. While necessary for long-term campaigns, a rigid reliance on depots curtails an army's freedom of action.

---

## Requisitions vs. Depots: The Revolutionary Transformation

Clausewitz historicizes the shift from the rigid eighteenth-century depot system to the French Revolutionary method of requisitioning. Under the old system, armies were tied to depots and the effective range of transport: " rations were cut to the barest minimum... and movement was much more limited." 

Napoleon and the French Revolutionary generals revolutionized logistics by forcing armies to live off the land:
> "They demonstrated that maintenance had to be regarded as a condition of war, and never as its object... Warfare based on requisition and local sources of supply is so superior to the kind that relies on depots, that the two no longer seem to be the same instrument... Requisitioning would automatically impose itself [in modern war]."

Living off the land granted modern armies the "utmost liberty" and a "degree of energy in war that otherwise would have been inconceivable."

---

## Base of Operations and Lines of Communication

Clausewitz is highly critical of the geometric theories of strategy popular in his day (such as Antoine-Henri Jomini’s), which treated the "base of operations" as a simple line or angle on a map. Clausewitz calls this "nothing but a toy... that rests on a series of substitutions at the expense of truth."

Instead, he describes the base as a spatial, three-tiered infrastructure:
1. Local resources immediately available to the army.
2. Depots established in the rear.
3. The wider country from which reinforcements and supplies are drawn.

The **lines of communication** are the "arteries" connecting the army to this base: "The only true lines of communication are those on which depots and hospitals, relay points and postal services, as well as commandants, field police and garrisons have been installed."

---

## Logistics and the Strain of Marches

Clausewitz notes that marches are the primary source of physical attrition: "Modern armies have long been accustomed to consider a fifteen-mile march as a day’s work." However, in large-scale strategic operations, this must be reduced to an average of **ten miles per day** to allow for rest days, during which "necessary repairs and maintenance can be carried out." Without these rest days, friction quickly destroys the physical cohesion of the army.
