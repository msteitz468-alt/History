## Definition

Triconfessional society is an analytical framework for understanding German religious and political history in which Protestantism, Catholicism, and Judaism are treated as three mutually constitutive communities whose histories are inseparable. Per George S. Williamson (ch. 10 of [[smith-oxford-modern-german-history-2011]]): "any account of the religious history of Germany must necessarily be 'triconfessional' in its approach, taking into account the experiences of Protestants, Catholics, and Jews, while exploring the dialectical relationships among them."

The framework insists that the transformations of each community — through Enlightenment, revival, political mobilization, emancipation struggles — "tended to be interpreted through the lens of confessional identity, a situation that reinforced antagonisms among Protestants, Catholics, and Jews while obscuring the degree to which theirs was, in fact, a 'shared history.'"

## Historical Background: The Westphalian Framework

The Peace of Westphalia (1648) established the structural baseline. Three confessions were legally recognized within the Holy Roman Empire: Lutheran, Calvinist (Reformed), and Catholic. Jews were entirely outside this system, negotiating individually for residential rights and subject to periodic violence. The principle of confessional parity between the Christian communities was established; the Holy Roman Empire exhibited "a degree of religious co-existence that, in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries at least, would have been unimaginable in either Great Britain or France."

Religious geography was materially visible: confessional differences were "immediately perceptible to a traveler thanks to contrasting styles of architecture, monuments, and dress." Protestant territories stretched from Württemberg through Franconia, Thuringia, Saxony to Prussia and Hanover; the ecclesiastical territories of the Reichskirche (archbishoprics of Trier, Cologne, Mainz; bishoprics of Münster, Paderborn, Würzburg) interrupted this bloc; dozens of smaller territories created further complexity.

## The Aufklärung and Confessional Transformation

All three communities underwent parallel but distinct Enlightenment transformations in the eighteenth century:

**Protestant Enlightenment:** University of Halle (founded 1694 as Pietist bastion) became home to key Protestant Aufklärer: Christian Thomasius (jurist), Christian Wolff (philosopher), Siegmund Jacob Baumgarten (theologian). Pietism and early Enlightenment shared a "regeneration" mission for individuals and civil society. By the 1760s, Neologists (led by Johann Salomo Semler) drew a sharp distinction between "church religion" (inherited dogma and ritual) and "private religion" (personal conviction and practical morality). The long-term effect: urban educated elites (especially men) distanced from formal church life; smaller-town and rural parishioners continued attending services. A split within Protestantism between educated secular culture and popular piety.

**Catholic Enlightenment:** Often treated as a "stepchild of historiography" due to the assumption that Catholicism and Enlightenment were fundamentally incompatible. But Habsburg evidence shows reform absolutism drawing on Jansenist theology. Maria Theresa slashed religious holidays and pilgrimages, pushed toward Gallican church-state model, ended Jesuit monopoly over theological education. Joseph II dissolved 800 Catholic monasteries, forbade contact with Rome, moved clerical education to state seminaries, and issued toleration patents for Jews, Protestants, and Greek Orthodox. "Although no other German prince went as far as Joseph II on the question of toleration, several Catholic rulers … adopted various aspects of the Austrian reformist agenda."

**Haskalah (Jewish Enlightenment):** See [[german-jewish-emancipation]] for detail. Moses Mendelssohn attempted to rejuvenate Judaism through contact with European philosophy while insisting on continued validity of Jewish law and Jewish particularity. The Haskalah created a Hebrew-language public sphere but also opened paths toward greater accommodation with gentile culture — in some cases culminating in conversion.

**Comparative observation:** All three Enlightenments involved reforming elites challenging established religious authority structures, promoting practical over ceremonial religion, and seeking accommodation with secular state power. All three faced "anti-enlightenment" revivals after 1815.

## The French Revolution and Confessional Reorganization

The Revolution's religious impact in Germany was dual:

1. **Institutional destruction:** Reichsdeputationshauptschluss (1803) resulted in secularization of three archbishoprics (Mainz, Trier, Cologne), 19 bishoprics, and 44 abbeys; dissolution of hundreds of monasteries. The elimination of the Reichskirche and collapse of the Holy Roman Empire (1806) created an "intensified intermingling of the confessions" — Catholics under Protestant rulers (Prussia and Baden), Protestants under Bavarian Catholic crown.

2. **Popular religious reaction:** "The sheer unpredictability of war served as a stark reminder of the old theological dictum that humans are not the masters of their own destinies." The 1790s saw upsurge in popular piety across all communities: Catholic pilgrimages restored (in defiance of government and church authorities); Württemberg Pietists identified Napoleon with Apollyon the Destroyer; Tsar Alexander with the Revelation judge on a white horse. In 1801, a group of 20 Pietists attempted to emigrate to the Holy Land; over the following two decades thousands emigrated to southern Russia in search of religious freedom.

## Post-1815 Revival Movements

One of the most distinctive features of post-Napoleonic European life was "a widespread and, to some degree, spontaneous outpouring of Christian religious engagement and activity."

**Protestant Awakening (Erweckungsbewegung):** Part of a broader revival across Germany, Switzerland, Scandinavia, Netherlands, Britain, and USA. In Germany: popular Pietistic movement drawing from farmers, craftsmen, traders, Moravian missionaries, wandering preachers. In Prussia: significant support from aristocrats and officers hostile to centralizing Stein-Hardenberg reforms and to Friedrich Wilhelm III's forced union of Lutheran and Reformed churches (1817). The Awakening operated outside regular church liturgy — conventicles, Bible study, "hours of edification" — organized by laypeople. It mobilized media aggressively: cheap Bibles distributed by Bible societies (British and Foreign Bible Society; two dozen German societies founded 1810s–1820s); overseas missions (Basel Missionary Society, 1815; Berlin, Leipzig, Bremen societies). Ernst Wilhelm Hengstenberg's Evangelische Kirchenzeitung (1827) became the vehicle for Lutheran orthodoxy attacking rationalism — "tabloid-style reports designed to expose the baseness and immorality of rationalism."

**Catholic Ultramontane Revival:** Moved from ecumenical intellectual awakening (1800s–1830s) to strictly confessional mass movement (1840s–1871). Key features: large-scale Marian pilgrimages (hundreds of thousands of participants); clerically led religious associations organized by occupational group; missions by Redemptorists, Jesuits, Franciscans causing "extraordinary scenes" of conversion, reconciliation, restoration of stolen goods. The revival was enabled by Prussian and Bavarian governments granting Catholic clergy a free hand after 1848 in exchange for counter-revolutionary political support. Jonathan Sperber's Popular Catholicism in Nineteenth-Century Germany (1984) is the standard account.

**Enlightened-Bourgeois Piety:** Alongside revivalism, a quieter bourgeois religiosity centered on domestic life and family (Heinrich Zschokke's Hours of Devotion, 1809–1816: 27 editions). This produced a "familiarization" of religion (Rebekka Habermas) and a "feminization" — religion migrating from public ritual to family celebration, figured as feminine. Parallel process in Judaism: Benjamin Maria Baader documents increased synagogue role for women; Fanny Neuda's 1855 Jewish version of Hours of Devotion.

## Confessional and Theological Conflicts

**Olaf Blaschke's "Second Confessional Era" thesis:** The nineteenth century was not a period of secularization but of "the formation and mobilization of powerful confessional cultures in Germany, which clashed repeatedly and frequently in the years 1815–1914." Evidence: Ultramontane expansion + militant liberal Protestantism. But Blaschke's argument is "undercut somewhat" by intra-confessional divisions within Protestantism and Judaism that Kulturkampf rhetoric tended to mask, and by the episodic rather than continuous nature of confessional conflict.

**Cologne Affair (1837):** Paradigm case of confessional-political conflict. Prussia required children of mixed marriages to be raised in the father's faith. Archbishop Clemens August von Droste-Vischering invalidated prior compromise agreements; Prussia arrested and imprisoned him. The affair mobilized Catholic opinion nationally and demonstrated how confessional conflict could articulate overlapping social and political grievances. Joseph Görres's inflammatory response framed it in terms of Catholic honor and sexual violation: "under no circumstances can the church be forced to bear mongrel bastards for another confession." Dagmar Herzog shows the Cologne Controversy became simultaneously a church-state issue and a debate about the bourgeois family.

**Intra-confessional splits (1840s):**
- "Friends of Light" (Protestant): breakaway congregations after crackdowns on rationalist clergy
- "German Catholics" (Deutschkatholiken): liberal Catholics opposed to Ultramontanism; gathered Catholic and Protestant radicals attracting Feuerbach's "religion of love," working-class identification, ecumenicism
- Reform Judaism divisions: communities in Breslau, Hamburg, Berlin breaking from Orthodoxy; radical Association for Reform in Berlin called for rejection of Jewish ritual laws and Sunday Sabbath

## Anti-Jewish Violence and the Emancipation Struggle

Anti-Jewish violence recurred at moments of political and economic crisis:
- **Hep-Hep riots (1819):** Triggered in Würzburg by attempt to implement Bavaria's relatively liberal Jewish law; spread through German cities. Debate: Stefan Rohrbacher emphasizes ideological hostility to emancipation; Manfred Gailus stresses bread-riot targeting of Jewish merchants identified as "profiteers" in economic crisis.
- **Violent episodes in 1830 and 1848–1849:** Again correlate with economic stress. 1849 Bavarian petition drive against Jewish emancipation (organized by conservative Catholic clergy) successfully persuaded Upper House to reject legislation already passed by Lower House.

Whatever their proximate causes, these conflicts "tapped into established narratives about Jewish 'greed,' 'usury,' and 'betrayal,' which persisted within large elements of the Christian population and shaped the understanding of even the most well-educated Germans."

## Resolution and Aftermath

Political unification (1871) under Prussian (Protestant) auspices did not overcome confessional divisions. The Kulturkampf (1870s) was motivated not simply by Protestant fear of Catholic political power but by liberal anxieties about mass democracy and women's activism, by opposition to Jesuit missions, and by a long history of conflict with conservative Catholic clergy dating to the 1840s. By the 1890s, some Protestant-Catholic accommodation developed on the basis of shared nationality and "Christian state" identity, partly in response to the "fourth confession" of avowedly atheist socialism — which many Christians conflated with the "third confession" of Judaism.

"Since the eighteenth century, Protestantism, Catholicism, and Judaism had undergone a series of dramatic transformations, bringing forth new forms of piety and new forms of self-understanding even as older beliefs and institutions were challenged or destroyed. What remained unchanged, however, was a tendency among Germans to define their religious situation in relation to that of their confessional rivals."

## Contestation

The triconfessional framework itself is contested. Critics note it can overstate Jewish integration into German political culture (given Jews' exclusion from civic life through most of this period) and understate the asymmetry between the three communities' power positions. The "shared history" framing risks obscuring the structural domination that made Jewish participation precarious. Blaschke's second confessional era thesis has generated scholarly debate about whether to emphasize re-confessionalization or ongoing secularization as the primary trend.
