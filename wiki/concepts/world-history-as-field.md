## Definition and Scope

World history is the branch of historical scholarship that takes as its subject
not a single community, nation, or civilization, but connections, comparisons,
networks, and systems that cross those boundaries. As Jerry Bentley put it,
the new world history "has focused attention on comparisons, connections,
networks, and systems rather than the experiences of individual communities or
discrete societies."

Marnie Hughes-Warrington (Ch. 2) argues that all history is in some sense world
history: every history depends on imagining a coherent and meaningful "world"
of some kind. World histories vary in narrative style, structure, and spatio-
temporal scope, and travel under many labels: universal history, ecumenical
history, comparative history, world-systems history, macrohistory, transnational
history, big history, global history. What they share is the explicit purpose of
constructing a meaningful "world" — the degree to which that purpose is made
explicit is what distinguishes world history from other history.

## The Ancient and Global Roots of World History

A central argument of CWH Vol. I, Part I is that world-historical thinking is
**not** a modern Western invention:

- **Indigenous and oral traditions**: Australian Aboriginal songlines and
  legends dealt with the history of the known world and its peoples; the Arokin,
  a professional group in the Yoruba kingdoms of West Africa, were tasked with
  remembering and recounting history orally. Sachsenmaier (Ch. 3) stresses that
  there was no categorical divide between oral and written world history — Sub-
  Saharan oral traditions fed into Arabic written scholarship and vice versa.
- **Greek**: Herodotus (c. 484–425 BCE), often called "Father of History,"
  depicted different cultures, though his narrative was structured by the
  Greek-barbarian (polis-tyranny) dichotomy. Universal history as a genre
  is usually credited to Ephorus (c. 405–330 BCE), shaped by the cosmopolitanism
  following Alexander's conquests. See [[reliability-of-herodotus]].
- **Chinese**: Sima Qian (c. 145–90 BCE) synthesized history in the *Shiji*
  (*Records of the Grand Historian*), 130 chapters, structured around the
  Middle Kingdom. The four-part division of official Chinese histories (annals,
  tables, treatises, biographies) persisted from the *Shiji* to the *Qingshi gao*
  (Draft History of the Qing, 1928).
- **Islamic**: Abu Ja'far al-Tabari (c. 839–923) used Biblical, Greek, Roman, and
  Persian sources to present history as an unbroken process of transmission.
  Universal-historical status often derived from construction out of *isnads*
  (unbroken chains of transmission). Ibn Khaldun (1332–1406), in the *Muqaddimah*
  and *Kitab al-'Ibar*, combined philosophy, geography, and social theory,
  developing cyclical theories of the rise and fall of powers that anticipate
  modern sociology.
- **Christian**: Eusebius (d. 340), Orosius (d. 417), and Otto of Freising
  (d. 1158) wrote universal histories on biblical timelines (creation, deluge,
  incarnation as periodization markers).

## Major Developmental Phases (as a modern field)

1. **Enlightenment universal history**: George Sale's 65-volume *Universal
   History* (1747–68); Bossuet's *Discourse on Universal History* (1681).
   Early modern European expansion exposed non-European chronologies (Chinese,
   Indian) that ran counter to biblical periodization, generating "history wars."
2. **19th-century professionalization and Eurocentrism**: The rise of the nation
   state as the frame for professional history pushed world history into disfavor.
   Hegel, Marx, and Weber placed Europe at the center of planetary visions.
3. **20th-century universalist responses**: Spengler (*Decline of the West*),
   Toynbee (*A Study of History*, civilizations), William H. McNeill (*The Rise
   of the West*, 1963; later emphasizing connections and webs of interaction).
4. **Modernization, dependency, world-systems**: Rostow and modernization
   theory; countered by dependency theory (Frank) and Immanuel Wallerstein's
   world-systems analysis (core/periphery/semi-periphery; *The Modern World
   System*, 1974–89). See [[divergence-convergence]].
5. **The relational/postcolonial turn**: Said's *Orientalism* (1978); Chakrabarty's
   *Provincializing Europe* (2000); transnational, new imperial, and gender world
   histories. See [[eurocentrism]] and [[gender-history]].
6. **Big History and global history**: Christian's [[big-history]]; the *Journal
   of World History* (1990), *Journal of Global History* (2005).

## Anthropology and World History (Goody, Ch. 11)

Jack Goody argues anthropology and world history have much to offer each other.
Both aspired to an ecumenical understanding of humanity but diverged
methodologically: history favored written sources, anthropology oral sources
and fieldwork. Anthropology helped world history escape Eurocentric assumptions —
e.g., showing that consultative/democratic forms of government and early forms
of capitalism are ancient and global, not uniquely modern or Western. Joseph
Needham's work on Chinese science undermined assumptions of Western
exceptionalism. Eric Wolf's *Europe and the People Without History* (1982)
modeled the systematic incorporation of non-literate societies into global history.

## Questions of Method (CWH VI)

[[bentley-cwh-v6-2015|CWH VI]] closes with two "questions of method" chapters that
sharpen this field's central problems. **Sanjay Subrahmanyam** (see
[[global-historiography]]) extends the "world history is nothing new" insight into
the early modern era, showing that **large-scale and comparative history writing
was a global practice** — Polybius and Sima Qian, the Arabic/Persian *tarikh*, the
rich Mughal historiographical field — long before nineteenth-century Europeans
claimed critical history as their own. **Carlo Ginzburg** (see [[microhistory]])
confronts the field's besetting tension between **scale and evidence**: against a
global history that risks dissolving into ethnocentric generalization, he argues
that **microhistory is an indispensable tool of world history**, since the intensive
reading of a single fragment can ground and illuminate the largest connections.
Together the two chapters reframe world history's method as a problem of
**connection and scale** rather than of coverage — consonant with the volume's
running insistence (against [[eurocentrism|Eurocentrism]]) that the impulse to
write the world is ancient and global.

## Emic vs. Etic

"World history" as a self-conscious disciplinary label is etic and modern. But
the underlying impulse — to construct a meaningful account of the world beyond
one's own community — is emic to many cultures and very ancient. This is the
central insight of Part I: world history is "nothing new."

## Limitations and Critiques

- World/global history remains overwhelmingly Anglophone and institutionally
  concentrated in the US and UK — a Eurocentrism that persists even in attempts
  to escape it (see [[eurocentrism]]).
- Large-scale synthesis risks erasing the specificity of local experience
  (the periodization problem; see [[periodization-world-history]]).
- Postmodern/postcolonial critics (Guha, Chakrabarty) argue the very concepts,
  periodization, and structure of world history can mask the historical
  activities of those "outside" the West.
