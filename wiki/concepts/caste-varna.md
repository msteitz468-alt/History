# Caste (Varna and Jati)

## Overview

**Caste** is the hereditary, hierarchical, endogamous system of social ordering that became,
in [[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|CWH IV]]'s words (Kaul, Ch 18), "the hallmark of social
organization in South Asia." It has two interlocking levels: the fourfold **varna** (the
ideal-typical ranked classes) and the proliferating **jati** (the actual birth-groups of
hereditary occupation and endogamy).

## Varna

The first reference to the fourfold **varna** appears in the **Later Vedic** period (see
[[vedic-period]]), in descending ritual status:

1. **Brahmana** — priests and ritual specialists.
2. **Kshatriya** — warriors and rulers.
3. **Vaishya** — traders and agriculturalists.
4. **Shudra** — menial labourers.

The order fused a **crystallization of occupational specialization** with strong "accents of
**ritual purity and hierarchy**." Later Vedic literature (the *Purusha Sukta*, Rig Veda
X.90) attributed the four to divine origin — emerging from the body of the Primeval Man — but
historians read varna as "a consciously worked out structure to **define and limit access to
resources and power**" among the diverse groups the expanding Vedic society encountered and
assimilated.

## Jati and Early Medieval Elaboration

Beneath varna proliferated the **jati** — the many real birth-groups defined by hereditary
occupation and **endogamy**. CWH IV stresses that the **early medieval period** (c. 300–900
CE) saw caste acquire "tremendous complexity": a proliferation of jatis, greater
**codification of caste law** in legal treatises, and (via the spread of state society and
**land grants**) the **acculturating** absorption of new regions and groups into the
brahmanical fold and caste society.

## Thapar: varna as theory, jati as practice; and the colour origin

[[thapar-history-of-india-v1-1966|Thapar]] (Vol. I) reconstructs caste's **[[vedic-period|Vedic]]
genesis** and presses the varna/jati distinction analytically. The Aryans arrived with only **three
classes** and "no consciousness of caste" — professions were not hereditary, and there were no
commensality or marriage taboos ("a bard am I, my father is a leech and my mother grinds corn"). The
**first step toward caste** was the exclusion of the conquered **Dasas** as beyond the social pale —
a distinction "largely that of colour" (the very word ***varna* means colour**), hardening into the
fourth (shudra) class. Thapar argues the **first three varnas were a theoretical brahmanical
framework** into which professions were arranged and rationalised (mixing of castes "explained" new
groups), while the **fourth was based on race *and* occupation**.

Her decisive point: **varna was an over-arching theory; *jati* (sub-caste) governed actual life.**
The system's continuance depended "not on the four-fold division but on the vast network of
sub-castes," tied to occupation and economic interdependence — so that an **individual** could not
move up, but a **whole jati** could (by collectively changing location and work), and each **new
ethnic group** entering India was absorbed as a fresh sub-caste. The doctrine of **Karma** then
furnished caste a philosophical justification (birth-rank as the fruit of past action), and
**Dharma** ("natural law" = the social/caste order) its sanction. Thapar also makes a
**historiographical** argument: caste localised authority in sub-caste relationships and village/guild
institutions, so that the supposed "Oriental despotism" of Indian kings was in fact checked — Indian
political power cannot be understood without analysing caste.

## Avari (2007) — Varna Origins and Social Impact

[[avari-india-ancient-past-2007|Avari]] (*India: The Ancient Past*, Ch. 4) treats the varna
system's emergence as something "quite novel, extraordinary and most negative in its social
impact," in contrast to what preceded it. The Iranian Aryans (closely associated with the
Rig-Vedic Aryans) practised a **threefold** division of society — priests, rulers, and producers
— that was common across the Indo-Iranian world. What was distinctive in Indo-Aryan India was
the split of the "producers" into two groups: free peasants and traders (vaishya) and a degraded
fourth class of slaves, labourers, and artisans (shudra). This resulted in a **fourfold** system
rigidly "compartmentalised on the basis of a religiously inculcated system of colour bar, known
as Varnashrama Dharma."

Avari is explicit that the division was **not** primarily between Aryan and pre-Aryan peoples.
"The main reason why many pre-Aryans would have been on the bottom rungs was that they were the
main craftsmen and workers with skills; but those very skills of menial and manual work were
derided on grounds of ritual impurity by the learned and the powerful, who could be either Aryan
or pre-Aryan." The "greatest stigma was attached to pollution of the upper castes by the lower."

The **textual sanction** for the fourfold system appears in the Purusha hymn (Rig-Veda Book X,
90.11–12), a later addition to the Rig-Veda:
> "The Brahman was his mouth, of both his arms was the Rajanya made. / His thighs became the
> Vaisya, from his feet the Sudra was produced."

Avari calls this "a pity, because caste is thereby legitimised by so-called religious sanction."
Since the Vedic texts were "revised by the learned brahmans, who were in close alliance with the
kshatriyas, there is a danger that the inviolate caste system as explained in the texts is a
particular form of ideological construct." He also notes that gotra — originally meaning those
who herded their cows in the same cowshed (and hence members of the same collective) — "later
became part of caste terminology, meaning a descent from a common ancestor."

On the varna/jati distinction: Avari uses "caste" and "varna" somewhat interchangeably in his
account of the Vedic period, discussing the fourfold varna hierarchy (brahman, kshatriya, vaishya,
shudra) as the operative system. He does not develop the jati/sub-caste proliferation as a
distinct analytical framework in these chapters; for that distinction see Thapar's treatment
elsewhere in this page.

### Challenges from Buddhism and Jainism

The varna system was directly challenged by the heterodox traditions that emerged from c. 600 BCE.
Both Mahavira and the Buddha "championed non-violence and a sort of primitive/early egalitarianism"
and "incessantly conveyed the message to their followers that the shudras had every right to be
teachers or monks, and there should be no discrimination in their treatment in the monasteries."
The Buddhist Sutta Nipata encapsulated the challenge: "No Brahman is such by birth. No outcaste is
such by birth. An outcaste is such by his deeds. A Brahman is such by his deeds." Most heterodox
activity arose in the **eastern clan states** (areas of modern Bihar, the Nepal border) where
Brahmanical authority was weakest and "among many ethnic groups there was considerable contempt for
Brahmanic sacrifices and caste rigidities."

The urban merchant class (vaishya) also chafed at caste restrictions: "Their newly found wealth
gave them the confidence to ignore any humiliation attached to their third place in the ritual
ranking within the caste system. They were irked by obsolete restrictions not just in trade but in
religion too."

## Significance

Caste structured South Asian society for more than two millennia, intertwining ritual,
economy, and political legitimation. CWH IV treats it not as a static essence but as a
**historical product** of state formation, surplus generation, and the incorporation of new
peoples — a recurring instrument by which elites organized an expanding, diverse social
order.

## Limitations and Critiques

The system's **emic** self-presentation (divine, fixed, fourfold) diverges sharply from its
**etic** analysis (fluid, jati-based, historically constructed for resource control). The
relationship between the tidy varna model and the messy jati reality, and the degree of
mobility within the system, remain debated.
