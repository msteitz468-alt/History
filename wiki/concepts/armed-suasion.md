# Armed Suasion

Armed suasion (in Edward N. Luttwak's terminology) is the shaping of other actors' behavior through the threat or latent presence of military force, even when that force is not actively employed in combat. It encompasses both negative suasion (deterrence: discouraging hostile acts) and positive suasion (compellence: persuading or coercing desired behavior). It operates in the horizontal dimension of strategy (adversary perceptions and reactions) and is conditioned throughout by the paradoxical logic of strategy.

## Definition and Distinctions
Armed suasion arises whenever governments choose to see the military strength of others as reassuring (or threatening) and adjust their actions accordingly. It is "a conflictual phenomenon, whose existence derives entirely from the possibility of war, even if most improbable."

It is distinct from generic "administration" or preparations for war. Aims of suasion reflect particular perceptions of specific others' policies and military structures. "Latent suasion" occurs without conscious intent.

Positive suasion (compellence) is generally more difficult than negative (deterrence).

## Paradoxical Logic in Armed Suasion
Because it is relational and conflict-conditioned, armed suasion is governed by the paradoxical logic (reversals, more can be less, action provokes counter-reaction).

- Static: Less extreme threats can be more effective because more plausible. Excessive strength can be circumvented or attacked (the "frustrated aggressor" turns to subversion or indirect means).
- Dynamic: Effective dissuasion can lead to circumvention (Soviet post-1945 direct force in Eastern Europe limited → subversion and client wars). Nuclear great-power dissuasion was "triumphant" in preventing direct war but coincided with prevalence of ~144 small-power/proxy wars 1948–1991 (many intense).

"Second-strike" capability as paradoxical remedy: vulnerable forces invite attack; only post-attack strength can dissuade. Pearl Harbor as classic: forward deployment intended as dissuasion invited the very attack it was meant to prevent (once Japan was deprived of other choices by embargo).

## Historical Patterns and Examples
- **Equality of results**: Successful defense by war (Korea 1950–53) vs. protection by armed dissuasion (post-armistice). Cost difference huge; outcome similar.
- **Hitler**: Won Czechoslovakia entirely by suasion (no fight); had to fight for Poland. Aftermath identical (conquest).
- **Romans**: Fought two centuries for Carthage and Iberia but obtained Hellenistic lands largely by intimidation after one battle.
- **Nuclear era**: Unprecedented great-power peace; proxy/small wars as circumvention. "Excessive" destructiveness limits usability to core interests.
- **Postheroic limits** (low-birth-rate advanced societies): Casualty aversion ("mammismo") shrinks the domain where armed suasion or war is politically tolerable. Only remote bombardment (no ground troops at risk) remains broadly acceptable. Examples: US abandonment after 18 dead in Somalia (1993); cancelled plans to seize Karadzic/Mladic despite overwhelming force (fear of retaliation on peacekeepers); Soviet minimalism in Afghanistan; British/French reluctance in Yugoslavia.

## Harmony with Other Dimensions
Armed suasion works best when harmonized with grand strategy (political objectives, alliances, economic tools) and lower levels. Disharmony (e.g., military posture contradicting diplomatic aims) produces reversal or circumvention.

## Historiography
Luttwak treats armed suasion as a normal, pervasive instrument of statecraft (not an exotic Cold War invention). It challenges purely kinetic or "use of force" definitions of strategy. Critiques may note difficulty of measuring "latent" effects or cultural variation in perceptions of threat/reassurance. Complements deterrence theory while embedding it in the broader paradoxical logic and levels framework.
