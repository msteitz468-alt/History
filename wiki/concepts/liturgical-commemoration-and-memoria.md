# Liturgical Commemoration and Memoria

**Naming the living and the dead aloud at Mass, binding a community across the boundary of death.** Els
Rose's chapter (Oxford Handbook, Ch. 43) examines the Merovingian liturgy of commemoration — above all the
**collectio post nomina**, the prayer following the public reading of names at the offertory — as a ritual
that "literally intertwines (*texuit*) the living and the dead to one common destiny."

## Definition and Scope

The reading of names — of living and dead members of the eucharistic community — was "a fixed feature of
every Merovingian mass." The primary source is the **Missale Gothicum** (Vat. Reg. lat. 317), made in
Burgundy in the final decades of the seventh century, probably for the cathedral community of Autun, which
preserves 69 unique *post nomina* prayers — the core witness to Gallican commemorative practice (see
[[merovingian-liturgy-and-lay-religion]]).

## A Heavenly Citizenship in Roman Civic Vocabulary

Rose shows how Roman civic language was repurposed for the heavenly community: *exterum* (foreigners) for
those excluded from the eucharistic body; *recensiri* (to enumerate, from the Roman censor) and *conscribere*
(as in *patres conscripti*) for the roll of heavenly citizens; God as heavenly *censor* — "*in medio
Hierusalem in congregacione sanctorum haec nomina… recensiri*." Membership and exclusion were real social
mechanisms: deletion from the diptychs amounted to excommunication, and the possessed (*energumeni*) were
barred from the recitation. The practice had Greek, Roman, and Jewish antecedents — baptism, in John
Chrysostom's phrase, inscribing one as a "citizen in heaven."

## Reform of the Living, and Royal Integration

The commemorative liturgy was also understood to **reform the living**: the Gothic Missal prays that the
sacrifices "be as beneficial to the correction (*ad emendationem*) of the living as they help the dying to
find rest." Royal authority was woven in — Guntram's edict (10 November 585) summoned the faithful to church
on Sundays and tied *emendatio* to "peace in this life and salvation of the people" (see
[[merovingian-polity]]).

## Carolingian Abolition

The Merovingian practice of reading names aloud was abolished under the Carolingians, who folded
commemoration into the eucharistic prayer (Charlemagne's *Admonitio generalis* 54; the Synod of Frankfurt,
794) — a reform justified by citing Pope Innocent I's letter to Decentius of Gubbio (416) objecting to names
being read before the consecration. The change is one more instance of the move from Merovingian local
variety toward Carolingian uniformity that runs through the Handbook's treatment of religion.
