# Merovingian Emporia and Landing Places

**A bottom-up network of river and coastal trading nodes — not yet the licensed Carolingian *wic*.** Dries
Tys's chapter (Oxford Handbook, Ch. 34) reconstructs the maritime and riverine exchange of the Merovingian
North Sea world, resisting the retrojection of the famous eighth-century emporia (Dorestad, Quentovic,
Hamwic) onto the sixth and seventh centuries and arguing for an organic, community-driven foundation
beneath the later state-controlled ports.

## Definition and Scope

"Emporia" here covers "a spectrum… ranging from informal seasonal landing places to formalized trading
settlements." Tys's central contribution is the concept of **landing places** (*anlégplaatsen*): "small,
often seasonal nodes where goods changed hands," operating "without royal licensing or toll
infrastructure" in the early Merovingian period.

## The River and Coastal Network

Northern Gaul's rivers — Rhine, Meuse, Scheldt, Seine, Somme — "was the essential circulatory system…
active throughout the 5th–7th centuries, not only from the 8th century onward as McCormick (2001)
implied." The North Sea coast (Walcheren/Domburg, coastal Flanders, Boulogne) supported an active maritime
exchange. Tys and Loveluck (2006) coined "**maritime material culture profile**" for the assemblage of
these coastal communities: high-frequency foreign goods (ceramics, glass, metal fittings) "obtained
apparently independently of aristocratic intermediaries."

## The Bottom-Up Thesis

The key claim is that "coastal rural communities accessed foreign goods without systematic aristocratic
mediation." Tys explicitly contests elite-demand models (including Wickham's *Framing the Early Middle
Ages*, 2005): "the presence of foreign goods in small rural cemeteries argues against elite gatekeeping of
distribution." This extends, for the coast, the argument Frans Theuws makes for inland northern Gaul (see
[[merovingian-economy]] and [[cross-channel-exchange-britain-and-gaul]]). The famous Carolingian emporia
appear as "a *second stage* superimposed on a bottom-up Merovingian foundation," grown "partly as elite
extraction mechanisms overlaid on pre-existing organic exchange networks."

## Historiography

Pirenne's thesis of a post-700 Mediterranean trade collapse is "treated as largely refuted for northern
Gaul; the real question is the nature of exchange, not its presence or absence." The live debate is
**top-down (Wickham, Devroey) vs. bottom-up (Tys, Theuws, Loveluck)**, a tension that runs through the
whole economic section of the Handbook.
