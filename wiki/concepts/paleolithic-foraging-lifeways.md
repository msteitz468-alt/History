## Definition and Scope

This concept describes the economy, diet, demography, and social organization of
Paleolithic foraging (hunter-gatherer) communities — and the revisionist argument
that Ice Age foragers were, by many measures, *affluent*: well-nourished,
leisured, and secure, rather than living in precarious scarcity.

Fernández-Armesto (Ch. 13) and Ehret (Ch. 14) jointly reconstruct Paleolithic
lifeways from archaeology, comparative ethnography of recent foragers (Hadza,
San), and material culture. The picture overturns the older assumption that
pre-agricultural life was "nasty, brutish, and short."

## Ice Age Affluence (Fernández-Armesto)

- **Abundance**: Ice Age foragers, especially the tundra hunters of glacial
  Eurasia, enjoyed "Ice Age affluence." The edge of the ice was a good place to
  be — large mammals had adapted by efficiently storing body fat. Animal fat is
  "the world's most energy-abundant source of food," yielding on average three
  times as much energy, calorically, as any other intake.
- **Nutrition**: Skeletal data suggest Ice Age people were, on average, *better
  nourished than most later populations*. In some communities people ate ~5 lbs
  of food a day; the high ascorbic acid content of animal organ meats provided
  ~5× the average American intake of vitamin C.
- **Leisure**: "High levels of nutrition and long days of leisure, unequalled in
  most subsequent societies, meant people had time to observe nature and think
  about what they saw" — a precondition for the symbolic culture and art of the
  period (see [[paleolithic-art-symbolic-culture]]).
- **Hunting strategies**: Drive lanes and corrals; driving game over cliffs or
  into bogs; trapping small animals (arctic hare). Killers enjoyed a "fat bonanza"
  achieved with modest effort.

This is the application to the Pleistocene of Marshall Sahlins's "original
affluent society" thesis (1972).

## Diet and Subsistence Balance

- Both hunting and gathering were essential. Gathering was more productive in
  caloric value per unit of energy expended; in known cases, hunting supplied
  about a third of nutrition. The migrations and accompanying demographic changes
  "would not have been possible without both hunting and gathering."
- Possible sexual division of labor (men hunting, women gathering) is speculated
  for the earliest specialization, but we do not know when it started or how rigid
  it was. See [[gender-history]] for the critique of the "Man the Hunter" model.

## Demography

- Most communities numbered fewer than ~100 people.
- Foragers limited family size: mothers cannot easily carry more than one or two
  infants, so large numbers of children are unsuited to a foraging life.
- Main "contraceptive": prolonged lactation (breastfeeding mothers are relatively
  infertile). Population stability — not growth — is the normal pattern in
  foraging communities.
- This makes the demographic growth that peopled the Earth genuinely puzzling: it
  *breaks* the normal pattern. Was population increase a cause or an effect of the
  migrations? (See [[paleolithic-global-dispersal]] and [[migration-in-human-history]].)

## Social Organization (Ehret)

Full syntactic language (see [[syntactic-language-origins]]) enabled larger local
groups and wider cooperative networks than were possible for Neanderthals (whose
residential groups were "hardly more than nuclear families").

- **Kinship reckoning**: The earliest kin terminologies may have resembled the
  "Iroquois" type (parallel cousins classed as siblings; cross cousins
  distinguished). Unilineal institutions (lineages, clans) did not yet exist.
- **Female kin coalitions**: The core social unit of 70,000–48,000 BCE may have
  been a *matrilocal coalition of close female relatives* (sisters, female
  parallel cousins, their daughters). Older women helped gather food for younger
  women burdened with un-weaned children and helped with childcare. Men gained
  access to marriage and reproduction with the women of these coalitions; good
  hunters made acceptable mates because meat enhanced child survival. This is
  modeled on the Hadza of Tanzania (Hawkes, O'Connell, Blurton Jones) and on
  reconstructed early Central African farming societies (Christine Saidi).

## Material Culture and Mind

Fernández-Armesto stresses that material culture yields clues to thought: "Your
house reflects your ideas about your place in the world." The mammoth-bone
dwellings of the Ice Age steppe (built by mammoth-hunters of what is now southern
Russia ~20,000 years ago) were "humanly reimagined" reconstructions of mammoth
nature — perhaps to acquire the beast's strength — yet hosted ordinary family life.

## Emic vs. Etic

"Affluence" and "foraging lifeways" are etic analytical frames. The lived
experience of Paleolithic communities is reconstructed indirectly, from material
remains and ethnographic analogy — both of which have limits.

## Limitations and Critiques

- Ethnographic analogy from modern foragers (Hadza, San) to Paleolithic peoples
  is contested: modern foragers are not "living fossils" and occupy marginal
  environments.
- "Affluence" may be overstated; skeletal samples are biased toward those who
  survived, and starvation episodes leave little trace.
- The female-coalition model of early social structure is a reconstruction from
  ethnography and historical linguistics, not direct evidence.
