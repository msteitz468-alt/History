# Visions and the Afterlife in Merovingian Gaul

**A rich vision literature that illuminates Merovingian religion — and the early, contested stirrings of
purgatory.** Isabel Moreira's chapter (Oxford Handbook, Ch. 42) recovers the otherworld journeys, dynastic
visions, and afterlife ideas of Merovingian Gaul, explicitly rejecting the "theological void" model: "vision
texts illuminate many aspects of Merovingian religion and culture." (Nineteenth-century editors "excised
visions and miracles from their editions," so the texts must be recovered from the *Acta Sanctorum* and
*Patrologia Latina*.)

## Definition and Scope

Merovingian writers used *visio, apparitio, somnium, oraculum,* and *fantasma* "loosely, sometimes
interchangeably," without maintaining rigid taxonomies — even though Augustine's tripartite theory,
Macrobius's five dream-types, and Gregory the Great's sixfold scheme were all available. Visions functioned
as **legitimating instruments** alongside documents: the *Passion of Praeiectus* uses a pre-natal vision,
where "a signed document could carry comparable weight in the public sphere."

## Dynastic Polemic and Women's Visions

Visions could be political. **Chilperic I** — Gregory's "Nero and Herod of our time" — received three
visions and became "the first French king reported to end in hell flames." Women's visionary culture is
prominent: **[[radegund-of-poitiers|Radegund]]** (the man-ship vision; the richly-clad-youth/Christ vision),
**Balthild** (a ladder with ascending angels), and **Aldegund of Maubeuge**, whose visions, dictated to her
confessor, "provide a rare glimpse into self-authored female spirituality" (see [[merovingian-queenship]],
[[merovingian-hagiography]]).

## The Vision of Barontus (678/679)

The chapter's centrepiece is the **Vision of Barontus**, a wealthy Frank at the monastery of Lonrey
(diocese of Bourges) who journeyed through four gates of paradise, was judged, accused of "three wives and
many adulteries," and ransomed at **12 solidi** — St. Peter commanding, "Ransom yourself, brother!"
(*Redime te, frater!*). Its hell contains two named bishops (Vulfoleodus of Bourges, Dido of Poitiers).
Theologically it is innovative: St. Peter's "starring role… as judge" displaces the intercessory patron
saints, making "the formality of a trial in which patron saints would intercede more distant" — a shift
linked to papal-primacy battles against Monothelitism (see [[merovingian-byzantine-relations]]). Two of its
ideas were contested by listeners: demon soul-snatching ("*multi non credunt*") and the displacement of the
patron saints.

## Purgation and Merovingian Reticence

A nascent idea of postmortem **purgation** appears but meets resistance. Caesarius of Arles rejected "a
dedicated third place in the afterlife"; the *Life of Fursey* (Péronne, 650s) includes purgation by fire,
but both its Merovingian author and Bede were "uncomfortable"; the *Life of Leudegar* furnishes "the first
recorded case of purgation imagery being used to restore the reputation of a secular leader in the West."
Moreira's verdict: "it was not until the Carolingian era that purgatory took a firmer hold on the Frankish
afterlife."
