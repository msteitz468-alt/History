# Curse Tablets (Defixiones / Katadesmoi)

## Definition and Scope

**Curse tablets** are inscribed objects — most often thin sheets of lead or
lead-alloy, but also broken pottery, gems, limestone, papyrus and wax —
intended to bind, harm, or compel the behaviour of a named target through
supernatural agency. The Greek term is *katadesmoi* ("things made to bind
down," "magic knots"); the Latin is *defixiones* ("things made to fix or nail
down"). Tablets were frequently rolled, pierced with a nail, and deposited in
liminal places — graves, wells, springs, sanctuary pits — where chthonic powers
could act on them. Of roughly 1,500 published curse tablets, about a quarter are
erotic. They are a first-class body of evidence for ancient popular religion,
magic, and social conflict, and a recurring category of [[ancient-graffiti]].

## Typology

From the North African corpus (Carthage and Hadrumetum especially, mostly 3rd c.
CE), of 120 published tablets: **54 concern chariot races, 9 gladiatorial
contests, 20 erotic magic, 17 legal/tongue-binding, 2 workplace competition, 20
miscellaneous.** The main functional types recur across the Mediterranean:

- **Legal/agonistic** — binding an opponent's tongue and supporters before a
  trial (e.g. the late-4th-c. BCE Kerameikos tablet naming Mnesimachos and
  associates at [[athens]], linked to disputes attested in the orator Lysias).
- **Competitive/spectacle** — cursing rival charioteers and their horses by
  faction (a Carthage tablet invokes *Iao/Iasdao*, variants of Yahweh, against
  the Green and White teams) or theatrical rivals (the Evandros actor curse,
  Athens).
- **Erotic** — compelling desire, including between women: the Hermoupolis Magna
  *katadesmos* (late 3rd/early 4th c. CE, 62 lines) commissioned by **Sophia** to
  attract **Gorgonia** at the bath-house; and the Minturnae tablet against Ticene
  of Carisius (*CIL* X.8249).
- **Theft/justice ("prayers for justice")** — the Uley tablets from Roman Britain
  ask Mercury to deny health to whoever stole a draught animal; a Delos tablet
  invokes the Syrian goddess Atargatis over a stolen necklace.

## Divine Agents and Ritual

Tablets typically invoke chthonic and liminal deities — **Hekate** (goddess of
magic, night and ghosts), underworld **Hermes** (patron of writing and magic,
assimilated to Egyptian Thoth), Persephone, and syncretic or foreign powers
(Atargatis at Delos; Iao at Carthage). Deposition in a grave transferred the
curse to the restless dead ("as this corpse lies useless…"). The formulaic
character — names inserted into pre-written templates, sometimes without
grammatical adjustment — shows professional spell-writers working from
collections, and the frequent imperfect Greek or Latin points to non-elite,
sometimes non-native, clients.

## Related Practices

Curse tablets sit within a wider magical repertoire: the Egyptian concept of
**heka** (creative/authoritative power), execration rituals over clay figurines
(Coffin Text Spell 103), the Greek and Demotic **Magical Papyri** of
Graeco-Roman Egypt, and apotropaic devices such as the *fascinum* (phallus)
against the evil eye. Roman authorities feared the practice: a curse tablet
naming **Germanicus** figured in the poisoning scandal of 19 CE (Tacitus,
*Annals* 2.69), and the Tuder (Todi) inscription (*CIL* XI.4639) records the
prosecution of a slave who attached council-members' names to tombs "so that a
curse could be put upon them."
