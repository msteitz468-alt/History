# Epicureanism

**Epicureanism** is the Hellenistic philosophical system of [[epicurus|Epicurus]] (341–271 BCE): ethical **hedonism** (pleasure as sole good, carefully defined), **atomic** physics, and **empiricist** logic. Taught from Epicurus’s Garden in [[athens|Athens]] from 307 BCE, it remained doctrinally conservative — [[lucretius|Lucretius]]’s *De rerum natura* (1st c. BCE) is still recognizably Epicurean — and survived as a living school into the high Empire (e.g. Diogenes of Oenoanda’s massive wall inscription, late 2nd c. CE).

## Definition and Scope

Alongside [[stoicism|Stoicism]] and the sceptical New Academy, Epicureanism was one of the great **sectarian** labels of post-Aristotelian philosophy. **Jonathan Barnes** (OHCW, 1986) places it after the shift by which philosophy became an “Art of Living,” ethics regent, and professional science largely migrated to [[alexandria|Alexandria]] while Athens remained the philosophical capital. Shared ethical goal with Stoics: *eudaimonia*/tranquillity (*ataraxia*) — but not necessarily political quietism; Barnes rejects reading quietism as mere reaction to a more tumultuous Hellenistic world.

### Parallel minor schools (Barnes)

Barnes maps Epicureanism beside parallel movements: **Pyrrho of Elis** (c.365–c.270) extreme scepticism (possibly influenced by Indian ascetics on Alexander’s expedition; influenced Arcesilaus); **Cyrenaic** radical bodily hedonism (Aristippus); Megarians/Dialecticians (Diodorus Cronus); **Cynicism** (Antisthenes, Diogenes of Sinope) — way of life, freedom/self-sufficiency, reject convention, ostentatious asceticism.

## Ethics (Barnes)

Epicurus referred “all choice and avoidance to health of the body and tranquillity of the soul.” Pleasure is **rationally selected** and **negatively** construed as absence of pain — “neither pained in the body nor troubled in the soul.” A sensual string of revelries is inferior; “the pleasant life is produced… by sober reasoning.” Virtue is required for the pleasant life (“not possible to live pleasurably without living sensibly and nobly and justly”); the just man is least troubled (fear of detection). Not crude sybaritism despite contemporary vilification. Epicurus **abjured politics** but prized **friendship** and society. To an external observer a sober Epicurean and a virtuous Stoic would look little different.

## Physics and Theology (Barnes)

- Nature = **bodies + void**; atoms indivisible, infinite in number/space, with size, shape, weight only; macroscopic bodies from hooked collisions.
- **Anti-teleological**: eyes not made for seeing (Lucretius); celestial bodies not divine intelligences.
- Gods exist in intergalactic tranquillity without anger or gratitude toward humans (“What is happy and indestructible neither is troubled itself nor causes trouble to others”).
- Soul is corporeal fine particles; dissolved at death → “death is nothing to us; for what is dissolved has no perception.”
- Natural vs empty desires; pain brief or blunt / counterbalanced by pleasure.
- **Clinamen** (atomic swerve): Epicurus’s major physical contribution, ethically meant to free volition from determinism (Cicero thought causeless swerve “disgraceful” for a scientist).

### Canonics vs Stoic dialectic

Epicureans reject dialectic as redundant; prefer “canonics” — confirmation/disconfirmation (later Philodemus *On Signs*). Criterion of truth: **all sense-impressions are true**; error comes from superadded opinion (simulacra physics). Stoics admit only “apprehensive impressions”; Academics attack with twins/eggs/wax apples. Lucretius: life is ruined if you will not believe your senses and avoid precipices.

## Interaction

- Contrasts with [[stoicism]] (virtue alone good; fate; political duty).
- Atomic inheritance from Leucippus/Democritus via the [[presocratics]].
- Latin reception: Lucretius; Ciceronian critique; later Roman garden circles (Philodemus fl. c.55 alters Epicureanism in the first-century “turbid waters,” per Barnes).
- Parallel minor movements: Pyrrhonism, Cyrenaics, Megarians, Cynics.

## End Conditions

Never “ended” by a single decree; thinned as Christian monotheism and Neoplatonism captured elite culture, but second-century CE inscriptions show continued public teaching. Its ethical vocabulary (*ataraxia*, fear of death, critique of superstition) remained available to sceptics and modern readers via Lucretius.
