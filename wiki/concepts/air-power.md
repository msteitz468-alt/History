# Air Power

**Air power** is the use of aircraft as an independent instrument of war and statecraft. As an analytical category it was given its enduring early American formulation by [[mitchell-william|William "Billy" Mitchell]] in *Winged Defense* (1925), coeval with — and partly converging on — the Italian theorist [[douhet-giulio|Giulio Douhet]]. Mitchell's own definition is deliberately broad: "Air power may be defined as the ability to do something in the air," or "the ability to do something in or through the air, and as the air covers the whole world, aircraft are able to go anywhere on the planet."

## The Revolutionary Claim

Mitchell's central thesis was that "the advent of air power has completely changed all former systems of national defense." Air power, "starting out as a mere auxiliary in observing for armies and navies," had within four years of the First World War "fought great battles against opposing air power, threw bombs down on hostile industrial centers, cities, railroads and ports," and proved decisive. The most concrete proof was that **aircraft dominate seacraft**: the 1921 bombing tests off the Virginia capes, sinking the submarine U-117, the destroyer G-102, the cruiser Frankfurt, and finally the dreadnought *Ostfriesland*, were for Mitchell "absolutely conclusive" and proved "the ability of aircraft to destroy ships of all classes on the surface of the water." From this he drew the radical conclusion that "Sea power as expressed in battleships is almost a thing of the past."

## The Abolition of the Frontier

For Mitchell, aircraft erased the geography of defense. "In a trice, aircraft have set aside all ideas of frontiers. The whole country now becomes the frontier." The old physical barriers no longer applied: "The frontiers in the old sense — the coast lines or borders — are no longer applicable to the air because aircraft can fly anywhere that there is air. **Interior cities are now as subject to attack as those along the coast.**" Against this attack the only remedy was aerial: "**Nothing can stop the attack of aircraft except other aircraft.**" Future invasions would skip the surface entirely — "Air craft do not need to pierce the line of either navies or armies. They can fly straight over them to the heart of a country and gain success in war."

## The American, Defensive Framing

Mitchell pitched air power as a *defense*, suited to an ocean-buffered United States — hence the pointed title *Winged Defense*. He classed the U.S. as a country "entirely self-sustaining but … out of the ordinary aircraft range," whose air force "would be able to protect the country from invasion and would insure its independence." Air power could sweep the sea lanes: a sufficient air force "can hold off any hostile shipping which seeks to cross the oceans and menace our shores," and "aircraft today are the only effective means of coast protection." This continental-and-hemispheric defensive emphasis distinguished him from [[douhet-giulio|Douhet]]'s doctrine of pure, unrestrained offense. Crucially, Mitchell **retained pursuit and interception**: drawing on the European war, he held that "the only effective defense against aerial attack is to whip the enemy's air forces in air battles," and his model defense of New York combined surveillance aircraft, searchlights, guns and, above all, rapidly rising **pursuit aviation** — though he insisted ground defenses alone were "absolutely incapable" of stopping an air attack.

## Organization: An Independent Air Force

A consistent theme is institutional autonomy. "The time has come when aviation must be developed for aviation's sake and not as an auxiliary to other existing branches." Mitchell demanded "a single Department of National Defense and under it a Department of Aeronautics, Department of the Army, and Department of the Navy," with a United Air Force providing "an aeronautical striking force designed to obtain control of the air and demolish whatever hostile land or water targets might be necessary." The air's voice "must be heard in the national councils on an equal basis with those of the Army and Navy," with a separate budget and a separate "air-going" personnel.

## Relation to Command of the Air and Strategic Bombardment

Mitchell shared Douhet's conviction that [[command-of-the-air|command of the air]] is decisive. He argued that air forces would "strike immediately at the enemy's manufacturing and food centers, railways, bridges, canals and harbors," sparing the months-long attrition of ground armies, and that "the influence of air power on the ability of one nation to impress its will on another in an armed conflict will be decisive." Winning the air contest was nearly war-winning: a nation that "jumps on its opponent at once will bring about a speedy and lasting victory." This links air power directly to [[strategic-bombardment]] — the destruction of an enemy's "vital centers" and "power to make war … this means the manufactories, the means of communication, the food products." Yet Mitchell tempered the doctrine with the realist qualification that **"everything begins and ends on the ground"**: armies must still hold "the land bases from which air forces or sea forces act," and "any decision in war is based on what takes place ultimately on the ground."

## Emic vs. Etic

In Mitchell's own (emic) usage, "air power" named both a capability and a programmatic cause — a rallying cry for an independent service. As an etic analytical term, "air power" became the umbrella label for the entire aerial domain of warfare: air superiority, strategic attack, interdiction, reconnaissance, and mobility. Mitchell already gestured at this breadth, noting air power's economic and civil uses (mapping, mail, forest patrol, pest control) alongside its military missions, and casting the airman as a member of a distinct "air-going people."

## Limitations and Critiques

Mitchell's predictions consistently outran the technology of his day. His continent-spanning bombers, gyroscopically guided aerial torpedoes that "hit great cities," and cities surrendering at "the mere threat of bombing" were aspirational; even his own 1921 aircraft were, in his words, "obsolescent war machines entirely unsuited for this work," some pursuit planes barely able to reach the target ships and return. His impact in his lifetime was muted — *Winged Defense* sold poorly, and isolationism deprived him of a credible threat to dramatize. As the editor of [[sources/jablonsky-roots-of-strategy-4-1999]] notes, his argument was also internally inconsistent: he insisted both that air defenses "were the only effective defense" and that the offensive bomber was decisive, an unresolved tension between his defensive American framing and the Douhetian offense his rhetoric drifted toward after 1926. The doctrine thus carried within it the lasting split in air-power thought between strategic-bombing enthusiasts and advocates of surface support and air superiority — a debate that long outlived him.
