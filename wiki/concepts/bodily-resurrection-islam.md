## Definition and Scope

*Qiyāma* (resurrection) in Islamic theology encompasses the physical re-embodiment of human souls at the Day of Judgment, followed by paradise (*janna*) with bodily pleasures and hellfire (*jahannam*) with bodily torments — all as described in the Quran and Prophetic traditions. The debate addressed in Discussion 20 of the [[ghazali-incoherence-of-philosophers|Tahāfut al-Falāsifa]] is not between believers and unbelievers about whether any afterlife exists, but between a philosophical tradition (derived from [[ibn-sina|Avicenna]] and al-Fārābī) and Islamic orthodoxy about whether the afterlife is purely spiritual or also bodily.

This distinction is doctrinally decisive: [[al-ghazali|al-Ghazālī]] classifies the denial of bodily resurrection as one of the three beliefs that make the Avicennan philosophers *kuffār* (unbelievers) — the third and final condemnation in the [[the-three-condemnations|book's conclusion]].

## The Philosophical Position

The philosophers acknowledge soul survival and afterlife reward and punishment. Their account, as al-Ghazālī presents it, has these features:

1. **Purely spiritual afterlife**: The soul endures after death either in great pleasure or great pain. The nature of this pleasure is *intellectual* — "the nourishment and enjoyment of the rational faculty consists in apprehending the intelligibles." Souls that acquired knowledge in this life enjoy union with the intelligible world; ignorant souls suffer the pain of deprivation.

2. **Hierarchy of pleasures**: Intellectual pleasures are categorically superior to sensory ones. The philosophers argue this from the nobility of angels (who lack sensory pleasures) and from human behavior (warriors sacrifice bodily comfort for honor; a man forgoes love for dignity). The Prophetic hadith "I have prepared for my righteous servants that which no eye has seen and no ear has heard" is read as confirming that the highest pleasures transcend ordinary description — and therefore must be intellectual, not sensory.

3. **Quranic descriptions as parable**: Paradise, fire, houris, and the physical details of the hereafter are *parables* crafted for the understanding of ordinary people, just as anthropomorphic descriptions of God (His "hand," "face," "settling on the throne") are figures of speech. The divine attributes are sanctified high above literal bodily description; the same principle applies to the afterlife.

4. **Ethical consequence**: The purpose of religious law is the *purification* of the soul — weakening its attachment to bodily appetites so that, at death, separation from the body causes minimal pain and union with the intelligible realm brings maximum joy. Works and worship serve psychic hygiene, not propitiation of a literally-rewarding deity.

## Al-Ghazālī's Counter-Argument

Al-Ghazālī's response in Discussion 20 is structured across several distinct moves:

### Concession and Demarcation (§§18–19)

He concedes most of the philosophical account: spiritual pleasures superior to sensory ones — granted; soul survival after bodily death — granted (though known through revelation, not reason, as his critique of Discussion 19 established). What is *not* conceded is the *denial* of bodily resurrection and bodily pleasures in paradise. The question is whether both can coexist, and al-Ghazālī argues they can and must: "What is there to stand in the way of realizing the combination of both kinds of happiness, the spiritual and bodily?"

### Anthropomorphism Analogy Rejected (§§23–25)

The philosophers argue that Quranic afterlife descriptions should be allegorized like Quranic anthropomorphisms (God's hand, face, etc.). Al-Ghazālī rejects the analogy on two grounds:

(1) Anthropomorphic utterances are amenable to metaphorical interpretation consistent with Arabic linguistic usage; afterlife descriptions in the Quran are *so specific and detailed* in their literal meaning that allegorizing them would require declaring the Prophet to have deliberately deceived the faithful — "obfuscation by making one imagine what is contrary to truth for the benefit of creatures. But this is what the position of prophethood is sanctified high above."

(2) The metaphorical interpretation of anthropomorphisms is *required* by rational proof — God cannot have a physical location or direction. No such rational proof establishes the impossibility of bodily resurrection: "What He has promised in the hereafter is not impossible in terms of the power of God, exalted be He. Hence, one must follow the apparent [literal meaning of the revealed] speech."

### The Three Alternatives and Their Refutation Refuted (§§27–43)

The philosophers produce a systematic argument that bodily resurrection is impossible regardless of which account of the human person one adopts. They divide the possible accounts into three:

**Alternative 1 — No subsisting soul; body only**: If the human is purely body + life-accident, then "resurrection" would be the creation of a *numerically new* human, not the return of the original. The term "return" (*rujūʿ*) requires something that persists — it cannot be fulfilled by creating a replica of what was annihilated.

**Alternative 2 — Soul survives; original body reconstituted**: The original body disintegrates — into earth, absorbed by worms, mixed with air and water. Even if God gathers the same parts, two problems arise: (a) a person who ate another's flesh has made the eaten body *part of their own body* — two souls cannot be returned to one body; (b) over a lifetime, the same matter was successively liver, heart, leg — to which organ is it returned?

**Alternative 3 — Soul returned to any body**: This collapses into the doctrine of metempsychosis (*tanāsukh*), which the philosophers themselves refute: if a bodily composition properly disposed to receive a soul automatically attracts one (from the celestial principles), then two souls would arrive at the one body.

Al-Ghazālī's objections (§§38–43):

To Alternative 3 (his primary target): He chooses this alternative and accepts it. The soul *is* a self-subsisting substance; resurrection is the soul's return to *a* human body — not necessarily the original matter. "He is what he is by virtue of his soul, not his body, since the parts of the body change for him from childhood to old age." This is theologically coherent and religiously mandated.

Against the "infinite souls / finite matter" objection: This rests on the philosophers' premise of an eternal world with infinite past time producing an infinite series of souls. Al-Ghazālī has already refuted world-eternity in Discussion 1. "For one who does not believe in the world's eternity, the souls that separate from bodies are finite and are not greater than the existing materials." And even if they were greater, God can create matter ex nihilo.

Against the transmigration objection: "There is no need to squabble about terms. What the religious law has conveyed must be believed. Let it be 'transmigration.' We only deny transmigration *in this world*." The resurrection is a new dispensation under different conditions — not the cyclic metempsychosis the philosophers rightly reject.

### The Divine Order Objection and Reply (§§51–56)

The philosophers' final argument: God's will is eternal and uniform; "You will not find any change in God's way" (Quran 48:23). If the divine act always follows one fixed pattern, then any resurrection-order would itself be eternal and cyclical, restoring the problem. A genuinely new order (creation → history → final resurrection → eternity) implies a change in the divine will, which is impossible for an eternal, simple will.

Al-Ghazālī replies: this objection simply *is* the doctrine of the world's pre-eternity restated. He has already shown it is not rationally improbable to posit "three divisions — namely, that God exists without the world, that He then creates the world according to the order we experience, and [that He] then begins a new order which is the one promised in paradise." The two underlying issues — temporal origination and the disruption of habitual causal order — have both been addressed. Discussion 20 closes by cross-referencing Discussions 1 and 17.

## Theological Stakes

Discussion 20 is the longest single discussion in the Tahāfut and the culminating argument of the entire work. It is where the difference between philosophical and orthodox Islamic eschatology is most stark, and where the logical connections to the book's other major themes (causality, divine will, world-eternity) are most explicitly drawn together. Al-Ghazālī is not simply defending popular religion; he is arguing that the philosophers' metaphysics, if consistently applied, undermines not only bodily resurrection but the very concept of divine omnipotence and prophetic truthfulness.

## Historiography

The philosophical content of Discussion 20 has been studied both as Islamic theology and as medieval philosophy of religion. M. E. Marmura's article "Al-Ghazālī on Bodily Resurrection and Causality in the Tahāfut and the Iqtiṣād" (*Aligarh Journal of Islamic Thought* 2, 1989) is the key secondary study, demonstrating that al-Ghazālī's position in Discussion 20 must be read in conjunction with his *Iqtiṣād fī al-Iʿtiqād* (the Ashʿarite theological treatise he wrote shortly after the Tahāfut). In the Iqtiṣād, al-Ghazālī makes clear that causality is not truly operative in nature and that miracles (and resurrection) are direct divine acts — a position he presents more cautiously in the Tahāfut's dialectical framing.

The question of how seriously al-Ghazālī takes Alternative 3 (soul returned to any matter) versus Alternative 2 (original matter restored) is contested. The *ḥadīth* he cites — rain at the resurrection mixing with earth like sperm — gestures toward a miraculous causal mechanism rather than strict matter-identity, suggesting he leans toward Alternative 3 while leaving the mechanism to God's unknowable treasury of causes.
