## Definition

"Sea of Islands" is the central metaphor and argument of Tongan scholar Epeli Hau'ofa's 1993/94 essay "Our Sea of Islands," which proposes a fundamentally different way of conceptualising Oceania and its peoples. The essay is treated in [[denoon-cambridge-pacific-islanders-1997]] as a paradigm-shifting intervention.

The core formulation, quoted verbatim: "There is a gulf of difference between viewing the Pacific as 'islands in a far sea' and as 'a sea of islands'. The first emphasises dry surfaces in a vast ocean far from the centres of power ... The second is a more holistic perspective in which things are seen in the totality of their relationships."

## The Isolation Thesis Critiqued

Hau'ofa's concept directly attacks the prevailing "isolation thesis" — the view that Pacific Islands are small, poor, and isolated, incapable of self-sufficiency. As Hau'ofa's essay argues (quoted in [[denoon-cambridge-pacific-islanders-1997]]): "Nineteenth-century imperialism erected boundaries that led to the contraction of Oceania, transforming a once boundless world into the Pacific Island states and territories that we know today. People were confined to their tiny spaces, isolated from each other ... This is the historical basis of the view that our countries are small, poor and isolated."

Crucially, Hau'ofa argues this isolation is *colonial in origin*, not inherent: "This assumption, however, is no longer tenable as far as the countries of central and western Polynesia are concerned ... The rapid expansion of the world economy since World War II ... had a liberating effect on the lives of ordinary people ... The new economic reality made nonsense of artificial boundaries, enabling the people to shake off their confinement and they have since moved, by the tens of thousands, doing what their ancestors had done before them."

## The Historical Argument

Hau'ofa grounds the concept in pre-colonial Pacific history. Oceanic cosmologies "comprised not only land surfaces, but the surrounding ocean as far as they could traverse and exploit it, the underworld with its fire-controlling and earth-shaking denizens, and the heavens above with their hierarchies of powerful gods and named stars and constellations that people could count on to guide their ways across the seas. Their world was anything but tiny."

Migration, trade, intermarriage across vast oceanic distances was not the exception but the norm. Exchange systems included "the early Lapita obsidian trade, the Yapese Empire, the Vitiaz Straits, the Kula, and the Tongan Empire." Islanders maintained "webs of relationships span[ning] Pacific and continental societies."

## Relation to MIRAB and Dependency Theory

Hau'ofa's essay is explicitly a recantation of his own earlier academic position. "In his earlier works Hau'ofa, like other scholars, embraced the 'dependency theory' perspectives of a single regional economy articulating relations between European-controlled metropoles and Pacific peripheries." He had accepted the MIRAB model — small island nations characterised by dependency on Migration, Remittances, and Aid sustaining a Bureaucracy — which described "a Pacific [that] could never again be self-reliant, not because it lacks resources, but because it was locked into an economy 'that will not allow them to be'."

The sea-of-islands concept rejects this view not by denying migration or remittances, but by reframing them: "The resources of Islanders were never restricted to their land areas, but included their relatives abroad and the seas between them. Today's resource areas have expanded into Pacific Rim nations. It is not charity but the labour of family members abroad — elite and working class — that produces remittances. To label this 'dependency' is belittling, and masks relationships of dominance."

## The Network Model

Hau'ofa proposes replacing the "centre–periphery" model with a "network model based on Pacific modes of interaction" that demonstrates "the linked networks of population movements along well-established paths, and their Pacific centre."

This maps onto anthropological work on path-based identity in Melanesia and Vanuatu: "Man is the tree taking root, while the local group is the canoe exploring the world along established 'roads'." Identity is not place-bound stasis but "expressed from the juncture of stasis and mobility."

## Emic Dimension

Hau'ofa's concept draws on and validates emic Pacific worldviews. Maori scholar Witi Ihimaera is quoted: "The concepts of centre and margin are challenging ones for any minority culture ... From our perspective Maori literature is the centre — for if you are Maori and looking out, you do so from your own centre. This is the subversive viewpoint we have taken."

## Limitations and Contestation

The source notes that the sea-of-islands paradigm is most applicable to "the countries of central and western Polynesia" and "may be untenable also of Micronesia." It is less clearly applicable to Melanesia, where outmigration has historically been more limited and where colonial configurations (no free-entry rights to metropolitan countries for citizens of PNG, Solomon Islands, Vanuatu) constrain mobility.

The MIRAB model's proponents note that dependency relationships persist regardless of reframing: Hau'ofa himself acknowledges "when the flows of resources within the region are added up, Australia and New Zealand still come out well ahead. For what they give you in aid they receive in return a great deal more in the forms of export earnings and repatriation of profits on investments."

## Historiographical Significance

The sea-of-islands concept has been widely adopted in Pacific studies as an organising metaphor that places Islander agency, mobility, and world-historical centrality at the fore, rather than treating the Pacific as a passive object of external forces. [[denoon-cambridge-pacific-islanders-1997]] uses it explicitly as the framing of its concluding chapter, "The End of Insularity."
