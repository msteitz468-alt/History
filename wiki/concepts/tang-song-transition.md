# The Tang–Song Transition

## Definition and Scope

The **Tang–Song transition** is the cluster of profound social, economic, political, and cultural
changes that transformed China between the late [[tang-dynasty|Tang]] (8th–9th c.) and the
[[song-dynasty|Song]] (960–1279) — so far-reaching that an influential tradition treats it as the
**birth of "early modern" China**. As [[kuhn-die-song-dynastie|Dieter Kuhn]] frames it, the Song was
**"a new society reflected in its culture."** The transition is the analytical key to why Song China
looks so different from what came before.

## The changes

- **Social: aristocracy → literati-gentry.** The hereditary **medieval aristocracy** that had
  dominated the Tang was destroyed (in the late-Tang collapse and the wars of the Five Dynasties)
  and replaced by a new elite of **scholar-officials** (*shi-da-fu*) defined by **classical
  education and the examinations** rather than by birth — the change Kuhn places at the centre.
- **Political: the examination state.** The **[[chinese-examination-system|examination system]]**
  expanded enormously and became the principal route to office, producing a more bureaucratic,
  centralized, civilian (less aristocratic-military) state served by a meritocratic-in-principle
  officialdom.
- **Economic: the commercial and urban revolution.** A surge in agriculture (Champa rice), trade,
  money (paper currency), and great cities — the **[[commercial-revolution|commercial revolution]]**
  — shifted China's centre of gravity to the **commercializing south** (a shift accelerated by the
  [[an-lushan-rebellion|An Lushan]] disaster).
- **Cultural: printing and Neo-Confucianism.** The spread of **woodblock [[printing-revolution|
  printing]]** democratized texts and underpinned the examination culture, while
  **[[neo-confucianism|Neo-Confucianism]]** gave the new literati class its philosophy.

## Emic vs. Etic

Strongly **etic**: the "Tang–Song transition" and the "Naitō hypothesis" are modern (Japanese and
Western) historiographical constructs. Song contemporaries were aware of profound change (and of
their break with the Tang aristocratic past), but the periodizing thesis is the historians'.

## Limitations and Critiques

- **"Early modern" / "Chinese Renaissance"?** The Naitō claim that the Song inaugurates a "modern"
  epoch is contested — critics warn against reading European "modernity" into China and note strong
  continuities, and debate whether the changes were as abrupt as the model implies.
- **How meritocratic?** The examination elite still required leisured study, so gentry families
  reproduced their status; "aristocracy to meritocracy" is a simplification.
- **Where to put the line** (late Tang? Five Dynasties? early Song?) is itself disputed.
