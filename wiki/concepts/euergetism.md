## Definition

**Euergetism** (from Greek *euergetes*, "benefactor") is the institution by which the
**wealthy financed the public life of the Greek city** — buildings, festivals, grain
supplies, corn-funds, embassies, the gymnasium — in return for **public honours**: decrees of
praise, statues, crowns, front-row seats, and titles. It is one of the defining social
mechanisms of the [[hellenistic-polis|Hellenistic city]] (and later of the Greek cities under
Rome), analysed by J. K. Davies in CAH VII.1 ch. 8.

## How It Worked

In the Hellenistic city, public revenues (court fees, harbour and market dues, monopolies,
rentals, taxes) were "often inadequate even for regular outgoings," and treasuries were
frequently empty when extraordinary demands arose — major buildings, fortifications, corn
purchases, military expeditions. The expedients all focused on **the rich**:

- **Liturgical magistracies** — offices redefined so the holder paid part of his
  administration's costs;
- **Loans** to the state (sometimes unrepaid);
- **Voluntary gifts** (*epidoseis*);
- **Foundations** — capital sums held in trust, the annual income funding a named cult or
  charitable purpose in perpetuity.

In return the benefactor displayed the key social quality of ***philotimia*** ("love of
honour," conspicuous magnificence) and received civic gratitude.

## Significance

Davies stresses that euergetism reflects a **shift in internal power relations** within the
city. What had changed from the classical period was "not so much the intensity of local
patriotism as internal power relationships": **few civic assemblies could now coerce their
rich inhabitants** without losing more than they gained or provoking the intervention of a
king or his *strategos*. The participatory democracy of the fifth and fourth centuries thus
gave way to **government by wealthy elites** working through democratic forms — with
benefaction as the price of, and reward for, their dominance.

The same logic linked city to crown: the wealthiest benefactors were often **courtiers**, and
kings made gifts to cities (corn, buildings) as *philanthropa* — so that euergetism became a
hinge between civic finance and royal finance. The institution outlasted the kingdoms,
becoming central to the civic life of the Greek East under the Roman Empire.
