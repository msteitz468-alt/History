# Seasian Classic Periodization (Miksic & Goh)

**Seasian Classic periodization** is the etic chronological framework used throughout [[miksic-goh-ancient-southeast-asia-2017|Miksic & Goh, *Ancient Southeast Asia* (2017)]] for the region they term **Seasia**. It divides the last three millennia of regional prehistory and early history into Preclassic, Protoclassic, Early / Middle / Late Classic, and Postclassic phases. The scheme is not an indigenous dynastic calendar and does not map one-to-one onto the wiki’s global period framework; it is an analytical tool for coordinating archaeology, art history, and external written sources across mainland and island Southeast Asia.

## Emic and Etic Status

The labels are **etic**. Classical Maya and central Mexican “Classic” periodization and the Indonesian archaeological service’s period divisions supplied models; Miksic and Goh adapted them to Seasian evidence rather than translating an emic royal era or Buddhist *sasana* chronology. Local actors used regnal years, Indianized era names (Śaka, Sanjaya), Chinese reign titles, and religious calendars. “Early Classic,” “Middle Classic,” and related terms would have been unintelligible to the people under study. The framework is therefore a historian’s grid, not a reconstruction of how Seasians periodized themselves.

## Phase Table

| Phase | Dates (Miksic & Goh) | Principal markers |
|---|---|---|
| Preclassic | c. 1000 BCE–1 CE (last ~1,000 years of prehistory in their usage) | Complex settlement, permanent architecture/monuments, stone sculpture, bronze then iron; no local writing; institutionalized India–China–Seasia contact by mid-phase |
| Protoclassic | c. 1–600 CE | Fragmentary foreign then local written sources; early Indic icons; [[funan|Funan]], early [[pyu-city-states|Pyu]] centres, intensifying Preclassic systems rather than sudden revolution |
| Early Classic | 600–900 CE | Local historical and archaeological sources become adequate; permanent temples; Chinese diplomatic recognition; [[srivijaya|Srivijaya]], [[dvaravati|Dvaravati]], Sailendra/[[mataram-kingdom|Mataram]] Java |
| Middle Classic | 900–1200 CE | Peak monumentalism ([[angkor|Angkor]], [[pagan-kingdom|Bagan]]); [[chola-invasion-srivijaya-1025|Chola raid 1025]]; east Javanese polities after central Java’s “dark age” |
| Late Classic | 1200–1400 CE | Last flourish then shrinkage of Angkor after [[jayavarman-vii|Jayavarman VII]]; disintegration of Classic culture as cities and maritime trade grew; [[majapahit|Majapahit]]; early Islam in north Sumatra |

## Preclassic Logic

Miksic and Goh call the last ~1,000 years of prehistory **Preclassic** because writing did not yet exist in Seasia, yet “many of the social and technological attributes of the Classic period… already existed”: complex settlement patterns, sophisticated technology, permanent architecture and monuments, and stone sculpture. India–China–Seasia contact began in the mid-Preclassic; exchanges were institutionalized before written sources.

Metallurgical sequence: bronze entered mainland Seasia in the early Preclassic; iron in the middle of the period. Insular early metallurgy is poorly known. First metal advances often took the form of adornment rather than tools or weapons. By mid-Preclassic much of Seasia was linked by a bronze-drum interaction sphere from north Vietnam to New Guinea—the sphere of the [[dongson-culture|Dongson]] and related drum traditions. Concurrently, Chinese conquest of formerly culturally Seasian south China began ~1,000 years of Vietnam’s incorporation into Chinese imperial structures, while western Seasia appropriated South Asian cultural traits.

### Rejection of “Revolution” Stadialism

The authors reject Western-style Palaeolithic–Neolithic–Bronze–Iron **revolution** models for Seasia. Change was gradual and evolutionary. Early copper and bronze working was not followed rapidly by large polities or complex settlement; early metallurgists look like part-time farmer-craftsmen. Elaborate settlement patterns and wealth disparities appeared roughly 500 years later, around the same time as iron, “though this correlation… cannot be assumed to represent a cause and effect relationship.” Seasian chronological schemes that merely parallel Western Three Age models are “merely descriptive”; more radical rethinking is needed, and schemes should not treat each period as an advance toward a goal. Some Seasian prehistorians reject “lithic” ages altogether for regional chronology; others defend them. The Hoabinhian toolkit spans hunting-gathering and farming, undermining neat correlations of tool type with economy.

## Protoclassic and Early Classic

**Protoclassic** (~2,000–1,400 years ago in their framing of first foreign and local written sources) marks the first six centuries CE of appropriating Indian icons and intensifying Preclassic systems rather than a civilizational revolution. North Vietnam is the major **exception** to continuity: Chinese invasion from the late Preclassic into the Protoclassic entrenched Chinese cultural elements there, setting a trajectory distinct from the rest of Seasia.

The **Early Classic** begins at **600 CE** because local historical and archaeological sources then become adequate. Several major changes cluster at this threshold: Funan disappeared; new Khmer centres appeared; Pyu foci shifted (Sri Ksetra rising as Beikthano, Halin, and other Protoclassic centres declined); the Dvaravati cultural complex arose; Srivijaya appeared; and a major civilization centre formed in central Java. The Early Classic frame of 600–900 “matches closely the careers” of Chinese-named Heling/Shepo polities in Java and the long Srivijaya–Tang relationship, whose end coincides with the Early Classic’s close.

## Middle and Late Classic

The **Middle Classic** is dated **900–1200 CE**. In Java, after the 919–929 shift of power from central to eastern Java, “for the next 300 years, the entire Middle Classic, no more Javanese temples were built from permanent materials”—authors call this a “dark age in Java” relative to the Early Classic temple boom. On the mainland, Angkor was “at the height of its glory” at the end of the Middle Classic; “the empire’s fate took a different turn in the Late Classic.”

The **Late Classic** (**1200–1400 CE**) begins with the last flourish of Angkor. Jayavarman VII may have ruled until about 1219; after him Angkor’s empire began to shrink and no more major temple complexes were built—parallel in timing to the end of Bagan’s peak monument-building. As Classic Southeast Asian culture disintegrated, **cities and maritime trade grew**. Yuan policies encouraging trade fostered Chinese colonies and Seasian maritime growth; after the Ming replaced the Yuan in 1368, harsh commercial restrictions cut Chinese interaction, but “this did not… have any appreciable effect on the Seasian economy,” indicating that “China was not the main engine of the region’s trade.” In the 1330s overseas Chinese communities were still scarce; by 1400 several Chinese colonies numbered 5,000 or more people. Late Classic cities do not support Paul Wheatley’s theory that “ceremonial centers” (temples and staff) formed the nuclei of urbanization, nor is there evidence that forts or markets were the core of Seasian urbanization in this period.

## End of the Classic (1400)

The authors end the Classic at the “somewhat arbitrary date of 1400,” justified because “Majapahit was the last major example of elaborate Classic culture to form and prosper. Its decline after 1400 and the rise of Muslim Melaka serve as a rationale.” By the beginning of the Postclassic in 1400, at least three north Sumatran kingdoms were under Muslim rulers: Samudera-Pasai, Aru, and Lamuri—markers of the process of [[islamization-of-southeast-asia|Islamization]] that redefines the Postclassic.

## Relation to Other Processes and Polities

- **contains** Preclassic → Postclassic nested phases of Seasian historical archaeology.
- **preceded_by** deep prehistory and Neolithic/early metal sequences that the framework does not fully re-periodize.
- **concurrent_with** [[indianization-of-southeast-asia|Indianization]], [[indian-ocean-trade|Indian Ocean trade]], and the career of the [[southeast-asian-port-polity|Southeast Asian port-polity]].
- **contrasts_with** Vietnam’s long Sinified chronology and with stadial “revolution” models imported from European prehistory.
- Anchors narrative timelines for [[srivijaya]], [[mataram-kingdom]], [[dvaravati]], [[pyu-city-states]], [[khmer-empire]], [[majapahit]], and related pages.

## Limitations and Critiques

1. **Etic grid vs. emic eras.** Dynastic names (Sailendra, Angkor, Majapahit) cut across or sit inside phases; the framework can flatten political discontinuity into archaeological sameness and vice versa.
2. **Arbitrary edges.** 600, 900, 1200, and especially 1400 are convenient round numbers justified by clusters of change, not single catastrophes. The end of Classic at Majapahit’s decline / Melaka’s rise is openly “somewhat arbitrary.”
3. **Uneven fit by region.** North Vietnam’s Chinese incorporation is acknowledged as exceptional; other areas (Philippines, eastern Indonesia, upland interiors) enter the Classic narrative unevenly because of source poverty.
4. **Technology not destiny.** The framework’s own critique of technology-driven stage theories must be applied reflexively: iron, writing, and permanent temples mark phases without causing them in a simple way.
5. **Urban theory load.** Late Classic material is used against Wheatley’s ceremonial-center urbanization model; readers should treat that as an argument within the book, not a settled regional law.
6. **Wiki dual periodization.** Pages tagged with this scheme should also carry the wiki’s global `period` fields (Early Middle Ages, High Middle Ages, etc.) so Seasian and world frameworks remain cross-walkable.

## Historiography and Source Notes

The scheme is inseparable from the book’s method: equal weight on archaeology and texts, skepticism toward Chinese over-centralization of Seasian polities, and insistence that Preclassic complexity already existed before writing. Contested elements include the unity of “Pyu” and “Dvaravati” as cultural labels, the degree of “darkness” in Middle Classic Java (few permanent temples is not absence of culture), and whether 1025 or commercial dispersal better explains Srivijaya’s transformation. For periodization debates more broadly, see [[southeast-asia-as-region]] and [[indianization-of-southeast-asia]].
