# Adatrecht and the Reception Theory (Dutch Indonesia)

The Dutch colonial doctrine that elevated unwritten Javanese customary law (*adat*, rendered as *adatrecht* — "adat law") as the normative law of the Netherlands Indies, thereby subordinating the [[sharia|Sharīʿa]] to a secondary, locally conditioned status. Analyzed by Wael Hallaq in [[hallaq-sharia-2009|*Sharīʿa*]] (ch. 14) as the Southeast Asian case of the [[colonial-transformation-of-islamic-law|colonial transformation of Islamic law]] — formally distinct from the British Indian approach (see [[anglo-muhammadan-law]]) but driven by the same structural imperatives.

## Background: Law in the Pre-Colonial Archipelago

When Dutch ships arrived in Java in 1596, they encountered a dual judicial system under the Mataram Kingdom: *pradata* law (royal jurisdiction based on Indian-Javanese codes, for serious offenses) and *padu* customary law (*adat*, unwritten, governing everyday communal life). Sultan Agung (r. 1613–45) introduced significant Sharīʿa elements into Mataram justice, beginning an Islamization of Javanese law that remained incomplete and contested.

The adat and the Sharīʿa were not in pre-colonial practice understood as opposed systems: "before the end of the nineteenth century, adat and Sharīʿa appear to have been viewed as complementary and intertwined." The colonial construction of adat as the "true" native law — separate from and superior to Islamic law — was itself a colonial invention.

## The Dutch East India Company and Non-Interference

The Dutch East India Company (VOC), like the British East India Company in its early phase, left native law largely alone: it "resolved to respect local law — another way of saying that, by and large, they could not have cared less — except where commercial interests were at stake" (Lev, cited by Hallaq). Partial exception: when the British temporarily displaced the Dutch (1811), Governor Raffles declared all land state property — a principle the Dutch adopted after retrieving the colony in 1814.

## The Nineteenth-Century Turn: Codes and Courts

By mid-century, the Dutch *rechtsstaat* ideology required written, systematized law. A penal code for natives was promulgated in 1873 — a near-copy of the Dutch national code (itself based on the French). Criminal jurisdiction remained in Dutch hands; the Sharīʿa courts (*priesterraden* or "priest-courts," reorganized by the Dutch in 1882) were given collegial structure but denied enforcement powers and were made dependent on Dutch *Landraden* courts for validation of their decisions.

The Sharīʿa courts' dependency on Dutch validation "continued in Indonesia until very recently." This structural subordination — not formal abolition — was the characteristic instrument of jural colonization in the archipelago.

## Snouck Hurgronje, Van Vollenhoven, and the Invention of Adatrecht

The decisive ideological move was made by two Dutch Orientalists:

**Christian Snouck Hurgronje** coined the term *adatrecht* and "discovered" adat as a legal system distinct from and competing with the Sharīʿa. Hailing from a tradition of scholars who viewed Islam as a political threat ("very much in the same vein as the French saw this religion and its law in Algeria"), Snouck framed adat and Sharīʿa as rival normative systems.

**Cornelius van Vollenhoven** elevated this discovery into a "science." He argued that *adat*, not the Sharīʿa, exercised the dominant sway over Archipelago populations, that Islamic law was "thin on the ground and virtually irrelevant," and that any weakening of adat would "open the floodgates to Islam." He compiled an extensive catalog identifying eighteen varieties of adat — a striking reduction given that the Archipelago comprised over a thousand islands, each with local variants.

Van Vollenhoven's compilation did more than describe: it transformed. "Once written, the adat 'violated a primary principle of adat law theory, that the adat lived in local tradition. Now, written, it lived in books, which Dutch judges, and Indonesian judges half a century later, used as if they were codes.'"

The orality of adat had been functional: it distributed legal knowledge communally rather than concentrating it in a specialist class. Writing ended this: "if no writing is required, then no commentaries are needed; thus, no commentators or jurists can become the locus of either legal or epistemic authority." Codification of adat was simultaneously the codification of Dutch legal authority over it.

## The 1927 Declaration and Reception Theory

Under the direct influence of van Vollenhoven's school, the Dutch government declared in 1927 that *adat*, not the Sharīʿa, was the normative law of the Netherlands Indies. The corollary doctrine was **reception theory**: Islamic law was valid in a given locale only insofar as it had been "received" into local adat practice. This reduced the Sharīʿa from an independent normative order to a secondary, locally conditioned supplement to Dutch-recognized customary law.

The consequence was to set a precedent that Indonesian courts continued to apply after independence: Islamic courts had to have their decisions validated by civil (formerly Dutch) courts. This dependency "continued in Indonesia until very recently."

## The Counter-Response: Islamic Intensification

Paradoxically, the restriction of the Sharīʿa by colonial law intensified Islamic observance. As had happened in British Malaya, "Islamic norms and institutions began to infiltrate, like never before, most societies" — a process driven both by Sumatran and other reformers and by the response of native elites who pursued Islamic legitimacy as an alternative to declining political authority. The influence of Cairo's al-Azhar (undergoing reform under Muḥammad ʿAbduh) and Najdite jurisprudence reached Java through Javanese scholars studying in the Ḥijāz, creating "a counter-movement that generated resistance not only to the secularized national elite but to the centuries-old and venerable adat as well."

This dynamic — colonial restriction of Islamic law producing Islamic revivalism — would be replicated "a century later, not only in modern Malaysia and Indonesia but also, and even more pronouncedly, in multiple corners of the Muslim world."
