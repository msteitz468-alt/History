# Original Sin and Grace (Augustinian Doctrine)

The doctrine of **original sin**, **prevenient grace**, and **predestination** worked out by
**[[augustine-of-hippo|Augustine]]** is the single most influential theological legacy of Latin
antiquity, the frame within which the medieval schoolmen and the sixteenth-century Reformers alike
argued about sin and salvation. Augustine himself called its defense the *causa gratiae*, "the case
for grace."

## The Doctrine

Augustine's turning point came around 395, answering Simplicianus's questions on Paul
(*Ad Simplicianum*): grace, not free human choice, has "the upper hand" — "What have you that you
did not receive?" Against the **[[pelagianism|Pelagian]]** view of sin as reversible imitation, he
held that humanity is "tied into" Adam's guilt in "the most intimate and irreversible manner
possible" — by physical descent from the common father of the race — so that fallen human beings
are not merely tempted but wounded, unable *not* to sin without grace. Human evil is not superficial
choice but a *discordia*, a permanent inner dislocation, visible even in slips of the tongue and
involuntary desire (a psychology Brown repeatedly likens to Freud's account of the unconscious).

Freedom, accordingly, is not a starting condition but something healed and achieved — real only
"in comparatives," greater and fuller freedom, culminating when knowledge and love are so united
that action flows from being "drawn" by delight (*Trahit sua quemque voluptas*). God gives not only
commands, blessings, and sanctions but *love* itself — the Spirit that "makes alive" against the
"letter that killeth." Baptism, for Augustine, does not complete a cure but begins a "prolonged and
precarious convalescence": the baptized Christian is still the wounded traveler of the Good
Samaritan parable, recovering for life in the "Inn" of the church. In his last decade this deepened
into an explicit doctrine of **predestination** and the **gift of perseverance**: God alone, in his
inscrutable Wisdom, determines who is finally saved — "a doctrine for fighting men," Brown argues,
meant to fuel effort, not passivity, though it provoked a "revolt of the monasteries" at Hadrumetum
and in southern Gaul.

## Contested Significance

The doctrine was contested from the start. **[[pelagius|Pelagius]]** thought it dissolved moral
effort; **[[julian-of-eclanum|Julian of Eclanum]]** charged that inherited guilt made God "the
persecutor of newborn children" and smuggled **[[manichaeism|Manichaean]]** dualism into the church
through the back door of concupiscence. Modern debate continues over how far Augustine's account of
sexual desire as a mark of the Fall shaped Western attitudes to the body — a charge Brown warns
against inflating into "demonizing" Augustine as "the evil genius of Europe." Paradoxically, Brown
argues, Augustine's harsh universal anthropology produced a *more* inclusive church than Pelagian
perfectionism: a communion of sinners embracing the whole of lay society, rather than a spotless
elite of saints.
