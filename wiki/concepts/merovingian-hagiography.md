# Merovingian Hagiography

**Saints' lives as a major medium of Merovingian political and ethical argument — not naïve piety but
sophisticated persuasion.** Jamie Kreiner's chapter (Oxford Handbook, Ch. 24) treats hagiography as "a
major part of Merovingian media culture," arguing every text "involved a double argument: first, that a
particular person ought to be praised… and second and more fundamentally, that these accomplishments
represented a legitimate ethics in the first place."

## Scale and Significance

The numbers are striking. Heinzelmann counts **509 men and women** from Merovingian Gaul celebrated in
hagiography — **43% of all saints** commemorated in Gaul/France between the dawn of Christianity and 1500.
[[gregory-of-tours|Gregory of Tours]] alone wrote six miracle-and-saints collections plus a translated
seventh (some 400 printed pages); Van Uytfanghe counts 21 texts certainly written 600–750, plus 24
possibly Merovingian — and "if sermons, liturgies, and histories resembling hagiography were included,
the number would probably double." Crucially, "there was no process resembling canonization": "a saint
was simply a person who received any of these forms of attention."

## Hagiography as Political and Ethical Argument

Hagiographers wrote "overwhelmingly" about people "involved in the highest circles of Merovingian
government," and the texts are "autobiographical," reflecting "the world of [their] author as much as…
the world of [their] ostensible subject." Marie-Céline Isaïa: hagiography is "not just a model for
individuals to follow… [but] a normative discourse that attempts to change the measures by which an entire
community or society sees and evaluates itself."

- **The *Life of Sadalberga*** (c. 680) is read as "a calculated vision of the Merovingian kingdom,"
  weaving legal, spiritual, and genealogical arguments (comparing her to Jerome's Paula and Eusebius's
  Helena). See [[merovingian-queenship]].
- **The *Life of Leudegar*** (bishop of Autun, murdered late 670s) "carefully rationalized Leudegar's
  actions at court," encoding criticism of King Childeric II (drunk in church at Easter) "to suggest that
  [he] had failed to fulfill his royal responsibilities."
- **The interpolated *Life of Germanus of Auxerre*** is counter-historiography: a seventh-century
  reviser inserted Peregrinus as an eighth apostolic bishop to add Auxerre to the seven cities Gregory had
  listed, arguing that leadership requires only receptivity "to the subtle messages that God sent" — *not*
  the hereditary apostolic blood Gregory claimed for his own family (see [[merovingian-historiography]]).

## Rhetoric, Memory, and Audience

Hagiographers designed texts with **mnemonic strategies** — "scenic spaces," significant objects, evoked
"sounds, tastes, smells." Persuasion theory held that minds "naturally tended to become complacent or
'lukewarm' (*tepescere*) when times were good," so literature supplied "an alternate source of energy."
The political class "spoke to each other through stories or *fabulae*," prizing the advisor who was
*iocundus in fabulis* — "a delightful storyteller" — and even comedy (Jonas of Bobbio's slapstick) served
argument.

## A Seventh-Century Shift

"In the seventh century especially, [hagiographers] were more likely to focus on what their protagonists
had accomplished when they were alive… the rate of miraculous activity at saints' tombs dropped" — a
change paralleling Byzantine and East Syrian debates over postmortem miracles, and connecting to the
afterlife questions surveyed in [[visions-and-the-afterlife-merovingian]].
