# Coercion and Consent in Nazi Germany

The central interpretive axis of recent [[nazi-germany]] historiography:
was the regime sustained primarily by terror and coercion, or by the
willing consent — even enthusiasm — of ordinary Germans? An etic
framework: contemporaries did not pose the dichotomy in these terms.

## The consent school

- **Robert Gellately** (*Backing Hitler*): the Nazis "did not need to use
  widespread terror against the population to establish the regime"; terror
  targeted "small groups of social outcasts" with majority approval; the
  publicized camps and denunciation behavior show a "self-policing society."
- **Hans-Ulrich Wehler**: a "broad consensus" backed the regime from the
  outset — "unreserved agreement" between "the rule of the Führer and the
  opinion of the people"; rejected calling it primarily "a terror regime."
- **Götz Aly** (*Hitler's Beneficiaries*): "the Third Reich was not a
  dictatorship maintained by force" — material prosperity and complicity in
  plunder bound "well-fed parasites" to the regime.
- **Johnson & Reuband** (oral-history survey): Nazism "so immensely popular
  among most Germans that intimidation and terror were rarely needed."
- **Frank Bajohr**: *Zustimmungsdiktatur* ("dictatorship by consent");
  **Peter Fritzsche**: the "voluntarist turn" (see [[volksgemeinschaft]]).

## Evans's rebuttal (2015, ch. 7 — his British Academy lecture; attributed)

**The 1933 terror wave targeted the organized working class, not
"outcasts."** SPD+KPD had 13.1 million votes to the Nazis' 11.7 million in
Nov. 1932. ~100,000 held in "protective custody" in 1933, 600+ dead in
custody; 3,000 SPD leaders arrested/tortured after the June 1933 ban; the
Köpenick blood-week (91 killed by SA torture); Mecklenburg's SPD
Minister-President Johannes Stelling tortured to death and dumped in a
river; union headquarters raided nationwide 2 May 1933. Coercion also
reached Catholics, liberals, and the conservative coalition partners
themselves — culminating in [[night-of-the-long-knives-1934]] victims Bose,
Jung, Klausener, Schleicher: "coercion over virtually the entire political
spectrum was seldom more openly in evidence." Nor was the takeover legal:
the Enabling Act passed only because Göring refused to count elected
Communist deputies.

**The law, not the camps, was the principal instrument of terror.** The
mid-1930s dip in camp numbers (~4,000–5,000 — the consent school's key
statistic) reflects repression shifting to the courts: prison population
69,000 (1932) → 122,000 (1937); 23,000 political prisoners in state
prisons in 1935; 5,255 high-treason convictions in 1937 alone; new capital
and "malicious gossip" offenses enforced by Special Courts and the
People's Court (Fraenkel's normative/prerogative dual state).

**Coercion beyond the [[gestapo|Gestapo]]**: ~200,000 Block Wardens by 1935
(~2 million with deputies by the war), Labour Front officials, compulsory
Hitler Youth (8.7 of 8.9 million eligible by 1939), forced Party
membership for civil servants, coerced Winter Aid giving, punitive labor
reassignment (1 million+ workers by 1939) — "a polymorphous, uncoordinated
but pervasive system of control" of which the Gestapo was "only one small
though important part."

**Plebiscites prove nothing**: voters marched to polls by stormtroopers,
booths removed or labeled "only traitors enter here," "no" voters beaten
or committed, forged returns exceeding electorates in places. Evans: a
free 1934 vote "might even have failed to win a majority."

**Denunciation was rare and parasitic on the apparatus**: Lippe (pop.
176,000) saw 3–51 denunciations/year; 42% of Augsburg denouncers were
Party members; only ~26% of Düsseldorf Gestapo cases began with public
denunciations. Denunciation "would have been meaningless" without the
coercive agencies that acted on it.

**The survey evidence cuts the other way**: Johnson & Reuband's sample
skewed to the most Nazi-socialized cohorts; on their own three-question
test only 18% answered yes to all three; their respondents recalled that
"all people became cautious. They simply didn't speak with people anymore."

## Evans's synthesis

Neither pure coercion nor pure voluntarism: propaganda worked where it
tapped existing beliefs and had "some relationship to reality"
(employment, order, prestige — Kershaw's Bavaria study), but Goebbels
himself (15 March 1933) spoke of "terrorising the other 48 per cent" as a
transitional necessity. Consent "can only be measured in situations in
which individuals can choose between real alternatives" (Dick Geary);
Longerich shows Germans grew *less* willing as antisemitic policy
radicalized, with fear and later guilt-denial masquerading as
indifference. Evans's stated verdict: "the absolute centrality of
violence, coercion and terror to the theory and practice of German
National Socialism from the very outset" — terror touched, "at one level
or another... the great majority" of Germans before 1939, which is what
explains its seamless extension to Poland in September 1939 (Kristallnacht
as the template for burning Polish synagogues).

## 1933 Evidence (Evans, *The Coming of the Third Reich*, 2003)

Vol. I supplies foundational cases for both poles:

- **Coercion masked as legal/voluntary process**: the Furtwängler affair —
  the conductor's 11 April 1933 open letter defending Jewish musicians met
  not with confrontation but financial strangulation of the Philharmonic's
  subsidies until he begged for a Reich takeover (26 October 1933).
- **Consent without enthusiasm**: the 1 April boycott — staged SA compliance
  against genuine public passivity, scattered counter-demonstrations, and
  pre-boycott rush buying. "The general lack of public opposition to the
  action was striking, but so too was the general lack of public enthusiasm
  for it."
- **Elite predisposition**: 300 professors publicly petitioned for Hitler in
  March 1933, 700 in May — including the Jewish law professor Walter
  Jellinek, who thought the antisemitic measures limited and justified, and
  was then purged himself. Evans's thesis (attributed): "a large majority of
  the educated elites in German society, whatever their political allegiance
  up to that point, were already predisposed to embrace many of the
  principles upon which Nazism rested."
- **The fear baseline**: Haffner on why the anti-Nazi 56% caved — "The
  simplest... nearly always the most basic reason was fear. Join the thugs to
  avoid being beaten up." And the nod-and-wink decision mechanism (leadership
  hints translated into violence below) recorded at
  [[nazi-seizure-of-power-1933]].

## Limitations

The dichotomy itself is increasingly criticized as false: consent was
manufactured under conditions where dissent was dangerous, and coercion
was targeted and selective, resting on denunciation from below.
