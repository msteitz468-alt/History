# Classical Roman Jurisprudence

The apogee and aftermath of Roman legal science — the **"high classical" law** of the Antonine and
Severan jurists and the **"epiclassical" law** of the third century to Diocletian. This is the body
of jurisprudence that, transmitted through Justinian's *Digest*, became the **doctrinal bedrock of
most modern legal systems**. Treated in [[bowman-cah-v12-2005|CAH XII]] Ch. 7 (**David Ibbetson**,
high classical; **David Johnston**, epiclassical).

## High classical law (Ibbetson)

The century after c. 130, and above all under the [[severan-dynasty|Severans]], saw legal science
reach its peak. Two elements were central: the **jurists** and their **scientific approach to legal
thought**.

- **The jurist-bureaucrat**: from Hadrian, jurists were fully integrated into imperial service — a new
  type of *lawyer-bureaucrat* holding office (praetorian prefect, *a libellis*) while still writing,
  advising, and disputing. Most were **provincials, not senators**; standing now rested on
  **professional merit** (Hadrian's reform of the *ius respondendi*).
- **The five jurists**: a remarkably short, intense flowering. The later **Law of Citations** (426)
  privileged five — **Papinian, Paul, Gaius, Ulpian, Modestinus** — of whom Ulpian and Paul alone
  furnish **half of Justinian's *Digest***. Their genres: systematic/pedagogic works (above all
  **Gaius' *Institutes***, the enduring model) and analytical works (the great **commentaries** *ad
  Edictum* and *ad Sabinum*; casuistic *Digesta*, *Quaestiones*, *Responsa*; monographs).
- **Legal science**: radically **individualistic** (no jurist's view beyond challenge; the old
  **Sabinian/Proculian schools** faded after Hadrian), yet united by a shared conviction that law was
  a **self-consistent, rational system** (the *ars boni et aequi*) to be harmonized — possible
  precisely because formal law-making was, for a time, restrained.

### The sources of law shift to the emperor

The classical period coincided with the **eclipse of the old law-sources** and their replacement by
**imperial law-making**: comitial *leges* lapsed; *senatus consulta* (increasingly mere ratifications
of the *oratio principis*) faded after Caracalla; Hadrian's **codification of the praetor's Edict**
(by Salvius Julianus) ended magisterial legislation. **Imperial constitutions** now had the force of
*lex*, in several forms: **edicta** (general legislation — e.g. the [[constitutio-antoniniana-212|Constitutio
Antoniniana]]), **mandata** (administrative instructions), **decreta** (judicial decisions), and —
most important in practice — **rescripta** (responses: *epistulae* to officials, *subscriptiones* to
private petitioners), the latter giving citizens **free authoritative legal advice**.

## Epiclassical law (Johnston)

Convention ends the "classical" period with **Severus Alexander (235)**, but Johnston (following
Schulz) stresses **no sudden collapse** — the years 235–305 were a **consolidation** of late-classical
trends. Two changes define the **"epiclassical"** age:

- **The eclipse of the independent jurist**: juristic effort was redirected from writing in one's own
  name to **composing rescripts in the imperial chancellery**. The surviving rescripts (clustering
  238–46, 259–60, and above all under **Diocletian**, ~1,200) remained **faithful to classical law** —
  Diocletian's chancellery was **classicizing**, resisting "hellenization."
- **The rise of the compilation**: around 300 appear the first anthologies/epitomes of classical law —
  the official **Codices Gregorianus (291) and Hermogenianus (295)** of imperial rescripts (the
  foundation of Justinian's later *Code*), Hermogenian's *iuris epitomae*, and the influential
  pseudonymous **Pauli Sententiae** — simple, clear, doctrinal works that became "the model for the
  future."

## Significance

Roman classical jurisprudence is the **intellectual summit of ancient law** and the most enduring of
Rome's legacies: through the *Digest* and *Institutes* it shaped — and still shapes — the civil-law
tradition of continental Europe and beyond. Its history also tracks a constitutional shift: from a law
made and refined by **independent jurists** to a law emanating from the **imperial chancellery**, the
classicizing rescripts of Diocletian forming the bridge before **Constantine's** characteristic *lex
generalis* and its "Greek and eastern influence."
