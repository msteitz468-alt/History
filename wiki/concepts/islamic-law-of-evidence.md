## Definition and Scope

The Islamic law of evidence governs how claims are proved before the [[qadi-judiciary]]. It centers on **shahāda** (oral testimony), but encompasses **bayyina** (evidence broadly), **yamīn** (oath), **iqrār** (confession/acknowledgment), and **qarāʾin al-aḥwāl** (circumstantial indicants). The Sharīʿa "accords primary importance to oral testimony (shahāda), regarding it as the most evincive of all forms of evidence (bayyina)" — so strongly associated that the two terms became nearly interchangeable, though technically bayyina is the broader category.

The moral-epistemic foundation is distinctive: "The general principle by which written records, unaccompanied by oral testimony, are rejected as having no evidentiary value ultimately rests on the assumption that in documentary evidence there is no moral accountability. In the nature of it, oral testimony does not allow for avoidance of this accountability." The entire evidentiary system is thus anchored in personal moral responsibility before God, not in documentary or institutional verification.

## Oral Testimony (Shahāda)

### Definition

Shahāda is "attestation with regard to a right of a second party against a third" — distinguishing it from iqrār, which is "a right owed to a second party by the attesting party." Testimony must involve the **certain knowledge** (ʿilm al-yaqīn) of having seen or heard a particular event; all testimony based on probability or conjecture is inadmissible.

### Taḥammul vs. Adāʾ

The jurists distinguish two stages: **taḥammul** (charging the testimony — the act of receiving and internalizing information) and **adāʾ** (discharging it — conveying it before a magistrate). Almost anyone can perform taḥammul, including slaves, non-Muslims, minors, and the temporarily insane. The conditions for adāʾ are far more stringent and are assessed at the time of testimony, not at the time of witnessing.

### Qualifications for Adāʾ

A witness discharging testimony must be:

1. **Free** (according to some jurists — the Ḥanafites require freedom at the time of adāʾ)
2. **Of majority age** (a minor who witnessed an event must wait until adulthood to testify)
3. **Muslim** (a non-Muslim may witness events but must convert before testifying in most areas)
4. **Capable of speech** — though Mālik permitted testimony by signs, and some Ḥanbalites by writing
5. **Capable of accurate reporting** (ḍabṭ) — not absent-minded, careless, or obtuse
6. **Possessed of personal rectitude** (ʿadl) — defined as not having committed a grave sin (kabīra) and not persisting in minor sins. Grave sins include abandoning the pillars of religion; habitual immoral behavior (fisq); and practicing unseemly professions (dancers, singers, clowns)
7. **Male**, when testifying in ḥudūd or qiṣāṣ matters
8. **Free of a qadhf conviction** (false accusation of zinā)
9. **Free of conflict of interest** (tuhma) — a witness may not testify for relatives or in matters that personally benefit him; testimony against a long-standing enemy or rival tribe is also invalid

### Testimony of Women

"The majority of jurists permit the testimony of women in most areas of the law, with the exception of ḥudūd and qiṣāṣ." The standard formula in non-ḥudūd/qiṣāṣ civil matters: one man and two women. "It is a universal doctrine that one woman's testimony is both admissible and sufficient in matters related to midwifery, forensic examination of the female body, nursing and other areas of testimony where women are knowledgeable and men cannot intrude."

### Blind Witnesses

Ḥanafites reject the testimony of a blind person at both taḥammul and adāʾ stages. Mālikites, Ḥanbalites, and Twelver Shīʿites permit blind persons to testify in matters that "completely depend upon hearing."

## Witness Numbers by Offense or Transaction Type

| Matter | Witnesses Required |
|---|---|
| Zinā (adultery/fornication) | Four trustworthy adult males, simultaneously present |
| Theft, highway robbery, alcohol | Two males |
| Marriage, divorce, guardianship, agency | One male and two women |
| Pecuniary contracts (general) | One male witness and an oath |
| Midwifery, female bodily examination | One woman (sole testimony sufficient) |
| Expert testimony (doctor, master-builder) | One specialist witness (solitary admissible) |

Producing more witnesses than the legal minimum (niṣāb) does not strengthen a case for most schools; some Mālikite jurists considered additional testimony corroborative.

## Character Investigation (Tazkiyya)

The qāḍī "not only conducts cross-examination of the witnesses but also employs individuals whose task it is to check, in the community, on the witnesses' character." This background investigation (tazkiyya) is mandatory in ḥudūd and qiṣāṣ matters. Abū Ḥanīfa and the Twelver Shīʿites held that in non-ḥudūd/qiṣāṣ suits, character investigation should not occur unless the defendant objects to the witnesses' probity.

## Secondary Testimony (Al-Shahāda ʿalā al-Shahāda)

When a primary witness cannot attend court due to illness or other cause, two secondary witnesses (shuhūd farʿ) may convey the primary witness's testimony (shāhid aṣl) to the qāḍī. This requires the primary witness to have dictated (istirsāl) the content to the secondary witnesses, unless they had already heard the original testimony before another qāḍī.

This form of testimony is generally admitted in non-punitive matters (pecuniary contracts, marriage, divorce, manumission). Ḥanafites reject it in ḥudūd and qiṣāṣ — "matters that do not tolerate the slightest of doubt." Note that al-shahāda ʿalā al-shahāda is also barred in ḥudūd cases under the general maxim that ḥudūd must be averted at the existence of the slightest doubt. Shāfiʿites and Ḥanbalites require that the reason for the primary witness's unavailability continue to hold until the qāḍī renders verdict; if the primary witness becomes available, he must appear in person and secondary testimony becomes inadmissible.

## Written Evidence

Written records have severely limited evidentiary standing. The Shāfiʿite doctrine (and a Ḥanafite opinion) holds that a qāḍī cannot act on the basis of a record he himself once wrote but no longer remembers. A witness's testimony based solely on his own written notes, which he does not recall writing, is invalid. The rejection of unaccompanied documentary evidence is grounded in the moral-epistemic principle: oral testimony holds witnesses personally accountable in a way that written documents cannot.

## Confession (Iqrār)

Confession is ordinarily the strongest form of self-generated proof, requiring no additional witnesses. To be admissible, a confession must be made freely (without coercion), by a person who is adult, sane, and (in ḥudūd matters) free. In ḥudūd cases — unlike in other areas of law, where confession is irrevocable — a confession may be retracted, canceling the ḥadd penalty (except in qadhf). The Ḥanafites also hold that a drunkard's confession is not binding as evidence of his own intoxication.

---

## Circumstantial Evidence (Qarāʾin al-Aḥwāl)

Circumstantial indicants (qarīna) are admissible in some contexts but carry less weight than direct testimony. For instance, pregnancy out of wedlock is deemed by some schools (Ḥanbalites, Mālikites) as evidence of zinā, but the woman may rebut this by claiming rape; the counter-claim can itself be supported by circumstantial evidence (e.g., witnesses to her screaming, or a claim of impregnation during sleep). These contexts show that the law is not absolutist in excluding circumstantial indicants — they operate as amāra (indicants) that can tip scales when direct proof is unavailable.

## Oaths (Yamīn)

When a plaintiff lacks bayyina, the defendant is called to swear an oath. Oaths may be intensified (taghlīẓ) in non-pecuniary litigation by referencing a list of divine attributes or by facing the Kaʿba. The defendant is normatively the oath-taker, but in certain cases the qāḍī may require a plaintiff's oath (e.g., a wife seeking spousal support from an absent husband). Both parties may be asked to take mutual oaths (taḥāluf) in disputes over sale price or object characteristics; the contract is then cancelled.

If the defendant refuses to take the oath (nukūl), the Ḥanafites hold that the qāḍī must find for the plaintiff. The Mālikites, Shāfiʿites, and some Ḥanbalites instead revert the oath obligation to the plaintiff (radd al-yamīn): if the plaintiff swears, he wins; if he refuses, judgment is against him. The refusal of the defendant functions as the equivalent of the plaintiff producing one witness, such that the plaintiff's own subsequent oath produces the combined weight of "witness plus oath" required in pecuniary matters.

Oaths are "evidence because they represent and constitute a means to counter a claim." The Mālikites hold that once an oath is taken it terminates the dispute permanently; other schools permit the plaintiff to return with new evidence and launch a new suit.

## Liability of False Witnesses

False testimony resulting in conviction and death penalty: all schools except the Ḥanafites condemn the false witness to the same punishment. Ḥanafites hold that death from false testimony is indirect homicide (qatl bi-sabab), requiring blood-money (diya). If one of two witnesses retracts testimony after implementation, he pays half the victim's loss. If one of four zinā witnesses retracts or contradicts the others on details of the sexual act, he and the other witnesses are charged with qadhf and penalized.

A false witness "will never be able to testify again" and faces the additional taʿzīr punishment of humiliating public display (tashhīr) in the marketplace after Friday prayer — including face-blackening and potential beating and imprisonment.

---

## Possession as Evidence

Testimony alone that a plaintiff owns property is "deemed, if not supported by any further evidence, to be weaker than the fact of possession (thubūt yad) by the defendant." Possession is a preponderant factor in favor of the party who holds it; when both parties produce equivalent testimonial evidence, the party in possession generally retains the property. Ḥanafites divide the value between two equally-supported claimants when a third party holds possession without proof of ownership.

## Historiography

Islamic law of evidence has attracted sustained scholarly interest because of the tension between its theoretical oral-testimony primacy and the rich documentary record (sijillāt, ḥujaj) that actually underpinned commercial and property transactions in practice. Scholars including Hallaq and Brinkley Messick have argued that written instruments played a more significant role in actual commercial litigation than the theoretical hierarchy suggests ("Commercial Litigation," 203–12).

The testimony rules for women and the witness number requirements have been central to modern Islamic legal reform debates, particularly regarding gender equality before the law.
