# Trepanation

**Trepanation** (also trephination) is a medical procedure involving the removal of a piece of a living human’s skull. Archaeological examples frequently show evidence of survival, indicating that the operation was sometimes successfully performed in prehistoric contexts.

## Definition and Scope

The glossary of the encyclopedia records:

> "trepanation: Medical procedure involving the removal of a piece of a living human’s skull. Some skulls bear traces of the survival of multiple trepanations." (range_08:22–26)

This concise definition captures both the technique and the bioarchaeological evidence of patient survival (healed bone margins around the opening).

## Context in Prehistoric Europe

Trepanation is attested from the Neolithic onward in European skeletal assemblages. Common interpretations include:
- Therapeutic intervention for head trauma, cranial pressure, or neurological conditions.
- Ritual or magical practice.
- Experimental or status-related surgery.

The glossary notes the possibility of multiple successful operations on the same individual, demonstrating both technical skill and post-operative care in prehistoric societies.

## Relation to Other Concepts

- Contributes to understandings of health, disease, and social response in [[periods/neolithic]], [[periods/chalcolithic]], and later periods.
- Linked to broader discussions of ritual and ideology (see glossary entries for related practices).
- Contrasts with other forms of body modification or post-mortem treatment.
- Evidence often derives from the same burial contexts that inform [[concepts/tumulus]], [[concepts/urnfield-culture]], and elite or special-status treatment.

## Geographic and Chronological Scope

Documented across temperate and Mediterranean Europe from the Mesolithic/Neolithic transition through the Iron Age. Survival rates vary; some series show high frequencies of healed trepanations, suggesting the procedure was not invariably fatal.

## Historiography

Trepanation was recognized in the nineteenth century through examination of crania from barrows and other monuments. Early debates centered on whether openings were ante- or post-mortem and on the tools and techniques employed (flint, obsidian, or metal). The 2004 glossary entry is deliberately neutral and descriptive, emphasizing the survival evidence as the key archaeological signature.
