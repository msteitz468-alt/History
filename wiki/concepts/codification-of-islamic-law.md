## Definition

Codification of Islamic law refers to the process by which the plural, jurist-generated, school-based body of [[fiqh]] was extracted from its organic hermeneutical environment and compressed into state-issued codes, statutes, and acts. The term designates both a technique and a structural transformation: it describes the conversion of a non-state, community-based legal system into an instrument of sovereign will.

Hallaq distinguishes the pre-modern [[sharia]] — whose operative form was "the intellectual and hermeneutical work of private individuals, jurists whose claim to authority was primarily epistemic, but also religious and moral … not political in the modern sense of the word, and … not subject to the fluctuations of legislation, reflecting the interests of a dominant class" — from the codified residue, which is law-as-will-of-the-state.

## Historical Development

The Ottoman **Mecelle-i Ahkam-ı Adliye** (1870–77) was the first systematic attempt to codify portions of [[fiqh]] doctrine (Hanafi commercial and civil law) in the format of European-style articles. Even this partial codification announced the structural shift: sovereignty over what counted as law passed from the juristic profession to the state apparatus.

Post-independence nationalist governments accelerated the process. The **1917 Ottoman Law of Family Rights** codified personal status law — the last domain the Sharīa still occupied — and spread across Lebanon, Jordan, and Syria. Egypt's 1929 family law, Tunisia's 1956 Code of Personal Status, Pakistan's 1961 Muslim Family Laws Ordinance, and the Indonesian 1991 Compilation of Islamic Law all continued this trajectory in different registers.

Hallaq identifies five principal **devices** by which codification reshaped the surviving fiqh:

1. **Necessity (ḍarūra)** — transposed from a limited substantive-law exception to an all-encompassing interpretive principle capable of overriding the textual corpus.
2. **Procedural exclusion** — registrars and courts were instructed to enforce only certain rules (e.g., minimum marriage ages), effectively nullifying fiqh provisions without formally abrogating them.
3. **Takhayyur and talfīq** — "selection" of opinions across schools and "amalgamation" of previously incompatible doctrinal elements to produce new composite rules. Both techniques were forbidden in classical [[madhhab]] jurisprudence.
4. **Neo-ijtihād** — interpretive freedom unmoored from [[usul-al-fiqh]] hermeneutics, exemplified by Tunisia's 1956 prohibition of polygamy on the ground that perfect equity among wives is practically impossible.
5. **Siyāsa shar'iyya expansion** — the ruler's prerogative to administer law was extended beyond recognition, becoming a near-unlimited state prerogative over procedure and legislation.

## Structural Consequences

Hallaq insists that codification did not merely reform Islamic law — it terminated the conditions under which the Sharīa had operated as a system:

- **Elimination of pluralism (ikhtilāf)**: The code eliminates "almost all such juristic and hermeneutical possibilities, leaving both the litigants and the judge with a single formulation and, in all likelihood, a single mode of judicial application."
- **Severance from Arabicate hermeneutics**: The methodology of [[usul-al-fiqh]], grounded in Arabic grammar and the inter-textual disciplines, was not reproducible within state law faculties.
- **Homogenization of the legal subject**: Codification serves the modern state's drive to produce uniform, interchangeable citizens; fiqh's case-by-case, context-sensitive adjudication resisted this.
- **Entexting and politicization**: The surviving fiqh residue, once transplanted into state structures, became subject to politics in ways wholly foreign to the pre-modern system.

## Gender and Codification

A significant theme in Hallaq's analysis is that codification restructured family law in ways that typically *reduced* women's effective rights despite reform rhetoric. Pre-modern fiqh gave wives extensive procedural recourse and recognized husbands as equally capable of *nushūz* (marital disobedience). Modern codes contracted the definition of nushūz to exclusively female failure to obey the husband-as-head-of-household. The 1917 Ottoman Family Law reduced multi-layered fiqh rights to brief articles whose "brevity deprives the modern court of the full view of these rights."

The reengineering drew directly from French civil law models (notably the 1804 Code civil's doctrine of wifely obedience) rather than from independent Islamic reasoning. "Nationalism has always been a masculine conception that subordinates the feminine."

## Relationship to Nation-State Sovereignty

Codification is analytically inseparable from [[islamic-law-nation-state|state monopolization of law]]. The code is the legal instrument of the modern state project: "it is eminently arguable that unifying and homogenizing the law — and all that which constitutes the world of the Subject — is one of the primary concerns of the modern state." Even when codified law closely followed fiqh doctrine, the act of codification announced that it was the state — not the juristic community — that determined what was law.

## Historiographical Note

Hallaq argues that the vocabulary of "reform" (iṣlāḥ) mystifies this transformation by embedding it in a narrative of progress. The accurate description is structural displacement: "To call this transformation 'reform' is to engage, obliquely and often unconsciously, in political discourses that find their origins in colonialist ideology and cultural technology."
