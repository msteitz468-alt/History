## Definition and Scope

German regionalism refers to the historically persistent attachment of Germans to particular territorial, linguistic, and cultural sub-units — principalities, kingdoms, free cities, landscapes, dialects — that coexisted with, competed against, and in many ways preceded pan-German national identity. *Heimat* (literally "home" or "homeland") is the defining German concept for this localized place-attachment: "the word Germans reach for to express the attachments of place," simultaneously denoting a specific place and landscape, a particular set of associations in both spatial and temporal terms.

Regionalism in Germany was not simply pre-modern remnant. It was actively constructed, cultivated through literature, walking culture, illustration, photography, association life, and civic institutions across the entire period covered by the Oxford Handbook. Its relationship to nationalism was not one of simple supersession; rather, the two co-existed in tension and mutual implication throughout modern German history.

## Historical Roots: The Territorial State

The deep structural foundation of German regionalism was the political fragmentation of the Holy Roman Empire. "It has been conventional to begin consideration of the second half of the eighteenth century by noting that there was no single Germany, but only 'the electoral princes, 94 spiritual and lay princes, 103 counts, 40 prelates, 51 free towns, in all some 300 separate "territories."'" While the majority of Germans lived in the great territorial states rather than micro-principalities, the multi-layered sovereignty of the Empire produced multiple foci of loyalty — Reich, territorial state, confession, locality — that the nation-state never fully displaced.

The emergence of territorial patriotism (*Vaterlandsliebe* for one's specific territory rather than Germany as a whole) was a product of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. The Oxford Handbook's chapter on "Origins of Modern Germany" argues that territorial elites in places like Hesse, Pomerania, and Württemberg developed their own networks, churches, and administrative traditions oriented toward territorial rather than national frames. Unlike in France or England, where regional nobles looked increasingly to national patronage networks, "the emerging territorial elites looked increasingly toward territorial sources of influence and income." ## Enlightenment Walking Culture and the Construction of Heimat

The late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries saw the elaboration of *Heimat* as a cultural concept through the practice of walking (*Wandern*). Friedrich Ludwig Jahn's *Volksthum* (1810) devoted its final chapter to "walks in the Fatherland" (*vaterländische Wanderungen*), arguing that Germans needed to experience "the many places of their Fatherland and thereby bind together the 'limbs of an outstretched people.'" Jahn drew on predecessors like Ernst Moritz Arndt and Johann Gottfried Seume, who had walked through German territories recording the diversity of place.

This walking culture established practices of "sociography" — active documentation of local social networks, landscapes, customs, and histories — that would fill the civic culture of nineteenth-century Germany through associations, museums, and illustrated publications.

## The Nineteenth Century: Pluralism and the National Frame

The foundational tension of German regionalism in the nineteenth century was between the celebration of diversity and the imperatives of national unity. Theodor Fontane's *Wanderungen durch die Mark Brandenburg* (5 volumes, 1862–1882) exemplified one resolution: exploring local particularities in loving, patient detail, with the implicit argument that German variety constituted a deeper richness than French or British centralization could offer. Fontane's motto — "see how the good lies so near at hand" — inverted the conventional hierarchy of exotic and local.

The illustrated weeklies (*Illustrirte Zeitung*, *Gartenlaube*) provided the mass-market medium for Heimat culture. The *Gartenlaube* (founded 1853) "without actually mentioning Heimat, their appeal to readers was saturated with place sentimentality in the German mode." Late nineteenth-century photography books — hundreds of them cataloguing German cities, regions, and landscapes — "made German places look as though the path to the present had consisted only of building, never rebuilding."

Civic associations of every kind — singing clubs, gymnastics societies, natural history societies, hiking clubs — dedicated themselves to "filling German space with sound" or systematically documenting local flora, fauna, and customs. These associations were simultaneously local in focus and national in scope, producing a distinctive federalism of the cultural sphere.

## Regionalism Under National Unification

[[German-unification]] under Prussian leadership in 1871 did not suppress regional identity but complicated it. The new [[german-empire]] was explicitly "an 'everlasting alliance' of states," with Bavaria, Württemberg, and Saxony retaining their armies, legal systems, and strong regional identities. Protestant-Prussian foundation myths claiming that Frederick the Great and Bismarck fulfilled Germany's historical destiny were contested in Catholic areas, where attachment to Habsburg or to the more locally rooted traditions of southern and western Germany remained strong.

The Rhineland is a case study in competing regional and national loyalties. Having experienced French administration from 1794–1815, the Rhineland retained French legal codes (the Rhenish law survived Prussian annexation), and many Rhinelanders "did not see themselves as an integral part of the Prussian monarchy, but as an independent region — administered by Prussia, but having more in common with France than with the government in Berlin." As late as 1837, the Prussian Minister of the Interior felt he was "in a foreign country occupied by Prussia" when visiting the region.

## Heimat and National Socialism

The Oxford Handbook documents the Nazi appropriation of Heimat rhetoric as a vehicle for authoritarian mobilization. The early scenes from Riefenstahl's *Triumph of the Will* — "bird's eye view of Germany from above, the peasant costumes, young working men calling out the name of their Heimats" — represented "a paean to the unity in diversity of German place." The Autobahn program, directed by Fritz Todt, was explicitly framed as revealing "a 'colorful succession of alternating landscape images'" — the road-builders would "reconstruct" beauty where it had "already disappeared."

Ricarda Huch's 1933 resignation letter from the Prussian Academy of the Arts is a counter-instance: she insisted that Nazism's centralization, force, and "limitless self-praising" were "neither German nor healthy," and that "about what is German there are many opinions." Her invocation of German diversity against centralized authoritarianism preserved the pluralist dimension of the Heimat concept against its nationalist appropriation.

## Post-1945: Expulsion, Loss, and Reconstruction of Place

The catastrophe of 1945 shattered established Heimat relationships for millions. Between 1945 and 1949, twelve million Germans were expelled from eastern and central Europe — the "towns of East Prussia became 'names that no one named'" (Marion Gräfin Dönhoff). The expellees created a distinctive postwar Heimat culture focused on loss: organizations like the *Bund der Heimatvertriebenen und Entrechteten* (BHE) kept alive attachments to places that no longer existed within German borders.

In both Germanies, postwar reconstruction was accompanied by an "outburst of localist activism" that tried to rebuild Heimat feelings from the rubble. But "it was all so very false in its heartfelt sincerity. The books of photographs produced in the decades after the war — and there were hundreds of them, in every city and region — made German places look as though the path to the present had consisted only of building, never rebuilding."

In East Germany, the SED state paradoxically encouraged Heimat sentiment as a substitute national identity. By the 1970s, "many East German citizens actively cultivated a sense of Heimat within socialism, creating, by the 1970s, a genuine if fragile sense of belonging." The village of Kella on the inner-German border — studied by anthropologist Daphne Berdahl — exemplifies how local place-making persisted even under extreme state surveillance, with "collective rituals like the Palm Sunday procession" sustaining Heimat feelings independent of political ideology.

## Historiography and Analytical Significance

German regionalism has undergone significant reassessment in recent scholarship. Earlier historians (notably Mack Walker, *German Home Towns*, 1971) treated the attachment to the *Heimatstadt* and territorial particularism as obstacles to German modernization — a dimension of the *Sonderweg* argument that Germany's distinctive character derived from its failure to develop a nationally unified civic culture. More recent scholarship (Celia Applegate, *A Nation of Provincials*, 1990; Alon Confino, *The Nation as a Local Metaphor*, 1997) has reframed regionalism as constitutive of German national identity rather than as its antithesis: Germans understood themselves *as* Germans through their attachments to particular places.

The Oxford Handbook, particularly Chapter 3 (Celia Applegate), treats place-attachment as "the most intimate form of national self-understanding" — not opposed to nationalism but embedded within it. The Handbook also highlights the exclusionary dimension: the sense of Heimat has been "both inclusive in its embrace of places in the plural and profoundly exclusive of those who are deemed placeless." Jews, migrants, and minorities have repeatedly been constructed as the placeless others against whose rootlessness German Heimat attachment defined itself.
