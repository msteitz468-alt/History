## Definition and Scope

The *sekkan* system refers to the institutional arrangement by which the head of the Northern House of the [[fujiwara-clan|Fujiwara clan]] dominated the Heian court through the twin offices of *sesshō* (regent for a minor emperor) and *kampaku* (regent for an adult emperor), combined with a systematic marriage strategy that ensured the reigning emperor was always the Fujiwara leader's maternal grandson. The term *sekkan jidai* ("era of the sesshō and kampaku") is the conventional Japanese periodization for 967–1068, when "an unbroken succession of Fujiwara held the office of regent" (McCullough, CHJ Vol. 2, p. 5), though the system's foundations were laid from 857 onward.

The sekkan system is distinct from ordinary court office-holding: it was not grounded in the statutory (*ritsuryō*) code, had no fixed legal basis, and required reappointment by each new emperor rather than continuing across reigns. Its authority was nonetheless real — the regent was "the greatest concentration of governmental power and wealth in Japan" (CHJ Vol. 2, p. 76).

## The Two Regental Offices

**Sesshō** (*sessho*): regent for a child or minor emperor. The *sesshō* could act fully as the emperor in approving official documents, conducting ceremonies, and in all other official capacities — in the words of a twelfth-century courtier, "the *sesshō* in effect became the emperor."

**Kampaku**: regent for an adult emperor. Unlike the *sesshō*, the *kampaku* acted for the emperor only in his dealings with the Council of State and was not otherwise empowered to represent him; he "remained a minister to the emperor" (CHJ Vol. 2, p. 77). In practice, as the post became routinely filled and the alternation between the titles a mechanical function of the emperor's age, the practical significance of the distinction eroded, though "the terminological distinction itself was always faithfully maintained."

**Nairan** ("private inspection"): a third regental title, first employed in the time of Tokihira and Sugawara no Michizane. It gave its holders powers mostly identical with *kampaku*, but of lower official status, and crucially allowed the holder to take an active part in the business and meetings of the Council of State — unlike *sesshō* and *kampaku* holders, who were "generally removed from direct involvement in the Council's affairs." Active political managers like [[fujiwara-michinaga|Fujiwara no Michinaga]] preferred the *nairan* title precisely because it allowed direct administrative leadership over the Council.

## The Institutional Origins

The first formal recognition came in 857, when Fujiwara no Yoshifusa was appointed Chancellor (*daijō daijin*), and in 866, when an imperial rescript directed him to take charge of government as regent (the title *sesshō* itself being only implied). This was "an ad hoc arrangement without legal basis in the statutory code or in any of the amendments thereto" (CHJ Vol. 2, p. 77). The arrangement remained ad hoc throughout: the regent had to be reappointed by each succeeding emperor.

Fujiwara no Mototsune consolidated the position further, seeking "a combination of the ad hoc office of regent with a statutory ministerial post" while ensuring the regency stood apart from and superior to the regular statutory organs. Mototsune's appointment as *kampaku* in 887 gave that title its first clear use for an adult-emperor context.

The distinction between *sesshō* and *kampaku* was first clearly articulated in the time of Fujiwara no Tadahira (880–949), who resigned the *sesshō* office and was appointed *kampaku* when Emperor Murakami reached adulthood.

Until Kaneie's day, the chieftainship of the Fujiwara clan was held by the clansman with the highest statutory office and rank, regardless of who was regent. From Kaneie onward, the regental position became fully independent, the regent resigning his Council of State titles on appointment.

## Marriage Politics

The Fujiwara monopolized regental power not through force but through systematic marriage strategy: placing Fujiwara daughters as imperial consorts, ensuring that the resulting children became heirs to the throne, and positioning the Fujiwara leader as the reigning emperor's maternal grandfather — the natural regent for a minor grandson. This strategy is characterized in the CHJ as "the game of marriage politics" played "with consummate skill, aided by the good fortune of usually having available a supply of eligible imperial consorts" (CHJ Vol. 2, p. 5).

The mechanism worked as follows: emperors were frequently persuaded to abdicate before reaching manhood (early abdication had been common since the mid-seventh century and "was often a welcome escape from the ceremonial demands of the position, palace intrigue, and the constant requests for favors"). The child emperor who succeeded was then a Fujiwara grandson, for whom the clan head served as *sesshō*. When that emperor reached adulthood, the regent became *kampaku*; when the emperor eventually abdicated in favor of another Fujiwara grandson, the cycle continued.

The exclusion of rival clans from high office proceeded in parallel. In 850, four of nineteen senior ministers (*kugyo*) were Fujiwara; by Yoshifusa's death in 872, seven of eighteen; by 972, eleven of nineteen; and at Michinaga's death in 1028, twenty-two of twenty-five, with the remaining three Minamoto all closely related to Michinaga by marriage.

The system's dependence on successful marriage politics was also its structural vulnerability: when Yorimichi (992–1074) failed to produce a Fujiwara-born imperial heir, he could not maintain the regency without blood ties to the emperor, and "control of the court slipped from regental hands as the imperial line... reasserted its authority" (CHJ Vol. 2, p. 72).

## Government Under the Sekkan System

Despite the conventional image of the regency as purely ceremonial, the CHJ argues that the regent and Council of State ministers "were actively engaged in the formulation and implementation of practical, mundane policy for a broad range of problems with nationwide import" (CHJ Vol. 2, p. 76). Regency government supervised:

- Diplomatic, cultural, and commercial relations with foreign states
- Defenses against foreign piratical invaders
- Domestic revolt and warfare in the provinces
- Repeated attempts to regulate shoen growth
- Continuous adjustment of statutory institutions to evolving social and economic conditions

The historiographical consensus, however, as summarized in the CHJ, holds that "the terms 'regency government' and 'regency period' are misnomers in the sense that the regency was not the significant distinguishing characteristic of the period but simply an adjustment in, or relatively limited transformation of, the central government mechanism centering on the Council of State." The genuinely new phase in Heian history is attributed instead to the change in the government's policies toward provincial administration and taxes at the beginning of the tenth century.

Government finances under the sekkan continued to depend on tax revenues from public lands through provincial administrations, but shoen grew substantially in importance. The regent, as the most influential potential patron, became the primary beneficiary of commendations — one of Michinaga's contemporaries complained "hyperbolically that there was not a needle's breadth of land in the country that did not belong to him."

## End of the System

The system ended not with a formal abolition but with demographic failure. Emperor Go-Sanjō (r. 1068–73), the first emperor in 170 years whose mother was not a Fujiwara, reasserted monarchical powers. The subsequent [[insei|insei]] system of retired-emperor rule displaced Fujiwara regency as the dominant mechanism of political power for the final century of the Heian period.

## Historiography

The sekkan system was long described as a simple usurpation — powerful nobles displacing emperors and governing in their name. Modern Japanese historiography, reflected in the CHJ, substantially revises this: the regency is better understood as a formalization of existing power rather than a seizure of new power, as an adjustment of the Council of State rather than its replacement, and as dependent throughout on the imperial institution for its legitimacy. The emperor "receded to a position above politics" but was never displaced from constitutional centrality; not even the most powerful regent could "govern autocratically in complete disregard of the emperor and the Council of State" (CHJ Vol. 2, p. 75).
