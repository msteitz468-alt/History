# The Politics of Land

The **politics of land** is the analytical framework, developed by [[wickham-medieval-europe-2016|Chris Wickham]]
out of Marc Bloch, for understanding medieval political power as a function of **how
rulers and elites controlled agricultural land and the peasant surplus it
produced**, rather than of constitutional forms, ideologies, or "feudal" institutions
taken on their own terms. It is the interpretive spine of Wickham's materialist,
comparative history of the Middle Ages. The full comparative development for 400–800
is in [[wickham-framing-the-early-middle-ages-2005|Framing the Early Middle Ages]] (2005);
*Medieval Europe* (2016) generalises the frame across the whole Middle Ages.

## Definition

The framework rests on one structural fact and one resulting choice:

- **All pre-industrial wealth is agricultural.** Over four-fifths of the population
  were peasants; there were no factories; towns held under a fifth of the population
  even after 1200. The decisive economic relationship is therefore the **extraction
  of surplus** (rent, or — where states taxed — tax) from peasant cultivators by
  landowners and rulers, "backed up by potential force." Markets, towns, artisan
  production, and everything historians call economic "development" were paid for
  out of that surplus.

- **States rest on either taxation or landowning.** A polity can fund itself by
  **taxing** the land (paying salaried soldiers and officials) or by **owning** land
  and granting it out in return for military and political service. This choice
  determines the state's solidity. In the ideal-type land-based (rent-based) state,
  the bulk of a ruler's wealth comes only from rent-paying inhabitants on land he
  directly controls; administration is simpler (tax rudimentary or absent); officials
  and the whole army are based on the land; and all political reward is dominated by
  the politics of land—cessions of land and rents for loyalty. Structural risks are
  finite land (gifts reduce future capacity to reward), difficulty reclaiming land
  except by force, and regional fragmentation unless political culture strongly
  favours the centre.

## The Tax / Land Distinction (the load-bearing claim)

Wickham's central comparative claim is that **tax-raising states are always
structurally more solid than land-based ones.** Salaried soldiers and officials "can
simply cease to be paid"; the disloyal are cheaply removed. A ruler whose resources
are all in land must "tread more carefully," because aristocrats' landed resources
are hard to take back. Hence:

- The **Roman Empire** ran a large, permanent, *paid* army on heavy land taxes — a
  very coherent structure. The **demise of the Roman fiscal system in the west** is
  therefore the primary reason the early medieval successor states were weaker,
  more fragmented, and economically simpler. See [[post-roman-transformation-of-the-west]].
- **Byzantium**, the **Arab caliphates**, and (briefly) **Umayyad al-Andalus** never
  abandoned the land tax, and so sustained professional armies, literate
  bureaucracies, and strong central power throughout — the comparative *control case*
  against which the Latin west's weakness stands out. The loss of this model in 1204
  is Wickham's "[[fourth-crusade-1204|failure of alternatives]]."
- **Carolingian Francia** is the instructive exception that proves the rule: it ruled
  a very large territory *without* taxation, through a vigorous **public** politics
  of royal office and assembly inherited from Rome — but this was always more fragile
  than a fiscal state, and it was this public order that fragmented in the eleventh
  century.

## The Fragmentation of Powers

Following **Marc Bloch**, Wickham holds that a land-based society *tends toward*
decentralisation: in a zero-sum game, "the more you granted away land, the less you
had," and impoverished rulers command less future loyalty. Fragmentation was **not
inevitable** — strong, watchful rulers (the Carolingians; Norman England; Castile)
held large land-based polities together — but the possibility was always latent, and
it was realised in the **[[feudal-revolution-debate|"feudal revolution"]]** of
c. 950–1100, when public power dissolved into a **cellular network** of bounded local
lordships and castles.

## The Reinvention of Public Power

The framework also tracks the *return* of taxation to the late-medieval west. As
states rebuilt fiscal systems (Ch. 11–12 of *Medieval Europe*), they had to obtain
the **consent** of those who paid — producing parliaments, taxpayer communities, and
a public sphere stronger than any since the Carolingian assemblies. Thus the same
analytic that explains early-medieval fragmentation explains the late-medieval
growth of representative politics: both follow from where the resources of the state
came from. See [[late-middle-ages]].

## Emic vs. Etic

This is an **etic** framework — an analyst's model, explicitly Marxian in lineage
(Wickham quotes Marx's *Eighteenth Brumaire* on people making "their own history, but
not... under circumstances they themselves have chosen"). Medieval actors experienced
their world through the **emic** categories of *lordship, honour, fidelity, oath, and
office* — and Wickham insists these mattered causally (Henry II would not break his
oath to Louis VII at Toulouse in 1159 despite military superiority). The framework
does not replace those categories; it explains the material structure within which
they operated. Comparative history also requires a standard vocabulary: aristocracy,
peasant, public power/state, and exchange must be defined for cross-regional use
because ordinary-language terms diverge across national traditions. “Aristocrat”
itself is etic for late Antiquity and the early Middle Ages—no single contemporary
legal label; status was fluid and regionally variable.

## Wickham, *Framing the Early Middle Ages* (2005)

### Landed armies and localization

Once armies were landed in all Romano-Germanic kingdoms, land-based military power
created land-based and potentially localized politics. Across Romano-Germanic cases,
soldiers sought land as status: Germanic armies for the most part constituted elites;
Roman armies did not (except leaders). Wickham contrasts this with caliph ʿUmar I’s
hard decision (640/2) that Arab armies remain in barrack towns on pay (*ʿaṭāʾ*) without
acquiring land. Shared problem of the West: potential localization of the politics of
land. Shared remedies: kings far richer than anyone else; court aggregation via
movable wealth/treasure; inherited ideology of public power (until late Carolingian
foundering). Three anti-decentralization procedures: public ceremonial; concentration
of public wealth; capillary justice/law. Visigoths stressed the first, Merovingians
the second, Lombards the third; Carolingians all three ([[tax-based-vs-land-based-states]]).

### Francia: scale, scatter, and patronage

- Olympiodoros’s figures (c.425) for Rome-based senatorial rents (5,000+ pounds of
  gold annually for many houses) imply hyper-rich wealth on the scale of provincial
  taxation—magnitudes Constantinopolitan and western provincial elites never matched.
  Only Mediterranean-spanning hyper-rich would be seriously damaged by the empire’s end;
  most elites owned inside single regions.
- Merovingian wills (Aredius 572 to Abbo 739) list the largest secular non-royal
  properties anywhere in Wickham’s post-Roman world—sustained by royal scale, office
  perks, gifts and confiscations ([[konigsnahe]]).
- Dual aristocratic levels fourth–eighth centuries: sub-regional owners (Aquitaine,
  Neustria scale) and city-territory owners, linked by patronage/faction. Sub-regional
  **scatter** of major estates made kingdom fragmentation into local autonomies
  difficult and enforced co-operation at *Teilreich* courts—opposite to late
  tenth-century county/castle localization. Here the politics of land **aided**
  centralization at sub-regional level.
- Aristocrat–entourage politics of land: *satellites*, *leudes*, later *vassi*,
  maintained by gifts or *precariae*—enormously expensive, yet without Bloch-style
  “fragmentation of powers” before 800.
- Local power was typically **patronage/mediation**, not territorial seigneurie:
  scattered intercut estates made fluid court-focused power more important than local
  coherence—facilitating survival of autonomous peasant landowning in interstices
  (notably the Rhineland) ([[peasant-mode-of-production]]).
- Political crisis could undermine control: Murbach’s plea to Charlemagne (774–800)
  that many *mancipia* escaped service in a 740s *turbatio* and claimed to be free for
  a generation near the Carolingian heartlands—practical control varied village to
  village.

### Comparative aristocracies and extraction

- **Lombard Italy**: even major magnates (Gaidoald of Pistoia; Taido of Bergamo)
  owned across two–three contiguous city territories only; by the eighth century
  sub-regional range dimly visible under the Ostrogoths had gone. Only pope, king,
  and southern dukes could match Bertram or Abbo.
- **Localization** (except Francia): real continuities between Roman world and early
  Middle Ages are at city-and-territory level. Extra-local economic relationships
  beyond that level had to be carried by the state—eastern empires could (most
  successfully Egypt); Romano-Germanic kingdoms could not. Only northern Francia
  retained extra-local exchange throughout, in the context of an extra-local
  aristocracy ([[aristocratic-demand]]).
- **End of western tax** (c.450–650 Gaul/Spain; faster mid–late sixth-century Italy;
  early fifth-century Britain) removed the state as mediating force; by the seventh
  century peasants faced landowners more directly.
- **Legal unfreedom ≠ slave mode**: many *servi/mancipia* were unfree tenants whose
  economic relation matched free tenants; Wickham reserves “slave” for the slave mode
  (plus domestic servile labour). Plantations need markets *and* slave supply together—
  rarely both after 400 (Bloch’s slaves → unfree tenants in western war zones; free
  wage labour in the prosperous East).
- **Rentenlandschaften** (Kuchenbuch): ninth-century Francia shows regionally distinct
  rent/service patterns largely independent of which great lord owned—Carolingian
  manorialization was not fully homogenizing (absent in southern Francia).
- **Fragmentation of large landowning** is the key peasant-autonomy parameter more even
  than simple extent of aristocratic land. Using Gramsci’s hegemony: Lucca and
  Aphroditō—aristocrats not strongest but ideologically hegemonic; Paris and Rhineland—
  dominate by force; Galatia and Jēme—neither fully dominant nor hegemonic (structural
  setting for revolt).
- **Estate intensification** (Ch. 5): landlords intensified direct control where
  exchange and tax made it profitable; where exchange contracted, simpler tenant-rent
  extraction predominated ([[bipartite-manor]]).

### Northern and English tenure

Early Anglo-Saxon large hide-units fit Maitland’s “superiority” (tribute from free
followers), not exclusive Roman estates; Faith’s inland/outland–warland dualism marks
slow growth of exclusive tenure. Major resource for strong northern kings, as for
aristocrats, was directly controlled land; tribute alone was never enough for stable
state-building.

## Limitations and Critiques

- The tax-vs-land dichotomy can flatten **hybrid** fiscal arrangements (England's
  *geld*, Italian urban tolls, the German *advocacy* system) into a binary.
- Critics of the **[[feudal-revolution-debate|feudal-revolution thesis]]** (the
  documentary-mutation and anti-feudalism schools) argue the "fragmentation" Wickham
  builds on is partly an artefact of changing records; the wiki keeps both readings.
- The strong materialist priority — that "almost everything... was paid for by the
  surplus which [peasants] handed over" — is a deliberate analytical wager, more
  contested by cultural and religious historians than by economic ones.
- As a generalisation across 1,000 years and the whole continent it is, by Wickham's
  own admission, a frame for asking questions rather than a complete account of any
  one region. *Framing* (2005) already shows that land-based politics need not
  equal immediate localization (Merovingian scatter *aided* court centrality).
