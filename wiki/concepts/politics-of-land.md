# The Politics of Land

The **politics of land** is the analytical framework, developed by [[wickham-medieval-europe-2016|Chris Wickham]]
out of Marc Bloch, for understanding medieval political power as a function of **how
rulers and elites controlled agricultural land and the peasant surplus it
produced**, rather than of constitutional forms, ideologies, or "feudal" institutions
taken on their own terms. It is the interpretive spine of Wickham's materialist,
comparative history of the Middle Ages.

## Definition

The framework rests on one structural fact and one resulting choice:

- **All pre-industrial wealth is agricultural.** Over four-fifths of the population
  were peasants; there were no factories; towns held under a fifth of the population
  even after 1200. The decisive economic relationship is therefore the **extraction
  of surplus** (rent, or — where states taxed — tax) from peasant cultivators by
  landowners and rulers, "backed up by potential force." Markets, towns, artisan

**Ch. 5 refinement (Wickham 2005).** Landlords intensified direct control (accounting, surveillance, cash-cropping, wage labour on demesnes) where exchange opportunities and tax demands made it profitable. Where exchange contracted after the Roman period, simpler tenant-rent extraction predominated and direct management became marginal except in high-demand niches (e.g. vineyards). Intensification was a response to sale possibilities for surplus, not an inherent stage of “feudal” evolution. (See also [[bipartite-manor]].)

**Wickham, *Framing the Early Middle Ages* (2005) Ch. 4 refinement.** Aristocratic landed wealth was the concrete mechanism of the politics of land. In post-Roman Gaul royal gifts, confiscations, and family *proprium* vs. *fiscus* distinctions produced, already by 600, a court world resembling the central Middle Ages more than the fifth century. Land remained transactional at the margins; commitment to local domination was often incomplete because estates were scattered and intercut. (See also [[post-roman-transformation-of-the-west]].)
  production, and everything historians call economic "development" were paid for
  out of that surplus.

- **States rest on either taxation or landowning.** A polity can fund itself by
  **taxing** the land (paying salaried soldiers and officials) or by **owning** land
  and granting it out in return for military and political service. This choice
  determines the state's solidity.

## The Tax / Land Distinction (the load-bearing claim)

Wickham's central comparative claim is that **tax-raising states are always
structurally more solid than land-based ones.** Salaried soldiers and officials "can
simply cease to be paid"; the disloyal are cheaply removed. A ruler whose resources
are all in land must "tread more carefully," because aristocrats' landed resources
are hard to take back. Hence:

- The **Roman Empire** ran a large, permanent, *paid* army on heavy land taxes — a
  very coherent structure. The **demise of the Roman fiscal system in the west** is
  therefore the primary reason the early medieval successor states were weaker,
  more fragmented, and economically simpler. See [[post-roman-transformation-of-the-west]].
- **Byzantium**, the **Arab caliphates**, and (briefly) **Umayyad al-Andalus** never
  abandoned the land tax, and so sustained professional armies, literate
  bureaucracies, and strong central power throughout — the comparative *control case*
  against which the Latin west's weakness stands out. The loss of this model in 1204
  is Wickham's "[[fourth-crusade-1204|failure of alternatives]]."
- **Carolingian Francia** is the instructive exception that proves the rule: it ruled
  a very large territory *without* taxation, through a vigorous **public** politics
  of royal office and assembly inherited from Rome — but this was always more fragile
  than a fiscal state, and it was this public order that fragmented in the eleventh
  century.

## The Fragmentation of Powers

Following **Marc Bloch**, Wickham holds that a land-based society *tends toward*
decentralisation: in a zero-sum game, "the more you granted away land, the less you
had," and impoverished rulers command less future loyalty. Fragmentation was **not
inevitable** — strong, watchful rulers (the Carolingians; Norman England; Castile)
held large land-based polities together — but the possibility was always latent, and
it was realised in the **[[feudal-revolution-debate|"feudal revolution"]]** of
c. 950–1100, when public power dissolved into a **cellular network** of bounded local
lordships and castles.

## The Reinvention of Public Power

The framework also tracks the *return* of taxation to the late-medieval west. As
states rebuilt fiscal systems (Ch. 11–12 of *Medieval Europe*), they had to obtain
the **consent** of those who paid — producing parliaments, taxpayer communities, and
a public sphere stronger than any since the Carolingian assemblies. Thus the same
analytic that explains early-medieval fragmentation explains the late-medieval
growth of representative politics: both follow from where the resources of the state
came from. See [[late-middle-ages]].

## Emic vs. Etic

This is an **etic** framework — an analyst's model, explicitly Marxian in lineage
(Wickham quotes Marx's *Eighteenth Brumaire* on people making "their own history, but
not... under circumstances they themselves have chosen"). Medieval actors experienced
their world through the **emic** categories of *lordship, honour, fidelity, oath, and
office* — and Wickham insists these mattered causally (Henry II would not break his
oath to Louis VII at Toulouse in 1159 despite military superiority). The framework
does not replace those categories; it explains the material structure within which
they operated.

## Limitations and Critiques

- The tax-vs-land dichotomy can flatten **hybrid** fiscal arrangements (England's
  *geld*, Italian urban tolls, the German *advocacy* system) into a binary.
- Critics of the **[[feudal-revolution-debate|feudal-revolution thesis]]** (the
  documentary-mutation and anti-feudalism schools) argue the "fragmentation" Wickham
  builds on is partly an artefact of changing records; the wiki keeps both readings.
- The strong materialist priority — that "almost everything... was paid for by the
  surplus which [peasants] handed over" — is a deliberate analytical wager, more
  contested by cultural and religious historians than by economic ones.
- As a generalisation across 1,000 years and the whole continent it is, by Wickham's
  own admission, a frame for asking questions rather than a complete account of any
  one region.
