# The Material Culture of Archaic Greece

The **material culture of Archaic Greece** — the physical texture of life between the
eighth and sixth centuries — is known chiefly from archaeology and, increasingly, from
the **figured arts**, whose detailed depiction of life makes pottery and sculpture a
prime historical source. [[boardman-cah-v3p3-1982|CAH Vol. III, Part 3]] (Ch. 45b, John
Boardman) surveys it.

## Towns, walls, and houses

Knowledge of the **mainland cities is poorer** than of the colonies or the East Greek
foundations: continuous occupation has left even sixth-century **Athens** an
ill-defined, "shapeless settlement, muddled by its past until lawgivers and a tyrant
take it in hand" (it is unclear whether it even had a city wall). For monumental walls
and planning one looks elsewhere — the great circuit of **Smyrna**, the Polycratean
walls of **Samos** with their towers and ditch. **Orthogonal town-planning** appears
early (seventh-century Smyrna after its destruction; eighth-century Megara Hyblaea, with
a reserved agora) but is easiest to read in the western colonies and at **Paestum**.
**Houses** stayed humble — small one-roomed structures with an open porch, the richest
(Smyrna) two-storied with brick on stone socles; the courtyard house appears late.
"There were no tyrant palaces in Greece."

## The orientalizing impetus

Boardman frames the transformation between the eighth and sixth centuries as the
**[[orientalizing-revolution|orientalizing]]** — the consequence of the metal-seeking
*emporia* ([[al-mina|Al Mina]] in the east, [[pithecusae|Ischia]] in the west) that
brought a flow not only of metals but of finished goods and **craftsmen**. The east
gave Greece **luxury techniques** (gold granulation and filigree, hardstone gem-cutting,
ivory-carving, faience), the new **bronze cauldron** with griffin/lion and siren
attachments (replacing the Geometric tripod), Phrygian-pattern bronze belts, the flowing
**chiton**, and the mould for clay relief figurines (the **"Daedalic" style**). But
Boardman insists the borrowings were "**hardly more than a catalyst**": there was no
"Assyrian" or "Babylonian" period of fashion, only gradual infiltration; the products
"differ so markedly from their putative models" that they are wholly Hellenic. Tellingly,
**Greek narrative art flourished where oriental influence was *slightest*** (Athenian and
island vases), the eastern animal-friezes being mere "banal surface-fillers."

## Monumental art: temples and sculpture

The catalysts produced the two great new monumental arts. Knowledge of **Egyptian** art
after c. 650 led the Greeks to work their white island **marble** at life size and more
— the **kouroi and korai** (grave-markers, dedications, and cult images) — and to give
the temple-houses (*oikoi*) monumental form with **architectural orders**: the **Doric**
in the homeland, the **Ionic** in the East Greek world. The **temple** was the only
major architecture of the age and "must have dominated the towns" — "a physical
expression of the patron deity but also a demonstration of the wealth and labour-command
of the ruling, generally aristocratic, class." (Boardman notes the engineering relied
more on **manpower than skill** — rule-of-thumb, no national measurement standards — with
exceptions like Eupalinus's Samos tunnel.)

## A world of images

By the sixth century "the Greek lived in a world of icons" — figure-decoration covered
clay vases (serving many uses), shield-bands, furniture, dress (Athena's peplos bore the
gigantomachy), even finger-rings; the great national sanctuaries held narrative treasure
(the Chest of Cypselus, the Sicyonian and Siphnian treasuries at Delphi). Rulers were
"great propagandists" who used myth and religion through poets and **artists**, the more
lasting messages conveyed **visually**. Because mythological actors were shown in
**contemporary dress**, this art is a rich source for daily life — the **symposium**
(reclining on *klinai*, adopted enthusiastically c. 600), exotic furniture, **perfumed
oils** (the male *aryballos*, strigil and sponge), and the **komast** dancers and satyrs
out of whom **Attic drama** grew.

## Trade goods, arms, and ships

The figured arts also document the economy: specialist potters' quarters (Corinth's
scented-oil *aryballoi*, the deliberate **export models** Athens made for Etruria — the
Nicosthenic amphora, Tyrrhenian amphorae), the bronze vessels that travelled to Celtic
and Illyrian tombs (the **Vix crater**), the colossal dedications (Cypselus's gold Zeus,
the Samian griffin-crater). They are also our best source for **[[hoplite-warfare|hoplite
arms]]** (helmets, corselets, the round shield) and for the **ships** — the oared
warship and bireme, the fuller-bodied merchantman menaced by pirates. **Burial
monuments** (the kouros grave-marker, the inscribed Athenian *stelai* curbed by Solon's
sumptuary laws) and **sanctuary dedications** (treasuries from c. 600, tripods, kouroi)
provided arenas for "personal ostentation in a public setting."

## Significance

- A reminder that the **revolution in the quality of Greek life was real but its
  technological basis modest** — "changes of degree rather than substance," driven by the
  orientalizing flow of goods, craftsmen and techniques onto a confident Greek base.
- The **figured arts as historical evidence** — for warfare, dress, the symposium, cult,
  and the birth of drama — and as the medium through which an aristocratic, then civic,
  culture stamped itself on what became Classical (and, via Rome, Western) civilization.
- The Athens of [[pisistratus|Pisistratus]] "beginning to take a form which was to be the
  pattern of all Classical cities."
