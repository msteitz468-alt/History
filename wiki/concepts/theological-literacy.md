# Theological Literacy (Late Antique and Early Medieval)

**Theological literacy** denotes the unequal distribution of formal capacity to understand, debate, and reproduce sophisticated Christian (and later Islamic) doctrine among members of the same religious community in the late antique and early medieval Middle East.

## Emic vs. etic

- **Emic:** Teachers and bishops repeatedly state that not everything a teacher grasps can be grasped by the public taught (Anastasius of Sinai); that the faith would be unjust if only sophisticates could hold it (Gregory of Nazianzus); that each understands according to capacity (Ephrem).
- **Etic:** Historians who treat confessional identity as if all members shared elite doctrinal competence project modern mass-education uniformity backward. [[tannous-making-medieval-middle-east-2018|Tannous]] treats uneven literacy as a **structural condition** of post-Chalcedonian church formation and early Muslim–Christian contact.

## Relation to other concepts

- Underpins the category [[simple-believers]].
- Explains why [[post-chalcedonian-confessional-formation|schools, sacraments, and competition]] mattered more for everyday belonging than conciliar formulae alone.
- Parallel problem for early Islam: many of the Prophet’s late mass converts likely had limited understanding of the full implications of membership while Islam was still being elaborated.

## Evidence from Tannous (2018)

- **Numerical dominance of the unlearned:** Origen (*Contra Celsum*) is used as epigraph for the claim that the “vulgar and illiterate” far outnumber the philosophically trained among Christians.
- **Pastoral design for unequal capacity:** Gregory of Nazianzus: the faith would be unjust if only sophisticates could hold it; Anastasius of Sinai: not all a teacher grasps can be grasped by the public; Ephrem: each understands according to capacity.
- **Access to books even among elites was limited:** George of the Arabs (d. 724) wrote a liturgy commentary for those lacking Fathers’ books; Jacob of Edessa sometimes could not answer without commentaries at hand; Timothy I later hunted texts across confessions.
- **Clerical literacy ideals rose slowly:** Justinian forbade ordaining the illiterate, yet Heraclius still exiled Cyrus of Edessa for poor Gospel reading (620s–30s); a late-eighth-century Miaphysite patriarch could still be found “simple.”
- **Schools as response:** confessional competition drove educational arms races ([[qenneshre|Qenneshre]] and peers) to staff sacraments and boundaries — see [[post-chalcedonian-confessional-formation]].
- **Parallel for early Islam:** late mass converts and selective Qurʾānic knowledge among early Muslims; layering of knowledge within the *umma* as within the churches.

## Limitations

- Literacy here is **theological/doctrinal**, not only alphabetic reading; some who could read Scripture still lacked speculative training.
- Evidence is mediated by the learned who diagnose the simple’s limits — with pastoral and polemical agendas.
