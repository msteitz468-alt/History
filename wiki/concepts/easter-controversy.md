# Easter Controversy

The Easter (or Paschal) controversy was a recurring early medieval dispute over the correct method for calculating the date of Easter, arising because different Christian communities used incompatible computistical cycles. The most consequential instance in British history pitted the Roman/Continental method against the divergent 84-year cycle used by the Irish and British ("Celtic") churches descended from Iona's monastic tradition, resulting in the two communities sometimes celebrating Easter on different dates entirely. The dispute was resolved for Anglo-Saxon Northumbria at the [[events/whitby-synod-664|Synod of Whitby]] (664), where King Oswiu ruled in favor of Roman practice — championed by Wilfrid, who argued for conformity with the wider universal Church centered on St. Peter — over the Ionan/Columban position represented by Bishop Colmán, who subsequently withdrew to Iona and then Ireland rather than conform.

## Emic and Etic

The controversy is an emic category: contemporaries themselves identified, argued over, and named the dispute as a matter of correct religious observance, not a modern historian's retrospective label.

## Significance

Whitby's resolution was a pivotal moment in aligning the English church with continental Roman practice rather than the insular Irish/Ionan tradition, with lasting consequences for the institutional and cultural orientation of Anglo-Saxon Christianity toward Rome.
