# Norse Paganism

The pre-Christian religious system of Viking-Age Scandinavia, displaced by the
[[christianization-of-scandinavia|conversion]]. Per
[[helle-cambridge-history-scandinavia-v1-2003|the Cambridge History]] (Roesdahl &
Meulengracht Sørensen), it was **polytheistic and diffuse**, with no dedicated
priestly class and no special sacred buildings: cult was led by farmers, chieftains,
and kings in domestic settings, with sacrificial feasts (*blót*) in autumn, at the
solstices, and in spring. Religion was "woven into life and culture," not
administered by priests.

## The Pantheon and Cosmology

Two divine families: the **Æsir** (Odin, Thor, Baldr, Heimdal) and the **Vanir**
(Njord, Freyr, Freyja). **Thor** protected farmers and fought the forces of chaos;
**Odin** was the complex god of warriors, poets, wisdom, magic, and death, patron of
the aristocracy. The cosmos (Miðgarðr) was precarious, perpetually threatened by the
giants and destined for destruction and rebirth at **Ragnarök** — a moral structure
fundamentally unlike Christianity's. The fertility god **Freyr** is represented by
the bronze figure from Rällinge (Sweden).

## Cult and Burial

Major cult centres included **Old Uppsala** (described by Adam of Bremen, though his
account of the temple and sacrifices is regarded as exaggerated). The
[[oseberg|Oseberg]] (c. 834) and Gokstad ship burials exemplify lavish pagan
mortuary practice. The pantheon and myths are known chiefly from later sources —
[[saga-literature|Eddic and skaldic poetry]] and [[snorri-sturluson|Snorri
Sturluson]]'s Prose Edda — written down in Christian Iceland centuries later, which
complicates reconstruction.

## Historiography

The dating and even the coherence of "Norse religion" are contested: most evidence
postdates conversion and is filtered through Christian authors. Nineteenth-century
national-romanticism produced a distorted "noble barbarian" image now superseded by
archaeological and interdisciplinary research. See [[runestones]],
[[norse-scandinavia]].
