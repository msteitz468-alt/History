# Holy war (analytical concept)

A narrower subset of religious warfare emphasizing apocalyptic or purgative extremities (Dupront): war as world-changing, celebratory or total in character, with the enemy fully demonised and the goal eschatological transformation. Distinguished from institutional crusade and from just war. Broader medieval usage also covers sanctified violence authorized by religious authorities (papal crusade; juristic jihad) without requiring Dupront’s total extremity.

## Relation to Religious Warfare

Housley uses the term for the most radical Taborite and Münsterite episodes (purgative, "wash his hands in the blood"; New Jerusalem kingdom). These come closest to "holy massacres." The broader category "religious warfare" allows for sanctification without such totality (economy of violence, just-war constraints).

## From Cobb, *The Race for Paradise* (2014) — jihad and crusade compared

[[cobb-race-for-paradise-2014|Paul M. Cobb]] supplies a medieval Mediterranean comparative layer focused on Islamic [[islamic-law-of-jihad|jihad]] and Latin crusade rather than late-medieval European radicalism.

### Independent development

Cobb asserts **no evidence of connection** between the concept of jihad in Islam and the later Latin concept of crusade. Family resemblance comes from shared universal monotheism, but the concepts developed **independently** with important differences.

### Institutional differences (Cobb’s load-bearing comparison)

| Dimension | Crusade (Latin) | Jihad (classical Islamic) |
|---|---|---|
| Authority | Papal authorization central | Caliph/imam should authorize; in practice caliphs often only retroactively sanction jihads preached by the ulema |
| Rewards | Martyrdom/salvation **plus** Church institutional protections for family/property | Martyrdom/salvation; lacked the same institutional property/family package |
| Centralization | Votive loyalty to Church/pope | Less centralized; could become “privatized” conquests founding rival regimes |
| Duration | Temporary (vow / death / absolution) | Perpetual communal obligation (with *kifaya*/*ʿayn* variations) |
| Goal (crude contrast) | “Rescuing sacred **land**” | “Rescuing **souls**” (conversion; *dhimmi* alternative for People of the Book) |

Caliphs were not clerics/priests and did not in practice preach jihads the way popes preached crusades; by c. 1050 Abbasid caliphs had largely shed real authority beyond symbolic delegation—yet remained potent emblems of Islamic unity.

### Holy war as political instrument (not continuous civilizational program)

Against Franks, jihad was a **political instrument** used intermittently by ambitious leaders, not a continuous civilizational campaign. Predecessors and successors of Zangi–Nur al-Din–Saladin tried just as hard; the sequential success of those three creates an optical illusion of a continuous “countercrusade” (see [[crusade-historiography]], [[processes/crusades]]). Under Nur al-Din, jihad was “but one plank in a much broader platform” of pious-just rule (madrasas, hospitals, justice, Circle of Equity)—challenging narrow counter-Crusade framing. The most far-reaching religious-history factor of the period, Cobb argues, was the “Sunni Revival” (reappraisal/reform in law, theology, devotion), whose wellsprings had little to do with the religious threat of the Crusades.

Neither Islam nor Latin Christianity is “inherently” violent in Cobb’s framing: classical jihad doctrine developed in a medieval imperial context of warrior-elites and conquest as a **juristic system to control violence**—conditions for starting/ending war, limits on lawlessness, targets, noncombatants, merit (detail under [[islamic-law-of-jihad]]).

## Critiques

- Overly narrow if used exclusively for Dupront-style extremities (excludes most crusades and defensive sanctification).
- Risk of polemical use to pathologise opponents.
- Equating jihad and crusade as interchangeable “holy war” erases Cobb’s independent-development and institutional-difference claims; comparative analysis requires keeping the legal and institutional systems distinct while noting family resemblances.
