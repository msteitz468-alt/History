## Definition

**Courtly culture** is the comparative analytical frame ([[kedar-cwh-v5-2015|CWH V]],
Ch 7, Geary et al.) for the royal and imperial courts of Eurasia in the Middle
Millennium — western Europe, [[byzantine-empire|Byzantium]], the
[[islam|Islamic world]], India, China, and Japan. Across all of them the court was
the place where a **specialized community of individuals** carried out the functions
of power and simultaneously **created the cultural forms and ceremonial rituals that
represented power** to itself and to outsiders. The chapter's central claim: despite
enormous variation in size, layout, and structure, the intense competition for
royal favor everywhere generated **codes of conduct** distinguishing courtiers (men
and women) from outsiders, and the courts borrowed and exchanged practices,
prestige goods, and values until they formed, in effect, **a single Eurasian system
of exercising and representing power**.

## The Spectrum of Courts

The chapter ranges the courts along axes of scale, fixity, and formality:

- **China — the largest and most organized.** Tang Chang'an held nearly a million
  people in an 8.6 × 9.7 km grid; the court was walled off from the city and
  structured into four constituencies — the civil, military, and imperial
  bureaucracies and the women's quarters — radiating administrative reach to every
  county (Grand Councilors, Three Cabinets, Six Ministries, the Censorate, the
  [[song-dynasty|Song]] Hanlin Academy, ~500,000 troops near the capital, and a
  graded hierarchy of empresses, consorts, and eunuch attendants).
- **Byzantium — court blended into the city.** The Great Palace of
  [[constantinople|Constantinople]] adjoined Hagia Sophia and the hippodrome; most
  courtiers lived in the city and reported for duty. The emperor was God's
  viceregent, but the center of religious authority lay with the Patriarch next
  door, not in the palace.
- **Japan — the Chinese model adapted.** Grid-plan capitals laid out by geomantic
  principles, with Buddhist temples and Shinto shrines sited nearby.
- **India — less formally structured.** The king's palace interior (women's quarters
  *antahpuram*, princes' houses, pleasure gardens) for the ruler and his intimates.
- **Western Europe — the least fixed of all.** Itinerant courts of great estates or
  rural palaces (residence, throne hall, chapel), only gradually — and influenced by
  fortification architecture — coalescing into fixed centers like Paris or
  Westminster. Even by 1500 Western monarchies "had hardly begun to create fixed
  royal courts comparable to those of Asia."

## The Core Argument

Not every court was a center of real political power (some were largely ceremonial),
and the makeup and roles of courtiers "differed enormously across Eurasia."
Nonetheless, courts everywhere performed the same dual work — **wielding power and
representing it** — and a shared courtly logic emerged through exchange: novel and
precious objects of display and consumption circulated between courts, which
"directly or indirectly borrowed practices and values from other courtly cultures."
The result was a trans-regional **grammar of rank, ritual, favor, and
self-presentation** — a Eurasian commonality that justifies treating the court as a
single comparative object. This is one of the volume's two "Eurasian commonalities"
(the other being [[cultural-crystallization]]).
