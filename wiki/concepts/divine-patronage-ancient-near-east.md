# Divine Patronage in the Ancient Near East

**Divine patronage** is the analytical model — argued at length in
[[sources/noll-canaan-israel-antiquity-2013|K. L. Noll's *Canaan and Israel in Antiquity*
(2013)]] — that the **common religion** of the ancient Near East was the **patron–client
political structure projected onto the divine realm**. It is an *etic* framework historians
use to explain why ANE religions, despite local variety, share a single deep grammar; but it
was also *emic* in the sense that ancient kings explicitly described their gods in patron–
client terms.

## The patron–client structure (the human tier)

Every ANE government was royal. Beneath whatever label historians give it — chiefdom,
city-state, territorial state, empire — lay a **patron–client relationship** made necessary
by a vulnerable agrarian society. A man (almost never a woman) gathered "mighty warriors,"
presented himself to a community as a **savior**, and promised to defend his clients from
raiders and famine and to enrich them by despoiling outsiders. In exchange the clients
rendered taxes in kind and loyalty. The system fixed every person's station: king → warrior
aristocracy → free commoners → slaves, with everyone addressing any superior as "lord"/"lady"
and presenting themselves as "slave."

## The three-tier divine hierarchy

The same structure became religion. The relationship was **three-tier**:

1. **Patron god** (top tier) — chose the human patron.
2. **Human patron / king** (middle tier) — ruled by the god's mandate.
3. **Human clients** (bottom tier).

The patron god was not the only god but held unique status: most powerful *and* most just,
ruling a pantheon that mirrored human society (great cosmic gods, then gods of crafts and
childbirth, then messenger gods — *angeloi*, "angels"). Marduk in the *Enuma Elish* is the
type case; Noll notes the biblical god functions identically in Psalm 82 (bringing justice to
the council of gods) and Ps. 74.12–17 (creating the world). This religion was **state-
sponsored, state-enforced, and obligatory** — participation was required even of those who
preferred another god.

## Henotheism, monolatry, monotheism

Noll uses divine patronage to redefine the usual religion labels:

- **Henotheism** — one god possesses far greater power than all others combined; every ANE
  patron god was the king of a henotheism. Humans could worship many gods so long as the
  patron stayed supreme — "a religious umbrella under which every god could find a place."
- **Monolatry** ("worship of only one") — a rare *intolerant* henotheism in which lesser
  gods are forbidden worship though not denied existence. Much of the Bible is monolatrous:
  Yahweh as a "jealous god" (Deut. 5.7–10) bans the worship, not the reality, of other gods.
- **Monotheism** (only one god, no other supernatural agents) — on Noll's reading the Bible
  is *not* monotheistic unless one stretches the term to include the angels, demons, and
  minor gods that fill it. In a henotheistic culture, the atheist of Psalms 14 and 53 says
  "there are *no gods*" (plural); "a monotheist is nothing more than a failed atheist."

## Significance and limits

The model explains the temple economy (the patron god's house collected the taxes that fed
the cult and the crown), the politics of legitimacy (a usurper needed the god's mandate), and
the trajectory of [[yahwism-and-israelite-religion|Israelite religion]] from henotheism
toward exclusive monolatry. Its limit is its breadth: by design it flattens local theology
into a single template, and it relies heavily on reading the Bible as a representative ANE
text rather than a special case — a move the biblical-maximalist tradition resists.
