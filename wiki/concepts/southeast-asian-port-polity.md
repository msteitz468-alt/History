# The Southeast Asian Trading City (Port-Polity)

The characteristic political and economic form of the
[[southeast-asian-age-of-commerce|age of commerce]] was the **cosmopolitan port-city** — the entrepôt
where the city and the state were effectively **coterminous**. Anthony Reid
([[reid-southeast-asia-age-of-commerce-v2-1993|Vol. 2]], Ch. II) argues that the commercial boom drove a
period of **sustained urban growth** in which power shifted decisively from older agrarian capitals
(those resting on tribute in labour and rice) to **trade-based coastal cities**. The supreme symbol of
the shift: in **1432** the trading kingdom of **Ayutthaya** sacked and ended [[angkor|Angkor]], the
greatest of the hydraulic agrarian capitals, and when a Cambodian capital was re-founded it sat not on
irrigation works but at the **Phnom Penh** river-junction entrepôt. In Burma the Mon port of **Pegu**,
and in Java the Muslim north-coast cities (**Demak, Japara, Tuban, Gresik, Surabaya**) that overthrew
[[majapahit|Majapahit]], tell the same story.

## The monsoon-entrepôt logic

The entrepôt existed because of the **monsoon** (Malay *musim*, "seasonality"). Winds blew dependably
north April–August and south December–March, so shipowners made one long run on a following wind and
returned within the year on the reverse monsoon, minimising time at risk. Because Chinese, Japanese,
Indian, Gujarati, and Arab ships could not all be present at once and could not complete the
Cairo-to-Canton route in a single vessel, **entrepôts arose at the intersection of the monsoonal trading
sectors** — the Straits of Malacca above all — where traders could await the change of winds or the
arrival of partners, populating whole quarters of the city for months. A **succession** of great
entrepôts held this role: [[funan|Funan]]'s Oc Eo, then **Champa** and **Srivijaya**, then fifteenth-
century **Pasai** and **[[melaka|Melaka]]**; after the Portuguese took Melaka (1511) trade scattered to
**Aceh, Banten, Patani, Johor, Ayutthaya, Makassar, Brunei, and Hoi An**; and by c. 1650 Dutch
**Batavia** assumed Melaka's old dominance.

## A de-urbanised legacy: cities bigger in 1600 than in 1850

Reid's striking demographic claim overturns the assumption of a "traditionally rural" Southeast Asia:
the age-of-commerce cities were **genuinely large** — Asian cities were in general bigger than European
ones before the seventeenth century — and the region was *more* urbanised in 1600 than at any time
before the twentieth century. **Colonialism de-urbanised it**: European colonial cities were "compact
islands" that usurped the commerce of the Asian centres without letting Asians migrate to share the
wealth, so the towns shrank. The areas that escaped colonial rule longest prove the point — **Bangkok**
held ~10% of Thailand's population, the Burmese capital ~10–13% — while in heavily-colonised Java the
urban share *fell* through the nineteenth century. Aceh, Banten, Makassar, Mataram's capitals, and
Ayutthaya were cities on a European scale.

## Urbanism and capitalism: the van Leur–Meilink-Roelofsz debate

The chapter culminates in the question of whether these cities generated **capitalism**. Reid finds
genuine commercialisation — by 1400–1630 (peaking 1570–1630) a large share of the population produced
for the world market and relied on imported cloth, ceramics, and coin; bottomry, profit-sharing, and
interest-lending were well established; a cosmopolitan **merchant class** (the *orang kaya*, the female
traders, the resident foreign communities) flourished. But against **J. C. van Leur** (who saw Asian
trade as mere low-status "peddling"), Reid endorses **M. A. P. Meilink-Roelofsz**: the leading European
cities had developed **impersonal institutions to pool and safeguard capital** — stock exchanges, banks,
chartered companies — that were "totally absent" in Southeast Asia, where banking remained personal
(goldsmiths, money-lenders). Behind this lies the decisive difference: **security of person and property
"against the whim of an arbitrary sovereign."** European visitors astonished Southeast Asians by their
property rights ("they were all kings," thought the Banten embassy of 1682); in Southeast Asia, where
**the city *was* the state** (no autonomous commune as European feudalism or Confucian Japan permitted),
law and ideology placed few constraints on royal power. The **market repeatedly strained against the
palace** — an emergent individualist/mercantile ethos is visible in Melaka's law codes and in Hamzah
Fansuri's merchant-traveller imagery — and the urban **mob** could topple favourites, but commerce could
not **institutionalise** its power against [[southeast-asian-absolutism|absolutist kingship]].

## Significance and critiques

The port-polity is the institutional core of the age of commerce and the key to its **fragility**:
because wealth and power fused in a single ruler-and-city, the collapse of an entrepôt (Melaka 1511,
Makassar 1669, Banten 1684) could extinguish a whole commercial world at a stroke — a vulnerability the
[[dutch-east-india-company|VOC]] exploited. The **van Leur/Meilink-Roelofsz** debate over whether Asian
commerce was "modern" or mere peddling remains live, and Reid's "absent institutions / insecure
property" explanation for why Southeast Asia did not generate autonomous capitalism has been criticised
as too close to a Weberian "what Asia lacked" framing; **Victor Lieberman** and others stress the real
administrative and fiscal sophistication of the mainland states. As a portrait of the cosmopolitan
entrepôt as the signature form of the era, however, it is foundational.
