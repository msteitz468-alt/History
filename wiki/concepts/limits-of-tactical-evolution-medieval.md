# Limits of Tactical Evolution in Medieval Warfare

Etic concept describing the absence of linear, cumulative, or systematic development in Western European battlefield tactics and army organization 1000–1300. Change occurred, but it was uneven, contingent, personality-dependent, and lacked institutional mechanisms for refinement or transmission.

## Core Thesis

> "The thirteenth century was truly the age of cavalry. This does not mean that they enjoyed an uncontested supremacy – far from it – but that it was clearly the actions of the cavalry, and the way in which they were handled, that dictated events. Paradoxically, as we have seen, infantry were now better armed than ever before. ... But it was only in quite exceptional circumstances that cavalry were totally dominant... All medieval armies were the products of particular conditions, and their tactics were a result of the contingencies of the situation as understood by the commander. There was no doubt a predilection to rely on cavalry... But in the day-to-day business of war, foot nearly always played a major role. ... Sometimes, a particular commander reflected very carefully on his task and came up with an army and tactics which were evidently appropriate – as Edward I did for his wars. But there was no means to refine and give continuity to such ideas – Edward II’s disasters testify to that. War was part of the fabric of medieval society, but it was not institutionalized... In the thirteenth century, it was taking on a new professionalism. This showed itself first and most clearly amongst the cavalry... Victory in battle depended, as victory always does, on the personality of the commander and his ability to adapt forces and employ them in particular circumstances – but it also depended on his having a large core of loyal troops around which his army could be built and manoeuvred. Unlike a modern general, the medieval commander had to do that without a staff, without a logistics service and, above all, without an institutional memory to serve him." (Ch. 13)

## Key Limits

**No linear pattern**: Older historiography posited clean evolution (couched-lance shock supremacy → longbow infantry revival). In reality "there was never anything like this neat pattern of evolution and any examination shows a much more diverse experience of battle." Infantry played vital roles from the tenth century onward (Montpensier 892, Hastings, Tinchebrai 1106, Brémule 1119, etc.). Mass cavalry charges remained rare and risky throughout; repeated small-unit charges (per Templar Rule) were more characteristic where control existed. (Ch. 12–13)

**Organizational and social constraints**: Armies were transient retinue collections drawn from an "individualistic ethos." Tight formation and repeated execution were difficult without monastic-style discipline (Templars). "Control of troops was the key to victory... such control must have been very difficult to achieve..." Banners and personal leadership helped but were fragile. (Ch. 12)

**Contingency and personality**: Each victory "was sui generis." Cohesion and good leadership (Guérin at Bouvines, Simon de Montfort at Muret, Charles of Anjou at Benevento/Tagliacozzo) could produce success with small or heterogeneous forces; their absence produced disaster (Aragonese at Muret, Otto IV at Bouvines, Edward II at Bannockburn). Lessons did not accumulate: Edward I's balanced infantry/cavalry methods in Wales/Scotland "were not passed on to the next generation."

**Absence of institutional memory**: "There was no mechanism to bring ideas together in a systematic way." War remained the province of the proprietorial class rather than a state institution with permanent staffs, training schools, or archival doctrine. "the only repository of military memory, beyond that of individuals, lay in the households of major rulers, but these were very few and far between." (Ch. 3/13)

**Uneven 13th-c. professionalism**: Greater discipline visible at Muret and Bouvines (cavalry in contingents under leaders; French repeated charges), yet armies "remained the product of contingencies." Infantry revival (schiltrons, Courtrai foot) appeared in specific regional/political contexts (urban communes, Scottish national resistance) without general adoption or refinement across the West.

## Relation to Proprietorial Framework

These limits were structural consequences of proprietorial warfare: ad hoc armies, individual equipping, land-based wealth that discouraged standing forces, and elite social exclusivity. Tactical ideas remained personal or retinue-specific rather than cumulative doctrine.

## Examples of (Non-)Development

- Bouvines 1214: French success from disciplined repeated charges under Guérin + Philip's overall control; allied failure from lack of central command and poor infantry handling (Ch. 13).
- Edward I vs. Edward II: competent use of archers + cavalry at Maes Moydog and Falkirk (schiltrons broken by archery + assault); no transmission, leading to Bannockburn disaster (Ch. 13).
- Courtrai 1302: Flemish urban foot defeated French knights through terrain, trenches, and dense formation — not part of a general "infantry revolution" but a contingent victory (Ch. 13).

## Historiography Note

Corrects the "neat pattern" narrative of Oman/Delbrück (knight shock → longbow). Emphasizes source-critical diversity and social causation over technological determinism. Complements Verbruggen's demonstration of conroi organization and infantry capability.
