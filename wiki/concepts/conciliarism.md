# Conciliarism (the Conciliar Movement)

**Conciliarism** is the doctrine that a **general council of the Church** holds authority superior
to the pope and may judge, correct, and even depose him — the ecclesiology that the
**[[great-schism-1378|Great Schism]]** thrust to the centre of Latin Christendom.
[[jones-ncmh-v6-2000|NCMH VI]] (Howard Kaminsky, Ch. 16) presents it as the great constitutional
response to the crisis of the divided papacy.

## Origins

Conciliar ideas had **canon-law roots** long before the schism: glossators had asked whether a
heretical or incorrigible pope could be judged, and the corporate maxims of canon law (the *sanior
et maior pars*, *[[late-medieval-political-thought|quod omnes tangit]]*) implied that the Church, as
a community, retained authority over its head. But it was the **Great Schism (1378–1417)** — two,
then three, rival popes, none able to compel the others' resignation — that turned theory into a
practical programme. If the *via cessionis* (mutual resignation) and the *via facti* (force) both
failed, the *via concilii* — a general council representing the whole Church — seemed the only way
to restore unity.

## Theory and Practice

Theologians and canonists of **Paris** (Jean Gerson, Pierre d'Ailly) and others argued that the
council embodied the Church and could act even against the popes' will. The movement reached its
height in two councils:

- **Pisa (1409)** — summoned by cardinals of both obediences, it deposed both rival popes and
  elected a third; but since the deposed popes refused to step down, it produced **three** popes
  instead of two.
- **Constance (1414–1418)** — backed by the Emperor **Sigismund**, it finally ended the schism,
  securing the removal of all three lines and electing **Martin V (1417)**. Its decree ***Haec
  sancta* (1415)** declared that the council held its power "immediately from Christ" and that all,
  "even the pope," must obey it in matters of faith, schism, and reform; ***Frequens*** required
  regular future councils.

## The Fifteenth-Century Defeat (NCMH VII)

[[allmand-ncmh-v7-1998|NCMH VII]] (Antony Black, Ch. 3) traces the movement's **defeat** in the
fifteenth century. After Constance healed the schism, the **Council of Basel (1431–1449)** pushed
the conciliar programme to its radical extreme — claiming standing authority over the pope and
even electing an antipope (Felix V) — but it **overreached and disintegrated**, discredited by its
own intransigence while the restored popes (Eugenius IV, who countered with the reunion Council of
**Florence, 1439**) reasserted monarchical authority. The papacy resisted *Frequens*, and by **Pius
II's bull *Execrabilis* (1460)** condemned appeals to a council as heretical. The conciliar idea
survived as a latent constitutional tradition (and a French "Gallican" weapon) but ceased to be a
practical check — leaving the path open to the **Renaissance papacy** (see [[papacy]]).

## Significance and Limits

Conciliarism is the late-medieval Church's experiment in **constitutional, representative
government** — the ecclesiastical parallel to the secular rise of [[english-parliament|parliaments]]
and estates, and an application of the same corporate and consent-based ideas. It resolved the
schism. But it did not endure: the restored papacy resisted *Frequens*, the Council of Basel
(1431–1449) overreached and failed, and by **1460 (Pius II's bull *Execrabilis*)** the popes
condemned appeals to a council as heretical, reasserting **monarchical** authority. Conciliarism
nonetheless left a lasting alternative tradition of Church government — and its critique of papal
absolutism fed into later reform and, ultimately, the Reformation.
