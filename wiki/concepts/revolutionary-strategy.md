# Revolutionary Strategy

Revolutionary strategy is what Lawrence Freedman calls "[[strategy]] from below" — the
strategic problem faced by "the underdogs, or at least those claiming to act on their
behalf, who faced a large gap between desired ends and available means." For these
actors "strategy was most challenging": they "had to mobilize support in ways that
would not invite suppression," and the recurring military dilemmas — "annihilation
versus exhaustion, direct battle or indirect pressure" — reappeared "in forms that
revealed their military origins." The professional revolutionary was, with the
Napoleonic Wars, a consequence of 1789, which proved "that what had happened once could
happen again." The central, dividing question across the whole tradition was the one
that also runs through the nonviolent variant: whether the weak should seize power by a
decisive minority act, or grow into it through the mobilized consciousness of the mass.

## Marx and Engels: the proletariat as strategic agent

[[strategy|Strategy]] for Marx "had to be grounded in class struggles." His role "was
comparable to Clausewitz's in the military sphere": "As Clausewitz provided a theory of
war, Marx provided a theory of revolution." But where Clausewitz theorized from the
experience of war, "Marx developed his theory prior to his experience of revolution, and
then at once found its application problematic." The 1848 revolutions exposed four
defects in the construct: the proletariat had to become "not just a class in itself, but
also a self-aware political force, a class for itself" (the **problem of
consciousness**); class loyalty competed with nation and religion; the real class
structure was "extremely complex," not polarizing into two camps; and the premise "that
a bourgeois revolution must precede the proletarian" left workers backing the very class
that would exploit them. Marx's own verdict — "Men make their own history … but not under
circumstances existing already, given and transmitted from the past" — was, Freedman
notes, "a profound though simple strategic insight."

The split between **insurrection and evolution** opened immediately. Engels treated
insurrection "as an art quite as much as war," demanding audacity once begun — "the
defensive is the death of every armed rising" — yet by his 1895 "testament" he concluded
that "the time of surprise attacks, of revolutions carried through by small conscious
minorities at the head of masses lacking consciousness is past." Modern firepower, rail
mobility, and wide boulevards had made the barricade useful only for its "moral rather
than material effect"; universal suffrage now offered the German SPD a path by which
"we shall grow into the decisive power in the land."

## Anarchism: Bakunin against Marx

Anarchism supplied the great rival tradition. Proudhon "dealt with the
problem of strategy by not advocating any course that needed one." Mikhail Bakunin went
further, "asserting the creativity of destruction" — "Only life itself, freed from all
governmental and doctrinaire fetters … is capable of creation." Where Marx saw
revolutions as constructive events arising from economic shifts, Bakunin saw them as
"supremely unpredictable affairs" that "make themselves." His "propaganda of the deed"
demoted words for dramatic action, which slid (via Nechayev's nihilism) toward terror
and assassination. Their break was strategic: Marx held that a revolution could not
"survive without central direction and coercive capacity," while Bakunin warned with
prescience of "the ruling of the majority by the minority in the name of the alleged
superior intelligence of the second" — the rise of a new oppressive elite under
socialism. The anarchists "understood how the means employed shaped the ends achieved,
but by ruling out all effective means as potentially corrupting" were left as "angry
critics," demonstrated by the futility of the CNT in the Spanish Civil War.

## The revisionist split and Lenin's vanguard

Marx's determinism doomed the activist to a dilemma: wait for conditions to ripen, or
"find a way of accelerating the pace of change." Eduard Bernstein took the first road —
his *Evolutionary Socialism* (1898) judged that "the movement [was] everything, the goal
nothing." Rosa Luxemburg answered with the **mass strike**, change as "the spontaneous
product of the movement in ferment," consciousness awoken "as if by an electric shock."
Against both, [[lenin-vladimir|Lenin]] drove the logic to its hard conclusion in *What
Is to Be Done?* (1902): "Social-Democratic consciousness had to be brought to the workers
from without," and the revolution required "a tightly controlled and disciplined party"
of professional revolutionaries. The **vanguard party** subordinated spontaneity to
organization; Luxemburg condemned its "ultra-centralism," "full of the sterile spirit of
the overseer." Lenin's 1917 victory — won, Freedman argues, because "he survived" and
because his isolation freed him from responsibility — made his reading canonical and
shifted "the center of the movement … from Berlin to Moscow," after which "European
Marxism as a source of strategic innovation never recovered."

## The iron law of oligarchy and the politics of myth

The conservative "neo-Machiavellian" critique held that "a minority would always rule
over a majority." Robert Michels's study of the SPD found that organization itself
betrays the cause: "from a means, organization becomes an end," for "Who says
organization, says oligarchy" — his **"iron law."** Pareto's "circulation of elites"
pitted foxes (guile) against lions (force). The strategic payoff of this school was the
power of consciousness: Mosca's "political formula," Le Bon's suggestible crowd, and
above all Georges Sorel's **myth** — an image "beyond refutation, nonlogical and
irrational" whose "only real test … was whether it could drive a political movement
forward." Weber, meanwhile, set the master frame for all of it: an **ethic of
responsibility** weighing consequences against an ethic of "ultimate ends," insisting
that "the attainment of 'good' ends" may require "morally dubious means."

## Nonviolent resistance: Gandhi and King

The alternative tradition inverted the means. Gandhi's *satyagraha* — "a combination of
truth, love, and firmness" — rested on "the inseparability of ends and means: violent
methods could not deliver a peaceful society." Its strategic logic, sharpened by American
activists, was less spiritual than tactical. Richard Gregg described **"moral
jiu-jitsu,"** in which nonviolent resistance to a violent attacker causes him to "lose
his moral balance," amplified by mass media that made "defenseless men and women
accepting vicious assaults" into "wonderful news." James Farmer (CORE) and Bayard Rustin
treated nonviolence as a weapon judged by "effectiveness not purity of motive." King's
Birmingham (1963) campaign was the perfected form — a "nonviolent version of 'Propaganda
of the Deed'" designed "to create such a crisis and foster such a tension that a
community which has constantly refused to negotiate is forced to confront the issue."
The mechanism was **coalition and media coercion**: economic boycott plus provoked police
brutality, so that "the contrast between the dignity of the protestors and the brutality
of the police created a stunning media spectacle." Nonviolence held the moral high ground
not because "segregationist hearts could be turned" but because it kept it.

## Limitations and Critiques

The tradition's deepest fault line is the **spontaneity-versus-organization** problem,
which it never resolved: Marxists who built disciplined parties produced the Leninist and
Stalinist autocracy that Bakunin had predicted, while anarchists who refused power were
left "waiting for the people to take an initiative that they could support." Michels's
iron law suggests the corruption was structural, not personal. Nonviolence faces its own
boundary condition: Niebuhr warned it could become "support for the status quo," and
Gandhi's "conviction that his methods would work against the Nazis was barely credible."
Freedman's verdict is that nonviolent success was contingent on a particular adversary —
"it depended on a moral authority" and on opponents (the British, southern businessmen)
who could be embarrassed. Against an enemy indifferent to publicity or willing to
massacre, the strategy could "result in greater harm." The civil-rights victories also
showed the limit of political over economic change — "the vote did not feed the children
or pay the rent" — and the movement "turned angrier" toward Black Power and riots once
the clear-cut cause was won.
