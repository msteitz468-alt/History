## Definition and Scope

**Sunni Islam** — or, in Hodgson's preferred formulation, **Jama'i-Sunni** Islam — is the majority tradition of Islam that accepts the historical caliphate as valid and grounds religious authority in the [[hadith|Prophetic hadith]] corpus and community consensus (ijma') rather than in the privileged 'ilm (knowledge) of a designated imam from the Prophet's family. The term "Jama'i" emphasizes allegiance to the *jama'ah* (the Muslim community as a whole); "Sunni" emphasizes adherence to the *sunnah* (normative practice) of the Prophet as transmitted through hadith.

Hodgson warns against equating "Sunni Islam" with "orthodox" Islam, since Shi'i communities are equally "Shari'ah-minded" and equally committed to the Shari'ah. The relevant distinction is not orthodoxy vs. heterodoxy but different theories of religious authority: "The supremacy, or at least the crucial importance, of the Shari'ah has been accepted not only in most Islamic currents, but even by most sorts of opinion within each current — whether mystic or literalist" (Hodgson).

## Emergence — From Jama'ah Solidarity to Sunni Identity, c. 750–945

### The Jama'i-Sunni Position before the 'Abbasids

The Jama'i-Sunni position was not a deliberate program but an orientation: acceptance of the Muslim community as it had actually developed, with all its compromises, as preferable to the risks of revolutionary purification. This contrasted with the Khariji insistence on absolute righteousness and the Shi'i insistence on the privileged imamate. The Jama'i position was summed up in the saying attributed to Muhammad that his community would never agree on an error — making ijma' a doctrinal guarantee. Those who would become the Sunnis "were willing to settle for whomever the bulk of the community, the jama'ah, found it politic to accept as ruler, hoping that the weakness of one man or another would be less harmful to the community as a whole than would the dissensions any other principle must occasion" (Hodgson).

### The 'Abbasid Triumph and the Consolidation of Sunni Shari'ism

The 'Abbasid revolution disappointed the Piety-minded who had hoped for a genuinely Islamic polity, driving a wedge between the Shi'ah (who drew the conclusion that the true imam must be 'Alid) and the Jama'ah (who accepted the 'Abbasids while remaining as autonomous as possible). The 'Abbasids, professing to acknowledge the [[sharia]], gave formal standing to the qadi courts and effectively recognized the [[ulama]] as the legitimate interpreters of Islamic law. This created the institutional basis for Jama'i-Sunni identity.

### The Four Constituents of Classical Sunni Identity

By the end of High Caliphal times (c. 945), Jama'i-Sunni Islam rested on four pillars:

1. **The four madhhab schools of [[fiqh]]** — Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi'i, Hanbali — each tolerated by the others as validly Muslim. "In effect, most Muslims, except for Shi'is, ultimately became Hanafis or Shafi'is, while in the Maghrib and Spain the Malikis prevailed" (Hodgson).

2. **The canonical hadith collections** — the six books, especially those of Bukhari and Muslim, as the authoritative corpus of Prophetic sunnah.

3. **The [[ulama]] as the 'heirs of the Prophet'** — the self-organizing scholarly class whose consensus (expressed in accumulated fatwas) constituted binding ijma'.

4. **A minimalist theory of the caliphate** — the Sunni 'ulama arrived at a theory requiring the caliph to be of Qurayshi stock, of sound faculties, and minimally capable of administrative functions. He needed the recognition of the community but did not need to be the wisest or most pious. Unity of the Dar al-Islam under a single caliph was maintained as an ideal even as political fragmentation advanced.

## Sunni Piety — The Hadith Folk and the Mihna

The most important vehicle of distinctively Sunni popular piety in High Caliphal times was the **Hadith folk** (Ahl al-Hadith), whose populistic, textualist piety — centred on the uncreated Qur'an and the Prophetic hadith — gained wide support among the lower urban classes, especially at Baghdad.

Their defining confrontation was the **Mihna** (833–848): the 'Abbasid caliphs al-Ma'mun, al-Mu'tasim, and al-Wathiq imposed a Mu'tazili-backed inquisition requiring scholars to declare the Qur'an "created." **Ahmad Ibn Hanbal** (780–855) was the exemplary resister: imprisoned, threatened with death, he refused to recant. "His steadfast refusal gave heart to the others" (Hodgson). After al-Mutawakkil abandoned the Mihna (848), the Hadith folk — identifying themselves with the entire Sunni jama'ah — triumphed politically and doctrinally. Ibn Hanbal became the patron of a madhhab school (Hanbali) and the model for later Sunni assertions of scholarly independence from caliphal theology.

The Hadith folk's key doctrinal commitments:

- The Qur'an is "not Creator but not created either" — God's eternal speech somehow coextensive with God's being.
- God is the sole true actor in the universe; human free will is subordinate to divine omnipotence.
- All the Companions of the Prophet are to be revered on a common basis; God will sort out their disputes.
- The jama'ah of Muslims, as actually constituted, carries a special divine blessing.

These positions were opposed by the Mu'tazilis, who accused the Hadith folk of idolatrous "association" (shirk) in treating the Qur'an as a quasi-divine entity, and of dishonoring divine justice by making God the author of evil acts.

## Sunni Islam and the Limits of "Orthodoxy"

Hodgson insists on reserving "orthodoxy" for positions that are established either officially or socially, and rejects its equation with Jama'i-Sunnism per se:

> "The uncautious reader — and too many scholars — can get trapped into an identification of other forms of Islam not only as 'unorthodox' but as somehow more or less 'un-Islamic' — an attitude we have no right to import into the materials." (Hodgson)

The relevant concept is "Shari'ah-mindedness": a family of orientations, Sunni and Shi'i alike, that placed the Shari'ah at the center of Muslim life. Within this family, Jama'i-Sunni Shari'ah-mindedness had a particular character: it identified religious truth with the community as actually constituted, gave authority to the consensus of the 'ulama, and looked to the hadith corpus as the primary witness to Prophetic sunnah.

## Sunni Islam and [[early-sufism|Sufism]]

Early [[early-sufism|Sufism]] developed in close proximity to the Hadith folk and the Jama'i-Sunni milieu. Most early Sufis "were Jama'i-Sunnis, at any rate" (Hodgson). They accepted the Shari'ah as a necessary baseline — "the law is externally mandatory" — while seeking to go beyond its external discipline into inward mystical experience. The relationship between Sunni Shari'ism and Sufism was thus one of mutual dependency: Sufism operated within the Sunni framework while transforming it from within.

## Historiography

The category "Sunni Islam" is heavily contested in recent scholarship. Earlier Western scholarship often treated it as the normative center of a faith against which "heterodox" movements were measured. The post-Marshall Hodgson generation of Islamic studies (Brown, El-Shamsy, Hallaq, Cooperson) has emphasized the contingency of Sunni identity formation — it was not pre-given but constructed through the same historical struggles Hodgson describes. The collection's coverage is strong through Hodgson; thin on the post-945 consolidation period and the regional variations in Sunni identity across the High Middle Ages.
