# Aristocratic Demand

Wickham’s thesis that **elite (especially aristocratic) demand for craft and bulk goods** was the principal motor articulating exchange systems in 400–800, more than independent commercial “markets” in a modern sense. Where aristocracies remained wealthy and dense (northern Francia; Nile Egypt via fiscal demand), exchange complexity stayed high; where aristocracies simplified or fragmented, ceramic and other archaeological proxies show contraction. Core argument of [[wickham-framing-the-early-middle-ages-2005|Framing]] Ch. 11, prepared by Chs. 4–5 on wealth and land management.

## Definition

### Exchange types

“Exchange” for Wickham means any transfer of goods beyond the immediate locality (broader than “trade”). He divides by relation to the profit motive:

1. **Commercial exchange** — commodity sale.
2. **Gift / reciprocity** — social links as express purpose (privileged for reading Britain and Ireland archaeologically).
3. **Redistribution** (Polanyi) — transfers to political/tenurial power and reassignment (tax, tribute, booty, estate-rent movements).

In economic-system analysis he privileges **redistribution** over gift-exchange, except in parts of Britain and Ireland. Fiscal structures can structure exchange (army supply, tax in kind); in the land-based post-Roman west, **aristocratic land-derived surplus** largely substituted for lost fiscal articulation.

### Method: ceramics over coinage; bulk over luxury

- **Ceramics** are the firmest guide to bulk exchange; written sources are a poor guide for 400–800.
- **Coin distributions** often track public administration and gift exchange; only **copper** coins guide non-luxury exchange, and copper was not minted in the post-Roman West. Carolingian silver *denarius* was high-value (c. £12 of 2004 money by 794 Frankfurt bread prices); prosperity and coin scarcity can coexist on excavation.
- **Luxury exchange is economically marginal** as restricted prestige goods. African Red Slip (ARS) was utilitarian in late Roman Africa/Italy but a luxury in sixth-century western Britain and Ireland.
- **Bulk exchange** (foodstuffs or artisanal goods between regions/sub-regions) is the principal marker of economic scale; cloth, iron, and ceramics (in that order) are key markers. Luxury and local exchange always exist; bulk does not.
- **Peacock production scale** (household production → household industry → individual workshops → nucleated workshops → manufactory → factory) is the standing interpretive frame; nucleated workshop was normally the most elaborate level in the period.

### Causal core

In societies without mass buying-power, elites (landowners and tax beneficiaries) determined the scale of demand that created productive complexity and long-distance bulk transport; peasantries accessed better goods as free-riders on elite-created economies of scale. Conversely, if elites were restricted in wealth, bulk markets shrank toward peasant demand. In tribal northern systems with little elite–peasant lifestyle distinction, no bulk market is visible.

**Paradox**: post-Roman peasants, giving less to landowners, were better off, yet lost access to non-local and complex goods because large-scale artisanal infrastructure had depended on landowner wealth. Elaborate productive patterns and large-scale bulk exchange are above all signs of **exploitation and hierarchies of wealth**.

Intensification of estate management (demesnes, tight accounting, cash-cropping, wage labour) is a response to sale possibilities and tax demands, not an inherent stage of “feudal” evolution ([[bipartite-manor]], [[politics-of-land]]). Without commercial networks, intensification is pointless and demesnes tend to be leased out—landlords return to simplest surplus extraction, rent.

## Application: regional ceramic and wealth maps

### Four parameters of post-Roman socio-economic change

1. Strength and geography of **fiscal systems** and state-backed goods movement.
2. Strength and geography of **private landowning wealth** and rent channels.
3. Impact of **war and destruction**.
4. Degree of prior **structural integration** into the Roman world-system.

Elite demand underpinned **regional** complexity; fiscal systems underpinned **interregional** bulk commerce in this period. Causal factors **internal to regions** take primacy over long-distance relationships (explicit anti-[[pirenne-thesis]] weighting).

### Mediterranean world-system and its end

- Late Roman Mediterranean bulk exchange was facilitated by the state, then generated commercial structures that outlasted the fiscal motor (longer in the West) but could not survive indefinitely without it.
- African ARS and amphorae: by later sixth–seventh centuries increasingly restricted to major centres; after c.700 African productions vanish outside the region and ARS ends—a “catastrophe-flip” ending a half-millennium artisanal tradition **without** needing the Arab conquest of Proconsularis (698) as cause. End of African hegemony parallels western fiscal breakdown.
- Eastern interregional network (Egypt–Constantinople grain; Syrian oil) collapsed by 700 after Persian/Arab cuts and Umayyad fiscal provincialization—yet most eastern regions retained high regional productive sophistication (major exception: Byzantine heartland). Paradox: a stable polycentric system vanished without most regions becoming substantially poorer—underscoring the fiscal motor of interregional bulk links.
- Most bulk exchange was always **regional**, not Mediterranean-wide; Mediterranean-wide distributions were atypical even in 400.

### c.800 three-tier ranking (wealth and exploitation, not a “league table”)

1. **Most complex**: Egypt; then northern Francia; Levant; probably Byzantium (nucleated/individual workshops; Egypt near-manufactory at Antinoupolis).
2. **Fragmented sub-regions with pockets**: southern Italy, southern Gaul, Andalucía, Africa, northern Italy, central Spain, East Anglia.
3. **Insufficient wealth for independent exchange**: inland Greece, SE Spain, Midland/southern England, Wales, Ireland, Denmark (household industry).

Northern Frankish heartland exchange complexity was **second only to Egypt** when all variables unfold—despite general eastern Mediterranean articulation remaining high.

### Northern Francia as land-based substitute for fiscal infrastructure

Seventh-century Frankish medium-distance exchange fits the geographical range of aristocratic landowning; large-owner rent-movements and buying-power were the Frankish equivalent of late Roman fiscal support for exchange. Super-regional secular and ecclesiastical landowning almost matched Egypt’s fiscal infrastructure and strengthened as Mediterranean infrastructures weakened ([[tax-based-vs-land-based-states]]). By 800 renewed agrarian intensification is mainly restricted to **northern Francia** (rich aristocracy) and **Egypt/Levant** (powerful Umayyad/Abbasid state)—crystallizing in Charlemagne’s half-century via elite wealth and army needs rather than planned royal market development.

### Lombards, Levant, Egypt

- **Lombard aristocrats**: more urbanized but poorer than Franks; Aistulf’s *maiores et potentes* (750) = soldiers with ~seven tenant-houses; greatest Lombard owners (five-plus *curtes*) outclassed by middling Frankish *proceres*.
- **Levant**: high exchange allowed rich urban owners and independent peasantries to coexist; urban wealth partly from exchange itself; microregionalized like Italy/Spain but each microregion far more complex.
- **Egypt**: integrated hierarchical market (Nile, sub-regional specializations) despite moderate/restricted private wealth after 640—state fiscal demand unifies what private aristocracies cannot.

### Spain and the eighth-century low point

Spanish Meseta TSHT networks marked structural separation from Mediterranean ARS; TSHT proper ends c.500. Coastal SE Spain (Alicante–Murcia) saw extreme seventh–ninth-century simplification toward peasant-mode demand. Ceramic histories do **not** track Leovigild or 711 as socio-economic turning-points. The eighth century is generally the western Mediterranean low point for economic complexity—stripped imports plus falling local demand as aristocracies were less wealthy in 700 than in 400.

### North: emporia, tribal systems, England

- Major eighth-century North Sea *emporia* were nearly all **royal developments**, not spontaneous growth.
- Wales, Ireland, Denmark: dendritic luxury imports to political centres; weak bulk internal exchange; bulk exchange in Denmark awaits aristocratic crystallization over the peasantry.
- England’s slow shift from land-units of superiority to manorial demesnes (Hurstborne Priors c.900 first unequivocal English bipartite-style evidence) tracks rising demand and feudal-mode extraction.

### Urbanism

Private exchange scale depended on aristocratic wealth and taste; a strong impulse to urbanism was aristocrats’ choice to live in cities. Regional western urban differences after fiscal support was removed are best explained by aristocrats’ wealth, taste, and urban residence (plus dukes/counts, bishops, abbots as monumental builders). By 800 Arab and Frankish spheres in the western Mediterranean were delineated (except Sicily), allowing aristocracies several generations to re-root—ninth–tenth-century complexity lies outside the book’s period.

### Material culture

Enormous simplification in material culture “cannot and should not be argued away”: direct consequence of weakened fiscal and aristocratic resources and of peasant economic autonomies ([[demonetisation-of-the-post-roman-west]], [[peasant-mode-of-production]]).

## Historiography

Positions against Pirenne-style long-distance primacy; against over-reading documentary commerce and coin; against Hodges that Charlemagne consciously developed markets to buttress the state (markets diagnose landed wealth). Privileges archaeology—especially ceramics—over written sources for bulk systems. Complements Sarris’s fiscal-materialist de-monetisation story but generalizes the motor as **elite demand** (fiscal *or* aristocratic) and insists regional internal parameters dominate. Contested by commercial-maximalist readings and by cultural histories that resist exploitation-centred accounts of “complexity.”
