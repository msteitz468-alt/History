## Overview

Persian poetry developed from Arabic models but diverged substantially in its preferred genres, metrical sensibility, and thematic range. By the eleventh century, a set of distinctively Persian poetic forms had crystallized that defined the high cultural tradition for the following millennium. Hodgson notes that "Persian poetry, both in its verse patterns and in its subject matter, has proved more congenial than Arabic poetry to modern Western tastes" and has been more frequently translated—though the most celebrated translation (Fitzgerald's *Rubaiyat of Omar Khayyam*) exemplifies "the dangers as well as the creative possibilities" of recreative translation ([[hodgson-venture-of-islam-v2-1974]]).

## Shared Features with Arabic Poetry

Persian poetry shared with classical Arabic poetry the convention of public oral recitation rather than private reading; the expectation that individual lines stand up independently as verbal gems; the interweaving of verse and prose in mixed compositions; the use of syllable-length rather than stress for meter; and the prestige of formal virtuosity. In panegyric especially, the conventions of courtesy, hyperbole, and established conceit carried over from Arabic.

However, Persian poetry took its "very independent way" in both form and spirit. Persian meters, though named by Arabic terminology, were substantially different in pattern and tone. The verse had "a genuine firmness and regularity" replacing what Hodgson calls Arabic's "almost impressionistic use of metre and rhyme." Rhymes tended to be exact and often "elaborately multisyllabic."

## Major Forms

### Qasida (Ode)
The monorhymed ode, vehicle of panegyric, retained high theoretical prestige in Persian as in Arabic but with characteristic modifications: the classical opening with the "deserted camp site" disappeared; panegyric became more elaborate in its conceits; and individual panegyrists could achieve the highest literary reputation (Anvari, Khaqani). The qasida was also used for elegy (marthiya) and for devotional verse.

### Ghazal (Lyric)
The ghazal—a short monorhymed lyrical poem, usually on love or wine, conventionally terminating with the poet's pen-name (takhallus)—became the most beloved and most widely practiced form in Persian literature. Technically it is like the opening two-line unit of a qasida, but each couplet is formally complete in itself rather than contributing to an extended argument. The ghazal demands that "practically everything depended on the images used"—a compressed economy of implication within a small compass.

Ghazal subjects by convention included love (the beloved being conventionally a beautiful young man or boy; mention of loving a woman was considered indecorous); wine; the nightingale and rose as emblems of spiritual longing; and increasingly, under Sufi influence, every mundane theme invested with overtones of mystical yearning. With master poets like Hafiz (14th century), the mundane and spiritual became inseparable.

### Ruba'i (Quatrain)
The ruba'i (plural: ruba'iyyat) is "an epigrammatic form... used for impressionistic effects." Technically it consists of four hemistiches with the rhyme pattern aaba (the third line typically unrhymed). Each quatrain must make a complete point—"the force of the ruba'i had to come from a single beautifully made point, disciplined by exactitude of verbal pattern." Omar Khayyam's ruba'iyyat (the basis of Fitzgerald's Rubaiyat) are the most famous example in Western reception; the form was also used extensively for Sufi aphoristic verse.

### Mathnawi (Couplet Poem)
The mathnawi—an "extended poem in rhyming couplets that could be extended indefinitely"—was the most flexible of Persian forms, "eminently suited to every kind of dignified narrative." Freed from the constraint of a single maintained rhyme (each couplet rhymes internally, not with others), the mathnawi could run to any length. [[firdawsi]]'s Shah-Namah (c. 60,000 couplets) and [[rumi]]'s Mathnawi-ye Ma'nawi (c. 25,000 couplets) are the great extremes of its range. Between these poles it served for romantic narrative (Nizami's Khamsa), Sufi allegory ('Attar's Mantiq al-Tayr), and didactic wisdom (Sa'di's Bustan). Hodgson notes: "The Persian equivalents of the novel are to be found as mathnawis."

## The Persianate Poetic Image

A crucial feature of Persian poetry is its repertoire of standard images, drawn from nature, Sufi symbolism, and Iranian mythology. These images were not ornamental but functional: they were the primary vehicles of meaning. A beautiful face = the full moon; a graceful figure = a cypress; a moth and flame = the soul consumed by divine love; the nightingale (bulbul) singing to the rose = the soul longing for unattainable divine beauty. The cup of Jamshid (which could see all events), the longevity and tragedy of Faridun, Alexander's quest for the Water of Life: all are mythological images from [[shahnameh]] tradition deployed as stock symbols, but with their resonances depending on the reader's knowledge of the whole story. "What counts is what the poet can do with them, how well he can make his references to them serve his turn."

## The Sufi Transformation

From the twelfth century onward, Sufi imagery saturated Persian poetry of all genres. The resulting situation is one where "it becomes difficult (or, more correctly speaking, irrelevant) to try to sort out the mundane from the spiritual elements in his work"—Hodgson's description of Hafiz applies broadly. This development did not replace the earlier courtly and romantic traditions but merged with them, so that even expressly mundane poems might carry Sufi overtones and even Sufi devotional poems might use the language of wine and love in their literal sense.

## Translation Problems

Hodgson devotes extended attention to the problem of translating Persian poetry into Western languages. The fundamental problem is that "any translation omits much of value, and many translations import very alien elements into the poet's work—diction and metres and even rhymes far from the poet's own." Fitzgerald's Rubaiyat is the exemplary case: by arranging separately composed quatrains into a continuous narrative sequence, it "denatures the original perhaps even more than do his free renderings." Such re-creations "may be commended when intended to enrich the receiving literature"—Fitzgerald's was—"but they do not serve well as study translations for understanding the original writer and his culture."
