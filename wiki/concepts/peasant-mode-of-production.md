# Peasant Mode of Production

Analytical category Wickham uses for rural societies in which **direct cultivators control most of the surplus** and aristocratic or state extraction is weak, intermittent, or absent. Contrasts with rent-extracting aristocratic estate systems (the feudal mode in Wickham’s sense) and with tax-extracting fiscal regimes. Not a romantic “free peasantry” ideal type, but a structural description of surplus flows, ranking, and collective capacity. Developed in [[wickham-framing-the-early-middle-ages-2005|Framing the Early Middle Ages]] Part III.

## Definition

### Four agrarian modes

Wickham adopts Marx’s modal distinctions—**slave mode** (directed plantation labour), **feudal mode** (surplus taken as rent from subsistence peasants), **agrarian capitalist mode** (wage labour)—and adds a fourth: the **peasant mode of production**, for economies where landlords or the state do not systematically take surplus.

### Ideal-type peasant mode

Drawing on Sahlins, Boserup, and Meillassoux:

- Basic production unit is the **individual household**.
- Exchange is **reciprocal and socially embedded**, not accumulation-oriented.
- Surpluses are not easily accumulated (given away or consumed).
- Little productive specialization.
- Ranking by **generosity**, not permanent class.
- Freer leisure when surplus is not extracted to outsiders—members “do not work so hard.”

A **peasant** is a settled cultivator (or more rarely pastoralist), producing largely for subsistence, doing at least some agricultural work personally, and controlling labour on the land. Rich peasants who cease to farm any land directly become “medium landowners,” structurally dominant village elites at the foot of a ladder that could lead into the aristocracy.

### Tribal ranked societies

One common version is “tribal” ranked peasant-mode society: a ruler is less exclusive landowner than leader of free people bound by mutual obligation; free peasants owe intermittent tribute and military service but are economically autonomous; rights in land often overlap, lacking exclusive feudal-mode ownership. Absence of exclusive ownership rights is a good (though not reverse-necessary) sign of such societies. Wickham applies “tribal” to kin-named Irish and English units and to peoples named without kin ideology.

### Tenancy and productive control

Even under tenancy, strategic productive choices rest with the peasant family on its plot; landlord intervention is “against the grain” of the labour process and only partly successful without constant coercive attention. Early medieval lords in most regions relaxed that attention as exchange motives faded ([[aristocratic-demand]], [[bipartite-manor]]).

## Application: regional evidence

### Two patterns of autonomous peasant economy

1. **North European tribal form** — England, Wales, Ireland, Denmark, Mauretania; probably parts of Spain by the eighth century; also *forestis*/*gualdus* islands in Francia/Italy. Clients not tenants; light tribute.
2. **Romano-Germanic leopard-spot pattern** — where Roman landowning survives: peasant-proprietor microregions interspersed with feudal-mode estates (e.g. Île-de-France “dark”; lighter elsewhere—Lucca, middle Rhine, Galatia, Jēme, Malling).

### Chronology of the high point of peasant autonomy

The earliest Middle Ages was the low point of aristocratic dominance over western peasantries:

| Region | Approximate high-autonomy window |
|---|---|
| Francia | 6th–7th c. (always relatively strong aristocracy) |
| Italy | late 6th–early 8th c. |
| Spain | 8th–9th c. |
| Britain | early 5th–9th c. |

By 1000 (Francia, Italy, England) and 1100 (Spain) the peasant mode was vestigial; feudal aristocratic logic was supreme—as it had always been in much of the eastern Mediterranean.

### Northern societies and state-building

All societies of the North were in large part or entirely peasant-mode c.600; by 800 most were moving feudal (England most, Ireland least). Without coercive landowning predominance of aristocrats—including kings—over the peasantry, political resources stayed too limited for fully fledged state structures ([[tax-based-vs-land-based-states]]). Early English hide-units fit Maitland’s “superiority” model (tribute from free followers) more than Roman exclusive estates; Faith’s inland (dependent, rent-like) / outland-warland (extensive, lower tributes) dualism marks the slow growth of exclusive tenure.

### Eastern villages and western settlement

- **Eastern Mediterranean**: institutional village organization (*koinotēs*) was strongest; packed house-courtyard forms and village community identity as a social actor remained the stage across Egypt, Levant, and Anatolia 400–800. Limestone Massif prosperity largely village-level medium and small owners (Tate; Wickham).
- **Western Mediterranean**: the early Middle Ages made *village identity* newly available without yet producing eastern-style strong communities; settlement irregularity is normal peasant artefact. Lombard Italy shifts from *fundi* (ownership units) toward *vicus*/*locus* territorial identity from ~700; Byzantine Italy retains *fundi* until incastellamento.
- **Rhineland (Dienheim)**: free peasantry kept distance from monastic patronage—aristocratic structures more threatening than inviting—unlike Lucca’s deeper clienteles.
- **Valle Trita** (vs S. Vincenzo al Volturno, 779–873): best western case of peasant mode confronting feudal monastery with prolonged success until the 881 Arab sack.

### Free–unfree and village coherence

Free–unfree status mattered differently by tenure regime: bridgeable under single-lord Paris-basin villages; sharper where free owners, free tenants, and unfree mixed (Lucca); structural to household exploitation where peasant owners dominated (England). Analyses treating “slaves” as uniform across regions are “basically flawed.” Weak villages push peasants toward external patrons; easy patronage (especially by elites aiming at aristocratic entry) weakens villages—both reduce autonomy.

### Resistance and the flip to feudal mode

Physical resistance likeliest where status fell fast, geography was marginal/silvo-pastoral, dominators were divided, and patronage ties were weak (Saxony, Trita, Flexum, Asturias, etc.). The economic shift is threefold: (1) steady strengthening of aristocratic status/wealth inside or beside the peasant mode; (2) a “catastrophe-flip” to feudal economic logic; (3) reduction of remaining peasant-autonomy enclaves under overall feudal dominance. Aristocratic political hegemony via patronage runs parallel but distinct. When constraints fail (multipolar ownership, law/custom, royal opposition, court focus), patronage turns to coercion and local economic logic “flicks over.”

### Post-Roman balance and material culture

Overall (exception: Frankish Gaul), post-Roman aristocracies were poorer; by the seventh century more peasant owners, lower tenant exploitation, and little or no tax left a temporary balance favouring peasants—though artisanal access worsened. Features often read as early medieval “failure” (weak artisanal complexity, simpler technologies, restricted population) Wickham prefers as **functional** to economies largely dominated by peasants rather than lords. Demographic abatement after political crisis may follow peasant-mode leisure logic (Boserup): work less, abandon poorer land, possibly restrict births when rent/tax pressure relaxes.

Fragmentation of large landowning is the key peasant-autonomy parameter more even than simple extent of aristocratic land: aristocratic power was all-pervasive only in the Île de France among Wickham’s seven case studies, because only there are large single-owner blocks well documented.

## Historiography

Etic Marxian mode-of-production analysis applied comparatively; deliberately rejects slavery-to-serfdom teleology for 400–800 and the reading of law-codes as descriptive peasant sociology (charters preferred as locally specific). Positions against Goffart-style “aristocratic privatization of tax” as simple elite windfall; against catastrophist decline narratives that ignore peasant economic logic; against Hodges-style markets-as-state-building. Complements [[politics-of-land]] and [[aristocratic-demand]]; tensions with accounts that treat early medieval material simplicity as pure collapse rather than redistribution of surplus control. Contested by cultural historians wary of mode language and by maximalist readings of early manorialism.
