## Definition and Scope

The Ḥanafī school (Arabic: al-madhhab al-Ḥanafī) is one of the four canonical Sunnī legal schools ([[madhhab]]) of [[sharia|Islamic law]]. It takes its name from Abū Ḥanīfa, the 8th-century Iraqi jurist whose circle (ḥalaqa) in Kufa became the doctrinal seed of the school. The school is historically dominant across Iraq, the eastern Islamic lands (Iran, Transoxiana, Central Asia), South Asia, and — most decisively for its long-term reach — the Ottoman Empire.

## Founding and Early Formation

Hallaq emphasizes that Abū Ḥanīfa himself did not found a "school" in any institutional sense; what the tradition identifies as "the first and second tiers" of Ḥanafī authoritative doctrine are the positions attributed to Abū Ḥanīfa, his leading disciple Abū Yūsuf (d. 798 CE), al-Shaybānī (d. 805 CE), and al-Ḥasan b. Ziyād. Subsequent generations of jurists — the "third tier" — produced the massive fatwā and [[fiqh]] literature that in practice constituted "the most dominant" layer of the school's operative doctrine:

> "In Ḥanafite law, [fatāwā] formed the third tier of authoritative legal doctrine, reflecting the contributions made by jurists who flourished after the first masters of the school, Abū Ḥanīfa, Abū Yūsuf, Shāybānī and al-Ḥasan b. Ziyād, who contributed the first and second tiers. In sheer size and in the daily reality of legal practice, however, the third tier was the most dominant."

The school was rooted in the traditions of the Kufan/Iraqi scholarly environment, which emphasized rational legal reasoning (ra'y) in cases where explicit scriptural guidance was absent. This methodological orientation — while debated in [[usul-al-fiqh]] — distinguished early Ḥanafism from the more ḥadīth-oriented approaches of the Medinan tradition associated with [[maliki-school|Mālik b. Anas]].

## Geographic Dominance and Political Adoption

The Ḥanafī school achieved hegemony across the largest contiguous swathe of the Islamic world, partly through scholarly prestige and partly through deliberate dynastic patronage:

- **ʿAbbāsid caliphate**: Abū Yūsuf served as chief qāḍī (qāḍī al-quḍāt) under Hārūn al-Rashīd, the first holder of that office, giving the school early prestige at the imperial center.
- **Saljūq and successor dynasties**: The Saljūqs, committed Sunnīs ruling from Iran eastward, drew heavily on Ḥanafī jurists and the madrasa infrastructure built by Niẓām al-Mulk in the 5th/11th century, which housed Ḥanafī and [[shafii-school|Shāfiʿī]] ḥalaqas side by side in Baghdad.
- **Ottoman Empire (decisive adoption)**: The Ottomans adopted Ḥanafism as the official [[madhhab]] of the Empire, making it the school of the entire state apparatus. Every chief qāḍī dispatched from Istanbul was Ḥanafī; advancement in the imperial judiciary presupposed Ḥanafī training in the royal [[madrasa|madrasas]] of Istanbul. Hallaq describes the effect: "The legal profession, law students and legists of non-Ḥanafite persuasion were encouraged to, and indeed did, migrate to the Ḥanafite school in search of career opportunities." In Greater Syria, where Shāfiʿīs formed the majority at the Ottoman conquest (922–23/1516–17), by the end of the 19th century "only a tiny minority of Shāfiʿītes remained in that region, the rest having become Ḥanafites." See [[qadi-judiciary]] for the Ottoman judicial hierarchy.

Under the Mughals of South Asia (1556–1757), Ḥanafī law similarly governed the official judiciary, with the chief judicial officer (the ṣadr) administering a system of Ḥanafī courts from the imperial center.

## Doctrinal Characteristics

Within the Ḥanafī school's operative legal literature, several features stand out:

- **Cumulative tradition**: Later school texts never abrogated earlier ones; the founding fathers' doctrines remained "enmeshed in the much later fatwā literature and author-jurist compilations." This made Ḥanafī law a layered accretion rather than a revised code.
- **Formal hierarchy of authority**: Uniquely among the four schools, Ḥanafism maintained an explicit three-tier hierarchy of doctrine (founding masters → first-generation disciples → later jurists), which structured how [[mufti-and-fatwa|muftī]]s weighed competing opinions.
- **Marriage contract flexibility in practice**: Court records from 16th-century Ottoman Cairo show Ḥanafī and Mālikī judges adjudicating cases with marriage contract stipulations formally associated with the [[hanbali-school|Ḥanbalī]] school, attesting to the school's practical flexibility at the level of application.
- **Mukhataṣar tradition**: The Ḥanafī school developed authoritative abridgments (mukhtaṣars) of the fiqh corpus that served both as pedagogical texts and as authoritative summaries of operative doctrine in the ḥalaqa and [[madrasa]].

## Relationship to the Ḥalaqa and Madrasa

The school's doctrine was transmitted and developed through the study-circle ([[madrasa|ḥalaqa]]) system. The professor-jurist (muṣannif) and the jurisconsult ([[mufti-and-fatwa|muftī]]) were the two institutional vectors through which Ḥanafī law evolved in response to social conditions. The Ottoman royal madrasas of Istanbul became exclusively Ḥanafī institutions, cementing a connection between school affiliation and state career that had no precedent in earlier Islamic history.

## Relationship to Other Schools

The four schools — Ḥanafī, [[maliki-school|Mālikī]], [[shafii-school|Shāfiʿī]], [[hanbali-school|Ḥanbalī]] — recognized each other's legitimacy within [[taqlid-and-ijtihad|taqlīd]]. Nelly Hanna's study of Ottoman Cairo found that "it was not unusual for one person to buy a house one day according to Ḥanbalī law and get married next day according to Mālikī or Shāfiʿī law. By assessing the specific differences between the schools of law … people deliberately chose the school that best defended their interests in any particular case or transaction." This shopping across schools was a recognized feature of practical legal culture across the pre-modern Islamic world.

## Historiography

Western scholarship long characterized the post-formative period of Islamic law as one of stagnation, with school doctrine merely piling commentary upon commentary. Recent scholarship — most prominently Hallaq himself — has demonstrated that the cumulative textual tradition of the Ḥanafī school (and the others) was the vehicle through which "legal change — as a response to changing social reality — was modulated." The collection is strong on Ḥanafī practice under the Ottomans via legal history monographs; thinner on the formative period and the eastern (Transoxianian and Indian) tradition.
