# Maritime Strategy

**Maritime strategy** is the analytical framework set out by [[corbett-julian|Julian
S. Corbett]] in *Some Principles of Maritime Strategy* (1911) for waging war "in
which the sea is a substantial factor." Its defining move is to subordinate naval
operations to a larger strategy that coordinates fleet and army together with the
political, economic, and diplomatic instruments of national power. It is the
theoretical core of the British or "maritime" school of strategy, and it stands in
deliberate contrast to the doctrine of [[sea-power]] associated with
[[mahan-alfred-thayer|Mahan]].

## Why "Maritime," Not "Naval"

Corbett's first principle is that naval power is rarely self-sufficient. "By maritime
strategy," he writes, "we mean the principles which govern a war in which the sea is
a substantial factor. Naval strategy is but that part of it which determines the
movements of the fleet when maritime strategy has determined what part the fleet must
play in relation to the action of the land forces; **for it scarcely needs saying that
it is almost impossible that a war can be decided by naval action alone.**" Unaided
naval pressure "can only work by a process of exhaustion," its effects "slow," because
"men live upon the land and not upon the sea": "great issues between nations at war
have always been decided … either by what your army can do against your enemy's
territory and national life or else by the fear of what the fleet makes it possible
for your army to do."

The theory of war therefore "reveals that embracing them both is a larger strategy
which regards the fleet and army as one weapon." Naval strategy "is not a thing by
itself," but "only a part of maritime strategy — the higher learning which teaches
us that for a maritime State … army and navy must be used and thought of as
instruments no less intimately connected than are the three arms ashore."

## Corbett's Hierarchy of Strategy

Corbett distinguishes three nested levels. **Major strategy** — also called "grand"
— treats all the elements of national power: in his words, the conduct of war for
"a world-wide maritime Empire" turns on "the mutual relation of naval, military, and
political considerations" (see [[grand-strategy]]). **Maritime strategy** sits beneath
it, governing the "mutual relations of your army and navy in a plan of war." Beneath
that is **naval strategy**, which he calls "minor strategy," and which "can begin to
work out the manner in which the fleet can best discharge the function assigned to it"
only after maritime strategy has fixed that function. The "paramount concern, then, of
maritime strategy is to determine the mutual relations of your army and navy in a plan
of war."

## Command of the Sea as Control of Communications

Corbett's most consequential redefinition is of "command of the sea." It is **not**
analogous to the conquest of territory: "You cannot conquer sea because it is not
susceptible of ownership," nor "subsist your armed force upon it." The "only positive
value which the high seas have for national life is as a means of communication."
Therefore:

> "Command of the sea, therefore, means nothing but the control of maritime
> communications, whether for commercial or military purposes. The object of naval
> warfare is the control of communications, and not, as in land warfare, the conquest
> of territory."

From this several practical corollaries follow. Command "may exist in various degrees":
it may be "general or local," and "permanent or temporary." Crucially, the loss of
command by one side does not pass it automatically to the other: "the normal position
is not a commanded sea, but an uncommanded sea," and "it is this state of dispute with
which naval strategy is most nearly concerned." Because command is only the control of
communications, the **decisive battle is not always necessary** — Corbett mocks the
"doctrine … that you cannot move a battalion oversea till you have entirely overthrown
your enemy's fleet" as deserving "gibbeting." Even "permanent general command can never
in practice be absolute."

## The Fleet in Being

Where a power is "too weak to win command by offensive operations," it "may yet succeed
in holding the command in dispute by assuming a general defensive attitude." The naval
expression of this is the **fleet in being**. For Corbett this is not passive: "At sea
the main conception is avoiding decisive action by strategical or tactical activity, so
as to keep our fleet in being till the situation develops in our favour." Its essence is
"mobility, not rest" — "Everything was counter-attack." A naval defensive "means nothing
but keeping the fleet actively in being — not merely in existence, but in active and
vigorous life." The right at stake at sea is "the right of passage"; commerce warfare he
prefers to call "Commerce prevention" rather than "destruction," the capture of property
being "the ultimate sanction of the interdict which we are seeking to enforce."

## Limited War and the British Way

Maritime strategy is bound up with [[limited-war]]. Following Clausewitz's distinction
between limited and unlimited war, Corbett argues that the sea uniquely enables the
limited form:

> "that limited war is only permanently possible to island Powers or between Powers
> which are separated by sea, and then only when the Power desiring limited war is able
> to command the sea to such a degree as to be able not only to isolate the distant
> object, but also to render impossible the invasion of his home territory."

The instrument is **combined operations** — what Clausewitz called "war limited by
contingent" and the eighteenth century called "conjunct operations" — conducted with a
"disposal force" rather than the whole national army. The army becomes a projectile
fired by the navy: its "weight and mobility … are beyond its intrinsic power" precisely
because "naval and military action can be combined." The model is **Wellington in the
Peninsula**: "The real secret of Wellington's success — apart from his own genius — was
that in perfect conditions he was applying the limited form to an unlimited war. Our
object was unlimited. It was nothing less than the overthrow of Napoleon … but that
success [at sea] had given us the power of applying the limited form." Pitt's campaigns
against Canada, the Crimea, and the Russo-Japanese seizure of Korea are his other
worked cases.

## The Clausewitzian Foundation

The whole edifice rests on Clausewitz. Corbett insists that "war in a fundamental sense
is a continuation of policy by other means" — "a form of political intercourse in which
we fight battles instead of writing notes." The "primordial question" of any war plan is
"What will the war be about?" — its political object — and this "is the first and the
most far-reaching of all strategical questions." Like Clausewitz, Corbett warns that
wars "free from political control" tend to "move of their own accord toward the absolute
form" — toward [[total-war|total war]]; rational policy keeps war from drifting to "the
'absolute' ideal." But limitation never means avoiding battle: "All forms alike demand
the use of battles … it is by fighting we have to gain our end."

## Emic and Etic

Maritime strategy is partly **emic** — Corbett presents it as the articulation of a real
"British tradition" practised by Drake, Pitt, and Wellington and theorized only belatedly,
a school "which too many writers both at home and abroad quietly assume to have no
existence." It is also **etic**: an analytical taxonomy (major / maritime / naval
strategy; limited / unlimited war; general / local and permanent / temporary command)
imposed by the historian to "determine the normal" against which any plan can be judged.
Corbett is explicit that theory only educates judgment and cannot replace it.

## Contrast with Mahan

The concept is `contested` chiefly through its tension with [[sea-power]] as theorized by
[[mahan-alfred-thayer|Mahan]]. Where Mahan made command of the sea a quasi-territorial,
zero-sum prize to be won by the **concentrated battle fleet** in **decisive action**,
Corbett held command to be "nothing but the control of maritime communications" — divisible,
often local and temporary, normally *in dispute*, and frequently securable without a great
fleet battle at all. Where Mahan's logic pulls toward the offensive and the massed fleet,
Corbett rehabilitates the strategic defensive and the fleet in being, and treats the navy
as one arm of a combined, army-and-fleet instrument serving policy. Contemporaries felt the
rivalry keenly — one reviewer dubbed Corbett "Super Mahan," while critics warned his
de-emphasis of the decisive fleet action and his stress on limited war would have "a
disastrous effect upon the Navy."

## Limitations and Critiques

Corbett's treatment of **trade defence** is the weakest link: the source notes "Corbett's
underestimation of the importance of convoys, which would be the source of so much criticism
in the next war." He himself conceded that "Modern developments and changes in shipping and
naval material have indeed so profoundly modified the whole conditions of commerce protection,
that there is no part of strategy where historical deduction is more difficult or more liable
to error." More broadly, the doctrine of limited war "traverses the current belief that our
primary object must always be the enemy's armed forces," and so was attacked by continental
theorists "who have a natural difficulty in conceiving a war plan that does not culminate in
a Jena or a Sedan."
