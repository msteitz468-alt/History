## Definition

German nationalism in the nation-state era refers to the ideological and political dynamic by which competing visions of the German nation — who belongs, what the nation requires, how to achieve and defend it — shaped, destabilized, and ultimately destroyed the political order between 1871 and 1945. Unlike pre-1871 nationalism (which was aspirational, seeking unification), post-1871 nationalism faced the practical challenge of creating national unity within an existing state while managing tensions between civic and ethnic conceptions of nationhood, between state-directed and radical-activist forms, and between inclusion of Germans abroad and exclusion of non-Germans within.

Source: [[smith-oxford-modern-german-history-2011]], Chapter 22 (Pieter M. Judson, "Nationalism in the Era of the Nation State, 1870–1945").

## Emic vs. Etic

As an **emic** concept, "the German nation" was invoked by virtually every political actor from liberal nationalists to radical pan-Germans to Social Democrats. The content of the invocation varied enormously — who counts as German, what the nation requires, whether nationality is civic or ethnic — but the rhetorical form ("we speak for the national community") was near-universal.

As an **etic** category, historians analyze German nationalism by disaggregating these competing uses, identifying the structural conditions under which one or another version became dominant, and tracing the relationship between nationalist ideology and state practice.

## Major Phases

### Phase 1: Wilhelmine nationalism — civic to ethnic shift (1871–1914)

The proclamation of the German Empire created a paradox: nationalists who had worked for unification now had to **create** the unified national society whose existence they had presumed. "The project of nationalizing Germany's citizens faced several unexpected obstacles": regional loyalties, confessional divisions, and the abstract quality of a new Prussian-dominated state for citizens of Baden, Saxony, or Württemberg.

Early nationalism was largely **liberal and Protestant**: National Liberal parties associated Germanness with Protestant bourgeois culture. The Kulturkampf was the political expression of this equation — Catholicism was excluded from full national belonging because it was associated with "feminine dependence," foreign (Roman) loyalty, and irrationalism. Heinrich von Treitschke's definition of Germanness was explicitly Protestant-bourgeois.

By the 1890s, the framework shifted from civic-liberal to **ethnic-racial**. The Pan German League (1891), Navy League (1898), and Society for Germandom in the Eastern Marches (1894) popularized ethnic definitions of nation that saw the German state as incomplete: the real German nation included ethnic Germans (*Volksdeutsche*) across Austria-Hungary, Russia, and Eastern Europe. These "radical activists" challenged both the government's half-measures and the civic understanding of nationhood.

**The Eastern question**: Prussian policy in Polish-speaking territories involved a contradictory mixture of assimilation (language bans, Colonization Commission) and exclusion (expulsion of non-citizen Polish and Jewish workers, 1885). The deeper contradiction: the more nationalists emphasized German ethnic distinctiveness, the less they could believe in the assimilation of Slavic populations. If Germans and Poles were fundamentally different peoples, assimilation could not resolve the "Polish question." This structural contradiction between assimilationist and exclusionary logic would not be resolved until the Nazi period chose genocide.

**State vs. movement tension**: Nationalist associations became increasingly uncontrollable by the government. The Navy League's radicals demanded faster naval buildup than Tirpitz thought prudent; the Pan German League attacked the Kaiser after the 1908 Daily Telegraph affair. "Mass mobilization around nationalist issues might threaten the very legitimacy of the state it was meant to reinforce."

### Phase 2: World War I and nationalist radicalization (1914–1919)

The war exposed and deepened tensions within German nationalism:

- Women demanded expanded citizenship rights as national co-contributors through home-front sacrifice
- Workers expected union recognition in exchange for wartime cooperation
- The German Fatherland Party (1917) mobilized around annexationist war aims, explicitly rejecting the Reichstag Peace Resolution (1917)
- Brest-Litovsk (March 1918) appeared to vindicate nationalist imperialism, then was followed by Western collapse

Germany's defeat in the West was perceived as inexplicable given victory in the East. The "stab-in-the-back" legend (*Dolchstoßlegende*) attributed defeat not to military failure but to domestic betrayal by Jews and Communists — a conspiratorial antisemitism that became the foundational myth of radical right nationalism.

### Phase 3: Weimar — ethnic nationalism and the "diaspora" concept (1919–1933)

Defeat and the Paris Peace Settlement transformed German nationalism in several ways:

**The diaspora concept**: The dissolution of Austria-Hungary placed millions of German-speakers (6 million in Austria, 3 million in Czechoslovakia, smaller groups in Poland, Romania, Yugoslavia) under non-German rule. Nationalists invented the concept of *Streudeutsche* ("scattered Germans") — a German diaspora suffering under hostile neighbor-states. Germany positioned itself as protector of these minorities, which justified its entry into the League of Nations (under Stresemann) while keeping revision of eastern borders on the agenda. The "diaspora" argument was never really about the democratic self-determination of these populations; it was about territorial revision.

**Volkstumsarbeit**: Cultural organizations were funded (by both Weimar and later Nazi governments) to maintain German cultural identity in Eastern European border communities. These organizations distributed periodicals, founded libraries, built schools, and organized holiday rituals. What actual German-speakers in these communities wanted was often irrelevant — Silesian plebiscite voters in 1921 chose Germany for religious and economic reasons, not nationalist ones.

**Rise of Nazi nationalism**: The NSDAP's electoral breakthrough in 1930–1932 occurred in the context of economic depression and the exhaustion of established parties. Nazi nationalism differed from older forms: Hitler's rejection of *Bildung* (academic culture) as the criterion for national leadership, and the explicitly racial-egalitarian promise (all racial Germans deserve respect regardless of class position), represented a **social democratization of nationalism** that alarmed traditional conservative nationalists even as they eventually voted Nazi to defeat Communism.

### Phase 4: Nazi Volksgemeinschaft and empire (1933–1945)

See [[volksgemeinschaft]] for detailed treatment. Key points from the nationalist perspective:

**Radicalization of membership**: The Nuremberg Laws defined racial citizenship; eugenics defined who could reproduce within the community; the category of "community aliens" (*Gemeinschaftsfremde*) expelled social deviants. Nationalism contracted to the racially pure while simultaneously expanding territorially.

**Territorial revision as nationalism**: The annexation of Austria (*Anschluss*, 1938) and Sudetenland (Munich Agreement, 1938) could be understood in traditional German nationalist terms as correcting the injustices of 1919. Most German nationalists understood it this way. What they did not anticipate was Hitler's much more radical vision of **continental empire** — the occupation of all of Poland, the invasion of the Soviet Union — which went beyond the correction of Versailles toward the creation of a racial empire that would make Germany the world's leading power.

**Nationalist ideology vs. Nazi reality**: Traditional nationalists who hoped to use the Nazis to restore conservative order found themselves increasingly marginalized. The Pan German League, whose racial antisemitism had helped create the intellectual conditions for Nazism, was too focused on educated *Bildungsbürger* leadership and too conservative to adapt to Nazi mass mobilization. By 1932 they voted Nazi out of fear of communism, not enthusiasm for Nazi egalitarianism.

## Analytical Issues

### Civic vs. ethnic nationalism

A classic debate in nationalism studies (Hans Kohn's 1944 formulation) distinguished "western civic nationalism" from "eastern ethnic nationalism," assigning Germany to the latter category. Recent scholarship (including Judson's chapter) has "effectively demolished" this dichotomy: "wherever we encounter it in mid-twentieth-century Europe, nationalism rested on the idea of a prior national community defined by shared culture if not ethnicity." France itself expelled Alsatians in 1918 on the basis of descent, not language use or loyalty.

### Manipulation thesis

Hans-Ulrich Wehler's influential "manipulation thesis" held that Wilhelmine nationalism was primarily a tool of ruling elites to deflect popular attention from social conflicts — "social imperialism." Judson contests this: "popular nationalism in Germany was far more a product of the imaginative rhetorical and organizational strategies devised by activists than it was a product of state manipulation." The nationalist associations were frequently *more* radical than the government and represented genuine mass politics, not elite ventriloquism.

### Was German nationalism exceptional?

Judson's chapter concludes with a direct engagement with the question of German exceptionalism ([[german-sonderweg]]): "during this period German nationalism looks remarkably similar to other forms of nationalism in Europe, even taking into account the specificities of the German case. Racialized forms of nationalism could be found across Europe, from Ireland to Romania." The dynamic sense of victimization, the assertion that co-nationals suffered under hostile neighbor-states, constituted "critical components of Hungarian and Italian nationalism as well." Hannah Arendt's observation is invoked: the "nationalization of European society … largely succeeded in replacing the rights of man with the rights of nations" — making genocide not uniquely German but a more general potentiality of European ethnic nationalism in the twentieth century.

This is not to deny the catastrophe of Nazism but to locate it within a pan-European structural context rather than treating it as an aberrant product of uniquely German pathologies.

## Contested Aspects

- Was there a "good" German civic nationalism that was distorted by a "bad" ethnic nationalism? Or were they always interwoven?
- How much did the specific historical sequences (1871 unification, 1914 war, 1918 defeat, 1933 seizure of power) determine the outcome vs. structural features of German nationalism?
- Is German nationalism a "case of" the general category, or does the Holocaust require treating it as sui generis?
