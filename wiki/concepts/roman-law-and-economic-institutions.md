# Roman Law and Economic Institutions

**Bruce Frier and Dennis Kehoe** apply the tools of **New Institutional Economics** (NIE) —
transaction costs, the Coase theorem, bounded rationality, path dependence, principal-agent
theory — to Greek and especially Roman legal sources, arguing that "law and legal institutions
helped determine the forms within which economic activity was organized," shaping the
distribution of wealth between state and citizens, city and countryside, elite landowners and
peasants, and masters and slaves. Drawn from Ch. 5 of
[[scheidel-cambridge-economic-history-greco-roman-world-2007|The Cambridge Economic History of the
Greco-Roman World]] (2007); see [[new-institutional-economics]] for the general framework this
chapter applies.

## The Coase Theorem in Roman Law

The chapter's organizing case is the "cheese factory at Minturnae" (jurist Titius Aristo, first
century CE; Ulpian, D.8.5.8.5): under frictionless, zero-transaction-cost conditions, the initial
legal allocation of property rights — whether a neighbor can stop another's smoke emissions —
does not affect the efficient outcome, because the parties simply bargain around whatever rule is
set. But transaction costs (locating and contacting parties, monitoring, enforcement) are
pervasive in the real world, meaning the initial allocation of legal rights *does* matter to
efficiency once transaction costs are taken into account — and institutions arise, spontaneously
or by design, specifically to mitigate them.

## Institutions Addressing Information Asymmetry

The Roman slave market is Frier and Kehoe's central case study of an institutional response to
**adverse selection**. The curule aediles, magistrates overseeing Rome's slave and livestock
markets, imposed strict, no-fault liability on sellers to disclose latent disease, defects, and
prior flight-risk in slaves — a lemon-market problem in a market for an unusually complex good.
Buyers could rescind the sale within six months or recover the price difference within a year
(D.21.1.1.1, Ulpian). Buyers also demanded disclosure of a slave's *natio* (ethnic/national
origin) as an indirect quality-signaling device against information asymmetry; Ulpian records the
underlying logic explicitly: "there is a presumption that some slaves are good because they come
from a nationality that is not of bad repute" (D.21.1.31.21).

**Bounded rationality** (Herbert Simon) explains why ancient contracts, like modern ones, were
typically incomplete rather than covering every contingency; once established, institutions tend
to persist regardless of continued efficiency — Douglass North's **path dependence** — a pattern
the chapter traces through several Roman cases below.

## Risk Allocation in Tenancy

Pliny the Younger's letter (*Ep.* 9.37) on shifting Roman tenant farmers from fixed rent to
sharecropping illustrates deliberate risk allocation: fixed rent places crop and price risk on the
tenant, sharecropping divides it. Roman default rule, absent an express contract, placed the risk
of ordinary crop shortfall or price decline on the fixed-rent tenant (who controlled cultivation
and had local market information), with an exception carved out for catastrophic events like
hailstorms. Roman tenancy law also entitled tenants to recover the cost of "useful expenditures"
made on the landlord's property without requiring the landlord's prior consent — a default rule
encouraging tenant investment (jurist Scaevola, D.19.2.61pr., allows recovery even where the
tenant was later expelled for inability to pay a rent increase after making his own improvements).

By contrast, a husband administering his wife's dowry — also an agency relationship — could *not*
freely make improvement decisions: the jurist Paul (D.25.1.8) required the wife's assent before
useful expenditures on dowry property became recoverable, reflecting a "cooperative model of
marriage" that prioritized marital harmony over strict efficiency, reinforced by Augustan
legislation barring alienation of dowry land or manumission of dowry slaves without the wife's
consent (see [[ancient-household-economy]] for the household context).

## Partnership and Agency Law

Roman *societas* (partnership) law lacked juristic personality: partnerships dissolved on the
death or withdrawal of any partner, with the exception of the tax-farming *societates* of the late
Republic. Roman agency law more broadly lacked a category of a fully empowered agent acting for a
principal, a gap some scholars read as an institutional shortcoming limiting the growth of complex,
ongoing business enterprises. Consensual contracts — sale (*emptio-venditio*), lease
(*locatio-conductio*), partnership (*societas*), and mandate (*mandatum*), probably established by
the second century BCE — together with six *actiones adiecticiae qualitatis* remedies (third/second
century BCE) protecting third parties who contracted with agents, lowered transaction costs and
clarified agency liability; Frier and Kehoe suggest these were likely developed with elite Roman
interests specifically in mind, limiting principal liability.

**Athenian banking** supplies a comparative case: fourth-century-BCE Athenian banks, mostly owned
by foreign residents (since citizens were barred from owning land in Attica and thus had less need
of banking as a business), financed maritime commerce. The banker Pasion (a non-citizen) leased his
bank to his slave manager Phormion, who later married Pasion's widow Archippe and inherited the
bank — illustrating the use of slave managers to solve agency and monitoring problems while
limiting owner liability and blocking rival banks from poaching trained staff (source: the
Demosthenic corpus, via Apollodorus's speeches concerning his father Pasion's estate).

Rome's parallel solution was the **slave or freedman agent with a *peculium*** — quasi-independent
working capital that made the slave manager a de facto residual claimant with incentive to work
efficiently and avoid self-dealing, at the cost of the owner ceding centralized control. This also
reinforced elite avoidance of direct, "sordid" business involvement (D'Arms 1981) and reinforced
Roman social hierarchy by limiting the emergence of an independent artisan/entrepreneur class. The
**Heroninos archive** (Fayyum, Egypt, third century CE) shows Aurelius Appianus's estate managed
through village-level managers (*phrontistai*) reporting to the equestrian estate-holder Alypios —
an agency relationship apparently resting on personal elite trust rather than formal, enforceable
lease or mandate contracts: agency conducted, in effect, "in the shadow of the law."

## Path Dependence: Three Cases

Frier and Kehoe develop **path dependence** as a recurring theme:

1. **The slave-based villa economy** of late Republican Italy (intensive wine and olive production
   for Rome and Gaul) generated uneven growth benefiting a small landowning elite at the expense of
   free farmers' earning capacity and aggregate demand; heavy elite capital investment in slaves
   created an incentive to preserve the system even as its profitability conditions changed —
   paralleling the American antebellum South (Wright 1978) — until decentralization began in the
   first–second centuries CE.
2. **Roman imperial-estate leasing policy** (in Africa, Asia Minor, Syria, and Egypt — e.g. the
   *lex Manciana*, the *lex Hadriana*) protected small-scale tenant cultivators' secure tenure,
   providing stable state revenue and a political counterweight against senatorial and equestrian
   landowning elites, but locked land into small-scale use and discouraged alternative, possibly
   more productive, private investment, since private landowners had to match the terms offered on
   imperial land.
3. **Ptolemaic and Roman Egypt's institutional inheritance** — crown and temple "royal land" with
   secure smallholder tenure, cleruchic land, and Fayyum reclamation under Ptolemy II — delayed the
   emergence of a large-estate landowning elite in Egypt until the third century CE, unlike other
   Roman provinces; first-century-CE imperial *ousiai* estates functioned as rent-extraction rights
   rather than productive investment vehicles.

## Slavery as an Economic Institution

Citing Fenoaltea (1984), Domar (1970), and Eggertsson (1990), the chapter presents (as a
theoretical, not empirically settled, hypothesis) the idea that "pain incentives" give slave labor a
comparative advantage in effort- and land-intensive, low-technology sectors — agriculture, mining,
quarrying, public works — while care- and capital-intensive sectors (viticulture, domestic service)
favor reward-based incentives, including the prospect of manumission.

## Position in the Primitivism-Modernism Debate

Frier and Kehoe explicitly position their NIE reading as a modernist-leaning conclusion reached by
different means than the older Finley/Polanyi framework: "many curious economic institutions of the
Greek and Roman world were... the consequence of individuals struggling rationally... to maximize
their personal gain," so that "the 'invisible hand' of Adam Smith should no longer be counted as
generally missing from the ancient economy. Only, the hand was made flesh." See
[[ancient-economy-primitivism-vs-modernism]] for the fuller controversy.

## Significance

The chapter supplies the legal-institutional layer beneath the volume's account of
[[economic-efflorescence|Greco-Roman economic growth]]: contract law, guardianship, agency, and
tenancy rules are treated not as neutral background but as active determinants of who could invest,
who bore risk, and which sectors could scale — directly informing [[new-institutional-economics]]'s
application to the ancient world.
