# Arthashastra (Kautalya)

## Definition and Scope

The **Arthashastra** is one of the most important documents of ancient India concerned with diplomacy, political economy, statecraft, and secular law. Attributed to **Kautalya** (also known as **Chanakya**), the chief minister of [[chandragupta-maurya|Chandragupta Maurya]], it is a guidebook for monarchs and a rulebook for citizens. As Avari summarizes: 'While it is not a text of political philosophy, it deals with the issues of political craftsmanship in great detail. It is also concerned with civil and political institutions and the ways the ruler can operate them. Above all, it is a primer of secular law; after reading the precepts of the Arthashastra, no one can claim that ancient India was a lawless place.'

## Discovery and Transmission

The Arthashastra was lost to history for nearly two millennia. In 1904 an anonymous pandit from the Tanjore district delivered a palm-leaf manuscript of the ancient text to the Mysore Government Oriental Library. The librarian, **Dr. Shamasastry**, translated it for publication in the *Indian Antiquary* in 1905, and then — with encouragement from the Maharaja of Mysore — published the full text as **Volume 37 of the Bibliotheca Sanskrita of Mysore in 1909**. This discovery rescued the fame of Chandragupta's adviser Kautalya from obscurity.

## Structure and Contents

Dr. Shamasastry's translation of the Sanskrit text is laid out in **fifteen books**, each with a number of chapters. The English text contains approximately **a quarter of a million words**. The index lists **430 different items and issues** spanning a wide spectrum of subjects, and **336 different offences** for which fines are to be levied. Despite treating almost entirely material and worldly issues, the philosophical premise of the work is entirely Vedic in outlook, with the social hierarchy and dominance of brahmans and kshatriyas taken for granted.

Key themes include:
- **Diplomacy and inter-state relations**: only two forms of policy exist for a king — war or peace — operable in six different forms: 'agreement with pledges is called peace; offensive operation is war; indifference is neutrality; making preparations is marching; seeking the protection of another is alliance; and making peace with one and waging war with another is termed a double policy.'
- **Intelligence and espionage**: the collection of intelligence 'was particularly vital. It was the specific task of officially employed spies, informants and secret service agents, and its diligent use was of the utmost importance to the ruler.'
- **Duties of the king**: the king is advised to avoid 'betaking to others' women, appropriating others' wealth and injuring others; long sleep, fickleness, falsehood, gaudy dress, associates of low character and unrighteous actions are all condemned.'
- **Administration and law**: a 'quite harsh and unforgiving environment of rules and regulations is to be maintained by an extremely efficient and organised bureaucracy, whose officers wield great authority over every aspect of the lives and occupations of the people.'
- **Land, property, and economy**: extensive coverage of land purchase, mortgage, taxation, crown lands, and the rights of landlords, sharecroppers, and cultivators.
- **City planning**: describes a grid-plan ideal city with four gates named for Brahma, Indra, Senapati, and Yama, divided into sixteen blocks by caste occupation.

## The Mandala Theory and Seven Elements of the State

The Arthashastra's foreign-policy framework centers on the **mandala (circle of kings)**, the theory of a conquering king (**vijigishu**) who must understand his neighbours as either natural allies or enemies determined by their position in the circle. Allied to this is the **saptanga** (seven elements or limbs of sovereignty) — the theoretical constituents of a state's power — which together form the prescriptive ideal-type the text uses to frame statecraft.

## Relationship to Machiavelli

'What is generally termed Machiavellian in the context of historic rivalries of dynasties and ruling elites in European history was very much grounded in Kautalya's thinking **eighteen centuries before Machiavelli himself**.' The Arthashastra's frank embrace of espionage, duplicity, and realpolitik in inter-state relations makes the comparison a scholarly commonplace, though Avari notes the Arthashastra also contains much emphasis on the king's personal virtue and rule of law — elements less emphasized in the Machiavellian tradition.

## Dating Controversy

The dating of the Arthashastra is one of the most contested issues in ancient Indian scholarship. Several scholars believe the present text is from a later period and may have been written by more than one person: 'in ancient India there was quite often multiple authorship of the texts; also, the texts were refined and embellished long after the core sections had been written by the original author.' Notably, the **name of Chandragupta Maurya is not once mentioned** in the text — 'which, of course, leads to an understandable uncertainty among historians as to whether the Arthashastra describes the Mauryan, the pre-Mauryan or the post-Mauryan society.' The text's own opening sentence acknowledges it as 'a compendium of almost all the Arthashastras, which, in view of acquisition and maintenance of the earth, have been composed by ancient teachers' — suggesting Kautalya himself claimed only to synthesize prior traditions, not to be an entirely original author. Yet every chapter ends with the phrase 'Thus ends Chapter X from Book Y of the Arthashastra of Kautalya,' affirming authorial identity.

When the overall message of the Arthashastra is examined, it 'greatly confirms the picture of the early Mauryan world and society that is corroborated from other Indian and foreign sources,' lending weight to its use as a source for the Mauryan period even if absolute dating remains unresolved.

## Significance as a Source

Complemented by the Megasthenes' **Indica** (fragments surviving in Strabo, Arrian, and Diodorus Siculus), the Arthashastra provides the fullest picture of Mauryan governance. Together, 'our knowledge of the world of Chandragupta Maurya becomes more complete. It was indeed a highly ordered and well-regulated world.' For later periods, particularly the Gupta era, the Arthashastra is used as a **prescriptive ideal-type** — the blueprint the Mauryan state aspired to — to be triangulated against the Ashokan edicts and the archaeological record.

## Historiography

The Arthashastra's rediscovery in 1904 transformed modern understanding of ancient Indian political thought and the Mauryan state. Shamasastry's 1909 edition and 1923 translation remain the standard scholarly text. The debate between those who accept Kautalyan authorship and those who prefer a later (post-Mauryan) date of composition has never been resolved to consensus, but the text's corroboration of Mauryan social and institutional patterns from other sources makes it indispensable regardless of precise dating. Its Vedic philosophical premises (the hierarchy of brahman and kshatriya; hostility to heterodox sects including Buddhists and Ajivakas) distinguish it sharply from the Ashokan program that followed — the two texts together define the **two poles of Mauryan political thought**: danda-based realpolitik and dhamma-based ethical sovereignty.
