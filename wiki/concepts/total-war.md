# Total War

**Total war** is warfare in which a belligerent mobilizes the **whole of its society,
economy, and culture** for the war effort, pursues **unlimited aims**, and erodes the
distinction between **combatant and civilian** — so that entire populations become both
participants in and targets of the conflict. The concept is central to the modern
understanding of the **[[first-world-war-1914|First]]** and **[[second-world-war-1939|Second]]
World Wars**, and is the organizing frame of [[winter-cambridge-history-first-world-war-v1|Jay
Winter's *Cambridge History of the First World War*]].

## The elements of total war

- **Total mobilization of society and economy.** War production is directed by the state;
  whole workforces (including women and, in the colonial empires, conscripted and recruited
  labour) are mobilized; the home front becomes a theatre of the war. (See
  [[winter-cambridge-history-first-world-war-v2|"The State"]] on war economies.) Strachan Range 10 details the 1914 trigger: shell crises and gold-standard fragmentation forced central allocation (Germany's KRA 13 Aug) and state credit dominance, converting pre-war liberal economies into directed war economies far faster than anticipated. "The process of German mobilization had worked a more fundamental revolution in the mechanism of German credit than was realized at the time."
- **Unlimited aims.** As Winter's contributors note of 1917, "the logic of total war
  dictated that societies had no choice but to fight on" — the war became "about survival,
  not ideals," foreclosing compromise peace.
- **The erosion of the soldier/civilian distinction.** Blockade, submarine warfare, aerial
  bombing of cities, occupation, and forced labour made civilians direct targets — the
  "underlying trend towards a total war that abolished the distinction between soldier and
  civilian," which would "re-emerge, transformed," in the still greater violence of the
  Second World War. In the extreme, total war's logic underwrote mass atrocity and
  **genocide** (Winter reads the **Armenian genocide** as occurring "under the cover of"
  total war).
- **A global event.** Total war drew in the whole world — colonial soldiers and labourers
  (the Chinese Labour Corps; Indian, African, and Vietnamese contingents), distant economies,
  and neutral states — making the First World War, in Winter's phrase, "truly a world event."

## A contested concept

Historians dispute how "total" these wars really were. Winter's own contributors caution that
the **battlefield** rarely matched the totalizing image: on the Somme and at Verdun only a
handful of divisions were typically engaged on a given day, and the dominant reality was
**attrition** rather than continuous all-out combat. The aims, too, were not always
unlimited, and mobilization was never complete. "Total war" is thus best treated as an
**ideal-type and a tendency** — a direction in which industrial-age warfare moved — rather
than a literal description, and one applied retrospectively (the term was popularized by
Ludendorff in 1935).

**The Whitman genealogy.** [[whitman-verdict-of-battle-2012|Whitman (2012)]]
treats "total war" or "hard war" as the perennial *alternative* to contained
pitched battle — chevauchée, razzia, systematic pillage, siege, guerrilla,
Sherman's March, the bombing raid — which the [[jus-victoriae]] system held at
bay until the 1860s–70s ([[death-of-pitched-battle]]). He finds a
proto-justification for totality embedded in Grotius himself (a just cause
demands *all* one's forces), and traces the culmination through Clausewitz's
*Vernichtungsschlacht* to unconditional-surrender doctrine and twentieth-century
ideological victories that claimed the right to remake defeated societies —
against David Bell's *The First Total War*, he dates the decisive break to the
1860s, not the Revolutionary/Napoleonic era.

## Significance

The concept captures what made the World Wars categorically different from earlier
great-power conflicts: the fusion of mass industrial economy, the nation-state, and modern
ideology into wars that consumed entire societies. It links the military history of the wars
to their social, economic, and cultural history (the [[world-wars-era|World Wars Era]] as a
whole), and frames the trajectory from 1914 toward the even more total violence of 1939–45.

## Goebbels's "total war" — Evans, *The Third Reich at War* (2008)

Goebbels's Sportpalast speech of 18 February 1943 ("Do you want total war? . . . if necessary, more total and more radical than we can even imagine it today?"), delivered to a hand-picked audience of 14,000 with 200+ orchestrated interruptions, was described by contemporaries as "a feat of mass hypnosis" — and, Evans argues, was largely symbolic: Hitler's rival "Committee of Three" (Bormann, Lammers, Keitel) went moribund bickering over trivia like horse-racing bans, and concrete measures (9,000 Brandenburg business closures, January 1943) were abandoned within months. SD reports show ordinary Germans read the theatre as theatre ("groups ordered there . . . shouting yes to everything"). Evans also rebuts the "Blitzkrieg-as-cheap-war" legend: consumption had fallen from 71% to 59% of national income by 1938, and by 1944 Germany devoted 75% of GDP to war (USSR 60%, Britain 55%) — mobilization was near its ceiling long before the slogan.
