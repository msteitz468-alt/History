# Rong-Di-Yi-Ti (Chinese Ethnonyms for Northern Peoples)

## Definition and Scope

Early Chinese texts deploy a large and shifting vocabulary to designate non-Chinese peoples on the periphery of the Zhou political world. The four principal terms are **Yi** (夷), **Di** (狄), **Rong/Jung** (戎), and **Ti** (traditionally rendered interchangeably with Di in older Wade-Giles romanization), with **Man** (蠻) and **Hu** (胡) as additional important terms. These are **emic** designations — used by ancient Chinese writers — that modern scholarship routinely (and misleadingly) renders as "barbarians." Understanding these terms requires distinguishing their nominal geographic assignments from their actual political function and recognizing that they designate categories of **political relationship** more than fixed ethnic groups.

## Geographic Assignments (Nominal)

The concentric-zone cosmology of early Chinese texts assigned these peoples to directional slots:

| Term | Direction | Zone type | Source |
|------|-----------|-----------|--------|
| Man (蠻) | South | *yao* (allied, inner-foreign) | *Yü Kung*, *Chou li* |
| Yi (夷) | East | *yao* (allied, inner-foreign) | *Yü Kung* |
| Jung/Rong (戎) | West | *fan* (outer, non-assimilated) | *Chou li*, *Kuo yü* |
| Ti/Di (狄) | North | *fan* (outer, non-assimilated) | *Kuo yü*, *Tso-chuan* |

This directional assignment was idealized, not empirical. In the historical texts, Jung peoples appear in northern and northwestern locations; Ti groups are found throughout the north but also in areas that do not fit the directional scheme neatly.

## The Two-Tier Structure: Yao vs. Fan

A structural distinction of critical importance runs through these classifications. Early texts consistently differentiated **two tiers** of foreign peoples:

- **Yao peoples** (*Man-Yi* binome): "allied" or potentially assimilable foreigners, notionally living within the Chou territorial sphere or capable of being absorbed into it
- **Fan peoples** (*Jung-Ti* binome): "outer," independent, and potentially hostile foreigners, placed outside the nine provinces (*chiu chou*) and the authority of the Chou House

The *Chou li* chapter "Ta Hsing-jen" places the *fan* states outside the "nine provinces" (*chiu chou chih wai*), whereas the *yao* states are retained within the Chou territorial sphere. This distinction maps onto a later dyadic classification into *shu* ("tamed, cooked") and *sheng* ("raw, fierce") foreigners — a recurring structure in Chinese approaches to peripheral peoples. The peoples to the north and west (Jung and Ti) were regarded as more resistant to the civilizing influence of the center and therefore more alien than the southern and eastern peoples.

## The Term "Jung/Rong" (戎)

The term *jung* presents particular interpretive difficulty. Its general meaning relates to "martial," "military," "war," and "weapons." In the *Tso-chuan*, *jung* appears both as a people-name and as a common noun meaning "war-chariot" (*yü jung* = "to drive a war chariot"; *jung ch'e* = "war chariot"). Edward Shaughnessy has argued that in the Western Chou period, the term designated "enemy belligerents" rather than a specific ethnic group. Di Cosmo notes that "the 'foreignness' of the Jung, however, does not seem to be in question" despite this ambiguity.

Multiple sub-groups called "Jung" appear in the sources: Ch'üan Jung (defeated by King Mu c. 944 BCE), Hsü Jung, Western Jung, Yin Jung, Chiang Jung, Mountain Jung (*Shan Jung* = Wu-chung Jung), Yi-lo Jung, Mao Jung — suggesting the term covered a range of distinct groups, possibly not even linguistically or culturally related. "The term 'Jung' appears to indicate more than a single people and is regarded by some scholars not as an ethnonym but as a generic word for 'bellicose' or 'warlike'" (Di Cosmo).

## The Term "Ti/Di" (狄)

Whether "Ti" was a generic word for "northern foreigners," a specific ethnonym, or a political unit "cannot be determined" (Di Cosmo). The Ti were divided in the Spring and Autumn period into at least two major groupings:

- **Ch'ih (Red) Ti** — the more aggressive grouping; eventually extinguished by Chin in campaigns of the late sixth century BCE (594–588 BCE)
- **Pai (White) Ti** — traditionally less hostile; concluded a peace treaty with Chin in 601 BCE and were eventually incorporated into Chou states; gave rise to the state of **Chung-shan** (Hsien-yü), which survived until 295 BCE

Jaroslav Prušek attributed the appearance of the Ti and other peoples in northern China to transcontinental migrations triggered by the evolution of mounted nomadic cultures in Eurasia — a hypothesis consistent with the archaeological evidence for the [[northern-zone]] cultural complex.

## The Binomes and Their Political Function

Paired compounds (*binomes*) using these terms were extremely common:

- **Yi-Ti** — a generic stigma for "foreignness" or un-Chinese behavior; used in the *Ku-liang* and *Kung-yang* commentaries as the most emphatic designation for peoples or states outside the norms of Chou civilization. Critically, this label was applied to the Chou states **Chin**, **Ch'u**, and **Wu** when their behavior violated accepted norms, demonstrating that Yi-Ti was a **political judgment**, not an ethnic one.
- **Man-Yi** — associated with the inner-foreign (assimilable) tier; a binome for allied or absorbed foreigners
- **Jung-Ti** — associated with the outer-foreign (hostile) tier; a binome for peoples outside Chou political control. The *Chan kuo ts'e* applies "Jung-Ti" even to the powerful Chou state of **Ch'in**: "Ch'in shares the same customs as the Jung and Ti; it has the heart of a tiger or a wolf; it is greedy and cruel, and cannot be trusted when it comes to making a profit." This application of the frontier-people stigma to a Chou state confirms that "accusing a state or people of immorality or lack of virtue or even of inhumanity was to make a political denunciation of unscrupulous behavior that could be applied to an enemy regardless of 'ethnic' or 'cultural' differences" (Di Cosmo).
- **Ssu yi** ("four Yi") — a generic term for the sum total of peripheral peoples in all four directions; used in diplomatic protocol statements

## The Problem of "Barbarian" as Translation

The standard English translation "barbarian" for any of these terms is systematically misleading. There is no single Chinese analog to the Greek *barbaros*. The Chinese vocabulary distinguishes at minimum: (1) inner vs. outer foreigners (*yao* vs. *fan*); (2) assimilable vs. resistant peoples (Man-Yi vs. Jung-Ti); (3) peoples that can be governed by virtue vs. peoples that must be managed by force. "The distinction is hopelessly obscured when the blanket term 'barbarian' is used indiscriminately" (Di Cosmo). Different terms carried different political implications for how a Chou state should treat the people so designated.

## Political Rather Than Cultural Categories

Di Cosmo's core argument about these terms is that they functioned primarily as **political and military categories**, not as reflections of fixed cultural or ethnic boundaries. The evidence:

1. The same peoples or states could be labeled using the inner-foreign or outer-foreign binomes depending on their current political behavior, not on stable ethnic characteristics.
2. Chou states routinely allied with, incorporated, and even married into the Jung and Ti without this being seen as cultural contamination — the Chin ducal house had multiple Jung wives and half-Jung offspring who governed frontier territories.
3. The label Yi-Ti could be applied to Chou states (Chin, Ch'u, Wu) whose ethnic Chineseness was not in question.
4. The ethnic terms became largely emptied of specific content over time: "the two components had lost any residual ethnic significance and were simply meant to represent the general notion of 'foreignness' that was used to stigmatize a behavior not consonant with commonly accepted rules."

This political reading does not deny that real cultural differences existed. The Jung chief's speech of 559 BCE explicitly acknowledges difference in food, drink, clothing, and language between his people and the Hua. The point is that cultural difference did not map onto political behavior in any consistent way, and the ethnonyms tracked the latter more than the former.

## Historiography

The scholarly debate over these terms divides into two camps. The **culturalist** position (represented by scholars including Cho-yun Hsu in the *Cambridge History of Ancient China*) holds that the Spring and Autumn texts record the emergence of a genuine Hua-Hsia cultural consciousness, and the ethnonyms reflect a real, widening awareness of the civilizational gap between Chinese and non-Chinese. The **political-pragmatist** position (Di Cosmo, following aspects of earlier work by Creel and others) holds that the ethnonyms were instruments of political discourse that reflected military and strategic realities more than cultural self-awareness.

The *Ku-liang* and *Kung-yang* commentaries, the texts most often cited for the culturalist position, reflect ethical stances developed considerably later than the events they discuss — a consideration that undermines their value as evidence for Spring and Autumn period consciousness.
