# The Western Study of Sufism: Orientalism and Historiography

The historiography of [[sufism|Sufism]] in Western scholarship is itself a significant intellectual history,
shaped by the limitations of sources available to early researchers, the ideological assumptions of
European orientalism, and the gradual development of more philologically rigorous methods in the
twentieth century. Annemarie Schimmel's [[schimmel-mystical-dimensions-of-islam-1975|*Mystical
Dimensions of Islam*]] (1975) provides the most thorough survey of this tradition.

## Definition and Scope

The Western historiography of Sufism comprises scholarly attempts to explain the origin, nature,
and development of Islamic mysticism from outside the tradition itself. It is distinct from the
self-understanding of the Sufis, who traced their lineage to the Prophet Muhammad, and from
classical Islamic biographical and hagiographical literature (*tabaqāt*, *tadhkira*). Western
scholarship has been largely oriented toward the questions of *origin* (where did Sufism come from?)
and *authenticity* (is it native to Islam or a foreign import?).

## Early European Contact and the Formative Period of Western Study

Europe's first serious contact with Sufi ideas can be traced to the thirteenth century. The figure
of [[rabia-al-adawiyya|Rābiʿa al-ʿAdawiyya]], the woman saint of Basra, entered European literature
through Joinville, chancellor of Louis IX, and her story was used in a seventeenth-century French
treatise on pure love (Henri Bremond's circle). The Catalonian mystic Ramon Lull (d. 1316) shows
"remarkable influence of Sufi literature" (Schimmel).

Systematic scholarly treatment of Sufi texts became possible only after European orientalists gained
access to manuscripts in the nineteenth century. The first comprehensive Western book on Sufism was
Friedrich August Deofidus Tholuck's *Ssufismus sive theosophia persarum pantheistica* (Berlin,
1821), followed by his anthology *Blüthensammlung aus der Morgenländischen Mystik* (1825).
Remarkably, Tholuck — himself a Protestant theologian — recognized that "the Sufi doctrine was both
generated and must be illustrated out of Muhammad's own mysticism," a conclusion far in advance of
his contemporaries.

## Theories of Origin and Scholarly Debate

The central historiographical controversy in early Western Sufism scholarship was the question of
origin. Because most scholars assumed Islam itself to be a religion of dry legalism incapable of
generating mysticism, they sought Sufism's sources elsewhere. Several distinct theories emerged:

**Iranian/Aryan origin**: E. H. Palmer (*Oriental Mysticism*, 1867) held Sufism to be "the
development of the Primaeval religion of the Aryan race" — a view echoed in some German writing of
the Nazi period. Iranian elements have been argued more seriously by Henri Corbin and Seyyed Hossein
Nasr.

**Neoplatonic origin**: Many British scholars, including Reynold A. Nicholson in his foundational
introduction to Rūmī's poetry (1898), stressed Neoplatonic influence. The "Theology of Aristotle"
(actually Porphyry's commentary on Plotinus's Enneads) was translated into Arabic as early as 840,
putting Neoplatonism "in the air" (Nicholson). Nicholson himself, however, concluded that the early
ascetic movement "is a native product of Islam itself."

**Christian/monastic origin**: Scholars including Adalbert Merx, Arend Jan Wensinck, and Margaret
Smith emphasized the role of Syrian Christian ascetics and monks. The Swedish bishop Tor Andrae
produced what Schimmel considers the best studies in this field, also writing the classical study
of veneration of the Prophet in mystical Islam.

**Indian origin**: Alfred von Kremer (1868) and Reinhart P. Dozy (1869) argued for Hindu/Vedantic
influences on early Sufism; Max Horten wrote numerous articles on this thesis but could not produce
"stringent proof" for the early period. The debate was renewed by R. C. Zaehner's work on
[[bayazid-bistami|Bāyezīd Bisṭāmī]] and Indian *advaita* philosophy.

**Turkestani origin**: Richard Hartmann showed the importance of Central Asian influences; Ignaz
Goldziher noted parallels between Islamic mystical tales and Buddhist stories.

**Chinese/Taoist parallels**: The possibility of Taoist influence on Sufism was first raised by
Omar Farrukh; more recently Toshihiko Izutsu has drawn structural parallels between Taoism and
[[ibn-arabi|Ibn ʿArabī]]'s system.

## The Turn to Philology

The mature phase of Western Sufism scholarship is defined by close textual work on manuscripts and
primary sources. Three scholarly projects define this transition:

**Louis Massignon** devoted his scholarly life to [[al-hallaj|al-Ḥallāj]], producing the massive
study *La Passion d'al-Ḥusayn ibn Manṣūr al-Ḥallāj* as well as pioneering analysis of the
"mystical lexicon" — the technical vocabulary developed by the earliest Sufis.

**Reynold A. Nicholson** produced the still "unrivaled" critical editions and translations of
Rūmī's *Mathnawī* and lyrical poetry, and his *The Mystics of Islam* (1914) remains a standard
introduction.

**Hellmut Ritter**'s *Das Meer der Seele* on ʿAṭṭār is described by Schimmel as "the result of an
ideal combination of strict philology combined with aesthetic and religious understanding."

**Paul Nwyia** traced the "adventures of the mystics" in the Arabic language, demonstrating that
"thanks to the mystics, in the Arabic language, an authentic language, that of experience, was born"
— and that some definitions previously attributed to ninth-century masters can be dated much earlier.

**Fritz Meier** produced foundational studies on Sufi initiation, mystical states, and the Persian
orders; Schimmel describes his work as "penetrating."

**Henry Corbin** analyzed the structural systems underlying Ibn ʿArabī's thought and argued for the
importance of specifically Iranian (Ishrāqī) traditions within Sufism.

## Methodological Approaches

Western scholarship has approached Sufism through several distinct methods:

- **History of ideas** (vertical cut): tracing the development of doctrines over time
- **Phenomenological cross-section**: analyzing mystical states, methods, and symbolism across the
  tradition
- **Biographical/hagiographical analysis**: studying individual mystics
- **Lexical/linguistic analysis**: examining how Sufi technical vocabulary evolved (Massignon, Nwyia)
- **Structural analysis** (Corbin on Ibn ʿArabī): examining the philosophical architecture beneath
  mystical expression
- **Sociological analysis**: studying the formation and role of the Sufi orders (Meier)

## Key Methodological Cautions

Schimmel warns against several systematic distortions in the field:

1. **Taking imagery at face value**: Much Sufi poetry uses the imagery of wine, intoxication, and
   human love to describe divine states. Early Western readers (following Sir William Jones's
   influence on Ḥāfiẓ interpretation) often read this literally, producing "rather absurd views in
   wild confusion."
2. **The paradox problem**: Many sayings of Sufi masters that appear as theological or philosophical
   statements were "intended to be suggestive wordplay" or "koʾan" — paradoxes meant to shock the
   hearer into non-logical understanding. Taking them as systematic doctrine distorts their intent.
3. **The language problem**: Arabic and Persian Sufi style exploits the mathematical possibilities
   of the triliteral root system, producing wordplay and polysemy that "is lost in translation."
4. **Source availability**: Most sources available to nineteenth-century scholars were of "rather
   late origin" and rarely contained reliable information about the earliest stages of the movement.

## Contested Status

The question of whether Sufism is a "native product of Islam" or substantially shaped by external
influences remains actively contested. The consensus view since Nicholson has been that early
Sufism is organically Islamic, while secondary Neoplatonic, Christian ascetic, and (in some later
figures and regions) Iranian and Indian elements enriched it without displacing its Quranic and
Prophetic foundations. No consensus exists on the degree of Indian influence on specific figures
such as Bāyezīd Bisṭāmī.
