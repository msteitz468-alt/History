# Tax-Based vs Land-Based States

Wickham’s load-bearing comparative distinction for political and economic structure after Rome: a polity funds itself either by **taxing** land and people (paying soldiers and officials from central resources) or by **owning** land and extracting rent, granting land for service. Tax-raising states are structurally more solid; land-based ones tend toward fragmentation of powers. The distinction organises [[wickham-framing-the-early-middle-ages-2005|Framing the Early Middle Ages]] (esp. Ch. 3) and underpins the broader [[politics-of-land]] framework.

## Definition

### Ideal types

- **Tax-based (fiscal) state**: surplus extracted as assessed tax from nearly everyone; army and bureaucracy paid from public resources; rulers hold an independent power base separate from aristocratic goodwill and can dismiss subordinates cheaply by stopping salaries; capacity to intervene across territory remains high.
- **Land-based polity**: surplus extracted as rent from estates the ruler directly controls; the army and principal officials are themselves based on the land; political reward is dominated by the “politics of land”—cessions of land and rents for loyalty; public office and private landholding blur; local autonomy of aristocrats is the structural default.
- **Pre-state (northern) systems**: England, Wales, Ireland, Denmark and related societies where royal centrality long remained ad hoc and personal; state-building here tracks the creation of a landowning aristocracy rather than residual fiscal inheritance ([[peasant-mode-of-production]]).

Wickham defines an ideal-type “state” by five parameters (adapted from Claessen and Runciman): centralized legitimate enforceable authority (justice and army); specialized governmental roles and an official hierarchy outlasting office-holders; a concept of public power separable from the ruled and from individual rulers; independent and stable resources for rulers; and class-based surplus extraction and stratification. On this definition he calls the major Romano-Germanic kingdoms “states” without apology—even though they were land-based and weaker than eastern fiscal empires.

### Tax vs tribute

He separates **tribute** (lump sums from communities or individuals, determined by armed force, often arbitrary or irregular) from **tax** (assessments of relative wealth in land or movables). Accurate land tax requires documentation of amounts due and paid and up-to-date ownership data; without it the system becomes legalized plunder or ad hoc tribute. Tribute is unlikely to be as remunerative as exactly assessed tax because only the latter pinpoints who has the resources to pay.

### Hybrids and revisions

Tax and land are ideal types that historically intermix: Roman emperors were large landowners; most land-based systems extract some customs tolls and judicial fines and pay some mercenaries; gift-exchange of treasure remains central to court ritual. Twenty years after the 1984–5 articles, *Framing* keeps the opposition but revises three points:

1. After Berktay and Haldon, tax- and land-based systems are **sub-types of the same mode of production** (agrarian surplus forcibly extracted from the peasantry), not separate “ancient/tributary” vs “feudal” modes.
2. Western change took longer than a pure fifth-century fiscal collapse—Germanic armies mostly landed, but tax often continued for a time.
3. Army funding was rarely pure salary or pure land; the western fifth–sixth-century shift was a **balance change** from basically paid (with some land) to basically landed (with campaign supplies and royal largesse). Only the Umayyad and Abbasid states in the book rely on a fully salaried army.

See also [[politics-of-land]] and [[post-roman-transformation-of-the-west]].

## Application: regional patterning 400–800

### The fundamental post-Roman division

After imperial unity broke up, the tax/land resource division essentially separated the eastern Mediterranean (tax-based, more capacity to intervene) from most of the Latin West. Fiscal structures were nearly universally simpler than before: they disappeared in Romano-Germanic kingdoms as armies settled on land; they persisted but regionalized in Byzantium and the Umayyad caliphate. Western effective tax systems restart only from eleventh-century England and the late twelfth century elsewhere.

### Western fiscal breakdown — drivers

Wickham does not treat cultural “alienness” of Germanic elites as the driver. Three more important elements:

1. **War-induced confusion** disrupting institutions regionally.
2. **Reduced spending needs** once armies were landed, civil administration shrank to provincial scale, and Rome’s food supply left the agenda outside Italy.
3. **Social status of ethnically defined armies** as new aristocracies with wider ambitions than Roman armies (contrast caliph ʿUmar I’s decision that Arab armies remain in barrack towns on pay, *ʿaṭāʾ*, without acquiring land).

Fiscal collapse **postdated** the foundation of the barbarian kingdoms: Salvian’s 440s rhetoric and Sidonius’s letters imply continuing tax; Ostrogothic Italy systematically documents Roman fiscal procedures. The hyper-Romanist school (Durliat, Magnou-Nortier) that political structures had not changed by c.800 is rejected in detail.

### Francia

Sixth-century Merovingians taxed the Roman population (Franks remained immune); by the seventh century any taxation was generally seen as abuse. Land-tax imagery largely vanishes from later-seventh-century immunity privileges; royal lands (plus tolls and judicial fines), not tax rights, became the main political currency. Tax decline did **not** track Merovingian political power: kings retained immense lands, confiscation, and a *thesaurus* for gift-based army cores. Chlotar II (renounced *census novus* 614) and Dagobert mark the “catastrophe-flip” in which tax became one resource among many, not the essential one. Merovingians were not destroyed by the centrifugal politics of land; the Carolingians would be, after 720–830 unity and success faded.

- **Loire valley**: *inferenda*/*annona*/*tributum*/*agrarium* still appear into the early eighth century (Ardin 721; St-Denis Le Mans), increasingly standardized into tribute/rent rather than reassessed tax—so public exactions did not die at the early-seventh-century date much secondary literature assumes.
- **Northern heartland**: kings were strongest where taxation was weakest (Paris–Rhine)—evidence of how marginal tax was; northern weakness may track the end of the Rhine army and privatization of collection in the Rhine-supply catchment, not economic wasteland.
- **Decline path**: assessments more occasional and standardized; rates lessened; responsibility shifted city councillors → central officials → landowners; burden privatized into rents. Territorial tax-raising over non-tenants (*iqṭāʿ* / later *seigneurie banale* analogues) rarely occurred; main privatization was devolution of dues from a landowner’s **own** lands. Successful tax needs constant register-updating; its absence (including Charlemagne’s well-documented Francia) signals tax marginal or gone.

### Visigothic Spain and Lombard Italy

Visigothic military service became a public free-male obligation (plus unfree under Ervig), organized under dukes/counts or private *patroni*; private clienteles and land gifts made tax politically marginal while still funding palace and ceremonial. Ervig’s laws show tax sometimes converted into cessions of land to the fisc—a shift toward a land-based state—while huge royal patrimony kept kingship relevant until 711. Spanish historiography’s “proto-feudalism” debate tracks this translation of public obligations into personal dependence.

Lombard Italy lacked Francia’s top landowning levels; relative aristocratic poverty strengthened royal patronage and coherent kingship (office and *Königsnähe* major), even while the kingdom remained economically and socially fragmented between city territories. Capillary justice and law citation were Lombard strengths; after 774 the Franks borrowed from them.

### Comparative attachment protocols of land-based states

Three procedures guard land-based polities against disintegration: (1) public ceremonial in courts/capitals; (2) concentration of public wealth so players need royal largesse; (3) capillary intervention—usually justice and law if not tax. Visigoths stressed the first, Merovingians the second, Lombards the third; Carolingians all three. Tax-based emperors/caliphs used all three for success but did not need them the same way—their structures had an existence independent of aristocratic interests.

### Eastern continuity and provincialization

- **Egypt**: the land tax and fiscal system remained the main structure uniting the regional economy; after 640 almost no tax left Egypt (*muhājirūn*, Alexandria navy spent locally), so fiscal and commercial systems still fuelled each other region-wide—the state created the demand private owners could not, in 400 and 800 alike.
- **Umayyad Levant**: tax stayed in provinces (five Syrian provinces vs Egypt’s one); caliphal wealth made a few very rich without underpinning long-distance commercial movement; Abbasid recentralization on Baghdad restored regional-level economy.
- **Byzantium**: after 650, themes with local taxation in kind (money taxes near the Marmara) decentralized army supply, yet thematic hierarchies stayed subordinate to Constantinople; survival of medium-distance Aegean traffic was ultimately the work of the state.
- **Patronage**: late Roman *patrocinium* against tax was a spin-off of the fiscal state, not its substitute; Syria and Egypt, best-documented patronage zones, were where tax survived best long-run. Arab Egypt, cutting indigenous mediators, faced foot-dragging and revolt late Rome rarely matched.

### Northern state-building

South of the Channel a Roman-style aristocracy largely survived; north of Channel/Rhine it had to be created. Four variables: external political influence; tightness of king–aristocrat links; growth of economic differentiation; establishment of aristocratic landed power. Wide-ranging political power can begin without full estate-aristocracies (Ine, Godofrid), but stable coercive states require at least the beginning of feudal landowning dominance (Offa/Cenwulf Mercia). Tribute alone was never enough; markets diagnose private landed wealth rather than independently drive state-building (contra Hodges on Charlemagne).

### Francia 800 caveat

Tax-based states were generally richer and more durable, with less decentralization risk—even Byzantium at its weakest (c.650–750) was more coherent than the best land-based states (e.g. Lombard Italy). The caveat: in 800 Charlemagne’s Francia was almost certainly more militarily powerful than Irene’s Byzantium, and Frankish landed resources may briefly have exceeded Byzantine fiscal ones. Francia was unusual in the scale of landed wealth; by 900 Byzantium was rising while Francia fragmented. Northern Francia’s super-regional secular and ecclesiastical landowning almost matched Egypt’s fiscal infrastructure for articulating medium-distance exchange ([[aristocratic-demand]]).

### Re-establishing abandoned tax

Taxation once abandoned is hard to rebuild. Arab Spain began taxing after 711 but a full working system took two centuries and a civil war. Byzantine mid/late-eighth reorganization was easier because basic fiscal structures never fully broke down. Richer Francia never succeeded—no army-pay incentive; even Charles the Bald’s 860s Danegeld was not maintained. No Latin Western state managed regular assessed tax again until late-tenth-century England.

## Historiography

Positions this distinction against Dopsch (continuity) and Pirenne (Mediterranean commercial break) as a *structural* rather than ethnic or commercial explanation of post-Roman change. Rejects Durliat/Magnou-Nortier hyper-Romanist fiscal continuity; prefers land settlement over Goffart’s hospitalitas-as-tax-shares model while allowing hybrid ad hoc billeting; follows Haldon (not Hendy) on Byzantine thematic pay; treats patronage as stabilizer rather than late-Roman pathology. Complements and sometimes tensions with Sarris (fiscal/monetary maximalism) and NCMH I transformationist readings. Wickham’s own revisions (Berktay/Haldon on mode of production; slower western chronology; hybrid army funding) are load-bearing for using the model carefully.

## From Mitchell, Later Roman Empire (2015)

Source: [[mitchell-later-roman-empire-2015]].

- **Explicit engagement with Wickham.** Mitchell restates Wickham’s strong vs weak states: tax-financed imperial systems (Roman Empire; successor eastern empires of Byzantium and the Abbasids) vs western successor kingdoms (Franks, Visigoths, Lombards) whose rulers used land (direct domain or gifts to aristocrats) for political-military support; tax systems are impersonal/institutionalized, land systems rest on personal aristocratic loyalty.
- **Material mapping without the vocabulary (Ch 9–10).** Institutionalized tax (not conquest tribute) defines Roman subjecthood vs western kingdoms; Rome’s population rested on tax-extraction capacity and shrank when it was lost; Egyptian/Anatolian magnates capturing tax collection blur state tax and private rent; Vandal Africa kept the tax system while Justinianic re-occupation reimposed extractive taxation; western tax-base shrinkage as land was ceded drove post-Roman reorientation. Security failure is the mechanism that destroys tax infrastructure and forces land-based/local extraction—“as the infrastructure created by Roman administration and taxation was removed, cities shrunk in size and lost their significance.”
