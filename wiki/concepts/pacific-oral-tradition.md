## Definition and Scope

Pacific oral tradition encompasses the full range of Indigenous forms for producing, preserving, and transmitting historical knowledge in [[oceania]] without reliance on writing: creation accounts, genealogies, chants, voyaging and migration narratives, nature lore, and customary law. As Jocelyn Linnekin summarized, "A composite history told through indigenous genres would consist of oral accounts, related in many languages and taking widely varied forms. Some would explain the origins of gods, humans, plants and animals; others would recount great voyages or the rivalries and conquests of great chiefs. Chanted, spoken, or sung, the narratives might not relate events in chronological order."

Source: [[jones-matsuda-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v1-2022]], ch. 12 (Tuimaleali'ifano and D'Arcy).

## Historical and Historiographical Development

### Indigenous Practice and European Contact

The majority of Pacific Islanders' "millennia of history was recorded and conveyed orally," giving Pacific history a fundamentally multidisciplinary character: "much Pacific history has become multidisciplinary to incorporate non-literate sources such as oral traditions, linguistic patterns, and material remains." When Protestant missionaries arrived beginning in the late eighteenth century, Islanders recognized that "the secret to the visitor's power and wealth was a vehicle in which words could travel without sound or signals." The first known Pacific Islander to achieve literacy in his own language was Tahitian chief Pomare II, who "could write notes in a legible hand, tolerable good sense and pretty well spelt" by 1804. Within forty years of missionary arrival, Pacific literate cultures had been established — a transition that "took a millennium for western Europeans and North Americans."

### Academic Historiography (1960s–1990s)

Pacific Island historians began in the late 1960s to "explore ways of giving Islanders a greater voice in their historical narratives," rejecting the passive-victim framework and asserting that Islanders "were portrayed as actively responding to Europeans according to their own cultural rationale."

A methodological watershed was the import of Jan Vansina's framework from African Studies: his 1961 *Oral Tradition* and 1985 *Oral Tradition as History* "discussed the component elements of oral traditions and how historians could use these to unlock pasts only partially revealed in written sources." Vansina also "noted that oral traditions recorded in written texts also revealed themselves as social products with messages used to convey social values and signal cultural priorities."

P.M. Mercer (1979) concluded that Polynesian oral traditions "were the best preserved and went back 500 years, while Micronesian (with the possible exception of Kiribati whose traditions of Tabontebike-Karongoa extend to 1550 CE) and Melanesian traditions only went back two to three generations, or five at most with any accuracy."

By the 1980s, Greg Dening and Marshall Sahlins were combining "historians' interest in process with anthropology's interest in cultural structures influencing actions." Sahlins argued for "the need to 'explode the concept of history by the anthropological experience of culture'": "different cultural orders have their own modes of historical action, consciousness, and determination — their own historical practice."

I-Kiribati historian Kambati Uriam articulated what became a widely shared position: "oral traditions contain a close approximation of the past as lived, experienced and understood in the people's own words long before the coming of the Europeans to our shores."

### Decolonization and Institutional Development

Post-1945 decolonization generated "demand, and removed barriers to former colonial subject peoples writing their own history from their own perspectives." New Pacific universities — University of Hawaii (1907), USP (1968), University of Papua New Guinea (1965), National University of Samoa (1982), University of Fiji (2004), among others — produced a new generation of Islander historians. The 1981 Denoon and Lacey edited collection *Oral Tradition in Melanesia* sought to "broaden the focus of history to the vast majority of the world's population beyond political elites who rarely wrote their own history."

In Aotearoa New Zealand, "a commitment to rectify the confiscation of Māori land from the 1980s onwards led to a resurgence in investigation and publication of Māori traditions to demonstrate connection to place before the Waitangi Tribunal."

## Typology of Pacific Oral Traditions

Rawiri Taonui (Polynesian oral tradition specialist) identified six overlapping elements:

| Element | Character |
|---|---|
| Creation traditions | Origins of gods, humans, plants, animals; cosmology |
| Age of demigods | Shamanic cycles; superhuman ancestors (Maui foremost) |
| Nature lore | How the natural world works; relationships between species |
| Customary lore | Social structure, hierarchy, proper conduct |
| Migration/voyaging traditions | How peoples reached their islands; greatest mix of history and symbolism |
| Tribal histories | Claims to place; connections to locality through to the present |

### Creation and Shamanic Layers

Creation traditions "outline the origins and relationships between all things." Across Polynesia, origin narratives centre on "the birth of all life and objects from the marriage between the Skyfather and Earthmother" (Rangi and Papa in Māori tradition). Four great gods recur across Polynesia — Tangaroa (sea/heavens), Tane (forests/birds/insects), Rongo (peace/cultivation), Tu (war). Niel Gunson traces the earliest stratum to shamanic cycles "dating from the Paleolithic era": "The shamans developed the first oral literature in the form of chants, spells and myth cycles," featuring the trickster Maui and sky voyager Tafaki.

### Migration Traditions

Voyaging and migration traditions "contain the greatest mix of history and symbolism and are therefore the most problematic to interpret." Nineteenth-century ethnographers (Percy Smith, Fornander) read them too literally; later scholars (Sorrenson, Orbell) read them as largely symbolic. The process of *shadowing* — "each generation or new wave of colonizers places their particular story onto the corpus of traditions and tends to place pre-existing traditions back into the realm of early migrations and demigods" — complicates chronological interpretation but does not negate historical content.

Polynesian voyaging traditions show that landscape and seascape features of homelands were systematically reproduced in naming practices: "long or large bays carry similar names across Polynesia derived from *whanga* (bay) and *loa* (large or long)" — Fagaloa (Samoa), Whangaroa (New Zealand), Hanaloa/Pearl Harbor (Hawaii).

## Oral Tradition as Historical Evidence: Strengths and Limits

### Strengths

- **Depth**: Polynesian traditions demonstrably preserve information 500 years old; Kiribati traditions (Tabontebike-Karongoa lineage) extend to 1550 CE.
- **Coverage**: "For each of the major island groups, Hawai'i, Society Islands, Marquesas, Sāmoa, Tonga, New Zealand, and the Cook Islands, the amount of published and unpublished vernacular material compares favourably with the survivals from other primal cultures."
- **Precision in transmission**: H.E. Maude's twenty years in Kiribati led him "to realize that here was a society possessing an innate historical sense far transcending our own. In listening to, and still more when transcribing, these oral traditions one could not fail to be impressed by the anxiety of those present to ensure that the narrative was absolutely word perfect and in the exact form in which it had been transmitted through the generations."
- **Cultural specificity**: Oral traditions provide evidence on religion, kinship, social organization, and technology not recoverable by archaeology or linguistics alone.

### Limits

- **Layering**: "The past relates to the present in oral traditions by what is termed layering. Layering causes the past to appear as a layered composite of elements which originated at different times but exist together in the institutions of the present." Traditions are "living documents which alter over time in response to changing historical situations."
- **Shadowing**: Successive colonizing waves place earlier events back into mythological registers.
- **Genealogical manipulation**: In contexts where genealogies determine land ownership and title succession (Samoa, Fiji), traditions are subject to deliberate alteration. Colonial land commissions (Fiji's 1888–1965 Lands Commission producing Yavusa Tukutuku Raraba statements) froze "living documents" into legally binding but static forms.
- **Melanesian depth**: Melanesian traditions typically go back only two to five generations with accuracy — much shallower than Polynesian.
- **Interpretation without ethnographic competence**: As Gunson warns, traditions must be read using "textual criticism, poetic conventions and standard principles of historical analysis" and understood "in the context of social evolution and change and not treat them as static and unchanging forms."

## Case Study: Samoan Genealogical History

Samoan oral tradition illustrates both the depth and the institutional complications of Pacific oral history:

- Samoan and Tongan traditions record the **Tui Manu'a maritime polity** (c. 900–1200 CE), under which the Tui Manu'a, based in the Manu'a group, "ruled Sāmoa, Lau in Fiji, Rotuma, Niue, Tokelau, Rarotonga, and possibly further afield into parts of Kiribati and Vanuatu as part of a maritime empire."
- Pre-Tongan hegemony traditions acknowledge a strong Fijian presence in Savai'i; three major Savai'i districts trace their names to the Fijian chief Tui Fiti.
- The office of **Tafa'ifā** (holder of four sacred titles) was invented after Tongan departure ~1200 CE to replace the Tui Manu'a structure; oral history extends this lineage back through Queen Salamāsina to the war goddess Nafanua.
- Succession disputes: the Samoan Land and Titles Court operates without clearly disclosing "which version of genealogy and history is considered the most accurate," generating unresolvable multiplying disputes.
- In Fiji, colonial commissioners treating 1888–1965 Yavusa Tukutuku Raraba sworn statements as permanent truth, rather than as historically situated documents, has produced a situation where "the iTaukei of Fiji are forced either to continue to live a lie in the past or to abandon their lands for another structure."

## Regional Coverage

- **Polynesia**: best preserved; vast vernacular literature in Hawaiian, Māori, Samoan, Tongan, Tahitian, Marquesan, Cook Island, Tuamotuan, and Rarotongan, published extensively in the *Journal of the Polynesian Society* (from 1892) and Bishop Museum bulletins.
- **Micronesia**: moderately preserved; Kiribati exceptional; elsewhere depth limited.
- **Melanesia**: shallowest — typically two to five generations; exceptions in some Papua New Guinean groups.

## Historiography

The field's central tension is between treating oral traditions as literal historical records (nineteenth-century ethnographers) versus as purely symbolic narratives without historical content (mid-twentieth-century critics). The mature position, associated with Vansina, Mercer, Gunson, and Uriam, treats oral traditions as social products whose historical kernel can be isolated through comparative analysis, cross-referencing, cultural contextualisation, and awareness of the mechanisms of layering and shadowing.

A parallel development — Islander historians writing from Islander perspectives — has since the 1980s produced "a more Island-orientated narrative" that provides a counterweight to "Eurocentric histories which dominate the current historiography of the Pacific." However, "powerful constraints remain: local court/tribunal/government archives of oral traditions and histories remain captive to colonially created 'native administrations.'"
