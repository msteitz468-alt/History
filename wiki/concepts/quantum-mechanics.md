# Quantum Mechanics

The branch of physics describing the behavior of matter and energy at
atomic and subatomic scales, developed collaboratively across the early
twentieth century and marked by phenomena — indeterminacy, wave-particle
duality, quantization of energy — with no counterpart in classical physics.

## Definition and Scope

Quantum mechanics replaced the deterministic, continuous framework of
classical mechanics with a probabilistic formalism in which physical
quantities (energy, angular momentum) take discrete values and outcomes of
measurement are described by probability amplitudes rather than certain
predictions. Its development involved Max Planck's 1900 quantization of
blackbody radiation, Einstein's 1905 explanation of the photoelectric
effect via light quanta (photons), Niels Bohr's atomic model, and the full
mathematical formalisms developed independently by Werner Heisenberg
(matrix mechanics) and Erwin Schrödinger (wave mechanics) in the mid-1920s.

## Intellectual Genesis and Debate

Einstein, despite his foundational 1905 contribution, became one of quantum
mechanics' most prominent critics, famously objecting to its probabilistic
character ("God does not play dice") in extended debates with Niels Bohr
over the theory's completeness and interpretation — a controversy examined
in this collection under [[einsteins-god|Einstein's invocations of "God" in
physics]].

## Key Distinctions

Quantum mechanics is distinguished from classical physics by its
probabilistic predictions, the uncertainty principle (fundamental limits on
simultaneously knowing certain paired properties, such as position and
momentum), and quantum entanglement, whose implications for locality and
realism remain subjects of ongoing physical and philosophical inquiry.

## Historical and Scientific Significance

Quantum mechanics underlies the technological foundation of the modern
world — semiconductors, lasers, nuclear technology (including the
[[manhattan-project|Manhattan Project]]), and modern chemistry — making it,
alongside general relativity, one of the two pillars of twentieth-century
physics.
