# Mongol Tactics

**Emic:** Mongol commanders executed a repertoire of steppe maneuvers — arrow storm, caracole,
feigned retreat, double envelopment, Fabian avoidance — codified in Yasa rolls and rehearsed in
**[[nerge]]** hunts. **Etic:** [[may-mongol-art-of-war-2016|Timothy May]] argues the Mongols did not
invent these tactics but **perfected** them through discipline, training, and integration into a
permanent army structure that supported multi-front operational strategy.

## System and intelligence

As the Mongol army evolved, "it developed tactics that were no longer wholly derived from
pastoralist steppe warfare" yet "their tactics provided the foundation for an operational strategy
that enabled them to fight on several fronts and allowed the Mongol empire to expand at a steady
and planned rate rather than by means of haphazard conquests scattered over vast territories."
(L436–437) Campaigns opened with intelligence: the Mongols "did not begin a campaign without
accumulating intelligence" from merchants, spies, **alginci** scouts, and expeditionary forces,
relayed through the **yam** post system. (L450–451, L461) Scouts "often operated more than 50km
ahead of each column and on the flanks, making it very difficult for an enemy to launch a surprise
attack." (L461)

Whenever a khan mobilized, Juvaini recorded that commanders "produce these rolls and model their
actions thereon; and proceed with the disposition of armies or the destruction of provinces and
cities in the manner therein prescribed." (L438–441) — the lost Great Book of Yasa as operational
template.

## Arrow storm and concentrated firepower

The **arrow storm** (or shower) was "the most common tactic practised by the Mongols." They
"enveloped their enemy, then shot a hail of arrows. The number of arrows that hit was described
as a phenomenon of nature." (L476–477) At 200–300 m range shooting was less accurate but could
"disrupt the enemy's formation"; once broken, "the Mongols charged." (L477–478) Archers did not
aim at individuals but loosed arrows "at a high trajectory into a predetermined 'killing zone' or
area target" — weakening morale while staying beyond retaliation. (L478–479) On occasion they
dismounted and used horses as cover. (L479)

May stresses that the Mongols "were perhaps the first to use [concentrated firepower] to its
maximum effect in all aspects of war, from the arrow storm to batteries of siege weapons," directing
fire "by means of banners, fire signals, and whistling arrows" — a generation before European
elites fully developed massed missile doctrine. (L483–485)

## Caracole

The Mongols combined the arrow storm with hit-and-run **caracole** tactics, "changing horses
regularly to keep them fresh." (L490) Approximately 80 men in each jaghun participated, 20 acting
as heavy cavalry; "each jaghun sent 20 men per wave." Waves "fired several arrows as they charged,
and then circled back to the Mongol lines after completing their charge, having loosed their final
shot roughly 40–50m from the enemy line before wheeling around" — close enough to pierce armor,
far enough to evade counter-charge. (L490–491) While circling back they used the **Parthian shot**.
With 60 arrows per man the barrage could continue "for almost an hour." (L491)

Marco Polo observed the same method in the late thirteenth century: the Mongols "keep perpetually
riding round and shooting into the enemy" and, because "they do not count it any shame to run away
in battle, they will [sometimes pretend to] do so, and in running away they turn in the saddle and
shoot hard and strong at the foe." (L504–505) May notes the parallel to European pistol-armed
caracole of the sixteenth–seventeenth centuries. (L509–511) The Secret History's **chisel**
formation — repeated charges from multiple groups until a signal unleashed attacks "from all
directions" — was probably a variant of caracole rather than a single shock charge. (L514–520)

## Feigned retreat

The **feigned retreat** was "a classic tactic of steppe warfare practised since ancient times": a
token force charged, retreated, and drew pursuit; at a pre-arranged point flanking forces attacked
while the token force wheeled to the front. (L525–526) The most renowned example was **[[battle-of-kalka-river-1223|1223]]**,
when Jebe and Sübedei retreated before Kipchaks and Rus' "deep into the steppe for several days
until they reached the Khalkha River," where the main force destroyed the pursuers. (L527–528)

Polo confirmed the tactic's logic: in flight the Mongols inflicted "vast volleys of arrows...
turning round upon their pursuers, who are fancying that they have won the battle"; when enough
horses and men were killed or wounded, "they wheel round bodily, and return to the charge in
perfect order." (L531–532) Like other steppe tactics, feigned retreat avoided close combat until
the advantage was gained. (L535)

## Fabian tactics

**[[fabian-strategy|Fabian tactics]]** differed from feigned retreat: they involved "the avoidance
of all direct contact with the enemy" until the Mongols found ideal ground or regrouped dispersed
columns for a surprise attack. (L540–541) The army might "divide into small groups to prevent it
from being surrounded when necessary, and then regroup and launch a surprise attack on the enemy
at a more opportune time." (L540) Constant proximity exhausted defenders "whether in the open or
in a fortress." (L541)

Against enemies who planted spears to block cavalry, the Mongols withdrew the bulk of their force
while detachments harassed; when hunger, thirst, or false confidence drew the enemy out, the main
body returned and destroyed them. (L542–543)

## Flanking, double envelopment, and open-the-end

Whenever possible the Mongols surrounded enemies using **[[nerge]]** training. Zhao Hong's
*Meng-Da Bei-Lu* describes operational procedure: on contact the main body "extended in front over
as great a distance as possible so as to overlap the flanks of the hostile force"; skirmishers and
scouts then reported "local topography, lines of communication, and the strength and disposition of
the opposing troops." (L559–560) Chinggis Khan encircled along both sides of rivers when terrain
permitted. (L565–566)

**Double envelopment** stemmed from nerge/battue hunting: wings wrapped enemy flanks and tightened
the circle "forming a dense mass from which it was difficult to escape" — achievable even when
outnumbered, thanks to archery and mobility. (L569–570) At campaign scale, after Vladimir (1237)
Mongol columns proceeded "tümän by tümän in järge formation and take and destroy every town,
province and fortress they came to," encircling regions before closing in. (L571) Prisoners and
levies sometimes attacked the front while Mongol columns reappeared on flanks or rear. (L573)

The **open-the-end** tactic left a deliberate gap in the encirclement: "In their panic and desire
to escape through this gap, the enemy often discarded their weapons to flee faster and rarely
maintained their discipline. The Mongols then attacked them from the rear" — as at Mohi (1241).
Dalantai noted this was used when the enemy might fight to the death if fully trapped. (L567–568)

## Deception and psychological warfare

The Mongols "realized it was more efficient to convince a city or fortress to surrender without
resistance rather than to be drawn into a siege" and cultivated a reputation for massacre as
deterrent — "not carried out in wanton blood lust" but to discourage revolt, resistance, and
rebellion. (L605–606) Barfield: they "were extremely conscious of their small numbers and employed
terror as a tool to discourage resistance against them." (L607–609) Propaganda exaggerated army
size (Möngke's 40,000 men rumored as 100,000 in 1258) and exploited enemy dissension. (L613–614)

**Deception** on the battlefield included:
- Five campfires per soldier to magnify numbers (1204, vs. Naiman). (L615–616)
- Dust raised by branches tied to horse tails to simulate reinforcements. (L616)
- Dummies on spare horses and single-file riding to mask numbers. (L616–617)
- Herding oxen and horses into enemy lines to disrupt formations. (L617–618)
- **Bush formations**: small groups advancing at low profile, especially at night or in poor
  visibility. (L619–620)
- Using Christian auxiliaries against Muslims and vice versa (Joinville). (L621)

## Weather magic

The Mongols also employed **supernatural tactics**, chiefly **weather magic** by a shaman (**jadaci**)
using **rain stones** "thought to be imbued with the power to control weather." (L626–627) After
luring an enemy from base, the jadaci could summon rain or even summer snow; the Mongols sheltered
in rain gear and attacked disoriented foes. (L627–628)

Bar Hebraeus records Ögödei (or Tolui, in alternate accounts) summoning a three-day July downpour
against a Jin field army after it left support; Rashid al-Din describes stones washed in water
producing "cold, snow, rain and blizzards at once" even in midsummer, followed by Mongol assault
on weakened Jin troops. (L628–631)

## Operational strategy

May treats tactics and strategy as integrated. Mongol **strategy** exploited **high mobility**:
troopers had three to five mounts, enabling endurance-based warfare "not employed again until the
twentieth century, when armies became mechanized." (L636–637)

**Preparation** included census, intelligence accumulation, declaration of war (surrender/tribute or
destruction), strategy and commander selection at a **quriltai**, rendezvous points, and
mobilization. (L641–642) Denis Sinor: "Mongol strategy at its best was based on a very careful
planning of the military operations to be performed, and the essence of it lay in a very rigid
timetable to which all Mongol commanders were expected to adhere strictly." (L643–646) Generals
kept independence within the timetable; campaigns could exploit weather and surprise (winter river
crossings, lean horses). (L649–652)

### Travel by columns

Invading armies followed **several routes of advance**: against Khwarazm, Chinggis Khan used "at
least four and perhaps five routes"; in Russia, Sübedei, Batu, and Möngke approached from three
directions, with further three-pronged advances into Eastern Europe — columns ultimately
converging on the enemy's center of power (Samarqand; Budapest). (L656–657) Scouts and schedules
let them "march divided but fight united," appearing on many fronts before opponents could
concentrate. (L658)

### Annihilation of the field army

Multi-pronged advance suited the preferred method: **destroy the opposing field army** before deep
penetration. Refugees from smaller places fled to the capital, depressing morale and straining food
and water — as at Bukhara before its fall. (L665–666) With the field army gone, sieges proceeded
without serious relief; outer strongholds yielded prisoners for siege work and experience. (L667)

### Pursuit of leaders

After defeating field armies the Mongols targeted enemy **leaders** to prevent rallying. Chinggis
Khan learned from early failures to eliminate opposing commanders; "the merciless pursuit of enemy
commanders evolved into a standard operational procedure." (L672–673) Muhammad Khwarazmshah was
pursued to the Caspian by Jebe and Sübedei; Jalal al-Din to the Indus; Bela IV of Hungary received
"no breathing room" after Mohi. (L673–674) Pursuit columns doubled as intelligence-gathering and
disruption missions, keeping local rulers' forces at home. (L674–675)
