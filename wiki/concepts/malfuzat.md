# Malfūẓāt

*Malfūẓāt* (singular: *malfūẓ*; from Arabic *laafaza*, "to utter") are collections of the sayings, conversations, and anecdotes of Sufi masters, recorded by disciples. In South Asian Islam they became a distinctive literary genre and a major historical source.

## Nature and Development

The classical Sufi handbooks (Sulamī's *Ṭabaqāt*, Qushayrī's *Risāla*) already consist to a large extent of apophthegmata of the masters of old. The Indo-Muslim innovation was the *systematic daily recording* of a living master's conversations — in effect, a diary of khānqāh life — rather than retrospective compilation.

According to Schimmel, the *malfūẓāt* constitute "a valuable source for our knowledge of life outside the court circles. They are a necessary corrective of the official historiography; they allow us interesting glimpses into social and cultural problems that the official authors wittingly or unwittingly overlooked" ([[schimmel-mystical-dimensions-of-islam-1975]]).

Khalīq Aḥmad Nizami, the leading modern historian of medieval Indian Sufism, has emphasised their importance for understanding social and cultural history below the level of the court.

## Key Examples

- **Fawāʾid al-fuʾād** ("Benefits of the Heart"): Conversations of [[nizamuddin-auliya]], compiled between 1307 and 1322 by his disciple and poet Amīr Ḥasan Sijzī Dihlawī. The first major example of the genre in India; notable because after a year's compilation, Niẓāmuddīn himself began actively helping fill lacunae in the manuscript.
- **Khayr al-majālis**: Conversations of Chirāgh-i Dehlī (d. 1356), edited by Nizami (Aligarh, 1959)
- **Surūr aṣ-ṣudūr**: Conversations of Ḥamīduddīn Nagōrī (d. 1274), compiled by a grandson

In later periods, *malfūẓāt* were "fabricated with more or less pious intent," limiting their reliability as sources. The distinction between authentic contemporary records and pious later compilations is thus critical for their use as historical evidence.

## Historical Value

As a genre, the *malfūẓāt* are valuable for:
1. **Social history**: khānqāh daily life, relations between Sufis and state, economic arrangements, domestic life of saints
2. **Intellectual history**: which books were read, which theological positions were discussed, how Sufi theory was applied in practice
3. **Linguistic history**: early use of vernacular Punjabi and Urdu in a formal literary context
4. **Political history**: the attitude of Sufi orders toward the Delhi Sultanate and Mughal rulers, visible in unguarded conversations rather than formal treatises

## Related Forms

Letters (*maktūbāt*) of Sufi masters — most famously Aḥmad Sirhindī's 534 letters, 70 addressed to Mughal officials — served a related function: circulated for edification, they record theological positions and political interventions. The *maktūbāt* genre produced Sirhindī's honorific title *mujaddid* and constitutes a major source for the [[naqshbandiyya]] in India.

Biographical dictionaries (*tadhkira*) of saints complement *malfūẓāt*: Mīr Khurd's *Siyar al-auliyāʾ* (history of the Chishtī order, written ca. 1330s) is described by Schimmel as "still the most trustworthy account of the Chishtī silsila and of medieval Chishtī khānqāh life."
