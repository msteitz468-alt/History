# Classical Greek Art (Archaic to Classical)

## The transformation

**Classical Greek art** is the style that emerged in the fifth century BCE from the
**Archaic** tradition — the period in which Greek artists, as [[lewis-cah-v5-1992|CAH Vol.
V]] (Ch. 8a, J. J. Pollitt) argues, learned to combine the inherited **idealism** (art as
*eidos*, "form-and-idea," built on *symmetria*, the commensurability of parts) with a new
**empirical naturalism** (the desire to record actual sense-experience). "It is the
interplay between traditional idealism and a new empiricism that gives the art of the
Classical period its distinctive character."

## Late Archaic naturalism (c. 520–480)

The decisive innovation came in red-figure **vase painting** (the technique shifted c. 525):
a new focus on the single expressive figure, the **invention of foreshortening** by the
red-figure "Pioneers" (Euphronios, Euthymides, Phintias) — "one of the most profound changes
ever to have occurred in the history of art" — a **democratization of subject matter** (the
everyday citizen in the gymnasium and symposium, perhaps reflecting the new Cleisthenic
democracy), and a new interest in **facial emotion**. In sculpture the rigid **kouros** type
was abandoned around 480 for figures (the "Kritios Boy") that allowed internal movement.

## The Severe Style and the Persian Wars (c. 480–450)

The free-wheeling naturalism was then disciplined into the **Early Classical "Severe
Style."** Pollitt links its sobriety directly to the **Persian Wars**, which Aeschylus and
Herodotus read as a **moral** victory of self-restraint (*sophrosyne*) over *hybris*: the new
art is austere, spare, and preoccupied with **states of consciousness** and the significance
of **change**. Its supreme monument is the **temple of Zeus at Olympia** (470–457), whose
metopes and pediments explore **character (*ethos*) and emotional reaction (*pathos*)** —
the Lapith-and-Centaur clash re-enacting the Persian Wars as "civilization over barbarism."
The era also produced the **Charioteer of Delphi**, the bronze **Artemision god**, Myron's
**Discobolus**, the **Tyrannicides**, and the historical paintings of **Polygnotus** — and
saw the first appearance of **portraits** of real men (Themistocles, Miltiades) and
historical subjects (the Marathon mural in the Stoa Poikile).

## High Classical: the Parthenon and the dual nature (c. 450–430)

"The story of High Classical Greek art is for the most part the story of Athenian art," and
above all of the **[[parthenon|Periclean building program]]**. Pollitt reads the
**[[parthenon|Parthenon]]** as holding two modes of perception in tension: its calculated
*symmetria* and 9:4 proportion express timeless *eidos*, while its famous **refinements**
(curvature, column tilt) force the viewer to ponder "the uncertain nature of his own
perceptions." He sees this dualism as parallel to the philosophical split between
**Pythagorean idealism** (mathematical truth underlies the world) and the **Protagorean
relativism** of the [[sophists]] ("man is the measure of all things") — currents Pericles'
circle (Anaxagoras, Protagoras, Phidias) knew directly.

## Late fifth century: the impressionist turn

After the Peloponnesian War and the plague, Pollitt argues, the balance "tipped toward"
subjectivity. A **second revolution in painting** introduced **graphic perspective**
(Agatharchos) and **shading/*skiagraphia*** (Apollodoros); in sculpture the **"wet/wind-blown
drapery"** style (the Nike parapet, the Nike of Paionios) pursued fleeting impression. The
elegance can be read as **escapism** from a darkening age; its sombre counter-current appears
in the white-ground funerary *lekythoi* and in the **Erechtheum**, "a shrine to the same
irrational forces that haunted Euripides in the *Bacchae*."

## The artist as intellectual

A key social fact: in the fifth century **sculptors and painters first became figures of
public prominence** — Polygnotus honoured by the Amphictyonies, **Phidias** the *episkopos*
of Pericles' program and an intellectual of his circle, the flamboyant rivals **Zeuxis** and
**Parrhasios**. Several were **literate theoreticians**: Polyclitus' ***Canon*** (a
quasi-Pythagorean treatise on proportion) and Ictinus' book on the Parthenon assert that the
artist "deserved to be taken seriously." Because many artists in Athens were **non-citizens**
who later moved on, the Phidian style rapidly **"Athenianized"** Greek art (the one great
holdout being Polyclitus of Argos).
