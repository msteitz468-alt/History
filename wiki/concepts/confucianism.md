# Confucianism

## Overview

**Confucianism** is the **ru** (儒, "scholar") tradition that became the dominant state
ideology and ethical system of imperial China and the wider
**[[east-asian-cultural-sphere|East Asian cultural sphere]]**. In
[[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|CWH IV]] (Yao, Ch 16) it is examined specifically as **statecraft and
state ideology**: how a tradition descended from ritual masters and educationists came to
"shape a unique type of statecraft" and a "Confucian sphere" across East Asia.

## Origins (emic and etic)

The very name is **etic**: "Confucianism" was coined by sixteenth-to-nineteenth-century
Jesuit missionaries (on the model of "Buddha-ism," "Christ-ianity") and can mislead about the
tradition's nature. The **emic** tradition, *ru*, is older than [[confucius|Confucius]],
rooted in the **ritual masters, musicians, and educationists** of the Shang and early
[[zhou-dynasty|Zhou]] who preserved the **rites-and-music** (*li yue*) order. Two features
distinguish it: an association with **ritual and political institutions**, and a grounding in
**education**.

## Evolution

- **Confucius** (551–479 BCE) sought to restore the early Zhou ritual order through moral
  cultivation and the ideal of the *junzi* (gentleman).
- **Mengzi (Mencius)** held human nature **innately good**, perfectible by extending the
  moral mind; he championed "humane government" (*ren zheng*) against despotism.
- **Xunzi** held human nature **innately bad** but transformable by ritual and learning,
  giving law (*fa*) and ritual propriety (*li*) a central place — a more pragmatic strand.
- **Han synthesis:** under **Dong Zhongshu** and **Emperor Wu** of the
  **[[han-dynasty|Han]]**, an eclectic Confucianism absorbing Yin-Yang and Five Elements
  cosmology became the **official ideology** (other schools suppressed), the cult of
  Confucius a **state sacrifice**, and the doctrine of **Heaven–human interaction** both
  justified and restrained imperial power.

## Confucianism and the State

Confucianism supplied the **moral legitimation** that the [[qin-dynasty|Qin]] Legalist state
had lacked: the Han fused Legalist administration with Confucian ethics, and the
**civil service examination** system (rising under the [[han-dynasty|Han]], defining under
the [[tang-dynasty|Tang]]) institutionalized Confucian learning as the gateway to office.
Pressured by **[[buddhism|Buddhism]]** and Daoism after the Han, Confucianism retreated into
a "three-in-one" division of labor (Confucianism for politics, Daoism for spiritual life,
Buddhism for the afterlife) before the late-Tang **Neo-Confucian revival** (Han Yu)
reclaimed administrative dominance.

## Limitations and Critiques

Confucianism's exclusivity under the Han (the suppression of rival schools) and its
hierarchical, patriarchal social vision are central to critiques; its relationship to
"religion" is contested (it functioned as state cult, ethical system, and philosophy at
once). The boundary with **Legalism**, Daoism, and Buddhism was porous, and the unified
"Confucianism" is partly a retrospective construction.

## Significance

As the longest-running governing ideology in world history, Confucianism shaped the political
culture not only of China but of **Korea, Japan, and Vietnam** — a key pillar of the
[[east-asian-cultural-sphere|East Asian cultural sphere]] and China's distinctive
contribution to the [[axial-age|Axial Age]].

Its foundational texts are now being ingested directly: the **[[confucius-analects-watson|Analects]]**
of Confucius and the **[[mencius-ivanhoe|Mencius]]** (two of the Four Books), with the rival Warring
States schools of **[[han-feizi-watson|Legalism (Han Feizi)]]** and **[[zhuangzi-watson|Daoism
(Zhuangzi)]]**.
