# Penance in Merovingian Gaul

**Penance as a lifelong, public, and socially inclusive practice — not the "void" that older scholarship
imagined before the Irish penitentials.** Kevin Uhalde's chapter (Oxford Handbook, Ch. 45) demolishes the
"penitential void" thesis (Gaudemet, Poschmann, Vogel) and shows that "penance was a major theme in
ecclesiastical life in the fifth and sixth centuries" (Meens).

## Definition and Scope

Uhalde identifies three structural elements of penance: **temporal** (conversion as the start of a continuous
lifelong process), **judicial** (self-scrutiny before a divine tribunal), and **intellectual** — *metanoia*
as *megalê sunesis*, "great awareness" (the *Shepherd of Hermas*). This inheritance was alive in Gaul: the
*Shepherd* circulated widely (cited in the *Life of Genovefa*, c. 540); Cassian's *Conferences* — "massively
influential" in Merovingian monastic libraries — taught that "the truest judge of repentance" is the
penitent's own conscience; Tertullian's *exomologesis* supplied the public bodily performance ("groaning,
weeping… prostrating yourself at the feet of the priests"); and Origen catalogued seven "remissions of sins"
beyond formal penance.

## Penance as a Visible, Lifelong State

Far from being a deathbed ritual, penance was a recognized social *ordo*. Sidonius Apollinaris listed three —
"monk, clergyman, penitent" — visible in daily diet, dress, and demeanour. The tombstone of **Carusa of
Lyon** (d. 520) records that she "did penance twenty-two years and lived in peace sixty-five years," refuting
any deathbed-only model. The *Life of Eligius* describes confession before a priest at age 25 and severe
self-imposed penance, with the hagiographer exclaiming "O *felix penitentia* — that so swiftly attracts the
mercy of Christ." Penance was also **socially inclusive**, crossing "boundaries of social and religious
status, of time and space, and of life and death" — the *Passion of Praeiectus* seats three penitents at an
Easter dinner where mockers fall through the floor, teaching that "no one… needs to despair of forgiveness,
so long as they are willing to be converted."

## The Irish Contribution and the "Void" Myth

[[columbanus|Columbanus]] arrived in Francia c. 591 bringing "medicines of penance" (Jonas of Bobbio) — the
practice of **regular private/tariffed penance** that the Irish *peregrini* spread on the Continent (see
[[merovingian-monasticism]], [[migrants-and-minorities-in-merovingian-gaul]]). Uhalde argues that Jonas's
remark was misread by Gaudemet and Vogel as evidence of a "penitential void"; it actually concerned the lack
of ascetic rivals for Columbanus's particular style of monasticism, not an absence of penance. The Irish thus
enriched, rather than created, an already-vigorous penitential culture.
