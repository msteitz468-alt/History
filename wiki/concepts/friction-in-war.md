# Friction in War

## Definition — The Canonical Passage

Book One Chapter Seven opens with a confession of analytic difficulty: the concept
it introduces is almost impossible to convey to someone who has not experienced combat.

> If one has never personally experienced war, one cannot understand in what the
> difficulties constantly mentioned really consist, nor why a commander should need any
> brilliance and exceptional ability. Everything looks simple; the knowledge required does
> not look remarkable, the strategic options are so obvious that by comparison the simplest
> problem of higher mathematics has an impressive scientific dignity.

The crucial formulation follows:

> **Everything in war is very simple, but the simplest thing is difficult.** The
> difficulties accumulate and end by producing a kind of friction that is inconceivable
> unless one has experienced war.

Clausewitz then defines friction's function precisely: "Friction is the only concept that
more or less corresponds to the factors that distinguish real war from war on paper."

## The Traveler Analogy

To make friction tangible, Clausewitz offers a celebrated simile:

> Imagine a traveler who late in the day decides to cover two more stages before nightfall.
> Only four or five hours more, on a paved highway with relays of horses: it should be an
> easy trip. But at the next station he finds no fresh horses, or only poor ones; the
> country grows hilly, the road bad, night falls, and finally after many difficulties he is
> only too glad to reach a resting place with any kind of primitive accommodation. It is
> much the same in war. Countless minor incidents — the kind you can never really foresee —
> combine to lower the general level of performance, so that one always falls far short of
> the intended goal.

The implication is powerful: no plan survives contact with reality unmodified, not because
plans are poorly conceived, but because execution occurs in a medium — war — that inherently
resists.

## What Friction Encompasses

Friction is not a single obstacle but an aggregate of many. Clausewitz identifies several
specific sources:

**Danger.** Chapter Four makes the connection explicit: "Danger is part of the friction of
war. Without an accurate conception of danger we cannot understand war." The physical risk
of combat alters judgment in ways that no peacetime calculation can anticipate. Even the
senior commander, though insulated from direct physical danger, carries the accumulated
anxiety of those in his command.

**Physical effort.** Chapter Five treats physical exertion as "one of the great sources of
friction in war. Because its limits are uncertain, it resembles one of those substances
whose elasticity makes the degree of its friction exceedingly hard to gauge." Exhaustion
degrades every calculation.

**Intelligence failure.** Chapter Six on intelligence identifies unreliable information as
"one of the most serious sources of friction in war, by making things appear entirely
different from what one had expected." The famous passage: "Many intelligence reports in
war are contradictory; even more are false, and most are uncertain." The commander who
"planned the operation and now sees it being carried out may well lose confidence in his
earlier judgment" — yet "self-reliance is his best defense against the pressures of the
moment."

**Chance.** Friction "is everywhere in contact with chance, and brings about effects that
cannot be measured, just because they are largely due to chance. One, for example, is the
weather. Fog can prevent the enemy from being seen in time, a gun from firing when it
should, a report from reaching the commanding officer. Rain can prevent a battalion from
arriving..." Weather is Clausewitz's paradigm case because it is both unpredictable and
omnipresent.

**The atomism of armies.** Perhaps the most profound observation: friction does not exist
at the level of the plan but at the level of individuals. "The military machine — the army
and everything related to it — is basically very simple and therefore seems easy to manage.
But we should bear in mind that none of its components is of one piece: each part is
composed of individuals, every one of whom retains his potential of friction."

## War as Movement in a Resistant Element

Clausewitz introduces a second metaphor as strong as the traveler analogy:

> Action in war is like movement in a resistant element. Just as the simplest and most
> natural of movements, walking, cannot easily be performed in water, so in war it is
> difficult for normal efforts to achieve even moderate results. A genuine theorist is
> like a swimming teacher, who makes his pupils practice motions on land that are meant
> to be performed in water. To those who are not thinking of swimming the motions will
> appear grotesque and exaggerated. By the same token, theorists who have never swum, or
> who have not learned to generalize from experience, are impractical and even ridiculous:
> they teach only what is already common knowledge: how to walk.

This is also a critique of doctrinal prescriptions: any theory that specifies exact
procedures on paper teaches "only how to walk" — it does not account for the water.

## Friction vs. "Obstacles" in Planning

Clausewitz distinguishes friction from ordinary planning obstacles. A planning obstacle is
a foreseeable constraint — the width of a river, the capacity of a road — that can in
principle be incorporated into the plan. Friction, by contrast, is structurally
unforeseeable: it is "the kind you can never really foresee." It arises from the aggregate
of innumerable "countless minor incidents" each trivial in isolation, lethal in
combination. This is why no prescriptive checklist can eliminate it: "it is a force that
theory can never quite define."

## The Chapter Eight Summary

Book One Chapter Eight, "Concluding Observations," identifies the four elements that
"coalesce to form the atmosphere of war": danger, physical exertion, intelligence, and
friction. Together they form "a single concept of general friction." The only lubricant
is combat experience: "Habit hardens the body for great exertions, strengthens the heart
in great peril, and fortifies judgment against first impressions." Experience cannot be
simulated fully in peacetime — which is why Clausewitz urges that maneuvers "involve some
of the elements of friction" to train officers' judgment.

## Implications for Doctrine

The friction concept carries a profound anti-doctrinal implication. If war is inherently
a "resistant element," then any positive doctrine — a set of rules claiming "do X and Y
will result" — is structurally misleading. In Book Two Chapter Two Clausewitz makes this
explicit under the heading "A Positive Doctrine is Unattainable": "it is simply not
possible to construct a model for the art of war that can serve as a scaffolding on which
the commander can rely for support at any time." Friction is one of three properties that
make positive doctrine impossible (alongside the positive reaction of the enemy and the
uncertainty of all information). See [[clausewitz-on-theory]] for the full Book Two
argument.

Theory's role is therefore not to provide operational rules but to *educate* the commander
— "to guide him in his self-education, not to accompany him to the battlefield." What
will help him is not a manual but developed judgment, and judgment develops through study
and experience, not through the mechanical application of principles.

## Paret's Account of the Genesis of the Concept

In his introductory essay "The Genesis of On War," Paret traces friction back to a
specific historical moment: Clausewitz first used the term "during the campaign of 1806
to describe the difficulties Scharnhorst encountered in persuading the high command to
reach decisions, and the further difficulties of having the decisions implemented." By
1812, in his tutorial essay to the Crown Prince, Clausewitz had developed the concept
fully. He wrote: "The conduct of war resembles the workings of an intricate machine with
tremendous friction, so that combinations which are easily planned on paper can be executed
only with great effort. Consequently the commander's free will and intelligence find
themselves hampered at every turn." The essay concluded that friction always has a
"psychologically inhibiting effect" and that "psychic energy must therefore take a part in
overcoming it." This insight — that friction is not merely physical but psychological —
carried directly into On War.
