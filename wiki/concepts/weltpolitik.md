## Definition and Scope

*Weltpolitik* (world politics, or global power politics) was the foreign policy doctrine of the German [[german-empire]] under [[wilhelm-ii]], formally articulated in 1897 with the appointment of Alfred von Tirpitz as Secretary of the Reich Marine Office and Bernhard von Bülow as Foreign Minister (later Chancellor 1900–1909). It expressed the ambition that Germany should become a global imperial power commensurate with its rapidly growing industrial strength and population — acquiring a colonial empire, a world-class navy, and a "place in the sun" (*Platz an der Sonne*) in the international order.

The term simultaneously designated:
1. A foreign policy orientation (overseas empire, naval expansion, global commercial presence)
2. A domestic political strategy (*Sammlungspolitik* — rallying bourgeois and conservative forces against Social Democracy through imperial nationalist appeals)
3. A social Darwinian worldview (Germany as a young, vigorous civilization in competition with older, declining empires)

## Origins and Intellectual Context

Smith's Chapter 14 situates *Weltpolitik* within the transformation of German domestic politics in the 1890s. The SPD received more votes than any other party in the 1890 Reichstag election; demographic trends ensured continued Socialist expansion. The "enemies of the Reich" (Socialists, Catholics, national minorities) commanded a majority of the popular vote from 1898 onwards. In this context, *Weltpolitik* was conceived as a way to "deflect social conflict and absorb the Catholic minority in a common imperial project" — a politics of national distraction.

Its intellectual foundations included:
- Alfred von Tirpitz's reading of A.T. Mahan's *The Influence of Sea Power on History* (translated into German in large print runs)
- Social Darwinist frameworks of competition among civilizations and races
- Bülow's vision that "the rest of the world was in political decay" while Germany's population growth and industrial strength demanded world power — "One is either a hammer or an anvil"
- The [[kulturkampf]] vocabulary of *Kulturvolk* extended globally: Germany as bearer of civilization in competition with other powers

## Strategic Logic: The Tirpitz Risk Theory

The core naval strategy rested on what historians call the *Risikoflotte* (risk fleet) or Tirpitz Plan. Germany would build a battle fleet of two-thirds the size of Britain's, concentrated in the North Sea. In order to protect its global empire, Britain would risk naval battle with Germany in the North Sea only at the cost of losing enough ships to become vulnerable to a third power. Therefore, Britain would either concede colonial territory or seek accommodation with Germany.

"In their calculations, Germany would have to increase the German Navy to two-thirds the size of the British, challenging British superiority in the North Sea. In order to protect its wider hegemony, Britain, they supposed, would concede a share of its colonies, thus making the playing field of imperial competition more equal."

## Implementation and Failure

**Naval program**: The Reichstag approved in 1898 nineteen large battleships, eight armored cruisers, twelve large cruisers, and thirty light cruisers at a cost of 408 million marks; the 1900 law doubled the battleship program to thirty-eight. The Reichstag agreement included an unusual clause removing future appropriations from parliamentary control — "a remarkable act of its own disempowerment."

**Colonial ventures**: German colonial acquisitions had peaked in the 1880s. After 1897, *Weltpolitik* produced a series of diplomatic crises rather than colonial gains: Morocco (1905), Algeciras Conference (1906), Bosnia Crisis (1908), Agadir/Morocco (1911). "In a series of crises, Germany found itself increasingly isolated, with only a comparatively weak Austria-Hungary as an ally."

**Result**: Rather than forcing Britain to concede colonies, the naval build-up "set off an arms race and a cold war, and drove Great Britain into the arms of France, its erstwhile imperial rival." The 1904 Entente Cordiale and 1907 Anglo-Russian Entente completed Germany's encirclement.

**Economic contradiction**: *Weltpolitik* promised industrial benefits (naval contracts smoothing cyclical fluctuations in heavy industry) but created fiscal crisis: military spending consumed 75–90% of the federal budget, and the question of how to pay for it dominated domestic politics from 1897 to 1914. Conservative refusal to accept the Bülow inheritance tax (1909) — preferring indirect consumption taxes that fell on the poor — exemplified the contradiction between demanding military power and resisting its costs.

## Domestic Political Function

*Weltpolitik* served as the mechanism for *Sammlungspolitik* — the gathering (*Sammlung*) of agrarian and industrial interests, bourgeois and conservative parties, into a coalition behind naval expansion and directed against Social Democracy. Bülow "attempted to rally political support for this great power venture by bringing together agrarian and industrial interests, and bourgeois and conservative parties, including the Catholic Center."

The effort had partial success: the Center Party supported the first and second naval bills. But the Navy League split over the question of anti-Catholic rhetoric — "precisely this moderation proved untenable to an increasing number of radical nationalists, who saw in Catholic integration a dilution of the national character." *Weltpolitik* thus deepened confessional tensions it was meant to transcend.

Its domestic failure was more fundamental: "Whatever sympathy Socialist workers may have had for the spread of European culture, their pointedly anti-chauvinistic 'alternative culture' … militated against identification with the great power politics of German imperialism. The Navy League, founded on Tirpitz's initiative and with the Kaiser as patron, tried to bring workers into its membership, but the effort was a complete failure."

## Relationship to Radicalization

*Weltpolitik*'s diplomatic failures produced "cumulative disillusion" among radical nationalists (Peter Walkenhorst's term cited by Smith). "Dismayed by the diplomacy of imperialism, they increasingly concentrated their energies on continental expansion to the east and southeast and on the necessity of armament build-up for a coming war, which seemed the only road to world power." This shift from global to continental ambition — combined with the Army Bill of 1913 (the largest peacetime increase in army history) — prefigured the strategic orientation that shaped German decision-making in 1914.

## Distinction from Related Concepts

**[[nationalism]]**: *Weltpolitik* was an expression of nationalism but specifically its imperial-global variant, distinct from the cultural or ethnic nationalism of earlier periods.

**[[german-sonderweg]]**: The Sonderweg debate asks whether Germany's path was structurally different from Western liberal norms; *Weltpolitik* was the foreign policy expression of the social tensions the Sonderweg framework identifies domestically.

**Imperialism**: *Weltpolitik* was Germany's variant of the general European imperial competition analyzed by Hobsbawm (*The Age of Empire*, 1875–1914) — not exceptional but specifically German in its domestic political context and its diplomatic failure.

## Historiographical Significance

*Weltpolitik* occupies a central position in the Fischer Controversy over German war guilt. Fritz Fischer's *Griff nach der Weltmacht* (Germany's Aims in the First World War, 1961) argued that *Weltpolitik* provided the expansionist program that Germany pursued in 1914. Critics argued that the July Crisis was more contingent and the war not a calculated pursuit of hegemony. Smith's chapter suggests a middle position: "German responsibility is more precisely understood as a series of propositions" — the government did not will war, but *Weltpolitik*'s "imperatives … shut down alternatives, and gave a disproportionate voice to a nationalism that demanded great power status for a nation state that had palpably failed in its bid to become a great empire."
