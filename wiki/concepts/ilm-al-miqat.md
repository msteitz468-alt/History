# ʿIlm al-Mīqāt (Islamic Timekeeping Science)

## Definition and Scope

*ʿIlm al-mīqāt* — "the science of timekeeping" — was a specialized branch of Islamic astronomy dedicated to determining the times of the five daily prayers (*salat*), the direction of prayer toward Mecca (*qibla*), and the beginning of lunar months (particularly Ramadan). It was an applied science grounded in spherical trigonometry and mathematical astronomy, and it provided the institutional framework within which some of the most significant Islamic theoretical astronomy was produced.

Mīqāt was distinct from the theoretical astronomy of *ʿilm al-hayʾa* (see [[ilm-al-hayya]]) and from the observational-computational tradition of the *zij* (astronomical tables), though it drew heavily on both. Its defining characteristic was that its ultimate purpose was the correct performance of Islamic ritual obligations — making it a science whose social legitimacy was never in question among the religious establishment.

## Religious Foundation

Five daily prayers must be performed at specific times defined by solar position: dawn, just past solar noon, mid-afternoon, sunset, and the beginning of night. Determining these times precisely, especially for communities far from the Arabian Peninsula and across varying latitudes and seasons, required:

1. **Solar altitude calculations**: Finding the sun's altitude above the horizon at each prayer boundary.
2. **Spherical trigonometry**: Solving for angles on the celestial sphere, which required the development of the sine, cosine, tangent, and cotangent functions and the spherical sine and cosine laws.
3. **Instrument construction**: Building and calibrating sundials, astrolabes, and quadrants capable of field use.

The *qibla* problem — determining the great-circle direction from any location to Mecca on a spherical earth — was closely related to timekeeping and required the same trigonometric apparatus. Saliba identifies the qibla problem as a primary driver for the development of spherical trigonometry:

> "The qibla, being literally the direction one must face while praying, and knowing that the globe is supposed to be spherical, meant that one had to solve for the angle his own local horizon makes with the great circle that passes through his own zenith and the zenith of Mecca." — Saliba, *Islamic Science and the Making of the European Renaissance*, ch. 5

These calculations were not available in the Greek tradition, and Indian mathematics was insufficient to solve them completely. Islamic mathematicians therefore developed the full body of spherical trigonometric laws as an original contribution. Saliba notes: "Once that was done, there was little left to discover in the field of trigonometry."

## The Muwaqqit: Mosque Astronomer

The institutional embodiment of mīqāt was the *muwaqqit* — an astronomer employed by a mosque specifically for timekeeping duties. The muwaqqit was a religious functionary whose income came from the mosque's *waqf* (religious endowment). The position was institutionalized across the Islamic world from approximately the thirteenth century onward, and thousands of mīqāt manuscripts have been identified (primarily through the work of modern historian David King).

The significance of the muwaqqit for the history of science is substantial: it meant that a mosque could be a site of scientific employment, and that advanced mathematical astronomy could be pursued as a religious duty. The case of [[ibn-al-shatir|Ibn al-Shāṭir]] (d. 1375) demonstrates this most dramatically. Ibn al-Shāṭir was the muwaqqit at the Umayyad Mosque in Damascus. His timekeeping duties required him to construct sundials and other instruments. While doing so, he also produced planetary models whose mathematical structure — eliminating Ptolemy's eccentrics, using the [[tusi-couple|Ṭūsī Couple]] for Mercury — was later found to be identical to Copernicus's. Revolutionary theoretical astronomy was produced within, and funded by, a mosque timekeeping office.

> "Ibn al-Shāṭir (d. 1375) of Damascus, of the great astronomical fame, was only a muwaqqit (Timekeeper) at the Umayyad mosque in the same city. As a functionary of the mosque he must have derived his livelihood from the religious endowment of the mosque, and just like a judge was also considered a religious functionary." — Saliba, ch. 5

## Relationship to Theoretical Astronomy

Mīqāt literature was the applied outlet for theoretical astronomy. The spherical trigonometry developed to solve the qibla problem "became subservient to other religious requirements, as much of it was used in almost every branch of miqāt literature" (Saliba). It also served the theoretical astronomy of hay'a. The two traditions — mīqāt as religiously mandated application, hay'a as theoretical model-building — reinforced each other technically and legitimized each other institutionally.

The practical tables produced for mīqāt (*mīqāt tables*) were mathematically related to astronomical tables (*zijes*) that could serve astrological purposes. This created a zone of ambiguity: Ibn al-Shāṭir's *al-Zij al-jadīd*, designed for religious timekeeping, could also function as a tool for astrologers. The line between the religiously legitimate (mīqāt) and the religiously suspect (astrology) could blur in practice even when it was sharply drawn in theory.

## Significance

ʿIlm al-mīqāt is one of the clearest empirical refutations of the "conflict thesis" for science and religion in Islamic civilization. A science requiring advanced mathematics and astronomical observation was born directly from religious obligation, institutionalized in the mosque, and funded by religious endowment. The greatest theoretical astronomer of 14th-century Islam — whose work influenced Copernicus — conducted his research as a mosque employee whose job title was religious.

Mīqāt also illustrates the broader pattern Saliba identifies: Islamic religious requirements did not impede mathematical and astronomical science; they *generated* it, by creating precise technical problems that demanded rigorous solutions.
