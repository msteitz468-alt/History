## Overview

"The invention of the Native" is a historiographical concept describing the ideological mechanism by which European colonial administrations homogenised the vast diversity of Pacific Islander peoples into a single, undifferentiated category — "the Native" — that legitimated rule and structured policy. As [[denoon-cambridge-pacific-islanders-1997]] puts it: "The colonisers' most significant ideological achievement was the invention of the Native, a category embracing all non-Europeans. The Native—singular and masculine—lacked European virtues such as application and foresight. His mind—the Native Mind—worked in mysterious ways." The concept is both a description of colonial ideology and a critical analytical tool for historians examining how colonial knowledge produced the subjects it claimed only to describe.

## Definition and Scope

The "Native" was an invented singular figure constructed by colonial officials, missionaries, planters and their ideological apparatus. Its features were:

- **Universal masculine default**: "The Native—singular and masculine" erased the diversity of gender, language, polity, and culture across hundreds of distinct Island societies.
- **Deficiency framing**: The Native lacked "European virtues such as application and foresight." Australia's *Official Handbook of the Territory of New Guinea* (1937) stated: "it goes without saying that the natives have no expressions for ideas quite unknown to them, such as gratitude, chastity, modesty, humility, &c."
- **The Native Mind**: A pseudo-psychological construct positing a fundamentally alien cognition. The Methodist mission chairman in Fiji (1923) cautioned a colleague to "take the NATIVE MIND into consideration" when delegating any responsibility to Fijians.
- **Racial extinction trope**: The first Resident Commissioner of the Solomon Islands "was convinced that Melanesians were doomed." This extinctionism justified plantation development over village production and undergirded decades of paternalist policy.
- **Extension — the Native Mother**: A gendered sub-category invented to explain depopulation through deficient maternal instinct rather than colonial disruption. See [[pacific-depopulation]].

The concept operated across colonial powers: British, French, German, Japanese and Australian administrators each deployed cognate versions. Japanese designated Micronesians as "third-class people (*santo kokumin*)" on a racial hierarchy below Koreans, Okinawans and Japanese themselves; a naval officer described them as having "no concept of progress" and "no sense of industry or diligence."

## Colonial Ethnocentrism as Overlay

[[denoon-cambridge-pacific-islanders-1997]] situates European racial prejudice as an overlay on pre-existing Islander ethnocentrisms: "Colonialism is perhaps better seen as the interaction of many competing ethnocentrisms, with European racial prejudice forming an overlay." Melanesian village ethnocentrisms — suspicion of those outside one's kin group, contempt for their customs — were structurally similar to imperial racial attitudes; the difference was "technology and organisation, accidents of history which conferred immense advantages on one side."

Colonial governments exploited Islander ethnocentrisms through divide-and-conquer: "kiaps extended the reach of government and 'friendly tribes' threw in their lot with Europeans against old enemies." Planters deliberately mixed recruits from different language groups "in order to forestall agitation." The invention of the Native thus depended on, while simultaneously denying, Islander diversity.

## Ideological Functions

1. **Legitimation of rule**: If the Native lacked foresight and rationality, governance by Europeans was self-evidently beneficial.
2. **Labour mobilisation**: Head taxes, indenture systems, and restrictions on Islander cash-cropping were all justified by the Native's alleged inability to participate as an equal in the cash economy. An official in Fiji observed that "even two generations would not suffice to 'see a nation of careless children changed to a nation of workers.'"
3. **Land dispossession**: Colonial assumptions about "waste and vacant" land — the legal mechanism of dispossession in New Caledonia, the Solomon Islands, and elsewhere — rested on the premise that Islanders did not rationally use or own their territory.
4. **"White prestige"**: Europeans "expected each other to keep social distance from the Native and to uphold 'white prestige'. Almost all Europeans believed themselves superior in intelligence, character and ability, and thought nothing was worse than Going Native."

## Limits and Islander Agency

The chapters also document systematic Islander resistance to the Native category:
- Village cooperatives (e.g. the Tonga Ma'a Tonga Kautaha, 1909), though suppressed, demonstrated Islander economic rationality.
- Tolai entrepreneurs built copra driers; Karkar Island Lutheran teachers planted coconuts and managed their own plantations in the 1930s.
- The [[mau-movement]] in Western Samoa directly contested New Zealand's paternalist premise that Samoans were "political incompetents."
- Circular labour migration gave Islanders choices: "When copra prices plummeted with the Great Depression, Solomon Islanders ceased producing copra and grew more food."

## Historiography

The concept is central to the interpretive frame of [[denoon-cambridge-pacific-islanders-1997]] (chapters 7–8). It draws on and converses with:
- Raymond Firth's earlier usage in Pacific anthropology.
- The literature on colonial discourse (Said's *Orientalism*; Stoler's work on colonial knowledge).
- Pacific-specific work: Macnaught, *The Fijian Colonial Experience*; Keesing and Corris, *Lightning Meets the West Wind*.
- Vicki Lukere's work on the "Native Mother" (PhD thesis, ANU, 1997) and Jolly, "Other Mothers" (on Fiji and Vanuatu).

The concept is contested: defenders of British indirect rule (e.g. Scarr, *Fiji: A Short History*) argue the system reflected genuine concern for Indigenous welfare; critics (Lai, *Broken Waves*; Gillion, *The Fiji Indians*) argue it protected settler interests under a humanitarian veneer.

**Resolution status**: Open — the debate about whether any colonial "trusteeship" genuinely served Islander interests continues in Pacific historiography, complicated by the diversity of colonial regimes and Islander responses.
