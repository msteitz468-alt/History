# The Origins of the English Parliament

The **English parliament** originated in the thirteenth century as the great council of the
realm acquired a name, an institutional core, and — decisively — **elected representatives
of the localities**. [[abulafia-ncmh-v5-2000|NCMH V]] (D.A. Carpenter, Ch. 12) treats its
emergence as "the central constitutional development" of the thirteenth-century English
state, the framework out of which the late-medieval "parliamentary tax-based state" grew.

## From Great Council to Parliament

In one sense parliament was "simply a new name for an old institution" — the assembly of
king and great men that dated to Anglo-Saxon times. Two things were new. First, the meetings
gained a more formal **institutional core** with the creation in the 1230s of a sworn inner
royal council. Second, and more importantly, they gained a "**novel political power**" from
the king's increasing dependence on **extraordinary taxation**, which in practice required
the common consent of the realm. The twelfth-century kings had not needed such taxes;
**[[henry-iii-of-england|Henry III]]**, by contrast, "came again and again to parliament to
beg taxation, and, again and again, was refused it unless parliament could choose his chief
ministers." Refusal of supply was parliament's great lever.

## The Coming of the Commons

The representative element developed in stages:

- **1254** — two knights elected per county summoned to say what tax the counties would
  grant (the answer: none).
- **1258–1259** — the reforming **Provisions of Oxford and Westminster**, driven partly by
  the lesser tenants-in-chief and gentry, addressed local grievances.
- **1264** — **[[simon-de-montfort|Simon de Montfort]]** ordered each county to elect two
  knights to represent "all of the county."
- **1265** — Montfort summoned knights **with burgesses from the towns** — "the first
  meeting of the embryo House of Commons."
- **1290–1295** — under **[[edward-i-plantagenet|Edward I]]**, knights and/or burgesses
  were summoned to the tax-granting parliaments; after the war emergency of the 1290s the
  pattern was "set," and the Commons "had arrived."

Underlying this was the rising **independence and importance of the gentry** — the county
knights and the wider class of "esquires" who dominated local government office. Once the
shires were formally represented (from 1290), parliaments could no longer concede taxation
on the realm's behalf through the haphazard attendance of a few magnates. Edward, a "master"
at managing the new assembly, secured the great tax of 1290 by, among other concessions,
**expelling the Jews** (see [[edward-i-plantagenet|Edward I]]).

## Significance and Limits

Parliament fused the organs of government (household, the courts of king's bench and the
bench, the exchequer, the council) with the realm's major interest groups, and made the
king's power depend on **consent**. This "completed the thirteenth-century refashioning of
the English state." Yet it was an exclusive consensus: it rested on the exploitation of the
peasantry, who "probably gained little" from reforms that spoke of helping "the people," and
it institutionalised the exclusion of the Jews. The principle invoked — the Roman-law maxim
"**what touches all shall be approved by all**" — gave consent an ideological charter, but
participation was confined to the propertied.

## Historiography

J.R. Maddicott's work is central. Debate turns on whether parliament's representative
character was driven primarily by **fiscal necessity** (the king needing taxes) or by
**political ideology** and the genuine rise of the gentry; and on how far Montfort's
summonses of 1264–1265 were decisive precedents versus the routinisation under Edward I
after 1290. Carpenter stresses that the **key point of change** came when Edward needed
regular taxation in the 1290s.
