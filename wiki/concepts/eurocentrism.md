## Definition and Scope

Eurocentrism is the (often unexamined) assumption that European or Western
experience is the norm, the standard, or the engine of world history — and that
developments elsewhere are significant chiefly insofar as they resemble, precede,
or contribute to Western developments. In its sharpest form, it is the assumption
that "if it didn't happen in Europe or the West, it probably wasn't original or
significant or influential."

Luke Clossey (Ch. 6) explores Eurocentrism through the world history of belief,
knowledge, and language. He notes that there has been little genuine "world
history" of ideas: "Most of our books about knowledge are in fact about Euro-
knowledge, rather than about the Wider World's ethno-knowledges." The very term
"ethno-knowledge" implicitly distinguishes local traditions from "knowledge"
pure and simple — which is tacitly assumed to be European and universal.

## How Eurocentrism Operates

Clossey assesses Eurocentrism across four "worldish" topics:
1. **Hominization** (how our ancestors became human) — even this field carries
   subtle Eurocentrism because so many who study it are European/Western (a theme
   echoed in the history of Paleolithic archaeology; see [[behavioral-modernity]]).
2. **The Axial Age** — the idea of a simultaneous spiritual transformation
   across Eurasia.
3. **The European Scientific Revolution** — the assumption that European science
   "colonized" world thought.
4. **Secularization / religious decline** — the assumption that religion is in
   decline in the modern world.

In all four, Eurocentrism survives in subtle and not-so-subtle forms despite
growing awareness of its limitations. Even non-Eurocentric world history is
hard to imagine without simply adopting another "centrism."

## Historical Roots

- **Enlightenment and 19th century**: Hegel, Marx, and Weber placed Europe at
  the center of planetary visions; "the rest of the world was seen as too far
  behind the Western engine to be seriously studied as a guide or reference."
- **Professionalization**: The professionalization of history on European models
  heightened the sense of European exceptionalism. Even critics of the West
  (e.g., Nehru's *Glimpses of World History*) often treated the West as
  paradigmatic of historical processes in general.
- **Marxist historiography**: Communist societies retained the vision and spirit
  of Marx's Eurocentric account of world history.

## Twentieth-Century Critiques

- Oswald Spengler (declining West as one civilization among many)
- Arnold Toynbee (multiple distinct civilizations)
- William McNeill (connections between civilizations)
- The Négritude movement and world-systems scholarship
- **Edward Said**, *Orientalism* (1978) — the foundational text linking
  representation and power in European accounts of Asia and Africa
- **Dipesh Chakrabarty**, *Provincializing Europe* (2000)
- **Eric Wolf**, *Europe and the People Without History* (1982)
- Joseph Needham's work on Chinese science (China possibly the world's
  technological leader until the 15th century — see [[technology-and-innovation]])

These had limited impact on mainstream historiography until after the collapse
of Communism, when the breakdown of Cold War divisions gave new impetus to
research on global processes and connections.

## Eurocentrism and the modern (CWH VII)

[[mcneill-cwh-v7-2015|CWH VII]] makes anti-Eurocentrism a working method for the
post-1750 world. Danielle Kinsey (Ch 13) identifies a **"pervasive Eurocentrism
embedded in the concepts of modernity and modern imperialism"**: too often modernity
is ascribed only to Western developments and "modern imperialism is collapsed down
into European imperialism," casting European empires as the exceptional deliverers of
global modernity to a passive "Rest" — a story shared, she notes, by celebrants
(Ferguson) and critics alike. She draws on Said's *Orientalism* (and its own critique
for over-emphasizing European representations) and Chakrabarty's "provincializing
Europe," and offers **"multiple modernities"** as a corrective. Crucially, Kinsey
observes that the **discipline of history and its archives were themselves shaped by
Enlightenment thought**, making over-emphasis on European action "difficult to avoid."
Elsewhere the volume's anti-Eurocentrism is structural: Sugihara's
[[industrialization|multipolar industrialization]], Wong's [[self-strengthening]],
and the late/contingent [[great-divergence|Great Divergence]] all displace Europe as
the sole engine of the modern. Arend (Ch 11) supplies the countervailing caveat: the
**modern [[international-law|international legal system]] genuinely is a Western
creation** that globalized with the sovereign-state system — a reminder that
naming European origins is not always Eurocentric distortion.

## Emic vs. Etic

"Eurocentrism" is an etic, critical term applied retrospectively by (mostly late
20th-century) scholars to characterize earlier scholarship. It was not a self-
description. Clossey notes the symmetry: a non-Eurocentric history would simply
adopt another "centrism" — he cites the imagined *Beijing History of the World*
(published "in the year 2174") as a thought experiment.

## Limitations of the Critique

- Anti-Eurocentrism can itself become formulaic, substituting one centrism for
  another or flattening real differences in historical trajectories.
- The world/global history field that critiques Eurocentrism remains
  overwhelmingly Anglophone and institutionally concentrated in the US and UK —
  the critique has not escaped the condition it names.
- Distinguishing legitimate attention to European developments (which were, in
  some periods, genuinely globally consequential) from Eurocentric distortion
  is analytically difficult.
