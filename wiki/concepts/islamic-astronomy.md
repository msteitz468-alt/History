# Islamic Astronomy

The mathematical astronomy (*ʿilm al-hayʾa*) developed in the Islamic world from the 8th century — the
most sophisticated astronomical tradition between Ptolemy and Copernicus. Built initially on Greek and
Indian inheritances absorbed through [[the-translation-movement|the translation movement]], it rapidly
moved beyond them: Muslim astronomers refined observational technique in great observatories (Baghdad,
Maragha, Samarqand), compiled the *zīj* astronomical tables, and — crucially, in George Saliba's account
— mounted a sustained **theoretical critique of Ptolemy**.

The objection was not observational but philosophical-mathematical: Ptolemy's equant violated the
principle of uniform circular motion. To repair it, astronomers of the [[maragha-school|Maragha school]]
devised new theorems — above all the [[tusi-couple|Ṭūsī couple]] of [[nasir-al-din-tusi|Naṣīr al-Dīn al-Ṭūsī]] and
the lunar and planetary models of [[ibn-al-shatir|Ibn al-Shāṭir]] — that reproduced Ptolemy's predictions
with physically consistent models. These same mathematical devices reappear in
[[islamic-science-and-copernicus|Copernicus]], a central thread of the argument that Islamic astronomy
helped make the European Renaissance.
