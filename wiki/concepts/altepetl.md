# Altepetl (Mesoamerican City-State)

## Definition and Scope

**Altepetl** (Nahuatl; pl. *altepeme*) designates the **city-state** as the basic political and economic unit of the Central Mexican Symbiotic Region (CMSR) and, more broadly, of post-state [[mesoamerica-culture-area|Mesoamerica]]. In Adams’s survey synthesis of the Willey–Ekholm–Millon culture-area package, after state-level organization was established the city-state was “the basic and stable unit,” combinations of which formed kingdoms and empires (Adams 2000; CHNPA II.1 Ch.1).

The concept is **emic** in its Nahuatl label and ethnohistoric content, and **etic** when applied comparatively to earlier or non-Nahuatl polities (e.g. Classic Maya petty states, Mixtec *cacicazgos*). It should not be collapsed with the Greek *polis* or the generic [[city-state]] without noting Mesoamerican specifics (calpulli/calpultin base, tribute-empire nesting, ethnic self-identification).

## From CHNPA II.1 (Charlton Ch.11, 2000)

### Structure of the altepetl

Within the CMSR at contact, permanent agricultural communities ranged from a few households to large urban centers ([[cholula|Cholula]], [[tenochtitlan|Tenochtitlan]]–Tlatelolco). Bray (1972) is the touchstone: each city-state was a **territorially based unit of variable size and complexity**, governed by one or more hereditary rulers, including rural dependencies; the government and tribute center sat in a town or city with major temples, marketplace, palace, and occupational specialists. An altepetl was usually associated with a dominant self-identified ethnic group, though it often encompassed subordinate ethnic or linguistic groups (Charlton 2000).

Integrative hierarchy above the household:

| Level | Role |
|---|---|
| *Calpultin* (barrios/wards) | Local corporate neighborhoods |
| **City-state (altepetl)** | Primary political–economic integration |
| Confederation | Temporary alliances; usually little tribute extraction |
| Tribute empire | Higher-order incorporation of city-states via conquest |

Alliances and confederacies were typically temporary without significant tribute; military conquest involved tribute payment by the vanquished.

### City-state economy

City-states were also the **basic economic units** of the CMSR in A.D. 1519. Commoners produced basic foods and craft products supporting themselves and the nobility. Some products moved as tribute toward [[tenochtitlan|Tenochtitlan]]; other goods entered the market system as tribute redistributions or [[aztec-economy|pochteca]] trade items. Economic variability among city-states reflected history, location, demography, and relations with the Aztecs—yet an integrated multi-scalar system (local, regional, centralized) linked urban and rural settlements through interdependent tribute- and market-based relationships (Charlton 2000).

### Post-Tula devolution and reaggregation

Charlton frames A.D. ~1150–1519 as a classic secondary-state trajectory: breakup of the Tula macrostate → **devolution into city-states** → reaggregation into the Aztec macrostate. After Tula’s fall (a process more than a single event, c. 1150/1200), multi-ethnic communities and small-scale regional polities filled the CMSR. By ~1250, militaristically competitive city-states were present; unlike the fortified hilltop epi-Teotihuacan centers ([[xochicalco|Xochicalco]], [[cacaxtla|Cacaxtla]], Teotenango), Early Aztec city-states were generally not hilltop fortresses—warfare remained small-scale and fortification was not a major settlement-location factor (Charlton 2000; cf. Bray, Brumfiel, Calnek).

**Period 2 (1250–1350):** community building, land reclamation, population growth; Brumfiel’s “score of small, autonomous, internally unstable and mutually hostile political domains.” **Period 3 (1350–1428):** Acolhua and Tepaneca expansion on a grander but still fragile scale; until Azcapotzalco’s fall, hegemony rested on an “unstable complex of payoffs and alliances” (Brumfiel). **Period 4 (1428–1519):** Triple Alliance victory over the Tepaneca enabled durable expansion; Tenochtitlan partly solved leadership and Basin-internal revolt problems and, using the city-state model, effectively transformed itself into a **Basin-wide city-state** while leaving local dynasties in place beyond the core (Charlton 2000).

### Triple Alliance and the nested altepetl

By 1519 nearly all southern CMSR city-states (and most northern ones) lay under the Triple Alliance confederacy headed by Tenochtitlan, with Tlacopan and Texcoco as allied city-states. Administrative integration of conquered areas was weak: extensive **indirect control**, local dynasties retained as administrators, *calpixque* coordinating tribute deliveries—further structural incorporation unusual (Charlton 2000). Independent city-states and confederacies (Cholula, Huejotzingo, Tlaxcala, etc.) and the western [[purepecha|Purépecha/Tarascan]] empire remained outside or on the frontier of this nest.

Settlement signature of the empire: Triple Alliance capitals (Texcoco, Tlacopan, Tenochtitlan–Tlatelolco) were founded on ground with little or no previous occupation—possibly a break with earlier administrative models—while many new villages and hamlets also appeared on previously unoccupied land (Charlton 2000).

## Relation to Earlier Mesoamerican Polities

Adams’s culture-area summary treats the city-state pattern as pan-Mesoamerican once states existed: Classic Maya emblem-glyph polities (modal ~2,000 km²), Mixtec *cacicazgos*, and Postclassic Central Mexican *altepeme* are historically distinct but structurally analogous stable building blocks. The [[mesoamerican-state-formation|state-formation process]] repeatedly reverts to this grain after imperial collapses ([[teotihuacan-collapse-550ce|Teotihuacan]], fall of Tula).

## Colonial central Mexico (Cline, CHNPA II.2 Ch. 16)

Sarah L. Cline’s survey of colonial central Mexico makes the **altepetl** the hinge of Conquest continuity: the basic precontact political unit (Nahuatl “water and hill”) was a province-size city-state ruled by dynastic lords (*tlatoque*, sing. *tlatoani*), often with a central urban complex and subordinate outlying complexes. Simplest form: single dynastic *tlatoani*; complex altepetl had multiple *tlatoque* and divided rule. Subunits called *calpulli* or more commonly **tlaxilacalli** (often 2, 4, 6, or 8), each with some political hierarchy and unique religious cult; kinship and residence likely key to membership; ethnic minorities often clustered residentially. Elite headed by male dynastic *tlatoani* with hereditary *pipiltin* as advisors/functionaries; elite women (*cihuapipiltin*) more important for noble family linkages within/across altepetl than as rulers (Cline 2000).

**Tribute empire nesting.** The Triple Alliance empire was many conquered altepetl that kept considerable internal cohesion but paid tribute and acknowledged subordination — inherent instability as subordinates sought autonomy. Independent **Tlaxcala**, under regular Triple Alliance attack, allied with the Spaniards and reaped early colonial benefits; its status as a modern Mexican state is a legacy of alliance with Cortés (Cline).

**Colonial embedding, not replacement.** In central Mexico, [[encomienda]] practice directed tribute and labor of a given altepetl to a single encomendero; initially internal tribute/*coatequitl* mechanisms continued, with a Spaniard rather than the Triple Alliance as ultimate recipient. From the colonial native viewpoint, the altepetl remained the **highest indigenous-dominated organization**; early colonial altepetl could pursue localism because Spaniards preferred not to disturb structures producing taxes and labor (Cline). By mid-sixteenth century altepetl were remodeled as Spanish-style towns: main settlement **cabecera**, outlying units **sujetos** — Spaniards over-emphasized the central urban core and saw outlying components as subordinate where natives might see them as coequal. In Nahuatl documents from *ciudades*, the Spanish term was used; in *pueblos*, documents continued to call the polity **altepetl** (Cline).

**Gobernador and cabildo.** Political towns headed by a *gobernador* from mid-1540s/early 1550s (early on usually the dynastic *tlatoani*, but not dynastic succession — Spaniards could remove the recalcitrant). Cabildos by late 1550s/early 1560s with Spanish-style titles (alcaldes, regidores, alguaciles, escribanos keeping alphabetic Nahuatl records) plus officials with Nahuatl titles (e.g. *tlalpouhqui*). Outward form Spanish, workings native: often more alcaldes/regidores than the Spanish model (each component unit represented); offices rotated by subunit rankings; fewer distinctions between former and current officeholders. Elections mandated by Spanish law; power apportionment and rotation often followed native practice — well documented for Tlaxcala (rotation among four component parts); disputed elections studied in the Cuernavaca region. Even at the colonial end, town governments vigorously pursued internal disputes and defended towns against Spanish or indigenous outside encroachments (Cline).

**Religious mapping.** Altepetl → doctrina seat; outlying settlements → visitas with nonresident clergy; each town and each *tlaxilacalli* received a Christian patron saint whose name entered the place name (e.g. Culhuacan → San Juan Evangelista Culhuacan) (Cline; see [[spiritual-conquest-and-colonial-church]]).

**Land and documentation.** Prehispanic and colonial Nahuatl land categories (*tlatocatlalli*, *calpollalli*, *pillalli*, *altepetlalli*, etc.) and town-government regulation of disputes continued; late colonial **primordial titles (*títulos*)** (none known before ~1650) establish legality of altepetl territorial claims amid population recovery and land pressure — composed with the Spanish legal system in mind but lacking standing to prove ownership (Cline). Full regional narrative: [[central-mexico-indigenous-colonial]].

## Evans & Webster 2001 (Berdan; Evans; Smith)

Frances F. Berdan’s Aztec Culture and History entry, Susan Toby Evans on Chiconautla, and Michael E. Smith’s Postclassic overview in [[evans-webster-archaeology-ancient-mexico-central-america-2001|Evans & Webster 2001]] add social structure inside the altepetl and Late Postclassic counts:

- Fundamental division of **nobles** vs **commoners** (*macehualli*) by birthright (plus extraordinary achievement early on). Nobles controlled land/labor/sumptuary display/polygyny/two-story houses; *tlatoani* selected from nobility; noble boys educated in *calmecac*; commoner boys martial training in *telpochcalli*; both sexes learned ritual song/dance in *cuicacalli*. Battlefield ennoblement increasingly curbed as polygynous noble progeny competed for elite posts. Intermediate luxury artisans and *pochteca* merchants accumulated wealth without full noble rights; *pochteca* also state spies/envoys (Berdan).
- City-states provided the context for economic specialization and the worship of particular patron deities; the Basin heartland mixed long-term Otomi, Culhuacan/Toltec-associated groups, successive Chichimec immigrants including the Mexica (reportedly last), and late luxury artisans from south/east Mexico — unified by Nahuatl as lingua franca and shared stratification, economy, religion, warfare, and apparel conventions with local hallmarks (Berdan).
- **Chiconautla** (NE edge of Lake Texcoco): Late Postclassic urbanized city-state capital (*altepetl*) with resident *tlatoani*, subject to Texcoco in the fifteenth century; important break-of-bulk point on a major trade route between [[tenochtitlan|Tenochtitlan]] and the Gulf lowlands (Evans).
- Smith: **150–200** Aztec city-states in the Late Postclassic; nobility roughly **2–5%**; commoners organized in *calpulli*; empire as Mexica–Acolhua–Tepanec alliance with Mexica dominant, Basin reorganization for loyalty/tribute, outer provinces combining tribute + markets, frontier alliances against strong enemies.

## Limitations

- *Altepetl* is primarily a Central Mexican ethnohistoric category; projecting it unchanged onto Formative chiefdoms or Classic Maya *ajaw* polities is analogical, not lexical.
- Size, ethnic composition, and degree of market vs tribute integration varied enormously.
- Archaeology and native history often diverge on exact city-state counts, boundaries, and ceramic–political correlations (Charlton’s method: treat history and archaeology as alternative portrayals).
- Colonial cabecera/sujeto remodeling and Spanish town ranks can mask multi-*tlatoani* coequality still operative in native practice (Cline).
