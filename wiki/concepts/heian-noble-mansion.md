## Definition

The Heian noble mansion, designated by modern scholars as *shindenzukuri* (literally "dwelling hall construction") from the name of its principal building, the *shinden* (dwelling hall), was the characteristic domestic architecture of the Heian court nobility from the ninth through twelfth centuries. No examples survive above ground — all were wooden and were consumed by fire or war in subsequent centuries — but abundant literary, pictorial, and scanty architectural evidence (in religious structures outside Kyoto) has allowed modern scholars to reconstruct their plans with reasonable confidence. The [[cambridge-history-japan-v2-heian]] notes that "it is unlikely that a resurrected Heian courtier would have much difficulty in identifying [scale models in Japanese museums] as the species of house in which he had once lived."

*Shindenzukuri* is both an emic and etic term: it names the style retrospectively but derives from a vocabulary the period itself used for domestic space.

## Architectural Features

The basic construction technique was shared with common Japanese domestic architecture: rectangular, single-story, post-and-beam structures with unpainted surfaces, gable or hip roofs (plank or cypress-bark shingled; tiles only on roof ridges), and plank floors elevated several feet above the ground on posts. Interior space was not divided by permanent partitions or walls. Instead, the configuration of post alignment, supplemented by curtains, blinds, and sliding or freestanding screens, created:

- A large central chamber (*moya*) at the core
- Oblong "eave chambers" (*hisashi*) on four sides
- Open verandas running the entire length and breadth of the building beyond the eave chambers
- The eave chambers divided from verandas by latticework partitions, usually opened or removed during the day

Furnishings were minimal: armrests, screens, oil-lamp stands, occasional cabinets and tables, curtained daises for sleeping, and charcoal braziers (the only artificial heat source). Living took place on bare plank floors cushioned by mats. There were no dedicated rooms; sleep, meals, and daily activities occupied the same undifferentiated space.

## Layout of a Senior Noble Mansion

A fully developed senior-noble establishment occupied a site of up to about 3.5 acres (roughly one city block) in the northeastern part of the capital near the imperial palace gates. The plan centered on the *shinden* (dwelling hall), which faced south toward a principal courtyard. Around it were "opposed houses" (*tai no ya*) on the east, west, and north, each connected to the *shinden* by roofed hallways (*watadono*). The opposed houses had rectangular "corridor wings" (*ro*) extending from their corners at right angles. A garden with man-made ponds and hills occupied the southern third of the site.

The [[cambridge-history-japan-v2-heian]] describes the best-documented example, Fujiwara no Sanesuke's Ononomiya mansion (c. 1030), in detail:

- Main site approximately 400 feet square in the northeastern city, with subsidiary properties across bounding streets on all four sides
- Central *shinden* and three opposed houses (east, west, north) connected by roofed hallways
- The largest structure measured approximately 80 by 50 feet; the eastern and western opposed houses about 80 by 40 feet; the northern opposed house about 70 by 40 feet
- Total floor space in main buildings approximately 13,000 square feet; hallways and corridor wings added another 5,000–6,000 square feet
- The southern portion of the site (roughly a third) devoted to gardens with ponds (the largest broad enough for a banquet island), smaller ponds, hills, and two Buddhist chapels
- Three gates in the encircling wall: west (the main formal entrance, a "quadripedal" roofed passageway), east, and north

## Spatial Functions

The allocation of space within the mansion reflected the distinction between private everyday life and public ceremonial life:

- **Private space** (daily living): the central chamber and the northern and eastern eave chambers of the *shinden*, together with the northern and eastern opposed houses
- **Ceremonial/formal space**: the western side of the *shinden* and the western opposed house and its corridor wings; the courtyard south of the *shinden* was used for ceremonies (archery, kickball) and on great occasions (imperial visit, marriage of a daughter) the central chamber itself was opened
- **Household staff space**: the corridor wings (*ro*) of the opposed houses, containing the attendants' office (*saburaidokoro*), escorts' office (*zuijindokoro*), administrative office (*mandokoro*), repairs office (*shuridokoro*), servants' office (*zoshikidokoro*), pages' office (*kodoneridokoro*), kitchens (*daibandokoro*), and pantry (*zensho*)
- **Ladies-in-waiting**: the roofed hallways (*watadono*), outfitted with screens and curtains as living quarters
- **Subsidiary uses**: subsidiary properties across bounding streets housed family members, household staff, storehouses (*mikuramachi*), and workshops

The northern opposed house was the conventional location for a nobleman's principal wife and small children. At Ononomiya, Sanesuke's daughter Chifuru occupied the eastern opposed house after her marriage, living there with her husband in a quasi-independent subhousehold with its own kitchen and offices.

## Density and the Public Style of Living

The mansion was densely populated. At Ononomiya, the permanent population included family members, ladies-in-waiting, servants, and miscellaneous attendants — "counted in three figures" — plus a daily stream of visiting kinsmen, officials, and monks with their own companions. "The chambers, corridors, and wings of Ononomiya must have been constantly alive with what to modern sensibilities might seem veritable hordes of people."

This density produced what the [[cambridge-history-japan-v2-heian]] calls "a public style of living." Privacy was minimal: couples' sexual relations were protected only by curtains while attendants slept nearby. To be completely alone at night was rare enough to be frightening.

The openness of the architectural plan reinforced the social effects of density. The physical structure itself produced the constant presence of others that characterized court life.

## Garden and Religious Space

The garden was not merely decorative but served multiple functions: contemplation and strolling, occasional banquets and entertainment, and access to the mansion's Buddhist chapels. At Ononomiya the larger chapel had monks' quarters, a kitchen, and bathing facilities, functioning independently; Sanesuke used it also as a private retreat and possibly as a library. A twelfth-century source quotes a fictional narrator's description of the chapel: "In the Buddha Hall itself, there are innumerable golden images, and the receptacles in front of them always contain thirty *koku* [about sixty bushels] of rice ... Those are the only ways of approaching it [by path through a park, or rowing across the lake]."

Buddhist chapels in noble gardens were a characteristic feature of mid- and late-Heian aristocratic life, part of the broader "nobilization of Buddhism" that made Buddhist monks omnipresent in the life of the court.

## Storehouses and Workshops

Subsidiary properties at senior-noble mansions contained storehouses (*mikuramachi*) holding everyday necessities — food, cloth, clothing, paper, dyestuffs, household furniture, dishes, utensils — as well as luxury items: aromatics, precious metals, religious statuary, Buddhist scriptures, medicines, ritual ware, heirlooms, and musical instruments. Workshops associated with noble mansions included those of seamsters, hatters, pictorial artists, lacquerworkers, carpenters, joiners, metalworkers, and metal casters (the last called in only as needed).

By the eleventh century, senior-noble households regularly included resident artisans who had migrated from the contracting government palace workshops, creating private centers of craft production that paralleled and eventually supplanted the palace ateliers.

## Residential Mobility and Fire

Heian nobles changed residences frequently. The divorce of residence from income and status, the prevailing pattern of uxorilocal marriage, and the constant threat of fire produced what the [[cambridge-history-japan-v2-heian]] characterizes as "a high degree of residential mobility." Fujiwara no Michinaga owned or controlled seven houses in the capital (three acquired through marital connections, four otherwise) plus villas at Katsura and Uji, and is known to have lived in most of them. The Emperor's Residential Compound (*Dairi*) burned fourteen times between 960 and 1082.

Fire was the characteristic enemy of the *shindenzukuri* mansion. Its wooden construction, curtains and paper screens as partitions, and open flame for light, heat, and cooking made conflagration nearly inescapable. No original Heian noble mansion survived intact; the evidence comes entirely from literature, painting, and inference.

## Historiography

The recognized scholarly authority on *shindenzukuri* is Ota Seiroku, *Shindenzukuri no kenkyu* (Tokyo: Yoshikawa kobunkan, 1987). The social history of the mansion as a site of domestic life draws on Yoshida Sanae's studies of Ononomiya and of Fujiwara no Sanesuke's family, based primarily on the *Shoyuki* diary.

The lack of any physical survivals makes all reconstruction partly inferential. The general reliability of modern plans and models is considered high by specialists, but some details (room use, household configuration) rest on extrapolation from literary evidence.
