## Definition and Scope

Adab designates both the cultivated personal character and the literary-intellectual corpus that defined the ideal of the Islamicate courtly class during the High Caliphate (c. 750–945 CE). In Hodgson's formulation, it was "the pattern of cultivated living which grew up around the court and in the provincial centres and was imitated yet more widely." The adib (possessor of adab) occupied a space between the Shar'i 'ulama', whose moral severity he acknowledged without fully sharing, and the Faylasufs, whose philosophical specialism he drew on selectively. Adab was, in Hodgson's terms, the dominant high culture of the High Caliphate's bureaucratic class. Source: [[hodgson-venture-of-islam-v1-1974]].

The concept is distinct from:
- [[islamicate]] civilization as a whole (adab is one strand within it)
- Shar'i scholarship (fiqh, hadith — the 'ulama' tradition)
- Falsafah (philosophy in the Greek tradition, practised by Faylasufs)

Adab was emphatically not identical to any of these, though it incorporated elements of all three. It was the synthesis that served the daily political and cultural life of the absolutist state.

## Social Basis: The Katibs

The primary carriers of adab were the **katibs** — administrative clerks and secretaries of the caliphal bureaucracy. Under the High Caliphate, this class had shifted from the old Iranian landed gentry of Sasanian times to a more urban, cosmopolitan, and socially mobile stratum drawn from Arab families, recent converts, and mercantile elements. This social fluidity gave adab a cosmopolitan character that distinguished it from its Sasanian predecessors. Adab was also eagerly cultivated by wealthy merchants and anyone who wished to participate in court society.

The adib's professional roles were those of secretary, clerk, and bureaucratic manager. The ideal of cultivated living (modelled partly on Aristotle's ethics in Arabic adaptation) gave social justification to a life of privilege in service of the absolutist state.

## Content: What an Adib Should Know

Adab imposed a comprehensive but hierarchically ranked curriculum of knowledge:

**Arabic language and literature** (highest prestige):
- Command of classical Mudari Arabic grammar and vocabulary — essential for both literary and religious reasons, since the grammarians had fixed classical Arabic as a closed system rooted in the speech of Muhammad's time
- **Shi'r** (poetry): the supreme art form, chanted publicly to conventional tunes, strictly formal in metre and convention; a major legacy of pre-Islamic Arabian culture. Key poets of the adab era include Abu Nuwas (d. c. 815), Abu-Tammam (d. c. 845), and al-Mutanabbi' (d. 965, considered the last great poet in the older style)
- **Saj'** (rhymed prose): sentences cadenced and end-rhymed; associated with state letters, sermons, and literary compilations; gradual prestige climbed through the period. Ibn-Durayd (d. 933) used it for a compilation of Bedouin anecdotes
- Knowledge of poetic rules set by the grammarians and of as many lines of verse as possible for oral citation

**History and geography** (high prestige):
- Annalistic chronicles (e.g., al-Tabari, d. 923) adapted from Syriac models
- Royal history in the Sasanian tradition: history as the record of hero-kings whose virtue or vice determined an age
- Geography for administrative, commercial, and literary purposes. Mathematical geography following Greek coordinates (al-Istakhri, d. c. 951); descriptive itinerary-geography with exotic curiosities. The Muslim educated class maintained a "more realistic image of the world than did any other civilized tradition" of the time — Hodgson
- Biographical dictionaries (e.g., al-Baladhuri's arrangement by genealogical families, d. 892)

**Natural science and Falsafah** (lower prestige, but desirable):
- Scientific curiosity expressed through a lens of belles-lettres rather than systematic inquiry: "When the adib studied 'biology', for instance, he was not so much interested in learning the structure of organisms as in finding out all the strange things that could be said of them" — Hodgson

## The Literary Tradition: Key Figures

**Al-Jahiz (d. 869)**: Mu'tazili theologian and master of Arabic prose; wrote innumerable tracts defending or attacking every party with urbanity and wit. His longer works provided philological and literary storehouses for katibs writing state letters. He is credited with winning the clerks to the Arabian tradition as a prime resource of adab, and with establishing a distinctive Mu'tazili school through literary means.

**Ibn-Qutaybah (d. 889)**: Grammarian and theological and literary critic; prose writer "in the spirit of adab, moderate exponent of new forms." Represented a strand seeking to reconcile adab cultivation with Hadith-minded piety.

**Al-Mas'udi (d. 956)**: Historian and geographer; combined historical narratives (organized by reigns) with surveys of geography, astronomy, and natural curiosity; embodied "a philosophical view of mankind which allowed him to transcend communal lines in his curiosity and in his appreciation."

**Al-Baladhuri (d. c. 892)**: Studied Muslim history from a "more secular viewpoint than his younger contemporary, Tabari"; wrote the massive history of Arab conquests arranged as regional monographs, and comprehensive genealogical histories of Muslim families with administrative detail.

## The Heritage Problem: Discontinuity and Its Consequences

Hodgson identifies the central cultural predicament of adab: unlike Europe (with Latin classics), China (with Confucian texts), or India (with Sanskrit), Islamdom had no broadly acknowledged pre-Islamic classics that could transcend communal allegiance. The reasons were structural:

- **Aramaic and Syriac** traditions of the Fertile Crescent were associated with dhimmi underlings; their secular literature was despised despite being a tacit substrate
- **Pahlavi** (Sasanian literary Persian) inspired adab substantially in spirit and content — especially the tradition of courtly wisdom, kingly virtue, and absolutism — but Pahlavi itself became inaccessible as Persian-speaking Muslims adopted Arabic script and Muslim Persian replaced Pahlavi
- **Greek** philosophy and natural science maintained prestige in translation but never achieved canonical standing in the way Latin did in Europe; even the Faylasufs could not fully replace the Greek originals
- **Biblical traditions** (Hebrew and Christian) were shunned by strict Muslims as corrupted texts; the rich humanistic literature of the Hebrew prophetic tradition was essentially excluded

The consequence: "In contrast to Europe, China, and even India... in Islamdom there came to be little direct contact with the great human works of the pre-confessional periods in the Axial Age." This "relative spiritual impoverishment" was also, paradoxically, a source of strength: a culturally self-sufficient civilization capable of expanding without losing internal cohesion.

## The Visual and Decorative Arts within Adab

Verbal arts held highest prestige, but adab culture also fostered a distinctive visual-arts tradition under court patronage. Key features:
- **Iconophobia**: 'ulama' barred figural art in religious contexts; painting was therefore a purely worldly luxury for courts and merchants, limiting its access to deeper religious-symbolic inspiration
- **Calligraphy** as major art: great calligraphers introducing new scripts were more renowned than painters; calligraphy unified diverse art media (ceramics, woodwork, metalwork, architecture) with a unifying thread of Arabic writing
- **"All-over" decorative art**: geometric and arabesque patterns, endlessly symmetrical and in principle infinitely extendable; replacing illusionistic naturalism with abstract pattern — a direct response to iconophobic constraints and an independent aesthetic achievement
- **Ceramics**: Chinese Tang porcelain imports under the Abbasids sparked a ceramic revival; unable to duplicate porcelain, Islamicate craftsmen developed their own tradition imitating its whiteness while developing independent designs
- **Architecture at Samarra**: monumental scale (the great mosque's pillared area larger than St. Peter's basilica) but hasty brick construction; decorative style deriving from Aramaic and Sasanian line-work

## Adab, Absolutism, and Political Legitimacy

Adab bore the burden of providing ideological support for the caliphal absolutist tradition. It was at the court that "the fashion was set, and the highest level of achievement displayed, in important intellectual and aesthetic dimensions of culture." The adibs were disposed to call the caliph "Shadow of God" and to subordinate religious specialists to court authority — adapting Islamic terminology to old agrarian monarchical principles. But unlike the Mazdean priesthood under the Sasanians, the adibs could not build a genuinely hierarchical religious establishment to underpin the absolutism.

The 'ulama' represented an almost diametrically opposed cultural ideal: "For the adibs, social rank and privilege were of primary importance... high culture was the end of social organization; the litterateur with his inventive gifts was the model of living. For the 'ulama', almost any sort of special privilege... was ruled out; justice among ordinary men and women was the end of social organization." The tension between these two ideals was never resolved. The adab culture flourished brilliantly but could not secure the absolutism's permanent legitimacy — and the absolutism collapsed in 945 when the [[buyid-dynasty]] occupied Baghdad.

## Historiography

Hodgson treats adab as a socio-political as much as a literary phenomenon — not merely a body of texts but a system of cultivation whose role was to legitimize the caliphal state. This framing distinguishes his approach from purely literary history (e.g., von Grunebaum's work on Arabic literature). Hodgson stresses the Islamic exclusivity that prevented older classics from achieving canonical standing — a structural argument distinguishing Islamicate civilization from all other major traditions. The collection has good coverage of the primary adab literature through the Translated Texts for Historians and Penguin/Oxford classics series.
