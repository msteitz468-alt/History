# The Ancient Novel (Greek and Roman)

The **ancient novel** is the modern label for a body of prose fiction widely written and
read during the first three centuries of the Roman Empire — the extant Greek "ideal"
romances (Chariton's *Chaereas and Callirhoe*, Achilles Tatius's *Leucippe and Clitophon*,
Longus's *Daphnis and Chloe*, Heliodorus, Xenophon of Ephesus) and the Latin novels
(Petronius's *Satyricon*, Apuleius's *Metamorphoses* / *The Golden Ass*). Drawing on
earlier epic, drama, and Hellenistic narrative, the novels nonetheless form what appears to
be a **new genre**, and they are an important comparative context for early-Christian
narrative literature such as [[luke-acts|Luke-Acts]] and the
[[apocryphal-acts-of-the-apostles|Apocryphal Acts]].

## Features Relevant to Social History

- **Female protagonists**: the romances center on young, often educated, resourceful women
  who undergo separation, danger, and reunion — unusually prominent female agency for ancient
  literature.
- **Enslaved characters**: slaves appear frequently, often as stock types ("slaves are
  deceitful and manipulative, nurses untrustworthy," in Whitmarsh's summary of the truisms),
  sorted into "good" and "bad" rather than rendered fully human — yet the genre's interest in
  reversal and disguise repeatedly blurs the slave/free line (free women mistaken for slaves;
  slaves masquerading as masters). This makes the novels a key witness to ancient ideologies
  of [[slavery-in-early-christianity|slavery]] and [[gender-history|gender]].
- **Polyphony**: the inclusion of diverse social classes is, for Bakhtin, part of the
  novel's emerging [[dialogism-and-carnival|polyphony]].

## Trajectory

Judith Perkins notes the genre effectively ceased after the fourth century and connects its
decline to the [[christianization-of-the-roman-empire|Christianization]] of the empire — as
Christian narrative forms (gospels, Acts, martyr acts, the Apocryphal Acts) absorbed and
displaced novelistic conventions.

## Limitations and Critique

As fiction, the novels cannot be read straightforwardly as social description; their slaves
and women are literary constructs serving plot and ideology. Their value is as evidence for
**representations** and assumptions — what a Greco-Roman audience took for granted about
status, gender, and truth — which is exactly how [[cobb-slavery-gender-luke-acts-2019|Cobb]]
deploys them intertextually against Luke-Acts.
