# Aksumite Kingship

## Definition

The Aksumite monarchy was an absolute, hereditary institution in which the king was the prime — and in practice unconstrained — political authority. The ideology of kingship evolved across the Aksumite period from a claim of divine or semi-divine descent to, after the Christianisation of the court under [[ezana-of-aksum|Ezana]], a royal office legitimated by Christian faith and divine favour. The institution is known primarily through royal inscriptions, coinage, and a single detailed external description of a king's ceremonial appearance (John Malalas describing king Kaleb, c. 530).

## Royal Titles

The Aksumite king bore three parallel titles in the kingdom's three main written languages:

| Language/Script | Title | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Ethiopic Ge'ez | **negus** (ngs) | king |
| ASAM-derived Ge'ez | **malik** (mlk) | king |
| Greek | **basileus** (ΒΑCΙΛΕΥC) | king |

The designation **king of kings** (negusa nagast) distinguished the Aksumite king from subordinate rulers (**basiliscoi** in Greek — "kinglets") of subject peoples. Rulers who were made tributary to Aksum retained their royal designation but in a diminutive form.

The title **atse** (widely used for Ethiopian monarchs in later centuries) is anachronistic before the fourteenth century. The first known holder was not an Aksumite king; no contemporary Aksumite source employs the term.

The standard title formula in Ezana's inscriptions ran: "king of Aksum and of [Himyar, Raydan, Saba, Salhan — Arabian territories — and Seyamo, Bega, Kasu — Horn and Nile valley territories], king of kings." The Arabian titles were by the fourth century essentially anachronistic prestige-claims, not indicators of active control; they were retained as reminders of earlier greatness (Huntingford's observation, endorsed by Phillipson).

## Divine and Semi-Divine Status

Pre-Christian Aksumite kings claimed divine or semi-divine descent through the formula "son of the unconquered/invincible **Mahrem**" — Mahrem being the war-god (equated with Ares in the Greek versions of the inscriptions). This claim appeared on stone inscriptions attributed to Ezana's predecessor Ella Amida (RIE 186) and in early inscriptions of Ezana himself. Other deities named in royal inscriptions: **Astar** (celestial), **Beher** (sea/coastal), **Meder** (earth). After Ezana's conversion to Christianity, the divine patronymic was discontinued and replaced by a human patronymic ("son of Ella Amida"). Coinage shifted from the crescent-and-disc symbol to the Christian cross.

## Succession and the Royal Clan System

Succession was patrilineal — from father to son, or between consanguineous brothers — but strict primogeniture cannot be demonstrated. Ezana followed his father Ella Amida; Kaleb followed his father; at least two of Kaleb's sons ruled successively. Rulers apparently adopted a new name on accession (a practice well attested in later Ethiopian history), which complicates the correlation of king-lists (in Ge'ez) with coin names (largely in Greek).

**Bisi names**: Each pre-Christian king, and some later ones, bore an additional title introduced by the term **bisi** (ΒICI in Greek), transliterated from the Ge'ez **be'seya / be'se** (= "man of..."). Each king had a distinct bisi name, not shared with father or son; eight total bisi names are recorded. Phillipson follows de Blois (1984) in interpreting these as matrilineal clan designators — unusual given that political authority transmitted patrilineally. Ezana's bisi name was "Alene." The system persisted into the sixth century (Kaleb's bisi name was "lzn," his son wzb's was "hdfn").

## Regalia and Ceremonial

The coinage provides the main evidence for Aksumite royal regalia from c. AD 270. Kings from Aphilas onward were shown crowned, typically over a headcloth. Some royal portraits show the king holding a sceptre-like object or spear; from the fifth century a cross or orb surmounted by a cross appears.

John Malalas (Chronicle 18.56, c. 560s) preserves an account by the Byzantine ambassador Julian of his reception by Kaleb (c. 530):

> "The emperor was naked, wearing from his belt to his loins gold-threaded linen clothing, and over his shoulders and stomach a tunic decorated with pearls, and bracelets in groups of five and gold bangles on his arms. Around his head was wound a gold-threaded linen turban with four cords on either side and a gold collar around his neck. He stood on top of four elephants which had a yoke and four discs, and upon them something like a tall carriage covered with gold leaf... The emperor of the Indians stood on high holding a small gilt shield and two spears, also gilt, in his hands. His whole senate stood likewise at arms, with flute-players providing music."

Phillipson notes that the small gilt shield and two gilt spears are corroborated by: (a) a gold coin of the fifth-century king mhdys depicting the king with these items; (b) a carved relief on Stela 4 at Aksum (fourth century) showing two spears and one shield; (c) a ceremonial iron spearhead (69 cm long, too thin for functional use) from the late-fourth-century Tomb of the Brick Arches at Aksum.

## Lower Administration

The inscriptions record that subordinate rulers of conquered peoples were made tributary and could be demoted or replaced by governors (archon in Greek; Aksumite Ge'ez equivalent unknown). By the early sixth century, Cosmas Indicopleustes records a governor named Asbas at Adulis, responsible to the Aksumite king — indicating that previously less-direct administration had been replaced by appointed officials in the most strategic areas. Tribute was collected in produce and labour before the coinage expanded; monetary payments may have supplemented this in later centuries.

## Church and State

After Ezana's conversion, the relationship between king and bishop was slow to formalise — not surprising given the pre-Christian king's claim to semi-divine status. Ezana's adoption of Christianity was accompanied by a weakening of his personal divine-descent claim. The subordination of the Ethiopian church to the Alexandrian Patriarchate — which became enshrined in tradition — may have been formalised only gradually; a "Disciplinary Canon of Nicaea" purporting to establish this legal basis is now recognised as a later forgery, with its first attestation in the seventh century.

## Historiography

Much of the evidence for Aksumite kingship is royal propaganda from the inscriptions and coinage. Very little is known about lower administration or internal political dissent. The question of dual or joint rule — raised by Constantius II's letter addressing "Ezana and Sazana" — is addressed by Phillipson as almost certainly a Roman misapprehension: Sazana (and Adepha) were likely Ezana's military commanders, not co-rulers. Joint rule was far more familiar to Romans (as *imitatio imperii*) than to Aksumites.

The king-lists (in Ge'ez) and the coin sequences frequently fail to correlate — a problem Phillipson attributes to the practice of throne-name adoption on accession and to the different scripts/languages of the two sources.
