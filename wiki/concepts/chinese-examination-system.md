# The Chinese Examination System (Keju)

## Definition and Scope

The **examination system** (科举, *keju*) was the competitive written examination by which the
Chinese imperial state recruited its **civil officials** on the basis of mastery of the
**Confucian classics** — one of the most distinctive institutions of any pre-modern state, and the
mechanism that bound the empire, the bureaucracy, and the gentry elite together for over a
millennium. As the [[cambridge-history-of-china|Cambridge History of China]] traces, its logic ran
from the [[han-dynasty|Han]] establishment of an Imperial Academy and classical curriculum, through
its crystallization as a genuine competitive examination under the **Sui (c. 605) and Tang**, to its
maturity under the **Song**, and its persistence (with the rigid "eight-legged essay") under the
[[ming-dynasty|Ming]] and [[qing-dynasty|Qing]] until its **abolition in 1905**.

## How it worked

- **Curriculum**: mastery of the **Confucian canon** (and, in the Tang, also literary composition;
  the *jinshi* "presented scholar" degree, prized for its literary demands, outranked the *mingjing*
  "classics" degree).
- **Tiers**: a ladder of examinations (local, provincial, metropolitan, and palace) selecting an
  ever-smaller elite; success brought a **degree**, eligibility for office, and immense prestige.
- **Meritocratic in principle**: open (with exceptions) to commoners and decided by anonymous
  performance, it was the nearest any pre-modern state came to recruitment by **merit** rather than
  birth — though preparation required years of leisured study, so in practice it favoured the
  landed **gentry**.

## Significance

- **State and elite fused.** The examinations channelled the ambitions of the gentry into service
  of the throne and tied elite status to the **classical curriculum** the state defined — a powerful
  force for cultural unity and political stability across dynasties and even alien conquest regimes.
- **A Confucian state.** By making the classics the gateway to power, the system entrenched
  **[[confucianism|Confucian]]** values as the empire's official ideology and the gentry as their
  bearers.
- **East Asian model.** Korea and Vietnam adopted versions; the idea of a merit examination later
  influenced Western civil-service reform.

## Emic vs. Etic

Thoroughly **emic** — *keju* was a self-conscious, theorised institution with its own vast
literature and culture (examination halls, cribs, cults of success). "Meritocracy" as the analytic
label is etic and modern.

## Limitations and Critiques

- **Meritocracy qualified**: the years of study it demanded privileged the wealthy; quotas,
  recommendation, and purchase of rank coexisted with it.
- **Intellectual conservatism**: critics (then and now) blamed the rote mastery of the canon — the
  Ming-Qing **"eight-legged essay"** — for ossifying Chinese thought and impeding practical and
  scientific learning, a charge central to late-Qing reform debates before its 1905 abolition.
