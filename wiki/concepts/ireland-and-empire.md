## Definition and Scope

"Ireland and empire" designates the field of inquiry investigating Ireland's historical relationship with British imperial power—simultaneously as a territory subject to colonization and as a source of colonizers, administrators, soldiers, and missionaries who extended and maintained the Empire. The concept occupies contested analytical ground: Was Ireland a British colony? Were Ulster Protestants settler-colonists? Is the Irish Republic "postcolonial"? These questions remain genuinely disputed and the field has been characterized by "well-tempered schisms" between empiricist historians, postcolonial theorists, and cultural critics.

As Stephen Howe argues in [[jackson-oxford-modern-irish-history-2014]] (Ch. 4), the alternative to simplistic "either-orism" on the colonial question has often been the frustrating consignment of Ireland to ill-defined residual categories—"hybrid," "ambivalent," "anomalous." Ireland "did not fit the patterns" explored by most general works on the British Empire or on British identities and imperialism.

## Historical Dimensions

### Norman to Tudor: The "Incomplete Conquest" (1170–1603)

The arrival of Richard fitz Gilbert (Strongbow) in 1170 is conventionally taken as the beginning of English involvement in Ireland, though to project modern national labels backward is anachronistic. By the early sixteenth century the English Crown had a long-established if dubiously legitimate claim to sovereignty, but the conquest remained incomplete, with Irish lords retaining substantial autonomy. Hugh O'Neill's rebellion (1595–1603) brought the conflict to a crisis, involving Spanish support, and was only crushed at enormous cost. Historians disagree on how far a coherent English/British Protestant ideology of colonization can be discerned in this period—the parallel with North American colonial ideology has been made but disputed.

### Plantation and "Complete Conquest" (1603–1691)

The era of Tudor-then-Stuart monarchy saw Ireland shift from "English" to "British" claims. The Ulster Plantation (from 1607), Cromwellian conquest (1650s), and Williamite wars (1689–91) produced the most nearly consensual scholarly verdict that Ireland occupied a colonial position—though even here historians such as Ciaran Brady and Raymond Gillespie note the coexistence of two English images of Ireland: as sovereign kingdom and as arena for colonial exploitation.

William Smyth's *Map-making, Landscapes and Memory* (2006) argues for a radical break produced by plantation; Estyn Evans's earlier work stressed continuity. James Belich's global settler colonial typology (*Replenishing the Earth*, 2009) does not fit Ireland well: Irish colonization was never "explosive" in Belich's terms, rates of growth were comparatively slow, and the analogy with North American or Australasian colonial urbanization breaks down.

### The Union and the Colonial Question (1801–1921)

With the [[act-of-union-1801]], Ireland's formally colonial status ended—in theory it became a full and equal part of the British imperial core state. Yet historians of the nineteenth century have debated hotly whether Ireland's position remained colonial, since in some respects it was more subordinated under the Union than before. Terrence McDonough identifies two political reasons why most historians avoided colonial frameworks for the 19th century: anxiety about endorsing IRA models, and a "turn to Europe" that predisposed analysts to see Ireland within the European mainstream rather than with "Third Worldish" parallels. McDonough describes a bridge of three cables across this neglect: postcolonial and Marxist literary theory, political institution studies, and economic development theory perspectives.

The specificity of 19th-century debates included: how British official "minds" and policies treated Ireland; changing racial attitudes and anti-Irish prejudice; the role of empire in Ireland's political and cultural life; and the comparative standing of Irish nationalists with anticolonial movements elsewhere in the empire. Daniel O'Connell was in the forefront of abolitionism; John Mitchel was firmly on the pro-slavery side. Irish-Indian nationalist connections were studied by several recent historians.

### Emigrants and Imperialists: The Paradox of the Diaspora

The most important dimension of Ireland's imperial relationship is also the most uncomfortable for nationalist self-presentation: the massive and multifaceted role of the Irish in building and sustaining British imperialism. The [[irish-diaspora]] supplied soldiers (Irish-born were over-represented in the British and Indian armies in the early nineteenth century, though this proportion fell steadily thereafter), senior officers (Wellington, Montgomery), colonial administrators, Catholic missionaries, Protestant evangelists, merchants, and settlers across the empire from India to Australia to South Africa.

Don Akenson emphasizes: "it is misleading to generalize about 'the Irish' in the colonial diaspora." One must disaggregate by confession, class, region, and destination—the Ulster Protestant experience differs fundamentally from that of the Munster Catholic laborer or the Anglo-Irish Protestant administrator.

The Irish were never numerous enough to shape imperial projects according to their own desires—there was never truly an "Irish empire" in political terms. The exception of Ontario (where the Irish were the largest population group to the end of the nineteenth century) and Montserrat prove the rule. An Irish "spiritual empire" through Catholic missionary networks (from the 1830s onward) is more defensible analytically.

### Postcolonialism and Ireland (Post-1921)

After the [[irish-free-state|Anglo-Irish Treaty]] (1921), the postcolonial question took new forms. For the Republic, most commentators treated empire as a matter of the past—though a minority argued Ireland remained subject to external domination (by Britain, the USA, the EU, or global capitalism) making it "neocolonial" rather than truly postcolonial. This argument has been advanced particularly in periods of economic vulnerability.

The most influential argument came from literary and cultural scholars applying Said's *Orientalism* (1978) framework to Ireland: Seamus Deane, Luke Gibbons, Declan Kiberd, and David Lloyd argued that a "Saidian" mode of analysis was fully applicable. These claims drew "much counter-fire" from empiricist historians on the grounds of weak historical grounding and comparative analysis. Joe Cleary's more recent work has been stronger on empirical foundations.

For [[northern-ireland]], two intertwined questions dominated: whether partition and continuing union should be seen as colonialist, and whether Ulster Protestant identities were settler-colonialist. Howe's own view, argued at length elsewhere, is that "the distinctively settler-colonial features of northern Irish society largely eroded or were dismantled during the nineteenth century"—though important aspects of the region's past can be captured through plantation and settler-colonial concepts.

## State of the Debate

Howe identifies a transformation of the landscape by the early 2010s: "what were still quite recently sometimes stark, ill-tempered schisms between historians and cultural critics, empiricists and theoreticists, and revisionists and anti-revisionists, have at least partially been replaced by a more transdisciplinary, multidirectional and complex conversation" (Ch. 4, [[jackson-oxford-modern-irish-history-2014]]). The former gulf between Irish and British imperial history studies is being substantially overcome.

Global developments since 2001—9/11, the "war on terror," the "new imperialism"—have added further analytical contexts that some scholars (Jonathan Gantt on Fenianism and terrorism) have applied to Irish history, though this generates resistance from Irish historians who find the vocabulary alien.

## Key Scholars and Works

- Nicholas Canny: *Making Ireland British, 1580–1650* (2001) — leading historian of early modern colonization
- Stephen Howe: *Ireland and Empire* (2000) — the most comprehensive critical survey
- Keith Jeffery (ed.): *'An Irish Empire'?* (1996) — pioneering collection

## Collection Coverage Note

Chapter 4 of the *Oxford Handbook of Modern Irish History* (Stephen Howe, "Colonized and Colonizers: Ireland in the British Empire") provides the most comprehensive recent historiographical survey of this field. It is strongest on the post-1980s historiographical debates; coverage of the early modern period defers largely to Nicholas Canny's body of work. The question of empire in Unionist and loyalist culture is identified as underresearched.
