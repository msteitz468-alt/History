# Computability

Computability theory (also called recursion theory) is the branch of mathematical logic studying which functions or problems can, in principle, be solved by a well-defined algorithmic procedure. It was established chiefly by [[actors/turing-alan|Alan Turing]]'s 1936 paper "On Computable Numbers," which introduced the abstract **Turing machine** as a formal model of mechanical computation, and independently by Alonzo Church's equivalent lambda calculus formalism. The **Church-Turing thesis** holds that the Turing machine model captures the full extent of what can be considered "effectively calculable" by any systematic procedure — a claim not provable in the ordinary mathematical sense but universally accepted as correctly delimiting computability. Turing's paper also proved the undecidability of the **Halting Problem** — that no general algorithm can determine, for every possible program and input, whether that program will eventually halt — establishing a fundamental limit on computation years before any electronic computer was built.

## Emic and Etic

The concept is an etic analytical framework of 20th-century mathematical logic; it had no equivalent emic articulation among earlier mathematicians, since it depends on the formal machine models Turing and Church introduced.

## Significance and Limitations

Computability theory underpins the entire subsequent field of theoretical computer science, distinguishing which problems are algorithmically solvable at all from the separate (and later) question of computational complexity — how efficiently a solvable problem can be computed. It says nothing by itself about practical runtime, only about in-principle solvability.
