# The Face of Battle (Keegan's Method)

The **"face of battle"** is the experiential method of military history introduced by
[[keegan-face-of-battle-1976|John Keegan]] in 1976: the study of battle from the standpoint of the
men who actually fought it — what combat did to their bodies, why they stood or ran, and what it
felt like — rather than from the commander's map. Its organising question is the one Keegan says
conventional military history evades: **"what is it like to be in a battle?"** This page records the
method as an analytical and historiographical framework; it is, in effect, the intellectual charter
of the wiki's [[hubs/warfare/warfare-hub|warfare hub]].

## The target: the "battle piece"

Keegan's method is defined against the conventional **"battle piece"** — the set-piece battle
narrative "as old as Herodotus." Dissecting General Napier's famous account of Albuera (1811) and
then three respected modern historians (Peter Young, David Chandler, Michael Howard), he isolates
the form's recurring, falsifying conventions:

- **Impossible uniformity of behaviour.** Everyone on a side acts alike and at once: "no individual
  turns tail and runs, drops down to sham dead or stands thunder-struck at the indescribable
  horror." Real crowds of frightened men never behave so uniformly.
- **Discontinuous movement.** Armies advance, exchange fire, "carnage ensues," and the enemy is
  "suddenly… over the steep" — with no mechanism shown for *how* one mass actually drove off the
  other.
- **A two-tier caste of "leaders" and "led."** The British are named senior officers; the enemy is
  a "tumultuous crowd," a "mighty mass," a "loosened cliff" — an "absolute division of all present
  into 'leaders' and 'led'."
- **The vanishing of the dead and wounded.** In the battle piece the casualties "dematerialize as
  soon as struck down"; no one steps over bodies, and the wounded — "combatant beings no longer but
  none the less… sentient" — simply disappear from the account.
- **The reduction of all battles to types.** General-Staff history teaches "that all battles fall
  into one of perhaps seven or eight types" and obey the "immutable" Principles of War — the same
  cartographic shorthand making "the battle of Cannae, 216 B.C., and… the Falaise Gap, 1944" look
  like one thing. The university historian "cannot easily accept" this.

## The positive program

Keegan's alternative rests on a duty he places above all others — that the historian must **make up
his own mind** about what happened, rather than hide behind anecdote, anthology, or staff doctrine:

1. **Recover the experience of all participants** — "the standpoint not only of an officer, but also
   of a private soldier, a non-combatant, a neutral observer, a casualty." Let combatants speak for
   themselves where the sources allow (richest from the Napoleonic wars onward; the "voice of the
   common man" only from the First World War), but weigh that testimony critically (the
   self-flattering **"Bullfrog Effect"** of old soldiers).
2. **Reconstruct from physical evidence** — the "known defensive qualities of armour plate,
   penetrative power of arrows, dimensions and capacities of the human body, carrying power and
   speed of the horse." This is the **Sachkritik** tradition of [[delbruck-hans|Hans Delbrück]] and
   the **"Inherent Military Probability"** of A. H. Burne: walk the ground, test the mechanics,
   reject what could not physically have happened.
3. **Treat the human factors as causes, not colour** — fear, **physical compulsion** (the factor
   "almost all military historians consistently underplay"), cohesion and comradeship, leadership by
   example, drink, religious preparation, and the **fate of the wounded** are central explanations
   of why battles went as they did.
4. **Account for the bodies** — what each weapon does to flesh, and what happens to the dead and
   wounded during and after the fight, is part of the analysis, not an embarrassment to be elided.

## What the method yields (the three cases)

Applied to [[hubs/warfare/late-middle-ages/hundred-years-war/battle-of-agincourt-1415|Agincourt]],
[[hubs/warfare/long-19th-century/napoleonic-wars/battle-of-waterloo|Waterloo]], and the
[[hubs/warfare/world-wars-era/first-world-war/battle-of-the-somme-1916|Somme]], the method
overturns received pictures: at Agincourt the longbow's arrows mostly "deflected" off plate and the
killing was done by the **crush** and the archers' close butchery, not the arrow-storm; at Waterloo
the great columns broke **from the rear first**, a "flight crowd" of men who could see nothing; at
the Somme the bombardment failed and the battle was decided by **machine guns versus walking men**.
In every case the decisive mechanisms are found at the level of the human body and the small group,
not the general's plan.

## Limitations and critiques

Keegan's method is deliberately **not** operational or strategic — it largely brackets *why* each
battle was won at the level of generalship and campaign (the very questions the
[[battle-analysis-template|battle-analysis template]] also requires). It depends on a **density of
personal testimony** that thins rapidly before c. 1800 and vanishes before c. 1500, so its full
application to ancient and medieval battles is constrained (Keegan reaches Agincourt only by the
physical-reconstruction route). Later critics note the risk of a "experience-of-battle" genre that
multiplies vivid detail without explaining outcomes. The wiki's resolution is to treat the face of
battle as **one required layer** of a battle analysis — the human and mechanical conduct of the
fight — sitting alongside the operational and strategic layers, not replacing them.
