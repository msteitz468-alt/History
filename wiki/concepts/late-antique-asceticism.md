# Late Antique Asceticism (Pagan and Christian)

The great fourth- and fifth-century efflorescence of **asceticism** — the disciplined "training" (*askesis*)
of mind and body in pursuit of self-transformation — practised by Christian **monks** and polytheist
**philosophers** alike. Treated in [[cameron-cah-v13-1998|CAH XIII]] Ch. 20 (**Peter Brown**), a companion
to his chapter on [[christianization-of-the-roman-empire|Christianization]].

## A shared pool of practice, divergent meanings

Brown's organizing insight: at first glance Christian and non-Christian ascetics look alike — the same
**fasting, sexual renunciation, indifference to wealth, disregard of status**, the same **instantly
recognizable dress** (the dark *tribonion* of the philosopher, the rough *schema* of the monk). [[julian|Julian]]
scanned the roads for his philosopher-mentor Maximus in the very years the **Life of Antony** (after 356)
was sweeping the empire. But Brown insists the chapter's real subject is the **diversity of *meaning***
attached to these "commonplace ascetic practices" by different groups — a diversity later flattened by the
homogenizing *koine* of the **Apophthegmata Patrum**, Palladius' **Lausiac History**, and **Cassian's**
Institutes and Conferences (all c. 420–440), which "imposed their hindsight and uniformity" on what had
been an "age of great diversity" and intense localism.

## Askesis as a "craft of the self"

For both traditions, *askesis* was a **"universal art of the soul"** — the conviction that the human person
was **refinable**, that "profound transformations of the human person were possible" through discipline
"initiated in response to a divine calling and brought to perfection by divine aid." Brown sets this in the
wider late-Roman culture of self-fashioning: rhetors, aristocrats and occultists alike claimed authority
from a lifetime's labour on the self (the ideal of *otium*; Gregory of Nazianzus invoking leaders whose
doctrine bore "the noble seal of a flesh worn down by prayer"). Asceticism was thus **one possible form of
achievement among many** in "a tense and ambitious age" — but also a **statement**: by touching primary
bodily needs and even threatening the links (marriage, wealth, culture) by which society reproduced itself,
the ascetic "could carry in [his] own body a whole alternative world-view."

## Pagan and Christian, jostling and overlapping

Brown stresses both **rivalry and convergence**. Polytheist philosophers (Eunapius' *Lives of the
Sophists*) and Christian monks "eyed each other with curiosity and disapprobation" — the philosophers of
Panopolis telling arriving monks they brought "olives to Panopolis"; Eunapius sneering that the Goths
exploited Roman respect for grey-cloaked monks. Yet the "subtle constraints of class and a shared moral
culture" also **blurred their differences**: Egyptian papyri show the Christian hermit **Paphnutius** and
the philosopher **Sarapion** receiving near-identical letters of admiration; Synesius of Cyrene could
invoke **St Antony and Zoroaster** together as "culture heroes" of a venerable "Alien Wisdom." The
"truly creative minds" of the age "had read each other's books and watched each other's behaviour."

## Significance

This chapter recovers the **fourth century as the laboratory of asceticism** — not the moment monasticism
was founded (its roots lay earlier, its institutional triumph came in the fifth–sixth centuries) but the
period of "an unprecedented range of interpretations" of the human person clustered around a common pool
of practice. The **monk** (and the **holy man**) emerged as a new social and spiritual force — the human
heroes of the [[christianization-of-the-roman-empire|Christianization]] of the empire — even as ascetic
discipline remained, in its bare techniques, a heritage shared with the dying world of the philosophers.
Brown's refusal to reduce asceticism to "hatred of the flesh and flight from a declining world" is itself a
plank of the volume's anti-"decline" reading of late antiquity.

## Update — monasticism and the holy man, 5th–6th c. (CAH XIV)

[[cameron-cah-v14-2000|CAH XIV]] (Rousseau, Ch. 25, **monasticism**; **Peter Brown**, Ch. 26, **holy men**)
follows the ascetic movement into its mature phase, when it had become **central to Christian society**.

- **Monasticism institutionalized.** What had begun as the heroic withdrawal of Antony and Pachomius
  developed into established communities with **rules, property and episcopal oversight** — from the lavras
  and coenobia of Egypt, Palestine (the Judaean desert, Gaza) and Syria to the western foundations. Monks
  became a **political and theological force**: the monastic crowds of Constantinople, Alexandria and Syria
  were decisive in the [[council-of-chalcedon-451|Christological controversies]] (Eutyches was an
  archimandrite; monastic mobs swayed councils).
- **The holy man** (Brown's signature theme). Across the fifth- and sixth-century Christian world the
  ordinary believer was taught to expect that **somewhere in his own time and region** lived a "servant of
  God" who, through prolonged ascetic labour, had won an exceptional **closeness to God** — able to heal,
  exorcize, intercede, and "stand in the breach" so the world was not destroyed (as Barsanuphius of Gaza
  reassured his monks during the **plague of 541**). The holy man served as **patron, arbiter and channel of
  the holy** in village society — a new locus of authority outside the civic and imperial hierarchies. Brown
  notes the sources privilege the **public** holy man, underplaying the equally valued power of prayer
  ascribed to reclusive figures and to **holy women**.

This is the spiritual face of the late-antique transformation: as the [[late-roman-cities|classical city]]
and its notables faded, the **ascetic and the bishop** became the new figures of authority. Source:
[[cameron-cah-v14-2000]] (Chs 25–26, Philip Rousseau and Peter Brown).
