# Hoplite Warfare

**Hoplite warfare** — armoured spearmen (*hoplitai*, from the *hoplon* shield) fighting
shoulder-to-shoulder in the dense **phalanx** — was the characteristic mode of Greek
land battle from the seventh century onward, and a development of major social and
political as well as military significance. [[boardman-cah-v3p3-1982|CAH Vol. III, Part
3]] (Ch. 42, Hammond, on its rise; Ch. 45b, Boardman, on the equipment) treats it.

## The phalanx and its panoply

In the earlier Archaic age the best-armed champions fought at close quarters while the
mass skirmished from a distance with javelins, slings and stones; cavalry held the
advantage and wars could degenerate into protracted guerrilla fighting. The
**hoplite revolution** changed this: as rising prosperity let more men afford **iron-
tipped spears, iron swords and bronze armour**, they formed "groups of elite warriors,
developed a close formation in which they helped one another, and fought their peers in
set battles." The full panoply (devised by the mid-seventh century) comprised the
round **shield** (clamped to the forearm by the *porpax* arm-grip and a rim *antilabe*,
so a man's shield protected his neighbour's flank), a **helmet** (the all-covering but
hot-and-deaf Corinthian type, and lighter Attic, Illyrian, Cretan and other variants),
a bronze **bell-corselet** (later the lighter linen corselet), **greaves**, and the
thrusting **spear** with butt-spike (*sauroter*); the sword was secondary. Battle was
fought "by a code as conventional as an athletic contest" — Tyrtaeus's "foot to foot,
shield to shield, crest to crest... chest to chest." Equipping a hoplite was "a
complicated and expensive matter," and the supply of metal — by the state or the
individual — was a real economic problem.

## Political significance

Hammond stresses that the new mode "was a phenomenon of political significance." Because
only the **well-to-do** could afford the equipment and the training time, the rise of
the phalanx **broadened and strengthened the propertied "top layer"** while light-armed
troops lost status — shifting the meaning of *aristoi* from birth toward **wealth-and-
warfare**, and giving its members new **solidarity** (in the phalanx "neighbours had to
help one another"). It marked the transition "from the aristocrat of hereditary
qualifications to the aristocrat of wealth," with some infiltration of able men grown
rich by trade. For **[[sparta]]**, with its large body of trained, equal, professional-
soldier *Homoioi*, "hoplite warfare might have been invented specifically to ensure
their success." The decisive early example was the **battle of Hysiae (669)**, where
Argive hoplites crushed Sparta; the **[[argos|Argive]]** and Spartan struggles, and the
[[magna-graecia|western]] colonial wars, were fought this way.

## Historiography

The link between the hoplite phalanx and political change — the "hoplite revolution"
thesis — is **debated** (Starr, in the same volume, warns that "the concept of an
independent political force termed 'the hoplite class' can be misleading," since
aristocrats stood in the phalanx beside middling farmers and there is no evidence the
*kakoi* acted as an organized group). The dating of the full panoply and the gradual
versus sudden adoption of massed tactics likewise remain disputed — Boardman notes the
phalanx itself is rarely depicted (vase-painters preferred heroic duels), so much is
inferred from arms dedications, scattered images, and the poets (Tyrtaeus, Archilochus's
thrown-away shield).
