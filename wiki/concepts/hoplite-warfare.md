# Hoplite Warfare

**Cartographic / reconstruction support:** Peter Connolly's detailed illustrations of hoplite equipment and phalanx formations.

![Greek hoplite equipment and phalanx in close order (Connolly, *Greece and Rome at War*, p. ~040 area)](assets/atlases/connolly-greece-and-rome-at-war-1998/connolly-p040.png)

*Reconstruction from [[sources/connolly-greece-and-rome-at-war-1998]].*

**Hoplite warfare** — armoured spearmen (*hoplitai*, from the *hoplon* shield) fighting
shoulder-to-shoulder in the dense **phalanx** — was the characteristic mode of Greek
land battle from the seventh century onward, and a development of major social and
political as well as military significance. [[boardman-cah-v3p3-1982|CAH Vol. III, Part
3]] (Ch. 42, Hammond, on its rise; Ch. 45b, Boardman, on the equipment) treats it.

## The phalanx and its panoply

In the earlier Archaic age the best-armed champions fought at close quarters while the
mass skirmished from a distance with javelins, slings and stones; cavalry held the
advantage and wars could degenerate into protracted guerrilla fighting. The
**hoplite revolution** changed this: as rising prosperity let more men afford **iron-
tipped spears, iron swords and bronze armour**, they formed "groups of elite warriors,
developed a close formation in which they helped one another, and fought their peers in
set battles." The full panoply (devised by the mid-seventh century) comprised the
round **shield** (clamped to the forearm by the *porpax* arm-grip and a rim *antilabe*,
so a man's shield protected his neighbour's flank), a **helmet** (the all-covering but
hot-and-deaf Corinthian type, and lighter Attic, Illyrian, Cretan and other variants),
a bronze **bell-corselet** (later the lighter linen corselet), **greaves**, and the
thrusting **spear** with butt-spike (*sauroter*); the sword was secondary. Battle was
fought "by a code as conventional as an athletic contest" — Tyrtaeus's "foot to foot,
shield to shield, crest to crest... chest to chest." Equipping a hoplite was "a
complicated and expensive matter," and the supply of metal — by the state or the
individual — was a real economic problem.

## Political significance

Hammond stresses that the new mode "was a phenomenon of political significance." Because
only the **well-to-do** could afford the equipment and the training time, the rise of
the phalanx **broadened and strengthened the propertied "top layer"** while light-armed
troops lost status — shifting the meaning of *aristoi* from birth toward **wealth-and-
warfare**, and giving its members new **solidarity** (in the phalanx "neighbours had to
help one another"). It marked the transition "from the aristocrat of hereditary
qualifications to the aristocrat of wealth," with some infiltration of able men grown
rich by trade. For **[[sparta]]**, with its large body of trained, equal, professional-
soldier *Homoioi*, "hoplite warfare might have been invented specifically to ensure
their success." The decisive early example was the **battle of Hysiae (669)**, where
Argive hoplites crushed Sparta; the **[[argos|Argive]]** and Spartan struggles, and the
[[magna-graecia|western]] colonial wars, were fought this way.

## The phalanx vs. Persia (CAH IV)

The Greco-Persian wars were the **proving-ground of the phalanx against a wholly different
military system**, and [[boardman-cah-v4-1988|CAH Vol. IV]] (Chs. 9–11, Hammond & Barron)
returns to it repeatedly. At [[battle-of-marathon-490bce|Marathon]] the "bronze men" — helmet,
cuirass, greaves, shield, and a 2.4 m thrusting spear — ran through the arrow-barrage and
**overwhelmed the lighter-armed Persian infantry** (wicker shields, short spears, trousered
quilt-armour) in close combat, where Greek armour and the **2-to-3 metre reach** told. The
recurring strategic problem was **terrain**: the phalanx was matchless on broken or narrow
ground but helpless against **cavalry and mounted archers** in the open. Hence the Greeks
fought where the Persians could not deploy their horse — the **pass at
[[battle-of-thermopylae-480bce|Thermopylae]]** (where Spartan **drill** — the feigned retreat
and wheel — magnified the advantage), and the **foothills at [[battle-of-plataea-479bce|Plataea]]**,
where the whole campaign was a contest to lure the enemy onto unfavourable ground. At Plataea
the decisive act was the hoplite charge that **overran the Persian wicker-shield barricade**;
once it came to "push and stab," Mardonius' infantry, "with nothing to shield them but a
desperate courage," were no match for the disciplined, bronze-clad line. The wars thus
displayed both the **superiority of the heavy infantryman in his element** and the **structural
vulnerability** that Greek commanders had to manage by choice of ground.

## The fourth-century tactical revolution (CAH VI)

[[lewis-cah-v6-1994|CAH Vol. VI]] (Chs. 4, 6, Seager) records the narrative episodes in
which the symmetrical citizen phalanx began to be **outmanoeuvred** — the prelude to its
eventual eclipse by the Macedonian system:

- **Light infantry: Lechaeum (390).** During the [[corinthian-war-395bce|Corinthian War]]
  the Athenian general **Iphicrates** used mobile, light-armed **peltasts** (javelin-men)
  to cut to pieces a Spartan *mora* of hoplites in the Corinthia — a famous demonstration
  that the heavy phalanx could be defeated by missile troops it could not catch, and a
  spur to the growing professionalization of fourth-century armies (mercenaries, the
  Persian use of Greek infantry; see [[achaemenid-satrapy]]).
- **The oblique order and the deep column: Leuctra (371) and Mantinea (362).**
  **[[epaminondas]]** overturned the convention of the strong right wing by massing his
  Theban left **fifty shields deep** against the enemy's best troops and advancing
  **obliquely**, holding the rest of his line back, so as to "crush the serpent's head."
  This concentration of force at the decisive point broke the Spartan line at
  **[[battle-of-leuctra-371bce|Leuctra]]** (the Sacred Band of [[pelopidas]] striking home
  before the Spartans could deploy) and again at **[[battle-of-mantinea-362bce|
  Mantinea]]**. The innovation was studied by **Philip of Macedon** — a hostage at Thebes
  in these very years — and is the direct ancestor of the Macedonian oblique battle line.

These developments mark the beginning of the end of the **amateur citizen hoplite** as
the arbiter of Greek battle (see also the warfare chapter treated under
[[greek-warfare]]).

## Classical panoply, census, and the citizen levy (van Wees)

[[sources/osborne-classical-greece-2000|Osborne (ed.), *Classical Greece 500-323 BC*
(2000)]], Ch. 4 (Hans van Wees, "The city at war"), gives the fullest Classical-period
account of the panoply, its social gatekeeping, and the levy's edges. The **round
hoplite shield** was roughly 90 cm across and weighed about 7-8 kg, faced in bronze over
wood and held by a double grip; combined with helmet and optional greaves and corslet it
defined heavy-infantry equipment. Hoplite service was **compulsory for adult male
citizens meeting a property census**, and that census was often set higher than the bare
cost of the armour, because military duty was **deliberately linked to political
privilege**. On general-levy size figures, van Wees estimates that **between a third and
half** of the Athenian citizen population could afford hoplite arms. **Light-armed
citizens** (*psiloi*) accompanied every levy but were institutionally marginalized:
granting them formal military status would have handed them a political claim rivaling
the hoplite class, so as a disorganized mass they were "safely ignored."

**Training** was mostly informal everywhere except Sparta — gymnasium exercise (wrestling,
running, javelin, discus), not formation drill — though elite standing units existed,
above all the Theban **Sacred Band** (about 300 men, organized in homoerotically-linked
pairs; Diodorus 12.70.1, Plutarch *Pelopidas* 18-19). Even Spartan training emphasized
general athletic fitness over combat-specific drilling: King Agesilaus reportedly awarded
hoplite prizes for the best physique, not for combat skill.

**Cavalry** developed late and remained low-status: it was absent from the Greek
coalition against Persia, hampered by poor terrain and the lack of horseshoes, saddle, or
stirrups, and viewed by elites as less prestigious than hoplite combat. Sparta's solution
was for wealthy citizens to fund cavalry horses ridden by the physically weakest, least
honour-conscious men (Xenophon, *Hellenica* 6.4.11); Athens nonetheless built a cavalry
force reaching roughly 1,000 by the start of the Peloponnesian War, prompting Sparta to
follow suit.

**Mercenary** supply was ancient, but demand rose sharply in the fourth century — longer
and more distant campaigns, need for specialist skills (archers, **peltasts**) citizen
hoplites lacked, and the fragmentation of alliances after Sparta's hegemony collapsed in
371. Peltasts (light javelin infantry from northwestern Greece, Macedonia, and Thrace)
could devastate hoplites caught on an exposed flank; heavy infantry reportedly came to
fear them "as little children fear the bogey-man." Iphicrates built a notably successful
Athenian peltast unit during the [[corinthian-war-395bce|Corinthian War]]. Citizens
nonetheless remained suspicious of mercenary loyalty even as their tactical value was
recognized.

**Ravaging and siegecraft.** Agricultural devastation — burning crops, smashing olive
presses, destroying farmhouses, driving off livestock — was arguably the essence of
interstate Greek warfare, though its real damage was often limited because populations
evacuated and harvested in advance; campaigns were constrained to two or three weeks by
food supply unless supported by a permanent fortified garrison (**epiteikhismos**), a
tactic that became major during and after the Peloponnesian War. Sieges were less common
than pitched battles but far from rare, and most cities and forts that fell were taken by
**treason** rather than direct assault. Siege technology advanced markedly across the
period: battering rams are first attested for Greeks at the Athenian siege of Samos
(440/439 BCE), torsion catapults are first mentioned in 399 BCE (Diodorus 14.42.1, 50),
and fourth-century siege towers and heavy artillery drove a shift toward double all-stone
walls with towers.

**Religious constraints** could override military necessity: Sparta missed Marathon (490
BCE) and most of Thermopylae (480 BCE) because campaigns could not begin before the full
moon during the Carneia festival, though states found workarounds — Argos manipulated its
own calendar in 419/418 to invade Epidaurus during the Carneia (Thucydides 5.54).

**The Macedonian transformation.** Philip II's Macedon converted Greek-style warfare into
something structurally different: a professional, full-time, intensively-drilled infantry
armed with the long two-handed pike (**sarissa**, roughly 5 m or more) and a smaller
shield, fighting in a still more tightly-packed phalanx. Funded by gold mines yielding
roughly 1,000 talents a year (see [[financing-greek-warfare]]), by 338 BCE Philip could
field an army matching the combined forces of the Greek city-states arrayed against him.
Naval warfare in this period is treated separately at [[greek-naval-warfare]]; state
financing of both land and sea forces at [[financing-greek-warfare]].

## Historiography

The link between the hoplite phalanx and political change — the "hoplite revolution"
thesis — is **debated** (Starr, in the same volume, warns that "the concept of an
independent political force termed 'the hoplite class' can be misleading," since
aristocrats stood in the phalanx beside middling farmers and there is no evidence the
*kakoi* acted as an organized group). The dating of the full panoply and the gradual
versus sudden adoption of massed tactics likewise remain disputed — Boardman notes the
phalanx itself is rarely depicted (vase-painters preferred heroic duels), so much is
inferred from arms dedications, scattered images, and the poets (Tyrtaeus, Archilochus's
thrown-away shield).

## From Hall, *Archaic Greek World* (2014)

[[sources/hall-history-archaic-greek-world-2014|Hall (2014)]] rejects the classic **hoplite revolution** package that ties sudden panoply + phalanx (ca. 675–650) to tyranny and a middling egalitarian *polis*.

**Gradual panoply, delayed massed tactics.** Archaeology and art history favour piecemeal adoption: late-eighth Argos warrior grave (bronze breastplate, conical helmet); Corinthian helmet from ca. 700; greaves late eighth on Crete / common in art ca. 675; large double-grip shield first ca. 700 on Attic amphorae, against earlier Geometric Dipylon/rectangular types. Seventh-century two-spear warriors may mark transitional open fighting. The **Chigi Vase** (mid-seventh Corinthian olpê) is the earliest unambiguous opposed-rank phalanx with piper — roughly a quarter-century after critical panoply elements, consistent with aristocrats first buying heavier armour for themselves and retainers (Snodgrass gradualism). Classical kit remains heavy (~31 kg armour plus ~7 kg shield); *ōthismos* and “unwritten rules” of ritual battle may be largely post-Marathon formalizations rather than primordial.

**Revolution thesis rejected.** Aristotelian evolutionism (*Pol.* 4.10.10: cavalry aristocracies → broader heavy infantry) lacks early documentation and models fifth-century naval explanations. Problems: little independent early monarchy/specialized cavalry (Archaic “cavalry” often wealthy infantry riding to battle); **no source** says tyrants rose on a hoplite class (Polycrates ≤15 hoplites; Pisistratus club-bearers then Argive mercenaries; Aristotle’s tyrant bodyguard is foreign). Literary alternatives that find mass fighting already in Homer and link it to eighth-century demography/egalitarian *polis* are also weak: “mass” ≠ Classical massed phalanx; epic may archaize or be seventh-century in form. The modern **Pheidon–Hysiae–hoplite** chronological package is a construct without secure ancient authority.

**Status in the ranks (Tyrtaeus and after).** Phalanx “teamwork” does **not** equal middling egalitarianism even classically: best troops (*aristoi*) stand front (and rear); outside Sparta men self-equip; breastplates are rare among Olympia dedications relative to helmets/shields/greaves; Solonian *zeugitai* (≥200 *medimnoi*) are far richer than a broad “hoplite class.” Tyrtaeus frs. 10–11 centre on noble *promakhoi* (*agathoi*/*esthloi*, Heraclid lineage shame) with light-armed *laos* crouching near full-armoured men — **class chauvinism**, not homoioi equality. The Anavysos Kouros of Croesus (ca. 530), if correctly linked to its base, memorializes an aristocrat “destroyed in the front rank.” **`contrasts_with:`** Sparta-as-model-egalitarian-hoplite-state; **`concurrent_with:`** elite friction explanations of [[greek-tyranny]].

**Source:** [[sources/hall-history-archaic-greek-world-2014|Hall (2014)]], Ch. 7 (“Fighting for the Fatherland”).

## From CHGRW Part I (Sabin / van Wees / Whitby 2007)

[[sabin-van-wees-whitby-cambridge-history-greek-roman-warfare-2007|CHGRW]] supplies the
most systematic multi-author treatment of panoply, social composition, battle process,
and training. Distinctive points relative to the CAH / Hall / van Wees (Osborne) material
above:

**Panoply and load.** Hunt (I.5): elements appear in the second half of the eighth century
(Argos earliest-known full panoply); after rapid spread, only slow minor changes
700–350 until the Macedonian phalanx equalled close-combat infantry. The *aspis*/*hoplon*
(~1 m diameter, ~16 lb, wood with optional metal shell, *porpax* + *antilabe*); full
bronze breastplate 30–40 lb; Corinthian helmet ~5 lb; primary weapon an ~8-ft thrusting
spear with *sauroter*. Full panoply cost estimated **75–100 drachmas** classical. Realistic
marching combat load: full panoply ≥30 kg, but most hoplites ~20 kg without thigh/shoulder
guards and with linen/leather corslet; poorest with helmet + shield only ~12 kg (Wheeler
I.7: early-fifth-century lightened kit ~12 kg / 25 lb — mobility and stamina, not
immobility, drove practice). Only spear and double-grip *aspis* were essential; other
equipment was individual discretion; classical shield blazons became civic letters rather
than personal insignia. Heavy shield + thrusting spear remained the *sine qua non* of
close-range superiority.

**Not helpless outside the phalanx.** Wheeler rejects the myth that hoplites could fight
only in dense ranks: final-phase individual combat, raids, amphibious ops, sieges,
marines; flexibility increased as body armour lightened. The double-grip round *aspis* did
not immediately create the [[phalanx]]; hoplites could fight one-on-one and run (Olympic
race in armour 520). The *aspis* (~7 kg) is lighter than a projected Republican *scutum*
(~10 kg).

**Social composition.** Van Wees (I.9): Solonian 200-*medimnoi* qualification for office
and militia required ~10 ha / estate ~1 talent — a leisure-class bracket, not small
independent 4–6 ha farmers. Yet **working-class and non-citizen hoplites were always
numerous**: Athens 431 had ~30,000 hoplites/cavalry (majority non-leisured; thousands
metics); Sparta 425 ~60% *perioikoi*; *neodamodeis* used as hoplites. Lower-status heavy
infantry is not a classical crisis innovation — Marathon fielded ~9,000; Thermopylae’s 300
Spartans accompanied by ~700 *perioikoi*; Plataea had equal Spartiate and *perioikoi*
numbers. Elite vs commoner heavy infantry already coexists in Homer. Legal obligation
gradient: Athens legally obliged the 200-*medimnoi* class to equip and join expeditionary
forces; poorer men at most morally obliged on general mobilization. Outside uniform
Sparta, equipment inequality on campaign reinforced social distance rather than erasing
it. The modern “hoplites as middle class” thesis is almost entirely invention (Aristotle
elsewhere identifies hoplites with the rich). See [[hoplite-revolution-debate]].

**Training and ethos.** Greeks claimed hoplite fighting needed only bravery/fitness (“as
natural as for a bull to use its horns”); archaic farmers had little time for serious
training. Classical competition produced *hoplomachoi* teachers, elite full-time units,
Theban drilling under Epaminondas, and late-fourth-century Athenian *ephebeia* (two
years) — yet regular citizens remained satisfactory hoplites, the last service cities
delegated to mercenaries. Leisure-class ideal preferred gentlemen who trained via
athletics, hunting, and dance rather than weapons- or formation-drill. Cohesion without
Roman-style physical discipline rested on shame culture and soldierly oaths (Hornblower
I.2). Xenophon insisted generalship exceeds *taktika* — logistics, combined arms,
outwitting opponents.

**Battle process.** Ancient writers presuppose audience knowledge of “normal” hoplite
battle (Herodotus’ Champions; Xenophon’s Coronea “unlike any other”) — hence modern
disagreement over *othismos*. Thucydides’ clearest account is Delium 424 (4.76–101).
**Battle by mutual consent** (*machē ex homologou*, Polybian term): offer battle by
deploying; declining cost honour; roots in champion duels with oaths, sacrifice, marked
“lists.” Pre-battle light-infantry skirmish in the *metaichmion* is attested only once
classically (Thuc. 6.69.2, Syracuse); *hiera* sacrifice in camp and *sphagia* to Artemis
Agrotera just before advance. Pre-battle wine as “liquid courage” is essentially only
attested for Spartan officers before Leuctra and is not characteristic. After formation
collapse, *tropē* (rout): casualties roughly even in formation then multiply for the
defeated; under agonal rules extended pursuit was irrelevant — possession of the field
and stripping armour mattered; formal end when the defeated requested truce for the dead.
Estimated casualties: victors ~5%, losers ~14% (discrepancy in the rout); high rates of
same-day death among seriously wounded (Krentz/Brulé estimates cited by Wheeler).

**Agonal ideal vs reality.** Among major mainland *poleis*, limited warfare channelled
violence into a single clash and often guaranteed political survival of both sides
(Wheeler). Mardonius’ speech (Hdt. 7.9b) is treated as satirical but accurate on agonal
traits: sharp single clash on a plain without manoeuvre or larger strategic aims. Hall
and Krentz flag that “ritualized” combat and limited aims were **not** the whole archaic
picture — Krentz (2002) and van Wees (2003) argue ritualization largely postdates the
Persian Wars and that wars of conquest were not uncommon earlier. From Persian conflicts
on, imperialism erased limited aims; light infantry novel in the Peloponnesian War more by
**numbers** than character; Iphicrates’ peltasts wrecked a Spartan *mora* at Lechaeum only
with an Athenian hoplite tactical base. Sekunda (I.11A): exclusive citizenship and
slender finances limited army expansion; fourth-century wealth decline reduced numbers
able to equip as hoplites.

**Ideology.** Hoplite close combat became the sole reputable male excellence (*andreia*);
horsemen, light infantry, marines, and rowers were cast as cowardly, effeminate, or
un-Greek — creating a social category without homogenizing class among hoplites
themselves.

## From the Oxford History of the Classical World (1986)

**Forrest (Ch.1).** Early armies centred on aristocrat + phratry entourage; the developed force depended on thousands of similarly equipped **hoplites** in (often) eight-deep line—helmet, corslet, greaves, round shields, thrusting (not throwing) spears—winning by cooperative weight. Random equipment elements appear as early as the eighth century; earliest vase representations of an **organized phalanx** do not much antedate the mid-seventh. Forrest argues gradual multiplication of “champions” rather than a single big bang, enabling political leverage (e.g. Cypselus’ army; Lycurgan reform). Marathon (490) later added proof of hoplite superiority over Persian forces and, per Aristotle as Forrest cites, popular political confidence at Athens.

**Hornblower (Ch.6).** The Peloponnesian War increased **professionalism** (politician and general as separate callings; mercenaries by the late fifth century; Xenophon’s Ten Thousand in 400); peltasts appear alongside hoplites; siege catapults develop c.400 (non-torsion) and c.350 (torsion, Thessaly); Epaminondas perfects tactical reserve after Delium 424 deepened the Theban file. Crawford (Ch.16, early Italy) notes that Roman conquest of Italy was a sequence of battles between heavy-armed free peasantries—implying free peasant heavy infantry among Etruscans and Samnites as well as Romans, a parallel social base for massed infantry outside the Greek *polis*.
