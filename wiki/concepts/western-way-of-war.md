## Definition

The thesis, associated with [[actors/hanson-victor-davis|Victor Davis Hanson]], that a distinctive and remarkably persistent Western military tradition originated with the Greek hoplite phalanx: an emphasis on decisive, face-to-face heavy-infantry battle among citizen-soldiers, seeking rapid, total military resolution rather than prolonged attrition, raiding, or maneuver-avoidance — a style Hanson argues recurs across Western military history from Greece through modern conventional warfare.

## Emic vs. Etic

Wholly etic: an interpretive historiographical framework constructed by a modern historian to characterize patterns across many different periods and armies, not a category used by the historical actors themselves.

## Significance and Contestation

The thesis has been influential in popular and some academic military history but is contested: critics argue it essentializes a supposed "Western" military culture, downplays the frequency of avoidance-of-battle strategies within Western history itself (see [[concepts/vegetian-warfare|Vegetian warfare]]), and understates the sophistication of decisive-battle traditions outside the Western world. It remains a live point of debate in military historiography over how far cultural, as opposed to technological, economic, or political, factors explain patterns of decisive battle-seeking.
