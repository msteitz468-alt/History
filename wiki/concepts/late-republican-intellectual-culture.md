# Intellectual Culture of the Ciceronian Age

The literary and intellectual life of the late Republic — overwhelmingly that of the
**Roman and Italian upper class** — in the great age of its **Hellenization**, when
Rome both absorbed and set out to rival Greek culture. Treated in
[[crook-cah-v9-1992|CAH IX]] Ch. 18 (Miriam Griffin).

## Education and the oral culture

Roman education was **private, unregulated, and increasingly Greek**: fee-paying
primary schools, then *grammatica* (language and literature, Greek and Latin — twenty
schools at Rome by the early 1st c.) and **rhetoric** (the secondary stage). The
censors' edict of **92 BCE** attacking the new schools of **Latin** rhetoric as
inferior to the Greek marks the institutionalization of the art. Griffin stresses the
power of **oral culture**: drama, recitation, and above all **oratory** — forensic and
political speeches as popular entertainment, the audience sensitive to prose rhythm —
were the heart of public life. Education could be an **instrument of social mobility**
for the educated Greek slave or freedman who taught it.

## The social setting: villas, libraries, dedication

Intellectual life was **social and aristocratic**, centered on the **villa** (Tusculum,
Antium, the Bay of Naples) and on **private libraries** — there being no public ones
until Caesar planned the first (with **Varro** as librarian). Great libraries came as
**war booty**: Perseus' to the sons of Aemilius Paullus, **Lucullus'** (open to Greek
scholars in Rome), and most famously **Aristotle's library**, seized by **Sulla** from
Apellicon and edited at Rome by Tyrannio — reviving the Peripatetic corpus. The
Hellenistic custom of **literary dedication** bound this world together (Cicero, Varro,
Atticus, Nepos, Caesar exchanging works), with **Atticus** as a great catalyst. Under
Caesar's dictatorship these courtesies were strained — Cicero felt he now dealt with "a
tyrant who expected flattery."

## Hellenization — and its anxieties

Intensified by conquest (the influxes after **Pydna** and the **Mithridatic Wars**
brought philosophers, rhetoricians, poets, and captives — Posidonius, Antiochus of
Ascalon, Philo of Larissa, Tyrannio, Theophanes, Archias), Hellenization "acquired its
own momentum." Yet it remained **ambivalent**: Greek culture was now that of a
**conquered people**, and to be **too Hellenized invited ridicule** (Albucius mocked in
Greek by Scaevola). Even Cicero practised concealment (Antonius "pretended ignorance" of
Greek, Crassus "pretended to despise it"). The Roman achievement was the **adaptation,
not mere translation, of Greek forms into Latin** — and the ambition, voiced by Cicero,
not only to emulate but to **equal or surpass** the Greeks (Ennius "the Roman Homer";
Cicero's rivalry with Demosthenes).

## The great works

The age produced an extraordinary harvest, mostly mediated through Greek models:

- **Varro** — "the most learned Roman of his age" — on the Latin language, **Roman
  antiquities** (*Antiquitates*, the framework Augustus would adopt), and agriculture;
- **Cicero** — the orations, the rhetorical works (*De Oratore*, *Brutus*), and, in his
  forced retirement, the **philosophica** (*De Republica*, *De Officiis*, *De Finibus*,
  the *Tusculans*) that forged a **Latin philosophical vocabulary** and naturalized
  Greek thought (Academic scepticism, Stoic ethics) for a Roman readership;
- **Lucretius** — the *De Rerum Natura*, the great **Epicurean** poem;
- the **neoteric poets** (Catullus, Calvus) — the new, learned, Alexandrian-influenced
  Latin verse;
- the jurists (the development of [[roman-private-law]]), and the historians (Sallust,
  the annalists, the memoirs).

## Significance

Griffin's theme: the late Republic — for all its political collapse — was the moment
when **Latin literature came of age** by mastering and contesting Greek culture,
creating the classical canon (Cicero, Lucretius, Catullus, Varro) on which the
Augustan age and all later European letters would build. "It was appropriate that
Cicero did not outlive the Republic; Varro survived, and his vision of Rome was adopted
by Augustus."
