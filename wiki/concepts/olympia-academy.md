# Olympia Academy

**Bern, ~1902–1905 (informal).** Self-mocking discussion circle of young intellectuals centered on Albert Einstein, philosophy student Maurice Solovine, and mathematician/inventor Conrad Habicht. Named with ironic pomposity after the ancient academy; Einstein “president,” Solovine drew a bust-with-sausages certificate.

## Definition and Scope

Regular evening sessions (often all night) reading and debating science, philosophy, and literature over frugal meals (sausage, Gruyère, tea). Einstein played violin; summer sunrise hikes on nearby mountains followed by dark coffee. Revenge pranks (smoke-filled room) and inside jokes punctuated the seriousness.

Core reading (per Isaacson range): David Hume’s *A Treatise of Human Nature* (empiricism, critique of absolute time/causality); Ernst Mach (*Analysis of the Sensations*, *Mechanics and Its Development*—skepticism toward Newton’s absolutes); Henri Poincaré (*Science and Hypothesis*—simultaneity, relativity principle); Baruch Spinoza (*Ethics*—determinism, God as lawful harmony); Sophocles’ *Antigone* (defiance of authority); Cervantes’ *Don Quixote* (tilting at windmills).

## Impact on Einstein

Instilled or reinforced: Hume/Mach skepticism toward unobservable absolutes (“concepts have meaning only if we can point to objects...”); preference for operational definitions (directly influenced simultaneity analysis in 1905 relativity); Spinozist cosmic religion and determinism (“God does not play dice”); anti-authoritarian, anti-nationalist stance. “The theory of relativity suggests itself in positivism... Hume... studied avidly... shortly before discovering the theory of relativity.”

Solovine and Habicht received the famous May 1905 letter heralding the four papers. Habicht’s brother Paul later collaborated on Einstein’s “Maschinchen” electrometer invention. Lifelong friendships endured; 74th-birthday postcard tribute from the two survivors.

## Historiography

Isaacson treats the Academy as the intellectual and emotional refuge that complemented the patent-office “cloister.” Not a formal institution but a bohemian salon whose reading list shaped Einstein’s philosophy of science more than formal Zurich courses. Pranks and humor preserved the “cheerful” character against later “respectable” academies.

**Grounding quotes (range 1)**: “A man perfectly and clearly erudite, imbued with exquisite, subtle and elegant knowledge...” (Solovine certificate). “For goodness’ sake... So that was the famous caviar!” (birthday incident). “Your members created you to make fun of your long-established sister Academies...” (Einstein 1953 tribute).
