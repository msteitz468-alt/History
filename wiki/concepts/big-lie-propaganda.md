# The Big Lie

**Definition.** A propaganda technique of asserting a falsehood so enormous
that audiences assume no one would dare invent it — credibility through
audacity. The canonical formulation is [[adolf-hitler|Hitler's]] in *Mein
Kampf* (1925): "the great masses of the people... more easily fall victim to
the big lie than to a little one, since they themselves lie in little things,
but would be ashamed of lies that were too big."

**The emic twist.** Hitler did not present the technique as his own method: he
attributed it to the Jews (specifically, to the alleged lie that Ludendorff
was responsible for losing the First World War). The passage is thus itself an
instance of the projection it describes — the antisemitic conspiracy theory of
*Mein Kampf* accuses its victims of the propaganda strategy the Nazi movement
perfected. A related maxim ("if you tell a lie big enough and keep repeating
it, people will eventually come to believe it") is popularly attributed to
Goebbels but is not found in his writings; attribution to Goebbels is itself
an illustration of how the concept circulates loosely.

**Etic career.** After 1945 the term became a general analytic label for
totalitarian propaganda (the wartime OSS psychological profile of Hitler used
it centrally), and later a fixture of political rhetoric in which each side
accuses the other of "big lie" tactics — e.g., its prominence in American
discourse after the 2020 U.S. election, and its use as book-title and framing
device in partisan polemic such as [[dsouza-big-lie-2017]], which builds an
entire argument on the charge that the label's users are its practitioners
(see [[fascism-and-the-political-left]]).

**Limitations and critiques.** As an analytic concept it is loose: it names a
rhetorical effect (audacity as credibility) rather than a measurable mechanism,
and empirical propaganda studies emphasize repetition, source trust, and
identity-protective cognition over sheer size of the falsehood. Its main
scholarly value is emic — documenting how the Nazi leadership theorized
deception — and its main modern function is accusatory rather than analytic.
