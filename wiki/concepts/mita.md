# Mit'a (Andean Rotational Labor)

## Definition

The **mit'a** (Quechua, "turn" or "shift") was the Andean institution of **rotational labor
service**: households of an [[ayllu|ayllu]] took turns owing labor to a lord or to the state,
in exchange for which the receiving authority was obliged to provide hospitality and
redistribute goods. Under the [[inca-empire|Inka]] the mit'a became the **backbone of imperial
political economy** — there was little tribute in goods; instead subject communities owed
**labor** (farming state and church lands, herding, building roads and storehouses, soldiering,
serving as **mitmaq** colonists), sustained by the surpluses held in state warehouses
(*qollqas*) under principles of asymmetrical **reciprocity**.

## The Colonial Transformation

[[cambridge-history-native-peoples-americas-v3-1|Volume III]]'s conquest chapters trace how the
Spanish **appropriated and perverted** the mit'a. Stripped of its reciprocal obligations, it
became a coercive draft — most infamously the **Potosí silver mita** instituted by Viceroy
Toledo (1570s), which conscripted Andean communities to deadly mine labor. The same word thus
names both a reciprocal indigenous institution and a colonial instrument of extraction, making
the mit'a a sharp illustration of how conquest hollowed out and weaponized native forms.
