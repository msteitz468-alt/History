# Greek Naval Warfare

**Naval warfare** in the Classical Greek world was built almost entirely around a
single ship type, the **trireme**, and — unlike hoplite land warfare, which drew on a
broad citizen militia — depended on large crews of rowers whose social composition made
naval power politically contentious. See [[hoplite-warfare]] for land warfare and
[[financing-greek-warfare]] for how navies were paid for.

## Trireme design and crews

The trireme carried a crew of up to **200 men**, measured roughly **37 m (120 ft) long**
by **6 m (20 ft) wide**, and could make about **9 knots** under oar — but had no capacity
for onboard cooking or sleeping and minimal storage, so triremes normally beached each
night and depended on shore support for supply. This near-total reliance on one design,
to the exclusion of other warship types, defined Greek naval warfare through the
Classical period.

## Trierarchs and command

The **trierarch** (ship's captain) was a wealthy citizen performing a public **liturgy**
— a one-year term of financial and administrative responsibility for hiring the crew and
maintaining the ship — rather than a professional naval officer. Under him served
**marines** (*epibatai*, typically about ten hoplites plus four archers) and specialist
officers: the helmsman, a lookout, a rowing-master who kept stroke with a pipe-player, a
purser, and a shipwright.

## Rowers and the politics of naval power

Rowers were drawn from **citizens** (chiefly the *thetes*, the poorest census class, who
needed the pay), **metics**, and **slaves** — citizen rowers sometimes brought a slave to
row in their place for extra wages. Citizen rowers were frequently outnumbered by
foreigners and slaves: Corcyra's fleet reportedly included some 800 slaves at one point
(Thucydides 1.55.1), and Athenian crews too were commonly described as "bought rather
than home-grown."

This composition had a political dimension. Elites hostile to extending political
standing to oarsmen disparaged their status even as naval service (especially after
[[battle-of-salamis-480bce|Salamis]]) became militarily indispensable — early Aristophanic
comedy mocks ordinary Athenians with the rowers' chant, *"to rhuppapai."* The contrast
with the esteem accorded hoplite service (see [[hoplite-warfare]]) reflects the same
property-and-status logic that linked hoplite duty to political privilege on land.

## Tactics

Fleet deployment signaled confidence and skill: a **single line** was standard for a
fleet that trusted its maneuvering ability, while a **double line** or a **defensive
circle** (sterns drawn in) signaled inferiority in seamanship. Two broad tactical styles
are described: an "old style," essentially an infantry battle fought from stationary
decks by boarding and missile fire (associated with Corinth and Corcyra), and a "more
modern style" relying on the bronze-sheathed **ram** and maneuver — breaking the enemy's
line (**diekplous**) or swinging around its flank (**periplous**).

## Blockade

Sustained **blockade** was tactically difficult: wooden hulls needed regular drying-out
ashore, and there was no reliable way to control open water without the surveillance
capacity of a modern navy. Triremes suited fast raiding far better than prolonged
blockade — Athens used raiding squadrons of up to roughly 100 ships to circle the
Peloponnese during the [[peloponnesian-war|Peloponnesian War]] rather than attempting a
close, continuous blockade.

## Performance, logistics, and manpower (CHGRW)

[[sabin-van-wees-whitby-cambridge-history-greek-roman-warfare-2007|CHGRW]] (Hunt I.5;
Krentz I.6; Strauss I.7; Gabrielsen I.8; de Souza II.13B) deepens the Osborne baseline.

**Performance.** Under oar, triremes could cover well over 100 miles in a day but were
specialized: little cargo, nightly (and often midday) landings for food/water/sleep,
vulnerable in rough seas. Average speeds perhaps 7–8 knots historically; *Olympias*
trials have not yet sustained 6+ knots (Tilley/Jordan two-level alternative flagged).
Naval skill required continuous professional practice (Pericles/Thuc. 1.142.9);
conscientious trierarchs paid experienced rowers extra; even Athens’ rower pool included
significant mercenaries from the Ionian islands; Spartan allies could hire a navy of
mercenary rowers. Success needed good men as well as ships — generals as coaches;
oarsmen intolerant of mistreatment; pay disputes prompted desertion/riots (Thuc. 7–8).

**Slave and free crews.** Slave rowers were widespread: of 1,000 Corcyraean Sybota
prisoners, 800 slaves; Peloponnesian crews typically 50–80% slave, Athenian perhaps
20–40% (IG i³ 1032); *Paralus* crew “all free Athenians” noted as exceptional (Thuc.
8.73.5). Crew hierarchy mixed solidarity with status: citizen top-bench rowers with
bonuses, non-citizens/slaves lower/worse benches, officers above, marines sharing elite
captain’s leisure — war could reinforce distinctions (van Wees I.9). Elite scorn of the
“naval mob” and sparse public naval imagery leave working-class naval identity doubtful.

**Logistics.** Limited storage; *Olympias* trials ~1 litre water/hour/rower; never more
than three days’ rations asked of rowers; Sicilian Expedition included 30 merchant ships
with specialists; one typical round ship’s 3,000 *medimnoi* grain could feed 24 trireme
crews a month. Ship-timber procurement required hard currency and diplomacy; Athens
cultivated Macedon and used imperial power to control timber, iron, copper, and flax
(Ps.-Xen. *Ath. Pol.* 2.11–12). Themistocles initiated (from 494) transformation of
Piraeus’ three natural harbours — Zea, Munichia, Cantharus — into fortified naval bases.

**Fleet size and growth.** By 480 Athens enlarged to 200 triremes; turning that into a
lasting power machine required a full naval organization (Gabrielsen). Imperial Athens
arguably held a **core fleet of ~400 ships**: Thucydides’ “300 seaworthy” means currently
fitted-out hulls, not total; 250 in commission in 428 and 400 annual trierarchs support a
larger total. Fleet grew by confiscating allies’ fleets after revolts (Thasos, Samos,
Mytilene, Naxos, Aegina) and by captures (e.g. 70 ships from Aegina 459). Second
Athenian Confederacy weaker; under Eubulus in the 350s Athens rebuilt to 300 triremes
matching fifth-century peak. Specialized troop- and horse-carriers (*stratiōtides*,
*hippagōgoi*) made most naval expeditions amphibious. See [[financing-greek-warfare]].

## Tactics and operations (Strauss / de Souza, CHGRW)

Naval and siege warfare were simpler, cheaper, and less lethal before c.500; major
developments began on eastern/western fringes via foreign contact then moved to mainland
— a “long, slow and bloody revolution” (Strauss). Climactic Ionian Revolt sea fight off
Lade: Greeks 353 triremes vs ~600 Phoenician; most Greeks fled; only Chios’ 100 stayed.

**Athenian way of war.** Speed and manoeuvrability — evade ram, turn, ram stern/amidships,
back away; or break oars. *Diekplous* / *periplous* / *anastrophē* meanings debated; light
hulls, normally only ~10 marines + 4 archers (vs up to 44 elsewhere); “as long as the
trireme was the ship of the line, Mediterranean warships were better suited as guided
missiles than fighting platforms.” Phormio 429: outnumbered 20 vs 77 still won through
professionalism (Thuc. 2.80–92 — Whitby praises the narrative). Sybota 433 (150 Corinthian
vs 120 Corcyrean + 10 Athenian): largest intra-Greek sea fight to date; “old imperfect
armament” — deck hoplites made it like a land battle; no *diekplous*. Double line used as
defence vs breakthrough (Heraclides of Mylasa; Arginusae 406). Signals by flags, trumpet,
shield flash. Beyond set battles: *guerre de course*, amphibious ops, piracy, limited
blockades (triremes too light for modern continuous blockade); Sphacteria 425 oarsmen
given light arms. Naval deaths from ramming, marine/archer weapons, drowning, shore
hoplites; unrecovered dead symbolized by empty coffin in Athenian public funeral (Thuc.
2.34.3).

**Polyremes (Hellenistic).** Rating numbers = files of oarsmen per side, not more than
three oar levels (Dionysius I of Syracuse began fours and fives in 399). Advantages:
greater beam and deck height for more marines, boarding/grappling over ramming, better
missile/catapult platforms — not higher speed. Smaller types remained important (Rhodian
*trihemioliai* ~144 crew). De Souza: fleets always secondary to armies; naval actions
served land objectives; almost all battles near land. Ramming best with light/fast trained
crews; Athlit bronze ram (~462 kg) designed for short penetration and hull-splitting.
Shipborne torsion artillery first clear at Salamis 306 (Demetrius) — mainly to neutralize
personnel for boarding. Ancient naval war was about harbours and routes, not open-sea
control.

## Historiography

Osborne/van Wees remains the concise social baseline; CHGRW adds operational, fiscal, and
Hellenistic naval architecture. Figures for crew and fleet sizes in the earlier Osborne-
derived material may reflect OCR numeral corruption in the local text — treat specific
counts as approximate and prefer CHGRW/epigraphic figures where they diverge. Realist
naval history (Hanson in CHGRW I.1) stresses fragile wooden ships powered by hundreds of
poor and slaves under harsh conditions, best understood via replica trials (Morrison/
Coates/Rankov *Olympias*), not literary evidence alone.
