## Definition and Scope

The **Mu'tazila** (Arabic: "those who stand apart" or "those who withdraw") constituted the first major school of Islamic speculative theology ([[kalam]]), flourishing c. 750–900 CE and remaining a significant intellectual current through the High Middle Ages. The school was characterised by an unusually high valuation of rational argument in theological matters, insistence on the absolute unity and justice of God, and the assertion of genuine human free will.

Mu'tazilism was never a single unified movement. It harboured wide internal disagreements on cosmological details, the precise scope of rational argument, and political questions. What united the school was a shared set of five doctrinal heads and a common commitment to rational theology as an obligation, not a luxury.

**Emic usage**: Members of the school called themselves *ahl al-'adl wa-l-tawhid* — "the people of justice and unity" — pointing to their two most distinctive doctrines.

**Etic usage**: "Mu'tazili" has been used both descriptively and pejoratively in the Islamic tradition; modern scholars use it as a term of art for this school of rational theology.

The Mu'tazila should not be confused with the broader tradition of [[kalam]], of which they were the first major school but not the only or final expression. They should also not be identified with [[islamic-philosophy|Falsafah]] (the Philosophic tradition): the Mu'tazilis operated within the framework of revealed religion and regarded the Qur'an as authoritative; they used rational argument to interpret and defend revelation, not to replace it.

## The Five Doctrinal Heads

Abu-l-Hudhayl al-'Allaf (d. 840) gave Mu'tazili doctrine its classical systematic form, organising positions under five heads:

### 1. Unity of God (*Tawhid*)
The absolute simplicity and unity of God was the Mu'tazilis' most philosophically elaborated position. It entailed:
- **Anti-anthropomorphism**: Qur'anic language describing God as having hands, a face, or as sitting on a throne must be interpreted allegorically. The Hadith folk's tendency to take such imagery literally was condemned as *tashbih* (assimilation of God to created things).
- **Rejection of dualism**: Manichaean or other dualist positions positing two eternal principles were refuted.
- **The attribute problem**: The Mu'tazilis argued that God cannot possess distinct real attributes — knowledge, power, will — that are "other than" His essence, since such attributes, being eternal, would constitute a plurality of co-eternal entities alongside God. This amounted to saying God knows "by His essence," not by a knowledge-attribute separable from Him. The Hadith folk and later Ash'aris found this position effectively emptied God of positive character.

### 2. Justice of God (*'Adl*)
God is perfectly and necessarily just. He cannot act unjustly or arbitrarily toward human beings. Crucially, this entailed:
- **Human free will**: If God is just in punishing human beings for their sins, human beings must be genuinely responsible agents who freely choose their acts. God cannot create acts of sin in humans and then punish them for those acts. The Mu'tazilis are accordingly described as the *Qadariyya* — asserters of human power (*qadar*) over their own actions.
- **God's obligation**: Most Mu'tazilis held that God is rationally *obligated* to reward the obedient and punish the wicked — a position the Ash'aris found blasphemous (it made God subject to human categories of obligation).

### 3. The Promise and the Threat (*Wa'd wa-Wa'id*)
God has genuinely promised reward to the obedient and threatened punishment to the wicked, and these promises and threats are binding. This had the practical implication of insisting on the moral seriousness of individual conduct over bare community membership. It also tended to elevate the Qur'an as the standard of judgment at the expense of hadith traditions, which the Mu'tazilis regarded with more suspicion than the Hadith folk.

### 4. The Intermediate Position (*Al-Manzila bayn al-Manzilatayn*)
A Muslim who commits grave sin occupies an intermediate position — neither a full believer (*mu'min*) nor an unbeliever (*kafir*). This doctrine had political origins: it allowed the Mu'tazilis to avoid condemning early caliphs as unbelievers while also refusing to grant them unconditional legitimacy. It was one of the school's most contested positions.

### 5. Commanding Right and Forbidding Wrong (*Al-Amr bil-Ma'ruf wa-l-Nahy 'an al-Munkar*)
The Mu'tazilis gave this Qur'anic obligation unusual emphasis, extending it to include positive political initiatives toward godly social order. Under Abbasid patronage this could translate into active intellectual policing — the *mihna* (Inquisition, 833–848) was the most dramatic expression.

## Cosmological Work

The Mu'tazilis did not merely defend existing Islamic positions but developed positive cosmological frameworks. Under the heading of divine unity, they had to account for God's creation and governance of the world. Their terminology — substance (*jawhar*), accident (*'arad*), existence (*wujud*), non-existence (*'adam*) — echoed Greek philosophical language, probably mediated through Christian theological disputations rather than direct engagement with the Greek texts being translated into Arabic.

Their cosmological positions included:
- God has power over everything; nothing lies outside His capacity.
- The world is created in time (*hadath*), not co-eternal with God.
- God creates through His essence, not through instruments distinct from Him.

The Mu'tazilis were also concerned with the question of the createdness of the Qur'an (*khalq al-Qur'an*): if the Qur'an is God's eternal speech, co-eternal with Him, then something other than God is eternal — a violation of absolute divine unity. The Mu'tazilis insisted on the createdness of the Qur'an; the Hadith folk regarded this as a dangerous rationalist assault on the sacred text's authority.

## Abbasid Patronage and the *Mihna*

Mu'tazilism flourished with active Abbasid court support from the reign of al-Ma'mun (r. 813–833) onward, reaching its institutional peak during the *mihna* (833–848 CE) — the Abbasid caliphal Inquisition that required scholars to affirm the doctrine of the createdness of the Qur'an. Scholars who refused, notably the great hadith scholar Ahmad ibn Hanbal, faced imprisonment and flogging. The *mihna* ultimately failed; Ibn Hanbal's resistance made him a popular hero, and the Abbasid court abandoned the policy under al-Mutawakkil (r. 847–861), who explicitly promoted the Hadith folk position. This marked the beginning of the Mu'tazila's political marginalisation.

The Mu'tazili intellectual tradition continued after the *mihna*'s failure, but it no longer commanded court patronage and increasingly found itself on the defensive against the Hadith folk and the rival [[kalam]] schools developing in opposition to it.

## Opposition and Decline

Hodgson identifies two main lines of opposition:

**The Hadith folk** (*Ahl al-Hadith*): Rejected all kalam disputation as bid'a (illicit innovation). For them, whatever was given by revelation needed no rational proof; to subject it to rational proof was to imply it might need defending, which undermined its authority. They tolerated seeming anthropomorphism, stressed divine omnipotence more than divine justice, and accepted hadith traditions (often at the expense of the Qur'an itself). Their intellectual hero was Ahmad ibn Hanbal (d. 855).

**The Ash'ari and Maturidi schools**: By the end of the ninth century, scholars who identified with the Hadith folk but were willing to engage in kalam disputation developed counter-systems. Abu-l-Hasan al-Ash'ari (d. 935) and Abu-Mansur al-Maturidi (d. 944) constructed systematic kalam frameworks that defended Hadith folk positions against Mu'tazili arguments, while retaining the philosophical vocabulary and argumentative forms of kalam. On the attribute question, al-Ash'ari accepted that God has real attributes but argued they are "not other than His essence." On free will and divine justice, he developed the doctrine of *kasb* (acquisition), avoiding both the Mu'tazili assertion of full human autonomy and the Hadith folk's sometimes fatalist rhetoric. See [[kalam]] for details.

## Interaction with Falsafah

The Mu'tazilis had a complex relationship with [[islamic-philosophy|Falsafah]]. They borrowed philosophical vocabulary and argument forms but maintained the priority of revelation. The first great Muslim philosopher [[al-kindi]] worked "closely with Mu'tazili thinkers in their attempt to eliminate anthropomorphisms from their faith" (*Venture of Islam*, Vol. 1, p. 1641), and the early Sufi al-Muhasibi (d. 857) — who introduced intellectual methods into the analysis of mystical experience — was "familiar with Mu'tazili positions" (*ibid.*, p. 588). The Mu'tazilis also engaged directly with the Isma'ili tradition, which had its own sophisticated rational cosmology derived from neo-Platonic sources.

The Mu'tazilis were also "at odds with the Hadithi movement" on almost all their main points, and their increasing isolation from the majority of the ulama was the central dynamic of their decline after the failure of the *mihna*.

## Legacy

Mu'tazilism did not disappear after its political marginalisation. It retained a significant following among the Mu'tazili-aligned Hanafi legal school and especially in Shi'i Islam, where the emphasis on rational argument in theology was compatible with the Shi'i commitment to the authority of the Imam as interpreter of revelation. Zaidi Shi'ism in particular preserved a living Mu'tazili tradition.

In the modern period, Mu'tazilism has been invoked by Islamic modernists and reformers (from the nineteenth century onward) as a precedent for rational, revisionist approaches to theology and jurisprudence — a use that somewhat distorts the historical school but reflects its enduring symbolic importance.

## Historiography

The history of Mu'tazilism was long obscured by the fact that most Mu'tazili texts were lost or preserved only in summaries by opponents. The recovery of texts through Yemeni Zaidi libraries (notably the manuscripts used by Josef van Ess in his monumental *Theologie und Gesellschaft*) has substantially transformed the field since the mid-twentieth century. J. Schacht's "New Sources for the History of Muhammadan Theology" (*Studia Islamica*, 1953) — cited by Hodgson — was an early contribution to this recovery. The relationship between Mu'tazili cosmology and Greek philosophical categories, once assumed to be a direct borrowing via the [[the-translation-movement|Translation Movement]], is now understood to be more complex: much Greek material probably entered Mu'tazili thought through the mediation of Christian theological disputations in Arabic.
