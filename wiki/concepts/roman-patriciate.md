# The Roman Patriciate

## Definition

**Patricians** (*patricii*) are the hereditary, closed aristocracy of the Roman
state — a small set of *gentes* (clans) defined by birth who, from the beginning
of the Republic, monopolized the major magistracies, the major priesthoods, the
interregnum procedure, and through their control of the pontificate and augural
college, the interpretation of religious and civil law.

The term is emic: *patricius* (from *pater*, "father") is the Roman word for this
status. The opposite category — everyone who is not *patricius* — is the
**plebs**, which ranges from wealthy land-owners to the landless poor. The
scholarly term "patrician-plebeian division" denotes the fundamental structural
cleavage of the early Roman citizen body.

The patriciate is both a sociological category and a political institution. It is
sociological in that membership was fixed by birth and transmitted through the
male line; it is institutional in that birth-right produced specific, documented
political privileges that were legally defended.

## Emic vs. Etic

This concept is substantially **emic** — the Romans themselves used *patricius*
with a clear, consistent meaning throughout the Republic. The scholarly analytical
work has gone into explaining *why* the division existed and *how* it was
maintained or eroded, rather than constructing a new category.

However, the patriciate is also an **etic** category in the sense that scholars
have imposed a degree of analytical clarity on what Roman sources present more
casually. In particular, the scholarly debate over whether the patriciate was
genuinely closed from 509 BCE or whether it progressively restricted its membership
during the fifth century (the "patrician coup" hypothesis) involves interpretive
frameworks not explicit in Roman sources.

## Origins

**The "patrician coup" debate.** The most important scholarly question about the
patriciate is *when* it became a closed hereditary caste. Three positions:

1. **Pre-Republican origin** (CAH VII.2, Drummond Ch. 5): The patriciate's monopoly
   of office, religion, and interregnum was in place from the Republic's foundation —
   the fasti from 509 BCE are entirely patrician until the fifth-century constitutional
   changes. On this view, the patrician exclusivity is inherited from or crystallized
   at the end of the monarchy.

2. **Early Republican crystallization**: The Republic's foundation was the moment
   of crystallization — a group of aristocratic families seized the new constitutional
   order and defined it in terms of hereditary qualification, excluding non-aristocrats
   who had previously had some access to power.

3. **"Patrician coup" hypothesis** (some modern scholars): The patriciate became
   a closed caste during the fifth century, gradually restricting previously open
   offices and inventing a genealogical qualification after the fact. The apparently
   plebeian names in the very earliest fasti are real, not patrician homonyms —
   evidence of an open system that was subsequently closed.

CAH VII.2 (Drummond) argues strongly against the "patrician coup" hypothesis. The
apparently plebeian consul-names in the early fasti are better explained as patrician
families of the same name later extinct, or as naming ambiguities in the tradition,
than as genuine plebeians holding the consulship and then being excluded. The
evidence for the patriciate's monopoly is consistent from 509 BCE onward.

## Political Monopoly

At the beginning of the Republic, the patriciate held:

- **The consulship** (and all subsequent chief magistracies until the Licinian-Sextian
  laws of 367 BCE): the two annually elected magistrates with *imperium*.
- **The dictatorship** when appointed.
- **The censorship** (from its creation, traditionally 443 BCE, to the Licinian-
  Sextian period).
- **The interrex procedure**: in the absence of consuls, an *interrex* (drawn from
  the patrician senators) held authority; this was a technical monopoly that the
  plebeians could not challenge, since it required patrician clan (*gens*) membership
  for its validity.
- **The pontificate and augurate**: the two major priestly colleges that controlled
  religious law, the calendar, the forms of legal procedure, and the taking of
  auspices required to validate all public action. Not opened to plebeians until
  the Ogulnian law (300 BCE).
- **The senate**: membership was predominantly if not exclusively patrician until
  the mid-fourth century; even when plebeians entered, the dominant families were
  patrician.

**Legal opacity.** The patrician monopoly of the pontificate gave patricians a
specific, institutionally reinforced advantage in private law: the *legis actiones*
(the forms of words required to initiate a lawsuit) were known only to the
pontifices and were not published until the Twelve Tables (c. 450 BCE) made them
publicly accessible. Even after publication, the calendar of *dies fasti* (days
on which legal proceedings were permissible) was kept secret until 304 BCE, when
Cn. Flavius, a freedman's son, published it in defiance of the patrician monopoly.

## Social Functions

Beyond formal institutional power, the patriciate had:

- **Clan solidarity (*gens*)**: The patrician *gens* — a group of families sharing
  a common *gentile* name and claiming common descent — provided social identity,
  political alliance, some religious functions (the *sacra gentilicia*), and in
  theory (though Drummond is skeptical of its practical extent) potential mutual
  support. The clan was NOT primarily an economic or military unit in the fifth
  century; it was a social and political one.
- **Patron-client relationships**: patrician families were typically the senior
  members of the patron-client web that structured Roman social relations. A
  patrician house collected "clients" (*clientes*) — free citizens of lower status
  who gave support (at elections, at law) in exchange for protection and assistance.
  The Lapis Satricanus inscription (c. 500 BCE, naming the *sodales* — band — of
  P. Valerius) is the earliest direct evidence for aristocratic retinue bands of
  this type.
- **Religious prestige**: control of the major priesthoods gave the patriciate a
  claim to be the proper intermediaries between the Roman community and its gods.
  This was not merely ideological — a military action taken without proper auspices
  was legally invalid, and the auspices belonged exclusively to magistrates who
  had been elected under patrician-controlled procedure.

## Decline

The Struggle of the Orders progressively dismantled the patrician monopoly:
- 445 BCE: ban on intermarriage rescinded (Canuleian law)
- 421 BCE: quaestorship opened to plebeians
- 367 BCE: one consulship must go to a plebeian (Licinian-Sextian laws)
- 356 BCE: first plebeian dictator
- 337 BCE: first plebeian praetor
- 300 BCE: pontificate and augurate opened to plebeians (Ogulnian law)
- 287 BCE: plebiscites given force of universal law (Lex Hortensia)

By 287 BCE the formal institutional monopoly of the patriciate was broken. What
emerged was a merged **nobilitas** — families with a consul among their ancestors
— in which wealthy plebeian families were incorporated into the ruling class
alongside the old patrician houses. The patriciate retained ceremonial significance
(the interrex procedure, certain priestly offices) but was no longer politically
decisive.

## Scholarly Controversies

The main live debates (beyond the "patrician coup" question above):

1. **Economic role of the patriciate**: Were patrician families dramatically wealthier
   than the wealthiest plebeians? The sources suggest overlap; Drummond argues the
   economic basis of the division was *not* primarily economic (as a Marxist
   model would predict) but was a matter of birth-right that cut across economic
   stratification. Some wealthy plebeians were as rich as middle-ranked patricians.

2. **The patriciate and the gentes**: What was the real institutional significance
   of the patrician *gens*? Traditional scholarship (Mommsen, Homo) attributed
   major political, military, and economic functions to the patrician clans; CAH
   VII.2 (Drummond) reduces their role — the *gens* was socially significant but
   not a primary political or economic institution.

3. **"Plebeian" names in early fasti**: Whether names like "Brutus" or "Publicola"
   in the tradition represent genuine early plebeians later blocked or extinct
   patrician houses is the key diagnostic argument about the patriciate's early
   history. See [[reliability-of-early-roman-tradition]].

4. **The religious monopoly**: Recent scholarship (North, CAH VII.2 Ch. 12) has
   emphasized that religious authority was a genuine, not merely symbolic,
   component of patrician power — the auspices were institutionally necessary, and
   their monopoly was real.

## Limitations and Critiques

The category "patriciate" can be reified into something more systematic and
organized than the reality: the patriciate was not a council, had no formal
meeting, made no collective decisions. Its "monopoly" was the aggregate of
individual families' birth-right claims rather than a coordinated patrician
program. In the fifth century, patrician magistrates often competed bitterly with
each other; the patriciate was unified primarily in its resistance to plebeian
admission rather than as a coherent ruling body with shared interests in all things.
