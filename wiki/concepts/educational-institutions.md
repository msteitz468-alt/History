## Definition

**Educational institutions** are, in Linda Walton's broad definition
([[kedar-cwh-v5-2015|CWH V]], Ch 5), "customary practices designed to transmit
collective learning" — the regularized means by which a society reproduces
knowledge across generations. Following David Christian, collective learning is the
distinctively human capacity; the family and household were its primary site
everywhere, but the Middle Millennium is above all the age in which **formal
institutions of higher learning** crystallized and spread, almost always entangled
with **religion** and with the **state**. Walton organizes the comparison around
three questions: what knowledge is valued and how is it transmitted; what is the
relationship of organized knowledge to the state; and how far does access to
knowledge determine social status and political power.

## The Major Institutional Forms

- **The Confucian examination system (China, and East Asia by extension).** The
  era's most distinctive and durable institution: state schools and a meritocratic
  civil-service examination that selected officials by mastery of the
  [[confucianism|Confucian]] classics, binding state and scholarly elite together.
  Emulated in Korea and Vietnam. The eleventh-century spread of printing widened
  access to texts and reshaped the examination world. See [[confucianism]] and the
  [[song-dynasty|Song]] examination state.
- **The Islamic madrasa.** As [[islam|Islam]] expanded, the transmission of
  religious-legal knowledge (*‘ilm*) became central; the madrasa institutionalized
  the training of scholars (*‘ulama’*) and jurists, frequently endowed (*waqf*) by
  rulers and elites — fusing piety, law, and the administrative needs of states.
- **The Christian university.** Latin Europe's distinctive contribution: the
  corporate, self-governing [[medieval-university|university]] (Bologna, Paris,
  Oxford) emerging from cathedral schools in the twelfth–thirteenth centuries,
  granting professional credentials in law, medicine, and theology, and absorbing
  Aristotle via Arabic transmission.
- **Buddhist monastic schools.** Across South, East, and Southeast Asia, Buddhist
  monasteries (the great example: [[nalanda|Nalanda]]) served as centers of both
  religious and secular learning, and as nodes in the trans-regional circulation of
  texts and scholars.
- **Beyond the world religions.** In regions not yet penetrated by Buddhism,
  Christianity, or Islam, priestly elites ran state-serving schools: the Aztec
  *calmecac* and *telpochcalli* and Inca instruction of elite youth are the best
  (largely Spanish-recorded) American examples. Sub-Saharan Africa outside Islamic
  zones left little evidence of formal institutions.

## The Core Argument

Walton's conclusion: the organization of knowledge "reflected not only diverse
cultural values and traditions, but also differing relationships between states,
which both utilized and controlled knowledge, and relationships among elites, whose
status depended on their access to knowledge and role in its transmission." Learning
served a **dual function** — a path to religious truth *and* a means of meeting the
secular administrative and legal needs of rulers — and these two functions
sometimes stood in productive tension. Access to organized knowledge was, almost
everywhere, a primary determinant of [[social-hierarchy-and-solidarity|social
status and political power]].

## Source Limitations

The comparison is unavoidably lopsided: abundant materials survive for the European
university, the Chinese examination system, Islamic and Buddhist schools, but little
for pre-colonial sub-Saharan Africa or the pre-1500 Americas beyond Spanish
accounts. A "globally inclusive analytical framework" is nonetheless possible
precisely because the underlying function — institutionalized transmission of
collective learning in the service of religion and state — was shared.
