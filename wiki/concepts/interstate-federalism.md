# Interstate Federalism (Interwar Economic Argument)

The economic case for federation among states, as argued within the interwar
federalist movement — documented here from Hayek's September 1939 essay in
the *New Commonwealth Quarterly* (journal of the New Commonwealth Society,
the British organization founded by David Davies to promote international
federation and collective security through law), published on the eve of
World War II. Hayek engages Lionel Robbins's *Economic Planning and
International Order* ("neither Staatenbund nor Einheitsstaat but
Bundesstaat") and Clarence Streit's *Union Now* proposal for federating the
Atlantic democracies.

## The argument

Hayek argues that interstate federation — whose "main purpose... is to
secure peace" — requires economic union, and that economic union in turn
structurally forecloses economic nationalism and planning at *both* levels:

- Political union without economic union does not last (Austria-Hungary);
  common defense requires common tariff, money, and finance.
- Free internal movement of goods, capital, and labor strips member states
  of independent tariff, monetary, and price-support policy — "the Union
  becomes one single market."
- Protectionism that commands consent within a national community cannot do
  so across heterogeneous peoples: would the French peasant subsidize the
  British chemical industry, or the Swedish workman pay more for oranges to
  help Californian growers?
- Since neither member states nor the federal center can legitimately wield
  full planning powers, "there would have to be less government all round if
  federation is to be practicable."
- Applied to socialism: the USSR "could never submit to the conditions which
  federation would impose... while, at the same time, retaining her
  socialist economy."
- Existing federations (US, Switzerland) show states evading tariff bans via
  disguised regulatory barriers, requiring federal constitutional restraint
  (Hayek cites the US due-process clauses).

Hayek closes by calling international federation "the logical consummation
of the liberal program," faulting nineteenth-century liberalism for its
entanglements first with nationalism (the liberation movements of Ireland,
Greece, Belgium, Poland, Italy) and then with socialism.

## Emic vs. Etic

Emic: the 1930s federalist movement's own vocabulary (federal union,
Bundesstaat). Etic: historians of European integration read the essay as an
early theorization of what later became the "market-making vs.
market-correcting" tension in the EU — a use Hayek did not live to endorse
in those terms.

## Limitations and Critiques

The essay is a partisan intervention: it welcomes federation partly
*because* it constrains socialism, which federalists of the left (and later
theorists of "social Europe") read as a bug, not a feature. Historians of
European integration debate how much lineage runs from interwar federalism
through Hayek to actual postwar institutions.
