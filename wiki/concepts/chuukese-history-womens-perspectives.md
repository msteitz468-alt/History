# Chuukese History — Women's Perspectives and Indigenous Methodology

A framework for decolonizing the history of Chuuk (Federated States of Micronesia)
by recovering women's roles, matrilineal authority, and Indigenous epistemologies
that have been suppressed, distorted or erased by 300 years of successive colonial
rule and by a male-dominated, outsider-centred scholarly tradition.

## Definition and Scope

Chuuk is a group of islands forming one of the four states of the Federated States
of Micronesia (FSM), with a population of approximately 50,000 — roughly half the
FSM total. Chuuk Lagoon, the geographic centre, is large enough that the islands
within its reef are divided into three administrative regions. The state endured
successive colonial rule: Spain (1521–1898), Germany (1885–1914), Japan (1914–1944),
United States (1945–1986), with self-governance restored on ratification of the FSM
Constitution in May 1979.

> "Colonial history has always presented a silent history of Micronesia in general
> and Chuuk in particular. To date, Chuuk still lacks a comprehensive written history."
> (Kim, ch. 4, in Jones & Matsuda eds., *Cambridge History of the Pacific Ocean*,
> Vol. 1, 2022)

## The "Silent History" Problem

Early foreign accounts — by Spanish, French, Scottish, and German visitors including
Alonso de Arellano, Dumont d'Urville, Andrew Cheyne, and Alfred Tetens — portrayed
Chuuk as "Dreaded Hogoleu," a place defined by warfare and violence. This reputation
became self-reinforcing in scholarship and popular perception, producing the
"Chuuk problem": the stereotype of Chuukese men as violent and Chuukese women
as silent, submissive, and domestic.

Kim argues this image arose from two interlocking forces:
1. **Colonial erasure**: American anthropological fieldwork since the 1940s focused on
   reproduction, sexuality, and domestic violence, rarely including women's own voices
   or Indigenous historical traditions.
2. **Methodological gap**: Most historical archives are "based on US views of the
   region and pay little attention to the local and Indigenous viewpoints." Historian
   David Hanlon observed that "colonialism, by its very nature, stands hostile and
   oblivious to Indigenous versions and visions of local pasts."

Sarah Smith noted "only one of these early ethnographic studies included Chuukese
women's reproduction and sexuality actually based on research with women (and
conducted) by a woman."

## The Matrilineal Reality: Chuukese Women as Leaders

Contrary to the colonial portrait, Chuuk is a **matrilineal society** in which
ancestry is traced through the female line and one's matrilineal clan determines
who may become chief. Kim argues this makes it also functionally a **matriarchal**
society:

> "In a matrilineal Chuukese society, ancestry is traced through the female line.
> One's matrilineal ancestry determines one's kinship clan and one's clan determines
> who becomes chief. Therefore, Chuukese women determine and decide who becomes chief."
> (Kim, ch. 4)

A Chuukese elder told Kim: "No, you have it wrong, my child. Women were much more
powerful than men in historic societies."

Colonial powers (missionaries included) disrupted: land ownership structures,
traditional leadership systems, and customary schools — all of which had been
spheres of women's authority. Christian missionaries "forbade and degraded traditional
practices" including dancing, tattooing, and local music, associated these with sexual
promiscuity, and suppressed the traditional knowledge women were the custodians of.

## Indigenous Historical Concepts

### Uruon Chuuk
The collection of Chuukese oral histories (*Uruon Chuuk: A Resource of Oral Legends,
Traditions and History of Truk*, compiled by T. Chipen, 1979) documents women as
present alongside men in stories of creation, warfare, kinship, navigation,
inter-island migration, and magic. The oral corpus directly contradicts the colonial
image of women as silent.

### Noos — Weaving as Historical Methodology
*Noos* literally refers to a *kieki* — a mat made from dried coconut and pandanus
leaves woven together. As historical concept: "Literally, noos refers to a specific way,
customs, or tradition that defines and identifies a group of people. It can also mean
historical telling in an orderly fashion." Access to stories is governed by kinship,
clan membership, and island affiliation:

> "Stories are protocol-driven because they are significant and hold sensitive
> information. There are times and places for stories to be told."

History told through noos is understood as relational and contextual ("it is about
connection, interconnection, relationships, and your space on that mat"),
not linear and universal.

### Nien Aroor — Chuukese Historiography
The second key Indigenous concept recognizes that different forms of history —
"sung, danced, chanted, spoken, carved, woven, painted, filmed, sculpted, and rapped,
as well as written" (David Hanlon's formulation) — are all valid historical forms.
Chuukese song poetry, per ethnomusicologist Brian Diettrich, has been "an important
means of engaging and marking history, particularly because texts reveal Chuukese
voices, memories and feelings."

### Fairo — Respect and Social Harmony
*Fairo* (broadly: respect, humility, modesty, courtesy — "to bow low, move from
below, or crawl from underneath") is the central organizing principle of Chuukese
social life. It governs family relationships, knowledge dissemination, environmental
practices, leadership, and gender relations. Chuukese historian John Roussan
explains that "the harmony and stability of a society is dependent on three things:
fairo, wealth of knowledge and valuables, and most importantly women. If a woman is
missing from this concept, then a society is not healthy."

### The Tur — The Central Metaphor
A *tur* is a woven cloth of coconut fibre. Kim uses Nienikachaw's chant and tur as
the organizing metaphor for Chuukese women's history. The tur "symbolizes *eterenges*
(family kinship) and the clan of Sopwunupi, the first and highest-ranking clan in the
Chuuk archipelago." Kintoki Joseph explains: "Interweaving of the fibres represents
the love and interdependence of each member of the lineage. The tur also harken
back to origins and ancestry, bringing a historic unity that cannot be destroyed by
earthly means."

The chant of Nienikachaw:
> *Revealing, revealing, revealing words. Revealing, revealing, revealing
> righteousness. Let us reveal the tur of Nienikachaw from Tonachaw. For to
> unloose the tur is to destroy the tur, and to destroy the tur is to end the unity.*

## Key Figures in Indigenous Tradition

### Nikoupwupw Fenu (Bearer of the Land)
The Chuukese creator goddess and origin of all life. In Chuukese language,
*nikoupwupw* means a woman who breastfeeds and *fenu* means land — she is "the
breast which feeds land." Catholic missionary Father Laurentius Bolling (1912–13)
recorded that she "lived at the bottom of the western Pacific. She piled volcanic rocks
onto her back and arms until they broke the surface, creating islands in the Caroline
Archipelago." She is "regarded as the procreator of humankind, mother matriarch,
and protector, guardian of Chuuk and all her creations."

### Nienikachaw
One of the most prominent women in Chuukese *Itang* (historic lore). Sister of Sou
Kachaw, highest ruler of the Kachaw entity. When she learned of conflict among
the Lords of the Chuuk islands, she travelled to Chuuk and climbed Mount Tonoachaw
to weave her *tur* — an act of peace-making and spiritual invocation.

### The Kachau Empire
Ancient Chuukese oral history describes the Kachau entity as a governance structure
covering as far east as Kosrae, west to Pohnpei, Chuuk, Marianas, Yap, and parts of
Palau — possibly including the Marshall Islands. The *sou* (lords) met regularly at
Unungonota on Weno Island, the administrative centre and the place where the first
peoples with Sou Kachaw established the first clans of Chuuk. This pre-colonial
political structure is absent from most published Micronesian histories.

## Colonial Sequence and its Effects on Women

| Colonial power | Period | Key impacts on women |
|---|---|---|
| Spain | 1521–1898 | Initial exposure; focus on Mariana Islands; Caroline Islands claimed but neglected |
| Germany | 1885/1899–1914 | Disruption of customary land-holding |
| Japan | 1914–1944 | Japanese administrative patriarchalism; disruption of matrilineal land authority |
| United States | 1945–1986 | Trust Territory; American anthropological framing; Americanization marginalized Indigenous voices |

Post-independence (1979), women remain "grossly under-represented in all island
governments" (Bennett, ch. 5, citing Pacific Islands Forum data).

## New Generation of Local Historians

Kim acknowledges PhD works by Anisto Walter, Margarita Cholymay, Gonzaga Puas,
Vidalino Raatior, L.J. Rayphand, and Joakim Jojo Peter as historians who are
"decolonizing and indigenizing" Chuukese history by prioritizing local history and
perspectives over outsiders' interests.

## Historiography

Kim's chapter intervenes in Pacific historiography from within: as a Chuukese
scholar with access to oral traditions through clan and kinship ties, she argues
against the separation of scholarship from the relational networks that Indigenous
knowledge depends upon. Her methodological stance echoes Māori scholar Linda
Tuhiwai Smith's call in *Decolonizing Methodologies* (1999) for "representing,
weaving (writing) histories, claiming, celebrating, remembering and indigenizing
gendering, envisioning, and connecting."

Judith Bennett's companion chapter (ch. 5) situates this within the broader debate
in Pacific history: the 1996 Pacific History Association conference saw an Indigenous
scholar raise an independent flag demanding more attention to "the Indigenous lens
and voice," and subsequent decades have seen new nodes of Pacific history research
emerge in universities within the Pacific Islands themselves.
