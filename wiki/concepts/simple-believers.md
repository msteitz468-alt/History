# Simple Believers

**Simple believers** are the great majority of religious adherents in the late antique and early medieval Middle East — overwhelmingly agrarian, mostly illiterate, and typically lacking formal theological training — whom church leaders themselves called “the simple.” The category is **not** a synonym for “simple-minded,” nor is it limited to the laity: monks, priests, and even bishops could be simple believers.

## Emic vs. etic

- **Emic:** Late antique Christian authors across confessions labeled ordinary Christians “simple” (Gk. *haplousteroi* / *idiōtai*; Syr. *pshiṭā* / *hedyoṭā*; later Christian Arabic). Gregory of Nazianzus, Anastasius of Sinai, Ephrem, Origen, and many Syriac pastoral writers design preaching and discipline for unequal capacity.
- **Etic:** [[tannous-making-medieval-middle-east-2018|Tannous]] elevates this pastoral category into a **historiographical lens**: once the simple are recentered, post-[[council-of-chalcedon-451|Chalcedonian]] schism, Christian–Muslim encounter, and gradual [[islamization-of-the-middle-east|Islamization]] look less like elite doctrinal contests and more like social processes among people who often could not fully understand the disputes that divided their leaders.

> "The great majority of Christians in the Middle East, I will suggest in Part I of this book, belonged to what church leaders referred to as ‘the simple.’ They were overwhelmingly agrarian, mostly illiterate, and likely had little understanding of the theological complexities that split apart the Christian community in the region. ‘Simple’ here does not connote ‘simple-minded,’ … nor should it be understood as a category restricted to the laity: there were monks, priests, and even bishops who were simple believers."
> — Tannous, Introduction

## Continuum, not two tiers

Binary elite/popular or clergy/laity models mislead. Tannous describes a **continuum of understanding**: theologically unschooled clergy and theologically literate laity both existed; interaction across the continuum shaped post-Chalcedonian Christianity. “Simple” also had a **rhetorical life** (heresy charged with seducing the simple; losses blamed on poaching) that did not always map onto real social groups.

## Lived confessional identity

Ordinary allegiance often rested less on doctrinal reflection than on loyalty to a holy man or bishop, local custom, kinship, occupation, or geography:

- Some simple Christians were confessionally unaware (e.g. Leontius’s actor among Christian Arabs who attended church “without realizing there was any difference between Christians”).
- Others knew affiliation but deferred debate to experts (Alexandrian Miaphysite shipmasters before Justinian, 560).
- Disputes over Chalcedon often used **non-doctrinal proofs**: control of holy places, fire-ordeals, miracle stories — tactics that avoided natures, *prosōpa*, and hypostases.
- ~250 years after Chalcedon, [[jacob-of-edessa|Jacob of Edessa]]’s world still shows ideological church separation without fully worked-out day-to-day confessional identity among Syria’s simple: shared Eucharist across rival labels, cross-confessional funerals and child donations to monasteries, clergy who would not observe canons.

> "we are dealing with a society where a separation between competing and rival churches has taken place at an ideological level, but where the actual day-to-day implications of the intellectual and doctrinal partition of the Christian community have not been fully worked out."
> — Tannous, Ch. 3

## Sacraments and community formation

Violence, polemic, and economic pressure all mattered less, for Tannous, than the **Eucharist and other sacraments** as tools uniting simple and learned. Yet precisely because sacraments were ubiquitous and powerful, in a theologically illiterate and fractured landscape they risked **detachment from confessional frameworks**: access to the Eucharist could matter more than the celebrant’s Christology — fostering the confessional indifference that reformers like Jacob fought.

## Early Islam and conversion

The same lens applies to early Muslims: late mass converts and their descendants often had limited grasp of a still-elaborating Islam. Earliest Christian–Muslim encounter is thus framed as **simple Christians meeting simple (or late-converted) Muslims**. Conversion from Christianity to Islam need not have been religiously total: one could become Muslim while retaining Christian practices and beliefs. See [[islamization-of-the-middle-east]].

## Analytical payoffs

1. **Layering of knowledge** within the same confession (see [[theological-literacy]]).
2. Confessional indifference/confusion as historically significant, not noise.
3. Schools, sacraments, and competition as the real engines of [[post-chalcedonian-confessional-formation|confessional formation]].
4. Demographic “Dark Matter”: community size ≠ literary survival (see [[dark-matter-historiography]]).

## Limitations and critiques

- Reconstructed largely from elite texts that *talk about* the simple; direct simple voices are scarce.
- Risk of flattening regional and confessional diversity if “the simple” becomes undifferentiated.
- Complementary to, not a replacement for, high-theology and institutional histories.
