# Cyrus Cylinder

Clay cylinder inscribed in Akkadian with the proclamation of Cyrus the Great after his conquest of Babylon in 539 BCE. It presents Cyrus as the restorer of the cult of Marduk, condemns the previous king Nabonidus, and claims a policy of returning deported peoples and restoring temples.

## Definition and Scope

The inscription is a classic piece of Achaemenid royal propaganda. It justifies the Persian takeover by portraying Nabonidus as impious and Cyrus as divinely chosen by Marduk. It mentions the return of images of gods and peoples to their homelands, which later interpreters (especially in the 20th century) anachronistically linked to a "charter of human rights" or policy of religious tolerance. In reality, it follows standard Mesopotamian royal rhetoric of the pious conqueror.

## Origin and Key Content

Issued shortly after the fall of Babylon. Key themes from the text and contemporary sources:
- Marduk's anger at Nabonidus leads him to choose Cyrus.
- Cyrus enters Babylon "without battle" or "in peace."
- Restoration of cults and return of exiles (including the Jewish return from Babylonian captivity, aligning with Biblical portrayal of Cyrus as the Lord's anointed).

## Historiography

The Cylinder was excavated in Babylon in 1879 and is now in the British Museum. Its modern fame exploded in the 1970s when the Shah of Iran used it for propaganda as the "first charter of human rights." Scholars (e.g., in Cambridge histories and Achaemenid studies) emphasize it as standard legitimating rhetoric rather than a revolutionary human-rights document. It must be read alongside Babylonian chronicles, the Bible, and Herodotus for biases. The "tolerant" image of Cyrus contrasts with the more coercive aspects of later Achaemenid rule and serves Persian self-presentation against Assyrian/Babylonian models.
