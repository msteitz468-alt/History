# Massive Retaliation

**Massive retaliation** was the declaratory doctrine associated with US Secretary of State John Foster Dulles and the Eisenhower "New Look" defense policy of the mid-1950s. It threatened "instant, massive retaliation" with nuclear weapons in response to aggression, whether nuclear or conventional, anywhere the United States chose to respond. The policy aimed to deter Soviet and Chinese expansion at far lower cost than maintaining large conventional forces for every contingency.

## Context and Rationale

After the Korean War and with the Soviet Union acquiring the atomic (and soon hydrogen) bomb, the Truman administration's NSC-68 had called for major conventional rearmament alongside nuclear buildup. The Eisenhower team sought to escape the fiscal burden of "containing" communism everywhere with ground forces. Nuclear weapons, especially with growing US delivery capability (Strategic Air Command), appeared to offer "more bang for the buck."

The doctrine was shaped by the British Chiefs of Staff global strategy paper of 1952, which concluded that "the primary deterrent against this or any other aggression should be the knowledge that it would invite immediate and crushing retaliation" and that "priority ought to be given to the long-range air forces of NATO. The Allies could not afford to build up sufficient conventional strength in Europe to defeat the Soviet Union in a clash of land armies." Sir John Slessor became a major propagandist for "the Great Deterrent".

On 30 October 1953 these conclusions were enshrined in NSC-162/2. It defined the basic problem as "meeting the Soviet threat" while avoiding "seriously weakening the US economy". It required:
1. "a strong military posture, with emphasis on the capability for inflicting massive retaliatory damage by offensive striking power";
2. forces "in readiness to move rapidly initially to counter aggression ... and to hold vital areas"; and
3. a mobilization base.

Explicitly: "In the event of hostilities, the United States will consider nuclear weapons to be as available for use as other munitions." (Freedman 1981: "The New Look", NSC-162/2).

## Dulles's Presentation

Dulles had long spoken of the "capacity to counter-attack ... by action of our own choosing" as the "ultimate deterrent". In his January 1954 speech to the Council on Foreign Relations he surveyed the cold war and argued that "Emergency measures are costly; they are superficial; and they imply that the enemy has the initiative." The solution was to place "more reliance on deterrent power and less dependence on local defensive power":

"Local defense will always be important. But there is no local defense which alone will contain the mighty landpower of the Communist world. Local defenses must be reinforced by the further deterrent of massive retaliatory power. A potential aggressor must know that he cannot always prescribe battle conditions that suit him. ... The basic decision that had been made was to ‘depend primarily upon a great capacity to retaliate, instantly, by means and at places of our own choosing’."

In a later *Foreign Affairs* clarification he stressed that it "should not be stated in advance precisely what would be the scope of military action if new aggression occurred. ... That is a matter as to which the aggressor had best remain ignorant." He repudiated sole reliance on "large-scale strategic bombing". The formula for response was that "a potential aggressor be left in no doubt that he would be certain to suffer damage outweighing any possible gains from aggression." (Freedman 1981: "Dulles and Massive Retaliation").

## Distinctions and Ambiguities

NSC-162/2 anticipated criticisms and noted that when "a stage of atomic plenty and ample means of delivery" arrived the result could be "a ‘stalemate, with both sides reluctant to initiate general warfare’". It distinguished (at least implicitly) between strategic and tactical nuclear weapons and recognized "that ‘as general war becomes more devastating for both sides the threat to resort to it becomes less available as a sanction against local aggression’".

The public impression, however, was of an undiscriminating threat of massive nuclear retaliation against any communist aggression. This impression was reinforced by statements such as Vice President Nixon's that "Rather than let the Communists nibble us to death all over the world in little wars we would rely in the future primarily on our massive mobile retaliatory power which we could use in our discretion against the major source of aggression at times and places that we choose."

## Criticisms and Limits

The doctrine faced immediate intellectual attack on grounds of credibility. As Soviet capabilities grew, the threat became "suicidal and therefore incredible". Critics described the choice as "suicide or surrender" or "holocaust or humiliation".

Freedman analyses the domestic-law analogy often employed by Dulles (locks on doors; community security system that punishes) and shows why it broke down strategically: "with airpower any country could put another country at its mercy. There were no problems of making the offender available for punishment. The sanction could be applied with, in all probability, a minimum of resistance. The offender, nevertheless, would not have to be apprehended by the forces of the law ... and so would remain a free agent. Moreover, he might well have means of replying to the punishment. The fact that a transgressor maintained options other than full compliance with the dictates of international law ... undermined the credibility of the threat of sanctions." (Freedman 1981: "MASSIVE RETALIATION").

An immediate and lasting result was that NATO strategy oriented around hope that nuclear weapons could be employed to favour the West. Yet even in Indo-China (Dien Bien Phu), nuclear use was considered but dismissed partly because it would still have required large ground forces. The "New Look" was in part retrospective — explaining how Korea "ought to have been fought" during the remaining period of US nuclear superiority — and economically attractive because "a nuclear strategy was far cheaper than a conventional strategy".

The policy provoked the counter-movement toward limited war theory.

## In Freedman's Account

Freedman places massive retaliation as the high-water mark of the "spirit of the offence" in the early nuclear age — an attempt to preserve usable nuclear advantage even after the loss of monopoly. Its decline was driven both by growing Soviet capabilities and by the intellectual work of the "formal strategists" who demonstrated the logical problems of relying on incredible threats. The British contribution and the economic logic were critical enablers. The doctrine was never as absolute in war plans as in public rhetoric, but the presentation shaped expectations and debates for years.
