## Definition

The Salafiyya is a current within Islamic reform — overlapping with but distinct from [[islamic-modernism]] — that holds the Quran, the Sunna (authenticated hadith), and the practice of the first generations of Muslims (*salaf*) as the sole authoritative sources for Islamic life, rejecting later accretions including classical jurisprudential *taqlid*, Sufi spirituality, intercession through saints, and popular devotional practices. In its earliest phase (associated with Rashid Rida and his journal *al-Manar*) it was compatible with selective engagement with modernity; in its later development it converged with Wahhabism and became more hostile to innovation.

Hodgson defines the Salafiyya in the context of Muhammad Abduh's legacy: "Some of his followers led a movement which eventually spread throughout the Arab lands and even to Malaysia: the 'Salafiyyah' movement among those 'ulamā' who sought to reassert a Sharī'ah-minded Islamic orthodoxy free of traditional Sufism and still more of local superstition."

## Emic vs. Etic

**Emic**: Proponents understand the Salafiyya not as an innovation but as a *return* — to the pure, uncorrupted Islam of the Prophet and his companions before later theological elaborations (kalam), juridical fossilization (taqlid), and Sufi accretions obscured it. The label signals authenticity, not novelty.

**Etic**: Historians recognize the Salafiyya as a distinctly modern phenomenon — a response to European hegemony that selected and constructed a usable past from the Islamic tradition, shaped in important ways by the same polemical and rationalist criteria it encountered in European thought. Its anti-traditionalism is itself a modern posture.

## Historical Development

### Origins in the Islamic Modernist Circle

The Salafiyya emerged from the intellectual circle of [[muhammad-abduh]] in Egypt, but represented a shift in emphasis. Where Abduh was willing to invoke rationalist principles (including a Mu'tazili-inflected view of reason's capacity to demonstrate moral truths) and engaged with Western positivism, the Salafi current prioritized strict textual fidelity and Shari-mindedness. The pivot figure was **Rashid Rida** (1865–1935), Abduh's Syrian disciple and the editor of *al-Manar* (The Lighthouse, 1898–1935), the most widely circulating Arabic periodical of religious reform. Rida moved progressively toward a Wahhabi-aligned Salafism, especially after World War I destroyed the Ottoman framework.

### Relationship to Wahhabism

The Salafiyya and Wahhabism shared a commitment to textual purism and opposition to popular Sufi devotion, but had different intellectual genealogies (Egyptian modernist vs. eighteenth-century Arabian purist) and initially different political valences. Their convergence accelerated after Ibn Saud consolidated control of the Hijaz (1924–25) and the Saudi-Wahhabi state became the primary patron of Salafi publishing and education. By the inter-war period "this movement was shy of any radical social changes. Eventually it found itself in close sympathy with the Wahhâbî movement in Arabia in its more conciliatory twentieth-century form, dedicated to a restoration of a pure Shar'î life adjusted to Modern technology but with a minimum of concessions to Modern ideas of human relations."

### Pan-Islamic and Arab Nationalist Dimensions

The Salafiyya's pan-Islamic potential was real but ambiguous. Its potential universalism "tended to amount to a demand only for a pan-Arab nationalism on a Muslim basis" because Arabic was exalted as the sacred language and Arabs as the special heirs of the prophetic mission. This made the Salafiyya simultaneously a resource for pan-Arab ideology and a limiting factor on genuinely transnational Muslim solidarity.

### Decline in Inter-War Egypt

Under the political conditions of the inter-war period — the rise of inter-faith Arabism, the social crisis in Egypt, the need for a nationalism broad enough to include Christian Arabs — the purely Salafi position "lost popularity." The Muslim Brotherhood (founded 1928 by Hasan al-Banna, a product of the Salafi milieu) represented a political radicalization that absorbed Salafi religious commitments but added mass organization and activist engagement.

## Core Commitments

1. **Return to the Quran and authenticated Sunna** as sole normative sources; rejection of medieval jurisprudential opinions (*taqlid*) as binding.
2. **Opposition to Sufism** and popular devotional practices (saint veneration, intercession, dhikr ceremonies with music) as innovations (*bid'a*) not sanctioned by the salaf.
3. **Rejection of kalam** (scholastic theology) in its classical forms as unnecessarily philosophical.
4. **Compatibility with modern science and technology** in principle — the Salafiyya distinguished modern technical knowledge from Western cultural values and accepted the former while rejecting (in theory) the latter.
5. **Arabic as the privileged language** of Islamic authenticity — driving both a linguistic-nationalist and a theological claim.

## Contested Dimensions

The Salafiyya is contested on multiple axes:

- **Internal**: The degree of permissible engagement with modern knowledge, political action, and state institutions has been disputed continuously from Rida onward. "Quietist" Salafis emphasize religious education and withdrawal from politics; "activist" Salafis (converging with the Muslim Brotherhood tradition) emphasize social and political transformation.
- **Historical legitimacy**: Critics argue that "returning to the salaf" involves a constructed and selective reading of early Islamic history rather than genuine historical recovery. The selection of what counts as authentic salaf practice is itself shaped by modern polemical needs.
- **Relationship to violence**: Post-1980s developments (jihadist appropriation of Salafi vocabulary) are entirely outside the period covered by this source but have retroactively complicated the term's scholarly use.

## Limitations and Critiques

Hodgson's implicit critique is structural: the Salafiyya's rejection of the Persianate-Sufi heritage as "decadence" reflected a real decline in those institutions' functional capacity but misread their historical significance. The apparent advantages of Islamicate cosmopolitanism — urban character, high social mobility, geographical focus — had been "largely destroyed" by European hegemony before the Salafis arrived to condemn them; the Salafi critique thus targeted a casualty of hegemony while attributing the damage to internal moral failure.
