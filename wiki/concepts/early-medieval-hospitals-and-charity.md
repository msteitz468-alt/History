# Hospitals, Public Health, and Charity in Merovingian Gaul

**The hospital as an idea imported from Byzantium, and the small but real Frankish institutional
response to poverty and disease.** Peregrine Horden's chapter (Oxford Handbook, Ch. 14) treats the
hospital as "not an obvious solution… an idea, not a reflex," arising in specific circumstances. Gaul
acquired roughly **20 secure hospital foundations** (perhaps 40 by generous counting) in the sixth and
seventh centuries — "small in both absolute and relative terms" against the Byzantine world's 160-plus
charitable facilities, but a genuine institutional novelty.

## Disease and Public Health

Contemporary medicine had "no concept resembling modern notions of infection or contagion," working
instead with **miasma** — yet Gregory of Tours used *contagio* of the 588 Marseille plague and "gives
a strikingly accurate depiction of the stuttering progress" of bubonic plague (*Yersinia pestis*),
imagining "micromiasmas… in bales or containers." Standard responses were religious — fasts, vigils,
rogation processions, dividing a city among protecting saints. The chapter's most striking document is
the **sole surviving early-medieval example of movement-restriction as disease control**: Gallus of
Clermont ordered Desiderius of Cahors to "send guards" to the diocesan frontiers to stop traders
returning from the Rodez fairs from carrying the Provençal plague home.

## Hospital Foundations

The hospital originated in the fourth-century Byzantine world and spread slowly west.

- **First Gallic hospital — Caesarius of Arles (bp. 502–542):** a "*spatiosissima domus*" with beds,
  bedding, and a carer, set beside the basilica so patients could "listen undisturbed to the holy
  office" — "quite possibly… the first" Christian hospital in Gaul, and selective in admissions
  (Caesarius "wanted exemplary patients who had attended to the needs of their souls first").
- **Royal hospital at Lyon — Childebert I and Ultrogotha (c. 542):** confirmed by the Council of
  Orléans (549), which labelled any appropriator of its endowment a "*necator pauperum*" (killer of
  the poor) and admitted both the sick and pilgrims.
- **First leper hospital:** built by Bishop Agricola at Chalon-sur-Saône (Caesarius's hometown).
- **Radegund's house at Athies** for needy women — "the mistress of the palace was a servant of the
  poor" (see [[radegund-of-poitiers]]) — and **Praeiectus's** *xenodochium* at Clermont "following the
  manner of the easterners," with doctors for ~20 patients.

## Charity beyond the Hospital

Most relief was not institutional. The **matricula** — a register of approved local poor — provided
"essentially just a regulated outdoor relief," its members sometimes taking on shrine duties. With
perhaps **30% of Francia in church hands** by the eighth century (one bishop holding land "as big as
half a modern département"), the Council of Tours (567) urged each city and village to "feed its poor"
so they "do not wander off to cities elsewhere" — a hint of parish-level relief growing with the
parish network. The Church preferred vetted small groups to "uncontrolled throngs."

## Chronology and Historiography

Hospital foundation was essentially absent before 500, concentrated in the sixth–seventh centuries,
and "withered" in the eighth — perhaps from Charles Martel's secularization of church property (see
[[charles-martel]]) and the deurbanization of the early Carolingian world (see
[[merovingian-cities]]). Founders were "royal, including royal women, members of the lay aristocracy,
bishops and monks, or nuns," the predominant bishops being "those with experience at court" (see
[[merovingian-polity]], [[merovingian-episcopate]]). Sternberg's database underlies the numbers; Brown's
work on poverty and power frames the interpretation.
