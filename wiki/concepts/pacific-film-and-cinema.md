## Definition and Scope

Pacific film and cinema encompasses the full history of moving-image production in,
about, and increasingly by the peoples of Oceania from the late 1890s to the present.
Mawyer frames this history around a fundamental tension and eventual transformation:
the shift from a **cinema of Oceania** — films made predominantly by Euro-American,
Australian, and (from the mid-twentieth century) Japanese outsiders who projected
metropolitan imaginaries onto Pacific worlds — to an **Oceanian Cinema** — works by
Indigenous and local filmmakers who "seize control of the camera" and produce
restorative, place-grounded representations.

> "The region's film history has seen a dramatic turn from a cinema of Oceania to what
> might be called an Oceanian Cinema as decolonizing and empowering works by Indigenous
> and local filmmakers emerged in the last decades of the twentieth century."

The concept treats film both as a window onto shifting Euro-American beliefs about
Oceania and as an active historical force shaping colonial administration, tourism,
economic development, and the self-understandings of Pacific peoples.

## Historical Development

### Origins (1896–1919)
Film arrived in Oceania almost simultaneously with its invention. Local newspapers
recorded film screenings in Auckland in 1896 and Honolulu in 1897. One of Edison's
crews shot Hawaiian youths diving as early as 1898 (*Kanaka Diving for Money, No. 2*;
*Boys Diving HI6361*), among the oldest surviving footage from Oceania. From the
outset, early films oscillated between claims to actuality (travelogue, ethnographic
documentary) and romantic othering: "This tension between actuality and unreality is
among the most poignant conventions of early Pacific filmmaking."

The Cambridge Anthropological Expedition to the Torres Strait (1898, led by Alfred
Court Haddon) produced short films of cultural practices including songs and dances.
Early footage in Micronesia and New Guinea — *Neu-Guinea* (1904–6),
*Voelkenkunliche Filmdokumente aus der Sudsee* (1910) — documented colonised
societies under German control. New Zealand filmmaker Alfred Whitehouse shot
approximately ten shorts between 1898 and 1900; George Tarr's *Hinemoa* (1914) is
generally regarded as New Zealand's first feature film. Gaston Méliès (brother of
Georges) shot at least four films in Tahiti and Aotearoa New Zealand in 1912–13, all
subsequently lost — a reminder that 75–90 per cent of all pre-1929 film no longer exists.

Narrative fiction set in Pacific locales began immediately. *The Shark God* (1913),
*Hearts Adrift* (1914, starring Mary Pickford), and *Aloha Oe* (1915) established
representational tropes — the female volcano sacrifice, the receptive indigenous woman
saving the European colonist — that would persist for decades.

### Silent Era and the Conventions of Escapism (1920s–1930s)
Post-WWI Hollywood increasingly exploited Pacific settings for romance and adventure.
*Moana: A Romance of the Golden Age* (1926, Robert Flaherty) is generally credited as
among the first documentaries, yet relied on staged scenes and narrative conventions
borrowed from fiction. *White Shadows in the South Seas* (1929) and *The Pagan* (1929)
similarly blended claims to actuality with colonial fantasy.

By the mid-1920s a stable repertoire of conventions was established: the noble/dangerous
native, the sexualised indigenous woman, the purifying force of tropical nature, colonial
resource extraction as adventure. *Tabu: A Story of the South Seas* (1931) won the
Academy Award for cinematography. *Bird of Paradise* (1932) and *King Vidor's* version
entrenched the volcano-sacrifice trope. *Mutiny on the Bounty* (1935) inaugurated what
became a recurring Hollywood obsession with the Tahiti–Bounty story.

The shift away from location shooting in the Pacific itself — made possible by the advent
of sound, which required studio conditions — concentrated Hollywood's "Pacific" largely
on Kauai and Oahu: "by the end of the thirties and until the late twentieth century when
relocalization, democratization, and Indigenization of Paciﬁc ﬁlmmaking can be said to
have begun, relatively fewer commercial ﬁlms were actually produced on islands other
than Kauai and Oahu."

### War, Propaganda, and Ethnographic Film (1940s–1950s)
World War II transformed Pacific film in two directions simultaneously. Feature war films
(*Wake Island*, 1942; *Jungle Patrol*, 1944) presented Pacific Islanders as largely invisible
pawns in an Allied-versus-Axis struggle, abstracting the islands into strategic geography.
At the same time, visual anthropology became a standard ethnographic tool: *The
Polynesians of Kapingamarangi* (1947–50) and *Mokil* (1948) promoted images of fixed,
unchanging cultural practices and the value of preservation-through-documentation.

Nuclear testing in the Marshall Islands from the early 1950s brought most of the world's
colour film stock to the atolls: "at the time of the Bravo tests (1954) most of the world's
existing colour ﬁlm stock was brought to the Marshall Islands." The resulting 6,000+ hours
of footage was declassified only in 1997. *South Pacific* (1957) simultaneously became a
pinnacle of Pacific misrepresentation.

Japanese atomic-monster (kaiju) films beginning with *Gojira* (1954) introduced a
non-Western representational tradition with its own colonial valences: "sub-plots included
substantial representations of Paciﬁc Islanders which both differed from yet echoed
Euro-American representational tropes of primitivism, benign colonialism, and a desire
for restoration of colonial ties between Japan and the islands severed by the Americans."

### Decolonisation, Television, and the Rise of Indigenous Filmmakers (1960s–1980s)
The political revolutions of the 1960s radicalised film as a medium and its study as a
discipline; film scholars embraced literary theory and began treating films as texts to be
decoded. Smaller cameras and portable sound equipment democratised production.

The most important development of the 1970s was the emergence of an Indigenous
film aesthetic. Māori director Barry Barclay's television series *Tangata Whenua* (1974)
brought Māori tradition to a mainstream audience. In Micronesia, the Micronesian
Transition Series (from 1969) saw Micronesian writers and directors take creative control.
Dennis O'Rourke's *Ileksen: Politics in Papua New Guinea* (1978) inaugurated a new age
of documentary ethnography from newly independent nations.

The 1980s saw reflexive ethnographic documentary — O'Rourke's *Cannibal Tours*
(1987) and *Half Life: A Parable for the Nuclear Age* (1986) — alongside assertively
Indigenous feature filmmaking. Merata Mita's *Bastion Point* (1981) and *Patu!* (1983)
appropriated news footage to create counter-readings of national events. Barry Barclay's
*Ngati* (1987) and Mita's *Mauri* (1988) established Māori directors as feature filmmakers.
The affordability of video equipment in Hawai'i catalysed a community of videographers
as "a weapon for change among political activists and cultural stewards" (Vilsoni
Hereniko, cited in source).

### The Emergence of Oceanian Cinema (1990s–2000s)
Mawyer reads the 1990s as a period of transition — and terminal statement — for the
cinema of Oceania. John Patrick Shanley's *Joe vs. the Volcano* (1990), with its
self-conscious deployment of Hollywood Pacific conventions, suggested the non-Indigenous
cinema had exhausted itself. The decade saw nostalgic revivals (*Waterworld*, 1995;
*Rapa Nui*, 1994; *The Island of Dr. Moreau*, 1996) alongside the consolidation of
Oceanian Cinema: Barry Barclay's *Te Rua* (1991), Lee Tamahori's *Once Were Warriors*
(1994), Sima Urale's *O Tamaiti* (1997). In Barclay's thinking, this "process was one
where ﬁlmmakers were responsible for taking control of one's own images in a potently
place-based, place-addressing, yet community-transcending manner."

Niki Caro's *Whale Rider* (2002), drawing on Witi Ihimaera's novel, marked a watershed
in which non-Indigenous but locally embedded filmmakers "fundamentally realign the
camera's eye from the island out." Taika Waititi's career — *Boy* (2010),
*What We Do in the Shadows* (2014), *Thor: Ragnarok* (2017) — extended Māori and
Pacific sensibilities into Hollywood genre filmmaking while sustaining allusive engagement
with Oceanic histories and contemporary dispossession.

## Representational Regimes (Typology)

Mawyer identifies a persistent set of representational frameworks structuring the cinema
of Oceania, which function as "anchors for representations of Oceania in ﬁlm,
institutionalized over time as conventions":

| Framework | Definition |
|---|---|
| Romanticism / Pastoral | Idealised, escapist visions of Pacific worlds; includes "Oceanic Gothic" |
| Classicism | Pacific worlds as a Golden Age analogous to classical Greece or Rome |
| Pacific Naturalism | Pacific as nature-space where the human is not, or is barely, present |
| Ecologicalism | Pacific as fragile ecological balance easily disturbed, leading to typhoons or volcanic cataclysm |
| Instrumental Naturalism | Nature as ready-to-hand resource for outsider extraction |
| Insular Marginalism | Islands as distant, bounded, fragile, lost at sea |
| Insular Determinism | Human agency constrained by island nature, including "human nature on islands" |
| Primitivism / Exoticism | Indigenous peoples as pre-modern Other |
| Sacralism | Pacific worlds as sites of spiritual encounter |
| Teratological | Pacific islands as home of monsters and madness (horror/sci-fi genres) |
| Militarism | Pacific as theatre of strategic violence and purification |

These frameworks intersect; any given film may deploy several simultaneously. They
have operated "below the threshold of audience awareness, with profound implications
for day-to-day encounters, tourism and island development projects, colonial
administration, and economic, political, and social dynamics from local to global scales."

## Historiography

**Source base and scholarly gaps.** The cinema of Oceania has received "relatively
meagre attention despite being the superseding medium for the region's representation
since the twentieth century's dawning." A small number of "usual suspects" (Flaherty's
*Moana*, the Bounty films, *Tabu*) have dominated scholarly attention; many genres and
individual films remain unexamined. The fragmentary survival rate of early film (75–90%
of pre-1929 film is lost) forces historians to extrapolate from newspapers, stills, and
filmmakers' memoirs.

**Approaches.** The chapter models an approach integrating: film-text analysis (genre,
narrative convention, visual tropes); production history (conditions of financing,
technology, location); reception history (what films did to audiences outside Oceania);
and a postcolonial critical framework examining how films upheld or contested the
"self-legitimating logics of the region's colonial regimes." Mawyer draws on the work
of Vilsoni Hereniko, Barry Barclay (*Our Own Image*, 1990), and the broader literature
on representation of Oceania in Western media.

**Key questions (open).** How did film representations uphold colonial logics? How have
they shaped tourist behaviour and economic realities in the Pacific? How much have
Pacific Islanders' experiences of themselves been mediated by the same representational
machinery that shapes outsider imaginations? Much work "remains to be done."

**Collection coverage note.** The source provides a substantive chapter-length treatment
(Mawyer, ch. 42, [[hattori-samson-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v2-2023]]). No other source
in the current collection directly addresses Pacific film history.
