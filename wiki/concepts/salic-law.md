# Salic Law (Pactus Legis Salicae)

**The law code of the Salian Franks**, issued by [[clovis|Clovis]] between **508 and 511** as the
*Pactus Legis Salicae* — sixty-five chapters that make it, "after the Visigothic Law, the oldest
example of a written code for a barbarian kingdom." Its importance for Geary is paradoxical: a
monument of supposedly "Frankish" custom that is itself a **Roman** artefact.

## Definition and Scope

The Pactus is written **in Latin**, and "written law was certainly not a barbarian tradition; the
very act of codifying traditional custom… could only originate under the influence of Roman law."
Its substance is overwhelmingly a tariff of **fines and penalties** aimed at "limit[ing] feuds or
revenge on the part of family groups" — describing a settled society of peasants and herdsmen, not
the conquering army of Clovis. It contains **no Christian elements**, which suggests it codified
pre-conquest Toxandrian custom.

## Authorship and the four rectores

The historian Jean-Pierre Poly proposed that the four *rectores* named as redactors "represented
four high-ranking 'imperial German' officers of the fourth century" — men with the authority to
keep the peace and quell violence. On this reading the code's roots lie in the [[barbarization-of-the-roman-army|late-Roman frontier command]], not in a timeless Germanic past.

## Notable provisions

- **Wergeld and gender**: the wergeld of a woman of childbearing age was **three times** that of an
  ordinary man or of a woman under twelve or over forty — a valuation of reproductive capacity.
- **No legal nobility**: the Pactus knows no defined noble stratum among the Franks; the basic
  distinction is between *ingenui* (free men, "also called simply *Franci*") and the unfree — unlike
  the later Alemannic and Bavarian laws. Higher wergeld came only through proximity to the king (the
  *leudes*, *antrustiones*), implying [[clovis|Clovis]] had "effectively exterminated" any prior
  Frankish nobility. See [[merovingian-franks]].
- **The "Salic land" clause**: a famous chapter excluded women from inheriting "Salic land." Its
  original meaning is unclear, but it was "resurrected in the fourteenth century by French jurists
  eager to avoid seeing the French crown pass to the king of England," becoming the doctrine of
  Salic succession. Frankish practice was in fact more flexible: by the later sixth century Chilperic
  allowed daughters to inherit Salic land where there were no sons.

## Limitations and Critiques

Geary warns against reading the Pactus as a transparent window onto "Germanic" society: it is a
codification, in a Roman idiom, by men trained in Roman administration, of the custom of one Frankish
group at one moment. Like the appeal to Tacitus (see [[ethnogenesis]]), treating it as the timeless
constitution of the Franks mistakes a constructed text for an ancestral essence.

## Oxford Handbook of the Merovingian World (2020)

Alice Rio's chapter in the [[sources/effros-moreira-oxford-handbook-merovingian-world-2020|Oxford Handbook of the Merovingian World]] reads the 65-title *Pactus Legis Salicae* — "probably issued late in the reign of [[clovis|Clovis]]" — through Patrick Wormald's argument that its written form was "an ideologically driven afterthought, designed to project royal authority," not a working code. Its ethnic distinctions are now seen as "contributing to the *engineering* of ethnic identity rather than merely reflecting it: a way of inventing tradition" (see [[ethnogenesis]]). The code mandated torture for slaves (PLS 40.4) and discriminated against women's inheritance (PLS 59.6) — a rule a Marculf formulary calls an "impious custom." Across the volume, PLS underpins analyses of [[gender-in-merovingian-gaul|gender]], [[merovingian-childhood|childhood]], and wergeld (see [[merovingian-legal-culture]]).
