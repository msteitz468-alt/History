# Identity and the Cold War

**National identity** during the Cold War is defined as "the set of values, attributes, and practices that members believe characterize the country and set it off from others." It answers "central if vague questions: Who are we? What are we like? Who are we similar to and different from?" Identities carry "heavy affective weight" and shape foreign policy as well as being shaped by conflict. "Identity makes conflict, and conflict makes identity." (Jervis, Ch. 2 "Identity and the Cold War")

## Soviet and American Identities

American identity during the Cold War included "democracy; individualism and voluntarism as contrasted to strong direction – let alone compulsion – from the government; opposition to concentrated power, especially when wielded by the government; the belief in a supreme being that supplies meaning to life; and a faith that this model or 'way of life' can, should, and eventually will be adopted by others as well." The United States was "founded to be different from the rest of the world (meaning Europe), and it would or at least could remain uncorrupted." "We have it in our power to begin the world over again." (Thomas Paine, quoted in Jervis). This produced "American exceptionalism" with deep roots; unlike Europe, "the United States never had a bourgeois revolution or a strong socialist movement."

Soviet identity "held out its system as one that would eventually spread throughout the world, but its content was very different in being built around the proletariat, the centrality of class conflict, and the transformation of individuals and societies." "From its inception, the Soviet Union had claimed to be an experiment in socialism, a superior alternative to capitalism, for the entire world. If socialism was not superior to capitalism, its existence could not be justified." An additional aspect was "the Soviet Union as a superpower, equal in status and rights to its rival." Soviet leaders "yearned to be treated as equals by the people they consider doomed." Brezhnev placed "great store in the Basic Principles Agreement of 1972 because it seemed to ratify their equality." (Jervis, Ch. 2)

## Symmetries and Asymmetries

Four major similarities heightened conflict rather than dampening it:

1. Each implied "a form of universalism" – values could spread; both "founded not on nationality or myths of blood and common heritage, but on ideas."
2. Both "saw themselves as the standard-bearers of progress and modernity."
3. Both were "second-image" thinkers: "states' foreign policies were deeply influenced by their domestic systems."
4. Each was "prone to expect and seek transformations of politics." For the USSR, "Kto-Kogo – who-whom"; US often reacted to setbacks by "seeking major changes."

One shared belief restrained conflict: "Each side believed that time was on its side, and that if war could be avoided, the long term would bring not only survival but victory."

Four asymmetries were crucial:

- "Soviet identity came from the top down" while American was "much less self-conscious."
- Soviet pivoted on "what it could be" and should lead the world to be; American on "what American society actually was."
- Soviet "grew out of an explicit ideology... formed in explicit opposition to capitalism"; American "developed more slowly" from differentiation from Europe/Britain.
- "The most important implication of the asymmetries was that domestic reverses and the failure of the world to move in desired directions would be corrosive to the Soviet regime and identity." American identity "was left relatively unscathed by the Cold War."

## How Identities Operate and Shape the Conflict

Identity "cannot be completely internal because it forms in response to others." "To hold an identity is to set a boundary, to separate Self from Others." Conflict leads to exaggerating differences. In the Cold War, "each side shunned anything that smacked of the other." Examples: addition of "under God" to Pledge, "in God we trust" as motto, rejection of "compulsion" in community measures.

In the Third World, "the power of identities and the related fact that each side understood the Cold War as a clash of social systems explains much." The region "represented the uncommitted states and peoples. What was at stake was nothing less than each side's view of the rightness of its cause, the universalism of its values, and the answer to the question of whose side history was on." Khrushchev: "The Soviet Union is for change. It believes that it is now in the political arena and it is challenging the capitalist system..." Ideas "cannot be chained or burned." (Jervis quoting Vienna 1961 exchange)

## Détente, Identity, and the End

Détente failed because "the two sides had incompatible expectations." US saw it "as a way to maintain the status quo in the face of American weakness"; Soviets "as a way to attain equal status and gains in the Third World." "Being treated as an equal was both a necessary part of a robust policy in the Third World and a valued end in itself."

The Cold War "ended when Soviet identity shifted, and Reagan refused to reciprocate Soviet concessions until he believed that this was occurring." Gorbachev's "new thinking" contradicted "key elements of Soviet identity": priority to lowering tensions over "supporting progressive movements"; sources of tension in "traditional dynamics of international conflict" rather than class conflict. "Once class conflict was dropped, little remained of the unique Soviet identity and mission in the world." "The Cold War ended only when one side's identity did; it could not have ended peacefully otherwise." American identity "did not need Communism."

## Historiography Note (from this source)

The chapter stresses that while traditional IR sees states as "enemies by position," identity and interest shaped each other. "Each side's interest in many questions was defined in part by its identity." The conflict over the Third World "is not explicable" in pure power terms. (Jervis, "Identity and the Cold War," Ch. 2 in Leffler & Westad CHCW Vol. II)
