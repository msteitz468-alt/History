# Liturgy and Lay Religion in Merovingian Gaul

**The Gallican liturgy as both community-builder and hierarchy-marker, with a laity that participated,
resisted, and appropriated.** Lisa Kaaren Bailey's chapter (Oxford Handbook, Ch. 44) examines the place of
lay people in Merovingian worship, rejecting both the "clerical exclusion" thesis (MacMullen, McLaughlin —
laity systematically shut out) and any uncritical story of seamless "unity": the liturgy was "simultaneously
community inscription AND hierarchy inscription."

## Gallican Diversity

There was "no real basis for the claim that in the west a single, uniform way of celebrating the Eucharist
existed" (Hen). The *Expositio antiquae liturgicae Gallicanae* (attributed to Germanus of Paris) outlines a
standard mass structure, but **regional variation was real** — a mosaic of local traditions that the volume's
editors contrast with later Carolingian uniformity (see [[liturgical-commemoration-and-memoria]],
[[merovingian-religious-architecture]]).

## Lay Participation and Its Limits

Active lay participation is well documented: congregations singing "in a loud and measured voice, like the
clergy, some in Greek and some in Latin," the kiss of peace, prayer responses, the *Pater Noster*, and lay
psalm-chanting. Sermons were standard — a late-seventh-century council required bishops to preach every
Sunday and feast — with diverse homiletical collections (Caesarius's confrontational sermons; the widely-
copied, encouraging *Eusebius Gallicanus*). Yet participation was eroding: the Council of Agde (506) had to
**mandate communion at least three times a year**, "many com[ing] to church as spectators rather than
participants." Spatial exclusion was partial — the Council of Tours (567) barred laity from the altar area
during the liturgy but allowed access "for prayer and communication," and there is debate (Markus, Kieckhefer)
over whether screens were symbolic or visual barriers, with no confirmed iconostasis in the period.

## Private Chapels and Lay Resistance

Wealthy laypeople maintained **private oratories and villa churches**, a source of friction: Breton priests
were condemned for saying mass in private homes, and a mid-seventh-century Council of Chalon complained that
lay patrons "oppose the bishops and… do not permit those clerics that served at that oratory to be
controlled by the archdeacon." The laity also shaped practice through appropriation: the child
[[radegund-of-poitiers|Radegund]] led a procession with a wooden cross; Genovefa organized vigils against
Attila; some of Caesarius's congregation left before the sermon, so "he locked the church doors"; and King
Chilperic "composed hymns and masses of his own accord."

## Conclusion

Bailey's revisionist conclusion: the laity were "simultaneously objects of liturgical socialization and
agents who shaped liturgical practice through resistance, appropriation, and patronage" — another instance of
the Handbook recovering the vitality of Merovingian religion from the charge of a "theological void."
