## Definition and Scope

The Shari'ah — literally "the way" or "the path" — is the comprehensive sacred law of Islam, embracing ritual obligation, personal ethics, family relations, commercial exchange, criminal procedure, and public order. It is not identical to "law" in the narrow Western sense; Hodgson notes that "'Law' by itself answers to only one aspect of it unless one has clearly in mind the 'Law' of Moses." It is more accurately described as the total normative order under which Muslims were to live, covering everything from how to perform the ritual ablution to whether a given sale contract was licit. The Shari'ah was never fully codified in a strict sense; it existed as a living body of scholarly interpretation subject to continuous elaboration.

The Shari'ah must be distinguished from [[fiqh]] (jurisprudence), which is the scholarly discipline by which the Shari'ah is derived and applied, and from the four subsidiary roots (usul al-fiqh) on which fiqh operates: the [[quran]], the [[hadith]], ijma' (consensus), and qiyas (analogy).

## Development — The Elaboration of the Shari'ah, c. 750–945

### The Piety-Minded Origins

The Shari'ah did not emerge fully formed from the Qur'an. It grew out of the activism of the Piety-minded (ahl al-piety) factions of Marwani and early 'Abbasid Islam — groups committed to making the Qur'anic ideal of individual moral responsibility before God the actual governing norm of the Muslim community, the [[abbasid-caliphate|'Abbasid]] Ummah. These circles were marked by "an intense sense of the historical challenge of Islam as this was embodied in the Qur'an" (Hodgson). Their demand was that Muslim public life conform to what Mu'hammad had sanctioned, not merely to the customs of the garrison towns or the arbitrary will of the caliph.

### al-Shafi'i and the Systematization of the Sources

The most decisive single step in Shari'ah elaboration was taken by Muhammad b. Idris **al-Shafi'i** (d. 820). Working in Egypt after earlier study in Mecca, Medina, and Baghdad, al-Shafi'i proposed to show in his *Kitab al-Umm* that the entire Shari'ah could be derived from the Qur'an — not by treating the Qur'an as a legal code, but by showing that the Qur'an presupposed Muhammad not only as its deliverer but as its authoritative interpreter. This gave Muhammad's sunnah practice a theological status it had not clearly possessed before: he became "the divinely certified exemplar, whose practice itself had a revelatory status" (Hodgson).

Al-Shafi'i's system (usul al-fiqh) rested on four interlocking sources:

1. **The Qur'an** — primary, but requiring interpretation through the Prophet's example.
2. **The [[hadith]]** — reports of the Prophet's words and acts, validated by chains of transmitters (isnad) leading back to Muhammad. Al-Shafi'i insisted these alone, and no merely local custom, were authoritative.
3. **Ijma'** — consensus of the entire Muslim Ummah; al-Shafi'i restricted valid consensus to community-wide agreement (ultimately attributed to a hadith guaranteeing the community would not agree on error).
4. **Qiyas** — analogical reasoning from established hadith precedent to novel cases, substituting for arbitrary personal judgment (ra'y).

The method was "intensely factualistic, allowing almost no leeway for private fancy" and depended on rigorous knowledge of the Mu'dari Arabic of Muhammad's time (Hodgson).

### The Madhhab Schools

Al-Shafi'i did not work in isolation. Four surviving Jama'i-Sunni **madhhab** schools (chosen ways) crystallized from the earlier provincial traditions of fiqh:

- **Hanafi** — traced to Abu-Hanifah of Iraq; most accommodating of analogy and commercial customization; dominant in Iraq, Central Asia, later the Ottoman Empire.
- **Maliki** — traced to Malik b. Anas of Medina; clung to the practice of the Medinan community; dominant in the Maghrib, Spain, and much of sub-Saharan Africa.
- **Shafi'i** — al-Shafi'i's own school; spread widely in Egypt, Syria, East Africa, and Southeast Asia.
- **Hanbali** — traced to Ahmad b. Hanbal (780–855); maximally hadith-based, minimizing qiyas; dominant in parts of Arabia; base for later Wahhabi reformism.

Each school tolerated the others as Muslim, however erring. "In effect, most Muslims, except for Shi'is, ultimately became Hanafis or Shafi'is, while in the Maghrib and Spain the Malikis prevailed" (Hodgson). Shi'i groups developed their own parallel fiqh systems, the Jafari and Zaydi, appealing to the 'ilm of the imams rather than Sunni hadith chains. See [[shia-islam]].

### The Two Orders of Justice under the 'Abbasids

The 'Abbasids gave formal standing to the Shari'ah by requiring their qadi (judge) courts to follow it, even persuading Piety-minded scholars to serve as judges. But the Shari'ah "was never allowed to mould the whole social order" (Hodgson). The caliphal court maintained its own court of mazalim (redress of wrongs), in which the vizier or caliph's representative could impose penalties without Shar'i due process — including torture and summary punishment that the Shari'ah's own mild criminal law would not sanction. This produced a structural duality: Shari'ah norms governed the local urban level (the mosque, the market, the family), while the court culture rested on an essentially pre-Islamic absolutism that could never fully legitimize itself in Shar'i terms.

## Scope and Substance

The Shari'ah governed five domains:

1. **Ritual** — the five pillars (shahada, salat, zakat, hajj, Ramadan fast) regulated in minute detail; God judged by intention, not merely performance.
2. **Family law** — marriage (up to four wives with equal rights), inheritance (obligatory shares including daughters and widows, no primogeniture), divorce (easier for husbands, financially constrained), guardianship.
3. **Commercial law** — emphasis on real equivalence in exchange, ban on riba (interest), use of hiyal (legal stratagems) to accommodate mercantile practice.
4. **Criminal law** — notably mild by pre-modern standards; required witnesses; penalties including corporal punishment for specific Qur'anic offenses (hudud); conviction difficult; Hodgson notes "most pre-Modern Muslim rulers felt bound to save their subjects from the results of applying it intact."
5. **Public order** — the individual's rights before the community; the muhtasib (market inspector) as enforcement officer at the local level.

The overall tendency was individualistic-egalitarian: corporate bodies had no Shar'i standing; each free adult male stood immediately before God. The Shari'ah was "the norm of the Muslim community as a community" (Hodgson).

## Shari'ah-Mindedness as a Cultural Formation

Hodgson's preferred term for those who placed the Shari'ah at the center of their religious life is "Shari'ah-minded" rather than "orthodox." This formation was not confined to Sunni Islam: Shi'i communities developed their own Shar'i systems. What distinguished the Shari'ah-minded across all confessional lines was the primacy given to the literal Shar'i norm as against mystical, philosophical, or chiliastic alternatives. "The supremacy, or at least the crucial importance, of the Shari'ah has been accepted not only in most Islamic currents, but even by most sorts of opinion within each current — whether mystic or literalist" (Hodgson).

The intellectual disciplines associated with Shari'ah-mindedness extended beyond law into history, grammar, and literary criticism — all oriented toward exact understanding of the Arabic of the Qur'an and hadith corpus.

## Historiography

**Source quality**: Hodgson's *Venture of Islam* vol. 1 provides the most analytically sophisticated account of the Shari'ah's formation available in the collection, embedding it in the social history of the Piety-minded factions and the 'Abbasid state. It should be supplemented by Schacht's *Origins of Muhammadan Jurisprudence* (not yet ingested) for detailed legal-historical analysis and by Hallaq's *Shari'a* for more recent revisionism.

**Scholarly debates**: The question of the historicity of hadith — whether the isnad system actually preserves reliable Prophetic reports — has been contested since Ignaz Goldziher and Joseph Schacht argued most hadith were pious retroactions. Hodgson's treatment acknowledges the problem directly: al-Shafi'i's system "was necessarily, if unwittingly, founded on a pious fiction" in that it claimed to rest on the historical Muhammad while actually resting on the figure of Muhammad as elaborated in Piety-minded circles. More recent scholars (Motzki, Schoeler) have argued for greater authenticity in some strands. This is an open controversy; see controversies/ for filing.

**Collection coverage**: Moderate-to-strong for the classical formative period (c. 750–1050); thin for later developments, Ottoman kanun, and colonial-era reform.
