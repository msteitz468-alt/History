## Definition and Scope

The *Manṭiq al-Ṭayr* ("The Speech of the Birds," or "Conference of the Birds") is the most celebrated work of [[attar-farid-al-din|Farid al-Dīn ʿAṭṭār]] (c. 1145–c. 1221) and the foundational allegorical text of Persian Sufi poetry. It is a *mathnawī* — a long poem in rhyming couplets — that deploys a sustained allegory of the mystical path: all the birds of the world gather, choose a leader, and set out on a journey in search of the mythical Sīmurgh, the king of birds. Passing through seven valleys that represent the stages of the mystical way, suffering losses at every stage, only thirty birds survive the journey. At the destination, having been reduced to nothing, they discover themselves and the Sīmurgh simultaneously in a great mirror — because *sī murgh* in Persian means "thirty birds." The seeker and the sought are identical; the divine is found within the purified self.

Schimmel calls this "the most ingeni­ous pun in Persian literature, expressing so marvelously the experience of the identity of the soul with the divine essence" ([[schimmel-mystical-dimensions-of-islam-1975]]).

## The Allegory

### The Seven Valleys

The journey through seven valleys maps onto the Sufi understanding of the stages (*maqāmāt*) of the mystical path:

1. The Valley of the Quest (*ṭalab*)
2. The Valley of Love (*ʿishq*)
3. The Valley of Gnosis (*maʿrifa*)
4. The Valley of Independence/Detachment (*istighnāʾ*)
5. The Valley of Unity (*tawḥīd*)
6. The Valley of Bewilderment/Amazement (*ḥayra*)
7. The Valley of Poverty and Annihilation (*faqr u fanāʾ*)

The losses at each stage are not failures but necessary purgations: the birds who cannot continue are those who cannot relinquish the attachments — to reputation, to learning, to spiritual achievement itself — that each valley demands. Only the thirty birds who have been completely emptied are able to arrive.

### The Sīmurgh Pun

The resolution of the allegory turns on a bilingual pun: *sīmurgh* (the mythical bird-king, a word of Middle Persian origin) sounds like *sī murgh* (thirty birds in Persian). When the thirty surviving birds look for the Sīmurgh in the great mirror, they see themselves — they *are* the Sīmurgh. The poem thus enacts the Sufi doctrine of *fanāʾ* (annihilation of the ego-self in God) without stating it discursively: the allegory arrives at the conclusion that what the soul seeks is what the soul already is, once purified.

This convergence of seeker and sought is parallel to Rūmī's story of the man who knocks at his friend's door: sent away when he answers "I" to "Who is there?", he is admitted when — after a year of burning in the fire of absence — he answers "Thou": "Now since thou art I, come in, there is no room for two I's in this house" (M 1:3056–64).

### The Bird Symbolism

The *Manṭiq al-Ṭayr* elaborated and systematized the equation of soul with bird that was already present in Islamic and pre-Islamic thought (Avicenna's philosophical tale of the floating man, Ghazzālī's *Risālat al-Ṭayr*). ʿAṭṭār's specific innovations:

- Each bird species becomes a spiritual type: the nightingale (lover of the rose, unable to leave earthly beauty), the parrot (seeker of immortality), the hawk (attached to the king's wrist), the peacock (attached to its beauty), the duck (half-earthly, half-divine)
- The frame narrative provides a dialogic structure in which each bird presents an excuse for not undertaking the journey; the hoopoe (Hudhud — the bird that served as Solomon's messenger in the Quran) refutes each excuse with a story. This *story within story* structure became a model for Rūmī's *Mathnawī*
- The destination — the Sīmurgh — is described through negative theology: it cannot be characterized, it can only be reached by becoming nothing

## Influence and Transmission

The *Manṭiq al-Ṭayr* became, in Schimmel's words, "one of the favorite story books of Persian literature" ([[schimmel-mystical-dimensions-of-islam-1975]]), generating a cascade of bird imagery and Sīmurgh symbolism across subsequent Persian, Turkish, Urdu, and other Islamicate literatures:

- Rūmī's falcon symbolism, his nightingale images, and his reed flute (the soul cut from its home and longing for return) all build on ʿAṭṭār's bird world
- The nightingale-rose dyad — the central image of [[sufi-symbolism]] — was greatly elaborated after the *Manṭiq al-Ṭayr* established the nightingale as the soul bird par excellence
- The name of Sheikh Ṣanʿān (who fell in love with a Christian maiden in the poem's most famous embedded tale) "has found its way even into Kashmiri and Malay literature" and "has become one of the best-known symbols of true loving surrender, regardless of religious traditions, reputation, name, or fame" (Schimmel, [[schimmel-mystical-dimensions-of-islam-1975]])
- The figure of the Sīmurgh was taken up by ʿAṭṭār's older contemporary Suhrawardī in his illuminationist philosophy as a symbol of the First Intellect

In Turkey, the expression *can kuşu uçtu* ("his soul bird has flown away") for death attests to the deep cultural absorption of this imagery. In Anatolia, the birds of ʿAṭṭār's allegory remained reference points for [[yunus-emre|Yūnus Emre]] and the Bektashi poets.

## Frame Structure and Narrative Innovation

The *Manṭiq al-Ṭayr* perfected the frame-tale mathnawī structure: a central allegorical narrative that contains hundreds of embedded stories, anecdotes, and poetic digressions. ʿAṭṭār "invented" the specifically mystical ending to this structure — the revelation at the destination — but the architectural principle was inherited and transmitted. Rūmī adopted and extended the structure in his *Mathnawī*, although (characteristically) he allows himself to be diverted by associations and sounds in ways ʿAṭṭār does not.

Schimmel describes ʿAṭṭār's narrative talent as his most remarkable quality: "his narrative, and even dramatic, talent is beautifully displayed" throughout his works, which are "indeed storehouses of anecdotes and lively tales" ([[schimmel-mystical-dimensions-of-islam-1975]]).

## The Story of Sheikh Ṣanʿān

The embedded tale of Sheikh Ṣanʿān (MT 77 ff.) — a senior Sufi who falls in love with a Christian girl in a dream-vision and, in his love, "changed the rosary for the infidel's girdle" — is the most frequently cited story from the poem. Its significance:

- Dramatically enacts the principle that love of God transcends all external religious boundaries
- Poses the question of whether divine love can override religious law (the sheikh tends swine for his Christian beloved, a triple violation)
- Is resolved in the poem by the prayers of his disciples and a divine dream sent to the girl, who herself converts
- Has been interpreted both as a celebration of love's power and as an ultimately orthodox resolution in which the transgression is redeemed

The story circulated independently across Islamicate, Kashmiri, and Malay literary traditions. See [[persian-sufi-poetry]].
