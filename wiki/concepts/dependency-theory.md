# Dependency Theory (Dependencia)

**Dependency theory** holds that the underdevelopment of the global "periphery" is not a
backward starting condition but an **actively produced outcome** of the periphery's
subordinate incorporation into a world capitalist economy dominated by a "core" of rich
nations. Its most famous popular expression is Eduardo Galeano's
[[galeano-open-veins|*Open Veins of Latin America*]] (1971): "the history of Latin America's
underdevelopment is… an integral part of the history of world capitalism's development."

## Origin and development

- **Structuralist roots (1949).** The Argentine economist **Raúl Prebisch** and the UN
  **Economic Commission for Latin America (ECLA/CEPAL)** argued that the global terms of trade
  structurally favored manufactured-goods exporters (the **center**) over primary-products
  exporters (the **periphery**), so free trade transferred wealth from poor to rich — the
  rationale for **import-substitution industrialization** (see [[industrialization]],
  [[latin-america-modern]]).
- **Radicalization (1960s–70s).** The *dependentista* school turned this into a theory of
  systemic exploitation: **André Gunder Frank**'s "the development of underdevelopment" (a
  metropolis–satellite chain draining surplus upward at every link); **Theotônio dos Santos**;
  **Fernando Henrique Cardoso & Enzo Faletto** (a subtler "dependent development" allowing for
  local agency); and, popularly, **Galeano**. It is closely allied to Immanuel Wallerstein's
  [[afro-eurasian-world-system|world-systems analysis]] (core/semi-periphery/periphery) and to
  Walter Rodney's *How Europe Underdeveloped Africa* (see
  [[impact-of-atlantic-slave-trade-on-africa]]).

## Core claims

1. **Underdevelopment is relational, not original** — the periphery was *made* poor by the
   same process that made the core rich; "our wealth has always generated our poverty."
2. **Unequal exchange and surplus drain** — the [[global-silver-trade|silver]], the
   [[plantation-economy|plantation]] monocultures, and later the export economy and foreign
   investment continuously transfer value to the metropolis (Galeano's "open veins").
3. **A comprador local elite** — a "commission-agent bourgeoisie"/latifundista oligarchy that
   profits by mediating the drain rather than by national development.
4. **Continuity across regimes** — colonial extraction, nineteenth-century free-trade
   dependence, and twentieth-century US imperialism (the IMF, multinationals) as one chain.

## Limitations and critiques

Dependency theory is heavily **contested** and, by the 1980s, displaced in mainstream
economics. Critiques: it is **mono-causal and externalist**, downplaying internal institutions,
demography, and policy; it struggles to explain the **East Asian** "tigers," which industrialized
*within* the world economy; it can be **empirically loose** (Galeano especially) and
deterministic. Economic historians counter with **institutionalist** accounts (Douglass North;
Engerman–Sokoloff on factor endowments; Acemoglu–Robinson on extractive vs. inclusive
institutions) that locate the roots of divergence in colonial *institutions* rather than simple
extraction, and colonial historians (e.g. [[burkholder-johnson-colonial-latin-america|Burkholder
& Johnson]]) stress negotiated complexity over a single narrative of pillage. The dispute is
laid out at [[causes-of-latin-american-underdevelopment]]. Even so, dependency thinking
remains influential in framing global inequality and decolonial critique, and ECLA's terms-of-trade
insight retains empirical force.
