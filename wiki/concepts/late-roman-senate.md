# The Late Roman Senatorial Order

The **"total revolution"** in the Roman senatorial order across the fourth century — from a single body
of ~600 men at Rome to two great senates of thousands, recruited largely through the imperial
bureaucracy. Treated in [[cameron-cah-v13-1998|CAH XIII]] Ch. 6 (**Peter Heather**).

## The starting point

At 300 the senatorial order was the ~600 members of the **senate of Rome** — status essentially
**inherited** (though leavened by a trickle of provincial entrants), some claiming descent from the
Gracchi and Scipios. The senate as an *institution* had little political power; its importance lay in the
men — among the **wealthiest landowners of the empire**, whose riches (not the body's authority) had
fuelled the senatorial pretenders of the third-century anarchy.

## The three phases of transformation

- **Constantine — a second senate.** The new senate of **Constantinople** (from 324/330) began with the
  lesser status *clari* (not *clarissimi*), but was endowed with praetorships and incentives to grow.
  Heather situates it in Constantine's **post-Licinius governing problem**: having seized an East where
  "he knew nobody," he had to build a body of support from scratch — the new senate was a fresh **mark of
  status with which to attract the eastern landowning elites** he needed to govern (Eusebius: "new
  dignities that he might give tokens of his favour to a larger number of people").
- **Constantius II — the geographical split.** He divided the order on **purely geographical** lines
  (eastern senators of Rome re-registering at Constantinople), raising the new body to full *clarissimus*
  status and making the two senates serve West and East respectively. A **recruiting campaign** (Themistius
  touring the eastern cities, 358–9) drew in the richer **curials** — Constantius' ideal senator combining
  cash, land, office-holding, and a first-rate education.
- **Valentinian & Valens — the fusion of birth and service.** The pivotal **legislation of 372**
  consolidated all the empire's separate ladders of rank — the palatine bureaucracy, occasional high
  office, the army — into **one unitary system** crowned by a common **senatorial status**, and divided
  the order into **three grades**: *clarissimi* (lowest), *spectabiles*, and *illustres* (praetorian
  prefects, urban prefects, consuls, top generals). By c. 400 some **3,000 jobs in each half of the
  empire led to senatorial rank** (against ~180 high bureaucrats, none senatorial, in 249) — an
  "extraordinary transformation."

## From aristocracy of birth to aristocracy of service

The cumulative effect: senatorial status ceased to designate a body marked by **biological descent** (an
element that never quite vanished, especially at Rome) and became **the ultimate prize aimed at by all
who climbed the empire's career structures** — "an aristocracy of service." Two career patterns coexisted:
the **traditional senatorial *cursus*** of the old Roman families (Symmachus' honorary praetorship →
governorships → the urban prefecture) alongside the new **bureaucratic paths** to the senate. In the
fifth century the active senate narrowed again (only working *illustres* counted), but the fourth-century
revolution was not reversed: a vastly broader cross-section of the landowning elite now held senatorial
rank, and **high status was firmly tied to imperial service**.

## Significance

Heather's chapter shows how the late-Roman aristocracy was **remade around the imperial bureaucracy** —
the institutional counterpart to [[late-roman-emperorship|Kelly's]] negotiated, service-based government.
The creation of the **Constantinople senate** gave the East its own governing class and prefigured the
distinct trajectory of Byzantium; the **merging of birth and service** dissolved the old Republican
conception of the *ordo* into a graded "aristocracy of service" that would define late-antique and
early-Byzantine society.
