## Definition and Scope

Teotihuacan religion was not a codified orthodoxy enforced by any identifiable institution, but a complex of practices, beliefs, iconographic programs, and ritual obligations that served a range of interests — from the Teotihuacan state at the top to individual households at the bottom. As Cowgill notes, "it is unlikely that any social entity could rule on orthodoxy versus heresy. Probably different people held somewhat different notions of their deities, and likely the same person was not consistent from time to time" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 8).

Teotihuacan inherited and reworked earlier Mesoamerican practices and concepts, adapting them to the needs and constraints of a city of 70,000–100,000 people. Some ritual forms — such as composite theater censers and candeleros — appear to be inventions of the urban period. Others, like the Old God (Huehueteotl) and the Feathered Serpent, derived from pre-Teotihuacan antecedents.

A key interpretive principle: Teotihuacan imagery relies on what Cowgill calls "polythetic core entities" (PCEs) — entities represented by images sharing a polythetic core of features, with not every image exhibiting every feature. Parts are used as abbreviations for wholes; elements of one PCE are used as qualifiers in images that primarily represent a different PCE. Much apparent complexity or inconsistency in Teotihuacan religious imagery reflects this systematic visual grammar rather than theological confusion.

## Principal Deities

### The Storm God

One of the two principal deities, the Storm God was a pan-Mesoamerican figure with specific Teotihuacan elaboration. His PCE core includes: an unhumanly large and curled upper lip; fangs protruding from the upper jaw; a reduced or absent lower jaw; a snub nose; prominent rings around the eyes (often called "goggles," though anachronistically); a large split tongue; large green earspools; and male attire. He often holds an undulating lightning serpent or has a flower depending from his mouth.

The Storm God embodied both beneficent and destructive aspects: "some images of the Storm God emphasize beneficent aspects and are linked to water, gentle rain, and flourishing crops, while others have a more threatening aspect and are linked to violent lightning storms and war" (Ch. 8). These aspects are intergraded rather than representing separate deities. Storm God imagery appears in murals (notably at Techinantitla), painted ceramics, and the distinctive Storm God jar form, which persisted throughout the city's history.

The Storm God nose pendant — a recognizable diagnostic — was worn as actual regalia by some sacrificial victims in the Feathered Serpent Pyramid, probably as an insignia of rank or office.

### The Feathered Serpent

The second principal deity, the Feathered Serpent is related to but not identical with the later Aztec Quetzalcóatl — Aztec attributes of the Feathered Serpent cannot be safely projected onto the Teotihuacan form. At Teotihuacan, the Feathered Serpent "has been linked to the beginning of time" (López Austin et al. 1991, cited Ch. 8) and appears to have been partnered with the Storm God throughout the city's history.

Feathered Serpent imagery is prominent early and continues into the terminal phase. The FSP (Feathered Serpent Pyramid) gave the deity monumental civic expression and its desecration around 300–350 CE may mark a shift in the ideological role of the Feathered Serpent relative to other religious priorities.

### The "Great Goddess" — A Contested Concept

Furst (1974), Pasztory (1997), and Berlo (1992) proposed a "Great Goddess" as the principal deity of Teotihuacan, associated with water, fertility, and agricultural abundance. This concept has been substantially critiqued. Cowgill is explicitly skeptical: "it is highly unlikely that there was a single overarching goddess who took precedence over the Storm God and the Feathered Serpent" (Ch. 8). Paulinyi (2006) has provided a detailed methodological critique of Great Goddess identifications, arguing that features attributed to the goddess are typically qualifiers embedded in images of other PCEs, not diagnostics of a separate deity.

The large stone monolith known as the Diosa de Agua (Water Goddess), found at [[teotihuacan]], does represent an important female deity, but she is distinct from the hypothesized overarching Great Goddess. The famous Tepantitla mural figure often identified as the Great Goddess is, in Cowgill's analysis, primarily a "flowery mountain" PCE (a volcanic sacred mountain associated with fertility and water) with some human qualifiers, including a Storm God nose pendant — not a representation of a female deity.

### The Old God (Huehueteotl)

A good, clearly defined PCE equivalent to the Aztec Huehueteotl. He takes the form of a bent and wrinkled old man, seated in tailor fashion, one hand clenched while the other is cupped, with prominent upper canine teeth, and a circular fire basin on his head ringed by lozenge-shaped fire signs. Most examples are andesitic stone, highly standardized. They occur across the full socioeconomic range of residential compounds, from humble Tlajinga 33 to the Ciudadela palaces, suggesting use in compound-wide domestic ritual not primarily connected with the state.

### The Butterfly

A well-defined PCE associated with deceased souls. The butterfly appears frequently as an adorno on composite censers used in commemorating and communing with the dead. It is abbreviated to a part (such as the proboscis) and remains unmistakable in Teotihuacan imagery. Berlo notes its presence in Teotihuacan-derived censers exported to highland Guatemala.

## Iconographic Program

Teotihuacan's visual program differs markedly from other Mesoamerican traditions. Rather than celebrating individual rulers or dynastic events, Teotihuacan imagery emphasizes multiplicity and replication: "frequently a number of humans or other beings are shown in a mural or stone relief, all with the same attire and all in the same pose" (Ch. 8). This contrasts with Maya emphasis on individual portraiture and Zapotec avoidance of repetition. The effect conveys social categories as homogeneous — an ideological statement about collective identity or rank.

Scenes depict processions, ritual performances, and personages emerging from portals to other worlds — almost never mundane activities. Compound-specific murals suggest that the right (and duty) to perform certain ceremonies was "owned" by individual apartment compounds, pointing to decentralized ritual practice operating alongside state religion.

## Ritual Calendar and Cosmology

Teotihuacan probably maintained the 260-day ritual calendar used throughout Mesoamerica, cross-referenced with a 365-day vague year and likely a 52-year calendar round. Alfonso Caso demonstrated that Teotihuacanos observed combinations of thirteen numbers and twenty day signs. The canonical orientation of Teotihuacan streets and structures — approximately 15.5 degrees east of north — probably encodes the August 12 sunset direction, the date of the Mesoamerican world-creation in Classic Maya belief.

Sugiyama (1993) argues that multiples of 260 Teotihuacan measurement units (TMU, c. 83 cm) materially embedded the ritual calendar in civic-ceremonial space: the Sun Pyramid was originally close to 260 TMU on each side; the distance from Stage 4 of the Moon Pyramid to the south side of the Ciudadela is close to 2,600 TMU. Cross-in-circle pecked-dot figures at Teotihuacan and elsewhere in central Mexico frequently contain exactly 260 dots, probably used for divination.

## Ritual Settings and Whose Interests They Served

Cowgill distinguishes ritual settings (civic-ceremonial, neighborhood, apartment compound, individual) from the interests represented (state/highest elites, lesser elites, neighborhoods, sodalities, compound groups, households, individuals). The relationship between setting and interest is not one-to-one: small-scale domestic ritual in candeleros may have served state interests as much as individual ones; large civic ceremonies served individual needs alongside state ideology.

Candeleros — small single- or twin-chambered ceramic ritual objects — are associated with frequent but small-scale domestic events, "perhaps carried out by a single individual" (Ch. 7). Their innovation without pre-Teotihuacan antecedents, and their disappearance after the state's collapse, suggests they were somehow connected to state interests even when used domestically.

## Lack of Named Rulers in Religious Imagery

A defining feature of Teotihuacan's ideological program is the near-total absence of identified individual rulers in durable media — no stone stelae with named persons, no identified royal tomb, no secure dynastic portraits. This contrasts sharply with Classic Maya practice of celebratory ruler portraiture. The meaning of this absence is contested: see [[teotihuacan-political-organization]] for the debate. Cowgill suggests early rulers may have been too powerful to need self-portraiture; alternatively, representations may exist but not yet be recognized.

## Limitations and Gaps

The lack of readable texts makes reconstruction of Teotihuacan religion necessarily inferential, relying on iconographic analysis, spatial context, and comparison with later Mesoamerican traditions whose relationship to Teotihuacan practice is uncertain. The language of Teotihuacan is unknown, preventing phonetic decipherment of its 229+ standardized signs. Many of those signs "went out of use long before Aztec times," compounding interpretive difficulty (Ch. 8).
