# Working Towards the Führer

## Definition and Scope

"Working towards the Führer" describes the dynamic in the Nazi regime whereby subordinates in party, state, and military agencies anticipated Hitler's wishes (often without explicit orders) and competed with one another to implement ever more radical measures in line with what they believed the Führer desired. The phrase originates in a 1934 speech by a Nazi functionary: "Working towards the Führer along the lines he would wish." Kershaw adopted it as the leitmotiv for his analysis (explicitly credited to Jeremy Noakes & Geoffrey Pridham documentary collection) and the title of Chapter 13 of *Hubris*.

It is not a claim of constant direct micromanagement by Hitler. Rather, it explains cumulative radicalization through a combination of:
- Hitler's charismatic authority (power resting in the expectations and projections of followers, per Max Weber).
- Institutional polycracy and deliberate encouragement of competition ("teams of technical experts... guided by the Fuehrer's vision").
- Subordinates' desire for favour, career advancement, and ideological alignment.
- Hitler's own ideological fixations (antisemitism, struggle, Lebensraum) providing the broad "lines he would wish" without detailed blueprints.

The concept distinguishes the regime from both a conventional bureaucracy (with clear orders) and pure chaos; it produced "the most extreme proposals tended to be the ones adopted."

`part_of:` [[processes/rise-of-nazism]] and Nazi state structures. `analogous_to:` other charismatic or personalist dictatorships but distinctive in its bureaucratic-technocratic implementation.

## Causal Drivers

- Hitler's worldview (fixed early in Vienna/Munich per Range 01; elaborated in *Mein Kampf*): racial struggle, elimination of Jews as prerequisite for national regeneration, expansion for Lebensraum.
- Structural conditions of Weimar collapse and post-1933 power vacuum: old elites discredited; new agencies proliferated without clear hierarchy.
- Charismatic bond: millions "believed in it"; field marshals obeyed a former corporal.
- Incentive structure: advancement came from proving radicalism, not caution. "All roads led back to him for final arbitration."
- Hitler's style: secretiveness, laziness on detail, sudden interventions on "his" issues, preference for personal loyalty over institutional rules. He "consciously encouraged factional competition and institutional duplication as a governance technique."

`caused_by:` combination of Hitler's personal ideological commitment + the social/political motivations of German society and movement activists. `enabled:` the "given and imposed conditions" of modern bureaucratic society under charismatic rule.

## Major Phases (in Range 01–13 context)

- Pre-1933: Hitler as "drummer" (Trommler) who rallied; early DAP/NSDAP already showed his dominance once he discovered oratory (Sept 1919 entry).
- 1933–34: Machtergreifung and Enabling Act opened space; "working towards" began immediately in anti-Jewish measures (boycott, Civil Service Law) and Gleichschaltung as agencies raced to prove zeal.
- 1934 (Röhm purge onward): After Hindenburg death, total power; the dynamic accelerated on the "Jewish Question" and foreign policy.
- By 1936 (end of *Hubris*): the concept fully operative. Subordinates competed on rearmament, anti-Jewish policy, and expansion without waiting for detailed directives. Ch. 13 synthesizes how this produced the regime's radical trajectory.

## Geographic and Institutional Spread

Primarily within the German Reich and its expanding control (Austria 1938 onward in Nemesis). Operated across:
- Party (Gauleiter, SA/SS competition).
- State bureaucracy (competing ministries).
- Military (Wehrmacht vs. emerging SS empire).
- Economy (businesses "working towards" autarky and rearmament for favour).

## Interaction with Other Processes

- `produced:` cumulative radicalization on antisemitism (from 1933 boycott to later "solution") and foreign policy risks.
- `enabled:` the Holocaust's "twisted road" without requiring a single written Führer order for every step (though Hitler's personal commitment was indispensable).
- `contrasts_with:` conventional totalitarian models emphasizing top-down command (totalitarianism theory); or pure functionalism that downplays Hitler's role.
- `concurrent_with:` [[processes/weimar-democracy-and-its-collapse]], rearmament, Gleichschaltung.

## End Conditions

Continued and intensified through 1945; the dynamic did not end with total power in 1936 but reached its most destructive form in the war years (Hunger Plan, Generalplan Ost, Final Solution). Defeat in 1945 and Hitler's death terminated it. The concept remains a key historiographical tool for explaining how a modern state could produce genocide and total war without constant explicit central direction.

## Historiography

Kershaw's development of the concept was a deliberate attempt to overcome the intentionalist/functionalist divide of the 1970s–80s (Cumberland Lodge conference divisions noted in Preface). It gives Hitler indispensable personal/ideological agency ("without him... different") while locating the mechanism in structural and social factors ("society... galvanized by Hitler"). "A biography of Hitler... from the 'wrong' direction" — structuralist historian integrating the personal. Primary evidence: the 1934 functionary speech, Goebbels diary reports, agency competition records, Hitler's own vague but consistent ideological pronouncements.

Critiques (not in this range): some argue it understates Hitler's direct interventions or overstates spontaneity; others see it as compatible with "moderate intentionalism." Kershaw himself stresses it does not absolve Hitler of "prime moral responsibility."
