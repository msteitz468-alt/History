# Lachrymose Conception of Jewish History

The **lachrymose conception of Jewish history** (from Latin *lacrima*, "tear")
is Salo Wittmayer Baron's disparaging label for the interpretation of the
pre-modern Jewish past — especially under medieval Christendom — as a
near-continuous record of oppression, expulsion, and martyrdom. Baron, the
foremost twentieth-century Jewish historian, launched his critique in his 1928
essay "Ghetto and Emancipation," arguing that the nineteenth-century founders of
scientific Jewish historiography (*Wissenschaft des Judentums*) had
systematically exaggerated Jewish misery in order to justify and dramatize the
case for modern emancipation.

## Emic vs. Etic

The term is **etic** — an analytic and polemical category applied by historians
to a body of earlier historiography, not a self-description. Baron's target was
the *narrative posture* of writers like Heinrich Graetz, for whom the medieval
diaspora was chiefly a "vale of tears." Baron's corrective insisted that medieval
Jews also possessed communal autonomy, legal standing, economic function, and
cultural vitality, and that emancipation itself involved losses (of corporate
self-government) as well as gains.

## The Twin Myth and the Neo-Lachrymose Variant

Baron's revisionism, per [[cohen-under-crescent-cross-1994|Mark Cohen]], had an
unintended counterpart. The same nineteenth-century historians who drew Jewish
life under Christendom in lachrymose terms **invented its bright foil**: the
"[[jews-under-islam-myth-and-countermyth|myth of the interfaith utopia]]" — the
idealized Andalusian "Golden Age" of Jewish flourishing under Islam. Lachrymose
Christendom and utopian Islam were two halves of one rhetorical structure.

Cohen extends the vocabulary with the **"neo-lachrymose conception of
Jewish-Arab history"**: the post-1967 (post–Six-Day War) revisionism that
re-described the Islamic past itself in lachrymose terms — Islam as an innate,
continuous persecutor of Jews from Muhammad onward, the doleful equal of
Christian Europe. Cohen reads this "countermyth" as a dialectical reaction to
Arab antisemitism and to the propagandistic Arab embrace of the utopia myth, and
argues it distorts the past as badly as the utopia it opposes.

## Significance and Critique

The lachrymose/neo-lachrymose framework is now a standard analytic tool for
diagnosing how present political needs (emancipation, Zionism, the Arab–Israeli
conflict) shape the writing of medieval Jewish history. Its limitation, critics
note, is that "anti-lachrymose" correction can itself slide into complacency —
minimizing real persecution (the [[first-crusade-1095|Rhineland massacres]], the
[[almohads|Almohad]] forced conversions, expulsions) in the name of balance.
Cohen's own position is that the goal is neither tears nor utopia but structural
explanation of measurable differences in outcome.
