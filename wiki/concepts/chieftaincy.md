# Chieftaincy in Africa

## Definition and Scope

Chieftaincy refers to forms of political authority in Africa in which individuals exercise leadership by managing multiple forms of powerful knowledge — ritual, judicial, military, diplomatic, and other kinds. No single analytical model explains the multiple phenomena of "chieftaincy": the term describes individuals whose status and influence is "extremely diverse" across the continent (Justin Willis, [[parker-reid-oxford-modern-african-history-2013]], ch. 12).

A broad pattern can nonetheless be observed: "Across much of the continent, individuals who possessed political authority in precolonial societies did so most effectively not by monopolizing a single kind of power, but by dealing in multiple forms of powerful knowledge. In the colonial period and subsequently, this brokering of knowledge has acquired a new productive potency, serving as a means to both mediate and reproduce a distinction between tradition on the one hand and the state on the other."

## Precolonial Authority: Hierarchy and Heterarchy

Early anthropological work (Meyer Fortes and E. E. Evans-Pritchard, *African Political Systems*, 1940) asked why some societies had hierarchical, centralised authority while others did not. They identified a "quintessentially African political system" in which the ruler embodied "essential values" and "mystical" ideas of community well-being.

**The evolution of this framework**: subsequent scholarship replaced the vision of patriarchal power based on kinship and ritual hierarchy with an argument for the **heterogeneous, overlapping, and multiple** nature of influence:

- In equatorial Africa, "big men" competed to accumulate wealth and followers using the language of kinship, supplemented by skills in warfare, judging cases, and sponsoring rituals of well-being
- Among the Mbundu (Angola), authority worked at lineage level through rituals of well-being
- In nineteenth-century Mende (Sierra Leone) and among the Krobo (Ghana), eighteenth-century lineage heads were subordinated by war-chiefs
- In Ibadan (Nigeria), precolonial big men were war-chiefs whose position rested on the ability constantly to generate loot to reward followers

The key analytical shift: authority rested on the ability to manage **multiple forms of knowledge**, not the possession of a singular superior form. "Adepts were many and varied…inventing new ways of configuring, storing and using what must have been an ever-shifting spectrum of possibility" (Guyer and Belinga on equatorial Africa).

**Heterarchy vs. hierarchy in Buganda**: even the kabaka lived in a "crowded spiritual world" in which there were many different ideas about community and well-being. "Heterarchy was a strategy used both to constitute a hierarchical polity and to curb the king's power."

**Prophets and the limits of knowledge-consolidation**: the Maasai laibons were "a political failure" — unable to consolidate brief ascendancy achieved in the late nineteenth century. Among the Nandi (Kenya) and Tonga (Zambia), by contrast, some individuals built prophecy into a repertoire of knowledge allowing new centralising authority to develop — until interrupted by colonial rule.

## Colonial Transformation: Chiefs of the Government

European colonial states were chronically short of resources. "Indirect rule" — reliance on local forms of authority — was "a pragmatic answer to this fundamental weakness" (Terence Ranger). Chieftaincy was at the heart of this, and Mahmood Mamdani's analysis identifies "decentralised despots" as the most baleful legacy of colonial rule: chiefs freed from constraints of custom by colonial coercive support but outside the systemic restraints placed on formal colonial rule.

**The constructive vs. invented debate**:
Critics have long argued that colonial chiefs were either a complete colonial invention or that established authorities were transformed into coercive, acquisitive, individualistic figures. Case studies:
- Kaguru (Tanganyika): paramount chief creation was "a handmaiden to imperialist domination as well as a means to power for some opportunistic Kaguru"
- Kikuyu (Kenya): appointed chiefs were "creatures of the British administration" whose authority rested "not on reciprocal relationships…but on force"
- Botswana: "chiefly autocracy was intensified and corrupted by British overrule"

However, there were "limits to invention." Colonial rule could not sustain chiefs who relied entirely on official support — as the abolition of warrant chiefs in south-eastern Nigeria demonstrated. Tom Spear argues that colonial officials could themselves become "subject to local discourses of power that they neither fully understood nor controlled."

**The linguistic homogenisation**: colonialism applied a European-derived vocabulary — chief, chef coutumier, hauptlinge, chefe — to individuals in very varied political relationships. This linguistic homogenisation placed all holders of authority together in the "imaginary world of custom," in collective contrast to the sphere of state authority.

**French vs. British approaches**: French officials were generally less enthusiastic about chiefs than British counterparts and more willing to eliminate those who got in their way. But more recent research suggests the contrast is overstated — French officials too faced the challenge of asserting control with limited resources and sought local agents, expressing their role in similar language of "tradition vs. state."

**Gender exclusions**: the colonial remodelling of chieftaincy was generally patriarchal. Women who had possessed forms of influence in precolonial societies were largely excluded from the new colonial world of "chieftaincy," with exceptions such as among the Mende of Sierra Leone, where some women successfully transformed previous forms of authority into colonial chieftaincy.

**The gatekeeper model**: colonial chiefs have been described as "gatekeepers" standing between local society and the state, dominating traffic between the two. This liminal position was "one of moral peril as well as political power" — making chiefs creatures of a no-man's land between the laws of the state and the mores of their communities. But the separation was also an illusion that could be manipulated: chieftaincy became "fundamentally implicated in structuring the contrast between tradition and modernity."

## Postcolonial Persistence

Despised by nationalist politicians and abandoned by late colonial governments in favour of local councils, chiefs looked extremely vulnerable at independence. In 1958, Lucy Mair wrote confidently that "It is quite easy to predict that the chiefs will eventually disappear from the scene." Several newly independent states abolished chiefs entirely (Tanzania, Sudan, Uganda) or sought to reduce their political significance (Ghana under Nkrumah).

Yet across much of the continent by the 1980s–1990s, chiefs were returning to prominent political and administrative roles:
- Ghana: "while chiefs may appear to have lost their power, they actually wield a remarkable degree of influence over the political life of the country"
- Northern Sudan: "native authorities" abolished in the 1970s steadily regained state recognition
- Burkina Faso: Moose chiefs survived two attempts at abolition
- South Africa: democratic transition reaffirmed rather than ended chieftaincy
- South-eastern Nigeria (Igbo): "traditional rulers" thriving in the 1980s, having displaced the local government created in the 1950s

**Kenya as exception**: there colonial chiefs' children translated themselves into businessmen and politicians at independence, leaving lesser chiefs' descendants entirely incorporated into the civil service without customary authority claims.

## Why Has Chieftaincy Proved Durable?

Willis argues the resilience of postcolonial chieftaincy derives from two sources:

1. **Creative continuity in political culture**: local patterns of thought and practice surrounding the proper exercise of authority display "considerable continuity over time." As suggested of western Cameroon, "although…'legitimate authority is based on descent and the ancestors', the importance of chiefs is also very much bound up with current politics."

2. **The productive dichotomy between tradition and modernity**: the state has failed, in the eyes of many Africans, to meet the expectations of its people. This "widespread perception" makes the discursive distinction between traditional authority and the state "so useful." Chieftaincy benefits from "the powerful tension which continues to exist along the fault line between these two spheres." Chiefs can "insist that they are distinct from the state and to deny responsibility for its shortcomings and failures: its corruption, inability to provide services and security, and lack of local accountability."

**Modern chiefs are not confined to a world of hallowed tradition**: Nigerian paramounts bestow chieftaincy titles on politicians; Igbo "traditional rulers" "self-confidently borrow cultural elements derived from European, African and Christian origins." Carola Lentz's comparative study of northern Ghana big men depicts a chief combining "different fields of action" — tradition, commerce, professional employment. The Zimbabwean chief who declared "I have the power of the soil" was also a Catholic schoolteacher.

**Where chieftaincy has not flourished**: among the Maka (Cameroon), chiefs failed because of popular association with immoral witchcraft power. In Kenya, access to the powerful resource of tradition came to lie in the hands of elders rather than formally designated chiefs.

## Historiography

The scholarship has moved through several phases:
- Early colonial anthropology (Fortes, Evans-Pritchard): chieftaincy as natural expression of African political culture
- Nationalist critique (1950s–1970s): chieftaincy as colonial invention and obstacle to national unity
- Constructivist analysis (1980s–present): chieftaincy as dialectical product of colonial encounter, with "limits to invention" (Spear)
- Post-1990s revival studies: chieftaincy as ongoing resource of "tradition" in conditions of postcolonial state failure or disappointment

The key unresolved tension remains between: (a) colonialism as having fundamentally transformed governance by converting community leaders into coercive civil servants; and (b) chieftaincy as embodying "creative continuity" that confounds teleological assumptions about modern governance.
