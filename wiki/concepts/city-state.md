# City-State

## Definition

A **city-state** is a small, autonomous polity consisting of a city together with
its dependent hinterland of towns, villages, and farmland. City-states typically
occur in clusters of **peer-polities** sharing a common culture, competing and
warring with one another, with occasional ephemeral hegemonies that collapse back
to autonomy. The concept is central to [[yoffee-cwh-v3-2015|CWH Vol. III]] and is
the most-developed comparative framework for early [[urbanization|urbanism]]
(Nichols & Charlton 1997; Hansen's Copenhagen Polis Centre).

## The classic clusters

- **Sumer ([[uruk|Mesopotamia]])** — Early Dynastic Uruk, Ur, Lagash, Kish, Nippur:
  densely packed, separated by a few days' travel, each controlling a modest
  territory, perpetually at war — the template city-state world of the
  [[early-bronze-age|Early Bronze Age]].
- **Greek polis** (Morris & Knodell) — by 500 BCE **500+ poleis**, each an *asty*
  (town) plus *chora* (territory of a few dozen to ~2,500 km²), most with only
  hundreds to ~10,000 residents. In theory independent, often dominated by
  stronger neighbors (Sparta's Peloponnesian League). The polis was simultaneously
  **city and state**.
- **Maya** ([[tikal|Tikal]], Calakmul, etc.) — competing dynastic centers.
- **Renaissance Italy** — the historical source of the European concept.

## A distinctive case: the Indus

The [[mohenjo-daro|Indus]] "city-states" (Kenoyer, Wright) break the dense-cluster
mold: only ~5 cities scattered across >1 million km², separated by hundreds of
kilometers ("islands of urbanism in a sea of villages"). If each controlled its
surroundings, individual Indus "city-state" territories would reach 100,000–
170,000 km² — calling the very model into question.

## The Greek polis: emergence from the Dark Age (CAH III.1)

[[boardman-cah-v3p1-1982|CAH Vol. III, Part 1]] supplies the **historical genesis** of
the polis that the comparative model treats as a type. Hammond (Ch. 17) argues the
city-state grew **directly out of the [[greek-dark-age|Greek Dark Age]]**: the Dark
Age unit was the scattered **village-encampment** (*kome*), but in the Geometric period
these separate communities **combined** — first for corporate religious action above
the level of family worship (the cult of **Athena Poliouchos**, "guardian of the
polis," on the Spartan acropolis is emblematic), then for **joint political action**.
The earliest such communities arose among the **Dorian** states — **Sparta, Megara,
Corinth, Argos** — and, in response, among their non-Dorian neighbours (Asine, Tegea).
On the Asia Minor coast, Old **Smyrna** offers "the first certain and unambiguous
apparition of the organized Hellenic *polis*." The process was a **synoikism** (the
fusing of villages) catalysed by the return of trade, art, and literacy from the east
(via [[euboea|Euboea]]) — so the polis is, in origin, the political crystallization of
the Dark Age recovery rather than a survival from the Mycenaean palace world.

## Coercion vs capital (Tilly)

Charles Tilly's spectrum — **coercion-intensive** (territorial states, military
domination) vs **capital-intensive** (city-states, commerce and coinage) — helps
explain divergence: Sparta sat at the coercive extreme (helot labor), while most
Greek poleis pursued capital-intensive paths (trade, the new technology of
**coined money**, lowered transaction costs).

## City-states and the distribution of power

The comparative city-state literature undercut the assumption that early states
were uniformly despotic palace-economies, opening the way to [[heterarchy]] and
councils/assemblies/oligarchies. See [[hierarchy-vs-heterarchy-early-cities]].
