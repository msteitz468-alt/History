## Definition and Scope

This page covers the major intellectual and literary movements of the German-speaking lands between approximately 1810 and 1890, as surveyed by Ritchie Robertson (ch. 12 of [[smith-oxford-modern-german-history-2011]]). The period begins with the late Romantics and their retreat from the early Romantic program of fusing art and life, and ends with the emergence of Naturalism under the influence of Zola and Darwin. The arc moves from idealist aesthetics through religious crisis and political radicalism toward scientific positivism and realist fiction.

The period's intellectual life cannot be separated from its political and social contexts: the Napoleonic occupation, the Vormärz political repression, the 1848 revolutions and their failure, the founding of the [[german-empire]] (1871), and rapid industrialization all left direct traces in literary and philosophical production.

## Romanticism and Its Retreat (c. 1800–1830s)

Early Romanticism (Friedrich von Hardenberg/Novalis, Friedrich Schlegel, Ludwig Tieck) had harbored grandiose ambitions to fuse art and life. The late Romantics represented in Robertson's chapter inherit this legacy but retreat from its maximalism.

**E.T.A. Hoffmann (1776–1822):** His masterpiece Der goldne Topf (The Golden Pot, 1814) is subtitled "A Modern Fairy-Tale" — the fairy-tale genre transferred from timeless fairyland to modern Dresden. The "happiness now enjoyed by Anselmus" in the story's resolution is "life in poetry, where the holy harmony of all things is revealed as the deepest secret of nature." This represents a typically late Romantic solution: the ideal realm is accessible but requires retreat from bourgeois society rather than its transformation.

**Nationalist applications of Romanticism:** The nationalist turn used Romantic valuation of folk tradition for political purposes. Arnim and Brentano (Des Knaben Wunderhorn, 1805–1808) and the Grimm brothers (Kinder- und Hausmärchen, 1812–1814) claimed to document authentic German Volksgeist. Robertson notes that their claims "have not worn well": Arnim and Brentano drew many poems from printed sources; at least one Grimm informant was of Huguenot descent; the Grimms revised tales across editions, "creating an artificial fairy-tale style, and introducing a harsh moral message" (the Cinderella sisters' eyes pecked out by pigeons; Snow White's murderous mother replaced by stepmother to protect nuclear family). "The Grimms promoted conservative family values, and the conception of the creative and enduring German people (Volk)." Their Historical School of Law colleague Friedrich von Savigny (1779–1861) saw law as expression of specific historical tradition rather than universal values — both represent the organic-historical turn away from Enlightenment universalism.

## Religious Crisis and Radical Criticism (1830s–1840s)

The historical-critical study of Christianity, going back to Lessing and Göttingen Bible scholars, intensified into a full-scale crisis of belief:

**David Friedrich Strauss (1808–1874):** Life of Jesus, Critically Examined (1835–1836) opposed both naïve credulity and rationalizing explanation of miracles. Much of the Gospels is "myth," but this need not undermine religion, which "is founded on spiritual conviction, not historical facts." Yet in Robertson's reading, Strauss "prepared the way for Feuerbach" by making the historical unreliability of the Gospels visible.

**Ludwig Feuerbach (1804–1872):** Essence of Christianity (1841) explained religion as compensation for actual misery: "human beings, conscious of their own imperfection and unhappiness, imagined a supreme and perfect being, but ought instead to transfer their energies from heaven to earth and work to improve their own condition." This argument provided Karl Marx (1818–1883) with a basis for socialism; he wrote to Feuerbach that the "unity of man with man based on the real differences between men" could replace the illusory divine unity.

This sequence — Strauss → Feuerbach → Marx — represents one of the period's most consequential intellectual trajectories, moving from theological criticism through anthropological humanism to political economy.

## Junges Deutschland (Young Germany) and Political Literature

"Junges Deutschland" was not an organized movement but a loose grouping of liberal writers including Karl Gutzkow (1811–1878), Heinrich Laube (1806–1884), and Theodor Mundt (1808–1861) who wanted to discuss society critically in fiction. The most notorious was Gutzkow's Wally, die Zweiflerin (1835): a young woman's relationship with a cynical, morally emancipated lover ends in her suicide; the novel caused offense through "sharp criticism of Christianity and … sexual boldness."

## Georg Büchner: Radical Realism

Robertson identifies Georg Büchner (1813–1837) as the most consequential radical writer of the period. Büchner died at 23 but produced:

- **Woyzeck** (written 1836–1837, published posthumously): a soldier driven to murder his unfaithful lover under the pressure of poverty and scientific experimentation. Woyzeck is "a man so bruised by poverty and exploitation that his sanity disintegrates." The sympathy is entirely with the victim; the oppressors are a military officer (who embodies unthinking social prestige) and a doctor (who uses Woyzeck as a dietary experiment).
- **Danton's Death** (1835): revolutionary tragedy set during the Terror
- Scientific foundation: Büchner was a natural scientist who wrote a dissertation on the nervous system of barbels; his science was based on Naturphilosophie seeking to comprehend nature as "the manifestation of a primordial law, a law of beauty" — contrasting with the later positivist science of his brother Ludwig.

## Johann Nestroy: Viennese Comic Drama

Johann Nestroy (1801–1862) dominated Viennese popular theater and was "often called a theatrical Dickens." He contended with censorship but brought contemporary realities on stage, including railway travel (Eisenbahn-heiraten, 1844) — evidence that [[german-industrialization]]'s social effects entered literary representation rapidly.

## Heinrich Heine: Poetry and Radical Cosmopolitanism

Though not discussed at length in Robertson's chapter within my range, Heine (1797–1856) appears as a point of reference. His dispatches from Berlin in the 1820s provide evidence for the popular spread of völkisch ideas; as a cosmopolitan Jewish German intellectual, he represents a counter-pole to ethnic nationalism. See [[german-nationalism-formation]] for context.

## Realist Fiction (1848–1890)

**The Zeitroman (Novel of the Times):** Karl Immermann's Die Epigonen (1836) represents present-day Germany as "a shallow, insincere sequel to the greater eighteenth century," criticizing both the outdated aristocracy and the industrial bourgeoisie in whom "petty rational calculation has replaced aesthetic sensibility." Louise Otto-Peters (1819–1895) in Schloß und Fabrik (1846) explores class conflict as bourgeoisie supplants aristocracy and may in turn be displaced by proletariat.

**Poetic Realism:** From the 1880s the term "poetic realism" was retrospectively applied to mid-nineteenth-century German fiction. Otto Ludwig (1813–1865) demanded writing "true to actual life, while veiling its coarse and gross elements." The Novelle (short prose narrative) became the dominant prestigious form — partly through teaching utility, partly through its respectable Romance-language pedigree. Sophisticated narrative technique (interlocking frames, oral vs. written storytelling contrast) serves to "explore the intrinsic ambivalence of characters' motives and values." Key example: Theodor Storm's Der Schimmelreiter (1888).

**Science and Materialism in Realist Fiction:** Realism accepts the authority of science, but a different science from Büchner's Naturphilosophie — the "grimly factual positivist and materialist science" of Ludwig Büchner's Kraft und Stoff (Force and Matter, 1855). Earlier writers had worried about science's dehumanizing implications (Stifter's Der Condor, 1840; Droste's geological time vistas). Realist writers incorporated scientific worldview without the earlier anxiety.

**Ecological dimensions:** Wilhelm Raabe's Pfisters Mühle (Pfister's Mill, 1884) "contrasts old, pre-industrial, idyllic Germany with the new world of industrial prosperity that requires the chemical pollution of natural resources" — one of the earliest literary treatments of industrial environmental degradation.

**Historical drama and tragedy:** The period's "fascination with history and historical progress" produced recurring dramatic motifs of "necessary failure" — the great historical individual whose fate is determined by historical forces rather than individual agency. This connects German dramatic theory to Hegel's philosophy of history.

## Hegel and Schopenhauer as Philosophical Anchors

**Hegel (1770–1831):** While already established before 1810, Hegel's philosophy dominated German intellectual life through the early Victorian period. His suggestion that "the coming era of world history would be the time of the Germanic peoples" fed nationalist self-consciousness. His dialectical method generated the Young Hegelian movement (Strauss, Bauer, Feuerbach, Marx) that moved progressively from theological to anthropological to political critique.

**Schopenhauer (1788–1860):** Though not directly discussed in Robertson's chapter within my range, Schopenhauer's pessimism and his influence on Nietzsche, Wagner, and Thomas Mann are part of the period's intellectual history. His World as Will and Representation (1818, expanded 1844) offered a non-idealist metaphysics opposing Hegel's rational progressivism.

## Relationship to Nationalist and Confessional Dynamics

German literary and intellectual culture was not secular or "beyond" religious conflict. Williamson (ch. 10 of [[smith-oxford-modern-german-history-2011]]) shows that art and scholarship "served as alternative sites for the articulation of religious and confessional standpoints and antagonisms":

- Protestant intellectuals identified modern aesthetic and scientific culture as Protestant achievement (Kulturprotestantismus)
- Catholic clergy rejected German classical culture as "atheist or pagan" and encouraged Catholic reading culture as insulated alternative
- Jewish intellectuals (Heymann Steinthal, 1860s) articulated "Kulturjudentum" — identifying roots of modern science and literature in Hebrew Bible modes of thought — in explicit response to Protestant and Aryanist exclusion

By the 1830s, "the basic features of what became known as Kulturprotestantismus were already evident in the writings of Hegel and Schleiermacher." After 1848 this was "fused onto a liberal nationalism that would serve to legitimate the 'small German' solution to the national question in Germany."

## Limitations and Gaps

Robertson's chapter (my range: lines 4829–5154 of source) focuses on literary production and does not fully cover:
- Wagner's music dramas and their relationship to nationalism
- The Nietzsche-Wagner relationship and its implications
- The full scope of German philosophical development (Kant, Hegel, Schopenhauer, Nietzsche as a connected sequence)
- Women writers beyond brief mention (Otto-Peters)
- The relationship between popular and elite literary culture

## Historiography

Robertson follows an intellectual-cultural history approach emphasizing the internal logic of literary and philosophical development alongside its social contexts. The chapter resists both pure idealist history of ideas (texts as autonomous) and vulgar social determination (texts as mere reflections of class interests). It implicitly engages the debate between those who see German idealism as providing the philosophical foundation for nationalism and authoritarianism (the Sonderweg tradition) and those who emphasize the progressive, critical, and cosmopolitan strands within the same intellectual tradition (Heine, Büchner, Feuerbach, Marx).

**Collection coverage:** The Bibliotheca Alexandrina collection includes significant primary source texts (Goethe, Schiller, Heine, and others are likely present through various series); coverage of critical-analytical literature on this period is probably moderate through European intellectual history titles.
