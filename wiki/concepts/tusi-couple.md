# The Ṭūsī Couple

## Definition

The Ṭūsī couple is a geometric theorem, devised by the mathematician-astronomer [[nasir-al-din-tusi|Naṣīr al-Dīn al-Ṭūsī]] (d. 1274), that generates **linear oscillating motion from two combined uniform circular motions**. The theorem states:

> If two spheres touch internally, and the diameter of the larger sphere is twice that of the smaller, and if the larger sphere moves uniformly in one direction while the smaller sphere moves at twice the speed in the opposite direction, then the point of tangency will oscillate back and forth along the diameter of the larger sphere.

In modern terms: the superposition of two circular motions of radii *r* and *r/2* moving in opposite directions at angular velocities ω and 2ω produces a linear oscillation with amplitude 2r along the diameter of the larger circle. The resulting motion is strictly rectilinear — not merely approximately so — which was the theorem's distinctive mathematical power.

Saliba describes it as a device for producing "a straight motion by combining two circular motions, a result that was to have tremendous effects on later astronomers" ([[saliba-islamic-science-renaissance-2007]]).

## Intellectual Genesis

Al-Ṭūsī appears to have first perceived the connection when analyzing Ptolemy's latitude theory. Ptolemy's model for the latitudinal motions of Mercury and Venus required the inclined carrying plane to *seesaw* — tilting north and south — while Ptolemy's offered solution (attaching "small circles" to the tips of the inclined diameter) caused an intolerable wobbling that destroyed the longitudinal motion already computed. Al-Ṭūsī declared this arrangement "beyond what was permitted in the craft (*khārij ʿan al-ṣināʿa*)" in his *Taḥrīr* (1247).

His insight was that the oscillating motion Ptolemy needed could instead be produced by two uniform circular motions, which respected the Aristotelian requirement that all celestial motions be circular. The *Taḥrīr* contains a rudimentary version. A full formal proof appeared in the *Dhayl-i Muʿīnīya* (composed between 1247 and 1260/61) and was then republished in mature form in the **al-Tadhkira fī al-Hayʾa** (Memoir on Astronomy, ca. 1260/61), the text in which the theorem is now standardly located.

## Formal Statement (al-Tadhkira)

Two spheres (equivalently, two circles in the plane version) are arranged so that:
- Sphere A (radius R) contains sphere B (radius R/2) touching internally at point P.
- A rotates uniformly at angular velocity ω.
- B rotates at 2ω in the **opposite** direction.

The point P then moves along the diameter of A. After A has rotated through angle θ, P has displaced along the diameter by 2R·sin(θ) — equivalent to the diameter-directed component of a circular motion of radius R, i.e., it oscillates sinusoidally along AB's diameter. In the limit where the radii become equal (a degenerate version), the path collapses to a point, but for the canonical 2:1 ratio the path is perfectly linear ([[saliba-islamic-science-renaissance-2007]]).

## Applications by Ṭūsī

Al-Ṭūsī applied the couple in the *Tadhkira* to construct reformed models for:

1. **The Moon**: Deployed the couple to eliminate Ptolemy's equant-like construction (the deferent moving uniformly around the Earth's center rather than its own center) and to remove the prosneusis problem.
2. **The Upper Planets (Saturn, Jupiter, Mars, Venus)**: Used to produce the uniform motion that Ptolemy had approximated through the physically impossible equant, making all spheres rotate on axes passing through their own centers.

Al-Ṭūsī explicitly acknowledged failure for **Mercury**: "although he succeeded in solving the equant problem of the models of the moon and the upper planets, he was hoping to complete his task later on by solving the equant problem of Mercury, to which he had no new things to add at the time" ([[saliba-islamic-science-renaissance-2007]]). This problem was solved later by [[ibn-al-shatir|Ibn al-Shāṭir]], and a variant double-application was used by Quṭb al-Dīn al-Shīrāzī (d. 1311).

## Transmission and Later Use

The theorem's fecundity is attested by the chain of astronomers who adopted it:

- **Quṭb al-Dīn al-Shīrāzī** (d. 1311, al-Ṭūsī's student): used a double application of the Ṭūsī couple for his Mercury model — "a significant step, not only because he succeeded where Ṭūsī had already declared failure" ([[saliba-islamic-science-renaissance-2007]]).
- **Ibn al-Shāṭir** (d. 1375, Damascus): used the couple in his model for Mercury, combined with [[urdi-lemma|ʿUrḍī's Lemma]] for the other planets.
- **ʿAlāʾ al-Dīn al-Qushjī** (d. 1474): used it in his model for Mercury.
- **Shams al-Dīn al-Khafrī** (d. 1550): used it in producing four mathematically equivalent models for Mercury's motion.
- **Nicolaus Copernicus** (d. 1543): used the Ṭūsī couple in his *Commentariolus* and *De Revolutionibus* for his Mercury model and for the latitude theory, in the same structural connection that Ṭūsī had first identified — the link between the genesis of the couple and the latitude problem: "Later on in the *Commentariolus*, and while describing the motions of the planet Mercury, Copernicus goes further by clearly making reference to the second connection between the motions of Mercury and the motions in latitude" ([[saliba-islamic-science-renaissance-2007]]).

Saliba notes that Copernicus reached "the same connection between the two phenomena" — the latitude problem as the heuristic for the couple — independently or through textual transmission, which Saliba argues is the more parsimonious explanation given the mathematical identity of the models.

## Relationship to ʿUrḍī's Lemma

The Ṭūsī couple and [[urdi-lemma|ʿUrḍī's Lemma]] are complementary tools devised around the same time at the [[maragha-school|Maragha observatory]]:

| Tool | Author | Method | Primary target |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ṭūsī couple | Al-Ṭūsī (d. 1274) | Two concentric circles, 2:1 ratio, opposite rotation | Lunar and planetary equant; latitude seesawing |
| ʿUrḍī's Lemma | Al-ʿUrḍī (d. 1266) | Parallelogram of equal lines at equal angles | Upper-planet equant |

Saliba describes both as "epoch makers" that "allowed several generations of astronomers to think differently about Ptolemaic astronomy" ([[saliba-islamic-science-renaissance-2007]]). Ibn al-Shāṭir unified both in his system.

## Significance for the History of Science

The Ṭūsī couple is the single most cited example in the argument for the transmission of Islamic astronomical mathematics to Copernicus. Its appearance in the *Commentariolus* — a preliminary manuscript not intended for publication — in the same structural context as in the *Tadhkira*, using the same technique for the same problem, constitutes the core of Saliba's and related scholars' (Kennedy, Swerdlow, Neugebauer) transmission argument. The couple thus represents a direct link between the [[maragha-school|Maragha school]] of the 13th century and the Copernican revolution of the 16th.

## Historiography

The identification of the Ṭūsī couple in Copernicus was made by E. S. Kennedy and Victor Roberts in the 1950s–60s, extended by Noel Swerdlow and Otto Neugebauer. Saliba's contribution in *Islamic Science and the Making of the European Renaissance* (2007) is to embed this mathematical finding in a broader argument about the continuity and social embeddedness of the Islamic critique of Ptolemy.

The question of whether Copernicus had direct textual access to the *Tadhkira* or to later works incorporating the couple (such as those of Ibn al-Shāṭir) remains debated. That he used the device is not disputed; how he came to know it is.
