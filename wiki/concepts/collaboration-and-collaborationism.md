# Collaboration and Collaborationism

The analytical distinction — associated with Stanley Hoffmann and central to
[[paxton-vichy-france-1972]] — between **collaboration d'état** (the Vichy
state's pursuit, for reasons of state, of a negotiated place in a
German-dominated Europe) and **collaborationism** (the ideological commitment
of the Paris ultras — Doriot's PPF, Déat's RNP, the German-embassy-funded
press — who wanted France remade in the Axis image).

## Emic origin

The word itself is emic: Article 3 of the [[franco-german-armistice-1940]]
required French officials to "collaborate faithfully" with the occupation
authorities, and Pétain nationalized it at Montoire — "I enter into the way
of collaboration" (31 October 1940). Paxton's German-archive finding gives
the state variant its modern meaning: collaboration was a **French
initiative**, offered repeatedly to an indifferent Germany, aimed at a
favorable place in the peace, cheaper occupation, and the recovery of
sovereignty — not an ideology. Its practitioners were "a selection of Third
Republic notables from one end of the political spectrum to the other,"
united by antibolshevism and the primacy of internal order.

## The collaborationists

The ideological collaborationists were structurally separate: the occupied
Paris press and parties siphoned off the interwar fascist leaders to "the
gaudier but less independent" stage, and Vichy (Laval and Darlan alike, with
Abetz's help) kept Doriot from office. Only at the end — Darnand and Déat
entering the government in December 1943, the Milice war on the maquis — did
the two streams merge, in what Paxton calls the final paroxysm of a moribund
dictatorship.

## Limitations and critiques

The distinction can be drawn too cleanly: the state collaborators built the
police machinery the ultras later wielded; the anti-Jewish statutes were
state policy, not ultra agitation (see [[vichy-anti-jewish-laws-1940]]); and
individual trajectories (Pucheu, Marion, Déat) crossed the line freely. It is
also specific to France — regimes like Quisling's Norway fuse the two types.
Postwar French usage collapsed both into the single moral category of
*collabo*, which is precisely what the analytical distinction resists.

## From Paxton and Hessler, *Europe in the Twentieth Century* (2011)

Paxton and Hessler set out a four-part taxonomy of collaboration across occupied Europe that complements and generalizes the France-specific collaboration/collaborationism distinction above. Source: [[paxton-europe-twentieth-century-2011]] throughout.

**(1) Ideological collaboration** was, in their phrase, "more noisy than numerous." Hitler generally preferred to deal with established national leaders over homegrown fascist movements: Pétain over the French fascists, Regent Horthy over Ferenc Szálasi's Arrow Cross (which gained real power in Hungary only in October 1944), Ion Antonescu, who crushed his own Iron Guard in 1941. Anton Mussert remained a figurehead in the Netherlands; Vidkun Quisling was sidelined and then installed as a largely powerless minister-president in Norway only in February 1942. The Waffen-SS's foreign divisions numbered roughly 500,000 non-Germans by war's end, drawn mostly from Eastern Europe but including some 50,000 Dutch, 40,000 Belgians, 20,000 French, 6,000 Danes, and 6,000 Norwegians.

**(2) National-interest collaboration** was driven by territorial revisionism — Hungary and Romania each sought to recover or expand territory, which is part of why Romania fielded some 30 divisions on the Eastern Front. Slovak and Croatian statehood were themselves products of collaboration with Germany. The Ustasha regime in Croatia carried out terror against Serbs, Jews, and Roma that remains a live historiographical dispute: Serb historians have advanced a claim of roughly 1-in-6 Serbs in Croatia killed, a figure Croat historians (including Franjo Tudjman) have rejected; Paxton and Hessler cite Milovan Djilas's *Contested Country* on the dispute's persistence.

**(3) Economic collaboration** ranged from Albert Speer's post-1943 dispersal of German production into occupied territory (partly to escape Allied bombing) to French aluminum production running above prewar levels, to Ferdinand Lehideux's plan for a joint Franco-German-Italian automobile conglomerate, alongside dairy-region prosperity and a flourishing black market.

**(4) Passive acquiescence** — simply going along without ideological commitment, national calculation, or economic profit-seeking — was, in Paxton and Hessler's account, the condition of the majority of Europeans under occupation.
