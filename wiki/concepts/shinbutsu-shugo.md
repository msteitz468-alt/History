## Definition and Scope

*Shinbutsu shugo* (神仏習合) denotes the ritual and philosophical **combinations** (*shugo*) between indigenous kami (*shin*) and buddhas or bodhisattvas (*butsu*) that structured religious life in Nara and Heian Japan and persisted as the dominant cultic framework through most of the medieval period. The term is distinct from "syncretism" in a general sense: what was at stake were specific, locale-grounded associations between the particular kami of a given shrine and the particular buddhas and bodhisattvas of the adjacent temple. Each shrine-temple complex (*jinguji* multiplex) generated its own set of combinations, embedded in spatial arrangements, ritual calendars, and economic relationships.

The term "Shinto" almost never appears in the literature of the Heian period. As the CHJ notes: "The literature of the Heian period is striking in the total absence of the word 'Shinto.'" What existed instead was "a marked emphasis on sites of cult which were, for the duration of the period and beyond, a conglomerate of shrines and temples" (CHJ, ch. 8). A unitary, doctrinally autonomous Shinto emerged only in the Edo period and even then was heavily indebted to Neo-Confucianism, Taoism, and yin-yang practice.

## Theoretical Basis: Honji Suijaku

The dominant intellectual framework justifying kami-buddha combinations was the theory known as **honji suijaku** (本地垂迹), traditionally associated with the Lotus Sutra. According to this theory, the Body of Essence of the Buddha employed "expedient devices" (*hoben*) to manifest itself in various guises in the world in order to guide beings toward salvation. One such guise was the kami. It therefore became customary to identify specific kami as hypostases (*suijaku*, "trace manifestations") of specific members of the Buddhist pantheon (*honji*, "original ground").

Identification was made through symbolic correspondences. The CHJ gives the example of the main kami of the Kasuga Shrine — whose theriomorphic emblem was the deer — being identified with Fukukensaku Kannon, whose iconography called for a deer hide on its shoulder: "People looked at the presence of the deer in the shrine, then in the temple, saw it as a subtle and mysterious indication of the essential identity of the kami and of the bodhisattva, and thus combined them" (CHJ, ch. 8). Such naturalized associations pervaded the multiplex world and spread through aristocratic and popular culture.

## Institutional Framework: The Jinguji and the Twenty-Two Shrines

The institutional vehicle for kami-buddha combinations was the **jinguji** (shrine-temple complex), in which a Buddhist temple was built in proximity to a Shinto shrine and both were administered as a single cultic entity. Most major shrines in the Heian period were organized this way. The state-sponsored **Twenty-two Shrine** system linked the most important shrine-temple multiplexes of the Kinai region to the imperial lineage, requiring the emperor to dispatch messengers and offerings at major rites.

Buddhism tended to supply the dominant institutional and doctrinal framework. Temples developed administrative supremacy over the adjacent shrines, and the theoretical definition of the kami was generally left to Buddhist prelates. Yet the realm of shrines was not absorbed: "clear demarcations prevented chaotic syncretism from occurring, and called instead for combinations whose elements retained some of their integrity" (CHJ, ch. 8). The cult world of combinations became central to medieval Japan precisely because neither pole became wholly dominant in the Heian period.

## Geographic Expressions: Kunisaki and Kumano

Two regional systems illustrate the range and depth of shinbutsu shugo in practice.

**Kunisaki** (Kyushu): An elaborate system organized by Tendai mountain ascetics who mapped the entire Kunisaki peninsula onto the Lotus Sutra, building as many temples as there are chapters and as many buddha-images as Chinese graphs in the text. The peninsula was thus conceptualized as a physical embodiment of scripture, and Hachiman was identified as a bodhisattva named Nimmon. The ritual aim was protection of the state against foreign invasion.

**Kumano** (Kii peninsula): Three major shrines (Hongu, Shingu, Nachi) became a nexus for mountain asceticism combining Shingon and Tendai esotericism with Pure Land cosmography. Kumano came to be seen as "a physical gate to the transcendental space of the Pure Land, and then as a Pure Land in this world." Retired Emperor Uda pioneered aristocratic pilgrimage there in 907; by the end of the Heian period, retired emperor Go-Shirakawa had made the journey thirty-four times.

## Social and Political Dimensions

Kami-buddha combinations were not merely theological. They were embedded in:
- **Lineage politics**: Major shrine-temple multiplexes were dedicated to the ancestral kami of great aristocratic houses (Kasuga-Fujiwara; Iwashimizu-imperial house/Minamoto). Access to these cults was a mechanism of social legitimation.
- **Land and taxation**: Multiplexes accumulated private landholdings (*shoen*) as rewards for successful ritual and as commendations from nobles seeking spiritual protection and tax avoidance. "Ritual came to be ever more connected to economics" (CHJ, ch. 8).
- **Political authority**: The more politically unstable conditions became, the more intense ritual activity grew. Multiplexes were called upon to perform rites during rebellions, epidemics, celestial irregularities, and succession crises.

## Late Heian Disruption

The dominant paradigm of state-sponsored kami-buddha combinations came under pressure in the second half of the Heian period from two directions:
1. **Pure Land heterodoxy**: The claim that sole reliance on Amida Buddha ensured rebirth in the Pure Land, making the worship of kami unnecessary. "This caused immediate divisions, whose nature was obviously political, because the rejected kami were in fact those that represented the ideology of the state in the shrine-temple multiplexes" (CHJ, ch. 8).
2. **Ecstatic mass movements**: Popular cults (goryō, onryō, Shidara) and danced nembutsu spread outside the multiplex framework, reaching social strata not integrated into aristocratic ritual.

The Insei period (from 1086) attempted to reinforce the kami-buddha combination world as a tool of imperial legitimation, but by the late twelfth century the framework was under fundamental stress.

## Historiography

Scholarly debate centers on:
- Whether the phenomenon is best described as "syncretism" (merging) or "combinatorics" (structured coexistence). The CHJ author (Allan Grapard) argues for the latter, emphasizing spatial and institutional embeddedness over doctrinal blending.
- The role of Kukai and Saicho: medieval scholars attributed most combinatory rationales to them, but modern scholarship is skeptical about such clean attributions.
- The post-Meiji narrative: the Meiji-era *shinbutsu bunri* (separation of kami and buddhas, 1868) retrospectively constructed a "pure Shinto" that had not existed, distorting how historians read the Heian evidence.
