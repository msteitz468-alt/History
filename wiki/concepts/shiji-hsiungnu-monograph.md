# Shih Chi Hsiung-nu Monograph (Chapter 110)

Chapter 110 of [[sima-qian|Sima Qian's]] *Shih chi* (*Records of the Grand Historian*, c. 91 BCE) is the **foundational text** for the historiography of the [[xiongnu|Xiongnu]] and, more broadly, the oldest sustained ethnographic-historical account of Inner Asian nomadic peoples in the Chinese literary tradition. It established the template for "northern monographs" in all subsequent Standard Histories, and its interpretive framework — a binary north/south polarity between nomadic peoples and the Chinese Central States — shaped scholarly thinking about the steppe frontier for two millennia. No prior Chinese text had treated northern peoples as independent objects of sustained historical investigation.

## Content and Structure

Chapter 110 is one of the *liezhuan* (biographical and monographic) sections of the *Shih chi*. Its structure moves through three phases:

**1. Ethnogenealogy (opening passages)**: Traces the Xiongnu from a descent-line of the Xia dynasty through a succession of northern peoples — Shan Rong, Xianyun, Xunyu, Quanrong, Rong and Di, and finally the unified Xiongnu empire under [[modu-chanyu|Modu Chanyu]]. The genealogy claims to span from the time of the sage kings Yao and Shun through the Eastern Zhou to the Qin-Han transition.

**2. Ethnographic description of Xiongnu society**, covering:
- *Pastoral nomadism*: animals bred (horses, cattle, sheep, camels, donkeys; rare crossbred equines), seasonal migration patterns ("they move about according to the availability of water and pasture"), informal land tenure ("each of them has a portion of land")
- *Burial customs*: use of inner and outer coffins, gold and silver ornaments, human sacrifice at the ruler's death; notably no earthen mounds, trees, or mourning garments
- *Society and laws*: brief, clear legal code ("those who draw the sword one foot [out of the scabbard] are sentenced to death; those who steal lose their properties"); no written language; laws transmitted orally
- *Military organization and tactics*: lifelong training beginning in childhood (children ride sheep, shoot birds and mice with bow and arrow); cavalry supremacy derived from daily life with animals; use of moon phases to determine attack or retreat; rewards for heads taken; pursuit of immediate material gain in battle rather than honor
- *State sacrifices and ritual*: the Chanyu's daily obeisances to sun and moon; three annual assemblies (including the great autumn census); use of the *wu* and *chi* days of the ten-stem calendar
- *Language*: unprecedented inclusion of Xiongnu loanwords (titles such as *yanzhi* "queen," cultural terms including *ch'iung-lu* "tent/yurt," *lo* "kumiss," *mi-li* "dried curd," *su* "fat/butter")

**3. History of Han-Xiongnu relations**: From the Qin campaigns through Modu Chanyu's consolidation, the battle of Baideng (200 BCE), the [[heqin-policy|heqin ("peace and kinship") policy]], and the military campaigns of Wei Qing and Huo Qubing under Han Wudi. Sima Qian provides detailed economic analysis of the cost of these wars, documenting military expenditures, famine, population transfers, and the strain on Han state resources.

The chapter closes with Sima Qian's personal colophon, in which he admits he was "not free to speak openly" — evidence of political constraint on his account.

## Sources Used by Sima Qian

Di Cosmo (2002) identifies five categories of sources on which Sima Qian drew:

1. **Personal observation and travel**: Sima Qian was "regarded as one of the most widely traveled men of his time." He personally inspected the northern fortifications built by Qin general Meng Tian in 110 BCE, when he accompanied Emperor Wu on a border inspection tour (*Shih chi* 88, 2570).

2. **Oral accounts from people with direct Xiongnu knowledge**: Most important is the speech attributed to Zhonghang Yue, a Chinese renegade living at the Xiongnu court. His reported reply to Han diplomatic pressure contains a detailed description of Xiongnu customs and way of life. Sima Qian was also personally acquainted with military men with extensive firsthand Xiongnu knowledge, including General Li Guang and Su Jian.

3. **Prisoners of war**: Both sides produced prisoners who were taken back and could provide information. Sima Qian drew on returnees from both directions.

4. **Contemporary written documents and memorials**: Especially the memorials of Chao Cuo, already discussed at length elsewhere in the *Shih chi*, which provide the documentary foundation for chapters on Han frontier policy.

5. **Xiongnu people in Chinese society**: Kan Fu (the Xiongnu slave who traveled with Zhang Qian to Central Asia over thirteen years) and Jin Midi (heir to the Xiuqu king's throne, captured and employed as a horse-tender at the Yellow Gate Palace).

Before the *Shih chi*, Sima Qian notes, "there were no intellectual 'containers' in which factual historical and geographical data could be stored and transmitted." Earlier texts included scattered loanwords from around 300 BCE (*Yi Chou shu*, chapter 36) and mythological references to northern peoples in the *Shan-hai ching* and the cosmological sections of the *Huai-nan Tzu*, but none constituted serious ethnographic or historical inquiry.

## The Two Analytical Strands: Di Cosmo's Reading

Nicola Di Cosmo (*Ancient China and Its Enemies*, 2002, chapters 7–8) provides the most sustained critical analysis of chapter 110 and argues that it contains two analytically distinct orientations that must be separated.

### Strand 1: The Empirical-Descriptive

Sima Qian applied to Inner Asian peoples the same method he applied as Grand Astrologer (T'ai-shih-ling): systematic empirical observation, recording, and identification of patterns. Di Cosmo describes this as "somewhat similar to what the Greek called autopsy, 'seeing things for oneself.'" The resulting ethnographic data was, Di Cosmo argues, genuinely unprecedented — "there was no obvious precedent that Ssu-ma Ch'ien could have used for inspiration." Earlier Chinese accounts of northern peoples "are embedded in a web of metaphysical theories and mythological beliefs that bear no relation to their ethnographic or geographic reality."

Sima Qian consciously distanced himself from the mythological tradition, as he explicitly states in the concluding remarks to *Shih chi* chapter 123: "as for the strange beings illustrated in the 'Basic Annals of Yü' and in the Shan-hai ching, I do not dare speak about them." This commitment to empirical verification drove the "paradigm shift from the moralistic or chronachistic accounts of the past to the historically and ethnographically rich report that Ssu-ma Ch'ien was able to produce."

The empirical content of chapter 110 can be partially verified against the archaeological record. Sima Qian's description of gold and silver ornaments in Xiongnu burials is confirmed by the Warring States sites at Xigoupan and Aluchaideng. His identification of the specific animals bred by nomads (including camels, donkeys, and rare equine hybrids) corresponds closely to the actual composition of steppe herds. His observation that "each of them has a portion of land" — interpreted by Di Cosmo as evidence of customary, non-transferable grazing rights for families and clans — represents the first Chinese recognition of seasonal nomadic migration as a regular cycle rather than random wandering.

### Strand 2: The Normative-Cosmological

Simultaneously, Sima Qian inserted the Xiongnu into the cosmological framework of Han correlative thought. This operated on two levels:

**(a) Ethnogenealogy**: Chapter 110 opens with the assertion:

> "The ancestor of the Hsiung-nu was a descendant of the ruling clan of the Hsia dynasty, named Shun-wei. As early as the time of emperors Yao and Shun and before there were people known as Shan Jung, Hsien-yün, and Hsün-yü; they lived in the northern marches (man) and moved around following their herds." (*Shih chi* 110, 2879–82; trans. Watson)

Di Cosmo argues this is not historical documentation but a constructed narrative: "the 'ethnogenealogy' presented by Ssu-ma Ch'ien is based not on 'anthropological' or documentary evidence, but on a historical correlation that aimed to establish a precise connection with the past and to demonstrate that the Hsiung-nu filled the same antithetical position to China that had previously been played by other foreigners." The sole criterion for linking peoples across more than a millennium is geographic (they all lived to China's north). Sima Qian himself admitted having no records for the actual history of the Xiongnu royal house.

The genealogy served *reductio ad notum*: it reduced a frightening novelty — a unified steppe empire — to familiar categories. "These identifications served the purpose of depriving the new enemy of his most frightening feature, the mysterious nature of his threat." The construction also established the pendular, cyclical pattern of alternating north-south power that organized the historical narrative of passages I–VII of chapter 110, and which dominated later historiography.

Sima Qian's construction also served an ideological function: it represented "the unfolding of the Chinese march into foreign territories almost as a 'manifest destiny'" — with the sage kings' conquests of the north as a template for Han expansion.

**(b) Astrological cosmology**: In *Shih chi* chapter 27 ("Treatise on the Heavenly Officials"), Sima Qian was — for the first time in Chinese intellectual history — "the first astrologer to have crossed the boundaries of the Chinese political and cultural sphere to include the non-Chinese, and the northern nomads in particular, in the correlative cosmological apparatus." The system of "allocated fields" (fen-yeh), which during the Eastern Zhou had correlated celestial zones with specific Chinese states, was extended by Sima Qian to encompass Inner Asian peoples:

> "[The Lodge of] Mao is called Mao-t'ou: this is the star of the Hu [i.e., the Hsiung-nu], and presides over funerary matters. The Lodge of Pi is called Han-ch'e, and symbolizes military engagements on the borders, it presides over hunting with bows and arrows. Between the Lodges of Mao and Pi there is the T'ien-chieh [Heavenly Route] constellation; to the yin [northern] side of it there are the yin countries, to its yang [southern] side there are the yang countries [i.e., the Central States, China]." (*Shih chi* 27, 1305–1306)

The Xiongnu were assigned to the yin (northern, cold, female, dark) principle; the Central States to the yang (southern, warm, male, bright) principle. Northern peoples' corresponding planets were Moon, Venus, and Mercury; China's were Sun, Jupiter, Mars, and Saturn. The astrological prognostications derived from these correlations were applied to predict the outcome of military campaigns.

Di Cosmo's interpretation of this move: "By fully integrating the Yi and the Ti at one end of the binary combinations that were thought to form the cosmic patterns of a dialectically conceived universe, Ssu-ma Ch'ien guaranteed these foreign peoples a perennial place within the cosmology and history of China." The north was thereby made ideologically explicable and, in a sense, controllable.

## Biases and Limitations

Di Cosmo identifies several significant limitations:

1. **Invented genealogy without documentary foundation**: The ethnogenealogy links peoples across more than a millennium on purely geographic grounds. Many of the peoples linked were absorbed by Chinese states, migrated, or disappeared — making their ancestral connection to the Xiongnu purely notional.

2. **Selective representation of Eastern Zhou frontier relations**: In tracing the antithetical relationship between Chinese states and northern peoples, Sima Qian omits that Chou states frequently allied with northern peoples against Chinese rivals. "This demarcation line never existed because the political picture was extremely fluid." The consistent China-vs.-north binary is an anachronistic construction.

3. **China-centric frame**: The normative framework assigns Inner Asia's historical significance entirely in terms of its relationship to China. The Xiongnu matter because they matter to China. As Di Cosmo summarizes: "the history of the nomads came into existence, as it were, because it was relevant to China."

4. **Cosmological overlay on historical causation**: The astrological prognostications in chapter 27 do not represent historical reasoning; they employ the correlative, prescriptive mode to "explain" events by appositional juxtaposition (an eclipse followed by a Xiongnu raid) rather than causal analysis.

5. **Incomplete and partially inaccurate ethnography**: The double coffin described as a Xiongnu burial norm is found only in some Xiongnu sites and may reflect elite practice or Chinese influence; the claim of human sacrifice at the ruler's death cannot be confirmed archaeologically (no "imperial" Xiongnu grave has been found). Some details may derive from Xiongnu informants who embellished accounts or from Chinese preconceptions.

6. **Self-censorship**: Sima Qian's colophon to chapter 110 admits he was "not free to speak openly." The extent to which this constrained the monograph's content is unknown.

## The Historiographical Legacy: The "Fourth Frontier"

Di Cosmo identifies chapter 110 as creating what he calls the "fourth frontier" — a historiographical frontier alongside the material, cultural, and diplomatic frontiers he analyzes. "Two elements contributed to the appearance of the 'master narrative' of the north that to this day, partly consciously, partly unconsciously, informs our knowledge and conceptualization of the frontier: the great expansion of historical knowledge collected and transmitted by Ssu-ma Ch'ien, and the particular frame in which he inserted it."

The *Shih chi* established not only the content of Chinese knowledge about the Xiongnu but the structural model for every subsequent "northern monograph" in the Standard Histories. Ban Gu's *Han shu* chapter 94 — the next major Xiongnu account — is largely a verbatim reproduction of chapter 110 in its first portion. Ban Gu, however, adds "strong derogatory expressions, such as that the Hsiung-nu had human faces but hearts of beasts" — characterizations absent from Sima Qian — and notably omits Sima Qian's name entirely (a deliberate erasure, Di Cosmo suggests, reflecting Ban Gu's disagreement with his predecessor's more sympathetic approach). Later T'ang-dynasty responses to Inner Asian peoples (Uighurs, etc.) were still patterned on the "Hsiung-nu topos" first established in chapter 110.

The consequence for scholarship: "Virtually all we know about the rise of the Hsiung-nu and the transformation of the frontier in the Ch'in-Han transition derives, in a historical sense, from the Shih chi, as does the model itself of a monograph on the Hsiung-nu." Scholars who rely on chapter 110 without critical distance reproduce not only Sima Qian's data but his ideological structure — the assumption that Inner Asian history is essentially a product of its relationship with China, and that nomadic peoples constitute the permanent yin to China's yang.

Di Cosmo's methodological prescription: the two strands must be disentangled. The empirical-descriptive content can be assessed against the archaeological and comparative record. The normative-cosmological content should be treated as primary evidence for early Han intellectual history — how Han thinkers conceptualized the cosmos and China's place within it — not as evidence about the Xiongnu.

## Comparison with Herodotus

Di Cosmo twice draws the comparison to Herodotus: Sima Qian was "regarded as one of the most widely traveled men of his time" (as Herodotus was in the Greek tradition), and like Iordanes drawing on Priscus's residence at Attila's court, Sima Qian relied on firsthand informants who had lived among the Xiongnu. The comparison illuminates the methodological innovation: what Sima Qian did for the Xiongnu is structurally analogous to what Herodotus did for the Scythians — producing the first sustained ethnographic treatment of a steppe nomadic people by a sedentary civilizational historian, using a combination of personal travel, firsthand informants, written documents, and a reflective method that sought to distinguish between verified information and report.
