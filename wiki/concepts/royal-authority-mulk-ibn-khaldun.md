# Royal Authority (Mulk) — Ibn Khaldun's Theory

## Definition

**Mulk** (royal authority) is the form of power that arises when [[asabiyyah|group feeling]] reaches its maximum concentration in a ruling individual or family. In [[ibn-khaldun|Ibn Khaldun's]] framework, mulk is not an accident of history or a divine gift standing apart from natural causation — it is the *necessary destination* of strong group feeling, "the natural goal of group feeling."

> "Royal authority and large dynastic power are attained only through a group and group feeling. This is because aggressive and defensive strength is obtained only through group feeling which means mutual affection and willingness to fight and die for each other." (*Muqaddimah*, Ch. III, §1)

Royal authority is defined operationally. It belongs only to those who: (1) dominate subjects, (2) collect taxes, (3) send out military expeditions, (4) protect frontier regions, and (5) have no one over them who is stronger than they. Authority that cannot meet all five criteria is "defective and not royal authority in the real meaning of the term" (*ibid.*, §21).

## The Genesis of Mulk from Group Feeling

Mulk does not appear suddenly. [[the-muqaddimah|The Muqaddimah]] traces its emergence through a causal sequence:

1. A group living under harsh desert or pastoral conditions develops intense [[asabiyyah|asabiyyah]] — mutual solidarity, willingness to fight and die together.
2. One lineage within the group emerges as dominant through the superiority of its particular branch of group feeling.
3. That dominant lineage produces a leader who, by nature, seeks to monopolize glory: "he is too proud to let others share in his leadership and control over the people."
4. The leader concentrates royal authority in his own house, excluding the broader tribe.
5. At this point mulk fully crystallizes: "he claims all glory for himself and does not permit the people to share in it."

The transition from shared group solidarity to concentrated royal authority is described as *unavoidable* — "something that happens to dynasties of necessity." The same forces that built the group up push inexorably toward the concentration of power.

## Mulk and the Monopoly of Glory

Once established, royal authority by its "very nature" pursues three tendencies that are simultaneously its defining features and its seeds of destruction:

**1. Claiming all glory exclusively.** The ruler cannot tolerate shared sovereignty. This is grounded in what Ibn Khaldun calls *ta'alluh* (an innate quality of self-divinization or egotism) present in human animal nature. Politics itself requires it: "Were various persons, liable to differ among each other, to exercise it, destruction of the whole could result." Yet in monopolizing glory, the ruler alienates the very group members whose solidarity had made his rise possible.

**2. Going in for luxury.** Royal authority necessarily brings prosperity, and prosperity brings luxury. Once a nation gains "the upper hand and takes possession of the holdings of its predecessors," its standard of living rises irreversibly. "Every new generation wants to surpass the preceding one in this respect, and so it goes right down to the end of the dynasty."

**3. Preferring tranquility and quiet.** After the hard-won attainment of royal authority, the ruling group stops doing "the tiresome chores they had been used to undertake while still in search of it." Rest replaces exertion; comfort replaces savagery.

All three tendencies are structurally produced by mulk and cannot be avoided. Together they produce the erosion of the group feeling that had made the dynasty possible.

## The Three-Generation Law

Ibn Khaldun calculates the lifespan of royal authority at approximately three generations (c. 120 years):

- **First generation**: Retains desert toughness, shared glory, bravery, and rapacious habits. Group feeling is at its peak. The dynasty is feared.
- **Second generation**: Transitions from desert life to sedentary culture. Privation gives way to luxury. The ruler begins claiming sole glory; others grow accustomed to subservience. Many old virtues remain, but weakened.
- **Third generation**: Has "completely forgotten the period of desert life and toughness, as if it had never existed." Group feeling "disappears completely." The dynasty requires foreign soldiers for its defense and is vulnerable to any challenger with fresh asabiyyah.

> "The third generation, then, has completely forgotten the period of desert life and toughness, as if it had never existed. They have lost the taste for the sweetness of fame and group feeling, because they are dominated by force. Luxury reaches its peak among them." (*Muqaddimah*, Ch. III, §12)

## Defective and Full Royal Authority

Ibn Khaldun distinguishes degrees of mulk:
- **Full royal authority**: A ruler who meets all five operational criteria above, with group feeling strong enough to dominate all competitors in a territory.
- **Provincial authority**: Rulers who govern their own people but remain subordinate to a larger dynasty.
- **Defective authority**: Authority that cannot defend frontiers, collect taxes, or suppress internal rivals — a condition common in aging dynasties surviving on institutional inertia rather than real group feeling.

He cites the late Abbasid caliphs, controlled by Turkish military commanders, as the paradigm case of defective royal authority: the caliph held symbolic legitimacy while real mulk passed to others.

## Mulk vs. Caliphate

Ibn Khaldun distinguishes three types of governance derived from the same base of group feeling:

| Type | Principle | Goal |
|---|---|---|
| Natural royal authority | Force / animal nature | Whatever the ruler desires |
| Political royal authority | Rational policy | Worldly welfare of subjects |
| Caliphate (*khilafah*) | Divine law | Welfare in this world and the next |

The caliphate is mulk *plus* Sharia: it "serves, like the Prophet, to protect the religion and to exercise political leadership of the world." The practical requirement for Qurashite descent in the caliph is reduced by Ibn Khaldun to a requirement for *group feeling* — the Quraysh were required because they commanded the strongest group solidarity among the Arabs, not because of genealogical blessing alone. When Qurashite group feeling dissolved, the condition lost its operative force.

## Historical Examples in This Range

Ibn Khaldun illustrates his theory of mulk throughout Chapter III with examples drawn from:
- The Sinhajah dynasty in the Maghrib, whose royal authority collapsed when Arab group feeling was destroyed and the Almohads — carrying stronger Masmudah group feeling — replaced them.
- The Umayyad dynasty in Spain: when Arab group feeling was exhausted, the reyes de taifas emerged — rulers wielding royal titles without the group feeling to back them ("like a cat that by blowing itself up imitates the lion").
- The Abbasids: as Arab group feeling eroded, power passed to Turkish, Persian, and Daylamite clients, until the Mongols destroyed the caliphate entirely.
- The Almohads and Almoravids: dynasties that rose with strong tribal group feeling combined with religious calling, conquered older polities, then underwent the same luxury-erosion cycle.
