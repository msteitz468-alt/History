## Definition and Scope

This page documents the **dialectical method and intellectual programme** that [[al-ghazali|al-Ghazālī]]
articulates in the prefaces and four introductions to the [[tahafut-al-falasifa|Tahāfut al-Falāsifa]]
(*Incoherence of the Philosophers*, c. 1095 CE). The method is a deliberately asymmetric form of
philosophical dialectic: al-Ghazālī enters the debate not as an affirmer of a rival doctrine but as
a prosecutor demanding that the philosophers prove their claims by their own logical standards. The
programme is to expose the internal incoherence of Avicennan metaphysics rather than to replace it
with a fully articulated alternative.

This methodological self-positioning is foundational to understanding all twenty Discussions of the
*Tahāfut* — and is the single most important thing al-Ghazālī says in his own voice before the
argument begins.

Source: [[ghazali-incoherence-of-philosophers]] (Marmura translation, prefaces through Discussion 1,
lines 1280–3599 of the raw text).

## The Three-Part Methodological Declaration

### 1. Demanding without affirming

Al-Ghazālī opens his introduction with an explicit statement of prosecutorial stance:

> "I do not enter into [argument] objecting to them, except as one who demands and denies, not as
> one who claims [and] affirms. I will render murky what they believe in [by showing] conclusively
> that they must hold to various consequences [of their theories]."

The rhetorical strategy that follows from this: al-Ghazālī does not need to establish a positive
counter-doctrine. He only needs to show that the philosophers' arguments fail by the philosophers'
own criteria — that they produce contradictions the philosophers cannot resolve, or that they invoke
"rational necessity" (*ḍarūrat al-ʿaql*) for claims that are not in fact evident to all rational
people. He makes this explicit: "All of what you have stated, there is nothing but [an expression
of] unlikelihood and the drawing of an analogy with our resolve and will... sheer deeming of
something as unlikely, without demonstrative proof, is not sufficient."

### 2. Coalition strategy: uniting all theological schools

Al-Ghazālī explicitly refuses to defend any single theological position:

> "I, however, will not rise to the defense of any one doctrine but will make all the sects as one
> group against them. For the rest of the sects may differ from us in matters of detail, whereas
> these [philosophers] challenge the [very] principles of religion."

This is a significant move: the *Tahāfut* is not a strictly [[asharism|Ashʿarite]] text, despite
al-Ghazālī's personal theological commitments. He deliberately uses arguments available to
Muʿtazilites ([[kalam|kalām]] rationalists) and even the Karrāmiyya — rival theological schools he
elsewhere criticizes — in order to present the widest possible front against the philosophers. He
acknowledges this openly, noting that he will at times "force on them at one time necessary
adherence to Muʿtazilite doctrine, at another to that of the Karrāmiyya."

This coalition framing explains why some scholars have found the *Tahāfut* philosophically
inconsistent: al-Ghazālī is not working from a single fixed positive doctrine but strategically
employing multiple dialectical resources.

### 3. Selection of the strongest arguments

Al-Ghazālī acknowledges that he has deliberately selected the philosophers' *strongest* arguments
for refutation, omitting weaker ones:

> "There is no virtue in lengthening matters. Let us, then, delete from their proofs whatever belongs
> to the category of the arbitrary or of that which is feeble imagining... Let us [instead] confine
> ourselves to bringing forth those [proofs] that leave an impact on the soul and that are able to
> arouse doubt in the best speculative thinkers."

This is methodologically honest but also strategically shrewd: if the best arguments fail, the
weaker ones fall with them. Discussion 1 (the eternity of the world) is explicitly characterized as
the *strongest* terrain for the philosophers: "in general, their discussion in the rest of the
metaphysical questions is weaker than their discussion in this."

## The Fourth Introduction: Detaching Metaphysics from Mathematics and Logic

The fourth introduction — a rhetorical intervention that has received less scholarly attention than
it deserves — addresses a specific **social tactic of the philosophers**: their claim that
metaphysics cannot be understood without first mastering mathematics and logic. Al-Ghazālī
identifies this as a "trick" (*ḥīla*) designed to silence critics:

> "One of the tricks these [philosophers] use in enticing [people] when confronted with a difficulty
> in the course of an argument is to say: 'These metaphysical sciences are obscure and hidden,
> being the most difficult of the sciences for intelligent minds. One can only arrive at knowing
> the answer to these difficulties through the introduction of mathematics and logic.'"

His response distinguishes three components:

1. **Arithmetic**: has no relation whatsoever to metaphysics. The claim that it is a prerequisite
   is "nonsense" — comparable to claiming grammar requires arithmetic.
2. **Geometry** (concerning the spherical shape and movements of the heavens): al-Ghazālī is
   willing to "concede all this to them, either dialectically or out of conviction." Geometrical
   conclusions about the cosmos have no bearing on metaphysical conclusions about creation.
3. **Logic**: the philosophers are correct that logical training is needed for metaphysics — but
   wrong to claim they own logic. Al-Ghazālī notes that what they call "logic" is what the
   theologians call "The Book of Reflection" or "The Cognitions of the Intellects." The name
   change is a prestige play, not a substantive difference.

His response is to do the *Tahāfut* entirely in the philosophers' logical idiom — to "dispute with
them in this book in their language" — and then show that the philosophers have failed to meet the
very conditions they themselves set for demonstrative proof.

## The Twenty Discussions: Scope and Division

Al-Ghazālī concludes his introductions with a table of contents for the twenty Discussions. The
division is significant:

**The three heresies (*kufr*)** — doctrines al-Ghazālī classifies as outright unbelief:
- Discussion 1: the world's pre-eternity (see [[eternity-of-the-world]])
- Discussion 13: God knows only universals, not particulars
- Discussion 20: denial of bodily resurrection

**Mathematical and logical sciences**: explicitly exempted from criticism — "there is no sense in
denying them or disagreeing with them."

**The remaining seventeen Discussions**: classified as "innovations" (*bidaʿ*) or blameworthy
errors short of apostasy — mistakes about divine unity, attributes, divine knowledge, the nature
of heaven, the soul, and causation.

This taxonomy matters because al-Ghazālī does not argue that philosophy as such is condemned —
only that certain specific metaphysical claims exceed what philosophy can demonstrate and conflict
with what revelation requires.

## Wahm (Estimation) as Diagnostic Concept

A recurring analytical tool in the *Tahāfut*'s introductions and Discussion 1 is **wahm** — the
faculty of estimation or imagination. Al-Ghazālī deploys this Avicennan concept against Avicenna's
own positions. The argument takes the following form:

- The philosophers appeal to what seems obviously impossible (e.g., an eternal will producing a
  temporal event with no mediating change)
- Al-Ghazālī shows that many such "obvious impossibilities" are in fact only products of *wahm* —
  the faculty grasping what it is accustomed to from sensory experience and projecting it onto the
  unseen
- Just as *wahm* wrongly insists there must be empty space beyond the finite sphere of the world
  (since we have never experienced a finite body without something beyond it), *wahm* wrongly insists
  there must be a temporal "before" before the beginning of time, or a change in God when He creates

This is an **epistemological argument**: the compelling feel of the philosophers' intuitions about
eternity and causation is produced by a faculty that is appropriate in its ordinary domain (sensory
experience of ordinary objects) but generates illusions when applied to questions about God, time,
and the origin of the universe.

## The Declared Non-Aim: Not a Positive Metaphysics

The *Tahāfut* explicitly defers positive doctrine:

> "In this book we have undertaken only to muddy their doctrine and throw dust in the face of their
> proofs with that which would reveal their incoherence. We have not undertaken to defend a specific
> doctrine... As regards affirming the true doctrine, we will write another book concerning it
> after completing this one... and we will name it *The Principles of Belief*."

This self-limitation has been read in two ways:
- **Sceptical reading**: al-Ghazālī is a sceptic about metaphysics generally, not just about the
  philosophers' metaphysics; his positive theology is essentially fideist
- **Strategic reading**: the *Tahāfut* is a polemical instrument serving a positive programme that
  al-Ghazālī had articulated elsewhere (above all in the *Iḥyāʾ ʿUlūm al-Dīn*); the disclaimer is
  a genre convention

The *Iḥyāʾ* and the *Mishkāt al-Anwār* (Niche of Lights) show that al-Ghazālī did hold a positive
metaphysical theology — but the *Tahāfut* is deliberately not the place where it is articulated.
