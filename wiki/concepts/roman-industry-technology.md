# Roman Industry and Technology

The manufacturing and technical capacity of the Roman Empire — pottery, metals, textiles, and the
machines that supported them — and the long-running debate over whether Rome was technologically
**stagnant**. Treated in [[bowman-cah-v11-2000|CAH XI]] Ch. 25 (**Kevin Greene**), who argues that
the period brought **intensification rather than innovation**, and revises the older pessimism
(Bloch, Finley) about Roman technology in light of archaeology.

## Industry within an agrarian economy

Greene begins from the consensus (Hopkins, Duncan-Jones): **agriculture was pre-eminent**, most
people worked the land, and industry "depended on a backward technology and was rarely organised in
large units." Yet the products of industry abound. He stresses that the **industry/agriculture
line is blurred**: most rural industries (garum, iron-working, salt) were run from villa estates
and their workers farmed seasonally; processing wine, oil, or leather was as "industrial" as making
pottery; and aristocrats openly owned commercial sources of profit (brick-clay, the Sestii's
network from agriculture to terra sigillata). Industry was organized in a **multiplicity of small
workshops** — even mass-produced terra sigillata and the imperial mints were subdivided — worked by
**free, freed, and servile labour** (manual labour honoured on tombstones, against aristocratic
disdain), and stamped with makers' names for management, not advertising.

## Pottery: technology transfer

**Terra sigillata** (the glossy red tableware) is the best-documented industry. There were "simply
no technical innovations" in its production, but **"technology transfer" was rapid and wide**:
provincial potters from Britain to Africa learned to make Mediterranean fine wares, successively
**driving the earlier Italian and Gaulish products from the market** (the production centre
migrating Arezzo → south Gaul/La Graufesenque → central Gaul/Lezoux and the German Rhineland;
African red-slip ware dominating the Mediterranean from Hellenistic to Byzantine times). Firing
"tallies" and name-stamps reveal a finely divided labour process (mould-maker, workshop-owner,
bowl-finisher) across small units coordinated by a kiln operator and a merchant (*negotiator
cretarius*).

## Metals and mining: intensification, not innovation

Metal resources, concentrated in the western provinces, became **imperial property**, regulated by
**procurators** but worked through **leases to individuals and *societates*** on a share-cropping
basis (the **Vipasca** mining regulations from Portugal; the *procurator aurariarum* of Dacia). The
**Alburnus Maior wax tablets** show **free contract labour**, not slaves, doing the skilled work,
drawing migrants from across the empire. Roman metallurgy shows **sound practical competence** —
controlled alloy "recipes" matching Pliny's, the gradual debasement of the denarius — but the
techniques (and mine-drainage by adapted Egyptian water-lifting devices) were **scaled up, not
invented**; mining was "intensification rather than innovation" (Laurion ran unchanged from Greek
to Byzantine times).

## Textiles

Long under-studied, textiles were "of enormous importance" (more so than pottery economically).
Greene (with A. H. M. Jones) disposes of the "archaic fantasy" of purely domestic homespun:
**weaving was largely a professional occupation and clothing an object of trade**, involving great
specialization (the Pompeian wool trade). But Jongman's revision shows even Pompeii's textile
workshops were **small** (no single export industry dominating; <10% of the population employed) —
"a welter of small-scale crafts catering for a local elite." Technology was minimal and
**labour-intensive** (the weighted vertical loom; the horizontal loom arriving only in the third
century, for elaborate fabrics).

## The technology debate: "technology of Roman date"

Greene's central argument addresses the famous claim of Roman **technological stagnation**:

- There was **"never really any such thing as Roman technology, but rather technology of Roman
  date"** — incorporating Greek (Alexandrian), and indigenous "barbarian" elements. The stereotype
  of **Greek theory vs. Roman practical application** is misleading, since much "Greek" engineering
  was Alexandrian and Roman in date.
- **Ships** were built shell-first (mortice-and-tenon), as large as any before the fifteenth/
  nineteenth centuries (limited by demand, not technique); Celtic planked and skin boats showed
  Rome's neighbours' technology was not inferior; river craft (the Zwammerdam boats) developed
  pre-Roman traditions.
- **Hydraulics** were sophisticated: the **inverted siphon** (the supposed Roman "failure" to use a
  Greek invention is "the victory of attitudes over evidence"); the full range of **water-lifting
  devices** (force-pumps, bucket-chains, the saqiya gear, chain-pumps with cranked handles) in
  general use from Wales to Egypt.
- **Water-mills** are the key case: against Bloch and Finley's view that Rome failed to apply
  water-power, **archaeology shows water-mills in common use by the third century**, especially in
  the north-western provinces (forts, villas, and towns "normally equipped with powered mills"; the
  Barbegal complex serving a town, not exporting). The barrier to wider medieval-style mechanization
  was **economic and social, not technical**: no power shortage forced it, and — unlike medieval
  lords — "neither individuals nor the state in the early Roman empire conceived of using 'feudal'
  powers" to enforce milling monopolies and suppress hand-mills.

## Significance and contest

Greene reframes the High Empire's industrial economy as one of **large-scale output, organized in
small units, with wide diffusion of competent "appropriate technology"** — revising the pessimistic
orthodoxy of technological stagnation by showing (through archaeology) that devices once thought
medieval were Roman, and that Rome's failure to mechanize further was a matter of **demand and
social structure, not ignorance**. The scale of textile and other production, and the true extent
of water-power, remain debated as evidence accumulates.
