# Persianate Islamic Culture

Persianate Islamic culture refers to the distinctive civilizational synthesis that emerged in the eastern Islamic world from the ninth century onward, in which Persian language, administrative practice, court ceremonial, and literary aesthetics were revived and fused with Islamic religion and law rather than displaced by the Arabic-dominant culture of the early caliphate. Persian (New Persian, written in Arabic script) re-emerged as a prestige literary and administrative language alongside Arabic, which retained primacy as the language of scripture, law, and theology.

The [[samanid-dynasty|Samanid dynasty]] (819–999), ruling from Bukhara and Samarkand as nominal vassals of the Abbasid Caliphate, was the decisive patron of this synthesis: Samanid courts sponsored New Persian poetry (culminating later in Firdawsi's *Shahnameh*), employed Persian-speaking bureaucratic elites drawing on Sasanian administrative models, and presided over a cultural environment in which Persian identity and Islamic piety were not seen as in tension.

This Persianate model — a culturally Persian, religiously Islamic elite synthesis — subsequently spread far beyond Iran through Turkic and Mongol successor states (Ghaznavids, Seljuks, Timurids, and eventually the Mughals of India and the Ottoman court), becoming the dominant courtly idiom across much of Islamic Central and South Asia for nearly a millennium, distinct from the Arabic-centered culture of the Middle East and North Africa.
