## Definition and Scope

Byzantine anti-Latin polemic refers to the body of theological and polemical literature produced by Greek-speaking Orthodox writers from approximately 850 to 1453, directed against the beliefs and practices of the Western (Latin) Church. The literature spans a spectrum from sophisticated theological treatises debated at ecumenical councils to scurrilous popular lists of Latin "errors" and "customs." The defining issues shifted substantially across the centuries, contrary to the assumption that the same concerns — especially the Filioque — remained constant throughout.

Kolbaba's central analytical finding is that this polemic, even during the Crusade era, is almost entirely absent of direct engagement with the Crusades as an institution. There is no reasoned Byzantine refutation of the concept of holy war, no theological discourse against the crusade indulgence or monastic knighthood, and no anti-Latin polemical literature explicitly addressing crusading violence. The Crusades shaped the polemic indirectly and profoundly — above all through their effect on Byzantine identity anxiety and on the practical political situation of the Greek church — but the content of the debates remained theological.

## Key Themes and Arguments

### The Internal-Identity Logic of the Polemic

Kolbaba's most important methodological insight is that anti-Latin polemic became fierce and prolific not when an issue was intrinsically important to the relationship with the West, but when that issue became contested *within* the Byzantine Orthodox world. Debates about Latin practices exploded in intensity precisely when Byzantines themselves were divided. This removes Latins from the center of the picture: the literature explicitly about Latins was implicitly about Byzantines defining themselves against internal dissenters.

This logic explains the sequence of dominant issues:

- **Azymes (unleavened bread)** dominated the eleventh century not because of the 1054 schism per se, but because Byzantine reconquest of Armenia, Syria, and southern Italy had reintegrated populations who used unleavened bread (Armenians) or were associated with it (Jews). Polemic weaponized azymes as a marker distinguishing "orthodox Romans" from heretics inside the empire. When Greeks realized Latins also used unleavened bread, they applied the same internal logic to them.
- **Papal primacy** became the burning issue after 1204, when the Latin occupation forced Greek clergy to take oaths of obedience to the pope or be deposed. It became contested *within* the Greek church (some bishops compromised; others fled) and therefore required fierce polemical defense.
- **The Filioque** became dominant in the Palaiologan period (after c. 1270), when John Bekkos converted to the view that the Latin position was theologically defensible, splitting Byzantine opinion and for the first time requiring systematic internal defense of the Eastern position.

### The 1054 "Schism" and Its Retrospective Magnification

The events of 1054 — the dispute between Cardinal Humbert and Patriarch Michael Keroularios — were largely insignificant at the time. They received little attention within the empire; the first known references to a "schism" date from the early twelfth century. The confrontation was substantially individual (Humbert versus Keroularios) rather than institutional (Rome versus Constantinople). Keroularios' synod condemned the papal legates as impostors, not the pope or Western church generally. Peter of Antioch rebuked Keroularios for his erroneous claim that the pope had been cut from the diptychs since the sixth council. Byzantine writers in 1054 still referred to Westerners as "Romans," not yet as "Latins" or "Franks" in the later hostile sense. The West was not yet perceived as a monolithic threat. Popular anti-Latin sentiment in this period was meager.

### The Azymes Controversy (11th Century)

The azymes dispute, far from originating with the Latin-Byzantine confrontation, arose first in Byzantine polemics against Armenians. The earliest anti-azyme treatises were weapons in debates with Armenian communities reintegrating into the empire after reconquest. Patriarch Peter III of Antioch, the first eleventh-century figure to write against azymes, lived in a city where Armenian-Chalcedonian conflicts had resulted in church burnings. When Byzantine polemicists discovered that Latins also used unleavened bread, they "often seem too preoccupied with contemporary Armenian and Jewish polemics to evaluate properly the Latin position" (Erickson, cited in Kolbaba). The azyme issue was fierce because it was internal — a debate about who counted as orthodox, not simply a debate with outsiders.

### Papal Primacy (12th–13th Centuries)

Before 1204, Byzantine denials of papal primacy were largely "academic" in tone and lacked heat (Gill). The issue was not internally contested: Greeks uniformly rejected it; only Latins asserted it. After 1204, the Latin Empire forced obedience to the pope as a practical condition of keeping one's church; some Greeks compromised, many did not. The resulting internal split made the issue urgent and generated fierce polemic. Lists of Latin errors, the most popular and virulent genre, only began mentioning papal primacy after 1204 (first appearance: Constantine Stilbes' list, compiled after 1204).

### The Filioque (Palaiologan Period, after c. 1270)

About 70 percent of all anti-Latin polemical works in the Palaiologan period addressed the procession of the Holy Spirit; roughly half of those explicitly concerned the Filioque. Barlaam the Calabrian wrote twenty-one anti-Latin treatises, fifteen concerning Trinitarian relations and ten explicitly about the procession. This dominance is explained by the conversion of John Bekkos (patriarch 1275–82) to the view that the Latin position was compatible with the Greek fathers. For the first time Byzantines were divided on the Filioque, requiring sustained internal defense. The charge of Latin sophistication also shifted the tone from condescension to defensiveness: Byzantine theologians who previously condescended to "barbarian" Latins now feared the Latins had surpassed them.

### Barlaam's 1339 Speech to Pope Benedict XII

The speech crystallizes Kolbaba's paradox. Emperor Andronikos III sent the bilingual Calabrian monk Barlaam to Avignon to seek military aid and discuss reunion. To the pope's demand that reunion precede military assistance, Barlaam replied: "It is not so much difference in dogma that alienates the hearts of the Greeks from you, as the hatred that has entered their souls against the Latins, because of the many great evils that at different times the Greeks have suffered at the hands of Latins." Barlaam admitted the emperor desired union but had to pursue it secretly because popular hostility — rooted in the violence of the Crusades, especially 1204 — made open negotiation politically impossible. The speech thus identifies the Crusades as the cause of the passional barrier to reunion while confirming that the formal doctrinal agenda (reunion negotiations, Filioque treatises) remained structured around theological categories, not military grievance.

### Tone and Changing Self-Perception

The tone of anti-Latin writing evolved from condescending superiority to anxious defensiveness:

- **9th–11th centuries**: Photios and his contemporaries treated Western errors as products of ignorance and lack of education; their epithets borrowed the classical heresiological vocabulary (arrogance, rashness, insolence) rather than ethnic or racial categories. Latins were "rustic cousins," barbarians capable of being corrected by more learned Easterners.
- **12th century**: Growing awareness of Latin intellectual sophistication; borders permeable (the controversy under Manuel I over "The Father is greater than I" was driven by ideas flowing in both directions). The tone of condescension was "shaken" when Alexios I was convinced by Latin arguments in a 1112 debate.
- **After 1204**: Popular polemic exploded (lists of errors including what Latins ate and wore, not just theology); simultaneously, Palaiologan intellectuals began to admire or even adopt Latin learning. At the Council of Florence (1438–39), the Greek delegation was defensive, not condescending: "The Latins are sophists."

## Development Over Time

| Period | Dominant Issue | Driver |
|---|---|---|
| 850–1054 | Not primarily Latin-focused (iconoclasm; internal identity) | Internal Byzantine disputes; limited Latin contact |
| 1054–1095 | Azymes | Internal dispute over Armenian/Jewish identity markers applied to Latins |
| 1095–1204 | Azymes; growing emphasis on papal primacy | Crusader installation of Latin patriarchs; Filioque gaining but not dominant |
| 1204–1261 | Papal primacy (dominant) | Latin Empire forces oath of obedience; Greek church internally split |
| 1261–1350 | Filioque (dominant) | Bekkos' conversion splits Byzantine opinion; Palaiologan intellectual crisis |

## Scholarly Significance

Kolbaba's essay challenges two entrenched assumptions:

1. **That the Filioque was always the central issue**: The survey of extant texts shows that azymes and papal primacy were each dominant in their respective centuries; the Filioque achieved dominance only in the Palaiologan period, and only because it became internally contested.

2. **That the Crusades directly generated anti-Latin religious polemic**: The survey finds no Byzantine theological literature refuting holy war, the crusade indulgence, or monastic knighthood. Anti-Latin polemic increased in quantity and virulence during the Crusade period but addressed theological topics (azymes, Filioque, primacy). The Crusades operated as a radicalizing background condition and a source of hatred and distrust, not as explicit polemical targets.

The essay also demonstrates that Byzantine anti-Latin attitudes were shaped primarily by internal Byzantine debates about identity — who counted as "orthodox" and "Roman" — rather than by external Latin provocation alone.
