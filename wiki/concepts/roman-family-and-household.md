# The Roman Family and Household

The structure of the Roman **family and household** — its vocabulary and ideals, marriage, and the
relations of fathers, mothers, and children — at the core of daily life and the transmission of
property and rank. Treated in [[bowman-cah-v11-2000|CAH XI]] Ch. 29 (**Richard Saller**), who
stresses the **gap between the law's patriarchal rules and actual family practice**.

## Family vocabulary and the nuclear core

Latin had **no word for the nuclear family**. ***Familia*** meant variously the property, the
*paterfamilias* and those in his *potestas*, the agnatic lineage (≈ *gens*), or — most commonly —
the **slave staff**; the wife in a marriage *without manus* was **not in her husband's familia**.
***Domus*** (the household, including coresident kin, slaves, and dependants) became the preferred
focus for evaluating a man's background under the Principate — a shift from agnatic *familia/nomen*
(Cicero) to the wider *domus* of blood and marriage ties (Pliny), reflecting the rise of **new
families** who made their way through strategic marriage. For the core family Romans simply said
*uxor liberique*. At the centre of family ideals stood ***pietas*** — not mere filial obedience but
the **reciprocal bond of affectionate duty** owed by fathers to children as much as children to
fathers, making the Roman family "less hierarchical... than is sometimes suggested."

## The simple household, not the extended family

Saller dismantles the image of the Roman **extended/multiple-family household**. Latin literature
holds up **no three-generation ideal** (unlike Chinese culture); the tensions of joint households
are absent from the sources; commemoration rarely links grandfather and grandchild or adult
brothers. Above all, **demography forbids it**: with short life expectancy and late male marriage,
only ~1 Roman in 10 was born while his paternal grandfather lived. **The simple (nuclear) family
formed the core of the Roman household.**

## Marriage in law and practice

A *matrimonium iustum* was made simply by a citizen man and woman living together with **marital
intent** (*affectio maritalis*) — the state certifying little but eligibility. The dominant form
was marriage **without manus**: the wife stayed in her father's *familia*, and her **non-dotal
property remained her own**, protected by jurists (no major gifts between spouses; the *SC
Velleianum*). **Divorce** was easy and unilateral ("sending a messenger"), the dowry returned. This
made the Roman wife, in law, "a rather odd unit" — **independent in property** and free to leave.

But Saller insists the **law was not behaviour**:

- A wide **age-gap** (men first marrying in their late twenties, women in their late teens; wider
  still with remarriage) fostered a **paternalistic** husband–wife relationship (Pliny and his
  young Calpurnia).
- The cultural **monopoly of public authority by men** subordinated wives despite the law; husbands
  often managed wives' estates; women's legal property independence was in practice "forfeited" by
  some.
- Many in Italy and most beyond were **ineligible for *matrimonium iustum*** through lack of
  citizenship, living instead in ***contubernium* or *concubinatus*** — honourable, monogamous
  arrangements, not moral decline.

On the **Christianity debate**, Saller takes a measured line: pagan philosophers (Musonius Rufus,
Plutarch) anticipated some Christian marital precepts (criticizing the sexual double standard,
exposure), but their reach was tiny and their behaviour unchanged (Marcus Aurelius took a concubine
after his wife's death); the conjugal ideal of affection was **traditional, not an invention** —
and the real broad transformation came with the **Church's "democracy of sexual shame"** preached
to all converts.

## Fathers, mothers, and children

The law gave the ***paterfamilias* lifelong *patria potestas*** — physical coercion (even, in
theory, life and death), a **monopoly of property ownership** (children of any age owning nothing,
holding only a *peculium*), and control over children's marriages — prompting Gaius' boast that "no
other men have such power over their children as we have." But Saller shows this was **mitigated in
practice**:

- **Demography** gutted it: with a 35–40-year generation gap, most Romans **lost their fathers
  before adulthood** — by age 25 only a third still had a living father, so most senators needed
  **outside patrons**, not fathers, to advance.
- The lethal power was **not used against adult children** after Augustus (social pressure against
  it was severe), though fathers did **expose newborns**.
- Romans **distinguished son from slave**: the whip, the badge of servility, was advised **against**
  for freeborn children (Quintilian, ps.-Plutarch); paternal authority was conceived as
  **beneficent and obeyed**, not coercive like the master's.
- Among the **poor**, fathers gained little leverage (income came from labour; adult sons supported
  themselves). Among the **propertied**, the threat of disinheritance was real but constrained
  (custom and the *querela inofficiosi testamenti* pressing fathers to leave the patrimony to *sui
  heredes*).
- Crucially, the **mother** — independent in property, owing her children moral responsibility —
  **complicated and could break paternal power** (the Regulus case, where a mother's bequest forced
  her son's emancipation). The dynamics of the Roman family were therefore **not simply
  patriarchal**.

## Significance and contest

Saller's family is the **social and economic foundation** of the Roman world — the unit through
which property, rank, and *pietas* passed — but one whose **legal patriarchy was heavily softened
in practice** by demography, custom, female property, and affection. His revision (against the
older image of the all-powerful *paterfamilias* and of moral decline) reshaped Roman family
history, though the frequency of divorce, the impact of Christianity, and the reality behind the
legal rules remain debated.
