## Definition

"Two Laws Together" is both the title of a painting by Aboriginal Australian artists Wen ten Rubuntja and Chips Mackinolty and, as employed analytically by Karen Nero, a conceptual frame for the coexistence and necessary integration of indigenous and introduced (Western statutory) legal and environmental knowledge systems in the contemporary Pacific. Nero uses the painting and its title as the closing analytical statement of Chapter 11 of the *Cambridge History of the Pacific Islanders* — positioning legal and epistemological pluralism as the deepest challenge facing Pacific resource management.

The painting's formal structure enacts the concept: "The Western half of the landscape is a straightforward presentation of the land in European perspective: in the background is an easily recognisable representation of a mountain, in the foreground a large gum tree. To one who is not trained to see within Aboriginal perspectives, the Aboriginal half appears to be simply geometric forms without meaning. It takes special training even to begin to read the circles to represent waterholes or mountains, the arcs as people, the wavy lines as tracks laid down by ancestors during the Dreaming." The painting does not resolve these two systems into one; it places them in the same frame, making visible both the depth of their difference and the necessity of their coexistence.

## Scope and Application

Nero uses the concept broadly across Pacific contexts, not limited to Aboriginal Australia:

**Fisheries**: Indigenous marine conservation practices "involve concepts of property, laws and personal relationships qualitatively different from Western practices." Management practices used in the past — "restricting seasons, size, gear, species taken, and protecting breeding habitats — coincide well with Western techniques," but their social infrastructure (chiefly taboos, kinship-based enforcement) has been disrupted. The challenge is "integrating modern and traditional knowledge" under conditions where "many resources previously managed have been over-exploited."

**Land and mineral rights**: Every major mining case in PNG and Irian Jaya turns on the collision between statutory property law (minerals vest in the state; Indonesian constitution identifies minerals as state property) and customary claims (Nasioi, Amungme, Wopkaimin attachment to specific landscapes as sacred). The Kwajalein landowners' re-occupation of their island to halt missile tests; the Lihir islanders negotiating for years before "conceding" that the PNG government was "entitled to share 'their' royalties" — these enact two-laws-together in legal negotiation.

**Watershed management**: The Pohnpei case is Nero's most concrete positive example. A 1987 watershed protection law failed because "local communities had been insufficiently involved and rejected regulations which failed to recognise traditional practices." The subsequent Watershed Steering Committee — combining local and state government agencies, the College of Micronesia, the Department of Agriculture, and Nature Conservancy, plus a 1991 pilot project co-initiated by four Kitti villages — succeeded because it recognised "that the forest is not considered common property but has been regulated by traditional authorities" and that "communities have a greater stake than government managers."

**Semantic incommensurability**: The concept also operates at the level of economic language. Tahitian economic terms map onto categorically different logics: tarahu ("employ") connotes borrowing-with-reciprocal-payment; aufaura'a 'avae (wages) derives from tribute paid to chiefs. "No indigenous concept approximates capital." The legal/economic systems embedded in Western contractual frameworks impose meanings that Island peoples do not share.

## Distinction from Related Concepts

"Two Laws Together" is related to but distinct from:
- **Legal pluralism** (the academic study of coexisting legal orders): Nero's use is not academic-formal; it is analytical-historical, focused on practical management outcomes.
- **Invented tradition / cultural essentialism**: Nero explicitly warns against romanticising indigenous practices: "indigenous practices should not be romanticised: they did not extend to all resources currently exploited, nor do the wider social relationships operate in the ways that they once did." The concept is not nostalgic.
- **Syncretism**: "Two Laws Together" preserves the distinctness of each system rather than merging them into a hybrid. Rubuntja/Mackinolty's painting keeps the two halves of the landscape formally separate while placing them in a single frame.

## Significance in the Wiki

As Nero closes the chapter: "The past few decades have marked the beginnings of communications across paradigms of resource management in pursuit of a common goal, for it is clear that environmental degradation is the most critical issue facing the Islands. International political and economic considerations allowed a major restructuring of resource definition in the Law of the Sea negotiations in the 1970s; today metropolitan powers at times join international conservation organisations and indigenous peoples in attempts to mediate solutions, but the multiplicity of interests makes it difficult to bridge the gulfs of understanding."

## Limitations and Critiques

Nero does not develop "two laws together" as a formal theory; it is an evocative closing frame. Its extension from Aboriginal Australian painting to Pacific-wide resource management involves a geographic generalisation the source does not fully substantiate. The concept risks implying equal status for both legal systems when, in practice, statutory law backed by state coercive power routinely overrides customary claims — most starkly in Irian Jaya, where Indonesian constitutional and agrarian law stripped the Amungme of compensation rights. "Two laws together" describes an aspiration and an analytical necessity more than a political reality.
