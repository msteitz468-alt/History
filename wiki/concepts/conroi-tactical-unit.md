# Conroi (Medieval Tactical Unit)

The *conroi* (also conroten, conrois; related to batailles/bataelgen/battaglie and bannières/banieren) was the basic small tactical unit of armoured cavalry in high medieval western European armies. It was commanded by its lord and formed the building block for larger formations (*batailles*).

## Definition and Scope

A *conroi* was a close-knit group of knights (and often their immediate retinue) who rode and fought together under a single leader. They advanced and charged as a coherent body. Larger "batailles" or "eschieles" were formed by grouping multiple *conrois*.

The term is Germanic in origin (scara lineage) and entered the lingua romana between the 6th and 9th centuries, indicating that such troop divisions pre-dated the classic high-medieval knight.

## Characteristics from Contemporary Sources

- **Leadership**: Often led by the lord at the head; sometimes the standard-bearer went in front with the banner.
- **Formation density**: Explicitly close-packed. Ambroise (Estoire) and the Itinerarium: men stood so close "that an apple thrown into the air could not fall without hitting a horse or a man." Guiart (1304): ranks of knights "so closely packed that a glove could not fall between them."
- **Cohesion**: Fought "very much jammed up together" when attacking in close formation.

- **Cohesion**: Fought "very much jammed up together" when attacking in close formation.
- **Tactical role**: Enabled mutual support, reduced individual risk, allowed effective shock charges, and prevented enemy penetration. Commanders could manoeuvre small units more flexibly than a single mass.

## Training and Cohesion

Tournaments served as realistic training for conroi tactics. Knights assembled in conrois under their lords or regional groups and practiced orderly advances and charges. Individual breaking of ranks ("poindre as premiers de la rote") was strongly condemned.

> "the separate individual charge made by knights who abandoned the protective ranks of the conroi in order to rush ahead into battle, for in so doing they destroyed the cohesion of the unit. If on the other hand they attacked in close order, there was no risk of the enemy breaking through."

Dense formation repeatedly praised: "They were serried in ranks / As if they were people forged in iron..." ; "the barons are so closely packed as they advance / That if you throw a glove on their helmets / It would not fall to ground within a mile."

## Larger Formations (Batailles)

Conrois combined into *batailles*. The royal French army at Cassel (1328) deployed in ten *batailles* with 177–196 banners. In the Latin Empire under Henry of Constantinople, the army was divided into fifteen *battles* of ~20 knights each (emperor's own ~50), with attacks delivered in staggered lines.

## Evidence from France (Ch. 10) on Tactical Integration (Range 03)

"Within these larger unities, knights seem to have been ordered in much smaller units of between 10 and 40, called conroi. In English armies of the late twelfth and thirteenth centuries, the knight–banneret seems to have been a minor commander with a force upwards of 13 knights, and was usually distinguished by carrying a square banner rather than a pennant. There were milites bannerii in the army with which Philip III attacked Foix in 1271."

"these were not standardized units like modern sections, platoons and companies, and 'battles' varied enormously in size, being tactical units rather than organizational entities. Moreover, what little evidence we have shows that even within the smallest units there was little continuity of service in most cases. Moreover, considerations of rank disrupted these units as elements in a command structure."

This reinforces the ad hoc, retinue-based, rank-sensitive nature of high-medieval tactical organisation. Conroi served as the building blocks within acies/batailles/schiera but did not create standing formations or overcome the social fragmentation of armies. ## Historiographical Importance

Verbruggen uses the term and its attestations (vernacular poetry, chronicles) to demonstrate that medieval cavalry fought in organised small units with cohesion, not purely as individual heroes (correcting aspects of Delbrück and Oman). The conroi is the practical answer to the problem of fear, honour culture, and the need for mutual support in the charge. Banners and devices (liveries) identified units and fostered cohesion (e.g., Otto recognised by his conroi's device at Bouvines).

Earlier historians (Delbrück, Oman, Lot) often reduced knightly combat to individual duels by overlooking phrases such as *cum suis*, *avec sa gent*, *cum sua acie*. Careful reading shows that named nobles usually fought at the head of their *conroi*, not alone. Apparent "individual encounters" frequently involved entire small units.

The *conroi* system explains how medieval commanders could exercise meaningful control before contact and why reserves (a compact group falling on a disorganised enemy) were so decisive. Once committed to hand-to-hand fighting, withdrawal was extremely difficult; influence came mainly through uncommitted reserves.

## Relation to Carolingian Predecessors and Later Practice

Direct terminological and functional descendant of the Carolingian *scara*. The same principles of small-unit cohesion under a lord, close-order charge, and formation from basic building blocks persisted into the 14th century (Courtrai, Bannockburn era).

## Sources and Grounding

Primary evidence drawn from vernacular chronicles and poetry (Ambroise, Guiart, Itinerarium Latin parallel, Galbert, etc.). Verbruggen stresses that only vernacular gives clear technical vocabulary; Latin chroniclers use shifting classical terms (ordines, turmae, agmina, acies) that blur distinctions.
