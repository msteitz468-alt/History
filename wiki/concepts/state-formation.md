# State Formation

The process by which dispersed, relatively egalitarian societies are consolidated
into territorial polities with institutionalized hierarchy, specialist personnel
(scribes, priests, soldiers), and centralized control over surplus. It is one of the
central problems of the comparative study of early civilizations and the necessary
backdrop to [[concepts/divine-kingship|divine kingship]].

## Kemp's Account (Egypt)

Barry Kemp, in [[sources/kemp-ancient-egypt-anatomy-2018|*Anatomy of a Civilization*]],
frames Egyptian state formation as the entrenchment of **dominance hierarchy**, balanced
by **deference**, and crucially mediated by the human "transcendental" imagination, which
converts a dominant individual into the abstract office of *kingship* and an
agglomeration of people into a *nation*. On his account the decisive instrument is not
coercion alone but myth, symbol and institution. He notes Egypt's hierarchies emerged
markedly later than in the Near East — by perhaps four thousand years — yet then
crystallized with exceptional speed into the unified state around 3100 BCE.

Kemp models the predynastic process as a **competitive game** of escalating feedback: rough
initial equality among proto-states is upset by small advantages that "reinforce
themselves," driving "a trajectory towards a critical point where one player has accumulated
sufficient assets to outweigh the threats posed by the others" — one winner engulfing the
rest. On recent radiocarbon dates the whole sequence (Nagada I–IIIA) was accomplished in
"six or seven centuries (c. 3750–3085 BCE)," and the First Dynasty "began as a state
territorially as large as most that were to occupy the lower Nile valley until modern
times" — with "no long process of growth from a spread of city-states" as in Mesopotamia.
Kemp rejects the **hydraulic hypothesis** (civilization arising from collective irrigation):
"in the case of ancient Egypt one can state that this was not so." Once dominance hierarchy
began it entrenched itself through delegated specialists — "scribes, priests, soldiers."
Even the **First Intermediate Period** he re-reads not as mere collapse but as a release of
local energy and competing patriarchal centres — state-formation logic re-running. Crucially
the resulting **myth of unity** left Egyptians "incapable of visualizing the polycentric
pattern of their own early political growth" (see [[events/unification-of-egypt-3100bce]],
[[concepts/egyptian-creation-myth-and-cosmogony]]).

## Limitations and Critiques

Neo-evolutionist "band–tribe–chiefdom–state" typologies have been heavily criticized for
teleology; recent work (Graeber & Wengrow) questions whether early states were as
uniform or as inevitable as the classic models imply.
