## Definition and Scope

This concept page documents al-Ghazālī's systematic critique, as developed in Discussions 2–12 of the *[[tahafut-al-falasifa|Tahāfut al-Falāsifa]]*, of the Avicennan-Farabian philosophers' account of God — specifically their claims about the world's everlastingness after its supposed eternity, God's status as "Maker," the demonstrability of divine unity, the inadmissibility of real divine attributes, divine simplicity (genus/differentia impossibility), and whether God can know Himself or others. Al-Ghazālī works as a prosecutorial critic rather than a constructive theologian: he deploys the philosophers' own argumentative standards to show that none of their conclusions has been demonstratively proved.

The source is [[ghazali-incoherence-of-philosophers]] (Michael Marmura's 2000 translation, used throughout). All quotations follow Marmura's English rendering. The Avicennan position being attacked draws on [[ibn-sina|Ibn Sīnā's]] system; [[al-farabi|al-Fārābī]] is occasionally cited as well.

This page covers Discussions 2–12. Discussion 13 (God's knowledge of particulars) is addressed separately as part of Agent C's scope.

## Discussion 2: The World's Post-Eternity — Can It Be Annihilated?

### The Philosophers' Argument

The philosophers contend that the world, even granting (counterfactually, from their perspective) that it was temporally originated, cannot be annihilated. Their argument draws on the hylomorphic account of matter (*hyle*): substances are self-subsisting, and the nonexistence of a self-subsisting thing after its existence is inconceivable, regardless of whether it was originally pre-eternal or originated. Matter does not perish — it only changes forms. Even water "destroyed" by fire persists as vapor; the *hyle* simply exchanges the form of water for the form of air.

Four Muslim theological positions on annihilation are surveyed and found philosophically deficient by the philosophers:

1. **Position 1**: annihilation is an existent God creates in His own essence — falsified because it makes the Eternal a receptacle for temporal happenings.
2. **Position 2** (unnamed group): accidents cease of themselves; substances endure through divine-created endurance — falsified because it generates an infinite regress (the endurance must itself be made to endure).
3. **Position 3** (Ashʿarites): accidents cannot endure; substances are annihilated when God ceases to create motion, rest, combination, and separation in them.
4. **Position 4** (second Ashʿarite group): same, with slight variation on the mechanism.

The conclusion: none of these accounts succeeds, so there is no coherent way to uphold the possibility of annihilation — and therefore the world, even if created, cannot cease to exist.

### Al-Ghazālī's Response

Al-Ghazālī's response here is notably restrained. He does not defend any of the four Muslim positions in detail but challenges the philosophers on the conceptual status of nonexistence. His key move:

> "With what [argument] would you deny someone who says: 'The bringing about of existence and annihilation obtains through the will of the one endowed with power. Thus, if God wills, He brings about existence; and, if He wills, He annihilates. This is the meaning of His being powerful in the [most] perfect [sense]'?" (Discussion 2, §26)

On nonexistence as a genuine occurrence: the philosophers object that "nonexistence is not a thing; how did it proceed from Him?" Al-Ghazālī replies that nonexistence can be apprehended by the mind as an occurrence even if it is not an entity — and that its occurrence can therefore stand in a relation to the power of God, just as its occurrence in accidents is universally acknowledged by both parties. He presses this with a dilemma about whether the privation of blackness (which follows from the occurrence of whiteness) is eternal, temporal, or neither — forcing the philosophers to acknowledge that at least some privations are intelligible occurrences. His conclusion: so long as an eternal will can intelligibly produce temporal events, there is no bar in principle to God's producing annihilation.

The deeper strategic point: the philosophers have given no *demonstration* that annihilation is impossible — they have only shown that the four positions on offer are unsatisfactory. The gap between "these accounts are inadequate" and "annihilation is impossible" is precisely the space al-Ghazālī pries open.

## Discussion 3: Is God a Real "Maker" of the World?

### The Core Charge

Al-Ghazālī argues that the philosophers' use of the term "maker" (*fāʿil*, agent/enactor) for God is *metaphorical*, not real — on the philosophers' own principles. His argument proceeds on three fronts:

**Front 1 — Agency requires will.**
"'Agent' is an expression [referring] to one from whom the act proceeds, together with the will to act by way of choice and the knowledge of what is willed." (Discussion 3, §4) But on Avicennan principles, the world flows from God as light from the sun — necessarily, by compulsion, without choice. The sun is not an agent in the real sense; neither is the Avicennan God. The philosophers admit God has "no attribute at all" and that whatever proceeds from Him proceeds "by compulsory necessity." Real agency, al-Ghazālī argues, is confined to willing beings; calling the lamp an agent of light or the wall an agent of shadow is "ventur[ing] excessively into metaphor."

**Front 2 — An act must be temporally originated.**
The world, for the philosophers, is eternal. But "action" means bringing something from nonexistence to existence through creation. What has always existed cannot be "brought into existence." Therefore the world, on Avicennan grounds, cannot be an act of God in any real sense.

**Front 3 — From the one, only one can proceed (the unity axiom).**
On Avicennan principles, "from the One only one proceeds." But the world is composed of vastly varied things. If only one thing can proceed from the absolutely simple First Principle, how does the world's multiplicity arise? The philosophers answer through the [[emanationism-avicennan|emanationist hierarchy]]: a chain of intellects, each generating an intellect, a soul, and a sphere below it — multiplicity arising through mediation. Al-Ghazālī attacks this as incoherent (see Discussion 3's critique of the triad emanation model) and argues that even on their own terms the mechanics do not work: the origin of genuine composition in the hylomorphic framework is never explained.

### Al-Ghazālī's Verdict

The philosophers have publicly used religious vocabulary — "God is the Maker of the world" — but have privately voided that vocabulary of its meaning:

> "Our sole purpose in this question is to show that you have used these terms as an affectation, without [the proper] ascertaining of their real meaning; that God, according to you, is not an agent in the real sense nor the world His act in a real sense; and that the application of such a term on your part is metaphorical, having no basis in reality." (Discussion 3, §33)

Al-Ghazālī's own response to the implicit demand "what do *you* say?": he refuses to commit to a philosophical account of how God produces plurality, insisting instead that the prophets have revealed what must be accepted and that investigating "quality, quantity, and quiddity" of the divine act is "presumption and a coveting of what is unattainable" (Discussion 3, §79). Reason cannot render what the prophets report impossible; that is sufficient.

## Discussion 4: Can the Philosophers Demonstrate That God Exists?

### The Problem

Al-Ghazālī charges that the philosophers cannot consistently demonstrate the existence of a First Cause (their "First Principle"), because they allow an actual infinity of past events — and the standard argument from the impossibility of an infinite regress of causes therefore fails on their own principles.

The standard cosmological argument they offer:

> The world either has a cause or does not. If it does, the cause either has a cause or does not. This regress must terminate; the terminus is the uncaused First Principle. (Discussion 4, §5)

Al-Ghazālī's objection: this argument requires the impossibility of an infinite causal regress. But the philosophers themselves affirm that past time has no beginning — meaning past events form an actual infinity. If an actual infinity of past events is possible, why not an actual infinity of past causes? The philosophers cannot block infinite causal regress using an argument they cannot apply to infinite temporal regress without inconsistency.

The human-soul problem: Avicenna, al-Fārābī, and "the exacting philosophers" hold that individual human souls are self-subsisting substances that survive death. Since the species *homo sapiens* has existed through an infinite past (on the philosophers' account), and since souls do not perish, there exist — at every moment of the philosophers' eternal universe — an actually infinite number of extant individual souls. These souls stand in relations of temporal precedence to one another. This is precisely the kind of ordered infinite series that the philosophers claim is impossible for causes. Their attempts to distinguish soul-series from causal series are, al-Ghazālī argues, "sheer arbitrariness" (Discussion 4, §14).

Conclusion: whoever holds to the eternity of the world "has no way of reaching [the point] of affirming the First Principle, for this [very] difficulty" (Discussion 4, §20).

## Discussion 5: Can the Philosophers Demonstrate Divine Unity?

### The Two Arguments and Their Failures

The philosophers offer two proofs that God must be unique (there cannot be two Necessary Existents):

**Proof 1**: If there were two necessary existents, the species "necessary of existence" would be shared by both, while each differs from the other individually. This implies that each is composed of genus + differentia — composite beings require a composer — so neither is truly the Necessary Existent.

Al-Ghazālī's objection: this proof assumes the impossibility of divine composition and multiplicity from the outset. The demonstration is circular. Proof of the unity of the First Principle would require first demonstrating that any uncaused, eternal being must be absolutely simple; that has not been shown.

**Proof 2**: If two existents are each "necessary," one cannot depend on the other. Yet one would need a differentia that the other lacks, and any differentia means the thing-with-the-differentia needs the differentia — it is therefore "possible" (dependent), not "necessary."

Al-Ghazālī's objection: the terms "necessary" and "possible" are being used equivocally. If "necessary" means only "having no efficient cause," then an eternal being with eternal attributes could satisfy this condition, even if the attributes subsist *in* the essence (receptive cause) without having an efficient cause. No demonstration has ruled out the possibility of two eternal uncaused beings whose differentiation is itself eternally just how things are.

He concludes that the argument for divine unity rests on an assumed identification of "Necessary Existent" with "absolutely simple, uncomposed being" — which is precisely the thing needing demonstration, not a premise available to them for free.

## Discussion 6: The Denial of Real Divine Attributes

### The Philosophers' Position

Avicennan philosophers (along with the Muʿtazila) deny that God has *real* attributes additional to His essence — i.e., that God's knowledge, power, and will are genuine realities distinct from (even if eternally co-present with) His essence. Their argument: if knowledge and essence are two distinct things, one must depend on the other. Either both exist independently (making them both necessary existents — contradicting unity), or each depends on the other (neither is necessary), or one depends on the other and is therefore an effect. In any case, an attribute separate from the essence compromises the Necessary Existent.

The philosophers therefore collapse all divine "attributes" into the essence itself:

> "Will would be nothing other than Power itself, Power nothing other than Knowledge itself, Knowledge nothing other than the Essence itself. All, then, reduces to the Essence itself." (Discussion 5, §27)

### Al-Ghazālī's Response

Al-Ghazālī targets the argument's core premise — that a receptive relation between attribute and essence entails the attribute's being an "effect" requiring an efficient cause. He distinguishes:

- **Efficient cause**: what brings a thing into existence from without
- **Receptive cause**: the substrate in which a quality inheres

Our knowledge subsists in our essence (our essence is its receptacle), but our essence does not *efficiently* cause our knowledge. Similarly, God's attributes could co-eternally subsist in His essence without His essence being their efficient cause:

> "If you mean that the essence is a receptacle and that the attribute does not subsist by itself without a receptacle, this is conceded. But why should this be impossible? For it to be referred to as 'ancillary,' as 'accidental,' as an 'effect'... does not change the meaning, if the meaning is nothing other than its being subsistent in the essence in the way attributes subsist in the things that have attributes." (Discussion 6, §16)

The deeper move: demonstration only shows that the regress of efficient causes must terminate. It does not prove that the terminus must be an absolutely attribute-less simple being. The philosophers have smuggled in an extra premise — the absolute simplicity of the First Principle — that no argument they offer actually establishes.

Al-Ghazālī also attacks the philosophers who deny *all* attributes (including self-knowledge) as reducing God to something less noble than humans, angels, and even beasts:

> "Sufficient is the shame on you with this doctrine... angel, man, and every one among the rational beings knows himself and his principle, and knows others, whereas the First knows only Himself. He is thus deficient in relation to individual people." (Discussion 6, §48)

This becomes the launching point for Discussions 11 and 12.

## Discussion 7: Divine Simplicity — No Genus or Differentia

The philosophers argue that the First Principle cannot share a *genus* with anything else and cannot be differentiated from anything by a *differentia*, because genus-differentia composition implies compositeness, and compositeness requires a composer (hence is incompatible with being the uncaused First). The First therefore has no definition and falls outside the entire Aristotelian categorial framework.

Al-Ghazālī's response here is briefer and more structural: the philosophers' denial of divine genus-differentia composition rests on exactly the same undemonstrated premise as their denial of divine attributes — namely, that the First Principle must be absolutely free of any form of composition or multiplicity. "There is no proof for [this]. There thus remains... [the question:] is it in what respect impossible for the chain of effects to terminate with two causes... [that] differ in meaning, as there is between redness and heat in the same place?" (Discussion 7, §11). The denial of two uncaused causes (two gods) has not been demonstrated.

He further notes that the philosophers' whole structure — denial of attributes, denial of unity-claims, denial of generic composition — forms a chain in which each step presupposes the others. The edifice is "a structure that is weak in its standing, similar to a spider's [web]."

## Discussions 8–10: Is the First Not a Body? Can the World Be Shown to Have a Maker?

### Discussion 8 (God Not a Body)

The philosophers argue that God cannot be a body, because every body is composite (form + matter), and composite things require a composer. Al-Ghazālī's response is consonant with Discussions 6–7: the argument against divine corporeality relies on the claim that bodies are temporally originated (because never devoid of temporal accidents) and that whoever accepts temporal origination of bodies needs a maker. But the argument works *only* for someone who accepts temporal origination:

> "From this it becomes clear that whoever does not believe in the temporal origination of bodies is fundamentally incapable of erecting a proof that the First is not a body." (Discussion 9, §15)

This is a key structural point: the philosophers' arguments against materialism presuppose createdness, which they deny.

### Discussion 10 (The World Has a Maker)

The same logic applies to the existence of a Maker at all. The materialists' doctrine — that the world's bodies are eternal, uncaused, and that only forms and accidents have causes — is not refutable on the philosophers' own principles:

> "Whoever reflects on what we have said will know the inability of the one who believes in the eternity of bodies to claim for them a cause; and the necessary consequence for him is materialism and atheism, as openly declared by a group. For these [latter] are the ones who [in reality] have fulfilled the requirements of the speculation of [the philosophers]." (Discussion 10, §4)

Al-Ghazālī does not say the philosophers are materialists — he says that their philosophical commitments (eternity of bodies, necessary emanation) provide no principled obstacle to materialism. They have no ground to stand on against those among them who openly draw that conclusion.

## Discussion 11: God's Knowledge of Others

### The Philosopher's Dilemma

Those philosophers (led by Avicenna) who hold that God knows things other than Himself through "universal knowledge" have no consistent demonstration for this position. Al-Ghazālī attacks both of Avicenna's arguments:

**Argument 1**: The First is not in matter; whatever is not in matter is pure intellect; a pure intellect has all intelligibles "laid bare to it."

Al-Ghazālī's response: the conclusion is illicitly embedded in the premises. "If you mean by [pure intellect] that which apprehends intellectually the rest of things, this would be the very thing sought after and the point at dispute." The syllogism is of the form "if p then q; not-p; therefore not-q" — a formal fallacy (denying the antecedent). Not being in matter may be *a* condition for self-knowledge, but the philosophers have not shown it is the *only* barrier.

**Argument 2**: God's act entails His knowledge of the act; since the world is His act (by emanation), He must know it.

Al-Ghazālī's response: knowledge of the act follows only for *voluntary* acts, not for natural necessity. "According to you [philosophers], God enacted the world by way of necessity from His essence, by nature and compulsion, not by way of will and choice... And just as the sun has no power to stop light and fire [has no power] to stop heating, the First has no power to stop His acts... This mode, [even] if metaphorically named an 'act,' does not at all entail knowledge for the agent." (Discussion 11, §14)

Even granting that some knowledge is entailed, it would cover only the first effect (one simple intellect directly emanated from God) — not the entire causal chain below it.

## Discussion 12: God's Knowledge of Himself

Al-Ghazālī presses the critique further: the philosophers cannot even demonstrate that God knows *Himself*, once they deny will and temporal origination. Their argument (from the First's not being in matter) was shown to be fallacious in Discussion 11. Their alternative argument — "the world divides into living and dead; the First is most noble; therefore He is living; every living being is aware of itself" — is shown to be an arbitrary assertion:

> "Why is it impossible that there should proceed from that which does not know itself that which knows itself, either through many intermediaries or without an intermediary? If that which renders this impossible is [the consequence] that the effect becomes nobler than the cause, why should it be impossible for the effect to be nobler than the cause? This is not [self-evident] to the natural intelligence." (Discussion 12, §7)

Al-Ghazālī has noted that Avicenna himself concedes that God does not know temporal particulars (that would require change in His essence). But if knowledge of particulars can be denied without deficiency, the philosophers have no principled basis for insisting that knowledge of universals or self-knowledge is required for divine perfection.

Final verdict: "it is by necessity that they are compelled to deny also His knowledge of Himself, since nothing gives evidence for this except will, and nothing proves will except the world's temporal origination." (Discussion 12, §8). The entire philosophical theology collapses into circularity once temporal origination and genuine divine will are denied.

## Al-Ghazālī's Underlying Strategy Across Discussions 2–12

Several structural moves recur throughout this section of the *Tahāfut*:

1. **Regress termination ≠ simplicity**: Demonstration proves only that the causal regress must terminate. It does not prove that the terminus must be absolutely simple, attribute-less, or without quiddity. The philosophers covertly add this premise.

2. **"Necessary Existent" as a concealed equivocation**: The term *wājib al-wujūd* (Necessary Existent) is used sometimes to mean "that which has no efficient cause" and sometimes to mean "that which has no dependency of any kind." The two meanings are not the same, and the second is never demonstrated.

3. **Denial of will → denial of agency → denial of knowledge**: A chain of consequences runs from the philosophers' core commitment to necessary emanation: if God does not will, He is not a real agent; if He is not a real agent, He does not have real knowledge of the world; if the argument for self-knowledge also requires will, He cannot even be shown to know Himself.

4. **The materialist challenge**: On the philosophers' own principles, they cannot refute materialism. This is al-Ghazālī's sharpest argumentative weapon in Discussions 4 and 10: the philosophers have erected a theological superstructure (God as First Principle, Maker, Knower) on philosophical foundations that cannot support it.

5. **No need for positive doctrine**: Al-Ghazālī explicitly disclaims the need to defend any particular Islamic theological account: "We have not plunged into this book in the manner of one who is introducing [doctrine], our purpose being to disrupt their claims — and this has been effected." (Discussion 3, §79)

## Historiography

Modern scholarship has substantially revised the older reading of this section as a straightforward "refutation of philosophy" in favor of a more complex picture:

- The *Tahāfut* does not simply defend Ashʿarite theology against philosophy; al-Ghazālī uses philosophical methods and standards throughout, and his Discussions 2–12 largely take Avicennan epistemological norms as given.
- Marmura's 2000 translation (the source for this page) is the standard scholarly English edition; his introduction stresses that al-Ghazālī's target is always Avicenna specifically, not "the Greek tradition" in general.
- The question of whether al-Ghazālī's critique of divine attributes (Discussion 6) constitutes a defense of the Ashʿarite doctrine of real attributes additional to the divine essence is debated: he argues the philosophers have not *disproved* real attributes, but he does not in this text establish that they *exist*.
- Averroes' *Tahāfut al-Tahāfut* responds to all these discussions from an Aristotelian-Avicennan perspective, accusing al-Ghazālī of sophistry and misrepresentation of Aristotle.
