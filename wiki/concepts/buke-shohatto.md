# Buke Shohatto (Laws for the Military Houses)

The *Buke Shohatto* ("Laws for the Military Houses") was the foundational legislative code
governing Japan's daimyo class throughout the [[edo-period|Edo period]] (1603–1868). First
promulgated in August 1615 — weeks after the final destruction of the Toyotomi at Osaka — it
defined the obligations, prohibitions, and norms of conduct for feudal lords under the
[[tokugawa-shogunate|Tokugawa Shogunate]]. Supplemented and reissued at each succession, it was
the primary legal instrument through which the bakufu maintained its hegemony within the
[[bakuhan-system|bakuhan system]].

## Origin and Promulgation

The code was issued at Fushimi in the name of the second Shogun, [[tokugawa-shogunate|Tokugawa
Hidetada]], with the retired Shogun [[tokugawa-ieyasu|Ieyasu]] resident nearby at Nijo Castle in
Kyoto — making the occasion, in Sadler's phrase, "a suitable opportunity to impress on the feudal
and Court nobles a sense of their future responsibilities." The Fushimi promulgation was preceded
by a Noh performance and banquet; the laws were read aloud to the assembled daimyo by Konchiin
Suden at the instance of Honda Masanobu before the entertainment began. A daimyo oath imposed in
1611 already bound the lords to obey Shogunal ordinances, not harbor rebels, and not shelter
samurai guilty of rebellion or homicide; the 1615 code gave those obligations formal legal content.

The code carried on the legislative traditions of Minamoto Yoritomo's Kamakura Shogunate and the
amendments introduced by Toyotomi Hideyoshi. Sadler describes it as "neither very bulky nor
particularly original... entirely directed to the purpose of keeping the Tokugawa family in a
position of complete and unassailable domination in the Empire."

## The Thirteen Articles (1615)

1. **Letters and arms** — "The way of letters and arms, of archery and horsemanship must be
   cultivated with all the heart and mind." Military laxity and cultural neglect are both
   forbidden; in times of peace, disorder must not be forgotten.
2. **Moderation in pleasure** — Drinking parties must be kept within proper limits. "Devotion to
   women and gambling is the primary cause of loss of one's fief."
3. **No refuge for lawbreakers** — Daimyo must not harbor persons who have broken the law, even
   when law conflicts with reason: "Law may be contrary to reason, but reason is no excuse for
   breaking the law."
4. **Expulsion of rebels and murderers** — "Savage and unruly retainers are edged tools for
   overthrowing the Empire."
5. **No intercourse with persons of other fiefs** — Revealing a fief's secrets to outsiders "is
   a sign of a desire to curry favor."
6. **Castle repair requires authorization** — Even repairs must be reported; new construction is
   forbidden. "A castle with a wall more than 100 chi (1,000 feet) is harmful to a fief."
7. **Report innovations and factions in neighboring fiefs** — Early warning of sedition is
   mandatory.
8. **Marriages must not be privately arranged** — Alliance marriages are a political act requiring
   bakufu approval. "To form parties through marriage, that is the source of nefarious plots."
9. **Service attendance regulated** — Daimyo coming to Edo for service must not lead excessive
   retinues. Lords below a million *koku* and above two hundred thousand may not exceed twenty
   horsemen; others in proportion.
10. **Dress distinctions** — Costume must reflect status; soldiers and ordinary retainers may not
    wear figured silks, brocade, or purple-lined garments without permission.
11. **Palanquins for lords only** — Common retainers and soldiers may not ride in palanquins; the
    right belongs to lords of provinces, their distinguished family members, senior retainers,
    doctors, astrologers, the elderly, and the sick.
12. **Economy for samurai of all fiefs** — "The wealthy are apt to be ostentatious and so the poor
    ashamed of their inequality. Nothing is more reprehensible."
13. **Able men for official positions** — "The art of government lies in obtaining the right men.
    If there are capable men in a fief it is sure to be prosperous."

## Enforcement and Practical Use

The code was "velvet bag full of gunpowder" (Tokutomi's phrase, quoted by Sadler): even its most
anodyne articles — the exhortation to economy, the prohibition of immoderate revelling — could be
used as pretexts for punishing daimyo the bakufu wished to weaken. "Even a platitude was useful to
the Shogunate." Under the first five Tokugawa shoguns, roughly 13.2 million *koku* of daimyo
territory changed hands through attainder (*kaieki*) and forced transfer (*tenpō*), processes
legitimated by the Buke Shohatto. A charge of "luxury or levity" was sufficient to compel a daimyo
to retire, be confined to his domain, have his revenue reduced, be forced to exchange domains with
another lord, or be dispossessed entirely.

The code was reread aloud before assembled daimyo at each new shogun's accession, a ritual
affirmation of the hierarchy that persisted throughout the Edo period. The third Shogun Iemitsu
expanded it to 21 articles in 1635, adding the [[sankin-kotai|sankin-kōtai]] (alternate attendance)
obligation as a formal requirement.

## Relationship to the Daimyo Oath (1611)

The 1611 oath preceded the full code and bound daimyo specifically to obey Shogunal laws, exclude
rebels, and not shelter murderers or rebel samurai: "Should any of us disobey any of these articles,
after a proper inquiry has been held into the facts, he shall be liable to be dealt with strictly
according to the law." The 1615 Buke Shohatto gave this oath substantive content by spelling out
what conduct was forbidden.

## Ieyasu's Legislative Philosophy

Ieyasu did not regard the Buke Shohatto as innovation. His legislative principle — stated in
documents attributed to him — was to "transmit and not to create": the code amplified and
curtailed the ancient regulations of the Minamoto house. He studied Ashikaga precedents, consulted
scholars like Hosokawa Fujitaka who had deep knowledge of Muromachi laws, and when he acquired new
territories (Kai from Takeda Shingen, the Kanto from the Hojo) he retained existing laws with
minimal alteration, lightening only the taxation. Continuity with the past legitimated the Tokugawa
settlement as restoration rather than usurpation.
