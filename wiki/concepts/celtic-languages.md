# Celtic Languages

## Definition and Scope

Celtic languages form a branch of the Indo-European family attested from the Early Iron Age onward across temperate Europe, the British Isles, parts of Iberia, and through migrations into Anatolia (Galatians) and elsewhere. The label 'Celtic' derives from classical ethnonyms (Keltoi, Galatae, Galli) applied by Greek and Roman authors, but the linguistic reality is defined by shared sound changes, vocabulary, and grammar reconstructed from limited continental inscriptions and the better-preserved Insular traditions.

Grounded in range_06 (Cunliffe 2018 index and back matter references for Ch. 14–15 and related entries): the languages are a core thread of long-term Celtic identity, persisting where material culture and political structures changed or disappeared.

## Survival in the Post-Roman West (Ch. 14 grounding)

The languages survived the Roman period and the transition to early medieval polities (Celtic survival 379–91). Key evidence and dynamics from range:

- Under the Romans (ad 1–400) 379–84: differential Romanization; Latin overlay in some zones but Celtic speech persisted in western and northern fringes.
- Ethnic readjustments (ad 300–500) 384–90: movements and settlements affecting language distributions, including immigration from south Wales and south-west England to Brittany (Armorican peninsula) (ad 300–500) 386, 387, 387–9.
- Extent of Celtic language (ad 300–500) 386, 387, 388 documented in Brittany, with Breton language 33, 388–9; Vannetais, Breton dialect 388–9; Waroc, Breton chieftain associated with 388.
- Ogham and early Christian inscribed stones 386 as markers of Insular Celtic literacy in Ireland and western Britain.
- Dálriata kingdom 387 (Gaelic-speaking polity linking Ireland and western Scotland).
- Communities of the Middle Ages and later 390–1: persistence into medieval period despite later pressures (e.g. Viking raids 390).

Romanization zones 381–2 and Romanization of Britain 380 show areas of stronger vs. weaker linguistic shift; Ireland largely outside direct Roman control preserved Goidelic continuity.

## Historiography and Methodological Notes

The source emphasizes that language provides a more continuous thread than archaeological 'packages' (Hallstatt/La Tène). Debates on origins (Atlantic vs. central European) and spread (mobility vs. lingua franca adoption) are reflected in the 2nd edition's framework. aDNA is anticipated as future resolver of mobility questions. The survival into post-Roman west (Brittany immigration, Insular Ogham/vernacular traditions) illustrates how linguistic identity can decouple from political or material dominance.
