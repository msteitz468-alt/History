# Enigma (cipher machine)

The Enigma was an electromechanical rotor cipher machine used by German armed forces and some civilian organisations before and during World War II. It generated a polyalphabetic substitution cipher that changed with each keypress, using a set of rotors, a reflector, and a plugboard (in military versions) to produce an enormous number of possible configurations.

## Historical Role

Early versions were commercial. The German military adopted and modified it (adding the plugboard, additional rotors for naval use). It was believed by the Germans to be unbreakable for practical purposes.

## Breaking Enigma

Polish cryptanalysts (Rejewski et al.) achieved the first breaks in the 1930s using mathematical methods and early "bombe" machines. After the fall of Poland and France, the work moved to Bletchley Park in Britain. Alan Turing and the team developed improved "bombes", "crib"-based methods, and organisational systems that allowed daily reading of large volumes of Enigma traffic (Ultra intelligence). Naval Enigma ("the Shark") was particularly difficult and critical for the Battle of the Atlantic.

The German "Fish" (Lorenz) teleprinter cipher was broken with the Colossus.

## Significance

The compromise of Enigma traffic provided the Allies with high-level insight into German operations, dispositions, and intentions while the Germans remained largely confident in the machine's security until late in the war.
