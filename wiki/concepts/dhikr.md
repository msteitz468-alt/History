## Definition

Dhikr (Arabic: "remembrance" or "invocation") is the distinctive devotional practice of [[tariqa-orders|tariqa Sufism]]: the systematic repetition of formulas, syllables, or names of God, accompanied by controlled breathing, posture, and bodily attention, designed to focus consciousness on the divine presence. In its private form, each murîd disciple practices the dhikr formula assigned by his pîr. In its communal form, dhikr assemblies brought together murîds and often laypeople in collective chanting that could produce states of ecstasy.

Dhikr is distinguished from *fikr* (meditation or reflection, more discursive and open-ended). Hodgson renders the contrast as "fikr = meditation, dhikr = invocation." The dhikr is more structured, more repetitive, and more physiologically engaged than fikr.

## Development

Originally a simple form of "remembrance" of God through recited formulas (itself Quranic: "Remember God often," Quran 33:41), dhikr became the central distinguishing practice of the [[tariqa-orders]] as those orders crystallized in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries. The two "distinctive marks of the new Sufism" in Hodgson's account are "its organization into formal 'orders', tariqahs" and "its concentration on a formal method of mystical worship, the dhikr."

## Technique

A "sophisticated array of techniques was developed for producing the desired results": breathing patterns were carefully prescribed; posture was regulated; the formula was uttered with "attention directed to particular parts of the body at particular moments of the breathing." The rosary (introduced from India, Hodgson notes) tracked frequency. At advanced stages, the murîd was to "sense" rather than articulate the formula. Visual imagery of the pîr's face was sometimes prescribed as an aid to focusing attention on ideal humanity and hence on God.

The psychic process was mapped in technical manuals: "certain colours of light were expected to appear mentally at certain stages of the process." High degrees of "organic self-control" were achievable.

## Communal Dhikr

Group dhikr sessions supplemented and sometimes supplanted the individual salât (ritual prayer). Held in the khâniqâh lodges of the [[tariqa-orders]], they attracted both initiated murîds and lay congregants. Hodgson describes the general dynamic:

> "In principle, any ecstatic response to the stimuli of the chanting should be spontaneous and individual; a man might rise from the circle and fling himself about, or faint, or shout; in practice it very early became widely accepted that all present should associate themselves to some degree in the more explicit ecstatic activity aimed at, once it had begun."

Music (samâ') — especially devotional song — commonly accompanied dhikr assemblies. This was the most contested element: [[ulama]] disapproved of music in general and suspected that beautiful singing voices moved hearers for the wrong reasons. Some Sufis avoided samâ'; others, including the Mawlawiyya (Mevlevis) founded around [[rumi|Jalâluddin Rûmî]], developed it into a highly formalized art, including the whirling dance (samâ') for which the "Whirling Dervishes" became known.

## Ecstasy vs. Discipline

A persistent tension within tariqa Sufism was the relationship between dhikr as a means (preparing the heart for divine grace) and dhikr as an end (producing ecstatic states directly). Hodgson notes that "many writers ceased to describe the dhikr and the results it could produce as merely a means of readying the heart for response to a divine grace that could not be achieved by any sort of technique in itself; they wrote as if the dhikr practice could lead, by way of simply human initiative, to a state of consciousness which embodied all the cosmic harmony that was to be desired."

The drift toward pure ecstasy at the expense of comprehensive moral discipline was a recurring concern among the most respected Sufi masters. Hodgson's assessment is sharp: "everyday mysticism, with its comprehensive moral discipline, was often neglected in favour of ecstatic mysticism." Eventually, "purely physical techniques of stimulation were to be used to produce more easily a state of consciousness regarded as the equivalent of what more ascetic techniques of self-control produced."

## Social Function

The group dhikr served as the primary communal activity of the [[tariqa-orders]], distinguishing them from the mosque assembly and giving them an identity independent of political authority. The khâniqâh — the building in which dhikr was housed — was explicitly "eminently private" (Hodgson), funded by waqf endowments rather than state patronage. This independence from political structures was both a cause and a consequence of the orders' trans-political social role.

## Contrasts with Salât

The salât (obligatory Muslim prayer, five times daily) is a state-associated institution performed in the mosque; it is politically embedded. The dhikr is a private, civically autonomous institution performed in the khâniqâh; it is politically independent. The two together constitute what Hodgson calls the "two faces" of Islamic religious life in the Middle Periods: the outward Shari'ah face managed by the 'ulama, and the inward Sufi face managed by the pîrs.
