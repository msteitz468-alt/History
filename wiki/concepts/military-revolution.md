# The Military Revolution

## Definition

The "Military Revolution" is the thesis that early modern Europe (conventionally
between roughly 1500 and 1700) underwent a transformation in the conduct of war
— driven by gunpowder weaponry, new infantry tactics and drill, the
*trace italienne* (star-shaped artillery fortification), much larger and more
permanent armies, and the fiscal-bureaucratic states needed to pay for them —
and that this transformation both reshaped the European state and gave Europeans
the military edge that underwrote their global expansion. The concept was coined
by **Michael Roberts** (1955) for the Dutch and Swedish armies of 1560–1660 and
globalized by **Geoffrey Parker** (*The Military Revolution*, 1988), who linked
it explicitly to "the rise of the West." Related framings include the
**"gunpowder empires"** (the Ottoman, Safavid, and Mughal states) and the
**"fiscal-military state."**

## The Argument

In its strong form the thesis holds that a cluster of innovations — handheld
firearms replacing the bow and the armored knight, volley fire and the disciplined
drilling of mass infantry, artillery-resistant fortification, and the
exponential growth of army sizes — constituted a genuine revolution that
diffused outward from a European core. Because European states had to mobilize
unprecedented fiscal and administrative resources to sustain these forces, the
military revolution is tied to the growth of centralized states; because
European armies and warships carried these methods overseas, it is tied to
European imperial dominance.

## Critique (Jeremy Black, CWH VI)

In CWH Volume VI Jeremy Black mounts a sustained skeptical case (see
[[military-revolution-thesis]] for the full controversy):

- **Medieval precedents.** Larger armies, rising military expenditure, and new
  tactics all had medieval antecedents; the changes were incremental, not abrupt.
- **Technology vs. technique.** Gunpowder was an *agent* of change marshalled by
  doctrine and "cultural habit of use," not an independent cause. If gunpowder's
  earlier introduction in China produced no revolution, why should it have in the
  West? Borrowing, where it occurred (Japan, north India), worked as an "add-on"
  to existing structures, not a wholesale transformation.
- **Non-European dynamism.** The Ottomans transformed their army and navy
  between 1450 and 1600, made stronger musket barrels than their Christian
  rivals, and fielded a cohesive corps of gunners — change *outside* Western
  Europe. The Safavids and Uzbeks used gunpowder without adopting the tactics
  central to the revolution thesis.
- **Late and contingent Western superiority.** European land superiority over
  the great Asian empires is far from obvious before the 1750s in India, perhaps
  the 1790s–1800s, and not clearly until the 1830s–1860s in China. The Spanish
  conquest of the Aztecs owed more to local alliances and indigenous disunity
  than to weapons; Mughal success rested on winning Rajput support.
- **"Little war."** The historiography over-weights set-piece battles between
  state armies and neglects the raids, skirmishes, and irregular warfare —
  Cossacks, Zunghars, North American and Southeast Asian peoples — that were far
  more common and could undo the fruits of battlefield victory.

Black's conclusion: if "revolution" means abrupt, fundamental change, none
occurred in this period; change was generally incremental and locally
contingent, and warfare across much of the world was waged effectively without
reference to Western methods.

## Limitations and Use

The concept remains analytically productive as a way of relating war, state
formation, and finance, but its strong "rise of the West" version is widely
qualified. The most durable insight is the **link between military scale and
state capacity**; the most contested is the claim of a single diffusing European
model and its role in explaining European expansion.
