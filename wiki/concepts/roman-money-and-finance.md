# Roman Money, Banking, and Finance

The world of **Roman private finance** — elite money-lending, professional bankers, monetization,
and credit — in the High Empire. Treated in [[bowman-cah-v11-2000|CAH XI]] Ch. 26 (**Jean
Andreau**), whose dominant theme is **continuity** (the deaths of Nero and Commodus marked no
turning point) punctuated by a real **decline of professional banking** in the second–third
centuries.

## Two worlds of finance: elite financiers and professional bankers

Andreau distinguishes two strata:

- **Elite financiers** — senators, knights, and municipal aristocrats for whom **lending at
  interest** was a normal component of a great fortune, alongside land, slaves, and silverware
  (Trimalchio's *fundi, nummorum nummi, argentum, familia*; Pliny "almost wholly in land — yet I
  also lend at interest"). Though usury met **moral disapproval** (Plutarch), the highest men, even
  the emperor, lent openly, delegating management to slaves (*actores*, *dispensatores*) or
  intermediaries. They lent to and borrowed from **their own circle**, contracting loans to meet
  loans (*versura*) and transferring credits rather than cash — and increasingly had to move
  **capital from their provincial estates to Italy**.
- **Professional bankers** — money-changers and deposit-takers of the **plebeian city trades**
  (often freedmen, never of the aristocracy), regulated and confined to defined functions
  (exchange, coin-testing, deposits, loans). In the West the **argentarii** and **coactores
  argentarii** had a distinctive role: **providing credit at auctions** (the Pompeian tablets of
  L. Caecilius Iucundus), a practice unique to the Latin West and lasting four centuries. Regional
  variety was sharp: **Egypt** had **public banks** (*dēmosiai trapezai*, heirs of the Ptolemaic
  state banks) and private ones serving modest clients; the Greek **trapezitai** enjoyed higher
  social standing and more secure footing than their western counterparts.

A consistent pattern: the **elite lent to financiers of their own rank**, while the clients of
professional bankers were typically people of **more limited means**.

## Monetization and the "productive loan" question

Two debated questions:

- **How monetized was the empire?** Andreau weighs **Hopkins** (the even distribution of dated
  silver coins across regions implies a strongly integrated monetary economy) against
  **Duncan-Jones** (the regional variation in coin *types* tells against heavy inter-regional
  monetary trade). His balance: monetization was **real but relative** — less in country than city,
  varying by region — and coin evidence reflects **state expenditure** more than private finance,
  so radical positions should be avoided.
- **Were loans "productive"?** Neither elite financiers nor *argentarii* **methodically financed
  enterprises**, and they did not clearly distinguish production loans from consumption loans (most
  attested loans, as in classical Greece, were for consumption). But Andreau insists this does
  **not** mean finance was absent from economic life: the *coactores argentarii* granted
  **short-term commercial credit at the markets and auctions** (the *macellum magnum*, the wine
  port, the *forum boarium*), letting merchants restock and buy faster; and **maritime loans** to
  traders persisted on Athenian-style terms.

## Techniques and continuity

The instruments were carried over from the Hellenistic world: bank **transfers** between accounts,
**orders of payment**, and even **cheques** (attested in Ptolemaic Egypt and implied under the
Empire) — but **credit instruments never circulated freely** (cheques could not be endorsed or made
payable to bearer; there was no institutionalized clearing). Rates of interest, book-keeping, and
lending practice changed little.

## The decline of professional banking

Against this continuity, Andreau traces a clear **decline of professional banking in the second and
third centuries, especially in Italy**:

- The **nummularii** evolved into deposit bankers (first half of the second century) — but this was
  accompanied by a **marked reduction of credit at auctions**; the *argentarii* and *coactores
  argentarii* dwindle from the evidence after c. 150 (surviving only at Rome, Ostia, Portus) and
  **vanish entirely by the later third century**.
- The decline of **auctions** signals that the great constituents of aristocratic fortunes (slaves,
  houses, lands) had become **less fluid** and less often on sale, and reflects a contraction of
  the **redistributive trade** and the circle of medium-scale businessmen supplying Rome.
- The one known banker's failure — the future **Pope Callistus**, a slave-banker ruined under
  Commodus and sent to the Sardinian mines — illustrates the difficulties of the profession
  (variously linked to Commodan price rises or Cleander's grain speculation).

Meanwhile **eastern bankers and merchants** (trapezitai from Antioch and Synnada) begin to appear
in the western markets in the second century — a sign of shifting balance.

## Significance and contest

Andreau's picture is of a **sophisticated but non-developmental financial system**: real banking,
credit, and monetization, deeply embedded in social life, but never organized into a modern engine
of investment — and, in the West, **contracting** in the very period of the empire's apparent
prosperity. The degree of monetization, the "productivity" of credit, and the causes of the
banking decline remain contested, constrained by the impossibility of quantifying private financial
volume.

## The coinage in the crisis (CAH XII)

The financial world Andreau describes for the High Empire was transformed by the
[[third-century-crisis|third-century crisis]]: the steady **debasement** of the silver denarius and
antoninianus (from Caracalla's antoninianus of 215 to the silver collapse of c. 253–270), the
reforms of [[aurelian|Aurelian]] (274), [[diocletian|Diocletian]] (286–296, with the Price Edict of
301) and [[constantine-the-great|Constantine]] (the gold **solidus**), and the survival and then
overhaul of the Augustan tax system into the **iugatio–capitatio**. This monetary and fiscal history
— and Mireille Corbier's caution that debasement cannot be equated with proportional inflation — is
treated in [[late-roman-coinage]]; its wider economic stakes in [[late-roman-economy]].
