## Definition and Scope

**Blue Pacific regionalism** denotes the cluster of political, cultural, and historiographical ideas that reframe the Pacific Ocean and its island peoples as the active subjects — rather than the peripheral backdrop — of their own history. It encompasses two successive but related framings:

1. **Blue Continent** — a metaphor popularized by the Bishop Museum's 2013 Pacific Hall to assert that the ocean is a coherent historical unit comparable to a land continent, connecting rather than dividing its peoples.
2. **Blue Pacific** — the successor concept adopted by the Pacific Islands Forum (PIF), which drops the continental metaphor in favour of a proudly maritime identity, positioning Pacific Islander nations as "custodians of the world's largest, most peaceful and abundant ocean."

Samson (ch. 36) traces these concepts through a colour-coded thought experiment examining what "Black Pacific," "Yellow Pacific," "Red Pacific," and "White Pacific" framings reveal and conceal, concluding that blue "is historically neutral, by which I mean that it was never used as a colonizing label for perceived racial or cultural groups during the colonial era, unlike red, yellow, or black."

The concept is distinct from (though related to) the earlier Epeli Hau'ofa concept of a [[sea-of-islands]], which emphasised Indigenous inter-island connectivity from inside Islander cosmologies. Blue Pacific regionalism adds an explicit geopolitical and institutional dimension.

## Genealogy: From Blue Continent to Blue Pacific

### Historiographical precursors (1960s–2000s)

Pacific Studies as an academic field emerged in the 1960s–1970s alongside decolonization, emphasizing "Indigenous agency and the importance of local historical circumstances." Area-studies fragmentation, however, meant that Aotearoa New Zealand, Australia, Southeast Asian Studies, and Pacific Studies rarely shared frameworks; "the imperial history of the Pacific world — fragmented into at least a dozen separate programmes."

The maritime turn arrived later in Pacific scholarship than in Atlantic studies. Paul Gilroy's 1993 proposal to treat the Atlantic "as one single, complex unit of analysis" provided a model, but as Samson notes, "some of the new transnational, maritime-centred studies of ocean worlds began with the Atlantic before proceeding on to the Indian Ocean and others, and only then tackling the Pacific world." Pacific historians had been "not inclined to consider the environment as a significant influence on cultural and historical patterns" — ironic, given the oceanic environment's centrality to Pacific cultures.

Dennis Flynn's *Pacific Centuries* (1999) and related scholarship, Glynn Barratt's work on the Russian Pacific, and eventually Armitage, Bashford, and Sivasundaram's *Oceanic Histories* (2018) began to remedy this. Armitage et al. "made a deliberate choice not to begin their book with the Mediterranean or Atlantic, but instead with three oceans, the Indian, Pacific, and Atlantic, arranged in that order in order to divert pre-eminence from the European world."

### Raising the Blue Continent (2013)

The Bishop Museum's 2013 Pacific Hall opened with the mural *Anu'u Nu'u ka 'Ike* ("Learning step by step"), "created by over thirty students and master artists of Indigenous Pacific Island heritage," expressing "the ocean's physical, historical, and spiritual identities." Hawaiian practitioner Ramsey Taum framed "Raising the Blue Continent" as "taking a stand against the erosion of relationships by certain strands of Western political and economic philosophy," urging visitors to develop "new eyes" — "context changes content."

The Blue Continent concept quickly entered wider usage: a "Blue Continent Caucus" gathered at the 2015 Native Hawaiian Convention; Taiwanese museums deployed it for Austronesian heritage exhibitions; Pacific scholars cited it as a counterweight to "the privileging of continents" in Western historical imagination.

Samson offers a critical appreciation: the continent metaphor was "a powerful way of articulating something that was formative rather than responsive," but it "perpetuates a significant problem in historiography" — namely, the assumption that land masses are the normative location of history. "The Pacifc is simply a 'civilization without a center,' and Oceanic space of movement, transit, and migration in a longue durée of local peoples and broad interactions." The image of an ersatz continent "just doesn't capture the alterity and dynamism of Pacific worlds."

### Blue Pacific as institutional policy (2014–present)

The PIF's 2014 **Framework for Pacific Regionalism** proclaimed that "Pacific peoples are the custodians of the world's largest, most peaceful and abundant ocean, its many islands and its rich diversity of cultures." At the 2017 Forum, Sāmoa's prime minister Tuilaepa introduced the concept formally: "The Blue Pacific will strengthen the existing policy frameworks that harness the ocean as a driver of a transformative socio-cultural, political and economic development." The Forum plans to launch a full "Strategy for the Blue Pacific Continent" in 2050 — notably a "hybrid title that employs both the older continental conception as well as the newer Blue Pacific terminology."

The shift from "Continent" to "Blue Pacific" matters: where the former challenged continental prejudice by claiming continental status, the latter re-centres maritime identity positively — the spaces and places "are proudly maritime ones."

## Colour Mapping: What Earlier Framings Reveal

Samson structures the chapter as a colour-coded "kaleidoscope" of Pacific world histories, each colour illuminating a particular set of historical relationships:

### Black Pacific
European racial taxonomy named Melanesia (black islands) and coined "blackbirding" for the nineteenth-century trade in Melanesian labour — from guano mines west of Peru to Queensland sugar plantations and Fijian cotton fields. "Thinking resembling the racial justification for slavery was used to justify entrapment of Pacific Islanders." The Rabaul Strike of 1929, inspired partly by African American sailors who told Melanesian workers their wages were too low, connects Black Atlantic and Black Pacific histories. Marcus Garvey's trans-Pacific Black Star Line Steamship Corporation and W.E.B. Du Bois's 1936 Japan visit illustrate "a fledgling black internationalism" in the interwar period. African American servicemen in the Pacific War — dark-skinned men wielding the same weapons as white soldiers — catalyzed independence movements including Maasina Rule in Solomon Islands.

### Yellow Pacific / Asia-Pacific
European "yellow peril" discourse from Kaiser Wilhelm II onward constructed all Asians as one threatening race, producing anti-Asian legislation across Australia, Aotearoa New Zealand, and North America from the 1880s. Chinese *huashang* (traders), *huagong* (overseas labourers), *huaqiao* (sojourners), and later *huayi* (transnational ethnic Chinese investors) each represented successive waves of Pacific presence. Japanese and Filipino communities shaped borderlands from British Columbia to the Russian Priamur. The 1989 creation of **APEC** — a "direct English translation of the Japanese *Ajia taiheiyō chiiki*" — marked a transformation: "at least at the official level, 'yellow peril' discourse had given way to a more respectful (or at least pragmatic) understanding of the integration and importance of trans-Pacific economic ties." Helen Jun warns, however, that "black disenfranchisement is structurally related to new Asian immigration and the rise of the so-called Pacific Rim economy," cautioning against a focus solely on white racism.

### Red Pacific
The "Red Atlantic" historiography highlights Indigenous Americas peoples as active agents. A "Red Pacific" would require "a much longer timeline" given prehistoric Polynesian–American contacts (the sweet potato), the Spanish silver trade network from coastal Americas through Guam to the Philippines, and the eighteenth-century fur trade connecting Northwest Coast First Nations to Polynesian Kanakas. Indigenous Pacific sailors crewed vessels across the whole Pacific world; "with more familiarity and growing demand, Oceanian voyagers found niches ranging from proletarian kanaka to ennobled tourist." King Kalākaua of Hawaii's 1881 world tour (the first by any monarch in history) sought Indigenous-Asian alliances against Western imperialism — "a possible future for independent Indigenous sovereignty that was delayed but never forgotten."

### White Pacific
Euro-American colonial power mapped, partitioned, and exploited the Pacific from the late fifteenth century onward. Settler colonialism in Australia, Aotearoa New Zealand, and western North America built on ideologies of "vanishing" Indigenous races. Nuclear testing — "from the Australian outback to Bikini Atoll" — "subjected [Indigenous peoples] to catastrophic environmental destruction and radiation damage." American testing ended in 1962; France continued until 1996. "The killing white light was unleashed only on the side of the Western allies." Belau created "the first anti-nuclear constitution in world history in 1979." Dead coral reefs — "gray-white" — testify to ongoing environmental destruction: "White — an Asian colour of mourning — is on the rise in the Pacific world, in waters that used to nourish multicoloured reefs."

## Significance and Critique

### What Blue Pacific does

The Blue Pacific reframes rimworld geopolitics from inside Pacific Islander priorities. It responds to Cold War-era "rimbound thinking" such as Mary Boyd's 1970 characterization of Polynesia, Melanesia, and Micronesia as "three great shields that guard the sea and air approaches from East and Southeast Asia to Australia, New Zealand, Hawaii, and the United States." The concept provides a "touchstone for policies located firmly in local, island-centred priorities," connecting climate change, ocean stewardship, and development. It draws on Epeli Hau'ofa's call for a common Pacific identity to enable "the protection of the ocean for the general good."

### Critiques and tensions

**Continental residue.** Samson argues that "the concept's privileging of land masses as the normative location of history will perpetuate a significant problem in historiography." The Hawaiian proverb — "He Wa'a He Moku, He Moku He Wa'a! (A canoe is an island; an island is a canoe)" — better captures the maritime epistemology.

**Appropriation risk.** Rob Wilson warns that a Blue Pacific may become "a new refrain on a very old imperial tune for non-Indigenous academics and policymakers," noting that Hawai'i is "simply an easy place to write about, to laze and gaze around in for a week or two." The "Rush for Oceania" — scholars, activists, politicians focused on rising sea levels — risks a new form of extractive attention.

**Blue Economy tension.** The Forum's parallel promotion of a "Blue Green Economy" blurs the concept with development-finance frameworks that may invite "fresh exploitation by the rimworld, even as it generates 'a space of resistance' for exploited Pacific peoples."

**Rimworld persistence.** Even the Trans-Pacific Partnership "has no sovereign members inside the rim." China and Taiwan's competition for influence among Pacific Island nations represents a new iteration of rimbound geopolitics.

### Planetary extension

Samson concludes by extending the Blue Pacific metaphor to the Blue Planet: "Humanity experienced a collective paradigm shift when we first viewed our world from the moon: a precious, blue island in the black ocean of space... There were no borders; no political or economic blocs; no 'natural' geopolitics." Indigenous Pacific navigational knowledge — "the collection and careful conservation of drinking water, the preservation of canoe plants... and the cherishing of ancestral knowledge and wisdom" — offers a model for planetary stewardship. Stefanie Hessler's "Tidalectics" (building on Kamau Brathwaite's Caribbean historiography) provides one theoretical framework for this oceanic turn in environmental humanities.

## Historiography

**Pacific Studies tradition.** Island-centred analysis has been central to the field since its 1960s inception; unlike Atlantic history, "it has emphasized the central importance of Indigenous agency in detailed studies of culture contact in the islands. After the postcolonial revolution in the 1980s, Pacific Studies rarely succumbed to a totalizing, reductive view of colonial power."

**Howe and Kerry's framework.** Kerry Howe (*Nature, Culture, and History*, 2000) notes that Westerners "as fundamentally continental creatures, see islands as tiny dots of land and ignore the surrounding seas," and cautions that "different peoples may have different knowledge and perspectives" — resisting closure in favour of diversity.

**Oceanic histories scholarship.** Paul D'Arcy (*The People of the Sea*, 2006) argues that "to truly understand the history of Oceania, we need to map its cultural seascapes as well as those on land." The field has converged on maritime environment as both subject and analytical lens.

**Contested framings.** The Blue Pacific concept is simultaneously a political programme of Pacific Island Forum member states and an academic-historiographical intervention; these two registers create productive but sometimes competing pressures. Samson's chapter, the source for this page, models a reflexive, multi-perspectival approach — the "kaleidoscope" that "never produces the same image twice."
