# Jus Victoriae (Law of Victory)

**Definition.** The body of legal doctrine — dominant in European thought until the
nineteenth century, dead in modern international law — that treated war as a
*legitimate legal procedure* for settling disputes and asked two technical
questions: **how do we know who won?** and **what does the victor win by winning?**
It is the counterpart, and historically the predecessor, of the modern
[[just-war|jus ad bellum]] and jus in bello, which ask instead whether a war is
justified and how it may be humanely conducted.

**Emic vs. etic.** The *term* jus victoriae is etic — coined by James Q. Whitman in
[[whitman-verdict-of-battle-2012|The Verdict of Battle]] (2012). The *content* is
emic: eighteenth-century publicists such as [[grotius-hugo|Grotius]],
[[vattel-emer-de|Vattel]], and their contemporaries wrote extensively on proofs of
victory, the rights of conquest, booty, capitulation, and the "tacit contract of
chance" by which two sovereigns submitted a dispute to [[verdict-of-battle|the
chance of arms]].

## Core doctrines

- **The retreat rule.** The dominant answer to "who won": the victor is the side
  that forces the other to flee and holds the field — traced from Greek antiquity
  (the *tropaion* erected where the enemy broke) through the medieval convention of
  camping three days on the field to the eighteenth century. Its rival, the
  **casualties rule** (fewer losses wins), could conflict with it — at Malplaquet
  (1709) the French lost the field but inflicted far heavier losses — but the
  retreat rule was "overwhelmingly dominant" (p. 184). Commanders were not
  *obliged* to stop at the field's edge; the rule was, in H.L.A. Hart's terms,
  **power-conferring, not duty-imposing**: drive the enemy off and you gained the
  power to declare a legally recognized "complete victory," with its diplomatic
  fruits, without the risks of pursuit.
- **Proofs of victory** (Encyclopédie Méthodique, 1780s): hold the field longer;
  capture baggage, artillery, flags, standards, and drums; gather and bury the
  dead of both sides; stand ready to fight next day if the enemy declines. Booty
  law required lawful victory (control of the field) before plunder — codified
  from a 1393 Swiss soldiers' compact through Maximilian I's Articles of War,
  still in force in the eighteenth century.
- **The doctrine of pretexts.** A war fought "in form" on *any* colorable legal
  claim — even an insincere one — conveyed valid title; only pretextless war was
  *bellum ferinum*, "wild beast war" (Wolff). Vattel: "Pretexts are at least a
  sort of reparations that the unjust pay to the just."
- **The Kriegsmanier.** Alongside the strict law (Martens's *ius belli
  infinitum* conceded unlimited rights in principle), a customary "manner of war"
  — court-ceremonial discipline extended to armies, observed "above all since the
  introduction of regular troops" (Martens, 1789) — governed actual conduct.

- **Battle as settlement procedure.** A pitched battle, mutually accepted, was a
  wager: both sides tacitly contracted to abide by its outcome. Medieval lawyers
  framed this as a judgment of God (trial by battle writ large); eighteenth-century
  lawyers secularized it as a contract of chance — Pufendorf: a "tacit contract
  that he upon whom Fortune has smiled should be able to dictate whatever terms he
  chose," submitting the parties' fates to "the Dice of Mars." This is what made
  peace treaties enforceable and non-voidable for duress.
- **Rights of the victor.** Victory conveyed enforceable claims — to territory,
  dynastic successions, booty, contributions, and capitulations — because
  eighteenth-century wars were fought over legalistic, proprietary stakes
  (cf. [[proprietorial-warfare]]), not over causes or ideologies.

## Significance and limits

Whitman argues the jus victoriae, not humanitarian moralism, was what actually
*contained* war: clear ground rules about stakes and victory gave both sides
incentives to accept a single day's verdict rather than fight wars of devastation.
Its collapse in the 1860s–70s ([[death-of-pitched-battle]]) — when democratic and
nationalist publics refused to accept battlefield verdicts, and the revived
just-war framework made wars contests of righteousness — is on this reading the
tragedy behind [[total-war]].

**Critiques and Whitman's legal-theory defense.** Skeptics (e.g., Jeremy Black's
"Eighteenth-Century Warfare Reconsidered") doubt there "really were rules":
Black's puzzle — why did Frederick "restrict himself to gaining control of the
battlefield" despite usually losing more men than his enemies even in victory? —
can be answered tactically (pursuit risked desertion and disintegration). Whitman
accepts the tactical point but argues it is insufficient: commanders were
Holmesian "bad men," rational actors for whom the retreat rule's legal payoff (a
recognized "complete victory" convertible into peace terms) made restraint
profitable. Standard tests of "rule of law" all fail here (no court — Kant;
practice without *opinio juris*; losers like Maria Theresa refusing adverse
verdicts — Tom Tyler's test), so Whitman rebuilds the claim on Hart's
power-conferring rules and Weber's facilitative conception of law: "rule-oriented"
rather than strictly rule-governed (Josiah Ober). Henry Maine's verdict serves as
epigraph: eighteenth-century war was sanctioned "ferocity and cupidity" that
nonetheless found a way to "command obedience to a rule." The concept also fits
continental land war among monarchies; it travels poorly to naval, colonial, and
civil wars, which Whitman excludes.

**Whitman's normative coda.** The Conclusion argues modern law has *no*
functioning jus victoriae — only the "very poor legal doctrine" of unconditional
surrender (a WWII-era U.S. State Department invention, reapplied in Iraq) — and
that this moral maximalism makes wars *harder to end*: "Good law makes peace...
We need a jus victoriae to... permit a brokered peace" (p. 261). Acquisitive,
limited-stakes wars can be terminated by deal-cutting; crusades cannot.
