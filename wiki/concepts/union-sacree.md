# Union Sacrée

The **Union Sacrée** ('sacred union') was the French wartime political and social truce declared in August 1914 that suspended normal partisan and class divisions for the duration of national defence. It symbolized and enacted the fusion of left and right, republicans and clericals, socialists and the state around the single objective of defending France. The parallel German formulation was the **Burgfrieden** ('castle peace' or civic truce).

## Definition and Scope

The union sacrée was not the suppression of all differences but their subordination to the immediate task of defence. It was 'an entirely utilitarian formulation, with the single objective of defending France.' Only as the war lengthened would the ideological differences underpinning it become evident.

It was formalized by President Poincaré's appeal to the chamber of deputies on 4 August 1914, but the practical fusion had already occurred. 'Thus, when later the same day Poincaré called on the chamber of deputies for a union sacrée, he was formalizing what was already a fait accompli.'

All ninety-eight socialist deputies supported the vote for war credits. The CGT (Confédération Générale du Travail), having abandoned its revolutionary general strike in the face of the crisis, appealed to workers for co-operation with the government. On 4 August Léon Jouhaux, standing in front of Jaurès’s coffin, affirmed his faith in the justice of the French cause.

## Causal Drivers and Immediate Context

The union sacrée emerged from the rapid coalescence of French working-class organizations around national defence once war became unavoidable. 

- Jean Jaurès, the great internationalist and anti-militarist, had nonetheless been a French patriot who argued that if arbitration failed the proletariat must defend the nation's independence. His assassination on 31 July 1914 removed the pivot of the peace movement and transformed him into a symbol of defencism.
- 'They have assassinated Jaurès; we shall not assassinate France.' (Gustave Hervé, La Guerre sociale, 1 August 1914)
- The CGT had already moderated from revolutionary anti-militarism to anti-militarist defencism. Fear of government repression (the carnet B list of anti-militarists) and the practical impossibility of a general strike contributed.
- The government's decision (via Interior Minister Malvy) not to implement arrests under carnet B on 1 August sent a signal of conciliation that was reciprocated.

In Germany the equivalent Burgfrieden was sealed when the SPD voted as a bloc for war credits on 4 August. Kaiser Wilhelm II declared: 'I no longer recognize parties; I recognize only Germans.'

The drivers were defensive necessity framed against a reactionary enemy (Russia for Germany; Germany for France) and the widespread sense that internal divisions must be set aside.

`caused_by:` the collapse of the Second International's pacifist strategy and the general acceptance that war had come; `produced:` the political truce.

## Major Phases

**Pre-war preparation.** Long-term trends: the CGT's shift from 1908 revolutionary strike rhetoric to practical moderation; the SPD's reformism and growing collaboration with the state; the erosion of strict anti-patriotism on the French left.

**Late July 1914.** French socialists debated the Vaillant-Hardie proposal at an extraordinary meeting 14–16 July; Jaurès's motion passed 1,690 to 1,174. Demonstrations against war peaked 28–31 July but remained calm. The CGT abandoned the strike call on 29 July after Jaurès convinced it to prioritize unity.

**1–4 August.** Jaurès's death, the declaration of mobilization, and the CGT's pivot to co-operation. Poincaré's formal appeal. The symbol of Jaurès's coffin at Jouhaux's speech.

**August 1914 onward.** The government promoted the union sacrée locally (teacher and priest on the same platform). Symbolic committees included figures from across the spectrum (cardinal, pastor, chief rabbi, freethinker, royalist, bourgeois, socialist, syndicalist). On the most vital pre-war issue (three-year service law) the cabinet was reshuffled to balance expertise while drifting toward the centre-right.

In Germany the SPD's acceptance was accompanied by the expectation (not fulfilled) of constitutional and social reform as quid pro quo.

## Interaction with Other Processes

The union sacrée was the domestic counterpart to the international failure of socialism. It represented the triumph of national priorities over Internationalism.

`contrasts_with:` the pre-war ambitions of the Second International (general strike to prevent war); `enabled:` the sustained national effort and the later transition to more total forms of mobilization.

It was not a permanent erasure of divisions. 'Only as the war lengthened would the ideological differences underpinning it become evident.'

## Historiography

Contemporary and later accounts have sometimes presented the union sacrée as a spontaneous outburst of suppressed nationalism or as cynical elite manipulation. The source material shows a more utilitarian and negotiated reality: both the CGT and the socialists lacked immediate tactics for stopping mobilization once the crisis accelerated; fear of repression and the desire to preserve organizations played roles; Jaurès's personal authority had been steering toward a preventive but ultimately defencist stance.

The death of Jaurès was pivotal not because it suppressed dissent but because grief and outrage were shared across the political spectrum, turning a potential flashpoint for division into a unifying symbol. Many Frenchmen went to war 'specifically for Jaurès’s ideals: they were fighting a war of defence, the successful conclusion to which would lay the foundation for truly lasting Internationalism and even for a republican Germany.'

On the right the war vindicated virulent nationalism and the three-year law. The truce was therefore broad enough to contain both conceptions of France.
