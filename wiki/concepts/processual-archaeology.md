# Processual Archaeology (New Archaeology)

Processual archaeology (often called the "New Archaeology") emerged in the 1960s, especially in the United States, as a self-conscious rejection of traditional culture-historical archaeology. It sought to make archaeology a scientific, anthropological discipline capable of explaining cultural change through general laws and processes rather than merely describing sequences of "cultures."

Key tenets (as contrasted in European discussions):
- Archaeology as anthropology (explanation over description).
- Systems theory, ecology, adaptation, and optimization models.
- Hypothesis testing, middle-range theory, and ethnoarchaeology.
- Positivist epistemology (objective observation, law-like regularities).
- Rejection of normative views of culture in favour of adaptive, behavioural, and processual explanations.

In Europe, the reception was highly uneven (Hodder ed. 1991). Some countries (e.g. parts of Scandinavia, Britain to a degree) incorporated ecological and anthropological ideas. Many others resisted or transformed the package due to different intellectual histories (stronger Marxism, historicism, continental philosophy) and political experiences (reaction against the nationalist misuse of archaeology under fascism).

The "New Archaeology" never achieved the hegemonic status in Europe that it briefly held in North America. By the late 1970s/1980s even its American proponents were fragmenting, opening space for post-processual critiques.

### Eastern Bloc Specifics (Hodder ed. 1991 Range 05: Czechoslovakia, Hungary)
Under Soviet influence, New Archaeology arrived very late or marginally. In Hungary the "intellectual ferment of international scholarship characterising the latter half of the 1970s by-passed Hungary"; a 1976 debate was initiated by a non-archaeologist and met with defenses that "challenged the novelty of the New Archaeology." C14 data were scarce ("almost total lack"), neolithisation interpreted as arrival of new groups (Körös culture) rather than local process, so key debates on independent development vs. diffusion were "irrelevant." Adoption was more methodological (via joint foreign projects) than theoretical. Childe remained the stronger model after the "eclipse of vulgar-Marxist ideology."

In Czechoslovakia, New Archaeology "could begin to creep in" only after 1968 relaxed ideological pressure, but "few people had the courage to defend it openly" and economic isolation limited access. Theory writing (Marxian or otherwise) was curtailed.

These cases exemplify how political isolation, restricted literature/contacts, and pre-existing Marxist/historical frameworks produced highly selective and delayed engagement with the processual package.
