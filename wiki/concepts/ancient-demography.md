# Ancient Demography

The broader Greco-Roman-world demographic framework developed by **Walter Scheidel** in
[[scheidel-cambridge-economic-history-greco-roman-world-2007|The Cambridge Economic History of the
Greco-Roman World]] (2007) — covering mortality, fertility, migration, and population estimates
across archaic Greece through the high Roman empire. This page is the wider, comparative companion
to [[roman-demography]], which develops the Roman-specific figures (Frier's Egyptian-census
analysis, Ulpian's life table) in more depth; the two should be read together.

## Mortality and Fertility Regime

Broad scholarly consensus holds that ancient populations combined high fertility with high
mortality: mean life expectancy at birth is conventionally placed at 20–30 years. Roman Egypt's
roughly 300 surviving census returns (first–third centuries CE) yield model-life-table-consistent
estimates of 22–25 years (Bagnall and Frier 1994), though an alternative reading suggests urban
populations suffered substantially higher mortality than rural ones. "Ulpian's life table" (a Roman
annuities schedule) and North African tombstone data converge on life expectancy in the low
twenties, though all these sources are methodologically contested — epitaphs carry age and gender
bias, and skeletal age estimation is unreliable.

Mortality was heavily concentrated in early life: up to three or four times as many people may have
died before age ten as died after age sixty, though mortality remained substantial throughout life.
To sustain population at these mortality levels, the average woman who survived to menopause needed
4.5–6.5 live births; mean marital fertility was likely 6–9 births given divorce, widowhood, and
sterility. Fertility control shows no evidence of deliberate parity-based "stopping" in the Roman
Egyptian census data — a "natural fertility" regime — though divorce, easy for both sexes in Athens
and Rome and likely more common than in later Christian Europe, functioned as an unusual preventive
check. The Greco-Roman household pattern is classified as broadly "Mediterranean" in Hajnal's
tripartite typology (early female marriage, late male marriage).

## Population Estimates

Mediterranean-area population roughly quadrupled from the twelfth century BCE to the second century
CE in the lands that would become the Roman European provinces, an average growth rate near
0.1%/year; growth was slower (about 0.07%/year) in the already-developed eastern Mediterranean.
Comparable long-run rates: European Roman-province growth of 0.06–0.07%/year from the late
second-century-CE peak to 1800, and China's Han-to-Qing growth of roughly 0.1%/year (1–1800 CE).

**Roman empire, c. 165 CE (Table 3.1):** total population estimated at 59–72 million (alternate
readings run 63–76 million), roughly 55–60% in the European provinces and about 20% each in Asia
and Africa. Italy: 8–9 million (some readings 12–13 million); Egypt: 5–6 million, the highest
population density in the empire at 167–200 people/km²; Anatolia: 9–10 million; Greater Syria: 5–6
million.

**Classical Greece:** mainland Greece and the Aegean islands held roughly 2 million people at
their classical-period peak, 3–3.5 million including Thessaly, Epirus, and Macedonia; overseas
Greek settlements (Ionia, Sicily, southern Italy, the Black Sea) added roughly another 2 million.
Athens ranged from 250,000–300,000+ at the Periclean peak (430s BCE, including 60,000 adult male
citizens and roughly 100,000 slaves) down to 150,000–250,000 in the 320s BCE. Spartan citizens
peaked at roughly 25,000 (early fifth century BCE), sustained by up to 100,000 helots.

**Comparanda:** the Achaemenid Persian empire held some 20–25 million subjects at its early-fifth-
century-BCE height; the Seleucid empire never exceeded roughly 15 million (third century BCE);
Ptolemaic Egypt fluctuated between 4 and 7 million.

## Migration

Cavalli-Sforza's genetic reconstruction shows no massive population shifts across the Mediterranean
after the start of the Iron Age. The largest migration flow of the era was the slave trade, moving
millions of people into Greece and, especially, Roman Italy. Greek overseas emigration from the
archaic through Hellenistic periods totaled only a few hundred thousand people; by 14 CE, Roman
citizens of Italian origin resident in the provinces numbered no more than about 750,000.

## The Homeostatic Model

Scheidel frames long-run ancient population dynamics as a **homeostatic model**: population size
equilibrates with available resources through Malthusian preventive (fertility-reducing) and
positive (mortality-raising) checks. Technological and institutional change (in the Boserupian
sense) can shift the equilibrium "saturation point" upward before surpluses are again exhausted;
population size and the accumulated "stock of knowledge" are treated as the two fundamental,
interdependent drivers of long-run economic progress. Chattel slavery functioned as a
population-pressure release valve within this model — slaves could be coerced to work harder and
consume less than free laborers, supporting larger populations than a purely free-family-labor
system, a mechanism central to archaic and classical Greece and Republican Roman Italy and Sicily.

Scheidel argues ancient economies nonetheless fell into a **low equilibrium trap**: they could not
sustain, over the long run, the "critical minimum effort" needed to break into self-sustaining
intensive growth. He quotes James Wood's image of population and food supply able to "leapfrog over
each other... but never gaining any permanent improvements in well-being." Ian Morris's study of
Aegean well-being (tenth–fourth centuries BCE) argues for real improvement in mortality, morbidity,
nutrition, housing, and clothing across that span, though no comparable systematic survey yet exists
for the Roman world.

## Urbanization as a Population Sink

The Roman empire supported roughly 2,000 cities — more than 400 in Italy against only about 50 in
Egypt despite comparable populations, since Egyptian "villages" were often larger than Italian
"cities." Aggregate urban population across the empire is estimated at 7–9 million, roughly
one-eighth to one-ninth of the total. By the second century CE the five largest imperial cities
(Rome, up to 1 million; Alexandria, up to 500,000; Antioch, 150,000+; Carthage; Ephesus) together
held 1.5–2 million people; Rome alone was as populous as the twelve largest cities of 1500 Christian
Europe combined, a scale unmatched in Europe until London around 1800.

Scheidel, following Wrigley, treats cities as demographic **population sinks**: urban excess
mortality (more urban deaths than births, sustained only by continuous rural immigration) acted as a
Malthusian safety valve, absorbing rural natural population growth (roughly 0.1%/year or more) and
thereby delaying declines in marginal agricultural productivity. Over roughly a thousand years,
sustaining some 2,000 cities on this model may have required moving up to 40 million people from
countryside to city.

This urban-sink mechanism is Scheidel's entry into the long-running **"consumer city" (Sombart,
Weber, Finley) versus "producer city"** debate: he sides with Erdkamp in reading the consumer-city
model as describing the *mechanism* of surplus extraction and transfer, not a claim about economic
primitiveness, and holds it broadly applicable even where genuine urban manufacturing and trade
existed. Hansen's counter-model, the "farmer-citizen-city" (*Ackerbürgerstadt*), better fits many
archaic and classical Greek poleis where urban residents farmed land in the surrounding countryside
themselves. See [[consumer-city]] for the fuller treatment of this debate.

## The Roman Citizen-Count Controversy

Scheidel's chapter is a primary source for the long-running dispute over how to read the Augustan
census figures (4,063,000 citizens in 28 BCE, against 910,000 in 70/69 BCE) and what they imply for
Italy's total population — the "high count" versus "low count" debate. See
[[roman-population-census-count]] for the full controversy treatment.

## Significance

Scheidel's demographic framework underlies the volume's account of Greco-Roman
[[economic-efflorescence|economic efflorescence]]: population growth, urbanization, and slavery are
treated as interlocking parts of a single Malthusian-Boserupian system, rather than independent
developments.

## From Mitchell, Later Roman Empire (2015)

Source: [[mitchell-later-roman-empire-2015]].

- **Rome’s contraction.** Fourth-century Rome over 500,000 inhabitants; by mid-sixth century no more than a tenth—Procopius notes defenders could not man the 19 km walls in 537–8 and 546. Root cause was not only Gothic war and plague but acute loss of the tax-based food supply after Egyptian grain diverted to Constantinople and Vandal seizure of Carthage (439) cut African *annona* and tax-in-kind/cash to Rome; once Rome ceased being an imperial tax-consumer it had to buy food and population contracted toward ordinary large-city levels.
- **Next-tier Mediterranean giants.** Cautious ranges: Alexandria ~200,000–400,000; Antioch ~150,000–300,000; Carthage closer to Antioch than Alexandria. Size of Alexandria and Carthage is non-coincidental—they were the grain export ports for the two capitals, guaranteeing employment and bulk grain presence that buffered acute urban shortage.
