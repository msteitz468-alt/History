# British Tactical Learning Curve, 1916–18 (BEF)

Analytical concept describing the sustained, army-wide process by which the British Expeditionary Force on the Western Front developed and disseminated modern industrial-era small-unit tactics, artillery support methods, and combined-arms techniques between the Somme offensive of 1916 and the mobile operations of 1918.

## Core Elements (per Griffith)
- Shift from dense linear or wave assaults to flexible platoon/section all-arms groups (rifle sections, Lewis gun, grenadiers, rifle bombers) capable of independent manoeuvre under the protection of creeping barrages.
- Evolution of artillery from prolonged destructive bombardments (often ineffective against deep dug-outs) toward neutralising fire, precise counter-battery work, predicted fire, and “deep battle” support.
- Institutional mechanisms: training schools at multiple levels (“university of higher tactics”), manuals such as SS 143 (February 1917), and deliberate dissemination by GHQ and formation commanders (notably Ivor Maxse).
- Widespread rather than élite application: techniques pioneered by specialist raiding/bombing teams in 1915–16 were merged back into the mass infantry once validated.
- Explicit contrast with popular narratives that confine “tactical modernity” to German stormtroopers or Dominion/tank formations.

## Historiography Note
The “learning curve” thesis is contested in degree and timing. Griffith emphasises continuity and breadth within the BEF from 1915 onward and downplays the uniqueness of 1918 German methods. Other scholars stress remaining limitations in British practice, command style, or the decisive contribution of material superiority in 1918. The concept remains central to operational analysis of the Hundred Days and to comparisons of Allied vs Central Powers doctrinal dynamics.
