# The Late Roman City and the Fate of the Curial Class

The evolution of **municipal life** and the **curial class** (the city councillors, *decuriones*)
between the Severans and Constantine — and the long-debated question of whether the
[[third-century-crisis|third-century crisis]] produced a "crisis of the cities." Treated in
[[bowman-cah-v12-2005|CAH XII]] Ch. 9 (**Jean-Michel Carrié**), one of the volume's most strongly
**revisionist** chapters.

## The revisionist thesis: continuity, not crisis

Carrié rejects the old model in which the crisis brought "statization," militarization, and the
collapse of the self-governing city into a coerced, hereditary "caste system." His central
conclusions:

- The **Severi were continuators of the Antonines**, not precursors of Diocletian; the real
  institutional **break came only with Constantine**. The tetrarchy is a transitional phase, not the
  birth of the "dominate."
- The supposed Severan revolutions — the abolition of local laws for Roman law, the shift from taxing
  communities to taxing individuals, the enlargement of the procurators' powers — are **largely
  illusory**, products of reading the *Digest*'s Severan jurists as innovators when they restated
  older principles.
- The **"militarization" of the administration is a myth**: soldiers had long served as
  administrators, and the third-century wars actually *reduced* the army's presence in the *officia*,
  making it easy for Diocletian to **separate** civil and military careers and Constantine to unify
  bureaucrats and soldiers in a single (carefully distinguished) *militia*.

## The cities as relays of the state

Carrié's organizing insight: the cities were increasingly **used to make the imperial "public
services" function**. The Severan promotion of cities (from *vici* to *municipia* to *coloniae*, the
grant of councils to Egypt's metropoleis) was not liberalism but a way to **save on public
expenditure** by loading duties onto private individuals under the principle of **collective
responsibility**. The [[constitutio-antoniniana-212|Constitutio Antoniniana]] (212) levelled
municipal statuses and made all cities **Roman**, but — crucially — it **preserved local laws** (as
*customs*) and **did not abolish the poll tax** or city status. After Caracalla the right of **appeal
to the emperor** became a privilege of the **honestiores**, sharpening the status divide that now
mattered more than citizenship.

## The curiae: idoneitas, not heredity

Against the standard picture of a curial class crushed into hereditary servitude, Carrié argues:

- Curial recruitment rested on **idoneitas** (wealth and standing), **not legal heredity** — imperial
  law **never made curial membership hereditary**. Continuity within families was common but never
  automatic; there remained real **mobility** at the lower levels (the "reaper of Mactar"; the
  Canusium and Timgad alba show curiae *not* depopulated, indeed enlarged).
- **Electoral competition** for magistracies (*honores*) coexisted with authoritarian nomination to
  liturgies (*munera*) throughout — "more about permanencies than evolution."
- The genuine strain appears not in magistracies but in **liturgies** — the mounting demands of the
  *annona militaris* and tax-collection from the 260s, which made the *boulai* struggle to fill
  liturgical posts.

## The Constantinian break

The real change came with **Constantine**: the **confiscation of city revenues** to the *res
privata* (a fraction repaid), the death penalty for fiscal informers, and above all the powerful
**pull of imperial service** — the wholesale promotion of rich *equites* to **senatorial rank**, the
opening of the order, and (with toleration) the **clergy** as new exempt categories. Curiales sought
escape from the *munera personalia* not because of ruinous expense but to gain the **new forms of
social prestige** offered by the central *officia*, the senate, and the church — the "essential
contradiction" of a **"twofold city,"** in which strengthening the empire's service weakened the
cities that supplied it.

## West and East

Carrié stresses the persistent difference between the **Latin *civitas*** of the West (more loosely
tied to a regional capital, its elites oriented to landowning values) and the **Greek *polis*** of
the hellenized East (with deep civic traditions). The western provinces' earlier "monopolization" of
local life by landed oligarchies — and their being first to detach from Rome in the fifth century —
shows, he concludes, that **"the empire's fate was caught up with that of its cities."**

## Significance

This chapter reframes the late-Roman city: not a victim of crisis sliding into a frozen caste order,
but a **resilient institution** that the state deliberately preserved (limiting its own bureaucracy)
even as the rise of imperial and ecclesiastical careers slowly drained the curial elite. It is a
central plank of the volume's broader case for **continuity over catastrophe**.

## The fourth-century city: the "end of the classical city" (CAH XIII)

[[cameron-cah-v13-1998|CAH XIII]] Ch. 12 (**Bryan Ward-Perkins**) carries the story into the fourth and
fifth centuries. The ***civitas*** remained the **basic administrative unit** — defined by its **curia**,
not its size — and its administrative role was even **enhanced** by the demands of imperial taxation. But
the **curiales lost power and status** to the imperial service in two ways: the **confiscation of civic
revenues** and a sharply **increased land-tax** drained local wealth to the centre; and the new
**superstructure** of multiplied provinces, dioceses and prefectures put real authority in the hands of
imperial governors. Civic office turned from a coveted honour into a **burden to escape** (the *munera*,
enforced by floggings; the 192 laws *De decurionibus* mostly aimed at stopping decurions fleeing into
imperial service).

The decisive consequence Ward-Perkins traces is the **collapse of aristocratic civic munificence** — the
death of the **"classical city."** Once power and prestige lay in imperial careers, aristocrats saw "little
gain in spending on traditional civic amenities"; the epigraphic habit of recording local office and
benefaction **virtually disappeared**, replaced by inscriptions recording only imperial titles. The pace
varied regionally: **Italy and the North** lost the tradition early (only Rome's senators still building);
the **East** and **Africa** preserved a reduced munificence into the fifth century (revived briefly by
Valentinian I's restitution of 374) — but "very small beer" beside the High Empire, and **nothing survived
the Vandal invasion**. The other great transformation was the **Christianization of the cityscape** —
basilicas and episcopal complexes replacing the old monumental fora as the new focus of urban life and
patronage.

## Update — the death of the classical city (425–640, CAH XIV)

[[cameron-cah-v14-2000|CAH XIV]] (Liebeschuetz, Ch. 8) carries the story to the seventh century. Strikingly,
despite the fragmentation of the West, cities **everywhere — barbarian kingdoms and surviving empire alike —
evolved along parallel lines**, regional differences being "a matter of timing rather than substance."

- **Classical politics perished.** Self-government by the constitutionally defined **curiae** died out
  everywhere; cities came to be run by **undefined groups of powerful notables** (and, increasingly, the
  bishop). Libanius had already diagnosed why: the most powerful councillors did not mind their colleagues
  leaving, since it **concentrated power in their own hands**, while ordinary decurions fled the council's
  burdens (floggings, bankruptcy, tax-collection) for safer careers in the imperial service or church. The
  government legislated against the trend right up to Justinian — to little effect; by Anastasius's reign the
  notables were in control in the East, and new law simply recognized the fact (a factor in the Justinianic
  revival). In the West the provincial system vanished and kings ruled cities through **counts** (often local
  magnates), so cities probably enjoyed **more independence than at any time since Diocletian**.
- **The rise of the bishop.** The decisive positive development, East and West, was the growth of
  **episcopal power** and the **Christianization of the cityscape** — monumental churches and episcopal
  complexes becoming the structural and patronal heart of the town, especially within the contracted walled
  circuits.
- **Regional survival.** The full classical city (monuments, games, literate élite) survived best in the
  **East** — western/southern Asia Minor, Syria, Arabia, Palestine, Egypt — **right up to the Arab
  conquests** and beyond; in the West it lasted in Africa **only to the Vandal conquest**, and in southern
  Spain, Provence and (especially northern) Italy. A late-fifth/sixth-century **revival**, mainly in church
  building, appeared first in the East, then Ostrogothic Italy, Merovingian Gaul, and last Visigothic Spain.

This is CAH XIV's verdict on the long transition from the **classical *polis*** to the **medieval
bishop's town**. Source: [[cameron-cah-v14-2000]] (Ch. 8, J. H. W. G. Liebeschuetz).
