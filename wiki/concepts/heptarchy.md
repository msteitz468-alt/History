# Heptarchy

The **Heptarchy** ("rule of seven") is the traditional historiographical label for the
political order of pre-Viking Anglo-Saxon England, conventionally listing seven kingdoms —
[[kingdom-of-kent|Kent]], [[kingdom-of-wessex|Wessex]], [[kingdom-of-northumbria|Northumbria]],
[[mercia|Mercia]], [[kingdom-of-east-anglia|East Anglia]], Essex and Sussex.

## Emic vs Etic

The term is wholly **etic**: it is a later schematisation, not a category used by the
peoples it describes. [[sources/kirby-earliest-english-kings-2000|Kirby]] treats it as a
convenient shorthand that seriously distorts the reality. The "seven kingdoms" were never
a stable set: the number of polities was both larger (the [[tribal-hidage|Tribal Hidage]]
lists some thirty-odd peoples — Hwicce, Magonsæte, Middle Angles, Lindsey, the various
*-sæte* and *-ware* groups) and, at the level of genuine power, smaller, since at any
moment effective dominance lay with two or three over-kingdoms while the rest were
clients or sub-kingdoms.

## Limitations and Critique

Kirby's objection is that the Heptarchy implies seven roughly equal, stable kingdoms
evolving in parallel, whereas the political field was a fluctuating hierarchy of
[[bretwalda|overlordship]] in which lesser peoples were repeatedly absorbed, detached and
re-absorbed. The model also flattens the crucial distinction between the *gens* (people)
and the kingdom, and obscures the survival of British polities. Modern scholarship retains
"Heptarchy" only as a loose conventional label, preferring "the Anglo-Saxon kingdoms" and
analysis in terms of over-kingship and tribute.
