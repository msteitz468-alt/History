# The Raja-mandala (the Circle of Kings)

The **raja-mandala** ("circle of kings") is the theory of **interstate relations** at the
heart of [[kautilya|Kautilya's]] **[[kautilya-arthashastra|Arthashastra]]** (Books
VI–VII) — a geometric model of friends and enemies arrayed in concentric rings around a
central would-be conqueror, governed by the famous principle that **your neighbour is your
enemy and your neighbour's neighbour is your friend**. Together with the six-fold policy
(*shadgunya*), it forms the most systematic ancient theory of *realpolitik* and the
ancestor of the Southeast Asian [[mandala]] (galactic-polity) concept.

## The vijigishu and the circle

The model centres on the **vijigishu** — the "would-be conqueror," the ambitious king who
is "the fountain of policy." Around him Kautilya arrays the other states by geography:

- The king **immediately bordering** the conqueror is, by definition, the **enemy**
  (*ari*) — contiguity breeds conflict.
- The king **beyond the enemy** (the enemy's neighbour) is the conqueror's natural
  **friend** (*mitra*) — they share an interest in pressing the state between them.
- The pattern continues outward in alternating rings: the friend's enemy, the friend's
  friend, and so on.
- To the **rear** lie a rearward enemy (*parshnigraha*), a rearward friend (*akranda*),
  and their respective allies.

A "natural enemy" is one of equal birth occupying contiguous territory; calamity makes an
enemy **assailable**, helplessness makes him **destructible**. The circle is dynamic — its
relationships shift with the fortunes and weaknesses of each *[[kautilyan-statecraft|saptanga]]*
state.

## The six-fold policy (shadgunya)

Within the circle the king chooses among **six measures** of foreign policy, applying each
according to relative strength:

1. **Sandhi** — peace / making a treaty (when weaker);
2. **Vigraha** — war / hostility (when stronger);
3. **Asana** — neutrality / staying quiet (when of equal strength);
4. **Yana** — marching / preparing for war (when much stronger);
5. **Samshraya** — alliance / seeking shelter with another (when too weak to stand alone);
6. **Dvaidhibhava** — the "double policy" — making peace with one while warring on another.

The choice is purely instrumental, calibrated to the balance of power among the
*saptanga* limbs of each state — "acquisition and security are dependent upon peace and
industry," and the six-fold policy is "the source of peace and industry." Books VIII–XIV
extend this into doctrines of war, sieges, and "secret means" (assassination, sowing
dissension, occult intimidation).

## Significance and afterlife

The raja-mandala is the classic ancient statement of **balance-of-power realism** —
comparable to, and far more systematic than, anything in Greco-Roman political writing on
interstate relations. Its image of concentric circles of power radiating from an
exemplary centre became, via the broader Indic political imagination, the conceptual
seed of the **[[mandala]]** model that O. W. Wolters and others use for the classical
**[[southeast-asia|Southeast Asian]]** state (Srivijaya, Angkor) — though there the
emphasis shifts from Kautilya's hard *realpolitik* of bordering enemies to a softer
model of overlapping circles of allegiance and prestige. The doctrine's modern revival
(in Indian strategic thought) keeps Kautilya's name attached to the realist tradition.

## From Miksic & Goh, *Ancient Southeast Asia* (2017)

Light touch: [[miksic-goh-ancient-southeast-asia-2017|Miksic & Goh]] document the **Southeast Asian reception** of the Indic title package rather than re-deriving Kautilya's geometry. Seasian rulers assumed *rājādhirāja* / *maharajadhiraja*, *cakravartin* ("wheel-turner"/universal monarch), and *Dharmaraja*; Early Classic Cambodian *pon* also gained power by digging wells/ponds. The most famous overt *cakravartin* self-portrayal is Jayavarman II's 802 ceremony elevating him to that status — Sanskrit manuals emphasize the righteous ruler's qualities, not territorial borders or constitutional legitimacy. Angkorian titles evolved from *mratañ* / *Vrah kamrateñ añ* ("His Majesty"), first for gods, then via title inflation ~650 for men claiming kingship; rulers carried personal, reign, and posthumous names that complicate identification. Indian inscriptions' *sāmanta-maṇḍala* (circle of vassals of an overlord *rājādhirāja*) is noted, with the post-600 shift of *sāmanta* from king toward vassal/tributary chief in Indian feudalism historiography.
