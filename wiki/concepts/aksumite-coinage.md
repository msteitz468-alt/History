# Aksumite Coinage

## Definition

The **Aksumite coinage** was the numismatic system of the [[aksum|Kingdom of Aksum]], produced in three metals (gold, silver, copper) from approximately AD 270 to approximately AD 630–640. It constitutes the **primary chronological spine** for identifying and sequencing Aksumite kings: since no Aksumite royal tomb or stela bears a name, and few inscriptions can be securely dated, the coin sequence is the principal tool for establishing the royal succession. The coinage is also the most direct evidence for the ideological transition from pre-Christian astral symbolism to Christianity under Ezana.

Aksumite coinage was the **first sub-Saharan African coinage** to be produced by any state, and one of the few non-Mediterranean coinages of Late Antiquity. Its gold issues circulated internationally; its copper coins appear to have served primarily local exchange.

## Historical Context

### Origins (c. AD 270)
The coinage was initiated under **Endybis**, the first named king on coins. Both the precise date and the inspiration for the decision are debated. The coinage was clearly inspired by Graeco-Roman and possibly South Arabian numismatic practice — it adopted the Hellenistic convention of a royal portrait, Greek-language inscriptions, and standardised weights anchored to the Roman aureus. Affinities with Himyaritic (South Arabian) coinage are notable in module size and the practice of depicting a royal head on both sides.

Die-preparation and striking techniques were almost certainly derived from overseas expertise, but coin designs were essentially local. All known Aksumite coins carry portraits on both obverse and reverse — a feature shared with some Himyaritic issues but unusual in Graeco-Roman coinage. Gold coins framed the royal portraits with **cereal-stalks** (usually identified as wheat, possibly barley) on both sides.

### Pre-Christian Phase
Under Endybis and early successors, a **crescent-and-disc** symbol appeared centrally above the portrait on all gold coins — an astral symbol widespread in the ancient Near East and South Arabia. Inscriptions were in **Greek**. Endybis' king wore a headcloth (*cap*) on both sides of the coin; his immediate successor wore a **crown/tiara** on the obverse while retaining the headcloth on the reverse — a distinction maintained throughout the gold series.

The coinage of **Aphilas** (c. AD 300) introduced several innovations: multiple denominations in each metal (following a South Arabian subdivision system), and — for the first time — **selective gilding** of portions of the design after striking. This gilding (applied using mercury amalgam) is unique to the Aksumite series; it was exceedingly labour-intensive and its rationale is unknown. Aphilas' coins also introduced the full-face portrait on some denominations.

**wzb** (Wazeba), successor to Aphilas, made a striking departure: all his coins were inscribed in **Ge'ez** rather than Greek. Only two gold specimens are known. The reason for this shift may have been to promote local acceptance of coinage among a population accustomed to non-monetary exchange. **Ousanas/Ella Amida** resumed Greek inscriptions; a transitional coin combining a Ge'ez-inscribed obverse of wzb with a Greek reverse of Ousanas is attested — apparently a formal issue, not an error.

### Ezana and the Christian Transition
The reign of **Ezana** (who adopted Christianity, conventionally dated to the 330s–340s CE) is marked on coins by the replacement of the crescent-and-disc with the **Christian cross** above the royal portrait (Fig. 71 in Phillipson). This is one of the earliest datable instances of Christian symbolism on any coinage. Some silver and copper coins of Ezana lack any religious symbol — suggesting a transitional moment. Study of the gold dies indicates that the craftsman who engraved the last crescent-and-disc issue may be the same as the engraver of the first cross-bearing issue, testifying to the abruptness and deliberateness of the change.

Post-Ezana, **anonymous issues** (inscribed only "king" or "king of the Aksumites") proliferated, carrying a cross on some but not all specimens. The most common copper type bore the Greek motto ΤΟΥΤΟ ΑΡΕCΗ ΤΗ ΧωΡΑ (*may this be pleasing in the countryside*) — the word ΧωΡΑ (countryside/rural) explicitly distinguishing the rural circulation of copper from the urban elite sphere. From the mid-sixth century, similar mottos in Ge'ez became common on copper.

### Fifth Century
Following Ezana, the coinage entered a period of "monotonous stability" lasting until the late fifth century, with Greek inscriptions across all denominations. An exception: the reign of **mhdys** (possibly Matthias), known only from coins, where all issues bear Ge'ez legends. His unique gold coin (Staatsliche Münzsammlung, Munich) depicts a standing figure in Aksumite regalia on the obverse, and a winged Victory figure (copied from contemporary Byzantine coins) on the reverse — an unusual blend of local and imperial imagery. The copper coins of mhdys bear the motto "by this cross he will conquer" — a direct reference to Constantine's Milvian Bridge vision.

Die-engraving quality deteriorated across the fifth century. Errors in Greek inscriptions — omitted or confused letters — increased; it is apparent that after the reigns of Endybis and Aphilas, die-engravers were no longer literate in Greek.

### Sixth and Seventh Centuries (Kaleb and after)
Under **Kaleb** (early sixth century), a linguistic division was formalised: gold coins retained (increasingly corrupt) Greek inscriptions; silver and copper switched to **Ge'ez**. This pattern was maintained until the end of the series. Standards of production deteriorated across the board: metal fineness declined, portraiture became clumsy, inscriptions shrank to accommodate larger text.

**Ioel**, **Hataz**, and **Gersem** (second half of sixth century) reintroduced the full-face portrait, flanked on some issues by a cross on each side — possibly inspired by Justinianic Byzantine 40-nummi pieces (introduced 538/9). Late sixth-century kings wzn and agd issued coins with a distinctive reverse: an arch on pillared supports enclosing a gilded cross. **Armah** issued coins with a large cross flanked by two smaller ones (Calvary). The date of the Armah series is disputed between mid-sixth and mid-seventh centuries.

### End of the Coinage (c. AD 630–640)
The Aksumite coinage ended as suddenly as it had begun. The most likely cause was the Arab conquest of the Red Sea trade routes in the second quarter of the seventh century, which ended the international commercial relationships that had made the gold coinage viable and necessary. Post-cessation sites have yielded only Arabic gold and silver coins (at Debra Damo, ranging from the late seventh to mid-tenth centuries), with no evidence that any replacement local coinage was attempted.

## Significance

1. **Chronological tool**: The coin sequence is the primary framework for Aksumite royal succession and periodisation — indispensable because no other systematic royal record survives.
2. **African numismatic primacy**: Aksumite coinage was the first coinage produced by any sub-Saharan African state, and one of the longest-running non-Mediterranean coinages of Late Antiquity.
3. **Religious marker**: The switch from crescent-and-disc to the Christian cross under Ezana is among the best-documented material expressions of royal Christianisation in the ancient world.
4. **Linguistic evidence**: Shifts between Greek and Ge'ez inscriptions across the series provide evidence for the changing role of Hellenistic cultural reference within the kingdom.
5. **International trade indicator**: The gold coinage's weight standards (anchored to the Roman aureus), its distribution in Yemen and Egypt, and its debasement trajectory all index Aksum's integration into — and eventual exclusion from — Late Antique Indian Ocean commerce.

## Limitations and Debates

- **Start date**: The date of c. AD 270 for the first Endybis issues rests on stylistic comparison and weight analysis rather than documentary evidence; it is broadly accepted but not proven independently.
- **Royal sequence**: The coin sequence implies a rough royal succession but cannot establish exact reign lengths, co-regencies, or gaps. Several kings are known from coins alone, with no corroborating textual or epigraphic evidence.
- **Database size**: The surviving corpus of Aksumite coins is small relative to Mediterranean coinages. Single new discoveries can necessitate major revisions. The al-Madhariba hoard (Yemen, c. 1980) more than doubled the known Aksumite gold corpus at a stroke.
- **Methodology inconsistencies**: The better-preserved gold has been studied in greater detail than the more numerous copper coins; minor gold variants are sometimes listed as distinct types while copper variants are lumped together, complicating comparative analysis.
- **End date**: Whether the coinage ended c. 630 or c. 640, and the precise connection to Arab disruption of Red Sea trade, cannot be established to a tighter interval.

## Historiography

The foundational scholarly work is the 1906 Deutsche Aksum-Expedition documentation of coins recovered at Aksum (Littmann et al. 1913). Stuart Munro-Hay's extensive numismatic work, culminating in *Aksumite Coinage* (1984) and the catalogue co-authored with Juel-Jensen (1995), established the standard typological framework, though Hahn's system (1983, updated 2000) is in some ways less confusing for multi-denomination issues. Wolfgang Hahn's ongoing research and updates through 2010 have refined the sequence. The al-Madhariba hoard study (Munro-Hay 1989c) was a transformative publication. Blet-Lemarquand et al. (2001) and others have provided metallurgical analyses of silver fineness. The interpretation of coin iconography in relation to the Christianisation of Aksum has attracted significant scholarly interest; Phillipson's *Foundations* (2012) integrates the numismatic evidence with the broader archaeological framework.
