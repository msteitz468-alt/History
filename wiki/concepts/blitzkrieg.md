# Blitzkrieg

"Lightning war": operational doctrine emphasizing speed, surprise, and concentration of mobile forces (armor + mechanized infantry + close air support) to achieve rapid breakthroughs and deep encirclements (Kesselschlacht), bypassing strongpoints and disrupting command/logistics before the enemy can react. Contrasted with attritional or linear warfare of WWI.

## Key Elements
- Combined arms at operational level.
- Decentralized command (Auftragstaktik).
- Radio and air-ground integration.
- Psychological shock.

Practiced with great effect in Poland (1939), France/Low Countries (1940), and early Barbarossa, but strained by overextension, logistics, and Soviet recovery. Debated whether a fully coherent "doctrine" or emergent success.

## Hitler's short-war logistics conception (Paxton and Hessler, 2011)

[[paxton-europe-twentieth-century-2011|Paxton and Hessler]] frame Blitzkrieg as more than a tactical innovation — it was Hitler's structural answer to the problem that Germany "must win quickly." German logistics experts urged full conversion to a war economy, which would not be ready until roughly 1943; Hitler instead chose to accumulate matériel for one decisive strike at a time, sparing German civilians the sacrifices of a full war economy for as long as possible. The evidence for this short-war conception is concrete: Germany built no long-range bombers; the U-boat fleet stood at only 57 submarines (18 of them ocean-going) at the war's start; German women were kept at home rather than mobilized into war industry, unlike in Britain; marble was still being imported for Berlin building projects as late as 1942; consumer production stood only 3% below peacetime levels at the start of 1942; and most armament plants remained on a single shift as late as April 1942. Note Overy's *War and Economy in the Third Reich* directly challenges this "Blitzkrieg-economy" thesis (see Historiography below). Source: [[paxton-europe-twentieth-century-2011]].

The panzer division itself was conceived as a self-contained combined-arms force — some 300 tanks plus matching supporting arms (motorized infantry, artillery, engineers) moving at the same pace, with a fighter screen and Stuka dive-bombers (fitted with sirens for psychological shock) providing close air support; conventional horse-drawn infantry units followed behind to hold and consolidate gained ground.

## Historiography

Overy's *War and Economy in the Third Reich* challenges the "Blitzkrieg economy" reading Paxton and Hessler summarize above — Overy argues German rearmament was not deliberately limited to a shallow, short-war footing but was constrained by genuine resource bottlenecks, and that the regime intended deeper, more total mobilization from early on. The dispute bears on how deliberate vs. improvised German war planning was through 1941–42. Source: [[paxton-europe-twentieth-century-2011]].
