# Frankish Influence on Thirteenth-Century Byzantine Architecture

## Definition and Scope

Following the Fourth Crusade (1204), Latin Frankish rulers controlled much of
Byzantine territory for several decades — the Principality of the Morea (Peloponnese),
the Duchy of Athens, the island of Crete (Venice), and Cyprus. Western Gothic
architecture, then at the height of its dynamism under Louis IX of France, was
simultaneously the most impressive architectural tradition in Latin Christendom.
This concept addresses how much — and what kind — of Gothic influence Byzantine
builders accepted during the thirteenth century.

Bouras's study examines two distinct monument groups: (1) purely Gothic churches
erected by Latins for the Latin rite, and (2) Orthodox Greek churches built during
the same period in areas of Latin occupation, examined for traces of Frankish
influence.

## Key Arguments

**The main finding: influence was real but insubstantial, eclectic, and confined to
decorative details.** Bouras concludes that Frankish influence on Byzantine architecture
in the thirteenth century "was insubstantial and is evinced only in certain limited
and isolated formal elements in buildings that preserve the general style of the
mid-Byzantine period." These elements appear on mainland Greece and certain islands;
in Constantinople, Asia Minor, Thrace, and Macedonia they are completely absent.

**Geographic focus.** The Peloponnese (Morea) and Attica show the most Western
influence; Cyprus shows the most thorough eventual absorption of Gothic forms but
developed this only from around 1370. The Empire of Nicaea in Asia Minor and the
re-captured Constantinople show virtually none.

**What Byzantine builders did adopt.** Byzantine churches in the occupied regions
show selective adoption of Gothic decorative vocabulary: pointed arch windows and
doorways with jamb moldings; slender engaged columns at corners; Gothic-style
column capitals with crockets; pointed arcosolia; ribbed groin vaults (rarely and
in restricted size); colonnettes forming Gothic portals; dog-tooth ornament in
cornices. These features appear as "isolated formal elements" added to otherwise
traditional mid-Byzantine structures.

**What Byzantine builders refused to import.** The defining achievements of Gothic
architecture — vertical extension of interior space, accentuated dynamism of facade
and interior through structural vaulting, the resolution of main supports into
colonnettes corresponding to ribs — met with "totally negative" Byzantine response.
The ribbed cross-vault, when it appeared, was never widely disseminated. Byzantine
builders had no interest in the structural logic of Gothic; only in its ornamental
vocabulary.

**The Paregoritissa (Arta, 1294–96) as the closest case.** The church of the despot
of Epiros is an unusual case: it has genuine interior dynamism and Gothic formal
elements (ornamental trefoil arches, representational reliefs in Gothic style). But
Bouras shows that its acrobatic dome-support system uses Early Christian spolia
columns on corbels — improvised and original rather than derived from Gothic
structural technique.

**Typological independence.** Proposals that the Frankish presence introduced new
church types (elongated plans, bell towers, three-aisled timber-roofed basilicas,
cross barrel-vaulted churches) are refuted by Bouras: all these types have Byzantine
antecedents in Greece from the tenth century onward, and were technically simpler
solutions to dome-construction problems rather than imports.

**Reasons for the limited influence.** Two factors explain the boundary. First,
Byzantine architecture was "mature and self-contained, meeting to the full the
church building's liturgical needs and dogmatic symbolism" — a conservative climate
with no internal pressure to innovate toward Gothic. Second, the "great cultural and
religious divide between the invading Crusaders and the locals": donor inscriptions
from over seventy thirteenth-century Greek churches represent all social strata —
despots, local aristocracy, bishops, ordinary people — and reveal Orthodox identity
as a cultural barrier. Chatzidakis's parallel observation about Byzantine painting
applies: "characterized by the absence of Western influence."

**Selective, pragmatic adoption.** The Byzantine craftsmen who adopted Gothic
details often transformed them: at Blachernae in Elis, dentil courses of purely
Byzantine technique accompany a slender Gothic molding; at Hagios Georgios in
Aipeia, a double Gothic window replaces its standard quatrefoil rosette with a
Byzantine ceramic bowl. This points to ornamental variety as the motive, not
ideological or structural attraction to Gothic.

## Evidence and Examples

**Gothic churches built by Latins in Byzantine lands:**
- Hagia Sophia and Hagios Iakovos, Andravida (Peloponnese); Panagia and Hagios
  Nikolaos, Isova; Zarakas monastery, Stymphalia; churches on Crete built by the
  Venetians; the cathedral church of Hagia Paraskevi at Chalkis.
- Cyprus: an outstanding Gothic flowering from 1209, originating in northern France,
  unique in the East.
- The Cistercians at Daphni (Attica) remodeled the western stoa of the Byzantine
  katholikon and may have built the square cloister to its south.

**Orthodox churches with Gothic elements (ten in the Peloponnese alone):**
- Blachernae monastery, Elis: pointed arch windows, slender engaged columns,
  rain spouts, other Gothic elements in the western part.
- Hagios Georgios, Androusa: Gothic doorways with pointed arches and jamb molds,
  on a mid-Byzantine masonry building.
- Dormition of the Theotokos, Merbaka (Argolid): column capitals with crockets,
  jamb molds on arch porches, Gothic engaged columns in the sanctuary window —
  dated to the late thirteenth century.
- Omorphe Ekklesia, Galatsi (Athens): single-aisled chapel covered by three purely
  Gothic groin vaults.
- Panagia Paregoritissa, Arta (1294–96): Gothic trefoil arches, representational
  reliefs in Gothic style, unusual dome-support system.
- Hagia Theodora, Arta: outer stoa covered with Gothic-style pointed ribbed
  groin vaults.

## Scholarly Significance

Bouras's study demonstrates that the cultural encounter of the Frankish occupation,
despite a century of direct physical contact, produced no structural or typological
transformation in Byzantine ecclesiastical architecture. The adoption was ornamental
and eclectic — a taste for variety within an unchanged formal tradition. This
"micro-effect" parallels Grabar's finding for Islamic art: the Crusade encounter
produced far less cultural synthesis than the duration of contact might suggest.
The reason in both cases was a pre-existing, self-confident local tradition combined
with a sharp religious-cultural boundary.

The essay also establishes that the Palaiologos revival after 1261 (culminating in
the churches of Mistra) owed nothing to Gothic influence — it was an internal
Byzantine development.
