# Sachkritik (Delbrück)

**Sachkritik** ("thing-criticism" or "objective criticism") is Hans Delbrück's methodological innovation: the use of concrete military, logistical, and institutional realities as an independent check on literary sources.

## Core Principles

- Literary numbers and narratives (Herodotus on the Persian invasions, Polybius/Livy on Cannae, Caesar on his campaigns) must be tested against what was physically possible given:
  - Available agricultural surplus and pasture in the campaign theater
  - March rates and daily marching capacity of formed troops
  - Frontage and depth required by contemporary tactical systems (hoplite phalanx, sarissa phalanx, manipular legion)
  - Ration and water requirements for men and animals
  - Road and terrain constraints
- The historian's "objective judgment" (acquired by studying better-documented later periods) is applied to earlier, sparser evidence.
- Delbrück begins continuous, reliable military history with the Persian Wars precisely because only then do the sources provide enough detail for this cross-checking to work.

## Relation to Ranke and Clausewitz

Delbrück combined Leopold von Ranke's demand to clear away legend with Carl von Clausewitz's insistence that war has its own grammar but must remain subordinate to policy. The result is a military historiography that is both source-critical and strategically analytical.

## Influence and Controversy

- Established the modern operational/institutional approach to ancient warfare still used in staff-college teaching.
- Frequently produced sharp downward revisions of ancient army sizes (e.g., at Marathon and Plataea).
- Generated fierce debate with traditional philologists and with the Prussian/German General Staff historians who preferred heroic-scale numbers and decisive-battle doctrine.
- Directly informs later works in the collection (Archer Jones, Goldsworthy, the West Point histories, van Creveld on logistics as constraint).

## Limitations

Later archaeology, epigraphy, and demographic studies have sometimes supported, sometimes modified Delbrück's specific numbers. His method remains the standard first test even where details are revised.

## Medieval Application (Vol. III, *Medieval Warfare*)

Delbrück's [[delbruck-medieval-warfare-vol-3-1982|third volume]] turns Sachkritik on the Middle Ages, with two results that reshaped the field:

- **The systematic demolition of chronicle army sizes.** Reckoning by road capacity, march distance, and the time available to assemble levies, Delbrück cut the sources' figures by an order of magnitude or more: **Bouvines (1214)** — against scholarly figures of 100,000+, "neither of the armies was stronger than 8,000 men," possibly only ~5,000; **Bannockburn (1314)** — Fordun's 680,000 English is absurd, and "it is still quite possible… the superiority in numbers was on the side of the Scots"; **Nikopol (1396)** — not 400,000 Turks but "between 11,000 and 12,000 men"; **Crécy (1346)** — the English ~14,000–20,000 (from Wetewang's Calais roster), not the chroniclers' hosts. His standing joke: one chronicler gave the Christian army at Nikopol as 100,000 but its losses as 200,000.
- **The "tactical body" as the analytic key.** Sachkritik here is not only about numbers but about *what was organisationally possible*. Delbrück's signature medieval thesis is that the armoured knight fought as an individual, so the disciplined **tactical body** of antiquity dissolved — "the decisive reason for the change was the loss of the tactical body" — and was **reborn** only in the Swiss pike phalanx and the English longbow formation. He likewise dissolves cherished legends by source-genealogy: the **Arnold von Winkelried** sacrifice at Sempach is shown to be a sixteenth-century literary accretion that "attributes their own [Swiss] tactics to the enemy."
