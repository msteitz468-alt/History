# Fabian Strategy

The strategy of **deliberate battle-avoidance and attrition** — shadowing and
harassing a tactically superior enemy while refusing decisive engagement, trading
space and time to exhaust him. Named for [[fabius-maximus|Q. Fabius Maximus]],
dictator in 217 BCE; analysed in [[astin-cah-v8-1989|CAH VIII]] Ch. 3 (Briscoe).

## Definition and scope

The strategy is **not** mere passivity. As Briscoe formulates it (correcting the
caricature in the sources), the Fabian policy was that **pitched battles were to be
avoided in circumstances chosen by [[hannibal|Hannibal]] and favourable to him** —
not that battle was to be avoided absolutely. It allowed engagement on Roman terms
(Marcellus repeatedly fought Hannibal at Nola), and it depended on a positive
calculation:

- keep **vastly superior numbers** of legions in the field, forcing Hannibal
  either to divide his forces or to leave his Italian allies unsupported;
- exploit Rome's **manpower reserves** (citizen, Latin, allied) and **naval
  supremacy**, which together denied Hannibal reinforcement by land or sea;
- accept the heavy costs — the ravaging of Italy, enormous financial and human
  demands — in the confidence that Hannibal's army, unreplenishable, would
  eventually shrink below viability.

## Application

Adopted after **Lake Trasimene** (217) and, after the catastrophe at
**[[battle-of-cannae-216bce|Cannae]]** (216), governing Roman conduct of the war
in Italy until c. 210. Its most extreme form came in the immediate aftermath of
the two great defeats; thereafter Rome combined attrition with the systematic
**recapture of defecting cities** (Syracuse 212, Capua 211, Tarentum 209). The
strategy was vindicated by victory but was politically costly to Fabius, whose
caution was repeatedly challenged (the equalisation of Minucius' *imperium*, 217).

## Interaction

Contrasts with the **"forward strategy"** of [[scipio-africanus|the Scipios]] —
carrying the war to Spain and Africa rather than waiting in Italy — which CAH VIII
treats as the rival pole of Roman war policy and politics. The two are
complementary in hindsight: attrition held Italy while the forward strategy won the
war abroad.

## Limitations and critique

The strategy worked only because of the **structural preconditions** specific to
Rome — deep manpower, a loyal alliance system, and sea control. It is therefore not
a universally available doctrine but the exploitation of a particular strategic
asymmetry. As a generalised concept ("Fabian tactics") it has been applied widely
in later military and political theory, sometimes loosely.
