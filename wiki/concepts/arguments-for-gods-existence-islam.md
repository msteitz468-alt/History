## Definition and Scope

Classical Islamic theology developed a rich tradition of rational proofs for God's existence (*adillat wujud Allah*). These proofs were advanced primarily within *kalam* (scholastic theology) but were also pursued by the *falasifa* (Islamic Aristotelian philosophers). The main categories of argument, systematised by Fakhr al-Din al-Razi (d. 1210), are:

1. **Teleological arguments** (arguments from design / *dalil al-'inaya wa-l-ikhtira'*)
2. **Kalam cosmological arguments from creation ex nihilo** (*dalil al-huduth*)
3. **Arguments from particularisation** (*dalil al-takhsis*)
4. **The argument from contingency** (*dalil al-imkan*) — Avicenna's distinctive proof

The Kantian classification of arguments into teleological, cosmological, and ontological is explicitly applied by Shihadeh, providing a comparative framework with Western natural theology.

## The Prior Ethical Question: Why Prove God?

Before examining the proofs, the *mutakallimun* had to justify why rational proof (*nazar*) of God's existence is a *duty* at all. This question is essentially ethical and deeply contested — see [[islamic-theological-ethics]] for the full analysis.

In brief: Mu'tazilites held this duty to be grounded in rational ethics (reason alone establishes that one must avoid harm and seek knowledge of one's potential Judge); Ash'arites held it to be a religious (*shar'i*) duty established only by revealed religion; later Ash'arites under al-Razi's influence recast it as a prudential duty. The implications are significant: if God's existence can only be proved once one already has an obligation to prove it, and if that obligation itself requires God's existence, the whole enterprise faces a circularity that was openly acknowledged and debated. ([[winter-cambridge-companion-islamic-theology-2008]], ch. 10)

## Non-Rational and Competing Approaches

Not all classical scholars privileged rational proof:

- **Traditionalists** (including many Hanbalis): having a rationally unjustified but scripturally conformant belief in God is sufficient; *kalam* proofs are reprehensible innovations, "too obscure and precarious to serve as reliable bases for sound belief."
- **Ibn Taymiyya** (d. 1328): humans know God immediately by virtue of their innate primordial nature (*fitra*) instilled by God. For those with a sound *fitra*, no proof is needed; for those whose *fitra* has been corrupted, contemplation of God's "signs" in nature (*ayat*) suffices as a form of argument from design. He dismisses formal *kalam* proofs entirely.
- **Al-Ghazali** (d. 1111): secondarily a *mutakallim*, primarily a Sufi. Proofs may become compulsory for those plagued by doubt, but the superior method is Sufi spiritual discipline, which provides direct experiential "witnessing" (*mushahadah*) of God, rendering rational proofs "ultimately mediocre and primarily therapeutic."

## Teleological Arguments (Arguments from Design)

Arguments from design (*adillat al-'inaya wa-l-ikhtisar*) infer from manifestations of order and providence in the world to a God who produced them. They draw explicit qur'anic endorsement (e.g., 2:164 enumerating signs in the heavens, seas, winds, and alternation of day and night).

Fakhr al-Din al-Razi's taxonomy of the argument from design distinguishes two types of evidence:

- **Signs of providence** (*mana fi'*): advantages provided to conscious beings, pointing to a beneficent (*muhsin*) God.
- **Signs of order** (*ihkam, itqan*): masterly production in the world, pointing to a wise (*hakim*) and powerful God.

Al-Razi's "loci of discernment" (*mahall al-i'tibar*) span the entire observable cosmos: the human body and psyche, animals, plants, minerals, meteorological phenomena, elements, and in the celestial realm, the natures, magnitudes, and motions of spheres and planets. He illustrates with a refined argument from embryology: sperm, being simple or composed, cannot by any naturalist account explain the complex differentiated structure of the adult body — which requires a wise creator (Razi against Aristotelian pansomatic biology).

Crucially, Razi later concluded that arguments from design are **superior to all kalam and philosophical proofs** precisely because they are cumulative, engage sense and imagination alongside reason, and function simultaneously as cognitive evidences and as soteriological blessings that move the believer toward gratitude and submission. ([[winter-cambridge-companion-islamic-theology-2008]], ch. 10)

## Kalam Cosmological Arguments: Creation Ex Nihilo (*Dalil al-Huduth*)

The *kalam* argument par excellence is the argument from temporal origination:

> The world is temporally originated (*hadith*). All that is temporally originated requires a separate originator. Therefore, the world requires a separate originator. This originator must be pre-eternal.

**Support for the minor premise (the world is temporally originated)**: Early *mutakallimun* argued on the basis of classical *kalam* atomism: all beings (other than God) are bodies composed of atoms and accidents. The most widely used argument is the **argument from accidents** (*dalil al-a'rad*), apparently developed by the Mu'tazilite Abu Hashim al-Jubbai (d. 933):

> (a) Accidents exist in bodies. (b) Accidents are generated (have a beginning). (c) Bodies cannot be devoid of, or precede, accidents. (d) Therefore, bodies too are generated.

Averroes identified an equivocation in this argument: it establishes that individual bodies containing known generated accidents are themselves generated, not that the world as a whole is. It remains conceivable that the world is pre-eternal, involving infinitely regressing series of temporally originated things.

Later *mutakallimun*, starting from Juwayni (d. 1085), addressed this gap by arguing that an infinite past series of accidents is inconceivable. Proofs for the **impossibility of an actual infinite** were adopted partly from John Philoponus (d. c. 570): "When today's events are combined with past events, these will increase; without today's events, they will diminish. Increase and diminution in what is infinite are inconceivable. This indicates that [the series of past events] is finite with respect to its beginning." ([[winter-cambridge-companion-islamic-theology-2008]], ch. 10)

Razi ultimately examined all relevant arguments and admitted in a late work that no rational or revealed evidence decisively proves either creation or pre-eternity of the world — a sceptical conclusion that was influential on Ibn Taymiyya.

**Support for the major premise (what is temporally originated requires an originator)**: Two positions were taken by Mu'tazilites:

1. **Al-Ka'bi (d. 931)**: The premise is self-evident — when we see a building, we immediately know it had a builder. Razi rejects this: immediate knowledge of this premise would entail that every temporal event has a temporal cause (leading to infinite regress) and that it is preceded by time and matter — precisely what cannot be assumed.

2. **'Abd al-Jabbar (d. 1025) and most Mu'tazilites**: The premise is discursive and established by a complex analogy with human action (*qiyas al-shahid 'ala'l-gha'ib*). My act requires me as originator because it is temporally originated; the world is temporally originated; therefore it requires an originator. The "ground" (*'illa*) linking origination to requiring-an-originator is established by two *kalam* techniques: *al-sabr wa'l-taqsim* (elimination of alternative grounds) and *al-tard wa'l-'aks* (showing coextensiveness of dependence-on-me and coming-into-being).

Ash'arites rarely used the major-premise form of the argument directly — partly because their occasionalism (see [[occasionalism-islamic]]) denied that human acts are generated by human power, making the crucial analogy from observed human action inapplicable. They preferred the particularisation argument.

## Arguments from Particularisation (*Dalil al-Takhsis*)

Particularisation (*takhsis*) proofs are the main form used by early Ash'arites and widely used across schools. The underlying intuition: the world has no intrinsic necessity determining its features — it exists and is constituted as it is rather than otherwise. This requires a voluntary external agent who selects among possible alternatives.

Al-Baqillani (d. 1013) advanced two early forms:

1. **Temporal particularisation**: Identical things come into being at different times. Nothing intrinsic to a thing could make it more likely to occur at one moment than another. Therefore, there must be a voluntary agent (*mukhassis*) causing particular things to occur at particular moments.

2. **Qualitative particularisation**: Objects have different shapes, though each might conceivably have a different one. A thing cannot have a particular shape "by virtue of itself," since it would then have to have every possible shape simultaneously. A voluntary "shaper" (*musawwir*) must have determined the actual shape.

Both early arguments presuppose Ash'arite atomism and the rejection of natural causality: things do not come into being because of natural properties but because God continuously creates their accidents at each moment.

Juwayni extended particularisation to the world as a whole, eventually arriving at a crucial formulation:

> What is temporally originated is a possible existent (*ja'iz al-wujud*); for it is possible to conceive its existence rather than its non-existence, and possible to conceive its non-existence rather than its existence. Thus, since it is characterised by possible existence rather than possible non-existence, it will require a particularising factor, viz. the Creator. ([[winter-cambridge-companion-islamic-theology-2008]], ch. 10, citing Juwayni, *al-Shamil*)

This argument departs from the mere fact that the world *exists* (regardless of its composition), since it is equally possible that it did not. This brings Juwayni's modified particularisation argument close to Avicenna's argument from contingency and "paves the way for a synthesis of the two arguments in later kalam."

## Avicenna's Argument from Contingency (*Burhan al-Siddiqin*)

[[ibn-sina]]'s proof from contingency (*dalil al-imkan*) is the most philosophically sophisticated proof in the Islamic tradition and one of the most influential in the history of philosophy.

Avicenna claims it is a purely metaphysical proof, resting entirely on an analysis of *existence qua existence*, without consideration of physical features of the world:

> "Reflect on how our proof for the existence and oneness of the First and His being free from attributes did not require reflection on anything except existence itself... existence inasmuch as it is existence bears witness to Him." (*al-Isharat wa'l-Tanbihat*, cited in ch. 10)

**The proof's structure**:

1. There is existence (undeniable starting premise).
2. Every existent, by virtue of itself, is either *possibly existent* (*mumkin al-wujud*) or *necessarily existent* (*wajib al-wujud*).
3. A possibly existent being can exist or not exist; it requires an external cause to "tip the scale" toward existence — its existence is *necessitated* by its cause.
4. A series of possibly existent things cannot continue ad infinitum; it must terminate in a necessarily existent being.
5. If the indubitable existing thing is necessary in itself, that is God. If it is possible, it ultimately requires the necessary existent. Either way, a necessary existent (God) must exist.

**Is the proof ontological or cosmological?** Avicenna presents it as ontological (drawing only on a priori conceptions). But al-Razi (and modern scholars) argue it is in fact cosmological: the premise "there is existence" draws on our awareness of possible (contingent) existents, not necessary ones; the proof therefore "infers the existence of the necessary [existent] from the [actual] existence of the contingent." Two further moves are still needed to identify the necessary existent with the traditional monotheist's God: proving that the necessary existent is simple, one, and unique; and showing that nothing in the perceptible world is necessarily existent.

**Al-Razi's assessment**: despite these objections, the argument is a genuine advance — the first attempt to ground the proof in an analysis of existence itself. The synthesis of Juwayni's modified particularisation argument and Avicenna's contingency proof became the organising framework for later *kalam* proofs for God's existence.

## Historiography

William Craig, *The Kalam Cosmological Argument* (New York, 1979) brought the *huduth* arguments to wide attention in analytic philosophy of religion. Herbert Davidson, *Proofs for Eternity, Creation and the Existence of God in Medieval Islamic and Jewish Philosophy* (New York and Oxford, 1987) remains the standard historical survey. Michael Marmura, "Avicenna's Proof from Contingency for God's Existence in the *Metaphysics of the Shifa'*", *Mediaeval Studies* 42 (1980) addresses the cosmological-vs.-ontological dispute directly. Toby Mayer, "Ibn Sina's *Burhan al-Siddiqin*", *Journal of Islamic Studies* 12 (2001) argues for Avicenna's originality.

The field sits at the intersection of Islamic intellectual history, philosophy of religion, and the history of metaphysics. A significant recent development is the use of *kalam* arguments in contemporary analytic theism (Craig's "Kalam cosmological argument" in philosophy of religion is directly adapted from classical sources). This gives the medieval debates unexpected contemporary resonance.
