# The Biosphere and the Cold War

The years 1945–1991 (especially the late phase ~1970–1990) constituted “the most bizarre epoch in the history of the relationship between humanity and the biosphere.” World population more than doubled (to 5.3 billion by 1991); economic output roughly quintupled. Cheap energy enabled chopping mountaintops, felling billions of tropical trees, etc.

Green Revolution (scientific crop-breeding of dwarf wheat and rice plus fertilizers, pesticides, machinery, irrigation) doubled or quadrupled yields in many places. Originated with Japanese semi-dwarf genes and the Mexican program (Rockefeller/Ford funded). India became a food exporter; Southeast Asia (IR-8 rice) saw yields double in the Philippines and self-sufficiency achieved in Indonesia and Malaysia. China: despite Lysenkoism and Cultural Revolution setbacks, by 1990 “95 percent of Chinese rice and maize production came from recently developed high-yield varieties”; rice yields roughly doubled and wheat quadrupled 1950s levels. Africa was largely bypassed.

USSR: Lysenkoism blocked advances; Virgin Lands scheme (1954 onward, over 300,000 volunteers, 42 million ha in Kazakhstan and Siberia) initially boosted harvests but caused wind erosion, dust bowls, and nutrient depletion; maize campaign without proper hybrids/fertilizer failed; forced grain imports even from Romania.

Environmental costs were heavy: monocultures invited pests leading to persistent organochloride pesticides (bio-accumulative, eutrophication, dead zones); irrigation caused salinization and waterlogging; dams flooded villages and blocked fish migration; reduced agricultural biodiversity.

US Interstate highway system (1956 National Interstate and Defense Highways Act, 41,000 miles by 1991) — largest public works project in history, with explicit military rationale — produced direct effects (habitat fragmentation, erosion, pollution) and indirect (suburban sprawl, trucking dominance, car culture) while also enabling greater access to parks and environmentalism.

Soviet priority on railroads (BAM 1974–1984, 3,500 km, 60 new towns, military/China-border rationale) vs. very limited asphalt roads.

Nuclear-weapons production created extreme pollution (US Hanford released millions of curies; Soviet Maiak/Cheliabinsk-65 “most dangerously polluted place on earth,” at least 130 million curies released officially; Lake Karachai “most radioactive place on earth,” 24 times Chernobyl radioactivity). Overall nuclear programs “probably killed a few hundred thousand people, at most a couple of million” (mostly slow cancers); wastes remain lethal for >100,000 years.

Military-industrial expansion (USSR 30–40% of industrial production military; China Third Front 1964–72) plus agriculture, transport, and nuclear projects produced “ecological tumult.” Late Cold War surge in environmental awareness was intertwined with antinuclear and anti-chemical activism and dramatically accelerated by Chernobyl (1986).
