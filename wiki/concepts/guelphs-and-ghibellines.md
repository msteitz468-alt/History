# Guelphs and Ghibellines

**Guelphs** (pro-papal) and **Ghibellines** (pro-imperial) were the two great factional labels
that divided the city-states of northern and central Italy in the thirteenth and fourteenth
centuries. [[abulafia-ncmh-v5-2000|NCMH V]] (Louis Green on Florence, Ch. 15d; John Pryor on
the maritime republics; David Abulafia on Sicily) shows how the labels began as a reflection of
the **papal–imperial struggle** but quickly became vehicles for **local faction and
inter-city rivalry** that long outlived their original meaning.

## Origins

The names are Italian corruptions of the German dynastic war-cries: **Guelf** from **Welf** (the
house of Otto IV, the papacy's candidate), **Ghibelline** from **Waiblingen**, a Hohenstaufen
castle (the party of **[[frederick-ii-hohenstaufen|Frederick II]]**). The terms entered Italian
politics only after **Gregory IX** revived the papal–imperial conflict (from 1227) and Frederick
II's victory at **Cortenuova (1237)** drew the communes into broadly **pro-papal Guelf** and
**pro-imperial Ghibelline** blocs. In Florence, the chroniclers traced the local feud to the
murder of **Buondelmonte dei Buondelmonti** (1216) — a pre-existing aristocratic vendetta onto
which the wider labels were grafted.

## From Ideology to Faction

Green and Pryor both stress that the labels were largely **flags of convenience**:

- In **Pisa**, the Visconti faction was tagged "Guelf" and the Gherardesca "Ghibelline," but
  "these were merely overt tags and the factions really formed according to personal and group
  interests." Pisa as a city was staunchly Ghibelline (imperial); Genoa was more often neutral
  or Ghibelline; Florence, Lucca and the papal allies were Guelf.
- The division operated at **three nested levels**: between popes and emperors; between rival
  **cities** (Guelf Florence vs Ghibelline Pisa and Siena); and between **factions within** each
  city, where the losers in a local feud would seek the opposing party's outside help.
- After the **Hohenstaufen collapse** (Manfred killed at Benevento 1266, Conradin executed 1268),
  the labels detached almost entirely from their imperial origins. The **Guelf** cause fused with
  the **Angevins** ([[charles-of-anjou|Charles of Anjou]]), the papacy, and the **Florentine
  banking houses** that financed them; "Ghibelline" survived as the badge of the anti-Angevin,
  anti-papal opposition (and later of [[sicilian-vespers-1282|Frederick III's Sicily]]).
- The feud could even subdivide: in Florence the victorious Guelfs split into **"Blacks"**
  (Donati, pro-Boniface VIII) and **"Whites"** (Cerchi) — the conflict that exiled **Dante**.

## Significance

The Guelf–Ghibelline division structured Italian politics for over a century. It shows how a
**transregional ideological conflict** (papacy vs empire) was localised into the dense world of
the [[commune-movement|communes]] and [[signoria|signorie]], where it became indistinguishable
from clan vendetta and commercial rivalry. As the jurist's and chronicler's evidence shows, by
the late thirteenth century the labels named alliances and enmities more than principles.
