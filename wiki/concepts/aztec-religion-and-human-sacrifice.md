# Aztec Religion and Human Sacrifice (the Fifth Sun)

The religion of the [[aztec-empire|Aztec]] world bound a vast pantheon, a layered cosmos, and a calendar
of dramatic ceremonies into a single conviction: that the universe was **fragile and required constant
nourishment** — above all human blood — to keep moving. Documented for the wiki through Frances Berdan's
[[berdan-aztecs-2021|*The Aztecs*]]. Long sensationalised, Aztec sacrifice is best understood, with
Berdan, as the logical core of a coherent cosmology of **reciprocal debt** between humans and gods.

## The Five Suns

The Aztecs believed the universe had been **created and destroyed four times**; they lived in the
**fifth age, Nahui Ollin (Four Movement)**, ruled by the sun god **Tonatiuh** and fated to end in
earthquakes. The fifth sun was born at **Teotihuacan**, where the humble god **Nanahuatzin** leapt into a
fire to become the sun — but the sun and moon hung motionless until the gods **sacrificed themselves** and
**Quetzalcoatl/Ehecatl** set them moving with his wind. Humanity itself existed only because Quetzalcoatl
retrieved the ancestral bones from the underworld and the gods **bled themselves** onto them. The lesson
was foundational: **the gods sacrificed for humans, so humans owe a perpetual blood-debt in return.**

## The layered cosmos and the gods

The universe was a vertical stack — **thirteen heavens**, an earthly plane, and **nine underworld layers
(Mictlan)**, through which ordinary souls journeyed four perilous years after death. (Special deaths won
better fates: warriors and sacrifices accompanied the sun; women dead in childbirth bore it westward;
the drowned went to Tlaloc's green paradise.) The vast pantheon clustered around Aztec concerns —
**creation** (the remote bisexual **Ometeotl**; the capricious all-seeing **Tezcatlipoca**),
**rain/fertility** (**Tlaloc** and the mother-goddesses — the objects of the *most* monthly ceremonies),
and **sun/warfare** (Tonatiuh; the Mexica patron **Huitzilopochtli**). Deities were **protean**, shifting
guises and merging identities. The Mexica's own charter myth — **Huitzilopochtli** born fully armed at
**Coatepec** to slay his sister the moon (**Coyolxauhqui**) and his brothers the stars — cast their
patron as the sun who must daily **fight off the darkness**, fuelled by sacrificial blood.

## Sacrifice as cosmic maintenance

Human sacrifice (most often **heart-extraction** atop a temple) was thus **integral, not incidental**:
it repaid the gods' debt, **fed the sun and the earth**, and kept the cosmos in motion. Victims were war
captives (the goal of the [[aztec-empire|"flowery wars"]]) or reverently-treated god-impersonators; late
in the empire, mass sacrifices also **advertised a ruler's power**. The **Templo Mayor** of Tenochtitlan
was the ritual centre of this world — a man-made Coatepec with twin shrines to **Tlaloc and
Huitzilopochtli**, at whose foot lay the great dismembered-**Coyolxauhqui** stone; the Mexica enlarged it
thirteen times, and modern excavation has found 200+ buried offering-caches with tens of thousands of
artefacts and the remains of 450+ animal species and sacrificed humans. Ritual ran on the **18-month
solar calendar** (each 20-day month its own ceremony of fasting, procession, dance, and sacrifice) and on
the dread **New Fire ceremony** every 52 years, when — the world poised to end — fires were extinguished
and rekindled in a sacrificed man's chest.

## Significance and debate

Aztec religion is a prime case that the Americas developed **complex theology and cosmology
independently** of the Old World, and it frames the [[aztec-empire|empire's]] "culture of warfare" (war
*was* a religious obligation to feed the sun). The **scale** of sacrifice is debated: some early Spanish
figures (e.g. claims of 80,000 at one dedication) are now read as **propaganda or exaggeration**, by
conquerors with reason to demonise; archaeology confirms the practice but not the largest numbers, and
scholars weigh genuine cosmology against political theatre and against the Spanish need to justify
conquest. The reciprocal-debt logic, the Five Suns, and the Templo Mayor cult, however, are securely
attested.

## Expansion ideology (Charlton / Conrad & Demarest)

Thomas H. Charlton's survey of Central Mexico after Tula ([[cambridge-history-native-peoples-americas-v2-1|CHNPA
II.1]] Ch. 11) does **not** treat ritual mechanics of sacrifice in detail; its load-bearing claim for this
page is political-ideological. Among the factors cited as significant for Triple Alliance expansion
between A.D. **1430 and 1519** are structural changes after the fall of Azcapotzalco (destruction of local
noble houses, succession crises, reduced resistance — Brumfiel) **plus the ideology of war for sacrificial
victims** (Conrad and Demarest 1984:44). In Charlton's late-period synthesis, motivations for participation
in imperial expansion include **religious fervor**, the **cult of captive sacrifices**, and individual
social advancement (alongside structural tribute needs of a growing nobility and factional/ethnic dynamics).
These are listed as partial, multi-causal factors — not a monocausal "sacrifice empire" thesis — and sit
beside his insistence that descriptions of motives do not fully account for all expansion and complexity.

The Templo Mayor excavations (Matos Moctezuma), which Berdan and Charlton both flag, remain the principal
archaeological witness to the imperial cult at [[tenochtitlan|Tenochtitlan]]. For the event that opened the
expansion era, see [[triple-alliance-formation-1428]].
