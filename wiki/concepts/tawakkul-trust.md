## Definition and Scope

Tawakkul — complete trust in God and self-surrender to Him — is one of the most important and extensively discussed stations on the Sufi path. The Swiss scholar Benedikt Reinert devoted a monograph to the subject that Schimmel describes as "a model for future research into the concepts of early Sufism." Its definition was, from the outset, a point of doctrinal contestation: was tawakkul an attribute of the faithful at large, a consequence of perfect faith, or the apex of renunciation (zuhd)?

The most concise theological grounding is the argument from tawḥīd: "God, in His absoluteness, is the only actor, and therefore man has to rely completely upon Him." Related logic draws on divine predestination: rizq ("daily bread") is guaranteed from pre-eternity by God's name ar-Razzāq ("He who bestows sustenance"), so striving to attract or refuse anything is ultimately vain. The Prophetic tradition states: "If ye had trust in God as ye ought He would feed you even as He feeds the birds."

Tawakkul in its interiorized sense means to realize tawḥīd by root and branch: to rely upon or fear any created being would be shirk khafī, "hidden associationism" — a subtle form of the greatest sin in Islam. In this sense, true tawakkul produces not passivity but a radical realignment of the soul's orientation.

## Historical Development

Dārānī, a spiritual descendant of Ḥasan al-Baṣrī, defined tawakkul as "the apex of zuhd" (renunciation). The discussion began in earnest in the ninth century, probably with **Shaqīq al-Balkhī** of the Khurasanian school. Dhūʾn-Nūn defined it as "complete certitude" (al-yaqīn al-kāmil). Throughout the ninth century, the Baghdad and Khurasanian schools shaped the discourse:

- **Muḥāsibī** (representative of orthodox moderate Sufism): tawakkul varies in degree with the degree of faith; it is not absolute passivity.
- **Sahl at-Tustarī**: combined active life with complete inner tawakkul — a synthesis the tradition found important.
- **Junayd** (d. 910): taught his disciples to regard even their earning as performed in the spirit of supererogatory worship, not as a means of sustenance. The question of earning (kasb) and trust were among the most debated in early Sufism.

The classical text is Abū Ṭālib al-Makkī's Qūt al-qulūb, which devotes sixty large folio pages (with fine print) to tawakkul — more than to any other aspect of Sufism. Through Ghazzālī's Iḥyāʾ ʿulūm al-dīn, these ideas became the standard formulation of medieval Islam.

## Tawakkul in Practice: The Extreme Forms

The early Khurasanian tradition generated vivid, sometimes disturbing, illustrations of tawakkul taken to its limit:

- Sufis wandering desert paths without provisions, without fear of lions or highway robbers — relying entirely on divine provision. Ironically, one of the leading masters of tawakkul, **Abū Turāb an-Nakhshabī**, was devoured by lions in the desert.
- A dervish falling into the Tigris who, asked whether he wished to be saved, said "no," and asked whether he wished to die, again said "no" — "for what have I to do with willing?"
- Ibrāhīm ibn al-Khawāṣṣ, who wandered deserts without provisions (ʿalāʾ t-tawakkul), refused even the company of Khiḍr (the immortal patron of travelers) lest that companionship constitute reliance on a created being — and cited Abraham's refusal of Gabriel's aid when thrown into Nimrod's fire, rewarded when God changed the fire into a rose garden.
- A prohibition on stretching out one's hand even to grasp a dried-up melon skin, if it had not arrived of its own accord.

These extremes were criticized even within the Sufi tradition. The Prophet's own saying — "First tie your camel's knee, and then trust in God" — was regularly invoked against passivity.

## The Orthodox Critique and the Moderate Resolution

Neither strict Hanbalite orthodoxy nor the moderate Sufis accepted extreme tawakkul uncritically. The key objections:

1. **Economic and social fabric**: if all Muslims abandoned earning, the empire would collapse. This argument appears explicitly in Sufi texts and was taken seriously.
2. **Prophetic practice**: the Prophet Muhammad worked, traded, and tied his camel. He set the normative model.
3. **The danger of spiritual pride**: paradoxically, demonstrating tawakkul by extreme means could become its own form of performance and attachment.

The moderate resolution, articulated by Junayd and consolidated by Ghazzālī, reinterpreted tawakkul as a spiritual attitude rather than an external practice: to perform one's work and earning in the spirit of worshipful surrender, without treating them as the real source of sustenance. In this interiorized form, tawakkul remained "an important element of Muslim piety" well into the modern period.

In the course of time, this interior disposition was also distinguished from the stoic acceptance of blind fate. The Muslim term for hopeful trust, ḥusn aẓ-ẓann ("to think well of God"), carries a personal relational quality — God's kindness is specific, not impersonal necessity.

## Tawakkul and Tawḥīd

The deepest theological grounding of tawakkul lies in its relationship to tawḥīd. If God is truly the only agent, then any reliance on secondary causes — human planning, provision, protection — implicitly posits those causes as real powers alongside God, a subtle shirk. Perfect tawakkul is therefore an experiential realization of divine unity, not merely an ethical disposition. This connection between tawakkul and tawḥīd was made explicit by Dhūʾn-Nūn and became standard in subsequent manuals.

The result of genuine tawakkul is inner peace: once every feeling and thought is directed toward God without secondary causes, neither humans nor animals can harm the mystic. The stories of Sufis unharmed by lions must be read in this framework — not as miracles of divine protection but as illustrations of a consciousness that has transcended the dimension in which harm becomes possible.

## Historiography

The primary locus classicus is Abū Ṭālib al-Makkī's Qūt al-qulūb (late 10th c.), supplemented by Ghazzālī's Iḥyāʾ (11th c.) and all major Sufi manuals. Reinert's monograph (cited by Schimmel but not named in full here) is the most focused modern scholarly study.
