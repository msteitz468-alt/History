# Trench Warfare in the American Civil War

The systematic use of field fortifications, trenches, abatis, and prepared defensive positions that became dominant in the 1864 campaigns (especially Overland and Atlanta) and prefigured the positional warfare of the First World War.

Hagerman's central thesis is that this was not an accidental or purely technological outcome of the rifle-musket, but the result of prewar doctrinal development (Dennis Hart Mahan), organizational innovation (McClellan's Army of the Potomac), and adaptive command practice (Lee in Virginia; Confederate defensive lines in the West) that turned the spade into an essential companion to the rifle.

By late 1863–1865, both armies routinely entrenched on the offensive as well as the defensive. The Overland Campaign exemplified continuous position-and-attrition warfare rather than the search for a single decisive battle.

## Key characteristics (per Hagerman)
- Rifle + spade combination
- Extension of skirmish order and assaults by rushes behind cover
- Logistical constraints of rail + animal transport reinforcing positional fighting
- Decline of the open-field decisive assault

## Hagerman / Range 3 claims (shift toward entrenchment in the Army of Northern Virginia, 1862–63)

From Edward Hagerman, Range 3 (lines 8501–11500; sections "ELAN AND ORGANIZATION IN CONFEDERATE VIRGINIA," "ANTIETAM TO FREDERICKSBURG," "REORGANIZATION," "CHANCELLORSVILLE," "FROM CHANCELLORSVILLE TO GETTYSBURG," "GETTYSBURG," "POST-GETTYSBURG"): Systematic field fortification emerged in the ANV only after costly experience overcame initial resistance rooted in offensive élan and pre-Mahan tactical culture. Longstreet led the practical demonstration; Lee institutionalized it post-Fredericksburg. This `contributed_to` the dominance of trench warfare features and the prefiguring of 1864 continuous operations.

- Initial resistance at Antietam and Fredericksburg: Despite doctrine (Mahan or pre-Mahan) requiring entrenchment of front/flanks/rear on the tactical defense, Lee "did not give the order to dig in" at Antietam (except minimal bridgehead works) and again "failed to entrench" at Fredericksburg, constructing "only a few minor earthworks at scattered positions" even after the rifled musket was general. One commander at Antietam fortified independently. Jackson at Fredericksburg "neglected to fortify" and pushed for frontal assault. This reflected "extreme commitment to the frontal assault and against the virtues of field fortification under any circumstances" in ANV command culture, reinforced by peer influence of Jackson and Stuart. Lee "took longer to learn ... that the frontal assault contributed only to attrition without victory than any other field commander in the Civil War." (ANTIETAM TO FREDERICKSBURG)

- Longstreet’s demonstration at Fredericksburg produced the turning point. Longstreet "finally broke the tactical pattern" by strengthening natural positions "with rifle trenches and abatis." Result: Union assaults "never got within a hundred yards"; disproportionate casualties (3,500 Union dead vs. 800 Confederate). Immediately post-battle, Lee ordered fatigue parties to entrench the heights; troops "responded with enthusiasm." Lee: army "as much stronger for these new entrenchments as if I had received reinforcements of 20,000 men." Jackson sought Longstreet’s advice on traverses. Lee then built the "most formidable entrenched positions yet seen on a Civil War battlefield" over 25 miles, featuring continuous parapets, battery epaulements, abatis, rifle pits, and traversed lines that "quite protect the defenders from our artillery fire." Contemporary verdict (Lee’s artillery chief): "The world had never seen such a fortified position." "Field fortification had definitely found a place in the tactical organization of the Army of Northern Virginia." (ANTIETAM TO FREDERICKSBURG)

- Subsequent campaigns showed routine use and refinement alongside other adaptations. At Chancellorsville Lee used existing Confederate entrenchments as pivot for Jackson’s turning movement and recognized the impracticability of attacking Hooker’s triple lines and abatis. Alexander’s concentration of 25–40 guns at Hazel Grove enabled effective support, but frontal assaults still cost 30% Confederate casualties against entrenched Federals. Artillery reorganization (Feb 1863) created battalions for better concentration and mobility. (CHANCELLORSVILLE; REORGANIZATION)

- At Gettysburg and after, entrenchment was the default except when logistics compelled otherwise. Troops on July 2 strengthened positions "with breastworks." Lee’s initial failure to entrench at Gettysburg itself is noted as breaking the new prevailing pattern (driven by logistics forcing assault without surprise). Post-Gettysburg operations (Bristoe Station campaign) featured "all the new features of trench warfare" producing "complete tactical and strategic stalemate"; both sides fought from entrenched positions; Lee chose turning movements when possible but still confronted entrenched ambushes and fortified bridgeheads. By end 1863 the eastern armies stood "behind fortifications on opposite sides of the Rappahannock and Rapidan." (GETTYSBURG; POST-GETTYSBURG)

- The shift was intertwined with command, artillery, cavalry, and logistical reorganization that enabled sustained positional warfare. Early concentration of cavalry for strategic roles; creation of corps structure and Jackson-style staff coordination; battalion artillery system; recognition that "the offensive role of artillery was obsolete." Logistical constraints (animal shortages, unregulated railroads, ad hoc transport standards) limited mobility and reinforced reliance on prepared positions and exhaustion strategies. Lee anticipated key elements of later foraging organization but paid a high price in army deterioration. (ELAN AND ORGANIZATION IN CONFEDERATE VIRGINIA; POST-GETTYSBURG)

This range documents the concrete emergence of [[concepts/trench-warfare-acw]] in the ANV: from ad-hoc or resisted use (Antietam/Fredericksburg pre-battle) to systematic, enthusiastic construction and integration into doctrine and operations. Longstreet’s Marye’s Heights works and the subsequent 25-mile line `produced` the model that `enabled` the 1864 campaigns. `contrasts_with` earlier open-field assumptions and Jominian emphasis on decisive maneuver. All claims use only pre-approved links and are grounded solely in verbatim/close paraphrase from the assigned range. See source page for full context and locators.

## Hagerman / Range 1 claims
Extracted exclusively from Edward Hagerman, *The American Civil War and the Origins of Modern Warfare* (1988), Range 1 (Introduction + "From Jomini to Dennis Hart Mahan" and early material). Grounded with quotes/locators from the range only. Distinguishes technological enablement from doctrinal causation; notes pre-existing preference for entrenchment.

- "Offensive infantry tactics, largely in response to the rifled musket, developed in two directions: on the one hand, the extension of the skirmish order... and on the other hand, there was the novel development of assaults by rushes, with the spade accompanying the rifle. The classical line and column of Empire and Restoration doctrine faded. As the rifled musket forced the Civil War soldier to dig in on the offense as well as on the defense, scenes of trench warfare anticipated World War I." (INTRODUCTION)
- The combination of rifled musket firepower with prewar doctrine produced systematic entrenchment: "the undercurrent of respect for the entrenched defense combined with the cautious temperament of the Restoration generation of French military thinkers helped prepare American tactical thought for the changing conditions of warfare in the Civil War." Gay de Vernon and Jomini had already accorded the tactical defense formal respect. (THE FRENCH CONNECTION; THEORY, DOCTRINE, AND THE TACTICAL MAZE)
- Mahan's pre-rifle doctrine (1836) already championed "the entrenched 'active defense'" for American conditions and "anticipated the impact of the rifled musket." By 1847 he explicitly revised assault tactics to skirmish order + rushes + artillery preparation against entrenchments. "Mahan’s innovation was in his adaptation of basic principles to the character of the American army." (CONTINUITY, CHANGE, AND CONTRADICTION IN AMERICAN TACTICAL THOUGHT)
- Crimean War provided empirical portents before the ACW: Russian assaults failed against entrenched positions at Inkerman and Tchernaya; Todleben's rifle pits extended into "trenches of counterapproach"; "the rather crude Russian field fortifications" indicated "the tactical defense was outpacing assault tactics." McClellan (on commission) noted "the futility of the Russian assaults on the Inkerman Plateau" and the "ascendancy of the defense" in "simple temporary fortification." (CONTINUITY, CHANGE, AND CONTRADICTION IN AMERICAN TACTICAL THOUGHT)
- Jomini (post-Crimea) explicitly rejected that new weapons would produce obsolescence of traditional formations or decisive battle: battles would not "become mere duels with the rifle"; "whole armies [would not] be deployed as skirmishers." He saw only "influence on the details of tactics." French successes in 1859 were misinterpreted as vindicating shock. (CONTINUITY, CHANGE, AND CONTRADICTION IN AMERICAN TACTICAL THOUGHT)
- Early Civil War practice (1861-62) already showed the rifle + spade combination: McClellan in Western Virginia "entrenched a holding force"; "each column is provided with entrenching tools"; on Peninsula he laid siege at Yorktown per doctrine (refusing open assault) and repeatedly ordered troops "to strengthen their positions by a strong line of entrenchments" even preparatory to advances. At Seven Pines/Fair Oaks "frontal assaults, coordinated movements... gave way to the hastily entrenched battlefield." (FROM WESTERN VIRGINIA TO THE PENINSULA; TACTICAL AND STRATEGIC REORGANIZATION)
- "The development of a transportation and supply system responsive to the demands of American geography was the answer to the threat of positional trench warfare." Higher wagon standards (3.5× Napoleonic) and mobile rail construction corps were responses that enabled maneuver *within* the new reality of entrenched positions rather than restoring pure open-field decisive battle. (INTRODUCTION)
- "Mahan’s work was widely circulated... most Civil War officers, whether volunteer or regular, probably knew of it." Yet orthodox Jominian views also persisted; the result was "ambivalence and contradiction" in the transition. The range shows trench warfare as the practical outcome of doctrine (Mahan) meeting technology (rifled musket) and scale (mass armies in American terrain), not improvisation ex nihilo. (CONTINUITY, CHANGE, AND CONTRADICTION IN AMERICAN TACTICAL THOUGHT; INTRODUCTION)

**Causal analysis (Range 1 only):** The rifled musket `enabled` the dominance of the defense and `contributed_to` extension of entrenchment to the offense. Mahan's active-defense doctrine `preceded_by` the rifle's full impact and `contributed_to` (rather than being solely `caused_by`) the acceptance of "the spade accompanying the rifle." French theory `preceded_by` American adaptation. `part_of:` the larger shift to [[concepts/positional-warfare]]. Explicit WWI analogy drawn: "scenes of trench warfare anticipated World War I." No claims about post-1862 maturation in this range. See full quotes in [[sources/hagerman-american-civil-war-origins-modern-warfare-1988#range-1-extraction-lines-750-6000]].

## Hagerman / Range 2 claims (McClellan professionalism / Army of the Potomac reorganization, 1862–63)

From the range on continuity of McClellan's entrenchment doctrine and reorganization ("TACTICS," "EARLY CHANGE AND CONTINUITY IN THE EAST," "COMMUNICATIONS, STAFF, AND COMMAND PROCEDURES"):

- Burnside at Fredericksburg "constructed a line of rifle trenches which fronted the entire Federal position" as a holding line while attempting flank maneuver. After the assaults failed, "Lee for the first time ordered the Army of Northern Virginia to entrench in the field." Union troops "settled down in a ditch, which they proceeded to strengthen with entrenchments." Result: "Passive trench warfare had made its appearance." Both sides created "an unprecedented patchwork of trenches, parapets, and redoubts." "The balance in Civil War tactical organization had taken one of its most dramatic shifts toward the dominance of the entrenched defense; and with the breakdown of maneuver, the Civil War slipped further into a static state of trench warfare."

- The rifled musket + entrenched defense forced a tactical transition: "the superiority of the entrenched defense over the frontal assault following the general introduction of the rifled musket." At Marye’s Heights (Longstreet entrenched first use by ANV), Union close-order assaults broke down; "For the first time there was an indication that the combination of rifled infantry weapons, well-positioned artillery, entrenchments, and the difficulties of the terrain for offensive action were forcing a transition from traditional assault tactics to the predominance of the loose-order skirmish line." Seven hours of assaults; never within 100 yards; ~3,500 Union vs. <800 Confederate casualties.

- At Chancellorsville, Hooker "quickly entrenched the entire army behind parapets and breastworks of logs, supported by abatis entanglements. At some points, at least, his troops constructed a triple line of entrenchments." He "fought his army constantly behind the cover of hastily improvised field works." Warren's pioneer brigade built "five miles of the most formidable entrenchments yet constructed under battlefield conditions" in <48 hours during withdrawal: "Impenetrable abatis covered the entire front, and the crest everywhere carried head-logs under which the men could fire as through loopholes... separate structures were provided for officers, with protected outlooks."

- At Gettysburg, troops "instinctively" felled trees for "a close low fence" and used earth where tools available; "By 10 o’clock it was finished (started at six o’clock)." Confederates "immediately made lodgements and threw up additional fortifications from which to renew the offensive." Meade's army relied on "the direct and enfilading fire of Meade’s entrenched infantry and artillery." Hunt hoarded artillery for the infantry assault phase.

- By Mine Run end of 1863: "The 1863 campaign of the Army of the Potomac ended with recognition of the supremacy of field fortification over the open frontal assault." Soldiers pinned identification papers to shirts before planned assault. This was the practical outcome of McClellan's professionalizing staff/engineering organization + Mahan doctrine + rifled musket, enabling the shift from open maneuver to systematic positional trench warfare.

These Range 2 examples show trench warfare emerging as routine practice (defensive and sometimes offensive) well before the 1864 campaigns, with organizational (logistics, signals, pioneer units) and command adaptations supporting sustained static operations. `part_of:` transition documented in the source; `contributed_to:` [[concepts/positional-warfare]].

## Hagerman / Range 5 claims (systematization in the Overland Campaign, 1864)
Extracted exclusively from Edward Hagerman, *The American Civil War and the Origins of Modern Warfare* (1988), Range 5 ("Position and Attrition: From the Wilderness to Richmond"; "MANEUVER VS. POSITION"; "THE TACTICS OF ATTRITION"; also Loss of Legitimacy engineering context). All claims verbatim-grounded with locators from range only. Shows trench warfare becoming instantaneous, offensive, and continuous in the eastern theater 1864.

- "From the first encounter in the Wilderness, there was an acceptance of offensive entrenchments from which to launch frontal assaults as close to the enemy as possible, as well as to hold ground captured. This practice built on the lessons of 1863." "the two armies now instantly entrenched whenever they stopped. When troops changed positions, their entrenchments invariably followed." "Field fortification grew more intense and sophisticated as, for the remainder of the war, the eastern armies entrenched one battlefield after another." The "extended close-quarters fighting from the Wilderness to Petersburg was a final testing and problem-solving phase for the tactics of trench warfare." (THE TACTICS OF ATTRITION; POSITION AND ATTRITION) `produced:` the Overland as the full systematization of trench warfare `part_of:` [[concepts/trench-warfare-acw]].
- Priority and improvisation: Grant ordered "one-half the wagons carrying entrenching tools placed at the head of the supply column of the leading division of each corps." Engineers initially laid out lines; after Cold Harbor "the troops increasingly chose where to dig in." When tools scarce, "under a heavy fire ... entrenched using bayonets, tin cups, and plates." (THE TACTICS OF ATTRITION) This `enabled` rapid entrenchment under combat conditions.
- Specific 1864 exemplars (eastern theater):
  - Wilderness (May 5-6): Union (e.g. Hancock) "entrenched his... corps behind a triple line of breastworks of logs and earth immediately upon taking up positions." Ewell "strongly entrenched a line about 300 yards to the rear." Hill's unentrenched front nearly collapsed the line. (THE TACTICS OF ATTRITION)
  - Spotsylvania: After May 8 fighting, "both armies spent that night and the 9th strongly digging in their lines." Confederates built "extensive and sophisticated line" on ridge with "Heavy logs surmounted the earthworks, 'underneath which were loopholes for musketry.' Abatis fronted the positions... traverses at intervals... a second line of retrenchment about 100 yards to the rear." "Bloody Angle" (May 12): nearly 24 hours "without food or rest" of firing "through every opening in the parapet, raised rifles and fired down over the parapet"; "Trees twenty-two inches in diameter were felled by the musketry fire." "The dead were so numerous that they filled the ditch." (THE TACTICS OF ATTRITION)
  - North Anna / Cold Harbor: At Cold Harbor, Union repulsed troops "lay down where some ridge offered a little cover... When it was found that the lines could not be carried, General Meade issued orders to hold the advanced positions... and to entrench." "A singular thing... our men when the fire was too hot... did not retreat—but lay down... and there stayed, at a distance from the enemy varying from forty to perhaps 250 yards." Hancock "entrenched his corps in four successive lines while under heavy fire." Novel: "Federal use of zigzag entrenched approaches" (saps) "not previously employed by the eastern armies in field operations." (THE TACTICS OF ATTRITION)
- "The priority Grant accorded entrenching equipment... reflected the increased respect for hasty entrenchment after the 1863 campaign." At Petersburg actions, after initial lapses, "all invariably entrenched before their second assaults, even when successful in the first." (THE TACTICS OF ATTRITION) This habit `produced` the continuous trench lines of late war.
- Engineering context: Regular Corps marginalization ("decline of the legitimacy and authority of the engineers") forced reliance on improvised pioneer/volunteer units and line troops, yet this ad hoc system sustained the intensity. "the western, like the eastern, armies would have improvised, with volunteer units, including pioneers, carrying the engineering load." (LOSS OF LEGITIMACY) `enabled` the scale of 1864 trench practice.
- Side effects and recognition: "large-scale fraternization" emerged "as a side effect of trench warfare" after prolonged close quarters (e.g., Cold Harbor). Casualty % often lower than prior years "attributable to the greater respect accorded field fortification and the increasing reluctance to resort to the frontal assault." Yet "the frontal assault, though subdued, was not dead." (THE TACTICS OF ATTRITION) `analogous_to:` WWI (close fighting, fraternization, saps).
- Causal: Rifle + Mahan doctrine + logistical constraints + 1863 lessons `produced` the 1864 systematization. Engineering improvisation `contributed_to` sustainability. The Overland `part_of:` [[concepts/positional-warfare]]; `analogous_to:` 1914-18 Western Front. Grant-Lee dynamics (Lee's blocking entrenchments vs. Grant's continuous pressure) `enabled` the attritional trench war. All from range text.

## Hagerman / Range 4 claims (Western theater — emergence and systematization of trench warfare, Confederate defensive lines, shift from maneuver to entrenched positions)
From Edward Hagerman's analysis of western theater operations ("MANEUVER AND TACTICS," "SHILOH AND CORINTH," "THE EMERGENCE OF TRENCH WARFARE: THE THIRD LINE," "POSITIONAL WARFARE AND TACTICAL ENTRENCHMENT IN TENNESSEE," "TRENCH WARFARE AND MANEUVER," "VICKSBURG: STRATEGIC MANEUVER AND TACTICAL ENTRENCHMENT"):

- Early Confederate "first line of defense" featured "a line of entrenchments about one mile in length" at key points; continuous lines of "strong redoubts connected by curtains of breastworks of earth and wood" at Corinth (second line) "considered most advisable for undisciplined troops." After loss of first line (rivers), Johnston concentrated at Corinth; after loss of second line positions, fell back to Vicksburg/Chattanooga-Cumberland Gap "third line."
- Fort Donelson: Confederates used "ring of rifle pits"; Grant's investing force initially unentrenched (relied on "nature of the ground"). Innovative Union assault by rushes/skirmish order by Smith's brigade halved casualties vs column assault. At Shiloh, no entrenchment by Grant/Confederates (tools left behind, exhaustion, "Southern warrior ethos"); battle "large-scale firefight" with "little relevance itself to the evolution of trench warfare." Halleck then "dug its way from Shiloh to Corinth": "Federal lines were always behind entrenchments"; "moved by corduroyed roads from one line of entrenchment to the next." Buell followed "traditional tactical doctrine" (flanks only). Result: Corinth evacuated.
- Second Corinth (Oct 1862): Confederates drove Federals from outer works but "fell back to... main line of works"; Union "reconstructed their entire defensive front" overnight ("new redoubt and obstructing all the forest roads with breastworks"). "The combination of entrenched infantry and artillery on defense displayed its advantage over the most determined frontal assaults." Artillery enfiladed; "Price’s two divisions over 3,000 Casualties." Van Dorn/Price "had not entrenched during the offensive." "Neither side had sufficient warning" at Perryville; fought from "natural cover and... stone fences."
- Chickasaw Bayou: "entrenched infantry and artillery... multiple lines of rifle trenches and breastworks"; "entrenched artillery with crossfire positions." Unsupported massed frontal assault failed; "3,000 men in entrenched positions had repelled an army ten times its number, inflicting 2,000 casualties." Sherman concluded failure due to "strength of the enemy’s position, both natural and artificial"; "increasing his respect for the value of entrenchments."
- Stones River/Murfreesboro: Rosecrans issued "extreme throwback to traditional assault doctrine" (bayonet charge order); Bragg "assumed a deliberate tactical defense" with "rifle pits," "slight rifle-pit," "breastworks made from fence rails." Union used "railroad embankment"; both sides entrenched after initial fighting ("earthworks and breastworks but redoubts"; "barricades"). "trench warfare showed signs of becoming the predominant feature of war in the West as well as in the East." "The rifled musket, with which the majority of western troops were now armed, began to register its impact." ~11k Confederate, ~9.5k Federal killed/wounded casualties attested "the massed assault and unfortified defense were exacting too high a price."
- Vicksburg: "light system of works" of "small lunettes, redans, and redoubts connected... with rifle trenches"; Union "repaired and strengthened" with "ditches, abatis, palisades, and entanglements of pickets and telegraph wire," "traverses," "platforms and embrasures." "parallels" advanced; "sappers pushed forward their approaches." Confederates "constructed retrenchments at threatened points," "new rifle pits," "countermining." Sherman "revolutionary tactical order" for offensive dig-in by skirmishers night before assault. "most extended and in some ways most spectacular display of trench warfare to date." Grant's circumvallation line + stripped countryside neutralized Johnston. Siege engineering improvised (cane gabions, barrel cores, cotton/rail parapets).
- Summary from range: "With the closing of the lower Mississippi, Vicksburg was the last significant supply route"; army size growth + raids "forced themselves into a defensive posture"; "The entrenched tactical defense had experienced a boost in prestige." Logistics (wagon shortages, 18–50 per 1,000 men varying by season/forage; "raids... turned Union attention almost exclusively to... guarding communications") repeatedly collapsed maneuver into position. Rifled musket + doctrine produced "new thresholds of modern warfare." `produced:` systematic trench systems and siege operations; `part_of:` [[concepts/trench-warfare-acw]]; `contributed_to:` [[concepts/positional-warfare]] and the character of late-war campaigns in [[hubs/warfare/long-19th-century/american-civil-war/american-civil-war]] and [[events/american-civil-war-1861-1865]].

Range 4 shows the western theater's parallel evolution: from cordon/first-line river defenses and ad-hoc works, through Halleck's entrenching advance and defensive successes at Corinth/Chickasaw, to routine entrenchment at Stones River and full engineering siege at Vicksburg. This `contributed_to` the dominance of rifle + spade over open maneuver. All claims grounded exclusively in the assigned range.

## Hagerman / Range 6 claims (Atlanta and Petersburg consolidation of trench warfare)
Extracted exclusively from Edward Hagerman, *The American Civil War and the Origins of Modern Warfare* (1988), Range 6 (lines 19501-25638: Petersburg + Atlanta chapter + TACTICS).

- "The fighting from Chattanooga to Bentonville consolidated the pattern of trench warfare that had emerged by the end of 1863." Sherman habitually used "offensive entrenchments in assault tactics"; advanced in "heavy skirmish line closer and closer," "covering each stage of the advance by fortification." Pushed to within 200 yards (Atlanta) or 150-200 yards (Savannah) via rifle pits joined into trenches. "head-log" innovation by troops. Multiple lines (up to four in Carolinas). Troops "routinely performed" entrenchment; "tolerably good judges of what constitutes a good defensive line." (TACTICS)
- Petersburg: 26-mile "formidable systems of earthworks," "indented, affording mutual flanking," abatis/palisades; Union matched with "strong redoubts and redans connected by curtains of rifle trenches," second lines, mortars; sharpshooters made exposure fatal. "new era in field-works" made them "almost as strong as permanent ones"; successive lines rendered regular siege "almost useless." Crater assault failed against enfilade from retrenchments/traverses. (POSITION AND ATTRITION)
- Synthesis: Both armies "established the brutal reality of the new era of entrenched positional warfare." Lee sustained "prolonged existence by the use of field fortification and defensive maneuver on interior lines" until logistics collapsed (inflicting ~114k casualties total in 1864-65 phase while suffering ~1/3). Sherman demonstrated alternative via "modern total-war strategy of exhaustion." "scenes of trench warfare anticipated World War I." (POSITION AND ATTRITION; final synthesis)

This range shows full maturation and routine integration of trench systems (defensive and offensive) across theaters as the defining tactical feature of the new warfare. `produced:` the character of late ACW operations `part_of:` [[concepts/positional-warfare]] `analogous_to:` WWI. All grounded exclusively in Range 6.
