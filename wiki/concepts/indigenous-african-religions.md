## Definition

**Indigenous African religions** is an analytical category covering religions created by African communities that have relied primarily on the inspiration of prophets, spirit mediums, and elders rather than on sacred texts originating outside the continent. They are characterized by:

- **Ethnic particularity with openness to borrowing:** they foster a distinct cultural and ethnic identity, assimilating outside ideas in ways that do not directly challenge that identity
- **This-worldly orientation:** they are 'highly instrumental in emphasis, seeking to enhance the physical, economic, and cultural well-being of their adherents in this world rather than in a world to come' (Robert Baum, in [[parker-reid-oxford-modern-african-history-2013]])
- **Civil religion:** followers generally do not proselytize; they assume people born into or living in the community will practise its tradition
- **Tolerance of plural practice:** adherents are often less concerned about simultaneous use of Christian or Muslim ritual as long as members fulfil obligations to the religious community
- **Supreme being plus lesser spirits:** most African religions have a central deity who begins the process of creation and judges life after death, alongside lesser gods, spirits, and ancestors who address particular concerns and appeal to different social groupings

Over one thousand African religions exist, with significant differences within and between ethnic groups. Religious concepts and practices are often transmitted through oral traditions and intensive instruction during initiation rites; knowledge is hierarchically controlled, restricted to those who have achieved appropriate status or demonstrated responsible handling of the power that accompanies its acquisition.

## Limitations and Critiques

The term 'indigenous African religions' is itself a Western academic construct. Okot P'Bitek (1970) argued that Africans were 'atheistic in their outlook' — by which he meant that European concepts of 'religion' failed to describe highly varied African beliefs and practices, and that channelling African cultures into the monotheistic and metaphysical strictures of 'world religions' deformed them. John Mbiti's influential textbook portrayed indigenous religions as 'relatively static', changing only when Islam and Christianity arrived — a view now substantially rejected by historians. The 'Hamitic Hypothesis' (John Hanning Speke, 1860s) attributed African religious and cultural achievements to Caucasian wanderers, denying African agency — a theory with tragic consequences in Rwanda and Burundi where it legitimized Belgian favouritism toward Tutsi elites.

The premise of 'stasis' before colonial encounter has been refuted empirically: E. E. Evans-Pritchard documented major changes in Nuer religion in the nineteenth century; Matthew Schoffeleers demonstrated the Mbona cult's precolonial dynamism in Malawi; Iris Berger showed spirit medium traditions changing in the interlacustrine region. Indigenous African religions were never static; they responded actively to environmental change, the Atlantic slave trade, migration, state expansion, and colonial conquest.

## Historical Development and Change

### Precolonial Dynamism

Oral traditions, combined with linguistic, archaeological, and documentary sources, can recover significant religious change before colonialism. The Nuer prophet Ngundeng Bong — whose spirit 'Deng' derived from a Dinka origin — led military campaigns against slave traders and the British before dying in an anti-colonial revolt. New spirit cults emerged in response to the Atlantic slave trade: in the Casamance (Baum's Diola research), slave-trade-related shrines transformed the structure of religious authority; John Janzen documented the spread of the Lemba cult across Equatorial Africa and the Atlantic diaspora. Rosalind Shaw traced Temne (Sierra Leone) witchcraft beliefs back to centuries of slave-raiding terror.

### Colonial Era

Colonial conquest was experienced by many communities as evidence of widespread witchcraft: the visible suffering of defeat, disease, and displacement demanded an explanation within existing cosmological frameworks. Anti-witchcraft movements proliferated across West and Central Africa and the interlacustrine region. In Southern Rhodesia, the Mwari cult's priests mobilized the Shona revolt of 1896. The Maji Maji rebellion (Tanganyika, 1905–07) was organized around the prophet Kinjikitile's shamanic revelation of medicine that would protect warriors — a multi-ethnic religious movement that united peoples of south-eastern Tanganyika and became a symbol for Tanzanian nationalism. The Xhosa Cattle Killing of 1856 (Nongqawuse's prophecies) directly linked European expansion to witchcraft contaminating the land. The Diola prophet Alinesitoué Diatta (who taught only two years before being exiled and dying in 1944 at age 23) introduced the Kasila ritual, prohibited colonial cash crops, and sparked a female prophetic tradition in the Casamance.

Colonial authorities criminalized witchcraft accusations but often misunderstood African testimony: French administrators in Senegal confused Diola expressions for witchcraft activity ('to eat in the night', 'to see in the night') with cannibalism, leading to a series of unjust trials in the late 1920s.

### Robin Horton's Conversion Model (and Critiques)

In 1971, Robin Horton proposed an influential theory: most African religions had a two-tiered cosmological structure (supreme being governing the 'macrocosm'; lesser spirits governing the 'microcosm'). Colonialism shattered the microcosm by integrating Africans into a vast, impersonal social world, generating demand for a religion focused on a universal supreme being — hence conversion to Islam or Christianity. Critics such as Humphrey Fisher argued this model romanticized the capacity of indigenous religions to develop supreme-being-centred monotheism on their own. Later scholars (Baum and others) argued that Horton underestimated the internal heterogeneity and mobility of African societies before colonialism, and that conversion was often layered, reversible, and not simply a response to macrocosmic disruption.

### Urban Survival and Postcolonial Marginalization

Contrary to predictions, indigenous African religions proved highly adaptable to urban contexts. Monica Hunter (Wilson) and Philip Mayer studied Xhosa religion in South African townships; studies documented thriving indigenous traditions in urban southern Ghana and Nigeria. The West African cult of Mami Wata addressed postcolonial quests for wealth, power, and sexual expression, often in urban settings.

In the postcolonial era, no African head of state has publicly identified as a practitioner of an indigenous African religion (Baum, in [[parker-reid-oxford-modern-african-history-2013]]), though Togo and Benin celebrate Vaudou as national heritage. Spirit mediums (*mhondoro*) played central roles in Zimbabwe's liberation struggle, disciplining the guerrilla army; Zimbabwe named two major roads after them after independence. Indigenous oathing rituals mobilized Mau Mau fighters in Kenya. Practitioners of indigenous religions have been systematically disadvantaged by the channelling of NGO and development aid through Christian and Muslim organizations.

Neo-traditionalist movements such as Kenya's Mungiki can be understood partly as efforts to reassert indigenous religions in national and ethnic life. In South Africa, the Xhosa healer Nokuzola Mndende left the ANC because of its insistence that African culture was best represented by African-Initiated Churches, which denounced her as a witch.

## Relation to 'World Religions'

The relationship between indigenous African religions and Islam and Christianity is not simply one of replacement. Conversion was often gradual, layered, and incomplete; reversion or movement between faiths occurred. Second-generation Christians sometimes sought conscious rapprochement with their parents' culture ('reprise'). Pentecostal services that initially banned drums as associated with ancestor veneration later reintroduced them as heritage. Spirit possession traditions dismissed as 'sorcery' under colonialism have spread even into some northern Nigerian Muslim communities, filtering into the toolkits of reformist enemies of *bori* practitioners.

Robin Horton and Humphrey Fisher's 1970s debate on African conversion initiated a large literature; more recent synthetic work by Baum and others emphasizes that Horton's dichotomy between macrocosm and microcosm was too schematic, and that the dialogue between indigenous traditions and world religions has been complex and ongoing rather than a one-way replacement.

## Emic / Etic Distinction

The category 'indigenous African religions' is an *etic* (analytical) one; practitioners typically identify by community, deity, or specific ritual practice rather than using this collective label. 'Traditional religion', 'ancestral religion', 'ethnic religion', and specific community names (Diola religion, Mwari cult, Vaudou) are used in various contexts.

## Historiography

Prior to the 1970s, the field was dominated by anthropologists, colonial administrators, and missionaries; historians and scholars of religion paid little attention. The discipline assumed stasis. Evans-Pritchard's *Nuer Religion* (1956), despite his ahistorical training, opened the way for historical approaches. Ranger and Kimambo's 1972 collection *The Historical Study of African Religion* was foundational. Jack Goody and Jan Vansina both argued that oral cultures' homeostatic tendencies made historical recovery of religious change extremely difficult; a growing number of scholars have contested this, demonstrating that sustained multi-source research can reconstruct meaningful religious history.

The major unresolved methodological challenge is that oral traditions about religious history are difficult to obtain: knowledge is hierarchically restricted; religious change is often unconscious and thus not remembered; and dissident traditions tend to be edited out. Environmental oral traditions (droughts, famines, epidemics) have proven more tractable, revealing close connections between ecological and religious change.
