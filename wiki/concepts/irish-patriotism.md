## Definition and Scope

Irish patriotism designates the political culture of the Protestant settler elite in Ireland from approximately the 1690s through the 1790s, in which the colonial-descended ruling class defended its rights against encroachment by the Westminster parliament and the British executive while simultaneously excluding the Catholic majority from those same rights. The term is analytically distinct from [[irish-nationalism]], which succeeded it and rested on different premises of popular sovereignty and national self-determination.

Joep Leerssen's influential formulation defines patriotism as "the stance of an early modern community defending its own historically defined and frankly sectional liberties, in the same way that the *parlements* of contemporary France resisted the encroachments of the Crown, or Dutch patriots defended the forms of republican government against the monarchist pretensions of the House of Orange" (quoted in [[jackson-oxford-modern-irish-history-2014]], Ch. 2). On this reading, Protestant Irish patriots were not nationalists in any modern sense: they were defending specific corporate privileges, not invoking an inclusive popular nation.

J. G. Simms applied the borrowed term "colonial nationalism" to this phenomenon, capturing the paradox of a settler population simultaneously developing a new collective identity tied to Irish soil while excluding the indigenous Catholic majority.

## Historical Development

### Origins in Constitutional Conflict (1690s–1750s)

The successive constitutional disputes that created patriot consciousness arose from practical grievances: commercial restrictions protecting English economic interests, intrusion of English placemen into Irish offices, and the growing tendency of English opinion to dismiss Irish Protestants as simply "Irish." William Molyneux's tract of 1698 is the canonical starting point, arguing Ireland's connection was to the Crown of England alone and not to the Westminster parliament. The Wood's halfpence controversy of the 1720s (on which Jonathan Swift wrote polemically) crystallized the issue: Swift could address his pamphlet "to the whole people of Ireland" while insisting that opposition "had nothing to do with 'the Irish' but arose instead from among 'the true English people of Ireland.'"

This self-contradictory stance—defending an Irish political community while denying Irishness to Catholics—marks the defining tension of patriotism throughout its life.

### Protestant Patriotism and Its Limits (1750s–1780s)

The creation of a resident lord lieutenant under Viscount Townshend (1767–72) removed the local face of British government and allowed political grievances to be more easily cast in national terms. The American crisis supplied an external catalyst: the Volunteer movement backed constitutional demands, and the "constitution of 1782–3" appeared to grant legislative independence. In practice, however, the Irish Parliament remained unaccountable to any significant portion of Irish opinion and an instrument of executive patronage.

Contemporaneously, Protestant patriots developed an extravagant enthusiasm for Gaelic antiquity—the work of Charles Vallancey and others presented early Gaelic Ireland as a literate, sophisticated civilization linked to the ancient Mediterranean. This imaginative appropriation of the Gaelic past for Protestant identity was, as Sean Connolly notes, "as heroic" in its way as earlier acts of colonial self-definition—yet it stopped short of political inclusion of Catholics.

### Catholic Exclusion and the Jacobite Alternative

While Protestant patriots defended "Irish" rights, the political allegiance of the majority Catholic population before mid-century lay with the exiled Stuart dynasty. Irish Jacobitism has been analyzed as a form of "proto-nationalism"—fostering collective identity based on language, history, and religion—but it had no common ground with Protestant patriotism, for whom the Glorious Revolution of 1688 was the ultimate source of their liberties. The two traditions could not synthesize until the ideological revolution produced by America and France.

### Transition to United Irishmen (1790s)

Protestant patriotism reached its zenith in 1782 and then stalled. The parliamentary reform campaign of 1783–5 failed; the question of Catholic political rights was shelved. Into this vacuum came the French Revolution and the Society of United Irishmen (founded 1791), which transcended the sectional limits of patriotism by calling for "an equal representation of all the people in parliament." The United Irishmen initially held to the traditional patriot goal of a sister kingdom under a shared crown but by 1795 reconstituted as a clandestine republican movement. This represented the decisive supersession of patriotism by nationalism in its modern form.

## Historiography

The scholarship on Protestant patriotism has been shaped by the broader revisionist debates in Irish history. J. G. Simms's "Colonial Nationalism 1698–1776" (1976) provided an early framing. Leerssen's work (especially *Mere Irish and Fíor-Ghael*, 1988) argued for the analytical separation of patriotism from nationalism and stressed its specifically early-modern, sectional character. Jacqueline Hill's *From Patriots to Unionists* (1997) traced the trajectory from Dublin civic patriotism through to early Unionism, showing how Protestant libertarianism eventually flowed into the [[ulster-unionism]] stream.

The question of how much the patriot tradition owed to and influenced broader Atlantic and European constitutional debates has been enriched by James Livesey's *Civil Society and Empire* (2009), which situates Irish patriot thought within the imperial management of civic republicanism.

## Collection Coverage Note

The *Oxford Handbook of Modern Irish History* (Jackson, ed., 2014) provides synthetic coverage of the patriot tradition primarily through Sean Connolly's chapter on patriotism and nationalism (Ch. 2) and Alvin Jackson's chapter on Loyalists and Unionists (Ch. 3). Primary sources for the tradition—Swift, Molyneux, Grattan's speeches—are underrepresented in the collection. The transition from patriotism to nationalism is well treated; the internal variety of patriotism in different Irish regions and confessional communities is thinner.
