## Definition

**Social hierarchy and solidarity** is Susan Reynolds's analytical frame
([[kedar-cwh-v5-2015|CWH V]], Ch 4) for the social structure of Middle-Millennium
societies. Its central thesis: **every society of which there is any record in this
period was more or less unequal**, yet most people "took their inequality for
granted and regarded it as just, so long as those above them treated them according
to their society's ideas of justice." Hierarchy (the ranking of elements within a
whole) and **solidarity** (the sense of a society or polity as a natural, bounded
whole) went together — solidarity did not exclude internal conflict, and hierarchy
did not mean equality was never valued. Equality "between such people within each
rank as ought to be equal" was prized; calls for *wider* equality were very rare,
and even the most radical "do not seem to have extended to women, servants, or
slaves."

## Key Claims

- **Universality of inequality, justified by reciprocal obligation.** Rulers and
  elites everywhere were held to expectations of "proper and just behavior";
  legitimacy rested on meeting them. Government "came in layers," and growing wealth
  and economic complexity generated new elites and more elaborate systems of law,
  bureaucracy, and governance.
- **Solidarity through (often fictive) kinship.** Societies from the local to the
  imperial were envisaged as solidarities linked by assumed genealogical connection,
  myths of common origin, shared custom, and common interest — bonds frequently
  manipulable and frequently invented.
- **Hierarchy and solidarity coexist, not oppose.** A strong sense of belonging to a
  ranked whole was the normal condition; egalitarian movements that sought to flatten
  the hierarchy (rather than demand justice within it) were exceptional.

## The Critique of "Feudalism" and Borrowed Categories

The chapter is also a methodological warning, drawing on Reynolds's well-known work
(*Fiefs and Vassals*; *The Middle Ages without Feudalism*). The greatest obstacle
to a global social history of 500–1500 is the **domination of the field by European
categories**:

- **"Feudalism"** is an eighteenth/nineteenth-century construct that fused two
  different ideas (vassalage-and-fiefholding among elites; landlord–peasant relations
  in the Marxist sense). It fits the European evidence poorly and fits other
  societies worse still when they are "squashed into it" via *fief*, *vassal*,
  *enfeoffment*.
- Other transplanted words mislead too: *serf*, *guild*, *manor*, *gentry*,
  *demesne*, *cess* all carry locally specific European baggage that distorts
  comparison. Reynolds urges deliberately **generic, untechnical language**.
- The chapter also refuses the **teleology of the "rise of Europe"** — searching
  pre-1500 for the seeds of later European dominance breeds the false binaries of
  "dynamic Europe / timeless East" and "Western liberty / Oriental despotism." This
  bears directly on the wiki's [[indian-feudalism-debate]] (where Reynolds's critique
  is a central reference) and on [[eurocentrism]].

## Limitations and Contestation

Reynolds writes from medieval-European expertise and concedes the unevenness of the
evidence (Eurasia and North Africa are far better recorded than the rest of the
world). The frame is explicitly **anti-comparativist in method even as it
generalizes** — its core generalization (universal-but-just-seeming inequality) is
broad enough that critics may find it under-specified, while its rejection of
"feudalism" remains contested among historians who still find the term analytically
useful for landlord–peasant relations.
