# The Balance of Power

The **balance of power** is the principle — and the practice — by which states act to prevent
any one power from achieving **hegemony**, by forming counterbalancing coalitions against the
strongest. It is the organizing logic of the modern European **states system**, consciously
invoked from the late seventeenth century (above all against Louis XIV) and explicitly written
into the **Peace of Utrecht (1713)**. From [[new-cambridge-modern-history-v6|*The New Cambridge
Modern History*, Vol. VI]].

## Emic and etic

The phrase is both **emic and etic**: contemporaries used it (the Utrecht treaties speak of
preserving the "balance of power in Europe"), and historians and IR theorists use it
analytically. Its roots lie in Renaissance Italy's "equilibrium" of five states (see
[[italian-renaissance-state-system]]), generalized into European diplomacy as the
[[italian-wars-1494|Italian Wars]] scaled the model up to the continent.

## The principle in practice

- **Anti-hegemonial coalitions.** When one power threatens dominance, others combine against
  it: the Grand Alliance against **Louis XIV** in the **[[war-of-spanish-succession|War of the
  Spanish Succession]]** (1701–14) is the classic case — fought precisely to prevent a union of
  the French and Spanish crowns.
- **The "holder of the balance."** A power (often **Britain**) throws its weight to the weaker
  side to keep equilibrium — the basis of Britain's long policy toward the Continent.
- **Codification at Utrecht (1713).** The settlement that ended the Spanish Succession war
  divided the inheritance to preserve the balance and named the principle explicitly — a
  landmark in the history of international order.
- **Mechanisms:** shifting alliances, partitions (e.g. of Poland), subsidies, and limited wars
  to "adjust" the balance rather than annihilate rivals.

## A contested concept

The balance of power is debated. Realists treat it as a near-automatic regularity of
international politics; critics note it was also an **ideology and rhetoric** used to justify
self-interested policy, that it often failed to prevent hegemonic bids (or produced endless
war), and that it could license cynical acts (the partitions of Poland) in equilibrium's name.
Whether it is a *law*, a *policy*, or a *justification* remains disputed.

## Significance

The balance of power structured European (and later global) international relations from
Utrecht through the **Concert of Europe** (1815) to the alliance systems of 1914 and the
bipolar [[cold-war|Cold War]] standoff. It is the counterpart, in the relations *between*
states, of [[absolutism]] and the fiscal-military state *within* them — the operating logic of
the modern [[strategy|state system]].
