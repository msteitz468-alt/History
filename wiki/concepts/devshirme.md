# The Devshirme and the Slave Household (*Kapıkulu*)

The ***devshirme*** ("collection") — the **periodic levy of Christian boys** from the
Ottoman Empire's own subjects, who were converted to Islam and trained as the sultan's
**slave-servants (*kapıkulu*, "slaves of the Porte")** to staff the army and the highest
offices of government. One of the most distinctive Ottoman institutions, and the backbone of
the centralized empire from the fourteenth to the late sixteenth century. From Colin Imber's
[[imber-ottoman-empire-1300-1650|*The Ottoman Empire, 1300–1650*]].

## The system

Because the supply of war-captive and purchased slaves was insufficient, the sultans levied
recruits **from among their own Christian (mostly Balkan) subjects** — illegal under Islamic
law (which forbade enslaving protected non-Muslim subjects, *dhimmis*), but practised anyway,
beginning sometime in the fourteenth century (already attested in a 1395 lament by the
Metropolitan of Thessaloniki). Officials toured the Christian villages and "collected" boys
typically at a rate of **one in forty households**. Detailed selection rules (from *The Laws
of the Janissaries*) excluded only sons (needed on the farm), orphans ("undisciplined"),
married men, craftsmen, and the too-tall or too-short — seeking healthy, trainable village
boys of about 14–18.

The recruits were converted to Islam and sorted: the ablest entered the **palace school** for
training as administrators (rising to become provincial governors, viziers, even grand
vizier); the rest were enrolled as **[[janissaries]]**, the slave-infantry corps. As legal
**slaves of the sultan**, they could be promoted, demoted, or executed at his will, and (in
principle) could not marry or pass on status — preventing the formation of a hereditary
nobility.

## Significance: a meritocracy of slaves

The *devshirme*/*kapıkulu* system gave the Ottoman state a **loyal, centrally controlled élite
owing everything to the sultan** — a striking contrast to the hereditary aristocracies of
Europe. The "highest posts of the empire" were open to men of humble, non-Muslim, non-Turkish
origin; political office (outside the religious-legal professions) was dominated by
**first- or second-generation converts**, and discrimination ran on religious, not ethnic,
lines. This is central to Imber's portrait of the empire as **dynastic and meritocratic rather
than nationally Turkish**.

## Decline

The system eroded from the late sixteenth century. As the **[[janissaries]]** expanded
(7,886 in 1527 → ~39,000 by 1609) to meet the demand for firearm-bearing infantry in the
[[ottoman-empire|"military revolution"]], the old levy could no longer supply the numbers, and
the corps was opened to **Turks and other free Muslims** — including, increasingly,
Janissaries' own sons. The slave basis of recruitment became "no longer relevant," the
*devshirme* fell into disuse, and the *kapıkulu* turned from a disciplined household into a
large, hereditary, and politically assertive body.
