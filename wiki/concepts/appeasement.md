## Definition

Appeasement was Neville Chamberlain's deliberate, activist policy of identifying and
resolving German grievances against the Versailles settlement through negotiation, in
order to "remove the danger spots one by one" and avoid a repeat of the uncontrolled
escalation of 1914. It was not drift or passivity — Chamberlain flew to Germany three
times in fourteen days during the Czechoslovak crisis of September 1938 (Berchtesgaden,
Godesberg, Munich) and took the diplomatic initiative repeatedly (e.g., sending Lord
Halifax to meet Göring in November 1937). Its high point and most famous application was
the [[munich-agreement-1938]].

## Emic vs. Etic

**Emic**: before 1938, "to appease" was an ordinary, uncontroversial diplomatic verb.
Chamberlain and his supporters understood appeasement as principled conflict-resolution —
removing the *causes* of German resentment (the perceived injustices of Versailles)
rather than merely containing German power. Chamberlain's private letters show genuine
moral distaste for Hitler ("is it not positively horrible... he is half mad") combined
with confidence that face-to-face negotiation, which he believed had worked in his
earlier career in labor-management relations, could work with Hitler too.

**Etic**: after the Munich settlement failed to prevent German seizure of the rest of
Czechoslovakia (March 1939) or the outbreak of war (September 1939), "appeasement" became
a byword for weak or cowardly concession to aggression — an epithet rather than a
neutral policy label. This etic sense shaped a generation of postwar Western leaders'
reflexive rejection of negotiation with perceived aggressors (cited examples: Anthony
Eden at Suez, 1956; U.S. policy toward the Viet Cong, 1965).

## Rationale (as understood by its architects)

1. **War-avoidance**: the trauma of World War I losses (village war memorials, mutilated
   veterans, France's demographic "hollow years" of the 1930s) and fear of even worse
   destruction from aerial bombing and gas made another general war seem unthinkable to
   avoid if it could possibly be avoided.
2. **Versailles-as-disease theory**: the appeasers held that Nazism was a "political
   disease caused by the Versailles Treaty" — remove the grievances (German-speaking
   populations under foreign rule, disarmament restrictions, the demilitarized Rhineland)
   and the "fever" would subside. This assumed Hitler had a *finite* set of demands.
3. **Anti-Bolshevism**: many appeasers, including Chamberlain associates Lord Halifax and
   Sir Horace Wilson, valued Hitler's Germany as a bulwark against Soviet/Bolshevik
   expansion into central Europe; Chamberlain's predecessor Stanley Baldwin said "if
   Hitler moves east, I shall not break my heart."
4. **Domestic-order fear**: a new war was expected to trigger revolutionary upheaval on
   the 1917 pattern; appeasers had "no stomach for another bout with the social demands
   of total war."

## Applications and Limits (per Paxton and Hessler)

Applied to the [[remilitarization-of-the-rhineland-1936]] (no Allied military response),
the [[anschluss-1938]] (no Allied response to a popular fait accompli), and above all the
[[munich-agreement-1938]] (transfer of the Sudetenland). It reached its limit with
Chamberlain's Commons declaration of March 31, 1939, guaranteeing Polish independence —
see [[remilitarization-of-the-rhineland-1936]] causal chain and the Polish crisis leading
to the German invasion of September 1, 1939.

## Contested Status

Genuinely contested among historians, not merely in retrospect:
- **A. J. P. Taylor** (*Origins of the Second World War*, 1961) argued Hitler was a
  pragmatist pursuing goals similar to Stresemann's and Bismarck's, and placed
  significant blame on British and French rigidity, especially over Poland in 1939 — the
  most sustained scholarly challenge to the "moral failure" reading.
- **"Lost opportunity" school** (e.g., Telford Taylor's *Munich: The Price of Peace*,
  1979) holds that firmer resistance — especially over the Rhineland in 1936 or
  Czechoslovakia in 1938 — could have triggered a German military coup against Hitler or
  at least delayed and weakened him; Paxton and Hessler treat this as speculative and
  probably overstated, noting Chief of Staff Ludwig Beck's resignation over the 1938
  crisis but the inaction of other generals, and noting that resisting over the Rhineland
  in 1936 risked casting France as the aggressor and consolidating Hitler's domestic
  position.
- Historians broadly agree appeasement achieved none of its stated goals: it preserved
  neither Czechoslovakia nor peace. It is judged, per Paxton and Hessler, to "have had no
  defenders, on either moral or pragmatic grounds" in its Munich application — but the
  broader legitimacy of negotiation-as-policy (versus the specific 1938 decisions) remains
  a live historiographical question, not simply resolved by hindsight.
