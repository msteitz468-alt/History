# NAGPRA (Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act)

## Definition

The **Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act (NAGPRA)**, enacted by the
United States Congress in **1990**, is federal legislation requiring federally funded
institutions — museums, universities, and government agencies — to identify, inventory, and
repatriate human remains, associated funerary objects, and sacred objects linked to
federally recognized Native American tribes. It fundamentally restructured the relationship
between American archaeology and Indigenous communities
([[snow-gonlin-siegel-archaeology-native-north-america-2020]]).

## Historical Development

Through most of the twentieth century, the display and retention of Native American human
remains was standard practice in American museums. The Smithsonian Institution's National
Museum of Natural History once displayed an entire wall of human skulls. As American Indian
civil rights movements strengthened from the 1960s onward, such exhibits were increasingly
challenged by tribal activists, and human skeletal displays gradually disappeared from
American museums and archaeological sites.

Native American organizations simultaneously petitioned Congress for legislation protecting
unmarked graves and mandating the return of human remains and sacred objects affiliated
with living federally recognized tribes. The timing coincided with a broader shift in
American archaeology away from excavation and toward site preservation, creating common
ground between tribal advocates and professional archaeologists. American Indians,
archaeologists, and politicians together shaped the language of NAGPRA, which was signed
into law in 1990.

## Legal Requirements

NAGPRA imposes several specific obligations:

1. **Inventory and notification**: Institutions receiving federal funds must inventory
   their collections of Native American human remains and associated funerary objects,
   and notify affiliated tribes.
2. **Repatriation of affiliated remains**: Human remains and funerary objects linked to
   extant federally recognized tribes must be offered for repatriation upon tribal request.
3. **Sacred objects**: Coverage extends to sacred objects, whether or not they came from
   graves.
4. **Dispute resolution**: The law created a review committee to adjudicate disputed
   claims.
5. **Unaffiliated remains**: The law left unresolved how to treat skeletal remains that
   are clearly of Native American origin but cannot be identified with a specific living
   community — a gap that generated major controversy in the **Kennewick Man** case
   (9,000-year-old remains found in Washington State in 1996).

## Impact on Archaeology

NAGPRA had multiple effects on the discipline:

- **Forced analysis**: The requirement to inventory thousands of boxed skeletal collections
  provided an opportunity to complete important osteological analyses that had never been
  conducted, even as those remains were subsequently repatriated.
- **Tribal consultation**: The law normalized and institutionalized consultation with
  descendant communities. Oral traditions are now increasingly recognized as sources for
  testable archaeological hypotheses.
- **Changed research ethics**: The ethical framework of American archaeology shifted
  substantially. Where earlier generations dismissed Native American oral tradition and
  dug graves without tribal consultation, contemporary archaeology integrates tribal
  participation into research design.
- **Tension over ancient remains**: Disputes over skeletal remains too old to be affiliated
  with a specific modern tribe — most prominently Kennewick Man — exposed unresolved
  tensions between scientific access and tribal claims of cultural continuity.

## Application

Federal funding is the key trigger for NAGPRA compliance, extending the law's reach to
virtually all professional archaeological institutions in the United States. The law does
not apply in Canada or Mexico, though both countries have developed analogous frameworks.
Canadian institutions use Heritage Resource Management (HRM) frameworks; Mexican archaeology
is administered through the **Instituto Nacional de Antropología e Historia (INAH)**.

## Critique and Limitations

Critics from within archaeology have argued that NAGPRA's definition of "cultural
affiliation" imposes continuity assumptions on ancient populations whose relationships to
modern tribes are scientifically uncertain. Critics from within tribal communities have
argued that the law's procedures are burdensome and that compliance has been slow.
The unaffiliated remains category remains a persistent source of legal and ethical conflict.
Despite these tensions, most assessments within the profession treat NAGPRA as a net
positive for the scientific rigor and ethical standing of American archaeology.
