## Definition and Scope

The Quran (Arabic: *al-Qur'an*, meaning "the recitation") is the central sacred scripture of Islam: a collection of divine messages that Muhammad received as revelation and that were recited liturgically, memorized, and eventually compiled into a fixed written text. Hodgson is precise about the term's etymology: "The messages collectively were called the Qur'an, which means 'recitation'." The name thus encodes the oral character of the text's original medium — these were not written compositions but auditory revelations meant to be voiced aloud. The Quran is both the primary source of Islamic theology and, in Hodgson's framing, the documentary record of a community forming itself in response to a prophetic challenge.

Distinct from scripture in the Judaic or Christian sense, the Quran is not a narrative history or a law code. It is a body of divine speech, addressed directly to Muhammad and through him to humanity, covering moral exhortation, cosmological vision, legal guidance, historical example, and liturgical call. Its function was simultaneously devotional, judicial, and communal.

## The Nature of Revelation

Hodgson stresses that the revelatory experience was understood by Muhammad as auditory rather than visionary in its primary form, though "there was not only audition but ocular vision" at certain moments. The physiological character of the episodes — described by some sources in terms that have been compared to epileptic states, a parallel Hodgson notes but treats with appropriate skepticism — was less important than their interpreted meaning: these were messages from a God who was utterly transcendent and who demanded human response.

The early revelations were cast in "ecstatic rhyming prose" (*saj'*), formally distinct from the tribal poetry (*shi'r*) the Quraysh would have recognized, though the Quran explicitly denies being poetry. This distinction mattered: Arabian tradition attributed poetic genius to spirit familiars (*jinn*), a claim Muhammad rejected. "He insisted, in the Qur'an, that he was the prophet of the Ummah, of the confessional community" — a bearer of divine message, not an inspired craftsman.

### Composition and Preservation

During Muhammad's lifetime, revelations were received piecemeal, sometimes in response to specific community situations. They were arranged into *surahs* (chapters), many of which accumulated multiple revelations over time. They were memorized by specialists called *Qari'* ("Qur'an-reciters"), who "specialized in reproducing and teaching the whole of the constantly growing Qur'an." Hodgson notes an important distinction: "this is sometimes called 'Qur'an readers' in English, but" the *Qari'* were reciters in the oral sense — the text existed first and primarily as sound. A fixed written canon was established under Uthman, suppressing variant recensions in circulation among different garrison cities; this act itself became a point of controversy (see [[second-fitna-680]]).

## Content and Theological Vision

Hodgson offers a sustained reading of the Quran's theological world. Its primary concerns:

**The divine transcendence and human accountability.** "In the Qur'an it was early made clear that human beings face a fundamental choice" — between acknowledging God's reality and rejecting it. God (Allah) is utterly beyond human categories; the Judge is "utterly transcendent, incommensurate with all that was to be judged." This transcendence does not produce distance but heightens moral urgency: human beings are accountable.

**The Day of Judgment.** Quranic eschatology depicts the final reckoning in vivid sensory terms — "painted both the blessedness of those who will be saved and the fate of those who will be punished with all evil things the heart dreads." These are not merely future events but present realities shaping conduct.

**The prophetic chain.** The Quran presents Muhammad as the seal of a line of prophets — Adam, Noah, Abraham, Moses, Jesus — all bearing the same essential message, corrupted by their communities over time and now restored in final, definitive form. Truth is not lost but forgotten: "the truth is forgotten — unless a prophet arises to recall it."

**The miracle of the text itself.** Unlike earlier prophets who performed signs, "he claimed no wonder but the Qur'an itself; this, however, he regarded as undeniable. He challenged any man to produce its like." The formal and aesthetic excellence of the Quran — its literary *i'jaz* (inimitability) — functioned as its own authentication.

**The human situation.** "The Qur'an puts the human situation in powerful images, drawn from" the natural and social world familiar to its first audience: the desert, the caravan, the night sky, the Day of Assembly. Its cosmology included "several heavens, the divine Throne, and heavenly books," but these are always in service of the central moral address to human beings.

## The Quran and Community Formation

A crucial insight in Hodgson's analysis is that the Quran is inseparable from the process of community formation it accompanied. "As the Muslim community developed, the character of the Qur'anic messages became more explicitly oriented to the community experiences, often down to seemingly petty details. It was filled with repeated exhortations to support the community efforts, notably the military excursions, and with regulations of community procedure." This means:

- The Meccan surahs (earlier) tend toward eschatology, moral urgency, and cosmic vision
- The Medinan surahs (later) tend toward law, social regulation, and community governance
- The two bodies cannot be understood apart from the historical trajectory of the [[early-muslim-community]]

The Quran thus functions simultaneously as sacred text and as constitutional document for the first Muslim polity. Hodgson notes that "the 'constitution of Medina', which settled the position of the several elements in Medina when Muhammad established himself there, was not a Qur'anic document — but the Qur'an makes it sufficiently clear that the new way is for everyone."

The Quran also made Islam ritually autonomous. The direction of prayer (*qibla*) shifted from Jerusalem to Mecca; the Islamic fast of Ramadan superseded the Jewish fast days; "Islam thus became ritually independent of previous monotheisms."

## The Quran in Broader Religious Context

Hodgson situates the Quran within what he calls the "Irano-Semitic confessional" tradition: the broad family of scripturally grounded, individually adherent, prophetically organized religions spanning from Judaism and Christianity through Zoroastrianism. The Quran both inherits this tradition — presupposing "acquaintance with various cosmic figures and episodes from those traditions" — and claims to supersede it by restoring the original, uncorrupted revelation. It is "a synthesis and fulfillment of the Abrahamic line" while rejecting key elements, notably the idea of a chosen people: "the Qur'an rejected the idea of a chosen people."

The Quran's terminology "reflects traditions from a variety of religious communities but presupposes no" single prior tradition as its sole source. This eclecticism-in-service-of-supersession is characteristic: the Quran is aware of other scriptures but claims to render them dispensable.

## Historiography

**Source status.** The Quran is the only document from the formative Islamic period whose text we can treat with near-certainty as authentic. Hodgson states: "We can rely on the text of the Qur'an itself as" a primary document, though interpreting it requires "resort to reports" about the occasions of revelation (*asbab al-nuzul*) — reports whose reliability is contested.

**Interpretive challenges.** "The Qur'an cannot be read as a discursive book, for abstract information or even, in the first instance, for inspiration. The sequence of its bits and pieces was not determined by logical or narrative order" but by the accidents of revelation history and the editorial decisions of early compilers. This structure creates perpetual interpretive difficulty.

**Scholarly debates.** Major disputes include: the dating and ordering of individual surahs; the degree to which the received canonical text faithfully represents Muhammad's revelations; the extent of Jewish and Christian influence on Quranic theology and imagery; the role of Uthman's compilation in suppressing alternative readings; and the relationship between Quranic prescriptions and later developed Islamic law (*shari'a*). Hodgson references Bell, Arberry, and other translators as part of a substantial Western scholarly tradition engaging these questions, while acknowledging that objective scholarly access to the Quran's historical formation faces methodological limits.
