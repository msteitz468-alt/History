## Definition and Scope

Latin Averroism refers to the cluster of philosophical positions derived from close reading of Averroes' (Ibn Rushd's) Long Commentaries on Aristotle in the Latin universities, especially Paris and (later) Padua. The term is contested because the thinkers so labelled did not form a self-conscious school and often rejected the designation. Modern historians use it to identify a set of positions — above all the doctrine of the unity of the material intellect — that generated sustained theological controversy and a series of ecclesiastical condemnations.

The philosophical positions associated with Latin Averroism include:

1. **The unity of the potential intellect** (*unitas intellectus possibilis*): Averroes held that the material or potential intellect — the receptive faculty by which humans know universal truths — is a single, numerically one substance shared by all humans rather than being multiplied in individual persons. This directly threatened doctrines of personal immortality and individual divine judgment. See [[unity-of-intellect]].
2. **The eternity of the world**: Following Aristotle and Averroes, Latin Averroists held that the world had no temporal beginning, contradicting Genesis.
3. **Determinism and necessitarianism**: The causal closure of the natural order left little room for miracles or providential intervention.
4. **Double truth (disputed)**: Some historians attributed to Latin Averroists a doctrine of "double truth" — that something might be true in philosophy and false in theology. Most scholars now reject this as a caricature; the actual position of figures like Siger of Brabant was that philosophy revealed what Aristotle and Averroes taught, while theology revealed revealed truth, and where these conflicted, theology took precedence.

## Historical Development

### Origins: Michael Scot and the Entry of Averroes

The systematic availability of Averroes' Long Commentaries in Latin dates to the work of Michael Scot (active c. 1210–35), working first at Toledo and then at the court of Frederick II Hohenstaufen. Scot translated the Long Commentaries on *De Caelo*, *De Anima*, and eventually the *Metaphysics*. The lemmatized format of these commentaries — in which the entire commented text appears as lemmata within the commentary — also provided new Latin translations of Aristotle's own works alongside Averroes' exegesis.

The text that became most controversial was Averroes' *Long Commentary on De Anima*, which contained the fullest exposition of his unity-of-intellect doctrine. The transmission included also a text by Averroes' own son, Abū Muḥammad ʿAbdallāh ibn Rushd, *On the Possibility of Conjunction*, which clarified and in some respects complicated his father's position.

### The Paris Controversy (c. 1255–1277)

By the 1250s, Averroes' commentaries were being read alongside Aristotle's natural philosophy texts in the Paris Faculty of Arts. The "radical Aristotelians" — including Siger of Brabant (c. 1240–c. 1284) and Boethius of Dacia — defended Averroist positions on the intellect and the eternity of the world as the philosophically correct reading of Aristotle, while acknowledging the authority of faith on questions where faith and reason diverged.

Thomas Aquinas responded to this tradition in two works: *De unitate intellectus contra Averroistas* (1270) and the *Summa contra Gentiles*. Aquinas argued that Averroes had misread Aristotle and that the correct Aristotelian psychology was compatible with individual intellects — and thus with personal immortality.

The Bishop of Paris, Étienne Tempier, issued condemnations of 219 philosophical propositions in 1277, targeting positions associated with radical Aristotelianism, including several drawn directly from Averroism. These condemnations had complex effects: they may have inadvertently encouraged new directions in natural philosophy by making some Aristotelian conclusions theologically suspect.

### The Paduan Tradition (c. 1300–1550)

Latin Averroism found its most sustained institutional home at the University of Padua, where it persisted as an active philosophical tradition into the sixteenth century. Figures such as John of Jandun (c. 1285–1328), Marsilius of Padua (c. 1275–1342), and later Pietro Pomponazzi (1462–1525) continued the Averroist philosophical tradition. Pomponazzi's *De Immortalitate Animae* (1516) revisited the intellect controversy, arguing on Aristotelian grounds that the soul's immortality could not be philosophically demonstrated.

### The Renaissance Edition and Decline

The culmination of the Latin Averroist tradition was the Giuntine edition of Aristotle's complete works with all of Averroes' commentaries, published in Venice in eleven volumes between 1550 and 1552. The principal translator was Jacob Mantino (d. 1549), who translated from Hebrew versions of Averroes (since most Renaissance scholars knew Hebrew better than Arabic). This massive editorial project appeared precisely as the tradition it represented was declining: the humanist turn toward Greek originals, the Reformation, and the rise of new natural philosophies progressively marginalized the Averroist synthesis.

> "From the mid-thirteenth to the mid-sixteenth century at least, students of philosophy in Western Europe, following the Peripatetic tradition, used the works of Avicenna, al-Ghazālī, and Averroes as an integral part of their syllabus. In the course of the sixteenth century chairs in Arabic began to be set up in European universities, and the foundations for the modern discipline of Oriental Studies were laid. But this professionalism in the study of Arabic marked the end of the period in which Arabic philosophy was part of the fabric of the European intellectual tradition." (Burnett, in [[adamson-taylor-cambridge-companion-arabic-philosophy-2005]], ch. 18)

## The Hebrew Intermediary

A distinctive feature of Latin Averroism in its later phases is the role of Hebrew translations and commentaries. At least thirty-eight of Averroes' commentaries were translated into Hebrew from the thirteenth century onward, and Jewish scholars composed "supercommentaries" on these — most notably [[gersonides]], whose supercommentaries on Averroes' epitomes and middle commentaries (composed 1321–24) became the most popular such works in the Hebrew tradition and were read as authoritative readings of Averroes. These Hebrew versions became the primary textual route into Averroes for Renaissance Latin scholars, who relied on Jewish translators to render them into Latin.

The reasons are enumerated in the source: first, Christian scholars were more likely to know Hebrew than Arabic (due to interest in Biblical studies and the Kabbalah); second, Jewish scholars were available in Italy, especially after 1492; third, Hebrew was considered close enough to Arabic that the intermediary was not thought to distort the original significantly.

## Relationship to Jewish Averroism

Within the Jewish philosophical tradition, Averroes served as the primary vehicle for Aristotelian science throughout the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries. [[Hasdai Crescas]] (c. 1340–1410/11) represents the Jewish intellectual equivalent of the Thomistic response to Averroism: his *Or Hashem* (*Light of the Lord*) prefaced a systematic critique of Aristotelian–Averroist natural science with a rigorous presentation of that science — just as al-Ghazālī had done in Islamic philosophy. Crescas's critique was directed at the same "weak premises" that undermined, in his view, the Averroist account of God, the world's eternity, and the soul's immortality. See [[jewish-philosophy-in-islam]].

## Contested Dimensions

The label "Latin Averroism" as applied to the thirteenth-century Paris masters is contested. Fernand Van Steenberghen argued that the Paris masters were not primarily Averroist but rather radical Aristotelian, and that the "double truth" attribution is anachronistic. The category remains useful as a shorthand for the cluster of positions that generated controversy, but it should not be taken to imply doctrinal unity or self-conscious identification with Averroes over and above Aristotle.

The term used by the Paris masters themselves — *ens philosophi* or *philosophi* — pointed to Aristotle; it was the *Commentator*'s reading of Aristotle that they followed. Their opponents, including Aquinas, likewise framed the dispute as one about the correct reading of Aristotle.

## Historiography

The category "Latin Averroism" was largely constructed by nineteenth-century historians of medieval philosophy, particularly Ernest Renan (*Averroès et l'averroïsme*, 1852). Twentieth-century scholarship has substantially refined and partially dissolved the category. The Cambridge histories (NCMH and companion volumes) treat the Paris controversies in detail. The collection's coverage of this topic is strong for the medieval period via the NCMH volumes and moderate for the Paduan Renaissance phase.

[[adamson-taylor-cambridge-companion-arabic-philosophy-2005]] (ch. 18, Burnett) situates the Averroist reception within the full arc of Arabic–Latin translation, providing context that the narrowly scholastic accounts typically omit.
