# Roman Power (External–Internal Framework)

An analytical framework, developed most fully in [[harris-roman-power-2016|W. V. Harris, *Roman Power* (2016)]], for studying the Roman state's capacity to determine the actions and experience of others — combining **raw physical power** (*Macht*) and **institutionalized power** (*Herrschaft*) — across roughly a thousand years (c.400 BC–AD 641). The framework is **etic** (modern analytical categories applied to Rome) while remaining attentive to emic Roman vocabulary (*potestas*, *imperium*, *libertas*, *mos maiorum*, *fides*, *virtus*).

## Core Claim

Harris's distinctive move is to treat **external power** (domination of non-Romans, frontier control, military success abroad) and **internal power** (class, slavery, gender, family, political institutions, ideology) as a single linked problem. Periods of aggressive external expansion (middle Republic, to Augustus) correlate with a warrior aristocracy, high citizen military participation, and competitive elite discipline; periods of external contraction or stasis correlate with different internal distributions of power (absolute monarchy, professional army, religious authority, social alienation). Cross-period comparison within Roman history is privileged over premature end-points (Constantine, 410, 476) or purely cross-cultural empire comparison.

## Three Dimensions

1. **National / imperial power** — the expanding (then contracting) group of "Romans" over other inhabitants of the empire and along the edges.
2. **Social differentiation** — class, slavery, gender, family, and political institutions; not reducible to one axis.
3. **Time** — discontinuities as well as continuities across the millennium; programmatic periodization at **AD 16** (Tiberius slows expansion; absolute monarchy consolidated) and **AD 337** (Constantine's death; cohesion frays; western-collapse factors emerge).

## Periodization Thesis

| Boundary | Harris's rationale |
|---|---|
| c.400 BC | Accessible start of the imperial arc (Camillus / Veii context) |
| AD 16 | Tiberius decelerates expansion; finishes off old aristocratic political supremacy |
| AD 337 | Post-Constantine internal cohesion weakens; next generations produce western-collapse factors |
| AD 636–42 | After [[battle-of-yarmuk-636|Yarmuk]] and loss of Syria/Egypt, Byzantium is no longer an "empire" in Harris's sense until much later (Basil II); true end of the Roman Empire as empire |

This end-date is a **position**, not wiki consensus: it sits beside traditional western focus on [[fall-of-the-western-roman-empire-476|476]], Gibbonian continuation to 1453, and Jones/CAH-style cut-offs around 600–602.

## Continuities Harris Stresses

- **Slavery** as water in which other power relations float (millennial structure; see [[slavery|slavery]]).
- Co-optation of **local men of property** as an organizational technique of control (Italy, then provinces).
- Elite competition and (later) monarchical need to contain successful generals.

## Limitations and Critiques

- Single-author synthesis spanning ten centuries necessarily compresses specialist debates; Harris privileges English secondary literature and elides full source-criticism of ancient authors and environmental causation (explicitly deferred).
- Periodization at 16 and 337 is programmatic and contestable (other breaks: Actium, Severans, 235, 476, 602, 1204).
- "Empire" as a scalar category after 636 is definitional; Byzantine specialists often reject disinheritance of the eastern state from "empire."
- Power theory is eclectic rather than a single school application; Mann's "organizational techniques" appear, but this is not a full Mannian or neo-Marxist model.

## Historiography

Harris writes against (1) reluctance to call republican expansion imperialism; (2) "vicarious identification" with Justinian's empire (after Brent Shaw); (3) premature endings of Roman history in textbooks. The 2016 book extends the argument of *War and Imperialism in Republican Rome* (1979) across the high and late empires and insists that mid-republican military success and late-antique military failure illuminate each other.

## Harris, *Roman Power* (2016) — definition, MPR, formal/informal empire, techniques

Power is “the power of human beings to determine the actions and experience of other human beings”; analysis must be **three-dimensional** (national/imperial domination; social class plus institutions, slavery, gender, family; time/discontinuity). Standard definitions omit **durability/insistence** (“power… is more or less insistent”). Abstractions (*mos maiorum*, *libertas*, rhetoric, Stoicism) and myths exercise power; even in centralized states power is “extremely diffuse” (village policemen, petty tax-gatherers, local magnates). Roman thinking was exceptionally **legalistic** (*potestas*, *imperium*). Principal aim: “how much the character of the Roman state’s constantly changing external relations was determined by its constantly changing internal power structures, and vice versa.”

**Rise factors.** Turchin’s cohesiveness/asabiya is necessary but not sufficient: also needed are aggressive willingness for war, elite capacity to mobilize, and “organizational techniques… to retain and make use of what has been won.” Across 400 BC–AD 16 what stands out is “deep devotion to merciless war… especially strong in the social elite… together with great creativity in imposing permanent control.” Highest distinguishing feature is Andreski’s **military participation ratio (MPR)** — higher under the Republic until 90 BC enfranchisements than imperial Britain or Qing China — linked to constant large-scale enslavement of POWs freeing citizen labour for war. Mid-second century BC guesstimate: **≥50%** of healthy male citizens saw service sometime; by mid-second century AD **~4%** of living citizen males were serving or had served — state less belligerent and less ready for major conflicts. Formal vs informal empire: “To equate the Roman Empire with the area delimited by the external frontiers of the provinces is a mistake” — provinces + vassal/“client” kings + free cities (Strabo 17.839). Road network (vigorous to Trajan) and Augustus’s vehicle/animal courier system were key organizational techniques; Pliny *NH* 3.39 articulates a “civilizing mission” of Italy as single fatherland. High-empire external power also rested on **terror** — periodic “cutting the grass” punitive violence (Uspe 49; Jerusalem; panegyrical approval from Pliny to Constantinian Franks-to-beasts spectacles).

**Deceleration and mid-empire turning points.** World-rule rhetoric coexisted with knowledge of plentiful outsiders; turning points under Hadrian, at Philip the Arab’s death (244–9), and arguably Aurelian. Conquest ideology weakened but did not vanish; the Severan forum arch was “as far as we know, the last monument in Rome to claim that emperors had made the empire grow larger.”

**Two contractions.** Power contracted in two major phases: **370s–430s** (outer limit 455) and **560s to 636–41**. Harris rejects taboo on “decline”: “no thinking historian of political power will hesitate.” After Yarmuk Roman power “was greatly reduced, and over the next two generations it steadily declined still further”; Muslim conquests mark a Mediterranean cultural divide greater than any between second-century BC Roman victories and Ottoman collapse. Mid-century under Constans II Byzantium held large Asia Minor, small European hinterland, some Greek mainland, major islands, dwindling Maghreb, some Italian tracts — little else. Marked late-fourth-century decline “from the highest ranks of imperial service downwards, in people’s belief in the Roman state” (including that it should be served without personal gain). Ch. 8: compare builders and losers of empire; multi-causal eastern failure (Justinian’s over-reach, religious fissures, Islamic vigour, weak provincial national consciousness, invasion–fiscal vicious circle); material shortages of manpower/money in late antiquity; psychological factors include mid-republican violence/discipline vs late-antique **religious intolerance** and **weak national consciousness**.

## Woolf, *Rome: An Empire's Story* (2012) — power as pattern (alongside Harris)

Woolf is a complementary long-arc account, not a rival power-theory system. Where Harris links external and internal *Macht/Herrschaft*, Woolf treats **empire as a moving pattern** (tidal wave, avalanche, **resonance**) that crowds out smaller patterns and then loses coherence — "a movement through historical time, not a fixed set of institutions." For modern readers the puzzle is **longevity**, not only decline.

**Emic senses of empire.** Jupiter's prophecy in Virgil's *Aeneid* 1 (empire without end; people of the toga) scripts empire as "written into Roman DNA"; the *Aeneid* became educational baseline across Italy and the western provinces. Rule meant the toga-people over the defeated, sanctioned by uniquely pious favour of the gods. Latin *imperium* primarily meant temporary personal "command," and only late the total territory of Rome; Augustus was first never to relinquish it.

**Institutional advantage (Italian rise).** Woolf's Italian-hegemony argument tensions pure militarism-first accounts: comparative advantage "must have been institutional" — location not unique, hinterland unimpressive vs Etruria/Campania, no tech edge, Romans not plausibly "more militaristic than their opponents." Class solidarity among elites (Rome siding with aristocrats) seeded an aristocratic empire that could feel, to the propertied, "more like a movement that benefited those drawn into it."

**Humanitas and ideology.** Late-Republican cultural project aimed at *humanitas* (civilization and common humanity), not a closed *Romanitas* parallel to Hellenism — "a truly imperial civilization" in aspiration. Polybius located cohesion partly in extraordinary public and private respect for the gods; later Aristides stressed inclusiveness and citizenship.

**High-empire durability (Woolf emphasis).** Woolf roots survival less in subject psychology alone than in **stopping expansion**, **tributary fiscal consolidation**, monarchical coordination, and provincial preference for the principate over Republican predation: moving from conquest-state toward sustainable taxation "really saved" the empire (booty persisted — e.g. Trajan's Dacia — but the long-term trend was fiscal). Christianity later proved "a less effective imperial ideology than had been the traditional state cults" (schism; independent episcopal authority).

**End of story.** Like Harris, Woolf is prepared to end the Roman *imperial* story with the mid-seventh-century Arab conquests rather than 476 alone; he doubts later Islam simply continues the same pattern of antique political empire.

## From Gibbon, Decline and Fall (1776–1788)

Attributed material from [[sources/gibbon-decline-and-fall-1776]] bearing on the *scale and character* of Roman power (not Harris’s framework; Gibbon is a separate, older analytic voice).

- **Military establishment (Antonines).** Gibbon’s “most liberal computation” will not allow the entire establishment by sea and land “at more than four hundred and fifty thousand men”—formidable, yet equalled (he claims) by a modern monarch confined to a single Roman province.
- **External ceiling.** Augustus’s permanent bulwarks (Atlantic, Rhine/Danube, Euphrates, deserts) and Hadrian–Antonine non-expansion define high-empire power as containment-plus-deterrence rather than endless conquest.
- **Internal form of power.** Absolute monarchy “disguised by the forms of a commonwealth”; military force as “blind instrument of oppression” when virtue fails the prince.
- **Goths as breakers of power.** First considerable Gothic history under Decius: a people “who afterwards broke the Roman power, sacked the Capitol, and reigned in Gaul, Spain, and Italy.”
- **Union purchased by demilitarization.** Firm coalition of members “purchased by the loss of national freedom and military spirit”; servile provinces expected safety from mercenaries and a distant court (General Observations)—Gibbon’s link of external vulnerability to internal power structure, in a moral-civic register rather than Harris’s MPR metrics.
