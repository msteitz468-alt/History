## Definition

**Sarapis** (also Serapis) was a god given a royal installation at
[[alexandria|Alexandria]] by [[ptolemy-i-soter|Ptolemy I Soter]] — the **Greek
interpretation of the Egyptian Osiris-Apis** (Oserapis, Osor-Hapi), the fusion of the
dead-and-resurrected god Osiris with the sacred Apis bull of Memphis. Represented in
Greek form (Bryaxis' famous over-life-size enthroned statue), Sarapis gave the Greek
population of Egypt access to the "miracle-working powers" of an Egyptian deity in a
familiar Hellenic idiom, and became the patron god of the Ptolemaic capital — his great
temple, the **Serapeum**, completed by Ptolemy III Euergetes.

## A Cult of Counterpoint, Not Fusion

Sarapis is often described as a deliberate dynastic invention to *unify* Greeks and
Egyptians, but CAH VII.1 ch. 5 (E. G. Turner) firmly rejects this: "Soter was **not**
creating a cult intended to unify Greeks and Egyptians." The two communities did not
"share hymn-books in the Serapeum"; **Sarapis was worshipped under that name by Greeks**,
while Egyptians continued to worship **Osiris-Apis** in their own temples at Memphis. The
cult is thus a prime illustration of Turner's (and Préaux's) larger thesis about
Ptolemaic Egypt — *interpenetration without fusion*, the *interpretatio Graeca* and the
*interpretatio Aegyptiaca* in counterpoint rather than merger (see
[[ptolemaic-egypt]]). Alongside Sarapis, **Isis** (suckling the infant Horus — a
prefiguration, Turner notes, of the Madonna) drew genuine Greek devotion.

## Spread and Significance

From Alexandria the cult of Sarapis (and of Isis) spread along the trade routes across
the Mediterranean — Delos, Athens, eventually Rome — becoming one of the major
"oriental" mystery cults of the Hellenistic and Roman worlds, and a vehicle of
Graeco-Egyptian religious exchange for centuries. It is a key case study in
[[hellenistic-world|Hellenistic]] religious syncretism and in how a ruling dynasty
could shape (without dictating) the religious life of a mixed society. (A minority view,
noted by Turner, even traces the name's origin to an Iranian epithet via the Xanthus
trilingual — a reminder of how contested the god's genealogy remains.)
