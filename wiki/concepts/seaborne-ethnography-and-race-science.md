## Definition and Scope

"Seaborne ethnography" designates the written, drawn, or toponymic outcomes of "fleeting encounters with Pacific Islanders by European voyagers unfamiliar with their languages, practices, or ideas" (Douglas, ch. 48, [[hattori-samson-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v2-2023]]). Douglas contrasts this with "systems": the deductive appropriations of seaborne ethnographic facts by European savants operating from metropolitan armchairs. The chapter traces the entanglement of these two cognitive modes — facts and systems — in generating European knowledges about Pacific peoples from Magellan's first contact (1521) through to the flourishing of scientific race theory c. 1850.

The key analytical insight is that seaborne ethnography was shaped by "contexts and by traces of the actions and agency of local people." Indigenous behaviour — refusing trade, expressing suspicion, asserting territorial rights — was not merely received passively by European observers but shaped, and often distorted, what those observers recorded and theorized. The chapter follows this "dialogic" quality of encounter-knowledge across three centuries.

Douglas warns that she cites historically pejorative terms about Indigenous peoples in context, "to emphasize that they are historical rather than natural constructs; and to expose their absurdity to critical scrutiny."

## Phase 1: Seaborne Ethnography before the Science of Man (1521–1749)

### Magellan / Pigafetta: The Island of Thieves (1521)

On 6 March 1521, Magellan's expedition sighted islands at the end of nearly four months crossing the empty Pacific. Pigafetta's account of the encounter — in which Chamorro Islanders from Guam came aboard and took goods — records the Spanish reaction: Magellan "landed with forty armed men, & burned forty or fifty houses, with many of their boats, and killed seven of them." Pigafetta then switched to the "ethnographic present": the men "go naked," are "olive" in colour, "well built"; the women "are beautiful & delicate & whiter than the men"; "This people is poor, extremely ingenious, & very thievish." Magellan initially labelled the islands *de las Velas Latinas* (of the lateen sails), registering admiration for local seamanship. But this label vanished; "los Ladrones" — Island of Thieves — endured on global maps until the twentieth century as "a cartographic calumny which long endured as a signified (meaning) unmoored from its referents, the local actions that had provoked it."

The Chamorro were later colonized by Spain (1668), when the group was renamed the Marianas. The derogatory appellation Ladrones "was scarcely challenged in global mapmaking until the nineteenth century."

### Dampier: Descriptive Contrasts without a Racial System (1686, 1700)

William Dampier's travel narratives were "wildly popular" and became primary ethnographic sources for subsequent savants. At Guam (1686) he recorded the islanders as "Copper-coloured, like other Indians" and admired their proa canoes: "the best of any Boats in the world." In New Guinea (1700) he described physical variation: "a sort of very tawny Indians, with long black Hair" who seemed chiefs, and "shock Curl-pated New-Guinea Negroes" many of whom were "Slaves." He attributed "treacherous," "shy and roguish" behaviour to the inhabitants of New Britain — behaviour Douglas reads as "instances of Indigenous agency: determination to interact on their own terms and resolute refusal to engage in 'a friendly Commerce.'" Thwarted, Dampier "resorted to pre-emptive use of gunfire 'to strike some terrour into the Inhabitants.'"

Dampier's implicit contrast of "Indians" and "Negroes" carries "profound ethnological implications" but does not constitute a racial system. His primary criterion of human difference was "a deeply ethnocentric conception of relative civility." He believed "Negroes" were capable of civilization through trade. "Unlike apologists for the slave trade or nineteenth-century raciologists, his confidence that 'Negroes' were capable of civilization through trade is evident."

## Phase 2: Seaborne Ethnography and the Natural History of Man (1749–1795)

### Buffon: Climate Theory and Pacific Data (1749)

In 1749 Buffon published a groundbreaking catalogue of "varieties in the human species" — the first treatment of man as a subject of natural history. His Pacific data came almost entirely from Dampier. He used Dampier's contrasts between "Papuans or Negroes" of New Guinea and "less black" New Hollanders to argue that "the climate" was "the first and almost unique cause of men's colour." Buffon's concept of "race" was ambiguous and did not constitute a taxonomy: he used it in a narrow genealogical sense (synonymous with "kind," "variety," "nation") and an "extended" sense (climatically induced "singular resemblance" in widely dispersed peoples). "It is inappropriate to attribute a modern racial system to Buffon."

### J.R. Forster: Two "Great Varieties" (1778)

Johann Reinhold Forster, naturalist on Cook's second voyage (1772–5), devoted nearly two-thirds of his *Observations* to "the human species." He split Pacific peoples into "two great varieties": one "more fair, well limbed, athletic, of a fine size, and a kind benevolent temper" (Tahitians, Marquesans, Tongans, Easter Islanders, Māori), the other "blacker, the hair just beginning to become woolly and crisp, the body more slender and low, and their temper, if possible more brisk, though somewhat mistrustful" (New Caledonians, New Hebrideans). He supposed the two varieties were "descended from two different races of men" — but as a Lutheran pastor believed "all mankind" were "of one species" and "only accidental" variations. His equation of "darker skin colour with primordiality, cannibalism, and absence of civility ominously foreshadowed aspects of nineteenth-century racialist discourses."

### Blumenbach: Five Varieties to "Principal Races" (1781–1806)

Blumenbach drew on Pacific materials to illustrate his monogenist thesis of diversity within a single human species. In his second edition (1781) he made the "new southern world" a fifth human variety; in his third edition (1795) he renamed his "five principal varieties" as "Caucasian," "Mongolian," "Ethiopian," "American," and **"Malay"** — the last encompassing South Sea Islanders. He hypothesized an east–west cline in the Pacific: from Tahitians (lighter) through "Mulattos" to New Hebrideans approaching "Papuans and New-Hollanders," who converged with the "Ethiopian" (African) variety.

A critical methodological error: Blumenbach "anachronistically ascribed his own taxonomic agenda to Quirós" — misreading Quirós's varied descriptions of Islander skin tones as a pre-existing racial classification, partly caused by Dalrymple's mistranslation of Spanish *loro* ("between white and black") as English "Negroes." This example illustrates how systematic human classification could be retrospectively attributed to sources that did not practice it.

By 1797, writing in German, Blumenbach relabelled Latin *varietates* ("varieties") as *Hauptrassen* ("principal races") and endorsed Kant's definition of race as "a stable, 'classificatory distinction of the animals of one and the same stem stock [Stammes], insofar as it is inevitably hereditary'" — a key step towards the biologization of race.

## Phase 3: Seaborne Ethnography and the Science of Race (1800–1850)

### Georg Forster vs. Kant: Empiricism versus Deductive Teleology

The younger Forster anticipated his father's binary empirically in his *Voyage Round the World* (1777). At Malakula (Vanuatu), Cook disparaged the inhabitants as "the most ugly, ill-proportioned people" he had seen — a reaction Douglas traces to Malakulan "indifference to European goods and refusal to trade provisions." Forster the savant, however, admired their "great sprightliness" and was genuinely curious.

In a 1786 paper, Forster challenged Kant's concept of hereditary race, arguing that "everything in holistic nature 'is connected by nuances' and 'does not follow our classifications.'" He cast Kant as the "biased systematist" against his own role as "clear-sighted and reliable empiricist." Kant responded defending teleological necessity: "you find in experience what you need only if you know beforehand what to look for." With respect to "the Papuans," Kant salvaged theory by reconfiguring them as "not aborigines" but "foreigners," displaced by unknown causes — circular reasoning to rescue his schema. Douglas reads this interchange as the fundamental opposition of voyaging naturalist (inductive) and sedentary philosopher (deductive) that structured race-science for the next half century.

### Quoy, Lesson, Hombron and Dumont d'Urville: Hardening Racial Taxonomy (1817–1840)

Four successive French expeditions (Freycinet 1817–20; Duperrey 1822–5; Dumont d'Urville 1826–9; Dumont d'Urville 1837–40) deployed surgeon-naturalists as anthropological observers under the taxonomic authority of Cuvier — whose "a priori conviction that racial inequality was a real, immutable product of human physical organization, especially cranial structure and the size of the brain" set the framework, even as he paid lip service to induction.

**Quoy** began with a humanist position but hardened. His first chapter on "the Papuans" — the category Papuan being historically ambiguous, applied to the inhabitants of the Papuan islands near Waigeo — found Indigenous behaviour confounding his racial presumptions: the Papuans' "mixing of individuals" produced "a multitude of nuances" making it "hard to determine the component races, 'denatured' by 'fortuitous crossings.'" He stole six skulls from a tomb and sent them to Gall (the phrenologist), whose "moral and intellectual faculties" readings Quoy took as confirming "mistrust" and "dreadful penchant for murder" — racialized attributes. Douglas argues: "I also read traces of the textual impact of Indigenous agency in that sustained humanist logic, testament to the prudent tactics the inhabitants adopted to control the ship's presence without overt menace or violence."

After Dumont d'Urville's voyage, Quoy froze Forster's fluid "two great varieties" into "two very distinct races" — "yellow" and "black" — as "real 'zoological characters'" and bleakly predicted the black race would "remain stationary in its ignorance and barbarity" under European influence. "Tonal shift" between his two chapters was not simply European discourse but "also an empirical register of his disconcerting exposure to the unpredictable, 'mistrustful' behaviour . . . of 'the black race', particularly in Vanikoro, in stark contrast to the 'joyous', sexually complaisant welcome given by the 'yellow race.'" Indigenous agency — different tactics of encounter management in different communities — thus materially shaped racial theorization.

**Lesson** produced a "convoluted regional racial hierarchy" subdividing "Oceanians" (limited to the eastern Pacific), "Carolinians" (Mongolic), Papuans, Tasmanians ("Caffro-Madagascan" branch), and "Australians" at the base. Personal experience regularly confounded his presumptions: he initially assigned Buka islanders to "the race of the Papuans" on physiognomy, then described nearby New Irelanders as a "Negro race" who "differed much" from Bukans — then corrected "much" to "little." Lesson later fantasized that Papua New Guineans were "evidently" descendants of ancient Egyptians on the basis of armband and headrest similarities.

**Dumont d'Urville** (1832) published the seminal paper that coined the tripartite Pacific nomenclature — **Melanesia** (from Greek *melas*, black — "the homeland of the black Oceanian race"), **Polynesia**, **Micronesia** — alongside a map partitioning the Pacific Islands by these racial-geographic categories. He transmuted Brosses's and Forster's conjectural migration histories "into chilling biological reality and colonial fact; a 'law of nature', resulting from 'organic differences' in the 'intellectual faculties' of the diverse races, ordained that the black 'must obey' the others 'or disappear' and that the white 'must dominate.'" His vitriol against Vanikorans was at odds with his own pragmatic account of friendly encounters there — the contradiction again traceable to the mismatch between Indigenous encounter tactics and racial schema.

**Hombron** (Dumont d'Urville's final voyage, 1837–40) went fully polygenist: three "natural families" of human species created sequentially; the "family of blacks" ranked lowest as "inferior species" of "primitive human creations." The expedition's phrenologist Dumoutier, conversely, was "an egalitarian humanist and staunch monogenist" who cast fifty-one plaster busts in the field through "patient negotiation, tact, and prolonged intimacy with Indigenous subjects" — busts that "preserve a powerful, intensely moving individual presence which disrupts the project of racial typification" even as they were systematically labelled with Dumont d'Urville's regional racial categories.

## Significance

1. **Terminology as colonial technology**: Place-names (Ladrones, Melanesia) embedded pejorative or racial meanings into cartographic infrastructure that persisted for generations.
2. **Indigenous agency distorted into racial character**: "Suspicion and hospitality" at Vanikoro, "refusal to trade" at Malakula — contextual tactics — were read as stable "natural" racial traits. European emotional responses to encounter were rationalized as objective scientific observation.
3. **Fact–system feedback loop**: Seaborne facts were appropriated by systems (savants in Europe); those systems then shaped what subsequent voyagers looked for and how they interpreted what they found. Kant's remark — "you find in experience what you need only if you know beforehand what to look for" — was, in practice, a description of how the science of race operated.
4. **Monogenism–polygenism spectrum**: The period 1780–1850 saw the progressive hardening of "varieties" into "principal races" and ultimately into "species" — from Buffon's transient climatic differences to Hombron's permanent divine creation of separate human families.
