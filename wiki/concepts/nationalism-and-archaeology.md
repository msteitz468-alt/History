# Nationalism and Archaeology

Nationalism and archaeology examines the intimate and often problematic relationship between archaeological research and the construction, legitimation, or contestation of national (or ethnic, regional) identities.

Archaeology supplies material "evidence" for claims of deep historical roots, continuity, superiority, or territorial rights. In return, nationalist projects have funded, directed, and sometimes distorted archaeological work.

European examples are especially stark because of the 19th–20th century nation-building context and the extreme case of Nazi Germany (Kossinna's methods used to claim "Germanic" territories in Poland and elsewhere; Himmler's Ahnenerbe).

The volume edited by Hodder (1991) shows how different national experiences produced different theoretical responses:
- In some cases, heavy ideological use of the past led to Marxist or critical reactions in the 1980s.
- In others, it produced a retreat into atheoretical description.
- Post-1989 Eastern Europe saw rapid re-evaluation of perspectives formed under Soviet domination.

A central theme is the need for strong theoretical (especially critical) archaeology precisely to guard against uncritical nationalist uses of the past.

## China: "Archaeology is national history or it is nothing"

The Chinese case (from [[liu-chinese-neolithic-2004|Li Liu 2004]]) is among the starkest
non-European examples. Su Bingqi: "The objective of modern Chinese archaeology is to construct
national history." Modern method was introduced by **foreigners** — Andersson at
[[yangshao-culture|Yangshao]] (1921), Licent and Teilhard de Chardin in the Ordos, Bohlin at
Zhoukoudian — but **Andersson's proposal that the Yangshao derived from the Near East** was
rejected by a generation of Western-trained Chinese scholars (Li Chi, from 1926) determined to
prove **indigenous origins**. The three flagship pre-1950 expeditions — Zhoukoudian (*Homo
erectus*), the Shang capital **Yinxu at [[anyang|Anyang]]** (oracle bones, bronzes), and the
Neolithic **Chengziyai** ([[longshan-culture|Longshan]]) — were chosen to anchor a continuous
Chinese cultural lineage. **Fu Sinian** distinguished "semi-Chinese" (*ban Han*) studies of
Sino-foreign contact from the truly important "completely Chinese" (*quan Han*) project of
building national history, against both **foreign diffusionism** and the **Doubters of
Antiquity** (*yigupai*) who attacked the textual record.

After 1950, under communism, a **multi-ethnic** nationalism shifted emphasis from the
Central-Plains *Zhongyuan* core to **multi-regional** development — institutionalised in Su
Bingqi's [[quxi-leixing|*quxi leixing*]] model and Fei Xiaotong's *duoyuan yiti*. Marxist
**Morgan-Engels** evolutionism additionally required all societies to pass from matrilineal to
patrilineal forms (a scheme recent osteology and mtDNA do not support). These pressures
converge on the live dispute over the **[[xia-dynasty-historicity|historicity of the Xia]]**:
the state-funded **Xia-Shang-Zhou Chronology Project** (1996–99, ~17 million yuan, 200+
experts) drew criticism precisely for its nationalist and methodological framing. See also the
debate over [[origins-chinese-civilization-monocentric-vs-multiregional|monocentric vs.
multi-regional origins]].

## Sources

Hodder (ed.) 1991 is a primary source for national case studies of these dynamics across Western, Northern, and Eastern Europe.

### Eastern European Examples from Hodder ed. 1991 (Range 05)
Post-WWI peace settlements in Central Europe redrew political boundaries that "did not coincide with ethnic boundaries, which in turn gave rise to strongly chauvinistic political views and arguments which often employed historical and archaeological data in order to prove their particular 'truths'." In Hungary, this produced acute sensitivities around ethnic attribution of assemblages (e.g., Bielo Brdo culture debates linking finds alternately to Slavic, Avar, or Hungarian groups; prehistory and Conquest-period homeland studies). "Most of the monographs and articles published on this subject revealed a strong ethnic interest and a preoccupation with the ethnic attribution of specific archaeological features, house types, tools, etc."

These pressures interacted with Soviet-imposed Marxism: "In the Marxist framework 'class' was the only accepted form for the description of social categories for a long time," delaying adoption of ranked or stratified society models until the late 1970s. Archaeological data were sometimes pressed into service for national/ethnic claims even as official ideology demanded class-based evolutionary narratives (often as "ideological garnish").

Czechoslovak and Hungarian chapters show how nationalist/chauvinist uses of the past compounded the constraints of Soviet-era ideological control, reinforcing the volume's broader argument that strong critical theory is required to counter uncritical deployment of archaeology for contemporary political identities. Post-1989 opening is framed as an opportunity (and risk) for re-evaluating these embedded national traditions.
