# Financing Greek Warfare

**Financing Greek warfare** covers how *poleis* and later Hellenistic kingdoms paid for men, ships, and campaigns — and how the means of payment reshaped institutions. The modern baseline is Vincent Gabrielsen’s chapter in [[sabin-van-wees-whitby-cambridge-history-greek-roman-warfare-2007|CHGRW]] (I.8), which treats booty, naval centralization, imperial revenue, and domestic fiscal devices as one system rather than as a side-issue of tactics. The long-duration process narrative is at [[processes/financing-greek-warfare]]. Cross-links: [[greek-warfare]], [[greek-naval-warfare]], [[trireme]], [[hoplite-warfare]], [[mercenaries-in-greek-classical]], [[athens]], [[sparta]].

## Booty as constant

Gabrielsen (CHGRW I.8) stresses that **booty united wealth and military power**: power seized wealth, and wealth enhanced power. Across the centuries from Homeric raids to Alexander’s conquests, for all the tactical and technical change, “war brought profits.” Booty was both a *raison d’être* and an indispensable *means* of warfare — especially for states that monopolized legitimate force — yet it seldom appears among formal “causes” of war, because private booty-chasers and communal warriors were conceptually segregated in the sources. The universal convention (Xen. *Cyr.* 7.4.73) held that captors owned persons and property of a taken city; “theft” versus “booty” was a victor’s framing distinction.

Van Wees (CHGRW I.9) cautions against overestimating pure economic motives: countryside plunder after evacuation was usually residual; captives rarely yielded huge ransoms (standard ransom c. 200 drachmas around 500 BCE, later often ~100); evenly matched wars wiped gains with losses. City sacks and extortion could yield large sums (pre-Persian-War extortion up to ~100 talents; Dionysius I allegedly 1,000 talents from one Etruscan temple), but rarely exceeded siege costs except for clearly superior powers. Booty, ransom, and enslavement were generally means of *meeting* war costs rather than primary goals.

## Naval finance and the monopolistic state

Ships were expensive to build, maintain, and staff; the rise and fall of Greek navies tracks concentrated political wealth (Strauss, CHGRW I.7; Gabrielsen I.8). Thucydides’ slogan — “War is not a matter of weapons, but of money which gives weapons their usefulness” (1.83.2–3) — became military wisdom. The naval sphere first made commissariat and **pay (*misthos*)** predominantly state responsibilities; Eretria by law institutionalized oarsmen’s *misthos* for distant campaigns already in the late sixth century. Operational pay commonly ran at 3 obols or, more usually, 1 drachma per day; the often-cited “one talent per trireme hull” lacks solid source support and understates open-ended operating costs.

Naval pay also became a **tactical weapon**: higher rates induced enemy crews to defect (e.g. Xen. *Hell.* 1.5.4). Alternative finance modes — state-level plunder and extortion, temple treasure (force or loans), foreign paymasters (Sparta–Persia from 412) — were inadequate, politically costly, or hostile. Classical **[[athens]]** instead rerouted domestic private wealth via the **trierarchy**: a rich man assigned for one year to captain a state-owned trireme and bear part of its running costs. Most Greek naval states used trierarchs, but Athens’ form was regime-specific — a democratic obligation on the wealthy that never fully satisfied infinite sea-power demands without exhausting the propertied class.

Army public funding remained incomplete longer: the earliest secure *misthos* for hoplite service (a Lindos decree, possibly c. 440) distinguishes soldiers paid publicly from those paid privately; part of expeditionary budgets was still soldiers’ own money into the fourth century.

## Imperial and domestic revenue (Athens as case)

Centralization of Athenian naval finance began under the Peisistratids (warfare funded from a five-percent tax) and was sealed when new ships (Corinth purchase 491; Themistoclean programme 483/2) were paid entirely from public funds and became public property. From 478/7 the **[[delian-league|Delian League]]** added a massive external fiscal branch: treasury on Delos with annual ~460 talents from non-ship contributors; removal to Athens 454; conversion of ship contributions to cash; the 425 assessment raised tribute-payers toward ~400 cities and yield toward ~1,000–1,500 talents (vs ~175 cities / 400 talents in 433/2). The 431 “iron reserve” of 1,000 talents remained untouched until 412; sacred treasuries on the Acropolis funded dockyards and walls (Callias decree).

Pericles’ pre-431 inventory (Thuc. 2.13) listed ~600 talents annual tribute, 6,000 talents surplus, 500 talents uncoined precious metal, temple monies, and gold plating on Athena’s statue — an implicit admission that imperial revenue might fail under escalated war. Major outlays included >1,400 talents for the Samos siege (440–39), ~2,000 talents for Potidaea, and nearly 6,000 talents borrowed from sacred treasuries 433/2–423/2. Whitby (CHGRW I.3) notes that Thucydides recognized naval costs better than most ancient writers yet deliberately soft-pedalled how close Periclean strategy came to bankrupting Athens early in the war and the financial overhaul (probably Cleon’s) visible in inscriptions.

After Mytilene (428/7) Athens needed funds “over and above regular sources”; thereafter foreign paymasters, allied contributions, plunder/extortion/protection-sale, and domestic devices alternated with shifting intensity to 323/2. The **Second Athenian League** (378/7) replaced imperial *phoros* with consent-based *syntaxeis* (known yields include Chabrias’ ~60 talents/year from islanders). After 411 plunder and extortion became central props — a hegemonial power “going raider” (Chabrias’ Naxos booty; Iphicrates and Timotheus paying forces from captives and loot). Domestic **eisphora** intensified from the 420s (428/7 levy of 200 talents); the Military Fund (*stratiōtika*) by 373 received eisphorai and wartime surpluses; later reforms included *proeisphora* (378/7), annual eisphora from 347/6, *symmories*, *syntrierarchy*, *antidosis*, and *epidoseis*. Gabrielsen’s conclusion: despite reforms, plunder and extortion remained indispensable for hegemonic financial independence.

## Mercenaries and standing forces

Mercenaries were not necessarily more expensive *per se* but often year-round; Isocrates claims Athens wasted 1,000 talents on them in the Social War alone (7.9). “Self-funding” generals were forced toward soft targets and ally extortion; Aeneas Tacticus warned never to hire more mercenaries than one’s own citizens (risk of takeover). See [[mercenaries-in-greek-classical]].

Regular pay later enabled **Macedonian** troops to operate outside the traditional campaigning season and train beyond neighbour standards — forces coming to resemble a standing army (Sekunda, CHGRW I.11A). Serrati (CHGRW II.14) unifies Philip–Alexander–Diadochoi–kingdom finance as war-oriented systems: plunder primary for many (especially Seleucids and early Successors), institutional tithes and estates for others (Ptolemies, Hieron) — monies used to wage further war rather than civilian prosperity.

## Historiography

CHGRW Part I is the most authoritative recent multi-author treatment of Greek war finance as a system. Literary sources (Thucydides, Xenophon, Demosthenes, Isocrates) understate equipment crises and fiscal improvisation that inscriptions (dockyard superintendent lists, tribute assessments) correct. Gabrielsen’s oligopolistic vs monopolistic typology of legitimate violence (private raiders coexisting with communal campaigns until Athens monopolized sea-violence) is the chapter’s master frame and should be read with Hall on interstate norms ([[greek-warfare]]).
