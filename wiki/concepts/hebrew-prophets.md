# The Hebrew Prophets and Biblical Literature

The **classical prophets** of [[kingdom-of-israel|Israel]] and [[kingdom-of-judah|Judah]]
— Amos, Hosea, Isaiah, Micah, Zephaniah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel — and the **biblical
literature** that the crisis of the Assyrian and Babylonian centuries produced. The
prophetic movement is the period's most consequential cultural achievement: it turned the
political catastrophe of two small kingdoms into the **scriptural and ethical foundation of
Judaism and, through it, of Christianity and Islam**. [[boardman-cah-v3p2-1991|CAH Vol. III,
Part 2]] (Chs. 29–31, T. C. Mitchell) treats the prophets as historical actors and surveys
the forms of Hebrew literature.

## The prophets in their political setting

Mitchell embeds each prophet in the events of his age — the prophets are **political actors**,
not detached mystics:

- **Amos and Hosea** (mid-8th c.) — denounced the **social polarization** of the prosperous
  age of Jeroboam II and Uzziah: the oppression of the poor by a rich minority, and cultic
  formalism without justice (see [[kingdom-of-israel]]).
- **Isaiah of Jerusalem** (from c. 740) — counsellor to **[[kingdom-of-judah|Ahaz]]** in the
  Syro-Ephraimite War (warning against the Assyrian alliance) and to **[[hezekiah|Hezekiah]]**
  in 701; condemned injustice and empty worship, named **Assyria as Yahweh's instrument**, and
  introduced the figure of an **ideal future ruler** (the "Messianic" passages). His younger
  contemporary **Micah** carried a parallel message and the same hope.
- **Zephaniah and Jeremiah** (late 7th c.) — supported **[[josiah|Josiah's]]** reform;
  **Jeremiah**, of a priestly family, prophesied from Josiah's thirteenth year to beyond the
  fall of Jerusalem, **counselling submission to Babylon** (for which he was beaten, imprisoned
  in a cistern, and nearly killed), and looking past disaster to restoration. His scribe
  **Baruch ben Neriah** (whose sealing may survive) wrote his scroll, which **Jehoiakim burned**
  column by column.
- **Ezekiel** (exiled 597) — prophesied to the [[babylonian-exile|exiles in Babylonia]], moving
  from warnings of Jerusalem's fall to the visions of restoration (the **valley of dry bones**,
  the ideal Temple).

## Emic and etic

**Emically**, a prophet (*nābî'*) delivered **a message from Yahweh to the people** — most often
as poetry, in the forms of **warning, condemnation, exhortation, and salvation-oracle**. The
prophets understood themselves as covenant enforcers, recalling Israel to the terms of its
agreement with Yahweh. **Etically**, modern scholarship reads them as the carriers of an
**ethical monotheism** and, through the **Deuteronomistic** reform and historiography, the
architects of the biblical account of the monarchy — judging every king by cultic fidelity.

## The forms of Hebrew literature

Mitchell's Ch. 31 surveys the literary types preserved in the Old Testament and attested in
inscriptions: **king-lists and annals** (the lost "Books of the Chronicles of the Kings"), the
**chronicle** form behind Kings' synchronistic framework, **law codes** (the Decalogue, the
Book of the Covenant, the Deuteronomic and Holiness codes — paralleling Mesopotamian and
Hittite codes), **covenant/treaty** documents (Deuteronomy itself, structured like a Near
Eastern treaty), **contracts** (Jeremiah's sealed-and-open deed of sale, cf. later Avroman and
Qumran parallels), **wisdom literature** (Proverbs, Job, Ecclesiastes; *məšālîm*, riddles,
fables, **acrostics**), **hymns, laments, and prayers** (the Psalms), and the **prophetic
oracles** themselves. Hebrew epigraphy — the **Gezer Calendar**, the **Siloam inscription**,
the **Lachish** and **Arad ostraca**, the **Mesad Hashavyahu** plea, the **Moabite Stone** —
supplies the contemporary documentary base.

## The Deuteronomistic History

A central etic construct: the books **Joshua–Judges–Samuel–Kings** form a connected history
written from the **theology of Deuteronomy**, judging the monarchy by the standard of cultic
fidelity and centralization on Jerusalem. Mitchell treats **Kings** as compiled c. 586–539,
probably by prophetic circles, from named earlier sources — a theological explanation of why
the kingdom fell. The **Book of the Law** found under [[josiah|Josiah]] (623/622), identified
with an early Deuteronomy, is the reform's charter and the linchpin of the whole construct.

## Contested questions

The field is among the most disputed in ancient history:
- The **date and unity of Isaiah** — whether chapters 40–66 (referring to [[cyrus-the-great|
  Cyrus]] by name) are eighth-century prediction or exilic composition ("Second Isaiah");
  Mitchell notes the majority view favours later authorship.
- The **date and origin of Deuteronomy** — from Mosaic to post-exilic.
- The **historical reliability of Chronicles and Daniel** — Mitchell uses Chronicles
  critically but not dismissively; he sets Daniel aside as a source.
- The **origin of the synagogue** — possibly in the Babylonian Exile, but unattested as a
  building before the late centuries BCE.

## Limitations and Critique

The surviving corpus is **only the religiously acceptable remnant**: heterodox literature in
the Canaanite–Phoenician tradition (the Yahweh-and-"his-Asherah" invocations at **Kuntillet
Ajrud**) was eliminated in transmission. The prophets' own polemic shapes the picture of a
pervasively apostate Israel needing reform. Read as history, the prophetic and Deuteronomistic
literature is **a partisan source whose theology is itself the object of study** — but it is
also the most articulate self-witness any ancient Near Eastern people has left.
