# Roman Aristocratic Politics

How the Roman Republic was actually governed between the Punic Wars and the
Gracchi — an **oligarchy operating through popular forms**. Analysed in
[[astin-cah-v8-1989|CAH VIII]] Ch. 6 (Astin), who both sets out the dominant
modern model and registers its limits.

## The constitutional setting

Polybius' famous "**mixed constitution**" — magistrates (monarchic), Senate
(aristocratic), assemblies (democratic) — but Astin stresses that even Polybius saw
the **Senate** as central. Magistrates (two **consuls**, the **praetors**, the
**censors**, ten **tribunes of the plebs**) held *imperium* or special powers for a
single year. The **assemblies** elected officials, passed laws, and declared war,
but did not deliberate, could not amend, and were structurally weighted toward the
wealthy (the **comitia centuriata**' first-class advantage). The **Senate** —
~300 life-members, the only true deliberative body — controlled the treasury,
provinces, armies, and diplomacy; its *senatus consulta*, though formally advisory,
were normally the effective decisions. Only once (the 200 war on Macedon) is a
senatorial recommendation recorded as rejected by an assembly — and that was soon
reversed.

## The factional model and its critics

The influential modern analysis (**Gelzer**, **Münzer**, **Scullard**) holds:
- power rested not on law but on the **social nexus** — *clientela*, *amicitia*,
  kinship, the chains of obligation through which votes could be mobilised;
- **nobilitas** (descent from a consul) conferred a hereditary electoral advantage,
  and a few families held a disproportionate share of consulships;
- politics was therefore primarily **personal and family competition for office,
  honour, and *gloria*** — not a contest of programmes or ideologies; and
- leading families formed enduring **"factions"** whose rivalries structured public
  life.

Astin records the **serious criticisms** (his own, Brunt's, Millar's): the
prosopographical criteria for reconstructing "factions" are often weak; *nobilitas*
and *amicitia* have been over-defined; the dominance of "consular families" is
overstated (every generation produced new men); the share of votes controllable by
social pressure was exaggerated; and individuals cannot be slotted into
self-contained factions in a small, cross-tied elite. **What survives the critique:**
aristocratic competitiveness and the patronage nexus *were* the major sources of
political power and the chief restraint on the popular elements — yet **electoral
competition was largely divorced from policy**, because the great decisions lay with
a Senate not subject to re-election.

## The cursus honorum and oligarchic self-regulation

The system regulated itself against over-mighty individuals. After
[[scipio-africanus|Scipio Africanus']] near-decade of continuous *imperium* and
Flamininus' meteoric rise, a series of rules constrained the career-ladder
(*cursus honorum*): the praetorship made a prerequisite for the consulship (197);
the ten-year bar on iterating a magistracy; the **lex Villia annalis (180)**
fixing minimum ages and a two-year interval between curule offices; and finally the
**total ban on second consulships (c. 151)**. No dictator was appointed after 202.
The same impulse drove the concern with ***mores*** — censorial *nota*, the
**sumptuary laws** (lex Orchia 182, Fannia 161, Didia 143), and the prosecutions
([[cato-the-elder|Cato]] the great practitioner; the "trials of the Scipios") — a
defensive self-regulation of an elite whose new imperial wealth was straining its
own values.

## Erosion toward 133

The system's "forces for change" (Astin): the **fading of great diplomacy** after
146; the growing readiness to **override the cursus rules** for popular military
favourites (the repeated illegal consulships of [[scipio-aemilianus|Scipio
Aemilianus]], 147 and 134, against the Senate's wishes); the **ballot laws** (lex
Gabinia 139, lex Cassia 137) shielding the vote from social supervision; the
**recruiting crises** (boycotts, the imprisonment of consuls by tribunes from 151);
and the building **agrarian crisis** ([[agrarian-crisis-roman-republic]]). These
converged in the explosion of **133** (the Gracchi — CAH IX).

## Limitations and critique

The factional model is **contested** (see above): it risks reducing all politics to
competition for office and ignoring genuine issues of policy and the social-economic
divisions that erupted after 133. Astin's balance — competitive oligarchy *plus*
real popular institutions *plus* emergent structural strain — is the cautious modern
position.

## Flower, *Roman Republics* (2010)

Flower’s multi-republic scheme ([[roman-republics-periodization]]) reframes mid-Republican aristocratic politics as the culture of successive **republics of the *nobiles*** (her Republics 3–5: roughly 300–180, 180–139, 139–88), not a single static oligarchy. Under those republics, she stresses, **individual competition and ambition supported rather than subverted** stable government: compromise and cooperation created the *impression* of consensus after fierce debate. *Res publica* for her implies participation of the governed, transparency, and unity of citizens transcending permanent parties — fundamentally at odds with fixed factional allegiance. Power was shared by election among those meeting census and military-service requirements, not a closed hereditary caste; family status had to be reproduced each generation.

Peaceful institutional reform is central to her second-century picture: the *lex Villia annalis* (180) as a decisive marker fixing minimum ages and the *cursus* hierarchy; calendar shift of the political year to 1 January (153); first permanent jury court for *repetundae* (149); and the phased **secret-ballot laws** (139 *Gabinia* through 104 *Domitia*) as the most useful marker of her unstable final *nobiles* republic. Reform calls by Gaius Gracchus or Livius Drusus assumed reform was integral to *nobiles* ethos, not alien to the system.

After the 80s collapse, Flower draws a sharp contrast with Ciceronian-era obstruction: **Cato/Bibulus-style obstruction had no real connection** to third/second-century negotiation and consensus-building. Sulla’s enlarged multitiered senate (opinion-makers vs lifelong jurors; automatic recruitment from ex-quaestors; career pyramid 20–8–2) did not fulfill the role assigned to it and helped produce the rise of Pompey, Crassus, and Caesar; narrower first-century use of *nobilis* (consulars/descendants) was a closing of ranks against Sullan novices and Italians. Ciceronian *regret* for lost republican culture, she notes, was not matched by practical legislation to re-create older behaviors. Source: [[flower-roman-republics-2010]].
