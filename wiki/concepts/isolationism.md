# Isolationism

## Overview
Isolationism is a category of foreign policy advocated by leaders who assert that their nation's long-term interests are best served by keeping the affairs of other countries at a distance. In United States history, isolationism peaked during the interwar period (1920s-1930s), leading to the passage of the Neutrality Acts and strong political resistance against joining World War II, championed by groups like the America First Committee.

## Interwar Context and Lindbergh's Role
The interwar isolationist movement was driven by a belief that American participation in the First World War had failed to solve European problems and had suppressed civil liberties at home (a view strongly held by Lindbergh's father, Congressman C.A. Lindbergh Sr.). Charles Lindbergh became the movement's most prominent spokesperson, arguing that the U.S. was protected by the Atlantic and Pacific oceans and should build a massive, defensive air force rather than intervening in foreign conflicts. The movement collapsed immediately following the attack on Pearl Harbor.
