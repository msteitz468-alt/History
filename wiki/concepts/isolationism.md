# Isolationism

## Overview
Isolationism is a category of foreign policy advocated by leaders who assert that their nation's long-term interests are best served by keeping the affairs of other countries at a distance. In United States history, isolationism peaked during the interwar period (1920s-1930s), leading to the passage of the Neutrality Acts and strong political resistance against joining World War II, championed by groups like the America First Committee.

## Interwar Context and Lindbergh's Role
The interwar isolationist movement was driven by a belief that American participation in the First World War had failed to solve European problems and had suppressed civil liberties at home (a view strongly held by Lindbergh's father, Congressman C.A. Lindbergh Sr.). Charles Lindbergh became the movement's most prominent spokesperson, arguing that the U.S. was protected by the Atlantic and Pacific oceans and should build a massive, defensive air force rather than intervening in foreign conflicts. The movement collapsed immediately following the attack on Pearl Harbor.

## The isolationist self-description (Flynn 1944, attributed)
[[flynn-john-t|John T. Flynn]] — chairman of the America First Committee's New
York chapter — rejected the "isolationist" label as an administration smear:
in [[flynn-as-we-go-marching-1944|*As We Go Marching*]] he distinguishes
genuine "internationalism" (world cooperation; he notes many League of Nations
supporters opposed US entry into WWII) from "imperialism" wearing
internationalist dress, and argues the same administration that broke up the
1933 London Economic Conference in defense of domestic autarky later claimed
the internationalist mantle against its critics.

## Hartz: isolationism and messianism as one dialectic (1955)

[[hartz-louis|Hartz]] ([[hartz-liberal-tradition-1955]]) reframes isolationism not as a
discrete policy tradition but as one pole of a single "Americanist" psychology: "An absolute
national morality is inspired either to withdraw from 'alien' things or to transform them: it
cannot live in comfort constantly by their side." Hence Jeffersonian withdrawal →
[[wilson-woodrow|Wilsonian]] crusading → Harding's backlash ("One cannot be half American and
half European") → the New Deal's "New Isolationism" (Nye Committee, neutrality acts, the
America First defections of Stuart Chase, Jerome Frank, and Charles Beard) → WWII
interventionism → Cold War globalism. Harding's nationalism is "actually three faceted:
isolationism, Whiggery, and liberal hysteria" — the 1920s red scare inseparable from the
retreat from the League. Hartz also notes that before WWI the Lockean creed weighed *against*
empire: Bryan and the Anti-Imperialist League held "a stronger grip on 'Americanism'" than
Mahan and TR, and the 1898–1900 debate was strikingly free compared with the WWI-era
suppression of dissenters like Randolph Bourne. See [[lockean-consensus]].
