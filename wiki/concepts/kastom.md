## Definition

Kastom (also spelled *kastam*, *kastomu*) is the Melanesian Pidgin English term for "custom" as a politicised idiom of collective identity, tradition, and political legitimacy. It emerged prominently in the 1970s during the independence movements of Papua New Guinea, Solomon Islands, and what is now Vanuatu, where it served as the cultural foundation of nationalist rhetoric.

As [[denoon-cambridge-pacific-islanders-1997]] defines the concept: "In the Pidgin Englishes of Melanesia, politicised tradition has come to be called kastom ... Islanders again struggled to redefine and revalue their 'custom' in terms of how these practices might serve, or subvert, the new imperatives of national identity and unity."

The key analytical point made by Lindstrom (the section's author) is that kastom is **not the same as actual tradition**: "Custom is neither fixed nor unconsciously experienced: its definitions and evaluations are constantly debated ... Melanesians have long been remaking those worlds: they have a tradition of remaking tradition."

## Distinction: Emic vs. Etic

- **Emic:** Kastom is what Melanesian communities, politicians, and activists call their own cultural practices when asserting them as politically legitimate — as something that defines who belongs to the national community and how members should behave.
- **Etic:** Scholars (Lindstrom, Keesing, Hobsbawm's "invention of tradition" tradition) treat kastom as a modern political resource — a selective, constructed appeal to a past that never existed in the form invoked.

## Origins and Political Context

Kastom's salience as a political term rose through two earlier waves:

1. **Cargo cults and colonial resistance:** During the colonial period, movements such as the John Frum movement on Tanna revived exchanges, dance, and kava drinking — "kastom received a positive interpretation to counterbalance negative, Christian readings of Melanesian culture." Colonial encounter made people look hard at their cultural practices: some were abandoned, some revived, some remade.

2. **Independence politics (1970s–1980s):** The term spread region-wide. Narokobi's "The Melanesian Way," developed in his Papua New Guinea *Post-Courier* column between 1976 and 1978, argued that "Melanesians are guided by a common cultural and spiritual unity." "Constructions of kastom contributed to broader political rhetorics such as 'Melanesian socialism', Fijian vakavanua (the 'way of the land'), and 'the Melanesian Way'."

Kastom served independence movements because: "Political self-determination could not be achieved without first of all determining cultural identity. The cultural traits of the Papua New Guinean make him what he is... and not a Fijian or even an Australian."

## Institutional Embodiment

Kastom was woven into Pacific constitutions and legal structures:
- Vanuatu's constitution "declares the state to be 'founded on traditional Melanesian values'" and established a National Council of Chiefs to advise on custom
- Papua New Guinea's constitution contains similar provisions
- Solomon Islands in the 1980s recognised island councils of chiefs
- In Vanuatu, kastom was invoked to support customary land tenure, vernacular education, and local consensus-based decision-making

## Contradictory Capacities

The analytical heart of the kastom concept is its ambivalence. As [[denoon-cambridge-pacific-islanders-1997]] argues: "Kastom can serve as an idiom of national identity and as a mechanism of state regularisation and control; but it may enunciate opposition and resistance with equal facility."

On one hand: "Once again, but on a larger scale, kastom provided a rhetoric to define the national community for its citizens."

On the other: "Where state discourse over-inflates shared kastom as a rhetorical adhesive, people may point to unshared custom to reject and withdraw from that state." The Bougainville rebellion and Vanuatu's internal revolts were "phrased partly in an idiom of unshared kastom."

Gender is a key site of kastom's contested use: "Debates about the utility of custom have focused on the consonance of tradition and democracy and on the position of women. Some cite kastom to reduce women's public role; others denounce kastom as an inadequate primer of modern gender relations." Some Melanesian women explicitly "criticise kastom as a male tool used to subordinate women."

## Scholarly Debates

The "invention of tradition" debate is central to kastom scholarship. Key positions:
- **Keesing ("Creating the Past")** — kastom represents a selective, politically motivated invention of tradition by elites that may misrepresent or suppress actual historical diversity
- **Lindstrom** — the borrowing/trading of customs is itself a long-standing Melanesian practice; "it makes better sense to talk about a broad, regional cultural heritage, rather than the traditions of single community"
- **Trask ("Natives and Anthropologists")** — indigenous scholars resist outside scholars "pontificating about indigenous identities"; authenticity debates are less important than political outcomes
- **Narokobi (*The Melanesian Way*)** — the most articulate advocacy of Melanesian cultural distinctiveness as a real and defensible political foundation

## Limitations

The concept is specifically Melanesian and does not straightforwardly translate to Polynesian contexts (where fa'aSamoa, or the Tongan "way," serve analogous but structurally different roles given chiefly hierarchy). The Fijian *vakavanua* is closely related but embedded in a different chiefly political structure.
