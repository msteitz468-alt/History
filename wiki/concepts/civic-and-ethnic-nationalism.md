# Civic and Ethnic Nationalism

The civic/ethnic typology is the dominant scheme for classifying modern
[[nationalism]]. **Civic nationalism** holds that "citizenship and national
identity are congruent," so that "all the citizens of a civic nation-state are
regarded as sharing the same national identity," resting solely on common
citizenship and shared political institutions. **Ethnic nationalism**, by
contrast, "is based on a perception of blood relation and common descent." Azar
Gat and Alexander Yakobson, in *[[sources/gat-nations-2013|Nations]]* (2013),
treat the distinction as real but "greatly overdrawn," arguing that almost no
[[nation]] is divorced from [[ethnicity]].

## The standard typology (emic and etic)

"The distinction between civic and ethnic nationalism was made by Hans Kohn on
the basis of earlier ideas by Ernest Renan and Friedrich Meinecke, and has been
in common use ever since." Civic nationalism is "defined as belonging to a
political community, state, and territory, whereas ethnic nationalism is based
on a perception of blood relation and common descent." The two are mapped onto a
geographic and moral divide: "The civic nation has been identified with a benign
Western European liberal model epitomized by Britain and France, whereas
xenophobic ethnic nationalism supposedly characterized Central and Eastern
Europe." A further claim grounds the split historically: "in Central-Eastern
Europe emergent nations preceded and created their states, rather than the other
way around as in the West. Lacking a political definition, the nations of
Central-Eastern Europe were obliged to define themselves in ethnic terms."

The labels carry normative weight. As Yakobson notes, "'Civic' and 'ethnic' are
both loaded terms. 'Ethnic nationalism' is widely used in a sense that is at
least mildly pejorative," and "anything designated as 'civic' is bound to enjoy
greater prestige in modern liberal-democratic parlance than anything called
'ethnic.'" The terms are thus partly emic — used by actors and states, which
"carefully eschew" the ethnic label — and partly etic analytical categories.

## Gat & Yakobson's critique

Gat treats the dichotomy as "largely misleading, both with respect to Europe and
elsewhere," demonstrating that "whatever their civic features, national
identities are hardly ever divorced from shared ethnic realities." Even the
paradigm "civic" nations rest on an ethnic nucleus: Britain and France are
"simply cases wherein a state based on a strong ethnic nucleus, the English
people and the langue d'oïl-speaking population of northern France . . .
successfully spread their rule and culture through a combination of coercion and
dominance." France, "the paradigmatic 'civic' nation, has been entirely
intolerant toward all other ethnic identities and cultures within the country,"
while many "'ethnic' nations grant their national minorities . . . extensive
cultural and even political autonomy." The kin element survives even there:
*patrie* "is literally the land of the fathers," and Sieyès held the French
"descended from the Gauls and Romans."

Civic nationalism, on this view, "generates assimilation into the ethnonational
community, either as an explicit ('republican') requirement or as a tacit
assumption" — the French "formal requirement" versus the British "tacit
assumption" that newcomers "would sooner or later become 'like us.'" The verdict
is blunt: "The naive ideological fiction that civic nations rest purely on
citizenship and shared political institutions has been just that: at best an
ideological expression of a desire for tolerance . . . and at worst a deep state
of 'false consciousness.'" "Undoubtedly, all nations have a strong civic
element," but "very few nations do not rely on a sense of shared kin–culture
identity as the basis for civic cooperation."

In place of the dichotomy Gat proposes a **more helpful distinction**: whether a
nation's concept of citizenship "leans toward territory and culture or more
toward descent and culture" — both formulations "incorporate elements of
ethnicity." "Hardly any nation exists based solely or even mainly on political
allegiance to state and constitution."

## Modernity: the will of the people

The typology belongs to the modern release of nationalism. Gat argues that the
sweeping processes of modernization — industrialization, urbanization, mass
society, schooling, and conscription — "rather than inaugurating nationalism, at
one and the same time released, transformed, and enhanced it, while greatly
increasing its legitimacy." Popular sovereignty was central: "once people . . .
are given the freedom to express and enact their will, they almost invariably
elect to live in their own national state," and "free government has scarcely
existed in the absence of a national community" (citing J. S. Mill, 1861). After
Alsace-Lorraine was stripped from France, Renan "advanced a voluntarist concept
of nationality as a 'daily plebiscite'" — yet Gat insists "the perception of a
common nationhood strongly correlates with shared kin–culture identity." See
[[nationalism]].

## Cases

- **Immigrant societies** (USA, Canada, Australia, New Zealand): often called
  "quintessentially civic," yet "the immigrant countries' common national
  identity is far more substantial than that." Immigrant communities "merge into
  a shared, amalgamated new culture," with intermarriage rising "steeply" from
  the third generation, producing "new and very distinctive peoples." The United
  States rests on "a deep-rooted, widely shared, and proudly manifested American
  culture, in effect a genuine American ethnic identity." The *Federalist*
  invoked "a people descended from the same ancestors, speaking the same
  language, professing the same religion." Canada is the contrast: the Québécois,
  un-merged and territorially concentrated, make it "a binational state of
  sorts."
- **Latin America**: new ethnicities forged "through processes of integration,
  hybridization, and amalgamation" — the *mestizo* idealized in post-1917 Mexico,
  with "the spread of the Spanish language" and "the fusion of the Spanish and
  Indian grand traditions into collective national histories."
- **Sub-Saharan Africa**: colonial borders "drawn . . . with little regard for
  ethnic realities" left states without a "core ethnic majority" or
  "Staatsvolk." With "about 3,000 different linguistic ethne," purely civic
  nation-building is "truly extraordinary," and the continent has been "rife with
  intra- and interstate wars of an ethnic character."
- **Asia**: Indonesia, "as multiethnic as can be," is "far from being based on
  civic principles alone" because the state "successfully created a shared
  supra-culture, above all a common language" — "probably the postcolonial
  nation closest to historical France." India, "the model and great hope of
  postcolonial, multiethnic 'civic' nationalism," is "a civic and highly
  pluralistic nation," yet its unity rests on "a broadly defined Hindu identity."

## Yakobson's normative chapter

Yakobson (ch. 7) addresses the liberal-democratic state, which is rarely
ethnically neutral. Civic nationalism, "that insists on the full congruence
between nationhood and citizenship," includes all who wish to belong but offers
those "who regard themselves as belonging to a distinct national group" no
"official acknowledgment of their identity," and may pressure them to assimilate.
The ethnic model, distinguishing national identity from citizenship, "gives
legitimacy . . . to the existence of national minorities" but risks
"undermining the minority's status as fully-fledged and equal members of the
civic community." Even where minority rights are honored, "the national identity
of the nation-state itself reflects that of the majority rather than of the whole
citizenry." "Both civic and ethnic nationalism have their inherent advantages,
yet each comes at a price." Kymlicka's point is endorsed: "all the liberal
states do in fact give preference to a particular ethnicity, their own, as
reflected most notably in their standard language."

## Limitations and Critiques

Gat and Yakobson do not discard the categories but deny they form a clean
dichotomy. Their objections:

- **The "civic" exemplars are ethnic.** France, Britain, even Switzerland and
  Belgium are "actually divided and dominated politically by intensely ethnic
  categories" or built on a dominant ethnic nucleus.
- **Civic nationalism is not more liberal.** Yakobson disputes "the assumption
  that civic nationalism is inherently more liberal than ethnic nationalism,
  stressing its tendency to deny or even suppress ethnocultural differences
  within the civic nation."
- **The historical premise is false.** The claim that Eastern nations preceded
  their states while Western states preceded their nations is challenged by Gat's
  case for [[premodern-european-nationhood]] and the [[antiquity-of-nations]].
- **Purely civic nationhood is rare, not normal.** Switzerland and Singapore are
  exceptions mistaken for types; "the exception . . . has been regarded as
  typical."

The authors stress this is "a descriptive not a normative book"; their target is
not the value of tolerance or minority rights but the empirical claim that
nations can rest on civic allegiance alone. The dichotomy nonetheless retains
analytical use as a spectrum — territory-plus-culture versus descent-plus-culture
— and remains contested across the scholarly literature.
