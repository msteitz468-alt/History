# Luke-Acts

**Luke-Acts** is the modern scholarly designation for the two-part narrative comprising the
Gospel of Luke and the Acts of the Apostles, understood as a single literary and theological
work by one anonymous author (conventionally "Luke"). The Gospel narrates the life and
teaching of Jesus; Acts continues the story into the spread of the Jesus movement from
Jerusalem to Rome, centered on Peter and then Paul. Together they are the longest single
contribution to the New Testament and a principal narrative source for
[[origins-of-christianity|the origins of Christianity]].

## Emic vs. Etic

- *Emic*: the texts present themselves as an ordered account "of the things accomplished
  among us" (Luke 1:1–4), addressed to a "Theophilus," claiming to draw on prior sources
  and eyewitnesses.
- *Etic*: "Luke-Acts" as a unified object of study is a twentieth-century analytic
  construct (Cadbury). Treating the two books as one work — with shared authorship, style,
  theology, and a prologue linking them — is a scholarly decision, not a datum of the text.

## As a Historical Source

Luke-Acts is read both as a window onto the early Jesus movement and, increasingly, as a
**literary composition** that selects, shapes, and silences. [[cobb-slavery-gender-luke-acts-2019|Cobb]]
and others read it as a **polyphonic** narrative ([[dialogism-and-carnival|Bakhtinian
dialogism]]) that incorporates many voices — including those of women and the enslaved —
even where its dominant frame marginalizes them. Its social world reflects the realities of
[[slavery-in-early-christianity|Roman slavery]], patronage, and [[kyriarchy|kyriarchal]]
hierarchy.

## Dating and Genre (contested)

- **Dating**: the Gospel of Luke is conventionally dated c. 80–90 CE (scholarly consensus);
  Acts was traditionally placed soon after, but the Acts Seminar and others argue for a
  composition extending into the **second century**.
- **Genre**: debated — variously read as ancient biography, history, epic imitation
  (Homeric), or as participating in the [[ancient-novel|novelistic]]/menippean literature of
  the early empire (prison breaks, shipwrecks, the "we" passages of Acts). Cobb treats it as
  part of the developing menippean genre.

## Limitations and Critique

Using Luke-Acts as history requires distinguishing the author's theological program from the
events narrated; its portrayals of women, the poor, and the enslaved are shaped by
first-century rhetorical conventions and by the author's controlling hand, and cannot be
read naively as social description.
