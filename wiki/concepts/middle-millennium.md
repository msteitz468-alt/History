## Definition

The **"Middle Millennium"** is the periodization device by which
[[kedar-cwh-v5-2015|CWH V]] frames the thousand years from **500 to 1500 CE** as a
coherent unit of world history. The label is deliberately chosen to avoid both
"the Middle Ages" — a Eurocentric and Europe-specific term, defined by its
relation to classical antiquity and the Renaissance — and "the medieval period,"
with its connotations of a backward interlude. Instead the editors define the unit
by a global process rather than a European chronology: it is the age in which
**webs of exchange and conflict thickened** across Afro-Eurasia (and, in a muted
and separate parallel, within the Americas) until, by 1500, a "first globalization"
of trade, religion, technology, and ideas approached genuinely trans-hemispheric
scope.

The frame maps onto the wiki's [[early-middle-ages]] (600–1000), [[high-middle-ages]]
(1000–1300), and [[late-middle-ages]] (1300–1500) period pages — though CWH V's
unit begins a century earlier, at 500 CE, folding in the age of [[justinian|Justinian]],
the Plague of Justinian (541–750), and the rise of [[islam|Islam]].

## The Organizing Argument

Kedar and Wiesner-Hanks identify **three types of "regular trans-civilizational
interaction"** that bound large-area units together and structure the volume:

1. **War and conquest that led to the formation of states** — Byzantium, Sui–Tang
   and later China, the Abbasid Caliphate, the [[mongol-empire|Mongols]], the Aztec
   and Inca empires.
2. **The development of [[afro-eurasian-world-system|trade networks]]** — increasingly
   interlinked until, c. 1250–1350, they formed a single loose system (Abu-Lughod's
   eight circuits) spanning England to China.
3. **The emergence of religious ecumenes** — the expanding Buddhist, Christian, and
   Islamic "world religions" as durable arenas of cultural integration (more durable,
   the editors note, than most empires — China excepted).

Beneath these documented "regular" interactions they posit a **substratum of
sporadic transmission** — slaves, captives, single travellers, migrating tales and
techniques — that left few records but mattered (their examples: the
Barlaam-and-Josaphat legend carrying the Buddha's life-story into the Christian
saints' calendar; the diffusion of paper from China to Islam to Europe; the
apparent transfer of metallurgy from the Andes to Mesoamerica). Their methodological
watchword: **"documentation should not be equated with reality."**

## Emic vs. Etic

The Middle Millennium is a purely **etic** construct — no one living within it
conceived of a unified 500–1500 epoch, and (the editors stress) almost no one
before 1500 could conceive of both hemispheres at all. The chapter demonstrates
this through the period's **cartography** (world maps grew more accurate and more
cross-culturally borrowed — Korean *Kangnido*, al-Idrisi, the portolans — yet
remained partial) and its **historiography** (which was overwhelmingly
civilization-centric; the lone near-exception, [[rashid-al-din|Rashid al-Din's]]
*Compendium of Chronicles*, was itself a product of Mongol imperial integration).

## Limitations and Critiques

- **Arbitrary boundaries.** 500 and 1500 are round numbers; the editors concede the
  unit is an analytic convenience, not a set of sharp historical breaks. Different
  regions enter and exit its characteristic dynamics at different times.
- **Afro-Eurasian bias.** The "thickening webs" thesis is strongest for the Old
  World; the Americas and Oceania are integrated internally but remain outside the
  trans-hemispheric system, so the frame fits them less well — a tension the volume
  acknowledges by treating them in their own right (Smith, MacCormack).
- **Continuity with the Cambridge series.** The frame extends the network thesis of
  [[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|CWH IV]] (*states, empires, and networks*) and anticipates
  the "proto-globalization" conclusion (Olstein, Ch 25). Whether "Middle Millennium,"
  "post-classical," or "the global Middle Ages" is the best label is itself an open
  question in world-historical [[periodization-world-history|periodization]].
