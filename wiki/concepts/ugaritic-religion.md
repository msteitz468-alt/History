# Ugaritic (Canaanite) Religion and the Ras Shamra Texts

The **mythology and cult of Late Bronze Age Canaan**, known above all from the
**alphabetic-cuneiform literary tablets of [[ugarit|Ugarit]]** (Ras Shamra) — the
single most important source for [[canaanite-civilization|Canaanite]] religion, and
the closest comparative background to the religion of the Hebrew Bible. Before these
texts, Canaanite belief was known only from hostile Old Testament polemic and the
late, garbled **Sanchuniathon** tradition (via Philo of Byblos and Eusebius). Treated
in [[edwards-cah-v2p2-1975|CAH Vol. II, Part 2]] (Ch. XXI(b) §V, Drower).

## The pantheon

- **El** (Lutpan, "the Kindly One") — the **high god**, an old white-bearded figure,
  "creator of creatures" and "father of mankind," dwelling "where the two rivers meet
  the two oceans," father of the "seventy gods." Pre-eminent but remote: he plays
  little active part in the surviving myths and has no attested temple at Ugarit.
- **Baʿal** (Hadad; "Aliyan, the Victor") — the **storm-god**, "Rider on the
  Clouds," lord of lightning, rain and fertility, depicted treading the mountain
  tops with thunderbolt in hand. The central, dynamic figure of the myths. "Baʿal"
  ("Lord") was also a title of local city-gods (Melqart = the Baʿal of Tyre). His was
  the largest temple at Ugarit.
- **El's consort Athirat/Asherah** ("Lady Athirat of the Sea," Mistress of the
  gods); **ʿAnath**, Baʿal's warrior-sister who "wades in blood" like Kali; **ʿAthtart
  / Astarte** (Ishtar's West-Semitic form); **Dagan** (Baʿal's father, corn); the
  death-god **Mot** and the sea-god **Yam**; **Resheph** (pestilence/war); the
  craftsman-god **Kothar-and-Khasis** (said to hail from Caphtor/Crete).

## The myths

The great poems, copied by King Niqmaddu's scribes but embodying much older
tradition:
- **The Baʿal Cycle** — Baʿal's combat with **Yam** (the chaos-sea), the building of
  his palace, and his death-and-return struggle with **Mot** (drought/death), during
  which the earth's fertility fails and the inadequate ʿAthtar cannot replace him
  until ʿAnath revives him. Long read as a vegetation/fertility drama; the volume
  leans toward Baʿal as the **rain-god** whose seasonal eclipse and return mirror the
  agricultural year.
- **The Legend of Aqhat** — son of King Danel; ʿAnath covets his bow and has him
  killed, bringing drought; his sister Pughat sets out to avenge him (the ending,
  presumably his resurrection, is lost).
- **The Legend of Keret (Kirta)** — a king who loses his house and, by El's
  guidance, wins a wife and heirs, then falls sick and faces a rebellious son. Read
  variously as the memory of a North-Syrian raid on Edom or as a dynastic myth of
  Ugarit.

## Cult and afterlife

Canaanite temples were simple (anteroom, pillared hall, raised sanctuary with
altar); the two great Ugaritic temples of Baʿal and Dagan crowned the tell. Much
Israelite **sacrificial vocabulary and festival structure** has Ugaritic parallels,
and the **Solomonic temple** plan (forecourt, "Holy of Holies," Tyrian workmen) may
follow Canaanite design. Divination (liver-models, hepatoscopy) and medico-magical
incantations were borrowed from Babylonia. The Canaanites had no Egyptian-style faith
in the soul's survival (Aqhat denies immortality), yet richly furnished the family
vaults beneath their houses and fed the dead through libation-pipes.

## Why it matters: the contested edges

- **Biblical background.** The Ras Shamra texts revolutionized study of the Hebrew
  Bible: shared phraseology with the **Song of Deborah, the Psalms and the
  Pentateuch** (some argued for a 2nd-millennium date for those passages), the
  El/Baʿal/Asherah pantheon behind Israelite religion and its polemics, and shared
  sacrificial/festival terms. *How* directly Israel inherited from Canaan is the
  contested core.
- **Sensational claims questioned.** The volume is **cautious** about lurid
  reconstructions: it finds **no proof** in the texts that *qdš* meant "sacred
  prostitute" in the 2nd millennium, nor that the *mlk* sacrifice was the burning of
  infants (and "Moloch" is dismissed as a non-existent god) — guarding against
  reading later Phoenician/Carthaginian or biblical-polemical horrors back into LBA
  Ugarit.
- **Literary world.** Set epithets and numerical/parallel formulae link Ugaritic
  poetry to **Homer** as well as to Hebrew verse — evidence of a shared eastern
  Mediterranean literary koine (Mycenaean merchants at Ugarit "must have listened to
  the priestly musicians singing their lays of Baʿal").

## Significance

Ugaritic religion is the **best-documented form of Canaanite religion** and the
indispensable comparative frame for the **Hebrew Bible** and for Phoenician religion,
recovered almost entirely through the chance survival of one city's temple library —
a vivid case of how a single archive can rewrite the religious history of a region.
