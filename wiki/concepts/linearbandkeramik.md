# Linearbandkeramik (LBK)

The **Linearbandkeramik** (or Linear Pottery culture, often abbreviated LBK) is the archaeological construct for the earliest farming communities of temperate (central and western) Europe, dating primarily to the later sixth and fifth millennia BCE. It is defined principally by its distinctive fine pottery decorated with incised linear bands and by its settlement on loess soils in longhouse villages.

## Definition and Scope (per Champion et al. 1984)

Champion, Gamble, Shennan & Whittle treat the LBK as the primary vehicle for the initial spread of agriculture into temperate Europe north and west of the Balkans and Carpathians. It exemplifies the "direct colonization" model on the south-east/north-west axis:

"the apparent role played by direct colonization in many areas (Fig.5.7) notably on the south-east/north-west axis as well as in the central Mediterranean and to the immediate east of the Carpathians."

"primary agricultural settlements (of the so-called Linienbandkeramik or Linear Pottery culture), again often substantial, were regularly sited on deposits of loess, a well drained and easily tillable soil."

The culture is associated with the "loess belt" from the Paris Basin eastward through central Europe, with settlements frequently located on the edge of loess deposits near streams or rivers.

## Settlement and Subsistence

LBK sites are characterized by substantial timber longhouses, often arranged in rows or nucleations, with evidence of mixed farming focused on cereals (emmer, einkorn, barley) and livestock (cattle prominent in temperate zones, sheep/goats, pigs). 

"the frequent siting on the edge of loess deposits reflects also location near streams or rivers, an important factor in the maintenance of fertility."

Later secondary colonization moved off the prime loess onto interfluves and poorer soils.

House forms vary in size; some very large longhouses are interpreted as possible communal structures rather than elite dwellings. The basic social unit appears to have been the family (nuclear or extended), within largely egalitarian communities with "little internal differentiation."

## Historiography and Critiques

Within the culture-historical framework, the LBK was treated as the material signature of a migrating farming population spreading from the southeast. The 1984 authors retain the colonization emphasis but stress environmental adaptation (loess preference), possible indigenous contributions ("Even with colonization there may regularly have been an indigenous contribution of population and skills"), and the inseparability of economy and society. They critique older views that equated pottery styles directly with ethnic groups and note the reduction over time in the scale of cultural groupings.

Post-1984 work (aDNA in particular) has refined the demic vs. cultural diffusion debate, showing substantial farmer migration with local admixture, consistent with the textbook's balanced presentation. The LBK is no longer seen as a monolithic "people" but as a widespread adaptation with regional variants.

## Collection Note

The LBK remains the canonical case study for the mechanics of farming dispersal into temperate Europe. Claims of uniform "colonization" must be tempered by the evidence for local contributions and environmental selectivity documented here.
