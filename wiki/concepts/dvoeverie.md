# Dvoeverie
## Definition
**Dvoeverie** (Russian: *двоеверие*, meaning "double-faith" or "dual belief") is a historical and analytical concept used to describe the coexistence and syncretic blending of Orthodox Christian liturgy and doctrine with pre-Christian Slavic pagan beliefs and superstitions among the East Slavic population of [[kievan-rus|Kievan Rus′]] and medieval Russia. 

Under dvoeverie, the Christianized masses adopted the outward sacraments and rituals of the Church while continuing to practice pagan rites, often mapping the attributes of pre-Christian deities onto Christian saints.

## Context and Origin
The term *dvoeverie* was first used by medieval Russian churchmen (such as St. Theodosius of the Caves in the eleventh century) as a term of admonition and condemnation for the persistence of heathen practices among the newly baptized population (l. 1019). 

Examples of this syncretism include:
* **Saintly Mapping:** The attributes of **Perun**, the pagan god of thunder, were transferred to Saint Elijah (Elijah the Prophet), who was associated with thunder and rain. The agricultural fertility god Veles was mapped onto Saint Blaise or Saint George.
* **Agricultural Rites:** Pagan rituals associated with the seasonal agricultural cycle (such as the spring *Maslenitsa* or midsummer *Ivan Kupala*) were integrated into the Christian calendar, becoming associated with church holidays.
* **Household Spirits:** Popular belief in nature spirits, such as the *domovoy* (house spirit), *leshy* (forest spirit), and *rusalka* (water nymph), persisted alongside belief in Christian angels and saints.

## Limitations and Critiques
* **Analytical Validity:** Modern historians and anthropologists debate the extent to which *dvoeverie* represents a conscious "double-faith" or simply the typical syncretism that occurs in any newly Christianized society (such as in early medieval Western Europe or Latin America). 
* **Superficiality vs. Synthesis:** Some church historians (like Golubinsky) argued that Christianity remained merely a superficial veneer over a stubbornly pagan populace for centuries. In contrast, other scholars argue that the East Slavs did not maintain two separate belief systems, but rather forged a single, synthesized "Russian folk Christianity" in which pagan elements were fully Christianized and given new Orthodox meaning (l. 1019-1023).
