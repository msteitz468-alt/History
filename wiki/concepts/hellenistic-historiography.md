## The Core Problem

The century from Alexander's death (323) to Raphia (217) is, paradoxically, both
well-documented in its own time and badly preserved for ours. The period had been
"fully covered" in universal histories and specialised works — Walbank counts
**forty-six known authors** who wrote about the Hellenistic age — yet *all of them
are lost*, surviving only as fragments quoted by later writers. The historian of
the third century works almost entirely at second or third hand. This is the
defining methodological condition of the field and the reason so much of
[[walbank-cah-v7-1-1984|CAH VII.1]] is hedged with "probably," "the date is
disputed," and reconstruction from inscriptions and papyri.

Walbank speculates on the causes of this "holocaust" of texts: the contemporary
*[[koine-greek|koine]]* idiom did not appeal to later Atticising copyists; local
histories survived in too few copies; and above all the sheer bulk of many works
invited abridgement, so that "a kind of literary Gresham's law" let inferior
résumés drive the originals out of circulation.

## The Lost Primary Historians

Five lost writers stamped their version of events on the surviving tradition:

- **[[hieronymus-of-cardia|Hieronymus of Cardia]]** (d. c. 250) — by far the most
  important; a participant-historian (archivist to Alexander, then officer under
  Eumenes, Antigonus I, Demetrius, and Gonatas) whose lost *Histories* are the
  ultimate source of [[diodorus-siculus|Diodorus]] XVIII–XX, our only continuous
  narrative of the Diadochi to Ipsus.
- **Duris of Samos** (c. 340–c. 260) — tyrant of Samos; his *Macedonica* was
  sensational and hostile to Macedon, a "tragic"/emotional style Polybius despised.
- **Timaeus of Tauromenium** (c. 350–255) — the great historian of the western
  Greeks, source for Agathocles; devised the Olympiad-year dating Polybius adopted.
- **Phylarchus** (covering 272–219) — emotional, partisan for the Spartan
  revolutionaries, the source behind Plutarch's *Agis* and *Cleomenes*.
- **Aratus of Sicyon** — whose thirty-book *Memoirs* are a self-justifying apologia
  for the [[achaean-league|Achaean League]], a corrective to Phylarchus.

## The Surviving Tradition

Everything must be reconstructed through later, derivative writers:
**[[polybius|Polybius]]** (our only first-rate and continuous source, but beginning
his main narrative only in 220); **Diodorus Siculus** (a compiler whose worth equals
that of his source — invaluable for transmitting Hieronymus); **Trogus/Justin** (a
garbled epitome, but the sole authority for many events); **Plutarch** (not history
but "full of the stuff of history," who largely transmits — perhaps partly created —
the personalities of the Diadochi); **Arrian's** *Events after Alexander* and
**Appian's** *Syriaca*; with Pausanias, Pliny, Athenaeus, and Memnon of Heraclea
filling gaps. After 300 there is **no consecutive narrative** of the eastern
Mediterranean until Polybius — the Chremonidean War and the early Syrian Wars must
be pieced together from scraps.

## The Documentary Turn

Because the literary tradition is so thin, **the documentary sources carry
exceptional weight** — this is the methodological signature of modern Hellenistic
history:

- **Inscriptions**: on stone, contemporary, and (Walbank argues) generally more
  reliable than literary statements, though always to be read as public/political
  pronouncements. They illuminate ruler-cult, civic life, festivals, *asylia*,
  arbitration between cities, royal letters, and — crucially — chronology (Attic,
  Delphic, and Boeotian archon-lists; the increase of Aetolian votes in the
  Delphic Amphictyony as an index of [[aetolian-league|Aetolian]] expansion).
- **[[ptolemaic-papyri|Papyri and ostraca]]**: a wealth of evidence *for Egypt
  alone*, densest c. 259–215, illuminating prices, wages, rations, landholding,
  family size, and the daily reach of government — but skewed by the under-publication
  of demotic relative to Greek texts, which silences the lower, native social strata.
- **Coins**: royal portraiture and propaganda, monetary standards (the Attic
  standard of the Antigonids and Seleucids vs. the lighter Ptolemaic/Phoenician
  standard), and — through die-studies and hoards — chronology.
- **Archaeology**: city plans, temples, and the Ptolemaic temple-building that
  documents relations between the dynasty and the Egyptian priesthood; the Greek
  city at [[ai-khanoum|Ai Khanum]] in Afghanistan.

## The Silence of the Conquered

A recurring theme of the volume: **all the sources are Greek or derived from Greek,
and written from the viewpoint of the dominant class.** Manetho and Berosus wrote
Egyptian and Babylonian history *in Greek for Greeks*, but no native account of the
Diadochi, of Seleucid Asia from a Persian/Babylonian view, or of Ptolemaic Egypt
from an Egyptian view survives. "The voices of the natives and those of the poor are
equally silent." This bias is structural, not incidental, and frames every
interpretive controversy about the period (e.g. the degree of Greek–native fusion).

## Why It Matters

The fragmentary, documentary-dependent source-base explains the wiki's coverage
pattern for the Hellenistic age: confident on broad structures (kingship, the city,
the economy) where inscriptions and papyri are dense, but cautious and disputed on
narrative detail, exact dates, and motive — exactly where lost narrative historians
would once have spoken. Compare the far richer narrative tradition for
[[peloponnesian-war|fifth-century Greece]] (Thucydides) and the still-living one for
[[alexander-the-great|Alexander]] (Arrian, Plutarch, Curtius).
