# Carolingian Kingship and Royal Government

## Definition

The kingship of the [[carolingian-empire]] fused three inheritances: **Frankish** warrior
rulership, the **Roman** idea of public authority (revived through the rereading of Latin legal
texts by royal counsellors), and a **Christian, Old Testament–flavoured** theology of the anointed
king as a *minister* answerable to God for his people. In [[mckitterick-ncmh-v2-1995|NCMH II]]
(Nelson, Ch. 15) it is analysed not through "conquests and coronations" but as a working system of
resources, household, and consensus.

## The Bases of Power: Land and Palaces

Carolingian government rested materially on the **royal demesne** (the *fisc*) — estates derived from
the old Roman *civitates* and imperial lands, augmented by Carolingian family lands in the Meuse,
Moselle, and Rhineland and by the confiscated fiscs of conquered dynasties (Lombardy, Bavaria). Nelson
stresses that a core of royal lands tended to **remain intact** across reigns (dynastic discontinuity
could even preserve an undivided demesne), and that its careful management — horses for the war-band,
the sustenance of the household — was "the prerequisite for the kingdom's survival," though no law
declared it inalienable.

Nelson also **qualifies the model of royal itineracy**: kings made prolonged stays at a few central
places rather than constantly touring. Pavia was the effective Lombard capital; Oviedo the Asturian seat;
and from 794 **Aachen** became Charlemagne's *sedes regni*. Most of Charlemagne's vast empire (some
1,200,000 sq km) was **never visited by its ruler at all** — royal travel was essentially for campaigning.

## Reward and Consensus

The fisc supplied "the wherewithal to reward followers": estates confiscated from the faithless were
regranted to the faithful, binding the aristocracy to the king. Government worked through **assemblies**
of king and magnates (where royal authority was ritually affirmed and policy set), through counts and the
**[[missi-dominici]]**, and through the written **[[carolingian-capitularies|capitularies]]**. Crucially,
because the Carolingian state — unlike Rome — did **not tax its elites**, royal power depended on consensus,
the distribution of *honores* (offices and lands), and "annual gifts," rather than on a fiscal-bureaucratic
apparatus.

## Significance and Debate

This model frames the [[nature-of-carolingian-government|central controversy]] of the volume: whether the
apparatus of fisc, *missi*, capitularies, and Christian *ministerium* amounted to a genuine reconstitution
of public authority, or whether kingship remained fundamentally personal, land-based, and consensual —
sustained by conquest and unravelling when conquest stopped. Either way, the Carolingian ideal of the
**Christian king responsible for the moral welfare of his subjects** profoundly shaped all later medieval
European kingship. See also [[divine-kingship]].

## Historiography

Nelson (NCMH II) reconstructs kingship from capitularies (notably the *Capitulare de Villis* on estate
management), royal wills (especially well-evidenced in Wessex), Nithard, and the *De Ordine Palatii*. She
deliberately deflates older images of either a Roman-style bureaucratic state or a purely peripatetic
warrior kingship, presenting instead a resource-based, consensual, ideologically Christian monarchy.
