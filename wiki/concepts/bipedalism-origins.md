## Definition and Significance

Habitual upright, two-legged (bipedal) locomotion is, in modern paleoanthropology,
the **defining characteristic of the hominid lineage** — nothing that was not an
upright biped is counted as a hominid. This criterion replaced earlier candidates
(the large brain; the precision grip of "man the toolmaker"; small canines) after
the brain-first model — sustained for nearly half a century by the fraudulent
[[tattersall-world-from-beginnings-2008|Piltdown]] skull (exposed 1953) — was
abandoned. As Tattersall notes, the criterion carries a logical caveat: it is an
*assumption* about our ancestor, so a fossil should be judged a possible hominid by
not being *excluded*, rather than by passing a derived-trait threshold.

## The Skeletal Evidence

Bipedality is diagnosed from the lower-limb and pelvic skeleton:
- **Knee/leg:** in bipeds the femur slants inward to the knee so the feet pass close
  together and weight transmits straight forward; the tibial knee surface sits at a
  right angle to the shaft. *Australopithecus anamensis* (~4.2 mya) shows this — the
  oldest hominid known for sure to have walked upright on the ground.
- **Pelvis:** broad and shallow (bowl-like, supporting the viscera) rather than the
  long, narrow ape pelvis. [[australopithecus-afarensis]] ("Lucy") shows a radical
  reorganization toward uprightness, though not identical to ours.
- **Foramen magnum:** forward-placed (under the skull) in upright forms — a feature
  claimed for the earliest candidates *Sahelanthropus* (~6–7 mya) and *Ardipithecus*.
- **Retained arboreal traits:** longer arms, curved finger/toe bones, and small
  hip-joint surfaces in australopiths indicate they were not fully emancipated from
  the trees — "have-your-cake-and-eat-it-too" locomotion, confirmed for open ground
  by the 3.5-mya Laetoli trackways.

## Competing Functional Explanations

Tattersall surveys the candidate "advantages" and stresses that the decisive
question is not the immediate function but the *ancestral predisposition* — an
ancestor that already habitually held its body upright (as chimps do when foraging
in trees in open country):
- **Freeing the hands** for carrying and manipulating objects.
- **Vigilance** — seeing predators farther across open country.
- **Locomotor efficiency** over open ground.
- **Thermoregulation** — minimizing the surface exposed to overhead tropical sun
  while maximizing heat loss and catching cooling breezes (important for protecting
  the brain).
- **Owen Lovejoy's reproductive model** — provisioning, pair-bonding, concealed
  ovulation, and increased birth rate; widely *contested* (the large male–female
  body-size difference of *A. afarensis* fits polygyny, not pair-bonding).

The framing connects to the ecology of the earliest hominids as **prey, not
primarily hunters** (Brain; Hart & Sussman's *Man the Hunted*): edge-dwelling
omnivores in fragmenting forests, more like macaques than apes ecologically.

## Limitations

The deep record (~7–4.4 mya) is sparse and ambiguous; for *Sahelanthropus*,
*Orrorin*, and *Ardipithecus* a "clearly demonstrable bipedal form" is disputed,
and *Ardipithecus ramidus* may have moved in a way with no living analog. Whether
bipedalism arose once or several times among these experimental early forms is
unresolved. The "why" remains genuinely open — multiple advantages likely
co-occurred, and the critical step was simply standing up in the first place.
