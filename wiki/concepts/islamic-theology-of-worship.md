## Definition and Scope

**'Ibada** (worship, servitude) is the central practical category of Islamic religious life and, in the classical theological tradition, its deepest ontological description of the human condition. The Qur'an employs four forms of the root '-b-d in 277 instances: the verb *'abada* (124 times), the gerund *'ibada* (9 times), the noun *'abd* (servant/slave, singular and plural, 130 times), and the active participle *'abid* (12 times). This density is itself a theological statement: humanity's defining relationship to God is one of *service*.

Islamic theology of worship must be distinguished from:
- **Fiqh** of worship: the jurisprudential specification of how ritual acts are validly performed (prayer, fasting, pilgrimage, alms). See [[sharia]] and [[fiqh]].
- **Sufism** as spiritual method: the inner disciplines of the mystical path. See [[sufism]] and [[philosophical-sufism]].

The theological dimension asks *why* worship, *what it means ontologically*, and *how ritual and ethical action are grounded in the structure of reality*. This is the domain of [[kalam]] in its encounter with Sufi anthropology, brought to fullest expression in the tradition running from the early Sufis through [[al-ghazali]] and [[ibn-arabi]].

## The Ontological Imperative: Servant and Lord

The cornerstone of the Islamic theology of worship is the ontological asymmetry between creator and creature. God is the **Necessary Being** (*wajib al-wujud*): that which is and cannot not-be. Everything else is a **possible thing** (*mumkin*): something that has no inherent claim on existence and exists only through God's sustaining act.

This asymmetry is not merely metaphysical; it is *moral and practical*. As Chittick summarises Ibn 'Arabi's position: servants are "essentially nonexistent and accidentally existent through the Real." The logical consequence is that worship — orientation of the whole person toward God — is the natural expression of what creatures actually are. Failure to worship is therefore not merely disobedience but a kind of ontological confusion: claiming for oneself a *haqq* (right, reality, worthiness) that belongs only to God.

The Arabic word **haqq** is pivotal here. It means simultaneously "truth," "reality," "right," "worthy," and "due." Knowledge of the Real (*al-Haqq*, one of God's names) makes *practical demands* on the knowing soul: "when servants know their actual, ontological status vis-à-vis their Lord, they find themselves called upon to put themselves right, to 'worship God sincerely', to strip themselves of any claim to the rights of lordship" (Chittick, paraphrasing the tradition; [[winter-cambridge-companion-islamic-theology-2008]]).

The goal of worship is therefore to give everything that has a *haqq* its *haqq*. This is "the realisation of *tawhid*" — not merely professing monotheism but living in accordance with the ontological structure that monotheism describes.

## 'Abd and Rabb: Servant and Lord as Relational Categories

The Qur'an employs the divine name *rabb* (Lord) approximately 900 times, but *never without ascription to a servant or servants* (e.g., "your Lord," "Lord of Moses," "Lord of the Worlds"). Ibn 'Arabi's reflection on this point reveals the relational character of both categories: God is Lord *in respect of* having servants; to be a creature is to be, by definition, in a relationship of *marb ub* (vassalage) to the Lord.

The five meanings of *rabb* (per Ibn 'Arabi): God brings about the well-being (*muslih*) of His servants; He is their nourisher (*mughadhdhī*), nurturer (*murabbī*), master (*sayyid*), and owner (*malik*). Every divine name — Merciful, Pardoner, Severe in Punishment — designates a distinct modality of God's lordship over His servants.

This means that God's attributes are not abstract metaphysical properties but *relational realities* constituted in the act of creation and worship. The Lord is Lord only in relation to the servant; the servant is servant only in relation to the Lord.

## Knowledge, Servanthood, and the Divine Image

The tradition insists that worship cannot be separated from knowledge (*'ilm*). Islam begins with the two *shahadahs* (witnessings), which express what the believer knows. But the theology of worship presses further: true knowledge of God is "never-ending" because God's infinite reality cannot be exhausted by human understanding.

The Qur'anic statement that "God created Adam in His image" is interpreted (via the hadith "He who knows himself knows his Lord") to mean that human beings uniquely recapitulate *all* the divine names. Whereas other creatures manifest only some of God's attributes, "Man alone was 'taught all the names' (2:31)." This is not an informational claim but a *structural* one: humans have the capacity to recognize the *haqq* of all things — the reality and proper due of everything that exists.

This unlimited capacity also explains the emphasis on **sincerity** (*ikhlas*): purifying one's worship of everything but God. Because humans can serve *any* divine name — or, more dangerously, any finite reality elevated to the status of an object of worship (*ma'bud*) — the task of directing service solely toward the One God is correspondingly difficult and all-encompassing.

## Faith and Works

The Qur'anic and hadith tradition does not separate belief (*iman*) from practice (*'amal*). The theology of worship grounds this unity ontologically: "Knowing the Lord is not separate from knowing and actualising servanthood."

[[kalam]] had long debated the relationship of faith to works. The Mu'tazilites (see [[mutazilism]]) required works for the full status of believer; the *murji'a* deferred judgment on grave sinners; the Ash'arites (see [[asharism]]) generally treated faith (*iman*) as primarily assent, with works as its expression but not its constituent. The Sufi theological tradition (represented in this chapter by the commentary tradition on [[quran]] and the *Ihya'* of [[al-ghazali]]) tends toward a more integrated view: the ontological situation of the servant *calls forth* worship not as external obligation but as internal necessity. One who truly knows one's own status *cannot* fail to worship; failure to worship is evidence of a defect in knowledge.

Al-Muhasibi (d. 857), whose *al-Ri'aya li-huquq Allah* is an early systematic treatment, frames this as the "rights of God" (*huquq Allah*): the servant who knows God recognizes that God has *rights* (*huquq*) upon the servant — analogous to the rights of a master upon a vassal, but grounded in the deeper asymmetry of Necessary and possible being.

## Vicegerency and Nearness

Worship is not only the appropriate response to the creature's ontological poverty. It also attracts God's love and enables what the tradition calls **vicegerency** (*khilafa*): the servant who has truly submitted to God's authority is brought into God's proximity and becomes, in a qualified sense, God's representative in creation.

The Qur'anic verse "Surely thou art upon a magnificent character (*khuluq 'azim*)" (68:4), addressed to Muhammad, illustrates the trajectory: the perfect servant, who asks help from no one but God and realizes *tawhid*, is given "a divine gift" of character that reflects the divine names. This is not identification with God but a mode of *nearness* (*qurb*) — achieved through servanthood, not bypassing it.

The contrast of nearness and distance maps onto the eschatological contrast of Paradise and Hell: "Those who fail the test of living up to servanthood remain distant (hell), but those who pass the test are given nearness (Paradise)" (Chittick, [[winter-cambridge-companion-islamic-theology-2008]]).

## Ritual and Its Theological Meaning

The theology of worship gives the ritual acts of [[sharia]] — prayer (*salat*), fasting (*sawm*), pilgrimage (*hajj*), alms (*zakat*), and the profession of faith (*shahada*) — an ontological significance beyond mere legal obligation. Each is a structured enactment of the servant–Lord relationship: the body oriented toward God, the wealth offered to God, the ego disciplined by God's command.

The Sufi tradition (especially from al-Muhasibi onward) adds an interior dimension: the *heart* must enact what the body performs. Sahl al-Tustari's formative experience — his awareness that his heart was "in prostration to God" just as his body was in formal prayer (*salat*) — illustrates the integration of outward observance and inward state that the classical tradition aimed at. This connection is elaborated in [[al-ghazali]]'s *Ihya' 'ulum al-din*, which structures its treatment of worship through the same integration of outward (*zahir*) and inward (*batin*) dimensions.

## Ibn 'Arabi's Synthesis

[[ibn-arabi]] gives the most systematic metaphysical account of worship in classical Islamic thought. The key moves:

1. **Ontological servanthood as universal fact**: All created things are servants of the Lord *of* them — that is, all things have a divine name as their Lord, of which they are the vassals. Human beings are unique because they can be servants of *any* name, which is both their danger and their dignity.

2. **The divine image and human comprehensiveness**: Because Adam was created in the image of *Allah* (the name that synthesizes all names), humans alone can give everything its *haqq*. This is "the realisation of tawhid" — not merely theological assertion but existential integration.

3. **Worship as movement from weak to strong being**: The servant's journey through worship is not "a movement from place to place, but from a weak mode of being to a strong mode of being." Praiseworthy character traits (*akhlaq mahmuda*) are "modalities of being and light harmonious with the Real" — they are *ontological gains*, not merely moral performances.

4. **The meaning of Muhammad's character**: The Qur'anic praise of Muhammad's "magnificent character" (68:4) is read by Ibn 'Arabi as a statement about the *realization of tawhid* through worship. Muhammad's character is the Qur'an enacted — "only God's character is essentially and irrevocably 'magnificent'; if Muhammad has a magnificent character, it is because he is a servant who asked help from no one but God."

## Historiography

The theology of worship as a distinct field sits at the intersection of [[kalam]], [[fiqh]], and [[sufism]] and has not always been treated as a unified subject in Western scholarship. Key primary sources:
- Al-Muhasibi, *al-Ri'aya li-huquq Allah* (9th c.) — systematic early treatment
- Al-Sulami, *Haqa'iq al-tafsir* (d. 1021) — Sufi Qur'anic commentary on servanthood
- Al-Qushayri, *al-Risala* (d. 1072) — compendium including treatment of servanthood
- [[al-ghazali]], *Ihya' 'ulum al-din* — definitive synthetic treatment; Book IX covers invocations
- [[ibn-arabi]], *al-Futuhat al-Makkiyya* — most systematic metaphysical account

Modern scholarship: Franz Rosenthal's *Knowledge Triumphant* (1970) documents the centrality of knowledge in the worship theology; Sachiko Murata and William C. Chittick, *The Vision of Islam* (1994), give the most accessible modern synthesis.
