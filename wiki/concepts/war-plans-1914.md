## Definition

The set of detailed mobilization and initial-offensive plans drawn up by the general staffs of the major European powers before 1914, of which Germany's [[concepts/schlieffen-plan|Schlieffen Plan]] (envisioning a rapid knockout blow against France through Belgium before turning east against Russia) is the best known. France's Plan XVII envisioned an immediate offensive into Alsace-Lorraine; Russia's Plan 19 (and its revisions) governed mobilization timetables against Germany and Austria-Hungary; Austria-Hungary maintained separate, partially incompatible plans (War Case R against Russia, War Case B against Serbia).

## Emic vs. Etic

Wholly emic: these were classified contemporary military documents, not later historiographical constructs, though historians' comparative analysis of how they interacted is itself a later etic framework.

## Significance and Contestation

Historians have long debated how far rigid, railway-timetable-driven mobilization plans constrained civilian decision-makers' room for diplomatic maneuver in the July 1914 crisis, effectively pushing statesmen toward war once mobilization began (the "war by timetable" thesis) — a view associated with A.J.P. Taylor and others but contested by historians who emphasize that political decisions, not plans alone, determined the outbreak of war. The plans' rigidity, and Germany's related decision to violate Belgian neutrality under the Schlieffen Plan's logic, are central to ongoing debates over the [[controversies/causes-of-the-first-world-war|causes of the First World War]].
