## Definition and Scope

Avicennan emanationism is the cosmological theory, developed by [[al-farabi|al-Fārābī]] and systematized by [[ibn-sina|Ibn Sīnā (Avicenna)]], according to which the existence of the entire universe derives from a single, absolutely simple First Principle through a necessary, unintentional chain of intellectual emanations. It is the Islamicate adaptation of Neoplatonic emanationism, filtered through Aristotelian cosmology. The theory is a central target of Discussion 3 (and, tangentially, Discussions 4–5) of [[al-ghazali|al-Ghazālī's]] *[[tahafut-al-falasifa|Tahāfut al-Falāsifa]]*. Source: [[ghazali-incoherence-of-philosophers]] (Marmura translation, 2000), Discussion 3, §§36–79.

---

## The Theory

### The Axiom: From the One, Only One Proceeds

The entire structure rests on a metaphysical axiom attributed to the emanationist tradition: **from the absolutely One (simple, uncomposed) First Principle, only one thing can directly proceed**. Multiplicity in the effect requires multiplicity in the cause; since the First is absolutely without multiplicity, only a single, simple first effect can emanate from it.

### The Hierarchy

From the First Principle, a chain of ten Intellects and nine Celestial Spheres is generated:

| Level | Emanated Entity | Generates |
|---|---|---|
| First Principle | — | First Intellect |
| First Intellect | Apprehends: self / principle / self-as-possible | Second Intellect + Soul of Outermost Sphere + Body of Outermost Sphere |
| Second Intellect | Same triple apprehension | Third Intellect + Soul of Fixed Stars + Body of Fixed Stars |
| Third Intellect | — | Fourth Intellect + Soul and Body of Saturn's sphere |
| ... (continues) | — | ... |
| Tenth Intellect (Active Intellect) | — | Sublunar matter (subject to generation and corruption) |

"From the existence of the First Principle the first intellect emanated, it being a self-subsisting existent, neither body nor imprinted in body, that knows itself and knows its principle." (Discussion 3, §43)

The triple structure of each lower level arises from the triple cognitive act of each intellect: (1) its apprehension of the First Principle produces the next intellect; (2) its apprehension of itself produces the soul of the celestial sphere at that level; (3) its awareness of itself as *possible* (not necessary) of existence produces the body of the sphere.

### Multiplicity from Unity

The mechanism is designed to explain how multiplicity arises from the absolutely simple One without violating the One-from-One axiom:

> "Nothing proceeded from the First Principle except one [thing]: namely, the essence of this intellect by which it apprehends itself intellectually. It has as a necessary consequence — not, however, from the direction of the Principle — that it apprehends the Principle intellectually. In itself it is possible of existence; but it does not derive [this] possibility from the First Principle, but [has it] due to itself." (Discussion 3, §46)

The key move: the first effect has intrinsic features (its own possibility, its self-knowledge, its knowledge of its principle) that are not "from" the First Principle but are necessary concomitants of its existence. These intrinsic features generate the triad at the second level. Multiplicity is thus mediated, never proceeding directly from the One.

The Active Intellect (tenth in the series) governs the sublunar realm: the four elements, minerals, plants, and animals arise from the interactions of the spherical motions and the Active Intellect's influence on sublunar matter.

## Al-Ghazālī's Critique (Discussion 3)

### On the "Agent" Question

Al-Ghazālī's primary charge in Discussion 3 is that the philosophers' use of terms like "Maker," "agent," and "act" for God is *metaphorical*, not real, on their own principles. Real agency requires will; the Avicennan First Principle does not will — it emanates by necessity, as light from the sun:

> "This does not pertain to action in anything. Indeed, whoever says that the lamp enacts the light and the individual enacts the shadow has ventured excessively into metaphor and stretched it beyond [its] bound." (Discussion 3, §4)

The philosophers counter that "agent" covers both voluntary and natural causation as species of a genus; al-Ghazālī denies this: "Real action is that which comes about only through will." (Discussion 3, §13) The inanimate is not an agent; fire does not kill in the full sense — the one who throws the victim into the fire kills, because the killing proceeds from will.

### On Eternity as Incompatible with "Act"

Even granting that God could be a cause, the world's being eternal means it cannot be God's *act* in any real sense. The meaning of "act" requires temporal origination — bringing something from nonexistence to existence. An eternal, always-existing world cannot have been "brought into existence":

> "The meaning of 'the created' is 'an existent after nonexistence'... what [already] exists cannot be brought into existence. Hence, the condition of the act [to be something enacted] is for it to be temporally created. But the world, according to [the philosophers], is pre-eternal." (Discussion 3, §18)

The philosophers counter (Discussion 3, §20–23) that the act attaches to the agent *inasmuch as it exists*, not inasmuch as it was preceded by nonexistence; and that the relation of world to God is like the relation of water movement to the eternally moving hand — an eternally proceeding effect is still an effect. Al-Ghazālī replies: this is at most a causal relation, not an act relation. "Naming that which is not created out of nothing an 'act' is pure metaphor, having no reality." (Discussion 3, §29)

### The Triad Mechanism: Laughter-Worthy

Al-Ghazālī reserves his sharpest rhetoric for the triad-generation mechanism by which the first effect generates three things from three cognitive acts:

> "What is the difference between this and someone who — knowing the existence of a man who is absent, [knowing] that [such a man] is possible of existence, [knowing] that he apprehends himself and his Maker — then [goes on to] say: 'The existence of a celestial sphere follows necessarily from [this man's] being possible of existence'? To this it would then be said: 'What relationship is there between his being possible of existence and the existence from him of a celestial sphere?'" (Discussion 3, §75–76)

The relationship between a cognitive act (self-knowledge) and a generated cosmological entity (a celestial sphere's soul) is postulated without demonstration. Al-Ghazālī finds the mechanism arbitrary: "I do not know how [even] a madman would in himself be satisfied by the likes of such postulates." (Discussion 3, §76)

### Al-Ghazālī's Positive Stance

When pressed for his own account of how God produces plurality, al-Ghazālī explicitly declines the challenge:

> "What is there to prevent one from saying that the First Principle is knowing, powerful, willing; that He enacts as He wishes, governs what He wills, creates things that are varied and things that are homogeneous as He wills and in the way He wills? The impossibility of this is known neither through rational necessity nor through theoretical reflection." (Discussion 3, §79)

The prophets have revealed the fact of creation; reason cannot show it to be impossible; that is sufficient. "Think on the creation of God and do not think on the essence of God" — a saying attributed to the Prophet and cited twice in these discussions. Investigating the mechanics of divine production is not within human capacity.

## The One-from-One Axiom as a Strategic Target

The One-from-One axiom is also the foundation of the philosophers' arguments for divine unity (Discussion 5). If from the absolutely simple One only one thing can proceed, then the First must be absolutely without internal multiplicity — which is used to rule out real divine attributes (Discussion 6), genus-differentia composition (Discussion 7), and the possibility of two uncaused beings (Discussion 5).

Al-Ghazālī's critique of the emanationist hierarchy in Discussion 3 thus weakens the entire philosophical theology. If the triad-generation mechanism is arbitrary, then the One-from-One axiom is not a self-evident principle but a postulate that the philosophers have not demonstrated. And if that axiom fails, the arguments built upon it in Discussions 4–7 lose their foundation.

## Context: Neoplatonic Sources

The emanationist hierarchy derives ultimately from Plotinus's *Enneads* (3rd century CE), in which the One produces Nous (Intellect), which produces Soul, which produces the material world — each level flowing necessarily from the one above it, without deliberation or will. Al-Fārābī adapted this into an Aristotelian cosmological framework compatible with Islamic monotheism; Avicenna refined the ten-intellect, nine-sphere structure. The move from Plotinus's three hypostases to a ten-stage cascade reflects the incorporation of Ptolemaic astronomy (nine planetary spheres) into the emanationist picture.

The theory circulated in the Islamic world partly through the *Theologia Aristotelis* (an Arabic paraphrase of Plotinus's *Enneads* IV–VI, mistakenly attributed to Aristotle), which gave emanationism a spurious Aristotelian pedigree. Al-Ghazālī is aware that he is attacking an Avicennan synthesis, not Aristotle directly.

## Historiography

- Modern scholars broadly agree that al-Ghazālī's critique of the triad mechanism is effective as a negative argument: the philosophers have not demonstrated *why* the first effect's triple cognitive structure produces the specific triad of intellect, soul, and body. They have asserted a correlation without explaining it.
- Averroes (*Tahāfut al-Tahāfut*) responded that al-Ghazālī misunderstood Aristotelian causation and that the emanationist picture is actually a better account of necessary causation than the voluntarist alternative.
- Contemporary scholarship (Adamson, Wisnovsky, Gutas) reads the Avicennan synthesis as a sophisticated adaptation, not a naive transplant, of Neoplatonism — which makes al-Ghazālī's "this is laughable" rhetorical moves somewhat undermine his philosophical rigor.
- The question of whether al-Ghazālī *privately* accepted something close to emanationism in his Sufi writings (the *Mishkāt al-Anwār* in particular) is a live debate in the scholarship.
