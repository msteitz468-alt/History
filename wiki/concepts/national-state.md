# National State

The **national state** is Azar Gat and Alexander Yakobson's term (*Nations*, 2013;
[[sources/gat-nations-2013]]) for what is usually called the "nation-state," extended
backward to embrace its premodern forms. A national state is one where "the state
roughly encompassed and remained largely confined to an entire generally distinct
ethnic space ... identified with a particular *Staatsvolk*." It is the polity in which
"ethnos/people and state converge[d]," centered on "a particular majoritarian
*Staatsvolk*."

Gat's central revisionist claim is that such states "were also quite prevalent during
premodern times and outside Europe." What "historical sociologists refer to" as
**"territorial states,"** or as **"dynastic kingdoms,"** "in the great majority of cases
... [were] in effect ... national monarch[ies], wherein ethnos/people and state
converged." The sociological labels imply a state "held together by state power and
monarchic legitimacy alone"; Gat argues instead that "premodern and not only modern
states tended to be national states," resting on "ethnic bonds of affinity, solidarity
and mutual cooperation."

## Three Polity Types

*Nations* organizes premodern polities into a typology, of which the national state is
the middle term:

| Polity type | Relation to the ethnos |
|---|---|
| Petty-state / city-state | Encompasses only **part** of an ethnic space, which is "divided among a multiplicity of city-states"; not a [[nation]] |
| Territorial national state | The state is **roughly congruent** with one ethnos / *Staatsvolk* — a national monarchy |
| Empire | Expands **beyond** its ethnic core "to rule over a larger multiethnic realm" |

The city-state (e.g. Sumer, the Canaanites, Phoenicians, Greeks, Maya, Yoruba, Hausa)
was a kin–culture community belonging to a wider city-state cluster sharing one ethnic
space, but "never proximate[d] a congruence between a state and an entire culture/ethnos."
The empire ruled across ethne. Only the territorial national state achieved the rough
people-and-state congruence that, for Gat, "by that very definition" makes it national.

## Premodern and Non-European Cases

Gat deliberately sets Europe aside in this section ("we will avoid Europe altogether ...
turn our attention to other regions and periods") and builds the case from elsewhere:

- **Ancient Egypt** — "the first large state" and "first national state." Unlike Sumer it
  "emerged quite early as a unified state, congruent with a distinct people of shared
  ethnicity," and "the state and the people, and the congruence between them, lasted for
  nearly three millennia." Its imperial overlordship in the Levant was "merely the
  periphery of the state and people of Egypt." The Theban prince's revolt against the
  Hyksos ("I will save Egypt and overthrow the Asiatics") shows rulers appealing to
  "national support and legitimacy."
- **China** — "the largest and most enduring ancient people and state," a civilization
  "fundamentally congruent with a unified Chinese state and a Chinese people." Gat marshals
  the Tang mandarin examination system, village schools, public reading of decrees, mass
  pan-Chinese myth and ritual, and Han universal military conscription as premodern
  versions of the very tools (literacy, mass education, conscription) usually credited
  with forging *modern* nations.
- **The ancient Near East** — "nascent national states": the Israelites, Ammon, Moab, and
  Edom ("congruence of state and ethnicity or peoplehood"); the Aramaean states; Elam,
  where "state and culture converged" for nearly two millennia; the Third Dynasty of Ur,
  described by a non-modernist historian as "the first bureaucratic national state";
  Hammurabi's Babylonia; and Assyria, "a national state which raised mass peasant infantry
  armies."
- **The Zulu kingdom** — a national state forged in the nineteenth century "from the
  ethnically related Nguni-speaking Bantu," "and yet there was nothing modern about it" —
  Gat's proof that the national state needs neither literacy nor European conditions.

## "Were Empires Ethnically Blind?"

The hinge of Gat's argument is his answer to the question heading Chapter 4's third
section: **no**. Empires have been "described by many historical sociologists as pure
elite and class power structures," but "ethnicity was at least as significant in their
make up." "Almost universally they were either overtly or tacitly the empires of a
particular people or ethnos" — "nearly all of them were grounded in and relied upon a
dominant ethnic nucleus." The empire's "rulers and most of the elite came from it," "for
its survival the empire relied on the loyalty and devotion of that core," and its "state
symbols and official culture tended to reflect those of the imperial people/ethnos."

The Achaemenid Persian Empire, "famously tolerant toward local ethnicities," "was anything
but ethnically blind": Persians and Medes were "the main beneficiaries of the empire and
by far its most trusted element," monopolizing the satrapies, generalships, and high
office, while Darius I "reinforced the centrality of Persian identity as its official
culture." Assyria, even as it grew cosmopolitan through deportations and Aramaean
incorporation, never ceased to be Assyrian. Even Egypt and China are best read as **empires
that doubled as gigantic national states**, "wherein state, people, and culture converged,"
their multiethnic peripheries dwarfed by the national core.

It follows that national states "appeared only in those cases in which a rough congruence
between an ethnos and a state occurred." Empires were the "black holes" that swallowed
budding national states — "a major source of the prevalent optical illusion regarding the
absence of such states in premodern times" — while at the same time imperial pressure
"sometimes served as a catalyst for the crystallization of national states around them."

## Emic and Etic

The national state is largely an **etic** category: a "vocabulary" choice by which Gat
relabels the sociologists' "territorial state" / "dynastic kingdom" as a national
monarchy. But it claims an **emic** foundation in the documented self-understanding of
historical peoples — Egyptians who "knew very well that foreigners were foreigners,"
Sumerians who "considered Sargon and his 'sons of Akkade' to be foreigners," Judeans who
internalized the Torah as a national epos, Medes whom Herodotus has "collected ... into a
nation [*ethnos*]."

## Limitations and Critiques

**Contested.** Modernists deny that the term "national" can be applied before modernity:
premodern ethnicity was supposedly apolitical, and premodern states "supra-ethnic," held
together by dynastic legitimacy rather than peoplehood. To them, calling a territorial
state a "national state" is an anachronism. Gat's rebuttal — that the difference between
premodern and modern national states is one of **degree of integration and mobilization**,
not of kind, since "a rough congruence between ethnos and statehood ... resulted from
ethnic bonds of affinity, solidarity and mutual cooperation" — is itself the matter in
dispute. He also concedes the evidentiary problem: "the sources on the illiterate masses
are close to non-existent," so claims about whether peasants shared the elite's national
culture "must always be qualified and seen as a matter of degree." The full controversy is
treated under [[antiquity-of-nations]].
