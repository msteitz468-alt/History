## Definition

The **Neolithic Revolution** is V. Gordon Childe's term (1936) for the
transition from foraging to farming — the adoption of domesticated plants and
animals and the sedentary, food-producing way of life built on them. Childe
deliberately framed it as a *revolution* on a par with the Industrial
Revolution in its transformational consequences, even though (as he recognized)
it unfolded over millennia rather than decades. In his scheme, farming gave
people a reliable, storable food supply that permitted sedentism, population
growth, and food surpluses, which in turn set the stage for his later "Urban
Revolution" (cities and states).

This is the foundational concept of the [[neolithic]] period and the framing
device of *The Cambridge World History* Vol. II. It is both an **emic-adjacent
analytical label** (coined by a historian, not by the people it describes) and,
within the discipline, a contested one.

## Emic vs. Etic

Wholly **etic**: "Neolithic" and "Neolithic Revolution" are nineteenth- and
twentieth-century scholarly impositions. Neolithic people had no concept of
living in a "New Stone Age" or of participating in a "revolution." The terms
encode a modern, progressivist reading of the past — which is precisely the
source of the critique below.

## The two terminological problems

Barker & Goucher stress that **"Neolithic" is widely regarded as
unsatisfactory** but has no agreed replacement, and that scholars routinely (and
often unconsciously) slide between three incompatible senses of the word in a
single argument:
1. a **chronological** period descriptor ("the Neolithic period") — which
   misleadingly implies *everyone* then farmed, when in fact many people lived by
   mixed or purely foraging economies;
2. an **economic** descriptor ("Neolithic people" = farmers, as opposed to
   "Mesolithic people" = foragers living alongside them);
3. a **material-culture "package"** descriptor (pottery, polished stone, certain
   house and burial forms, plus domesticates) — hence talk of foragers "adopting
   part of the Neolithic package."

## Critiques and limitations

- **Not a single event or origin.** Childe (following Westropp's 1872 ladder)
  imagined a broadly linear, diffusionary process. The radiocarbon revolution
  and subsequent archaeology show *multiple independent hearths* with different
  domesticates and timings — see [[agricultural-origins]].
- **Childe's climate mechanism was wrong in detail.** He thought the early
  Holocene brought *aridity* (his "oasis hypothesis"), driving people and animals
  together at water sources; in fact Southwest Asia got *wetter*. The broader
  claim — that the Pleistocene–Holocene climate transition was the context for
  the shift — remains the dominant view. See [[why-did-agriculture-begin]].
- **Not "easy street."** Childe's implication that farming was an obvious
  improvement is contradicted by bioarchaeology (declining health) and by
  ethnography of "affluent" foragers (the !Kung San). Adoption of farming was
  often resisted, partial, reversible, and slow.
- **"Package" is contingent, not necessary.** Semi-sedentary villages occurred
  without agriculture; agriculture occurred without villages; pottery preceded
  farming in East Asia by millennia. The elements Childe bundled together in fact
  decouple.

Despite the critiques, no better organizing concept has displaced it, and the
core Childean insight — that food production was a world-historical threshold
with cascading demographic and social consequences — remains intact. The wiki
uses "Neolithic" as a labelled convenience while flagging the slippage above.
