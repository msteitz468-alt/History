## Definition

The process by which elements of distinct religious, cultural, artistic, or linguistic traditions merge or blend when brought into sustained contact, producing new composite forms rather than simple replacement of one tradition by another. Classic historical instances in this wiki's coverage include the [[concepts/heracles-melqart-syncretism|assimilation of Phoenician Melqart to Greek Heracles]] and debates over religious blending in the spread of Islam into new cultural regions (see [[controversies/syncretism-vs-authenticity-islam-in-asia]]).

## Emic vs. Etic

Mostly an etic scholarly category applied by historians and anthropologists to characterize processes that participants themselves often did not name as such, though some traditions (e.g., interpretatio graeca/romana in antiquity, where one culture explicitly identified its own deities with a foreign culture's) involved conscious, emic acts of religious equation.

## Significance and Contestation

Syncretism is a recurring feature of cultural contact across history — visible in ancient Mediterranean religion, the spread of world religions into new regions, and colonial-era cultural encounters — but the concept is contested on two fronts: some scholars argue "syncretism" implies an illegitimate contamination of a supposedly "pure" original tradition, a framing increasingly rejected since all traditions are themselves products of earlier blending; and, in some contexts, insiders and outside scholars disagree over whether a given blended practice represents authentic religious development or a corruption to be measured against an idealized orthodox standard (an active tension in cases such as [[controversies/syncretism-vs-authenticity-islam-in-asia]]).
