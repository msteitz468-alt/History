## Definition and Scope

This is the analytical frame for **warfare itself** in the Middle Millennium —
why wars were fought, by whom, and how (recruitment, equipment, organization,
strategy, tactics, logistics) — as set out by Clifford J. Rogers
([[kedar-cwh-v5-2015|CWH V]], Ch 6). Its organizing *leitmotif* is the recurring
**conflict between [[pastoral-nomadism|nomadic steppe societies]] and agro-urban
civilizations**, and the central puzzle of the age's military history: *how were the
nomads, despite vast disadvantages in manpower and resources, so often able to
defeat and overrun the settled empires?* The period closes with the answer being
reversed — by gunpowder.

## The Steppe Advantage

For roughly a thousand years, from the Turkic khaganates to the rise of the
[[mongol-empire|Mongols]], steppe ways of war changed remarkably little and
remained formidable against China, Persia, and Byzantium alike:

- **The composite recurved bow**, shot from horseback at the gallop in any
  direction, gave high rates of fire and range.
- **Universal cavalry skill and hardiness.** Nomad boys lived on horseback;
  most men were warriors "inured to hunger, thirst, and bad weather." Small
  populations fielded armies that were almost entirely expert light cavalry.
- **Mobility and strategic reach** that sedentary infantry-and-fortress armies
  struggled to counter; settled states often resorted to fomenting steppe civil
  wars to prevent the "naturally fissiparous" nomadic polities from consolidating —
  because a *united* steppe was militarily terrifying.
- The same dynamic recurs in the chapter's survey of China, Sasanian/Islamic
  Persia, and Byzantium, each of which built its military around the steppe threat.

This connects directly to Khazanov's chapter on [[pastoral-nomadism|nomadic
migrations and conquests]] (Ch 14) and to the [[mongol-empire|Mongols]] (Ch 20), the
supreme expression of steppe military power.

## The Gunpowder Transition

The decisive reversal came at the end of the period. Rogers (a key voice in the
**"military revolution" debate**) argues that by 1500:

- On the **battlefield**, advantage had already shifted in the fourteenth century
  from cavalry (which fights only by attacking) to dense, well-armed **infantry** —
  English longbows, steel crossbows, pikes, and hardened plate armor (Milanese
  quenched-steel) — favoring the defense.
- **Handguns and artillery** matured: by 1400 a handgun ball carried ten times the
  kinetic energy of a longbow arrow; siege artillery undid the old defensive
  inertia of fortifications, making conquest and internal consolidation easier for
  states with strong artillery trains (Charles VII's France, the
  [[ottoman-empire|Ottomans]], Ferdinand and Isabella's Spain). Hussites and Ottoman
  Janissaries pioneered field firearms.
- Gunpowder gave **both the Ottomans and the Europeans** a decisive edge that ended
  the millennia-long steppe advantage and enabled large-scale conquest.

## Warfare and the Americas

The chapter closes by contrasting Afro-Eurasian and Native American warfare. In the
Americas — isolated from Old World military development — armament was overwhelmingly
of stone, bone, leather, and wood (limited bronze among Aztec, Inca, and Maya), and
war aimed at disabling and **capturing** enemies (for enslavement, adoption, or
ritual sacrifice/revenge) rather than killing them. European steel, horses, guns,
and a "more bloody and devouring" style of war produced the lopsided military
advantage Columbus recognized at once — a structural prelude to the sixteenth-century
conquests.

## Contested Points

The chapter sits inside the larger **"military revolution" debate** (Rogers's own
field): when, where, and how decisively gunpowder transformed war and state power,
and whether the European military edge of 1500 was already decisive or only became
so later. The thesis that gunpowder *caused* the rise of centralized "gunpowder
empires" (Ottoman, later Mughal, Safavid) is influential but debated.

## Sequel: the Early Modern Debate

Where Rogers (CWH V) closes with gunpowder *beginning* to reverse the steppe
advantage by 1500, Jeremy Black (CWH VI) carries the story into 1400–1800 and
turns sharply skeptical about its standard culmination — the
[[military-revolution|"Military Revolution"]] thesis. The two chapters frame a
continuous argument: Rogers shows the genuine late-medieval shift (infantry over
cavalry, artillery over fortification), while Black insists the early modern
sequel was incremental rather than revolutionary, that gunpowder was an "agent"
marshalled by technique and culture rather than an autonomous cause, and that
non-European powers (above all the Ottomans) transformed their militaries on
parallel, independent tracks. The "gunpowder empires" hypothesis flagged as
debated here is exactly what Black's chapter contests. See
[[military-revolution-thesis]].
