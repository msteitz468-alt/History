# The Emperor and His Advisers (the High-Imperial *Consilium*)

How the High-Imperial emperor actually **made decisions** — through a flexible circle of advisers
(*amici*, *comites*, the *consilium*) drawn from both the senatorial and equestrian orders.
Treated in [[bowman-cah-v11-2000|CAH XI]] Ch. 4 (**Werner Eck**); the developed form of the
relationships analysed for the Julio-Claudians in [[imperial-court]].

## The dependent autocrat

Eck's framing corrects the source-driven image of an "all-powerful and always busy" emperor: in
reality the *princeps* "relied on others in almost all aspects of his decision-making" and "could
not operate without them." Because everything flowed from his person, **anyone who came into
contact with him could influence his decisions** — senators and equestrians by their status,
freedmen and slaves by their proximity — and the chief arena for such influence was the **court**
(the palace, the suburban villas). But for ongoing government the emperor needed **established
structures of advice**. The Senate was unsuitable for day-to-day or secret business (succession,
war); the model was the magistrate's ***consilium*** — but, freed from the annual electoral
cycle, the emperor could draw on the **same advisers over long periods**, embodying continuity.

## A council of both orders — not a fixed cabinet

Eck's central argument, against an older view, is that there was **no fixed standing cabinet** of
officials and jurists (the idea, projected onto Hadrian "the reformer," owed more to the late-
antique *consistorium* and the 19th-century cabinet than to the evidence). Instead the emperor
retained his **basic freedom to summon whom he judged suitable** for each question — while
respecting existing hierarchies. The recurring documentary formula — Domitian's letter to Falerio
(*adhibitis utriusque ordinis splendidis viris*, "leading men of both orders"), an Antonine
papyrus, and the **Tabula Banasitana** (a balanced list of senators and equestrians sealing a
grant of citizenship) — shows the *consilium* as a body of **outstanding members of the
senatorial and equestrian orders**, the balance reflecting "the main socio-political powers at the
imperial court."

- **Senators** (the larger pool, always present in Rome) supplied most advisers — the emperor
  himself being a senator, "one of us" (*unus ex nobis*), and consulars carrying decades of
  varied experience. Being an adviser was never an *office*: it is never recorded in a senatorial
  *cursus*, and the "status" of ***amicus principis*** was honorific and informal.
- **Equestrians** were fewer (before equestrians replaced freedmen in the Palatine offices, only
  the praetorian prefects and the city prefects of corn and watch were resident in Rome). The
  **praetorian prefects** had a natural place (the emperor's armed protectors, by the later first
  century men of long careers); the *principes officiorum* (the heads of the bureaux) joined them
  through the second century.

## *Amici*, *comites*, and the salaried *consiliarius*

The emperor wove around himself a loose, shifting circle of **amici** (loyal senior senators and
equestrians, the term used "almost as a title" but never official). When he **travelled** (often
for years), he was attended by **comites** ("companions," a position prestigious enough to record
in a *cursus*) and by the bureau-heads who could only function in his presence (*ab epistulis*,
*a libellis*); the resident officials (the *a rationibus*, the prefects of corn and watch, the
city prefect) stayed in Rome. On tour the **circle of available advisers was small**, so the
*comites'* influence was correspondingly large. From the time of **Marcus Aurelius** a new,
**official salaried post — the *consiliarius Augusti*** — appears (entitled to privileges, and
recordable in a career inscription); Eck stresses this was an **equestrian** post and did *not*
denote a body of permanent jurists (the legal experts Hadrian consulted — Celsus, Julianus,
Neratius Priscus — were *senatorial amici*, never called *consiliarii*).

## The eclipse of the freedmen

Eck traces the **disappearance from the public picture of the powerful freedman adviser** after
the Julio-Claudians: as **equestrians replaced the *liberti Augusti*** at the head of the
administrative bureaux (from the Flavians on), the visible dominance of a Pallas or Narcissus
vanished. After Nero, all emperors "thought it important to allow only members of the senatorial
and equestrian orders to feature as their official advisers" — freedmen still influenced decisions
**behind the scenes** (arranging *beneficia*, like Vespasian's *ministri* or the concubines
Caenis and Marcia) but were not paraded. Only **Commodus** broke the rule, naming the freedman
**Cleander** (his former tutor) in an official *consilium* — a sign of the late-Antonine drift of
influence back toward intimates.

## Significance and contest

Eck's account replaces the older model of a **bureaucratic imperial cabinet** with a **flexible,
status-conscious council** rooted in the two orders — a structure that kept the Senate central to
high politics down to Commodus while the equestrian administration grew beside it. The degree of
**regularization** of the *consilium* in the second century, the role of jurists, and whether
Hadrian "reformed" the advisory structure remain debated — Eck arguing for continuity and
flexibility over institutional innovation.
