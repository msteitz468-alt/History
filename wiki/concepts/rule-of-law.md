# Rule of Law

The principle that government coercion is bound by general, abstract, prospective,
and equally applied rules, known in advance — so that citizens face a framework of
law rather than the discretionary will of rulers or officials. Both an **emic**
ideal invoked by historical actors (English parliamentarians, American founders,
German liberal jurists) and an **etic** analytical standard historians and
political scientists apply to regimes.

The most systematic twentieth-century restatement is Part II of
[[hayek-constitution-of-liberty-1960|Hayek's *Constitution of Liberty*]] (1960),
on which this page currently draws; Hayek is a partisan of the tradition he
reconstructs, and his genealogy below is recorded as an attributed position.

## Core Definition (Hayek's formulation)

Hayek defines law in its ideal form as "a 'once-and-for-all' command that is
directed to unknown people and that is abstracted from all particular
circumstances of time and place" — general abstract rules versus specific
commands (a distinction he takes from Savigny). Coercion by the state loses its
evil character to the extent it is exercised only through such rules known in
advance: "Provided that I know beforehand that if I place myself in a particular
position, I shall be coerced and provided that I can avoid putting myself in such
a position, I need never be coerced." Crucially, the rule of law is for Hayek not
itself a law but "a rule concerning what the law ought to be, a meta-legal
doctrine or a political ideal" — effective only while it forms part of a
community's accepted moral tradition, and therefore binding on the legislature
itself, not just the executive. He distinguishes "laws" in the merely formal
sense (anything a legislature passes) from substantive/material laws — general
rules governing relations among persons — and holds that only the latter satisfy
the ideal. He connects the whole apparatus to the [[knowledge-problem]]: because
no lawgiver can foresee particular circumstances, law can only extend each
individual's ability to predict and plan on his own local knowledge.

Related distinctions from the same argument: **liberty vs. "liberties"** ("a
condition in which all is permitted that is not prohibited by general rules"
versus enumerated privileges for particular groups); **leges vs. privilegia**
("What is privilege to some is, of course, always discrimination to the rest");
and liberalism vs. democracy — "Liberalism is a doctrine about what the law ought
to be, democracy a doctrine about the manner of determining what will be the
law." Hayek holds that majority power is legitimate only when limited by common
principles, that unlimited democracy "becomes an absurdity if it turns into
unlimited government," and that the modern danger lies less in the general laws
majorities pass than in the discretionary powers they delegate to administrators.

## Component Doctrines

- **Generality, abstractness, prospectivity** of rules; certainty of law, which
  Hayek credits as a major cause of Western prosperity ("There is probably no
  single factor which has contributed more to the prosperity of the West than the
  relative certainty of the law").
- **Equality before the law** — with Hayek's concession that no fully
  satisfactory formal test distinguishes legitimate classification from disguised
  discrimination against known persons.
- ***Nulla poena sine lege*** — "the most important consequence of the ideal";
  first codified in the Austrian penal code of 1787 and the French Declaration of
  Rights.
- **Separation of powers and judicial independence**, including judicial review
  of the *substance* of administrative decisions, not merely their *vires*.
- **No expropriation without just compensation.**
- **Procedural safeguards** (habeas corpus, jury trial) — valuable but, Hayek
  argues, powerless without the substantive belief in binding general rules; "To
  rely on procedure alone to produce justice is the fallacy of modern liberalism"
  (Keith-Lucas, quoted approvingly), which "made possible the legality of
  totalitarian regimes such as Hitler's" — see [[nazi-germany]].

## Hayek's Genealogy (attributed position)

Hayek's chapter 11 reconstructs the rule of law as an evolved achievement — "a
by-product of a struggle for power rather than as the result of deliberate aim":

- **Classical Athens** — *isonomia* (equality of laws to all), associated with
  Solon, imported into English usage from Italy in the late 16th century; Hayek
  insists classical Athens knew genuine individual liberty under law.
  **Aristotle**: "it is more proper that the law should govern than any one of
  the citizens," echoed later as Harrington's "empire of laws, not of men."
- **Rome** — the classical private law of the Republic (Cicero: *omnes legum
  servi sumus ut liberi esse possimus*, "we are all slaves of the law that we may
  be free"), reversed from the 2nd century CE onward as imperial will rose above
  law, culminating in Justinian's codification.
- **Medieval doctrine** — the state could only "find" and declare law, not make
  it; Magna Carta (Bracton's "Constitutio Libertatis") mattered less for its
  original content than for its 17th-century reinterpretation as a weapon —
  a partly acknowledged "misinterpretation" (McKechnie).
- **The 17th-century English struggle** — practically ignited by disputes over
  royal economic monopolies: the Case of Monopolies (1602) and the Petition of
  Grievances (1610: to be governed "by the certain rule of law... and not by any
  uncertain and arbitrary form of government"). Sir Edward Coke's *Institutes*
  ("the golden and straight metewand of the law, and not... the incertain and
  crooked cord of discretion"); the 1641 abolition of Star Chamber (Maitland: "a
  court of politicians enforcing a policy, not a court of judges administering
  the law"); Civil War pamphlet debates producing no-retroactivity, restricted
  discretion, and early separation-of-powers and written-constitution ideas;
  even Cromwell in 1654: "In every Government there must be somewhat
  fundamental, somewhat like a Magna Carta, which must be standing, be
  unalterable."
- **Locke and 1688** — the *Second Treatise* read as codification of the
  already-victorious doctrine: the legislature "cannot assume to itself a power
  to rule by extemporary arbitrary decrees."
- **The 18th-century consolidation** — Act of Settlement (1701) securing
  judicial independence; [[classical-liberalism|the Whig canon]]: Hume (English
  history as evolution "from a government of will to a government of law"),
  Blackstone, Burke, Paley.
- **The American contribution (ch. 12)** — the colonists made the old "higher
  law" explicit and judicially enforceable by writing it down; Madison's
  federalism insight (dividing power between nation and states checks both — see
  [[interstate-federalism]]; Acton: "Of all checks on democracy, federalism has
  been the most efficacious"); the Ninth Amendment compromise over
  [[hamilton-alexander|Hamilton's]] Federalist No. 84 objection to enumerating
  rights; Marbury v. Madison (1803) as institutionalization, not invention, of
  judicial review; the "peculiar and partly accidental" substantive-due-process
  era; and the defeat of [[roosevelt-franklin-d|Franklin Roosevelt's]] 1937
  Court-packing bill as a vindication of constitutionalism (see
  [[great-depression]]). Hayek's admiration is qualified: the U.S. Constitution
  "is still an experiment... we must not regard it as containing all wisdom."
- **The German *Rechtsstaat* (ch. 13)** — Continental liberals faced what
  England did not: an entrenched absolutist bureaucracy. Kant's categorical
  imperative read as the rule-of-law principle generalized into ethics;
  Humboldt's minimal-state conflation criticized; [[frederick-the-great|
  Frederick II's]] 1751 code initiating Continental codification; [[prussia|
  Prussia's]] 1797 law subjecting administrative disputes to ordinary courts;
  Gneist's independent administrative courts (1860s–70s) as "the crowning piece
  of the Rechtsstaat." France, by contrast (following Tocqueville), left
  centralized administrative power untouched through the Revolution. Hayek's
  verdict: "the completion of the structure designed to serve the ideal of the
  rule of law more or less coincided with the abandonment of the ideal" — the
  [[welfare-state]] ideal supplanted the liberal one just as the machinery was
  finished. He also indicts Dicey's "fundamental mistake" — judging German
  administrative courts by the French system — for stigmatizing administrative
  law in the Anglophone world and thereby "block[ing] the development which
  would have offered the best chance of preserving" the rule of law.

## Decline and Revival (Hayek's account, chs. 15–16)

Hayek proposes the rule of law itself — character, not volume, of government
activity — as the criterion separating admissible from inadmissible economic
policy: compatible are a reliable monetary system, standards, general safety and
factory legislation, non-monopolistic state enterprise, and rule-bound licensing
enforceable as a right; incompatible are price and quantity controls (inherently
discretionary) and "distributive justice," which "requires an allocation of all
resources by a central authority" — the bridge to his critique of the
[[welfare-state]] and the [[socialist-calculation-debate]].

His decline narrative: the demand for "substantive" justice against "merely
formal" equality (Anton Menger 1890: "there is no greater injustice than to
treat as equal what is in fact unequal!"; Anatole France's "majestic equality"
jibe, 1894); four German movements — legal positivism, historicism, the
*Freirechtsschule*, and the "jurisprudence of interest" — eroding legal limits
on the state; Kelsen's pure theory of law identifying the state with the legal
order ("The despotically governed state also represents some order of human
behavior. This order is the order of law"); Voegelin's 1927 observation that
natural-law adherence had become "a kind of social disgrace" in Germany;
Darmstädter's 1930 warning that the doctrine had "opened the doors to the
victory of the fascist and bolshevist will of the State" (see
[[weimar-republic]]); Soviet legal theory (Pashukanis — later purged) predicting
law's "withering away" (see [[communism-and-fascism]]); in England, Hewart's
*The New Despotism* (1929) dismissed as reactionary, and the Laski circle
(Jennings, Robson, Finer) redefining the rule of law as whatever parliament
enacts — Finer: "in a democracy right is what the majority makes it to be"; in
America, the public-administration movement and legal realism (Jerome Frank)
shaping New Deal legal culture. Post-war revival: Radbruch's recantation
("positivism... has rendered the German legal profession defenseless against
arbitrary and criminal laws" — cf. [[german-sonderweg]] debates and
[[natural-law]] revival), Britain's Franks Report (1957), and the International
Commission of Jurists' Act of Athens (1955), though Hayek criticizes the 1959
Declaration of Delhi for diluting the ideal into social-economic entitlements.

## The Trilogy Extension: *Law, Legislation and Liberty* (1973–79)

Hayek's late restatement shifts the diagnosis from doctrine to constitutional structure. Vol. 1 concedes that "the first attempt to secure individual liberty by constitutions has evidently failed": Montesquieu-style separation of powers did not prevent legislatures from acquiring the powers it was meant to deny them, because the same assembly makes rules of just conduct and directs government ([[nomos-and-thesis]]). He reinterprets British liberty as resting not on separation of powers but on courts applying "a law existing independently of anyone's will" (the common law), and adds the positivism genealogy in sharper form (Vol. 2): Hobbes's *non veritas sed auctoritas facit legem* → Bentham → Austin → Kelsen ("norms prescribing human behaviour can emanate only from human will, not from human reason"), with Kelsen's own concession quoted — "from the point of view of the science of law, the law (Recht) under the Nazi-government was law (Recht)" — and the verdict that "legal positivism is in this respect simply the ideology of socialism … and of the omnipotence of the legislative power." Against Radbruch: "The distributive justice at which socialism aims is thus irreconcilable with the rule of law." Vol. 2 also supplies the epistemology: only a *negative* test of injustice exists, paralleling Popper's falsificationism; positivism rightly showed there are no positive criteria of justice but wrongly concluded there are no objective (negative) ones — "If this were true, the whole basis of classical liberalism would collapse." Vol. 3's remedy is the two-assembly [[demarchy|model constitution]]; the constitutional-history lineage there runs Vile (Civil War: "parliament could be as tyrannical as a king") → Locke → *Cato's Letters* → Paley → James Wilson, dating the modern breach to Parliament's 1766 claim of unlimited sovereignty. All attributed — the same Whig-partisan caveats as above apply.

## Limitations and Critiques

- Formal legality is compatible with substantively unjust rules (the "wicked
  legal system" objection); legal positivists and realists dispute Hayek's claim
  that generality alone constrains tyranny — and Hayek himself concedes the
  classification problem has no formal solution.
- Ronald Hamowy's critique (later incorporated into the Definitive Edition
  apparatus he edited): Hayek's criteria of generality and certainty may not
  actually exclude the interventions Hayek wants to rule out.
- Hayek's genealogy is explicitly selective — a Whig lineage
  (Athens–Rome–Coke–Locke–Hume) constructed to legitimate his own
  classical-liberal project; his "decline" framing is interpretation, not
  neutral historiography. His treatment of legal positivism as causally
  implicated in Nazi legality is itself contested in post-war jurisprudence
  (the Hart–Fuller debate).
