## Definition and Scope

Fiqh is the technical jurisprudential discipline through which Islamic scholars (the [[ulama]]) derived and elaborated the [[sharia|Shari'ah]]. Hodgson renders it as "jurisprudence," noting that it "dealt with every case of conscience, not only in ritual but in private interpersonal relations." The word itself was practically synonymous with "inquiry" (ra'y) in the earliest period, before the discipline became systematized: "Originally, all the schools of legists, and not merely the Hanafis (as later) were accused by the Hadith folk of using ra'y, which was practically synonymous with fiqh, 'inquiry'" (Hodgson).

Fiqh must be distinguished from the [[sharia]] itself: fiqh is the method of derivation, the Shari'ah is the resulting normative order. The sources fiqh operates on are the four *usul al-fiqh* (roots of jurisprudence):

1. **The [[quran]]** — primary but insufficient alone as a legal code.
2. **The [[hadith]]** — reports of the Prophet's practice (sunnah), validated by isnad chains.
3. **Ijma'** — consensus of the Ummah (in practice: the accumulated fatwas of recognized 'ulama').
4. **Qiyas** — analogical reasoning extending hadith or Qur'anic precedents to novel cases.

## The Technical Operations of Fiqh

Working from the four roots, the faqih (jurisprudent) had to:

- **Establish chronological priority**: determine which Qur'anic verses or hadith had been superseded by later ones (naskh, abrogation). A verse prescribing prayer toward Jerusalem was superseded by one prescribing the Ka'bah; the faqih had to know both the historical sequence and the grounds of supersession.
- **Determine the degree of legal weight**: whether a given act was obligatory (with worldly penalty for violation), merely recommended, indifferent (permitted), discouraged, or forbidden. The distinction between acts bearing enforceable penalties and those left to divine judgment in the afterlife was of major practical significance, clarified above all by Bukhari's hadith collection.
- **Construct legal definitions**: What constitutes a "sale," a "gift," "property"? These definitional categories required independent reasoning beyond the hadith, and it was here, Hodgson notes, that Roman law concepts likely entered through Christian episcopal practice.

The resulting body of fiqh was case-based rather than codified: not "case law" in the common-law sense, but "law case by case, which could govern the whole field of ethics, cult, and private and even public law" (Hodgson).

## Al-Shafi'i's Systematization (d. 820)

The key figure in the systematization of usul al-fiqh as a formal discipline was **Muhammad b. Idris al-Shafi'i**. Before him, each regional school of legists operated with a mixture of local custom, hadith, personal judgment, and appeal to Medina precedent. Al-Shafi'i's innovation was to insist:

- Only Prophetic hadith (not custom of the caliphs or early associates) was authoritative as a second source.
- Hadith must be validated by an unbroken isnad leading to the Prophet himself.
- Qiyas, not arbitrary ra'y (personal opinion), was the permitted method of legal extension.
- Ijma' meant community-wide consensus, traceable to tacit Prophetic approval.

This did not eliminate all personal judgment — al-Shafi'i himself had to allow supplementary principles to guide qiyas — but it radically constrained it. The Hanafis defended their early masters' use of ra'y by calling it "preferability" (istihsan), a supplementary principle other schools used in different forms.

## The Madhhab Schools and Isnad Criticism

After al-Shafi'i, the provincial traditions of fiqh crystallized into four Jama'i-Sunni madhhab schools: **Hanafi**, **Maliki**, **Shafi'i**, and **Hanbali**. Their disputes were often formally about ritual minutiae (hand positions in salat, purification requirements), but reflected deeper differences in the weighting of the four roots.

Parallel to legal fiqh, a specialized discipline of **isnad criticism** ('ilm al-rijal — "knowledge of the men") developed to evaluate the reliability of hadith transmitters. Critics traced the recorded character of each transmitter and rejected those whose virtue, memory, or doctrine were suspect. This was ultimately a method of enforcing religious homogeneity rather than strictly historical accuracy: "isnad criticism amounted to selecting those reports that were circulated by persons who respected the same sorts of authorities as did the critic" (Hodgson). The six canonical Sunni hadith collections (especially those of **Bukhari** and **Muslim**) emerged from this discipline.

## Fiqh and the Limits of Caliphal Authority

A significant consequence of the maturation of fiqh was the structural limitation it placed on caliphal power. Because the [[ulama]] held that legal authority derived from the Shari'ah as elaborated through fiqh — not from the caliph's decree — they constituted an autonomous "clerical class" whose fatwas could constrain the sovereign. The [[abbasid-caliphate|'Abbasid]] caliphs conceded this in practice, permitting the qadi courts to follow Shar'i procedure. The supplementary court of mazalim (caliphal equity jurisdiction) existed precisely because the Shari'ah's procedural rigor was often incompatible with effective governance.

## Fiqh in Personal and Commercial Life

For private individuals, fiqh in practice meant consulting a mufti (jurisconsultant) of one's madhhab on matters of ritual and personal conscience. If a dispute reached the qadi's court, the judge ruled according to his own madhhab. Commercial law was heavily influenced by fiqh, and legists like Abu-Hanifah developed *hiyal* (legal stratagems) to reconcile the formal ban on interest with the needs of mercantile life — subterfuges that allowed functionally interest-bearing transactions while observing the letter of the prohibition.

## Historiography

The study of the origins of fiqh was transformed by Ignaz Goldziher's *Muhammedanische Studien* (1889–90) and Joseph Schacht's *Origins of Muhammadan Jurisprudence* (1950), which argued that most fiqh doctrine was back-projected onto the Prophet through fabricated hadith. Wael Hallaq's later work rehabilitated some early hadith material and emphasized the genuine development of legal reasoning within early Muslim communities. The collection's coverage of this scholarly debate is thin — Hodgson provides the structural-historical account but not the detailed philological analysis found in Schacht or Hallaq.
