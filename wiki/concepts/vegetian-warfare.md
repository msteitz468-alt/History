## Definition

"Vegetian warfare" (or Vegetian strategy) denotes a style of campaigning that deliberately avoids decisive pitched battle in favor of ravaging enemy territory (chevauchée), besieging or starving out fortified positions, and wearing down an opponent's economic base and will to resist — following Vegetius's counsel that a good commander should subdue an enemy "by famine rather than by the sword" since battle risks everything on a single throw of fortune.

## Emic vs. Etic

Partly emic: Vegetius's own text was read, copied, and cited as practical doctrine throughout the medieval period, so medieval commanders were often consciously following (or invoking) Vegetian precepts. The "Vegetian warfare" label itself, however, is an etic historiographical framework coined by modern medievalists to characterize a recurring strategic pattern across many different conflicts.

## Significance and Contestation

The concept, associated with the [[concepts/medieval-campaign-style|proprietorial character of medieval campaign style]], has been used to explain the relative rarity of major pitched battles across much of medieval European warfare — commanders with limited, non-renewable feudal forces had strong incentives to avoid catastrophic single-battle losses. It is often set against the [[concepts/western-way-of-war|"Western Way of War"]] thesis, which emphasizes a persistent Western preference for decisive infantry battle; historians debate how universally the Vegetian pattern applied and whether apparent battle-avoidance reflected doctrine, logistics, or simple caution.
