# Termination Policy

U.S. (and briefly proposed Canadian) program to end federal trusteeship and special tribal status—presented as “freedom from supervision” and “political equality,” experienced by Native communities as existential threat to governments and homelands. Peak legislative moment: [[termination-era-1953]]; renounced by Nixon 1970; Menominee Restoration 1973 signals official end in scholarly reading.

## Definition

**Etic label** for a policy complex: Congressional “sense” that named tribes be freed from federal supervision (HCR 108); unilateral transfer of civil/criminal jurisdiction to states (PL 280); individual termination statutes dissolving trust for specific tribes; administrative assimilation (public schools, health-service transfer); and urban relocation promoting permanent off-reservation residence. Contrasts_with: [[indian-reorganization-act-1934]] community reconstitution; contrasts_with / followed_by: [[tribal-self-determination]] and government-to-government rhetoric.

part_of: [[reservation-period-1880-1960]]; opposed_by: National Congress of American Indians and allied tribes; contributed_to: [[pan-indian-movement]] (homeland nationalism)

## Core US measures (Hoxie)

### Legislative apex, 1953
- **House Concurrent Resolution 108:** “sense of Congress” that named tribes be “freed from federal supervision” at an early date—marking departure from protecting Native communities as policy goal.
- **Public Law 280:** unilaterally turned civil and criminal jurisdiction on most reservations in **California, Minnesota, Nebraska, and Wisconsin** over to the states, abolishing reservation police/courts there; Eisenhower called it a step toward “complete political equality.”
- See event page: [[termination-era-1953]].

### Termination statutes and scale
- 1954 laws set procedures for **Klamaths (Oregon), Menominees (Wisconsin)**, and tribes in Utah, Oregon, Texas; Southern Paiutes terminated **1957**; Menominees last cut off **1961**.
- BIA “negotiated” while telling leaders termination was inevitable.
- By Nixon’s **1970** renunciation: over **13,000 tribal members** and **1.3 million acres (445,000 hectares)** “freed” of federal control—tiny fraction of estate/population but large enough to inject fear into every Native community.

### Administrative assimilation
- Public-school enrollment of Indians rose **>60%** in the 1950s while federal school attendance stayed steady (related: [[indian-boarding-schools]] displacement by public schools).
- From **1952** Indian hospitals began transfer to states; **1954** Indian health responsibility moved from BIA to Public Health Service—goal gradual end of distinct federal Indian institutions.

### Relocation
- BIA Relocation Branch **1954**; by 1961 offices in Chicago, Los Angeles, Denver, San Francisco, San Jose, Oakland, Dallas, Cleveland had supervised **>30,000** moves; of ~**122,000** Indians who moved to cities 1940–1960 government assisted probably **≤¼**; critics charged up to **75%** returned home; urban centers pan-Indian by definition (enabled: [[pan-indian-movement]]).

## Defeat and incomplete repair

- NCAI Feb 1954 “Declaration of Indian Rights”: reservations as “ancestral homelands,” tribes as nations within a nation—once made, termination could not be justified as “in the best interests” of Indians (Hoxie).
- **Menominee Restoration Act 1973** (Ada Deer / DRUMS, NCAI, allied anthropologists)—Washburn: scholar’s phrase “signaled the official end of the policy of termination”; Klamaths successful a decade later; other smaller groups never regained federal protection.
- Washburn identifies termination with Eisenhower administration **1953–61** and bipartisan belief that reservation/trust held Indians back economically; Sen. Arthur V. Watkins (Utah) a leading figure; results “disastrous”; Menominees coerced (scholars’ conclusion); Klamath case more ambiguous (per-capita timber desire).

## Canadian analogue

- June **1969** Trudeau/Chrétien **White Paper**: terminate special Indian treatment including Indian Act 1876 rights and pre-/post-Confederation treaties; Trudeau in Vancouver: “we say we won’t recognize aboriginal rights.”
- Native “Red Paper” *Citizens Plus* (1970) demanded treaty-rights preservation, special status, compensation for aboriginal title; occupations/demonstrations → White Paper withdrawn **March 1971**.
- analogous_to: [[termination-era-1953]]; contributed_to: [[pan-indian-movement]]; contrasts_with: [[tribal-self-determination]].
- Earlier absorption logic: Canadian 1920 compulsory enfranchisement bill (Duncan Campbell Scott: continue until “there is not a single Indian in Canada that has not been absorbed into the body politic”)—passed then repealed late 1921 (Hoxie).

## Contestation and lingering logic

**Contested: yes.** Proponents: equality, end of “wardship,” economic integration. Opponents: destruction of treaty-protected homelands and governments. Washburn notes assimilation assumption persisted even after 1950s repudiation—e.g. Interior Secretary Stewart Udall (1967): “gradualist — gradual termination” while Great Society funding surged. Alaska ANCSA 1971 corporate model contrasts_with IRA tribes while seeking to avoid lower-48 trusteeship. Policy formally renounced; fear memory and incomplete restorations remain structural.
