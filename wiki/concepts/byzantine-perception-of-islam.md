# Byzantine Perception of Islam and Islamic Perception of Byzantium

## Definition and Scope

The mutual perception of Byzantines and the Islamic world across the 12th and 13th centuries — the central Crusade period — is a distinct historiographical field examining how each civilization understood, stereotyped, and represented the other. This page covers primarily the **Islamic perception of Byzantium** during the Crusade era, as analyzed by Nadia Maria El-Cheikh in the volume *The Crusades from the Perspective of Byzantium and the Muslim World* (Dumbarton Oaks, 2001).

The field is distinguished from the history of Byzantine-Muslim political and military relations (Vasiliev's focus) and from Byzantine theological polemic against Islam, which is a separate tradition. El-Cheikh concentrates on Arabic-Islamic prose sources — universal chronicles, local histories, geographical works, biographical dictionaries — from the 12th and 13th centuries, deliberately excluding hero-cycle epics (Sirat 'Antar, Sirat Dhat al-Himma) whose dates and authorship are too uncertain.

## Key Sources

The core Arabic sources El-Cheikh analyzes:

- **Ibn Jubayr** (d. 1217): Andalusian traveler; his *Rihla* (voyage 1183–85) provides firsthand observations of Byzantine-built sites in Egypt and Syria, and a garbled but revealing account of events in Constantinople in 1182.
- **Yaqut** (d. 1229): geographer; *Mu'jam al-buldan* traces Byzantine origins and geography.
- **al-Qazwini** (d. 1285): cosmographer; *Athar al-bilad* contains detailed descriptions of Constantinople's monuments, stressing their talismanic character.
- **Ibn al-Athir** (d. 1233): historian; *al-Kamil fi al-tarikh* provides the clearest Arabic account of the Latin sack of Constantinople in 1204.
- **al-Idrisi** (fl. 1154): geographer at Roger II of Sicily's court; writes before the Latin conquest.
- **al-Harawi** (d. 1215): based on his own travels; calls Constantinople "greater than its reputation."
- **Jamal al-Din Ibn Wasil** (d. 1298): Ayyubid historian.
- **al-Jazari** (d. 1338): Damascene historian; includes the merchant 'Abdallah's extended firsthand account of late-13th-century Constantinople under Andronikos II Palaiologos.
- **Ibn al-'Adim** (d. 1262): Aleppine historian.

Modern historiography: A. A. Vasiliev (*Byzance et les Arabes*, 1950, the foundational work on military and political relations) and Marius Canard (whose extensive bibliography covers sociocultural, diplomatic, and economic dimensions) are the two dominant figures. André Miquel's *La géographie humaine du monde musulman* and A. Shboul's work on al-Mas'udi extended the cultural dimension.

## Major Themes

### Terminology and Identification

Arabic sources called the Byzantines **al-Rum** throughout the 12th–13th centuries. Upon the arrival of the Crusaders, Arab authors initially confused Franks with Byzantines — both called al-Rum. The new term **al-Ifranj** was gradually coined for the Franks, reflecting an emerging and durable distinction. By the later 12th century, Muslims clearly distinguished Byzantines (al-Rum) from Crusaders (al-Ifranj), though both terms could still be used loosely to mean "Christian." The older term **Banu al-Asfar** ("sons of the yellow/blond one") continued to be applied to Byzantines, traced genealogically through Esau son of Isaac.

### Image of Byzantium: Continuities

The 12th–13th century Arabic sources perpetuated a well-established positive image of Byzantium:
- **Physical beauty**: Byzantines described as white, blond, sturdy — attributes highly valued in Arabic typologies of beauty.
- **Artistic and architectural supremacy**: Ibn Jubayr, al-Qazwini, and others reiterate the tradition (reaching back to al-Jahiz, d. 868) that Byzantines are "the most skilled nation in painting" and supreme builders. Byzantine craftsmen are credited with the Citadel of Cairo's stonework, mosques in Harran and Damascus.
- **Constantinople's greatness**: Even after the disasters of 1182 and 1204, Arab authors maintained the city's prestige. Al-Harawi: "Constantinople is a city greater than its reputation." Al-Qazwini: "Nothing was ever built like it." The merchant 'Abdallah (1293) describes a prosperous, cosmopolitan city with a Muslim quarter and a Jewish quarter.
- **Talismanic monuments**: Arab authors attributed magical-talismanic power to Constantinople's monuments — the Column of Justinian (with Constantine's equestrian statue), the Column of Theodosius, the Horologium (clock), the bronze horses. This derives from the association of Apollonius of Tyana ("father of talismans") with Byzantine territory.

### Image of Byzantium: Ruptures

A significant **break** occurred in the 12th–13th century:
- **Disappearance of moral censure**: Earlier Arabic sources (9th–11th centuries) were full of accusations against Byzantines — sexual immorality, treachery, women's improper behavior. These moral critiques vanished entirely from 12th–13th century texts. Al-Qazwini's sole character comment is positive: "they are playful and joyful."
- **Disappearance of the knowledge debate**: Earlier texts debated Byzantine versus Greek learning; later texts only confirm Byzantine custody of ancient Greek knowledge, without the earlier polemical edge.
- **The Crusader displacement**: El-Cheikh argues that the arrival of the Franks redirected Islamic animosity. Usama ibn Munqidh's famous line encapsulates the shift: "When one comes to recount cases regarding the Franks, he cannot but glorify Allah and sanctify him, for he sees them as animals possessing the virtues of courage and fighting but nothing else." The rhetoric and stereotyping formerly aimed at Byzantines transferred to the Franks.

### The Crusade Context

The political context of 12th–13th century Islamic perception of Byzantium:
- The Byzantine Empire was politically and militarily weakened following Manzikert (1071); Alexios I Komnenos stabilized it but could not prevent subsequent decline.
- The Crusaders, unlike Byzantines, made political alliances against both Muslim and Byzantine interests — eventually sacking Constantinople in 1204.
- Arab authors were aware of the **massacre of Latins in Constantinople in 1182** (though Ibn Jubayr garbles the events, confused in his account of who conquered whom) and of the **Latin conquest of 1204** (clearly reported by Ibn al-Athir and acknowledged by Yaqut and Ibn Wasil).
- After 1204 and the Byzantine restoration (1261), a **Byzantine-Egyptian (Mamluk) alliance** emerged against shared Mongol, Turkish, and Western threats. Sultan Qalawun exchanged sworn friendship with Emperor Michael VIII in 1281. This political alliance reinforced the softening of Islamic attitudes toward Byzantium.
- The new importance of Jerusalem in Muslim discourse (fada'il al-quds literature; Islamic conquest of Jerusalem by Saladin in 1187) temporarily displaced Constantinople from its symbolic pre-eminence in Islamic apocalyptic tradition, though Constantinople remained culturally admired.

### Constantinople in Islamic Imagination

Arab authors concentrated disproportionately on Constantinople relative to the empire as a whole. Major monuments — Hagia Sophia, the Column of Justinian, the Column of Theodosius, the Horologium, the bronze horses — served as "prototype symbols," the "medieval tourist paradigm" (following Netton). Islamic monuments in the city — the tomb of Abu Ayyub al-Ansari (participant in the 7th-century Arab siege), the mosque of Maslama (leader of the 715–717 expedition) — "Islamized" the city, giving Constantinople a symbolic Muslim genealogy that allowed inclusion in pilgrimage literature (as in al-Harawi's *Kitab al-isharat*).

## Scholarly Significance

El-Cheikh's study demonstrates that the Crusades transformed the Islamic literary image of Byzantium in ways largely favorable to the Byzantines — a counterintuitive finding. The entry of a third party (the Franks) shifted the binary Islamic-Byzantine antagonism, redirecting Arab cultural rhetoric. The picture is one of selective continuity (praise for beauty, craftsmanship, Constantinople's grandeur) combined with conspicuous rupture (the disappearance of moral critique).

The approach also illustrates the limitations of Arabic sources for this period: they reflect palace culture, the views of 'Ulama' and senior bureaucrats; popular attitudes are largely inaccessible. The tendency toward compilation and systematization in 12th–13th century Arabic historiography means later sources frequently reproduce earlier traditions verbatim rather than reporting contemporary observation.
