# Indo-Muslim Mystical Poetry in the Regional Languages

## Definition and Scope

Indo-Muslim mystical poetry in the regional languages designates the corpus of devotional and mystical verse produced by South Asian Muslims (and some Hindus) in Sindhi, Punjabi, Pashto, Bengali, Dakhni, and Urdu — as distinct from the Persian and Arabic literary tradition that coexisted with it. The tradition is unified by:

1. **Vernacular mediums** linked to indigenous musical modes rather than Persian quantitative metres
2. **Folk-tale vehicles** (the love stories of Hīr-Ranjhā, Sassuī-Punhun, Sohnī-Mehanwal etc.) allegorised as mystical journeys of the soul
3. **The feminine longing soul**: the soul is systematically depicted as a woman yearning for union with the divine, an inheritance from Hindi devotional poetry (bhakti) adapted into an Islamic framework
4. **Village imagery** (gardening, weaving, spinning, water) rather than the courtly rose-and-nightingale imagery of Persian poetry
5. **Sustained devotion to the Prophet** alongside Sufi martyrological figures (Ḥallāj above all)

This tradition is emic: the poets themselves identified as Muslims working within Sufi lineages. Schimmel warns against interpreters — "particularly the Hindus who studied this aspect of Indo-Muslim religious life" — who read the tradition as proof that "Indian advaita mysticism gained a complete victory over Islamic monotheism" ([[schimmel-mystical-dimensions-of-islam-1975]]).

## Sindhi

### Precursors (16th–17th centuries)

Reliable tradition traces Sindhi mystical poetry to Qāḍī Qāḍan (d. 1546), whose few surviving verses "distinctly show all the peculiarities of later poetry." The 16th century also saw a Sindhi Qādirī group migrate to Burhanpur in central India, using Sindhi verses in their spiritual concerts. The 17th-century poets Shāh ʿAbduʾl-Karīm and Shāh ʿInāt developed the vernacular tradition further.

### Shāh ʿAbduʾl-Laṭīf of Bhit (1689–1752)

The undisputed master. His *Shāha jō risālō* ("The Book of Shāh"), first published by German missionary Ernst Trumpp in 1866, contains thirty chapters (*sur*) organised by musical mode — partly traditional Indian *rāgas*, partly original modes invented by Laṭīf himself. The *surs* work through folk tales: Sassuī, Sohnī-Mehanwal, Maruī, Nūrī-Tamāchī, Sorathi. In each, the heroine's longing and suffering allegorise the soul's journey toward divine union. See [[shah-abdul-latif]].

A characteristic technique: Laṭīf translates into poetry the critical moment of each tale — Sohnī swimming across the Indus at night with a clay jar, Sassuī dying in the desert — and finds in that extremity the Sufi motifs of annihilation (*fanāʾ*), interior journey, and God's reciprocal seeking of the soul (citing Rūmī's *Mathnawī* 1:1741: "Not only the thirsty seek the water, but the water seeks the thirsty as well").

The *Risālō* is grounded in the classical Sufi tradition: "Shāh ʿAbduʾl-Laṭīf did use the inherited language of the Sufis; he himself acknowledged his indebtedness to Rūmī, and his work cannot be fully appreciated without a thorough knowledge of the different currents of Sufism in Iran and India." His language is "compact" and "opaque," working through allusion and "oblique expressions" — "one may best characterise these verses as sighs, as heartbeats of a longing soul."

### Sachal Sarmast (1739–1826)

Second only to Laṭīf. Wrote in Sindhi, Siraiki, Urdu, and Persian. More direct and intoxicated than Laṭīf — "whatever came into his heart with no thought for the consequences." His Sindhi verse reveals unity of being in "short, jubilant verses": God is "Abū Hanīfa and He is Hanuman, / He is the Koran and He is the Vedas." His long litanies of Sufi martyrs — Sarmad, Ḥallāj, ʿAṭṭār, Shams, Bullhē Shāh — place him in the tradition of *ʿAṭṭār* of Nishapur; Schimmel notes he has been called "the ʿAṭṭār of Sind." See sachal sarmast.

### Later Sindhi mysticism

The tradition continued after the British occupation of Sind (1843); a new Arabic alphabet introduced in 1852 fostered a written Sindhi literature including mystical poetry and prose. Political Sufi figures (the Pīr Pagarō and his *ḥurr* followers) operated into the 20th century.

## Punjabi

### Farīduddīn Ganj-i Shakar (d. 1265)

A few lines in "Old Panjabi" attributed to the great Chishtī saint of Pakpattan may represent the earliest stratum of Punjabi mystical verse — transmitted through women's songs.

### Mādhō Lāl Ḥusayn (1539–93)

First clearly documented Punjabi Sufi poet. He sang of divine love through the story of Hīr in simple Punjabi verses; his anniversary (*Mēlā Chirāghān*, "Fair of Lamps") is still celebrated in Lahore near the Shalimar Garden.

### Sulṭān Bāhū (d. 1691)

Known for his *Sīḥarfī* ("Golden Alphabet"), in which each line ends with the exclamation *hū* ("He"). His imagery is village-based: "Alif: Allah is like the jasmine plant which the preceptor planted in my heart." The poem grounds Sufi recollection (*dhikr*) in the agrarian world — "by water and the gardener of negation and positive statement" — combining the *lā ilāha illā Allāh* formula with the Quranic image "He is nearer to you than your jugular vein" (Sūra 50:16).

### Bullhē Shāh (1680–1752)

Greatest of the Punjabi mystical poets, considered "the Rūmī of Punjab." He used the Hīr-Ranjhā narrative for mystical meditation, as did his contemporary Hāshim Shāh. Wārith Shāh's *Hīr* (completed ca. 1794) gave the tale its classical form; his mystical key identifies: "Our soul is the tragic heroine Hīr, / Our body is the lover Ranjhā, / Our spiritual preceptor is the Yogi Balmath." See [[bullhe-shah]].

After 1800, the Punjabi language was used almost exclusively by Sikhs as a literary medium; Punjabi Muslims turned to Urdu for sophisticated poetry while continuing to enjoy vernacular ballads and folk songs.

## Pashto

The most important Pashto mystical poetry coincided with the Aurangzeb period. The Roshaniyya movement, founded by Pīr-i Rōshan (Bāyezīd Anṣārī, d. 1585), represents the earliest Pashto literary corpus proper. His *Khayr ul-bayān* in rhymed prose first applied Persian metrics to Pashto; its full-fledged pantheism was vehemently condemned by the orthodox preacher Akhūnd Darwāza.

Raḥmān Bābā (d. 1709), of the [[chishtiyya]], is regarded as the finest Pashto mystical poet: "sadness and ascetic feeling that are not too frequent in the mystical poets of this period," imagery indebted to Sanāʾī, themes of transience and the necessity of preparation for death. His verse is more sober than the Sindhi-Punjabi tradition, consistent with the Ghaznid literary heritage.

## Bengali

*Marifati* folk songs — sung on rivers, imagery of water hyacinths and tiny waves — give Bengali mystical poetry a distinctive softness. Muhammad's role as the boatsman who guides the soul is a recurrent figure. Theoretical works appeared in Bengali by the 17th century (Ḥājjī Muḥammad's *Nūrnāma* on *waḥdat al-wujūd* and light mysticism). As in Sind and Punjab, the Naqshbandiyya later tried to curb emotional excess in Bengali Sufi expression.

## Urdu

Urdu (in its early southern form *Dakhni*) was first used for mystical instruction by Gīsūdarāz of Golconda (d. 1422). It developed through 15th–16th-century mystics in Bijapur and Gujarat. The first translation of a Sufi classic into Dakhni Urdu was ʿAynuʾl-Quḍāt's *Tamhīdāt* (17th century).

The poet Walī of Deccan visited Delhi in 1707 (the year of Aurangzeb's death) and attached himself to the Naqshbandī master Shāh Saʿdullāh Gulshan, catalysing the shift from Persian to Urdu as the fashionable literary medium in the capital.

Khwāja Mīr Dard (1721–85) wrote "the first mystical poetry in Urdu." His small Dīwān of approximately 1200 verses is renowned for its concision and melancholy beauty — imagery of mirrors, footprints in the desert, reflections in water: "Alas, O ignorant one: at the day of death this will be proved: / A dream was what we saw, what we heard, a tale." His mystical system (*ṭarīqa muḥammadiyya*) is expounded in his large Persian prose works (*ʿIlm ul-kitāb*, 1770; *Chahār Risāla*, 1775–85). See [[mir-dard-khwaja]].

Muhammad Iqbal (1877–1938), though a critic of "Pirism" and devotional Sufism in organised form, drew deeply on classical Sufi imagery and his treatment of Ḥallāj (mediated by Louis Massignon) shows, in Schimmel's judgement, "extraordinary insight into the phenomenon of the enthusiastic mystic." Iqbal reinterpreted rather than rejected the Sufi heritage: "his imagery, like that of most Turkish, Persian, or Urdu poets, is largely colored by Sufi symbolism." See [[muhammad-iqbal]].

## Technical Forms

- **Kāfī / Wāy**: alternation between solo-recited main verses and choral refrain summarising the content; indigenous Indian prosody, not Persian quantitative metres
- **Sīḥarfī ("Golden Alphabet")**: acrostic poem following the Arabic/Persian alphabet, with a refrain attached to each letter-verse (Sultan Bāhū, Mīrzā Khān Anṣārī)
- **Dhōrā**: short lyric stanza form common in Punjabi
- **Musammat / Ghazal**: adapted Persian forms employed in Urdu after ca. 1700
- Musical modes (*sur*, *rāga*): the Sindhi tradition in particular is inseparable from its musical settings; the *Risālō*'s thirty *surs* each have distinctive melodic identities

## Interaction and Wider Significance

The tradition interacted closely with the [[bhakti-movement]]: shared rejection of learned theology (*mollā*-bashing is a standard motif in both), use of vernacular, imagery of feminine longing. The distinction maintained by Schimmel and Nizami is that the Islamic core — love of the Prophet, Quranic reference, adherence to *sharīʿa* — remained identifiable even in the most "intoxicated" Sindhi and Punjabi poets.

The folk tale vehicle enabled transmission to illiterate populations — "verses that are veritable gems" (Dard), easily remembered and sung. The tradition thus served a social function analogous to the Chishtī open-table: spiritual access regardless of literacy or caste.

## Limitations and Critiques

**Syncretism debate**: Hindu interpreters and some Western scholars have read the tradition as evidence of advaita mysticism overwhelming Islamic monotheism. Schimmel resists this: Punjabi and Sindhi mystics "none of them neglected the love of the Prophet," and the Prophetological framework remains operative even in Sachal's most ecstatic verses.

**Orthodox critique**: The Naqshbandī reform movement (Sirhindī, Makhdūm Muḥammad Hāshim of Thatta, Shāh Walīullāh) consistently criticised the emotional and syncretistic elements; Naqshbandī literary practice favoured Persian prose and sober dhikr over vernacular song and samāʿ.

**Collection coverage**: Schimmel's survey is authoritative but dated (1975). Dedicated scholarly editions of Sachal, Bullhē Shāh, and the *Risālō* have proliferated since; the wiki currently reflects only Schimmel's synthesis.
