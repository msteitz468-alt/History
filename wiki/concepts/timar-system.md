# The Timar System

The Ottoman system by which the revenues of agricultural land were assigned as
**non-hereditary fiefs (*timars*)** to cavalrymen (***sipahi***) in return for military
service — the institution that, alongside the **[[devshirme|slave household]]**, financed the
army and organized the provinces of the classical Ottoman Empire. From Colin Imber's
[[imber-ottoman-empire-1300-1650|*The Ottoman Empire, 1300–1650*]].

## How it worked

The state recorded land and its yield in periodic **cadastral surveys** and assigned blocks of
that revenue as ***timars*** to **sipahi** cavalrymen. The timar-holder did not own the land or
its cultivators: he held a **right to collect specified taxes** from the peasants (*reaya*)
farming it, in exchange for serving on campaign — mounted, armed, and bringing armed retainers
in proportion to his fief's value. Larger grants (*zeamet*, *has*) supported senior officers,
governors, and the sultan's own demesne. The system thus **converted agricultural surplus
directly into cavalry** without the treasury having to pay cash, and it gave the central
government, through the survey registers, a precise knowledge of provincial resources and the
military service owed.

This rested on the legal distinction (see [[ottoman-law-kanun]]) between the
**tax-paying subjects (*reaya*)** and the **military/servant class (*askeri*)** who lived from
fiefs or treasury salaries. The peasant held his tenement by contract and could pass it to his
heirs; the timar-holder was a salaried servant, not a feudal lord, and his fief reverted to the
state.

## Transformation and "decline"

Imber argues the timar system was undermined less by "corruption" than by **military and fiscal
change**. The [[ottoman-empire|"military revolution"]] from the late sixteenth century shifted
warfare toward **firearm-bearing infantry**, making the *timar* cavalry less central; the
[[janissaries]] and paid musketeers expanded, and the **treasury needed cash** to pay them. A
1590 attempt to re-survey the provinces and divert timar revenues to the treasury failed,
alienating timar-holders without finding much new income, and by 1600 "the old system of
distributing and controlling resources in the provinces was defunct." Revenue increasingly went
to **tax-farming (*iltizam*)** to raise cash, and dispossessed timar-holders and unemployed
musketeers swelled the **[[ottoman-empire|Jelali revolts]]** that convulsed Anatolia. The
result was a less centralized, more cash-and-tax-farm-based provincial order in the seventeenth
century.

## Significance

The timar system is the **agrarian-military backbone of the classical Ottoman state** —
comparable in function to, but distinct from, European feudal tenure (the holder a removable
servant, not a hereditary lord). Its breakdown is a central thread of the Ottoman
seventeenth-century crisis, marking the transition from the cavalry-and-survey order of the
apogee to the tax-farming, infantry-heavy state that followed.
