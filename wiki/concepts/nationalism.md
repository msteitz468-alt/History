# Nationalism

## Definition

Aviel Roshwald, in [[mcneill-cwh-v7-2015|CWH VII]], defines the **nation** as "a
population larger than one of personal acquaintance, to which shared, heritable
identity is ascribed, and in whose name political authority over a bounded territory
is claimed." **Nationalism** is then "the aspiration or active effort to achieve,
maintain, or expand the scope of a nation's shared identity, self-governance, and/or
power." A distinguishing mark of nationhood is that its sense of belonging is carried
by cultural traditions and commemoration that can exist apart from the state and
survive long stateless periods — Polish nationhood between the partitions (1790s) and
the republic (1918) being his example.

By the end of the twentieth century, with the collapse of Soviet internationalism,
the **nation state had achieved near-planetary acceptance** as the standard framework
for legitimate territorial sovereignty — "a sudden transformation after millennia in
which most people lived in multinational empires most of the time."

## Emic / Etic

Both. Nationalism is an **emic** force — actors mobilize, fight, and die in its name —
and an **etic** analytical category (Anderson's "imagined communities," Gellner,
Hobsbawm, Anthony Smith). Roshwald draws on Anderson's definition while insisting,
against pure modernists, that pre-modern proto-national identities existed.

## Origins: popular sovereignty

Roshwald's core thesis: what is distinctive about **modern** nationalism (from the
17th–18th centuries) is (a) its global diffusion as the default framework for
statehood and (b) its **close association with popular sovereignty**. So long as
sovereignty resided in one body — the divine-right monarch — the state's unity was
embodied in his person. Once **subjects became citizens** and sovereignty shifted to
the people (rhetorically, as in the French republics; de facto, as in Britain's
gradual democratization), a new question arose: what undergirds the unity of a
sovereign that now numbers tens of millions? The answer was a **shared national
identity** transcending its parts. The democratic ideal and the national ideal became,
in Sieyès's 1789 formula ("the Third Estate is a complete nation"), two sides of one
coin. The [[atlantic-revolutions|Atlantic revolutions]] — English, American, French,
and Latin American — are the watershed.

## Pre-modern antecedents

Roshwald rejects strict modernism: ethnicity (a "myth of shared ancestry") has been
"a key object of political control" wherever states existed — Assyrian deportations,
the Qing Banner system, the ethnic defections that aided Cortés. Genuine pre-modern
**expressions of national identity** include Periclean Athens, the Jewish revolts
against Rome, the Han appeal against the Yuan, and the 1320 Declaration of Arbroath.
What changed after 1750 is scale, diffusion, and the fusion with popular sovereignty.

## The imperial paradox

The very dynamics of **imperial** expansion facilitated nationalism's spread, and the
earliest classic nation states (the Netherlands, Britain, less neatly France) were
precisely the most committed to **overseas commercial and colonial empire** — "the
modern nation state was born amidst ever more entangling global networks of
exploitation, migration, cultural transfer, and exchange." Charles Tilly's
competitive, war-driven European state system ("coercion and capital") drove the
deepening interpenetration of state and society. Yet nationalism's global form
carried "distinctively Euro-Atlantic ideas about authority, consent, and identity"
outward — a tension at the heart of [[decolonization]] and post-colonial
state-building (David Bell's paradox: nationalist leaders must often "scramble to
define — and coercively consolidate — the identity of the very collectivity" they
claim to represent).

## Limitations and critiques

- **The dark side:** popular sovereignty was invoked by liberal republics and by
  "repressively authoritarian regimes that murdered millions in the very name of the
  people." Roshwald and Levene link the rise of the homogenizing national state
  directly to twentieth-century [[genocide]] — the greatest concentration of which
  occurred in the lands of the collapsed Habsburg, Ottoman, and Romanov empires.
- **Modernist vs. perennialist debate:** how old are nations? Roshwald takes a
  middle position.
- **Universal form, diverse content:** whether Euro-Atlantic models of nationhood can
  accommodate genuinely diverse identities and a stable, egalitarian world order
  remains, he concludes, an open question.
