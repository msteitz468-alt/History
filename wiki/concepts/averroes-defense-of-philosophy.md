## Definition and Scope

Across the Physical Sciences discussions of the [[tahafut-al-tahafut]], Averroes delivers a sustained methodological verdict on al-Ghazālī's philosophical approach and articulates a comprehensive position on the relationship between philosophy and religion. This is not a separate treatise but is embedded throughout his rebuttals of [[ghazali-on-causality]], [[ghazali-on-the-soul]], and the discussion of the philosophers' position on bodily resurrection. The verdict has three components: (1) a characterization of al-Ghazālī's dialectical method and its failings; (2) a principled account of why philosophical inquiry must be insulated from theological subordination; and (3) a demarcation of the domains of philosophy and religion that protects both. This page connects to [[philosophy-versus-theology-in-islam]], [[tahafut-al-tahafut]], and [[tahafut-al-falasifa]].

## Averroes' Verdict on al-Ghazālī's Dialectical Method

### The Charge of Sophistry

The word "sophistry" (safsata) appears repeatedly in Averroes' replies, and its use is precise. Al-Ghazālī is not merely wrong; he is arguing in bad faith or with insufficient rigor:

> "To deny the existence of efficient causes which are observed in sensible things is sophistry, and he who defends this doctrine either denies with his tongue what is present in his mind or is carried away by a sophistical doubt which occurs to him concerning this question."

This is the sharpest charge Averroes deploys. It implies that occasionalism — the denial of natural causation — is not a position any serious thinker can consistently hold, because it contradicts what "is present in his mind." The person who genuinely tried to live without causal reasoning could not navigate even the most basic practical situation.

More specifically, Averroes charges that al-Ghazālī "did not distinguish between what is self-evident and what is unknown" — he treated the non-existence of an explicitly identified cause as evidence for the non-existence of causes in general, which is a logical error. Things whose causes are not perceived are "still unknown and must be investigated," but this does not license the inference that observable causal connections are illusory.

### Confusing His Opponent's Conclusions with Premises

In his reply to al-Ghazālī's second point on the burning example, Averroes identifies a more specific dialectical vice:

> "Ghazali, to confuse his opponent, here regards as established what his opponent refuses to admit, and says that his opponent has no proof for his refusal."

Al-Ghazālī claimed to have refuted the philosophers' necessitarianism about the First Agent earlier in the book, and on that basis argued that God could create burning without the fire. But the philosophers never admitted that the First Agent acts voluntarily without a natural intermediary — they hold the opposite. Assuming the conclusion of a disputed prior argument as a premise in a new argument is a recognizable dialectical sophism.

### The Incoherence of the Asharite Theological Programme

Averroes identifies what he sees as the deepest problem with the Asharite theology underlying al-Ghazālī: having dismantled causal necessity, the theologians have made it impossible to draw any inference from the world to God's properties — including intelligence, wisdom, and providence. If everything is merely the direct and arbitrary output of divine will, then:

> "There would no longer be any wisdom in the world from which it might be inferred that its agent was wise... The man who denies the necessity of any item of knowledge must admit that even this, his own affirmation, is not necessary knowledge."

The self-refutation is complete: a theology that denies natural necessity destroys the evidential basis for rational theology. And yet the Asharite theologians do appeal to the orderly design of the world as evidence for God's intelligence ("harmony in the world indicates that its agent possesses mind") — which implicitly reinstates causal inference. Averroes identifies this as a contradiction at the heart of the Asharite programme:

> "All this is full of sophistry and is without sense, and the theologian who did this was Abu-l-Ma'ali" [al-Juwayni, Ghazali's own teacher].

### Al-Ghazālī's Late Concession and Its Significance

In the third point of Discussion 1, al-Ghazālī effectively concedes Averroes' core position — that things have natures from which acts flow — and tries to explain miracles through secondary modifications of those natures (God altering the fire's property so it does not act beyond its own body, or conferring a protective property on Abraham's body). Averroes acknowledges this concession but notes it changes al-Ghazālī's position significantly:

> "When Ghazali saw that the theory that things have no particular qualities and forms from which particular acts follow, for every thing is very objectionable, and contrary to common sense, he conceded this in this last section and replaced it by the denial of two points..."

The two points al-Ghazālī still denies are: (a) that things always must act on one another in the way they usually do (blocking conditions are impossible), and (b) that particular forms require particular matters. Averroes accepts the first: blocking conditions are real and philosophers themselves acknowledge them. On the second, he shows that al-Ghazālī's own theological commitments (about what is impossible) already implicitly commit him to material conditions — "there is no difference between our simultaneous negation and affirmation of a thing and our simultaneous denial of part of it and affirmation of the whole."

## The Philosophy–Religion Demarcation

### Philosophers Do Not Dispute Religious Principles

Averroes' most important positive statement about philosophy and religion in these discussions is his account of why the ancient philosophers did not comment on miracles:

> "We do not find that any of the ancient philosophers discusses miracles, although they were known and had appeared all over the world, for they are the principles on which religion is based and religion is the principle of the virtues; nor did they discuss any of the things which are said to happen after death."

The reason is not ignorance or indifference but a principled demarcation: every science has its principles, which students of that science must accept without philosophical disputation. Religion is a practical science whose principles — including the miracles that authenticate prophetic authority — must be accepted on the authority of the prophets. To philosophically scrutinize or refute these principles is to undermine the social and moral function of religion:

> "It is necessary for every man to concede the principles of religion and invest with authority the man who lays them down. The denial and discussion of these principles denies human existence, and therefore heretics must be killed."

This is a strong claim: philosophical disputation of religious principles is not merely impious but socially destructive, because religion is the necessary basis for moral order. The philosopher who has attained deep learning and discovers a philosophical explanation for a religious principle should not divulge it:

> "It is enjoined upon him that he should not divulge this explanation and should say 'all these are the terms of religion and the wise', conforming himself to the Divine Words, 'but those who are deeply versed in knowledge say: we believe in it, it is all from our Lord'."

This doctrine of selective esoteric disclosure anticipates the treatment in Averroes' *Fasl al-Maqāl* (Decisive Treatise), where he argues that philosophical truths must not be communicated to those without the capacity to receive them, and that scriptural language uses modes appropriate to different audiences.

### The Wisdom of God as the Basis of Both Philosophy and Religion

Averroes concludes Discussion 1 with a statement of what might be called his theological naturalism — the claim that natural necessity and divine wisdom are one and the same:

> "Existents are divided into opposites and correlates, and if the latter could be separated, the former might be united, but opposites are not united and correlates therefore cannot be separated. And this is the wisdom of God and God's course in created things, and you will never find in God's course any alteration. And it is through the perception of this wisdom that the intellect of man becomes intellect, and the existence of such wisdom in the eternal intellect is the cause of its existence in reality."

The natural order of causal necessity is not a constraint on God but an expression of divine wisdom. The intellect that perceives natural order perceives divine wisdom. Philosophy and true religion, therefore, do not conflict — they have different audiences and different modes of access to the same truth. The theologians' error (on Averroes' account) is that they mistake a rhetorical-religious presentation of truth for the whole of truth, and then try to defend it by dismantling the rational structure that underlies it.

### On the Soul and Afterlife

In Discussion 3, Averroes extends the demarcation to the afterlife. Whether the soul survives death, and in what form, is a question the philosophers approach through the philosophy of mind; but the specific doctrines of bodily resurrection and post-mortem states are religious principles not subject to philosophical negation. He neither affirms the philosophers' denial of bodily resurrection nor philosophically demonstrates it. The charge that the philosophers (particularly Avicenna and al-Fārābī) denied bodily resurrection was central to al-Ghazālī's *Tahāfut al-Falāsifa*, but Averroes declines to accept responsibility for that position on behalf of "the learned among the philosophers."

This evasion is itself a methodological stance: questions about the fate of the individual soul after death belong, at the level appropriate for mass communication, to religion; at the level appropriate for the learned, they belong to the demonstrative sciences of the soul. Al-Ghazālī has blurred the demarcation by importing religious concerns into philosophical debate and using philosophical argument to attack religious positions — in both cases trespassing the appropriate boundaries.

## Averroes and the Unity of the Intellect

The Physical Sciences discussions do not directly address the famous [[unity-of-intellect]] doctrine (that there is one material intellect shared by all humanity), which Averroes elaborated elsewhere. But the passage in Discussion 3 on soul-individuation — where he discusses the possibility of numerically distinct souls surviving in a "subtle matter" — is relevant context: Averroes entertains this view without fully endorsing it, suggesting his views on soul-individuation were still developing or were deliberately held back from public statement.

## Reception

In Latin scholasticism, these discussions were read (after the thirteenth-century translations) as a systematic defense of the autonomy of philosophy from theology — "the Double Truth" thesis, falsely attributed to Averroes by his critics, but roughly implied by his demarcation. Siger of Brabant and Boethius of Dacia developed positions in this direction, provoking the [[the-three-condemnations]] of 1277. Thomas Aquinas disputed the interpretation of Averroes extensively, including on the unity of the intellect and the relationship of demonstrated conclusions to revealed doctrine. Whether Averroes himself held a "double truth" doctrine or rather a hierarchical esotericism (one truth, multiple presentations) remains debated.

In the Islamic world, Averroes' defense of philosophy against al-Ghazālī was less immediately influential. The [[occasionalism-islamic]] position he attacked remained dominant in Asharite kalam, and Averroes himself was marginalized in the eastern Islamic world while gaining his greatest influence in al-Andalus and then in Latin Europe. The broader question of [[philosophy-versus-theology-in-islam]] that his debate with al-Ghazālī crystallizes was never definitively resolved in the Islamic tradition.
