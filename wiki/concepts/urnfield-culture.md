# Urnfield Culture

The **Urnfield Culture** (or Urnfield tradition/phenomenon) is the etic archaeological label for a broad late Bronze Age cultural complex across temperate and parts of Mediterranean Europe, c. 1300–800 BCE (overlapping the transition to Early Iron Age in some regions). It is defined primarily by a new burial rite of **urned cremation** in flat cemeteries (as opposed to earlier barrow or collective traditions), together with a high degree of uniformity at the elite level in bronze metalwork, fine pottery styles, and symbolic motifs.

## Definition and Scope

"Over much of central and western Europe a considerable degree of cultural uniformity was established during the final centuries of the second millennium... The most characteristic feature was a new burial rite of urned cremation, and the discovery of many cemeteries of this sort has given the name 'Urnfield' to this cultural tradition and this period." (Champion et al. 1984, p. 270, lines ~3570s in range).

The generally accepted classification uses Paul Reinecke's scheme: **Hallstatt A and B** belong to the late Bronze Age Urnfield period (with Hallstatt C–D continuing into the Iron Age). 

"The pottery vessels selected for use in burials frequently conformed to a standard type, with a tall cylinder-shaped neck... There was also considerable homogeneity of bronze types, especially weapons and ornaments, and particularly pins. New techniques for making complex sheet bronze objects were developed, and applied to armour and to vessels, especially buckets and cups... There was also great uniformity in the use of symbols, which clearly had an important but unknown ideological meaning. Sun symbols and birds' heads, among others, are regularly found decorating bronze vessels, armour and other objects."

Local variation existed: "A number of local groups within the Urnfield culture can be distinguished, mainly on the basis of styles of pottery and metalwork, but also showing a wide variation in the actual form of burial (e.g. Württemberg... Bohemia... The Lausitz group in southern Poland and eastern Germany is particularly well known for its well-researched cemeteries and its numerous fortified sites."

## Geographic Spread

"The transition to this Urnfield culture may have begun earliest in parts of central Europe, perhaps in Hungary, but within a short space of time the new complex incorporated many areas further west, including much of Italy... By soon after 1000 BC, and possibly even earlier, further areas still had been included, and the Urnfield complex spread as far as Belgium, southern and western France and north-eastern Spain. This cultural tradition was maintained until towards the end of the eighth century."

Northern Europe (Montelius Periods III–V) showed some connections (related swords, sheet bronze armour/vessels) but retained more continuity and did not fully adopt the cremation rite. Western areas often lack consistent burial evidence and are studied via metalwork hoards and typology.

## Relation to Social Differentiation and Exchange

The Urnfield phase is part of the broader **Bronze Age social differentiation** in temperate Europe, building on earlier prestige economies (Wessex, Unetice) but marking shifts in burial symbolism and possibly the basis of power toward control of subsistence alongside prestige goods (see [[processes/bronze-age-social-differentiation]]). 

"Connections with the Urnfield world can be seen in the adoption of related sword types and in the appearance of sheet bronze objects such as armour and large vessels."

It coincides with continued long-distance exchange of exotica (amber from Baltic sources still circulated) and craft specialization in bronze production. Fortified sites continue or proliferate in some groups (e.g. Lausitz).

## Historiography and Links to Broader Concepts

Treated in the source as an etic construct reflecting elite-level cultural uniformity and interaction rather than a monolithic ethnic or "people" entity (consistent with the book's post-radiocarbon, post-Childe critique of culture-as-people equations; see [[concepts/culture-historical-archaeology]], [[concepts/culture-concept-childe]]). 

The spread of the cremation rite and associated metal styles (including into Italy as Proto-Villanovan) is linked to wider transformations at the end of the second millennium, including the collapse of Mycenaean palace systems and reorientation of exchange networks.
