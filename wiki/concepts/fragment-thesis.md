# The Fragment Thesis

Louis Hartz's ([[hartz-louis]]) comparative theory of the societies "struck
off" from Europe by migration: when **a part of a European nation is
detached from the whole and hurled outward onto new soil, it loses the
stimulus toward change that the whole provides** and "lapses into a kind of
immobility." The fragment carries the ideology of its moment of departure —
and only that — and, freed from the European struggle that ideology was
embedded in, universalizes it, buries it beneath a new nationalism, and
develops it to a fullness Europe never permitted. First stated for the
United States in [[hartz-liberal-tradition-1955]] (the
[[lockean-consensus]]); generalized to five societies in
[[hartz-founding-new-societies-1964]].

This is an **etic** framework: no settler ever described their society as a
"fragment" — indeed Hartz argues the whole psychology of the fragment is
built on *denying* its fragmentary character.

## The Mechanism: Extrication → Atrophy → Unfolding

1. **Extrication.** The fragment escapes its original European enemy (the
   Puritan flees Laud; the seigneur of New France never meets Voltaire).
2. **Atrophy of the future.** Because European ideologies generate one
   another by "contagion" — feudalism provokes the Enlightenment, Whiggery
   inspires the Jacobin, the Jacobin inspires the socialist — escaping the
   past enemy also forecloses all the future ones it would have bred.
   "Marx fades because of the fading of Laud." There is "no shrinking of
   the European past at any point which does not shrink the European
   future as well."
3. **Interior unfolding.** Freed from competitors, the fragment ideology
   develops potentials Europe strangled: Jacksonian democracy and the New
   Deal "burgeon because both the right and the left are missing"; the
   Australian Labour Party masters its scene "with an ease unheard of in
   the annals of European socialism." Hartz calls these "Cinderella"
   stories: Bossuet, Locke, and Cobbett, miserable men abroad, "all wake
   up in worlds finer than any they have known."

The process is presented as deterministic — "choice is not the word to
use" — until external impact restores objectivity and converts
determinism back into choice.

## The Three Patterns

| Pattern | Departure point | Fragments | What atrophies | What unfolds |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Feudal** | pre-Enlightenment Catholic/corporate Europe | Latin America, French Canada | Whiggery, Jacobinism, hence Marxism ("he needs the Jacobin as the Jacobin needs the Whig") | clerical-corporate traditionalism; Catholic syndicates; caudillo and Perón–Vargas authoritarian-labor forms; affinity for Salazar-style corporatism |
| **Liberal (bourgeois)** | 17th–18th-c. Whig/bourgeois England and Holland | United States, English Canada, Dutch South Africa | conservatism and socialism alike (no Maistre, no Marx) | petty-bourgeois democracy: Whiggery collapses (no aristocracy to fight or lean on), peasant becomes entrepreneur-farmer, proletarian becomes Gompers-style "wage-conscious" unionist |
| **Radical** | early-19th-c. British working-class radicalism (Cobbett, Chartism) | Australia (and, weaker, British South Africa) | doctrinaire 20th-c. socialism (no Webbs, no Laski — "the spirit of Harold Laski loses its source") | Labour ascendancy after the 1890s strikes; "mateship" nationalism; caucus discipline and distrust of the doctrinaire intellectual |

Australia is the limit case: radicalism is the "last" ideology, so the
radical fragment "just got in under the wire" before global integration
made fragmentation impossible.

## Nationalism as Buried Ideology

Being "merely a part, isolated from a whole" is psychologically
intolerable, so the fragment converts its ideology into a substitute
nationalism: feudalism returns as the French-Canadian *race*, liberalism
as the American Way of Life ("Americanism"), radicalism as the Australian
Legend of mateship. The conversion is done not by the migrants but by
their **children**, for whom the fragment genuinely is a nation — hence
the recurring literary "discovery" of the national essence (Whitman,
Furphy) which is really "something very old and European." The
nationalism then polices the fragment's boundaries: absorbing immigrants
("Americanization"), and raising the cry of treason against enemies who
"do not, and cannot, exist" — the Institut liberals of 1860s Quebec,
McCarthy-era Communists, Duplessis's Padlock Law. **"The nationalism does
not arise because of the enemies: in fact it arises because the enemies
do not, and cannot, exist."**

## The Racial Question

The European ideologies "do not know race," so each fragment improvises
racial formulations from its inherited ethic: Suárez "lies hidden beneath
the Latin-American encomienda, Calvin beneath the slavery of Dutch South
Africa." Feudal fragments absorb non-Europeans into hierarchy (harsh but
status-based, not property/race-based); Enlightenment fragments face an
all-or-nothing logic — during slavery total exclusion via property and
race theories (making "liberal slavery" harsher in practice than feudal),
after emancipation a compulsively egalitarian inclusion — which is why
their internal racial struggles are the most doctrinaire (Calhoun and
Garrison as rival "Americanisms"). Socialist formulations of the race
question (S. P. Bunting in South Africa) barely arise in the bourgeois
fragments at all.

## The Return of the Future

The theory's Cold-War-era payoff: the world revolution the fragments fled
has circled the globe and "hurled back at" them — "the future has caught
up with them." Fragment reaction takes forms unknown to European
conservatism: the impulse to flee again ([[isolationism]]), the treason
cry (McCarthy, Verwoerd), and a hysteria born of instinctive remedies
that no longer work ("Loyal America deports the Communist, but the
Bolshevik revolution is still there"). In feudal fragments delay makes
the encounter explosive — the innovator's impatience feeds extremism
(Castro, the Quebec Liberation Front); Marxism can now promise a single
leap across all the suppressed stages. Enlightenment fragments handle
domestic modernity within their frame but cannot *understand* a world
that "must achieve modernity through methods other than the Mayflower
voyage." Hartz predicts the fragment world's dissolution by new
generations who will "announce, outright, that the fragment is a
'fragment'" — and reads this as moral liberation: "it brings, for the
first time in the fragment settings, the hope of philosophy."

## Limitations and Critiques

- **Idealist single-factor determinism.** The theory explains societies
  almost entirely by ideological inheritance at departure; economic
  structure, geography, and institutions enter only as "complexities."
  The frontier thesis (Turner) is explicitly demoted to a symptom of
  fragment self-misunderstanding.
- **Indigenous and enslaved peoples appear only as objects** absorbed
  into or excluded from the settler ethic — the framework is about the
  European fragment, not the society as a whole. Later
  [[settler-colonialism]] scholarship (Wolfe, Veracini) inverts the
  emphasis, putting dispossession at the center; it is in effect the
  successor comparative framework, and rarely cites Hartz.
- **Empirical strain in the cases.** Specialists have contested nearly
  every national application: colonial Latin America was never simply
  "feudal"; English Canada's Tory streak is arguably more than a "touch"
  (Gad Horowitz's influential "Tory touch" amendment used Hartz's own
  framework to explain Canadian socialism); Australia's Whig/capitalist
  elements exceed the theory's allowance, as Hartz himself concedes.
- **The multiple-traditions critique** of the American case (Rogers
  Smith) applies a fortiori to the comparative version: ascriptive
  hierarchies were not derivative of the imported European ethic.
- The book's Part Two country studies were written by separate authors
  (Morse, McRae, Rosecrance, et al.) who did not fully share the
  framework — Hartz's preface concedes the essays "include diverse
  points of view."

Despite the critiques, the thesis remains the classic statement of
comparative settler-society divergence by ideological origin, and the
standard foil for later comparative work.
