# Historiography of Ancient Warfare

How Greeks, Romans, and modern scholars have written about ancient war — from pragmatic classical field guides and the literary “paradox of war,” through the German *Handbuch* tradition of battle reconstruction, to the late twentieth-century expansion into combat experience, institutions, and society. The three introductory chapters of [[sabin-van-wees-whitby-cambridge-history-greek-roman-warfare-2007|CHGRW]] Vol. I (Hanson, Hornblower, Whitby) are the wiki’s primary synthesis; later thematic chapters add methodological correctives.

## Scope

- **Emic:** ancient historians, poets, and tactical writers as both *sources for* war and *participants in* a culture that made war a central literary preoccupation — without, Hornblower insists, treating war as the “natural state of affairs.”
- **Etic:** modern analytical traditions — technical reconstruction and [[sachkritik|Sachkritik]], social/economic/cultural military history, [[face-of-battle-method|face-of-battle]] combat experience, experimental archaeology, epigraphy and papyrology.

## Ancient military literature

Classical Greek military writing began as **pragmatic field guides** — Xenophon’s *Cavalry Commander*, Aeneas Tacticus on defending fortified places — not as religious-philosophical treatises or state-censored works. By Hellenistic and Roman times the literature grew more academic (Heron and Philo on catapults; Posidonius and Asclepiodotus on the Macedonian phalanx) and antiquarian (Frontinus, Polyaenus on stratagems); Vegetius’ *Epitoma Rei Militaris* (c. AD 400) is the outstanding practical handbook for late Roman officials (Hanson, CHGRW I.1).

Ancient historiography itself was treated as a **branch of oratory**: structure and style mattered as much as factual accuracy for an author’s reputation; audiences expected truth but found literary merit easier to assess than veracity, so credibility often sufficed (Whitby, CHGRW I.3, following Wiseman and Wheeldon). Literature also shaped participants: Alexander modelled behaviour on Achilles and studied Herodotus and Xenophon; Julian read Alexander historians — reality and representation intertwine (Whitby).

## Hornblower’s paradox of war

Hornblower’s central puzzle (CHGRW I.2): Greek and Roman literature professes dislike of war yet is fascinated by it; literary prominence of war is **disproportionate** to its actual frequency and significance. In neither culture was war glorified as the natural state of affairs. Partial resolutions include: male ideology selecting formal combat as a status activity; Thucydidean narrowing of historiography after Herodotus; athletic/epinician language as displaced aggression; delight in technicality rather than blood; Roman-period transmitters’ military taste shaping survival of texts; and the vastness of lost literature (small statistical sample). Epigraphic and documentary evidence often records conflict-avoidance and settlement where literature is silent.

Quantitatively: even in Thucydides’ Peloponnesian War there were only **five major land battles** against **101 poliorcetic incidents** (Krentz, this volume), yet Delium and Mantinea occupy about one-eighth of Books 4–5 — battles dominate literary space far beyond frequency. Homeric anatomical wound detail is rarely imitated in later historiography; sack-horrors (*Iliou Persis*) *are* copied (Virgil *Aeneid* 2; Thuc. 7.75.5). Pre-battle exhortation speeches should not be dismissed generically as *topoi* (contra Hansen 1993); each on its merits — Thuc. 5.69.2 implies such speeches were a recognized real-life form (Hornblower).

## The German school and Sachkritik

The first systematic modern school was largely **German** — *Handbücher* of Delbrück, Droysen, Köchly & Rüstow, Kromayer & Veith; *RE* articles under *legio*, *phalanx*. Hanson judges them aristocratic, sometimes militaristic/nationalist, treating war as timeless tactical science or extension of politics/diplomacy with little social-economic reference (CHGRW I.1).

**Hans Delbrück** combined officer service (Franco-Prussian War), Reichstag membership, royal tutorship, and a Berlin chair. His **Sachkritik** — testing ancient operations against scientific plausibility and contemporary German army practice — often rejected Herodotus or Caesar via wooden modern analogies, drawing long English criticism. His lasting positive contribution: army size, casualties, and expenditures must be assessed within scientific, geographical, and demographic limits — “the reality of the thing.” After WWI, European armies without horses and edged weapons diverged radically from phalangites and legionaries, accelerating the decline of applied Sachkritik among classicists.

The twentieth-century “war and politics” lineage continues via **W. K. Pritchett** (Hanson’s pre-eminent Greek military historian of the century) and surveys by Adcock, Anderson, Keppie, Lazenby, Webster, and others — vocabulary, recruitment, equipment, tactics — still largely within a positivist texts–inscriptions–archaeology triad. Hanson argues theory has not yet taught how soldiers were armed, arrayed, or fought; that work will persist.

## Social expansion and face of battle

Interwar anthropology and sociology expanded *topics* (mercenaries, fortification finance, sailors’ status) before methods. The French structuralist school (Vernant, Vidal-Naquet, Ducrey, Garlan) treated armies as social institutions reflecting class tension, youth rites, or pre-state ritual — war as wide as society itself. By the 1970s the old Delbrück–Kromayer–Tarn species of military historian was nearly extinct outside Roman military archaeology; few classicists identified as pure military historians.

**John Keegan’s *The Face of Battle* (1976)** produced a school reinvestigating Greek and Roman warfare as “what it was like” for combatants — phalanxes and legions as formations of young men asked to kill and die under wretched conditions, not only tactical units or social institutions (Hanson; see [[face-of-battle-method]]). Parallel openings included frontiers and [[roman-grand-strategy|grand strategy]] (Luttwak and critics), gender, and landscape archaeology. Field imbalance remains: dozens of treatments of the hoplite push or Marathon, still no comprehensive account of Epaminondas’ Boeotian army (Hanson).

Hanson states that until CHGRW there had never been a comprehensive successor in any modern language to **Kromayer & Veith’s *Heerwesen und Kriegführung der Griechen und Römer*** — no encyclopedia combining tactical/strategic and cultural issues from the early Iron Age to the end of Rome.

## Reconstructing combat (Whitby)

Whitby’s reconstruction method (CHGRW I.3): distinguish (a) **reliable evidence**, (b) **plausible inference** from geography or comparanda, and (c) **speculation** that something “must” have happened to produce an outcome. Whatley’s WWI-derived critique applies — participants see only a small part, distort their own roles; ancient battle reality was chaotic.

Core cautions:

- **Author-participants are not automatically reliable** (Ammianus forgets Amida’s orientation, is biased for Julian; Thucydides may under-explain Amphipolis while inventing Syracuse-harbour emotion he did not witness).
- **Fallacy of military knowledge**: preferring Arrian over Diodorus at the Granicus because Ptolemy was a soldier ignores Callisthenes’ encomiastic line and Arrian’s topographical problems (Whitby).
- **Numbers**: even Thucydides may have nearly *doubled* Spartan strength at Mantinea 418 by omitting one organizational level; Sphacteria dimensions are factually wrong.
- **National and family memory** control Marathon, Thermopylae, Lade, and early Roman wars until Polybius offers external control.
- **Siege narratives** are a suspect literary zone: Thucydides’ Plataea shaped Priscus and Procopius; Diodorus recycles motifs.
- **Logistics** are poorly recorded; only Alexander’s Persian conquest has reasonably sustained information (and even that needs modern reconstruction — Engels 1978).
- **Opposing accounts** (Callinicum AD 530: Procopius vs Malalas) show that scholarly agendas shape adjudication.
- **Survival bias**: non-literary-prestige campaign narratives often failed medieval copying; only the first five of Polybius’ forty books survive complete; large fractions of Livy, Dio, and Ammianus are lost. Non-classical opponents rarely left matching literary records (exceptions: Behistun; Shapur’s *Res Gestae*; Hannibal’s Greek historians used by Polybius).

**Closing principle:** reconstructions must stay tentative and admit significant gaps (Whitby).

## Later methodological notes from CHGRW body chapters

- **Wheeler (I.7A):** Athenocentric sources skew generalizations; regional development was uneven. Demosthenes and Polybius romanticize archaic “open” warfare; Ephorus’ missile-ban treaty is golden-age invention.
- **Sabin (I.13A):** Hellenistic/mid-Republican “battle pieces” are formulaic and of highly variable reliability; Delbrück quoted Appian’s Cannae to show how one bad source could mislead entirely. Cross-link [[sabin-lost-battles-model]].
- **Goldsworthy (II.3):** no Principate foreign-war narrative as detailed as Caesar’s *BG*; frontier archaeology underdetermines function — hence divergent grand-strategy readings.
- **Gilliver (II.4):** until recently most Roman-army studies ignored the army as a *fighting force*; battle narratives stuffed with *topoi* need not be wholly unreliable (pitched battles often were formulaic in life); casualty ratios in the sources are notoriously wild (Boudicca 200:1; Pharsalus 75:1 or 30:1).
- **Rance (II.10):** late tactica’s interest in Hellenistic phalanx material answers topical needs for compact infantry, not mere antiquarianism.
- **Gabrielsen / van Wees (I.8–9):** self-conscious reversals of Weberian state-war evolutionism, “hoplite middle class,” and navy-makes-democracy orthodoxy.

## Greek vs Roman military historiography

Hanson stresses a structural divergence: Roman evidence spans a millennium and Scotland-to-Middle-East geography across many languages, making comprehensive mastery nearly impossible; Greek parameters are narrower chronologically and geographically, making speculation easier amid archaic literary gaps. The computerized *Thesaurus Linguae Graecae* reopened old controversies via vocabulary retrieval but risks returning military studies to pure philological word-gathering unless subordinated to larger arguments.

## Limitations

- CHGRW is a **thematic multi-author survey**, not a complete history of national scholarly traditions.
- Hanson’s chapter is itself a positioned overview (emphasizing face-of-battle and cultural expansion; less attentive to quantitative and epigraphic Roman military archaeology schools).
- Methodological rules of thumb do not eliminate irreducible source conflicts; the wiki records disputes on controversy pages rather than adjudicating them here.
