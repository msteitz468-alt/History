# Merovingian Epigraphy

**Some 3,500 inscriptions — Latin and runic — as an underused, independent witness to the Merovingian
world.** Mark A. Handley's chapter (Oxford Handbook, Ch. 26) surveys the epitaphs, graffiti, building
texts, and inscribed objects of Gaul c. 300–750, stressing both their evidential value and their
"devastatingly fragmented" publication. Epigraphy offers a check on the textual sources — most pointedly
where Gregory of Tours's hostile portraits can be set against a subject's own monument.

## Scale and the Problem of Publication

About **3,500 extant or recorded Christian inscriptions** survive, spanning "all of Gaul and… the entirety
of the period from the end of Roman control… through the rise of Clovis to the end of Merovingian rule."
Publication is uneven — Le Blant's nineteenth-century editions remain "indispensable but outdated," the
new *RICG* series (since 1975) covers only a few provinces — yet new finds keep appearing (48 in *L'Année
épigraphique* for 2000–2007 alone).

## Royal Epigraphy

"The Merovingians were the single most commemorated dynasty of their time" — **nine known royal epitaphs**
against one or none for most other barbarian dynasties. [[venantius-fortunatus|Venantius Fortunatus]]
composed three (a queen and two princes). Two cases are historiographically pointed:

- **Austrechild's epitaph** (Guntram's queen, d. 580) praises her as "mother of kings… the light of her
  homeland, the world, and the court," exactly inverting Gregory's hostile portrait — yet "the opportunity
  to compare and contrast Gregory's evidence with… contemporaneous evidence on the same subject has been
  universally missed" (see [[gregory-of-tours]]).
- **Childebert I's sarcophagus lid** from Saint-Germain-des-Prés (*GILDEBTUS REX FR*) is "the earliest
  original text referring to a Merovingian king as *Rex Francorum*" — predating the charter and edict
  examples usually cited (Reimitz's Edict of Guntram of 585; Goetz's pre-596 charters), but "almost
  entirely overlooked." A direct contribution to the [[frankish-ethnogenesis]] debate.

## Frankish Runes

"Little known" but real: a "growing corpus" of Frankish runic inscriptions — the Freilaubersheim fibula
("Boso wrote the runes, Daþina greeted you"), the Charnay fibula, the bilingual Chéhéry stone, the
Saint-Dizier sword pommel (*alu*), the Ichtratzheim silver spoon. Their significance is pointed: "a person
who wants to argue that the Franks were merely an evolved form of the late Roman army… has to be able to
explain the use of a Germanic language and the runic script by the Franks" — see [[ethnogenesis]] and
[[merovingian-army]].

## Distribution and Social Profile

**Trier dominates** — over 1,300 published inscriptions, "6½ times larger than the next biggest civic
corpus" (Vienne); "in the entirety of the late-antique and early medieval West, only Carthage and Rome have
more." About 70% are urban (episcopal sees), the great rural exception being Neuvicq-Montguyon. The
epigraphic population "largely exclud[es] the poor": a seventh-century Lyon epitaph cost **11 solidi**, and
workshops pre-carved formulae (the unfinished epitaph of Leonidius leaves blanks for age and date). Status
is recorded across the spectrum — 152 secular ranks (kings to slaves), 312 ecclesiastics (bishops to
catechumens) — including two manumission epitaphs from Briord freeing slaves "for the redemption of the
soul" (see [[migrants-and-minorities-in-merovingian-gaul]]).

## Inscriptions, Monasticism, and Saints' Cults

New finds rebut the assumption that continental monasteries lacked internal epigraphy: the **Hamage**
nunnery yielded "hundreds of inscribed goblets" — names, *Amen*, and the cheerful *Mitte plino* ("fill me
up") — and Luxeuil and Monte Cassino add more, confirming "that monasticism was everywhere" beyond the
textually-attested centres (see [[merovingian-monasticism]]). Some **450 religious graffiti** survive (Trier
131, Bielle 127, Minerve 95), alongside building inscriptions (Theudechild's monastery at Sens) and one
inscribed relic-list from Céleyran.
