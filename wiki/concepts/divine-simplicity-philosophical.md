## Definition and Scope

**Divine simplicity** is the philosophical thesis that God (the First Principle, the Necessary Existent) admits no internal composition of any kind — no composition of matter and form, no composition of genus and specific difference, no composition of essence and existence, and no composition of subject and attribute in any real (as opposed to purely relational or logical) sense. In the Islamic philosophical tradition, this doctrine was worked out in dialogue with Aristotle's *Metaphysics*, Neoplatonic metaphysics, and the emerging kalām tradition.

This page focuses on the arguments for divine simplicity as defended by Averroes in the *Incoherence of the Incoherence*, where he reconstructs and defends the Peripatetic position against al-Ghazali's dialectical refutations.

## The Five Kinds of Composition Denied

Averroes (summarizing and defending the philosophers) enumerates five types of composition that must be denied of the First Principle:

### 1. Quantitative Composition (Corporeity)
The First Principle cannot be a body, because a body is divisible — actually or at least imaginatively — into extended parts. Everything that is a body is in some respect composite and conditioned. The First is unconditioned. This rules out any doctrine identifying God with the heavens, the sun, or any other physical entity.

### 2. Matter/Form Composition
Bodies in the sublunary world are composed of matter (the passive, receptive principle, the ground of potentiality) and form (the active, actuating principle, the ground of specificity). Even the heavenly bodies, for Averroes (against Avicenna), are not composed of matter and form in the ordinary sense — their incorruptibility proves the absence of ordinary potentiating matter. The First Principle, as the ground of all actuality, must be pure form or pure act — no matter, no potentiality. This is also what it means for the First to be "pure intellect" (intellect being precisely the abstraction of form from matter, realized to its fullest in the First).

### 3. Genus/Specific-Difference Composition
A thing that can be defined through genus and specific difference is composite in a logical sense: the generic nature (e.g., "animality") and the differentiating nature (e.g., "rationality") are two distinct constituents. Averroes argues that this logical composition mirrors real composition: the genus is in potency relative to the specific difference, which actualizes it. Therefore, whatever can be defined in the strict Aristotelian sense is composite and — being a conjunction of potency and act — is subject to being caused and being transitory. The First Principle, as uncaused and necessary, cannot admit this kind of composition.

The key move: terms like "existent," "substance," and "intellect" are applied to God not univocally (with the same definition as when applied to creatures) but *per prius et posterius* (analogically, with God as the first and primary term and creatures as derivative). This means there is no strict genus shared between God and the separated intellects (angels), so no specific-difference composition is required to individuate the First.

> "The term 'intellect' is predicated analogically of the separate intellects according to the philosophers, and there is among them a first intellect which is the cause of all the other intellects."

### 4. Attribute/Subject Composition
The Ash'arite theologians held that God has real attributes (knowledge, power, will) that are eternal but additional to the essence. Averroes argues this is incoherent on Peripatetic grounds:

- If the attributes are themselves necessary existents, there is a plurality of necessary existents (essence plus each attribute).
- If the attributes are conditioned by the essence (the essence is their "receptive cause"), then the First is a compound — something in which a conditioning element and a conditioned element are joined — and compounds require an external cause for their composition.
- If the attributes exist in the essence without being necessary existents and without requiring an additional cause, then "attribute" and "subject" collapse into a single reality described from different perspectives — which is the Peripatetic position.

The philosophical resolution: what are called "attributes" in theological language are relational and negative descriptions of one simple essence. Knowledge, power, will, and life, when their imperfect creaturely connotations are stripped away, all reduce to the one reality of pure self-subsistent actuality that is the cause of all order.

> "When the one identical entity is viewed in so far as something else proceeds from it, it is called capable and acting, and in so far as it is viewed as differentiating between two opposite acts, it is called willing, and in so far as it is viewed as perceiving its object, knowing, and in so far as it is viewed as perceiving and as a cause of motion, it is called living."

### 5. Essence/Existence Composition
In all things other than the First, *quiddity* (what a thing is) is distinguishable from *existence* (that it is). One can know what a triangle is without knowing whether any triangles exist. Averroes (following al-Farabi against Avicenna) argues that in the First Principle, this distinction collapses: the First's quiddity *is* its existence; its necessary existence is its very nature, not an accident or addition to a prior quiddity.

Averroes rejects Avicenna's doctrine that existence is an accident additional to quiddity in all existents, including God. This doctrine generates an infinite regress: if existence is an accident, we can ask whether this accident itself exists, requiring a further existence, and so on. For Averroes, "existence" in the broad ontological sense is predicated analogically — and in the case of the simplest existents, it is identical with the quiddity.

## The Simplicity/Unity Connection

Divine simplicity is analytically connected to divine unity. A genuinely simple First Principle cannot be multiplied: individuation (producing two of the same kind) requires matter, and differentiation (producing two different kinds at the same level) requires a shared genus plus specific differences. The First has no matter and admits no univocal genus-sharing. Therefore, there can be only one First Principle.

## The Composition Problem for the Ash'arites

Averroes' most pointed argument against Ash'arite attribute-realism runs as follows. Suppose the First Principle has an essence *E* and attributes *A*₁, *A*₂ … additional to *E*. Then:

1. The *E*-*A* compound is either self-sufficient or it requires a cause for the conjunction. If self-sufficient, it is a compound that exists necessarily by itself — but compounds cannot be necessary by themselves, because the conjunction of their parts is itself a fact that requires explanation.
2. Any compound requires a "composing cause" to explain *why* these particular parts are together. The First Principle, which has no cause of any kind, cannot be a compound.
3. Furthermore: if the attributes need the essence as their "receptive cause" (substratum), then the First is something that receives attributes — i.e., it is a subject, and subjects are material or at least quasi-material. This is inconsistent with incorporeality.

The Ash'arite response — that the conjunction is eternal and requires no cause — is rejected by Averroes as illegitimate: it assimilates eternal conjunction to the case of necessary existence, but eternal conjunction between parts is not itself the same as necessity of existence. The First is necessary precisely because it has no conditions; an essence conditioned by its attributes and attributes conditioned by their substratum are not in this sense unconditional.

## Averroes' Critique of Avicenna on Simplicity

Averroes and Avicenna agree that the First is simple, but disagree on the *grounds* and *logical path* to this conclusion:

- **Existence as accident**: Avicenna treats existence as something additional to essence even in God (though in God's case the addition is necessary rather than contingent). Averroes rejects this as both philosophically false (generating an infinite regress) and theologically misleading (it makes God's being a matter of a quiddity "receiving" existence, which is a mode of composition).
- **Possible/Necessary dichotomy**: Avicenna's division of all existence into "what has a cause" (possible) and "what has no cause" (necessary) is insufficiently grounded; the genuine proof of God's simplicity proceeds through the impossibility of composition in what is purely actual.
- **Matter in heavenly bodies**: Avicenna alone attributes ordinary matter to the heavenly spheres; Averroes denies this. If the heavens have no ordinary matter, they are already a paradigm case of form-without-ordinary-matter — closer to the divine model than Avicenna allows.

## Historiography

Divine simplicity has been a contested doctrine in the Western theological tradition (debated in Christian scholasticism via the Latin Averroes) and in Islamic philosophy. The question of whether simplicity is compatible with any positive predication of attributes (omniscience, omnipotence, etc.) remains a live issue in philosophy of religion. In the Islamic context, Averroes' treatment represents the fullest Peripatetic defense; subsequent Islamic philosophy in the Eastern lands (Mulla Sadra's "gradations of being" in the 17th century) moved away from this position.
