# Imperial China

The standard historiographical periodization for the roughly 2,100-year span of centralized dynastic rule in China, conventionally bounded by the Qin unification of 221 BCE — which ended the fragmented [[concepts/hundred-schools|Warring States]] period and established the first unified Chinese empire under a single emperor (*huangdi*) — and the fall of the Qing dynasty in the 1911–1912 Xinhai Revolution, which replaced the imperial system with a republic.

Across the roughly two dozen dynasties spanning this era (Qin, Han, the periods of division, Sui, Tang, Song, Yuan, Ming, Qing, among others), the term denotes continuity in core institutional features rather than any single unbroken regime: a centralized bureaucratic administration staffed increasingly (from the Sui-Tang period onward) through the [[concepts/chinese-examination-system|imperial examination system]], the ideology of the Mandate of Heaven legitimizing (and delegitimizing) dynastic rule, and a persistent cultural and political self-conception of China as a unified civilizational-political entity even during periods of actual territorial division or foreign (Mongol Yuan, Manchu Qing) dynastic rule. Historians use "imperial China" as a convenient umbrella distinguishing this long bureaucratic-dynastic era both from the pre-imperial Bronze and early Iron Age polities that preceded it and from the modern republican and post-1949 communist periods that followed.
