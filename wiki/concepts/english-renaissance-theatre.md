# English Renaissance Theatre

The system of professional, commercial, court-sponsored, and private theatrical production in England from the opening of the first purpose-built public playhouse (The Theatre, 1576) until the closure of the theatres by Parliament in 1642. Key features:

- Open-air amphitheatres (Globe, Rose, Fortune, etc.) on the margins of the City or in liberties; indoor hall theatres (Blackfriars, Paul's, Cockpit) for more elite audiences.
- Joint-stock sharer companies (Lord Chamberlain's / King's Men the most stable and successful) owning costumes, playbooks, and shares in playhouses; hired actors and boy apprentices.
- Repertory system: large number of plays in active rotation, new plays every few weeks, heavy reliance on collaboration, revision, and recycling of old stock.
- Regulation by the Master of the Revels (licensing, censorship) and, at times, by the City of London (plague closures, anti-theatre sentiment).
- Court performances as prestige and supplementary income.
- Playwrights as professional writers working inside (or sometimes against) the companies.

Shakespeare's career is the central and best-documented example of this system at its height. The commercial and collaborative nature of the institution shaped what was written, how it was performed, and how it survived (or did not) in print.
