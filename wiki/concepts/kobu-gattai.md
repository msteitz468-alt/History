# Kōbu Gattai

**Kōbu gattai** (公武合体, "union of court and military") was the [[bakumatsu]] political program that sought to resolve Japan's crisis by creating a cooperative governing structure linking the imperial court (*kō*, the *kuge* aristocracy) and the Tokugawa shogunate (*bu*, the military government), rather than overthrowing the shogunate entirely. It was the principal alternative to the radical [[sonno-joi|sonnō jōi]] program and was explicitly favored by [[emperor-komei]] until his death in 1867.

## Definition and Scope

Kōbu gattai rested on the premise that neither the court nor the shogunate could independently address Japan's foreign crisis. The shogunate possessed military capacity and administrative infrastructure; the court possessed legitimating authority and, increasingly, political leverage over treaty ratification. Under kōbu gattai, both institutions would consult each other, the shogunate would defer to imperial wishes on major policy questions (particularly foreign affairs), and together they would strengthen Japan's defenses before eventually renegotiating the unequal treaties.

The concept is sometimes translated as "union of court and camp" or "merger of aristocracy and warrior class." Its political implication was reform of the dual-government system rather than its abolition.

## Kōmei's Version

[[Emperor-komei]] articulated kōbu gattai as the solution to Japan's crisis from at least 1858 onward, explicitly contrasting it with sonnō jōi: "He also expressed concern over unrest within the country and urged a policy of kōbu gattai, the union of aristocracy and military. This phrase summed up Kōmei's ideal of cooperation by the court and shogunate in driving out the foreigners, as opposed to the more familiar sonnō jōi, and it would figure prominently in the discourse of the late Tokugawa period" (Keene).

For Kōmei, kōbu gattai had three practical components:
1. The shogunate would consult the court before signing major international agreements
2. The shogunate would work toward abrogating the unequal treaties
3. Both institutions would jointly suppress radical (sonnō jōi) activism that threatened domestic order

The Kazunomiya marriage (1861) — the shogun Tokugawa Iemochi's marriage to Kōmei's sister — was the kōbu gattai faction's most significant political achievement, premised on the shogunate's pledge to expel foreigners within seven to ten years, securing the court's cooperation in stabilizing politics.

## Distinction from Sonnō Jōi

The critical distinction is that kōbu gattai preserved the shogunate as an institution, while sonnō jōi — as practiced by Chōshū and radical *shishi* — required the shogunate's destruction. Kōmei was "fiercely outspoken in his opposition to those whose 'respect for the emperor' involved the overthrow of the shogunate." He genuinely valued the Tokugawa political system as a source of stability and of his own material support (the shogunate provided the court's annual stipends).

The sonnō jōi faction, when ascendant at the court in 1862–1863, used the kōbu gattai framework rhetorically while pursuing its own agenda — a tension that eventually produced the Coup of the Eighth Month (September 1863), in which the kōbu gattai faction, supported by Satsuma and Aizu domain forces, expelled sonnō jōi radicals from Kyōto.

## Fate of the Program

Kōbu gattai as a governing program collapsed with the [[meiji-restoration-1868]]. The shogunate's failure to abrogate the unequal treaties, its military weakness (demonstrated by the catastrophic failure of the Second Chōshū Expedition, 1865–1866), and the death of [[emperor-komei]] in early 1867 removed its institutional foundations. Without Kōmei's personal commitment to the shogunate's legitimacy, the court's new advisers (many from Satsuma and Chōshū) were free to use imperial authority against the shogunate rather than in cooperation with it.

Ironically, some structural elements of kōbu gattai survived into the Meiji period: the new government combined court legitimacy (imperial sovereignty) with a military-administrative apparatus descended from samurai institutions — a synthesis that bore family resemblance to what kōbu gattai moderates had envisioned, though in a form the shogunate could not have accepted.
