## Definition and Scope

The legal specialists of the formative period (*fuqahāʾ*; sing. *faqīh*) were a class of private, religiously motivated scholars who made the study, articulation, and teaching of Islamic law their primary concern in the roughly 700–770 CE generation. They are distinguished from the qāḍīs (judges) in several ways: they held no state office as such; their authority derived from learning rather than appointment; their motivation was explicitly pious rather than administrative; and their legal knowledge was technical and systematic in a way that the typical judge's was not.

Hallaq's formulation is precise: "The locus of legal expertise, therefore, was not the qādis, but rather a group of private individuals whose motivation to engage in the study of law was largely a matter of piety ... they were driven above all by a profoundly religious commitment which demanded of them, among other things, the articulation of a law that would deal with all the problems of society."

This category is etic but maps reasonably onto the *fuqahāʾ*/*ahl al-ʿilm* self-designation the sources use. It should not be confused with the later fully institutionalized *muftī* office, which was a later crystallization of a role these figures performed informally.

## Historical Context of Emergence

The legal specialists emerged from the generation that flourished "between 80 and 120 H (roughly between 700 and 740 AD)." Their activity grew from and through the **mosque circle** (*ḥalaqa*; pl. *ḥalaqāt*): regular gatherings in mosque courtyards where scholars engaged in discussion, debate, and instruction. Some *ḥalaqāt* were devoted to Quranic exegesis, some to Prophetic narrative, and some were "of an exclusively legal nature."

Prior to this generation, Islamic law was elaborated chiefly through caliphal ordinance, Companion precedent (*sunan*), and tribal custom. What was new about 700–740 CE was:

1. The emergence of **personal, systematic study** of religious texts as a discipline
2. The **textualization** of law — working with law as a textual activity, not merely as transmitted practice
3. The **epistemic claim** that knowledge of the law (rather than political office) was the proper foundation of legal authority

This last development had profound long-term consequences: "those men in possession of a greater store of knowledge grew more influential than others less learned, gaining in the process — by the sheer virtuousness of their knowledge — an authority that began to challenge the legal (but not political) authority of the caliphs."

## Geographic Distribution

A statistical survey of an early biographical source (*ṭabaqāt* literature) covering roughly 80–120 H identified 84 leading legal specialists across the major centers of the early Islamic world:

| Center | Number | Percentage |
|---|---|---|
| Medina | 22 | 26.2% |
| Kufa | 20 | 23.8% |
| Basra | 17 | 20.2% |
| Syria (Damascus) | 9 | 10.7% |
| Mecca | 7 | 8.3% |
| Yemen | 5 | 6.0% |
| Egypt (Fustat) | 3 | 3.5% |
| Khurasan | 1 | 1.2% |

The Hejaz (Medina + Mecca) and Iraq (Kufa + Basra) together produced "close to 70 percent of the entire body of legal scholarship." This geographical pattern reflects the concentration of Arab and Arabicized populations in these regions. Syria occupied a secondary position; Egypt and Khurasan were marginal at this early stage.

## Named Specialists and Areas of Expertise

The sources associate particular legal specialists with particular competencies, demonstrating an early functional differentiation within the scholar class:

**Medina**: Qāsim b. Muḥammad, Sulaymān b. Yasār (both d. ca. 110/728), Saʿīd b. al-Musayyab, Khārija b. Zayd (d. 99/717, expert in inheritance law and notarial documents — "he developed proficient knowledge in 'writing documents for people'"), Rabīʿat al-Raʾy (d. 136/753, held a circle of ~40 students), ʿUrwa b. al-Zubayr (d. 94/712)

**Mecca**: ʿAṭāʾ b. Abī Rabāḥ (d. 105/723, outstanding competence in ritual law, issued reliable fatwās), Mujāhid b. Jabr, ʿAmr b. Dīnār (d. 126/743)

**Kufa**: Saʿīd b. Jubayr (d. 95/713), ʿĀmir al-Shaʿbī (d. 110/728, enormous *ḥalaqa*, exceptional command of legal precedent and *sunan māḍiya*), Ibrāhīm al-Nakhaʿī (d. 96/714), Ḥammād b. Abī Sulaymān (d. 120/737)

**Basra**: Muḥammad b. Sīrīn (d. 110/728), Muslim b. Yasār, Qatāda b. Diʿāma al-Sadūsī, Abū Ayyūb al-Sakhtiyānī (d. 131/748)

**Syria**: Makhūl (d. 113–118/731–736)

**Yemen**: Ṭāwūs (d. 106/724)

The Medinese scholar Shihāb al-Dīn Zuhrī (d. 124/742) occupies a special position: commissioned by the caliph ʿUmar II to collect Prophetic *ḥadīth*, and associated with early abrogation (*naskh*) theory, he bridges the scholarly and caliphal spheres. Statistical analysis of his transmitted doctrine shows that "the majority of references go back to the Companions, not to the Prophet" — evidence for the still-transitional status of Prophetic *ḥadīth* at this stage.

## Areas of Legal Activity

**Quranic hermeneutics and abrogation (*naskh*)**: The specialists tackled internal Quranic contradictions by developing chronological sequencing of revelation — which verses superseded which. This was "the rudimentary beginnings of the theory of abrogation (*naskh*), a theory that later stood at the center of legal hermeneutics."

***Ḥadīth* transmission and collection**: Many specialists transmitted Prophetic reports, though at this stage *ḥadīth* was not yet synonymous with Prophetic Sunna nor a clearly dominant legal source. Fabrication was widespread; even distinguished scholars like Qatāda b. Diʿāma and Ḥasan al-Baṣrī are cited in technical *ḥadīth* criticism as having attributed inauthentic material to the Prophet — "it must be stressed, however, that notwithstanding these failings, the very same scholars are depicted in the sources as pious men."

***Raʾy* (discretionary reasoning)**: The dominant mode of legal reasoning in this generation. Before the rise of *ḥadīth* as a privileged source, *raʾy* carried a positive valence — "sound and considered opinion." About two-thirds of Zuhrī's transmitted doctrine consisted of *raʾy*; Qatāda's was 62 percent *raʾy*. The later derogatory connotation of *raʾy* as "arbitrary reasoning" was a product of the traditionalist-rationalist conflict of the mid-second/eighth century onward.

**Fatwā issuance**: Legal specialists issued opinions (*fatwās*) on questions of ritual, commercial, and family law. These opinions circulated informally; they were not yet institutionally regulated. The specialist's epistemic authority alone gave the fatwā its force.

## Relationship to the Judiciary

The emerging doctrine from ca. 715 CE was that judges should — and increasingly did — consult the legal specialists on difficult cases. This consultation was normative (customary) in the east, never formally mandated; in Andalusia it became obligatory (*mushāwars*). The specialists were "a fixture of the court even when they were not physically present in it. When they were not attending hearings, it was common practice for judges to write to them asking their opinion."

Although judges were "not legally bound by the expert opinions of these jurists, in reality they conformed to them nearly always." This de facto authority without formal legal power is what Hallaq elsewhere calls "epistemic authority" — authority rooted in recognized knowledge rather than office.

The specialists and the judiciary were not entirely distinct circles: some specialists served as judges, and the court scribe apprenticeship was a recognized pathway into legal scholarship. But the two roles were analytically distinct: the judge was a state functionary; the legal specialist was a private scholar.

## The Traditionalist-Rationalist Divide

By ca. 120/740 CE, a binary tension was emerging within the specialist class between:

- **Proto-traditionalists** (*ahl al-ḥadīth*): inclined to ground legal positions in Prophetic and Companion reports, even if not yet insisting on *ḥadīth* exclusively
- **Rationalists** (*ahl al-raʾy*): reasoning primarily from considered opinion (*raʾy*) and established local practice, without grounding in textual *ḥadīth*

The "methodological awareness of the traditionalists as defined... was a development belonging to the second half of the second century H, and cannot be said to have crystallized any earlier." In the generation under discussion, only "the vaguest beginnings" of this conflict are discernible. The sharper the conflict became, the more negative the connotation of *raʾy* became — transformed from "discretionary reasoning" to "arbitrary opinion."

## Contribution to Legal Epistemology

The legal specialists' most lasting contribution was epistemic: they established the principle that "legal knowledge as an epistemic quality was to be the final arbiter in law-making." This principle displaced both caliphal authority and tribal seniority as the basis for legal rulings. By making piety-knowledge (rather than office or lineage) the source of authority, they created the foundation for:

- The doctrine that a judge must be (or consult) a legal expert
- The later *madhhab* system, in which legal schools were organized around recognized scholarly authority
- The transformation of caliphal law into mere sunna — and its eventual replacement by juristic doctrine as the authoritative source

The generation of 700–740 CE thus marks what Hallaq calls "the first major development in the production of permanent forms that were to survive into, and contribute to, the further formation of later Islamic law."

## Historiography

The principal sources are biographical dictionaries (*ṭabaqāt*) of jurists, primarily al-Shīrāzī's *Ṭabaqāt al-Fuqahāʾ* and Ibn Ḥibbān's *Thiqāt* and *Mashāhīr*. These works were composed one to three centuries after the subjects they describe and carry the standard risks of retrospective idealization, anachronistic projection of later school categories, and selective memory. Modern scholarship (Motzki, Goldziher, Schacht, Hallaq) has debated the authenticity of *ḥadīth* material transmitted by figures in this generation, with the consensus now leaning toward a more nuanced position than Schacht's strong skepticism — recognizing authentic early material while acknowledging substantial later accretion.
