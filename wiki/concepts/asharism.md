## Definition and Scope

**Ash'arism** (Arabic: *al-Ash'ariyya*) denotes the school of Islamic speculative theology ([[kalam]]) founded by Abu-l-Hasan al-Ash'ari (d. 935 CE) and his followers, which became — alongside [[mutazilism|Maturidism]] — one of the two dominant schools of Sunni kalam from the tenth century onward, eventually achieving near-orthodoxy status in much of the Sunni world.

Ash'arism is not a simple rejection of rational theology but a particular *use* of rational argument: it employs the vocabulary and methods of kalam to defend positions aligned with the Hadith folk (*Ahl al-Hadith*) against Mu'tazili rationalism and against the cosmological system of [[islamic-philosophy|Falsafah]]. Its central intellectual move is to use philosophy's own tools to undercut the Philosophic claim that the world is governed by inherent natural laws and enduring rational essences.

**Emic usage**: Ash'aris identified themselves as defenders of the Sunna and as members of *Ahl al-Sunna wa-l-Jama'a* (the People of the Prophetic Practice and the Community).

**Etic usage**: Modern scholars use Ash'arism to designate the school and its characteristic doctrines; the relationship between al-Ash'ari's own historical positions and the subsequent school named after him remains contested, since many works attributed to him may be later.

## Founding Context

Al-Ash'ari had initially been a student of the leading Mu'tazili scholar al-Jubba'i (d. 915). His dramatic break with Mu'tazilism — reportedly announced publicly in a mosque — was followed by the construction of a new kalam system that accepted the Hadith folk's substantive positions while defending them through systematic rational argument rather than mere appeal to authority.

His exact original positions are uncertain: "what positions were actually adopted by al-Ash'ari is not yet clear; as eponym of a school of thought, he has been ascribed all the basic positions taken later by the school; and not all the works bearing his name are genuine" (*Venture of Islam*, Vol. 1, p. 2069–72). Nevertheless, certain key formulations can plausibly be traced to him.

## Core Doctrines

### On Divine Attributes
The [[mutazilism|Mu'tazilis]] had denied that God possessed distinct attributes (knowledge, power) lest these be co-eternal with God and thus violate absolute divine unity. Al-Ash'ari "seems to have insisted that God did have attributes, and to have met the Mu'tazilis' point by saying, neatly, that they were not other than His essence" (*ibid.*, p. 2085). This preserved the full effectiveness of a God who knows, wills, and acts, without conceding a plurality of co-eternal realities. The logical categories inherited from Greek philosophy were retained but prevented from undermining the effectiveness of divine intervention in history.

### On Faith (*Iman*)
Against the Mu'tazili intellectualisation of faith as sheer propositional assent (*tasdiq*), al-Ash'ari incorporated legal definitions that made faith include assent, verbal affirmation, and action — "though the assent was primary" (*ibid.*, p. 2113). This preserved the human quality of the Qur'anic texts without sacrificing the need for systematic doctrinal definition.

### On Human Freedom and Divine Power: The *Kasb* Doctrine
The most philosophically elaborate Ash'ari contribution was the doctrine of **acquisition** (*kasb* or *iktisab*): God alone creates all actions directly and immediately, but superadds to some human actions a special quality of voluntary acquisition by which, by God's will, the individual becomes a voluntary agent and morally responsible. This allowed the Ash'aris to affirm God's absolute omnipotence (nothing happens except by God's direct will) while maintaining human moral responsibility (God creates a quality of "acquiring" the act in the human agent). Critics found this doctrine obscure to the point of incoherence; defenders argued it was the most honest description of the actual moral situation.

The problem was illustrated through al-Ash'ari's famous question to his Mu'tazili teacher about the three brothers: one in Paradise, one with a lower post who died young, one in Hell — with God having allowed the middle brother to die young to prevent future sin but not having done the same for the third. No system of merit and free will could resolve this. The Ash'ari response was to say that good and evil, and the laws of logic themselves, are what God decrees them to be: "it is presumptuous for human beings to attempt to judge God (or to justify Him!) on the basis of categories God has laid down only for human beings" (*ibid.*, p. 2232–34).

### Atomist Cosmology: The Philosophical Anti-Philosophy
The Ash'ari school's most radical and philosophically sophisticated contribution was an atomist cosmology constructed specifically to refute the Philosophic world view. Against the Faylasufs' world of enduring substances with essential natures and causal powers, the Ash'aris asserted:

- The world is composed of **atomic points** (*jawahir farda*), each at a given moment in space and time.
- There is **no inherent continuity** between atomic points — no enduring substances, no essential natures, no natural laws.
- The only continuity is the **will of God**, who creates every atomic point anew at every moment.
- If an object appears to endure, it is that "God creates at every moment a new set of atomic points corresponding to what was there" (*ibid.*, p. 2255–57).
- "Certain sequences and combinations of atomic points answer to God's customary ways; but none is necessary" (*ibid.*).

This had several consequences:
- **No such thing as miracle**: Every event is equally an act of divine will; miracles are merely breaches in God's customary patterns, not violations of necessary laws. But for that very reason, miracles cannot be proved to establish prophetic credentials on their own — only the quality of the prophetic message can establish prophecy.
- **No natural causation**: Fire does not burn cotton by inherent causal power; God creates the burning whenever He pleases, in conjunction with fire's presence. This became the target of Averroes' (Ibn-Rushd's) most devastating critique.
- **The primacy of historical knowledge**: "The only dependable knowledge was historical knowledge — and knowledge of the truth revealed in historical moments" (*ibid.*, p. 2274). Generalisation is hazardous; universal essences are epistemologically unavailable. This was the direct inverse of the Philosophic claim that only universal knowledge is true knowledge.

Hodgson characterises this as "militantly (and, one might add, philosophically) anti-Philosophical" (*ibid.*, p. 2278) — a philosophical system that used philosophy to destroy the Philosophic claim to universal knowledge.

## The Ash'ari vs. Faylasuf World Views

Hodgson provides an extended contrast between the Philosophic and Ash'ari world views that illuminates both:

| Dimension | Faylasufs | Ash'aris |
|---|---|---|
| Ontology | Enduring substances with essential natures | Atomic points recreated each moment by God |
| Causation | Inherent natural laws | God's customary but contingent patterns |
| God | Necessary being, timeless First Cause | Omnipotent sovereign who creates by arbitrary will |
| Knowledge | Universal essences, timeless demonstrable truth | Historical particulars, revealed event-knowledge |
| History | Exemplification of timeless types; nothing inherently new | All-important drama of divine-human encounter |
| Human dignity | The wise and philosophical elite | The average believer whose acts conform to Muhammad's community |
| Salvation | Intellectual purification, release into the realm of the changeless | Resurrection and divine judgment of individual believers |

## Social and Institutional Position

The Ash'ari school was associated with the **Shafi'i legal school** (while the rival Maturidi school was associated with the Hanafis). By the latter tenth century several schools of kalam "were actively combating each other, sometimes violently" (*ibid.*, p. 2116). The Ash'aris went furthest toward strict adherence to Hadith folk positions while developing "a highly sophisticated interpretation of cosmology which moved as far as logically possible from the positions of Falsafah" (*ibid.*, p. 2122–23).

Kalam of every school remained suspect in the eyes of many ulama, who rejected all speculative theology as bid'a. The Ash'ari school's claim to be defending Sunni orthodoxy never fully silenced this opposition within the High Caliphal period and beyond.

## Legacy

Ash'arism became the dominant form of kalam in most of the Sunni world from the eleventh century onward, especially after the patronage of the Seljuk Sultanate and the institutional embedding of Ash'ari theology in the newly founded madrasa system (including the Nizamiyya madrasas founded by the vizier Nizam al-Mulk from 1067). Al-Ghazali (d. 1111), the most influential Islamic theologian of the High Medieval period, worked largely within the Ash'ari framework while also incorporating Sufism — a synthesis that profoundly shaped subsequent Sunni identity.

## Historiography

The Ash'ari school's relationship to its eponym, and the exact development of its characteristic doctrines, has been debated since Richard Frank's and Josef van Ess's systematic work on early kalam. Hodgson notes the textual problem: many works attributed to al-Ash'ari are of uncertain authenticity, and the school's later development attributed to him positions that may have been later elaborations. The atomist cosmology in particular — which has attracted the most philosophical attention — may have been systematised by later Ash'aris rather than fully worked out by al-Ash'ari himself.
