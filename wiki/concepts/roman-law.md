# Roman Law

The body of law developed in Rome from its earliest recorded codification, the [[twelve-tables-450bce|Twelve Tables]] (c. 450 BCE), through classical jurisprudence under the Principate, to its final systematic compilation under Justinian in the sixth century CE. It is conventionally divided into the early jus civile (citizen law, rooted in custom and the Twelve Tables), the later jus gentium (law applied to relations involving non-citizens, developed by praetors administering an increasingly cosmopolitan empire), and the mature classical jurisprudence of professional jurists (Gaius, Papinian, Ulpian) writing under the early Empire.

## Development and Legacy

Roman law evolved through the practical rulings (edicts) of magistrates, especially the praetor urbanus and praetor peregrinus, and through the accumulated written opinions (responsa) of jurists whose interpretations gradually acquired quasi-legislative authority. This organically developed body of law and commentary was ultimately gathered, systematized, and in places rewritten by the emperor Justinian's legal commission under [[tribonian|Tribonian]] in the [[codification-of-roman-law|Corpus Juris Civilis]] (529–534 CE), which preserved classical Roman legal reasoning for transmission to medieval and modern Europe, forming the foundation of the later civil-law tradition dominant across continental Europe and much of the world.
