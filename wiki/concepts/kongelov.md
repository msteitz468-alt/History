## Definition and Distinctiveness

The Kongelov (Lex Regia) of 1665 was the written constitutional document that formalised absolute monarchy in Denmark-Norway. It is unique in European history as "an actual constitutional basis for absolutism and for succession" (L15902–15903) — no other absolutist state in early modern Europe committed its absolutism to a written fundamental law of this kind. Where French or Swedish absolutism rested on declarations, ordinances, or reinterpretations of existing law, Denmark produced a constitutional text.

The document declared the Danish king to be "a free, most highly authorised Absolute Hereditary King, so that everything that can best be said or written about an absolute, sovereign Christian Hereditary King shall also apply and be understood in the best and most favourable sense concerning Denmark and Norway's Absolute Hereditary King" (L15904–15908, quoting Lex Regia art. 26, printed in Himmelstrup and Møller, Danske forfatningslove 1665–1953, p. 22).

The Lex Regia was kept secret for a long time after its promulgation — "It was unique that Danish absolutism was based on a constitution. Danish absolutism soon assumed theocratic traits" (L16823–16826). The document also set out "punishment to be inflicted on anyone seeking to limit royal power" (L16903–16905), making explicit the suppression of resistance that the coup of 1660 had enforced by intimidation.

## Emic/Etic Distinction

Emic: The Kongelov was understood by its authors and by contemporaries within the idiom of natural law and contract theory. The Danish crown "strongly emphasised that the abolition of the coronation charter and the transfer of power had taken place with the consent of the estates" (L16814–16816). Theologically, the king was also presented as acting under divine guidance: the estates "had also indirectly been guided by God" (L16817). Denmark and Norway thus "viewed absolute monarchy as a transfer of power (translatio) from the estates of the realms based on natural law" (L16809–16810), as distinct from the Swedish theocratic framing.

Etic: Historians treat the Lex Regia as the legal crystallisation of a political coup. The text's emphasis on consent was a post-hoc rationalisation: "the king carefully ensured that the abolition of the old coronation charter as well as the introduction of the new principles of government were carried out according to the formalities which were applied by the contract doctrine" (L16820–16822), masking what the same source calls "a coup d'état" (L16900–16901). The natural-law framing was quickly overlain by theocratic justifications: "Danish absolutism soon assumed theocratic traits, and in the testament of the later absolute monarch, Christian V, the earlier power of the rigsråd was presented as usurped" (L16836–16838), bringing Danish and Swedish absolutist theory into convergence by the end of the seventeenth century.

## Context: The Path to the Kongelov

The Kongelov was the culmination of a sequence: (1) the Karl X Gustav Wars of 1657–60 drove Denmark to military defeat and forced a meeting of estates to resolve the peace; (2) at the 1660 estates meeting, Frederik III exploited the conflict between the nobility (defending their tax exemption) and the lower estates to convert Denmark to hereditary monarchy; (3) early 1661 saw the 'Act concerning hereditary autocratic government' circulated for signature under armed coercion; (4) only in 1665 was the theoretical basis formalised in the Lex Regia. The gap of four years between the political act (1661) and its constitutional text (1665) reflects the crown's care to present absolutism as a settled and legitimated order.

## Scope

The Kongelov applied to Denmark and Norway as a dual monarchy. Its logic was explicitly extended: "apart from the Acts of Sovereignty for Norway, Iceland and the Føroyar" issued alongside the 1661 declaration (L15927–15928), all constituent territories of the Oldenburg monarchy came under the same absolute frame. The 1665 text explicitly names "Denmark and Norway's Absolute Hereditary King" (L15907–15908).

## Limitations and Critiques

1. The Kongelov placed few formal restrictions on royal power, which made it less a constitution in the limiting sense than a legitimating instrument.
2. Its secrecy for years after promulgation meant it functioned more as internal state document than public constitutional law.
3. The natural-law framing was ideologically unstable: within a generation Danish absolutism had adopted the theocratic language it initially rejected, eroding the distinction from Swedish absolutism.
4. In practice, the absolutist state was incapable of administering without the manor-house networks of the old nobility; "the new regime was obliged to make use of the administrative apparatus and the local expertise which were to be found in the manor-houses of the nobility" (L17333–17335), suggesting formal absolutism coexisted with considerable practical devolution.
