## Definition and Scope

Fanāʾ (annihilation, passing away, "un-becoming") and baqāʾ (subsistence, persistence, duration in God) are the terminal concepts of the Sufi path — the goal toward which all stations and states have been preparatory. Together they describe the dissolution of the individual ego-consciousness in the divine unity and the subsequent mode of existence that follows.

The word fanāʾ defies easy translation. Schimmel notes that the German term Entwerden — the "un-becoming" of Meister Eckhart — is closer than "annihilation" or "being naughted," since it is the opposite of "becoming" (werden). It is not the Buddhist or Hindu concept of nirvana: "Islam lacks the idea of karma and accepts the reality of the individual soul. Fanāʾ is, in the beginning, an ethical concept." It must also be distinguished from ittiḥād ("union"), which presupposes two independent beings and was rejected as heretical, and from ḥulūl ("indwelling" or incarnation of the divine in man), which was the accusation leveled at Ḥallāj.

Toshihiko Izutsu's interpretation (cited by Schimmel as the best available) defines fanāʾ as "the total nullification of the ego-consciousness, when there remains only the absolute Unity of Reality in its purity as an absolute Awareness prior to its bifurcation into subject and object" — the state the Sufis call jamʿ ("unification/collectedness"). The Sufi experiences the return to the moment when God was, and there was nothing else. Crucially, "Fanāʾ is certainly a human experience, but man is not the subject of this experience. The subject is rather the metaphysical Reality itself."

## Three Stages of Fanāʾ

The tradition distinguishes three nested stages:

1. **Ethical annihilation** — the exchange of one's own base qualities for the praiseworthy qualities (akhlāq Allāh) by which God has described Himself in the Quran. The alleged Prophetic tradition takhallaqū bi-akhlāq Allāh ("qualify yourself with the qualities of God") provides the scriptural anchor.

2. **Annihilation in vision** — the soul is surrounded by the primordial light of God; all objects become transparent, seen in their divine ground.

3. **Annihilation from one's vision of annihilation** — immersion in the wujūd ("existence" / "finding") of God. The word wujūd originally means "being found," and this is what the mystic experiences: not that he has found God, but that God is found in him, as the sole remaining subject.

Ḥallāj (d. 922) used the allegory of the moth and the candle to map all three onto the epistemology of certainty: ʿilm al-yaqīn ("knowledge of certitude") when the moth sees the candle; ʿayn al-yaqīn ("vision/essence of certainty") when it draws near and feels the heat; ḥaqq al-yaqīn ("reality of certainty," the gnostic's station, fulfilled in fanāʾ) when it is finally consumed.

## Baqāʾ: The Second Separation

Baqāʾ is not simply the aftermath of fanāʾ; it is a qualitatively new mode of being. Izutsu's formulation: "Man is resuscitated out of the nothingness, completely transformed into an absolute Self. The multiplicity becomes visible again — but in a changed form, namely as determinations of the one Reality." In baqāʾ the mystic acts completely through God.

This is the "second separation" (al-farq al-thānī) or "gathering of the gathering" (jamʿ al-jamʿ). The classic Prophetic reference is Sūra 8:17, addressed to Muhammad after the Battle of Badr: "Mā ramayta idh ramayta — you did not cast when you cast, but God did it." This verse became the canonical scriptural locus for baqāʾ: the prophet acting, but as a transparent instrument of divine will.

Most Sufis linked baqāʾ with prophetic activity in the world. This distinction between the "saintly" and "prophetic" aspects of fanāʾ and baqāʾ gained importance in later theory, especially among the Naqshbandiyya.

Sanāʾī (d. c. 1131) makes the connection with the dervish community explicit: "When you seek baqāʾ, request it from the dervishes — the dervishes are the warp and woof of the garment of baqāʾ" (Ḥadīqat al-ḥaqīqa, SD 186).

## Fanāʾ, Faqr, and Poverty

The tradition drew a close parallel between fanāʾ and faqr (poverty). Rūmī identifies them explicitly (Mathnawī 5:672). For ʿAṭṭār (d. c. 1221), poverty and annihilation constitute the seventh and final valley on the path to God in the Manṭiq al-ṭayr, after search, love, gnosis, independence, tawḥīd, and bewilderment.

The phrase that became standard in later Sufism encapsulates this identification: al-faqr idhā tamma huwa Allāh — "when faqr becomes perfect (complete), it is God." The heart annihilated in absolute poverty lives in the eternal richness of God; absolute poverty becomes absolute richness. This formula spread through the Qādiriyya and Naqshbandiyya orders as far as Malaysia.

Hujwīrī's formulation of true poverty verges on fanāʾ:

> "The dervish is a place over which something is passing, not a wayfarer following his own will." (Kashf al-Maḥjūb, H 29)

## Paradoxical Locutions: The Shaṭḥiyāt

In the state of fanāʾ, mystics sometimes uttered expressions incompatible with orthodox formulations, called shaṭḥiyāt (singular: shaṭḥ) — "theopathic locutions." The most famous are:

- **Ḥallāj**: Anā ʾl-Ḥaqq — "I am the Truth/God" (al-Ḥaqq being one of the names of God)
- **Bāyezīd Bisṭāmī**: "Glory be to Me! How great is My Majesty!"

Rūzbihān Baqlī (d. 1209) devoted an entire book to collecting and interpreting these paradoxes. His approach — existential and authentic rather than dismissive — allows insight into the depth of mystical experience that human language cannot adequately express.

Rūmī's explanation of Ḥallāj's anā ʾl-Ḥaqq uses the image of iron thrown into a furnace: it "becomes so hot that it regards itself as fire" (Mathnawī 2:1445), though a substantial union cannot be achieved. The iron does not become fire; it becomes so thoroughly pervaded by fire that, from its own perspective, the distinction has disappeared.

Rūmī also cites Kharaqānī's saying — "there is no dervish in the world, and if there be a dervish, that is nonexistent" — and provides the most concise clarification: "Like the flame of the candle in the presence of the sun, he is (really) non-existent, (though he is) existent in formal calculation" (Mathnawī 3:3669–73).

## The Black Light

Later mystics elaborated the phenomenology of fanāʾ through the image of the Black Light: when divine light fully appears in the mystic's consciousness, all things disappear rather than becoming visible. This apparent blackout is not darkness but the light of the Absolute-as-such, which is itself invisible and appears as nothing. "To discover the clarity of this black light is to find the green water of life, which, according to the legends, is hidden in the deepest darkness — baqāʾ, persistence in God, is concealed in the very center of fanāʾ."

The image is related to the Arabic and Persian mystical term for the divine essence as "Black Light" (nūr aswad), found in Rūzbihān Baqlī's Sharḥ-i Shaṭḥiyyāt and in the Nūrbakhshī school in Iran.

## Fanāʾ and the Soul: The Primordial Covenant

The tradition connects fanāʾ to a return to the ontological condition of the Day of the Primordial Covenant (the alast), when God addressed the pre-eternal souls: "Am I not your Lord?" (alastu bi-rabbikum; Sūra 7:172). Junayd describes the goal as returning to the state "before he existed" — recovering the condition that obtained before individual existence introduced a veil of createdness:

> "Unification is this: that one should be as he was before he existed." (Kashf al-Maḥjūb, H 282–83)

The mystic cannot completely and substantially be annihilated in God during earthly life — the veil of createdness cannot be lifted entirely. Fanāʾ is thus a partial, temporary, or asymptotic experience, as Kalābādhī (d. 990/995) describes: "Fanāʾ may consist of being absent from his own attributes, so that he appears to be really mad... yet he is preserved to perform his duties to God" (K 131).

## Fanāʾ and Tawḥīd

Fanāʾ is the experiential consummation of tawḥīd. Ghazzālī's formulation in the Iḥyāʾ defines the muwaḥḥid (true confessor of unity): "Whoever looks at the world because it is God's work... does not look save to God... and of such a person it is said that he is annihilated in unification and that he is annihilated from himself" (G 4:276).

True tawḥīd, then, means to forget tawḥīd — a state "in which the sharp-sighted are blinded and men of reason are confused" (L 33). The all-pervading divine presence, witnessed by the muwaḥḥid, finds its ultimate expression in the saying attributed variously to early Sufis: "I never saw anything without seeing God therein" — and in Shiblī's more extreme version: "I never saw anything except God."

## Distinctions from Heresy

The tradition carefully demarcated fanāʾ from two rejected positions:

- **Ittiḥād** (union): presupposes two independently existing beings that merge; rejected because it posits the independent prior reality of the human self.
- **Ḥulūl** (indwelling/incarnation): the divine descending into and inhabiting the human; the accusation leveled at Ḥallāj by orthodox critics and at Christian Christology by Muslim theologians.

Fanāʾ avoids both: the mystic is not united with God (which would require two parties) nor does God dwell in him (which would make God dependent on a body). Rather, the self-as-illusion dissolves into the only real existence, which was always and only divine.

## Historiography

Fanāʾ and baqāʾ are among the most studied concepts in Islamic mysticism. Key primary sources: Junayd's letters, Kalābādhī's Taʿarruf, Hujwīrī's Kashf al-Maḥjūb, Qushayrī's Risāla, Rūzbihān Baqlī's Sharḥ-i Shaṭḥiyyāt. Key modern scholarly works include Massignon's study of Ḥallāj (which coined the phrase "le désir essentiel" for the divine love-dynamic), Izutsu's philosophical analyses, and Schimmel's synthesis (*Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, chapter 3).

The comparison with nirvana (rejected by Schimmel and most Islamic studies specialists) reflects an older, comparative-religion approach that did not adequately account for Islamic monotheism's insistence on the irreducibility of divine-human difference. The ḥulūl accusation against Ḥallāj remains contested: Massignon's defense argued his language was that of a lover, not a doctrinal claim of divine incarnation.
