# The Late Roman Economy and the Question of Fragmentation

The economy of the [[third-century-crisis|third-century empire]] and the long-debated question:
**did the economic unity of the Roman world break apart in the crisis?** Treated in
[[bowman-cah-v12-2005|CAH XII]] Ch. 12 (**Mireille Corbier**, "Did the economic unity of the empire
become fragmented?"), the analytical capstone of the volume's economic chapters. Corbier's answer is
deliberately **anti-catastrophist and methodologically pluralist**: the empire was never a single
economy but a layering of economies at **different scales**, and "fragmentation" is the wrong lens.

## Scales of analysis, not one economy

Corbier's organizing argument: the imperial economy must be read at **several scales at once** —
the **official/monetary economy** of the state (taxation, the army's annona, the mints) is only the
**"upper level,"** above a vast **rural society of largely self-sufficient local economies** knit
together by a **network of cities**. The empire-wide "Roman economy" is therefore **"only one of
several possible scales"** of analysis — and not the one in which most economic life was actually
lived. The old **general theories** (Weber's ancient city, Polanyi's substantivism, Finley's
status-bound non-developmental economy, Braudel's *longue durée*) are useful provocations but
**cannot serve as a single explanatory key**; the evidence demands disaggregation.

## Did unity fragment? — the shifting weight of regions

The "fragmentation" thesis points to the political splintering (the [[gallic-empire|Gallic]] and
[[palmyra|Palmyrene]] secessions), the regionalization of the coinage and mints, and the rise of new
regional **capitals** (Trier, Milan, Sirmium, Antioch). Corbier concedes a real **shift in the
economic weight of regions** — the relative rise of the provinces (especially **Africa** and the
**East**) and the long-debated relative decline of **Italy** — and concedes that the tetrarchic
multiplication of imperial residences redistributed demand. But she insists these were **gradual
realignments, not a shattering of an integrated market**:

- The **cities do not testify to decline** — they persist as the framework of economic and fiscal
  life (the argument of [[late-roman-cities|Carrié's chapter]]); urban continuity is the strongest
  evidence against collapse.
- Regional specialization and inter-regional trade (African oil and grain, eastern goods) **continue**;
  the new capitals and the army's redeployed demand could have **positive** local economic effects.
- The monetary regionalization is largely a feature of the **official** economy and of crisis-era
  political division, not proof that the underlying rural and urban economies disintegrated.

## Labour: slavery, the colonate, and continuity

Corbier deflates two staples of the catastrophist narrative:

- **Slavery was not in catastrophic decline**: there is no proof of a third-century collapse of the
  slave supply or a wholesale switch to other labour forms; slave and free labour coexisted as before.
- **The colonate was not a sudden third-century innovation enserfing the peasantry**. The famous
  **Constantinian constitution of 332** (*[[roman-colonate|CTh 5.17.1]]*) tying *coloni* to their
  estates is read as a **fiscal measure** — binding registered taxpayers to their place of
  assessment under the new iugatio–capitatio (see [[late-roman-coinage]]) — not the legal birth of
  medieval serfdom. There is **no proof of a general worsening of the peasantry's legal status before
  the mid-fourth century**, and the binding reflects a **competition between the emperor and the
  landowners for control of labour**, not a simple class enslavement. (The developed late-Roman
  colonate belongs to a later page, [[roman-colonate]].)

## Irreversible change without catastrophe

Corbier's verdict is finely balanced. She **rejects "catastrophe,"** but she does *not* claim
unbroken continuity: many third-century changes had **"irreversible consequences."** The
fourth-century economy is **structurally different** from the second-century one — a more heavily
**taxed**, gold-anchored, regionally-organized, state-directed economy, with a transformed
relationship between cities, landowners and the fisc. The point is that this transformation was a
**reconfiguration**, achieved through the crisis, not the disintegration of a once-unified market
economy. The empire remained **"a rural society held together by a network of cities."**

## Significance

This chapter supplies the volume's **economic synthesis** and a methodological manifesto: abandon the
grand single theories and the rhetoric of "decline and fall," read the economy at **multiple scales**,
and distinguish the **official/monetary** sphere (where crisis and reform are loud) from the **deep
rural and urban structures** (where continuity dominates). It aligns the economic history with the
volume's broader [[third-century-crisis|revisionist case]]: **change, reconfiguration, and survival**
rather than collapse.

## The fourth-century rural and urban economy (CAH XIII)

[[cameron-cah-v13-1998|CAH XIII]] Chs. 9–10 (**C. R. Whittaker & Peter Garnsey**) carry the economy into
the fourth century — and form one of the volume's strongest **anti-"decline"** statements. The land
remained the source of nearly all wealth and revenue (the *annona militaris* the chief tax, devoted as
ever to army pay). Their arguments:

- **No general agrarian crisis.** Third-century war damage was real but **regionally patchy** and largely
  **recovered** once frontiers stabilized (Themistius: the population returned to the land after
  Adrianople); the Balkans suffered no *Katastrophe* (no major city fell to Goths or Huns in the 4th–5th
  c.). Fresh archaeology in Gaul/the Rhineland shows **far more fourth-century material** and continued
  occupation than once thought.
- **The "*agri deserti*" deflated.** The mass of legislation on deserted land was **fiscal, not economic**
  in concern; over-taxation was **not** a general problem (Diocletian's new land-tax on Italy aside);
  Lactantius' picture of crushing taxation is biased. Marginal land going out of use is "marginal to total
  production." Manpower-shortage arguments rest on anecdote and on the (fiscally-motivated)
  **[[roman-colonate|colonate]]** laws.
- **Barbarian settlement** (*laeti*, *dediticii*, *tributarii*, and Theodosius' Gothic *foederati*)
  **maintained cultivation and supplied recruits**, but settling allies who paid no tax and obeyed their
  own chiefs **removed regions from central control** — feeding the fifth-century growth of local warlords
  and the rise of the great **patrons** (Salvian's poor "preferring barbarians to Roman tax-collectors").

The thesis: the late-Roman economy shows **continuity, adaptation and regional variation, not
empire-wide collapse** — the loss was of **central fiscal control**, not of production.
