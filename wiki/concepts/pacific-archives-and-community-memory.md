## Definition and Scope

Pacific archives and community memory designates the contested field in which post-colonial Pacific Island archival institutions negotiate between Western recordkeeping frameworks inherited from colonial administrations and the oral, communal, and kinship-based knowledge systems of Islander communities. The core tension, as Alefaio and Halter summarise, is that "traditional archival practice has a narrow focus, with characteristics and requirements which can hinder Pacific Island archives from connecting with their communities" — above all, the primacy of written evidence over oral memory is a colonial imposition that fails Pacific realities. ## Colonial Origins of Pacific Archival Collections

Archival collections in the Pacific "first emerged as a result of European exploration, as visiting collectors gathered a variety of objects, manuscripts, and maps related to their economic, political, military, or scientific interests. These rare collections were transported and stored outside the Pacific, where many remain today." Government bureaucracies formalised colonial record-keeping, and independent national archives were created as countries transitioned to independence in the 1970s — the same decade that saw global professionalisation of archival practice and the formation of the Pacific Regional Branch of the International Council on Archives (PARBICA, est. 1981), now encompassing seventeen Pacific Island states and territories.

The National Archives of Fiji (NAF) was established in 1954 as the Central Archives of Fiji and the Western Pacific High Commission, becoming the NAF in 1971. As one of the region's most developed archives, it serves as the principal case study for community-oriented archival innovation.

## Archival Paradigm Shifts: From Evidence to Memory

Sir Hilary Jenkinson's early twentieth-century Jenkinsonianism held that the archivist should be "perhaps the most selfless devotee of Truth the modern world produces," conserving "every scrap of Evidence" without prejudice. Terry Cook charts a shift in the 1930s from custodians of evidence to active selectors of cultural memory, but this shift was slow and uneven in the Pacific. Evidence and memory "evolved in archival discourse in a kind of creative tension" — archives claimed to preserve collective national memory while systematically omitting Islander voices shaped by centuries of colonisation.

The digital era introduces a further paradigm: Cook argues that archiving is increasingly "a participatory process in which the community is involved in 'collaborative evidence- and memory-making'," requiring "a shift in core principles from exclusive custodianship and ownership of archives to shared stewardship and collaboration; from dominant-culture language, terminology, and definitions to sensitivity to the 'other' and as keen an awareness of the emotional, religious, symbolic, and cultural values that records have to their communities."

## Structural Challenges to Pacific Archives

Pacific archives face overlapping crises:

**Climate and disaster**: "The tropical climate of the South Pacific is a major threat to the safe care of aged and brittle records." Cyclone Heta (Category 5, 2003) destroyed the Niue archives. Only New Caledonia, Tahiti, and Fiji have working conservation laboratories; restoration materials are unavailable locally and prohibitively expensive to import.

**Under-resourcing and weak political will**: UNESCO research (1998) found that "very few decision-makers and Pacific Island leaders will link good governance and accountability to the efficient management of public sector records." Nauru, Tokelau, and Tonga lacked national archives at the time of writing. The NAF operated for over four decades with "three entire levels missing" from its organisational chart, making staff retention impossible and eroding institutional capacity.

**Lack of formal training**: "There is not now, nor has there ever been, any formal education for archives, records management, or document conservation in Fiji." Distance learning packages are too expensive; PARBICA's biennial conference provides the only consistent capacity-building.

**Technology risk**: Digital tools offer efficiencies but in the Pacific are "often deployed on a project basis, leading to information silos and legacy systems whose usability over time is not planned for."

## Community Distrust and the Colonial Legacy

The deepest obstacle is structural distrust. In post-colonial societies, archives are seen "primarily as extraneous institutions whose primary focus is to preserve records created by former colonial masters (with their prejudices and limited understanding of the concerns of the colonized) for use by themselves and Western academics." As Ketelaar argued, archives are "a source of power and have been used by those in power as a means of dispossessing and dominating entire populations." Records written in colonial-era bureaucratic English, located in urban capitals distant from rural communities, create further practical barriers. For Fijians unfamiliar with the administrative history of government, independent research in the archives is effectively impossible.

Indigenous oral tradition "contains scientific knowledge pertaining to architecture, seafaring, medicine, agriculture, fisheries, genealogies, and migration histories" and is "dynamic, put through numerous feedback loops, and evolving through phases of transmission between generations." The colonial archival preference for literacy over orality was a political choice, not an epistemic one.

## Community Outreach: The NAF Case Study (2012–2019)

To overcome distrust, NAF innovated across three strategies:

**Village visits via Indigenous protocols**: NAF worked through the Fijian Administration (itself a colonial institution), presenting *sevusevu* — a ceremonial offering of yaqona (kava) — to legitimately enter village spaces. *Sevusevu* "reinforces assumptions about rank, allowing members within a community to define their status relative to others" and "ensures that visitors are protected from spirits whilst in the village." It functions as culturally appropriate consent: "once the sevusevu is presented and accepted all the doors to the village have been opened." On the 2013 Rotuma Day Outreach, NAF brought land records, genealogical records, and historical photos to an island "two days to reach by boat" — "the first time [Rotumans] were given access to their documentary heritage."

**Community participation in archival work**: In 2014, NAF invited the public to transcribe indentured labourer registers (150th anniversary of Melanesian indenture in Fiji). Over three days, one hundred volunteers worked in university computer labs. Descendants were "overcome with emotion upon sighting these records"; the organizing committee chair reported, "Without the presence of National Archives most of the descendants will still rely on the verbal history relayed to them through stories. They are now confident that what they know is something that was recorded during that time then and there." The process acknowledged "the Indigenous owners of knowledge — in this case, the indentured labourers and their descendants."

**Co-creating archives via social media and digitisation**: In 2012 NAF assumed ownership of 2,000 hours of audiovisual footage and 200,000 historical photographs. A 1-million-FJD project restored 97 per cent of the footage; the repackaged television programme *Back in Time* ran over four seasons (150+ episodes). Facebook posts of historical images generated crowd-sourced contextual knowledge: a post of a 1947 Beqa fish drive "reached over 100,000 Facebook accounts" and prompted an Indigenous Fijian to describe "the various clans involved, and the conservation protocols put into place two to three years before the fish drive." The process turned uncatalogued photographs into co-produced community records. The University of Cambridge Museum of Archaeology and Anthropology repatriated 3,000 historical Fijian photographs to NAF in 2018 for public outreach.

Community engagement produced a virtuous cycle: NAF's outreach budget rose from 20,000 FJD in 2013 to 100,000 FJD by 2018; staff establishment grew from 19 to 36; and government credibility was sufficient to secure 1 million FJD for audiovisual preservation.

## Women's Under-representation and the Gendered Archive (Ch. 38)

Safua Akeli Amaama's chapter extends the critique of Pacific archives to gender. Pacific archival collections "typically reflect their respective colonizers' interests, are written in colonial languages, and are confined in different and far-flung collections. Furthermore, underlying these archives lies a complex variety of gender issues." Women's narratives are systematically absent: the author's own research on leprosy in colonial Sāmoa (1890s–1920s) found that "aside from the European missionary sisters who managed the care of leprosy sufferers, very few records documented the experience of women who supported family members or were themselves leprosy sufferers." Records were "created by men, most of whom were either working in government, were chiefs of a village, or were affiliated with the church."

The grandmother figure in the chapter — Palepa Ioane Akeli (1923–2008) — exemplifies the private archive: a well-worn exercise book kept with her bible documenting life story and genealogy, covering the Mau movement, birthing outside hospitals, and a papal visit. These private records "lie outside institutional repositories" and "are buried under or absent from the state and church archives which tend to focus on the accounts provided by European missionaries."

The concept of an **"archive of feelings"** (Ann Cvetkovich) helps recover what institutional archives omit: "the feelings of ordinary people in the archival record, especially the stories and experiences of women and queers, have been neglected in the field of historical trauma studies." Addressing this "requires commitment towards analysing archives" and reconfiguring cataloguing metadata to include women's archival documents. Christen's argument is invoked: "incorporating Indigenous knowledge systems into library and archive practices will not just enhance relationships and create access to records, but more importantly, it has the potential to decolonize archival practices and modes of access."

Women's archives pertaining to the Mau resistance movement in Sāmoa are held not in Sāmoa but in Geneva, "not only distant but also restrictive by requiring permission to access."

## Digital Futures and Ongoing Tensions

Digital platforms offer expanded access but introduce new power imbalances. Archives New Zealand digitising records and making them available online benefits Pacific-based scholars, but such access "depends on colonial historical ties to various groups, and research topics of interest." The National University of Sāmoa's developing National Digital Library (funded by UNDP) illustrates the hardware/software infrastructure challenge. Court records pertaining to land and titles have direct economic implications: "In judicial systems from New Zealand to Sāmoa, the cultural knowledge of land rights, related in orally remembered genealogies and recorded in written land records, continues to have vital economic significance in proving descendants' rights."

The Pacific Virtual Museum (PVM), led by the National Library of New Zealand and National Library of Australia, aims to provide "an online portal that provides an easy single access point to digitised Pacific cultural heritage items held in different museum, gallery and library collections around the world."

Civil society organisations — "women's crisis centres in Fiji and Vanuatu," LGBTQI community organisations — are also archival actors whose records must be preserved alongside government collections, "since their stories are just as important in providing a broader view of the political and social landscape in the Pacific."

## Historiography

The chapter draws on an archival-studies literature (Cook, Ketelaar, Wareham, Christen, Jenkinson) that has moved from custodian paradigms to activist/participatory frameworks. Evelyn Wareham's 2002 survey remains foundational for the regional picture; Alefaio and Halter update it with empirical evidence from the 2012–2019 NAF programme. Akeli Amaama situates the gender-blindness of Pacific archives in a comparative frame drawing on Australian Aboriginal archives (Harkin), the Mau movement in Sāmoa, and feminist trauma studies (Cvetkovich). The Journal of Pacific History (est. 1966) created its first section "From the Archives" in its inaugural issue; since 1968 the ANU Pacific Manuscripts Bureau has provided microfilm and audio collections, though accessibility from within the Pacific remains a problem.
