# Literacy in the Roman Empire

The **extent and uses of reading and writing** in the Roman world — a culture saturated with the
written word yet in which only a minority could read it. Treated in [[bowman-cah-v11-2000|CAH XI]]
Ch. 30 (**Greg Woolf**), engaging William Harris's *Ancient Literacy*.

## A world full of writing — read by few

Woolf opens from the **paradox**: any visitor to a Roman town met writing everywhere (epitaphs,
Pompeii's painted election notices, statue bases, bronze laws, religious calendars, graffiti, wax
tablets, amphora labels, coin legends) — yet **very few inhabitants could read even simple texts,
fewer still write their own name**. The current consensus (largely from *a priori* argument) is
that **only a small fraction — perhaps under 10–15%** — were literate in Greek or Latin. Crucially,
literacy was a feature of the **urban and military** versions of Roman civilization, **not the
countryside**: in Roman Britain inscriptions cluster on the frontiers and in towns, and "no
separate rural writing practices can be attested." Most people met writing only when they visited
the town.

## Measuring the unmeasurable

Two problems beset any estimate: **defining literacy** (signing one's name? reading an inscription?
the "slow writers" of the papyri) and the **paucity of evidence**. Woolf weighs the *a priori*
arguments for low literacy — above all that **many subjects could not even understand spoken Latin
or Greek** (most languages of the empire were never written; Aramaic, Libyan, Gaulish, Punic
coexisted) — and the absence of the modern preconditions for mass literacy (printing, cheap
materials, Protestantism, elementary schooling). But he cautions against assuming Rome was just a
variant of "restricted literacy": its **uses of writing were far more varied and widespread** than
in Pharaonic Egypt or Achaemenid Persia, and it may have taken "an alternative route." The best
hard data — the **Egyptian papyri** — show illiteracy **correlating with social status** (two-thirds
of one cavalry unit could not sign for a hay allowance) and **women much less literate** than men,
while also showing writing pervading even village life (the Tebtunis *grapheion*).

## How illiterates coped, and the symbolic word

Illiterates managed "with the help of literates": **scribes** and ***hypographeis*** (relatives
signing on another's behalf — men for their wives), public proclamation by heralds, and the
ever-present **spoken word** (Woolf insists the silent texts must be given "a soundtrack" of
stallholders, heralds, and speeches). Illiteracy meant **dependency** and theoretical exclusion
from some offices. Yet because reading was not confined to a scribal clerisy, literacy was
**attainable in theory** even if rarely attained.

## The unity and meaning of Roman writing

Despite surviving only in scattered exceptional caches (the **Vindolanda** tablets, **Dura**
parchments, the Vesuvian business archives of **Caecilius Iucundus** and the **Sulpicii**, the
**Alburnus Maior** wax tablets, the **La Graufesenque** firing tallies), Roman writing practices
show "a remarkable degree of unity" across the empire — in **military** records, **personal
letters** (shared epistolary formulae from senators to soldiers), and **commercial documents**
(written contracts and accounts wherever "substantial amounts of money, rather than prominent
individuals, were involved" — the non-citizen Dacian miners, the Gaulish-speaking potters). Woolf's
governing theme: Roman writing always blended the **utilitarian and the symbolic** — Iucundus'
witnesses signing in order of status, the autograph as a gesture of intimacy, and above all
**epigraphy's symbolic permanence** (the hierarchy of media: bronze for laws and treaties, stone
altars for the most prestigious vows, perishable placards for lesser ones) — writing as a way to
**memorialize and personalize** the act, including the reciprocal relationship of gods and men.

## Significance and contest

Woolf reframes Roman literacy not as a single measurable level but as an **aspect of Roman cultural
style** — the written word pervasive and powerful as symbol and instrument, yet actively wielded by
few — illuminating society, economy, religion, and government at once. The actual literacy rate, and
whether Rome reached unusually high levels by pre-modern standards, remain contested on slender
evidence.
