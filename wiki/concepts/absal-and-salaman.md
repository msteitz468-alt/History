## Definition and Scope

Absāl and Salāmān are the two named human figures who appear in the second half of Ibn Ṭufayl's *Ḥayy ibn Yaqẓān*, after Hayy has completed his solitary philosophical-mystical ascent. As a pair, they function as a typological contrast representing the two possible orientations of a genuinely religious person toward revealed tradition: the contemplative-allegorical and the social-literalist. Their encounter with Hayy tests the harmony of philosophy and religion and produces the tale's sobering political conclusion.

## The Characters as Introduced

Both Absāl and Salāmān are presented as sincere adherents of a "certain true religion, based on the teachings of a certain ancient prophet." The religion is unnamed and the island deliberately unlocated — Ibn Ṭufayl is describing a structural type, not a specific historical community. Both men "had taken instruction in this religion and accepted it enthusiastically. Both held themselves duty-bound to abide by all its laws and precepts for living."

Their difference emerges not in their piety but in their cognitive dispositions toward the religion's symbolic content:

**Absāl** "was the more deeply concerned with getting down to the heart of things, the more eager to discover spiritual values, and the more ready to attempt a more or less allegorical interpretation." He naturally gravitates toward the bāṭin (inner meaning) over the ẓāhir (literal surface). He prefers solitude over society: "he was naturally a thoughtful man, fond of contemplation and of probing for the deeper meanings of things." He studies languages to pursue allegorical interpretation more deeply. These traits mark him as the philosophical type within a religious framework — the figure who stands at the threshold between exoteric religion and esoteric philosophical truth.

**Salāmān** "was more anxious to preserve the literal and less prone to seek subtle intentions. On the whole he avoided giving too free rein to his thoughts." He prefers communal life: "he was by nature chary of too much independent thinking or doing. In staying with the group he saw some means of fending off demonic promptings, dispelling distracting thoughts, and in general guarding against the goadings of the devil." Salāmān ends up as the ruler of the island community — the political-religious type who governs through law and symbol.

Their divergence on solitude versus society causes their parting: Absāl goes to Hayy's island; Salāmān remains to govern the community.

## The Encounter Between Hayy and Absāl

When Absāl and Hayy meet on the island, their encounter is initially comic — Hayy has never seen a clothed human, and Absāl cannot make himself understood in any language Hayy knows. Absāl teaches Hayy speech. Once communication is established, Hayy narrates his entire philosophical journey, culminating in his mystical ascent. Absāl's response is the tale's crucial pivot:

"Hearing Hayy's description of the beings which are divorced from the sense-world and conscious of the Truth — glory be to Him — his description of the Truth Himself, by all His lovely attributes, and his description, as best he could, of the joys of those who reach Him and the agonies of those veiled from Him, Absāl had no doubt that all the traditions of his religion about God, His angels, bibles and prophets, Judgement Day, Heaven and Hell were symbolic representations of these things that Hayy Ibn Yaqẓān had seen for himself. The eyes of his heart were unclosed. His mind caught fire. Reason and tradition were at one within him."

This recognition — that religious tradition and philosophical vision describe the same reality — is the text's primary intellectual claim. Absāl is the figure who can make this recognition because his cognitive disposition was already oriented toward inner meanings. He then becomes Hayy's devoted student, and the two travel together to the civilized island.

## Salāmān and the Failure of Philosophical Instruction

On the civilized island, Salāmān presides as its leading man. Hayy attempts to teach the community — including Absāl's select circle of more thoughtful believers — and fails completely. The group recoils when Hayy "rose the slightest bit above the literal or began to portray things against which they were prejudiced." They make "a show of being pleased with Hayy, but in their hearts they resented him."

Salāmān, as the communal-political figure, represents the principle that religious communities require authoritative law and shared symbolic frameworks. He does not appear in direct dialogue with Hayy, but his role as community leader makes him the implicit justification for Hayy's final decision to withdraw: "He urged them to hold fast to their observance of all the statutes regulating outward behavior and not delve into things that did not concern them."

## Symbolic Interpretation

The pair encodes a typology that runs through Islamic philosophy and Sufism:

**Absāl** corresponds to the category of the *ʿārif* (the gnostic, the spiritually knowing), the person capable of ascent beyond literal religion to its experiential truth. His allegorical bent, his solitude, his linguistic cultivation, and his immediate comprehension of Hayy all mark him as the philosophical-mystical type who bridges falsafa and dīn.

**Salāmān** corresponds to the category of the *faqīh*-politician (the jurist-ruler), the person who governs through law and symbol and whose cognitive style is literal and communal. He represents neither ignorance nor bad faith — he is sincere, capable, and pious — but his cognitive mode is constitutively unsuited for unmediated philosophical truth.

The tale's resolution — that both men and the community they lead must remain within their respective modes — suggests that this typology is not a hierarchy in which one mode simply supersedes the other. The Salāmān type is not on a path that leads to the Absāl type; these are structurally different orientations, both necessary, neither convertible into the other by instruction.

## Ibn Sīnā's Prior Use

The names Absāl and Salāmān appear earlier in Ibn Sīnā's (Avicenna's) tale *Salāmān wa-Absāl*, which survives only in summary. In Ibn Sīnā's version (as reported by Naṣīr al-Dīn al-Ṭūsī), Salāmān represents the practical intellect attached to the body and world, while Absāl represents the theoretical intellect seeking separation from the material. Ibn Ṭufayl's reuse of the names is deliberate — he explicitly says his tale takes up where Ibn Sīnā's "Oriental wisdom" left off — though he transforms their valences in a narrative rather than allegorical frame.

## Historiography

The allegorical reading of Absāl and Salāmān as philosophical types is broadly accepted, though the exact identification varies. Goodman (1972) emphasizes the pair as embodying two valid modes of religious life rather than a simple hierarchy. Atiyeh and Corbin stress the Neoplatonic and Sufi valences. Strauss's school reads Salāmān as the political-religious authority whose dominance the tale ultimately — and subversively — challenges, while Hayy's withdrawal is ironic rather than sincere. The question of whether Ibn Ṭufayl endorses the communal-literalist mode (Salāmān) as genuinely valid or merely as a necessary accommodation to human weakness is central to the debate.
