# The Peloponnesian League

The **"Peloponnesian League"** (a modern coinage; the Greeks said **"the Lacedaemonians and
their allies"**) was the system of alliances through which [[sparta|Sparta]] led much of
the Peloponnese from the mid-sixth century, and the **focal point of mainland Greece** for
any land war as Persia neared the Aegean. [[boardman-cah-v4-1988|CAH Vol. IV]] (Ch. 6,
L. H. Jeffery) describes its structure, members, and limits.

## Structure — a wheel, not a web

The League grew after Sparta's victory over Tegea (mid-sixth c.): defeated states (Tegea)
and free states alike enrolled, swearing loyalty **to Sparta alone** — "to follow the
Lacedaemonians wherever they lead" — but **not to each other**. Jeffery's image is exact:
"a circle centred on Sparta, with the spokes of a wheel but not necessarily the added
cross-links of a web." Members thus remained **free to quarrel among themselves** (Corinth
vs. Megara, Phlius vs. Cleonae) except when mustered for an agreed campaign. Crucially,
**League meetings to vote on war could be held only at Sparta**, so as summoner Sparta
controlled the initiation of any project — though in 432 she had to be "pushed by Corinth"
into convening one.

## Membership and the cement of anti-Argive feeling

The core members were Peloponnesian: [[corinth|Corinth]] (the most influential after Sparta,
providing a large fleet-contingent and commanding the Isthmus), **Sicyon**, the cities of
the **Argolid**, **Elis**, perhaps **Achaea** and **Megara**, and ambiguously
[[aegina|Aegina]]. For many, **common hostility to [[argos|Argos]]** — Sparta's permanent
rival, pointedly **outside** the League — was "the cause, or at least the cement, of their
membership." Control of the great panhellenic sanctuaries (Olympia via Elis, Nemea contested
by Corinth and Argos, the Isthmus by Corinth) added a religious-political dimension to
League rivalries.

## Limits — naval weakness and the Helot brake

The League was "not geared technically, nor perhaps psychologically, to oppose a big naval
advance" — its fleet depended on coastal members, and its leader [[cleomenes-i|Cleomenes]]
repeatedly **declined distant naval commitments** (Samos 517, the Scythians 513, the Ionian
Revolt 499). The deepest constraint on Spartan-led action was always the **fear of Helot
revolt** in Messenia and Laconia, which made long campaigns and heavy losses dangerous.
Corinth's independence was the other limit: it twice **refused to follow Cleomenes against
Athens** (507/6) and blocked the proposed restoration of Hippias (c. 504).

## Significance and critique

The League made Sparta the **natural leader of the Greek resistance** in 480–479, yet its
loose, Sparta-centred structure and its members' mutual feuds limited its coherence — the
same weaknesses that would later fracture in the Peloponnesian War. Because Sparta "did not
produce historians or orators," its institutions are reconstructed from **Herodotus,
Thucydides, and later inscriptions**, and the **timing of members' enrolment** and the
**uniformity of the oath** remain uncertain.
