# Egyptian Formalism (the Canon)

The deliberate maintenance, across nearly three thousand years, of a uniform **canon** of
proportion, pose, and representational convention in Egyptian art and architecture — the
composite "aspective" human figure, the grid system of proportion, the standardized
temple and tomb forms. To modern eyes this can read as stagnation; Kemp argues it is the
opposite: the conscious upkeep of a **model**.

## Kemp's Reading

In [[sources/kemp-ancient-egypt-anatomy-2018|*Anatomy of a Civilization*]] (Ch. 3),
formalism is the visible signature of the [[concepts/great-tradition-little-tradition|Great Tradition]]:
a centrally cultivated ideal that accommodated substantial change in outlook *beneath* an
unchanging surface. The canon was a vehicle of the state's
[[concepts/divine-kingship|ideology of order]] — [[actors/maat|Maat]] made visual —
and its stability was an achievement of bureaucratic and priestly transmission, not
mere inertia. Departures (the Amarna style under [[actors/akhenaten]]) are legible
precisely because the norm was so firmly held.

## Mechanisms

Kemp's key term is the **"ideal type"** — a universal feature of mind that the Egyptians
"elevated to the pinnacle of intellectual and aesthetic desirability." Because the canon was
centred on **court patronage**, it was "self-perpetuating, automatically selecting and
promoting those artists with a natural aptitude for absorbing the range of ideal types."
Three essential elements define the style: composition in **horizontal registers**; the
intimate integration of **hieroglyphs with figures**; and the reduction of each figure to a
**characteristic profile** recombined into a composite ("aspective") image. From the Middle
Kingdom a **grid of 18 squares** (soles to hairline) fixed human proportion. The style's
very modularity — breakable into parts, teachable from approved models, scalable to
monumental size — is what made it useful to the state.

Formalism advanced by **extinguishing rivals**: predynastic Decorated Ware pottery painting
declined "into simple squiggles and then vanishes" once court art emerged; the minor
provincial **seal-carving** tradition was "snuffed out" when officialdom adopted the scarab.
Architecture ran the same course — Kemp periodizes it **Preformal → Early Formal → Mature
Formal** (New Kingdom) **→ Late Formal** (Thirtieth Dynasty–Roman) — anchored on the
**tent-shrine archetype** that [[actors/djoser|Imhotep]] translated into stone at the Step
Pyramid and that the Ptolemaic temple of **Horus at Edfu** still faithfully reproduced two
and a half millennia later. Hieroglyphic signs themselves never changed their ideal-type
outlines for the whole dynastic period. Kemp's deeper point: this apparent timelessness was
"far from... spontaneous" — a deliberate selection and modification creating a vocabulary
for "the constant **invention of tradition**" (see [[concepts/preformal-egypt]]).
