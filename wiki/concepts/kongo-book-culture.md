## Definition and Scope

"Kongo book culture" designates the ensemble of practices, values, and conceptual frameworks through which people in the [[kingdom-of-kongo|Kingdom of Kongo]] and the wider Kikongo-speaking region engaged with books, literacy, and writing from the late fifteenth century through the nineteenth century. Brinkman & Bostoen (ch. 9) use the emic phrase **"to make book"** — recorded by British traveller James Tuckey in 1818 when a Loango nobleman was promised his son would go to England "to make book," i.e., to study — as a window into the Kongo conceptualization of literacy as a domain of power, cosmopolitan distinction, and social transformation, not merely a technical skill.

The concept challenges the widespread characterisation of pre-colonial Africa as "the oral continent par excellence." Ethiopia, the Swahili coast, West African Islamic cultures, and the Kongo kingdom all developed substantial book cultures before colonial rule; the Kongo case is distinctive in that its literacy practices entered through Christianity and were subsequently extended into trade, yielding two overlapping but distinct book-concept clusters that can be traced linguistically across the Kikongo Language Cluster.

## Origins: Books Enter Kongo, 1482 onward

Contact between Portugal and Kongo was established in 1482. Among the earliest items the king of Portugal sent were "many books," and subsequent shipments are documented in the archive record (Brásio 1952). The Kongo nobility — led by [[afonso-i-kongo|Afonso I]] — rapidly embraced literacy: one source describes the king falling asleep over books and forgetting to eat and drink while absorbed in scripture, though Brinkman & Bostoen note the hagiographic character of that letter. More tellingly, Afonso also used literacy instrumentally: he requested a copy of Portuguese law, maintained administrative account books on royal instruction, and used letter-writing as a tool of political centralisation — ensuring communication between the capital Mbanza Kongo and provincial governors.

Books were initially scarce and expensive. In the 1650s, paper cost a chicken per sheet, and catechisms could be bought at a price equivalent to a slave. Two German printers were sent over early but left, as "the land was not healthy for Germans." Books were so valued that people would "sell all they have to buy a manuscript or a book and if they have one, they always carry it by hand with their rosary" (Vatican document, late sixteenth century).

The first Kikongo-language catechism was printed in 1624 by the Jesuit Mateus Cardoso. It spread from Luanda to Mbanza Kongo and into Soyo; copies were scarce enough that missionaries hand-copied it at night. The Spanish Capuchin Antonio de Teruel requested as many as seven books in Kikongo — a catechism, sermons, a calendar, a vocabulary in four languages (Latin, Italian, Spanish, and "Congolese"), and a grammar — indicating the vitality of Kongo-language print culture in the seventeenth century.

Literacy also acquired near-ritual status. Brinkman & Bostoen cite the Italian Capuchin Brusciotto de Vetralla (1650s): in processions, those who had learned the alphabet "stick a piece of paper in the form of a card on their forehead so as to be recognized as a student." Books served ecclesiastical administrative functions — baptism registers, matrimony records, lists of confessions, and rolls of knights in the Order of Christ were all maintained in book form.

## Shift: From Christian to Trade "Bookness" (18th–19th c.)

By the later seventeenth century the Kongo kingdom had decentralised (see [[kongo-decentralization|Kongo Decentralization]]). Fewer European missionaries frequented the region, the Kongo Catholic Church developed its own direction, and the supply of books from Europe contracted. In the coastal broker-states north of the Congo River — Kakongo, Ngoyo, and Loango — a different book culture emerged in connection with overseas trade: logbooks, inventories, account books, and contracts. Julius Falkenstein reported that "a Mukanda was issued for everything thinkable." Monteiro (1875) documented that traders used small written slips — called "books" or *mikanda* by locals — to record quantities of coffee weighed during transactions.

In this northern coastal milieu, "to make book" came to denote anything to do with writing: ticket, letter, contract, or book. The possession of unreadable books (a German traveller mocked noblemen who showed him a book lacking beginning, end, and title, which they "of course" could not read) and of "glassless glasses" that served no optical purpose both point to a regime in which the symbolic and social valence of books and reading paraphernalia was as important as functional literacy — what Brinkman & Bostoen, following Goody, call "non-syntactical, non-textual uses of writing."

## Linguistic Evidence: Words for "Book" in the Kikongo Language Cluster

Brinkman & Bostoen trace book-vocabulary diachronically across the Kikongo Language Cluster — a methodological innovation that links cultural history to historical linguistics.

**1. *Mukanda* / *nkanda* (Bantu stem *-kanda*, "skin, cloth")**
The oldest attested Kongo term for book-related concepts. First recorded in the 1624 Cardoso catechism as *omucanda* (for "epistle," "scriptures"). The stem *-kanda* is widely attested across Bantu languages with the meaning "skin"; in Kikongo it came to mean anything written — book, letter, ticket, contract. Its usage is geographically concentrated within the borders of the ancient Kongo kingdom, where it spread alongside the Christian church's administrative practices. Tone patterns in Ciluba and other interior languages suggest it spread outward from the Kongo heartland as a loan, demonstrating that the cultural influence of Kongo reached deep inland.

**2. Portuguese loan *divulu* / *riuulu* (from *livro*)**
First attested 1624 in the Cardoso catechism as *maliuru* (plural) and *mudiuulu* (locative, "in a book"). The deep phonological nativisation — *l* becomes *d* before *i*, *r* becomes *l*, vowel epenthesis breaks the *vr* cluster, Bantu noun-class prefixes added — indicates the loan had been in Kisikongo (the Kikongo variety of Mbanza Kongo) for some time before 1624. This loan persisted through the seventeenth century in Christian contexts but had almost disappeared by the twentieth century, surviving only in a few South Kikongo varieties.

**3. Dutch loan *buka* (from *boeken*, Dutch plural of *boek*)**
Attested in the Kakongo region by the 1770s in French missionary sources, where it combines with *kanda* as *i buka a m'kanda*. Brinkman & Bostoen argue this is a Dutch rather than English loan because the phonological nativisation strategy mirrors Dutch plural *boeken* (with nasal apocope producing a schwa, then nativised to *a* in Kikongo). The Dutch presence at the coast explains this loanword. By the twentieth century, *buka* is nearly extinct, found only in the Mayombe area near the Cabinda border.

**4. English loan *buku* (from *book*)**
First attested in Bentley's 1887 Baptist Missionary Society dictionary. By that date already widespread from the coast to the interior (attested in Kisikongo near Mbanza Kongo, East Kikongo north of the Inkisi River, and Central Kikongo Kimanyanga). By the twenty-first century, *buku* is the dominant and universal term for "book" across the entire Kikongo-speaking region, while *mukanda*/*nkanda* is retained as an alternative in many varieties but specialised to "letter" in the northern coastal regions. The dominance of the English loan over all others reflects the shift from the Christian-Portuguese to the British-trade phase of Kongo's global connections.

**5. French loan *liv(a)la* (from *livre*)**
Limited to the coastal Ngoyo and Kakongo regions, attested from the late nineteenth century; now nearly extinct.

## Interpretation: Cosmopolitanism and Power

The key argument of Brinkman & Bostoen is that Kongo book culture should not be read as a passive reception of a European practice, but as an active, strategic appropriation. Literacy entered through the Christian church but was immediately taken up by the Kongo nobility as a political instrument — for centralisation, administration, correspondence, and the performance of cosmopolitan distinction. Books acquired sacred, ritual, and symbolic dimensions that exceeded European "modern" book culture of the period: the gospel book was kissed by political leaders as a form of blessing; books appeared "miraculously" in Kongo conceptions that troubled European missionaries.

The linguistic layering of Portuguese, Dutch, French, and English loanwords across different regions and centuries is evidence that the "Lusophone bias" in studies of the Kikongo Language Cluster is misleading. The English loan's ultimate dominance, spread from coastal trading contexts into the entire region including the old Kongo heartland, shows that a "cosmopolitan image of Kongo's global interactions" more accurately captures the history than a framework centred on Portugal alone. From the late fifteenth century, when Kongo elites first started "to make book," the Bantu term *-kanda* spread far beyond the kingdom's borders, demonstrating Kongo's cultural outreach into Central Africa.

This connects directly to broader arguments about [[kongo-cosmopolitanism|Kongo cosmopolitanism]] as an elite practice tied to extraversion (see also [[kongo-nineteenth-century-cosmopolitanism|Kongo Nineteenth-Century Cosmopolitanism]]).

## Historiography

**Source base and its limits.** The chapter relies on the Brásio *Monumenta Missionaria Africana* collection, missionary accounts, travellers' reports (Tuckey, Monteiro, Bastian), and fieldwork data gathered by the KongoKing project team in 2012 across the DRC, Republic of Congo, and Cabinda. The limitation acknowledged by the authors is that early documents were written in Portuguese, so Kikongo book-vocabulary is invisible until the 1624 catechism. The sources also have a Lusophone bias that the authors consciously counteract.

**Debates.** The chapter engages critically with the framing of Africa as an "oral continent" (Derive 2008, partially qualified by Finnegan 2007) and with the assumption that modern Western book culture provides the universal model for "bookness" (critiqued via Hofmeyr & Kriel 2006). There is no consensus on exactly when *buku* entered the Kikongo Language Cluster from English; the first half of the nineteenth century is a lacuna in Kikongo linguistic documentation.

**Methodological innovation.** The combination of historical-linguistic diachrony (Proto-Bantu reconstruction, nativisation patterns, geographic distribution mapping) with cultural history of material objects is the chapter's distinctive contribution. The method allows causal claims about when and through which contacts specific vocabulary — and therefore specific book-culture practices — entered different regions, cutting across the purely textual focus of most Kongo historiography.

**Collection coverage.** *The Kongo Kingdom* (Bostoen & Brinkman eds., Cambridge 2018) is the primary source for this page. The collection has moderate-to-strong Kongo coverage but is weaker on post-1700 Atlantic and diaspora dimensions; see [[kongo-diaspora-brazil|Kongo Diaspora in Brazil]] for the Atlantic dimension.
