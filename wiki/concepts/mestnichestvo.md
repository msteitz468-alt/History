# Mestnichestvo

## Definition
**Mestnichestvo** was a rigid hierarchical system of state and military appointments in Muscovite Russia under which a noble's position in service had to correspond to the historical standing of his family and his own birth rank within that family (L2108-2110, L2538-2540). No individual could be appointed to a post ranking lower than someone whose family was historically subordinate to his own.

## Context and Origin
The system was formalized in 1475, when boyar families were entered into the state genealogical book and their service appointments were recorded in registers (L2544). The Muscovite boyars valued their family "honor" extremely highly, as any downgrading of a family member could permanently damage the pedigree's future service prospects (L2544). 

The system resulted in extreme cumbersomeness and military inefficiency (L2538-2540). For example, a Muscovite army was divided into five regiments:
1. Big (main) regiment (highest honor)
2. Right wing
3. Advance guard (equal to rear guard)
4. Rear guard (equal to advance guard)
5. Left wing (lowest honor) (L2540-2542)

Assigning commands to these units required painstaking pedigree calculations that ignored military talent. In times of crisis, the government was forced to declare certain campaigns exempt from *mestnichestvo*, or keep high-ranking but incompetent boyars in Moscow "for advice" while sending talented men of lower rank to command in the field (L2542-2544). Even absolute rulers like Ivan IV were unable to dismantle the system, which was jealously guarded by the aristocracy.

## Limitations and Critiques
While designed to preserve noble pedigree and prevent favoritism among the elite, *mestnichestvo* fostered division and squabbles over rank among the boyars, preventing them from organizing as a cohesive estate against royal absolutism (L2108-2110, L2544). In 1682, during the reign of Tsar Theodore, the system was finally abolished to allow for administrative centralization and merit-based advancement (L2466, L2546). The genealogical registers were publicly burned to prevent future disputes (L2466).
