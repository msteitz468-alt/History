# Sabin Lost Battles Model

The Sabin model is a purpose-designed conflict simulation and comparative analytic framework for reconstructing ancient land battles where primary evidence is thin or contradictory. It treats battle as the interactive product of force, space, time, and command rather than a static diagram or pure narrative.

## Core Design Principles (Grounded in the Text)

The model is intentionally "broad brush" and playable, avoiding the impenetrable equations of formal operational analysis while still enabling quantitative comparison and dynamic refighting. It is expressed primarily in verbal rules with simple dice/command mechanics, designed both for scholarly hypothesis testing and for wargaming.

Key structural elements:
- **5 × 4 zone grid**: Battlefields divided into a chessboard-like array of zones (each 300–1,200 m). Units occupy specific zones; movement and attacks are zone-to-zone (orthogonal only). This abstracts distance while capturing flank/rear threats and "revolving door" dynamics.
- **Unit representation and Fighting Value (FV)**: Armies broken into discrete units (typically ~500–few thousand men) classified by type (heavy infantry, light infantry, cavalry, elephants, etc.) and quality (veteran / average / levy). A composite FV score aggregates numbers, type strengths, and quality.
- **Command points and activation**: Each player turn begins with a dice roll for commands. Commands are spent to activate groups of units for movement/attack. Brilliant commanders receive bonuses. This models the friction of ancient C2 (primitive communications force delegation and pre-planning).
- **Sequence and "spent" status**: Battles last up to ~10 turns (~25 min real time per turn depending on scale). Combat causes progressive "spent" (fatigue, disorder, ammunition depletion, morale erosion) rather than immediate mass killing. Spent units fight worse and are vulnerable to collapse.
- **Morale and rout mechanics**: One-sided slaughter occurs primarily after one side loses cohesion and flees. The model tracks how accumulated physical and mental effects (from the interacting tenets of deployment, manoeuvre, and fighting) overcome an enemy's will to stand.
- **Terrain, camps, and key zones**: Limited terrain effects (hills, rivers as double-edged); camps and key zones affect deployment, morale, and victory.

The model explicitly incorporates player decisions to simulate the "battle of wits." Outcomes across multiple refights reveal robust patterns even when individual runs vary with luck.

## Relation to Earlier Work by Sabin

The full model extends Sabin's prior "battlefield clock" insight (wide-ranging manoeuvres imply prolonged infantry clashes lasting hours, not minutes) and "stand-off" hypothesis (close-quarter mêlée is sporadic and tentative until one side breaks; decisive killing happens in pursuit).

## Use and Value

- Adjudicates source disputes by "best fit" with comparative data from the full corpus of ~35 engagements (e.g., army frontage vs. size scaling; feasible odds; elephant and cavalry effects; duration of combat).
- Makes traditional battle diagrams dynamic: "come to life."
- Generates new questions about feasible alternative tactics (e.g., at Cannae: what if the Romans had handled the wings differently?).
- Distinguishes grand tactical/general's-eye view from micro-tactical "face of battle."
- Provides a methodological bridge between narrative history, quantitative analysis, and wargaming/simulation techniques used by actual armed forces.

## Limitations (per Sabin)

- Not a precision predictive tool; margins of error in ancient data are large. "Spurious precision" must be resisted.
- Broad-brush: cannot resolve fine-grained tactical details (e.g., exact wheeling manoeuvres within a zone).
- Assumes a degree of commonality across the Greek–Hellenistic–Roman–Punic world over centuries (justified by technological/cultural continuity and ancient authors' own cross-period citations).
- Best used alongside, not instead of, source criticism.
