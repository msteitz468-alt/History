## Definition and Scope

"The Modern Islamic Predicament" names the structural condition, analysed systematically by Hodgson in the Epilogue to *The Venture of Islam* (1974), that Muslim societies face in the era of global technicalism. It is not a single event or crisis but a compounded situation produced by the intersection of three historically specific features:

1. The legacy of the [[great-western-transmutation]] — the transformation originating in Western Europe that deprived the Nile-to-Oxus region of its former central role in the Oikoumene and undermined the social and economic structures of [[islamicate|Islamicate civilization]].
2. The position of most Muslim-majority lands on the low-investment side of the **development gap** — the divide between highly technicalized and low-investment societies that Hodgson treats as the most consequential single fact of twentieth-century global politics.
3. A distinctive cultural-historical position in which the pre-Modern heritage that might provide resources for modern vision is simultaneously the most urgent need and the most difficult to access, because the breach in living tradition is more severe on the non-Western side of the development gap.

Hodgson is careful to distinguish the Modern Islamic Predicament from simple backwardness or civilizational failure. He treats it as a structural position produced by world-historical forces, not by any inherent deficiency of the Islamic tradition.

## The Development Gap and Its Compound Effects

Hodgson identifies five categories of problems arising from global technicalism, arguing that each takes a more acute form on the low-investment (predominantly Muslim) side of the development gap:

**1. Disruption of cultural traditions.** All technically modern societies experience rapid cultural change that outpaces the capacity of inherited traditions to provide guidance. In the West, however, there is basic continuity — "from the ox-cart to the automobile" — because the new has grown from the old. In Islamdom, the break is "harsh and drastic": modernity arrives as an external imposition rather than an internal development. Muslim specialists operate in traditions they do not fully share and cannot fully participate in.

> "To make a turn upon a common usage, it may be said that the heart of Modern world problems is that a traditional West, benefiting from the moderating effects of continuity, contrasts painfully to the rest of the world, which is untraditional where it matters most and, instead, starkly, unrelievedly modern." (*Venture of Islam*, Vol. 3, Epilogue)

**2. Pressure on natural resources.** Technicalization everywhere creates pressure on land and natural resources. In Western lands, the most prominent form is the destruction of wilderness and environmental poisoning. In Muslim-majority lands, the more elementary threat predominates: mass poverty, food insecurity, and the "population explosion" — the inability of local technical capacity to increase food production fast enough to keep pace with population growth.

**3. Need for social planning under conditions of urgency.** The interdependence produced by technicalism requires coordinated social planning. In Western societies, this poses problems of political accountability and individual liberty. In Muslim-majority societies, the same need arises under conditions of desperate urgency (mass poverty, colonial legacy), and the danger of "arbitrary human manipulation on an unprecedented scale" is correspondingly greater. Hodgson notes that whether planning takes neo-Sharī'ah, "directed democracy," or communist form, the same fundamental question arises: who controls the planners?

**4. Lettered mass culture and education.** Technicalism requires universal literacy and mass education, but producing it without sacrificing intellectual creativity is a problem for all societies. In Muslim-majority lands, this problem is compounded by poverty, by the presence of an overwhelming Western rival whose recent publications even specialists cannot keep up with, and by a single "popular" audience that makes sophisticated cultural production economically marginal.

**5. The need for moral vision.** Hodgson regards the erosion of inherited moral allegiances and the need for a new integral vision as the deepest of all modern problems. In the West, some leisure exists for experiments in moral reformulation. In Islamdom, "there hardly seems time for this." The most active minds are overwhelmed by immediate survival crises, and Islam tends to function as "a loyalistic slogan, or a façade for received Modern values, without showing the way to genuinely new solutions."

## The Muslim Position in Global Moral Unity

Hodgson stresses that the Modern Islamic Predicament cannot be understood as an isolated Muslim problem. By the mid-twentieth century, Muslim peoples had been "enmeshed in all the international moral issues that have sprung from the technicalistic world order." He traces this through:

- Muslim complicity with and against Nazism in the 1930s and 1940s, which he treats as a moral test passed imperfectly by Muslim societies (many offered sympathies to Nazi Germany as an alternative to Western imperialism, including in some cases explicit anti-Jewish fanaticism, though most eventually did not actively collaborate).
- Participation in UN-based international governance and the growing world public opinion expressed through bodies such as UNESCO, where alignments based on communism, fascism, or liberal capitalism proved more decisive than differences among Islam, Christianity, and Hinduism.
- The joint character of the world's central problems: the development gap is as much a problem for Western societies (shaping their military commitments, foreign policy, and psychological life) as for Muslim-majority ones.

Hodgson argues that the problems are "not merely alike; they can probably not be fully solved in one place till they are solved everywhere else too." This rules out both isolationist Muslim solutions and the advice of Western market economists who tell low-investment lands to wait patiently for trickle-down convergence.

## Structural Obstacles Within the Heritage

Hodgson identifies internal obstacles in Islamic intellectual culture that compound the external structural predicament:

**Romantic apologetic.** Under the pressure of self-identification against European hegemony, modern Muslim intellectuals (documented by H.A.R. Gibb in *Modern Trends in Islam*) have systematically refused to face historical realities that would disturb their idealized image of the Islamic past. This "wilful ignorance" prevents the honest historical engagement the tradition's own norms would require.

**Failure regarding the Jewish tradition.** Hodgson argues that one of the "spiritual tragedies" of Islam has been the failure to maintain an active confrontation with Jewish thought. Since Islam and Christianity are both "specialized developments out from the Hebrew prophetic tradition" (Christianity as a venture in redemptive love; Islam as a venture in prophetic social justice), both are incomplete without engagement with what they share and distinguish from their common source. Zionism has, in Hodgson's view, catastrophically distorted this already damaged relationship.

**Unresolved tension between Sharī'ah-mindedness and Sufism.** Hodgson argues that Muslim theologians of the future must go beyond Junayd, Ghazālī, and Ibn al-'Arabī in finding ways to hold together the inward-life dimension of Qur'ānic experience and its role as a guide to social relations. The historical resolution through esotericism — leaving exoteric communalism intact while pursuing esoteric universalism separately — is no longer adequate in a world where "every idea and every standpoint is subject to repeated public investigation without respect for authority."

## Resources Within the Heritage

Despite diagnosing the predicament severely, Hodgson argues the Islamic heritage contains genuine resources for response (treated more fully in [[islamic-heritage-modern-conscience]]):

- A world-wide cosmopolitan scope that does not require prior validation of local cultural forms.
- An integral vision that refuses sharp sacred/secular distinction.
- A deep-seated historical consciousness that, if honestly deployed, would support rather than resist critical engagement with the tradition's own formation.

## Possible Outcomes

Hodgson closes by acknowledging genuine uncertainty. Even if Muslims succeed in working through the deepest implications of their heritage, Islam as an identifiable institutional tradition may not persist indefinitely. He envisages the possibility that Islam's most creative long-term thrust may come through its great secular literature — especially Persian poetry — "like a secret leaven long after they have forgotten they were once Muslims." He treats this not as a failure but as one possible mode of a tradition's continuation.

## Relation to Other Concepts

The Modern Islamic Predicament is distinguished from:

- [[political-islam]]: Hodgson does not regard communalist or neo-Sharī'ah political movements as adequate responses to the predicament; they represent a reactive form that may intensify rather than resolve the deeper tension.
- [[islamic-modernism]]: The Modernizing Muslim response as such is seen as largely inadequate because of its romantic apologetic and failure to engage honestly with history.
- Simple colonial exploitation theory: Hodgson acknowledges the structural effects of Western hegemony but resists reducing the predicament to external causation alone; it has internal intellectual and spiritual dimensions that external forces cannot fully explain.
