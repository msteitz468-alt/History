## Definition and Scope

Gender in modern Germany refers both to the historical reality of changing relations between men and women in German society from the mid-eighteenth century onward, and to the analytical framework by which historians study those relations. The two centuries covered here (1760–1960) encompass the rise and mutation of the "separate spheres" ideology — the division of social life into a male public world of work, politics, and money, and a female private sphere of reproduction and domesticity — through industrialization, two world wars, National Socialism, and postwar reconstruction.

Gender history has become, in Joan Scott's influential formulation, "a constitutive element of social relationships based on perceived differences between the sexes … and a primary way of signifying relationships of power." It is not merely an additive history of women's experiences but a fundamental reanalysis of traditional historical subjects — the state, industrialization, war, nationalism, fascism — from the perspective of how they were gendered.

## The Separate Spheres Ideal: Origins and Elaboration (c. 1760–1860)

### Pre-Industrial Households

Before the separate spheres ideal emerged, German households were neither "private" nor, in the modern sense, nuclear. The *Ganzes Haus* (whole household) combined production, reproduction, and consumption: peasant and artisan families "raised children and consumed goods, but they were also institutions that produced goods and drove the economy." Apprentices, servants, and journeymen inhabited the same household alongside the marital couple. Marriage was governed by property, family alliances, and state regulation of population. "The relationship between husband and wife was, legally and socially, a hierarchical one, the husband or *Hausvater*, being the head and master of the household."

### The Bourgeois Revolution in Family Form

In the late eighteenth century, a new family model emerged among bourgeois civil servants, professionals, and entrepreneurs, shaped by Enlightenment and Romantic ideas. The family was reconceived as "a space of affection, nurturance, and self-expression — a private and reproductive space separated from the world of production and money-making." Childhood itself, influenced by Rousseau, was reimagined as requiring attentive care-giving and intellectual cultivation. Marriage was to be grounded in "mutual affection and companionship" rather than property calculation.

This new model introduced a radical "polarized notion of gender difference and complementarity: the husband as protector and breadwinner; the wife in the domestic sphere taking care of the home and children." Women were conceived as "biologically fitted solely for domestic and reproductive roles within the family because of their passive, emotional, nurturing, and spiritual natures, while men possessed the rationality, independence, strength and self-control that equipped them for roles in the public sphere."

Recent social-historical research (Rebekka Habermas on the Munich Roth household, 1820s–1830s) suggests that separate spheres as daily reality came later than the ideological prescription: even among the bourgeoisie, "the lives of wife and husband were actually becoming closer and more intertwined, not rigidly separated" in the first decades of the nineteenth century. "Domestic sociability" — mixed-gender conversation and visiting — persisted as a social practice that complicated the ideological division.

### Separate Spheres and Liberal Political Culture

The separate spheres ideal was not merely domestic but political. Enlightenment claims for male rational autonomy "were implicated in the promotion of a more dynamic and self-regulating conception of civil society" and of parliamentary institutions. Women, "because of their passivity and lack of self-control, would fulfill their destinies not as autonomous agents, but as dependents, their sexuality serving the ends of reproduction and pleasing their husbands."

The linkage of masculinity with military service crystallized during the Napoleonic Wars. After Prussia's defeat in 1806–1807, the "Prussian reform movement sought to mobilize subjects to patriotic resistance against French occupation with the vision of a unified German *Volk*." Men would serve as "a 'brotherhood' of soldiers defending home, hearth, and fatherland"; women's patriotism would be "fulfilled by their maternal roles: raising German children, giving emotional sustenance, and providing charity." The introduction of universal male conscription in 1814 institutionalized this gendered division, "leveling age-old distinctions between men (class, religion, geography) while intensifying those between men and women." ## Industrialization and the Sex-Segregated Economy (c. 1850–1914)

Industrial capitalism "built upon, incorporated, and, in some ways, intensified" the gender inequalities of the separate spheres model rather than undermining them. What emerged was "a sex-segregated economy in which women were tracked into the lowest paid, lower skilled jobs, and into female sectors of industry, such as textiles, garment-making, and food processing." Women "disproportionately worked in less modern and non-unionized sectors of the economy, such as domestic service and industrial homework."

The "double burden" — simultaneous wage labor and domestic responsibility — emerged as a structural feature of working-class women's lives. Gender bias operated through multiple mechanisms: limited formal training (inheritance of the guild system); discriminatory wage practices; definitional exclusion from "skilled" labor categories (skill was "in part a social construct, the result both of employer exploitation and the demands of many male workers, whose work and family identities, as breadwinners and heads of households, were closely intertwined").

Bismarck's social insurance legislation (1880s) — disability, health, and old-age insurance — was "crafted specifically with male workers in mind. Predicated upon the norm of the male breadwinner, this form of welfare assumed the active, politically-enfranchised citizenship of its recipients." Women and children received a different form of welfare: "poor relief and protective legislation" that reinforced their dependent status. Protective labor legislation (11-hour day limits, maternity leave) "codif[ied] a sexual division of labor in the workplace and the family."

## The Women's Movement and Feminist Challenge (1860s–1914)

An organized bourgeois feminist movement emerged in the 1860s, by the turn of the century connected to a massive umbrella organization, the *Bund Deutscher Frauenvereine* (BDF). Feminists demanded improved legal status, access to higher education, better employment, and eventually the vote. Distinctively, they often made these arguments in the language of maternalism and gender difference — "precisely because of their value as caregivers and 'spiritual mothers,' women should be given expanded rights and roles outside the home." This "turn[ed] separate spheres on its head, while at the same time accommodating its notion of gender difference."

Bourgeois German feminism was, in some historians' assessment, "more conservative than its counterparts in England and the USA" — one reason being the Prussian ban on women's political activities (1850–1908). The radical wing of the movement (*Bund für Mutterschutz*) agitated for women's reproductive rights and the rights of homosexuals, single mothers, and their children.

## World War I: Transformation of Gender Relations

World War I was "the most important single event undermining separate spheres." The war transformed women's roles in three ways: (1) women entered male jobs in armaments, transportation, and postal services in large numbers as industrial labor shortages developed; (2) "many women themselves experienced a new sense of freedom, responsibility, and importance" as household heads and primary breadwinners; (3) "domesticity itself was transformed" — in harsh economic conditions, it "meant scrounging for food, waiting in long lines, rationing."

Men's gender identities were equally disrupted. Trench warfare was "a mechanized, depersonalized killing field" that offered "terror and disempowerment, the rates of 'shell shock,' a newly discovered psychiatric illness, being one symptom of that." Male bonding in *Männerbunde* (male associations) that "celebrated a militarized ideal of group loyalty as the basis for manhood" would persist after the war, shaping fascist political movements.

## Weimar Republic: Gender Crisis and the "New Woman"

The Weimar years (1918–1933) brought women's suffrage (1918), thousands of women entering political office, and the emergence of the "New Woman" — urban, independent, sexually active, androgynous in appearance. This "alleged undermining of the patriarchal family" became "a powerful symbol of the breakdown of the social fabric" and an object of intense political contestation.

The Nazis mobilized gender anxieties in building their movement, calling for "emancipation from emancipation" and the restoration of the patriarchal family. Women came late to voting for the Nazis (only after 1929 with the Great Depression); before that, "the majority of them did not vote along gender lines in the interest of women's emancipation. Instead, many voted for conservative and antifeminist parties — DNVP and Catholic Center party — whose gender messages were similar to the Nazis."

The historiographical debate over German women's contribution to Nazi electoral success is connected to the [[german-sonderweg]] argument: some historians attribute the conservatism of German feminism — "more conservative, nationalist, and maternalist relative to its counterparts in England and the USA" — as paving the way for capitulation to Nazism in 1933. More recent scholarship has contested this "Sonderweg view" by "showing both the ubiquity of maternalism in western feminism, and its empowering and emancipatory aspects."

## National Socialism: Antifeminist Backlash and Racial Eugenics

The Third Reich pursued "an extreme antifeminist backlash aimed at restoring male authority in the family and society." Nazi policies dismissed women from government posts, imposed university admissions quotas (10%), fired married female civil servants, and offered incentives for young women to leave the workforce. However, these policies were reversed with rearmament, creating chronic contradictions in Nazi gender policy.

More radically, Nazi gender and reproductive policy pursued racial eugenics that paradoxically "destroyed the private bourgeois family." For the "racially fit," pronatalist policies included crackdowns on abortion, family loans, and the *Lebensborn* program (SS breeding centers). For the "racially unfit," antinatalist policies progressed from forced sterilization to mass murder.

Gender structured experiences of persecution: Jewish women were "often more in touch with the pervasiveness of anti-Semitism in daily life" and more likely to advocate emigration, while Jewish men were more likely to be the first to emigrate due to belief they faced greater physical peril. In concentration camps, "women utilized certain gender-specific survival skills — social-networking and care-giving skills — that helped women inmates create support networks." Women arriving at Auschwitz with young children were often "sent immediately to the gas chambers, the camp authorities not wanting to deal with the problems of separating mother and child."

## Postwar Reconstruction: Divergent Paths in East and West

Both Germanies confronted severe gender disruption in 1945: "large numbers of female-headed households, their husbands and fathers having perished in the war or been incarcerated in POW camps." Mass rape of German women by Red Army soldiers, fraternization with Western occupiers, and returning traumatized soldiers produced intense "anxiety and crisis surrounding this apparent breakdown in masculinity, gender, and the family."

**West Germany** rebuilt state and civil society around "obsessive concerns about restoring the private nuclear family of traditional gender roles." Child benefit programs were designed for the norm of "the male wage earner supporting dependent wife and children." The 1957 Pension Reform Act continued to tie benefits to the wage contract, disadvantaging women workers. Wives were not granted full legal equality with husbands in civil code until 1957; a 1959 Constitutional Court ruling was required to strike down fathers' authority over children. The "normal family" became "a marker of liberal democracy, distinguishing West Germany from the authoritarian statism of both National Socialism and socialist East Germany."

**East Germany** substituted the "state-citizen" model for the family-centered citizenship of the West. State policies greatly encouraged women's workforce participation: recruitment programs, equal pay legislation, daycare facilities, expanded maternity leave. "In East Germany, in contrast to both the contemporary West and to previous eras, women did not face in the same way the need to choose between motherhood and a career." Yet "the division of labor in the East German family remained largely what it had always been: the burden fell almost exclusively on women," which continued to disadvantage them in work and politics.

## Historiography

Gender history in Germany has developed through three overlapping phases: (1) the "women's history" of the 1970s, which added women's voices to the existing record; (2) the social history of gender in the 1980s–1990s, analyzing the interconnections between gender, class, and the labor market; and (3) the "cultural history of gender" influenced by Joan Scott, treating gender as a "primary way of signifying relationships of power" that structures all historical domains, not just the lives of women.

Key debates include: the relationship between German feminism's conservatism and Nazi mobilization (the Sonderweg question); the degree to which the Nazi state's gender policies represented continuity with or rupture from the bourgeois family ideal; the relationship between women's labor force participation and welfare state design in divided Germany. The bibliography is substantial: Ute Frevert, Kathleen Canning, Karen Hagemann, and Ann Goldberg (author of the Oxford Handbook chapter) represent different phases and orientations of this scholarship.
