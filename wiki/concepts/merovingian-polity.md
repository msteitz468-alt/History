# The Merovingian Polity (Court and Administration)

**How the Frankish kingdom actually governed: a network of courts and courtiers, not a bureaucratic
state.** Yitzhak Hen's chapter (Oxford Handbook, Ch. 10) reconstructs Merovingian government as an
adaptable institution rooted in late-Roman civic ideology yet "less rigid and more adaptable to the
changing reality of late antique and early medieval Gaul," tied to local secular and ecclesiastical
elites. Its continuity across reigns — and its survival even under powerless kings — is the chapter's
central theme, reinforcing the broader case against the [[rois-faineants|"do-nothing kings"]] image.

## Definition and Scope

From the start the court was framed in Roman civic terms: Remigius of Reims's letter to
[[clovis|Clovis]] (c. 481) urged him to "summon to your side counsellors" and keep "your court open
to all." Gregory styles Clovis "consul" and "Augustus," yet the Merovingians "made no attempt to
usurp the imperial title, nor did they employ the term *sacrum*" — a deliberately **non-sacral**
kingship.

## Four Institutions

Hen identifies four loosely-defined organs: the **chancery** (*scrinia*), the **treasury**
(*fiscus*), the **high court of justice**, and the **royal council** — "none… clearly defined or
described in detail." Concretely:

- **Treasury (*fiscus*).** "Gaining control of it was of utmost importance": Fredegar marks
  [[chlothar-ii|Chlothar II]]'s 613 triumph by the kingdom falling to him "with all its treasure,"
  and [[dagobert-i|Dagobert I]]'s rise by repeated treasury seizures. Even queens "as dominant and
  obstinate as [[brunhild|Brunhild]] or [[fredegund|Fredegund]] had limited access to the *fiscus*."
- **Chancery (*scrinia*).** Only **38 original royal documents** survive, all linked to Saint-Denis;
  documents were authenticated by a *referendarius* with the royal seal (set in wax mixed with royal
  hair).
- **The king's audience (*placitum*)** was court life's central act — Chlothar II hearing petitions
  at Bonneuil (616), Dagobert riding a Burgundian judicial circuit (628) dispensing justice "rich
  and poor alike."

## Officials, Careers, and Social Mobility

Gregory lists *comites*, *domestici*, the *maior domus*, and *nutricii* as the court's core, with
"three missions: administrative and fiscal management; welfare of king and family; formation of loyal
local elite." There was no fixed *cursus honorum*: the courtier **Conda** rose from *tribunus* →
*comes* → *domesticus* → *conviva regis* across five reigns, proving the court was an institution
with infrastructure independent of any one king ("continuity was the name of the game"). Mobility ran
both up and into the Church: the goldsmith **Eligius** joined the mint under Chlothar II, became
Dagobert's closest counsellor, then bishop of Noyon; ex-courtiers "slipped effortlessly into high
ecclesiastical offices" (Desiderius → Cahors, Audoin → Rouen), bound by an epistolary "emotional
community" (see [[merovingian-epistolary-culture]]).

## The Mayor of the Palace and the Itinerant Court

At the administrative apex stood the **mayor of the palace** (*maior domus*), controlling the
*scola regis* and authority over *duces*. The **Edict of Paris (614)** had Chlothar II maintain three
separate courts with three mayors (Neustria, Austrasia, Burgundy) — "one Merovingian king, with
three distinct courts." From the mid-seventh century the [[pippinids|Pippinid]] mayors "overshadow
their kings," holding office "as if by hereditary right." Yet the court did not travel far (Reims,
Orléans, Paris, Soissons were the chief *sedes regiae*), and the king's presence "still generated
authority and legitimation" even when, as Einhard records of Childeric III, he was a figurehead. The
court was also a cultural centre: [[venantius-fortunatus|Venantius Fortunatus]] arrived for the
Sigibert–Brunhild wedding (Metz, 566), and Dagobert's "staff school" trained officers and bureaucrats
(Riché).

## Historiography

Hen's model replaces the old picture of administrative collapse with one of adaptive continuity,
explaining how the kingdom withstood minorities and regencies (see [[merovingian-queenship]]) without
disintegrating. It dovetails with [[merovingian-legal-culture]] (the polity as "a small state with
the memory of a big one") and with the bishops' parallel corporate power (see
[[merovingian-episcopate]]).
