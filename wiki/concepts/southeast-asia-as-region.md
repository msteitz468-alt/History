## Definition

"Southeast Asia" as a distinct political and geographic region is a twentieth-century construction, not an ancient or natural category. The term is both emic (adopted by regional states and organizations) and etic (imposed by outsiders as an analytical framework). Its contested character lies in the question of whether it describes a genuine cultural-historical entity or a colonial/Cold War administrative convenience that has since been naturalized.

## Origin and Early Use

The concept "emerged almost by accident from World War II." At the Quebec Conference in August 1943, the Western Allies created a separate South East Asia Command (SEAC) embracing Burma, Malaya, Sumatra, and Thailand. The Potsdam Conference (July 1945) extended SEAC's responsibility to cover the rest of the Netherlands East Indies and Indochina south of the sixteenth parallel. "This military expedient provided a cohesive framework for a region which had never previously been seen as a distinct geopolitical area."

The Chinese term "Nanyang" (Southern Ocean) had long designated the region from a Chinese perspective, but as an area of overseas Chinese settlement rather than a political entity in its own right. Kenneth Landon's 1949 book *Southeast Asia: Crossroad of Religions* used the term for an academic audience; the Journal of Southeast Asian Studies and the Southeast Asian Studies programs (Cornell, Yale, SOAS) institutionalized it in the 1950s–60s.

## Pre-Colonial Status

"No single empire had dominated the whole region in pre-colonial times." The pre-colonial region was connected by seaborne trade (see [[southeast-asian-age-of-commerce]]) and shared some structural features (Indie statecraft, port polities, wet-rice agriculture, Theravada Buddhism in the mainland belt) but was not politically unified. The colonial powers drew boundaries that frequently cut across pre-existing political and cultural relationships: the Anglo-Dutch treaties of 1824 and 1871 "separated Sumatra and the Malay peninsula," while colonial rule "smothered bitter feuds such as the traditional enmity between Burma and Thailand, or stemmed age-old developments like the southern expansion of the Vietnamese people."

## Colonial Period Reshaping

Western colonial rule paradoxically both created the preconditions for a "Southeast Asian" regional identity and prevented its political expression. By the twentieth century colonial boundaries were "clearly delineated and civil wars were over." The region acquired shared features: secular administration, modernized bureaucracy and judiciary, Western-educated elites, urban middle classes, and economies partially integrated into the world market. The Japanese "Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere" disrupted this colonial order but "proved to be more a political firebreak than a catalyst for regional cohesion."

## Post-Independence Problematic

Newly independent states were "almost invariably the successor states of former colonies," and "modern frontiers largely followed the lines laid down by the colonial powers." Working out external policy thus involved: domestic needs; continuing relationships with former colonial powers; reactions to the great powers; and evolving intra-regional relationships that "might involve both conflict and co-operation."

The concept was politically contested throughout the Cold War: communist states (Vietnam, Laos, Cambodia, and briefly Burma) rejected the ASEAN framework that claimed to speak for "Southeast Asia." ASEAN's membership was geographically incomplete until the 1990s. The Organization of the Islamic Conference and the Non-Aligned Movement offered alternative frameworks of belonging to some regional states.

## Scholarly Debates

1. **Cultural unity vs. diversity**: Do the shared features of Indie statecraft, mainland Buddhism, and Malay-world Islam constitute a genuine civilizational zone, or is "Southeast Asia" primarily defined by what it is not (China, India)? Paul Mus, O. W. Wolters, and Anthony Reid argued for genuine regional coherence; critics emphasized the diversity (Theravada/Mahayana, island/mainland, Muslim/Buddhist/Christian).

2. **Colonial construction**: Whether the category is primarily a colonial-administrative construct that distorts historical analysis is a live debate in area studies. The argument that "Southeast Asia" reproduces colonial-era administrative boundaries rather than indigenous political geographies has force, particularly given the exclusion of ethnic minorities (Karen, Hmong, Moro, Papuan) from national narratives.

3. **ASEAN's normative claim**: ASEAN effectively claimed to speak for "Southeast Asia" as an institution, but this claim was contested by the Indochinese states for most of the Cold War. The expansion of ASEAN to include Vietnam (1995), Laos and Myanmar (1997), and Cambodia (1999) made the institutional and geographic concepts roughly coextensive — but by that point the institutional character of ASEAN had also changed.

## Emic Adoption

By the late twentieth century, "Southeast Asian" identity had been substantially internalized by regional elites and state institutions. "By the end of the 1980s the countries of Southeast Asia seemed secure within their frontiers and were more concerned with economic prosperity than strident nationalism." ASEAN had helped naturalize the concept as a positive self-description, even as the cultural diversity underlying it remained enormous.

## From Miksic & Goh, *Ancient Southeast Asia* (2017)

[[miksic-goh-ancient-southeast-asia-2017|Miksic & Goh]] approach regional identity from the **deep past** rather than the 1943 SEAC coinage already documented above. They treat broad distribution of the [[mandala]] idea of government as evidence that Seasia is "more than an arbitrary geographical unit," and they stress **mainland vs insular evolutionary trajectories**: elaborate moated/ramparted permanent settlements developed mainly on the mainland (Myanmar, central/NE Thailand, E Cambodia/W Vietnam) and never in insular Seasia — linked to seasonality vs equatorial climate, storage, sedentism, and hierarchy differences. Permanent settlements with earthen ramparts/depressions appear >4,000 years ago from Myanmar to Vietnam (some "ramparts/moats" may be natural features). Demography of Classic Seasia is still based on "unexamined assumptions"; few projects sample for population estimates. Urban form comparisons: 13th-c. Hangzhou uniquely lacked agricultural land inside the wall and pioneered the "shophouse" form later characteristic of Seasia; Ibn Battuta's mid-14th-c. description of Chinese garden-cities (every inhabitant a garden/field with house in the middle) implies overseas Chinese colonies need not have diverged sharply from existing Seasian settlement patterns. Analogous_to [[southeast-asian-port-polity]]; contrasts_with purely modern-administrative definitions of the region.
