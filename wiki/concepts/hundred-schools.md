# The Hundred Schools of Thought

The traditional Chinese term for the remarkable flourishing of competing philosophical, political, and ethical traditions during the Spring and Autumn and (especially) Warring States periods of ancient China (roughly sixth through third centuries BCE), an era whose intense interstate rivalry and political fragmentation is generally understood by historians as having created unusual space and demand for itinerant philosophers offering rival visions of correct governance, social order, and cosmology to competing rulers.

The major schools traditionally grouped under this heading include Confucianism (Confucius and later Mencius and Xunzi, emphasizing ritual propriety, hierarchical social relations, and cultivated virtue), Daoism (Laozi and Zhuangzi, emphasizing accordance with the natural Way and skepticism of imposed order), Mohism (Mozi, emphasizing impartial universal love and utilitarian governance), Legalism (Han Feizi and Shang Yang, emphasizing strict law, centralized authority, and state power — ultimately adopted by the Qin state that unified China), and the School of Names (associated with logic and paradox). This period of philosophical pluralism effectively ended with the Qin unification of 221 BCE, whose Legalist-inspired state suppressed rival schools (most notoriously in the burning of books and scholars), though several traditions — Confucianism above all — were later revived and became central to subsequent Chinese imperial ideology.
