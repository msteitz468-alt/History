## Definition

Irving "Ben" Rouse developed the dominant taxonomic framework for Caribbean archaeology over more than sixty years. His approach established a time–space diagram as the primary organizational tool: time on the vertical axis (determined initially through stratigraphy and seriation, later calibrated by radiocarbon dating) and space on the horizontal axis (organized by island, island group, and water passage). Within this chart, names distinguish "peoples and cultures" classified by comparing ceramic styles and associated material traits.

The classification was made hierarchical by grouping local styles into **series** (ending in *-oid*) and dividing series into sub-regional **subseries** (ending in *-an*). Rouse's four principal series for the Caribbean Ceramic Age were:

- **Saladoid** — earliest Ceramic Age colonists moving north from South America through the Lesser Antilles
- **Ostionoid** — post-Saladoid redware tradition of the Greater Antilles
- **Meillacoid** — incised-line tradition centered on Hispaniola, spreading into Cuba, Jamaica, and the Bahamas
- **Chicoid** — late pre-contact tradition of Hispaniola associated with Classic Taíno

For the pre-ceramic period, Rouse classified the earliest assemblages as the **Casimiroid series** (Lithic Age, flaked-stone complexes) and the ground-stone assemblages as the **Ortoiroid series** (Archaic Age), naming each for type-sites.

Rouse organized the entire sequence of Caribbean prehistory into three Ages corresponding to three supposed separate migrations:
- **Lithic Age** — defined by flaked-stone tools
- **Archaic Age** — defined by ground-stone tools and formal shell tools; absence of pottery
- **Ceramic Age** — defined by pottery

He further distinguished cultural sub-groupings within the Ceramic Age Taíno-affiliated traditions: **Classic Taíno** (eastern Cuba, Hispaniola, Puerto Rico), **Western Taíno** (Jamaica, central Cuba), **Eastern Taíno** (northern Lesser Antilles, Virgin Islands), and **Lucayan Taíno** (Bahama archipelago).

Rouse's background in Linnaean biological classification (he received his bachelor's degree in plant science from Yale's Sheffield Scientific School in 1934) strongly shaped his taxonomic approach. He introduced the scientific method to Caribbean archaeology roughly thirty years before it became standard, and his systematic methodology provided the foundation for the Classificatory/Historical School of American Archaeology. He identified four sequential levels of Caribbean archaeological interpretation: artifactual research (late 18th century onward); chronological ordering (from the 1920s); culture definition and "peoples and cultures" (from the 1950s); and sociocultural research (from the 1980s — a development Rouse viewed with skepticism, believing chronology and spatial distribution had to precede sociological study).

Rouse's time–space diagram emphasizes water passages as organizing spatial units, based on the observation that archaeological complexes facing each other across water passages are often more similar than those at opposite ends of the same island. Pottery — comprising up to 90% of artifacts at most Ceramic Age sites — served as the primary diagnostic material.

## Contested Aspects

The framework faces fundamental critiques, organized around three principal problems:

**1. Calibrated radiocarbon dating destabilizes the chronology.** Rouse's original chronologies were built on cross-dating before radiocarbon was widely used. When Dave D. Davis calibrated radiocarbon dates from Antigua, he found that assemblages previously arranged in discrete chronological sequence overlapped substantially. Similar problems have been documented for Haiti, Puerto Rico, and other islands. Uncorrected dates on marine shells are often 400 years too old; calibrated dates between AD 750–950 can be offset by more than a century. The "hard-and-fast" temporal lines between series are an artifact of these dating problems.

**2. The "three Ages = three migrations" model does not fit the evidence.** Keegan and Hofman argue that flaked-stone tools are a significant component of Archaic and Ceramic Age sites throughout the region, and that pottery is present in all three "Ages." The boundaries between Ages are "becoming increasingly hazy." Archaic Age pottery (the "Pre-Arawak Pottery Horizon") has been documented as early as 2600 BCE at multiple sites across Cuba, Hispaniola, Puerto Rico, and the Virgin Islands — collapsing the clean division between Archaic (aceramic) and Ceramic Ages. Starch-grain analysis has further shown that cultigens traditionally associated with Ceramic Age introductions were already being cultivated during the Archaic Age.

**3. Broad series names mask diversity and impose false homogeneity.** The assumption that pottery series represent discrete "peoples and cultures" with shared language and culture is challenged by the linguistic evidence: Bartolomé de las Casas reported three mutually unintelligible languages spoken on Hispaniola alone. The name "Taíno" implies a homogeneity that ethnohistoric and material evidence does not support. The linear genealogy "Saladoid begat Ostionoid begat Meillacoid begat Chicoid" does not explain cases such as the simultaneous appearance of Ostionoid redware in Jamaica and Puerto Rico, which contradicts the model of Puerto Rico as the exclusive origin point.

Rouse never excavated a stratified site and had to rely on potsherds rather than whole vessels to evaluate cultural change through time — a significant methodological constraint acknowledged by his colleagues.

## Historiography

Keegan and Hofman (2017) identify two main prior approaches: the Rousean classification framework dominant from a North American perspective, and a second approach emphasizing *modo de vida* (mode of life) developed by Cuban, Dominican, and Venezuelan archaeologists working within a Marxist historical-materialist framework. Both approaches have "reached the limits of their usefulness."

They propose a third approach — a "kaleidoscope" or chaos model — that emphasizes diversity and variability rather than classification into fixed boxes. Under this model, nothing was ever exactly the same at any time or place; indigenous populations designated Lithic Age and Archaic Age were not necessarily culturally distinct but used different means to achieve comparable standards of living. Pottery series (Saladoid, Huecoid, Ostionoid, Meillacoid, Troumassoid, Suazoid, Chicoid) are retained as communicative shorthand but are understood to represent socially distributed identities distributed across large geographical areas, not monolithic cultural entities or distinct migrating peoples.

The dominance of English-speaking archaeologists has skewed the synthesis: scholarship published in Spanish and French received far less consideration, particularly work by Cuban and Dominican archaeologists whose detailed site studies form much of the primary evidence for the Lithic and Archaic Ages.

The framework remains the baseline against which all Caribbean archaeological interpretation is calibrated; no comprehensive alternative taxonomy has replaced it, even as its underlying migration model has been substantially rejected.
