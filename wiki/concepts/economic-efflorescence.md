# Economic Efflorescence

A period of **sustained but ultimately reversible premodern economic growth** — in which
both aggregate output and per-capita consumption rise slowly over centuries, only
eventually to stagnate or decline — as distinct from the self-sustaining, compounding
growth of the modern industrial economy. The term (Jack **Goldstone**) provides a
vocabulary for growth that is real yet bounded, avoiding both the "no growth" primitivism
of the Finley school and the anachronistic "takeoff" language of modernism.

## Application to Antiquity

The editors of the [[scheidel-cambridge-economic-history-greco-roman-world-2007|Cambridge
Economic History of the Greco-Roman World]] argue that classical antiquity saw "one of the
strongest economic efflorescences in premodern history": Mediterranean population roughly
doubled (c. 20M in 800 BC → c. 40M by AD 200), the largest cities grew far faster (Athens
~40,000; Alexandria ~300,000; Rome ~1,000,000), per-capita consumption rose 25–50%, and
monetization, urbanization, and staple trade all expanded. Growth rates were on the order
of **0.05–0.1% per annum** — two orders of magnitude below modern expectations, but
unprecedented for the ancient world and unmatched again for centuries.

The concept links antiquity to a comparative research program (E. A. Wrigley's "advanced
organic economy," premodern efflorescences in Song China and early-modern England) aimed
at isolating which variables produced the northwest-European industrial breakthrough and
which were already present in — but insufficient within — earlier efflorescences.

## From Mitchell, Later Roman Empire (2015)

Source: [[mitchell-later-roman-empire-2015]].

- **Late antique regional efflorescences.** Mitchell documents multiple late-antique regional efflorescences in the secure East and sheltered Africa: Syrian limestone villages and Negev irrigation settlements; S Asia Minor coastal/Cilician commercial boom; Egyptian estate economy feeding Constantinople; African ARS/oil export system; fifth-century Antioch’s material peak. These are not empire-wide “growth” but security-conditioned, *annona*-linked, and regionally uneven—compatible with transformation rather than uniform decline until mid/late sixth-century shocks. “During long periods of peacetime, urban culture thrived, the population rose both in the cities and in the countryside, and the economy benefited from long-term stability… Africa established itself as the economic powerhouse of the late Roman western Mediterranean.”
