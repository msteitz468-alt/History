## Definition and Scope

Austronesian historical linguistics treats linguistic units — words, affixes, phonemes, place-names, personal names — as artefacts: "an artificial sequence of sounds with conventional meaning and use." Like an archaeologist measuring a pottery sherd, "historical linguists use sophisticated and proven techniques to note the structure of such words, their content (meaning) and context, and then attempt to discover their origin, composition, function, and time depth."

The field reconstructs protolanguages at multiple levels — Proto Austronesian (PAn), Proto Oceanic (POc), Proto Central Pacific (PCP), Proto Polynesian (PPn) — and uses both reconstruction and borrowing detection to infer prehistoric contact, migration, and the introduction of new technologies and plants into [[oceania|the Pacific]].

Source: [[jones-matsuda-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v1-2022]], ch. 11 (Geraghty).

## The Austronesian Language Family

The Austronesian family comprises the languages of island Southeast Asia, the Pacific Islands, and Madagascar. Its reconstructed ancestor, **Proto Austronesian (PAn)**, is believed to have been spoken in Taiwan "around 5,000 years ago by ancestors of the speakers of all Austronesian languages." Robert Blust's *Austronesian Comparative Dictionary* (in progress) is the standard reference for PAn reconstruction.

### Hierarchical Reconstruction Levels

| Proto-Language | Approximate Date | Region | Key Reference Work |
|---|---|---|---|
| Proto Austronesian (PAn) | ~5,000 BP | Taiwan | Blust & Trussel (in progress) |
| Proto Oceanic (POc) | ~3,300 BP | Bismarck Archipelago | Ross, Pawley & Osmond (5 vols., 1998–2016) |
| Proto Central Pacific (PCP) | ~3,000 BP | Fiji region | Geraghty, Pawley (various) |
| Proto Polynesian (PPn) | ~2,800–2,600 BP | West Polynesia (Tonga/Samoa region) | POLLEX (Biggs, Clark, Greenhill) |

POc is "the putative language of the earlier Lapita people who are believed to have arrived in the Bismarck Archipelago some 3,300 years ago." PCP is reconstructable from Fijian, Rotuman, and Polynesian languages; it "was probably spoken by their ancestors — the 'Lapita' people, believed to have been the first settlers of Fiji some 3,000 years ago."

PPn reconstructions are compiled in **POLLEX**, an ongoing project initiated by Bruce Biggs in Auckland.

## Reconstruction: Value and Limits

The Comparative Method reconstructs putative ancestral words (prefixed by asterisk) by comparing cognates across daughter languages and identifying regular sound correspondences. The method is valuable but carries significant interpretive risks when applied to cultural history:

### What Reconstruction Can Show
- Regular sound changes linking contemporary languages to a parent.
- That certain basic vocabulary (e.g., PPn *rua 'two', *vale 'house') existed in the protolanguage.
- Subgrouping structure (e.g., Tongic vs. Nuclear Polynesian divide).

### What Reconstruction Cannot Reliably Show

Reconstruction lists "tend, of necessity, to err on the side of inclusion, so contain problematic reconstructions, and are not always honest in telling the user so." Irregular reflexes — indicating possible later borrowing rather than direct inheritance — are "seriously underused" as flags. The safest interpretive stance for non-basic vocabulary is: "if the Proto Polynesians had boxes, they may have called them *pusa'" — not that they necessarily had boxes.

Documented hazards include:
- **PPn *tusi 'book'** — probable missionary coinage from 'mark with colour', spread by nineteenth-century translators.
- **PPn *faɂo 'nail'** — earlier meaning 'wooden peg'.
- **Words for metals, iron, gold, silver** reconstructible to Proto Austronesian despite speakers having had no knowledge of them, because post-PAn borrowing spread the terms through western Austronesian languages (Mahdi's "maverick protoforms").
- **PPn *faasua 'giant clam'**, ***gasau 'arrow'**, ***saka 'boil food'** — borrowed from Fiji into Polynesian, not inherited Proto Polynesian.
- **PPn *mei 'breadfruit'**, ***pulaka 'swamp taro'** — borrowed from Micronesia.
- **PPn *pusa 'box'**, possibly ***kumete 'wooden bowl'** — probable Dutch loanwords.

The conclusion is not that reconstruction is worthless but that "reconstructions be used with caution" and that detecting borrowings is often more historically productive than pure reconstruction "because its conclusions are usually more secure — a spurious loanword is rarer than a spurious reconstruction."

## Borrowing: Detection and Significance

Borrowing (also termed 'copying', 'loaning', 'horizontal transfer') is the process by which words pass between languages, regardless of genetic relationship. It can obliterate ancestral vocabulary entirely (as in Niuatoputapu, where Tongan borrowing displaced the original language completely) or leave "indirect inheritance" traces that mimic direct descent.

### The 'Embeddedness' Test

The primary criterion for identifying a loanword is *embeddedness*: "the strongest argument that a word was borrowed from language A to language B is that the words sound and mean the same, and the word is more strongly embedded in language A than in language B." Embeddedness is assessed phonologically, etymologically, and morphologically:

- **Phonological embeddedness**: loanwords contain sound patterns inconsistent with the borrowing language's historical changes. When sound correspondences between two languages are well understood, "etymological borrowing" (where speakers apply known correspondence rules) can make loans appear native — a key dating device.
- **Etymological embeddedness**: if a word has a clear internal etymology in language A but none in B, it originated in A.
- **Morphological embeddedness**: if a word is morphologically analysable (decomposable into meaningful parts) only in one language — e.g., Fijian *ivāvādā* ('instrument to facilitate stepping') vs. borrowed Samoan *vavata* with no analysable meaning — the language with internal structure is the source.

### Recording Accuracy and Phonemic Precision

A prerequisite for all linguistic historical work is accurate transcription. Many eighteenth- to twentieth-century Pacific wordlists fail to mark **vowel length** and **glottal stops**, both phonemic in most Pacific languages. The word spelled *ua* could be any of sixteen distinct Polynesian words depending on length and glottalization. Failure to record these features "has led to false claims of relatedness" — for example, falsely connecting Samoan *faɂi* 'plantain' with Tahitian *fēɂī* (different vowel lengths) or misidentifying Samoan *lātū* (with both vowels long, a Fijian loan) as a reflex of PPn *fatu 'chief'.

## Compound Artefacts and Onomastics

Beyond reconstruction, two underused tools are:

### Compound Words and Semantic Extension
Compound names reveal prehistoric contacts and introductions. Examples:
- Fijian *jaina* < *vudi ni Jaina* 'banana from China' — origin encoding.
- Fijian *banidakai* 'fence for guns' for the physic nut *Jatropha curcas* — the plant was introduced as a bullet-defence hedge.
- West Polynesian *ukamea* 'iron' = probably *uka* 'cord' + *mea* 'reddish' — earliest metal known was likely copper wire.

### Onomastics (Place-Names and Personal Names)
Place-names in the Central and Eastern Pacific reveal directional naming conventions (winds, sun positions) rather than the European "commemorative" tradition assumed by earlier scholars:
- *Tokelau* = 'north wind'; *Toga/Tonga* = 'south wind'; *Viti/Tahiti* = '*sunrise/east*'.
- Suffixation strategies: *-a* meaning 'place of' or 'abundance of' (e.g., *Niua* < *niu* 'coconut' + *-a*); *-(C)aga* similarly.
- Settlement in East Polynesian outliers may have resulted from navigational mistakes, not deliberate commemoration — Tikopia and Anuta named after Cikobia and Yanuca in northeast Fiji, suggesting their discoverers believed they had arrived at known Fijian islands.

Personal names frequently record technology introductions (Wālesi names in Fiji mark arrival of wireless radio; Tānoa name for kava bowl records its introduction from Tonga ~250 years ago, "supported by non-linguistic evidence").

## Linguistic Chronology of Pacific Prehistory

### Near Oceanic Substrates in Austronesian Languages
Proto Oceanic "borrowed a number of features from non-Austronesian languages." Evidence of Papuan substrate influence appears in four areas of eastern Melanesia (Santa Cruz group, western Vanuatu, southern Vanuatu, New Caledonia and the Loyalties) in the form of: "extensive lexical replacement, verb serialization and quinary numeral systems, labio-velar consonants, and accretion of articles and subject markers." In New Caledonia, possible Australian substrate is also proposed: 20,000 years ago the landmass was accessible from Australia with no sea gap greater than 30 km, and species names in New Caledonia appear to have been borrowed from an extinct language rather than formed by semantic extension.

Fiji also shows evidence of an Oceanic substrate in the form of "competing reconstructions" and "dual reflexes" — forms that cannot derive from a single Proto Central Pacific phoneme — most likely from Oceanic languages spoken there before the current Fijian languages were established.

### Proto Polynesian Origins in Eastern Fiji
The Polynesian languages were originally closely related to Tokalau Fijian (eastern Fiji): "it is well established that Proto Polynesian divided into Tongic and Nuclear Polynesian, but this may have begun in Fiji rather than Western Polynesia, since there is evidence from place-names that this major division in Polynesian languages was already present in Fiji." Lau place-names preserve reflexes of the Tongic sound change *r > ∅ and the Nuclear Polynesian change *c > ∅ that are otherwise not found in Fiji.

### First Millennium BP: Borrowings and the 'Tongan Empire'
Beginning ~1,000 BP, West Polynesians extended hegemony across much of the Pacific, leaving loanword traces "as far away as Kiribati, Pohnpei, Mokil, the eastern Solomon Islands, Vanuatu, the Loyalty Islands, and New Caledonia." Geraghty lists fifty-four Kiribati nautical terms of which twenty to twenty-five are Polynesian loans, "mostly from a Nuclear Polynesian source rather than Tongan." Fijian loanwords in Polynesian — notably war vocabulary (*kolo* < Fij *koro* 'fort', *bakola* 'dead body of enemy', *gasau* 'arrow') — reflect the spread of warfare from Fiji to West Polynesia following the kula (red feather) trade conflict.

### Recent Prehistory: Shunting Technology
The sailing technique of shunting (tacking by moving the sail to the other end of the canoe) spread from Micronesia eastward through Fiji to West Polynesia. Linguistic evidence: Fijian *ivāvādā* (morphologically analysable: 'instrument to facilitate stepping') was borrowed into Samoan as *vavata* (opaque), indicating Fiji as the source. Tongan nautical terminology shows at least ten clear Fijian loanwords in sailing construction, with no Tongan loans in Fijian nautical vocabulary.

### Agricultural Introductions
Two PPn words for 'breadfruit' (*kulu* and *mei*) indicate one is a loanword; *mei* traces to Proto Micronesian *mai* and was probably introduced along with new salt- and storm-tolerant varieties hybridized in the eastern Carolines ~1,000 BP. Similarly, PPn *pulaka* 'giant swamp taro' was borrowed from Micronesian *pwulaka* (Proto Chuukic, itself from Palauan *brak*). The word *vesi* for the tree *Intsia bijuga* in Fiji may ultimately derive from Malay *besi* 'iron' (ironwood), itself from Sanskrit *vāśī* 'sharp-pointed adze' — "the first known instance of the prehistoric transfer of a word from India to the Central Pacific."

## What Linguistics Cannot Do

The chapter's central argument: linguists' contribution to Pacific prehistory has been undervalued by archaeologists who default to genetics while ignoring "relevant research by linguists, historians, and ethnomusicologists." At the same time, linguists caution against "overliteral interpretations of the protolexicon" (Sutton) and the uncritical extraction of cultural history from reconstruction lists without verifying embeddedness, checking for borrowing, and accurately transcribing phonemic distinctions.

## Historiography

Linguistic evidence for Pacific prehistory was used from first European contact but fell out of fashion when archaeology appeared to offer more "scientific certainty." A "modest resurgence" has occurred in recent decades through "collaborative studies of 'culture history'" (Kirch and Green). Key methodological debates: (a) whether proto-language reconstructions reliably attest to material culture, (b) how to detect and date borrowing, (c) how to use onomastics without the excesses of "extreme diffusionism." The Biggs warning — "if we want to use the other fellow's last we had better know exactly what it is good for" — remains the field's methodological watchword.
