## Definition

The **Seleucid era** (Greek *Seleukis*; later Syriac "era of the Greeks" / *anno
Graecorum*; Babylonian reckoning from the accession of Seleucus) is a continuous
linear count of years dated from [[seleucus-i-nicator|Seleucus I's]] recovery of
Babylon in **312/311 BCE** — the event from which his independent power, and his
later kingdom, was reckoned to begin. It is the **first era in history to count years
continuously from a fixed epoch** rather than restarting with each king's reign or by
local eponymous magistrates.

## Significance

This was a conceptual innovation of the first order. Earlier Near Eastern and Greek
dating restarted at every reign (regnal years) or rotated through annual magistrates
(Athenian archons, Roman consuls) and Olympiads — systems that fragment the past and
demand king-lists to convert. A fixed epoch counting forward indefinitely made dates
directly comparable across reigns and regions, and (uniquely for the ancient world)
gave the [[seleucid-empire|Seleucid kingdom]] a unified chronological spine usable
from Mesopotamia to the Mediterranean. The model would later be imitated by other
Hellenistic and Near Eastern eras and is a conceptual ancestor of the continuous
*anno Domini* count.

Because it was used in **Babylonian cuneiform king-lists and chronicles** (one such
king-list published only in 1974), the Seleucid era also gives modern historians an
unusually firm chronological anchor for Seleucid history, partially offsetting the
failure of the Greek literary tradition to provide a secure chronology for the third
century (see [[hellenistic-historiography]]).

## Note on Precision

Because the Babylonian and Macedonian calendars set the year's start at different
points, the era has two slightly offset reckonings (epochs in spring 311 and autumn
312 respectively), which is why Seleucid-era dates are conventionally written as a
split year (e.g. 312/311). It long outlived the dynasty, remaining in use among
Syriac Christians and others well into the medieval period.
