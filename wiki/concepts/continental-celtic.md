# Continental Celtic

Continental Celtic is the modern linguistic classification for the branch of Celtic languages once spoken across mainland Europe, comprising Gaulish (Gaul, modern France/Belgium/parts of Switzerland and Germany), Celtiberian (central Iberia), Lepontic (northern Italy), and Galatian (spoken by the Celtic Galatians who settled central Anatolia in the 3rd century BCE). All Continental Celtic languages were extinct by the early medieval period, having been progressively displaced by Latin following Roman conquest and, in Anatolia, by Greek. They survive only fragmentarily in inscriptions, coin legends, and place names, in contrast to the **Insular Celtic** branch (Goidelic and Brythonic languages), which survived as spoken vernaculars in Ireland and Britain into the modern era and includes Irish, Scottish Gaelic, Welsh, Cornish, and Breton.

## Emic and Etic

The Continental/Insular division is a purely etic, modern comparative-linguistic classification; no ancient Celtic-speaking population would have recognized or used such a category.

## Debate

Historical linguists have long debated whether the Continental/Insular split reflects a genuine early linguistic divergence within Celtic or is primarily an artifact of which languages happened to leave enough evidence, and survive long enough, to be classified at all; the geographically-based P-Celtic/Q-Celtic classification is a partially overlapping but analytically distinct scheme.
