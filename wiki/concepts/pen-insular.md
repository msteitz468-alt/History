# Pen/Insular

**"Pen/Insular"** (and the plural **"Pen/Insulae"**) is [[barnes-archaeology-east-asia-2015|Gina
Barnes's]] deliberately coined shorthand for the combined area of the Korean **Pen**insula and
the **Insular** Japanese archipelago, treated as a single analytical field distinct from, and
developmentally "downstream" of, the China Mainland. In her usage:

- capitalised **"Peninsula"** is reserved for Korea specifically;
- **"continental"** embraces both the China Mainland *and* the Korean Peninsula (Korea is
  physically continental, unlike Japan);
- **"Pen/Insular"** groups Korea + Japan when their shared, lagged trajectory is the point.

The term is a tool for stating one of the book's central comparative claims compactly: that Korea
and Japan underwent the **Neolithic-to-state transition together and late**, as
[[pen-insular-state-formation|secondary states]] formed through contact with already-operating
Mainland polities, and that the two shores of the Korea Strait should be read as one connected
zone (see the [[yellow-sea-interaction-sphere|Yellow Sea Interaction Sphere]]).

## Why It Matters Analytically

Barnes uses "Pen/Insular" to license a string of structural contrasts that recur through the
book:

- **No indigenous chiefdoms in the Pen/Insulae during the Mid-Holocene**, when feasting- and
  shaman-based ranking was already emerging on the Mainland — despite the Pen/Insular
  [[jomon-culture|Jomon]] possessing elaborate exchange networks and material culture.
- **A shared package crossing the strait**: wet-rice technology, dolmen/cist burial, ditched
  settlements and Songguk-ri-type pit-buildings move from the southwestern Peninsula
  ([[mumun-culture|Mumun]]) into Final Jomon North Kyushu, producing the [[yayoi-culture|Yayoi]]
  by "bilateral acculturation."
- **A common deep prehistory of glacial land-bridges**: Barnes's allied coinages "East Asialand,"
  "Yellow Plain," and "Seto Plain" name the landmasses exposed at low sea-stand that made early
  human movement into Korea and Japan possible.

## Status

The terminology is Barnes's own and is not standard across the field, so it should be
attributed to her; but the underlying comparative move — refusing to silo Korean and Japanese
archaeology — is widely shared. It is an *etic* device, useful precisely because the modern
national categories (Korea, Japan) are anachronistic for the periods it covers.
