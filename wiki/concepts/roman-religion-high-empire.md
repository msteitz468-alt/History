# Religion under the High Empire

The religious life of the Roman world in the first two centuries CE — its vigorous traditional
public cult, the omnipresent imperial cult, the romanization and hellenization of provincial
religion, the spread of the "oriental" mystery cults, and the slow emergence of a **"new mood"**
that would make the Christian future intelligible. Treated in [[bowman-cah-v11-2000|CAH XI]] Ch. 35
(**Wolf Liebeschuetz**), written, he warns, **with hindsight** to foreground what mattered for
Christianity — but insisting the age was itself **conservative, prosperous, and backward-looking**.

## The empire as the engine of religious change

Liebeschuetz's organizing thesis is that **the fact of the Roman empire itself** was the chief cause
of religious change. In the West it spread **Roman cults** through colonies, veterans, soldiers, and
administrators (**romanization**); in the East it accelerated **hellenization**, the diffusion of
Greek language, culture, and religion. The amalgamation of cities and peoples into one vast unit
favored **syncretism** and the movement of cults far from home — the so-called **"diaspora
religions."** The evidence is overwhelmingly **inscriptions** (from village and city alike)
supplemented by literature and archaeology; the best-documented eastern sanctuary is **Zeus
Panamaros** near Stratonicea.

## Public religion and the imperial cult, dynasty by dynasty

Traditional **public religion** and the **imperial cult** remained the framework of civic life, and
emperors used religion conservatively to legitimate their rule:

- **Vespasian** authenticated the new dynasty with **oracles, healing miracles** (the Alexandrian
  cures), and **temple-building** (the Temple of Peace). The Flavians cultivated **deification**;
  **Domitian** pushed furthest with **"dominus et deus"** ("lord and god").
- **Trajan** presented himself as **Jupiter's representative on earth** and assimilated himself to
  **Hercules**. His **reply to Pliny** on the Christians of Bithynia set the legal frame: Christians
  were not to be sought out, but if denounced and convicted, were to be executed **for the name** —
  for obstinate refusal to sacrifice to the gods and the emperor.
- **Hadrian** embodied a **cosmopolitan Hellenism**, building the back-to-back **Temple of Venus and
  Roma** and lavishing benefactions on Greek sanctuaries and oracles (Didyma, Claros).
- **Marcus Aurelius** held a **remote, impersonal Stoic divinity** (the rational world-soul), yet was
  initiated at **Eleusis** and credited with the **"rain miracle"** of the Marcomannic wars.
- **Commodus** embraced **eastern cults** and styled himself **Hercules**.

The **imperial cult** was, in most cities, prominent and woven into civic life; temple-building for
emperors climaxed c. AD 80–140 and then declined — though the **cult itself did not**, its festivals
remaining a fixed part of civic routine.

## Provincial religion and romanization

Beneath the imperial frame, provincial religion was reshaped by Rome and Greece:

- In **Gaul and Britain**, native cult was **monumentalized** (Romano-Celtic temples) and gods
  paired with Roman ones by **interpretatio Romana**; Celtic features (divine triads, the *genii
  cucullati*) persisted under classical forms.
- On the **Danube**, settlers and soldiers spread Roman cult; distinctive cults flourished —
  Pannonian **Silvanus**, the anonymous **Danubian rider gods**.
- In **North Africa**, the Punic **Ba'al Hammon** became **Saturn**, the supreme regional god; the
  ancient practice of child sacrifice gave way to the **molchomor** (substitute-lamb) offering and
  then lapsed.
- In the **East**, **hellenization** romanized and Graecized native gods (Men, the great Syrian
  city-gods; Baalbek's triad as **Jupiter, Venus, and Mercury**), while ancient non-Greek traditions
  — Egyptian temple ritual, Iranian Zoroastrianism, and above all **Judaism** — remained live.
  Imperial support made **civic cults prosper as never before** in the first and second centuries.

## The "oriental" and mystery cults

The cults of foreign gods — important **out of proportion to their numbers** as harbingers of the
new religiosity:

- **Cybele** (the Magna Mater) and her consort Attis, with the bloody **taurobolium** (bull-blood
  baptism).
- **Isis** — the most **polyonymous** deity of all (the aretalogy in which she claims all names), with
  elaborate ceremonies, daily liturgy, and personal initiation (Apuleius' *Metamorphoses*). Egyptian
  cults expanded above all in the **West** (ports and river-routes, the Danube via north-Italian
  settlers), peaking and then declining across the second century.
- **Mithras** — the one eastern cult that both **expanded and likely originated** in this period.
  Despite the Persian name, its relation to Iranian religion is remote; it looks **invented** by a
  single founder working at Rome. Its small underground **Mithraea**, dominated by the
  **tauroctony** (Mithras slaying the bull), held communal meals and **seven grades of initiation**
  (the soul's ascent through the planetary spheres). Mithras was above all the god of **fides** —
  oaths and loyalty — which suited soldiers and officials; but it was a **men-only "religious society
  rather than a religion,"** private, socially humble, spread by soldiers and customs-officials from
  Rome and Ostia to the frontiers (Britain, Germany, the Balkans).

## The "new mood": henotheism, morality, and the inner soul

Liebeschuetz's most important argument: beneath this diversity a **common mood** was forming, in
several converging trends —

1. **Toward one supreme god.** A philosophical, henotheistic view (an "ultimate controller") reached
   a far wider public than before — through Cynic preachers, **theological oracles** (Apollo of
   Claros proclaiming "the self-generated one god" at Oinoanda), and the imperial monarchy's own
   model of single rule. The polyonymous invocation of gods (Zeus-Helios-Serapis, Sol Invictus
   Mithras) was **henotheism, not monotheism**, but it tended to undermine polytheism.
2. **Religion fused with morality.** Increasingly the gods were thought to demand **moral conduct**,
   not just correct ritual — a concern central to the philosophers (Seneca, Musonius, Epictetus,
   Marcus' *Meditations*) and to humbler worshippers (the "confession inscriptions" of Phrygia and
   Lydia, where gods police village justice).
3. **The divine soul and personal salvation.** A growing belief in the **divine origin of the soul**
   strengthened hope for its **survival** and bred ritual (initiation) to purify it; it strained the
   central rite of **blood sacrifice** (criticized as unworthy of a spiritual god — Demonax,
   Apollonius of Tyana), foreshadowing the fourth-century suppression of sacrifice.
4. **Direct access to the divine.** A tendency to **bypass civic priesthoods** — healing visions
   (Aelius Aristides' *Sacred Tales*), **holy men** (Apollonius of Tyana), **astrology** and **magic**
   serviced by professional experts, and revealed **books** (the **Chaldaean Oracles**, the
   **Hermetic** writings) claiming more-than-human authority — accustomed people to a **book-based,
   individualist religion** and made the Christian Bible easier to accept.
5. **Demons and the problem of evil.** The transcendent god needed **intermediaries**; the
   moralization of the gods bred a proliferation of **evil demons** (an idea owed largely to
   **Judaism**) to explain the world's wickedness — and the casting-out of demons (exorcism).

**Christianity combined** all these strands — revealed texts, theology, moral purity, and above all
**confident salvation of the soul** — and grew through the period, though civic paganism and
Christianity long flourished side by side; only the **third-century crisis** dealt civic religion the
blow from which it never recovered.

## Death, burial, and the valuation of the individual

A concrete index of the shift: from the mid-first century, burial changed from **cremation to
inhumation**, and the wealthy turned to elaborately carved **marble sarcophagi**. Their mythological
imagery (Dionysus and the sleeping Ariadne; Hercules, Endymion) hints — ambiguously — at hope of
survival, and the **private, temple-like** mausoleum and the god-statue given the deceased's features
mark a striking **new valuation of the private individual** over civic merit, with serious long-run
implications for so civic a religion as classical paganism.

## Significance and the conservative counter-current

Liebeschuetz balances his teleology with a corrective: the age was **conservative and
backward-looking**, and the most quantitatively impressive fact is the **sheer volume of traditional
religious activity** — feasts, processions, games, theatricals ("there was no entertainment that was
not religious"), no longer to be dismissed as cold or impersonal. A creative tradition of
**scepticism** ran alongside (Juvenal, Lucian's mockery, Sextus Empiricus' agnosticism, Pliny's
naturalism, Marcus' bleak doubt) — material Augustine would later turn against paganism itself. The
High Empire was thus **simultaneously** the high-water mark of monumental civic paganism **and** the
seedbed of the inward, moralized, salvation-seeking religiosity that Christianity would inherit.
