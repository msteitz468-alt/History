## Definition and Scope

**Goryō** (御霊) and **onryō** (怨霊) are two related but distinct categories of dangerous spirit belief that shaped ritual life, political culture, and popular religion throughout the Nara and Heian periods. Both concerned the disruptive power of the dead, but differed in mechanism and source:

- A **goryō** originally denoted the spirit of a departed person, but came to denote any disruptive force — whether the spirit of a wronged aristocrat or a cosmic power — held responsible for epidemics, fires, droughts, and other calamities.
- An **onryō** was specifically the **vengeful spirit of a deceased person** forced to an unjust death, whose passion prevented normal Buddhist rebirth and caused them to remain in an active state of wrath (*tatari*), manifesting as disease, earthquake, or disaster.

The key distinction: "an onryō is always the vengeful spirit of a deceased person, whereas a goryō may be a force of another nature" (CHJ, ch. 8).

## Historical Origins

The goryō and onryō traditions had Chinese precedents, related to Han-dynasty beliefs about the relationship between unjust deaths, cosmic imbalance, and retributive natural disaster. In Japan the two traditions developed together through:
- Interactions between Buddhism and kami worship
- The dialectic between "nature" and "culture" (the belief that human ethical behavior acted as an agent of cosmic change)
- Interactions between aristocratic political culture and popular religion

The main onryō cults originated:
- **Imperial Prince Nagaya** (d. 729) — first recorded case
- **Fujiwara no Hirotsugu** (d. 740)
- **Imperial Prince Sawara** ("Emperor Sudo") (d. 785)
- **Imperial Prince Iyo** (d. 807)
- **Tachibana no Hayanari** (d. 842)
- **Sugawara no Michizane** (d. 903) — the culminating and most famous case

## The Gion Festival and Summer Rites

The most important goryō cult originated at the Gion shrine-temple multiplex in 876, centered on the deity Gozu Tennō ("Bull-headed king of the devas"). Festive rituals aimed at pacifying disruptive forces took place in summer — a season prone to epidemics. By 1013 these had developed into the famous Gion Festival of Kyoto, featuring elaborate processions with decorated floats, popular dances, and music.

## The Kitano Cult: Sugawara no Michizane

The onryō of **Sugawara no Michizane** (845–903) became the most famous and historically consequential vengeful spirit in the Japanese tradition. Michizane, Minister of the Right, was accused by the Fujiwara of plotting against the throne, swiftly exiled to Kyushu, and died there in 903.

The sequence of retributive events was striking:
- Fujiwara no Tokihira (who had falsely accused him) died in 909
- Tokihira's nephew Yasuakira, named crown prince, died young in 923
- The court pardoned Michizane posthumously, restoring his rank — but calamities continued
- In 930, a lightning bolt struck the imperial palace, killing three courtiers; Emperor Daigo fell into despair and died
- Michizane's spirit became associated with lightning, linking to ancient agricultural thunder-cults

The thaumaturgist Nichizō claimed in 941 to have visited the hells in catalepsy and encountered Michizane's spirit, surrounded by demons, demanding a proper cult in exchange for protecting the state. A shrine was established at Kitano in 947. Michizane was eventually enshrined as *Temman Daijizai Tenjin*, with associations drawn from both Daiitoku (a wrathful Buddhist manifestation) and Mahesvara (the bull-riding Supreme Lord of the Buddhist pantheon).

The Kitano phenomenon illustrated the political dimension of onryō belief: "what needed to be pacified were not only the spirit of Michizane but also the worries of the aristocrats and the fears of the populace as well" (CHJ, ch. 8). Overt political criticism being impossible for commoners, the cult provided a framework for oblique popular judgment on Fujiwara manipulation of the court. Michizane was eventually recast as the patron of poetry and learning; his cult continues to the present day.

## Ritual Responses: Exorcism and Pacification

Onryō spirits were addressed through:
- **Chōbuku-hō** ("rite to control and vanquish"): exorcism rituals that manipulated and pacified spirits prevented by the force of their passion from rebirth
- **Kasai matsuri** ("calamity rites"): rites performed at the site of fires and disasters, linked to yin-yang specialists (*onmyōji*)
- **Goryo-e** (festive propitiation): summer festivals including the Gion Festival, involving processions, dances, and music that pacified disruptive forces through popular celebration

## Relationship to Political Instability

The CHJ notes a direct correlation between recorded ominous events and political turbulence. From Emperor Heizei to Emperor Go-Sanjō (806–1073), 1,686 ominous occurrences were officially recorded: 653 earthquakes, 134 fires, 91 epidemic outbreaks, 356 calamitous heavenly events, and 367 "ghostly events." The peak of ominous events coincided with the rise of Fujiwara regency power under Mototsune. The logic was clear: "people claimed that natural disasters impacting their own lives were a manifestation of discontent on the part of the very same Heaven" that the court claimed to represent.

Thaumaturgists — Buddhist monks with esoteric ritual training, often experienced in mountain asceticism (*shugendo*) — mediated between the aristocracy's guilty conscience, popular fear, and the spirits themselves.

## Historiography

The goryō/onryō complex has been studied primarily in the context of:
- Political history of the Fujiwara regency
- History of popular religion vs. state religion in Japan
- The concept of *mappo* (the latter day of the law) and eschatological anxiety

Key secondary literature: Murayama Shuichi, *Honji suijaku* (1974); Kuroda Toshio, "Chinkon no keifu" (1990); Robert Borgen, *Sugawara no Michizane and the Early Heian Court* (1986).
