# Great Rhetra

The **Great Rhetra** is the primary ancient text (via Plutarch) for early Spartan political structure: tribes and obes, the gerousia with kings, and provisions for the *dêmos*. [[sources/hall-history-archaic-greek-world-2014|Hall (2014)]] Ch.8 examines wording, dating, and the role of popular participation without equating it with egalitarianism.

## Text and provisions (Document 8.1 in Hall)

Plutarch (*Lyc.* 6) preserves a Delphic *rhêtra* for Spartan governance: found sanctuaries of Zeus Syllanios and Athena Syllania; “tribe the tribes (*phylai*) and obe the obes”; establish a council of thirty elders together with the *arkhêgetai* (kings); hold the Apellai each season between Babyka and Knakion and so introduce and set aside proposals, “but the right to speak in opposition and power are to belong to the *dêmos*.” A further clause (sometimes called a “rider,” attributed by Plutarch to kings Polydoros and Theopompus): “But if the *dêmos* speaks crookedly, the elders and the *arkhêgetai* are to be setters-aside.”

## Emic / etic

- **Emic:** a Lycurgan ordinance from Delphi, founding *eunomia*.
- **Etic:** an Archaic (or later-edited) constitutional document filtered through the [[concepts/spartan-mirage|Spartan mirage]] and fourth-century Aristotelian commentary. Hall’s principal source path: Plutarch explains arcane provisions via Aristotle, and Aristotle’s school wrote a lost Spartan constitution — so the information may derive from at least the fourth century; tracing earlier is difficult.

## Hall on Rhetra and Tyrtaeus

Tyrtaeus fr. 4 (via Plutarch *Lyc.* 6; cf. Diodorus 7.12.5–6) is often taken to paraphrase the Rhetra. Hall rejects special pleading that the Rhetra is not the oracle Tyrtaeus had in mind: despite prose form vs verse oracles and differing emphasis, Tyrtaeus contains the central features of both Rhetra and “rider” — proposals by kings and elders; *dêmos* sovereign if not giving crooked counsel; shared *kartos/kratos* vocabulary. The set-aside clause would then represent not a later corrective to abuses but retention of a veto within a single document. Tyrtaeus never names [[actors/lycurgus-of-sparta|Lycurgus]].

**Dating trap:** Suda’s Tyrtaeus date (Ol. 35 = 640–637) is often used to date the Rhetra before mid-seventh century, but Suda’s basis is unclear; if Tyrtaean poems are continuous Spartan tradition rather than one man, a precise constitutional date is lost. Hall finds no compelling reason to treat the Rhetra as a retrospective Heraclid foundation fiction and associates it, with Plutarch, with the joint reign of Polydoros and Theopompus in the **early seventh century** — making it “one of the earliest pieces of evidence for constitutional arrangements in Greece.”

## Constitutional content

Most authority rests with two hereditary kings and an aristocratic council of thirty (including the kings). Final decisive authority rests with the *dêmos*, but the veto preserves the principle of consensus. Right of popular opposition may have been revoked by Aristotle’s day or limited to the five ephors (not mentioned in the Rhetra; Aristotle attributes them to Theopompus).

Popular participation ≠ egalitarianism: “at the same time as the Rhetra endorsed the final decision-making authority of the *dêmos*, it also made it fairly explicit that its role was primarily that of ratifying proposals formulated by the kings and the aristocratic council.” The *dêmos* could not emerge as a political force before collective self-recognition and boundary definition (upper boundary *kaloi kagathoi* vs *dêmos* early if porous; lower boundary harder). *Phylai* as roughly equal citizen subdivisions presuppose that definitional work.

## Limitations and critiques

Single full wording in Plutarch; possible Tyrtaeus allusion; dating and authenticity contested; Lycurgan packaging is later invention even if early-seventh constitutional elements are real (Hall). Must be read against [[concepts/spartan-mirage]].

## From the Oxford History of the Classical World (1986)

George Forrest ([[sources/boardman-oxford-history-classical-world-1986|OHCW 1986]], Ch.1) does not quote the Rhetra text but situates the early-seventh-century **Lycurgan package** as tradition’s way of explaining Sparta’s distinctive military-political order: training that made Sparta the most efficient Greek power; dual hereditary kingship retained; perioikoi with limited domestic independence; helot labour underwriting homoioi “equals” defined by training survival, land allotment, and obedience. Spartans “were beginning to grope towards a definition of the citizen as a member of a society who was automatically entitled to certain rights”—the same citizenship-as-rights problem the Rhetra (on other readings) tries to institutionalize for kings, *gerousia*, and *damos*. Forrest’s emphasis is functional (helot fear + military system + equality among survivors) rather than documentary reconstruction of the oracle wording; Hall’s text-critical treatment of Document 8.1 remains the wiki’s primary Rhetra analysis. **`part_of:`** the wider [[concepts/spartan-mirage|Spartan mirage]] tradition that packages reform under Lycurgus.
