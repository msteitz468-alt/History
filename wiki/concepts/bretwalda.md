# Bretwalda (Brytenwealda)

**Bretwalda** (variant *brytenwealda*) is the title the *[[anglo-saxon-chronicle|Anglo-Saxon
Chronicle]]* applies, under the year 829, to [[ecgberht-of-wessex|Ecgberht]] of
[[kingdom-of-wessex|Wessex]] and, retrospectively, to seven earlier kings — the same kings
Bede had listed (*HE* II.5) as holding *imperium* over the southern English: Ælle of Sussex,
[[ceawlin-of-wessex|Ceawlin]], [[æthelberht-of-kent|Æthelberht]] of Kent,
[[raedwald-of-east-anglia|Rædwald]], [[edwin-of-northumbria|Eadwine]],
[[oswald-of-northumbria|Oswald]] and [[oswiu-of-northumbria|Oswiu]].

## Meaning and Dispute

The word has been read two ways: as "wide-ruler" (*bryten* = "wide, broad") or as
"ruler of Britain" (*Bret-* = "Briton"). [[sources/kirby-earliest-english-kings-2000|Kirby]]
is sceptical of the grander reading. He argues there was no continuous, transmissible
**office** of overlord and no institutional content to "Bretwaldaship"; Bede's list is a
literary construct selecting kings who at various points exercised a hegemony of tribute
and military leadership over other kings, with long gaps and no necessary succession. The
Chronicle's application of the term to Ecgberht is a piece of ninth-century West Saxon
self-promotion grafted onto Bede's earlier scheme.

## What the Hegemony Actually Was

The reality behind the word was **over-kingship**: a powerful king compelled lesser kings
to render tribute, provide military service and accept his arbitration, often confirmed by
marriage alliances, hostages and (after conversion) ecclesiastical patronage. It was
personal and precarious, collapsing on the overlord's death or defeat — as when
[[penda-of-mercia|Penda]] broke Northumbrian dominance at the [[battle-of-the-winwaed-655|Winwæd]],
or when the northern hegemony passed south to [[mercia|Mercia]].
