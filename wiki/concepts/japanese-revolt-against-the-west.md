# The Japanese Revolt Against the West (1900–1945)

The broad current of twentieth-century [[japan|Japanese]] thought that **turned against the
Western modernity Japan had so deliberately imported** — a critique of capitalism,
materialism, liberalism, and "civilization and enlightenment" that ranged from agrarian
nostalgia and cultural philosophy to the ideology of militarist expansion. From the
[[cambridge-history-japan-v6-twentieth-century|Cambridge History of Japan Vol. 6]] (Tetsuo
Najita & H. D. Harootunian, ch. 14).

## Definition and Scope

The "revolt against the West" was the dialectical counterpart to
[[meiji-modernization|Meiji *bunmei kaika*]]: having reinvented itself on Western models,
twentieth-century Japan generated a powerful intellectual reaction against the costs of that
transformation — the social dislocation of rapid [[japanese-economic-miracle|capitalist
industrialization]] (labor and tenant unrest, the conspicuous new rich, party corruption) and
the sense that something authentically Japanese had been lost. Najita and Harootunian trace
its forms across the spectrum, from socialist and liberal critiques (treated separately by
Duus & Scheiner, ch. 13) to the **restorationist, culturalist, and particularist** strands
that fed [[japanese-militarism|militarism]].

## Major strands

- **Restorationist revolt.** Radical nationalists — **Kita Ikki**, **Ōkawa Shūmei** — called
  for a "Shōwa Restoration": a violent "cleansing" (through terror and coup) of a polluted,
  Westernized domestic politics as the precondition for **expelling Western imperialism from
  Asia**. Ōkawa's **Pan-Asianism** (drawn partly from his study of British-ruled India) framed
  an inevitable apocalyptic "war to end wars" between a "just" Asia and a "despotic" West — an
  ideology that converged with, and justified, military aggression and the
  [[japanese-imperialism|Co-Prosperity Sphere]].
- **Culturalism.** A retreat from politics into the affirmation of Japanese **culture** and
  aesthetic tradition as a realm of wholeness against Western fragmentation and rationalism.
- **Cultural particularism.** The assertion of Japan's unique *kokutai* and spiritual essence
  against universalist Western categories.

## The debate on overcoming modernity

The current's most famous moment was the **1942 symposium "Overcoming Modernity"** (*kindai no
chōkoku*), held in the first year of the [[pacific-war-1941-1945|Pacific War]] and gathering
literary critics, philosophers (the **Kyoto School**'s Nishitani Keiji), and the *Bungakukai*
and Romantic-school writers. The participants framed the war as a **"world-historical"
spiritual confrontation** between "Western intelligence" and "the blood of the Japanese," a
chance to **transcend the modernity** Japan had absorbed since the Meiji "civilization and
enlightenment" — to heal cultural fragmentation and alienation by recovering a "cultural
wholeness." The later critic **Takeuchi Yoshimi** made the symposium the classic text for
analyzing how an anti-Western intellectual revolt became entangled with militarist ideology.

## Interpretation and debate

The chapter is `contested:` in its assessment. The revolt against the West is **double-edged**:
a genuine and intellectually serious critique of the alienations of capitalist modernity
(shared, in different keys, with Western thinkers), yet one that in its restorationist and
particularist forms **lent itself to ultranationalism, fascism, and the justification of
imperial war**. Postwar scholarship has debated how far the "overcoming modernity" thinkers
were complicit in militarism versus pursuing a legitimate philosophical critique — a question
sharpened by the postwar return of similar themes in [[japanese-economic-miracle|affluent]]
Japan's debates over its own modernity and identity.
