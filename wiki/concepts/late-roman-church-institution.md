# The Church as a Public Institution

How, after Constantine, the Christian **church became a public institution of the Roman empire** — its
bishops sharing the privileges and precedence of imperial officials, its councils convened as instruments
of imperial policy, its hierarchy mapped onto the secular administration. Treated in
[[cameron-cah-v13-1998|CAH XIII]] Ch. 8 (**David Hunt**).

## Bishops at court

By the 380s, Hunt notes, the church was to Ammianus Marcellinus "an unremarkable component" of the public
world: Christian basilicas shared the urban skyline, and bishops were "recognizably prominent in the
affairs of their communities and of the empire." Ammianus complained that **church councils strained the
*cursus publicus*** — bishops travelling at state expense to the synods the emperor summoned. After
Constantine's "imperial takeover of Christian institutions," **"church" and "state" were inseparable
categories**: bishops became "a new breed of *amici Caesaris*," eager to "appeal to Caesar" as arbiter of
their disputes, while the emperor — though an unbaptized layman — never doubted his **responsibility to
forge ecclesiastical unity**. The **Council of [[council-of-nicaea-325|Nicaea]] (325)** was the model:
Constantine turned the church's conciliar mechanism into an instrument of his design and the bishops into
"something resembling imperial agents and courtiers" — a process Hunt likens to **Augustus' transformation
of the Senate**, complete with exaggerated mutual deference and the secular enforcement (exile) of dissent.

## A hierarchy mapped on the state

Nicaea gave formal recognition to an **ecclesiastical organization based on that of the Roman state**:

- **The city as the unit**: each *civitas* and its territory had one **autonomous bishop** and dependent
  clergy; a bishop's status required a city to his name (hence the rule that villages got a presbyter, not
  a bishop, and the subordinate status of **rural bishops**, *chorepiscopi*). The number of sees grew
  unevenly — a single bishop in remote Scythia, **hundreds** in North Africa (where Catholic–Donatist
  rivalry multiplied competing sees).
- **The province and the metropolitan**: Nicaea prescribed **twice-yearly provincial synods** under the
  bishop of the chief city — the **metropolitan** — who summoned the council, vetted episcopal
  appointments, and held a veto. With governor and metropolitan in the same capital, "secular and
  ecclesiastical organization converged."

## The friction of the model

The neat coincidence of church and state structures did not always work "on the ground." **Apostolic
prestige clashed with administrative rank**: Jerusalem (the "cradle of the faith") resented subordination
to its metropolitan at Caesarea; Arles (claiming foundation by St Trophimus sent by Peter) challenged
Vienne; the continual **redrawing of provincial boundaries** unsettled metropolitan claims (Basil of
Caesarea's feud with Tyana over Valens' new province). Bishops increasingly argued, with Innocent of Rome,
that "the church of God should [not] be changed to suit the flexibility of worldly requirements" — the
seed of the church developing its **own** logic of authority alongside the state's.

## Significance

This chapter traces the **institutionalization of Christianity as a department of public life** — the
structural counterpart to the religious transformation treated in [[christianization-of-the-roman-empire|the
Christianization of the empire]]. The church acquired wealth, legal privilege, and a career structure (the
episcopate as a path to local and imperial influence), its bishops becoming **public figures and power-
brokers** (Ambrose, Cyril). Yet the very mapping of church onto state also generated the **tension between
ecclesiastical and imperial authority** that would run through late antiquity — bishops who owed their
public standing to the emperor, but who increasingly claimed an autonomy the emperor could not override.

## Update — the organization of the church, 5th–6th c. (CAH XIV)

[[cameron-cah-v14-2000|CAH XIV]] (Ch. 24, **the organization of the church**) shows the institution at
maturity. The **episcopal hierarchy** mapped onto the empire (see/city, metropolitan/province) was crowned
by the great **patriarchates** — Rome, Constantinople, Alexandria, Antioch, Jerusalem — whose rivalries
(above all **Alexandria vs Constantinople**) drove the [[council-of-chalcedon-451|Christological
councils]]. Chalcedon's **canon 28** elevated Constantinople to near-parity with Rome, fuelling the
later **papal claim to primacy** and the slow estrangement of East and West. The church had become a great
**property-owner and welfare provider** (the bishop now the effective governor of the
[[late-roman-cities|late-antique city]], dispensing charity, ransoming captives, building), its wealth and
courts (the *episcopalis audientia*) a parallel administration. As the western state dissolved, the **Roman
papacy** emerged as an independent power (Gregory the Great, from 590, governing Rome amid the
[[lombards|Lombard]] wars) — the institutional thread carrying the ancient church into the medieval West.
Source: [[cameron-cah-v14-2000]] (Ch. 24).

## Harris, *Roman Power* (2016)

Harris treats bishops and priests as a **new privileged order** whose immunities were derogations of state power. Already under Constantine, bishops enjoyed privileges no other category had: clerics free from taxation and compulsory public services (*CTh* 16.2); some on public payrolls; clerical trial by bishops; unsupported episcopal testimony accepted as true (Sirmondian Const. 1, 15). Major bishoprics became extremely wealthy and welfare sources in the fourth century (Ammianus on the bishop of Rome and the 366 Damasus strife); only in the fifth century did the Roman see establish western ascendancy. The role attracted ambitious administrators—Ambrose of Milan, praetorian prefect’s son, high office ~age 34, then bishop without clerical preparation.

After 312 Christianity mattered for state power in two main ways: (1) real-world power to an ecclesiastical hierarchy mainly indifferent to state interests; (2) imminent potential for divided religious loyalty in the army. Constantine’s 318 grant of episcopal jurisdiction over Christian lawsuits (*CTh* 1.27.1) was a momentous derogation. Fourth-century bishops seldom held direct political power, but Callinicum (388) showed Theodosius retreating from punishing synagogue destruction—a significant weakening of the state’s limited monopoly of force; by the 390s bishops commanded gangs of religious enforcers (*ecclesiastica manus*, *CTh* 16.10.19 / Sirm. 12 of 407). Sixth–seventh centuries: senior churchmen took political/diplomatic missions (Gregory of Antioch and the 589 mutiny; Pope Gregory in Byzantine Italy; Patriarch Sergios with Heraclius); Cyrus was both patriarch of Alexandria and governor 631–640 and later surrendered Egypt to the Muslims. Justin II deferred provincial-governor nomination to “most holy bishops and principal inhabitants” (Justinian Novels 149). Church officials paid no taxes and were not subject to ordinary criminal law; fiscal monasticism grew (Aphrodito cadastre 525–40: monasteries ~1/3 of land). Source: [[harris-roman-power-2016]].

## Gibbon, *Decline and Fall* (1776–1788) — Chs. XV–XVI, XX–XXI, XXVII–XXVIII

[[sources/gibbon-decline-and-fall-1776|Gibbon]]’s institutional church is the independent republic that becomes Constantine’s privileged order — load-bearing for the [[concepts/gibbonian-decline-thesis|Gibbonian decline thesis]]. All *as Gibbon frames it*.

- **Excommunication and conscience:** Excommunicated lost oblations and friendship; governors held “the keys of Hell and of Paradise”; absolute command over consciences more grateful to pride than despotic arms (Cyprian type).
- **Pre-Constantinian wealth:** Cyprian’s 100,000 sesterces ransom fund; Pontus donation 200,000; “before the close of the third century many considerable estates were bestowed on the opulent churches of Rome, Milan, Carthage, Antioch, Alexandria.”
- **Paul of Samosata trial:** Aurelian enforces episcopal deposition — proof that Christian property, privileges, and internal policy were acknowledged by magistrates.
- **Constantine’s gift package:** “Constantine gave them security, wealth, honors, and revenge”; ~1,800 bishops (1,000 Greek / 800 Latin); clergy more numerous than legions, tax- and service-exempt; cathedrals of Constantinople and Carthage ~500 ministers each.
- **Free election as last republican privilege:** Subjects “enjoyed in the church the privilege which they had lost in the republic, of choosing the magistrates whom they were bound to obey.”
- **Civil jurisdiction and sanctuary:** Trial of bishops by peers; arbitration without appeal; sanctuary in Christian temples — “prepared the triumph of the Roman pontiffs, who have trampled on the necks of kings.”
- **Preaching monopoly under monarchy:** Ruin of civil liberty silenced tribunes; “the ears of monarchs were never invaded by the harsh sound of popular eloquence, till the pulpits of the empire were filled with sacred orators.”
- **Bequests:** Free bequest to the Catholic church eight years after Milan; from Constantine to Justinian, ~1,800 churches enriched by unalienable gifts — Heaven purchased “if he maintained the idle at the expense of the industrious.”
- **Ambrose type:** Priest holding a king’s conscience “may inflame, or moderate, his sanguinary passions” (basilicas, Theodosius’s penance).
- **Priscillian as Inquisition prototype:** Ecclesiastical and secular powers divide roles; spiritual guilt expressed “in the mild language of pity and intercession.”

## From Mitchell, Later Roman Empire (2015)

Source: [[mitchell-later-roman-empire-2015]].

- **No universal orthodoxy.** By early fifth century the religious landscape was irrevocably changed, but “universal orthodoxy” in patristic literature misrepresents reality. Major schisms: Donatists (Africa), Melitians (Egypt), Arians vs Nicene across the East, Monophysites vs Chalcedonians later—bridged (attemptedly) at Nicaea 325, Constantinople 381, Ephesus 431/449, Chalcedon 451, Constantinople 553. Smaller “heresies” thrived in rural strongholds (Epiphanius’s *Panarion*; Basil’s canons on Anatolian encratite groups; Nicene orthodoxy minority in central Anatolia).
- **Hierarchy calqued on provinces.** After Constantine: one bishop per city (plus *chorepiscopi* in Asia Minor estates); metropolitan convened twice-yearly provincial meetings. Bishops chosen by popular acclamation and fellow-bishop confirmation. Sees: Rome supreme in West (challenged only by Ambrose’s Milan 374–97); Carthage strong regionally then nullified by Vandal Arian conquest 439; East—Alexandria (Nicaea can. 6) and Antioch (Oriens); Constantinople’s rise (can. 3 of 381: primacy of honor after Rome as New Rome) affronted Alexandria and seeded Chalcedonian–Monophysite political rivalry. Chalcedon can. 28 + Zeno’s Henotikon → Acacian schism 484–519; Justinian later annulled sacred/secular distinction by acting as God’s agent in all matters.
- **Bishops’ secular role.** Where municipal government remained strong (Africa into early fifth century), secular and ecclesiastical administration stayed distinct—“deliberately and self-consciously secular” public life. Where civic government weak, bishops led: Theophilus/Cyril at Alexandria; Basil of Caesarea; Synesius of Ptolemais (defense 412); Sidonius at Clermont; sixth-century Syrian bishops organizing resistance/embassies vs Persia.
- **Tolerance of diversity until Justinian.** Jewish communities thrived and attracted god-fearers; imperial Jewish legislation rhetoric was harsh yet preserved worship privileges; persecution sporadic/local. Montanists and Novatians show close Jewish–Christian borders in Anatolia. “Until the time of Justinian, tolerance of diversity was more normal than outright religious warfare.” Novatian schism (post-Decian rigorism) remained vigorous in Constantinople and Asia Minor into the fifth century—disciplinary schisms long outlived their original circumstances.
