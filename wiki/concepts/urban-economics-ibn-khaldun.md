## Definition

The economic analysis embedded in Chapter IV of [[ibn-khaldun]]'s [[the-muqaddimah]], constituting one of the earliest systematic accounts of how city size determines prosperity, how prices are structured across urban hierarchies, and why Bedouin populations are structurally excluded from large cities. The analysis is part of [[ilm-al-umran]] and is grounded in Ibn Khaldun's observation of the Maghribi and Egyptian urban system in the 14th century.

---

## The Labor-Surplus Theory of Urban Prosperity

The foundational argument: individual human beings cannot satisfy their own needs in isolation. Civilized life requires cooperative labor. But cooperative labor produces more than those who perform it require: "What is obtained through the co-operation of a group of human beings satisfies the need of a number many times greater than themselves."

Ibn Khaldun's example: grain production requires a smith (tools), a carpenter (tools), plowmen, harvesters, and others. Their combined labor yields food for far more people than the laborers themselves. The surplus above subsistence requirements is available for exchange, export, or luxury consumption.

This generates a self-reinforcing spiral:
1. Large population → more cooperative labor → larger surplus
2. Surplus → luxury demand → craft specialization
3. Craft specialization → higher skilled-labor value → more profit
4. More profit → more luxury demand → back to step 2

"When civilization (population) increases, the (available) labor again increases. In turn, luxury again increases in correspondence with the increasing profit, and the customs and needs of luxury increase. Crafts are created to obtain (luxury products). The value realized from them increases, and, as a result, profits are again multiplied in the town."

Ibn Khaldun explicitly identifies profit as "the value realized from labor" — an anticipation of labor-value reasoning that would not appear in European economic thought until the 17th–18th centuries.

## Urban Hierarchy and Differential Prosperity

Ibn Khaldun applies this framework to a concrete hierarchy of Maghribi cities: Fez > Tlemcen > Oran/Algiers > smaller towns > villages. Each level of the hierarchy is strictly better-off than levels below it, at every social stratum: "The situation of a judge in Fez is better than that of a judge in Tlemcen, and the same is the case with all other population groups."

The inequality is not a matter of hoarded treasure or moral virtue. Ibn Khaldun explicitly corrects the popular belief that Cairo is wealthy because its inhabitants are naturally more generous or have more property hoarded: "The reason is that the civilization (population) of Egypt and Cairo is larger than that of any other city one might think of. Therefore, (the inhabitants of Egypt) enjoy better (living) conditions."

Evidence from the bottom of the social scale: A beggar in Fez asks for luxuries — meat, butter, cooked dishes, utensils — because the ambient surplus makes such gifts possible. A beggar in Tlemcen asking for the same "would be considered with disapproval and treated harshly and chased away." The hierarchy of urban prosperity descends continuously to villages where labor does not even cover bare necessities.

## Price Structure: Necessities vs. Luxuries

Ibn Khaldun's price theory inverts intuition:

**In large, prosperous cities:**
- Necessary foodstuffs (grain, legumes) are *cheap*: everyone produces or imports them, the supply exceeds demand, storage against contingency is the only reason to pay any price at all
- Luxury goods and skilled labor are *expensive*: many wealthy buyers compete for limited supply of artisans and rare goods; workers can be selective because food is cheap; government levies on markets add to costs

**In small, poor cities:**
- Necessities are *expensive*: small supply, fear of shortage drives hoarding, few producers
- Luxuries are *cheap* (or absent): no demand from an impoverished population

Three reasons labor is expensive in prosperous cities: (1) high luxury demand; (2) workers' willingness to hold out for higher wages because subsistence is easy; (3) wealthy patrons bidding against each other for exclusive access to skilled workers.

**Government levies**: "Customs duties and other duties that are levied on (foods) in the markets and at the city gates on behalf of the ruler... enter into the price of foodstuffs. Prices in cities, thus, are higher than prices in the desert, because customs duties and other duties and levies are few or non-existent among (the Bedouins)." This burden increases "especially in the later (years) of a dynasty" — a political economy observation linking fiscal extraction to the dynastic cycle.

**A case study in agricultural cost**: In Muslim Spain, after Christian military pressure pushed Muslims onto poor coastal soils, the cost of improving marginal land (fertilizer, intensive labor) was built into food prices, making Spain exceptionally expensive. Ibn Khaldun notes this is a structural cost phenomenon, not a supply shortage: Spanish Muslims were the most agriculture-devoted people he knew of, with virtually no one who did not farm.

## Bedouin Exclusion from Large Cities

Because large cities have high labor costs, high luxury-inflated prices for goods, and government levies on transactions, Bedouins cannot afford to settle in them. Bedouin income is structurally low: generated in environments with little labor demand, it yields little profit and no accumulation. "Every Bedouin who is attracted to city life quickly shows himself unable (to compete) and is disgraced."

The only exception: a Bedouin who has already accumulated property beyond subsistence — through trade, raiding, or service to a dynasty — can blend into urban life. "This is the way the civilization of cities begins": the earliest urban settlers are always Bedouins who have pre-accumulated enough surplus to survive city prices.

This analysis closes the loop with [[cities-and-sedentary-decline-ibn-khaldun]]: the same luxury that makes a city prosperous makes it inaccessible to Bedouin populations, preventing the renewal of asabiyyah-bearing populations from below. The city becomes demographically sealed against the very source of the political energy that created it.

## Historiography

Scholars who have read Ibn Khaldun as an economic theorist include Joseph Schumpeter (*History of Economic Analysis*, 1954), who noted his anticipation of labor-value theory, and more recently Benedikt Koehler (*Early Islam and the Birth of Capitalism*, 2014), who situates the Muqaddimah in the context of medieval Islamic commercial culture. The price-structure analysis in sections 12–13 of Chapter IV is Ibn Khaldun's most technically precise economic reasoning and has received less scholarly attention than his political sociology. It is grounded in direct observation of the Maghribi urban system; Ibn Khaldun lived in and traveled between Fez, Tlemcen, Tunis, and Cairo.
