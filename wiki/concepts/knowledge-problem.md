# The Knowledge Problem

Hayek's reformulation of the central question of economic and social order:
the knowledge relevant to economic decisions exists only as dispersed,
fragmentary, often tacit knowledge of "the particular circumstances of time
and place," never given to any single mind. The economic problem of society
is therefore not allocation of given resources to given ends but "a problem
of the utilization of knowledge which is not given to anyone in its
totality." Documented here from the two essays reprinted in
[[hayek-individualism-economic-order-1948]].

## Formulation

**"Economics and Knowledge"** (presidential address, London Economic Club,
10 November 1936; *Economica* 1937) posed the germinal question: equilibrium
theory is a "Pure Logic of Choice," tautological until supplemented by
propositions about how dispersed individual knowledge is acquired and comes
to correspond — "How can the combination of fragments of knowledge existing
in different minds bring about results which, if they were to be brought
about deliberately, would require a knowledge on the part of the directing
mind which no single person can possess?" Hayek named this the **division of
knowledge**, "the central problem of economics as a social science,"
parallel to the classical division of labor, and mocked the perfect-market
assumption as readmitting "that skeleton in our cupboard, the 'economic
man'... in the form of a quasi-omniscient individual." Footnotes credit
[[mises-ludwig|Mises]]'s "geistige Arbeitsteilung" as a source.

**"The Use of Knowledge in Society"** (*American Economic Review*, September
1945) supplied the canonical statement: statistics available to a central
planner necessarily abstract away the particulars (location, quality,
timing) that decisions turn on, so planning must devolve on "the man on the
spot"; the price system functions as "a kind of machinery for registering
change" that lets producers "watch merely the movement of a few pointers."
The famous passage: "The marvel is that in a case like that of a scarcity of
one raw material, without an order being issued, without more than perhaps a
handful of people knowing the cause, tens of thousands of people whose
identity could not be ascertained by months of investigation, are made to
use the material or its products more sparingly." Hayek quotes Whitehead —
"civilization advances by extending the number of important operations which
we can perform without thinking about them" — to class the price system with
language among unconsciously evolved institutions, and attacks Schumpeter's
claim that consumer valuations "ipso facto" value the factors of production
as conflating one observing mind with knowledge actually dispersed among
many.

## Generalization in *The Constitution of Liberty* (1960)

[[hayek-constitution-of-liberty-1960|*The Constitution of Liberty*]] turns the
economic argument into the foundation of political philosophy: "the case for
individual freedom rests chiefly on the recognition of the inevitable
ignorance of all of us concerning a great many of the factors on which the
achievement of our ends and welfare depends." Civilization begins "when an
individual can use more knowledge than he has himself acquired"; "the sum of
the knowledge of all the individuals exists nowhere as an integrated whole."
Corollaries: freedom cannot be confined to uses known in advance to be
beneficial ("Freedom granted only when it is known beforehand that its effects
will be beneficial is not freedom"); its value lies with the unknown few ("The
freedom that will be used by only one man in a million may be more important
to society... than any freedom that we all use"); and its benefits flow mainly
through *others'* use of it — "unfree majorities have benefited from the
existence of free minorities." Experts have no special claim to coordinate:
"no human mind can comprehend all the knowledge which guides the actions of
society," hence the need for an impersonal coordinating mechanism. Law itself
is the same device applied to conduct: general rules extend each person's
ability to plan on local knowledge (see [[rule-of-law]]); monopolistic
single-channel welfare provision forecloses the discovery process (see
[[welfare-state]]); and pluralistic, decentralized science funding beats
"planned science" for the same reason.

## The mature statement in *Law, Legislation and Liberty* (1973–79)

The trilogy generalizes further. Vol. 1: "the necessary and irremediable ignorance on everyone's part of most of the particular facts which determine the actions of all the several members of human society" is the source of the central problem of social order; even "the possibility of justice rests on this necessary limitation of our factual knowledge." The constructivist error is named the **synoptic delusion** — "the fiction that all the relevant facts are known to some one mind." Vol. 2 ties rules themselves to ignorance: "Rules are a device for coping with our constitutional ignorance. There would be no need for rules among omniscient people"; likewise "justice is an adaptation to our ignorance." Vol. 3 ch. 15 supplies the famous formulation of **competition as a discovery procedure**: "Competition is thus, like experimentation in science, first and foremost a discovery procedure. No theory can do justice to it which starts from the assumption that the facts to be discovered are already known" — even "the lowest costs at which a thing can be produced are exactly what we want competition to discover," and the proper test of policy is comparative ("whether the results of a given policy exceed or fall short of the results of other available procedures"), not distance from perfect-competition ideals. See [[catallaxy]] for the price-signal mechanics.

## Role in the calculation debate

The concept is Hayek's deepening of the [[socialist-calculation-debate]]:
where [[mises-ludwig|Mises]] argued planning lacks the *prices* to
calculate, Hayek argued it lacks — and cannot assemble — the *knowledge*
prices condense. His 1945 essay notes Mises's 1920 thesis "was greeted with
a howl of derision," and that by 1945 even Trotsky, [[lange-oskar|Lange]]
(who "promises Professor von Mises a statue in the marble halls of the
future Central Planning Board"), and Lerner conceded the necessity of market
relations to economic accounting.

## Emic vs. Etic

Emic to Hayek and the Austrian tradition; etic as historians' shorthand for
the epistemic critique of central planning and, later, a foundation of
Hayek's theory of spontaneous order.

## Limitations and Critiques

Market socialists then, and mechanism-design and computational economists
since, contest whether the problem is as intractable as Hayek claimed
(large-scale computation revived the "mathematical solution" debate);
intellectual historians note the concept's later libertarian uses often
exceed Hayek's own, and that "The Use of Knowledge in Society" acquired its
canonical status retrospectively, during the post-1974 Hayek revival.
