# Roman Consumption and Living Standards

An assessment of **how well ordinary Romans lived** — diet, income, stature, and material
comfort — during the early empire, and of whether the high empire saw genuine per-capita
economic advance rather than mere population growth. Treated by **Willem Jongman** in
[[scheidel-cambridge-economic-history-greco-roman-world-2007|CEHGRW]] Ch. 22, "The Early
Roman Empire: Consumption."

## The thesis: an "advanced organic economy"

Jongman argues Roman **national income** exceeded that of any pre-industrial European
state before the Industrial Revolution, driven mainly by sheer population size (60–70
million in the early empire). More controversially, he argues **per-capita incomes** may
also have been "remarkably high for a pre-industrial economy" — Rome as what E. A. Wrigley
called an "advanced organic economy," hugging the Malthusian ceiling as closely as a
pre-industrial economy could. Two qualifications temper the thesis: (1) the prosperity did
**not last** — it declined and grew more unequal in late antiquity; (2) even at its height,
gains were **unevenly distributed**, with an ever-smaller elite controlling an ever-larger
share of the surplus above subsistence over time.

## Measuring subsistence and income

Minimum subsistence cost is estimated at **115 sesterces/year** (250 kg wheat-equivalent,
~2,000 kcal/day given Roman age structure and life expectancy e0=25; Hopkins' method,
refined by Jongman and Van Laethem). Above that floor:

- **Elite incomes** were vast multiples of subsistence: senators (census minimum 1–1.2
  million HS) ~520x; knights (400,000 HS) ~208x; decurions (100,000 HS) ~52x, at a
  conventional 6% return on capital. Pliny the Younger's estimated annual income (~1.2
  million HS) was ~10,000x subsistence.
- **Ordinary wages**: free adult male wages of roughly 500–1,000 HS/year; legionary base
  pay 900 HS under Augustus; municipal scribes at Urso earned 800–1,200 HS; subsistence
  annuities to freedmen/foster children (n=13) had a median of 480 HS.
- **Slave prices** (Delphi manumission records) rose from ~3,500 kg wheat-equivalent
  (early 2nd c. BCE) to ~7,000 kg (late 1st c. BCE), implying wages well above subsistence
  and rising through the late Republic — read via Finley's model (slave price = capitalized
  value of the wage the owner extracts above the subsistence he must provide). The trend
  reversed by late antiquity: Diocletian's Price Edict shows the slave price back down to
  ~3,000 kg wheat.
- **Aggregate elite wealth**: the political elite (senators, knights, ~100,000
  decurions empire-wide) held a minimum aggregate wealth of ~13 billion sesterces, yielding
  a minimum aggregate elite income of ~800 million HS/year — roughly comparable in scale to
  total state expenditure, and, on some assumptions, larger.

## Diet, meat, and material comfort

Cereals were the caloric base, but **wine** (~100 liters/year per capita, ~1/4 of caloric
intake) and **olive oil** (~20 liters/year, another ~1/4) supplied roughly half of average
caloric intake — correcting the popular image of an overwhelmingly cereal diet. Wine and
oil (grown under the Mediterranean two-field system) yielded roughly five times the
calories per hectare of wheat, permitting higher population densities than grain alone
could sustain (potentially 15–20 million in Italy on a half-wine/oil, half-cereal diet).

**Meat**, especially pork, functions in Jongman's analysis as an indicator of "intermediate
prosperity" — too costly for the very poor, attractive to those just above subsistence, and
not disproportionately consumed by the very rich. Bone-deposition data show meat
consumption rising sharply from the late Republic to a peak in the early empire, then
declining from the 2nd century CE, most sharply outside Italy. Romanization also introduced
larger livestock breeds (roughly double the meat yield), which disappeared with Roman rule.

**Public grain distributions (*frumentationes*)** supplied ~33 kg wheat/month to each of
200,000 adult male citizens in Rome — about half the subsistence needs of a free population
of ~720,000 — later supplemented with oil, pork, and cheap wine, plus cash *congiaria* and
child-support *alimenta*.

## Biological evidence: stature

Skeletal evidence corroborates the textual/monetary picture. Roman-period German males
(Koepke) averaged 169.4 cm — taller than the same region's 19th-century population.
Herculaneum skeletons (Bisel excavation, 1982) show men averaging 169 cm and women 155 cm,
"a figure only achieved in England in the 18th century" — even the 1960s Naples population
was shorter. Individual cases show wide variance (a tall, athletic, well-nourished skeleton
alongside a shorter one bearing signs of malnutrition and hard labor; a 14-year-old slave
girl with dental hypoplasia consistent with infant starvation or illness). Average stature
appears to decline from the late 2nd century CE onward; Valentinian's 367 CE decree setting
minimum recruit height at 165 cm — itself probably near the top of the actual range —
suggests further decline in late antiquity.

## The turning point: the Antonine Plague

Jongman identifies the **[[antonine-plague-165|Antonine Plague]]** (from c. 165 CE) as the
hinge. Population fell afterward, but — unlike after the **Black Death** in late medieval
Europe — the plague did **not** raise the bargaining power or living standards of ordinary
survivors. Instead late antiquity saw growing oppression of the masses and increasing
concentration of wealth among a smaller, far richer elite (senatorial fortunes reaching
roughly five times Pliny's). Public building, a proxy for prosperity, peaked in Italy in the
late Republic/early empire, then fell sharply under Marcus Aurelius (a roughly 30-year
hiatus in imperially funded construction, 160–190 CE, linked to the plague), recovering
under the Severans before declining again. A parallel pattern of elite benefaction in Roman
Asia Minor (Zuiderhoek's dataset) shows roughly two-thirds of documented elite gifts dating
to the 2nd century CE.

## Historiography — the Roman GDP / per-capita income debate

Whether the Roman economy achieved genuine per-capita growth, and how large its GDP and
per-capita income actually were, is a live and unresolved dispute running through this
chapter and Lo Cascio's fiscal chapter (Ch. 23) alike. Positions, stated neutrally:

- **Hopkins** — a cautious, subsistence-based bottom-up estimate: assuming the population
  (60 million) produced only minimum subsistence plus a seed reserve, GDP would be ~9
  billion sesterces; allowing more realistic surplus, GDP of 12–15 billion HS (1.3–1.5x
  subsistence); Hopkins judges it unlikely per-capita income exceeded 2x subsistence, and is
  skeptical of much higher estimates because they would imply an implausibly low
  effective tax rate.
- **Goldsmith** — an earlier, more optimistic estimate placing per-capita income at
  roughly 3x subsistence (aggregate GDP ~20 billion HS on some figures) and a higher money-
  stock estimate; Lo Cascio notes an apparent internal tension in Goldsmith's own work,
  since Goldsmith elsewhere characterized the Roman economy as "stagnant," with no
  significant population or output growth — a tension this dispute has not resolved and
  which the curator has not yet adjudicated.
- **Duncan-Jones** — a more granular fiscal reconstruction from literary and epigraphic
  evidence, yielding a bold money-stock estimate of ~20 billion sesterces by the mid-2nd
  century (implying ~3x subsistence); Hopkins calls this implausibly high given the weak
  monetization of the rural sector, and Lo Cascio notes the method "can be questioned."
- **Temin** (2006) — methodologically closer to Goldsmith (skeptical of a purely
  subsistence-bound Malthusian model) but critical of Goldsmith's specific figures; argues
  that provincial prices and wages were systematically lower than the Rome/Italy data
  dominating both Hopkins' and Goldsmith's estimates, which would lower the nominal GNP
  estimate without necessarily changing the relative surplus above subsistence.

**Resolution status**: open. Despite sharply differing absolute GDP figures, Goldsmith,
Hopkins, and Duncan-Jones converge on the state's share of GDP being "a few percentage
points, no more than 10 percent and perhaps much less" (see
[[processes/roman-imperial-taxation-and-fiscal-system]]). The dispute over the *level* of
per-capita income and whether the high empire departed from the standard Malthusian
pre-industrial ceiling remains unsettled.
