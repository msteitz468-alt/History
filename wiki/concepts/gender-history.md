## Definition and Scope

Gender history is the study of how culturally constructed systems of difference
based on perceived sexual difference have structured human societies, and how
those systems changed over time. Merry Wiesner-Hanks (Ch. 10) calls gender
"the oldest category of difference in human history."

The key analytical distinction: **gender is not sex**. Gender is "a culturally
constructed system of differences based on physical, morphological, and anatomical
differences between the sexes" — distinct from biological sex (itself more complex
than conventional dualities imply; some societies recognize third or in-between
genders). As Joan Scott put it in 1986: "Gender is a constitutive element of
social relationships based on perceived differences between the sexes, and gender
is a primary way of signifying relationships of power."

Gender history is distinct from women's history: it studies the construction and
negotiation of *both* masculine and feminine roles, and the relations of power
between them.

## Gender as a Structuring Principle

Most human communities have assumed dual gender roles, often projected
symbolically onto the cosmos (male/female, yin/yang, private/public). In all
societies that have left records, gender dualities have been interpreted as
hierarchies, with the male side valued as stronger or more positive. Gender
dichotomies have been experienced as natural and intrinsic — despite being
culturally constructed.

Gender structures intersect with other world-historical themes:
- **Family and kinship**: gender shapes and is shaped by family structures
  (see [[family-and-biopolitics]]); marriage practices, descent rules, and
  citizenship rights are gendered.
- **Migration**: gender relations shape who migrates and how (see
  [[migration-in-human-history]]).
- **Colonialism and domination**: strongly held notions of gender and family
  shaped the cultural and legal structures of colonial domination. Conversely,
  encounters with different gender systems (e.g., Southeast Asian marriage
  practices that allowed easy divorce and women's public commerce) shocked
  European and Chinese observers and prompted attempts at regulation.
- **Nationalism**: gender shaped national identities and symbols (e.g., "Mother
  India").

## Historiographical Development

- **Pre-1970s**: Most historians (mostly male) took existing gender roles for
  granted and saw the past through male eyes — "history" was in fact "male history."
- **19th-c. women's movement**: First stirrings of attention to gender roles.
- **Second-wave feminism (mid-20th c.)**: Feminist historians showed the extent
  to which history was male history; began writing women's history (though early
  efforts often trapped women's history within pre-existing categories of class
  or nation).
- **1980s**: Increasing study of both male and female history through the
  category of gender, especially in the Anglophone world.
- **Gay rights movements and queer theory**: Questioned traditional gender
  categories at deeper levels; generated the history of sexuality. A key debate:
  are modern attitudes to gender and sexuality distinctively modern — products
  of the same processes that produced modernity itself?
- **The cultural turn**: Gender and sexuality historians were drawn to the broader
  shift from studying the past "as it actually happened" (Ranke) to studying how
  historians and others *construct* accounts of the past. Foucault (discourse and
  power) and Gramsci (hegemony) were influential.

## Relationship to World History

Gender history and world history moved along parallel tracks for decades before
intersecting. Both broke from the nation state as the organizing frame, but in
different directions: gender history worked with *differences*; world history
looked for *connections*. Gender history veered toward cultural history; world
history stayed closer to materialist traditions. Their intersection has shown
that world history is itself gendered in content and construction, and that
favored concepts, narrative forms, and periodization frameworks have rendered
many women and men invisible (a point also made by Judith Zinsser).

## Application to Prehistory

Modern accounts of Paleolithic societies emphasize the role of women as much as
(or more than) men. The "Man the Hunter" model dominant until the 1970s — which
imagined a Paleolithic world of male acquisition/production and female
reproduction shaped by biology rather than culture — has been revised by feminist
scholarship highlighting the central role of women in production (gathering),
the constructed nature of Paleolithic families, and the importance of households
in demographic behavior. Given women's importance as gatherers, they likely
played a significant role in the transition to agriculture. See
[[behavioral-modernity]] and [[family-and-biopolitics]].

## Gender, the State, and Trans-Regional Networks (CWH IV)

[[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|CWH IV]] (Wells & Yao, Chs 3 & 7) extends gender history into
the period 1200 BCE–900 CE by asking how **[[state-formation|state formation]],
[[empire|empire-building]], and [[trade-network-formation|trans-regional networks]]
transformed relations between men and women**. The chapter deliberately rejects
*both* a "grand narrative" of net loss (states/trade subordinating women who had
been freer in village/tribal society) *and* one of net gain (networks opening new
opportunities) — assuming **neither net gain nor net loss**, since the forms were
too various for simple conclusions.

**Endogamy expanded from kin to new categories.** The chapter's organizing
insight: state/empire/network formation reshaped marriage by widening the category
within which unions were *endogamous* — from kin to **socio-economic class, civic
identity, or religious allegiance**. Examples: Indian *varna*/*jati* endogamy
codified over the Vedic period (and challenged, unsuccessfully, by [[ashoka|Ashoka's]]
[[buddhism|Buddhism]]); Greek citizen-class endogamy (the Athenian *epikleros*
married to keep property in the patriline); Roman *conubium* restricted to citizens
until [[caracalla|Caracalla]]'s 212 CE universal grant made all free residents a
single legally endogamous body; religion-defined endogamy under the
[[caliphate|caliphate]] (Muslims, Christians, Zoroastrians marrying within faith,
with conversion-driven assimilation under the Abbasids).

**A Mediterranean–China comparison** structures the chapter:
- *Mediterranean:* the contrast of Athens (citizen wives as legal minors under male
  guardianship) with **Sparta** (women uniquely owning/inheriting property — tied to
  male absence at war, and rolled back after the loss of Messenia in 369 BCE); the
  Roman shift from *conubium cum manu* to *sine manu* (giving wives property autonomy
  as the empire's commercial opportunities expanded), then its **reversal** in the
  post-Roman West as wealth re-localized into landholdings.
- *China:* the deepening of patriarchy, patriliny, and patrilocality from Shang
  ancestor-worship and the Western Zhou **Lineage Law (*zongfa*)** privileging the
  principal wife's eldest son, consolidated by the Han embrace of [[confucianism]]
  (state and society as "the hierarchical family writ large," the emperor as "Son of
  Heaven").

**Women's public and religious power.** Wells & Yao stress that women exercised real
public power *through* these structures — Tang **Empress Wu**, the Fujiwara empresses
of Heian Japan, and especially **religious office**: Tang Daoist and Buddhist nuns
(e.g. the princess Yuzhen; Master Ruyuan, made an "Altar-Presiding Bhadanta" and
"State Master") converted spiritual standing into wealth and political influence —
illustrating how the [[universal-religions|new religions]] opened gendered avenues
of authority alongside the household and court. Compare [[family-and-biopolitics]].

**Discourses of gender and sexuality (Ch 7).** A companion chapter reads the era's
*literature* as a register of how states, empires, and networks reshaped ideals of
masculinity, femininity, and desire. War and forced separation pervade the verse of
empire (the Chinese "Song of Yan" and *Ballad of Mulan*; the Greek shift from the
*Iliad's* combat to the *Odyssey's* domestic women). A recurrent plot — lovers
separated by the dangers of "cities, trade, and states" and reunited only at home —
runs across the **Greek novel**, the biblical *Judith* and *Esther*, early Christian
saints' lives, and Tamil/Sanskrit romance (Kalidasa's *Shakuntala*). The chapter
notes a contrast in the valuation of eroticism: redirected toward God or liberation
in Christian and Indian renunciant traditions, but represented as positively good in
Chinese/Japanese (Daoist, Tantric, Mahayana, Tang literati) literature. In Tang
China the **idealization of femininity shifted** from mere beauty to "bookish beauty"
— learned women paralleling the rise of the examination-based literati elite.

## Gender, Family, and Religion in the Middle Millennium (CWH V)

[[kedar-cwh-v5-2015|CWH V]] (Susan Mosher Stuard, Ch 3) carries gender history
across 500–1500 CE, organized around the **family** as the unit that polities
"mirrored" — almost all paternal and hierarchical — and around the era's
characteristic **drift from flexible to more rigid gender systems**, "particularly
as they applied to women." Patriarchal assumptions everywhere were structured by
religion, while local law and *custom* (Islamic *‘urf*) could erode or modify
religious prescription. A recurring driver of greater female subordination was
**war, conquest, and invasion** (and possibly population crowding in this age of
demographic increase); the Americas and parts of sub-Saharan Africa appear partial
exceptions.

Stuard's method is comparative and resists a single narrative:
- **Islamic world & South Asia.** Shari‘a specified a marriage gift (*mahr*) and
  daughters' inheritance (smaller than sons'); women were property-owners who could
  sue in court, and elite women (Rabi‘a of Basra; the Ayyubid regent Dayfa Khatun;
  Radiyya of Delhi) wielded real power, often through male intermediaries. In
  northern India the **kulinism** introduced by Vallala Sena of Bengal (hypergamy —
  women could marry up to three ranks above but never below) produced Brahmin
  dowry-collecting polygamy and female infanticide; "everyone believed in
  [[caste-varna|caste]]."
- **Central & East Asia.** Turkic and Mongol women drove wagons, managed
  encampments, and could rule with husbands, their authority backed by their own
  lineages (William of Rubruck; Ibn Battuta's astonishment at the Golden Horde
  khatuns). In China, dowry passed to daughters; under the [[song-dynasty|Song]]
  women grew more identified with husbands' lineages and divorce became rare;
  **foot-binding** began at the Song court and spread widely only under the Ming;
  printing (11th c.) fueled a surge in women's poetic writing, and literate mothers
  educated examination-bound sons. Japan produced Murasaki Shikibu's *Tale of Genji*.
- **Europe.** Two family patterns — a Mediterranean large-family/early-female-marriage
  type (Byzantium, Italy, Spain) and a northern small-family, late-marriage,
  neolocal type. The twelfth-century shift from bride-gift (*morgengab*) to
  **dowry** paid by the bride's father (Italy after 1140, then Spain/southern
  France) curtailed women's control of wealth and their workplace roles. English
  *feme sole*/*feme covert* law; convents and the **Beguines**; women authors
  (Hildegard of Bingen, Anna Komnene's *Alexiad*, Trotula of Salerno). Jewish women
  had divorce rights, more literacy, and commercial careers, lived in precarious
  proximity to persecution.
- **Religion and women.** Every major faith was hierarchical and paternalistic yet
  offered some safeguards: the Buddha admitted women to enlightenment before monks
  subordinated nuns; Christianity ordained only men; Scholastic
  [[social-hierarchy-and-solidarity|Aristotelianism]] (Aquinas) gave western Europe
  a sharply polar gender theory. Female mystics and saints (Catherine of Siena)
  carved out an "alternative experiential Christianity." The universal religions
  "all spoke of man as the generic norm," rendering women "in a sense, less human."

This complements the CWH IV finding (above) that state/empire/network formation
reshaped endogamy and gendered authority; CWH V extends it through the
intensification of patriarchy in the High and [[late-middle-ages|Late Middle Ages]].

## Gender and the First World War

[[paxton-europe-twentieth-century-2011|Paxton]] (ch. 4) documents the war's uneven but real transformation of women's status in the belligerent states. As male labour was drained to the front, women moved into skilled factory and heavy-industry work previously closed to them: female employment at Krupp armaments in Germany rose from 2,000 to 28,000 by 1918, a third of French munitions workers were female by 1918, and Britain's female labour force reached 3 million. The change was most marked for **middle-class women**, who gained access to office work and teaching, greater personal independence (unchaperoned outings, smoking in public, living alone), and, via Marie Stopes's 1918 birth-control guides (*Married Love*, *Wise Parenthood*) and Victor Margueritte's bestselling novel *La Garçonne* (1922), a new public discourse of female sexual and social autonomy. This "emancipated woman" also provoked a backlash that interwar [[generic-fascism|fascist movements]] could later exploit. Politically, most belligerent states extended suffrage after the war (Lady Astor, elected 1919, was the first woman seated in a European parliament). Postwar, demobilized soldiers often reclaimed prewar jobs from women, but — per Paxton — "women no longer seemed out of place in a wider range of work," a durable shift in expectation even where employment reverted. This is a First-World-War-specific episode within the longer gender-history arc; compare the CWH-derived material above on how state and empire formation reshaped gender roles in earlier eras, see also [[wwi-cultural-disillusionment]] for the parallel intellectual ferment, and [[war-economy]] for the labour-mobilization structure within which this change occurred.

## Emic vs. Etic

"Gender" as an analytical category is etic and modern (post-1970s in its current
sense). But gender *systems* — emic structures of meaning and power — exist in
every documented society. The analytical task is to study emic gender systems
without imposing modern Western categories.

## Limitations and Critiques

- Risk of anachronism: applying modern gender categories to societies that
  organized sexual difference very differently.
- Extreme social constructivism risks depriving historians of any anchor in
  biological reality.
- The category itself emerged from a specific (Western, Anglophone) intellectual
  context and may not travel cleanly to all societies.
