# Science and Religion in Islam

## Definition and Scope

The relationship between the natural sciences and religious authority in Islamic civilization is a major historiographical question whose answer has profoundly shaped the narrative of world science. The dominant older model — imported from the European experience of the Galileo affair and the "warfare thesis" of Draper and White — assumed that religion inevitably impeded scientific inquiry, and sought the "decline" of Islamic science in the supposedly strangling effect of theological orthodoxy. Saliba's chapter 5 argues systematically that this model does not fit the Islamic evidence and must be replaced.

The Islamic case is not one of conflict between science and religion but of a structured, productive negotiation in which religious requirements *generated* new scientific disciplines, religious scholars *were* the astronomers, and mosque institutions *funded* cutting-edge theoretical research. At the same time, the relationship was not uniformly harmonious: astrology faced sustained religious opposition, and the precise border between permissible and impermissible astronomical knowledge was always contested and sometimes blurred.

## The Al-Ghazali Problem

The most influential version of the conflict thesis pins the "decline" of Islamic science on the philosopher and theologian al-Ghazali (d. 1111 CE) and his *Tahafut al-Falasifa* ("Incoherence of the Philosophers"), which attacked the Aristotelian philosophical tradition — the same tradition within which Islamic astronomy operated. The argument, repeated in textbook histories, is that al-Ghazali's critique delegitimized rational inquiry in Islamic society and thereby stifled scientific creativity.

Saliba's empirical evidence from the history of Islamic astronomy refutes this directly. The most innovative period of Islamic theoretical astronomy — the work of [[nasir-al-din-tusi|Nasir al-Din al-Tusi]] (d. 1274), [[qutb-al-din-al-shirazi|Qutb al-Din al-Shirazi]] (d. 1311), [[ibn-al-shatir|Ibn al-Shatir]] (d. 1375), Nizam al-Din al-Nisaburi (d. 1328), and [[al-khafri|Shams al-Din al-Khafri]] (d. 1550) — came *after* al-Ghazali, not before. These astronomers produced original mathematical theorems, challenged Ptolemaic astronomy at its foundations, and pressed the critique of Aristotelian cosmology to the point of near-collapse. Al-Ghazali did not strangle Islamic science; he preceded its most productive century and a half.

The proper reading of the evidence, Saliba argues, is that scientific creativity and religious authority in Islamic civilization coexisted not in tension but in alliance. The [[tahafut-al-falasifa|Tahafut]] targeted the *philosophical* tradition ([[islamic-philosophy|falsafa]]) — especially its Aristotelian metaphysics and its claims about the eternity of the world and the nature of causation — not the mathematical-astronomical sciences, which operated under different institutional and epistemological conditions.

## The Hay'a Solution: Science Redefined to Fit Religious Society

The most concrete mechanism by which astronomy made peace with religion was the invention of a new disciplinary category: *ʿilm al-hayʾa* ("the science of the configuration [of the world]"), distinct from the older astrology-linked Greek tradition. See [[ilm-al-hayya]] for a full account. The essential maneuver was to sever descriptive, mathematical astronomy from astrological prediction. Hay'a texts described and modeled planetary motions without drawing any conclusions about those motions' influence on sublunar human affairs.

This redefinition was "religiously guided away from astrology" (Saliba). Since astrology implied determinism — that planetary positions shaped human fate — it undermined individual moral responsibility and thus came into direct conflict with Islamic theology. By expurgating astrological doctrine, the new astronomy became acceptable to the religious establishment and could even be embraced as an aid to worship. The consequence was paradoxical from a conflict-thesis perspective: religious pressure produced a *more rigorous* and *more purely theoretical* astronomy than the Greek tradition had known.

> "The very discipline of hay'a was itself born within the critiques of the religious circles that frowned upon anyone who sought the guidance of the stars in the same way the astrologers did." — Saliba, ch. 5

## Religion as a Driver of Scientific Innovation

Saliba identifies specific religious requirements that generated entirely new scientific disciplines:

**1. The Qibla Problem.** The Islamic obligation to face Mecca in prayer required knowing the precise direction from any location on a spherical earth. Solving this problem required the angle between the local horizon and the great circle connecting the observer's zenith to Mecca's zenith. This demanded trigonometric functions — sine, cosine, tangent, cotangent — and spherical trigonometric laws (the spherical sine law, the spherical cosine law) that did not exist in the Greek tradition and were insufficient in the Indian tradition. The qibla problem was thus a primary driver of the development of spherical trigonometry as an independent mathematical field. "Such a religious commandment… was one of the reasons that gave rise to a most sophisticated discipline of spherical trigonometry" (Saliba).

**2. ʿIlm al-Miqat (Timekeeping).** The requirement of performing five daily prayers at precisely determined times, and of determining the beginning of Ramadan by lunar observation, drove the creation of a specialized science of Islamic timekeeping. See [[ilm-al-miqat]].

**3. ʿIlm al-Faraʾid (Inheritance Law).** The precise division of estates according to Quranic prescription required a specialized mathematical tradition. Saliba names this discipline alongside miqat and hay'a as products of religious requirements.

These three cases demonstrate a pattern: Islamic law and practice presented mathematically demanding problems; scientists solved those problems; the solutions fed back into the wider mathematical and astronomical toolkit. Trigonometry, which eventually became "subservient to other religious requirements, as much of it was used in almost every branch of miqat literature," also advanced "the mother discipline of astronomy just as much" (Saliba).

## The Social Location of Scientists: Mosque, Madrasa, and Court

One of Saliba's most important findings is the double identity of the leading theoretical astronomers. The hay'a tradition was not produced by a secularized intellectual class separated from religious life, but by men who were simultaneously major religious scholars:

- **[[nasir-al-din-tusi|Nasir al-Din al-Tusi]] (d. 1274)**: Produced the [[tusi-couple|Tusi Couple]], a foundational mathematical theorem of Islamic astronomy, while being an acknowledged authority on Ismaʿili and Twelver Shiʿite thought. To some Shiʿite biographers he was *primarily* a religious figure.
- **[[qutb-al-din-al-shirazi|Qutb al-Din al-Shirazi]] (d. 1311)**: Wrote major astronomical commentaries and served simultaneously as a judge (qadi) in Sivas and Malatiya (1282) and as a diplomatic intermediary between the Ilkhanids and the Mamluks. His Quranic commentary (*Fath al-mannan fi tafsir al-Qurʾan*) attests to wide mastery of religious disciplines.
- **Nizam al-Din al-Nisaburi (d. 1328)**: Wrote voluminous astronomical commentaries alongside an elaborate multi-volume Quranic commentary (*Gharaʾib al-Qurʾan*).
- **[[ibn-al-shatir|Ibn al-Shatir]] (d. 1375)**: The astronomer whose lunar model was later found to be identical to Copernicus's, and whose technical treatment of Mercury used the same Tusi Couple Copernicus used — held the post of *muwaqqit* (timekeeper) at the Umayyad Mosque in Damascus. His livelihood came from the mosque's religious endowment.
- **[[al-khafri|Shams al-Din al-Khafri]] (d. 1550)**: The most sophisticated Islamic astronomer of the sixteenth century and a demonstrator of how mathematics functions as a language of science, was also an official Shiʿite jurist in Safavid Iran, issuing fatwas on Shiʿi doctrines.

The muwaqqit position deserves special notice. It was a mosque office, funded by religious endowment (*waqf*), whose occupant determined prayer times using astronomical instruments and calculations. The muwaqqit was thus simultaneously a religious functionary and a practicing astronomer. Ibn al-Shatir's career exemplifies how the mosque itself became a site of scientific patronage for theoretical astronomy — not a site of its suppression.

A parallel example from medicine reinforces the pattern: Ibn al-Nafis (d. 1288), who discovered pulmonary blood circulation through his critical commentary on Avicenna's *Canon*, was simultaneously a practicing Shafiʿi lawyer who lectured at the Masruriya madrasa.

## The Distinction Between Astronomy and Astrology

Saliba insists on a crucial distinction that the conflict thesis tends to collapse: Islamic religion opposed *astrology*, not astronomy. Astrology was opposed because it attributed causal power over human affairs to planetary positions, thereby undermining moral agency and religious obligation. The opposition to astrology was not obscurantism; it was grounded in coherent theological reasoning.

Astronomy — once redefined as the mathematical description of planetary motions *without* astrological interpretation — was not only tolerated but actively cultivated. The religious establishment funded it (through waqf and mosque employment), incorporated it into madrasa curricula (at least for hay'a texts), and provided its leading practitioners. The Safavid Iranian seminaries, Saliba notes from interviews with graduates, still include hay'a texts in their curricula in the modern period.

The boundary between the two was nonetheless porous. Astronomical tables (zijes) designed for timekeeping could also be used for casting horoscopes; Ibn al-Shatir's *al-Zij al-jadid* is a case in point. "It is in such areas that the disciplinary borders begin to be blurred" (Saliba).

## The Philosophical Dimension: Astronomy Against Aristotle

The hay'a tradition also had a tense relationship with the Aristotelian philosophical framework (see [[islamic-philosophy]]) that it nominally accepted as its cosmological basis. Astronomers increasingly found that Aristotelian premises about the celestial realm were either impossible to reconcile with Ptolemaic models or internally incoherent.

This critique was not anti-religious — it was a technical debate conducted mostly within institutions that were also religious. What it produced was a sustained erosion of Aristotelian cosmological certainty, pressed across three centuries by figures who were both astronomers and religious scholars. Saliba sees in this erosion the remote intellectual roots of the collapse of Aristotelianism that defines the European Scientific Revolution. The route runs from Ibn al-Shatir's Damascus to Copernicus — not through a secular break with religion, but through a tradition embedded in mosque and madrasa.

> "This association with religion, contrary to what one would expect when using the European paradigm of conflict between science and religion, was apparently very healthy, and continued to support astronomers, one after the other, even at times when the astronomers' only source of income was provided by the religious institutions in which they served." — Saliba, ch. 5

## Historiography

The conflict thesis for science and religion in Islam derives largely from:
- The Draper-White "warfare" historiography of the 19th century, developed in the European Protestant context and uncritically applied to Islam.
- The figure of al-Ghazali, whose *Tahafut* was read as a blanket condemnation of rational inquiry rather than a specific philosophical polemic.
- Ignorance of the full chronological extent of Islamic astronomical innovation, most of which post-dates al-Ghazali.

Revisionist scholarship — represented by Saliba, A. I. Sabra, David King (on the muwaqqit institution), and others — has systematically dismantled this model. Key contributions include:
- Saliba's reconstruction of a socially embedded, religiously supported astronomical tradition lasting from the 9th to the 16th century.
- King's documentation of hundreds of muwaqqit manuscripts showing the scale of mosque-based astronomical production.
- The demonstration that the Maragha Observatory and its successors, which produced the most innovative Islamic astronomy, were funded by Ilkhanid and later patrons who also supported religious institutions.

The alternative is not a thesis of harmony without tension but a more nuanced account of structured negotiation: religion shaped the *purpose* and *social location* of science; science responded by reformulating itself in religiously compatible terms; the result was a tradition that was both innovative and institutionally stable across nearly seven centuries.
