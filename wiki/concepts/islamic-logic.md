Islamic logic (Arabic: *mantiq*) designates the centuries-long tradition of formal reasoning developed by philosophers in the Islamic world, rooted in Arabic translations of Aristotle's Organon but transformed — especially by Avicenna — into a distinctive system that diverged significantly from its Greek sources and from parallel Latin scholastic logic. The tradition spans roughly 800–1400 CE and passed through three recognized phases: a Farabian period of close Aristotelian commentary, an Avicennian revolution that recentered the discipline, and a "Golden Age" of formal modal logic in the 13th–14th centuries.

## Definition and Scope

Mantiq encompasses the full range of subjects treated in Aristotle's Organon — categories, propositions, syllogistics, demonstration, dialectic, rhetoric, poetics, and sophistry — but the Arabic tradition progressively narrowed its practical focus. As Ibn Khaldun (d. 1406) observed in retrospect, later logicians "dropped the investigation of the syllogistic with respect to matter" (i.e., the applied books: Posterior Analytics, Topics, Rhetoric, Poetics, Sophistical Fallacies) and concentrated on narrow formal questions of terms, propositions, and the syllogism itself: "they have treated in a penetrating way; they look into it in so far as it is a discipline in its own right, not in so far as it is an instrument for the sciences."

The tradition is thus distinct from — though interacting with — [[kalam]] (Islamic theology) and [[fiqh]] (Islamic jurisprudence), both of which eventually incorporated logical methods.

## Major Phases

**Transmission Phase (750s–900s CE).** The first Arabic logical texts were translated from Syriac in the 750s, beginning with Ibn al-Muqaffa' (d. 757). The Alexandrian curriculum transmitted to Syriac Christianity had been limited to the Categories, On Interpretation, and the assertoric syllogistic of the Prior Analytics. By the mid-9th century, scholars in the circles of al-Kindi (d. ca. 870) and Hunayn ibn Ishaq (d. 873) were producing higher-quality translations; Hunayn had particular veneration for Galen and made available many Galenic logical works. Commentaries by Alexander, Ammonius, and Themistius followed.

**Farabian Phase (900s–1050s CE).** The Baghdad Peripatetics — centered on Abu Bishr Matta ibn Yunus (d. 940) and [[al-farabi]] (d. 950) — shifted the tradition from summary learning to close Aristotelian exegesis. Al-Farabi "came to conceive his task as clearing away misinterpretations of the Aristotelian text, many the result of the Syriac summaries, and reviving true Peripatetic doctrine after a period of rupture." By ca. 1000 the Baghdad Peripatetics produced a heavily annotated Arabic Organon accurate enough for close philosophical work. Al-Farabi also made the crucial political-strategic move of integrating logic with Islamic jurisprudence: in his *Short Treatise on Reasoning in the Way of the Theologians*, he showed that theological arguments and juristic analogies were reducible to Aristotelian syllogisms. This opened the madrasa to logical instruction. Al-Ghazali (d. 1111) later endorsed logic as indispensable for jurisprudence, cementing its institutional place.

**Avicennian Revolution (1020s–1200s CE).** [[ibn-sina]] (Avicenna, d. 1037) transformed the tradition. Claiming the right by his own "intuition" (*hads*) to judge and correct Aristotle, he reinterpreted the Prior Analytics, introduced novel innovations — including a division of syllogisms into "conjunctive" and "repetitive" types — and developed a sophisticated modal logic anchored in the *dhati/wasfi* distinction (roughly: de re vs. contextual readings of modal propositions). After Avicenna, the reference point for Arabic logicians was Avicenna's system, not Aristotle's text directly.

**Golden Age and Post-Classical Phase (1200s–1400s CE).** Fakhr al-Din al-Razi (d. 1210) pioneered an expanded, penetrating treatment of formal questions. Al-Katibi's *Shamsiyya* (13th c.) — a concise manual organized around Terms, Propositions, and Syllogisms — became the canonical teaching text. Nearly one-third of the *Shamsiyya* is devoted to modal logic, reflecting the Avicennian inheritance. Al-Tusi (d. 1272) and later commentators continued to modify and extend Avicenna's system rather than return to Aristotle.

## The Modal Syllogistic

The most technically distinctive feature of Arabic logic is its modal syllogistic, developed in response to genuine problems in Aristotle's Prior Analytics. Aristotle's treatment of necessary propositions was internally inconsistent: a de re reading of modality validated certain syllogisms but invalidated conversions; a de dicto reading did the reverse. The Farabian tradition tried to rescue Aristotle through careful textual distinctions (per se vs. per accidens necessary propositions). Avicenna rejected the Farabian approach and instead introduced the *dhati/wasfi* distinction: a *dhati* reading takes the modal operator as belonging to the subject as such (essentially), while a *wasfi* reading takes it as belonging to the subject only as it is currently described. This allowed Avicenna to preserve Aristotle's syllogistic results while rejecting his conversions — or more precisely, to argue that Aristotle's own statements should be read charitably in whichever mode preserved validity. Averroes (d. 1198) sided with the Farabian approach and criticized Avicenna's division of syllogisms.

## Reception and Resistance

Logic's integration into Islamic intellectual life was contested. In the 930s, the grammarian Abu Sa'id al-Sirafi publicly mocked the Baghdad Peripatetics: "The world remains after Aristotle's logic as it was before his logic . . . you can dispense with the ideas of the Greeks as well as you can dispense with the language of the Greeks." Ibn al-Salah (d. 1245) condemned logic as an access road to philosophy (itself suspect); Ibn Taymiyya (d. 1328) conceded the formal validity of the syllogism while deriding its practical elaboration as "flesh of a camel found on the summit of a mountain — the mountain is not easy to climb, nor the flesh plump enough to make it worth the hauling." Despite such resistance, logic became a standard component of madrasa education by the 12th–13th centuries.

## Relation to Other Traditions

Arabic logic was transmitted to Latin Christendom from the 12th century onward, shaping scholastic logic. The *Shamsiyya* and similar Arabic texts share structural features with Latin treatises — the division into Terms, Propositions, and Syllogisms is common to both — because both descend from Aristotle. However, the modal logic of the Arabic tradition, especially the *dhati/wasfi* distinction, had no direct Latin parallel and was largely unknown to Western scholastics. Averroes' logical commentaries were more influential in the Latin West than Avicenna's logical innovations.

## Historiography

Source quality is strong: Tony Street's chapter in Adamson and Taylor's *Cambridge Companion to Arabic Philosophy* (2005) provides a systematic account from the translation movement through the Golden Age, using Ibn Khaldun as a historical frame. The collection includes the relevant texts (Katibi's *Shamsiyya*, al-Farabi's logical works, Avicenna's *Shifa'*). Scholarship on Arabic modal logic has accelerated since the 1990s, with Wisnovsky, Lameer, and Thom producing technical studies. The tradition was for long neglected by historians of logic who focused on Latin scholasticism; its recovery as a sophisticated independent tradition is a development of the last three decades.

## Collection Coverage Note

The Bibliotheca Alexandrina's collection for Islamic intellectual history is strong for the formative and classical periods (9th–13th centuries). The *Cambridge Companion to Arabic Philosophy* provides a reliable secondary synthesis. Primary texts in translation (Avicenna's *Shifa'*, al-Farabi's logical writings) are available in the Asian Classics / Oxford World's Classics streams. Coverage of post-1300 Arabic logic (the commentarial tradition on the *Shamsiyya*) is thin.
