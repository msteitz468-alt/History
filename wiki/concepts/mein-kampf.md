# Mein Kampf

Hitler's autobiography-manifesto, composed during his
[[beer-hall-putsch-1923|post-putsch]] "fortress incarceration" at Landsberg —
dictated to Emil Maurice and Rudolf Hess at publisher Max Amann's suggestion
(the title likely Amann's), heavily edited by Amann, Hanfstaengl, and others.
It sold modestly until the 1930 electoral breakthrough, then became a
bestseller and near-compulsory possession under the Third Reich.

## Content

Per Evans ([[evans-coming-of-third-reich-2003]]): turgid and tedious, but
unambiguous that racial conflict is "the motor... of history" and that Jewish
influence must be exterminated — "The nationalization of our masses will
succeed only when... their international poisoners are exterminated." Volume 2
laid out the *Lebensraum* doctrine tied to the destruction of the USSR: "The
boundaries of the year 1914 mean nothing at all for the German future...
the end of Jewish rule in Russia will also be the end of Russia as a state."
The Lebensraum concept reached Hitler through Hess from Karl Haushofer's
geopolitics.

## The "Blueprint" Question

Evans explicitly disputes the reading of *Mein Kampf* as a blueprint for
1933–45: "It was nothing of the kind." His framing (attributed): the tactical
foreign-policy emphases shifted with circumstance (France the enemy in 1925,
Britain a potential ally against Bolshevism), but the durable core across all
Hitler's writings of the period was (1) living-space in the East and (2)
annihilationist antisemitism — with the *how* and *when* undetermined. This
position sits within the intentionalist–functionalist debate recorded at
[[german-antisemitism]] and [[the-holocaust]].
