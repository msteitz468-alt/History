# The Medieval University (Studium Generale)

## Definition

The **medieval university** was a self-governing **corporation** (*universitas*) of
masters or students, devoted to advanced study, that emerged from the cathedral and
urban schools of the twelfth century. The contemporary term was ***studium generale***
— a school drawing students from across Christendom and granting a teaching licence
(*licentia ubique docendi*) valid everywhere. The university is the institutional
crown of the [[twelfth-century-renaissance|twelfth-century renaissance]] and one of
medieval Europe's most enduring inventions.

## Origins

[[luscombe-ncmh-v4-2004|NCMH IV]] (Peter Landau, Ch. 5; David Luscombe, Ch. 12) traces
the university's birth to two prototypes:

- **Bologna** — the centre of [[ius-commune|Roman and canon law]] from **Irnerius**
  (early 12th c.), where the foreign students organised themselves into corporations
  (*universitates*) of *Citramontani* and *Ultramontani* governed by **elected student
  rectors**. Frederick Barbarossa's privilege ***Authentica Habita* (1155)** and a
  privilege of Lucius III (1184) granted the law teachers and students legal protection
  and autonomy from the city — evidence that an organised scholarly community already
  existed. Bologna was the **student-run** model, and its mass secessions seeded rival
  schools (Vicenza, then Padua, 1222).
- **Paris** — growing out of the cathedral school of Notre-Dame and the masters of the
  schools (Abelard's milieu), it became the great centre of **theology and the arts**
  and the **master-run** model (a guild of teachers), formally recognised around 1200.

## Structure and Significance

The mature university comprised a faculty of **arts** (the trivium and quadrivium of
the liberal arts) and the higher faculties of **theology, law, and medicine**; it
conferred degrees, enjoyed corporate privileges and clerical status, and could defend
its autonomy through the ultimate weapon of **secession** (*cessatio*). Oxford emerged
from a migration from Paris; Bologna's law and Paris's theology became the templates
for the universities that spread across Europe in the thirteenth century and after.

The university institutionalised the [[ius-commune|learned law]] and scholastic
theology, produced the professional jurists and theologians who staffed the
[[papal-monarchy|papal monarchy]] and royal governments, and created a self-perpetuating
European institution of higher learning unmatched in the medieval Islamic *madrasa* or
Byzantine traditions in its corporate autonomy.
