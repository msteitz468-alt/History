# Communism

Communism is the political-economic ideology and international movement aiming at a classless, stateless society founded on collective ownership of the means of production, in which class exploitation and the state itself — theorized by Marx as an instrument of class domination — would ultimately wither away. Marx and Engels presented communism as the inevitable historical successor to capitalism, to be reached through proletarian revolution and an intermediate "dictatorship of the proletariat." Lenin's development of Marxist theory (Marxism-Leninism) provided the organizational doctrine — a disciplined vanguard party seizing and holding state power — that most 20th-century communist movements and states adopted in practice.

## Emic and Etic

As an emic category, "communism" was the self-description of ruling parties and revolutionary movements across the 20th century (the Soviet Union, the People's Republic of China, Cuba, and others), each claiming fidelity to Marxist theory while diverging sharply in practice — from Stalinist central planning and forced collectivization, to Maoist agrarian mobilization, to the more market-tolerant "Eurocommunism" of certain Western European parties. As an etic historical category, "communism" is used comparatively to analyze this family of movements and regimes, none of which achieved Marx's theorized stateless, classless endpoint; critics and many historians instead describe the 20th-century communist states as having produced durable, often highly repressive one-party bureaucratic regimes.

## Limitations and Debate

Scholars distinguish sharply between communism as Marx's theoretical aspiration and communism as the practical governing system of 20th-century states, and debate how much continuity of doctrine versus contingent, nationally specific improvisation explains the wide variation among self-described communist regimes.
