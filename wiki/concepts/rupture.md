# Rupture (French WWI Operational Concept)

**Rupture** (French: *rupture* of enemy lines) was the central operational goal of French strategy in the 1914–1915 period: achieving a penetration or breakthrough of the German fortified front on the Western Front, followed by exploitation with reserves to take the enemy in the rear, disrupt communications, and force a general retreat. It represented the adaptation of prewar decisive-battle thinking to the new conditions of trench warfare.

## Definition and Scope

Rupture meant creating a breach wide and deep enough that follow-on forces (reserves, cavalry) could pass through and operate in open country beyond the enemy's initial and secondary defensive positions. It was not an end in itself but the prerequisite for restoring mobility and achieving decisive results ("drive the enemy toward the northeast and prepare a subsequent action ... against his communications").

Key elements in planning:
- Choice of point(s) of attack (shoulders of the Noyon salient: Artois for Vimy/Cambrai; Champagne for Mézières).
- Concentration of "powerful means of action" (infantry, especially heavy artillery).
- "General action" (supporting attacks across front to fix enemy and mask main effort).
- "Decisive action" on chosen sector.
- Immediate exploitation.

Foch (March 1915 note to Joffre): "The form of this offensive always remains the same. It includes: a general action along the entire front; a decisive action, a central attack, on the portion of the front which provides the opportunity for the desired result, breaking through the enemy." The decisive sector should be one "where the enemy could not reestablish his fortified position a short distance to the rear."

## Development in 1914–1915 (Doughty)

- **Post-Marne/Oct 1914**: Franchet d’Espèrey proposed choosing a point, concentrating troops and especially heavy artillery, and driving through. Joffre approved but circumstances disrupted. Fifth Army attack (Oct 12–14) failed due to lack of artillery; revealed difficulties of breakthrough against entrenched positions.
- **Nov–Dec 1914 planning**: Operations Bureau studies shifted from single general offensive to attacks on shoulders of Noyon salient (Artois main, Champagne supporting). "Enemy forces within the salient ... will find themselves squeezed between the two jaws of a vise." Assumed sufficient (or soon sufficient) resources.
- **Dec 1914–Mar 1915 execution**: Principal attacks in Artois (Tenth Army, Vimy objective: "occupation of this crest will undoubtedly have a significant effect and will result in a breakthrough") and Champagne (Fourth Army: "The purpose of the operation is to make a breach in the enemy line"). Supporting attacks to fix. Continuous battle tactics used to maintain momentum after initial penetration.
- **1915 renewal**: After Gorlice-Tarnów, renewed emphasis on rupture to relieve Russia. "Seizing Vimy Ridge" as key to breakthrough. September double offensive explicitly aimed at rupture and exploitation (cavalry prepared to advance to Mons/Mézières; reserves positioned close).

Joffre's orders: "The moment has come to resume the offensive that will drive the enemy toward the northeast..." Objective twofold: hold enemy in place and "make a breach ... then exploit this success with reserve troops by taking the enemy in the rear and forcing him to retreat."

## Why It Failed (per source)

- Insufficient artillery (esp. heavy/long-range) and ammunition for wire-cutting, counterbattery, and destruction of reverse-slope/second positions.
- Narrow attack fronts vulnerable to flanking fire and rapid German reinforcement.
- Enemy adaptation: multiple defensive bands (second band 4–5 km back, third 15 km), immediate counterattacks, barrages.
- Logistics/reserves issues: reserves often not close enough or committed piecemeal; bad weather (rain, fog, snow) repeatedly disrupted prep and exploitation.
- Intelligence and reporting failures (false breakthrough reports, e.g. Sept 1915 Trench of Tantes).
- German ability to shift (interior lines) faster than French could achieve decisive effect.

By Oct 1915: "Despite the lavish use of artillery and loss of thousands of lives, Joffre’s huge offensives in Artois in May–June and in Artois and Champagne in September–October had accomplished very little." Joffre himself later blamed "insufficient artillery."

## Relation to Other Concepts

- **Partial offensive / nibbling**: Supporting or interim actions while preparing for rupture.
- **Continuous battle**: Tactical method intended to convert rupture into exploitation.
- **Attrition (usure)**: By mid-1915 Joffre acknowledged elements of attrition while still seeking rupture; "drain the enemy and attrit him."
- **Methodical battle**: Later doctrinal reaction emphasizing detailed artillery preparation over rapid continuous pressure.

## Historiography

Doughty emphasizes that French leaders were not blind to difficulties; they understood the material prerequisites (Joffre repeatedly told politicians not to expect great results until siege weapons ready) but strategic necessity (multifront pressure, Russian survival, occupied territory) overrode tactical caution. Internal debates (Foch aggressive, Castelnau more cautious on means, Pétain critical of execution and reserves) are well documented. The concept's persistence despite repeated failure illustrates the tension between France's grand strategy and battlefield reality.
