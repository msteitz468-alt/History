# Generic Fascism

Whether "fascism" names a single generic phenomenon beyond Mussolini's
Italy — and if so, what defines it — is the founding question of fascism
studies. This page records the definitional field with Paxton's *Anatomy
of Fascism* ([[paxton-anatomy-of-fascism-2004]]) as its spine, since that
book is both a definition and a survey of all the rival definitions.

## The emic origin

"Fascism was the major political innovation of the twentieth century, and
the source of much of its pain" — and, unlike conservatism, liberalism,
and socialism, it "was still unimagined as late as the 1890s" (Paxton).
The name comes from *fascio* (bundle), from the Latin *fasces*; before
1914 the symbol was usually claimed by the **Left** (the Fasci Siciliani
of 1893–94 were a peasant revolt; the fasces appear on the Lincoln
Memorial). Officially, Fascism was born in Milan on 23 March 1919, when
some hundred veterans, pro-war syndicalists, and Futurists founded the
Fasci di Combattimento to "declare war against socialism… because it has
opposed nationalism." Its 1919 program was "a curious mixture of veterans'
patriotism and radical social experiment, a kind of 'national socialism'"
— women's suffrage, the eight-hour day, partial expropriation of wealth,
seizure of Church properties — a program on which Mussolini won 4,796 of
315,165 Milanese votes in November 1919, and which the movement then
shed step by step as it rooted itself in Po Valley anti-socialism
("They ask us what is our program?… We want to govern Italy").

## Rival etic definitions

- **Marxist agent theory** (Third International, hardened under Stalin):
  fascism as "the open, terroristic dictatorship of the most reactionary,
  most chauvinist and most imperialist elements of finance capital."
  Paxton's verdict: it "denied fascism's autonomous roots and authentic
  popular appeal" and ignored human choice; empirically, "real
  capitalists, even when they rejected democracy, mostly preferred
  authoritarians to fascists" — though after power, "capitalism and
  fascism made practicable bedfellows (though not inevitable ones, nor
  always comfortable ones)."
- **Totalitarianism theory** (Friedrich & Brzezinski 1956; see
  [[communism-and-fascism]]): fascism and Stalinism as one genus defined
  by the aspiration to total control. Paxton's critique: it is "blind to
  th[e] fundamental character of the Nazi governing system" — Hitler,
  "totally unlike Stalin, came into power with the assent and even
  assistance of traditional elites," whereas Stalin ruled a civil society
  the revolution had already leveled; it also mistakes the disorderly
  fiefdom-reality of fascist rule for an efficient machine.
- **Flynn (1944)**: the fullest contemporary economic-structural
  definition — fascism as an eight-point policy syndrome (totalitarian
  government, dictator, state-operated capitalism under bureaucracy,
  syndicalist/corporative organization, planned autarky, debt-financed
  purchasing power, militarism as spending mechanism, imperialism), grown
  from parliamentary deficit politics; explicitly severed from
  anti-Semitism and national character ("the impartial microbe"), and
  aimed polemically at the New Deal ("the good fascism"). Full statement
  at [[as-we-go-marching-thesis]]; the post-1990 consensus definitions
  below reject its economics-first premise, treating racial doctrine and
  paramilitary violence — which Flynn brackets — as constitutive.
- **Nolte (1963)**: fascism as "resistance to transcendence," bounded to
  its 1919–45 epoch.
- **Griffin (1991, 1995 reader, 2007)**: "Fascism is a genus of political ideology whose mythic core in its various permutations is a palingenetic form of populist ultranationalism" — the most widely accepted one-sentence definition. Paxton (bibliographical essay): Griffin's "zeal to reduce fascism to one pithy sentence seems to me more likely to inhibit than to stimulate analysis..." Now ingested ([[griffin-nature-of-fascism-1991]] + [[sources/griffin-fascism-1995]] + 2007): the definition is offered explicitly as a Weberian ideal type — no definition "can be 'true' in the descriptive sense, but only useful"; the rival definitions on this page are all "competing ideal types." Griffin drops the negations and style, includes Nazism emphatically, supplies [[para-fascism]] boundary and retrodictive model. The 1995 reader adds primary grounding: Mussolini "Our myth is the nation, our myth is the greatness of the nation!"; Papini/Marinetti "rejuvenate Italy" / "most beautiful Futurist poem"; Codreanu "spiritual resurrection of the race"; Mosley "Caesarian movement"; Levi on the "deadly trunk of fascism." Full statement at [[palingenetic-ultranationalism]].
- **Payne (1995)**: typological description in three clusters (ideology
  and goals; the fascist negations — antiliberalism, anticommunism,
  anticonservatism; style and organization), compressed to "a form of
  revolutionary ultranationalism for national rebirth… based on a
  primarily vitalist philosophy… structured on extreme elitism, mass
  mobilization, and the Führerprinzip, positively valu[ing] violence as
  end as well as means" — offered explicitly as a Weberian ideal type,
  "used only in a tentative sense," against both the "either-or"
  reduction of all fascisms to one identity and radical nominalism
  (Allardyce). Payne critiques Griffin's definition as over-inclusive
  ("palingenetic" and "populist" fit non-fascist cases like Bolivia's
  MNR, 1952) and Nolte's six-point minimum as all negation and no
  positive content. With Nolte and De Felice he holds fascism "a
  historical phenomenon primarily limited to Europe during the era of
  world wars" — Japan (following Kasza) "a somewhat pluralistic
  authoritarian system" closer to the Wilhelmine Second Reich, apartheid
  South Africa "taxonomically confused" as fascism, Peronism "most but
  not all" of the characteristics. Full apparatus (three-sector typology,
  retrodictive theory) at [[payne-typology-of-authoritarian-nationalism]];
  Paxton calls his *History of Fascism* "the most authoritative narrative
  history… but it describes better than it explains." Ingested:
  [[payne-history-of-fascism-1995]].
- **Payne (1980)** — the original statement, now ingested
  ([[payne-fascism-comparison-definition-1980]]). *Fascism: Comparison and
  Definition* opens from the premise that "fascism is probably the vaguest
  of contemporary political terms" — the word itself "has no implicit
  political reference" (fascio = bundle), most movements called fascist
  never used the name, and Italian Fascists codified doctrine "only ex
  post facto." Against Nolte's six-point fascist minimum (antimarxism,
  antiliberalism, anticonservatism, leadership principle, party army, aim
  of totalitarianism), which "correctly states the fascist negations, but
  postulates three primary characteristics derived especially from German
  National Socialism," Payne offers the **Table 1 "Typological Description
  of Fascism"** in three clusters — the tripartite structure suggested to
  him by Juan Linz at Bergen in June 1974, "the specific content... my
  own":
  **(A) The fascist negations**: antiliberalism, anticommunism,
  anticonservatism (with willingness for tactical alliances, most commonly
  with the right).
  **(B) Ideology and goals**: a new nationalist authoritarian state "not
  merely on traditional principles or models"; a "regulated, multiclass,
  integrated national economic structure, whether called national
  corporatist, national socialist, or national syndicalist"; empire or "a
  radical change in the nation's relationship with other powers"; "an
  idealist, voluntarist creed," aiming at "a new form of modern,
  self-determined, secular culture."
  **(C) Style and organization**: aesthetic structure of meetings/symbols
  with "romantic and mystical aspects"; attempted mass mobilization with
  "militarization of political relationships" and a mass party militia;
  "positive evaluation and use of, or willingness to use, violence";
  "extreme stress on the masculine principle" ("no other kind of movement
  professed such complete horror at the slightest suggestion of
  androgyny"); "exaltation of youth above other phases of life"; a
  tendency toward "an authoritarian, charismatic, personal style of
  command, whether or not the command is to some degree initially
  elective."
  The description is "a loose wide-spectrum description," "not... a
  rigidly reified category," and applies **only to interwar European
  movements, not to a presumed category of fascist regimes** — since "only
  in the case of Germany were the full implications of a fascist doctrine
  — in the form of its most radical variant — carried out by a regime in
  power." Payne rejects Nolte's "aim of totalitarianism" criterion
  ("fascist movements never projected a state theory with sufficient
  centralization and bureaucratization to make complete totalitarianism
  possible") and Nolte's "resistance to transcendence" (fascist culture
  was "a specific attempt to achieve a modern, normally atheistic form of
  transcendence" — a direct byproduct of the Enlightenment's "modern,
  secular, Promethean concepts," not opposition to it). He likewise notes
  H.-J. Puhle's economic critique of Nolte but holds Puhle "goes too far
  in giving fascism a conservative slant."
- **Sternhell (1989/1994)**: fascism as a coherent ideology formed in
  pre-1914 France ("neither Right nor Left"), an "integral part of the
  history of European culture" — the maximal ideological reading, opposed
  by **Arendt**, for whom Nazism "owed nothing to any part of the Western
  tradition… dreaming the stupid dream of producing the void." Now
  ingested ([[sternhell-birth-fascist-ideology-1994]]): "the Fascist
  ideology represented a synthesis of organic nationalism with the
  antimaterialist revision of Marxism," becoming "a conceptual system when
  the rejection of materialism and rationalism becomes the essence of a
  total political outlook... accompanied by a strong cultural pessimism...
  and a cult of elitism and violence." Sternhell also draws the sharpest
  fascism/Nazism boundary in the field: since "the criterion of German
  national socialism was biological determinism" and racism "was not a
  necessary condition for the existence of fascism," "a general theory
  that seeks to combine fascism and Nazism... is not possible" — directly
  against Nolte and every unified generic model, including partially this
  page's. Full statement at [[sternhell-thesis]].
- Other candidates Paxton surveys and finds wanting: psychoanalytic
  readings (repression no worse in Germany than Britain); Arendt's
  atomized mass society (Weimar society was "richly structured" — Nazism
  spread by capturing intact clubs: "two Germans, a discussion; three
  Germans, a club"); developmental-dictatorship theory (fascism "did not
  wish to develop the economy but to prepare for war"; Italy grew faster
  before 1914 and after 1945); Lipset's lower-middle-class "extremism of
  the center" (recruitment cut across all classes); political religion
  (useful analogy, weak explanation); cultural studies (decodes
  self-presentation but rarely asks how influential it was —
  "Hollywood, Beale Street, and Madison Avenue probably gave more trouble
  to fascist dreams of cultural control than the whole liberal and
  socialist opposition put together").

## Paxton's five-stage processual model

Against the search for a static "fascist minimum," Paxton defines fascism
by what fascists **did**, through a cycle of five stages: **(1)** the
creation of movements; **(2)** their rooting in the political system;
**(3)** their seizure of power; **(4)** the exercise of power; **(5)** the
long duration — radicalization or entropy. Each stage is a prerequisite
for the next, but nothing requires completion: most societies produced
Stage One movements; only Italy and Germany reached regimes; "only in
Nazi Germany did a fascist regime approach the outer horizons of
radicalization." Load-bearing findings at each stage:

- **Rooting** requires a faltering liberal state and available political
  space: Po Valley squadrism succeeded because the liberal state would
  not break strikes and local elites armed the Blackshirts (in the first
  half of 1921 the squads destroyed 59 People's Houses, 119 Chambers of
  Labor, 107 cooperatives); Dorgères's French Greenshirts failed because
  the French state sent gendarmes immediately. "Fascist interlopers
  cannot easily break into a political system that is functioning
  tolerably well."
- **Getting power** never happened by election or coup: the
  [[march-on-rome-1922|March on Rome]] "was a gigantic bluff that
  worked"; Hitler was "hoist into office by a backstairs conspiracy"
  ([[nazi-seizure-of-power-1933]]). "The fascist route to power has
  always passed through cooperation with conservative elites." Every
  authoritarian regime that could rule alone crushed its fascists instead
  (Antonescu and the Legion, Franco and the Falange, Salazar and the
  National Syndicalists).
- **Exercising power** is a **four-way tug-of-war** among leader, party,
  state apparatus, and traditional civil society, within Fraenkel's
  **dual state** (normative vs. prerogative). Fascist regimes "functioned
  like an epoxy: an amalgam of two very different agents, fascist
  dynamism and conservative order, bonded by shared enmity toward
  liberalism and the Left."
- **Radicalization**: fascist regimes need "an impression of driving
  momentum — 'permanent revolution'" — or decay into "a tepid
  authoritarianism" (Franco, Salazar); war is "indispensable for the
  maintenance of fascist muscle tone (and, in the cases we know, the
  occasion for its demise)."

## Paxton's definition (2004)

> "Fascism may be defined as a form of political behavior marked by
> obsessive preoccupation with community decline, humiliation, or
> victimhood and by compensatory cults of unity, energy, and purity, in
> which a mass-based party of committed nationalist militants, working in
> uneasy but effective collaboration with traditional elites, abandons
> democratic liberties and pursues with redemptive violence and without
> ethical or legal restraints goals of internal cleansing and external
> expansion."

Underlying the behavior are nine **"mobilizing passions"** (fascism "was
an affair of the gut more than of the brain"): a sense of overwhelming
crisis; the primacy of the group and subordination of the individual;
belief that one's group is a victim, justifying any action against
enemies internal and external; dread of the group's decline under
individualistic liberalism, class conflict, and alien influences; the
need for a purer community, by consent or exclusionary violence; the
need for authority by natural chiefs (always male), culminating in a
national chieftain incarnating the group's destiny; the superiority of
the leader's instincts over abstract reason; the beauty of violence and
efficacy of will in the group's service; the right of the chosen people
to dominate others without restraint, "right being decided by the sole
criterion of the group's prowess within a Darwinian struggle."

## Boundaries

Paxton's boundary-drawing is as load-bearing as the definition:

- **vs. classical tyranny**: fascism is "a phenomenon of failed
  democracies" (Salvemini: "giving up free institutions" to street
  acclamation); predemocratic dictatorships "lack the manipulated mass
  enthusiasm and demonic energy of fascism." Simply being murderous is
  not enough (Idi Amin was not a fascist).
- **vs. military dictatorship**: "while all fascisms are always
  militaristic, military dictatorships are not always fascist."
- **vs. authoritarianism** — "one of the most essential" boundaries:
  authoritarians rule through intermediary bodies (Church, landowners,
  army), prefer a demobilized, passive public, and a strong-but-limited
  state; "fascists want to engage and excite the public." Hence Franco's
  Spain ("always more Catholic than fascist"), Salazar's Portugal (which
  *suppressed* its fascist movement in 1934), and [[vichy-france]]
  ("certainly not fascist at the outset, for it had neither a single
  party nor parallel institutions") fall on the authoritarian side —
  consistent with the placement in [[paxton-vichy-france-1972]].
- **Functional equivalents**: Paxton treats symbols as non-diagnostic
  ("There is no sartorial litmus test for fascism") and admits functional
  equivalents outside the classic cases — Milošević's Serbia ("a de facto
  dictatorship with fervent mass support engaged in the killing of men,
  women, and children… it seems appropriate to recognize a functional
  equivalent when it appears"); the first Ku Klux Klan as "arguably a
  remarkable preview of the way fascist movements were to function in
  interwar Europe" (the earliest phenomenon "that can be functionally
  related to fascism is American"); imperial Japan, by contrast, is
  "better understood as an expansionist military dictatorship" — "It was
  as if fascism had been established in Europe as a result of the
  crushing of Mussolini and Hitler"; Peronism as national-populist
  developmental dictatorship (no diabolized enemy, no expansionist war,
  workers' income share *raised*); al-Qaeda/Taliban not fascist because
  "they have not 'given up free institutions,' since they never had any."

## Limitations and critiques

The processual definition is itself contested. Griffin and the
"new consensus" object that defining fascism by behavior and alliances
dissolves its ideological core; Sternhell that it understates the
coherence of fascist ideology; De Felice ended by doubting any generic
category works at all. From the opposite flank, the
Hayek–Goldberg–D'Souza line ([[fascism-and-the-political-left]],
[[road-to-serfdom-thesis]]) rejects the field's right-wing placement of
fascism altogether — Paxton's own emphases (fascism's anti-socialist
violence, its conservative-elite alliances, its preservation of property)
are Position 1's core evidence, while his documentation of the
syndicalist pipeline, the 1919 "national socialism," and fascism's
"neither Right nor Left" self-description supplies material Position 2
also cites. The wiki records that dispute without adjudication.

## Griffin (2007): Fascism as Political Modernism

*Modernism and Fascism* extends the palingenetic model of [[palingenetic-ultranationalism|The Nature of Fascism (1991)]] into a full theory: "fascism itself can be seen as a political variant of modernism." The culminating definition (ch. 6): "FASCISM is a revolutionary species of political modernism originating in the early twentieth century whose mission is to combat the allegedly degenerative forces of contemporary history (decadence) by bringing about an alternative modernity and temporality (a 'new order' and a 'new era') based on the rebirth, or palingenesis, of the nation"; shorthand: "a form of programmatic modernism that seeks to conquer political power in order to realize a totalizing vision of national or ethnic rebirth." The old modern/anti-modern dichotomy (Hewitt's "crucial axis") is "transcended (aufgehoben) in almost Hegelian fashion": fascism is a form of political modernity *and* a radical reaction against actually-existing modernity, seeking "the temporality of the new." Griffin proposes that "'modernism', 'the [[revitalization-movement]]', and (in the case of the two regimes) 'the gardening state' should be considered definitional components of generic fascism," inserted into Emilio Gentile's cluster (totalitarianism, political religion, charismatic politics, palingenetic myth, anthropological revolution, organic nationalism) — and distances himself from the "wild-goose chase for the 'fascist minimum'."

Applied to Paxton's five stages: fascist movements arose in the "acutely liminoid conditions" of interwar high modernity; the seizure of power occurred only in Italy and Germany ("two case studies are an inadequate empirical basis for a generic ideal type of fascism's 'life-cycle' beyond the movement stage"), in each case through "a nomic crisis of national proportions" plus "the collusion or acquiescence of political elites prepared to lever the fascist leader into power." In power, the regimes' cult of permanent revolution left "no exit strategy, no way of turning off the perpetuum mobile"; the "anthropological revolution" was "intrinsically unrealizable." Fascism's syncretism (Mussolini's "great river"; the "Rorschach test" of Italian modernism) is Wallace's mazeway resynthesis writ large. Methodological coda contra Bosworth-style pure empiricism, quoting Tim Mason: "If we can now do without much of the original contents of the concept of fascism, we cannot do without comparison"; "Nazism was a peculiar part of something much larger." (Source: [[griffin-modernism-and-fascism-2007]].)

## Payne's 1980 conclusion: movements, regimes, and the boundedness of the era

Ch. 9 of [[payne-fascism-comparison-definition-1980]] states the positions
the 1995 *History* later consolidates. Against "a rigorous 'either-or'
approach": reducing all fascisms to one identity is "distortive and
inaccurate," radical nominalism "has the opposite defect." Six varieties
of European fascism (paradigmatic Italian; German National Socialism —
"the most extreme or radical form... the only fascistic movement to
achieve a total dictatorship"; Catholic-traditionalist Falangism; the
"mystical, kenotic" Romanian Legion; Szálasi's Hungarism; and the
abortive top-down fascisms of east-European authoritarian regimes).
Because most fascist parties never passed the movement stage — blocked by
the "40-percent barrier" and dependence on allies who usually overwhelmed
them — "fascist regime" can be used only loosely: the real genus is the
**syncretic national authoritarian regime**, which Payne argues "has
become the most common new political form of the twentieth century," in a
seven-type spectrum from the Hitler regime ("the only completely fascist
regime-system," yet "only one specific form of fascism") through
Mussolini's semipluralist dictatorship, the wartime satellites, syncretic
dictatorships with fascist components (Spain 1937–45, Romania 1940–44),
failed top-down semifascisms, conservative/bureaucratic-nationalist
regimes (Estado Novo, Vargas, Piłsudski, Horthy), and praetorian regimes
(the Greek Colonels, post-1964 Brazil, post-1973 Chile).

The 1980 retrodictive preconditions (compressed to three variables,
anticipating the 1995 apparatus at
[[payne-typology-of-authoritarian-nationalism]]): **cultural** — the
nationalist/militarist/Social-Darwinist doctrines of the WWI generation
fused with vitalism and the hero cult, "most strongly developed in the
German- and Italian-speaking worlds and their cultural hinterland";
**political** — *not* the breakdown of established democracy ("none of the
fully established liberal democratic systems... broke down") but defeat
and status deprivation striking polities "where the transition to
mobilized liberal democracy had just gotten underway"; **social** —
expanded middle sectors unadjusted to industrial liberal democracy;
"concretely, it affected seriously only the new countries of the 1860s —
Germany, Italy, and Austria-Hungary." Perspective correction: "Before
1939, antifascists, both voters and activists, always outnumbered
fascists in Europe as a whole"; the Falange's 0.7 percent "was much
nearer the norm than the 38 percent won by the Nazis in 1932." Fascism's
militarized style put host polities in "a state of political war," which
no system tolerates long — movements had "to win power in less than a
generation and in some cases within only a few years."

Why it cannot recur in recognizable form: the imperialist dynamics of the
1860s states were destroyed in 1945, and "the cultural underpinnings of
generic fascism have been completely eroded... All competing ideological
forces now share a common humanist materialism." Ch. 7 applies the test
outside Europe (Japan, China, South Africa, Latin America, Peronism) and
finds no full case — Perón, who called Mussolini "the greatest man of our
century" while vowing to "avoid his errors," comes nearest, with
Qaddafi "nearer the style and typology than anyone since Perón" among
later candidates; Bardèche (the one noteworthy postwar fascist
intellectual to define fascism) himself calls Third World fascisms
"false fascisms." What *does* recur are five fascist-derived features:
permanent nationalist one-party authoritarianism; charismatic leadership;
synthetic ethnicist ideology; corporatist/syndicalist/partial-socialist
political economy; and voluntarist activism — "to that extent the
influence of fascism will continue to be felt for years to come, not
least among some of the most vociferous formal antifascists."
