## Definition and Scope

This page documents al-Ghazālī's argument in Discussion 17 of the *[[tahafut-al-falasifa|Tahāfut al-Falāsifa]]* against the philosophical doctrine that causes and effects are necessarily connected — that fire *must* burn cotton when brought into contact with it, that eating *must* produce satiety, that decapitation *must* produce death. Al-Ghazālī denies this necessity and argues that what we observe is a *habitual* conjunction established and continuously maintained by God's will, not a logical or natural necessity inherent in things themselves.

This argument is distinct from but closely related to [[occasionalism-islamic]]. Where that concept page covers the broader occasionalist tradition in Islamic thought (including the Ash'arite mutakallimūn before al-Ghazālī), this page focuses specifically on al-Ghazālī's *philosophical* articulation of the critique, his precise formulation of what is and is not impossible, and his two-part response to the strongest objection. Discussion 17 is the single most cited passage in the *Tahāfut* in Western philosophy of science and religion, and its argument must be distinguished from a blanket skepticism about causality.

## The Target: Necessary Concomitance

Al-Ghazālī opens Discussion 17 by identifying the first of four points in natural science where the revealed law requires contention with the philosophers:

"Their judgment that this connection between causes and effects that one observes in existence is a connection of necessary concomitance, so that it is within neither the realm of power nor within that of possibility to bring about the cause without the effect or the effect without the cause." (*Natural Sciences*, Introduction, §19)

The examples he catalogs span all of natural philosophy: "the quenching of thirst and drinking, satiety and eating, burning and contact with fire, light and the appearance of the sun, death and decapitation, healing and the drinking of medicine, the purging of the bowels and the using of a purgative, and so on to all that is observable among connected things in medicine, astronomy, arts, and crafts." (Discussion 17, §1)

His reason for contending with this is explicit: if causal connections are logically necessary, then miracles are impossible. The philosophers then interpret Qur'anic accounts of miracles — the staff of Moses becoming a serpent, the revivification of the dead, the splitting of the moon — as metaphors, because literal readings would require violations of logical necessity. Al-Ghazālī takes this as a challenge to the entire prophetic tradition.

## The Central Claim

"Their connection is due to the prior decree of God, who creates them side by side, not to its being necessary in itself, incapable of separation. On the contrary, it is within divine power to create satiety without eating, to create death without decapitation, to continue life after decapitation, and so on to all connected things." (Discussion 17, §1)

The key term is *jār al-'āda* — the habitual course, the custom (*'āda*) God has established. Fire burns cotton not because burning is logically entailed by the nature of fire and cotton, but because God habitually creates burning whenever fire contacts cotton. The conjunction is real and reliable — it is not arbitrary or random — but it is contingent on divine will, not logically necessary.

## The Burning Cotton Argument

Al-Ghazālī focuses on the paradigm case: fire in contact with cotton.

**The philosophers' position (as al-Ghazālī reconstructs it):** Fire acts as an agent by its nature, not by choice. It cannot refrain from burning a receptive substrate. Fire is fire; burning is what fire does when it contacts cotton. To say otherwise is to claim that fire's heat could be present without any burning effect, which is self-contradictory.

**Al-Ghazālī's first reply:** "The one who enacts the burning by creating blackness in the cotton, causing separation in its parts, and making it cinder or ashes is God, either through the mediation of His angels or without mediation. As for fire, which is inanimate, it has no action. For what proof is there that it is the agent? They have no proof other than observing the occurrence of the burning at the juncture of contact with the fire. Observation, however, only shows the occurrence of burning at the time of the contact with the fire but does not show the occurrence of burning *by* the fire and that there is no other cause for it." (Discussion 17, §5)

This is a point about the limits of inductive inference: regular co-occurrence does not establish causal agency. Al-Ghazālī draws a parallel: the infusion of life, sight, and hearing into the animal sperm is not caused by the father placing the sperm in the womb. No one says these powers are *produced by* the father, even though they *occur with* his act. The father is a preparatory condition, not the efficient cause. The same is true of fire and burning.

### The Blind Man Analogy

Al-Ghazālī illustrates with a striking thought experiment:

"If a person, blind from birth, who has a film on his eyes and who has never heard from people the difference between night and day, were to have the film cleared from his eyes in daytime, then open his eyelids and see colors, such a person would believe that the agent causing the apprehension of the forms of the colors in his eyes is the opening of his sight and that, as long as his sight is sound, his eyes opened, the film removed, and the individual in front of him having color, it follows necessarily that he would see, it being incomprehensible that he would not see. When, however, the sun sets and the atmosphere becomes dark, he would then know that it is sunlight that is the cause for the imprinting of the colors in his sight." (Discussion 17, §6)

The point: habitual accompaniment deceives observers into identifying the wrong cause. Only a disruption of the habit reveals the true causal structure. Our confidence that fire burns cotton rests on the same kind of habitual familiarity — it does not rest on knowledge of the true causal mechanism.

### The Bestower of Forms

Al-Ghazālī notes that the more rigorous Avicennan philosophers themselves already concede a version of his point: they attribute the "imprinting of forms" in matter not to secondary causes but to the "bestower of forms" (*wāhib al-suwar*), an angelic intellect. "The imprinting of the form of color in the eye comes from the bestower of forms, the sun's appearance, the healthy pupil and the colored body being only 'readiers' and preparers for the receptacle's acceptance of these forms." (Discussion 17, §8) If the philosophers themselves deny that the sun directly causes vision, al-Ghazālī argues, they have already abandoned the necessity thesis for the most paradigmatic perceptual case.

## The Skepticism Objection and Al-Ghazālī's Response

The philosophers raise the most powerful objection to al-Ghazālī's position (Discussion 17, §§13–17): if causal connections are not necessary but only customary, then rational life becomes impossible. One would have to allow that:

- A book left in a house might return as a horse or a dog
- A jar of water might have turned into an apple tree
- A human walking toward you might have spontaneously generated from a fruit in the marketplace
- One could not trust any prediction about the future

"If someone leaves a book in the house, let him allow as possible its change on his returning home into a beardless slave boy — intelligent, busy with his tasks — or into an animal; or if he leaves a boy in his house, let him allow the possibility of his changing into a dog; or if he leaves ashes, let him allow the possibility of its change into musk." (Discussion 17, §13)

### Al-Ghazālī's Two-Part Answer

**First reply** (Discussion 17, §§14–17): The objection assumes that denying necessity entails uncertainty about what will happen. But al-Ghazālī has not denied that God creates knowledge in us. "We are not rendered skeptical by the illustrations you have given because God created for us the knowledge that He did not enact these possibilities. We did not claim that these things are necessary. On the contrary, they are possibilities that may or may not occur. But the continuous habit of their occurrence repeatedly, one time after another, fixes unshakably in our minds the belief in their occurrence according to past habit." (Discussion 17, §15)

God creates both the habitual sequence of events *and* the habitual knowledge that this sequence holds. Our certainty about fire burning cotton is real and reliable; it is just not *logically* necessary — it is divinely established habitual certainty. Miracles are possible because God can, at prophetically significant moments, fail to create the burning even when the contact occurs, and simultaneously create in witnesses the knowledge that something unusual has happened.

**Second reply** (Discussion 17, §18): Al-Ghazālī offers a more concessive approach. Even granting that fire "is created in such a way that, if two similar pieces of cotton come into contact with it, it would burn both" — even granting fire's habitual burning as a kind of natural property — it remains within divine power to act on the fire or on the body in contact with it. God or the angels can create "a quality in the fire which restricts its heat to its own body so as not to transcend it." He cites the empirical example of talc: someone who covers themselves with talc and sits in a fiery furnace is not burned. "The one who has not witnessed this will deny it." (Discussion 17, §18) Strange protective properties exist in nature; their existence is not logical nonsense. The burning of Abraham without harm is the same kind of phenomenon, only further along the spectrum of unusual natural properties.

## What Al-Ghazālī Does NOT Claim

Al-Ghazālī explicitly defines the limits of divine power through a careful account of the impossible:

"The impossible is not within the power of being enacted. The impossible consists in affirming a thing conjointly with denying it, affirming the more specific while denying the more general, or affirming two things while negating one of them. What does not reduce to this is not impossible, and what is not impossible is within divine power." (Discussion 17, §29)

Therefore:
- God *cannot* make blackness and whiteness coexist in the same respect in the same subject, because blackness is defined partly as the negation of whiteness.
- God *cannot* create a will without knowledge of what is willed, because "will" is defined as the seeking of a known object.
- God *cannot* change genera in the strong sense — substance into accident, blackness into a cooking pot — because "there is no common matter" between such categories; one would simply cease and the other begin.
- God *can* move a dead man's hand such that ordered writing results, because the writing is well-designed and indicates the knowledge of its *actual* agent (God), not the dead man. This does not erase the distinction between voluntary movement and tremor; al-Ghazālī argues that we apprehend this distinction in ourselves directly.

The upshot: al-Ghazālī's is a *theologically bounded* occasionalism, not global skepticism about causality and not the claim that anything whatsoever is possible.

## Connection to Miracles

The argument of Discussion 17 is framed explicitly as the foundation for the affirmation of miracles. Al-Ghazālī lists three Qur'anic miracles the philosophers cannot accommodate: the staff of Moses becoming a serpent, the revivification of the dead, and the splitting of the moon. He argues that if material is receptive of all forms — "earth and the rest of the elements change into plants, plants into blood, blood into sperm, sperm into animal" — then God can cycle matter through these stages at miraculous speed without violating any logical necessity. Miracles are disruptions of *habit*, not violations of *logic*. (Discussion 17, §19)

## Significance and Influence

Discussion 17 has been read as:

1. **An anticipation of Humean skepticism about causation.** David Hume's argument in the *Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* that we observe constant conjunction but not necessary connection has structural parallels to al-Ghazālī's argument. The relationship is debated; there is no clear documentary transmission through to Hume, though the *Tahāfut* circulated in Latin under the title *Destructio Philosophorum* and influenced later scholastic discussions.

2. **A defense of prophetic miracles within an intellectually rigorous framework.** Al-Ghazālī is not retreating from philosophy; he is using philosophical tools to clear conceptual space for miracles.

3. **A statement of Ash'arite occasionalism at the level of systematic philosophy.** Earlier Ash'arite mutakallimūn (theologians) had held similar doctrines but in the context of *kalām* (dialectical theology) rather than as a direct engagement with Aristotelian natural philosophy. Al-Ghazālī bridges the two registers.

4. **A target for Averroës.** [[ibn-rushd|Ibn Rushd]] in the *Tahāfut al-Tahāfut* argued that al-Ghazālī's position destroys the possibility of science, medicine, and practical knowledge. Averroës held that the denial of necessary causal connection makes the connection between fire and burning as arbitrary as the connection between fire and cold — an absurdity. This exchange defines a major fault-line in medieval Islamic philosophy.

## Historiography

The primary source is Marmura's English translation of the *Tahāfut al-Falāsifa*, Discussion 17. The most important secondary scholarship includes:

- Michael Marmura's own notes to the translation, which trace the argument's internal logic carefully.
- Barry Kogan's work on Averroës's response to al-Ghazālī on causation.
- Taneli Kukkonen's analyses of al-Ghazālī's occasionalism and its relation to earlier Ash'arite atomism.
- The debate about Hume and al-Ghazālī, surveyed in Majid Fakhry's *Islamic Occasionalism* (1958) and more recent revisionist work questioning direct influence.

The collection at Bibliotheca Alexandrina is strong for this topic: the *Tahāfut* itself is in hand; the *Tahāfut al-Tahāfut* (Averroës's response) would complement it. Coverage of the later reception in Latin scholasticism is absent.
