# Settlement Archaeology

**Settlement archaeology** is the study of the full spatial and hierarchical patterning of human occupation in a landscape — site locations, densities, sizes, functions, and interrelationships — rather than isolated monuments or artifact assemblages.

## Key Themes in the Source

One element "conspicuously missing from much of earlier archaeology was a concern with the size and density of the population." Large-scale landscape studies using survey and aerial photography now allow estimates of prehistoric population figures and "document the entire pattern of human use of a region and its resources, and the range of types of settlement occupied." (Champion et al. 1984, Ch.1 / range lines ~550-565)

Particular interests include:
- Locations chosen for occupation sites (indicating immediate economic basis).
- Range of sites used by a single community.
- Emergence of site hierarchies (some sites acting as centres of power or authority).

## Methodological Shift

Earlier work focused on typology and chronology of artifacts (especially pottery and metalwork). Settlement archaeology requires new fieldwork designed for new questions, full or near-full excavation of sites to recover spatial patterns, and integration of environmental data (pollen, soils).

"The structures and artifacts of a settlement site, for example, and the patterns in which they are found can be used as the key to reconstructing the patterns of behaviour of its occupants." (Champion et al. 1984, Ch.1 / range lines ~575-585)

## Relation to Other Approaches

Settlement studies are inseparable from subsistence reconstruction and social organization analysis. Site hierarchies and monumental public architecture (palaces, temples, elaborate funerary monuments) are treated as expressions of social differentiation. "The effect of a social hierarchy in stimulating change has also been studied." (Champion et al. 1984, Ch.1 / range lines ~680-690)

## Application: The Chinese Neolithic (Liu 2004)

[[liu-chinese-neolithic-2004|Li Liu's *The Chinese Neolithic*]] is a sustained application of
settlement archaeology to [[early-china-state-formation|state formation]] in the
[[yellow-river-valley|Yellow River valley]], explicitly **rejecting the artifact "checklist"**
definition of the state in favour of spatial and mortuary patterning at three nested scales —
**household, community, and region**:

- **Settlement-hierarchy analysis**: natural breaks in site-size histograms yield settlement
  tiers, correlated with administrative levels and population (2 tiers ≈ simple
  [[chiefdom]]; 3 ≈ complex chiefdom; **4 ≈ state**, as at [[erlitou|Erlitou]]).
- **Rank-size analysis**: log-normal curves indicate integrated systems; **primate** curves a
  dominant single center ([[taosi|Taosi]] early phase, Erlitou); **convex** curves competing,
  weakly-integrated polities ([[yiluo-basin|Yiluo]], central Henan).
- **Mortuary statistics**: the **coefficient of variation (CV)** of grave goods and the **R²**
  correlation of grave size to goods measure burial differentiation (egalitarian Group I sites
  CV<0.9, R²≤0.21; stratified Group II sites CV>1.3, R²≥0.39), with the *ercengtai* ledge a
  status marker.
- **Household archaeology** (Wilk & Rathje; Kent): reading floor assemblages for feasting,
  craft specialisation, and gendered tool kits as indices of complexity.

Liu also flags method caveats: traditional Chinese surveys were biased toward likely-inhabited
areas (full-coverage survey at Yiluo found 200+ sites in 200 km²), and highland/lowland site
sizes are partly depositional artifacts.

## Wickham, Framing (2005) — late antique / early medieval settlement

[[wickham-framing-the-early-middle-ages-2005|Wickham, *Framing* (2005)]] (esp. Chs. 7–8) treats settlement
pattern as a first-class comparative variable for **400–800**, with explicit limits on what form can prove
about landowning.

### Two caesurae; method caveat

Two durable frames: (1) East/West difference in the importance of **villages** versus more dispersed
western Roman rural settlement; (2) the end of the western rural **villa** around the fall of the western
empire, replaced by varied post-Roman patterns ([[end-of-the-roman-villa]]). Settlement patterns do **not**
stably define economic relationships: aristocrats can dominate concentrated or dispersed landscapes;
peasant autonomy can take either form. Late Roman western dispersal ≠ necessarily only great estates;
later western agglomeration often signals seigneurial control—the opposite of the late Roman inference.

### Levant and Egypt: village worlds

Across studied Syria and Palestine, a high point of village settlement falls in the fifth or sixth century
(often extending into the seventh–eighth); contraction is irregular and rarely before the eighth; structural
stability (few major form changes beyond churches) until contraction/abandonment. Concentrated unplanned
stone courtyard-house villages were normal almost everywhere; isolated villas/estate-centres and Umayyad
palaces existed but were marginal. Wickham rejects early apocalyptic general causes of Syro-Palestinian
abandonment (plague 541–2, Arab conquest 630s, Abbasid capital shift 750, nomads); revised dates push
depopulation later (e.g. Limestone Massif ninth/tenth). LaBianca’s intensification/“abatement” language is
useful but does not erase real agrarian and demographic decline—crises were local within a landscape that
can still be treated as a block. At **Nessana** (Negev), papyri (510s–680s) show local soldier-farmers and
owner-cultivators without external landowning; by the 680s a locally rich headman family still operated
within community representatives and judges—wealth concentrated at village level ([[roman-syria]],
[[roman-egypt]]).

### Western Mediterranean after 500; northern house groups

For Tuscany (analogously Lazio/Spain), post-500 patterns separate into: (1) continuance of dispersed
hierarchy of simpler estate-centres and scattered peasant houses; (2) articulation around relatively
concentrated villages; (3) complete breakdown of hierarchies (e.g. south-eastern Chianti)—with overwhelming
shift to wood building as cultural as much as economic change. South of a rough line east from the Rhine
mouth, villages were less coherent (smaller houses, more *Grubenhäuser*, less orderly layouts—Frankish–
Alemannic culture-area). Anglo-Saxon England belongs with this southern group despite North Sea coastal
origins: long-houses abandoned in the extreme simplicity of the first Anglo-Saxon century (Mucking, West
Stow); clearer modularity only after ~600 ([[merovingian-rural-settlement]], early-middle-ages Britain).

### Ceramic simplicity and the peasant mode

Weak utilitarian artisanal traditions (esp. hand-made, localised ceramics) and weak settlement hierarchy
are empirical markers of [[peasant-mode-of-production|peasant-mode]] dominance—e.g. fifth–eighth-century
southern/eastern England, Denmark, NE Ireland; aceramic Wales/rest of Ireland; Alicante coast late
seventh–ninth centuries; south-eastern Chianti sixth–eighth centuries vs Valdelsa estate centres. Either
marker alone is suggestive; both together support peasant economic autonomy with more confidence
([[scandinavian-peasantry]]).
