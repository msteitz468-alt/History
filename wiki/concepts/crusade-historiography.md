## Definition and Scope

The historiography of the crusades encompasses all scholarly and popular writing about the crusading movement, from the earliest medieval chronicles of 1095 down to the present. Because the crusades engaged the military, religious, and political interests of Latin Christendom, Byzantium, the Islamic world, and the Jews, historical accounts have always been shaped by where, when, by whom, and in whose interests they were written. Giles Constable, in his contribution to Laiou and Mottahedeh's *The Crusades from the Perspective of Byzantium and the Muslim World* (Dumbarton Oaks, 2001), provides the most systematic treatment of this topic to date and organizes the Western historiography into three broad periods.

One foundational question underlies all modern scholarship: what was a crusade? Contemporaries had no single agreed term — crusades were referred to by terms indicating movement or travel (*peregrinatio*, *iter*, *via*, *expeditio*, *passagium*) often combined with reference to Jerusalem, the Holy Land, or the cross. The word *crozada* appeared first in Spain and southwestern France in the early thirteenth century, and *crusade* was not common in English before the eighteenth century. This terminological indeterminacy opened the door to enduring definitional disputes.

## Origins and Development

### Period I — Medieval to c. 1600

During the first and longest period, the crusades were part of a living struggle in which the Muslim world remained a continuing threat to Western Christendom. Historical accounts were essentially ongoing propaganda for the movement and are inseparable from it. Early chronicles — the anonymous *Gesta Francorum*, Guibert of Nogent, Fulcher of Chartres, Albert of Aachen, Raymond of Aguilers — each presented the crusade from the perspective of a particular hero (Bohemund, Baldwin, Godfrey, Raymond of St. Gilles). The greatest crusader historian of the period, **William of Tyre**, wrote his *Chronicon* from the perspective of a Latin born in the east, recording "for the everlasting memory of the faithful of Christ" how God "wanted to relieve the long-lasting oppression of His people."

The process Constable calls the *affabulation* of the First Crusade began immediately: later accounts reworked earlier ones through the lens of the crusade's ultimate success (the capture of Jerusalem), so that what the crusade "should have been" overshadowed what it actually was. By the fourteenth century, the crusades had become part of national mythologies. Humanist historians such as Benedetto Accolti (1464) wrote histories of the First Crusade explicitly to inspire new campaigns against the Turks. Throughout this period, vernacular crusading epics — above all the cycle of Godfrey of Bouillon — dominated popular perception.

### Period II — 1600–1800 (Enlightenment Critique)

The second period opened with Jacques Bongars's critical collection of primary sources, *Gesta Dei per Francos* (1611), and Thomas Fuller's *Historie of the Holy Warre* (1639), called the first serious general history to treat the crusades as fully in the past and to question their legitimacy. The period is dominated by Enlightenment rationalism. **Voltaire** (in his book on the crusades, 1751, incorporated into his *Essai sur les moeurs*) called the crusaders adventurers and brigands motivated by "the thirst for brigandage." **Edward Gibbon** described their principle as "a savage fanaticism," though he expressed grudging admiration for their spirit. The pro-Catholic **Louis Maimbourg** (1670s) offered the opposite confessional tilt. Constable cites Laetitia Boehm's judgment that Enlightenment historiography "cultivated with regard to the crusades a one-sidedness of treatment from which the nineteenth century only slowly freed itself."

### Period III — 1800 to Present (Modern Scholarship)

The third period began under romanticism and nationalism, which rehabilitated the Middle Ages. The major multi-volume histories of **Friedrich Wilken** (Germany, 1807–32) and **J. F. Michaud** (France, 1812–22) introduced scholarly apparatus while retaining enthusiastic tones; Michaud's 1877 illustrated edition (with Gustave Doré engravings) marks the high point of romantic-nationalist enthusiasm. The Académie des Inscriptions launched the *Recueil des historiens des croisades* (critical primary-source editions in Latin, Greek, Arabic, Armenian, and Old French) from 1824. **Leopold von Ranke** gave the first impulse to critical source study in his Berlin seminar; his student **Heinrich von Sybel** placed the First Crusade on a new scholarly footing in his 1841 *Geschichte des ersten Kreuzzugs*, which included the first sustained treatment of crusading historiography.

In the twentieth century two dominant narrative syntheses appeared: **René Grousset** (1934–36), an Orientalist who saw the crusades in east-west terms, and **Steven Runciman** (1951–54), a Byzantinist who concluded in his *History of the Crusades* that the crusades were "a tragic and destructive episode" and "a long act of intolerance in the name of God." The six-volume Wisconsin *History of the Crusades* (1955–89), edited by Kenneth Setton, covered factual history, art, and the impact on East and West in detail. The shorter *Oxford Illustrated History of the Crusades* (ed. Riley-Smith) gave greater space to late-medieval crusading, the military orders, and crusading ideology.

The decisive methodological shift came with **Carl Erdmann's** *Entstehung des Kreuzzugsgedankens* (1935; English: *The Origin of the Idea of Crusade*, 1977), which traced the ideological origins of holy war in papal reform theology, the Peace and Truce of God, and the concept of the *milites sancti Petri*, rather than treating the crusade as a sudden eruption. Etienne Delaruelle and Paul Alphande'ry worked along parallel lines, emphasizing the popular, eschatological, and charismatic dimensions of the movement.

## Scholarly Debates

### Traditionalists vs. Pluralists

The central modern definitional controversy pits **traditionalists** against **pluralists**:

- **Traditionalists** (leading figure: Hans Eberhard Mayer) hold that a true crusade must be directed toward the east — either to assist Christians there or to liberate Jerusalem and the Holy Sepulcher. They regard crusades in Spain, northern Europe, or against heretics as corruptions or deformations. For them the crusading movement effectively ended with the fall of the crusader states.
- **Pluralists** (leading figure: Jonathan Riley-Smith) hold that the defining feature of a crusade, whatever its objective, is **papal authorization** combined with the vow, the cross, and the indulgence. They extend the history of crusading geographically (Spain, Baltic, Egypt) and chronologically into the early modern era.
- A third position emphasizes the **spiritual/psychological** definition: the inner spirit, eschatological motivation, and popular response of crusaders. For Alphande'ry and Delaruelle, the only "true" crusade was the First, marked by mass popular religious enthusiasm. Subsequent crusades became increasingly institutionalized and, in a sense, spiritually diluted.
- A fourth position, associated with **Ernst-Dieter Hehl**, defines a crusade as a war fought at the order and authority of God — a *Deo auctore* war — in which Urban II's innovation was to fit the crusade into a "historical-theological schema" or "theology of war."

Constable himself, while counted among the pluralists for his work showing that contemporaries regarded the Second Crusade's Iberian and Baltic campaigns as part of the same movement, is reluctant to exclude "popular" crusades or to deny that a spiritual orientation toward Jerusalem was essential.

### Economic and Colonial Interpretations

The Enlightenment interpretation of the crusades as driven by greed has been revived by modern economic theorists. An article in the *Journal of European Economic History* (1992) analyzed the crusades as a supply-side response to Muslim competition with the Latin Church's monopoly over salvation ("a pure credence good") and as a wealth-maximizing strategy of ecclesiastical expansion. **Joshua Prawer's** work on the Latin Kingdom raised the question of whether the crusader states should be understood as **colonies** in the modern sense — a question debated vigorously in the 1980s and still unresolved.

### Crusades against Christians

The use of crusading against Christians (the Albigensian crusade, the Fourth Crusade's sack of Constantinople in 1204, later political crusades) was contested by contemporaries as well as by later historians. For traditionalists it represents a deformation of the crusading ideal; for pluralists it follows logically from the definition by papal authorization. The Fourth Crusade's diversion to Constantinople has generated more than a century of scholarly dispute over whether it was planned or accidental.

### Numbering and Periodization

There is no scholarly consensus on how to number the crusades. The standard sequence (First through Eighth or Ninth) counts only major general expeditions; it excludes smaller crusades, the Children's Crusade, the Albigensian, Spanish, and Baltic crusades, and various unauthorized popular movements. Mayer noted that "even the numbering of crusades I–IV is a dubious affair."

### When Did "Crusade" Emerge as a Term?

Contemporaries used no single term. The earliest known use of *crozada* is in Spain and southwestern France in a charter of October 1212. *Croisade* remained rare in medieval French; *crusade* only became common in English in the eighteenth century. This means that the concept was constructed retrospectively — as Christopher Tyerman has argued, the First Crusade only appeared as the beginning of a coherent movement "retrospectively when that movement existed, after 1187."

### Non-Western Historiography

Constable notes that the historiography of the crusades as seen from outside the West has been comparatively thin. The major general histories of the crusades by Grousset, Runciman, and Mayer contain no sustained treatment of non-Western perspectives. The Dumbarton Oaks symposium (1997) that produced Laiou and Mottahedeh's volume was explicitly designed to address this gap by examining the crusades from Byzantine and Muslim perspectives. Non-Western sources — the *Alexiad* of Anna Komnene, Arabic chronicles, Armenian accounts, Hebrew chronicles of the 1096 Rhineland massacres — exist and are used by scholars, but the secondary literature synthesizing them is recent and still developing.

## Limitations and Critiques

- **Presentism and political interest.** Constable argues that writings on the crusades must always be interpreted in light of the position from which they were written. Nineteenth-century scholarship was shaped by romanticism and nationalism; twentieth-century hostility reflects decolonization, anti-war sentiment, and the Middle East conflict. Popular depictions of the crusades as "violent white supremacist colonialism" (John Ward, 1995) have gained currency in the general public even as scholars have become more nuanced.
- **Western-centrism.** The field has been dominated by Western scholars working primarily on Western sources, with Byzantine and Muslim perspectives underrepresented in general syntheses.
- **The definition impasse.** No satisfactory definition of a crusade has won universal assent. Each of the four main definitions (traditionalist, pluralist, spiritual/psychological, theological-war) excludes or distorts some phenomena that were clearly understood as crusades by participants.
