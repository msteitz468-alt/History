## Definition

Ibn Khaldun's four-generation rule states that dynastic prestige (including the group feeling, ([[asabiyyah]]), and personal qualities that sustain it) characteristically exhausts itself over four successive generations. The claim is developed in [[the-muqaddimah|the Muqaddimah]], Chapter II, section 14, "Prestige lasts at best four generations in one lineage."

The rule is part of Ibn Khaldun's broader argument about the [[the-dynastic-cycle-ibn-khaldun]]: luxury and sedentary life ([[bedouin-and-sedentary-society]]) erode the desert virtues that produced political power, and this erosion follows a predictable generational sequence.

## The Four Generations

Ibn Khaldun describes each generation's epistemic and practical relationship to the founding glory:

**Generation 1 — The Builder**
Knows from direct experience what the glory cost: "the builder of the glory (of the family) knows what it cost him to do the work, and he keeps the qualities that created his glory and made it last." Active knowledge, full asabiyyah, personal investment in maintaining the conditions that produced power.

**Generation 2 — The Inheritor with Personal Contact**
Learned from the founder directly and thus knows the principles, if not from personal struggle: "The son who comes after him had personal contact with his father and thus learned those things from him. However, he is inferior in this respect to (his father), in as much as a person who learns things through study is inferior to a person who knows them from practical application."

**Generation 3 — The Traditionalist**
Has no personal contact with the founder; relies on transmitted accounts and imitation. "The third generation must be content with imitation and, in particular, with reliance upon tradition. This member is inferior to him of the second generation, in as much as a person who relies (blindly) upon tradition is inferior to a person who exercises independent judgment."

**Generation 4 — The Destroyer**
Has lost the qualities that built the dynasty and — crucially — does not know that he has lost them. He believes the prestige is natural to his lineage, not earned through effort:

> "He has lost the qualities that preserved the edifice of their glory. He (actually) despises (those qualities). He imagines that the edifice was not built through application and effort. He thinks that it was something due his people from the very beginning by virtue of the mere fact of their (noble) descent, and not something that resulted from group (effort) and (individual) qualities."

This fourth-generation member keeps away from those who share his group feeling, treats them as inferiors, and expects obedience he has not earned through the qualities obedience requires (humility and respect toward followers). They revolt; leadership transfers to a related branch with more intact qualities. The dynasty declines.

## Corroboration from Islamic Sources

Ibn Khaldun marshals several Islamic references to support the four-generation claim:

- **Quranic support**: the Torah passage "God... visiting the sins of the fathers upon the children unto the third and the fourth (generations)" is read as indicating that four is the generational limit of either transmitted guilt or transmitted prestige
- **Prophetic hadith**: Muhammad's description of Joseph as "the noble son of the noble (father) of the noble (grandfather) of the noble (great-grandfather)" is taken to indicate that four successive generations of nobility constitutes the maximum
- **Historical example from Khosraw and al-Nu'man**: Khosraw asked al-Nu'man (king of al-Hirah) whether any Arab tribe exceeded others in nobility; the criterion given was "three successive ancestors who were leaders, and where the fourth generation, then, was perfect" — four generations as the ceiling of dynastic prestige

The families identified as meeting this standard in pre-Islamic Arabia: the house of Hudhayfah b. Badr (Qays), the house of Hajib b. Zurarah (Tamim), the house of Dhu'l-Jaddayn (Shayban), and the house of al-Ash'ath b. Qays (Kindah).

## Scope and Flexibility

Ibn Khaldun qualifies the rule: dynasties may collapse in fewer than four generations under unusual pressure, or may persist into the fifth or sixth in a state of decline without complete collapse. The four-generation schema is a typical pattern, not an iron law. The underlying mechanism — progressive epistemic distance from the founding conditions of power — is what matters; the four-generation frame is the modal expression of that mechanism.

## Nobility Without Group Feeling

Section 14 also contains a distinct argument about prestige in cities versus prestige in tribal groups. Urban inhabitants may claim "noble" ancestry, but Ibn Khaldun argues this is a metaphorical or fictive form of prestige:

> "Isolated inhabitants of cities can have a 'house' only in a metaphorical sense. The assumption that they possess one is a specious claim."

Real prestige (a "house" in Ibn Khaldun's technical sense) requires group feeling, because prestige is the product of qualities exercised through collective action, not mere ancestry. City dwellers who lost their asabiyyah generations ago and retained only the memory of noble descent are engaged in a "delusion." The Israelites in his own time are his most emphatic example: great noble lineage (patriarchs, prophets, kings), but millennia of exile without group feeling have made the claim hollow. He extends the same critique to Averroes (Ibn Rushd), who defined prestige as depending on "ancient settlers in a town" — a mistake, Ibn Khaldun argues, that arose because Averroes had no experience of group feeling.

## Analytical Significance

The four-generation rule is one of the more specific and falsifiable claims in the Muqaddimah. It translates the abstract theory of asabiyyah decay into a concrete demographic prediction: a dynasty should expect serious internal weakness within roughly a century (assuming 25-year generations). Whether this matches observed Islamic dynastic lifespans has been a subject of quantitative study by later historians of the Islamic world.

The rule also has a structural implication: it means no dynasty is inherently stable, and political renewal must always come from outside the established ruling family — either from a collateral branch retaining desert virtues, or from an entirely new group. This forecloses the possibility of indefinitely self-sustaining hereditary monarchy in Ibn Khaldun's model, making [[asabiyyah]] the only genuine basis of political legitimacy.
