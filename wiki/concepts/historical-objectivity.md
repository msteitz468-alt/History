# Historical Objectivity

Whether, and in what sense, historians can produce knowledge of the past that is more
than a projection of their present standpoint. Primary source for this page: Richard J.
Evans, *In Defence of History* (1997), chs. 7–8, drawing on Peter Novick, *That Noble
Dream* (1988) and Thomas Haskell's response.

## Three Classic Positions

**Carr's teleological objectivity.** For E. H. Carr (*What is History?*, 1961) an
objective historian is not one who merely gets facts right (mere "antiquarianism") but
one who can "rise above the limited vision of his own situation" and "project his
vision into the future" — objectivity as alignment with history's direction. Evans's
critique: Carr's direction was a Soviet-style planned economy, and his fourteen-volume
*History of Soviet Russia* systematically omitted the "defeated alternatives" — the
civil war, the Cheka's terror, the victims — because they were not "progressive."
Isaiah Berlin called it "the most monumental challenge of our time to that idea of
impartiality"; Trevor-Roper: "No historian since the crudest ages of clerical bigotry
has treated evidence with such dogmatic ruthlessness." The teleology collapsed with the
USSR in 1990 — as did Fukuyama's mirror-image "end of history" teleology, confounded by
resurgent religious fundamentalism. Objectivity cannot be conformity to an imagined
future.

**Elton's documentary objectivity.** Geoffrey Elton: the past has independent reality;
the historian becomes "the servant of the sources," asking no questions "except those
posed by the documents themselves." Evans's critique: Elton's own work refutes him. His
preface to *The Tudor Revolution in Government* declared "government must be strong to
be worth having"; his *Policy and Police* exonerated Thomas Cromwell while (per
Lawrence Stone's charge of *suppressio veri*) omitting Cromwell's 1534 memo "to have
substantial persons in every good town to discover who speaks or preaches" for papal
authority. Preconceptions denied are preconceptions unexamined. Yet the Elton–Stone
dispute also shows the constraint works: Elton had to answer the charge by reference to
the 1534 memo, not by politics.

**Postmodernist dissolution.** Objectivity as a power effect; all interpretations
equally valid within their perspectives (Somekawa and Smith: shift "the grounds for the
assessment of integrity from the absolute or objective truth to the moral or
political"). See [[postmodernism-and-history]]. Evans's rebuttal: two contradictory
answers to the same question (did big business put the Nazis in power?) cannot both be
correct; total relativism self-refutes (Boghossian) and licenses fascist or Stalinist
history "without fear of contradiction except on moral or aesthetic or political
grounds."

## The Holocaust-Denial Limit Case

Evans treats Holocaust denial as the empirical refutation of "all interpretations are
equally valid." Deborah Lipstadt: denial is "antisemitic ideology," not "responsible
historiography," and debating deniers grants unearned legitimacy; the "both sides"
framing on US campuses reflected the relativist climate. A university history
department's formulation, quoted approvingly: legitimate revision "is not concerned
with the actuality of these events; rather it concerns their historical
interpretation — their causes and consequences." The deniers' pseudo-scholarly
apparatus is "easily unmasked as a sham" — which is only possible if footnotes are more
than a rhetorical "reality-effect." Jane Caplan: theories that "resist the claims of
truth... cannot assign responsibility clearly" trivialize precisely "a recent history
of lives, deaths, and suffering." (Note the identity-epistemology corollary Evans also
attacks: if only lived experience licenses history, the oppressed could not write the
history of their oppressors — Boghossian — and history would reduce to autobiography;
right-wing versions include Bridenbaugh's 1962 AHA address against historians of
"foreign origins" and Braudel's claim that only the French can really write French
history.)

## The Novick–Haskell Resolution (Evans's position)

Novick concluded the objectivity ideal is "essentially confused" and the discipline,
as a community of common purpose, "had ceased to exist" — yet his own book, Evans
notes, is objective "in most normal senses": it neither distorts evidence nor treats
positions unfairly. Haskell's repair: do not confuse objectivity with **neutrality**.
Objectivity is "that vital minimum of ascetic self-discipline that enables a person
to... abandon wishful thinking, assimilate bad news, discard pleasing interpretations
that cannot pass elementary tests of evidence and logic, and... enter sympathetically
into the alien and possibly repugnant perspectives of rival thinkers." Detachment, not
indifference: politically committed history is legitimate and "only damages itself if
it distorts, manipulates or obscures historical fact in the interests of the cause it
claims to represent" (Evans's examples of such damage: Bernal's *Black Athena*,
Boswell on medieval homosexual identity).

Evans's concluding formulation (the book's most-quoted passage): "it really happened,
and we really can, if we are very scrupulous and careful and self-critical, find out
how it happened and reach some tenable though always less than final conclusions about
what it all meant." Truth about patterns in history is "discovered not invented, found
not made" (Novick's correspondence definition), though — Haskell's caveat — via
imaginative construction that goes well beyond the raw materials.

## Limitations

The concept remains contested: Evans's synthesis is one position within
[[postmodernism-and-historical-knowledge]], not a resolution of it. His account of
Carr as "essentially a Stalinist" is itself polemical (Carr was anathematized by
official Soviet historiography). The Evans-vs-critics exchange (Jenkins, Munslow,
2000 edition afterword) is not covered by the current source base.
