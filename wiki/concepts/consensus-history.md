# Consensus History

The dominant school of post-war American historiography, holding that
American history is characterized by broad agreement on fundamentals —
private property, individual rights, capitalist democracy — rather than by
the class and sectional conflict emphasized by the earlier Progressive
school (Beard, Turner, Parrington). An **etic** historians' framework,
though it analyzes an **emic** phenomenon: the actual belief-world of
Americans who, in Hartz's terms, lived inside an unexamined Lockean creed.

## Principal statements

- **Richard Hofstadter**, *The American Political Tradition* (1948) —
  American political fights occur within "the horizon of property and
  enterprise"; ironic-critical register.
- **Daniel Boorstin**, *The Genius of American Politics* (1953) —
  celebratory: American "givenness" happily spared the country ideological
  politics.
- **Louis Hartz**, *[[hartz-liberal-tradition-1955|The Liberal Tradition in
  America]]* (1955) — the most theoretical version: the
  [[lockean-consensus]], produced by the absence of feudalism, is real,
  total, and *dangerous* — the source of conformity and McCarthyite
  "Americanism." Hartz is a consensus historian who deplored the consensus.

## Context and critiques

The school flourished in the Cold War climate, when American unity against
communism made conflict-centered history seem overdrawn — critics (Higham,
"The Cult of the 'American Consensus,'" 1959) charged it with projecting
1950s complacency backward. From the 1960s it was displaced by: the **New
Left** recovery of class, race, and violence; the **republican synthesis**
(Bailyn, Wood, Pocock) arguing the Founders spoke classical-republican, not
Lockean, language; and **Rogers Smith's "multiple traditions"** thesis
(*Civic Ideals*, 1997) that ascriptive hierarchies (slavery, nativism,
patriarchy) were co-equal American traditions, not deviations from
liberalism. Yet the persistence-of-liberalism question keeps returning;
Hartz remains the standard citation for the narrowness of the American
ideological spectrum.

## Limitations

Underweights the enslaved, Native Americans, and other groups outside the
consensus; struggles with the [[american-civil-war-1861-1865|Civil War]],
the largest conflict in American history (Hartz treats it as the exception
that proves the rule — see the "Reactionary Enlightenment" of
[[fitzhugh-george]] and [[calhoun-john-c]]); and tends to conflate the
absence of European-style ideology with the absence of conflict.
