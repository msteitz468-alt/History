# Threat That Leaves Something to Chance

A threat in which "the final decision is not altogether under the threatener's control." The uncertain element — accident, third-party action, momentum of events, imperfect rationality, or the inherent unpredictability of escalation — supplies credibility that a fully rational, fully controlled threat may lack.

## Rationale

When a threat is too terrible to be believed if left to deliberate choice (e.g., nuclear retaliation that would also destroy the threatener), making the outcome depend on events one does not fully control can make the danger real to the adversary. Schelling described this as turning strategy into "a contest of endurance, nerve, obstinacy and pain" — a competition in risk-taking rather than a competition in pure damage or pure defense.

## Connection to Brinkmanship and Limited War

Brinkmanship is "the deliberate creation of a recognizable risk of war, a risk that one does not completely control." Limited war acquires special value because it can serve as a device for generating and managing shared risk: each increment raises the probability that things will get out of hand, thereby putting pressure on the side with the lower tolerance for that risk.

## Examples in Schelling's Treatment

- Forward deployments or trip-wire forces whose involvement is not fully discretionary.
- The inherent risk that a limited conventional or theater-nuclear exchange will escalate.
- "Competition in risk" during crises where each side takes steps that make general war more likely unless the other yields.

The concept is closely tied to the game of Chicken and to Schelling's broader analysis of how uncertainty and loss of control can be assets in coercive bargaining.
