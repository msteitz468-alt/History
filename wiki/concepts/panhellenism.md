# Panhellenism

[[sources/hall-history-archaic-greek-world-2014|Hall (2014)]] Ch.11 treats **panhellenism** as an emergent, incomplete internal dimension of Greek identity (Olympia, Delphi, genealogies, amphictyonies) intensified by the Persian Wars, distinct from — but later entangled with — the external construction of the barbarian ([[concepts/invention-of-the-barbarian]]).

## Definition and scope

Panhellenism here means practices and discourses that construct a shared Hellenic identity across poleis: common sanctuaries and games, genealogical claims, amphictyonic organization, and eventually anti-Persian ideology. It is **not** a continuous eighth-century “renaissance” ideology already fully formed. Hall phases the great sanctuaries against that early consensus.

## Phasing of panhellenic festivals (Hall vs eighth-century consensus)

Against an eighth-century consensus that Olympia et al. already expressed panhellenism:

- Early phases: local dining and cult.
- Eighth century: broader dedications.
- Monumental temples and treasuries mainly late seventh–sixth century.
- Pythian and Isthmian games (re)instituted 582; Nemean 573.
- Olympic 776 date (from Hippias of Elis’ late-fifth-century *stadion* list) is archaeologically dubious; formal organization may be early sixth century creation — “many doubt whether the Olympic Games were really as ancient as Hippias pretended.”

Panhellenic identity was thus largely a **sixth-century elite project**, not an automatic product of Geometric expansion.

## Internal diversity and Greek ethnos

Hall stresses concurrent diversity of Greek communities ([[concepts/greek-ethnos]]): dialect, cult, and political form (polis vs ethnos) remained varied even as elite networks linked sanctuaries. Panhellenism coexists with, rather than erases, local identities. Genealogical charters (Hellen’s sons; Dorian/Ionian/Aeolian stems) organize difference as much as unity.

## Relation to the Persian Wars

Persian conflict (from Ionian Revolt through 480–479) intensified and politicized Hellenic self-definition. Post-479 victory monuments and alliance rhetoric (e.g. Serpent Column roster of states that “fought the war”) present a selective Hellenic coalition, not the entire Greek world. Hall keeps this intensification analytically distinct from the later binary stereotype of the barbarian, though the two processes interact after Marathon and Salamis.

## Emic / etic

- **Emic:** Greeks spoke of Hellenes, common sanctuaries, and (later) shared enemies; *Panhellenes* as a term has specific poetic/political uses.
- **Etic:** “panhellenism” is a modern analytical label for the cluster of practices and ideologies; avoid reading Classical anti-Persian ideology back into the eighth century.

## Limitations

Elite-centered evidence (dedications, games victors, oratory); silent majorities and ethnos polities underrepresented; festival dates and Hippias’ Olympic list require source criticism; First Sacred War tradition must not be used uncritically to date amphictyonic panhellenism ([[controversies/first-sacred-war-historicity]]).

## From the Oxford History of the Classical World (1986)

**Griffin (introduction, [[sources/boardman-oxford-history-classical-world-1986|OHCW 1986]]).** Greek culture was competitive: successors outdid predecessors; great Panhellenic occasions at Olympia and Delphi centred on athletics; Athenian drama was ranked by judges—shared competitive forms across communities without political unity.

**Taplin (Ch.2).** Homer shaped Greek historical culture more than he recorded one time: a persuasive, universally known model of heroism, nobility, the good life, and the gods for a millennium—indirect panhellenic cultural cohesion from Ionia c.700 BCE onward.

**Hornblower (Ch.6).** Herodotus’s ingredients of *to hellenikon* (“Greekness”: shared blood, language, religion, customs) show mid-fifth-century common identity most strengthened by the Persian menace of 499–479. Classical history is then a **history of failure to achieve political unity**: Sparta would not, Athens could not, impose it indefinitely as Macedon and Rome later did. Federal experiments met *phthonos*.

**Parker (Ch.11).** Heroes in the cult sense were typically local and partisan (village/tribe loyalty, battle aid); **Heracles** the rare Panhellenic exception. Every formal social grouping was also a religious grouping “from the smallest to the largest,” including Greeks as a nation at Panhellenic sanctuaries—**embedded religion** parallel to embedded economy.

**Price (Hellenistic chapters, R5 claims).** Panhellenic instruments persisted and adapted under the kings: eligibility for mainland games used to prove Greekness (Sidon via Theban foundation myth); Olympics as venue for Alexander’s exile-restoration letter; shared Hellenic identity vs Gauls 279; royal advertising of care for Greek freedom (Ptolemy II’s procession figure “Corinth” alluding to the League of Corinth).
