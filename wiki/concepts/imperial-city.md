# The Imperial City

## Definition

An **imperial city** is the capital of a territorial empire — a city whose form,
scale, and survival are inseparable from the empire it commands. Part VI of
[[yoffee-cwh-v3-2015|CWH Vol. III]] compares three: Neo-Assyrian
[[nineveh|Nineveh and its sister capitals]], Aztec [[tenochtitlan|Tenochtitlan]],
and [[rome|Rome]]. They share a recurring structure and a recurring vulnerability.

## Shared features

- **Population beyond the local carrying capacity.** Imperial cities grew far
  larger than their hinterlands could feed (Assyrian capitals; imperial Rome ~1
  million; Tenochtitlan ~200,000), and so depended on **tribute, taxation, and
  long-distance transport** from the provinces.
- **Forced and voluntary migration.** They were **population attractors** — by
  coercion (Assyrian mass **deportation** of ~4 million; Aztec war captives;
  Cahokian and Cahokia-style relocation) and by opportunity — making them
  **cosmopolitan and multiethnic**.
- **Monumental centers and state religion.** A ceremonial core as **axis mundi**
  (Templo Mayor; the Temple of Aššur; Rome's temples and fora), staging the
  empire's ideology (cf. [[ritual-and-urban-performance]]).
- **Concentration of consequences.** Yoffee stresses that this unprecedented
  clustering of people produced **disease, violence, slavery, and subjugation**
  "of unprecedented kinds and scales."

## Shared vulnerability — the "burdens of empire"

Because the imperial city's existence depended on extracting resources from a
periphery whose loyalty was often coerced, it carried structural fragility:
- Neo-Assyrian capitals collapsed fast (614–612 BCE) once provincial loyalty
  failed.
- Tenochtitlan **depleted its own hinterland** (artificial scarcity) and fell in
  1521 to disease plus its resentful tributaries.
- Rome's "burdens of empire" lay in provisioning and the costs of scale.

## Divergence

The cases differ in **how power was assembled**: Assyrian capitals by deportation
and military command; Tenochtitlan via the multi-centric **altepetl** and a
tribute network; Rome — distinctively — by **inclusivity** (admitting foreigners
to citizenship) and a mosaic of treaties rather than a uniform war machine. The
comparison is also a corrective to Rome's [[eurocentrism|Eurocentric]]
exceptionalism: imperial cities belong on one comparative "dissecting table."
