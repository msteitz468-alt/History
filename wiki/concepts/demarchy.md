# Demarchy and Hayek's Model Constitution

**Etic/prescriptive** concept: Hayek's name for the ideal that "democracy" originally denoted — majority rule limited to general rules — and the constitutional design he proposed to realize it. The culmination of *Law, Legislation and Liberty* III: *The Political Order of a Free People* (1979). Recorded here as attributed political theory, not description.

## The diagnosis: the miscarriage of the democratic ideal

Vol. 3 opens under a Xenophon epigraph — the Athenian Assembly crying "that it was monstrous if the people were to be prevented from doing whatever they wished" against Socrates' insistence on acting only "in accordance with the law." Hayek's claim is that democracy follows a "regular course": "after a glorious first period in which it … operates as a safeguard of personal freedom because it accepts the limitations of a higher nomos, sooner or later it comes to claim the right to settle any particular question in whatever manner a majority agrees upon." His two datum-points: late-fifth-century Athens (Demosthenes: "our laws are no better than so many decrees") and Britain in **1766**, when Parliament "explicitly rejected the idea that in its particular decisions it was bound to observe any general rules not of its own making." Aristotle is enlisted — where "the many are sovereign not as individuals but collectively … such a democracy is not a constitution at all" — and the modern terminus is "totalitarian democracy" and "plebiscitary dictatorship."

The mechanism is structural, not moral. "The tragic illusion was that the adoption of democratic procedures made it possible to dispense with all other limitations on governmental power." An assembly empowered to grant particular benefits "must become one in which bargains or deals among the majority rather than substantive agreement on the merits … will decide"; the resulting "will of the majority" is fictitious. Consequences Hayek catalogues: the "enormous and exceedingly wasteful apparatus of para-government" (trade associations, unions, professional bodies) organized to divert favor; parties reduced to "coalitions of organized interests"; government that "becomes their slave — as in Britain"; and the origin of the [[concepts/social-justice|"myth of social justice"]] itself, "largely the product of this particular democratic machinery, which makes it necessary for the representatives to invent a moral justification for the benefits they grant to particular interests." His paradigm case: the Trade Disputes Act 1906, "the most fateful law in Britain's modern history," passed not by majority conviction but because the Liberal majority depended on Labour votes. Kant supplies the theoretical ground: "welfare has no principle … and therefore is incapable of a general principle." Hence: "Only limited government can be decent government."

Hayek is emphatic that this is not anti-democratic: democracy as the method of peaceful change of power "is an ideal worth fighting for to the utmost, because it is our only protection … against tyranny," one of the "paramount though negative values, comparable to sanitary precautions against the plague." But he draws the line exactly: "I must frankly admit that if democracy is taken to mean government by the unrestricted will of the majority I am not a democrat, and even regard such government as pernicious and in the long run unworkable."

## The term

Because "democracy" had been captured by unlimited-majority practice (and abused in "people's democracies"), Hayek proposes a new name built on Greek *archein* (rule, as in mon-archy) rather than *kratein* (brute strength): "adopting the term **demarchy** for the ideal for which democracy was originally adopted when it gradually supplanted the older expression isonomy, describing the ideal of an equal law for all" (ch. 13; first floated in his 1968 pamphlet *The Confusion of Language in Political Thought*). The intellectual lineage he claims for divided, law-bound legislative power: the Civil-War-era realization (via M. J. C. Vile) that "parliament could be as tyrannical as a king"; Locke ("promulgated established Laws, not to be varied in particular cases"); *Cato's Letters* (1721) on legislators bound by their own laws; Paley (1785) — Parliament "knows not the individuals upon whom its acts will operate … which always produces impartial and commonly advantageous regulations"; and James Wilson's American rejection of Blackstonian parliamentary sovereignty. The opposing doctrine of unlimited sovereignty he traces to Bacon ("a supreme and absolute power cannot conclude itself") and calls a constructivist superstition: "The basic source of social order … is not a deliberate decision to adopt certain common rules, but the existence among the people of certain opinions of what is right and wrong."

## The model constitution (ch. 17)

Explicitly not proposed for adoption by countries with firm constitutional traditions — "My purpose in presenting such a sketch is not to propose a constitutional scheme for present application" — but to clarify principles, and possibly serve new states and supranational design.

- **Basic clause:** in normal times coercion is permissible "only in accordance with the recognized rules of just conduct," alterable only by the Legislative Assembly. Hayek claims this "would by itself achieve all and more than the traditional Bills of Rights were meant to secure," protecting unenumerable liberties without enumeration.
- **Legislative Assembly (the *nomothetae*,** reviving the Athenian term): confined to rules of just conduct (nomos). Composition is the scheme's signature: each age cohort elects, "in the calendar year in which they reached the age of 45," representatives from its own midst for a single non-renewable **15-year term** — an assembly of 45-to-60-year-olds, one-fifteenth replaced annually, average age 52.5. Ex-members receive honorific neutral employment (e.g., lay judgeships) to age 70, securing independence from party discipline; prior members of the Governmental Assembly or party organizations are ineligible; removal only for gross misconduct by peers; salary constitutionally pegged to the average of the twenty highest government posts. (Compare his ch. 13 sortition thought-experiment: five hundred citizens by lot for twenty years would give "a more representative sample of the true opinion of the people" than the party auction.)
- **Governmental Assembly:** organized like existing parliaments (parties, periodic election, cabinet as executive committee), directing government within the Legislative Assembly's law. Hayek even floats disenfranchising government employees and pensioners for *this* assembly's elections, as interested parties.
- **Constitutional Court:** professional judges plus former members of both assemblies, resolving competence conflicts — often by ruling "that nobody at all was entitled to take certain kinds of coercive measures"; bound by its own precedent.
- **Sovereignty "resides nowhere"** in the resulting multi-tier structure, "unless it temporally resides in the hands of the constitution-making or constitution-amending body."
- **Emergency powers:** the Legislative Assembly (via a standing committee) may confer emergency powers on government but thereby "renounce[s] the powers it normally possesses," retaining only revocation — because "'Emergencies' have always been the pretext on which the safeguards of individual liberty have been eroded."
- **Finance:** the Legislative Assembly fixes the uniform rule apportioning the tax burden; the Governmental Assembly decides the amount and direction of spending — so those who vote expenditure feel its cost. The uniform rule "can have no place for any overall progression of the total tax burden" (though direct-tax progression offsetting regressive indirect taxes is allowed).

## Reception and critiques (attributed)

- The scheme was received even by sympathizers as the trilogy's weakest, most rationalist moment — an irony pressed by critics: the great enemy of [[concepts/constructivist-rationalism|constructivist design]] closes his trilogy by designing a constitution from first principles. Hayek's pre-emptive answer is the stated non-adoption purpose and Vol. 1's argument that a spontaneous order's *framework* may legitimately be designed.
- The age-cohort electoral mechanism has never been attempted; public-choice theorists question whether 15-year unaccountable terms solve capture or relocate it.
- The disenfranchisement aside and the bar on progressive taxation are the provisions most often cited by critics as revealing the substantive politics beneath the procedural design.
- The term "demarchy" was later borrowed (John Burnheim, 1985) for a different proposal — government by sortition — with which Hayek's usage should not be confused, despite his own lot-drawing thought experiment.
