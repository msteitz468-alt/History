# Interpolity Law (the Origins of Global Law)

## Definition

**"Interpolity law"** is Lauren Benton and Adam Clulow's term (CWH VI) for the
repertoire of shared legal *practices* — not a shared body of doctrine — through
which diverse political communities structured their relations across cultures in
the early modern world (1400–1800). It is offered as a third path between two
older accounts of the origins of international law: the claim that a universal
law of nations already existed, and the claim that international law was a
European script imposed on the world.

## The Three Older Accounts and the Middle Way

- **A universal law of nations (C. H. Alexandrowicz, 1967).** A universal
  international law, formulated by Europeans like Grotius but drawing heavily on
  Asian practice, was already present in the early modern period, letting
  European and Asian powers share legal concepts. Critics found little evidence
  for such cross-cultural understanding.
- **International law as colonial product (Anghie and others).** International law
  was forged out of the "dynamic of difference" of the colonial encounter, as
  European jurists (from Vitoria onward) worked to reconcile empire with a system
  of sovereign states.
- **Benton & Clulow's synthesis.** Neither a genuine universal law nor a purely
  European imposition existed. Instead there was a **"crude parallel
  morphology"**: diverse polities shared enough basic characteristics that legal
  actors could rely on **analogies** rather than true cross-cultural
  understanding. Europeans were not the originators of these practices but the
  chief beneficiaries of their wide recognition — their overseas success rested
  partly on manipulating existing legal routines.

## The Three Rubrics

Benton and Clulow analyze interpolity law through three widely shared categories:

1. **Protocol** — the highly stylized diplomatic and legal rituals that
   legitimated authority and undergirded sovereignty. A wide range of officials
   agreed that prescribed acts, correctly performed, confirmed legitimacy, even
   as they disagreed on the proper content. Failure could be disastrous (envoys
   expelled, embassies voided); chartered companies were especially vulnerable
   and resorted to specialists (Thomas Roe), deception (the VOC's "king of
   Holland"), or absurd improvisation. Legal rituals — trials, executions,
   *padrões*, possession ceremonies derived from Roman law — also marked
   jurisdiction and territorial claims.
2. **Jurisdiction** — the scope of legal authority over persons, places, or
   activities. Early modern polities were typically **legally plural**, with
   overlapping and competing jurisdictions; no state held a monopoly on legal
   authority. Foreign merchant communities were widely allowed to govern their
   own members (Melaka's polyglot port; Armenian and Jewish traders; the
   Calicut "Moorish Governor"). At sea, ships were both "islands of law" and
   "vectors of law"; the Portuguese *cartazes* and Japanese *shuinjō* turned
   vessels into mobile outposts of sovereign authority.
3. **Protection** — the substance of much interpolity negotiation: protection
   traded for **tribute** and submission. The Ottoman vassal system, the Chinese
   tributary system (Ming protection of Melaka, 1403–19), and Southeast Asian
   overlord–tributary relations all ran on this logic, as did European overseas
   enterprise — the VOC as "protector" of Ternate in exchange for clove
   monopoly; the British "subsidiary alliances" in India.

## The Nineteenth-Century Shift

These practices supported an early modern understanding of **sovereignty as
divisible** — a bundle of capacities distributed across irregular spaces. Around
1800, clusters of jurisdictional conflict (settler claims, extraterritoriality)
drove a shift toward sovereignty as a **territorial monopoly** at the apex of a
hierarchy of jurisdictions. Europeans increasingly invoked a single standard of
"civilized" diplomacy and abandoned the protocols (North America after 1763;
the Mughal court by 1842–3). This change unfolded **within a world of empires**,
not as the triumph of a global order of nation-states. The "civilized standard"
and territorial sovereignty that emerged is the world of modern
[[international-law|international law]] — the consent-based, **positivist** order
(Westphalian sovereignty, treaties and custom) traced by Arend in
[[mcneill-cwh-v7-2015|CWH VII]]. Benton & Clulow's recovery of early modern
jurisdictional plurality is a deliberate counterpoint to that tidy Westphalian
narrative.

## Significance and Limits

The concept reframes the origins of international law as global and practical
rather than European and doctrinal, and connects legal history to the wider
themes of [[intercultural-relations-early-modern|cross-cultural encounter]] and
the [[early-modern|construction of a global world]]. It is contested: it
deliberately rejects Alexandrowicz's universal law of nations and resists the
view of non-European law as a "problem" for Western jurists to solve.
