## Definition and Scope

"Greater Britain" denotes the idea that the British nation-state was not bounded by the British Isles but extended across the globe through settler colonialism — a transoceanic ethnic and cultural community of British descent sharing "blood, language, religion and laws" (Seeley's phrase). In its emic (contemporary) usage it was simultaneously a descriptive claim (this community exists and is growing) and a prescriptive one (it should be institutionally organised). In its etic (analytical) usage it is a category for studying the cultural and political integration of the British settler world, recently interrogated by David Armitage ('Greater Britain: A Useful Category of Historical Analysis?', *American Historical Review*, 1999) and Duncan Bell (*The Idea of Greater Britain*, Princeton UP, 2007).

The concept must be distinguished from:
- **Formal Empire** (Crown colonies, protectorates, India) — "Greater Britain" specifically referenced the *settler* empire of self-governing colonies: Australia, Canada, New Zealand, the Cape
- **Imperial Federation** — a specific institutional proposal for a supra-parliament; "Greater Britain" was the ideological substrate that made federation seem plausible
- **Commonwealth** — James Froude in *Oceana* (1885) proposed dropping "empire" in favour of "Commonwealth" for the settler colonies; the same community, a different name

## Emic Usage — How Contemporaries Employed It

Charles Dilke's *Greater Britain* (1868), 'the Victorian bestseller of autumn 1868', captured 'the sense of Australian settlement as a "New World" in its democratic ethos and politics, its conspicuous prosperity and migrant success, its culture of aspiration and opportunity'. Dilke 'had seen the future and it looked like Australia'. His revised edition, retitled *Problems of Greater Britain* (1890), shifted to the question of closer imperial unity.

J.R. Seeley's lectures (*The Expansion of England*, 1883) gave the concept its most influential academic formulation: the development of a "Greater Britain" was "an event of enormous historical significance in world affairs." Seeley envisaged 'a great homogenous people, one in blood, language, religion and laws, but dispersed over a boundless space'. Lord Rosebery (later Liberal prime minister) flattered Australian audiences by describing Empire as 'a league of nations', 'a commonwealth of nations'. Gladstone understood Britain as 'head servant in the great household of the world'.

Richard Jebb popularised the term "colonial nationalism" in his 1905 travel book, arguing that the dual British-Australian identity — what W.K. Hancock in 1930 called living simultaneously in two worlds of 'felt experience' — was not contradictory. As Hancock put it, Australians belonged 'in a supra-state that was the Empire. Their nationalism was also their imperialism.' The 'Meaney thesis' has since argued that 'British race patriotism was dominant [in Australia] until the 1970s'.

## Etic Assessment — Historiographical Critique

The concept has been critiqued for its racial exclusivity (India was 'always outside this projection in ethnic nationalism') and for obscuring non-British connections in Australian history. Marilyn Lake argues that the "insiderism" of British-world historiography has 'reinforced an understanding of Australian history as bounded by "Britishness", isolated from the countries and cultures of its own region'. Recent scholarship stresses that racial identity was consolidated *against* Asian and Pacific neighbours, not simply *from* British origins: 'it was only during the late-Victorian atmosphere of looming crisis that colonial identities became securely anchored within a pan-British ethnic framework' (Stuart Ward).

The concept also obscures strong trans-Pacific identification among radical liberals with the United States, and the ways Australasian nation-building was shaped by encounters with the Chinese and Japanese empires.

## Institutional Expression and Limits

- **Imperial Federation Movement**: active from the 1880s; divided both colonists and British politicians over what constitutional structure could represent both the British Isles and the empire's diversity. Imperial federation ultimately failed.
- **Colonial Conferences (from 1887)**: the practical substitute — regular Defence Conferences that coordinated military and naval policy without creating a supra-parliament.
- **Telegraphic cable (1872)**: technology did more to create a practical Greater Britain 'than all the Imperial Federation propositions'.
- **Australian Federation (1901)**: the Federation Act gave the new Commonwealth membership of a 'pan-Britannic community' while preserving colonial shibboleths — tariffs, exclusionist immigration policies, state control over resources. One modern scholar calls Australian nationalism at federation 'not the repudiation of imperialism but its confident vanguard' (John Darwin).
- **Joseph Chamberlain** pressed for imperial preference (*Zollverein*) and a 'closed system of imperial preference' from 1895–1903 — the most radical institutional bid to realise Greater Britain economically, though ultimately defeated.

## Racial Dimension

The concept was inseparable from racial thinking. Henry Parkes's 'crimson thread of kinship', Charles Pearson's *National Life and Character: A Forecast* (1893) — which predicted ultimately dominant oriental societies — and George Craig's *Federal Defence of Australasia* (1897), dedicated 'to those noble sons of the British race at Home or in the Colonies', all expressed the defensive racial nationalism that underpinned Greater Britain. Hancock noted that 'at Federation Australia was 95 per cent British and distrusted the remaining 5 per cent.'

## Limitations and Critiques

- Conflates *ethnic* solidarity with *political* solidarity; in practice the two pulled apart (colonial tariffs, exclusionist immigration legislation challenged imperial unity)
- Marginalises Indigenous peoples, Chinese Australians, Pacific Islanders, and others who composed significant portions of the colonial population
- The "insiderism" critique: situating Australia within a British world can reproduce historical blindness to the Asia-Pacific regional context that shaped Australian development
- Imperial Federation failed; "Greater Britain" as an institution never materialised; what survived was cultural identification and trade integration, not political union
