# Sonnō Jōi

**Sonnō jōi** (尊王攘夷, "revere the emperor, expel the barbarians") was the dominant political slogan of the anti-foreign, pro-imperial movement in Japan from approximately 1853 to 1864. It fused two distinct imperatives — loyalty to the imperial institution and rejection of Western intrusion — into a single ideological platform that served as the primary mobilizing idiom for the radical *shishi* (men of high purpose) of the [[bakumatsu]] era.

## Definition and Emic vs. Etic Dimensions

As an emic concept, sonnō jōi expressed the beliefs of its proponents literally: the emperor was the sacred center of Japan's political and spiritual identity (as taught by the Mito school of nativist Confucianism), and the presence of Western barbarians on Japanese soil was a defilement of the "Divine Land" requiring forcible expulsion. As an etic analytical category, historians treat sonnō jōi as the ideological vehicle through which lower-ranking samurai translated grievances about Tokugawa rule into a legitimating anti-shogunate program: "revering the emperor" implied undermining the shogun.

The phrase's internal tension was its political dynamite. Reverence for the emperor was nominally compatible with the Tokugawa system (the shogun derived legitimacy from imperial appointment). But if the emperor could be shown to oppose the shogunate's foreign policy — as [[emperor-komei]] demonstrably did from 1858 onward — then loyalty to the emperor required opposing the shogunate.

## Origins

The *jōi* (expel the barbarians) impulse predated the full slogan. From the 1790s, Russian encroachment in Hokkaido and the Kuriles prompted nationalist intellectuals and some domain officials to demand coastal defense and exclusion of foreign vessels. The Mito school, associated with the Tokugawa collateral domain of Mito, developed a rigorous philosophical basis for imperial loyalism (*kokutai*, the national polity) combined with xenophobia. Tokugawa Nariaki (1800–1860), the daimyo of Mito, became the leading political patron of the jōi cause before Perry's arrival.

Perry's arrival in 1853 transformed jōi from a fringe intellectual position into a mass political movement. The word "jōi — expulsion of the barbarians — became the battle cry of the advocates of national defense" immediately after Perry's visit, as Keene records, with Shimazu Nariakira of Satsuma proposing to "exterminate" the foreigners once Japan had re-armed. From this moment, the combined slogan sonnō jōi gave the movement its distinctive double valence.

## Relationship to Kōmei's Position

A critical interpretive issue is that [[emperor-komei]] himself did not fully embrace the sonnō jōi program as articulated by its radical adherents. He shared the *jōi* component passionately — he petitioned the gods to send a *kamikaze* against the foreigners, ordered prayers for the expulsion of barbarians, and refused to sanction the unequal treaties. But he was "fiercely outspoken in his opposition to those whose 'respect for the emperor' involved the overthrow of the shogunate."

His preferred alternative was **kōbu gattai** (公武合体, "union of court and military") — cooperative action by emperor and shogunate to strengthen Japan and eventually expel the foreigners through legitimate means, not mob violence. Keene draws this distinction explicitly: sonnō jōi aimed at *replacing* the shogunate with direct imperial rule; kōbu gattai aimed at reforming the dual system. Kōmei's death in 1867 removed the principal obstacle to the sonnō jōi faction's appropriation of imperial authority for revolutionary ends.

## Movement Actors and Tactics

The movement's social base was primarily *shishi* — lower-ranking samurai, often from outside the Tokugawa inner circle, concentrated in Chōshū and Satsuma domains. Their tactics evolved from:

- **Petitioning**: flooding the court and shogunate with memorials demanding expulsion of foreigners
- **Assassination**: killing officials perceived as "traitors" (collaborators with foreigners or the shogunate), with notes left explaining the act as "divine punishment" — a practice that terrorized Kyōto from ca. 1861 onward
- **Domain militarism**: pressuring domain lords to take anti-foreign military action (e.g., Chōshū shelling Western ships in the Straits of Shimonoseki, 1863)
- **Court infiltration**: placing sonnō jōi nobles in key court positions and issuing imperial orders for jōi military action

The movement's peak was 1862–1863, when sonnō jōi sympathizers effectively controlled access to the court and secured an imperial edict ordering the shogun to carry out jōi. Emperor Kōmei's own words were instrumentalized by the movement in ways he did not sanction.

## The Coup of the Eighth Month (1863) and Decline

The movement's dominance ended abruptly. On the night of September 29–30, 1863, the kōbu gattai faction — led by Prince Nakagawa and supported by Satsuma and Aizu domain troops — bolted the nine gates of the Gosho and expelled the radical sonnō jōi nobles and their Chōshū allies from the court. Seven nobles fled with Chōshū forces back to the domain. An imperial edict declared that the jōi faction had "conspired with the Chōshū domain to misrepresent my commands."

The Chōshū domain's subsequent attempt to retake the capital by force ended in catastrophic defeat at the [[hamaguri-gate-battle-1864|Battle of the Hamaguri Gate]] (August 1864). The battle left over 28,000 buildings in Kyōto destroyed by fire. The First Chōshū Expedition (1864–1865) followed, further suppressing the radical movement.

After 1864, the *sonnō jōi* slogan was progressively de-emphasized by Chōshū and Satsuma leadership, who recognized that expelling Western powers was militarily impossible and that the pragmatic goal was reforming Japan's government and military. The Satsuma-Chōshū alliance of 1866 was built on this more pragmatic program. By the time of the [[meiji-restoration-1868]], the slogan had been effectively shed in favor of "enrich the country, strengthen the army" (*fukoku kyōhei*).

## Analytical Limitations

Historians debate whether sonnō jōi should be understood as:
- A sincere ideology held by its proponents (Keene treats it largely as genuine belief, particularly for Emperor Kōmei)
- A political instrument used by domain leaders to mobilize samurai resentment and challenge the shogunate
- A transitional ideology that, paradoxically, produced its opposite — a modernizing, Western-engaging Meiji state — by first destroying the institutional structures that blocked reform

The movement's social destructiveness (the Kyōto terror of 1861–1864) and its ultimate displacement by Meiji modernization have led some historians to treat it as a mere phase in an inevitable modernization narrative. The CLAUDE.md's causation principles caution against this: sonnō jōi was not a predictable stepping-stone but a genuine political force whose outcome was contingent on Kōmei's death, the Satsuma-Chōshū alliance, and the shogunate's particular weaknesses.

## Relation to the Meiji Restoration

Sonnō jōi provided the [[meiji-restoration-1868]] with its essential resource — the claim that the emperor's authority superseded the shogun's — but the Restoration's actual program contradicted the movement's literal demands. The Meiji state opened Japan further, imposed Western institutions, and suppressed the samurai class that had been sonnō jōi's social base. The irony is stark: a movement that aimed at expelling barbarians produced the most rapid and thorough Westernization in modern history.
