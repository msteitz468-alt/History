# Auftragstaktik (Mission Command)

*Auftragstaktik* (translated as mission-oriented command or mission command) is a decentralized style of military command that stands in direct opposition to *Befehlstaktik* (detailed control or command by directives). Under *Auftragstaktik*, a commander issues a subordinate officer a clear mission (what to achieve) and explains the underlying intent (why it must be achieved), but leaves the method of execution entirely to the subordinate's discretion based on local circumstances.

---

## Theoretical Framework

In *Command in War*, Martin van Creveld frames *Auftragstaktik* as a highly effective response to the fundamental challenge of command: **managing uncertainty**. A command system has two choices when dealing with uncertainty:
1. **Increase information processing capacity**: Attempt to centralize all data, creating a larger hierarchy to process reports and issue detailed directives (*Befehlstaktik*). This frequently leads to information overload, delay, and micro-management.
2. **Increase tolerance for uncertainty**: Decentralize decision-making, allowing the parts of the organization to act independently without waiting for centralized orders (*Auftragstaktik*).

By pushing the authority to act down to the lowest level where ground-truth information resides, *Auftragstaktik* minimizes communication delays and maximizes responsiveness.

## Key Historical Evolution

### 1. The Prussian Roots (Moltke the Elder)
Following the Prussian defeats of 1806, reformers like Gerhard von Scharnhorst realized that rigid, top-down control was obsolete in the face of mass armies. Helmuth von Moltke the Elder codified this into Prusso-German doctrine during the wars of German unification:
> "A favorable situation will never be exploited if commanders have to wait for orders from above. The subordinate commander must understand the general intent, and be resolved to act on his own initiative."

During the [[hubs/warfare/long-19th-century/wars-of-german-unification/battle-of-koniggratz|Königgrätz Campaign (1866)]], Moltke managed three separated armies using brief, general telegrams, relying on army commanders to coordinate their movements and march to the sound of guns.

### 2. The Interwar and World Wars Era
In the interwar period, Hans von Seeckt and the Weimar Reichswehr expanded this doctrine, training every soldier to perform the duties of the rank above them. In WWII, *Auftragstaktik* enabled the rapid, independent operations of Panzer divisions during the Blitzkrieg, bypassing strong points and exploiting gaps without waiting for General Staff approval.

### 3. The 1973 Yom Kippur War (Optional Control)
During the 1973 war, the Israeli Defense Forces (IDF) relied on a highly developed form of decentralized command. When central headquarters was initially blinded by Egyptian and Syrian surprise attacks, local commanders in the Sinai and Golan Heights took the initiative, organizing ad hoc defenses and launching local counterattacks, which eventually stabilized the fronts. Van Creveld terms this "optional control"—the ability of headquarters to intervene optionally while leaving primary execution to the field.

## Preconditions for Success

*Auftragstaktik* is not merely the absence of orders; it requires a highly disciplined, cohesive institutional culture:
- **Shared Doctrine**: Subordinates and commanders must share a common conceptual framework so they analyze problems similarly.
- **Mutual Trust**: Commanders must trust the competence of their subordinates, and subordinates must be willing to accept responsibility and risk.
- **Clarity of Intent**: The commander's intent must be simple, clear, and focused.
- **Initiative Over Obedience**: Subordinates are expected to deviate from original orders if the situation on the ground changes, provided they remain faithful to the commander's intent.
