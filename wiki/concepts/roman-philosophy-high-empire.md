# Philosophy under the High Empire

The state of **Greek philosophy** in the first two centuries CE — the post-Hellenistic
reconstitution of the schools, the rise of **Middle Platonism**, the flowering of **Roman
Stoicism**, and the shared turn toward **authority, systematization, and "likeness to God."**
Treated in [[bowman-cah-v11-2000|CAH XI]] Ch. 32 (**John Dillon**), reaching back to the first
century BCE for context.

## The decisive break: schools without a centre

Dillon's pivotal date is **86 BCE**, when Sulla's recapture of Athens destroyed the **Academy and
the Lyceum** and scattered the philosophers to Rome and Alexandria. Thereafter the four Hellenistic
traditions — **Platonist, Aristotelian, Stoic, Epicurean** — continued vigorously, but (Platonists
and Aristotelians especially) **without any formal institutional school** or "central, validating
authority" to pronounce orthodoxy. What replaced them were **small, personal schools** set up by
individuals and continued a generation or two by chosen successors (Antiochus of Ascalon in Athens;
Plutarch's at Chaeronea; Albinus at Smyrna; Numenius at Apamea; Atticus and Taurus at Athens). The
exception was the **Epicureans**, who kept a continuous succession of scholarchs (attested by
Plotina's letters to Hadrian).

## The turn to authority and systematization

The accent of the age was on **authority and tradition**:

- The **"rediscovery" of Aristotle's esoteric works** and their editing by **Andronicus of Rhodes**
  launched the great **commentary tradition** on Aristotle, culminating at the end of the period in
  **Alexander of Aphrodisias** (whose inaugural *On Fate* is preserved).
- A **revival of Pythagoreanism** (with its pseudepigrapha) fed the Platonist drift toward
  transcendence.
- Against the dogmatists, a **revived scepticism** (Aenesidemus' ten *tropoi*, refined by Agrippa,
  systematized at the end of the century by the doctor **Sextus Empiricus**) influenced medicine but
  little subsequent Platonism.
- **Marcus Aurelius** (AD 176) **established four official, salaried chairs** at Athens — one for
  each major tradition — creating the "successions" later mistaken for "the Academy."

Without a central authority, the schools nonetheless produced **considerable systematization**:
**commentaries** (on Plato's *Timaeus*, on Aristotle), **introductions** (*eisagogai* — the
*Didaskalikos* of Alcinous, Apuleius' *De Platone*, Diogenes of Oenoanda's great Epicurean
inscription), and **treatises** on set topics (fate, the soul, the first principle, ethics).

## Middle Platonism

The dominant intellectual movement was **Middle Platonism** — a reassertion (against Antiochus'
Stoicizing) of a **transcendent, immaterial God**, and the working-out of his relation to the world
through **intermediate entities** (the demiurge, the World Soul, the Forms as the **thoughts of
God** — the *kosmos noetos*, first clearly in the Jewish philosopher **Philo of Alexandria**). Its
figures — Eudorus, Plutarch, Albinus, **Numenius** of Apamea (the Pythagorizing Platonist who drew
on Judaism and Zoroastrianism and influenced **Ammonius Saccas**, teacher of Plotinus), and Atticus
— prepared the ground for the **Neoplatonism** of the third century.

## Roman Stoicism

The four great Stoic figures of the period were "to a certain extent peripheral" and practical
rather than technical: the maverick **Posidonius** of Apamea (admitting a Platonic tripartite soul);
and the Roman moralists **Seneca**, **Musonius Rufus**, the freed-slave **Epictetus** (his
*Discourses* recorded by Arrian), and the emperor **Marcus Aurelius** (the *Meditations*). Their
Stoicism was **popular and eclectic** — borrowing freely from Plato and Aristotle for "moral uplift"
— centred on the self-sufficiency of virtue, freedom from passion (*apatheia*), and submission to
fate and the course of nature. Technical school Stoicism had largely "atrophied" (Plutarch attacks
Chrysippus, not living Stoics).

## The shared goal: likeness to God

Uniting all the schools, Dillon argues, was a **concern with personal salvation and man's relation
to the divine** — the Platonist (and adopted Stoic) ideal of ***homoiosis theoi***, "likeness to
God" (from Plato's *Theaetetus*), and the image of **philosophy as a journey** through life. He
cautions against the dismissive label "religious philosophy": the divine concerned these thinkers
no more than it had Plato or the Stoa, and did not subordinate philosophy to religion or dull
interest in its other branches.

## Significance

The philosophy of the High Empire was a period of **consolidation and transition** — the schools
reconstituted on personal rather than institutional lines, the energies of the age poured into
**commentary, systematization, and the ethics of the self** — producing both the practical Stoic
moralism that is its most famous legacy (Epictetus, Marcus) and the **Middle Platonist metaphysics**
that would issue in Neoplatonism and shape Christian thought.
