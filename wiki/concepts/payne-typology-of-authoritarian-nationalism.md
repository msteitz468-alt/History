# Payne's Typology of Authoritarian Nationalism

Stanley G. Payne's analytical apparatus, the working framework of
[[payne-history-of-fascism-1995|*A History of Fascism*]] (1995). He treats
"generic fascism" explicitly as a Weberian ideal type — "an abstraction which
never existed in pure empirical form" — restricted to interwar European
*movements*, not a presumed category of fascist regimes.

## 1. The three-sector typology (Table I.2, "Three Faces of Authoritarian Nationalism")

Interwar authoritarian nationalism divides into three distinct, often
mutually hostile camps:

- **Fascists** — revolutionary ultranationalists (Italian Fascism, National
  Socialism, [[falange|Falange]], [[iron-guard|Iron Guard]],
  [[arrow-cross|Arrow Cross]]), grounded in vitalist/Sorelian philosophy and
  genuinely interested in changing class and status relationships;
- **The radical right** — wished "to destroy the existing political system of
  liberalism root and branch" but hesitated at fully novel authoritarian
  forms, harkening to reorganized monarchism or neo-Catholic corporatism
  (Action Française, the Heimwehr, Carol II's Romania); often *more*
  imperialistic than fascism's social-revolutionary wing;
- **The conservative authoritarian right** — grounded in traditional religion,
  seeking legal continuity and only partial authoritarian transformation
  while "freezing much of the status quo" (Horthy, Dollfuss/Schuschnigg,
  Salazar, [[franco-francisco|Franco]], Piłsudski's successors).

Military relations discriminate sharply: the rightist sectors could count on
army backing, fascists could not — "when the new system was led by a
general — Franco, Pétain, Antonescu — the fascist movements were relegated to
a subordinate and eventually insignificant role."

### The original 1980 statement (Table 2, "Three Faces of Authoritarian Nationalism")

Now ingested: [[payne-fascism-comparison-definition-1980]] gives the
typology's first full articulation, with discriminating criteria the 1995
recension compresses:

- **Philosophy/culture:** the conservative right (and much of the radical
  right) "based themselves upon religion more than upon any new cultural
  mystique such as vitalism"; "the Sorelianism and Nietzscheanism of core
  fascists was repudiated." The radical right spans the whole spectrum —
  Spanish variants as Catholic as the conservatives, central-European ones
  embracing "vitalist and biological doctrines not significantly different
  from those of core fascists."
- **Legal continuity:** the conservative right "wished... to avoid radical
  breaks in legal continuity if at all possible"; the radical right "wished
  to destroy the entire existing political system of liberalism root and
  branch" yet "harkened back to a reorganized monarchism or an eclectic
  neo-Catholic corporatism."
- **Mass politics:** the conservative right "sometimes managed to exceed
  the fascists in mobilizing mass support" among rural and lower-middle
  strata; the radical right "was normally the weakest of all three sectors
  in terms of popular appeal," relying on elite manipulation of the power
  structure.
- **The military:** the radical right would "accept outright pretorianism";
  fascists "sought only the neutrality or in some cases the partial
  support of the military while rejecting genuine pretorianism, realizing
  full well that military rule per se precluded fascist rule." Hitler's
  power became complete only with "total predominance over the military";
  Mussolini "developed a syncretic or polycratic system which recognized
  broad military autonomy while limiting that of the party."
- **Social policy:** all three preached social unity, but for the rightist
  sectors this "tended to mean freezing much of the status quo," while
  fascists "were in general more interested in changing class and status
  relationships in society."
- **Imperialism:** the conservative right generally avoided aggressive
  imperialism; "elements of the radical right were frequently more
  imperialistic than the moderate or 'leftist' (social revolutionary)
  elements within fascism."
- **Development:** "contrary to a common assertion, economic development
  was a major goal of groups in all three categories" (notable exception:
  the early Portuguese Estado Novo); fascists were "the most
  'modernizing'" sector.

Payne notes the analytic kinship to Arno J. Mayer's counterrevolutionary /
reactionary / conservative triad (*Dynamics of Counterrevolution in
Europe*, 1971), while stating his criterial content differs. Ch. 2 of the
1980 book supplies the right's genealogy: four roots of the new
authoritarian right (Catholic corporatist doctrine — with Schmitter's
definition of corporatism quoted as the best working one; "authoritarian
liberalism" from Bismarck's last year, Sonnino's *Torniamo allo Statuto*,
and João Franco's 1907–8 Portuguese decree dictatorship; neomonarchism —
Action Française as prime exemplar, "integral nationalism" converting
monarchism from dynastic principle into complete system; and the
instrumental modernizing radical right — the Italian Nationalist
Association, whose Rocco corporate state was statist, social-Darwinist,
and imperialist, and whose Sempre Pronti militia attacked the Bologna
Camera del Lavoro in July 1919 "before the minuscule new Fascist movement
was ready or willing to do so"). Precursors of the *revolutionary*
collectivist-nationalist line run instead through France (Boulangism,
Déroulède's League of Patriots, the Marquis de Morès, Barrès's "socialist
nationalism" of 1898) and Austria-Bohemia (Schönerer's Linz Program of
1882; the Czech National Socialists and Sudeten German Workers Party of
1898/1904, the latter racialized by 1913 and renamed NSDAP by war's end).
Payne warns against reifying "prefascism": Bonapartism-as-first-fascism
(Thalheimer) fails because the Second Empire "never attempted a party
movement, much less a new political militia," and Boulangism lacked
coherence — "both Boulanger and Déroulède were men of the nineteenth
century who had only a limited tolerance for violence."

## 2. The typological description of fascism (Table I.1)

Three clusters — **Ideology and Goals** (vitalist philosophy, a new
nationalist authoritarian state, a corporatist-leaning but flexible national
economy, positive valuation of violence, empire or radical change in the
nation's status); **The Fascist Negations** (antiliberalism, anticommunism,
anticonservatism — though with willingness to ally with rightist sectors);
**Style and Organization** (mass party militia, extreme elitism and the
Führerprinzip, exaltation of youth and masculinity, romantic-mystical
aesthetics of politics). Payne's one-sentence compression:

> "fascism may be defined as 'a form of revolutionary ultranationalism for
> national rebirth that is based on a primarily vitalist philosophy, is
> structured on extreme elitism, mass mobilization, and the Führerprinzip,
> positively values violence as end as well as means and tends to normatize
> war and/or the military virtues.'"

(The printed tables I.1/I.2 survive only as image objects in the ingested
text; the clusters above are reconstructed from Payne's surrounding prose,
which expounds them in full.)

## 3. Varieties and regime types

Payne identifies **five national varieties** of fascism (paradigmatic Italian
Fascism; German National Socialism — the most radical and the only one to
achieve total dictatorship; Catholic-traditionalist Spanish Falangism; the
mystical, "kenotic," semireligious Romanian Legion; Szálasi's Hungarism), and
**seven types of syncretic national authoritarian regime**, from the fully
fascist Hitler regime through Mussolini's semipluralism, genuine satellite
fascisms (Ustasha, Arrow Cross) vs. non-fascist satellites (Vichy, Tiso's
Slovakia), syncretic dictatorships with subordinated fascist components
(Spain 1937–45, Romania 1940–41), failed top-down semifascisms, praetorian
regimes, and limited authoritarianisms preserving liberal forms (Horthy,
Piłsudski, PRI Mexico). Fascism itself is one of six revolutionary
mass-movement types since the 1790s (Jacobin, anarchist, Socialist,
Leninist, Fascist, populist).

## 4. Structural laws of fascist success and failure

- **The 40 percent barrier:** no fascist movement approached an electoral
  majority; "no fascist leader ever seized power exclusively on his own" —
  all required conservative-right allies (and Hitler and Mussolini both began
  as multiparty coalitions).
- **The preemption paradox:** "fascist movements required political freedom
  to have a chance to win power." Wherever a nonfascist authoritarian regime
  closed that freedom preemptively — Austria, Hungary, Romania, Portugal —
  popular fascist movements were suppressed and could reach power only
  through outside (German) military intervention. Across ch. 5 and 9 Payne
  shows the commonest fate of a fascist movement was repression *by* the
  authoritarian right.
- **Why Italy first:** only Italy combined a protracted crisis, an
  intermediate development level, a sophisticated political culture, and a
  genuine revolutionary-left challenge; elsewhere in the 1920s crises "were
  more quickly resolved by simple military intervention."

## 5. The retrodictive theory (ch. 13)

Payne's signature inductive contribution: significant fascist movements
(~20 percent or more of the electorate) emerged in only five countries —
Italy, Germany, Austria, Hungary, Romania (Spain and Croatia partial cases
under unique crisis conditions). The converging preconditions:

- **Cultural:** fin-de-siècle intense nationalism, militarism, and Social
  Darwinism fused with vitalism and the hero cult; a pre-existing nationalist
  movement at least half a generation old; cultural space opened by
  secularization (Romania the hybrid exception).
- **Political:** a comparatively **new state** (all five founded/unified in
  the 1860s–70s); liberal democracy present but **not consolidated** — no
  country with pre-WWI or generation-old democracy succumbed, yet
  near-democratic conditions were *necessary* for fascist mobilization;
  sharp party fragmentation (stable party systems — Britain, France, the Low
  Countries — were "largely immune").
- **Social/economic:** a real or perceived leftist menace; lower-middle-class
  base as the most consistent (not universal) stratum; **no single economic
  common denominator** except the perception that crisis stemmed from defeat
  or foreign exploitation; the military no longer dominant in politics.
- **International:** status deprivation (defeat, "mutilated victory"), and —
  except for Italy and Germany — a foreign role model, though only movements
  with autochthonous roots succeeded.

"Not one of the factors... was of any great significance by itself... Only if
the majority of them converged in a given country between the wars was it
possible for a truly fascistogenic situation to develop." Payne adds the
corrective that antifascists always outnumbered fascists in interwar Europe:
the Falange's 0.7 percent of 1936 "was much nearer the norm than the 38
percent won by the Nazis in 1932."

**Epilogue application:** all preconditions disappeared after 1945 (military
disaster, Cold War bipolarity, consolidated Western democracy, prosperity),
so classic fascism cannot recur in its 1919–45 form; post-1945 successors
divide into moderate authoritarian-nationalist parties, a "radical right"
that (unlike historic fascism) accepts a place on the right and defends
established interests, and marginal terroristic neofascism.

## Application at monographic depth: Spain (Payne 1987)

*The Franco Regime* ([[sources/payne-franco-regime-1987]]) operationalizes the typology on its founding terrain. The Spanish radical right (Acción Española, Calvo Sotelo's Bloque Nacional, Carlism) is distinguished from the fascist Falange by reliance on traditional elites and clericalism versus mobilizational national syndicalism; Payne's retrospective judgment is that high Franquism (1937–59) was built more on Calvo Sotelo's ideas than José Antonio's. Chapter 23 adds the comparative continuum: Mussolini's Italy (most revolutionary tension), Franco's Spain (middle), Salazar's Portugal (least — never fully confessional, façade elections, hence NATO by 1949), sharing authoritarianism, corporative economics, bureaucratized nonrevolutionary state parties, semipluralism, and (1937–45) imperialist foreign policy. Payne also imports O'Donnell's Latin-Americanist "bureaucratic authoritarianism" for Portugal and late-phase Francoism while insisting 1930s Spanish politics was far more mobilized than pre-1970 Latin America; and his Franco–Tito comparison (civil-war victors, Tito proportionately bloodier in 1945 yet better received in the West) underwrites his complaint that Franco was made "primary resident ogre of western Europe" disproportionately.

## Emic vs. etic

Entirely etic: no interwar actor used these categories, and most movements
called "fascist" rejected the name. Payne builds them retrodictively from
the completed record.

## Limitations and critiques

The check-list approach is criticized (notably by
[[paxton-anatomy-of-fascism-2004|Paxton]]) as static — describing what
fascism *was* rather than how it *worked* and radicalized; Griffin's
one-sentence palingenetic definition (which Payne critiques as
over-inclusive — "populist" and "palingenetic" fit non-fascist cases like
Bolivia's MNR) competes as the field's compact alternative. The
fascist/radical-right boundary requires case-by-case judgment calls (Spain,
Austria, Japan) that critics find circular. Griffin's own critique (now
ingested — [[griffin-nature-of-fascism-1991]]): the "elaborate tripartite
'typology' is somewhat cumbersome," and it is odd that fascism alone should
need "style" and "negations" to supplement ideological goals as a
definition — though he counts Payne's "the most successful paradigm of
generic fascism" in circulation and cites Payne's own "new state / new
culture" language as corroborating the palingenetic core. Griffin also
contests Payne's Europe-boundedness claim (true only "if considerable
weight is placed on the word 'primarily'"), applying his type to Brazil's
Integralism, Japan's Tōhōkai, and South Africa's AWB; his [[para-fascism]]
category maps closely onto Payne's radical/conservative right sectors. See
[[generic-fascism]] for the full definitional field and
[[palingenetic-ultranationalism]] for the competing type.
