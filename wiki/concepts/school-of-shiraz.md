## Definition and Scope

The "School of Shīrāz" is a retrospective historiographical label for the tradition of philosophical and theological activity centered on the city of Shīrāz in southern Iran (modern Fārs province), running from approximately the mid-14th century to the early Safavid period (c. 1500–1600). The city became the major intellectual center of the eastern Islamic world after the Mongol destruction of Baghdad, surviving the Mongol invasions largely intact because its rulers prudently submitted rather than resisting.

Adamson notes that "some scholars have referred to these philosophers as forming a 'school of Shīrāz,'" but considers the more accurate description to be a "duel of Shīrāz" — given the intense personal and philosophical hostility between the tradition's two principal 15th-century figures, Jalāl al-Dīn Dawānī and Ṣadr al-Dīn Dashtakī ([[adamson-philosophy-islamic-world-2016]]).

## Historical Development

### Foundation: al-Ījī and al-Jurjānī (mid–late 14th century)

Philosophical activity at Shīrāz can be traced to **Ṣadr al-Dīn al-Ījī** (d. 1355), a highly placed judge under the Mongol Īl-Khāns and a significant theologian who developed Ashʿarite kalām in dialogue with Avicennan philosophy. His works include speculative cosmological arguments: he questioned whether the earth might be revolving, and wondered whether transparent celestial spheres were philosophically necessary. He also wrote an influential summary of Islamic doctrine that became required reading for centuries.

**al-Jurjānī** (14th–15th c.) visited Shīrāz and wrote a commentary on al-Ījī's theological work that was so celebrated it was studied alongside al-Ījī's text all the way into the modern era, including at al-Azhar University in Cairo. The competition between Māturīdī scholars (fleeing the Mongols from central Asia) and Ashʿarites like al-Ījī intensified theological debate in Shīrāz: "Philosophical theology is like any other business: it thrives under the pressure of competition."

### The Dawānī–Dashtakī Rivalry (late 15th century)

The most intense episode in Shīrāz's philosophical history was the personal and intellectual feud between **Jalāl al-Dīn Dawānī** (d. c. 1502) and **Ṣadr al-Dīn Dashtakī** (d. c. 1498), and the continuation of that feud by Dashtakī's son **Ghiyāth al-Dīn Dashtakī**. Both Dawānī and the elder Dashtakī claimed to be eleventh-generation intellectual descendants of Avicenna, their chains of teachers converging at [[nasir-al-din-tusi]] and his student Quṭb al-Dīn al-Shīrāzī — "whose name incidentally reminds us that he was yet another major intellectual associated with our new favorite city."

The feud was total: face-to-face debates, polemical treatises, and glosses-on-glosses attacking each other's commentary-on-commentary. Ghiyāth al-Dīn Dashtakī devoted most of his writings to attacking Dawānī. Key disputed questions included:

- Whether God has an essence, and if so what it is
- The univocal vs. analogical interpretation of "existence" as applied to God and creatures
- The validity of "Ibn Kammūna's sophistry" (an argument for divine uniqueness that Dawānī accepted and Dashtakī rejected)
- Solutions to the Liar Paradox

### The Confessional Dimension

The rivalry may have had a Sunni–Shiite dimension. Dawānī appears more comfortable with Ashʿarite Sunni kalām as transmitted from al-Ījī; the Dashtakīs were critical of Ashʿarite theology going back to al-Ghazālī, suggesting possible Shiite sympathies. The question cannot be definitively resolved, since once the Shiite Safavids took over Iran (c. 1501), any Shiite identity would be expressed more openly and any Sunni affiliation might be publicly adjusted.

## Core Philosophical Debates

### Existence: Univocal vs. Analogical

Dawānī followed al-Ṭūsī's analogical position: existence means something fundamentally different when applied to God versus creatures. God just *is* existence; creatures merely *participate* in existence. This has a Sufi flavor, tending toward the "oneness of existence" (*waḥdat al-wujūd*) doctrine — things depend entirely on God's presence in them for whatever dependent reality they have.

Dashtakī followed [[fakhr-al-din-al-razi]]'s univocal position: "existence" means the same thing whether said of God or creatures. The difference between God and creatures lies not in the type of existence but in the *essence*: God has no essence at all, while creatures have particular essences that make them contingent. Dashtakī thus preserves God's radical simplicity differently from Dawānī — not by making God uniquely identical with existence, but by denying He has any essence to be identified with.

### The Liar Paradox

Both camps produced treatises on the Liar Paradox, following in a tradition stretching from [[nasir-al-din-tusi]] (who had diagnosed it as a self-reference problem) through al-Kātibī and al-Taftazānī. Dawānī proposed that the paradoxical statement fails to qualify as a meaningful statement at all, since the ability to be consistently true or false is a prerequisite for meaningful assertion. Ṣadr al-Dīn Dashtakī offered a more sophisticated diagnosis: the paradox arises from a clash between "first-order and second-order truth" — the statement is about itself, meaning its truth-value depends on the truth-value of its own reference, generating the infinite regress.

### Commentary as Philosophy

A distinctive feature of Shīrāz philosophy is the dominance of commentary and gloss form. Dawānī wrote commentaries on al-Bayḍawī, al-Ījī, and al-Taftazānī, as well as a "self-commentary" on one of his own works. Ghiyāth al-Dīn Dashtakī explained that his father wrote almost nothing except glosses because this *increased* originality: one could focus on genuinely new points without rehearsing established ground. Adamson endorses this: "we should avoid assuming that the dominance of commentary and glosses in these centuries is a sign of philosophical or intellectual stagnation."

## Significance and Legacy

The school of Shīrāz represents the immediate pre-Safavid intellectual environment that shaped Mullā Ṣadrā (Ṣadr al-Dīn Shīrāzī, 1571–1636), who studied in Shīrāz before moving to Isfahan. Ṣadrā's great synthesis — drawing together Avicennism, [[illuminationism]], philosophical Sufism, and Shiite theology — was built on the foundations laid by the Shiraz tradition. The centuries of debate over the nature of existence, the proper interpretation of Avicenna, and the limits of demonstration gave Ṣadrā the tools for his own systematic philosophy.

The commentary and gloss tradition of Shīrāz also shaped the Ottoman and Mughal philosophical traditions — works by al-Ījī, al-Jurjānī, and al-Taftazānī were studied in madrasas across the Islamic world into the modern period.

## Collection Coverage Note

Coverage of the Shīrāz tradition in the Bibliotheca Alexandrina is weak. Adamson notes that the Ottoman and Mughal philosophical traditions "remain almost entirely untouched by secondary literature in European languages," and the pre-Safavid Shīrāz tradition is only slightly better covered. No primary texts by Dawānī or the Dashtakīs are ingested; the coverage here is entirely from Adamson's secondary treatment.
