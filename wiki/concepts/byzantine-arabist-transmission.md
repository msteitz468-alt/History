## Definition

The Byzantine and Arabist transmission channels are the hypothesized routes by which
mathematical Islamic astronomy — specifically the Maragha-school results of al-ʿUrḍī, al-Ṭūsī,
and Ibn al-Shāṭir — reached Nicolaus Copernicus and other Renaissance scientists in the
15th–16th centuries without passing through Latin translation. This mode of transmission is
distinctly different from the earlier [[the-translation-movement|Translation Movement]] of the
medieval period: rather than wholesale Arabic-to-Latin translation, it operated through
Byzantine Greek intermediaries, technically literate Arabists who could read the original texts,
and personal networks of scholars.

Saliba argues this channel is the necessary explanation for the mathematical identities between
Copernican and Maragha-school astronomy identified by Neugebauer, Kennedy, Roberts, Hartner,
and Swerdlow. See [[islamic-science-and-copernicus]].

## The Byzantine Route

Byzantine astronomers began traveling to Islamic lands in the 14th century to acquire the latest
astronomical knowledge. Gregory Chioniades (14th c.) is the documented early case. Over
roughly two centuries of Byzantine-Islamic contact, Maragha-school results were absorbed into
Byzantine Greek astronomical literature, whose technical terminology (as Saliba notes) bore
"closer resemblance to the Arabic sources, from which it was derived, than to the classical
Greek texts such as those of Ptolemy."

With the fall of Constantinople to the Ottomans in 1453, Byzantine scholars and their books —
already carrying Islamic mathematical astronomy — moved westward into Italy. Because the
Renaissance intellectual environment privileged Greek, these texts were read in their original
Greek rather than translated into Latin. The Islamic mathematical content arrived in European
learned circles embedded in Greek-language sources.

Otto Neugebauer pursued this route and identified Vatican manuscript Gr. 211 — a Byzantine
Greek text containing a version of the Ṭūsī Couple — as a possible conduit. However, this
manuscript lacks the *proof* of the Couple, whereas the letter-correspondence between Ṭūsī's
Arabic proof and Copernicus's Latin proof (down to identical alphabetic labels for geometric
points) requires that Copernicus had access to a source containing the full proof. The Byzantine
route alone cannot fully account for the evidence.

## Renaissance Arabists: A European Network

Concurrent with the Byzantine channel, a network of European scholars in the 15th–16th
centuries acquired direct competence in Arabic and access to technical Islamic scientific manuscripts.

**Guillaume Postel (1510–1581)**: The best-documented case. Postel owned:
- Vatican manuscript Arabo 319, a copy of Ṭūsī's *Tadhkira fī ʿilm al-hayʾa* with Postel's
  Latin marginal annotations — demonstrating his ability to read highly technical astronomical
  Arabic. The manuscript was well preserved around the chapter containing the statement and proof
  of the Ṭūsī Couple.
- Bibliothèque Nationale manuscript of al-Kharaqī's *Muntahā al-idrāk fī taqāsīm al-aflāk*
  (1138/9), with the inscription "ex libris guilielmi postelli" and the date "G. postellus
  Constantino­poli 1536" — acquired during the French diplomatic mission to Suleiman the
  Magnificent that produced the Franco-Ottoman capitulations of 1536.

Postel was appointed professor of mathematics and oriental languages at the Collège Royal
(later Collège de France) — a direct reward for the Constantinople mission. He was Copernicus's
exact contemporary (Copernicus died 1543, Postel was dismissed from the Collège the same year).

**Leo Africanus (al-Ḥasan b. Muḥammad Ibn al-Wazzān, d. ca. 1550)**: North African polymath
who taught Arabic at Bologna — on the Venice-Florence corridor where much Renaissance
intellectual exchange concentrated. He was Copernicus's contemporary. His student Jean-Albert
Widmanstadt (1506–c.1559) was also an Arabist familiar with technical Islamic scientific texts.

**Andreas Alpagus (d. 1522)**: Spent approximately 15 years in Damascus, returned to assume
the chair of medicine at Padua in 1505 — possibly overlapping with Copernicus's time in that
region. He produced new Latin translations of Avicenna's medical works directly from Arabic.
His presence in Damascus would have given him knowledge of Islamic astronomy, including the
reform tradition associated with Ibn al-Shāṭir (d. 1375) — Damascene — a century earlier.

**Patriarch Niʿmatallāh (d. 1590)**: A Syriac Jacobite patriarch who fled Diyār Bakr and
arrived in Venice, then Rome, carrying astronomical manuscripts from the Islamic world. His
library, acquired by Cardinal Ferdinand de Medici, formed the basis of the Medici Oriental Press.
Niʿmatallāh served on the committee that designed the Gregorian calendar reform (1582),
contributing astronomical parameters from Islamic sources more precise than the available Greek
or medieval Latin values.

## The Medici Oriental Press

Cardinal Ferdinand de Medici's Arabic press published, among other texts, 3,000 copies of a
reworking of Euclid's *Elements* — not from the Greek original or a standard Arabic translation,
but from Ṭūsī's 13th-century mathematical reworking. The disproportionate print run (3,000
Euclid copies vs. 1,500 Arabic Bibles; 1,033 Euclid copies sold vs. 934 Bibles) suggests the
true market was European scholars seeking the Islamic mathematical tradition, not missionaries
converting Muslims. This confirms active demand for Islamic scientific texts in 16th-century
Europe.

## Scientific Instruments as Evidence

Parallel to the textual transmission, Islamic scientific instruments reached Europe through
direct copying and hybrid production:

- Antonio de Sangallo the Younger (1484–1546) — the architect of St. Peter's Basilica — made a
  detailed drawing of a 9th-century Baghdad astrolabe, copying the Arabic maker's inscription
  (*ṣanaʿahu Khafīf ghulām ʿAlī b. ʿĪsā*) though apparently without knowing Arabic. This
  suggests Renaissance men of science "thought very highly of all scientific things coming to
  them from the Islamic world, even instruments that were made centuries earlier."

- The Arsenius family of Flemish astrolabists fitted new Latin-inscribed retes to existing Arabic
  astrolabe maters — requiring bilingual competence to use the resulting hybrid instruments.
  This provides a concrete instance of Islamic-to-European instrument transfer in the 16th century.

## The Shift from Medieval to Renaissance Transmission

Saliba identifies a qualitative change in how Islamic knowledge reached Europe between the
medieval and Renaissance periods:

- **Medieval mode** (the [[the-translation-movement|Translation Movement]]): Scholars waited for
  Arabic texts to be translated into Latin before absorbing their content; ideas arrived within
  the linguistic frame of the translations.
- **Renaissance mode**: Men of science became Arabists themselves. They could go directly to
  Arabic manuscripts and extract ideas without full translation. The ideas entered Latin scientific
  texts — including potentially Copernicus's — without the intermediate text being rendered into
  Latin at all.

This explains a pattern that otherwise appears paradoxical: original ideas developed in Arabic
in the 12th–15th centuries — including ideas written expressly *against* Greek tradition, not
to "preserve" it — reappear in Latin scientific texts of the 16th–17th centuries without any
known Latin translation having been made.

## The Unresolved Question

The identity of the specific text or person who transmitted the Maragha results to Copernicus
remains unknown. The evidence requires a source containing elements from ʿUrḍī, Ṭūsī, *and*
Ibn al-Shāṭir — suggesting a later commentary that synthesized all three, or an oral transmission
by an intermediary (such as Postel or a member of the Widmanstadt circle) who assembled these
elements from multiple manuscripts. Saliba proposes this as the primary open research question
in the history of Islamic-European scientific exchange.
