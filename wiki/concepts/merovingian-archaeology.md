# Merovingian Archaeology

**The archaeological study of post-Roman Gaul (fifth–eighth centuries) and the discipline's
own contested history.** Born in nineteenth-century France from the accidental unearthing of
furnished graves, Merovingian archaeology spent its first century reading row-grave cemeteries
(*Reihengräber*) through a nationalist and racial lens, then — especially after 1980 — turned
decisively from cemeteries to settlements, landscapes, trade, and the sciences. Bonnie Effros's
survey (Ch. 4) treats the field's history as inseparable from its findings: how the Merovingian
past was dug up is also the history of how modern nations imagined their origins.

## Definition and Scope

The discipline covers graves, settlements, churches, towns, coinage, and the material residue of
the Frankish kingdoms, and increasingly the bioarchaeological record (see
[[merovingian-bioarchaeology]]). It is *not* simply the illustration of texts: the volume's
governing claim is that material culture can *question* the written record rather than echo it.

- **Industrial revolution as catalyst.** Railways, urban growth, and intensified agriculture
  exposed (and often destroyed) cemeteries: "the modernization and growth of France's industrial,
  agricultural, and urban infrastructure led to the discovery (and, in many instances, the
  destruction) of evidence of its late and post-Roman past." Without that infrastructure the field
  "would have developed significantly later."
- **Cemetery-driven origins.** Early Merovingian archaeology was "driven initially by the
  serendipitous discoveries of previously unknown cemeteries," yielding weaponry, buckles,
  ceramics, glass, and bone. Local antiquarian-society members, not professionals, did the
  excavating, first identifying weaponed graves as "Frankish" in Normandy and Lorraine.

## Causal Drivers and Major Phases

- **Founding figures.** Abbé Jean-Benoît-Désiré Cochet (d. 1875) is styled "the father of
  Merovingian archaeology" — though he "often omitted site maps and artifact inventories from his
  publications." Jules Pilloy in the Aisne (1880s) pioneered combined stratigraphic, spatial, and
  typological analysis; Casimir Barrière-Flavy's personally funded 1901 survey catalogued **2,315**
  Merovingian-era cemeteries and claimed to read Frankish, Burgundian, and Visigothic ethnic zones
  from grave-goods — "conclusions that were challenged even in his own lifetime."
- **The dating problem.** The field's most vexing difficulty was chronology, dependent on coin
  finds and relative typology. Frédéric Troyon's insight at Bel-Air (1841) — that cemeteries
  accreted "over decades or even centuries" rather than recording a single battle — was decisive;
  radiocarbon dating reached archaeology only in the 1940s. "It took more than a century… to
  establish more accurate relative and absolute chronologies."
- **Racial science and its discrediting.** Late-nineteenth-century craniologists labelled
  dolichocephalic (long) skulls Germanic and brachycephalic (round) skulls Gallo-Roman; though
  "ultimately discredited due to their small sample sizes and overstated conclusions," such methods
  "buoyed an abiding popular… acceptance of racial distinctions between Gallo-Romans and Franks."
- **Édouard Salin, transitional figure.** An engineer, Salin excavated Lorraine cemeteries from the
  1920s–30s and showed the *blending* of Gallo-Roman and Frankish populations across generations —
  directly rebutting German claims of pure Germanic descent in eastern France — culminating in the
  four-volume *La civilisation mérovingienne* (1952–1959).
- **Post-1980 reorientation.** "Unlike in the period before World War II, cemeteries no longer
  constitute the primary focus of early medieval archaeological research in France." Attention
  shifted to landscapes, economy and trade (see [[merovingian-economy]], [[merovingian-emporia]]),
  church architecture (see [[merovingian-religious-architecture]]), and
  [[merovingian-rural-settlement|rural settlement]].

## Nationalism, Race, and the Politics of the Discipline

Merovingian archaeology was political from the start. In France under Napoleon III and the Third
Republic, scholars favoured a binary in which "Franks were the progenitors exclusively of the
Frankish royal house and aristocracy, whereas the Gallo-Romans were the ancestors of the French" —
which kept the discipline's national implications muted at home even as it flourished elsewhere in
Europe. Napoleon III founded the national antiquities museum at **Saint-Germain-en-Laye** (1862;
inaugurated 1867), expanded from a Gallo-Roman to an all-of-pre-Carolingian-France remit. In
Germany, scholars from Bonn defined *Kulturströmungen* ("cultural currents") to argue Germanic
continuity, and during the Second World War German settlement maps were used by National Socialists
to justify the colonization of Belgium and the Loire Valley. The Gallo-Roman/Germanic grave binary
"remained the most common trope of archaeological exploration" well into the post-war decades — the
**Frénouville** cemetery (Calvados; in use late-3rd–late-7th c., 650 graves, ≥801 individuals) was
still read in 1980 (Pilet and Buchet) as proof of an "exclusively indigenous" population, against
the post-war consensus that ethnicity was culturally constructed (see [[ethnogenesis]]).

## Institutional Trajectory

A recurrent theme is France's "largely decentralized and inconsistently funded" archaeology. Key
landmarks: the Belgian *tablettes* (1893) — preprinted excavation recording cards (grave number,
age/sex, orientation, artifact diagrams), "ingenious" but not widely adopted in France; the **Loi
Carcopino** (27 September 1941), the first law to regulate excavation; the founding of **AFAM**
(Association française d'archéologie mérovingienne, 1979); and **Inrap** (2001), the rescue-
archaeology institute. Inrap's legacy is "ambiguous": vastly more sites are dug under development
pressure, but "too many… have not been studied in their entirety," many reports remain unpublished,
and France lags the UK and Germany in isotopic and DNA analysis.

## Interaction with Other Fields

Merovingian archaeology now feeds, and is fed by, [[merovingian-bioarchaeology]],
[[merovingian-coinage|numismatics]], [[garnet-and-bead-trade|trace-element provenancing]], and the
study of [[merovingian-emporia|trade ports]]. It stands in productive tension with the text-based
field surveyed in [[merovingian-studies-historiography]].

## Historiography

The discipline's self-criticism is now part of its content: Effros frames two centuries of digging
as shaped at every turn by [[rois-faineants|the decline narrative]], by nationalism, and by the
grip of Augustin Thierry's *Récits des temps mérovingiens*, which "profoundly shaped the
exploration and vision of early French archaeologists." The post-war turn to settlement archaeology
and the sciences is the discipline's escape route from the ethnic-grave paradigm, but the
under-publication of rescue excavations remains its central methodological worry.
