## Definition

A **phylarch** (Greek *phylarchos*, "ruler of a tribe") was the title the late Roman
state gave to an allied Arab leader through whom it managed the Arab *foederati* of the
desert frontier. A **super-phylarchate** is the modern term for the arrangement — most
clearly instituted by the emperor **Justinian** around 528–529 — under which a single Arab
dynast was elevated over the ordinary phylarchs and given supreme command of Rome's Arab
allies across a very large stretch of frontier, coupled with high Roman court rank
(*patrikios*, *endoxotatos*). The paradigmatic holder was **al-Harith ibn Jabala**
("Arethas", the Jafnid/[[ghassanids|Ghassanid]] king); his Persian-Arab counterpart was
the Nasrid/[[lakhmids|Lakhmid]] leader al-Mundhir III ("Alamoundaros") at al-Hira. The
office was, unusually, made effectively **hereditary**, passing to al-Harith's son
al-Mundhir.

The super-phylarchate is best understood as an instrument of **indirect rule**: the state
elevated a chosen elite, concentrated resources and prestige on him, and used him as the
single conduit to a manpower reservoir it could not otherwise tax or command. Its two
functions were military (mobile Arab cavalry for the Romano-Persian wars) and religious
(the Jafnids became the political patrons of the Miaphysite/Monophysite church — see
[[council-of-chalcedon-451]] and its aftermath).

## Emic vs Etic

- **Etic:** "super-phylarchate", "Jafnid", "Nasrid" are all modern scholarly conventions.
  Contemporary Graeco-Roman texts speak of "Roman Arabs" and "Persian Arabs" by political
  allegiance; the dynastic labels derive from the later Arab-Islamic tradition and have
  thin ancient basis.
- **Emic:** the leaders themselves used a **double register** of titles — *malik* ("king")
  for their own tribal audience (attested for al-Harith at Jabal Says, and for Hujr of
  [[kinda]] under Himyar), and the Roman court grades (*patrikios*, *paneuphemos*) for the
  imperial audience. Neither Rome nor Himyar formally acknowledged the "royal" title.

## Analytical Frame — "Tribal Fixers" and Middlemen

Fisher analyses the super-phylarchs through the anthropology of **tribe/state relations**
(Barth, Salzman, Tapper, Lancaster, Beck, Gellner). The super-phylarch is a **middleman**:
created by the state to rule a "difficult and remote population," yet thereby able to
safeguard tribal interests and — because he alone controls access to tribal manpower —
capable of turning on his patron. The middleman's leverage is the **withdrawal of
manpower**; his structural weakness is Salzman's "middleman's dilemma," the "reluctant
bottom man" who is ultimately expendable (dramatised by the deposition and exile of the
Jafnid al-Mundhir to Sicily in 582).

Fisher prefers the label **"tribal fixer"** over his own earlier "secular holy man": the
super-phylarch was a purveyor and broker of resources (military, political, and
increasingly religious) who accrued a **moral-religious authority** adjacent to — but
distinct from — the divine authority of bishops, monks, and holy men (Peter Brown's late
antique holy man). Patronage of the cult of **St Sergius** (Rusafa/Sergiopolis) let the
Jafnids appeal across sectarian lines.

## The "Germans and Arabs" Comparison

The super-phylarchate is the eastern analogue of the Germanic *foederati* leadership of
the late Roman west (the European Science Foundation's *Transformation of the Roman World*
project). Arab dynasts, like [[clovis]] or [[theoderic-the-great|Theoderic]], had "double identities" (Roman title +
tribal kingship) and underwent state formation through sustained imperial contact. The
decisive **difference**: in the west Roman power collapsed and the *foederati* leaders
became independent kings; in the east Rome and Persia survived and *deliberately terminated*
their super-phylarchates (Rome deposing al-Mundhir in 582; Persia executing al-Numan III
and abolishing the Lakhmid client-kingship c. 602). Measured against Chris Wickham's
criteria for state-like structures (centralised authority outlasting officeholders, public
power, secure elite resource base, stratification), the super-phylarchs fall short of
Theoderic's Ostrogothic Italy precisely because their hierarchy was *supplied by the
patron state* rather than self-generated — a "tribal quasi-state" (Gellner) rather than a
kingdom.

## Contested Points

- Whether the super-phylarchs drew independent tax income and controlled territory, or were
  wholly dependent on state subsidy and plunder (evidence uncertain; Fisher leans toward
  some genuine economic base given the building inscriptions).
- Whether the abolition of the super-phylarchates c. 582–602 materially weakened Rome and
  Persia on the eve of the Arab conquests, or was strategically marginal.
- The relation of the super-phylarchate to the emergence of a unified Arab leadership under
  Muhammad — see the "second tier" argument on [[muhammad-and-the-rise-of-islam]].
