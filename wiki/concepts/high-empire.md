# High Empire

A historiographical period label for the Roman Empire at its territorial
and institutional peak, conventionally spanning the "Five Good Emperors"
from Nerva (96 CE) through Marcus Aurelius, and often extended through the
Severan dynasty to 235 CE, before the onset of the Crisis of the Third
Century.

## Definition and Scope

The label distinguishes this era — characterized by relative internal
stability, adoptive succession among the early Antonines, extensive
prosperity, and the empire's greatest territorial extent under Trajan —
from both the earlier, more turbulent Julio-Claudian and Flavian periods
(the early Principate) and the later "Dominate," the more overtly
autocratic, militarized, and administratively restructured empire that
emerged from the third-century crisis under Diocletian and Constantine.

## Origin (Etic)

"High Empire" is an etic, modern scholarly periodization rather than a term
contemporary Romans used of themselves; it is used throughout this
collection's Roman-period pages (covering the era's art, religion, social
structure, philosophy, and administration) as shorthand for this
conventional span.

## Applications and Examples

Institutions and social structures distinctive to this period — equestrian
administrative expansion, characteristic patterns of imperial religion and
philosophy, and the social structure Rome's elite maintained at its
territorial height — are treated across this collection's dedicated
High Empire-era pages; the [[militarization-of-the-roman-state|
militarization of the Roman state]] under the Severan dynasty marks the
period's transition toward the following Crisis of the Third Century.

## Limitations and Critiques

The boundaries of "High Empire" are not sharply fixed: some historians
extend it to include the Severans (235 CE), while others treat the Severan
period as a distinct transitional phase; the label also risks implying a
uniformity of prosperity and stability across nearly a century and a half
that in fact varied considerably by region and period.
