# Radiocarbon Revolution

The **Radiocarbon Revolution** refers to the transformation of prehistoric chronology and historical interpretation brought about by radiocarbon (¹⁴C) dating, beginning in the late 1940s and especially its calibration in the 1960s–70s.

## Definition and Method

Radiocarbon dating measures the decay of radioactive carbon-14 in organic materials (charcoal, bone, shell, seeds, etc.). Libby’s initial work provided an independent, absolute timescale for prehistory that no longer depended on cross-dating with historically anchored sequences in Egypt or Mesopotamia.

Key subsequent development: **calibration**. Comparisons with tree-ring sequences (especially the long-lived bristlecone pine) revealed a systematic discrepancy: uncalibrated radiocarbon dates before ~250 BCE are too young. The error increases with age (roughly 350 years at 2000 BCE, ~800 years at 5000 BCE). Calibration curves convert "radiocarbon years" into approximate calendar years.

## Impact on European Prehistory (per Champion et al. 1984)

Champion, Gamble, Shennan & Whittle devote substantial space in Ch. 1 to the revolution's consequences:

- Many traditional links between Europe and the Near East "are no longer tenable."
- Events previously thought contemporary were separated by centuries.
- "Above all, it has greatly lengthened the time-span of European prehistory; the neolithic period in particular can now be seen to have lasted millennia rather than just centuries."
- The need to rethink Childe's diffusionist model became acute: megaliths, metallurgy, and other innovations could no longer be explained as derivatives of earlier Eastern prototypes arriving via migration or influence.
- The old dependence on Near Eastern historical chronologies for dating Europe was broken.

The authors present a calibration graph (after R. M. Clark 1975) and note ongoing debates about the exact curve, especially for the early post-glacial.

Detailed discussion of calibration: "Cross-checks between dendrochronology and radiocarbon dating revealed a recurrent discrepancy between calendar and radiocarbon years such that from c.250 BC backwards, radiocarbon dates are too young. ... The error is about 350 years at 2000 BC, and 800 years at 5000 BC." The early post-glacial extrapolation is treated as a working estimate only. (Champion et al. 1984, Ch.1 / range lines ~460-495)

## Broader Historiographic Significance

- Destroyed the chronological scaffolding of culture-historical diffusionism.
- Enabled (and demanded) new questions: not "where did this come from?" but "how did local societies organize subsistence, settlement, exchange, and social relations?"
- Opened space for processual archaeology's emphasis on internal dynamics, adaptation, and systems.
- For prehistoric periods, radiocarbon (plus later refinements: AMS dating, calibration curves, Bayesian modeling, and complementary methods such as dendrochronology and varve counting) remains the primary absolute dating tool.

## Limitations and Developments

- Requires organic material; reservoir effects (marine, freshwater) and old-wood problems must be corrected.
- Precision varies; plateaus in the calibration curve create ambiguous ranges.
- Calibration itself has been repeatedly refined.
- Later methods (thermoluminescence, archaeomagnetism, uranium-series, cosmogenic nuclides) complement it, but ¹⁴C + calibration remains foundational for the Holocene and late Pleistocene.
