# Compellence

**Compellence** is the use of threats (or limited force) to induce an adversary to take a positive action or to cease an ongoing action — in Schelling's formulation, "inducing withdrawal or acquiescence." It is the active, offensive half of coercion; its mirror image is [[deterrence|deterrence]], which seeks only to prevent an undesired action.

Schelling introduced the term because "coercion" was too broad (it includes both preventing and causing) and "deterrence" was already strongly associated with the passive, defensive posture of threatening retaliation to preserve the status quo. "Deterrence demanded an opponent's inaction; compellence demanded action or ceasing adverse actions." "Deterrence was about the status quo and had no obvious time limits; compellence projected forward to a new place and could be urgent."

## Distinctions from Deterrence

- **Direction**: Deterrence preserves the status quo by making the cost of change prohibitive. Compellence alters the status quo (or stops an adverse change already underway) by making continued resistance more painful than compliance.
- **Initiative and timing**: The compeller must usually act first or create a deadline; the deterrer can wait. "Compliance with a deterrent threat can be rationalized as something that one was going to do anyhow." Compliance with compellence is visible surrender.
- **Credibility burden**: Compellent threats are often harder to make credible because the threatener must demonstrate willingness to suffer (or risk) costs to enforce them, and because the target can test resolve incrementally.
- **Examples of the difference**: "You will not cross this line or I will hurt you" (deterrence) vs. "You will withdraw your forces across that line or I will hurt you" (compellence). Blockade to prevent ships arriving (deterrence) vs. blockade to force ships already at sea to turn around (compellence).

## Techniques and Requirements (from *Arms and Influence*)

Successful compellence requires:
- A clear, specific demand (the target must know exactly what action or cessation will cause the threat to be lifted).
- A credible capability and will to inflict (or continue) the threatened pain.
- A perceived ability to withhold or modulate the pain contingent on compliance (the "assurance" side of the bargain).
- Often, commitment devices that remove the compeller's own option to back down cheaply.

Schelling emphasizes that brute force can sometimes achieve what compellence attempts (simply taking the desired object), but when brute force is too costly, too risky, or politically unacceptable, compellence through the "power to hurt" becomes the relevant instrument.

**Deterrence vs. compellence mechanics (direct from Ch. 2):** "To deter an enemy's advance it may be enough to burn the escape bridges behind me, or to rig a trip-wire between us that automatically blows us both up when he advances. To compel an enemy's retreat, though, by some threat of engagement, I have to be committed to move. (This requires setting fire to the grass behind me as I face the enemy, with the wind blowing toward the enemy.) I can block your car by placing mine in the way; my deterrent threat is passive, the decision to collide is up to you. But if you find me in your way and threaten to collide unless I move, you enjoy no such advantage; ... The threat that compels rather than deters often requires that the punishment be administered until the other acts, rather than if he acts."

Compellent campaigns benefit from "connectedness" to the demanded behavior so the target understands what will cause pressure to stop.

## Historical and Contemporary Reach

Although coined in the nuclear context, the category applies across eras: ancient sieges intended to force surrender rather than storm the walls; economic sanctions aimed at changing behavior; limited airstrikes or naval demonstrations meant to compel policy change; "no-fly zone" enforcement that is really about forcing a government to stop certain overflights or attacks.

In the 2008 afterword Schelling notes that nuclear weapons themselves, since 1945, have been used almost exclusively for influence (both deterrent and compellent postures) rather than on the battlefield. New nuclear states or even terrorists may find compellent value in possession and the threat of use greater than any direct destructive payoff.

## Relation to Other Concepts

- Closely related to [[armed-suasion]] (Luttwak), which treats both deterrence and compellence as forms of suasion by the latent or active threat of force.
- Overlaps with [[escalation-dominance]] and [[manipulation-of-risk]]: compellence often works by manipulating the shared risk that the situation will get out of hand.
- Distinct from pure brute force or conquest: the target retains the theoretical choice to comply or suffer; the compeller is trying to structure that choice.

## Critiques

Critics have noted that the clean theoretical distinction can blur in practice (many "deterrent" deployments have compellent effects or vice versa), that compellence frequently fails when the target values the stake more highly than the compeller anticipates, and that the abstract bargaining model underplays the role of honor, ideology, domestic politics, and misperception in real crises.
