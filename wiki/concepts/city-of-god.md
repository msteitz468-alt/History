# The City of God (*De Civitate Dei*)

The *City of God* is **[[augustine-of-hippo|Augustine's]]** longest and most consequential
work — twenty-two books written between 413 and c. 426 — and the origin of one of the most
durable frameworks of Western political theology: the doctrine of the **two cities**. Augustine
called it *magnum opus et arduum*, "a giant of a book."

## Occasion and Audience

The **[[sack-of-rome-410|sack of Rome]]** by Alaric in 410 drove pagan aristocratic refugees to
Carthage who blamed the disaster on the abandonment of the old gods. Augustine, convalescing after
his health failed in autumn 410 and prompted by the imperial commissioner Flavius Marcellinus and
by a literary debate with the young pagan noble **Volusianus**, answered them. His real target was
not popular cult but the literary, philosophical neo-paganism of aristocrats whose religion lived
in the classical texts (Vergil, Cicero, Macrobius) and in the Platonism of Plotinus and Porphyry —
"a paganism that existed only in libraries," which he chose to attack precisely because "he could
best reach the last pagans through their libraries." He cited Cicero some 119 times and treated
Porphyry as a worthier opponent than any Christian heretic.

## The Two Cities

Augustine's controlling idea, which he says he drew partly from the Donatist exegete **Tyconius**,
is that since the Fall the human race has been divided into two "cities" (*civitates*) — one
formed by love of God, the other by love of self and the world; one serving God and the loyal
angels, the other the Devil and the fallen. They are archetypally figured by Abel and Cain, and
Cain's founding of the first earthly city is paralleled by Romulus's fratricide of Remus in
founding Rome. The two cities are inextricably mixed in this life — within the church and the world
alike — and will be separated only at the Last Judgment. Rome's rise, driven by the "lust for
domination" and the "love of praise," is reduced to the universal law of empires, a "successful
brigandage" no different in kind from Assyria.

The key term for the Christian's stance toward the earthly city is *peregrinus* — resident alien,
pilgrim: intimate with and grateful for the goods of the earthly city, dependent on its order, yet
never finally identified with it. The book, Brown insists, is "about being otherworldly in the
world," not a summons to flight; its mellowed view of community and government reflects Augustine's
own long practice as a bishop.

## Significance

By detaching the fortunes of the church from the survival of the Roman state — Rome "punished, but
not replaced" — the *City of God* gave Latin Christendom a political theology that could outlast the
Western empire, and it did: the two-cities framework shaped medieval conceptions of the relation of
church and world for a thousand years, and the *peregrinus* remained a central category of Christian
political thought. It also stands as a monument of "Christian nationalism" in Brown's sense —
Scripture deliberately set up as a rival literary culture to the classical canon, "your Vergil"
juxtaposed at every turn with "our Scriptures."
