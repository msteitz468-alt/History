# The Growth of Administrative Posts (Equestrian and Freedman Government, AD 70–192)

The slow, undramatic expansion of the **non-senatorial administration** — the posts held by
**equestrians**, imperial **freedmen** (*liberti Augusti*) and **slaves** (*servi Caesaris*) —
between Vespasian and Commodus. Eck's chapter is the classic statement of the case **against** a
grand "Hadrianic reform": the administration grew by **differentiation, not by deliberate
overhaul**. Treated in [[bowman-cah-v11-2000|CAH XI]] Ch. 6 (**Werner Eck**); the financial
machinery analysed for the Julio-Claudians in [[imperial-finances]].

## The Augustan seed and four areas of work

Augustus had already created the non-senatorial offices — the *praefectus Aegypti*, the prefects
of the corn supply (*annona*) and the watch (*vigiles*), the financial procurators, and the
freedman/slave staff of the treasuries — but "only laid the foundations." By the start of
Vespasian's reign the administration entrusted to non-senators fell into **four areas**:

1. **Offices around the emperor** (*officia Palatina*): the *ab epistulis* (correspondence), *a
   rationibus* (central finance), *a libellis* (petitions), *a cognitionibus*, *a studiis*, *a
   memoria*, plus the **praetorian prefecture**.
2. **Posts of the city of Rome**: the *praefectus annonae*, *praefectus vigilum*, procurators of
   the gladiatorial schools, the water supply and public buildings.
3. **Posts for Rome and Italy**: procurators of the inheritance tax (*vicesima hereditatium*) and
   manumission tax (*vicesima libertatis*), the *patrimonium*, the *praefectus vehiculorum* (state
   courier system).
4. **Posts in Italy and the provinces**: the provincial **financial procurators**, the prefect of
   Egypt and his subordinates, the praesidial (governing) procurators of the lesser provinces.

Eck counts **about seventy** distinct administrative areas in existence at Vespasian's accession,
almost all concerned with the **finances** of empire and emperor.

## Expansion by differentiation, not reform

From Vespasian to Commodus the number of administrative departments grew to **at least c. 120**,
and the number of equestrian *procuratores* grew faster still. But Eck insists this was **not**
fundamental innovation:

- New financial procurators were created mechanically whenever a **province was annexed, divided,
  or upgraded** (Dacia, Arabia, the divisions of Pannonia and Moesia, Raetia/Noricum turned
  senatorial) — consequences of unrelated decisions, not administrative policy.
- Most new posts arose by **splitting off** part of an existing official's duties — a special
  procurator for the inheritance tax in one province, for a mining district, for the imperial
  *res privata* — which "involved little more than splitting off part of what had already existed."
- The supposed **Hadrianic reform** of equestrian administration rests almost entirely on
  late-antique notices (the *Historia Augusta*) that are "in many cases erroneous"; there is
  "hardly any evidence" for it. Posts evolved continuously, datable only by *terminus ante quem*.

## The equestrians displace the freedmen — but do not exclude them

The deepest change was the **replacement of freedmen by equestrians** at the head of the great
bureaux. Vitellius (AD 69) first appointed equestrians to *ministeria principatus*; **Domitian**
made the once-servile Palatine functions "more publicly acceptable as posts for free-born Roman
citizens" (Suetonius himself held *a studiis* and *a bybliothecis* under Trajan). By the end of
Trajan's reign all the leading posts around the emperor were equestrian. Yet freedmen were **not
excluded**: they continued as **subordinates** to equestrian procurators (an "unequal" or
"pseudo-collegiality"), Eck argues less as political watchdogs than as a system of **reciprocal
financial checks** and **continuity** (freedmen served longer). Below them the real clerical
**civil service** was the *familia Caesaris* — many thousands of imperial slaves and freedmen
whose decades of service gave them the **specialist knowledge** on which their equestrian
superiors depended, but who, barred by inheritance law from founding "freedman dynasties," left
the emperor's power over them unbroken.

## The equestrian career and its grades

A genuine **equestrian career** developed in the first and early second centuries — real but, Eck
stresses, "not as formally regularized as the senatorial *cursus*," giving the emperor freer rein.
After service as an equestrian officer, a man held a mix of military, fiscal, legal and general
posts; **general ability and experience, not specialist expertise**, governed advancement (no
"training of specialists"). Posts were ranked, and so paid, by **salary grade** — *sexagenarius,
centenarius, ducenarius*, and (from Marcus Aurelius) *trecenarius* (60,000–300,000 sesterces) —
the praetorian prefecture, prefecture of Egypt and *annona* standing above all. From about Marcus'
reign these grades acquired **honorific titles** mirroring the senatorial *vir clarissimus*: ***vir
egregius*** (ordinary procurators), ***vir perfectissimus*** (the great prefectures and top
Palatine posts), ***vir eminentissimus*** (the praetorian prefects alone). Unlike senatorial rank,
these titles were tied to **office**, not heredity, and so distinguished men by **service** rather
than birth.

## Significance and contest

Eck's chapter reframes the rise of the equestrian "bureaucracy" as a **gradual, organic
differentiation of a financial administration**, not a series of imperial reforms — and corrects
the older equestrian-centric narrative by insisting the development of the *areas of work* matters
more than which order eventually staffed them. The dating of individual posts, the reality of a
Hadrianic reform, and the precise count of departments and procuratorships all remain uncertain,
constrained by the epigraphic, *terminus-ante-quem* nature of the evidence.
