## Definition and Scope

The Islamic legal ethic refers to the distinctive moral-normative orientation that emerged within the first Muslim community during the first Islamic century (roughly 11/632–100/720 CE) — the period between Muhammad's death and the onset of systematic juristic activity. It is not yet a legal system. It is the *precondition* for one: the gradual internalization, by an expanding community of conquerors, converts, and their children, of Quranic values, the Prophet's exemplary conduct (*Sunna*), and a notion that Muslim life should be regulated by God's command rather than tribal custom alone.

The concept is primarily analytical rather than emic. Historical actors spoke of the *Quran*, *sunan*, *sira*, and *ray* (discretionary opinion) — not of a "legal ethic" as such. But the emergence of this ethic is, in Hallaq's account, the essential bridge between [[quranic-law]] (revealed legislation) and the mature apparatus of [[fiqh]] and [[sharia]] that came later.

## The Problem: Law Without Jurists

When Muhammad died in 11/632, he left behind "a small state and clear notions of justice, but with under-developed ideas of law and an even less developed judiciary." The early caliphate — Abu Bakr (11/632–13/634), Umar I (13/634–23/644), Uthman (23/644–35/656), Ali (35/656–40/661) — faced an enormous mismatch: the Quran commanded a distinct communal law, but the mechanisms for applying, interpreting, and extending that law did not yet exist.

The early *qadis* (judges) appointed to the garrison towns (*amsar*) — Kufa, Basra, Fustat, Damascus — were not trained jurists. Many were recruited from the ranks of pre-Islamic tribal arbitrators (*hukkam*), others were military commanders or administrators. Some were illiterate. Their rulings were often *ad hoc*: a property dispute resolved by asking the buyer whether he would have voided the sale had he found gold; a fraud case resolved by shaving the defendants' heads and beards. As Hallaq notes, "these solutions, dictated by practical considerations and ad hoc common sense, ran counter to the standard principles that evolved later on."

The proto-qadis' jurisdiction was also narrow: confined to the garrison towns and the Arab conqueror population, not extending to conquered non-Muslim communities, who "were deliberately left to govern themselves by their own denominational laws and canons."

## Phase 1: The Propagation of Quranic Values (632–ca. 660)

The primary vector of the emerging Islamic legal ethic was not judicial but didactic. Umar I recognized that booty alone could not maintain cohesion among the Bedouin armies, and that a shared religious identity had to be cultivated. His strategy:

- **Mosques in every garrison town**: The Friday prayer and sermon served "to imbue the Bedouins with religious values." The sermon included extensive Quranic passages and messages relevant to communal life.
- **Quran teachers** (*muallimun*): Deployed alongside military commanders, they propagated the Quranic worldview as the basis for communal identity.
- **Story-tellers** (*qussas*): A distinctive institution in which officials recounted Quranic narratives of ancient peoples, biblical figures, and the Prophet's biography. Many proto-qadis held the dual office of *qadi* and *qass*. Although initially some story-tellers were regarded as "little better than charlatans," those associated with the proto-qadis were generally pious men — the qadi Sulaym b. Itr, for example, "is characterized in the sources as a pious man who reportedly spent his nights reading the Quran."

The Quranic vulgate compiled under Uthman (ca. 35/656) was a crucial enabling step: it gave the legally-minded a *textus receptus* from which Quranic rules could be reliably extracted.

**What was applied**: At this stage, Quranic law was applied selectively — most consistently in areas where the Quran offered clear and detailed provisions (inheritance, marriage, ritual). In many other areas — including the prohibition on wine-drinking — enforcement was minimal or absent. The qadi Shurayh, portrayed as an archetypal judge of the first century, is reported to have publicly indulged in strong intoxicants without censure, revealing that "apart from certain highly regulated areas in the Quran (marriage, divorce, inheritance, etc.), there was little concern at the time for an Islamic system of legal morality."

**Sources of authority**: Proto-qadis drew on three sources — the Quran, *sunan* (models of good conduct, including those of the Prophet, the Companions, and earlier caliphs), and *ray* (discretionary opinion). These were not sharply distinguished. Sunan were "not legally binding narratives, but subjective notions of justice that were put to various uses and discursive strategies."

## Phase 2: The Growth of Religious Sentiment (ca. 660–710)

The generational shift after ca. 60/680 — when most of Muhammad's direct companions were dead — marks the decisive intensification of the Islamic legal ethic. The *subsequent* generation grew up under Quranic instruction from infancy, were regular mosque-goers, and had internalized the rudimentary religious morality as something self-evidently normative.

The contrast Hallaq draws is precise: in ca. 50s/670s, a qadi could drink wine in public without censure; by 89/707, qadi Imran b. Abd Allah al-Hasani of Fustat *resigned his post in protest* when the governor refused to allow him to enforce the Quranic penalty on a wine-drinker. "The change from an environment in which a qadi himself would indulge in drinking alcohol publicly to one in which another would resign a fairly lucrative post in protest against official interference with his attempts to punish the same behavior is indeed remarkable."

**The isolation of Prophetic Sunna**: Beginning in the 60s/680s, qadis and learned men began to transmit Prophetic biography as a *specialized oral genre* distinct from other sunan. Key markers:
- From ca. 66/685, Umayyad coins begin displaying the formula "The Messenger of God" (*Rasul Allah*) — the first numismatic attestation of Prophetic authority.
- The Dome of the Rock (72/691) bears the earliest inscription with this formula in architectural form.
- Non-Muslim sources (John bar Penkaye, writing 687/68H) depict the Prophet as a guide whose tradition the Arabs upheld "to such an extent that they inflicted the death penalty on anyone who was seen to act brazenly against his laws."

This specialization in Prophetic Sunna was epistemic and pedagogical simultaneously: epistemic because Prophetic discourse was increasingly understood as the indispensable hermeneutical key to the Quran; pedagogical because knowing and transmitting what the Prophet said or did required dedicated scholarly activity.

## The Three-Source Framework

By the late first/early second Islamic century, the sources of authority governing emerging Islamic law were crystallizing into three:

1. **The Quran** — supreme and non-negotiable. Even at the earliest stage, Quranic provisions in areas where clear rules existed (inheritance, marriage) were consistently applied.

2. ***Sunan*** — exemplary models of conduct. In the early period, these included the sunan of Abu Bakr, Umar I, Uthman, Ali, and other Companions, alongside an increasingly prominent Prophetic Sunna. Umar II (99/717–101/720) reportedly declared that qadis must be cognizant of the "rulings and sunan that came before them." The concept of *sunna* was itself pre-Islamic — any man of rectitude and charisma could establish a sunna — but its Islamization directed attention progressively toward the Prophet as its primary source.

3. ***Ray* (discretionary opinion)** — personal reasoning and judgment. The *ray* of recognized authorities — "the people of *ray*" (*ahl al-ray*) — constituted an authoritative source that overlapped with both sunan and, in embryonic form, with later *qiyas* (analogical reasoning) and *ijma* (consensus). Ijtihad was understood from the beginning as *ray* applied to *ilm* (knowledge of established norms).

What distinguished the formative period is that these three sources were not yet formally ranked or systematized. The Prophetic Sunna had not yet achieved its later status as the exclusive second source after the Quran; caliphal sunan were still regularly cited alongside Prophetic ones; and *ray* was not yet stigmatized as subjective deviation from authoritative text. The systematization of sources — *usul al-fiqh* — lay a century or more in the future.

## The Role of the Caliphate

Caliphs were not independent legislators but "integrally dependent on prior exemplary conduct and precedent." Hallaq's reading of the early evidence is that caliphal authority derived from their role as exemplary upholders of Quranic truth, not from a legislative office of their own. When asked about a case for which no precedent could be found, Umar II told his qadi to use his own *ray* — showing that the caliph, "with all his authority and might, first looked for precedent." Those caliphs enjoying the highest legal authority were specifically those seen as closest to the Prophet's example: Abu Bakr, Umar I, Uthman, and Umar II.

## Conclusions

The Islamic legal ethic was not the mechanical application of Quranic rules but the slow, multigenerational socialization of a new community into an Islamic normative order. It was propagated through mosques, sermons, story-telling, and the institution of the proto-qadi simultaneously. The crucial transition — from a community in which Quranic prohibitions were widely ignored to one in which their violation scandalized even low-ranking officials — took approximately one generation (ca. 650–710 CE). This transformation is the *prerequisite* for understanding why, by the late first/early second century AH, specialized juristic circles could begin the systematic elaboration of [[sharia]] from Quranic and Sunnaic materials.

## Historiography

Hallaq's account challenges two earlier orthodoxies: (1) Schacht's thesis (*Origins of Muhammadan Jurisprudence*, 1950) that Islamic law was substantially formed by the assimilation of provincial Roman practice, and (2) the revisionist thesis (Wansbrough, Crone) that the Quran's legal authority was itself a late construction. Hallaq argues that the evidence for a Quranic legal identity is present from the late Medinan period and that the formative development of the legal ethic was internal and primarily driven by Quranic socialization rather than by external borrowing. The debate on these questions remains open; see [[islamic-civilization-historiography]].
