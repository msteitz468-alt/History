# The ʿApiru / Habiru

A widespread Bronze Age term — **ʿApiru** (Ugaritic *ʿpr*), written logographically
**SA.GAZ**, formerly read *Habiru* — for a class of **stateless, marginal, often
lawless people** found from the Dynasty of Agade to the 11th century BCE across
Mesopotamia, Syria, Anatolia, Egypt and Canaan. They are conspicuous in the
[[amarna-letters|Amarna letters]], where they raid and destabilize the Egyptian
vassal-states of Palestine. The possible link between *ʿApiru* and Hebrew *ʿibrî*
("Hebrew") makes the term one of the most argued-over problems in Near Eastern
history. Treated in [[edwards-cah-v2p2-1975|CAH Vol. II, Part 2]] (Ch. XX, Albright).

## Not an ethnos but a social class

The volume's central point: the ʿApiru were **not a people** but a **social
category** — "heterogeneous ethnic origin," speaking "different languages, often
alien to the people in whose documents they appear." The logogram SA.GAZ equates
with Akkadian *ḫabbātu*, which Albright argues meant originally not "bandit" but
"**tramp, wanderer, roving agricultural worker, donkey-driver**" (from *ḫabātu*, "to
tramp, rove, cross over"), the sense "robber" being secondary. Neo-Sumerian texts
(the Lipit-Ishtar code, the "Curse of Agade," a Shulgi composition) show SA.GAZ
people as smugglers, tax-evaders and out-of-work caravaneers — people who "go where
they please" and "do not obey the laws." The word ʿApiru itself, Albright proposed,
means "**dusty one**" (one who trudges in the dust behind donkeys and chariots),
paralleled by the medieval pedlars' name *pies poudres* / "pie-powders."

## In the Amarna age

In the [[amarna-letters|Amarna letters]] the ʿApiru are the foes of princes and
Egyptian officials alike — "each prince accuses his enemies of being in league with
the ʿApiru." They are carefully distinguished from the **Sutu/Shasu** (true
bedawin): both are donkey-nomads, but the ʿApiru are "less nomadic." A triumphal
list of [[unknown|Amenophis II]] counts 3,600 ʿApiru among captives alongside Shasu,
Hurrians and Canaanite merchants. The career of **Labaya of Shechem**, the
ʿApiru-allied dynast who "gave the land of Shechem to the ʿApiru," is the one such
life the letters let us follow.

## The Hebrew question

Albright (writing here at the height of mid-century biblical archaeology) connects
the ʿApiru to the **donkey-caravan background of the Patriarchs** and identifies the
terms *ʿibrî* "Hebrew" with *ʿApiru* — "person from across or beyond" — and links
the Amarna Shechem episode (cautiously) to **Genesis 34**, holding that "the Hebrews
of central Palestine gained the upper hand in Shechem about this time." This is the
**contested core** of the page: that the ʿApiru phenomenon is part of the
prehistory of Israel.

## Status of the debate

- **Consensus:** ʿApiru = a trans-ethnic **social/economic class** of the stateless
  and displaced, *not* an ethnic group — this is secure and is the volume's own
  position.
- **Disputed:** the **ʿApiru → Hebrew** equation. The names are philologically
  relatable, and *some* ʿApiru may have been absorbed into emergent Israel, but the
  simple identification "ʿApiru = Israelites" is rejected by later scholarship: most
  ʿApiru were not Israelite and Israel was not simply ʿApiru. The volume's confident
  linkage reflects 1960s scholarship and should be read as **historiographically
  dated** (compare the cautious treatment of the [[unknown|Exodus]] in Section 4).

## Significance

The ʿApiru are a window onto the **social underside of the Bronze Age** — the
mobile, marginal, and dispossessed who shadow the palace economies — and a central
exhibit in the debate over **Israelite origins** and the historical value of the
biblical Patriarchal and Conquest traditions.
