# Hacienda System

The hacienda was the large landed estate that became the dominant rural institution of
Spanish America from the seventeenth century onward: a unit of production combining
agriculture and stock-raising, a labor regime, and a structure of social domination over a
resident workforce. In the Andes it grew at the expense of indigenous community
([[ayllu]]) lands, absorbing peasants as resident laborers (*colonos*, *yanaconas*,
*huasipungueros*) bound by [[debt-peonage|debt]], usufruct plots, and obligatory service.

## Major Features

- **Land concentration** — accumulation of community and crown land into private estates,
  accelerating with nineteenth-century liberal land privatization
  ([[andean-peasants-nation-making-19th-century]]).
- **Servile labor** — resident peons owed labor rents and personal service in exchange for
  access to subsistence plots; a regime of paternalist domination.
- **Persistence** — the hacienda survived independence and shaped rural society until the
  twentieth-century agrarian reforms ([[agrarian-reform-bolivia-1953]], the Peruvian reform
  of 1969) dismantled it.

## Limitations and Critique

Scholars debate whether the hacienda was primarily a profit-seeking enterprise, a
prestige-and-power holding, or a defensive response to labor scarcity, and how "feudal" its
labor relations really were. Its relationship to indigenous community lands — predator,
symbiont, or both — varied sharply by region.
