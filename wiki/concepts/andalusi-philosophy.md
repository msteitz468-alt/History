## Definition and Scope

Andalusi philosophy designates the tradition of rational inquiry conducted in [[al-andalus|al-Andalus]] (Muslim Iberia) from approximately the eleventh through the fourteenth centuries CE, encompassing Muslim, Jewish, and to a lesser degree Christian participants. It is a regional sub-tradition of the broader [[islamic-philosophy|Islamic philosophical]] world, but sufficiently distinctive in character, personnel, and intellectual preoccupations to warrant treatment as a coherent tradition in its own right.

The tradition is bounded geographically by the Iberian Peninsula and historically by the entry of significant philosophical activity under the taifa courts (post-1031) and its effective conclusion with the Christian reconquest and the dispersal of intellectual communities. Its defining features — a relative independence from eastern Avicennism, the centrality of Aristotelian commentary, a distinctive Jewish-Muslim intellectual exchange, and the political pressure of successive North African dynasties — mark it off from the Persianate philosophical world centered on [[ibn-sina|Avicenna]] that dominated the east.

## Causal Drivers

Several structural factors produced a distinctive Andalusi philosophy:

**Political fragmentation and court patronage.** The collapse of the western Umayyad caliphate in 1031 produced the *taifa* kingdoms — "faction" states based on individual cities. Regional princes competed for prestige by gathering scholars. Medicine especially flourished under taifa patronage (Adamson, ch. 22).

**Geographical remoteness from the east.** [[ibn-tufayl|Ibn Tufayl]] himself admitted that Avicennan texts were incompletely available in Andalusia. This insulation meant that [[al-farabi|al-Farabi's]] strict Aristotelianism — already receding in influence in Baghdad — retained paradigmatic authority in Spain long after the eastern tradition had moved to Avicennan synthesis.

**The convivencia.** For most of the period before the Almohad conquest, Muslims, Jews, and Christians lived and worked in proximity. Jewish thinkers wrote in Arabic; translations moved freely across communities. The convivencia enabled the Jewish appropriation of Muslim philosophical methods (notably al-Farabi and later Averroes) and the Sufi influence on Jewish Kabbalah (Adamson, ch. 22: "Andalusian philosophy in its entirety as a tradition within the larger tradition of philosophy in the Islamic world").

**The Almohad disruption.** The arrival of the [[almohads|Almohads]] (fully dominant from c. 1170) imposed a new Berber religious fundamentalism. Jewish communities were coerced or exiled, ending the convivencia. Yet the Almohad context paradoxically shaped major philosophical production: [[ibn-tufayl|Ibn Tufayl]] served two Almohad caliphs, and his *Hayy ibn Yaqzan* reflects Almohad ideology of natural religiosity. Averroes ([[ibn-rushd|Ibn Rushd]]) was likewise a product of the Almohad court, though ultimately fell out of favour.

## Major Figures and Sequence

The tradition can be read as three overlapping generations of Muslim philosophical activity, with parallel Jewish development:

**Ibn Hazm** (d. 1064): Jurist, literary author, logician. First significant Andalusi intellectual engaging both legal theory (Zahirism) and philosophical logic. Influenced by the Baghdad Peripatetic school indirectly.

**[[ibn-bajja|Ibn Bajja]]** (d. c. 1138): First systematic Aristotelian in the western Islamic world; author of the *Rule of the Solitary*. Drew on al-Farabi, shaped the theory of the intellect later elaborated by Averroes.

**[[ibn-tufayl|Ibn Tufayl]]** (d. 1185): Court physician to Almohad caliphs; author of *Hayy ibn Yaqzan*. Represents a Avicennan-mystical strand — the "chorizo in the Peripatetic paella" of Andalusi philosophy (Adamson, ch. 24). Introduced Averroes to the Almohad court.

**[[ibn-rushd|Averroes]] (Ibn Rushd)** (d. 1198): Chief judge of Cordoba; author of major Aristotelian commentaries, the *Decisive Treatise* on philosophy and law, and the *Incoherence of the Incoherence*. Called "the Commentator" in Latin Christendom.

**[[ibn-arabi|Ibn Arabi]]** (d. 1240): Born in Murcia, departed Andalusia at 30 for the east; the "Greatest Master" of Islamic Sufism. His mystical philosophy influenced Jewish Kabbalah.

**[[ibn-khaldun|Ibn Khaldun]]** (d. 1406): Historian and social philosopher; developed the theory of *asabiyya* (group solidarity) as the engine of dynastic cycles. Active in the Maghreb and Cairo.

## Characteristic Features

**Strict Aristotelianism over Avicennism.** Jewish and Muslim thinkers alike turned more to al-Farabi and Averroes than to Avicenna. Averroes' commentaries were translated into Hebrew and became primary texts for Jewish thinkers after Andalusian communities dispersed to southern France.

**Fusion of legal and philosophical training.** Ibn Hazm, Averroes, and Ibn Khaldun all held legal positions alongside philosophical activity. The genre of *usul al-fiqh* (legal epistemology) and the genre of philosophical commentary cross-fertilized.

**Translation into Latin.** Toledo served as the primary locus for Arabic-to-Latin translation. Scholars including Gerard of Cremona and Dominicus Gundissalinus produced Latin versions of Aristotle, al-Kindi, al-Farabi, Avicenna, and Averroes — fundamentally reshaping Latin scholasticism.

**Sufism and Kabbalah.** Ibn Arabi's teaching influenced the early development of Jewish Kabbalah in southern France. The parallel transmission of Aristotelian philosophy (Muslim → Jewish) and mysticism (Sufi → Kabbalistic) both originated in Andalusia.

## Historiography

Andalusi philosophy has often been treated as a mere conduit between the Islamic east and Latin Christendom — a way-station through which Aristotle travelled on his route to Thomas Aquinas. More recent scholarship, represented by Adamson (2016), insists on Andalusia as a philosophical world with its own integrity: its own debates (Avicennism vs. Farabism; reason vs. mystical vision), its own social structures (convivencia, taifa court patronage, Almohad pressure), and its own transmission patterns (Arabic → Hebrew rather than solely Arabic → Latin).

The figure of Averroes in particular has been mythologized. He was not the rescuer of philosophy from al-Ghazali, since in the Arabic-speaking east he had no readership after a few generations. His enormous influence operated almost entirely through Latin and Hebrew translation. The Andalusi tradition as a whole was, in Adamson's phrase, "a world unto itself."

## Collection Coverage Note

The Bibliotheca Alexandrina holds Peter Adamson's *Philosophy in the Islamic World* (2016), which provides the primary synthetic treatment of this tradition. Individual source texts — Averroes' commentaries, Ibn Tufayl's *Hayy ibn Yaqzan*, Ibn Khaldun's *Muqaddima* — are likely present in specialist translation series. Coverage of the Jewish dimension (Maimonides, Ibn Gabirol) and the Sufi transmission to Kabbalah would benefit from dedicated source ingests.
