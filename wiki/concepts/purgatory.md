# Purgatory

## Definition

**Purgatory**, in high and late medieval Latin Christianity, is the state or place of **purification after death** for souls destined for heaven but not yet perfect — "heaven's antechamber" in [[logan-history-of-the-church-in-the-middle-ages-2013|Logan]]'s phrase. It underwrote masses for the dead, indulgences, and a vast economy of intercession.

## Emic vs Etic

- **Emic:** defined by scholastic theology and popular practice as real purifying fire or process; suffrages of the living aid the dead.
- **Etic:** historians treat the "birth of purgatory" (Le Goff and critics) as a medieval doctrinal and social construction with earlier roots, not a simple apostolic given.

## Limitations and Contests

Orthodox Christianity did not adopt the same systematised purgatory; Protestant reformers rejected it. Within medieval studies, dating and social function remain debated.

## From F. Donald Logan, *A History of the Church in the Middle Ages* (2013)

### Centrality in late medieval life

Logan holds it nearly impossible to exaggerate purgatory’s significance: believed as firmly as the Eucharist, divinity of Christ, and Trinity (except heretics: Waldensians, Cathars, later [[wyclif-john|John Wyclif]]); role nearly as large as Eucharist and Virgin in daily devotion; no parish exempt from related practices. Death-focused attention of famine/plague decades met an almost universal afterlife belief including purgatory as heaven’s antechamber shortened by the living’s prayers and good works.

### Papal formulations

Innocent IV (1254 letter): souls dying repented but penance incomplete, or with only venial sins, are purged and helped by church suffrages. Clement V (document associated with Lyons II 1274) said virtually the same. [[boniface-viii|Boniface VIII]]’s 1300 Holy Year plenary indulgence extended to those who died en route to Rome after due confession—remitting purgatorial punishment for incomplete penance. Doctrine firmly established by late thirteenth century; Dante composed *Il purgatorio* in following decades.

Innocent IV (Logan quote): “The souls who died having repented but not having fulfilled their penance or who die with only venial but not mortal sins on their souls are purged and can be helped by the suffrages of the church.”

### Long development

Ambiguity between death and Last Judgement generated purgatory; early prayers for the dead imply an intermediate state. Perpetua’s 202 vision of helping a dead brother by prayer became a medieval preaching staple. Augustine (*City of God* 21) affirmed purgatorial fire between death and Last Judgement. Gregory the Great described upper hell (exit to heaven) vs lower hell (no exit). Bede’s Dryhthelm vision (HE 5.12) offered four places—heaven, hell, and two intermediate zones later conflated into purgatory—with living help via prayers, alms, Masses.

Late twelfth-century **St Patrick’s Purgatory** (Station Island, Lough Derg) circulated in Latin and nearly every vernacular. Dante’s *Il purgatorio* (completed 1319) contrasts hell’s hopelessness with purgatory’s hope—a seven-storey mountain above ground purging the seven deadly sins.

### Scholastic summary

Peter Lombard’s *Sentences* (~1157) set the agenda; William of Auvergne, Alexander of Hales, Bonaventure, Albertus Magnus, Aquinas developed details. Summary: particular judgement → heaven / hell / purgatory; stay length by unrepented venial sins and unfulfilled expiation; shortened by living’s prayers, Masses, alms; at Last Judgement remaining purgatory souls enter heaven; hell/heaven eternal.

### Social infrastructure: chantries and confraternities

From at least the seventh century, living help for the dead shaped church life; All Souls (2 Nov) universalized. From the thirteenth century: family prayers, anniversary Masses; wealthy founded **chantries**—Cambridgeshire 66 perpetual chantries fourteenth–fifteenth centuries; 1438 Canterbury archbishop founded an Oxford college for souls; 1547 English dissolution: 250+ London chantries (44 in St Paul’s), ~3,000 national. Guilds and lay confraternities (Florence ~100; English 1389 returns: Norfolk 164, Lincolnshire 123, Yorkshire 162) invariably prayed and Massed for deceased members. Commemoration of purgatory souls ranked with Eucharist and Virgin as principal late medieval devotion.

### Indulgences and Treasury of Merits

Indulgences rest on twofold consequence of sin: guilt (removed by contrition/confession) and temporal punishment (satisfied in life or purgatory). Clement VI (1343) expressed Treasury of Merits doctrine. Boniface’s 1300 jubilee was plenary. Initially only for oneself; **Sixtus IV (1476)** allowed application to purgatory souls by way of suffrage—prelude to later Lutheran critique (beyond Logan’s limits).

### Wyclif’s denial; Florence and the East

Among [[wyclif-john|Wyclif]]’s heterodoxies Logan lists denial of purgatory alongside residual-substance eucharistic teaching. At the [[council-of-florence-1439|Council of Florence]], Eastern theologians on intermediate afterlife “did not speak with one voice” but tended to reject a state of fire and fix final disposition only at Last Judgement; they **yielded** to the Latin view: immediate heaven/hell for good/wicked and a third place of purgation aided by prayers and suffrages of the living.
