# Evolution by Natural Selection

**Evolution** is descent with modification: new species arise from preexisting
species through the cumulative inheritance of variation over time. **Natural
selection** is the mechanism Charles Darwin and Alfred Russel Wallace proposed
to explain it. Its announcement in 1858–59 ranks with the Copernican and
Newtonian revolutions as a turning point in the history of human knowledge —
Copernicus and Newton had brought order and mechanism to the physical universe;
Darwin and Wallace extended both to the living world, which had been held to be
the product of special creation of immutable species.

## The Discovery (1858–1859)

The idea of descent with modification was not new — Lamarck, Buffon, and Erasmus
Darwin had all suggested it — but it was resisted while [[concepts/deep-time|deep
time]] was unrecognized and catastrophist special creation held sway. Darwin had
formulated natural selection by 1838 (crediting a reading of Malthus's *Essay on
Population* for the insight on the struggle for existence) and spent two decades
quietly amassing evidence. In February 1858 **Wallace**, feverish at Ternate in
the Moluccas, independently arrived at the same idea — also via Malthus — and
mailed Darwin a summary. To resolve the priority crisis, Lyell and Hooker
arranged a **joint paper read to the Linnean Society on 1 July 1858**, "On the
tendency of species to form varieties." It was calmly received. The following
year Darwin published *On the Origin of Species by Means of Natural Selection*
(24 November 1859); the first edition of 1,250 copies sold out the day it
appeared, provoking immediate and lasting public, religious, and scientific
controversy.

Darwin's importance lay not in proposing evolution but in **convincing** the
scientific world it had occurred, by marshalling overwhelming detailed evidence
for a credible mechanism.

## The Argument

The Darwin–Wallace argument rests on two observations and an inference:

1. **Overproduction.** Organisms produce far more offspring than can survive, yet
   population sizes stay roughly constant.
2. **Heritable variation.** Individuals within a species vary, and much variation
   is inherited (the genetic basis was unknown to Darwin; later genetics supplied
   it).
3. **Inference — natural selection.** Better-adapted variants leave relatively
   more offspring, so reproduction is non-random and advantageous traits
   accumulate. The result is not "nature red in tooth and claw" so much as
   differential survival and reproduction shifting a population's gene pool.

## Evidence in Action

Natural selection, once a hypothesis, is now demonstrated directly:

- **Darwin's finches.** Fifteen Galápagos species, identified by John Gould in
  1837, diverged in beak form by diet from a mainland ancestor; the Grants'
  decades of fieldwork on Daphne Major have observed selection and incipient
  speciation in real time.
- **The peppered moth.** Kettlewell's work on *Biston betularia* showed the dark
  *carbonaria* form rising to ~99% in polluted industrial areas (where dark bark
  hid it from predators) and receding again as pollution fell — selection
  observed within a century.
- **Land snails, antibiotic resistance**, and others extend the demonstration.

In the **fossil record**, lineages such as the horses (*Hyracotherium* to modern
*Equus* over ~70 Ma), cave bears, sea urchins (*Micraster*), ammonoids, and
foraminifera show descent with modification and selection at work. The pattern of
slow change or stasis punctuated by rapid bursts was named **punctuated
equilibrium** by Gould and Eldredge; debate over whether it or gradual change
better describes the record resolves, in practice, to "both occur."

## Significance and Limits

Evolution is treated by the scientific consensus as a **fact** (descent with
modification is observed); natural selection is its principal, though not sole,
mechanism. The theory required and reinforced [[concepts/deep-time|deep time]]
and gave coherence to [[concepts/mass-extinction|extinction]], biogeography, and
classification. Its reception was a major event in 19th-century intellectual and
religious history, and resistance persists (Rhodes notes ~45% of the U.S.
public reported not accepting human evolution). The fossil record is, by its
nature, an incomplete sample — gaps of non-preservation, not of non-existence —
so it can never furnish a continuous frame-by-frame film of descent; what it does
provide consistently endorses the theory.
