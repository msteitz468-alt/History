# The General Crisis of the Seventeenth Century

The **"general crisis of the seventeenth century"** is the thesis that the **expansion of the "long
sixteenth century" was followed, around 1620–1680, by a broad and roughly synchronous crisis** across
much of Eurasia — economic downturn, demographic decline, political convulsion, and the collapse or
contraction of states. Anthony Reid ([[reid-southeast-asia-age-of-commerce-v2-1993|*Age of Commerce*]]
Vol. 2, Ch. V) makes the case that **Southeast Asia belongs in this debate**, and that the crisis hit the
region especially hard and especially permanently.

## The evidence for a worldwide downturn

Reid assembles the global coincidence: **Japan** closed itself (the *sakoku* edicts forbidding overseas
sailing, 1635; the ban on silver export, 1668); **China** suffered famine, population decline, and the
collapse of the **Ming** dynasty (1644) and its replacement by the Qing; **England, France, Germany,
Spain, and Turkey** were convulsed by civil wars in 1620–50, the last three losing their former status;
and population declined across China, France, Holland, Denmark, Germany, Italy, and Spain in the second
half of the century. **Prices**, after a century of inflation, generally fell from c. 1640. Two
**measurable** common factors stand out: (1) a marked **decline in global trade** in 1620–50 (Seville,
Baltic, Venetian, and Leiden indices all turning down), caused partly by (2) a **contraction of the
silver and gold supply** — the collapse of **Potosí** output after 1630 and the fall, then prohibition,
of **Japanese silver** exports — that drained the "lubricating" bullion from the world economy. Behind
both, an increasing body of data on **global climatic cooling** (the **Little Ice Age**) points to an
agrarian-ecological dimension.

## The historiographical debate

The crisis is one of the great running debates of early-modern history, and Reid surveys it:
- **Eric Hobsbawm** (1954) — a Marxist reading: the crisis as the birth-pangs of capitalism bursting "the
  chrysalis of the old feudal order."
- **Hugh Trevor-Roper** (1959) — a political reading: the top-heavy **"Renaissance state"** with its
  swollen court, army, bureaucracy, and taxation provoked revolt. (Reid finds this "interesting" for
  Siam, Aceh, and Banten — the [[southeast-asian-absolutism|over-extended absolutisms]].)
- **Jack Goldstone** (1988) — a demographic reading: population pressure on limited grain pushed prices up
  in the sixteenth century and down in the seventeenth (argued for England, Turkey, China alike).
- The **climate** historians — the Little Ice Age as a global agrarian trigger (later synthesised by
  Geoffrey Parker, *Global Crisis*).

Whether these amount to a single connected "general crisis" or a coincidence of regional crises remains
contested.

## Reid's Southeast Asian case

Reid argues the downturn struck Southeast Asia **harder than most regions** because it had benefited most
from the boom: in the 1620s the region's pepper and spices made up **more than half** the value of
European homeward cargoes from Asia, and most China–Japan trade passed through its ports. When **Japan
stopped coming (1635)**, **Chinese** demand collapsed in the 1640s Ming crisis, and the silver supply
contracted, the [[southeast-asian-port-polity|trading cities]] lost their commercial lifeblood. But —
crucially — the region's **favourable ecology** (abundant food, low population density) meant the crisis
showed itself **less in mass population death than in a "retreat from the world economy."** The decisive
agent was the [[dutch-east-india-company|VOC]], "the undoubted winner of the seventeenth-century crisis
in Asia," whose monopoly violence (Banda 1621; the destruction of the Javanese ports; the 1677 treaty
that gave it a stranglehold over Mataram) ensured that in Southeast Asia the crisis was **far more
permanent than in Europe**: Europe's intercontinental trade revived in the eighteenth century, while
Asia's — and especially Southeast Asia's — kept declining. Reid pins the **turning point at 1629** (the
failed assaults on Melaka and Batavia; the trade indices turning down c. 1630), with the "death throes"
of the age of commerce running to the **1680s**. See [[southeast-asian-age-of-commerce]] for the regional
narrative and the "[[southeast-asian-age-of-commerce|origins of poverty]]" thesis it grounds.

## Significance and critiques

Placing Southeast Asia in the general-crisis debate is one of Reid's most influential moves, integrating
the region into **global early-modern history** rather than treating it as a backwater awaiting
colonisation. The chief critique — from **Victor Lieberman** — is that the **mainland** states (Burma,
Siam, Vietnam) do *not* fit a mid-century collapse: they were consolidating territorially and
administratively across exactly this period (*Strange Parallels*), so a single "crisis" overstates the
unity of the region and overweights the maritime/archipelago experience. The broader "general crisis"
itself remains debated — its reality, its synchronicity, and the relative weight of trade, bullion,
climate, and politics.
