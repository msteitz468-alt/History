# The Chinggis Exchange

The **Chinggis Exchange** is [[may-mongol-conquests-2012|Timothy May's]] term for the
vast, often unintended **transfer of goods, peoples, techniques, ideas, religions, and
disease** across Eurasia that the [[mongol-empire|Mongol Empire]] set in motion —
explicitly modelled on Alfred Crosby's **[[columbian-exchange-1492|Columbian
Exchange]]**, of which May argues it was the *precursor and enabler*. Just as Columbus's
landfall fused the biota and cultures of two hemispheres, the conquests of
[[genghis-khan|Chinggis Khan]] "caused a perceivable shift in technology, ideas,
culture, religion, warfare and many other areas" across the Old World. It is the
analytical core of May's claim that the Mongol moment is "the dividing point between the
pre-modern and the modern ages."

## Definition and scope

The Chinggis Exchange is *not* the simple claim that the Mongols caused everything
afterward — May opens his book by satirizing the "Mongol mania" that would trace the
Bolshevik Revolution, the Cold War, and even disco back to Chinggis Khan. The Exchange
is the set of transfers **directly linked to, or arising as a ramification of, Mongol
activity**, in which the Mongols were typically *facilitators* (securing routes,
moving populations, patronizing talent) rather than sole agents — "most of the Chinggis
Exchange was the result of the efforts of the subjects of the Mongols and outsiders."
Its precondition was the [[pax-mongolica|Pax Mongolica]]: the security and connectivity
of the largest contiguous empire in history.

## What was exchanged

- **Trade and wealth.** Secure routes, the *yam* postal relay, and *ortagh* merchant
  partnerships turned long-distance bulk trade into "an economically sound
  proposition" (Allsen); a single silver stratum and a flood of luxury goods moved
  from England to China (see [[pax-mongolica]], [[silk-roads]]).
- **Military technology.** The westward diffusion of **gunpowder** and gunpowder
  weapons, the counterweight trebuchet, and new siege and cavalry methods (the
  Sultanate of [[delhi-sultanate|Delhi]] adopting gunpowder by c. 1290; debated Mongol
  use on the western campaigns).
- **Administration.** A portable statecraft — the *yam*, decimal organization, the
  census, the *ortagh* — copied by successor and rival states.
- **Religion.** The spread of world religions under Mongol toleration and patronage:
  the Islamization of three of the four khanates, the spread of **Tibetan Buddhism**
  at the Yuan court, and the European missions (Carpini, Rubruck) that followed the
  conquests.
- **Disease.** The **[[black-death|Black Death]]** — bubonic plague carried west along
  the trade routes out of the central-Eurasian steppe, reaching **Kaffa** (1346) and
  thence the Mediterranean: "the most deadly result of the Mongol Empire."
- **Peoples.** Mass deportation of artisans, the **Turkicization** of Anatolia, Iran,
  and the western steppe, and the Tartar slave trade into Italy.
- **Art, food, and material culture.** The fusion of Chinese, Persian, Byzantine, and
  Italian styles in Ilkhanid painting (Rashid al-Din's workshops) and Yuan art; the
  spread of **blue-and-white porcelain** (Chinese clay + Iranian cobalt — possible only
  through the Exchange); the westward movement of the **printing press** (Korean
  movable metal type c. 1234, paper money in the Ilkhanate 1294, a plausible if
  undocumented line to Gutenberg); noodles, chickpeas, and hard wheat; and the *nasij*
  gold-brocade fashion ("Tartar cloth") that swept European courts.

## Significance and limitations

The Chinggis Exchange reframes the Mongols from a purely destructive "Tatar Yoke" into
the great **integrating force of Afro-Eurasia** — the Old-World counterpart of the
Columbian Exchange and, in May's argument, its precondition ("the Columbian Exchange is
in many ways a carry-over of the Chinggis Exchange"). Its chief limitation is the
attribution problem May himself foregrounds: many transfers (Turkic migration into
Anatolia, the spread of gunpowder) were *underway* before the Mongols and were
*accelerated* rather than *caused* by them, and the empire "did not have a huge impact
on Africa or the New World." The concept works best as a frame for **Eurasian
integration**, weakest when stretched into a single-cause theory of modernity.
