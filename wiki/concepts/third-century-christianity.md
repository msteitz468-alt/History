# Third-Century Christianity

The state of the Christian churches in the century before [[constantine-the-great|Constantine]] —
their geographical spread, social composition, internal diversity, and their relations with the Roman
state. Treated in [[bowman-cah-v12-2005|CAH XII]] Ch. 18 (**18a, Christianity 70–192**, by **Mark
Edwards**; **18b, Third-Century Christianity**, by **Graeme Clarke**). Clarke's organizing caution: the
**possibility of persecution was *not* "the obsessively dominant feature" of Christian life** that later,
martyr-venerating tradition made it.

## The diversity of the early church (Edwards, 70–192)

Edwards refuses a single "history of the church": **"the structure of the churches differed widely from
place to place, and what was plausible speculation in one city might be heresy in another."** His
region-by-region survey of 70–192 shows a **plural, contested Christianity** — "Jewish-Christian"
Ebionites, encratites (Tatian's *Diatessaron*), Marcion's dualism (a *good* Father against the *just*
Creator), the many "Gnostic" systems (Basilides, Valentinus), and the prophetic ecstasy of **Montanism**
in Phrygia — against which figures like **Irenaeus** of Lyons defined orthodoxy by appeal to the
**fourfold gospel** and the **consensus of the bishoprics**. Edwards' key structural claim: the death of
Commodus (192) marks the watershine after which "a history of 'the church' becomes conceivable," because
the **monarchical bishop** had become the linchpin — "the survival of Christianity depended on the
presence of the whole in every part," and wherever the episcopate was weak "there is no continuous story
to be told."

## Geographical spread: a growing but uneven minority (Clarke)

Clarke's first section maps the third-century spread, stressing how **fitful the evidence** is until the
Great Persecution and its aftermath suddenly illuminate it (the councils, the *Onomasticon*, the
inscriptions):

- **Strong and well-organized** in the great hellenized cities and their hinterlands — Rome, Carthage,
  Alexandria, Antioch — and across **North Africa** and **Asia Minor** (Phrygian gravestones suggest
  Christians at 20–30%+ of the population in some districts).
- **Thin or marginal** in the West (Britain "a marginal exotic religion"; the Germanies, Spain and much of
  Gaul weakly attested before mid-century) and **erratic in the countryside** everywhere.
- **Beyond the frontiers**: already established east of the Euphrates (Edessa, and via Bardaisan in
  Parthia/Persia/the Kushans) before Rome arrived — Christianity's boundaries were "by no means
  coterminous with the Roman frontiers."

The growth was real but **gradual**, constrained by Christianity's demanding entry (not a simple cult-act
but a **catechumenate** — lengthy doctrinal instruction and moral testing). Clarke's vivid snapshots: Pope
**Cornelius'** Roman clergy (c. 251) — 46 presbyters, 7 deacons, 52 exorcists/readers, "above 1500 widows
and persons in distress"; the **Cirta** church inventory (303) with its codices, plate, and stores of
clothing for charity; the African councils of 70–90 bishops; and the **Council of Elvira's** 81 canons,
revealing a "remarkable permeation of Christians into provincial society at all levels" — landowners,
magistrates, even holders of the imperial-cult **flaminate**.

## Christians and the state: persecution from below

Clarke's second theme overturns the lurid traditional picture. For most of the period **"persecution" was
sporadic, local, and driven by pressure from below** — popular agitation (often amid earthquake, plague,
famine) or occasional Christian provocation — not by imperial initiative; "the official attitude was
passive until activated." Christians were *ipso facto* on the wrong side of the law (Trajan's rescript to
Pliny; **Ulpian's** register of rescripts), but **arrest did not inevitably mean death**: the frequency of
released **confessors** (versus perfected martyrs) reflects governors' wide discretion and the standard
pattern of imprisonment-with-pressure-to-recant. The exclusivity of Christian worship was the
"sticking-point" (Perpetua: *"I am a Christian"*); willingness to **sacrifice** would secure release. Only
with the empire-wide edicts of **Decius (250)**, **Valerian (257–60)**, and finally the
**[[great-persecution-303|Great Persecution]] (303–11)** did persecution become **systematic and
imperially directed** — and even there Clarke (with the deflation of Eusebius' generalizations) insists on
distinguishing the rhetoric from the patchier reality. The **"toleration" of Gallienus** (260) gave the
church a generation of peace in which to consolidate.

## Significance

This chapter supplies the social-historical underpinning for the wiki's account of
[[christianization-of-the-roman-empire|Christianization]]. Its message is **anti-triumphalist and
anti-catastrophist at once**: by 300 Christianity was a **visible, well-organized, but still
minority** presence — strong in the eastern and African cities, thin in the West and the countryside,
internally diverse, and living not under constant martyrdom but under an intermittent "background of
insecurity." That a Constantine could emerge "from a family environment displaying some tolerance toward
Christianity" was "not totally unpredictable" — but neither were the probabilities high. The fourth
century's rapid emergence of Christians in official ranks shows how defective the third-century record is.
