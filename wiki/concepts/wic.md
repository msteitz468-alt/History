# Wic (Emporium)

**Wic** (plural *wics*; also *vicus*, *emporion*) is the modern historiographical term for a category of specialized, large-scale trading settlements that emerged along the shores of the North Sea and the English Channel in the seventh and eighth centuries CE. They are also frequently called **emporia** (singular *emporium*), following the vocabulary of contemporary Latin sources. Their emergence marks a decisive structural shift in the commercial geography of post-Roman northwestern Europe—from the fragmented, inland, estate-based local exchange of the sixth century toward a more integrated, monetized, interregional trading network.

---

## Definition and Physical Characteristics

Wics are archaeologically distinctive and differ sharply from contemporary rural settlements and from the late Roman *civitas* towns that preceded them. Their key features, as established by excavation at **Hamwic** (Southampton), **Lundenwic** (London), **Ipswich**, and their continental counterparts, include:

*   **Planned gridded street layouts**: Unlike the organic sprawl of rural settlements or the ruined shells of Roman towns (which early medieval populations generally avoided), wics were often laid out on a regular plan on previously uninhabited land adjacent to (but not within) earlier Roman sites.
*   **Specialized artisanal production**: Excavations reveal substantial evidence of organized craft production—glass-working, metalworking (iron, copper-alloy, silver), comb-making from antler, textile production—concentrated in distinct zones. This is not subsistence production; it implies professional artisans producing goods specifically for exchange.
*   **Portable commodity evidence**: Large quantities of imported pottery (Rhineland wheel-thrown wares, "Badorf" and "Tating" ware), wine amphorae, and lava quern-stones from the Rhineland document regular long-distance bulk trade.
*   **Coin finds**: Wics are among the most productive sites for the small, silver **[[sceatta|sceattas]]** that were the characteristic coin of the North Sea trading zone in the early eighth century. The density of coin loss indicates a fully monetized exchange economy.
*   **Large populations**: Hamwic covered approximately 42 hectares and is estimated to have housed 2,000–5,000 people—comparable to a small Roman town. This is an extraordinary concentration of non-agricultural population for the early medieval West.

## The Major Wics

| Site | Modern Location | Controlling Power (c. 700 CE) | Est. Foundation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Hamwic** | Southampton, England | Wessex | c. 680–720 CE |
| **Lundenwic** | Aldwych, London, England | Mercia / Kent | c. 600–650 CE |
| **Ipswich** | Ipswich, England | East Anglia | c. 620–650 CE |
| **Quentovic** | near Étaples, northern France | Frankish (Neustria) | c. 600–650 CE |
| **Dorestad** | near Wijk bij Duurstede, Netherlands | Frisian / Frankish | c. 630–650 CE |
| **Hedeby** | near Schleswig, Denmark | Danish | c. 720+ CE |

The **Frisian** merchants and sailors (*Fresones*) were the primary professional traders operating across the North Sea network, documented in Frankish sources as far inland as the Rhine and in English sources at London and York. Dorestad, at the junction of the Rhine delta, served as the hub of this Frisian-dominated commerce.

## The Wic and the North Sea Economic Zone

**Paul Loseby** (*NCMH*, Ch. 22) and **Stéphane Lebecq** argue that the emergence of the wics represents a structural reorientation of European commercial life:

*   **From Mediterranean to North Sea**: The late Roman economy was oriented along the Mediterranean—the internal sea that connected Rome, Alexandria, Carthage, Antioch, and Massilia. After the Arab conquests of the 630s–700s CE removed the southern and eastern Mediterranean from the post-Roman Christian sphere, long-distance commerce increasingly relocated to the **North Sea and Baltic** corridor.
*   **Royal foundations**: The wics were not spontaneous commercial growths. They appear in contemporary sources as controlled entities—English sources show that the king collected tolls at London and Southampton; Frankish sources document royal toll-collectors at Quentovic and Dorestad. The wic was thus an **instrument of royal fiscal policy** as much as a market, and its revenues were a significant component of early medieval royal income.
*   **The Pirenne thesis connection**: The relationship between the Arab conquests and the shift to the North Sea zone is the empirical core of the debate over the **[[pirenne-thesis|Pirenne thesis]]**. While Pirenne argued that Islam "broke" the Mediterranean and created the Middle Ages, most modern scholars accept that the shift was gradual and that the North Sea zone had its own independent developmental dynamic.

## Collapse and Transformation (9th Century)

The classic wics largely collapsed or transformed from the mid-ninth century onward, for reasons still debated:

*   **Viking raiding**: Sites like Quentovic (sacked 842 CE) and Dorestad (repeatedly raided from the 830s) suffered devastating attacks. The threat of Norse raiding made exposed, low-lying coastal sites highly vulnerable.
*   **Structural economic change**: The growth of more defended, inland market towns (*burhs* in England, Carolingian *portus*) reflected a reorganization of commercial activity that the wic's open, specialized model could not adapt to.

Their successors—the *burh*-towns, the Carolingian *portus*, and the early Scandinavian towns like Hedeby and Birka—represent the direct precursors of the medieval urban network.
