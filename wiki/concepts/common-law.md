# Common Law

The English common law tradition is a body of law built primarily through accumulated judicial precedent (*stare decisis*) rather than comprehensive legislative codification, originating in the 12th century as royal courts (the "king's law") began applying uniform legal principles across England in place of fragmented local and feudal custom. This distinguishes it structurally from the **ius commune** tradition of continental Europe, which developed instead from the systematic academic study and adaptation of Roman law (the *Corpus Iuris Civilis*) within medieval universities, and from later codified civil-law systems (e.g., the Napoleonic Code) that organize law around comprehensive written codes interpreted by judges rather than binding case precedent.

## Emic and Etic

"Common law" is both an emic category — the term used by medieval and later English jurists themselves to describe the law common to the whole realm as opposed to local or ecclesiastical law — and, in comparative legal history, an etic analytical category for classifying entire legal families (the "common law world" versus "civil law world").

## Spread and Limitations

Common law spread throughout the British Empire and forms the structural basis of legal systems in England, the United States, and much of the Commonwealth. Critics of a sharp common-law/civil-law dichotomy note substantial convergence in practice (statute has become dominant even in common-law systems; judicial interpretation matters greatly in civil-law systems) and caution against treating the categories as more rigid than they are in modern legal practice.
