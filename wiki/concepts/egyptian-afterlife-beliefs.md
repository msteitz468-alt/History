# Egyptian Afterlife Beliefs

The body of ancient Egyptian religious doctrine concerning death, judgment,
and continued existence after death, centered on the myth of
[[anubis|Anubis]] and Osiris and expressed across the Pyramid Texts, Coffin
Texts, and the Book of the Dead over some two millennia of continuous
elaboration.

## Definition and Scope

Egyptian belief held that a person possessed multiple spiritual components
— the *ka* (life-force), *ba* (individual character/soul), and *akh*
(transfigured spirit achieved after successful judgment) — whose continued
existence depended on proper burial, mortuary ritual, and preservation of
the body through mummification. Death was understood as a passage requiring
navigation of dangers and, centrally, judgment.

## Core Elements

The "Weighing of the Heart" ritual, overseen by [[anubis|Anubis]], measured
the deceased's heart against the feather of Ma'at (truth/cosmic order); a
heart burdened by wrongdoing was devoured by the monster Ammit, denying the
deceased continued existence, while a heart in balance allowed passage to
the Field of Reeds (Aaru), an idealized mirror of earthly life. Osiris, as
lord of the dead, presided over this judgment as the archetype of a
resurrected king.

## Historical Development

These beliefs, initially most elaborately developed for royalty (the
Pyramid Texts), gradually "democratized" over the Middle and New Kingdoms,
with mortuary texts and equipment (culminating in the Book of the Dead)
becoming accessible, in more modest form, to a much broader stratum of
Egyptian society who could afford at least simplified burial provisions.

## Limitations and Critiques

Surviving evidence is heavily weighted toward elite and royal burials, whose
lavish preservation has skewed the archaeological record relative to the
beliefs and practices of ordinary Egyptians, whose burials were far more
modest and far less likely to survive intact.
