# Macedonian Phalanx

The tactical infantry formation developed by Philip II of Macedon as the core of the reformed Macedonian army: dense ranks of heavy infantry (*pezhetairoi*) wielding the *sarissa*, a pike roughly 4–6 meters long, far longer than the Greek hoplite's spear. Held in both hands, the sarissa allowed several ranks of pikeheads to project simultaneously in front of the formation, producing an almost impenetrable frontal wall of points that outreached the classical Greek hoplite phalanx.

The formation traded individual mobility and flexibility for frontal shock power and defensive density, and depended on integration with other arms — Companion cavalry for the decisive flanking blow, hypaspists for tactical flexibility, and light troops for skirmishing — rather than functioning as a standalone solution. Philip and Alexander used the phalanx as a fixing/pinning force while cavalry delivered the decisive strike, a combined-arms doctrine that proved devastatingly effective against both Greek city-state hoplite armies and Achaemenid Persian forces. The formation and its variants persisted through the Hellenistic kingdoms until its rigidity and vulnerability to flank attack and broken terrain were exposed by Roman legionary flexibility at battles such as Cynoscephalae (197 BCE) and Pydna (168 BCE).

## Significance

The Macedonian phalanx exemplifies the shift from citizen-hoplite warfare to professionalized combined-arms tactics, and its later defeat by Roman manipular/legionary flexibility is a canonical case study in the trade-off between formation rigidity and tactical adaptability.
