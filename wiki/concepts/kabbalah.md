## Definition and Scope

The Hebrew word *qabbalah* means "tradition." Kabbalah designates the major tradition of Jewish mystical thought that emerged in medieval Provence and spread to Spain over the twelfth to fourteenth centuries. It is distinguished by:

- Emphasis on the **sefirot**: ten divine powers or attributes through which the utterly transcendent God (*ein sof*, "the infinite") manifests to creation
- Rich symbolic, numerological, and exegetical language drawing on the letters of the Hebrew alphabet and biblical texts, especially the opening vision of Ezekiel's *Chariot* (*merkabah*)
- An essentially esoteric character: Kabbalistic tradition emphasized oral transmission and deliberate obscurity in writing

Kabbalah is not a single school or text but a family of related mystical approaches. Modern scholars distinguish "contemplative/theosophical" Kabbalah (focused on the theory of the sefirot and their relationships) from "ecstatic/prophetic" Kabbalah (focused on meditative techniques for inducing mystical union with God or the Active Intellect) ([[adamson-philosophy-islamic-world-2016]]).

## Antecedents

**Late antique *merkabah* literature**: Esoteric texts describing the mystic's journey to behold God upon His throne (Ezekiel 1:1–28). The Mishnah and Talmud restrict teaching about the Chariot: "the chariot [may not be discussed] in the presence of one, unless he is a sage."

**Sefer Yeṣirah** (*Book of Creation*): An ancient text of uncertain date (already old when Saadia Gaon commented on it) that refers to "thirty-two paths of wisdom" by which God created the universe — the numbers 1–10 plus the twenty-two letters of the Hebrew alphabet. This anticipates the sefirotic numerology of Kabbalah.

**Neoplatonism**: Kabbalists built on the philosophical legacy of [[ibn-gabirol]], Abraham Ibn Ezra, and especially [[maimonides]], all of whom transmitted Neoplatonic ideas about divine ineffability. Moses of Burgos remarked that Kabbalists "planted their feet at the spot reached by the heads of the philosophers."

**Gnosticism**: The rich symbolic language and imagery of late antique Gnosticism influenced Kabbalah, though the precise historical transmission is difficult to reconstruct.

## The Sefirot

The central teaching of Kabbalah, developed most fully in the *Zohar* and related thirteenth-century texts. The ten sefirot are:

1. *Keter* (Crown) — shares God's infinity and ineffability; analogous to the Neoplatonic One
2. *Ḥokhmah* (Wisdom) — contains the essences of things; analogous to Plotinus' Intellect / realm of Forms
3. *Binah* (Understanding) — analogous to Neoplatonic Soul
4–10. Seven further sefirot through which God relates to the created world

The sefirot are associated with numbers, Hebrew letters, parts of the body, male and female principles (with frankly erotic language), and the elements of Ezekiel's Chariot. Their dynamic interrelation — including procession and return modeled on Neoplatonic emanation — constitutes much of Kabbalistic cosmological teaching.

The *ein sof* (God in Himself) is absolutely beyond all knowledge or speech: in this the Kabbalists agree with the negative theology of [[maimonides]]. But where philosophers stopped at negation, Kabbalists continue by describing God's self-manifestation through the sefirot — loosely analogous to the more attribute-friendly Ashʿarites relative to the Muʿtazilites, or to the divine "names" in [[ibn-arabi|Ibn ʿArabī]]'s mysticism.

## Historical Development

### Provence, late 12th century
The *Sefer ha-Bahir* (*Book of Brilliance*) appears in southern France, the earliest significant Kabbalistic text proper. Around it gathered a circle of Provençal rabbis including Abraham ben David ("Rabad") and his son Isaac the Blind, credited with the first fusion of Kabbalistic teaching with Jewish philosophical sources.

### Gerona, early 13th century
Isaac the Blind's students brought sefirotic theory to Catalonia. The movement gained enormous prestige when [[maimonides|Naḥmanides]] (Moses ben Naḥman), the most prominent rabbinic figure of his generation and a moderate voice in the [[maimonidean-controversy]], endorsed Kabbalah.

### Spain, late 13th century — the Zohar
The *Zohar* — the most celebrated Kabbalistic text — circulated in northern Spain from the late thirteenth century. Written in Aramaic and presenting itself as an ancient rabbinic travelogue through the Holy Land, Gershom Scholem demonstrated in the twentieth century that it is a medieval composition, probably by Moses of Leon or a circle around him.

### Abraham Abulafia (1240–c.1291): Ecstatic/Prophetic Kabbalah
The most distinctive practitioner of "prophetic" Kabbalah. Abulafia used meditative techniques — repetitive chanting of the letters of the Tetragrammaton (YHWH) combined with vowels, head-shaking, weeping, fixed hand gestures — to induce a direct vision of God or union with the Active Intellect. He associated the ten sefirot with the ten heavenly intellects of Aristotelian cosmology and wrote three commentaries on [[maimonides]]' *Guide for the Perplexed*. He also declared himself the Messiah and reportedly tried to obtain an audience with Pope Nicholas III (who died before the meeting could occur).

His approach was opposed by Solomon Ibn Adret (Rashba), who represented the theoretical Kabbalistic tradition and was the same scholar who imposed the Barcelona ban on teaching philosophy to the young in 1305.

## Relation to Philosophy

Kabbalah's relationship to Jewish philosophical rationalism is complex and contested:

- **Absorption**: Kabbalists absorbed Neoplatonic language and some Maimonidean categories into a coded symbolic framework. The direction of travel was from philosophy into Kabbalah (whereas Maimonides moved from biblical language into rationalist Aristotelianism).
- **Critique**: Rabad criticized Maimonides' intolerance of Jews who describe God in bodily terms, saying "many [have done so], and his betters."
- **Competition**: The Aristotelian vs. Neoplatonic contrast maps partially (not perfectly) onto the Maimonidean vs. Kabbalistic divide. Elijah del Medigo in the fifteenth-century Renaissance noticed that newly translated Platonic texts fit well with Kabbalistic ideas.

## Later Developments

Kabbalah continued to evolve after the medieval period. Expulsion from Spain in 1492 contributed to new developments (Lurianic Kabbalah in Safed). "Christian Kabbalists" in the Renaissance drew on sefirotic theory in their own theological speculation. Hasidic Judaism (eighteenth century onward) is among the most famous modern manifestations.

## Historiography

The modern academic study of Kabbalah was founded by Gershom Scholem (*Major Trends in Jewish Mysticism*, 1941; *Kabbalah*, 1974), who established the Zohar as a medieval text, traced the history of Kabbalistic ideas, and rehabilitated Kabbalah as a subject for serious historical inquiry. Subsequent scholarship has debated Scholem's specific attributions and the relationship of Kabbalah to philosophy. Adamson situates Kabbalah as the tradition that went beyond philosophy by going in the opposite direction — where philosophers translated biblical imagery into rationalist argument, Kabbalists absorbed philosophical concepts into symbolic biblical language.
