# Hillforts

## Definition and Scope

Hillforts are defended enclosures sited on hills, promontories, or other eminences, constructed primarily from the later Bronze Age through the Iron Age across Europe. They range from small refuges to large agglomerated settlements with complex ramparts, internal structures, and evidence of industrial, commercial, administrative, and ritual activity. While security and defense were dominant functions, many served as statements of tribal power and prestige, with ramparts often far more elaborate than purely military needs required. "Security and defense must be seen as the dominant function of hillforts, but these frequently impressive constructions must have served other, less material purposes." (range 03, cache lines 580-590 approx).

The term encompasses a wide variety of forms and scales. In Britain alone over three thousand structures of "notionally hillfort character" exist, with several hundred classic examples in south-central England.

## Key Features and Construction

Sites of physical eminence had longstanding significance as liminal or sacred places, locations for gatherings, or temporary refuge. Permanent occupation often paired with industrial and other activities. Rampart construction showed great variety but fell into broad categories:

- Timber-laced defenses: Kastenbau (boxlike compartments filled with rubble); Altkönig-Preist type (stone walls front, heavy internal timbers); variants with verticals front only or secured by weight. Culmination in the massive *murus Gallicus* of Late La Tène oppida: "ramparts of nailed box construction with an outer masonry facing and, on occasion, a substantial internal earthen support." (range 03, cache lines ~670-680).

- Dump construction: wide sloping earth ramparts without timber support, often with substantial ditches. In northern France, shallower but broader Fécamp-type ditches. Massive rubble ramparts (e.g., Otzenhausen, Germany).

Entrances: angled, overlapping, mazelike; inturned passages for Late La Tène oppida; massive timber gateways (sometimes doubled or trebled); footbridges or towers.

Hillforts were not a uniform evolution; builders chose methods based on workforce and needs. "The varying types of rampart construction cannot in any way be seen as regular developments over time." (range 03, cache lines ~715-720). The Late La Tène oppida and the mud-brick wall at the Heuneburg (Mediterranean imitation with bastions) stand apart.

## Chronology and Regional Development

Construction on significant scale began in the later Bronze Age (~ beginning of last pre-Christian millennium), possibly driven by population pressure, climatic change, polity shifts, warfare changes, and resource mobilization. In continental Europe, houses often aligned along ramparts or in regular rows inside (e.g., Wittnauer Horn, Switzerland; Altes Schloss near Potsdam).

In Britain: early examples (Rams Hill, Berkshire; Breidden, Powys); major sites from mid-millennium. Indigenous developments, "not a product of invading groups, as was once believed." (range 03, cache lines ~765-770). Timber-laced and massive earth defenses; inturned entrances. Vitrified forts in Scotland (ramparts intensely burned, likely hostile action or deliberate).

Ireland: explosion c. 1000–900 BCE (Rathgall, Mooghaun, Haughey’s Fort) with permanent occupation.

Continental large-scale excavations (beyond oppida like Manching and Mont Beuvray) have focused on defensive construction details.

## Exemplars and Functions

Danebury (Hampshire, England): triple-ramparted 5-hectare hillfort used ~550 BCE to early 1st c. CE. Innermost rampart massive with V-ditch (16.1 m from ditch base to crest). Complex mazelike entrances with sling-stone caches. Interior: houses (rectangular/circular) along E-W streets; >100 houses identified (not all contemporary); 2,400+ storage pits (up to 3 m deep, grain silos); small rectangular structures possibly temples; evidence of secular activities. "The enormous storage capacity implied seems far in excess of the needs of the occupants of Danebury, a number estimated to have been between 200 and 350 at any one time. It has been suggested that the primary function of Danebury was to act as a central place for the storage and protection of grain for the peoples of the surrounding landscape." (range 03, cache lines ~815-825 approx).

Maiden Castle (Dorset): prime example of massive ramparts far exceeding defense needs; prestige and ostentation. "Dominating the physical horizon, such great hillforts were tangible statements of tribal power." (range 03, cache lines ~585-590).

The trend toward centralization mirrored in oppida development in Late La Tène (2nd–1st c. BCE), halted or transformed by Roman occupation.

## Historiography and Interpretation

Early views sometimes attributed British forts to invaders; now recognized as indigenous. Emphasis shifted from purely defensive to multifunctional (economic, social, ritual, prestige). "With these sites, considerations of prestige and ostentation may be assumed." (range 03). Defenses often rebuilt or elaborated visibly from distance. Regional variation high; not all "hillforts" were permanently occupied or urban.
