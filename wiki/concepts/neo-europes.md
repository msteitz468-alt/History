# Neo-Europes

**Neo-Europes** are the temperate regions outside Europe — primarily North America north of Mexico, the humid pampa and adjacent areas of southern South America (Argentina, Uruguay, southern Brazil), Australia (especially the southeast and Tasmania), and New Zealand — in which populations of European descent came to form the great majority and in which European agriculture, livestock, and weeds largely supplanted indigenous biota.

Crosby uses the term to highlight the biogeographical and ecological dimension of European expansion: these were not merely colonies or conquests but new "Europes" created by the successful transplantation and explosive expansion of a portmanteau biota of Old World species in climates similar to Europe’s.

## Core Characteristics

- **Demographic dominance**: By the late 19th/early 20th centuries, 80–95+% European-descended populations in core areas (with important exceptions and mixtures, especially mestizo populations in parts of southern South America).
- **Agricultural transformation**: Shift to Old World staples (wheat, beef, mutton, dairy) and export-oriented production; these regions became the world’s leading exporters of wheat, beef, wool, and other temperate foodstuffs.
- **Ecological replacement**: Rapid spread of European weeds (dandelion, plantain, etc.), livestock (often feral or semi-feral at first), and crops; retreat or marginalization of native grasses, browsers (guanaco, kangaroo, moa), and human populations.
- **High photosynthetic potential + low competition**: Large areas with long summer days, suitable rainfall/soil, and indigenous biotas that proved less competitive against the introduced suite under heavy grazing and disturbance.

## Historical Trajectory

Early experiments (Norse in Vinland/Greenland; Iberian Atlantic islands) prefigured the pattern. Full realization followed 1492 with improved maritime technology and the biological "reunion" of the hemispheres. The 19th-century steam era produced the largest wave of human migration in history (over 50 million Europeans), which filled ecologically prepared lands.

Not all temperate or settler lands became equally "Neo-European" (e.g., Chile’s more limited European demographic share; limited permanent settlement in many tropical zones).

## Analytical Value

The concept forces attention to the biological preconditions of demographic replacement and global food-system asymmetry. It explains why European imperialism produced "swarming" and land appropriation in some places and different (often extractive or co-existence) patterns elsewhere. It remains central to environmental history and to debates over the Great Divergence, colonialism’s ecological legacies, and the making of the modern world economy.
