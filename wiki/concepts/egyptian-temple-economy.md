# Egyptian Temple Economy

The great temples of the [[actors/new-kingdom-egypt|New Kingdom]] as the central economic
institutions of the state — landlords, granaries, employers, traders and, in Kemp's
phrase, **"the reserve banks of their day."** In [[sources/kemp-ancient-egypt-anatomy-2018|*Anatomy
of a Civilization*]] (Ch. 6) Kemp insists that, "shorn of ideological nuance, temples
comprised a major sector of 'the state' as we would see it, working in a symbiotic
relationship with the palace," even as their own records stress institutional independence.

## Landholding and the Wilbour Papyrus

Temples held cultivable land — sometimes hundreds of kilometres away or in Nubia — plus
herds, fishing and fowling rights, flax fields, vineyards and beehives, much of it farmed
on lease for up to **30% of the crop** as rent. The **Wilbour Papyrus** (reign of Ramesses
V, c. 1146–1141 BCE) shows temple land in Middle Egypt subdivided into a "tapestry" of
holdings worked by the whole social spectrum — small farmers, temple staff, soldiers
(including Sherden), even the vizier — with a notably high proportion of **women** as
holders. A separate section covers **khato-land**, owned by Pharaoh but administered by
temples — the blurred crown/temple boundary in one document.

## Scale: Granaries, Offerings, Trade

- The **Ramesseum** granaries could hold ~**226,328 khar** of grain — a year's support for
  ~3,400 families (17,000–20,000 people), ~350 boat-loads to fill.
- Daily offering-lists were vast: **Medinet Habu** listed 5,500 loaves, 54 cakes, 204 jars
  of beer per day, redistributed to staff via the **Reversion of Offerings**.
- Temples ran **sea-going ships** (Ramesses II gave the Abydos temple of Seti I a trading
  vessel staffed with *shuty*), collected rents by barge fleet (the **Amiens-Baldwin
  Papyrus**: 21 barges, ~650 khar each), and held **mining rights** (gold at Wadi
  Mia/Kanais; galena in the eastern desert).
- The **Nauri Decree** of Seti I protected the Abydos temple's distant Nubian estates with
  penalties of 100 blows and an 80:1 fine — evidence the administration ran on overlapping,
  competing institutional claims rather than a unified system.

## Relation to the Wider Economy

Temple buffer stocks implicitly **stabilized prices** without official price-fixing
([[concepts/provider-state]]); but the redistributive core met individual demand only
roughly, leaving the rest to private exchange ([[concepts/birth-of-economic-man]]).

## The Ptolemaic subordination of the temples (Manning 2010)

By the [[ptolemaic-egypt|Ptolemaic period]] the temple sector — still the great coordinator
of *"economic land management, record keeping, and storage"* — became the principal
**[[ptolemaic-state-formation|bargaining]] constituency** of the new state.
[[sources/manning-last-pharaohs-2010|J. G. Manning]] shows the crown progressively **penetrating
and subordinating** it: by the end of the third century *"'royal' scribes replaced temple
scribes at Thebes, and temple granaries … were replaced (presumably in name only) by royal
granaries"*; the temples' traditional *"first fruits"* due was recast as the **apomoira** with
**tax farmers inserted as intermediaries**, and the temples paid the **syntaxis**. The
recurring **bilingual/trilingual priestly synodal decrees** (Canopus 238, the Rosetta/Memphis
decree 196) are, for Manning, the documentary record of *"the continual two-way bargaining
process between the king and the priesthoods."* See [[premodern-state]] and [[ruler-cult]].
