# Ibn Khaldun on Environment and Civilization

[[ibn-khaldun|Ibn Khaldun]]'s geographical and climatic theory of civilization, set out in the
prefatory discussions to Chapter I of [[ibn-khaldun-muqaddimah|the Muqaddimah]], constitutes one
of the most systematic pre-modern attempts to explain the unequal distribution of human civilization
across the earth's surface through physical causes — the heat of the sun, the moisture of the air,
and the biological effects of temperature on the power of generation.

---

## Definition and Scope

Ibn Khaldun argues that the geography of civilization is not accidental. The cultivated portion of
the earth — the quarter of the earth's sphere free from water and frost — is unevenly distributed.
Civilization is absent or minimal near the equator and in the far north; it reaches its peak
density in the third through sixth of the seven recognized geographical zones. This distribution
has a physical cause: the relationship between solar angle and the generation of life.

The theory belongs to the wider framework of [[ilm-al-umran|ʿilm al-ʿumrān]] (the science of
civilization). It answers the prior question — where does civilization exist at all and why? —
before Ibn Khaldun turns to his central question about how civilizations are structured internally
and why they rise and fall.

---

## The Geographical Framework: Seven Zones

Following Ptolemy's *Geography* and al-Idrisi's *Book of Roger* (composed for the Norman king of
Sicily Roger II in the mid-twelfth century), Ibn Khaldun accepts the division of the cultivated
earth into **seven zones** running east to west, stacked from the equator northward. Beyond the
equator to the south, and beyond the seventh zone to the north, lie waste and uninhabitable
regions.

Ibn Khaldun provides competing scholarly measurements of the zones' latitudinal extent, citing
Ptolemy, Ishaq b. al-Hasan al-Khazini, and Abu Jafar al-Khazini. In Ptolemy's version:

- Zone 1: to 16° north latitude
- Zone 2: to 20°
- Zone 3: to 27°
- Zone 4: to 33°
- Zone 5: to 38°
- Zone 6: to 43°
- Zone 7: to 48°

Beyond 64° north, civilization ends entirely because of cold and the absence of solar heat.

The longest day in each zone increases by approximately half an hour from zone to zone, from about
twelve and a half hours at the equator to sixteen hours at the seventh zone's northern boundary.

---

## The Climate Theory: Why Civilization Concentrates in the Middle Zones

Ibn Khaldun gives a systematic causal explanation for why the southern and northern extremes of
the inhabited world are sparsely populated:

**Southern emptiness (excessive heat):** Near the equator, the sun reaches its zenith twice each
year and its declination is minimal. Perpendicular solar rays produce sustained extreme heat. This
heat causes desiccation — it dries up water and destroys moisture — and with moisture destroyed,
the **power of generation** (*quwwat al-tawallud*) is eliminated. Minerals, plants, and animals
cannot reproduce. Ibn Khaldun notes that "the destruction caused by great heat is greater than that
caused by great cold, because heat brings about desiccation faster than cold brings about freezing."

**Northern emptiness (excessive cold):** Beyond the seventh zone, the sun's angle is so oblique
and its rays so weak that generation again becomes impossible — this time through freezing rather
than desiccation.

**Middle-zone fertility:** In the third, fourth, and fifth zones — roughly corresponding to North
Africa, the Mediterranean world, the Near East, Persia, and Central Asia — heat is temperate. The
sun's rays strike at angles that produce warmth without desiccation. Generation flourishes.
Civilization therefore "has its seat between the third and the sixth zones."

> "There is a medium degree of civilization in the third, fourth, and fifth zones, because the heat
> there is temperate owing to the decreased amount of light. There is a great deal of civilization
> in the sixth and seventh zones because of the decreased amount of heat there."

---

## Ibn Khaldun and His Sources: Agreement and Critique

Ibn Khaldun draws heavily on Islamic geographical tradition, particularly al-Idrisi and Ptolemy.
He accepts the sevenfold zonal division and the astronomical explanations of solar angle. However,
he criticizes **Averroes** (Ibn Rushd), who assumed the equator is a symmetrical axis — that what
lies south of it mirrors what lies north, and therefore the south should be as well-populated as
the north. Ibn Khaldun argues this fails because the southern hemisphere is covered by more water
than the northern, making the symmetry argument inapplicable:

> "As to the region south of the equator, it is made impossible by the fact that the element of
> water covers the face of the earth in the south, where the corresponding area in the north admits
> of generation."

He also acknowledges an empirical anomaly: classical philosophers concluded the equatorial zone
must be empty, but "continuous tradition" (reports from travelers) indicates it is inhabited — if
sparsely. He reconciles this by saying the equatorial regions are not fully empty, but civilization
there is minimal and barely deserves the name.

## Environment's Effects on Human Character

Beyond explaining the distribution of civilization, Ibn Khaldun argues that climate shapes the
**character, temperament, and intellectual capacities** of populations:

- Peoples of the extreme southern zones (equatorial Africa) are described as living closer to
  animal than rational life — "they live in thickets and caves and eat herbs and unprepared grain"
  — not from any innate racial deficiency but because the burning climate makes organized social
  life nearly impossible.
- Peoples of the temperate middle zones develop the fullest range of human social organization,
  crafts, sciences, and government.
- The northern extremes produce peoples of hardy constitutions but limited intellectual development
  because the cold suppresses certain vital faculties.

Ibn Khaldun explicitly links this to the distinction between [[sedentary-vs-bedouin-civilization|
Bedouin and sedentary civilization]]: environment determines whether the natural basis for complex
sedentary life exists at all. Where heat or cold prevents the accumulation of agricultural surplus
and dense settlement, only nomadic or quasi-nomadic life is possible.

---

## Historical Significance and Critique

Ibn Khaldun's environmental theory anticipates **geographical determinism** as a scholarly
tradition by several centuries. It shares structural assumptions with Montesquieu's *Spirit of the
Laws* (1748) and has been compared to twentieth-century geographical determinists including Ellsworth
Huntington and, more recently, Jared Diamond's arguments in *Guns, Germs, and Steel*.

The key distinctions:
- Ibn Khaldun's mechanism is **solar heat and the power of generation** — a physical-biological
  argument, not a racial one.
- He applies the theory primarily to explain where civilization can exist at all, not to rank
  civilizations hierarchically in the sense later European writers did.
- His environmental arguments operate alongside, not instead of, social-structural arguments:
  [[asabiyyah|group solidarity]], the sedentary/Bedouin distinction, and the dynastic cycle are
  the primary explanatory tools once the geographical threshold has been crossed.

Scholarly criticism of the environmental theory focuses on its circularity (the middle zones are
defined as temperate partly because that is where the known great civilizations are located) and on
its dependence on Ptolemaic cosmology, which misrepresents the actual climate of sub-equatorial
Africa.

## Historiography

**Sources:** Ibn Khaldun's primary geographical sources are Ptolemy's *Geography* (available in
Arabic translation), al-Idrisi's *Nuzhat al-mushtaq* (the *Book of Roger*, c. 1154), and Islamic
astronomical tradition. He also draws on al-Mas'udi, Ibn Khurradadhbih, and unnamed travelers'
reports.

**Debates:** Modern scholars disagree on how central the environmental theory is to Ibn Khaldun's
overall project. Aziz al-Azmeh and others read it as a borrowed conventional framework that Ibn
Khaldun does not develop further; Robert Irwin and Muhsin Mahdi treat it as the necessary
cosmological grounding for the human-social analysis that follows.

**Collection coverage:** The [[ibn-khaldun-muqaddimah|Muqaddimah]] (Rosenthal translation) is in
the collection. Al-Idrisi's *Book of Roger*, which Ibn Khaldun used as his cartographic model, is
not in the collection. The Ptolemy *Geography* is partially available through the Cambridge Ancient
History volumes but not in its Arabic transmission.
