# The Papal Monarchy (Institutions of the Reform Church, 1073–1216)

## Definition

The **papal monarchy** is the centralised governmental structure of the Latin Church
that emerged from the [[gregorian-reform|Gregorian Reform]] and reached maturity under
Innocent III (1198–1216). [[luscombe-ncmh-v4-2004|NCMH IV]] (I. S. Robinson, Ch. 11,
"The institutions of the church, 1073–1216") shows that contemporaries themselves
described it in **monarchical and quasi-feudal language**: the pope possessed "the
fullness of pontifical power, like a king in a kingdom," having delegated his charge
to bishops "just as a king does not diminish his royal power, although he has divided
up his kingdom among different dukes, counts and office-holders." The Third Lateran
Council (1179) was likened to a city with the pope as king, the cardinals as nobles,
the archbishops as consuls, and the bishops and abbots as the people.

## The Machinery of Government

- **The cardinals.** Constituted as the papal electorate by the **Election Decree of
  1059**, the College of Cardinals became both the pope's electors and his senate —
  the "nobles" of the ecclesiastical realm.
- **Legates.** Papal legates (*legati a latere*), often cardinals or trusted
  archbishops (Hugh of Lyon, Hugh of Die), projected papal authority into the
  provinces, holding reforming councils and enforcing decrees — "proven physicians"
  sent to heal the graver diseases of the Church.
- **General councils.** The reform papacy revived the general council as an instrument
  of government: **Lateran I (1123)** confirming the Concordat of Worms, **Lateran II
  (1139)** ending the schism of 1130, **Lateran III (1179)** regulating papal
  elections (the two-thirds rule), and **Lateran IV (1215)**, the high-water mark of
  the medieval Church under Innocent III.
- **The Curia and canon law.** A growing papal chancery, judicial apparatus, and
  fiscal system, working through the rapidly expanding body of [[ius-commune|canon
  law]] (Gratian's *Decretum*, c. 1140; the papal decretals), made Rome the supreme
  court and legislative centre of Latin Christendom.

## The Ideology: Vicarius Christi and Plenitudo Potestatis

Robinson traces two key formulae to their twelfth-century crystallisation:

- ***Vicarius Christi*** ("vicar of Christ"). Once applied to bishops and even to
  kings, the title became **exclusively papal** in twelfth-century theology (Honorius
  Augustodunensis, Anselm of Havelberg, Bernard of Clairvaux) and was adopted by
  Innocent III as the central expression of papal primacy — the pope "set between God
  and man . . . who judges all and is judged by no one."
- ***Plenitudo potestatis*** ("fullness of power"). Reviving a formula of Leo I,
  reformers held that Peter alone received the fullness of power, while other prelates
  were called only "to a share of the responsibilities" (*in partem sollicitudinis*) —
  the doctrinal basis of papal supremacy over the whole hierarchy.

## Significance

The papal monarchy is the institutional legacy of the reform: it made the papacy the
first centralised, law-governed sovereignty of medieval Europe, a model that secular
monarchies would emulate, and the engine of Latin Christendom's unification — but also
the standing rival of emperors and kings, and the target of the later conciliar and
anti-curial critiques.
