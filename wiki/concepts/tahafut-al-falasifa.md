## Definition and Scope

The ***Tahāfut al-Falāsifa*** (**Incoherence of the Philosophers**, literally something closer to "the reckless stumble of the philosophers into error") is a treatise composed by [[al-ghazali|Abū Ḥāmid al-Ghazālī]] around 1095 CE, directed primarily at the metaphysical system of [[ibn-sina|Avicenna]]. It is one of the most consequential texts in the history of Islamic philosophy — not because it ended philosophical inquiry (it did not), but because it permanently set Avicenna rather than Aristotle as the central reference point for philosophical debate in the eastern Islamic world, and because it established a critical method of philosophical theology that defined Ashʿarite intellectual practice for centuries. Source: [[adamson-philosophy-islamic-world-2016]].

The word *tahāfut* does not mean systematic "incoherence" in the sense of logical contradiction. It means a reckless, heedless plunging into error — an image of overconfident philosophers stumbling because they have exceeded what rational demonstration can establish.

## Composition Context

Al-Ghazālī composed the *Tahāfut* during his tenure at the Niẓāmiyyah madrasa in Baghdad (1091–1095 CE), at the height of his academic prestige. It was preceded by the *Maqāṣid al-Falāsifa* (Aims of the Philosophers), a sympathetic and accurate summary of Avicennan philosophy that al-Ghazālī wrote to ensure his readers understood what they were about to see criticized. The pair of works formed a calculated pedagogical strategy.

Al-Ghazālī himself noted the ironic risk: by carefully summarizing the philosophers before refuting them, he gave his readers accurate access to Avicennan ideas — just as Simplicius had preserved Philoponus by quoting him in the course of attacking him. The *Maqāṣid* in particular was later mistaken in Latin Christendom (where it circulated as "Algazel's philosophy") for a straightforwardly pro-Avicennan work.

## Structure and Method

The *Tahāfut* proceeds through a series of **discrete disputes**, each of which follows a dialectical pattern:
1. Al-Ghazālī states the philosophers' argument (drawn primarily from Avicenna)
2. He presents counterarguments, usually showing that the argument falls below the standard of demonstrative proof
3. He may present multiple counter-positions without necessarily endorsing any one positive doctrine

This last feature is significant: al-Ghazālī explicitly states that the *Tahāfut*'s purpose is "not to establish positive doctrine but only to identify the philosophers' mistakes" (Tahāfut §76). He is a prosecutor, not a witness.

## The Three Heresies

At the *Tahāfut*'s conclusion, al-Ghazālī identifies three Avicennan doctrines that cross the line from philosophical error into **apostasy** (*kufr*) — and which therefore carry the most serious religious and legal consequences:

| Doctrine | Why Heretical |
|---|---|
| Eternity of the universe | Denies God's creative act; makes God's will subordinate to necessity |
| God knows only universals, not particulars | Contradicts Quranic statements about God's omniscience of all things |
| Only the soul survives death (no bodily resurrection) | Contradicts the Quran's explicit teachings on resurrection |

Other philosophical errors are treated as more ordinary mistakes, comparable to those of [[mutazilism|Muʿtazilite]] theologians — wrong, but not apostasy.

## The Eternity Dispute

The *Tahāfut*'s first and most developed topic is the **eternity of the universe**. Al-Ghazālī summarizes several Avicennan arguments for the universe's eternity, then attempts to show that none achieves demonstrative force. The key argument he targets:

> If God has an eternal will to create, why does the universe not exist eternally? Any change in the moment of creation would require something to change in God, but God cannot change.

Al-Ghazālī's response invokes the Ashʿarite willingness to affirm divine **arbitrary choice**: God could eternally will that something happen *at a specific time*, without this requiring change in God. He uses the analogy of a person choosing between two equally appealing dates — you simply pick one. God can make an arbitrary choice between moments equally available for creation.

The deeper issue is al-Ghazālī's rejection of **necessitarianism**: Avicenna's God necessarily causes the universe, because everything God does He does necessarily. For al-Ghazālī (following [[asharism|Ashʿarism]]), this eliminates genuine divine agency. A true agent acts from choice and understanding; fire is not an agent, because it burns necessarily. God must be a genuine agent, which requires the possibility of free, arbitrary choice.

## Causation and Miracles

The *Tahāfut*'s treatment of causation (the discussion of miracles) is its most philosophically contested section, and has generated a long-running scholarly debate.

Al-Ghazālī attacks the claim that fire necessarily causes burning — a case where Avicennan natural philosophy intersects with Ashʿarite theological commitments. His argument:

> We observe that when fire touches cotton, burning follows. But this is **habitual** (*ʿādī*), not **necessary** (*wājib*). God creates the burning alongside the fire as His regular practice. He could create things differently — as He did when Abraham survived the fire unscathed (Quran 21:69). This explains miracles without requiring that God "breaks" the laws of nature: there are no such laws, only God's regular habits.

**Scholarly debate**: Does this passage commit al-Ghazālī to full **[[occasionalism-islamic|occasionalism]]** (God is the only real cause; secondary causes do nothing) or to a weaker position (secondary causes are real but do not necessitate their effects; God retains the power to override them)?

- **Occasionalist reading**: al-Ghazālī is an Ashʿarite occasionalist who denies that fire does anything at all; God creates burning "side by side" with fire as a regular habit
- **Moderate reading**: al-Ghazālī denies necessity but not causation; fire genuinely burns unless God intervenes; the miracle is God's choice not to intervene in His usual pattern

Adamson notes that al-Ghazālī may deliberately leave both options open, since either is sufficient to defeat Avicennan necessitarianism, which is his primary target.

## Wahm (Estimation) as Philosophical Diagnosis

A recurring diagnostic concept in the *Tahāfut* is **wahm** (faculty of estimation). Avicenna had used this term for a faculty that grasps non-sensory features of the world (like a sheep's perception of a wolf's hostility) but can also generate misleading impressions. Al-Ghazālī turns the concept against its originator: many of Avicenna's metaphysical arguments, including the arguments for the eternity of the universe, proceed on the basis of *wahm* — compelling quasi-perceptual impressions that masquerade as rational demonstrations. They feel obviously true (like the intuition that empty space must surround the universe) but are not demonstrable.

This critical use of *wahm* became a major tool in later [[asharism|Ashʿarite]] theological-philosophical writing.

## Legacy and Ironies

### Paradoxical Reinforcement of Avicenna's Status

The *Tahāfut*'s most ironic consequence was to cement Avicenna's central position in Islamic intellectual life. By treating Avicenna as synonymous with "the philosophers," al-Ghazālī permanently displaced Aristotle as the point of reference. Later debates — including [[ibn-rushd|Averroes']] *Tahāfut al-Tahāfut* (*Incoherence of the Incoherence*, also known in Latin as *Destructio Destructionis*) — took place in Avicennan rather than strictly Aristotelian terms.

### The Latin Misunderstanding

In Latin Christendom, only the *Maqāṣid* (al-Ghazālī's sympathetic summary of Avicenna) was known for an extended period. Latin readers thus took "Algazel" to be a faithful Avicennan — a misreading corrected only when Averroes' rebuttal reached Latin translators, and the full *Tahāfut* became accessible.

### Selective Critique, Not Total Rejection

Al-Ghazālī's *Deliverer from Error* makes clear that he rejected philosophy selectively, not wholesale:
- **Logic**: fully acceptable, indeed certain; not in competition with religion
- **Natural philosophy**: mostly acceptable
- **Ethics and politics**: borrowed from prophetic traditions anyway
- **Metaphysics**: riddled with errors, three of which constitute apostasy

This selective stance shaped the subsequent Ashʿarite tradition: later theologians absorbed Avicennan logic and epistemological standards into kalām, producing a sophisticated "philosophized theology" that al-Ghazālī had helped make both necessary and possible.

## Historiography

The myth that al-Ghazālī's *Tahāfut* effectively killed Islamic philosophy has been comprehensively dismantled by modern scholarship. Philosophy in the eastern Islamic lands continued actively after al-Ghazālī, absorbed his challenges, and developed further in new directions (Illuminationism, Mulla Sadra, etc.). The *Tahāfut* is now understood as one important moment in a continuing tradition rather than a termination point.

The question of whether al-Ghazālī was "basically an Avicennan philosopher" or "basically an Ashʿarite" remains genuinely contested — he was probably both, in different works and at different levels of analysis.
