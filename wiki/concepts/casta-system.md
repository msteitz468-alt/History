# The Casta System (Sociedad de Castas)

The **sociedad de castas** was the racially ordered social hierarchy of
[[colonial-latin-america|colonial Latin America]] — a "society of caste" in which
**Indians, Africans and their descendants, and the mixed-race *castas* were defined
as legal inferiors and discriminated against in practice**, while Spaniards and
Portuguese (and those who could pass as such) held wealth, office, and honor. It is
one of the most distinctive features of the colonial world and the matrix of modern
Latin American racial identity.

## Race, wealth, and culture

Burkholder and Johnson stress that the system was **not biologically rigid**.
Iberians believed a properly ordered society was hierarchical, divided into the
estates of nobles, clergy, and commoners (the first two enjoying *fueros* — special
tax and judicial privileges). Transplanted to a hemisphere of mixing races and
booming-and-busting economies, this produced a **more fluid order than Spain or
Portugal itself**: people of substantial property "were commonly presumed to be
white even if their color or appearance might suggest otherwise." **Race was
defined largely by wealth, lineage, and power** — and by *culture* (command of
Spanish/Portuguese, Christianity, dress, diet) — "rather than narrowly by biology."
In the first decades, mixed children and grandchildren of conquistadors and native
noblewomen moved in elite circles and married Europeans.

## Limpieza de sangre

The ideological root was **limpieza de sangre** ("purity of blood"), an Iberian
concept that began as **religious** discrimination against Jews and Muslims
(*conversos* / New Christians) but — because the "taint" was held to be inherited —
**functioned in practice as racial discrimination**. In the colonies it expanded to
cover Africans and their descendants, *castas*, the illegitimate, and sometimes
Indians, barring them from offices, guilds, universities, and elite marriage. Tellingly,
some of the surviving **Indian nobility** (e.g., the Cuzco Inca elite, the Tlaxcalan
nobility) successfully claimed limpieza de sangre as "Old Christians untainted by
bad races" — gaining access to colonial offices, but making their status dependent
on Spanish authorities rather than tradition.

## The mixed castes

Sexual relations across the three founding groups produced an elaborate vocabulary:

- **Mestizo / mestiço / mameluco / caboclo** — European + Indian.
- **Mulatto / pardo** — European + African (legally enslaved if the mother was a
  slave, since status followed the mother).
- **Zambo** — African + Indian.
- ***Casta*** — the umbrella term for any non-white who was not clearly an Indian.

By ~1800 over 45% of the Spanish Empire's ~14 million people were non-Indian, and
over 20% mestizo or pardo. As European women arrived and elites stressed pure
bloodlines, the status of *castas* fell and became associated with **illegitimacy**;
yet hard work, courage, and (above all) the embrace of Iberian cultural norms could
still let a *casta* rise. The famous **casta paintings** of eighteenth-century Mexico
catalogued the mixtures as an idealized taxonomy; **sumptuary laws** tried to bar
free black and casta women from rich dress and jewelry to keep a visible boundary.

## Women in the casta order

Law and custom tightly **circumscribed women's lives** — elite and middle women
supervised by fathers then husbands, "decent" women unable to walk unescorted,
work, or visit a man alone without scandal; rebels could be confined in convents or
*recogimientos*. Only **widows** achieved real economic independence. Poor women,
many heading households (38% in late-colonial Buenos Aires, 45% in Ouro Preto),
worked of necessity and **dominated petty retail and the markets**. The
[[encomienda|family]] — biological plus the wider web of marriage and *compadrazgo*
(godparentage) — was the basic social unit; elite **marriage was an arranged
economic-political alliance** sealed by **dowry** and *arras*.

## Limitations and critiques

The casta system is contested. Older scholarship took the elaborate caste vocabulary
at face value; recent work (Cope, Katzew, Martínez, Rappaport — "the disappearing
mestizo") stresses its **fluidity, performativity, and the gap between legal
taxonomy and lived practice**, especially in cities and in boom times, where the
correlation of race and class was weakest (it was strongest in remote rural areas).
The system's afterlife — *mestizaje* ideology, persistent colorism — makes it a live
subject in modern Latin American identity. It is inseparable from the
[[black-legend|moral debate]] over colonial rule.
