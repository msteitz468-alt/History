## Definition and Scope

The Mālikī school (Arabic: al-madhhab al-Mālikī) is one of the four canonical Sunnī legal schools ([[madhhab]]) of [[sharia|Islamic law]]. It is named after Mālik b. Anas, the great Medinan jurist whose authoritative collection of legal norms, the *Muwaṭṭaʾ*, became the school's foundational text. The school achieved dominance in the Maghreb, North Africa, al-Andalus (Muslim Iberia), and across sub-Saharan West Africa, where it remains the dominant school to the present day.

## Founding and Medinan Roots

Mālik b. Anas (d. 179/795) embodied the Medinan scholarly tradition, which privileged the living practice (ʿamal) of the Medinan community — seen as the most authentic continuator of Prophetic practice — alongside ḥadīth and Quranic interpretation. This gave Mālikī jurisprudence a strong emphasis on communal consensus and local custom (*ʿurf*) as sources of legal authority, an emphasis visible in the school's later regional diversity.

Hallaq notes that in Mālikī law "no formal hierarchy of substantive law was articulated, but the absorption of fatāwā into the works of author-jurists was as prominent and systematic as that exhibited in the Ḥanafite school." Unlike the [[hanafi-school|Ḥanafī school]]'s explicit three-tier hierarchy of authority, Mālikī doctrine accreted through the fatwā and fiqh literature without a formally ranked system of precedence.

## A Distinctive Waqf Rule

Among the four Sunnī schools, Hallaq highlights one structurally significant Mālikī peculiarity: "The Mālikite school was, among the four schools, singular in disallowing a founder to designate himself a trustee" of a [[waqf]] endowment. George Makdisi argued that this prohibition contributed to the school's relative decline in Baghdad and discouraged Mālikī members from creating madrasa endowments — an important structural factor in the school's geographic contraction to North Africa and Iberia, where its dominance was already established independently of the madrasa system's political patronage dynamics.

## Presence in al-Andalus

In the courts of Muslim Spain (al-Andalus), Mālikī procedure was distinctively formalized: "In the Mālikite courts of Muslim Spain the presence of these experts (known as mushāwirūn) was a requirement" — referring to the jurist-advisors who sat in court alongside the qāḍī. These mushāwirūn functioned similarly to the [[mufti-and-fatwa|muftīs]] of the eastern tradition, providing authoritative legal opinions that bound the court's deliberation. Their formal mandatory presence in Andalusian Mālikī courts is a feature without precise parallel in the eastern schools. See also [[qadi-judiciary]].

## Practice Across the Four Schools

Despite school boundaries, practice on the ground in pre-modern Islamic cities was fluid. Studies of Ottoman Cairo's courts show that "it was not unusual for one person to buy a house one day according to Ḥanbalī law and get married next day according to Mālikī or Shāfiʿī law." Mālikī judges in 16th-century Cairo adjudicated marriage contracts that included stipulations formally associated with the [[hanbali-school|Ḥanbalī]] school. The school's persistence in Ottoman Egypt and Syria alongside the officially dominant [[hanafi-school|Ḥanafī school]] attests to the continued vitality of Mālikī communities even in Ottomanized urban environments.

## Under the Mamlūks

The Mamlūk sultans maintained the tradition of four chief justices in Cairo and Damascus, one from each school. "Each city had chief justices belonging to the four schools, and no Shāfiʿite hierarchy was developed in legal education and judicial [administration]." The Mālikī chief justice participated in the Dār al-ʿAdl hearings alongside the Shāfiʿī, Ḥanafī, and Ḥanbalī counterparts, where cases were tried before the sultan or his viceroy with the four schools represented.

## Geographic Spread and Legacy

The Mālikī school's dominance across North Africa, the Sahara trade networks, and West African Muslim communities (from Timbuktu to Hausaland) gave it a reach far beyond the Arabic-speaking heartland. This expansion occurred largely through Ṣūfī networks and scholarly migration along trans-Saharan routes, largely independent of the dynastic madrasa patronage that shaped school dominance in the eastern Islamic world.

## Historiography

The Mālikī school's western and African dimensions are less well covered in the Bibliotheca Alexandrina collection than the [[hanafi-school|Ḥanafī]] and [[shafii-school|Shāfiʿī]] traditions. The formative period is recoverable through the *Muwaṭṭaʾ* and companion biographical literature; the medieval North African and Andalusian tradition is moderately covered. The post-1500 West African Mālikī tradition falls in the collection's weak zone for sub-Saharan Africa. See [[hallaq-sharia-2009]].
