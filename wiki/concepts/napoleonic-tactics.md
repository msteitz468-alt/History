# Napoleonic Tactics

The set of practical battlefield methods employed by the armies of the Napoleonic era (c. 1792–1815), encompassing infantry formations (line vs. column, square), the role of light infantry and skirmishers, artillery employment, cavalry shock action, and the coordination of the three arms. 

Napoleonic tactics are often contrasted with both the linear tactics of the mid-18th century and the dispersed, fire-intensive tactics of the later 19th century. In practice they represented an evolutionary synthesis that privileged combined-arms effects, the offensive use of mass at the decisive point, and the exploitation of victory through pursuit.

## Core Elements

- **Infantry**: Tension between the column (for movement and shock) and the line (for fire); the increasing importance of light infantry in skirmish order to screen and harass; bayonet charges more often as a threat or mop-up than sustained melee.
- **Artillery**: Massed batteries (grand batteries) to soften enemy formations before infantry assault; mobile horse artillery support.
- **Cavalry**: Heavy cavalry for shock against infantry or other cavalry; light cavalry for pursuit, screening, and exploitation. Reality of charges frequently involved hesitation or breaking before contact.
- **Combined arms**: Successful actions coordinated artillery preparation, infantry pressure, and cavalry intervention at the critical moment.

## Historiography and Debates

The extent to which theoretical prescriptions (drill manuals, regulations) matched battlefield practice is a recurring question. Muir and others emphasize eyewitness testimony showing frequent deviation, friction, and the primacy of morale and cohesion. The "line vs. column" debate remains a classic point of discussion, with evidence that both were used situationally and that pure versions were rare.
