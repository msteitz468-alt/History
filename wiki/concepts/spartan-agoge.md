# The Spartan Agoge

The famous **state-organized upbringing** of Spartan citizen boys — the system of communal rearing,
physical hardship, discipline and competition that produced the Spartan soldier-citizen and became, in
the ancient and modern imagination, the emblem of Sparta's unique way of life.

## The Traditional Picture (emic)

In the idealized tradition (Xenophon's *Constitution of the Lacedaemonians*, Plutarch's *Lycurgus*),
boys were taken from their families at age seven and raised in age-grade "herds" under state
supervision: rigorous physical training, minimal food and clothing (encouraging stealth and endurance),
choral and musical education, organized fights, the toughening rituals at the altar of **Artemis
Orthia**, and the shadowy **krypteia**. The system was credited to the lawgiver **Lycurgus** and
presented as the changeless foundation of Spartan virtue, equality (*homoioi*, "the similars") and
military supremacy.

## The Revisionist Critique (etic)

**Nigel Kennell** (*[[kennell-spartans-new-history|Spartans: A New History]]*, and his earlier *The
Gymnasium of Virtue*) is the leading sceptic. He argues that the agoge as later sources describe it was
**not a timeless archaic survival but substantially a reconstruction** of the **Hellenistic and Roman
periods** — the term *agōgē* itself is not securely attested early — staged as a self-conscious revival
of an imagined Lycurgan past, partly for an audience of admiring outsiders and tourists at Roman Sparta.
This is one instance of the wider **"Spartan mirage"**: the idealized image of Sparta, transmitted by
external and late sources, standing in for a social reality that changed over time. How much of the
Classical-era upbringing the later system preserved remains debated.

## Significance

The agoge is central both to **how Sparta worked** (the production of disciplined citizen-soldiers and
the subordination of family to state) and to **how Sparta has been imagined** — from Plato's idealized
education to modern authoritarian and militarist appropriations of "Spartan" rearing. Distinguishing the
historical institution from the mirage is a key problem of Spartan studies.
