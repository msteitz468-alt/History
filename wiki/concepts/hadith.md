## Definition and Scope

A **hadith** (pl. ahadith) is a report of the words, acts, or tacit approvals of the Prophet Muhammad, considered as evidence of his sunnah (normative practice). The hadith corpus became the second source of [[sharia|Shari'ah]] law after the [[quran]], and its elaboration is inseparable from the development of [[fiqh]] (jurisprudence) and the formation of [[sunni-islam]].

Each hadith consists of two parts:

- **Matn** (the text body of the report): what Muhammad said or did.
- **Isnad** (chain of transmitters): the sequence of named individuals through whom the report reached the collector, ideally unbroken from a Companion of the Prophet through successive scholars to the current speaker.

Hodgson defines the isnad as a method not of historical authentication in the modern sense but of establishing religious homogeneity: "isnad criticism amounted to selecting those reports that were circulated by persons who respected the same sorts of authorities as did the critic" (Hodgson).

## Development — From Local Custom to Prophetic Reports

### Early Stage: Diverse Authorities

Originally, "hadith reports were cited on the authority of diverse prominent early Muslims" — Companions, early caliphs, the Medinan community at large. The chain of transmitters (isnad) did not necessarily lead back to Muhammad; it needed only to reach the authority regarded as relevant to the point in question. Local custom (sunnah as practiced in Medina, or in Iraq) carried comparable weight.

### Under al-Mahdi and al-Rashid (775–809)

Hodgson traces a decisive shift under the 'Abbasid caliphs al-Mahdi and al-Rashid: "it had become increasingly accepted that hadith reports traced back to Muhammad himself had precedence over reports traced merely to his associates or to the first caliphs." This shift implicitly adopted a Shi'i position that the decisions of the early caliphs were not in themselves binding.

### Al-Shafi'i's Insistence on Prophetic Hadith Alone (d. 820)

The jurisprudent al-Shafi'i made the decisive theoretical step: Prophetic hadith, and only such hadith, were truly authoritative as the second source of law. Moreover, their Prophetic origin must be demonstrated by a complete isnad running unbroken from Muhammad through every link to the current scholar. This position "liberated legal thinking from traditional limitations, giving full play to the ideals developed among the Piety-minded — in whose circles it was, that reports about the Prophet had been proliferating" (Hodgson).

### The Problem of Fabrication

Al-Shafi'i's requirement for unbroken Prophetic isnads created an obvious problem: most hadith in circulation had isnads that did not in fact run all the way to Muhammad. Hodgson describes what happened:

> "Some of the pious had little hesitancy at simply inventing isnads — and in fact the hadith reports themselves as well — in a good cause; for they assumed (quite explicitly) that whatever was true and of value for Muhammad's community must have been said by Muhammad, as an agent of Providence, whether it was actually recalled by anyone or not — or even whether it had actually passed from his lips."

This was not mere fraud in a cynical sense: the assumption was that the figure of Muhammad, as elaborated in pious circles, was a legitimate and necessary enlargement of the historical man. Al-Shafi'i's system was thus "founded on a pious fiction" — claiming to rest on the historical Prophet while actually resting on the communal construction of who Muhammad must have been.

### Isnad Criticism and the Canonical Collections

To limit the proliferation of fabricated reports, the 'ulama developed the discipline of **isnad criticism** (*'ilm al-rijal*, "knowledge of the men"). Scholars compiled biographies of transmitters, evaluated their reliability, and rejected reports whose isnad included known transmitters of doubtful character, memory, or doctrine.

The result was not strict historical authentication but a self-consistent body of doctrine recognizable to the Jama'i-Sunni scholarly community. Six canonical Sunni hadith collections emerged from this process, c. 850–900:

- **Sahih al-Bukhari** and **Sahih Muslim** — the most rigorously sifted; considered especially holy.
- Four others (Abu Dawud, Tirmidhi, al-Nasa'i, Ibn Majah) — allowed "weaker" reports for a wider legal basis.

Shi'i communities maintained their own hadith collections, drawing on reports transmitted from the imams of the 'Alid line rather than from Muhammad's Companions.

## The Hadith Corpus Beyond Law

The hadith corpus was "never merely a source for law." It acquired an autonomous doctrinal weight:

- Some hadith reports became so entrenched that they had to be "explained away" even when they conflicted with established Shari'ah positions.
- The corpus contained reserves of pious opinion that sometimes ran counter to the fiqh — Hodgson cites the work of R. Brunschvig showing how greatly the positions upheld in the corpus could differ from received legal attitudes.
- The doctrine of the **uncreated Qur'an** — that the Qur'an was not merely a created thing but God's own eternal speech — was supported by hadith and became a major devotional commitment of the Hadith folk (Ahl al-Hadith), who were willing to endure persecution (the Mihna under al-Ma'mun) rather than recant it.

## Hadith in Historical Scholarship

The 'ulama extended the isnad method to general historical writing. The historian Ibn-Jarir al-Tabari (d. 923) built his universal history on the same principle: verbatim reports with their isnads, presented without authorial synthesis. Hodgson offers a detailed analysis of Tabari's treatment of 'Uthman's death, showing how the arrangement and selection of contradictory verbatim reports itself carried a sophisticated historical argument inaccessible to the naive reader but legible to the subtle one. Tabari's method "demonstrates the sort of intellectual subtlety that the Shari'ah-minded methods made possible at their best" (Hodgson).

## Historiography

The historicity of hadith is the most contested question in Islamic studies.

- **Goldziher (1889–90)** and **Schacht (1950)**: argued most Prophetic hadith were back-projected fabrications; the isnad developed after the fact.
- **Motzki (2000s)**, **Schoeler**: partial rehabilitation — some early hadith strata show signs of genuine early transmission before the isnad requirement was formalized.
- **Hodgson's position** (implicit): the historical Muhammad and the figure of Muhammad elaborated in hadith are not identical; the hadith corpus represents a legitimate but consciously constructed religious tradition rather than reliable biographical evidence.

The collection's coverage is primarily through Hodgson; specialist hadith scholarship (Goldziher, Schacht, Motzki) is absent.
