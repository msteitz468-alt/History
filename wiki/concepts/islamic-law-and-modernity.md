# Islamic Law and Modernity: Hallaq's Conceptual Framework

Wael Hallaq's central thesis in *Sharīʿa* is that the encounter between [[sharia|Islamic law]] and modernity cannot be understood without first grasping a structural incompatibility between two fundamentally different systems of governance: the pre-modern [[sharia|Sharīʿa]] and the modern nation-state. This conceptual framework, laid out in chapter 13 ("The conceptual framework: an introduction"), is the analytical foundation for all subsequent historical analysis of what happened to Islamic law under colonialism and reform.

## The Core Argument

Hallaq's argument begins with the observation that the modern nation-state is not a neutral administrative vessel into which any legal content can be poured. It is a historically specific form of power with inherent structural requirements: centralization, monopoly over legislative sovereignty, bureaucratic uniformity, surveillance, and the production of the national citizen as a subject. The [[sharia|Sharīʿa]], by contrast, is a decentralized, jurist-driven, socially embedded system that operated outside and largely independently of political power.

When the two systems met — whether through direct colonization or through the "indirect colonialism" of hegemonic pressure on nominally independent states like the [[ottoman-empire|Ottoman Empire]] — the Sharīʿa was not simply reformed or adapted. It was structurally displaced, because the nation-state system, "which harnessed modern technology most efficiently," could not coexist with an autonomous rival lawgiver. As Hallaq argues: "there is virtually no problem or issue in this history that does not hark back to the conceptual, structural and institutional discord that exists between the thoroughly indigenous Islamic/customary laws, and the European-grown imports that were the inevitable concomitant of the nation-state."

## Six Structural Incompatibilities

Hallaq identifies six axes of incompatibility between the Sharīʿa and the nation-state:

**1. Competing machines of governance.** Both the Sharīʿa and the nation-state are "machines of governance" — systems for organizing society and resolving disputes. Because they occupy the same functional niche, they cannot coexist without one subordinating the other. Islamic law could tolerate administrative competition from the political sovereign but could not tolerate substantive juristic intervention; the nation-state, conversely, "had developed even less tolerance to legislative, administrative and bureaucratic competition."

**2. Rival lawgivers.** Both systems produce law. The Sharīʿa, even under the Ottomans — "the most state-like dynasty of Islam" — retained its overwhelming dominance over the substance of the law. The nation-state, by contrast, requires monopoly over legislation as a defining feature of its sovereignty.

**3. Competing claims to ultimate legal sovereignty.** In Islamic juristic theory, political government (*siyāsa*) "stood subservient to the Sharīʿa." The ruler served the law, not the reverse. The nation-state, by contrast, makes jural sovereignty its defining attribute: "A nation-state without jural sovereignty is no state at all."

**4. Centrifugal vs. centripetal legal orders.** The Sharīʿa operated horizontally: the referential authorities of the qāḍī were other qāḍīs and muftīs; appeals did not travel upward in a political hierarchy but were heard by the succeeding judge. The nation-state's jural system is "perforce hierarchical from within, and answers to an external state hierarchy that both sustains and envelops it."

**5. Transparency vs. ideological concealment.** The modern state, drawing on Marxist analysis (Abrams, Althusser), "hides the domination of one class over others" through its self-presentation as a neutral arbiter of the common good — "a triumph of concealment." The Sharīʿa, lacking a class agenda and serving no particular class, "did not develop the need to hide itself behind an impenetrable ideology."

**6. Grass-roots vs. top-down law.** Islamic law "is generated at the very social level on which it is applied." Jurists "emanate from the very society and societal culture that they serve." Nation-state law, however democratically framed, "is superimposed from a central height in a downwards direction, first originating in the mighty powers of the state apparatus and thereafter deployed … to the individuals constituting the social order."

## The Colonial Vector

Hallaq argues that the drive to colonize was primarily economic: "material profit was the prime motive," and colonial law was the template for economic domination. Commercial codes came first, opening markets to European terms of exchange. Penal codes followed, establishing "law and order" commensurate with colonial imperatives. But neither commercial nor penal reform could be sustained without the full apparatus of the nation-state:

> "It soon became obvious that to install a long-term and efficient mechanism for the economic exploitation of the colonies, the nation-state system, with all its legal arsenal, had to be exported as an essential first act."

The collapse of indigenous social and political systems also created a structural opening for local elites who — supported by the colonizers themselves — stepped into the power vacuum, producing the "dictatorships" and "unprecedented forms of patriarchy" that characterize what Hallaq calls the Third World aftermath of colonialism.

## The Indirect Colonial Case: Hegemony Without Conquest

Hallaq distinguishes direct colonization (India, Southeast Asia) from "indirect colonialism" — European hegemony exercised over nominally independent states like the [[ottoman-empire|Ottoman Empire]] and Qājār Iran. In the indirect case, economic pressure (unequal treaties, capitulations, the opening of markets) forced legal reform from within, producing the same structural outcome — the displacement of Sharīʿa by state law — without formal conquest. The Ottoman Tanzimat and Egyptian reforms are the primary cases analyzed in chapter 15.

## Historiographical Significance

Hallaq's framework is explicitly contra the "legal pluralism" literature, which documents surviving pockets of alternative law under state systems. Hallaq acknowledges legal pluralism but argues that it does not undermine his thesis: what matters is "the systemic and exclusive legal dominance of the state," not the survival of marginal exceptions. His framework also challenges scholarship that treats the codification of Islamic law as a form of "reform" or "modernization" — for Hallaq, codification is inherently a move of state capture, transforming a living hermeneutical tradition into a tool of governance.

The framework draws heavily on Foucauldian concepts of surveillance and discipline, Marxist state theory (Althusser's Ideological State Apparatus, Abrams on the state as "triumph of concealment"), and comparative historical sociology (Tilly, Mann, van Creveld on the rise of the modern state). It has been criticized for overstating the coherence and uniformity of the pre-modern Sharīʿa and for insufficient attention to Muslim agency in the reform process.
