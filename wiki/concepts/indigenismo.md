## Definition

Indigenismo (indigenism) is a non-indigenous intellectual, artistic, and state-policy current that places indigenous peoples and cultures at the centre of national identity, while remaining produced and controlled primarily by mestizo and creole elites rather than by indigenous peoples themselves. It is an **etic** analytical and political label for a movement that was largely **emic** to white and mixed-race urban society, not to the indigenous communities it claimed to represent.

Xavier Albó notes explicitly that Ch. 25 of the *Cambridge History of Native Peoples of the Americas* does **not** include a history of indigenismo, "so influential in literature, philosophy, and the arts," precisely because "it was mainly internal to white urbanites and mestizos" and does not reflect the indigenous perspective the chapter centres. This exclusion is itself a historiographical act: it marks indigenismo as distinct from indigenous political agency.

**Emic/etic distinction.** Indigenismo is etic in the sense that it was a project *about* indigenous peoples, conducted *by* non-indigenous actors. Where indigenous individuals participated — like the Quechua photographer Martín Chambi or bilingual writers like José María Arguedas — they did so as partial exceptions within a discourse that otherwise spoke *for* rather than *with* indigenous communities. Katarismo and CONAIE represent the break from this pattern.

## Variants

### Intellectual Indigenismo (Peru, Bolivia, 1900–1940)

The Cusco university rebellion of 1909 and the discovery of Machu Picchu (1911) created conditions for a new interest in pre-Columbian civilisation. Luis E. Valcárcel's *Tempest in the Andes* (1927) prophesied: "One day the men of the Andes will descend like the hordes of Tamerlaine." José Carlos Mariátegui sought a synthesis of Marxism and indigenous community structures. Peruvian historian Jorge Basadre called growing Indian consciousness "the most important phenomenon of Peruvian culture." But José María Arguedas criticised this indigenismo for emphasising "the wretched, mistreated Indian" without envisioning "Andean natives as the historic creators of a culture and a nation."

### State or "Official" Indigenismo (1940–1970s)

The First Inter-American Indigenist Congress at Pátzcuaro (1940) established a Mexico City headquarters (sponsored by the OAS) and national branches in many countries (Ecuador 1942, Colombia 1943, Bolivia 1945, Peru 1946, Chile 1968). The United States provided major funding. Official indigenismo was premised on the concept of the "Indian problem" — a deficit to be overcome by assimilation into dominant society — and was explicitly integrationist: "official indigenism, still predicated on the concept of the 'Indian problem,' tended toward a policy of assimilating indigenous peoples into the dominant society."

The Andean Mission (*Misión Andina*, 1950s), under UN auspices, operated in Ecuador, Peru, and Bolivia. In the 1960s "development decade" it aligned with community development, colonisation, and integrated rural development programmes.

In Peru, President Leguía (1919–1930) pursued a "false indigenism" (Thomas Davies's term): he supported the Tawantinsuyo Society enough to be named honorary president of its Sixth Congress, yet simultaneously established a conservative "Trusteeship of the Indian Race" to marginalise independent indigenous activism. In Bolivia, Saavedra's party was known as the "Cacique Party" in the countryside but massacred Aymaras at Jesús de Machaca in 1921. These cases illustrate the general pattern: official indigenismo served state interests in cooptation rather than indigenous interests in autonomy.

### Limitations and the Indigenous Critique

- Indigenous peoples "by and large could not present themselves and achieve full status as citizens in these circles without mediation by culturally privileged intellectuals."
- State indigenismo treated ethnicity as a problem of backwardness rather than a resource for political organisation.
- [[katarismo]] and [[south-american-indigenous-rights-movement|CONAIE]] explicitly defined themselves against indigenismo: indigenous peoples speaking for and governing themselves, not being spoken for by mestizo intellectuals or managed by state agencies.
- The ILO's replacement of Convention 107 (1957, assimilationist) with Convention 169 (1989, rights-based) represented the international institutional equivalent of this shift.

## Historiography

Indigenismo produced a large literary canon (Ciro Alegría, Jorge Icaza, José María Arguedas) and a significant body of social science. Anthropological critiques from the 1970s onward — and the indigenous movements themselves — reframed it as a colonialist project of benevolent control. Albó's refusal to include it in a chapter written from the indigenous perspective is itself a pointed historiographical statement, consistent with the advocacy stance of the *Cambridge History* chapter.
