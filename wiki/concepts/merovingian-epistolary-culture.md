# Merovingian Epistolary Culture

**Over 500 surviving letters reveal communication as essential infrastructure of the Merovingian world.**
Andrew Gillett's chapter (Oxford Handbook, Ch. 25) surveys letters sent within, to, and from Gaul between
the late fifth and mid-eighth centuries — his checklist totals **563 letters** — distinguishing the
"epistolary-collection habit" (what survives) from the "quotidian use of channels of communication" (what
was lost).

## Definition and Scope

Three forms of evidence survive: manuscript letter collections, letters embedded verbatim in other texts,
and narrative references to letters. By contrast with Egypt's thousands of documentary papyri, Gaul
preserves only elite and ecclesiastical manuscript transmission — so the surviving letters are a biased
sample. A manuscript copy omits "the address on the outside of a folded sheet… the seal… the *subscriptio*
in the sender's autograph hand." Delivery was always person-to-person, by a "simple carrier (*portitor*)"
or an "envoy (*legatus*) who would deliver a substantial oral message and perhaps also read out the actual
letter."

## The Main Collections

- **[[venantius-fortunatus|Venantius Fortunatus]]** — 11 *libri*: five prose letters and "more than sixty
  letters in verse," plus ~50 epistolary poems to Radegund and Agnes; "about a third of his verse letters
  are written to secular figures (with another third to his patron, [[gregory-of-tours|Gregory of
  Tours]])."
- **Epistolae Austrasicae** — 48 letters (late 5th–late 6th c.), the only Merovingian collection with a
  table of contents, spanning correspondents "from 14 Gallic bishops to two eastern emperors, two
  empresses, an exarch" — "an astonishingly broad range… across the Mediterranean."
- **[[columbanus|Columbanus]]** — 7 letters from Luxeuil and Italy.
- **Desiderius of Cahors** — two *libri* (15 sent, including to kings Dagobert I and Sigibert III; 21
  received), plus three rare letters by his mother **Herchenefreda** preserved in his *Vita*.
- **Boniface and Lull** — 150 letters, a multi-author institutional collection.
- **Epistolae Arelatenses** — 56 papal letters on the archbishopric of Arles. Cassiodorus's *Variae* also
  preserves two letters to [[clovis|Clovis]] (see [[merovingian-byzantine-relations]]).

## Communication as Infrastructure

Gregory of Tours's *Histories* (Books V–X) give "abundant narrative evidence" of routine letter use:
"kings who regularly issue letters of instruction to their senior military and civilian officials… and
also, regularly, to bishops," sending *epistolae cum auctoritate* to whole regions. Trial scenes (Hist.
X.19) reveal "the role of chancellery officials in providing authenticating signatures; the use of trained
scribes; and the maintenance of archival copies in shorthand," and Gregory describes access to the
*evectio publica* (public transport) for royal messages. The system also reached the Mediterranean:
[[brunhild|Brunhild]] used Austrasia's ties to Constantinople to lean on Pope Gregory I (who granted
Syagrius of Autun the pallium because the emperor Maurice supported Brunhild's wish). Letter-writing was
taught: **Marculf's Formulary** supplied model royal, seasonal, and recommendation letters, and the
Epistolae Austrasicae may have served as a diplomatic training collection.
