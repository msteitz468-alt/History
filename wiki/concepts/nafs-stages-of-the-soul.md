## Definition and Scope

The nafs ("soul," "self," "breath" — feminine in Arabic) in Sufi psychology designates the lower self, the seat of base instincts and appetites, what the biblical tradition calls "the flesh." It is the primary obstacle on the path to God, the internal opponent that must be trained and eventually transformed — not killed — so that even its energies serve the divine end.

The concept has a direct Quranic basis. Three Quranic expressions provide the foundational taxonomy:

1. **An-nafs al-ammāra biʾs-sūʾ** — "the soul commanding to evil" (Sūra 12:53): the nafs in its unreformed state, driving the person toward sin and base appetite. This is the starting condition of the spiritual path.

2. **An-nafs al-lawwāma** — "the blaming/reproaching soul" (Sūra 75:2): approximately conscience; the nafs that monitors and criticizes its own failures. The Quranic God swears by this soul, indicating its dignity.

3. **An-nafs al-muṭmaʾinna** — "the soul at peace/rest" (Sūra 89:27–28): the purified soul, addressed by God: "O tranquil soul! Return to your Lord, well-pleased and pleasing [to Him]." This is the goal of the Sufi's ethical labor.

The Sufis called the struggle against the nafs "the greater Holy War" (al-jihād al-akbar), citing a Prophetic tradition: "The worst enemy you have is [the nafs] between your sides" (L 12). This internal combat takes precedence over any external warfare.

## The Nafs as Phenomenology

The early sources treat the nafs as something almost externally perceptible, not merely a psychological category. Several stories tell of mystics seeing their nafs outside the body:

- Taking the form of a black dog that wanted food but had to be trained and sent away
- Emerging from the throat as a young fox or a mouse (Hujwīrī, H 206)
- Appearing as a disobedient woman (the gender of nafs is feminine in Arabic)
- Manifesting as a restive horse or mule that must be kept hungry and mortified before it can carry its rider to the goal

Other classical imagery: the nafs as Pharaoh (self-centered ruler who claimed divinity and was drowned), as Abraha (intruder into sacred space), as a snake (which can be rendered harmless by the spiritual master's power, as an emerald blinds a serpent in folk belief), as a pig (ʿAṭṭār and Sanāʾī: those who obey pig-like nature will themselves be changed into pigs).

The most precise image, conveying the Sufi's actual program, is the nafs as a disobedient camel or horse: the point is not destruction but domestication. "The lower faculties are not to be killed, but trained so that even they may serve on the way to God."

## The Greater Holy War: Methods of Training

The chief instruments for training the nafs are:

**Fasting (ṣawm)**: "Hunger is God's food by which He quickens the bodies of the upright." The "alchemy of hunger" was first articulated by Shaqīq al-Balkhī (Khurasanian school), who claimed forty days of constant hunger could transform the darkness of the heart into light. The classical prescription: qillat al-ṭaʿām ("little food"). Some mystics practiced the ṣawm dāwūdī (eating one day, fasting the next) to prevent the body from habituating to either condition.

**Sleeplessness (sahar)**: Spending nights in prayer rather than sleep; "the eye is weeping instead of sleeping." The ideal: qāʾim al-lail ("spending nights upright in prayer"). The counterintuitive reward: Shāh Kirmānī did not sleep for forty years, was finally overcome by sleep — and in sleep saw God, who told him: "You found Me by means of those nightly vigils: if you had not sought Me there, you would not have found Me here" (Hujwīrī, H 138).

**Seclusion (chilla / arbaʿīn)**: Forty-day retreats of intense prayer and isolation, derived from Moses's forty-day fast (Sūra 7:138). The dark room is treated as a tomb, the frock as a shroud. The sheikh supervises closely because the seclusion can produce spiritual danger for weaker adepts — excessive self-focus rather than God-focus.

**Little speech (qillat al-kalām)**: "Little food, little sleep, little talk" — the classical threefold formula.

**Continuous activity**: "Leisure (farāgh) is an affliction." It is better to dig purposeless ditches than to spend a moment in idleness that gives the nafs opportunity.

**Opposing the nafs's appetites**: The fundamental axiom — the adept acts exactly contrary to the nafs's wishes. Every indulgence, however small, constitutes an opportunity for the nafs to regain control.

## The Nafs's Subtlety: The Spiritual Danger of Piety Itself

The most important warning in Sufi psychology concerns the nafs's capacity to corrupt even devotion:

> "The nafs has a rosary and a Koran in its right hand, and a scimitar and a dagger in the sleeve." (Rūmī, Mathnawī 3:2554, cited from Dhūʾn-Nūn)

Even constant acts of worship or prayer can become a pleasure for the nafs; the mystic must therefore break every kind of habit, for otherwise "his nafs will overcome him in a subtler way" (N 98). "The pleasure derived from works of obedience should be avoided, for that is fatal poison" (N 83). This observation — that spiritual practice itself can feed the self — was the insight of Muḥāsibī's psychological analysis in his extensive treatment of riyāʾ ("hypocrisy/ostentation") and is one of the most sophisticated contributions of Sufi thought.

The path is not from sin to virtue but from visible sin through virtue to the complete transparency of the self before God. At this final stage, the nafs al-muṭmaʾinna is not merely quiet but actively aligned with the divine will:

> "Aslama shayṭānī — My Satan has become a Muslim and does whatever I order him." (Prophet Muhammad, as reported in the Sufi sources)

## Ikhlāṣ: Absolute Sincerity as the Nafs's Opposite

The primary virtue that confronts the nafs is ikhlāṣ — "absolute sincerity," the complete elimination of self-interest from every act. "A prayer without ikhlāṣ is of no avail; a religious thought that is not born out of this sincerity is meaningless, even dangerous." The mystic who acts with perfect ikhlāṣ forgets even his own virtuous deeds in his exclusive orientation toward God — he cannot calculate reward, for calculation implies a self-interested agent behind the act.

The logical extreme of this demand produced the malāmatiyya ("those who are blamed"): mystics who deliberately concealed their virtuous deeds and even performed apparently reprehensible acts in public in order to ensure that no ostentation could attach to their piety (see [[early-sufism]]).

## The Nafs and the Structure of the Path

In the broader framework of the [[maqamat-and-ahwal|maqāmāt]], the stages of the nafs function as a developmental psychology underlying the outer sequence of stations. The wayfarer begins with the nafs commanding to evil (nafs ammāra); progresses, through repentance and striving, to the blaming nafs (nafs lawwāma); and may reach, through grace and sustained effort, the nafs at peace (nafs muṭmaʾinna). This schema is not static: the nafs can relapse from any stage, and the sheikh's role is precisely to detect and correct such regressions.

The nafs is ultimately not annihilated but transfigured. The goal is not its destruction but the point at which it "becomes a Muslim" — fully surrendered, fully aligned, its energies no longer opposed to but continuous with the movement of love toward God. At this point, the distinction between self-mortification and self-expression dissolves: every act of the purified nafs is, simultaneously, an act of God through the transparent human instrument.

## Historiography

The nafs doctrine is one of the earliest and most consistent elements of Sufi thought, present from the first generation of Baghdad and Khurasanian masters. Muḥāsibī's Riʿāyat ḥuqūq Allāh is the founding psychological text. Hujwīrī's Kashf al-Maḥjūb (pp. 202–206) provides systematic treatment. Ghazzālī's Iḥyāʾ ʿulūm al-dīn (esp. the quarter on the "Destructive Vices") is the most comprehensive medieval synthesis.

Modern scholarship has treated the nafs primarily as a moral psychology, but the phenomenological descriptions (nafs appearing as animals) have attracted interest from scholars of Islamic demonology and popular religion. The relationship between the Sufi nafs doctrine and ancient Near Eastern and Hellenistic soul-tripartition (Platonic / Neoplatonic) remains an open question in the historiography.
