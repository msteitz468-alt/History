## Definition and Scope

The proto-qāḍī is an etic analytical category designating the early Islamic judicial official whose office had not yet differentiated into the specialized, legally bounded institution that the mature qāḍī represented from the mid-second/eighth century onward. The term captures a transitional figure — simultaneously arbitrator, administrator, tax official, police chief, story-teller, and conflict resolver — who cannot be reduced to any single modern bureaucratic role.

Hallaq defines the limit of the category chronologically: "Whatever change this office subsequently underwent was by no means sudden. From the ninth decade of the Hijra [ca. 700s CE], the qadi's office increasingly was limited to conflict resolution and legal administration." Prior to that watershed, the proto-qāḍī's appointment typically bundled together functions that later generations would regard as categorically distinct.

## Functions and Character

The proto-qāḍī's role combined:

1. **Tribal arbitration** — continuing the pre-Islamic role of the *ḥakam* (arbiter), the proto-qāḍī settled disputes among Arab tribal groupings within and across garrison towns
2. **Financial administration** — overseeing military stipends (*ʿaṭāʾ*), estate divisions among soldiers' heirs, and collection functions
3. **Policing** — appointment as chief of police (*shurṭa*) was common and explains why proto-qāḍīs had enforcement personnel at their disposal from an early date
4. **Story-telling and public instruction** — an activity rooted in mosque-based religious narrative
5. **Legal dispute resolution** — adjudicating conflicts among Muslims, and by the later first century H among non-Muslim communities as well

The distinction between the proto-qāḍī and the mature qāḍī is illustrated by the fate of ʿAbd al-Raḥmān al-Jayshānī, judge of Egypt who, "sometime during the 130s/750s, was immediately reappointed there as a tax-collector" after dismissal from the judgeship — confirming the interchangeability of these roles at the administrative level.

## Epistemic Authority and Legal Knowledge

A defining feature of the proto-qāḍī was that legal expertise was not a prerequisite for appointment. Hallaq notes that the Egyptian judge Ghawth b. Sulaymān "is said to have been a shrewd and seasoned qāḍī (i.e., he understood people and was highly skilled as a conflict mediator) but to have lacked a mastery of law as a technical discipline." Governors and caliphs would sometimes inquire prior to appointment whether a candidate was a legal specialist — suggesting it was not assumed. This contrasts sharply with the classical-period ideal that a judge must possess *fiqh*.

The proto-qāḍī relied on caliphal precedent, the *sunan* of Companions, and *raʾy* (considered opinion) as the principal sources for his decisions. He would consult the caliph directly on difficult cases, and seeking caliphal opinion was a frequent practice at least through the mid-Umayyad period. This consultative relationship reflected the fact that the legal specialists (*fuqahāʾ*) had not yet crystallized as an epistemic authority superior to the ruler.

## Chronological Development

The proto-qāḍī phase covers roughly 20–80 H / 640–700 CE, with a transitional zone through the early second century H / 720s CE. Key markers of the transition away from the proto-qāḍī type include:

- **Caliphal appointments displacing gubernatorial ones**: The caliph Sulaymān b. ʿAbd al-Malik (r. 96–99/714–717) "seems to have been the first caliph to appoint judges directly from Damascus, thereby initiating the policy of removing from local governors the authority to make such appointments." This centralization was consolidated under the Umayyads and brought to completion by the early Abbasids.
- **Narrowing of functions**: The Umayyad judge Tawba b. Nimr (115–120/733–737) "refused to interfere in tribal disputes" and returned such matters to tribal chiefs — an explicit rejection of the proto-qāḍī's traditional arbitral role.
- **Geographical expansion**: Judges were appointed to new towns — Alexandria, Ḥimṣ, Khurasan — as Muslim settlement spread beyond garrison centers, bringing the office into contact with new communities and new legal complexity.

## Court Procedures in the Proto-Qāḍī Phase

Even before specialization, proto-qāḍīs developed embryonic court procedures that later became standard:

- **Written records**: Sulaym b. ʿItr (before 60/679) is credited as the first judge in Egypt to keep written records of rulings, after parties disputed the terms of a verdict. The practice was irregular and piecemeal through the end of the first century.
- **Court witnesses (*shuhūd ʿadl*)**: The use of witnesses to attest to facts and claims was inherited from pre-Islamic practice and Quranic prescription; the proto-qāḍī employed them without yet a settled procedural doctrine.
- **Inter-judge written communication**: By 100/718, correspondence between judges (*kitāb al-qāḍī ilā al-qāḍī*) had become a "fairly established practice," already attested by court witnesses.

## Historiography

Hallaq's reconstruction relies primarily on prosopographical-biographical sources (*akhbār* literature): al-Kindī's *Akhbār Quḍāt Miṣr* and Wakīʿ's *Akhbār al-Quḍāt* for Egyptian and Iraqi judges respectively. These sources present problems of anachronism — later norms are sometimes projected backward — but they preserve granular detail about individual judges unavailable elsewhere. Hallaq treats them critically, noting that some accounts (e.g., Bligh-Abramsky's reading of the first caliphal appointment) involve overlooking earlier evidence.

The concept of the proto-qāḍī implicitly contests Schacht's thesis that early Islamic law was primarily a caliphal product, by showing that the locus of legal elaboration was migrating from the administrative apparatus toward private scholarly circles from the very beginning of the judicial institution.
