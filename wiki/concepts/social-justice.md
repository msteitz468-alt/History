# Social (Distributive) Justice

Both an **emic** political ideal — the demand that society's distribution of income, wealth, and opportunity conform to some standard of desert or need — and, since Hayek, the target of one of the twentieth century's most sustained **etic** critiques. This page records the concept's career and Hayek's attack in *Law, Legislation and Liberty* II: *The Mirage of Social Justice* (1976) as an attributed position, alongside the standing counters. The wiki does not adjudicate.

## The term's history

Hayek dates the modern usage to "apparently not much older than a hundred years" before his writing (i.e., mid-19th century). He credits **John Stuart Mill** with fusing "social" and "distributive" justice — society should treat all equally well who "have deserved equally well of it … This is the highest abstract standard of social and distributive justice" — and argues Mill was "wholly unaware" that this desert standard, unlike his other four senses of justice (which govern individual conduct), describes a state of affairs no one's conduct produces, and so "leads straight to full-fledged socialism." Behind Mill lies the older search for substantive fairness in exchange: Hayek notes that "a thousand years of vain efforts to discover substantively just prices or wages were abandoned" when the late schoolmen concluded such formulae were empty and that competitive prices reached without fraud, monopoly, or violence "was all that justice required" — the tradition from which Locke drew the classical-liberal principle that "it is the way in which competition is carried on, not its result, that counts."

## Hayek's mirage argument (attributed)

The volume's title thesis: "in a society of free men (as distinct from any compulsory organization) the concept of social justice is strictly empty and meaningless." The steps:

1. **Category restriction.** "Strictly speaking, only human conduct can be called just or unjust … To apply the term 'just' to circumstances other than human actions or the rules governing them is a category mistake." Nature "can be neither just nor unjust."
2. **Society is not an agent.** Government (an organization) can act justly or unjustly; society (a spontaneous order) cannot: "if it is not the intended or foreseen result of somebody's action that A should have much and B little, this cannot be called just or unjust." The complaint against market outcomes treats society as "the new deity to which we complain and clamour for redress."
3. **Origin in anthropomorphism.** The demand arises from interpreting "the results of the spontaneous ordering of the market … as if some thinking being deliberately directed them" — the same primitive personification Hayek indicts throughout the trilogy ([[concepts/constructivist-rationalism]]).
4. **Meaningful only in a command order.** "'Social justice' can be given a meaning only in a directed or 'command' economy (such as an army) in which the individuals are ordered what to do." Hence enforcing it converts the [[concepts/catallaxy|catallaxy]] into an organization: "So long as the belief in 'social justice' governs political action, this process must progressively approach nearer and nearer to a totalitarian system" — "the Trojan Horse through which totalitarianism has entered."
5. **Value is not merit.** Prices and wages signal what people ought to do, "not so much to reward people for what they have done"; there is "strictly no such thing" as "value to society" (the match-magnate vs. the artist). Hayek criticizes even capitalism's popular defenders (Smiles, Alger, Sumner) for claiming the market rewards the deserving, and admits a "real dilemma" in how far to encourage the young to believe effort guarantees success.
6. **Rule-of-law incompatibility.** Against Radbruch's claim that a socialist community could be a *Rechtsstaat*: "The distributive justice at which socialism aims is thus irreconcilable with the rule of law," since a target distribution requires commanding particular people to particular tasks ([[concepts/rule-of-law]]).
7. **Equality of opportunity**, extended beyond facilities government provides anyway, "becomes a wholly illusory ideal, and any attempt concretely to realize it apt to produce a nightmare."
8. **The atavism thesis.** The craving for social justice is "an attempt to satisfy a craving inherited from the traditions of the small group but which is meaningless in the Great Society"; socialism "is simply a re-assertion of that tribal ethics whose gradual weakening had made an approach to the Great Society possible," manifesting with nationalism as "the two greatest threats to a free civilization." Its sociological career runs from genuine concern for the destitute to "simply the slogan used by all groups whose status tends to decline," with trade unions "the first of such groups" to clothe sectional demands in its aura. The international test exposes the limit: unions loudest domestically "are regularly the first to reject such claims raised on behalf of foreigners."
9. **Rights need duty-bearers.** In the ch. 9 Appendix: "Nobody has a right to a particular state of affairs unless it is the duty of someone to secure it." His verdict on the 1948 Universal Declaration's social and economic articles: a universal right assuring "to the peasant, to the Eskimo, and presumably to the Abominable Snowman, 'periodic holidays with pay' shows the absurdity of the whole thing"; the new rights "could not be enforced by law without at the same time destroying that liberal order at which the old civil rights aim."

His hoped-for outcome: that the term be recognized as "intellectually disreputable, the mark of demagogy or cheap journalism which responsible thinkers ought to be ashamed to use."

**What Hayek concedes.** The critique is not a rejection of all redistribution: "There is no reason why in a free society government should not assure to all protection against severe deprivation in the form of an assured minimum income, or a floor below which nobody need to descend" — provided it operates outside the market's remuneration mechanism. See [[concepts/welfare-state]] for his parallel aims/means distinction.

**Hayek and Rawls.** Unexpectedly, Hayek claims "no basic quarrel" with Rawls's *A Theory of Justice* (1971), reading Rawls as agreeing that selecting specific distributions as just must be "abandoned as mistaken in principle" and objecting mainly to Rawls's retention of the label "social justice." Hayek even offers his own veil-of-ignorance-style test: "the best society would be that in which we would prefer to place our children if we knew that their position in it would be determined by lot." Rawls scholars have generally judged Hayek's reading too irenic — the difference principle licenses far more institutional redistribution than Hayek's floor — making the "agreement" itself a small interpretive controversy.

## Counters and critiques (attributed)

- **The rules themselves are chosen.** Critics (including Rawlsians and institutionalist economists) reply that while particular market outcomes are unintended, the *framework* — property law, inheritance, corporate form, monetary policy — is deliberately maintained and could be otherwise; predicating justice of the basic structure is therefore no category error. Hayek's own concessions (legislative correction of class-biased case law in Vol. 1; the minimum floor) are cited as admitting the point.
- **Moral force of holdings.** G. A. Cohen and others argue that foreseeable, avoidable aggregate outcomes of maintained institutions are morally attributable even if unintended by any single actor.
- **The atavism thesis** is speculative evolutionary psychology; sympathetic critics accept the knowledge-problem economics while rejecting the tribal-instinct diagnosis as unfalsifiable.
- Catholic social teaching (from Taparelli through *Rerum Novarum* to the present) uses "social justice" for the ordering of institutions toward the common good and denies that the term reduces to command-economy distribution.
