## Definition

Competitive submission denotes the strategic pattern, visible in the Ieyasu-Hideyoshi relationship (1584–1598) and in Ieyasu's handling of subordinates like Date Masamune, wherein a powerful actor performs formal deference to a superior rival in order to:

1. avoid a destructive conflict that neither side can win cleanly,
2. preserve assets and momentum for a future in which the relationship of dominance may shift,
3. accumulate credit with the nominal superior while simultaneously constructing a position of future strength.

The proverb cited repeatedly in Sadler's account: "Ieyasu won the Empire by yielding" ("Ieyasu makete tenka wo toru"), and its variant "Ieyasu won the Empire by retreating," are the Japanese formulations of this concept. Both refer to a pattern of behavior over a sixteen-year period that eventually resulted in Tokugawa supremacy after Hideyoshi's death.

## Emic vs. Etic Status

The proverb itself is emic in the sense that it appears in Tokugawa-period chronicles as a recognized observation about Ieyasu's method. Whether Ieyasu consciously followed a theory of "yielding" or simply made rational decisions in specific situations that retrospectively form a pattern is not answerable from the sources. Tokutomi Soho's interpretation — that Ieyasu's submission was a calculated long-term strategy — is an etic analytical frame applied to the emic proverb.

## Key Instances (from Sadler chapters 11–21)

**Submission on terms (1586)**: When Ieyasu agreed to visit Hideyoshi at Osaka, he did so only after securing three specific conditions protecting his succession, negotiating an extraordinary concession (Hideyoshi's mother as hostage), and laying detailed military contingency plans before departing. The submission was real — he publicly deferred to Hideyoshi before the assembled daimyo — but the terms were entirely Ieyasu's.

**The Kantō transfer (1590)**: Ieyasu accepted the exchange of his ancestral five provinces for the eight Kantō provinces without public objection, even though the exchange removed him from his strategic position on the Tōkaidō and placed him behind the Hakone barrier. He immediately began developing Edo as a power base. The proverb "Ieyasu won the Empire by retreating" explicitly refers to this moment.

**The Date Masamune affair**: Ieyasu coached Masamune on exactly how to respond to Hideyoshi's threatening summons — the performance of weeping submission while making it subtly clear that his retainers might resist — and then intervened with Hideyoshi on Masamune's behalf, collecting Masamune's "fervent gratitude" while appearing to serve Hideyoshi's interests. This is competitive submission in its purest form: the senior subordinate manages a conflict between his superior and a junior party to benefit all three while tilting the credit toward himself.

## Limitations and Critiques

The concept risks teleological reasoning: it makes Ieyasu's eventual supremacy look inevitable and therefore makes his every concession look like a calculated step toward that outcome. In reality, many of Ieyasu's submissions were genuine responses to superior force, and the outcome was not certain. Hideyoshi could have forced an open rupture, or died with a competent adult heir, or arranged Ieyasu's succession differently.

The concept also understates the degree to which Hideyoshi was a willing participant. Hideyoshi understood Ieyasu's strength and chose not to destroy him at several points (notably after Nagakute, and when Honda Tadakatsu dared the Toyotomi host at Ryusenji). Hideyoshi was not fooled; he was also managing a relationship he found useful. The submission was mutual in its calculated quality.

`contrasts_with:` The Hōjō model — the alternative strategy of defiance and reliance on geographic defense — which ended in extinction at Odawara (1590). The Hōjō's failure illustrates the limits of the non-submission option when the opponent has overwhelming resources.

`analogous_to:` The strategic behavior of Tokugawa Japan toward Western powers in the mid-nineteenth century — initial resistance followed by calibrated accommodation, while building internal capacity — though this analogy should not be pressed too far.

## Historiography

Tokutomi Soho's multi-volume biography of Ieyasu (Kinsei Nihon Kokuminshi) is the major source for this interpretive frame, and Sadler draws on it explicitly throughout. The formulation has been influential in both Japanese and Western historiography of the Sengoku period. Feminist and social historians have complicated the frame by noting that the "yielding to win" narrative focuses entirely on elite male actors and ignores the costs of continued warfare imposed on populations who had no strategic option of submission.
