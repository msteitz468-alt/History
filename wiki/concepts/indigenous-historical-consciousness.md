# Indigenous Historical Consciousness

## Definition

Indigenous historical consciousness names the diverse ways Native North American peoples have constructed, transmitted, and authorized knowledge of the past — through origin narratives, clan histories, prophetic cycles, place-based memory, winter counts, [[wampum]] records, oratory, ritual reenactment, and other genres — often on terms that do not map cleanly onto Euro-American chronology or documentary history. In *The Cambridge History of the Native Peoples of the Americas* Volume I Part 1, Peter Nabokov’s opening chapter makes this subject a first-class counterpart to Euro-American historiography (Washburn & Trigger, ch. 2).

There is no single “Indian viewpoint.” As Alfonso Ortiz put it, each tribe, band, or community has its own sovereign history. David Beaulieu contrasts a European pluralistic “tree with branches” model with Navajo Ruth Roessel’s “forest of many different and varied trees” of independent tribal historiographies. Nabokov’s bibliographic essay noted (as of 1996) that no book-length survey yet covered the diversity of historical consciousnesses among Native North American peoples.

## Emic and Etic

**Emic:** Communities distinguish sacred/origin time from pragmatic event memory; some knowledge is restricted (ritual specialists, age-grades); recounting may be morally consequential. Specialized roles include Pima boys trained four nights in smoke-talk origin narratives (Russell); Navajo ritual singers (*hataalii*) who recite scrupulously memorized mythistories in curing ceremonies; and Ojibwa (Anishinabe) *kanawencikewinini* (“preserve man”) who read birchbark scroll mnemonics (Vennum).

**Etic:** Historians and anthropologists analyze these genres as historical sources with their own evidentiary rules, not as “myth” opposed to “history.” Anthropologists and folklorists have recovered indigenous theories of history under labels including traditional history (Evans-Pritchard), folk history (Hudson), ethno-ethnohistory (Fogelson), and “historicity” (Ohnuki-Tierney’s preferred term for collective understanding of history, avoiding the implication that consciousness is always conscious).

Nabokov opens with a 2 January 1971 Doris Duke Oral History interview in Tuba City, Arizona: an elderly Navajo man, shown McGregor’s Southwestern Archaeology map of Paleo-Indian Beringia routes, said other peoples might have come that way but “us Navajos came a different way,” offered cicada-emergence and corn-kernel origin narratives, refused to explain “medicine” secrets for fear of falling to pieces, and dismissed Desert Culture projectile-point sequences as invented theory—attributing Clovis/Folsom points to horny toads as elders had seen. The translator linked long-term secrecy of origin knowledge (rather than the Treaty of 1864) to Navajo strength as the largest nation/reservation in the United States. Nabokov reads Navajo and Euro-American modes of communicating distant origins as “genres passing in the night.”

## Features of Native Historical Storytelling

Drawing on Douglas R. Parks (Arikara) and related material, Nabokov lists recurring features: lessened concern with dates/periods and “timelessness” with displacement of narrative elements; nondevelopmental organization (e.g. typology by animal-beings); focus on the interpersonal even for high-moment events; concern for accuracy via oral repetition; and humor as performative spice. Loretta Fowler’s Wind River Arapaho consultants criticized structural-ecological anthropology as missing the point: Arapahoes see history as a mythological process that is a conceptual framework for interpreting and shaping social action, not necessarily related to observers’ recorded events.

American Indian “mythistory” (McNeill’s term) is often cloaked as sacred utterance: promiscuous public speech can be perilous because stories remain imbued with world-creating powers, requiring taboos—especially dense in Navajo culture. History may function as a scarce “limited good” (Foster); some contemporary Native cultural leaders formally regloss seemingly secular political history as “religious property” to safeguard intellectual heritage from outsiders.

Fieldworkers find American Indian historical narratives, behaviors, and symbols falling at many points between idealized poles of chronology (history) and cosmology (mythology). Indian languages show few monolithic notions of the past—e.g. Navajo *atk'idaa* means experiences stacked through time yet also includes sacred healing power (Pratt). Scholarly dichotomies of cyclical vs linear time (Eliade, Hultkrantz, Berger, Diamond, Lévi-Strauss’s cold/hot societies, Highwater) oversimplify; Nabokov argues such binaries disservice both sides and ignore internal Euro-American extremes from fundamentalism to Hayden White relativism.

## Genres and Temporal Categories

Nabokov uses William Bascom’s triad as a clumsy but helpful outsider tool: **myths** (sacred, nonhuman characters, earlier world, absolute truth); **legends** (secular or sacred, today’s world, human characters, factual/historical); **folktales** (secular, out of place/time, human and nonhuman, fictional entertainment). Each genre can repository some historicity; genres bend under historical stress and produce hybrids.

Indigenous temporal categories often double as genres:
- **Koyukan** *Kk'adonts'idnee* (“Distant Time”) explains origins of all beings.
- **Zuni** *chimiky'ana'kowa* (“The Beginning”) marks genesis as historical truth.
- **Tillamook** sequence: myth age, era of transformations (trickster-transformer), period of true happenings (Elizabeth Jacobs).
- **Nunamiut** distinguish *Itchaq amma* (ancient), *Ingalagaan* (in-between), *Ipani* (living memory), placing “true history” (*koliaqtuaq*) often in the ancient era.
- **Tlingit** *cagun* / *ha cagun* (“our cagun”) encompasses sib/clan origin and destiny—including totemic animal encounter, powers and prerogatives, place in the universe, and fate of unborn descendants (de Laguna).
- **Tewa** traditions repeatedly use “when it has been four times” for simultaneous time and space; outside the four sacred mountains, units of twelve are normative because genesis migration took twelve steps (Ortiz).

Citing Firth’s “plasticity of myth” and van Baaren on adaptability without automatic secularization, Nabokov recounts Fred Eggan’s comparison of two Hopi accounts of a mid-1850s Hopi–Navajo clash forty years apart: the earlier was detailed and accurate; the later fell into a mythic canon, sanctioned Hopi norms, chartered cultural-territorial divides, and was “on its way to becoming tradition and myth.”

## Geographical Memory and Place

Vine Deloria, Jr. diagnoses Indian historicity as spatially located versus Christian temporal interpretation: “No one can say when the creation story of the Navajo happened, but everyone is fairly certain where the emergence took place.” Landscape functions as book (Binghams); Chumash sites as story display; Hopi travertine dome emergence; Hidatsa sacred-arrow landing; Eastern Pomo myths retaining vanished landmarks (McLendon); Crow/Hidatsa return to birth and village sites; Northwest Coast crest acquisitions as place-histories. Pictographs and petroglyphs function as Young’s “metonyms of narrative” locking mythistoric accounts to place (e.g. Katcina Cult entry into Pueblo country; postcontact Canyon del Muerto horse-and-cross scenes; Makah three-masted schooner at Ozette).

Eastern Choctaws revere two Nani Waiya sites ~1.5 miles apart near Pearl River headwaters outside Philadelphia, Mississippi: a Woodland Period earthwork “mother mound” as migration destination and a natural hill with cave of emergence. Nabokov argues this dual creation/migration charter protects territorial claims as both incoming “chosen people” and aboriginal “first people,” possibly reflecting seventeenth-century multi-group ethnogenesis ([[choctaw-nation]]; [[north-american-mound-building]]).

## Contact, Prophecy, and Colonial Critique in Native Genres

Creation myths incorporate Europeans: Okanagan Harry Robinson’s twins-and-paper story explains White capacity to lie and future power/loss; Acoma Edward P. Hunt’s origin twins make Nautsiti mother-path traits of Whites who disappear east; Mandan John Brave’s Lone Man/First Creator tale derives red-headed White men from maggots. Prophecy (Percy Cohen; Jarold Ramsay’s “retroactive prophecy”) dramatizes pre-Contact events as prophetic of White consequences—epidemics, liquor, money, armies, laws, missionaries, schools—including New England seaboard Christianized reformulations (Simmons), Spokane volcano prophecy, Zuni decay visions, Jicarilla Apache flood end-of-world by fire, and Smohalla’s mid-1880s Wanapum resurrection prophecy.

Divergent first-contact narratives reverse emphasis: Scollon & Scollon contrast English impersonal framing of 1715 French–Chipewayan contact with personalized Fort Chipewayan narratives centered on Fallen Marten and Cree–Chipewayan credit for discovering the French. Charles Hudson reports Anglo-American Carolina versions casting Catawbas as amicable land-givers, while Catawba accounts portray ancestors as too nice to avaricious Euro-Americans ignorant of reciprocity etiquette. Margaret Williamson reinterprets the Pocahontas–John Smith episode as two cultures vying to assimilate each other—[[powhatan-confederacy]] strategies of adoption and arranged marriage to fold Europeans into the confederacy. Keith Basso’s Western Apache *‘agodzaahi* (“historical tale”) “shoots” morals into targets and preserves the “why” of historical action rather than particular “what.”

Kenneth Morrison reads northeastern Algonquian (Abenaki, Maliseet, Micmac) Windigo/Gluskabe tales accommodating Christian cosmology as intimate-encounter history; Madronna Holden finds Coast Salish Trickster/Transformer satire of missionaries and employers. Robert Brightman finds Missinippi Cree historical thought juggling progressivist and anti-modern storylines—hairy-heart ancients, trade-era trader duplicity, dwarf stories indicting modern overcrowding—not simply cyclical/ahistorical. Paul Radin’s Winnebago French-contact legend welds dissynchronous elements to charter the Decora mixed-blood dynasty. Catherine McClellan found Tlingit and Athabaskan high tolerance for temporal discontinuity, so some 1898 Gold Rush accounts credit both a European prospector and mythic Wealth Woman.

## Oratory, Ritual, and Material Mnemonics

Native historicity is enacted in formal oratory: Tlingit memorial speeches enumerate pedigrees via *at.aow* heirlooms and *shuka* generations; Cherokee Keetowah *Eloh'* migration account of seven clans was printed bilingually in 1896; treaty oratory (e.g. Medicine Lodge 1867) typically opened with each speaker’s history before addressing White impact (Fixico); Hopi Yukeoma’s 1911 White House plea rested entirely on emergence myth and land-right stones against schooling—“myth was the first, last, and only diplomatic argument a Hopi could advance.”

Ritual reenacts migration and ethnogenesis: Ojibwa *mitewiwin* songs/processions map Lake Superior “resting places” (Vennum); Crow Tobacco Society (*basussua*) adoption rituals recapitulate ethnogenesis from Hidatsa split via brothers’ vision-quests. Public commemorations include Pueblo tricentennial reenactment of the 1680 Pueblo Revolt relay-runner network and the Lakota Bigfoot Memorial Ride (322 miles Cheyenne River to Pine Ridge) for the 1890 Wounded Knee centennial on a *washigila* “wiping the tears” prototype.

Material culture carries multi-scale history (Nabokov via Braudel-scale framing): longue durée sanctified relics (ossuaries, bundles, kivas from pithouses, Mandan Okipa lodge); conjuncture trade goods elaborating Kwakiutl potlatch cosmology (Sahlins) and Micmac copper pot’s four “lives” (Martin); surface-oscillation mnemonics including Yakima string balls, Ojibwa birchbark migration charts, Arikara stick-sheaf genesis, Plains winter counts (*waniyetuyawapi*) and Blackfeet *sinaksin*, Pima calendar sticks (annals from 1833; Covered Wells stick ending 1936 reservation fencing). Haudenosaunee [[wampum]] belts and condolence canes formalize diplomatic-historical pledges (see that page). Ceremonial regalia could resist imposed lifeways (Plains Ghost Dance shirts/dresses; Oto Faw Faw frock coats; Pomo Bole Maru regalia; California Ghost Dance earthlodges).

## Oral Tradition as Evidence — Debates and Case Studies

Folkloric legends sit in scholarly debate: Robert H. Lowie (1915, 1917) declared oral traditions historically worthless (“I cannot attach to oral traditions any historical value whatsoever under any conditions whatsoever”); later Ewers shared skepticism of oral tradition as substitute for the written word. Ethnohistorians contend collective memories of even acculturated communities can complement written reconstructions using corroborative methods (Vansina). Nabokov counters that Lowie’s own Lemhi Shoshone and Assiniboine tales—told when political confidence hit rock bottom—record attitudinal history and contests for cultural hegemony rather than disinterested chronology.

Positive case studies in Nabokov’s survey:
- **Gordon M. Day (1959):** St. Francis Abenaki accounts of Rogers’ Rangers’ 1759 village destruction preserved accuracy ~200 years not by fixed-form memorization but by content’s importance to Abenaki identity ([[seven-years-war-1756]]).
- **Andrew O. Wiget:** Hopi narratives of the 1680 Pueblo Revolt match Spanish documents on motivation, friary shape, number of priests killed, and date after 300 years, while carrying shared values.
- **W. D. Strong:** Naskapi trickster-cycle monsters with large heads/trunks/teeth as possible “dim but actual tradition” of mammoths—distinct from fossil-rationalizing myths; Nabokov stresses such memories are more interesting as commentary than documentation.

## Autohistory, Fiction, and Synthesis

Georges E. Sioui (Huron/Wendat) proposed “autohistory” studying correspondences between Amerindian and non-Amerindian sources, replacing evolutionary Western bias with sacred-circle interdependence. Joe Sando (Jemez) argued Western history should grant parallel plausibility to in-situ creation oral history. From the 1970s, Native fiction incorporated historicities: McNickle, Silko’s *Ceremony*, Welch’s *Fools Crow*, Erdrich’s North Dakota trilogy, and Momaday’s *The Way to Rainy Mountain* (1969), which multivocally braided autobiography, objective history, and Kiowa ethnic tradition—modeling modern Indian history “in the old Indian way.”

Nabokov’s synthesis (via H. Stuart Hughes on cultural symbols): Indian senses of the past are structured to highlight selected meanings and preferable social processes; high value is placed on maintaining Indian conceptual autonomy, sometimes requiring retroactive enhancement of “facts,” personalizing the past and warning moral consequences for the “history of the future,” without necessarily jettisoning accuracy. The past is like a collective dowry/cultural capital requiring synthesis, accretion, and refurbishment—and non-Indian historians must weave Native historicities into bedrock accounts.

Washburn & Trigger (ch. 2) note a related theoretical risk: claims of an irreconcilable traditional “core” of Native values surviving massive lifeway change (Martin; Sioui) may honor Native values yet, in extreme idealist form, minimize rationality in ways Native people may find denigrating. Ethnohistorians need a middle ground between extreme rationalism and extreme idealism (Bailey identified the problem decades earlier).

## Relation to Ethnohistory

Pairs with [[ethnohistory]] and [[native-north-american-historiography]]. Where ethnohistory often began from European documents to reconstruct Native pasts, attention to indigenous historical consciousness insists that Native genres are themselves historical practice. Nabokov argues even enlightened ethnohistory that depicts Indians as active agents (Sandos & Burgess; Calvin Martin’s “bifurcated Indian”) is only half the solution; the next step is braiding Native American historical philosophy and metahistorical frameworks into many-voiced accountings for Indian pasts before, during, and after Euro-American relations, while Cohn insists colonial structures must not be ignored.

## Limitations

- Extreme cultural relativism (every account equally valid, incommensurable) is rejected by the CHNPA co-editors even as they value alternative histories; interpretations can be judged by internal coherence and correspondence with factual evidence.
- Translation and performance context are easily lost on the page.
- Not all communities share the same genres or openness to publication; some knowledge is restricted as sacred or as “religious property.”
- AIRFA (1978) consultants’ dichotomy of “world” religions (commemorative) vs “tribal” religions (continuing) risks delegitimizing documentably historical syncretic Native spiritual expressions (David White).
- Museums and first-person presentation (e.g. NMAI’s Pathways of Tradition omitting dates to avoid linear timelines) raise their own issues of evidence and audience.
