# Early Medieval Jewelry

## Definition and Scope

Early medieval jewelry (c. A.D. 400–1000, with focus on Migration Period and Viking Age contexts) encompasses personal ornaments produced by Germanic, Scandinavian, Anglo-Saxon, and related "barbarian" societies. Techniques and styles derived heavily from Roman methods but developed distinctive forms such as bracteates, square-headed brooches, and garnet cloisonné work. Jewelry served both decorative and status-signaling functions, often produced in specialized workshops.

## Key Techniques (from archaeological evidence)

**Construction:**
- Fabrication: mechanical manipulation and joining of sheets of metal by hammering, folding, and soldering.
- Casting: two-piece molds used rather than ancient lost-wax; metal melted in crucibles and poured; rough edges filed and polished. Bronze or silver pieces often coated with silver or gold.
- Used crucibles with residue and broken molds found at workshop sites such as Birka.

**Decorative techniques:**
- Chasing or engraving with pointed tool to displace or gouge metal.
- Stamping or die-impressing with relief design.
- Repoussé: designs hammered from the reverse.
- Punches for embellishment.
- Filigree (wire work): patterns of plain or decorative beaded wires soldered to surface. Wire produced by strip twisting, block twisting (early), or drawn through draw plate (from seventh–ninth centuries in northern Europe).
- Granulation: small spheres of gold or silver soldered onto surface (produced by heating small pieces until surface tension forms spheres; difficult to solder accurately but impressive en masse).
- Enameling and inlays: cloisonné (materials set into small cells fabricated by soldering upright metal strips); garnet cloisonné common on Merovingian jewelry. Millefiori glass (colored rods fused and sliced) used e.g. in Sutton Hoo shoulder clasps.
- Glass beads produced in workshops (e.g. Ribe).

Materials: Gold, silver (often from Arabic coins by later periods), copper, bronze, iron for functional pieces. Bone, walrus ivory, glass (Rhineland), amber, semiprecious stones (quartz, rock crystal, jet, garnet). Garnets came via Roman trade.

## Geographic Spread and Workshops

Workshops known from Scandinavia (Helgö, Birka, Ribe, Hedeby). Techniques spread across early medieval Europe with Migration Period peoples and Viking networks. Central designs on Scandinavian Migration period gold pendants (bracteates) were stamped with dies.

## Interaction with Other Processes

Jewelry production was closely tied to trade (precious metals, garnets, glass), elite status display, and craft specialization at emporia. It continued Roman technological traditions while innovating in wire drawing and polychrome effects. Glittering effects (filigree + granulation) were impressive by firelight.

## Limitations and Critiques

Archaeological evidence (artifacts, tools, workshop debris) is primary; historical sources limited. Many conclusions drawn from examination of finished objects. Post-2004 research (e.g. more precise provenancing of garnets, experimental replication) has refined understanding of supply chains and individual workshop styles.
