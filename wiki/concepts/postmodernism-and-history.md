# Postmodernism and History

The application of post-structuralist literary and philosophical theory to the
discipline of history: the claim, in its strong form, that the past is accessible only
as "text" or "discourse," that historical facts are constructed rather than discovered,
and that no interpretation can be preferred to another on evidential (as opposed to
moral, aesthetic, or political) grounds. Primary source for this page: Richard J.
Evans, *In Defence of History* (1997) — a committed critic, not a neutral surveyor
(see [[postmodernism-and-historical-knowledge]] for the dispute itself).

## Intellectual Genealogy (etic reconstruction)

- **Saussure**: the relation of signifier to signified is arbitrary (dog/chien/Hund),
  though language remains a coherent system of differentiation.
- **Derrida** radicalized this: meaning shifts with every utterance — language is an
  "infinite play of significations" with no "transcendental signified"; nothing exists
  outside text/discourse. Historians' belief that they rationally discover the past is
  "logocentric" illusion.
- **Barthes** (1968): written history is "an inscription on the past pretending to be a
  likeness of it, a parade of signifiers masquerading as a collection of facts";
  footnotes and quotation produce only a "reality effect."
- **Foucault**: truth and knowledge are products of power, not cognition; each age's
  dominant "discourse" frames what can be thought; history is "a fiction of narrative
  order imposed on the irreducible chaos of events in the interests of the exercise of
  power."
- **Hayden White**, *Metahistory* (1973): historians "prefigure their field of enquiry"
  with the same literary tools as novelists (analyzing Michelet, [[rankean-historicism|Ranke]],
  Tocqueville, Burckhardt alongside Balzac); events are not intrinsically tragic or
  comic but "emplotted" as such; "there are no grounds to be found in the record itself
  for preferring one way of construing its meaning rather than another."
- Radical popularizers: **Keith Jenkins** ("postmodernity is precisely our condition:
  it is our fate"; professional history as "expressions of how dominant ideologies
  currently articulate history"), **Frank Ankersmit** ("Autumn... has come to Western
  historiography"; content is "a derivative of style"; historians should study other
  historians rather than an unknowable past), **Patrick Joyce** (social history
  dissolved because "the social" is indistinguishable from its discursive construction),
  **Elizabeth Deeds Ermarth** (time itself a fictional construct).

## Claims at Stake

1. **Sources → texts.** The Rankean primary/secondary distinction (formalized by
   Momigliano: original authorities = eyewitnesses and contemporary remains) is
   attacked as fetishizing documents; sources reflect only other texts, so history
   collapses into literary study (Spiegel's summary of the threat).
2. **Facts → constructions.** Facts are "constructed artifacts no different in
   cognitive origin than any made thing" (Nancy Partner's summary of the position).
3. **Causation → discourse.** Causal analysis is "merely an element in the
   arbitrarily-constructed discursive formation of professional historiography"
   (Jenkins); see [[causation-in-history]].
4. **Objectivity → power.** Interpretations win because their advocates hold power,
   not because they fit evidence; see [[historical-objectivity]].

## Contributions Conceded Even by Critics

Evans (1997), while attacking the strong claims, credits postmodernism with: forcing
methodological self-consciousness and reflexivity; closer attention to language and
the "surface patina" of documents; liberating history from social-science determinism
and restoring individuals to history (see [[microhistory]] — Ginzburg's Menocchio,
Natalie Zemon Davis's *Return of Martin Guerre*, Darnton's *Great Cat Massacre*);
reinstating narrative and good writing (Schama's *Citizens*, Figes's *A People's
Tragedy*); opening new subjects — memory, commemoration, gender, the symbolic
dimensions of power; and enriching the study of nationalism by shifting focus from
social bases to constructed national identities. Lawrence Stone, an opponent, conceded
the linguistic turn "taught us to examine texts with far more care and caution than we
did before."

## Limitations and Critiques

- **Self-refutation** (Paul Boghossian's argument, endorsed by Evans): if a claim and
  its opposite can both be true relative to perspectives, postmodernism must admit
  realism is as true as postmodernism; "facts about truth independent of particular
  perspectives are presupposed by the view itself." Likewise, sequential time is
  presupposed by the very term "post-modern."
- **The Holocaust limit case.** Evans: "Auschwitz was not a discourse. It trivializes
  mass murder to see it as a text. The gas chambers were not a piece of rhetoric."
  White himself retreated in *Probing the Limits of Representation* (ed. Friedländer),
  conceding the facts of the Holocaust "closed off" certain emplotments — abandoning
  his central tenet. Total relativism supplies no criterion against Holocaust denial
  (Lipstadt; see [[historical-objectivity]]) or against fascist and Stalinist histories
  generally (Christopher Norris's warning about "a massively falsified consensus").
- **The de Man affair (1987)**: revelation of Paul de Man's 180 articles for a
  Nazi-controlled Belgian paper (1940–42), including antisemitic pieces, and Derrida's
  contradictory defence (infinite interpretability in theory, but one "correct" reading
  of de Man in practice) — Alan Spitzer's verdict: both sides appealed to common
  standards of factuality; new documents transformed the present "irrespective of the
  views they held in the present."
- **Sociological deflation** (Evans, explicitly speculative): postmodernism as
  compensation for academics' lost status in the Reagan–Thatcher era, placing "total
  intellectual power in the hands of the academic interpreter"; its trajectory follows
  the standard arc by which insurgent methods (cliometrics, social history) settle into
  sub-specialisms — "a revolt... reduced to a style" (George Melly's phrase).

## Emic/Etic Note

"Postmodernism" is largely an etic label; several of those tagged with it denied the
term's coherence. Evans's own usage lumps a spectrum from moderate (LaCapra, whose
"dialogue with the past" model Evans adopts) to what he calls "vulgar postmodernism"
(Jenkins). Sympathetic accounts (Jenkins, Munslow) charge Evans with caricature; the
2000 edition's afterword responds. This page records the debate through Evans's lens
and should be balanced when a postmodernist-side source is ingested.
