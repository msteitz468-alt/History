## Definition

Universal hylomorphism is the philosophical doctrine, most fully articulated by [[ibn-gabirol]] in his *Fons Vitae* (*Fountain of Life*), that **everything** apart from God — including supposedly immaterial entities such as intellect and soul — is composed of matter (*hyle*) and form (*morphe*). The term combines the Greek words for these two Aristotelian categories ([[adamson-philosophy-islamic-world-2016]]).

The doctrine is called "universal" because it extends the Aristotelian matter-form analysis beyond the physical world to which Aristotle himself had applied it, applying it instead to all created reality, spiritual and material alike.

## The Argument

Ibn Gabirol's argument for universal hylomorphism begins with an analysis of predication and divine unity:

1. Whenever we describe a subject, we implicitly posit a duality: the subject and the property attributed to it
2. Such a duality is equivalent to matter (subject) and form (property)
3. God is absolutely one; therefore no such duality applies to Him
4. But *everything else* involves such duality — therefore everything else has matter and form

The argument then extends downward through the hierarchy of being. Even incorporeal entities like intellect and soul are multiply determined — they have various forms — and wherever there are multiple forms there must be a matter to receive them (*Fons Vitae*, 13, 217). Ibn Gabirol provides fifty-six arguments for the existence of a spiritual world of incorporeal substance below God (*Fons Vitae*, Book III), and argues that these substances must have matter.

## Matter Without Extension

A crucial qualification: matter as understood by Ibn Gabirol is not the same as body. Bodies have extension in the three spatial dimensions — they have quantity. Matter, by contrast, is what underlies all forms, including quantitative forms (*Fons Vitae*, 26). It is what remains in a Aristotelian thought experiment: strip away all forms from a thing; the bare subject remaining is matter (14). Such matter is abstract and unperceivable — you would never encounter a heap of it lying in the middle of a room — and its abstractness is precisely what makes it plausible that there is matter in spiritual things: they too have a subject that receives various forms.

## Roots in Plotinus

Adamson notes that universal hylomorphism is not as original as it might appear: Plotinus had already spoken of the nascent intellect — the simple effect that the One produces before it becomes Intellect proper by turning back toward the One — as a kind of matter, a *potentiality* that receives the Platonic Forms. Ibn Gabirol makes this "tentative suggestion" of Plotinus into an explicit and fully articulated doctrine.

## Reception

**In the Latin West**: The *Fons Vitae* was translated into Latin in the twelfth century, and "Avicebron" (as Ibn Gabirol was known — his Jewish identity was unsuspected) was notorious in the Latin scholastic tradition for this claim. Thomas Aquinas, Bonaventure, and others cited and refuted him on the immateriality of angels. If universal hylomorphism were correct, angels (spiritual substances) would have matter, making them material in some sense — a conclusion Aquinas found unacceptable.

**In the Jewish tradition**: Later Jewish readers were uneasy with the *Fons Vitae*'s lack of biblical content; it was hard to tell from the text alone what religion its author held. [[judah-hallevi]] saw Ibn Gabirol's universalist philosophical system as a potential threat to Judaism from within.

**In Kabbalah**: The Neoplatonic framework of ibn-gabirol, including his language about divine unity and the hierarchy of spiritual substances, was absorbed into [[kabbalah]].

**Among Christian Aristotelians**: Universal hylomorphism was decisively rejected. Aquinas argued that intellectual substances (angels, human intellects) must be purely formal — pure form without matter — to account for their capacity for universal knowledge.

## Significance

Universal hylomorphism represents the most radical extension of the matter-form analysis in medieval philosophy. It simultaneously: (a) preserves God's absolute uniqueness by excluding Him alone from the hylomorphic analysis; (b) provides a continuous metaphysical account of all created reality from pure intellect to terrestrial body; and (c) creates a framework in which even spiritual things are "material" in a technical sense, making the concept of matter much more abstract and general than Aristotle intended.
