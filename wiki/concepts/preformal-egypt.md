# Preformal Egypt

Barry Kemp's term ([[sources/kemp-ancient-egypt-anatomy-2018|*Anatomy of a Civilization*]],
Ch. 3) for the phase and register of Egyptian culture **before the codified Pharaonic
canon dominated** — the self-organizing, locally varied votive and architectural practice
that the court [[concepts/great-tradition-little-tradition|Great Tradition]] gradually
overlaid but never fully erased. It is one stage in Kemp's stylistic periodization of cult
architecture: **Preformal → Early Formal → Mature Formal** (New Kingdom) **→ Late Formal**
(Thirtieth Dynasty–Roman). Preformal is the "before" against which
[[concepts/egyptian-formalism|Egyptian formalism]] is defined.

## Character

Preformal material is marked by **small, heterogeneous, individually deposited votive
objects** — figurines of humans, animals and birds — and architecture of informal,
non-canonical plan. Crucially, this material "in no way reflects" the standardized divine
iconography of the later pharaonic pantheon: the votives at the early temple of Horus at
Hierakonpolis, for instance, do not correspond to the formal deities the later record
assigns there.

## The Site Evidence

- **Elephantine**: a preformal shrine tradition persisted continuously from the Early
  Dynastic Period through the Old Kingdom — nearly a thousand years (c. 3085–2117 BCE) —
  with a small group of craftsmen "retaining forms and techniques over... the entire first
  six dynasties."
- **Medamud**: an Old Kingdom polygonal brick enclosure with twin oval mounds, winding
  corridors and a possible sacred grove — "the most striking example of Preformal
  architecture from Egypt," and important precisely because it sits *well within* the
  historic period.
- **Coptos colossi**: ~4.1 m limestone figures bearing predynastic symbols — a non-formal
  sculptural line that "could have been... developed further into a tradition of somewhat
  surreal renderings," but was superseded by the court's literal naturalism.
- Comparable votive deposits at **Tell Ibrahim Awad, Tell el-Farkha, Abydos, Hierakonpolis**.

## Significance

The persistence of Preformal practice for roughly a third of Egyptian history shows that
the formal canon spread "only through fitful royal patronage," not a general programme —
and that beneath the uniform surface of [[concepts/egyptian-formalism|formal art]] a
self-defining [[concepts/great-tradition-little-tradition|Little Tradition]] of religious
spontaneity continued. When elements of it surface later under official patronage (the
domestic god Bes, the Late Period sacred-animal cults), Kemp reads them as folk culture
made explicit.
