# Pan-Africanism

**Pan-Africanism** is the idea and movement asserting the **solidarity of all African and African-descended
peoples** — and, in its political form, the goal of African unity and liberation from colonial and racial
domination. Documented for the wiki through the **UNESCO [[gha-vol7-colonial-1880-1935|General History of
Africa, Vol. VII]]**, it is the connective ideology behind [[african-nationalism|African nationalism]] and
[[decolonization|decolonization]].

## Origins in the diaspora

Pan-Africanism arose first in the **African diaspora** of the Americas and the Caribbean, among
intellectuals confronting slavery's legacy and racism: figures like **Edward Blyden**, **Henry Sylvester
Williams** (who convened the first **Pan-African Conference**, London 1900), **W. E. B. Du Bois** (organizer
of the **Pan-African Congresses** from 1919), and the mass movement of **Marcus Garvey** ("Africa for the
Africans," the UNIA). It found cultural expression in **Négritude** (Senghor, Césaire, Damas) — the
affirmation of Black cultural identity and dignity.

## Toward African liberation

Over the twentieth century the movement's centre of gravity shifted **from the diaspora to the continent**.
The **5th Pan-African Congress (Manchester, 1945)** — attended by **Kwame Nkrumah**, **Jomo Kenyatta**, and
others — turned Pan-Africanism decisively toward **African independence**, and its veterans led the
liberation of their countries. After independence it took institutional form in the **Organization of
African Unity (1963)** — though here it split between **Nkrumah's** vision of immediate political union and
the more gradualist, sovereignty-preserving line that prevailed (and which kept the colonial borders; see
[[decolonization]]).

## Significance and tensions (contested)

Pan-Africanism supplied African nationalism with a **transcontinental solidarity and ideological depth**,
and it remains a live ideal (the African Union). But it has always been **contested**: between political
union and looser cooperation, between a racial/diasporic and a strictly continental definition of "Africa,"
and between the rhetoric of unity and the reality of the nation-states that decolonization produced. The
**Italian invasion of Ethiopia (1935)** — the violation of Pan-Africanism's great symbol — was a
radicalizing moment for the whole movement.

## The cultural renaissance (GHA VIII)

The **UNESCO [[gha-vol8-since-1935|General History of Africa, Vol. VIII]]** (Section V, chs. 17–22) devotes
unusual attention to **socio-cultural change** — the cultural face of African self-assertion that accompanied
political liberation:

- **Modern literature** flowering as a vehicle of African voice and critique — **Chinua Achebe** (*Things
  Fall Apart*, 1958), **Wole Soyinka** (the first African Nobel laureate in literature, 1986), Ngũgĩ wa
  Thiong'o, and the Francophone **Négritude** poets (**Senghor**, Césaire, Damas);
- the politics of **language** (writing in European vs. African languages — Ngũgĩ's turn to Gikuyu; the
  spread of Swahili and Hausa as supra-ethnic languages);
- **religion** (the continued growth and Africanization of Christianity and Islam, and independent churches);
- **the arts, philosophy, and education** as fields of decolonizing the African mind.

This cultural renaissance is, in the volume, the deeper substance of Pan-Africanism — the reclaiming of
African dignity, identity, and creativity after colonial denigration (cf. [[orientalism|Said's]] critique of
the colonial gaze).
