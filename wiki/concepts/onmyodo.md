## Definition and Scope

**Onmyōdō** (陰陽道, "the Way of Yin and Yang") was a Japanese system of cosmological divination, calendrical science, astrology, and ritual derived from Chinese yin-yang (*in-yō*) theory but developed into a distinctively Japanese institutional and practical form. It was administered through the **Bureau of Yin and Yang** (*Onmyōryō*) and provided the framework within which the Japanese court interpreted natural phenomena, managed time and space, and sought protection against cosmic threats.

Unlike the Chinese original, which kept divination and astronomy in roughly equal balance across large bureaucracies, the Japanese version strongly emphasized divination over astronomical observation, and was far smaller in scale: where the Chinese bureau employed approximately 1,413 people, the Japanese bureau by 720 had only eighty-nine officers — and its initial staff were all of Chinese or Korean descent.

## Institutional History

The Bureau of Yin and Yang was reorganized by the Yōrō code of 718. It was governed by a director responsible for astronomy, calendar, and interpretation of natural irregularities (*i*), who reported celestial anomalies secretly to the emperor. Under four administrative aides worked:
- Six **Masters of Yin and Yang** (*onmyōji*), specializing in divination (*uranai*) and geomancy (*fusui*)
- Scholars (*hakase*) supervising four major fields: Yin and Yang, Calendar, Astronomy, and Time

The Bureau was **disbanded in 820** under the early Heian government, which chose instead to rely on Shingon and Tendai esoteric Buddhism for many functions the bureau had previously performed. By 842 (the Jōwa Incident, marking Fujiwara ascendancy), the government requested Buddhist monks to perform rituals that would previously have been the domain of onmyōji.

Despite this, the traditions of yin-yang practice survived and were absorbed into:
- The ritual practices of the **Abe** and **Kamo sacerdotal lineages** (who remained hereditary specialists)
- Esoteric Buddhist practice (especially Tendai and Shingon)
- The world of shrine ritual (purification rites, directional taboos)

## Core Practices

### 1. Directional Taboos (*Kata-imi*) and Alternatives (*Kata-tagae*)

From the time of the Jōgan reformulations (869–71), practices of directional taboo proliferated. It was believed that celestial forces determined spatial zones that should not be crossed at certain times or in certain directions. Aristocrats planning travel, construction, or breaking of earth consulted specialists (*onmyōji*) to determine whether spatial-temporal correlations were favorable.

The palliative of **kata-tagae** (directional alternative) involved spending a night at a different location before proceeding to one's intended destination, thus "resetting" the directional matrix. This elaborate practice "gradually degenerated" into a social game involving aristocratic hospitality and entertainment, and was "more or less abandoned toward the end of the Heian period" (CHJ, ch. 8).

### 2. Stellar and Planetary Rituals

From the middle of the ninth century, interest in astrology intensified dramatically at court. Abnormal stellar movements were interpreted as signs of impending state-level calamity, triggering rituals by Tendai and Shingon specialists.

One major practice was **hommyōshō**: the identification of a person's "fate star" (*hommyō*) — a celestial body holding sway over individual destiny. Originally limited to the emperor, it spread throughout society. Debate over the proper identification method (birthday vs. birth year) was adjudicated in 961 by Kamo no Yasunori (yin-yang specialist) against a rival Buddhist interpretation. Eventually the seven stars of Ursa Major replaced the five planets as the primary referents, and rites on one's birthday to one's "personal" star of Ursa Major became widely practiced.

The most popular stellar rites concerned the **Polar Star** (Hokuto, Ursa Major), which the government had prohibited in 796 but which returned forcefully during the Heian period. Esoteric Buddhism incorporated these rites through the cult of the bodhisattva Myōken (Sōnshō-ō), promising longevity to those who performed the associated rituals.

### 3. Calamity Rites (*Kasai Matsuri*)

From 960 — the year fire first destroyed the imperial palace — the Masters of Yin and Yang began performing rites to placate calamitous forces. Documents from the Abe sacerdotal lineage reveal that the pillars of imperial palace buildings were decorated with swords bearing inscriptions combining Chinese Taoist imagery (dragons, tiger, Polar Star, Ursa Major, emblems of Lao-tzu) with requests for protection from the four directional forces. Some swords were of Korean origin. This shows that "within the imperial palace alone absolutely all available forms of cultic practice were used: yin and yang practices, Taoism, 'Shinto' rituals colored by Korean shamanism, and the entire panoply of rituals offered by the Taimitsu and Tomitsu forms of Esoteric Buddhism" (CHJ, ch. 8).

### 4. Era-Name Changes and Ritual Deflection

A characteristic response to catastrophic events was the symbolic changing of the year-era name (*nengō*). Two types existed: positive changes marking auspicious portents, and negative (protective) changes aimed at deflecting impending catastrophe. This strategy "kept the Fujiwara regents in control" but also meant that thaumaturgists "in some way controlled the aristocrats."

## Relationship to Chinese and Buddhist Systems

Onmyōdō drew on several Chinese traditions that the Japanese government officially did not condone (particularly Taoism), but which entered Japan through Buddhist transmission. Almost all Indian and Chinese astronomy and astrology reaching Japan arrived via Buddhist monks, especially of Shingon and Tendai lineages. The result was that:
- The Japanese did not use the term "Taoism" — Taoist practices became part of Esoteric Buddhist praxis and Shugendo
- The **Sukuyōdō** ("Way of Lodgings and Planetoids"), an Indo-Chinese astrological synthesis, became a primarily Buddhist matter
- Yin-yang practices infiltrated shrine purification rituals through the sacerdotal lineages (Nakatomi, Urabe, Abe, Kamo)

## Key Specialists and Lineages

The Abe and Kamo families became the hereditary lineages of onmyōdō specialists. The most famous practitioner was **Abe no Seimei** (921–1005), whose father Abe no Haruaki wrote the document concerning the protective swords at the imperial palace (the document is quoted in the CHJ). Kamo no Yasunori was a major figure in the 961 debate over hommyōshō identification.

## Significance

Onmyōdō represents a distinctive Japanese synthesis: Chinese cosmological science filtered through Buddhist institutional channels, applied to the immediate political and existential anxieties of Heian court life. It provided an interpretive framework in which natural disaster, political crisis, and individual fate were all legible as products of cosmic forces that could be identified, predicted, and — crucially — ritually managed.
