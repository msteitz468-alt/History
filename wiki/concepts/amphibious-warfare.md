## Definition and Scope

Amphibious warfare in its hardest form is the **opposed landing** — projecting
ground combat power from the sea onto a defended shore. Allan R. Millett's study
of the interwar American, British, and Japanese experiences distinguishes this
from the long-established practice of landing where there is no resistance. By
World War I, all three naval powers shared "a similar basic concept for
successful landing: land where there is no opposition from ground forces." The
nineteenth-century pattern — the British in Egypt (1882) and China (1900), the
Americans at Santiago and Manila (1898), the Japanese at Port Arthur — relied on
unopposed disembarkation, where "the principal danger in such an operation was an
attack upon the invasion force by the opposing navy." The interwar conceptual
leap was to ask whether a landing force could "capture a defended base" — could
"come ashore prepared to fight." As Millett frames it, "All three nations …
required amphibious capability, but the United States alone identified the need to
conduct an opposed landing." This is a leading case of [[military-innovation|military innovation]]:
a doctrine and a force built, in peacetime, for a mission no one had yet executed.

## The Shadow of Gallipoli

The Allied expedition to the Dardanelles in 1915 was "the most ambitious
operation of the war" and "also its most celebrated failure." Most observers drew
the conclusion that opposed landings were impossible: "Tactical pundits claimed
that the slaughter of the British 29th Division on 'V' and 'W' beaches proved that
amphibious assaults could not prevail against prepared defenses armed with machine
guns and artillery." Yet, Millett notes, World War I "also provided evidence that
amphibious landings were not necessarily a forlorn hope" — the German assaults on
Courland and Oesel in the Gulf of Riga, and the 1918 Zeebrugge raid. The
divergence in lessons is the crux of the concept's contested character. Britain
read Gallipoli as confirmation of futility; the US Marine Corps, in contrast, read
it as "a laboratory for identifying and correcting the operational flaws" — a set
of solvable problems rather than "a fated Attic disaster." The Japanese Army,
"having also studied the Gallipoli operation," concluded "that the army could no
longer rely principally on the navy to organize amphibious operations" and that
future landings must be faster and self-supporting.

## United States: The Success Story

The challenge "fell to the United States Marine Corps." The Corps was a service
with a survival problem — small, obscure, and threatened by army proposals to
absorb it. The 1919–1920 review of War Plan Orange supplied the answer: "An
amphibious assault specialty represented a focused wartime mission that would once
and for all make it distinct from the army." Strategy drove the requirement.
Defeating Japan demanded "an extended naval campaign across the vast spaces of the
central Pacific Ocean," and because the Washington treaty limited Pacific bases,
the fleet "would have to wrest bases from a Japanese defense force." Commandant
John A. Lejeune grasped this in 1920 and commissioned Earl H. Ellis, whose
Operations Plan 712, "Advanced Base Force Operations in Micronesia" (endorsed 23
July 1921), gave the mission "a valid geographic theater and a specific enemy."

The doctrinal capstone came when the Marine Corps Schools, "alarmed by rumors of
its own demise," turned an effort to write a textbook into "an organizational
'barn-raising' of doctrine-writing" that produced the **Tentative Manual for
Landing Operations in 1934**, "the most comprehensive and detailed effort to think
through the numerous problems involved in amphibious operation." Promoted in 1938
to Fleet Training Publication 167 under the chief of naval operations, it became
"the authoritative guide within the Navy Department." The renamed Fleet Marine
Force (1933) then tested doctrine in annual **Fleet Landing Exercises (FLEXs)**
from 1935; FLEX-3 (1937) and FLEX-4 (1938) drew in the army. These exercises
clarified the combined-arms recipe: isolate the objective, "pound the defenders
into a stupor with naval gunfire and close air support," then mount "a violent
assault by a combined arms team … over a broad front," with rapid reinforcement
by artillery and tanks. Higgins boats and other landing craft remained the
binding material constraint. Open debate in service journals, Congressional
hearings, and a sympathetic president (Franklin Roosevelt, "an honorary marine")
kept the specialty alive despite chronic underfunding.

## Britain: Institutional Ambivalence

Britain "had every reason to maintain some sort of amphibious assault force" but
"did not have such a force." The Dardanelles trauma "was not the only, nor the
most significant, reason for the neglect." The staff colleges produced manuals —
revisions of *The Manual of Combined Operations* through 1938 — but "had no force
outside the school system," and field exercises (Bay of Bengal 1924, Moray Firth
1928, the Humber 1934) showed "little more than the initiative of local
commanders." The Madden Committee (1924) urged a Royal Marines "striking force,"
but "the Admiralty decided that it could not ask for the money." Amphibious work
was treated as "combined operations," loading it "with all the interservice
conflicts that beset the British armed forces," and Europe, not the Pacific, was
the priority theater. Only in 1938 did the Inter-Services Training and Development
Centre open at Eastney with a tiny staff and £30,000. As Hastings Ismay conceded,
"our strong suit was command of the sea," yet Britain had "fall[en] behind Japan
and the United States in amphibious development."

## Japan: The Army's Force

Ironically, "the Japanese Army was the world's foremost amphibious force in 1921,"
and "Japan alone possessed the doctrine, tactical concepts, and forces for such
operations in 1939." The army, not the navy, owned the mission: the Army
Transportation Department at Ujina ran transports, shipping engineer regiments,
and "research and development … especially in the design of landing craft,"
including "a steel, armored, and self-propelled landing craft." Doctrine
progressed from the 1924 "Summary of Amphibious Operations" to the 1932 "Outline
of Amphibious Operations," tested in joint exercises and "developed in the hard
school of the real ocean, which extracted payment in the form of wrecked landing
craft and dead soldiers." The navy fielded separate Special Naval Landing Forces
for small central-Pacific atolls. But the China war diluted this lead: the
"especially designated" divisions were bled on the mainland, and easy unopposed
landings bred a "victory disease" that pulled the army back toward its old method.

## Limitations and Critiques

The concept is genuinely contested. Gallipoli sustained a durable view that
opposed landings were impossible, and even after the FLEXs American doctrine
conceded only that "amphibious assaults might be possible, but not easy" — "Army
opinion remained even more skeptical." Doctrine repeatedly outran capability:
naval gunfire shells "designed to penetrate steel ships did not work well against
field fortifications," landing craft were too few and too slow, and ship-to-shore
movement was "too slow and disorganized." Japan's primacy proved brittle, undone
once "the American armed forces altered the conditions under which the Japanese
had to operate." Britain's failure was "timing, not ingenuity." Millett's central
claim — that strategic requirement (the Pacific island problem under War Plan
Orange) plus organizational self-interest (a Marine Corps seeking a mission), not
technology, decided which power solved the problem — is itself an interpretive
position about how innovation happens.
