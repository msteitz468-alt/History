# Kyriarchy

**Kyriarchy** (from Greek *kyrios*, "lord/master," + *archē*, "rule") is an analytical
concept coined by the feminist biblical scholar **Elisabeth Schüssler Fiorenza** to name
domination as an interlocking system rather than a single axis. Where "patriarchy" centers
rule by men over women, kyriarchy describes the **intersecting hierarchies** of gender,
class, status (free/enslaved), and ethnicity through which a few elite free men dominated —
and through which the dominated could themselves dominate others (e.g. a free woman over an
enslaved woman).

## The Analytical Point

- Domination is **pyramidal and intersectional**: "to be a woman was always to be a slave or
  a free woman; to be a slave was always to be either a male or female slave" (Sheila
  Briggs). The free/enslaved and male/female axes (and Jew/Greek) operate together.
- It reframes the model image of oppression from a binary (men/women) to a **structure** in
  which positions are relative and multiply determined — anticipating later intersectional
  theory.
- For Schüssler Fiorenza feminism is "a theory and practice of justice that seeks not just to
  understand but to change relations of marginalization and domination"; kyriarchy is paired
  with a **hermeneutics of suspicion** that reads texts (and scholarship) as products of, and
  participants in, these structures.

## Application

The concept anchors feminist readings of ancient and biblical texts, including
[[cobb-slavery-gender-luke-acts-2019|Cobb's]] reading of [[luke-acts|Luke-Acts]]: the Bible
is treated as a **kyriarchal text** whose enslaved and female characters
([[enslaved-truth-teller|enslaved truth-tellers]]) must be deliberately re-centered against
the grain. It is essential for analyzing [[slavery-in-early-christianity]], where gender,
status, and ethnicity cannot be separated.

## Limitations and Critique

As an etic, presentist analytical tool it imports modern justice commitments into the study
of antiquity; critics ask whether ancient actors experienced their world in these terms.
Its defenders reply that all interpretation is interested, and that naming the structure
makes the silencing of the marginalized visible.
