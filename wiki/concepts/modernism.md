# Modernism (maximalist ideal type)

Etic analytical concept. In conventional ("minimalist") usage, *modernism* names the avant-garde artistic and literary experimentation of roughly 1850–1945, usually with left-progressive connotations. This page records the **maximalist ideal type** constructed by Roger Griffin in [[griffin-modernism-and-fascism-2007|*Modernism and Fascism* (2007)]], on which fascism itself becomes a form of modernism — see [[political-modernism]] and [[generic-fascism]].

## The Definition

Griffin's formal ideal type (ch. 4, verbatim core): "MODERNISM is a generic term for a vast array of heterogeneous individual and collective initiatives undertaken in Europeanized societies in every sphere of cultural production and social activity from the mid-nineteenth century onwards. Their common denominator lies in the bid to achieve a sense of transcendent value, meaning, or purpose despite Western culture's progressive loss of a homogeneous value system and overarching cosmology (nomos) caused by the secularizing and disembedding forces of modernization." An earlier discursive version (ch. 2) glosses it as "countervailing palingenetic reactions to the anarchy and cultural decay allegedly resulting from the radical transformation of traditional institutions, social structures, and belief systems under the impact of Western modernization." Modernism is thus "an attempted rebellion against Modernity carried out in order to inaugurate a new modernity" — where capital-M "Modernity" is modernity discursively constructed as decadence.

Periodization: modernism becomes a dominant cultural force between the 1860s and 1945, once Enlightenment/liberal/capitalist myths of progress lost their partial hegemony (Jameson's "crucial year" 1857: *Madame Bovary* and *Les Fleurs du Mal*). Griffin's ideal-typical phases of modernity itself: early modernity pre-Revolutionary (1730–89) and post-Revolutionary (1790–1850); high modernity from ~1850, divided fin-de-siècle (1850–1914) and inter-war (1918–39); late modernity (1945–2001). Postmodernism, on this schema, is not a new era but "a particular current or 'school'" of late modernity — one that has "developed [its] own cultural revitalization movement with its own rites and cultic discourse presided over by... prophetae."

## Epiphanic vs Programmatic Modernism

The type's central internal distinction, offered as two poles of sentiment rather than exclusive categories:

- **Epiphanic modernism** (after Joyce's "epiphany") cultivates unexpected, unsustainable "moments of this-worldly revelation" — Kermode's *kairos* breaking through *chronos* — with no design to change society. Paradigms: Kafka, Proust's madeleine, Woolf's "moments of being," Baudelaire's *l'azur*.
- **Programmatic modernism** is the pole "in which the rejection of Modernity expresses itself as a mission to change society, to inaugurate a new epoch, to start time anew. It is a modernism that lends itself to the rhetoric of manifestos and declarations." Exemplars: Nietzsche, Gropius, the Futurist manifestos, *La Voce*, Jünger's *The Worker* — and, at the political extreme, Hitler and Stalin "making history."

The "membranes" between the poles are porous: artists shift between them (Yeats, Jünger, Blok, Johst; [[evola-julius|Evola]] from Dada to Traditionalist fascism; Benn from Nazism back to apolitical poetry). Beyond aesthetics, programmatic modernism "morphs into **social modernism**" — collective projects to regenerate society's health (Theosophy and the occult revival, the Lebensreform/Monte Verità milieu, nudism, eugenics and racial hygiene, Nordau's "muscular" Zionism, Galton's eugenics-as-new-religion) — and thence into [[political-modernism]].

## The Primordialist Grounding

What makes the type "primordialist" (named by analogy with Anthony Smith's primordialist theory of nationalism) is its anthropological substructure, assembled transdisciplinarily:

- **Peter Berger's "sacred canopy"**: culture supplies a *nomos*, a "shield against terror"; anomy is "unbearable to the point where the individual may seek death in preference to it"; the opposite of the sacred is "not just the profane but, at a deeper level, chaos."
- **Terror Management Theory** (building on Ernest Becker's hero-systems — "cultures as basically styles of heroic death denial"): self-esteem and group identification are immortality strategies; nationalism is "an ideology particularly well-suited to buffer death-anxiety, since the national group is usually highly reified." Post-Enlightenment "weak symbolization" makes modernity "an era of permanently fragmented, weakened symbolization" — a perpetual nomic crisis.
- **Temporality**: Griffin reworks Kermode's scheme into *Cronus* (Goya's Saturn — entropic, devouring human time) vs *aevum* (transcendent time); rituals "rewind a society's cosmic clock." Koselleck's temporalization of history ("History no longer takes place in time, but through time") relocates utopia inside the historical process.
- **The rite of passage and the liminoid**: van Gennep's triad, Victor Turner's liminal/liminoid distinction, and Wallace's [[revitalization-movement]] model. Under modernity, liminoidality "becomes a perpetually open-ended condition which denies closure" — societies locked in permanent transition; the "dialectic of the liminoid" turns this anguish into palingenetic programmes. Broch's *The Sleepwalkers* is Griffin's master case: modern anomy breeds "the doubly strong yearning for a Leader... the Healer who by his actions will give meaning to the incomprehensible events of the Age, so that Time can begin anew."

Nietzsche's active vs passive nihilism supplies the psychology of "creative destruction": destruction becomes the condition of "Yes-saying life."

## Fascism's Place

The payoff, developed across [[political-modernism]], [[generic-fascism]], and [[modernist-state]]: fascism "is one of the forms taken by political and cultural modernity, but it is simultaneously a radical reaction against the political forms of 'actually existing modernity'" — the Hewitt dichotomy (modern vs anti-modern) "transcended (aufgehoben) in almost Hegelian fashion." Griffin's resolving formula, via Proust: "every 'although' is a misunderstood 'because of'" — precisely because fascism was intrinsically modernist, it could embrace some aesthetic modernisms and damn others as decadent. He equally rejects direct-lineage reductionism (Virilio's Marinetti-to-Auschwitz line "has minimal empirical content"; fascism's links to cultural modernism are "rhizomic rather than arborial").

## Limitations and Critiques

Griffin flags the type's own constructedness at length: it is a Weberian "thought-picture" whose "conceptual purity... cannot be found empirically anywhere in reality"; a Popperian "historical interpretation" that "cannot be refuted"; "a work of speculative syncretism rather than of specialist scholarship." Substantive objections recorded in the book: the primordialist anthropology (Berger, Becker, TMT, Wallace) is contested within its home disciplines (Gell's demolition of plural temporalities is confronted directly); minimalist modernism scholars resist the semantic enlargement as dilution; and applying "modernism" to Nazi perpetrators risks (though Griffin explicitly refuses) aestheticizing or relativizing the crimes — his account is written with a "consciously 'anti-revisionist' stress" on them. Emilio Gentile's independent, converging definition of modernism (an ideology/culture/movement resolving the problems of industrial modernity "by elaborating solutions consistent with its own vision of modernity") is cited as corroboration.
