# Flexible Response

**Flexible response** was the strategic doctrine that replaced (or at least supplemented) Massive Retaliation in US and NATO policy during the 1960s. It sought to provide a range of military options — conventional, tactical nuclear, and strategic nuclear — so that the West would not be forced into an immediate choice between surrender and all-out nuclear war in response to aggression.

## Key Elements

- **Graduated escalation.** The alliance would meet aggression at the level at which it occurred and seek to control and limit the conflict. Conventional forces were strengthened to raise the nuclear threshold.
- **Tactical / theater nuclear weapons.** These were retained (and in some versions expanded) as a bridge between conventional defence and strategic nuclear exchange.
- **Strategic options.** Even at the strategic level, the United States moved toward "multiple options" and later "selective options" rather than a single massive spasm.

The doctrine was driven by the recognition that massive retaliation had lost credibility as Soviet nuclear forces grew. It was also influenced by limited-war theorizing (Kissinger, Osgood, Kaufmann et al.) and by the formal strategists' work on bargaining and escalation control.

## NATO Adoption and European Resistance

After considerable European resistance (fears that the United States was trying to "decouple" its strategic deterrent from Europe and force Europeans to bear the conventional burden), flexible response was adopted as NATO's official strategy in MC 14/3 in 1967. It represented a compromise: stronger conventional forces + continued reliance on the threat of nuclear escalation, but with more steps on the ladder.

Europeans were disturbed by the implication that a conventional war was, in some way, a limited event, a "soft" option, equivalent almost to the "phoney war" of 1939-40. The Europeans had an experience of two prolonged vicious and bloody twentieth-century wars. "It did not need nuclear weapons to make war a miserable and horrible affair."

"Thus even when the distinction between limited and total war was meaningful to Europeans, as it was to some, it was not synonymous with the distinction between the use of conventional and nuclear explosives." For Europeans the distinction between deterrence and defence was far more pertinent. "All war had to be deterred. If the Soviet Union believed that a war would only be fought at a conventional level, not entailing a serious risk of nuclear war, then it might be tempted to try its luck."

To talk of a "threshold" below which nuclear weapons would not be used, or a "pause" during which these weapons would not be used, eased the Soviet risk calculus too much. The absence of a clear link between America’s nuclear arsenal and the defence of Europe would leave the Europeans vulnerable to intimidation by the Soviet Union.

The "trip wire" concept allowed local defence to serve as a trigger for the US nuclear deterrent even if not a full blocking force. "Even with larger conventional forces, the ‘trip-wire’ concept could still operate. Though a nuclear response to invasion would now be less automatic, the aggressor would still have to accept a certain risk."

## Conventional Defence Debates

The 1952 Lisbon force goals were abandoned; hope was placed in tactical nuclear weapons. Schmidt argued that a local defence known by the enemy to be capable of blocking an attack would be as good a deterrent as the threat of nuclear retaliation. Liddell Hart proposed militia forces providing a deep network of defence with less transport and shorter training. Norstad spoke of the "Shield" of conventional forces and the "Sword" of nuclear retaliation, allowing a "pause" for reconsideration.

McNamara and his aides (Enthoven and Smith) argued that after correcting for paper divisions and relative effectiveness, NATO and the Warsaw Pact had approximate equality on the ground by the mid-1960s. "The only satisfactory escape from the dilemma lay in NATO’s having adequate conventional forces."

## Fire-Break Theory and Critiques

The fire-break between conventional and nuclear war was presented as the most obvious discontinuity: "Because of differences in the effects of conventional and nuclear explosives, violations of the fire-break are readily detectable; thus there is no ambiguity of the kind of war being fought." Conventional war rules were "simple and clear".

Brodie severely criticized the fire-break theory as far too mechanical. It assumed both sides would not hesitate to outbid each other up to the limit but would not consider further escalation. He preferred reliance on "the aims, intentions, or fears of the respective opponents". The apparent rejection of the fire-break by the Soviet Union had worked to NATO’s advantage.

## Relationship to Other Concepts

Flexible response presupposed some degree of [[escalation-dominance|escalation dominance]] or at least the ability to manage escalation. It was in tension with pure [[assured-destruction|assured destruction]] thinking, which saw little value in limited nuclear options. The Schlesinger Doctrine of the 1970s was in many ways an extension of the flexible/selective options logic into the strategic forces themselves.
