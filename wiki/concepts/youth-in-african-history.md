## Definition and Scope

"Youth" in African contexts is a socially constructed — and often contested — category that may accommodate people across a wide range of biological ages. Sub-Saharan Africa is the world's most youthful region (44% of population under 15 in 2006), yet population figures do not accurately reflect who counts as "youth" in local cultural and social terms. A 25-year-old may consider herself "only a child"; a 13-year-old girl who has married may be treated as an adult. A man in his forties may act as a youth at a funeral while being addressed as an elder at a civic ceremony.

The concept functions as what Deborah Durham calls a "social shifter": being youth involves "discourses about power and authority, responsibility and the grounds of legitimate governance, sources of autonomy and of interdependence, and the obligations and exploitations that make up membership in such social groups as family and nation-state." Youth, in other words, is a site where critical issues of power and agency are worked out, not a neutral biological stage.

This concept page is analytically distinct from [[education-and-literacy-in-africa]] (the process page), [[women-and-gender-in-african-history]] (which addresses young women's specific experience), and [[african-urbanization]] (which documents the urban contexts where much youth history unfolds).

## Emic vs. Etic Uses

**Emic** (used by historical actors): Many African societies have specific terms for a life stage between childhood and full adulthood — "youth" with distinctive social roles, obligations, and restrictions. East African pastoralist systems (Maasai *murran*; Nuer age sets) formalised this into structured cohorts. West and Central African societies often had less clear-cut rites of passage, with a longer undifferentiated transitional period.

**Etic** (applied by scholars and states): Colonial administrators categorised "detribalised" young men in cities as a social problem; nationalist movements deployed "youth" as a political identity; international agencies use biological age brackets (up to 15, 16, 29, or 34 depending on the country and policy) that often conflict with local cultural categories. Contemporary African governments both celebrate youth as "the promise of the future" and fear them as a "primary source of twenty-first-century instability."

## Major Theoretical Traditions

**Functionalism (1940s–1960s).** Classic anthropological studies — Evans-Pritchard on the Nuer, Victor Turner on the Ndembu, Audrey Richards on Bemba girls' *chisungu* initiation — described age grades and rites of passage as mechanisms for reproducing stable social systems. Even youthful rebellion, articulated in ritual, was seen as reinforcing elder authority. Marc Schloss's study of the Ehing of Casamance shows an extreme case: initiation held every twenty-five years meant that at one ceremony witnessed in 1979, initiates ranged from eighteen months to their late twenties. Two-year-olds became "elders" with social privileges; men in their twenties who missed the ceremony remained technical "youths."

**Marxist and Political Economy Turn (1970s–1980s).** Claude Meillassoux argued the youth/elder distinction was fundamentally a class division: elders monopolised the means of production and reproduction (land, livestock, marriage rights), compelling youth to surrender their labour. Achille Mbembe emphasised the apparently totalising oppression of gerontocratic and colonial regimes. This framing was analytically useful but limiting — it underplayed the dynamic agency of young people and the situational character of age statuses.

**Social Constructionist and Agency Turn (1990s–present).** Youth reconceived not as a biological given but as a social discourse uncoupled from age. Scholars highlight not the integration of youth into society but the tensions and instabilities at the heart of gerontocratic power. Key interventions: Deborah Durham on youth as a "social shifter" in Botswana; Jonah Abbink and Ineke van Kessel, eds., *Vanguards or Vandals* (2005); Jon Christiansen, Mats Utas, and Henrik Vigh, eds., *Navigating Youth, Generating Adulthood* (2006); Alcinda Honwana and Filip de Boeck, eds., *Makers and Breakers* (2005). "Makers and breakers" captures the dual image of African youth as both vandals and victims, creators and destroyers.

## Historical Dynamics

**Youth and Labour.** Being youth has historically been aligned with providing labour: youth are the people over whom elders have rights — to their work, their bodies, their marriages. The Atlantic slave trade intensified this: those who were transacted became coded as "youth" even when they were not biologically young. Colonial labour systems extended the coding: older African men were called "boys" by white employers. The post-independence era combined shrinking formal employment with rising educational expectations, producing what Donal Cruise O'Brien called a "lost generation" with a "high order of built-in frustration" and what Mbembe called the African university's "disapprentisship of life."

**Youth and the Slave Trade.** In much of West and Central Africa, elders had rights in persons — they could pawn or sell youth. As the trans-Atlantic slave trade intensified, those transacted came to be characterised as "youth" regardless of biological age. The correlation between disposability and "youth" is a structural feature of African political economy that predates colonialism and persists in disguised forms.

**The Colonial Labour Market and Generational Conflict.** Colonial labour markets inserted young people into new cash economies that gave them resources to challenge elder authority. Benedict Carton's study of generational conflict in South Africa shows young men and women "caught between competing patriarchal powers" — local lineage elders demanding tax payment and labour obedience, and colonial employers demanding cheap able-bodied workers. These powers were "mutually constituting even as they competed over youth."

**Urban Youth Culture.** The most studied domain. Cities created new youth cohorts outside lineage control: compounds, dance societies, literary clubs, sports teams, gangs. J. Clyde Mitchell's 1956 analysis of kalela dancers in the Northern Rhodesian Copperbelt — young men dressing in neat white shirts and pressed trousers on weekend afternoons, singing about their own sophistication and belittling other "tribal" groups — is a foundational text. Mitchell saw kalela as simultaneously creating a new "traditional" tribal geography and a new urban modernity. Terence Ranger's response found in kalela an embodied memory of the trauma of slavery and colonial occupation. The SAPE movement (*Société des Ambianceurs et des Personnes Elégantes*) of Brazzaville — sapeurs saving their wages in Paris to buy designer clothes for competitive display at home — is the postcolonial iconic case: political critique and appropriation of global consumer culture simultaneously.

**Youth and Education.** Schools created age-based cohorts independent of traditional gerontocratic control and gave young men shortcuts to authority — as clerks, interpreters, bureaucrats — that bypassed the normal wait for elderhood. Jean-François Bayart: "the era of the Whites became the era of insolence, when 'children,' 'their mouths on fire,' came out of their silence." Nationalist movements made "youth" a political banner; most African independence leaders were young men, and some have clung to the youth label for decades. Postcolonial regimes often reappropriated traditional hierarchies — casting the head of state as "father" and dissenting students as wayward children needing discipline. University campuses became sites of political confrontation; the student movements of the 1990s democratisation wave and the 2011 Arab Spring in North Africa recall anti-colonial student organising from the 1930s–50s.

**Youth, Violence, and Child Soldiers.** Richard Reid argues that in nineteenth-century East Africa, young men were both primary victims and leading perpetrators of violence, seeking to establish manhood within or against established social orders. The contemporary child soldier — draped in bandoliers, wielding weaponry — is an icon of African conflict that extends these older dynamics. Paul Richards's account of Sierra Leonean child soldiers situates their actions within family structures, popular media (Rambo films), failed-state political economy, Cold War weapons proliferation, and diamond mining circuits. Nicolas Argenti's *The Intestines of the State* on the Cameroonian Grassfields theorises youth violence as "belated history" — an embodied working-out of historical traumas of slave raiding and colonial violence.

**Gender and Youth.** Early anthropology focused overwhelmingly on male youth. Female life stages received attention primarily as problem cases (the difficult new bride). Audrey Richards's *Chisungu* (1956) on Bemba girls' initiation was a pioneering exception. More recent scholarship attends to girls' lives outside the home — in schools, churches, Pentecostal movements — and to "transactional sex" as both a survival strategy and an expression of young women's sexual agency. Jennifer Cole's work on Madagascar argues transactional sex has continuities with past sexual practices and is driven by emotional as well as economic incentives.

**Youth and Religion.** Churches and new religious movements have long been important for African youth. Pentecostal movements offered young people participation in civil society denied them in established churches and politics. Rijk van Dijk's analysis of Born-Again preachers in Malawi (9–30 years old) shows them promoting a church with power "beyond the clutches of tradition and its gerontocracy," directly opposing state cultural-authenticity projects.

## Limitations and Critiques

1. The category "youth" risks naturalising a Western (and specifically bourgeois) life-stage model — an intermediate period "outside" the household between dependent childhood and independent adulthood — that does not map onto the lived experiences of many African young women, whose lives centre on households throughout.

2. Emphasising youth as inherently rebellious or marginalised risks "overestimating the extent to which transitions to adulthood were smoothly and regularly achieved in earlier periods" while neglecting the creative agency and social interdependence that also characterise being young in Africa.

3. The identification of Africa as a "continent of youth" in contemporary discourse — i.e., marginalised from global adulthood as it is marginalised from the global economy — risks reproducing colonial tropes of African infancy. As Argenti and Durham note, nineteenth-century abolitionist literature depicted Africa as "robbed of its youth" by the slave trade; today's discourse depicts Africa as "robbed of its adulthood."

4. The field remains heavily shaped by anglophone, East and Southern African case studies. West Africa and Francophone Africa are underrepresented in the comparative literature.
