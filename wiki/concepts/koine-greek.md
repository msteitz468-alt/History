## Definition

**Koine** (Greek *koinē* "common [tongue]") is the common form of Greek — based chiefly on
Attic, with Ionic and other elements — that emerged in the wake of [[alexander-the-great|
Alexander's]] conquests and became the **administrative, commercial, and literary lingua
franca of the [[hellenistic-world|Hellenistic world]]**, from the Aegean to Egypt, the
Near East, and inner Asia. It superseded the patchwork of older regional dialects for most
public purposes and was the spoken and written Greek of the courts, cities, and settler
populations.

## Significance

- **The vehicle of Hellenization.** Koine carried Greek civic and intellectual culture
  across the [[seleucid-colonization|colonised East]] — the language of the
  [[hellenistic-polis|gymnasium]], the chancellery, and the inscription. It is the Greek of
  the [[ptolemaic-papyri|Ptolemaic papyri]] and of the thousands of Hellenistic decrees on
  stone.
- **A thin top layer.** Yet, as J. K. Davies stresses (CAH VII.1 ch. 8), the diffusion of
  koine has "influenced — or misled — our vision" of a unified Hellenistic culture: it was
  largely the language of the towns and the ruling stratum, over a [[hellenistic-polis|plural
  society]] of many spoken languages (twenty-two in the kingdom of Pontus alone) and
  persisting non-Greek tongues (Egyptian demotic, Aramaic, Babylonian).
- **A lasting legacy.** Koine became the language of the **Septuagint** (the Greek Hebrew
  scriptures made at [[alexandria|Alexandria]]) and of the **New Testament** and early
  Christianity, and remained the language of the eastern Roman/Byzantine world for
  centuries — the single most durable cultural product of the Hellenistic age.
