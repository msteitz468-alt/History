# New Man (Hombre Nuevo)

Guevara's vision of the ideal socialist citizen: a selfless, conscious, morally transformed individual whose labor and daily life are motivated by revolutionary duty, solidarity, and the collective good rather than material incentives, profit, or coercion. Central to Che's economic and cultural program in Cuba (1960s) and to his critique of both capitalism and "actually existing socialism."

## Core Ideas

In essays such as "Socialism and Man in Cuba" (1965), Che argued that the transition to socialism required the simultaneous creation of new economic structures and a new human consciousness. Material incentives (wages, bonuses) were seen as capitalist holdovers that reproduced egoism. Moral incentives, voluntary labor, and education would produce the "new man" who works for the joy of contributing to the common project.

The concept drew on humanist Marxism, Martí, and Che's own experience of austerity and self-sacrifice in the guerrilla war.

## Implementation and Tensions

As Minister of Industries, Che attempted to apply moral incentives in economic planning. The approach clashed with Soviet advice favoring material incentives and with practical difficulties of low productivity and scarcity. Anderson shows Che's growing frustration with bureaucratic resistance and the limits of consciousness-raising under conditions of underdevelopment. The 1965 farewell letter and turn to internationalism can be read partly as escape from these domestic contradictions.

## Legacy and Critiques

The "new man" became official rhetoric in Cuba and inspired volunteers (internationalist brigades, literacy campaigns). Critics (including later Cuban reformers and external analysts) argue it was utopian, ignored human nature and economic realities, and was used to justify coercion and austerity. It remains a touchstone for debates over socialist motivation, consciousness, and the relationship between economic base and human transformation.
