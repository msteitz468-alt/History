## Definition

*Sulh-e kull* (Arabic/Persian: peace with all, universal conciliation) is the political and moral philosophy articulated by [[abulfazl-allami]] in the *Akbar-Namah* to describe the organizing principle of [[akbar]]'s court culture and imperial policy. As Hodgson explains in [[hodgson-venture-of-islam-v3-1974]], *sulh-e kull* meant for Abulfazl "not the elimination of conflict—for it was assumed that some persons were wicked and must be suppressed—but such generosity and forgiveness to all that no occasion was left for dissension."

The concept has both a political dimension (interconfessional governance without discrimination by religion) and an individual moral dimension: through Akbar's spiritual guidance, a disciple could achieve an inner state of being "inwardly at peace with all persons, whatever their attitude toward one." The next stage beyond was *mahabbat-e kull* ("universal love") — "a more positive, constructive sentiment of fostering all persons and their material welfare, whatever their blindness."

## Emic vs. Etic Usage

*Sulh-e kull* is emic — a phrase used by Abulfazl and those in the Akbari intellectual tradition. Modern historians have used it as a shorthand for Akbar's policy of religious toleration and interconfessional coexistence, though this use risks flattening the concept's internal structure (the distinction between political and spiritual dimensions, and the sequence from conciliation to love to Sufi union).

## Intellectual Sources

Abulfazl drew the concept from Sufi vocabulary while reorienting it toward civilization and governance:

1. **Sufi tradition of *ulh-e kull***: In late Sufi usage, the phrase referred to a personal spiritual condition of non-attachment and openness — being beyond particular loyalties in a state of divine absorption. Abulfazl's innovation was to make this a social and political ideal, not just a personal mystical one.

2. **Falsafah tradition**: The ideal of the philosopher-king who rules justly for all subjects regardless of their particularity provided a philosophical grounding for non-communal rule.

3. **Mongol-Timurid heritage**: The Mongol tradition of ruling multi-religious populations without enforcing a single religion ("the universalist sense of civilization could readily flourish [in the military institution]; there it had a certain precedent as far back even as the time of the historian Rashiduddîn Falullâh and his even-handed world history") provided a historical legitimacy for *sulh-e kull* that did not require Islamic sanction.

## Key Assertion

"The great error was bigotry: to reject a human achievement because its maker was of the wrong allegiance is contrary to the good sense of the civilized man, and to the first requirements of *ulh-e kull*."

This is a positive civilizational claim — not merely "do not persecute" but "recognize worth wherever it appears." It motivated the Sanskrit-to-Persian translation projects at Akbar's court, the incorporation of Hindu artistic and architectural traditions into Indo-Islamicate culture, and Abulfazl's citations of Hindu philosophical traditions even where his treatment was technically inadequate.

## Limitations

Hodgson notes explicitly that *sulh-e kull* was a court-level ideology: "The genteel courtly universalism, satisfactory for allowing privileged groups of diverse traditions and persuasions to share in a common brilliant culture, seems to have done little for humane relations beyond the thin layers of the highly privileged classes." It did not address peasant-level social injustice, and the economic exploitation of the agrarian population continued regardless of the confessional policy at court.

## Contrast with Communalism

*Sulh-e kull* stands in explicit opposition to both Muslim and Hindu communalism:
- **Muslim communalism** (Shari'ah-minded): The principle that the Islamic community's revealed legal order must be maintained as a social whole, exclusive of those outside it. This view was represented by the Shari'ah-minded ulama who opposed Akbar and by [[sirhindi-ahmad|Ahmad Sirhindi]]'s Mujaddidi movement.
- **Hindu communalism**: The principle of caste solidarity and Brahmanical authority as the basis of social order, expressed in "a stricter interpretation of caste rules" and resistance to Muslim egalitarianism.

## Later Influence

The concept shaped how later Mughal rulers understood their legitimacy. Even [[aurangzeb]], whose policies systematically reversed *sulh-e kull* in practice, had to engage with its hold on court culture. The *Dabistan-e Mazahib* (1657), a survey of all religions written by a Parsi author in Persian, represents the deepest flowering of the tradition's intellectual influence in a non-imperial context. The phrase itself has been revived in modern South Asian interfaith discourse as a historical precedent for religious pluralism.
