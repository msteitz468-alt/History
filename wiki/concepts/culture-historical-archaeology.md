# Culture-Historical Archaeology

**Culture-historical archaeology** (German *Kulturgeschichte*) is the dominant paradigm of prehistoric archaeology from the late 19th century through the mid-20th century. It seeks to reconstruct the history of "peoples" or ethnic groups through the spatial and temporal distribution of recurring assemblages of artifacts ("cultures").

## Core Assumptions (Childe's formulation)

Gordon Childe (heavily referenced in Champion et al. 1984) defined a culture as "a constantly recurring assemblage of types such as houses, pottery and burial rites" and regarded it as "the physical manifestation of a particular social or ethnic group."

Change was explained primarily through:
- Diffusion ("influence") of traits from one culture to another.
- Migration or conquest when one culture replaced another in a region.
- The goal was a kind of political/military history of prehistoric "peoples" reconstructed from material remains.

The approach was closely tied to the Three Age System for ordering and to typological refinement of artifact sequences (especially pottery and metalwork) within local regions.

## Treatment in Prehistoric Europe (1984)

Champion et al. provide a clear critical summary in Ch. 1:

- This school "concentrates on the study of artifacts, particularly pottery and metalwork. The economy has received little attention, while statements about the nature of society have tended to be based on intuitive impressions and dubious historical analogies."
- In the German tradition, the presentation of the material culture sequence "has tended to be regarded as an end in itself."
- "In their place, it became important to ask where a feature observed in a particular culture had come from. A concern with tracing connections ... largely precluded the possibility of the independent invention of such features."
- The pursuit of links to the Near East was necessary for chronology before radiocarbon dating; this chronological dependence was "tended to be confused with the dependence of prehistoric Europe on the East for all its advances."

The 1984 authors explicitly position their thematic, subsistence/settlement/social focus as a departure from this legacy.

"Throughout the late nineteenth and early twentieth century the amount of prehistoric material of all kinds accumulated steadily, and rudimentary local sequences were gradually evolved, based mainly on stratigraphy and typology, though with no firm absolute chronology." The next major advance was Childe's pan-European synthesis, but the underlying paradigm remained culture-historical. (Champion et al. 1984, Ch.1 / range lines ~285-320)

## Critiques and Supersession

- **Radiocarbon revolution**: Removed the need for diffusionist cross-dating and demonstrated that many innovations were earlier in Europe than supposed Eastern sources.
- **Processual / New Archaeology** (1960s+): Shifted questions from "who influenced whom?" to "how did societies adapt, organize production, and structure social relations?" — see [[concepts/processual-archaeology|Processual Archaeology]].
- **Post-processual and national traditions** (see Hodder ed. 1991): Highlighted the socio-political embedding of culture-history (e.g., Kossinna's settlement archaeology and its use by Nazi Ahnenerbe for ethnic claims; "Kossinna syndrome" in post-war German archaeology). Emphasized that material culture is meaningfully constituted, not a passive reflection of ethnic groups.
- **Ethnicity and archaeology**: Modern work treats "cultures" as etic analytical constructs, not direct proxies for self-conscious peoples. Grave goods, pottery styles, etc., are active in identity negotiation rather than straightforward ethnic markers.

## Enduring Value

The detailed typological and distributional work produced under culture-historical auspices remains indispensable primary data. Refined chronologies and regional sequences built in that tradition are the foundation that later interpretive frameworks use.
