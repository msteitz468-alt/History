## Definition

*Da'wa* (Arabic, literally "call" or "invitation") in its Qur'anic sense (Q16:125,
"invite to the way of your Lord with wisdom") refers broadly to inviting others
to Islam. In the 20th century the term acquired a narrower, organizationally
specific meaning: an effort by committed Muslims to "redirect" other, already
Muslim, believers onto a more rigorously observant "right path," rather than
converting non-Muslims. This redirected sense is the one central to the
[[islamic-revival-late-20th-century|late-20th-century Islamic Revival]] across
Asia.

## Earlier sense: the medieval Ismaili da'wa

Long before its 20th-century revivalist meaning, *da'wa* named a concrete **organizational institution** in
medieval [[ismaili-movement|Ismaili]] Shi'ism. Here the *da'wa* ("summons") was a **hierarchical, often
clandestine mission** headed by the *hujja* ("proof" of the Imam) and staffed by *da'is* ("summoners") who
called believers to recognize the living Ismaili Imam. It sustained the movement through the *dawr al-satr*
(period of concealment, c. 765–909), built the [[berbers|Kutama]] following that produced the
**[[foundation-of-the-fatimid-caliphate-909|Fatimid Caliphate]]**, and under [[al-muizz-li-din-allah|al-Mu'izz]]
was headed by the chief judge as *da'i al-du'at* ("chief da'i"), coordinating regional da'is from Ifriqiya to
Sind. This institutional sense — distinct from the modern grassroots-revival usage below — is treated in
[[jiwa-fatimids-rise-2018|Jiwa, *The Fatimids: 1*]]. See [[qadi-al-numan]].

## Emic Usage

For the movements that use it, *da'wa* names both a religious duty and an
organizing method. Hasan al-Banna's Muslim Brotherhood (Egypt, founded 1928)
devised the operational model: campus-based *tarbiya* (education/formation)
delivered through gender-segregated study circles (*halaqa*), which in turn
formed small cell-like "family" units (*usra*). This method was treated by
practitioners as apolitical religious self-improvement even where it fed
directly into political organizing. University campuses became the key sites
of *da'wa* activity because they offered a captive, educated, socially mobile
population undergoing exactly the kind of dislocation (urbanization,
industrialization, loosened family structure) that made revivalist appeals
persuasive.

The Brotherhood's *tarbiya*/*halaqa*/*usra* template was exported and adapted
across Asia: Malaysia's Muslim Youth Movement (ABIM, founded 1970s) implanted
*halaqa* nationwide; Indonesia's *usra* movement was widespread in mosques by
the mid-1980s, reading al-Banna, Mawdudi, and Sayyid Qutb (and, distinctively,
also engaging with Iranian Shi'i thinkers such as Ali Shari'ati out of
fascination with the Iranian Revolution's anti-imperialist appeal, despite
Sunni-Shi'a sectarian difference); in Hong Kong, Indonesian migrant domestic
workers' visible piety was itself described as part of "the so-called da'wa
movement" spreading through Asia since the 1970s. [[tablighi-jamaat|Tablighi
Jama'at]], though organizationally distinct from the Brotherhood, pursues a
parallel grassroots-revival mission and is often discussed alongside *da'wa*
activism in Central Asia and China.

## Etic Usage

Historians and social scientists use *da'wa* (or "the da'wa movement") as a
descriptive label for this entire family of 20th-century grassroots revival
organizing — distinguishing it analytically from (a) proselytization aimed at
non-Muslims, (b) state-directed Islamization imposed from above, and (c)
explicitly political parties seeking state power (though these frequently
overlap in practice, e.g. Malaysia's PAS grew directly out of Brotherhood-style
*da'wa* organizing). Scholars such as Chiara Formichi treat *da'wa* as the
connective organizational technology underlying the broader Islamic Revival,
even where movements using it diverge sharply in politics, from apolitical
personal piety to militant political Islamism.

## Limitations and Critiques

- The term's flexibility is also a limitation: "da'wa" is used by movements as
  ideologically diverse as apolitical Tablighi Jama'at and explicitly
  revolutionary organizations, making it a weak predictor of political outcome
  on its own.
- *Da'wa* organizing structures have historically subordinated women: in the
  Muslim Brotherhood, ABIM, and Malaysia's Pan-Malayan Islamic Party (PAS),
  women's sections were admitted only as second-tier, gender-segregated
  auxiliaries, even where individual women (e.g., Zaynab al-Ghazali in Egypt)
  achieved outsized organizational influence.
- State and scholarly usage sometimes conflates *da'wa* activism with political
  Islamism or militancy; the concept itself does not entail a particular
  political program, and many *da'wa* movements (e.g., Tablighi Jama'at) are
  explicitly non-political.
