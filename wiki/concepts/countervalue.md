# Countervalue

**Countervalue** targeting (sometimes called counter-city or counter-economy) directs nuclear strikes against the enemy's population centers, industrial base, and other high-value non-military assets rather than against military forces (**counterforce**).

In the classic formulation associated with mutual assured destruction thinking, countervalue forces are deliberately aimed at things the opponent leadership is presumed to value above all else — major cities and the economic capacity to sustain a modern society. The threat is one of punitive, societal destruction rather than military disablement.

## Strategic Properties

- **No first-strike incentive from the weapons themselves.** A force that can only destroy cities and cannot effectively attack the other side's nuclear delivery systems provides its possessor with little or no military advantage from striking first. Schelling described such forces as "profoundly defensive."
- **Second-strike / retaliation logic.** Countervalue forces are optimized for survivability and the ability to inflict "unacceptable damage" after an enemy first strike. This was central to the logic of assured destruction.
- **Deterrence by punishment.** The mechanism is the threat of unbearable pain rather than denial of military objectives.

From the formal strategists analysis: Amster and Sherwin anticipated that with long-range missiles "cities were extremely vulnerable — soft, immobile and sprawling." Deterrence would work "through the punitive threat of irresistible hurt to the enemy’s social and economic structure, rather than through the prospect of victory in combat." Even after war began, measured nuclear attacks would "emphasize what might happen in the future rather than to punish past aggression."

## Critiques

Critics argued that countervalue targeting was morally monstrous (explicit planning for the massacre of tens or hundreds of millions of civilians) and strategically incredible in many scenarios. It also left little room for controlled or limited nuclear use if deterrence failed at lower levels.

US declaratory policy oscillated. Early massive retaliation rhetoric was largely countervalue in effect. McNamara explored city-avoidance/counterforce options before settling on an assured-destruction (countervalue-heavy) public posture. Later selective-options thinking sought to reintroduce more counterforce and counter-leadership options.
