# Museum and Library of Alexandria

The **Museum** (*Mouseion*, "shrine of the Muses") and great **Library** of [[alexandria|Alexandria]] were the central research institutions of the [[hellenistic-world|Hellenistic world]] — the institutional home of [[eratosthenes|Eratosthenes]], Euclid, the major Homeric critics, and much of the period's poetry and exact science. [[thonemann-hellenistic-age-2016|Thonemann]] (*The Hellenistic Age*, ch. 4) demythologizes both while stressing their real creative power.

## The Museum (per Strabo)

Strabo (early first century CE) gives the only detailed description: the Museum was part of the **palace complex**, with a covered walkway, a hall with seats, and a large house containing a **common dining-room** for the learned men, who held possessions in common, headed by a **priest** formerly appointed by the king (later by Caesar). It was a **shrine of the Muses**, not a modern museum of artefacts. Timon of Phlius (third-century satirist) mocked the inmates as free-dining "bookish scribblers, arguing away interminably in the chicken-coop of the Muses."

## The Library: Evidence and Myths

Hard evidence is thin. Precise location is unknown (guessed as part of the Museum complex; no physical remains). Foundation date and founder are uncertain; surviving pointers favour the early years of [[ptolemy-ii-philadelphus|Ptolemy II Philadelphus]] (283–246 BCE). Ancient and medieval volume figures (**200,000–700,000**) are demonstrably fantastic: at most **tens of thousands** of papyrus rolls, perhaps considerably fewer. There is no good reason to think the Library was destroyed by a single fire; like most ancient libraries it probably died slowly by neglect, natural decay, and mice.

An Oxyrhynchus papyrus lists Hellenistic librarians — all first-rank intellectuals: the poet **Apollonius of Rhodes** (*Argonautica*), **[[eratosthenes|Eratosthenes of Cyrene]]**, **Aristophanes of Byzantium** (first lexicographer and critical editor of early Greek epic and lyric). The great age ended abruptly in **144 BCE** when Ptolemy VIII ("Kakergetes"/"Malefactor"/Physkon) expelled most of the scholarly community, apparently for favouring a dynastic rival.

## What It Produced

Third- and second-century developments in mathematics, geography, natural science, humanistic scholarship, and poetry are largely attributable to Museum "bookish scribblers" (see [[hellenistic-science]]). The Museum paid little attention to **philosophy** — Athens kept that monopoly ([[epicurus]], [[zeno-of-citium]], Academy, Lyceum). There was no hard line between science and poetry: Archimedes' *Cattle Problem* is 22 elegiac couplets; Eratosthenes presented mathematical results in verse with royal thanks.

## Royal Patronage

The Ptolemies — especially Ptolemy II — stand out as willing financiers of math, science, and technology. One research project specifically attributed to royal initiative aimed to increase the power and range of **torsion artillery**. No independent Greek *polis* could have produced an object like the Antikythera mechanism. Scholars were royal appointees housed and fed in the palace.

## Relation to Existing Accounts

[[alexandria]] and [[hellenistic-science]] (Lloyd, CAH VII.1 ch. 9) already cover foundation myths, half-million-roll claims, and research-vs-teaching ethos. Thonemann's contribution is the **sceptical inventory**: Strabo's physical description, deflation of volume and fire myths, librarian list, 144 expulsion as terminus of the great age, and the Museum as palace-embedded shrine rather than modern research university.
