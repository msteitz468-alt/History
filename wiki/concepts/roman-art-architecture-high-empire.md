# Art and Architecture under the High Empire

The visual culture of the Roman world from the **Flavians to the Antonines** — its imperial
monuments, its concrete architecture, and the constant interplay of metropolitan Rome with
regional traditions. Treated in [[bowman-cah-v11-2000|CAH XI]] Ch. 34 (**Roger Ling**), surveying
the period dynasty by dynasty.

## Metropolitan and regional traditions

Ling frames the period as a tapestry of **interacting traditions**: the dominant **Greek** (cut
stone and marble, the orders, idealized realism), the **Italian/Roman** (with its developing
**brick-faced concrete**), and the regional — **Celtic** (curvilinear art, verandah temples),
**Punic** North African, **Egyptian**, and the **Near Eastern** "conceptual" arts. The emperor and
his circle were the chief arbiters, but local traditions everywhere interacted with metropolitan
fashion.

## The Flavians (69–96)

**Vespasian** used architecture pragmatically to distance himself from Nero: rebuilding the
Capitoline temple, erecting the **Temple of Peace**, and above all replacing Nero's Golden House
lake with the **Flavian Amphitheatre (Colosseum)** — conservative in plan and stone façade but a
masterpiece of scale and barrel-vaulted concrete. **Domitian** built the vast brick-faced concrete
**Domus Augustana** on the Palatine (by the architect Rabirius — the palace that gave Western
languages the word), the **Forum Transitorium**, and the **Arch of Titus** (its deeply cut,
illusionistic reliefs of the Jewish triumph and Titus' apotheosis). The Vesuvian cities, buried in
79, preserve the **Fourth Pompeian Style** wall-painting.

## Trajan (98–117)

The Spaniard **Trajan**, funded by the spoils of Dacia, built the last and greatest extension of
central Rome — the **Forum of Trajan** (by **Apollodorus of Damascus**), with its **Markets** (a
triumph of vaulted concrete commerce), the **Basilica Ulpia**, and **Trajan's Column** (a 30-m
marble shaft with a continuous spiral relief narrating the Dacian Wars in "vertical perspective,"
holding the emperor's ashes — thrice imitated later). His reign multiplied **arches** (the great
one at **Beneventum**) and provincial foundations (Timgad, Bostra), and the **Library of Celsus**
rose at Ephesus.

## Hadrian (117–138)

The philhellene **Hadrian** reshaped the visual culture through his own personality:

- His **portrait** was revolutionary — the first **bearded** emperor (the fashion lasting two
  centuries), with the new technique of the **drilled iris and pupil**; his ~250–300 surviving
  portraits outnumber any other emperor's, alongside the haunting Hellenic images of **Antinous**.
- In architecture a **modernist** (he quarrelled with and executed Apollodorus): the **Pantheon**
  (a vast plain concrete dome over a richly ornamented interior), the back-to-back **Temple of Venus
  and Rome**, his **Mausoleum**, and above all his **Villa at Tivoli** — his "architectural
  self-portrait," a landscape of daringly curvilinear, "pumpkin"-domed, mosaic-clad buildings.
- His **Hellenism** transformed **Athens** (the completed Olympieion, the Library, the new quarter
  and arch, the **Panhellenion** — "the greatest imperial provincial benefaction ever"), and the
  **classicizing marble sarcophagus** rose to popularity among the elite as cremation gave way to
  inhumation.

## The Antonines (138–192)

Under **Antoninus Pius** peace and prosperity continued the Hadrianic classicism (the Temple of the
Deified Hadrian with its **province personifications**; the **Great Antonine Altar** at Ephesus;
provincial building from the Antonine Baths of Carthage to the Temple of Bacchus at Baalbek; the
benefactions of **Herodes Atticus** across Greece). **Marcus Aurelius** commemorated his northern
wars with the **Column of Marcus Aurelius** (a darker, deeper-cut, more frontal reworking of
Trajan's Column) and the surviving **bronze equestrian statue**. **Commodus** completed the column
and ended in the megalomaniac **bust as Hercules** that "inspired his assassination."

## Significance

Ling's conclusion: by the close of the Antonine age the Roman world was "visually a splendid place"
— the empire interacting fruitfully with its Republican past and its regional cultures, drawing
above all on the still-potent **Greek** tradition for decoration and art, while generating
**breathtaking new architecture** from the **concrete tradition** (the Flavian, Trajanic, and
Hadrianic vaults and domes). The artistic harvest was not merely imperial but a **corporate
achievement**, in which even the poorest could share the great public monuments.
