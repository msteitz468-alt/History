# National Syndicalism

The doctrinal synthesis in which the syndicalist critique of democracy and
bourgeois materialism is transferred from the proletariat to the **nation**
as revolutionary subject. By the end of the First World War,
[[revolutionary-syndicalism]] had transformed into national syndicalism under
the slogan "The Fatherland Should Not Be Denied, It Should Be Conquered,"
with the **UIL** (Unione Italiana del Lavoro, Milan, 1918) as its
institutional vehicle and *Pagine libere*, *Il rinnovamento* (De Ambris),
and *L'Italia nostra* as its press. On
[[sternhell-thesis|Sternhell's]] account it supplied "the ideological
definitions and program of the original fascism."

## Doctrine

- **Productionism and producers vs. parasites**: only those who produce and
  sacrifice belong to the collectivity; the frame descends from
  [[berth-edouard|Berth]]'s distinction between "commercial and usurious
  capitalism" (the enemy) and industrial capitalism — "not a Marxist
  distinction," Sternhell notes, but one that "finally became one of the
  foundations of the social and economic doctrines of fascism."
- **Partial expropriation** ([[de-ambris-alceste|De Ambris]], 1919):
  confiscate speculative war profits ("sharks") into an investment fund for
  demobilized soldiers, sparing productive capital; distinguish the
  financial debt to bondholders from the "moral debt" to soldier-peasants —
  and his 1917 slogan "The Land to the Peasants." Presented to Fascist
  cadres at the Porta Romana School, Milan, **9 June 1919**, under
  Mussolini's auspices; the June 1919 Fascist program's socioeconomic
  platform was "almost an exact replica."
- **Corporatism**: [[panunzio-sergio|Panunzio]]'s "Program of Action" (March
  1919) — population organized into "organic classes," classes into
  corporations, social administration transferred to corporations, with a
  restricted "aristocratic" parliament; Lanzillo's bicameral corporatist
  scheme in *Il Popolo d'Italia*; and the **Carta del Carnaro** (Fiume,
  September 1920, drafted by De Ambris) as the "constitutional" expression —
  see [[fiume-expedition-1919]].
- **The proletarian nation**: [[corradini-enrico|Corradini]]'s bridge concept
  ("Italy is the proletarian of the world") plus Olivetti's "imperialism of
  the workers" bound syndicalist elitism to nationalist conquest.

## Internal split and end conditions

Until 1920 the doctrine accommodated "economists/structuralists" (De Ambris,
Leone, Labriola), for whom socioeconomic change was the end and nationalism a
means, and "ethical revolutionaries" (Olivetti, Panunzio, Lanzillo, Orano),
for whom the revolution was moral and the corporatist machinery secondary.
The Fiume failure and the 1920 factory occupations forced the split: the
ethical wing became Fascists (Rossoni founding **Fascist syndicalism**;
Olivetti's 1921 "Manifesto dei Sindacalisti": "The nation is above classes");
De Ambris judged fascism "an antirevolutionary instrument manipulated by the
bourgeoisie" and went into exile. The label was later adopted by the Spanish
[[falange|Falange]] (its full name: Falange Española de las JONS — the
Juntas de Ofensiva Nacional-Sindicalista).

## Payne's assessment (1980)

[[payne-fascism-comparison-definition-1980|Payne]] confirms the centrality
("the central role in [Fascism's] conceptual orientation was played by
revolutionary syndicalists who embraced extreme nationalism") but tempers
Sternhell's proto-fascist reading: Panunzio and Olivetti "were not devotees
of 'creative violence' and direct action for its own sake," Sorelian myth
"had little place in their schemes of national education," and "they did
not necessarily support Italian imperialism" — Panunzio published *Il
concetto della guerra giusta* (1916) distinguishing just from imperialist
war, and in 1920, as a Fascist, a booklet supporting the League of Nations.
By ~1910 most had abandoned Marxism, exploiting Corradini's "proletarian
nation" concept from as early as 1907.

Emic/etic note: "national syndicalism" is the actors' own term (Mussolini
used it of the 1919 program); "socialist-national synthesis" is Sternhell's
etic label for the broader pre-1914 convergence.
