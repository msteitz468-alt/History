# Anglo-Muhammadan Law

The hybrid legal system produced by British colonial rule in India through the translation, codification, and reinterpretation of Islamic [[fiqh|jurisprudence]] by British administrators and Orientalist scholars. The term was used by the British themselves; Hallaq's analysis in [[hallaq-sharia-2009|*Sharīʿa*]] (ch. 14) treats it as the paradigmatic case of "jural colonization" — the structural dismantling of the Sharīʿa through its capture by a colonial state.

## Definition and Scope

"Anglo-Muhammadan law therefore was the law that the British created, or caused to be created, in their Indian colony. The designation refers less to the fact that it was the British who determined a particular application of the law in an Islamic judicial and juristic context, and much more to the fact that it was a heavily distorted English legal perspective on Islamic law that was administered to Muslim individuals" (Hallaq, ch. 14).

The system was hybrid in a precise sense: it was not raw English law imposed on Muslims, nor was it the Sharīʿa as it had been practiced in India. It was a third entity — Islamic law as read through English legal categories, stripped of its Arabicate commentarial tradition, severed from customary law, and administered within an English institutional framework. "It may even be argued that Anglo-Muhammadan law at times involved the forceful application of English law as Islamic law" (e.g., *Abul Fata v. Russomoy Dhur Chowdhury*, 1894, on waqf law).

## Origins: The Hastings Plan and Jones's Digest

The immediate context was the Hastings Plan (from 1772), which established a multi-tiered colonial court system in Bengal. British judges sat at the top, assisted by local qāḍīs and muftīs (*mulavis*) on questions of Islamic law. The British distrusted these native legal advisers — finding the "staggering variety of opinion" in Islamic jurisprudence unmanageable — and sought a fixed, codified text that would make native law directly accessible without native mediation.

Sir William Jones (1746–94), Oxford classicist and leading Orientalist, proposed to Hastings the creation of a "complete digest of Hindu and Mussulman law." The justification cast Islamic law as "unsystematic, inconsistent and mostly arbitrary" — a characterization that gave Max Weber the basis for his famous concept of *Kadijustiz*. Yet Jones himself acknowledged the "wonderful analogy between the works of the Arabian and English lawyers," suggesting the problem was not legal but political: eliminating "a complete check on the native interpreters of the several codes."

The chosen vehicle was al-Marghīnānī's *Hidāya* (a twelfth-century Ḥanafī manual), translated into Persian and then into English by Charles Hamilton (1791). Jones himself translated *al-Sirājiyya* (an inheritance treatise) from Arabic a year later.

## The Mechanics of Transformation

Hallaq argues that **translation functioned as codification**: "by severing [the Hidāya] from its Arabicate interpretive and commentarial tradition, it ceased to function in the way it had done until then." The translation accomplished three goals simultaneously:

1. **Elimination of native intermediaries.** By making Islamic law directly accessible to British judges, the muftīs and mulavis were eliminated "as jural middle men, leaving the British with the sole power and prerogative to adjudicate in the name of Islamic law."

2. **Replacement of hermeneutics.** The Islamic system depended on ongoing commentary and interpretive practice (*ijtihād* within the [[madhhab|madhhab]] tradition). Translation severed this: the text was now fixed, interpreted through English legal methods, producing the caricature of a "timeless" Islamic law incapable of internal change.

3. **Silencing of customary law.** Islamic law in practice was intertwined with local customary law (*'urf*). By fixing a classical text as "the" Islamic law, customary law was cast as "primitive" and excluded from official recognition.

## Shape of the Hybrid System

The resulting system combined Islamic personal status law (on the terms set by the translated texts) with English institutional forms, English evidentiary standards, and English principles of "justice, equity and good conscience." Key features:

- **Criminal law was entirely replaced** by British law between 1790 and 1861. Islamic criminal law — including the rights of victims' families under the law of retaliation (*qiṣāṣ*) and blood-money (*diya*) — was abolished on the grounds that "the state" alone held the right to punish. By 1861, "no trace of Islamic criminal law was to survive."
- **Personal status law (marriage, divorce, inheritance)** was nominally retained but administered through English courts, English procedure, and the fixed translated texts rather than through living juristic practice.
- **Waqf law** was particularly contested. In *Abul Fata v. Russomoy Dhur Chowdhury* (1894), the Privy Council applied English trust law rather than Islamic waqf doctrine; this was only reversed by the Mussalman Waqf Validating Act (1913).
- **Land law** was transformed by the English concept of absolute ownership (*dominium*), replacing the usufruct-based entitlements of pre-colonial Muslim practice. This structural shift enriched the *zamindār* landlord class at the expense of the agrarian countryside.

## The Role of Native Collaboration

Anglo-Muhammadan law was not produced by the British alone. A Westernized Indian legal elite — trained in British institutions, employed in the colonial courts — participated in its production. Figures like Sir Syed Mahmood co-produced this hybridity, often while simultaneously resisting British domination. This created "one of the most typical ironies of colonized peoples: what they deemed to be the unjust law of occupation was the only available and legitimate means by which they could bargain against their occupiers."

The [[british-raj|British Raj]] actively cultivated a class of educated men who were, in Macaulay's formulation, "Indian in blood and color, English in taste, in opinions, in morals and in intellect."

## Systemic Consequences

Anglo-Muhammadan law produced a set of structural consequences that extended well beyond the legal domain:

- The displacement of the [[madhhab|madhhab]]-trained jurist by the Western-trained lawyer as the authoritative interpreter of Islamic law
- The loss of the [[waqf|waqf]] system's economic autonomy and its capacity to fund Islamic educational institutions independently of the state
- The transformation of Islamic law from a grass-roots, community-embedded system into a state-administered personal status law applicable only to Muslims as a religious category
- The production of "a new notion of wealth whereby ownership qua ownership of land became a desideratum" — a structural economic shift tied to colonization

## Historiography

The term "Anglo-Muhammadan law" has been used by both colonial administrators and subsequent scholars of Islamic law in South Asia. Hallaq's analysis draws on the work of Cohn, Anderson, Dirks, Singha, and Kugle to frame the creation of Anglo-Muhammadan law as an episode of colonial knowledge production — a case where Orientalist scholarship and legal administration were structurally entangled. The framework challenges the view that the British were simply "applying" pre-existing Islamic law to Muslim litigants.
