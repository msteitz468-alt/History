# Writing and Record-Keeping in Early Cities (Information Technologies)

## Definition

**Information technologies** — writing, but also non-script systems of
counting, sealing, and recording — were among the means by which early cities
were administered: tools for keeping track of goods, services, and people, and
for making a large, heterogeneous population "legible" (James Scott) and
governable. This is the theme of Part II of [[yoffee-cwh-v3-2015|CWH Vol. III]]
and one of Childe's criteria for the city ([[urbanism]], [[urban-revolution]]).

## The administrative-origins thesis

The volume's strongest cross-cultural claim: **writing originated as a tool of
economic administration, not of literature or even of religion.**

- **[[uruk|Mesopotamia / Uruk]]** (Nissen) — proto-cuneiform (~3300 BCE) grew out
  of a long sequence of accounting devices: clay **tokens** (from the Neolithic),
  tokens sealed in **bullae**, **numerical tablets**, **stamp and cylinder seals**,
  and standardized **beveled-rim bowls**. The first texts are economic records and
  **lexical lists**; early writing did not yet encode spoken language. The
  *archaeological* phasing comes from Warka: [[edwards-cah-v1p1-1970|CAH Vol. I,
  Part 1]] (Ch. VIII, Mallowan) defines the **"Protoliterate"** by the appearance
  of tablets, distinguishing the older, more pictographic **Uruk 4** series from
  the **Uruk 3** tablets already written in identifiable **Sumerian** — the
  stratigraphic anchor for the token→pictograph→language trajectory.
- **[[anyang|China / Anyang]]** (Wang Haicheng) — the oracle-bone inscriptions
  look religious (divinations to royal ancestors), but most divination bones are
  uninscribed and inscriptions are display; the *motive* for writing was
  administrative control — inventory and accountability (armies, harvests,
  rations, field surveys). Everyday records were on perishable bamboo/wood.
- **Maya** (Danny Law) — glyphic writing is bound up with cities and kingship
  (royal emblem glyphs, dedications, dynastic history) but, as among the
  [[tikal|Classic Maya]], it served display and political legitimacy as much as
  bookkeeping; there was no Maya word for "city."
- **Inka khipu** (Gary Urton) — the Andean **knotted-cord** system shows that a
  complex empire ([[tiwanaku|Andean]] successors, the Inka **Tawantinsuyu**,
  14th–15th c. CE) could run a sophisticated administration — census, tribute,
  ration distribution — **without phonetic writing** at all.

## Non-script and the limits of writing

A key corrective: writing is one option among many. Tokens, seals, standardized
containers, and khipu performed core administrative functions; the khipu proves
that "information technology" is not coextensive with "writing." Even where script
existed, much was never written (Nissen: writing was used "only when unavoidable").

## What writing did not encode

The volume stresses gaps: origins of goods, the chain from producer to consumer,
and most social knowledge went unrecorded. Records "make legible" selectively —
serving the controllers, not a neutral archive (Scott).

## From memorandum to history (CAH XII–XIII)

[[edwards-cah-v1p2-1971|CAH Vol. I, Part 2]] (Frankfort, Gadd) traces the next
step. For its first centuries writing was used purely for **memoranda about
material things** (lists of commodities, quantities, persons) plus non-utilitarian
**lexical lists** for training scribes. The decisive shift comes in
[[early-dynastic-sumer|Early Dynastic II–III]], when **kings adopt writing for
religious and political ends** — inscriptions meant "to perpetuate their acts to
posterity," and soon "to impose upon the present by reference to the past." This
is the threshold of *history* proper, reached in Babylonia and Egypt at about the
same time. Gadd also stresses that script invention was **not unique to Sumer**:
the contemporary, undeciphered **Proto-Elamite** of Susa and the later **Indus**
script were entirely independent repertoires — evidence against any single origin
of writing — even if the *development* of cuneiform was wholly Sumerian work.

## Contested points

- Whether Egyptian hieroglyphs were **independently invented** or stimulus-diffused
  from Mesopotamia (Nissen and Frankfort argue the latter — the *idea*, not the
  forms; the Egyptian Ch. XI of CAH v1p2 agrees the Sumerian contribution was
  "mainly suggestive").
- Whether early writing is best seen as a cause or a consequence of urban
  complexity (the volume: mutually constitutive — neither dominates).
