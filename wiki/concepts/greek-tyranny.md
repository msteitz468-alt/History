# Archaic Greek Tyranny

**Tyranny** (*tyrannis*, a loan-word from [[lydia|Lydia]] carrying overtones of
oriental wealthy despotism) was the seizure and personal rule of a *polis* by one man —
the recurrent political development of the prosperous Archaic age. The Greek **tyrant**
was not (yet) a byword for cruelty but simply an unconstitutional sole ruler.
[[boardman-cah-v3p3-1982|CAH Vol. III, Part 3]] (Ch. 42, N. G. L. Hammond) gives a close
account through the Peloponnesian examples.

## The link with prosperity

Thucydides observed the connection: "as Greece became more powerful and made more of the
acquisition of wealth than before, then generally **tyrannies were established as the
revenues became greater**." Hammond, following the near-contemporary poets **Solon and
Theognis** rather than the later "demagogue" theory of Aristotle, explains the mechanism:
a traditional, more-or-less hereditary **oligarchy** was disrupted by the "swing toward
capitalism" of the [[greek-colonization|colonizing]] age; new opportunities for personal
aggrandizement were exploited ruthlessly by those already powerful; **faction (*stasis*)
grew between rival oligarchic leaders**; and out of that bloodshed "the result is one-man
rule." The struggle was thus, at first, **not rich against poor but oligarch against
oligarch** — the common people, "without experience of power, showed their witlessness in
trusting those who abused their trust." Herodotus put the same analysis in Darius's
mouth: personal feuds in an oligarchy lead to dissension, then bloodshed, then *monarchia*.

## The great Peloponnesian tyrannies

- **Corinth — the Cypselids (c. 657–583).** The closed, endogamous **[[corinth|Bacchiad]]**
  oligarchy, strained by the new commercial wealth, fell to **Cypselus** (657) — himself a
  Bacchiad by marriage and office, leading a faction within the clan (and aided by
  [[argos|Pheidon]]). His son **Periander**, rated one of the Seven Sages, ran a powerful
  commercial-colonial state but acquired the later reputation of every crime. The
  Cypselids styled themselves *basileis* ("kings"), claimed impeccable Dorian-Heraclid
  descent, and dedicated lavishly (the Chest of Cypselus, the gold bowl from Heraclea) —
  "a change not of principle but of family."
- **Sicyon — the Orthagorids (c. 655–556).** A century-long dynasty culminating in
  **Cleisthenes** (c. 600–570), victor at the Pythian Games, commander of the
  Amphictyonic forces in the [[delphi|First Sacred War]], who insultingly renamed the
  three Dorian tribes ("Hogmen, Assmen, Swinemen") to spite Argos and its hero Adrastus.
- **Megara — Theagenes**, who won the people "by slaughtering the cattle of the rich"
  before seizing power.

These three formed "a continuous area of tyrant-land, uniquely at peace with one another
for perhaps a century": **"tyrant rarely ate tyrant,"** for it was in their common
interest to combine against the exiles and cultivate "the media" — **[[delphi|Delphi]] and
Olympia**.

## How tyrants ruled, and fell

Far from social revolutionaries, the tyrants **conserved the aristocratic order**,
killing or banishing rival houses but otherwise governing with moderation, justice and
public works (Corinth's fountains and the **diolkos** ship-runway across the Isthmus;
Megara's aqueduct; the Leucas canal), funded by harbour and market dues rather than
direct taxation. Aristotle credited their long lives to respect for law, popular favour,
and military success. Their fall — Corinth c. 583, Sicyon c. 556 — was an occasion for
"thanksgiving and rejoicing in freedom" (the **Isthmian and Nemean Games** were founded
in the wake), but Hammond stresses the **"poisonous effects"** revealed afterward: the
suppression of initiative and trust, and a relapse into *stasis* (vividly in Theognis's
Megara, where "the people are different... the good men of old are worthless now"). Into
this Peloponnesian flux stepped **[[sparta]]**, "exempt from tyranny herself," which from
c. 560 systematically **put down the tyrants** in favour of Laconizing oligarchies — the
ideological engine of the [[peloponnesian-league|Spartan Alliance]].

## The Athenian variant: Pisistratus

[[boardman-cah-v3p3-1982|CAH III.3]] Ch. 44 (Andrewes) supplies the best-documented
example in **[[pisistratus|Pisistratus]] of Athens** (tyrant intermittently from c. 561,
firmly 546–528/7). It confirms the Peloponnesian pattern — Pisistratus rose out of
**aristocratic faction** (the "plain," "coast" and "hill" parties), reaching power not by
social revolution but, after two failures, "with mercenaries and money" — while showing
the tyrant at his most constructive: he **left [[solon|Solon's]] constitution intact**
("the city retained its laws, except as regards appointment to office"), reconciled the
nobility, aided the farmers, and fostered the great public cults (the Panathenaea, the
City Dionysia and the birth of drama). His rule was remembered as "the golden age of
Cronus" — the benign pole of Archaic tyranny, against which the harsher reputation of his
sons (and of a Phalaris) stands out.

## Significance

- The defining **political pathology of the Archaic polis** — the way the strains of new
  wealth on old aristocracies were resolved, and a recurring stage between aristocracy and
  the broader constitutions to come.
- A **constructive as well as destructive force**: the tyrants built infrastructure,
  patronized the arts (Arion's dithyramb at Corinth, "tragic choruses" at Sicyon), and
  fostered cults (Dionysus) of universal appeal — yet bequeathed the *stasis* that
  weakened the city-states.
