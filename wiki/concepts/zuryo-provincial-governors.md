## Definition and Origin

Zuryō (受領, literally "recipient custodian") denotes the class of provincial governors in Heian Japan who bore sole personal financial responsibility for all state assets within their provincial jurisdiction. The term distinguishes custodial governors from "commissioned" (nin'yō) provincial officials who held formal appointments but no fiscal accountability.

The institution was not created by a single edict but crystallized gradually out of reforms beginning in 731, when the government instituted the discharge (geyu) system requiring outgoing governors to submit to a comprehensive accounting by incoming successors. The Board of Discharge Examiners (Kageyushi), probably established between 795 and 797 and reorganized permanently from 825, was the administrative machinery that enforced this accountability. By the mid-9th century, the governor of Bungo Province (879 petition) could complain that his commissioned assistants obstructed his administration, and the Council of State's response — granting the governor a judicial monopoly over all but the highest-ranking officials — marks the decisive institutional moment. By 897, mere commissioned officers were ruled entirely unaccountable to the Board of Discharge Examiners.

## Structural Features

### The Custodial Principle
The core of the zuryō system was that one official — normally the governor (kokushi) — assumed personal responsibility for the entire stock of government assets: stored tax-grain (shōzei), public-allowance rice (kugeto), granaries, and official inventory. Incoming governors were required to inventory all such assets and detect any deficiencies within 120 days; failure to do so made the incoming governor jointly liable. Outgoing governors could not receive new appointments until their successors issued a discharge certificate (geyujō).

This adversarial, fundamentally bilateral structure of accountability differed from the collective bureau-level accountability presumed by the organic ritsuryo code (ritsuryō). It produced what the CHJ calls a "custodial governorship" (zuryō kokushi), as distinct from the original collegial model in which governor, vice-governor, executive officer, and inspector shared responsibility.

### Concentration of Power
The geyu system rapidly concentrated functional authority in the hands of a single zuryō governor:

- **879**: Governor of Bungo granted judicial monopoly; commissioned officers ruled unable to pass judgment on gun magistrates.
- **897**: Commissioned officers ruled wholly unaccountable to the Board of Discharge Examiners.
- **Early 10th century**: Governors began bringing personal staffs of civil and military assistants — something the organic code had prohibited.

### Revenue Base
The zuryō's compensation came primarily from public-allowance rice (kugeto) — the governor's share of the interest income from the provincial stored tax-grain loan system (suiko). The allotment of shares in 757 gave the governor 6 units, the vice-governor 4, the executive officer 3, and so on downward. This personal stake in the loan revenue system gave the governor a structural incentive to maximize suiko collections and created a tension with local gentry who sought to divert the same stored grain.

By the 10th century, governors also commanded discretionary levies (rinji zōyaku — "extraordinary irregular levies") that had emerged as provincial precedent since the Jōgan era (859–877) and had no basis in the original ritsuryo code.

### "Remote Appointments" and Non-Resident Governors
Not all holders of provincial governor titles were custodial governors. "Remote appointments" (yonin) conferred the income of a provincial governorship without requiring physical presence — a device used as late sinecure compensation for capital officials. Prince-of-the-blood provinces (shinno ninkoku), established from 826, were the most formalized version: provinces of Kazusa, Hitachi, and Kōzuke were assigned to imperial princes who received the title of supreme governor (taishū) and the accompanying income while remaining in the capital. Their vice-governors (ōsuke) served as the actual custodial governors.

## The Zuryō as Fiscal Intermediary

By the 10th century, the zuryō occupied a structurally ambiguous position between the capital and the countryside:

- He was legally responsible for producing specified revenue quotas from his province.
- He was practically dependent on local gentry (tato, gunji) to manage cultivation, collect dues, and provide the commodities the capital required.
- He was empowered by provincial precedent to impose discretionary taxes (rinji zōyaku) but had no direct access to census-based population controls, which had become largely fictitious.

The Miyoshi Kiyoyuki sealed memorial of 914 and Sugawara no Michizane's 891 memorial against the dispatch of tax-grain auditors (kenzeishi) both articulate the logic: good governors needed broad discretionary authority precisely because the ritsuryo tax system could no longer be literally applied. To function economically, governors had to resort to extralegal arrangements (the fictional lending-back of tax-grain, negotiated damage reports, selective enforcement of suiko quotas) in order to meet their legal obligations. They were, as CHJ vol. 2, ch. 4 states, "forced to resort to illegal actions in order to meet their legal obligations."

### Evaluation and Sanction
A zuryō could be penalized in three ways:
1. Bureaucratic reprimand from central accounting offices for revenue shortfalls.
2. Discharge dispute before the Board of Discharge Examiners, initiated by a successor.
3. Impeachment by subordinate local officers — which Kiyoyuki found outrageous and sought to restrict.

The 988 Owari Province petition (Owari no kuni gebumi) is the fullest surviving example of the third mechanism in practice: gun magistrates and tato farmers compiled a 31-article indictment against an incumbent zuryō governor, demonstrating both the institutionalization of gentry political agency and the limits of gubernatorial power.

## Zuryō and Shōen

The zuryō's relationship to [[shoen]] was structurally ambivalent. Shōen proprietors sought exemption from his irregular levies; he routinely challenged new reclamation within shōen boundaries and sought to bring it back under taxable status. Yet the same discretionary power that threatened shōen could also protect them: "this power of the kokuga was most important for the later development of the shoen, as it enabled the governors to establish partially immune holdings in their provinces without permission from Kyoto" (CHJ vol. 2, ch. 4). Some governors established their own tsukuda (possessory fields) distributed across a province under gentry management, appropriating a share of the proceeds.

## Decline

The zuryō institution did not disappear with the end of the Heian period but was gradually absorbed into the emerging warrior governance structures. As the 10th and 11th centuries progressed, the kokuga (provincial headquarters) evolved toward an estate management agency, with zuryō appointments increasingly granted for the purpose of accomplishing special projects or raising funds for capital ceremonies. The institutional logic of the zuryō — one accountable custodian extracting maximum revenue through negotiation with local gentry — was eventually superseded by the superimposition of jitō (military land stewards) under the Kamakura shogunate.

## Historiography

The zuryō has been the subject of major Japanese historical scholarship: Yoshimura Shigeki, Abe Takeshi, Murai Yasuhiko, Toda Yoshimi, and Satō Shin'ichi are among the principal researchers. The CHJ vol. 2, ch. 4 synthesizes this body of work, emphasizing the geyu discharge system as the institutional generator of the zuryō and the importance of discretionary taxation (rinji zōyaku and shōzei koeki exchange) in understanding how the 10th-century provincial state actually functioned. The 988 Owari petition is the single most important primary source for the mature institution.
