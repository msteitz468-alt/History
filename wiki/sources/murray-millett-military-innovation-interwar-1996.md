# Military Innovation in the Interwar Period (Murray & Millett, eds., 1996)

The wiki's anchor source for **[[military-innovation|military innovation]]** and the cluster of
interwar doctrines that decided the opening campaigns of the Second World War. Its method is
**controlled comparison**: hold the period (1918–1939) and the conditions (austerity + rapid
technological change) constant, vary the national institution, and ask *why* the Germans built
a war-winning armored force while the French and British did not; why the Americans and Japanese
built carrier fleets while the Royal Navy fell behind; why Britain alone fielded an integrated
radar air-defense system in time for 1940.

## The framework: three lenses on innovation

The editors asked every contributor to analyze their case through three concepts:
- **Strategic framework** — the strategic problem the service faced, how it viewed potential
  enemies and its mission, and the lessons it drew from the First World War.
- **Organizational factors** — service administration, national/cultural preferences,
  **professional military education**, and whether the institution's culture encouraged or
  smothered new ideas.
- **Doctrinal framework** — the seriousness of the service's commitment to *doctrine*: drawing
  the right lessons from 1914–1918 and incorporating them into training.

Their conclusion (chs. 8–10) is on the [[military-innovation]] page: successful innovation was
less about lone visionaries or even technology than about **institutional culture, a correctly
posed strategic problem, realistic doctrine tested by experiment, and senior leaders who
protected the innovators** — with Germany's combined-arms armor and Dowding's air-defense system
as the models, and the "innovation ignored" of anti-submarine warfare as the cautionary tale.
