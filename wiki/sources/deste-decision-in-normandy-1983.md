# Decision in Normandy (Carlo D'Este, 1983/2015)

Carlo D'Este's detailed operational history of the largest amphibious invasion in history and the eighty-day campaign that followed. A major revisionist work that strips away the mythology surrounding Montgomery's generalship in Normandy, particularly the claim that the "master plan" always envisioned the British/Canadian Second Army acting solely as a holding shield on the eastern flank while the Americans broke out in the west.

The book covers the full arc: the long road to the Second Front, the evolution of the Overlord plan and the Overlord commanders, D-Day and the battle for Caen, the controversial operations Epsom, Goodwood and Cobra, the breakout and encirclement at Falaise, and a searching examination of the "Normandy Myth." D'Este argues that Caen was vital to the original plan, that the failure to secure it early had grave consequences, and that Montgomery's post-war account was a retrospective construction that did not match contemporary planning documents and actions.

## Consolidated Volume Synthesis Note

D'Este's *Decision in Normandy* is the single most detailed operational study of the 1944 campaign in the collection. Its core contribution is the demolition (with contemporary planning documents, Dempsey notes, Eisenhower correspondence, and participant interviews) of the post-war "Montgomery master plan" narrative that the British/Canadian role was always intended solely as a holding action to draw German armour while the Americans broke out in the west. Pre-D-Day evidence shows aggressive intent to seize Caen and the Caen-Falaise Plain early for airfields, maneuver space, and initiative. The failure to do so produced six weeks of costly attrition that the British Army (a "wasting asset") could ill afford. GOODWOOD was largely Dempsey's conception to break the stalemate with massed armour; its mixed results and Montgomery's optimistic public claims produced a major furore. The Falaise controversy is presented with primary evidence from all sides; no single villain but command friction, competing priorities, and German determination played roles. The book pairs perfectly with D'Este's own *Bitter Victory* (Sicily) for patterns of cautious planning and missed opportunities, and with West Point Vol. 2 / Citino and van Creveld for the broader operational synthesis and logistics realities. It supplies the deep tactical/operational and command-detail layer the warfare hub requires for Normandy.

## Historiography Note

D'Este is sharply critical of the retrospective "master plan" story. He demonstrates through planning documents, contemporary correspondence, and Dempsey's notes that early emphasis on Caen was real and that its capture was essential to the shield concept and to British maneuver space. The failure to take it on D-Day or in the first weeks forced costly set-piece battles (Epsom, Goodwood) that the British Army's manpower situation could ill afford. Goodwood is treated as Dempsey's attempt to break the stalemate with armor; its limited results and Montgomery's public claims produced a major "furore." The Falaise controversy is presented with primary evidence from all sides; no single villain but systemic issues in command relationships and exploitation. Complements (and sometimes corrects) the official histories, Hastings' *Overlord*, and the West Point/Citino account. Excellent on the "face of battle" for senior commanders under pressure and on the gap between intent, execution, and postwar narrative. Pairs with D'Este's own *Bitter Victory* (Sicily) for pattern of cautious planning and missed opportunities in the Mediterranean/Western campaigns.
