# The Age of Extremes: The Short Twentieth Century 1914–1991 (Eric Hobsbawm)

The capstone of **Hobsbawm's** four-volume modern history: his interpretation of the **"short twentieth
century"** from the outbreak of the First World War to the collapse of the Soviet Union. Sixth source of
**Gap 1**; the interpretive spine for the **[[world-wars-era|World Wars Era]]** and the
**[[cold-war|Cold War]]**.

## The Argument

Hobsbawm divides the century into three movements:

- **The Age of Catastrophe (1914–1945).** Two world wars, the Russian Revolution and the rise of
  Communism, the Great Depression, the collapse of liberal capitalism's confidence, fascism and Nazism,
  and the near-self-destruction of bourgeois civilization — the era when "the world ceased to work."
- **The Golden Age (c. 1947–1973).** An unprecedented post-war boom — sustained growth, the mixed
  economy and welfare state, mass consumption, and a "social and cultural revolution" that transformed
  everyday life (the decline of the peasantry, the rise of youth culture, women's emancipation) — under
  the stabilizing standoff of the Cold War.
- **The Landslide / Crisis Decades (1973–1991).** The end of the boom, the unravelling of the Soviet
  system and its collapse in 1989–91, the crisis of both the Communist and the social-democratic models,
  and a world left "in disorder and without a system" of clear successor.

Throughout, Hobsbawm stresses the century's **extremes** — of mass death and of material progress — and
reads the short century as bounded by the two great crises of 1914 and 1991.

## Significance

*The Age of Extremes* is one of the most influential single-volume interpretations of the **twentieth
century**, and the natural narrative backbone for the wiki's [[world-wars-era|World Wars Era]] and
[[cold-war|Cold War]] periods. Its "Golden Age" and "Age of Catastrophe" framings recur throughout
modern historiography. (On the USSR it is best read alongside less sympathetic accounts.)
