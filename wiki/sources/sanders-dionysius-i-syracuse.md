# Dionysius I of Syracuse and Greek Tyranny (Lionel J. Sanders)

A full-length **reassessment of [[dionysius-i|Dionysius I]]** (c. 430–367 BCE), the most powerful Greek
ruler of his day — and a study of how the **image of the "tyrant"** was made. The ninth source of the
Phase-9 batch.

## The Argument

Dionysius comes down to us as the archetypal **tyrant in the worst sense** — cruel, suspicious,
power-mad — the model behind Plato's critique of tyranny and the "sword of Damocles" anecdote. Sanders
argues this picture is **largely the creation of a hostile tradition**:

- the surviving narrative (Diodorus) draws ultimately on the bitterly **hostile Timaeus**, displacing the
  lost, well-informed and more favourable **Philistus** (Dionysius's own associate);
- the **Academy** and the Platonic tradition, stung by the failure of philosophy's engagement with
  Syracuse, had a motive to **blacken** the tyrant's reputation as propaganda.

Seen past this distortion, Sanders contends, Dionysius was a **capable and significant ruler**: he forged
the strongest state in the Greek world, **defended Greek Sicily against Carthage** (the wars, the siege
and capture of **Motya**), extended Syracusan power into south Italy and the Adriatic, and patronized
culture. The book is thus both a biography and a case-study in **historiography** — how sources and
ideology manufacture a reputation.

## Significance

The study gives the wiki a **revisionist, source-critical** treatment of the great Western Greek tyrant
and a clear example of the broader problem of the **hostile "tyrant" tradition** in Greek
historiography. It enriches the wiki's coverage of **[[western-greek-tyranny|Western Greek tyranny]]**,
[[syracuse|Syracuse]], and the long Sicilian struggle with [[carthage|Carthage]].
