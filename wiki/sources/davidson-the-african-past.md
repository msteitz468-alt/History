# The African Past: Chronicles from Antiquity to Modern Times (Davidson, 1964)

A landmark **anthology of primary sources** for African history, assembled by Basil Davidson to
demonstrate — against the then-dominant colonial dismissal — that Africa possesses a rich,
documented past. It gathers the written and oral record from Greco-Roman antiquity to the colonial
period and binds it with Davidson's interpretive commentary.

## What it is

The book reproduces extracts from the major external and internal sources for pre-colonial Africa,
arranged by region and era: ancient Nubia/Kush, Aksum and the *Periplus* for the Red Sea coast; the
**East African (Swahili) coast** through al-Mas'udi, Ibn Battuta, the **Kilwa Chronicle**, and the
Portuguese; the **Western Sudan** (Ghana, Mali, Songhai) through al-Bakri, Ibn Battuta, Ibn
Khaldun, and Leo Africanus; the forest kingdoms, Kongo, and Great Zimbabwe through Portuguese
accounts; and the era of the slave trade and European penetration. Davidson's purpose is openly
**historiographical** (see below): to show that the materials for an African history exist and
have simply been ignored.

## What it contributes

- **[[african-historiography]]** — a new concept page on the recovery of the African past and its
  four kinds of evidence (Arabic documentary sources, European accounts, oral tradition,
  archaeology) — Davidson's central theme.
- Primary-source enrichment of the **[[swahili-coast]]** (the Kilwa Chronicle, Ibn Battuta, the
  *Periplus*) and the Western Sudan ([[ghana-empire]], [[mali-empire]] via al-Bakri, Ibn Battuta,
  Leo Africanus).

## Reading note

Africa is a flagged gap region and the anthology is rich, so it is read closely for its documentary
voices; Davidson's nationalist-era framing and each source's own bias are flagged, not adopted.
Complements the already-ingested UNESCO **General History of Africa** and Cambridge History of
Africa coverage with the **primary-source record itself**.
