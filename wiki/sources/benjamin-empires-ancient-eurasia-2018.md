# Empires of Ancient Eurasia: The First Silk Roads Era, 100 BCE–250 CE

Craig Benjamin (Professor of History, Grand Valley State University), Cambridge
University Press, *New Approaches to Asian History* series, 2018.

## Overview

A thematic synthesis of the **First Silk Roads Era** — the roughly 350-year period
(c. late 2nd century BCE to mid-3rd century CE) during which the Afro-Eurasian world
zone was linked into a single interconnected exchange system. Benjamin's central
argument: large-scale Silk Roads trade and cultural diffusion were **predicated on the
political and economic stability of four great imperial states** — the [[han-dynasty|Han Dynasty]],
the [[kushan-empire|Kushan Empire]], the [[parthian-empire|Parthian Empire]], and the
[[roman-empire|Roman Empire]] — but were equally dependent on **pastoral-nomadic and
mercantile intermediaries** (Xiongnu, Yuezhi, [[sogdians|Sogdians]]) who controlled the
stretches of the network no agrarian empire ever fully held. The network was therefore
*trans-ecological* (David Christian), not merely trans-civilizational.

The book treats the silk-driven luxury trade as the engine but insists the truly
world-historical cargo was **non-material**: religions (above all [[spread-of-buddhism|Buddhism]]),
art (Gandharan/Mathuran sculpture), technologies, languages, and — late in the era —
**epidemic disease**. Its closing argument is one of systemic **vulnerability**: the
near-simultaneous collapse of Han, Kushan, and Parthian power plus Rome's
[[crisis-of-the-third-century|third-century crisis]] dismantled the integrated network.

## Conceptual Framework

Benjamin reads the era through four overlapping macro-historical lenses, all of which
become or update wiki concept pages:

- **Big history** ([[big-history]]) — the Silk Roads as a "goldilocks" gear-shift that
  magnified *collective learning*, the human capacity to store and exchange information.
- **World-systems theory** ([[world-systems-theory]]) — Wallerstein's framework (world
  economy / world empire / minisystem), extended back to antiquity by Frank & Gills and
  Abu-Lughod; the Roman–Parthian relationship read as an ancient "world economy."
- **The human web** ([[human-web]]) — J.R. & W.H. McNeill's reconceptualization of
  history as thickening webs of connection.
- **Ancient globalization** ([[proto-globalization]]) — Justin Jennings's eight trends
  (time–space compression, standardization, homogenization, vulnerability, etc.) applied
  to the First Silk Roads Era.

The term *Silk Roads* itself (die Seidenstrassen) was coined by Ferdinand von Richthofen
in 1877 and is anachronistic to the ancient actors — a historiographical point Benjamin
foregrounds.

## Volume Synthesis Note

Benjamin's book supplies the wiki with its first **specialist, thesis-driven account of the
First Silk Roads Era as a single integrated system** — a corrective and complement to the
collection's existing Silk Roads coverage ([[hansen-silk-road-2012|Hansen]], the Cambridge
volumes). Where [[hansen-silk-road-2012|Hansen]] is revisionist-minimalist (local trade,
military subsidy, the routes as "a new history" of small communities), Benjamin is
synthetic-maximalist: he reconstructs the **four-empire architecture** (Han, Kushan,
Parthian, Roman) and the **nomadic intermediaries** (Xiongnu, Yuezhi, Sogdians) that made
trans-Eurasian exchange possible, and frames the whole through big history, world-systems
theory, the human web, and ancient-globalization theory. The two are not in contradiction
so much as different scales — Hansen on what moved on the ground, Benjamin on the imperial
and ecological system that let anything move at all; both agree the romantic "endless
caravans of silk" image is a myth and that the cargo that mattered most was non-material
(religion, art, disease).

Three contributions are distinctive. First, the book closes the wiki's most serious gap on
the **Kushan Empire** — supplying a full dynastic line (Kujula → the two Vimas → Kanishka →
Huvishka → Vasudeva), the Rabatak Inscription, the Begram treasure, and the
Gandharan/Mathuran art that carried [[spread-of-buddhism|Buddhism]] east — material the
existing CWH/Thapar coverage only sketched. Second, it gives the wiki its **causal spine for
the era's opening**: the [[xiongnu]]→[[yuezhi]]→[[zhang-qian-mission-138bce|Zhang Qian]]
chain reaction, with an explicit counterfactual (no Yuezhi rejection → no Kushans → arguably
no Silk Roads). Third, it supplies a **systemic account of the era's close** — the
near-simultaneous third-century imperial collapses plus epidemic disease as the
"vulnerability" of an over-connected world ([[collapse-of-first-silk-roads-era-250]]).

A cross-volume tension worth flagging: Benjamin endorses the **revisionist reading of Rome's
[[crisis-of-the-third-century|third-century crisis]]** (serious but not existential), which
sits alongside the wiki's [[fall-of-rome-causes]] controversy and the more catastrophist CAH
framing — a productive disagreement rather than a factual conflict. On Kushan chronology
(Kanishka's accession) the book follows Harry Falk's c. 127 CE; the field remains open, and
the dispute is recorded in [[kanishka]] and [[kushan-empire]] rather than promoted to a
standalone controversy page.
