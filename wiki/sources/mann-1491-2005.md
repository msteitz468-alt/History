# 1491: New Revelations of the Americas Before Columbus (Charles C. Mann, 2005)

The wiki's foundational narrative source for the **pre-Columbian Western Hemisphere**, opening the
Americas leg of the collection's biggest remaining gap. Mann frames the book around the overturning of
what he calls **"Holmberg's Mistake"** — the long assumption that Native Americans were timeless,
unchanging primitives living lightly on a pristine continent — and the three revisionist findings that
replaced it: the Americas held **more people**, for **longer**, who **shaped their landscapes** far more
profoundly than was believed. The post-Columbian "wilderness" the European settlers found was in large
part an **artefact of catastrophe** — the depopulation of a managed continent by introduced epidemics.

## Volume Synthesis Note

*1491* is the wiki's **first narrative spine for the pre-Columbian Western Hemisphere** — the opening of
the collection's long-flagged "Weak — archaeology only" Americas gap — and it enters not as a neutral
survey but as the **revisionist ("High Counter") case**, organised around the overturning of
[[holmbergs-mistake|Holmberg's Mistake]]: the myth of the timeless, ahistorical, ecologically passive
Indian. Mann's three revisions structure the nine pages created here. **More populous** →
[[pre-columbian-american-population]] (enriched: Dobyns, the 90–112M high count, Henige's critique) and
its dark corollary [[virgin-soil-epidemics|the Great Dying]] (the epidemics that often ran ahead of
Europeans, emptying a managed continent). **Earlier** → [[clovis-first-vs-pre-clovis]] (enriched with
Monte Verde 1997) and the recognition of **independent American Neolithic Revolutions**
([[maize-domestication|maize from teosinte]]) producing autonomous cradles — the Andean
[[norte-chico|Norte Chico]] (contemporary with Sumer) and Mesoamerica's [[olmec|Olmec]]-to-
[[maya-civilization|Maya]] lineage. **More engineered** → [[terra-preta|the built Amazon]] and the
[[pristine-myth|managed-landscape thesis]] (the "virgin wilderness" as post-catastrophe regrowth). The
[[haudenosaunee-confederacy|Haudenosaunee]] coda turns the argument outward: Native America as a
participant that shaped the modern idea of liberty, not merely conquest's victim. The umbrella
[[pre-columbian-americas]] page ties it all together and reconnects the wiki's older archaeology-derived
pages ([[teotihuacan]], [[aztec-empire]], [[inca-empire]], [[cahokia]], [[moche]], [[tiwanaku]]).

The single unifying thread is **agency**: against both the "Noble Savage" and "feeble barbarian" poles,
Mann's Americas are full of people who **made history and made landscapes** — bred an impossible staple
crop, built cities in a rainless desert and an "impossible" rainforest, invented zero, and wrote
constitutions. The book's method is synthetic science journalism, not original research, and its great
value to the wiki is **connective and reframing**: it gives the scattered archaeological pages a narrative
and an argument.

**Cross-source tensions and what remains.** *1491* deliberately champions the revisionist case, and the
pages flag the standing counter-critiques on-page — **Betty Meggers** (Amazonian carrying capacity, the
mega-Niño ceiling), **David Henige** and the low counters (population), **Thomas Vale** ("the myth of the
humanized landscape"), and the skeptics of the Constitution-influence thesis. The **magnitudes** —
population totals, pre-Clovis dates, the extent of *terra preta*, the reality of the influence thesis —
remain genuinely open; the *direction* of the revision is now mainstream. This is the **overview and
demography** layer of the Americas gap; the specialist sources staged next will deepen and balance it:
**Berdan** on the Aztecs and **D'Altroy** on the Inca (civilizational depth), the primary sources
(**Popol Vuh**, **Broken Spears**, **Bernal Díaz**, **Guamán Poma** — Indigenous and conquest voices),
and **Burkholder & Johnson** / **Galeano** on the colonial-and-modern Latin America that followed contact.
