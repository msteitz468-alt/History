# The Cambridge History of Japan, Vol. 4: Early Modern Japan (Hall ed., 1991)

The deep, interdisciplinary authority on **early modern Japan (c. 1550–1800)** — the
"daimyo phenomenon" and the sixteenth-century unification, the establishment of the
Tokugawa hegemony, and the long "Great Peace" of the Edo period. Its task here is to
**deepen and refine** the existing early-modern spine pages ([[azuchi-momoyama-period]],
[[edo-period]], [[oda-nobunaga]], [[toyotomi-hideyoshi]], [[tokugawa-ieyasu]], [[sakoku]],
[[shogunate-bakufu]], [[samurai]]) with current scholarship, and to add discrete-topic
pages for the institutions and culture the spine assumed.

The volume's organizing thesis (Hall's Introduction) is that the Azuchi-Momoyama and Edo
periods form **one continuous institutional transformation** — the birth and maturation of
the **bakuhan system** (a shogunate ruling the country through a coalition of autonomous
daimyo domains, *han*) resting on the **kokudaka system** of land management born of
Hideyoshi's cadastral survey. The deep process beneath unification was *hei-nō-bunri* — the
separation of a disarmed peasantry in the villages from a samurai class drawn off into the
daimyo's castle towns — and the conversion of warriors from enfeoffed fief-holders into
salaried, bureaucratic stipendiaries. Western historians read this as "early modern";
Japanese historians as "late/centralized feudal" (*kinsei*) — a periodization debate the
volume foregrounds.

## Volume Synthesis Note

CHJ Vol. 4 did for the wiki's **early modern Japan** what Vols 1 and 3 did for the ancient
and medieval periods: it put scholarly authority and analytical depth behind the spine that
[[mason-caiger-history-of-japan|Mason & Caiger]] and [[sansom-short-cultural-history-japan|Sansom]]
had sketched — creating six discrete-topic pages ([[bakuhan-system]], [[kokudaka-system]],
[[hideyoshi-korea-invasions-1592-1598]], [[christianity-in-japan]], [[tokugawa-economy]],
[[chonin-culture]]) and deepening eleven existing ones. Its through-line is the editor's
explicit thesis: the Azuchi-Momoyama and Edo periods are **one continuous institutional
transformation** — the birth and maturation of a *sui generis* early-modern order that is
neither European "absolutism" nor simple "feudalism."

The institutional core is the **[[bakuhan-system|bakuhan]]/[[kokudaka-system|kokudaka]]**
settlement. [[toyotomi-hideyoshi|Hideyoshi's]] cadastral survey, by registering one cultivator
per parcel and assessing all land in rice yield, abolished the overlapping-rights order of the
[[medieval-shoen|medieval shōen]], accomplished ***hei-nō-bunri*** (the separation of a
disarmed peasantry from a castle-town [[samurai|samurai]] class), and made the hegemon supreme
proprietor — the template [[tokugawa-ieyasu|Ieyasu]] inherited. Hall's central revision is that
the Tokugawa **ruled *through* the daimyo, not over them**: the shogun held a quarter of the
land and governed the rest via a balanced coalition of *shinpan*/*fudai*/*tozama* *han*,
controlled by the Buke shohatto, attainder, and *sankin-kōtai* — "the shogun's government …
essentially an expanded version of *han* government" (with Bolitho restoring the *han* as the
only government most Japanese knew). Over the seventeenth century the samurai were converted
from enfeoffed fief-holders into salaried, bureaucratic stipendiaries — the deep social
"subcurrent" of the age.

The volume's **foreign** thread (Elisonas) shows the same state turning outward and then
slamming shut: the "inseparable trinity" of war, trade, and piracy culminated in
**[[hideyoshi-korea-invasions-1592-1598|Hideyoshi's catastrophic Korea invasions]]** (which
devastated Korea, drained the Ming, and hastened the Toyotomi fall), while the
**[[christianity-in-japan|Kirishitan century]]** — Christianity entering on the Portuguese
trade and rooting in Kyūshū — provoked the anti-Christian persecution whose logical endpoint
was **[[sakoku|seclusion]]** (the *sakoku-rei* fusing the ban on travel, the reduction of the
European presence, and the extirpation of Christianity). The **economic and cultural**
chapters (Furushima, Nakai & McClain, Tsuji, Bitō, Najita, Hanley, Shively) trace what the
long peace produced: a **[[tokugawa-economy|commercial and agricultural revolution]]** that
made Japan one of the most urbanized societies on earth, a low-status but wealthy
**[[chonin-culture|chōnin]]** society generating [[genroku-culture|Genroku culture]], a
fiscal crisis driving the three great reforms, and a **secularized, plural intellectual
world** ([[tokugawa-intellectual-life]]; [[neo-confucianism|Neo-Confucianism]] without
examinations, Sorai's historicism seeding kokugaku) — all read, against the older Marxist
frame, as the broadly shared foundation of Japan's later modernization.

Where it touches other volumes it reinforces and refines: it gives the Mason political spine
its deep institutional underpinning, supplies the *bakuhan*/*kokudaka*/chōnin concepts that
Mason and Sansom assumed, and connects to the China/Korea pages ([[ming-dynasty]];
[[hideyoshi-korea-invasions-1592-1598|the Imjin War]]) and the comparative
[[neo-confucianism|East Asian Confucian]] story. It is the authoritative base for early
modern Japan to ~1800; the nineteenth- and twentieth-century Cambridge volumes (5–6) remain
to give the later periods the same depth.
