# Slavery, Gender, Truth, and Power in Luke-Acts and Other Ancient Narratives

Christy Cobb's revised dissertation (Palgrave Macmillan, 2019) reads three enslaved female
characters (each a *paidiskē*, "slave-girl") in the two-part early-Christian narrative of
[[luke-acts|Luke-Acts]]: the slave-girl who identifies Peter at the high priest's fire
(Luke 22:54–62), **Rhoda** who answers the door at Mary's house (Acts 12:12–19), and the
spirit-possessed prophesying slave-girl at Philippi whom Paul exorcises (Acts 16:16–18).
Cobb argues that, against antiquity's assumption that enslaved bodies could yield truth
only under torture, these three women function as uncoerced **truth-tellers** —
[[enslaved-truth-teller|the enslaved truth-teller]] — whose marginal "outsidedness" lets
them see what the powerful free men around them cannot.

## Method and Argument

The book braids three methods: Bakhtinian [[dialogism-and-carnival|dialogism, polyphony,
and carnival]]; Kristeva's intertextuality (reading the Lukan scenes against the
[[ancient-novel|Greek and Roman novels]] — Chariton's *Callirhoe*, Achilles Tatius's
*Leucippe* — and the [[apocryphal-acts-of-the-apostles|Apocryphal Acts]]); and Schüssler
Fiorenza's feminist hermeneutics, organized by the concept of [[kyriarchy]] (the
intersecting domination of gender, class, and status). It also reads
[[slavery-in-early-christianity|material culture]] — slaves on Greco-Roman funerary
monuments — as a feminist source alongside the texts.

## Historical Content Drawn On (the wiki's interest)

- The scale and ideology of Roman slavery: enslaved persons perhaps 10–20% of the empire
  (Scheidel), treated as bodies/property, denied *logos*, subject to corporal and sexual
  use; the "social death" of the slave (Patterson) and the bond between truth and torture
  (duBois). See [[slavery-in-early-christianity]] and the general [[slavery]] page.
- The persistence of slavery's logic **into** early Christianity (Glancy): baptismal
  equality (Galatians 3:28) coexisting with continued enslavement; manumission ambiguous.
- Dating and genre of [[luke-acts|Luke-Acts]] (Luke c. 80–90 CE; Acts possibly into the
  second century per the Acts Seminar) and its place among ancient novelistic literature.

## Note on Scope

As a tightly focused literary-theological monograph, this source touches a small set of
pages deeply rather than generating a broad network. Its durable contribution to the wiki
is (1) a synthesis of the historiography of ancient/early-Christian slavery centered on
enslaved women, and (2) concept pages for the textual genres (Luke-Acts, the ancient novel,
the Apocryphal Acts) and analytical frameworks (kyriarchy, Bakhtinian dialogism) through
which the social world of early Christianity is studied.
