# The East India Company: The World's Most Powerful Corporation (Roy, 2016)

Tirthankar Roy (economic history, LSE) wrote this short volume for Penguin's
**"The Story of Indian Business"** series (introduction by Gurcharan Das). It
retells the familiar story of the [[english-east-india-company|English East India
Company]] from **1600 to the early nineteenth century**, but "by using an angle
of vision somewhat unusual in the historical scholarship": the Company as a
**business organization** and as a "window into the distinctive globalization
that occurred in the Indian subcontinent" — merging "the rules of business and
economics" with "the rules of politics."

## Roy's Central Arguments

1. **A divided organization ("split personality").** The Company was a
   state-backed monopoly that was also "an umbrella for private enterprise
   conducted by its own employees." Its design deliberately left overseas branches
   "somewhat autonomous from the head office": salaried servants were permitted
   **private trade**, and the Crown's grant of war-making powers "empowered the
   overseas branches relatively more than it did the head office, for after all,
   it was the branch office that had to fight the wars." Roy locates the origin of
   empire in this very **ambiguity** — agents who were "not servants of London"
   but "not subjects of the Indian kings either."

2. **Scale, not efficiency.** The company form's real advantage over the Indian
   **family-and-community firm** was not productivity (private trade proved the
   monopoly firm inefficient) but **scale** — joint stock plus monopoly charter
   let it "operate on a scale of business that was infinitely larger" than Indian
   firms. The institutional contrast: the English **association/joint-stock** form
   was impersonal, risk-embracing, and able "to change with the times," whereas
   Indian enterprise confined risk and credit "within the community" of caste and
   kinship and was "risk-averse." This is Roy's answer to why such a firm arose in
   Europe, "rather than in… India or China."

3. **Contract as the real innovation.** "The real innovation the Company
   introduced in India [was] the idea and the practice of contract" — large-scale
   advance contracts for textiles (orders of "millions of yards of cloth… involving
   hundreds of villages," cash paid "more than a year in advance," with quality and
   design specified). Because there was **neither effective contract law nor
   European access to community enforcement**, principal–agent relations "were
   fraught with problems" (weavers absconding with advances, brokers taking bribes).

4. **Empire from discord, not partnership.** Roy engages the **"congruence of
   interests"** thesis of Christopher Bayly and the **"Anglo-Bania order"** of
   Lakshmi Subramanian — that empire grew from mutual dependence between Indian
   merchants and the Company amid Mughal collapse — but adds a distinctive twist:
   the partnership "was backed up neither by contract law nor by social bonding,"
   and so the **endemic breach of contract and distrust** "could still fuel the
   Company's desire to control political power by making it more interested in
   securing the political means to enforce contracts." "A theory of discord
   supplies an alternative view of colonization just as plausible as the theory of
   partnership." Empire was neither planned by London nor a simple business
   strategy (cf. Holden Furber's antithesis between "those who stood to profit from
   the extension of empire" and the London merchants who "profited solely through
   trade").

5. **Against the drain theory.** Roy's most controversial claim: the nationalist
   and Marxist **"drain of wealth"** account of Indian poverty is mistaken. The
   Indo-European trade was tiny relative to the Indian economy (textile exports
   "1–2 per cent" of subcontinental cloth production in 1795, the whole activity
   "not… more than a thousandth part of the economy"); the silver inflow was "too
   small in the first place" to move prices; Company corruption (Clive's loot) was
   too brief and small, and came largely "from the treasury of the nawab," not from
   ordinary, already-poor Indians. The long-run **services-account deficit** that
   nationalists called a drain was largely **legitimate payment for imported
   skills** (engineers, professors, doctors, railways, banks) — "a price for
   skill-building" whose returns India "is being reaped today." See
   [[drain-of-wealth-colonial-india]].

6. **The three port cities as cosmopolitan modernity.** [[bombay]], [[madras]],
   and Calcutta — founded by autonomous branches as "mini-kingdoms" — "reduced the
   weight of caste and community and made unorthodox alliances possible,"
   introducing "cosmopolitanism, the hallmark of urban modernity." Their
   English-enforced commercial law drew migrant merchants and bankers as the Mughal
   successor states weakened; "80 per cent of factory employment in 1900 was
   located in or near Bombay, Calcutta and Madras."

Roy draws on the **"patrimonial state"** concept (Julia Adams) for the
quasi-sovereign chartered firm, and engages K.N. Chaudhuri (balance of payments),
Om Prakash and Sushil Chaudhury (Indo-European and domestic trade), and Kranton &
Swamy (contract enforcement). See [[company-state]].

| The Company and Indian History | Roy's analytical conclusion: scale, contract, the drain critique |

## What This Source Adds to the Wiki

Roy supplies the **economic-institutional reading** that complements the wiki's
existing diplomatic and military accounts of the EIC
([[clulow-mostert-dutch-english-east-india-companies-2018|Clulow & Mostert]],
[[spear-history-of-india-v2-1965|Spear]], [[cambridge-history-of-india-v5|CHI V]]).
Its three durable contributions to the network: the **company-state as a divided,
"patrimonial" organization** ([[company-state]]); the **discord-not-partnership**
theory of conquest; and the **revisionist critique of the drain theory**
([[drain-of-wealth-colonial-india]]), which sits against the dependency reading
already in the wiki via [[causes-of-latin-american-underdevelopment]] and Galeano.
It deepens [[early-modern-commerce]] by extending the "problem of distance/agency"
into the specific Indian contract regime.
