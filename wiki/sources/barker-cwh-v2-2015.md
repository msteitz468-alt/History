## Series Note

*The Cambridge World History* (7 volumes / 9 books, 2015) is the most
comprehensive Anglophone world history reference series. Volume II is the
agriculture volume: its organizing theme is the origin and impact of food
production — "arguably the most important event in world history" (Barker &
Goucher, p. 1). It deliberately uses an **expanded, overlapping timeframe**
(12,000 BCE – 500 CE) because the transition to farming happened at radically
different times in different places: incipient in the late Pleistocene in some
regions, still diffusing into the first millennium CE in others (sub-Saharan
Africa, the Pacific). The volume blurs the archaeology/history line by design.

**Editor-in-chief of series**: Merry E. Wiesner-Hanks (University of Wisconsin–Milwaukee)

**Volume editors**: Graeme Barker (University of Cambridge) and Candice Goucher (Washington State University)

**Publication**: Cambridge University Press, 2015. ISBN 978-0-521-19218-7. 669 pages (PDF).

## Structure of the Volume

The 23 chapters fall into **two groups** (Barker & Goucher, Introduction):

- **Group 1 — Thematic/methodological (Chs. 2–7):** global-scale essays
  illustrating the range of methods used to study agricultural origins —
  archaeogenetics, historical linguistics, bioarchaeology of health and diet,
  the archaeology of communities, pastoralism, and the agriculture–urbanism
  nexus.
- **Group 2 — Regional narratives + paired case studies (Chs. 8–23):** eight
  region-by-region overviews (Chs. 8, 10, 12, 14, 16, 18, 20, 22), each
  followed by a focused case study of a single site or site cluster (Chs. 9, 11,
  13, 15, 17, 19, 21, 23) so that the non-specialist reader can see how
  archaeologists reason from a real "archaeological record."

There is no separate conclusion chapter; the Introduction's final section
("A world with agriculture," pp. 23–25) serves as the volume's synthesis.

## Volume Synthesis Note

*Written 2026-06-10 after full ingestion of all 23 chapters.*

**What this volume is.** CWH Vol. II is the wiki's **keystone source for the
[[neolithic]] and for global [[agricultural-origins]]**. It does for food
production what Vol. I did for the Paleolithic: a state-of-the-2015, deliberately
*global* and *multi-disciplinary* synthesis. Its evidentiary base is
overwhelmingly **archaeological and archaeo-scientific** (archaeobotany,
zooarchaeology, [[archaeogenetics]], stable isotopes, organic residues,
[[historical-linguistics-and-agriculture|historical linguistics]]) — which is why
the volume's first move (Chs. 2–7) is methodological. It created the Neolithic
period page (previously absent) plus the core process/concept architecture for
the whole post-Paleolithic wiki.

**The single argument that unifies 23 chapters: there was no one "Neolithic
Revolution."** Every regional chapter independently confirms the editors' thesis —
food production arose in **many independent hearths**, by **many varied, partial,
and reversible pathways**, was frequently **resisted, costly, and slow**, and was
driven by region-specific mixtures of climate "push" and social/ideological
"pull" ([[why-did-agriculture-begin]]). The old linear ladder (Westropp → Childe,
[[neolithic-revolution]]) is dismantled chapter by chapter:
- **Pre-domestication cultivation** deep in the late Pleistocene (SW Asia, China).
- **The "package" decouples everywhere** — pottery before farming (East Asia,
  18,000 BP), villages without agriculture, agriculture without villages,
  vegeculture instead of cereals (New Guinea, ISEA).
- **Animals before crops** in Africa, reversing the usual sequence.
- **Health declined** with farming ([[agriculture-and-human-health]]) and foraging
  was often "affluent" — so the transition needs explaining, not assuming.

**Two cross-cutting structural debates recur at opposite ends of the world**, now
captured as paired controversy pages:
1. **Demic vs. cultural diffusion** — did farming spread by migrating farmers or
   adopting foragers? [[demic-vs-cultural-diffusion]] (Europe, ancient-DNA-led)
   and its Pacific twin [[austronesian-dispersal]] and African cousin
   [[bantu-expansion]] are the *same debate* in three theatres; the emerging
   answer everywhere is **leapfrog colonization + heavy assimilation**, not simple
   replacement.
2. **Climate vs. human agency** as the driver — the volume repeatedly insists
   climate was "not a simple forcing agent" (Nanchoc, Peru; the European LBK;
   the Tichitt reinterpretation), foregrounding decision-making, feasting,
   ritual, and risk management.

**Methodological throughline.** The thematic chapters establish that **archaeology
+ archaeo-science are primary evidence** for the deep past — and that *every
dating is provisional* because archaeogenetics and residue analysis move fast.
This is the standing justification for the wiki's chronological-uncertainty
protocol on all Neolithic pages.

**Collection-bias mandate honored (per CLAUDE.md).** This volume is the *best the
collection offers* for several otherwise weak/absent regions, and the relevant
pages say so explicitly: it pushed **sub-Saharan Africa** (Lane, Tichitt, Bantu),
**Southeast Asia/Pacific** (Barton, Kuk — a region the collection is otherwise
*absent* on), the **Americas** (Pearsall, Nanchoc), **South Asia** (Mehrgarh,
three pathways), and **Japan** (Jomon/Yayoi) from "absent/weak" toward "moderate"
*for the Neolithic specifically*. Later phases (CAH, NCMH, specialist prehistory)
will deepen SW Asia/Europe but will **not** match Vol. II's breadth for the
under-resourced regions — so these pages are flagged as the high-water mark.

**Cross-volume tensions and forward links.** (a) Vol. II's
[[agriculture-and-urbanism]] chapter (heterarchy, Jenne-jeno) explicitly hands off
to **CWH Vol. III** (*Early Cities*), the next Processing-List file — the
heterarchy/hierarchy debate should be expanded then. (b) The
[[secondary-products-revolution]] and [[chalcolithic]]/[[early-bronze-age]] period
pages (still to be created) are the natural bridge to the Bronze Age volumes.
(c) Standing dangling links left intentionally for future sources:
[[chalcolithic]], [[early-bronze-age]], [[younger-dryas]] (referenced but not yet
a page), [[geographic-determinism]], [[pre-columbian-population-debate]]. (d) The
volume's overlapping-chronology design means Vol. III and Vol. IV will *re-cover*
some of this ground from the urban/state angle — flag updates rather than
creations there.
