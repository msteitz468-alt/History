# The Cambridge History of the Native Peoples of the Americas, Volume III: South America, Part 1

One of three volumes (North America, Mesoamerica, South America) in the Cambridge
History of the Native Peoples of the Americas. Volume III, edited by Frank Salomon
(ethnohistorian of the Andes) and Stuart B. Schwartz (historian of colonial Brazil),
treats the indigenous peoples of South America from first peopling to the present
across two physical parts. **Part 1** (this source page) covers source-criticism and
ethnographic foundations, the deep archaeological sequence (Paleoindian through the
Inka), and the first century of European invasion (to c. 1580).

## Volume Synthesis Note

The volume's controlling argument is that the history of native South America must be written
**from the indigenous side of the encounter** and on **indigenous time-depth**, not as a
prologue to European conquest. Salomon's opening source-criticism chapter and MacCormack's
survey of early ethnography establish the methodological problem — almost everything we know
is mediated through colonial documents and European categories — and the rest of Part 1 is in
effect a demonstration that archaeology and critical ethnohistory can nevertheless recover deep,
autonomous histories. The recurrent analytical motif is the **coast–highland–forest dynamic**:
South American complexity is read as the interplay of radically different ecological zones,
exploited through [[vertical-archipelago|verticality]] and the [[ayllu]] in the Andes, and
debated (Meggers vs. Roosevelt) for [[amazonia|Amazonia]].

For the wiki, this source **closes the largest remaining gap in the Americas coverage**: it
supplies the connective archaeological spine of the Andes ([[chavin|Chavín]] →
[[andean-middle-horizon|Wari/Tiwanaku]] → [[chimor|Chimor]]/[[aymara-kingdoms|Aymara]] →
[[inca-empire|Tawantinsuyu]]) that earlier ingests (D'Altroy on the Inka, Mann's *1491*)
referenced but did not document, and it adds the previously absent lowland, Caribbean, and
Southern Cone strands. It also deepens the **historiographical** layer with the myth/history
debate, the maritime-foundations and Amazonian-complexity controversies, and the
[[senorios-naturales|chiefdom]] debate.

It is consistent with, and enriches, already-ingested material. Its conquest chapters
([[andean-colonial-transformation-1532-1580]], [[caribbean-demographic-collapse-1492-1580]],
[[conquest-of-coastal-brazil-16th-century]]) dovetail with [[burkholder-johnson-colonial-latin-america|Burkholder & Johnson]] and the
[[spiritual-conquest-and-colonial-church|spiritual-conquest]] and [[encomienda]] pages, viewed
now from the native rather than the colonial vantage. The chief cross-volume tension is the
**Amazonian complexity** question, where Roosevelt's maximalist reading sits against the more
cautious environmental-limitation tradition; this is preserved as an open controversy rather
than resolved. Part 2 (later colonial period through the twentieth century) remains to be
ingested.
