# Ancient Southeast Asia (Miksic & Goh, 2017)

A single-volume **archaeological and historical synthesis** of Southeast Asia ("Seasia" in the
authors' shorthand) from deep prehistory through the protohistoric and Classic eras to c. 1600 CE.
Miksic and Goh argue that the region is a legitimate unit of analysis — not merely "not India, not
China" — defined by maritime connectivity, high biodiversity, and local appropriation (not passive
"influence") of South and East Asian symbolic systems. The book is the wiki's first dedicated
**whole-region archaeological** source for ancient Southeast Asia.

## Argument in Brief

- **"Seasia"** is a real historical-cultural region: Chinese *nanyang*, Indian Suvarnadvipa/
  Suvarnabhumi, Ptolemaic Aurea Khersonesos — but also an internal coherence built on sea routes,
  monsoon logistics, and shared dimensions of Classic culture (urbanization, maritime trade,
  religion, technology/art, monumental construction, relatively high status of women).
- Periodization is **archaeological-art-historical**, not dynastic: **Preclassic** (c. 1000 BCE–1 CE)
  → **Protoclassic** (1–600 CE) → **Early Classic** (600–900) → **Middle Classic** (900–1200) →
  **Late Classic** (1200–1400) → **Postclassic** (1400–1600). Classic = permanent materials,
  inscriptions, Chinese diplomatic recognition, shared art styles.
- **"The sea unites, the land divides"**: subregions are defined by river systems, massifs, the
  isthmian corridor, and island hinterlands; exchange networks and specialization explain early
  complexity better than simple diffusion or isolation.
- South Asian contact is **appropriation and localization** (Wolters), not one-way Indianization:
  Seasians in India, foreign enclaves at ports (Khao Sam Kaeo), and selective uptake of writing,
  gods, and kingship models.
- Maritime archaeology (Belitung, Intan, Cirebon, Java Sea wrecks, etc.) and ceramic sequences are
  first-class evidence alongside epigraphy and Chinese texts.

## Volume Synthesis Note

Miksic and Goh supply the **archaeological spine** that the wiki's earlier Southeast Asia
ingests lacked. Tarling (Taylor/Hall) gave discrete **political** pages for the early kingdoms;
Reid gave the **Age of Commerce** social history; Coe gave **Angkor** depth. This volume ties
those polities to **sites, ceramics, shipwrecks, and settlement patterns** across a single
periodization from Preclassic interaction spheres (Dongson drums, Khao Sam Kaeo, Sa Huynh–Kalanay)
through Protoclassic Funan/Oc Eo and Pyu cities, Early Classic Srivijaya and Sailendra/Mataram
monumental Java (Borobudur, Prambanan, Belitung wreck), Middle Classic Bagan–Angkor peaks and the
**1025 Chola raid**, Late Classic Majapahit/Sukhothai/early Islam, and Postclassic Melaka–Ming–
Portuguese reordering.

The organising contribution is twofold: (1) the **Seasian Classic periodization** as an explicit
analytic frame (now a concept page), and (2) the insistence that South Asian materials were
**appropriated and localized**, not received as pupils — which revises the tone of the
[[indianization-of-southeast-asia]] process without discarding trade as a driver. Empirical
mandala descriptions (Srivijaya oath geography, Champa submandalas, *muang* pluralism) thicken
[[mandala]] beyond Wolters' theoretical sketch. Maritime archaeology (Belitung, Intan, Cirebon,
Java Sea wrecks) plugs [[indian-ocean-trade]] and [[southeast-asian-sea-cultures]] into closed
finds rather than texts alone.

**Tensions flagged (not adjudicated):** Wolters' non-recovery of Srivijaya after 1025 vs.
continued Chinese Sanfoqi missions; Oc Eo coinage local vs. foreign readings; Angkor Wat funerary
vs. non-funerary interpretations; Majapahit "fall to Islam 1478" vs. gradual coastal
transformation. **Remaining forward-links** deliberately not created this pass: Lan Na, Lan Xang,
Temasek as standalone places, Demak/Banten, individual wreck pages beyond Belitung, Kedah as its
own polity page, Yijing biography.

**Coverage impact:** Critical Gap 3 (Southeast Asia) moves from "political skeleton + early-modern
commerce" to a continuous **prehistory → Postclassic archaeological narrative** for the region.
The collection remains thin on mainland specialist monographs (Higham, etc.) and post-1600
narrative outside Reid/Tarling modern volumes.
