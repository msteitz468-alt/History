# The Byzantine Economy

Angeliki E. Laiou and Cécile Morrisson, *The Byzantine Economy* (Cambridge Medieval
Textbooks, Cambridge University Press, 2007). A concise chronological survey of the
economy of the Byzantine Empire from the fourth century CE to the fall of Constantinople
in 1453, organised around demography, agriculture, manufacturing, the urban economy,
trade, monetary developments, and the role of the state and ideology. The book
distinguishes the **economic actions of the state**, the **productive role of the city**,
and the role of **non-state actors** (landlords, artisans, money-changers), and closes by
comparing Byzantium to the medieval West, concluding that it was among the most successful
pre-industrial **mixed economies**.

## Volume Synthesis Note

Laiou and Morrisson's central argument is that the Byzantine economy was a **coherent,
flexible, pre-industrial mixed economy** — "probably the most successful example of a mixed
economy in the European Middle Ages" — in which the state was for centuries the dominant but
never the sole economic actor, and market forces always operated. The book's structure is a
chronological arc (late-antique prosperity → seventh-century crisis and "shift to medieval
structures" → state-led recovery, 8th–10th c. → the eleventh–twelfth-century "age of
accelerated growth" as the state recedes → post-1204 "small-state economics" and
subordination to Italian commerce), capped by an explicit East–West comparative chapter. The
wiki captures this as a spine process page plus a dedicated monetary-system page (the *solidus*/
*nomisma* as "dollar of the Middle Ages," the 1092 Komnenian reform, and the late debasement
to a silver standard) and a **concept page** quarantining the interpretive "mixed economy"
thesis as a contested position rather than wiki-voice fact.

What it adds to the wiki: a genuine gap-fill. Byzantium was previously represented by the
[[byzantine-empire]] actor page and political/military coverage, but had **no dedicated
economic-history spine**. This ingest supplies one, and connects it to existing structures —
[[late-roman-economy|the late Roman economy]] (its antecedent), [[latin-romania-economy|Latin
Romania]] (its post-1204 successor), the [[carolingian-economy|Carolingian]] and
[[medieval-europe-asia-trade|Italian-trade]] comparanda. Cross-volume tension worth noting:
Laiou and Morrisson's rehabilitation of the eleventh–twelfth-century economy and their
insistence that the collapse was "political in the first instance" sit alongside the wiki's
existing NCMH-derived narrative of Komnenian decline — complementary rather than
contradictory, but the economic optimism is a distinct interpretive layer.
