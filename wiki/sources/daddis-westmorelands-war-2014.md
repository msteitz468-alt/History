# Westmoreland's War: Reassessing American Strategy in Vietnam (Daddis, 2014)

Gregory A. Daddis's *Westmoreland's War* is a major revisionist study that directly challenges the long-dominant portrayal of General William C. Westmoreland and MACV as pursuing a simplistic "strategy of attrition" centered on search-and-destroy operations and body counts. Daddis argues that Westmoreland and senior U.S. Army leaders developed and sought to implement a **comprehensive campaign strategy** suited to the multifaceted nature of the war in South Vietnam.

The book contends that the standard narrative reduces a complex political-military effort to a single word ("attrition") and thereby misrepresents both what Westmoreland intended and what MACV actually attempted on the ground. Instead, Westmoreland faced (and planned for) three interlocking missions: containing the growing conventional threat from North Vietnamese Army (NVA) units, building the Republic of Vietnam Armed Forces (RVNAF/ARVN), and conducting pacification and population security operations (the "parallel war").

## What it contributes

- Detailed reconstruction of Westmoreland's actual concept of operations and how it evolved from 1964–1968.
- Demonstration that MACV tracked far more than body counts: population security, economic development, political stability, and Hamlet Evaluation System (HES) metrics were central to measuring progress.
- Analysis of the "parallel war" — the civil-military pacification effort (CORDS, Revolutionary Development, etc.) as an integral, not marginal, component of the campaign.
- Reassessment of the ARVN advisory and training mission as a core strategic pillar, not an afterthought.
- Critique of how the "myth of attrition" was constructed after the war and how it has distorted understanding of American strategy.
- Honest acknowledgment (especially in the Conclusion) that even a well-conceived and faithfully implemented strategy may not have been sufficient given the weaknesses of the South Vietnamese state and the limits of external military power in a revolutionary post-colonial context.

## Key Claims (Range 01 — front matter, Preface, Introduction, Ch. 1, early Ch. 2; exclusive cache grounding)

**Thesis and "myth of attrition"**:
- Daddis's core argument: "At its core, it argues that Westmoreland developed a comprehensive military strategy for Vietnam, one not confined to simple attrition of enemy forces." The "attrition" label became "an unquestioned truth" in popular narratives but is a reductive shorthand that "served as a convenient short-hand for journalists, politicians, and even military commanders during the 1960s." (Preface)
- "Perhaps, however, such explanations are overly reductive." Westmoreland "was not the unthinking officer who is presented so contemptuously in many history books." (Preface)
- "Central to this work’s argument is the contention that Westmoreland not only devised a sound military strategy but that the general was not the unthinking officer..." It is "possible... to devise a sound strategy and still lose." (Preface)

**Westmoreland's three missions / comprehensive campaign**:
- "the general 'had not one battle, but three to fight: first, to contain a growing enemy conventional threat; second, to develop the Republic of Vietnam’s Armed Forces (RVNAF); and third, to pacify and protect the peasants in the South Vietnamese countryside. Each was a monumental task.'" (Introduction, quoting Phillip B. Davidson)
- Westmoreland "had to develop a concept of operations for the employment of US forces in South Vietnam, provide advice and assistance to the RVNAF, support civil operations and pacification, and advise both the Commander in Chief, Pacific (CINCPAC) and the Joint Chiefs of Staff on strategic issues." "Political, economic, and psychological victory is equally important, and support of Revolutionary Development is mandatory." (Introduction)
- "Westmoreland embraced this broader definition of strategy instead of focusing strictly on destruction of the enemy’s military forces." Army 1961 definition: strategy as "the art and science of developing and using the political, economic, psychological, and armed forces of a nation, during peace and war, to afford the maximum support to national policies..." (Introduction)

**Cold War doctrinal/intellectual context (containment, decolonization, Maoist theory, limited war)**:
- Containment "guided Cold War strategy for decades." Decolonization "forced dismantling of European rule" created vulnerability to "communist subversion." (Ch. 1)
- US Army officers "delved into the writings of Mao, Lenin, Ché Guevara, and Vo Nguyen Giap." Mao's theories "integrated, encompassing and relating various fields: military, political, economic, sociological and psychological." "Wars could no longer be won by military means alone." (Ch. 1)
- "Prevailing doctrine increasingly viewed insurgency and counterinsurgency as 'political, social, and economic rather than military problems' and 'struggles for men’s minds, rather than territory.'" Flexible response and "spectrum of warfare" codified in 1962 FM 100-5. (Ch. 1)
- Counterinsurgency doctrine: "those military, paramilitary, political, economic, psychological, and civic actions taken by a government to defeat subversive insurgency." Civic action included "construction of schools, hospitals... assistance in agricultural planting..." "Military action, doctrine suggested, was 'only a part of the overall internal defense and development effort.'" (Ch. 1)
- Officers studied Philippines (Magsaysay/Lansdale civic action), French *guerre révolutionnaire*, Malaya; emphasized political reform, "revolutionary development," and that "security is development." (Ch. 1)
- Westmoreland at West Point (1960–63) "mandated counterinsurgency instruction be included in the core curriculum" and created a Counterinsurgency Committee; "54 hours of instruction on counterinsurgency over a cadet’s four-year West Point experience." (Ch. 1; also Introduction)

**Early transition from advice to war**:
- Incremental US involvement from MAAG (1950s) through MACV (1962); "the United States did not rush frantically to war in 1965." Senior officers (Williams, McGarr) understood "holistic" approaches: "Military operations alone are not sufficient for success... an over-all plan at Government level embracing political, psychological, economic, administrative and military action is necessary." (early Ch. 2)
- "Clearing and holding" concept articulated by McGarr. CIP (Counterinsurgency Plan) had aims matching Westmoreland's later three missions. (early Ch. 2)
- "By the time William Westmoreland arrived in Saigon in mid-1964, key US officials had been debating strategic issues for nearly five years and most understood that any policy for Vietnam required more than simply military might." Westmoreland brought "perspectives gained from his tenure at West Point" and "personally contributed to the study of unconventional warfare." (Ch. 1 / early Ch. 2)
- Political crises (Diem overthrow) and RVNAF weaknesses drove shift from advice/support to combat troops, but "Westmoreland warned against American escalation until US leaders were assured that the GVN was 'established on [a] reasonably firm political, military, and psychological base.'" (early Ch. 2)

## Key Claims (Range 04 — Ch. 6 "Training an Uncertain Army" + Conclusion "When Strategy May Not Matter"; exclusive cache grounding from /tmp/daddis-ranges/range_4_9201-12083.txt)

**ARVN/RVNAF development and advisory effort (Ch. 6 core pillar)**:
- The war "was always intended to retain a Vietnamese character." Westmoreland repeatedly stressed "the bulk of the military support of Revolutionary Development must rest on the shoulders of the ARVN." (8 December 1966 speech, Box 44, Speeches, WPUSC)
- RVNAF faced "diverse missions": conventional main-force operations, territorial security, and direct support to pacification/RD. "RVNAF Counterinsurgency Roles and Missions" explicitly tasked ARVN divisions with both combat and "support of Revolutionary Development." (4 February 1965 document)
- US advisory effort evolved from MAAG conventional training (1954 onward, focused on standard divisions for Korea-style invasion) to post-1965 emphasis on "Mobile Advisory Teams" (MATs) embedded with ARVN to improve small-unit tactics, leadership, and RD integration. "MATs" became key vehicle for transferring skills after US combat forces arrived. (Turner, "Mobile Advisory Teams in Vietnam"; Weyand debrief)
- Persistent structural problems: "leadership problems," high desertion rates, political reliability concerns, corruption, inadequate pay/equipment parity with US forces until late, and cultural disconnects. American advisors faced "language barriers," "cultural misunderstandings," and frustration that "the Vietnamese were leaving everything to the Americans." ARVN often seen by population as "puppet army." (multiple notes, e.g., 51–67, Bergerud, Brigham, Khuyen in Sorley)
- Westmoreland and MACV pushed ARVN into greater RD role from 1966: "In late 1966, Westmoreland spoke at a Mobile Training Team Course and emphasized that 'the bulk of the military support of Revolutionary Development must rest on the shoulders of the ARVN.'" 40 percent of ARVN infantry battalions in direct RD support by March 1967. Yet US forces still bore main combat burden. (MACV Monthly Evaluation Reports; Heintges to Westmoreland)
- Tet performance and Vietnamization: ARVN showed improvement in some areas during/after Tet 1968 but "still faced fundamental issues." Post-Tet, pressure for Vietnamization accelerated ARVN expansion and US advisory shift, but "the problem of ARVN leadership did not go away." "Vietnamization" transferred burden but could not instantly fix "leadership, corruption, or promotion pathologies." (JCS History Part III; Clarke, Advice and Support)
- Core tension: "Training an uncertain army" — the long-term pillar was always to make the war Vietnamese, yet GVN political weaknesses, regime instability, and ARVN's internal problems limited what external advisors and training could achieve. "Saigon refusing to have Vietnamese forces under US command" compounded coordination issues. (MacGarrigle; Komer)

**Conclusion: limits of strategy, nation-building challenges, post-colonial realities, uncomfortable truth that comprehensive strategies can still fail**:
- "It is possible... to devise a sound strategy and still lose a war." Talented American generals developed and implemented a comprehensive political-military strategy encompassing containment, RVNAF development, and pacification "and still lose." (foreshadowed in intro; explicit in Conclusion framing)
- "the United States attempted to do too much" unlike Korea (where internal resolution was largely left to ROK). Attempting both to "repel external (NVN) aggression *and* perform nation-building for South Vietnam" created fatal dependence: "they try to do everything... So the Vietnamese don’t rely on themselves." (Post Mortem on Vietnam Strategy; Holbrook via Sorley; Kahin & Lewis)
- Fundamental limits of external military power in post-colonial revolutionary context. "there was no magic solution to the VC/NVA threat." The South Vietnamese state faced deep legitimacy, corruption, leadership, and social issues that US strategy could not fully overcome despite comprehensive design. "GVN and pacification" problems were structural. (MACV Command History 1966; multiple debriefs)
- "War take-over by South Vietnam—When?" remained the unanswered question; Vietnamization became in some analyses "the model or paradigm of a new strategy of retreat." (U.S. News; Schmitz)
- Uncomfortable truth: even "sound" comprehensive strategies can fail when the political base (GVN legitimacy, RVNAF reliability as independent force, popular support) is fundamentally weak and the enemy enjoys sanctuary, reinforcement, and superior political will/time. "Faith in military power" had inherent limits in this environment. (Conclusion synthesis; Giap/PAVN perspectives; "no magic solution")
- "MACV conceded in 1966 'there was no magic solution to the VC/NVA threat.'" External actors cannot substitute for indigenous political will and institutional strength. The "uncomfortable truth" is that strategy, however comprehensive, operates within constraints of the host nation's character and the broader international/post-colonial dynamics.

**Key claims (verbatim-grounded; ~15+ structured):**

1. **1964–65 deterioration and Tonkin-driven escalation (grand strategy reactive):** "In early August, US destroyers had engaged North Vietnamese torpedo boats in the Gulf of Tonkin. The incidents, still controversial to this day, enabled Johnson to secure from Congress a resolution taking 'all necessary measures to repel any armed attacks against the forces of the United States and to prevent further aggression.' ... the Tonkin incident compelled Johnson to accept greater responsibility for the south’s survival. ... If Johnson was not to 'lose' South Vietnam like Truman 'lost' China, escalation seemed the only option." (Ch. 2, early cache). McNamara post-visit: "the South Vietnamese must win their own fight" yet recommended increased pressure; "had to choose between two alternatives: either expand our military support of Vietnam or withdraw."

2. **Westmoreland's political focus and initial resistance to US ground troops:** Westmoreland "at least was emphasizing to his superiors and his staff the political nature of the war." "In an October cable to the JCS chairman, Westmoreland expressed his concern that unless a 'fairly effective government' existed in Saigon, 'no amount of offensive action by the United States either in or outside of South Vietnam has any chance by itself of reversing the deterioration underway.'" (Ch. 2). Ambassador Taylor and MACV resisted introduction of American ground forces due to political atmosphere.

3. **Pleiku, Flaming Dart, Rolling Thunder, Marines at Da Nang, rapid expansion:** "When NLF forces raided a US base at Pleiku on 7 February, killing eight Americans and wounding another 60, administration officials believed they had no choice but to retaliate." Johnson ordered Flaming Dart air strikes. "In early March, Johnson, still hoping that Rolling Thunder air strikes might hold the line in Vietnam, authorized the landing of two US Marine Corps battalions at Da Nang to secure the local air base and installations. Westmoreland intended to use these initial American units for base security around the Da Nang 'enclave.' It did not take long, however, for the mission, and the troops to support that mission, to rapidly expand." (Ch. 2).

4. **Krulak on Marines' "balanced strategy" and similarity to Westmoreland:** Lt. Gen. Victor Krulak (later critic): Marines' concept "from the start, involved fighting the Vietnam battle as a multipronged effort. They aimed to bring peace and security to the people in the highly populated coastal regions by conducting aggressive operations against the guerrillas and expanding the pacified areas as rapidly as they were totally secured." Krulak also noted training/support of local forces + going after larger units. "Despite later Marine criticisms of Westmoreland’s concept of operations, this 'balanced strategy,' as Krulak dubbed it, would look remarkably similar to the MACV commander’s own 'multipronged' approach to the war in South Vietnam." (Ch. 2).

5. **Assumptions behind 1965 troop increases (incl. "fallacious" population security link):** "The assumptions behind the rapid increase in American troops were many: the enemy was abandoning guerrilla warfare in favor of larger battles along the lines of Maoist theory; US force could influence Hanoi’s will; and, perhaps most significant (and fallacious), population security would lead to political stability inside South Vietnam." Westmoreland requested 44 combat maneuver battalions by early June 1965 "because 'force ratios' were changing in favor of the enemy." Convinced US troops could "successfully take the fight to the VC" and "give us a substantial and hard-hitting offensive capability on the ground to convince the VC that they cannot win." (Ch. 2).

6. **US strategic contradiction on military power vs. politics:** "Westmoreland, along with most of Johnson’s top advisors, presupposed that American military power could effect political change in a foreign land. Yet these senior leaders also believed military power alone could not sustain the Saigon government. ... On one hand, most Americans never questioned their faith in military force being able to achieve larger political goals. On the other hand, they well accepted that force alone could not achieve their objectives. Such inconsistencies soon would be played out on the political-military battlefields south of the seventeenth parallel." (Ch. 2).

7. **"Long pull" and no quick results:** Westmoreland to Army Chief of Staff Harold K. Johnson, 24 June 1965: "we are in for the long pull." Two days later: "We are deluding ourselves if we feel some novel arrangement is going to get quick results. We must think in terms of an extended conflict; be prepared to support a greatly increased effort; give the commander on the scene the troops that he requires and the authority to deploy these troops in accordance with his best judgment." (Ch. 2). "As Westmoreland wrote the Joint Chiefs in June 1965, 'It is difficult, if not impossible for me to imagine how we can commit and sustain US forces, air, surface and naval, without backing them up for the long pull by mobilization of manpower, industrial and training resources at least to a limited degree.'"

8. **MACV mission statement holistic (not pure attrition):** "In 1965, as the general drafted his concept of operations, the mission of US forces in South Vietnam 'was to assist and support the RVNAF [South Vietnamese Armed Forces] in their efforts to defeat communist subversive insurgency, and to accelerate effective GVN control over the country.' MACV thus confronted a military and political problem... Because of this mission, Westmoreland did not subscribe to an attrition strategy aimed solely at killing the enemy. ... Westmoreland and his staff unmistakably incorporated nonmilitary means into their strategic planning." (Ch. 3 opening). "the insurgency in South Vietnam must eventually be defeated among the people in the hamlets and towns." (June 1965 cable).

9. **Population as ultimate center of gravity / end, not enemy body count:** "defeating the enemy’s ground forces served only as a means to the larger end of securing the south’s population. He held no illusions that US operations to dislodge communists from local areas would have a 'lasting effect' unless the ARVN could retain control of these areas." "Westmoreland likened the political subversives to 'termites' which unrelentingly were eating away at the foundation of the Saigon government. Concurrently, main force units, or 'bully boys,' waited for the opportune moment to strike at the weakened structure with crowbars. As Westmoreland recalled, if the Americans and their South Vietnamese allies were to be successful, 'Neither facet could be ignored.'" "the main purpose of defeating the enemy through offensive operations against his main forces and bases must be to provide the opportunity through RD [revolutionary development] to get at the heart of the matter, which is the population of South Vietnam." (Ch. 3, 1966 statement).

10. **Westmoreland's "three battles" (explicit):** "As his chief intelligence officer recalled, 'Westmoreland had not one battle but three to fight: first, to contain a growing conventional threat; second to develop the Republic of Vietnam’s Armed Forces (RVNAF); and third, to pacify and protect the peasants in the South Vietnamese countryside.' Both the threat and the mission required a broad concept of the operations, one that a simple word like attrition could not fully characterize." (Ch. 3).

11. **Three-phase sustained campaign (detailed; "one war" before Abrams):** Phase I (1965): commit US/allied forces "necessary to halt the losing trend by 1965" — secure bases, defend centers, preserve/strengthen RVNAF. Phase II (1966): "resume the offensive to 'destroy enemy forces' and reinstitute 'rural construction activities'" — "participate in clearing, securing, reserve reaction and offensive operations as required to support and sustain the resumption of pacification." Phase III: "defeat and destruction of the remaining enemy forces and base areas." "Westmoreland’s official report on the war included the term 'sustained campaign.'" "one war" integration: "Probably the fundamental issue is the question of the coordination of mission activities in Saigon... It is abundantly clear that all political, military, economic, and security (police) programs must be completely integrated in order to attain any kind of success..." (Ch. 3).

12. **"Myth of Attrition": shorthand error; three pillars (attrition + pacification + ARVN training); Reston "Undefinable War":** "If continuity best described American military strategy in Vietnam, the same could be said of US grand strategy." Critics "conflate grand and military strategy". "As seen in the previous chapter... Reston’s advice suggests that catchphrases like 'attrition strategy,' 'counterinsurgency strategy,' and 'pacification strategy'—while mainstays of the war’s literature—fail to convey the true nature of Westmoreland’s approach. In fact, as MACV’s commander formulated his strategic concept in 1965, he appreciated fully the complexity of the threat... 'the war in Vietnam is a political as well as a military war. It is political because the ultimate goal is to regain the loyalty and cooperation of the people...'" (Amb. Lodge on "broad strategy": destroy enemy + "restore progressively the entire country to GVN control" + "support 'rural construction' with comprehensive attention to the pacification process"). "Westmoreland’s strategy rested on the three pillars of attrition, pacification, and ARVN training. Broad in nature, it was difficult to define. Journalists, and future scholars, turned increasingly to the word 'attrition' as convenient shorthand despite Reston’s warnings about imprecise definitions. One part of the three-pillared strategy was being mistaken for the whole." "Westmoreland used the phrase attrition... yet he early on recognized that killing the enemy was not an end unto itself. More important, attrition served as a means to express concern over maintaining morale during the 'long pull.'"

13. **Honolulu Conference goals (Feb 1966): explicit non-kinetic + attrition rate targets:** "MACV would increase the South Vietnamese population living in secure areas by 10 percent, multiply critical roads and railroads for use by 20 percent, and increase the destruction of VC and PAVN base areas by 30 percent. To make sure the president’s directives were not ignored, Westmoreland was to augment the pacified population by 235,000 and ensure the defense of political and population centers under government control. The final goal directed MACV to 'attrite, by year’s end, VC/PAVN forces at a rate as high as their capability to put men in the field.'" "The Honolulu conference exemplified the immense array of tasks Westmoreland had to accomplish." (Ch. 3).

14. **Body counts one of many indicators only:** "while body counts captured the attention of the Johnson administration and the American media, they constituted only one of many statistical indicators MACV employed for tracking the progress of an extremely complex war." US Army officers believed killing enemy important, but "neither Westmoreland nor the American forces he led were blind to the realities of unconventional warfare. The claim that 'several years were to pass before the American command began to accept the crucial role of population security' simply does not hold up when one scrutinizes the documentary evidence." (Ch. 3).

15. **PROVN report (1966) and substantial incorporation (not muted):** PROVN ("A Program for the Pacification and Long Term Development of South Vietnam") placed "strong emphasis on nonmilitary means" and "victory" only by "bringing the individual Vietnamese, typically a rural peasant, to support willingly the Government of South Vietnam (GVN)." Criticisms of "preoccupation 'with purely military activities'". "In truth, the report provided few truly new ideas." Westmoreland to Volney Warner (one author): "we 'were already doing pretty much all of what was recommended by PROVN.'" "A report in early 1967 stated 'that of the 140 recommendations in the PROVN report, 53 had been implemented and 45 had been partially implemented.'" Some proposals (e.g. US "social innovators", cross-border ops) unfeasible due to Johnson's limits on war. (Ch. 3).

16. **HES (Hamlet Evaluation System) instituted 1967; security + development metrics:** "In mid-1966, McNamara requested an improved method for measuring the progress of nonmilitary programs and the following year MACV instituted the Hamlet Evaluation System. The HES graded hamlets in both security and development areas, assessing NLF military activities, local defense force capabilities, and progress in health, education, and welfare. Over 220 US district advisors reported their findings on a monthly basis. With roughly 13,000 hamlets, the statistical data generated by HES soon overwhelmed MACV’s staff; the system alone generated a monthly average of 90,000 pages." Limitations: "data turbulence"; "HES did not evaluate populations outside of rural hamlets—some 3.5 million people"; "CORDS advisors found it impossible to visit all of their hamlets in a given month, and even when they did visit, much of what they saw was filtered through an American lens unfamiliar with the intricacies of Vietnamese society." (Ch. 3).

17. **Enclave strategy (Gavin) rejected (initiative, time, dual threat):** Gavin argued MACV spreading too thin; enclaves like "blowing out a balloon"; "If a 'village is fought over five or six times,' ... 'the whole pattern of life in the village will be altered.'" Westmoreland/MACV: enclaves would become "magnets" for NLF attacks; "concede the initiative, 'the most fundamental principle to be applied to counterinsurgency warfare,' to the enemy"; "an inglorious, static use of U.S. forces in overpopulated areas with little chance of direct or immediate impact on the outcome of events." JCS Wheeler: "No one ever won a battle sitting on his ass." Joseph Alsop: enclave meant "surrendering all the rest of South Vietnam to the Viet Cong and the North Vietnamese." (Ch. 3). Westmoreland: enemy already held upper hand; time factor critical.

18. **Order of battle / infiltration measurement difficulties (no deliberate cover-up per Daddis):** Infiltration ~4,500/mo (1966 est.), projected >6,000/mo (1967). "How big is the enemy?" diverged MACV/CIA. Debate on counting "self-defense" / "secret self-defense" (women/old, rarely armed, limited military capability per Abrams) and political infrastructure. Abrams (1967): press reaction to "inflated figures" of greater concern than capabilities; "When we release the figure of 420–431,000, the newsmen will immediately seize on the point that the enemy has increased about 120–130,000." Compromise ~235,000 enemy combatants. "Claims of a conspiratorial coverup by Westmoreland, however, ring hollow. President Johnson, Secretary McNamara, and the Joint Chiefs all were well aware of the CIA-MACV debate." (Ch. 3).

19. **Chieu Hoi (Open Arms) as GVN responsibility (mixed results):** Saigon program from 1963 "to 'subvert the morale of enemy cadre and troops and call upon them to leave their ranks and rally to the national cause.'" ~30,000 ralliers by early 1966. MACV authorized hiring Hoi Chanhs as scouts. "GVN officials, however, offered only tepid support"; "the only good Viet Cong is a dead Viet Cong"; "Open Arms initiative 'was promising, it bogged down because of GVN indifference.'" NLF threats to families. (Ch. 3 end).

20. **Broader context: Hanoi also struggled with multifaceted strategy; US constraints from Cold War limits:** Hanoi Resolution 12 (Dec 1965) emphasized military; Le Duan vs. Giap debates. "Even as escalation posed special problems for Le Duan and Hanoi’s leadership, the US response enhanced the National Liberation Front’s legitimacy." "Westmoreland and his staff, though, realized that 'counterinsurgency was too narrow a focus to cope with the arrival of the North Vietnamese army in the south.'" Johnson limits (no wider war/China intervention per Korea precedent) shaped options; Great Society social engineering exported to VN for security. (Ch. 3 end).

## Historiography Note

Daddis writes as both a serving U.S. Army officer/historian and a deliberate revisionist. The work is grounded in extensive use of MACV records, command histories, and contemporary metrics that previous critics often dismissed or overlooked. It is one of the strongest archival rebuttals to the "Westmoreland as attrition general" caricature. Critics from the Krepinevich/Nagl school argue that Daddis underplays the institutional and cultural dominance of the big-unit, firepower-heavy approach on the ground and the persistent marginalization of pacification until after Tet. Daddis himself concedes in the Conclusion that even a comprehensive strategy faced insurmountable obstacles created by the nature of the South Vietnamese state and the broader Cold War/post-colonial environment.

## Volume Synthesis Note

Daddis restores complexity to the picture of American strategy under Westmoreland. The war was never reducible to "attrition" alone. MACV planned and tracked a three-pronged campaign that explicitly included political, economic, and advisory dimensions alongside conventional operations against main-force units. The fact that this strategy still failed does not prove it was never attempted or was merely a cover for body-count warfare. Daddis forces a more precise debate: not whether Westmoreland had a strategy, but whether any U.S. military strategy could have succeeded given the political and social realities inside South Vietnam and the sanctuary and reinforcement advantages enjoyed by Hanoi. This work is indispensable for any serious analysis of the strategic level of the American war in Vietnam and provides essential balance to the dominant critical narratives already in the wiki.
