# Catherine the Great: Portrait of a Woman (Robert K. Massie, 2011)

Robert K. Massie's major biography of Catherine II (1729–1796, r. 1762–1796). Born Sophia Augusta Fredericka of Anhalt-Zerbst, a minor German princess, she traveled to Russia at fourteen, converted, married the future Peter III, endured eighteen years at the court of Empress Elizabeth, seized power in the 1762 coup, and ruled for thirty-four years as one of the most consequential monarchs in Russian history. Massie presents her as both a woman and an empress: intellectually ambitious, politically pragmatic, personally passionate, and acutely aware of the limits of autocratic power in a vast, serf-based empire.

The book covers her formation in a loveless provincial German family, the political marriage arranged with Prussian backing, the difficult Grand Duchess years, the coup with the Orlovs, her long reign of expansion (Black Sea, partitions of Poland), Enlightenment posturing (Nakaz, Legislative Commission, correspondence with Voltaire and Diderot), the Pugachev rebellion, the Potemkin partnership, cultural patronage, and twilight years focused on succession and the French Revolution's shadow. A substantial "Coda" reflects on her self-image and historical stature.

Massie complements the more structural and social-historical treatment in prior ingests (Cambridge History of Russia Vol. II; Freeze, Russia: A History). It supplies rich narrative detail, verbatim primary texture, and character analysis that justify a high-resolution detailed biography page.

## Volume Synthesis Note

(Populated after full reconciliation.)

Massie supplies the richest single-volume narrative treatment of Catherine's personal formation and the emotional/political mechanics of power at the Russian court. It complements the structural analyses from Cambridge Russia Vol. II (empire, nationalities, serfdom, economy) and Freeze by restoring the human scale: the lonely German girl who read her way into rulership, the calculated risk of the 1762 coup, the long partnership with Potemkin as both lover and viceroy, and the persistent gap between Enlightenment rhetoric and the realities of serfdom and autocracy. The book justifies and supplies primary texture for the detailed biography page in hubs/biographies/age-of-expansion/.

Collection note: Russia post-1500 remains a known coverage gap per CLAUDE.md; this source + prior reference volumes (Cambridge V2, Freeze, Riasanovsky) materially improve the Age of Expansion treatment for the Romanov empire's westernization, expansion, and governance paradoxes.
