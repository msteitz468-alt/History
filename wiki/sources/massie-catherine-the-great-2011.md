# Catherine the Great: Portrait of a Woman (Robert K. Massie, 2011)

Robert K. Massie's major biography of Catherine II (1729–1796, r. 1762–1796). Born Sophia Augusta Fredericka of Anhalt-Zerbst, a minor German princess, she traveled to Russia at fourteen, converted, married the future Peter III, endured eighteen years at the court of Empress Elizabeth, seized power in the 1762 coup, and ruled for thirty-four years as one of the most consequential monarchs in Russian history. Massie presents her as both a woman and an empress: intellectually ambitious, politically pragmatic, personally passionate, and acutely aware of the limits of autocratic power in a vast, serf-based empire.

The book covers her formation in a loveless provincial German family, the political marriage arranged with Prussian backing, the difficult Grand Duchess years, the coup with the Orlovs, her long reign of expansion (Black Sea, partitions of Poland), Enlightenment posturing (Nakaz, Legislative Commission, correspondence with Voltaire and Diderot), the Pugachev rebellion, the Potemkin partnership, cultural patronage, and twilight years focused on succession and the French Revolution's shadow. A substantial "Coda" reflects on her self-image and historical stature.

Massie complements the more structural and social-historical treatment in prior ingests (Cambridge History of Russia Vol. II; Freeze, Russia: A History). It supplies rich narrative detail, verbatim primary texture, and character analysis that justify a high-resolution detailed biography page.

- **Claim:** Johanna Elizabeth’s rejection of her firstborn daughter (disappointment at a girl, no maternal affection, handover to wet nurses) in favor of the later-born handicapped son Wilhelm produced a permanent emotional wound in Sophia that drove lifelong craving for approval, warmth, and self-mastery.
  Verbatim grounding: “It was told me that I was not very joyfully welcomed.… My father thought I was an angel; my mother did not pay much attention to me. A year and a half later, she [Johanna] gave birth to a son whom she idolized. I was merely tolerated and often I was scolded with a violence and anger I did not deserve. I felt this without being perfectly clear why in my mind.” (Catherine, Memoirs, quoted in ch. 1 Sophia’s Childhood); Massie adds: “The harm done to this small daughter by Johanna’s open display of preference marked Sophia’s character profoundly. ...   Context: Stettin birth 2:30 a.m. 21 April 1729; Johanna 16, wanted son, difficult delivery, then favoritism after Wilhelm (rickets); Memoirs written ~40 years later with resentments still simmering; Wilhelm’s clinical death account contrasts emotional suppression.

- **Claim:** Babet Cardel’s gentle, cultured Huguenot tutoring (French classics, logic, wit) contrasted with Pastor Wagner’s rigid, threatening Lutheran instruction and provided the model of dignity in subordinate station; this shaped Catherine’s preference for reason over coercion and her later self-presentation as enlightened.
  Verbatim grounding: “She had a noble soul, a cultured mind, a heart of gold; she was patient, gentle, cheerful, just, consistent—in short the kind of governess one would wish every child to have.” (Catherine to Voltaire/Grimm, quoted ch. 1); on Wagner: “I am convinced in my inmost soul that Herr Wagner was a blockhead.” “All my life I have had this inclination to yield only to gentleness and reason—and to resist all pressure.” (Catherine, Memoirs, ch. 1); lessons began with La Fontaine then Corneille, Racine, Molière; Wagner terrified with Last Judgment, threatened cane, refused rational answers.
  Context: Governess entrusted with education in Stettin/Zerbst; Babet recognized belligerence as loneliness; Wagner chosen by devout father; Johanna tried to “drive the devil of pride out of her” by calling ugly/impertinent and enforcing deference rituals.

- **Claim:** Childhood lessons in concealment of pride (forbidden speech, forced deference to visiting women of rank) became a deliberate survival tactic that Sophia later deployed at the Russian court during crises.
  Verbatim grounding: “Sophia obeyed. Threatened, she drew around herself a cloak of meekness, deference, and temporary submission.” (Massie narrative synthesizing ch. 1); “Here, too, an example was set by Babet Cardel: a woman of gentle birth who accepted her inferior position as a governess but still managed to preserve a self-respect, dignity, and pride that raised her, in Sophia’s eyes, higher than her own mother.”
  Context: Provincial German noble etiquette training; travels with Johanna; Babet as counter-model to mother.

- **Bestuzhev's surveillance regime and Choglokova as "jailor and spy"**: Bestuzhev imposed Maria Semenovna Choglokova (24, Elizabeth's cousin, "virtue and fertility" example) as senior governess to isolate couple politically and ensure fidelity/heir. Decree: Catherine's role "to inspire a sincere love... and bring forth the heir." Choglokova "simple-minded … uneducated … cruel … malicious … capricious … self-serving" (Catherine's Memoirs). Forbidden letters/conversations with outsiders; all remarks vetted ("such talk would displease the empress"). Catherine: "Made a royal prisoner." (Cache lines ~1-100; pp.77-79; context: post-marriage isolation 1745-46 onward; also Choglokov promoted to Peter's governor 1747, servants dismissed, "grand duke and I were now forced to remain inseparable.")

- **Peter's character, marital failure, and psychological/physical issues**: Peter played with toy soldiers (Holstein uniforms) in marital bed at night with Madame Krause (50s) joining until 2am; violin scraping, dog training/whipping from 7am-late; "He would not have sacrificed these amusements even if he had known they were killing me." Beat English King Charles Spaniel by collar/tail with whip handle; Catherine interceded, "this only made him redouble his blows. I returned to my room in tears. After the dog, I was the most miserable creature in the world." Refused Russian bath (Lent), threatened fortress. Obsessed with Holstein ("little corner of the earth... marvelous paradise"); "Never did two minds resemble each other less. We had nothing in common in our tastes or ways of thinking." (Catherine Memoirs). Possible phimosis (Castéra suggestion) or psychological inhibition (orphanhood, smallpox scars, no peers); wedding night unconsummated (drunk, "How it would amuse my servants"); nine years no sleep elsewhere. Catherine: "It seems to me that I was good for something else." (Cache ~100-350; pp.82-84,99-100; context: 1746-48 Oranienbaum/Peterhof; Moscow 1748-49.)

- **Catherine's grief, near-suicide, and resilience under tyranny**: Father's (Christian Augustus) death March 1747 (stroke, age 56; Catherine 17); forbidden personal letters (Foreign Office drafts only); Elizabeth via Choglokova: "a grand duchess of Russia was not permitted to mourn for more than a week 'because, after all, your father was not a king.'" Catherine: "it was true that he was not a reigning sovereign, but he was my father." After private Elizabeth tirade (accused betraying to Prussia, "I know it was my fault that the marriage had not been consummated", "I know quite well that you alone are to blame if you have no children"; "young women who did not love their husbands always wept"), Catherine: "I took a large knife and lay down on a sofa, intending to plunge it into my heart. Just then, one of my maids came in, threw herself on the knife... the knife was not very sharp and would not have penetrated my corset." House collapse May 1748 (Razumovsky estate): foundation slid on thaw; Catherine rescued, bruised/shocked; servants/workers killed; Elizabeth displeased by Catherine's terror ("wished to look upon the whole occurrence as a mere trifle"). (Cache ~140-400; pp.80-85; context: 1747-48.)

- **Intellectual awakening and "republican soul" from reading**: "In those days I read romances only." Favorite: *Tiran the Fair* (transparent skin wine-drinking princess); Peter: "highwaymen eventually hanged." Then: Madame de Sévigné letters; Father Barre *General History of Germany* (vol/week); Bayle's *Dictionnaire Historique et Critique* (freethinker precursor to Montesquieu/Voltaire, read cover to cover). Later isolation winter 1754-55 post-Paul: Tacitus *Annals* ("caused a singular revolution in my brain, to which, perhaps, the melancholy cast of my thoughts at this time contributed. **Serfdom realities and early policy (post-accession 1762):** “By the mid-eighteenth century, most Russian serfs had become possessions, chattel; in fact, slaves... **Primary sources details (Catherine Memoirs editions and biases, correspondence, Dashkova, ambassador reports, legislation):**
- “Catherine’s life divides into two halves almost equal in length. From 1729 to 1762, she was a German princess and a Russian grand duchess; from 1762 until her death in 1796, she was the empress of Russia. The primary source of information about the first half of her life is her own Memoirs, which begin with her earliest recollections and continue to 1758, when she was twenty-nine and under stress at the court of Empress Elizabeth. Naturally, her memoirs display the subjective perspective of any memoir writer; even so, they are invaluable.”

- Dashkova: *Memoirs* (two volumes, 1840).
- Ambassador reports and memoirs (Ségur defended Jones “both at the time and later in his memoirs”; Ligne, Harris, etc.).
