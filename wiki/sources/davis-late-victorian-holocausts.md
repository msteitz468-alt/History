# Late Victorian Holocausts (Mike Davis)

Mike Davis's *Late Victorian Holocausts* (2001) argues that the great tropical famines of **1876–79** and
**1896–1902** — in India, Qing China, Brazil, the Horn and elsewhere — were not natural disasters but the
violent intersection of **El Niño/Southern Oscillation (ENSO) drought** with **the world market and
colonial policy**, and that they are the moment in which the **"Third World"** — the global periphery of
poverty and dependency — was *made*. Eleventh source of **Gap 1 (Modern)**, and the wiki's companion to
[[hochschild-king-leopolds-ghost|Hochschild]] on the human cost of high [[imperialism|imperialism]].

## The Argument

Davis braids three strands:

1. **Climate.** The famines coincided with exceptionally strong **ENSO** events; Davis uses the new
   climatology of "teleconnections" to show how a single oceanic-atmospheric oscillation could fail the
   monsoon across India, China and Africa simultaneously.
2. **Political economy.** Drought need not mean famine. What turned crop failure into mass death was
   **market integration and ideology**: grain was exported from famine zones at the height of starvation
   (India under Lytton continued grain exports to Britain), relief was withheld or made deliberately
   punitive on **Malthusian and laissez-faire** grounds (the Temple wage, set below subsistence), and
   railways moved food toward paying demand rather than toward the hungry. The Qing state, by contrast,
   with its older granary and relief tradition now hollowed out, offers a comparative case of declining
   capacity.
3. **The making of the Third World.** The famines, Davis argues, were the demographic face of the **Great
   Divergence's** dark side: the forcible incorporation of Asia, Africa and Latin America into a
   London-centred world economy as dependent, deindustrializing peripheries — the origin of the
   nineteenth-century gap between a rich "First World" and an impoverished "Third."

## Significance

The book is the wiki's anchor for the **colonial production of famine**, for **environmental history at
global scale** (ENSO as a world-historical actor), and for one materialist account of the **origins of
global inequality** — to be read against, and alongside, the New Imperialism narrative of
[[hobsbawm-age-of-empire|Hobsbawm]] and the imperial-knowledge critiques of [[said-orientalism|Said]].
