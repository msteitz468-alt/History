# Gettysburg (Stephen W. Sears, 2003)

**Source page for Stephen W. Sears's acclaimed operational and narrative history of the 1863 Gettysburg Campaign and the Battle of Gettysburg (1–3 July 1863).** The book is grounded in rich primary testimony and provides the definitive modern treatment of Lee's second invasion of the North, the largest and costliest battle of the American Civil War, and its immediate aftermath. Sears portrays Gettysburg as the turning point in the Eastern Theater: the end of major Confederate offensives northward, a decisive Union defensive victory under a new commander (Meade, on his fourth day in army command), and a demonstration of the limits of offensive infantry assaults against prepared positions supported by artillery. The work balances high-level operational context and command decisions with granular eyewitness accounts of the fighting.

**Sears-Gettysburg-R1-01: ConfederateArroganceFromPriorVictories**
**Grounding quote:** "the universal feeling in the army was one of profound contempt for an enemy whom they have beaten so constantly...." "What happened in the three days of Gettysburg, July 1–3, 1863, was in some measure a result of that arrogance. To be sure, there was good reason for Robert E. Lee and the Army of Northern Virginia to be contemptuous of the Yankees. At Fredericksburg the previous December they had beaten them easily, and two months ago at Chancellorsville they had beaten them again..."
**Locator:** Introduction near opening
**Analysis:** Sears asserts that the shared attitude of contempt in both armies shaped expectations for a decisive battle in Pennsylvania. Lee marched north expecting a third victory on enemy soil with higher stakes than prior eastern fights. This mindset, the text states, contributed directly to the campaign's character and outcome at Gettysburg by influencing risk acceptance and reconnaissance priorities.

**Sears-Gettysburg-R1-02: VicksburgCrisisTriggersPennsylvaniaInvasion**
**Grounding quote:** "The Confederacy was greatly in need of such a victory in that early summer of 1863. Vicksburg was besieged and almost certainly beyond saving... **Locator:** Introduction
**Analysis:** Sears states that the May 15 Richmond conference was triggered by the western crisis, specifically Grant's moves against Vicksburg and Johnston's inability to relieve it. The invasion was approved as an eastern offset rather than direct reinforcement to Mississippi. This framing established the campaign's strategic stakes and explains Lee's willingness to accept high operational risk.

**Sears-Gettysburg-R6-10: PickettChargeCasualtiesNarrative**
**Grounding quote:** "By the best estimate, better than half of the 13,000 Confederates who made Pickett's Charge were casualties—6,600. Because the Federals did not record their Gettysburg casualties on a day-by-day basis ... their casualties in repelling Pickett's Charge can only be estimated.
