## Overview

Kevin M. Kruse and Julian E. Zelizer's *Fault Lines: A History of the United States Since 1974* (W. W. Norton, 2nd ed. 2025; orig. 2019) is a synthetic political and cultural history of the United States from the mid-1970s (post-Watergate, post-1960s) through the 2024 election and the opening months of a second Trump administration. At ~196k words / 564 pages it provides a single-volume narrative of how the US moved from mid-century efforts at consensus to a deeply divided polity organized around persistent "fault lines."

The core thesis: the economic, racial, political, and (later) gender/sexuality divisions long present in American life were not healed after the 1960s; instead, institutions, politics, and especially media were rebuilt in the 1970s–2020s to accommodate and even reward competition, fragmentation, and conflict rather than to pursue common ground. A once relatively unified postwar media environment fractured into partisan channels (cable, talk radio, internet, social platforms) that confirmed and deepened pre-existing biases. Economic shifts eroded middle-class security; racial and cultural backlash politics hardened; both parties moved away from the center. The result is a nation that appears less and less "united" with each passing decade, even as markets and popular culture sometimes converged.

The book opens with Obama's 2017 farewell warning about inequality, race, and polarization, then traces the long build-up from 1974 onward through 16 chronological chapters plus Epilogue. It is written in a unified voice, aimed at general readers and students, and updated in the second edition to incorporate the full first Trump term, COVID, January 6, Biden years, and early second Trump term.
