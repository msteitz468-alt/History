# Theophilus of Edessa's Chronicle

A reconstructed lost chronicle that is one of the most important **Christian sources for the Arab
conquests and the Umayyad Caliphate** — and a case-study in how historical knowledge circulated across
the Greek, Syriac and Arabic worlds of the early Islamic Near East. The tenth source of this Phase-5
batch; reconstructed by **Robert G. Hoyland**. For the author see **[[theophilus-of-edessa|Theophilus of
Edessa]]**.

## The "Common Source" Problem

No manuscript of Theophilus's chronicle survives. Hoyland reconstructs it from a striking convergence:
three later chronicles in three languages — the **Greek** *Chronographia* of **Theophanes the
Confessor**, the **Arabic** chronicle of **Agapius of Manbij**, and the **Syriac** chronicle of
**Dionysius of Tel-Mahre** (preserved through Michael the Syrian and the anonymous *Chronicle of 1234*) —
share so much common matter for the seventh and eighth centuries that they must descend from a shared
"**eastern source**," identified as the chronicle of **Theophilus of Edessa** (d. 785). By laying the
dependents side by side, Hoyland recovers its outline and much of its content.

## Content and Value

The reconstructed chronicle covers the **late sixth to mid-eighth centuries**: the last Roman–Persian
wars, the **Arab conquests** of Syria, Mesopotamia, Egypt and beyond, the **[[umayyad-caliphate|Umayyad
Caliphate]]** and its civil wars, and the **Abbasid revolution**. Its great value is that it is an
**early, non-Muslim, contemporary-ish witness** to the rise of Islam, written by a Christian living
*inside* the Caliphate and independent of the Arabic-Islamic tradition that shaped most later accounts —
a crucial control for historians reconstructing the much-debated first century of Islam.

## Significance

Theophilus's chronicle is a linchpin of the modern study of the **"circulation of historical knowledge"**
across the late-antique and early-Islamic Near East, showing how a single Syriac text could feed Greek,
Arabic and Syriac historiography alike. It joins the wiki's growing dossier of **eastern-Christian
sources for the seventh–eighth centuries**, complementing the Syriac Miaphysite chronicles already
ingested.
