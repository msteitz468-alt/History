# The Archaeology of Elam (Potts, 2nd ed. 2016)

D. T. Potts, *The Archaeology of Elam: Formation and Transformation of an Ancient
Iranian State*, 2nd edn (Cambridge World Archaeology; Cambridge University Press,
2016 [1st edn 1999]). The definitive English-language synthesis of Elam — the great
literate civilization of southwestern Iran (Khuzistan + Fars + the Zagros) from the
late 4th millennium BCE through its afterlives under the Parthians and Sasanians.

This source is **Top-100 Structural Source #33** (section: Ancient Iran & Mesopotamia).
It is the spine source for the wiki's thin **Iran/Elam** coverage and substantially
extends the existing [[elam|Elam]] page beyond its Bronze-Age focus into the
**Neo-Elamite, Achaemenid, Elymaean, and Sasanian** periods that were previously absent.

## Central argument

Potts' organising thesis is that **"Elam" is not a single state but a sequence of
distinct polities and identities** ("many Elams") sharing a geographic core and a
recurring **highland–lowland dialectic**: the union of the irrigated lowland of
**Susiana** (around [[susa|Susa]]) with the highland zone (**Anshan**/[[anshan|Tal-i
Malyan]], Simashki, Fars) supplied the metals, stone, timber, and manpower that made
Elam Mesopotamia's perennial eastern rival and trading partner. He applies an explicitly
**Braudelian** frame (longue durée structures vs. event-history), insisting on the
discontinuities — Elam repeatedly dissolves and reconstitutes — against older narratives
of a continuous "Elamite empire."

## Volume Synthesis Note

Potts's *Archaeology of Elam* is, at root, a sustained argument **against treating Elam as
a continuous civilization and against the centre–periphery model** of the ancient Near
East. Its organising claim — "Elam is an artificial construct, a name coined by
Mesopotamian scribes" and imposed on heterogeneous highland and lowland peoples who shared
no single identity before the 18th century BCE — converts the subject from a national
history into a study of **construction, transformation, and ethnogenesis**. Against
Braudel, Potts explicitly rejects the *longue durée*: what strikes him is "the periodic
realignment and transformation of Elam," which "emerges transformed after each brutal
encounter with an 'other.'" This frames the whole arc the volume uniquely supplies to the
wiki — Awan, Shimashki, the sukkalmah, Middle Elamite, **and** the previously-absent
Neo-Elamite, Achaemenid, Elymaean, and Sasanian phases.

Three contributions reshape existing wiki pages. First, the **Anshan-primacy thesis**
(after Vallat 1980): the core of Elam lay in highland **Fars**, not lowland Susa, and the
familiar "Elam = Khuzistan" is a *pars pro toto* extension — which is why
[[anshan|Anshan/Tal-i Malyan]] now gets its own page and why the [[cyrus-the-great|Persian]]
title "king of Anshan" matters. Second, the **severance of Proto-Elamite from historical
Elam**: a ~900-year gap and a clean cultural rupture make the [[proto-elamite|Susa III]]
horizon "pre-Elamite," correcting the intuitive continuity older overviews assume. Third,
the **Elamite–Persian ethnogenesis**: Elamite language, gods, and personnel survive at the
heart of the [[achaemenid-empire|Achaemenid]] administration (the Persepolis archive), so
the [[sack-of-susa-646bce|646 BCE catastrophe]] did not end Elam so much as redistribute it
into its conquerors.

Against already-ingested sources, Potts **largely confirms but reframes** the CAH (Hinz)
and [[liverani-ancient-near-east-2014|Liverani]] accounts that built the original [[elam]]
page: the fratriarchal sukkalmah triumvirate, the highland–lowland resource dialectic, and
the Akkadization of Susa all stand, but Potts is far more sceptical of the king-lists, more
insistent on discontinuity, and far richer on the post-1100 BCE afterlives that those
Bronze-Age-focused sources omit. The one quiet tension is interpretive register: where
Liverani is comfortable speaking of long-run "modes of production," Potts foregrounds
contingency and rupture — a difference of historiographical temperament more than of fact.

Methodologically the volume also injects two durable cautions into the wiki: the
**taphonomic alluviation warning** (deep Khuzistan silt hides early sites, so "absence" is
often invisibility) and the **radiocarbon-plateau** limit on dating the 4th–3rd millennium
horizons — both now flagged on [[elam]] and [[proto-elamite]].
