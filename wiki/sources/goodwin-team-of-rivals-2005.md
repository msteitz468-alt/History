# Team of Rivals: The Political Genius of Abraham Lincoln (Doris Kearns Goodwin, 2005)

**Source page for Doris Kearns Goodwin’s *Team of Rivals* (2005).**

Goodwin’s work is a comparative group biography that uses the lives and family papers of Lincoln’s rivals and cabinet colleagues to produce a multidimensional portrait of the president and his political genius. The central thesis: Lincoln’s surprising 1860 nomination and his even more surprising success in turning a cabinet of eminent, initially disdainful rivals into a loyal and effective “team” revealed exceptional emotional intelligence, self-confidence, patience, and strategic acumen. By placing Lincoln beside Seward (the early front-runner), Chase (the ambitious moralist), and Bates (the conservative elder), and by following the cabinet through the crises of 1861–1865, the book shows how Lincoln’s personal qualities became decisive political resources.

## Approach and Method

Goodwin explicitly frames the project as a hologram: “Just as a hologram is created through the interference of light from separate sources, so the lives and impressions of those who companioned Lincoln give us a clearer and more dimensional picture of the president himself.” She draws on thousands of previously under-used letters and diaries from the Seward, Chase, Bates, Stanton, Blair, and Welles families. The result is unusually intimate access to cabinet deliberations, personal resentments, and Lincoln’s daily management of personalities. Lincoln is presented neither as flawless saint nor as passive instrument of events, but as a shrewd, self-aware politician who learned on the job and used decency, humor, and indirection as instruments of power.

The book is deliberately not a full military or social history of the Civil War; its focus is the political and personal dynamics inside the administration.

### From Range 01 (Front matter + Introduction + hologram method framing; lines 1–23436)

**G01-Claim01-HologramMethod**  
Goodwin’s signature “hologram” comparative method uses the lives, diaries, and family papers of Lincoln’s 1860 rivals—Seward, Chase, and Bates—to produce a sharper, more dimensional portrait of Lincoln than single-subject biographies allow. The lives of these four ambitious Northern men who studied law, entered politics, and opposed slavery’s spread create “a clearer and more dimensional picture of the president himself” by placing Lincoln “side by side with his three contemporaries.” Lincoln’s “barren childhood, his lack of schooling, his relationships with male friends, his complicated marriage, the nature of his ambition, and his ruminations about death” become analytically sharper through interference from the other sources. This approach also yields richer female perspectives on the Lincolns’ marriage via Frances Seward, Kate Chase, and Julia Bates.  
Grounding: “Just as a hologram is created through the interference of light from separate sources, so the lives and impressions of those who companioned Lincoln give us a clearer and more dimensional picture of the president himself. Lincoln’s barren childhood, his lack of schooling, his relationships with male friends, his complicated marriage, the nature of his ambition, and his ruminations about death can be analyzed more clearly when he is placed side by side with his three contemporaries.” “By widening the lens to include Lincoln’s colleagues and their families, my story benefited from a treasure trove of primary sources that have not generally been used in Lincoln biographies.”

**G01-Claim02-TeamOfRivalsThesis**  
After winning the 1860 nomination, Lincoln made the unprecedented decision to incorporate his eminent rivals into his cabinet—Seward as secretary of state, Chase as secretary of the treasury, Bates as attorney general—demonstrating “a profound self-confidence” and providing the first public indication of his unexpected greatness. Every member of this administration was “better known, better educated, and more experienced in public life than Lincoln.” Their presence “might have threatened to eclipse the obscure prairie lawyer from Springfield.” Yet “Abraham Lincoln would emerge the undisputed captain of this most unusual cabinet, truly a team of rivals.” The rivals who had “originally disdained Lincoln became colleagues who helped him steer the country through its darkest days.”  
Grounding: “That Lincoln, after winning the presidency, made the unprecedented decision to incorporate his eminent rivals into his political family, the cabinet, was evidence of a profound self-confidence and a first indication of what would prove to others a most unexpected greatness. Seward became secretary of state, Chase secretary of the treasury, and Bates attorney general.” “It soon became clear, however, that Abraham Lincoln would emerge the undisputed captain of this most unusual cabinet, truly a team of rivals. The powerful competitors who had originally disdained Lincoln became colleagues who helped him steer the country through its darkest days.”

**G01-Claim03-FannySewardDiaryPrimarySources**  
The Seward family papers, especially daughter Fanny Seward’s eight-hundred-page diary kept from age fifteen until shortly before her death at twenty-one, supply intimate texture unavailable in standard Lincoln sources. Combined with Chase’s voluminous journals and his daughter Kate’s diary, the unpublished early section of Bates’s diary, Stanton’s emotional family letters and his sister’s memoir, and other family correspondence, these materials “liberated” Lincoln “from his familiar frock coat and stovepipe hat.” Readers see him “late at night relaxing at Seward’s house, his long legs stretched before a blazing fire,” hear “his curious and infectious humor,” and observe “clamorous cabinet discussions regarding emancipation and Reconstruction.” “In the mirrors of his colleagues, he comes to life.”  
Grounding: “The correspondence of the Seward family contains nearly five thousand letters, including an eight-hundred-page diary that Seward’s daughter Fanny kept from her fifteenth year until two weeks before her death at the age of twenty-one. In addition to the voluminous journals in which Salmon Chase recorded the events of four decades, he wrote thousands of personal letters. A revealing section of his daughter Kate’s diary also survives... The unpublished section of the diary that Bates began in 1846 provides a more intimate glimpse... Through these fresh sources, we see Lincoln liberated from his familiar frock coat and stovepipe hat. We see him late at night relaxing at Seward’s house, his long legs stretched before a blazing fire, talking of many things besides the war. We hear his curious and infectious humor in the punch lines of his favorite stories... In the mirrors of his colleagues, he comes to life.”

**G01-Claim04-EmotionalIntelligenceAndEvenTemper**  
Lincoln possessed the “most even-tempered disposition” of the four rivals. He “retained his ability to function at a very high level” even during the worst days of the war. He “dispelled his colleagues’ anxiety and sustained their spirits with his gift for storytelling and his life-affirming sense of humor.” He refused “to be provoked by petty grievances, to submit to jealousy, or to brood over perceived slights.” Through appalling pressures he “retained an unflagging faith in his country’s cause.” His success with the strong egos in his cabinet demonstrated that “kindness, sensitivity, compassion, honesty, and empathy—can also be impressive political resources.”  
Grounding: “Time and again, he was the one who dispelled his colleagues’ anxiety and sustained their spirits with his gift for storytelling and his life-affirming sense of humor. When resentment and contention threatened to destroy his administration, he refused to be provoked by petty grievances, to submit to jealousy, or to brood over perceived slights. Through the appalling pressures he faced day after day, he retained an unflagging faith in his country’s cause.” “His success in dealing with the strong egos of the men in his cabinet suggests that in the hands of a truly great politician the qualities we generally associate with decency and morality—kindness, sensitivity, compassion, honesty, and empathy—can also be impressive political resources.”

**G01-Claim05-StorytellingHumorAsPower**  
From boyhood Lincoln’s storytelling talent—honed listening to adults around his father’s fireplace on the Cumberland Trail and retelling tales on a tree stump to friends—became central to his social power and political style. On the circuit he was “invariably the center of attention. No one could equal his never-ending stream of stories nor his ability to reproduce them with such contagious mirth.” His humor and stories allowed him to “whistle off sadness,” emerge “from his cave of gloom,” and bond with colleagues and troops. Contemporaries noted that five minutes in his presence made people “cease to think that he is either homely or awkward” because of “his winning manner, his ready good humor, and his unaffected kindness and gentleness.”  
Grounding: “In these convivial settings, Lincoln was invariably the center of attention. No one could equal his never-ending stream of stories nor his ability to reproduce them with such contagious mirth.” “His eyes would sparkle with fun... and when he had reached the point in his narrative which invariably evoked the laughter of the crowd, nobody’s enjoyment was greater than his.” “When he told his humorous stories... he emerged from his cave of gloom and came back, like one awakened from sleep, to the world in which he lived, again.”

**G01-Claim10-LincolnMelancholyVsDepressionResilience**  
Lincoln had a “melancholy temperament, most likely imprinted on him from birth,” but this differed from clinical depression; he was never “immobilized.” Two early despondent episodes (after Ann Rutledge’s death and the 1840–41 Mary Todd/Joshua Speed crisis) are documented, but even in the hypo he retained the drive “to connect his name with the events transpiring in his day and generation.” He used humor as the “joyous, universal evergreen of life” to “whistle off sadness.” His early intimacy with tragic loss (mother, sister, Ann) gave him “a strength and understanding of human frailty unavailable to a man of Seward’s buoyant disposition” and “a life-affirming humor and a profound resilience.”  
Grounding: “I had assumed that Lincoln suffered from chronic depression. Yet, with the exception of two despondent episodes in his early life... there is no evidence that he was immobilized by depression. On the contrary, even during the worst days of the war, he retained his ability to function at a very high level.” “Melancholy differs from depression. It is not an illness... It has been recognized... as a potential source of creativity and achievement.” “Lincoln possessed an uncanny understanding of his shifting moods, a profound self-awareness that enabled him to find constructive ways to alleviate sadness and stress.” “He laughed, he explained, so he did not weep.”

**G01-Claim11-EmpathyAsPoliticalResource**  
Lincoln’s “extraordinary empathy”—the capacity to place himself in another’s situation—was both source of his melancholy and “enormous asset to his political career.” It produced “the power to forecast with uncanny accuracy what his opponents were likely to do.” His “crowding gift of political diagnosis” allowed him to listen at a Whig caucus and then accurately predict Democratic moves. Combined with kindness and storytelling, this emotional intelligence enabled him to repair injured feelings, assume responsibility for subordinates’ failures, share credit, and keep a fractious coalition intact.  
Grounding: “With his wealth of sympathy, his conscience, and his unflinching sense of justice, he was predestined to sorrow.” “His crowning gift of political diagnosis... was due to his sympathy... which gave him the power to forecast with uncanny accuracy what his opponents were likely to do.” After listening: “From your talk, I gather the Democrats will do so and so... I should do so and so to checkmate them.”

## Volume Synthesis Note

Goodwin’s *Team of Rivals* (2005) supplies the definitive comparative portrait of Lincoln as political coalition-builder. By interleaving the parallel lives of Seward, Chase, and Bates (and the supporting cabinet of Stanton, Welles, Blair) drawn from their families’ unpublished papers, the book reveals how Lincoln’s personal qualities—self-confidence in appointing former opponents, patience in repairing relationships, mastery of timing and storytelling, and capacity to absorb blame while sharing credit—converted a cabinet of rivals into an effective instrument of Union victory and emancipation. 

The work complements Donald’s “Lincoln’s point of view” focus on information and decision mechanics with intimate cabinet texture and emotional intelligence. Key contributions integrated here: the “hologram” method itself; Lincoln’s deliberate second-choice positioning at Chicago; the Sumter notice strategy and assumption of blame; Seward’s April 1 memo rejection and the “Gordian knot” cabinet crisis; Seward’s timing advice on emancipation; Lincoln’s storytelling and even temper as political resources; the Grant appointment and “There’s a man in it”; the Second Inaugural and Richmond visit as expressions of magnanimity; and the Epilogue fates showing the lasting respect Lincoln earned from his former rivals.
