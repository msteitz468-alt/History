# The Grand Strategy of the Byzantine Empire (Edward N. Luttwak, 2009)

Luttwak's study of how the eastern Roman (Byzantine) empire survived for centuries against vastly superior enemies through a distinctive combination of diplomacy, intelligence, prestige, dynastic politics, and military methods that emphasized maneuver, economy of force, and the operational level over decisive battle or total conquest. It is explicitly positioned as the sequel to his *The Grand Strategy of the Roman Empire* (1976), revealing a "richer body of strategy" adapted to chronic weakness rather than abundance.

The book argues that Byzantium invented or perfected methods of "grand strategy" in the full sense (higher statecraft + theater + operational + tactical), with heavy reliance on non-military instruments because it lacked the material superiority of the earlier united empire.

## Historiography Note

Luttwak brings a strategist’s eye to Byzantine military and diplomatic texts that specialists had mined for tactics but not always for grand strategy. He highlights continuity with Roman inheritance while stressing adaptation to new realities (steppe threats, Islamic conquest, resource scarcity). Strengths: close reading of manuals (Strategikon emphasis on avoiding battle, intelligence, allies); integration of "soft power" (religion, prestige, marriages) as strategic tools. Potential critiques: possible over-attribution of deliberate system to practices that evolved ad hoc; less attention to economic base or internal politics than some Byzantine historians (e.g. Haldon, Kaegi). Complements recent flourishing of Byzantine studies and primary text editions.

## Consolidated Synthesis (main-thread)

Luttwak argues that the Byzantine empire survived 800+ years against superior enemies not by superior strength but by a distinctive grand strategy that integrated diplomacy (envoys, religion as statecraft, imperial prestige, dynastic marriages, protocol/gifts), superior information/intelligence, and military methods emphasizing maneuver, containment, small-unit action, relational/elastic defense, and economy of force over decisive attrition battles or total destruction. 

“Operational code” here signifies a set of observed and imputable behavioral norms (Luttwak explicitly adopts the term from Leites’ study of the Politburo and notes its resonance with the “Byzantine Generals Problem” in computer science). Luttwak holds that the foundation of this code and grand-strategic practice was already in place before Justinian. The code was codified in the continuous tradition of military treatises from Maurikios' Strategikon through the 10th-c. renaissance (De Velitatione, Praecepta Militaria, Leo VI, Nikephoros Ouranos) to Kekaumenos, while naval power (dromons + Greek fire used defensively) and fortifications (Constantinople) provided force multipliers.

Part One shows the "invention" in response to the 5th-c. Hunnic crisis (tactical copy of composite bow/mounted archery + operational mobility; shift from Roman-style infantry attrition). Part Two details the "myth and methods" of diplomacy as primary instrument, with case studies (Pechenegs as lever against Rus/Magyars; Bulgaria as created/controlled ally then rival with title concessions; Arab sieges contained by prestige + tribute + disunity exploitation; Seljuk personal diplomacy). Part Three analyzes the art of war (classical inheritance adapted; Strategikon as core; 10th-c. shift to offensive territorial reconquest; Herakleios' deep strategic maneuver vs. Persia as exemplar).

Contrast with Roman grand strategy (Luttwak's earlier book and the associated controversy): abundance allowed more direct preclusive or hegemonic control; Byzantium's chronic weakness forced richer, more sophisticated use of persuasion + method + information. Scholarly reception of Luttwak’s Roman thesis (pro: Brunt, Badian, Dyson; contra: Mann, Isaac, Whittaker, Mattern) is noted as background; the Byzantine study supplies the sequel that reveals a more developed system.
