# The New Cambridge Medieval History, Volume 3: c. 900–c. 1024 (1999)

*The New Cambridge Medieval History, Volume 3: c. 900–c. 1024*, edited by Timothy Reuter. Cambridge University Press, 1999.

This volume is the primary academic backbone for the **long tenth century** — the post-Carolingian era, c. 900–c. 1024, spanning the dissolution of the Carolingian political order and the emergence of the durable political geography of medieval Europe. Chronologically it picks up where [[mckitterick-ncmh-v2-1995|NCMH II]] (c. 700–c. 900) closes, on the threshold of the post-Carolingian world, and runs to the eve of the High Middle Ages and the reform papacy covered by NCMH IV. Its central interpretive preoccupation is the debate over whether the decades around the millennium witnessed a "**feudal revolution**" (*mutation de l'an mil*) — a sudden, totalising shift from Carolingian public order to privatised, castle-based seigneurial lordship — or whether that picture is an artefact of changing documentation. Reuter, in a long and influential Introduction, declines to synthesise: he holds that the model fails for important regions (Germany, England, León, southern Italy), that its key planks (the slavery-to-serfdom shift; "feudalism" as a coherent system of fiefs and vassalage) are themselves under sustained challenge, and that "any attempt to offer a synthesis at this stage would be futile."

The volume covers a Europe of striking unevenness. The Latin west is variously source-rich (Italy, Catalonia, Cluny's archive) and source-poor (Burgundy, the emergent eastern states), narrated by a "golden age" of historian-hagiographers (Widukind, Liudprand, Thietmar, Flodoard, Richer, Dudo, Adhémar, Radulf Glaber) whose smooth positivist surfaces have been "deeply pitted" by source-criticism. Byzantium and Islamic Spain/Sicily, by contrast, are court-centred, urbanised, taxing, legislating polities for which the western periodisations simply do not fit — for Byzantium the period is a "golden age," not an "age of iron." The emergent realms of Rus′, Poland, Bohemia and Hungary have no contemporary indigenous narratives at all, only later mythologising origin-histories, and their histories are "the most contestable and contested" in the volume.

## Volume Synthesis Note

NCMH III is, above all, a volume about **fragmentation and the divergence of historiographical traditions** — and Reuter's editorial achievement is to refuse the false coherence that a single narrative would impose. Its organising argument, set out in his Introduction and threaded through the regional chapters, is the **"feudal revolution"/*mutation de l'an mil*** debate ([[feudal-revolution-debate]]): did the decades around 1000 see a real, rapid collapse of Carolingian public order into privatised, castle-based seigneurial lordship (Duby, Bonnassie, Poly-Bournazel, Bisson), or is that picture an artefact of newly loquacious documentation (Barthélemy) and of a "feudalism" invented by twelfth-century lawyers (Reynolds)? Reuter holds synthesis to be "futile," and — crucially — shows the model **fits west Francia and Catalonia far better than Germany, England, León, or southern Italy**. The volume thus stands as a sustained warning against generalising the experience of one region (or one national historiography) across the whole of Latin Europe.

A second, unifying thread is the **detachment of the Carolingian inheritance from the Carolingian dynasty**. As the patrilineal line failed in east and west alike, "Carolingian traditions, attached to kingship itself" (Nelson) were taken up by new houses — Ottonians, Capetians, the West Saxon kings of England — and even exported to the new peripheral kingdoms. Nelson's argument that the **survival of realms** (by no means assured c. 920) owed less to royal resources than to the **aristocracy's investment in a "regnal community,"** and the drift toward **indivisible, associative succession**, recurs from the Ottonian Reich to England to León. The volume's deepest structural contrast is therefore between the **strong itinerant monarchies** (the [[ottonian-dynasty|Ottonian Reich]], with its [[reichskirche|imperial church]]; precociously administered [[kingdom-of-england-late-anglo-saxon|late Anglo-Saxon England]]) and the **fragmented realms** ([[capetian-dynasty|west Francia]], the [[kingdom-of-italy|kingdom of Italy]]) where banal lordship engrossed public power.

A third thread is the **millennial expansion of Latin Christendom** and the parallel **drawing of new kingdoms into the two churches**. The Otto III–[[gerbert-of-aurillac|Sylvester II]] *Renovatio* nexus links Cycles 2 and 4: the [[synod-of-gniezno-1000|Act of Gniezno (1000)]] and the royal church of [[stephen-i-of-hungary|St Stephen]] (1000/1001) midwifed Christian, Latin-rite kingdoms in [[boleslaw-i-chrobry|Poland]], [[premyslid-bohemia|Bohemia]], and [[hungary-arpad|Hungary]] — while [[kievan-rus|Rus′]] took Orthodoxy from Byzantium at the [[conversion-of-vladimir-988|conversion of 988]], fixing the confessional fault-line of Europe. The settlement of Scandinavians ([[treaty-of-saint-clair-sur-epte-911|Normandy]]) and the end of Magyar raiding at the [[battle-of-lechfeld-955|Lechfeld]] belong to the same century-long turn from raiding to Christian statehood.

Finally, the volume insists that the **western periodisations do not travel** to the court-centred, urbanised, taxing-and-legislating polities of the Mediterranean. For [[macedonian-dynasty|Macedonian]] Byzantium the long tenth century was "as much a golden age as an age of iron" — a passage from defensive equilibrium to the reconquests of Phokas, Tzimiskes, and [[basil-ii|Basil II]] (the [[conquest-of-bulgaria-1018|annexation of Bulgaria, 1018]]) — and for the [[caliphate-of-cordoba|Caliphate of Córdoba]] it was the apogee of al-Andalus (Abd al-Rahman III, al-Hakam II, al-Mansur) before the *fitna* collapse of 1009–1031.

**Cross-volume tensions.** NCMH III continues directly from [[mckitterick-ncmh-v2-1995|NCMH II]], inverting its theme: where Vol. 2 narrated Carolingian *consolidation*, Vol. 3 narrates post-Carolingian *devolution*, while stressing the continuity of Carolingian ideology detached from the dynasty. Its sustained scepticism toward the *mutation féodale* is the medieval counterpart of the **transformation-vs-catastrophe** debate the wiki tracks for the fall of Rome ([[nature-of-carolingian-government]], the [[feudal-revolution-debate]]): in both cases the volume's authors resist a dramatic, datable "fall" in favour of contested, uneven, source-dependent change. The Byzantine and Andalusi chapters quietly rebuke the Eurocentric, Latin-west framing of "the tenth century," exactly as the wiki's Cambridge World History material insists on a polycentric Afro-Eurasia.
