# The Oxford Handbook of Modern African History (Parker & Reid, eds., 2013)

A thematic OUP handbook, 27 chapters, on modern African history (deep-time structures through the
present, weighted to c. 1800–present). It is organized **thematically, not chronologically** —
ecology, demography, the slave trades, statehood, identity, the colonial encounter, religion, social
life, and culture — and self-consciously stresses African agency, continuity across the colonial
"rupture," and the historiography of the field. Its value to this wiki is **social and cultural
depth**: it fills the dimensions the political/dynastic backbone (GHA, CHA) leaves thin.

Ingested via the **deployed-subagent strategy** (CLAUDE.md). Substantive text: lines **677–13,614**;
lines 13,615+ are the Index.

## Volume Synthesis Note

Parker & Reid's *Handbook* lands on a wiki whose African coverage was already strong on **polities and
politics** (the GHA and Cambridge History of Africa backbones: empires, dynasties, the slave trade,
colonial conquest, nationalism, decolonization). Its distinctive contribution here is therefore
**social, cultural, and environmental depth** — the dimensions a political narrative leaves thin. The
24 new pages add Africa's environmental and demographic history, the lived experience of the colonial
state (law, labour, chieftaincy, ethnicity), the religious landscape (African Christianity and Islam as
*African* phenomena, indigenous religions, the independent-church and Pentecostal explosion), the social
fabric (education, gender, urbanization, health, youth), and cultural production (visual art, music,
material culture, media).

The volume's method is its message: it insists on **African agency** and **continuity across the
colonial "rupture,"** treating the colonial period as one episode in a longer African history rather than
its hinge. This reframes several existing controversies — the invention-of-tradition debate
([[chieftaincy]], [[ethnicity-in-africa]]), the demographic-homeostasis debate
([[disease-and-demography-in-africa]]), and the integrated character of Africa's four slave trades
([[african-slave-trades-comparative]]). Where it overlaps the political backbone it was deliberately set
to **link, not duplicate** — so the wiki now reads African history along two complementary axes,
political-narrative and thematic-social.
