# Robert M. Citino, *Death of the Wehrmacht: The German Campaigns of 1942* (2007)

University Press of Kansas, Modern War Studies series. A focused operational study of the Wehrmacht in 1942 across the Eastern Front (Crimea, Kharkov, summer offensive) and North Africa (Gazala to El Alamein). Citino places the year's events in the long arc of Prussian-German military tradition: the last flowering of Bewegungskrieg (war of movement) followed by its structural breakdown.

This source is a core Tier 4 title for the warfare hub (WWII section). It supplies the German operational lens, explicit framing of 1942 as the end of an era for German command and maneuver practice, and parallel treatment of the two southern theaters. Complements Glantz/House (Soviet archival detail on Blau approach) and West Point WWII volumes (where Citino himself contributed chapters).

## Volume Synthesis Note (main thread, after all ranges)

Citino's *Death of the Wehrmacht* (2007) delivers a powerful operational and cultural interpretation of 1942 as the moment the centuries-old Prussian-German tradition of *Bewegungskrieg* reached both its apogee and its structural end. Spring victories (Kerch/Trappenjagd under Manstein, 2nd Kharkov annihilation, Gazala/Tobruk under Rommel, Sevastopol) are presented as the last "classic" *Kesselschlachten* — front-loaded, concentric, relying on surprise, air-ground synergy (Richthofen), and aggressive subordinate initiative — executed with numbers inferior to the enemy yet achieving decisive results at low cost.

The summer campaigns (Operation Blue / Fall Blau on the East Front and the parallel North African drive) are the "debacle." Even at the moment of apparent triumph (Voronezh, Millerovo, Rostov, deep into the Caucasus and onto the Volga "gates"), the old method failed to destroy the enemy army. Reasons grounded in the text: 
- Enormous distances and "arithmetic" shortages (trucks 80-85% deficient, many non-motorized allied divisions, replacement crisis).
- Hitler's interventions and the post-Moscow centralization (radio enabling real-time meddling; loss of *Selbständigkeit der Unterführer*).
- Soviet adaptation (strategic retreat / flight instead of standing to be encircled; Order 227; new armies; counterstrokes from the start).
- The "empty" or "ordinary" victories that produced territory but not annihilation.

The twin disasters at El Alamein (material wall stops Rommel at culmination) and Stalingrad (Uranus encirclement while 6th Army is already attrited) mark the public death of the tradition. Citino contrasts the 1942 spring "moral tonic" and "precision watch" (Militär-Wochenblatt) with the reality that classic encirclement was no longer possible against industrialized coalitions in vast spaces. The book supplies the German-officer-corps lens and explicit long-historical framing (Frederick, Moltke, Blücher parallels) that complements the Soviet-archival detail in Glantz/House and the survey chapters Citino himself wrote for the West Point History of WWII.

### Ch. 6 — Mersa Matruh aftermath and approach to El Alamein (late June 1942)
British XIII Corps slipped the trap (Gott’s corps retreated to Fuka per Auchinleck); X Corps left "in the lurch" due to unsigned orders, ambiguous language, radio failure ("one of the war’s classic 'left in the lurch' moments"). Germans took ~8,000 prisoners at Matruh total; own losses "negligible" but "any losses at all were becoming intolerable." Panzerarmee demonstrated "mastery of operational-level maneuver" but British clearly improving in matériel/air/armor. Rommel reached El Alamein west edge June 30, planning to take it "on the run."

Verbatim (Rommel): "There are always moments in which the commander’s place is with the troops rather than with the staff. The notion that 'the battalion commander alone is supposed to keep up the morale of the troops is sheer nonsense.'"

British recovery: 8th Army "not so much demoralized as 'bewildered'"; "brave but baffled." New formations (51st Highland, 44th, 18th Indian, 9th Australian) + veterans (1st Armoured, 50th, 2nd NZ — regarded by Rommel as elite). Centralized artillery under Auchinleck. Panzerarmee at Clausewitz culmination point: ~50 tanks (mostly Mark IIIs), divisions 1,500–2,000 men, air support outrun after 300-mile advance in a week. Used ~6,000 captured British trucks, 25-pdrs, rations, and gasoline. Rommel "lived permanently on the wrong side" of the culmination point.

### Ch. 6 — First Battle of El Alamein (July 1–~July 10, 1942)
Plan: 90th Light penetrate south of El Alamein box and wheel to sea; Afrika Korps wheel right to encircle armor. Entered with 52 serviceable tanks. Assault 3:15 A.M. July 1 broke down immediately under massed SA artillery (Auchinleck centralized control "complete surprise"; Germans expected only weak 50th Division remnants). 90th Light panic ~1530 hours, stopped by Divisional Commander/Chief of Staff. Afrika Korps late (sandstorm + escarpments); stormed Deir el Shein (18th Indian Brigade), 1,200 prisoners but 18 tanks lost (35% of total armor).

July 2: Attempt "to widen the break-in into a breakthrough" failed vs. 1st Armoured on Ruweisat + Robcol. July 3: Full mobile battering ram (15th/21st Pz, 90th Light, Ariete/Littorio) failed; NZ mobile columns smashed Ariete in one hour. "It was a signal moment, perhaps the signal moment, in the desert war." Rommel halted, extricated 26 remaining tanks at night, replaced with Italian infantry. "The presence of the latter at the front could mean only one thing: the war of movement had come to a halt."

Auchinleck exploited Italian weakness: Australians destroyed Sabratha at Tel el Eisa (July 10). Operation Splendour (July 22) British disaster (6th NZ overrun; 23rd Armoured "real Balaklava" — 100/106 tanks lost).

Quote (Rommel): "The front had now solidified. In operational terms, the British were in their element... In this Stellungskrieg, the decision went to the side that fired off more ammunition."

### Ch. 6 — Alam Halfa (Aug 30–Sept 6, 1942)
Rommel offensive after August hiatus (recovered to 229 German + 243 Italian tanks). Plan: diversionary infantry fix attention; mobile group crashes southern minefields, 25–30 mile drive east, wheel north past Alam Halfa to sea. Kesselring fuel guarantee doubted.

Battle: Minefields far tougher. RAF + preregistered artillery savage on 90th Light. Afrika Korps hits ridge (44th Inf + 22nd Armd: dug-in Grants, 6-pdrs) evening Aug 31 after sandstorm. Charge breaks up; 22 tanks lost of 100 in two hours. Sept 1: single-division attack (fuel) fails. Hedgehog under nonstop bombardment; night bombing terrifying. Rommel nearly killed. Retreat ordered Sept 3; back near start line by Sept 6 ("six-day race").

Commanders lost early (Bismarck KIA; Kleemann/Nehring wounded). British (Montgomery) fought "as defensively as any force in history." No operational maneuver. "the traditional German operational pattern met the Grand Alliance for the first time." 3,000 German casualties/55 tanks/700 vehicles; "its ability to maneuver" lost. 15,600 bombs on tiny area; "literally nailed my army to the ground." ULTRA gave Montgomery every message in hours.

### Ch. 7 — Directive 45, split, and exzentrisch operations
Blue had failed (Soviets fled rather than stand for Kessel). Directive 45 (23 July): Edelweiss (AG A/List) main effort (Caucasus oil/ports/passes). Fischreiher (AG B/Weichs; 6th Army primary): Don bend, Stalingrad, Volga block. Split despite prerequisites unmet. Result: Aufspaltung/exzentrisch (exact opposite of concentric/"march separately and strike united"). AG A resources stripped (Hoth transferred for Stalingrad priority). 300-mile separation, 200-mile gap. Halder: "senseless pile-up"; "unprofessional and defective." Systemic (Soviet space + limited Wehrmacht resources), not merely personalist. Survival now on allies (Romanians/Hungarians/Italians). 200k German casualties August (new high).

### Ch. 7 — Caucasus: Edelweiss (Great Lunge then Brake)
July 26: "most successful in the history of the German army" in speed. 1st Pz Army (Kleist/Mackensen III Pz: 13th Pz) crosses Manych (first into Asia), reaches Maikop Aug 9 (stealth + muscle; Brandenburger/NKVD uniforms). 13th Pz ~250 miles in 17 days. Maikop only original objective achieved — but "classic example of fundamental demolition" (wells concreted).

Brake (Aug 1+): priority reversal; fuel/air lost. Mackensen runs dry day 3, sits 4 days. Air/Sturmoviks to Stalingrad. Terrain hell: "mountainous primeval forest... streams/rivers as routes"; horses die; wounded evacuation nightmare. 17th Army stalls (Novorossiisk taken Sept 6; Soviets retain mountains). Tuapse/Suchumi unreachable. Hitler relieves List Sept 9, takes personal command of AG A.

Terek/Ordzhonikidze (late focus): Oct 25–26 Mackensen envelops 37th Army near Nalchik (~11k prisoners early). 13th Pz reaches Gizel (5 miles/2 km from Ordzhonikidze) Nov 2–3. Bitter cold/snow; Stukas/Sturmoviks feast. Nov 6 Soviet counter penetrates thin flanks, cuts supply. Snow grounds Luftwaffe. Breakout Nov 9. "The drive on Ordzhonikidze had failed, as had the drive on the oil fields of Grozny—and the Caucasus campaign itself." "a hard-driving Panzer corps stopped, but still churning its legs, less than two kilometers away." By late Sept: 8 Soviet armies vs. 2 German. "no real Soviet flank." "Stellungskrieg with a vengeance."

### Overall (Ch. 7)
"vast bulge" Black Sea–Caucasus–Volga/Don. 625k prisoners (FHO), 7k tanks, 416 aircraft — "far lower than the nearly 4 million" 1941; "Russian army will enter the winter campaign weakened, but not fatally so." 200k German casualties August. Maikop only objective. "Not an inevitable defeat" in pure numbers but systemic limits shown. "Stellungskrieg with a vengeance."

## Claims Block — Range 05 (Ch. 8–9 cache: lines ~8201–10500): "The End: El Alamein and Stalingrad" + "Conclusion: The Death of the Wehrmacht"

### Core Thesis and Radio Centralization (Ch. 9)
- "In those brief six months, an entire way of war that dated back centuries had come to an end. The German traditions of maneuver-based *Bewegungskrieg*, the notion that 'war is an art, a free and creative activity,' the belief in the independence of the subordinate commander within his own sphere of competency: each and every one of these bedrock beliefs had taken a pounding... and in fact had revealed themselves to be no longer valid."
- Radio killed independent command: "gave the high command a precise, real-time picture of even the most rapid and far-flung operations. It also allowed staff and political leaders alike to intervene in the most detailed and, from the perspective of field commanders, the most obnoxious way possible." Staff officer: "This technical advantage had psychological disadvantages, however. Specifically, it caused the supreme command to interfere too often with the leadership of the armies under it. It also caused many army commanders to substitute the opinions of the high command for their own decisions."
- Historical contrast: Seydlitz to Frederick at Zorndorf; Derfflinger. Hitler takes heads (Bock, List, Halder, Heim). "Hitler did not kill the concept of flexible command... Radio did."
- 1942 victories were the last Kesselschlachten in the 300-year line from Great Elector to Bock. Small 1942 cauldrons "the last in a long line of German operational-level triumphs."
- Close calls: "13th Panzer Division... just a single mile outside of Ordzhonikidze"; Hube’s 3rd Pz to Volga suburbs; pioneers "just a few hundred yards away"; Rommel’s right wing "a mere half hour’s ride by armored car from Alexandria." "Rarely have the advance guards of a defeated army ever come so tantalizingly close to their strategic objectives."

### El Alamein (Ch. 8)
- British methodical "British way of war" (set-piece, attrition, "crowning" the infantry attack with armor) vs. German maneuver. Montgomery: infinite pains + contingency planning. "At El Alamein, therefore, 8th Army was not simply launching an offensive; it was returning to a distinctively British way of war."
- Panzerarmee state: culmination point passed (fuel, men, tanks ~50 serviceable); "Rommel was convinced of the correctness of his decision... to attack, in order not to be attacked." "The presence of the [Italian infantry] at the front could mean only one thing: the war of movement had come to a halt."
- "Rommel was... bringing down the curtain on the oldest tradition in German military history."

### Stalingrad / Uranus / Winter Storm (Ch. 8)
- Soviet "deep battle" / consecutive operations doctrine (Tukhachevsky/Isserson legacy revived): shock groups, echelons for exploitation, "unbroken chain" rather than single decisive battle.
- Uranus: echeloned concentrations vs. threadbare Romanian flanks (e.g. 70 tubes/km); "Luftstoss." Link-up at Sovietsky 22 Nov seals Kessel. Hitler: "6th Army will hedgehog itself and await further orders."
- Winter Storm: 6th Pz + 23rd reaches Mishkova but stalls; 6th Pz diverted for Little Saturn. Airlift far below needs (~117 t/day actual vs 500–750 required). 266–672 Ju-52s lost.
- "The Luftwaffe, too, suffered a major defeat at Stalingrad."

### End Conditions and Historiography (Ch. 9)
- "The Wehrmacht never demodernized operationally. In its bedrock conceptions of battle and campaigns, in its emphasis on the genius and the will of the commander over material factors, it wasn’t all that modern to begin with."
- Soviets "turned the tables": Wehrmacht now the pupil.

### North Africa Halts (Ch. 6)

**Mersa Matruh aftermath and push to El Alamein (late June 1942):** After British XIII Corps slipped the trap at Mersa Matruh, X Corps was left in the lurch due to communications breakdown ("one of the war’s classic 'left in the lurch' moments"). Auchinleck concentrating artillery under army control. Panzerarmee at culmination: ~50 tanks total, divisions 1,500–2,000 men, air support outrun. "Rommel was convinced of the correctness of his decision... to attack, in order not to be attacked."

Afrika Korps late (sandstorm + escarpments); stormed Deir el Shein (18th Indian Brigade), took 1,200 prisoners but lost 18 tanks (35% of total armor). Rommel pinned two hours under fire:

> Again heavy British fire struck our ranks. From the north, east, and south British shells screamed down on us. Tracer-shells from British flak went whizzing through the unit. The attack came to a standstill in the heaviest possible fire.

July 2: Attempt "to widen the break-in into a breakthrough" (den Einbruch zum Durchbruch zu erweitern) failed against 1st Armoured on Ruweisat + Robcol. July 3: Full mobile force (15th/21st Panzer, 90th Light, Ariete, Littorio) as battering ram; New Zealand mobile columns smashed Ariete (artillery and most tanks overrun in one hour). **Alam Halfa (Aug 30–Sept 6, 1942):** Window before US aid overwhelming. Rommel plan: refight Gazala—diversionary infantry assaults fix British, mobile group (90th Light, XX Motorized Ariete/Littorio/Trieste, Afrika Korps 15th/21st Pz, recon group) crashes southern minefields, drives 25–30 miles east, wheels north past Alam Halfa ridge to sea. "the long reaction time of the British command and troops." "the fait accompli of a finished operation." Odds estimated 3:1 tanks, 5:1 aircraft against. Staff "did not believe that we could break through to the Nile" (Mellenthin) but "voluntary retreat wasn’t in the cards."

Battle: Minefields far tougher; RAF and preregistered artillery (NZ and 7th Armd) savage 90th Light. Afrika Korps reaches ridge evening Aug 31 after sandstorm; charge against dug-in Grants/6-pdrs/artillery of 44th Div + 22nd Armd breaks up. 22 tanks lost of 100. Next days: fuel immobilizes; hedgehog under nonstop bombing (15,600 bombs on small area; night raids with flares). Rommel:

> There was one low-flying attack after another by British fighter bombers against my troops, causing them extraordinary losses. A mass of vehicles stood burning in the desert. ... A maneuver by large formations, the establishment and coordination of march schedules—these things seemed no longer possible.

Retreat ordered Sept 3; back to start line by Sept 6 ("six-day race"). Commanders lost early: Bismarck KIA, Kleemann/Nehring wounded. Rommel nearly killed. "the traditional German operational pattern met the Grand Alliance for the first time." Loss of "its ability to maneuver" more precious than the 3,000 casualties/55 tanks. ### Caucasus and Stalingrad Halts (Ch. 7)

**Directive 45 and dual offensive setup (July 23, 1942):** After Blue "Luftstoss" and Bock fired. Edelweiss (AG A, List, 5 armies planned) main effort: cross Don, destroy south of Rostov, overrun Black Sea coast, seize passes/Military Roads, Maikop/Grozny/Baku. Fischreiher (AG B, 6th Army Paulus primary): clear Don bend, seize Stalingrad, block Don-Volga, push to Astrakhan. "splitting up the original operation into two (Aufspaltung)." "exzentrisch" (eccentric) rather than concentric. Violated "march separately and strike united." Halder objected; "senseless pile-up of mobile formations around Rostov." **Caucasus Great Lunge then Brake:** July 26 onward, "most successful in the history of the German army" in mph. Heat 100°F+. III Pz Corps (Mackensen) first into Asia, Maikop Aug 9 (stealth + muscle). 13th Pz ~250 miles in 17 days. Maikop only strategic objective achieved—but "classic example of fundamental demolition." Then Aug 1: priority to Stalingrad; Hoth/4th Pz shifted to AG B; 11th Army diverted. AG A to 3 then 2 armies. Fuel crisis (Mackensen sat 4 days at critical moment; "Lack of fuel made any further movement... And how much we might have achieved!"). Air diverted; Sturmoviks appear. Terrain: "mountainous primeval forest"; rivers as roads; kitchens/ammo on horses; wounded evacuation nightmare. Novorossiisk taken Sept 6 after bitter fighting (Soviets hold mountains south). Tuapse/Suchumi stalled in passes. Hitler relieves List Sept 9, takes AG A command himself (stormy Vinnitsa scenes; mocks Halder's WWI office experience; fury over Elbrus ascent). Halder later dismissed.

Late focus east on Terek. Oct 25–26: Mackensen III Pz Corps (13th/23rd Pz + Romanians) envelops 37th Army at Nalchik (Stukas decapitate HQ; ~11,000 prisoners early). Wheels east. 23rd Pz closes Ossetian Road at Alagir Nov 1. 13th Pz (von der Chevallerie after Gott wounded) to Gizel (5 miles from Ordzhonikidze) Nov 2; two km from city by Nov 3 (II/66, II/93, I/99 Mountain Jägers, assault guns, engineers ready for road). "working like a precision watch." Nov 6 Soviet counterattack penetrates flanks, cuts 13th Pz; snow grounds Luftwaffe. Breakout Nov 9. "The drive on Ordzhonikidze had failed, as had the drive on the oil fields of Grozny—and the Caucasus campaign itself." "a hard-driving Panzer corps stopped, but still churning its legs, less than two kilometers away."

By end Sept: 8 Soviet armies vs. 2 German. "no real Soviet flank." "Stellungskrieg with a vengeance."

**Stalingrad approach and entry decision:** 6th Army secondary, fuel crises, immobilized late July. Kalach Kessel Aug 7–11 (XIV + XXIV Pz meet SW of Kalach; Luftwaffe seals; 35k+ prisoners, 270 tanks/560 guns in pocket; Don bend total 57k prisoners/~1k tanks). "first real encirclement of the 1942 campaign." Hube 16th Pz reaches Volga north of city Aug 23 after 35-mile land-bridge dash; narrow corridor, immediately counterattacked. Same night: heaviest Luftwaffe strike since Barbarossa (~600 bombers, incendiaries; fires 40 miles). Hoth delayed south; flanks with VI Romanian then wide maneuver to link Sept 2. Zhukov counter Sept 5 distracts but fails. 

Stalingrad: 600k pop, 30-mile linear urban strip/fortified bridgehead on Volga. "The Germans could never put it under siege." "conquered its way into an impasse." City fight = Stellungskrieg then Festungskrieg/Rattenkrieg ("the meter replaced the kilometer... city plan replaced the General Staff’s map" — Doerr). "the only decision consonant with its history and traditions." Assault began ~Sept 5. (Urban details to early Nov: Rodimtsev 13th Guards critical; Tractor Works Oct 14; Hubertus Nov 11 pioneers fail; divisions crossed off angriffsfähig list; Hitler Führerbefehl Nov 16 to continue.)

**Overall dual campaign conclusion (Ch. 7):** "vast bulge" from Black Sea to Caucasus to Volga/Don. 625k prisoners (FHO), 7k tanks, 416 aircraft—but "far lower than the nearly 4 million" in 1941; "Russian army will enter the winter campaign weakened, but not fatally so." 200k German casualties August alone (new high). Systemic, not just Hitler: Soviet space + limited German resources made concentration impossible. "the war with the Soviet Union had placed burdens on the Wehrmacht that were far heavier than the traditional German way of war could bear." Halder had shared underestimation of Soviets.
