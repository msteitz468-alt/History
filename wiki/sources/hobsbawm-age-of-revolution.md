# The Age of Revolution: Europe 1789–1848 (Eric Hobsbawm)

The first and most famous volume of **Eric Hobsbawm's** four-book history of the modern world — the
account of the **[[dual-revolution|dual revolution]]** that, between 1789 and 1848, "forms the greatest
transformation in human history since… men invented agriculture." Second source of **Outstanding Sources
Gap 1**, and the interpretive backbone for the **[[long-19th-century|Long 19th Century]]**.

## The Argument

Hobsbawm's organizing idea is the ***dual revolution***: the near-simultaneous **French Revolution**
(political) and **British Industrial Revolution** (economic), which together remade the world. The
period's great event, he insists, was not the triumph of "industry" or "liberty" in the abstract but of
a specific thing — ***bourgeois-liberal capitalism***:

- **the Industrial Revolution** in Britain — the breakthrough to self-sustaining industrial growth, the
  factory, the new working and middle classes;
- **the French Revolution** — the political template of the modern world: the overthrow of the *ancien
  régime*, the language of nation, citizen, rights, left and right, and the Napoleonic propagation of
  these across Europe;
- their fusion into the **conquering bourgeois** order, propagated from a "Franco-British base" across
  the globe, and the new ideologies it generated — **liberalism, nationalism, socialism**.

The book runs from the world of the 1780s through the revolutionary and Napoleonic upheavals to the
springtime of peoples in **1848**, where the volume ends and *[[hobsbawm-age-of-capital|The Age of
Capital]]* takes up.

## Significance

*The Age of Revolution* is the **classic interpretation of the birth of the modern world** and a
foundational text of social and economic history. Its "dual revolution" thesis frames the wiki's whole
Long-19th-Century period and connects the wiki's existing [[atlantic-revolutions|Atlantic revolutions]]
and [[nationalism|nationalism]] pages to the industrial transformation.
