# Livy, *Hannibal's War* (*Ab Urbe Condita*, Books 21–30)

The **third pentad** (here ten books) of Livy's *From the Foundation of the City* — "our most detailed
and exciting ancient account of the **[[second-punic-war-218-201bce|Second Punic War]]**," Hannibal's
sixteen-year war against Rome (218–201 BCE). The first of two Livy selections in this Phase-4 batch
(with **[[livy-mediterranean-empire|*Rome's Mediterranean Empire*]]**). For the author and his method
see **[[livy|Livy]]**.

## The Decade of Books

Books 21–30 run from the outbreak of war over **Saguntum** to the final victory:

- **21–22** — Hannibal's crossing of the Alps; the cascade of Roman disasters at the **Trebia**, **Lake
  Trasimene**, and the catastrophe of **[[battle-of-cannae-216bce|Cannae (216)]]**; Fabius "the
  Delayer" and his strategy of attrition.
- **23–27** — the war's long middle: the defection of **Capua** and much of southern Italy, the
  campaigns in Sicily (the fall of **Syracuse** and the death of Archimedes) and **Spain**, the
  disaster and death of the elder Scipios, and the rise of the young **[[scipio-africanus|Publius
  Cornelius Scipio]]**; the **Metaurus (207)**, where Hasdrubal's relieving army is destroyed.
- **28–30** — Scipio's conquest of Spain, his invasion of Africa, the recall of Hannibal, and the
  decisive **[[battle-of-zama-202bce|battle of Zama (202)]]** that ends the war and breaks
  **[[carthage|Carthage]]** as a great power.

## Livy the Narrative Artist

Hoyos stresses that the *Ab Urbe Condita* is a **literary masterpiece** before it is a research work.
Livy's versatile Latin moves from "the harrowing vividness of Hannibal's Alpine march" and the doomed
valour of Trasimene to the dry recording of election returns; he gives all his principals **carefully
crafted speeches** (Quintilian thought him "more eloquent than can possibly be described") to dramatize
debate and reveal character. Famous set-pieces and *sententiae* abound: **Maharbal's** rebuke after
Cannae — *"You know how to win a victory, Hannibal; you do not know how to use one"* — and the tragedy
of **Sophoniba** in Book 30.

## The Moral and Patriotic Frame

For Livy the war is "the **zenith of Roman heroism, virtue, and toughness**." After Cannae he declares
"no other nation… would not have been crushed by such a disaster"; the allies stayed loyal because
Roman authority was "just and tolerant"; the Senate sternly returns the parole-breaker as "a person of
truly un-Roman character." Roman leaders — the prudent **Fabius Maximus**, the steady elder Scipio,
the brilliant younger Scipio — embody *gravitas* and selfless patriotism, while Hannibal is drawn as a
figure of dazzling generalship marred (in the Roman tradition Livy inherits) by cruelty and
faithlessness. This patriotic, exemplary, senatorial coloring must be read with the source-criticism
set out on **[[livy]]**.
