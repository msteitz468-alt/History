# Tacitus, *Agricola* and *Germany* (*Germania*)

Tacitus's **two earliest works**, both written in **98 CE** as the terror of Domitian lifted under
Nerva and Trajan: the *Agricola*, "the first history of Britain," and the *Germania*, "the first
detailed description of the ancient Germans." Each is a unique survivor of its genre. Companion to the
already-ingested **[[tacitus-annals|*Annals*]]**; author page **[[tacitus|Tacitus]]**.

## The *Agricola*

A biography of **[[agricola|Gnaeus Julius Agricola]]** (40–93), Tacitus's father-in-law and the
governor who completed the conquest of **[[roman-britain|Britain]]** (governor 77–84). It is several
things at once:

- **An encomium and act of piety** — a substitute for the funeral oration, written in love for a dead
  kinsman, and opening with a defence of writing biography at all after Domitian had men executed for
  praising the wrong people.
- **An apologia** — Agricola (and, implicitly, Tacitus) served loyally under a tyrant; the work argues
  that "great men can exist even under bad emperors," that obsequiousness was not the only alternative
  to fruitless martyrdom.
- **The first connected account of Roman Britain** — its peoples, its geography, and the campaigns that
  carried Roman arms to the Highlands, culminating in the victory at **Mons Graupius** (84) over the
  Caledonians under **Calgacus**.

Its most famous passage is the speech Tacitus puts in the mouth of the British chieftain **Calgacus**
before the battle — the ancient world's sharpest indictment of imperialism, voiced by its victim:
*"They make a desert and call it peace"* (*solitudinem faciunt, pacem appellant*), and "robbery,
butchery, rapine, the liars call empire." Other epigrams: the Britons mistook for "civilization" what
"was a part of their enslavement."

## The *Germania*

A short ethnographic monograph on the **[[germanic-peoples|peoples of Germania]]** — the lands "beyond
the Rhine and Danube." Following the conventions of ancient ethnography, it moves from the land and its
origins (the Germans as an indigenous, "pure and unmixed" people), to general customs (kingship and
warbands, the assembly, religion, the *comitatus* bond of chief and retainers, marriage, feud and
hospitality), then a survey of the individual tribes from the Rhine to the Baltic and the
half-mythical north. Throughout, German hardihood, chastity, loyalty and love of freedom are held up
as a **moral mirror** to Roman luxury and servility — though Tacitus also notes their drunkenness,
gambling and indolence, and warns that their disunity is Rome's protection.

## The Historical Setting (Birley's Introduction)

Birley frames both works against Rome's long, anxious encounter with the **northern "barbarians"**:
the Gallic sack of Rome (386 BCE) and the proverbial *"Vae victis"*; the Cimbri and Teutones — the
first Germans Rome met — crushed by **Marius** (101 BCE); Caesar's crossing of the Rhine and Channel;
**Augustus's** drive to the Elbe, ended by the destruction of Varus's three legions in the
**[[battle-of-teutoburg-forest-9ce|Teutoburg Forest (9 CE)]]** under **[[arminius|Arminius]]**, after
which the Rhine became the lasting frontier; **[[claudian-conquest-of-britain-43ce|Claudius's invasion
of Britain (43)]]**; the **Boudican revolt (60)**; and the **Batavian Revolt** of 69–70 under Julius
Civilis — the elite Batavian and Tungrian units recurring through both texts and through Agricola's
own Caledonian campaign.

## The Dangerous Afterlife of the *Germania*

The *Germania*'s textual survival was "something of a miracle" — a single ninth-century Hersfeld
manuscript, rediscovered and made public in 1455. Its reception is the darkest of any classical text.
German humanists (Conrad Celtis) seized on it; sixteenth-century writers stressed chapter 2 (the
Germans as a "pure and unmixed race"); **Montesquieu** traced the English separation of powers to "the
forests of ancient Germany"; **Fichte's** *Speeches to the German Nation* invoked it for nationalism.
Then **Gobineau**, **Houston Stewart Chamberlain**, and finally the Nazi race theorist **H. F. K.
Günther** turned it into a charter for "Aryan" supremacy and even (via ch. 12, on the death penalty)
for eliminating "degenerate" elements — so that **Momigliano** could call it "among the hundred most
dangerous books ever written." The *Agricola*, by contrast, fed national histories (Boece, Holinshed,
Camden) and political mottoes (Bacon's mis-quoted *"imperium et libertas"*, revived by Disraeli),
while Calgacus's "desert… peace" became a perennial reproach to empire.
