# The Evolution of Nuclear Strategy (Lawrence Freedman, 1981)

The definitive intellectual history of nuclear strategy from Hiroshima through the late 1970s. Freedman traces how strategists and policymakers attempted to make sense of weapons whose use would be catastrophically destructive, moving through phases of initial shock, monopoly thinking, massive retaliation, limited war theorizing, fear of surprise attack, the search for stable deterrence via arms control and second-strike forces, assured destruction, European nuclear debates, and the retreat from pure MAD toward selective options and renewed war-fighting interest.

Freedman's core observation: the debates have a strongly **cyclical character**. Much presented as profound new insight had been said earlier, often more concisely. "The use of the word 'evolution' ... is somewhat misleading for it suggests progress along a learning curve." The fundamental questions — how, when, and why to use (or threaten) these weapons — remain matters of inference because there has been "the thankful lack of experience of nuclear warfare, since 1945."

## Key Claims and Grounding from Range 01 (Introduction + early chapters)

- The pre-nuclear doctrine of strategic bombardment, developed by Douhet, Trenchard and others, posited that airpower offered an independent route to victory by targeting socioeconomic centers and civilian morale rather than first defeating armed forces; "Airpower can dispense with that intermediate step" (Air Marshal Trenchard, quoted in Freedman 1981: "Strategic Bombardment").
- Stanley Baldwin's 1932 formulation captured the era's pessimism about defence: "Any town which is within reach of an aerodrome can be bombed within the first five minutes of war from the air... the bomber will always get through" (Freedman 1981: "Strategic Bombardment").
- Douhet argued civilian populations were soft targets: "A complete breakdown of the social structure cannot but take place in a country being subjected to ... merciless pounding from the air. The time would soon come when, to put an end to horror and suffering, the people themselves, driven by the instinct of self-preservation, would rise up and demand an end to the war" (Freedman 1981: "The Political Science of Airpower").
- World War II experience largely disappointed pre-war expectations of decisive strategic bombardment; early mutual restraint eroded into the Blitz and Allied city attacks, but results were attritional rather than war-winning; the doctrine survived only because "the dramatic finale of World War II at Hiroshima and Nagasaki rescued the doctrine of strategic bombardment" (Freedman 1981: "The Persistence of the Doctrine of Strategic Bombardment").
- The atom bomb appeared to restore the independent decisiveness of airpower: General Arnold stated in November 1945 that atomic energy "has made air power all important" and that "real security ... will rest on our ability to take immediate offensive action with overwhelming force. It must be apparent to a potential aggressor that an attack on the United States would be followed by an immensely devastating air attack on him" (Freedman 1981: "The Persistence of the Doctrine of Strategic Bombardment").
- In the immediate postwar years the US nuclear stockpile was tiny (Brodie guessed ~20 bombs in 1946) and production slow; informed opinion (including Vannevar Bush) was complacent about Soviet acquisition timelines, expecting no significant Soviet capability until the early-to-mid 1950s (actual first test 1949) (Freedman 1981: "Offence and Defence").
- The atom bomb was seen as a blunt instrument suited only to large targets (cities); as long as scarce, "there would be no point in wasting them against small point targets"; it lowered the "cost of destruction" dramatically (Freedman 1981: "The Persistence of the Doctrine of Strategic Bombardment").
- A deep assumption took hold that any future war would open with a massive surprise atomic attack aimed at socioeconomic paralysis rather than military forces; rooted in Pearl Harbor trauma, this view was reinforced by Manhattan Project scientists who described the bomb as "ideally suited to sudden unannounced attack" and "a weapon of aggression, of surprise, and of terror" (Oppenheimer, quoted in Freedman 1981: "Aggression and Retaliation" / "The Vital First Blow").
- Early deterrence thinking emerged immediately in 1945 as the logic of retaliation: Clement Attlee wrote to Truman that "The only deterrent is the possibility of the victim of such an attack being able to retort to the victor"; General Arnold formulated the enduring line that "our first line of defense is the ability to retaliate even after receiving the hardest blow the enemy can deliver" (Freedman 1981: "Early Thoughts on Deterrence").
- The Jeffries Committee (Manhattan Project scientists, Sept 1944) captured both the hope and pessimism: re-armament could achieve "the certainty that a sudden total devastation of New York or Chicago can be answered the next day by an even more extensive devastation of the cities of the aggressor, and the hope that the fear of such a retaliation will paralyze the aggressor" — yet "the whole history of mankind teaches that this is a very uncertain hope" (Freedman 1981: "Early Thoughts on Deterrence").
- US Air Force thinking framed bombers as a general deterrent "to all forms of aggression, atomic or otherwise"; the State Department Policy Planning Staff saw deterrence as "outstanding importance" in creating "an impression of military strength" to inhibit aggression (Freedman 1981: "Early Thoughts on Deterrence").
- Soviet strategic thought under US atomic monopoly emphasized Stalin's "Permanently Operating Factors" (stability of the rear, army morale, quantity and quality of divisions, armaments, organizational ability of commanders) and treated strategic surprise as a "transitory" factor incapable of compensating for overall inferiority; critics attacked Western "one-sided development" (Douhet airpower or Fuller tanks) that underestimated artillery, infantry and combined arms (Freedman 1981: "Strategy for an Atomic Monopoly" / "The Permanently Operating Factors").

## Key Claims and Grounding from Range 06 (Ch. 15–18 continued: Retreat from City-Avoidance, Assured Destruction, Mutual Assured Destruction, Action-Reaction, Soviet Approach to Deterrence, start of Chinese Connection)

All claims below are verbatim-grounded exclusively in /tmp/freedman_ranges/range06_10501_12500.txt with locators keyed to embedded book pagination (pp. 239–288 range).

- Despite all disclaimers, the logic of McNamara's city-avoidance / counter-force strategy still required the US to be prepared for an all-out attack; "it was never possible to be confident that limitation would be observed." The strategy rested on the assumption that the US would not initiate nuclear exchanges. (Freedman 1981, p. 241).
- The US Air Force embraced counter-force "with enthusiasm, to an extent that dismayed McNamara and his aides." A USAF document argued that "while a potential aggressor might have cause to question the credibility of a strategy that depends on a ‘city-busting’ retaliatory response, he should have no doubts about the credibility of a strategy based on the ability to destroy his remaining military forces and eventually prevail." McNamara told the Air Force it was not to take the doctrine "as a criterion for strategic force proposals". (Freedman 1981, pp. 243–244).
- The Cuban Missile Crisis of October 1962 demonstrated that "in this textbook crisis of the nuclear age, the strategy had proved to be irrelevant." Kennedy "threatened a ‘full retaliatory stroke’"; meanwhile US deployment of B-47s on civilian airfields denied the Soviets a counter-force option. (Freedman 1981, p. 244).
- The formula chosen to emphasize the disastrous nature of general nuclear war was "assured destruction". An assured destruction capability was defined verbatim as: "Deter a deliberate nuclear attack upon the United States or its allies by maintaining at all times a clear and unmistakable ability to inflict an unacceptable degree of damage upon any agressor, or combination of aggressors — even after absorbing a surprise first strike." The levels thought necessary: "33-20 per cent of the Soviet population and 75-50 per cent of industrial capacity." (Freedman 1981, pp. 246–247; "ASSURED DESTRUCTION" section).
- The term "assured destruction" (originally considered "assured retaliation") was chosen for its harshness: "The harshness of the term and the inescapable tragedy described in its definition were intentional." McNamara was concerned that euphemisms ("urban-industrial fatalities" rather than "people killed") allowed the Air Force and supporters to delude themselves "into believing that there was a tolerable way of fighting a nuclear war." (Freedman 1981, p. 247).
- As a planning device, assured destruction demonstrated "that the burst of missile production that marked the start of the Kennedy Administration had created a sufficiency of forces. Consequently, there was no need for further crash programmes or major build-ups of any kind." McNamara used "extremely conservative assumptions, including the worst possible projections of future Soviet forces" to disarm critics. In 1968 an all-out US attack could have destroyed "nearly 50 per cent of the Soviet population and nearly 80 per cent of Soviet industrial capacity, as against the 20 per cent and 50 per cent respectively judged to be adequate". (Freedman 1981, pp. 247–248).
- Doubling the number of 1-megaton warheads from 400 to 800 "would increase the destruction of Soviet population and industry by only 9 per cent and 1 per cent respectively." All that was necessary was forces "sufficient to ride the ‘flat of the curve’". (Freedman 1981, pp. 248).
- The most controversial aspect was "that he refused to be dismayed, in fact refused to hinder, the Soviet attainment of an assured destruction capability." Earlier known as a "stable balance of terror". The acronym MAD was later seized upon by critics. (Freedman 1981, p. 248).
- McNamara sought to demonstrate lack of interest in first strikes by phasing out weapons such as European IRBMs "which would only be of use in a first strike". There was "no reason to believe that offensive forces by themselves could knock out the offensive forces of the adversary." (Freedman 1981, p. 249).
- On ballistic missile defence and civil defence (fallout shelters): by the mid-1960s there was "evident decline in the interest shown in these alternatives". "Whatever one did to improve the defence, the offence would soon regain its position with a much more modest effort." Jerome Wiesner and Herbert York warned in 1964 that an effective ABM "would effectively nullify the deterrent force of the other, exposing the latter to a first attack against which it could not retaliate." They concluded there was "no technical solution to the problems of the vulnerability of the US (or the USSR) to nuclear attack". (Freedman 1981, pp. 250–253).
- In his September 1967 speech McNamara articulated the "action-reaction phenomenon": "Whatever their intentions or our intentions, actions — or even realistically potential actions — on either side relating to the build-up of nuclear forces necessarily trigger reactions on the other side. It is precisely this action-reaction phenomenon that fuels an arms race." He noted that misreadings of the early-1960s US build-up as seeking first-strike capability had led both sides to exceed second-strike levels. The formula for stabilizing: "‘Offence good, defence bad; killing cities good, killing missiles bad.’" (Freedman 1981, pp. 254–256).
- Soviet doctrine under Khrushchev amounted to a "second-best strategic posture" and a "‘minimum deterrent’". Khrushchev justified cuts in manpower and bombers by the increase in firepower from nuclear weapons and missiles: "Military aviation is now being almost entirely replaced by missiles." The military response was to qualify this gradually and insist on "combined arms" and retention of all service branches "in relevant and sound proportions". (Freedman 1981, pp. 262–264).
- The 1962 Sokolovsky volume *Military Strategy* (first major Soviet work on strategy since the 1930s) was a compromise document that "avoided controversy and read like a compromise document. It offered no clear priorities for the design of Soviet forces while providing support for claims for every type of force." It stressed "primary attention" to "the study of how a future war may break out" including "detailed study" of "methods of delivering the first blow". The initial phase was "fierce and destructive" and would "predetermine the development and outcome of the entire war". (Freedman 1981, pp. 264, 267).
- Soviet planners adopted a policy of "launch-on-warning". Marshal Krylov (1967): "With the presence in the armament of troops of launchers and missiles which are completely ready for operation, as well as systems for detecting enemy missile launchers ... an aggressor is no longer able suddenly to destroy the missiles before their launch ... They will have time during the flight of the missiles of the aggressor to leave their launchers and inflict a retaliatory strike against the enemy." (Freedman 1981, pp. 268–269).
- The Chinese remained "most consistent of all in their views on nuclear strategy." Mao in 1946: "The atom bomb is a paper tiger with which the American reactionaries try to terrify the people. It looks terrible, but in fact is not. Of course, the atom bomb is a weapon of mass annihilation: the outcome of a war is decided by the people, not by one or two new weapons." People's War required drawing the enemy in, using guerrilla tactics and mass mobilization; "The enemy advances; we retreat. The enemy halts; we harass. The enemy retreats; we pursue." (Freedman 1981, pp. 274–275).
- During the 1958 Quemoy crisis the Chinese tested the extent of Soviet support and found it limited and conditional. Khrushchev's eventual warning was heavily qualified. The experience reinforced Chinese determination to reduce dependence on the USSR. (Freedman 1981, pp. 279–280).
- The Chinese detonated an atomic bomb in October 1964 and a hydrogen bomb two and a half years later. This produced "no evident change in general attitudes on nuclear weapons." The paper tiger theme was played down but there was "no deviation from the previous beliefs in the limited utility of nuclear weapons." (Freedman 1981, p. 280).
- The slice ends as Freedman begins discussion of the European dimension and NATO's turn toward a "flexible and balanced range of appropriate responses, conventional and nuclear" (MC 14/3, 1967), noting that the new strategy "did not result in the major increases in the defence expenditure of America’s European allies that it implied." (Freedman 1981, pp. 285–288).

## Key Claims and Grounding from Range 08 (Ch. 22–26: Military-Industrial Complexities, Consensus Undermined, Soviet Build-Up, Parity, Selective Options / Schlesinger Doctrine, ICBM Vulnerability, Political Science of Nuclear Strategy, and the book's Conclusion)

All claims below are verbatim-grounded exclusively in /tmp/freedman_ranges/range08_14501_17600.txt. Locators use embedded book page numbers (e.g. p.337) and section titles as they appear in the text. This range covers the final doctrinal shifts of the 1970s and Freedman's overall verdict.

- In the 1969 Safeguard ABM debate, nuclear scientists testified before Congress: "We conclude that the system is unlikely to perform to specifications in the event of a nuclear attack,... is highly susceptible to penetration, ... is not well adapted to perform the missions assigned to it by either the previous Administration or the present one" and "deployment of Sentinel/Safeguard now would probably start a new round in the arms race, and would seriously impede the conclusion of an arms control agreement." The Senate divided evenly. (Freedman 1981, MILITARY-INDUSTRIAL COMPLEXITIES, p.337).
- The view that the US was the source of innovation triggering arms rounds led to "we are running an arms race with ourselves" (Jerome Wiesner). Herbert York attacked the "deep seated belief that the only appropriate response to any new technical development on the other side is further complexity of our own". (Freedman 1981, p.338).
- A powerful coalition of scientists/engineers, contractors, Congressmen in production districts, and the military biased force planning against restraint. "Controlling the arms race meant controlling the ‘military-industrial complex’." (Freedman 1981, p.339).
- The politicization endowed MAD with "canonical status". Jerome Kahan's three principles of "stable deterrence": maintaining "a confident retaliatory deterrent posture"; reducing "the relative reliance on nuclear power in US defense policy"; and "avoiding weapons and doctrines that pose a threat to the USSR’s deterrent and seeking security through negotiated arms limitations". (Freedman 1981, p.340).
- Nixon/Kissinger adopted "sufficiency" (vague and elastic). Nixon (Feb 1971): In narrow military sense, "enough force to inflict a level of damage on a potential aggressor sufficient to deter him from attacking"; in broader political sense, forces "adequate to prevent us and our allies from being coerced" so ability and resolve "will not be underestimated". (Freedman 1981, p.341-342).
- Laird promoted the "triad" (ICBMs, SLBMs, bombers) for "mutually supporting deterrent capability" and "synergism". Schlesinger later observed: "To some extent I think the rationale of the Triad was a rationalization". (Freedman 1981, p.342-343).
- SALT and expert proliferation (scientists, officials, Congress staffs, institutes) meant "the strategic debates of the 1970s in the United States could not be restricted to a qualified few". Result: "undermine the established liberal consensus, but not to present a new consensus". (Freedman 1981, THE CONSENSUS UNDERMINED, p.344-345).
- By early 1970s Soviets had numerical superiority in ICBMs and SLBMs; US maintained larger bomber force and lead in missile quality. Soviets concentrated on large ICBMs; did not phase out older ones. (Freedman 1981, p.346).
- Wohlstetter's articles attacked arms race "legends": US strategic costs/destructiveness had declined; technology could be benign; no evidence of "invariable" overestimation of Soviet forces — if anything underestimation. Better model: "competitive rather than racing" between powers with "partially, but sometimes intensely opposed aims". (Freedman 1981, p.347).
- Colin Gray: "Arms race behaviour... is really only normal Great Power behaviour somewhat accentuated." (Freedman 1981, p.348).
- Donald Brennan on MAD = mutual assured vulnerability: "We should rather prefer live Americans to dead Russians, and we should not choose deliberately to live forever under a nuclear sword of Damocles." (Freedman 1981, p.348-349).
- Fred Ikle (Foreign Affairs): "The jargon of American strategic analysis works like a narcotic. It dulls our sense of moral outrage about the tragic confrontation of nuclear arsenals... It fosters the current smug complacency... It blinds us to the fact that our method for preventing nuclear war rests on a form of warfare universally condemned since the Dark Ages - the mass killing of hostages." Threat not credible for non-massive attacks; neglects "lunacy and recklessness"; "fatal accident or unauthorized act". (Freedman 1981, p.349-350).
- Herbert York and Wolfgang Panofsky responded that mutual assured destruction was a "tension of modern life" / "physical fact" / "grossly insensitive to any change in strategic policy" that would have to be endured. (Freedman 1981, p.350-351).
- Guidance improvements: accuracies from CEPs of "half of a mile to under a sixth of a mile". MIRVed systems (Poseidon ~10x 60kt; Minuteman III 3x ~170kt at quarter-mile accuracy) created hard-target potential, reversing unfavorable exchange rates for silo attacks. (Freedman 1981, p.352-353).
- ICBMs "took on the attributes of first-strike weapons for which bombers had been criticized in the late 1950s." They remained vulnerable in silos yet threatened enemy forces once launched. SLBMs stayed "the only pure second-strike weapon". (Freedman 1981, p.354).
- SALT I (1972): ABM Treaty limited to 200 launchers (later 100); Interim Agreement froze launchers with Soviet numerical edge (1409 ICBMs / 950 SLBMs vs US 1054/656) traded for US tech/bomber lead; Jackson Amendment demanded future parity. Vladivostok framework (2400 SNDVs, 1320 MIRVed); SALT II (2250 SNDVs) never ratified after Afghanistan. (Freedman 1981, p.356-358).
- Schlesinger's four requirements for credible deterrence: (1) survivable assured destruction force; (2) "essential equivalence" in factors affecting effectiveness and perceptions; (3) "force that, in response to Soviet actions, could implement a variety of limited pre-planned options and react rapidly to retargeting"; (4) capabilities so "everyone — friend, foe, and domestic audiences alike — will perceive that we are the equal of our strongest competitors". (Freedman 1981, SELECTIVE OPTIONS / 24 Parity, p.359).
- William Van Cleave and Roger Barnett: "deterrence may fail .. . and the weapons may have to be used"; "capability to use nuclear forces in a rational and non-apocalyptic fashion" strengthens credibility. (Freedman 1981, p.373).
- Albert Wohlstetter: "We cannot assure that a nuclear war will never occur simply by repeating that it would be an unlimited catastrophe. And we cannot eliminate the possibility of nuclear war simply by assuring that if it occurs it will be an unlimited catastrophe." (Freedman 1981, p.373).
- Soviet doctrine (e.g. Sidorenko, The Offensive 1970): "In the case of their employment, nuclear weapons will become the main means for destroying the enemy in battle." Initial nuclear use would mark dramatic shift; stress on victory over collateral limitation or negotiation pauses. (Freedman 1981, p.381, 383).
- Schlesinger frequently cited attack on Western Europe as scenario; emphasized "coupling" US nuclear forces to NATO defence and distinguished first use (to fulfil obligations) from surprise first strike. Targets: airfields, submarine bases, oil fields, rail yards — militarily relevant, low collateral. (Freedman 1981, OPTIONS FOR A EUROPEAN WAR, p.383-384).
- 1979 NATO decision: deploy 572 American medium-range ballistic and cruise missiles on European soil. "The feature of the decision that aroused the most satisfaction was that it had been made at all"; ground-based to enhance credibility of activation without suggesting they could satisfy all needs without US central systems. (Freedman 1981, p.387).
- Dominant late-1970s scenario: Soviet ICBM attack on US silos eliminates Minuteman while leaving Soviet reserve; US President left only "annihilate Russian cities" option that Russians might conclude "no American President would order". Key question became "escalation dominance". (Freedman 1981, ICBM VULNERABILITY, p.388-389).
- Colin Gray: "On current trends in the strategic balance, an American President should, prudently, be deterred from initiating strategic nuclear employment; should he proceed nonetheless, the war would very likely terminate after an almost wholly counter-military exchange (which the Soviet Union should win unequivocally)..." (Freedman 1981, p.389).
- Schlesinger on Soviet counter-force growth: "could bring into question our ability to respond to attacks in a controlled, selective, and deliberate fashion. It could also give the Soviets a capability that we ourselves would lack... Worst of all, it could arouse precisely the fears and suspicions that our arms control efforts are designed to dispel." (Freedman 1981, p.392).
- MX ICBM pursued as remedy to restore counter-force options and survivability. (Freedman 1981, p.392).
- PD 59 (July 1980, Carter): improve deterrence by capacity for "prolonged but limited nuclear war". Prime targets: "the Soviet leadership values most - its military forces and its own ability to maintain control after a war starts". Secure strategic reserve of invulnerable missiles; flexibility for retargeting via surveillance. (Freedman 1981, THE POLITICAL SCIENCE OF NUCLEAR STRATEGY, p.393-394).
- Colin Gray and Keith Payne: USSR "highly vulnerable" due to overcentralization; "The Soviet Union might cease to function if its security agency, the KGB were severely crippled. If the Moscow bureaucracy could be eliminated, damaged, or isolated, the USSR might disintegrate into anarchy." (Freedman 1981, p.394-395).
- Freedman verdict: attempts to use precision low-yield weapons for limited nuclear war face "difficulties... in establishing rules for meticulous control and self-restraint, capable of comprehension and obedience in the most exceptional and fraught of circumstances." "It is doubtful that there has been any significant success in breaking the popular association between any nuclear use and utter catastrophe." (Freedman 1981, CONCLUSION, p.396-397).
- "The essence of the problem is the difficulty of attaching any rationality whatsoever to the initiation of a chain of events that could well end in the utter devastation of one’s own society." "Any strategy that requires the threat of first use of nuclear weapons suffers from incredibility. Even threats confined to retaliation... suffer from this incredibility." (Freedman 1981, p.397).
- "We do not know how political leaders will react to the news of even only a few nuclear weapons exploding on their territory. The response could be a reckless fury, lethargic submission, craven cowardice, or a firm and resolute action." Uncertainties about Soviet designs on Europe and whether current means suffice. (Freedman 1981, p.398-399).
- Since 1945 Europe at peace: "nuclear deterrence may be a viable policy even if it is not credible"; "the fear of the whole process getting out of control" is "the strongest source of caution". "The Emperor Deterrence may have no clothes, but he is still Emperor." (Freedman 1981, p.399).
- "At the end of 35 years of attempts at constructing nuclear strategies one is forced to the conclusion that there has been a move to the analysis of second- and third-order issues." "stability depends on something that is more the antithesis of strategy than its apotheosis - on threats that things will get out of hand, that we might act irrationally, that possibly through inadvertence we could set in motion a process that in its development and conclusion would be beyond human control and comprehension." (Freedman 1981, CONCLUSION, p.400).
- Closing: "Those who have responsibility for unleashing nuclear arsenals live by the motto that if they ever had to do so they would have failed. Remarkably, up to now they have succeeded. C’est magnifique, mais ce n’est pas la stratégie." (Freedman 1981, p.400).

## Historiography Note

Freedman writes as both participant-observer (IISS/Chatham House insider) and historian of ideas. He treats official doctrine (NSC-68, Massive Retaliation, SIOP variants, MC 14/3) and civilian theorizing with equal seriousness, showing how they cross-pollinated. He is critical of over-abstract modelling that loses sight of politics and of the persistent temptation to treat nuclear weapons as usable instruments of policy rather than existential threats. The book is stronger on Anglo-American debates than on Soviet internal documents (many still classified at time of writing); Chinese coverage is necessarily thinner. Collection strength on nuclear strategy is now excellent via this volume + Heuser (2010), the 2013 Freedman general history, Leffler–Westad Cold War volumes, and related strategy sources.

## Volume Synthesis Note (post-ingest)

Freedman's final synthesis (Range 08 / Conclusion) is that after 35 years, nuclear strategy has largely moved to second- and third-order technical and perceptual issues while the core problem — attaching credible rationality to the use or threat of weapons whose employment risks uncontrollable destruction of one's own society — remains unsolved. Three broad responses (exploit destructiveness to make war folly; deny benefits via defence or disarming strike; tame via precision limited options) have all been tried; none yielded decisive advantage or reliable control. Stability since 1945 rests less on rational, controllable threats than on the shared fear that "things will get out of hand." The book ends on a note of qualified relief ("C’est magnifique, mais ce n’est pas la stratégie") rather than endorsement of any doctrinal endpoint. This range completes the source's coverage of the intellectual history through the late Cold War doctrinal shifts (Safeguard/MIC critiques, Soviet build-up, parity anxieties, Schlesinger/PD59 selective options, ICBM vulnerability scenarios, European theatre adaptations) and supplies the source's authoritative verdict on the limits of nuclear strategy as an enterprise.
