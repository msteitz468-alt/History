# DeVries, *Infantry Warfare in the Early Fourteenth Century: Discipline, Tactics, and Technology* (1996)

Kelly DeVries's study argues that the early fourteenth century witnessed a widespread and consistent revival of effective infantry tactics across Europe. In nineteen battles from Courtrai (1302) to Crécy (1346), infantry forces — often smaller, communal or national levies fighting on foot — secured victory through disciplined defensive stands in close-order formations, exploiting terrain to neutralize cavalry charges. Technology (including the longbow) was secondary to cohesion, leadership, and the decision to fight on foot. The English adoption of dismounted tactics after Bannockburn is a direct legacy.

## DeVries's Core Thesis (grounded)

- Infantry (communal, national, or levied) won most of the studied battles through **solid, disciplined defensive lines** rather than offensive charges or missile superiority.
- Troops fought "primarily, and in most cases entirely, on foot." Cavalry, when present, frequently dismounted (Flemish at Courtrai; English after learning from Scots).
- Terrain (marshes, ditches, hills, pits) repeatedly canalised and neutralised cavalry mobility.
- Uniformity remarkable across Flanders, Scotland, Swiss, Greek, and English contexts.
- Defeats of infantry armies (Mons-en-Pévèle, Cassel) often resulted when the foot abandoned the defensive stand and charged.
- Technology secondary: "victory came not because of superior technology, even when the longbow was used, but due to a solid and disciplined infantry line making a defensive stand."

## Range 05 Extraction: Boroughbridge (1322) and Cassel (1328) (lines ~22001-26500 of source)

**Source context (DeVries range 05):** This range covers the Boroughbridge and Cassel chapters. Focus on English adoption of dismounted "Scottish" schiltrom tactics in a successful defensive action against rebels at Boroughbridge (positive model accelerating the shift away from Bannockburn-style cavalry reliance) and the Flemish defeat at Cassel when they abandoned a strong defensive hill posture under French harassment (artillery + burning of lands), providing a clear counter-example to Courtrai-style infantry successes. Includes chronicle details, source criticism, and explicit links to the broader infantry tactics thesis and English tactical evolution. Verbatim quotes grounded exclusively in this range.

### Boroughbridge (1322)
- Harclay's force (northern infantry, "almost all of these troops were infantry soldiers armed with spears or pikes"; knights dismounted) blocks bridge and ford over the Ouse. "Sending his horse and those of his men to the rear, he ordered all his knights on foot and some pikemen at the northern end of the bridge, and opposite the ford or passage of water, he ordered other pikemen in a schiltrom, according to the manner of the Scots, to oppose the knights and the horses on which the enemy sat." (Chronicon de Lanercost via DeVries range 05)
- Hereford's bridge assault met by pikes "thrust at the earl from all sides. And he fell immediately and was killed on the bridge with his standard-bearer and [other] knights." Lancaster's ford attack slowed by archery and density; rebels retreat after Hereford's death. "This was a battle won by infantry. Harclay's infantry formation... was the determining factor in his victory over the more 'knightly' cavalry force..." (DeVries range 05)
- Outcome: ends Lancaster rebellion. Explicitly tied to English tactical change: "Never again would an English army fight as it had at Bannockburn. From this time until the end of the Middle Ages the chief tactic of the English was to dismount most of the cavalry and to order them in a solid infantry formation—in a line or, if warranted by geography, as at the ford at Boroughbridge, in a schiltrom. This once powerful and proud cavalry-based army had become little more than, as John E. Morris termed it, 'mounted infantry.'" "(DeVries range 05)"
- Positive example (alongside negative Bannockburn) drove adoption: "perhaps it is better to recognize the influence of this positive image of infantry warfare rather than the negative one received by the English at their defeat at Bannockburn..." (DeVries range 05)

### Cassel (1328)
- Flemish rebels hold defensive hillside position (easy to defend like ditches or marsh; cavalry charge would be disorganized). French under Philip VI (large army with oriflamme) camp below and refuse uphill assault, using artillery and burning countryside/monastery to harass and provoke. Flemings, impatient and discouraged after days of inaction and devastation, charge down the hill hoping to surprise or capture the king. "The Flemings had wisely selected terrain which was easy to defend. A hillside... meant that a cavalry charge would be disorganized and fatiguing, allowing a solid Flemish infantry line to withstand easily the mounted shock combat of the knights." (DeVries range 05)
- Result: French victory. Flemings suffer heavy losses (contemporary claims high; modern estimates ~50% or 7–8k range in broader context); French casualties light. "At the end of this day, the French were victorious. Few rebels escaped the slaughter." (DeVries range 05)
- Explicit contrast to Courtrai successes: infantry loses when abandoning strong defensive posture. "unlike the battle of Courtrai and Mons-en-Pévèle, few contemporary descriptions of the battle of Cassel favor the Flemings or mourn their defeat." "it is still possible to reconstruct what occurred at the battle of Cassel, and to understand why the Flemish infantry lost to the French cavalry there." (DeVries range 05)

## Range 07 Extraction (Laupen 1339 and Morlaix 1342)

**Source context (DeVries range 07):** This range covers the Laupen chapter (X) and Morlaix (XI) in detail, with primary chronicle grounding, tactical reconstruction, and explicit ties to the infantry revival thesis, Bannockburn-derived English learning, and precursors to the mature HYW system at Crécy.

### Laupen, 1339 (Bernese/Swiss infantry vs. noble coalition)
- **Date/scale:** 21 June 1339 (siege of Laupen begun 10 June); small-scale open-field battle (Bernese ~6,000 infantry total incl. 1,000–1,200 from the three forest cantons; opponents fielded large noble cavalry + infantry, chronicler numbers exaggerated at 16k–30k).
- **Actors/region:** Bernese (pro-Confederation, anti-Habsburg lean) under knight Rudolf von Erlach (per Justinger) vs. Freiburg + Kiburg allies (counts of Kiburg, Neuchâtel, Gruyères, Nidau, Valengin, Aarburg, lord of Vaud + Savoy ties; some Austrians noted). Western Europe / Swiss frontier of Empire.
- **Narrative (grounded):** Freiburgers besieged Laupen (formerly their holding, now Bernese garrison of 600) "with catapults brought on carts by horses." Bernese mustered infantry army, marched to relieve. "The Bernese force marched to Laupen, but when they saw 'that the reported large size of their enemy was valid,' the leaders decided not to attack the Freiburgers while they were camped in the steep and forested terrain around the town. Instead, they determined to order their troops, all infantry, as 'a small wedge'—the Conflictus Laupensis uses the term 'parvus cuneus'—with troops facing out on all sides on top of a small hill near to the town." (DeVries range 07)
- **Engagement:** "This was after all the goal of the Freiburgers, and they quickly prepared for the fight. Their 'new knights' were particularly enthused by the prospect of engaging the Bernese in warfare: they waved their swords in the air in excitement and raced to attack their opponents." Some Bernese fled (~2,000 per Conflictus), but remainder stood. "The Freiburg cavalry reached the Bernese formation first. The Conflictus recounts that they initially rode 'frightfully' around the Bernese formation, waited for an infantry assault to soften the target, and then attacked 'fiercely' the Bernese line themselves. The Bernese reacted by standing solidly in formation against the attacks." Quote: "The Bernese, bursting the chains of all their fear, like Samson, received the assault of the Freiburgers. And they took all of their Standards and slew the standard-bearers and many others, all infantry, and the rest they put to a pitiable flight. When those who were circling with their horses charged to their aid, the Bernese without delay either killed all of them or put them to flight." (Conflictus Laupensis, p. 309; DeVries range 07)
- **Outcome:** Large numbers of Freiburgers killed (counts of Nidau and Valengin, lord of Vaud); booty 27 banners + 80 crowned helmets. Bernese losses 1,000–1,500 (Jean de Winterthur 1,000; Conflictus 1,500). No prolonged pursuit; returned to Laupen/Bern rejoicing. "In comparison with other early fourteenth-century battles the Swiss conflict at Laupen ... was quite small. ... Yet the tactics used by the Bernese infantry and the outcome they caused against the Freiburger cavalry were very similar to almost all of these other battles." (DeVries range 07)
- **Tactical analysis (per DeVries):** Bernese chose battlefield (hill), solid wedge defensive formation facing outward; cavalry charges uphill into spear-wielding infantry failed. "the infantry stood so solidly at Laupen that one must wonder if their formation could ever have been defeated by simply charging into it." Contrast to Cassel where French drew Flemings out. Freiburg leadership "exulted in their chivalric numbers and saw no need for any other strategy except for an immediate attack". (DeVries range 07)
- **Historiography/grounding:** Primary: Conflictus Laupensis (Bernese, written shortly after in Bern; "one of the very few medieval military narratives written within a year or two"); supported independently by Jean de Winterthur (c.1347), Heinrich von Diessenhoven (c.1361), Cronica de Berno (1405), Conrad Justinger (c.1421). Delbrück uses it but disputes numbers. All sources Bernese-leaning; no Freiburg counter-narrative survives in detail.
- **Links to thesis:** Exemplifies "anti-Habsburg, pro-independent Swiss military force" victory via infantry solidity. Feeds [[hubs/warfare/late-middle-ages/rise-of-the-swiss]] (open-field confirmation after Morgarten ambush). "The trend had continued." (DeVries range 07)

### Morlaix, 1342 (English developing system in HYW context)
- **Context (HYW precursors / Bannockburn learning):** Post-Esplechin truce (1340) and "Crisis of 1341," Breton succession crisis (Montfort vs. Blois/Charles of Blois backed by Philip VI) drew English intervention. "the battle of Morlaix is less important for the history of the Hundred Years War than for the history of military tactics during the first half of the fifteenth century." Explicit continuity: "the similarity of the tactics at Morlaix and those at other battles fought in the beginning of the fourteenth century demonstrates the validity of the narrative here. It also indicates that the battlefield tactical practice of the English, generalship for lack of a better word, was by 1342 known and exercised at every military engagement. As at Boroughbridge, Dupplin Moor, and Halidon Hill, the main tactic of the English troops was to form a solid line or mass of infantry, with even the knights dismounted, and then to force their opponents to charge into it." (DeVries range 07)
- **Forces:** English under William of Bohun, earl of Northampton (with Derby, Oxford, Robert of Artois etc.), landed Brest 18 Aug 1342, >3,000 (chroniclers note mostly archers in Mauny/Northampton forces); "Northampton's army necessitated transport by 14 large ships and many small ones." French/Blois: "perhaps to as many as 15,000" (Burne); Knighton 20,000; Murimuth 3,000 knights + 1,500 "Janissaries" + "populari" without number; "a huge army" (Baker). Battle 30 Sept 1342 near Lanmeur, 7 mi from Morlaix.
- **English preparation:** "in the complete darkness of night moved to meet their enemy." "And in the morning they took their position about one league from the enemy in front of a wood, and they dug pits and ditches around them and covered them over with hay and grass. And after the sun rose, they prepared themselves for battle." "Part of this preparation, according to Adam Murimuth, was to dismount the knights and have all the English soldiers fight in one line as infantry." (Henry Knighton p.25; Adam Murimuth pp.127,227; DeVries range 07)
- **Course:** Blois arrayed in three lines (Knighton); first (Geoffrey Charnys/Charny + many knights) "Immediately conquered ... and sent into flight." Second charge: "Seeing the small number of English soldiers, they were taken by an aggressive spirit and undertook to overwhelm the English troops. ... And wishing to trample the infantry under the feet of their very powerful horses, they charged dangerously at them." But "having been drawn into a narrow cave ... they fell one on top of the other into the concealed pits which the enemy had made." French fled; >50 knights + many populari slain; Geoffrey Charnys + others captured. English "retreated, 'on account of a [continuing] fear of the large numbers of enemy,'" did not pursue; returned to (unsuccessful) siege of Morlaix. (DeVries range 07)
- **Archers at Morlaix:** Present in force ("all chroniclers who mention the numbers in both Mauny's and Northampton's armies indicate a force that included mostly archers"), but "not positioned as at these other battles [Dupplin Moor, Halidon Hill, Crécy]." Integrated into the infantry line using other weapons "because with his small numbers of troops, he would have made his infantry line so thin that the French charges would probably have been successful." (DeVries range 07)
- **Significance for HYW precursors / system development:** "In this instance, the French chose to make cavalry charges, either two or three of them, and each charge met with failure as it was unable to penetrate the English infantry line or to cause it to rout." Direct link to Bannockburn learning curve (post-Dupplin/Halidon adoption of dismounted tactics + terrain traps). "the English had won the battle." Morlaix demonstrates the tactics "known and exercised at every military engagement" by 1342. Feeds [[hubs/warfare/late-middle-ages/hundred-years-war]] and [[events/hundred-years-war-1337]] as early application; precursor to Crécy maturation. "Despite Geoffrey le Baker's contention that the soldiers on both sides fought better than their counterparts at Halidon Hill, Crécy, or Poitiers, very few modern accounts of the battle exist."
- **Sources (DeVries range 07):** Geoffrey le Baker (most descriptive), Henry Knighton, Adam Murimuth (English chroniclers of varying length); one short French: Cuvelier, Chronique de Bertrand Guesclin (1380-87). No contemporary Breton narratives; Jean le Bel, Froissart, Grandes Chroniques silent. "There is perhaps no battle fought between the forces of England and France during the Hundred Years War that received less comment than did this one."

## Range 08 Extraction: Staveren (1345), Vottem (1346), and Crécy opening (1346) (lines ~35501-39500 of source)

**Source context (DeVries range 08):** This range covers Ch. XII "The Battles of Staveren, 1345, and Vottem, 1346" (Low Countries rebellions) and the opening of Ch. XIII "The Battle of Crécy, 1346" (Edward III's 1345-46 campaigns and army details). Provides "further cases of defensive infantry success" confirming the uniformity of infantry tactics 1302–1347 (solid, disciplined, "tightly bunched" lines on chosen/fortified ground defeating cavalry-based hosts). Staveren/Vottem parallel Courtrai/Bannockburn patterns (ditches for concealment/hindrance, cohesion vs. disordered charges, pride as factor). Transitions to Crécy as "matured" English application (large infantry/archer force, chevauchée-to-prepared-stand, ditches/pits/wood experiments from Morlaix onward now with longbow + dismount + contract army). Feeds source page thesis/Section Plan (XII/XIII), Crécy hub (strategic context, OOB, mechanisms), HYW/infantry pattern hubs, and Bannockburn comparanda. All verbatim-grounded from range only.

### Staveren (1345)
- Frisians (rebel infantry) vs. William, count of Hainault/Holland/Zeeland (cavalry-based). William crosses Zuider Zee; Frisians had fortified (ditches/embankments) but "abandoned their fortified positions and ordered a defensive infantry line in front of them" upon seeing the attackers' difficulty. William's force "charged into this solid infantry formation in disarray." Frisians used "'very large and heavy pikes, their long swords and axes and falchions,' killing all they faced 'without pity and without mercy.'" Lack of numbers proved the attackers' downfall; "Most were left dead on the battlefield." (Direct from range (DeVries range 08))
- Tactical/historiography note: Limited contemporary tactical detail; only Récits and Froissart's third redaction discuss battlefield tactics. "But it is only in the Récits and Froissart's third redaction that a discussion of tactics on the battlefield can be found." (DeVries range 08)

### Vottem (1346)
- Liège rebels (infantry, "standing well armed and tightly bunched to resist the lords") vs. archbishop's (cavalry-based) force. Rebels chose site outside Liège "because it lay on the main road... the route which would have to be taken by the archbishop." Positioned "two days before" with "large ditches, not ones like those at Courtrai, made to disrupt a cavalry charge, but like those at Staveren, made to hide in" (dual purpose: conceal + hinder if lost). Archbishop "ordered his cavalry in three lines and began to charge" but "proceeded incautiously" with pride ("They did not doubt that they would obtain victory... charged with impetus"). Charge "became disordered"; "As at Staveren, the rebels prevailed." (Direct quotes (DeVries range 08))
- More chronicle coverage (Jean le Bel, Jean d'Outremeuse, Récits, Saint-Trond chronicle, etc.).

### Crécy opening (1346) and English maturation
- Edward III's main force (April 1346 order from Portsmouth; ~15,000, "most of whom were infantry or archers"; 700–1,000 ships needed). Multiple English armies operated with "much success" in Brittany/Gascony; French king "chose not to attack the smaller English armies, but instead to do battle with the main force at Crécy-en-Ponthieu." Campaign: chevauchée "through the countryside, pillaging and devastating all the land." (Direct (DeVries range 08))
- English system context (building on Morlaix 1342): At Morlaix, troops "with their backs against a dense wood... dug ditches and pits along the flanks of the infantry line and across the battlefield." "As the Scots, Flemings, and Swiss all without the benefit of skilled longbowmen had found, this tactic was able to narrow and confuse the charge..." Knighton: French drawn into "'antrum obturatum,' a 'narrow cave'" of defeat. Now matured at Crécy with longbow + dismount + prepared ground + paid/contract force. (Direct mechanism quotes (DeVries range 08))
- Broader pattern: Contemporaneous "at Staveren in 1345 and at Vottem in 1346, the rebellious armies, infantry forces, defeated their cavalry-based opponents" via solid lines/ditches/cohesion. Supports "uniformity in tactics" and "infantry revival." (Direct (DeVries range 08))

## Range 02 Extraction: Arques (1303) and Mons-en-Pévèle (1304) (lines ~7501-11500 of source)

**Source context (DeVries range 02):** This range covers the Arques and Mons-en-Pévèle chapters (II–III). Focus on post-Courtrai Flemish operations showing continuity and evolution of infantry tactics (dismounted close-order with goededags/pikes; defensive stands), as well as limits (force division, loose order vulnerability, inability to convert tactical success, wagon fort evolution, heat/fatigue effects). Includes detailed conflicting source accounts, ambiguous outcomes, and how these battles illustrate why prepared defensive infantry often prevailed but could be defeated when abandoning the stand or poorly ordered. Feeds source page, courtrai-war hub/event (continuation of the conflict), broader infantry revival in hubs/warfare. Verbatim quotes grounded exclusively in this range.

### Battle of Arques (1303)
- William of Jülich divided the Flemish force into three contingents near St. Omer: Yprois (red insignia) closest to town, St. Winoksbergen in middle with supply train, main (Furnes/Cassel, William leading) in rear. "The first division... ordered closest to the town. The second... ‘a good league distant’... And the third contingent, the largest Flemish force... camped in the rear." (DeVries range 02)
- Yprois routed ~60 French bidauts at Arques. French garrison (frightened by Courtrai memory) marched out; cavalry bypassed the formed Yprois "grosse bataille" and shattered the unprepared middle contingent ("unprepared for battle and in loose order").
- Main Flemish force dismounted and formed a circular defensive "circle like a bowl or a crown" with goededags and pikes forward (explicitly "not a line, as the Flemings had formed at Courtrai"). "It was an impenetrable defensive formation which did not necessitate the use of terrain to be effective." (DeVries range 02)
- Conflicting accounts: Annales Gandenses describes prolonged circling with little penetration and French withdrawal; other sources (Guiart eyewitness) describe repeated charges in four lines that pushed Flemings back but failed to break them, followed by orderly French retreat under pursuit.
- Outcome ambiguous: French forced from field but Flemings suffered heavy losses (esp. middle contingent), could not assault St. Omer, did not hold the field. Casualty figures wildly exaggerated (e.g. 15,000 total per one source). William later criticized for dividing forces; Annales attributes his later misfortunes to divine judgment post-Courtrai.
- Tactical note: Post-Courtrai French reluctance to frontal assault on formed Flemish foot; preferred flanking/circling. "Post bellum enim Curtracense numquam Franci ausi sunt... Flamingos semper pedes pugnantes insimul cum tota acie impugnare, sed semper eos circumequitantes..." (Annales Gandenses via DeVries range 02)

### Battle of Mons-en-Pévèle (1304)
- After Arques, Flemings raided but faced French main army (Philip the Fair with oriflamme) plus prior defeat at Zierkzee. Modern est. French ~3,000 knights + 10,000 infantry (contemporary claims >100,000); artillery (trebuchets etc.) destroyed by Flemings early. Flemings ~12,500–15,000, all on foot with goededags/lances, high morale ("stimulated rather than dejected").
- Flemings formed "a defensive formation similar to that which had been successful at Courtrai, but with the addition of a quasi-fortification made with their wagons and carts at their rear and on their flanks" (wheels removed to prevent encirclement). "There also seems to have been some marshes and ditches... the marshes and ditches had little effect on the outcome." (DeVries range 02)
- Peace parliament failed (contradictory accounts of who sought it and French movements during talks). Battle 18 Aug.
- Course: French opened with fierce cavalry charges into the line. Flemings initially held and prevailed frontally in prolonged fighting; French flanks reached the wagon fort and sacked camp but were repelled. Heat/fatigue hit French harder (heavy armor, some died "through the weight of their armor and the summer heat").
- Decisive phase: Flemings attacked the French camp, nearly killing Philip (bodyguard mostly slain, oriflamme destroyed, king stunned on ground). Most sources: Philip rallied and led counterattack (mace in one account); Flemings broke and fled in rout, pursued into night. Annales Gandenses divergent: Philip "forced and compelled" to flee; Flemings plundered, celebrated on field, withdrew mainly for lack of food (watched French recover dead by torchlight).
- Outcome: High casualties both sides (modern est. Flemish 7–8,000 incl. William of Jülich). Effective end of the 1302–1305 Flemish independence movement; treaties of Marquette (1304) and Athis-sur-Orge (1305) followed.
- Tactical note: Direct Courtrai-style line + wagon fort evolution; limits shown when defenders attack or force divided. "Only at ... Mons-en-Pévèle ... was it an infantry charge against infantry opponents which was the climactic point... the defensive infantry formation was able to withstand... " but defeats occurred "when the attacks made by the defending troops" (i.e., abandoning the stand). (DeVries range 10 cross-ref in context)

### Source Criticism (within range)
- Annales Gandenses "generally been seen as accurate in its account of the Flemish rebellion of 1302-1305, but its details of the battles of Arques and Mons-en-Pévèle are often unreliable." Many sources contradict on key points (formations at Arques; whether Philip rallied or fled at Mons; overall victor at Mons). Verbruggen's reconciliation attempt "simply cannot be done." Numbers wildly inflated. (DeVries range 02)

## Range 09 Extraction: Main Crécy (1346) Chapter (lines ~39501-44000 of source; pages ~162–178 in DeVries)

**Source context (DeVries range 09):** This range covers the core Crécy chapter (XIII). Focus on English application of post-Bannockburn lessons (dismounted men-at-arms + archery in flanks formation), why the defensive stand succeeded against larger French force, detailed source criticism (especially archer "herce" placement debate), comparison back to earlier infantry stands (Courtrai), rain effect, king's speech, ditches/pits, Genoese failure and French self-inflicted disorder, solid English formation holding from dawn to vespers. Feeds enrichment of Crécy battle analysis, HYW hub, Bannockburn lineage, source page, strategy/tactics. Verbatim quotes grounded exclusively in this range.

### English Formation and Deployment at Crécy
- Dismounting: "dismounting to stand alongside the rest of the infantry. Even the sixteen-year-old Edward, the Black Prince, who was in command of the first line, was dismounted." (DeVries range 09)
- Archer placement debate and conclusion: "Where the English archers were located in this formation is somewhat of a mystery when one looks at the narrative accounts. ... Villani ... claims that the archers were unusually placed `come detto è addietro' or behind the English and Welsh infantry. Baker ... and the Récits ... maintain the more conventional English archer placement, along the flanks of each line." "But it is Froissart who has most confused modern historians ... `herce' or `erce' ... " Detailed historiography of the term (Lloyd, George, Morris, Oman, Delbrück, Burne, Hardy, Bradbury, Sumption). Conclusion: "No doubt the debate over Froissart's meaning of `herce' will continue. But it certainly should not be an issue at Crécy, as both the more contemporary chronicles of Geoffrey le Baker and the Récits d'un bourgeois de Valenciennes claim a formation of archers along the flanks of the infantry." "And as this was the established formation used by Edward III as seen before at Halidon Hill and later at Neville's Cross and Poitiers these battle formations asserted also by Froissart it seems odd to conclude that another formation, identified as a `herce,' might be used at Crécy. The archers can only have been ordered along the flanks of the infantry lines." Baker quote (via DeVries): "`Sagittariis eciam sua loca designarunt, ut, non coram armatis, set a lateribus regis exercitus quasi ale astarent, et sic non impedirent armatos neque inimicis occurrerent in front, set in latera sagittas fulminarent.'" (DeVries range 09)
- Additional elements: "It seems certain that there were also some early gunpowder weapons with the English forces which were used in the battle, although where these were placed in the English formation cannot be determined from the original sources." (DeVries range 09; cites Villani, Grandes chroniques, Froissart, etc.; notes ongoing debate but "seemingly undeniable proof")

### English Preparation and King's Speech
- Stood all day: "The English army stood all day in their lines, `from dawn to vespers,' claim Geoffrey le Baker and Henry Knighton. They ate a meal, and they listened to a speech from their king." (DeVries range 09)
- Edward's exhortation: "Edward exhorted his troops not to fear the attacks of their enemy, but to put their faith in `God and the Blessed Virgin,' who would help them to be victorious. He also told them that he wanted to fight the French cavalry and not the infantry, although his reasons for this desire are not recorded. Finally, the king ordered that the English soldiers were not to succumb to greed by taking any prisoners or seeking for booty before the battle was over. If this was done, the English line would be weakened and the battle might be lost." (DeVries range 09; notes some sources vary: Chronique Normande alone on fighting cavalry; Jean le Bel alone on no prisoners/booty)
- Ditches: "During their wait for the battle to begin, the English also dug ditches in the battlefield, if Geoffrey le Baker is to be trusted. ... `the English . . . quickly dug many holes in the earth in front of the first line, one foot deep and one foot wide, so that if it happened that the French cavalry were able to attack them, the horses might stagger because of the holes.'" Latin: "`Anglici . . . effodierunt in parvo tempore multa foramina in terra coram acie prima, profunditatem unius pedis et eandem latitudinem habente quolibet illorum, ut, si, quod abfuit, equites Gallicorum ipsos nimis fuissent insecuti, equi ad foramina titubassent.'" (DeVries range 09)

### French Arrival and Scale
- English observed French: "Perhaps most importantly, the English troops watched as the French soldiers continued to arrive. Ultimately, they saw a large force composed of numerous knights in the most expensive and impressive military attire, large numbers of Genoese crossbowmen, and a seemingly unending multitude of other infantry." Philip "had been ardently following the English army, realizing, Jean de Venette insists, that `he had been duped and tricked and was sorrowful therefore.'" Scouts confirmed English position and formation. (DeVries range 09)
- Numbers: Contemporary estimates French cavalry 12,000–30,000, foot 60,000+ (e.g. Richard Wynkeley/Murimuth: 12k cav +60k inf; le Bel 20k cav +100k+ foot; Burton 30k cav). English far smaller. Herald of Chandos: "whoever saw the strength and power of the king of France could only describe it as a great marvel." (DeVries range 09)

### Rain, Opening, and Genoese Attack
- Rain shower: "Sometime during the last phases of the French march to the battlefield, and while the English army stood in their formations, there fell a heavy rain shower, complete with thunder and lightening. ... it made the field of action muddy it had not rained previously for more than six weeks... There may also have been other effects of the rain, in particular on the bowstrings of the Genoese crossbowmen. Both Jean de Venette and the Grandes chroniques note that 'the strings of the Genoese crossbowmen . . . were soaked by the rain and shrank, so that when it was time for them to be drawn against the English, they were, woe is me! useless.' The same effect was not felt by the English longbowmen, however, as 'they had quickly protected their bows by putting the bowstrings on their heads under the helmets.'" (DeVries range 09)
- Battle start: "The battle began with the sound of trumpets, drums, and other French musical instruments. These were sounded, claims the Récits d'un bourgeois de Valenciennes, 'to cause fear' among the enemy. But as a scare tactic they were ineffective, especially as the English were able to answer back with their own noise-makers, their cannons, which were discharged also at the beginning of the battle. Through it all, Edward III's soldiers stood solidly in their formation." (DeVries range 09)
- Genoese approach as standard tactic: "the Genoese crossbowmen began their approach towards the English formation. As seen in the battle of Courtrai, this was a standard French tactic: the crossbowmen were to get within range of the enemy's line, let loose at least one volley (or perhaps more if they had the time and ability to reload), and in this way cause confusion and possibly flight among their opponent's force. Ultimately it was hoped, and had often been proved, that this tactic would enable the main part of the French army, the vaunted cavalry, to charge..." (DeVries range 09)
- Failure: "the attack of the Genoese crossbowmen failed miserably, as these archers were completely stopped in their approach by a counter-attack of English longbowmen; some sources indicate that the Genoese were unable to discharge even one shot. Once this attack failed, Philip had lost his tactical advantage. What followed were errors compounded by pride. The Genoese retreated, but were not allowed back to their lines as Philip VI, perceiving their flight to be treasonous, ordered their destruction by his own cavalry. This order was accepted with enthusiasm by the French cavalry, but all it ultimately did was to add further hindrances to the many cavalry charges which followed against the English infantry lines." (DeVries range 09)

### French Charges and English Response
- Charges not ordered: "the French cavalry charged forward to attack the front line of English infantry. Although the archery exchange had proved a defeat for the French army, its main body, the cavalry, armed with lance and sword, was still an impressive and formidable force, which in the past with its ordered charges had often caused infantry foes to flee in panic before even encountering them. This had not, however, happened at Courtrai, Arques, Mons-en-Pévèle, or Morlaix; nor would it happen at Crécy. In fact, it seems that while several charges were made during the battle, none could be called 'ordered', and this may be the cause of their ultimate failure." (DeVries range 09)
- Effect of archery + disorder: Initial charge disrupted by fleeing/wounded Genoese. "This was followed by an archery onslaught from the English longbowmen who, flushed with confidence by their success against the Genoese, continued to fire their arrows at the oncoming troops." "The initial charging cavalry were first disrupted by the fleeing, dead, and wounded crossbowmen over and through whom they were forced to ride. ... Most contemporary battle commentators report that the arrows of the English longbows caused the death of many men and horses. ... the effect on the charge was the same: increased disruption and confusion. As Jean le Bel puts it: `On their side the archers fired so skillfully that some of those on horses, feeling the barbed arrows, did not wish to advance, while others charged forward as planned; some resisted them tirelessly, while others turned their backs on the enemy.'" (DeVries range 09)
- Flanks directed charges: "With the archers on the flanks, the French charges became directed at the center of the English front line, the section commanded by the Black Prince." (DeVries range 09)
- English solidity: "Although the fighting was intense, these lines received the disordered French charges without breaking, and eventually, as darkness fell, Philip VI left the battlefield followed by his army." (DeVries range 09)

### Tactical Analysis and Why It Worked (DeVries)
- "Because of the numerous excellent original sources on the battle, the tactics of Crécy are simple to discern. So too are the causes of victory for the English and defeat for the French." 
- English: "Edward III chose the battlefield with an eye to its defensibility. He found a ridge between two woods on which to order his troops. The woods would protect his flanks; to protect his rear he had a wagon fortress constructed. He also may have ordered the digging of ditches to add further to the security of his position. (The rain which fell just prior to the battle helped as well.) Edward's soldiers, fewer in number than their French opponents, were ordered as infantry in a solid defensive formation on this high ground. Finally, lines of archers were positioned along the flanks of the English formation."
- Edward's intent: "Edward knew what he wanted to happen at the battle of Crécy. He told his troops that he wished to fight the French cavalry. He wanted to provoke them to charge uphill, through the hindrances of a constant archery deluge and over ditches which would cause disorder among those charging into his solid infantry formation."
- Philip understood but could not wait: "Philip VI also knew what the English king desired. He recognized his opponent's formation and understood its purpose. He could see it ordered on the ridge at Crécy, and in 1328 he had encountered a similar formation at Cassel." "To this point the French king and his soldiers had not done anything that could be considered incautious. ... But the attack of the Genoese crossbowmen failed miserably... What followed were errors compounded by pride." (DeVries range 09)

### Courtrai Comparison (Direct from Range)
- "It is perhaps no mistake that both Jean le Bel and Giovanni Villani compare the battle of Crécy to the battle of Courtrai. There are certainly many similarities between the two battles: Edward III choosing the battlefield, utilizing the natural terrain to protect his flanks, and placing his troops, fewer in number than their French opponents, in a solid defensive formation. There are also several differences, the most notable of which was the participation on both sides of large numbers of archers. But the similarity which impressed Le Bel and Villani was the way the two armies finally met in combat, one a charging cavalry and the other a solid defensive line of infantry. As at Courtrai, the infantry soldiers stood in unity, without giving way to their attackers. It was a great victory, one which would eventually supersede in importance not only the battle of Courtrai but almost all other medieval battles." (DeVries range 09)

### Outcome Notes (from range)
- Heavy French losses among nobility (Alençon, Flanders, Blois, etc.; King of Bohemia special treatment: body washed, linen, litter to Germany with English king/earls present for office of the dead). English losses far smaller. (DeVries range 09)

## Range 06 Extraction: Cassel (continuation, 1328), Dupplin Moor (1332), and Halidon Hill (1333) (lines ~26501-31000 of source)

**Source context (DeVries range 06):** This range provides continuation analysis of Cassel (focusing on the surprise camp attack and final crown formation), the full tactical reconstruction of Dupplin Moor (Disinherited vs. larger Scottish host), and the setup/formation for Halidon Hill (Edward III's army modeled directly on Dupplin). Strong emphasis on English/disinherited post-Bannockburn adaptations: dismounted men-at-arms in solid defensive lines + flanking longbowmen to disrupt charging infantry. Explicit contrast to Cassel (where Flemish offensive break + lack of supporting archery doomed them). ### Cassel continuation (surprise attack and defeat)
- Flemish surprise attack while French rested (hot part of day): Flemings swept down silently, passed through tents, reached Philip's tent before being beaten back. Philip shaken (needed aid to arm). Flemings fell back into crown formation but were surrounded and defeated once French rallied around royal banners. Leaders (e.g. Nikolaas Zannekin) killed. (DeVries range 06)
  Grounding: "the Flemings swept down onto them and onto their king who... was taking his 'accustomed nap.'"; "the infantry attacked without noise and almost in silence... They passed through many tents..."; "After the unsuccessful surprise attack, the remnants of the Flemish army fell back into a crown formation to make a final defensive stand... the leonine descendants of the Franks stabbed at them with lances wet with blood..."; "At the end of the battle the chroniclers report that the field was heaped high with dead rebels." (citing Chronique de Flandre, Continuatio chronici, etc.)
- Historiography revision: Modern views (Oman, Perroy) see it as "vindication of cavalry warfare" and sequel to Bouvines/Courtrai reversal, but DeVries argues it is more accurate to blame infantry rashness in breaking defensive doctrine. Flemings chose hilltop (Courtrai/Mons tradition), taunted for three days; Philip used harassment (skirmishes, artillery, burning lands) to provoke. Once they broke into disorganized attack, cavalry exploited. Reformed crown had "some success" but numbers too reduced. (DeVries range 06)
  Grounding: "To many modern historians, the battle of Cassel is a vindication of cavalry warfare..."; "But can this battle be dismissed as a simple case of knight over foot...?"; "Perhaps then it is inaccurate to credit the cavalry with this victory, as Oman and Perroy have done; it may be more precise instead to blame the infantry for their own defeat. Had they not broken from their defensive position..." (references to Bannockburn etc.)

### Dupplin Moor (1332)
- Disinherited (~1,500, mostly on foot except ~40-44 German mercenary cavalry under Edward Balliol, Henry Beaumont et al.) vs. much larger Scottish army (chroniclers 20k–40k under Earl of Mar). Scots in 2–3 divisions; Disinherited fought almost entirely on foot in defensive posture. (DeVries range 06)
  Grounding: "On August 11, the disinherited met a much larger Scottish army at Dupplin Moor..."; "The Scots ordered their army in either two or three divisions... Opposing them was a force which would also fight on foot, except for a contingent of 40-44 German mercenaries..."
- Scots charged first (confident, taunts); initially pushed English back 20–30 feet. English archers on flanks "blinded and wounded the faces of the first division of the Scots by an incessant discharge of arrows," causing disarray; smaller squadrons forced to mass into larger body (crowding/loss of cohesion). Battle: sunrise to afternoon, push of infantry masses. (DeVries range 06)
  Grounding: "The Scots made the first charge... In the first onset... the Scots drove back the English some twenty or thirty feet..."; "'blinded and wounded the faces of the first division of the Scots by an incessant discharge of arrows'"; "The smaller squadrons, so cut by the archers, were forced to cling to the larger army... the Scots massing together were pressed one into the other."; "the battle lasted from sunrise into the afternoon as the two infantry armies pushed each other back and forth."
- Outcome: Scottish leaders fled on horseback; leaderless troops unable to disengage; Disinherited advanced uniformly, killing/routing the rest. Balliol occupied Perth, crowned at Scone. Edward III pleased. (DeVries range 06)
  Grounding: "the disinherited soldiers began to dominate their opponents, both because of the unity of their defensive formation and because of their attendant archery fire... Soon the disheartened Scottish leaders... mounted their horses and fled... the disinherited had begun a steady uniform advance..."; "A few days later they occupied Perth, and on September 24, Edward Balliol was crowned..."

### Halidon Hill (1333) setup and formation
- Dupplin success led directly to Halidon: Edward III supported Balliol openly (broke Treaty of Northampton), joined Berwick siege, forced battle by hanging hostages. English (~10k per Burton; all to fight on foot "against the ancient tradition of their fathers" per Baker) + large archers. Formation explicitly modeled on Dupplin: three infantry lines in arc at base of hill, archer wings; strictly defensive. Edward III cited Dupplin ("an even more disparate number... had been defeated quite handily") to his troops while recalling past Scottish successes. (DeVries range 06)
  Grounding: "The battle of Dupplin Moor was followed less than a year later by the battle of Halidon Hill..."; "Edward ordered his army similar to the formation used by the disinherited at Dupplin Moor. He formed three lines of infantry at the base of the hill in the shape of an arc, with the wings composed of archers. It was strictly a defensive formation."; "Edward III knew that an even more disparate number of Scottish soldiers had been defeated quite handily by the disinherited at Dupplin Moor..."; "all expected to fight on foot, despite Geoffrey le Baker claiming that this was 'against the ancient tradition of their fathers.' A large number of archers was also present..."
- Scots (larger) took hill; English defensive; single combat (English Robert Benhale vs. unnamed Scot + dog); stalemate broken when Scots charged (aiming for Balliol at rear) in mass. Archers harassed as at Dupplin; hand-to-hand; Scots unable to break line. (DeVries range 06; full outcome in adjacent ranges)

Explicit contrast to Cassel (offensive infantry break + lack of supporting archery doomed Flemings). Dupplin/Halidon as successful tests; Edward III applied lessons directly. ## Conclusions Synthesis (DeVries range 10)

**Overall thesis on uniformity of infantry tactics 1302–1347:** "There is but one main conclusion to this study: infantry armies fought battles during the early fourteenth century using distinctive, and in most cases, decisive tactics. This occurred not just in Flanders or in France or in Scotland, as some historians have insisted, but throughout Europe. The number of battles fought in such a short period of the Middle Ages is a historical fact. The constancy of infantry victories may surprise some, but no less surprising should be the uniformity in tactics used by all these infantry armies throughout such a large geographical region." (DeVries range 10)

**Characteristics of the infantry armies:** "the only consistent characteristic of those armies described here as infantry armies is that they primarily, and in most cases entirely, fought on foot. The Flemings at Courtrai, Arques, Mons-en-Pévèle, and Cassel, the Scots at Loudon Hill and Bannockburn, the Catalan Company at Kephissos, Andrew Harclay's force at Boroughbridge, the disinherited at Dupplin Moor, the Bernese at Laupen, the Frisians at Staveren, and the Liégeois at Vottem all fought using, as far as the original sources are concerned, only infantry soldiers. Robert Bruce even went so far, according to John of Trokelowe and Thomas Walsingham, to insist that his army at Bannockburn only fight as infantry, so that they would not suffer a similar defeat as that at the battle of Falkirk." While each had "different social backgrounds, military experience, and motives for fighting," the foot-soldier emphasis was uniform. (DeVries range 10)

**English dismounting legacy:** "So too were the English troops at the battles of Halidon Hill, Morlaix, Crécy, and Neville's Cross, induced to dismount the cavalry and order as infantry after suffering defeat against the Scots at Bannock-burn, or, if we are to agree with T.F. Tout and J.E. Morris, influenced by Harclay's victorious 'Scottish' infantry tactics at Boroughbridge. Whatever the initial cause of their dismounting, it was, as Geoffrey le Baker claims in his description of the battle of Halidon Hill, 'against the ancient tradition of their fathers.' Soon, however, that ancient tradition was completely supplanted by the new, victorious tactics of the English army." (DeVries range 10)

**Why cavalry failed against prepared foot; limits of infantry (when they charged):** "But it was, in almost every battle, the cavalry of these armies charging against infantry opponents which led directly to their defeat. Only at Dupplin Moor, Halidon Hill, and Neville's Cross was it an infantry charge against infantry opponents which was the climactic point of the battle, and in each of these conflicts the defensive infantry formation was able to withstand the attacking infantry troops." The two clear infantry-army defeats (Mons-en-Pévèle, Cassel) occurred when the defenders abandoned the stand: at Cassel "Philip VI harassed the Flemings into a charge down the hill by artillery fire and by burning the countryside around the battlefield. This attack also reached the French camp and threatened the king before a counter-attack stopped the Flemings." (DeVries range 10)

**Pre-battle preparation and forcing the issue:** Infantry armies typically chose the site "In all but four battles, Arques, Mons-en-Pévèle, Dupplin Moor, and Halidon Hill, the infantry armies chose the site of the battle." Ditches/pits, woods, rivers, hills, and wagon fortresses "meant that opponents had only one course of attack: a frontal assault." Examples include Courtrai (Lys + water-filled ditches), Bannockburn (New Park + pits), Morlaix ("narrow cave"), Crécy (ridge + Bois de Crécy). (DeVries range 10)

**Longbow role (not a 'killing machine'):** "The English in these battles had in the longbow a technological advantage which no one else had, and they used this weapon not as 'a killing machine', as some historians have contended, but instead to narrow their opponents' charges and to protect against flank attacks. [...] Their purpose was simply to narrow and confuse the attacker's charge so that when it fell onto the infantry troops, it did so in a disrupted and relatively impotent manner. (The English cannons at Crécy seem to have been used in the same way.)" Exemplified at Halidon Hill (Burton: smaller squadrons "cut by the archers, were forced to cling to the larger army, and in a short time, the Scots massing together were pressed one into the other") and Crécy (Jean le Bel on archers causing horses to refuse, bolt, or resist). (DeVries range 10)
