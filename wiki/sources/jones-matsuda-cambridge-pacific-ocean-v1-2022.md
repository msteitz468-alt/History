# The Cambridge History of the Pacific Ocean, Vol. 1: The Pacific Ocean to 1800

Volume 1 of *The Cambridge History of the Pacific Ocean* (Jones & Matsuda eds.;
D'Arcy gen. ed., Cambridge UP, 2022) surveys the history of the Pacific — "around
one third of the Earth's surface" — from the deep geological and biological past
and the first human colonization through to the eve of sustained European
dominance c. 1800. Its organising move is to treat the [[pacific-ocean|Pacific
Ocean]] itself as a historical actor, an [[oceanic-history|oceanic history]] told
through marine ecosystems, deep time, Indigenous knowledge systems, genetics,
linguistics, oral tradition, and voyaging rather than as a backdrop for European
discovery (Europeans appear only in Part VI).

It complements the already-ingested island-centred histories: where Denoon and
Fischer centre the Islanders, this volume centres the ocean and brings in the
Pacific Rim — China's maritime political economy, Southeast Asian sea cultures,
the Northwest Coast of the Americas, and Polynesian reach to Chile.

## Volume Synthesis Note

The volume's defining argument is methodological: the [[pacific-ocean|Pacific]]
is not a backdrop for human action but a historical agent, and its history must
be written as an [[oceanic-history|oceanic history]] that decentres European
discovery and treats marine science, geology, genetics, linguistics, oral
tradition and experimental voyaging as co-equal sources. Europeans appear only in
the final part; the deep past, the natural world, and Indigenous knowledge systems
come first. This inverts the older convention (which would "begin more
definitively with European presence") and aligns the wiki's Pacific coverage with
Hau'ofa's [[sea-of-islands|"sea of islands"]] reframing already captured from
Denoon.

Against the wiki's existing Pacific network — built from
[[denoon-cambridge-pacific-islanders-1997|Denoon]] and
[[fischer-history-pacific-islands-2002|Fischer]], which centre the Islanders — this
volume centres the **ocean and its Rim**. It adds whole domains the island
histories lacked: the geological birth of islands and the science that studied
them; the genetic/linguistic/oral-tradition toolkit for deep history; Pleistocene
and Austronesian colonization seen from the Asian seaboard; and the Pacific Rim
maritime worlds of China, eastern Indonesia, the American Northwest Coast and the
Andes–Mesoamerica shell-and-metal exchange. It substantially enriches three
existing pages (Austronesian chronology, New Guinea's 50,000-year past, voyaging
capacity) and supplies the maritime-Asia counterweight — China's political economy
of the sea, the Zheng family's [[chinese-maritime-mercantile-resurgence|mercantile
state]], and the European "[[spanish-lake|Spanish Lake]]" — that the islander
histories only glimpsed.

Cross-volume tension worth flagging: the volume's strong "ocean as actor" framing
and its celebration of connection sit in productive tension with the more
conflict-and-isolation-focused archaeological narratives elsewhere in the wiki, and
with the Eurocentric periodization the wiki's framework necessarily imposes (the
volume deliberately resists "ancient/contact/colonial" stages in favour of "deep
time"). Vol. 2 (since 1800; Hattori & Samson) remains in the raw/ queue.
