# Wizard: The Life and Times of Nikola Tesla — Marc J. Seifer (1996)

**Full citation**: Seifer, Marc J. *Wizard: The Life and Times of Nikola Tesla — Biography of a Genius*. New York: Birch Lane Press, 1996 (cited here in the Citadel Press paperback reprint, 2011). Listed in the project's Top 50 Biographies.

This is the definitive archival biography of **Nikola Tesla** (1856–1943) — Serbian-American electrical engineer and inventor, father of the polyphase alternating-current (AC) system, the induction motor, the Tesla coil, foundational wireless and radio technology, remote control, and a lifelong dreamer of global wireless power transmission. The book covers Tesla's birth in Smiljan (military frontier of the Austro-Hungarian Empire, in modern Croatia), his education and breakdown, his emigration to America in 1884, his brief, abrasive employment under Thomas Edison, the discovery of the rotating magnetic field, the alliance with George Westinghouse that won the "War of the Currents," the Niagara Falls power project, the Columbian Exposition triumph (1893), the celebrity high-society years, the Colorado Springs experiments (1899), the Wardenclyffe Tower venture financed and then abandoned by J. P. Morgan (1901–1906), the bitter Marconi priority dispute over radio, the long decline into poverty and eccentricity, the late claims of a "death ray" / particle-beam weapon, and his death in 1943 followed by the FBI seizure of his papers.

## Overview and Thesis

Seifer presents Tesla as a figure of genuine, era-defining genius whose practical triumphs (AC power, the induction motor, the resonant transformer, radio fundamentals) made the electrified twentieth century possible — and whose visionary overreach (wireless power for the whole planet, transmission "without wires" of energy and intelligence, weapons of ultimate defense) left him broke, marginalized, and posthumously mythologized. The central tension the book works is between **Tesla the rigorous engineer** and **Tesla the prophet**: the same intuitive, image-driven mind that "saw" complete working machines before building them also generated unfalsifiable claims that alienated financiers and the scientific establishment.

A second through-line is **money and patronage**: the book is structured around Tesla's relationships with capital — Edison, Westinghouse, the Wall Street circle (J. P. Morgan, John Jacob Astor, J. P. Morgan Jr.), and the press barons — and argues that Tesla's recurring inability to convert revolutionary invention into durable financial control (the torn Westinghouse royalty contract; the Morgan terms at Wardenclyffe; the loss of radio priority litigation in his lifetime) was as decisive in his fate as any technical setback.

Seifer also takes the **Tesla mythology** seriously as a historical object: the occult/avatar legend, the Martian-signals episode, the death-ray and free-energy claims, and the Cold-War-era government interest in the seized papers. He distinguishes the documented inventions from the speculative ones while resisting both debunking dismissal and credulous worship.

## Methodological Notes

- Two decades of archival work: Tesla Museum (Belgrade), Library of Congress, Columbia University, Butler Library, and corporate/government archives (Westinghouse, J. P. Morgan, FBI, Office of Alien Property).
- Heavy use of Tesla's own articles, lectures, and patents, plus the contemporary technical (*Electrical Review*, *Electrical Engineer*) and popular press, and the correspondence of intimates (Robert and Katharine Johnson, George Scherff, B. A. Behrend, Kenneth Swezey).
- Psychobiographical interpretation of Tesla's compulsions (germophobia, fixation on the number three, photographic mental imagery, aversion to pearls and round objects) as both creative engine and social handicap — explicitly interpretive, drawing on Seifer's psychology training.
- Adjudicates priority and credit disputes (Tesla vs. Edison, vs. Marconi, vs. Reginald Fessenden, vs. Elihu Thomson) using patent dates and lecture records.
- Treats the speculative late inventions and the FBI-papers affair as reconstructions from fragmentary evidence, flagged as such.

## Key Contributions to the Wiki

- The definitive narrative-and-archival life of Tesla for the Long 19th Century and World Wars Era — a major figure in the [[processes/second-industrial-revolution|Second Industrial Revolution]] previously absent from the wiki.
- Grounds the **War of the Currents** (AC vs. Edison's DC) in business and patent history, not just technical lore.
- Detailed treatment of the **Niagara Falls power project**, the **1893 World's Columbian Exposition** lighting contract, and the **Wardenclyffe** wireless venture and its financing.
- Primary material on the **Tesla–Morgan** and **Tesla–Westinghouse** patron relationships and the **Marconi radio priority dispute** (resolved in Tesla's favor by the U.S. Supreme Court in 1943, after his death).
- The FBI / Office of Alien Property seizure of Tesla's papers (1943) and the Cold-War death-ray / particle-beam afterlife.

## Volume Synthesis Note

Seifer's *Wizard* enters the wiki as the anchor source for **Nikola Tesla** and, with him, the human and financial drama behind the [[processes/war-of-the-currents|War of the Currents]] and the [[processes/electrification|electrification]] of the modern world — a dimension of the [[processes/second-industrial-revolution|Second Industrial Revolution]] the collection had documented technologically but not biographically. Its strongest contribution is archival grounding for the *patronage* structure of late-19th-century invention: the book is organized around Tesla's relationships with capital (Edison, Westinghouse, the elder and younger Morgan, Astor, Ryan), and it makes the argument — persuasive on the evidence it presents — that Tesla's failure to retain financial control of his inventions (the released Westinghouse royalty; Morgan's 51% at Wardenclyffe) was as decisive in his ruin as any technical setback.

The volume sits in productive tension with a purely technological reading of electrification: where the [[processes/war-of-the-currents|currents war]] is often told as transformer economics settling a standards question, Seifer restores the contingency — the lectures, the propaganda (Kemmler), the Columbian and Niagara contracts won by underbidding, the personalities. It also supplies the wiki's first sustained treatment of the **radio priority** question ([[events/marconi-radio-priority-dispute-1943]]), where Tesla's foundational patents and the 1943 Supreme Court ruling complicate the Marconi-centric standard account.

Its weaknesses are equally clear and flagged on the relevant pages: Seifer is sympathetic to the point of credulity on the unverified late inventions ([[concepts/wireless-power-transmission|global wireless power]], [[concepts/death-ray-teleforce|teleforce]], free energy, the "missing papers"), and his psychobiographical framing of Tesla's compulsions, while illuminating, is interpretive. The wiki treats the documented engineering as established and the speculative-technology and conspiracy material as Seifer's reconstruction. A future cross-check against a more sceptical Tesla life (e.g. W. Bernard Carlson, *Tesla: Inventor of the Electrical Age*, 2013) would let the wiki promote the credulity question to a proper controversy page.
