## Overview

Mogens Herman Hansen's *Polis* (2006) is the standard modern introduction to the ancient Greek city-state, distilling the results of the Copenhagen Polis Centre (1993–2003). It argues that the polis was simultaneously a city and a state, that there were roughly 1,500 poleis in the Archaic and Classical periods (not just Athens), and that the Greek case is one of 37 identified city-state cultures in world history. The book corrects Athens-centric views and provides precise criteria, quantitative data, and a comparative framework.

## Key Arguments (to be expanded with subagent claims)

- Polis = city + state + city-state.
- ~1,500 poleis; most small (hundreds to a few thousand residents); territories typically small.
- High pre-industrial urbanization, market/trade economies, assembly decision-making, leagues and federal states.
- Modern macro-states often structurally closer to ancient city-state cultures than to ancient macro-states in key respects.
- Clear distinction between the polis of the Archaic/Classical periods and its transformed Hellenistic form (mostly dependent but retaining internal self-government).

## Historiography and Collection Context

Hansen is the leading authority; the book rests on the Centre's exhaustive inventory rather than selective literary sources. See also the Centre's larger *An Inventory of Archaic and Classical Poleis* (2004). This source is the primary anchor for the wiki's treatment of the Greek polis as a political and urban type.

## Volume Synthesis Note

Hansen 2006 is the definitive short synthesis of the Copenhagen Polis Centre’s work. Its distinctive contributions are: (1) the rigorous demonstration that “polis” meant city + state simultaneously for the Greeks and that the overlap was near-total; (2) the quantitative scale (~1,500 poleis, ~7.5M+ population, high urbanisation via Ackerbürger); (3) the comparative framing (one of 37 city-state cultures; four recurring features; modern macro-states often resemble them more than ancient macro-states); (4) the rejection of Athens-centric and “polis destroyed 338 BCE” narratives in favour of long continuity with transformation in the Hellenistic/Roman periods; and (5) the systematic critique of Weber’s ideal type versus prevailing ancient-historian orthodoxy, resolved in Weber’s favour on most points by the Centre’s data. This source anchors the wiki’s treatment of the Greek polis as political form and urban phenomenon.
