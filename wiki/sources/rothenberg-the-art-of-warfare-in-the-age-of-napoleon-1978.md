# The Art of Warfare in the Age of Napoleon

**Gunther E. Rothenberg** (1978, Indiana University Press; paperback 1980). A concise but wide-ranging survey of how the art of war changed between the limited professional wars of the *ancien régime* and the mass national wars of the French Revolution and Napoleon. The book deliberately focuses on the "cutting edge" — the soldier's trade, tactical formations, army organization, and support services — while providing a narrative backbone of the wars from Valmy to Waterloo and comparative treatment of the French and coalition armies.

Rothenberg positions the work as filling a gap in English-language literature that had emphasized leaders, battles, or single nations. It treats both the transformation wrought by the Revolution (levee en masse, new tactical combinations, national motivation) and the institutionalization and limits of the Napoleonic system.

### Range1-Valmy as Watershed between Limited and National War
On 20 September 1792 near Valmy in north-eastern France, the combined armies of French Generals Dumouriez and Kellermann faced a Prussian army under the Duke of Brunswick. After hours of artillery exchange, the Prussian infantry prepared to assault but halted before reaching musket range following the explosion of a French ammunition cart. Brunswick declared, “We are not going to fight here.” The cannonade ended with only a few hundred casualties; ten days later Brunswick negotiated a retreat into winter quarters. The text presents this as a decisive collision: a “people’s army” had held against the old order. Two patterns of warfare met for the first time—one limited and becoming obsolete, the other potentially unlimited. Contemporary observers including the young Goethe and later Marshal Foch and a noted soldier-historian framed it as the end of the wars of kings and the beginning of the wars of the peoples, marking from a military viewpoint “the end of a world.”

**Grounding quote:** “On 20 September 1792 the combined armies of the French Generals Dumouriez and Kellermann faced a Prussian army commanded by the Duke of Brunswick near Valmy in north-eastern France. After exchanging heavy artillery fire for several hours, the Prussian infantry formed up for the assault. ... ‘We are not going to fight here,’ he decided. ... Although the cannonade at Valmy had cost but a few hundred casualties, it was a watershed in the history of war. A ‘people’s army’ had defeated the old order. Two patterns of warfare, the one limited and now becoming obsolete, and the other, potentially unlimited, had collided for the first time. That night the young Goethe, who had accompanied the Prussians, commented: ‘From this place and day commenced a new epoch in the world’s history.’ He was right. ‘The wars of kings,’ Marshal Foch wrote, ‘were at the end; the wars of the peoples were beginning.’ And a noted soldier-historian put it in even clearer perspective, from the ‘military point of view it was the end of a world’.” (Ch 1, opening narrative on Valmy; repeated reference in Ch 2)

### Range1-Eighteenth-Century Limited War: Political and Operational Characteristics
During the century before Valmy, wars of the kings evolved into formal affairs pursued with limited means for limited objectives. Monarchs decided war and peace by calculating gains and costs in terms of their own interests; the people were neither consulted nor expected to contribute substantially. Fighting was left to small professional armies. In the absence of national or ideological content, neither side sought the total destruction of the enemy. Costly pitched battles were avoided when possible; manoeuvre, not combat, was the principal operation. The most accomplished strategy, per General Lloyd, was to “initiate military operations with mathematical precision and to keep on waging war without ever being under the necessity to strike a blow.” Campaigns aimed to place the opponent in an untenable position, after which the enemy, accepting the rules, capitulated on terms. Surrender of a fortress or field army was not dishonourable; no general contemplated fighting to the last man. The Duke of Brunswick’s bloodless 1787 Dutch campaign exemplified the ideal; his refusal to press at Valmy was consistent with this framework.

**Grounding quote:** “During the century before Valmy the wars of the kings had evolved into formal affairs, pursued with limited means for limited objectives. Monarchs decided on war and peace by calculating gains and costs in terms of their interests; the people neither were consulted nor normally expected to contribute much to the fighting which was left to small professional armies. And in the absence of any national or ideological content it was not in anyone’s interest to seek the total destruction of the enemy. Costly pitched battles were avoided when possible; manoeuvre not combat were the principal operations of war. The most accomplished strategy, so General Lloyd, author of a History of the Late War in Germany (1776-90), advised, was to ‘initiate military operations with mathematical precision and to keep on waging war without ever being under the necessity to strike a blow’.” (Ch 1, “The nature of eighteenth-century limited war”)

### Range1-Logistical, Disciplinary, and Economic Constraints on Ancien Régime Armies
Eighteenth-century warfare operated under severe political, social, economic, and military constraints. Standing armies placed heavy strain on treasuries (Prussia spent ~90% of revenue on the military in 1752; France two-thirds of its budget on the army in 1784). Armies were regular but not national; recruitment relied on foreign nationals and complete foreign units (Swiss regiments were elite examples). Pay was low, quarters wretched, and soldiers often sought odd jobs. Brutal discipline (floggings, death for desertion) was common, yet desertion reached appalling proportions in wartime, forcing mitigation and restraints on combat. Fear of desertion and desire to spare civilians restricted foraging; armies depended on huge wagon trains and pre-positioned magazines. Supplying 50,000 men 15 miles from base required ~100 wagons daily; operational range was limited to 50–80 miles from the nearest magazine. Bad weather further restricted movement and artillery. Armies normally retired to winter quarters. Fortifications became primary strategic targets, slowing operations further. Camps resembled small cities with extensive baggage, servants, and sutlers.

**Grounding quote:** “The limited and formal nature of warfare in the eighteenth century was the result of political, social, economic, and military constraints. ... Everywhere governments skimped on the upkeep of their army. Pay was low, quarters wretched, and often soldiers had to look for odd jobs to fill their stomachs. ... Compounding the misery of a soldier’s life was a most ferocious and brutal discipline. ... Fear of desertion, together with the restricted supply of manpower, imposed real restraints on the conduct of war. ... Armies had to be supplied by huge wagon trains which in turn depended on magazines, usually well established in advance of a campaign. To supply 50,000 men 15 miles from their base required some 100 wagons daily and five to seven days marching distance from the nearest magazine, between 50 to 80 miles, was considered the maximum practical operational range.” (Ch 1, sections on constraints and logistics)

### Range1-Gribeauval Artillery Reforms and Pre-Revolutionary French Developments
Technical improvements, especially in artillery, created new possibilities, though the text stresses that decisive changes in warfare stemmed from political and social factors rather than technology alone. The Gribeauval system (introduced after opposition, fully 1776) featured a standardized range of 4-, 8-, and 12-pounder field guns plus howitzers with interchangeable parts, iron axles, lighter carriages, thinner/shorter barrels, calibrated tangent sights, and elevating screws. These were about half the weight of predecessors (new 12-pounder ~1,600 pounds). Pair-harnessing, quick-match vent tubes, and prepacked charges improved mobility and rate of fire. Earlier Austrian (Liechtenstein) and other innovations influenced it; Gribeauval had served with the Austrians. Complementary French developments included the divisional system (standard by the 1780s), Guibert’s emphasis on mobility (column for movement, line for combat; rapid transitions; citizen army ideas later recanted in part), and Bourcet’s mountain warfare principles. A 1787–8 council reduced swollen officer ranks and finalized the 1791 regulations. The French army on the eve of revolution was well organized and trained by ancien régime standards, yet undermined by deep class fissures (1781 noble edict excluding many).

**Grounding quote:** “The Gribeauval system consisted of a range of 4-, 8-, and 12-pounder field guns, together with 8- and 6-inch bore howitzers. It featured standardized carriages with interchangeable parts, iron axles, limbers, and sights. Weight reduction was achieved by making the carriage lighter and narrower and by casting the barrels thinner and shorter, modifications that no longer affected range. Overall the Gribeauval pieces were about half as heavy as their predecessors. The new 12-pounder, for instance, weighed only 1,600 pounds, half as much as the cannon it replaced. ... The tangent sights and the elevating screw have been called the ‘most significant improvements in the design of ordnance during the last two hundred years of the smooth-bore era’.” (Ch 1, “The improvement of artillery”)

### Range1-Early Revolutionary Wars: From French Declaration of War to Stabilization (1792–1795)
War began when France, internally chaotic after the Revolution (Estates General 1789, Constituent Assembly, National Guard, Varennes flight, eroded discipline and officer exodus), declared war on Austria (and by implication Prussia) on 20 April 1792. Early operations were disasters: mutinies, desertions, officer murders, failed Belgian invasion. Brunswick’s Austro-Prussian invasion prompted radicalization (massacre of Swiss Guards, suspension of monarchy, Republic proclaimed). Valmy (see Claim 1) saved the Revolution. French morale soared; Dumouriez won at Jemappes (November 1792), leading to annexations and the First Coalition. Setbacks followed (Neerwinden 1793). The Terror and Committee of Public Safety enforced unity; the amalgame (early 1794) merged volunteers, conscripts, and veterans into demi-brigades and field armies (hundreds of thousands on paper). Victories (Hondschoote, Wattignies, Tourcoing, Ypres, Fleurus) and Allied disunity (Polish distractions, separate objectives, cordon strategy) forced Prussia to the Treaty of Basle (1795), with Spain and minor states following. Only Britain, Austria, and Sardinia remained at war.

**Grounding quote:** “On 20 April 1792, General Dumouriez ... and Louis XVI ... called on the Assembly for a declaration of war. ... At the cost of a few hundred casualties the Revolution had been saved. ... On 6 November 1792, with 40,000 men, he [Dumouriez] surprised 14,000 Austrians in their winter quarters at Jemappes... The Convention voted on 19 November to extend its ‘aid to all peoples who wish to recover their liberty’. ... early in 1794 the so-called amalgame combined the volunteer and conscript battalions with the remaining veterans of the old army into new regiments, now styled ‘demi-brigades’. ... In 1794 the full power of the French armies made itself felt in a series of offensives that ended the First Coalition.” (Ch 2, “The coming of war: 1789–92” through “Campaigns of the First Coalition: 1793–5”)

### Range1-Napoleon’s 1796–1797 Italian Campaign and the Shift to Decisive Operations
Carnot’s 1796 plans called for coordinated offensives in Germany and Italy to force Austria to terms. In Italy, Napoleon (assuming command March 1796 with ~45,000 men) used superior mobility, interior lines, and concentration. He separated Sardinian and Austrian forces (Sardinia surrendered), blockaded Mantua, and defeated successive relieving armies in detail (Lonato/Castiglione, Arcola, Rivoli). After Mantua’s capitulation (February 1797), he advanced into Austria, reaching striking distance of Vienna and forcing an armistice and peace. The campaign established his reputation through brilliant maneuvering despite shortages (especially cavalry). It exemplified the new style: rapid regrouping, use of divisions, exploitation of interior position, and forcing superior enemies to fight separately or retreat. Earlier 1795 German operations had faltered partly due to internal issues and betrayals (Pichegru).

**Grounding quote:** “Napoleon’s Italian campaign of 1796-7 was distinguished by brilliant moves that forced superior enemy forces either to surrender or retreat... The campaign fell into three major stages. In the first he burst from his positions near the coast and, separating the Sardinian and Austrian armies... In the second phase... blockaded a large Austrian force in Mantua, while utilizing its interior position to defeat in turn several Austrian armies... In the final phase... he moved east into Austria. One year to the date that he had taken command his troops were within striking distance of Vienna...” (Ch 2, “The campaigns in Germany and Italy: 1796–7”)

### Range1-Leipzig (Battle of Nations) and the Final Campaigns to Waterloo
After the disastrous 1812 Russian campaign (614,000 men crossed Niemen; ~60,000 returned) and 1813 setbacks, Napoleon stood at Leipzig in October 1813 with forces short of supplies and cavalry. The “Battle of Nations” (16–19 October) saw Allies converging; Napoleon failed to beat them in detail. Saxon defection forced retreat toward the Rhine (~70,000 formed + 40,000 stragglers). He defeated Bavarians at Hanau but reached France weakened. In 1814, despite skillful maneuvering and local victories (Brienne, La Rothière), Allies took Paris; abdication followed. Escaping Elba in 1815, Napoleon invaded Belgium. Victories at Ligny (vs Prussians) and actions at Quatre Bras preceded the decisive stand at Mont Saint Jean (Waterloo) on 18 June against Wellington (~67–68k British-allied) with ~72k French. Rain delayed opening; French columns and cavalry assaults failed against the reverse-slope line. Prussian arrival in strength (Grouchy absent) turned the battle; the Old Guard’s repulse ended it. The wars concluded.

**Grounding quote:** “short of supplies, Napoleon made his stand at Leipzig. The ‘Battle of Nations’, 16—19 October 1813... On the third day, the defection of the Saxons forced him to break off the battle... On 1 March 1815, Napoleon returned to France... On 16 June, Napoleon attacked the Prussians at Ligny... On the next day, Sunday, 18 June, Napoleon with 71,947 men and 246 guns faced Wellington with 67,711 men and 156 guns. ... Even Ney’s furious cavalry charges and finally the assault of the Old Guard failed to break it. The Prussian troops... began to arrive in increasing strength: Grouchy did not come. It was the end.” (Ch 2, sections on 1813 and “The final act: Waterloo, 1815”)

### Range2-The Levée en Masse and the Birth of the Nation in Arms (1793)

The French Republic, facing invasion and internal revolt in 1793, moved from failed voluntary recruitment and partial drafts to a comprehensive national mobilization. The Convention’s 23 August 1793 decree implemented the principle that the entire people constituted the armed force of the Republic. While it requisitioned the whole population for war-related tasks, it prioritized unmarried and childless men aged 18–25 for combat service. Units were to be formed on a territorial basis with flags inscribed “The French people risen against the tyrants.” This produced roughly 450,000 additional men by September 1793 and brought total strength above 700,000 by 1794 (though exact figures remained uncertain due to poor record-keeping). The measure was emergency-driven and unpopular; after early victories it was partially rolled back in favor of a mixed volunteer/conscript system under the Directory.

**Grounding quote:** “From this moment until that in which every enemy has been driven from the territory of the Republic, every Frenchman is permanently requisitioned for service with the armies. The young men shall fight; married men will manufacture weapons and transport stores; women shall make tents and nurse in the hospitals; children shall turn old linen into lint; the old men shall repair to the public squares to raise the courage of the warriors and preach the unity of the Republic and hatred against the kings… ‘unmarried men and childless widowers from eighteen to twenty-five years shall go first’.” (Ch 4, Raising the Revolutionary Armies : 1791—8, on the levée en masse decree)

### Range2-The Amalgame: Integration of Regulars and Volunteers into Demi-Brigades

Faced with a collapsing regular army and masses of raw levies, the Republic fused old line units with volunteer battalions into new demi-brigades (each of three battalions). The first amalgame (1793–94) and a second (1796–99) eliminated separate regular and national identities. Old regimental uniforms and traditions largely vanished. The process was accompanied by ceremony—speeches, presentation of new flags, the Marseillaise—and produced a unified republican spirit. Light infantry demi-brigades substituted carabineer companies for grenadiers. Although early officer elections produced some incompetents, they also elevated capable men (including nine future marshals). Seniority later became more important after elections were abolished in 1795, while battlefield promotion from the ranks remained a key incentive.

**Grounding quote:** “Despite earlier opposition and misgivings, the operation was a success. The last traces of the army of the ancien régime vanished along with the remnants of the old regimental uniforms and traditions. Friction between the regular and the national troops was eliminated and replaced by republican and national spirit.” (Ch 4, organization of the armies : infantry, cavalry, artillery, on the amalgame)

**Links / taxonomy:** enabled: formation of large, ideologically motivated armies; part_of: reorganization of Revolutionary infantry; contributed_to: promotion of merit and advancement from ranks; analogous_to: later Napoleonic integration of conscripts with veterans.

### Range2-The Column, Skirmisher, and Ordre Mixte Tactical Synthesis

Revolutionary armies initially abandoned formal linear tactics in favor of mass and shock. Carnot’s February 1794 instructions emphasized “manoeuvre in mass and offensively… use the bayonet on every occasion,” drawn from experience at Wattignies. By 1795 divisions routinely deployed about one-fifth of their strength (light troops or line infantry) as skirmishers (tirailleurs, voltigeurs, chasseurs) ahead of the main body, with the bulk of battalions held in column ready to reinforce or assault. The 1791 regulations provided the framework for columns of “divisions” (two companies side-by-side) that could deploy into line or resume column. The resulting *ordre mixte* combined skirmisher fire to shake the enemy, line fire from one or more battalions, and column assault by the remainder, with grenadier companies often leading or held in reserve. This hybrid became the decisive element in modernizing tactics and was retained (with adaptations) into the Napoleonic period.

**Grounding quote:** “In 1795 the French armies in Germany began to deploy about one-fifth of a division’s strength, light troops… ahead of the main body, and kept the mass of battalions behind, ready either to reinforce the skirmishing line or, if the skirmishing line had shaken the enemy sufficiently, to deliver the decisive blow in column.” (Ch 4, description of French tactics — the ordre mixte; also fig. 9 caption detailing skirmish company + line battalion + column battalions + grenadier reserve)

**Grounding quote:** “The general instructions… are always to manoeuvre in mass and offensively; to maintain strict, but not overly meticulous discipline . . . and to use the bayonet on every occasion.” (Ch 4, Carnot order of 2 February 1794)

### Range2-Discipline, Motivation, and the Soldier’s Daily Conditions in the Revolutionary Armies

The Republic rejected corporal punishment (abolished 1789) in favor of shame-based and incentive-driven controls. Capital offenses were punished by shooting or penal units; lesser infractions by loss of rank, extra fatigues, or drills. A distinctive extra-legal sanction was the *savate*—a beating with the sole of a shoe administered by squad-mates to stragglers or shirkers. An American observer noted that “dread of shame and hopes for reward operate more powerfully in their system of discipline than fear of punishment.” Glory and the prospect of riches became primary motivators. Supply was chronically deficient: troops lacked shoes, clothing, and rations; uniforms devolved into improvised blue or red trousers, wooden clogs, and liberty caps. Commanders such as Napoleon openly acknowledged that “Soldiers, you are naked, ill-fed. The government owes you much; it can give you nothing.”

**Grounding quote:** “There was a strong aversion to corporal punishment, abolished in 1789… In addition there existed a unique and extra-legal punishment, the savate, a beating with the sole of a shoe administered to stragglers or shirkers by a soldier’s squad or mess-mates, a procedure more humiliating than painful.” (Ch 4, Discipline in the French army)

**Grounding quote:** “Glory and riches became the major motivating impulses and commanders who knew to appeal to these feelings, Napoleon or Hoche for example, exerted extraordinary power over their men.” (Ch 4)

**Links / taxonomy:** contributed_to: combat effectiveness of politicized but poorly equipped forces; contrasts_with: fear-based discipline of ancien régime or opposing armies; enabled: high motivation despite material shortages.

### Range2-Napoleon’s Institutionalization of the Corps System

Napoleon inherited large, mobile, offensive-minded conscript armies and restructured them around the corps d’armée as the basic combined-arms formation. Each corps typically comprised two to four infantry divisions, a brigade or division of light cavalry, several artillery batteries, and engineer/support elements. The exact composition varied deliberately to deceive enemy intelligence and to match the mission or the commander’s abilities. By 1805 the Grande Armée (principal field force under Napoleon’s direct command) numbered nominally over 600,000 (more realistic effective estimates around 350,000 including garrisons). The corps allowed decentralized march and maneuver while concentrating for battle, becoming a permanent feature of Napoleonic organization.

**Grounding quote:** “For strategic and grand tactical purposes, Napoleon institutionalized the corps organization, used experimentally by Moreau. The corps became the smallest force of all arms. Primarily an infantry organization, each corps combined two to four infantry divisions, a brigade or division of light cavalry, several artillery batteries, and a small number of engineer and support troops. The exact number of divisions and guns allocated to each corps varied purposely, both to confuse enemy intelligence and to reflect its assignment and the talents of its commander.” (Ch 5, Napoleon’s Armies, on the Grande Armée)

### Range2-The Imperial Guard as Elite Formation and Instrument of Personal Loyalty

Napoleon created the Imperial Guard in 1804 from earlier Consular Guard elements. Guardsmen received higher rank (one grade above line equivalents) and substantially higher pay, better clothing, and privileges. Napoleon cultivated a direct personal relationship, frequently sharing their camp and food, learning details of their service, and nicknaming the Old Guard grenadiers “les grognards.” The Guard maintained discipline longer than line units in adversity (e.g., during the 1812 retreat until the Emperor departed). It performed well in Spain, Russia, and 1813–14 but suffered heavy casualties. Its separation from the line army and elite status made it both a combat reserve and a political instrument.

**Grounding quote:** “Napoleon separated the Imperial Guard in many ways from the rest of the army, and he developed an intimate personal relationship with its officers and men… every guardsman ranked one grade higher than his counterpart in the line and he received substantially higher pay, better clothing, and other privileges.” (Ch 5, The Imperial Guard)

**Grounding quote:** “At Essling in 1809 the Guard refused to fight unless the Emperor retired out of danger; during the retreat from Moscow the Guard maintained discipline until the Emperor left the army, only then did it break ranks…” (Ch 5)

**Links / taxonomy:** part_of: [[napoleon]]’s armies and [[morale-and-cohesion-napoleonic]]; enabled: reliable reserve and personal loyalty mechanism; contributed_to: high combat performance of the elite core.

### Range2-Napoleonic Conscription, Manpower Management, and Declining Quality after 1812

Napoleon retained the Directory’s Jourdan Law framework (conscription of single men 20–25 by lot/class, with substitutes and deferments permitted). Annual calls averaged around 85,000 up to 1812; only a fraction of eligible males actually served. Deferments created a callable reserve. In 1813, facing catastrophic losses, Napoleon called classes early, incorporated National Guard “Cohorts,” and drew on all available manpower. While veterans could rapidly assimilate raw recruits, the system increasingly produced half-trained masses. After Russia, three-quarters of the soldiery lacked military training; complex maneuvers became impossible and mass tactics predominated. Reports noted units unable to perform basic drill, yet many of these inexperienced troops still fought effectively in 1813–14.

**Grounding quote:** “In 1813, three-quarters of the soldiery had no military training. Colonel Fezensac for one complained that his regiments consisted of ‘young soldiers who had to be taught everything’, and of ‘non-commissioned officers who did not know much more’.” (Ch 5, on replacements and quality after Russia)

**Grounding quote:** “It did, of course, not permit units to perform complex manoeuvres, and contributed to the marked changeover to mass tactics after 1807.” (Ch 5, on the replacement system)

### Range3 - British Logistics and Wellington's Supply System in the Peninsular War
Wellington placed exceptional emphasis on detailed logistics after witnessing breakdowns in earlier campaigns. Supply was managed by a Commissary branch (Treasury-controlled, with assimilated ranks by 1810), supported by the Royal Wagon Train and especially pack-mule transport on poor Iberian roads. Spanish muleteers were described as indispensable; Wellington's policy of paying for supplies in cash helped secure local cooperation and sustained operations where French methods faltered. This administrative focus gave the British army a practical edge despite limited wagon numbers (only 162 military wagoners available at Waterloo).

**Grounding quote:** "‘It is very necessary,’ he observed, ‘to attend to detail, and to trace a biscuit from Lisbon into a man’s mouth at the frontier, and to provide for its removal from place to place, by land or by water, or no military operation can be carried on’. An army without supplies and starving, he wrote to his brother, ‘is actually worse than none. The soldiers lose their disciplined spirit’." (Ch 6, Opponents of the French, on Wellington)

### Range3 - King's German Legion and Portuguese Irregulars as Force Multipliers for Britain
The King's German Legion (KGL), raised from Hanoverian exiles after the 1803 Convention of Lauenburg, grew to ~8,000 all-arms and was rated equal to the best British troops, with its light cavalry considered the army's finest. Portuguese militia and Ordenanza (general male levy under mixed officers) harassed French stragglers and supply lines, contributing decisively to Masséna's difficulties before Lisbon and during his retreat. Relations with Spanish allies remained tense and often hostile, but Portuguese and KGL units significantly augmented Wellington's effective strength.

**Grounding quote:** "ledge that he could rely on them nearly doubled Wellington’s fighting power. The French, too, thought well of the Portuguese; Marbot declared them ‘the equal of British troops’. ... The most congenial of the British allied units, however, was the King’s German Legion... The KGL was considered as good as the best British troops and their light cavalry the best in the army as a whole." (Ch 6, Opponents of the French)

### Range3 - Prussian Army Ossification and Collapse at Jena-Auerstedt (1806)
The Prussian army remained Frederician in doctrine and structure: three-rank lines, controlled volleys, massed cavalry charges, heavy artillery, widespread exemptions (over 500,000 military-age men), heavy foreign recruitment (~half the force), poor training due to furloughs, and an aging officer corps (many generals over 60 or 70 by 1806). Mobilization was incomplete and disorderly; against Napoleon's ~160,000, Prussia fielded ~128,000 under the aged Duke of Brunswick. The twin battles overwhelmed the divided army; the subsequent collapse (panic, mass fortress surrenders) was more disgraceful than the field performance itself.

**Grounding quote:** "On 10 October 1809, Napoleon drove in the Prussian advance guard and compelled Brunswick to fall back on his lines of communications. The divided Prussian Army promptly was overwhelmed in the twin battles at Jena and Auerstädt. ... The real disgrace was the collapse of the army and state in the days after the defeat; the panic, the surrender of strong fortresses, the abject servility of the civil servants towards the conquerors." (Ch 6, Opponents of the French, Prussian section)

### Range3 - Prussian Reforms 1807–1813: Scharnhorst, Krümper System, and New Brigade Tactics
Defeat forced radical overhaul. Scharnhorst led the Military Reorganization Commission; a major officer purge occurred. The Krümper system trained supernumeraries briefly for reserves (total numbers trained remained modest). Mixed brigades (all arms, standardized strength) became the basic unit; two brigades formed a corps in war. Infantry tactics emphasized phased commitment: fusilier skirmishers/soutiens, musketeer attack columns with half-batteries, grenadier/musketeer follow-on elements, and cavalry/horse artillery reserve. Landwehr (militia) was raised locally but initially suffered severe shortages; it fought with élan by late 1813 after integration with regulars.

**Grounding quote:** "After 1806, however, the reformer hoped to develop a general staff system in which collective intelligence would offset thejndividual genius of Napoleon. ... The Prussian reformers also introduced permanent higher tactical formations... In battle, the brigade deployed its two fusilier battalions partly in skirmish line, partly as support detachments, soutiens, about 150 paces behind. Then, another 300 paces back, came the first major combat element, three musketeer battalions, normally in attack columns some 40 men wide..." (Ch 6, Opponents of the French, Prussian reforms)

### Range3 - Russian Army Characteristics, Strengths, and Limitations
The Russian army relied on serf conscription (often a day of mourning for recruits), brutal/erratic discipline (knout, gauntlet), religious motivation, and high fighting qualities among common soldiers. It featured strong artillery (many guns, massed fire doctrine) and large Cossack irregular cavalry (cheap but limited). Officer corps suffered divisions (aristocrats vs. foreigners), pretentious staff work, poor high-level planning, and rivalries. Reorganization under Alexander I created divisions and some corps, but the army remained slow, encumbered by huge inefficient trains, conservative in tactics (bayonet emphasis due to musket issues), and ill-suited for sustained operations far from its base. It contributed decisively in 1812 but was hesitant afterward.

**Grounding quote:** "Overall, though tough, numerous, and formidable in defence of their own country or in fighting its traditional enemies, the Russian army was ill-equipped for operations in areas far away from its own centre of power. ... One British observer, Major General Campbell, commented that the Russians were encumbered with an enormous, but inefficient, train, ‘resembling a horde of Asiatics’. ... These shortcomings were accompanied by conservative fighting methods." (Ch 6, Opponents of the French, Russian army)

### Range3 - French Staff Superiority and Persistent Technical/Organizational Limits (All Armies)
Pre-1792 trends continued; France led in staff doctrine (Bourcet influence) and execution via Berthier (technical translator of Napoleon's decisions, masterful at detailed movement planning, e.g., Danube bridges at Aspern). Opponents (Austrian, Prussian, Russian) had weaker permanent staffs and suffered from monarchs and entourages interfering. All armies faced slow communications (man/horse), unreliable signals, inadequate maps, and road constraints. Wellington had strong field intelligence (cavalry, guerrillas, Grant) but still faced limits. Staff service bred line-officer jealousy everywhere.

**Grounding quote:** "Berthier’s role was purely technical, he did not have advisory functions, but merely translated the imperial orders into practice. ... Although Napoleon has been faulted for his over-centralized command style, French staff work remained superior to that of his opponents until 1812. Austrian, Prussian, and Russian staff work was poor for most of the period... Staff work throughout the period was hampered by technical limitations. Communications remained slow, largely dependent on the speed of man and horse." (Ch 7, Staff Problems...)

### Range3 - Siege Warfare, Fortifications, and the Lines of Torres Vedras
Despite the era's mobile-war reputation, sieges remained numerous and fierce. Classic Vauban trace (star fort with bastions, ditches, parallels, saps, breaching batteries) persisted; outworks and detached forts delayed attackers. Wellington's Lines of Torres Vedras (natural strength + engineering, protecting Lisbon base) exemplified effective fortified camps. Field works were usually simple open-rear earthworks. Urban/street fighting (e.g., Saragossa 1808–9) turned houses/convents into strongpoints, leading to high engineer casualties and atrocities after assaults (Badajoz, San Sebastian sacks by British; similar French/Russian incidents). Engineers were chronically short across armies.

**Grounding quote:** "By contrast, Wellington’s famous Lines of Torres Vedras not only had great natural strength, but they also protected his major port and, if necessary, evacuation beach. ... Such a siege took place at Saragossa from 19 December 1808 to 20 February 1809. ... After opening the siege it took the besiegers 29 days to break through the main line, only to find themselves confronted by a ring of fortified convents, public buildings, churches and houses." (Ch 7, Fortifications section)

### Range3 - Medical Services, Casualty Evacuation, and Disease as Decisive Factor
French services (Larrey's flying ambulances, Percy's Wurst wagons/stretcher bearers) were relatively advanced but still inadequate; Guard priority care was better but collapsed in Russia. Other armies lagged (few ambulances, reliance on civilian hospitals or mules/oxcarts; Russian Feldschers distrusted). Battlefield evacuation was primitive; surgeons performed rapid amputations under horrific conditions. Disease killed far more than combat (estimates of 10:1 overstated but directionally correct); key examples include Walcheren fever (thousands dead from malaria with minimal battle losses) and the 1812 Russian campaign. British service improved markedly under McGrigor (hospitals, statistics, evacuation) with Wellington's support, though still imperfect.

**Grounding quote:** "Disease, of course, killed or disabled more men than did battles and physicians at the time estimated that for every combat death there were ten from disease. ... Larrey’s invention became the basis for the French medical evacuation vehicles during the Empire. ... ‘The only conveyance for these poor cripples with broken arms and legs,’ Bell wrote, ‘were some mules sent up by the commissary.’ ... Of the 39,214 men sent to Walcheren in April 1809, only 4,000 remained fit for duty when the army was withdrawn in December. There were but 106 battle casualties, but 4,175 died from the ‘Walcheren fever’." (Ch 7, Medical Services)

## Volume Synthesis Note
Robert M. Rothenberg’s *The Art of Warfare in the Age of Napoleon* (1978) supplies the essential institutional and doctrinal layer for the Napoleonic period that complements the operational focus of Chandler and the experiential/battalion-level detail of Muir. It traces the shift from *ancien régime* limited war (manoeuvre, magazines, small professional forces, brutal discipline, avoidance of battle) to Revolutionary mass mobilization (levée en masse, amalgame, ordre mixte combining skirmishers, line, and column) and Napoleon’s refinement (permanent corps d’armée, Imperial Guard as loyalty instrument, centralized yet flexible command). Comparative chapters on coalition opponents highlight systemic weaknesses (Prussian ossification before 1806 reforms, Russian logistical drag, Austrian caution) while noting British advantages in cash-paid logistics and allied auxiliaries (KGL, Portuguese). Technical and support sections cover Gribeauval artillery, staff (Berthier’s translator role), fortifications/sieges (continued importance, Torres Vedras), and medical realities (disease far outstripping battle deaths; Larrey’s ambulances). The appendices provide useful tabulated data on major actions. Rothenberg’s work is strongest on the “fighting level” and army-as-institution rather than grand strategy or specific battles; it reinforces that political/social changes, not weapons alone, drove the transformation. It enriches the warfare hub with precise descriptions of army structures and the soldier’s world, and supplies ready cross-references for existing battle analyses (Austerlitz, Jena, Wagram, Leipzig, Waterloo). Extraction artifacts (mojibake) were minor and quotes were grounded faithfully.
