# Delbrück, History of the Art of War, Vol. III: Medieval Warfare (1982 trans.)

Hans Delbrück (1848–1929) wrote military history as **critical history**: every battle account is
tested against what was physically and logistically possible. Volume III applies that method —
[[sachkritik|Sachkritik]] — to the millennium from Charlemagne to the Burgundian Wars, and reaches
two organising conclusions: that with the rise of the **mounted knight** the disciplined *tactical
body* of antiquity **dissolved** (knights fought as individual heroes, not as a drilled unit), and
that the Late Middle Ages saw its **rebirth** in new infantry systems — the Swiss pike square above
all. See [[delbruck-hans|Delbrück (actor)]] for the man and the method's later influence.

## Delbrück's central theses (for this ingest)

1. **Sachkritik and the numbers.** Chronicle army sizes are systematically impossible; Delbrück
   re-estimates them from road capacity, marching order, and supply. Charlemagne's and the
   Lechfeld armies were a few thousand, not the tens or hundreds of thousands of the sources.
2. **The dissolution of the tactical body.** The armoured knight fought for personal honour as an
   individual; medieval armies lacked the drilled cohesion of the Roman legion or Greek phalanx —
   "tactical bodies" gave way to a sum of single combats.
3. **The rebirth of infantry.** Disciplined mass infantry returned in three late-medieval forms —
   the **Swiss pike phalanx**, the **English longbow** formation (dismounted men-at-arms + archers),
   and the Hussite **Wagenburg** — the immediate ancestors of the early-modern [[military-revolution]].
4. **Constitution and army.** Throughout, military organisation is read as an expression of
   political constitution (the book's subtitle: "within the framework of political history").
