# The New Cambridge Medieval History, Volume 1: c. 500–c. 700 (2005)

*The New Cambridge Medieval History, Volume 1: c. 500–c. 700*, edited by Paul Fouracre. Cambridge University Press, 2005.

This volume provides the primary academic backbone for the transition from the late Roman world to the Early Middle Ages. Chronologically, it begins around 500 CE (the transition to post-Roman regimes in the West) and closes around 700 CE (the threshold of the Carolingian rise and the consolidation of the Umayyad Caliphate).

## Volume Synthesis Note

**Overall argument.** The New Cambridge Medieval History, Volume 1 stands as the definitive English-language reference for the transition from Late Antiquity to the Early Middle Ages (c. 500–700 CE). Its guiding interpretive framework — rooted in the "Transformation of the Roman World" paradigm developed in the 1990s European Science Foundation project — consistently argues for **continuity, fusion, and adaptation** over catastrophic discontinuity. The Germanic kingdoms did not "destroy" Rome; they appropriated, distorted, and ultimately preserved key Roman institutional forms — the written law code, the episcopal church, the Latin language, the idea of territorial sovereignty — in new political configurations. Similarly, the Arab conquests did not eradicate the Mediterranean world; they restructured it, redirecting the economic and cultural flows that had previously unified the northern and southern shores. The volume's most powerful contribution to the wiki is this sustained anti-catastrophist reading of events that superficially appear as collapse.

**Cross-section connections emerging at volume level.** Several themes run invisibly across the volume's geographic-thematic structure and become visible only in retrospect. (1) The **wergild/Eigenkirche/leges nationum complex** (Cycle 4, Chs. 20–24) is the legal and ecclesiastical infrastructure for the political transformation traced in the regional narrative chapters (Cycles 2–3): the same devolution from Roman central authority that Wickham describes as producing the land-based aristocracy produced, in the religious sphere, the proprietary church system. These are two faces of the same structural transformation. (2) The **Christianisation narrative** across Cycles 3–4 (Anglo-Saxon conversion, Irish missions, the Synod of Whitby) is the British Isles counterpart of the continental ecclesiastical consolidation described in the Frankish and Visigothic chapters — and the theological debates (Monothelitism for the East, Easter calculation for the North) are parallel symptoms of the same structural challenge: how to maintain doctrinal unity across a now-fragmented political space. (3) The **North Sea commercial revival** (wics, sceattas — Cycle 4) is the economic counterpart of the Pippinid/Arnulfing political consolidation described in Cycle 3 (the Battle of Tertry, 687): both are seventh-century northwestern European developments that point toward the Carolingian synthesis of the eighth century, which lies just beyond this volume's chronological horizon.

**What this volume adds to the wiki.** Before NCMH I, the wiki's coverage of the period 500–700 CE rested primarily on [[cameron-cah-v14-2000|CAH XIV]] (Late Antiquity to c. 600 CE) and [[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|CWH IV]] (the global frame). NCMH I fills the critical seventh-century gap that CAH XIV barely touches (CAH XIV ends at c. 600) and provides regional depth that CWH IV's global panorama cannot supply. Specifically, it provides: the only systematic treatment of Merovingian political devolution and the rise of the Mayors of the Palace; the foundational narrative of the British Isles' Christianisation; the primary source-based analysis of the barbarian law codes; the Pirenne thesis controversy in its proper evidentiary context; and the art-historical and numismatic frameworks (Insular art, sceattas) that are absent from all other ingested volumes. It also flags a specific narrative gap: the planned chapter on seventh-century Lombard Italy was never submitted (Preface, p. xiii), leaving this wiki's coverage of that topic dependent on the Cycle 2 Lombard Invasion chapter and on future specialist ingestion.

**Cross-volume tensions.** (1) **NCMH I vs. CAH XIV on the pace of post-Roman change**: CAH XIV (Cameron et al.) tends to emphasize the dramatic disruption of the fifth- and sixth-century transitions and is more willing to describe the loss of Roman administrative capacity as a genuine "fall." NCMH I's contributors (especially Halsall, Wickham) more strongly resist the collapse narrative, treating the Germanic kingdoms as essentially functional successor states. Both readings are present in the wiki; the [[causes-of-the-fall-of-the-western-roman-empire]] controversy page captures the interpretive tension. (2) **NCMH I vs. CWH IV on the Pirenne question**: [[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|CWH IV]] (Benjamin) frames the Early Middle Ages as a period of expanding Afro-Eurasian connectivity, which is broadly consistent with the Post-Pirrenne McCormick synthesis (continued but transformed Mediterranean contact plus North Sea growth). NCMH I's economic chapters (Loseby, Lebecq, Blackburn) provide the empirical detail that grounds this global argument in the specific mechanisms of the wic network and the sceatta coinage. No contradiction, but complementary resolution levels. (3) **The missing Lombard chapter**: Until a specialist volume on seventh-century Italy is ingested, the wiki's Lombard narrative remains thinner for the 7th century than for the 6th — this is flagged in [[early-middle-ages]] and in this source page, and constitutes a priority target for future ingestion (see `Outstanding Sources.md`).
