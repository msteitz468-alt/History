# The Chinese Neolithic: Trajectories to Early States (Li Liu, 2004)

A processual archaeological study of the formation of complex societies in
prehistoric China, c. 7000–1500 BC, concentrated on the middle and lower
[[yellow-river-valley|Yellow River valley]] — the region in which the first
Chinese states evolved. Liu reads settlement and burial patterns through
anthropological theory (agency + process, chiefdom-to-state evolution, cycling)
and argues that social change in Neolithic China was driven by **multiple
interacting factors in no predetermined sequence**, against both unilineal
Marxist evolutionism and the cultural-historical nationalism that has dominated
Chinese archaeology. Its analytic core is the [[longshan-culture|Longshan]] period
(3rd millennium BC) as the platform for state emergence, culminating in the
[[erlitou-culture|Erlitou culture]] as the earliest archaeologically demonstrable
state.

This page anchors the wiki's coverage of early Chinese social complexity. It
complements [[cambridge-history-of-ancient-china]] (textual + art-historical
backbone) and [[maisels-early-civilizations-of-the-old-world-1999]] (comparative
checklist framing), supplying the settlement-archaeology and social-evolutionary
depth those lack.

## Argument in Brief

- The "**Longshan culture**" is a constructed archaeological category, not a single
  people; its interpretation has tracked both new ceramic finds and the politics of
  building a national history (see [[nationalism-and-archaeology]],
  [[quxi-leixing]]).
- Longshan-period polities reached real socio-political complexity (Taosi, Rizhao)
  but remained **small and cyclical** — they rose and collapsed without producing a
  durable internally specialized ruling apparatus.
- The standard "**checklist**" definition of the state (writing, metallurgy, cities,
  stratification) is rejected as a criterion: these traits appear individually,
  asynchronously, and without clear social correlates in Neolithic China. Liu
  measures complexity instead by **settlement hierarchy and mortuary differentiation**
  ([[settlement-archaeology]]).
- By that measure the **Erlitou culture** (~1900–1500 BC) is the first state: a
  four-tier settlement hierarchy with a primate centre (Erlitou, 300 ha), a palatial
  complex, and specialised bronze/ritual production — *regardless* of whether it was
  the [[xia-dynasty|Xia dynasty]].
- State formation in the [[central-plains-china|Central Plains]] is best modelled as
  **competitive cycling among peer polities** punctuated by climatic and
  environmental shifts, not as a single linear ascent (see
  [[early-china-state-formation]]).

## Volume Synthesis Note

Liu's central achievement is to make **settlement archaeology** the arbiter of social
complexity in early China, displacing two older frameworks: the cultural-historical /
nationalist reading that matches pottery horizons to textual dynasties, and the Marxist
unilineal evolutionism that forced societies up a fixed matrilineal-to-patrilineal,
egalitarian-to-class ladder. In their place she reads **household, community, and regional
settlement and mortuary patterns**, yielding a picture of [[longshan-culture|Longshan]] society
as a mosaic of small, **cyclical [[chiefdom|chiefdoms]]** whose rise and decline left no
durable ruling apparatus.

The book's most important and counterintuitive argument is that the first state was **created,
not evolved, and not from the most complex chiefdom**. The grandest Longshan polity,
[[taosi|Taosi]] (300 ha, primate rank-size, ~3,300 km²), **collapsed** and became a periphery;
the first state — [[erlitou-culture|Erlitou]] in the [[yiluo-basin|Yiluo basin]] — grew instead
from the *less* integrated, corporate, group-oriented competing systems of central Henan. This
is the empirical heart of [[early-china-state-formation]] and a general caution (relevant to
[[mesoamerican-state-formation]] and [[andean-state-formation]]) against treating statehood as
the apex of a complexity gradient.

Methodologically, Liu **rejects the trait "checklist"** definition of the state. Writing,
metallurgy, walls, and stratification appear in Neolithic China individually, asynchronously,
and without clear social correlates — bronze, for example, predates any tie to hierarchy by
~1,000 years. She substitutes Wright's criterion of an internally specialised, **delegated
administration**, archaeologically visible as Erlitou's four-tier hierarchy, palatial complex,
and attached bronze workshops. This lets her sidestep the [[xia-dynasty-historicity|Xia
question]]: Erlitou is a state whether or not it was the [[xia-dynasty|Xia]].

Across the wiki the volume supplies the **settlement-archaeology and social-evolutionary
spine** that the [[cambridge-history-of-ancient-china|Cambridge History of Ancient China]]
(textual/art-historical) and [[maisels-early-civilizations-of-the-old-world-1999|Maisels
(1999)]] (comparative checklist) leave thin. One cross-source tension is worth flagging: Maisels
applies a Childe-style **urban-revolution checklist** to early China, exactly the approach Liu
argues against — a productive disagreement now recorded on [[state-formation]] and
[[xia-dynasty-historicity]]. Liu predates the fullest publication of [[liangzhu-culture|Liangzhu]]
and the discovery of Shimao, so her peripheral and lower-Yangzi data are dated in places; her
**model**, not her gazetteer, is what this ingest preserves.
