# Stalin, Volume 2: Waiting for Hitler, 1929–1941 (Stephen Kotkin, 2017)

Second volume of Stephen Kotkin’s landmark biography. Covers the period when Stalin moves from consolidating personal dictatorship to violently reshaping Soviet Eurasia and positioning the USSR as a great power on the eve of war with Nazi Germany. Stalin is now omnipresent: micromanaging the party-state, grain collections, armaments, foreign policy, and cultural affairs. The book is largely set in his office and mind.

Central episodes per Kotkin: (1) 1929–33 collectivization of agriculture and the accompanying social/economic catastrophe; (2) the August 1939 Pact with Hitler and its consequences. The 1936–38 Great Terror is treated as a defining feature of the regime but not the single central episode. Parallel biographies of Stalin and Hitler (eleven years his junior); the two dictators from the peripheries whose regimes draw closer. “It was not only the German people who turned out to be waiting for Hitler.”

Rich evidentiary base: Stalin’s working personal archive (RGASPI), colossal party/state records, holiday correspondence with detailed instructions, real-time notetakers, published congress/reception transcripts, press, selective access publications by others, and international archives.

## Volume Synthesis Note
Kotkin positions 1929–33 collectivization (and the social catastrophe it produced) and the 1939 Pact as the two defining episodes of Stalin’s regime in this period. The 1936–38 Terror is the regime’s most notorious feature but is analyzed as the logical extension of “terror as statecraft” rather than the singular pivot. The volume is set largely inside Stalin’s office and mind, with rich use of visitor logs, holiday correspondence, and archival granularity. Parallel lives of Stalin and Hitler culminate in the intersection that reshapes Eurasia. The Coda captures Stalin’s final pre-war miscalculations and the human/system limits exposed on 22 June 1941. Complements Vol. 1 by showing the mature despotism and great-power maneuvering; deepens understanding of how the party-state machinery and Stalin’s personal control produced both rapid industrialization/military buildup and catastrophic human cost + strategic surprise.
