# The Dutch and English East India Companies: Diplomacy, Trade and Violence in Early Modern Asia

Edited by **Adam Clulow** and **Tristan Mostert** (Amsterdam University Press,
2018; Asian History series). An edited volume of nine essays plus introduction,
grown out of a December 2015 Heidelberg conference. It studies the
[[dutch-east-india-company|VOC]] and the [[english-east-india-company|EIC]]
**as a pair** — reviving the older comparative habit of Masselman and Furber —
across Southeast, East, and South Asia.

## Central Argument

The companies were **formidable but not exceptional**. Founded in the first decade
of the seventeenth century and gifted expansive powers (to conduct diplomacy, raise
armies, seize territory), they nonetheless "did not move into an empty arena."
Early modern Asia "stood at the centre of the global economy and was crowded with
powerful states that wielded economic, military and cultural resources that
outstripped the most influential polities in Europe." The companies' "sharpest
weapons" were repeatedly "unexpectedly blunted"; for every success there were
failures. Their longevity is explained not by European superiority but by a
**continual process of adaptation and accommodation to existing Asian structures**.
Following Philip Stern, the editors urge assessing the EIC as a "body-politic on
its own terms" rather than a trading firm that strayed into empire; following
Blussé, Wills, and Andrade, they place the companies firmly inside Asian networks
of goods, people, and diplomacy.

The volume's recurring tension: the companies were **"powerful on the waves but
weak on land"** — a structural weakness that shaped diplomacy, drove dependence on
local brokers, and explains both the recruitment of Asian mercenaries (Clulow) and
the use of company ships as "navies for hire" by Asian rulers (Good).

## Volume Synthesis Note

The volume's single argument, sustained across nine essays, is that the Dutch and
English East India Companies were **formidable but not exceptional** — interlopers in
a crowded, dynamic, wealthy early modern Asia whose longevity owed to *adaptation*,
not domination. The organising image is "powerful on the waves but weak on land,"
which the chapters successively complicate: Clulow shows the VOC so short of soldiers
it imported Japanese mercenaries; Good shows even the EIC's maritime power turned to
an Asian ruler's ends under Nader Shah; van Meersbergen and Matsukata show the
companies as supplicants in Mughal and Tokugawa diplomatic systems they did not
control; Chaiklin and Nadri show them dependent on Indian craftsmen, demand, and
broker-financiers; van Ittersum insists that where the companies *could* dictate
terms (Banda), they did so through devastating violence, not commerce. Andrade's
epilogue makes the historiographical case explicit, tracing the field from Marx and
Weber's "transformative VOC" to today's consensus on Asian resilience and parallel
"global early modernisation."

For the wiki this source does three things. First, it **deepens the gap regions** the
collection is weakest on — early modern South and Southeast Asia, Tokugawa foreign
relations, and Safavid/Afsharid Persia — with concrete, archivally-grounded detail
(new pages on Makassar, Ternate, Bengal, Surat, Bombay, Nader Shah). Second, it
**reframes the two company actor pages** away from the older "first modern
corporation" triumphalism toward the company-state-as-interloper reading already
latent in the wiki's [[interpolity-law]] and [[early-modern-commerce]] pages. Third,
it promotes a **standing controversy** —
[[european-exceptionalism-early-modern-asia]] — that connects to existing material on
the [[indian-ocean-trade|Indian Ocean trade]], Reid's age-of-commerce thesis on the
VOC page, and Tracy's CWH VI point (already on both company pages) that the companies
*depended on* the Asian world they entered. There is no sharp contradiction with
already-ingested sources; rather, Clulow & Mostert supply the micro-level evidence for
a revisionist consensus the wiki had only stated at the synthesis level.
