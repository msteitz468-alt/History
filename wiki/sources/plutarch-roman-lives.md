# Plutarch, *Roman Lives* (a selection of eight)

An Oxford World's Classics selection of eight **Roman Lives** from Plutarch's *Parallel Lives* — the
Roman halves of his paired Greek-and-Roman biographies. The fourth Phase-4 primary source. (The
collection holds this *Roman Lives* selection in place of the Processing List's "Greek Lives"; both
are selections from the one work. For Plutarch the man and the design of the *Parallel Lives*, see the
author page **[[plutarch|Plutarch of Chaeronea]]**.)

## The Eight Lives

Spanning the Roman Republic from its expansion to its fall:

| Life | Subject | Greek pair |
|---|---|---|
| **Cato the Elder** | the censor and moralist (234–149) | Aristides |
| **Aemilius Paullus** | victor over Macedon at [[battle-of-pydna-168bce|Pydna]] (168) | Timoleon |
| **Tiberius and Gaius Gracchus** | the reforming [[tiberius-gracchus|tribunes]] (133, 123–121) | Agis & Cleomenes |
| **Marius** | the seven-times consul and army reformer (157–86) | Pyrrhus |
| **Sulla** | the dictator (138–78) | Lysander |
| **Pompey** | "the Great" (106–48) | Agesilaus |
| **Caesar** | the conqueror and dictator (100–44) | Alexander |
| **Antony** | the triumvir (83–30) | Demetrius |

Together they form Plutarch's portrait gallery of the **late Republic's crisis** — from the
disciplined expansion embodied by Cato and Aemilius to the ambition, civil war and collapse embodied
by Marius, Sulla, Pompey, Caesar and Antony.

## Method and Use

Stadter stresses Plutarch's **biographical purpose**: each Life opens with family, nature and
formative influences, builds through a rhetorically-shaped (not strictly chronological) narrative
around a few peaks, and closes at the subject's death, using **anecdotes** to reveal *ēthos*. He
**focalizes through his protagonist** (so Pompey, Caesar and Antony each appear differently in their
own Lives), adapts his source-histories freely (abridging, conflating, compressing, personalizing),
and shapes each Roman Life to **parallel its Greek pair** — Pompey's ruinous love of honour mirroring
Agesilaus's, Antony's susceptibility mirroring Demetrius's. The result is a source of the first
importance for the late Republic, to be read with awareness of its moral and artistic design.
