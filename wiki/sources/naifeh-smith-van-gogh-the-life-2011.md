# Van Gogh: The Life (Naifeh & Smith, 2011)

**Source page for Steven Naifeh and Gregory White Smith, *Van Gogh: The Life* (Random House / Profile Books, 2011).**

The most comprehensive modern biography of Vincent Willem van Gogh (1853–1890). Using the brothers' letters as the central spine and adding extensive archival and medical research, Naifeh & Smith present Vincent as a man of "fanatic heart" whose all-or-nothing intensity drove both repeated vocational failures and the creation of an art of unprecedented emotional and chromatic force. The book is particularly notable for its detailed reconstruction of the final weeks in Auvers and its conclusion that Vincent was shot accidentally by local boys rather than by his own hand.

## Methodological Notes

- Primary reliance on the nearly 900 surviving letters between Vincent and Theo (plus family correspondence), many quoted at length.
- Dutch and French archives, medical records from Arles, Saint-Rémy, and Auvers, and re-interviews with descendants and local witnesses (especially around the death).
- Heavy use of earlier scholarship (Hulsker dual biography, Doiteau on the Secrétan brothers, Rewald oral histories) while offering a new synthesis on the shooting.
- Strong biographical-psychological framing: temperament as the key to both life trajectory and artistic choices.
- Limited formal analysis of individual paintings; the emphasis is on the life that produced them.

## Key Contributions

- The fullest account of the Zundert childhood, the parsonage culture, and the early failures as art dealer, lay preacher (Borinage), and bookseller.
- Detailed treatment of the Dutch years (The Hague with Sien, Nuenen weavers, *The Potato Eaters*, Antwerp academy attempt) as the foundation of Vincent's commitment to peasant subjects and dark palette.
- Paris 1886–88: the decisive exposure to Impressionism, divisionism, Japanese prints, and the move to a lighter, brighter, more broken color.
- Arles 1888: the explosion of production (200+ works), the Yellow House project, the Gauguin visit and rupture, the ear incident, and institutionalization.
- Saint-Rémy 1889–90 and Auvers 1890: the disciplined late output amid mental crises; the final canvases; the disputed shooting of 27 July 1890 and Theo's immediate aftermath and collapse.
- The "fanatic heart" thesis as the unifying explanation for both vocational disasters and artistic breakthrough.
