# Che Guevara: A Revolutionary Life (Anderson, 1997 / rev. 2010)

The definitive English-language biography of Ernesto "Che" Guevara (1928–1967), Argentine physician turned revolutionary, central figure in the Cuban Revolution (1956–1959), architect of the "new man" ideology in post-victory Cuba, and architect of failed attempts to export armed revolution to the Congo (1965) and Bolivia (1966–1967). Anderson's work demythologizes while documenting the man behind the Korda icon: his asthma, his intellectual formation, his evolution from traveler to Marxist guerrilla to state minister to internationalist foco theorist, his role in La Cabaña executions, his break with the Cuban leadership, and the circumstances of his capture and execution in Bolivia.

This source directly addresses the wiki's documented gap in post-1750 Americas narrative coverage (see overview.md and places/latin-america-modern.md). It supplies the first high-resolution treatment of the Cuban Revolution as event/process (previously noted as lacking dedicated pages) and the central actor page for Che himself. It will update [[periods/cold-war|Cold War]] (Third World/ Latin American fronts, foco vs. protracted people's war debates), [[places/cuba]], and related Cold War pages while creating the core nodes for Latin American revolutionary movements.

## Argument in Brief

- Che's life was a deliberate arc from bourgeois Argentine youth through transformative travels (motorcycle diaries, leprosy work, Guatemala 1954 coup) to full commitment as a professional revolutionary alongside Fidel Castro.
- In the Cuban Revolution he was both military commander (Santa Clara decisive) and political enforcer ("Supreme Prosecutor" at La Cabaña); his writings and practice crystallized the *foco* (foquismo) theory: a small armed vanguard could ignite revolution without waiting for objective conditions.
- As a Cuban minister he pursued radical economic policies and the "new man" (hombre nuevo) ideal of selfless socialist consciousness; these collided with Soviet-aligned pragmatism, leading to his 1965 departure letter and internationalist campaigns.
- The Congo and Bolivia expeditions were practical tests of foco theory; both failed due to local party opposition, terrain/logistics, and US/Bolivian counterinsurgency (with CIA involvement). Che's death became the seed of his global myth.
- Anderson stresses paradoxes: Che the humanist physician vs. the executioner; the romantic vs. the ruthless tactician; the internationalist who died isolated. The myth has largely detached from the documented man.

## Historiography Note (for source page)

Anderson's is the first major post-Cold War, non-Cuban, non-polemical biography with primary access that had been denied earlier researchers. It supersedes earlier hagiographies (e.g. Cuban official accounts) and hostile works. It draws on Che's own diaries (motorcycle, Sierra, Congo, Bolivia) and letters. Later scholarship (e.g. on foco theory in revolutionary war literature) builds on or critiques the practice described here. The 1997 exhumation and repatriation material is unique to Anderson's on-the-ground reporting. Strong on the personal/psychological; thinner on macroeconomic debates inside revolutionary Cuba (complements other Cold War economic sources). No major contradictions with core CHCW or strategy sources on the broader context; supplies the granular Che-centric narrative they lack.
