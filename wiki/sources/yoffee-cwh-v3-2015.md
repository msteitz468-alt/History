# The Cambridge World History, Volume III: Early Cities in Comparative Perspective, 4000 BCE–1200 CE

**Editor:** Norman Yoffee (Professor Emeritus, Near Eastern Studies & Anthropology,
University of Michigan; ISAW, NYU). **Series editor-in-chief:** Merry E. Wiesner-Hanks.
Cambridge University Press, 2015. ~670 pp., 27 chapters in 6 thematic parts.

## Series Note

Volume III of the seven-volume Cambridge World History. Where [[christian-cwh-v1-2015|Vol. I]]
covered to 10,000 BCE and [[barker-cwh-v2-2015|Vol. II]] covered the spread of
agriculture, Vol. III addresses the transformation by which "the world became a
world of cities," from the earliest cities in Mesopotamia and Egypt (4th millennium
BCE) through the imperial and medieval cities of Rome, Baghdad, Jerusalem,
Tenochtitlan, and Cahokia. It is the direct successor to [[agriculture-and-urbanism]]
(the urbanism thread opened in Vol. II) and the principal foundation for the wiki's
[[urbanization]] process and [[places/|places]] directory.

## Structure of the Volume

A comparative experiment. Six thematic parts, each = three specialist city
case studies + one co-written synthesis chapter. The editor's premise (Ch 1):
early cities can and should be compared cross-culturally, following V. Gordon
Childe's "urban revolution" (1950) but moving beyond trait-lists toward
questions of performance, information, landscape, power, creation, and empire.

## Volume Synthesis Note

*The Cambridge World History, Volume III* is a deliberate **experiment in
comparison**: rather than a regional or chronological survey, it isolates six
*features* of early urban life — performance, information technology, landscape,
the distribution of power, creation, and empire — and tests each against three or
four city case studies drawn from across the world and across four and a half
millennia. Its provocative juxtapositions (Rome with Tenochtitlan, Athens with
Jenne-jeno, Jerusalem with Cahokia) are the point: Yoffee's wager is that early
cities *can* be compared without either essentializing "the city" or denying each
city its distinct evolutionary trajectory. For this wiki the volume is the
foundation of the [[urbanization]] process, the [[urbanism]] concept, and the
entire [[memphis|places/]] directory (15 city pages — the directory was empty
before this ingest).

The volume's strongest cross-cultural claim concerns **information technology**:
writing originated as a tool of *economic administration*, not literature or
religion. [[uruk|Uruk]]'s proto-cuneiform grew out of tokens, seals, and ration
bowls; [[anyang|Anyang]]'s oracle bones, for all their ritual content, are shot
through with bookkeeping; and the Inka **khipu** proves that a complex empire
could be run with no phonetic writing at all. Its most important *analytical*
advance is **[[heterarchy]]**: the demonstration, above all at
[[jenne-jeno|Jenne-jeno]] and [[teotihuacan|Teotihuacan]], that large, dense,
complex cities did not all concentrate power in a despotic ruler. This overturns
the top-down assumptions built into Childe's [[urban-revolution]] and the
"oriental city," and supplies the wiki with the [[hierarchy-vs-heterarchy-early-cities]]
and [[what-defines-a-city]] controversies. A second recurring move is the
**dissolution of the urban–rural dichotomy**: urbanization is always also
[[ruralization]] (Uruk's central-place hinterland, Teotihuacan's Axotlan,
Tiwanaku's qochas, the Aztec altepetl).

Read against the volumes already ingested, Vol. III is the **direct successor to
the [[agriculture-and-urbanism]] thread** opened in [[barker-cwh-v2-2015|Vol. II]]:
the agricultural surplus and storage institutions of the [[neolithic|Neolithic]]
are the precondition for the first cities, and the same independent hearths
(Mesopotamia, China, Mesoamerica, the Andes, the Indus, West Africa) recur in a
new key. It also opens the wiki's first substantial coverage of the
[[chalcolithic|Chalcolithic]] and [[early-bronze-age|Early Bronze Age]] periods —
though only thematically, through the lens of cities. Those period pages remain
**spine entries to be deepened** by the Cambridge Ancient History (Phase 2) and
[[barker-cwh-v2-2015|the later CWH volumes]]; the volume is comparative and
city-centric, not a narrative of states, dynasties, or events, and the wiki should
not over-read period-level confidence from it.

Finally, the volume is methodologically self-aware in ways worth carrying forward.
Its contributors repeatedly warn against **analytical anachronism** (Houston &
Garrison on importing Chicago-school "middle classes" and "sprawl" onto the Maya;
Terrenato on the [[eurocentrism|Eurocentric]] over-attention to Rome). Yoffee's
conclusion adds the experiential criterion that "cities must be awesome" and
stresses that early cities matter in the present because they **instantiate
historical imagination** — a theme captured in [[founded-cities]] and the
[[jerusalem|Jerusalem]]/[[baghdad|Baghdad]]/[[cahokia|Cahokia]] cases. The one
cross-volume tension to flag: the volume's heterarchical, anti-essentialist
treatment of the city sits against the more hierarchical state-formation narrative
that the CAH and CWH IV will bring — a productive friction to track when those
volumes are ingested.
