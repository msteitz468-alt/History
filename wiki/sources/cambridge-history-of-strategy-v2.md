# The Cambridge History of Strategy, Volume II (2024)

The second volume carries the comparative history of **strategic practice** from the
Napoleonic Wars to the twenty-first century. With Volume I it forms a single project; the
conceptual and comparative payoff is captured on **[[strategy]]** and
**[[strategic-practice-across-civilizations]]**.

## Argument

Around the **age of revolutions** strategy making changed: the Enlightenment, the Industrial
Revolution, mass conscript armies, and the consolidation of bordered **territorial states**
reshaped how ends were linked to means, and international law increasingly cast the **state**
as the only legitimate war-maker — a norm then contested by non-state actors (guerrillas,
revolutionaries, terrorists, would-be caliphates). Yet the volume's recurring finding is
**continuity in the messiness of practice**: from Napoleon (who "had little time for"
dialogue and signalling) through the American Civil War and the World Wars, contributors
repeatedly find strategy "haphazard and incoherent if not lacking altogether," better
described as **muddling through** than as far-sighted prioritisation. Its Conclusion
("Practising Strategy: Problems and Challenges") names the recurring failures: treating
strategy as one-sided, the **instability of objectives**, and the **faulty alignment** of
objectives and resources.

## What it contributes

- Completes the **[[strategy]]** concept and the **[[strategic-practice-across-civilizations]]**
  comparison (the modern half of the evidence base).
- A pointer to **modern-era gaps**: its chapters on the World Wars, the Cold War, nuclear
  strategy, decolonisation, and recent wars mark topics the wiki's post-1800 coverage should
  later build out from dedicated sources (the Cambridge histories of WWI, WWII, and the Cold
  War are present in the collection for this).
