# Xenophon, *The Expedition of Cyrus* (*Anabasis*)

"The most exciting adventure to survive from the ancient world" (Rood) — and the **first known
military memoir**. The *Anabasis Kyrou* ("Cyrus's March Up Country") narrates the revolt of the Persian
prince **Cyrus the Younger** against his brother King Artaxerxes II, his death at the battle of
**Cunaxa (401 BCE)**, and the epic fighting retreat of his stranded Greek mercenaries — the **Ten
Thousand** — across Mesopotamia, Kurdistan and the Armenian highlands to the Black Sea. The fifth
Phase-4 primary source. For the author see **[[xenophon|Xenophon of Athens]]**; for the events,
**[[march-of-the-ten-thousand-401bce|the march of the Ten Thousand]]**.

## The Work

Seven books: Cyrus's march from Sardis to Mesopotamia and his death at Cunaxa (Books 1–2); the
treacherous murder of the Greek generals and the election of new commanders — Xenophon among them
(3); the march through the mountains to the famous cry of **"The sea! The sea!" (*Thalatta!
Thalatta!*)** at Mount Theches (4); and the troubled journey along the Black Sea coast, the service for
the Thracian Seuthes, and the handover to Sparta (5–7). Xenophon, silent in the early books, comes to
the fore only after the generals' deaths, presenting himself as the **model leader** — accessible,
sharing the toil, even chopping firewood.

## A Memoir That Hides Its Author

Rood stresses that the *Anabasis*'s "beguiling simplicity" is deceptive (T. E. Lawrence: "charming…
but cunningly full of writing tricks… pretentiously simple"). The work is **not formally a memoir**:
Xenophon refers to himself in the **third person**, and the *Hellenica* (3.1.2) attributes the
expedition's account to one **"Themistogenes of Syracuse"** — read already in antiquity (Plutarch) as
Xenophon's **pseudonym**, a device to make his self-flattering narrative "more acceptable." Modern
scholarship reads the work variously as:

- an **apologia** defending Xenophon's conduct as a commander (and possibly against a rival account,
  the alleged *Anabasis* of Sophaenetus behind Diodorus);
- **panhellenic propaganda** — demonstrating, by the Ten Thousand's escape, the **vulnerability of the
  Persian empire** and the case for a Greek invasion of Asia (the project Agesilaus, and later
  Alexander, would pursue);
- and, simply, the work of "one of the great generic experimenters of antiquity," a new kind of
  narrative eluding the categories of history, ethnography and memoir.

## Themes and Value

Beyond the adventure, the *Anabasis* is a unique portrait of a **Greek army as a mobile *polis***:
the soldiers elect their own generals, debate policy in assembly, hold athletic games, make vows,
consult seers and read omens (a propitious sneeze) — co-operation and rivalry mirroring the life of
the city-states they came from. Xenophon's own motive, he says, was to become a **friend (*philos*)**
of Cyrus, on an ethic of reciprocity rather than pay; his teacher **Socrates** warned that serving
Cyrus (a backer of Sparta) might endanger him — and indeed Xenophon was **exiled from Athens**,
settling at **Scillus** near Olympia (described with elegiac nostalgia) before later removals.

## Afterlife

The *Anabasis* had an "extraordinary afterlife": Arrian titled his Alexander history *Anabasis
Alexandrou* after it; it became the standard **school text** of Greek prose; and the cry **"Thalatta!
Thalatta!"** — the only Xenophon phrase in the *Oxford Dictionary of Quotations* — echoes through
Heine, Joyce's *Ulysses*, and modern uses of "Anabasis" for famous retreats (the Czech Legion in
Siberia, Dunkirk 1940, the 2003 march on Baghdad).
