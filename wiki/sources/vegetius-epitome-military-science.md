# Vegetius, *Epitome of Military Science* (*Epitoma rei militaris*)

The most influential military treatise in Western history: a late-Roman handbook by
**[[vegetius|Vegetius]]** arguing that Rome's strength lay in **training and discipline** and that her
decline could be reversed by restoring the army of old. The fourth source of this Phase-5 batch.

## The Four Books

- **Book 1 — the recruit.** Selection (region, trade, age, physique, the value of countrymen over
  city-dwellers) and the rigorous **training** of the recruit: the military step, running, swimming,
  drill at the post, striking "with the point, not the edge," weapons-handling, the entrenched camp.
  Frames the whole work with the thesis that "the Romans conquered all peoples solely because of their
  military training."
- **Book 2 — the legion.** The organization, ranks, officers and administration of the ideal legion,
  Vegetius's model unit, contrasted with the weakened forces of his own time.
- **Book 3 — strategy and tactics** (the most famous book): marching, supply and **logistics**, camps,
  battle formations, generalship, and a celebrated set of **maxims** — on morale, deception, the
  husbanding of force, and the wisdom of subduing an enemy by manoeuvre, hunger and stratagem rather
  than risking all on a pitched battle.
- **Book 4 — fortification, siegecraft and naval warfare**: the defence and attack of walled places,
  siege engines, and operations at sea.

## Significance

After antiquity the *Epitoma* became **the most copied secular Latin text of the Middle Ages**: it was
the manual of war for Carolingian, Byzantine-influenced, crusading and Renaissance commanders, drawn on
by writers from the ninth century to **Machiavelli's** *Art of War* and beyond, and the source of the
tag *"si vis pacem, para bellum."* As both a window onto Roman military ideals and the great channel of
**classical military science** to later Europe, it is among the most consequential technical texts of
antiquity.
