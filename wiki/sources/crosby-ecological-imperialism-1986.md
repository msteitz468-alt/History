# Ecological Imperialism: The Biological Expansion of Europe, 900–1900 — Alfred W. Crosby (1986; new edition with preface)

Alfred Crosby’s *Ecological Imperialism* explains why people of European descent came to form the bulk of the population (and control the most productive agricultural lands) in the temperate zones of the world — North America, southern South America (especially the pampa), Australia, and New Zealand — while European expansion produced very different demographic and ecological outcomes in the tropics.

The core argument is that military, technological, and organizational advantages were important but secondary. The decisive factor was **biology**: the success of a "portmanteau biota" of Old World weeds, livestock, crops, and pathogens that accompanied European migrants and transformed New World and Australasian ecologies into "Neo-Europes" favorable to European humans and their associated species. Native biotas and populations were displaced or marginalized not primarily by conquest but by ecological competition in which the European suite had decisive advantages in temperate climates similar to those of Europe.

## Key Thesis

Europeans succeeded spectacularly in creating Neo-Europes because their accompanying organisms (the "portmanteau biota") were pre-adapted to similar climates and out-competed local species in the niches that mattered for agriculture and settlement. Weeds prepared ground; livestock multiplied "explosively" with little competition or predation; diseases (and the broader immunological advantage) weakened or removed human competitors. This was not symmetric: few New World or Australian organisms traveled successfully back to Europe in the same way.

The process had deep roots (Norse experiments, Atlantic island stepping-stones) but reached full force after 1492 with better ships, knowledge of winds ("volta do mar"), and the Columbian reconnection. The 19th-century steamship era produced the greatest human migration in history, filling the ecologically prepared lands.

The result: temperate-zone "Neo-Europes" became the world’s dominant food exporters and demographic reservoirs for European-descended populations, while tropical zones (Africa, much of Asia, equatorial Americas) saw different patterns of interaction, extraction, and limited permanent European settlement.

## Historiography / Collection Note

This is the book that gave "ecological imperialism" and "Neo-Europes" their modern currency in historical writing. It builds directly on Crosby’s *The Columbian Exchange* (already ingested) by shifting focus from the initial exchange and Great Dying to the longer-term biological replacement and demographic swarming that followed in suitable climates.

It sits at the intersection of environmental history, world-systems analysis, and critiques of Eurocentrism. It engages (without naming it as such in the 1986 text) questions later sharpened by works such as Pomeranz’s *Great Divergence*. The  new preface situates the argument against both Victorian racialism and 1960s–70s political-economy emphases on brutality/capitalism alone, insisting on the ecological component that made certain imperialisms demographically transformative.

The Bibliotheca Alexandrina now contains both of Crosby’s foundational works. This source supplies the detailed mechanisms and comparative geography needed for the wiki’s treatment of the post-1492 Atlantic world, the Long 19th Century migrations, the making of modern food systems, and transregional processes. It directly fills Critical Gap 10 (World Systems and Transregional History) alongside Abu-Lughod, Hansen, Alpers, and Hall.

## Volume Synthesis Note

Crosby’s *Ecological Imperialism* supplies the mechanistic core for understanding why European expansion produced full demographic and ecological replacement ("Neo-Europes") in temperate zones but qualitatively different outcomes elsewhere. The portmanteau biota — weeds as pioneers, livestock exploding in predator/competitor-free niches, Old World diseases exploiting long isolation — acted as an ecological advance guard that prepared landscapes for European settlers far more effectively than arms alone. Early medieval experiments (Norse, Crusaders) failed for lack of scale and biotic support; Atlantic islands served as working prototypes; post-1492 winds mastery and the Columbian reconnection scaled the process. New Zealand stands as the clearest case study of the full sequence (pre-contact baseline → rapid adoption + disruption → disease-driven decline + biotic transformation → pakeha dominance). The 19th-century steam-powered migration wave filled the prepared lands, producing regions that by the late 20th century supplied a disproportionate share of the world’s tradable temperate foodstuffs. 

The work complements the already-ingested *Columbian Exchange* by extending the analysis from initial asymmetry and the Great Dying to sustained replacement and modern global consequences. It is essential grounding for the wiki’s coverage of transregional environmental history, the making of the modern food system, and Gap 10 world-systems material. It also informs controversies around geographic determinism and the relative weight of biology vs. culture/technology in the rise of European hegemony.
