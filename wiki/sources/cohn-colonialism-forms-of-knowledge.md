# Colonialism and Its Forms of Knowledge (Bernard S. Cohn)

Bernard Cohn's *Colonialism and Its Forms of Knowledge: The British in India* (1996) shows how British
rule in India was built not only on force and revenue but on **the production of knowledge** — language,
law, survey, census, ethnography, history and the museum — through which the colonizer made an unfamiliar
society **knowable, classifiable and governable**. Twelfth source of **Gap 1 (Modern)**, the empirical
companion to [[said-orientalism|Said's *Orientalism*]] and the wiki's anchor for the concept of
**[[colonial-knowledge|colonial forms of knowledge]]**.

## The Argument

Cohn argues that "the conquest of India was a conquest of knowledge." The British assembled a repertoire of
**"investigative modalities"**:

- **The historiographic and the survey** — mapping land, codifying tenures, fixing revenue.
- **The enumerative** — the **census**, which sorted Indians into bounded, countable categories of caste,
  religion and tribe, hardening fluid identities into administrative facts.
- **The museological and the surveillance** — collecting, displaying and policing.
- **The legal and the linguistic** — codifying "Hindu" and "Muslim" law from selected texts and learning
  vernaculars so as to rule through them; the famous reading of **dress, ritual and the colonial durbar**
  as a semiotics of authority.

The effect was not neutral description but the **reification of "tradition"**: caste, communal religion,
and a timeless "customary" India were in significant part *colonial constructions*, produced by the very
acts of governing and knowing.

## Significance

Cohn turns Said's discursive critique into **concrete administrative history** — showing exactly how
imperial knowledge was made and what it did. He is foundational to the wiki's treatment of
[[imperialism|imperialism]] as a project of classification and governance, and to the historiography of
caste, communalism and the colonial state in [[south-asia|South Asia]]. He also helped found the
**"colonial governmentality"** literature (after Foucault) that the [[imperialism]] page draws on.
