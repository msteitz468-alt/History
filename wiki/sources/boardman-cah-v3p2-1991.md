# The Cambridge Ancient History, Vol. III, Part 2 (1991)

**The Assyrian and Babylonian Empires and other States of the Near East, from the
Eighth to the Sixth Centuries B.C.** Edited by John Boardman, I. E. S. Edwards,
N. G. L. Hammond and the late E. Sollberger, with the assistance of C. B. F. Walker.
Cambridge University Press, 2nd edition, 1991.

This is the **Near Eastern empire volume** of the CAH — the narrative spine for the
two centuries in which Mesopotamia ruled the ancient Near East twice over: first the
[[neo-assyrian-empire|Neo-Assyrian Empire]] under the great warrior-kings from
**Tiglath-pileser III (744)** to **Ashurbanipal**, the largest empire the world had
yet seen; then, after Assyria's sudden collapse (**[[fall-of-nineveh-612bce|Nineveh
612]]**), the short, brilliant [[neo-babylonian-empire|Neo-Babylonian (Chaldaean)
Empire]] of **Nebuchadnezzar II**, builder of the wonder-city of
[[babylon|Babylon]] and destroyer of [[fall-of-jerusalem-587bce|Jerusalem]]. It
closes with [[nabonidus|Nabonidus]] and the absorption of Babylon by
[[fall-of-babylon-539bce|Cyrus the Persian in 539]].

Around that imperial spine the volume sets the **other states caught in its
gravity**: [[kingdom-of-israel|Israel]] (destroyed 722) and [[kingdom-of-judah|
Judah]] (exiled 586) — the political matrix of the Hebrew Bible's prophetic and
Deuteronomistic literature; [[phoenicians|Phoenicia]] and its great westward
[[phoenician-colonization|colonization]] that planted [[carthage|Carthage]]; the
steppe [[scythians|Scythians]] and [[cimmerians|Cimmerians]] whose raids shook
Anatolia and the Near East; the [[thracians|Thracians]] of the Balkan hinterland;
the Anatolian kingdoms of **Phrygia** (Midas) and **[[lydia|Lydia]]** (Gyges to
Croesus); and **Egypt's** [[kush-meroe|Kushite (25th)]] and **Saite (26th)**
dynasties.

It is paired with two companion volumes read separately:
[[boardman-cah-v3p1-1982|Vol. III Part 1]] (the Balkans, Near East and Aegean,
tenth–eighth c.; on disk, already ingested) and Vol. III Part 3 (the expansion of
the Greek world, eighth–sixth c.; markdown on disk).

## Structure of the Volume

The volume is divided into two parts. **Part I (chs. 21–28)** is the Assyriological
and Babylonian core — political narrative reign-by-reign, then two civilization
chapters. **Part II (chs. 29–35)** sweeps the surrounding world clockwise from the
Levant through the Mediterranean, the Black Sea steppe, Anatolia, and Egypt.

## Volume Synthesis Note

CAH III.2 is, above all, the volume of **the two Mesopotamian empires and their
shadow**. Its narrative spine is the rise of the [[neo-assyrian-empire|Neo-Assyrian
Empire]] from Tiglath-pileser III's reorganization (744) to the Sargonid zenith under
Sennacherib, Esarhaddon and Ashurbanipal — the first state to hold the entire Fertile
Crescent and briefly Egypt — followed by its astonishingly sudden collapse
([[fall-of-nineveh-612bce|Nineveh, 612]]) and the brief, brilliant
[[neo-babylonian-empire|Chaldaean]] aftermath of [[nebuchadnezzar-ii|Nebuchadnezzar
II]] and [[nabonidus|Nabonidus]], extinguished in turn at
[[fall-of-babylon-539bce|Babylon (539)]]. Read against [[boardman-cah-v3p1-1982|III.1]],
the pairing tells one continuous Near Eastern story: III.1 is the recovery from the
[[bronze-age-collapse-1200bce|Bronze Age Collapse]] to c. 800; III.2 is the imperial
climax and the handover to Persia. The whole arc covers barely two and a half
centuries yet contains two complete imperial life-cycles.

The volume's organizing insight — visible only across its parts — is that **the
"other states" are legible mainly as functions of Assyrian and Babylonian power**.
[[kingdom-of-israel|Israel]] falls (722) and [[kingdom-of-judah|Judah]] is exiled
(586) because they lie on the imperial road to Egypt; the [[babylonian-exile|Exile]]
and the prophetic-Deuteronomistic literature it generated ([[hebrew-prophets]]) are
the religious precipitate of imperial conquest. [[phoenicians|Phoenicia]]'s great
westward [[phoenician-colonization|colonization]] — planting [[carthage|Carthage]],
[[gades-tartessos|Gades and Tartessos]] — is read by Culican partly as a release of
mercantile energy under and around Assyrian-then-Babylonian pressure on
[[tyre|Tyre]]. Even the steppe [[scythians|Scythians]] and [[cimmerians|Cimmerians]]
enter "history" only when their raids strike Assyria, [[phrygians|Phrygia]] and
[[lydia|Lydia]]; and Egypt's [[kush-meroe|Kushite]] and [[saite-egypt|Saite]]
dynasties are framed by the Assyrian invasions that ended Nubian rule and installed
the Saite line. The book thus models the Near East as a single system organized by
the Mesopotamian core.

Methodologically the volume is **strongly bifurcated**, and this is its chief tension
with the rest of the wiki. The Assyriological and Babylonian chapters (Grayson,
Brinkman, Oates, Wiseman) rest on a dense, well-dated cuneiform record — eponym lists,
royal annals, the Babylonian Chronicle, astronomical diaries — and carry full Cambridge
authority. The peripheral chapters are far softer: the Israel/Judah narrative (Mitchell)
is a conservative harmonization of the Hebrew Bible with Assyrian synchronisms that
modern minimalist and archaeological scholarship has since contested; the Scythian and
Thracian chapters (Sulimirski, Mihailov) preserve a **culture-historical, pots-equal-
peoples framing** that I flagged on every page as now qualified (Taylor's appended notes
already signal the retreat). The volume is therefore most reliable at its Mesopotamian
center and progressively more dated toward its geographic edges — a gradient worth
remembering when these pages are cross-read against newer monographs.

For the wiki as a whole, III.2 **converts a thinly-sketched horizon into a densely
linked one**. It supplied the full Sargonid actor set, the Chaldaean empire and its
civilization, the Judaean catastrophe and Exile as a *process*, the Phoenician West as
a *process* with its own colonial nodes, and the Anatolian hinge — [[lydia|Lydia]] and
the **invention of [[coinage|coinage]]** at [[sardis|Sardis]] — that links the Near
Eastern world forward to the Greek economic revolution of CAH III.3 and IV. Its richest
forward connections are three: to **Persia** (the volume literally ends at Cyrus's gates,
handing [[achaemenid-empire|the Achaemenids]] their inheritance), to **Greece** (coinage,
the Ionian cities pressed by Lydia, Greek mercenaries and Naucratis in Saite Egypt), and
to **the Hebrew Bible** (the Exile as the matrix of canonical Judaism). The standing
[[controversies|controversies]] it sharpens — the historicity of the Sennacherib campaign(s)
against [[hezekiah]], the reliability of Herodotus on Egypt and Lydia, and the ethnogenesis
behind the "Scythian" and "Phrygian" labels — are all now seeded for later sources to
revisit.
