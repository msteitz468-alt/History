# Pliny the Younger, *Complete Letters*

The collected correspondence of **[[pliny-the-younger|Pliny the Younger]]** — nine books of polished
literary letters plus the official **Book 10** with the emperor **[[trajan|Trajan]]** — the richest
single source for the social and political world of the early-second-century Roman elite. Added at the
user's request as the eleventh source of this Phase-4 batch. For the author see **[[pliny-the-younger|
Pliny the Younger]]**.

## The Collection

**Books 1–9** gather letters Pliny composed for publication, each a miniature essay on a single subject
addressed to a named friend (among them **[[tacitus|Tacitus]]**, to whom he writes about oratory and
history, and whose fame he hopes to share). They range across the law courts and the Senate, literary
recitations and patronage, the management of his estates, the manumission and treatment of slaves,
education and benefaction at Comum, ghost stories, and his affection for his young wife Calpurnia —
together a self-portrait of the ideal cultivated senator and a panorama of elite life. **Book 10**
preserves Pliny's actual dispatches as **governor of Bithynia-Pontus** and Trajan's terse replies — a
unique survival of working Roman provincial administration.

## Two Documents of the First Importance

- **The Vesuvius letters (6.16, 6.20)** — addressed to Tacitus, the only eyewitness account of the
  **[[eruption-of-vesuvius-79ce|eruption of 79 CE]]** and of the death of **Pliny the Elder**; the
  source of the volcanological term "Plinian."
- **The Christians correspondence (10.96–97)** — Pliny asks Trajan how to handle Christians brought
  before him: he describes their worship ("they met before dawn… and sang a hymn to Christ as to a
  god"), his test (invoking the gods, cursing Christ), and his executions of the obstinate. **Trajan's
  rescript** — Christians are not to be hunted out, anonymous accusations are to be ignored, but the
  convicted who will not recant are to be punished — is the **foundational text of Roman legal policy
  toward Christianity** and the earliest substantial external (pagan) testimony to Christian practice.

## The Political Background (Walsh's Introduction)

Walsh sets the letters against the **evolution of the principate**: the hollow Augustan "dyarchy" of
*princeps* and Senate; the servility of the Senate under Claudius and the terror under **Nero** and
above all **Domitian** (whose memory Pliny, like Tacitus and Juvenal, blackens — to be balanced against
Suetonius); and the celebrated relief of the "good emperors" **Nerva** and **Trajan**, whose
relationship with the Senate Pliny idealizes in the *Panegyricus* and embodies in Book 10.

## Significance

The *Letters* are the **fullest social document of the high Empire**, the model of the Latin literary
epistle (descending to the Renaissance humanists), and a primary source for **early Christianity**, the
**Vesuvius disaster**, and the administration of the provinces. They also weave together this batch of
sources: Pliny is the friend and addressee of **[[tacitus|Tacitus]]** and the patron of
**[[suetonius|Suetonius]]**.
