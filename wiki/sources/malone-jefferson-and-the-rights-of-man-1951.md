# Jefferson and the Rights of Man — Dumas Malone (1951)

**Jefferson and His Time, Volume 2.** Comprehensive narrative biography covering Thomas Jefferson 1784–1792: his mission to France as Minister Plenipotentiary (with Adams and Franklin, later alone); the rich personal and intellectual life in Paris as "Minister of Enlightenment"; the Maria Cosway relationship; travels in France, Italy, Germany, the Netherlands, and England; close observation of the twilight of the Old Regime and the opening of the French Revolution (through the Estates-General and early 1789); advice on the American Constitution to Madison; return to the United States; appointment as first Secretary of State under Washington; service in New York and Philadelphia; the emerging cabinet struggles with Alexander Hamilton over finance, the Bank, foreign policy, and constitutional interpretation; the "Storm over the Rights of Man"; and the beginnings of party division up to Washington's re-election in 1792.

## Overview / Thesis

This volume shifts the scene from the "charming" but local Virginia of Volume 1 to the "far richer and more colorful background" of Paris, Versailles, London, New York, and Philadelphia. Jefferson participated in "far more complicated movements and events" than in his first period. The eight-and-a-half years have biographical unity because Jefferson was concerned primarily with foreign affairs throughout. Malone presents Jefferson as continuing to view his age as an enlightened liberal: the goal of human freedom and happiness through expanding knowledge remained central. In France he was "better able to do the sort of things he wanted to do, and to be the sort of man he wanted to be" than at any other time in public office — a detached philosopher enjoying music, architecture, science, and cultivated friendship while still serving the young Republic's commercial interests.

On the French Revolution Jefferson approved the early "philosophical" phase as the victory of reason and the adoption of American principles (the Rights of Man), but counseled moderation and "reformation," not violent overthrow or class warfare. His personal influence (chiefly on Lafayette) was slight but consistently moderating. The great political struggle of the volume, however, was domestic: the feud with Hamilton, which "would have arisen if there never had been a French Revolution." It was fought on lines of funding, assumption, the Bank of the United States, commercial policy, and the proper scope of the new Constitution. Washington's unanimous re-election at the end of 1792 marks a logical terminal point — the rudimentary parties of 1792 would become fully organized afterward, and the international situation changed sharply in 1793 with Britain entering the European war.

## Methodological Notes

- Strict chronological narrative maintained so that development of mind and character can be observed before later political controversy obscured outlines.
- Heavy reliance on Jefferson's correspondence (especially letters to Madison, Short, and others), diplomatic dispatches, the Anas (later notes), contemporary newspapers, and French and British records then available.
- Acknowledges that materials for these years are "positively embarrassing" in volume yet sometimes disappointing in gaps (especially on certain negotiations).
- Treats both the European mission and the early cabinet service with "sufficient fullness" while giving due proportion to private friendships and intellectual pursuits.
- Avoids both inflating Jefferson's direct role in the French Revolution and minimizing the high purposiveness that distinguished him among statesmen of the time.

## Key Contributions

- The most detailed and balanced portrait of Jefferson's Paris years available at mid-century, especially the "Minister of Enlightenment" activities (spreading knowledge of America, sending home seeds, models, books, reporting on agriculture and society).
- Full treatment of the Maria Cosway "sentimental adventure" as a window into Jefferson's emotional life and the famous "Dialogue between the Head and the Heart."
- Authoritative account of Jefferson's observations and dispatches on the French Revolution 1787–1789, his qualified approval, and his moderating counsel.
- The clearest anatomy of the origins of the Jefferson-Hamilton conflict in concrete policy disputes (Bank bill, commercial discrimination, foreign policy toward Britain and France) rather than in abstract French Revolutionary ideology.
- Demonstration that Jefferson remained an "opportunist with respect to immediate ends and particular means" while holding fast to fundamental principles of liberty and self-government.

## Range 02 Claims

- **Jefferson meets Maria Cosway and spends a day with her in September 1786**: "Then twenty-seven years old, she had a slim figure and graceful carriage, and her small head was crowned with a mass of curly golden hair. Though of an English family she had been born in Italy, where she had studied art and music. She spoke a melange of languages and liked Italian best. (narrative of the Sentimental Adventure, September 1786; [[actors/jefferson-thomas]], [[actors/cosway-maria]], [[places/paris]]). - **Jefferson conspires to extend time with the Cosways and Trumbull**: "In this mad moment he sent a 'lying messenger' to the old Duchesse de la Rochefoucauld d'Anyille, saying that, just as he had started on his way to dine with her, important dispatches arrived requiring his immediate attention. Then he went surreptitiously to St.-Cloud for dinner, saw sights with his gay companions on the way back to Paris, and ended a memorable day with a visit to the harpist and composer Krumpholtz." (Sentimental Adventure, September 1786; [[actors/jefferson-thomas]], [[actors/cosway-maria]], [[actors/short-william]] implied via Trumbull context). Demonstrates uncharacteristic spontaneity.

- **Maria Cosway's marriage and background**: "Maria, coming to England from Italy after the death of her father and the decline of the family fortunes, had made a marriage of convenience with [Richard Cosway] five years before... Richard Cosway, who was about Jefferson's age, had come from England partly for a holiday, but he had portraits to paint... 'although a well-made little man, very like a monkey in the face.'" (Sentimental Adventure; [[actors/cosway-maria]]). Explains her availability for daily companionship.

- **Daily expeditions with Maria Cosway in September 1786**: "He left record of some places and things they saw together that September: 'The Port de Reuilly, the hills along the Seine, the rainbows of the machine of Marly, the terrace of St. Germains [Saint-Germain-en-Laye], the chateaux, the gardens, the statues of Marly, the pavilion of Lucienne [Louveciennes].' - **Jefferson's deep emotional response to Maria**: "In speaking of these experiences afterwards Jefferson invariably employed the language of friendship and sentiment, not passion. But... there can be no doubt that he fell deeply in love during that golden September, and there is no reason to suppose that the lady was displeased... He found her a lovely, talented, capricious creature ~ half woman and half child." (Sentimental Adventure; [[actors/jefferson-thomas]], [[actors/cosway-maria]]). "Illicit love-making was generally condoned in that society... this was the time; but he gave no ready handle to later political foes."

- **Wrist accident and its association with Maria Cosway**: "The delicious expeditions ended abruptly... when the middle-aged widower fell and dislocated his right wrist. It was by one of those follies from which good cannot come, but ill may.'" (to William S. Smith, Oct. 22, 1786; Sentimental Adventure; [[actors/jefferson-thomas]], [[actors/cosway-maria]], [[actors/short-william]]). "It is a good guess... that she was with him... and that he connected the unlucky accident with her in his own mind."

- **Maria's immediate response and Jefferson's continued pursuit despite injury**: "Oh I wish you was well enough to come to us tomorrow to dinner and stay the evening. ... I would serve you, and help you at dinner, and divert your pain after with good musik." (Maria to TJ, soon after Sept. 18, 1786; Sentimental Adventure; [[actors/cosway-maria]]). Explained to Jay the duty to aid American agriculture by adapting productions and markets.

- **Italy leg for rice machine and other observations**: "From Marseilles he went through Toulon... to Nice... he now hired some of these useful mules to take him through the Col de Tende... In Lombardy he did [see rice and machines]... He was impressed with the insalubrity of the culture of wet rice... The export of Piedmont rice in rough form was under penalty of death, but he took this occasion to defy the law. Noted Parmesan cheese process in detail; inquired about whale oil and tobacco trade.

- **Social observations and aesthetic notes during travels**: "'Fall down and worship the site of the Chateau di Saorgio'... 'Imagine to yourself, madam, a castle & village hanging to a cloud in front, on one hand a mountain cloven through to let pass a gurgling stream; on the other a river...' Noted scale of plants by cold resistance on his birthday.

- **Olive trees and seeds/plants sent home**: "Of all the gifts of heaven to man, it is next to the most precious, if it be not the most precious... If bread came first, oil was surely second." (to Drayton, July 30, 1787; travel aftermath). Efforts to introduce olive trees and upland rice (later mountain rice from Africa) to South Carolina/Georgia spanned years; he later listed adding a useful plant as among greatest services. Smuggled and shipped specimens despite disappointments.

- **Patsy Jefferson during and after the trip**: "Patsy... was in her fifteenth year when her 'dear papa' went on his southern tour... 'Until now you have not kept your word the least in the world.'... She had inherited her father's height... If not a talented girl she was on her way to becoming a cultivated young woman... In a single letter he said such things as these: 'Of all the cankers of human happiness none corrodes with so silent, yet so baneful an influence, as indolence... - **Harpsichord and family reunion plans**: "There was another and much more exciting prospect: Polly was coming from Virginia... 'Then, indeed, shall I be the happiest of mortals'... The harpsichord he had ordered from London... Not until late August did John Trumbull... report that the instrument was really on its way... it arrived, toward the end of the month [November], in good condition." (family section; [[actors/jefferson-thomas]]).

April, May, June, and July... stipulated that she must cross in a vessel which had made at least one voyage but was not more than four or five years old... Confirmed preference for American conditions over French social system while valuing specific plants, architecture, and graces for import.

- **The winter of 1788-1789 in Paris was exceptionally severe and compounded by bread shortages**: "Great cold carries off men here, as it does cattle with us, and for the same reasons, the want of being housed and fed." ("A Diplomat Awaits His Leave 1788-1789"; [[places/paris]]). Jefferson noted the continuing "distress for bread, lasting all summer," as a major cause of popular discontent.

- **Jefferson's daughters Patsy and Polly suffered prolonged illness during the winter**: "for more than two months Patsy and Polly were ill — the latter seriously, he said, without describing the malady." ("A Diplomat Awaits His Leave 1788-1789"; [[actors/jefferson-thomas]]). They recovered by February and visited the Hotel de Langeac frequently.

- **Jefferson removed his daughters from the Abbaye de Panthemont in late April 1789**: "Beginning in late April his daughters were regular members of his establishment, for he then removed them from the Abbaye de Panthemont." ("A Diplomat Awaits His Leave 1788-1789"; [[actors/jefferson-thomas]]). A family tradition attributed the timing to an impulsive letter from seventeen-year-old Martha expressing a desire to become a nun.

- **William Short remained absent in Italy during the winter of 1788-1789**: "who was lonely in the continued absence of William Short, still traveling in the privileged climate of Italy." ("A Diplomat Awaits His Leave 1788-1789"; [[actors/jefferson-thomas]], [[actors/short-william]]). Short later returned to Paris and continued assisting Jefferson.

- **Jefferson strongly advised Short to return to America for long-term happiness and career**: "For affection and the long habit of your society have rendered it necessary to me. And how much more so will it be when I have parted with my daughters? ... the happiness of his own country was 'more tranquil, more unmixed, more permanent.'" ("A Diplomat Awaits His Leave 1788-1789", letters to Short; [[actors/jefferson-thomas]], [[actors/short-william]]). He recommended honorable public station or success at the bar.

- **Jefferson commissioned Trumbull for a composite image of Bacon, Locke, and Newton**: "as the three greatest men that have ever lived, without any exception, and as having laid the foundation of those superstructures which have been raised in the physical and moral sciences." ("A Diplomat Awaits His Leave 1788-1789", February 1789 instructions; [[actors/jefferson-thomas]]). He sent a design for three ovals on one large canvas.

- **In May 1789 Jefferson offered the secretaryship to John Trumbull contingent on Short's return to America**: "It was in May, 1789 —after the Estates-General had assembled and after Trumbull had bought for him a strong traveling trunk ... that Jefferson made Trumbull the offer of the secretaryship, contingent on Short's return to America after Jefferson himself came back to France." ("A Diplomat Awaits His Leave 1788-1789"; [[actors/jefferson-thomas]], [[actors/short-william]]). Lodging and board were included.

- **Jefferson's July 4, 1789 dinner for Americans served as a leave-taking occasion**: "this was a more memorable occasion, not merely because it preceded the fall of the Bastille by only ten days, but also because it was regarded as a leave-taking party." ("A Diplomat Awaits His Leave 1788-1789"; [[actors/jefferson-thomas]], [[actors/lafayette]]). Eight Americans signed an address honoring his "exertions in favour of that country and for the general happiness of mankind."

- **Jefferson viewed the French struggle for rights with calm optimism in early 1789**: "Upon the whole, it has appeared to me that the basis of the present struggle is an illumination of the human mind as to the rights of the nation, aided by fortunate incidents; that they can never retrograde, but from the natural progress of things, must press forward to the establishment of a constitution which shall assure to them a good degree of liberty." ("Revolution Begins and a Mission Ends 1789", letter to Dr. Richard Price; [[actors/jefferson-thomas]]). He expected gradual progress rather than doctrinaire upheaval.

- **Jefferson witnessed the opening session of the Estates-General at Versailles on May 5, 1789**: "he witnessed the august ceremony of the opening session of the Estates-General next day. 'Had it been enlightened with lamps and chandeliers,' he said, 'it would have been almost as brilliant as the opera.'" ("Revolution Begins and a Mission Ends 1789"; [[places/versailles]], [[actors/jefferson-thomas]]). He attended Versailles almost daily thereafter and reported events carefully.

- **Jefferson sided with the Third Estate in the controversy over voting by orders**: "Jefferson's sympathies being with the popular interest, he sided with the Third Estate in this famous struggle, and blamed the impasse on the nobility..." ("Revolution Begins and a Mission Ends 1789"; [[actors/jefferson-thomas]]). He noted that "the progress of light and liberty" among aristocrats had fallen short and feared a check to the previously orderly revolution.

- **Jefferson offered private counsel to Lafayette on joining the Third Estate**: "he did not hesitate to give private counsel to Lafayette in this situation." ("Revolution Begins and a Mission Ends 1789"; [[actors/jefferson-thomas]], [[actors/lafayette]]). He urged Lafayette not to delay too long in following his conscience and principles despite noble instructions from Auvergne.

- **Jefferson drafted a Charter of Rights in early June 1789 for possible presentation by the King**: "It was a draft of a charter, containing 'all the good in which all parties agree.'" ("Revolution Begins and a Mission Ends 1789", sent June 3 to Lafayette and Rabaut de Saint-Etienne; [[actors/jefferson-thomas]], [[actors/lafayette]]). It proposed annual Estates-General meetings, tax and appropriation control, personal liberty, press freedom, renunciation of aristocratic privileges, national debt assumption, and a grant to the King.

- **Jefferson listened to the debates when the Third Estate assumed the name National Assembly**: "Jefferson listened to most of the debates on that fateful day and reported the critical situation to John Jay." ("Revolution Begins and a Mission Ends 1789", June 17, 1789; [[actors/jefferson-thomas]]). He wrote that "the fate of the nation depends on the conduct of the King and his Ministers."

- **Jefferson regarded the King's June 23 royal session declarations as a surrender to the aristocracy**: "the speech and declaration of the King now seemed to him a surrender to the aristocracy against the wishes of Necker and Montmorin at the behest of the Court." ("Revolution Begins and a Mission Ends 1789"; [[actors/jefferson-thomas]], [[places/versailles]]). The Assembly refused to disperse and "seem[ed] to rise in their demands."

- **Jefferson saw the June 27 reunion of orders as a triumph driven by popular pressure**: "To Jefferson it seemed that the triumph of the National Assembly on June 27 — when at the command of the King the two privileged orders joined that body —was owing to the popular indignation aroused by the royal session and the resulting disaffection of the soldiers." ("Revolution Begins and a Mission Ends 1789"; [[actors/jefferson-thomas]]). He believed the King and Court were scared into the step.

- **Jefferson witnessed the July 12 clash in the Place Louis XV that signaled the start of popular insurrection**: "On Sunday, July 12, Jefferson passed in his carriage through the Place Louis XV, now the Place de la Concorde. ... he believed that this affair was the signal for universal insurrection." ("Revolution Begins and a Mission Ends 1789"; [[places/paris]], [[actors/jefferson-thomas]]). Parisians were already panicked by foreign troops and Necker's dismissal.

- **Jefferson obtained the story of the Bastille's fall directly from Ethis de Corny on July 14**: "On Tuesday, July 14, the American Minister was visiting at the home of Louis-Dominique Ethis de Corny ... when that member of the city committee returned from the Bastille." ("Revolution Begins and a Mission Ends 1789"; [[places/paris]]). He reported muskets seized, prisoners freed, the governor and a city officer beheaded, Lafayette named commander of the bourgeois militia, and demolition begun; he later contributed to the widows of those slain.

- **Jefferson and Dugald Stewart witnessed the King's procession to Paris after the Bastille**: "The King's carriage was in the center, flanked by the deputies on foot in two ranks. Lafayette was in the lead on horseback, and the bourgeois militia were before and behind, while thousands of citizens armed with muskets, pistols, swords, pikes, scythes, and pruning hooks lined the streets..." ("Revolution Begins and a Mission Ends 1789"; [[places/paris]], [[actors/jefferson-thomas]], [[actors/lafayette]]). He called it an amende honorable without precedent.

- **Jefferson annotated Lafayette's draft declaration of rights in July 1789**: "In July, before presenting a declaration to the Assembly, Lafayette sent another draft to Jefferson, and on this the latter made 'a few annotations.'" ("Revolution Begins and a Mission Ends 1789"; [[actors/jefferson-thomas]], [[actors/lafayette]]). He wanted to omit "property" and "the care of his honor," retaining only "the care of his life, the power to dispose of his person and the fruits of his industry ... the pursuit of happiness and resistance to oppression."

- **Jefferson advocated broad popular participation in government on American principles**: "We think in America that it is necessary to introduce the people into every department of government as far as they are capable of exercising it; and that this is the only way to ensure a long continued & honest administration of its powers." ("Revolution Begins and a Mission Ends 1789", July 19, 1789 letter; [[actors/jefferson-thomas]]). He viewed administration of justice as the best sphere for such participation, since "the execution of the laws is more important than the making them."

- **Jefferson's leave of absence was finally granted at the end of August 1789**: "By the end of August the situation had changed considerably ... The day after the Assembly adopted the Declaration of the Rights of Man and the Citizen ... he had word that his leave of absence had been granted." ("Revolution Begins and a Mission Ends 1789"; [[actors/jefferson-thomas]]). He hosted a dinner for Lafayette and leaders of both wings of the patriotic party (Mounier, Duport, Barnave, Lameth) to promote agreement on the constitution.

- **Jefferson declined an invitation to advise a French committee on the constitution project**: "Toward the end of July, the spokesman for a committee appointed to draw up a project for a constitution wrote him a charming letter inviting him to a conference. ... But Jefferson begged off on the ground of the pressure of diplomatic business, with an implication of impropriety." ("Revolution Begins and a Mission Ends 1789"; [[actors/jefferson-thomas]], [[actors/lafayette]]). He later participated informally in reconciliation efforts among moderate royalists and more radical patriots.

## Volume Synthesis Note

This volume transforms Jefferson from the Virginia-centric figure of Vol. 1 into a transatlantic statesman and the central domestic antagonist of Hamiltonian consolidation in the early republic. The Paris years (1784–1789) emerge as the richest period of personal cultivation and "Enlightenment" activity in his life — architecture (Maison Carrée / Virginia Capitol), agriculture (olive, rice, moldboard), books (200+ for Madison), Notes dissemination, and the intense but sentimental Cosway relationship that produced the Head/Heart dialogue, revealing a man capable of "generous spasm of the heart" yet ultimately governed by duty and reason. His qualified endorsement of the early French Revolution as "reformation" driven by "illumination of the human mind," his moderating counsel to Lafayette, and his generational sovereignty letter ("earth belongs in usufruct to the living") add philosophical depth that will inform later positions.

The return and cabinet service (1790–1792) establish the template for the first party system. Jefferson's opposition to the Bank (strict construction, "boundless field of power"), his retaliatory commercial program, his use of the "political heresies" note on Paine's Rights of Man, and the private 21 objections transmitted to Washington frame the conflict as one over the nature of the Union and the distribution of power rather than mere personal rivalry or French ideology. The dinner bargain over assumption/residence (with Madison's acquiescence) and the subsequent public attacks via Fenno's paper mark the open break. Washington's unanimous re-election closes the volume at the moment rudimentary factions solidify into the Republican interest.

Cross-volume: continues Vol. 1's emphasis on chronology and the whole man; anticipates later volumes' treatment of the 1793–1801 foreign crises and party battles. Adds key actors (Cosway, Short, Madison as full collaborator) and places (Paris, Versailles, Philadelphia) to the network. Strengthens the detailed biography with verbatim primary texture (Dialogue, Bank opinion, "tree of liberty," generational letter) and multi-level counterfactuals around the Hamilton feud and the decision to remain in office.

Collection note: Malone Vol. 2 supplies the indispensable mid-century scholarly narrative for 1784–1792; later diplomatic histories and the full Papers allow refinement but do not displace this synthesis for the Paris "flowering" and the domestic origins of the party contest.
