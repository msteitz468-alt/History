# Cities of Commerce: The Institutional Foundations of International Trade in the Low Countries, 1250–1650

Oscar Gelderblom's *Cities of Commerce* (Princeton, 2013) is a specialist
monograph in [[new-institutional-economics|institutional economic history]] that
asks how long-distance trade in Europe was organized and protected **before the
rise of strong territorial states**. It does so through the successive commercial
primacy of three cities in the Low Countries — [[bruges|Bruges]],
[[antwerp|Antwerp]], and [[amsterdam|Amsterdam]] — between 1250 and 1650, the
sequence Fernand Braudel made famous (Venice → Antwerp → Genoa → Amsterdam →
London).

## Core Argument

Gelderblom develops a third explanation for institutional change in European
commerce, against the two dominant accounts:

- **Against North / Acemoglu-Johnson-Robinson (strong states):** the strongest
  late-medieval and early-modern states were also the most belligerent, and the
  early expansion of European trade (11th–12th c.) happened when sovereigns were
  weak. Central courts (the Paris *Parlement*, the Burgundian *Grand Conseil*,
  the imperial *Reichskammergericht*) staffed by Roman-law professionals were too
  slow and too ignorant of mercantile usage to adjudicate cross-border commercial
  disputes.
- **Against Greif (private-order solutions):** reputation networks, merchant
  [[hanseatic-league|guilds/nations]], the community responsibility system, and
  the *lex mercatoria* were real and important — but they worked **only because
  urban governments supported and embedded them**. They were not substitutes for
  public institutions.

His alternative: **urban competition.** Europe's political and legal
fragmentation produced its own solution in the form of rivalry between cities
that competed to attract footloose merchants by continuously adapting their
legal, commercial, and financial institutions. Cities — not sovereigns — created
**inclusive, [[open-access-institutions|open-access institutions]]** because they
faced **economic** constraints (the credible threat that merchants would
relocate to a competing gateway), not the **political** constraints (limited
government, mercantile control of assemblies) emphasized by North and Stasavage.
This is captured in the process page [[urban-competition-commercial-institutions]].

Three conditions made the Low Countries the cutting edge of this dynamic: (1) a
dense network of cities with direct access to tradable surpluses at home and
abroad; (2) **footloose merchants** free to choose between ports serving the
*same* hinterland (unlike sole gatekeepers Lübeck, Venice, or pre-16th-c.
London); and (3) the **political autonomy** of commercial cities, giving their
magistrates the fiscal resources and legal power to adapt institutions. Crucially,
adaptiveness depended *paradoxically* on the freedom of merchants to choose their
own rules and the institutional heterogeneity this created: cities amended local
custom with foreign mercantile usages but refrained from codifying a single
preferred set, leaving merchants the largest possible menu of choices — an
**evolutionary, "adaptive-efficiency" selection process** (North's term, turned
against North's conclusion).

## What It Adds to the Wiki

This is the wiki's anchor source for the **institutional turn** in early-modern
European economic history and for the mechanics of how the
[[commercial-revolution|medieval commercial revolution]] was carried into the
era of global trade. It supplies the link between the abstract framework of
[[new-institutional-economics]] and concrete urban practice, and it gives the
[[great-divergence]] debate a sharp causal claim: institutional change persisted
in Europe (vs. the Middle East or China) not because of strong states but because
of a uniquely dense field of competing autonomous cities. It complements
[[early-modern-commerce|the organization of early modern trade]] (Trivellato/Parker)
from the European institutional side.

## Reliability and Bias

Strongly argument-driven and openly revisionist; Gelderblom selects three success
cases and reads the rest of Europe through them, so the thesis is strongest where
multiple gateways shared a hinterland and weaker as a general law (he concedes
London's early monopoly position was different). The quantitative appendices on
violence and Hanse collective action give the central claims an empirical spine.
Where it contradicts North and Greif, those contradictions are the substance of
[[institutional-foundations-european-trade|the controversy page]] rather than
factual disputes.
