# CAH Vol. IX — The Last Age of the Roman Republic, 146–43 B.C. (Crook, Lintott & Rawson eds., 2nd ed. 1992)

The direct sequel to [[astin-cah-v8-1989|CAH VIII]], narrating **the Fall of the
Roman Republic**. The editors begin at **146** (the destruction of Carthage and
Corinth — "the apogee of Roman fortunes") to supply the background to the **tribunate
of Tiberius Gracchus (133)**, and close with the **death of Cicero (43)** as the
symbolic demise of the Republic. The volume's organising question: how the era's
social, economic, legal, intellectual, and religious changes related to the story of
political collapse — civil strife, dictatorship, and the end of the Republic.

## Volume Synthesis Note

CAH IX narrates **the Fall of the Roman Republic** — from the apogee of 146 to the
death of Cicero in 43 — and its deliberate two-part structure (narrative chs. 1–12,
synchronic thematic analysis chs. 13–19 + Epilogue) is itself the volume's
argument: that the political collapse can only be understood against the **social,
economic, legal, intellectual, and religious transformation** of the age. The
ingested pages now carry that whole arc across the wiki.

**The political arc.** The narrative half traces a single escalating logic: the
**[[agrarian-crisis-roman-republic|agrarian crisis]]** and the **Gracchi** opened the
fatal split of **[[optimates-populares|optimates and populares]]**;
**[[marius]]** and the **[[marian-army-reforms|client army]]** made the soldier loyal
to his general; the **[[social-war-91-88bce|Social War]]** enfranchised Italy and
loosed the violence of the 80s; **[[sulla]]** proved an army could seize Rome and gave
the [[sullan-constitution|reaction]] its model; **[[pompey]]** showed the order could
not contain the extraordinary command; the **[[first-triumvirate|First Triumvirate]]**
seized the distribution of empire; and **[[julius-caesar|Caesar]]** — through the
**[[caesar-gallic-war-58-50bce|conquest of Gaul]]** and the
**[[roman-civil-war-49-45bce|civil war]]** — made himself **[[caesars-dictatorship|dictator
for life]]**, whose **[[julius-caesar-assassination-44bce|murder]]** brought not
liberty but the **[[second-triumvirate|proscriptions]]** and the death of
[[cicero|Cicero]]. The lesson of [[sulla]] — that the legions answered to their
commander, not the state — recurs as the volume's deepest single thread.

**The thematic half** supplies the structural diagnosis. **Cloud** shows a
[[roman-public-law|constitution]] with no word for "constitution," unable to solve
the over-mighty magistrate; **Crook** shows the [[roman-private-law|civil law]] in its
most creative phase ("jurists' law"); **Richardson** shows
[[roman-provincial-system|"provincial administration"]] accreting by default from
military command, the governor barely checked. **Nicolet** and **Purcell** show an
[[roman-economy-late-republic|economy]] of growth, not catastrophe, and a
[[plebs-urbana|capital]] whose "problems were the problems of success"; **Griffin**
the [[late-republican-intellectual-culture|cultural flowering]] (Cicero, Lucretius,
Catullus, Varro); **Beard** a [[roman-republican-religion|religion]] embedded in
politics, changing but not declining.

**The keystone is the Epilogue** — captured in
**[[fall-of-roman-republic|Causes of the Fall of the Roman Republic]]**. The editors'
verdict frames everything: the Republic neither simply "declined" (moral corruption is
rejected) nor was merely "assassinated" by the dynasts; rather, the **institutions of a
city-state could not run an empire** — the manpower/land squeeze, the over-mighty
commands lured by Alexander's ghost, the widened *populus*, debt, violence, and above
all the **inability to conceive reform except as putting the clock back**. The age was
one of **rapid advance, not decline**; Augustus "applied the brake." What was lost was
the elite's *libertas* and rule of law; what was gained was the **basis of empire** —
enfranchised Italians and provincials, a professional army, the command-paradigm for
the emperors — and the reduction of "the independent points of decision-making to,
ultimately, just one."

**Cross-volume tensions.** CAH IX dovetails with [[astin-cah-v8-1989|CAH VIII]]
(Gabba's *socii*, the agrarian crisis, the eroded *cursus*) and carries the eastern
pages to their close: [[ptolemaic-egypt]] to the brink of 31, the [[seleucid-empire]]
extinguished by Pompey's annexation of Syria (64), and the [[hasmonean-dynasty|Jewish
state]] absorbed in 63. It deliberately **revises** the older catastrophist and
"decline" narratives (of the economy, of religion, of the constitution) that earlier
scholarship — and the Romans themselves — had taken for granted. Forward, it hands to
**CAH X** the world the Republic's collapse had created: Octavian/Augustus, the
Principate, Cleopatra, and the integration of the Mediterranean it had conquered but
could not govern as a city-state.
