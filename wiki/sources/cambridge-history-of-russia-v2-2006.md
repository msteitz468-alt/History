# The Cambridge History of Russia, Volume II: Imperial Russia, 1689–1917 — Dominic Lieven (ed.)

## Source Summary

The second volume of *The Cambridge History of Russia* covers the imperial era from Peter I’s assumption of power (1689) to the revolution of March 1917 and the fall of the Romanov dynasty/empire. It provides comprehensive coverage of political, economic, social, cultural, diplomatic, and military history across 228 years.

Key characteristics of the era: emergence of Russia as a core member of the European concert of great powers; full-scale Westernisation of the ruling elites; the empire as a diverse, complex polity encompassing Russians and many non-Russian peoples.

The volume is deliberately thematic and comprehensive rather than narrowly narrative. It includes dedicated chapters on all major social groups, non-Russian nationalities and government policies toward them, women, law, the Orthodox Church, police, and the revolutionary movement. Seven chapters treat diplomatic/military history and Russia’s evolution as a great power in detail. Vignettes (e.g., Nizhnii Novgorod as portrait of a city) illustrate broader themes.

Editor Dominic Lieven (LSE) stresses diversity of perspectives and prioritises comprehensiveness. The volume draws primarily on American/British scholarship but aims to be legitimate in Russian eyes as well. Contributors include established leaders and emerging scholars.

It addresses periodisation challenges (e.g., peasant history spans volumes) but uses 1689–1917 as the best fit for the imperial proclamation under Peter to the monarchy’s fall.

## Key Themes and Historiography

Major unifying themes: Russia as empire (core vs. periphery, management of nationalities, identity geographies); Westernisation of elites alongside persistence of autocracy and peasant world; cultural and intellectual contributions to Europe/world; social structures (elites, middling groups, peasants); law, gender, Orthodoxy; state institutions (central/provincial gov, finance, police); military and great-power status; path from reform (Alexander II) to revolution amid war.

The volume gives weight to "unfashionable" core topics (foreign policy, fiscal/military systems, economy) often sidelined in recent scholarship. It treats the empire’s diversity seriously (non-Russians, women, legal order). Editor notes that micro-history can be as illuminating as broad surveys.

- Key acquisitions: "the Baltic provinces, Ukraine and Poland". Baltic opened trade routes; Ukraine/"New Russia" core of agriculture and industry; Poland "biggest thorn" but contributed economically and as glacis. (ll. 4308–4318). `produced:` economic growth; `contributed_to:` security and great-power status.

Objective reasons: "predator states" subjecting globe; alternative "unappetising". (ll. 4319–4321). `caused_by:` European balance-of-power context.
- Success 1700–1815: "creation of an effective military and fiscal state apparatus. This Peter I and his successors achieved." Advantages: less feudal baggage than Western Europe; "second wave" not lumbered by venal institutions. "autocracy and its alliance with a serf-owning nobility was an exceptionally effective (and ruthless) mechanism for mobilising resources". (ll. 4323–4337). `produced:` premier iron producer in 18th c.; great-power status.
- Turning point Crimean War (1854–6): "British and French fought and moved with the technology of the industrial era"; Russian finances collapsed; serf-based army lacked reserves. - Peripherality dilemmas: "A gap had emerged by 1900 between ... ‘First World’ ... and the ‘Second World’ countries of Europe’s southern and eastern periphery." Russia "poorer and politically less stable". "Building a political nation" harder on periphery. Empire as "huge burden on the Russian people". (ll. 4587–4637). `produced:` vulnerability to social revolution; `followed_by:` post-1917/1991 opportunities.
- Ottoman comparison: Ottomans "failure to sustain their power had disastrous consequences for millions of their Muslim subjects" (expulsions, colonisation). Russia "protected the Russian people from such a fate" but "at a great price in terms of economic exploitation and the denial of rights and freedoms". (ll. 4435–4449). `contrasts_with:` Russian success vs Ottoman decline until mid-19th.
- Pre-modern vs modern empire: "Before the middle of the nineteenth century almost all empires had been federations of aristocratic elites." Post-1850 "dilemma of modern empire": continental scale + multiethnicity vs nationalism/democracy. **Ch. 4 Russian culture in the eighteenth century (Hughes):**
- "The limited social range of the eighteenth-century Russian public and the overarching influence of the court also seemed to distinguish Russia from more developed Western societies." Few opportunities outside state provisions; "the only place you could publish was with state presses"; "the only institution that offered rigorous training... was the Imperial Academy of Arts". "As has often been observed, the bourgeoisie was missing." (ll. 6075–6076). `produced:` court-dominated high culture; `contrasts_with:` Western private/commercial sectors.
- "true culture was incompatible with despotic government and that the arts could never flourish if ‘enforced by the knout’." (l. 6075). `contributed_to:` tensions in Westernisation.
- Religious and secular art intertwined: "Nearly all the leading artists started their careers painting icons and frescoes"; "There was far more demand for icons than for portraits." "Orthodox scruples about ‘graven images’ hampered the development of Russian sculpture." First public monument (Falconet’s Peter I equestrian, 1782) was remarkably late. (ll. 6107–6108). `part_of:` interdependence of religious and secular culture.
- Westernisation debates: Karamzin mourned "loss of national identity"; foreigners applied "Orientalist discourse". Coxe: "It retains many traces of its ancient Asiatic pomp, blended with European refinement." (ll. 6089–6097). `produced:` hybrid identity.
- "In the first decade of the twenty-first century the cultural history of eighteenth-century Russia is still being written." Shift away from Soviet ideological frameworks; new studies of court, Potemkin, Catherine as patron. (ll. 6109–6113). `preceded_by:` Soviet-era distortions; `contributed_to:` ongoing re-evaluation.

**Ch. 5 Russian culture: 1801–1917 (Bartlett):**
- Chaadaev (1836) on Russian cultural nullity: "We have never taken the trouble to invent anything ourselves"; "we have disfigured everything we have taken". Russia "neither part of the continuum of European or Asian civilisation". (ll. 6139–6149). `contributed_to:` sense of inferiority from brusque Europeanisation.
- Contrast 1909 critic: Russia’s "remains of barbarism" now envied; "they are our teachers". (ll. 6158–6160). `produced:` reversal; Russian avant-garde pre-eminence.
- Landmarks 1830s: Pushkin *Eugene Onegin* (1823–31), Briullov *Last Day of Pompeii* (1833), Gogol *Government Inspector* (1836), Glinka *A Life for the Tsar* (1836). "This was also the year in which Pushkin launched *The Contemporary*". (ll. 6201–6204). `produced:` "Golden Age" of poetry.
- Under Nicholas I: Uvarov "Orthodoxy, autocracy and nationality"; Academy of Arts uniformed civil servants upholding classical ideals; Italian opera installed 1843; "the already low prestige of Russian music". Pushkin story notes "surprise that there are any novels written in Russian". (ll. 6181–6199). `contributed_to:` constraints on native development.
- By 1917: "it was no longer possible to claim that Russia had merely borrowed from other cultures, and contributed nothing original of its own." "In the space of a hundred years, the country’s artistic life had been transformed". Diaghilev Saisons russes, Stanislavsky, Tolstoy/Dostoevsky translations, Stravinsky *Rite of Spring* (1913). (ll. 6157–6168). `produced:` cultural identity "distinctive precisely for its strong national character".
- "the feelings of inferiority which were the residue of Russia’s brusque Europeanisation in the eighteenth century gave way to a pride in national achievements." (l. 6165). `followed_by:` post-1917 constraints but pre-revolution flowering.
