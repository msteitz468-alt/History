# Hayek — The Road to Serfdom (1944; Definitive Edition, ed. Caldwell, 2007)

The most influential anti-planning polemic of the twentieth century, ingested
here **as a primary source in intellectual history**: the founding text of the
[[road-to-serfdom-thesis]] and the root of Position 2 in
[[fascism-and-the-political-left]]. (The economics of the calculation debate
belong to the Economics wiki, where this text is separately ingested; this
page covers its historical argument, wartime context, and reception.)

## Argument summary

- **Thesis** (chs. 5–8): central planning presupposes a value consensus free
  societies lack; the resulting deadlock drives delegation and dictatorship;
  "economic control… is the control of the means for all our ends"; planning
  and the Rule of Law are incompatible. Full statement on
  [[road-to-serfdom-thesis]].
- **Mechanisms** (chs. 10–11): totalitarian selection favors the unscrupulous
  (lowest-common-denominator cohesion + the enemy principle — "the 'Jew' or
  the 'kulak'"); propaganda must extend to facts, perverting language
  ("planned freedom") and science under both Nazi and Soviet regimes.
- **Genealogy** (ch. 12 + 1933 memo): Nazism as "the culmination of a long
  evolution of thought" running through the German socialist camp —
  Sombart, Plenge, Lensch, Spengler, Moeller van den Bruck; "the socialists
  of the Left approached more and more to those of the Right."
- **Application to Britain** (chs. 13–14): Carr, Waddington, Crowther, Acland,
  and the 1944 Labour program as carriers of "the Germany of twenty or thirty
  years ago"; organized capital *and* organized labor jointly drive the
  monopoly-planning drift.
- **Prescription** (chs. 15–16): a federation with negative, rule-of-law
  powers, not international planning (which would require "a Herrenvolk");
  no blueprint — "a policy of freedom for the individual is the only truly
  progressive policy."
- **Hedges Hayek insisted on**: not inevitability ("If they were, there would
  be no point in writing this"); not laissez-faire (ch. 3's accepted
  interventions; ch. 9's subsistence floor); not conservatism (1956:
  conservatism "in its paternalistic, nationalistic, and power-adoring
  tendencies… is often closer to socialism than true liberalism").

## Volume Synthesis Note

For this wiki the book matters three ways. First, as **primary source for
the fascism-spectrum controversy**: Position 2's founding text turns out to
be more hedged than its popularizations — Hayek accepts a welfare floor,
rejects laissez-faire dogma and the conservative label, and disclaims
inevitability; the Definitive Edition's apparatus (Knight's report, the
Samuelson correspondence) documents how the "inevitability thesis" caricature
became the standard reading on *both* sides. Second, as **an artifact of
wartime intellectual history**: the 1933 "Nazi-Socialism" memo → Beveridge →
Reader's Digest → Mont Pèlerin arc is itself a case study in how ideas
acquire institutional carriers, with the [[beveridge-report-1942]] as the
planning consensus it answered. Third, as the **origin point of the
communism–fascism structural comparison** later formalized by Arendt and the
Cold War totalitarianism school ([[communism-and-fascism]]).

Cross-source tension worth tracking: Hayek's ch. 12 genealogy versus
Paxton's account of the radical right ([[paxton-vichy-france-1972]] and the
forthcoming *Anatomy of Fascism*). Knight's 1943 objections — militarism,
antisemitism, and parliamentary collapse understated — anticipate the
academic consensus critique almost verbatim; the wiki records the dispute on
[[fascism-and-the-political-left]] without adjudication. When Payne, Griffin,
and Flynn (*As We Go Marching*, also 1944 and cited alongside Hayek by
Position 2) are ingested, their treatments should be checked against both
this text and its caricature.
