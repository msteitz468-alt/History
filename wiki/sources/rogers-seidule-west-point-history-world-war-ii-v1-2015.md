# The West Point History of World War II, Volume 1 (Rogers & Seidule, eds.)

Volume 1 of the West Point History of World War II series, general editors Clifford J. Rogers and Ty Seidule. It comprises an introductory chapter, five chapters adapted from the larger West Point History of Warfare e-book, and a conclusion, making it self-standing. Contributors include leading historians: Steve R. Waddell (Road to War), Richard J. Overy (Britain Stands Alone; People and Economies at War), Robert M. Citino (German years/Blitzkrieg), Geoffrey P. Megargee (Barbarossa and the Beginnings of the Final Solution), Edward J. Drea (Japan Strikes: Pearl Harbor to Midway), and conclusion by Rogers.

This is the "West Point style" operational/tactical treatment — emphasis on decisions, consequences, maps, and military art. Highest priority for the WWII section of the warfare hub.

### Soviet coercive mobilization and production revival
- "After the initial German invasion, with steel production reduced by half, coal production cut by more than half, and the industrial labor force down by 35 percent, the only course left for the rump Soviet economy... was to focus all production on guns, to the virtual exclusion of butter. Output in the civilian industrial sector... fell by 1943 to only 46 percent of what it had been in 1937..." (Chunk1 ~90-110). Gosplan restructuring left "only the slenderest of margins for civilian consumption." Coercive system: factory allotments, hot meals tied to work, gulag for shirkers; unknown perished from hunger among non-workers. Result: "remarkable revival of military output... Between 1942 and 1945, the Soviet Union exceeded German production of almost all major classes of army weapons and aircraft, despite possessing an economy only a fraction of the size..."
- Gulag output example: prisoners produced "30.2 million mortar shells and 9.2 million antipersonnel mines" (Chunk2).

### US superabundance, War Production Board, and output dominance
- "Of all the combatant nations, only the United States proved able to provide both guns and butter in plenty... The American experience resulted from... the economics of superabundance." GNP "expanded by 87 percent between 1938 and 1945, almost doubling aggregate output." By 1943 Ford alone produced more military equipment than Italy. "the United States was producing more war goods than the rest of the world put together."
- War Production Board (under Donald Nelson) supervised resources without rigid state control. Kaiser Shipyards highlighted in production surge.
- "In 1942 the Americans built 112 major fighting ships, while the Japanese built 21. In 1943 the figures were 206 to 19."
- `[[actors/roosevelt-franklin-d]]` `produced:` [[processes/war-economies-wwii]] `contributed_to:` [[events/world-war-ii-1939-1945]]

### Lend-Lease impact
- USSR received "$10.7 billion of aid" (food 55-60% later years, plus trucks, rails, radios). "American supplies were vital to the Soviet war effort." Stalin privately: "without Western aid, the Soviet Union 'could not have continued the war.'"
- Britain principal beneficiary ($30B, mostly military + food); consumer spending fell only 13%. Reciprocal aid from Empire.
- Charts/captions detail categories (military, industrial, agricultural). Debate in historiography on exact contribution to specific victories (Moscow/Stalingrad vs. later).
- `contributed_to:` [[processes/war-economies-wwii]] `enabled:` Soviet/British survival in [[events/world-war-ii-1939-1945]] (via [[actors/roosevelt-franklin-d]] [[actors/stalin-joseph]] [[actors/churchill-winston]])

### German production paradoxes, Speer rationalization, and home front
- Pre-Speer: high early diversion to war but "military production remain[ed] surprisingly low"; "absence of a central committee... fostered widespread confusion"; military demands interrupted mass production for modifications. Per capita consumption down 25%+ by 1942.
- Hitler decree Dec 1941 + Speer (from Feb 1942) + Milch brought rationalization, industrial dispersal, ruthless foreign/forced labor exploitation. "not so much a production miracle as the final realization of what had up until now been a large but inefficiently mobilized war potential." Peak output Sept 1944 despite bombing (shift to oil/transport targets hurt later).
- "guns will make us powerful; butter will only make us fat" (Göring). Forced labor (incl. Auschwitz camp internees in arms factories); millions of compulsory workers.
- Efficiency contrast: "For every tank or plane Germany produced in 1942, it drew on a national resource base of 655 industrial or foreign workers and 1,161 tons of steel... For the Soviet Union... 162 workers and 145 tons of steel. American factories too were much more efficient."
- `[[actors/adolf-hitler]]` `contributed_to:` [[processes/war-economies-wwii]] (via Speer era); contrasts_with US/Soviet in same.

### Home front contrasts (coercion vs. patriotism/moral commitment)
- Soviet: "mixture of coercion and patriotic propaganda" ("Great Patriotic War"); gulag labor; women in combat roles (400k+).
- Germany: coercion + "cajoled the labor force... to continue the fight to the bitter end" even when defeat inevitable.
- US/UK: "fewer sacrifices, and a strong sense of moral commitment to a progressive cause"; consumer spending protected; women workforce surge but debated integration; strikes/protests allowed.

- "At the midpoint of the war in 1942, then, both sides had good reasons to hope... The key question was whether England and Russia could stay in the fight long enough to allow for that overwhelming preponderance of force to develop."
- Roosevelt/Churchill better appreciated "the truly immense industrial might of the United States" enabling multi-front (Pacific rollback, aid to USSR/UK, bombing, cross-Channel buildup).

**Key extractions (Pacific leaders: MacArthur, Nimitz, Yamamoto, Spruance; decisions at Philippines, Coral Sea, Midway):**

- **MacArthur (Philippines defense decisions, pre-Midway, ~3155–3292):** “Prewar defense plans called for the Americans to deny the use of Manila Bay to the Japanese by concentrating forces for a defensive stand on the Bataan Peninsula. However, General Douglas MacArthur, the commander of the newly established U.S. Army Forces, Far East, aimed to fight the Japanese invaders on the landing beaches on Luzon and defend the entire island. He argued successfully to the War Department that the islands could be held if more U.S. weapons and equipment were made available to the Filipino divisions then beginning to mobilize. A highly visible result of his efforts was the deployment of thirty-five B-17 heavy bombers to the islands, intended as a deterrent to Japanese aggression. Twelve of these were destroyed on the ground during the attacks of December 8, and two days later, only fourteen heavy bombers remained.” 
  - Consequence: “Over the rest of December, experienced Japanese naval pilots flying superior aircraft followed up their initial attacks and steadily whittled away at MacArthur’s surviving air force in the Philippines. When the main Japanese landings in Lingayen Gulf north of Manila began on December 22, only a handful of aircraft remained...”
- **Open city and withdrawal (Dec 1941, ~3308–3310):** “MacArthur declared Manila an open city and moved his headquarters to the island fortress of Corregidor at the entrance to Manila Bay. His remaining forces would withdraw to the mountainous Bataan Peninsula, fighting a phased delaying action to allow for food and supplies to move to Bataan.”
- **Explicit critique of decision (Luzon, ~3570):** “Contrary to prewar plans, General Douglas MacArthur attempted to repel a Japanese attack on Luzon by deploying troops to the beaches rather than concentrating on the Bataan Peninsula. The defenders were pushed back anyway, but in the retreat lost many of the supplies that had been moved out to coastal positions.”
- Later note (post-departure, ~3474, 3548–3551): “After MacArthur left for Australia... ordered MacArthur to leave the Philippines, at a time of his choosing... MacArthur departed Corregidor on March [date context].”

- **Nimitz (Coral Sea positioning and pre-Midway decisions, ~3815–3821):** “Admiral Chester W. Nimitz, commander in chief of the Pacific Fleet since December 31, 1941, quickly positioned two carriers, Lexington and Yorktown, in the Coral Sea. He was looking for a showdown with the Japanese fleet.” (Context: “After Pearl Harbor, the U.S. Pacific Fleet had been reorganized into four task forces, each with a carrier as its center and screened by cruisers and destroyers.”) “In mid-March U.S. cryptanalysts broke the main Japanese naval cipher, JN-25. Decrypted communications (code-named Ultra intelligence) then made clear to the Allies that a major enemy amphibious thrust against Port Moresby was imminent, to be spearheaded by two fleet carriers.”
- **Outcome of Coral Sea (tactical draw but strategic effect reducing Midway force, ~4723–4737):** “American and Japanese fleets had both had enough, and each withdrew, ending the first naval engagement where opposing ships never sighted each other and aircraft did all the fighting. The battle was a tactical draw: each fleet had one carrier sunk and another damaged. But in the broader picture, the outcome favored the Americans: the Japanese had to postpone their amphibious assault on Port Moresby and, more important, had to weaken the size of their carrier task force at the subsequent battle of Midway by one-third.”

- **Yamamoto (Midway plan and prior decisions, ~4742–4750, 5802–5805):** “On paper, the Japanese enjoyed overwhelming superiority, but Admiral Isoroku Yamamoto, commander in chief of the Combined Fleet, scattered his forces among widely separated commands. There was the strike force, set to attack Midway from the northwest, which had four fleet carriers, two battleships, and two cruisers. It would neutralize Midway’s defenses, enabling a separate invasion force—with one light carrier, two battleships, and eight heavy cruisers—to approach Midway from the southeast. The reserve, divided into a main force and a guard force, had three battleships and a light carrier, but was six hundred miles west of the strike force.” “The Japanese plan for the Battle of Midway was typically elaborate. The Americans’ ability to read Japanese codes, however, prevented them from being distracted by the secondary movement against Alaska.”
- **Background and Pearl Harbor decision (biographical/prior choice, ~6057–6145):** “Isoroku Yamamoto... both shaped and executed naval strategy in the first years of the Pacific War. ... A firm believer in the value of aircraft carriers, Yamamoto argued that ‘in modern warfare battleships will be as useful to Japan as samurai swords.’ ... As commander-in-chief of the Combined Fleet, Yamamoto planned the Pearl Harbor strike and received much of the credit for its success. But the American carriers had not been destroyed, and Yamamoto persuaded his hesitant colleagues to support another attempt to knock out the U.S. Navy’s operational capability before more ships could sail from American dockyards and it would be too late. The result was the Japanese defeat at Midway (June 1942), though this outcome was due at least as much to luck and ULTRA intelligence as it was to the complexity of Yamamoto’s operational plan.” (Career notes: graduated Japanese naval academy, Tsushima 1905, Harvard 1919–1921, attaché in Washington, Naval Air Corps headquarters 1930–1933, vice-minister 1936–1939.)
- **Later fate note (range context, ~6167):** “In April 1943, an American aircraft killed Yamamoto while he was flying to the Solomon Islands on an inspection tour.”

- **Spruance (Task Force 16 actions at Midway, ~5089–5095):** “...they were followed by strike aircraft from the Yorktown, launched at seven o’clock a.m. One hour later, Admiral Raymond Spruance’s Task Force Sixteen, organized around the carriers Hornet and Enterprise, sent its planes into the air—a risky move, since the Japanese flattops were barely within strike range.”
- **Supporting attacks and futility of early phases (~5095–5100):** “During this interval, Army Air Forces B-26s flying from Midway’s still-operational runways attacked the Japanese carrier force, as did U.S. Navy torpedo planes. Around eight o’clock, B-17s and sixteen U.S. Marine dive-bombers from Midway plus two torpedo squadrons from the carrier Hornet launched high-level bombing, dive-bombing, and low-level torpedo attacks against the enemy carriers. These were futile despite great bravery and loss of life. Torpedo Squadron Eight, for example, lost all fifteen of its aircraft, and half the marine planes were shot down, mostly by veteran Japanese pilots in swift, high-performance Zero fighters.”
- **Critical window and outcome (10:20–10:30 a.m. dive-bomber success, ~6040–6050):** “Between 10:20 a.m. and 10:30 a.m., U.S. Navy dive-bomber strikes left three Japanese carriers blazing wrecks. A Japanese submarine would sink the stricken carrier the next day. A few hours later, around 5:00 p.m., Hiryu suffered catastrophic damage from four 1,000-pound bomb hits on her forward deck.” (Also: “Lucky timing was a major factor in the dramatic success of the American dive-bombers...” “Without centralized command and control, the Japanese CAP pilots had no sense of the overall battle.
