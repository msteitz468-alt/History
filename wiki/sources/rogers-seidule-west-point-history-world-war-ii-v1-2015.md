# The West Point History of World War II, Volume 1 (Rogers & Seidule, eds.)

Volume 1 of the West Point History of World War II series, general editors Clifford J. Rogers and Ty Seidule. It comprises an introductory chapter, five chapters adapted from the larger West Point History of Warfare e-book, and a conclusion, making it self-standing. Contributors include leading historians: Steve R. Waddell (Road to War), Richard J. Overy (Britain Stands Alone; People and Economies at War), Robert M. Citino (German years/Blitzkrieg), Geoffrey P. Megargee (Barbarossa and the Beginnings of the Final Solution), Edward J. Drea (Japan Strikes: Pearl Harbor to Midway), and conclusion by Rogers.

This is the "West Point style" operational/tactical treatment — emphasis on decisions, consequences, maps, and military art. Highest priority for the WWII section of the warfare hub.

**Norway (airpower lesson feeding 1940):**
- "The real difference in Norway, however, was airpower. The X Fliegerkorps... omnipresent... broke up Norwegian resistance... kept the highly superior Allied naval forces at bay... convinced them that Norway was suddenly very much a backwater."
- Allied intervention (Trondheim pincer): "Command and control... a nightmare, with each of the on-site commanders actually reporting back to London or Paris"; lacking AA/AT; "constant and heavy air attack"; "dodging German Stukas without any antiaircraft guns"; reembarked after failure.
- Narvik temporary Allied capture (3 June) then withdrawal.
- "Exercise Weser required an exceptional degree of coordination among air, land, and sea units... Army units had fought with their usual skill... The loss of much of Germany’s tiny surface fleet, particularly its destroyers... was significant but also unavoidable."

**Plan evolution:**
- Original: "An early version of the OKH plan... looked rather like a replay of the 1914 Schlieffen Plan... main goal was to reach the coast of the Channel in order to seize bases and airfields from which to prosecute an air war against Britain, rather than any sort of decisive victory."
- Mechelen: "A command aircraft... accidentally put down in Mechelen... The Mechelen incident thus led to a complete redesign of the offensive."
- Manstein (31 Oct 1939): "General Erich von Manstein, a brilliant operational planner, worried that the great wheel through Belgium would push back the French but not knock them out. He proposed shifting about half of the German armored forces to Army Group A and punching through the supposedly impassable Ardennes—thus trapping the advancing Allied forces in a 'cauldron'..."
- Final (24 Feb): "Hitler liked Manstein’s concept... committed an even higher proportion... to the southern 'sickle cut'... It was the mightiest mechanized force that the world had ever seen: 134,000 men, 41,000 vehicles, 1,250 tanks, and 362 reconnaissance vehicles."

**Case Yellow / Fall of France:**
- Ardennes/Meuse (12-13 May): "the Panzers entered the Ardennes without incident—a long snake of tanks, trucks, and reconnaissance vehicles fifty miles long. A single sortie by French or British bombers could have wreaked havoc on it..." Guderian "forced his way across the Meuse that evening on his own initiative."
- Sedan: "the French Second Army... took the kind of pounding that only Polish veterans could have understood... tanks—a huge mass... heavy artillery... ceaseless divebombing of the Stukas. Units of the French Second Army broke in panic even before the Germans were over the river in force."
- Dinant (Rommel): "the defenders were in some disorder, as the French 18th Infantry Division (which had just arrived, without antitank guns or artillery) replaced the retreating 1st Cavalry Division."
- "By the end of the day on May 13, the Germans had torn a great gash in the French line some fifty [miles]."
- Abbeville 20 May: "the destruction of the Allied army to the north: a million and a half men. It was the greatest Kesselschlacht... in military history up to this point."
- Guderian: "He ignored his orders and continued on, undertaking a 'reconnaissance in force' that included—no surprise—his entire XIX Panzer Corps."
- Dunkirk: "By the end of May, the German offensive had hounded the Allies back to the last port still in their hands: Dunkirk... Hitler's decision to halt the Panzers at the Dunkirk perimeter... Rundstedt issued such an order on the evening of May 24... Hitler was not dictating to the generals at Dunkirk so much as he was adjudicating a dispute among them." "friction." "British aircraft flying from bases in Britain could now intervene." Dynamo: "some 200,000 men, along with 140,000 French and Belgian troops." "The British lost some 68,000 men during the Battle of France—about a quarter..."
- Case Red/armistice: "119 divisions in the front line... facing a French force that amounted to no more than 50 divisions... The French were forced to sign an armistice... on June 22, 1940, at Compiègne... in the exact same spot, and in the exact same rail car... that Germany had signed the armistice of 1918."
- Verdict: "No military campaign is perfect, but in Case Yellow, the German armed forces came about as close as anyone has—through good planning, aggressiveness, and no small amount of luck." "A lot had broken right for the Germans... circumstances had conspired to hand the Wehrmacht a perfect storm of opportunities."

**Britain Stands Alone / Battle of Britain:**
- "From July 1940 until the Axis invasion of the Soviet Union in June 1941, Britain and the British Empire stood alone..." Churchill: "if necessary alone." "We shall never surrender."
- Support: dominions (Canada/Australia/NZ/South Africa/India); US (cash-and-carry, destroyers-for-bases Aug 1940); SOE 22 July 1940; political warfare ("to set Europe ablaze"); bombing from 11 May (Mönchengladbach); "103 nights of raiding between May and August."
- Air defense: "Great Britain had developed a highly sophisticated air-defense system... Fighter Command, led by Air Chief Marshal Sir Hugh Dowding... radar chain... radio direction finding... Royal Observer Corps... This control structure meant that Dowding could deploy his fighters in the most efficient way..." 11 Group / 12 Group.
- Aircraft: "The new monoplane high-powered fighters—the Supermarine Spitfire and the Hawker Hurricane—were... considerably superior to any of the other major German aircraft, particularly the Junkers Ju87 'Stuka'... German fighters... very limited combat time over England—generally little more than twenty minutes..."
- Phases: Probing late June–early Aug. Eagle Day delayed to 13 Aug (weather). Airfield attacks 12 Aug–6 Sept ("fifty-three raids on air bases, though only thirty-two of these were fighter stations"; only brief unavailability due to dispersal/repair). London from 7 Sept (invasion prelude). 15 Sept: "a force of two hundred bombers attacked London. A quarter of the planes were shot down or damaged, a rate of loss considerably higher than previous raids and insupportable..." 298 Luftwaffe aircraft lost 7–15 Sept. Daylight suspended; Sea Lion postponed indefinitely 19 Sept.
- Production/pilots: UK 1,900 fighters June–Sept vs Germany 775. UK pilots ~1,400 start, 1,500 late Sept (rotation by Park to avoid fatigue). "Fighter Command had an average of 1,500 pilots on hand by the second half of September."
- Blitz: Night from ~7 Sept; >43,000 dead over 9 months; focus on ports/shipping/aero industry (Coventry 14 Nov etc.); ~85% bombs on port cities. "The change in German strategy to the bomber offensive threatened to be a more dangerous development for the British because there was no effective defense against the night bomber."
- Outcome: "The Battle of Britain did not result in the decisive defeat of either air force, but it did ensure that Britain avoided German invasion, while demonstrating to the German side that it faced a powerful and resourceful enemy..."

**Cross-cutting (from range):**
- "Case Yellow... compelled the entire world to realize the power of modern joint operations combining airpower with mechanized, combined-arms warfare."
- French shortcomings: "Based on their experience in the last war, the French were expecting a bloody and methodical clash of arms... Gamelin’s strategy and operational design did not work in 1940... but that is a very different thing from saying that they made no sense at the time." "The pace of battle had definitely accelerated, and French doctrine did not anticipate it in the way that German doctrine did."
- Poland contrast: "Unlike in Poland, the Allies did try..." "They had won so easily in Poland at least partially because the Poles’ two principal allies stood by passively and watched."

- **Inconsistent national self-determination and Eastern Europe map (Road to War):** "In redrawing the map of Eastern Europe, the victors allowed geostrategic considerations to trump their own rhetoric of national self-determination. - **Launch and overall importance of Barbarossa (intro to Megargee chapter, ~lines 399-452):** "On June 22, 1941, Adolf Hitler launched the Wehrmacht against the Soviet Union. This was a battle of two totalitarian states, two competing nations, and two rival ideologies..." "The importance of Germany’s invasion of the Soviet Union is difficult to overstate. It opened up a front that would decide the Second World War in Europe. ... More people fought, and more died, on the Eastern Front than on all the war’s other fronts combined. Nearly 75 percent of German combat fatalities, almost four million men, fell there. As for the Soviets, their losses are literally incalculable, but the best estimates put them in the range of nine million military and fifteen million to twenty million civilian deaths. Millions of those deaths occurred because of deliberate German policies, including the beginnings of the so-called Final Solution of the Jewish Question." - **Context after France 1940; Stalin and Hitler decisions (uncomfortable partnership, ~1308-1732):** Stalin "adopted a two-part strategy. First, he did all he could to maintain friendly relations with Germany. He increased the flow of the raw materials... He rebuffed British attempts... And he downplayed German intrusions..." "At the same time... Stalin brought in new senior leadership, which began to reorganize and modernize the Red Army." Hitler saw "Russia as a potential living space (Lebensraum) for Germans, a land peopled by inferior Slavs, and the center of the worldwide 'Jewish-Bolshevik' conspiracy." Practical reasons: "Germany’s long-term strategic outlook was not good. ... Key commodities such as oil, coal, and food were already running short." "if he conquered Russia, he could knock out Britain’s last potential ally on the Continent. ... Germany would still gain the resources it would need..." "most of the senior officers believed that the Wehrmacht would defeat the Red Army easily in a single campaign, leading to the collapse of Stalin’s government." - **German operational plans and flaws (OPERATIONAL PLANS, ~1741-2001):** "The Wehrmacht would attack with three army groups. ... Army Group North headed through the Baltic states to Leningrad... Army Group Center advanced on Moscow via Minsk and Smolensk. And Army Group South aimed for Kiev and beyond to the Donets Basin." "The plan called for a three-week pause after the initial advance, to allow the armies to rest, refit, and resupply..." "The German plan contained huge flaws. Its central assumption, that the Soviet regime would collapse after a military defeat, was doubtful." "the Germans grossly underestimated the Soviets’ military capabilities. True, the Red Army was not the Wehrmacht’s equal qualitatively, but its size... The Germans believed that the Red Army numbered two million men. The true number by June 22 was already over five million." Logistical: "The invading force would number three million Germans (plus a half million allies), 600,000 vehicles, and 625,000 horses. ... The Russian railroads... were a different gauge... The roads in the Soviet Union were also too few, and most of them were unpaved..." "most of it marched at the same pace as Napoleon’s army: its troops on foot, its supplies and artillery horse drawn." "the generals were taking an enormous risk." - **Racial war planning and orders (RACIAL WAR, ~2112-2303):** Hitler March 30, 1941 address: "This would be a war of extermination, he said. A race war. ... Soviet soldiers were not to be treated as comrades. Bolshevik commissars and intelligentsia were to be eliminated." "Brauchitsch had told the senior commanders on March 27 that the troops 'have to realize that this struggle is being waged by one race against another, and proceed with the necessary harshness.'" "The army entered into an agreement with the Food Ministry to starve as many as thirty million Soviet citizens..." "The army and the SS agreed that special units, including the Einsatzgruppen, would operate behind the lines to eliminate anyone who seemed to pose a threat—especially Communists and Jews..." "The army issued orders that all Red Army political commissars be shot immediately upon capture, that civilians suspected of resistance ('active or passive') could also be shot out of hand... and that German troops need not be punished for crimes against Soviet civilians. The army also failed to make adequate preparations to house or feed the millions of prisoners of war it expected to capture." - **Soviet plans, preparations, and miscalculations (SOVIET PLANS AND PREPARATIONS, ~2328-2751):** "In April 1941 Stalin declared a 'special threatening period of war' and started a secret, partial mobilization..." "The Soviet General Staff assumed that the Germans would need ten to fifteen days in which to deploy their main attack..."
