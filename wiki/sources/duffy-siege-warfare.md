# Siege Warfare: The Fortress in the Early Modern World (Christopher Duffy)

The classic study of **early modern fortification and siege warfare**, tracing the transformation of
European warfare around the **gunpowder fortress** from the French invasion of Italy in **1494** to
**1660**. The fifth of the 7 remaining available Processing-List sources (Phase 12).

## The Argument

Duffy reconstructs the **revolution in fortification** provoked by mobile siege **artillery**:

- **The artillery shock (1494).** Charles VIII's French army swept through Italy with a powerful mobile
  siege train that toppled the tall, thin medieval walls in hours — exposing the obsolescence of
  vertical fortification.
- **The bastioned response — the [[trace-italienne|*trace italienne*]].** Italian engineers answered
  with a new design: **low, thick, earth-backed ramparts** fronted by angled **bastions** that eliminated
  dead ground and let defenders sweep the walls and ditches with flanking fire. This **angle-bastion
  system** spread across Europe and, with the Iberian empires, around the world.
- **The dominance of the siege.** Because such fortresses could not be stormed cheaply, warfare became
  dominated by **prolonged, expensive sieges**, vast garrisons, and the slow mathematics of
  approach-trenches — pushing up the **scale and cost of war** and the size of armies.

## Significance for the Military Revolution

Duffy's account is a cornerstone of the **[[military-revolution|"military revolution"]]** debate. Geoffrey
Parker built his influential version of that thesis around precisely this fortress revolution: the
*trace italienne* drove the **growth of armies**, the fiscal expansion of the **state**, and (via
exported fortification) European military advantage overseas. Critics dispute how decisive the fortress
was, but no account of early modern war can omit it — and Duffy's is the standard technical treatment.
