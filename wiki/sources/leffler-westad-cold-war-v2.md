# The Cambridge History of the Cold War, Vol. II: Crises and Détente (2010)

The second volume covers the Cold War's **most dangerous crisis and its partial relaxation
(c. 1962–1975)** — from the Cuban Missile Crisis to the high tide of détente, with the
conflict's centre of gravity shifting into the decolonizing **Third World** (Vietnam, the
Middle East, Cuba, Africa). Ingested to build out the wiki's [[cold-war|Cold War]] structure.

## Range 01 Extraction — Leffler & Westad CHCW Vol. II (range 01)
**Key claims with verbatim grounding (exclusively from /tmp/chcw-v2-ranges/range_01.txt; Ch. 1 Gaddis pp. 1–21; Ch. 2 Jervis pp. 22–43; Ch. 3 Cooper start pp. 44–57+):**

- **Grand strategy definition and prevalence:** "if by 'grand strategy' one understands the calculated use of available means in the pursuit of desired ends"; "grand strategies dominated Cold War statecraft. They could no longer be deployed when military operations began, and retired when hostilities ended." "The grand strategies of the United States, the Soviet Union, and their allies therefore evolved in relation to one another, much as competitive species do within common ecosystems." (Ch. 1 Gaddis, opening; "Stalin's grand strategy" section)

- **Stalin's objectives:** 1. "continue the acceleration of history his predecessor Vladimir Ilich Lenin had begun" via industrializing Russia as base for revolution; 2. "a fusion of traditional Russian imperialism with Marxist–Leninist ideology" to extend boundaries for safety of socialist state; 3. "await the self-destruction of capitalism" via "internal contradictions" leading to wars among capitalists. "It was a curiously passive program for a revolutionary, but it reflected Stalin's conviction that the forces of history were on his side." Stalin "anticipated a 'cold war,' and developed a grand strategy for conducting it." (Ch. 1 Gaddis, "Stalin's grand strategy")

- **Roosevelt vs. Stalin asymmetry:** "No equally comprehensive strategy for confronting the Soviet Union emerged anywhere in the capitalist world before 1945." Roosevelt "assumed the best of Stalin's intentions" and "deferred generously to Stalin's postwar territorial demands" while expecting Wilsonian institutions (UN). "Roosevelt allowed for the possibility that a 'cold war' might not happen. Stalin regarded it as inevitable." (Ch. 1 Gaddis, "Roosevelt's response")

- **Kennan/containment feedback:** Containment based on Soviet "internal contradictions" (lack of legitimacy, no free elections, multinational empire overexpansion). "Democracy embodied legitimacy... If they could muster the self-confidence in their institutions... then future Soviet leaders could hardly continue to see history as on their side." Marshall Plan, NATO, West German/Japanese rehab, Tito defection "ensured" Stalin's strategy "of exploiting capitalist rivalries lay in ruins." (Ch. 1 Gaddis, "Kennan and containment")

- **Globalization of stalemate (NSC-68/Korea):** Americans "no more prepared than the Athenians had been to suffer setbacks with equanimity." Korea "convinced Truman and his advisers that the authors of NSC-68 were right: any part of the world threatened or even apparently threatened by international Communism – industrial or not – would have to be protected." Shifted US "from a strategy meant to retain the initiative by distinguishing vital from peripheral interests" to yielding initiative. (Ch. 1 Gaddis, "The global Cold War")

- **Ideological stalemate and Khrushchev reforms:** Post-Stalin, "as his successors watched the growing prosperity and political legitimacy of postwar capitalism, they lost any illusions that its self-destruction was imminent." East German 1953 revolt convinced Khrushchev "'socialism' had to be given 'a human face.'" De-Stalinization (1956 speech) "had the unintended effect of encouraging attacks on Soviet authority." Hungary suppressed "by harsher means than Stalin had ever employed." Berlin Wall 1961 "gave up any pretense that the people they governed preferred 'socialism' over democratic capitalism." Sino-Soviet split made "fragmentation of international Communism... irreversible." (Ch. 1 Gaddis, "Stalemate: ideology")

- **Nuclear stalemate:** H-bombs "at least a thousand times more powerful"; "for only the second time in modern military history" ecological constraint (non-use of poison gas). US "did nothing to assist the rebellious East Germans, Poles, or Hungarians" fearing catastrophe. 1962 Cuba "brought the world as close as it came during the Cold War to a nuclear war" but "Soviet and American leaders agreed tacitly not to use nuclear weapons again to try to break the Cold War stalemate." (Ch. 1 Gaddis, "Stalemate: nuclear weapons")

- **Tails wagging dogs / small power leverage:** "In so delicately balanced a situation, small shifts in allegiance could make big differences." Non-Aligned Movement (Tito-Nehru-Nasser); allies like Ulbricht, Rhee, Chiang "coerced" security guarantees by "flaunting their weakness." Vietnam: Johnson intervention after Diem ouster; Ho "acted on his own" and "these large Marxist–Leninist states let a small one tell them what to do." (Ch. 1 Gaddis, "Stalemate: tails wagging dogs")

- **Détente as stabilization failure:** By 1969 converging interests in cooling off; Nixon-Kissinger "most radical shift in American grand strategy since NSC-68." Objectives: Vietnam exit without appearance of force-out, Soviet arms/economic/Third World talks, China opening, restore presidential authority. "It all seemed to have worked" by end 1972 (Beijing/Moscow trips, SALT I). But "shaky foundations": required "carefully controlled 'linkage'"; assumed "equal distribution of calculable benefits" but rivalry "propelled by incalculable fears"; "conflated stability with justice." Brezhnev pursued détente but "new generation of experts" pushed seizing initiative in Third World (Vietnam irresolution + new Soviet power projection + Cuba model). "Détente... instead destabilized the priorities of both superpowers." (Ch. 1 Gaddis, "Détente: a failure of stabilization")

- **Soviet strategic overstretch:** Post-détente collapse, Brezhnev "concluded... that the global balance of power required demonstrations of resolve wherever Moscow's reputation might be at stake." Aid to insurrections in Yemen, Ethiopia, Afghanistan (1979 massive intervention "at great cost to the Afghan people, to the Soviet Union itself, and to its own anti-imperialist reputation"). Abandoned isolation from global economy; "hooked on the artificially high price of [oil]" post-1973; "contract of credit" and "sharp decline in oil prices" early 1980s hurt. Military spending "as much as 25 percent of gross domestic product" (US ~5% on larger base). Helsinki 1975: Brezhnev "committed his country to the principle that sovereignty could no longer shield brutality" for boundary recognition; this "formally endorsed the argument that the Soviet Union's adversaries had been making throughout the Cold War." (Ch. 1 Gaddis, "Soviet strategic overstretch")

- **End via Reagan/Gorbachev:** "By 1981, the Soviet Union had many enemies" (China/Deng, Charter 77, Polish pope, Thatcher, Solidarity/Wałęsa, Reagan). Gorbachev "abandoned the Soviet Union’s revolutionary-imperial aspirations and set out to make his state a normal member." But "to give up its ideology, to relinquish its sphere of influence... without at the same time transforming that state into a multiparty democracy and a market economy – was to hollow it out from within." (Ch. 1 Gaddis, "Reagan, Gorbachev, and the end of the Cold War"; "A Clausewitzian conclusion")

- **Identity definition and weight:** "national identity can be seen as the set of values, attributes, and practices that members believe characterize the country and set it off from others." "Identity is at work when people say 'We must act in a way that is true to what we are'"; "heavy affective weight, and this helps explain why scholarly arguments about the Cold War are often very bitter." (Ch. 2 Jervis, opening)

- **American identity content:** "democracy; individualism and voluntarism as contrasted to strong direction... opposition to concentrated power... the belief in a supreme being... a faith that this model or 'way of life' can, should, and eventually will be adopted by others as well." "American exceptionalism... has deep roots"; "founded to be different from the rest of the world... and it would or at least could remain uncorrupted." "We have it in our power to begin the world over again." (Jervis quoting Paine)

- **Soviet identity content + superpower aspect:** "built around the proletariat, the centrality of class conflict, and the transformation of individuals and societies." "If socialism was not superior to capitalism, its existence could not be justified." "the Soviet Union as a superpower, equal in status and rights to its rival." Brezhnev et al. "placed great store in the Basic Principles Agreement of 1972 because it seemed to ratify their equality." "It has always been one of the paradoxes of Bolshevik behavior that their leaders have yearned to be treated as equals by the people they consider doomed." (Jervis quoting Kissinger memo)

- **Symmetries heightening conflict:** Universalism (values not bounded); both "standard-bearers of progress and modernity"; both "second-image" thinkers (domestic regime nature primary); "prone to expect and seek transformations of politics" (Kto-Kogo for USSR). Shared restraint: "Each side believed that time was on its side." (Ch. 2 Jervis)

- **Asymmetries and Soviet vulnerability:** Soviet "came from the top down"; American "much less self-conscious" giving "flexibility... and a resilience." Soviet "pivoted not on what Soviet society was, but what it could be"; American "based on a view of what American society actually was." Soviet "grew out of an explicit ideology... formed in explicit opposition to capitalism"; American from differentiation from Europe/Britain. Consequence: "domestic reverses and the failure of the world to move in desired directions would be corrosive to the Soviet regime and identity." American "left relatively unscathed." (Ch. 2 Jervis, "Symmetries and asymmetries")

- **Identity and Third World:** "the power of identities and the related fact that each side understood the Cold War as a clash of social systems explains much." Third World = "uncommitted states and peoples"; "nothing less than each side's view of the rightness of its cause, the universalism of its values, and the answer to the question of whose side history was on." Khrushchev (Vienna 1961): "The Soviet Union is for change. It believes that it is now in the political arena and it is challenging the capitalist system just as that system had challenged feudalism in the past." "Ideas do not belong to any one nation and they cannot be retracted." (Ch. 2 Jervis)

- **Détente and identity incompatibility:** US saw détente "as a way to maintain the status quo in the face of American weakness"; Soviets "as a way to attain equal status and gains in the Third World." "Being treated as an equal was both a necessary part of a robust policy in the Third World and a valued end in itself." "détente could never end the Cold War: it could at best only make the struggle safer and more predictable." "The Cold War ended only when one side's identity did; it could not have ended peacefully otherwise." (Ch. 2 Jervis, "Détente, identity, and the end of the Cold War"; "Identity and the end of the Cold War")

- **Economic instruments vs. military:** US principal non-military instruments: "building an international economic system conducive to economic prosperity; engaging in persuasion, providing incentives, and occasionally imposing economic sanctions; and... promoting a robust US economy that could serve as... a beacon for the benefits of a free, enterprise-based, market-oriented economy." "Economic policy, broadly interpreted, was an essential complement to the policy of deterrence." In end, USSR "collapsed because of internal economic weaknesses" contrasting "robust performance" of US allies and tigers. "Communism did not work well economically." (Ch. 3 Cooper, opening)

- **1960s growth contrast & Soviet figures:** "The 1960s was a decade of high global economic growth." Western Europe/Japan "extraordinary growth"; US merchandise imports +170% 1960–70. USSR official: 7.0% 1960s (CIA est. 5.1%); "USSR in the early 1960s was on a roll." Trade with West rose sharply in détente (exports to Comecon $2.8B 1960 to $34B 1975); oil key earner. "In the Soviet Union, 15 to 17 percent of GDP was devoted to... military, and an even larger... portion... to investment, which... was used very inefficiently." Standards "increased respectably during the 1960s." (Ch. 3 Cooper)

- **US domestic economic Cold War tools:** Kennedy "get the economy moving again" via tax cuts (1964), investment credit; defense spending rose with Vietnam but NASA to $5.1B peak 1965 for technological frontier demonstration (moon landing 1969). (Ch. 3 Cooper)

- **Sanctions & trade denial architecture:** No MFN for most Communist states (high Smoot-Hawley tariffs persisted); COCOM strategic export controls with allies; credits generally denied. 26 specific US sanctions episodes 1960–75 (Cuba embargo from 1960 persists; full on N. Vietnam 1964; etc.). Jackson-Vanik amendment blocked 1972 MFN deal over emigration. (Ch. 3 Cooper)

- **Kennedy Round as Cold War economic offensive:** Proposed 1962 for "bold new round"; benefits explicitly included "enhancing its capacity to bear burdens of defense" + "promoting the strength and unity of the West, proving the superiority of free choice... maintaining US leadership of the free world" vs. "Sino-Soviet world." (Ch. 3 Cooper)

## Range 02 Extraction (full subagent; CMC + nuclear stalemate from /tmp/chcw-v2-ranges/range_02.txt)
- After the Cuban missile crisis, neither side wanted to risk a repetition along the heavily armed divide in Europe – as the Americans showed by wary responses to upheavals in Czechoslovakia (1968), Poland (1970, 1980–81), and all of East-Central Europe (1989). "status quo of a split Berlin and Germany" (Hershberg, Cuban missile crisis ch., ~p.86)
- The waning of superpower tensions fostered speculation that Kennedy and Khrushchev, had they lasted in power longer, might have ended the Cold War altogether. That seems unlikely. Neither yielded fundamentally incompatible views of ideology or the legitimacy of the postwar international order. (Hershberg)
- Though military friction in Europe subsided, the superpowers repeatedly clashed indirectly in the Third World – and success in Cuba may have facilitated disaster in Vietnam. (Hershberg)
- In 1965, when Johnson decided to bomb the north and send hundreds of thousands of US troops to the south, his entire national security team consisted of missile crisis veterans; McNamara later acknowledged that his experience with the quarantine directly influenced his thinking on the bombing. "if Khrushchev had 'blinked,' Castro never did – and nor did Ho Chi Minh" (Hershberg)
- Furious at being forced to back down – and submit to mortifying close-range inspection – Moscow resolved to catch up in the nuclear competition as rapidly as possible and never again be vulnerable to American pressure. (Hershberg)
- US leaders rationalized the rapid expansion of the Soviet ICBM force as a stabilizing component of what McNamara dubbed mutual assured destruction (MAD), and Nixon and Brezhnev enshrined it in 1972 as a state of nuclear parity. (Hershberg)
- Some scholars argue the crisis reinforced a process of superpower "nuclear learning" – increasing judiciousness and responsibility, cementing a stable "long peace" – but this runs up against evidence that in late 1983 nuclear tensions led to another comparably perilous trip to the brink. (Hershberg)
- More than any other single Cold War event, the Cuban missile crisis stimulated a voluminous historiography... partial yet substantial release of Russian, Cuban, CIA, and other formerly inaccessible primary source materials has spawned a new generation of accounts. (Hershberg)
- Both Khrushchev and Kennedy come off looking worse in terms of their actions before the crisis – more irresponsible and reckless... yet, once in the crisis, their shared achievement in escaping it appears even more impressive. (Hershberg)
- On the morning of September 12, 1963, President John F. Kennedy directly confronted the reality of nuclear stalemate... "neither the U.S. nor the USSR can emerge from a full nuclear exchange without suffering very severe damage and high casualties." (Burr/Rosenberg, "Nuclear competition in an era of stalemate, 1963–1975", p.89)
- "There is no way... no matter what we do, to avoid unacceptable damage in the U.S. if nuclear war breaks out." (General Johnson to Kennedy, NESC 1963 report briefing; Burr/Rosenberg)
- Having contemplated preemptive options during the Berlin crisis, Kennedy now recognized that the strategic advantage the United States had long enjoyed – the ability to destroy much of the Soviet strategic nuclear arsenal in a preemptive strike – was gone. (Burr/Rosenberg)
- "We can’t assume nuclear war won’t happen... This God Damn poker game." (Secretary of State Dean Rusk; Burr/Rosenberg)
- US presidents wanted to avoid nuclear war but nevertheless agreed that a robust nuclear posture... was the deeply implicit threat that Washington needed in order to play a central role in shaping world affairs. (Burr/Rosenberg)
- During the 1960s, the United States deployed over a thousand intercontinental ballistic missiles (ICBM), hundreds of submarine-launched ballistic missiles (SLBMs), and took the arms race in a new qualitative direction by developing accurate multiple independently targetable re-entry vehicles (MIRVs). (Burr/Rosenberg)
- The Soviets, determined never to be outmatched again in a crisis, began to field a formidable ICBM force. Before Moscow reached strategic parity... a stalemate had emerged, where neither side could launch a preemptive strike... without incurring horrific losses. (Burr/Rosenberg)
- The emergence of strategic parity at the close of the 1960s provided the context for superpower détente. (Burr/Rosenberg)
- The US nuclear weapons stockpile... stood at 29,000 in 1963 and peaked in 1966–67 at nearly 32,000... during 1969 to 1975, brought the total number of US ICBM and SLBM warheads from over 2,500 to over 7,000. (Burr/Rosenberg)
- US war plans posited huge nuclear strikes... the SIOP remained essentially the same until the mid-1970s. (Burr/Rosenberg)
- In Europe... both the Soviet Union and the United States stockpiled thousands of tactical nuclear weapons on European soil... both superpowers searched for "flexible response" options to raise the threshold for nuclear weapons use. (Burr/Rosenberg)
- The Pentagon fielded growing numbers of theater and tactical nuclear weapons during the 1960s... by 1967, it had stockpiled over 7,000. (Burr/Rosenberg)
- In October 1967, NATO approved MC-14/7, which emphasized the need for both conventional and nuclear options... (Burr/Rosenberg)
- By the late 1960s, Defense Support System satellites provided at best a twenty-seven minutes warning... some analysts argue that the "U.S. strategic posture gravitated to [launch-on-warning] option" by the early 1970s. (Burr/Rosenberg)
- Nixon and Kissinger... employed the "madman" strategy... but Kissinger eventually recognized that nuclear threats in diplomatic risk-taking had become too dangerous to use. (Burr/Rosenberg)
- The most significant achievement [of SALT I, 1972] ... was the ABM Treaty, which sharply checked an arms race in this area by barring national missile defense systems altogether. (Burr/Rosenberg)
- Brezhnev made a significant concession in Vladivostok... agreed to an overall ceiling of 2,400 strategic launchers... of which 1,320 could be equipped with MIRV technology, while not counting either American nuclear systems "forward-based" in Europe... (Burr/Rosenberg/Savranskaya/Taubman)
- Kennedy and Johnson overestimated both the threats and promises facing them... Caution ended up prevailing in the Berlin crisis and the Cuban missile crisis. (Costigliola, "US foreign policy from Kennedy to Johnson")
- In a telling Cold War discourse, a senior adviser recalled that Kennedy "always regarded the Chicom nuclear explosion as likely to be historically the most significant and worst event of the 1960s." (Costigliola)
- Kennedy called Latin America "the most dangerous area in the world." ... Castro survived... Trying to eliminate Castro helped set in motion a chain of events leading to the worst confrontation of the Cold War. (Costigliola)
- Kennedy announced... the Alliance for Progress. He called for sweeping changes... pledged $20 billion... The Kennedyites expected that this investment would double real growth rates... (Costigliola)
- Despite their soaring rhetoric about democracy, the Kennedyites chose anti-Communist stability rather than risk radical change. (Costigliola)
- By the end of his presidency, JFK had raised [military advisers in Vietnam] from 600 to 16,000. (Costigliola)
- On June 10, 1963, at American University, Kennedy called for a relaxation of tensions and "genuine peace" with the Soviet Union... In August 1963, US, Soviet, and British negotiators signed the treaty to ban all but underground nuclear tests. (Costigliola)
- "A nuclear war will be the death of all of our hopes..." (Johnson, two weeks after taking office; Costigliola)
- The Berlin Wall ended the hemorrhage of East Germans while allowing West Berliners their freedom... The Cuban crisis subsided with the missiles withdrawn and the Americans promising not to invade the island unless provoked. (Costigliola)
- Brezhnev’s highest foreign-policy priority was to prevent war... the blood of Soviet soldiers sanctiﬁed the postwar European borders that they were determined to preserve. (Savranskaya/Taubman, "Soviet foreign policy, 1962–1975")
- Achieving strategic parity... established the Soviet Union as a true superpower, able to expand its power and influence globally, while actively pursuing détente with the West. (Savranskaya/Taubman)
- The formal basis for the [Czechoslovakia] invasion, which subsequently became known as the Brezhnev Doctrine, was the claim that in the spirit of proletarian internationalism, every Communist party was responsible not only to its own people, but also to "other socialist countries and to the entire Communist movement." (Savranskaya/Taubman)
- As a result of very fast-moving and productive negotiations, the USSR and West Germany signed a non-aggression pact (the Moscow Treaty) in 1970... The process was completed in 1971 by a Quadripartite Agreement on Berlin... (Savranskaya/Taubman)
- Brezhnev and the Politburo approved the development of three new MIRV-capable ICBMs: the SS-17, the SS-18... and the SS-19... (Savranskaya/Taubman / Burr/Rosenberg context)
- Signed on May 26, 1972... SALT I did not actually reduce any armaments; rather, it froze the number of nuclear weapons... (Savranskaya/Taubman)
- The Final Act of the CSCE was signed in Helsinki on August 1, 1975... Brezhnev’s goals seemed to be achieved: postwar European borders were confirmed in an international agreement... (Savranskaya/Taubman)
- Détente began to unravel in the Third World... the fall of the last colonial empire – the Portuguese – at the very peak of détente triggered just such a Soviet response in Angola. (Savranskaya/Taubman)
- In de Gaulle’s view, change in East–West relations required, ﬁrst of all, the intensiﬁcation of concrete exchanges... de Gaulle’s spectacular visit to the USSR in June 1966... arguably marked the apogee of the Gaullist politique à l’Est. (Bozo, "France, 'Gaullism,' and the Cold War")
- De Gaulle began to spell out a road map... of how to eﬀectively overcome the Cold War. He did so most famously in his press conference of February 4, 1965... two decades of East–West confrontation in Europe had led to a dead end... the stalemate could be ended only by calling into question the very logic of blocs. (Bozo)
- France’s withdrawal from the integrated organization, announced in March 1966, therefore, was not only the result of the general’s quest for "independence"... but also designed to promote his vision of a new European order. (Bozo)
- The Soviet-led invasion of Czechoslovakia at the end of the summer came as a dramatic conﬁrmation... the détente that began to emerge was very diﬀerent from the one envisioned by de Gaulle. (Bozo)
- The adoption of the Harmel Report illustrated the limits of de Gaulle’s revisionist design on the Western alliance. Far from triggering the "disintegration" of the Western bloc, France’s withdrawal... actually led to a reaﬃrmation of US leadership... (Bozo)
- US attempts to use Marshall aid as a mechanism to oblige the states of Western Europe to co-operate more closely... accomplished little. (Ludlow, "European integration and the Cold War")
- American policy-makers were closely involved with the Schuman Plan from the very outset... John McCloy... played a crucial role in pressurising Bonn... US lobbying in favour of European integration was even more obvious... in the lengthy struggle to secure agreement to the planned EDC. (Ludlow)
- At the heart of Adenauer’s strategy lay the notion of Westbindung – of using institutional links such as the ECSC or EEC to tie the Federal Republic securely to the Western bloc. (Ludlow)
- Participation in the integration process could be further used by German leaders to demonstrate their ongoing Western alignment even when a degree of engagement with the Eastern bloc became possible. (Ludlow)
- In the French case, the clearest link is to be found in Paris’s enforced volte-face towards Germany between 1947 and 1950... it was in order to accommodate this defeat that France would resort to European integration and launch the Schuman Plan. (Ludlow)
- The British... brieﬂy but disastrously experimented with a policy of actively opposing the planned EEC... One of the key factors which brought to an end this abortive British attempt... proved to be American pressure... (Ludlow)
- Cold War considerations played a signiﬁcant role in both Harold Macmillan’s and Harold Wilson’s applications to the EEC... central to the calculations of the prime minister in particular was the belief that Britain needed to become involved in the discussions then under way amongst the Six about extending their successful economic co-operation into the ﬁeld of foreign-policy co-ordination – the so-called Fouchet Plan negotiations. (Ludlow)

## Range 03 Extraction (full subagent; European integration, Gaullism/détente in Europe, Eastern Europe, Mediterranean, Third World context from /tmp/chcw-v2-ranges/range_03.txt)
**Ludlow – European integration and the Cold War**:
- “A clear strand of Cold War logic thus ran through the soul-searching which preceded the UK’s 1961 bid.” Britain needed to end isolation from the Six to play a central role countering threats to NATO solidarity.
- Labour’s 1967 membership request “also had a Cold War dimension”: “the diplomatic fall-out from de Gaulle’s March 1966 withdrawal from NATO’s integrated military command” pushed Wilson to underline European credentials so the UK could counter Gaullist challenge.
- “The Cold War thus substantially reduced the number of countries which could participate in the initial stages of European integration. This was almost certainly a necessary precondition of European integration’s success.” Soviet bloc countries excluded; also neutrals and forced neutral status.
- Divided Germany “ensured a de facto equivalence in population and geographical size between France and Germany. This proved extremely valuable in allowing the structures of European co-operation to be set up on the basis of equality of representation and voting weight amongst all four ‘large’ member states.”
- “protective Atlantic cocoon”: East–West conflict “shaped the very international system into which the first European bodies were born.” US foreign-policy leadership + hierarchy in Atlantic institutions (NATO, IMF, GATT, Bretton Woods) provided security, monetary stability, and commercial liberalisation. Europeans treated Washington as first port of call. This removed need for early EEC to handle defence/security or monetary stability; allowed focus on attainable tasks of customs union and common agricultural policy, both reached by 1968. “Quite how valuable this protective Atlantic cocoon was… became apparent once the overarching system began to show signs of strain from the mid-1960s onwards.”
- EEC success 1958–64 “was undoubtedly a major ingredient in the growing confidence of many Western European politicians during the 1960s.” No longer bound to look to Atlantic for how a modern economy should be run; “slightly smug” gratification inclined them to look askance at US policy and steer more autonomous course.
- Germany’s emergence as foreign-policy actor “owed a great deal to the European integration process.” Bonn’s low profile in early years gave way; “Germany’s role in defusing the EEC crisis of 1963 had… been the first significant break”; Schröder took lead.
- Western European success, stability, prosperity “did so much to destabilise Communist rule in Eastern Europe as the Cold War came to an end.” East Germans, Poles, Czechs conscious of quality-of-life disparity and Western confidence vs. Eastern gloom; fear of Germany became “steadily more anachronistic” as FRG became advocate of multilateralism. Successor regimes quickly adopted “return to Europe” rhetoric. “The expansion of the EU in May 2004 to include eight former members of the Communist bloc should in many ways be seen as the moment when the Cold War division of Europe was definitively consigned to the history books.”
- “European integration and the Cold War have thus never been entirely immune from interaction… Neither caused the other… But it appears clear that their paths intersected at multiple points… European integration was profoundly shaped by the early Cold War and continued to be affected by the East–West struggle over the next forty years; its success, moreover, played a role in bringing the Cold War to a peaceful end and has guided the destinies of both halves of the once divided continent in the years since 1989.”

**Hanhimäki – Détente in Europe**:
- “The main purpose of this chapter is to argue that European détente was, ﬁrst and foremost, a European project… détente actually began (and continued far longer) in Europe.” Division of Germany at heart; “it was ultimately his [Brandt’s] policy of multiple ‘openings’ – most signiﬁcantly to the USSR, Poland, and East Germany – that ushered in an era of détente in Europe.”
- “the relaxation of East–West tensions in Europe was a result of a European challenge to the excesses of bipolarity. Some of these challenges came in the form of nationalistic needs – be it Charles de Gaulle’s eﬀort to lift France’s international status or, most signiﬁcantly, Willy Brandt’s pursuit of Ostpolitik.” Harmel Report 1967 and Davignon Report 1970 showed agreement on improved East–West relations and interallied cooperation. Culmination: CSCE 1975, “where Europe’s postwar era ﬁnally came to an end.”
- Early 1960s crises (Berlin Wall 1961, Cuban missile crisis 1962) “cruel reminders of the central role that the Soviet Union and the United States continued to play.” Europeans “appeared as powerless bystanders”; “the Cold War appeared primarily, if not exclusively, as a game which could be decided only by the two principal protagonists.” Europe “increasingly like a sideshow” (Vietnam, Africa, Middle East, US-PRC triangle more urgent).
- “the Cold War international system was not a simple hierarchical construction… Multipolarity existed under the cloak of bipolarity, and the weak inﬂuenced the policies of the strong.” Use Tony Smith’s “pericentrism”: “junior actors may have interests, passions, and types of leaders wanting to take advantage of what they perceive to be an international contest to give shape to domestic, or regional, or even global organizations of power.”
- De Gaulle (sometimes “a neutralist for nationalistic reasons”): ruled over decade after 1958; withdrew France from NATO’s integrated military structure (1966), pursued independent nuclear (force de frappe), strengthened Franco-German special relationship (1963 treaty), embarked on independent initiatives with Eastern Europe (Romania) and USSR; visited Moscow; recognized PRC without consulting US; “Vive le Québec Libre” (Montreal 1967). “Of all the centrifugal tendencies in the history of NATO during the Cold War, it was the prominent role embraced by France under de Gaulle to lead a more independent Europe that caused the severest headaches in Washington.” Unsuccessful overall but expressed “a certain sentiment not only in France but in Free Europe as a whole” for Europe to have interests not identical with US.
- West German doubts about US leadership (none of de Gaulle’s global pretensions); focus on reunification. “The first steps towards Ostpolitik, the so-called ‘policy of movement,’ was in large part a reaction to the Wall, the apparent lack of Western commitment to German uniﬁcation, and the failure of the Hallstein Doctrine.” Adenauer policies failed to advance unification substantially.
- Harmel Report (NATO, late 1967): emphasis on military preparedness + “new” stress on negotiations with Warsaw Pact. “the ‘multilateralization of détente’.” Reykjavik 1968 reaffirmed commitment and favored Mutual Force Reductions talks.
- Bucharest Declaration (Warsaw Pact, summer 1966) and 1967 foreign ministers’ meeting repeated call for all-European security conference.
- August 1968 Warsaw Pact invasion of Czechoslovakia (“Prague Spring”): “a true reﬂection of the sentiments of those who ran the Soviet Union” – “a determination never to permit a socialist country to slip back into the orbit of the West” (Dobrynin memoir). Brezhnev Doctrine. Initially seemed death blow to détente; “Ostpolitik is completely blocked” (Birrenbach to Rusk, Sept 1968).
- Brandt chancellor after 1969 elections; Annäherung (rapprochement) though perhaps not enough Wandel (change). “Wandel durch Annäherung.” Treaties: German-Soviet (Aug 1970), Four Power Berlin (Sept 1971), Basic Treaty East-West Germany (Dec 1972). Soviets/East Germans eager for different reasons (consolidate division vs. Brandt’s transformation goal). FRG and GDR joined UN 1973.
- CSCE: Helsinki Accords signed 1 Aug 1975 (35 nations, heads of state); biggest European multilateral gathering since WWII. Long history (Molotov 1954 proposal; Budapest appeal 1969 after Prague; Finnish mediation; Dipoli 1972; Geneva/Helsinki negotiations). Four Baskets: I (traditional security/inviolability of borders); II (economic); III (human rights – “perhaps most controversially”); IV (“rarely mentioned – was perhaps the most important of all: it called for follow-up conferences, thereby ensuring that the accords would become a ‘living’ document”). “the signing ceremony… was as much the beginning of a process as it was an end.” “the CSCE did mark a certain rebirth of Europe.” “signaled the emergence of human security as an important and recognized aspect of international relations.” Basket III later manifesto for dissidents; allowed possibility of border changes by “peaceful means.” Andropov: “We are the masters in this house.” Kissinger reportedly uninterested (“written ‘in Swahili’”). West Europeans used it to advance Davignon Report ideas toward common foreign/security policy. East–West trade rose nearly sixfold 1970–1979 (financed by Western loans that later bankrupted some bloc countries). “One thing the Conference has already achieved: to get it accepted for the ﬁrst time by Communist states that relations between peoples… should be the subject of multilateral discussion” (Elliott 1974).
- European détente “a complex and constantly evolving process… sprang from the national aspirations of several countries; it represented a rebellion of sorts against the formation of tight blocs.” Brandt as symbol. “The détente in Europe that was launched in the late 1960s, however, was very diﬀerent from its Soviet–American counterpart. It was nurtured and driven by European leaders like Brandt and was embodied in European institutions like the CSCE.” Practical results difficult to measure (open-ended; no single mileposts like SALT); “for all their ambiguities – perhaps because of their ambiguities – were of great consequence.” Unlike superpower détente (widely proclaimed dead by 1979), European process “lingered on into the 1980s” (Belgrade, Madrid, Ottawa follow-ups); CSCE still exists as OSCE. “Getting to the point of signing the Helsinki Final Act on August 1, 1975, had required a collective change in the mindsets of leaders on both sides of the Iron Curtain.” Brandt Nobel 1971: Europe would “grow beyond the European Economic Community and develop into a union which will be able to assume part of the responsibility for world aﬀairs, independently of the United States but ﬁrmly linked with it.”

## Volume Synthesis Note

CHCW Vol. II covers the middle Cold War (c. 1962–1975): the Cuban Missile Crisis as the most dangerous moment, the shift to strategic stalemate/nuclear parity, the move from confrontation to détente (Nixon-Kissinger, Brandt Ostpolitik, Helsinki/CSCE), the center of gravity in the Third World (Vietnam as the great hot war, Middle East oil/power struggles, Cuban internationalism), European integration/Gaullism tensions with US leadership, Eastern European evolution from Stalinism to reform pressures (Prague Spring, Polish protests), Sino-Soviet split, and the social/economic underpinnings (Western consumer society/consensus vs. Eastern growth limits, debt, and eventual delegitimization). It frames détente as both a European project (Brandt, CSCE Basket III human rights as “living document”) and a superpower charade (Nixon-Kissinger using China to contain USSR while pursuing symbolic military agreements that did not resolve underlying instabilities). Key theme: the limits of bipolar structures, the influence of “pericentrism” (junior actors shaping outcomes), and how Western economic success and openness contributed to Eastern crisis and the Cold War’s eventual peaceful end.
