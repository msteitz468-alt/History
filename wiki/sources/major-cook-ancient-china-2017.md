# Ancient China: A History (Major & Cook, 2017)

A single-volume narrative survey of early China — the **East Asian Heartland
Region** — from the Neolithic to the Three Kingdoms (c. 7000 BCE–280 CE),
written to incorporate the flood of archaeologically recovered texts and
artefacts that has reshaped the field since the 1970s. Major and Cook organise
the story around the slow, contingent coalescence of "many diverse cultures"
into the first recognisably Chinese empires, against the folk myths of an
isolated and unchanging China.

This page consolidates and extends the wiki's coverage of the Chinese dynastic
spine. It complements [[liu-chinese-neolithic-2004|Liu (2004)]] (settlement
archaeology of state formation, Neolithic–Erlitou) and
[[cambridge-history-of-ancient-china|CHAC]] (philological/art-historical
backbone), supplying a continuous political narrative through the Han and the
Three Kingdoms that those leave thinner.

## Argument in Brief

- "**China**" is an anachronism before the empires: the Heartland Region was a
  mosaic of cultures that converged culturally, linguistically and politically
  only slowly. The book's organising unit is the
  [[east-asian-heartland-region|East Asian Heartland Region]], not "China."
- The Neolithic is best understood as a **[[jade-age|Jade Age]]** — a long era
  in which worked jade, not metal, was the medium of elite ritual and status,
  from Hongshan and [[liangzhu-culture|Liangzhu]] to [[longshan-culture|Longshan]].
- The **Three Dynasties** (Xia–Shang–Zhou) are a partly mythologised native
  framework; [[xia-dynasty|Xia]] remains an "enigma," [[erlitou|Erlitou]] is the
  archaeological reality behind the early-Bronze-Age horizon, and the
  [[shang-dynasty|Shang]] is the first textually attested state (oracle bones).
- The long arc from **[[zhou-dynasty|Western Zhou]] ritual kingship** through the
  [[spring-and-autumn-period-771-bce|Spring and Autumn]] and
  [[warring-states-period-475-bce|Warring States]] fragmentation to
  [[qin-unification-221-bce|Qin unification]] is the political core; the
  [[han-dynasty|Han]] then institutionalises the empire and an imperial
  Confucian–cosmological synthesis.
- Intellectual history is read through **excavated manuscripts** as much as
  transmitted texts: the "hundred schools" ([[confucianism]], [[mohism]],
  [[daoism]], [[legalism]]) are recovered partly from tombs.

## Volume Synthesis Note

Major and Cook's organising move is to dissolve "China" as a primordial actor and
replace it with the **[[east-asian-heartland-region|East Asian Heartland Region]]**
— a geographic stage on which many cultures interacted and only gradually fused.
This reframing carries the book's two polemical targets, the myths of "isolated
China" and "unchanging China," and aligns it with the multi-regional /
[[quxi-leixing|quxi leixing]] reading of Chinese origins ([[liu-chinese-neolithic-2004|Liu]],
[[origins-chinese-civilization-monocentric-vs-multiregional|the origins debate]])
rather than the old Central-Plains-centric nationalist narrative
([[nationalism-and-archaeology]]).

The book's signature periodisation is the **[[jade-age|Jade Age]]**: it treats the
Chinese Neolithic as defined less by ceramics or incipient metal than by an
elaborate jade-working ritual economy (Hongshan pig-dragons, Liangzhu cong and bi,
Longshan goblets), making worked jade the long prelude to bronze ritual.
This usefully bridges [[liu-chinese-neolithic-2004|Liu's]] settlement archaeology
and the later [[shang-dynasty|Shang]] bronze ritual order.

For the wiki the volume's main payload is the **continuous dynastic narrative**
from [[zhou-dynasty|Western Zhou]] ritual kingship through the
[[spring-and-autumn-period-771-bce|Spring and Autumn]] and
[[warring-states-period-475-bce|Warring States]] eras to
[[qin-unification-221-bce|Qin unification]] and the [[han-dynasty|Han]] imperial
synthesis, closing on the [[three-kingdoms-period-220-ce|Three Kingdoms]]. It
supplies actor depth (Liu Bang, Emperor Wu, Wang Mang) and the empire's
institutional and cosmological scaffolding (the imperial Confucian–Five-Phases
synthesis, the Salt and Iron debate, court–eunuch–consort-clan politics) where the
wiki previously had isolated actor stubs.

Methodologically the book's most current contribution is reading early Chinese
thought and religion through **excavated manuscripts and tombs** — Mawangdui,
Guodian, Baoshan, the Zeng Hou Yi assemblage — rather than the transmitted
canon alone. This is its real edge over older surveys and its main complement to
[[cambridge-history-of-ancient-china|CHAC]]: where CHAC is philological and
art-historical, Major and Cook narrate the politics and weave the archaeology
into it. No sharp contradiction with already-ingested sources emerged; the
volume mostly fills and connects, and defers to Liu on state-formation theory.
