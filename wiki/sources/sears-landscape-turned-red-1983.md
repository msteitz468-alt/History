# Landscape Turned Red: The Battle of Antietam (Stephen W. Sears, 1983)

**Source page for Stephen W. Sears's acclaimed operational and narrative history of the 1862 Maryland Campaign and the Battle of Antietam (17 September 1862).** The book is grounded in a rich base of primary testimony, including the extraordinary Carman-Gould collection of veteran recollections compiled decades after the fight. Sears portrays Antietam as the bloodiest single day in American history and a pivotal turning point: the high-water mark of the Confederacy's first invasion of the North, the catalyst for the Emancipation Proclamation, and a demonstration of both the limits of limited-war thinking and the operational constraints of Civil War command and control.

**Sears-LTR-R1-01: AntietamSingleDayCasualtyRecord**
**Grounding quote:** "Of all the days on all the fields where American soldiers have fought, the most terrible by almost any measure was September 17, 1862. The battle waged on that date, close by Antietam Creek at Sharpsburg in western Maryland, took a human toll never exceeded on any other single day in the nation’s history. So intense and sustained was the violence, a man recalled, that for a moment in his mind’s eye the very landscape around him turned red."
**Locator:** "Introduction, first paragraph"
**Analysis:** Sears positions Antietam as the bloodiest single day in U.S. military history through its unmatched casualty total and sustained intensity. The eyewitness image of the landscape turning red captures the visceral scale of violence that set it apart even from other Civil War engagements. This establishes the Maryland campaign as a decisive military and political turning point whose outcome would reshape the war’s direction across multiple dimensions.

**Sears-LTR-R2-01: LostOrderDiscoveryByIndianaCorporal**
**Grounding quote:** "Sergeant John M. Bloss and Corporal Barton W. Mitchell of Company F were chatting idly when Mitchell noticed a bulky envelope in the tall grass nearby and picked it up. Scattered through the text were names the Yankee soldiers knew only too well—Jackson, Longstreet, Stuart. It concluded, “By command of Gen. R. E. Lee” and was signed, “R. H. Chilton, Assist. **Locator:** "Ch. 3, meadow encampment of 27th Indiana outside Frederick, September 13, 1862"
**Analysis:** The envelope contained Lee's complete operational plan for the Harper's Ferry operation, including the division of the Army of Northern Virginia into multiple separated columns. Mitchell and Bloss immediately recognized its value beyond the cigars and escalated it through company and regimental command without delay. This chance find in an abandoned Confederate campsite gave the Federals an exact, dated blueprint of enemy movements and timetable that no prior cavalry reports or civilian intelligence had supplied.
