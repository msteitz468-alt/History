# Cleopatra: A Life (Schiff, 2010)

**Source page for Stacy Schiff, *Cleopatra: A Life* (Little, Brown, 2010).**

The definitive modern biography of Cleopatra VII, last queen of Ptolemaic Egypt. Schiff presents Cleopatra not as the seductive temptress of Roman propaganda or Hollywood fantasy but as a shrewd, multilingual, politically astute Hellenistic ruler who maneuvered brilliantly (if ultimately unsuccessfully) at the intersection of Egyptian, Greek, and Roman power. The book restores the context of the late Ptolemaic court, the Alexandrian War, the relationships with Julius Caesar and Mark Antony, and the economic and diplomatic realities that shaped her reign. It is especially strong on how Roman sources (and later Western culture) deliberately distorted her image to magnify the victory of Octavian/Augustus and to police the dangers of female power.

## Methodological Notes

- Thorough source criticism of the surviving ancient accounts (Plutarch's *Life of Antony*, Appian, Cassius Dio, Lucan, Cicero's letters, Suetonius, Josephus), noting their biases, distances from events, and rhetorical agendas.
- Rejects the “vamp” or “femme fatale” cliché; emphasizes Cleopatra’s education, administrative competence, linguistic fluency (she was the first Ptolemy to learn Egyptian), and use of spectacle, coinage, and cult to project legitimacy.
- Restores the Ptolemaic context: dynastic incest, sibling rivalry, the importance of Alexandria as a cosmopolitan capital, the economic power of Egypt’s grain, and the realities of Roman debt and intervention.
- Treats the relationships with Caesar and Antony as political alliances as much as (or more than) romantic ones, while acknowledging the genuine personal and strategic bonds.
- Careful with the sparse primary evidence (almost no papyri from her reign survive in Alexandria) and the heavy overlay of Augustan propaganda.

## Key Contributions

- The clearest account of Cleopatra’s early life, the sibling wars with Ptolemy XIII, the exile in Syria, and the famous “sack” arrival in Caesar’s quarters in 48 BCE.
- Detailed reconstruction of the Alexandrian War (48–47 BCE), the role of the royal palace as a fortress, the harbor fighting, the library fire, and Cleopatra’s political calculations.
- Strong treatment of the Caesar period in Rome (46–44 BCE), the birth of Caesarion, and Cleopatra’s position as a foreign queen in the capital.
- The Antony years: the “Donations of Alexandria,” the Parthian campaign, the marriage question, the propaganda war with Octavian, and the battle of Actium (31 BCE).
- The final days in Alexandria: the siege, the suicides of Antony and Cleopatra, the fate of the children, and Octavian’s triumph.
- Historiographical clarity on how Augustus shaped the enduring image of Cleopatra as the dangerous Eastern seductress who had to be destroyed for Rome’s (and the West’s) sake.
