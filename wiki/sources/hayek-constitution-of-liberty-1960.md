# Hayek, *The Constitution of Liberty* (1960)

**The Definitive Edition**, ed. Ronald Hamowy, vol. 17 of *The Collected Works of
F. A. Hayek*, series ed. Bruce Caldwell (University of Chicago Press, 2011).

Hayek's magnum opus of political philosophy: a systematic restatement of
classical-liberal principles for the twentieth century, written at Chicago in the
1950s and published in 1960 — deliberately timed for the centenary of the
publication of Mill's *On Liberty* era and conceived, per Caldwell, as the
constructive counterpart to the polemical [[hayek-road-to-serfdom-1944|Road to
Serfdom]] (1944). Where *Serfdom* warned against central planning, *Constitution*
lays out the positive case: what freedom is, why civilization depends on it, and
what legal and institutional order (the [[rule-of-law]]) preserves it. The
Postscript, "Why I Am Not a Conservative," is among the most cited essays in
twentieth-century political thought.

Structure: **Part I — The Value of Freedom** (chs. 1–8: the epistemic case for
liberty, progress, responsibility, equality, majority rule); **Part II — Freedom
and the Law** (chs. 9–16: coercion, the historical evolution of the rule of law
from ancient Athens through the English Whigs, the American founding, the German
*Rechtsstaat*, and its twentieth-century decline); **Part III — Freedom in the
Welfare State** (chs. 17–24: unions, social security, taxation, money, housing,
agriculture, education); **Postscript**.

For the wiki, the book is source material at three levels: (1) a primary document
of Cold War-era liberal thought (its 1960 publication and reception are
themselves historical facts); (2) an influential — and contested — work of
*intellectual history* (the "two traditions" of liberty, the rule-of-law
genealogy); (3) the theoretical foundation for the later Hayek works already
ingested ([[hayek-individualism-economic-order-1948]],
[[hayek-fatal-conceit-1988]]).

## Composition and Reception (from Hamowy's introductory essay)

- Conceived after the hostile reception of *The Road to Serfdom* (1944), as
  Hayek's systematic positive statement of the principles of a free society;
  written during his Committee on Social Thought years at Chicago, finished
  on his 60th birthday (8 May 1959), published February 1960.
- Caldwell reads it within Hayek's "Abuse of Reason" project — the response to
  scientism and the planning mentality; the argument extends the
  [[knowledge-problem]] from the [[socialist-calculation-debate]] into
  political and legal theory.
- Reception was strikingly thin: published 9 Feb 1960, reviewed in only about a
  dozen periodicals. Sidney Hook's hostile *New York Times Book Review* piece
  ("his economic philosophy points the road to disaster") set the early tone;
  Jacob Viner's scholarly review praised the learning but judged the conclusions
  "far too doctrinaire." Hayek complained privately (March 1960) that without
  friends like Henry Hazlitt the book "would not yet have had a decent review."
  The book's stature
  grew over decades, becoming a foundational text for post-war neoliberal and
  libertarian thought and — after 1989 — widely cited in post-communist
  constitutional debates. Margaret Thatcher's apocryphal-but-emblematic "this
  is what we believe" gesture attached to this book.
- Hamowy (himself a historian and early critic of ch. 15's concessions) supplies
  a critical apparatus of over 200 added citations; the Definitive Edition moves
  Hayek's massive scholarly notes to footnotes.

## Argument Map (where the book's content lives in the wiki)

- **Part I (chs. 1–8)** — definition of liberty, the epistemic case, the two
  traditions, responsibility, equality/merit, majority rule, the employed
  majority → [[classical-liberalism]] (core), [[knowledge-problem]]
  (generalization), [[constructivist-rationalism]] and
  [[true-and-false-individualism]] (two-traditions restatement),
  [[cultural-evolution]] (1960 statement of undesigned institutions).
- **Part II (chs. 9–16)** — coercion, law vs. command, the full historical
  genealogy (isonomia → Rome → Coke → Locke → Whigs → American
  constitutionalism → Rechtsstaat), safeguards, admissible economic policy,
  the decline of the law (legal positivism, Weimar, Soviet theory, Laski
  circle) → [[rule-of-law]] (core), [[extended-order]] (Polanyi's polycentric
  order), [[several-property]] (property as anti-coercion device),
  [[interstate-federalism]] and [[madison-james]] (federalism as check).
- **Part III (chs. 17–24)** — the welfare state sector by sector: unions,
  social security, progressive taxation, money/inflation, housing,
  agriculture, education → [[welfare-state]] (core), [[beveridge-report-1942]]
  (Hayek's ch. 19 critique), [[road-to-serfdom-thesis]] (1960 refinements and
  self-revisions).
- **Postscript** — "Why I Am Not a Conservative": the
  liberal-conservative-socialist triangle, the "unrepentant Old Whig"
  self-identification → [[classical-liberalism]]; Hayek's readings of the
  American founders → [[madison-james]], [[jefferson-thomas]].
