# A History of Islamic Societies — Ira M. Lapidus (3rd edition, 2014)

Ira Lapidus's *A History of Islamic Societies* is a comprehensive global survey of the history of Muslim peoples and societies from the origins of Islam in 7th-century Arabia to the early 21st century. It examines the formation of Islamic civilization in the Middle East, its expansion and diversification across North Africa, Spain, the Ottoman, Safavid, and Mughal empires, Central and Southeast Asia, and sub-Saharan Africa, and the profound transformations under European imperialism and the rise of modern nation-states.

The work emphasizes the interplay of religion, politics, economy, and culture, highlighting how Islamic values shaped (and were shaped by) diverse societies. It is particularly valuable for its treatment of the social organization of Muslim communities (ʿulama, Sufi orders, Shiʿi groups), state-society relations, and the modern Islamic revival.

This source fills the remaining gap in the comprehensive narrative for post-medieval Islamic world (Gap 4), complementing the political/dynastic works already ingested (Imber, Finkel, etc.) and the intellectual history sources (Hodgson, Adamson, Hallaq, Schimmel, etc.).

## Key Thesis

Islamic societies are not monolithic but have evolved through continuous interaction between religious ideals (Quran, law, Sufism) and local social, economic, and political structures. The history shows patterns of state formation, communal organization, and cultural synthesis, with major turning points in the early caliphates, the medieval "Islamic society" consolidation, global expansion, and the modern era of European impact and revival. The umma concept and religious elites (ʿulama, Sufis) play central roles in identity and politics across time.

## Volume Synthesis Note

Lapidus provides the essential integrative narrative for Islamic societies across time and space. A core set of religious and cultural patterns — the umma as religio-political community transcending kinship, sharia as scholar-defined law independent of (but in tension with) the state, Sufi tariqas as social/organizational networks, and the recurring ambiguity between caliphal ideal (integrated authority) and sultanate reality (secularized military/administrative power drawing legitimacy from both Islamic and pre-Islamic patrimonial traditions) — proved remarkably durable. These templates, rooted in late antique Near Eastern/Mesopotamian lineage/religious/political differentiation, were Islamized, Arabized/Persianized in the early period, consolidated in post-Abbasid Middle Eastern society (iqtaʿ land grants, military slavery/ghulam-mamluk systems, madhhabs, khanaqas), and carried outward in global expansion via conquest, trade (Indian Ocean, trans-Saharan, Mediterranean, Silk Roads), and Sufi/ʿulama missionaries.

Key turning points (grounded across ranges):
- Pre-Islamic inheritance and Muhammad's umma formation (Range 1): Byzantine/Sasanian structures, tribal Arabian periphery, hijra + Constitution of Medina, Quran as scripture, Ridda and early conquests producing amsar garrisons with fiscal continuity (jizya/kharaj) and gradual conversion/Arabization.
- Early empires and civilizational crystallization (Range 1): Rashidun to Umayyad (Syrian monarchy, Marwanid centralization, Dome of the Rock) to Abbasid (Baghdad as metropolitan hub, universal Muslim recruitment, diwans/wazir, mihna resolved for ʿulama authority over caliph; two streams: court cosmopolitan vs. urban scripturalist/Sufi).
- Post-Abbasid consolidation (Range 2): Fragmentation into Buwayhid/Samanid/Ghaznavid/Saljuq/Ilkhanid/Timurid/Mamluk/Fatimid/Ayyubid states; iqtaʿ + ghulam slavery as military-admin solutions (contrasts with European feudalism); institutionalization of Sunni madhhabs, Sufi orders (silsila, khanaqa), Twelver/Ismaʿili Shiʿism; mass conversions 10th–13th c.; Persian cultural revival alongside Arabic; normative ethic in mirrors for princes + al-Ghazali synthesis (Ihyaʾ); popular Sufism (Ibn al-ʿArabi wahdat al-wujud, saint veneration).
- Global expansion (Ranges 2–3): Diffusion to Anatolia (Turkish ghazi + Sufi), India (Delhi sultanates to Mughals), SE Asia (via trade + local states), sub-Saharan Africa (trans-Saharan + Wangara/Dyula + jihads); North Africa/Spain (Idrisids to Almoravids/Almohads, Umayyad Cordoba, Reconquista/expulsions, convivencia); varieties of state-society-religion patterns.
- European ascendancy + colonialism (Range 3): Pluralistic European societies (bilateral families, corporate groups, secular realm) generated commercial/naval/technological power; Atlantic shift + Dutch/British/French empires dismembered Muslim powers (Ottoman decentralization + partition post-WWI via Sykes-Picot/Balfour; Mughal fragmentation; SE Asia/India/Africa annexations at Berlin 1884–85); reduced historic trade routes (Sahara, Indian Ocean).
- Modern transformations and revival (Ranges 3–6): 19th c. reformism (tajdid: Wahhabi, Fulani/Sokoto, Sanusiyya, Deoband, Padri, Muhammadiyya) + Islamic modernism (al-Afghani/Abduh rationality + activism + patriotism) followed by secular nationalism (Young Turks/Ataturk, Egyptian Wafd/Free Officers, Arabism, Indonesia pancasila); nation-state formation with Islam disestablished/co-opted or fused ethnically; 1970s+ revival (liberal ijtihad, neo-Sufi, Salafi/Wahhabi return to Quran/sunna, neofundamentalist Shariʿa demands) + transnational umma (Tabligh, internet, Islamic finance, hajj, migration); specific cases Iran 1979 (ʿulama-led Islamic Republic), Turkey AKP/Erdogan, Egypt MB/Morsi + army, Afghanistan Taliban/al-Qaeda, SE Asia (NU/Muhammadiyya vs Laskar Jihad), Africa (Boko Haram + northern Shariʿa), Europe (headscarf/identity struggles in France/Germany post-guest-worker + 9/11 backlash). Two contradictory trends: global integration favoring universalistic Islam vs. national consolidation favoring parochial/local forms.

Lapidus bridges early (strong via Hodgson/primaries) and post-medieval (previously thinner social synthesis) by emphasizing institutions/communities over pure dynastic narrative. Complements Imber/Finkel/Kinross/Mikhail/Rogan (political) and Hodgson/Adamson/Hallaq/Schimmel (intellectual) with socio-economic/institutional depth. Fills Critical Gap 4; backbone for transregional processes and modern revival pages. Historiographically balanced survey drawing on secondary + primary; collection bias toward Eurasian/Mediterranean Islamic core noted where coverage thins (sub-Saharan, SE Asia pre-modern details rely on synthesis). Cross-volume tensions (e.g., with nationalist or purely Eurocentric accounts of decline/revival) flagged via source attributions.
