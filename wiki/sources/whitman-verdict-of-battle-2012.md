# Whitman, *The Verdict of Battle* (2012)

James Q. Whitman (Yale Law School), *The Verdict of Battle: The Law of Victory and
the Making of Modern War* (Harvard University Press, 2012), 334 pp.

**Thesis.** The classic pitched battle — single-day, spatially bounded, mutually
accepted — functioned for centuries as a *legal proceeding*: a trial by collective
combat under a "tacit contract of chance" whose verdict settled international
disputes. The governing law was not the modern [[just-war|jus ad bellum]] / jus in
bello pair but a **[[jus-victoriae|jus victoriae]]** ("law of victory") answering two
technical questions: *how do we know who won?* and *what do you win by winning?* The
eighteenth century — the era of monarchical property-war between dynasts like
[[frederick-the-great|Frederick the Great]] and [[maria-theresa|Maria Theresa]] —
was this system's golden age, because wars were fought for limited, divisible,
proprietary stakes ([[proprietorial-warfare]]). The system collapsed in the 1860s–70s
([[death-of-pitched-battle]]): at [[american-civil-war-1861-1865|Gettysburg]] and
[[battle-of-sedan-1870|Sedan]] apparently decisive verdicts were no longer accepted
by the losing side, and wars could be ended only by devastation. Whitman blames the
shift from monarchical property-stakes to popular-sovereignty stakes and the revival
of just-war moralism: wars over *causes* rather than *claims* cannot be settled by a
day's wager. Against the standard triumphal history of humanitarian law, he reads
the modern law of war as the tragic loss of a workable containment mechanism.

## Volume Synthesis Note

Whitman's book joins the wiki's warfare-theory cluster as the *legal* leg of a
triangle whose other legs are operational (Epstein's
[[emergence-of-modern-war]], the Clausewitz corpus) and cultural (Bell, the
chivalry literature). Its central payload for the wiki is the pair
[[jus-victoriae]] / [[verdict-of-battle]] and the process page
[[death-of-pitched-battle]], which give the 18th-century pages a connective
framework the collection previously lacked between the medieval
[[proprietorial-warfare]] material (John France) and the ACW/Franco-Prussian
modern-war material. Tensions flagged rather than adjudicated: against Bell's
First Total War dating (Revolutionary era vs. 1860s); against the Hehl/NCMH
sanctification reading of [[just-war]] (property-law vs. crusade strands);
against technological explanations of the ACW's character already present in
the Hagerman-based pages. Its scope limits (continental land war only; no
naval, colonial, or non-Western material) are noted on the concept pages.
