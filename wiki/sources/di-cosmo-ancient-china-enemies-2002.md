# Ancient China and Its Enemies (Di Cosmo, 2002)

Cambridge University Press, 2002. 400 pp.

## Argument

Di Cosmo challenges the Sinocentric view of China's northern frontier as a permanent civilizational divide between a superior Chinese culture and undifferentiated "barbarian" nomads. He argues instead that: (1) pastoral nomadism emerged gradually in the Northern Zone through a complex Bronze/Iron Age archaeological process intertwined with Chinese material culture; (2) Chinese states' attitudes toward northern peoples were primarily driven by political and military strategy, not cultural superiority; (3) the "long walls" (Great Wall precursors) were instruments of **military penetration and occupation**, not passive defense; (4) the Xiongnu empire formed in direct response to Qin/Han pressure on the steppe; and (5) Ssu-ma Ch'ien's *Shih chi* created the master narrative that trapped later scholarship in a binary framework.

## Key Claims (filled by subagents)

- [[modu-chanyu]] forged the Xiongnu empire c. 209 BCE partly in reaction to Qin pressure on the Ordos
- The "Great Wall" precursors were offensive occupation infrastructure, not purely defensive barriers
- The *heqin* (ho-ch'in) policy failed because of structural incompatibility between Xiongnu and Han understandings of international obligation
- Chinese ethnic labels (Yi, Di, Rong, Ti) reflected political and military contexts, not fixed cultural categories
- [[sima-qian]] invented the binary master narrative of China vs. nomads that dominated subsequent historiography
