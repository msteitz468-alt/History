# Balkan Prehistory: Exclusion, Incorporation and Identity

**Douglass W. Bailey**, *Balkan Prehistory: Exclusion, Incorporation and
Identity* (London: Routledge, 2000). Bailey, then Lecturer in European
Prehistory at Cardiff University and a fieldworker in Bulgaria and Romania,
wrote the first accessible English-language synthesis of the south-east
European Neolithic and Copper Age — a region he notes had "previously been
inaccessible to western scholars" because of language barriers and the
severing of the archaeological landscape along modern national borders.

## Scope and method

The book covers **6500–2500 BC** (with framing chapters reaching back to the
local Upper Palaeolithic from c. 50,000 BP and forward into the Early Bronze
Age). "The Balkans" for Bailey means **northern Greece, Bulgaria, southern
Romania, Serbia, the eastern Hungarian Plain, and north-west Anatolia** — a
unit defined by shared archaeological developments rather than by geography or
nationhood.

Three methodological commitments shape the book:

1. **Original sources over culture-history.** Bailey works from primary site
   reports and deliberately minimises the named "culture" complexes
   (Krivodol–Sălcuța–Bubanj, Boian, Körös, etc.), arguing many "have little
   interpretive value beyond bare description." He writes in absolute
   chronology and modern geography instead.
2. **Economy demoted.** Against Gordon Childe's economy-as-engine
   ([[neolithic-revolution|Neolithic Revolution]]), Bailey treats subsistence
   change not as a prime mover but as one strand within a broader
   reorganisation of how people arranged themselves, their places, and their
   things. The significance of sheep, wheat, copper, or gold lies less in
   nutrition or function than in **consumption, display, and identity**.
3. **Three levels of explanation, deliberately juxtaposed.** Bailey opens and
   closes chapters with **fictional vignettes** of named individuals (Bogdan,
   Lilka) set against the dry regional data and the high-level interpretive
   synthesis — an explicit experiment in writing prehistoric lives at the
   scale of the individual.

## Central argument

Bailey's thesis is that the post-6500 BC transformation of the Balkans was
**not** primarily an economic revolution (Childe) or a population replacement
(Gimbutas), but a revolution in **how people arranged themselves, their
places, and their things** — a shift from the mobile, flexible, verbally
negotiated existence of foragers to the **anchored, durable, inflexible** world
of houses, villages, tells, and cemeteries.

The book's most distinctive contribution is the interpretive triad of
**[[exclusion-incorporation-projection|exclusion, incorporation and
projection]]** — three spatial-temporal processes through which built
environments and material culture arranged people and things. Two further
claims follow:

- **The house, household, and village were the era's key social
  institutions.** The shift from oval pit-huts (loose, unbounded camps where
  activity spilled outward) to rectilinear surface structures (bounded,
  subdivided houses packed into ordered villages, rebuilt over generations
  into **tells**) created tangible, durable boundaries that locked groups into
  fixed places. Bailey reads tells, the burning of houses, the burial of
  infants under house floors, and the proliferation of figurines as
  components of an emergent **ideology of the household**.
- **Fifth-millennium society was riven with tension, and its grandest displays
  were "illusion."** Against the prevailing image of Neolithic communalism and
  sharing, Bailey argues that exclusion and hoarding were as frequent as
  sharing, and that the brilliant mortuary displays at [[varna|Varna]], the
  ordered villages, and the anthropomorphic figurines projected a **preferred,
  fictional reality** — an imposed, inflexible order papered over the mobile,
  fissioning, conflict-prone actuality of daily life.

On the **post-4000 BC transformation** Bailey sides with Alasdair Whittle
against Marija Gimbutas: the disappearance of tells and the rise of mound
cemeteries reflect gradual, local, indigenous shifts in "what was appropriate
to do, and where and when" — not an invasion of Kurgan horse-warriors. See
[[indo-european-origins]].

## Reliability and use in this wiki

The site-level and chronological content is conventional and dependable. The
interpretive framework is Bailey's own and is filed here as an attributed
position (see [[exclusion-incorporation-projection]]), not as established fact.
Where Bailey contradicts the steppe-migration reading of the late-fifth/
fourth-millennium change, both positions are preserved on
[[indo-european-origins]]. The book complements [[boardman-cah-v3p1-1982|CAH
Vol. III.1]] (Garašanin's culture-history backbone): Bailey supplies the
social-theoretical and household-scale interpretation that the older CAH survey
lacks.
