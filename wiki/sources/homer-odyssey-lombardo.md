# Homer, *The Odyssey* (Stanley Lombardo translation)

The second of the two great epics attributed to **[[homer|Homer]]**, the *Odyssey* is the foundational Western story of **homecoming (nostos)**, cunning intelligence (**mêtis**), identity, and the recovery of household and kingship after the Trojan War. It is the sequel and complement to the *Iliad*: while the *Iliad* explores wrath, glory, and mortality in war, the *Odyssey* explores survival, return, recognition, and the reestablishment of order in the domestic and political spheres. Together they form the core of the Greek — and Western — literary canon.

## Subject and Structure

The poem narrates the return of **[[odysseus|Odysseus]]** from the Greek victory at Troy to his rocky island kingdom of Ithaca. It is not a simple travelogue: the first four books (the "Telemachy") focus on the crisis in Ithaca during his absence — the suitors devouring his estate, his son **Telemachus** coming of age, and his wife **Penelope**'s stratagems. Only in Book 5 do we meet Odysseus himself, trapped on the island of the nymph Calypso. The central books (5–12) recount his escape, arrival among the Phaeacians, and the long flashback of his wanderings (Cyclops, Circe, the Underworld, Sirens, Scylla and Charybdis, the Cattle of the Sun). Books 13–24 depict his disguised return to Ithaca, his testing of loyal and disloyal servants and family, the bow contest, the slaughter of the suitors, the reunion with Penelope, and the final pacification of the island.

Twenty-four books. The structure artfully interweaves multiple storylines and uses embedded tales (the "apologoi" in Books 9–12) to delay and enrich the central nostos.

## Themes

- **Mêtis (cunning intelligence) vs. force (biê)**. Odysseus survives by wit, disguise, lies, and patience rather than Achilles-style heroism.
- **Homecoming, identity, and recognition**. The poem is obsessed with tokens, tests, and the slow recovery of name, status, and relationships after twenty years.
- **Xenia (guest-host relations)**. Proper and improper hospitality structures almost every episode.
- **Family, gender, and household**. Penelope's fidelity and intelligence; the education of Telemachus; the faithful slave Eumaeus; the dangers of absent kingship.
- **The gods**. Athena's patronage, Poseidon's wrath, the divine council that sets the plot in motion. The gods are partisan but ultimately support order and justice.
- **Mortality, memory, and song**. Like the *Iliad*, the *Odyssey* meditates on what endures: kleos through song, the household as the site of meaning.

## The Oral Tradition and the Homeric Question

As with the *Iliad*, the *Odyssey* is oral-derived poetry. It employs the same formulaic system. The two poems were likely composed by the same monumental poet (or closely related singers) in the late eighth century BCE in Ionia. The "Homeric Question" — single author or many, historicity, relative date of the two epics — is treated on the dedicated controversy page and in the **[[homer|Homer]]** actor page. The *Odyssey* is generally considered slightly later than the *Iliad* and shows awareness of it.

## Significance

The *Odyssey* gave later Greeks (and the West) their archetype of the clever survivor, the faithful wife, the maturing son, and the ideal of home as the supreme value after war and wandering. It shaped Greek ideas of hospitality, storytelling, and the tension between civilization and the wild. It is the fountainhead of the novel, travel literature, and countless retellings (Virgil's *Aeneid* reworks it, Joyce's *Ulysses* modernizes it). With the *Iliad* it is the model and measure for all later epic.

## Volume Synthesis Note

The *Odyssey* (Stanley Lombardo translation) is the foundational Western epic of homecoming (*nostos*), cunning intelligence (*mêtis*), and the restoration of household and identity after war and wandering. As the sequel to the *Iliad*, it shifts focus from the wrath and mortality of battle to survival, endurance, recognition, and the reestablishment of order in the domestic and political realms. The poem opens not with Troy's fall but with the crisis on Ithaca (suitors devouring the estate, Penelope's stratagems, Telemachus coming of age) and only gradually reveals Odysseus himself — first trapped with Calypso, then among the Phaeacians telling his own tales of the Cyclops, Circe, the Underworld, Sirens, and the Cattle of the Sun, before the disguised return, testing of loyalty, bow contest, slaughter of the suitors, and emotional reunion with Penelope (via the immovable olive-tree bed) and Laertes.

The hero succeeds not primarily by force but by wit, disguise, patience, and alliance with Athena, embodying the tension between heroic glory abroad and the value of home (*oikos*). Key themes include *xenia* (proper and violated hospitality as structuring principle), social order (loyal servants vs. suitors), prophecy and omens, the power of song and memory (Demodocus, the Sirens, embedded tales), and divine justice tempered by human agency. The poem ends not with total vengeance but with Athena and Zeus imposing oaths of peace and prosperity, affirming kingship and friendship "as before."

As oral-derived poetry (formulaic, traditional, monumental composition in the late 8th century BCE in Ionia), the *Odyssey* fuses Late Bronze Age memories, Dark Age realities, and the poet's contemporary world. It cannot be read as literal history (see [[homeric-poems-as-history]] and the [[homer|Homer]] actor page) but is primary evidence for Early Iron Age/Archaic Greek values: hospitality norms, gender roles, household as center of meaning, and the ideal of the clever survivor who chooses mortality and family over immortality.

For the wiki this is the essential primary complement to the already-ingested *Iliad* (homer-iliad source). It directly populates and grounds the actor pages for Odysseus and Penelope, the place Ithaca, and enriches coverage of Homer, the Trojan War background, Early Iron Age Aegean society, and Mediterranean mythic geography. It fills the Odyssey gap in core ancient primaries (Gap 9) and strengthens the network around oral epic, *mêtis*, *xenia*, and nostos as processes.

Limitations: the translation is modern and performance-oriented (Lombardo); the text is one version of a fluid oral tradition; geography and "history" are mythic rather than documentary. It should be read alongside the Iliad source, the Homer actor page, the Homeric controversy, CAH Vol. II discussions of the heroic age, and later receptions.
