# The Cambridge Ancient History Vol. VII Part 2 — The Rise of Rome to 220 B.C.

**Editors**: F.W. Walbank, A.E. Astin; contributing editors M.W. Frederiksen, R.M. Ogilvie; assistant editor A. Drummond  
**Published**: Cambridge University Press, 1989 (2nd edition)  
**ISBN**: 0 521 23446 8  
**Scope**: Roman history from the earliest settlement traces through the end of the First Punic War and the provincial settlement (220 BCE), with a chapter on Republican religion and a full chronological appendix and consular fasti.

This volume is the companion to [[walbank-cah-v7-1-1984|CAH VII.1]] (The Hellenistic World, 1984) — together they constitute Volume VII of the 2nd edition. Where VII.1 closes Polybius' *symploke* at 217 BCE looking east, VII.2 supplies the western half of that same threshold, ending just before the Second Punic War.

## Volume Synthesis Note

CAH VII.2 is the foundational secondary reference for Rome from its first secure
historical traces (~509 BCE) through the provincial settlement after the First Punic
War (241–220 BCE). Its 36,000+ lines represent the most rigorous multi-author
treatment of this period currently in the collection.

**What the volume argues, cumulatively.** The editors' central thesis emerges through
accumulation rather than declaration: Rome's rise was neither inevitable nor
structurally predetermined, but was the product of specific institutional choices —
the flexible citizenship policy, the commonwealth framework of 338 BCE, the willingness
to absorb enemies as allies — combined with an unusual capacity to absorb catastrophe
(Caudine Forks, Trebia, Cannae in the following volume) and continue. The volume
deliberately assigns the most contested early chapters (1–3) to four scholars with
different approaches, refusing the false confidence of a single authoritative account.

**Methodological pluralism as a deliberate editorial choice.** Ogilvie/Drummond on the
sources and early archaeology; Momigliano on the alien wisdom tradition; Cornell on
the constitutional and military history of the Republic; Staveley on the constitution
at its mature phase; North on religion — these are substantially different methodological
registers. The editors do not harmonize them. The wiki has preserved these methodological
distinctions in individual pages rather than synthesizing them into a false coherence.

**The religion chapter as a reorientation.** North's Ch. 12 is the single most
revisionist contribution in the volume. Where Chs. 1–11 can be read as refinements
of the dominant Anglophone tradition, North explicitly dismantles the frameworks of
Warde Fowler, Latte, and Dumézil and replaces developmental narrative with structural
analysis. His argument that Roman religion had no autonomous existence — that it was
the medium of Roman public life rather than a layer added to it — has consequences for
every other page in this wiki that touches Republican political culture.

**Cross-volume tensions.** The most significant tension with material already ingested
is between **Cornell's account of fifth-century Rome** (Chs. 4–8: a heavily reconstructed
narrative from unreliable annalistic sources, presented with appropriate hedging) and
the more confident accounts in CAH IV and CAH VI (Asheri, Picard) of Carthage and
Syracuse in the same period. The First Punic War (Scullard, Ch. 11) is the best-covered
topic in the collection with the most cross-source verification: Polybius, Diodorus,
and Livy converge on the main events even when they disagree on naval numbers.

**Chronological note.** The Appendix (Drummond) makes clear that virtually all dates
before c. 300 BCE are reconstructed from a Varronian framework that was itself a
mid-first-century BCE creation, built from consular lists that ancient historians knew
were defective and that include the spurious "dictator-years." Events before 300 BCE
should be understood as approximately rather than exactly dated unless independent
archaeological or comparative chronological confirmation exists.

**What this volume adds to the wiki.** Before this ingest the wiki had minimal coverage
of Roman Republican constitutional and military history. The volume now provides:
- Full coverage of the Republican constitution from its founding through the mature
  third-century oligarchy (Staveley; Cornell Chs. 7–8)
- The fullest available account of the Romano-Punic treaty series
- Authoritative narrative of the Pyrrhic War, First Punic War, and provincial settlement
- The revisionist account of Republican religion (North)
- The Philinus treaty controversy as an open interpretive dispute
- Detailed source pages for Carthage, the Roman Republic, Pyrrhus of Epirus, Hiero II

**What remains thin after this ingest.** The volume's coverage of **Magna Graecia** in
the third century is derivative; the coverage of **North Africa** outside Carthage and
Sicily is absent. The [[mercenary-war-241-237bce]] page is complete but the subsequent
Barcid Spanish strategy and its consequences lead directly into a coverage gap (no
post-241 BCE secondary sources yet ingested). The [[outstanding-sources]] register
should flag Polybius (Walbank's commentary) and Lazenby's *The First Punic War* as
the highest-priority next acquisitions for this period.
