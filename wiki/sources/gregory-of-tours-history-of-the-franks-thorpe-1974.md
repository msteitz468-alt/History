# Gregory of Tours, *The History of the Franks* (*Decem Libri Historiarum*)

The ***Ten Books of Histories*** (*Decem Libri Historiarum*), conventionally titled
*The History of the Franks* (*Historia/Historiae Francorum*), is the foundational
narrative source for Merovingian Gaul and the chief reason the sixth century in the
post-Roman West is visible at all. Its author, **[[gregory-of-tours|Gregory]]**, was
Bishop of [[tours|Tours]] from 573 until his death in 594; he wrote the *Histories*
across his entire episcopate, the last events treated in detail falling in 591. This
ingest is of the **Lewis Thorpe translation** (Penguin Classics, 1974), whose long
introduction supplies the apparatus (manuscript tradition, dating, credibility,
genealogies) summarised below.

The work is the prime witness for: the conversion of **[[clovis]]** and the orthodox-
Catholic self-definition of the Franks against the Arian Goths and Burgundians; the
partition of the realm among Clovis's sons; the fratricidal civil wars of the later
sixth century driven by the rivalry of Queens [[brunhild|Brunhild]] and
[[fredegund|Fredegund]]; the [[gundovald-affair-585|Gundovald pretender affair]]; and
the texture of episcopal, monastic, and ordinary life in Gaul. See the period pages
[[late-antiquity]] and [[early-middle-ages]] and the process page
[[frankish-ethnogenesis]].

## Author, Date, and Composition

Gregory (538–594) was a Gallo-Roman of senatorial rank from Clermont-Ferrand, kin to a
dense network of bishops — he boasted that of the eighteen previous bishops of Tours all
but five had been his relatives (see [[merovingian-episcopate]]). His education was
ecclesiastical, not classical: he read the Psalms, Gospels, Acts and Epistles, and only
parts of the *Aeneid*; his Latin is the everyday speech of sixth-century Gaul. Besides
the *Histories* he wrote a large hagiographic corpus — the *Glory of the Martyrs*, the
books on St Julian and on the *Miracles of St Martin*, the *Life of the Fathers*, the
*Glory of the Confessors* (together his "seven books of Miracles"), plus minor works
(see [[merovingian-hagiography]]).

He worked as a contemporary chronicler rather than a reflective philosophical historian:
recording events in quasi-diary order as he heard of them, then going back to interpolate
some sixty-eight largely ecclesiastical chapters into the earlier books. The probable
sequence: Books **V–X** are the contemporary history proper (begun c. 575 with the murder
of [[sigibert-i|Sigibert]]); Book **I** prefixes a universal history from Creation to the
death of St Martin (397), covering, by Gregory's reckoning, 5,596 years in 35 chapters;
Books **II–IV** then close the gap from 397 to 575.

| Book | Coverage | Span | Character |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | Creation → death of St Martin of Tours | to 397 CE | universal/biblical, from Eusebius–Jerome–Orosius |
| II | 5th c. Gaul → death of [[clovis]] | 397–511 | barbarian invasions, Clovis's conquests & conversion |
| III | Clovis's sons; Burgundy, Thuringia, Auvergne | 511–c. 548 | partition and fraternal wars |
| IV | to the murder of [[sigibert-i|Sigibert I]] | c. 548–575 | [[chlothar-i|Chlothar I]]'s reunification & re-partition |
| V | civil wars open; Chilperic ascendant | 575–580 | passionate prologue lamenting Frankish fratricide |
| VI | to the assassination of [[chilperic-i|Chilperic I]] | 580–584 | persecution, taxation, Chilperic as "Nero and Herod" |
| VII | the [[gundovald-affair-585|Gundovald]] pretender | 584–585 | the siege of Comminges |
| VIII | [[guntram|Guntram]] and Childebert II | 585–586 | the Treaty of Andelot prelude, plague |
| IX | Treaty of Andelot; embassies | 587–589 | diplomacy, convent revolt at Poitiers |
| X | to 591; review of the bishops of Tours | 590–591 | epilogue, Gregory's own list of works |

Gregory closed Book X with a plea to future copyists never to abridge the *Histories* —
which they promptly ignored, producing a **six-book version** within a generation (later
extended by the continuator [[fredegar|Fredegar]]). See [[merovingian-historiography]].

## Method, Bias, and Reliability

Thorpe's verdict: "within the limits set by his circumstances Gregory of Tours was a
painstaking and accurate recorder of events" — J. J. Ampère called him "the Herodotus of
the barbarian age." On the credit side: much of Books V–X he saw himself (he appears in
67 of the last six books' 265 chapters); he transcribed documents in full; he marked
hearsay with *fertur/ferunt*; he admitted uncertainty; his investigation of when a
Frankish leader was first titled *king* is genuine source-critical research quoting lost
fourth-century authorities. On the debit side, four biases must be discounted:

1. **Providential/moral schema.** History is divine judgement. Kings prosper or fall by
   their piety, their defence of the Church, and their respect for the relics and
   sanctuary of **St Martin** — not by strategy or economics.
2. **Episcopal/senatorial viewpoint.** Gregory writes for the Gallo-Roman episcopate as
   the true guardians of the cities and moral tutors of the kings; church-friendly rulers
   have their vices condoned.
3. **Partisanship among the kings.** He idealises the pious **[[guntram|Guntram]]** of
   Burgundy and demonises **[[chilperic-i|Chilperic I]]** and Queen
   **[[fredegund|Fredegund]]**, to whom he attributes a long train of assassinations.
4. **Invented speeches.** Like classical historians he composes dramatic conversations
   to fit the moment; these convey his judgement, not verbatim record.

For the pre-540 material (most of Books I–IV) he had only books and hearsay; reliability
rises sharply from Book III.32 onward and is at its highest in Books V–X.

## Claim List

*(Synthesised from per-book extraction. Each claim is grounded in a verbatim quotation from
the book indicated.)*

**Books I–II (Creation → death of Clovis, 511).** Book I is biblical/universal chronology
(from Eusebius, Jerome, Orosius, Victorius) closing with the death of St Martin (397);
Gregory opens with a creedal profession ("so that whoever reads me may not doubt that I am
a Catholic") and apologises for his grammar. Book II carries the Frankish material: the
search for the first Frankish king ("Many people do not even know the name of the first
King of the Franks"); the long-haired kings from Pannonia via Thuringia; Childeric's exile
and the broken-token restoration; and the [[clovis|Clovis]] cycle — Soissons/Syagrius,
the ewer, the Alemannic vow, baptism by Remigius ("Bow your head… Sicamber"),
[[battle-of-vouille-507|Vouillé]], the consular honours at Tours, Paris as capital, and
the liquidation of rival kinglets. [[late-antiquity|Late-antique Gaul]] is sketched via
Aetius, Attila at Orléans, and the Arian Burgundian and Visigothic settlements.

**Books III–IV (the sons of Clovis → murder of Sigibert I, 575).** The four-way partition
"along *civitas* boundaries"; [[theuderic-i|Theuderic I]]'s Thuringian and Auvergne wars;
[[chlodomer|Chlodomer]]'s Burgundian war, death at Vézeronce, and the murder of his
children (the scissors-and-sword scene); [[childebert-i|Childebert I]]'s Spanish campaign;
[[chlothar-i|Chlothar I]]'s reunification (558), the Chramn revolt, and re-partition among
four sons; [[sigibert-i|Sigibert I]]'s Avar wars and marriage to [[brunhild|Brunhild]];
[[chilperic-i|Chilperic I]]'s garrotting of Galswintha (origin of the
[[fredegund|Fredegund]]–Brunhild feud); Sigibert's assassination at Vitry by Fredegund's
agents.

**Book V (575–580).** The passionate **prologue** against fratricide ("brother shall rise
up against brother"; "Concord… Discord"); Chilperic's seizure of Brunhild and the Merovech
affair; the Praetextatus trial; Chilperic's tax drive, amphitheatres, alphabet, and
Trinitarian decree (debated by Gregory to his face); Roccolen's threat to burn
[[tours|Tours]]; [[guntram|Guntram]]'s adoption of [[childebert-ii|Childebert II]].

**Book VI (580–584).** Chilperic's heavy taxation and new counts, forced baptism of Jews,
feeble verse, mutilation as punishment; the assassination at Chelles and Gregory's
obituary verdict — "the **Nero and Herod** of our time"; "he died deserted by all."
Brunhild's intervention "with a vigour which would have become a man."

**Books VII–VIII (584–586): the [[gundovald-affair-585|Gundovald affair]].** The Byzantine-
backed pretender raised on a shield at Brives; dukes Mummolus and Desiderius and Bishop
Sagittarius; the siege and sack of Comminges; Gundovald's betrayal and death; the recognition
of the infant [[chlothar-ii|Chlothar II]] (Guntram's paternity doubts settled by oath of
"three bishops and three hundred leaders"); Fredegund's sanctuary at Paris and the altar-
murder of Praetextatus; the Council of Mâcon.

**Books IX–X (587–591).** The **Treaty of Andelot (587)** — Guntram adopts Childebert II as
heir, mutual-inheritance clause, Brunhild's dower-cities restored; the revolt of the nuns at
[[radegund-of-poitiers|Radegund's]] convent of the Holy Cross led by the royal nun **Clotild**
(daughter of Charibert) against Abbess Leubovera, with Gregory a judge (see
[[merovingian-monasticism]]); the treason trial of Bishop Egidius of Reims; and the
**epilogue** — Gregory's list of his own works, his review of the eighteen previous bishops
of Tours, his final dating "in the twenty-first year after my consecration," and the famous
plea to copyists: "I conjure you all… that you never permit these books to be destroyed, or
to be rewritten, or to be reproduced in part only… Keep them in your possession, intact."

## Volume Synthesis Note

Gregory's *Histories* is the spine to which the wiki's Merovingian secondary literature is
the commentary. Where Geary explains the *structures* — ethnogenesis, the senatorial
episcopate, the seventh-century Pippinid capture of the Church — and the Oxford Handbook
supplies the archaeology that corrects the texts, Gregory supplies the **events and the
voice**: the only contemporary who watched the civil wars of the 570s–580s from inside a
cathedral city and named the actors. Read critically — discounting the providential frame,
the pro-Guntram/anti-Chilperic partisanship, and the invented speeches — it remains the
indispensable witness to how a Gallo-Roman bishop understood the violent making of Francia.

Its central historical claim, which later scholarship has both used and resisted, is that
the Franks' success was a function of **orthodox Catholic kingship**: Clovis's conversion
made the Franks the legitimate Catholic power against the Arian Visigoths and Burgundians,
and every later disaster is divine punishment for fratricide and impiety. This is precisely
the ideological construction Geary and the Handbook historicise rather than accept. The
tension between Gregory's moral narrative and the modern structural account is itself a
running feature of [[merovingian-historiography]].
