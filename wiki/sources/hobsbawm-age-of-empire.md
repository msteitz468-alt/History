# The Age of Empire: 1875–1914 (Eric Hobsbawm)

The third volume of **Hobsbawm's** modern history: the **belle époque** of bourgeois civilization that
was also the age of the **New Imperialism** and the road to catastrophe in 1914. Fifth source of **Gap
1**; completes the Long-19th-Century arc.

## The Argument

The period 1875–1914 is, for Hobsbawm, the paradoxical climax of the bourgeois nineteenth century — its
greatest confidence and its self-destruction in embryo:

- **The New Imperialism.** The "**[[imperialism|scramble for Africa]]**" and the partition of much of
  Asia and the Pacific — driven, in Hobsbawm's reading, by the economics of a more competitive,
  monopolizing **capitalism** (after the Long Depression of 1873–96) as much as by strategy and
  nationalism — divided most of the globe among a handful of powers.
- **The transformation of capitalism** — the **second industrial revolution** (steel, chemicals,
  electricity, the combustion engine), the rise of big corporations and finance, and the integration of
  a genuinely global economy.
- **Mass politics and its tensions** — the extension of the franchise, mass political parties,
  **socialist and labour movements**, the new nationalism, women's emancipation, and the cultural ferment
  of the *fin de siècle* and early modernism.
- **The slide to war.** The great-power rivalries, arms races and alliance systems that the imperial,
  capitalist and national dynamics generated, ending the "long peace" of 1815–1914 in the **First World
  War**.

## Significance

*The Age of Empire* is the standard interpretive account of the **pre-1914 world** — the link between
late-nineteenth-century capitalism, imperial expansion, mass politics and the coming of the World Wars.
It completes the wiki's Long-19th-Century narrative and hands forward to the [[world-wars-era|World Wars
Era]] (taken up in *[[hobsbawm-age-of-extremes|The Age of Extremes]]*).
