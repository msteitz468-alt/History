# Alan Turing: The Enigma — Andrew Hodges (2014)

**Full citation**: Hodges, Andrew. *Alan Turing: The Enigma*. Princeton: Princeton University Press, 2014 (updated edition of the 1983 original; the edition tied to the film *The Imitation Game*). The standard scholarly biography.

This is the foundational modern biography of Alan Mathison Turing (1912–1954), the mathematician whose 1936 paper “On Computable Numbers” defined the universal machine (the theoretical basis of the modern computer), who was the chief scientific figure in the British wartime cryptanalytic effort at Bletchley Park that broke the German Enigma (and other) ciphers, and who after the war pursued both theoretical computer design (ACE) and practical work on the Manchester Mark I while suffering the consequences of a 1952 conviction for gross indecency that led to chemical castration and his death by cyanide in 1954.

## Overview and Thesis

Hodges presents Turing’s life as the intersection of pure mathematics, the existential emergency of 1939–45, the birth of the digital age, and the mid-century British state’s treatment of a homosexual scientific intellectual. The book argues that Turing was not merely a “codebreaker” or “father of the computer” in the popular sense, but the person who gave a precise logical formulation of “mechanical process” that made general-purpose computation conceivable, and who then applied that insight under conditions of total war. 

The personal and the universal are inseparable: Turing’s 1936 abstraction of the “universal machine,” his physical machines at Bletchley and Manchester, his interest in morphogenesis and pattern, and his own “difference” as a gay man in 1950s Britain are all expressions of the same drive to understand what can be made mechanical or formal and what cannot. The tragedy is not only personal (suicide at 41 after state-mandated “treatment”) but historical: a mind that helped win the war and sketched the future was destroyed by the very state it served when that mind’s private life became visible.

The 2014 preface and post-film context note the rise in public recognition (Obama’s 2011 mention of Turing alongside Newton and Darwin) while underscoring that in Turing’s lifetime the Bletchley work remained secret and his personal life was criminalized.

## Methodological Notes

- Hodges is himself a mathematician (Tutor in Mathematics at Wadham College, Oxford; collaborator with Roger Penrose on fundamental physics). Technical explanations of the computability paper, Enigma rotor logic, Colossus, and early computer architecture are unusually reliable for a biography.
- Primary sources: the Turing family archive (letters, school reports, photographs); papers held by Robin Gandy and other colleagues; Bletchley Park veterans’ accounts (pre- and post-official history releases); the then-available government records; Turing’s own published and unpublished writings; contemporary scientific correspondence.
- Strong integration of the abstract (the 1936 paper) with the concrete (actual bombe design, “crib” methods, policy impact on Overlord).
- Balanced on character: Turing’s social gaucheness, intensity, loyalty to friends, and occasional cruelty or obliviousness are documented alongside genius.
- The personal story (Christopher Morcom, 1952 arrest and trial, relationship with Arnold Murray, the “apple” and cyanide) is treated with compassion but without sensationalism.
- Limitations (noted by Hodges): full Bletchley documentary release came after the first edition; some personal details rely on memory decades later; the exact mechanics of the final days remain partly inferential.

## Key Contributions to the Wiki

- The single richest grounded narrative of Turing’s life from childhood through the 1936 paper, Bletchley Park (the “chief backroom boy”), post-war computing projects (ACE, Manchester), the 1952 prosecution, “organo-therapy,” and death.
- Precise treatment of the logical content of “On Computable Numbers” and why it mattered for the possibility of the stored-program computer.
- Detailed picture of the organizational and technical reality of Enigma breaking (not just the myth) and Turing’s specific contributions.
- Historiographic framing of the relationship between pure science, total war, secrecy, and personal freedom in the mid-20th century.
- Primary-source texture for concepts (Turing machine, computability, the imitation game/Turing test), places (Bletchley Park, King’s College Cambridge, Manchester), and the collision between individual and state in Cold War Britain.
