# Atlantic History: A Critical Appraisal — Jack P. Greene and Philip D. Morgan (eds., 2009)

A multi-author critical appraisal of Atlantic history as an analytic construct and field of study. Developed from a 2005 American Historical Association session organized under the National History Center. The editors (Greene and Morgan) frame the volume as an assessment of the promises, achievements, and limitations of "Atlantic history" after it had become institutionalized in the 1990s–2000s.

The volume is structured to mirror the historical Atlantic world itself: national/imperial spheres of activity, impacts back on the Old Worlds (Europe, Africa, indigenous Americas), and competing or complementary spatial frameworks (continental, hemispheric, global).

## Overview and Thesis

Atlantic history is defined as the study of "the creation, destruction, and re-creation of communities as a result of the movement, across and around the Atlantic basin, of people, commodities, cultural practices, and values" (citing John Elliott). It is presented not as a rigid new field that must subsume all others, but as a useful **framework, angle of vision, or arena of analysis** for the early modern era (roughly 1450/1492 to ~1825).

The editors offer "guarded enthusiasm": the approach has generated valuable transnational, comparative, and connective work, but faces legitimate criticisms and should not be applied indiscriminately. They provide six propositions for productive Atlantic history.

## Key Claims and Historiography

The volume is itself a work of historiography. It documents the shift from Atlantic history as a "perspective" to (for some) a "field," catalogs achievements in breaking national frames, commodity studies, diasporas, and entangled histories, while honestly airing criticisms.

Major debates foregrounded:
- Coherence vs. multiplicity of "Atlantics" (North/South; national; color-coded diasporas).
- Boundedness: always connected to Indian Ocean, Pacific, Asia (silver, textiles, cowries, tea).
- Relation to imperial history: permeable boundaries, but not merely rebranded empire.
- Continental vs. Atlantic (esp. for indigenous interiors and later U.S. history).
- Global vs. Atlantic (Coclanis emphasizes rice, silver, conjunctures beyond the basin).
- Emphasis on connections vs. internal developments and comparison.

The editors' propositions (paraphrased/synthesized):
1. Balance connections and contrasts (Elliott's Spanish-British comparison as model).
2. Pursue interactive, border-crossing studies (commodities, ideas, people; Catholic Atlantic; scientific knowledge).
3. Focus on leading-edge sectors (maritime, islands as hubs/stepping-stones, borderlands/middle grounds).
4. Give greater weight to exchange of values, ideas, and religious/scientific networks.
5. Re-examine traditional imperial topics (ideology, law, governance) from broader perspectives (legal pluralism, negotiated authority, Mancke, Benton, Pagden).
6. Attend closely to chronology and phases (Bailyn/Elliott schemas of contested marchlands → integration/consolidation → creole triumphalism/emancipation; caveats about uniformity).

## Part II: Old Worlds and the Atlantic — Detailed Synthesis (Range 2)

This section supplies the core material for understanding reciprocal impacts. Chapters examine how Atlantic integration affected (and was shaped by) the societies of the Americas, Africa, and Europe. Key theme: impressive but limited and uneven integration; agency on all sides; old-world structures often resilient or reoriented rather than supplanted.

**Indigenous America (Amy Turner Bushnell and related synthesis)**: Atlantic contact produced "crises and transformations of invaded societies." Coastal Brazil, Caribbean, Florida missions, New Mexico, California, and trans-Appalachian frontiers saw mission systems, environmental shifts (livestock "plague of sheep"), resistance (Chacato Revolt, Pueblo), and new transfrontier economies (cattle hunters, raiding). Indigenous groups retained substantial agency through adaptation, flight, and selective engagement. Vast interiors (Amazon margins, Great Plains, Northwest) experienced indirect effects (horses, disease) but remained largely autonomous. Atlantic framework integrates these with European/African flows but reveals coastal bias; interiors require complementary continental lenses.

**Africa and the Atlantic, c. 1450 to c. 1820 (Philip D. Morgan)**:
- Baseline: Atlantic "played little role in most Africans’ lives." No indigenous seafaring tradition of note; dugouts limited; "Africans used waterways, but generally not the sea." 
- Scale by 1820: ~10 million transported; "about four Africans arrived in the Americas for every European." Trade value sixfold rise; slaves >90% of exports by 1780s. Ports and African maritime specialists (Kru, canoemen) emerged.
- Agency: "voluntary partnership"; "Africans called the tune"; Europeans "perched precariously"; "subordinate symbiosis." Africans "eager, discriminating consumers" (textiles dominant). Terms of trade shifted "inexorably in their favor" (3–4× price rise by 1800); sellers' market pre-abolition.
- Regional variation: Seven zones. Upper Guinea least integrated (resistance, fragmentation, internal demand); West-Central Africa dominant (~half of all slaves), shorter Middle Passage, "single overarching culture."
- Impact: Economic "modest" ("ratio of overseas trade to domestic economic activity was far lower"); local production resilient; guns "secondary role"; "already underway" military changes. New polities (Asante, Dahomey) but fragmentation persisted. Demography: Atlantic Africa population "fairly stable" (~20–23M); New World crops offset losses; retained more slaves internally. Social: internal slavery enlarged/depersonalized; "patriarchy was enhanced"; gerontocracy; stratification intensified. "colossal human tragedy" for the enslaved.
- Diaspora: Middle ground between continuity and creolization. Identities "flexible and inchoate" pre-embarkation; ethnic labels crude. Specific retentions (Kongo in Brazil/St. Augustine; Stono; Akwamu leadership in revolt) alongside broad shared principles and creolization. Two-way movement: returnees, Brazilian diaspora to Slave Coast (terms like "Nago" fed back).
- Verdict: "Africans were more important to the Atlantic world than the Atlantic world was to Africans." Atlantic one diaspora among several.

**Europe and the Atlantic (Carla Rahn Phillips)**:
- Politics: Rivalries from 15th c. (Portugal/Castile → Tordesillas 1494) drove exploration; English/French challenges (Drake, Canada, Caribbean) intertwined with European wars (Armada genesis in Atlantic raids; Thirty Years' War global; Seven Years' War decisive for Atlantic map; American Revolution entangled Bourbon finances and Florida/Louisiana).
- Economy: Spanish silver (Potosí peak late 1580s) and commodities (hides, sugar, cochineal) fueled 16th-c. expansion and Price Revolution; fleets and House of Trade centralized but porous. Portuguese Brazil: sugar then gold (~14,600 kg/yr peak) linked Lisbon to global flows. 17th-c. downturn and 18th-c. boom tied directly to American production/slave demand. Profits fed European states, markets, and material culture (dyestuffs, sugar, tobacco); debates (Hobsbawm extractive vs. O’Brien complexity) unresolved but "Atlantic world was important for the political and economic development of Europe."
- Broader: Migration (hundreds of thousands registered); cultural feedback; Atlantic not Europe's only theater (Asia competition) but central to state power and early modern transformations through ~1825.

These chapters ground the volume's propositions (balance contrasts; leading sectors; chronology; values/ideas) and objections (interiors neglected; connections vs. internal; global linkages). They demonstrate Atlantic history's value for connective and comparative questions while flagging limits for deep old-world or continental narratives.
