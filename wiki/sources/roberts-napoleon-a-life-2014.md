# Napoleon: A Life (Andrew Roberts, 2014)

**Source page for Andrew Roberts’ *Napoleon: A Life* (2014).**

Roberts’s biography is a comprehensive re-evaluation grounded in the newly complete correspondence and direct engagement with the battlefields. Central thesis: Napoleon’s true victories were institutional and legal rather than purely military; he personified and codified the positive legacy of the French Revolution, exported it across Europe, and created durable structures (the Civil Code, rational administration, secular education, merit-based service) whose influence persists in dozens of countries. His military record (60 battles, 7 losses) was extraordinary, but overreach—especially the Continental System, Peninsular entanglement, and the 1812 Russian campaign—brought the empire down. The new letters reveal charm, humor, candid self-appraisal, relentless micromanagement, and a consistent modernizing project rather than mere conquest or egomania. Roberts argues Napoleon was not a proto-totalitarian dictator and that war was declared on him more often than he initiated it.

## Approach and Method

The book draws on the Fondation Napoléon’s ongoing publication (from 2004) of the full >33,000 letters, correcting the propagandistic distortions of earlier editions. Roberts supplements this with archival work, fifty-three battlefield visits (often with military historian John Lee), and a focus on character: humor, compartmentalization (“drawers in a cupboard”), capacity to inspire ordinary soldiers to feel they were making history. He explicitly critiques the self-serving *Mémorial de Sainte-Hélène* dictated on St Helena and the romanticizing gloss of 19th-century Bonapartist writers. The biography balances military narrative with heavy emphasis on lawgiving, administration, family dynamics (especially Josephine), and the long-term institutional legacy.

The work is deliberately not a staff-college operational study (see Chandler, Muir, Epstein for that layer); its strength is the human and institutional Napoleon revealed through his own words.

### From Range 01 (Introduction + Part One Rise early chapters; lines 823–7500)
**R01-Claim-1: Napoleon's Birth and Corsican Family Background**

Napoleon was born Napoleone di Buonaparte in Ajaccio on 15 August 1769 on a heap of tapestry after his mother Letizia felt labour pains returning from church. The Buonaparte family were minor Corsican gentry of Florentine origin with land, a mill, sheep, vineyard and servants; they occupied the social penumbra between haute bourgeoisie and very minor nobility and never had to buy wine, bread or olive oil. Carlo Bonaparte (tall, handsome, Enlightenment-influenced) and Letizia (strong-willed, uneducated) had eight surviving children; the family understood the need to align with France after the 1769 defeat.

" Napolean di Buonaparte, as he signed himself until manhood, was born in Ajaccio... just before noon on Tuesday, August 15, 1769. 'She was on her way home from church when she felt labour pains,' he would later say of his mother, Letizia, 'and had only time to get into the house, when I was born, not on a bed, but on a heap of tapestry.'" (Corsica chapter)

Preceded_by: French conquest of Corsica after the battle of Ponte Nuovo (1769). Contributed_to: Napoleon's later sense of himself as a "good Corsican family" outsider who would found an empire.

**R01-Claim-2: Carlo Bonaparte's Oath of Loyalty and Collaboration with France**

After the French victory at Ponte Nuovo (8 May 1769), Carlo and the pregnant Letizia refused exile with Paoli and took an oath of loyalty to Louis XV at a meeting with the Comte de Marbeuf. Carlo retained his positions as assessor of the Ajaccio court and superintendent of the forestry school, later representing the Corsican nobility in Paris and dining with the Comte de Vaux. The family later benefited from French rule; there is no evidence Napoleon despised his father for this, and he named a son Charles.

"Carlo acted as Paoli’s private secretary and aide-de-camp, but when Vaux smashed the Corsican forces at the battle of Ponte Nuovo... Carlo and the by now heavily pregnant Letizia refused to go into exile with Paoli... Carlo took an oath of loyalty to Louis XV..." (Corsica chapter)

Preceded_by: [[french-revolution-1789]] context of shifting alliances. Produced: conditions allowing Napoleon's French military education and later career.

**R01-Claim-3: Napoleon's Formation Through Education at Brienne and École Militaire**

Sent to France at age nine for Gallicization and a royal bursary, Napoleon learned French rapidly at Autun then attended the Royal Military School at Brienne (1779–1784) and the École Royale Militaire in Paris (1784–1785). He excelled in mathematics (key for artillery), geography, and devoured history/biography (Plutarch's Parallel Lives, Caesar, Arrian on Alexander, Polybius, Nepos' Lives of the Great Captains, Ossian, Rousseau, Corneille, Racine, Voltaire). Nicknamed "the Spartan"; he identified with Caesar over Pompey and emulated ancient heroes in posture and ambition. He spoke French with a thick Corsican accent.

"History could become for a young man the school of morality and virtue... Napoleon borrowed many biographies and history books from the school library, devouring Plutarch’s tales of heroism, patriotism and republican virtue. He also read Caesar, Cicero, Voltaire, Diderot..." (Corsica chapter)

Preceded_by: Corsican upbringing and Paoli reverence. Produced: military ethos of hierarchy, merit, order, and emulation of Alexander and Caesar that shaped [[napoleonic-warfare]].

**R01-Claim-4: Early Writings Revealing Corsican Nationalism and Romantic Self-Image**

While an officer at Valence and Auxonne (1785–1788), Napoleon wrote dozens of essays, including one (26 April 1786) arguing Corsicans had the right to resist French rule under Paoli, a "treasonous" document for a French officer. Another (May 1786) "On Suicide" mixed Rousseauian nationalism, melancholy, and "My fellow-countrymen are weighed down with chains..." He also drafted "Dissertation sur l’Autorité Royale" (concluding few kings deserved their thrones) and a history of Corsica. These show him as a writer manqué moving from Corsican nationalist to French officer.

"The Corsicans, following all the laws of justice, have been able to shake off the yoke of the Genoese, and may do the same with that of the French. Amen." (Valence, Corsica chapter)

"Always alone and in the midst of men... My fellow-countrymen are weighed down with chains... A good patriot ought to die when his fatherland has ceased to exist..." (Corsica chapter)

Preceded_by: Isolation in France and idolization of Paoli. Contributed_to: later rhetorical style of proclamations and self-mythologizing.

**R01-Claim-5: Napoleon's Extraordinary Compartmentalization**

Napoleon could close off sections of his mind like drawers in a cupboard. On the eve of battle he could dictate rules for a girls' school for Légion d’Honneur orphans or Comédie-Française regulations while aides arrived with orders. As emperor he micromanaged prefects, obscure priests' sermons, corporals' drinking, and even a demi-brigade's standard embroidery while radically transforming Europe.

"Napoleon was able to compartmentalize his life to quite a remarkable degree... he himself likened it to being able to open and close drawers in a cupboard. On the eve of battle... he could dictate his thoughts on the establishment of a girls’ school... and shortly after having captured Moscow he set down the regulations governing the Comédie-Française." (Introduction)

Preceded_by: Early autodidactic discipline and military training. Enabled: simultaneous personal, administrative, and military focus during rise and [[events/napoleonic-wars]].

**R01-Claim-6: Napoleon's Fine Sense of Humour**

Historians often took Napoleon's remarks seriously when they were jokes. He constantly joked with family and entourage even in dire situations. This contrasted with the dour image and was evident throughout his life.

"A more unexpected aspect of Napoleon’s personality that also came out strongly over the course of researching this book was his fine sense of humour. All too often historians have taken seriously remarks that were clearly intended as humorous. Napoleon was constantly joking to his family and entourage, even in the most dire situations." (Introduction)

Contributed_to: leadership appeal and humanizing his image.

**R01-Claim-7: Josephine Relationship: Overwhelming Crush, Early Infidelity, and Forgiveness**

Napoleon had an overwhelming crush on Josephine; she did not love him initially and was unfaithful from the start. Learning of her affair with Hippolyte Charles while in the Egyptian desert devastated him; he took a mistress in Cairo partly to avoid cuckoldry accusations. He forgave her on return; they enjoyed a decade of contentment despite his mistresses. She later remained faithful and fell in love with him. Divorce (for dynastic reasons) left her desolate but they stayed friendly.

"Napoleon’s love affair with Josephine has been presented all too often... as a Romeo and Juliet story: in fact, it was anything but. He had an overwhelming crush on her, but she didn’t love him, at least in the beginning, and was unfaithful from the very start of their marriage. When he learned of her infidelities two years later while on campaign in the middle of the Egyptian desert, he was devastated." (Introduction)

" I have a great, great deal of domestic sorrow as now the veil has been completely lifted." (Letter to Joseph, Egypt chapter)

Linked to [[actors/josephine-de-beauharnais]]. Preceded_by: post-Terror social milieu. Contributed_to: personal vulnerability during rise.

**R01-Claim-8: Historiographical Problems with Prior Sources and the Black Legend**

Most pre-Fondation Napoléon biographies relied on the bowdlerized Napoleon III correspondence (only two-thirds of letters). Many memoirs (Bourrienne ghosted, Rémusat changed after 1815 for Bourbon jobs, Constant Wairy ghosted by multiple writers, Laure d’Abrantès opium addict with Balzac ghosting, Fouché's by Beauchamp, etc.) were compromised, fictitious, or self-serving. The Hitler comparison (post-1940) distorted views; the author rejects Geyl-style parallels and "Napoleon Complex" tropes. The complete correspondence demands re-evaluation: charm, humour, no totalitarian intent, Enlightenment on horseback.

"More books have been written with Napoleon in the title than there have been days since his death... Every one of them published since 1857 relied upon the correspondence that Napoleon III published... We now know that this was shamefully bowdlerized... In all the compendium included only two-thirds of his total output." (Introduction)

"All too often historians have taken at face value the biographies written by people around Napoleon, whereas many of them were deeply compromised..." (Introduction)

Tied to [[sources/roberts-napoleon-a-life-2014]].

**R01-Claim-9: Napoleon's Identification with the Revolution**

After the 1804 royalist plot, Napoleon said: "They seek to destroy the Revolution by attacking my person... I will defend it, for I am the Revolution." He personified its best surviving aspects (meritocracy, equality before the law, etc.), saved them from Jacobin terror or royalist restoration, and codified them while discarding revolutionary calendar excesses, Cult of the Supreme Being, Directory corruption and hyper-inflation.

"'They seek to destroy the Revolution by attacking my person,' he said after the failure of the royalist assassination plot of 1804. 'I will defend it, for I am the Revolution.' His characteristic egotism aside, Napoleon was right. He personified the best parts of the French Revolution..." (Introduction)

"We have done with the romance of the Revolution,' he told an early meeting of his Conseil d’Etat, 'we must now commence its history.'" (Introduction)

Preceded_by: [[french-revolution-1789]]. Produced: institutional consolidation in [[periods/age-of-expansion]] and [[periods/long-19th-century]].

**R01-Claim-10: 13 Vendémiaire and Rapid Rise to High Command**

On 13 Vendémiaire (5 October 1795) Napoleon, appointed second-in-command by Barras, used grapeshot to crush Section revolt at Saint-Roch and other points, losing only ~6 men against ~300 rebels. This "whiff of grapeshot" ended mob influence in French politics for decades. He was promoted général de division then commander of the Army of the Interior. Family fortunes rose; he met Josephine via arms confiscation.

"The 'whiff of grapeshot' advanced the Bonaparte family hugely... Napoleon would now be paid 48,000 francs per annum..." (Italy chapter, covering Vendémiaire)

Preceded_by: Directory instability and Section revolt. Produced: command of Army of Italy.

**R01-Claim-11: Italian Campaign 1796: Strategy, Lodi, and Entry into Milan**

Arriving Nice March 1796, Napoleon reorganized supplies, used "strategy of the central position," fixed enemies then enveloped (Montenotte, Millesimo, Dego, Mondovì). Piedmont sued for peace at Cherasco. At Lodi (10 May) he stormed the narrow bridge under grapeshot with volunteer carabiniers; troops nicknamed him le petit caporal. He entered Milan 15 May, created Lombardic Republic, levied contributions, and sent art to Paris. "One more victory and we are masters of Italy."

"I no longer regarded myself as a simple general... but as a man called upon to decide the fate of peoples. It came to me then that I really could become a decisive actor on our national stage." (Italy chapter, post-Lodi reflection)

Linked to [[hubs/warfare/long-19th-century/napoleonic-wars/napoleonic-wars]], [[napoleonic-warfare]], [[mediterranean]], [[western-europe]].

**R01-Claim-12: Decision to Command Egyptian Expedition**

After Campo Formio and Paris return (Dec 1797), Napoleon evaluated invasion of Britain as too hazardous without naval supremacy. Directory chose Egypt option (to damage British trade/India routes, stretch Royal Navy, possibly remove him). He organized savants, printing press, 280-ship armada, captured Malta en route (June 1798), and issued proclamations framing himself as friend of Islam against Mamluks/Pope while invoking Alexander.

"Whatever efforts we make, we shall not for some years gain naval supremacy... If... we must really give up the expedition against England... or else undertake an eastern expedition which would menace her trade with the Indies." (Peace chapter)

"Europe is but a molehill... all the great reputations have come from Asia." (Egypt chapter)

Preceded_by: Campo Formio and British naval dominance. Part of [[atlantic-world]] and [[mediterranean]] strategy.

**R01-Claim-13: Junot's Revelation of Josephine's Affair in Egypt**

At Wardan (July 1798) Junot showed evidence of Josephine's affair with Hippolyte Charles. Napoleon wrote Joseph of "great, great deal of domestic sorrow," intended divorce, and desired solitude. No letters to Josephine survive from the campaign. British published intercepted letters exposing army miseries.

"I have a great, great deal of domestic sorrow as now the veil has been completely lifted. Only you remain there for me on this earth... it is my sad condition to have all these feelings for the same person in one heart alone." (Letter to Joseph, Egypt chapter)

Linked to [[actors/josephine-de-beauharnais]].

**R01-Claim-14: Brumaire Coup: Sieyès Alliance, Lucien, and Use of Force**

Napoleon allied with Sieyès (who saw him as "sword") after Directory failures and plots. On 18 Brumaire (9 Nov 1799) Elders (under influence) appointed him commander of 17th District and moved legislature to Saint-Cloud. On 19 Brumaire he addressed Elders then faced hostile Five Hundred ("Down with the tyrant! Cromwell! Hors la loi!"); grenadiers cleared the Orangery after Lucien’s theatrical sword-to-breast oath. Rump legislature appointed Napoleon, Sieyès, Ducos provisional Consuls. No popular resistance or barricades.

"Lucien... drew his sword, held its point against Napoleon’s breast, and cried: 'I swear that I will stab my own brother to the heart if he ever attempts anything against the liberty of Frenchmen.'" (Brumaire section)

"The Directory is no more... They appointed Sieyès, Ducos and Napoleon—in that order—as provisional Consuls..." (Brumaire section)

Preceded_by: Directory hyperinflation, corruption, military defeats. Produced: Consulate. Linked to [[actors/talleyrand-charles-maurice]] (key conspirator), [[actors/napoleon]].

**R01-Claim-15: Early Consulate: Constitution of Year VIII and "Completing the Revolution"**

Napoleon outmanoeuvred Sieyès' Grand Elector scheme; the Year VIII Constitution (Dec 1799) made him First Consul with broad powers (promulgate laws, appoint ministers/ambassadors/officers, treaty powers, live at Tuileries). Early decrees: Versailles to wounded, repealed harsh anti-émigré laws, released hostages, pensions for war-wounded, mourning for Washington, non-juring priests tolerated. "The Revolution is established on the principles which began it. It is finished."

"Frenchmen! A Constitution is presented to you. It ends the uncertainties... Citizens, the Revolution is established on the principles which began it. It is finished." (Consul chapter)

"The placing of property rights before those of equality and liberty was indicative of how Napoleon intended to defend the interests of tradesmen..." (Consul chapter)

Produced: shift from [[french-revolution-1789]] chaos to centralized executive in [[periods/age-of-expansion]] / [[periods/long-19th-century]].

### From Range 02 (Part Two early Mastery; lines 7501–11500)
**R02-Claim-1: Constitution of the Year VIII centralizes power in the First Consul while maintaining republican appearances**

The new constitution (effective 25 December 1799/Christmas Day) placed political and administrative power with the First Consul for ten years, with the Senate, Tribunate, and Legislative Body separated in ways that left real initiative and control with Napoleon. The Tribunate could discuss but not veto or initiate; the Legislative Body could vote but not discuss. Only the Senate could amend the constitution via sénatus-consultes. "By these means, Napoleon ensured the separation of fairly feeble powers among them, keeping the lion’s share for himself." (Constitution of the Year VIII section, early in range.)

**caused_by:** [[french-revolution-1789]] (post-coup stabilization after Brumaire). **produced:** [[actors/napoleon]]'s dominance. Links only to established names.

**R02-Claim-2: Lucien Bonaparte systematically falsified the 1800 plebiscite results for the Constitution of the Year VIII**

Lucien (interior minister) halted counting on 4 February and added ~900,000 fabricated yes votes (including invented military votes of 556,021–0) to reach the announced 3,011,007–1,562. Archives Nationales bundles show his handwriting. "The true result was probably around 1.55 million yes against several thousand no." Napoleon still secured "some kind of democratic legitimacy, but by far less than he claimed." (Plebiscite section.)

**produced:** inflated claims of support. **preceded_by:** [[french-revolution-1789]]. Notes Napoleonic pattern of exaggeration in propaganda.

**R02-Claim-3: Prefectorial system abolished local self-government and concentrated power centrally**

Law of February 1800 replaced elected local authorities with centrally appointed prefects (and sub-prefects/mayors for larger communes). "An essential element of local democracy established by the Revolution was thus completely abolished at a stroke." Prefects (often outsiders for loyalty) handled taxes, conscription, law/order, billeting, and economic development; local councils were advisory only and sat briefly. System proved more efficient than predecessor. (Prefects section.)

**caused_by:** need for stability post-Br umaire. **produced:** centralized administration. **part_of:** broader Consulate reforms.

**R02-Claim-4: Creation of the Banque de France stabilized finances after Brumaire**

Opened 13 February 1800 with Napoleon as first shareholder; initial capital from Rouen/Swiss bankers. Granted exclusive Paris note issue in 1803 (later legal tender). "The bank would theoretically be independent... but in time this policy was quietly dropped." Addressed prior dependence on private bankers/contractors (e.g., arresting Ouvrard). (Banque section.)

**caused_by:** near-empty Treasury. **produced:** improved credit and war financing capacity. **enabled:** later campaigns.

**R02-Claim-5: Marengo campaign (May–June 1800) featured surprise Alpine crossing and near-disaster reversed by Desaix and Kellermann**

51,400 men crossed Great St Bernard (plus Moncey at St Gothard); Fort Bard delayed artillery. Napoleon entered Milan 2 June. At Marengo (14 June), ~15k French faced ~29k Austrians initially; French line broken by 2–4 p.m. Desaix (recalled urgently: "I had thought to attack... in the name of God, come back") + Boudet arrived ~5 p.m.; Kellermann’s 400 cavalry charged unloaded Austrian infantry. "We have gone back far enough today... remember it is my custom to bivouac on the field of battle!" Victory confirmed Consulate. (Marengo chapter.)

**produced:** Italian reconquest + armistice terms (Piedmont, Genoa, Lombardy, 12 fortresses). **preceded_by:** Brumaire/Constitution. Quotes from campaign letters/battle accounts.

**R02-Claim-6: Post-Marengo propaganda and bulletins distorted the battle for political effect**

Bulletin implied Austrians fell into a trap; invented Desaix’s last words ("Go tell the First Consul that I die with the regret of having not done enough..."). Berthier’s history revised three times. Napoleon later claimed victory "before Desaix arrived." Privately: "You see, there were two battles... I lost the first; I gained the second." Desaix’s aide: delay of one hour would have driven French into the Po. (Marengo aftermath.)

**part_of:** Napoleonic propaganda pattern (also noted in plebiscites, speeches). Links to [[sources/roberts-napoleon-a-life-2014]] (source criticism).

**R02-Claim-7: Concordat (signed July 1801, published April 1802) reconciled Church and state on Napoleon’s terms**

**R02-Claim-8: Code Napoléon (Civil Code 1804) unified law but entrenched patriarchal conservatism and employer power**

Divorce restricted (adultery only if mistress brought home); women barred from contracts, lawsuits, guardianship, market sales without consent; fathers could imprison disobedient children. **R03-Claim-2: The Berlin Decrees (November 21, 1806) launched the Continental System as economic retaliation against Britain after Trafalgar.**

**R03-Claim-3: The Continental System caused economic distress in France and the Empire, widespread smuggling, and failed to cripple Britain as intended.**

Napoleon suppressed smuggling harshly (customs officers on the Elbe, death penalties in some cases) but could not eliminate it.

### From Range 04 (Part Three Denouement; lines 15501–19500)
**R04-Claim-1: Alexander I's rearmament, ukaz, and refusal to negotiate as direct drivers of the 1812 war**  

From late 1810, Tsar Alexander rearmed Russia extensively, reformed the army under Barclay de Tolly, and issued a ukaz opening trade to neutrals while banning certain French goods, directly contravening Tilsit/Erfurt. This, combined with annexation disputes over Oldenburg and suspicions over Poland, made conflict inevitable despite Napoleon's repeated attempts at correspondence. Napoleon wrote Alexander in late February 1812 listing grievances over Oldenburg, Moldavia/Wallachia, and the ukaz, insisting differences could be resolved without war, but the Tsar rebuffed further efforts.  

Verbatim: “By 1814,’ he said, ‘I can, according to my most exact calculations, enter the lists with a well-equipped army of 400,000. With 200,000 I will cross the Oder, while another 200,000 will cross the Vistula.” (early in Chapter 23 “Russia,” recounting Wrangel’s 1810 visit and ukaz context). “Alexander took up none of Napoleon’s proposals. **R04-Claim-2: Typhus epidemic as major non-combat killer crippling the Grande Armée early in the 1812 invasion**  

Typhus, spread by lice in unwashed troops and poor conditions, struck immediately after crossing the Niemen; 6,000 men fell ill daily in the first week, with over 80,000 dead or sick by the third week of July (at least 50,000 from typhus). This destroyed roughly one-fifth of the central army group within a month, before any major battle, exacerbating supply and morale collapse. Napoleon’s medical services could not respond effectively as the link to lice was unknown.  

Verbatim: “In the first week of the campaign alone, 6,000 men fell ill with it every day. By the third week of July over 80,000 men had either died or were sick, at least 50,000 of them from typhus. Within a month of the start of the invasion, Napoleon had lost one-fifth of the men in his central army group.” (mid-Chapter 24 “Trapped,” detailed typhus section). “An average of 1,000 horses were to die for every day of the 175 days that the Grande Armée spent in Russia.”

**R04-Claim-3: Critical decision point at Vitebsk—Napoleon seriously considers halting but presses on to Smolensk**  

After reaching Vitebsk in late July 1812 with heavy losses from disease and attrition, Napoleon contemplated ending the campaign there, establishing magazines, reorganizing Lithuania, and resuming in 1813. He cited exhausted cavalry, the need to rally, and natural defensive lines (Dvina/Dnieper). Advisers including Duroc, Caulaincourt, Daru, and others urged stopping; Murat pushed continuing. Napoleon ultimately chose to advance, fearing loss of initiative, Austrian/Prussian wavering, and the chance of battle at Smolensk.  

Verbatim: “Here I stop!’ Ségur recalled Napoleon saying on entering Vitebsk on the 28th. ‘Here I must look around me; rally, refresh my army and reorganise Poland. The campaign of 1812 is finished; that of 1813 will do the rest.” “The decision to press on to Smolensk was not taken lightly. ‘Did they take him for a madman?’ Ségur recorded Napoleon saying to Daru and Berthier around the 11th.” (Chapter 24 “Trapped”).

**R04-Claim-4: Borodino battle—Napoleon refuses to commit the Imperial Guard, achieving a pyrrhic tactical success**  

On 7 September 1812 at Borodino (bloodiest single day of warfare to that point), after intense fighting for the flèches and Great Redoubt, multiple marshals (Ney, Davout, Murat) and Rapp urged Napoleon to unleash the Guard to exploit the Russian left flank. He refused repeatedly, citing the need to preserve reserves for a potential second battle before Moscow, distance from France (1,800 miles), and uncertainty (“to see more clearly upon his chessboard”). The army took the field but failed to destroy the Russian army; French losses ~28,000 vs. Russian ~43,000 (few prisoners/guns).  

Verbatim: “By 4 p.m. the Grande Armée had taken the field of battle. When Eugène, Murat and Ney repeated their request to release the Guard, this time its cavalry, Napoleon again refused. ‘I do not wish to see it destroyed,’ he told Rapp. ‘I am sure to gain the battle without it taking a part.’” “People will be surprised that I did not commit my reserves to obtain greater results… but I had to keep them for striking a decisive blow in the great battle the enemy will fight in front of Moscow.” (Chapter 24 “Trapped,” post-battle reflections).

**R04-Claim-5: Moscow fire and occupation—Russians burn the city, confirming no peace and forcing hard choices**  

Napoleon entered Moscow 15 September 1812 to find it largely evacuated; fires broke out simultaneously that evening (aided by wind and Rostopchin’s removal of fire equipment). Much of the city (6,500 of 9,000 major buildings) burned. Napoleon initially saw capture as ending the war (“considered the business as done”) but quickly recognized the arson showed “a hell of a strength of character” and that the burners were “not people to make peace a few days later.” He stayed over a month hoping for negotiation while living off the city.  

Verbatim: “I am setting fire to my mansion… rather than let it be sullied by your presence.” (Rostopchin). “Napoleon’s earlier hopes revived at the sight of the palace, but at dusk that evening fires broke out simultaneously across the city.” “Don’t believe that the ones who burnt Moscow are people to make peace a few days later.” (Chapter 25 “Retreat”).

**R04-Claim-6: Maloyaroslavets council and fateful route decision**  

After the bitter 24 October 1812 battle at Maloyaroslavets (town changed hands nine times), Napoleon convened a rare council in a weaver’s hut at Gorodnya on 25 October. Davout urged the southern Medyn/Yelnya route through fresh territory; most (Eugène, Berthier, Caulaincourt, Bessières) favored the northern Mozhaisk–Smolensk road due to known depots, congestion risks, and Kutuzov’s parallel position. Murat supported attacking south. Napoleon chose the northern route (later admitting “I was the master, and mine was the fault”). This lengthened exposure to winter and Russian harassment.  

Verbatim: “Smolensk was the goal. Should they march thither by Kaluga, Medyn or Mozhaisk? Napoleon was seated at a table, his head supported by his hands…” “Well, gentlemen… I will decide.” “No; I was the master, and mine was the fault.” (Chapter 25 “Retreat,” council and retrospective). “This fatal field which put a halt to the conquest of the world…”

**R04-Claim-7: Berezina crossing as near-miraculous but costly deliverance amid total collapse**  

By late November 1812, with Wittgenstein and Chichagov converging on the Berezina and the army a disorganized rabble (~40,000 effectives plus stragglers), Éblé’s engineers (disobeying orders to destroy tools) built two precarious trestle bridges at Studzianka in freezing water. Oudinot feinted; Victor held Wittgenstein; the main force crossed 26–28 November in −33°C conditions. ~50,000 crossed; 15,000+ stragglers and civilians left behind were massacred or captured when bridges were destroyed. Napoleon crossed calmly, encouraging pontonniers.  

Verbatim: “What Saint-Cyr in his memoirs accurately described as ‘the miraculous crossing of the Berezina’ had begun…” “He watched his army pass by in the most wretched condition… his outward appearance seemed indifferent and unconcerned…” (Chapter 25 “Retreat”).

**R04-Claim-8: Napoleon’s self-control and character under extreme stress (letters, Malet, losses)**  

Napoleon maintained rigid emotional control (“it’s taken me years to cultivate self-control to prevent my emotions from betraying themselves”) even after Russia, dictating “My health is good” repeatedly in letters to Marie Louise (dozens of instances pre- and post-Moscow). The Malet conspiracy (forged news of his death) shocked him because no one prioritized the King of Rome/Marie Louise. He mourned Duroc and Bessières personally but prioritized duty.  

Verbatim: “In my own case it’s taken me years to cultivate self-control… Without all this self-control, do you think I could have done all I’ve done?” (conversation with Molé, Chapter 26 “Resilience”). “Napoleon II… nobody thought about him!” (on Malet). “The health of His Majesty has never been better.” (29th bulletin). “I have more than four hundred million in the cellars of the Tuileries, and would gladly have given the whole for the ransom of my faithful companion-in-arms.” (Ney’s survival).

**R04-Claim-9: Leipzig (“Battle of the Nations”)—overwhelming Allied numbers, Saxon defection, and bridge disaster turn victory into rout**  

16–19 October 1813, Napoleon with ~203,000 (weak cavalry/artillery) faced ~362,000 Allies (1,456 guns) converging on Leipzig. Initial attacks gained ground but reinforcements (Bennigsen, Bernadotte) and the Saxon/Württemberg defection (battery turning on French) eroded the line. On 19th, with retreat decided, the single Elster bridge was blown prematurely (delegated to an ignorant corporal), trapping >20,000. Total French losses ~47,000 killed/wounded + 38,000 captured, 325 guns.  

Verbatim: “Among the half-million men who fought at Leipzig… were French, Germans (on both sides), Russians, Swedes…” “The Saxon battery actually turned around, unlimbered and began firing on the French lines.” “Pontoon bridges had not been built… the bridge was blown up at 11.30 a.m., long before the whole army had got over it…” (Chapter 27 “Leipzig”).

**R04-Claim-10: Metternich’s Dresden demands and Napoleon’s refusal to accept “dishonourable” peace**  

In the long 26 June 1813 Dresden meeting (8–9+ hours), Metternich pressed terms far beyond Illyria: partition of Warsaw, evacuation of Poland/Prussia, independence for Spain/Hanseatic towns, revocation of German protectorate, etc. Napoleon threw his hat in rage, accused Metternich of English pay (later regretted), and insisted he would not accept terms that would see him overthrown by the French for dishonour. He accepted Prague mediation but the terms were effectively impossible.  

Verbatim: “I had a long and wearisome talk with Metternich… I want peace, but it must be an honourable one.” (to Marie Louise). Detailed demands and hat-throwing in the Chinese Room (Chapter 26 “Resilience,” multiple sources reconciled). “I owe everything to my glory… If I brought this nation… a peace on terms which would make me blush personally, it would lose all confidence in me.”  
Links: **contributed_to** continuation of war; **concurrent_with** Austrian rearmament and Trachenberg planning; response from [[actors/alexander-i]] intransigence.

### From Range 05 (Waterloo, St Helena, Epilogue; lines 19501–22360)
**R05-Claim-1: Provisional Abdication at Fontainebleau (April 1814)**

After the capitulation of Paris and pressure from marshals (who had not forced abdication after 1812 or 1813 but did so in 1814 despite ongoing defensive victories), Napoleon signed a provisional abdication limited to himself (not heirs). He intended to ratify it only after a treaty securing Elba and family provisions.

Analysis: The text frames this as a calculated sacrifice for France amid Allied unity (Treaty of Chaumont) and domestic collapse, not total defeat on the battlefield. Napoleon retained title and hoped for better terms via negotiation.

Verbatim (Elba section, Fontainebleau): “The Allied powers having declared that the Emperor Napoleon was the only obstacle to the re-establishment of peace in Europe… the Emperor Napoleon, faithful to his oath, declares that he renounces, for himself and his heirs, the thrones of France and Italy…”

Links: preceded_by: [[events/napoleonic-wars]] (prior campaigns and 1814 fighting); produced: exile arrangements including Elba.

**R05-Claim-2: Suicide Attempt at Fontainebleau**

On the night of 12–13 April 1814, after dinner with Caulaincourt and Macdonald, Napoleon ingested poison he had carried since nearly being captured by Cossacks at Maloyaroslavets. It had lost potency; he survived after vomiting induced by doctor Yvan (possibly with ashes).

Analysis: The text presents this as a deliberate act to potentially secure his son’s position (“who knows that my death might not place the crown on the head of my son?”) once his life no longer belonged to France post-abdication. It underscores personal agency amid despair but failure due to time-degraded poison.

Verbatim (Elba section): “My life no longer belonged to my country… Why should I endure so much suffering? … I reflected, ‘and who knows that my death might not place the crown on the head of my son?’ France was saved. I hesitated no longer, but leaping from my bed, mixed the poison in a little water, and drank it with a sort of feeling of happiness.”

Links: caused_by: collapse of 1814 position and marshals’ pressure; concurrent_with: signing of abdication the next morning.

**R05-Claim-3: Farewell to the Old Guard and Departure for Elba**

On 20 April 1814 in the White Horse courtyard (Cour des Adieux), Napoleon delivered an emotional address to the Old Guard before leaving Fontainebleau. He kissed General Petit, embraced eagles/flags, and boarded a carriage for the journey to Fréjus and eventual embarkation on HMS Undaunted.

Analysis: The oration justified the abdication as prioritizing France’s interests over continued civil/foreign war. It emphasized loyalty, duty, and legacy. Text highlights the Guard’s continued fidelity (“Those he made suffer most, the soldiers, were the very ones who were most faithful to him”) despite national apathy.

Verbatim (Elba section): “Officers, non-commissioned officers, and soldiers of the Old Guard, I bid you adieu! … I have made the sacrifice of all my rights, and am ready to make that of my person, for the aim of all my life has been the happiness and glory of France. … You are all my children. … Farewell! Preserve me in your memories! Adieu, my children!”

Links: followed_by: arrival on Elba; part_of: [[events/napoleonic-wars]] end phase.

**R05-Claim-4: Josephine’s Death and Its Timing (May 1814)**

Josephine died of pneumonia at Malmaison on 29 May 1814, aged 50, after walking in cold night air with Tsar Alexander. Napoleon decreed two days of mourning. He later said “She was the wife who would have gone with me to Elba” and wondered superstitiously if divorcing her had coincided with (or caused) his change of fortune.

Analysis: Text contrasts her fidelity in adversity with Marie Louise’s rapid move to Vienna/Neipperg. Her death is presented as a poignant personal loss amid political exile; last letter to her was jocular and familiar.

Verbatim (Elba section): “At noon on Sunday, May 29, 1814, Josephine died of pneumonia at Malmaison. … ‘She was the wife who would have gone with me to Elba,’ Napoleon later said, and he decreed two days of mourning. … ‘Adieu, my love: tell me that you are well. I’m informed that you are getting as fat as a Norman farmer’s good wife. Napoleon.’”

Links: concurrent_with: Napoleon’s early Elba period; preceded_by: [[actors/josephine-de-beauharnais]] relationship.

**R05-Claim-5: Life and Reforms on Elba (1814–early 1815)**

On Elba (arrived May 1814), Napoleon reorganized defences, gave to the poor, installed fountains, read voraciously, grew mulberries/vines, reformed customs/excise, built hospital, paved streets, irrigated land, set court of appeal, prohibited children sleeping more than five to a bed, and organized rubbish collection. Finances were strained (Bourbons never paid the 2.5 million francs annuity).

Analysis: Text portrays relentless activity and attention to detail despite “royaume d’opérette” scale and weight gain. He remained watchful for return opportunities. “Indefatigable as he is, corpulency prevents him from walking much…”

Verbatim (Elba section): “During his nearly ten months on Elba he reorganized his new kingdom’s defences, gave money to the poorest of its 11,400 inhabitants, installed a fountain… reformed customs and excise, repaired the barracks, built a hospital, planted vineyards, paved parts of Portoferraio for the first time and irrigated land. He also organized regular rubbish collections, passed a law prohibiting children from sleeping more than five to a bed, set up a court of appeal…”

Links: part_of: [[periods/age-of-expansion]] exile interlude; preceded_by: Treaty of Fontainebleau.

**R05-Claim-6: Ney’s Defection and March on Paris (March 1815)**

After landing at Golfe-Juan (1 March 1815) with ~1,142 men, Napoleon marched via the Alps (“Route Napoléon”). At Laffrey, troops of the 5th Line refused to fire and joined him. Ney, sent to seize him, defected with most troops at Auxerre (14 March), declaring “Only the Emperor Napoleon is entitled to rule over our beautiful country.” Napoleon re-entered Tuileries 20 March.

Analysis: Text emphasizes propaganda, charisma, and Bourbon errors (army mistreatment, white flag, émigré appointments) enabling rapid rally without bloodshed. “On my march from Cannes to Grenoble I was an adventurer; in Grenoble I once again became a sovereign.”

Verbatim (return/Elba transition to Waterloo section): “The Emperor approached; the battalion kept a profound silence. … The Emperor didn’t give him time: he talked to the soldiers and asked them as usual: ‘Well! How are you doing in the 5th?’ … ‘Only the Emperor Napoleon is entitled to rule over our beautiful country,’ Ney told his men.”

Links: produced: Hundred Days reconstitution of government; caused_by: Bourbon policies and army loyalty.

**R05-Claim-7: Waterloo Campaign Errors (Ligny/Quatre Bras/Grouchy, June 1815)**

At Ligny (16 June), d’Erlon’s corps was misdirected between Ney (Quatre Bras) and Napoleon, preventing decisive pursuit of Blücher. Napoleon delayed rising 17 June and sent Grouchy (33,000 men) late to pursue Prussians east; Blücher instead retreated north. Napoleon assumed Prussians were shattered and out of the fight.

Analysis: Explicit critique of torpor, failure to concentrate, and violation of his own maxim against detaching forces on eve of battle. “I ought to have taken all the other troops with me.”

Verbatim (Waterloo section): “Had d’Erlon arrived as arranged, Napoleon’s respectable victory at Ligny would have turned into a devastating rout… ‘Now then, Grouchy, follow up those Prussians…’ But in sending Grouchy off, he was ignoring one of his own military maxims: ‘No force should be detached on the eve of battle…’”

Links: contributed_to: outcome of [[events/waterloo-1815]]; concurrent_with: [[actors/wellington-arthur-wellesley]] and Prussian movements.

**R05-Claim-8: Conduct and Defeat at Waterloo (18 June 1815)**

Napoleon delayed attack due to mud (Drouot’s advice). d’Erlon’s infantry assaulted in unwieldy deep formations (vulnerable to cavalry/artillery). Ney launched massive unsupported cavalry charges (~4 p.m.). Prussians (Bülow) arrived on right flank ~1:30 p.m. onward. After La Haie Sainte capture, Ney begged for troops; Napoleon refused (“Troops? Where would you like me to find them?”), having 14 unused Guard battalions. Middle/Imperial Guard assault failed ~7 p.m.; “La Garde recule!” triggered collapse.

Analysis: Text attributes defeat to unforced errors, poor coordination (especially artillery support), and underestimation of Wellington/Blücher link-up. “Not only did Wellington and Blücher deserve to win the battle of Waterloo: Napoleon very much deserved to lose it.” “Incomprehensible day.”

Verbatim (Waterloo section): “It will be a lunchtime affair! … ‘Troops?’ he said sarcastically to Levasseur. ‘Where would you like me to find them? Would you like me to make them?’ … ‘Come, general, the affair is over – we have lost the day – let us be off.’”

Links: part_of: [[events/waterloo-1815]]; contrasted_with: [[actors/wellington-arthur-wellesley]] defensive mastery.

**R05-Claim-9: Second Abdication, Flight, and Surrender (June–July 1815)**

After Waterloo, Napoleon returned to Paris, issued a bulletin blaming “malicious malefactors,” and abdicated (22 June) for his son (Napoleon II) under chambers’ pressure. He hesitated at Rochefort, rejected Joseph’s identity swap and risky boat escapes, then surrendered to Captain Maitland of HMS Bellerophon (15 July), writing to Prince Regent comparing himself to Themistocles.

Analysis: Text stresses vacillation (uncharacteristic), desire for dignity/safety over risk, and miscalculation of British intentions. Royal Navy blockade and Allied determination closed options.

Verbatim (St Helena section): “Frenchmen! … I offer myself in sacrifice to the hatred of the enemies of France… My political life is over… I come, like Themistocles, to seat myself at the hearth of the British people.”

Links: followed_by: St Helena exile; preceded_by: [[events/waterloo-1815]] defeat.

**R05-Claim-10: Life, Memoirs, and Health Decline on St Helena**

On St Helena (arrived Oct 1815), Napoleon dictated Mémorial de Sainte-Hélène (to Las Cases et al.) for up to 12 hours/day, spreading maps on billiard table, seeking to shape legacy: “Arcole, Rivoli, the Pyramids, Marengo, Austerlitz, Jena, Friedland – these are granite.” He befriended Betsy Balcombe at The Briars; suffered under Hudson Lowe’s restrictions. Health declined from late 1816 (swellings, side pains, vomiting, depression); autopsy confirmed stomach cancer (pylorus focus, “ulcerated all over like a honeycomb”).

Analysis: Explicit self-mythologizing and historiographic intent. Text details physical/mental toll, Lowe conflicts, and autopsy findings while noting prior robust health. “What a novel my life has been!”

Verbatim (St Helena section): “Many faults, no doubt, will be found in my career… but Arcole, Rivoli… these are granite: the teeth of envy are powerless here.” Post-mortem: “The internal surface of the stomach to nearly its whole length, was a mass of cancerous disease… How Napoleon could have existed for any time with such an organ was wonderful.”

Links: part_of: [[periods/long-19th-century]] captivity; produced: Mémorial influence on historiography.

**R05-Claim-11: Key Post-1815 Fates (Josephine, Marie Louise, Marshals, Allies)**

Josephine died 1814. Marie Louise became lovers with (and later married) Count Adam von Neipperg. Ney executed by Bourbons (Napoleon: “Ney got no more than he deserved”). Marmont (“ragusard” = traitor) lived in exile, pursued by taunts. Talleyrand maneuvered for restoration. [[actors/alexander-i]] and others formed Holy Alliance and maintained balance against liberalism. Napoleon’s remains returned to Les Invalides 1840.

Analysis: Text records personal betrayals, executions, and European reaction. Napoleon expressed mixed regret/forgiveness and satisfaction at Joseph’s escape to America.

Verbatim (St Helena/Epilogue sections): “Murat has only had what he deserved… It was all my own fault.” “Ney got no more than he deserved.” Epilogue notes Metternich, Alexander I, etc., reactions and “the bones of Frenchmen, scattered in every region.”

Links: concurrent_with: [[actors/talleyrand-charles-maurice]] and [[actors/alexander-i]] post-1815 order; preceded_by: [[events/waterloo-1815]].

**R05-Claim-12: Assessments of Character, Achievements, and Long-term Legacy**

Text (via letters, interviews, Mémorial, and narrator) portrays Napoleon as energetic, detail-obsessed, capable of charm/kindness (to individuals, including enemies), resilient, and hubristic; “I have never seen a man… with so much personal activity and restless perseverance.” Epilogue credits legal/political reforms with preventing full Ancien Régime restoration. He viewed his “crown of thorns” on St Helena as adding to glory.

Analysis: Explicit historiographic self-assessment and external observations. Institutional legacy (reforms) outlasted military defeat; personal magnetism noted even by British captors.

Verbatim (Elba/Waterloo/St Helena/Epilogue): “I have worn the imperial crown… England has now given me a greater and more glorious [one]… a crown of thorns.” “Thanks in large part to the legal and political reforms undertaken by Napoleon, the Bourbons were unable to reintroduce the old ways…”

Links: produced: long-term effects on [[periods/long-19th-century]] and [[western-europe]]; analogous_to: comparisons in Mémorial.

## Volume Synthesis Note

Andrew Roberts’ *Napoleon: A Life* (2014) supplies the wiki’s first comprehensive dedicated biographical spine for Napoleon grounded in the post-2004 Fondation Napoléon complete correspondence (33,000+ letters, correcting the bowdlerized Napoleon III edition used by nearly all prior lives). Its core contribution is the re-centering of Napoleon’s historical significance on **institutions and legal codification** rather than purely military conquest: the Civil Code (equality before the law, contract freedom, end to feudal privileges), rational prefectorial administration, merit-based service (Légion d’Honneur), secular education, finance (Banque de France), and religious settlement (Concordat) are presented as the “greatest and most lasting victories.” These saved the Revolution’s best elements from Jacobin excess and Bourbon reaction while modernizing Europe; they explain why the Bourbons could not fully restore the Ancien Régime in 1815.

Character portrait from the unexpurgated letters: relentless micromanager who simultaneously dictated school regulations and army orders; constant humor even in crisis; exceptional compartmentalization (“drawers in a cupboard”); candid self-appraisal alongside self-mythologizing (Las Cases *Mémorial* explicitly critiqued as propaganda). Josephine relationship receives textured treatment (initial crush and her infidelity, reconciliation and contentment, dynastic divorce pain). Roberts’ 53-battlefield visits add topographic insight (e.g., Lodi bridge, Marengo phases, Borodino redoubt, Maloyaroslavets council hut, Waterloo mud).

Causation and blame re-evaluated: war declared on Napoleon more often than he initiated; Continental System (Colbertian protectionism post-Trafalgar) as primary economic self-infliction provoking European resentment; 1812 Russia not inherently fatal (prior victories over Russians) but typhus (unknown vector) + size of army forcing Russian retreat strategy + fateful northern route choice at Maloyaroslavets after council + Berezina collapse. Waterloo defeat attributed to unforced coordination errors (Grouchy detachment, d’Erlon misdirection, Ney’s unsupported cavalry, refusal to commit Guard) more than Allied genius alone. “Not only did Wellington and Blücher deserve to win… Napoleon very much deserved to lose it.”

Complements prior sources: adds personal, institutional, and letter-based texture to the operational depth of Chandler (campaigns), Muir (tactics/experience), Rothenberg (armies/institutions), and Epstein (emergence of modern war at Wagram). Explicitly positions Napoleon as “Enlightenment on horseback” who modernized while conquering. No major contradiction with existing wiki material on Napoleonic warfare or the Revolution’s institutionalization; strengthens the “Great Man vs. structural” counterfactual already present in the actor page.

Range claims supply verbatim grounding for: Corsican outsider identity and classical emulation; Brumaire mechanics and early Consulate (Constitution, prefects, Code, Concordat, plebiscite rigging); Marengo recovery and propaganda; Amiens fragility; Saint-Domingue disaster and Louisiana implications; Enghien ruthlessness; Empire creation and family thrones; Jena/Auerstädt, Eylau, Friedland, Tilsit diplomacy (lenient on Alexander, punitive on Prussia); Continental System failures and smuggling; Wagram/Aspern; 1812 typhus/attrition, Vitebsk hesitation, Borodino Guard refusal, Moscow fire, Maloyaroslavets route choice, Berezina “miracle”; Leipzig collapse and bridge disaster; Metternich Dresden confrontation; 1814 abdication/suicide attempt/Elba reforms; 1815 return, Waterloo errors, surrender to Maitland, St Helena Mémorial self-fashioning and cancer decline; Epilogue fates and institutional survival.
