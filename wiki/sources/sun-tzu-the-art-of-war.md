# The Art of War (Sun Tzu, trans. Lionel Giles 1910)

This is the foundational East Asian military treatise attributed to Sun Tzu (Sun Wu), a general of the state of Wu during the late Spring and Autumn or early Warring States period (traditionally 6th–5th c. BCE, with text likely compiled later). The Giles 1910 edition includes a substantial translator's introduction on the historical Sun Tzu, the transmission and authenticity of the text, traditional commentators, and appreciations, followed by the 13 chapters with footnotes.

The work emphasizes that "the acme of skill is to subdue the enemy without fighting," prioritizing intelligence, deception, terrain, morale, timing, and economy of force. It has influenced Chinese, Japanese, and later Western strategic thought (e.g., Liddell Hart's indirect approach, modern business and irregular warfare applications).

## Historiography Note

The *Art of War* is the earliest surviving systematic Chinese military treatise. Giles (British Museum, 1910) provides critical apparatus noting that the received text shows signs of compilation and later accretions; he critiques earlier European translations (Amiot, Calthrop) for inaccuracies and omissions. Traditional Chinese commentaries (from Cao Cao onward) layer philosophical, historical, and tactical glosses. The work's aphoristic style lends itself to broad application beyond battlefield (statecraft, business, irregular conflict). For the wiki, it supplies the non-Western pole of the strategic canon, emphasizing cognitive/psychological dimensions and the preference for winning without fighting—contrasting yet comparable to Greco-Roman and modern Western emphases. Collection coverage for early East Asian military thought is moderate; this primary translation anchors claims.

**Range summary paragraph:** These middle chapters present Sun Tzu's framework for tactical mastery as the controlled application of knowledge, concealment, and adaptability: first render oneself invulnerable through superior disposition and preparation, then apply direct and indirect energy (with endless combinations) to exploit the enemy's weak points while dividing his forces and shaping his perceptions through deception and baits. Maneuvering requires turning disadvantage into advantage via speed and timing while preserving the army's spirit and logistics; variations demand that the general blend advantage with disadvantage and ignore even sovereign commands when militarily necessary. Terrain and marching rules, together with systematic observation of enemy signs, enable the commander to occupy favorable ground first and strike at the right moment. Throughout, the text stresses that victory flows from making no mistakes, husbanding strength, and forcing the enemy into positions where his own actions contribute to his defeat, all grounded in intimate knowledge of self, enemy, and circumstances.

## Consolidated Volume Synthesis

The *Art of War* (Sun Tzu, Giles 1910) supplies the foundational primary text for East Asian strategic thought in the wiki. Its core thesis—"All warfare is based on deception" and "supreme excellence consists in breaking the enemy's resistance without fighting"—establishes an enduring emphasis on foreknowledge, positioning, adaptability, moral cohesion, and economy of force that contrasts with and complements Western traditions (Machiavelli's virtù, Clausewitz's friction and "war is the continuation of politics," Liddell Hart's indirect approach).

Giles' apparatus situates the 13-chapter treatise in its Warring States context while noting textual layers and the value of the 11+ commentators. Key recurring principles across ranges: the five constant factors and seven comparisons for forecasting; the direct/indirect dialectic producing inexhaustible combinations; mastery of terrain and the nine situations with tailored responses (especially desperate ground to forge unbreakable unity); fire as adjunct under precise conditions; and above all the five-spy system (local, inward, converted, doomed, surviving) as the "divine manipulation" enabling foreknowledge. The text repeatedly warns against prolongation, ruler interference, and the five dangerous faults of generals.
