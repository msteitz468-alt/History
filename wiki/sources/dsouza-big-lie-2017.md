# D'Souza — The Big Lie (2017)

**What this is.** A *New York Times*-bestselling political polemic published in the
first year of the Trump presidency, arguing that fascism and Nazism were ideologies
of the left; that the American Democratic Party is their true institutional heir
(via slavery, the Ku Klux Klan, Jim Crow, Progressive eugenics, and the New Deal);
and that the contemporary American left's application of the "fascist" label to
Trump-era conservatism is a Freudian "transference" — a "big lie" that inverts
perpetrator and victim. The book is a sequel in method to Jonah Goldberg's *Liberal
Fascism* (2008) but more aggressively partisan and campaign-oriented.

**Why it is in the wiki.** As the fullest recent statement of the "fascism is
left-wing" thesis — a position with roots in Hayek and Flynn and points of contact
with Sternhell's and De Felice's scholarship on fascism's socialist origins, but
rejected by mainstream academic fascism studies. Because the thesis is contested,
its claims are filed per the Contradiction Protocol as an attributed position at
[[fascism-and-the-political-left]] rather than merged into period/event/actor pages;
the serious comparative question underneath is at [[communism-and-fascism]]. Its
opening treatment of Hitler's *Mein Kampf* passage also occasions the concept page
[[big-lie-propaganda]].

## Argument Summary

**Chs. 1–2 (the transference thesis).** The book's frame is Freudian "transference":
the left, D'Souza argues, accuses Trump-era conservatism of the fascism it itself
embodies. He invokes Hitler's *Mein Kampf* "big lie" passage (see
[[big-lie-propaganda]]) and a "double lie" structure — denying both that Trump is
fascist and that the alleged fascist traits (reaction, capitalism, racism) belong to
fascism at all. He redefines the American right as classical liberalism and "left" as
statism, the definitional move on which everything else depends. Real scholars
(Paxton, Payne, Evans, Sternhell, De Felice, Gregor) are quoted selectively; Paxton
is accused of "knowingly participating in the big lie." Giovanni Gentile is elevated
to "the Karl Marx of Fascism," his obscurity presented as deliberate left-wing
suppression; the 1919 Fascist and 1920 NSDAP platforms are juxtaposed with the 2016
Democratic platform.

**Chs. 3–4 (genealogy).** Fascism is derived from the "crisis of Marxism": Mussolini's
socialist career, Sorel's revolutionary myth, and socialist-to-fascist converts
(Valois, Déat, Doriot, Mosley) are read as continuity rather than rupture — "not a
conversion but a maturation." Ch. 4 argues Hitler's *Lebensraum* was modeled on
Jacksonian Indian removal (stretching qualified claims by Toland, Rich, and Snyder
into "Nazi DNA was in the Democratic Party from the very beginning") and extends
Stanley Elkins's 1959 plantation/concentration-camp comparison into present-day
claims about Black urban pathology.

**Chs. 5–6 (American race politics).** Uses James Q. Whitman's *Hitler's American
Model* documentation of Nazi jurists studying U.S. race law (the 5 June 1934 Justice
Ministry meeting) while accusing Whitman and Katznelson of a "big lie" for blaming
"America" rather than "the Democratic Party" — the party-substitution device that
ignores twentieth-century realignment. Ch. 6 covers Progressive-era eugenics
(Indiana 1907, Davenport, Laughlin, Grant, Sanger) and its documented links to Nazi
sterilization law, then escalates to equating Planned Parenthood with the Kaiser
Wilhelm Institute and legal abortion with "genocide on a scale that exceeds the Nazi
Holocaust." Hofstadter's *Social Darwinism in America* is cast as a deliberate
postwar cover-up.

**Chs. 7–9 (present-day application).** FDR's New Deal is presented as American
fascism (Tugwell's and McCormick's Mussolini enthusiasms; "Ecco un dittatore!");
Wilson as "proto-fascist"; the Frankfurt School (Adorno's F-Scale, Marcuse's
"Repressive Tolerance") as the postwar rebranding operation that pinned fascism on
the right; campus incidents of 2017, antifa, and George Soros as latter-day
brownshirt apparatus. Ch. 9 abandons historical argument for a prescriptive
"denazification" program — one-party Republican dominance, National Guard deployment
against universities, "lawful physical force" against antifa — polemical advocacy,
not historiography.

## Volume Assessment

**Strengths.** The book's foundation — that the American right is Lockean
classical liberalism, categorically distinct from the European monarchist/
reactionary right on which the fascism label was minted — is well grounded
(cf. Hartz, *The Liberal Tradition in America*). Its documented material is
extensive and largely real: Mussolini's Marxist career and the
socialist-to-fascist converts, the Sorelian pipeline, Gentile's statist
philosophy, Progressive eugenics and its Nazi reception, Democratic authorship
of slavery and Jim Crow, the 1934 Nuremberg-Laws drafting meeting (via
Whitman), 1930s American elite enthusiasm for Mussolini. It engages the
academic literature by name rather than ignoring it.

**Weaknesses noted at extraction.** Academic fascism studies rejects the
book's central genealogy, and several specific evidentiary points are shaky by
D'Souza's own admission or by provenance: the Wilson "history with lightning"
quote (he concedes it lacks archival corroboration), the Sanger-KKK speech and
Hitler–Madison Grant "Bible" claims (disputed sourcing), the J20 arrest
figures (most prosecutions later collapsed). Critics also press the party-
realignment objection against the Democratic-continuity thesis; the book
answers with the *Hillary's America* argument that realignment is itself
overstated. Ch. 9 is prescriptive political advocacy rather than history and
is not used by the wiki.

**Handling.** Contested interpretive claims from this source are cited as
attributed positions via [[fascism-and-the-political-left]], not in wiki
voice; its documented factual material may inform pages where independently
corroborated.
