# The Prehistoric World; or, Vanished Races

**Author:** Emory Adams Allen. Central Publishing House, Cincinnati, 1885. ~600 pages, heavily illustrated. Popular synthesis. (Read from Project Gutenberg e-text #2873.)

## Book Note

A best-selling Gilded-Age survey of "prehistoric times" in sixteen chapters, moving from the geological framing of deep time and Palaeolithic Europe (river-drift men, cave-men) through the Neolithic, Bronze, and Iron Ages of Europe, then to the Americas (early man, the Mound Builders, the Pueblo country, the Nahua and Maya, and ancient Peru). Allen was a popularizer, not a field archaeologist, but he had several of the leading American prehistorians review individual chapters, so the book transmits mainstream professional opinion of the mid-1880s to a mass audience rather than fringe speculation.

The book is ingested **only for its historiographical content**. Its substantive claims about the past are obsolete and in places simply wrong, but as a document of *how prehistory was conceived* it is unusually clear and explicit. Three features make it useful to the wiki:

1. **The deep-time / antiquity-of-man revolution as living controversy.** Allen's introduction is an extended apologia for the then-recent (he says "hardly fifty years") scientific extension of human antiquity far beyond the biblical ~6,000 years, framed as a battle against scriptural chronology comparable to the earlier reception of heliocentric astronomy. See [[antiquity-of-man|the antiquity-of-man debate]].

2. **The three-age system in popular form.** The European chapters organize the past as Stone → Bronze → Iron exactly on the Thomsen/Lubbock model, complete with the Palaeolithic/Neolithic split (Lubbock's coinage) and the assumption of a long, possibly unbridged gap between Palaeolithic and Neolithic. See [[three-age-system|Three Age System]].

3. **The Mound Builder question at its turning point.** Allen's American chapters sit squarely in the [[mound-builder-myth|Mound Builder controversy]]. Notably, he is *transitional*: he repeatedly concedes that the works are "hard to distinguish from the Indians," cites Iroquois mound-burial and Natchez/Pueblo parallels, and entertains continuity with living tribes — even while preserving the romantic "vanished people" framing of his subtitle.

## Claims Extracted (grounding quotes)

All quotes are from Project Gutenberg e-text #2873 (line numbers from the converted plain-text file used in ingestion).

### Deep time and the antiquity of man

- The whole enterprise is framed as recovering history from non-textual evidence: "we use this term [prehistoric] in the same sense as Dr. Wilson uses it: that is, to express the whole period disclosed to us by means of archæological evidence, as distinguished from what is known through historical records." (range ~2070)
- On the recency and contested status of human deep time: "Hardly fifty years have passed since scientific men began to attribute to the human race an antiquity more remote than that assigned them by history and tradition. At first these views met with general opposition, much as did the theory of the present system of astronomy when it was first proclaimed." (range ~2044)
- On reconciliation with scripture: "the researches of some of the most eminent men and learned divines have amply shown, that there are no data given in the Scriptures on which to base an estimate as to the antiquity of man." (range ~2056)
- Geology and archaeology as the twin "sister sciences" that unlocked the past, with the nebular hypothesis of Laplace supplying the cosmological frame. (range ~2026, ~2062)

### The three-age framing

- The book's European chapter sequence is itself the evidence: Early Geological Periods → Men of the River Drift (Palaeolithic) → Cave-Men → Antiquity of the Paleolithic Age → Neolithic Age in Europe → Bronze Age in Europe → Iron Age in Europe (chapters II–VIII).
- Stadial/progressivist thesis stated as the book's purpose: to show "that man has lived in the world an extremely long time... that his first state was very low and rude — that he has risen to his present high estate by means of his own exertions continued through long ages." (range ~2070)

### The Mound Builders

- Definition by ignorance: "We know them only by reason of their remains, and as these principally are mounds, we call them the 'Mound Builders.'" (range ~2130)
- The romantic "vanished superior race" reflex: "The Indians of the prairies seem to be intruders on a fairer civilization... In the presence of their imposing ruins, we can not think that nomadic savages built them." (range ~2032)
- But the crucial transitional concession (chapter heading, ch. X): "Hard to distinguish them from the Indians." And in the text: "Recent investigation and historical evidence unite in showing that some comparatively recent Indian tribes formed and used mound structures," with the Iroquois eight-to-ten-year bone-mound custom cited. (range ~2164)
- Continuity hypotheses entertained: Natchez "possibly a remnant of the Mound Builders," and "lines of resemblance between the Pueblo Tribes and the Mound Builders" (ch. XI heading, range ~349 TOC).
- [[cahokia|Cahokia]] singled out as "the most stupendous example of mound builders' work in this country," in the fertile "Great American Bottom." (range ~2226)

## Reliability and Use

Treat every factual assertion as period opinion, not evidence. Specific obsolete claims to flag if encountered elsewhere: the Calaveras skull and Pliocene/Tertiary "Table Mountain" Californians (range ~313, long since exposed as a hoax/intrusive); a sunken Pacific continent explaining Polynesian remains (range ~2038); and the unilineal savagery→civilization scale throughout. The book's worth is as a citable witness for [[antiquity-of-man]], [[three-age-system]], and [[mound-builder-myth]], and as colour for the broader [[pre-columbian-american-population|pre-Columbian population debate]].
