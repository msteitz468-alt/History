# The American Way of War: A History of United States Military Strategy and Policy

**Russell F. Weigley** (1973, Macmillan; part of "The Wars of the United States" series). The foundational modern study of the distinctive pattern in American military strategy from the Revolution through the Vietnam era. Weigley argues that the United States, blessed with resources and lacking a continuous tradition of limited war or European-style balance-of-power politics, tended to wage war for complete military victory — the destruction of the enemy's armed forces and will to resist — rather than for limited political objectives through attrition or maneuver.

The book contrasts an early "strategy of attrition" (Washington, Greene, limited resources era) with the emergence and dominance of a "strategy of annihilation" (U.S. Grant in the Civil War, carried into WWII), while tracing the intellectual architects (Mahan for sea power, Mitchell for air power) and the perplexities of the nuclear age.

### Range1-Washington Attrition Strategy Shaped by Military Poverty
Washington’s generalship was a strategy of attrition (or “erosion”) compelled by the Continental Army’s consistent inferiority in numbers, training, discipline, equipment, and naval support. He interpreted American weakness as requiring a primarily strategic defensive focused on preserving the army’s existence rather than defending territory or seeking decisive battle; British sea power made geographic defense impossible, so the army itself became the indispensable asset whose survival could outlast British political will. Active elements were limited to intelligence-driven raids on detachments and outposts (Trenton, Princeton, Stony Point) that maintained morale and stimulated recruiting without risking the main force. Washington adhered rigorously to the principle of concentration/mass and avoided general actions against the main British army except when politically unavoidable (e.g., Brandywine to defend Philadelphia).

**Grounding quote:** “Washington’s was a generalship shaped by military poverty. ... the strategy of the American armies in the Revolutionary War had to be a strategy founded upon weakness. ... His ultimate object as Commander in Chief must be to remove the British armies altogether from the insurgent provinces; ... Washington believed that his military method must be that of a strategic defensive.” (Part One, Ch. 1 on Washington; also “It has even been called a War of Posts. That we should on all Occasions avoid a general Action...” — Washington letter quoted early in Ch. 1.)

### Range1-Greene Partisan War Violates Concentration to Exploit Irregulars
Nathanael Greene developed a strategy of partisan war that deliberately violated Washington’s cherished principle of concentration. He divided his already weak field force (e.g., dispatching Morgan west and Lee/Marion east) to subsist more easily, tempt the British into similar dispersion, and coordinate with southern partisan bands (Marion, Sumter) that harassed supply lines and outposts. This created opportunities for his army to strike detachments (Cowpens) while partisans prevented British concentration. Greene explicitly described running to exhaust the enemy: “We fight, get beat, rise and fight again.”

**Grounding quote:** “Greene was not only depending upon the partisan bands to help protect him from disaster but exploiting their presence to move toward a strategy of unconventional war, in which he would set orthodox military dicta such as the principle of concentration on their heads. ... ‘I have been obliged to practice that by finesse which I dared not attempt by force,’ said Greene... ‘W e fight, get beat, rise and fight again.’” (Ch. 2 on Greene; also “You see that we must again resume the partisan war.” — Greene.)

### Range1-Greene Exhaustion of Cornwallis via Pursuit and Partisans
Greene’s most distinctive operational art was using superior mobility, partisans, and terrain to lead Cornwallis on exhausting chases (notably the race to the Dan River after Cowpens). By denying the British provisions and forcing continuous marches in bad weather, Greene ensured that even tactical defeats (Guilford Court House, Hobkirk’s Hill, Eutaw Springs) left the British too weakened to hold the interior. Partisans stripped the countryside behind the British while Greene’s army recovered in friendly districts; this reversed the British southern conquest without needing to destroy their main field force outright.

**Grounding quote:** “Greene determined to lead Cornwallis on as long a chase as possible, northward across the Carolinas... until Cornwallis would regret the destruction of his stores and without them, and with the partisans biting at his heels... would exhaust himself. ... When Greene learned that Cornwallis had burned his stores, he is supposed to have exclaimed, ‘Then, he is ours!’” (Ch. 2 on Greene; “The Americans would be less dangerous if they had a regular army.” — British observation.)

### Range1-British Southern Strategy Failures: Loyalism and Place-Occupation
British strategy in the South relied on finding reservoirs of Loyalist support and occupying places to restore royal authority, but these assumptions proved illusory. Dispersal into outposts invited partisan raids; reprisals and policies forcing choices between rebellion and loyalty (ending paroles, church hostility) re-ignited resistance. Even after major successes (Charleston, Camden), the British could not convert occupation into lasting pacification because the countryside remained under partisan control and no decisive destruction of Greene’s army was achieved before exhaustion set in.

**Grounding quote:** “the British from beginning to end hoped that by occupying some place, sooner or later they would find themselves welcomed and surrounded by Loyalists... Whatever the strength of loyalism might have been... there was never enough of it... The British and Loyalists responded with reprisals which provoked further restlessness.” (Ch. 2 on Greene; also Burgoyne/Saratoga discussion showing similar overextension and harassment failures.)

### Range1-Federalist vs Jeffersonian Naval and Defensive Conceptions
Federalists (Washington/Adams administrations, Stoddert) pursued a stronger navy including ships of the line (proposals for twelve 74-gun vessels) to deter invasion by exploiting transatlantic distance and to protect commerce, alongside coastal fortifications. Jeffersonians emphasized economy, suspicion of standing forces and navies, and a purely defensive system of gunboats (hundreds authorized) plus militia for harbor defense, explicitly rejecting offensive capability: “this species of naval armament is proposed merely for defensive operations.”

**Grounding quote:** “Stoddert recommended the building of twelve seventy-four-gun ships of the line. ... ‘When the United States shall own twelve ships of seventy-four guns,’ he said, ‘... confidence may be indulged that we may then avoid those wars...’” vs. Jefferson: “It must be superfluous to observe that this species of naval armament is proposed merely for defensive operations; it could not ‘become an incitement to engage in offensive maritime war...’” (Ch. 3 The Federalists and the Jeffersonians).

### Range1-War of 1812 Strategic Mismatch: Offensive Aims, Defensive Means, No Design
Political goals were offensive (compel Britain to respect neutral rights or conquer Canada to remove the rival power from North America), yet military policy remained defensive and passive (gunboats, militia reliance, forts). No coherent strategy existed; efforts scattered across the frontier rather than concentrating on the vital Montréal/St. Lawrence objective. Privateers inflicted real damage but operated without central direction. Successes (Perry on Erie, Plattsburg, New Orleans, Jackson) were local tactical improvisations.

**Grounding quote:** “the political goals of the Jeffersonian leaders... were incongruously offensive. ... There was no strategy for the war beyond general agreement... that Canada ought to be attacked. ... the Americans continued to dissipate its already necessarily limited offensive efforts in scattered sideshows...” (Ch. 3; also “the United States under the most modest interpretation of its purposes thus went to war intending to attack Canada, an offensive goal.”).

### Range1-Post-1812 Maritime Defense System: Board of Engineers Forts + Navy
After 1815, Congress and the Board of Engineers (including Bernard, Totten) formalized a system in which the Navy was first reliance, but coastal fortifications (system of batteries, masonry works from Maine to New Orleans) would protect naval bases, deny invaders secure harbors, reduce the small army’s dispersal problem, and support commerce protection. The theory held that strong forts would always repulse naval attacks and allow the fleet to operate freely; construction proceeded (slowly) while the Navy shifted to smaller vessels for commerce protection and anti-piracy.

**Grounding quote:** “the report of the Board of Engineers... became the basic statement of a strategy of national maritime defense... The first reliance of the United States against attack from overseas must be upon the Navy. ... [fortifications] would be essential for the support of the Navy itself... The enemy would be able to get no foothold on a sheltered harbor...” (Ch. 4 The Age of Winfield Scott; echoes earlier 1794 measures).

### Range1-Scott’s Limited, 18th-Century-Style Maneuver and Economy in Mexico
Winfield Scott, the preeminent strategist of the era, conducted the Veracruz-to-Mexico City campaign in the style of limited eighteenth-century war: strict regard for civilian rights and property, purchase (not requisition) of supplies, preference for siege and flanking maneuver over costly assaults, and avoidance of all-out battle to minimize casualties and avoid inflaming Mexican resistance. He aimed at political paralysis of the capital rather than destruction of the Mexican army, accepting armistices when advantageous and using amphibious innovation (purpose-built landing craft) only as means to limited ends. He remained “an old-fashioned general of the eighteenth-century mold.”

**Grounding quote:** “Scott eschewed the sort of costly assault... preferring instead to save lives through a formal siege of the eighteenth-century style... Throughout his campaign, Scott avoided all-out battle whenever it was possible to do so and husbanded lives by substituting maneuver for combat with all the frugality of a general of the ancien régime... ‘Too much blood has already been shed in this unnatural war...’” (Ch. 4 The Age of Winfield Scott; also contrast with Taylor’s bludgeon approach and later Civil War revelation of Scott as “a museum piece”).

### Range1-Emergence of Professional Strategic Study via West Point, Mahan, and Jomini (Early Scott Era)
The War of 1812 exposed the lack of strategic education. Postwar professionalization at West Point (Thayer superintendency from 1817, Mahan faculty from 1832 after French study) introduced systematic study of war as science and art (Halleck: strategy, fortification/engineering, logistics, tactics; plus policy of war). Jomini (via O’Connor translation and Mahan) became the principal interpreter of Napoleonic strategy to Americans; Halleck’s 1846 *Elements of Military Art and Science* defined strategy as “the art of directing masses on decisive points” and endorsed the offensive while applying Jominian concentration and interior-lines ideas, tempered by defensive realities for the United States.

**Grounding quote:** “Halleck... owed his ideas on strategy mainly to Jomini... ‘Strategy’ he said, ‘is defined to be the art of directing masses on decisive points, or the hostile movements of armies beyond the range of each other’s cannon.’ ... ‘The first and most important rule in offensive war is, to keep your forces as much concentrated as possible.’” (Early Ch. on Mahan/Halleck, following Scott discussion; also “the greatest military need... was an education in the principles of strategy for the American officer corps.” — end of Ch. 3.)

### Range2-Lee's Offensive-Defensive as Napoleonic Strategy for the Confederacy
In the American Civil War, Robert E. Lee concluded that a purely passive defensive strategy modeled on George Washington's was unsustainable for the Confederacy due to its extensive frontiers, states' rights pressures requiring dispersal of limited forces, and the Union's advantages in sea power and proximity. Lee therefore adopted an offensive-defensive approach: seizing the initiative at chosen points to force the Union to react, concentrating forces despite overall inferiority, and aiming to destroy enemy armies in climactic battles rather than merely defending territory or wearing down the opponent. This shifted Confederate strategy in Virginia from President Jefferson Davis's preferred frugal defense toward Napoleonic goals of decisive battlefield annihilation, even as the Confederacy lacked the manpower to sustain the resulting costs. The approach produced initial successes such as the relief of Richmond but repeatedly exhausted Lee's own army.

**Grounding quote:** “It is only by concentration of our troops... that we can hope to win any decisive advantage.” “... we must decide between the positive loss of inactivity and the risk of action.” Lee resolved upon the risk of action, determining to attack McClellan despite his inferiority in numbers... he hoped wrecking that wing and compelling McClellan either to shift all his forces north of the Chickahominy... or to withdraw everything south... Either defensive reaction would force McClellan into awkward crossings... (Napoleonic Strategy: R. E. Lee and the Confederacy; around lines 830–860 in cache)

### Range2-Scott's Anaconda Policy and the Clash with Popular Napoleonic Expectations
At the outbreak of the American Civil War, Winfield Scott, as Commanding General, proposed an Anaconda policy: a gigantic siege using Union naval power to blockade Southern ports, strangle the Confederate economy (cotton exports and imports of war material), and gradually dismember the Confederacy via deliberate approaches along rivers. This remained consistent with his pre-Napoleonic, cautious conception of war from the Mexican War. However, Northern public impatience—fueled by the image of brief, climactic Napoleonic battles—forced Scott to yield to the “On to Richmond” clamor, leading to the premature advance that became First Bull Run (Manassas). The episode illustrated the tension between limited, attritional or political strategies and the popular demand for swift, decisive annihilation-style victories.

**Grounding quote:** “He proposed to subject the Southern Confederacy to a gigantic siege, employing Union naval power to blockade the Southern ports and gradually to strangle the Confederate economy... On land, he proposed to delay major actions until superior armies could be equipped and trained, whereupon he would have conducted a war of regular, deliberate approaches...” The newspapers soon labeled it, with a hint of derision, the “Anaconda policy.” (Napoleonic Strategy: R. E. Lee and the Confederacy; early in range section on Scott)

### Range2-Mahan and Halleck's Cautious Foundations of American Strategic Studies
The earliest systematic American strategic writing, by Henry Wager Halleck and his mentor Dennis Mahan (chair of the “Napoleon Club” at West Point), remained fundamentally cautious and only ambiguously Napoleonic despite deference to the emperor. Halleck shrank from deep invasion, emphasized concentration over celerity inside enemy territory, warned of the dilemmas of making war feed war (provoking resistance or failing to subdue), and diluted offensive prescriptions. Mahan endorsed celerity and romanticized the “climactic Napoleonic battle of annihilation” with descriptions of artillery preparation, impetuous columns, and sweeping cavalry, yet devoted much of his *Out-Post* treatise to prudent reconnaissance, screening, and defensive works. Both ultimately aligned more with Jominian limited war for territorial objectives (as in Scott's Mexico campaign) than with Clausewitzian overthrow of enemy armies. They remained “in harmony with the cautious, un-Napoleonic approach to war exemplified earlier by George Washington and in Dennis Mahan’s day by Winfield Scott.”

**Grounding quote:** “To do the greatest damage to our enemy with the least exposure to ourselves... is a military axiom lost sight of only by ignorance to the true ends of victory.” “If an offensive strategy was to be adopted, it was by implication to be a strategy of attrition or a political strategy, not a strategy of annihilation.” (The Founding of American Strategic Studies; lines ~90–115 in cache)

### Range2-Impact of Rifled Weapons on Napoleonic Tactics and the Persistence of the Legend
The American Civil War demonstrated that the rifled musket (adopted as the .58-caliber Springfield) and rifled artillery destroyed the conditions that had made Napoleonic tactics decisive. Defending infantry could now deliver multiple effective shots at 400–600 yards, rendering close-up artillery preparation impossible and exposing attacking infantry to prolonged, devastating fire. Earthworks further neutralized artillery. Despite this, “the fascination of Napoleon and the Napoleonic battle still gripped the military imagination,” leading commanders (especially on the Confederate side) to persist in close-order frontal assaults that extracted horrific casualties without achieving the destruction of enemy armies in a single blow. This technological shift made true Napoleonic annihilation battles largely illusory.

**Grounding quote:** “With Civil War rifles... each defending soldier could get off multiple shots against an advancing adversary... So destructive did rifled muskets and cannons prove themselves to be against attacking infantry... that attackers could win battlefield decisions if at all only through immense sacrifices of their own manpower. Yet the fascination of Napoleon and the Napoleonic battle still gripped the military imagination.” (The Founding of American Strategic Studies / transition to Napoleonic Strategy; lines ~210–235 in cache)

### Range2-Lee's Seven Days and Second Manassas as Attempts at Annihilation
Upon taking command of the Army of Northern Virginia, Lee seized the initiative against McClellan on the Peninsula, concentrating north of the Chickahominy to wreck the Federal right wing and force awkward crossings or a base shift to the James, with the explicit hope of an “Austerlitz battle which would effect a strategy of annihilation.” Coordination failures (especially Jackson's) led to bloody repulses at Mechanicsville and Gaines's Mill, followed by further costly assaults at Glendale and Malvern Hill. At Second Manassas, Lee achieved a closer approximation of Napoleonic method—fixing Pope with Jackson then routing him with Longstreet's flank attack—but still at high cost (19% casualties in killed/wounded) without destroying the Federal army. These campaigns broke the immediate Federal threat to Richmond but exemplified the manpower price of the offensive-defensive.

**Grounding quote:** “While McClellan was entangled in the Chickahominy crossings... Lee hoped for nothing less than to destroy the Federal army: to find the opportunity for an Austerlitz battle which would effect a strategy of annihilation.” “Under ordinary circumstances the Federal army should have been destroyed.” (Napoleonic Strategy: R. E. Lee and the Confederacy; lines ~1180–1270 and ~1315–1325 in cache)

### Range2-Lee's Gettysburg Campaign and the Limits of Napoleonic Strategy
After Antietam and Chancellorsville, Lee invaded the North again, believing victories on Northern soil were required to break the Union will for recognition of Confederate independence. Despite skillful initial maneuver, the campaign culminated in three days of battle at Gettysburg. Lee attacked rather than forcing the Federals to do so, pressed frontal assaults (including Pickett's Charge) despite terrain disadvantages, ammunition concerns, and incomplete concentration, and suffered ~23,000 casualties. The offensive capacity of the Army of Northern Virginia was thereafter “virtually dead.” Lee mastered maneuver and the offensive-defensive in Virginia but was “too Napoleonic”: his passion for the strategy of annihilation and the climactic decisive battle ultimately destroyed his own army's capacity rather than the enemy's.

**Grounding quote:** “Lee counted on the prowess his troops had displayed so often in Virginia to win that battle, and with it perhaps the war... Lee himself attacked, and attacked again, through three days of battle.” “If I had had Stonewall Jackson with me, so far as man can see, I should have won the battle of Gettysburg.” “Like Napoleon himself, with his passion for the strategy of annihilation and the climactic, decisive battle as its expression, he destroyed in the end not the enemy armies, but his own.” (Napoleonic Strategy: R. E. Lee and the Confederacy; lines ~1640–1700 and ~2140–2145 in cache)

### Range2-Grant's Vicksburg Campaign as Emergence of Long-View Annihilation Practitioner
Ulysses S. Grant rose above the Napoleonic fixation on single decisive battles that characterized many Federal commanders in the East. In the Vicksburg campaign (autumn 1862–summer 1863), he pursued a consistent strategic goal—the opening of the Mississippi—while retaining multiple options, rising above reverses (e.g., Chickasaw Bluffs), cutting loose from his line of communications to live off the country, splitting Confederate forces, and imposing disproportionate losses through maneuver before the siege. He viewed battles as means, not ends, and achieved the territorial objective (plus large prisoner hauls) at relatively low cost. This demonstrated an ability to master the long series of events rather than seeking an immediate Austerlitz.

**Grounding quote:** “Grant showed himself free from the common fixation of his contemporaries upon the Napoleonic battle as the hinge upon which warfare must turn. Instead, he developed a highly uncommon ability to rise above the fortunes of a single battle and to master the flow of a long series of events...” In the active phase he “marched 180 miles, fought five battles, split the forces opposing them in two, clamped Vicksburg into a siege, and imposed over 7,000 casualties upon the enemy while suffering fewer than 4,500 themselves.” (A Strategy of Annihilation: U. S. Grant and the Union; lines ~2655–2690 in cache)

### Range2-Grant's 1864 Strategy of Annihilation: Continuous Pressure to Destroy Lee's Army
As Commanding General, Grant adopted a strategy of annihilation aimed at the literal destruction of the principal Confederate armies (Lee's Army of Northern Virginia and Johnston's Army of Tennessee) through concentration and mass. Unlike predecessors or Lee, he accepted that rifled firearms made single-battle decisiveness impossible and instead extended battle across the entire campaign: “Wherever Lee goes, there you will go also,” keeping the enemy army always within grip, fighting continuously, and using superior resources to outlast the opponent until the enemy army disintegrated. He coordinated simultaneous pressure all along the line while targeting both armies and supporting resources. The first month alone (Wilderness through Cold Harbor) cost the Army of the Potomac ~55,000 casualties while inflicting ~32,000 on Lee, shifting the war toward exhaustion but returning decision through prolonged pressure rather than dramatic victory.

**Grounding quote:** “Lee’s army... will be your objective point. Wherever Lee goes, there you will go also.” “The campaign now begun was destined to result in heavier losses, to both armies, in a given time, than any previously suffered; but the carnage was to be limited to a single year...” “It was better to fight him outside of his stronghold than in it.” (A Strategy of Annihilation: U. S. Grant and the Union; lines ~2835–2905 in cache)

### Range3-Grant-Style Campaigns as the Unavoidable Model for Decisive Land Warfare
In the aftermath of the World War, American military observers concluded that no superior alternative had emerged to the costly, frontal approach U.S. Grant employed against the Army of Northern Virginia in 1864-65. European armies in 1914-18, facing continuous fronts that precluded flanking, repeatedly fell back on variations of Grant’s battering tactics—repeating attacks like Cold Harbor in modernized form—to bleed opponents into exhaustion. Political demands for swift victory and the absence of better methods reinforced reliance on direct destruction of enemy field armies, even as rifled weapons, machine guns, and entrenchments made such assaults extraordinarily bloody. The result was mutual attrition rather than decisive maneuver, confirming for many that Grant’s method, however repellent, remained the practical path to victory against a determined continental adversary when maneuver alone failed.

This legacy shaped interwar American doctrine, which viewed the destruction of enemy armies through mass and fighting as essential. Industrial productivity enabled sustained mass armies and prolonged conflict, making pure economic pressure (such as Mahanian blockade) too slow against large continental powers and elevating army destruction as the only route to acceptable timelines of victory.

**Grounding quote:** "To accomplish their defeat, no one had yet, unhappily, developed a method better than Grant’s against the Army of Northern Virginia. ... O r rather, since the habit of digging into the earth begun by Lee’s and G rant’s armies had now become universal, the belligerents alternated in repeating Grant’s attack at Cold Harbor." (Strategy and the Great War of 1914-1918 section, around cache lines 697 and 725)

### Range3-Indian Wars Legacy and Army Isolation from Civilian America
Before 1917 the U.S. Army interpreted strategy almost wholly in narrow military terms and maintained its posts and outlook in considerable physical and intellectual isolation from the broader civilian society. This stemmed directly from its long experience as “Indian fighters” on frontier posts inherited from the Indian wars era. ### Range3-Sherman Strategy of Economic and Popular Targeting as Model for Expanded War

### Range3-Grantian Convictions Reaffirmed: Destruction of Enemy Armed Forces as the Objective of Warfare

### Range3-Annihilation vs. Attrition Controversy: Preference for Annihilation When Forces Suffice

### Range3-Industrial Revolution, Total War, and the Necessity of Army Destruction

### Range3-Mahanian Sea Power Dilemmas and the Shift Toward Broader American World Power Instruments

### Range5-MacArthur's Reapplication of Pacific War Amphibious Envelopment at Inchon
Douglas MacArthur drew directly on his experience from the Pacific campaigns of 1941-45, particularly amphibious end runs begun in New Guinea, to plan the Inchon landing as a deep envelopment. He viewed a force landed at Inchon as an anvil against which the Eighth Army would break the North Korean army in a battle of annihilation, severing main supply routes through Seoul while the Pusan perimeter forces attacked from the south. ### Range5-Fundamental Limits of Sea and Air Power Isolation on a Peninsula Versus Pacific Islands
The Korean peninsula's connection to the Asian mainland exposed the limits of reapplying Pacific War (1941-45) methods of isolation by sea and air power. In the Pacific, islands could be cut off completely from assistance by naval blockade and air power, enabling decisive victories at relatively low cost. ### Range5-Civilian Strategy Intellectuals' Critique of Massive Retaliation and Call for Limited War to Restore Policy-Power Linkage
Civilian strategists (Osgood, Kissinger, Brodie, Kaufmann) attacked the Dulles doctrine as abandoning combat as an instrument of policy and leaving no options between total war and stalemate. Osgood argued that "the decisive limitation upon war is the limitation of the objectives of war" and that "power and policy must be harnessed together"; limiting aims was key to keeping wars limited and making force a rational tool. Kissinger traced American "theory of war based on the necessity of total victory" to history and warned that renouncing lesser uses of force placed the international order at the mercy of the most ruthless actor. They advocated graduated deterrent systems, tactical nuclear options (with caveats), and capacities for limited war to deter and respond to peripheral Communist advances without automatic escalation or surrender of initiative. Korea illustrated the improvisational costs of lacking such capacities.

**Grounding quote:** "The real strategic question facing the nation has never been whether or not to adhere to containment ... but rather by what means to implement containment so as to be able to avoid total wars, to keep wars limited, and to fight limited wars successfully." ### Range5-Wohlstetter's "Delicate Balance of Terror" and the Instability of Vulnerable Retaliatory Forces
Albert Wohlstetter demonstrated that the nuclear balance was far less stable than the "two scorpions in a bottle" image suggested. Small technical or numerical edges could create preemptive incentives because the American SAC bomber force (and early missiles) was highly vulnerable on the ground to blast effects. Airborne alert averaged only ~4% of the force; hardening, dispersal, and other measures offered limited assurance against improving Soviet capabilities. A Soviet first strike could cripple the retaliatory force, tempting preemption or leaving the United States dependent on it. This analysis reinforced critics of "sufficient" or finite deterrence and pushed toward second-strike invulnerability (hardened/mobile forces) and counterforce thinking. It directly challenged complacent reliance on massive retaliation.

**Grounding quote:** "the nuclear balance was unstable enough ... that relatively small technical advances of one power over the other could bring disproportionate strategic advantages. One of the nuclear powers consequently might exploit such an advantage in a preemptive strike..." ... "what counts in basic deterrence is not so much the size and efficiency of one’s striking force before it is hit as the size and condition to which the enemy thinks he can reduce it by a surprise attack." (Strategies of Deterrence and of Action section, Wohlstetter discussion and Brodie response)

**Links/taxonomy:** contrasts_with: assumptions underlying [[deterrence]] in massive retaliation; contributed_to: debates over vulnerability, second-strike, and counterforce.

### Range5-Morgenstern's Oceanic System: Sea Power for Invulnerable Second-Strike Deterrent
Oskar Morgenstern argued that land-based forces remained too vulnerable to preemption and proposed an "Oceanic System" leveraging American access to the seas. Nuclear submarines in random movement (Polaris program) and nuclear-powered seaplanes could provide highly invulnerable retaliatory capacity without fixed bases the enemy could target. This would be cheaper and more effective than hardening against ever-more-powerful Soviet missiles; land-locked Russia would struggle to match maritime flexibility. It represented a modern application of sea power principles to ensure a secure deterrent force capable of riding out a first strike, restoring some initiative through technological choice that imposed asymmetric burdens on the opponent.

**Grounding quote:** "movable bases at sea would be more secure than movable bases on land ... Movable retaliatory bases under the sea would be still more invulnerable ... Therefore Morgenstern recommended a new application of sea power: to use sea power to ensure an invulnerable capacity for nuclear retaliation against the enemy’s homeland." ... "It is good policy to choose that weapon whose anti-weapon imposes the greatest possible strain on the production facilities and military efforts of the opponent." (Strategies of Deterrence and of Action section)

**Links/taxonomy:** contributed_to: [[deterrence]] (invulnerable second-strike); analogous_to: traditional sea power advantages; part_of: post-Sputnik responses.

### Range5-Reversion to Annihilation Strategy in Vietnam Despite Limited-War Theory and "New Strategy" Rhetoric
Despite Kennedy-era emphasis on flexible response, counterinsurgency, and a "whole new kind of strategy" for wars of national liberation (Special Forces/Green Berets, nation-building), American conduct in Vietnam rapidly reverted to historic patterns. The enclave approach gave way to search-and-destroy aimed at destroying enemy forces and base areas. Aerial campaigns against the North applied World War II analogies (e.g., sustained POL attacks on Germany) despite intelligence that North Vietnam's primitive economy and external supply lines made such interdiction/bombing unlikely to compel Hanoi. Westmoreland's three-phase plan explicitly sought "the defeat and destruction of the remaining enemy forces and base areas." Limited-war theorizing had adjusted Army tactics but not its core strategic tradition of seeking decision through destruction of the enemy's armed forces. The result was massive escalation (hundreds of thousands of troops) without decisive results against an elusive enemy.

**Grounding quote:** "the history of American military strategy had given no place to static defense ... if the means for a more active campaign existed; this fact goes far to explain the shift ..." ... "to destroy enemy forces," to invoke again the old strategy of annihilation. ... "If the war had been conducted with 'a whole new kind of strategy' of counterinsurrectionary war, instead of with old strategies seeking the destruction of the enemy and his logistical systems by means of highly mechanized forces that had a hard time hitting him, conceivably the war might have gone better." (Strategies of Action Attempted: To the Vietnam War section)

## Volume Synthesis Note
Russell F. Weigley’s *The American Way of War* (1973) is the definitive intellectual and institutional history of U.S. military strategy from the Revolution through Vietnam. Its central thesis—that abundant resources and a democratic political culture produced a persistent preference for “strategy of annihilation” (complete destruction of the enemy’s armed forces and will, exemplified by Grant in 1864–65 and carried into WWII unconditional-surrender campaigns) over strategies of attrition, limited political objectives, or prolonged maneuver—structures the book’s five parts.

Part One establishes the early pattern under resource constraints: Washington’s strategic defensive/attrition to preserve the army, Greene’s innovative partisan exhaustion, and the mismatch of offensive political aims with defensive military means (Jeffersonian gunboats vs. Federalist cruisers; War of 1812). Part Two traces professionalization (Mahan/Halleck/Jomini at West Point) and the Civil War turning point: Lee’s costly Napoleonic offensive-defensive versus Grant’s acceptance of rifled-weapon realities and substitution of continuous, simultaneous pressure for single-battle decisiveness. Part Three follows the rise of sea power (Mahan/Luce), air power (Mitchell), and interwar planning. Part Four shows the traditions applied in WWII: Mahanian fleet destruction + economic strangulation in the Pacific; Grantian mass/concentration for army destruction in Europe. Part Five examines the nuclear-era “perplexity”: atomic revolution, Korea’s exposure of limits on annihilation methods and island-isolation techniques when applied to peninsulas/mainland, civilian strategy intellectuals’ (Brodie, Wohlstetter, Schelling, Osgood, Kissinger) critiques of massive retaliation, debates over deterrence stability and limited war, and Vietnam’s reversion to search-and-destroy despite new COIN rhetoric.

Weigley’s work supplies the strategic-culture backbone for the ACW hub (linking Grant’s methods directly to the 1864–65 campaigns already analyzed tactically) and broader strategy hub. It complements *How the North Won* (operational/logistical “how”) by explaining the deeper intellectual drivers and long-term continuities. The annihilation–attrition distinction enriches [[concepts/annihilation-and-exhaustion]] and [[concepts/american-way-of-war]].
