# Abolition: A History of Slavery and Antislavery (Drescher, 2009)

The capstone synthesis of Seymour Drescher's work on the rise and fall of slavery — a global,
comparative history that frames abolition not as an inevitable moral progress but as one phase in a
recurring **cycle** of enslavement and emancipation.

## What it contributes

- Deepens **[[abolition-of-slavery]]** with Drescher's **four-phase cyclical** framing and the
  **"reversion"** argument — that the twentieth century saw coerced labour return on a vast scale,
  so abolition was not an irreversible linear triumph.
- **[[williams-thesis-abolition-debate]]** — a new controversy page promoting the Williams-vs-Drescher
  dispute (did slavery end because it had ceased to pay, or against economic interest?) from inline
  text to a dedicated page.

## The two core theses

1. **Econocide (against Williams).** The British West Indian slave economy was **thriving** at
   abolition — its share of British trade and its output rising, not falling. Abolition therefore
   *destroyed* valuable property ("econocide") and cannot be explained as the shedding of a
   declining sector.
2. **Popular mobilisation.** What made British abolition possible was an **unprecedented mass
   movement** — petitions, boycotts, public opinion — the first sustained humanitarian
   mobilisation of a national public around the suffering of distant strangers.

## Reading note

Not a gap region and the abolition narrative is well-trodden, but Drescher's framing is
distinctive; written from his well-known theses plus the volume's confirmed four-part structure,
