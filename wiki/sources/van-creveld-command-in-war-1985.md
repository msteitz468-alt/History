# Command in War (Martin van Creveld, 1985)

A seminal study of Command, Control, and Communications (C3) systems throughout military history. Van Creveld defines **command** as a perpetual quest for certainty—specifically, information about the enemy, the environment, and one's own forces. As armies grow larger, move faster, and employ more specialized weapons, the information required to run them expands geometrically. 

Van Creveld argues that the history of command is a race between the growing demand for information and the capacity of command systems to meet it. Technological tools (telegraph, radio, computers) are not cures in themselves; rather, they can act as both the cure and the disease by increasing specialization and organizational complexity. Successful command systems manage uncertainty not by trying to enforce absolute centralized certainty (which leads to information overload), but by structuring the organization to operate on less information through decentralization, trust, and junior initiative.

## Volume Synthesis

1. **The Nature of Command and Uncertainty**: Command is a goal-oriented process designed to coordinate armies to execute missions under the pressure of time, danger, and incomplete information. The core problem of command is **managing uncertainty**. Confronted with a gap between the information required to act and the information available, a command system has two choices:
   - **Increase information-processing capacity**: Attempt to centralize all data at headquarters to issue detailed, centralized directives (*Befehlstaktik*). This typically leads to information overload, high decision thresholds, delay, and extreme system vulnerability.
   - **Increase tolerance for uncertainty**: Structure the organization to operate on less information by decentralizing decision-making (*Auftragstaktik*). This pushes authority to the lowest level where ground-truth resides, utilizing low decision thresholds and self-contained units.

2. **The Directed Telescope**: Formal military hierarchies naturally suffer from "information pathology," where reports moving up the chain are delayed, summarized, and sanitized by intermediate echelons. To verify reports and bypass this friction, successful commanders throughout history (such as Napoleon, Wellington, and Moltke) employ a "directed telescope"—the deployment of trusted aides, staff officers, or scouts directly to critical points to gather unfiltered, unstructured, and immediate ground-truth.

3. **Decentralization and Initiative (Auftragstaktik)**: Developed in response to the loss of tactical control on 19th-century battlefields (caused by dispersed infantry and breech-loading firepower), the Prussian-German system of *[[concepts/auftragstaktik|Auftragstaktik]]* (mission command) represents the mature solution to uncertainty. Subordinates are issued general intent (the "why") rather than detailed orders (the "how"), allowing them to exploit local opportunities and take disorder in stride. This system requires high trust, shared doctrine, and organizational stability.

4. **The Pathologies of Centralized C3 (Vietnam)**: The massive communications infrastructure and computer-driven systems deployed by the U.S. in Vietnam illustrate the backfire of centralization. Rather than resolving uncertainty, the geometric growth of data led to quantitative abstraction (statistics, body counts, "nonsense on stilts") that blinded strategic planners. The availability of real-time communication enabled senior commanders to micro-manage firefights from helicopters, paralyzing junior initiative and turning the directed telescope into a tool of tactical paralysis.

- **The Historical Solutions to the Battlefield Control Dilemma** (ll. 1598-1606): "Always unable to command the whole of their forces the whole of the time, commanders devised various solutions for dealing with the problem. ### Chapter 3: The Revolution in Strategy [Jena 1806] (Range 1)

- **Logistical Simplicity and Strategic Command Independence** (ll. 1672-1676): "In an age when nine-tenths of all supplies (by weight) consisted of food and fodder and could be collected on the spot, when the relative backwardness of the banking system enabled large sums in cash to be levied directly from occupied towns and used to pay the troops or purchase their supplies, and when a soldier was supposed to require only one new coat every two years, it was possible to go on campaign without worrying too much about the rear." — Martin van Creveld argues in [[sources/van-creveld-command-in-war-1985]] that pre-1800 armies could temporarily break away from their lines of communication because they were largely self-supporting. - **Autonomous Corps Size and the Expansion of Strategic Range** (ll. 1718-1725): "Perhaps the most important single characteristic of the new corps, and one that was critical in making the revolution in command possible, was their sheer size. The time thus gained, and the fact that the corps were roughly interchangeable and able to exchange roles without further ado, cut the frequency with which information had to be passed between the army and its headquarters; or, which amounts to the same thing, made it possible to extend the range over which information could be usefully sent." — In [[sources/van-creveld-command-in-war-1985]], the creation of the autonomous corps is identified as the key organizational innovation of Napoleonic command. - **Unified Command as a Strategic Liberator** (ll. 1820-1826): "The first and probably most important fact to be noted about Napoleon’s command system is that the emperor himself served as his own commander in chief. Napoleon, moreover, was his own master; he did not have to summon endless councils of war or argue with royal hangers-on as did his opposite numbers in the Allied armies in 1805 or in the Russian one in 1812. - **The Shift in Strategic Intelligence in Dispersed Warfare** (ll. 1888-1892): "The widely dispersed way in which armies now began to operate, combined with their sheer size, meant that reports coming from a single spy or even cavalry patrols on the movements from Y to Z of Corps X were not of much significance. ### Chapter 4: Railroads, Rifles, and Wires [Königgrätz 1866] (Range 2)

- **The Nature of Command vs. Technological Novelty** (ll. 2748-2751): "command, rather than being simply an assortment of technological marvels around which organizations and procedures are built, consists instead of a series of processes—each of them as old as war itself—by which the technological means at hand are pressed into service." — Van Creveld argues that command is defined by its fundamental human and organizational processes rather than the technological tools it utilizes. - **Growth in Army Sizes and the Command Imperative to Disperse** (ll. 2790-2796): "too large “either to live or to advance”; concentrated in a single mass they “could not even exist,” their only remaining option being to fight. The concentration of such forces, reasoned Prussia’s chief of staff, was “in itself, a calamity,” for the growth of major formations—the corps, which now numbered well in excess of 30,000 men each—had made it impossible to maintain any one of them along any given axis of advance. Hence it had become imperative “to march separately but fight jointly,” a method requiring strong self-contained formations, good advance knowledge of the enemy’s movements, and excellent communications between forces widely dispersed in space." — The massive increase in nineteenth-century army size created severe logistics and command limitations, forcing armies to spread out to survive. This operational necessity of marching separately but fighting jointly represents a major development in [[concepts/strategy]] and [[hubs/warfare/strategy]], illustrating how modern operational art managed the friction of large-scale troop movements and [[concepts/logistics-and-supply-clausewitz]].
- **The Railroad's Impact on Strategic Standardization and Mathematical Calculation** (ll. 2814-2818): "reliance on the railroad implied an increased emphasis on scientific, mathematical calculation as opposed to the intuition of old. Coordinating men, weapons, supplies, and trains, as well as maximizing each railway line’s capacity and preventing congestion, demanded a type of painstaking staff work not unfamiliar to the members of Berthier’s état major général but raised to a new level of accuracy in both space and time." — The introduction of railways transformed [[concepts/strategy]] from an art based on commander intuition into a scientific, mathematical engineering exercise managed by centralized general staffs. This evolution is central to the analysis in [[sources/van-creveld-command-in-war-1985]] and [[hubs/warfare/strategy]], contrasting with the more improvised operations of the Napoleonic era, such as those at the [[hubs/warfare/long-19th-century/napoleonic-wars/battle-of-jena-auerstedt]].
- **Breech-Loading Rifles and the Loss of Tactical Control** (ll. 2830-2836): "In the absence of novel means of tactical communication, the spreading out of troops and the altered firing position led to a sharp decrease in the amount of control that junior commanders were able to exercise over their men. To put it another way, the advent of the new weapons—or rather their spread from a handful of Jäger and chasseurs (light infantry) to the infantry as a whole—meant that for the first time in history troops could no longer fight standing up, in full view of each other and of their own commanders. Entire armies turned into clouds of uncontrollable skirmishers, especially when on the attack." — The defensive power and prone firing position made possible by breech-loading rifles forced troops to disperse and seek cover, which severely degraded tactical control. This disconnect between technological weapons advances and tactical communication tools represents a major source of [[concepts/friction-in-war]] on the battlefield, which is a key theme in [[sources/van-creveld-command-in-war-1985]] and [[hubs/warfare/warfare-hub]].
- **The Strategic Utility and Defensive Bias of the Wired Telegraph** (ll. 2841-2848): "It was dependent on wire, which meant that the stations had to be fixed in space, and was consequently more useful to the defense than to the attack. The unwieldiness of the equipment at hand (wagons, wires, spools, poles), as well as that of the apparatus needed for setting up the service in difficult terrain, confined its use almost exclusively to major headquarters and strategy; using it for tactical purposes other than those of siege warfare was difficult if not impossible. The telegraph line ran along the railways and the process of mobilization would have been inconceivable without its aid; once the railhead was left behind, however, the use of the telegraph became problematic, with the obstacles increasing in direct proportion to the mobility of the campaign." — The physical constraints of wired telegraphy—requiring fixed stations and heavy equipment—created a natural bias favoring defensive operations over mobile, offensive campaigns. This dynamic directly impacts [[concepts/defense-vs-attack-clausewitz]] and poses a major challenge to mobile [[concepts/strategy]], as discussed in [[sources/van-creveld-command-in-war-1985]] and [[hubs/warfare/strategy]].
- **The Strategic Risks and Vulnerability of Telegraph Communications** (ll. 2888-2895): "In tactical matters, too, one will do well not to rely on the telegraph, since it is so extraordinarily vulnerable and very unreliable . . . the enemy, treason, one’s own artillery fire, and even the weather are all capable of interrupting the service at any time. The more important the lines, the greater these dangers. In most cases, i.e. in the presence of the enemy, the telegraph can never replace personal messages . . . the telegraph, like the railway, will only be of use to the tactician in the most exceptional of cases, and he will do well not to place too much trust in either. The same is not true for the strategist who . . . will often be tempted to employ this modern means of rapid communication. Let him beware, however, not to go too far, or else the power of this difficult-to-control medium may turn against him." — Because the telegraph was physically vulnerable to weather, enemy action, and accidents, relying on it created substantial tactical and strategic risks. Van Creveld notes that over-reliance on this medium could backfire on the strategist, highlighting the persistent presence of [[concepts/friction-in-war]] in modern communication networks. This strategic warning is documented in [[sources/van-creveld-command-in-war-1985]] and relates directly to [[concepts/strategy]] and [[hubs/warfare/strategy]].
- **Prussian Command Friction and Lack of Mutual Understanding in 1866** (ll. 3134-3136): "Those who would see in the Prussian command system a beautifully coordinated machine, operating in perfect accord with no friction among the well-oiled cogs, will have to look at some campaign other than the one of 1866." — Contrary to later historical myths of a flawless, machine-like staff system, the 1866 campaign was plagued by insubordination, unilateral movements, and senior officers who did not comprehend Moltke's strategic design. This demonstrates the pervasive influence of [[concepts/friction-in-war]] even within highly trained staffs during the [[hubs/warfare/long-19th-century/wars-of-german-unification/battle-of-koniggratz]], as analyzed in [[sources/van-creveld-command-in-war-1985]].
- **The Myth of Königgrätz as a Masterpiece of Centralized Command** (ll. 3664-3671): "To call Königgrätz a masterpiece of command, as many historians have, is thus slightly misleading. The battle was an unforeseen one, fought as a last-minute improvisation against an enemy whose whereabouts had not previously been discovered for forty-eight hours even though he was only a few miles away. The chief of staff had no part in planning it, and it may indeed have been fought somewhat against his will." — Rather than being a beautifully planned and executed operation, the [[hubs/warfare/long-19th-century/wars-of-german-unification/battle-of-koniggratz]] was a highly improvised, chaotic encounter battle characterized by severe intelligence failures, subordinate insubordination, and communication breakdown. The battle was won despite these failures, demonstrating that [[concepts/friction-in-war]] and decentralized initiative determined the outcome, as analyzed in [[sources/van-creveld-command-in-war-1985]].
- **Strategic Safety Margins and Limit on Span of Control** (ll. 3196-3199): "Though Moltke was working with subordinates who were totally lacking in comprehension for whatever strategic plans he may have entertained and who on occasion abused the independence granted them, those plans were sufficiently flexible to accommodate errors; that is, a large safety margin was left to ensure that mistakes would not develop into catastrophes." — To mitigate the lack of lateral communication and subordinate comprehension, Moltke built large safety margins into his [[concepts/strategy]], keeping each army separately strong and limiting the high command's span of control. This flexibility allowed the Prussian army to absorb operational blunders and successfully manage [[concepts/friction-in-war]], as described in [[sources/van-creveld-command-in-war-1985]] and [[hubs/warfare/strategy]].
- **The Limits of Dispersed Command in the 1866 Campaign** (ll. 3454-3457): "All in all, and the use of the telegraph notwithstanding, the campaign thus confirmed the centuries-old experience that a force more than 30,000 men strong could only be commanded when concentrated in one block, and that, once dispersed, forces larger than this could not be commanded." — Despite the introduction of the telegraph, the 1866 campaign demonstrated that large, dispersed forces could not be controlled in detail from a single headquarters, and that victories were achieved by independent corps commanders operating on their own. This represents a key finding in [[sources/van-creveld-command-in-war-1985]] regarding the limits of centralized command systems during the [[hubs/warfare/long-19th-century/wars-of-german-unification/battle-of-koniggratz]].
- **The Directed Telescope Function of General Staff Officers** (ll. 3724-3730): "Among the staff officers of each field army Moltke had trusted friends who could be relied upon to supplement the official reports with private correspondence; whereas the officers themselves, far from spending their day writing... were mostly found riding, alone or in twos, all over the theater of operations. Observing events at first hand and with a practiced eye, they took action where action was called for; went on reconnaissance missions, not infrequently at the risk of their lives; carried important messages; and served as telescopes for their commanders." — Prussian General Staff officers functioned as a [[concepts/directed-telescope]] for their commanders, bypassing formal reporting channels by traveling directly to the front to observe, carry private correspondence, and report critical updates. This operational practice, analyzed in [[sources/van-creveld-command-in-war-1985]], allowed headquarters to keep abreast of events despite poor telegraph and lateral communications.
- **Organizational Stability, Promotion by Seniority, and Risk Tolerance** (ll. 3755-3761): "With the Prussian staff officers spending much of their careers in a single institution... an organization came into being in which every officer knew all the others well and which was thoroughly “run in,” eingespielt... This stability, caused partly by a promotion system based mainly on seniority, cut down the amount of information that had to be processed within the organization and also helps explain the Prussian (and later, the German) Army’s extraordinary ability to pick itself up and recover from defeats. With their livelihood and advancement quite secure for a long time into the future, moreover, junior commanders in particular had no mortal fear of committing mistakes, which is the first prerequisite for learning." — The high stability and seniority-based promotion system of the Prussian General Staff fostered a deep mutual familiarity and high trust environment ("eingespielt"). This organization cut down internal information needs and insulated junior officers from the fear of making mistakes, encouraging initiative and helping the army overcome [[concepts/friction-in-war]] and recover from defeats, a theme detailed in [[sources/van-creveld-command-in-war-1985]].
- **The Decentralization of Command and Rise of the Company Commander** (ll. 3780-3786): "a decentralization of command proceeding from the top down, involving in particular a delegation of responsibility to company commanders (Hauptleute, a title that speaks for itself), who became the single most important link in the entire chain of command. The realization that a company is the largest unit that can be directly commanded by a single man under the conditions of dispersion and mobility characteristic of the modern battlefield led to the system of command that stood behind many of the German successes" — The loss of battlefield control due to increased dispersion forced the Prussians to decentralize tactical authority, delegating significant decision-making power down to company commanders. This operational philosophy, which underpins [[concepts/auftragstaktik]], adjusted command structures to the realities of modern warfare and is central to the analysis in [[sources/van-creveld-command-in-war-1985]] and [[hubs/warfare/warfare-hub]].
- **Strategic Decentralization and the Preeminence of Strategy over Tactics** (ll. 3791-3800): "the General Staff—together with the telegraph—was used to monitor the operations of the field armies, which were granted an unprecedented degree of independence. Independence, combined with constant, intelligent monitoring, made possible a new degree of flexibility. Indeed, so flexible were Moltke’s plans that it proved possible to accommodate his subordinates’ moves, and their blunders, to the point of ensuring that even a battle that was tactically lost—as actually happened at Königgrätz—would result in strategic victory. Battle, which Clausewitz had compared to cash payment in business transactions... thus lost part of its importance, and became more like a long-deferred check finally entered into one’s account. To put it simply, the way to overcome the loss of tactical control did not consist in forcibly imposing order upon disorder... Instead, tactical command was decentralized, and a system of war was adopted that made strategy more, and tactics less, important." — Moltke responded to the chaotic nature of the modern battlefield not by attempting to enforce central control, but by granting the field armies autonomy. Under this doctrine of strategic decentralization, which prefigures [[concepts/auftragstaktik]], the broader operational [[concepts/strategy]] became resilient enough to absorb tactical blunders, modifying the view of [[actors/clausewitz-carl-von]] on battle as a decisive "cash payment." This change is a milestone in [[hubs/warfare/strategy]], [[hubs/warfare/long-19th-century/wars-of-german-unification/battle-of-koniggratz]], and [[sources/van-creveld-command-in-war-1985]].
- **Strategy as a System of Expedients Beyond Initial Contact** (ll. 3805-3809): "the true essence of the Prussian command system was not to try to foresee every move in war as if it were a railway timetable. On the contrary, the new means... were used in order to balance independence with control and thus create true flexibility. To Moltke, strategy was always “a system of expedients,” and he always insisted that planning should go only as far as the first encounter with the enemy." — Moltke’s conception of [[concepts/strategy]] as "a system of expedients" rejected the notion of rigid, long-term plans in favor of flexibility and adaptability. Planning was limited to the initial encounter, acknowledging the presence of inevitable battlefield [[concepts/friction-in-war]] and uncertainty. This is a foundational concept within [[hubs/warfare/strategy]] and [[sources/van-creveld-command-in-war-1985]].

### Chapter 5: The Timetable War [Somme 1916 / Ludendorff 1918] (Range 3)

- **Transition of War from Art to Science and Neglect of Moral Forces** (ll. 3913-3917): "War itself, long regarded as the province of art, now came to be thought of as a science, and consequently as subject to systematic study and analysis in the same way that physics or chemistry is. Clausewitz’s warning concerning the incalculable moral forces governing war was often overlooked, and his discussion of the correct use of numbers in time and space was regarded as the key to his doctrine.6" — Van Creveld argues in [[sources/van-creveld-command-in-war-1985]] that late 19th-century General Staffs, influenced by a scientific age, tried to reduce warfare to an exact science. By focusing heavily on mathematical calculations of numbers, space, and time, they ignored the warnings of [[actors/clausewitz-carl-von]] about the unpredictable [[concepts/moral-forces-in-war]] and [[concepts/friction-in-war]] that govern actual combat, leading to an over-reliance on rigid systems and a neglect of proper [[concepts/clausewitz-on-theory]].
- **Inflexibility of Railway Logistics in Strategic Planning** (ll. 3942-3946): "Railways, however, are by nature an inflexible instrument; their use cannot be improvised—or so people, following the great Moltke, thought7—and their effectiveness is conditional on adherence to strict timetables kept under rigid centralized control. Since the Railway Department, owing to the role that it played in mobilization and deployment, had become the most important branch of every General Staff, its mentality came to govern the planning of war as a whole." — According to [[sources/van-creveld-command-in-war-1985]], the industrialization of transport via railways imposed severe constraints on [[concepts/strategy]]. The rigid schedules required to run strategic railways meant the planning process itself took on an inflexible, centralized character. This railway mentality dominated General Staff planning, treating [[concepts/logistics-and-supply-clausewitz]] as an engineering puzzle rather than a dynamic military operation, which directly contradicted Helmuth von Moltke the Elder's view that strategy is a system of expedients and resulted in plans that could not be easily altered.
- **The Desk-Bound Command and Loss of Touch with the Front Line** (ll. 4014-4016): "In the event, the reliance placed by the German Army upon modern means of communication to make the realization of its plans possible proved to be mistaken. In the absence of a properly organized corps of motor vehicles and dispatch riders, General Headquarters proved too desk-bound and soon lost touch with events at the front.20" — Van Creveld highlights a major failure of centralized command during the 1914 campaign. While the German plan relied on modern communications to coordinate massive armies, the lack of mobile links like motor vehicles and dispatch riders meant that the desk-bound General Headquarters lost contact with the rapidly moving front. This communication gap meant they could not wield a [[concepts/directed-telescope]] to verify reports, introducing severe [[concepts/friction-in-war]] that crippled their ability to execute their [[concepts/strategy]] and forced subordinate commanders to act blindly or on local instincts.
- **Command by Wire and the "Telephonitis" Divorce from the Battlefield** (ll. 4127-4133): "As the general became more and more bound to his office, and, consequently, divorced from his men, he relied for contact not upon the personal factor, but upon the mechanical telegraph and telephone. They could establish contact, but they could accomplish this only by dragging subordinate commanders out of the firing line, or more often persuading them not to go into it, so that they might be at the beck and call of their superiors. In the World War nothing was more dreadful to witness than a chain of men starting with a battalion commander and ending with an army commander, sitting in telephone boxes, improvised or actual, talking, talking, in place of leading, leading.28" — Van Creveld analyzes how the immobility of wire communications created a command culture of "telephonitis," described in [[sources/van-creveld-command-in-war-1985]] as a state where generals were physically and psychologically divorced from their men. The need to remain near telephone boxes to receive centralized orders prevented subordinate commanders from exercising on-scene leadership. This mechanical reliance destroyed the personal factor and [[concepts/military-genius]] required to adapt to [[concepts/friction-in-war]], turning commanders into passive voice-transmitters rather than active leaders.
- **Rigid Timetable Planning as a Self-Defeating Attempt to Control Battlefield Chaos** (ll. 4331-4339): "Under this system, carefully laid plans rigorously and undeviatingly carried out are regarded as the one way to overcome the inevitable confusion of the battlefield. Not only is strict centralization required in order to prevent any faux pas by subordinates, but operations must be staggered so as to allow for the reestablishment of proper order and control at the end of each phase. Planning and obedience—twin sides of the same coin—are regarded as the key to victory; any opportunism, or any mutual cooperation not explicitly provided for, is discouraged if not prohibited, it being assumed that the commander in chief alone is in possession of all the facts and therefore able to introduce whatever changes in the plan may be required. To prevent waste and secure coordination, rigid adherence to plans is prescribed; yet, as the above case study shows, such adherence in the end is conducive to generating waste and hindering coordination.44" — Van Creveld critiques the British command structure at the Somme, which attempted to banish [[concepts/friction-in-war]] by enforcing a highly centralized and pre-calculated timetable. By treating planning and obedience as absolute, the British system discouraged horizontal cooperation and subordinate initiative. This rigid execution of [[concepts/strategy]] took on a life of its own, but because the centralized commander was actually poorly informed, this lack of flexibility ultimately generated massive waste and prevented the exploitation of tactical successes.
- **Decentralized Tactics vs. Centrally Prescribed Formations** (ll. 4744-4755): "While the overall strategic direction of the German campaign thus left something to be desired in failing to provide the required flexibility, on the tactical level it was an immense—and, as the next few months showed, repeatable—triumph that must be regarded as the direct precursor of the spectacularly successful Blitzkrieg techniques employed early in World War II.68 This triumph was served by a technology virtually identical to that employed by Haig at the Somme, yet it was achieved by means of a command system as different from the British one as it is possible to imagine. Where the British had—no doubt, partly owing to the relatively sketchy training of their troops—feared disorder above all things, the Germans accepted it as inevitable and sought to circumvent the problem by putting a heavy emphasis on independent action by subordinate commanders and even by individual men. Where the one attack advanced in carefully dressed lines toward objectives straight ahead, the men forbidden to look left or right, the other was given no prearranged tactical objectives beyond the initial ones and flowed forward in small, loose groups, maintaining neither alignment nor formation and expected to cooperate with, but not depend on, their neighbors on both sides.69" — In [[sources/van-creveld-command-in-war-1985]], the German tactical system of 1918 is shown as the polar opposite of the British model at the Somme. Rather than fearing disorder, the Germans integrated it into their tactical doctrine, using small, independent stormtroopers who moved without prearranged alignment or rigid objectives. By decentralizing execution and prioritizing lateral cooperation, they overcame the communications bottleneck that plagued centralized command systems. This development represents a milestone in the [[hubs/warfare/warfare-hub]] and illustrates the practical application of [[concepts/auftragstaktik]] to circumvent the limitations of World War I communication technology.
- **Decentralized Command: Lower Decision Thresholds and Subordinate Initiative** (ll. 4768-4772): "As compared with the British command system at the Somme, the German one thus placed reliance on lower decision thresholds and fewer controls, combined with self-contained units and (it cannot be emphasized too often) at least the attempt to wield a directed telescope of superior effectiveness. The case in favor of the German system is made all but unanswerable by the fact that it worked for the British, too, in the few cases where it was applied, and this in spite of the allegedly inferior training of the troops." — Van Creveld highlights that the German system succeeded by decentralizing authority, relying on lower decision thresholds, and organizing self-contained tactical units. They also utilized a [[concepts/directed-telescope]] to bypass formal communication channels and gather direct intelligence. This decentralized system, a core feature of [[concepts/auftragstaktik]], allowed subordinate commanders to make rapid decisions on the spot, showing that decentralization is far more effective at handling battlefield uncertainty than the rigid control systems of the British.
- **Systemic Vulnerability of Machine-Age Armies to Logistical Disruption** (ll. 4808-4812): "Without a firm directing hand providing for the uninterrupted flow of supplies, replacements, and reinforcements a machine-age army will cease to function within a matter of days in the same way as an automobile factory deprived of its supply of parts. Insofar as such an army consists of more specialized parts and to that extent is more dependent on mutual cooperation, its disintegration may possibly be more rapid and more complete than that of a preindustrial force." — Van Creveld outlines how the transition to industrialized, machine-heavy armies transformed the nature of [[concepts/logistics-and-supply-clausewitz]]. Because modern armies depend entirely on a continuous flow of manufactured ammunition, fuel, and replacement parts rather than local foraging, they function like factories. If their centralized lines of communication are cut, the systemic interdependence of their parts makes them highly vulnerable to sudden and complete collapse, introducing a massive logistical dimension to [[concepts/friction-in-war]] that requires careful strategic management.
- **The Confrontation with the Enemy's Independent Will** (ll. 4840-4843): "The essence of war, to quote Clausewitz once again, is a confrontation with the enemy’s independent will. That will is constrained by, but not absolutely subject to, the means at his disposal. Coming on top of the normal environmental uncertainty governing all human affairs, this fact renders war difficult if not impossible to calculate.72" — In [[sources/van-creveld-command-in-war-1985]], Van Creveld emphasizes that attempts to run military command as a deterministic management system are fundamentally flawed. War is defined by a clash with the independent will of the adversary, as originally formulated by [[actors/clausewitz-carl-von]]. This adversarial dynamic, combined with environmental uncertainty, means that war can never be calculated like physics or chemistry. It represents the core of [[concepts/friction-in-war]] and explains why plans must be flexible enough to handle the difference between [[concepts/absolute-vs-real-war]].
- **Decentralization and Taking Disorder in Stride** (ll. 4866-4868): "The alternative was to extend Moltke’s principle of free cooperation at the highest level downward, place greater responsibility on the shoulders of commanders down the line, and take disorder in stride as inevitable and even, insofar as it affected the enemy as well, desirable." — Van Creveld shows how the German command heritage adapted to the chaotic nature of machine-age warfare. Instead of attempting to forcibly impose order on the battlefield—a method that failed catastrophically in 1806 at the [[hubs/warfare/long-19th-century/napoleonic-wars/battle-of-jena-auerstedt]]—the Germans pushed Helmuth von Moltke's principle of decentralized cooperation down the chain of command, which is the hallmark of [[concepts/auftragstaktik]]. By training commanders to accept and exploit disorder as a tactical opportunity, they turned the chaos of [[concepts/friction-in-war]] against their enemies, a concept that was central to their strategic success at battles like the [[hubs/warfare/long-19th-century/wars-of-german-unification/battle-of-koniggratz]] and in the 1918 offensive.

### Chapter 6: Masters of Mobile Warfare [Yom Kippur 1973] & Chapter 7: Helicopter and Computer [Vietnam] & Chapter 8: Conclusion (Range 4)

- **Operational Mobility and Speed in Blitzkrieg** (ll. 4911-4928): "The rise of the motor vehicle once again made it possible for the decisive margin of superiority to be provided by speed rather than by sheer mass. Seen from this point of view, armored warfare has more in common with the campaigns of Napoleon than with those of Moltke or Haig, and hence the methods employed to command it are, unsurprisingly, also similar." — This claim links armored warfare under Blitzkrieg and mobile defense directly to Napoleonic command principles. Rather than relying on massive, slow-moving railway concentrations, mobile command requires speed and flexibility, which can be found in [[concepts/strategy]], [[hubs/warfare/strategy]], and [[hubs/warfare/warfare-hub]], and is documented in [[sources/van-creveld-command-in-war-1985]].
- **Decentralization and Initiative in WWII Mobile Command** (ll. 4936-4945): "Like Napoleon, but in charge of forces whose mobility was far superior and which consequently spread over much larger spaces, the World War II panzer leader was forced to decentralize the chain of command and rely on intelligent initiative at every rank, beginning with the lowest" — This claim argues that the operational speed and vast geographical spread of armored warfare forced commanders to decentralize authority. This decentralization necessitated an organizational reliance on junior initiative at all levels, a core aspect of [[concepts/auftragstaktik]] linked to [[concepts/strategy]], and required tools like the [[concepts/directed-telescope]] to maintain cohesion.
- **Heinz Guderian and Fritz Fellgiebel's Development of Radio-Based Command** (ll. 4972-4980): "Thus the credit for recognizing the importance of the question, for the first successful attempts at its solution, and for the first brilliant demonstration of how armored command ought to operate belongs essentially to two men: Heinz Guderian... and General Fritz Fellgiebel... Between them these men developed the principles of radio-based command that, in somewhat modified and technically infinitely more complex form, are still very much in use today." — This highlights how Heinz Guderian and Fritz Fellgiebel developed the systematic use of radio networks. By establishing structured operating procedures and allocating radio equipment to lower echelons, they enabled the decentralized coordination crucial for mobile [[concepts/strategy]] as detailed in [[sources/van-creveld-command-in-war-1985]].
- **Radio's Reversal of Commander Immobilization** (ll. 4982-4993): "Radio, by making communication between any two points possible, reversed this trend; once again, as in the days of Gustavus or Marlborough, commanders could be found far forward." — Radio technology reversed the historical trend toward specialization and immobilization that had pushed commanders further into the rear. By enabling two-way communications on the move, commanders could return to the front to observe battles firsthand, reshaping operational [[concepts/strategy]] and command execution in the [[hubs/warfare/warfare-hub]].
- **IDF Command Philosophy: Improvisation over Pre-Planned Detail** (ll. 5031-5038): "A proper command system should be able to set itself goals, and then strive to attain those goals in spite of the clear realization that things will go wrong, but also in the confidence that, when they do go wrong, the system will be able to overcome the obstacles. ... The IDF normally takes the second of these ways. [to lay down general objectives only and to start going at once]" — This claim, illustrated by Mordechai Gur, highlights the IDF command philosophy of relying on general objectives and filling in the details during execution. This approach relies on flexibility to overcome [[concepts/friction-in-war]] and aligns with the mission-oriented ethos of [[concepts/auftragstaktik]], as analyzed in [[sources/van-creveld-command-in-war-1985]].
- **IDF's Doctrine of Improvisation and Resourcefulness** (ll. 5058-5069): "The balance between human and material resources being what it was, a heavy premium had to be put on compensating factors of a spiritual nature: individual daring (heaza), maintenance of aim (dvekut bamatara), improvisation (iltur), and resourcefulness (tushia), all of which still remain key elements of the fighting doctrine that the IDF systematically inculcates into, and demands of, troops and commanders at every level." — Outlining the early constraints of the IDF (lack of signaling gear and heavy weapons), this claim shows how spiritual and moral factors served as compensating resources. These factors represent the [[concepts/moral-forces-in-war]] and characteristics of [[concepts/military-genius]] required to operate successfully under extreme material scarcity.
- **Dayan's Mission-Style Orders in 1956** (ll. 5077-5089): "We shall organize separate forces for each of the main military objectives, and it will be the task of each force to get there in one continuous battle, one long break, to fight and push on, fight and push on, until the objective is gained . . . [This system] also suits the character of our army and of our officers. ... Don’t signal me during the fighting for more men, arms, or vehicles." — This claim outlines Moshe Dayan's execution of highly independent, mission-style command in 1956. By assigning clear objectives and axes of advance without providing mid-battle reinforcements or detailed management, Dayan pushed decentralization to its limits, reflecting [[concepts/auftragstaktik]] in military [[concepts/strategy]].
- **Dayan's System of Organized Chaos in 1956** (ll. 5111-5118): "the heavy emphasis on improvisation and the absence of a strong controlling hand meant that 'our capacity for misadventure is limitless' ... Nevertheless, against an Egyptian Army whose plans were based on the elimination of any need for mutual support and coordination ... Dayan’s system of 'organized chaos' ... was effective enough." — Dayan's decentralized command approach succeeded operationally but suffered from severe coordination failures and friendly fire incidents. This illustrates how the reduction of control introduces extreme [[concepts/friction-in-war]] and misadventure, yet can still be highly effective against rigid adversaries, a key dynamic of [[concepts/auftragstaktik]] discussed in [[sources/van-creveld-command-in-war-1985]].
- **Rabin's Doctrine of Armored Command on the Move** (ll. 5132-5139): "commanders and headquarters [of armored forces] must be able to gather intelligence, process it, prepare orders, and issue them while on the move. ... An armored commander should be so trained as to make him as little dependent on his superior as possible in deciding how to act." — Rabin's 1960 doctrine formalizes armored mobile command, arguing that commanders must make decisions and issue orders dynamically on the move rather than waiting for detailed planning. This minimizes dependence on higher echelons, reinforcing [[concepts/auftragstaktik]] and operational [[concepts/strategy]].
- **IDF Rule of Action: "When in Doubt—Attack"** (ll. 5140-5143): "gaps and moments of uncertainty would always appear.
