# The Wealth of Nations (Adam Smith)

The founding text of modern **economics** (1776) — **[[adam-smith|Adam Smith's]]** inquiry into how
nations grow rich, and the charter of **[[classical-political-economy|classical political economy]]** and
economic liberalism. Eighth source of **Gap 1**; a primary source underpinning the wiki's modern
economic-history pages.

## The Argument

In five books Smith builds a systematic account of how wealth is produced and distributed:

- **The division of labour** (the famous pin factory) as the fundamental source of rising productivity,
  limited by the extent of the market.
- **Markets, prices and the "invisible hand"** — how self-interested exchange, mediated by competition,
  coordinates production and allocates resources without central direction, so that "it is not from the
  benevolence of the butcher… but from their regard to their own interest" that we get our dinner.
- A sustained **attack on mercantilism** — the doctrine that wealth is bullion and that trade is
  zero-sum — and the case for **free trade** and against monopolies, guilds and most state restrictions.
- The **components of price** (wages, profit, rent) and a labour-grounded theory of value; capital
  accumulation as the driver of growth.
- The proper, limited but real **functions of the state** (defence, justice, public works and
  institutions) — Smith was no doctrinaire of pure laissez-faire.

## Significance

*The Wealth of Nations* made economics a discipline and provided the **intellectual foundation of market
capitalism** and nineteenth-century free-trade liberalism. It shaped the economic policy of the
industrializing world, framed the classical school (Ricardo, Malthus, Mill) and, by reaction, Marx's
critique. It is one of the most consequential books of the modern era.
