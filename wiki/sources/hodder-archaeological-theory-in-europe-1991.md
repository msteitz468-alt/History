# Archaeological Theory in Europe: The Last Three Decades

**Editor:** Ian Hodder. First published 1991 by Routledge (reissued 2015 as Routledge Library Editions: Archaeology, Volume 33). Multi-author survey of theoretical developments c. 1960–1990.

## Book Note

This volume examines why and how archaeological theory developed differently across European countries in the decades after the dominance of Anglo-American New Archaeology (processual, positivist, anthropological). Hodder and contributors argue that European archaeology remained more deeply historical, more open to Marxist and continental philosophical influences (Marx, Gramsci, Hegel, Croce, Lévi-Strauss, Braudel, Habermas, etc.), and more explicitly socially and politically engaged than its North American counterpart.

The book rejects a simple universal sequence (culture-historical → processual → post-processual). Instead, it documents highly contingent national trajectories shaped by institutional structures, political history (including fascist and communist regimes), and reactions to the misuse of archaeology for nationalist/racial ends (notably Kossinna and the Nazi Ahnenerbe).

Chapters provide country-by-country accounts, with Hodder's introduction framing the contrasts and the socio-political stakes. A Postscript 1990 updates some discussions.

This is a key source for the wiki's treatment of the historiography of archaeological theory, the socio-politics of the past, nationalism and archaeology, and the distinctive European contributions to post-positivist and critical approaches.

## Volume Synthesis Note

**Core contribution (from full 5-range Deployed Subagent extraction + main reconciliation, 2026-06-26):** Hodder (ed.) 1991 is the single best contemporary survey of how archaeological theory developed (or failed to develop) across a wide sample of European countries during the critical decades 1960–1990. It documents the uneven, often minimal, impact of the Anglo-American "New Archaeology"/processual package and the persistence of distinctively European emphases: deep historical sensibility, strong Marxist orientations (varying from vulgar to structuralist to critical), and explicit awareness of the socio-political embedding of archaeological knowledge.

**Key regularities across national cases:**
- Rejection of a single "culture-historical → processual → post-processual" sequence as adequate for Europe. Most countries followed contingent trajectories shaped by local institutions, intellectual milieux (Marx, Gramsci, Croce, Lévi-Strauss, Habermas, etc.), and political history.
- Stronger concern with the social and political functions of archaeology than in contemporaneous North American work. Archaeology is "always socially engaged."
- Widespread recognition that the Nazi misuse of prehistory (Kossinna's settlement archaeology, Ahnenerbe, ethnic claims) produced both a post-war retreat into empiricism/culture-history in some places and a demand for critical, reflexive theory in others.
- Eastern European cases show Soviet-imposed Marxism often becoming dogmatic "ideological garnish" or politically risky; post-1968/1956/1989 openings were decisive for theoretical space.

**Major divergences:**
- **Western/Northern** (Britain, Scandinavia): Earlier and more substantive engagement with social anthropology, ecology, and systems ideas; earlier and sharper internal critiques leading to post-processual, hermeneutic, feminist, and critical strands.
- **Mediterranean/Iberian** (Spain, Italy, Greece): Heavy nationalist or fascist legacies produced delayed or hybrid Marxist + methodological awakenings; continuity paradigms (Greece) or Crocean idealism (Italy) created distinctive obstacles and reactions.
- **Central/Eastern** (West Germany, Poland, Czechoslovakia, Hungary): "Kossinna syndrome" and Soviet constraints produced widespread distrust of grand theory or explicit "theories without theoretical archaeology." Settlement archaeology, source criticism, and historicity approaches persisted or were adapted; New Archaeology arrived late or selectively.

**Theoretical implications:**
- European experience demonstrates viable alternatives between dogmatic positivism and relativistic chaos: hermeneutic/critical perspectives that retain commitment to methodological rigour while foregrounding meaning, power, ideology, and the social construction of knowledge.
- The volume itself functions as an argument for opening theoretical debate across national borders and for treating the history of archaeology as itself theoretically and politically loaded.
- Post-1989/1990 reflections (especially in the Eastern chapters) express both optimism about loosened ideological constraints and caution about new risks (ethnic unrest, theoretical chaos) without sustained critical discussion.

This source supplies the essential comparative historiography for the wiki's concepts in archaeological theory, the socio-politics of the past, and nationalism-and-archaeology. It complements later sources (e.g. Trigger 2003) by documenting the specific European 1960–1990 moment when post-processual and critical perspectives were emerging in dialogue with (rather than simple replacement of) older national traditions.

## Range 05 Claims (Central/Eastern Europe: Czechoslovakia and Hungary; Postscript 1990 Reflections; Synthesis on Diversity, Marxism, Socio-Politics)

**Exclusive source:** Read only /tmp/hodder-ranges/range_05_11701-15311.txt. Verbatim quotes grounded here; locators use original book pagination (e.g. p.260) + range line references where directly matching. Focus: Eastern European theoretical developments under Soviet influence; 1990/Postscript-style reflections; overall points on national diversity, role of Marxism, history vs. theory, socio-political embedding.

### Czechoslovakia (Neustupný chapter, with 1990 comment; range lines ~1–560; orig. pp. 248–271)
- 1990 reflection on not rewriting under prior regime (range lines 1–10, p.260): "decided not to rewrite my contribution in 1990 but to comment on It. I did not write anything untrue in the original text which precedes, but I could not write everything I wanted to without running the risk of its being the last thing I would ever write. I particularly regret that I could not discuss the content of individual theories. This was something reserved for the former archaeological bureaucratic elite which was supposed to make its judgements in conformity with Communist Party policy."
- Limited practical impact of official ideological training despite intensity (p.260, range ~10–30): "I stressed the ideological training that Czechoslovak archaeologists received in the 1950s and 1960s. Why has it had such a limited effect on archaeological theory? The answer may be that the training was on a rather general, often dogmatic, level which made it difficult to use in specific instances. Moreover, the official 'Marxism' of that time included many theses based on nineteenth-century ethnography (e.g. 'matriarchy' as a stage in the development of humankind) which were hardly acceptable to most archaeologists but could not be discarded while party ideology was in control."
- Distinction between political opposition to communism and acceptance of Marxism as social theory (p.260, range ~25–35): "Surprisingly, political ideology seems not to have influenced the development of theory, as very few archaeologists accepted Marxism as a political theory. Most of them, however, adopted it as the one social theory to which they knew no alternative. I remember that some of my colleagues were strongly opposed to communism as a political doctrine but accepted many of the Marxian philosophical theses. This situation lasted until the 1960s."
- 1968 Soviet invasion as turning point shifting ideology from Marxism to pure political doctrine for bureaucracy; relaxation of pressure (p.260–261, range ~35–45): "Everything changed drastically after the Russian invasion of Czechoslovakia in 1968. The state ideology ceased to rely on Marxism, which was replaced by a purely political doctrine supporting the party bureaucracy ideologically. Marxism was still taught in schools but nobody believed it any more, so ideological pressure was relaxed. This allowed a climate in which an anti-theoretical archaeology could flourish. Fortunately, the New Archaeology could begin to creep in at that time, although few people had the courage to defend it openly."
- Post-1968 economic/political isolation blocked Western literature and contacts; theory itself became dangerous (p.261, range ~45–60): "Because the new political situation deepened the economic crisis of the country, access to new western literature and 'archaeological science' became very difficult, if not impossible. ... Many archaeologists, including the present author, ceased to write on theory, concentrating instead on non-theoretical topics: theory became dangerous, whether Marxian or not, as it could engender conflict with some segment of the currently valid ideological doctrine."
- Note on community size, artefact wealth, and theoretical density (p.261, range ~70–95): "The number of archaeologists in any one community can have an important influence on its theoretical interests: because with a lot of work to be done outside pure theory, and a limited number of people involved, we cannot expect the field of enquiry to be covered densely (many significant questions will not be discussed at all, other problems will receive attention only from time to time). ... It may be no accident that major theoretical volumes often appear in countries whose archaeological record is one-sided and comparatively poor. ... In respect of its wealth of archaeological evidence Czechoslovakia occupies a middle position between the north and north-west of Europe on the one side and the south-east of our subcontinent on the other."

### Hungary (Laszlovszky & Siklódi chapter; range lines ~560–~1740; orig. pp. 272–298)
- Pre-WWII German influence and post-WWII imposition of Soviet-style Marxism with no local intellectual ancestry (p.272–274, range ~670–680): "Hungarian archaeology has traditionally been strongly influenced by German schools. ... A strong Soviet influence began to dominate the scene from the late 1940s and particularly from the 1950s, and this had little, if any, intellectual ancestry in Hungary. Following the fundamental political changes in the country after the Second World War, Marxism became the official ideology, and archaeological studies were affected. Marxism basically meant vulgar Marxism in the dogmatic Stalinistic period of the 1950s, when the entire theory was reduced simply to the tenet of class struggle and the relationship between base and superstructure."
- Ideological garnish on traditional work; avoidance of real theory (p.274, range ~680–710): "It must none the less be emphasised that only a small portion of archaeological works published during this period is characterised by this obligatory theorising. Most publications followed the theoretical principles of the former literature with some kind of ideological garnish. It was a general practice in archaeological articles written according to the traditional framework that Marxism only appeared in the form of periodic and mostly non sequitur quotes from Marx and Engels. ... No archaeological book discussing theoretical questions has been published in Hungary in the past twenty-five or thirty years, and the Regeszeti Kizikönyv ('Handbook of archaeology') published in 1954 is little more than a compendium of practical advice for would-be archaeologists lacking any kind of theoretical digressions."
- Key indigenous directions: Gyula László's "archaeological ethnography" (practical, ethnographic-inspired reanimation of finds, social reconstruction from cemeteries in phases, "creative uncertainty") (p.276–277, range ~730–810): " 'Archaeological ethnography' ... drew its inspiration from several sources, most important among which was Hungarian ethnography ... 'One of the greatest pitfalls of history - and of archaeology - is that, intentionally or unintentionally, we tend to approach the past armed with our modern concepts' (László 1977: 56). These general problems led László to coin the phrase 'creative uncertainty' to describe the practical application of archaeological theories. Since our methods and tools are restricted, our conclusions cannot be absolute."
- Historicity tradition (Mócsy, Bóna): archaeology as synthetic historical discipline aiming at full past reality beyond specific categories or sources (p.277–279, range ~810–890): "According to this idea archaeology should, by recognising the pitfalls of traditional methods and archaeological categories, represent a higher level in the perception of historical reality. ... archaeology can only become a historical discipline if it attains a level of historicity from where it can no longer hope to advance further with the help of its specific methods. ... He interpreted the data as parts of a homogenous historical process, not as distinct, unrelated cultures, periods, horizons..."
- Late, partial reception of New Archaeology; bypassed ferment; Childe's strong personal/theoretical influence during 1950s isolation (p.281–285, range ~980–1120): "It is extremely difficult to evaluate the effect of the New Archaeology on Hungarian scholars because the intellectual ferment of international scholarship characterising the latter half of the 1970s by-passed Hungary. ... The impact can be demonstrated for prehistory ... at the time of his visit to Hungary - 1955 - Hungarian archaeology was almost completely sealed off from international research ... This largely explains why in the 1960s after the eclipse of vulgar-Marxist ideology, Childe's scientific oeuvre became a model for Hungarian archaeologists."
- Ethnic attribution sensitivities rooted in post-WWI political boundaries and chauvinism; "class" monopoly in Marxist framework delayed ranked/stratified society concepts until late 1970s (p.286–287, range ~1210–1250): "This can basically be ascribed to the fact that the political boundaries drawn up in the course of the peace negotiations following the First World War did not coincide with ethnic boundaries, which in turn gave rise to strongly chauvinistic political views and arguments which often employed historical and archaeological data in order to prove their particular 'truths'. ... In the Marxist framework 'class' was the only accepted form for the description of social categories for a long time. Thus in Hungarian archaeology and historiography, new paradigms such as ranked or stratified societies only found their way into the interpretational framework from the second half of the 1970s."
- Ethnography central due to surviving peasant society into 20th c.; sociology limited as "bourgeois pseudo-discipline"; analytical methods often isolated or for show (p.288–290, range ~1300–1370): "In Hungary and in other countries of Central and Eastern Europe industrialisation began later than in western Europe. Thus, peasant society and its archaic cultivation techniques ... survived into the twentieth century. Consequently, ethnography occupied a central place in Hungarian social science and in some periods these studies were strongly linked to political issues. ... sociology, sensu stricto, was exiled from academic life as a 'bourgeois pseudo-discipline' that could not be reconciled with Marxism. ... appendice s of this kind inserted at the end of archaeological articles very often only gave a semblance of modernity."
- Core explanation for lack of theoretical books/debates (p.290, range ~1380–1410): "The other basic reason why methodological and theoretical ideas were never set down in purely theoretical terms is that as long as methodological problems only were debated, the discussions remained internal to archaeology; a review of theoretical questions would have involved a rethinking of ideological issues as well. During most of the period surveyed in this chapter these issues remained taboo because ideology and philosophy were part and parcel of the institutionalised section of the political system (Harding 1982)."

### Postscript/1990 Reflections and Cross-Volume Synthesis Points on Diversity, Marxism, History, Socio-Politics (from chapters + index cross-refs in range end matter)
- National diversity as central (recurrent; index entries confirm framing): European trajectories highly contingent on political history, institutional structures, and reactions to past (Nazi or Soviet) misuse of archaeology; no uniform culture-historical → processual → post-processual sequence. Eastern cases illustrate how Soviet-imposed Marxism often produced "vulgar" reduction or garnish rather than vibrant debate; local traditions (ethnography in Hungary, dense empirical work in Czechoslovakia) persisted alongside or despite it.
- Marxism under Soviet influence: frequently dogmatic/vulgar in 1950s, reduced to class struggle/base-superstructure with limited specific applicability; accepted philosophically more than politically; post-1968/1956 shifts relaxed but did not eliminate entanglement of ideology with state power. "Theory became dangerous" (Czech); "ideological 'chains'" (Hungary conclusion).
- History vs. theory tension: Hungarian "historicity" explicitly elevates synthetic historical reconstruction over archaeological categories or isolated methods; Czech work emphasizes topics over solutions due to limited personnel and data wealth. Empiricism (ethnographic analogies, practical problem-solving) prized; abstract philosophy avoided when it risked ideological conflict.
- Socio-politics embedding (core of volume): Political changes directly limited/widened "Lebensraum of academic life" (p.273). Access to literature, conferences, and foreign contacts was gatekeeper for new ideas (New Archaeology arrived late or via personal mediation). Post-1989 opening (Czech 1990 comment; Hungary "now that the country has literally lost its chains") expected to produce polarization and enrichment but also "theoretical chaos" unless basic issues can be debated without restriction. Archaeology's role in ethnic/national claims (chauvinistic use of data post-WWI borders) underscores need for critical theory.
- Overall: "the link between political and historical changes ... and archaeological theory must definitely be considered" (p.273). "it is to be hoped that Hungarian archaeology will preserve its sensitivity to practical, empirical questions ... Only thus can we hope that Hungarian archaeology can loosen its ideological 'chains' ... and will be moving in a direction, where 'it is not the increase of tangible finds, but the refinement of observations that will enrich our archaeology and its perception of the past.' (László 1980: 153)."
