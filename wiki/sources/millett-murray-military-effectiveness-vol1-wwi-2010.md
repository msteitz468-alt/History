# Military Effectiveness, Volume 1: The First World War (Millett & Murray, eds., new ed. 2010)

This is Volume 1 of a three-volume comparative study examining why some military forces succeed while others fail. The project originated in the early 1980s under the auspices of Andrew Marshall (Director of Net Assessment, OSD) to analyze the performance of military institutions in the first half of the twentieth century. The editors selected seven major powers with common experience in the world wars for consistency: the United States, Great Britain, France, Germany, Italy, Russia (Soviet Union), and Japan.

The study is structured around three periods: WWI (this volume), the interwar period (Vol. 2), and WWII (Vol. 3). A common guidance framework essay directs the case studies.

## Framework: The Effectiveness of Military Organizations
The core analytical tool (Chapter 1 by Millett, Murray, and Watman) evaluates military organizations across four interconnected levels:

- **Political level**: The ability of the state and its military to secure resources, set appropriate goals, and maintain civil-military relations that support effective strategy.

- **Strategic level**: The formulation of war aims, allocation of resources, and coordination of military means with political ends; assessment of the enemy and the international environment.

- **Operational level**: The planning and conduct of campaigns and major operations; the linking of tactics to strategic objectives.

- **Tactical level**: The actual employment of forces in battle; unit performance, doctrine, training, and leadership at the point of contact.

Excellence at one level does not guarantee success at others. Example from the text: German tactical brilliance in both world wars was undermined by strategic myopia, turning battlefield advantages into catastrophic defeats.

The framework emphasizes that effectiveness must be judged by outcomes at each level, not just victory or defeat in battle. It incorporates factors like manpower, budgets, innovation, adaptation, and cultural/institutional influences.

## New Edition Introduction (Murray and Millett)
The 2010 introduction reflects on the study twenty years later. It notes that while the 1980s context (Cold War, NATO vs. Warsaw Pact) shaped the choice of periods, the volumes remain a baseline for understanding military performance. Key additional insights:

- Innovation in peacetime occurs under uncertainty about the next war's nature, timing, and context.
- Technological change presents enormous challenges.
- Adaptation in wartime often occurs too slowly and at terrible cost.
- Cultural and intellectual frameworks shape armed forces; military functionalism mixes with non-military social values.
- The study "barely scratched the surface" of these issues.

The editors stress the enduring value of history for understanding war's fundamental nature (quoting General Mattis on studying the past). They apply lessons to post-9/11 conflicts (Iraq 2003, Afghanistan), noting failures to recognize insurgency, inadequate long-term strategy, and the costs of ignoring history (parallels to Vietnam and 1920 Iraq revolt).

The project highlights persistent problems in military institutions across cultures and time.

1. **Primacy of political and strategic levels; lower-level virtuosity cannot redeem higher errors (core framework implication)**:
   "No amount of operational [or tactical] virtuosity ... redeemed fundamental flaws in political judgment. Whether policy shaped strategy or strategic imperatives drove policy was irrelevant. Miscalculations in both led to defeat, and any combination of politico-strategic errors had disastrous results, even for some nations that ended the war as members of the victorious coalition. Even the effective mobilization of national will, manpower, industrial might, national wealth, and technological know-how did not save the belligerents from reaping the bitter fruit of severe mistakes [at this level]. This is because it is more important to make correct decisions at the political and strategic level than it is at the operational or tactical level. Mistakes in operations and tactics can be corrected [admittedly at a cost]. But political and strategic mistakes live forever."
   Loc: Introduction xviii (citing Millett and Murray, “Lessons of War,” The National Interest (Winter 1988)).
   Targets: update [[concepts/military-effectiveness]], [[concepts/strategy]], [[hubs/warfare/strategy]], [[hubs/warfare/warfare-hub]]; source.

3. **Military effectiveness requires capacity to adapt; most organizations fail at this**:
   "All military organizations get the next war wrong to a certain extent. The historical record suggests the opposite; instead of adapting their doctrine and approach to reality, for the most part military leaders and their organizations have attempted to make reality fit their preconceived notions until they and their forces fail or face obvious failure."
   "Part of the effectiveness of military forces must be their ability to adapt to the actual conditions of combat. There are some continuities with peacetime, as the armed forces that prepare themselves rigorously and honestly before a war generally adapt more effectively to the entirely new conditions that war presents."

4. **Schweinfurt raids (1943) as concrete example of doctrinal rigidity and failure to adapt at operational level**:

Nevertheless, the Eighth Air Force, after five months of dreadful slaughter of its bombers, still held to its doctrinal belief that large formations of B-17s, unescorted by fighters, could fly deep into Germany, do massive damage to specific targets, and suffer what the U.S. Army Air Forces’ theoreticians had termed “acceptable losses” in the process. ... In the second raid, more than a quarter of the bombers failed to return and virtually all of the surviving B-17s that flew that mission were damaged. The disaster of the second Schweinfurt raid in late October 1943 finally disabused the Eighth Air Force and the USAAF leaders of their prewar notions of self-defending bomber formations, but far too late for the crews and their aircraft scattered over the German countryside in burning wreckage."

6. **Peacetime innovation moves only as fast as organizational factors allow; acceptance conditions**:
   "Our work on innovation in the interwar period helped clarify some of the unfinished business in Military Effectiveness. One conclusion was that peacetime innovation moved along only as fast as organizational concerns influenced the process. Only external political intervention could speed the process, usually by appointing military zealots to key positions and providing greater financial resources. Armed forces, however, accepted innovation only if it was tied to clear strategic challenges, organizational enhancement, and operational clarity. Interservice cooperation seldom occurred; transnational same-service cooperation did. We also came to appreciate the role of innovators, disciples, and the importance of field testbed units. 7. **Michael Howard on the unique professional difficulties (uncertainty; "practising on dummies")**:
   "There are two great difficulties for which the professional soldier, sailor, or airman has to contend in equipping himself as commander.
