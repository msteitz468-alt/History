# The Man Who Knew Infinity — Robert Kanigel (1991)

**Full citation**: Kanigel, Robert. *The Man Who Knew Infinity: A Life of the Genius Ramanujan*. New York: Charles Scribner’s Sons, 1991 (Washington Square Press trade paperback 1992/2013). Finalist for the Los Angeles Times Book Award (Science). One of the project’s Top 50 Biographies.

This is the standard modern biography of Srinivasa Ramanujan Aiyangar (1887–1920), the self-taught Indian mathematical prodigy from Kumbakonam, Tamil Nadu, and of his five-year collaboration (1914–1919) with G. H. Hardy at Trinity College, Cambridge. The book covers Ramanujan’s Brahmin boyhood beside the Cauvery, discovery of Carr’s *Synopsis*, the filling of his notebooks with thousands of results, years of door-to-door search for patrons in Madras, the famous 1913 letter to Hardy, the fraught voyage to England in wartime, the “singularly happy collaboration” that produced twenty-one major papers, the onset of illness (likely tuberculosis and perhaps other factors), Ramanujan’s return to India as a hero in 1919, his death at 32 in Madras, and Hardy’s lifelong effort to secure his legacy. It is simultaneously the story of Hardy, the fastidious, atheist Cambridge analyst whose “flint and stone” personality met Ramanujan’s devotional intuition.

## Overview and Thesis

Kanigel frames the book as a clash of cultures and a near-miracle of recognition: an “inscrutable intellect and a simple heart” from the narrow, temple-centered, goddess-trusting world of Sarangapani Sannidhi Street transported into the “glittering world of Cambridge” at the exact moment when Europe went to war. The central question is not merely “how did Ramanujan do it?” but how social and educational systems nearly crushed him and how a single English mathematician’s decision to take a wild, unproven letter seriously changed both men and the history of mathematics. Ramanujan was *svayambhu* — self-born, self-taught, guided by dreams of Namagiri — yet his genius required Hardy’s editorial rigor, institutional shelter, and stubborn advocacy to become visible to the world. The book insists on the contingency: “so nearly did events turn out otherwise.” It is also about what societies do (or fail to do) with genius once found, and about the limits of formal proof versus the “thoughts of God” that Ramanujan claimed his equations expressed.

Hardy is the indispensable second protagonist: the product of Winchester and Trinity, a man of ice-clear aestheticism who hated religion yet revered mathematical beauty, who protected Ramanujan’s inspiration while slowly teaching him the Western canons of rigor. Their collaboration — “a singularly happy” one — is portrayed as one of the great cross-cultural intellectual friendships, forged in cold rooms, high tables, and hospital beds during the Great War.

## Methodological Notes

- Primary material: Ramanujan’s three notebooks and the “lost notebook”; the full 1913–1919 Hardy–Ramanujan letters (Trinity/Cambridge University Library); the “Family Record” compiled by relatives; extensive interviews and oral histories collected by Kanigel in South India (1987–1990) and England; P. C. Mahalanobis’s recollections; Hardy’s 1937 Harvard lectures (*Ramanujan*) and *A Mathematician’s Apology*; contemporary Madras newspaper accounts and university records.
- Kanigel is a science writer, not a professional mathematician; he consulted experts (including Richard Askey, Bruce Berndt, and others) for technical accuracy while keeping the mathematical ideas accessible. He is explicit about gaps: many dates in the early Madras years are approximate; some family stories are contradictory; Ramanujan left almost no personal letters of a non-mathematical nature.
- Strong on lived texture (food, caste, ritual bathing, the “English chill,” vegetarianism at High Table, the sanatorium regime) and on the emotional costs of cultural dislocation.
- Balanced on both men: documents Ramanujan’s rigidity and periods of withdrawal, Hardy’s emotional reserve and later guilt-tinged idealization.

## Key Contributions to the Wiki

- The richest single narrative of Ramanujan’s life, from the thwap of wet clothes on the Cauvery to the hero’s funeral in Madras, grounded in verbatim family testimony and letters.
- Detailed portrait of the 1913 letter, the vetting process (Baker and Hobson said no; Hardy said yes), the voyage, the first Cambridge papers, and the partition function / tau / mock theta work.
- Unmatched treatment of the Hardy–Ramanujan relationship as both mathematical partnership and human story: the “flint and stone,” the shared walks, the hospital visits, Hardy’s later Harvard lectures and lifelong guardianship of the legacy.
- Cultural and historiographic framing: the meaning of *svayambhu*, the role of Namagiri, the colonial educational system that both enabled and nearly destroyed Ramanujan, the question of how many unrecognized Ramanujans exist.
- Primary-source texture for the history of early 20th-century mathematics, Anglo-Indian intellectual exchange during WWI, the Royal Society election of 1918, and the posthumous publication of the notebooks.

### Range 02 Claims (Chapter Two; lines 1825–3154)
- Around 1903, [[actors/ramanujan-srinivasa|Srinivasa Ramanujan]] first obtained George Shoobridge Carr's book. "It first came into his hands a few months before he left Town High School, sometime in 1903." (1. THE BOOK OF CARR)
- The book was *A Synopsis of Elementary Results in Pure and Applied Mathematics*, a compilation of ~5,000 equations. "In essence, the book was a compilation of five thousand or so equations..." (1. THE BOOK OF CARR)
- Carr's *Synopsis* covered algebra, trigonometry, calculus etc. "Algebra, trigonometry, calculus, analytic geometry, differential equations—great chunks of mathematics as it was known in the late nineteenth century..." (1. THE BOOK OF CARR)
- In the preface Carr wrote that he had "merely indicated the salient points of a demonstration..." "The difference in the effect upon the mind between reading a mathematical demonstration, and originating one wholly or partly, is very great." (1. THE BOOK OF CARR)
- In 1904 Ramanujan entered Government College, Kumbakonam ("the Cambridge of South India") on scholarship. "In 1904... he entered Kumbakonam’s Government College with a scholarship" (2. THE CAMBRIDGE OF SOUTH INDIA) [[places/kumbakonam|Kumbakonam]]
- At Government College, professor [[actors/seshu-iyer-p-v|P. V. Seshu Iyer]] sometimes left him to work independently; one effort on infinite series judged “ingenious and original.” "One math professor, P. V. Seshu Iyer... “ingenious and original,” Seshu Iyer judged it." (2. THE CAMBRIDGE OF SOUTH INDIA) [[actors/seshu-iyer-p-v|P. V. Seshu Iyer]]
- Ramanujan failed English composition at Government College and lost his scholarship. "at Government College, he failed English composition... his scholarship was taken away." (2. THE CAMBRIDGE OF SOUTH INDIA)
- In early August 1905, the seventeen-year-old Ramanujan ran away from home toward Vizagapatnam. "In early August 1905, Ramanujan, seventeen years old, ran away from home." (3. FLIGHT)
- In early 1906 Ramanujan went to Madras and attended Pachaiyappa’s College; new math teacher showed notebooks to principal who awarded partial scholarship. "It was to Pachaiyappa’s... College that Ramanujan was bound... Ramanujan’s new math teacher... awarded him a partial scholarship." (4. ANOTHER TRY) [[places/madras|Madras]]
- He failed the F.A. examination at Pachaiyappa’s in 1906 and again in 1907. "In December 1906, he appeared again for the F.A. examination and failed. The following year... failed again." (4. ANOTHER TRY)
- By 1908 Ramanujan was out of school without degree or regular job, tutoring while family faced financial strain. "Out of school, without a job, he hung around the house in Kumbakonam." (4. ANOTHER TRY and 5. THE NOTEBOOKS) [[places/kumbakonam|Kumbakonam]]
- While working through Carr between 1904–1907 he began keeping notebooks in “a peculiar green ink.” "it was in working through Carr’s Synopsis... that he began keeping them in earliest form." (5. THE NOTEBOOKS)
- The notebooks were idiosyncratic: an “example” often a wholly new theorem; notation differed (e.g. Greek letter for modulus). "Ramanujan’s notebooks formed a distinctly idiosyncratic record... an “example”—normally... was for Ramanujan often a wholly new theorem." (5. THE NOTEBOOKS)
- Ramanujan connected mathematics to metaphysics: 2^n − 1 represented “the primordial God and several divinities.” "One idea Ramanujan bruited about dealt with the quantity 2^(n) − 1... stood for “the primordial God and several divinities.”" (6. A THOUGHT OF GOD)
- “An equation for me has no meaning unless it expresses a thought of God.” "So he wasn’t being silly... when later he told a friend, “An equation for me has no meaning unless it expresses a thought of God.”" (6. A THOUGHT OF GOD)
- By late 1908 his mother decided “Enough is enough” and arranged his marriage. "Enough is enough, his mother decided. And sometime probably late in 1908 she moved decisively to invoke... an arranged marriage." (7. ENOUGH IS ENOUGH) [[actors/ramanujan-komalatammal|Ramanujan's mother]]
