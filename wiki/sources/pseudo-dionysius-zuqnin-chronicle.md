# Pseudo-Dionysius of Tel-Mahre, *Chronicle* Part III (the *Chronicle of Zuqnin*)

A Syriac world chronicle compiled c. **775/776** at the monastery of **Zuqnin** near Amida — its third
part our principal surviving witness to the lost Church History of **[[john-of-ephesus|John of
Ephesus]]**, and one of the great sources for the **sixth-century eastern Roman world** seen from the
Miaphysite, Syriac side. The fifth source of this Phase-5 batch.

## The Work

The chronicle (long wrongly ascribed to the ninth-century patriarch Dionysius of Tel-Mahre, hence
"Pseudo-Dionysius") is a **universal chronicle in four parts** from Creation to the compiler's own day
under the early Abbasids. **Part III**, covering roughly **488–578**, reproduces — often word for word —
the **second part of the lost Church History of John of Ephesus**, a Miaphysite bishop and historian who
**lived through** the events he records. Through it we recover John's narrative of the reign of
**[[justinian|Justinian]]** and his successors as experienced by the persecuted **Miaphysite** Church.

## Its Great Set-Piece: the Plague

Part III's most celebrated passage is John of Ephesus's **eyewitness account of the
[[justinianic-plague-541|Justinianic plague]]** (from 541): travelling across Asia Minor toward
Constantinople, he describes the depopulated countryside, the abandoned villages and unharvested fields,
the mass graves and the breakdown of the city — one of the most vivid descriptions of catastrophic
mortality to survive from antiquity, and a key independent witness alongside Procopius.

## Significance

The chronicle is a foundational text of the **Syriac historiographical tradition** and a precious
counterweight to the Greek, Chalcedonian sources for the age of Justinian: it preserves the
**Miaphysite** perspective on the doctrinal struggles, the imperial persecutions, and the great plague,
and it documents the world of northern Mesopotamia on the contested **Roman–Persian frontier**. For the
later parts it becomes an original witness to the early Islamic period.
