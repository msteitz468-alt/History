# Cicero, *The Republic* and *The Laws* (*De re publica*, *De legibus*)

The two great works of **[[cicero|Cicero]]'s** political philosophy (54–51 BCE), his Roman answer to
**Plato's** *Republic* and *Laws*: a theory of the **best commonwealth**, the **ideal statesman**, and
the **natural law** underlying all just human law. The seventh source of this Phase-4 batch. For the
author see **[[cicero|Cicero]]**.

## *De re publica* (On the Commonwealth)

A dialogue set in 129 BCE among **Scipio Aemilianus** and his friends. Against Plato's invented city,
Cicero's Scipio prefers to anatomize a **real** one — Rome — defining the *res publica* as "the
property of the people" (*res populi*), a community bound by **shared law and common interest**
(*iuris consensu et utilitatis communione sociatus*). Its theoretical core is the **[[mixed-constitution|
mixed constitution]]**: like **[[polybius-histories|Polybius]]**, Cicero holds that the three simple
forms (monarchy, aristocracy, democracy) each decay, and that the **balanced blend** of all three —
realized historically in Rome — is the most stable and just. Book 2 traces Rome's constitutional growth
as the cumulative work of many generations; Book 3 debates **justice** (the Carneadean attack and its
answer); the lost later books set out the *optimus civis*, the ideal statesman who guides the state.

The work closes with the **Dream of Scipio** (*Somnium Scipionis*) — answering Plato's Myth of Er — in
which the elder Scipio Africanus appears to his grandson in a vision of the cosmos, revealing the
smallness of earthly fame, the immortality of the soul, and the heavenly reward reserved for those who
serve their country. Transmitted separately with Macrobius's Neoplatonic commentary, the *Dream* became
one of the most influential texts of the Middle Ages.

## *De legibus* (On the Laws)

In the companion dialogue Cicero, "assuming the mantle of a legislator after the Platonic manner,"
grounds law itself. **True law is not human enactment but right reason in accordance with nature**:
eternal, universal, divine — "one eternal and unchangeable law… valid for all nations and all times."
Justice is natural, not conventional; an unjust statute is no true law. On this Stoic foundation Cicero
then sketches an idealized code of religious and magisterial law for his best state, closely modelled on
Roman institutions.

## Significance

These dialogues transmitted to the West the doctrines that became foundational to European political
and legal thought: the commonwealth as a partnership in justice, the **mixed/balanced constitution**,
and above all **[[natural-law|natural law]]** — the idea, carried through the Roman jurists, Aquinas,
Grotius and the Enlightenment, that human law is answerable to a higher moral order. The *Dream of
Scipio* shaped medieval cosmology and the literature of vision; the constitutional theory fed directly
into the thought of **Montesquieu** and the **American founders**.
