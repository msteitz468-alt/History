# Athens after the Peloponnesian War (Barry S. Strauss)

A study of how **[[athens|Athens]]** recovered from catastrophic defeat — the political, social and
economic history of the city from the restoration of democracy in **403 BCE** to the **[[kings-peace-386bce|King's
Peace]] of 386**. The second source of the Phase-9 batch.

## The Argument

Strauss explains Athenian policy through the interaction of two forces:

- **Class.** The Peloponnesian War had fallen unequally on Athenian society: the **thetes** (the poorer
  citizens who rowed the fleet and powered the democracy and empire) versus the **hoplite and elite**
  classes. Differential war losses and economic dislocation shaped postwar politics.
- **Faction.** Borrowing a model from political anthropology, Strauss treats Athenian politics as driven
  by **factions** — small, loosely organized competitive groupings around leaders — rather than modern
  "parties," and traces their shifting alignments.

His central finding is the **resilience of Athenian democracy and of Athenian imperial ambition despite
defeat**: after the trauma of the **[[thirty-tyrants|Thirty]]** and the remarkable **amnesty and
reconciliation of 403**, Athens rebuilt its manpower, its navy and its confidence, took an assertive role
in the **[[corinthian-war-395bce|Corinthian War]]** (rebuilding the Long Walls with Persian money after
**Conon's** victory at Cnidus), and reached again — within limits — for naval hegemony, until the
Spartan-Persian **King's Peace** checked it.

## Significance

The book is a model of how to combine **social history (class, demography, economy)** with **political
narrative**, and a key study of one of the most instructive episodes in Greek history: a defeated
imperial democracy that neither collapsed nor abandoned its ambitions, but adapted. It illuminates the
domestic underpinnings of the wiki's fourth-century narrative (the Corinthian War, the road to the
Second Athenian Confederacy).
