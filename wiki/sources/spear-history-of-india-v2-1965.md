# A History of India, Volume Two (Percival Spear, 1965)

Volume Two of the Penguin *History of India*, the continuation of [[thapar-history-of-india-v1-1966|Thapar
Vol. I]] from **1526 to the mid-twentieth century** — the **Mughal empire**, the **rise of British
power**, the **Raj**, the **national movement**, **Partition**, and **independent India**. Its
organising argument is the **continuity of the Mughal and British periods**: Spear insists British
India was "deeply indebted to Mughal India," that the Raj developed trends already present under the
Mughals, and that the two centralising imperial systems should be read together rather than as a
colonial rupture.

This volume **creates** almost the entire modern-India apparatus (the collection had only the East
India Company pages) — the Mughals and their rulers, the British conquest and Raj, the Indian National
Congress, Gandhi, Partition, and Nehru's republic.

## Volume Synthesis Note

Spear Vol. II completes the **Gap-3 India backbone** begun by [[thapar-history-of-india-v1-1966|Thapar
Vol. I]], carrying the subcontinent from the Mughal conquest (1526) to the Nehruvian republic — and in
doing so it supplies a large, previously-empty slice of **Gap 1 (modern history, 1750–present)** for
South Asia. Where the collection had only the East India Company pages, the wiki now holds the Mughal
empire and its great rulers, the British conquest and Raj, 1857, the national movement, Gandhi,
Partition, and Nehru's India — a connected modern narrative.

The volume's **organising thesis** is continuity: Spear deliberately reads the **Mughal and British
periods as one story**, "British India deeply indebted to Mughal India." Three imperial systems — the
[[delhi-sultanate|Sultanate]]/[[mughal-empire|Mughal]], the [[british-raj|British]], and (he implies)
the [[jawaharlal-nehru|Nehruvian]] republic — inherit and rework the same centralising apparatus (the
revenue state, the bureaucratic "steel frame," rule through Indian agency). Two further threads run the
length of the book: the **Hindu–Muslim relationship** — from [[akbar|Akbar's]] inclusive imperial
sanctity, through [[aurangzeb|Aurangzeb's]] orthodox reaction and Sirhindi's "germ of Pakistan," to
[[muhammad-ali-jinnah|Jinnah's]] two-nation theory and [[partition-of-india-1947|Partition]] — and the
**deep irony of the Raj**, that the English-educated class and liberal-constitutional ideas Britain
fostered became the engine of the nationalism that expelled it.

The volume's **limitation** is its standpoint, flagged on the pages it generated: Spear writes from
within the mid-20th-century **British liberal-imperial tradition** — a continuist, relatively benign
Raj; a defence of [[aurangzeb|Aurangzeb]] against the "ogre" legend; a reading of [[indian-rebellion-1857|1857]]
as backward-looking protest rather than proto-national war. He is candid about costs (the "financial
bleeding" of early Bengal, the Permanent Settlement's social damage) but predates the **economic-drain,
subaltern, and nationalist revisionisms** (deindustrialisation, the famines, 1857-as-freedom-struggle)
that the wiki notes for later balancing as the Cambridge History of India, Basham, and other sources
are ingested.

**Cross-source and forward connections.** Vol. II picks up Thapar's Delhi Sultanate and Bhakti threads
(the Mughal sequel; the Sikh militarisation), confirms the [[english-east-india-company|EIC]] and
[[indian-ocean-trade|Indian Ocean]] pages from the European-companies angle, and links to the Africa
and global-modern gaps (the Cold War / non-alignment via [[jawaharlal-nehru|Nehru]]). With this ingest
the **India spine is complete from prehistory to 1947/64**; the remaining Gap-3 work is the
**specialist/primary** India material (Basham, the Cambridge History of India, the Arthashastra, the
Mughal memoirs) and, still largely open, **Southeast Asia** (Reid, Coe, the Cambridge History of
Southeast Asia).
