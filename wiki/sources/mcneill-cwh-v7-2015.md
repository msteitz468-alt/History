# The Cambridge World History, Volume VII — Production, Destruction, and Connection, 1750–Present (Book 1)

## Series Note

Seventh and final volume of the Cambridge World History (general editor Merry E.
Wiesner-Hanks), the second of the two **double volumes** that close the series.
Volume VII is split into two physical books: **Book 1 "Structures, Spaces, and
Boundary Making"** (the material situations — environment, agriculture, technology,
energy, population, disease; the political movements — international law, nationalism,
imperialism, self-strengthening, decolonization, genocide, communism and fascism;
and key world regions) and **Book 2 "Shared Transformations?"** (family, urbanization,
migration, religion, science, sport, music, commodities, and specific moments such as
the Atlantic revolutions, the Cold War, and 1989). It is the sequel to
[[bentley-cwh-v6-2015|CWH VI]] (1400–1800) and closes the series.

The volume's organizing claim, set by editors McNeill and Pomeranz, is that since
~1750 humanity has lived through an uneven but world-altering transition built on
three intertwined dynamics: **production** (the shift to fossil fuels, exploding
population, and industrialization), **destruction** (world wars, genocide, and
environmental damage at planetary scale), and **connection** (globalization). The
editors mark 1750 as a watershed — the Seven Years' War as the first truly global
war, the Atlantic revolutions, the defeat of the Zunghars and the long victory of
sedentary states over nomads, and the onset of unprecedented demographic and
economic growth — while insisting the twentieth century is the most distinctive
phase of all. The volume is, in effect, the wiki's entry point into the
**Anthropocene** and the periods (21 Long 19th Century, 22 World Wars Era, 23 Cold
War, 24 Contemporary) it had no coverage of before this ingest.

## Structure

- **Introduction** (ch. 1): Pomeranz & McNeill on production, destruction, and
  connection as the organizing frame and 1750 as a periodization.
- **Part I — Material Matrices** (ch. 2–6): energy, population, and environmental
  change / the Anthropocene (McNeill); the economic history of agriculture
  (Federico); global industrialization, multipolar (Sugihara); the history of world
  technology (Josephson); a new world of energy (Smil).
- **Part II — Population and Disease** (ch. 7–10): demography and population
  (Livi-Bacci); population politics (Bashford); disease and world history (Harrison);
  the politics of smallpox eradication (Manela).
- **Part III — Politics** (ch. 11–17): the evolution of international law (Arend);
  nationalism (Roshwald); assessing imperialism (Kinsey); self-strengthening and
  responses to European power (Wong); decolonization (Duara); genocide (Levene);
  communism and fascism (Strayer).
- **Part IV — World Regions** (ch. 18–23): the Middle East (Voll); East Asia
  (Selden); Latin America (Charlip); Africa (Cooper); the United States (Tyrrell);
  the economic history of the Pacific (Frost).

## Volume Synthesis Note

**The argument.** CWH VII Book 1 advances a single organizing thesis across its
twenty-three chapters: that since ~1750 humanity has lived through an **uneven,
world-altering transition** built on three intertwined dynamics named in the title —
**production** (the [[fossil-fuel-energy-transition|fossil-fuel transition]],
[[demographic-transition|exploding population]], and [[industrialization]]),
**destruction** ([[world-wars-era|world wars]], [[genocide]], and the environmental
damage of the [[anthropocene|Anthropocene]]), and **connection** (globalization). The
editors mark 1750 as a watershed (the first global war, the
[[atlantic-revolutions|Atlantic revolutions]], the long victory of sedentary states
over nomads, demographic and economic take-off) while insisting the **twentieth
century — especially the post-1950 [[great-acceleration|Great Acceleration]] — is the
most distinctive phase of all.** The volume is, above all, the wiki's entry into the
**Anthropocene** and the four periods (21 [[long-19th-century|Long 19th Century]],
22 [[world-wars-era|World Wars Era]], 23 [[cold-war|Cold War]], 24
[[contemporary|Contemporary]]) it previously had no coverage of.

**What it adds to the wiki.** This is the single most consequential ingest yet for the
**post-1750 world**, the collection's largest standing gap. It builds the **four
missing period pages** and the load-bearing structural pages of modernity — the
[[anthropocene|Anthropocene]], the [[great-acceleration|Great Acceleration]], the
[[fossil-fuel-energy-transition|fossil-fuel transition]], [[industrialization]], the
[[agricultural-transformation-modern|modern agricultural transformation]], the
[[demographic-transition]], [[population-politics]], and
[[disease-in-modern-history|the modern history of disease]] (anchored by the
[[smallpox-eradication|eradication of smallpox]]). It supplies the era's politics —
[[nationalism]], [[imperialism]], [[self-strengthening]], [[decolonization]],
[[international-law]], [[genocide]], and [[communism-and-fascism]] — and **six modern
regional surveys** ([[middle-east-modern|Middle East]], [[east-asia-modern|East Asia]],
[[latin-america-modern|Latin America]], [[africa-modern|Africa]], the
[[united-states|United States]], and the [[transpacific-economy|transpacific
economy]]). It adds the standing **transition pages** for 1789, 1914, 1945, and 1991,
and resolves the [[world-health-organization|WHO]] and [[environmentalism]] as
first-class pages.

**As sequel to CWH VI.** Volume VII is the terminal volume of the Cambridge World
History and the direct continuation of [[bentley-cwh-v6-2015|CWH VI]]. The two share a
**deep methodological commitment: the late-and-contingent [[great-divergence|Great
Divergence]]** and a sustained anti-[[eurocentrism|Eurocentrism]]. CWH VI argued the
gap between European and Asian cores opened late and rested on "coal and North
America"; CWH VII carries the story forward and shows the divergence **partially
reversing** — Sugihara's multipolar, labor-intensive [[industrialization]] diffusing
fastest of all after 1950, and the [[east-asia-modern|East Asian]] and
[[transpacific-economy|transpacific]] resurgence recentering the world economy
(China surpassing the US as the largest energy user in 2010). The wiki's
[[industrious-revolution|industrious revolution]] thesis thus runs as a continuous
thread from CWH VI's early-modern households to CWH VII's industrial Asia.

**Cross-volume tensions to track.** (1) **"Globalization" as a category.** The volume
is unusually self-critical about it — Cooper (Africa) and several others treat it as
uneven, contingent, and reversible rather than a unilinear telos; this sharpens the
caution the wiki attached to [[proto-globalization]]. (2) **The Great Divergence
remains open** at [[great-divergence]]: CWH VII reinforces the late/contingent case
but partly relies on Allen's high-wage thesis, which belongs to the early/institutional
camp. (3) **Imperialism vs. nation state.** Kinsey (with Burbank & Cooper) insists
empire, not the [[nationalism|nation state]], was the dominant modern political form —
a useful corrective to the nation-centric framing of the rest of the volume and of
much of the wiki. (4) **The military-revolution and Anthropocene-dating debates** are
recorded without adjudication (see [[military-revolution-thesis]], [[anthropocene]]).

**A note on the source itself.** The collection file labeled "Vol 7 (Complete) Parts 1
& 2" in fact contains **only Book 1, "Structures, Spaces, and Boundary Making."** Book
2, "Shared Transformations?" — urbanization, migration, family and sexuality, religion,
science, music, sport, the commodities (rubber, drugs, the automobile), and the
"moments" chapters (the Atlantic revolutions in depth, the Cold War, 1989) — is
**absent**, exactly as Book 2 of CWH VI was. This is the volume's chief gap: the wiki
gained the structural, political, and regional architecture of the modern world but
still lacks the social-cultural and event-level texture, and several discrete events
(both world wars, the Russian and Chinese revolutions, the Holocaust, the Great
Depression, the major colonial and Cold War wars) have no dedicated pages. These are
flagged in the period pages' Collection Coverage notes and remain the top sourcing
priority for periods 21–24.
