# Flynn, *Country Squire in the White House* (1940)

John T. Flynn's *Country Squire in the White House* (Doubleday, Doran, 1940) is
the first of his three anti-Roosevelt books — preceding
[[flynn-as-we-go-marching-1944|*As We Go Marching* (1944)]] and
[[flynn-roosevelt-myth-1948|*The Roosevelt Myth* (1948)]] — and the opening
document of what became Old Right Roosevelt revisionism (see
[[roosevelt-and-new-deal-revisionism]]). Published in May 1940 against the
third-term campaign, it is, in Flynn's own framing, "not a biography... It is an
attempt to explain the New Deal in terms of the man who sponsored it": a
character study arguing that [[roosevelt-franklin-d|Franklin D. Roosevelt]] was
a Hudson Valley "country squire" — an amiable, well-born amateur without settled
economic convictions — whose [[new-deal|New Deal]] was therefore improvisation
governed by politics rather than a program.

## Argument and Evidentiary Character

The book's method is character explanation: "It is an attempt to explain the
New Deal in terms of the man who sponsored it" ("A Warning," dated 23 May
1940). Its recurring devices:

- **The "tide" theory.** FDR's rise is credited to impersonal political waves
  (the 1910 anti-Taft wave, the Wilson wave, the 1932 anti-Hoover wave) rather
  than talent: "there was no special reason why he should be named to the
  Senate." The famous 1911 Sheehan fight is re-credited to Edward M. Sheppard
  and the Osbornes, FDR "thrust to the front for the value of his name."
- **The mythmaking frame.** The book opens with the Coolidge myth ("no such
  person ever lived as that strong, silent combination of homespun wisdom and
  superhuman cunning known as Calvin Coolidge") to relativize any great-man
  reading of the presidency, citing W. A. White's *A Puritan in Babylon* as
  the corrective model.
- **Personality, not ideas.** "He was not a thinker... What he knew he picked
  up mostly in conversation"; "Roosevelt thinks in terms of personalities."
  The recurring formula is FDR being "sold" ideas in "a few brief
  conversations" — the Warren gold theory, the Cummings court-packing plan,
  the 50,000-plane program.
- **Attribution stripping.** FDR's famous phrases and speeches are
  systematically reassigned: "Happy Warrior" to Judge Proskauer, "New Deal" to
  Moley, "brain trust" to the NYT's James Kieran, the first inaugural to
  "several minds — Roosevelt himself contributed little or nothing. He was the
  actor who read it." The 1932 acceptance-speech anecdote (FDR beginning
  Howe's rival draft unread, then switching to Moley's) is sourced to Moley's
  *After Seven Years*.
- **Platform-vs-practice juxtaposition.** Chapter III quotes FDR's 1932
  statements at length from Rosenman's *Public Papers* Vol. I with page
  citations — anti-dole, anti-deficit ("Stop the deficits"), anti-regimentation,
  25% spending-cut pledge — set against the post-1933 record.

**Sourcing quality is mixed and weaker than *The Roosevelt Myth*.** The
best-documented material: the Rosenman *Public Papers* quotations (paged),
Moley's *After Seven Years*, the James Roosevelt earnings figures from an
authorized *Collier's* interview (20 Aug 1938), Flynn's own *Harper's* piece
on the Social Security reserve (Feb 1939), and one first-person footnote
("Related to the author by the late Senator William E. Borah" — the "I
experimented with gold and that was a flop. Why shouldn't I experiment a
little with silver?" anecdote). Much of "The White House, Inc." (Ch. VIII) —
Eleanor Roosevelt's estimated ~$1.2M in earnings, Elliott's Hearst salary, the
$2.5M family total — is bare assertion or attributed to unnamed congressmen
and an unnamed agent's flyer. Pre-1933 biographical detail leans on Lindley,
Ludwig, and Moley rather than Flynn's own reporting. Internal slips flagged:
the "Happy Warrior" speech once dated 1914 (elsewhere correctly 1924);
inconsistent Consolidated Automatic Merchandising promotional figures;
unemployment cited variously as 11 and 15–16 million for different moments
without clean labeling.

## Key Claims (attributed Flynn 1940)

- **The country-squire thesis.** FDR as heir of his father's Hyde Park
  squire role: amiable, well-born, credulous, "vacillating, indecisive, but
  impulsive," with no economic convictions — hence a New Deal of politics and
  improvisation. His 1920s business ventures ("six or seven of these
  promotions, only one of which turned out well" — Consolidated Automatic
  Merchandising, International Germanic Co., a dirigible line) show "an eager,
  credulous mind"; his American Construction Council presidency (industry
  self-regulation, 1922) is read as "the very essence of the NRA" avant la
  lettre.
- **Governorship as preview.** NY's $15M surplus (end 1930) became a $90M
  deficit by end 1932 while FDR "roundly denounc[ed] Hoover, who was doing the
  same thing for the nation"; the ignored Moses report before the Bank of
  United States failure; four months' delay over Sheriff Farley's tin box;
  Walker tried only after the nomination was secured.
- **1933 continuity.** The bank-closing orders "were in no sense different
  from those that had been prepared by Ogden Mills"; what changed was mood,
  not substance — "the immensity of the disaster had, without changing the
  problem, opened the road for the new President to do almost anything he
  chose to do."
- **NRA genesis in business, not the Brain Trust.** The codes descend from
  Chamber of Commerce planning (Harriman committee Feb 1931, Litchfield
  Share-the-Work 1932) and the Wagner-conference draft ("I have seen that
  bill, and it contains the germ of everything, save the licensing clause");
  codes written by their industries (Steel Code by the Iron & Steel
  Institute) made "the beginning of the corporate state, only we called the
  corporatives codes"; struck down not 5–4 but unanimously.
- **The "Big Gun."** $22bn of borrowed relief/recovery money "without a
  single penny of taxes," Congress's abdication of itemized appropriation,
  and clientelist dependence of governors and mayors on the President explain
  the 1936 landslide; the spending rationale evolved from crisis relief to
  pump-priming to "the deficit was not a deficit; it was an asset."
- **Social Security resisted, not championed.** Flynn's emphatic contrarian
  claim: FDR "held it up" — refusing Wagner's 1934 bill, temporizing ("The
  time is not ripe for old-age pensions yet"), junking his own committee's
  plan, and accepting a reserve-fund design he and Morgenthau later admitted
  was wrong. Likewise the Black 30-hour bill: "one reason he jumped to the
  support of the NRA was to defeat the first minimum-wage and maximum-hour
  bill."
- **The war turn (May 1940).** From the October 1937 recession FDR "has been
  playing with this subject," and defense is the one spending category
  conservatives will not resist; the prediction: the President has reached
  "the tragic point where the only thing that can save his regime is to take
  the country off into a war hysteria."
- **Not a dictator — a paver of the way.** "Roosevelt is no dictator... He
  has none of the blazing certainty... that marks, for instance, a mind like
  Hitler's or Mussolini's"; but he "has the stuff that paves the way for
  dictators," leaving "breaks and cracks" for a more ruthless successor.
- **Closing indictment.** "Seven years after he took office there are eleven
  million unemployed, private investment is dead, the farm problem is
  precisely where he found it... The cost of all this has been twenty-two
  billion dollars, all yet to be paid. If it has all happened that way, it is
  because Franklin D. Roosevelt is that way."
