# Rosalind Franklin: The Dark Lady of DNA (Maddox, 2002)

**Source page for Brenda Maddox, *Rosalind Franklin: The Dark Lady of DNA* (first published HarperCollins 2002).**

This biography provides the definitive corrective account of physical chemist Rosalind Franklin (1920–1958). Maddox reconstructs Franklin's life from her childhood in London through her scientific training at Cambridge, her carbon research in wartime England and post-war Paris, her critical work on DNA structure at King's College London, and her final years researching Tobacco Mosaic Virus (TMV) and RNA at Birkbeck College. The book serves as a major historiographical correction, showing that Franklin was not a hostile helper hoarding her data, but an exceptionally skilled experimentalist whose data and X-ray photographs (particularly Photo 51) were accessed without her consent and proved vital for Watson and Crick's double-helix model.

## Methodological Notes

- Extensive use of Franklin's personal letters and correspondence with family and colleagues.
- Direct examination of laboratory notebooks and reports (BCURA, King's College London, Birkbeck College).
- Interviews and correspondence with key participants: Francis Crick, James Watson, Maurice Wilkins, Raymond Gosling, Aaron Klug, Vittorio Luzzati, Donald Caspar, and Franklin's family.
- Reconciles the timeline of structural discoveries to show her independent progress toward the double helix.

## Key Contributions

- **Corrective Narrative:** Corrects the "Rosy" caricature in Watson's *The Double Helix* by showing her scientific brilliance, warm friendships, and love of travel and the outdoors.
- **DNA Contribution:** Documents the precise parameters she discovered (A and B forms, hydration states, helical structure, phosphate backbone on the outside) and how these reached Watson and Crick.
- **Coal and Carbon Science:** Highlights her earlier pioneering work on the micro-structure of coals, distinguishing graphitizing and non-graphitizing carbons.
- **Virus Crystallography:** Details her later highly successful work at Birkbeck, establishing the helical structure of TMV and starting structural work on the polio virus.
