# The Cambridge History of War, Vol. 2: War and the Medieval World (2020)

The medieval volume, edited by **Anne Curry** and **David A. Graff** — a global, comparative
military history of the millennium c. 600–1500, consciously written against the older
Europe-centred tradition that "began with Sir Charles Oman" in the 1880s.

## Key grounded claims used in this pass

- The infantry-revolution thesis: "massed infantry determined the outcome of all major battles" —
  Courtrai 1302, Bannockburn 1314, Crécy/Poitiers/Agincourt, the Swiss at Granson/Murten/Nancy
  (Curry, p. 350, citing Clifford Rogers).
- Crécy campaign army: Edward III "raising an army of 14,000–15,000, including 8,000 foot, of whom
  the majority were longbowmen" (Curry, p. 371); French armies "amorphous and poorly coordinated
  between the different types of troops, which helps to explain their vulnerability" (p. 370).
- First Crusade logistics: "about 4,000 km from Paris to Jerusalem"; by Christmas 1097 at Antioch
  "the army was starving"; at Ma'arra (Jan 1099) "some crusaders resorted to cannibalism"; Western
  (English/Genoese) fleets the key resupply (France, pp. 272–73).
- Dorylaeum (1 July 1097): a vanguard of "perhaps 2,500 knights" and a rearguard of "3,500 some
  5 km behind," ambushed by Turkish horse-archers (France, p. 282).
- Steppe operational logistics, via the Strategikon and al-Jahiz: the Turkish horseman "changes
  to another [horse] without dismounting"; a 762 CE Uighur raid fielded "4,000 horsemen [with]
  40,000 horses" (de la Vaissière, ch. 6).
- Mongol decimal army (minghan/tümen), multi-column invasion so "the enemy lost the opportunity to
  coordinate," and siege via "detachments of engineers from north China" (Hautala, ch. 11).
