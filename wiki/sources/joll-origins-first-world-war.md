# The Origins of the First World War (James Joll)

James Joll's *The Origins of the First World War* (1984) is the classic scholarly synthesis of the most
debated causal question in modern history: **why did Europe go to war in 1914?** Rather than argue a
single thesis, Joll organizes the problem **thematically**, laying out each order of explanation and
weighing it — which makes the book the wiki's anchor for the **[[causes-of-the-first-world-war|origins
controversy]]**. Thirteenth source of **Gap 1 (Modern)**.

## Structure of the Argument

Joll works through successive layers of causation:

1. **The July Crisis (1914)** — the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand at Sarajevo and the
   five-week chain of ultimatums, mobilizations and alliance obligations that followed; the question of
   contingency versus inevitability.
2. **The alliance system** — the Triple Alliance and Triple Entente, and whether they preserved or
   destabilized the balance of power.
3. **Militarism and the arms race** — the naval rivalry, the cult of the offensive, and **mobilization
   timetables** (the Schlieffen Plan) that compressed decision time.
4. **The primacy of domestic politics** — Eckart Kehr's and Fritz Fischer's argument that **internal
   social tensions** (the German *Sammlungspolitik*, Russian instability) drove external aggression: war
   as an escape from domestic crisis.
5. **Imperialism and economic rivalry** — colonial and commercial competition (Weltpolitik) as background
   pressure.
6. **The "mood of 1914"** — the harder-to-measure domain of public opinion, social-Darwinist and
   nationalist ideas, and the willingness of elites and publics to accept war.

Joll's method is to insist that **no single layer suffices**: the war's origins lie in the interaction of
the immediate crisis with deep structural and ideological conditions.

## Significance

The book defines the wiki's treatment of WWI causation and supplies the framework for the
**[[causes-of-the-first-world-war|origins controversy]]**, including the **Fischer thesis** on German
responsibility that reopened the question in the 1960s. It anchors the war-origins half of the
[[world-wars-era|World Wars Era]] and complements [[hobsbawm-age-of-empire|Hobsbawm's]] account of the
slide to 1914.
