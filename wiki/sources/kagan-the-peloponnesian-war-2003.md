# Donald Kagan, *The Peloponnesian War* (2003)

The standard modern single-volume narrative history of the Peloponnesian War (431–404 BCE). Kagan draws on Thucydides as the indispensable contemporary source but supplies the missing final seven years, integrates later ancient evidence (Diodorus, Plutarch, inscriptions, archaeology), and provides a clear strategic and political analysis focused on why the war happened, how it was fought at the grand strategic level, the critical decisions, and why Athens ultimately lost.

## Historiography / impact note

Kagan is the leading modern authority on the Peloponnesian War, known for his multi-volume earlier work and this accessible synthesis. He stresses that the war was not inevitable in the way some structural readings suggest, but resulted from specific decisions by identifiable leaders. He is critical of Thucydides on certain points (e.g., Pericles' strategy, the Sicilian expedition) while admiring his analytical power. The book is narrative-driven, focusing on the interplay of intelligence, chance, personality, and policy rather than purely socio-economic or "face of battle" perspectives.

Strength for the wiki: provides the authoritative political/strategic backbone and detailed event chronology for all Peloponnesian War pages. Excellent for causal analysis, war aims, and the role of individuals (counter to purely structural views).

Limitations: Less emphasis on the actual fighting mechanics (see Hanson, Sabin) or modeling (Sabin); some scholars debate Kagan's interpretations of motives and the "inevitability" question.

## Primary Claims from the Text (Grounded Extractions)

All claims are verbatim-grounded or close paraphrase from Kagan’s text with locators (chapter titles, Thucydides citations as used by Kagan, or specific sections). Organized by the book’s structure. Claims emphasize decisions, individuals, resources, contingency, and the war’s departure from traditional Greek norms.

### Causes, Road to War, and Archidamian Phase (Parts One–Three)
- **Theban attack on Plataea (431 BCE) and outbreak**: “On a cloudy night early in March 431 over three hundred Thebans sneaked into Plataea guided by Nauclides... The Thebans expected the unprepared Plataeans to surrender peacefully... the Plataeans began to mount a resistance... Just before dawn they set upon the Thebans... A heavy rain had by now begun to fall, and the Plataean women and the towns slaves... threw stones and tiles at the invaders... Many were caught and killed, and before long the survivors were forced to surrender.” Plataeans executed 180 captives “regardless. By traditional standards of Greek warfare this was an atrocity, the first of many...” Athenians reinforced with 480 men + 110 women. (THE THEBAN ATTACK ON PLATAEA (431); Ch. 6)
- **Archidamus and Spartan strategy**: Archidamus delayed at Oenoe hoping to prevent war; invasion of Attica occurred late May. Spartans invoked “freedom for the Greeks” (2.8.4) but expected a short war (max 3 years); Archidamus alone understood Athens’ walls, navy, and empire allowed indefinite resistance. True motive was fear of Athenian power. (WAR AIMS AND RESOURCES (432–431); Ch. 5–6)
- **Plague (430–429 BCE)**: Killed ~4,400 hoplites + 300 cavalry + perhaps one-third of the population. Crowding from Pericles’ evacuation policy made Athens especially vulnerable. “after the second invasion... the Athenians... changed their minds and held Pericles responsible...” Religious interpretations and “war is a violent teacher” (3.82.2) led to terror executions. (THE PLAGUE (430–429); Ch. 7)
- **Leadership transition and Cleon**: Pericles died after re-election but weakened; “no other could hold consistent policy.” Cleon (from trade/manufacturing class, not aristocracy) emerged as most violent advocate of war and empire; “the most violent of the citizens” (Thuc. 3.36.6). (PERICLES’ LAST DAYS; THE “NEW POLITICIANS”)
- **Pylos/Sphacteria (425 BCE) turning point**: Demosthenes used light-armed and terrain; 292 Spartiates (including 120 full citizens) surrendered — “the most unexpected event in the war.” Spartans offered peace; Athenians (led by Cleon) grasped for more and rejected. Cleon honored; new tribute assessment tripled. (PYLOS AND SPHACTERIA; Ch. 12)
- **Brasidas’ Thracian campaign**: Innovative use of persuasion, night attacks, and mixed forces; captured Amphipolis by surprise. Killed with Cleon at Amphipolis 422; Amphipolitans adopted him as founder-hero. (BRASIDAS’ THRACIAN CAMPAIGN)
- **Peace of Nicias (421 BCE)**: Sworn after deaths of Brasidas and Cleon removed chief obstacles. Status-quo ante with exceptions (Thebes kept Plataea; Athens kept Nisaea). Sparta failed to compel allies to return Amphipolis or demolish Panactum; peace “unsatisfactory” in manner and substance. (THE COMING OF PEACE)
- **Mantinea (418 BCE) and uneasy peace**: Coalition (Argos, Mantinea, Elis, Athens) vs. Sparta. Terrain and Spartan discipline decisive; “greatest battle... for a very long while among the Hellenes.” Aftermath: Spartan caution increased; Athens pursued aggressive policy again. (THE BATTLE OF MANTINEA; Ch. 18)

### Sicilian Expedition and Disaster (Part Five)
- **Decision for Sicily (415 BCE)**: Segesta appeal + fear of Syracuse controlling Sicily + Athenian desire for empire. Initial 60 ships (limited aims); Nicias’ “trick” escalation to 100 triremes + 5,000 hoplites produced “vast armada” with unlimited goals. Alcibiades advocated grand strategy (“capture both Sicily and Carthage”); Nicias warned against new empire while old unsecured. (THE DECISION; Ch. 20)
- **Home front — Hermae and Mysteries**: Mutilation of herms and parody of Mysteries produced panic, informers, and conviction of Alcibiades (in absentia). “It seemed to be an omen... and to have been done on behalf of a conspiracy.” (THE HOME FRONT)
- **Early operations and first battle (Anapus, 415)**: Lamachus killed; tactical success but no strategic result. Cavalry shortage hurt pursuit. (THE FIRST ATTACK ON SYRACUSE)
- **Gylippus and reversal (414–413)**: Gylippus arrived with small force; built counterwall across Athenian lines. “the enemy cavalry and javelin throwers gave them the advantage over the Athenian hoplites. The cavalry proved to be decisive...” Plemmyrium lost; “almost all of Sicily... now joined with and came to the aid of the Syracusans.” (THE BESIEGERS BESIEGED)
- **Demosthenes’ night attack on Epipolae and final defeat**: Initial success turned to chaos in moonlight; 2,000–2,500 killed. “the greatest disaster yet suffered by the Athenians.” Eclipse delayed retreat; final naval defeat in Great Harbor; land retreat ended at Assinarus. “All that remained of the great Athenian army was destroyed...” (DEFEAT AND DESTRUCTION; Ch. 25)
- **Kagan’s judgment on Nicias**: “Nicias played a more central role” than Alcibiades in the disaster. Lethargic, overconfident, careless leadership; refused withdrawal after Epipolae out of fear for reputation and safety. “a series of errors of commission and omission.” (A JUDGMENT ON NICIAS)

### Ionian War, Oligarchy, and Final Campaigns (Part Six)
- **Post-Sicily recovery and Persian factor**: Athenians showed resilience; rebuilt fleet. Sparta gained Persian subsidies but faced unreliable allies and Tissaphernes’ divide-and-bleed policy. Lichas forced new treaty conceding Asian Greeks to Persia. (AFTER THE DISASTER; WAR IN THE AEGEAN)
- **Oligarchic coup (411 BCE)**: War weariness + fear after Sicily enabled plot at Samos and Athens. Four Hundred seized power; terror followed. Fleet at Samos remained democratic, recalled Alcibiades, and opposed the regime. Euboea revolt caused “greater panic than ever before.” Moderates overthrew extremists; brief “Five Thousand” regime praised by Thucydides. (THE REVOLUTIONARY MOVEMENT; THE COUP)
- **Hellespont campaigns**: Cynossema (411) — Thrasybulus’ speed and Peloponnesian disorder gave Athenian victory. Cyzicus (410) — Alcibiades lured Mindarus; full encirclement; famous Spartan letter “The ships are lost. Mindarus is dead. The men are starving.” (THE WAR IN THE AEGEAN)
- **Lysander and Aegospotami (405 BCE)**: Lysander’s personal patronage network, Ephesus base, and collaboration with Cyrus (pay raised to 4 obols) proved decisive. Athenian fleet caught on beach at Aegospotami; “all but ten ships” lost. “they had lost the war.” (THE EMERGENCE OF LYSANDER; THE BATTLE OF AEGOSPOTAMI)
- **Surrender and aftermath (404 BCE)**: Long Walls demolished to flute music. Harmosts and decarchies imposed by Lysander personally. Terms included ancestral constitution and following Sparta. Thirty Tyrants installed; democracy restored 403 after civil war. Sparta proved unable to govern an empire. (THE FATE OF ATHENS; THE RESULTS)

### Historiography and Kagan’s Method
- Thucydides indispensable but incomplete (ends 411); Diodorus valuable for later years via Ephorus and Oxyrhynchus historian. Plutarch provides character, inscriptions, and lost sources despite biographical purpose. Kagan supplements with modern scholarship, inscriptions, and archaeology while focusing on decisions, individuals, and contingency. War reversed democratic trends, intensified faction, and violated traditional hoplite norms — “a fundamental departure from the traditional character of the Greek way of warfare.”
- Overall thesis: Not inevitable; specific choices by leaders (Pericles, Cleon, Alcibiades, Nicias, Brasidas, Lysander) and miscalculations of resources and resolve determined outcome. Athens’ advantages (navy, empire, manpower) squandered by overreach (Sicily) and internal division.

## Consolidated Volume Synthesis Note

Kagan’s single-volume narrative completes Thucydides and supplies the authoritative modern political/strategic account of the entire Peloponnesian War. It stresses contingency, leadership, and the clash of two different Greek societies (democratic-imperial Athens vs. oligarchic-land-power Sparta). The Sicilian expedition emerges as the decisive overreach that shifted momentum; the Ionian War and Lysander’s mastery of Persian money and personal networks delivered the final blow. The war brutalized Greek conduct, exhausted resources, and produced no stable new order — Sparta proved a poor hegemon. This source provides the backbone chronology, causal analysis, and individual portraits needed for all Peloponnesian-related pages in the wiki. Complements Hanson (fighting experience) and Sabin (modeling) perfectly.

**Pericles' leadership under fire, last days, death (430-429 BCE):**
- Athenians voted (against Pericles) to send ambassadors to Sparta for peace. Pericles re-elected general spring 429 but "had only a few months to live." Disease "lingering, 'using up his body slowly and undermining the loftiness of his spirit'" (Plutarch, Pericles 38.1). Friends and family (sister, legitimate sons) died in plague; obtained citizenship for son by Aspasia. (PERICLES UNDER FIRE; PEACE NEGOTIATIONS; CHAPTER EIGHT Pericles’ Last Days (429))
- In final days, hearing friends praise his victories: "they had not spoken of the greatest and most beautiful thing. For no one of the Athenians now alive has put on mourning because of me" (Plutarch, Pericles 38.4). "In September 429, two years and six months after the outbreak of the war, he died." Last recorded speech: characteristics of statesman “To know what must be done and to be able to explain it; to love ones country and to be incorruptible” (2.60.5). (THE DEATH OF PERICLES; Ch. 8)
- "The death of Pericles deprived Athens of a leader with unique qualities. He was a military man and strategist of stature, but even more a brilliant politician of the rarest talents. He could decide on a policy and persuade the Athenians to adopt and remain committed to it, to restrain them from overly ambitious undertakings, and to encourage them when they lost confidence." No other could hold consistent policy. (Ch. 8)

**Political/leadership changes and Cleon:**
- "The most notable of his attackers was Cleon, who had opposed Pericles for several years. Cleon belonged to a new class of politicians in Athens: not aristocrats but rich men whose wealth came from trade and manufacturing, rather than from the traditional source, land." Aristophanes mocked him as tanner; "voice 'roared like a torrent'"; Thucydides "the most violent of the citizens" (3.36.6); "harsh and bullying." Aristotle: Cleon "seems to have corrupted the people more than anyone by his attacks; he was the first to shout while speaking in the assembly, first to use abusive language there, first to hitch up his skirts [and move about] while addressing the people..." (PERICLES UNDER FIRE; Ch. 7)
- "The death of Pericles brought a great change to Athenian political life. 'Those who followed him,' said Thucydides, 'were more equal to one another...'" (CHAPTER NINE THE “NEW POLITICIANS” IN ATHENS; Ch. 9)

**Corcyra and minimal deterrence (pre-war/early):**
- Corcyra sought defensive alliance (epimachia) with Athens, first such in Greek history. "The chances are great that the innovative Pericles was its author." Athenians sent only 10 ships (then +20), "of more symbolic than military value"; orders "not to fight unless the Corinthian fleet sailed against Corcyra itself... 'These orders were given in order not to break the treaty' (1.45.3)." Policy of "minimal deterrence." At Sybota (Sept 433), Athenians intervened when Corcyraeans routed but allowed Corinthians to withdraw after reinforcements arrived. "Operationally, the Athenians’ effort had succeeded: Corcyra and their fleet had been saved. But the policy of 'minimal deterrence' was a strategic failure..." Corinthians now determined to bring Sparta in. (ENTER ATHENS (433-432); THE BATTLE OF SYBOTA; Ch. 3)

**Strategy, resources, early shifts:**
- Athenian advantages: ~300+ triremes + allies (Corcyra, Chios, Lesbos); annual income ~ (cut off but "vast advantage"); hoplites 13k fighting age +16k reserves vs Peloponnesian ~60k estimate. Pericles: "remain quiet, take care of their fleet, refrain from trying to extend their empire in wartime..." (2.65.7). Raids (Methone, etc.) intensified but not full shift. (WAR AIMS AND RESOURCES; Ch. 5; EPIDAURUS; Ch. 7)
- Phormio's naval successes in Gulf of Corinth (20 Athenian ships vs larger Peloponnesian forces): used weather, maneuver, tight circle to disorder enemy; captured 12 ships. Later Brasidas sent as adviser to Cnemus. Attempt on Piraeus via Megara failed due to Spartan timidity. (SPARTAN ACTION IN THE NORTHWEST; ENTER PHORMIO; THE SPARTANS ATTACK PIRAEUS; Ch. 8)
- Spartan turn to sea and Persia after land strategy failed; intercepted envoys executed. (Ch. 7-8)
- By end of 429/8: Attica free of invasion; reserve fund down to ~1,450 talents usable. "The original strategy for victory had failed, and the Athenians had as yet formulated no substitute." (Ch. 8)

**Mytilene/rebellion precursors and terror elements:**
- Lesbos (Mytilene chief city) provided ships to Athenian expeditions (e.g., 50 with Pericles to Epidaurus area). Rebellion chapter opens with empire strains. (EPIDAURUS; Ch. 7; start Ch. 9)
- "It was becoming clear that the Spartans needed a new offensive strategy..." Embassy to Persia; terror reprisals (executions). War party (Cleon et al.) active after Pericles out. (Ch. 7-8)

**Primary Claims from the Text (Grounded Extractions) — Range 5 (lines 17001-21000): Sicily: decision, home front, first attacks, early siege (416–414 BCE)**

**Causes, appeals, and initial decision:**
- Impetus from Sicily: Segesta (aided by Leontini) appealed vs Selinus + Syracuse (depouplated Leontini). Main argument: “If the Syracusans, who had depopulated Leontini, were not punished and, after destroying their allies who were still left, took power over all of Sicily, there was the risk that at some time in the future, as Dorians to Dorians and as kinsmen and colonists of the Peloponnesians, they might send them help with a great force and help destroy the power of Athens” (6.6.2). Also traditional ties + cost-bearing offer. “The truest explanation” (Thucydides): “they longed for the rule of the whole island” (6.6.1). Athenians portrayed as “ignorant of the magnitude of the island and of the number of its inhabitants both Greek and barbarian” (6.6.1). Kagan: previous 427-424 fleet (up to 12k Athenians) had traveled Sicily; “The Athenians’ consideration of Segesta’s request... demonstrates little of what could be characterized as reckless exuberance.” Ambassadors checked money (Segestans deceived with 60 talents coined silver = month’s pay for 60 ships). Assembly March 415 voted 60 ships under Alcibiades, Nicias, Lamachus “to settle affairs in Sicily in whatever way they judged best for Athens” (6.8.2). “Nicias was chosen as general for the expedition ‘against his will, because he thought that the city had made a wrong decision’ (6.8.4).” Size identical to 424 force = limited goals initially. “A surprise attack directly on the city from the sea might succeed with only sixty ships... In the worst case, if the entire expedition was destroyed, it would have to be considered a great misfortune but not a strategic disaster.”

**Nicias vs Alcibiades debate and escalation (planning):**
- Second assembly days later for equipping. Nicias sought reconsideration: Spartans “still recovering”; “We must not... reach out for another empire until we have made the one we have secure” (6.10.5). Carthage had failed to conquer Sicily. Allies “a barbaric people.” Syracuse not dangerous if united (“if the Syracusans were in control it is not likely that an empire would attack another empire” 6.11.3). Personal attack: Alcibiades endangering state “for his own glory and profit.” Alcibiades: Sicily “seriously unstable”; diplomacy + Sicels; Spartans “without hope or initiative” (no fleet threat beyond past invasions); must aid allies (“We must assist them, for we have given our oath” 6.18.1); “by always coming eagerly to the aid of those who called upon us, whether Greek or barbarian” (6.18.2); victory in Sicily = “control of all Greece”; Athens “active by its very nature” (“A city that is active would quickly be destroyed by a change to passivity” 6.18.7). Nicias, realizing failure, “thought he might change their minds if he exaggerated the size of the force it required” (6.19.2) — proposed 100 triremes, 5,000 hoplites + light (omitted cavalry despite later emphasis on enemy horse/grain). Assembly gave “full powers.” Kagan verdict: “Against his intentions, Nicias managed in the second assembly to convert an expedition of moderate size with limited goals and liability into a vast armada burdened with great ambitions and expectations, whose failure could bring disaster... Without his intervention they would undoubtedly have sailed against Sicily in 415, but there could have been no chance of their embarking on a major catastrophe.”

**Home front — sacrilege and panic:**
- Spring 415 mood: “A passion came upon all of them equally to sail off. The older men thought that either they would conquer or at least that such a great force could not come to harm... The mass of the people and the soldiers hoped to get money at the moment and to make an addition to their empire from which they would have a never-ending source of income” (6.24.3). June 7: Hermae faces/phalluses smashed city-wide overnight. “It seemed to be an omen for the voyage and to have been done on behalf of a conspiracy to make a revolution and destroy the democracy” (6.27.3). Mysteries parody charges against Alcibiades et al. (house of Pulytion). Investigation: rewards/immunity; council commission. Pythonicus accusation; slave testimony. Alcibiades denied, offered immediate trial (soldiers/sailors away would hurt him); enemies delayed (“Let him sail off now with good luck... When the war is over let him come back here and defend himself” Plutarch Alc. 19.4). Fleet sailed mid-June: “the most expensive and glorious armament coming from a single city with a purely Greek force that put to sea up to that time” (6.31.1). “It looked more like a display of power and wealth before the rest of the Greeks than an expedition against enemies” (6.31.1).
- Witch hunt post-departure: Teucrus (parodists + 18 Hermae attackers; Alcibiades absent from list); Diocleides (false: ~300 in Dionysus theater, extorted, denounced 42 incl. councilors; torture authorized by Peisander; panic, armor worn, council to Acropolis; Diocleides later exposed, executed). Andocides (his hetairia under Euphilitus/Meletus did Hermae). Agariste (Alcibiades + Axiochus + Adeimantus in mysteries). Thessalus formal accuser. Salaminia sent; Alcibiades fled Thurii. Kagan: parodies “privately with no ability or intention to influence anyone”; Hermae “a more serious affair... Organization, planning... politically motivated... It can be no coincidence... just before the expedition... Some Athenians believed the Corinthians were responsible.” Aimed at Nicias’ piety/omens. “The conspirators had no reason to anticipate the confusion the revelations about the mysteries would cause.” “The unexpected additional outrages destroyed whatever chances the bizarre plot may have had for success.” Also “repercussions from Alcibiades’ involvement... would ultimately contribute greatly to its disastrous failure.”

**Early campaigns / first attacks (415):**
- Force at Corcyra: split thirds; Italy landing rebuffed (Taras/Locris no water; Rhegium neutral, only beach/market — “perception of the vast size... seemed that the Athenians had come to conquer... not... to help their allies”). Segesta 30 talents only. Strategy at Rhegium: Nicias minimal (Selinus pay demand, display, home; “they should not endanger the state by spending its own resources” 6.47). Alcibiades: gain cities + Sicels, attack Syracuse/Selinus unless terms. Lamachus: direct Syracuse attack while unprepared (“as soon as possible fight a battle near the city while the city was unprepared and most panic-stricken” 6.49.1); occupy Megara Hyblaea. Lamachus backed Alcibiades. Naxos welcomed; Catana by trick. 10 ships to Syracuse harbor (no fleet); ultimatum/scout. Syracusans (after Corcyra news) debated: Hermocrates correct on intent, urged Carthage/Corinth/Sparta + Italy fleet; Athenagoras denied coming or oligarchic plot claim; unnamed general: prepare envoys anyway. “When they learned that the Athenians had landed at Rhegium, they finally began to take some measures... on the assumption that war was coming swiftly” (6.45). No fleet prepared. Athenians raided; stragglers killed by cavalry (“an omen for the future”).
- First attack: tricked Syracusans to Catana march; landed Anapus south (Olympieum), fortified vs cavalry. Battle: Athenians 8 deep (Argives/Mantineans right, Athenians center, allies left); reserve square. “superior discipline and experience of the Athenians and their allies carried the day.” Rain/thunder broke Syracusans. Pursuit checked by cavalry. “For the Athenians it was a tactical victory without strategic result: Syracuse stood fast... Instead of launching a siege at once, however, the Athenians set up a trophy... and sailed back to Catana.” (Kagan: season + “send to Athens for cavalry” 6.71.2; contemporaries “Nicias-delays”; “by calculating too carefully and delaying and being overly cautious he destroyed the opportunity” Plutarch Nicias 16.8). “Cavalry was essential to the capture of Syracuse. Had it been available from the first, the Syracusans would have been forced to surrender; no outside help could have saved them. The lack of provisions for cavalry is particularly astonishing in that Nicias himself had emphasized its importance...” (6.20.4). He omitted from list and never fixed despite time/Rhegium.

**Syracusan response + Alcibiades at Sparta:**
- Reforms: poorer as hoplites + training; generals 15→3 (Hermocrates) full powers; walls extended, garrisons/palisades; envoys Corinth/Sparta (“make war against the Athenians openly and more persistently” 6.73). Camarina neutral (“since they were allied to both... aid neither” 6.88.1) — size influenced. Some Sicels joined; Etruria ships later, Carthage failed. 
- Alcibiades (fugitive) at Sparta: adapted lifestyle; speech “far from being limited to an assault on Syracuse... meant to take the entire island and more... southern Italy, Carthage... Iberia... rule over the entire Hellenic people” (6.90). “Let no one... believe you are deliberating only about Sicily, for the fate of the Peloponnesus is also at stake” (6.91). Send army + Decelea fort. “The true patriot is not the man who, having lost his own homeland, does not attack it, but the one who tries in every way to recover it” (6.92.4). Kagan: claims “so patently false” on remaining generals; “one of his claims ought to have cast doubt”; “a defeated and hunted man... We must marvel at his boldness and imagination and at the enormity of his bluff.” “He had not yet commanded an Athenian victory... all his plans had produced strategic failures.” Mantinea plan unoriginal repeat of 427-424; expanded force “frighten the Greek cities of the west into neutrality or opposition.”

**Early siege start (414):**
- Cavalry arrived + Sicilian allies (~650 total); seized Epipolae (Syracusans late guarding); Labdalum fort (supplies); Circle at Syce. Northern wall toward Trogilus. Syracusan counterwall (stone/timber + towers). Athenians raided/destroyed it midday (300 hoplites + lights; Nicias/Lamachus); got inside Temenites gate but insufficient to hold; trophy. Then southern wall to Great Harbor. Marsh fighting: Lamachus killed (“The brave and bold Lamachus... died fighting”). Nicias ill. Syracusans briefly took undefended Circle section (repulsed by Nicias’ fire + fleet). Near enclosure; “no longer thought they could win the war, for no help had come... from the Peloponnesus” (6.103.3); peace talks, new generals, treason rumors. Nicias overconfident vs 4 Peloponnesian ships (Gylippus); “Nicias did none of [intercept, complete walls, guard approaches, blockade] , with disastrous results.” Gylippus via Euryalus/Himera (~3k +200 cav from Himera/Selinus/Gela/Sicels); Gongylus slipped in first, reversed surrender talk. Gylippus seized Labdalum, started north counterwall. Nicias moved to Plemmyrium (water/firewood distant; “the crews began to deteriorate”; “dangerously divided Nicias’ forces”).

**Primary Claims from the Text (Grounded Extractions) — Range 6 (lines 21001-25000): Sicily: besiegers besieged, defeat and destruction (414–413 BCE)**

**Gylippus, counterwall, and reversal of the siege:**
- Gylippus continued the counterwall using Athenian stones; challenged to battle. First battle site kept his cavalry out of play; he lost but took blame, winning loyalty. "A new opportunity arose when Gylippus’ counterwall finally met the line of the Athenian wall to Trogilus, forcing Nicias to fight or forfeit all hope of enclosing the city." Second battle in open: "the enemy cavalry and javelin throwers gave them the advantage... The cavalry proved to be decisive, driving the exposed Athenian left flank before it, which caused a general rout"; Athenians saved only by round fort. "The battle brought Gylippus a great strategic victory: the Syracusans built their counterwall across the line of the Athenian siege wall."
- Corinthian ships (Erasinides) arrived unhindered; >2,000 men; "All hope of enclosing Syracuse and starving it into surrender with their present forces was now gone." Gylippus fortified Euryalus pass, recruited allies, sent for more from Sparta/Corinth; Syracusans trained for sea battle.
- (THE DISASTER IN SICILY; Ch. 23–24, THE SIEGE OF SYRACUSE (414))

**Nicias’ letter and Athenian response:**
- Nicias sent letter fall 414: Athenians on defensive; "Gylippus was recruiting reinforcements and planning an attack on the Athenians by land and sea"; situation "beyond repair." Blamed ship/crew deterioration from campaign length and blockade. "must either recall the force that is here or reinforce it with another just as large, infantry and a fleet and a great deal of money" (7.15). Also asked relief due to illness.
- Kagan: "Nicias’ message painted a darker picture than reality justified. The Athenians were still superior at sea... The lion’s share of the responsibility for the situation rested with Nicias’ lethargic, overconfident, and careless leadership." Specific failures: allowed Gylippus and Gongylus through; neglected Epipolae approaches; moved navy/supplies to untenable Plemmyrium; "he could have dried and repaired his ships in rotation".
- Athenians voted reinforcements + keep Nicias; sent Eurymedon ahead, Demosthenes to follow with main force. "They showed constancy and determination to carry through what they had begun, in spite of setbacks." Nicias seen as "talisman of victory" due to character, past success, name (nike); "less than two years after the gods had been insulted... the Athenians refused to excuse from service the one man most beloved of the gods".
- (NICIAS’ LETTER TO ATHENS; THE ATHENIAN RESPONSE; Ch. 23–24)

**Decelea, reinforcements, capture of Plemmyrium:**
- Spartans invaded Attica March 413; Agis fortified Decelea: "Instead of a city Athens became a garrisoned fortress" (7.28.1). Slaves deserted (~20,000, including Laurium mines); livestock taken; supplies rerouted expensively around Sunium. Thracians (hired but late) massacred Mycalessus: "butchered the people, sparing neither old nor young... They also assaulted a boys’ school: 'The children had just come in, and they cut down every one of them'" (7.29.5).
- Peloponnesian reinforcements planned (helots/neodamodeis, Boeotians, Corinthians). Athenians also reinforced.
- Gylippus attacked Plemmyrium with land assault + naval diversion (80 Syracusan triremes). Captured all three forts; Athenians won sea fight but "The loss of Plemmyrium brought bewilderment and discouragement to the army" (7.24.3). Lost sails/tackle for 40 triremes + 3 ships; supplies cut off. "almost all of Sicily... now joined with and came to the aid of the Syracusans".
- (SPARTA TAKES THE OFFENSIVE; THE FORT AT DECELEA; REINFORCEMENTS; THE CAPTURE OF PLEMMYRIUM; Ch. 24)

**Naval battles and Great Harbor:**
- In Corinthian Gulf, Polyanthes reinforced epoteis (catheads); smashed Athenian outriggers in head-on; "For the first time a Peloponnesian fleet had fought a numerically superior Athenian fleet to a standstill." Syracusans adopted thickened catheads + stay-beams for harbor fighting where diekplous/periplous impossible.
- Great Harbor battle (3 days): Syracusans land attack drew attention; sudden re-attack when Athenians eating; javelin boats under oars; "The Athenians were able to escape disaster only by fleeing to safety behind the merchantmen and the palisade." Dolphins sank 2 Syracusan pursuers. 7 Athenian ships sunk; Syracusans dominated harbor.
- (THE BATTLE IN THE GREAT HARBOR; Ch. 24)

**Demosthenes’ night attack on Epipolae:**
- Demosthenes arrived with ~73 ships, ~5,000 hoplites + light troops. Planned immediate assault on counterwall. Night attack (early August): surprised Euryalus garrison, took fort, tore down parts of counterwall. "Gylippus and his troops, dazed by this daring and unexpected tactic, tried to stop the rampaging Athenians".
- Then confusion: Athenians disordered in pursuit; Boeotians rallied; "In the dim light of the moon the advancing Athenians could not tell whether the men running toward them were friends or foes". Dorian paeans (Syracusans + Argives/Corcyraeans) indistinguishable; "Finally, when they had once been thrown into confusion, they attacked one another in many different parts of the battlefield, friends against friends and citizens against fellow citizens... they even came to blows" (7.44.8).
- New arrivals unfamiliar with terrain; many fell off cliffs; Syracusan cavalry finished stragglers at dawn. "between two thousand and twenty-five hundred men were killed, and all hope of a quick victory at Syracuse abandoned." "the greatest disaster yet suffered by the Athenians".
- (THE SECOND ATHENIAN ARMADA; THE NIGHT ATTACK ON EPIPOLAE; RETREAT OR REMAIN?; Ch. 24)

**Eclipse, final naval battle, decision to retreat by land:**
- Demosthenes urged immediate withdrawal while sea superiority held. Nicias resisted: hoped Syracusans short of money (claimed 2000 talents spent + borrowing), internal party for surrender; feared soldiers would accuse him of bribery on return ("that their generals had been bribed to betray them and withdraw"); "he preferred, if he must, to take his chances and meet his own death himself at the hands of the enemy" (7.48.4). Compromise move to healthier base rejected.
- Lunar eclipse 27 Aug 413 (9:41–10:30 p.m.); soothsayer: wait "thrice nine days". Nicias accepted as unfavorable; "refused to discuss further the question of their departure until they waited thrice nine days" (7.50.4). Deserters informed Syracusans.
- Final harbor battle: Syracusans blocked exit with anchored boats + chains + boards. Athenians tried breakout with "iron hands" grapples; Syracusans used hides on prows. Confined waters negated Athenian skill/experience; missiles inaccurate from ships; shouting drowned stroke calls. Routed; fled to shore. Demosthenes proposed second attempt with 60 ships but "the morale of the men had collapsed entirely. They refused the generals’ orders to board the ships again and insisted on endeavoring to escape by land."
- (ECLIPSE; THE FINAL NAVAL BATTLE; Ch. 25)

**The final retreat and destruction:**
- ~40,000 (half soldiers) set out for Catana via Anapus then highlands. "They looked like nothing more than a city, and one of considerable size, sneaking away in flight after being reduced by a siege" (7.75.5). Abandoned sick/wounded; shame over unburied dead.
- Nicias: "You should realize that wherever you settle down you are immediately a city and that there is no other city in Sicily that could easily sustain an attack from you" (7.77.4); "it is men that make up the city and not walls or ships empty of men" (7.77.7).
- Harassed constantly by cavalry/javelin; blocked at Monte Climiti / Cava Castelluccio ravine (Acraean Bald Rock wall); rainstorm interpreted as divine disfavor; fell back.
- Hermocrates' trick (false "Syracusans who had hoped to betray" message) + own fear delayed first night march; another day packing allowed full blocking.
- Demosthenes' division trapped in olive grove near Cacyparis; surrendered ~6,000 on terms ("none of them would be killed..."). Demosthenes tried suicide.
- Nicias' division reached Assinarus; "the Athenians no longer had a plan, only a blind wish to escape and a parching thirst." Discipline collapsed at river; "Since they were forced to go forward in a close mass they fell on top of and trampled one another; some were killed immediately, impaled on their own spears... The Syracusans stood along the opposite bank... threw missiles down... The Peloponnesians also came down and butchered them, especially those in the river. And the water immediately became spoiled, but it was drunk, nonetheless, though it was muddy and full of blood, and most of them even fought over it" (7.84).
- "All that remained of the great Athenian army was destroyed at the Assinarus." Nicias surrendered to Gylippus. ~1,000 survivors from his force reached Catana. Athenians and allies put in stone quarries: half-pint water, pint food daily; 70 days then most sold; survivors 8 months.
- Thucydides: "the greatest action of all those that took place during the war... the most glorious for those who won and the most disastrous for those who were defeated... total destruction... only few of many came back home" (7.87.5-6).
- (THE FINAL RETREAT; THE FATE OF THE ATHENIANS; A JUDGMENT ON NICIAS; Ch. 25)

**Kagan’s judgment on responsibility:**
- "Alcibiades was the author of the Sicilian expedition, but Nicias played a more central role." Nicias' "failed rhetorical trick" in 415 escalated modest force to massive one. Technical error: omitted cavalry. Then "a series of errors of commission and omission": failed to complete single wall circuit; wasted time on dissidents; "did not send a squadron to intercept Gylippus’ arrival"; "did not mount a competent blockade"; "did not fortify and guard Epipolae"; moved to Plemmyrium. After 414, "Nicias refused to withdraw out of fear for his reputation and safety." After Epipolae defeat, "seized on the lunar eclipse as a last chance to avoid the inevitable and let slip away the Athenians’ final opportunity to escape."
- (A JUDGMENT ON NICIAS; Ch. 25)

**Primary Claims from the Text (Grounded Extractions) — Range 7 (lines 25001-29000): After the Sicilian disaster (413-412); War in the Aegean (412-411); Revolutionary Movement (411); Hellespont campaigns and restoration (411-410)**

**Aftermath of Sicily and Spartan naval revival in Aegean:**
- Spartan fleet under Astyochus unites with forces from Caunus; sails to Syme and sets up "a trophy of victory over the twenty ships of Charminus." Athenians (fewer than 70 triremes vs ~90) seek battle to avenge but Astyochus refuses. "With the Peloponnesian fleet united, the xymbouloi conducted their investigation of the charges against Astyochus, ultimately acquitting him and confirming him in office."
- Lichas (Spartan spokesman) denounces prior treaties with Persia to Tissaphernes: “It was scandalous,” he said, “that the King still claimed to rule all the territory that he and his ancestors ruled in the past, for that would mean that all the islands would again be enslaved by him, as well as Thessaly, Locris, and everything as far as Boeotia; instead of freedom the Spartans would bring the Greeks subjugation to the Persian Empire.” “Unless the agreements were improved... the Spartans would not abide by these, nor did he ask for support on such terms” (8.43.3-4). Tissaphernes walks out. (Note: later Lichas participates in third treaty conceding Asia Greeks.)
- Orders from Sparta: move war to Hellespont, to Pharnabazus' satrapy.

**Rebellion at Rhodes (Jan 411) and importance of Euboea:**
- Oligarchs from Rhodes persuade Spartans: 94 ships sail to Camirus; "Together with Lindus and Ialysus, Rhodes went over to the Peloponnesians in January 411." Athenians (75 triremes) arrive too late from Samos. Spartans beach ships mid-Jan to spring. "It was now that the failure of Athens to capture Miletus took its toll."
- Athenians depose Phrynichus and Scironides, replace with Leon and Diomedon; attack Rhodes, defeat Rhodian army, base at Chalce for raids.
- Pedaritus (at Chios) appeals for help; killed in counterattack. Chians "were blockaded still more than before by land and sea and there was great famine there" (8.56.1).
- Spartans ignore urgent Euboea appeal (encouraged by Boeotian capture of Oropus) to try rescue Chios, but retreat to Miletus at sight of Athenians: "seeing that it was no longer possible for them to bring help to Chios without a naval battle" (8.60.3).
- Euboea vital to [[places/athens]]: when entire island revolted later "there was greater panic than ever before. For neither the disaster in Sicily, though it seemed great at the time, nor any other event had ever before frightened them so" (8.96.1). "Euboea 'was of more value to them than Attica'" (8.96.2). Athenians head to Samos instead of direct to Euboea to be able to respond either way. (THE IMPORTANCE OF EUBOEA)

**New treaty with Persia (Feb 411 at Caunus):**
- Spartans eager for reconciliation after Rhodes funds prove insufficient; negotiate formal treaty requiring ratification. "All the territory of the King that is in Asia shall belong to the King; and about his own territory the King may decide whatever he wishes" (8.58.2). Abandons European claims (Lichas concession) but firm on Asia. Key: reference to "the King’s ships"; Persians maintain Peloponnesian forces only until King's ships arrive, then loan repayable at war's end. "the Spartans... formally abandoned the Greeks of Asia and their own role as liberators, a deeply embarrassing concession." Spartans would not have agreed "unless the failure of the campaigns since the Sicilian disaster had convinced them that they could hope to win the war in no other way."
- Tissaphernes' fear: Spartans might "be compelled to fight a naval battle and lose, or... ravage the mainland in search of subsistence" (8.57.1); wants fleet at Miletus under control.

**Spartan move to Hellespont and early gains (May 411):**
- Dercylidas leads overland army; incites uprisings in Abydos and Lampsacus (Milesian colony). Strombichides recovers Lampsacus with 24 ships but "could not dislodge the Spartans from their foothold on that vital waterway." Establishes "a fortress and a lookout post for the whole Hellespont" at Sestos (8.62.3).
- Chios action: Peloponnesians (36 ships) gain upper hand vs 32 Athenian but no decisive victory; blockade continues. Strombichides forced north, leaving only 8 Athenian ships at Chios. Astyochus slips past Samos with >100 warships, challenges Athenians who refuse due to "suspicious of one another" from internal conflict at [[places/athens]]. "Suddenly the situation had completely reversed: Athens had lost control of the sea and the initiative in the war, and was torn by civil strife."

**Athenian revolutionary movement and oligarchic conspiracy (411):**
- "Since the onset of fighting in 431 the Athenian people had demonstrated a remarkable unity through twenty years of war..." No coups for a century after tyranny. Persian entry and ambitions of [[actors/alcibiades]] allow "long-dormant forces hostile to democracy in Athens" to attack the regime. 411 = 100th anniversary of liberation from tyranny and establishment of democracy.
- Aristocratic tradition (Theognis, Pindar, "Old Oligarch" pamphlet): nobles inherently superior; democracy gives power to "vulgar people (poneroi) rather than to the good (chrestoi)". Desire for eunomia (Spartan/Corinthian style): "the best and most qualified men make the laws... the people would, of course, fall into servitude."
- War burdens + loss of leadership like [[actors/pericles]] and [[actors/cleon]] + hetairiai increase pressure for curtailment. "The originator of this movement, however, was the exile [[actors/alcibiades]], who was motivated, as ever, not by ideology but by self-interest." Promises return with Tissaphernes support if democracy replaced by oligarchy. "But even more than the influence and promises of Alcibiades, of their own accord, the trierarchs and the most important men among the Athenians at Samos were eager to destroy the democracy" (8.47.2).
- Thrasybulus (moderate democrat) pushes recall of Alcibiades for Persian alliance as "the only safety for [[places/athens]]"; willing to accept temporary limits (no democracy for Persian trust, end pay for office, Five Thousand of hoplite class) but not narrow Four Hundred. "The category that best suits him is the traditional designation 'moderate'."
- True oligarchs (Phrynichus, Peisander): Phrynichus resists Alcibiades throughout, "showed himself, beyond all others, the most eager for the oligarchy... Once he set to work he revealed himself as the most reliable" (8.63.3). Peisander turns against Alcibiades, leads violent narrow oligarchs.
- Phrynichus' plot against Alcibiades: writes to Astyochus warning of plot (assumes Alcibiades still with Spartans); Astyochus reveals to Tissaphernes and Alcibiades. Second letter offers defeat plan at Samos. Alcibiades disbelieved as "a man who was not trustworthy" (8.51.3). "The collapse of their negotiations with Tissaphernes put an end to the oligarchic conspirators’ interest in restoring Alcibiades and led to the establishment of a new treaty between Sparta and Persia. The first attempt against democracy in Athens had failed."

**Peisander’s mission and the Coup (late Dec 412–June 411):**
- Peisander leads embassy to [[places/athens]]: survival depends on Persian help via Alcibiades; must "adopt a different form of democratic government" (8.53.1). "Unless we are governed more sensibly and place the offices, to a greater extent, into the hands of a few" (8.53.3). Assembly accepts out of fear, despite dislike of "the oligarchy"; sends envoys to negotiate. Peisander eliminates Phrynichus via (false) treason charge over Iasus/Amorges/Miletus failure.
- Visits hetairiai: "plan together to overthrow the democracy" (8.54.4). At Tissaphernes, Alcibiades' position insecure; demands (Asia coast cities, islands, King's ships on coast) rejected. "The angry Athenian envoys believed that Alcibiades had deceived them." Moderates (Thrasybulus) abandon; true oligarchs "let Alcibiades alone, since he refused to join them, and besides, he was not a suitable man to come into an oligarchy" (8.63.4). Now determined to destroy democracy.
- At Athens: assassinations (Androcles + others "secretly"); terror silences opposition. "If anyone should speak in opposition, he was immediately killed in some convenient way" (8.66.2). Assembly intimidated into appointing syngrapheis (incl. probouloi) with full powers at Colonus Hippius (not Pnyx). Motion suspends graphe paranomon. Peisander: no pay for public service (except limited archons/prytanies); council of Four Hundred "to rule in whatever way they thought best, with full powers" (8.67.3); list of Five Thousand (hoplite census+). "The assembly passed these measures without dissent... the coup had succeeded."
- "The provision made for the Five Thousand was a fraud." Four Hundred seize council house June 9 411 with armed support; pay off old councilors. Appoint generals (4 extreme oligarchs + moderates incl. Theramenes). Immediately seek peace with Sparta "on any terms tolerable" (incl. possibly surrender walls/fleet/autonomy). Agis rejects unless surrender maritime empire; Athenian counterattack drives Spartans off.

**Four Hundred in power, Samian resistance, and fall:**
- Deceptions to moderates: promise future Five Thousand constitution (never implemented); claim ratified by Five Thousand. Send embassy to Samos. Samian oligarchic plot (Three Hundred) fails due to democratic leaders (Leon, Diomedon, Thrasybulus, Thrasyllus); amnesty declared. Paralus crew reports (with lies) terror in Athens; soldiers nearly stone oligarchs but moderates restrain. **Battle of Arginusae (406): innovative tactics, great Athenian victory:**

"The Athenians, no less aware of their tactical inferiority, arranged their order accordingly, and in a manner unique in Greek naval history. The center had thirty-five ships in a single line, but these took up a position just in front of Garipadasi, the more western of the two main islands. The island prevented a Spartan diekplous in the center, just as the staggered double lines on the wings made the maneuver impossible there. The Athenians aligned the wings with twice the regular distance between the ships... The double gap also gave the Athenians a longer line, which protected them against the periplous while also enabling them to outflank the enemy." "As Callicratidas began his advance, 'the Athenians came out against him, extending their left wing out towards the open sea' (Xenophon, Hellenica 1.6.29). The question would be put in the most prejudicial language: whether or not the generals were guilty 'for not rescuing the men who had won the victory in the naval battle' (Xenophon, Hellenica 1.7.9). The penalty would be death to the convicted and the confiscation of their property." "the generals would be tried together, with one vote of the assembly deciding the fate of all." "A man claiming to be a survivor... recalled how drowning men... had asked him to tell the Athenians that 'the generals had not rescued the men who had shown themselves the best in the service of their country' (Xenophon, Hellenica 1.7.11)." (THE TRIAL OF THE GENERALS)
- "Euryptolemus, cousin and close associate of [[actors/alcibiades]], dared to speak on behalf of the accused. He charged Callixeinus with having made an illegal motion and in so doing called forth the graphe paranomon..." "some of the prytanies... refused to put this question to a vote on the grounds that it was illegal... It happened by sheer chance that Socrates had been chosen by lot to serve as a council member for that year... his tribe happened to hold the prytany for that month... Socrates himself happened on that day to be serving as prostates the presiding officer of the assembly. Alone among the prytanies, he held fast to his position and refused to put the question to a vote... 'I was the only one of the Prytanies who was opposed to the illegality, and I gave my vote against you [the Athenian people]; and when the orators threatened to indict and arrest me, and you insisted and shouted out, I decided that I must run the risk on the side of law and justice rather than to be on your side against justice because of fear of prison or death' (Plato, Apology 32b-c)." "Euryptolemus bravely rose again, suggesting different procedures that would still deal with the accused harshly but would allow them to be tried separately... In the end, however, parliamentary maneuvering undid that victory... the assembly this time voted what the council had proposed: to put all eight generals to death, including the two who had never returned." (THE TRIAL OF THE GENERALS)
- "Not long afterwards the Athenians repented and voted to bring charges against those who had deceived the people... Callixeinus was one of five so accused and arrested. They all escaped before their trial, but when Callixeinus returned to the city, 'he was hated by everyone and died of starvation' (Xenophon, Hellenica 1.7.35)." "For executing the generals the Athenians have been rightly censured through the ages, but the argument that has been made from ancient to modern times that such misdeeds are especially characteristic of democracies is far from the mark... It is precisely the general adherence to law and due process by the Athenian democracy that makes this departure from the norm so notorious." "Besides the loss of the generals for 406/5, [[places/athens]] also was deprived of the services of two other experienced commanders... Thrasybulus was not chosen as a general in the election of 405, and Theramenes, though elected, was disqualified... [[places/athens]] now had to face the challenge posed by [[places/sparta]] and Persia without the experience of most of its best commanders, and those who were selected in their stead must have been unnerved by the fate of their predecessors." (THE TRIAL OF THE GENERALS; THE RESULTS OF THE BATTLE)

**Lysander's return, Aegospotami, destruction of Athenian fleet (405):**
- "For all these reasons the Spartans once again sought peace... Athens rejected Sparta’s offer... the fact remains that a majority... did reject the peace... continuing distrust of the Spartans after their breach of faith during the Peace of Nicias..." "The trouble with this plan, however, was that Cyrus remained satrap... During the winter of 406/5... Sparta’s allies in the Aegean and on the Asian mainland met at Ephesus... petitioned the Spartans to reinstate [[actors/lysander]] to his command... the law provided that a man serve as navarch only once in his life, the Spartans appointed Aracus as nominal navarch and named [[actors/lysander]] as his secretary (epistoleus) and vice-admiral. Everyone understood that this was merely a legal fiction." Cyrus "had to report that the Great King’s money had all been spent... he took a remarkable step: summoning [[actors/lysander]] to Sardis, he appointed the Spartan as satrap in his place over the province of the Persian Empire. He left him the entire amount of money available and assigned him the right to collect all tribute due. Trusting the Spartan’s loyalty but not his prudence, he asked [[actors/lysander]] not to attack the Athenians until he returned." (ANOTHER SPARTAN PEACE OFFER; THE RETURN OF LYSANDER)
- "In Cyrus’ absence, meanwhile, [[actors/lysander]]’s personal goals required that he undo the influence of the late Callicratidas... At Miletus... he resorted to the trickery and deception... Although in public he uttered approving words about the end of factionalism in Miletus, he privately encouraged his supporters to rebel against the democracy. Resorting to political murder, they killed some 340 opponents in their homes and in the marketplace and drove more than a thousand others from the city. In place of the democracy they installed their own faction at the head of an oligarchy dependent on and fiercely loyal, not to [[places/sparta]] but to [[actors/lysander]]." "'where the lion’s skin will not reach, it must be patched with the fox’s' (Plutarch, Lysander 7.4; 8.4)." "To reach Miletus [[actors/lysander]] had been compelled to sail southward, past the Athenian fleet at Samos... the Athenians... made no effort to intercept [[actors/lysander]]. Their hesitation... part of the legacy of the execution and exile of the generals who had won at Arginusae... No leader had emerged from their number, and all must have been timid and suspicious, remembering the fate of their predecessors." (THE RETURN OF LYSANDER)
- "on his departure from Miletus [[actors/lysander]] soon turned the strategic situation to his favor... he raced westward across the Aegean, taking islands, raiding Aegina and Salamis in [[places/athens]]’ home waters, and finally landing in Attica itself... Lysander evaded them by racing back across the southern Aegean to Rhodes. From there he hurried north along the coast; safely passed Samos... and made his way to the Hellespont, 'to prevent the merchant-ships from sailing out and against the cities that had rebelled against the Spartans' (Xenophon, Hellenica 2.1.18)." "From his base at Abydos [[actors/lysander]] gathered an army... attacked the key city of Lampsacus by land and sea, taking it by storm. This success put the Spartans on the doorstep of the Propontis..." Athenians "arrived at their base at Sestos, from where they moved the fleet about twelve miles up the Hellespont to a place called Aegospotami, which lay about three miles across the strait from Lampsacus." (THE BATTLE OF AEGOSPOTAMI)
- "The decision to situate the Athenian fleet there was controversial from the first, for the area contained only a beach, without a proper harbor... the Athenians would have to divide and scatter their forces repeatedly to make the twenty-four-mile round trip to the main base at Sestos." "Six Athenian generals led the force at Aegospotami... they failed to formulate a brilliantly original strategy, but relied on the obvious approach of moving their fleet to the harbor at Lampsacus each morning and challenging [[actors/lysander]] to come out and fight." "It was at this moment that none other than [[actors/alcibiades]] made a dramatic reappearance... from his castle had observed the standoff. Riding down to the Athenian camp on horseback, he offered his advice and assistance. He urged the generals to move their base to Sestos... announced that two Thracian kings had promised him an army... The Athenian generals, however, had good reason to doubt... The renegade had also set unacceptable conditions for his help—namely, a share in the command... no Athenian general would have dared to yield any part of the command to an exile twice condemned by the Athenian people... 'if they were defeated they themselves would get the blame but that the credit for any success would go to [[actors/alcibiades]].' Instead, they told him that 'they were the generals now, and not he' (Diodorus 13.105.4), and then ordered him to leave." (THE BATTLE OF AEGOSPOTAMI)
- "They now returned to the original tactics, but the delay and inaction had a deleterious effect on discipline and morale. The men became careless, going off to seek food and water as soon as their ships touched the beach, without taking proper precautions for security..." "On the fifth day the rotation in command fell to Philocles, who appears to have had a plan... With thirty ships he sailed out in the direction of Sestos... His notion seems to have been to persuade [[actors/lysander]] that the Athenians had finally tired... The plan was a promising one, but it would need skillful and confident command, great discipline, and excellent timing..." "On that day, however, the Athenian fleet was poorly supplied with these qualities... As soon as he saw Philocles’ departure, [[actors/lysander]] was quick off the mark and cut the Athenian squadron off before it was able to get far downstream. With superior forces he turned on Philocles, routed his unit, and then headed for the main Athenian force behind him... the Athenians at Aegospotami were stunned to see the scattered remnants of Philocles' squadron fleeing toward them with [[actors/lysander]]'s victorious fleet in hot pursuit. Panic and paralysis ensued, and many ships were caught on the beach without their crews." "The Athenians' confusion encouraged [[actors/lysander]] to put a corps of soldiers on land under Eteonicus to seize their camp, while his own victorious ships were already hauling away the beached Athenian triremes... Of the great Athenian navy all but ten ships were captured or sunk. [[actors/lysander]] had reversed the outcome of Cyzicus, but the defeated Athenians had no ally to restore their fortunes, and with their own treasury empty, they could not afford to build another fleet. Thus, they had lost the war." (THE BATTLE OF AEGOSPOTAMI)

**Results, prisoners, Lysander's system, siege and negotiated fall of [[places/athens]] (405-404):**
- "After swiftly sending word of his great victory to [[places/sparta]], [[actors/lysander]] found between three and four thousand Athenian prisoners, which constituted about a tenth of the entire enemy force... the decision... rested in less calculating hands, and the vengeful allies insisted on the death penalty... the Spartans and their allies voted to kill all their Athenian prisoners." "Xenophon... reports how [[places/athens]] received the news of Aegospotami: 'The Paralus [one of two swift ships used for special missions] arrived at [[places/athens]] at night and announced the disaster, and a wailing came from the Piraeus, through the Long Walls, to the city, one man passing the word to another, so that on that night no one slept. They wept not only for the men who had been killed but even more for themselves, thinking that they would suffer the kind of fate they had imposed on the Melians... and on the Histiaeans and Scionaeans and Toroneans and Aeginetans and many others among the Hellenes' (Xenophon, Hellenica 2.2.3)." (THE RESULTS OF THE BATTLE)
- "In the Straits [[actors/lysander]] soon regained control of policy, and there were no further massacres. Instead he offered reasonable terms to cities allied to [[places/athens]], and they surrendered without a struggle. He even permitted Athenian garrisons and officials to leave in safety on condition that they go only to [[places/athens]]. This latter gesture... was in fact a canny tactical move: [[actors/lysander]] knew that [[places/athens]] was too strong to storm and could thus be taken only by siege, and he wanted as many hungry people in the city as possible to minimize the time it could hold out. To achieve the same purpose he placed garrisons at Byzantium and Chalcedon on either side of the Bosporus and decreed the death penalty for anyone bringing grain to [[places/athens]]." (THE RESULTS OF THE BATTLE)
- "His arrangements in these two cities set the pattern for the system he meant to establish everywhere under his control. He stationed garrisons in them under commanders called harmosts, not 'on the basis of aristocratic birth or of wealth, but he put control of affairs into the hands of members of his political faction and those connected to him by personal ties, and he put them in charge of rewards and punishments' (Plutarch, Lysander 13.4). Everywhere he found them he replaced democratic governments with oligarchies of his own loyalists, often consisting of boards of ten men called 'decarchies,' made up of those personally close to him. Before long the 'liberator of the Greeks' was collecting tribute from the cities under his control, and the Spartan government ratified all these arrangements." "Samos, alone, resisted; there the democratic ruling faction, fiercely loyal to [[places/athens]], killed its aristocratic opponents and prepared to endure the Spartan siege. [[actors/lysander]] left 40 ships for the purpose, taking 150 more toward Attica." (THE RESULTS OF THE BATTLE; THE FATE OF ATHENS)
- In Attica, met "the entire Peloponnesian army at the precinct of the Academy... It was the first time in more than a century that both Spartan kings were in the field simultaneously." Thebans and Corinthians suggested destruction; supported by Agis and [[actors/lysander]] "on their own initiative and without the approval of the Spartan assembly" (Pausanias 3.8.6). Athenians offered to join alliance keeping walls and Piraeus; Agis sent them to Sparta. Spartans at Sellasia: "ordered the envoys 'to go back from that very spot and, if they wanted a peace of any kind, to return with a better proposal' (Xenophon, Hellenica 2.2.13). At the least, the Athenians must agree to breach the Long Walls for a distance of more than a mile..." "A man by the name of Archestratus arose in the Athenian council and proposed the acceptance of the Spartan conditions, but even in their desperate state the Athenians would not hear of it. They imprisoned Archestratus... and passed instead the motion of [[actors/cleon]] to forbid in the future any similar proposal." (THE FATE OF ATHENS)
- "Even [[actors/cleon]] could not postpone peace negotiations forever, however... Now Theramenes—the very man who had taken part in the initiative to save [[places/athens]] from defeat in 411, and who had stepped forward to overthrow the Four Hundred when they were about to betray the city to the Spartans—risked danger again in an effort to ward off disaster." "He proposed instead to seek out [[actors/lysander]] to learn the Spartans’ true intentions—namely, did they really mean to destroy [[places/athens]] and its people? ... he told the assembly that he had discovered 'something of great value' (Lysias 13.9) to [[places/athens]] and asked the people to vote him full powers to negotiate peace." Stayed with [[actors/lysander]] at Samos "for some three months." "On his return early in March 404 he explained his long absence by saying that the Spartan had kept him there against his will and then sent him off with the same message as Agis': he had no power to discuss peace terms; for those the Athenians must go to the ephors in [[places/sparta]]. That explanation is most implausible..." "He must have persuaded the Athenians that he had made significant progress in the long discussions with [[actors/lysander]] and was now in a position to achieve a more satisfactory peace." (THERAMENES NEGOTIATES A PEACE)
- "That, in any case, was the result, for ultimately the Spartans agreed to a settlement that left [[places/athens]] intact, its people alive and free, if not fully autonomous." "Theramenes hoped to salvage what he could from the situation, but he must have understood that [[places/athens]] must give up its empire, its fleet, and its walls, for [[places/sparta]] would accept nothing less. His goals were to save the city, its people, their freedom, and as much independence as possible." "The most fervent of that group [wanting destruction] were the Thebans, along with the Corinthians. It was a Theban, Erianthus, who formally proposed that 'the city be leveled and the countryside left as a pasture for sheep' (Plutarch, Lysander 15.2)." "It would not have been difficult for Theramenes to persuade [[actors/lysander]] that razing [[places/athens]] would leave its territory prey to its increasingly powerful and ambitious rival to the north. It would not be in the interests of either [[places/sparta]] or [[actors/lysander]] to contribute to the growth of a state that had given [[places/sparta]] frequent trouble during the war... be far wiser, Theramenes could point out, to retain a friendly and unthreatening [[places/athens]] as a buffer and a check on Theban ambitions." (THERAMENES NEGOTIATES A PEACE)
- "In a postwar [[places/athens]] [[actors/lysander]] would have preferred a tight oligarchy made up entirely of his close supporters—perhaps a decarchy supported by a garrison... Lysander’s success and the extraordinary honors paid him in various cities had already made him an object of concern and jealousy to the Spartan kings and other leading figures. 'He was the first Greek to whom the cities raised altars and made sacrifices as to a god' (Plutarch, Lysander 18.3); the restored Samian oligarchs... changed the name of their chief festival from the Heraea to the Lysandreia." "Such antipathy must already have existed, so Theramenes could justifiably argue that the establishment of a narrow oligarchy, blatantly under [[actors/lysander]]'s control, would give rise to a unified opposition of the kings and his other enemies to his plans... It would be more stable and safer... to install a broader, more moderate regime." "One critical element supporting the power of [[actors/lysander]] was his close relationship with the Persian prince Cyrus... Cyrus’ own position was now in danger. Recalled to Susa, he found his father, Darius II, on his deathbed. His demise would bring to the throne Cyrus’ unfriendly older brother as Artaxerxes II who, at the least, would put an end to Cyrus’ command in the west and with it his power to help [[actors/lysander]]. That could well change the balance of power in the situation... It was very much in [[actors/lysander]]’s interests, Theramenes could argue, to make a reasonable peace and install a friendly regime in [[places/athens]] before Darius died and the news reached Greece." (THERAMENES NEGOTIATES A PEACE)
- "The peace terms they offered were these: the Long Walls and the walls of Piraeus were to come down; [[actors/lysander]] would decide how many ships [[places/athens]] could keep (that number would, of course, be very small); the Athenians would give up all the cities they controlled but keep the land of Attica; they must permit all exiles to return home (most of these would be oligarchs friendly to [[places/sparta]]); the Athenians would be governed by their ancestral constitution (what this meant was unclear and soon became an object of fierce contention); and the Athenians were to have the same friends and enemies as the Spartans and follow them wherever they might lead. (This in effect turned Athenian foreign policy over to Spartan control.)" "These terms may seem harsh, but less so in light of the fact that the Athenians had feared that the Spartans would refuse anything short of unconditional surrender and the destruction of [[places/athens]] and its people or their enslavement." (THERAMENES NEGOTIATES A PEACE)
