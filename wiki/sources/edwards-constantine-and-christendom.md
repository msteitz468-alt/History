# Constantine and Christendom

Three texts that document not so much the historical **[[constantine-the-great|Constantine]]** as the
**Christian legend built around him** — his own (disputed) theology, the cult of the Cross associated
with his mother Helena, and the great medieval forgery that made him the founder of papal temporal
power. The eighth and last source of this Phase-5 batch.

## The Three Texts

- **The Oration to the Saints** — a Greek apologetic address, a defence of Christianity, **transmitted
  as Constantine's own**. Its authenticity is one of the classic problems of Constantinian scholarship;
  Edwards argues for a genuinely Constantinian origin, which would make it a unique window onto the
  emperor's **personal religious thought** — his providential reading of history, his hostility to
  paganism, and his sense of a divinely appointed role.
- **The Greek and Latin Accounts of the Discovery of the Cross** — the legends of the ***Inventio
  Crucis***: how **Helena**, Constantine's mother, was led to discover the **True Cross** at Jerusalem
  (in the developed Latin version, through the Jew Judas, later Bishop Cyriacus). Pious fiction, but a
  key text of relic-cult and of the growing Christian sacralization of Constantine's house.
- **The Edict of Constantine to Pope Silvester — the [[donation-of-constantine|Donation of
  Constantine]]** — the eighth-century forgery in which a grateful Constantine, cured of leprosy and
  baptized by Pope **Silvester**, supposedly grants the papacy primacy over the other patriarchates and
  **temporal dominion over Rome, Italy and the western provinces**.

## Significance

The volume traces the transformation of Constantine from emperor into **myth and charter**. The
**Donation** became the most consequential forgery in Western history — cited for centuries to support
papal claims to temporal authority, and finally exposed as a fraud by **Lorenzo Valla** (1440) in a
landmark demonstration of philological and humanist criticism. Together the three texts illuminate how
the **Constantinian settlement** of Christianity generated, over centuries, the legends and legal
fictions on which the medieval relationship of **Church and Empire** was built.
