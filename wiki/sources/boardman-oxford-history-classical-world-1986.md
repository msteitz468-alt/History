## Overview

*The Oxford History of the Classical World* (Boardman, Griffin, Murray eds., 1986) is a single-volume multi-author survey of Greek and Roman antiquity from the archaic polis and Homeric poetry through the high Roman Empire and the late-antique transition. Thirty-two specialist essays plus Griffin’s Introduction and Chadwick’s Envoi combine political outline with literature, philosophy, religion, art, and social history. For the wiki it is a mid-1980s Oxford synthesis layer: useful for attributed narrative frames, cultural chapters thinner in pure political sources, and cross-links among already-dense CAH/Hall/Walbank/Flower coverage — not a first-choice for contested military or archaeological detail superseded by later scholarship.

## Key Arguments (volume-level)

1. **“Classical” as western inheritance, not absolute standard.** Griffin’s Introduction abandons exclusive Renaissance classicism while insisting Greece–Rome remain specially linked to western institutions, myth, and form.
2. **Polis as the central Greek political form** (Forrest), with Sparta and Athens as divergent experiments within a competitive multi-polis ecology.
3. **Cultural genres as historical evidence** — Homer, lyric, drama, historiography, philosophy, and art chapters treat literature/art as primary windows, not decoration on a political spine.
4. **Hellenistic as a real world-system** after Alexander (Price; Lane Fox; Barnes): monarchies, mixed cities, expanded Greek literary and scientific culture.
5. **Rome’s expansion and Greek cultural conquest as mutual transformation** (Crawford–Rawson–Brown; later art/literature chapters): military success paired with hellenization of elite culture.
6. **Principate as governmental and cultural settlement** (Stockton, Purcell, Augustan poetry/Virgil chapters): not merely one-man rule but a re-ordered elite and literary public.
7. **End of antiquity is gradual** (Chadwick Envoi): third-century crisis, Diocletian–Constantine settlement, Christianization, and barbarian kingdoms reframe rather than instantly erase classical structures.

## Volume Synthesis Note

OHCW is a **well-trodden interpretive overlay**, not a first-pass structural source. Political narrative (Forrest, Hornblower, Price, Crawford, Rawson, Stockton) largely confirms and attributes mid-1980s Oxford consensus already dense in CAH, Hall (Archaic), and republican/imperial military pages. The **highest wiki value** lay in chapters thinner in pure political sources:

1. **Hellenistic philosophy** (Barnes) — first full wiki anchors for Stoicism/Epicureanism and their founders, with Late Stoa (Epictetus, Marcus) and Lucretian reception.
2. **Drama and classical literary culture** (Levi; Murray on historians; Nisbet/Lyne/Griffin on Latin poetry) — dramatists and late-Republican/Augustan poets as network nodes.
3. **Religion and art** (Parker; Boardman; Ling; Wilson) — orthopraxy/embedded cult and man-centred art folded into existing concept pages.
4. **Envoi** (Chadwick) — gradualist end-of-antiquity frame (3rd-c. crisis → Diocletian–Constantine → Christianization debates → 476 as little-noticed formal end) on late-antique pages.
