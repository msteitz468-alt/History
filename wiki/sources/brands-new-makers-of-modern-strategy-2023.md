# The New Makers of Modern Strategy from the Ancient World to the Digital Age (Hal Brands ed., 2023)

This is the major 2023 successor volume in the "Makers of Modern Strategy" series (following Edward Mead Earle's 1943 original and Peter Paret's 1986 expansion). Edited by Hal Brands, it contains 45 essays by leading historians and strategic thinkers. It deliberately broadens the scope backward to the ancient world (Thucydides, Polybius, Sun Zi) and forward into the post-Cold War and digital eras (Xi Jinping, irregular warfare, new domains, intelligence revolution, grand strategy in a competitive world). 

The volume emphasizes both the enduring "logic" and "grammar" of strategy (echoing Clausewitz via Gaddis's conclusion) and the necessity of continual reinterpretation in light of new contexts, technologies, and power distributions. It was conceived and written against the backdrop of renewed geopolitical competition.

## Historiography Note

This volume consciously positions itself as the third iteration of a project begun in 1943 amid total war and continued in 1986 at the end of the Cold War. Brands' introduction and Gaddis' conclusion frame the enterprise around the tension between time-bound historical fidelity and the search for enduring strategic logic/paradoxes. Strengths: extraordinary breadth (ancient to digital, Western + major non-Western cases), high-caliber contributors, and explicit engagement with contemporary relevance. Limitations: still primarily elite/leader-focused strategic thought; the post-Cold War section was written before the full Ukraine war and some recent developments. The collection is authoritative for updating the intellectual history of strategy and for cross-referencing with operational and theoretical sources already in the wiki. It reinforces that "strategy is the indispensable art" precisely when competition sharpens.

## Consolidated Volume Synthesis

The 2023 *New Makers of Modern Strategy* (Hal Brands, ed.) is the third major iteration of the "Makers" project (after Earle 1943 and Paret 1986). It expands the canvas dramatically: from ancient foundations (Thucydides, Polybius, Sun Zi) through the classic modern theorists (Machiavelli, Clausewitz, Jomini, Mahan) to the nuclear age, the post-Cold War unipolar moment, and contemporary competition with peer rivals (China under Xi, Russian hybrid/irregular approaches, North Korean survival strategy, jihadist evolution).

Key threads across the 45 essays:
- **Timeless logic vs. contextual adaptation**: Strategy has a core (politics primacy, interaction with thinking adversary, friction, power in unexpected places) but every era redefines its meaning and requirements (Freedman Ch.1; Brands intro; Gaddis conclusion).
- **Primacy of politics and grand strategy**: From Richelieu's *raison d'état* and equilibrium, Napoleon's single-point concentration, Adams' democratic restraint + expansion, Wilson's League vision, to US "grand strategy of inhibition" in the nuclear age and Xi's "national rejuvenation" blending economic, military, diplomatic, and narrative power.
- **Irregular, hybrid, and new-domain warfare**: Persistent conflict as governance tool (Congo, North Korea provocations); Soleimani-style proxy networks and Gerasimov's asymmetric "active measures" upgraded with cyber/disinfo; RMA and reconnaissance-strike concepts originating in US-Soviet competition and now central to offsets and cross-domain coercion.
- **Nuclear and limited war dilemmas**: The "elusive" nature of nuclear strategy (Brodie to Schelling to Gavin); stability vs. extended deterrence tensions; limited war in Korea and post-9/11 COIN/CT as adaptations under nuclear shadow.
- **US dilemmas of dominance and retrenchment**: Unipolar overstretch, RMA hopes vs. reality, surge vs. withdrawal, pivot to Asia, and recognition that technology and offsets cannot substitute for coherent political strategy (Griffin, Adamsky, Moyar).
- **Non-Western and anti-imperial agency**: Sun Zi logic, Tecumseh's confederacy, anti-imperial resistance (Gandhi et al.), Japanese maritime vs. continental choices, Mao nested war, Nehru non-alignment, jihad evolution, Kim dynasty brinkmanship, and Xi's restoration model.

The volume updates and complements the 1986 Paret edition by adding ancient depth, more non-Western cases, and detailed treatment of post-1986 developments (RMA, hybrid war, China, intelligence revolution, new domains). It reinforces the wiki's emphasis on causation (political ends driving military means, organizational and perceptual factors in competition), link taxonomy (preceded_by vs. caused_by; produced vs. enabled), and historiography (how strategic ideas are reinterpreted and sometimes misapplied).

Strengths for the collection: authoritative, multi-author, explicit engagement with "why" questions of strategy-making across scales and eras. Limitations: still primarily focused on state/elite actors and Western intellectual traditions (though broader than predecessor); written before full Ukraine war and some recent tech shifts. ### Key Claims on Post-Cold War US Strategy Dilemmas (Ch. 35, with cross-references to foundations in Ch. 34)
- After the Cold War's end and Soviet dissolution, unipolarity created a core dilemma for US [[concepts/grand-strategy]]: how to exercise dominance when no peer rival existed. The George H.W. Bush administration's Cheney/Powell/Wolfowitz process produced the "Regional Defense Strategy" (RDS) and Base Force as the enduring template—shifting from global Soviet deterrence to regional contingencies, "presence," a "two-major regional contingency" (two-MRC) force-sizing construct, and "reconstitution" hedging against future threats—while pursuing a "zone of peace/freedom" and preventing new rivals.
- The strategy-resources mismatch was structural from the start: Bush-era planning responded heavily to "peace dividend" budgetary pressures, with the two-MRC and reconstitution concepts becoming "high canon" for defense planning despite the unipolar moment being recognized as transitory. Later administrations treated primacy more as an assumption than a deliberate objective to be actively preserved, leading to overstretch.
- Clinton's "enlargement" of the "core" of market democracies (via Lake's 1993 framework) largely extended the prior RDS template through diplomacy/economic statecraft, NATO enlargement, and selective interventions (e.g., successful Bosnia/Kosovo air campaigns after early Somalia/Balkans setbacks), but "assertive multilateralism" and humanitarian/peacekeeping emphasis increased operational tempo even as forces shrank, creating unsustainable burdens and exposing US ways of war to adversaries (notably Chinese analysts studying RMA vulnerabilities in logistics/bases/carriers).
- The 1991 Gulf War showcased US qualitative overmatch (stealth, precision, C2, logistics) from the late-Cold War "second offset," raising debates over an emergent "revolution in military affairs" (RMA) but also highlighting limits: the US was the only power able to project decisive force globally, yet willingness and resourcing remained the binding constraints.
- Post-9/11, George W. Bush reframed the era as ending the "years of repose," extending prior "zone of freedom"/enlargement rhetoric into a Manichean "balance of power that favors freedom," "axis of evil," and explicit preemption doctrine in the 2002 National Security Strategy (adapting "imminent threat" to rogue/terror actors seeking WMD). The military was to be transformed for agility/precision while maintaining "strengths beyond challenge" to dissuade peers. Afghanistan's early success (SOF + precision) seemed to validate low-footprint regime change, but Iraq's shift to insurgency/civil war exposed limits.
- The 2007 Iraq "surge" (informed by Keane/Kagan and theater adaptation toward population protection) reversed violence trends and saved the campaign, but at the cost of straining forces, forcing rapprochement elsewhere (e.g., Six-Party Talks on North Korea, limited responses to Iran/Syria/Georgia/Russia), and accelerating Afghanistan deterioration. Bush's broader agenda revealed the limits of post-Cold War American power.
- Obama entered emphasizing overextension (post-2008 crisis) and "retrenchment + engagement": ending the "bad war" in Iraq, limiting Afghanistan to a time-bound surge with exit strategy (despite military recommendations for population-centric COIN), "leading from behind" in Libya (multilateral air support handed off quickly), and budget discipline (Gates' initial cuts + BCA sequestration forcing ~$1T in defense reductions). The 2012 Defense Strategic Guidance pivoted emphasis to Asia-Pacific, evolved Europe posture, and explicitly abandoned sizing for "large-scale, prolonged stability operations."
- Late Obama crises (Syria "red line" reversal and Russian-brokered chemical deal; Russia in Ukraine/Crimea; ISIS caliphate; China South China Sea island-building/militarization) illustrated the unipolar era's end, with renewed great-power competition and the limits of prior assumptions. Obama ruefully noted "it’s harder to end wars than it is to begin them."
- Lasting legacies of the unipolar moment (1989–~2014): successful expansion of the "zone of freedom" and alliances (no major-power war), but failed or disappointing "enlargement"/nation-building efforts toward Russia/China and backlash states; persistent mismatch between strategy ambitions and force structure/modernization (Base Force lineage carried too many missions; reconstitution hopes shifted to RMA/offset bets that largely underdelivered); adversaries studied and adapted to US operations. Krauthammer's "unipolar moment" anticipated its end in a pre-WWI-like structure of renewed competition.

### Key Claims on RMA, Ogarkov/Marshall, and Intellectual Foundations of Post-Cold War Strategy (Ch. 36, cross-referenced to Ch. 34/35)
- The IT-RMA (information technology revolution in military affairs) emerged from Cold War "competition of learning": US "offset strategy" (1970s onward) created technological foundations (PGMs, sensors, stealth, C2) for AirLand Battle/FOFA to counter Soviet mass without nuclear use. Soviets (led by Marshal Nikolai Ogarkov) first conceptualized it as a true "revolution in military affairs" or "military technological revolution," producing systematic theory on reconnaissance-strike (reconnaissance-strike complex) and reconnaissance-fire complexes—integrated ISR + automated C2 + precision standoff fires—rendering traditional massed maneuver obsolete in favor of effects, networks, and fires projection. US (led by Andrew W. Marshall/Office of Net Assessment) later adopted, adapted, and operationalized it as "network-centric warfare" and "defense transformation."
- Ogarkov and Marshall were the central figures: Ogarkov drove Soviet theorizing (late 1970s–1980s) despite lacking the tech, emphasizing discontinuity, forecasting/foreseeing methodologies (multidisciplinary, history + math), and non-techno-centric views. Marshall recognized the Soviet diagnosis, used it as a "looking glass," commissioned historical studies of interwar innovations/military effectiveness, and drove US adoption (e.g., 1992 net assessment memo on RMA; post-Gulf War/Kosovo validation). Their work popularized RMA as applied concept, reconnaissance-strike/network-centric as operational architecture, and military innovation studies as a scholarly/practitioner sub-discipline.
- Additional legacies: Marshall/ONA birthed "competitive strategy" (long-term peacetime efforts to shape adversary behavior/investments via net assessment, exploiting organizational/bureaucratic proclivities and imposing costs—e.g., US bomber developments forcing Soviet air defense spending). Ogarkov indirectly informs contemporary Russian "cross-domain coercion"/"strategic deterrence" (integrated nuclear/conventional/sub-conventional/nonmilitary influence under nuclear aegis, with conventional precision enabling nonnuclear victory options and calibrated escalation). Both exemplify how competition produces cross-fertilization of strategic concepts.
- RMA concepts fed directly into post-Cold War US practice (Ch. 35): Gulf War as demonstration; Bush-era transformation/2001 QDR ambitions for decisive victory; persistent hopes that technology/offsets (including later "third offset") could substitute for force structure or reconstitution.

### Key Claims on Peacetime Competition/Strategy Foundations and Earlier Analogues (Ch. 34, with echoes in Ch. 32/33/36)
- [[concepts/strategy]] and [[concepts/grand-strategy]] in peacetime differ from wartime: focus on deterrence/dissuasion via revelation or concealment of capabilities; high uncertainty about future war character; risk aversion; long feedback loops. Arms competitions and arms control can be deliberate strategic tools rather than reflexive (e.g., cost-imposition or denial strategies to shape adversary choices).
- Successful historical examples include Admiral Fisher's "plunging" in the Anglo-German naval race (continuously advancing British designs/quality/readiness via industrial advantages to force German responses that were costly and suboptimal; Tirpitz's battleship focus was self-defeating as it ignored British vulnerabilities like trade). US Cold War approaches similarly imposed monetary/technological/operational costs on the Soviets (e.g., low-altitude then stealth bombers forcing repeated Soviet air defense overhauls; Offset Strategy/AirLand Battle exploiting Soviet echeloned mass and scripted operations).
- Action-reaction models of arms races (e.g., McNamara/Schelling influences in Ch. 32 context) are oversimplified; better views (Marshall, Wohlstetter) emphasize organizational behavior, bureaucratic cultures, misperceptions, and domestic constraints over unitary rational actors. Soviet spending reflected internal priorities more than tight coupling.
- Arms control served competitive ends (e.g., SALT I locking quantitative parity while preserving US qualitative edges; Reagan INF eliminating a Soviet-favored class while shifting competition to US advantages in air/sea). Reagan's NSDD 75 (1983) synthesized: external resistance, internal pressure for Soviet change, reciprocal negotiations—leveraging US economic/ideological strengths and Soviet vulnerabilities.

### Additional Grounded Claims on Strategy Practice (Spillover Ch. 32/33, Informing Broader Context)
- In Vietnam (relevant to limited-war theory debates that carried into post-Cold War thinking), Johnson/McNamara's over-reliance on abstract limited-war theory (Schelling) and quantitative "Whiz Kids" approaches, combined with marginalization of military expertise and gradualism in Rolling Thunder, signaled weakness rather than resolve—prompting North Vietnamese escalation and invasion preparations. Restraint invited aggression; military preferences for intensity/mining/harbors/Laos operations were often vindicated postwar by enemy sources.
- Brezhnev's détente was a grand-strategic attempt to blend cooperation (summitry, recognition of equality, condominium in areas like Middle East) with competition (clients, military buildup, ideological prestige). It failed due to conflicting status perceptions, grey-zone client pressures (e.g., 1973 Yom Kippur War, Angola, Horn, Afghanistan), bureaucratic interests, personal/health factors (Brezhnev decline, Nixon resignation, Carter human rights), and the inherent contradiction of being both US partner and revolutionary leader. Détente was driven more by Soviet weakness than confidence.
