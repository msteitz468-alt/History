# CAH Vol. I, Part 2 — Early History of the Middle East

The second half of the first CAH volume, and the **hinge from prehistory to
history**. Where Part 1 ([[edwards-cah-v1p1-1970]]) classified peoples by
geological/cultural stage ("Neanderthal man," "Badarians," "Tell Halaf culture")
— "a cloak for anonymity," in the editors' phrase — Part 2 opens at the moment
**writing** comes into use in Mesopotamia, Egypt, and Elam, "enabling man to
record his existence as an individual." The volume covers the
[[early-bronze-age|Early Bronze Age]] through the [[middle-bronze-age|Middle
Bronze Age]] (c. 3000–1750 BCE): the first dynasties of Egypt and Sumer, the
pyramid-building Old Kingdom, the first empire (Agade) under [[sargon-of-akkad|
Sargon]] and [[naram-sin|Naram-Sin]], the Sumerian revival under the Third
Dynasty of Ur, the Egyptian Middle Kingdom, and — at the close — the appearance
of Indo-European speakers ("Greek") on the northern Greek confines and the first
palace civilization in Crete.

The editors' framing caution: early written records "offer the historian but
limited assistance; they generally refer to isolated incidents without giving
much indication of the background," and later chronicles that supplement them
"are not always reliable." Narrative biographical texts do not appear before the
mid-third millennium (the earliest being the tomb inscriptions of the Egyptian
official **Metjen**, d. reign of Sneferu, c. 2600 BCE). History here is therefore
still built largely from monuments and uninscribed material, with text as a thin
and uneven framework.
