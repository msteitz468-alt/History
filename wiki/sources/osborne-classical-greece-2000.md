# Classical Greece 500–323 BC (Short Oxford History of Europe)

Thematic survey of the classical Greek world from the Ionian Revolt / Cleisthenic
reforms to the death of Alexander, edited by Robin Osborne. Unlike a narrative
history, the volume is organised around structures and institutions — the economy,
the physical and political city, warfare as a social practice, political conflict and
thought, and private life — with the political-military narrative compressed into two
later chapters. It represents the state of Anglophone social and cultural history of
classical Greece at the turn of the millennium.

## Chapter Structure

1. The creation of classical Greece (Robin Osborne) — periodisation, sources, what makes the era "classical"
2. The economy (Paul Millett) — agriculture, mining, coinage, trade, banking, slavery, the household economy
3. The classical city (Rosalind Thomas) — citizenship, democratic/oligarchic institutions, civic ideals
4. The city at war (Hans van Wees) — hoplite and naval warfare as civic institutions, the finance of war
5. Political conflicts, political debates, and political thought (Josiah Ober) — stasis, democracy, oligarchy, political theory
6. Private life (James Davidson) — the oikos, women, children, sexuality, the symposium, household religion
7. The fifth century: political and military narrative (Lisa Kallet) — Persian Wars to the Peloponnesian War
8. The fourth century: political and military narrative — Spartan and Theban hegemonies to Philip and Alexander
9. Epilogue (Robin Osborne) — the Oedipus frame; the polis and Western self-scrutiny

## Reliability / Extraction Note

The working text was an OCR/PDF conversion in which many numerals (talent sums, tribute totals, troop and population counts, dates, interest rates) were garbled or dropped. All such reconstructed figures were integrated in hedged/approximate form; only canonical, independently well-attested figures (e.g. 192 Athenian dead at Marathon, 300 Spartans at Thermopylae, 292 Spartiates surrendered at Sphacteria, 31 poleis in the Hellenic League) were stated as exact. A note to this effect sits on the pages carrying the densest quantitative material ([[classical-greek-economy]], [[financing-greek-warfare]]).

## Volume Synthesis Note

Osborne's volume is organised as an argument about *what classical Greece was* rather than a chronicle of what happened. By front-loading structure — economy, the physical and political city, war as a civic practice, political thought, and private life — and compressing the political-military narrative into two late chapters, it treats the [[polis]] as a social system whose defining feature was the intensity of communal life and the resulting mutual surveillance of citizens. The recurring thesis, stated most sharply in Osborne's epilogue via the three retellings of the Oedipus story (Homer, Sophocles, Aristotle), is that the density of the citizen community generated an unprecedented capacity for self-scrutiny — in tragedy, sculpture, historiography, and philosophy — that became foundational for later European self-understanding.

Economically, the volume (Millett) insists that the Greek "economy" is largely a reconstruction of *Athens*, and an elite-skewed Athens at that, given the dominance of the Attic orators in the evidence; it frames classical Greece as belonging to "the Third World, but a third world in which there is no first world," sharing the Near East's agrarian-extractive base without its scale or irrigation-based stability. On warfare, van Wees's treatment feeds the wiki's [[hoplite-warfare]], the new [[greek-naval-warfare]] and [[financing-greek-warfare]] pages, and adds a revisionist position on phalanx density to [[hoplite-revolution-debate]].

Against already-ingested narrative-driven sources (CAH, Kagan-style political-military history), Osborne's contributors add depth on the *social embedding* of institutions rather than new events: the oikos/polis boundary ([[oikos]]), citizenship as a revocable political creation ([[polis]]), cult as civic infrastructure ([[polis-religion]]), and the fourth-century shift to an opportunistic, non-ideological interstate politics driven by small units and secret diplomacy ([[classical-antiquity]]). Two genuine source-critical tensions surfaced and were preserved rather than resolved: Osborne dates Thucydides' birth c. 455 against the wiki's existing c. 460 (flagged on [[thucydides]]), and the volume treats the "Peace of Callias" as a fourth-century construct unmentioned by contemporaries.
