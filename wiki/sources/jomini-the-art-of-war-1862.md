# The Art of War by Baron de Jomini (1862 translation by Mendell & Craighill)

This is the primary English-language text of Antoine-Henri Jomini's systematic treatise on the art of war (his "Summary of the Principles of the Art of War"). Translated at the U.S. Military Academy, West Point, and published in 1862, it presents Jomini's mature distillation of lessons from the Napoleonic wars and earlier campaigns. The translators' preface stresses its utility for a democratic republic without a large standing army: military knowledge must be widely diffused among the people.

Jomini divides the art of war into five branches (Strategy, Grand Tactics, Logistics, Tactics of the different arms, and Engineering) and focuses on the first three. He argues that while war as a whole is an *art* influenced by morale, genius, passion, and chance, **strategy** in particular can be regulated by fixed principles analogous to those of the positive sciences.

## Overview

Jomini presents a geometric and systematic view of strategy centered on the concentration of superior force at the decisive point in the theater of war. His "fundamental principle" is to bring the mass of one's forces successively against the decisive points of the theater while threatening or cutting the enemy's communications, all while securing one's own base and lines of operations.

Key recurring elements:
- **Theater of operations**, base of operations, objective points
- **Decisive points** (strategic and tactical)
- **Interior vs. exterior lines**
- **Lines of operations** (general, temporary, of maneuver, of supply)
- Distinction between strategy (bringing forces to the decisive point) and grand tactics (employing them there)
- Emphasis on logistics as practical movement of armies
- Recognition that morale, character of commanders, and political context remain decisive even when principles are followed

The work is prescriptive and didactic, aimed at instruction. Jomini repeatedly stresses that principles are tools for avoiding error rather than guarantees of victory.

## Key Claims (initial from front matter & structure)

- The art of war consists of five principal parts; strategy is the highest and most general.
- A commander must first settle with the head of state the political character of the war.
- Strategy begins with choice of theater, base, zone of operations, objective points, and lines.
- "Strategy embraces the selection of the theater of war... the determination of the decisive points... the most favorable direction for operations."
- Combat and morale introduce elements that cannot be fully reduced to scientific rules; war remains an art.
- The work was translated specifically to diffuse military knowledge in the United States.

## Historiography and Collection Note

This 1862 translation made Jomini's ideas accessible to American officers and influenced 19th-century military education. Later 20th- and 21st-century historiography (especially Freedman, Paret's *Makers of Modern Strategy*) positions Jomini as the clear, usable, but ultimately shallower counterpart to Clausewitz — the "false science" of principles versus the deeper dialectical understanding of war as a continuation of policy subject to friction and chance.

The collection previously covered Jomini only through secondary syntheses (Freedman, Paret, Brands, Heuser, Jones, etc.). This ingest supplies the primary text for direct claims and verbatim grounding.

### Definitions and Branches of the Art of War
- The art of war consists of five (or six) principal parts: Strategy, Grand Tactics, Logistics, Tactics of the different arms, and the Art of the Engineer (plus "Diplomacy in its relation to War" as essential for generals commanding armies). Strategy is the art of directing masses upon the theater of war.
- Recommended for novices: begin with Strategy (Ch. III) before returning to Ch. I–II; Napoleonic campaigns are the central historical reference.

### Chapter I: Relation of Diplomacy to War
- Wars classified by political character and military character (offensive to recover rights, politically defensive but militarily offensive, wars of expediency, with/without allies, intervention, invasion/conquest, wars of opinion, national wars, civil/religious wars, double wars). Different types influence "the nature and extent of the efforts and operations."
- Even "just" wars often become wars of expediency in practice. Statesmanship must foresee third-party interventions.
- Interventions (especially timely third-party into an ongoing war) offer great advantages if near own frontiers and with sufficient force. Distant interventions riskier.
- National wars (spontaneous popular uprising) are the most formidable; regular armies alone insufficient unless they hold every point. Requires mass proportional to resistance + justice/patience (examples Suchet in Spain, contrasts with brutal occupations).
- Avoid simultaneous major wars on opposite fronts.

### Chapter II: Military Policy
- Military Policy covers moral/institutional combinations (national passions, military institutions/reserves, finances, attachment to government, character of commanders, staff system, armament, military geography/statistics) that influence operations but belong neither purely to diplomacy nor strategy.
- Accurate military statistics (enemy resources) and strategic geography (theater, obstacles, permanent decisive points) are indispensable; ignorance causes repeated miscalculations.
- Key factors beyond pure strategy: allaying enemy passions vs. electrifying own; discipline vs. undisciplined bravery; cabinet control (Aulic councils weaken); judicious selection of generals (character, moral courage first, physical courage second; thorough grounding in principles over vast erudition).
- Military institutions: 12 essential conditions (good recruiting, organization, reserves, instruction, strict but non-humiliating discipline, rewards, special arms, superior armament, general staff, commissariat, assignment to command, military spirit of the people). "Iron weighs at least as much as gold"; war can support war via enemy territory.
- Military spirit (institutional, permanent) vs. temporary enthusiasm; both valuable. Invest army with social consideration; encourage firmness in reverses as well as success in advance. Good habits/order/discipline counter panics.
- Command: monarch command advantageous only with real genius (else intrigues). Best: tried general of character + high-ability chief of staff in harmony. Councils of war generally deplorable.

### Chapter III: Strategy (Core)
- **Definitions and course of a war**: Strategy begins with agreement on political character of the war, study of theater, selection of base, zone of operations, objective point, line of operations (direction promising greatest opportunities with least danger), fronts, positions, etc. Detailed flow through resistance, battle/maneuver, sieges, second objectives, intermediate support/reserves/têtes de pont, retreat to base.
- **Strategy embraces** (enumerated): theater selection/combinations; decisive points and favorable direction; base/zone; objective points; fronts/lines of defense/strategic positions; lines/zones of operations; strategic lines of maneuver; eventual bases/reserves; marches; depots relation; fortresses/sieges; intrenched camps; diversions/detachments.
- **Distinctions**: Strategy = art of making war upon the map / bringing greatest forces upon important points of theater or zone ("decides where to act"). Grand Tactics = posting troops on battlefield according to ground, bringing them into action ("decides the manner of execution"). Logistics = practical art of moving armies ("brings the troops to this point").
- **Fundamental Principle of War** (repeated maxim): "To throw by strategic movements the mass of an army, successively, upon the decisive points of a theater of war, and also upon the communications of the enemy as much as possible without compromising one's own." To maneuver to engage fractions of the hostile army with the bulk of one's forces. On the battlefield, throw the mass upon the decisive point at the proper time and with energy.
- **Bases of operations**: Portion of country from which army obtains reinforcements/resources, starts offensive, retreats if needed. Requirements: fortified points, good communications. Preference for perpendicular (esp. re-entrant angle/double) bases over parallel (examples Napoleon 1806 Saale, 1813 Bohemia, Russians 1812). Sea bases dangerous for large continental armies (Wellington Torres Vedras exception). Temporary/eventual bases and pivots of operations in enemy territory.
- **Theater of operations**: Geographic area; composed of fixed base, principal objective, fronts, zones/lines, obstacles, geographical strategic points, accidental intermediate bases, points of refuge. Features' value depends on the general's skill (Napoleon Saint-Bernard example). Distinguish general war theater from army's theater of operations.
- **Decisive points**: Locations (geographic permanent or accidental maneuver-based) capable of exercising marked influence on campaign or enterprise. Possession gives control of communications junctions, valleys, capitals, defiles. Flank opposite sea often preferred. Objective points of maneuver (destroy enemy forces) vs. geographical (capital, fort); political objectives subordinate to strategy.
- **Lines of operations**: Simple vs. double; interior (concentrate faster) vs. exterior. Maxims: direct upon center or one extremity (not both unless overwhelming superiority); interior/central preferable with equal forces; avoid over-crowding; change of line delicate; home-soil advantage. Historical validation: Archduke Charles at Würzburg, Napoleon 1796/1800/1805/1806.
- **Fronts, positions, pivots**: Strategic front (actual masses' positions) vs. front of operations (space between armies + marches beyond). Change of strategic front is one of the most important grand maneuvers. Pivots of operations = enduring material points of support (e.g., Verona 1796, Dresden 1813). Lines of defense (short, compact, concentration points) vs. eventual.
- **Strategic lines of maneuver**: Temporary lines for specific enterprises (distinct from general line of operations); can be concentric (decisive blow) or eccentric (after victory).
- **Old vs. new systems**: Old = methodical wars of position, tents, supplies at hand, covering every point. New (Napoleonic) = rapid marches (25 miles/day), bivouacs/requisitions, columns + skirmishers, aim at decisive points/armies rather than places. Modern marches are strategic applications of the fundamental principle.
- **Depots and logistics in strategy**: Commissariat must support operations; echelon depots on converging lines; war supports war via enemy territory when possible. Rivers/canals valuable. In hostile territory, depots still necessary (more numerous) and must be protected.
- **Mountains and distant invasions**: Mountains require valley control + covering multiple outlets; concentration harder; mobility + offensive action essential. Distant invasions (especially without secure nearby allied base) carry extreme risks; rare successes in history; "in four thousand years only five or six have been successful." Capital point: secure hearty constant alliance near field of operations.
- **Epitome of Strategy**: Strategy fixes direction of movements and decisive points; tactics executes. Apply fundamental principle via good use of bases/lines, interior lines where possible, proper direction, mobility, etc. "The whole of strategy is contained in these four examples" (Ulm, Jena, Ratisbon + Carnot 1793).

### Chapters IV–V: Grand Tactics, Battles, Mixed Operations
- Grand tactics: art of posting troops on battlefield per ground, bringing into action, fighting. Same fundamental principle (mass vs. fraction at decisive point) applies.
- Three kinds of battles: defensive, offensive, unexpected collision on march. Turning maneuver not universally required for decisive victory (examples Zama, Rivoli, Austerlitz).
- Twelve orders of battle (parallel, oblique, perpendicular on wings, concave/convex, echelons, combined center+extremity). Oblique order especially advantageous for inferior attacking force. Simple or sudden opportunist maneuvers generally outperform rigid pre-planned ones. Difficulty is simultaneous coordinated action of fractions.
- Over-extended turning maneuvers dangerous unless enemy general weak. Unexpected meetings require rapid application of principles.
- Surprises difficult post-firearms (camp surprises pre-dawn possible but subordinate to strategic combos). Coups de main on fortified places/intrenched camps: suppressive fire, prepared crossing means, small columns with reserves, vigorous execution; strategic context often outweighs raw strength of post.
- Diversions/detachments: dangerous if inconsiderate; few better than many. Permanent (double fronts, reserves) vs. temporary. Good demonstrations fix enemy while mass strikes elsewhere. Distant diversions rarely decisive.
- Passage of rivers: strategic choice of point (align with decisive points, allow perpendicular development post-crossing) more important than pure tactics. Deception + combined-arms security essential. Post-passage: avoid fatal subdivision; interior lines or extremity passage + rapid advance preferred.
- Retreats: most difficult operations. Slow, short marches with strong rear-guard generally best for large armies. Concentric or parallel (lateral) better than diverging/eccentric for beaten army. Methodical bridge-crossing preparations (têtes de pont, timing, deception) critical.
- Pursuits: direct upon flank; bold/active execution.
- Cantonments: compact, connected, covered by river/obstacles, with rapid assembly points, patrols, signals. Risk if enemy remains concentrated.
- Descents (maritime expeditions): extremely difficult across open sea vs. narrow straits. Rules for assailant (deceive landing point, safe anchorage, land strong force + artillery together); for defender (do not divide too much, concentrate at landing, keep communication with shore as base/retreat). Modern inventions changed combinations.

### Chapter VI–VII: Logistics, Troop Formations, Combined Arms
- Logistics: "the practical art of moving armies"; one of the most essential parts of the art of war. Bridges strategy and tactics. Duties include campaign preparations, orders, depots, recon, marches, guards, supply lines, hospitals, detachments, sieges, retreats, cantonments.
- Reconnoissance: espionage (deepest interior info), skilled light troops, prisoners (useful but risky), hypotheses of probabilities (often sufficient), signals. Multiply methods; trust none completely; always prepare multiple hypothesis-based courses of action. This ability = "the real secret of military genius."
- Infantry (primary arm): half-deep columns of attack (shallower preferred) for offensive (solidity + mobility + momentum); mixed/deployed first line + rear columns good for offensive-defensive. Avoid excessive depth. Squares vs. cavalry. Terrain, troop quality, national characteristics govern choices. Protect from artillery with ground cover.
- Cavalry (1/6 open country strength): rapidity + impetuosity for opening/completing victories, pursuits, covering. Charges best when enemy infantry already engaged/shaken; flank/rear during friendly frontal attack. Reserves decisive. Lance + saber; regular organization primary; irregulars (Cossacks) excellent for partisan work.
- Artillery: formidable offensively (shatter lines, prepare assaults) and defensively. Concentrate on decisive point; 2/3 fire on troops not counter-battery; supports always; enfilade effects powerful. Command by strategist-tactician.
- Combined arms: only fundamental rule is mutual support. Post/employ per ground, object, enemy designs. Study history + experience.

### Conclusion and Final Reflections
- War in its ensemble is not a science but an art (dramatic, passionate, personal qualities, inspirations control combats). Strategy (especially) can be regulated by fixed laws resembling positive sciences.
- Morale produces real physical effects (impetuous 20k often superior to apathetic 40k).
- No tactical system succeeds against bad morale. Good maxims (those indicating employment of greatest means at decisive moment and place) point out errors to avoid.
- Offensive: select maneuver objectives to cut up enemy successively. Defensive: protract and defer decisive battle until enemy scattered/exhausted.
- Strategic *coup-d'œil*: habitually reduce choices to right/left/center (zone, front, battle line) while applying mass-at-decisive-point principles.
- Firearms improvements affect tactical details but not grand strategic principles (mass on decisive point + combined arms remain decisive). No regulation should prescribe absolute formation; forbid very deep columns.
- "It would be difficult to add much to what has been said on this subject in Chapters IV. and V."

### Maritime Expeditions Sketch
- Distinction: descents across narrow sea (fewer difficulties) vs. long open-sea transport (much higher risk).
- Historical survey (ancient to 19th c.) illustrates principles, scale, weather/logistics/combined-arms effects, and rarity of large successful distant invasions without secure nearby base/alliance.
- Modern (gunpowder, compass, steam) changed naval combinations and colonial expeditions.
