# Alexander the Great (Robin Lane Fox)

Classic narrative history of **[[alexander-the-great|Alexander]]** by the Oxford ancient historian Robin Lane Fox. First published 1973; this is the 2004 Penguin reissue with updates. The book is deliberately not a conventional "life" — Lane Fox stresses the poverty and distortions of the source tradition and presents a careful reconstruction grounded in the five main surviving accounts plus topography and practical military considerations.

## The Approach

Lane Fox opens with a long **Preface** that sets the methodological tone:
- No contemporary narrative survives intact. More than twenty contemporaries wrote on Alexander; none are extant in full.
- The main sources (Arrian, Plutarch, Diodorus Siculus, Quintus Curtius Rufus, Justin) are late and derive from lost intermediaries (especially Ptolemy and Aristobulus for Arrian).
- The author rejects easy psychologizing and the "mission" myth; he treats Alexander as a product of Macedonian kingship, Philip’s inheritance, Greek culture (via Aristotle), and the practical problems of holding a vast empire.
- Strong interest in logistics, river crossings, siege engineering, and the day-to-day conduct of the army.
- "This book is a search, not a story."

The narrative follows a largely chronological campaign structure while pausing for source criticism, character assessment, and the policy of "fusion."

This source supplies the richest single-volume **narrative spine** of the campaigns among currently ingested material. It adds:

- Strong treatment of Alexander as heir to Philip and the fourth-century Macedonian military revolution.
- Detailed handling of the "fusion" policy (intermarriage, Persian court elements, recruitment) and the army's reaction.
- Topographical and logistical realism that complements more abstract treatments.

## Cross-links & Historiography Notes

Lane Fox sits between the older narrative tradition (Jouguet etc.) and modern contextual/revisionist work. His portrait is neither romantic "civilizer" nor pure brutal conqueror; he stresses contingency, Philip's legacy, and the practical limits Alexander faced once the empire was won. Will be contrasted in the actor page and detailed bio with Thomas's contextual emphasis and the CAH chapters (Bosworth/Hornblower).
