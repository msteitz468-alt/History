# Eisenhower in War and Peace — Jean Edward Smith (2012)

One-volume narrative biography of Dwight David Eisenhower (1890–1969), Supreme Commander of the Allied Expeditionary Force in Europe during World War II and 34th President of the United States (1953–1961). 885 pages. Smith portrays Eisenhower as a figure of principle, decency, and subtle political skill who led the largest multinational force ever assembled to victory in Europe and then gave the United States eight years of peace, prosperity, and measured progress at home and abroad. The work rehabilitates Eisenhower’s reputation as both general (beyond mere “chairman of the board”) and president (far more consequential and politically adept than the “benign fatherly caretaker” image).

## Key Claims & Themes (to be expanded via subagents)

- Eisenhower as the “right man at the right place at the right time” for coalition command; FDR chose him over Marshall precisely because of his skill with British and French.
- The hidden virtue of the pre-WWII seniority promotion system: 16 years as major preserved intellectual independence (no need to flatter for efficiency reports).
- Deliberate public persona of casual, folksy demeanor concealed acute political judgment and capacity for consequential decisions.
- Signature decisions: (1) Torch logistics compromises (truck reduction) for speed; (2) final D-Day go despite marginal weather; (3) “You boys must be crazy” rejection of nuclear relief at Dien Bien Phu; (4) dispatch of 101st Airborne to Little Rock to enforce court order; (5) pressure on Britain/France/Israel to withdraw from Suez.
- Presidency achievements: ended Korean War, no American combat deaths for eight years, balanced budgets, Interstate Highway System (user-funded), St. Lawrence Seaway, NDEA federal education aid, Warren/Brennan Court appointments, enforcement of desegregation.
- Farewell warning on the military-industrial complex was heartfelt, not afterthought.
- Political style: made every task look easy; high sustained approval (avg 64%); progressive conservative who accepted New Deal gains as permanent.

**2. Rise / Path to Power (expand with this range):**
Eisenhower's selection for TORCH command (greatest amphibious to date: 400+ ships, 116k men, 2 weeks at sea) was foreordained by position in London and FDR's insistence on American lead to minimize French resistance, despite "no combat experience, virtually no command time, and ... most junior lieutenant general" (range lines 1–30). "Admiral King nor Secretary Stimson believed the new operation would ever take place... In that context there was little risk in selecting Eisenhower." FDR/Churchill overruled military objections. Ike impressed British with "openness, his fairness, and his determination." Marshall relationship central: Ike cabled constantly; Marshall overruled Ike's choice of Hartle for Fredendall ("one of Marshall's men", ang lophobe, "unencumbered with charisma"; lines 170–200). Ike's prewar political seasoning (Pershing Paris, MacArthur Washington/Manila, War Plans) equipped him for coalition but he "muffed the decision concerning Darlan" by focusing military (lines 710–740: "With the exception of Marshall and MacArthur, Eisenhower had more political experience than any officer in the American Army... Yet he muffed... because he ignored everything he had learned about the fractious nature of French politics").
