# The Conquest of New Spain (Bernal Díaz del Castillo, c. 1568)

The conqueror's-eye account of the [[spanish-conquest-of-mexico-1519|conquest of Mexico]] — the Americas
gap's **Spanish-eyewitness** primary, paired with the Nahua [[leon-portilla-broken-spears|*Broken
Spears*]] to give both sides of the same event. Bernal Díaz, an ordinary soldier of Cortés's company,
narrates the two coastal explorations, the march on Tenochtitlan, the welcome and seizure of Montezuma,
the expulsion (*Noche Triste*), and the siege and capture of the island-capital under Guatemoc.

## Source Notes

Read against the [[leon-portilla-broken-spears|Nahua account]], Bernal Díaz reveals how **convergent**
the two sides can be on the *facts* while differing in framing: both record the **gold-barter** motive,
the **prophecy of bearded men from the east**, the pivotal role of the **interpreter [[la-malinche|Doña
Marina]]**, the Spaniards' **revulsion at human sacrifice**, the **Tlaxcalan alliance** and the **Cholula
massacre**, the noble and "divided mind" of **Montezuma**, and the brave last defence and cruel end of
**Guatemoc (Cuauhtemoc)**. Where the Nahua elegies grieve, Bernal Díaz justifies — a genuine "sense of
mission," Christian crusading zeal, and (by his lights) moderation — even as he candidly records
**massacre, torture, and forcible conversion**, and the endless, often disappointed **search for gold**
(the booty's "royal fifth" was finally captured by a French privateer — the spoils of Montezuma's empire
ending up in the French treasury). The two sources together make the conquest a **two-sided history** and
expose the central mechanism both attest: a few hundred Spaniards prevailed by **indigenous alliances**
brokered through Marina, plus steel, horses, and [[virgin-soil-epidemics|disease]] — not by lone European
superiority. Bernal Díaz is light on Aztec civilization itself (covered by [[berdan-aztecs-2021|Berdan]])
and unreliable on numbers and on his own Christian-providential framing.
