## Series Note

*The Cambridge World History* (7 volumes, 2015) is the most comprehensive
Anglophone world history reference series. It covers all of human history —
not just the post-writing record — integrating archaeology and history as
complementary approaches. Volume I serves two functions: it introduces the
conceptual and methodological frameworks for the entire series (Part I), and
it covers the Paleolithic era specifically (Part II). Chapters represent
state-of-the-art scholarship as of 2015 from a deliberately international
and multi-disciplinary author pool.

**Editor-in-chief of series**: Merry E. Wiesner-Hanks (University of Wisconsin-Milwaukee)

**Volume editor**: David Christian (Macquarie University)

**Publication**: Cambridge University Press, 2015. ISBN 978-0-521-76333-2.

Eleven thematic essays, not tied to specific periods or regions. These generate
concept pages and historiography notes that will be embedded in period, event,

- Ch. 2 (Hughes-Warrington): history of world history writing as a discipline

for wiki/periods/ pages covering Deep Prehistory through Behavioral Modernity,
and will create event and process pages for: *Homo sapiens* emergence, behavioral
modernity, Out of Africa dispersal, regional colonizations.

## Volume Synthesis Note

*Written 2026-06-10 after full ingestion of all 19 chapters.*

**What this volume is.** CWH Vol. I does double duty. **Part I (Chs. 1–12)** is
a methodological and historiographical primer for the *entire* seven-volume
series — it is the source of the wiki's framing concepts (big history,
divergence/convergence, periodization theory, world-history-as-field,
Eurocentrism, gender history, technology-as-driver, family/biopolitics) and of
two process pages (fire, migration). **Part II (Chs. 13–19)** is a
state-of-the-2015 synthesis of the **Paleolithic**, and is the backbone of the
wiki's five prehistoric period pages and the global-dispersal narrative.

**The spine of Part II is a single story told continent by continent.** A small
African-origin population of behaviorally modern *Homo sapiens* dispersed across
the entire habitable world in the Late Pleistocene
([[paleolithic-global-dispersal]]). The volume's regional chapters are best read
as *segments of one process*:
- **Origins** (Chs. 13–15, Fernández-Armesto & Ehret): emergence in Africa;
  symbolic culture, syntactic language, foraging lifeways; African language-family
  and food-production deep roots.
- **Eurasia** (Chs. 16–17, Hoffecker & Dennell): dispersal across cold Eurasia,
  the Neanderthal encounter and replacement, the deep antiquity of the *Homo*
  genus outside Africa.
- **The two ends of the earth** (Chs. 18–19, Hiscock & Waguespack): the
  open-water leap to Sahul and the final overland leap to the Americas.

**The two recurring structural debates.** The volume surfaces the *same two
arguments* independently at both ends of the dispersal, which the wiki now
captures as paired controversy pages:
1. **Early/coastal vs. late/interior colonization timing** —
   [[australasian-colonization-timing]] and [[clovis-first-vs-pre-clovis]] are
   structurally identical debates (contested early dates, submerged coastal
   evidence, genetic-vs-archaeological tension) on opposite continents.
2. **Megafaunal extinction: humans vs. climate** —
   [[pleistocene-megafaunal-extinction]] is genuinely cross-continental, and the
   Australian and American records pull in opposite directions (Australia: long
   human prelude but no kill sites; Americas: short prelude but clear kill sites).

**Methodological throughline.** Part I's insistence that *archaeology and genetics
are primary evidence for the deep past* (not a poor substitute for texts) is what
licenses the whole volume — and it is the standing justification for treating
dating method and confidence interval as first-class data on every prehistoric
page in the wiki.

**Collection-bias note honored.** Per CLAUDE.md, the volume's African material
(Ehret) was used to push the wiki's sub-Saharan/African coverage from "absent"
toward "weak" for the Paleolithic, with the gap explicitly flagged for
post-Paleolithic Africa in `overview.md`.

**Forward links left open for later volumes/phases.** A standing dangling link
to `[[reliability-of-herodotus]]` awaits the Phase 4 Herodotus primary source.
The Folsom culture is referenced but not yet given its own page (awaiting a
dedicated Americas source). These are intentional, not omissions.
