# War of Supply: World War II Allied Logistics in the Mediterranean (Dworak, 2022)

David D. Dworak's *War of Supply* is the definitive administrative and logistical history of the Allied effort in the Mediterranean Theater from the first major US offensive (Operation Torch) through the invasion of southern France (Dragoon) and the final campaigns in Italy. It argues that logistics was not merely supportive but often decisive: the ability to project, sustain, and adapt massive forces across vast distances and onto hostile shores determined what strategy was possible.

The book traces the Allies' steep learning curve as "novices" in North Africa, "journeymen" in Sicily and Italy, and eventual "masters of the craft" in southern France and the late Italian campaign. It details the development of the theater's base section system, the challenges of amphibious logistics (DUKWs, beach clearance, sunken-ship piers), the use of pack animals in mountainous terrain, rail and road reconstruction, support for French rearmament, integration of Black and Women's Army Corps units, disease control, and the constant tension between combat demands and sustainment capacity.

## What it contributes

- Detailed reconstruction of the **Mediterranean base section system** and how it enabled successive amphibious operations.
- Operational-level analysis of logistics for Torch, Husky, Avalanche, Shingle/Anzio, and Dragoon — showing how supply constraints shaped (and sometimes halted) campaigns.
- Institutional history of the US Army Service Forces (SOS) and combined US-UK logistics organizations in a coalition theater.
- Emphasis on innovation under pressure: improvisation, joint (Army-Navy) coordination, and the "tyranny of distance and shipping."
- Historiographical point that "behind the curtain" sustainment deserves equal weight with generalship and tactics.

## Historiography Note

Dworak writes from the perspective of a military historian focused on sustainment. The work draws heavily on official US Army histories (e.g., Center of Military History) and is strongest on American service forces. It fills a significant gap in the operational historiography of the MTO, where combat narratives often obscure the administrative story. Valuable for understanding why certain strategies succeeded or stalled (e.g., limits on exploitation after Dragoon, challenges of the Italian campaign). Complements broader WWII logistics studies and is directly relevant to modern expeditionary and joint logistics doctrine.

"Of all the landing craft destroyed or damaged at Fedala, not a single one was hit by enemy fire." Blamed on "lack of training and experience of the coxswains" and "lack of recovery craft." (Ch. 1).

In reality, the engineers moved supplies to the closest dump regardless of type of item they were carrying and dumped cargo at the water’s edge." (Ch. 1).
- "On the first two convoys the Center Task Force included only 260 supply personnel in a total force of more than 60,000." Assumption of local Arab labor failed: "the local labor to be limited, unskilled, and in need of constant supervision." (Ch. 1).
- Eastern TF beaches: "quickly found its beaches chaotic. Supplies were dumped with little thought or plan." British beaches had "limited throughput capability." (Ch. 1).

- By late Nov, British First Army 50 miles from Tunis but stalled; "An Allied attack on November 24 failed, and German counteroffensives on November 27 and December 1 forced an Allied withdrawal. Eisenhower decided to wait out the winter..." (Ch. 1).
- "The decision to eliminate a large number of cargo trucks from the initial assault convoys was not just a US military oversight." British also held trucks back. (Ch. 1).
- Base sections established per Table 1: Mediterranean BS December 1942 Oran, Brig. Gen. Tom Larkin / Col. Edmond Leavey; Atlantic BS December 1942 Casablanca, Brig. Gen. Arthur Wilson / ... (Table 1, Ch. 2 "Establishing the Theater").
- "Following the landings in North Africa, each task force handled support responsibilities in its own respective area of operations. ... there was no formal communications zone or single support agency." (Ch. 2).
- TUP-SUP: "shipping one deuce-and-a-half TUP, compared to shipping two fully assembled vehicles, saved 59 percent cargo space." "The army and General Motors developed a plan to ship these same trucks disassembled..." Assembly lines at Oran and Casablanca by end 1942; one day at Oran: 143 trucks. (Ch. 2).

The War Department ... sailed a convoy less than three weeks later loaded with war matériel, including 5,000 trucks, 2,000 trailers, and 400 dump trucks. Between late February and late March, the War Department shipped an additional 24,000 vehicles and more than a million tons of supplies." (Ch. 2).

- Larkin vs Hughes views on SOS command: tensions lasted "well into 1944." AFHQ G4 for policy; SOS for execution. (Ch. 2).
- "the logistics situation is one to make a ritualist in warfare go a bit hysterical." — Eisenhower (letter to Marshall) on single rail line handling "only nine trains per day." (Ch. 2).
- Eastern BS activated Feb 13, 1943 at Constantine (Brig. Gen. Arthur Pence). Ports: Bougie, Philippeville, Bône. (Ch. 2).

Rommel received "only 40 percent of the 120,000 tons of requested supplies" first 8 months 1942. (Introduction, Ch. 1).
- "By January 1944, Rommel noted that the shortage of supply trucks ... he could receive only 50 tons of supplies per day rather than the 400 tons that he needed." (Ch. 2).

The American logistical system was finding its stride." (Ch. 3 "The Fall of Tunisia").

- Sabotage response: Eastern BS contracted French to secure pipelines with fines; "Almost overnight the shooting and vandalism stopped." (Ch. 3).

- "Enabling this victory was the establishment of a functioning communications zone. The base section commanders had a personal interest in caring for the forces they supported." (Ch. 3).
- "The surrender of the Axis forces in Tunisia signaled an opportunity to reset Allied forces..." Sicily planning already under way from Casablanca Conference. (Ch. 3).

### Husky Planning & Lessons from Torch/Tunisia (sustainment context)
- "Despite the lessons of Torch, Husky hardly served as a role model for joint-service planning. Perhaps this was understandable, given that operations were still going on in Tunisia, but it was not inevitable. The planning effort did improve in terms of anticipating the need for a more balanced ground force that included the necessary combat and combat-support elements. However, work remained to be done in integrating the air forces into the planning effort."
- "Ultimately, the lack of a ground tactical plan from the Seventh Army provided the biggest challenge for logistics planners. It meant that any logistics plan needed to be flexible enough to accommodate any combat plan. This same lack of a ground tactical plan also inhibited planning coordination among the joint services."
- Air plan disconnect: "The air plan called for daily landings of massive quantities of supplies and equipment—but with no specifications regarding weight, size, or projected use. The air command apparently expected this level of support even though its resupply plan called for convoys every four days despite the fact that the theater was planning convoys for every fourteen days."
- "To support the force in Sicily and to respond to changes and unforeseen requirements, the Eastern BS developed an extremely flexible support plan. The general depot was at Mateur, Tunisia, due to its close proximately to air and ground bases, seaports, and the rail and road network. Truck units and rail moved supplies and equipment to either Bizerte or Tunis for sea transport."

### Mounting the Force (Eastern BS responsibility; first theater-mounted assault)
- "Brigadier General Arthur Pence received word in late April that the Eastern BS would have a large role in equipping and supplying Force 343 units for the invasion and for loading the invasion force and accompanying supplies on the assault craft, a process known as 'mounting.' At the same time, the base section was deeply involved in supporting the Allied drive across Tunisia."
- "The Husky mounting operation was a new challenge for AFHQ and the North African theater. The War Department or commanders of the European theater had prepared and mounted forces for the earlier Torch invasion. This was the first time the North African theater would mount a sizeable assault using its own forces, and there was virtually no information available on exactly how to conduct such an operation."
- "The Eastern BS was responsible for the US mounting effort. It was to equip all troops participating in Husky from North Africa, provide thirty days of supplies for these forces, transport units to the ports of embarkation, and then load them on assault vessels."
- "By July 5, the mounting of Husky was complete. With little experience or time for preparation, the Eastern BS had loaded 77,520 personnel, 17,165 vehicles, and more than 12,000 tons of supplies onto the Force 343 assault craft."
- Timing lesson: "The mounting operation showed that the theater needed to allow at least sixty days between the completion of planning and the earliest sail date: nine days to complete load plans, thirty days to waterproof supplies and equipment, seven days to shift cargo to match the ship-specific transport quartermaster plans, and fourteen days to load a division."
- "The two divisions that were already in North Africa, the 1st and 3rd Infantry Divisions, had little time for in-depth load planning based on the final version of the Husky plan. ... Units had only forty-eight hours to develop their supply requirements."

### Beach and Port Operations in Sicily; Engineer Special Brigade as Advance Base
- "The 1st Engineer Special Brigade was to remedy many of the problems experienced in the beaches of North Africa. ... Not only was the brigade to perform the engineering functions of opening up the beaches, but it was also to act as the de facto base section in Sicily until the SOS could flow sufficient troops in to assume the logistics mission."
- "Each shore group consisted of an engineer regiment plus other support units, such as a medical battalion, quartermaster battalion, naval beach battalion, as well as ordnance and signal companies. ... Each beach group had the responsibility to organize its respective beaches. This included the tasks of unloading ships, marking aids and obstacles to navigation, constructing beach exits, providing security, setting up beach dumps..."
- DUKWs: "The DUKWs were the recognized heroes of the Sicily landings for both US and British units. Used for the first time in actual combat, these vehicles proved themselves more capable than anyone had imagined. Due to the soft sand of the beaches, DUKWs ended up hauling the vast majority of supplies and equipment from the beaches to the inland dumps..."
- "By the end of D+3, the Seventh Army had successfully landed 66,235 men, 17,766 tons of bulk cargo, and 7,416 vehicles on Sicilian beaches and the small ports of Licata and Empedale."
- "By July, the end of July, the Seventh Army had landed 111,824 men, 104,734 tons of cargo, and 21,512 vehicles."
- "Husky’s beach operations were not perfect, but they had greatly improved compared to the North African landings eight months earlier. New equipment had solved many problems experienced during the earlier invasion..."
- "Supplies were generally deposited on the beaches and then moved to inshore dumps, but in some cases essential supplies were taken off of LSTs, placed into DUKWs, and driven right to the front lines (during darkness), bypassing the dumps entirely. This method represented a vast improvement in support of the combat forces compared to the process used on the beaches of French Morocco and Algeria."

### Drive Inland, Palermo Port Clearance, Island Base Section Establishment
- "By July 23, Palermo was secure, and engineers began to repair the damage that retreating Germans had inflicted on the port. The damage was extensive but not prohibitive. The Germans had sunk thirty-four vessels and barges into the main shipping channels and along the docks."
- "The port of Palermo was large enough to hold thirty-six LSTs and fourteen Liberty ships—a substantial improvement to the limited capability of the southern Sicilian beaches. Thanks to the engineers’ focused efforts, the port was at 30 percent capacity within a week of its fall..."
- "On September 1, the 6625th provisional Base Area Group became a formal base section, the Island BS, under the theater SOS and commanded by Colonel Robert Sears. ... with the activation of the Island BS, the Seventh Army was now free to focus on any follow-on missions. The combat phase of Husky ended on August 17; Patton and his staff were looking ahead. The SOS and its Island BS took on the unglorified task of reequipping the divisions, mounting forces for the upcoming Italian invasion..."
- "By D+20, sufficient army-level service forces were ashore to begin relieving the 1st Engineer Special Brigade from its base support mission."

### Assessment of Innovations and Persistent Challenges
- "The impetus behind the creation of beach organizations was similar to that behind the creation of the theater base sections and the SOS in general—to take the burden of administrative issues off the ground tactical commander..."
