# Carthage Must Be Destroyed (Richard Miles, 2010)

## Overview

Richard Miles's *Carthage Must Be Destroyed* is a cultural and ideological
history of Carthage from its Phoenician origins to its destruction in 146 BCE
and its long afterlife in the Roman and Western imagination. Where most
treatments of Carthage are organised around the [[second-punic-war-218-201bce|Punic Wars]]
and the figure of [[hannibal|Hannibal]], Miles deliberately decentres the
military narrative — already densely covered in this wiki via the
[[warfare-hub|Warfare Hub]] and Goldsworthy — in favour of recovering Carthage
as a civilisation: its religion, its identity, its commercial empire, and above
all the way its history was written by the people who annihilated it.

## Central Arguments

1. **History written by the destroyers.** Carthage left almost no surviving
   literature; in 146 BCE Rome dispersed Carthage's libraries to the Numidian
   princes and levelled the city, leaving the Greco-Roman account
   unchallenged. The negative stereotype of the treacherous, cruel,
   child-sacrificing Carthaginian (*[[punic-faith|fides Punica]]*) is therefore
   a Greek and then Roman ideological construction, not a neutral record. See
   [[punic-faith]].
2. **Heracles–Melqart as ideological battleground.** Miles's organising thesis:
   the syncretism between the Tyrian god [[carthaginian-religion|Melqart]] and
   the Greek [[heracles-melqart-syncretism|Heracles]] was contested terrain
   across the western Mediterranean. Carthaginians, Sicilian Greeks, and finally
   Rome each appropriated the hero-god to legitimise their imperial claims;
   Hannibal's march "in the footsteps of Heracles" was the culmination of a
   Carthaginian Heracles-ideology that Rome then captured for itself.
3. **A commercial empire, not a land empire.** Carthage grew from a Tyrian
   colonial network ([[phoenician-colonization]]) into the hub of a western
   Mediterranean trading world. Its political and military structures (citizen
   oligarchy plus mercenary armies) flowed from this mercantile base.
4. **The Sicilian crucible.** Carthage's defining external relationship before
   Rome was the centuries-long rivalry with Greek [[syracuse|Syracuse]]
   ([[carthage-syracuse-rivalry]]), which shaped its military institutions and
   its negative Greek press.

## Volume Synthesis Note

Miles's book enters a wiki already dense in Punic *military* history (Goldsworthy plus the
West-Point-grade [[warfare-hub|Warfare Hub]] coverage of the three Punic Wars and their
battles). Its value here is therefore overwhelmingly **cultural, religious, and
historiographical**, and the ingest deliberately steered new pages toward what the
battle-narratives omit: Carthaginian religion, the tophet debate, the foundation myth, the
Sicilian-Greek crucible, and above all the two interlocking interpretive arguments that give
the book its spine — the [[heracles-melqart-syncretism|Heracles–Melqart syncretism]] as the
contested ideological currency of the western Mediterranean, and [[punic-faith|*fides
Punica*]] as the Roman fabrication that has shaped every later image of Carthage.

The book's chief **tension with existing wiki content** is one of method rather than fact:
where the CAH/Goldsworthy spine narrates the wars from broadly Polybian foundations, Miles
systematically reads those same Greco-Roman sources as a hostile construction and privileges
material culture and onomastics. On several specifics he sides *against* Polybius (e.g.
believing Appian on Hasdrubal's monarchical ambitions). His Heracles thesis is a strong,
sometimes maximalist frame, flagged as such on the relevant pages. Pleasingly, the book
**converges** with the wiki's existing source-critical treatment of [[battle-of-himera-480bce|Himera]]
(Asheri/CAH already rejected the "clash of civilisations" reading), which strengthens
confidence in the shared conclusion. Net effect: the wiki now carries a recognisably
Carthaginian-perspective layer over a Roman-perspective military backbone, with the
[[carthaginian-child-sacrifice]] controversy and the [[punic-faith]] concept making the
historiographical stakes explicit.
