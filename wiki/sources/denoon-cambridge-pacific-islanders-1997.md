# The Cambridge History of the Pacific Islanders

A single-volume Cambridge regional history of Oceania — the islands of [[polynesia|Polynesia]],
[[melanesia|Melanesia]] and [[micronesia|Micronesia]], with New Zealand Maori — from
Pleistocene settlement to the late-twentieth-century "end of insularity." Edited by Donald
Denoon with Stewart Firth, Jocelyn Linnekin, Malama Meleisea and Karen Nero, it is the
collection's principal source for the **post-contact narrative of Oceania**, the wiki's chief
remaining regional gap (the archaeological/voyaging deep history was already supplied by
[[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|CWH IV]] via [[pacific-voyaging-and-settlement]]).

The volume is organized in two parts — **The Pacific to 1941** (chs 1–8) and **The Pacific
since 1941** (chs 9–13) — and is unusual among Cambridge histories for making
**historiography its opening subject** (ch 1) and sustaining a critical, reflexive stance
throughout. Editor-authored chapters carry named-contributor inset essays (e.g. Spriggs on
Holocene prehistory, Ward on land tenure, van Meijl on the Maori King movement, Laenui on
the overthrow of the Hawaiian monarchy, Lai on the Fiji coups), and Islander voices
(the *Kumulipo*, Hau'ofa's "Our Sea of Islands") are woven into the text.

## Volume Synthesis Note

The volume's controlling argument is reflexive and revisionist: **"Oceania" and the "Pacific
Islander" are as much inventions of outside scholars and administrators as descriptions of a
real human community** — yet beneath the colonial categories there is a genuine regional history
of voyaging, exchange and shared experience. The book opens, unusually for a Cambridge history,
with [[pacific-islands-historiography|historiography]] (ch 1), and sustains that critical stance:
the threefold Polynesia/Melanesia/Micronesia scheme is a nineteenth-century racial construct; the
"island Eden" of [[primitive-affluence|primitive affluence]] is a colonial-planner myth; the
colonial "[[invention-of-the-native|native]]" is an administrative fiction; and even "insularity"
itself is, in Epeli Hau'ofa's reframing, a misperception of a connected "[[sea-of-islands|sea of
islands]]."

Across its two parts the volume narrates a single trajectory: from autonomous chiefly societies,
through the cumulative shocks of [[pacific-resource-raiding|resource raiding]],
[[missionary-conversion-pacific|conversion]], [[pacific-plantation-economy|plantations]],
[[pacific-depopulation|depopulation]] and colonial rule, to the rupture of the
[[pacific-war-1941|Pacific War]], the [[pacific-nuclear-testing|nuclear age]], and a contested
[[pacific-decolonisation|decolonisation]] that left the material and ideological worlds
"remade." It is candid about its own unevenness — rich for Hawai'i, Maori
[[new-zealand|New Zealand]], [[samoa|Samoa]] and [[tonga|Tonga]]; thin for West Papua and much
of [[melanesia|Melanesia]] — and embeds Islander voices (the *Kumulipo*, Hau'ofa, named
contributors) that sometimes subvert the editors' own arguments.

For the wiki this source **closes the chief remaining regional gap**: the collection already had
the deep archaeological/voyaging history of Oceania ([[pacific-voyaging-and-settlement]], CWH IV)
and the [[transpacific-economy|transpacific economy]] (CWH VII), but no post-contact narrative of
the island societies themselves. It connects outward to the wiki's existing
[[decolonization|decolonization]], colonial-administration, and World-Wars / Cold-War material,
and supplies the Pacific theatre's social underside to the warfare-hub's operational accounts of
the Pacific War.
