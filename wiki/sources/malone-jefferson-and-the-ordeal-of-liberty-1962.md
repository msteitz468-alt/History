# Jefferson and the Ordeal of Liberty — Dumas Malone (1962)

**Jefferson and His Time, Volume 3.** Comprehensive narrative biography covering Thomas Jefferson from the end of 1792 through his election as President in February 1801: the final and most crowded phase of his service as Secretary of State under Washington (1793); the Proclamation of Neutrality and the Genet affair; the origins of party division and the unsuccessful congressional attack on Hamilton (Giles Resolutions); retirement to Monticello (1794–1796) for farming, rebuilding the house, and family; the "Mazzei letter" controversy and road to opposition; election as Vice President (1796); the crisis of the Quasi-War with France, Alien and Sedition Acts, and the Kentucky and Virginia Resolutions; assumption of party leadership; and the contested election of 1800 culminating in the tie with Burr and victory in the House.

## Overview / Thesis

This volume carries Jefferson from the end of 1792 (still Secretary of State after Washington's unanimous re-election) to his own election as President in February 1801 after the famous tie with his running mate. Malone presents the decade as one of ordeal for Jefferson personally and for the cause of liberty he identified with the "spirit of 1776." After the relative clarity of the revolutionary and Parisian years, Jefferson faced a "succession of difficult situations": defining and implementing fair neutrality amid world war and revolution; managing (or declining to manage) the emerging partisan warfare at home; enduring three years of deliberate retirement while the country moved toward the extremes he feared; and then, as Vice President, confronting the direct legislative assault on political opposition and individual freedom represented by the Alien and Sedition Acts.

The first two chapters are transitional. The rest fall into four main parts of unequal length. The year 1793 was "inordinately long" — the longest Jefferson had yet spent — and receives full treatment as the pivotal period in which the young Republic defined its posture toward Europe. Jefferson was the primary architect, under Washington, of the policy of fair neutrality. Retirement brought the tranquillity he craved and the chance to build and farm, but it was incomplete; he was drawn back into leadership of the opposition. The final crisis (1798–1800) tested whether organized political opposition and basic civil liberty could survive a half-war atmosphere. Jefferson viewed the ordeal as that of Liberty herself and sought to preserve or restore the principles of the Revolution.

## Methodological Notes

- Strict chronological narrative so that development of mind and character can be observed before later events and controversies obscure the outlines.
- Heavy reliance on Jefferson's correspondence (especially with Madison, Monroe, and others), the Anas, contemporary newspapers, congressional records, and French diplomatic materials then available.
- Acknowledges that materials for these years are voluminous yet sometimes disappointing on certain negotiations and private motivations; retirement years have thinner papers.
- Balances Jefferson's official actions, partisan role, intellectual and scientific pursuits, and personal life (especially the long labor of rebuilding Monticello and agricultural experiments).
- Avoids both inflating Jefferson's direct control over Republican strategy and minimizing his identification of the Republican cause with national independence and human freedom.

## Key Contributions

- The most detailed scholarly account of Jefferson's conduct of the 1793 neutrality policy and the day-to-day management of the Genet crisis.
- Clear anatomy of the retirement years as both genuine withdrawal and preparation for renewed leadership; full treatment of the physical reconstruction of Monticello and agricultural work.
- Authoritative narrative of the emergence of party leadership, the 1796 election, and Jefferson's vice-presidential years.
- Extensive analysis of the Kentucky and Virginia Resolutions in context, including Jefferson's original draft of the Kentucky Resolutions and the distinction between his and Madison's positions.
- Demonstration that the "ordeal" was as much about the survival of political opposition and free expression at home as about foreign policy.

## Range 01 Claims

- **Jefferson’s living arrangements and household in Philadelphia at the start of 1793**: At the beginning of the year, approaching fifty, Thomas Jefferson “was living in the house on the south side of High Street, often called Market, that he had occupied since his first weeks in Philadelphia as secretary of state, though he had told his landlord that he would vacate it before spring.” He maintained five servants, four horses, and the maitre d’hotel Petit (brought from France). He hosted non-official dinners (styled simply “Th: Jefferson”) before packing. (Early narrative in “The Secretary of State Carries On”; corroborated by chronology notes on shipping furniture/books ~April 9 and move to Schuylkill house.)

- **Jefferson’s firm prior decision to retire at end of Washington’s first term, announced in September 1792**: “Soon he expected to be wholly a private gentleman, for he had solemnly informed George Washington of his firm decision to retire at the end of the first term of this first President.” Washington was “biding his time, keeping his peace, hoping against hope that he could retain his first assistant.” The decision was driven by personal weariness and political principles rather than immediate world events. (Narrative opening; cross-referenced to Sept. 9, 1792 letter in footnotes and later Feb. 7, 1793 conversation memo.)

- **Washington’s reluctance to continue and Jefferson’s decision on Feb. 7, 1793 to stay on for months**: Washington “candidly admitted that he would have been chagrined if his re-election had not been by a ‘pretty respectable vote’” but felt no pleasure in another term. Jefferson informed the President of his postponement on February 7 (two days after the foreign intercourse bill passed the Senate). He cited personal attacks by Hamilton in the fall and the desire not to retire “under fire” or appear to lack firmness; he told TMR: “This must depend in some degree on the will of those who troubled the waters before. When they suffer them to get calm, I will go into port.” Washington was “much relieved.” Jefferson had said “perhaps till summer, perhaps autumn.” (Feb. 7, 1793 memo of conversation; letters to TMR Feb. 3 and Mar. 3; narrative in “The Secretary of State Carries On.”)  
  This decision **preceded_by** the escalating European war news and **contributed_to** his continued role in shaping neutrality policy. It was **enabled** by the foreign intercourse bill’s passage but **not caused_by** immediate foreign crises (which he did not yet fully know).

- **Cabinet consultations becoming more frequent and collegial on foreign affairs in winter 1792–1793**: The President was “now consulting the department heads as a group more frequently than hitherto, the Attorney General being included while the Vice President had dropped out.” Group conferences (sometimes called “executive council”) arose from crises; foreign and Indian affairs drove them more than domestic finance. Hamilton’s policies remained more distinctively his own. Jefferson cooperated well with Washington (common Virginian and agricultural interests; retained “reverence for Washington’s character and… respect for the older man’s judgment”) but had “considerably less than perfect” harmony with Hamilton. Randolph and (to some degree) Knox aligned more with Jefferson on foreign questions. (Narrative; Feb. 7 conversation notes.)

- **Jefferson’s strong objection to Washington’s suggested “coalition” with Hamilton (Feb. 1793)**: Washington “advanced his old arguments about effecting a ‘coalition’ with Hamilton.” Jefferson “objected to chiefly on domestic grounds.” He believed “a sacrifice of his ‘general system’ by either man was impossible” and renewed allegations of improper Treasury influence: “My wish was to see both houses of Congress cleansed of all persons interested in the bank or public stocks… I subscribe to the principle that the will of the majority honestly expressed should give law.” He confirmed Southern discontent due to “a ‘corrupt squadron’ in Congress under command of the Secretary of the Treasury.” Washington spoke of the “extreme wretchedness” of his own existence in office. (Memo of conversation, Feb. 7, 1793; narrative.)

- **Jefferson’s personal enjoyment of the Blanchard balloon ascent (Jan. 1793) and desire for faster travel home**: On a mild January day, with daughter Maria (age 14), Jefferson witnessed Jean-Pierre Blanchard’s first U.S. balloon ascent from the Walnut Street jail yard before ~40,000 people (per Dr. Rush). He paid for a ticket and made a substantial subscription. To Martha he wrote: “The security of the thing appeared so great, that everybody is wishing for a balloon to travel in. I wish for one sincerely, as instead of ten days I should be within five hours of home.” Blanchard traveled ~15 miles in 46 minutes and landed in New Jersey. (Jan. 1793 narrative, Account Book entries, letters; chronology notes Washington passport Jan. 9.)

- **Giles resolutions as a Virginian-led congressional attack on Hamilton’s Treasury conduct (Jan.–Mar. 1793)**: William Branch Giles (Virginia, Princeton graduate, studied under Wythe) offered resolutions (Jan. 23) seeking detailed accounts of loans, disposition of funds, and Sinking Fund operations. Hamilton responded with powerful reports. Jefferson had “no sufficient reason to suppose that he either instigated the attack or devised the tactics”; it was “best designated as Virginian.” Jefferson “thoroughly agreed with the severe criticisms” and had said similar things officially to Washington, but “kept himself aloof from ‘all cabal and correspondence on the subject of the government’” (per his Feb. 7 statement to Washington). (Detailed narrative in “An Attack that Failed: The Giles Resolutions”; *Annals*; Madison to Pendleton Feb. 23; TJ to TMR Mar. 3; John Taylor of Caroline pamphlet.)

- **Jefferson’s view that Giles resolutions defeat was due to “paper men”/financial interest, not merits (early Mar. 1793)**: After overwhelming rejection, Jefferson wrote TMR that elections had been favorable to Republicans south of Connecticut and that a “decided majority of the next Congress” would act in a “very different spirit.” He attributed the immediate defeat to “the votes of men who were attached to Hamilton and his policies by ties of financial interest.” He gave “no intimation whatever that he himself thought the resolutions went too far.” A rough draft (later supplied by descendants) contained stronger final resolutions (including calls for an independent treasurer and Hamilton’s removal) not offered by Giles; the actual Giles version was milder. Jefferson may have participated in private discussion or drafted an early version, but “the inception of the attack cannot be attributed to him in fairness.” (TJ to TMR Mar. 3; memo Mar. 2; narrative analysis; contrast with Hamilton’s earlier Catullus attacks on Jefferson.)

- **Recognition of new French government and “Catholic principle of republicanism” (late 1792 into early 1793 context in narrative)**: Jefferson laid down: “It accords with our principles to acknowledge any government to be rightful which is formed by the will of the nation substantially declared.” With Washington’s concurrence he instructed Morris that the U.S. could not deny other nations “that principle whereon our government is founded, that every nation has a right to govern itself internally under whatever forms it pleases… The only thing essential is the will of the nation.” He called this “the Catholic principle of republicanism.” Hamilton (thinking in terms of rulers, not the people) would not have concurred. Payments to France were suspended temporarily for regularity but viewed as temporary; Jefferson saw the National Convention as legitimate. (Narrative; letters to Morris Nov. 7 and Dec. 30, 1792; to Pinckney Dec. 30; cabinet consultations.)

- **Jefferson’s nuanced private and public stance on the French Revolution and execution of Louis XVI (early 1793)**: In a private letter to Short (Jan. 3) he chided “extreme warmth” in criticisms of Jacobins (at Washington’s injunction) while defending the republican element. He viewed the choice as now between revolution and King; “expunging” the King had become necessary. Publicly and in principle he supported the Republic (99/100 of Americans did, per him; Hamilton admitted the “popular tide” favored it). On the execution he later summarized (autobiography) that he would not have voted for it (“I should not have voted with this portion of the legislature”) but at the time held monarchs “amenable to punishment like other criminals” and wasted little sympathy on Louis when “more innocent men than he had died.” He still believed “the liberty of the whole earth depended on the issue of the contest.” Monroe reported Virginians saw execution and Lafayette’s fate as “incidents to a much greater one.” (TJ to Short Jan. 3; TJ to Madison Mar.; narrative; Monroe to TJ May 8; Carrington to Hamilton Apr. 26.)

- **Jefferson’s early articulation of U.S. neutrality policy (late Mar. 1793 letters)**: “The scene in Europe is becoming very interesting. Amidst the confusion of a general war which seems to be threatening that quarter of the globe, we hope to be permitted to preserve the line of neutrality. We wish not to meddle with the internal affairs of any country, nor with the general affairs of Europe. Peace with all nations, and the right which that gives us with respect to all nations, are our object. It will be necessary for all our public agents to exert themselves with vigilance for securing to our vessels all the rights of neutrality, and from preventing the vessels of other nations from usurping our flag.” This was “a major clue to the policy which Jefferson consistently pursued as Secretary of State” and contained “some of the germs of the policy later pronounced by his disciple Monroe in his famous Doctrine.” No thought of hitching the U.S. to any nation’s “war chariot.” Exception noted for Spain (control of Mississippi mouth). (Late Mar. 1793 to American representatives abroad; narrative in “Peaceful Intentions in a World of War.”)  
  **Preceded_by** earlier recognition principles; **produced** later formal Proclamation actions (per chronology anchors); **contributed_to** long-term U.S. policy.

- **Anticipation of Genet’s arrival and underestimation of trouble (early 1793)**: Jefferson knew from Col. William S. Smith that “Edmond Charles Genet was coming to replace the unhappy Ternant; and, fortunately for his own peace of mind, Jefferson did not anticipate the vast trouble the new envoy would cause.” Ternant learned of replacement via Smith; this affected his financial negotiations (application for 3 million livres on U.S. debt to France for provisions). (Narrative around Feb.–early 1793; chronology: Genet lands Charleston Apr. 8, arrives Philadelphia May 16.)

- **Jefferson’s personal burdens and desire for retirement amid 1793 events**: “It proved to be a momentous year in human history, and, except for his last one as governor of Virginia during the American Revolution, the most trying and discouraging that he had yet spent in public life. It was marked by the Terror in France and a scourge of yellow fever in Philadelphia.” He was “worn down with drudgery.” He moved to a little house on the Schuylkill near Gray’s Ferry ~early April (agreed with Moses Cox); shipped ~50+ packing cases of furniture/books to Richmond for storage; planned to return to Monticello as “the last long journey I shall ever take, except the last of all.” (Narrative opening of year; letters to TMR May; chronology notes on yellow fever Sept., departure Sept. 11 after informing Genet of recall request, formal resignation Dec. 31.)

## Range 02 Claims

- **Jefferson favored a consistent policy of neutrality in the European war from its extension to Britain onward, while remaining willing to consider belligerence on American continental questions such as the Mississippi**: "From the beginning to the end he favored a policy of neutrality." He viewed the United States as standing to gain from Old World distress via diplomacy rather than military entanglement, though "with respect to the interests of his own country, particularly its territorial interests, he was positively avaricious and at times sounded ruthless." (early spring 1793 context, "PEACEFUL INTENTIONS IN A WORLD OF WAR 65"; preceded_by Spanish concerns and 1792 executive council discussions; concurrent_with extension of war to Britain/Holland Feb. 1, 1793 and Spain March 7; contrasted with Hamilton's positions; enabled later U.S. territorial gains like Louisiana/Floridas; [[actors/jefferson-thomas]], [[actors/hamilton-alexander]], [[actors/washington-george]]).

- **Jefferson was prepared to consider war with Spain over the Mississippi navigation and viewed Spain as a waning power that could be challenged with relative safety**: Discussions in the executive council autumn 1792 showed Jefferson wanting to refer a Spanish communication to Congress (war declaration prerogative), while Hamilton talked most of peace and even broached a British alliance. Jefferson instructed American commissioners in Spain (Carmichael and Short) accordingly and welcomed reports that the French purposed to free Spanish colonies, instructing them "not to offer Spain any American guarantee of these." He had earlier been willing to guarantee Spanish possessions west of the Mississippi in exchange for cession of those on the east, but the situation changed. (autumn 1792–early 1793; TJ to C. W. F. Dumas, Mar. 24, 1793; memo of Oct. 31, 1792 and TJ to Carmichael and Short, Nov. 3, 1792/1793; contributed_to by Spanish-B British concert reports; preceded_by growing U.S. strength on western waters; [[actors/jefferson-thomas]], [[actors/hamilton-alexander]]; main thread should note Spanish agents Viar and Jaudenes).

- **Jefferson perceived the European war (now general, embracing the Atlantic world) as one between nations and also between revolution and counter-revolution, with Hamilton clearly aligned against the French Republic and Jefferson in support of it, though neither was simply "pro-French" or "pro-British" in the partisan sense**: Hamilton wrote Washington that the war "turns essentially on the point — What shall be the future government of France? Shall the royal authority be restored in the person of the successor to Louis, or shall a republic be constituted in exclusion of it?" Jefferson had long concluded France was the "natural friend" of the United States; he "hoped for French victory" but believed the U.S. could serve France better as a neutral, with plentiful provisions offsetting small armed might. He remained "a devoted friend of the French cause" while concluding belligerency would be "disastrous to the United States." (news of war extension received Philadelphia early April 1793; "PEACEFUL INTENTIONS IN A WORLD OF WAR"; concurrent_with French declaration on Britain/Holland Feb. 1 and Spain March 7; contrasted_with Hamilton's indiscretions with British minister Hammond; [[actors/jefferson-thomas]], [[actors/hamilton-alexander]], [[actors/washington-george]]).

- **Jefferson had constitutional and tactical objections to issuing a formal Proclamation of Neutrality (or using the word), though he supported warning citizens against unneutral acts and ultimately acquiesced in its issuance on April 22, 1793**: He argued a declaration of no-war was as much beyond executive competence as a declaration of war (prerogative of Congress/legislature); he suspected Hamilton wanted to bind future conduct by executive act and pressed executive powers to their limit. He preferred a declaration that the country was "in a state of peace" in which it was citizens' duty "neither to aid nor injure any of the belligerents," to be preserved by the Executive until Congress determined otherwise. He also saw it as premature, surrendering bargaining power with the British (who might buy neutrality with concessions on issues he had long pressed). The word "neutrality" was omitted from the final document at his instance, but he used it himself privately and publicly. He later called the instrument "badly drawn," "officious and improper," "cold caution," "milk and water views," even "pusillanimous" in private letters, though he enforced the policy assiduously. (Washington's questions submitted April 18 after return from Mount Vernon; unanimous agreement at first cabinet meeting April 19 to issue proclamation and receive French minister; drafting by Randolph; TJ memo April 18/May 6 and letters; "FAIR NEUTRALITY IN THEORY AND PRACTICE"; [[actors/jefferson-thomas]], [[actors/hamilton-alexander]], [[actors/washington-george]], [[actors/randolph-edmund]]; produced the April 22 Proclamation; [[events/proclamation-of-neutrality-1793]]).

- **Jefferson wrote a major April 28, 1793 "Opinion" to Washington defending the continuing validity of the 1778 Franco-American treaties (alliance and commerce) against Hamilton's arguments that they should be regarded as "temporarily and provisionally suspended" or voidable**: Hamilton cited Vattel ("useless, dangerous, or disagreeable") and argued change in government (especially to one inviting revolution/insurrection) provided "good and sufficient cause" for renunciation/suspension; he viewed the war as offensive/acquisitive on France's part, so the defensive guarantee of French West Indies did not apply. Jefferson laid down principles: nations (not kings like Louis Capet) have the right to change their government; "the people constitute the nation"; treaties were made between the two nations and remained in effect despite form-of-government changes. Dangers must be "imminent and the degree great"; "If possibilities would avoid contracts... there never could be a valid contract." He examined specific treaty provisions (warships/privateers/prizes, prohibition on enemies fitting privateers, West Indies guarantee) and found no real danger from them; renunciation would itself be a breach of neutrality "by giving just cause of war to France." He marshaled Grotius, Puffendorf, and Wolf against the selective Vattel quote and called the "scrap" culled for "immoral consequences" a fitting rebuke given Hamiltonian talk of "contracts." Washington had "never doubted the validity of the treaties"; Jefferson's paper became to all practical purposes the official U.S. position. (cabinet meetings post-April 17; Hamilton's two written opinions April/May 2; Jefferson's paper with covering letter; "FAIR NEUTRALITY IN THEORY AND PRACTICE"; produced official policy on treaties; [[actors/jefferson-thomas]], [[actors/hamilton-alexander]], [[actors/washington-george]], [[actors/randolph-edmund]]; [[events/proclamation-of-neutrality-1793]]).

- **Jefferson initially viewed Citizen Genet's mission positively and took him at face value as an "evangel of freedom," reporting that "It is impossible for anything to be more affectionate, more magnanimous than the purport of his mission" and that the French "offered everything and asked nothing" (no call for West Indies guarantee, commercial privileges in colonies, new liberal treaty offer)**: Genet landed Charleston early April (before Proclamation), arrived Philadelphia May 16, presented credentials May 17 via Jefferson. He received enthusiastic popular receptions, civic feasts, addresses, toasts, singing of Marseillaise, cap of liberty placed on his head at Oeller's Hotel dinner (~200 present), and other fetes; Jefferson noted "thousands collected on the beaches" for the Embuscade's prize arrival and "prodigious joy" among the "yeomanry of the city (not the fashionable part nor paper men)." Jefferson drafted friendly letters and was confirmed in his view of French friendliness. (news April 22; TJ to Madison May 19; to Washington; "IMPACT OF A MISSIONARY: CITIZEN GENET"; preceded_by advance reports from W. S. Smith Feb. 20 and Condorcet letter; concurrent_with popular "old spirit of 1776" rekindling; [[actors/jefferson-thomas]], [[actors/genet-edmond]], [[actors/washington-george]], [[places/philadelphia]]; [[events/citizen-genet-affair-1793]]; contributed_to later disillusionment).

- **Jefferson enforced "twin principles" of neutrality as primary duty of the Secretary of State: "that it is the right of every nation to prohibit acts of sovereignty from being exercised by any other within its limits; and the duty of a neutral nation to prohibit such as would injure one of the warring powers"**: He emphasized duties no less than rights. The U.S. would apply principles "impartially to both parties." He handled protests over the Grange (British merchant ship taken by Embuscade in Delaware Bay/American waters; prize brought to Philadelphia; British demanded restoration/compensation; U.S. ordered restoration of ship/cargo and release of prisoners via opinion of Randolph, May 1793; French consul actions in Charleston condemning prizes ruled legal nullity and disrespectful to U.S. sovereignty). He agreed the historic three-mile limit (cannon-shot range) should be adopted (smallest distance claimed by any nation; decided by Washington for practical reasons; acceptable to belligerents; Jefferson would have preferred wider jurisdiction). (post-Proclamation enforcement; TJ to Genet June 5; to Hammond/Ternant May 3/15; "FAIR NEUTRALITY IN THEORY AND PRACTICE"; produced successful fair-neutrality policy later attested by British (Grenville to Hammond Jan. 1794; Canning tribute 1823); [[actors/jefferson-thomas]], [[actors/genet-edmond]], [[actors/hamilton-alexander]]; [[events/citizen-genet-affair-1793]]).

- **Jefferson opposed Hamilton's proposed system of using customs collectors (responsible to Treasury) for espionage/information on neutrality violations, favoring grand juries/judges and ordinary legal machinery instead; he suspected it as an improper expansion of Treasury influence ("amply provided with business, patronage, and influence")**: After inconclusive conference, he wrote Randolph at length; Randolph's reply was disappointing ("fence-straddling"), admitting suspicions of revenue officers as "a corps, trained to the arts of spies" but accepting Hamilton's assurances and modifications (report to district attorneys/governors). Hamilton issued modified instructions Aug. 4. Jefferson described Randolph as "the most indecisive one I ever had to do business with... always contrives to agree in principle with one but in conclusion with the other"; cabinet votes often 2/2 with Randolph split "one of these supported Jefferson while the other went along with Hamilton and Knox." (May 1793 memos/letters; TJ to Madison May 12; to Monroe May 5; "FAIR NEUTRALITY"; contributed_to ongoing cabinet divisions; [[actors/jefferson-thomas]], [[actors/hamilton-alexander]], [[actors/randolph-edmund]]).

- **Jefferson initially took a moderate position on French privateers already armed/outfitted in U.S. ports and their prizes (e.g., Little Sarah/Little Democrat case), opposing immediate restoration to British owners or ordering vessels away but later recognizing U.S. responsibility to indemnify and charge against debt to France; he firmly opposed future arming/outfitting in U.S. ports on neutrality grounds ("the treaty leaving us free to refuse, and the refusal being necessary to preserve fair and secure neutrality")**: In cabinet (May 20), Hamilton/Knox favored restoration or ordering away; Randolph favored ordering privateer away; Jefferson opposed ordering either away (believing outfitting by Frenchmen before rules known); Washington approved Randolph's position. Genet did not cooperate; the vessel later sailed. Jefferson opposed Hamilton's force proposals.
