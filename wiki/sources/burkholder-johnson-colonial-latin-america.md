# Colonial Latin America (Burkholder & Johnson, 10th ed., 2019)

The standard textbook on the **colonial period** (c. 1492–1825) of Spanish America and Brazil — the
Americas gap's systematic account of the three centuries between conquest and independence. It is the
connective tissue between the wiki's pre-Columbian pages and the modern era: how a conquered hemisphere
became a set of European colonial societies, with their distinctive institutions (encomienda, the
*mita*, the hacienda), their silver-driven economy, their racial *sociedad de castas*, the Church, and
the Bourbon/Pombaline reforms that set the stage for independence.

## Volume Synthesis Note

Burkholder and Johnson's *Colonial Latin America* gives the wiki its first
systematic, full-length treatment of the **three colonial centuries (c. 1492–1825)**
— the long-missing middle term between the pre-Columbian civilizations and the
modern republics. Its overarching argument is that the colonial order was a
**process, not a plan**: privately financed, locally led conquests ([[pizarro-francisco|Pizarro]]
at [[spanish-conquest-of-peru-1532|Cajamarca]] copying Cortés) seized two
densely populated, wealthy cores; the Crowns followed *after*, slowly imposing
institutions of government, Church, and economy. The result was an empire whose
defining characteristic — across [[colonial-spanish-american-administration|administration]],
the [[spiritual-conquest-and-colonial-church|Church]], and the
[[encomienda|labor systems]] — was **negotiated, decentralized, "rooted" rule**
rather than the centralized absolutism the textbook of empire might suggest. The
**sale of offices** creolized the bureaucracy; the *patronato* fused Church and
Crown; local elites captured the machinery from within.

Three threads run through the whole volume and now structure the wiki's coverage.
**First, coercion and catastrophe**: the [[virgin-soil-epidemics|demographic
collapse]] (serial epidemics with region-varying nadirs) is the master fact, opening
the land to [[hacienda|estates]] and livestock and making forced labor — the
[[encomienda]] sequence, the [[potosi-and-colonial-silver|Potosí]]
[[mita-and-andean-reciprocity|mita]], the [[atlantic-slave-trade|African slave
trade]] — the foundation of the economy. **Second, a society ordered by race but
not rigidly by biology**: the [[casta-system|sociedad de castas]], where wealth,
lineage, and culture could whiten, *limpieza de sangre* could exclude, and a fluid
urban world contrasted with the rigid rural estate. **Third, the silver-and-slavery
world economy**: Potosí and Mexican silver feeding a [[global-silver-trade|global
bullion system]] via the fleet and the Manila galleon, the receiving end of the
Atlantic slave trade in Brazil and the Caribbean.

The volume's final movement — the [[bourbon-reforms|Bourbon and Pombaline reforms]]
and [[spanish-american-independence|independence]] — supplies the wiki's most
important new causal chain for the Americas. The eighteenth-century drive to
recentralize, tax, and defend the empire (intendants, *comercio libre*, expanded
armies, the [[spiritual-conquest-and-colonial-church|Jesuit expulsion]]) raised
revenue but **alienated the Creole elite**, who were the empire's necessary
partners. When Napoleon's 1808 invasion voided royal legitimacy, that loaded charge
exploded — not, at first, into a planned anticolonial revolt but into a **legitimacy
crisis** that Ferdinand VII's 1814 absolutism converted into wars of independence.
The deep continuity Burkholder and Johnson stress — that the [[hacienda|hacienda]],
the [[casta-system|racial hierarchy]], slavery, and the export economy **survived
the political rupture** — is precisely the bridge into the wiki's existing
[[latin-america-modern|modern Latin America]] page (Charlip/CWH VII): the
international/domestic matrix and dependency of the nineteenth century inherit
directly from the colonial structures mapped here.

**Cross-volume connections.** The book reframes and connects the wiki's
archaeology-derived Americas pages ([[inca-empire]], [[aztec-empire]],
[[maya-civilization]]) by giving the conquest its colonial sequel; it joins
[[mann-1491-2005|Mann's *1491*]] (the demographic-catastrophe argument, now extended
through the colonial epidemic waves) and the conquest primaries ([[leon-portilla-broken-spears|Broken
Spears]], [[diaz-conquest-of-new-spain|Bernal Díaz]]); and it ties the Americas to
the wiki's global-early-modern spine ([[global-silver-trade]],
[[atlantic-slave-trade]], [[catholic-reformation]]). The moral-historiographical
frame throughout is the [[black-legend|Black Legend]] debate. The standing
Americas-gap counterpart still to ingest is Galeano's *Open Veins of Latin America*
(the dependency-theory polemic) — which will productively *contest* this textbook's
more measured synthesis.
