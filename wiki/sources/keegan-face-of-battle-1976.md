# The Face of Battle — John Keegan (1976)

Keegan's purpose is stated as a question he says military history evades: **"what is it like to be
in a battle?"** Writing as a Sandhurst lecturer who had "never been in a battle," he sets out to
recover the experience of the ordinary combatant, and in doing so writes both a sustained critique
of how battles had been described and a demonstration of a better way.

## The argument (Chapter 1)

Keegan's target is the **"battle piece"** — the conventional set-piece battle narrative "as old as
Herodotus." Dissecting Napier on Albuera (1811) and modern historians (Peter Young, David Chandler,
Michael Howard), he isolates its recurring faults: an impossible **uniformity of human behaviour**
("no individual turns tail and runs, drops down to sham dead"); **discontinuous movement** (armies
advance, exchange fire, and the enemy is suddenly broken, with no mechanism shown); a **two-tier
caste** of named "leaders" and anonymous "led" / "mass"; and, most tellingly, the **dematerialising
of the dead and wounded**, who "dematerialize as soon as struck down." Against this he sets a
positive program (see [[face-of-battle-method]]): the historian must **make up his own mind**;
attend to **all** participants (private soldier, casualty, the wounded, the frightened), not just
the general; reconstruct from the physical evidence — weapon capabilities, the dimensions of the
body, the ground — in the manner of **Delbrück** and **Burne's "Inherent Military Probability"**;
and treat fear, compulsion, cohesion, drink, and leadership as first-class causes.

## What the three battles show

- **Agincourt (1415):** the longbow did not simply shoot the French down — armour "deflected" most
  arrows; the decisive killing was done by the **crush** (the packed French column driven onto the
  English by "unrelenting pressure from the rear"), the **tumbling effect**, and the archers'
  small-group butchery of fallen men-at-arms. Keegan demythologises the "wall of dead" and re-reads
  the **killing of the prisoners** as a threat-driven "cattle-drive," not a massacre of thousands.
- **Waterloo (1815):** Keegan breaks the day into **categories of combat** and shows why cavalry
  could not break **steady squares** (horses refuse "the line of serried steel"), why cavalry could
  "take" but not remove guns, and that the great French columns broke **from the rear first** — a
  Canetti-style "flight crowd" of men who could see nothing. He foregrounds **fatigue, smoke,
  noise, drink, and the dread of standing "to be cannonaded."**
- **The Somme (1 July 1916):** the **Pals battalions**; the week-long bombardment that failed
  (wire uncut, deep dug-outs intact, the wrong shells); the **race for the parapet** won by German
  machine-gunners; ~**60,000 casualties, ~21,000 killed, most in the first hour**; and a medical
  chain that left the wounded to die in no-man's-land. Keegan's controversial Auschwitz analogy for
  "those long docile lines… plodding forward… to their own extermination."
