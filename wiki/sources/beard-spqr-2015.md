# SPQR: A History of Ancient Rome (Mary Beard, 2015)

Mary Beard's *SPQR* (Profile Books, 2015; ~536 pp.) is a synthetic single-author
history of Rome's first millennium, from the traditional foundation (753 BCE) to the
**Constitutio Antoniniana of 212 CE**, when Caracalla made every free inhabitant of the
empire a Roman citizen. The title — *Senatus PopulusQue Romanus*, "The Senate and People
of Rome" — signals the book's governing interest: not the deeds of great conquerors but
the shifting relationship between Roman institutions and the ever-widening body of people
who counted as Roman.

## Argument and Method

Beard organises the book around a set of deliberately anti-triumphalist theses:

- **Growth, not decline.** The book is "about how Rome grew and sustained its position
  for so long, not about how it declined and fell, if indeed it ever did in the sense that
  Gibbon imagined." She rejects Constantine's conversion (337 CE) or the sack of 410 CE as
  natural endpoints in favour of 212 CE — a *culminating* moment rather than a terminal one.
- **Citizenship as the engine.** The expansion of Roman citizenship — extended to defeated
  Latins and Italians, to freed slaves, to provincials — is the thread that runs "almost a
  thousand years" from the regal period to Caracalla. Rome's capacity to absorb outsiders as
  citizens, rather than merely rule them as subjects, is offered as the structural
  explanation for its durability. See [[concepts/roman-citizenship]].
- **No grand plan.** "The Romans did not start out with a grand plan of world conquest."
  The motives behind expansion remain "one of history's great puzzles"; Rome grew inside a
  Mediterranean world of endemic violence and rival mini-empires, not by trampling peaceable
  peoples.
- **Not admiration.** Beard "bridles" at talk of "great" Roman conquerors. Roman victory was
  "undoubtedly vicious" — Caesar's conquest of Gaul "has not unfairly been compared to
  genocide," a charge Romans such as Tacitus ("they create desolation and call it peace")
  levelled at the time.
- **Skepticism about early Rome.** For the regal and early Republican centuries "there are no
  accounts written by contemporary Romans at all"; the story is "a bold work of reconstruction"
  squeezing pottery fragments and inscriptions. The Romulus and regal narratives are read as
  later Roman myth-history — culturally revealing, not literally reliable.
- **One millennium, many histories.** "There is no single story of Rome." Different periods
  demand different kinds of history; the history of Rome is not the history of Roman Britain or
  Roman Africa. Beard keeps her focus on the city and Italy but repeatedly "looks in at Rome
  from the outside."

Methodologically the book showcases how Roman history "is always being rewritten": new
priorities (gender, food supply, the lives of the poor) and new evidence (the Herculaneum
cesspit diet, Greenland ice-cap lead pollution, a Galen essay resurfacing in 2005) let the
ancient past "speak to us in a new idiom."

## Structure

A prologue on the uses of Roman history; then twelve chapters and an epilogue:

1. **Cicero's Finest Hour** — the Catilinarian conspiracy of 63 BCE as an entry point into
   late-Republican politics, evidence, and the problem of one-sided sources.
2. **In the Beginning** — foundation myths (Romulus/Remus, Aeneas), and how to read them.
3. **The Kings of Rome** — the regal period as reconstruction and myth.
4. **Rome's Great Leap Forward** — the early Republic, the plebeian struggle, expansion in Italy.
5. **A Wider World** — the Punic Wars and Mediterranean conquest.
6. **New Politics** — the Gracchi to the civil wars; the crisis of the Republic.
7. **From Empire to Emperors** — Caesar, the fall of the Republic.
8. **The Home Front** — society, family, slavery, everyday politics of the late Republic.
9. **The Transformations of Augustus** — the invention of one-man rule.
10. **Fourteen Emperors** — the Julio-Claudians to the Severans, and the "job" of emperor.
11. **The Haves and Have-Nots** — the social order of the imperial city.
12. **Rome Outside Rome** — the provinces and life across the empire.

Epilogue: **The First Roman Millennium** — 212 CE and the meaning of the endpoint.

## What It Adds to the Wiki

The wiki's Roman coverage is already dense (Classical Antiquity is the best-covered period
in the collection). *SPQR* contributes mainly as an **interpretive and historiographical**
layer: Beard's citizenship-centred, anti-decline framing; her source-critical treatment of
early Rome; and her insistence on ordinary Romans and the view from the provinces. Its
distinctive net-new contributions to the network are the **citizenship concept**, the
**212 CE edict** as a first-class event, and structured pages for the **foundation** and
the **regal period** as objects of Roman myth-making rather than as literal history.

## Volume Synthesis Note

*SPQR* sits alongside the wiki's existing Republican and Imperial narrative sources as the
strongest single statement of the **post-Gibbon consensus**: that "decline and fall" is a
misleading master-frame, that Rome's expansion is better explained by its assimilative
citizenship regime than by military genius or manifest destiny, and that the early
centuries are recoverable only as reconstruction. Where the wiki's warfare and event pages
carry the operational narrative (the Punic Wars, the civil wars, Actium), Beard supplies
the connective interpretation and a running source-critique. Her endpoint choice — 212 CE
rather than 410 CE — is itself a historiographical intervention worth recording: it reframes
the "end" of classical Rome as the completion of a citizenship project rather than a
collapse, and stands in productive tension with the wiki's Late Antiquity pages, which
retain the transformation-and-fall vocabulary for the later centuries Beard deliberately
leaves outside her frame.
