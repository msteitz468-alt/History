# Spartans: A New History (Nigel M. Kennell)

A modern survey of **[[sparta|Sparta]]** across its whole history — Archaic, Classical, Hellenistic and
Roman — organized around a sustained critique of the **"Spartan mirage."** The first source of the
Phase-9 (Greece & Rome Depth) batch. (Phases 7–8, Ancient Near East and Ancient China depth, are absent
from the collection; see the index note.)

## The Argument

- **The Spartan mirage.** Kennell builds the book on the recognition (Ollier's *mirage spartiate*) that
  almost everything we "know" about Sparta comes from **outsiders and admirers** — Xenophon, Plato,
  Aristotle, and above all the much later **Plutarch** — and from the Spartans' own carefully projected
  self-image. The austere, egalitarian, eternally Lycurgan Sparta of tradition is in large part an
  **idealized construct**, and the historian's first task is to see through it.
- **Institutions as history, not myth.** Against the picture of a changeless "Lycurgan" order, Kennell
  argues Spartan institutions **changed over time and were sometimes reinvented**. His signature case is
  the **[[spartan-agoge|agoge]]**, the state upbringing, which he locates in good part as a
  **Hellenistic and Roman reconstruction** — a self-conscious revival staged for an audience of tourists
  and antiquarians — rather than an unbroken archaic survival.
- **Oliganthropia and decline.** The deep structural problem of the Spartan system was the **relentless
  shrinkage of the full-citizen (Spartiate) body** — *oliganthropia* — which left Sparta unable to
  sustain its power after the catastrophe of **Leuctra (371 BCE)** and reduced the once-dominant state to
  a museum-piece under Rome.

## Coverage

The book runs from the Dorian settlement and the **conquest of Messenia** and **helotage**, through the
making of the Classical warrior-state and the **Peloponnesian War** hegemony, the fourth-century
collapse, the **Hellenistic** reform-kings (Agis IV, Cleomenes III, the tyrant Nabis), and finally
**Roman** Sparta, where the "Lycurgan" tradition became a heritage performance.
