# Roman Republics

Harriet I. Flower, *Roman Republics* (Princeton: Princeton University Press, 2010). A short analytical essay that rejects the standard unitary “Roman Republic” (traditionally ~509–49/27 BCE) and the long “Late Republican crisis” (133–49) as useful periodizations, and proposes instead **six successive republics** (plus transitional and non-republican phases) defined by internal political culture.

## Central Argument

Periodization is not optional packaging: it *is* historical thinking. The conventional Early / Middle / Late Republic scheme, with a quasi-biological rise–maturity–decay arc ending in an eighty-year “crisis,” flattens discontinuous political systems into one organism and teleologically loads the end. Flower’s alternative treats Roman republicanism as a **sequence of discrete political cultures**, each with its own rules, strengths, and failure modes — analogous to the numbered French republics, though the Romans themselves (lacking a definite article and a technical vocabulary for “republic”) did not name successive regimes this way.

The decisive breaks are internal and political (lex Villia Annalis 180; secret ballot from 139; Social War and the collapse of the last *nobiles* republic ~88; Sulla’s New Republic 81; formation of the “Big Three” alliance ~60), not merely military victories or the careers of famous individuals. On this map, **Sulla ends traditional republican politics** and founds a different sixth republic; the generation after him lives *after* the fall of the classic republics of the *nobiles*, in a post-shipwreck landscape — not inside a single continuous crisis from the Gracchi to Caesar.

## Distinctive Positions (Flower, not wiki voice)

- **“Crisis” is the wrong word** for 133–49: a crisis is acute and short; an eighty-year crisis is a contradiction. (Origin anecdote: Flower’s father on the misuse of “crisis” in *Cambridge Companion* chapter titles.)
- **At least six republics**, not one: pre-republican and proto-republican phases → Republics 1–2 (fifth/fourth-century experiments) → three successive **republics of the *nobiles*** (300–180, 180–139, 139–88) → **Sulla’s New Republic** (81–60, modified 70) → non-republican triumviral / dictatorial aftermaths.
- **The classic republican culture is the culture of the *nobiles***: compromise, short-term magistracies, electoral competition that *supports* stability. It is not a continuation of the archaic patrician order.
- **Violence after 133 is epoch-making**, not a symptom of a long decline: Nasica’s killing of Tiberius Gracchus and the cascade through 100 and the 80s destroy traditional political culture faster than generational “wear.”
- **Sulla’s settlement is not a “restoration”** but a revolutionary new constitution based on courts and law codes rather than *contiones* and ancestral deliberation; its instability is structural.
- **Republican government stops functioning around 60** (alliance of Pompey–Caesar–Crassus), not in 49: Caesar invades a community that has already lost republican shape; 49 is aftermath, not the fall itself.
- **No single long republic carried the seeds of its own destruction** in expansion or elite ambition as a unitary fate; each republic had different strengths and failure modes.

## Volume Synthesis Note

Flower’s essay is short, sharp, and **periodization-first**. It does not replace CAH IX narrative, Gruen’s institutional optimism, Meier’s *Krise ohne Alternative*, or Syme’s revolutionary sociology; it relocates the chronological frame in which those arguments operate. The wiki’s densest gain is the concept page [[roman-republics-periodization]] plus an attributed position on [[fall-of-roman-republic]]: *nobiles* republics end ~88; Sulla founds Republic 6 rather than restores; functional republican politics ends ~60; 49 is aftermath. Secondary pages receive compact attributed sections so that *nobiles* culture, Sullan *lex*-over-*mos*, Gracchan violence, Social War franchise politics, and the Big Three can be read against Flower’s map without overwriting existing CAH/Woolf/Harris/Duncan layers.

Methodologically the book models what [[periodization-world-history]] argues in general form: time maps are constitutive, anachronistic, and revisable. Its limits are those Flower states — internal politics prioritized; footnotes selective; no new primary corpus — so it should not be treated as a stand-alone narrative backbone for republican Rome.
