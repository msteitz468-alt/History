# The Industrial Revolution 1760–1830 (T. S. Ashton)

The classic short account of the **British Industrial Revolution** — the economic transformation that
forms one half of Hobsbawm's [[dual-revolution|dual revolution]]. Seventh source of **Gap 1**; deepens
**[[industrialization]]**.

## The Argument

Ashton presents the Industrial Revolution as a real and decisive economic break, centred on Britain
c. 1760–1830, and explains it through interacting causes:

- **cheap and abundant capital** (falling interest rates) financing investment;
- a cluster of **technical innovations** — in cotton textiles (the spinning jenny, water frame, mule,
  power loom), in **iron** (coke smelting, puddling), and above all the **steam engine** (Watt) — turning
  coal into mechanical power;
- **expanding markets** at home and overseas, population growth, transport improvement (canals, then
  railways), and a legal-institutional environment favourable to enterprise.

Famously, Ashton argues — against a purely grim picture — that industrialization, for all its harshness,
ultimately **raised living standards** and allowed Britain to escape the Malthusian trap that still
gripped much of the world ("a wave of gadgets swept over England"). This **"optimist" position** is the
classic pole of the long **standard-of-living debate**.

## Significance

Ashton's book is a foundational text of **economic history** and a lucid introduction to the causes and
mechanisms of industrialization. It anchors the wiki's account of the world's first
**[[industrialization|industrial revolution]]** and supplies the economic substance behind the political
narratives of the Long 19th Century.
