# Jefferson the Virginian — Dumas Malone (1948)

**Jefferson and His Time, Volume 1.** Comprehensive narrative biography covering Thomas Jefferson's first 41 years (1743–1784): family background and Virginia gentry formation; education at William and Mary under William Small and legal apprenticeship with George Wythe; law practice, planter life, marriage to Martha Wayles Skelton, and the building of Monticello; entry into the House of Burgesses; revolutionary pamphleteering (*A Summary View of the Rights of British America*); Continental Congress and authorship of the Declaration of Independence; Virginia statecraft (constitution of 1776, law revision, religious freedom, education, land reform); service as wartime governor during the British invasions of 1780–81; the death of Martha Jefferson; and his final Virginia-phase service in Congress before departure for France in 1784.

## Overview / Thesis

Malone presents Jefferson as a many-sided Enlightenment Virginian whose core commitments to individual liberty, self-government, reason, and improvement of the human condition were formed and tested in the specific soil of colonial and revolutionary Virginia. The volume stresses chronological development over static portrait: the young Jefferson is shown becoming the man whose later career would be unintelligible without this foundation. Jefferson was not a "fiery orator like Patrick Henry" or a commander like Washington; his distinctive contribution was as "draftsman of state papers and legislative bills and legal statutes." The American Revolution "precipitated his public genius," but the private man — planter, builder, reader, family head — remained continuous. Malone's explicit purposes: comprehensive (the whole man in proportion), historical (embedded in the age and place), and "true to his own chronology."

## Methodological Notes

- Strict adherence to Jefferson's own chronology so that "the major features of his mind and character can be observed before their outlines were obscured by political controversy."
- Heavy use of local records (Henrico, Goochland, Albemarle), Jefferson's own Account Books, Fee Book, Garden Book, and early letters.
- Contextual reconstruction of Virginia society, gentry culture, Williamsburg as "school of government," and the intellectual milieu (Enlightenment via Small, classical via Wythe, law as liberal education).
- Acknowledges thin sources for boyhood/youth and abundance only after governorship.
- Avoids both elevating Jefferson by denigrating contemporaries and whitewashing the "artificialities of aristocracy" he opposed from a privileged position.
- Cross-references to contemporaries (Washington, Henry, Mason, Wythe, the Randolph connection) and to the broader imperial crisis.

## Key Contributions

- The most detailed and balanced portrait of Jefferson's formative Virginia years available at mid-century.
- Authoritative treatment of the *Summary View* as a chart of political thinking and of the 1776 Virginia reforms (inheritance, religion, education, land).
- Full narrative of the 1780–81 governorship crisis (Arnold's raid, Tarleton at Monticello, flight, legislative inquiry) with emphasis on structural difficulties (militia collapse, paper money, geography) rather than personal failure.
- Integration of private happiness (marriage, Monticello, family) with public service.
- Honest treatment of slavery: early efforts at reform, provisions in the Summary View and Notes, yet personal involvement as planter and heir to the Wayles estate.

## Range 01 Claims

- **Birth and Shadwell setting (April 13, 1743, N.S.)**: "THOMAS JEFFERSON was born in a simple wooden house, in what is now Albemarle County, Virginia... The place was called Shadwell. Peter Jefferson, who owned it, had named it for the parish in London where his wife, Jane Randolph, had been christened..." The site overlooked the Rivanna with a finer western prospect to the Blue Ridge. "The vastness of this physical setting was not lost upon Thomas Jefferson; it was reflected in the largeness of his mind." (Intro/early; [[actors/jefferson-thomas]] [[actors/jefferson-peter]] [[places/shadwell]])

- **Move to Tuckahoe as toddler; earliest memories there**: "When he was two or three years old he was taken from it to Tuckahoe on the James, to an older and better house in a more thickly settled district... The earliest recollection of Thomas Jefferson was of being carried on a pillow by a mounted slave on the journey from Shadwell to Tuckahoe." (Early life; [[places/shadwell]] [[places/tuckahoe]] [[actors/jefferson-thomas]])

- **Peter Jefferson's character and stature**: "Peter Jefferson, a man of huge stature and legendary strength, was in his mid-thirties in 1743... He was no landless pioneer but an enterprising young planter... He performed memorable deeds as a surveyor in uncharted country..." Thomas later said that from age fourteen "his whole care and direction were thrown upon himself" after Peter's death, but credited his father as the first cause of his being well schooled. (Peter's life/surveys; [[actors/jefferson-peter]] [[actors/jefferson-thomas]])

- **Peter Jefferson's marriage to Jane Randolph and family connections**: Married October 1739 (Jane 19, Peter 32). "The rising young planter acquired no fortune by his marriage, but he gained for his unborn children a far more extensive and influential family connection than he himself had enjoyed." (Marriage and connections; [[actors/jefferson-peter]])

- **Peter's major surveys (Fairfax Line 1746; Virginia-North Carolina line 1749)**: Severe hardships ("prodigiously full of fallen timber and ivey"); carved "P. J." near the Fairfax Stone. With Joshua Fry produced the important 1751 map "superior to all others up to the time that Thomas Jefferson compiled his Notes on Virginia." (Surveying career; [[actors/jefferson-peter]])

- **Peter Jefferson's death (Aug 17, 1757) and will**: Left substantial property (~7500 acres total, >60 slaves). Thomas (nearly 14) received choice of Rivanna or Fluvanna lands, half the undisposed slaves, livestock share, books, mathematical instruments, cherry-tree desk and bookcase, and slave Sawney. No entail. "Peter was the chief early influence and more than anyone else caused his son to be well schooled." (Death and legacy; [[actors/jefferson-peter]] [[actors/jefferson-thomas]] [[places/shadwell]] [[places/monticello]])

- **Early schooling (English at Tuckahoe; Latin with Rev. William Douglas 1752–57; James Maury's log school 1758–60)**: At Maury's (near Southwest Mountains) he boarded with the family, read Greek and Latin authors in original, and formed close friendship with Dabney Carr. Maury was the first important teacher for ancient languages; Jefferson did not get his characteristic political or religious ideas from him. (Schooling; [[actors/jefferson-thomas]])

- **William and Mary entry (1760, age ~17); decisive influence of William Small**: "With the single but important exception of William Small, professor of natural philosophy, the members of the William and Mary faculty were Anglican clergymen..." Small gave the first lectures in ethics, rhetoric, and belles-lettres; "Small probably fixed the destinies of his life." Jefferson became a daily companion through lectures and informal talk. (College; [[actors/jefferson-thomas]] [[actors/small-william]] [[places/williamsburg]])

- **Introduction to George Wythe's law office via Small**: "To Jefferson he also opened the door of George Wythe's law office..." Wythe (then ~35) was already eminent; Jefferson read law under him primarily 1762 onward, pursuing it as a learned profession with notable devotion while reading at Shadwell or in Williamsburg. (Legal training; [[actors/wythe-george]] [[actors/jefferson-thomas]] [[places/williamsburg]])

- **Dinners at the Governor's Palace with Small, Wythe, and Fauquier**: "by happy chance, he was regularly admitted as a fourth member of the party which had been dining frequently at the Palace... he heard 'more good sense, more rational and philosophical conversations,' than at any other time in his life." (Williamsburg intellectual circle; [[actors/small-william]] [[actors/wythe-george]] [[places/williamsburg]])

- **Patrick Henry at the Capitol (Stamp Act speech, May 30, 1765)**: Jefferson stood at the door "listening spellbound." "As they remembered it half a century afterwards, he was then stopped with cries of 'Treason,'..." He regarded Henry as pre-eminent in the forensic field from that moment, though later noting Henry's intellectual limitations and contrasting with his own painstaking preparation. (Political awakening; [[actors/henry-patrick]] [[actors/jefferson-thomas]] [[places/williamsburg]])

- **Rebecca Burwell romance (1763–64; "Belinda") and aftermath**: Indecisive courtship; stammered at the Apollo Room dance; she married Jacquelin Ambler. Jefferson copied antifeminist passages into his literary notebook and temporarily sought satisfaction in male friendships. "From this unhappy experience cured him temporarily of his hankering for girls." (Early personal life; [[actors/jefferson-thomas]])

- **Early character sketch**: "unquestionably valued and probably craved public honors, as he craved human affection, but the sensitiveness of his nature caused him to abhor controversy, and to avoid any that did not center in an important idea." He assumed responsibilities by virtue of position and abilities "just as George Washington, George Mason, and George Wythe did." (Character; [[actors/jefferson-thomas]] [[actors/washington-george]] [[actors/mason-george]] [[actors/wythe-george]])

## Range 02 Claims

- **Legal studies under Wythe and rigorous program**: Began formal study ~1762; "From the date of his earliest records Jefferson was a careful draftsman..." Studied Coke upon Littleton (initially called "an old, dull scoundrel"); preferred its "crabbed text and uncouth learning" in retrospect to Blackstone's "honeyed" words. Kept commonplace books of cases; copied time-management verses ("Part with it as with money, sparing..."). (Legal apprenticeship; [[actors/wythe-george]] [[actors/jefferson-thomas]])

- **Foundations for a lawyer (languages, math, natural philosophy)**: Regarded Latin, French, mathematics, and natural philosophy as essential; continued scientific observations with Small until the latter left for England. (Education philosophy; [[actors/small-william]] [[actors/wythe-george]])

- **Music at the Governor's Palace**: Participated in weekly amateur concerts; "he afterwards reported that for twelve years before the Revolution he played no fewer than three hours a day." Music was "the favorite passion of his soul." (Cultural life; [[places/williamsburg]] [[actors/jefferson-thomas]])

- **Social life and boredom at Shadwell**: Danced at Raleigh Tavern, visited plantations, attended weddings; frequently expressed tedium ("All things here appear to me to trudge on in one and the same round..."). (Youthful social round; [[places/shadwell]] [[places/williamsburg]])

- **Rebecca Burwell episode detailed**: Stammered proposal; sensed her lack of interest in England trip; she engaged Ambler. Copied Miltonic and antifeminist lines into notebook. "Jefferson carried on this rather absurd affair mostly in his imagination." (Romantic disappointment; [[actors/jefferson-thomas]])

- **House of Burgesses and General Court observation**: Became acquainted with Pendleton (via Wythe), Peyton Randolph, Bland, and especially Henry. "From the time that he heard Patrick Henry as a student he regarded him as pre-eminent in the forensic field." Heard the Stamp Act speech May 30, 1765 (four resolutions passed, one by single vote). (Political education; [[actors/henry-patrick]] [[actors/wythe-george]] [[places/williamsburg]])

- **Henry's resolutions opposed by conservatives (Wythe, Randolph, Pendleton, Bland)**: Jefferson later implied approval of the boldest (expunged) resolution and sympathy with "young hot and giddy members." (Factional lines; [[actors/henry-patrick]] [[actors/wythe-george]])

- **First trip outside Virginia (1766, to New York/Philadelphia)**: Inoculated against smallpox (suffered no ill effects; lifelong interest in prevention); lodged with Elbridge Gerry; returned with books. (Early travel; [[actors/jefferson-thomas]])

- **Early views on time, study, and character**: Copied "Youth is not rich in time..." and similar maxims; combined craving for honors/affection with sensitivity that made him abhor unnecessary controversy. (Self-formation; [[actors/jefferson-thomas]])

## Range 03 Claims

- **Naming and early work on Monticello (by 1767–69)**: Grafted cherry trees; "translated 'little mountain' into Italian"; agreement for leveling the top; cellar dug by laborers. Chose the mountaintop despite immense difficulties (woods, soil, water); revealed "sensitiveness to natural beauty" and desire for extended view. (Monticello beginnings; [[places/monticello]] [[actors/jefferson-thomas]])

- **Architectural influences (Palladio primary; practical Virginia adaptation)**: Turned to books; most influenced by English imitators of Palladio and Palladio himself (Roman antiquity). Valued "simplicity, serenity, and reserve" plus dignity suited to the site. "Liberty was never anarchy to him, but freedom to seek the truth." Preferred chaste models; worked proportions mathematically. Early plan: central mass with wings, L-shaped service blocks, service rooms sunk below terraces. (Architectural ideas; [[places/monticello]])

- **First structure (southeast pavilion); move after Shadwell fire (1770)**: "The first structure that he erected was one of the outbuildings... He moved to this simple brick building in the November after the house at Shadwell burned." Bricks still being laid after the Declaration. (Early building; [[places/monticello]] [[places/shadwell]])

- **Courtship and marriage to Martha Wayles Skelton (New Year's Day 1772)**: Formal visits to The Forest (Charles City); "this was a real romance... ushered in the happiest period of his life... He never had the slightest doubt that he was well mated." Martha: slight, well-formed, large hazel eyes, luxuriant auburn hair; accomplished on harpsichord; amiable and sprightly. Music a special bond. (Marriage; [[actors/jefferson-thomas]] [[actors/jefferson-martha-wayles]])

- **Journey home and first night at Monticello (late Jan 1772)**: Delayed by visits; arrived late in three feet of snow; "only to find that the fires were out and the servants were all in bed." (Arrival; [[places/monticello]])

- **Daughter Patsy (Martha) born late Sept 1772; near loss and recovery**: "was recovered almost instantaneously by a good breast of milk." Reinforced preference for nature over doctors in infant cases. (Family; [[actors/jefferson-thomas]] [[actors/jefferson-martha-wayles]])

- **Walker affair (pre-marriage, 1768)**: Improper advances to neighbor's wife (Betsey Moore) while single during husband's absence; later privately admitted "when young and single I offered love to a handsome lady." Deeply regretted; no evidence of continuation after his own marriage. Sensitive and diffident toward women until married. (Personal history; [[actors/jefferson-thomas]])

- **Wayles inheritance (1773–74)**: John Wayles death 1773; Martha's share ~11,000+ acres + 135 slaves (incl. Hemings family); net after sales: Poplar Forest, Elk Island, Elkhill. "At no time in his entire life did he appear to be as prosperous as he was from 1774 until depreciation ensued." Heavy associated debt (Farrell & Jones) colored the rest of his life. (Inheritance; [[actors/jefferson-thomas]] [[actors/jefferson-martha-wayles]] [[places/monticello]])

- **Dabney Carr's death (May 1773) and burial on the mountain**: Closest friend; buried under the oak where they had read and talked. Jefferson grieved deeply and became second father to Carr's widow and six children. (Friendship and loss; [[actors/jefferson-thomas]])

- **Philip Mazzei settlement at Colle (1773)**: Early rising walk, land offer; Jefferson spoke Tuscan with the Italians; subscribed to the wine company. (Intellectual/social life; [[places/monticello]])

- **Creation of Virginia Committee of Correspondence (March 1773, Raleigh Tavern)**: With Henry, Lee, Carr et al.; first colony-wide; Jefferson helped draft and gained for Carr the privilege of introducing. (Revolutionary organization; [[actors/jefferson-thomas]] [[actors/henry-patrick]])

- **Domestic peak and political turn (1773–early 1774)**: "Until he took the turn of the road which led straight to political revolution, joys had predominated over cares and sorrows and he was an enviably happy man." Continued molding bricks, planting (detailed garden with Italian names), expanding records. (Domestic vs. public; [[places/monticello]] [[actors/jefferson-thomas]] [[actors/jefferson-martha-wayles]])

## Range 04 Claims

- **Continued Burgesses service and responses to Dunmore (1775)**: Served on committee drafting address to Governor blaming the "alarming situation" wholly on the British ministry; emphasized colonial self-government and debtor protections. (Pre-Congress politics)

- **Draft reply to Lord North's conciliatory motion (1775)**: "A proposition to give our money, accompanied with large fleets and armies, seems addressed to our fears rather than to our freedom." Insisted on colonial unity. (Imperial crisis; [[actors/jefferson-thomas]])

- **Journey to Philadelphia (June 1775)**: Crossed the Potomac ("he crossed his Rubicon"); purchased horse General; traveled with servants; ~10 days for ~325 miles. Lodged with Benjamin Randolph; dined at City Tavern. (Continental stage; [[actors/jefferson-thomas]])

- **Silent in debate but prompt and decisive on committees**: Adams called him "prompt, frank, explicit, and decisive"; friendship dated from this period. Reputation for literature, science, composition, and languages (French, Italian, Spanish). (Congress role; [[actors/jefferson-thomas]] [[actors/adams-john]])

- **Committee work on Declaration on Taking Up Arms (1775)**: Added to unsatisfactory draft; his version "not liked by the committee" for being "too harsh" (per Dickinson); some expressions incorporated. (Early Congress papers)

- **Return to Virginia convention (July–Aug 1775); election to Congress**: Received 85 votes (third); Henry chosen commander-in-chief of militia. (Virginia politics)

- **Peyton Randolph's death (Oct 1775)**: Jefferson present; attended funeral with crape. (Personal/political loss)

- **Letter to John Randolph (Aug 1775)**: "By the God that made me, I will cease to exist before I yield to a connection on such terms as the British Parliament propose"; preferred properly limited dependence on Britain to any other nation but would "lend my hand to sink the whole Island in the ocean" rather than accept parliamentary legislation. (Radicalization; [[actors/jefferson-thomas]])

- **Draft constitution for Virginia (sent via Wythe, mid-June 1776)**: Proposed free independent western colonies; convention used his charges against the King as preamble declaring the old government dissolved. (Constitutional drafting; [[actors/wythe-george]])

- **Appointment to Committee of Five (June 11, 1776)**: With Adams, Franklin, Sherman, Livingston; asked to draft the Declaration. (Declaration; [[events/declaration-of-independence-1776]] [[actors/jefferson-thomas]] [[actors/adams-john]])

- **Drafting the Declaration (June 11–28, 1776)**: "He consulted no book or pamphlet"; drew charges from his Virginia paper; philosophical paragraph similar to Mason's but ideas already his. Fastidious craftsman: weighed phrases, corrected even after adoption. Congress deleted slave-trade charge and reduced final paragraph; Jefferson thought it weakened but deletions generally strengthened. (Declaration mechanics; [[events/declaration-of-independence-1776]])

- **Mother's death (March 31, 1776)** and prolonged headache (spring 1776): "Our mother died" (bare record); incapacitated ~5 weeks. (Personal; [[actors/jefferson-thomas]] [[places/shadwell]])

- **Second daughter Jane's death (1775)**: Lived <18 months; bare record. (Family losses)

## Range 05 Claims

- **Bill for proportioning crimes and punishments**: Devoted more time than to all other Revisal work; meticulously crafted (beautiful penmanship, multilingual notes). Sought humane/rational punishments; limited capital to treason and murder; invoked lex talionis (castration for rape/sodomy in some cases); public labor as main alternative (later judged ineffective for reform). (Criminal law reform; [[actors/wythe-george]] [[actors/jefferson-thomas]])

- **Bill for religious freedom**: Framed early; "of all the contests... the most bitter... severest of his entire life." Regarded religion as strictly private; no compulsion to support worship; protected civil capacities. "It is error alone which needs the support of government. Truth can stand by itself." Enacted 1786 (Madison's help); received "infinite approbation in Europe." (Religious liberty; [[concepts/virginia-statute-for-religious-freedom]])

- **Bill for the more general diffusion of knowledge**: Primary "hundred" schools (3 years gratis for all white children); grammar schools to "rake from the rubbish" superior talent for higher education at public expense; one sent annually to William and Mary. "Realistic and discriminating democrat, or... informed and critical aristocrat." (Education reform)

- **Criticism and reform of William and Mary**: "Had not answered expectations"; as visitor (as governor) introduced professorships of law (Wythe), medicine, modern languages. Envisaged something like a state university. (Higher education; [[actors/wythe-george]] [[places/williamsburg]])

- **Family events 1777–78**: Only son born May 1777, died in third week; daughter Mary (Maria/Polly) born Aug 1778. (Family)

- **Work on Revisal at Monticello**: Purchased libraries of Peyton Randolph and Richard Bland (rare Virginia MSS); collecting old laws and newspapers; daily weather observations, theodolite, 1778 solar eclipse with Rittenhouse correspondence. Music and gardening continued. (Intellectual life amid war; [[places/monticello]])

- **Unionist outlook as governor (1779–81)**: "The most effectual aid... is where the general cause most needs it." Sent Virginia troops south after Charleston; established intelligence; dispatched Monroe as commissioner. (Unionism)

- **Treatment of Henry Hamilton (1779)**: Advised irons and close confinement in public jail dungeon as "war criminal" and to deter Indian barbarities; later somewhat relaxed per Washington but initially adamant. (Executive action; contrasted with usual humanitarianism)

- **Election as Governor (June 1, 1779)**: 67–61 on second ballot; viewed office as "burdens" and soon "public slave." Scrupulously observed constitutional limits; acted generally only with Council advice. (Governorship; [[actors/jefferson-thomas]])

- **Council colleagues and Madison friendship**: Included Page, Madison (beginning of intimate friendship), Walker. (Personal/political ties; [[actors/madison-james]])

- **Arnold raid (Jan 1781)**: Initial disbelief delayed full militia call by ~2 days; then energetic (store removals at Westham, riding, reports). Records lost in wagoners' mistake; Washington saw policy right and evils relatively small. (Invasion crisis)

- **Daughter Lucy Elizabeth's death (Apr 15, 1781)**: ~age 2; "Our daughter Lucy Elizabeth died about 10 o'clock a.m." (Family loss)

## Range 06 Claims

- **Council quorum failure and last official acts (late May–June 3, 1781)**: No quorum despite attendance; wrote final letters on boundary and accepted term end. "This was a good note for Peter Jefferson's son to end on." (Exit from office; [[actors/jefferson-peter]] [[actors/jefferson-thomas]])

- **Address to Illinois Indian chief (June 1781)**: Smoked pipe of peace (rare for him); framed war against tyranny in 1776 terms; expressed confidence peace was near because "all the world is against them." (Diplomatic/philosophical consistency)

- **Appeal to Washington (May 28, 1781)**: "the difficulty would be to keep men out of the field" if Washington came; foresaw enemy wasting the country and feared popular acquiescence. Washington's reply expressed obligations for zeal and hoped for continued friendship in retirement. (Union appeal; [[actors/washington-george]])

- **Tarleton's raid and flight (June 4, 1781)**: Cornwallis detached Tarleton (180 dragoons + 70 infantry) to disrupt government. Jack Jouett rode ahead via back trails to warn. Jefferson evacuated deliberately: sent guests first, family carriage hours later, himself last when enemy approaching. No terror shown. (Tarleton raid; [[places/monticello]])

- **Aftermath**: Joined family at Blenheim then Coles/Enniscorthy; took to Poplar Forest; fell from horse (Caractacus) and confined ~6 weeks. Family tradition and later enemies embellished the flight. (Flight and injury)

- **Assembly elects Nelson governor (June 12, 1781)**; inquiry resolution by Nicholas: "Resolved, That at the next session of Assembly an inquiry be made into the conduct of the Executive..." Jefferson wounded; requested specifications; prepared detailed written answers focused on Arnold raid. Nicholas presented no charges. (Inquiry; [[actors/henry-patrick]] — Henry discussed dictatorship talk)

- **Vindication (Dec 1781)**: Committee reported no information; General Assembly unanimously thanked him for "impartial, upright, and attentive administration" and declared "high opinion" of his "ability, rectitude and integrity." (Vindication; [[actors/jefferson-thomas]])

- **Assessment of governorship**: Structural failure of 1776 governmental agencies for war more than personal failure; "of all the war governors he was incomparably the greatest mind and the greatest man." Scrupulous legalism both strength and handicap. (Historiographic judgment)

- **Beginning of Notes on Virginia (Mar–Apr 1782)**: Assembled answers to Marbois queries while governor; worked at Poplar Forest and Monticello during "retirement"; sent manuscript late March/early April. Later enlarged. (Intellectual project; [[concepts/notes-on-the-state-of-virginia]] [[places/monticello]])

Representative from range 13001–15500.

## Range 07 Claims

- **Preparations for France and journey north (1784)**: Carried *Notes* manuscript (hoped for cheap Philadelphia printing); received ~20+ commissions for commercial treaties with Adams and Franklin. Deliberately studied New England commercial needs en route. Cordial reception in Massachusetts (chair in House, special committee). (Diplomatic appointment)

- **New Haven/Yale visit**: Discussed electrical apparatus, Ohio bones, William and Mary with Ezra Stiles; "impressed his host as a truly scientific and learned man." Later LL.D. from Yale. (Scientific reputation)

## Volume Synthesis Note

Malone's *Jefferson the Virginian* establishes the chronological and contextual foundation for understanding Jefferson as product and shaper of Virginia gentry Enlightenment culture. The volume demonstrates that Jefferson's core commitments (individual liberty under law, religious freedom, education as republican bulwark, westward expansion on republican terms) were formed and tested before 1784; later career is intelligible only against this Virginian background. Malone's strength is proportion and setting; he neither inflates nor diminishes the man by isolating him from his society. The work supplies the essential grounding for the wiki's Age of Expansion treatment of the American founding and for the detailed biography. Later volumes in the series (when ingested) will extend the synthesis; tensions with post-Boyd Papers scholarship (especially on slavery and the Hemings family) are noted on the detailed page and should be tracked in historiography sections. Collection coverage for this period is strong via this source plus supporting founding biographies.
