# The Making of Strategy: Rulers, States, and War (1994)

*The Making of Strategy: Rulers, States, and War*, edited by Williamson Murray, MacGregor Knox, and Alvin Bernstein, is a foundational comparative study of how different polities, from classical antiquity to the late twentieth century, constructed strategy. 

It complements the theoretical literature by providing deep, historical case studies of strategic practice, testing a common analytical framework across seventeen distinct states.

## Consolidated Key Grounded Claims

### Chapter 1: Introduction: On Strategy (ll. 393–690)
- **Definition of Strategy** (ll. 400–400): "strategy is a process, a constant adaptation to shifting conditions and circumstances in a world where chance, uncertainty, and ambiguity dominate." — Strategy is defined as an adaptive process in an ambiguous world, rather than a rigid system of rules or principles.
- **Geography and Air Power Doctrine** (ll. 459–459): "For geographic reasons, German airmen had to think about supporting the ground war as well." — Unlike British and American airmen who prioritized independent strategic bombing due to geographic insulation, the Luftwaffe was forced by continental geography and the threat of land invasion to prioritize ground support.
- **German Operational Obsession** (ll. 478–478): "The battlefield triumphs over Denmark, Austria, and France convinced them of the primacy of operational requirements over everything else." — German military planners misread history by allowing operational concerns (such as the Schlieffen Plan) to override critical political-strategic considerations.
- **The Stabbing-in-the-Back Myth** (ll. 480–480): "it had been “stabbed in the back” by liberals, revolutionaries, and Jews at home." — The sudden 1918 armistice created a domestic myth of betrayal that heavily distorted subsequent Weimar and Nazi German strategic decision-making in the [[world-wars-era]].
- **American Exceptionalism and NSC-68** (ll. 510–510): "It reflected accurately the ideological assumptions that underpinned American strategy." — NSC-68 framed the [[cold-war]] as a moral and ideological conflict rather than a conventional power balance, illustrating the influence of American exceptionalist ideology on strategy.
- **English Fiscal-Military State** (ll. 537–537): "More than any other factor, superior fiscal organization allowed England to challenge and eventually eclipse Bourbon France in the struggle for European mastery." — England's parliamentary system during the [[early-modern]] period facilitated voluntary taxation and large-scale public debt, which overcame the capital limitations that plagued absolute monarchs.
- **British Interwar Intelligence Failure** (ll. 577–577): "In the broadest sense, the British failure lay in the inability of its bureaucracy to assess the ideological and strategic threat posed by Hitler’s final goals." — The highly rigorous committee system of interwar Britain failed because it could not comprehend the revolutionary and ideological nature of the Nazi threat in the prelude to the [[world-wars-era]].

### Chapter 2: Athenian Strategy in the Peloponnesian War (ll. 691–1000)
- **Athenian Strategic Invulnerability** (ll. 726–726): "They could avoid all major land battles against a numerically and tactically superior enemy, stand behind their walls, leave their lands undefended, and maintain themselves indefinitely while striking the enemy from the sea." — Guided by Pericles, [[athens]] constructed the Long Walls to Piraeus and relied on imperial revenues and Black Sea imports to bypass traditional land warfare during [[classical-antiquity]].
- **Megarian Decree as a Strategic Test** (ll. 740–740): "If you yield to them you will immediately be required to make another concession which will be greater, since you will have made the first concession out of fear." — Pericles refused to rescind the Megarian Decree because he believed backing down from a Spartan threat would undermine [[athens]]' equal status and invite further blackmail.
- **The Financial Cost of Periclean Strategy** (ll. 790–790): "Pericles, therefore, could maintain his strategy for three years, but not for a fourth." — Pericles' passive strategy was financially exhausting, costing ~2,000 talents annually, which meant it was not sustainable for a long-term war.
- **Demosthenes’ Aetolian Expedition** (ll. 840–840): "Demosthenes’ plan was in fact brilliant, but he had conceived it hastily and executed it sloppily." — Demosthenes departed from Periclean defensiveness in 426 BCE with an amphibious plan to outflank Boeotia, but it failed due to speed and unfamiliarity with mountain terrain.
- **Pylos and Sphacteria Strategy** (ll. 844–844): "Without a blow being struck, Demosthenes’ novel action had already forced Sparta onto the defensive." — Demosthenes' establishment of a permanent base at Pylos in 425 BCE forced Sparta to halt its annual land invasions of Attica to protect its elite hostages.
- **Alcibiades' Peloponnesian Land Strategy** (ll. 864–864): "Alcibiades wanted Athens to join them in a quadruple alliance of democratic states to destroy Sparta’s position in the Peloponnesus" — In 418 BCE, Alcibiades constructed an alliance of democratic states to challenge Sparta on land inside the Peloponnesus, but it failed due to half-hearted Athenian execution at the Battle of Mantinea.
- **Nicias and the Sicilian Expedition Escalation** (ll. 874–874): "A modest expedition at small risk suddenly became a major campaign with vast forces, the destruction of which would bring disaster." — Nicias attempted to deter the Athenian assembly by exaggerating the required forces, but the assembly voted to approve the larger force, paving the way for total defeat.
- **The Cult of the Defensive** (ll. 906–906): "It encouraged the mental rigidity we might call “the cult of the defensive.”" — The Athenian way of war was hamstrung by a defensive rigidity that discouraged offensive land commitments, preventing a decisive victory over Sparta until the navy was destroyed at Aegospotami.

### Chapter 3: The Strategy of a Warrior-State: Rome and the Wars Against Carthage (ll. 1001–1370)
- **Roman Warrior Ethos** (ll. 1028–1028): "constant external danger and the perpetual need for military action molded an ethos that raised the capacity for violence to the status of virtue, a quality valued as good in itself." — The Middle Roman Republic developed a culture that celebrated violence and military command, leading to an almost continuous state of warfare during [[classical-antiquity]].
- **Decimation and Roman Discipline** (ll. 1040–1040): "selects five or eight or twenty of them by lot, so that those selected should be about a tenth of those who have been guilty of the act of cowardice. These selected are punished . . . without mercy." — [[rome]] maintained army discipline through decimation, executing a tenth of disgraced or defeated units by lot.
- **Roman vs. Spartan Manpower** (ll. 1050–1050): "it could also accept high casualty rates without changing its strategic goals." — Because [[rome]] had a large peasant-citizen levy, it could withstand massive casualties (like Cannae) that would have crippled Sparta's small citizen elite.
- **Just War and ius fetiale** (ll. 1079–1079): "no war was acceptable to the gods unless it was waged in defense of one’s own country or one’s allies." — The Roman Senate utilized the ius fetiale and the protection of allies as legal pretexts to justify aggressive expansion as defensive.
- **The Formula Togatorum** (ll. 1102–1102): "All had to provide foot soldiers and cavalry, according to the formula togatorum." — [[rome]]'s alliance system relied on Italian allies (socii) providing troop levies rather than paying direct monetary tribute.
- **Hannibal’s Center of Gravity Strategy** (ll. 1110–1110): "his strategy aimed, above all, at undermining that power by attacking and destroying the alliances on which it rested." — In the [[second-punic-war-218-201bce]], Hannibal targeted [[rome]]'s network of Latin and Italian alliances as the republic's primary center of gravity.
- **Causes of the First Punic War** (ll. 1133–1133): "the Romans risked war with both Carthage and Syracuse because they feared the geostrategic advantage Carthage might gain if it consolidated its hold on Sicily" — In 264 BCE, [[rome]] allied with the Mamertines to prevent [[carthage]] from controlling Messana and encircling southern Italy, sparking the [[first-punic-war-264-241bce]].
- **Fabian Attrition Strategy** (ll. 1233–1233): "Clearly, Rome had to avoid open battle with Hannibal at all costs; Fabius’s attrition strategy formed the basis of all subsequent Roman operations in Italy." — Following the disaster at Cannae, [[rome]] adopted a Fabian strategy of attrition and harassment to avoid pitched battles with Hannibal's superior tactical command.
- **The Capture of New Carthage** (ll. 1241–1241): "Scipio had realized that a base of operations that could be resupplied by sea was essential, and that his father had failed because Saguntum, his base, had not been far enough south." — Scipio Africanus captured [[new-carthage]] in 209 BCE to secure a southern sea-supplied base, silver mines, and hostages, turning the tide of the Iberian campaign.

### Chapter 4: Chinese Strategy from the Fourteenth to the Seventeenth Centuries (ll. 1371–1718)
- **Early Ming Nomadic Continuity** (ll. 1398–1398): "far from representing a reassertion of Chinese culture, the early Ming was a continuation of the Yuan empire under new auspices." — Rather than representing a pure restoration of Han culture, the early [[ming-dynasty]] preserved many of the institutional, military, and dress styles of the departing Mongol Yuan dynasty.
- **The Intellectual Gap on Steppe Security** (ll. 1416–1416): "The morally generated internal order of the classics was not enough: external forces utterly resistant to the sort of cultural suasion might threaten the very survival of society." — The classical Confucian curriculum left [[ming-dynasty]] officials intellectually unprepared for steppe security, focusing on moral order rather than pragmatic nomadic diplomacy.
- **Chia I's Anti-Nomadic Rhetoric** (ll. 1422–1422): "He was unwilling, for moral reasons, to treat with “barbarian” nomads on anything close to equality." — Chia I's classical rhetoric dominated the Ming court, making trade or equal diplomatic compromise with the nomads politically hazardous.
- **T'u-mu Debacle and Strategic Shift** (ll. 1522–1522): "It marked the last time a Chinese army actually left China proper and headed into the steppe to deal with the nomads, and as such brought a profound shift in the whole strategic stance of the dynasty." — The 1449 capture of the emperor at T'u-mu destroyed Ming offensive military prestige and forced the dynasty to shift permanently to a defensive strategy.
- **The Geostrategic Significance of the Ordos** (ll. 1526–1526): "The experience of both the Yuan and the early Ming had demonstrated that it was impossible to secure northern China without powerful influence, if not control, over the steppe." — The withdrawal of outposts from the Ordos allowed the Mongols to establish permanent bases, exposing the northern borders of [[china]] to chronic raiding.
- **The Creation of the Great Wall** (ll. 1540–1540): "When the unfeasibility of Ordos recovery became clear, others at court, most notably Yu Tzu-chün (1429-1489), began to call for a strictly defensive policy: wall building." — The high cost of recovering the Ordos forced the Ming court to adopt Yu Tzu-chün's 1474 plan of stone and adobe wall-building, creating the "Great Wall."
- **Chang Chü-cheng's Pragmatic Peace** (ll. 1584–1584): "Chang understood that the solution to the problems with Mongolia must involve compromise." — In 1571, Grand Secretary Chang Chü-cheng resolved the northern border crisis by allowing trade and granting titles to the Altan-khan, reducing defense costs.

### Chapter 5: The Making of Strategy in Habsburg Spain: Philip II’s “bid for mastery” (ll. 1719–2170)
- **Habsburg Domino Theory** (ll. 1772–1772): "if the defeat in those parts is a great one, the rest of the monarchy will collapse, for Germany will be followed by Italy and the Netherlands, and the Nedierlands will be followed by America; and Lombardy will be followed by Naples and Sicily, without the possibility of being able to defend either." — The [[habsburg-empire]]'s strategy rejected any territorial withdrawal in the Netherlands due to a domino theory where losing Flanders would jeopardize the rest of the empire.
- **The Italian Diplomatic-Military System** (ll. 1778–1778): "But Spanish hegemony rested equally upon a sophisticated “diplomatic system” - perhaps the first of early modern times, and certainly the only one operating in the Age of Philip II." — Spanish control in Italy relied on a system of permanent garrisons, marriage alliances, financial integration with Genoese bankers, and imperial fief manipulation.
- **Messianic Imperialism and Strategy** (ll. 1796–1796): "An aura of “messianic imperialism” pervaded policymaking at the court of Philip II." — Spanish policymakers in the [[early-modern]] era equated state interests with the cause of God, leading to cognitive dissonance and ignoring critical warnings about the Armada's logistics.
- **Failure of the Bid for Mastery** (ll. 1966–1966): "although Paul Kennedy correctly identified the Military Revolution, strategic overstretch, and shortage of money as the proximate causes of the failure of Philip II’s bid for mastery, a messianic imperialism fatally compounded them" — The Spanish empire's bid for hegemony was undone by the high cost of the Military Revolution, strategic overstretch, and religious dogmatism.
- **Administrative Fragmentation** (ll. 1847–1847): "This fragmentation of responsibility constituted a serious obstacle to effective planning" — High-level strategic planning in Spain was severely hampered by dividing regional and military decisions among separate councils (State, War, Indies, Finance).
- **Armchair Strategy of Philip II** (ll. 1903–1903): "For the king was, essentially, an armchair strategist." — Philip II managed minor administrative details from his desk but lacked field operational experience, which led to disastrous decisions like forcing the coordination of Parma and the Armada.
- **Volte-Face in English Relations** (ll. 1877–1877): "On 20 August 1585 Elizabeth signed a formal alliance with the Dutch - the treaty of Nonsuch" — English intervention in the Netherlands via the Treaty of Nonsuch and Francis Drake's raids triggered Spain's decision to launch the Armada.
- **The Fatal Flaw of the Armada Plan** (ll. 1923–1923): "how could a fleet cruising several miles offshore protect Parma’s vulnerable barges from the heavily gunned Dutch blockade squadron once they left the safety of Dunkirk and Nieuwpoort?" — The Armada plan was fatally flawed because it did not resolve how Parma's shallow-draft barges could safely rendezvous with the deep-draft Spanish fleet in the face of the Dutch blockade.
- **Day of the Barricades and French Paralysis** (ll. 1925–1925): "The “Day of the Barricades” made Guise the master of Paris, and shortly afterward he became “lieutenant-general of the kingdom.”" — Spanish subsidies to the Catholic League financed the 1588 uprising in Paris, paralyzing the French crown and preventing France from assisting England against the Armada.

### Chapter 6: The Origins of a Global Strategy: England from 1558 to 1713 (ll. 2171–2506)
- **Tension between Naval and Continental Strategy** (ll. 2176–2176): "Tension between England’s naval and imperial commitments and its periodic need to intervene with land forces on the European continent has characterized the making of strategy from the Elizabethan era to the present." — England's strategy was defined by a tension between isolationist navalism/colonialism and the continental necessity of preventing any single power from achieving European hegemony.
- **The Early Modern Fiscal Bottleneck** (ll. 2190–2190): "The problem lay in its government, a fiscally underdeveloped monarchy chronically unable to exploit the resources of its subjects." — Prior to 1688, England was unable to sustain a global maritime or large land strategy due to a weak tax base and lack of modern financial institutions.
- **The Financial Revolution and Strategic Capacity** (ll. 2216–2216): "In contrast, revenues during the Nine Years’ War totaled 32.7 million pounds, and during the War of the Spanish Succession 64.2 million pounds." — The post-1688 Financial Revolution (parliamentary taxation, land/excise taxes, and the Bank of England in 1694) unlocked public credit, transforming Britain into a global superpower capable of funding both land coalitions and absolute naval mastery.
- **Torrington's Fleet-in-Being Doctrine** (ll. 2228–2228): "as long as there was a “fleet in being” the French would not dare invade, even if the fleet in question were substantially weaker than their own." — Following the Battle of Beachy Head (1690), Arthur Herbert (Torrington) formulated the "fleet in being" doctrine, arguing that a weaker fleet, by remaining intact and avoiding decisive action, can successfully deter an enemy amphibious invasion.
- **French Shift to Guerre de Course** (ll. 2234–2234): "Louis had already reduced the naval budget by 45 percent when Vauban wrote his famous Memoire sur la course in which he advocated commerce raiding over seeking to destroy the allied fleet." — Faced with high costs of land warfare and famine in 1693-94, France abandoned its main battle fleet in favor of a privateer-led commerce-raiding strategy (*guerre de course*) designed to cripple English commerce.
- **English Mediterranean Expansion** (ll. 2240–2240): "The French turn to commerce raiding made an English global strategy not only possible but necessary." — France's abandonment of its main battle fleet freed English warships from defending the Channel, enabling them to establish year-round control in the Mediterranean.
- **Permanent Bases and Global Strategy** (ll. 2296–2296): "The emergence of Britain as a Mediterranean power marked a permanent change in the nation’s commitments. ... it involved the establishment of fixed overseas bases" — The acquisition of Gibraltar (1704) and Minorca (1708) gave Britain permanent, year-round naval bases to control the Straits, protect trade, and support continental diversions.
- **Cruisers and Convoys Act** (ll. 2312–2312): "No other action better symbolized the shift from a defensive main fleet strategy to one based on the protection of British interests worldwide." — The 1708 Cruisers and Convoys Act forced the Admiralty to systematically assign warships to protect merchant shipping and build smaller vessels for commerce defense, representing a shift toward global commerce protection.
- **Pragmatic Synthesis of British Strategy** (ll. 2338–2338): "A British global strategy emerged... not through agreement but because a series of ministries sought in part to cancel out each other’s policies." — Rather than a master plan, Britain's global strategy arose as a pragmatic, politically contested compromise between Whig continental land policy and Tory maritime isolationism.

### Chapter 7: A Quest for Glory: The Formation of Strategy under Louis XIV (ll. 2507–2896)
- **Louis XIV's Grand Strategy** (ll. 2514–2514): "For this proud king who adopted the sun as his emblem, the pursuit of his personal gloire drove the formation of strategy." — French grand [[strategy]] under Louis XIV during the [[early-modern]] period was motivated primarily by the quest for royal and dynastic prestige (*gloire*) rather than rational economic calculation.
- **Centralization of Decision-making** (ll. 2525–2525): "While Louis XIV sought to rule in a rational manner, he crafted his bureaucracy not to govern in his stead, but to insure that he alone governed." — In accordance with the principles of [[absolutism]], Louis XIV centralized administrative control in his own person, ensuring that his bureaucracy executed his will rather than functioning independently.
- **Transition to Centralized Warfare (Guerre de Cabinet)** (ll. 2559–2559): "From that point on Louis engaged in “guerre de cabinet,” in which he and his civilian advisers, in consultation with military commanders, drafted strategic and operational policy from the seat of government." — Following the deaths and retirements of traditional independent commanders in 1675, strategic decisions were firmly subordinated to the king and his civilian ministers.
- **Aristocratic Strategic Values** (ll. 2580–2580): "He disdained the commercial calculations of merchants, and believed the proper prize of war was territory, for land was the supreme good in the aristocratic value system." — French strategic goals reflected aristocratic values that prized territory and dynastic inheritance over mercantilist commercial calculations.
- **Fiscal Capacity and Social Structure** (ll. 2617–2617): "No one could rebuild the foundations of French finance so long as France’s strict social hierarchy conceded great privileges, including tax exemptions, to those at the top." — The structural fiscal capacity of France to wage long wars of attrition was crippled by tax exemptions for the wealthy nobility and clergy.
- **Logistical Strategy of Contributions** (ll. 2633–2633): "that one of the great advantages that you can take from this major expedition is that of allowing my army to live at the expense of the enemy country." — French strategy aimed to wage wars on foreign soil to spare the royal treasury and sustain large armies at the expense of enemy populations.
- **Fortification Strategy (Pre Carre)** (ll. 2658–2658): "always preach squaring things off, not the circle, but the pre." — Vauban advocated for the *pre carre* (squared-off dueling field) to replace a chaotic mix of frontier fortresses with a rationalized double-line barrier to protect the northern border.
- **Aggressive Means for Defensive Ends** (ll. 2713–2713): "Louis’ ultimate goals were essentially defensive but he pursued them by aggressive means." — To establish secure borders, France engaged in aggressive expansionism (such as the Reunions), which provoked defensive European coalitions.
- **Preemptive Strikes and Coalition Building** (ll. 2723–2723): "Finally, in 1688 Louis brought on a general war when he succumbed to what Paul Sonnino has labeled his “fatal predilection for the preemptive strike” by seizing Philippsbourg." — Aggressive deterrence and preemptive operations designed to secure French borders alarmed European powers and made general war inevitable.

### Chapter 8: To the Edge of Greatness: The United States, 1783-1865 (ll. 2897–3382)
- **The Core Strategic Paradox of the US** (ll. 2906–2906): "Most fundamentally, the American Weltanschauung contained an intense, seemingly moral imperative to expand the country’s “experiment” in liberal democratic government beyond its original borders, which required an aggressive strategy along the western frontier. Yet simultaneously strategists also recognized their paramount duty to safeguard the expanding continental homeland from European adversaries, which necessitated a defensive strategy along the eastern seaboard." — US strategy during the [[long-19th-century]] was defined by a tension between an offensive expansionist frontier policy and a defensive coastal posture against European powers.
- **Regionalism and Democratic Constraints** (ll. 2912–2912): "The country’s enormous expanse, primitive communications, and often vitriolic political disputes fostered a fractured regional or local outlook rather than a cohesive national one. As a result, strategy-making frquently contained a “populist” dimension." — Early American strategy was fragmented by regional disputes and political concerns about maintaining popular and electoral support.
- **Geographic Moat and Strategic Depth** (ll. 2914–2914): "The Atlantic Ocean served as a 3,000-mile wide moat; even the mightiest European nation could not project much military strength across it. And when Britain made near-heroic efforts to send reasonably powerful forces to the New World late in the War of 1812, the United States, like Russia, had such tremendous strategic depth that foreign conquest was virtually impossible." — The US was protected from European conquest by the Atlantic Ocean and its vast strategic continental depth.
- **Distrust of Standing Armies vs. Moderate Whig Reality** (ll. 2953–2953): "Most Americans embraced the Radical Whig view that regular standing armies were tyrannical institutions that posed a constant threat to liberty. ... Moderate Whigs, representing a second strand of British ideology, argued that a regular army was nevertheless compatible with liberty so long as constitutional safeguards constrained its despotic potential." — The US military system evolved from the tension between Radical Whig ideology (distrust of regular armies in favor of citizen militia) and Moderate Whig pragmatism (valuing military efficiency under constitutional limits).
- **The War of 1812 and Factionalism** (ll. 3042–3042): "Generals in the same theater rarely cooperated; in the Great Lakes region, naval and army officers fought separate wars. ... support for the war was strong in the South and West. Harnessing that enthusiasm meant attacking Canada through the Great Lakes region. Although this was the least effective road to success in Canada, Madison adopted it because he could not assemble requisite forces in the main theater." — Political and regional factionalism forced President Madison to adopt a flawed, logistically difficult invasion route of Canada, leading to strategic failure.
- **Polk's Peripheral and Direct Strategy in Mexico** (ll. 3097–3097): "The strategy was a peripheral one, designed to achieve his policy goals without striking at the heart of enemy power in Mexico City. ... the strategy failed because Mexico refused to capitulate. Responding to this unforeseen circumstance, the administration applied greater pressure by capturing Vera Cruz and then driving on Mexico City." — During the Mexican War, President Polk shifted from an initial peripheral strategy to a direct campaign to capture Mexico City when the Mexican government refused to surrender.
- **Union Strategy of Exhaustion and Annihilation** (ll. 3144–3144): "For the Confederacy, the North’s simultaneous pursuit of exhaustion and annihilation produced a vicious cycle." — The Union defeated the Confederacy by combining logistical exhaustion of southern resources in the West with army annihilation in the East.
- **Grant's Simultaneous Advances** (ll. 3152–3152): "Not until 1864 did Lincoln find a general, Ulysses S. Grant, of sufficient intellectual prowess and flexibility to perceive the virtue of simultaneous advances. Grant’s 1864 campaign plan demonstrated a strategic vision equal to Lincoln's; it embraced all theaters, put simultaneous pressure on all fronts, and worked “all parts of the Army together, and, somewhat, towards a common center.”" — The Union overcame the Confederacy's interior lines by coordinating simultaneous advances across all fronts under Grant.
- **Logistical Exhaustion and Raiding Strategy** (ll. 3154–3154): "The raiding strategy eliminated the need to garrison more territory and to protect supply lines since the raiding force moved rapidly and lived primarily off the land while destroying everything of military value in its path." — The Union solved its vulnerability to guerrilla attacks on long supply lines by launching fast-moving, self-sustaining raids (such as Sherman's March) that destroyed the Southern logistical base.

### Chapter 9: Strategic Uncertainties of a Nation-State: Prussia-Germany, 1871-1918 (ll. 3383–3803)
- **Prussian-German Army's Dual Function** (ll. 3388–3388): "its "personality” was a peculiarly Prussian strategic culture and its “idea” was its dual function as guarantor of domestic stability and executor of foreign policy." — The Prussian-German military was defined by its dual purpose: maintaining internal conservative order against social democracy and executing external national policy.
- **Emasculation of the War Ministry** (ll. 3421–3421): "Not only did these two reforms destroy the administrative unity that the army had enjoyed since the early nineteenth century, but they effectively removed all personnel matters from public perusal." — Reorganizations in 1883 stripped the Prussian War Ministry of personnel authority to prevent parliamentary oversight, destroying administrative unity and isolating the General Staff.
- **Domestic Prioritization over Military Expansion** (ll. 3431–3431): "The monarch bluntly stated that he preferred a small, well-disciplined, and reliable army to constitutional concessions, even if the latter resulted in a larger army." — The Prussian monarchy deliberately restricted the size of the army to keep it noble-dominated and politically reliable for domestic policing, prioritizing internal regime security over external military strength.
- **Schlieffen's Narrow Professionalism (Fachidiotie)** (ll. 3454–3454): "Blinded by Fachidiotie, they never sought to devise a concept of “grand strategy” that encompassed the political, diplomatic, economic, and psychological components of the national polity." — The Prussian-German General Staff rejected Clausewitzian political-military integration, focusing narrowly on technical details and treating general war as unavoidable.
- **The Schlieffen Plan's Operational Delusion** (ll. 3493–3493): "Mesmerized by visions of a gigantic Kesselschlacht, Schlieffen, in the words of Gordon Craig, “disregarded not only the demographic, technological, and industrial factors which affect the war effort of Great Powers in the modern age, but also the political and psychological forces which are apt to make peoples fight even against hopeless odds.”" — The Schlieffen Plan was a high-risk operational gamble born of despair, seeking a rapid two-front victory in complete isolation from diplomacy, the navy, and economic planning.
- **The Imperial Navy as a Domestic Political Tool** (ll. 3485–3485): "German “navalism” made more sense as an internal poltical move than as a maritime strategy." — The German navy's expansion under Tirpitz (associated with [[weltpolitik]]) was driven by domestic political mobilization and prestige rather than a viable maritime strategy against Britain.
- **Verdun as the Failure of Attrition Strategy** (ll. 3564–3564): "Specifically, the chief of the general staff proposed to win a war of attrition against Britain, France, and Russia by bleeding France’s forces white in the west." — General von Falkenhayn's 1916 offensive at Verdun was a strategy of attrition designed to bleed the French army white on a narrow front.
- **Impasse of Professional Strategies** (ll. 3574–3574): "More than any other battle, Michael Geyer has argued, Verdun showed “the complete disjunctive between strategy, battle design, and tactics, and the inability to use the modern means of war. But most of all, it showed, at horrendous costs, the impasse of professional strategies.”" — The slaughter at Verdun demonstrated the failure of narrow, purely technical military planning disconnected from larger political goals.
- **Unrestricted Submarine Warfare and Strategic Miscalculations** (ll. 3621–3621): "The Imperial navy’s bold strategic initiative in the form of unrestricted submarine warfare likewise suffered shipwreck. ... Far worse, failure to foresee that the major navy, the United States, could and would retaliate effectively against Germany led to final defeat." — The 1917 decision to launch unrestricted submarine warfare was a desperate gamble based on narrow statistical projections that ignored US industrial power and ultimately brought the US into the war, ensuring Germany's defeat.

### Chapter 10: The Weary Titan: Strategy and Policy in Great Britain, 1890-1918 (ll. 3804–4214)
- **Imperial Strategy-making as Pragmatic Response** (ll. 3813–3813): "British strategy, like British policy, was a series of pragmatic responses meant to safeguard British interests." — Pre-WWI British strategy lacked a unified grand design, instead relying on pragmatic diplomatic adjustments to manage relative decline and global commitments.
- **Submarine Commerce Warfare and Convoy Resistance** (ll. 3985–3985): "The navy’s resistance to convoying, finally overcome in 1917, was the result of two factors: a gross overestimation of the number of vessels requiring escorts, and the belief that civilian masters could not keep their ships in formation." — The Admiralty resisted the implementation of the convoy system due to flawed statistical assumptions and doubts about merchant marine discipline, nearly losing the war to German U-boats.

### Chapter 11: The Strategy of the Decisive Weight: Italy, 1882-1922 (ll. 4215–4744)
- **Compartmentalized and Authoritarian Strategy-making** (ll. 4224–4224): "The king, the prime minister, and the foreign minister alone directed foreign policy. Military affairs, by the constitution and precedents established during the recent wars, remained the preserve of the monarch and his war and navy ministers." — Italy's strategy-making was highly compartmentalized, with absolute royal control over secret diplomacy and military policy, which isolated the military command from diplomatic objectives and prevented coordinated national planning.
- **The Obsession with Romanita** (ll. 4247–4247): "The notion that the unification of Italy would be the first step toward the recreation of the Roman Empire had inspired several of the most important figures of the Risorgimento, particularly Giuseppe Mazzini. ... But the desire to emulate the Romans, known as Romanita, combined with inability to do so, plagued the nation for the next seven decades." — Italian grand strategy was plagued by the ideology of *Romanita* (the quest to recreate the Roman Empire), which drove successive governments into aggressive expansionism despite severe economic limitations.
- **The Strategic Failure in East Africa (Adowa)** (ll. 4273–4273): "Neither Umberto nor Crispi understood the force required to subdue Ethiopia. The inadequate army they had sent to Eritrea stumbled to military disaster at Adowa on 1 March 1896. Crispi resigned immediately, his power and Italy’s ambitions shattered." — The disaster at Adowa demonstrated a total mismatch between resources and imperial ambitions, forcing Italy to maintain a large domestic policing army and high taxation, exacerbating internal instability.
- **Equidistance and Detente with France** (ll. 4308–4310): "After 1900 the expanding detente with France occupied the small Italian foreign policy establishment." — Italy pursued a policy of "equidistance" under Vittorio Emanuele III, signing secret agreements with France that conflicted with its formal Triple Alliance commitments, ultimately paving the way for Italy's entry into World War I on the Allied side.
- **The Illusory "Strategy of the Decisive Weight"** (ll. 4566–4566): "The illusory strategy of the decisive weight nearly led to Italy’s ruin H11915-18 and actually did so in 1940-45." — The strategy of attempting to extract concessions by balancing between European alliances repeatedly led Italy into ruinous, unexpected wars (such as WWI and WWII) that far exceeded its economic and military capabilities.

### Chapter 12: Germany 1918–1945 (ll. 4745–5333)
- **Groener's Realist Strategic Transition** (ll. 4771–4771): "The central principle in Groener’s strategic thought was the Reichsgedanke, the idea of Germany as a single entity, with all its political and emotional connotations. ... Groener forced his senior service opponents to acknowledge strategic reality both at home and abroad: Germany was beaten, and further fighting would worsen rather than improve its position." — General Wilhelm Groener managed Germany's post-WWI collapse by forcing the Kaiser's abdication, cooperating with Social Democrats to preserve the military's stabilizing role, and realistically advising the government to sign the Versailles Treaty.
- **The Peacetime Militarization of Society** (ll. 4798–4798): "But in general military pundits supported the complete militarization of society in peacetime as a precondition for the effective conduct of war." — Interwar military analysts concluded that modern industrialized war required the complete, peacetime militarization of the German economy and society.
- **Ludendorff's Ideological Total War Concept** (ll. 4800–4800): "Ludendorff’s work... in his view, the “emotional unity of the nation” had become the decisive factor in war. ... Ludendorff directly named the internal enemies of the militarized Volksgemeinschaft (the national racial community): the Jews, the Roman Catholic Church, and the Socialists." — In *Der totale Krieg* (1935), Erich Ludendorff argued that [[total-war]] demanded the absolute racial and ideological unity of the nation ([[volksgemeinschaft]]), requiring the preemptive suppression of internal enemies.
- **Seeckt's Elitist Mobile Force Strategy** (ll. 4812–4816): "In November 1919 Seeckt became head of the Truppenamt... - **Stülpnagel's Desperate War of National Liberation** (ll. 4822–4826): "Stülpnagel’s “war of liberation” required the transformation of the Republic into an ultramilitaristic nationalist regime. ... Even then the situation would be “desperate,” but means had to be found that, “born of desperation,” would prove so powerful that they would “guarantee us either victory or destruction together with the enemy.”" — In 1924, Joachim von Stülpnagel proposed a desperate "war of national liberation" against France and Poland, combining realistic logistical assessments with an irrational, all-or-nothing goal of victory or total mutual destruction.
- **Wegener's Atlantic Sea Power Critique** (ll. 4832–4832): "Wegener’s realism consisted of a dispassionate analysis of Germany’s naval situation... sea power rested on two principal factors: a fleet and - a new, equally important consideration - geographic position." — Admiral Wolfgang Wegener criticized Tirpitz's focus on a North Sea battle fleet, arguing that sea power required geographical access to the Atlantic, which justified the future seizure of bases in Norway and France.
- **Groener's Strategy of Collective Security and Realism** (ll. 4846–4846): "The tasks given the armed forces by the responsible political leadership constitute the basis for their build-up and use. ... - **Hitler's Ideological Rearmament and Economic Bottlenecks** (ll. 4938–4946): "The task of German policy was simple: to conduct the struggle for Lebensraum ("living space") in the East. ... - **Wehrmacht's Complicity in the War of Annihilation** (ll. 4987–4987): "The attack on the Soviet Union, operation BARBAROSSA, was actually the only German military effort in the Second World War planned as a Blitzkrieg campaign to be completed within limited time and with limited forces. In accordance with Hitler’s aims, it was also planned as a racist war of annihilation. Military leaders, far from opposing the ideological barbarization of warfare, supported and even promoted it." — The Wehrmacht leadership fully supported and participated in the ideological barbarization and racist extermination of Operation Barbarossa, which was planned as a rapid Blitzkrieg and a war of annihilation in the East.

### Chapter 14: U.S. Strategy, 1920-1945 (ll. 5699–6480)

## Volume Synthesis Note

*The Making of Strategy: Rulers, States, and War* (1994) provides a comprehensive historical and comparative analysis of how different states from antiquity to the late twentieth century have formulated grand strategy. 

### The Seven-Factor Framework
The editors argue that strategy-making is not a rigid set of rules but a continuous process of adapting to uncertainty, shaped by seven core domestic and international variables:
1. **Geography**: The size, location, and natural insulation of a state (e.g., the protective 3,000-mile Atlantic moat of the [[united-states]], the island insulation of England, and the lack of defensible borders that generated a security obsession in Israel).
2. **History**: Path dependency and historical experiences, including traumatic events and myths (e.g., the German "stab-in-the-back" myth distorting interwar planning, or the Union Civil War legacy of raiding and annihilation).
3. **Nature of the State and Society**: The structure of government, regime type, and domestic politics. In the modern era, democratic constraints and public opinion restrict strategists (e.g., the populist dimensions of early U.S. strategy, the domestic preservation focus of the Prussian monarchy, or the compartmentalized royal diplomacy of Italy).
4. **Economic and Industrial Capacity**: The fiscal system and capacity to exploit national wealth for war. Modern global wars require massive industrial production and credit (e.g., the English parliamentary taxation system creating the first public debt market to out-finance France, or the U.S. standardization of Liberty Ships).
5. **Military Institutions and Doctrine**: The organizational culture, training, and strategic mindset of the armed forces (e.g., the Prussian-German General Staff's narrow operational focus (*Fachidiotie*) which neglected grand strategy, or the French interwar army's rigid "cult of the defensive").
6. **Strategic Culture and Ideology**: The shared beliefs, values, and assumptions that guide policy (e.g., Spanish messianic imperialism equating state policy with the cause of God, or the Soviet Marxist-Leninist view of class war and ideological encirclement).
7. **Individual Leadership**: The unique role of individual decision-makers in shaping, altering, or mismanaging strategy (e.g., Louis XIV's quest for personal *gloire*, Chamberlain's manipulation of cabinet reports to enforce appeasement, and Ben-Gurion's clear grasp of Israel's demographic limitations).

### Knox's Three Revolutions in Strategic Practice
MacGregor Knox synthesizes the modern transition of strategy as driven by three structural revolutions:
1. **Mass Politics**: Universal suffrage and mass mobilization forced governments to justify state actions in morally satisfying terms to the public. Strategy in the modern age became inextricably linked with propaganda and information control.
2. **Industrialization**: Ceaseless economic and technological flux transformed war from a contest of maneuvers to a test of global industrial capacity and mass production.
3. **Ideological Total War**: Ideologies like Nazism and Stalinism dismantled the traditional European distinction between combatants and civilians, making the total destruction of enemy societies and racial or class groups the ultimate objective of strategy.

### Enduring Continuities
Despite these revolutions, the volume emphasizes that the basic Clausewitzian nature of war remains unchanged:
- **Domestic Friction**: Strategic decisions are rarely the result of objective, rational planning; they are politically contested compromises between competing factions and bureaucratic organs.
- **Strategic Overstretch**: States repeatedly fail to align their political objectives with their physical and fiscal resources (e.g., Habsburg Spain fighting on multiple fronts, and Italy's pursuit of a Roman empire on an inadequate industrial base).
