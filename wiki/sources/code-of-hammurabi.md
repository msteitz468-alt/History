# The Code of Hammurabi (L. W. King translation)

## Source Summary

This is the classic English presentation of the law stele of **Hammurabi**, sixth king of the First Dynasty of Babylon (reigned c. 1792–1750 BCE, middle chronology). The text opens with a long prologue in which the gods Anu, Bel, and Marduk commission Hammurabi to establish justice, destroy the wicked, and protect the weak "so that the strong should not harm the weak." It then presents 282 casuistic laws ("If a man...") covering criminal procedure, theft, robbery, false accusation, judicial corruption, property, agriculture, irrigation, shepherding, commerce, wages and hire, family and inheritance, marriage, divorce, adultery, rape, slavery, debt, and personal injury. The epilogue asserts the permanence of these "precious words," places the stele in the temple E-Sagil before the image of Hammurabi as "king of righteousness," and pronounces elaborate curses on any future ruler who alters, effaces, or ignores them.

The code is not a modern-style legislation but a royal monument advertising the king's role as upholder of cosmic order (kittum and mīšarum). It is the best-preserved and most influential of the Old Babylonian law collections and became a touchstone for later Mesopotamian legal memory.

## Key Themes and Historiography

- **Social stratification**: Explicit distinctions between awīlum (free gentleman), muškēnum (commoner/subject), and wardum (slave), with graduated penalties and rights.
- **Talion and compensation**: "Eye for an eye" in certain injuries to persons of equal status; fines, restitution, or death for others.
- **Protection of the weak**: Repeated emphasis on widows, orphans, and the poor; royal justice as divine mandate.
- **Economic and family regulation**: Detailed rules on loans, interest, agents, wages, field rental, marriage contracts, dowry, inheritance, and slavery.
- **Divine kingship and justice**: The code presents law as extension of the gods' will through the king.

The Mari archives (already used in the wiki) show Hammurabi as a pragmatic coalition player rather than an all-powerful emperor; the Code is best read as ideological self-presentation after his major conquests rather than a daily working code. Earlier collections (Lipit-Ishtar, Eshnunna) and the "mīšarum" edicts provide context. The stele itself was looted to Susa; copies existed elsewhere.

## Volume Synthesis Note

The *Code of Hammurabi* (L.W. King translation of the diorite stele) is the best-preserved and most influential example of an Old Babylonian royal law collection. A long prologue invokes Anu, Bel, and Marduk commissioning Hammurabi "to bring about the rule of righteousness in the land, to destroy the wicked and the evil-doers; so that the strong should not harm the weak" and to rule "like Shamash." It is followed by 282 casuistic rules ("If a man...") and closed by an epilogue that dedicates the stele in the temple E-Sagil in Babylon "before the image of me, as king of righteousness," instructs future kings to observe the words, and pronounces elaborate divine curses on anyone who alters, effaces, or ignores them.

The laws themselves address accusation and proof (including the river ordeal), theft and robbery (with penalties scaled by whether property belonged to a god, the court, or a private person), judicial corruption, military and land tenure obligations of chieftains, agriculture, irrigation and dam negligence, shepherding, commerce and merchant-agent relations, wages and hire (day laborers, oxen, boats, artisans), family and inheritance (dowry, divorce, children of wives versus concubines, adoption), slavery and debt bondage (limited to three years), and personal injury (with talionic "eye for an eye, tooth for a tooth" applied according to the social rank of both victim and offender). The text explicitly recognizes three strata—awīlum (gentleman), muškēnum (commoner), wardum (slave)—with graduated rights and penalties.

Historiographically the Code is no longer viewed as a comprehensive working statute book but as a monumental ideological statement of royal justice issued after Hammurabi's major conquests of Larsa and other rivals. It belongs to a genre of Mesopotamian law collections (compare Lipit-Ishtar of Isin and the laws of Eshnunna) and the periodic mīšarum ("righteousness") edicts by which Old Babylonian kings proclaimed debt relief. The Mari archives (already reflected in the wiki) portray Hammurabi as one player among roughly equal kings rather than an all-powerful emperor; the Code's enduring fame far outstripped the modest and short-lived territorial extent of his actual rule.

For the wiki this primary supplies the concrete textual content that illustrates and grounds the existing coverage of [[hammurabi]], the [[babylon-first-dynasty]], and the [[middle-bronze-age]] in Mesopotamia. It complements the political narrative and "deflation" derived from the Mari letters with the actual rules on social order, economic life, family, and the king's self-presentation as protector of the weak. It significantly strengthens the collection's treatment of pre-classical Near Eastern law and society (Gap 9).

Limitations are explicit: the translation is early twentieth-century; the surviving stele text has lacunae; direct evidence that these rules were regularly applied in contemporary contracts is limited. The Code should be read alongside the CAH volumes and the Mari-based material already in the wiki.
