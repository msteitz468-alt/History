## Overview

Jack Tannous, *The Making of the Medieval Middle East: Religion, Society, and Simple Believers* (Princeton University Press, 2018), is a social-religious history of the late antique and early medieval Middle East that recenters **[[simple-believers|simple believers]]** — mostly agrarian, largely illiterate Christians (and, later, Muslims) who made up the demographic majority of their communities — against the learned elites whose texts usually dominate the field.

Two root questions structure the book: (1) what belonging to a church defined by sophisticated creeds and councils meant for people with little or no formal education; (2) how the Middle East went from a largely Christian region to one in which Christianity was a minority. Tannous argues that the second cannot be answered without the first. Post-[[council-of-chalcedon-451|Chalcedonian]] confessional fracture and later Christian–Muslim encounter both look different once confessional **indifference**, **layering of knowledge**, and the pastoral world of the simple are restored as central contexts.

**Central theses (preview):**
- Most Middle Eastern Christians were “the simple”; doctrinal disputes that split churches were poorly understood by most adherents (including many clergy).
- Intra-Christian competition after Chalcedon (schools, sacraments, polemic, violence) shaped the world Arabs found in the seventh century.
- Early Christian–Muslim interaction was often simple Christians meeting late mass converts and their descendants, while Islam itself was still being elaborated.
- Conversion from Christianity to Islam need not have been religiously momentous for ordinary people who retained Christian practices and beliefs.
- Medieval Middle Eastern history should be **transconfessional** and de-centered from an Islam-only millet framework; non-Muslims were the majority for centuries and channels of late antique tradition into Islam include shared daily life (“rubbing shoulders”), not only textual borrowing.

## Methodological Framework

- **Simple believers as Dark Matter:** the mass of ordinary adherents who do not write the surviving elite texts but whose needs and limits shaped leaders’ behavior and confessional outcomes.
- **Layering of knowledge:** unequal theological literacy within the same church; pastoral care, Eucharist, and schools as tools of community formation among the simple.
- **Transconfessional social history:** refuse equating medieval Middle Eastern history with Islamic history alone.
- **Source stance (Appendix I):** separate literary/theological influence, authenticity, and historical usefulness; inauthentic or literary texts can still document social realities.
- **Terminology (Appendix II):** seventh-century Syriac *ṭayyāyē* for conquerors read against sixth-century usage (not merely “nomad”).

## Volume Synthesis Note

Tannous offers the strongest single-volume social-religious corrective in the collection for the late antique → early Islamic Middle East. Three moves reorganize the field for this wiki:

1. **Simple believers as method.** Elite christology and ʿulamāʾ texts are necessary but insufficient; the pastoral category of “the simple” becomes a structural condition of confessional life, conversion, and early Muslim–Christian encounter.

2. **Christian–Christian before Christian–Muslim.** Post-Chalcedonian competition (sacraments, schools, polemic, indifference) is the matrix of the world Arabs found; [[qenneshre|Qenneshre]] exemplifies educational confessionalization and Greco-Syriac continuity into the Abbasid age.

3. **Transconfessional, dark-matter history.** Medieval Middle Eastern history is not Islamic history alone; literary abundance under Muslim rule does not prove early Muslim demographic hegemony; late antique material enters Islam’s “bloodstream” through co-residence as well as texts.

Tensions with already-ingested sources: Logan’s brief “Monophysite” sketch is superseded for specialist purposes by [[miaphysitism]] + Tannous’s social frame; conquest pages (Logan, Fisher) gain a religious-majority/minority and conversion layer they lacked; CAH/NCMH institutional narratives gain a bottom-up counterpart. Remaining gaps this volume does not fill: military operational history of the conquests; Maghrib and al-Andalus depth; systematic Jewish and Zoroastrian social history at the same resolution.
