# The Early Imperial Republic: From the American Revolution to the U.S.–Mexican War (Blaakman, Conroy-Krutz, Arista eds., 2023)

**Edited volume reframing the early United States (Revolution to U.S.–Mexican War) as an imperial republic from its origins, not a pure republic that later became imperial.** Draws on imperial history, Indigenous studies, and transnational approaches to show continuities with prior empires, Native adaptations, slavery's role in expansion, and global reach even in the "early" period.

## Argument in Brief

- The United States was "always imperial": officials and non-state actors consistently reached for imperial forms (fragmented sovereignty, boundary lines, clientage, public-private governance) to manage expansion and incorporation of new peoples/regions, often pieces of older empires.
- Native nations were not passive; they drew on long experience with multiple empires (Indigenous and European) to survive, confederate, negotiate, or resist; U.S. policy "imperialized" by centralizing control modeled on late British colonial reforms (Royal Proclamation 1763 → Trade and Intercourse Act 1796 boundary line system lasting to 1870s).
- Expansion was inseparable from slavery's empire: seizure of Native lands created space for plantations; illegal slave trade used jurisdictional fuzziness (e.g., Texas borderlands post-1808 ban); Indian Territory was eyed as "a slave state in embryo."
- Early U.S. empire projected globally via "corridors and enclaves" (Hawai'i, Liberia, Caribbean) even before continental consolidation; missionaries, colonization society agents, and slave traders operated with imperial ambitions, sometimes cloaked in liberty or civilizing language.
- Ideological tensions were constitutive: "empire of liberty" / federative empire visions squared revolutionary equality rhetoric with racial hierarchy and removal; peace reformers often accepted non-violent empire; military actors showed "mercenary ambivalence." Black critics (e.g., of colonization) were among the first genuine anti-imperial thinkers in U.S. history.

## Volume Synthesis Note

This volume supplies the wiki's strongest synthetic treatment of the early United States as an imperial formation from its founding. It bridges the colonial-republican divide by documenting the direct inheritance of British administrative tools (boundary line, superintendencies) and shows how slavery's expansion, Native dispossession, and global projections (Hawai'i, Liberia, illegal trade) were constitutive rather than incidental. Key addition to [[periods/age-of-expansion]] and [[periods/long-19th-century]]: explicit framing of "empire of liberty" as racialized imperial ideology with built-in contradictions, plus Native agency and interimperial entanglements often missing from older narratives. Complements prior coverage (e.g. from Cambridge World History Vols 6–7 and other modern sources) by filling the gap in post-1750 North American imperial analysis with Indigenous and transnational lenses. No major contradictions with existing wiki pages on Jefferson, Revolution, or Louisiana Purchase; enriches them with granular mechanisms and primary-grounded quotes.
