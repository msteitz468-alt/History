# The Cambridge World History, Volume IV: A World with States, Empires, and Networks, 1200 BCE–900 CE

**Editor:** Craig Benjamin (Professor of History, Frederik J. Meijer Honors College,
Grand Valley State University; co-author of *Big History: Between Nothing and
Everything*). **Series editor-in-chief:** Merry E. Wiesner-Hanks. Cambridge
University Press, 2015. ~700 pp., 24 chapters in two parts.

## Series Note

Volume IV of the seven-volume Cambridge World History. Where [[yoffee-cwh-v3-2015|Vol. III]]
traced the world becoming "a world of cities," Vol. IV traces the world becoming a
world of **states, empires, and networks** — the consolidation of large-scale
coercive political entities and the exchange webs (above all the [[silk-roads]])
that bound them. It deliberately expands the conventional "classical antiquity"
frame at both ends (1200 BCE rather than ~500 BCE; 900 CE rather than ~500 CE) to
fold in the Bronze–Iron transition, Late Antiquity, the early Islamic caliphates,
Tang China, and developments in the Americas and Oceania normally excluded from
"classical" narratives. This volume is the principal foundation for the wiki's
period skeleton across [[early-iron-age]], [[archaic-period]],
[[classical-antiquity]], [[late-antiquity]], and [[early-middle-ages]], and for the
[[state-formation]], [[empire]], and [[trade-network-formation]] process pages.

## Structure of the Volume

Three nested analytical scales (Benjamin's framing, Ch 1):
- **Global histories** (Part I, Chs 2–9) — thematic chapters treating economy,
  power/gender, slavery, the Axial Age, science, art, and pastoral nomadism across
  the whole period.
- **Trans-regional and regional perspectives** (Part II, Chs 10–24) — regional
  overviews of each "world zone," each paired with one or two close-up regional
  case studies (Baktria, fifth-century Athens, Confucianism, Silk Roads exchange,
  Pataliputra, Chaco Canyon, trans-Saharan trade).

Four **world zones** structure the regional coverage: Afro-Eurasia (the densest,
most networked), the Americas, Australasia, and Oceania. A recurring thesis is that
the period's defining novelty is not any single empire but the linking of these
states into trans-regional **networks of exchange** — the first Silk Roads era
(Han–Kushan–Parthian–Roman, to ~3rd c. CE) and a second (Tang–Abbasid–Byzantine,
from ~7th c. CE).

## Volume Synthesis Note

**The argument.** Benjamin's organizing claim is that the long "classical era" (1200 BCE–900
CE) is best understood not through any single empire but through the recurring triad of its
title — **states, empires, and networks**. The novelty of the age is the consolidation of
large coercive polities *and*, above all, the **exchange webs that linked them across regions**:
the volume is structured around two "Silk Roads eras" (a first, **Han–Kushan–Parthian–Roman**
system to the 3rd c. CE, and a second, **Tang–Abbasid–Byzantine** system from the 7th c.). Its
method is deliberately scalar — every region treated at three nested zooms (global theme →
interregional zone → a single regional case study such as Baktria, fifth-century Athens,
Pataliputra, Chaco Canyon, or trans-Saharan trade) — and deliberately planetary, holding
**four "world zones"** (Afro-Eurasia, the Americas, Australasia, Oceania) within one frame.
The wiki pages built from it return again and again to one motif: connection across distance —
silk, religion, sculpture styles, obsidian, turquoise, gold — as a primary engine of history,
not a backdrop to it.

**What it adds to the wiki.** This volume is the **spine of the wiki's "classical" coverage**.
It supplied or deepened the master narratives of the period's great states — Achaemenid
through Sasanian **[[parthian-empire|Persia]]**, the **[[roman-empire|Roman]]** and
**[[byzantine-empire|Byzantine]]** worlds, **[[han-dynasty|Han]]**–**[[tang-dynasty|Tang]]**
China, the **[[mauryan-empire|Mauryan]]** and **[[gupta-empire|Gupta]]** subcontinent — and
wove them together through the **[[silk-roads|Silk Roads]]** and **[[trans-saharan-trade|
trans-Saharan]]** networks. Its most distinctive contribution, however, is **breadth**: it is
the wiki's near-sole authority for whole world zones the collection otherwise neglects. The
**Americas** (Olmec, [[zapotec|Zapotec]], [[teotihuacan|Teotihuacan]], [[classic-maya|Maya]],
[[moche|Moche]], [[chaco-canyon|Chaco]]), **Oceania** ([[pacific-voyaging-and-settlement|Lapita
and Polynesian voyaging]], [[polynesian-chiefdoms|chiefdoms]], [[aboriginal-australians|
Aboriginal Australia]]), and **sub-Saharan Africa** ([[kush-meroe|Kush]], [[aksum|Aksum]],
[[garamantes|the Garamantes]], [[ghana-empire|Ghana]], [[nok-culture|Nok]]) now have honest, if
necessarily thin, foundations — each flagged as archaeology-and-external-sources dependent, per
the CLAUDE.md coverage map. Three standing controversies were also promoted from it:
[[north-american-states-debate]], [[indian-feudalism-debate]], and
[[pre-islamic-trans-saharan-trade]].

**Cross-volume synthesis.** Read whole, the volume's deepest through-line is that **the same
structural processes recurred independently across all four world zones**: state formation,
monumental urbanism, sacred/divine kingship, tributary hierarchy, and long-distance exchange
arose on their own terms in Mesoamerica, the Andes, Polynesia, and West Africa as they did in
Eurasia. The wiki captures this convergence in the parallel process pages
[[mesoamerican-state-formation]], [[andean-state-formation]],
[[north-american-mound-building]], and [[pacific-voyaging-and-settlement]], and in the repeated
authorial insistence — Lekson on Chaco, McNiven on Oceania, Burstein on Africa — that these
histories must be read in their own context, not measured against an Afro-Eurasian template or
dismissed (in J. M. Roberts' words, quoted to be refuted) as having "shaped" nothing.

**Tensions with already-ingested sources.** Vol. IV dovetails with but also reframes
**[[yoffee-cwh-v3-2015|CWH Vol. III]]** (*Early Cities*): where Vol. III read cities as ritual
or heterarchical *creations* (Teotihuacan's anonymous rule, Tikal the "dedicated city,"
Jenne-jeno without kings, Cahokia the planned "big bang"), Vol. IV re-reads several of the same
places as **nodes in economic and political networks** — Teotihuacan as an obsidian trade
empire, the Maya through divine kingship and the Long Count, Jenne-jeno as a trade hub feeding
Ghana. These are complementary lenses, recorded side by side on the shared pages rather than
overwritten, per the Contradiction Protocol. The volume also sharpens, without resolving, the
periodization tension at its own seams — the **"Axial Age"** (Ch 5) is presented as live debate,
and the **fall of Rome** and **Bronze–Iron transition** are handled as transformations rather
than simple collapses, consistent with the wiki's existing [[fall-of-rome-causes]] controversy.
