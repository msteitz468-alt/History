# Military Effectiveness, Volume 3: The Second World War (Millett & Murray, eds., new ed. 2010)

This is Volume 3 of a three-volume comparative study examining why some military forces succeed while others fail. The project originated in the early 1980s under the auspices of Andrew Marshall (Director of Net Assessment, OSD) to analyze the performance of military institutions in the first half of the twentieth century. The editors selected seven major powers with common experience in the world wars for consistency: the United States, Great Britain, France, Germany, Italy, Russia (Soviet Union), and Japan.

The study is structured around three periods: WWI (Vol. 1), the interwar period (Vol. 2), and WWII (this volume). A common guidance framework essay directs the case studies. This volume applies the framework to the actual conduct of the Second World War.

## Framework: The Effectiveness of Military Organizations (repeated from Vols. 1–2)
The core analytical tool evaluates military organizations across four interconnected levels:

- **Political level**: The ability of the state and its military to secure resources, set appropriate goals, and maintain civil-military relations that support effective strategy.
- **Strategic level**: The formulation of war aims, allocation of resources, and coordination of military means with political ends; assessment of the enemy and the international environment.
- **Operational level**: The planning and conduct of campaigns and major operations; the linking of tactics to strategic objectives.
- **Tactical level**: The actual employment of forces in battle; unit performance, doctrine, training, and leadership at the point of contact.

Excellence at one level does not guarantee success at others. German tactical brilliance in both world wars was undermined by strategic myopia. The framework emphasizes that effectiveness must be judged by outcomes at each level. It incorporates factors like manpower, budgets, innovation, adaptation, and cultural/institutional influences.

## New Edition Introduction (Murray and Millett)
The 2010 introduction (shared across the new editions) reflects on the study twenty years later. It reiterates the project's origins in 1982 correspondence with Andrew Marshall, the choice of three periods reflecting the 1980s strategic environment (NATO vs. Warsaw Pact), and the selection of the seven powers for comparability.

Key points from the text in this volume:
- The volumes provide an "important baseline" for thinking about military performance in the twenty-first century despite a changed strategic environment.
- Military institutions innovate in peacetime under uncertainty and ambiguity about the nature of the next conflict. Technological change presents enormous challenges; wrong choices are hard to correct.
- Part of effectiveness is the ability to adapt to actual conditions of combat. All organizations get the next war wrong to some extent; the more effective recognize flaws and adapt. The historical record shows the opposite is often true.
- Example: The disastrous second Schweinfurt raid (October 1943) repeated the errors of the first despite five months of slaughter, because USAAF leaders clung to the prewar doctrinal belief in self-defending bomber formations.
- Extraordinary difficulty political and military leaders have in assessing strategic issues. Geography, culture (general and military-organizational), political systems, and resources shape institutions differently.
- Absolute necessity of "getting it right" at the level of grand strategy and reconciling war aims with military means. "No amount of operational [or tactical] virtuosity redeemed fundamental flaws in political judgment... political and strategic mistakes live forever."
- The dismal record of military institutions in dealing with the difficulties war creates. Tactical/operational competence is expected but often lacking; strategic and political judgment matters most.
- Lessons applied to post-9/11: U.S. forces prepared brilliantly for the conventional phase in Iraq 2003 (two-division drive to Baghdad) but failed to recognize or adapt to the emerging insurgency. Senior leaders were excessively involved in tactical units at the expense of long-term strategy and operational concept. Units distributed evenly across Iraq instead of concentrating on the Sunni triangle. Mistakes replicated patterns from Vietnam 1965–66 and the 1920 Iraqi revolt (Haldane memoir).
- Quote from brigade commander (1st Armored Division, 2003–2004): Too many leaders excessively involved in tactical units at expense of long-term strategy; little conception of operational art at CJTF-7; by leaving early, ceded countryside to insurgents.
- In the twenty-first century the U.S. will use military forces again but must do so with clear understanding of the fundamental nature of war and the derivative nature of strategy — i.e., understand the history of warfare.

The editors note that these volumes represent "only a way to think seriously about the preparation of military forces for the challenges of the twenty-first century, not unchallenged answers."

## Historiography Note
The volumes are praised for coherence, depth on non-operational factors, and comparative value across seven cultures and three decades. Reviewers highlight insights on manpower, budgets, and institutional performance. The new edition intro acknowledges limitations (dated case selection by 2010 standards; insufficient original attention to innovation/adaptation/culture) while affirming the work as an enduring baseline. Essential for understanding WWII military performance and lessons for modern institutions. The editors stress that the volumes offer a way to think about preparation for future challenges rather than final answers.
