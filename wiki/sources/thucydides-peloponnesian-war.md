# Thucydides, *The Peloponnesian War*

The founding work of **critical contemporary history** — the austere, analytic counterpart to
[[herodotus-histories|Herodotus]]. Thucydides set out to record the war between Athens and Sparta
(431–404 BCE) which he believed "the greatest disturbance" ever to affect the Greek world, "not [as]
a showpiece for a single hearing" but as "**a permanent legacy** (*ktēma es aiei*)" useful to all who
would understand events that, "such is the human condition, will happen again at some time in the
same or a similar pattern" (1.22). The second of the Phase-4 primary sources.

## The Work

Eight "books" (a later division), beginning the narrative proper in Book 2 and breaking off
**unfinished** in the autumn of **411 BCE** — seven years before the war's end, though the text knows
the outcome:

- **Book 1** — the "Archaeology" (a rationalist sketch of early Greek power, to prove this the
  greatest war), the causes, and the *Pentecontaetia* (the fifty years 479–431 in which Athens turned
  the [[delian-league|Delian League]] into an empire).
- **Books 2–5.24** — the **Archidamian War** (431–421): the invasions of Attica, Pericles' funeral
  oration and the [[plague-of-athens-430bce|plague]], the Mytilenean revolt and debate, the *stasis*
  at Corcyra (3.82–3), Pylos/Sphacteria (425), Brasidas in Thrace, the Peace of Nicias.
- **Books 5.25–7** — the uneasy peace, the **Melian Dialogue** (5.84–116, the starkest statement of
  imperial *realpolitik*), and the catastrophic **[[sicilian-expedition|Sicilian expedition]]**
  (415–413).
- **Book 8** — the war after Sicily: revolt in the Aegean, Persian intervention, the oligarchy of the
  Four Hundred (411). Books 5.25–83 and 8 are notably disjointed — likely an early draft.

## Method

- **Secular, rationalist explanation.** Unlike Herodotus, Thucydides admits no gods, oracles or divine
  plan; he distinguishes the publicly-voiced grievances (*aitiai*) from the "truest cause… though
  unacknowledged" — Spartan **fear of growing Athenian power** (1.23).
- **Rigour and its limits.** He relies mainly on **oral testimony** cross-examined ("not… casual
  information or [his] own suppositions"), occasionally cites documents (treaties, inscriptions) and
  argues from confirmatory evidence (*tekmēria*) and likelihood (*eikos*). He is "not infallible"
  (Rhodes): he was taken in by the forged Pausanias–Xerxes letters, and over-rationalised the
  Peisistratid tyranny.
- **The speeches.** ~40 set speeches (and the Melian Dialogue) explore power, empire and decision —
  Pericles' Funeral Oration, the Mytilenean and Sicilian debates, the Corinthians at Sparta. By his
  own statement they mix the speaker's "overall intent" with what Thucydides "supposed would have been
  needed," in his own style; they are the chief site of the debate over his historical method.
- **Artful selection.** "There can be no history without selection and interpretation" (Rhodes):
  Thucydides dwells on episodes that yield **general points** (Corcyra on *stasis*, Melos on
  imperialism, Plataea, Pylos) and treats large but less illustrative campaigns "in a low-key manner."

## Reliability and Reception

For a century Thucydides was enthroned as the **ideal objective historian**; current scholarship
(Hornblower, Connor, Woodman, Badian) stresses instead his **partiality and artistry** — an "artful
reporter" for whom "objectivity was… an authorial stance," shaped by his aristocratic background, his
admiration for Pericles, his hatred of Cleon, and his twenty-year **exile** (after failing to save
Amphipolis, 424/3). Rhodes's measured verdict: "human, involved, and prejudiced," but "not
dishonest," and exceptionally thorough. He invented the analytic strand of history that
[[herodotus|Herodotus]] had not — the model for Polybius, Tacitus, and the modern discipline (see
[[classical-greek-historiography]]).
