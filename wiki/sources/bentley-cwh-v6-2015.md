# The Cambridge World History, Volume VI — The Construction of a Global World, 1400–1800 CE (Book 2)

## Series Note

Sixth volume of the Cambridge World History (general editor Merry E. Wiesner-Hanks),
the first of the two **double volumes** that close the series, reflecting the
expanding source base and complexity of the globalized half-millennium. Volume VI
is split into two physical books: **Book 1 "Foundations"** (global matrices —
environment, technology, disease; crossroads such as the Caribbean, Indian Ocean,
and Southeast Asia; and the maritime and land-based empires of Russia, Islam, and
Iberia) and **Book 2 "Patterns of Change"** (migrations, encounters, trade, law,
commodity flows, production, and religious systems). It is the sequel to
[[kedar-cwh-v5-2015|CWH V]] (500–1500) and is followed by CWH VII (1750–present).

The volume's organizing claim, set by editors Bentley and Subrahmanyam, is that
1400–1800 was the era in which **genuinely global connections were constructed** —
biological (the [[columbian-exchange-1492|Columbian Exchange]]), commercial (silver,
slaves, spices, the chartered companies), and cultural/religious (the worldwide
expansion of Christianity and Islam, the first sustained inter-civilizational
dialogues). This is the period the wiki's [[proto-globalization]] concept points
toward as its culmination, and where [[afro-eurasian-world-system|the Afro-Eurasian
world system]] becomes, for the first time, a genuinely planetary system spanning
the Atlantic and Pacific.

## Structure

- **Part One — Migrations and Encounters** (ch. 1–4): global migration; warfare and
  the military revolution; the first global dialogues (inter-cultural relations);
  legal encounters and the origins of global law.
- **Part Two — Trade, Exchange, and Production** (ch. 5–12): the Columbian Exchange;
  the slave trade and African diaspora; the organization of trade in Europe and
  Asia; entrepreneurs, families, and companies; silver in a global context; Dutch
  and English trade to the East; plantation societies; industrious revolutions.
- **Part Three — Religion and Religious Change** (ch. 13–16): the scholarly
  discovery of religion; Christianity in Europe and overseas; Islam in the early
  modern world; religious change in East Asia.
- **Part Four — Questions of Method** (ch. 17–18): early modern historiography;
  microhistory and world history.

## Volume Synthesis Note

**The argument.** CWH VI Book 2 advances a single, sustained thesis across its
eighteen chapters: that 1400–1800 was the era in which a **genuinely global
world was constructed** — not discovered, and not by Europe acting alone. Its
contributors return again and again to the same correction of the old "rise of
the West" narrative (Marx, Weber, Landes): the most advanced regions of Asia
matched Europe in commerce, productivity, and "industriousness" until late in the
period, and what European states added was the **fusion of armed force with
mercantile capital** (Hoerder's "armed trade") plus the **extractive resources of
the New World** (silver, plantation slavery). The volume is, in effect, an
extended brief for the **late-and-contingent Great Divergence** (Pomeranz), argued
from migration (Hoerder), warfare (Black, against the military-revolution thesis),
trade organization (Trivellato), silver (Flynn), plantations (Burnard), and
labor/consumption (Sugihara & Wong).

**What it adds to the wiki.** This volume builds the wiki's first real coverage of
the **early modern world (periods 19–20)**, which previously had no period pages at
all. It supplies the load-bearing pages for the construction of a global economy —
[[columbian-exchange-1492|the Columbian Exchange]], the
[[atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic slave trade]], the [[plantation-economy|plantation
complex]], the [[global-silver-trade|global silver trade]],
[[early-modern-commerce|the organization of early modern trade]], and the
[[industrious-revolution|industrious revolution]] — anchored by the
[[great-divergence|Great Divergence]] controversy that ties them together. It adds
the era's hybrid power, the chartered [[dutch-east-india-company|VOC]] and
[[english-east-india-company|EIC]] company-states, and the legal scaffolding of a
multipolar world ([[interpolity-law]]). On religion it carries the wiki's three
"world religions" — [[christianity]], [[islam]], [[buddhism]] — into their early
modern, globally expanding forms, adds the [[protestant-reformation-1517|Reformation]],
and registers the era's own intellectual self-reflection (the
[[comparative-religion|"discovery of religion"]], [[global-historiography|global
historiography]], and [[microhistory]]).

**As sequel to CWH V.** The volume is the empirical consummation of what
[[kedar-cwh-v5-2015|CWH V]] called [[proto-globalization]]: Olstein's two
"shortfalls" of the Middle Millennium — the **geographical** (the Americas as
"worlds apart") and the **depth** (local over global) — are precisely what close
here, as the Iberian voyages join the hemispheres and silver, slaves, and
missionary religion begin to bear on local life everywhere. The wiki's
[[afro-eurasian-world-system]] becomes, for the first time, a planetary system.
[[early-modern-migrations]] and [[intercultural-relations-early-modern]] continue
the migration and cross-cultural-encounter threads from CWH V's social and
religious chapters; [[military-revolution]] is the explicit early modern sequel to
[[military-history-middle-millennium]].

**Cross-volume tensions to track.** (1) **Teleology.** The "construction of a
global world" framing risks reading 1400–1800 backward from modern globalization —
the same caution Olstein attached to "proto-globalization." (2) **The Great
Divergence remains open**: CWH VI commits firmly to the late/contingent position,
but the early/institutional case (Landes; Allen's high-wage thesis, which the
volume itself partly relies on) is recorded at [[great-divergence]] without
adjudication. (3) **Civilizational coherence.** Cook's CWH V claim that "Islam
alone was a world civilization" sits awkwardly beside Hsia's portrait of Latin
Christianity cohering into a globally expanding civilization (see [[christianity]],
[[islam]]). (4) **The military-revolution dispute**: Black (CWH VI) dissolves the
strong form of the thesis that Rogers (CWH V) had carried up to 1500 (see
[[military-revolution-thesis]]).

**A note on the source itself.** The collection file labeled "Vol 6 (Complete)
Parts 1 & 2" in fact contains **only Book 2, "Patterns of Change."** Book 1,
"Foundations" — the empire-by-empire surveys (Ottoman, Safavid, Mughal, Ming–Qing,
Muscovy, Iberia) and the crossroads regions (Caribbean, Indian Ocean, Southeast
Asia) — is **absent**. This is the volume's chief gap: the wiki gained thematic and
trans-regional early modern coverage but still lacks dedicated actor pages for
several of the period's central land empires. These are flagged in the period
pages' Collection Coverage notes and remain a sourcing priority.
