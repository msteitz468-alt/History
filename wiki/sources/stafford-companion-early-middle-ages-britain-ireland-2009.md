# A Companion to the Early Middle Ages: Britain and Ireland, c.500–c.1100

Edited by **Pauline Stafford** (Blackwell Companions to British History, 2009). A
collaborative survey of the "Atlantic Archipelago" — Britain *and* Ireland — between the
end of Roman Britain and the aftermath of 1066, deliberately written **against
Anglo-centric teleology**. Stafford's editorial frame insists the period be read "as it
developed, rather than teleologically … from the viewpoint of its later shape," which
"necessitates the inclusion of Ireland" and the de-centring of southern England. The
volume pairs the rich but distinctive Irish sources against the patchier evidence for
northern and western Britain, and treats "Ireland," "Scotland," "Wales," and "England" as
modern terms that "correspond very imperfectly to the political geography, or to the
identities" of the early Middle Ages.

## Volume Synthesis Note

Stafford's *Companion* is, above all, a sustained argument **against teleology and
Anglocentrism**. Its programmatic move — to read the [[british-isles|Atlantic Archipelago]]
"as it developed, rather than … from the viewpoint of its later shape," and so to insist on
the inclusion of Ireland — gives the wiki its analytical backbone for early medieval Britain
and Ireland and dovetails with the **[[concepts/four-nations-british-history|four-nations]]**
frame already imported from Davies's *The Isles*. Where Davies supplies the long-range
deconstruction of the "island story," Stafford's specialists supply the granular early
medieval substance: the richer-than-England Irish sources, the clientship-and-kin society of
Ireland, the making of England, Alba, and Wales, and the dismantling of myths (the "Celtic
church," Cinaed mac Ailpín's genocide of the Picts, Clontarf as a Norse–Irish showdown).

Three cross-volume tensions are worth flagging. First, on the **English state**, Stafford
(via the source-criticism of charters) is more sceptical than the wiki's NCMH-derived pages
of any "precocious, bureaucratic" English precocity — a productive counterweight recorded on
[[concepts/bookland]] and [[controversies/british-national-identity-historiography]]. Second,
the volume **integrates** what NCMH treats in regional isolation: Irish, Scottish, Welsh, and
English histories are read comparatively (clientship vs. lordship, overkingship vs. shire
government). Third, it reframes **1066** not as an English terminus but as an archipelago-wide
process extending to c.1100 — the Norman penetration of Wales, the harrying of the north, and
the dynastic ties reaching Scotland (Margaret) and Ireland (the Uí Briain and Canterbury).
The collection is well served here: Stafford is now the specialist anchor for the whole
archipelago in this period, complementing the CAH/NCMH backbone and the Cunliffe and Davies
syntheses already present.
