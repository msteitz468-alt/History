# The Cambridge Economic History of the Greco-Roman World

Edited by **Walter Scheidel**, **Ian Morris**, and **Richard P. Saller** (all Stanford
University), Cambridge University Press, 2007 (ISBN 978-0-521-78053-7). The first
comprehensive one-volume survey of the economies of classical antiquity: 28 chapters,
768 pages of text, extending from the Aegean Bronze Age to late antiquity. The volume
deliberately begins where the original *Cambridge Economic History of Europe* (1941)
opened — "the decline of the Roman empire" — filling the seventy-year gap where no
single-volume overview of Greco-Roman economic history existed.

## Argument and Significance

The editors frame the field through Douglass North's definition of economic history —
explaining the **structure** and **performance** of economies through time. Their
central historiographical narrative:

- **1890s–WWI:** German debate between **primitivists** (Bücher: closed self-sufficient
  households) and **modernists** (Eduard Meyer, Rostovtzeff: ancient economies resembled
  early-modern Europe). Focus almost entirely on *performance*.
- **Max Weber** shifted attention to *structure* — how status ideology shaped production,
  circulation, consumption — but was largely ignored until mid-century.
- **1950s–1970s:** **Moses Finley** (drawing on **Karl Polanyi's** substantivism:
  reciprocity, redistribution, market as three modes of circulation) made structure
  dominant. *The Ancient Economy* (1973) argued citizen-status ideology braked markets in
  land, labor, and capital, forced elites onto chattel slavery, and produced the
  "consumer city." The "new orthodoxy" (Jones–Finley) stressed cellular self-sufficiency.
- **1980s onward:** **Keith Hopkins** re-introduced *growth* — a Keynesian
  general-equilibrium model in which taxes raised in the provinces and spent on the
  frontiers/Rome stimulated long-distance trade ("taxes-and-trade"). **Saller** estimated
  per-capita growth ~0.1%/annum in the west 200 BC–AD 100 (25%+ rise in consumption).
- **This volume's synthesis:** the Greco-Roman world experienced a real **economic
  efflorescence** (Goldstone's term) — population c. 20M (800 BC) → c. 40M (AD 200),
  rising per-capita consumption of 25–50%, extensive monetization, urban concentration
  (Rome ~1M), and staple (not just luxury) seaborne trade — while remaining "puny"
  by modern standards and never approaching the industrial breakthrough. The editors
  call this a distinctively **"Iron Age economics"** (contrast: Stone Age domestic mode,
  Bronze Age political economy), sitting within Eric Jones's "optimality band" of state
  strength.

The volume also dismantles the sharp twentieth-century **Greco-Roman / Near Eastern**
bifurcation (Finley's private-ownership West vs. palace/temple redistributive East),
while retaining "Greco-Roman world" as a useful analytical category.

## Volume Synthesis Note

This volume is the wiki's spine for **Greco-Roman economic history** and the anchor for
its treatment of the discipline's central debate. Its overall argument — that classical
antiquity experienced a real but bounded **economic efflorescence** (population c. 20M →
40M, per-capita consumption up 25–50% over 800 BC–AD 200, extensive monetization, staple
seaborne trade), driven less by technology than by institutions that kept states within
Eric Jones's "optimality band" — is captured in [[ancient-economy]],
[[economic-efflorescence]], and [[ancient-economy-primitivism-vs-modernism]]. The
recurring analytical through-lines across its 28 chapters map onto the wiki as follows:
the Finley/substantivist vs. modernist/growth axis ([[ancient-economy]]); the
consumer-city question ([[consumer-city]]); ancient demography as the master variable
([[ancient-demography]], [[roman-demography]]); and the New Institutional Economics turn
([[roman-law-and-economic-institutions]], [[new-institutional-economics]]).

Its chronological chapters let the wiki trace one continuous economic narrative from the
Mycenaean palace "mobilization" economy ([[mycenaean-palace-economy]]) through the
post-collapse trough and 8th-century recovery ([[early-iron-age-greek-economy]]), the
archaic commercialization ([[archaic-greek-economy]],
[[western-mediterranean-iron-age-economy]]), the classical Greek peak
([[classical-greek-economy]]), the Hellenistic royal economies
([[hellenistic-economy]], [[ptolemaic-egyptian-economy]]), Republican Italy's slave-villa
system ([[early-roman-italian-economy]], [[roman-economy-late-republic]]), the early
imperial efflorescence ([[roman-agrarian-economy]], [[roman-imperial-trade]],
[[roman-consumption-and-living-standards]], [[roman-imperial-taxation-and-fiscal-system]],
[[roman-provincial-economy]], [[roman-egyptian-economy]]), and the transition to late
antiquity ([[colonatus]], [[late-roman-economy]],
[[late-antiquity-decline-vs-transformation]]).

**Cross-volume tensions.** The volume's post-Finley "measure performance, not just
structure" stance sits alongside the wiki's existing Finley-inflected pages and its
[[ptolemaic-economy]] controversy; where its authors quantify growth from coarse proxies
(shipwreck counts, ice-core lead, survey data, animal bones) the wiki records both the
figures and the methodological caution. Two live scholarly disputes surfaced by the volume
were promoted to dedicated pages — the Roman census [[roman-population-census-count|high/low
count]] and [[late-antiquity-decline-vs-transformation|decline vs. transformation]] — and
the internal disagreement between Giardina and Corbier over the origins of the
[[colonatus]] is flagged in situ per the contradiction protocol. The volume's most
important addition to the wiki is a coherent, quantified, comparative frame that connects
Greco-Roman antiquity to the broader problem of premodern growth (Song China, early-modern
England) rather than treating it in isolation.
