# Foundations of an African Civilisation

## Source Overview

A comprehensive synthesis of knowledge about the peoples of the northern Ethiopian highlands and adjacent Eritrea from approximately 1000 BC to AD 1300 — covering the pre-Aksumite background, the Aksumite civilisation at its peak, and its transformation into the Zagwe dynasty. Published by James Currey (Boydell & Brewer), 2012.

Phillipson's overarching argument: **Aksumite civilisation was fundamentally indigenous**, developing from a local subsistence-farming base with selective South Arabian influence (script, architecture, some cult practices) rather than representing a colonial implantation. The book challenges older diffusionist readings that attributed Aksumite achievements to Arab or South Arabian origins.

## Key Interpretive Claims

1. **Indigenous foundations**: South Arabian influence in the first millennium BC was real but limited; the Aksumite state grew from local development, not colonisation.
2. **Phasing replaces "Pre-Aksumite"**: Phillipson proposes a phased chronological scheme (Phase i–iv) rather than the older "Pre-Aksumite/Aksumite" binary, because the transition was gradual.
3. **Coinage as state-formation evidence**: The sequence of Aksumite coins (starting Endybis, c. AD 270) provides the most reliable chronological spine for Aksumite kings and their religious affiliations.
4. **Decline is transformation, not collapse**: Aksum's abandonment as capital (c. AD 700) was a relocation eastward, not a collapse; Christian continuity was maintained through funerary rock-hewn churches in eastern Tigray.
5. **Zagwe as successor**: The Zagwe dynasty (c. AD 900–1270) continued Aksumite traditions, most visibly in the rock-hewn churches at Lalibela.

## Collection Coverage Note

This is the primary specialist monograph for Aksumite civilisation in the collection. Fills a major gap: the horn-of-africa is otherwise served only by GHA II–III (UNESCO), Reader (*Africa: A Biography*), and scattered chapters in CWH. No competing specialist monograph for this period and region is present.

## Volume Synthesis Note

Phillipson writes against two persistent errors in Aksumite studies: (1) the diffusionist assumption that South Arabian colonists created the civilisation, and (2) the catastrophist reading of Aksum's fall. Both are replaced by gradualist, indigenous-centred narratives. The book's most important contribution to the wiki is a detailed archaeological account of a civilisation the wiki had previously covered only through GHA and CWH summaries. Contradicts Reader's environmental-collapse thesis on timing (Reader suggests near-total environmental collapse by c. 800 CE; Phillipson shows Christian continuity and active church-building in eastern Tigray through the ninth–twelfth centuries).
