# The Cambridge History of Russia, Volume I: From Early Rus' to 1689 — Maureen Perrie (ed.)

## Source Summary

This is the first volume of the three-volume *Cambridge History of Russia*. It covers the period from the origins of Rus' (c. 900) to the end of the pre-Petrine era around 1689 (overthrow of regent Sophia, beginning of Peter the Great's effective rule).

The volume surveys the development of Russia through the Mongol invasions to the expansion of the Muscovite state, dealing with political, social, economic, and cultural issues under the Riurikid and early Romanov rulers.

It is organised primarily chronologically, with thematic chapters especially for later periods. Key themes: bases of political legitimacy; law and society; interactions of Russians and non-Russians; relationship of the state with the Orthodox Church.

The international team of authors incorporates the latest Russian and Western scholarship. It provides an authoritative account of the formative 'pre-Petrine' period before Europeanisation.

Focus is on territories ruled by Riurikid dynasty (10th-16th c.) and Romanovs (17th c.), using dynastic-political criteria. Acknowledges political continuity between early Rus' and Muscovy, while noting claims of Ukraine and Belarus to separate histories.

## Historiography and Approach

The volume uses dynastic-political criteria to define 'Russia' (Riurikid/Romanov ruled lands), avoiding anachronistic national projections. It balances chronological narrative for coherence with thematic depth for later periods.

Major themes addressed: political legitimacy (e.g., grand princely power, autocracy), law and society (Russkaia Pravda, Ulozhenie 1649, serfdom), Russians/non-Russians (conquests of Kazan/Astrakhan, Siberia, relations with Tatars, etc.), state-Orthodox Church (metropolitan, patriarchate).

Historiography: Incorporates post-Soviet Russian scholarship and Western views. Addresses debates on chronicle reliability (e.g., Primary Chronicle for early period), nature of Muscovite state (centralised vs. oligarchic), impact of Mongols, Europeanisation pre-Peter.

Note on pre-1462: Less space due to source scarcity and shifting boundaries; focuses on north-eastern Rus'.

On 21 July, three days after Marfa’s arrival in the capital, Dmitrii was crowned in the Dormition cathedral in the Kremlin." (lines 11034-11056)

Planned science/education, Western-style military training, foreign marriage to Marina Mniszech (Polish escort provoked riot). Assassinated 17 May 1606 by Shuiskii-led conspirators using anti-Polish pretext. "A decree of 1 February 1606 stated that those peasants who had fled during the famine years of 1601–2 ... were not to be returned ... Dmitrii preserved the institutions of slavery and serfdom ... Fled to Kaluga Dec 1609 after Sigismund's direct intervention and Skopin-Shuiskii advance. Murdered 11 Dec 1610 at Kaluga by Tatar prince Peter Urusov. "By the end of 1608, the only major cities to remain loyal to Shuiskii were Novgorod ... and Smolensk ... "At some time in the summer of 1606 a certain Ivan Isaevich Bolotnikov arrived in Putivl’, claiming that he had met Tsar Dmitrii at Sambor ... "The receipt of the patriarch’s letter in Nizhnii, in August 1611, served as the impulse for the organisation of a new liberation army. ... "For the Muscovite state, the seventeenth century was one of evolution and growth, rather than radical change. ... the state existed for the same purpose as it had in the sixteenth century and earlier – to serve the interests of the Muscovite ruling class. ... "Under Alexis, then, two prominent ‘new men’ came to rule Russia. ... "The number of chancelleries themselves grew in the seventeenth century as well. ... "The phrase boiarskaia duma ... has come to stand for the regular high councils ... In the seventeenth century, the competence of the council ... remained undefined ... Kotoshikhin tells us that ‘although [Tsar Mikhail Fedorovich] used the title “autocrat”, [he] could do nothing without [the boyars]’." (lines 12458-12528)
- Town governor (gorodovye voevody) system spread post-1613 to most districts; broad authority over defence/tax/justice/police via nakazy, reports, audits; subordinated guba/zemskii elders; clerks (pod’iachie) key for records. Avocational (court nobles, not specialists); feeding (kormlenie) common/tolerated; corruption (bribes, extortion) endemic but investigated via syshchiki/obysk. "By the 1620s most districts were under ... town governors ... with staffs of clerks and constables ... Responsibility for most aspects of defence, taxation, policing, civil and criminal justice ... The chancelleries recognised that central control could not rely entirely on quarterly or annual account submissions and therefore they continued to place greater reliance on subjecting outgoing governors to end-of-term audits." (lines 12672-13021)
- Wars/recovery: Deulino 1618 ceded Smolensk/Chernigov/Seversk to Poland; Stolbovo 1617 losses to Sweden. Smolensk war 1632-34 failed (Shein beheaded); Polianovka 1634 Wladyslaw renounced throne claim. Southern defence: Belgorod Line expanded 1630s-50s; foreign formation troops; Don cossack control tightened. Thirteen Years War 1654-67: Pereiaslav 1654, rapid gains (Vilnius, Smolensk), Swedish intervention, Andrusovo 1667 (left-bank Ukraine + temporary Kiev to Moscow). Chyhyryn campaigns 1677-78 vs Ottomans (evacuated/burned 1678); Golitsyn Crimean expeditions 1687/89 (failed Perekop but diverted Tatars). "The armistice signed at Polianovka on 4 June 1634 therefore left the Smolensk, Chernigov and Seversk lands in Polish hands. ... With the signing of the Andrusovo Armistice the Poles finally conceded Smolensk, Seversk and Chernigov to Muscovy ... and confirmed Muscovite sovereignty over left-bank Ukraine." (lines 13392-13450, 13946-13950)
- Non-Russian subjects (steppe/Siberia/Caucasus/Ukraine): Nogais weakened/dependent then displaced by Kalmyks (1630s raids); Belgorod/Simbirsk lines for colonisation/defence; iasak on natives as "eternally" paying subjects; Siberia advance to Enisei then halted at Qing (Albazin); North Caucasus Tersk fort maintained via payments to Kabardinians/Kumyks etc. Ukraine: Khmelnytskyi alliance Pereiaslav 1654 for protection vs Poland (Muscovy gained left bank autonomy initially eroded); Andrusovo partition; cossacks co-opted/controlled via garrisons. Conquest: indirect (co-opt elites, payments) to direct; fugitives/iasak drain; fort lines key. "The native peoples were to become ‘the iasak-paying subjects eternally’ ... Moscow proceeded cautiously from indirect rule in the borderlands to direct rule once the borderlands were more firmly integrated. ... Russia’s acquisition of eastern Ukraine was very similar to its annexation of Siberia." (lines 14623-14625, 14897-14899, 14761-14763)
- Economy base: silver (reminted thalers), monetarist policy; prices stable except war/crop failure (copper uprising 1662 from debasement). "The Russian economy in the period 1613–89 was quite sophisticated. The leaders of the hypertrophic Muscovite state were basically monetarists ... The currency was based on silver, primarily reminted thalers imported from other countries in Europe ... All commerce in Russia was based on cash or barter." (lines 14971-15000)
