# The Last Mughal: The Fall of a Dynasty, Delhi 1857 (Dalrymple, 2006)

A narrative reinterpretation of the **[[indian-rebellion-1857|Rebellion of 1857]]** as seen from
**Delhi** and the doomed court of **[[bahadur-shah-zafar|Bahadur Shah Zafar]]**, built on the
Persian/Urdu archive of the rebel city.

## What it is

The book follows the eighty-year-old emperor Zafar — a mystic, calligrapher, and serious Urdu poet
who presided over a brilliant late-Mughal cultural flowering in a Delhi that had long since lost
real power — as the mutinous sepoys of Meerut converge on his palace in May 1857 and proclaim him
their figurehead. It narrates the chaotic rebel siege-administration, the British assault and
recapture of the city, the savage **reprisals** (the sack of Delhi, the summary executions, the
shooting of the Mughal princes), Zafar's **trial**, and his **exile to Rangoon**, where the dynasty
of Babur ended in 1862.

## Distinctive arguments

- **An Indian-sourced account.** By mining the **Mutiny Papers**, Dalrymple recovers the revolt as
  lived in Delhi — the sepoys, the jihadis, the city's people, the court — rather than through
  British memoirs, restoring Indian voices and agency.
- **The religious dimension.** Controversially, he stresses the role of **religion** — the *ghazis*
  and "Wahhabi"-influenced preachers calling **jihad** against the British alongside the sepoys'
  material grievances — and reads 1857 as foreshadowing later confrontations between the West and
  Islamic militancy (a deliberately "combative" thesis on the origins of religious confrontation).
- **Company aggression and the loss of a world.** Continuing the theme of his *White Mughals*, he
  frames the revolt as a response to the increasingly **aggressive, evangelising, racially
  exclusive** late Company, and elegizes the cosmopolitan Indo-Islamic Delhi that 1857 destroyed.
