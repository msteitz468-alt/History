# A History of the Later Roman Empire, AD 284–641 (Stephen Mitchell, 2nd ed. 2015)

Blackwell History of the Ancient World survey covering the later Roman Empire from the accession of [[diocletian|Diocletian]] (284) to the death of [[heraclius|Heraclius]] (641). Mitchell’s four working principles (stated in the first-edition preface): chronological-geographic precision; preference for **historical narrative** over purely cultural explanation; primary witnesses quoted at length; and absorption of modern secondary literature. The second edition adds a full new **Chapter 13** on the fall of Rome (tax base, military capacity, plague demography) and revises Constantine, the Roman state, Asia Minor, and Egypt.

Mitchell deliberately frames the subject as the **later Roman Empire** (state and institutions, empire-wide) rather than “late antiquity” as a primarily eastern cultural-religious construct, while still integrating Brown-school religious and social themes in Chs 7–8 and 10.

## Core Argument (spine)

1. **Periodization is interpretive.** “Later Roman Empire,” “early Byzantine,” and “late antiquity” are not neutral labels — they encode state-centred, eastern-Constantinopolitan, or long-cultural (c.200–800) agendas respectively.
2. **Narrative first.** Chs 3–4 and 11–12 carry a continuous political-military story from the tetrarchy through Justinian to the Persian and early Islamic crises; thematic Chs 5–10 explain the state, barbarian kingdoms, religion, and economy that made that story possible.
3. **The Roman state remained a fiscal-military machine** (Chs 5, 9) whose capacity depended on taxation, army commands, and an ideology of emperorship — until provincial revenue and manpower were lost.
4. **Barbarian kingdoms were not pure external destroyers** (Ch 6) but political systems interacting with Roman institutions; western transformation and eastern survival are parallel problems.
5. **Christianization reordered public identity** (Chs 7–8) without simply replacing classical culture; conversion was social and political as well as doctrinal.
6. **Fall is real and multi-causal** (Ch 13): diminishing tax revenue, loss of military capacity, and mid-sixth-century demographic catastrophe (plague) — in dialogue with Wickham, Heather, Ward-Perkins, and the Brown school — reassert decline as a legitimate research agenda after decades of transformation-first scholarship.

## Volume Synthesis Note

Mitchell supplies the wiki’s best single-author **integrated 284–641 narrative spine** between the western externalism of Heather, the material catastrophism of Ward-Perkins, the fiscal-structural long arc of Wickham, and the cultural late antiquity of Brown. Methodologically he insists on **histoire événementielle** and primary quotation without surrendering institutional analysis: Chs 3–4 and 11–12 carry continuous political-military narrative; Chs 5–10 explain the fiscal-military state, barbarian kingdoms, religious identity, and provincial archaeology that make that narrative possible.

The durable interpretive contribution is **Chapter 13** (new in the 2nd edition): empire as a tax-funded military system; western collapse as invasion → tax-base collapse → army hollowed into a “shadow”; eastern implosion as estate fiscal leakage + military decay + **plague-maximizer** demography (against Wickham’s relative minimization of 541+ plague). Mitchell thus reopens decline as a legitimate research agenda after the Brown-school “transformation” generation, while absorbing Wickham’s tax-based vs land-based typology and ranking *Framing the Early Middle Ages* as the most important post-Jones survey. He sits as a **qualified catastrophist** on the wiki’s fall controversies — east and west together, not 476 alone — ending the story at Heraclius’s death and the loss of Egypt and the Near East (converging with Harris’s Yarmuk horizon and Woolf’s “three heirs”).
