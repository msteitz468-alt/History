# The Age of Capital: 1848–1875 (Eric Hobsbawm)

The second volume of **Hobsbawm's** four-book modern history: the quarter-century in which **industrial
capitalism became a global system** and the **bourgeoisie** reached the height of its confidence. Fourth
source of **Gap 1**; deepens the **[[long-19th-century|Long 19th Century]]**.

## The Argument

After the **defeat of the [[revolutions-of-1848|revolutions of 1848]]** — the failure of the romantic,
democratic and national hopes of the "springtime of peoples" — the period 1848–1875 saw the **worldwide
victory of capitalism** in a different, more pragmatic key:

- a **global economic boom** built on **railways, steamships, the telegraph**, free trade and the
  integration of a true **world market** — capitalism's most expansive and self-confident phase;
- the triumph of **liberalism** as the ideology of the ascendant **middle classes**, and the spread of
  industrial society;
- the **making of nation-states** by "revolution from above" — the unifications of **Italy** and
  **Germany**, the **US Civil War** and the defeat of the slave South, the Meiji opening of Japan —
  channels for capitalist modernization rather than popular democracy;
- the worldwide penetration of European capital and power, drawing Asia, Africa and Latin America into a
  Western-centred economy.

The volume closes around **1873**, as the long boom gives way to depression and the more aggressive,
imperial phase taken up in *[[hobsbawm-age-of-empire|The Age of Empire]]*.

## Significance

*The Age of Capital* is the standard interpretive account of **mid-Victorian capitalist globalization**
and bourgeois society, bridging the revolutionary 1789–1848 era and the imperial age. It supplies the
wiki's Long-19th-Century narrative for the decades of railways, free trade, and nation-building.
