# On Strategy: A Critical Analysis of the Vietnam War (Summers, 1982)

Harry G. Summers Jr.'s *On Strategy* is the seminal Clausewitzian strategic analysis of the Vietnam War from the U.S. perspective. Its core thesis, crystallized in the April 1975 conversation in Hanoi between the author and a North Vietnamese colonel, is that the United States achieved tactical and logistical success on the battlefield yet suffered strategic defeat because it fundamentally misidentified the nature of the war and therefore applied the wrong strategic framework.

The book is structured in two parts that deliberately mirror Clausewitz: Part I examines "The Environment" (national will of the people and Congress, and the multiple sources of friction—people, bureaucracy, danger, doctrine, and dogma); Part II ("The Engagement") applies the principles of war (objective, offensive, mass/economy of force/maneuver, unity of command, security/surprise, simplicity, plus coalition warfare) at the strategic level. An Epilogue updates the argument post-1982 and extracts enduring lessons for the use of force.

## What it contributes

- The canonical statement of "tactical victory, strategic defeat": superb logistics and battlefield performance rendered irrelevant by strategic error.
- Insistence that the Second Indochina War was not primarily a revolutionary/guerrilla war but an interstate war of aggression waged by the regular forces of North Vietnam; the VC/NLF was a supporting arm, not the decisive instrument (the final conquest of Saigon was executed by four NVA corps).
- Demonstration that U.S. strategy violated Clausewitz's "first, the supreme, the most far-reaching act of judgement": correctly identifying the kind of war on which one is embarking.
- Detailed critique, grounded in the principles of war, of how U.S. civilian and military leadership confused preparation for war with war proper, substituted management and systems analysis for strategy, and failed to link military means to achievable political ends.
- Revitalization of Clausewitz for the U.S. military after a generation of civilian-dominated limited-war theory; direct influence on post-Vietnam doctrinal reform and the conditions for future commitments (clear political objectives, public support, unity of command, etc.).
- Explicit contrast between 18th-century cabinet-war attitudes (war as government business, people as instrument) and the people's war reality of the 20th century.

## Key Claims (Range 01 populated)

**Range 01 (exclusive cache lines 1-2500):** Front matter, Foreword, Introduction ("Tactical Victory, Strategic Defeat"), Part I Ch. 1 "The National Will: The People", Ch. 2 "The National Will: The Congress", Ch. 3 "Friction: The People", Ch. 4 "Friction: The Bureaucracy" (partial to ~Clausewitz critique of rationalistic theory). All claims and verbatim quotes grounded exclusively in this slice. No outside knowledge.

**Core thesis grounding (Introduction):** Tactical success in logistics and battlefield engagements did not translate to strategic victory because the nature of the war was misidentified and the "missing link" of military strategy ("how" to employ means for political ends) was never supplied.

**National will / mobilization failures (Ch. 1-2):** President Johnson deliberately chose not to mobilize the American people or seek a declaration of war (to protect Great Society programs and avoid "total war" associations); Congress passed the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution instead, later disavowed; this created a "neo-18th century" cabinet-war dynamic (people as instrument of government) that left the Army vulnerable to friction and denied it the "strength of the passions of a people mobilized for war."

**Friction analysis (Ch. 3-4):** Natural antimilitarism amplified by draft policies, euphemistic language, and cold-blooded presentation of war; bureaucratic friction from civilian systems analysts (PPBS) dominating strategy formulation, military abdication of the strategic role, and confusion of "preparation for war" (organize/train/equip) with "war proper."

#### Friction (later Part I): Danger, Doctrine, Dogma (Ch. 5–7)
- Fears from Korean experience (nuclear destruction of homeland; Chinese land war in Asia intervention) led to "taking counsel of our fears" (Stonewall Jackson). "The key word is 'counsel' because if fears dominate thinking the resulting paralysis will only increase the hazard."
- Post-Korea doctrine distortion: "we drew precisely the wrong lesson from that war. Instead of seeing that it was possible to fight and win a limited war in Asia regardless of Chinese intervention, we again (as we had done with nuclear war) took counsel of our fears and accepted as an article of faith the proposition that we should never again become involved in a land war in Asia."
- Pre-WWII doctrine sound per FM 100-5 1939: "The conduct of war is the art of employing the Armed Forces of a nation in combination with measures of economic and political constraint for the purpose of effecting a satisfactory peace... The ultimate objective of all military operation is the destruction of the enemy’s armed forces in battle. Decisive defeat in battle breaks the enemy’s will to war and forces him to sue for peace which is the national aim."
- Post-WWII shift made destruction "an end in itself" (unconditional surrender); linkage to political peace dropped. Korea forced adjustment of objectives to status quo ante after Chinese intervention.
- Counterinsurgency dogma distortion (Ch. 7): Westmoreland stated "The Communists in Vietnam waged a classic revolutionary war." But per Thompson (revolutionary war "designed to reach a decisive result on its own"): "with hindsight it is clear that by Sir Robert Thompson’s own definition, he was exactly wrong... Even at the very end there was no popular mass uprising to overthrow the Saigon government. Their actions fit precisely Sir Robert Thompson’s definition of partisan warfare—they harassed and distracted both the United States and South Vietnam so that North Vietnamese regular forces could reach a decision in conventional battles."
- By 1970: "at least 80 percent of the day-to-day combat in South Vietnam was being carried on by regular People’s Army of Vietnam (PAVN) troops... Genuine black-pyjama southern guerrillas... had been decimated and amounted to no more than 20 percent of the communist fighting force" (Chalmers Johnson).
- Doctrinal mission creep: 1968 FM 100-5: "the fundamental purpose of the U.S. military forces is to preserve, restore, or create an environment of order or stability within which the instrumentalities of government can function effectively under a code of laws." Contrast pre-war: "the defeat of an enemy by application of military power directly or indirectly against the armed forces which support his political structure."
- Weyand (postwar): military can "defeat enemy forces on the battlefield... blockade... cut lines of supply... carry the war to the enemy"; but "there are also fundamental limitations... the Congress and the American people will not permit their military to take total control of another nation’s political, economic, and social institutions... The failure to communicate these capabilities and limitations resulted in the military being called upon to perform political, economic, and social tasks beyond its capability while at the same time it was limited in its authority to accomplish those military tasks of which it was capable."

#### Ch. 8: Tactics, Grand Tactics, and Strategy — Supreme Judgment and Nature Misidentification
- Verbatim Clausewitz (I:1): "The first, the supreme, the most far-reaching act of judgement that the statesman and commander have to make is to establish ... the kind of war on which they are embarking; neither mistaking it for, nor trying to turn it into, something that is alien to its nature. This is the first of all strategic questions and the most comprehensive."
- "As MILITARY PROFESSIONALS, it was our job to judge the true nature of the Vietnam war, communicate those facts to our civilian decision-makers, and to recommend appropriate strategies. As Clausewitz said, that was the first strategic question and the most comprehensive."
- "it is essential to distinguish the First Indo-China war between France and the Viet Minh from the Second Indo-China war between North Vietnam and South Vietnam. The first was a revolutionary war. The second was not. The forces that besieged Dien Bien Phu grew out of the guerrilla movement, the forces that captured Saigon did not grow out of the Viet Cong but were the regular armed forces of North Vietnam. This critical difference validates the official U.S. government position that the Vietnam war was caused by aggression from the North."
- Guerrilla operations misread as strategy: "we saw their guerrilla operations as a strategy in itself... We attempted to counter it by using such models as the British model in Malaysia." In reality: "the guerrilla war was a kind of economy of force operation on the part of North Vietnam to buy time and to wear down superior U.S. military forces. As Norman Hannah... wrote in 1975, 'In South Vietnam we responded mainly to Hanoi’s simulated insurgency rather than to its real but controlled aggression, as a bull charges the toreador’s cape, not the toreador.'"
- Ia Drang (Nov 1965): 32nd/33rd/66th NVA regiments vs. US 1st Cavalry Division. "On a strategic scale, a brilliant spoiling attack had completely derailed Hanoi’s hopes of earning a decisive victory before full American might could be deployed to South Vietnam. Moreover, in a head-on clash between an American and a North Vietnamese division, on the enemy’s chosen ground, the NVA unit had been sent reeling in retreat." "Now was the time for the United States to take the offensive." Instead: "instead of orienting on North Vietnam—the source of war—we turned our attention to the symptom —the guerrilla war in the south. Our new 'strategy' of counterinsurgency blinded us to the fact that the guerrilla war was tactical and not strategic."
- Negative aim (Clausewitz): "'the natural formula for outlasting the enemy, for wearing him down.' ... Basic to the success of a strategic defensive in pursuit of the negative aim, therefore, is the assumption that time is on your side. But the longer the war progressed the more obvious it became that time was not on our side. It was American rather than North Vietnamese will that was being eroded."
- Result: "tactical success is not necessarily strategic success... our tactical successes did not prevent our strategic failure and North Vietnam’s tactical failures did not prevent their strategic success... It caused the United States to deploy against a secondary force and exhaust itself in the effort."

#### Ch. 9: The Objective — Principle Evolution, Applications, and Failures
- Vietnam-era FM 100-5 (19 Feb 1962): "Every military operation must be directed toward a clearly defined, decisive and attainable objective. The ultimate military objective of war is the destruction of the enemy’s armed forces and his will to fight. The objective of each operation must contribute to this ultimate objective... Every commander must understand and clearly define his objective and consider each contemplated action in light thereof."
- Historical divergence: 1921 Training Regulations had explicit strategic ("the objective assigned military forces must be in consonance with the national objective") + tactical dimensions. By 1939 strategic under "conduct of war"; post-WWII strategic dropped, partially restored 1954. During Vietnam: "there were changes in both the strategic and tactical definitions of The Objective. What had been a clear relationship between military strategy and political objectives was lost in an abstruse discussion of national objectives, rejection of aggression, deterrence and the whole concept of a spectrum of war." 1968 edition: reduced to "'defeat of the enemy’s armed forces'" (eliminated "and his will to fight").
- Driving importance: "Our loss of focus on The Objective was particularly damaging, since this is the driving principle of war. Clausewitz’s clarification of the importance of the objective was one of his main contributions to understanding the nature of war... 'the political object is a goal, war is the means of reaching it, and means can never be considered in isolation from their purpose.'"
- US political separation: MacArthur: "the general definition which for many decades has been acceptable was that war was an ultimate process of politics; that when all of the political means failed, we then go to force." Contrasts Clausewitz: "it is clear that war should never be thought of as something autonomous but always as an instrument of policy."
- Enemy objective clarity vs. US diffusion: NVA/Lao Dong ultimate aim (1952 captured doc per Honey): "to install Communist regimes in the whole of Vietnam, in Laos, and in Cambodia." Collins (1973): "enemy strategy can be outlined quickly, since it was simple, concise, and consistent... Controlling and communizing all of Indochina have always been the foe’s overriding objectives." US: Arnold analysis found "some twenty-two separate American rationales... grouped into three major categories: from 1949 until about 1962, emphasis was on resisting Communist aggression; from 1962 until about 1968, the emphasis was on counterinsurgency; after 1968, preserving the integrity of American commitments was the main emphasis."
- MACV mission confusion (Westmoreland): "To assist the Government of Vietnam and its armed forces to defeat externally directed and supported communist subversion and aggression and attain an independent South Vietnam functioning in a secure environment." "Even a cursory mission analysis reveals two divergent specified tasks. The first... is clearly a legitimate military objective... The second... is more a political than a military objective. The confusion over objectives inherent in the MACV mission statement was to plague our conduct of the war."
- 1954 Ridgway analysis (Dien Bien Phu intervention): "If civilian authority finds the cost to be greater than the country can bear, then either the objectives themselves should be modified, or the responsibility for the risks involved should be forthrightly accepted. Under no circumstances... should the professional military man yield, or compromise his judgment..." Concluded cost "as great as, or greater than, that we paid in Korea"; intervention abandoned.
- Failure to isolate battlefield / sanctuaries: "In Vietnam such an adjustment [of objectives, as in Korea] was never made. Instead of focusing our attention on the external enemy, North Vietnam—the source of the war—we turned our attention to the symptom—the guerrilla war in the south—and limited our attacks on the North to air and sea actions only." Laos/Cambodia sanctuaries: "a self-inflicted wound. The myth of their neutrality gave North Vietnam an immense tactical and strategic advantage..."
- Post-Tet mission shift (1969, per Kissinger): "The new mission statement [which went into effect on August 15] focused on providing 'maximum assistance' to the South Vietnamese to strengthen their forces, supporting pacification efforts, and reducing the flow of supplies to the enemy." "This new mission statement formally announced our movement into the third category of political objectives—preserving the integrity of American commitments."
- Outcome of objective confusion: Kinnard 1974 survey: "'almost 70 percent of the Army generals who managed the war were uncertain of its objectives.' ... 'mirrors a deep-seated strategic failure: the inability of policy-makers to frame tangible, obtainable goals.'"

#### The Offensive (Ch. ~10)
- US strategic posture: entered "on the strategic defensive" in consonance with containment (precedent of Korea status quo ante; no action to shrink Soviet dimensions as in Hungary 1956). "In pursuit of its policy of containment the United States entered the Vietnam war on the strategic defensive. As we will see, our failure to appreciate what this strategic posture entailed contributed to our ultimate failure." (ll. ~20-30)
- NVA overall: strategic offensive for conquest of South Vietnam. Tactical offensive 1964 (regular army commitment to gain "spectacular victory—a Southern Dien Bien Phu" by slicing SV in half Pleiku-An Khe-Qui Nhon with 3 NVA divisions from Cambodia sanctuaries, supported by local VC). Reverted to tactical defensive post-Ia Drang 1965 defeat ("economy of force effort—Viet Cong guerrillas supplied and augmented by selected North Vietnamese regular units—while preserving the bulk of their regular forces in their homeland sanctuary"). Tactical offensive Tet 1968 and 1972 Eastertide. 1975: 17-division conventional attack for "total military victory." (ll. ~30-90, ~870-890)
- 1975 NVA prep (Dung account): Rear Service built "strategic route east of the Truong Son Range [Ho Chi Minh Trail] ... length of this route, added to that of the other old and new strategic routes ... is more than 20,000 kms. The 8-meter wide route of more than 1,000 kms ... With 5,000 kms of pipeline ... we were capable of providing enough fuel ... More than 10,000 transportation vehicles"; "To stage large scale annihilating battles, it was no longer advisable to field only independent or combined divisions. Larger mobile armies ... Army corps were gradually formed and deployed in strategic areas most vital to insure mobility." Oct 1974 Political Bureau conference assessed US resolve post-Watergate/Nixon resignation/economic woes: "Comrade Le Duan drew an important conclusion ... 'Having already withdrawn from the South, the United States could hardly jump back in, and no matter how it might intervene, it would be unable to save the Saigon administration from collapse.'" Chose Central Highlands as "extremely important area strategically" for main battlefield. (ll. ~120-170)
- Opening engagement Ban Me Thuot 10 Mar 1975: "nearly 3 divisions against the enemy’s [sic] one regular regiment of the 23rd division and 3 civil guard multibattalion units. Thus, the enemy was in a weak and isolated position." Ratios (Dung): "in this area ... infantry ... 5.5 of our troops for each enemy soldier. ... tanks and armored vehicles, the ratio was 1.2 to 1. In heavy artillery, the ratio was 2.1 to 1." (ll. ~1010-1030)
- US confusion of posture: "The Americans had designed a purely defensive strategy for Vietnam. It was a strategy that was based on the attrition of the enemy through a prolonged defense and made no allowance for decisive offensive action." (Lung). Westmoreland 3-phase campaign plan: Phase I halt losing trend; Phase II "take the offensive ... to destroy enemy forces and reinstitute pacification programs" (error: pacification ARVN task; should have been isolate battlefield); Phase III defeat if persisted. (ll. ~190-200, 310-330)
- Viable alternatives (not adopted): Gen. Cao Van Vien "The Strategy of Isolation" — fortify 17th parallel Dong Ha to Savannakhet + amphibious at Vinh/Ha Tinh "to cut off the North’s front from its rear"; JCS Aug 1965: deny "physical capability to move men and supplies through the Lao corridor..." by combined actions (neither approved/rejected); Westmoreland Apr 1967: "drive into Laos and possibly Cambodia and for an amphibious hook north of the DMZ" (blocked by LBJ unwillingness to mobilize reserves). Gen. Bruce Palmer Jr. (1977 USAWC): 5-division force (2US/2ROK/1ARVN) on DMZ line to Lao-Thai +1 US div highlands/Saigon area; Marines strategic reserve for DMZ/amphib threat; "four fewer divisions than the ten and two-thirds ... bulk ... fought on ground of their choosing which the enemy would be forced to attack"; "clear and understandable objective—a peace-keeping operation to separate the belligerents"; "much smaller U.S. logistics effort"; "Cut off from substantial out-of-country support, the Viet Cong was bound to wither on the vine." (ll. ~340-540, 610-630)
- Graduated response: civilian "slow squeeze" (McNaughton: "orchestration of activities which would proceed in crescendo fashion"; option to escalate/quicken at any point) adopted over JCS "hit hard and fast to obtain maximum impact with minimum loss" (like "pulling a tooth bit by bit rather than all at once"). (ll. ~280-310)
- Westmoreland (autobiography): "I suffered my problems in Vietnam because I believed that success eventually would be ours despite them, that they were not to be, as Napoleon put it, instruments of my army’s downfall." (Napoleon: tender resignation rather than execute defective plan.) (ll. ~390-410)
- Clausewitz: "Defense without an active purpose is self-contradictory both in strategy and in tactics." "A major victory can only be obtained by positive measures aimed at a decision, never by simply waiting on events. In short, even in the defense, a major stake alone can bring a major gain." NVA had major stake (conquest of Indochina); US "simply waiting on events." (ll. ~430-450)

#### Mass, Economy of Force, and Maneuver (Ch. 11)
- FM 100-5 19 Feb 1962 verbatim definitions as in cache. Clausewitz COG detailed (Alexander et al. army; capital; protector army; community of interest; leaders/public opinion). "Not by taking things the easy way ... but by constantly seeking out the center of his power, by daring all to win all, will one really defeat the enemy." (ll. ~650-740)
- US COG error: "by seeing the Viet Cong as a separate entity rather than as an instrument of North Vietnam, we chose a center of gravity which in fact did not exist. The proof ... was demonstrated during Tet-68, when, even though they were virtually destroyed, the war continued unabated." Global: mass vs. economy dilemma (NATO/elsewhere); Wheeler 1968 request genesis "an attempt by the Joint Chiefs of Staff to precipitate a reserve call-up and to obtain additional manpower to meet worldwide contingencies" (Schandler); "The troop request ... was designed to meet both global and local interests." (ll. ~740-840)
- NVA pre-US: 1964 tactical offensive "to gain a spectacular victory ... by slicing South Vietnam in half on the line from Pleiku through An Khe to Qui Nhon. Between October 1965 and April of 1966 he planned to commit three full North Vietnamese divisions ... jumping off from secure bases in Cambodia and closely supported by Viet Cong units ... Strong holding attacks in the vicinity of Saigon and against populated areas near the Demilitarized Zone would tie down reserves." Defeated Ia Drang (1st Cav). (ll. ~880-910)
- 1968: "Using their regular troops on the periphery to distract and overextend American forces (such as the Khe Sanh near the DMZ), they committed their economy of force units—the Viet Cong guerrillas—to attack the population centers." "Strategically, by attacking everywhere Giap had superior strength nowhere. Simply put, he failed to mass his forces, a strategic error which he committed knowingly, but an error nonetheless. Military victories are not won by violating military principles." (Palmer). ~half strength expended, 100k+ losses; tactical fail, strategic success vs. US alliance/will. (ll. ~930-950)
- 1972 Eastertide: "Leaving two divisions in Laos and one as a strategic reserve, North Vietnam committed some 12 divisions—a total of about 150,000 men—to the attack on South Vietnam. Supported by tanks, heavy artillery and mobile antiaircraft units, they had some initial success." "The bastards have never been bombed like they’re going to be bombed this time." (Nixon). By July reverted defensive; >100k deaths. (ll. ~960-970)
- 1975: "Their spring offensive of 1975 made classic use of the principles of Mass, Economy of Force, and Maneuver." Interior lines (Trail faster than ARVN reaction); "economy of force guerrilla units (by now composed mostly of North Vietnamese regulars) kept the South Vietnamese Army deployed in a counter-guerrilla mode with little ability to mass." "the army II Corps, ... covering a distance of 900 kms ... had been ordered to report in Bien Hoa and Ba Ria in 18 days. ... all 2,000 vehicles of the army corps [had] to cross six big rivers while having to fight the enemy en route." "cadres of the front staff had to admit somewhat ruefully that they could not draw maps quickly enough to catch up with the advance of our forces." Final Saigon: "Comrade Le Duc Tho told the corps commanders ... The enemy has 5 divisions against our 15, excluding the strategic reserve forces. Thus, we cannot fail to win victory." IV Corps after Xuan Loc closed east; II Corps southeast (Long Thanh etc.); 5th/8th div southwest (My Tho); I/III northwest/north (Dong Du, Cu Chi etc.). Saigon surrendered 1130 hrs 30 Apr 1975. (ll. ~980-1100)
- US: "Our use of these principles enabled us to frustrate the North Vietnamese offensives of 1965, 1968, and 1972." But "our strategic failure to apply the principles of The Objective and The Offensive caused us to fritter away our advantage in Mass, Economy of Force, and Maneuver in tactical operations — rather than apply them to a strategic purpose." (ll. ~1110)
- ARVN weakness: "Stationed in their home areas, with their families with them on their battle positions, they had great stability for counter-guerrilla operations ... The North Vietnamese economy of force guerrilla screen kept them deployed countrywide in this counter-guerrilla mode. ... it proved impossible to mass the South Vietnamese Army to meet the North Vietnamese attack. It was this faulty disposition rather than lack of fighting spirit that led to their defeat. This was proven at Xuan Loc, where the 18th ARVN division (whose families had moved to safety) fought heroically and virtually destroyed three North Vietnamese divisions." "the need for flexibility in both thought and action is perhaps the most critical." (Clausewitz: war "directed at an animate object that reacts.") (ll. ~1120-1160)

#### Unity of Command (Ch. 12)
- FM verbatim (ll. ~1210). Clausewitz on cabinet integration or combined leadership. (ll. ~1230)
- NVA: Giap "a member of the ruling Politburo, a Deputy Premier of the North Vietnamese Government, the Minister of Defense and Commander in Chief of the People’s Army of Vietnam." 1975 Dung: "From the very first page he details how the Political Bureau and the Central Military Party Committee worked together to formulate the strategic combat plan and to direct the actual combat operations." Le Duc Tho April 1975 at Dung HQ with "Hanoi’s detailed plan for the final assault on Saigon"; "the Politburo and Uncle Ton told him that he would not be allowed to return if he was not successful in his mission." "This Politburo decision went so far as to establish the specific combat commands for the campaign and name the field commanders." Hoopes: "For the enemy the war remained fundamentally ... a seamless web of political-military-psychological factors to be manipulated by a highly centralized command authority that never took its eye off the political goal of ultimate control in the South." (ll. ~1240-1270)
- US: President nominal CINC; MacArthur 1932 quote on head of state responsibility (ll. ~1280). "A certain grasp of military affairs is vital for those in charge of general policy." No serving officers in cabinet; coordination via SecWar/Navy then DoD/JCS (1947 success for interservice but political linkage deficient). (ll. ~1310-1360)
- NSC: Hoopes "President Kennedy ... scrapped the entire structure of the NSC." Relied on "irregular meetings at the White House attended by the President, Rusk, McNamara, and Bundy, augmented from time to time by the Chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff..." Johnson "inherited this somewhat amorphous set of arrangements." "I believe the decisions and actions that marked our large-scale military entry into the Vietnam War in early 1965 reflected the piecemeal consideration of interrelated issues, and that this was the natural consequence of a fragmented NSC and a general inattention to long-range policy planning." (ll. ~1420-1440)
- Komer: "no Vietnam 'high command' emerged to coordinate all aspects of Washington war management"; "The bureaucratic fact is that below Presidential level everybody and nobody was responsible ... not a single senior level official above the rank of officer director or colonel in any U.S. agency dealt full-time with Vietnam before 1969." "business as usual" with "peacetime planning, programming, financial, resource allocation and distribution procedures." (ll. ~1460-1480)
- Theater command: "the so-called strategic headquarters, Pacific Command, was located in Honolulu over 5,000 miles away." Westmoreland: "MACV functioned not directly under the Joint Chiefs of Staff in Washington but through CINCPAC. ... The White House seldom dealt directly with me but through the Joint Chiefs. ... not I but [Admiral U. S. Grant Sharp, Commander in Chief Pacific Command] was the theater commander in the sense that General Eisenhower, for example, was the theater commander in World War II. My responsibilities and prerogatives were basically confined within the borders of South Vietnam." Hoopes: "three separate or only loosely related struggles" (conventional, pacification, remote air war). No forward strategic HQ equivalent to MacArthur. (ll. ~1490-1520)
- Westmoreland envisioned "Southeast Asia Command" under his command in Saigon; analysis suggests better outside immediate zone (e.g. Philippines) to avoid internal affairs and gain theater perspective (Laos/Cambodia/Thailand). (ll. ~1520-1550)
- "Unity of command obtains unity of effort by the coordinated action of all forces toward a common goal." "Without a common goal it is impossible to have coordinated action or to obtain either unity of effort or unity of command. Our own definition predicted the outcome. Without Unity of Command we could never have 'decisive application of full combat power.'" (ll. ~1560)

#### Security and Surprise (Ch. 13)
- FM verbatim definitions. "Clausewitz, in fact, links them together and subsumes security as an element of surprise." Erfurth ties surprise to Economy of Force/Mass/Maneuver/Unity. (ll. ~1610-1660)
- "Basically surprise is a tactical device. It is very rare ... the one state surprises another, either by an attack or by preparations for war." Buildup time inhibits strategic surprise. (ll. ~1660-1690)
- Vietnam instances (all psychological/will, enemy knew physical capabilities): US ground intervention 1965; NVA Tet 1968; US Christmas bombing 1972. (ll. ~1710)
- 1965: "Policy makers in Hanoi were ... dead wrong in believing Washington would neither carry the air war into North Vietnam nor inject ground forces into the South." Johnson Aug 1964 public: "would not ... commit[] American boys to fighting a war that I think ought to be fought by the boys of Asia." "Consternation and disbelief were their initial reactions." "Strategic surprise had been achieved and in the 1965 Battle of the Ia Drang the North Vietnamese invasion had been thrown off the track." (ll. ~1720-1750)
- Tet 1968: "Giap’s Tet Offensive ... gained complete surprise." "Why did commanders ignore the ample evidence available to them? The answer is more psychological than military, more emotional than professional. They were victims of their own sturdy optimism and of General Giap’s shrewd staging of his deception campaign. ... It was inconceivable that the battered foe could bounce off the mat..." US intel officer: "If we’d gotten the whole battle plan, it wouldn’t have been credible to us." Tactical fail but "General Giap’s offensive drove a wedge between optimistic public pronouncements and the realities as seen on American television. ... our resolve obviously wavered, we began to fold our hands and started looking for a way out of the war." (ll. ~1760-1800)
- 1972 Christmas bombing: after May resumption and Oct halt (NVA interpreted as weakness). Nixon 9 May memo: "We have the power to destroy [North Vietnamese] war-making capacity. The only question is whether we have the will to use that power. What distinguishes me from Johnson is that I have the will in spades." "For the first time, B-52s were used in large numbers to bring the full weight of airpower to bear. ... concentrated use of all forms of airpower to strike at the vital power centers, causing maximum disruption in the economic, military, and political life of the country. ... The 11-day campaign ... It was apparent that airpower was the decisive factor leading to the peace agreement of 15 January 1973." (ll. ~1810-1840)
- Paradox: "the United States was able to achieve strategic surprise because of a lack of strategic security. In both instances the North Vietnamese were misled by the wealth of information they had available on American public and Congressional opinion." (ll. ~1850)

#### Simplicity (Ch. 14)
- FM: "Simplicity contributes to successful operations. Direct, simple plans and clear, concise orders minimize misunderstanding and confusion. If other factors are equal, the simplest plan is preferred." "To a large degree this last principle of war is the sum of all the others." (ll. ~1890)
- NVA simplicity in objective (conquest SV), unity (Politburo + Central Mil Party Comm), security. 
- US complexity: "conflicting and sometimes contradictory objectives of resisting aggression and counterinsurgency"; "unwittingly confused the tactical offensive for the strategic offensive and conducted the war on the strategic defensive in pursuit of a negative aim—counterinsurgency"; "The simplicity of North Vietnamese Unity of Command compared to the complex and convoluted system through which the United States prosecuted the war almost speaks for itself." "At the national level their Politburo and Central Military Party Committee worked in close coordination ... In Washington there was no such unity of effort. ... Strategic direction was fragmented among Washington, Honolulu and Saigon. Within Vietnam itself the command structure was convoluted and confused with overlapping authority and responsibility diffused among [MACV, ARVN JGS, USARV, FWMF]." "North Vietnamese Security was so pervasive that it even concealed the true nature of the war to the very end." (ll. ~1930-1960)
- Public opinion: "Our inability to explain in clear and understandable language what we were about in Vietnam undercut American support for the war." 
- Lifestyle contrast: "sitting in Saigon was like sitting inside the folded petals of a poisonous flower" (Herr); "Dial Soapers in Saigon" vs "grungy men in the jungle"; "base camp mentality." Heiser: "There is a need to establish standards of living for troops early in a campaign ... In the absence of such criteria, every unit will establish its own standards, usually high; and constantly strive to upgrade them. This places excessive demands on an already busy logistic system." Clausewitz: "The whole of military activity must ... relate directly or indirectly to [combat operations]. The end for which a soldier is recruited, clothed, armed, and trained, the whole object of his sleeping, eating, drinking, and marching is simply that he should fight at the right place and the right time." (ll. ~1980-2070)

## Historiography Note

Summers writes as both participant (Vietnam combat, negotiations) and institutional critic/educator. The work is deliberately narrow: "not a detailed account of day-to-day tactical operations or an examination of the many controversies of the war" but a focus on the application of military science/strategy. Its Clausewitzian lens and memorable formulation made it the single most influential U.S. military critique of the war inside the armed forces. It is frequently paired with (and sometimes opposed to) the "COIN school" represented by Krepinevich and Nagl. Later critics note it underplays the political constraints in Washington, the corruption and legitimacy deficits of the GVN, and the genuine popular base of the southern insurgency early on; its strength is the rigorous strategic-level diagnosis using the classic canon. Presumptive authority on the question of "what kind of war was this and did we fight the war we had?"

**Claim R04-13: Principles of War — full strategic/tactical dimensions (Appendix verbatim framework)**  
**Grounding (selected):** "The principles of war... are neither intended nor designed to be prescriptive; the principles of war, if understood and applied properly, should stimulate thought and enhance flexibility of action." US list: Objective, Offensive, Mass, Economy of Force, Maneuver, Unity of Command, Security, Surprise, Simplicity (from 1921/Fuller roots).  
**Objective (strategic):** "the strategic military objective of a nation at war must be to apply whatever degree of force is necessary to allow attainment of the political purpose or aim for which the war is being fought."  
**Offensive:** "the side that retains the initiative through offensive action forces the foe to react rather than act." Active defense required.  
**Mass/Economy of Force/Maneuver:** "out of [the enemy’s dominant] characteristics a certain center of gravity develops, the hub of all power and movement, on which everything depends." US faces bipolar + surrogates; geography (insular power needs lift); POMCUS flexibility tradeoffs.  
**Unity of Command:** "For every objective, there should be unity of effort under one responsible commander." Peacetime prep vs. war conduct diverge in limited war.  
**Security/Surprise:** "Security enhances freedom of action..." "To a large degree, the principle of surprise is the reciprocal of the principle of security." Open society challenges; presidential decisions provide volatility advantage.  
**Simplicity:** "In both the strategic and tactical dimension, guidance, plans, and orders should be as simple and direct as the attainment of the objective will allow." "simple and direct plans and orders cannot compensate for ambiguous and cloudy objectives." (Full Appendix ~1020-1380 cache)

## Volume Synthesis Note (updated with Range 04)

Summers recovers the "missing link" between political ends (why) and material means (what) by restoring the military strategist’s role in determining "how." The decisive error was treating a Clausewitzian war between states—whose center of gravity was the North Vietnamese armed forces and political will—as a Maoist people’s war whose solution was population-centric counterinsurgency inside South Vietnam. Once the nature of the war was misdiagnosed, every subsequent principle of war was applied to the wrong problem. 

Range 04 deepens this: coalition warfare exposed the fatal overreach (repel aggression + nation-build, creating ARVN dependence unlike Korea); Epilogue codifies enduring lessons via principles-as-interrogatories, insistence on clear attainable political objectives/definition of victory before commitment, recognition that military forces are a "battle-ax" (combat-optimized) not a nation-builder, and the requirement for honest public framing of interests (avoiding credibility gap). The Appendix supplies the canonical strategic/tactical elaboration of each principle as a non-prescriptive judgment framework. The book supplied the intellectual framework that helped the U.S. Army re-professionalize around strategy in the 1980s and remains the touchstone for any serious analysis of the strategic level of the Vietnam War. Quintessential lesson: "we must once again become masters of the profession of arms."
