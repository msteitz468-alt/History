# Hartz, *The Liberal Tradition in America* (1955)

Louis Hartz's classic single-thesis interpretation of American political
thought: because America was "born equal" — settled as a **fragment of
European liberalism without a feudal past** — it developed an irrational,
unanimous Lockean consensus rather than a genuine ideological spectrum. No
feudalism means no genuine conservatism (no Maistre) and no genuine
socialism (no Marx): both depend on the experience of class and revolution
that America skipped. The book traces the consequences from the Revolution
("a revolution without a social revolution") through Whiggery's failures,
the South's doomed "reactionary Enlightenment," the Horatio Alger era,
Progressivism and the failure of American socialism, to the New Deal and
Cold War messianism.

## Key Claims by Section

**A (Ch. 1–3).** The fragment/"born equal" thesis; "no Maistre, no Marx";
socialism as ideological (feudal-derived), not economic; "irrational
Lockeanism" and the danger of *unanimity* (not majority); single-factor
method defended, Turner and capitalist-growth theories subsumed; 1776 as a
revolution without a social revolution (no ancien régime, sober and
non-messianic, anti-power rather than power-seizing, "mopping up" of
feudal relics); Federalist Hobbesianism as phantom; the Constitution
"right only because their premises were wrong."

**B (Ch. 4–7).** Why American Whiggery failed: no aristocracy to fight or
ally with, no mob to denounce; the 1840 log-cabin "discovery of America"
and the law of Whig compensation; the American democrat as
Hercules-with-the-brain-of-a-Hamlet hybrid; Bank War theory vs. practice
(Bray Hammond); the Reactionary Enlightenment — Calhoun as "a profoundly
disintegrated political theorist," Fitzhugh's feudal socialism and its
four internal fractures, the Holmes/Hughes positivist wing; the Civil War
as "French Revolution in reverse" that is not one; Lincoln Americanizing
Whiggery; total postwar oblivion of Southern conservatism.

**C (Ch. 8–11).** "Americanism" as nationalized Locke — charm (Alger) and
terror (red scares); Sumner vs. the American Legion; conformity as "a
silent quality in the national atmosphere"; the Sombart question answered
structurally; Progressives trapped (the trust as psychological creation);
Progressive and Marxist historiography symmetrically blind (Beard;
Lerner's Supreme Court); the New Deal as submerged liberalism and the
"Roosevelt in Europe" counterfactual; the Depression as negative proof
against materialist explanation; Wilsonian messianism as isolationism's
polar counterpart; Palmer/McCarthy vs. Wilson/Willkie "within the single
American mind"; closing question — can a people born equal understand
peoples that must become so, or itself?

## Volume Synthesis Note

*The Liberal Tradition in America* is a single sustained comparative
argument rather than a narrative history: every chapter re-runs the same
experiment — set an American figure or movement beside its nearest
European analogue and show that the American case lacks the feudal
substrate that gives the European its meaning. The book's genius and its
vulnerability are the same thing: the one factor (absent feudalism →
unconscious Lockean unanimity) explains Whig failure, Jacksonian hybridity,
Southern intellectual fraudulence, socialist stillbirth, Progressive
shallowness, New Deal pragmatism, and Cold War hysteria alike.

For this wiki the book does three jobs. First, it anchors the
mid-twentieth-century historiographical layer: with the Progressive school
already represented as Hartz's foil, [[consensus-history]] now has its
most theoretical text on file, and the standing controversy
[[why-no-socialism-in-america]] has its primary anchor. Second, it
supplies the wiki's first sustained treatments of Jacksonian democracy and
antebellum Southern political thought ([[jackson-andrew]],
[[calhoun-john-c]], [[fitzhugh-george]]) — flagged on those pages as
Hartz-derived pending dedicated sources. Third, it completes a
1940s–50s cluster of rival diagnoses of American political drift already
ingested: Flynn's [[as-we-go-marching-thesis]] (America structurally
drifting toward fascism), Hayek's [[road-to-serfdom-thesis]] (collectivist
planning as the road to serfdom), and Hartz (nothing fundamental can
drift at all — the danger is immovable unanimity, not movement). The
three-way tension is itself a subject: all respond to the New Deal state,
and each reads McCarthy-era conformity differently.

Cross-volume tensions to watch: Genovese's paternalism thesis (if
ingested) directly contests Hartz's "fraud" verdict on the Old South;
Whitman's *Hitler's American Model* (ingested) complicates the claim that
American illiberalism was mere phantom; and the republican-synthesis
literature would contest the Lockean reading of the founding.
