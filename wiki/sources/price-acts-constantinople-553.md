# The Acts of the Council of Constantinople of 553

The documents of the **Second Council of Constantinople (553)** — the **Fifth Ecumenical Council** —
convened by **[[justinian|Justinian]]** to resolve the **[[three-chapters-controversy|Three Chapters
controversy]]**. The tenth and last source of this Phase-5 batch, substituting for the corrupt TTH 10.

## The Council and the Three Chapters

By the "**Three Chapters**" was meant not a set of decrees but **three targets**: the person and
writings of **Theodore of Mopsuestia** (d. 428), certain anti-Cyrilline writings of **Theodoret of
Cyrrhus** (d. c. 466), and the **Letter of Ibas of Edessa to Mari the Persian**. All three were
associated with the **Antiochene/"Nestorian"** theological tradition, yet two of the authors had been
accepted at **[[council-of-chalcedon-451|Chalcedon (451)]]**. **Justinian's** aim was to **conciliate
the Miaphysites** by demonstrating that the imperial church repudiated this Nestorian-leaning legacy —
condemning the Three Chapters while claiming to uphold Chalcedon. The council, dominated by the emperor,
duly **anathematized** the Three Chapters (by the doubtful device of posthumous condemnation) and also
condemned **Origen**.

## Vigilius and the Western Schism

**Pope Vigilius**, present in Constantinople, **resisted** — refusing to attend the council and issuing
his own *Constitutum* — but was coerced and ultimately **capitulated**, accepting the condemnation. His
surrender split the Latin Church: the bishops of **Africa**, **Aquileia** and **Istria** broke
communion with Rome over the betrayal of Chalcedon, beginning the **"Schism of the Three Chapters"** that
divided northern Italy for a century and a half.

## Significance and Debate

The council is, as Price notes, "by far the most problematic" of the early councils. The older verdict —
that it **narrowed and distorted Chalcedon**, **failed** to win back the Miaphysites, **perpetuated the
breach** with the Church of Persia (which revered Theodore), and **damaged East–West relations** — is
weighed against **Price's revisionist defence** of its theology as opportune and constructive
("**Neo-Chalcedonian**" — reading Chalcedon through Cyril of Alexandria). Either way it is a pivotal
moment in the long failure of the empire to heal the Chalcedonian schism, and a key episode in the
**caesaropapism** of Justinian's reign.
