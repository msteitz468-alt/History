# Strategy: The Logic of War and Peace (Edward N. Luttwak, rev. & enl. ed. 2002)

Revised and enlarged edition of Luttwak's 1987 work (original Belknap/Harvard). A foundational theoretical text in strategic studies that uncovers the "paradoxical logic" pervading strategy in war and peace. The logic is not the straightforward ends-means rationality of linear logic (common sense in non-conflictual domains) but one in which opposites interact, direct approaches often fail or reverse, and success can breed failure.

The text is organized in three parts:

- **Part I: The Logic of Strategy** — Core exposition of paradoxical logic (e.g., "if you want peace, prepare war" is not banality but reversal; bad roads can be good precisely because they are bad and less defended; efficiency is often self-defeating because the enemy reacts and anticipates).
- **Part II: The Levels of Strategy** — Vertical dimension: technical, tactical, operational, theater strategy (offense/defense, interdiction, surprise), and "nonstrategies" (naval, air, nuclear as domains where the logic applies differently or fails to cohere).
- **Part III: Outcomes — Grand Strategy** — Horizontal + vertical confluence at the highest level; armed suasion (deterrence and compellence effects of military posture even without use); harmony/disharmony between levels and instruments; whether strategy can be "useful."

Appendices include definitions of strategy, analysis of the 1991 Gulf War air campaign, and "Instant Thunder."

## Key Contributions (Grounded in Text)
- Strategy is pervaded by paradox because it is relational and adversarial: every action elicits reaction that can reverse intent.
- The "culminating point of success": beyond a point, further effort yields diminishing or negative returns.
- Levels of strategy do not automatically harmonize; what works at one level (e.g., tactical success) can undermine theater or grand strategy.
- Armed suasion: military strength shapes adversary behavior through threat of use, often more effectively when limited or latent; nuclear deterrence as extreme case of paradox (ready to use but must never use to gain benefit).
- Historical illustrations include Pearl Harbor as suasion failure, nuclear era small wars as circumvention of great-power dissuasion, Roman and other examples of reversal.

The work emphasizes that linear, "more is better" or direct approaches routinely fail in strategy, while seemingly inefficient, paradoxical, or limited actions can succeed.

## Historiography Note
Luttwak's work is widely cited in strategic studies but has been critiqued for over-generalizing paradox or underplaying cultural/historical specificity (cf. debates in Cambridge History of Strategy and practice-focused volumes). It remains a powerful corrective to overly rationalistic or "scientific" models of strategy. The revised edition incorporates post-Cold War reflections, including precision air power and "post-heroic" war (casualty aversion).

**Range summary and scope note**
