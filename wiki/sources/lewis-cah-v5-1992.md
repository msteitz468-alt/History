# CAH Vol. V — The Fifth Century B.C.

Second edition, edited by **D. M. Lewis, John Boardman, J. K. Davies and M. Ostwald**
(Cambridge University Press, 1992; first edition titled simply *Athens*). The volume
covers the Greek fifth century from the aftermath of the Persian Wars (479 B.C.) to the
fall of Athens at the end of the Peloponnesian War (404 B.C.) and its immediate
epilogue. Its organizing polarity is the **divergence of the victorious allies of
480–479** into two incompatible power systems: the **maritime, democratic, tribute-fed
Athenian empire** that grew out of the [[delian-league|Delian League]], and the
**land-based, oligarchic [[peloponnesian-league|Spartan alliance]]** — a polarity whose
working-out is the Peloponnesian War. Woven through the political narrative is the
volume's second great theme: the **first Classic age of European civilization** — the
art, architecture, drama, religion, philosophy and social transformation of fifth-century
Athens, treated not as a separate "cultural" appendix but in its historical, economic and
imperial context.

## Volume Synthesis Note

CAH V is the most **deliberately narrowed** of the Cambridge Ancient History volumes — it
"hardly strays beyond Greece" — and that narrowing is itself its central argument: for the
first and last time before Rome, the editors say, "great political and military power and
cultural importance are located in the same place," and described by historians of genius. The
volume's organizing structure is therefore a **double helix**: the political story of the
**divergence of the victors of 480–479** into two incompatible systems, twined around the
cultural story of the **first Classic age of European civilization**. The wiki's 33 new pages
and 14 enriched ones fall into the same two strands.

The **political spine** runs from the [[delian-league|Delian League]] (478/7) to the
[[fall-of-athens-404bce|fall of Athens]] (404). Its motor is the transformation Rhodes traces
"not necessarily of set purpose": a free anti-Persian alliance becoming, stage by stage
(Naxos, Thasos, the [[peace-of-callias-449bce|Peace of Callias]], the Coinage Decree), the
**Athenian empire** — the economic engine of the radical [[athenian-democracy|democracy]] that
[[ephialtes-reform-462bce|Ephialtes]] and [[pericles|Pericles]] built, paying the jurors, the
rowers, and the [[parthenon|Parthenon]] from allied tribute. The same growth is, in
[[thucydides|Thucydides']] analysis, the **"truest cause"** of the
[[peloponnesian-war|Peloponnesian War]]: Spartan fear of a power that could not stop growing,
and *ananke* (necessity) driving both sides. The war's three phases — the
[[archidamian-war|Archidamian War]] and the [[plague-of-athens-430bce|plague]], the
[[sicilian-expedition|Sicilian Expedition]], and the Persian-funded [[fall-of-athens-404bce|
Ionian War]] — work out the lesson Davies stated abstractly at the volume's start: the
[[city-state|polis]] could dominate the sea but could not sustain a stable land hegemony, and
the maritime empire and the democracy fell together.

The **cultural spine** is the volume's distinctive editorial choice — to treat art, drama,
religion, philosophy and society **in their historical and imperial context** rather than as a
separate appendix (Ch. 8a–8h). Hence the wiki now holds [[classical-greek-art|the Severe and
High Classical styles]] read against the Persian-War moral climate and the Periclean program;
[[athenian-drama|tragedy and comedy]] as the fused "religion and literature" of the civic
Dionysia, financed by the same liturgy system as the warships; the [[sophists|sophistic
enlightenment]] and the *nomos–physis* antithesis that the foreigners brought to the imperial
city; and the [[panhellenic-games|panhellenic festivals]] and [[polis-religion|civic cult]]
that bound the wider Greek world. The two strands meet in the
[[siege-of-melos-416bce|Melian Dialogue]] and the Funeral Oration — the empire's self-image and
its logic of power side by side.

The volume's **methodological lesson** is everywhere and is now a wiki page in its own right
([[classical-greek-historiography]]): the century is brightly lit where Thucydides is present
and dim where he is not (the [[peace-of-callias-449bce|Pentekontaetia]] reconstructed from the
tribute quota-lists; Book VIII unfinished; the last seven years left to Xenophon and Diodorus).
The editors deliberately let the source-base show — the **Athenocentric lens** treated as
itself a historical fact, the suspicion that Thucydides "played down what did not happen" and
saw allied loyalty through the eyes of his own class.

Two **cross-volume joins** are worth tracking. First, the volume **picks up exactly** where
[[boardman-cah-v4-1988|CAH IV]] left off (Salamis, Plataea), and its western chapter (Asheri,
Ch. 7) continues CAH IV's Sicily seamlessly — the fall of the [[western-greek-tyranny|
Deinomenid tyrannies]], [[ducetius|Ducetius']] Sicel revolt, and the rise of the democratic
[[syracuse|Syracuse]] that would destroy the [[sicilian-expedition|Athenian expedition]],
binding the Greek east and west into one story. Second, the volume **hands forward** to CAH VI:
it stops in 404 with Athens fallen but not destroyed, the [[fall-of-athens-404bce|Spartan
hegemony]] just beginning, and the fourth century — "a weakened Athens and a renascent Persia"
— already visible in its last decade.
