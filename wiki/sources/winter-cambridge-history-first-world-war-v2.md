# The Cambridge History of the First World War, Vol. II: The State (2014)

The second volume treats the war "from a predominantly political angle" — the **state at
war** and its transformation under the strain of total mobilization.

## What it contributes

- **[[war-economy]]** — the process page: the command war economy and the dramatic expansion
  of state power (1914–18).
- Enriches **[[total-war]]** (the state/economic dimension) and **[[first-world-war-1914]]**.

## Argument

Winter's contributors show that every belligerent state — whatever its political form — was
forced into an unprecedented assumption of control over economy and society, and was
"deformed" by the experience. Barry Supple's chapter on war economies documents the core
fact: the state's share of GDP leapt from ~10% to ~50% in France and Germany as "the market
became the enemy of national survival" and private capitalism was subsumed into state-directed
mobilization. The war thus stands as a turning point in the growth of the modern state — even
though much of the wartime apparatus was dismantled after 1918, to return in the 1930s and
the Second World War.
