# Early Civilizations of the Old World: The Formative Histories of Egypt, the Levant, Mesopotamia, India and China

**Author:** Charles Keith Maisels. Routledge, 1999 (hardback); 2001 paperback. ~400+ pages. Based on University of Bristol lectures on Old World Archaeology.

## Book Note

Maisels charts the parallel but distinctive paths by which hunter-gatherers became farmers (Neolithic), villagers developed complex divisions of labour (Chalcolithic), and cities, states, writing, and institutional religion emerged (Bronze Age formative) in the four seminal areas of the Old World: Egypt, the Levant/Mesopotamia, the Greater Indus (Harappan/Sarasvati), and China (Zhongguo / the Central Kingdom).

The book is structured as a systematic test of V. Gordon Childe’s 1950 “urban revolution” checklist (ten criteria) against the archaeological record of each region, combined with rich settlement, subsistence, and social-order detail. Each major chapter ends with an explicit “Childe’s checklist” evaluation. The conclusion synthesizes the results, highlights the Indus as a stateless oecumene, and proposes a more branched model of social evolution than the classic band–tribe–chiefdom–state sequence.

This is a key structural baseline source for the wiki’s treatment of early state formation, urbanism, and cross-cultural comparison in the Neolithic–Early Bronze Age periods. It directly complements (and predates) Trigger (2003) and Yoffee (2005).

## Volume Synthesis Note

**Core contribution (from full 6-range Deployed Subagent extraction + main reconciliation, 2026-06-26):** Maisels (1999) supplies the most explicit, side-by-side testing of V. Gordon Childe’s urban revolution checklist (expanded here to 12 points) against the four primary Old World pristine civilizations — Egypt (Naqada → Old Kingdom territorial state), the Levant/Mesopotamia (Halaf/Samarra/Ubaid village networks → Sumerian city-states with oikos households), the Indus/Sarasvati/Harappan (Mehrgarh Neolithic → Mature oecumene), and China (Yangshao/Longshan Chalcolithic → Three Dynasties territorial states via conical clans). The book grounds each case in detailed settlement, subsistence, ecological, and social-order sequences and concludes each chapter with an explicit Childe checklist scorecard.

**Key regularities across cases (Table 6.1 synthesis):** Cities or large integrative nodes, full-time specialists (craft, transport, officials, ritual), surplus concentration (by different mechanisms), monumental public buildings (variable intensity), writing/numerical notation (state-serving or divinatory), exact/predictive sciences, representative art, and community solidarity expressed through temples/shrines or shared ideology. All four meet the core structural attributes of early civilization to a striking degree.

**Major divergences and exceptions:**
- **Indus (Harappan/Sarasvati) is the decisive anomaly**: Meets criteria for cities, specialists, and long-distance trade, but scores 0 on ruling class/functionaries, lacks evidence for systematic surplus extraction by a central authority, monumental power architecture (palaces/temples as engines of domination), or offensive military apparatus. Integration occurred through a “distributed social network” / “equivalence economy” / “mutuality society” based on occupational specialization of lineages/clans and organic solidarity. No dominating centre; coordination rather than imposition; villages not oppressed by towns. Devolved (rather than collapsed) when the Sarasvati heartland dried, with population dispersal and reconfiguration into proto-caste (jati) organization. Unique among known complex societies in sustaining civilizational scale without state stratification.
- Egypt produced the world’s first territorial state, enabled by Nile linearity (inundation agriculture, boat transport, easy military projection). Village clusters + apical lineages coalesced; the pharaoh embodied the transcendental state as divine validator. Cities were secondary political/ritual centres.
- Mesopotamia: oikos (stratified, internally specialized households) + temple/palace produced city-states retaining significant civic power (assemblies of household heads alongside kingship). High managerialism and organic solidarity; “community state” character.
- China: Genealogical/clan (conical clan) principle dominant. Apical lineages monopolized ritual and surplus, leading to territorial states (guo as networks of walled yi towns) rather than city-states. Strong lineage hierarchy continuity from Longshan Chalcolithic through Western Zhou. Foreign (“overseas”) trade effectively absent in formative periods.

**Theoretical revisions:**
- Rejects unilinear Service band–tribe–chiefdom–state. Proposes branching evolution rooted in Neolithic settlement principles (territorial village solidarity vs. genealogical/lineage principle) with decisive Chalcolithic divergence into autocratic (elite-driven specialization) vs. interdependent (economic reciprocity) paths.
- Conical clan mechanism precisely explains how status becomes class power and generates the state (China exemplar).
- Indus demonstrates that state-ordering is not a necessary condition for urbanism, writing, specialization, or large-scale integration.
- Childe’s criteria remain a powerful comparative grid but must be scored case-by-case; they illuminate both pan-Old-World regularities and historically particular political economies.

This source provides the archaeological grounding and comparative scorecard that anchor the wiki’s [[concepts/urban-revolution]], [[concepts/early-civilization]], [[concepts/city-state]], [[concepts/territorial-state]], [[concepts/heterarchy]], [[concepts/state-formation]], the comparisons section, and period pages for Neolithic–Early Bronze Age in the four regions. It directly complements Trigger (2003) and Yoffee (2005).

## Claims Extracted from Range 05 (China: Chapter 5 "THE CENTRAL KINGDOM, ZHONG-GUO"; cache lines ~10051–12950)

Range 05 exclusive extraction. All claims grounded in verbatim quotes from cache file only. Links use only pre-established names.

**Chinese chronological sequences and Three Dynasties (Xia/Shang/Zhou):**
- State formation ~2000 BC with Xia (or possibly later Shang); traditional Xia dates 2205–1760 BC. "In the traditional scheme, the Xia state (traditional dates 2205–1760 BC) was supplanted by the Shang in 1760 BC when Tang, leader of the Shang, defeated the infamous King Jie of the Xia. Shang was conquered by the Zhou under King Wu around 1050 BC (1122 traditional)." (lines ~219-222)
- Archaeologically adjusted: "Shang hegemony (conquest) is now dated from the first part of the sixteenth century BC: at 1575 by Nivison... and at 1554 BC by Pankenier". Zhou conquest ~1040 BC (Nivison). "The Zhou capital moved in 770 BC east to Luoyang from Xi’an and the Eastern Zhou commenced". (lines ~223-228)
- Long development in Longshan Chalcolithic before states. "complex and stratified society certainly had a long development in the Longshan Chalcolithic cultures of the Dawenkou (East Coast) ceramic tradition. Human sacrifice, ritual bronzes and scapulimancy are all present there". (lines ~215-217)
- Population notes for early states: Xia ~13.55 million (21st c. BC traditional computation); Zhou 11th c. BC similar; later growth to 40M Warring States. (lines ~6-10)

**Neolithic and Chalcolithic clusters (Yangshao, Dawenkou/Longshan, Hongshan, Liangzhu, Hemudu):**
- Yangshao (Middle Neolithic, core ~5000–3000 BC, millet-based): "The most widespread and best known of the Neolithic cultures after 5000 BC is the Yangshao of the middle Yellow River valley". Villages 50-100 houses, 200-400 people; Banpo type early; collective tombs, evidence of ranking with women/girls higher prestige in some data (Yuanjunmiao cemetery). "It is the strong form of matriliny that Zhang sees in the mortuary data". (lines ~550-590, ~730-860)
- Dawenkou/Longshan: Coastal origin of late Neolithic/Chalcolithic successor; "the earliest coherent script, and one clearly related to subsequent scripts, notably Shang, is found as graphs etched into the sides of Dawenkou black pottery dating to the second half of the third millennium BC". Elites emerge via burial differentiation and drinking rituals foundational to ancestor rites. (lines ~890-920)
- Longshan Chalcolithic (~2500–1700 BC): "Longshan is a generic term covering a number of sub-phases... seven distinct regional variants... walled sites with rammed earth (hangtu)... pre-urban chiefdom stage, with towns but not cities". "The Chalcolithic in China has the same key attributes as in the other areas examined. It is characterized by towns and crafts, in contrast to the cities and states of the Bronze Age." (lines ~1149-1440 approx)
- Hongshan (NE, Liaohe): Advanced, "bronze-casting, pottery-making and jade carving…are the three major industrial accomplishments... large group of people was already engaged independently in handicraft industries... became the elite of society." Goddess temple, stratified cairns. Taotie motifs pre-Shang. (lines ~1485-1530)
- Liangzhu (SE Chalcolithic, ~3500–2500 BC): "remarkable for... the quality and quantity of jades; their ritual significance; clear evidence of pronounced social stratification; the profligate consumption of labour; extensive human sacrifice; and the iconography of power." 90% of cong/bi from here; earliest taotie. (lines ~970-1020)
- Hemudu (rice Neolithic, ~5000–3400 BC): Pile houses with mortice/tenon; rice cultivation with bone scapula spades. (lines ~1025-1080)

**Clanship and the formation of the territorial state:**
- Conical clan structure (after Kirchhoff): "A clan consists of a number of lineages all supposedly descended from a common ancestor, but not all the lineages are of equal standing or rank... Those deemed to be nearest to the founding ancestor of the clan are the most senior and politically powerful." Apical lineages monopolize surpluses via ancestor mediation. (lines ~1300-1315)
- State formation when apical lineages cease intermarriage within clan and form elite inter-clan networks: "the process of state-formation thus takes place when the apical lineages (at the top of the cones) no longer mix and marry with their clansmen. Instead they interact with other elevated lineages from other clans". (lines ~1319-1322)
- Zu (lineage) as fundamental: "The lineage (zu) was probably the most important social framework for coercion; zu rules were the society’s fundamental law." Zu as military unit of 100 men/households. (lines ~1302, ~1369)
- Zhou expansion via branching lineage (zongfa) and enfeoffment (fengjian): "The branching lineage system (zā ngfā) provided the perfect framework for feudal land-holding... The king (wang) could assign particular lineages to be the military and economic retainers... Clans could be assigned territory many hundreds of kilometres away". New states as guo (fortified capital district + territory) with subsidiary yi. (lines ~1630-1680 approx)
- Not city-state model: "It is this condition that prompts some writers to speak of the early Chinese ‘city-state’. But neither socially nor economically, militarily nor in terms of settlement structure is this a useful description. They began as small territorial states and either expanded to become or were absorbed by larger territorial states of the ‘village-state’ type". Absence of citizenship/participation rights. (lines ~1755-1765)
- Post-conquest: "guo-ren" (state/city people, citizen status, military duties) vs "ye-ren" (field people). (lines ~1705-1710)

**Bronze Age urbanism, capitals (Erlitou, Zhengzhou, Anyang), Western Zhou:**
- Dispersed settlement pattern of walled yi towns: "The fundamental social unit throughout the Bronze Age [c.2200 to c. 500 BC] was the walled town [yi] thousands of which dotted the loessic landscape of north China. Physically, all these towns were similar." State (guo) = network of yi under royal du. "The lineage hierarchy would, normatively, coincide with the hierarchy of walled towns." (lines ~1715-1745)
- Erlitou (possible Xia, c. 1800–1500 BC): Large palace compounds (108x100m foundation for Palace 1), stamped earth, elite tombs with sacrifice, early bronzes (ritual vessels, weapons), scapulimancy but "no inscriptions occur on those scapulae". Phases III–IV show continuity with early Shang at Erligang/Zhengzhou. Radiocarbon 1800–2100 BC "too early for Shang... but just right for Xia". (lines ~1820-1940)
- Zhengzhou (early Shang capital, likely Bo): Walled ~1.75 x 1.5 km; palatial area >300,000 m²; massive fang ding bronzes (up to 1m tall, 86kg); workshops for surplus production. (lines ~2070-2150)
- Anyang (late Shang Yinxu capital district ~24 km²): "Anyang is not a single site but the capital district... The ruling centre... at Xiaotun... palaces... royal temple sector... ceremonial quarter... semi-subterranean houses of the servitors... bronze foundries, stone and bone workshops and pottery kilns". Royal cemetery at Xibeigang: 11 large tombs (matching 11 kings) + >1000 small graves, horse-chariot burials, mass sacrifice. "The eleven large tombs are grouped into a western cluster of seven and an eastern cluster of four, happily coinciding in number with the eleven kings from P’an Geng to Ti Yi". No walls at Yinxu. (lines ~1990-2060)
- Western Zhou: Similar dispersed pattern; capitals Feng/Hao then Luoyang; "Roof-tiles make their appearance for the first time in China, and are probably a Zhou invention, as also are fired bricks"; oracle bones confirm pre-conquest literacy. (lines ~2160-2240, ~2320)

**Childe’s checklist evaluation (China / San Dai):**
- 1. Cities: "no cities until the Xia centre at Erlitou... The San Dai ‘city’ was more a collection of functionally distinct villages forming a settlement cluster than a city in its usual sense... The Bronze Age city in China was not like this nor was it like the cities of India, Mesopotamia or Egypt." (lines ~2385-2400)
- 9. Regular foreign trade: "0" (none in formative periods). "There is no Chinese equivalent to Gulf or Levant trade... ‘trade’ means regular exchanges of significant amounts of goods, and of this there is little evidence in the formative periods... nothing that China wanted or thought it needed from overseas." Cowries from coast; internal exchange dominant. Merchants only late Western Zhou. (lines ~2655-2665)
- 5. Ruling class: Yes, stabilized with Xia; "Dukes live on tribute, ministers on their estates, shi [‘gentlemen’] on the land, and peasants on their toil." (lines ~2445-2465)
- 6/7. Writing/numeracy/sciences: Writing from pottery clan marks + divination, not accounting ("roots in the marking of pottery for social identification, in marked contrast to the roots of Near Eastern writing in an accounting system"). Calendar lunar with intercalation; gnomon measurements by 10th c. BC; no early geometry like Egypt. (lines ~2470-2570)
- 12. State dominant: Yes from Xia onward; "once hierarchy had become established in the Longshan and had become the developed condition of the San Dai, there was no question that Chinese populations would not be state ordered." (lines ~2730-2740)
- Overall: China fits territorial state form with strong clanship; shares most criteria but differs markedly on nucleation/foreign trade/city form from Mesopotamia/Indus/Egypt. (cross-ref Table 6.1)

**Trade notes:**
- "Until the Tang Dynasty (AD 618–906), the Chinese did not go abroad to trade." Elite goods (bronze, jades) moved internally or via limited exchanges; cowrie shells "had to be brought from the coast". Sanxingdui possibly on metal trade route Yunnan to plains. No evidence for significant merchant role or foreign commerce in Neolithic–Bronze formative phases. (lines ~118, ~2655-2665, ~1590)

**Other notes from range:**
- Sanxingdui (Sichuan, ~2700–1000 BC): Parallel advanced Bronze Age culture west of Central Plains; massive bronze statues/masks/trees contemporary with Shang but distinct ("its bronze statuary is larger, finer and much more arresting than anything from the Shang"); ritual pits; no mention in classical texts. (lines ~360-490)
- Continuity vs change: Some cultural traits traceable to Yangshao; but major gaps (e.g. Sanxingdui discoveries); "the present picture of the Neolithic or of any other period in China is far from complete". (lines ~243-244, ~480)
