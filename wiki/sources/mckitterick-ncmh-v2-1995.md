# The New Cambridge Medieval History, Volume 2: c. 700–c. 900 (1995)

*The New Cambridge Medieval History, Volume 2: c. 700–c. 900*, edited by Rosamond McKitterick. Cambridge University Press, 1995 (fifth printing 2006).

This volume is the primary academic backbone for the **long eighth and ninth centuries** — the era of Frankish and Carolingian dominance in western Europe. Chronologically it picks up where [[fouracre-ncmh-v1-2005|NCMH I]] (c. 500–c. 700) leaves off, opening on the eve of the Pippinid/Arnulfing seizure of the Frankish throne and closing around 900, on the threshold of the post-Carolingian world covered by NCMH III. Its guiding argument is that despite the political turbulence of conquest and partition, these were "years of remarkably accelerated cultural and political formation, when ideologies and institutions were determined, social structures coalesced, and religious, intellectual and cultural traditions were established" (Conclusion, p. 848) — the laying of the foundation for European civilisation.

## Volume Synthesis Note

**Overall argument.** NCMH II is the definitive English-language reference for the long eighth and
ninth centuries — the era of Frankish and Carolingian dominance (c. 700–c. 900). Its governing
interpretive choice, announced in the Preface, is to abandon the old Cambridge Medieval History's
"preoccupation with empire and the simple, blow-by-blow account of conquests and coronations" in
favour of an analysis of **how power and authority actually worked** and of "the interaction between
rulers and ruled." The result is a volume that treats the [[carolingian-empire]] less as a story of
great kings than as a working system of resources, consensus, and ideology — and that consistently
**deflates triumphalist readings**: of the imperial coronation of 800 ([[coronation-of-charlemagne-800]],
whose practical significance Fouracre minimises), of Carolingian administration (the
[[nature-of-carolingian-government|optimistic-vs-pessimistic debate]] over the
[[missi-dominici|missi]] and [[carolingian-capitularies|capitularies]]), and of the
[[carolingian-renaissance]] itself (Contreni's "less schematic and more decentralised" reality). The
volume's own Conclusion nonetheless affirms a genuine, lasting achievement: these were "years of
remarkably accelerated cultural and political formation."

**Cross-section connections emerging at volume level.** Three arguments run invisibly across the
volume's four-part structure and become visible only in retrospect. (1) **The aristocracy is the hinge
of the whole system.** The [[carolingian-kingship|kingship]] of Part II (Nelson) ruled *through* an
untaxed, land-based [[carolingian-aristocracy|nobility]] bound by [[vassalage|honores and benefices]];
the political narrative of Part I — the rise of the Pippinids ([[charles-martel]], [[pippin-iii]]) and
the fragmentation after 840 ([[battle-of-fontenoy-841]], [[treaty-of-verdun-843]]) — is the same
aristocracy aligning with, then fighting over, royal patronage. The institutions chapters explain the
narrative chapters. (2) **Reform was a single enterprise spanning church, learning, and government.**
The *correctio* of [[carolingian-church-reform|canon law and liturgy]], the
[[carolingian-monasticism|monastic "power of prayer"]], the [[religion-and-lay-society|Christianisation
of lay life]] (Part III), and the schools and [[caroline-minuscule|corrected texts]] of the
[[carolingian-renaissance|renaissance]] (Part IV) are facets of one royally-driven project to order a
Christian society — the same impulse visible in the capitularies of Part II. (3) **Christianisation was
the era's great integrating and dividing force.** The conversion of the Saxons, the
[[cyrillo-methodian-mission|Slavs]], and the [[conversion-of-boris-i-864|Bulgars]], and the missionary
work of figures like [[boniface]], extended a common Latin-or-Slavonic Christian culture to the edges of
Europe — even as the [[byzantine-iconoclasm|iconoclast]] dispute, the "two emperors" problem, and the
Photian schism drove Latin and Greek Christendom apart, and the Slavonic liturgy split the Slavs between
Rome and Constantinople.

**What this volume adds to the wiki.** NCMH II fills the wiki's central **eighth–ninth-century gap**.
Before it, the period rested on [[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|CWH IV]]'s global frame and a single-source
[[carolingian-empire]] page. NCMH II supplies the entire Carolingian narrative spine (nine new
ruler/state actor pages), the institutional anatomy of the empire (kingship, aristocracy, vassalage,
economy, the bipartite manor, the coinage reform), the church (the papacy and the formation of the papal
state, monasticism, church reform, lay religion), and the intellectual world (the renaissance, Caroline
minuscule, Alcuin, Carolingian art). It also opens whole new regional fronts barely touched before: the
[[viking-age]] and [[norse-scandinavia|Scandinavia]], the [[first-bulgarian-empire|Bulgars]] and
[[great-moravia|Moravia]], Christian [[kingdom-of-asturias|Asturias]], [[muslim-conquest-of-sicily-827|Muslim
Sicily]], and the transformation of [[exarchate-of-ravenna|Byzantine Italy]] (including the birth of
[[republic-of-venice|Venice]]).

**Cross-volume tensions and continuities.** (1) NCMH II is the **direct successor to
[[fouracre-ncmh-v1-2005|NCMH I]]**: it picks up the [[post-roman-transformation-of-the-west|post-Roman
transformation]] at the point where the Pippinid mayors convert aristocratic dominance into kingship, and
Fouracre (author of the Frankish-Gaul chapter here and editor of Vol. I) carries his continuity thesis
forward — "in 814 as in 714, power on the ground lay in the hands of counts and bishops." (2) It
**complicates the Pirenne picture** inherited from NCMH I and [[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|CWH IV]]: the
[[carolingian-economy|economic chapters]] (Verhulst, Wickham, Blackburn) show a partially monetised,
North-Sea-and-emporia-oriented economy with a reviving silver coinage, neither a closed natural economy
nor a restored Mediterranean unity. (3) On [[byzantine-empire|Byzantium]], NCMH II views the empire
"from the West" — through iconoclasm's western reception, the two-emperors problem, and the Bulgar wars —
complementing the Constantinople-centred narratives of CWH IV/V. (4) The volume flags its own **gaps**
(Preface): the planned chapters on **law/legal practice** and on the **Russian lands** were not delivered
(law is scattered across chapters; the Rus' are deferred to NCMH III), so the wiki's coverage of
early-medieval legal practice and the eastern Slavs from this volume is correspondingly thin and awaits
later ingestion.
