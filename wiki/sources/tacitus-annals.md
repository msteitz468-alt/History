# Tacitus, *The Annals*

Tacitus's masterpiece and "Ancient Rome's greatest" work of history (Barrett): a year-by-year account
of the **Julio-Claudian emperors from the death of Augustus (14 CE) to the death of Nero (68 CE)**,
and the indispensable narrative for the early Roman Empire. The last of the five Phase-4 primary
sources in this batch. For the author see **[[tacitus|Tacitus]]**; for the system it anatomizes,
**[[principate|the principate]]**.

## What Survives

Of the thirty books of the combined *Histories* and *Annals*, about half survive; of the *Annals*
(traditionally sixteen books):

- **Books 1–6 — Tiberius (14–37 CE).** Book 5 is largely lost (a gap of c. 29–31, swallowing the rise
  and fall of **Sejanus**). The fullest and most admired portion: the only reign for which we have
  both beginning and end.
- **Books 7–10 and the start of 11 — lost,** taking with them the **entire reign of Caligula (37–41)**.
- **Books 11–16 — Claudius and Nero.** The narrative resumes mid-**Claudius** (c. 47) and breaks off
  two years before the end of **Nero** (66), in the midst of the post-Pisonian terror.

## Structure and the Annalistic Method

Tacitus is committed to the **annalistic format** — events followed year by year, organized around the
entry of the consuls — though in the *Annals* he feels freed enough from it to **open with the death of
Augustus rather than the consuls of 15**, because the transfer of power from Augustus to Tiberius marks
the symbolic moment when "the personal autocracy of Augustus became the institutionalized autocracy of
Rome." Frontier wars (Britain, Parthia, Armenia) are gathered into multi-year clusters, breaking the
strict annual scheme. The Tiberian books fall into two triads: Books 1–3 (the better first half, to c.
22) balanced against Books 4–6 (the descent into despotism after the rise of Sejanus from 23).

## Method, Sources and Bias

Tacitus drew on the **acta senatus** (senatorial proceedings — Syme argued these underpin his
achievement, supplying the named motions and counter-proposals), the **acta diurna** (the daily
gazette, whose triviality he notes), earlier historians, and occasional **oral testimony** from older
contemporaries; he cites direct consultation of the senatorial records only once (15.74). His promise
of ***sine ira et studio*** must be read critically: outright falsification is rare, but his selection,
his ascription of motive, and his reporting of rumour shape the reader irresistibly. The **senatus
consultum de Cn. Pisone Patre** — fragments found in Spain since the 1980s, "arguably the most
important Latin epigraphic discovery of the twentieth century" — now permits a direct test of his
account of the Piso trial against the primary decree.

## The Portraits

- **Tiberius** — the masterpiece: a complex, deceptive man whose virtues (shyness, generosity) Tacitus
  recasts as arrogance and hypocrisy, and whose character disintegrates under the corrupting force of
  the system Augustus built — "a powerful object lesson of the truth of Lord Acton's… sententia."
- **Claudius** — comparatively passive, dominated by his freedmen (**Narcissus, Pallas**) and wives
  (**Messalina, Agrippina the Younger**), who move to the centre of the narrative.
- **Nero** — promising at first under Seneca and Burrus, then collapsing into matricide, sexual
  excess, the **Fire of Rome** and the terror against the senators. Where the later books shift from
  the emperor's psychology to **dramatic set-pieces** (the murder of Agrippina, the Pisonian
  conspiracy), Nero is merely despicable, lacking the lost greatness that makes Tiberius tragic.

## Significance

The *Annals* is at once the **primary narrative source** for two-thirds of the Julio-Claudian century
and the **founding text of the European critique of tyranny** — the model of history as moral and
political anatomy, read by Gibbon and the Enlightenment as the classic indictment of absolute power.
