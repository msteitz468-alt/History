# The Cambridge Ancient History, Vol. III, Part 3 (1982)

**The Expansion of the Greek World, Eighth to Sixth Centuries B.C.** Edited by
John Boardman and N. G. L. Hammond. Cambridge University Press, 2nd edition, 1982.

This is the **Archaic Greece volume** of the CAH — the narrative and archaeological
account of how the Greek city-states, emerging from the Dark Age covered in
[[boardman-cah-v3p1-1982|III.1]], expanded across the Mediterranean and Black Sea
and transformed themselves at home between roughly **800 and 500 BCE**. Its two
great themes are stated in the preface: the **outward** movement — contact with the
older civilizations of the Near East and Egypt, then the explosive
[[greek-colonization|colonization]] that planted independent *poleis* from
Tartessos to the Pontus — and the **inward** one — the economic revolution, the
collapse of the old landed aristocracies, and the political experimentation
(lawgiving, tyranny, the early Athenian state) that produced Archaic Greek
civilization. It is the immediate background to the Persian Wars and the Classical
world of CAH IV.

It is paired with two companion volumes already ingested:
[[boardman-cah-v3p1-1982|Vol. III Part 1]] (the prehistory of the Balkans, Near
East and Aegean to c. 800) and [[boardman-cah-v3p2-1991|Vol. III Part 2]] (the
Assyrian and Babylonian empires, 8th–6th c.), with which it shares the Near
Eastern, Egyptian, Cypriot and Phoenician material from the *Greek* point of view.

## Structure of the Volume

The volume runs from **chapter 36 to chapter 45**, moving from the Greeks' contact
with the older civilizations (36), through the colonization process and its two
great theatres — west (37–38) and east/Aegean (39) — then surveying the Greek
mainland region by region (40–44), and closing with two synthetic chapters on
economy/society and material culture (45a–b).

## Volume Synthesis Note

CAH III.3 is the **Archaic Greece** volume — the account of how, between roughly 800 and
500 BCE, the Greek city-states emerged from the Dark Age covered in
[[boardman-cah-v3p1-1982|III.1]] and made themselves the most dynamic civilization of the
Mediterranean. Its two organizing movements, stated in Boardman's preface and recurring
across every chapter, are the **outward** and the **inward**. Outward: the renewed
contact with the older civilizations ([[orientalizing-revolution|the orientalizing
revolution]], the [[alphabet]], the debt to Egypt) and the explosive
[[greek-colonization|colonization]] that planted independent *poleis* from
[[gades-tartessos|Tartessos]] to the Black Sea — the [[magna-graecia|western Greeks]],
the [[ionia|East Greeks]], [[cyrene|Cyrene]]. Inward: the [[archaic-greek-economy|economic
transformation]] (the peasantization of the countryside, the rise of trade, cities and
[[coinage|coinage]]) and the political experimentation it forced — lawgiving,
[[greek-tyranny|tyranny]], and the reforms of [[solon|Solon]] — that produced the mature
[[city-state|polis]]. The volume ends at the threshold of the Persian Wars and the
Classical world of CAH IV.

The volume's deepest unifying argument, advanced most explicitly by Graham (Ch. 37) and
Starr (Ch. 45a) but pervading the whole, is that the **engine of the Archaic
transformation was the polis and the restless energy of its upper classes**, not any
single material cause. Colonization was "a product of the world of the independent
city-state"; the economic quickening was driven less by population or coinage (both
demoted to consequences) than by the aristocracy's "eager, ruthless drive" for *chremata*,
stimulated by the recovered Near East. The same restless competition appears as the
artistic *koine* and giant temples of Ionia, the orientalizing flair that absorbed
eastern technique into a wholly Hellenic idiom (Boardman's "catalyst, not model"), the
agonistic tyrant-courts of Corinth and Sicyon, and the faction that produced Solon and
Pisistratus. The volume thus models Archaic Greece as a single competitive system whose
parts — colonies, sanctuaries, tyrannies, trade — were expressions of one expansive
civic-aristocratic culture.

Methodologically the volume is a **showcase of how Archaic history must be written from
fragments** — the contemporary poets (Hesiod, Archilochus, Tyrtaeus, Solon, Alcaeus,
Theognis), the later narrators (Herodotus, Thucydides, the Atthidographers, Aristotle's
*Ath. Pol.*), and above all the **archaeology of pottery, cemeteries, sanctuaries and
inscriptions** that dates colonies, vindicates the literary tradition (Graham on the
foundation-dates), and supplies the only record for some regions (Crete, Illyris). Its
recurring caution is against **retrojecting later schemata** — Aristotle's
"demagogue" theory of tyranny, the "hoplite class" as an organized political force, the
"trade-league" reading of the Lelantine War, the systematized king-lists — and against
unitary explanations (Graham's "we only find ourselves in difficulty if we demand unitary
explanations"). This makes it a soft-edged volume at the points where it leans on late
tradition (early Athenian chronology, the Spartan Rhetra, the Sacred War, the precise
foundation dates), and an authoritative one where archaeology grounds it.

For the wiki, III.3 **completes the Archaic horizon** that III.1 opened and III.2 framed
from the Near Eastern side. It supplied the foundational pages for the Greek heartland
([[athens]], [[sparta]], [[corinth]], [[argos]], [[ionia]], [[boeotia]], [[thessaly]],
[[crete]]), the great processes ([[greek-colonization]], the
[[orientalizing-revolution]], the [[archaic-greek-economy]]), the institutions and
concepts ([[delphi]], the [[peloponnesian-league]], [[greek-tyranny]], the
[[gortyn-code]], [[coinage]], [[hoplite-warfare]], the [[cypriot-syllabary]],
[[archaic-greek-material-culture]]), and the actors ([[solon]], [[pisistratus]],
[[polycrates]], [[hesiod]]). Its richest forward connections are three: to **Persia** (the
volume repeatedly ends its threads — Ionia, Lydia, Polycrates, Croesus — at the Persian
conquest, handing the Aegean to the Achaemenids); to **the Classical polis** (Solon's
constitution, Cleisthenes-to-be, the Athenian drama and navy that Pisistratus prefigured);
and back to the **Near East** of [[boardman-cah-v3p2-1991|III.2]] (the same Lydians,
Saites, Phoenicians and Babylonians seen now from the Greek deck). It sharpens several
standing [[controversies|controversies]] — the causation of colonization (land-hunger vs.
trade), the historicity and partisanship of the Delphic oracle, the "hoplite revolution"
thesis, and the reliability of the Atthidographers and of Herodotus on Archaic dates.
