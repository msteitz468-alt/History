# The Cambridge Ancient History, Volume VI: The Fourth Century B.C. (2nd ed., 1994)

The capstone of the second-edition Greek CAH sequence. Where [[lewis-cah-v5-1992|CAH V]]
covered the fifth century to the fall of Athens (404), this volume carries the Greek
world from the Spartan victory through the Corinthian War, the King's Peace, the brief
Theban hegemony, the rise of Macedon under Philip II, and the conquests of Alexander,
ending with Alexander's death in **323 BCE**. It pairs the political narrative with a
rich set of **regional surveys** (Persian lands, the West, the North) and thematic
chapters on society, economy, the polis, culture, science, art, agriculture, and warfare.

Editors: **D. M. Lewis, John Boardman, Simon Hornblower, M. Ostwald.** Processed under
the Large-Volume Protocol. The senior editor (Lewis) is shared with Vol. V, hence the
`lewis-` source slug.

## Volume Synthesis Note

CAH VI is the volume of **the polis losing the argument with the future**. Where
[[lewis-cah-v5-1992|CAH V]] showed the fifth-century city-state at its cultural and
imperial apogee, Vol. VI follows the fourth century to the point where the autonomous
*polis* — its limits already exposed — gives way to **federation and, decisively,
monarchy**. The narrative chapters trace a relay of failed hegemonies: the
[[spartan-hegemony|Spartan hegemony]] (broken by its own *oliganthropia* and overreach at
[[battle-of-leuctra-371bce|Leuctra]]), the resurgent Athens of the
[[second-athenian-confederacy|Second Confederacy]], and the meteoric
[[thebes-hegemony-371bce|Theban hegemony]] of [[epaminondas]] that
[[liberation-of-messenia-369bce|freed Messenia]] and crippled Sparta forever, only to die
with its author at [[battle-of-mantinea-362bce|Mantinea]]. Xenophon's *Hellenica* ends
there, on the famous note of "even more confusion and disorder than before" — the perfect
emblem of a multipolar world exhausting itself. Into that vacuum steps
**[[philip-ii-of-macedon|Philip]]**, whose professional army and the
[[league-of-corinth|League of Corinth]] (after [[battle-of-chaeronea-338bce|Chaeronea]])
solve the problem the polis could not — the durable combination of Greek states — and arm
the [[alexander-the-great|Alexandrian]] conquest that ends the volume at
[[battle-of-gaugamela-331bce|Gaugamela]].

Three structural arguments run beneath the events. **First, the failure of the polis**
(Rhodes, Ch. 11): the city-state could not durably *combine*, and the future lay with the
*koinon* ([[greek-federalism]]) and the territorial monarchy — a thesis the whole volume
illustrates and the Epilogue confirms by reading Alexander as the *culmination* of
fourth-century autocracy (the prototype being not Philip but **[[dionysius-i|Dionysius I]]**
of Syracuse). **Second, the rehabilitation of Persia** (Hornblower, Chs. 3, 8a): the
"decadent" fourth-century empire is a Greek literary construct; the [[achaemenid-satrapy|
satrapal system]] was a resilient quasi-feudal bond whose loyalty (Mausolus, the western
satraps at the Granicus) explains Persia's durability, so that its fall is "to be laid at
the door of Darius III personally." **Third, the professionalization of power** — mercenary
armies, siege artillery, the oblique order ([[greek-warfare]]) — the military counterpart of
the political shift from amateur citizen to professional king's-man.

The volume's deliberate **method** is itself a theme. With no [[thucydides|Thucydides]] for
the fourth century ([[fourth-century-greek-historiography]]), the editors triangulate
Xenophon's pro-Spartan *Hellenica* against Ephorus/Diodorus, the *Hellenica Oxyrhynchia*,
Callisthenes and a richer epigraphy — and use that freedom to **shift the narrative focus
from Athens to Sparta and Thebes**, recovering a period "full of ideas and innovation" that
older scholarship dismissed as the confused prelude to Macedon. The new **regional surveys**
(Chs. 8–9) are the volume's other great contribution to the wiki, lighting up regions the
collection covers thinly: Achaemenid [[judah]] (the making of Second Temple Judaism and the
Samaritan schism), [[evagoras-of-salamis|Cyprus]], [[late-period-egypt|native Egypt]], the
[[celts|Celtic]] Iron Age, [[odrysian-kingdom|Odrysian Thrace]], and the
[[bosporan-kingdom|Bosporan]] grain-kingdom that fed Athens.

**Joins with the rest of the wiki.** Behind, the volume seams perfectly onto CAH V: it opens
where the [[peloponnesian-war]] left off, with [[lysander]] and the
[[thirty-tyrants|Thirty]], and carries [[sparta]], [[athens]], [[syracuse]] and
[[carthage]] forward from their fifth-century pages. Ahead, it hands off cleanly to the
**Hellenistic world**: Philip's army, the League of Corinth, the schools of [[plato]] and
[[aristotle]], the art of Lysippus, and Alexander at Gaugamela are the thresholds of CAH
VII. With this volume the **second edition of the Greek CAH is complete** — the wiki now
holds a continuous, source-grounded narrative of Greek history from the Bronze Age to the
death of Alexander.
