# The Cambridge History of the Native Peoples of the Americas, Volume III: South America, Part 2

The second physical part of Volume III (South America), edited by Frank Salomon and
Stuart B. Schwartz. Where [[cambridge-history-native-peoples-americas-v3-1|Part 1]] runs
from first peopling through the sixteenth-century invasion crises (to c. 1580), **Part 2**
treats the long aftermath: the establishment and maturation of colonial regimes in the
Andean and La Plata heartlands; the societies that resisted, adapted, or reorganized at
the **margins** of Spanish and Portuguese rule (the Araucanian south, western Amazonia,
the Chaco, the Brazilian sertão and north); the eighteenth-century rebellions; the
ambiguous place of indigenous peoples in the **nation-states** that emerged after
independence; and the indigenous politics, demographic recovery, and ethnic mobilization
of the twentieth century.

## Volume Synthesis Note

If [[cambridge-history-native-peoples-americas-v3-1|Part 1]] argued that native South
American history must be written on indigenous time-depth and from the indigenous side of
the encounter, **Part 2 extends that argument across the entire colonial and national
period** — refusing the conventional story in which indigenous peoples simply vanish into
"colonial society" after the conquest. Its controlling theme is **adaptive persistence**:
the colonial order did not erase native societies but reorganized them, and they in turn
reworked colonial institutions to their own ends. The [[reduccion|reducción]], [[mita]],
[[colonial-andean-tribute|tribute]], and the [[hacienda-system|hacienda]] appear here less
as machines of destruction than as arenas of struggle in which Andean households, *ayllus*,
and *curacas* preserved a margin of autonomy ([[colonial-andean-heartland-1570-1780]]).

A second organizing distinction is between the **heartland and the margins**. Where the
Quechua-Aymara core was deeply incorporated, vast zones — the
[[araucania-frontier-1573-1882|Araucanian south]], the [[pampas-horse-cultures|pampas]], the
[[chaco-frontier-1573-1882|Chaco]], [[western-amazonia-colonial-1540-1820|western Amazonia]],
and the [[northeastern-south-america-colonial-1500-1900|Orinoco-Guiana north]] — remained
unconquered for centuries, their peoples reorganizing around the horse, the gun, mission
refuge, and inter-imperial trade. The volume's signal contribution is to treat these
"margins" as history in their own right, and to show that the nineteenth-century
nation-states, not the colonial empires, were often the agents of final conquest
(the Argentine and Chilean campaigns of the 1870s–80s, the
[[amazon-rubber-boom|rubber frontier]]).

Third, the volume foregrounds **ethnogenesis** ([[south-american-ethnogenesis-colonial]],
[[ethnogenesis]]): colonialism generated *new* peoples and identities — mission peoples,
"colonial tribes," the Carib and Garifuna, Hispano-Guaraní Paraguay — as much as it
destroyed old ones. This frame carries through to the rebellion cycle
([[republic-of-indians-in-revolt]], [[tupac-amaru-rebellion-1780]]) and to the
twentieth-century resurgence ([[katarismo]], [[conaie]],
[[south-american-indigenous-rights-movement]]), where indigenous movements move from being
spoken *for* by [[indigenismo]] to speaking for themselves.

For the wiki, Part 2 **closes the post-conquest half of the South America gap** and dovetails
with already-ingested material: its colonial-Andes chapters extend
[[andean-colonial-transformation-1532-1580]] and
[[burkholder-johnson-colonial-latin-america|Burkholder & Johnson]]; its extractive-economy
chapters connect to [[potosi-and-colonial-silver]] and the dependency reading of
[[galeano-open-veins|Galeano]]; and its twentieth-century chapters give the wiki its first
substantial coverage of contemporary indigenous politics in the Andes and Amazonia. The
chief interpretive tension is the closing chapters' **advocacy stance** (Albó and
Maybury-Lewis write as engaged participants), preserved here as a noted historiographical
position rather than smoothed away.
