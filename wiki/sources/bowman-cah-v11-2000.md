# CAH Vol. XI — The High Empire, A.D. 70–192 (Bowman, Garnsey & Rathbone eds., 2nd ed. 2000)

The direct sequel to [[bowman-cah-v10-1996|CAH X]], covering the **"High Empire"** — the period
of the empire's greatest extent, stability, and prosperity, from the **Flavian** refounding of
the Principate after the civil war of 69 through the **Five Good Emperors** (Nerva, Trajan,
Hadrian, Antoninus Pius, Marcus Aurelius) to the death of **Commodus** (192) and the eve of the
third-century crisis. Where CAH X laid the Augustan foundations of the imperial system, CAH XI
shows that system at its **mature working** — the consolidated frontiers, the developed
administration, the urbanized and integrated provinces, and the cosmopolitan high culture of the
Antonine age (the Second Sophistic).

## Volume Synthesis Note

CAH XI is the portrait of the Roman empire **at its zenith** — the "High Empire" of c. AD 70–192,
the longest stretch of internal peace and material prosperity the Mediterranean world would ever
know. Its governing argument, recurring across every part, is that this was an empire that had
**stopped expanding and turned to consolidation**: the frontiers hardened (Whittaker's
chapter on the limes as a zone, not a line), the army settled into a peacetime role of garrison and
administration, the provinces **urbanized and integrated**, and the energies of the elite poured
not into conquest but into **euergetism, civic competition, and culture**. The forty pages created
from the volume cluster around this theme at three levels — the **central state** (the maturing
machinery of [[emperor-and-advisers-high-empire|the consilium]], the
[[high-imperial-senatorial-order|senatorial]] and [[equestrian-administration-high-empire|equestrian]]
orders, the [[roman-frontiers|frontier system]]); the **integrated provinces** (the updated
provincial pages from Spain to Judaea, plus new pages for [[rome-and-italy-high-empire|Italy]],
[[roman-britain|Britain]], and [[cyrenaica|Cyrenaica]]); and the **economy, society, and culture**
of a connected world (the [[roman-agrarian-economy|land]], [[roman-imperial-trade|trade]],
[[roman-demography|demography]], [[roman-social-structure-high-empire|the ordines]], and the
[[roman-literature-second-sophistic|Second Sophistic]]).

The volume's **narrative spine** runs from the Flavian refounding of the Principate after 69
([[vespasian|Vespasian]], [[titus|Titus]], [[domitian|Domitian]]) through the "Five Good Emperors"
([[nerva|Nerva]], [[trajan|Trajan]], [[hadrian|Hadrian]], [[antoninus-pius|Antoninus Pius]],
[[marcus-aurelius|Marcus Aurelius]]) to [[commodus|Commodus]]. Two structural turning points stand
out. First, **Trajan's wars** ([[dacian-wars-101-106|Dacia]], [[trajans-parthian-war-114-117|Parthia]])
mark the empire's greatest extent — and Hadrian's immediate retrenchment marks the strategic decision
to **stop** (the substance of the [[roman-grand-strategy|grand-strategy controversy]]). Second, under
Marcus the long peace breaks: the [[marcomannic-wars-166-180|Marcomannic Wars]] and the
[[antonine-plague-165|Antonine Plague]] are, in the volume's hindsight, the first tremors of the
third-century crisis that CAH XII will narrate. The volume's recurrent dialectic — between
**integration and resistance** — is sharpest in the provincial chapters and in [[roman-judaea|Judaea]],
where the [[bar-kochba-war-132-135|Bar Kochba War]] is the great exception to the age's pax.

What CAH XI **adds to the wiki as a whole** is the mature working-model of the imperial system whose
origins CAH X supplied. Where the Augustan volume showed the Principate being *invented*, this one
shows it *running* — and reveals that the system's strength (a light-touch central state resting on
self-governing, competitive cities and a co-opted provincial elite) was also the source of its
limits. The cultural chapters are the volume's distinctive contribution: a connected portrait of
**literacy**, **the Second Sophistic**, **philosophy**, **Galenic medicine**, **art and
architecture**, and **religion** that together document a single phenomenon — the **self-assertion of
a cosmopolitan, Greek-and-Latin elite culture** within the Roman order, and, beneath it, the slow
emergence of the inward, moralized, salvation-seeking **"new mood"** (Liebeschuetz) that would make
the Christian future intelligible.

**Cross-volume tensions.** The volume sits in productive tension with [[bowman-cah-v10-1996|CAH X]]
on periodization — both volumes claim the Flavians, and the "High Empire" is a historians' construct
laid over a continuous Principate. Its frontier chapter (Whittaker) deliberately **revises** the
older "grand strategy" model (Luttwak) still implicit in some earlier pages, a tension now captured
on [[roman-grand-strategy]]. And its treatment of the [[roman-imperial-trade|economy]] takes a
cautious, primitivist-inflected line that future volumes and specialist monographs (Brill/Routledge)
may sharpen into a controversy over the **scale and integration** of the Roman economy. These are
flagged for revisiting when CAH XII and the economic monographs are ingested.
