# The Cambridge History of the Pacific Ocean, Vol. 2: The Pacific Ocean since 1800

Volume 2 (Hattori & Samson eds.; D'Arcy gen. ed., Cambridge UP, 2023) carries the
[[oceanic-history|oceanic history]] of the [[pacific-ocean|Pacific]] from 1800 to
the present. It opens by "rethinking the Pacific" (climate change and endangered
island nations, Japan's "phantom empire", the shift from "Blue Continent" to "Blue
Pacific"), turns to subaltern and decolonizing approaches (archives, gender,
Hawaiian historiography, literature, film, the arts), then treats pre-colonial
European culture contact, the colonial era, the contested "Pacific Century"
(US power, World War II, the nuclear Pacific, China, decolonisation), and finally
"Pacific Futures" (climate, New Caledonia, new voyaging, sustainability).

Its through-line is methodological and political: it foregrounds Islander agency
and knowledge against "rimbound" continental narratives, completing the series'
project of treating the ocean as a connective Indigenous homeland.

## Volume Synthesis Note

Volume 2 completes the series' inversion of Pacific history: where the older
convention began with European arrival, this volume centres Islander agency,
knowledge and memory, and treats the modern era's defining forces — colonialism,
the World Wars, the nuclear age, decolonisation, globalisation and climate change —
as experiences *of* the ocean's peoples rather than events done *to* a passive
periphery. Its keyword is "rimbound": the editors warn against histories anchored
in continental reference points (Spain, Britain, the Cold War USA) and argue for an
[[oceanic-history|oceanic]] frame that runs from [[pacific-ancestral-knowledge-and-futures|ancestral
voices]] to the [[blue-pacific-regionalism|"Blue Pacific"]] regional identity.

Because the wiki's modern-Pacific network was already substantial (from Denoon and
Fischer), this ingest is as much **enrichment as creation**: it deepens seven
existing pages — most importantly the [[fatal-impact-debate|Fatal Impact debate]],
the [[pacific-nuclear-testing|nuclear Pacific]], [[pacific-decolonisation|island
nations since independence]], and Islanders' experience of [[pacific-war-1941-1945|World
War II]] — while adding whole modern domains the islander histories lacked: the
historiographical/subaltern turn (archives, Hawaiian historiography, literature,
film, the arts), [[japan-in-oceania|Japan's "phantom empire"]],
[[us-pacific-expansion|US Pacific expansion]],
[[china-pacific-relations-since-1949|China since 1949]],
[[latin-america-pacific-ambitions|Latin America's Pacific]], the
[[pacific-steam-and-telegraphy|steam-and-cable]] and
[[pacific-transport-communications-revolution|container/jet/internet]] integrations,
and the existential frame of
[[pacific-climate-change-and-sea-level-rise|climate change and rising seas]].

Cross-volume tension: the volume's Islander-centred, present-minded and explicitly
political stance (several chapters are activist or autobiographical) sits in
productive contrast with the more detached archaeological and imperial-administrative
sources elsewhere in the wiki — and, like Vol. 1, it resists the wiki framework's
Eurocentric periodization, organising instead around "deep time," "the colonial
era," and "Pacific futures." With Vols. 1 and 2 both ingested, the
*Cambridge History of the Pacific Ocean* is complete in the wiki.
