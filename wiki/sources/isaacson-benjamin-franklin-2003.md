# Benjamin Franklin: An American Life (Isaacson, 2003)

Walter Isaacson's one-volume life of Benjamin Franklin (1706–1790), organized
as a chronological narrative from Franklin's Boston boyhood through his
apprenticeship, printing career, scientific work, colonial politics, the
imperial crisis, the Revolution, his French diplomacy, and his final years as
elder statesman of the Constitutional Convention. Isaacson reads Franklin as
the first great exponent of an American middle-class ethos — practical,
tolerant, self-improving, civic-minded — and argues that this sensibility helped
"invent" the American character.

## Volume Synthesis Note

Isaacson's *Benjamin Franklin* is a popular narrative biography rather than a work of academic revision, and its value to the wiki is correspondingly as a rich, reliable single-life spine rather than as a source of contested new claims. Its organizing argument — that Franklin "invented," and continually reinvented, himself, and in doing so helped invent the American middle-class character — is the author's own thematic frame and is quarantined as such on the biography page (labeled as Isaacson's interpretation), while the documented facts of the life flow freely.

Three threads give the book its analytic payoff. First, the **arc of radicalization**: Isaacson (with Morgan and Gordon Wood) resists the Whig-teleological reading, showing a sincere royalist slow to break, whose conversion was as much a product of personal humiliation (the Cockpit) as of ideology — a useful corrective to the wiki's revolution pages, which can flatten the founders into pre-formed patriots. Second, the **fusion of realism and idealism in the French alliance**, which Isaacson (via Bailyn) frames as the origin of a durable American diplomatic mode; this connects Franklin's 1778 achievement forward to later inflection points already in the wiki. Third, the **family tragedy of William**, which supplies the biography's counter-hagiographic ballast — the same man who built a nation's civic tolerance could be coldly implacable toward his own loyalist son.

The book sits comfortably alongside the wiki's other Age-of-Expansion founder biographies (Adams, Jefferson, Washington, Hamilton) without contradiction, and alongside the other Isaacson lives already ingested (Einstein, Jobs, Musk, Leonardo) as a consistent methodological product: verbatim-quote-rich, interview-and-archive-grounded where possible, thematically framed, and honest about the subject's flaws.
