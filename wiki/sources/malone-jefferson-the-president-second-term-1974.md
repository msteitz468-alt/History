# Jefferson the President: Second Term, 1805-1809 — Dumas Malone (1974)

**Jefferson and His Time, Volume 5.** This volume completes the story of Jefferson's presidency. It carries the narrative from the triumphant re-election of 1804 through the second term (March 1805–March 1809) and the end of his official career of some forty years. Malone presents the second quadrennium as less glorious than the first — "seas were rougher and winds less favorable." Jefferson moved from the zenith of popularity after Louisiana to a nadir marked by the partial repeal of the embargo and the rejection of his final appointment. The administration was largely on the defensive against British and French aggressions on American commerce and neutral rights. Domestic drama centered on Aaron Burr; foreign drama on the British frigate Leopard's attack on the Chesapeake. The central policy experiment was the Embargo — an attempt at large-scale economic coercion without war.

## Overview / Thesis

Malone portrays a president who retained personal popularity and party leadership longer than many expected but who faced structural constraints (separation of powers, rudimentary party organization, a hostile or at best independent judiciary, sectional interests, and the overwhelming power of the warring European states) that limited his options. Jefferson's activism continued in characteristic forms (patronage of Lewis and Clark, continued efforts at economy and reform), but initiative in foreign affairs was repeatedly checked. He sought to avoid war while defending American rights through non-importation, then the Embargo, and finally non-intercourse. The Burr episode tested his commitment to republican legality against security fears; the Chesapeake crisis tested his preference for peace and economic weapons over naval or military confrontation. The volume emphasizes Jefferson's continuing devotion to the separation of powers and distrust of concentrated authority even while exercising executive leadership, the growing strains with "Old Republicans" led by John Randolph, and the personal and political costs of a policy that ultimately proved unsustainable. The term ends with the recognition of failure on key foreign policy hopes, the orderly transfer of power to Madison, and Jefferson's return to private life at Monticello.

## Methodological Notes

- Strict chronological narrative that shows the development of policy in real time, what Jefferson knew and when.
- Heavy reliance on Jefferson's letters and memoranda, cabinet papers, congressional debates and reports, contemporary newspapers, and diplomatic correspondence available to mid-20th-century scholars.
- Detailed reconstruction of the Embargo's origins, implementation difficulties, sectional impacts, and political backlash.
- Careful treatment of the Burr conspiracy from the administration's perspective, including relations with Wilkinson and the legal proceedings before Marshall.
- Attention to the social and political setting in Washington (separation of persons as well as powers) and Jefferson's efforts to bridge legislative-executive gaps through hospitality and persuasion.
- Acknowledges that this is a study centered on Jefferson as President, not a full history of the United States in the period.

## Key Contributions

- The most detailed scholarly account of the day-to-day conduct of the second term, especially the mechanics and political costs of the Embargo.
- Full anatomy of Jefferson's response to the Burr conspiracy and the Richmond treason and misdemeanor trials, including the subpoena controversy and Marshall's construction of treason.
- Clear narrative of the Chesapeake-Leopard affair and the subsequent diplomatic maneuvering (Rose mission, Monroe-Canning talks).
- Insightful analysis of Jefferson's relations with Madison (especially on the Monroe-Pinkney treaty), Gallatin, and the emerging opposition of John Randolph and the "tertium quid"/Old Republicans.
- Balanced assessment of achievements (party unity maintained for most of the term, orderly succession, Lewis and Clark fruits) against the frustrations and the acknowledged failure of hopes at the end.

## Range 01 Claims

- **The young Republic acquired the imperial province of Louisiana primarily due to European circumstances rather than solely American initiative.** “States were gravely endangered at New Orleans, the gateway to the Mississippi and its valley, but in the end, surprisingly, the young Republic acquired the imperial province of Louisiana. Credit should certainly not be denied the administration and its representatives for this magnificent acquisition of territory, whereby Jefferson made his most memorable contribution to the physical greatness of his country, but Bonaparte would not have sold Louisiana but for the setback to his colonial ambitions in St. Domingo and his determination to reopen the war in Europe.” (Introduction). This [[events/louisiana-purchase-1803]] produced both vast territorial benefits and fresh troubles for [[actors/jefferson-thomas]]; efforts to round out the acquisition by obtaining the Floridas failed. (See also [[places/new-orleans]]). [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]]

- **At the outset of Jefferson's second term the administration achieved a minor diplomatic success in settling the war with Tripoli, but no further successes of note followed.** “At the outset the administration gained a minor success in the relatively favorable settlement of the war with Tripoli, but there were to be no more diplomatic successes of any note. The period was marked by successive frustrations and it ended in the recognized failure of hopes.” (Introduction). This concluded key aspects of [[events/tripolitan-war-1801-1805]] amid steadily worsening external conditions for [[actors/jefferson-thomas]]. [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]]

- **Slow communication and obsolete information severely hampered American diplomacy during this period.** “Policies were formulated in Washington on the basis of information that was already obsolete, and instructions to American representatives abroad were often out of date before they were delivered.” (Introduction). These conditions contributed to faulty tactics and unrealistic strategies pursued by [[actors/jefferson-thomas]], [[actors/monroe-james]], and the administration; Jefferson himself had reduced the foreign service for economy. [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]]

- **Jefferson persisted in efforts to acquire West Florida by playing major powers against each other, employing methods that proved ill-timed and prolonged.** “Jefferson never wholly gave up hope that by playing the major powers against each other he could gain advantages for his own country. This persistence was nowhere better illustrated than in his efforts to acquire West Florida.” (Introduction). The French proved unwilling to pressure their Spanish ally, leading to domestic difficulties for [[actors/jefferson-thomas]]. [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]]

- **Jefferson maintained a policy of neutrality while seeking to protect American maritime interests amid intensifying conflict between Britain and France.** “A policy of neutrality was enjoined by American tradition and predominant sentiment, as well as by Jefferson's conviction that it was to the interest of his country to keep wholly out of this conflict. Thus he was confronted with the problem of both maintaining neutrality and protecting his country's interests in what amounted to a world war.” (Introduction). After Trafalgar (British supremacy at sea) and Austerlitz (Napoleon's land supremacy), commercial coercion was chosen as the weapon, leading to the embargo [[events/embargo-1807]] as the only alternative to war or submission. [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]]

- **The embargo laws imposed unprecedented restrictions on individual economic freedom since independence, yet Jefferson viewed them as a patriotic national enterprise.** “Since the embargo laws imposed on the economic freedom of individual Americans restrictions such as they had not known since the winning of independence... He adopted it in the first place as the only alternative to war or submission. It imposed on his countrymen greater hardships than he had anticipated, but he never ceased to believe that these should be borne in the spirit of patriotism.” (Introduction). The policy [[events/embargo-1807]] received overwhelming congressional support initially; [[actors/jefferson-thomas]] and [[actors/gallatin-albert]] administered it with authority granted by legislation, though enforcement proved especially difficult in Massachusetts and the Northeast. [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]]

- **Jefferson regarded the judiciary as obstructive in the Burr case but it rarely affected ordinary government operations.** “while Jefferson regarded the judiciary as obstructive in the case of Aaron Burr, rarely did it affect the ordinary operations of the government.” (Introduction). This occurred in the context of [[events/burr-conspiracy-1806-1807]] involving [[actors/burr-aaron]]. [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]]

- **On March 4, 1805, Thomas Jefferson was inaugurated for a second term under unpropitious circumstances, with the ceremony overshadowed by the Chase impeachment trial and Aaron Burr's valedictory.** “WHEN Thomas Jefferson was inaugurated as President of the United States for the second time — on Monday, March 4, 1805 — the echoes of the impeachment trial of Justice Samuel Chase, which ended on the previous Friday, had not yet died away. And the capital city was still talking of the extraordinary valedictory of Aaron Burr on Saturday...” (Presidential Re-entry; also Chronology). No quorum of either House was present; the address was only partly audible. (See [[places/washington-dc]]). [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]]

- **Jefferson's second inaugural address recognized apprehensions about territorial enlargement while defending the federative principle and devoting disproportionate attention to Indian policy.** “‘But,’ he asked, ‘who can limit the extent to which the federative principle may operate effectively? The larger our association, the less will it be shaken by local passions...’” (Presidential Re-entry). He spoke as a nationalist against localists and obliquely condemned ultra-conservative resistance to change among the Indians. [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]]

- **In the address Jefferson portrayed "crafty individuals" among the Indians who opposed progress by exalting tradition over reason.** “These persons inculcate a sanctimonious reverence for the customs of their ancestors; that whatsoever they did, must be done through all time; that reason is a false guide, and to advance under its counsel, in their physical, moral, or political condition, is perilous innovation...” (Presidential Re-entry). This marked one of his most striking public condemnations of the ultra-conservative mind. [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]]

- **Jefferson determined that his second term would be his last, following Washington's precedent of voluntary retirement after eight years.** “Genl. Washington set the example of voluntary retirement after 8 years. I shall follow it, and a few more precedents will oppose the obstacle of habit to anyone after a while who shall endeavor to extend his term.” (Presidential Re-entry; from early letter and context). He let intentions be known privately rather than announcing publicly. [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]]

- **Personal attacks on Jefferson's character and morals, revived from James Thomson Callender's charges, were publicly exhibited in the Massachusetts legislature and Federalist press around the second inauguration.** “The malicious charges originally made by James Thomson Callender had been exhumed in Massachusetts and exhibited in the legislature there amid the gloatings of the Federalist press.” (Presidential Re-entry). These focused on events from decades earlier and had no bearing on his presidential conduct. [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]]

- **On July 1, 1805, Jefferson privately acknowledged improper conduct toward Mrs. John Walker as a young man while denying all other charges of immorality.** “He privately acknowledges improper conduct toward Mrs. John Walker and denies all other charges of immorality.” (Chronology, July 1, 1805; elaborated in Presidential Re-entry). This admission was made in a letter to [[actors/smith-robert]]. [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]]

- **Thomas Ritchie published a "Vindication of Mr. Jefferson" in the Richmond Enquirer (August–September 1805) defending Jefferson's conduct as Virginia governor against charges of cowardice and treason during the Revolution.** “The 'Vindication' was based on documentary materials that were collected by Jefferson's friends... Ritchie amply demonstrated that the onetime Governor was not a 'dastardly traitor,' and that his conduct was not 'dictated by timidity.'” (Presidential Re-entry). Materials were gathered and attested by William A. Burwell and Isaac Coles. [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]]

- **During the "blessed interim" after inauguration, Jefferson devoted time at Monticello to his moldboard plow invention of least resistance, sending models domestically and abroad.** “in the spring and summer of 1805 his cherished invention diverted his mind, and the reception of it then and thereafter by the sort of people he valued most — farmers and savants — warmed his heart.” (Blessed Interim). He also described the executive as “but a machine, erected by the Constitution for the performance of certain acts according to laws of action laid down for me” when justifying an appointment decision to Benjamin Rush. [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]]

- **The treaty of peace with Tripoli (dated June 4, 1805) was concluded under Commodore Rodgers after earlier operations by Preble; it provided for prisoner release in return for a $60,000 ransom and required evacuation of Derne.** “In June, 1805, a treaty of peace brought to an end the war with Tripoli.” (A Minor Success; Chronology Sept. 6). Jefferson informed the Senate; the Senate consented April 12, 1806. This resolved [[events/tripolitan-war-1801-1805]] but drew partisan criticism over terms involving Hamet Caramalli. [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]]

- **On November 27, 1806, Jefferson issued a proclamation warning against an illegal expedition against Spanish possessions after receiving communications from Wilkinson.** “TJ receives communications from Wilkinson revealing a projected expedition against the territories of Spain... 27 TJ issues a proclamation warning against an illegal expedition against Spanish possessions.” (Chronology). This occurred amid the developing [[events/burr-conspiracy-1806-1807]] involving [[actors/wilkinson-james]] and [[actors/burr-aaron]]. [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]]

- **On March 5, 1806, a speech by John Randolph in the House opened a breach with the administration.** “Mar. 5 A speech of John Randolph in the House opens a breach with the administration.” (Chronology). This involved [[actors/randolph-john-of-roanoke]]. [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]]

- **The trial of Aaron Burr began in Richmond on May 22, 1807; Burr was acquitted of treason on September 1, 1807, and of misdemeanor on September 15.** “22 The trial of Aaron Burr begins in Richmond. ... Sept. 1 Burr is acquitted of treason. ... 15 Burr is acquitted of misdemeanor.” (Chronology). These events formed the core of [[events/burr-conspiracy-1806-1807]] and [[actors/burr-aaron]]'s case; [[actors/jefferson-thomas]] sent special messages on Burr (e.g., Jan. 22, 1807). [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]]

- **Early in August 1805 at Monticello, Jefferson learned that James Monroe's mission to Spain had completely failed.** “Early in August, 1805, while at Monticello, Jefferson learned that James Monroe's mission to Spain had completely failed. ... Spanish affairs were his chief concern while he was preparing for the next congressional session.” (Paris, Madrid, and Washington 1804-1805). This marked the start of a prolonged period of unsettlement. The mission's objects included settling Louisiana boundaries (with the Perdido as a fixed claim), acquiring the Floridas, and addressing spoliation claims; the claim to the Perdido boundary “had become a fixed idea with Monroe, Jefferson, and Madison.” (See [[actors/monroe-james]], [[actors/jefferson-thomas]], [[actors/madison-james]]). [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]]

- **Gallatin complained to Jefferson of the excessive cost of the navy under Robert Smith and predicted no progress toward a navy while the situation continued.** “Whatever the cause may be, I dare predict that whilst that state of things continues we will have no navy, nor shall progress towards having one.” (A Minor Success). This reflected tensions within the administration regarding naval administration. [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]]

- **Jefferson took the oath of office from Chief Justice John Marshall on March 4, 1805, in a crowded but sparsely attended Senate chamber.** “He took the oath of office from Chief Justice Marshall and delivered his address in a crowded Senate chamber, but no quorum of either House was present.” (Presidential Re-entry). (See [[actors/marshall-john]]). [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]]

- **States were gravely endangered at New Orleans; Jefferson frequently traveled between Washington and Monticello during this period.** “States were gravely endangered at New Orleans...” (Introduction); multiple entries such as “14 He leaves Washington for Monticello” and returns (Chronology, 1805 onward). (See [[places/new-orleans]], [[places/washington-dc]], [[places/monticello]]). [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]]

- **The embargo was approved December 22, 1807; Jefferson recommended it to Congress and was later confined with illness during enforcement debates.** “18 TJ recommends an embargo to Congress... 22 The embargo is approved.” (Chronology). This enacted [[events/embargo-1807]]. Later: “By or before this date the election of Madison as President is assured” and enforcement acts passed in 1808–1809. [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]]

- **On June 22, 1807, the Leopard-Chesapeake affair occurred; Jefferson issued his Chesapeake proclamation on July 2.** “22 The Leopard-Chesapeake affair occurs... July 2 TJ issues his Chesapeake proclamation.” (Chronology). This directly precipitated [[events/chesapeake-leopard-affair-1807]]. [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]]

- **On December 10, 1807, Jefferson explicitly stated he would not serve another term; Madison was inaugurated March 4, 1809, after which the embargo was partially repealed.** “10 He explicitly states that he will not serve another term as President.” and “4 Madison is inaugurated President... Embargo laws are repealed except as they relate to Britain and France...” (Chronology). [[actors/jefferson-thomas]]'s leadership style was described as distinctly personal and difficult to transmit. - Jefferson expressed personal fatigue with the presidency and viewed George Washington's precedent of rotation in office as obligatory, noting that if Aaron Burr had been elected in 1801 he might have sought to prolong his term. This stance by [[actors/jefferson-thomas]] (see also [[hubs/biographies/age-of-expansion/jefferson-thomas]]) was reinforced by the perceived threat to the Union from Burr's western activities and the desire to discountenance perpetuity in office. [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]]

- Jefferson received news of the Lewis and Clark expedition's arrival in St. Louis "with unspeakable joy" in autumn 1806, describing it as his greatest success as patron of western exploration. "I received with unspeakable joy the news..." (TJ to Lewis, Oct. 26, 1806). The expedition, authorized by Congress in 1803 on his request before the [[events/louisiana-purchase-1803]], was his major achievement in promoting charting of the trans-Mississippi waterways, despite his own limited western travel. [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]]; see [[actors/jefferson-thomas]].

- The Lewis and Clark expedition was planned and authorized prior to the Louisiana Purchase, with Jefferson inquiring of Spanish Minister Yrujo in late 1802 whether Spain would object to a Missouri-to-Pacific party framed as commercial but actually geographical. "The President has been all his life a man of letters, very speculative and a lover of glory..." (Yrujo to Cevallos, Dec. 2, 1802). Jefferson procured passports from Britain and France and obtained a $2500 congressional appropriation explicitly "for the purpose of extending the foreign commerce of the United States." This action by [[actors/jefferson-thomas]] predated and was independent of the [[events/louisiana-purchase-1803]]. [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]].

- Jefferson personally drafted the comprehensive instructions for Meriwether Lewis (finalized June 20, 1803, after Cabinet review), emphasizing latitude/longitude observations (suggesting birchbark for records), soil/animals/vegetables/minerals, extinct animal remains, wind/weather, Indian languages/diseases/morals, and friendly/conciliatory treatment of natives, while allowing an all-water route hope to the Pacific. "We value too much the lives of citizens to offer them to probable destruction..." (instructions, with additions from Gallatin and Lincoln). Gallatin urged broader observation of Missouri branches for settlement potential. This reflected [[actors/jefferson-thomas]]'s scientific and commercial priorities in conjunction with the [[events/louisiana-purchase-1803]]. [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]]; see [[actors/gallatin-albert]].

- Jefferson described Meriwether Lewis's qualifications in detail: "Of courage undaunted, possessing a firmness & perseverance of purpose which nothing but impossibilities could divert..." (sketch in TJ letter to Paul Allen, Aug. 18, 1813). Lewis (from Albemarle County) received training in Philadelphia from Barton, Wistar, Patterson, and Rush; Jefferson also consulted Ellicott. This selection and preparation by [[actors/jefferson-thomas]] enabled the expedition's success. [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]].

- In July 1804 (coinciding with the Burr-Hamilton duel), a delegation of 12 Osage men and 2 boys (described by Jefferson as the most gigantic he had seen, unused to liquor) arrived in [[places/washington-dc]]; Jefferson welcomed them warmly as "children" and "one family," inviting them to visit Philadelphia and New York, providing guides and gifts. "We are all now of one family, born in the same land, & bound to live as brothers..." (formal address, July 16, 1804, after initial welcome). This aligned with his instructions to Lewis and policy of gaining Indian friendship/trade. [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]]; see [[actors/jefferson-thomas]].

- Lewis sent specimens from the Mandan country (animal skins/horns/skeletons, minerals, plants, 4 magpies, a "burrowing squirel," a prairie hen) that reached Jefferson at [[places/monticello]]/[[places/washington-dc]] in 1805; Jefferson directed their distribution—minerals/plants to the American Philosophical Society, live specimens and some to Charles Willson Peale's museum, horns/skins/utensils kept for his Indian Hall at [[places/monticello]]. (Invoice and letters, Apr. 1805, received July; instructions to Lemaire). This fulfilled the scientific objectives set by [[actors/jefferson-thomas]]. [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]].

- On Feb. 19, 1806, Jefferson transmitted to Congress a map of the Missouri (to the Mandans), Lewis's statistical view of Plains Indians (location/population/activities), and related materials from other expeditions, describing the explorers' progress and noting they had "traced the Missouri nearly to its source, descended the Columbia to the Pacific ocean..." (Message of Feb. 19, 1806; annual message Dec. 2, 1806). The statistical view surpassed prior government knowledge for trade purposes. [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]]; see [[actors/jefferson-thomas]].

- Jefferson authorized William Dunbar (with Dr. George Hunter) to explore the Washita (Ouachita) River to Hot Springs, Arkansas, in Oct. 1804 (returning midwinter); he transmitted extracts and map to Congress in 1806 as partial fulfillment of broader plans for Red/Arkansas exploration. (TJ to Dunbar, Mar. 13 and July 17, 1804; Message of Feb. 19, 1806). This was one of the minor expeditions he dispatched alongside the major Lewis and Clark effort tied to the [[events/louisiana-purchase-1803]]. [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]].

- The Freeman-Custis expedition up the Red River (spring 1806, ~24 men) was intercepted ~600 miles upriver by Spanish forces and forced to retire; Jefferson reported Spanish encroachment and withdrawal to the Sabine in his Dec. 2, 1806 message. (Claiborne to TJ, Mar. 26, 1806; expedition details). This highlighted ongoing boundary uncertainties post-[[events/louisiana-purchase-1803]]. [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]].

- Meriwether Lewis's Sept. 23, 1806 letter (received by Jefferson Oct. 24, 1806) and William Clark's letter (published Oct. 9, 1806, drafted by Lewis) detailed the return journey, naming the three forks of the Missouri the Jefferson, Madison, and Gallatin; noting laborious portage at the falls, "almost inexcessable" mountains, subsistence on berries/dog meat at times, and 3555 miles total. (Lewis to TJ; Clark letter via brother). Jefferson made the Lewis summary available semi-officially to Samuel Harrison Smith for the National Intelligencer. [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]]; see [[actors/gallatin-albert]].

- Jefferson addressed Indian delegations (including Mandan chief Sheheke and Osages) in [[places/washington-dc]] on Dec. 30-31, 1806, with paternal good will, exhorting peace among themselves and with whites; some visitors danced at the theatre. (Addresses in Padover; National Intelligencer reports). He had earlier addressed Osages in 1804. [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]]; see [[actors/jefferson-thomas]].

- Congress passed compensation for the expedition (Act of Mar. 3, 1807): double pay for members, 1600 acres land west of the Mississippi for Lewis and Clark, 320 acres each for followers; Jefferson supported the expenditure as investment in knowledge and the country's future despite his general frugality. (Debates Jan.-Feb. 1807; final account in Jackson). [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]].

- Jefferson nominated Meriwether Lewis as governor of Louisiana Territory (approved); William Clark received a commission as Indian agent for the Territory (annual salary $1500) and brigadier general of militia, though his promotion to lieutenant colonel was rejected by the Senate (20-9) due to seniority concerns. (Nominations Feb. 28, 1807; Senate action Mar. 2; Dearborn to Clark Mar. 9, 1807). [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]]; see [[actors/jefferson-thomas]].

- Efforts to return Mandan chief Sheheke (who accompanied Lewis) faced major obstacles; a 1807 expedition with Clark turned back due to Arikara hostility (death of their chief in Washington); reconveyance succeeded only after Jefferson left office at high cost (~$10,000 total) via St. Louis Fur Company agreement arranged by Lewis. (Clark reports; agreement Feb. 24, 1809; Pryor letter). This caused later frustrations for Lewis. [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]].

- In fall 1807, William Clark directed digging at Big Bone Lick (Kentucky) for Jefferson, shipping numerous mammoth/elephant bones (plus tusk/femur for [[places/monticello]]); Jefferson sent most duplicates to the Institute of France along with a mountain ram horn and "fleecy goat" skin from the expedition. (Clark lists Oct./Nov. 1807; TJ to Lacepede July 14, 1808; Wistar correspondence). [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]]; see [[actors/jefferson-thomas]].

- Zebulon Pike sent Jefferson two grizzly cubs captured near the continental divide (autumn 1807); Jefferson forwarded them to Peale's museum, describing them as gentle (though one became cross when caged separately); they later escaped, were killed, and stuffed for exhibition. (Pike to TJ Oct. 29, 1807; TJ to Peale Nov. 6, 1807 and Feb. 6, 1808). Jefferson viewed them optimistically. [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]].

- Lewis announced plans (Mar. 14, 1807 prospectus in National Intelligencer and papers) for a three-volume work: narrative with map, geography/Indians, scientific/natural history; a Philadelphia printer issued a detailed prospectus; the journals remained largely unpublished in Jefferson's lifetime (Biddle/Allen edition 1814; full Thwaites 1904-05). [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]].

- Jefferson stated he received the "very first intimation of the plot" in a letter from George Morgan (received at [[places/monticello]] Sept. 15, 1806) describing Burr's visit and loose talk of western independence; this was reinforced by letters from John Nicholson and Gideon Granger (conveying William Eaton's disclosures of Burr offering him second command under Wilkinson for separation of western states). (TJ to Morgan Mar. 26, 1807; Granger Oct. 16, 1806; Eaton details). This crystallized awareness of the [[events/burr-conspiracy-1806-1807]]. [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]]; see [[actors/jefferson-thomas]], [[actors/burr-aaron]], [[actors/wilkinson-james]].

- Background meetings between Aaron Burr and [[actors/wilkinson-james]] occurred in 1804 (Philadelphia/New York, post-Weehawken); Burr later toured the West (1805), conferred with critics of the territorial government and Morales in New Orleans, and intrigued with British minister Merry and Spanish Yrujo for western separation or Mexico attack. (Merry dispatches; Burr letters to Wilkinson Dec. 1805/Jan. 1806). Wilkinson had resumed Spanish payments (~112,000 pesos) in 1804 as "Number 13." [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]]; see [[actors/burr-aaron]].

- Jefferson appointed [[actors/wilkinson-james]] governor of Louisiana Territory and Joseph Browne (Burr's brother-in-law) secretary (Mar. 1805 recess appointments, later confirmed narrowly); earlier appointments included James Brown and J. B. Prevost (Burr stepson) in Orleans Territory. (Act Mar. 3, 1805; commissions Mar. 11-13, 1805). Wilkinson received $2000/year + brigadier pay. [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]]; see [[actors/jefferson-thomas]], [[actors/burr-aaron]].

- Joseph H. Daveiss (Federalist US attorney Kentucky) warned Jefferson (letters received Feb./Mar. 1806, including list of 10 suspects starting with Breckinridge and ending with Harrison, plus Burr/Wilkinson) of a western plot; Daveiss charged Wilkinson as Spanish pensioner and linked Burr's 1805 western journey. (Daveiss to TJ Jan. 10/Feb. 10/July 14, 1806). Jefferson consulted [[actors/gallatin-albert]] and [[actors/smith-robert]] (via Dearborn) but discounted Daveiss as partisan. [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]].

- [[actors/gallatin-albert]] assessed Wilkinson (Feb. 12, 1806) as "extravagant and needy" who might sacrifice US interests for popularity in Louisiana but "of betraying his to a foreign country I believe him to be altogether incapable." (Gallatin to TJ). Jefferson relied on this and Dearborn's views over Daveiss. [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]]; see [[actors/gallatin-albert]].

- [[actors/randolph-john-of-roanoke]] introduced House resolutions (Feb. 24, 1806) on plurality of offices (last: no army/navy officer to hold civil office); bill passed Apr. 11 (64-34), aimed at Wilkinson (Senate postponed); supported by Eppes and T. M. Randolph. (Annals debates). Jefferson had earlier opposed similar combination for Mississippi Territory. [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]]; see [[actors/randolph-john-of-roanoke]].

- Jefferson reassured Samuel Smith (May 4, 1806) that he repented only the Lucas appointment and had "no doubt that I could have made a fitter appointment than General Wilkinson," attributing troubles to enemies and viewing Upper Louisiana as a "military station." (TJ to Smith). Smith praised Wilkinson highly in reply. [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]].

- Dearborn (in President's name) ordered Wilkinson (May 6, 1806) to proceed quickly from Louisiana Territory to Orleans Territory to command forces amid Spanish threats; Wilkinson delayed nearly all summer (citing wife's illness, accounts, Indian affairs) and sent Osages home via Pike instead. (Dearborn to Wilkinson; Wilkinson to Dearborn Aug. 2, 1806). This was interpreted by Smith/Wilkinson as victory for enemies. [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]].

- Wilkinson dispatched communications (via Lt. Thomas A. Smith, received by Jefferson Nov. 25, 1806) including an unsigned "anonymous" paper and confidential Oct. 21 letter describing a powerful association rendezvousing 8-10,000 men near Ohio rapids for descent to New Orleans then Vera Cruz/Mexico (British naval support expected); he vowed to compromise with Spaniards and defend New Orleans. (Wilkinson dispatches Oct. 20-21, 1806; "Confidential"). He identified no names but implied overtures to himself. [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]]; see [[actors/wilkinson-james]], [[events/burr-conspiracy-1806-1807]].

- Jefferson issued a proclamation Nov. 27, 1806 warning against "sundry persons" fitting out vessels, collecting arms/provisions, seducing citizens, and organizing a military enterprise against Spanish dominions (citing 1794 statute); it enjoined officials to seize property and prosecute, without naming Burr or alleging domestic separation. (Proclamation; Ford VIII). This followed cabinet meetings (Oct. 22, 24, 25) and preceded the annual message. [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]]; see [[actors/jefferson-thomas]], [[events/burr-conspiracy-1806-1807]].

- At cabinet meetings (Oct. 22-25, 1806), Jefferson and heads (including Madison, Gallatin) discussed border defense (Sabine, Spanish at Bayou Pierre/Arroyo Hondo, Freeman expedition turnback) and Burr's western preparations (boat-building on Ohio); they agreed on confidential warnings to western governors/attorneys for strict watch and arrest on overt act (treason/misdemeanor), plus sending John Graham; Wilkinson was under "very general suspicion" but action deferred. (TJ memos; Eaton/Granger disclosures). Gunboat dispatch was considered but limited by funds. [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]]; see [[actors/gallatin-albert]], [[actors/burr-aaron]], [[actors/wilkinson-james]].

- Wilkinson negotiated and signed the Neutral Ground Agreement (Nov. 5, 1806) with Spanish commander Herrera: Americans behind Arroyo Hondo, Spanish behind Sabine, intervening territory neutral pending diplomacy; this removed immediate border conflict before fresh US instructions (which conceded Sabine as temporary line) arrived. (Wilkinson-Herrera agreement). Wilkinson then proceeded toward New Orleans. [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]]; see [[actors/wilkinson-james]].

- Jefferson received Wilkinson's dispatches with "high satisfaction" on border resolution and conspiracy revelation; he focused on the immediate threat (doubting full British support and 8-10k figure), viewed Wilkinson as now aligned with government (despite past record), and called ministers to decide actions before issuing the proclamation. (Receipt Nov. 25, 1806; subsequent deliberations). [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]]; see [[actors/jefferson-thomas]], [[actors/wilkinson-james]], [[events/burr-conspiracy-1806-1807]].

## Range 04 Claims

- Jefferson made only a brief reference to the Burr expedition in his annual address to Congress on or about December 2, 1806 (nearly a month after his November 27 proclamation), without using the word "conspiracy" or naming supposed conspirators. Verbatim: "his only direct reference to the enterprise was brief; and he neither used the word "conspiracy" nor named supposed conspirators." Locator: annual message of Dec. 2, 1806 (Ford, VIII, 489-490); see also Annals, 9 Cong., 2 sess., pp. 915-925. Interpretation: [[actors/jefferson-thomas]] publicly emphasized restraint and the government's commitment to peace rather than alarm over [[events/burr-conspiracy-1806-1807]], communicating documents focused on the Spanish border situation while keeping recent Wilkinson revelations confidential. [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]]

- British Minister David Erskine reported to his government that belief in western separatist conspiracies involving Burr was "universally prevalent" in the capital, and that Jefferson had adopted "such strong measures" only on the basis of convincing information. Verbatim: "the belief was 'universally prevalent' that there were conspiracies for the separation of the western states and that Burr was involved in these." Locator: Erskine to Fox, Dec. 4, 1806 (FO, 5:52 [1]). Interpretation: External observers viewed [[actors/jefferson-thomas]]'s proclamation and actions as evidence-based responses to [[events/burr-conspiracy-1806-1807]] rather than overreaction. [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]]

- Senator William Plumer described Burr's talent for conveying impressions without explicit statements, noting that "in every thing he said or did, he had a design" and his language was "apparently explicit, and at the same time so covert and indefinite." Verbatim: "I found he possessed the talent of making an impression of an opinion upon the subject, on the person with whom he conversed, without explicitly stating or necessarily giving his sentiments thereon." Locator: Plumer observations following the proclamation (Memorandum, p. 539 and related entries). Interpretation: Highlighted the difficulty of proving [[actors/burr-aaron]]'s exact objectives in [[events/burr-conspiracy-1806-1807]] despite widespread reports. [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]]

- Burr purchased a claim (via Charles Lynch of Kentucky) to some 350,000 acres on the Washita (Ouachita) River, based on an old Spanish grant to the Baron de Bastrop whose conditions had not been met; tools were provided but no seeds or agricultural implements. Verbatim: "From Charles Lynch of Kentucky the adventurer purchased a claim to some 350,000 acres of land on the Washita (Ouachita) River." Locator: fall 1806 actions described in context of Jefferson's later interpretation. Interpretation: Served as ostensible pretext for [[actors/burr-aaron]]'s preparations in [[events/burr-conspiracy-1806-1807]], allowing recruitment under cover of settlement while the real designs were elsewhere. [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]]

- Jefferson interpreted Burr's plans (as of the proclamation period) as contemplating two distinct objects that might be pursued jointly or separately: "the severance of the union of these States by the Allegany mountains" or "an attack on Mexico," with the Washita settlement as a mere "ostensible" pretext. Verbatim: "One of these was the severance of the union of these States by the Allegany mountains; the other an attack on Mexico. A third object was provided, merely ostensible, to wit the settlement of a pretended purchase of a tract of country on the Washita, claimed by a Baron Bastrop." Locator: message of Jan. 22, 1807 to Congress (Annals, 9 Cong., 2 sess., pp. 39-43; AS.P., Misc., 1,468). Interpretation: [[actors/jefferson-thomas]] viewed [[actors/burr-aaron]]'s enterprise in [[events/burr-conspiracy-1806-1807]] as having major treasonable and expansionist dimensions beyond any legitimate settlement. [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]]

- Ohio Governor Edward Tiffin, acting after receiving information via John Graham and before full federal instructions, obtained legislative authority and on December 9, 1806, seized ten boats and some two hundred barrels of supplies on the Muskingum River (plus others in the boatyard), materially disabling the expedition before it could fully assemble. Verbatim: "On orders from the Governor ten boats were intercepted on the river on December 9 and others, some of them unfinished, were seized in the boatyard. Some two hundred barrels of supplies were confiscated." Locator: Dec. 9, 1806 actions in Ohio (Plumer entry of Dec. 24; TJ to Tiffin, Feb. 2, 1807). Interpretation: Local state action under [[actors/jefferson-thomas]]'s prior warning delivered the "lethal blow" to [[events/burr-conspiracy-1806-1807]] at its origin, reducing the flotilla to about ten boats and one hundred men by the time [[actors/burr-aaron]] joined it. [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]]

- A federal grand jury in Kentucky on December 5, 1806 (after examining testimony), declined to return a true bill against Burr or former Senator John Adair. Verbatim: "a federal grand jury declined to return a true bill either against him or former Senator John Adair, who was deeply implicated in his schemes." Locator: Dec. 5, 1806 Kentucky proceedings (known in Washington by Dec. 24; Abernethy account). Interpretation: Early judicial failure to indict aided [[actors/burr-aaron]] in [[events/burr-conspiracy-1806-1807]] by strengthening his position in local opinion and foreshadowing later difficulties in establishing illegality. [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]]

- By late December 1806 and early January 1807, [[actors/jefferson-thomas]] expressed to Plumer and in letters to Wilkinson that the conspiracy would be put down without much trouble, that the enterprise was already crushed, and that western officials (especially Ohio) had effectively stopped it before it reached [[places/new-orleans]]. Verbatim: "he had no doubt the conspiracy would be put down without much trouble or expense despite its extensive-ness"; "the enterprise was already crushed." Locator: Dec. 27, 1806 dinner conversation with Plumer (Memorandum, p. 544); TJ to Wilkinson, Jan. 3, 1807 (Ford, IX, 1-3). Interpretation: [[actors/jefferson-thomas]] credited timely warnings and state action for neutralizing [[events/burr-conspiracy-1806-1807]] with minimal federal military involvement. [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]]

- On January 16, 1807, John Randolph introduced a House resolution (passed in amended form) calling on the President for information on illegal combinations against the Union or expeditions against powers at peace with the U.S.; the first part passed 109-14, the second (after striking proposed measures language) 67-52. Verbatim: resolution text and votes in Annals, 9 Cong., 2 sess., pp. 334-359. Locator: House proceedings Jan. 16, 1807. Interpretation: Reflected impatience rather than panic; [[actors/randolph-john-of-roanoke]] and others sought authoritative information beyond newspapers while testing confidence in the executive during [[events/burr-conspiracy-1806-1807]]. [[actors/jefferson-thomas]] did not view it as a rebuke. [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]]

- In a private letter of January 11, 1807 to Rev. Charles Clay, [[actors/jefferson-thomas]] described Burr's enterprise as "the most extraordinary since the days of Don Quixot," with intent to place himself "on the throne of Montezuma, and extend his empire to the Allegany seizing on N Orleans as the instrument of compulsion for our Western States." Verbatim: "Burr's enterprise is the most extraordinary since the days of Don Quixot. ... He has meant to place himself on the throne of Montezuma, and extend his empire to the Allegany seizing on N Orleans as the instrument of compulsion for our Western States." Locator: TJ to Rev. Charles Clay, Jan. 11, 1807 (Ford, IX, 7). Interpretation: Confirmed [[actors/jefferson-thomas]]'s view of the scale and intent of [[events/burr-conspiracy-1806-1807]] as both separatist and expansionist. [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]]

- The cipher letter from [[actors/burr-aaron]] to [[actors/wilkinson-james]] dated July 29, 1806 (received in duplicate via Swartwout and Bollman) was transmitted by Jefferson to Congress with his January 22, 1807 message as key evidence; it stated funds obtained, rendezvous on the Ohio, "Protection of England is secured," "Wilkinson shall be second to Burr only," and the objective of Mexico. Verbatim: "I, Aaron Burr, have obtained funds, and have actually commenced the enterprise. ... Wilkinson shall be second to Burr only. ... The gods invite to glory and fortune." Locator: transmitted in message of Jan. 22, 1807 (Annals, 9 Cong., 2 sess., pp. 39-43; 1008-1012); Wilkinson had modified wording (e.g., omitting prior correspondence). Interpretation: Provided the "most damning document" placing [[actors/burr-aaron]]'s guilt "beyond question" in [[actors/jefferson-thomas]]'s public statement for [[events/burr-conspiracy-1806-1807]]. [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]]

- In his January 22, 1807 special message to Congress, [[actors/jefferson-thomas]] praised Ohio's prompt action, dismissed foreign power involvement, described Wilkinson as acting "with the honor of a soldier and fidelity of a good citizen," and referred to the "principal actor" whose guilt was "placed beyond question" while noting the expedition posed no grave threat to [[places/new-orleans]]. Verbatim: "the General acted 'with the honor of a soldier and fidelity of a good citizen'"; Burr's guilt "placed beyond question." Locator: special message of Jan. 22, 1807 (Annals, 9 Cong., 2 sess.). Interpretation: [[actors/jefferson-thomas]] sought to reassure rather than alarm while using the cipher evidence against [[actors/burr-aaron]] in [[events/burr-conspiracy-1806-1807]], though later criticized for prejudgment. [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]]

- The Senate hastily passed a bill (Jan. 23, 1807) suspending the privilege of the writ of habeas corpus for three months in cases of persons charged with treason etc. on presidential or state executive authority; the House rejected it the same day by 113-19 after debate emphasizing that the danger did not warrant suspension. Verbatim: bill text (Annals, 9 Cong., 2 sess., p. 402); "The vote, 113 to 19, represented an overwhelming victory for the freedom of the individual." Locator: Senate passage and House rejection Jan. 23, 1807 proceedings. Interpretation: [[actors/jefferson-thomas]] was not shown to have requested the measure (introduced by Giles); the rejection underscored commitment to individual liberty over temporary security measures tied to [[events/burr-conspiracy-1806-1807]] arrests by [[actors/wilkinson-james]]. [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]]

- Dr. Erick Bollman (after interview with [[actors/jefferson-thomas]] and [[actors/madison-james]]) admitted Burr's plan to invade Mexico and seize French artillery/shipping in [[places/new-orleans]] but denied any intention to separate the Union or revolutionize Louisiana; he expressed resentment toward Wilkinson and suggested U.S. government support for the enterprise. Verbatim: "He left no possible doubt of Burr's plan to invade Mexico"; "Bollman ... gave very damaging testimony" against Wilkinson. Locator: interview Jan. 25, 1807 and subsequent written statement (Madison notes; LC 28865-28870; Bollman to TJ Jan. 26). Interpretation: Bolstered view of illegal designs in [[events/burr-conspiracy-1806-1807]] while highlighting Wilkinson's unexpected defection and complicating legal handling of emissaries. [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]]

- Wilkinson caused the arrest and military transport (circumventing local habeas corpus writs) of Burr emissaries Bollman, Swartwout, and others from [[places/new-orleans]] to eastern ports on his own authority; Jefferson later noted the high-handed nature but initially supported the actions as necessary. Verbatim: "Wilkinson, who was in fact acting on his own authority, had caused Burr's emissaries to be arrested and to be embarked for eastern ports"; "his sworn statements were submitted to Congress by Jefferson without critical comment." Locator: Jan. 22, 1807 message and related Feb. 1807 letters (L. & B., XI). Interpretation: Placed [[actors/jefferson-thomas]] in position of defending or pallating [[actors/wilkinson-james]]'s conduct during [[events/burr-conspiracy-1806-1807]] while limiting options for treason charges. [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]]

- In letters and later reflection (e.g., to J. B. Colvin, Sept. 20, 1810 from [[places/monticello]]), [[actors/jefferson-thomas]] elaborated the principle that on great occasions "every good officer must be ready to risk himself in going beyond the strict line of law, when the public preservation requires it," citing necessity, self-preservation, and salus populi over written law in Wilkinson's New Orleans actions. Verbatim: "a strict observance of the written laws is doubtless one of the high duties of a good citizen: but it is not the highest. The laws of necessity, of self-preservation, of saving our country when in danger, are of higher obligation." Locator: TJ to Claiborne and Wilkinson Feb. 3, 1807; detailed restatement to Colvin (L. & B., XII, 418-422). Interpretation: [[actors/jefferson-thomas]] condoned [[actors/wilkinson-james]]'s past high-handedness in suppressing [[events/burr-conspiracy-1806-1807]] while issuing warnings for future conduct. [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-second-term-1974]]

- Bollman and Swartwout were committed for treason (without bail) by the D.C. circuit court shortly after the habeas suspension bill rejection; two Republican appointees favored commitment while Federalist Judge William Cranch dissented, citing the need to uphold constitutional protections against executive power. ## Volume Synthesis Note

Malone presents Jefferson's second term as the more difficult and less glorious phase of his presidency, a "voyage" on "rougher seas" with "less favorable" winds than the first. The administration was largely on the defensive against the aggressions of the major European belligerents (Britain after Trafalgar, Napoleonic France), forced to choose between war, humiliating submission, or economic coercion. The Chesapeake-Leopard affair crystallized the choice and propelled the Embargo as the chosen instrument — an ambitious, theory-driven experiment in "peaceable coercion" that sought to withhold American commerce until the great powers respected neutral rights and ended impressment. Malone gives the Embargo unusually extended treatment, detailing its legislative origins, the mechanics of enforcement (bonds, collectors, gunboats, militia), the geographic and sectional difficulties (especially New England), the progressive tightening of the laws, and the growing political and economic costs. He portrays Jefferson as deeply committed to the policy as a patriotic national enterprise and a demonstration of republican resolve, yet ultimately acknowledging its limits as external powers ignored or exploited it and domestic resistance mounted.

The Burr conspiracy and Richmond trials form the major domestic drama. Malone reconstructs Jefferson's gathering of information (from Wilkinson, Daveiss, Morgan, Granger/Eaton), the proclamation of November 1806, the reliance on state action (Ohio's seizure of boats) and Wilkinson's high-handed measures in New Orleans, and the public assertion of Burr's guilt. He details the legal proceedings before Marshall — the habeas cases, the subpoena duces tecum to the President, the narrow construction of treason requiring an overt act proved by two witnesses to the same act — that produced acquittal on both treason and misdemeanor charges. Malone notes Jefferson's errors (gullibility toward Wilkinson, public prejudgment) and the episode's role in exposing executive-judiciary tensions and the limits of presidential power, while arguing that the President could not have done less once convinced the Union was at risk. The trials also produced broader reverberations (congressional inquiries into Wilkinson, failed attempts to reform judicial tenure and treason doctrine).

By the end of the term, Jefferson's influence within the government had declined (illustrated by the Senate's blocking of Gallatin's nomination as Secretary of State and the rejection of William Short without consulting the outgoing President). The Embargo was partially repealed just before Madison's inauguration. Malone closes with Jefferson's own reflections on retirement — fatigue with office, joy at returning to Monticello and "the pursuits of my choice," financial strain from presidential service, and a sense of having steered the "solitary republic" through an "awful" station among nations. The volume underscores the structural constraints on a republican executive (separation of powers, rudimentary party organization, a divided political community in Washington, and the overwhelming realities of European power) while portraying Jefferson as a leader who preserved party unity longer than expected, avoided war, and upheld his commitment to economy and peace even amid frustration. Malone's account positions the second term as a sobering but instructive test of whether republican principles could be maintained under adverse international conditions — a test that revealed both the strengths and the limits of Jefferson's approach and the young republic itself.
