# The World of the Neo-Hittite Kingdoms: A Political and Military History

Trevor Bryce's 2012 Oxford monograph is the first full single-author political and
military history of the **Syro-Hittite ("Neo-Hittite") states** — the cluster of small
Iron Age kingdoms that carried the Hittite-Luwian inheritance forward in south-east
Anatolia and north Syria after the [[bronze-age-collapse-1200bce|fall of the Hittite
Empire]] (~1200 BCE) until their absorption by [[neo-assyrian-empire|Assyria]] (late
8th century BCE). It complements Bryce's earlier [[sources/bryce-kingdom-of-the-hittites-2005|
Kingdom of the Hittites (2005)]] (the Late Bronze Age empire) and his
[[sources/bryce-handbook-peoples-places-western-asia-2009|Handbook (2009)]] gazetteer,
and supersedes the brief treatment in [[boardman-cah-v3p1-1982|CAH Vol. III, Part 1]]
(Hawkins) as the working narrative.

## Core Arguments

- **Continuity, not migration.** The Syro-Hittite states arose *within* the south-eastern
  zone of the former Hittite Empire, not from a population fleeing south; the apparent
  "shift" of the land of Khatti to Syria reflects the loss of the Anatolian heartland.
  Carchemish, whose viceregal line survived the collapse as "Great Kings," is the pivot
  of continuity. Aligns with [[liverani-ancient-near-east-2014|Liverani]] and Hawkins.
- **"Neo-Hittite" is a modern convenience, defined chiefly by Luwian hieroglyphic
  monumental writing and Anatolian dynastic onomastics** — a cultural, not an ethnic or
  political, unity. Bryce stresses how porous the boundary with the [[arameans|Aramaean]]
  states was (Hamath, Sam'al, Unqi shift between categories over time).
- **The Biblical "Hittites"** (the *Hittim* of the Hebrew Bible) reflect later Iron Age
  contact with this Syro-Hittite world, not the Bronze Age empire — Bryce devotes a
  chapter to disentangling the conflation.
- **The political history is essentially the history of Assyrian pressure**: a 12th–10th-c.
  window of autonomy, then progressive subjection (tribute under Ashurnasirpal II and
  Shalmaneser III, the [[battle-of-qarqar-853bce|coalition at Qarqar]]), then outright
  provincialization by Tiglath-pileser III and Sargon II (Carchemish annexed 717,
  Kummukh 708), after which the monumental Luwian tradition ceased.
