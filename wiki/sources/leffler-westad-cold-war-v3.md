# The Cambridge History of the Cold War, Vol. III: Endings (2010)

The final volume covers the **late Cold War and its astonishingly swift end (c. 1975–1991)**
— the breakdown of détente, the "second Cold War," and the Gorbachev revolution that
dissolved the Soviet bloc and then the USSR itself. Ingested to complete the wiki's
[[cold-war|Cold War]] structure.

## What it contributes

- **[[soviet-war-in-afghanistan-1979]]** — the war that buried détente and helped break the USSR.
- **[[revolutions-of-1989]]** — the collapse of the East European communist regimes.
- Enriches **[[1991-end-of-cold-war]]** (Gorbachev, German unification, the Soviet collapse)
  and the [[cold-war]] period page.

## Interpretation

The volume stresses the **contingency and speed** of the ending — an "incredibly swift
transition" few foresaw — and centres the role of **Mikhail Gorbachev**: it was Soviet
**tolerance** (the abandonment of the Brezhnev Doctrine and the willingness to let Eastern
Europe go without force) that made 1989 possible. It thus frames the Cold War's end less as a
Western "victory" by [[containment]] than as a transformation driven from within the Soviet
system.
