# Medieval Frontiers: Concepts and Practices

Edited collection published by Ashgate in 2002 (reprinted by Routledge, 2016),
originating in a colloquium held at St Catharine's College, Cambridge, in
November 1998, with several additional commissioned essays. Edited by **David
Abulafia** and **Nora Berend** of the University of Cambridge. Fourteen chapters
examine the medieval frontier not as a single identifiable phenomenon but as a
family of concepts and practices, deliberately widening earlier collections
(notably Bartlett & Mackay, *Medieval Frontier Societies*, 1989, and Power &
Standen, *Frontiers in Question*, 1999) to give fuller weight to the
Mediterranean and Byzantine worlds alongside the more familiar Anglo-Celtic and
German-East frontiers.

The central methodological claim, argued in Abulafia's long introduction, is
that the "medieval frontier" was **"not so much an identifiable phenomenon, a
hard fact, as ... a conceptual tool used by historians"** — a zone of ambiguity
rather than a line. The volume's polemical edge is anti-jargon: Abulafia insists
on understanding frontiers "using the political language of the time" and
explicitly rejects "liminality" and postmodern framing.

## Significance for the Wiki

The volume is the anchor source for the [[medieval-frontier|medieval frontier]]
concept page and materially strengthens coverage of several frontier zones that
sit in or near the collection's thin regions — the Baltic and Central-European
frontiers (Eastern Europe), the Genoese Black Sea, and the eastern-Atlantic
Canaries. It supplies the conceptual scaffolding (frontier as zone, not line;
frontier as ideological construct) that reorganises how the wiki treats the

## Volume Synthesis Note

Read as a whole, the collection makes three cumulative arguments. First, that
across Latin Christendom, Byzantium and Islam alike, political space around 1200
was organised by **overlapping lordships, strong points and zones of influence**
rather than by sovereign linear borders — a claim advanced independently by
Holmes (Byzantine east), Ellenblum (Latin Jerusalem) and Abulafia (western
Mediterranean enclaves), and only qualified where natural defences or treaty
lines produced sharper edges (Nasrid Granada, the Danish sea-border). Second,
that frontier ideologies — Hungary as *antemurale*, the Danish crusade against
the Wends, papal claims to dominion over islands — were **constructed** to serve
royal, ecclesiastical or civic power, not neutral descriptions of geography
(Berend, Jensen). Third, that the same interpretive tools reach from the
Christian–Muslim Mediterranean all the way to the "Neolithic meets Medieval"
encounter in the Canaries, tying the study of medieval frontiers directly to the
prehistory of European overseas expansion and 1492.

The volume's principal tension with material already in the wiki is its
insistence on **fluidity against the map**: where narrative and cartographic
sources encourage hard Reconquista or crusader-state boundaries, this collection
recovers the porous, negotiated, castle-anchored reality beneath them. Its
weakness is the one common to conference volumes — coverage is a mosaic of case
studies, not a systematic survey — which the editors acknowledge.
