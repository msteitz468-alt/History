# This Vast Enterprise: A New History of Lewis and Clark (Craig Fehrman, 2026)

## Overview

Fehrman's 2026 history reframes the Lewis and Clark expedition (Corps of Discovery, 1804–1806) not as the story of two heroic captains but as a "vast enterprise" — a sprawling, federally funded operation involving a broad and unruly ensemble of Americans, French traders, enslaved York, Shoshone Sacagawea (and her son Pahmpi/Jean-Baptiste), and numerous Native leaders and nations whose worlds already shaped the Missouri and Columbia basins.

Core arguments:
- Land was the central stake for everyone: Natives farming/hunting/trapping, American settlers and elites seeking soil, commerce, and preemption rights; Jefferson's scientific/diplomatic/geopolitical ambitions layered on top.
- Success depended on far more than Lewis and Clark. Clark himself called it "this vast enterprise."
- The book uses rotating point-of-view chapters to immerse readers in each participant's world and choices, revealing contingency, Native power (Lakota checkpoints and tolls pre-expedition), moments of genuine cross-cultural communication, and the expedition's limited long-term diplomatic fruits.
- Popular myths (Sacagawea as indispensable guide; seamless American triumph; Lewis and Clark as lone agents) are corrected with evidence from journals, trader records, and recent scholarship.

The work is explicitly corrective and narrative-driven, aiming for immersion in multiple "versions of that past."

## Major Developments Covered

- Planning and launch of the Corps of Discovery as Jefferson's project in the wake of the Louisiana Purchase.
- The expedition's actual conduct: river travel, portages, diplomacy at gunpoint or gift-giving, survival, mapping, natural history collection.
- Native diplomacy successes and failures (Osage, Yankton, Lakota, Arikara, Mandan, Shoshone, Salish, Nez Perce, Clatsop).
- The role of "supporting" figures: York (skilled hunter, cultural broker, enslaved man who leveraged the journey), Sacagawea (teen mother whose presence and Shoshone ties mattered more for symbolism and practical intelligence than guiding), French-Indian interpreters (Drouillard, Cruzatte, Charbonneau), sergeants (Ordway, Gass, Pryor).
- The return delegations and their mixed results.
- Immediate aftermath and the long process of turning experience into published narrative and national myth.

## Geographic Scope

Trans-Mississippi West: St. Louis and Camp River Dubois (Illinois side) as start; full Missouri ascent and descent; Marias, Great Falls, Lolo Trail, Columbia to the Pacific (Fort Clatsop area); return via same or variant routes. Key Native territories: Lakota (Teton Sioux), Arikara, Mandan/Hidatsa, Shoshone (Agaidika), Salish, Nez Perce (Nimiipuu), Clatsop, and others encountered.

## Transition / Context

Follows immediately on the Louisiana Purchase (1803) and Jefferson's presidency. Preceded by British (Mackenzie 1793) and Spanish probes. Contributes to the opening of the "Long 19th Century" processes of U.S. continental expansion, the fur trade's last phases, and evolving U.S.–Native relations before the major removal and reservation eras.

## Historiography

Fehrman positions the book against the dominant popular narrative (heroic captains, Sacagawea as guide/icon, Manifest Destiny inevitability). It draws on the full modern journal editions and recent work emphasizing Native perspectives, the expedition's diplomatic/military character, and the agency of non-captain participants. Strong on primary quotation and multiple viewpoints; lighter on quantitative economic or environmental history. As a 2026 work it incorporates the latest scholarship on topics such as Lewis family health, York’s post-expedition life, and Sacagawea’s contested biography and Shoshone/Hidatsa identity debates. Collection coverage of the early U.S. republic / trans-Mississippi exploration was previously weak to absent; this source supplies a high-quality modern narrative anchor.

## Collection Coverage Note

This is one of the first substantial narrative sources for the early national period in the north-america section of the collection. It fills a noted gap for post-1800 United States expansion and Native–U.S. contact in the interior West. Complementary sources (Jefferson materials, later Custer/West biographies, tax/political histories) exist but lack this granular, multi-POV treatment of the iconic expedition itself. Future ingests should add primary journals (if available) and studies of the fur trade, Louisiana Purchase diplomacy, and specific nations (Lakota, Nez Perce, etc.).
