# Suetonius, *Lives of the Caesars* (*De Vita Caesarum*)

The "Twelve Caesars": biographies of **Julius Caesar and the first eleven emperors**, from
**[[augustus|Augustus]]** to **[[domitian|Domitian]]**, written under Hadrian (c. 119–130s) by the
scholar-courtier **[[suetonius|Suetonius]]**. With **[[tacitus-annals|Tacitus]]** the principal literary
source for the early Empire — and the founding model of European biography of rulers. The fifth source
of this Phase-4 batch. For the author see **[[suetonius|Suetonius]]**.

## The Twelve Lives

- **[[julius-caesar|Julius Caesar]]** (opening chapters lost), **[[augustus|Augustus]]** — the longest
  and most admiring, the architect of the principate;
- **[[tiberius|Tiberius]]**, **[[caligula|Caligula]]** (the horse, the cruelty), **[[claudius|Claudius]]**,
  **[[nero|Nero]]** (the artist-tyrant, the Fire) — the Julio-Claudians;
- **Galba, Otho, Vitellius** — the brief emperors of the civil-war year 69;
- **[[vespasian|Vespasian]]**, **[[titus|Titus]]** ("the darling of the human race"),
  **[[domitian|Domitian]]** — the Flavians, the last under whom Suetonius himself had lived.

## Method: Biography by Rubric

Suetonius's signature is the **thematic life** (*per species*). Rather than tell a story, he sets out
"the main themes" of a reign and then catalogues the man — birth and omens, rise to power, then a
topical inventory of appearance, dress, household, generosity, cruelty, sexual conduct, learning,
building and games — culminating in the death and its portents. He **quotes documents** (Augustus's
letters, edicts, the wording of wills) that a narrative historian would paraphrase, and he reports the
**scandalous and the trivial** without subordinating them to a plot. Francis Bacon found the effect
disorienting: Nero's deeds "gathered into titles and bundles, and not in order of time… seem more
monstrous." Edwards argues this is to misread him: the *Lives* are not failed narrative but a distinct
form whose subject is the **character of imperial power**.

## Value and Cautions

The central historiographical question of the *Lives* is the one Suetonius's own century still debated:
**how should an emperor behave** — how can one man rule absolutely "yet not be king"? His answer is a
gallery of exemplars and warnings (the **Augustus/Titus** ideal against the **Caligula/Nero** monster)
that fixed the Western vocabulary for praising and damning rulers. As evidence he is double-edged: his
archival access preserves unique documentary and biographical detail, but his indifference to chronology
and his appetite for anecdote make him unreliable for the course of events — best read **against
[[tacitus-annals|Tacitus]]** and Cassius Dio.
