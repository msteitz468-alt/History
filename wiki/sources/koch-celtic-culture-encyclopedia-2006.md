# Celtic Culture: A Historical Encyclopedia

**Editor:** John T. Koch. Managing Editor: Marion Löffler. ABC-CLIO, 2006 (5-volume set). Vol I: A–Celticism; Vol II: Celtomania–Fulup, Marc’harid; Vol III: G–L.

## Book Note

*Celtic Culture: A Historical Encyclopedia* is the major modern multi-volume reference work for Celtic studies, produced as part of the long-term 'Celtic Languages and Cultural Identity: A Multidisciplinary Synthesis' (Celticity Project) at CAWCS, Aberystwyth, under Koch's direction. It deliberately bridges the gap between outdated specialist philological handbooks and semi-popular treatments by offering 1569 entries (100 to >10,000 words) with scholarly reliability and accessibility. Volume I (A–Celticism) covers a wide alphabetical range of topics from early Continental Iron Age peoples and sites, through Insular saints, texts, kingdoms, languages and literatures (Welsh, Irish, Scottish Gaelic, Breton, Cornish, Manx), to modern cultural revival, Acts of Union, Celticism, and the history of the field itself. Volume II extends into Celtomania, detailed regional Christianity (Ireland, Scotland periods, Isle of Man, Wales, Brittany, Cornwall), saints (Colum Cille, Columbanus), heroic and medieval literature (Cú Chulainn, Culhwch ac Olwen, cywyddwyr, dindshenchas), languages (Common Celtic, Continental Celtic), and modern movements. Volume III covers Gaelic language and culture, Gaeltacht, Galatia and Galatian language, Gaul/Gaulish, Geoffrey of Monmouth, Gildas, Giraldus Cambrensis, Gododdin, Goidelic, Grail legend, Greek and Roman accounts, and many more G–L topics in literature, mythology, and history.

The editor's Introduction (and the project description) is explicit about methodology and the post-1990s historiographical moment: the linguistic reality of the Celtic language family is treated as an unchallenged scientific fact (analogous to 'Germanic' or 'Semitic'); the application of 'Celtic' to material culture (La Tène artefacts, etc.) requires greater circumspection because one-to-one correspondence with speakers cannot be assumed. The working definition is 'peoples and countries that do, or once did, use Celtic languages', with place- and group-name evidence used where literary sources are thin. Proper names are given in the relevant Celtic language forms where practical (to counter 'invisibility' of the languages and for etymological value). The project supplies a 10,000-item Bibliography (Vol V) and unified cross-referencing.

This source is authoritative for Insular Celtic textual and linguistic topics, the framing of Celtic identity after Celtoscepticism, and the full scope of the field from Hallstatt/La Tène to the 21st century (including diaspora). It complements archaeological syntheses (Cunliffe *The Ancient Celts*, Champion et al. *Prehistoric Europe*) by supplying depth on language, literature, medieval and modern periods, and explicit reflection on the category 'Celtic'.

## Volume Synthesis Note

**Core contribution (R01–R02 Deployed Subagent extraction + main reconciliation, 2026-06-26):** John T. Koch (ed.), *Celtic Culture: A Historical Encyclopedia* (ABC-CLIO, 5 vols, 2006), Vol I (A–Celticism) supplies the authoritative modern reference framing and topic inventory for the entire field of Celtic studies. The editor's Introduction (the primary dense prose available for Vol I) explicitly responds to the late-20th/early-21st c. "Celtoscepticism" crisis in British archaeology: "the relatedness and common origins of the Celtic family of languages remain unchallenged scientific facts"; the name 'Celtic' is "no more misleading or historically unjustified than such well-established... terms as... 'Germanic' or 'Semitic'". Non-linguistic culture (La Tène style artefacts, weapons, ritual sites) "now requires greater circumspection" — "there are few, if any, types... for which it is likely, or even reasonable, to expect... a one-to-one correspondence between those who used them and speakers of Celtic languages". The working definition is "peoples and countries that do, or once did, use Celtic languages" (plus an "index of connectedness"); Celtic place- and group names are "diagnostic of Celticity"; the Encyclopedia supplies Celtic-language forms for etymological and visibility reasons. Chronological scope: Hallstatt/La Tène later pre-Roman Iron Age to 21st c. Geographic scope foregrounds Insular (Ireland, Britain, Brittany/Armorica) while covering Continental (Gaul, Iberia, central/eastern Europe, Galatians of Turkey) + diaspora (Americas, Australia). The work is a product of the Celticity Project ('The Celtic Languages and Cultural Identity: A Multidisciplinary Synthesis', CAWCS Aberystwyth, directed by Koch since 1998); 338 contributors, 1569 entries, 10k-item Bibliography (Vol V). "How to use" details small-caps cross-refs, unified TOC (Vol I), Index and Bib (Vol V), Primary Sources vs Further Reading.

R02 (Vol I printed Contents list) provides the exhaustive A–Celticism topic inventory, enabling comprehensive page mapping: Continental Iron Age (Aedui/Haedui, Arverni, Brigantes, Bituriges, Boii, Catuvellauni, Boudicca, Bibracte, Alesia, Celtiberia, Arras culture, Alpine/Balkans/Adriatic Celts, Caesar, Agricola, etc.); Insular early medieval (Adomnán, Aneirin, Brigit, Arthur historical + saints' lives, annals Irish, Armagh Book, Alba name/derivation, Armes Prydein, various kings/saints/poets, Breton/Irish/Welsh texts); Languages & linguistics (Celtic languages + diaspora varieties, Celtiberian, Breton, Brythonic, Common Celtic, Celtic studies early history); Modern/cultural/political (Celticism, Celtic Twilight, Acts of Union Ireland/Wales/Brittany, revival literatures, institutions); Myth/deities, material culture (Celtic art pre-/post-Roman + 5-region influenced, calendar, music, agriculture); and many specific texts (Acallam na Senórach, Cath Maige Tuired, Brut y Brenhinedd, etc.).

**Reconciliation with prior wiki (Cunliffe 2018 + Prehistoric Europe):** Reinforces and sharpens the post-Celtosceptic linguistic criterion already seeded; adds explicit caution on La Tène = Celtic equation (updates la-tene-culture, celtic-art); foregrounds Insular textual/literary depth and modern identity politics (new depth for celticism, celtic-studies, arthurian-literature, bardic-order); supplies precise entry titles for actors (aedui, arverni, etc.), places (alba, armagh, many oppida/sites), and events (unions, specific battles). No contradictions; strong complementarity (Cunliffe = archaeological synthesis + Atlantic hypothesis; Koch = linguistic definition + full Insular + reference apparatus).
