# A History of the Church in the Middle Ages (Logan 2013)

F. Donald Logan's **A History of the Church in the Middle Ages** (Routledge, 2nd ed. 2013; 1st ed. 2002) is a **chronological narrative survey** of the Latin Christian church from the conversion of the Celtic and Germanic peoples to 1492. It sits between a brief textbook and an encyclopedia: enough narrative and institutional detail for a serious first map of the subject, with Further Reading lists pointing outward. The second edition revises the text in light of a decade of new scholarship and expands the introduction on Judaism and Islam.

## Logan's Frame

1. **Latin West as the unit.** "Medieval" here means the church of western Europe; Byzantium and Islam appear as external forces that shaped borders, opposition, and occasional contact, not as full parallel histories.
2. **Church = institution + soul.** History must treat both the external institution (papacy, councils, law, orders) and the inner spiritual life of Christian people (devotion, dissent, piety). Neither alone is enough.
3. **Chronology first.** The book is deliberately chronological and narrative; Logan holds that historical study begins with sequence, then invites deeper pursuit via Further Reading.
4. **Shared Christendom.** One Creed, one Latin learned language, and comparable liturgical forms bound disparate peoples from Iceland to Sicily — with local custom as flavour under an overarching church.
5. **1492 as pedagogical close.** The Epilogue uses 1492 (not 1517 or Fifth Lateran) as an arbitrary but defensible end-point inside a longer medieval–modern transition.

## Volume Synthesis Note

Logan is the wiki’s **single-volume chronological map of the Latin medieval church**. He does not replace NCMH institutional depth or Wickham’s materialist structure; he supplies the **narrative through-line** from Constantine and Gregory I to 1492 that those denser works assume. Three contributions matter most for the knowledge base:

1. **Integrated institutional + devotional history.** The same book carries Lateran IV’s annual confession, Gothic cathedrals, Abelard/Becket/Hildegard profiles, Cathars and friars, purgatory after the Black Death, and English parish calendar piety — forcing cross-links among pages the wiki often filed separately.
2. **Benchmark framing of the high Middle Ages.** Reform papacy + East–West estrangement + crusade ideal as the three “forms cast” after the mid-eleventh century — a clean analytical triad for period and process pages.
3. **Anti-teleology for the fifteenth century.** Explicit refusal to read the last medieval century only as Reformation prelude; Constance is not “the end of the medieval papacy”; lay piety is “vibrant,” not decadent.

Limitations remain those of a Western-survey textbook: Byzantium and Islam as external shaping forces; selective further reading rather than full apparatus; pedagogical end at 1492. Use alongside NCMH IV–VII, crusade specialists, and Wickham for structure and comparison.
