# Ancient Europe 8000 B.C. to A.D. 1000: Encyclopedia of the Barbarian World

**Peter I. Bogucki and Pam J. Crabtree, Editors in Chief**  
Charles Scribner’s Sons (Gale / Thomson Learning), 2004. Two volumes.

This is a major reference encyclopedia covering the archaeology and early history of temperate Europe from the beginning of the postglacial Mesolithic through the Early Middle Ages (Migration Period and early medieval "barbarian" polities). It deliberately focuses on the "Barbarian World" — the societies of northern, central, western, and eastern Europe that interacted with (but were distinct from) the classical Mediterranean civilizations.

**Key claims extracted (with grounding quotes + locators from range 01):**
- Bronze Age not a radical break: "Society did not undergo a radical transformation at the onset of the Bronze Age. Many of the social, economic, and symbolic developments that mark this period have their roots in the Late Neolithic." (range 01, ~lines 2815–2820).
- Metallurgy: bronze = copper + tin (or arsenic); tin sources western Europe (Brittany, Cornwall, Spain); long-distance trade networks established for copper/tin/amber/jet/gold. (range 01, Significance of Bronze and Central Europe articles).
- Burials and monuments: single graves under barrows/tumuli early BA; shift ~1200 B.C. to cremation urnfields in central/southern Europe; "Stonehenge, begun during the Late Neolithic, reached its zenith during the Bronze Age, when the largest upright sarsen stones and lintels still visible today were erected..." (range 01, ~2961–2965). Rich graves e.g. Bush Barrow, Leubingen; oak-coffin Denmark.
- Status/wealth: increasing differentiation; "The variation in the burials has led to the very reasonable view that the Bronze Age was characterized by increasing differences in the access by individuals to status, power, and wealth." (range 01).
- SE Europe E/MBA (c. 2200–1500 B.C. regional): cultures Somogyvar/Vinkovci/Kisapostag/Nagyrev/Hatvan/Maros; wattle-daub rectangular houses ~8-10x4-6 m; tells (Tószeg, Pecica); contracted inhumations with variable goods (gold in richest at Mokrin etc.); Middle BA shift to cremation, more weapons, palisaded/fortified sites (Feudvar example with Vatin pottery, mixed farming, possible beer production). Political: "tribes or chiefdoms" (Service model); not states. (range 01, Bankoff article).
- Central Europe: crops emmer/einkorn/barley + new spelt/millet/oats/broad beans; animals cattle/sheep/goats/pigs + "an increase in the exploitation of the horse—which remained fairly rare after its introduction in the Final Neolithic"; Secondary Products Revolution intensification (plough/ards, dairy, wool, traction); ritual hoards as votive (rivers, fens, damaged objects); exchange on "prehistoric world-system" (Danube axis, Anatolian contacts, amber from Baltic); Nebra sky disk (sun/moon/Pleiades/ship) at Mittelberg ~1600 B.C.; gold beakers (Fritzdorf etc.) in Atlantic networks. (range 01, ~5000–5300 lines).
- Italian Bronze Age: Apennine culture with transhumant pastoralism + mixed farming; terremare (Po Plain) grow large in Recent BA (Fondo Paviani 16 ha, Case del Lago 22.5 ha); collapse ~1200 B.C. of palafitte-terremare system; Final BA Protovillanovan urnfields; Frattesina (20 ha, glass/ivory/bronze/iron/amber craft + Mediterranean trade); Nuragic towers Sardinia (~7000 examples, 2000–600 B.C., corbeled domestic/watch/fortress farmsteads, "giants’ tombs"). (range 01, Pearce & Blake articles).
- Iberian: El Argar (SE Spain) segmentary mixed farmers with incipient ranking, plow/secondary products use, fortified sites; Bronce Valenciano/Mancha related (circular fortified settlements, less mortuary data). (range 01, Gilman).
- Continuity/references: Links to Varna (Copper Age), Linearbandkeramik maps, Bell Beakers provinces, Corded Ware, megalithic continuation, horse introduction Final Neolithic. (TOC/maps/cross-refs in range).

Sites: Oseberg (A.D. 834 woman with weaving tablets and unfinished braid), York (waterlogged evidence of production), Birka (A.D. 750–970; local + Byzantine silk; metallic salt preservation). - Viking North Atlantic settlement: "Iceland was traditionally settled c. 874, Greenland c. 985..." "pollen studies suggest that 90 percent of the dwarf birch and willow forests present at landnám were removed in the first century of settlement." "By A.D. 930 the Icelanders had set up a self-governing system of local and national things..." Christianity adopted 1000. Greenland: Eastern and Western Settlements; economy domestic stock + seals/caribou; walrus ivory key export; bishopric 1127; extinction Western ~1350, Eastern ~1450. Climate, Thule contact, and landnám-era decisions as factors. (range_06_39696_47634.txt: 670-910)
- Hofstaðir: Large hall (4x average floor space); pit house midden; ritual abandonment c. A.D. 1000 with beheaded sheep and roof-displayed skulls deposited; not one of first farms (peak c. 950–1000); working farm with iron smelting. (range_06_39696_47634.txt: 1340-1490)

## Volume Synthesis Note

This 2004 two-volume reference (Bogucki & Crabtree, eds.) is the most systematic archaeological synthesis in the collection for the “Barbarian World” — the societies of temperate, northern, central, eastern, and western Europe outside the classical Mediterranean core — from the opening of the postglacial (c. 8000 BCE) to the threshold of the High Middle Ages (c. 1000 CE). Its architecture deliberately interleaves editorial thematic essays (Discovering Barbarian Europe: methods, environments, data, dating, landscapes, trade, status, gender, ritual, ideology, warfare, language) with seven major chronological/regional parts. Each part opens with an editorial introduction and then delivers both broad syntheses and tightly focused site or feature entries. The result is a fractal map: the same societies appear at multiple scales — as contributors to continent-wide processes (Secondary Products Revolution, spread of metallurgy, oppida formation, Migration-period ethnogenesis) and as specific places with dendro dates, rampart lengths, grave goods, and workshop debris.

The volume adds irreplaceable granularity to the wiki. It supplies verbatim-grounded detail on scores of sites and assemblages that were previously represented only at the level of culture names or broad period statements: Star Carr and the Iron Gates for the Mesolithic; Achilleion, Bylany, Hambledon Hill, Sarup for the Neolithic transition; Varna, the Rudna Glava mines, and horse domestication for the Copper Age; Flag Fen, the Nordic oak-coffin horizon, Sintashta-Arkaim chariots, the Nebra disk, El Argar, the Nuraghi, and Frattesina for the Bronze Age; the Heuneburg mud-brick wall, Kelheim, Manching, Bibracte, the Vix krater, Danebury storage pits, Viereckschanzen, and the shift from Hallstatt Fürstensitze to La Tène oppida for the Iron Age; and, for the Early Middle Ages, the Childeric tomb (horses, signet, hybrid regalia), Jelling, Trelleborg, Staraya Ladoga, Hofstaðir ritual abandonment, Ipswich ware production, and the pig-bone proxy for Islamization in Iberia. These entries also furnish precise 2004-era interpretations and construction estimates that can now be cited against later revision.

Cross-section synthesis reveals several through-lines visible only at the whole-volume scale. One is the long-term intensification of the Secondary Products Revolution (plough, dairy, wool, traction, horse) and its social consequences — from Final Neolithic tell and long-barrow societies through Bronze Age “world-system” exchange (amber, tin, copper, gold) to the surplus economies that underwrote oppida and early medieval emporia. A second is the persistent tension between prestige display (rich single burials, torcs, wagons, cauldrons) and communal or ritual infrastructure (henges, hillforts, Viereckschanzen, feasting halls, bog deposits). A third is the recurring pattern of nucleation and then dispersal or abandonment (terramare collapse ~1200 BCE, Heuneburg fire ~540 BCE, widespread oppida abandonment mid-first century BCE, Western Settlement Greenland extinction) that the encyclopedia documents across millennia without forcing a single master narrative.

The work sits in productive tension with sources already ingested. It shares Champion et al. *Prehistoric Europe*’s culture-historical and settlement focus but supplies richer site monographs and 2004 bibliographic apparatus; it overlaps Cunliffe’s *Ancient Celts* and Koch’s *Celtic Culture* on Hallstatt/La Tène material and Atlantic connections yet uses a broader “barbarian” frame that includes non-Celtic groups (Nuragic Sardinia, Iberian cultures, steppe horizons, Slavs, Avars). It predates the major aDNA, strontium, and lipid-residue revolutions that have revised steppe chronology, Neolithization models, and mobility narratives since 2004 — these gaps are now explicitly flagged on the affected period and concept pages. The classical-derived “barbarian” lens itself is useful for emic/etic analysis but requires the counterweight of the more recent Celtic and theoretical sources.
