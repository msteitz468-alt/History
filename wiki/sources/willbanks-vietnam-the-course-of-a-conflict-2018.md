# Vietnam: The Course of a Conflict (Willbanks, 2018)

James H. Willbanks' *Vietnam: The Course of a Conflict* is a collection of his chapter-length studies that together provide a comprehensive operational history of the American war in Vietnam from the advisory years through the final collapse in 1975. As a former infantry advisor who served during the 1972 Easter Offensive and a long-time military historian at the US Army Command and General Staff College, Willbanks brings both practitioner insight and scholarly rigor.

The volume covers the evolution of US involvement: training indigenous forces, the introduction of Free World Military Assistance Forces, major conventional operations (e.g., Junction City), the pivotal 1968 Tet Offensive and Battle of Hue, intelligence issues, the CORDS/Phoenix pacification effort, the flawed Vietnamization exit strategy, Lam Son 719, the Battle of An Loc, and the final 55 days leading to the fall of Saigon.

## What it contributes

- Detailed, phase-by-phase operational narrative of the war's military course with attention to both American and South Vietnamese performance.
- Strong treatment of specific campaigns and battles suitable for warfare-hub depth analysis (OOB, phases, command friction, ARVN combat performance).
- Balanced attention to pacification (CORDS and Phoenix) alongside conventional operations.
- Insight into the challenges of security assistance, combined operations, and "Vietnamization" as an incomplete strategy.
- Use of Vietnamese perspectives and official histories alongside US sources.
- Author's direct experience (1972 advisor) informs analysis of later phases.

Cross-references: complements and supplies granular operational detail to [[krepinevich-the-army-and-vietnam-1986]], [[nagl-learning-to-eat-soup-with-a-knife-2005]], [[summers-on-strategy-1982]], and [[daddis-westmorelands-war-2014]]. ## Key Claims (Range 01 exclusive: front matter + Introduction + Ch. 1 advisory training + Ch. 2 Free World forces)

**War overview and early escalation (Introduction, lines ~170–450 approx in cache):**
- The war lasted from mid-1950s to mid-1970s, spreading to Laos and Cambodia. US involvement began 1950 supporting France against Viet Minh as containment; >$2.6B aid; French defeat at Dien Bien Phu 1954; Geneva partition at 17th Parallel creating DRV (Ho Chi Minh) north and State of Vietnam (Bao Dai) south.
- US backed Ngo Dinh Diem after 1955 election; Diem refused 1956 elections per Geneva. Insurgency grew; Hanoi authorized revolutionary violence at Fifteenth Party Plenum Jan 1959; Ho Chi Minh Trail; NLF founded 20 Dec 1960 (Hanoi controlled despite united-front appearance). 
- 1961: JFK increased aid ($65M military, $136M economic); advisors >3,200 by end 1961, now advising combat units at battalion/regimental level. MACV established 1962. PLAF/VC ~15k end 1961, NLF total ~300k end 1962. ARVN plagued by corruption, poor leadership, ineffective.
- Battle of Ap Bac 2 Jan 1963: small VC force defeated larger 7th ARVN Division. Buddhist crisis 1963 (self-immolations, raids on monasteries); Diem/Nhu assassinated in coup early Nov 1963 (US tacit approval).
- Johnson inherits; by mid-1964 VC 35k guerrillas +80k irregulars routinely defeating ARVN. Tonkin Gulf 2 Aug 1964 (Maddox attacked; 4 Aug "second attack" later false); Tonkin Gulf Resolution passed (only 2 dissenting votes) authorizing "all necessary measures." Retaliatory strikes. Rolling Thunder sustained bombing from Feb 1965 (ineffective due to restrictions).
- Mar 1965: first US ground troops (Marines) to Da Nang for airfield protection; soon offensive ops. Westmoreland requests more; 7 Army divisions +2 Marine divisions + brigades; 180k US troops end 1965, 350k mid-1966. Search-and-destroy attrition war. ~500k by late 1967. Tet 1968 massive attacks despite tactical defeat for Communists became psychological victory; LBJ does not run. Nixon Vietnamization; 1972 Easter Offensive blunted by airpower. Paris Accords 27 Jan 1973; US withdrawal Mar 1973; Saigon falls 30 Apr 1975. >58k US KIA, >300k wounded.

**Advisory effort and training indigenous forces (Ch. 1, ~lines 450–1060):**
- US training mission divided into five phases: (1) early French aid (MAAG-I 1950, 80% funding by 1954); (2) post-Geneva Franco-American (TRIM 1955, MAAG-V 1955); (3) US-only building conventional ARVN (1956+); (4) post-1965 US combat priority, ARVN shunted to pacification (secondary endeavor); (5) Vietnamization 1969+ (increased advisors to RVNAF training base and CORDS, expansion to >1M in uniform by 1973).
- Post-1954 objective: three-pillar security (Self-Defense Corps village militia; Civil Guard provincial; ARVN apex). ARVN built from VNA remnants (~150k 1954, "complete breakdown of combat capabilities," desertion/defection, politicized/corrupt officers, tainted by French association). US aimed to create field divisions mirroring US Army light infantry divisions for worst-case external PAVN invasion + internal security (Korea precedent dominant).
- "In the early years... some effort to train... for civic action and pacification. However, over time as the insurgency gained momentum, less and less emphasis was given to the special aspects of counter-guerrilla warfare; the focus shifted to preparing the force for... guerrilla main force units and an external invasion from North Vietnam." "the ARVN was structured and trained to meet a possible external attack by the PAVN, not the actual struggle against the National Liberation Front."
- Collective/unit training problematic: political appointee leaders prioritized intrigue; operational demands left no time; paramilitaries under civilian advisors, forcing ARVN into internal security further degrading training. Advisors (down to battalion by 1961+) responsible for training, staff, logistics, planning; initially barred from combat ops (loosened 1961). Many advisors lacked combat/COIN experience, Vietnamese language/culture; "revolving door advisors" from 12-month (often shorter) tours impeded rapport/trust. "The main qualification for being assigned as an advisor was often availability."
- Post-US troops 1965: "the buildup [had] eclipsed what had previously been an advisor ‘show’"; training "relegated to 'a secondary endeavor'"; priority fill to combat units; ~2,500 fewer advisors than authorized by 1968. ARVN low training state, poor leadership, high desertion (113k by end 1965). Tet 1968: ARVN "did not collapse and in most cases acquitted itself fairly well" despite problems.
- Vietnamization phase: advisors surged (7k to 11.9k to 14.3k field teams 1969-70); focus on RVNAF training base (56 centers by 1968); CORDS for RF/PF. Early tests mixed (Cambodia 1970 good with US; Lam Son 719 1971 retreat in disarray without US ground/advisors). 1972 Easter: ARVN held with advisors + US air (Quang Tri lost then retaken). Ultimate failure: wrong threat assessment (conventional prep vs. actual insurgency + later PAVN main force); lack of unity of effort (military vs. civilian nation-builders until CORDS 1967); focus on combat over training; advisor selection/tour length problems; "conscious decision to form the South Vietnamese forces in the image of US forces" creating dependence on US firepower/logistics ("by 1975, had 'forgotten how to walk' and 'could no longer afford to ride or fly'").
- Key lesson (T.E. Lawrence paraphrase): "Better they do it imperfectly than you do it perfectly, for it is their country, their war, and your time is limited."

**Free World Military Forces / "More Flags" (Ch. 2, ~lines 1150–1720):**
- >40 nations provided some aid; 5 provided direct military (ROK, Thailand, Australia, New Zealand, Philippines). ROK largest combat contribution (~50k peak); Australia/NZ self-funded (~8k+ Aus peak, ~1k NZ); Thai >11k (US funded ~$50M/yr); Philippines 1st Civic Action Group (engineers/medics/security, ~2k, US paid $39M 1967-69). ROK Capital Division + 2nd Marine Brigade deployed by Nov 1965; Australian task force (1 RAR + support) Jun 1965 under 173rd Airborne; NZ battery Jul 1965.
- Origins: US policy sought multilateralism for credibility/domestic/international legitimacy (SEATO protocol states). Rusk/McNamara Nov 1961 memo urged involvement of other nations "alongside of United States forces." Johnson April 1964 press conference hoped for "some other flags." 1 May 1964 Rusk cable: call on Free World for "political and material contributions"; "The nature and amount... not for the present as significant as the fact that their being made." US would pay full cost of military aid commitments (Dec 1964).
- SEATO ineffective ("shaky vehicle"); many members declined (domestic politics, risk). Shift to bilateral with "key allies." South Vietnamese government ambivalent/lassitude (preoccupied with internal turmoil; cultural/historical resistance to more foreign troops); Washington played "role of supplicant."
- Command: Westmoreland (COMUSMACV) assumed operational control of Free World Military Forces; retained national command identity. US provided admin/logistical support.
- Results limited: "the international response... mounting unpopularity... and Saigon's ambivalence doomed Johnson’s efforts. Ultimately, the war would remain America’s war." Total FW casualties 5,241 (4,407 Korean, 475 Aus/NZ). Peak contributions helped offset US force requests but fell far short of international consensus Johnson sought.

**Final phase trigger and Phuoc Long test case (Dec 1974–Jan 1975):**

**Tet Offensive (1968) as turning point (Ch. 6 / cache ~930–1400+):**
- "In the first two days of the Tet Offensive, which began on 30 January 1968, North Vietnamese and Viet Cong troops attacked 39 of South Vietnam’s 44 provincial capitals, five of six largest cities to include Saigon, 71 of 242 district capitals, some 50 hamlets, virtually every allied airfield, and many other key military installations. The offensive stunned the White House, MACV, and the American people." (cache lines ~933-939)
- "Adding to the impact of the surprise attacks was that they followed in the wake of assurances from both the military and the Johnson administration that progress was being made in the war and that the end was in sight." ## Historiography Note

Willbanks writes from a military historian's perspective informed by personal service as an advisor during the critical 1972 fighting. The collection format allows focused case studies of major operations while maintaining an overall chronological thread. It is particularly valuable for its attention to ARVN combat performance (often critically but fairly assessed) and the practical difficulties of pacification and Vietnamization. Vietnamese sources (translated) provide balance to purely American accounts. Like Daddis, it pushes against overly simplistic "attrition only" or "Army never adapted" narratives by showing the complexity and evolution of operations on the ground.

## Volume Synthesis Note

Willbanks provides the operational "course of the conflict" backbone that ties together the more analytical/critical works already in the collection. By focusing on specific campaigns and the evolution from advisory → conventional → Vietnamization → collapse, it supplies the concrete military history needed for high-resolution warfare-hub treatment of Vietnam. The collection highlights recurring themes: the tension between conventional and counterinsurgency requirements, persistent challenges with ARVN leadership and motivation, the difficulty of measuring progress in a hybrid war, and the limits of external support when the host government's political foundation was weak. It serves as an excellent primary source for fleshing out the hub's campaign and battle analyses while grounding claims in the experiences of those who planned and fought the war.
