# The Cambridge World History, Volume V: Expanding Webs of Exchange and Conflict, 500 CE–1500 CE

**Editors:** Benjamin Z. Kedar (Hebrew University of Jerusalem; crusades and
comparative frontier historian) and Merry E. Wiesner-Hanks (series
editor-in-chief). Cambridge University Press, 2015. ~700 pp., 25 chapters in
five thematic parts plus a concluding chapter.

## Series Note

Volume V of the seven-volume Cambridge World History. Where
[[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|Vol. IV]] traced a world of *states, empires, and
networks* across the long classical era (1200 BCE–900 CE), Vol. V carries the
network thesis forward into what its editors call the **"Middle Millennium"**
(500–1500 CE): a thousand years framed not as "the Middle Ages" (a Eurocentric
and Europe-only label) but as the age in which **webs of exchange and conflict
thickened** until, by 1500, a "first globalization" of trade, religion,
technology, and ideas spanned Afro-Eurasia and reached, in muted parallel, into
the Americas. The volume is organized thematically, not regionally: five parts
move from global structures (environment, gender, society, education, warfare),
through Eurasian commonalities (courtly culture, cultural crystallization),
growing interactions (trade, technology, science, nomadic migration), expanding
religious systems (Islam, Christendom, Buddhism), to comparative state
formation (China, the Mongols, Byzantium, the Western Sudan, Postclassic
Mesoamerica, the Inca). It is the principal foundation for the wiki's period
skeleton across [[early-middle-ages]], [[high-middle-ages]], and
[[late-middle-ages]], and the spine of its coverage of the medieval Islamic
world, Song China, the [[mongol-empire|Mongol Empire]], the Indian Ocean world,
and the Sahelian states.

## Structure of the Volume

The editors' organizing image (Ch 1) is the **expanding web**: distinct civilizational
"ecumenes" that over the Middle Millennium became progressively linked by trade,
religion, conquest, and the circulation of technology and ideas — connection that
also generated friction (crusade, jihad, nomadic conquest, persecution). The
recurring analytic devices are:
- **Three commercial zone-types** (Richard Smith, Ch 9): *engines* of production
  and exchange (China, the Islamic heartland), *passageways* (Central Asia, the
  Southeast Asian sea lanes), and *cul-de-sacs* (western Europe, West Africa).
- **The "world religion"** as a portable, civilization-binding system (Part IV):
  Islam, Christianity, and Buddhism each treated as an integrative network.
- **State formation as everywhere entangled with religion** (Arnason, Ch 18),
  surveyed comparatively from China to the Andes.
- **"Proto-globalization"** (Olstein, Ch 25) as the period's net outcome.

## Volume Synthesis Note

**The argument.** Kedar and Wiesner-Hanks frame the millennium 500–1500 CE not as
"the Middle Ages" — a Eurocentric, Europe-only label — but as the **"Middle
Millennium,"** an era defined by a global process: the **thickening of webs of
exchange and conflict** until, by 1500, a "first globalization" of trade, religion,
technology, and ideas approached genuinely trans-hemispheric scope. The volume is
built on three "regular trans-civilizational interactions" named in its introduction —
**conquest-driven state formation, trade networks, and religious ecumenes** — and is
organized thematically rather than regionally across five parts (global developments;
Eurasian commonalities; growing interactions; expanding religious systems; state
formations) plus Olstein's concluding synthesis. Its recurring image is the
**expanding web**: distinct civilizational ecumenes drawn progressively together by
commerce, faith, conquest, and the circulation of knowledge — connection that also
generated crusade, jihad, nomadic conquest, persecution, and, fatefully, the conduits
along which the [[black-death|Black Death]] travelled.

**What it adds to the wiki.** This volume is the **spine of the wiki's coverage of the
global Middle Ages**, building the period skeleton for the [[high-middle-ages|High]]
(1000–1300) and [[late-middle-ages|Late Middle Ages]] (1300–1500) and reframing the
[[early-middle-ages|Early Middle Ages]] as the Middle Millennium's opening phase. Its
single most important contribution is the **master process page**
[[afro-eurasian-world-system|the Afro-Eurasian world system]] (Abu-Lughod's
thirteenth-century eight circuits), to which the trade chapters attach the
[[commercial-revolution|European commercial revolution]], [[indian-ocean-trade|Indian
Ocean trade]], and an updated [[silk-roads|Silk Roads]]. It supplied the wiki's three
**"world religion"** pages — [[islam]], [[christianity]], and an expanded
[[buddhism]] — and its major **state-formation actors**: the
[[mongol-empire|Mongol Empire]] and [[genghis-khan|Chinggis Khan]], the
[[song-dynasty|Song]] and [[neo-confucianism|Neo-Confucianism]], a Middle-Millennium
[[byzantine-empire|Byzantium]], the [[western-sudan-states|Western Sudanic]] empires
([[mali-empire|Mali]], [[mansa-musa|Mansa Musa]], [[songhay-empire|Songhay]]), and the
American [[aztec-empire|Aztec]] and [[inca-empire|Inca]] empires. The thematic Part I
chapters anchored a set of analytical concepts —
[[environmental-history-middle-millennium|environmental history]] and its
[[societal-collapse-and-environmental-determinism|collapse-vs-resilience controversy]],
[[social-hierarchy-and-solidarity]], [[educational-institutions]],
[[military-history-middle-millennium|warfare and the gunpowder transition]],
[[courtly-culture]], [[cultural-crystallization]], [[science-transmission]] — and the
volume closes with [[proto-globalization]] and the framing concept
[[middle-millennium]] itself.

**Cross-volume synthesis.** Read whole, Vol. V is the **direct sequel to**
[[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|CWH IV]] (*states, empires, and networks*): the network thesis
of the classical era is carried forward and intensified into a genuine, if partial,
**Old-World system**. The two volumes share authors and devices — Wittrock's
[[axial-age|Axial Age]] (CWH IV) becomes his [[cultural-crystallization|"third
crystallization"]] of the tenth–thirteenth centuries (CWH V); the
[[silk-roads|Silk Roads]], [[pastoral-nomadism|pastoral nomadism]],
[[trans-saharan-trade|trans-Saharan trade]], and the American and African state pages
all gain their medieval continuation here. The volume's deepest through-line, echoing
CWH IV, is that **the same structural processes — state formation, sacred kingship,
monumental urbanism, tributary hierarchy, long-distance exchange — recurred
independently across the world zones**: the [[aztec-empire|Aztec]] and
[[inca-empire|Inca]] empires assembled the Americas' largest states precisely as the
Afro-Eurasian system reached its height, the two hemispheres still (in Olstein's
phrase) **"worlds apart."**

**Tensions and continuities with already-ingested sources.** Vol. V largely *extends*
rather than contradicts the wiki. It deepens [[yoffee-cwh-v3-2015|CWH III's]] cities
([[angkor|Angkor]] now read through its collapse debate; [[jenne-jeno]] and
[[tenochtitlan]] as nodes in trade and tribute systems) and carries CWH IV's African
and American foundations from ~900 CE to 1500. Two live periodization debates run
through it, recorded but not adjudicated per the Contradiction Protocol: whether the
"Middle Millennium" / "proto-globalization" frame illuminates or imposes teleology on
500–1500 (Olstein himself hedges with "worlds apart" and "proto-glocalization"), and
whether Cook's claim that **Islam alone was a "world civilization"** holds against the
case that Latin Christendom, too, cohered into one. Susan Reynolds's chapter sharpens
the wiki's [[indian-feudalism-debate]] by attacking the category "feudalism" itself,
and Radkau's environmental chapter promotes the long-pending **Diamond-collapse**
standing controversy to a full page.
