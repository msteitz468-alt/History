# The Kongo Kingdom: The Origins, Dynamics and Cosmopolitan Culture of an African Polity

**Authors/editors:** Koen Bostoen and Inge Brinkman (eds.), Cambridge University Press, 2018

**Coverage:** The [[kingdom-of-kongo]] from its origins (c. 1250 CE archaeological / c. 1390 traditional founding) through the nineteenth century and into the African diaspora in Brazil. Geographically centered on the Lower Congo region (present-day DRC Kongo-Central, Angola, Republic of Congo).

## Key Claims

**Ch. 9 (Brinkman & Bostoen — Kongo Book Cultures):**
- Books entered Kongo through Christianity, trade, and administration from 1482; earliest record is the King of Portugal sending "many books" to the Kongo king.
- [[afonso-i-kongo]] used literacy for political centralisation; letter-writing between capital and provinces was an instrument of governance.
- The Kikongo term *mukanda* ("skin/cloth" → "book/letter") is the oldest attested Kongo book-concept, first recorded 1624 in the Cardoso catechism; its geographic distribution in Bantu languages suggests Kongo spread it far inland.
- Four European loanwords for "book" entered the Kikongo Language Cluster at different times reflecting different contact histories: Portuguese *livro* → *divulu* (17th c., Christian context); Dutch *boeken* → *buka* (18th c., coastal trade, northern regions); English *book* → *buku* (19th c., now universal); French *livre* → *livla* (limited northern occurrence).
- The English loan *buku* now dominates the entire Kikongo-speaking region, displacing the Portuguese loan, demonstrating the shift from Christian-Portuguese to British-trade predominance in Kongo's global connections.
- "To make book" was an emic phrase (Tuckey 1818) denoting education and cosmopolitan aspiration, not only literacy in the modern sense; book possession acquired near-ritual status in Kongo society.
- In northern coastal broker-states (Kakongo, Ngoyo, Loango), "bookness" shifted from Christian to trade contexts (logbooks, contracts, trade tickets called *mikanda*).

**Ch. 10 (Vos — Kongo Cosmopolitans in the Nineteenth Century):**
- Extraversion (Bayart 1993): Kongo elites used external partnerships — Christianity, textiles, Portugal — as domestic political instruments; this strategy ultimately produced dependency as European power reversed patron–client relations.
- The Order of Christ was domesticated by the Kongo nobility as a title association independent of Portugal; investiture oaths shifted from anti-witchcraft pledges to colonial government obedience over the 19th century.
- After 1900 the Order was replaced by colonial military titles (colonel, major, lieutenant colonel) coveted by Kongo kings from Álvaro XIV onward as new status markers.
- Dom Álvaro de Agua Rosada (alias Tangi, b. c. 1870, son of Pedro V): exemplary "cog" in the colonial system who used his intermediary role to build his own patronage network; labelled *mundele andombe* ("black white man").
- Pedro V (r. 1860–91): solicited Portuguese military support and rubber-trade factory investment; his strategy made Mbanza Kongo a distribution centre for prestige goods but converted him into a colonial client after 1888.
- The democratisation of imported textile access (c. 1890s–1910s) eroded the old slave-trading oligarchy's monopoly on prestige goods.
- Gross asymmetries between Africa and Europe emerged only in the late 19th century; before that, European traders were effectively "subordinate" parties on the African coast (Northrup 2014).

**Ch. 11 (Heywood — Kongo Identity in American Diaspora):**
- Kongo people began arriving in Brazil as enslaved captives from the 1580s; their slave trade continued to the 1850s; they were recorded as "Angolas" or "Congos" in Brazilian records.
- Pre-existing Kongo identity — centred on royalty and Catholic Christianity in a Kongo idiom — was the foundation for diaspora identity formation; it was not constructed in Brazil.
- The *congadas* / *autos de congo* theatrical tradition, centred on the election of a "King of Congo," is the primary institutional carrier of Kongo identity; earliest documented occurrence: Recife, 1674 (*Reis Congos* crowned by Câmara records); by 1711 recognized by the Bishop.
- Kongo ambassadors' presence in Recife (1624–1643) during the Dutch occupation performed royal cultural memory publicly for enslaved Kongo people.
- 1734 letters from enslaved Kongo noblemen Dom Deonizio and Dom Jorge Manuel to the king of Portugal (AHU Caixa 30): direct primary evidence of Kongo nobles on Brazilian plantations insisting on their status as falsely enslaved freemen.
- Kongo's long Christian identity differentiated them from other African "nations" in Brazil; Portuguese slave-owners and officials recognised the Kongo kingdom as Christian with politically legitimate kings.
- The Kikongo language (Kisikongo variety) was used in the *congadas* to symbolically reaffirm links to the Mbanza Kongo heartland.

## Volume Synthesis Note

This volume's central contribution is the integration of cross-disciplinary evidence — historical linguistics, archaeology, art history, history, and anthropology — to address questions about Kongo that neither documentary nor ethnographic sources could answer alone. Three major arguments stand out at the whole-volume level:

**1. The kingdom's origins are deeper and more structurally complex than the missionary-centric tradition assumed.** Thornton (ch. 1) revises the standard founding narrative to show a multi-stage process of alliance and conquest reaching back to c. 1250–1330 CE in the Seven Kingdoms zone east of the Inkisi River, while MacGaffey (ch. 2) deconstructs the "centralized state" model to reveal a society organized by kinship and occult legitimacy as much as by administrative hierarchy. The eastern origin hypothesis receives significant support from both the Bayesian linguistics (Bostoen & de Schryver, ch. 3) and the KongoKing archaeological fieldwork in the Inkisi valley.

**2. The [[kikongo-language-cluster|Kikongo Language Cluster]]'s internal differentiation predates the kingdom and provides an independent timeline for the region's political history.** The major finding — that Seventeenth-Century Kikongo is ancestral only to Kisikongo (Mbanza Kongo variety), not to the full KLC — means the language evidence cannot be used to reconstruct a common pre-Kongo proto-society. The convergence zone of Central Kikongo instead reflects the kingdom's actual process of political and economic integration across pre-existing dialect boundaries.

**3. Kongo's cosmopolitan character was active, not reactive.** Whether examining Christian visual objects (Fromont, ch. 6), ceramics (Cranshof et al., ch. 7), tobacco pipes (Clist, ch. 8), books (Brinkman & Bostoen, ch. 9), or 19th-c. patronage networks (Vos, ch. 10) and diaspora identity (Heywood, ch. 11), the volume consistently shows Kongo elites selectively appropriating foreign forms and transforming them into instruments of local power and prestige. The concept of "extraversion" (Bayart, via Vos) unifies these chapters: the same strategy that enabled Kongo's extraordinary cosmopolitan engagement also ultimately created structural dependency on external actors as European power asymmetry grew.

**Cross-volume tensions with already-ingested sources:** GHA vols. IV–V (Vansina) and the Cambridge History of Africa series provide broader regional context but reflect a pre-KongoKing state of scholarship on Kongo origins and linguistics. The eastern origin hypothesis and the KLC phylogeny are new enough (2013–18) that they do not appear in those volumes; where they conflict with GHA accounts of Kongo origins or linguistic geography, this volume's findings should be given priority for Kongo-specific claims.
