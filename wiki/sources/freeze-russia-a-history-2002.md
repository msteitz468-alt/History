# Russia: A History — Gregory L. Freeze (ed.)

## Source Summary

*Russia: A History* (2nd ed., 2002), edited by Gregory L. Freeze, is a multi-author survey providing a state-of-the-art account from the recorded origins of Rus' (Kievan period) to the early twenty-first century. It allocates roughly one-third each to the medieval, imperial, and Soviet/post-Soviet eras, with particular strength in the modern period. Chapters are written by specialists (e.g., Janet Martin on early Rus'/Muscovy, Nancy Kollmann, Lewis Siegelbaum, William Fuller, Martin McCauley, and Freeze himself for several modern sections).

The volume draws heavily on post-Soviet archival access and a depoliticized scholarly environment. Key interpretive themes (from the editor's preface) include:
- The protracted and difficult process of state and societal development, with rulers often having only tenuous control outside major centers.
- Russia as a "poor state" and "weak state" constrained by geography, infrastructure, and lack of surplus.
- Peculiar social evolution (late formation of estates, intensification of serfdom, persistence of the commune into the 20th c.).
- Multinational character (Rossiia as empire; necessity of "de-Russifying" the narrative; role of non-Russian peripheries in stability and disintegration).
- Recurrent "times of trouble" (1598, 1917, 1991) exposing regime weakness.
- Rejection of simplistic myths of omnipotent autocrats or linear Western-style progress.

The work is particularly valuable for social and economic history, nationalities questions, and the post-1985 transition.

## Historiography and Approach

Freeze highlights the revolution in access to archives for 20th-c. topics, enabling more dispassionate analysis. The volume seeks to move beyond Cold War myths/counter-myths. Individual chapters reflect specialist expertise (e.g., social history of clergy/labor, military, nationalities). The editor stresses the limits of state power, the distinctiveness of Russian social structures, and the centrality of the non-Russian periphery.

This work complements the more continuous narrative of Riasanovsky (ingested 2026) by offering thematic depth, especially in social/economic and late 20th-c. chapters informed by fresh sources. It provides coverage to 2001, including the immediate post-Soviet "meltdown" and early Putin consolidation.

### 1914–1917: WWI and Road to February Revolution
- Durnovo memorandum Feb 1914: "In a memorandum of February 1914 P. N. Durnovo, a former Minister of Internal Affairs, implored the emperor to avoid war with Germany. The Kaiserreich, he argued, was a natural ally... But Durnovo was more prescient than persuasive: within months the government found itself helplessly sucked into the whirlpool of war." (lines 7569–7572). Contributed to [[russian-empire]] entry into WWI and regime vulnerability.
- Patriotic union ephemeral; "crisis was at the top": "War, however, proved particularly difficult for Russia, one reason being that the ‘crisis was at the top’—a mutual alienation that divided state and ‘Society’" (lines 7629–7631). Preceded_by pre-war fissures; caused_by Nicholas's distrust and personal command (1915).
- Progressive Bloc Aug 1915: centrist Duma parties "formed the ‘Progressive Bloc’ to press long-standing demands—above all, for a government ‘responsible’ to the Duma" (lines 7633–7634). Nicholas prorogued Duma and assumed army command.
- Institutions: Zemgor and War Industries Committees mobilized "Society"; "three competing centres of economic and political power emerged: the state bureaucracy, private industry and capital, and public organizations" (lines 7641–7642).
- 1916 Turkestan uprising: "the largest insurrection of 1916, claiming thousands of lives, erupted in Turkestan in response to a new conscription drive" (lines 7645–7646). Illustrated national minorities rebelliousness.
- Feb Revolution trigger: "On 23 February (8 March NS) a crowd of women seized the occasion of International Women’s Day to demonstrate against the high bread prices and food shortages... The next day crowds of strikers... By 28 February the tsarist ministers were under arrest" (lines 7657–7658). "The Volhynian regiment opened fire... the next day the same Volhynian unit... took sides with the crowds" (lines 7657). Produced dual power.
- Abdication: "his final act as emperor was characteristically illegal: in contravention of the 1797 Law of Succession, Nicholas abdicated not only for himself but also for his son" (lines 7659–7660). Followed_by Michael declining throne.
- Dual power: Duma Committee (propertied society) vs Petrograd Soviet (moderate socialists, "iron laws of a two-stage revolution"); "Order No. 1" democratized army (lines 7757–7778).
- Provisional Govt Programme: "vowed to hold free elections for a Constituent Assembly... create self-government at every level... The Programme promised to convoke a constituent assembly" (lines 7769–7775). Also grain monopoly 25 March "declaring all grain to be state property" (lines 7831–7832).
- April Theses: Lenin "demanded the elimination of dual power and transfer of ‘all power to the soviets’" (lines 7851–7852). "peace, bread, land, and workers’ control" (lines 7851).
- July Days: "soldiers and workers (with the aid of lower-echelon Bolshevik organs) staged an abortive insurrection... The government blamed the Bolsheviks, arrested several key leaders, and accused Lenin himself of ‘treason’" (lines 7949–7957). "Bolshevik leaders did less to lead than to follow popular radicalism" (per Rabinowitch ref, lines 7957–7958).
- Kerensky rise: after first coalition collapse over Ukrainian autonomy and land, "that government now became the personal instrument of Kerensky" (lines 7999–8000). "vain, egotistical, and poorly versed in the left-wing ideologies" (lines 8007–8008).
- Kornilov Affair: "Kornilov led loyal troops on a march towards Petrograd to restore order... Both accounts are true: the general did attempt a coup, believing that he had Kerensky’s support; and Kerensky did lose his nerve and renege" (lines 8032–8033). "Workers and paramilitary units known as Red Guards were mobilized... arrested Kornilov and disarmed his troops" (lines 8033). Enabled Bolshevik arming.
- Coming of October: CC vote "on 10 October, by a 10–2 vote, the Central Committee secretly endorsed Lenin’s theses" (lines 8219–8221). Opposed by "L. B. Kamenev and G. E. Zinoviev" (lines 8219). "the Petrograd soviet made a tactical decision... established the ‘Military Revolutionary Committee’" (lines 8223–8225). Seizure "on the night of 24 October, began seizing key centres... The Bolsheviks then informed the assembled soviet congress" (lines 8227–8228).

### Rebuilding State, Civil War, War Communism (1917–1921)
- Decrees and institutions: "torrent of decrees—like the land decree... They also sought to establish a monopoly on political discourse... early Bolshevik rule... was largely ‘rule by decree’" (lines 8249–8250). Retained tsarist bureaucracy, relabelled commissariats (lines 8251).
- Party-state: "Membership in the party itself grew exponentially, from a mere 23,600 members in January 1917 to 750,000 four years later... nomenklatura system" (lines 8261–8265). "This party apparatus became Stalin’s institutional base" (lines 8267).
- Cheka: "In December 1917... Lenin created a supreme political police—the ‘Extraordinary Commission to Combat Counter-Revolution and Sabotage’, known by its Russian acronym Cheka. At its head was a Polish Bolshevik, Feliks Dzerzhinskii... His empire constructed a network of prisons and labour camps" (lines 8281–8282).
- Constituent Assembly: "Bolsheviks garnered only a quarter of the votes... The result was a single, seventeen-hour session on 5–6 January: after passionate SR and Menshevik fulminations... Lenin simply dissolved the Assembly" (lines 8283–8284).
- Civil War actors/forces: Volunteer Army (Alekseev, Kornilov then Denikin); "Czech Legion"; Komuch (SRs under Chernov); Kolchak "Supreme Ruler" installed Nov 1918 "military dictator"; Denikin "Moscow Directive" 3 July 1919, captured Orel Oct; Wrangel, Iudenich, Budennyi counter (lines 8293–8310).
- Bolshevik advantages: "Geography afforded great strategic advantage: the Bolshevik hold on the central provinces... Ideology was also important... The socialist vision had not lost its lustre... Bolshevik nationality policy also contributed... promised national self-determination" (lines 8311–8319). "Intervention by the allies... had little military effect... Japan landed troops for the simple purpose of acquiring territory" (lines 8365–8371).
- War Communism: "attempt to create a socialist state, economy, society, and culture later known as ‘war communism’" (lines 8235). "food supply dictatorship" (lines 8391); "Committees of Poor Peasants (kombedy)" to "stok[e] the fires of intra-village revolution" (lines 8399); "‘formers’ (byvshie)... ‘disenfranchised’ (lishentsy)" (lines 8441–8444); "‘bagmen’ (meshchochniki)" black market (lines 8447).
- Revolutionary Culture: "Commissariat of Enlightenment" under Lunacharskii; debate Bogdanov (Proletkult, culture pre-condition) vs Lenin (culture superstructure, conservative tastes); "Proletarian Culture Movement (proletkul´t)" (lines 8453–8499). NEP continuation: eclecticism in arts, Mayakovsky etc., then "militants in the Komsomol... RAPP... pressed for a more rapid adoption of proletarian values" leading to cultural revolution (lines 9501–9508).

### Building Stalinism 1929–1941 (Siegelbaum)
- Five-Year Plan: "‘Five-Year Plan for Industrialization and Socialist Construction’... ‘Five in Four’... investment by 228 per cent, industrial production by 180 per cent... By 1932 gross industrial production... from 18.3 to 43.3 milliards" (lines 9591–9673). "Prometheanism... revolutionary maximalism" (lines 9659). "riotous optimism" (Nove).
- Collectivization: "‘Great Turn’" Stalin Pravda article; CC decree 5 Jan 1930 "collectivizing... ‘the huge majority of peasant farms’"; "liquidated as a class" 3 fates (resettle inferior, deport, camps); "By March 1930 an estimated 55 per cent... By June only 23 per cent... By July 1931... 53 per cent" (lines 9749–9760). "livestock... less than half what they had been in 1928" (lines 9761). Famine: "2.9 million lives in Ukraine and 4.2 million throughout the USSR in 1933" (lines 9927); "‘man-made’ or ‘artificial’" debate but "real output was well below 50 million tons" (lines 9927).
- Urbanization/"ruralization": Magnitogorsk "from 25 inhabitants in March 1929 to 250,000 by the autumn of 1932"; Moscow 2.2m to 3.6m; internal passports Dec 1932; "ruralization—the squeezing of the village into the city" (lines 9853–9857).
- Cultural Revolution: Shakhty 1928 "fifty-three mining engineers... ‘class vigilance’" (lines 9939); RAPP "proletarian hegemony"; abolition 23 Apr 1932 "single Union of Soviet Writers"; vydvizhenie proletarian advancement (lines 9959–9963).
- Neo-Traditionalism/Stakhanovism: Stakhanov 30 Aug 1935 "hewed 102 tons... more than fourteen times the norm"; "‘life has become more joyous’" (Stalin Nov 1935); Moscow Metro, folk ensembles (lines 10143–10186).
- Great Purges: Kirov assassination 1 Dec 1934 pretext; show trials (Zinoviev/Kamenev Aug 1936, Piatakov/Radek Jan 1937, Bukharin Mar 1938); military purge "35,000–40,000 officers... at least 35 per cent of the officer corps" incl. "three of the five Soviet marshals" (lines 10607–10613). "Yezhov... 259,450 persons... In mid-August 1937 torture was sanctioned"; official executions "353,074 in 1937, 328,618 in 1938" (lines 10209–10239). Getty: "no evidence of Stalin’s participation in Kirov’s assassination... ‘Indecision and chaos’"; Stalin "personally edited lists... signed tens of thousands of death sentences"; lower camp numbers vs Conquest (lines 10233–10241). Victims: party elite, officers, not disproportionately Old Bolsheviks/intelligentsia; family impact; "Rittersporn... attributing... to ‘plots’... real belief" (lines 10251–10262).
- Foreign policy: Popular Front pacts 1935; Munich "confirmed Soviet suspicions"; Nazi-Soviet Pact Aug 1939 secret protocols "occupied eastern Poland, the three Baltic republics, and... Bessarabia"; Winter War 1939–40 (lines 10395–10401).

### Great Patriotic War Opening (Fuller)
- Barbarossa: "At four a.m. (Moscow time) on 22 June 1941... Operation Barbarossa had begun... war ‘of extermination’" (lines 10491–10516). Stalin "not to shoot even if the Germans penetrated Soviet territory" (lines 10665–10673). "By the end of 1941... 1.5 million Soviet troops were dead while another 3 million were German POWs. Two million of the latter would perish" (lines 10595–10596).
- Weakness: Military purge impact "three-quarters of Soviet commanders had been in their posts for less than a year" (lines 10617); doctrine assumed short war "largely fought on the enemy’s soil"; "greatest blame... must rest with Stalin himself" (miscalc timing, no provocation, ignored Zhukov/Timoshenko) (lines 10619–10629).
- Phases: Moscow counter-attack Dec 1941 (Zhukov); Stalingrad "Operation Uranus" 19 Nov 1942 encircled; "Paulus and the remnants of his sixth army surrendered" Feb 1943 (lines 10716–10739); Kursk "largest tank battle in world history, with six thousand vehicles... Soviet victory" July 1943 (lines 10749–10751); Bagration destroyed 17 German divisions (lines 10753); Berlin encircled 25 Apr 1945, Hitler suicide, surrender 9 May (lines 10755–10799).
- Victory factors: German intelligence error (underestimated divisions/tanks/economy); "racist ideology" alienated Slavs, plunder vs rebuild; "partisan movement... 200,000" (lines 10807–10817). Soviet: "extreme centralization" enabled mobilization/evacuation "1,523 plants east"; "outproduced the Germans" 73k tanks, 82k aircraft 1943–5; talented generals (Zhukov, Konev, Rokossovskii etc.); Stalin "improved as a strategist" encouraged debate (lines 10865–10941). Lend-Lease: "10 per cent of all Soviet tanks and 12 per cent of all Soviet combat aircraft"; "eight million" kept in uniform (lines 10955–10957).
- Costs/postwar: "8.6 million Soviet troops and at least 17 million civilians"; "one-third of the national wealth"; 4th FYP exceeded 1940 by 40% industry; "mass deportation of over a million indigenous people of the Crimea, Caucasus"; Ukraine partisans "full-blown military operations" (lines 10979–11200).
