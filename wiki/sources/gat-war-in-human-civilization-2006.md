# War in Human Civilization (Azar Gat, 2006)

Comprehensive interdisciplinary study addressing the "riddle of war": why humans fight; whether war is rooted in human nature or a cultural invention of agriculture/civilization; how war shaped and was shaped by major transformations (forager → agrarian → state → modern industrial/liberal); and prospects for its elimination or decline. Draws on animal behavior, evolutionary theory/psychology, anthropology, archaeology, history, and political science to produce an integrated account from the first two million years through the nuclear age.

The central thesis: war is ancient and near-universal among humans, driven by evolved motivational complexes (resources, status, reproduction/kin selection, "web of desire"); it played a constitutive role in the emergence of the state and civilization (the Leviathan as solution to and product of endemic tribal warfare); modernity (markets, industry, liberal democracy, ultimate weapons) has altered the calculus in developed zones, producing a marked decline in war among affluent liberal democracies, though war persists elsewhere and new forms (terror, WMD proliferation) remain risks.

## Historiography Note

Gat synthesizes "ultimate" (evolutionary, biological) causes with "proximate" historical and cultural ones in a way few single-discipline works achieve. Challenges the post-WWII anthropological orthodoxy (war as late invention of civilization, Mead "War Is Only an Invention") with quantitative and qualitative data on forager lethal violence rates often comparable to or higher than state societies when adjusted. Engages realist IR theory (Morgenthau, Waltz, Mearsheimer) by insisting motives beyond "security dilemma" include positive gains (resources, women, status, glory). On modernity: not simple "democratic peace" but affluence + liberalism + nuclear weapons together make war between developed liberal states irrational and rare; however, weak/failed states, ethnic nationalism, WMD diffusion, and non-state actors remain vectors for violence. Method: extensive endnotes, article pre-prints in disciplinary journals for peer vetting. Potential critiques: weighting of certain anthropological datasets; relative brevity on some non-Eurasian state-formation trajectories; normative implications of evolutionary framing (addressed carefully).

## Consolidated Synthesis (main-thread)

Gat’s core answer to the riddle: war is deeply rooted in evolved human psychology and was ubiquitous in the forager state of nature (high per-capita violent death ~15–35% in many documented groups; raids/ambushes the lethal norm). Agriculture and sedentism scaled up group size, wealth concentration (livestock, stored crops), and therefore the stakes and organization of conflict; fortifications appeared with sedentism, not as proof war was invented then. The state (Leviathan) emerged in large part as a spin-off of war-making — the most effective war-makers created the institutions that suppressed internal feuding while projecting violence outward, enabling dense societies, division of labour, and "civilization" at the price of hierarchy and larger wars.

In the modern transformation (guns + markets → fiscal-military states; industrial wealth/power explosion; liberal democracy + nuclear weapons), the calculus flipped for affluent liberal polities: positive-sum growth, high opportunity costs of war, and ultimate weapons made major war between them irrational and rare. Conquest ceased to pay; Kantian factors (democracy + interdependence + IOs) plus nuclear deterrence reinforce the peace among advanced states. Pre-modern participatory polities (Athens, Rome) still pursued material agrarian gains through war. Liberal democracies proved superior at total war mobilization but are constrained in small wars.

Remaining risks: failed states, ethnic/nationalist conflict, terrorism, and WMD proliferation in non-liberal zones.
