# The Cambridge History of Scandinavia, Volume II: 1520–1870

Second volume of the standard English-language reference for Scandinavian
history, covering the close of the Middle Ages to the formation of the Nordic
nation states in the mid-nineteenth century. Edited by E. I. Kouri (Helsinki)
and Jens E. Olesen (Greifswald), with 38 contributing historians. Companion to
[[helle-cambridge-history-scandinavia-v1-2003|Volume I (to 1520)]].

The volume's arc: the Lutheran Reformation and the reorganisation of the Nordic
monarchies (1520–1600); demographic and economic expansion and its limits; the
rise and fall of the Scandinavian power states and the militarisation of
society; the turn to absolutism (Denmark 1660, Sweden 1680); eighteenth-century
society, economy and culture; the Napoleonic crisis and the 1814 reordering; the
nineteenth-century economic and social transformation; and the emergence of five
distinct nation states.

## Volume Synthesis Note

The concluding chapter (Kouri & Olesen, ch. 50) frames the whole volume as the
transformation of **two large conglomerate early-modern states —
[[denmark-norway|Denmark–Norway]] and Sweden–Finland — into five distinct nation
states**. The [[kalmar-union-1397|Kalmar Union]] ended with the Stockholm Bloodbath
(1520); the [[reformation-in-scandinavia|Reformation]] — "in the first place the
great revolution in religion and church" with "by-products in … constitutional
changes" — built national Lutheran state churches, enriched the crowns, and bound
Scandinavia into Protestant northern Europe while cutting its Mediterranean cultural
ties.

The volume's distinctive analytical thread is the link between **naval and
fiscal-military capacity and state formation**. Denmark and Sweden ran among Europe's
largest navies into the mid-seventeenth century; this gave [[swedish-empire|Sweden]]
the operational freedom for Baltic expansion in the [[struggle-for-baltic-supremacy|
struggle for *dominium maris baltici*]]. Sweden's imperial rise (peak at the 1660–61
peace treaties) and collapse in the [[great-northern-war-1700|Great Northern War]]
(1720–21) "structurally determined the subsequent nation-state geography by leaving
Finland and Norway as distinct administrative entities." The turn to
[[scandinavian-absolutism|absolutism]] (Denmark 1660, Sweden 1680) was the domestic
settlement of those wars.

The **Napoleonic Wars were the decisive hinge**: the 1808–09 war transferred Finland
to Russia as a Grand Duchy, and the [[treaty-of-kiel-1814|Treaty of Kiel]] (1814)
broke the Danish–Norwegian union and created the Swedish–Norwegian union (1814–1905).
"The period after the Napoleonic Wars prepared the ground for Finland, Norway, and to
some extent also for Iceland, to become independent national states."

The nineteenth-century [[scandinavian-nation-state-formation|nation-building]]
processes are shown to be **structurally parallel** across the five countries —
Romantic nationalism on language/folklore/history, voluntary associations and the
press, communications infrastructure, [[scandinavian-popular-education|mass
schooling]], and the shift from estate to class society — yet with divergent outcomes:
the old kingdoms consolidated within contracted borders, while Norway, Finland, and
Iceland built states almost from scratch. The volume's verdict: "nation states and
national principles, and not the ideas of [[scandinavianism|Scandinavianism]] …
decided the future for the Nordic countries."

[[helle-cambridge-history-scandinavia-v1-2003|Vol. I]], carrying the Nordic spine from
1520 to 1870 and substantially filling a thin region for the [[early-modern|Early
