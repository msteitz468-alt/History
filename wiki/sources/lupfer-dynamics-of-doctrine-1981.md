# Timothy T. Lupfer, *The Dynamics of Doctrine: The Changes in German Tactical Doctrine During the First World War* (1981)

A concise but influential study (Leavenworth Paper No. 4) of how the German Army on the Western Front developed new tactical doctrine in response to the stalemate of trench warfare. Lupfer analyzes two major shifts: the adoption of elastic defense-in-depth in 1916-17 and the infiltration/offensive tactics of 1918. The book focuses on the *process* of doctrinal change — observation, experimentation, dissemination through training, and command emphasis — rather than merely describing the tactics.

This is a key source for the [[hubs/warfare/world-wars-era|WWI]] section of the warfare hub, illustrating German military adaptation, the interplay of defense and offense in attrition warfare, and the "dynamics" by which doctrine evolves under pressure.

## Historiography / impact note

Lupfer's paper is a foundational Leavenworth study still cited in professional military education for how armies adapt doctrine mid-conflict. It highlights that change came from a combination of top-down direction (OHL) and bottom-up experience (frontline units and staffs like Lossberg's), disseminated through intensive training rather than just publications. The work is practitioner-oriented, drawing lessons for contemporary doctrine development.

Strength for the wiki: provides clear, concise explanation of the two signature German tactical evolutions of WWI and the institutional process behind them. Directly useful for analyzing 1917-18 battles in the hub using the 9 principles of war, combined arms, and command philosophy.

Limitations: Focused narrowly on German Western Front tactics; does not cover broader strategic or Eastern Front issues; pre-digital era sourcing.

### Range 3 (rest of Ch. 2 + Chapter 3 Tentative Generalizations + end; lines 2501-3100)
**1918 tactics completion and incorporation of prior lessons:**
- The 1918 offensive doctrine "incorporated many of the virtues of their elastic defense-in-depth and which avoided several of the pitfalls into which the Allied tactical doctrine had fallen." (Synopsis)
- "The new offensive doctrine described the complementary use of firepower and maneuver, using infantry forces which avoided strongpoints and plunged deeply into enemy positions, aided by highly efficient and effective artillery fire." (Synopsis)
- "These doctrinal changes were not invented by one mastermind. Rather, they were the product of a thorough collective effort which enlisted the advice and experience of every possible source." (Synopsis; see also Ch. 3 discussion of Geyer and OHL use of talent)
- The defense "no longer retained terrain arbitrarily and the success of the defense ultimately depended upon the skilled and timely use of counterattack forces." (Synopsis)

**Training program:**
- "The tactical doctrine was disseminated to the units through a remarkable training program, and the entire procedure was directed with the knowledge that conditions and unique situations would always affect the application of the tactical doctrine." (Synopsis)
- OHL "directed the talent of Geyer and others to derive principles from combat experiences." Process gave "the final product the wide ownership that eased the acceptance and application of the doctrine." (end Ch. 2 discussion of doctrine process)
- "There was also a remarkable tolerance of dissent within the process, but this tolerance did not weaken the determination to succeed." High rank not prerequisite for talent: "All large armies possess men of talent like Capt. Hermann Geyer; few armies use such talent so efficiently." (end Ch. 2)

**Generalizations on doctrine dynamics / process (Chapter 3):**
- "The process of deriving the doctrine was inductive, and the application was in the same inductive spirit." "Evidence from the battlefield was more respected than the doctrine, both in development and execution." (Ch. 2/3 transition)
- "Too often on the Allied side, tactics originally derived from experiences (either accurately or erroneously) became deductive formulas indiscriminately deemed appropriate for any situation, despite contradictory evidence or changed conditions." Contrast: "The Germans never attributed such certainty to their doctrine. This degree of uncertainty fostered a healthy curiosity and mental flexibility. No evidence was rejected or ignored simply because it did not 'fit' the preconceived scheme." (text on process)
- "For the Germans all tactical solutions were tentative: the Germans developed tactical doctrine inductively and applied and refined it in the same spirit." (Ch. 3)
- "This process still demands much talent and ability, and it still requires a deliberate search for evidence. Glib solutions do not replace hard work." (Ch. 3)
- Quote from Crown Prince Rupprecht: “There is no panacea. A formula is harmful. Everything must be applied according to the situation." (Ch. 3)
- Corporate staff effort under [[ludendorff]]: "It is perhaps his foremost merit, itself a sign of greatness, that he selected and never hesitated to employ the best men. . . . In fact, under the name of Ludendorff is comprised, exactly what a staff should be, a composite brain." (Ch. 2 notes, quoted from obituary in *Army Quarterly*)
- "OHL directed the talent of Geyer and others to derive principles from combat experiences. These principles were sufficiently general to apply to a variety of tactical conditions, but sufficiently precise and specific to insure common understanding and unity of effort. Their flexibility was their strength, for these carefully and accurately developed principles could be modified without being discarded." (text on 1918-era doctrine completion)
