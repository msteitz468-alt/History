# Isidore of Seville, *On the Nature of Things* (*De natura rerum*)

A concise treatise on the **cosmos, time and the natural world** by **[[isidore-of-seville|Isidore of
Seville]]**, written c. 612–615 for the Visigothic king **Sisebut** — a window onto how the **last
scholar of the ancient world** preserved and Christianized classical science for the Latin Middle Ages.
The twelfth and final Phase-5 source of this batch.

## The Work

*De natura rerum* is an **encyclopedic compendium of natural knowledge** in some 48 short chapters: the
divisions and reckoning of **time** (days, months, years, seasons, the calendar and Easter
**computus**); the **heavens** (the sky, sun, moon, stars, eclipses, the planets); the **elements** and
**meteorological** phenomena (clouds, rain, thunder, winds, the rainbow); and the earth, seas and their
parts. Isidore compiles his material from **classical and patristic sources** — Pliny the Elder, a lost
work of Suetonius, Ambrose, Augustine, Hyginus — simplifying and harmonizing pagan natural philosophy
with Christian doctrine, and often supplying allegorical-spiritual readings alongside the physical
explanations. It circulated widely (the editors chart three recensions) and, with Bede's later work of
the same name, became a standard medieval handbook of cosmology and time-reckoning.

## Significance

The treatise exemplifies **Isidore's method and his historical role**: the gathering of ancient learning
into accessible, orthodox compendia for a Latin world that could no longer reach the originals. It is a
key document of the **transmission of classical science** and of the early-medieval **computus**
tradition, and a companion to his vast *Etymologies*. As such it illuminates the intellectual culture of
**Visigothic Spain** at its height and the bridge Isidore built between antiquity and the Middle Ages.
