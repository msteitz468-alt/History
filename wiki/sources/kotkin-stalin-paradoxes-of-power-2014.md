# Stalin, Volume 1: Paradoxes of Power, 1878–1928 (Stephen Kotkin, 2014)

First volume of Stephen Kotkin's landmark three-volume biography of Joseph Stalin. Focuses on Stalin's rise from obscure Georgian seminary student to undisputed leader of the Soviet Union by 1928-29. Kotkin stresses the interplay of imperial Russian structural legacies, Bolshevik revolutionary culture, Stalin's personal agency and psychology, and the contingencies of civil war, succession struggles, and policy choices.

The book challenges myths: not a nonentity until late; active in 1917 and Civil War; consolidated power through party apparatus rather than pure terror initially; "Socialism in One Country" as pragmatic adaptation; early signs of the personal dictatorship that would produce the Great Terror and industrialization drive.

#### S06-01 Stalin Speech "On the Grain Front" at Institute of Red Professors (late May/early June 1928)
- Stalin delivered a major speech outlining immediate, wholesale agricultural modernization through large-scale collectivized farms and newly founded massive state farms where none existed.
- The speech reprised his January 20 Novosibirsk peroration and presented "his plan for the future 'collective farm revolution'" for the first time publicly.
- "Stalin spoke quietly, monotonously, and with long pauses... Of course, Stalin had a Georgian accent, which became especially noticeable when he got nervous. He spoke for about two hours without stopping. He frequently drank water from a glass. Once, when he lifted the carafe, it was empty. Laughter erupted in the hall. A person in the presidium handed Stalin a new carafe. Stalin gulped down nearly a full glass, then turned to the audience and said, with a mischievous laugh: 'There, you see, he who laughs last, laughs best! Anyway, I have finished.' Applause broke out." After recess he answered written questions (some irreverent).
- Speech published in *Pravda* (June 2, 1928). [[stalin-joseph]] [[soviet-collectivization]] [[bolshevik-party]] [[moscow]]

#### S06-02 Youth League Militants and Coercive Grain Procurement (spring/summer 1928)
- Communist Youth League membership had risen from 22,000 (late 1918) to more than 2 million (of nearly 30 million eligible); about one third of party members by late 1920s had once been Youth League members.
- Stalin’s apparatus dispatched armed Youth League militants (among others) to villages: they measured “surpluses” by the eye, smashed villagers on the head with revolvers, and locked peasants in latrines until they yielded grain stores.
- In parallel, police arrests under Article 107 and Article 58 spiked again in May and June 1928, provoking spontaneous “dekulakization.” Many peasants fled to cities or joined collective farms out of fear.
- [[stalin-joseph]] [[bolshevik-party]] [[soviet-collectivization]]

#### S06-03 Peasant Resistance and Regime Responses (1928)
- Some peasants organized resistance: “The grain reserves in the village will not be turned over to the government,” resolved a group in Western Siberia’s Biysk county (where Stalin had secretly visited). A poor peasant told the rural soviet chairman: “Give grain to us poor peasants. If not, we will take it by force. ... We must take all the grain and establish a clean soviet power, without Communists.” Others reported saying, “Let’s get our pikes and become partisans.”
- Rumors of foreign invasion and return of the Whites spread. One letter to Rykov’s government: “The peasantry is under the yoke of the bandit Stalin. The poorest peasant and worker is your enemy.”
- The siege generated evidence of the need for a siege, per OGPU reports of spreading “kulak” moods, Ukrainian “nationalist” moods, and “peasant” moods in the army. [[soviet-union]] [[stalin-joseph]] [[soviet-collectivization]]

#### S06-04 Bukharin Letters to Stalin and Ideological Alarm (May–early June 1928)
- Stalin had stopped speaking to Bukharin (just as he often refused to speak with his wife Nadya). Bukharin sent letters addressed to “Koba” trying to get through.
- Bukharin wrote: “I consider the country’s internal and external situation *very* difficult,” with no thought-through plan on taxes, goods, prices, or imports. He noted Jan Sten saying the 15th Party Congress had been *mistaken* and Trotskyites vindicated. “our extraordinary measures (necessary) are, ideologically, *already* being transformed, growing into a new political line.”
- Bukharin: “Collective farms, which will only be built over several years, will not carry us. We will be unable to provide them with working capital and machines right away.”
- Stalin did not respond. [[bukharin-nikolai]] [[stalin-joseph]] [[soviet-collectivization]] [[bolshevik-party]]

#### S06-05 June 27 Politburo Row and Stalin-Bukharin Confrontation
- At politburo meeting June 27, Bukharin, Tomsky, and Rykov declared party policy in rupture; Molotov denounced their declaration as “antiparty.”
- Stalin received Bukharin in his office: “You and I are the Himalayas—the others are non-entities” (per Bukharin’s wife’s memoirs). At a politburo meeting Stalin laced into Bukharin; when Bukharin divulged the flattery (including “non-entities”), Stalin shouted, “You lie. You invented this story to poison the other members of the politburo against me.” [[bukharin-nikolai]] [[stalin-joseph]] [[molotov-viacheslav]] [[bolshevik-party]]

#### S06-06 July 1928 Plenum: Economic Crisis and Shakhty Verdicts
- Joint plenum of Central Committee and Central Control Commission opened; first days Comintern affairs. July 6 Mikoyan reported foreign trade in “an extraordinarily strained situation.” Oil production exceeded domestic use but could not replace grain export revenues (tsarist levels perhaps one third at best).
- Night of July 6–7 (1:30 a.m. July 7), Andrei Vyshinsky read Shakhty trial verdicts in Hall of Columns. Four of 53 acquitted (incl. two Germans); four suspended sentences; eleven death sentences pronounced (six commuted); nearly forty imprisoned (mostly 4–10 years). No stand-alone transcript published. [[soviet-union]] [[stalin-joseph]] [[moscow]]

#### S06-07 Stalin "Tribute" Speech at July 1928 Plenum (July 9)
- Stalin defended extraordinary measures as saving the country because “we had no reserves.” “Those who say extraordinary measures are bad under any circumstances are wrong.”
- On grand strategy and “internal resources” only (no colonies, indemnities, or long-term loans): peasants “pay the state not only the usual taxes, direct and indirect, but they also overpay in relatively high prices for industrial goods, first of all, and, second, they underreceive in prices for agricultural produce. This is an additional tax on the peasantry in the interests of raising industry... something like ‘tribute’ [*dan’*], something like a supertax, which we are forced to take temporarily... without an additional tax on the peasantry, unfortunately, our industry and our country cannot make do.”
- Rejected raising grain prices (e.g. Sokolnikov proposal). “Is it necessary to close the ‘scissors’... Yes, unquestionably... Can we eliminate them now, without weakening our industry... No, we cannot.”
- “As we advance, the resistance of the capitalist elements will grow, the class struggle will become sharper, and Soviet power... will carry out a policy of isolation of these elements... a policy of suppression of the resistance of the exploiters.” “the movement towards socialism must lead to resistance by the exploiting elements... and the resistance of the exploiters must lead to an inevitable sharpening of the class struggle.”
- Stalin portrayed the peasant decision not to sell at low prices as a “grain strike,” “the first serious action, under the conditions of NEP, undertaken by the capitalist elements of the countryside against the Soviet government.” [[stalin-joseph]] [[soviet-collectivization]] [[soviet-industrialization]] [[soviet-union]] [[bolshevik-party]]

#### S06-08 Bukharin Plenum Response and Stalin Tactical Retreat (July 10–11, 1928)
- Bukharin (still fearing “opposition” trap) insisted on facts: some 150 major protests; “a revolt in Semipalatinsk, violence at the Leningrad and Moscow labor exchanges, an uprising in Kabardiya.” Between May 20 and June 15, thirteen violent conflicts at labor exchanges. He had read OGPU political mood reports personally.
- Bukharin: extraordinary measures had to be stopped (or “you’ll get an uprising of the peasant, whom the kulak will take on... The petit bourgeois spontaneity will rise up against the proletariat, smash it in the head, and as a result of the sharpest class struggle the proletarian dictatorship will disappear.”). Stalin shouted: “A terrible dream, but God is merciful (laughter).”
- Stalin polemicized with Tomsky: retreat without end would not strengthen worker-peasant alliance. “A policy of permanent concessions is not our policy.”
- Shock: plenum unanimously repealed the “extraordinary measures.” Grain prices soon raised; unauthorized searches/arrests and bazaar closings punishable; Article 107 cases against poor/middle peasants discontinued; amnesties for those behind bars. [[bukharin-nikolai]] [[stalin-joseph]] [[soviet-collectivization]] [[bolshevik-party]]

#### S06-09 Intrigue: Bukharin-Kamenev Kremlin Meeting (July 11, 1928)
- Stalin’s minions spread false rumor that Bukharin et al. had voted against Zinoviev/Kamenev readmission. Kamenev (from Kaluga internal exile) met Bukharin in Kremlin apartment (Sokolnikov intermediary; line tapped by OGPU).
- Bukharin (emotional; lips shaking): “We consider Stalin’s line fatal for the whole revolution. The disagreements between us and Stalin are many times more serious than they had been with you. Rykov, Tomsky, and I unanimously formulate the situation as follows: ‘it would be a lot better if in the politburo we had Zinoviev and Kamenev instead of Stalin.’”
- Bukharin: “He is an unprincipled intriguer, who subordinates everything to the maintenance of his own power. He shifts theory on the basis of who at any given moment he wants to remove.” Stalin “had made concessions” at the July plenum “in order to put a knife in us” and “was maneuvering to make us into schismatics.” Stalin “had not suggested a single execution in the Shakhty case,” sitting back while others did it, appearing the moderate.
- Bukharin mocked Stalin’s “tribute” and “sharpening of the class struggle” as “idiotic illiteracy.” Allies: himself, Tomsky, Rykov, Uglanov, some Leningraders (not Ukrainians, “bought off” by removing Kaganovich); “Yagoda and Trillisser are with us”; Voroshilov and Kalinin “went back at the last minute.” Orjonikidze “is no knight... at the decisive moment he betrayed us.” “there is a terrible fear of a split.”
- Bukharin warned Kamenev not to phone (eavesdropped) and that they were tailed; Kamenev took detailed notes. [[bukharin-nikolai]] [[kamenev-lev]] [[zinoviev-grigory]] [[stalin-joseph]] [[molotov-viacheslav]] [[soviet-union]] [[bolshevik-party]]

#### S06-10 Fallout from Bukharin-Kamenev Meeting
- Kamenev mailed notes to Zinoviev in Kaluga (highly likely intercepted).
- Rykov (summoned by Stalin) learned of the meeting and confronted Bukharin: “silly woman, not a politician.”
- Stalin could rely on Molotov (and Kaganovich); Bukharin “woefully lacked sufficient political calculation.” The episode implicated Orjonikidze and Yagoda (who submitted explanations to Stalin). “Bukharin’s conspiracy with Kamenev... handed Stalin a gargantuan gift.” [[bukharin-nikolai]] [[stalin-joseph]] [[kamenev-lev]] [[molotov-viacheslav]] [[soviet-union]]

#### S06-11 Molotov, Chinese Affairs, and External Observations (July 1928)
- Molotov closed Soviet plenum July 12 with report on training new specialists: RSFSR had only 117 Ph.D. students in technical subjects; labs and textbooks dated to 19th century.
- Sixth Party Congress of Chinese Communist Party concluded in Moscow (first outside China; 84 delegates; Mao stayed home). Moscow acceded to separate CCP army units but Stalin insisted under Guomindang flag despite Chiang massacres. Chiang seized Peking July 6.
- External view (Bakhmeteff letter Aug 1928): Stalin one of few “incontrovertible fanatics”; recognized need to place agricultural production in state hands like industry; kulaks (often just peasants with 2 horses/2-3 cows) performed gentry function but were being strangled per “Marxist logic.” [[molotov-viacheslav]] [[stalin-joseph]] [[soviet-industrialization]] [[soviet-collectivization]]

#### S06-12 Comintern Sixth Congress and Attacks on Bukharin/"Right" (July–Sept 1928)
- Sixth Comintern Congress opened Moscow July 17 (through Sept 1; >500 attendees from >50 parties; first since 1924).
- Stalin reached for truncheon against duumvirate partner: rumors Bukharin’s days numbered, next for internal exile to Alma-Ata. Molotov inserted into executive committee to ramp up “pogrom against ‘rightist tendencies.’”
- Bukharin stopped turning up at Comintern HQ (still nominally head). Stalin compelled Bukharin to write congress program documents then humiliatingly crossed out and rewrote everything; left declaration in Bukharin’s name.
- “Social fascism” slogan (lumping social democrats with fascists) forced through despite objections (Togliatti, Bukharin). [[bukharin-nikolai]] [[stalin-joseph]] [[molotov-viacheslav]] [[soviet-union]]

#### S06-13 Bukharin "Notes of an Economist" and Stalin Response (Sept 1928)
- Valerian Kuibyshev (Sept 19) told Leningrad party a five-year plan for industry would go forward ambitiously, dismissing “over-industrializing” critics.
- Bukharin responded in *Pravda* (Sept 30) “Notes of an Economist” (ostensibly vs. “Trotskyites” = Kuibyshev/Stalin): demanded balanced, “crisis-free” industrialization. “it is not possible to build ‘present-day’ factories with ‘future bricks.’” Predicted total market elimination + forced collectivization would produce unfathomable red tape.
- Stalin began but never finished written response. Over objections of Rykov/Tomsky/Bukharin, politburo reprimanded *Pravda* for publishing without Central Committee authorization.
- Stalin to Mikoyan (Sept 26 from Sochi): “No matter how well the grain procurements might go, they would not remove the basis of our difficulties... Many thought that removing the extraordinary measures and raising grain prices would be the basis of eliminating the difficulties. Empty hopes of empty Bolshevik liberals!” [[bukharin-nikolai]] [[stalin-joseph]] [[soviet-industrialization]] [[soviet-collectivization]] [[soviet-union]]

#### S06-14 Third Wave Coercive Procurements, Bread Rationing, and Bukharin-Stalin Exchange (fall 1928)
- A third wave of coercive procurements struck villages fall 1928 with greater force than first (Jan-Feb) or second (late April-early July).
- Before year’s end, regime formally announced introduction of bread rationing in the major cities.
- Bukharin (surmising later): “Instead of simply telling me, ‘We do not trust you, Bukharin... let’s part ways’... It was initially necessary to smear, discredit, trample, then it would no longer be a question of agreeing to my request to resign but instead ‘removal’ ‘for sabotage.’ The game is absolutely clear.”
- In 6-hour audience with Stalin, Bukharin: “I do not want to fight, because it will harm the party. If a fight starts, you’ll declare us renegades from Leninism.” “But we’ll call you organizers of famine.” Stalin immovable. [[stalin-joseph]] [[bukharin-nikolai]] [[soviet-collectivization]] [[soviet-union]]

#### S06-15 Orjonikidze Reconciliation Effort and Uglanov Removal (Nov 1928)
- Orjonikidze (back from Germany medical treatment) wrote long letter to downcast Rykov (Nov 1928): “A conversation with you and with others (Stalin) persuades me that there are no fundamental differences... I am frankly imploring you to bring about reconciliation between Bukharin and Stalin.”
- Stalin went after Nikolai Uglanov (onetime protégé, Moscow party boss, persecutor of Trotskyites who sided with Bukharin); replaced by Molotov late November.
- Bukharin finally obtained long-sought audience with Stalin (six hours). [[bukharin-nikolai]] [[stalin-joseph]] [[molotov-viacheslav]] [[soviet-union]]

#### S06-16 Coda Counterfactual Framing: "If Stalin Had Died" (from range)
- “HE WOULD DO IT. Stalin would force the collectivization of Soviet villages and nomadic steppes inhabited by more than 100 million people between 1928 and 1933... At least 5 million people, many of the country’s most productive farmers or herders, would be ‘dekulakized’... Those forced into the collectives would burn crops, slaughter animals, and assassinate officials.”
- Livestock collapse: horses 35M to 17M; cattle 70M to 38M; pigs 26M to 12M; sheep/goats 147M to 50M (Kazakhstan even worse). Nearly 40M suffered severe hunger/starvation; 5–7M died in famine (regime denied existence). “All the dogs have been eaten... the trees have been stripped of bark... the horse manure has been eaten.”
- Scholars arguing collectivization necessary for modernization “are dead wrong.” Market systems fully compatible with fast industrialization (incl. in peasant countries). Collectivization failed to deliver: procurements doubled but harvests shrank; “contribute[d] nothing on net to Soviet industrial growth.”

### Range 08 Extraction (lines ~30001–35000, exclusive cache; Civil War / 1917–1920 rise and front experience)
**All claims, quotes, locators, and framing from this range ONLY. Links restricted to established names.**

- Stalin as established operative by 1917 (Chiatura 1905 experience counter to “ill-suited to mass politics” claims); practical grounding in direct action/local networks vs. abstract theorizing.
- 1917 editorials (*Rabochii put’* Aug–Oct): “Protiv soglasheniia s burzhuaziei,” “My trebuem,” “Dve linii,” “Kontrrevoliutsiia mobilizuetsia—gotovtes’ k otporu” — hard-line against conciliation, for workers’/peasants’ government and soviet power. Confiscated editorial verbatim: “The existing government of landlords and capitalists must be replaced by a new government, a government of workers and peasants... If all of you act solidly and staunchly, no one will dare to resist the will of the people” (*Sochineniia* III: 390).
- Trotsky on October role: “I spent the decisive night of October 25–26 together with Kamenev in the offices of the Military Revolutionary Committee... I simply cannot answer the question of what precisely was Stalin’s role in those decisive days” (*Stalinskaia shkola fal’sifikatsii*, 26).
- Named People’s Commissar for Nationalities in first Sovnarkom; Pestkovskii: “Lenin... could not get along without Stalin for even a single day.”
- Tsaritsyn 1918 (grain/front): “warlord” with Voroshilov/Minin; direct procurement, resist full subordination to Sytin/Trotsky; “meet the violence of grain owners with the violence against grain owners” logic. Some grain delivered under dire conditions; city held; direct clashes with Trotsky (“atmosphere of Tsaritsyn, with its administrative anarchy, guerilla disrespect for the Center...”); Lenin brokered peace. Called Snesarev a “flaccid military leader.”
- 1919–20: Petrograd defense (1919); Southwestern Front commissar (1920); refused co-sign transfer of Budyonny cavalry from Lwów (contributed to Warsaw issues per analyses); nationalities praxis (Turkestan/Bukhara, critiques of Great Russian chauvinism vs. local nationalisms).
- Emerged with “much power” and practical knowledge of coercion/mobilization/terror despite “little glory” (Kotkin/McNeal). Lenin valued “ability ‘to exert pressure’.”
- Sources/grounding (verbatim in range): RGASPI (f.558, f.17); *Sochineniia* III; Trotsky memoirs (*My Life*, *Stalin*); Nosovich “Krasnyi Tsaritsyn”; Budennyi memoirs; Sukhanov/Rabinowitch; Pestkovskii; Lenin notes (PSS); 8th/10th Congress protocols. Kotkin cross-checks; notes Trotsky bias (downplays Stalin) and later Stalinist inflation of Tsaritsyn. Reliability: multiple telegrams/eyewitnesses strengthen claims; civil war taught “State coercion as the secret of success; mobilization, propaganda, military might, and terror.”
- Dictatorship compatible with private capital (cf. fascist Italy under Mussolini, who subordinated economics to political supremacy without destroying successful people via class struggle idées fixes).
- Global deflation hit revenues but Stalin’s January 20 Siberia speech made no mention of world economy as factor. No adverse turn compelled the turn.
- Russian state not simply “infamous strong-arming”: Alexander emancipated serfs; Stolypin reforms voluntary. Stalin ruled out Stolypin route of betting on independent yeoman farmers (kulaks).
- “NEP Russia could become socialist Russia” (Lenin’s last public speech) “solved” by negating capitalism via state-run industry and collectivization.
- NEP via its own middling success produced kulaks who produced the harvest. Class analysis (to which all top Bolsheviks subscribed) ensured NEP had to fail if it succeeded. A farmer with three cows in 1925 who had six by 1928 became a “class-enemy.”
- Bukharin presented no genuine alternative (lacked political heft/organizational base). Rykov (chair of politburo meetings, 15th Congress opener) was far more authoritative NEP proponent: gregarious, hearty, prudent, favoring fiscal discipline; put premium on class conciliation stability. Rykov did not dispute eventual large-scale mechanized “socialist” farms but held forced collectivization could simply not be done without destroying what existed. ### Range 10 Extraction (lines ~40001–45000, final analysis / synthesis range; mid-1924–1927 succession, opposition defeats, “Socialism in One Country”, China policy)
**All claims, quotes, locators, and framing from this range ONLY. Links restricted to established names.**

- Testament handling at XIII Congress (May 1924): read to delegations/council of elders only, not full congress or widely published. Reaction: “when these few pages written by Lenin were read to the members of the Central Committee the reaction was one of incomprehension and alarm” (Yaroslavsky account). Krupskaya had delayed handover; commission (incl. Stalin) controlled access. Stalin reelected GS.
- Stalin on Trotsky (ciphered telegram, early 1925): “It’s necessary to dismiss him from the Revolutionary Military Council.”
- “Socialism in One Country” article (Dec 1924): “victory of socialism (not in the sense of final victory) is absolutely possible in one country.” Institutionalized in Comintern; used against Trotsky.
- XIV Congress (Dec 1925): defeat of New Opposition (Zinoviev/Kamenev). Stalin-Bukharin alliance; Zinoviev lost Leningrad base (Kirov installed later).
- Institutional power: secretariat appointment power; replacement of local secretaries; “septet”/leading collective.
- China united-front policy: maintained support for Guomindang despite tensions; “armed revolution fighting armed counterrevolution”; major setback with Chiang’s April 1927 Shanghai coup and later breaks.
- Dzerzhinsky death (July 1926): immediate tributes, cult elements; replacement by Mezynski.
- Frunze death (Oct 1925): operation; rumors of pressure (Stalin/Zinoviev via medical commission); inconclusive.
- Verbatim: Stalin on kulaks/cooperatives (~Jan 1925): “This would have a gigantic significance... In the management of society even one kulak would be dangerous.”

#### S09-01 Kronstadt Rebellion (February–March 1921): Outbreak and Initial Regime Response
- Sailors and workers at Kronstadt (naval base near Petrograd) rose against Bolshevik policies amid post-Civil War hardships; issued resolutions demanding "Soviets without Communists," free trade, and end to one-party dictatorship while claiming loyalty to Soviet power without Communist monopoly.
- "1. Down with Communism and Communist power over the Russian Socialist Republic, for not implementing the interests of the majority of the working people of the Russian Socialist Soviet Republic. 2. Long live Soviet power, that is, that power which will realize the interests of the working peoples of the Russian Socialist Soviet Republic." Workers also demanded "a state without bloodshed" and "Long live truth, freedom of speech and the press in the free Socialist Republic." RGASPI, f. 2, op. 2, d. 561, l. 40.
- Regime initially portrayed events as SR/Menshevik plot or White Guard agitation; Cheka deputy chairman ordered intensified struggle against "counterrevolution." Politburo took hard line February 28. [[lenin-vladimir]] [[bolshevik-party]] [[soviet-union]]

#### S09-02 Kronstadt Rebellion: Revolutionary Committee, Hostages, and Party Resignations
- Kronstadt Revolutionary Committee formed (no former tsarist officers on the 15-member body, though some invited for defense planning); published *Izvestiya Vremennogo revoliutsionnogo komiteta*; slogan often rendered (by Miliukov and Soviet propaganda) as "Soviets without Communists."
- Regime took hostages including family of Kozlovsky (27 people) and Petrichenko (even those sharing only the name); at least 900 of 2,680 Communist party members and candidates at Kronstadt quit the party, many requesting publication of resignations.
- "The absence of evidence for Kozlovsky’s role was whitewashed." [[lenin-vladimir]] [[bolshevik-party]] [[soviet-union]]

#### S09-03 Suppression of Kronstadt (March 1921): Tukhachevsky, 27th Division, and Executions
- Tukhachevsky commanded crackdown; a Siberian infantry division considered "the absolute most reliable" refused orders. "If the 27th Division will not do it, no one will." Revolutionary tribunal sentenced insubordinate troops to execution (broadcast in newspapers March 15).
- Only three of fifteen Revolutionary Committee members captured (Perepelkin, Vershinin, Val’k); majority of political refugees later returned under amnesty. Many Communist leaders later executed in 1930s.
- "On March 15, a revolutionary tribunal sentenced many of the insubordinate troops to execution, which newspapers broadcast." [[trotsky-leon]] [[lenin-vladimir]] [[bolshevik-party]] [[soviet-union]]

#### S09-04 10th Party Congress (March 1921): Adoption of Tax in Kind and End of Requisitioning (NEP Birth)
- Congress replaced prodrazverstka (grain requisitioning) with prodnalog (fixed tax in kind); allowed peasants to sell surpluses after tax, marking shift to NEP.
- Lenin: "first of all we must convince, then coerce [*prinudit’*]. We have not been able to convince the broad masses." Trotsky had proposed similar tax-in-kind measures a year earlier but was rebuffed.
- "Why was the food requisitioning allowed to continue during the autumn of 1920 and the spring of 1921, when the civil war had been won and the famine crisis was already widespread?" (Figes cited in text). [[lenin-vladimir]] [[trotsky-leon]] [[bolshevik-party]] [[soviet-union]]

#### S09-05 10th Party Congress: Ban on Factions and "On Party Unity" Resolution
- Congress passed resolution "On Party Unity" banning factions and factional platforms within the party (last time non-apparatus resolutions were submitted for vote).
- Lenin argued putting disagreements before broad discussion and congress was "a great mistake" because "the party is sick."
- Workers’ Opposition and others opposed aspects but resolution passed; only they opposed "on party unity." [[lenin-vladimir]] [[bolshevik-party]] [[soviet-union]]

#### S09-07 Tambov (Antonovshchina) Uprising and Tukhachevsky’s Counterinsurgency (1921)

#### S09-08 Treaty of Riga (1921) and Anglo-Soviet Trade Agreement
- Treaty of Riga ended Polish-Soviet War; Poland gained western Belorussia and western Ukraine (~52,000 sq mi), becoming ~30% minority population (5m Ukrainians, 1.5m Belorussians, 1m Germans, 3m Jews). Entente recognized borders March 1923; Germany refused.
- Anglo-Soviet trade agreement (March 16, 1921) signed; Krasin involved. Lithuania’s independence reconfirmed amid Polish-Lithuanian Wilno/Vilnius disputes (Moscow did not intervene).
- Moscow later halted repatriation payments; never paid agreed 30m gold rubles (reduced from 300m claim). [[soviet-union]] [[bolshevik-party]]

#### S09-10 Defeat and Execution of Baron Ungern-Sternberg in Mongolia (1921)

#### S09-13 Stalin Appointed General Secretary (April 1922, 11th Party Congress)
- At 11th Party Congress (April 1922), Central Committee appointed Stalin General Secretary (formalized April 3). He relinquished prior government posts (nationalities, Rabkrin) to focus on party apparatus while retaining Kremlin office.
- Molotov had headed secretariat earlier; Lenin told him to "take up politics [policy], and delegate all the technical work to deputies." Secretariat handled passes to offices (up from 82,859 in 1920).
- "There was nothing ‘automatic’ about the process of Stalin’s elevation during the twenties… It took an uncommonly gifted man to navigate the treacherous waters of Bolshevik politics with the skill that he showed in those years."
