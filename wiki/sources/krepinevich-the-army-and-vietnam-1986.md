# The Army and Vietnam (Krepinevich, 1986)

Krepinevich's *The Army and Vietnam* is the definitive institutional and doctrinal history of why the United States Army failed to defeat the communist insurgency in South Vietnam despite overwhelming material superiority. It argues that the Army was captured by a deeply embedded "Army Concept" — a worldview forged in World War II and Korea that prioritized mid-intensity conventional war, large-unit operations, massive firepower, and attrition of enemy forces over the population-centric, political, and constabulary requirements of counterinsurgency.

The book traces this rigidity across three phases: the advisory years (1954–1965), when the Army had the opportunity but not the inclination to build South Vietnamese forces for internal war; the years of intervention (1965–1968), when MACV under Westmoreland pursued a "strategy of tactics" centered on search-and-destroy and body-count metrics; and the years of withdrawal (1968–1973), when even after Tet the service resisted fundamental change and fell back on technological fixes and Vietnamization.

## What it contributes

- Detailed reconstruction of the **"Army Concept"** as the central explanatory variable for U.S. strategic failure in Vietnam.
- Primary-sourced analysis of how personnel policies (short tours, career incentives), training institutions, organizational structures (ROAD divisions, airmobility), and command priorities systematically privileged conventional operations over pacification and advisory work.
- Case studies of specific programs (CIDG, CAP, CORDS, defoliation, HES, PROVN) and why they were marginalized.
- Critical examination of Tet 1968 as "defeat in victory" for the U.S. Army's approach.
- Closing argument on post-Vietnam lessons (or lack thereof) and the recurring preference for transforming low-intensity threats into conventional ones the Army knows how to fight.

## Historiography Note

Krepinevich writes as both insider (Army officer) and critic. His focus is organizational learning failure rather than high policy or Vietnamese agency. The work is widely cited in post-Vietnam "COIN school" literature and influenced the 2006 FM 3-24. It is the essential U.S. Army-centric counterpart to broader diplomatic and Vietnamese-nationalist accounts.

## Volume Synthesis Note

Krepinevich demonstrates that the U.S. Army's defeat in Vietnam was not primarily a failure of will, resources, or even tactics at the small-unit level, but a failure of institutional learning rooted in an organizational culture optimized for the wrong war. The "Army Concept" — mid-intensity conventional attrition with lavish firepower — was applied to an insurgency that could only be defeated by securing the population and destroying the political infrastructure (VCI). Advisory years offered a window that was squandered; intervention years saw the Concept applied at full scale; withdrawal years produced only limited adaptation even under Abrams. The book provides the essential Army-side institutional explanation that complements diplomatic histories (Leffler–Westad) and comparative COIN studies (Nagl).

Cross-volume: directly grounds [[army-concept]], enriches [[vietnam-war]] with precise causal mechanics (44-battalion request, Ia Drang validation, PROVN suppression, body-count incentives, defoliation costs, Tet "defeat in victory"), and supplies the negative case for [[learning-institution]] (concurrent with Nagl's positive British Malaya case). Strongly recommended for warfare hub doctrinal/strategy pages on limited/irregular war.

## Range 04 Extraction — Krepinevich The Army and Vietnam (remainder of "The Other War", Ch. 9 "Tet: Defeat in Victory", Ch. 10 "Paths Untaken, Paths Forsaken", Conclusions)

**Key claims with verbatim grounding (Ch. 8 remainder–Conclusions / locators from slice):**

- Claim title: "Ruff-Puffs (RF/PF) performance despite neglect (remainder 'The Other War')": RF/PF "accounted for roughly 30 percent of the VC/NVA combat deaths inflicted by the RVNAF" and "12-30 percent of all VC/NVA combat deaths (depending on the year), yet they consumed only 2-4 percent of the total annual cost of the war." "Except in 1968, it was more dangerous to serve in the RF and PF units than in the ARVN." "the only certain way of forcing the insurgents out into the open is to deny them access to their source of sustenance—the population—through an effective pacification program of which paramilitary forces are an integral part." (remainder Ch. 8, locs near table 6 discussion). Implication for [[vietnam-war]], [[counterinsurgency]], [[army-concept]] (mismatch with Concept).

- Claim title: "Army priority to big-unit ops over pacification (remainder 'The Other War')": Lt. Gen. Ewell: "I had two rules. One is that you would try to get a very close meshing of pacification . . . and military operations. The other rule is the military operations would be given first priority in every case." Operations such as MASHER/WHITE WING and THAYER II/PERSHING in Binh Dinh "freed over 10,000 war victims" but created refugees (Bong Son 7,806 to 27,652; Hoai An 7,514 to 17,622) with "lavish use of firepower" (over 132,000 artillery rounds in first op; 1,000 rounds per kill avg.), then units "quickly moved on". "the Army waged counterinsurgency on the cheap." (remainder Ch. 8). Linked to [[westmoreland-william]], [[vietnam-war]].

- Claim title: "Refugee creation as Army policy byproduct (remainder 'The Other War')": "Between 1964 and 1969 over 3 million South Vietnamese, 20 percent of the population, were refugees at one time or another as a result of the attrition strategy and the policy of population relocation." Army saw refugee flows as success ("people have fled in great numbers from communist regimes"); PROVN: "US-RVNAF bombing and artillery fire, in conjunction with ground operations are the immediate and prime causes of refugee movement". "In Vietnam the Army was not the guardian of great numbers of South Vietnamese refugees fleeing Communist oppression but, rather, a primary cause of this calamity." (remainder Ch. 8). Implication [[vietnam-war]].

- Claim title: "Neglect of VCI and intelligence focus (remainder 'The Other War')": Army intelligence "focused on the enemy’s big units." "the weight of the intelligence effort was aimed at discovering how many divisions they had, not how many were placed at the village level or the hamlet level." Komer: "to the total neglect of the guerrillas and the so-called Viet Cong infrastructure, the political-military apparatus that was really running the war." Phoenix (Phung Hoang) "small, poorly managed, and largely ineffective"; "only 3 percent of the vci killed, captured, or rallied were full or probationary Party members above the district level"; "only about 20 percent of the vci killed, captured, or rallied were accounted for by forces assigned to Phung Hoang" (Ruff-Puffs 39-50%). (remainder Ch. 8). For [[vietnam-war]], [[counterinsurgency]].

- Claim title: "Tet Offensive scale and Army view of victory (Ch. 9)": "Employing 100,000 troops, the VC/NVA launched assaults on Saigon as well as 36 of the 43 provincial capitals, 5 of the 6 autonomous cities, and 64 of the 242 district capitals." "The body count at the end of February was estimated at 37,000, with another 6,000 captured. The VCI bore the brunt of the losses... some estimates running as high as 30 percent." "General Westmoreland went so far as to compare Tet with the Battle of the Bulge—the last-gasp effort of an enemy on his last legs." "the Communists had utterly failed in their attacks." Khe Sanh diversion "drew most of the U.S. command’s attention"; Taylor: "Khe Sanh just looked like the last place you would want to have a large garrison." (Ch. 9). Linked to [[vietnam-war]], [[westmoreland-william]].

- Claim title: "Civilian shock and troop request fallout (Ch. 9)": Westmoreland had promised 1968 "the year when the allies would finally turn the corner"; Tet produced "full-blown review of U.S. strategy in Vietnam, ending in the explicit rejection of the Army strategy by the civilian leadership." Wheeler to Johnson: "the judgment is that he has the will and the capability to continue." "there is no doubt that the RD program had suffered a severe setback." Recommended "regain the initiative through offensive operations" and options up to +206,500 troops plus "relaxation of restrictions on operations in Cambodia, Laos, and North Vietnam." (Ch. 9). Implication for [[mcnamara-robert]], [[vietnam-war]].

- Claim title: "Warnke ISA alternative demographic frontier strategy (Ch. 9)": "the present strategy for prosecuting the war had failed". Proposed: forces "currently in or near the heavily populated areas along the coast should remain in place"; "Based just beyond the populated areas, the forces on the demographic frontiers would conduct spoiling raids, long-range reconnaissance patrols and, when appropriate targets are located, search-and-destroy operations into the enemy’s zone of movement". Eight advantages including lengthened LOCs, more forces for populated areas, reduced U.S. casualties, blunting future Tets. (Ch. 9). For [[counterinsurgency]], [[vietnam-war]].

- Claim title: "Clifford confrontation and no victory plan (Ch. 9)": "can anyone give me an idea when we could bring the war to a conclusion?" Chiefs: "they did not know when but that if the United States continued grinding down the enemy, the attrition would eventually become unbearable". Clifford: "What is our plan for military victory in Vietnam? The fact is we do not have a plan for military victory." "All we had was the advice from the military that if we continued to pour troops in at some unknown rate and possibly in an unlimited number for an unknown period of time that ultimately it was their opinion the enemy would have suffered that degree of attrition that would force the enemy to sue for some kind of peace." Westmoreland back-channel: priority "clear enemy main forces out of the populated regions" then "hard hitting offensive campaigns to invade base areas, interdict... and eliminate or evict VC/NVA". (Ch. 9). Linked to [[mcnamara-robert]], [[vietnam-war]].

- Claim title: "Wise Men and Johnson decisions (Ch. 9)": Wise Men (Acheson, Ball, Taylor, Ridgway, Bundy et al.) "coalesced around the position... that the present strategy... had lost the support of the American public." Recommended "concentrate on improving the RVNAF and phasing out U.S. forces while seeking a negotiated settlement." Results: Westmoreland recalled as CSA; troop ceiling "frozen at 549,500"; "none of the 200,000 additional troops requested were to be deployed"; partial bombing halt; LBJ "I shall not seek, and I will not accept, the nomination of my party for another term as your president." "The Army approach to the war in Vietnam had lost its credibility with the political leadership of the United States." (Ch. 9). For [[vietnam-war]].

- Claim title: "Tet: who really won (Ch. 9)": "from a traditional military viewpoint the Communists lost the Tet Offensive, they achieved victory in the most important strategic dimensions of insurgency warfare." Objectives achieved: "pacification program was dealt, in the words of COMUSMACV, a 'substantial setback'"; "800,000 refugees"; "succeeded in killing 1,001 American servicemen"; "imposing hardship on the civilian population" and terror (Hue atrocities); "worn down the will of the American people more quickly than MACV could wear down the insurgent forces." "Tet was a victory for Hanoi." "The Army had convinced itself that it had won the Tet Offensive; therefore, no soul-searching was necessary." Enormous casualties "proved it: the Army was winning." (Ch. 9 "Tet—Who Really Won?"). Central to [[vietnam-war]], [[army-concept]].

- Claim title: "Vietnamization launch and ARVN limits (Ch. 9 End Game)": "the primary focus of U.S. policy in Vietnam centered not so much on defeating the Communists as on transferring responsibility for the war effort to the GVN and withdrawing American troops—Vietnamization". RVNAF to 801,215 (incl. 335k ARVN, 218k RF, 179k PF) by FY70. "the problem with the RVNAF was not so much a shortage of men as a lack of leaders—the officers and NCOs who could make effective use of these forces." "the ARVN’s highly political (that is, corrupt) promotion practices." Two-phase program (upgrade firepower then self-sufficiency). (Ch. 9). Implication [[vietnam-war]].

- Claim title: "Post-Tet operations and 'one war' rhetoric vs. reality (Ch. 9)": Abrams "drew up a new, 'one war' plan (pacification was no longer to be considered the 'other' war)" with "population security, not the body count, would be the criterion of success." Yet "the 'kill vc' syndrome reached new heights"; Ewell's units "averaged over 1000 enemy per month... exchange ratios... peaking at 158 to 1". SPEEDY EXPRESS (9th Div Delta): 10,883 enemy killed for 267 US KIA (40.8:1) but "ratio of enemy killed to weapons seized of 14.6: 1" (normal 3-4:1); "many of those killed were not really combatants"; "population security in the area remained basically unaffected." Hamburger Hill: 50 US dead for 597 enemy on remote Apbia Mountain. Komer (Abrams deputy): "There was no change in strategy whatsoever. . . . The myth of a change in strategy is a figment of media imagination; it didn’t really change until we began withdrawing." (Ch. 9). For [[abrams-creighton]], [[army-concept]].

- Claim title: "Army failure metrics (Ch. 10)": "The Army failed on both counts [buying time for GVN with minimal expenditure; contributing to defeat of insurgents]. It expended human resources at a relatively high rate and material resources in a profligate manner as part of a strategy of attrition. Yet the Army achieved neither a quick victory nor the maintenance of support on the home front for a continued U.S. presence in Vietnam." "MACV attempted to adapt what had been the low-risk strategy of attrition in a mid-intensity conflict environment to a low-intensity conflict in the hope of achieving similar results. The nature of insurgency warfare, however, made such a strategic approach a high-cost, high-risk option". "the Army compromised its ability to successfully combat lower-phase insurgency operations at anything approaching an acceptable cost." (Ch. 10). For [[vietnam-war]], [[army-concept]].

- Claim title: "Invasion and Laotian incursion alternatives critiqued (Ch. 10)": Invasion of North: "would not have significantly altered the unfavorable circumstances"; risked China war; "A U.S. occupation of North Vietnam would have produced results any different from those produced by the 1946 French reoccupation"; prolonged deployments. Laotian incursion (EL PASO/Summers): "many officers regard [Summers' On Strategy] as the most incisive defense yet written on the role of the Army"; called for barrier force of "eight divisions (five U.S., two ROK, and one ARVN)" to cut Ho Chi Minh Trail, "VC to 'wither on the vine'". Unlikely to work: "enormous support requirements (over 18,000 engineer troops alone)"; NVA "end run around the barrier force by going through Thailand"; ROK participation doubtful; "fixed positions... constantly subjected to harassing fire... and required large numbers of support troops"; "would not have limited or reduced U.S. casualties or defeated the insurgent movement in South Vietnam." (Ch. 10). Linked to [[vietnam-war]].

- Claim title: "Enclave and demographic frontier as superior alternatives (Ch. 10)": Enclaves (Taylor/Ball): US "aid the RVNAF by controlling the densely populated coastal areas"; ARVN combat guerrillas inland; "accepted the stalemate imposed at the lowest cost to the United States in casualties and resources." Demographic frontier (ISA 1968): "expanded enclave approach"; "U.S. forces... situated along the entire narrow band of the populous Coastal Plain"; "priority of missions... first, to secure and pacify the densely populated Coastal Plain and, second, to move forward to the demographic frontier" with "Special Forces units... as strike teams"; "offered the best hope for strengthening the GVN and shortening the time needed before the South Vietnamese could handle the internal threat". "the strategies of attrition, invasion, and incursion were all heavily weighted toward... phase 3... [while] the nature of the war required that emphasis be placed, first and foremost, on the internal threat". "the Army, in fighting the war it was not prepared to fight, lost the opportunity to fight the war it knew how to win." (Ch. 10). For [[counterinsurgency]], [[vietnam-war]].

- Claim title: "Lessons unlearned — Weinberger doctrine (Ch. 10)": Post-war "renaissance of sorts in generating forces and doctrine for special operations" (re-formed SF groups, Ranger regiment, 1st SOCOM, light infantry divisions). But "conditions" (Weinberger/Meyer/Summers): commit only for "vital" interests, "troops committed are sufficient", "clear intention of winning", "popular and congressional support". "if the Army has learned any lessons from Vietnam, it has learned many of the wrong ones." Public support existed "from the time President Kennedy dispatched large numbers... to the Tet Offensive over six years later"; "vital interest" subjective (Korea 1950 example); "the military is more concerned about the intestinal fortitude of the executive and congressional leadership and the will of the American people than it is about the tough questions of its own doctrine and force structure." (Ch. 10 "Does It Matter?" / "Lessons Unlearned"). For [[army-concept]], [[united-states]], [[vietnam-war]].

- Claim title: "Post-Vietnam doctrine and institutions unchanged (Ch. 10)": "FM100-20 was most recently issued in January 1981. The manual is little more than a restatement of FM31-16, Counterguerrilla Operations, issued in 1967." "Search-and-destroy operations experience a rebirth... as 'strike campaigns.'" "winning the hearts and minds of the people finishes a poor second to seeking out main enemy forces and bringing them to battle." "the commander’s dilemma that existed in Vietnam persists". LIDS "have a number of mid-intensity conflict missions that overshadow low-intensity conflict contingencies"; "LID military intelligence assets appear to be oriented on conventional conflict intelligence requirements". "As in the Kennedy years, these modifications are motivated by a shortage of manpower resources and a belief that it really is a relatively easy proposition to shift gears from conventional war to counterinsurgency." Yarborough: "We didn’t cope with irregular warfare in Asia . . . and, rather than recognizing that we didn’t learn our lessons, we are turning back again in the hope that the next war will be a conventional war." (Ch. 10). For [[army-concept]].

- Claim title: "Author's final argument (Conclusions)": "Low-intensity warfare represents the most likely arena of future conflict for the Army, and counterinsurgency the most demanding contingency." "As in the period following the end of the Korean War, the Army is erecting barriers to avoid fighting another Vietnam War... The result has been that instead of gaining a better understanding of how to wage counterinsurgency warfare within the unique social, economic, political, and military dimensions... the Army is trying, through the six tests enunciated by Secretary Weinberger, to transform it into something it can handle." "America’s enemies are not going to play to its military strong suits; rather, they will exploit its weak points." "There exists today no persistent, direct, high-level emphasis for the development of an interdepartmental approach to the problem of foreign internal defense." "in spite of its anguish in Vietnam, the Army has learned little of value. Yet the nation’s policymakers have endorsed the service’s misperceptions derived from the war while contemplating an increased U.S. role in Third World low-intensity conflicts. This represents a very dangerous mixture that in the end may see the Army again attempting to fight a conventional war against a very unconventional enemy." (Conclusions). For [[army-concept]], [[vietnam-war]], [[counterinsurgency]], [[united-states]].
