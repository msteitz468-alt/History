# The Great Contradiction: The Tragic Side of the American Founding (Joseph J. Ellis, 2025)

## Overview

Joseph Ellis's 2025 book examines the "downside" of the American founding: the failure to end slavery and the failure to avoid Indian removal. While acknowledging the achievements of independence, the Declaration, and the Constitution, Ellis argues these were accompanied by two "unquestionably horrific tragedies" that the founders oversaw. The work is deliberately corrective, focusing on moral blindness, the gap between proclaimed ideals ("all men are created equal") and practice (slaveholding among the very authors of those ideals), and the long-term consequences for race relations in the United States.

Ellis treats the slavery contradiction as the more consequential of the two (greater impact on political/ideological values and constitutional compromises). He poses the tragedies as Shakespearean (products of inadequate leadership and moral blindness, hence avoidable) rather than Greek (inevitable). The book connects the founding era directly to modern America: "we are still living a story about the presumption of white supremacy that had its origins and first chapter in the American founding."

Structure moves from overviews and angles of vision, through contexts (including the Constitutional Convention compromises), to intimate "Southern Vistas" at Mount Vernon (Washington) and Monticello (Jefferson).

## Major Developments Covered

- The ideological and political contradictions at the heart of the Declaration and Constitution.
- The specific compromises at the 1787 Convention that embedded slavery (three-fifths, fugitive slaves, slave trade moratorium).
- George Washington's private evolution and 1799 will freeing his Mount Vernon slaves (with education and residency provisions), contrasted with broader planter class.
- Thomas Jefferson's complex stance at Monticello and in public life.
- The linked but secondary tragedy of Indian removal policy.
- The presumption of white supremacy as foundational, with lasting effects.

## Geographic Scope

Chesapeake (Virginia plantations), Philadelphia (Convention), broader thirteen colonies / early United States, with references to western expansion and Indian country.

## Transition / Context

Follows the Revolution and precedes the full implementation of removal policies and the sectional crisis. Situates the founding compromises as the origin point for 19th-century conflicts over slavery and race. Updates coverage in Age of Expansion (primary) and Long 19th Century (consequences and memory).

## Historiography

Ellis (author of Founding Brothers and American Sphinx) returns to the founding generation with an explicit tragic frame. Engages prior work on Washington slavery (Wiencek, Thompson) and Jefferson. Positions against triumphalist narratives; emphasizes contingency and moral choice. As a 2025 synthesis it incorporates recent demographic projections and contemporary resonances (e.g., "Make America Great Again" parallel drawn in text). Primary grounding in founders' papers and contemporary pamphlets.

## Collection Coverage Note

The collection has been weak on post-1750 narrative history for North America outside archaeology and select topics. This source supplies a high-quality, focused interpretive treatment of the moral/political contradictions at the founding, centered on slavery (with Indian removal as secondary). It complements existing actor pages for the founders and the American Revolution event page. Future work should add primary sources on the Convention, ratification, and specific removal campaigns, plus stronger Native-centered accounts.
