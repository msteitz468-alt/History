# Roots of Strategy, Book 4: Mahan, Corbett, Douhet, Mitchell (ed. Jablonsky, 1999)

A **primary-source reader** of the four men who, between 1890 and 1925, first assembled
the scattered ideas of their age into structured *theories* of **sea power** and **air
power**. Edited for the U.S. Army War College by David Jablonsky, it is a **Tier 1
theory source** for the wiki's [[hubs/warfare/strategy|Strategy]] sub-hub — the naval and
air complement to the land-warfare canon (Jomini, Clausewitz) already represented in
[[sources/murray-making-of-strategy-1994|The Making of Strategy]] and Luttwak.

Jablonsky's framing thesis (his introduction, "Strategy, Continuity, and Change"): all
four were **crusaders** writing in periods of revolutionary technological change, and
their lasting value lies "not so much in the originality of their thinking, but in their
being the first to assemble ideas, widely known at the time, into a collected, structured
order." The two naval theorists ([[mahan-alfred-thayer|Mahan]], [[corbett-julian|Corbett]])
used **history** to distill enduring principles and saw war in the Clausewitzian sense as
the continuation of policy; the two air theorists ([[douhet-giulio|Douhet]],
[[mitchell-william|Mitchell]]) declared **history irrelevant**, believed the airplane had
"broken the evolutionary continuity of the character of war," and subsumed political
direction in the imperative for **[[total-war|total war]]**.

## Jablonsky's comparative argument (introduction)

- **War & strategy.** Mahan and Corbett tie naval/maritime strategy upward to **grand
  strategy** and the full spectrum of national power; both grasp that limited political
  objectives can produce **[[limited-war|limited wars]]**. Douhet and Mitchell invert the
  Clausewitzian linkage — for them military means (the bomber) dominate political ends, and
  "if war was total, then limitations imposed by law and morality were irrelevant."
- **Mahan vs. Corbett.** Mahan: command of the sea is a quasi-zero-sum prize won by the
  **concentrated battle fleet** in **decisive action**; commerce-raiding is wasteful. Corbett:
  command of the sea is "nothing but the control of maritime communications," frequently
  *local* and *temporary*, so decisive battle is one option among several and a **fleet in
  being** may suffice. Corbett is the more Clausewitzian and the more flexible; Mahan the
  more influential.
- **Douhet vs. Mitchell.** Both demanded an **independent air force** and relegated armies
  and navies to defensive roles. Douhet: an all-**bomber** fleet, no defense possible ("aerial
  warfare admits of no defense, only offense"), aerochemical attack on cities, preemption.
  Mitchell (American, ocean-buffered): air power as *defense* of the hemisphere, retained
  **pursuit/interception** ("the only effective defense against aerial attack is to whip the
  enemy's air forces in air battles") — though after his 1925 court-martial his views drifted
  toward Douhet's.
- **Impact & legacy.** Mahan's book had "the greatest immediate impact" — it reshaped U.S.
  officer education and armed the navalism of Roosevelt and Lodge, but also accelerated the
  Anglo-German naval race that eroded the British supremacy it celebrated. Corbett's influence
  was narrower and resented (a civilian who never served), and was blamed for the cautious
  Grand Fleet strategy at Jutland. Douhet shaped the Luftwaffe, French and American bombing
  doctrine; Mitchell's impact was largely domestic and posthumous.
