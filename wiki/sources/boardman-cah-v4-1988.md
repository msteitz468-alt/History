# CAH Vol. IV — Persia, Greece and the Western Mediterranean, c. 525 to 479 B.C.

Second edition, edited by **John Boardman, N. G. L. Hammond, D. M. Lewis and M.
Ostwald** (Cambridge University Press, 1988; first edition 1926). The volume
covers the half-century in which the **Achaemenid empire** at its height collided
with the **Greek city-states**, the encounter that the editors frame as bringing
"the concepts of Greek and Barbarian, freedom and despotism into the sharpest
focus." It runs from the accession of Darius and the consolidation of the empire
to the Greek victories of 480–479 (Salamis, Plataea, Mycale) and the simultaneous
Greek victory over Carthage at Himera in Sicily.

It is processed under the **Large-Volume Protocol**: structural map → this source
page → one section cycle at a time (read → list affected pages → write complete
interlinked pages → commit to disk → section log entry → advance) → cross-section
synthesis → Volume Synthesis Note → file source → final log entry → commit & push.

## Volume Synthesis Note

CAH IV is built around a single organizing collision — the **Achaemenid empire at its
height against the Greek city-states** — but its lasting contribution to the wiki is
that it frames that collision **structurally and synchronically**, refusing the
nationalist "freedom versus despotism" melodrama even as it explains why later Greeks
told it that way. Murray's deep cause of the Persian Wars is not Persian wickedness but
**imperial mechanics**: an empire "held together essentially by mobilizing its subjects
for war" had, once it absorbed the Ionian and Phoenician navies, to expand into the
Mediterranean — "and must ultimately lead to disaster" ([[achaemenid-empire]],
[[ionian-revolt-499bce]], [[battle-of-marathon-490bce]]). The Greek victories of
480–479 ([[battle-of-thermopylae-480bce]], [[battle-of-salamis-480bce]],
[[battle-of-plataea-479bce]]) thus fix the **permanent western limit** of a system that
the eastern chapters show reaching an identical limit on the Indus. The volume's two
halves rhyme: the same expansive, tribute-gathering empire meets its bound against
[[scythians|Saka]] nomads and Greek hoplites alike.

A second through-line is the **simultaneous transformation of the Greek polis** that
made this resistance possible. The fall of the [[pisistratidae|Pisistratid tyranny]] and
[[cleisthenes|Cleisthenes']] [[cleisthenic-reforms-508bce|reforms]] produced
[[isonomia]] and [[ostracism]] at Athens; [[sparta]] and the
[[peloponnesian-league|Peloponnesian League]] under [[cleomenes-i|Cleomenes I]] gave the
Greeks a land hegemon; and across the Aegean the [[presocratics|Presocratic]] revolution,
[[polis-religion|civic religion]], [[coinage]] and the maturing
[[trade-network-formation|trade network]] mark a civilization coming into its own. The
volume lets its contributors **disagree about Herodotus** — the warrant for the standing
[[herodotus-reliability]] controversy created during this ingest — and that disagreement
is itself the methodological lesson: the same source underwrites the narrative spine and
is everywhere under suspicion.

The third and least expected contribution is the volume's **western third** (Chs. 12–16),
which the wiki had barely touched. These chapters reconstruct a **pre-Roman Italy and
Sicily** that develop in parallel with, and partly independent of, the Aegean drama: the
in-situ rise of the [[etruscans|Etruscans]] from the Villanovan Iron Age and their
Orientalizing transformation through [[pithecusae|Pithecusa]]; the Apennine
[[italic-peoples|Italic peoples]] and their Sacred-Spring diaspora; the
[[languages-of-italy|linguistic mosaic]] that maps the ethnic one; and the
[[western-greek-tyranny|Sicilian tyrannies]] culminating in [[gelon|Gelon's]] victory at
[[battle-of-himera-480bce|Himera]]. Asheri's chapter supplies the western analogue of
Murray's thesis — Carthage too is a **city-state becoming an empire**, its multiracial
mercenary army both its strength and its peril — and the volume itself insists on the
[[battle-of-himera-480bce|Himera]]–[[battle-of-salamis-480bce|Salamis]] synchronism as a
**panhellenic construct**, not a providential fact.

Two cross-volume tensions are worth tracking. First, on **Rome**: this volume's
[[rome|Tarquin Rome]] (Ridgway — "the Etruscans did not found Rome; the Tarquins turned
it into a city") is an Archaic Etruscan dependency, a very different object from the
comparative imperial capital of [[yoffee-cwh-v3-2015|CWH III]] — complementary, but the
join between the Etruscan city and the Republic is a gap to fill. Second, on
**[[carthage]]**: CAH IV's Magonid, empire-building Carthage of c. 535–480 (Alalia, the
Etruscan alliance, Himera) extends the earlier-city account of CAH III.2/III.3 and now
gives that page three source-volumes; the Greco-Punic wars it opens run on past this
volume's horizon.

The major **caveat** is the scan gap (Chs. 1–2, 3a): the **Achaemenid dynastic narrative
and Babylonia under Persia are not ingested here**, so [[achaemenid-empire]],
[[cyrus-the-great]] and [[babylon]] retain their pre-existing sourcing for the reigns of
Cyrus through Xerxes. A complete CAH IV scan, or Kuhrt's *The Persian Empire*, remains a
priority in `Outstanding Sources.md`.
