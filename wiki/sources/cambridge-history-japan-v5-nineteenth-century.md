# The Cambridge History of Japan, Vol. 5: The Nineteenth Century (Jansen ed., 1989)

The deep, interdisciplinary authority on **nineteenth-century Japan (1800–1900)** — the
transformation of a "feudal, decentralized, and status-bound" society into one "unified,
homogenized, and capitalist," and of a secluded island nation into "an active and aggressive
participant in world politics." Its task here is to **deepen and refine** the existing
Meiji-transition spine ([[meiji-restoration-1868]], [[meiji-period]], [[edo-period]],
[[sakoku]], [[samurai]], [[japanese-imperialism]], [[russo-japanese-war-1904]],
[[tokugawa-intellectual-life]]) with current scholarship, and to add discrete-topic pages.

Jansen's editorial thesis is that the century must be read **as a single arc** — the Meiji
Restoration is unintelligible without the late-Tokugawa *bakumatsu* decline that preceded it
and the constitutional and imperial state that followed. He frames the Restoration as one of
several great Japanese **transitions between civil and warrior rule**, each preceded by a long
preparation, each clothed in **archaicizing, revivalist rhetoric** — here the "return to
antiquity" of imperial restoration.

## Volume Synthesis Note

CHJ Vol. 5 gave the wiki's **nineteenth-century Japan** the depth that Vols 1, 3, and 4 gave
the earlier periods — putting scholarly authority behind the [[meiji-restoration-1868|Meiji
transition]] that [[mason-caiger-history-of-japan|Mason & Caiger]] had sketched, creating six
discrete-topic pages ([[opening-of-japan-1853-1858]], [[meiji-constitution-1889]],
[[ito-hirobumi]], [[meiji-modernization]], [[fukuzawa-yukichi]],
[[first-sino-japanese-war-1894-1895]]) and deepening nine existing ones. Its organizing thesis
is **Jansen's insistence on treating the century as a whole**: the Restoration is
unintelligible without the late-Tokugawa *bakumatsu* decline before it and the constitutional,
industrial, imperial state after it — and is best understood as one of Japan's great
**transitions between civil and warrior rule**, each preceded by long preparation and clothed
in archaicizing, revivalist rhetoric.

The arc runs: (1) the **late-Tokugawa crisis** — the Tempō famine and failed reforms, and the
[[opening-of-japan-1853-1858|forced opening]] by Perry and the unequal treaties — destroying
[[shogunate-bakufu|bakufu]] legitimacy while [[tokugawa-intellectual-life|Mito and nativist
thought]] relocated authority in the emperor (*sonnō jōi*, *kokutai*); (2) the **Restoration**
and the building of a centralized state — *haihan chiken*, conscription, and
[[ito-hirobumi|Itō's]] Prussian-model **[[meiji-constitution-1889|constitution of 1889]]**,
with the [[samurai|disestablished samurai]] both losing their estate (the Satsuma Rebellion)
and supplying the new leadership and the *jiyū minken* opposition; (3) **modernization** —
[[meiji-modernization|*bunmei kaika*]], the Iwakura mission, the *oyatoi* foreign employees
and their replacement, [[fukuzawa-yukichi|Fukuzawa's]] enlightenment, and the
[[industrialization|first non-Western industrial revolution]] built on
[[tokugawa-economy|Tokugawa foundations]]; and (4) the **drive to great-power status** —
Pyle's conservative reaction consolidating an emperor-centered orthodoxy, and Iriye's account
of the [[first-sino-japanese-war-1894-1895|Sino-Japanese War]] launching
[[japanese-imperialism|Japanese imperialism]] as the behavior of a modern state.

Two interpretive threads recur. **Continuity over rupture**: the Restoration was a legitimacy
transfer led from within the ruling class, Meiji growth an acceleration of the commercialized
Tokugawa economy, and the modern bureaucracy a descendant of the Tempō-era administrator. And
**the ambiguity of the Meiji achievement**: the same constitutional settlement that won
"enlightenment" and treaty revision left the executive and military insulated from
parliamentary control, and the same nationalism that powered modernization powered empire —
the seeds, as Iriye and Pyle note, of the twentieth-century crisis treated in Vol. 6.

Where it touches other volumes it reinforces and refines: it gives the Mason Meiji spine its
deep institutional and intellectual underpinning, supplies the constitution/modernization/
Sino-Japanese-War pages the spine assumed, connects to the China and Korea pages
([[qing-dynasty]]; [[korea]]), and hands the twentieth-century story — industrial society,
militarism, and the road to the [[pacific-war-1941-1945|Pacific War]] — to **Vol. 6**, the
last of the Japan backbone.
