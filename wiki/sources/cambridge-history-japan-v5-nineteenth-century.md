# The Cambridge History of Japan, Vol. 5: The Nineteenth Century (Jansen ed., 1989)

The deep, interdisciplinary authority on **nineteenth-century Japan (1800–1900)** — the
transformation of a "feudal, decentralized, and status-bound" society into one "unified,
homogenized, and capitalist," and of a secluded island nation into "an active and aggressive
participant in world politics." Its task here is to **deepen and refine** the existing
Meiji-transition spine ([[meiji-restoration-1868]], [[meiji-period]], [[edo-period]],
[[sakoku]], [[samurai]], [[japanese-imperialism]], [[russo-japanese-war-1904]],
[[tokugawa-intellectual-life]]) with current scholarship, and to add discrete-topic pages.

Jansen's editorial thesis is that the century must be read **as a single arc** — the Meiji
Restoration is unintelligible without the late-Tokugawa *bakumatsu* decline that preceded it
and the constitutional and imperial state that followed. He frames the Restoration as one of
several great Japanese **transitions between civil and warrior rule**, each preceded by a long
preparation, each clothed in **archaicizing, revivalist rhetoric** — here the "return to
antiquity" of imperial restoration.
