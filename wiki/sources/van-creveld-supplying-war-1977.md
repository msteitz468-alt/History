# Supplying War: Logistics from Wallenstein to Patton (Martin van Creveld, 1977)

A foundational study demonstrating that logistics — the practical art of moving armies and keeping them supplied — has been the most persistent and often decisive constraint on strategy throughout modern history. Van Creveld argues that before commanders can think about maneuver, battle, or grand designs, they must secure the prosaic requirements of food, fodder, ammunition, and transport. The book uses concrete historical cases to show how supply realities shaped (and frequently ruined) the plans of even the greatest captains.

It directly challenges the notion that strategy is primarily a matter of intellectual brilliance or tactical virtuosity, showing instead that armies are tethered by "umbilical cords" of supply whose length, capacity, and vulnerability determine what is possible.

## Key Framework
- **Logistics as the art of the possible**: Strategy is limited first by the need to feed soldiers (~3,000 calories/day) and animals, and to move the means to do so.
- **Magazine system** (17th-18th c.): Centralized depots, slow, tied to roads/rivers, suited to siege/linear warfare.
- **"Living off the country" / predatory system** (Napoleonic): Faster but brutal, limited range, caused political backlash and guerrilla resistance.
- **Railway revolution**: Enormous capacity but rigid (gauge, terminals, vulnerability to sabotage), led to "umbilical cord" dependence and WWI stalemate.
- **Motorization and mechanization** (WWII): Increased theoretical speed/range but introduced new frictions (fuel, spare parts, road wear, "tyranny of the truck"). Did not eliminate logistics constraints — often made them more complex.
- Recurring themes: tyranny of plunder vs. magazine discipline; the "critical distance" armies can operate from base; service-to-combat troop ratios; the trade-off between mobility and sustainability; why "friction" increases with technological complexity.

## Historiography Note

Van Creveld's work is widely praised for bringing logistics — long the "Cinderella" of military history — into the mainstream of operational analysis. It is essential reading for any serious student of strategy because it shows that many famous "failures of generalship" were in fact failures of supply planning or unavoidable constraints. Critics sometimes note that it underplays the role of human error or intelligence in some cases, or that its conclusions on post-1945 mobility are pessimistic. Nevertheless, it remains the standard starting point for understanding why armies could (or could not) do what their commanders wanted.

**Claims extracted exclusively from this range (verbatim grounding + locators):**

- **Optimum service-to-combat ratio is campaign-specific and context-dependent, not a fixed theoretical ideal:** "If, for any given campaign, this aim can only be achieved by having a hundred men pump fuel, drive trucks and construct railways behind each combatant, then 100:1 is the optimum ratio." (Ch. 8 conclusion)

- **Theoretical calculation of ideal ratios is practically impossible due to complexity and changing requirements:** "To work out this ratio with its thousands of component parts in theory, however, is an almost impossible task, involving as it does a model of tremendous complexity, worthy of a battery of computers presided over by a mathematical prodigy. Moreover, when all the calculations have been done, and the preparations made on the basis of them completed, there is always a strong possibility that a fresh strategic or political requirement will render them worthless." (Ch. 8 conclusion)

- **Twentieth-century (and earlier) armies prepared campaigns on an ad hoc basis rather than comprehensive logistic planning:** "In practice, there is scant evidence that the task has been attempted by the majority of twentieth-century (not to mention earlier) operational planners. Rather, most armies seem to have prepared their campaigns as best they could on an ad hoc basis, making great, if uncoordinated, efforts to gather together the largest possible number of tactical vehicles, trucks of all descriptions, railway troops, etc., while giving little, if any, thought to the ‘ideal’ combination which, in theory, would have carried them the furthest." (Ch. 8 conclusion)

- **Overlord logistic plans were an exercise in conservatism; actual success owed as much to disregarding them as to implementation:** "Nor, as we saw, were the results of the only comprehensive attempt that was made in this direction particularly encouraging. In spite, or perhaps because, of the fact that the plans for ‘Overlord’ made detailed provisions for the last prepacked unit of fuel, they quickly turned out to be an exercise in conservatism, even pusillanimity, such as has not often been equalled. Not only did the actual development of the campaign have little in common with the plans, but the logistic instrument itself functioned very differently from what had been expected. Consequently, it would hardly be an exaggeration to say that the victories the Allies won in 1944 were due as much to their disregard for the preconceived logistic plans as to their implementation." (Ch. 8 conclusion) See also [[operation-overlord-1944]].

- **Willingness (or lack) to improvise, override plans, and take risks was decisive:** "In the final account, it was the willingness - or lack of it - to override the plans, to improvise and take risks, that determined the outcome." (Ch. 8 conclusion)

- **Study of logistics on mobile operations ultimately points to the limits of pure intellect and the primacy of the moral over the purely physical (Napoleon):** "It is perhaps fitting that the present study - starting, as it did, with the determination to avoid ‘vague speculations’ and concentrate on ‘concrete figures and calculations’ - should end with an admission that the human intellect alone is not, after all, the best instrument for waging war and, therefore, understanding it. The intellect must play a central role both in planning and execution, if only because no better instrument is available to us; but to believe that war, or indeed any other aspect of human behaviour, can ever be grasped by means of the intellect alone is to give proof of a hubris like that evinced by those who built the Tower of Babel and deserving similar punishment. To recognize the truth of Napoleon’s dictum that, in war, the moral is to the physical as three to one; in the final account, this may be all that a study of the influence of logistics on mobile operations may be able to teach us." (Ch. 8 conclusion) Contributes to understanding why logistics often trumps command genius across eras. Links to [[actors/napoleon-bonaparte|Napoleon Bonaparte]], [[hubs/warfare/warfare-hub]], [[hubs/warfare/strategy]].

- **Era comparison on mobility (from Ch. 8 notes):** Strategic march speeds had not risen significantly despite mechanization per Huston and Muraise (Ch. 8 n.4); Liddell Hart dissented, arguing WWII mechanized advances were not "remarkably similar" to earlier days. Wheldon on machine-age armies also cited. Reinforces recurring limits of mobility and "tyranny of logistics" across periods (early-modern through world-wars-era).

These conclusions synthesize the book's case studies: logistics constraints persist and often dominate even as technology changes; ad hoc adaptation and moral factors frequently outweigh detailed planning or "genius."
