# CAH Vol. VIII — Rome and the Mediterranean to 133 B.C. (Astin, Walbank, Frederiksen & Ogilvie eds., 2nd ed. 1989)

The western and culminating counterpart to [[walbank-cah-v7-1-1984|CAH VII.1]]
(the Hellenistic East to Raphia, 217) and [[walbank-cah-v7-2-1989|CAH VII.2]]
(the rise of Rome to 220). Where VII.1 closed the East at Raphia and VII.2 closed
Rome's Italian consolidation at 220, **Vol. VIII narrates the half-century in which
the two narratives fuse**: the Second Punic War, the Roman conquest of the Greek
East, and the establishment of Mediterranean hegemony down to the eve of the
Gracchi (133 BCE). This is the volume in which Polybius' *symploke* — the
interweaving of West and East into a single field of affairs — is fully realized.

The editors stress that the period is **short but transformational**: a single
lifetime spans Hannibal's Alps and the sack of Corinth. Rome's acquisition of
empire is presented as inseparable from the internal transformation of Rome
itself — its politics, its society, its relations with the Italian allies, and
its cultural life.

## Volume Synthesis Note

**CAH VIII is the volume in which Polybius' *symploke* is realized** — the
interweaving of the Western and Eastern Mediterranean into a single field under Rome.
[[walbank-cah-v7-1-1984|VII.1]] closed the Hellenistic East at Raphia (217) and
[[walbank-cah-v7-2-1989|VII.2]] closed Rome's Italian consolidation at 220; this volume
fuses the two threads across the half-century from the
[[second-punic-war-218-201bce|Hannibalic War]] to the destruction of Carthage and
Corinth in **146**. Its deepest structural claim, recurring chapter to chapter, is that
**Rome's acquisition of empire and the internal transformation of Rome were a single
process** — the conquest abroad reshaping politics, society, agriculture, and culture at
home.

The volume's **central interpretive axis is the nature of Roman imperialism** (see
[[nature-of-roman-imperialism]]). W. V. Harris (Ch. 5) makes the strongest case for
**systemic Roman bellicosity** — war as the normal, profitable, glory-bringing
expectation of the aristocracy — applied to Spain, Cisalpine Gaul, and the cold-blooded
[[third-punic-war-149-146bce|Third Punic War]]. Errington (Chs 4, 8) and Derow (Ch. 9)
trace the same logic in the East: the [[freedom-of-the-greeks|"freedom of the Greeks"]]
as an instrument of control that hardened, through the Callicrates turning-point (180)
and [[third-macedonian-war-171-168bce|Pydna]] (168), into the bare demand for obedience
— *imperium* in the strict Roman sense — culminating in the
[[achaean-war-146bce|destruction of Corinth]]. Briscoe (Ch. 3) offers the countervailing
"defensive" reading. The [[day-of-eleusis-168bce|Day of Eleusis]] (168) is the emblem of
the whole: Rome dictating to a Hellenistic king without an army.

The volume also insists on treating **the Hellenistic kingdoms in their own terms**, not
merely as adjuncts to Rome (Habicht, Chs 10; Narain, Ch. 11): the Attalid apogee, the
revised, less villainous [[antiochus-iv-epiphanes|Antiochus IV]] and the
[[maccabean-revolt-167-160bce|Maccabean revolt]] sprung from Jewish initiative, the
[[seleucid-empire|Seleucid]] disintegration before [[parthian-empire|Parthia]], and the
[[greco-bactrian-kingdom|Indo-Greeks]] recovered almost entirely from their coinage.
The internal chapters (Astin Ch. 6, Gabba Ch. 7, Rawson Ch. 12, Morel Ch. 13) supply the
home dimension: the competitive [[roman-aristocratic-politics|oligarchy]] and its
self-regulating *mores*; the [[agrarian-crisis-roman-republic|agrarian crisis]] and the
[[roman-italian-alliance|allies' question]] building toward the Gracchi; and the
ambivalent [[hellenization-of-rome|Hellenization]] of Roman culture — the cultural
*symploke* answering the political one.

**Cross-volume tensions.** The volume gives **presumptive weight to Cambridge
reference treatment** but is itself internally divided (Harris vs. Briscoe on
imperialism), a division the wiki preserves on [[nature-of-roman-imperialism]] rather
than adjudicating. It revises earlier sources held in the wiki: Habicht's
[[antiochus-iv-epiphanes|Antiochus IV]] corrects the "forced-Hellenization / madman"
tradition; the Ptolemaic and Seleucid pages built from [[walbank-cah-v7-1-1984|VII.1]]
(Turner, Musti) are carried forward to their Roman-shadowed decline. The volume **closes
exactly where [[walbank-cah-v7-2-1989|VII.2]] and the Hellenistic narrative converge**,
and hands directly to **CAH IX** (the Gracchi and the last age of the Republic), for
which it has laid the structural groundwork — the agrarian crisis, the allies, the
eroded *cursus* conventions, and the personalised commands of
[[scipio-aemilianus|Scipio Aemilianus]].
