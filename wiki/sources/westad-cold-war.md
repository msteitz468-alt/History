# The Cold War: A World History (Odd Arne Westad)

Odd Arne Westad's *The Cold War: A World History* (2017) reframes the US–Soviet conflict as a **global**
contest over rival visions of **modernity** — not merely a European or superpower-bilateral affair, but a
worldwide struggle whose decisive battlegrounds lay in the **decolonizing Third World**. Sixteenth source
of **Gap 1 (Modern)**, and (with [[hobsbawm-age-of-extremes|Hobsbawm]] and [[judt-postwar|Judt]]) the
wiki's anchor for the [[cold-war|Cold War period]].

## The Argument

Westad's central claims:

- **Deep roots.** The Cold War's ideological poles — **capitalism/liberalism vs. socialism/collectivism** —
  were not born in 1945 but in the **late-nineteenth-century** transformations of industrial modernity,
  the labour question, and [[imperialism|imperial]] expansion. The United States and Russia were already
  rising continental powers with universalist creeds before 1917.
- **A global conflict.** The defining violence of the Cold War occurred not in a stable, "long-peace"
  Europe but across the **Global South** — China, Korea, Vietnam, Indonesia, the Congo, Angola, southern
  Africa, Iran, Afghanistan, Central America — where superpower rivalry intersected with
  **[[decolonization|decolonization]]** and nation-building, often with catastrophic local cost.
- **Modernity and ideology.** Both blocs offered the newly independent world a **model of development**;
  the Cold War was as much a competition of developmental promises as of armies.
- **Contingent end.** The Soviet collapse (1989–91) was neither inevitable nor simply an American
  "victory"; Westad stresses Gorbachev's choices and the system's internal exhaustion.

## Significance

The book supplies the wiki's **global** Cold War narrative, correcting the Eurocentrism of the classic
diplomatic accounts and tightly linking the Cold War to [[decolonization|decolonization]] and to the
[[imperialism|imperial]] history that preceded it. It complements [[judt-postwar|Judt's]] European focus
and Hobsbawm's structural "Golden Age," and it bears directly on the [[1991-end-of-cold-war|1991
transition]].
