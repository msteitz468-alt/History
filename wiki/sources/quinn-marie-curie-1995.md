# Marie Curie: A Life (Quinn, 1995)

**Source page for Susan Quinn, *Marie Curie: A Life* (first published 1995; Plunkett Lake Press 2011 reprint used here).**

A major modern biography that demythologizes Marie Skłodowska Curie (1867–1934) while celebrating her achievements. Quinn peels back layers of idealization from Ève Curie's 1937 portrait to reveal a woman who faced profound barriers as a Pole in partitioned Poland, as a woman in male-dominated French science, and as a public figure subjected to xenophobia, misogyny, and scandal. The book emphasizes Curie's intellectual clarity—that radioactivity is an "atomic property"—over mere heroic toil, and uses newly available sources (the post-Pierre journal, closed testimonials on the Langevin affair) to portray her full emotional range: profound grief, passionate attachments, vulnerability, and resilience.

Quinn balances the personal (childhood in Warsaw, governess years, marriage to Pierre, motherhood, widowhood, the 1911 Langevin scandal) with the scientific (isolation of polonium and radium, two Nobels, mobile X-ray units in WWI, Radium Institute). The result is a portrait of Curie as both singular genius and woman navigating the same prejudices as others, whose success depended on clarity of insight as much as dedication.

## Methodological Notes

- Extensive use of Marie Curie's own writings, lab records, and the 1906 journal (addressed to Pierre after his death).
- Access to closed files at the École de physique et chimie containing friends' accounts of the Langevin affair.
- Polish sources and family context (Skłodowski family, partitioned Poland).
- French institutional records (Academy of Sciences rejections, Sorbonne, Nobel processes).
- Contemporary press coverage of fame and scandal.
- Critical engagement with prior biographies (Ève Curie) to correct idealization.

## Key Contributions

- The most nuanced account of Curie's formation in Warsaw under Russian partition, her self-education, and move to Paris.
- Detailed reconstruction of the 1898 discovery period: pitchblende work in the shed, identification of polonium and radium as new elements, the insight into atomic radioactivity.
- Full treatment of the personal and institutional costs of fame: Pierre's death (1906), second Nobel (1911), Academy rejection, and the Langevin scandal that nearly destroyed her.
- Rehabilitation of Curie's WWI work (petites Curie mobile X-ray units) as both humanitarian and scientific.
- Honest portrayal of the Langevin affair as evidence of emotional capacity rather than mere "perfidy."
- Emphasis on Curie's scientific ideas (radioactivity as atomic property leading to modern atomic structure understanding) alongside the physical labor myth.
