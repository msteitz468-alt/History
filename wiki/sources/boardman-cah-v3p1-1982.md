# The Cambridge Ancient History, Vol. III, Part 1 (1982)

**The Prehistory of the Balkans; and the Middle East and the Aegean World, tenth
to eighth centuries B.C.** Edited by John Boardman, I. E. S. Edwards,
N. G. L. Hammond and E. Sollberger. Cambridge University Press, 2nd edition, 1982.

This volume is the chronological continuation of [[edwards-cah-v2p2-1975|Vol. II,
Part 2]] (which carried the eastern Mediterranean through the
[[bronze-age-collapse|Bronze Age Collapse]] to c. 1000 BCE). III.1 covers the
**recovery centuries** — roughly the tenth through eighth centuries BCE, the heart
of the [[early-iron-age|Early Iron Age]] and the threshold of the
[[archaic-period|Archaic Period]]. It is the wiki's primary CAH authority for: the
rise of the [[neo-assyrian-empire|Neo-Assyrian]] and [[babylonia|Babylonian]]
states out of the post-collapse trough; the appearance of [[urartu|Urartu]]; the
[[neo-hittite-states|Neo-Hittite/Syro-Hittite]] successor mosaic; the divided
Hebrew kingdoms of [[kingdom-of-israel|Israel]] and [[kingdom-of-judah|Judah]];
Libyan-dynasty Egypt; Cypro-Geometric Cyprus; and — at the volume's analytical
core — the regeneration of Aegean Greece from the [[greek-dark-age|Dark Age]]
toward the city-state, the [[geometric-culture-greece|Geometric culture]], and the
adoption of the [[alphabet|alphabet]]. It is paired with two companion volumes
read separately: Vol. III Part 2 (Assyrian & Babylonian empires 8th–6th c.;
EPUB on disk, ingested next) and Vol. III Part 3 (the expansion of the Greek
world; markdown on disk).

The volume's distinctive contribution is its **first-ever CAH survey of the
Balkans north of Greece** (Romania, the central Balkans, Albania) — added because,
as the Preface explains, postwar excavation there had finally made a synthesis
possible, and because Balkan developments bore on Aegean and Anatolian cultures
at the close of the Bronze Age. Per CLAUDE.md, the wiki treats archaeological
chapters as primary evidence and flags the uncalibrated-radiocarbon problem.

## Structure of the Volume

The 20 chapters (several subdivided as 18a–b, 20a–e) fall into three editorial
parts:

- **Part I — The Prehistory of the Balkans to 1000 B.C. (Chs. 1–5).** Region-by-
  region archaeological surveys from the Palaeolithic to the Bronze/Iron
  transition: Romania (Dumitrescu), the central Balkan Stone Age and Eneolithic
  and Bronze Age (Garašanin), and Albania (Prendi). Deep-time chapters that reach
  back far before the volume's nominal tenth–eighth-century frame.
- **Part II — The Middle East (Chs. 6–13).** The political narrative of the Near
  East in the early first millennium: Neo-Assyria 934–745 (Grayson), Babylonia
  c. 1000–748 (Brinkman), Urartu (Barnett), the Neo-Hittite states (Hawkins),
  Israel and Judah in two chapters (Mitchell), Cyprus (Karageorghis), and Libyan
  Egypt — the 22nd–24th Dynasties (Edwards).
- **Part III — The Balkans and the Aegean (Chs. 14–20).** The Early Iron Age in
  the central Balkans (Garašanin) and NW Greece (Hammond); the Greek Dark Age
  region by region — Central Greece & Thessaly (Snodgrass), the Peloponnese
  (Hammond), East Greece and the islands incl. Crete (Cook, Boardman); the
  Geometric culture (Boardman); and a composite final chapter on the earliest
  alphabetic writing, the Greek alphabet, and the languages of the Balkans
  (Isserlin, Jeffery, Crossland, Hainsworth, Polomé).

There is no separate conclusion; the Preface supplies the editorial synthesis.

### Section detail

**Cycle 1 — Balkan prehistory (Chs. 1–5, pp. 1–237).** Five archaeological survey
chapters reaching from the Palaeolithic to ~1000 BCE in Romania, the central
Balkans (Serbia/Bulgaria/Yugoslavia), and Albania. These are *deep-time* chapters
despite sitting in a tenth–eighth-century volume. Expected: new place/process
pages for the Balkan cultural sequences, an update to the steppe/Indo-European
migration material, and explicit handling of the **uncalibrated-radiocarbon
caveat** and the Cold-War-era culture-history framework. Likely cross-links to
[[neolithic]], [[chalcolithic]], [[corded-ware]]/steppe pages, and
[[indo-european-origins]] if present.

**Cycle 2 — Mesopotamian empires (Chs. 6–8, pp. 238–371).** The political spine of
the Near Eastern Iron Age. Grayson's Assyria chapter builds/expands the
**[[neo-assyrian-empire]]** actor page from Ashur-dan II's recovery through
Ashurnasirpal II and Shalmaneser III to the weak "interval" of 782–745 that
precedes Tiglath-pileser III (next volume). Brinkman's Babylonia and Barnett's
**[[urartu]]** chapters create/expand those actor pages. Flag the Assyrian
provincial-and-army apparatus as a process.

**Cycle 3 — Levant & Anatolia (Chs. 9–11, pp. 372–510).** Hawkins on the
**[[neo-hittite-states]]** (the Luwian/Aramaean successor mosaic in N. Syria and
SE Anatolia) and Mitchell's two chapters on **Israel and Judah** from the
division of Solomon's kingdom through Jehu to Jeroboam II and Uzziah. Create actor
pages for the two kingdoms and key event pages (Shoshenq I's campaign; the Omride
dynasty; Jehu's coup). Major historiography section on **text-vs-archaeology** for
the Hebrew kingdoms (the same minimalist/maximalist tension flagged in II.2).

**Cycle 4 — Cyprus & Egypt (Chs. 12–13, pp. 511–581).** Karageorghis on
Cypro-Geometric Cyprus and the **Phoenician colonization of Kition** (bridge to
the wider [[phoenician-expansion|Phoenician diaspora]]); Edwards on **Libyan
Egypt**, the 22nd–24th Dynasties, Shoshenq I (biblical Shishak — cross-link to
Cycle 3), the fragmentation of the monarchy, and the onset of the **Nubian/Kushite**
advance under Kashta and Piye (sets up the 25th Dynasty in later volumes).

**Cycle 5 — Balkans & NW Greece EIA (Chs. 14–15, pp. 582–656).** Garašanin's
central-Balkan Early Iron Age (regional groups, metal hoards) and Hammond's
**Illyris, Epirus and Macedonia** — the latter important for Macedonian
ethnogenesis and the NW-Greek/Dorian migration question (a Hammond signature
argument; flag as interpretive). Place pages for Macedonia/Epirus/Illyria as
historical regions.

**Cycle 6 — Greek Dark Age regions (Chs. 16–18, pp. 657–778).** The regional
archaeology of the Aegean recovery: Snodgrass on Central Greece & Thessaly,
Hammond on the **Peloponnese** (and the emergence of the city-state from the Dark
Age), Cook & Boardman on East Greece, **Euboea (Lefkandi), the Cyclades, and
Crete**. Updates the existing [[greek-dark-age]] period page heavily; place pages
for Lefkandi/Euboea, Argolis, Lefkandi as the key reviving site. Depopulation vs.
continuity debate.

**Cycle 7 — Geometric culture & the alphabet (Chs. 19–20a–e, pp. 779–900).**
Boardman on the **[[geometric-culture-greece|Geometric culture]]** (pottery
sequence, the figured style, society and trade) and the composite ch. 20 on the
**[[alphabet]]**: Isserlin on the Canaanite→Phoenician alphabetic line, Jeffery on
the Greek adoption (vowels), Crossland/Hainsworth/Polomé on Greek dialects and the
Illyrian/Thracian/Daco-Moesian languages. Concept pages for the alphabet as a
technology and for the Greek dialect map; cross-link to [[phoenician-expansion]]
and [[literacy]].

## Volume Synthesis Note

*(written after all seven section cycles, in the cross-section synthesis pass)*

**The volume's argument.** CAH III.1 covers the "**Prehistory of the Balkans, the
Middle East and the Aegean World, Tenth to Eighth Centuries B.C.**" — the centuries of
the Early Iron Age that follow the [[bronze-age-collapse-1200bce|Bronze Age Collapse]]
and precede the Archaic florescence. Its unifying theme, visible only at the
whole-volume level, is **recovery and reconfiguration out of the collapse**: across
three great regions the volume traces how new political and cultural forms crystallized
on the ruins of the Bronze Age world. In the **Near East**, the
[[neo-assyrian-empire|Neo-Assyrian Empire]] revived from its ninth-century nadir to
become the first true territorial empire, while around it the successor mosaic took
shape — [[neo-hittite-states|Syro-Hittite]] and [[arameans|Aramaean]] states,
[[urartu|Urartu]], a recovering [[babylonia-iron-age|Babylonia]], the divided Hebrew
[[kingdom-of-israel|kingdoms]], [[phoenicians|Phoenician]] sea-power, and a fragmented
[[libyan-dynasties-egypt|Libyan Egypt]]. In the **Balkans**, a continuous culture-
historical development ran from the Eneolithic metal-using cultures through the
[[illyrians|Illyrian]], [[phrygians|Phrygian/Brygi]], and [[epirus|Epirote]] peoples of
the south-west. In the **Aegean**, the [[greek-dark-age|Greek Dark Age]] gave way to
the Geometric recovery, the birth of the [[city-state|polis]], the
[[euboea|Euboean]]-led orientalizing trade, and the adoption of the
[[alphabet|alphabet]] — the threshold of historical Greece.

**What it adds to the wiki.** The volume created 20 new pages and updated 17, filling
the **Early Iron Age** spine that earlier volumes (CAH II.1–II.2) had carried only to
the brink of 1000 BCE. Its most important single additions are the **Iron Age Near
East** (the foundation narrative of the Neo-Assyrian Empire; Urartu; the Syro-Hittite
states; the two Hebrew kingdoms and their datable synchronisms from
[[battle-of-qarqar-853bce|Qarqar]] and the [[jehu-revolt-841bce|Jehu revolt]]) and the
**genesis of Greek civilization** (the [[euboea|Euboean]] orientalizing lead, the
emergence of the polis, and the long-missing [[alphabet|alphabet]] page now created and
resolving a dozen dangling links). It connects directly to the existing Aegean Dark Age
material from CAH II.2, which it was explicitly written to continue.

**Cross-volume tensions.** Two methodological caveats recur and are flagged on the
affected pages. (1) The **Balkan chapters** (Garašanin, Hammond) work in a Cold War-era
**culture-historical / "pots-and-peoples"** idiom, equating pottery and burial styles
with named ethnic groups and migrations — an approach later prehistory (and ancient
DNA) treats with caution; see [[indo-european-origins]] and [[illyrians]]. (2) The
volume's **radiocarbon dates in Part 1 are uncalibrated** (old half-life) and run
centuries too young against modern calibrated chronologies — any absolute prehistoric
date taken from it must be flagged. Against other sources, the volume gives the Hebrew
kingdoms a far firmer external chronology than the biblically-driven CAH II.2 account
of the united monarchy, and its **Euboean-first** thesis for the orientalizing trade
and the alphabet sharpens (and partly corrects) the older Phoenician-centred narrative
in [[edwards-cah-v2p2-1975|CAH II.2]]'s treatment of [[phoenicians|Phoenician]]
colonization.
