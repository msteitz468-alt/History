# The Race for Paradise: An Islamic History of the Crusades

**Paul M. Cobb**, Oxford University Press, 2014. A continuous narrative of Frankish–Muslim conflict from the eleventh-century Mediterranean (Sicily, al-Andalus) through the Levantine crusades, the Mamluk expulsion of the Franks, and the Ottoman late-medieval confrontation, written from the standpoint of **Islamic history** — the past of people who lived under Muslim rule — and almost entirely from Islamic sources.

## Core Thesis

Cobb rejects both the older **triumphal** Latin narrative and the modern **lachrymose** reverse narrative (Muslims as passive victims of an unprovoked European assault). His central claims:

1. **Periodization from the Islamic side.** Medieval Muslims had no word for “Crusades” (*al-hurub al-salibiyya* is a modern neologism). They experienced Frankish aggression as one phase of a longer Mediterranean collision beginning in **eleventh-century Sicily and al-Andalus**, not at Clermont in 1095, and not ending cleanly at Acre in 1291.
2. **Geography.** The story is Mediterranean-wide: Maghrib, al-Andalus, Sicily, Syria-Palestine, Egypt, Anatolia, Iraq, the Balkans — not a Levant-only episode interrupting an otherwise self-contained Islamic history.
3. **Agency.** Muslim responses were diverse, contingent, and often inter-Muslim first: Fatimid–Saljuq rivalry, Syrian disunity, Zangid and Ayyubid state-building, and Mamluk imperialism shaped outcomes as much as Frankish zeal.
4. **Sources.** Arabic chronicles, travelogues, jihad literature, and biographies supply a self-standing narrative, not mere “colour” for a Latin frame.

## Methodological Notes

- **Emic categories:** *Ifranj* / Franks; *dar al-islam* / *dar al-harb*; *jihad*; *ghazi*; not “crusade” as a medieval Arabic category.
- **Hodgson influence:** Cobb cites Hodgson’s *Venture of Islam* (oikumene, “Islamdom”) as framing influence.
- **Relation to other wiki sources:** Complements [[laiou-mottahedeh-crusades-byzantium-muslim-2001]] (Byzantine + jihad-theory essays) and Latin-military works ([[france-western-warfare-in-the-age-of-the-crusades-1999]], [[france-victory-in-the-east-1994]]); supplies the continuous Islamic *narrative* those volumes do not. Contrasts with Maalouf (*Crusades through Arab Eyes*) as popular rather than scholarly; sits beside Hillenbrand (*Crusades: Islamic Perspectives*) as narrative where Hillenbrand is thematic.

## Volume Synthesis Note

Cobb’s book is the wiki’s first continuous **Islamic-side narrative** of the Frankish–Muslim Mediterranean conflict. Where [[laiou-mottahedeh-crusades-byzantium-muslim-2001]] multiplies perspectives thematically (jihad theory, Byzantine holy war, art, commerce) and Latin military monographs reconstruct Western operational art, Cobb supplies a single chronological story built from Arabic chronicles and geographies: from Norman Sicily and the taifa collapse through Zangid and Ayyubid state-building to Mamluk expulsion and the Ottoman–Nasrid endgame.

Four arguments structure the volume and now anchor wiki updates:

1. **Reperiodization.** *Al-hurub al-salibiyya* is a modern neologism; medieval Muslims framed a longer *Ifranj* problem beginning in eleventh-century Spain and Sicily and not “satisfactorily” closed until Ottoman Balkan power and the fall of Granada (1492). The Clermont-to-Acre frame is a European story, not an Islamic one.
2. **No coherent “countercrusade.”** Zangi–Nur al-Din–Saladin form an optical succession; jihad language was intermittent and political. The deeper religious-history driver of the age is the **Sunni Revival**, not Frankish holy war. What the Frankish presence *did* aid was the **legitimacy and longevity of military elites** (Zangids, Ayyubids, Almoravids, Mamluks).
3. **Permanent vs ephemeral losses.** Levantine Frankish states came and went; **Sicily and al-Andalus** did not return to Muslim rule. Jerusalem-centric scorekeeping that says “Muslims won” understates Mediterranean costs.
4. **Memory, not rediscovery.** Ambient Ottoman and Maghribi memory of Franks (epics, petitions, panegyric, chronicle copying) undermines the claim that Muslims forgot the crusades until nineteenth-century nationalism.

Limits for the wiki: Cobb is not a battle-analysis source (Warfare Hub depth stays with France, Verbruggen, et al.); numbers should be treated as chronicle-scale; and the book’s nonspecialist English bibliographic sketch is a starting map, not a full apparatus. Used with NCMH IV, Hillenbrand, and the Dumbarton Oaks volume, it rebalances the wiki’s crusades cluster from Latin-process definition toward Islamic experience.
