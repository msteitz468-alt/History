# The Evolution of Strategy: Thinking War from Antiquity to the Present (Beatrice Heuser, 2010)

This is a magisterial survey tracing the history of Western thinking about strategy – the employment of military force as a political instrument – from antiquity to the present. Heuser assesses sources from Vegetius to contemporary American thinkers, with particular focus on strategy since the Napoleonic Wars. She explores the evolution of strategic thought, the social institutions, norms and patterns of behaviour within which it operates, the policies that guide it and the culture that influences it. Ranging across technology and warfare, total warfare and small wars as well as land, sea, air and nuclear warfare, she demonstrates that warfare and strategic thinking have fluctuated wildly in their aims, intensity, limitations and excesses over the past two millennia.

The book challenges simplistic notions of a continuous 'Western way of war' or linear progress toward greater destructiveness, showing instead dialectical shifts between paradigms (e.g., battle of annihilation vs. limited war, offensive cult vs. defensive, total mobilisation vs. professional elites).

## Historiography Note

Heuser's work is a landmark synthesis of strategic thought, drawing on an extraordinary range of sources while focusing on the interplay of ideas with social, political, cultural, and technological contexts. It explicitly engages with and often revises earlier narratives (e.g., the 'Western way of war' thesis associated with Hanson). Strengths: breadth, attention to non-linear dialectics (e.g., total vs limited, offensive vs defensive, annihilation vs coercion), and coverage of all domains. Limitations: primarily Western (with some comparisons); the 2010 cut-off means post-2010 developments (e.g., hybrid warfare in Ukraine, AI, cyber evolution) are absent. Complements operational histories (Murray/Millett, West Point) and theoretical works (Luttwak). For the wiki, it provides the essential intellectual backbone for updating strategy-related pages across periods, highlighting how concepts like 'victory', 'limited war', and 'deterrence' have been reinterpreted over time. Collection coverage for 20th-c. strategic thought is strong via this and related sources; ancient/medieval thinner but anchored by primaries.

Grounding: "Corbett was more modest... ‘working command’... 1. Methods of securing command: (a) By obtaining a decision. (b) By blockade. 2. Targets: [[concepts/maritime-strategy]], [[actors/corbett-julian]], [[concepts/grand-strategy]], [[hubs/warfare/strategy]], [[concepts/limited-war]].

- **Blue water school vs. bricks-and-mortar school:** Blue water (Colombs, Bridge, Mahanians): prioritize navy/fleet over coastal fortifications; flexible sea-going force for strategic defence; convoys or cruisers for commerce. Bricks-and-mortar (army side, Wolseley, Jervois): coastal forts cheaper/safer than "floating defences"; invasion fears with steam. Mahan nuanced: fortresses shelter offensive fleet. Grounding: "The ‘bricks-and-mortar school’ were derided by the blue water school... John Colomb... secure naval bases... overseas bases... coal... dry docks." (pp. 226–227). Bridge: "three divisions of naval warfare... coast defence, colonial defence, and defence of commerce... adequate sea-going force" (Bridge 1907). Wolseley: "it is essentially necessary... powerful army... to defend our own shore." Targets: [[hubs/warfare/strategy]], [[hubs/warfare/naval-and-air-power-theory]], [[actors/mahan-alfred-thayer]], [[actors/corbett-julian]].

- **Blockade evolution (close vs. distant; tech impact):** Historical examples (Peloponnesian, Napoleonic Continental System, US Civil War); British shift post-1888 from close (St Vincent) to distant/open (Howe) due to torpedoes; Fisher: "It is not invasion we have to fear... it’s starvation!" Grounding: "They divided blockades into... ‘close blockade’... ‘open’ or ‘distant’ blockade... UK moved... to distant blockades... planned almost exclusively for distant..." (pp. 229–230; Olivier 2004). Targets: [[hubs/warfare/strategy]], [[concepts/sea-power]].

- **Amphibious/maritime operations and jointness:** Colomb on fine line between command of sea for descent and descents without it; mutual reinforcement (command + land forces + landings). Callwell: "British Army is a projectile to be fired by the Navy"; only joint force causes extensive damage; navy enables army "finishing stroke." Bridge: naval campaigns rarely end war alone. Grounding: "Philip Colomb wrote that ‘the dividing line between attempts to gain the command of the sea in order to facilitate a descent upon the land... is an exceedingly fine one’... ‘certain conditions – command of the sea, sufficient and well-handled land forces...’" (Colomb 1891; p. 230). Callwell/Bridge quotes (pp. 230–231). Targets: [[concepts/maritime-strategy]], [[actors/corbett-julian]], [[hubs/warfare/strategy]].

- **Fleet in being and gunboat diplomacy (deterrence/coercion pre-nuclear):** Colomb: inferior force "in being" as "shield"; British empire strategy of "deterrence, coercion and bluff." Gunboat diplomacy = use/threat of limited naval force for disputes or against nationals; "island grabbing"; practised by others; roots of later nuclear coercion. Grounding: "Sir Philip Colomb took up the view that a naval force ‘in being’, even if numerically inferior... might deter an invasion... navy as a ‘shield or guard’..." (p. 231). "Gunboat diplomacy is the use or threat of limited naval force... in the furtherance of an international dispute..." (Cable 1971; pp. 232–233). Targets: [[concepts/maritime-strategy]], [[concepts/limited-war]], [[hubs/warfare/strategy]].

- **French Jeune École (matériel/weak-power strategy):** For weaker vs. stronger (France vs. UK): commerce raiding (guerre de course), small/fast ships/torpedoes/subs over capital ships; "shamelessly flee from the strong; shamelessly attack the weak"; precursors Paixhans (exploding shells, speed), Grivels (cruiser warfare to hit "sources of the enemy’s prosperity"). Grounding: "‘shamelessly flee from the strong; shamelessly attack the weak’... cruiser Strategy to harass a superior foe... hit directly commerce and industry..." (Richild Grivel; pp. 235–236). "The group of French naval thinkers who became known as the ‘young school’... package" for limited capabilities. Targets: [[hubs/warfare/strategy]], [[concepts/sea-power]] (contrast).

- **French Mahanian/historical school (Daveluy, Darrieus):** Decisive annihilation battle, concentration, offensive à outrance, moral force; dismissed guerre de course as aberration or for colonies/slow vessels; "Destroy the enemy and you will have both... simultaneously." Influenced by Mahan + Prussian land writers; entered WWI with this mindset. Grounding: "‘The aim of combat … is the annihilation of the enemy’... ‘There are no two ways of fighting... seek out and destroy’... ‘mass or concentration of forces’." Targets: [[actors/mahan-alfred-thayer]], [[hubs/warfare/strategy]], [[concepts/sea-power]].

- **German naval thought (Tirpitz risk fleet context):** Embraced (partly) fleet-in-being despite Tirpitz; dissenting voices (Valois, Maltzahn, Galster) for cruisers, small war on commerce, coastal defence over capital ships/command fetish; Tirpitz risk challenged by geography (Channel/North Sea/Atlantic concentration, third-party threat). Grounding: "put paid to Tirpitz’s risk Strategy as it supposed a threat to Britain from a third party." "Victor Valois... against the commitment to capital ships... Targets: [[hubs/warfare/strategy]], [[concepts/sea-power]].

- **WWI naval lessons (blockade, Jutland, U-boats, amphibious):** RN distant blockade starved populations (~900k deaths Germany/Austria); Jutland (only major fleet clash, indecisive); Gallipoli amphibious failure; German U-boat guerre de course (unrestricted) brought US entry (despite WWI knowledge of prior effect); Fisher hoped blockade would force fleet action, Corbett saw pressure on civilians. Grounding: "The British naval blockade of Germany gradually greatly affected... starvation... around 900,000 deaths... Jutland (Skagerrak)... 31 May 1916." - **WWII naval lessons and shifts:** US more Mahanian (carriers decisive: Midway, Leyte Gulf; Pacific focus); RN communications protection; Jutland disappointment led some Germans to reconsider; battleship yielded to carrier; "jointness" reinforced (land decision enabled by sea lift/supply); pure naval vs. continental fantasy ended; faith in Trafalgar myth paled. Grounding: "The US navy emerged... with a Mahanian emphasis... decisive naval battles still at the centre... aircraft-carriers... US victories at Midway... Leyte Gulf." Targets: [[hubs/warfare/strategy]], [[hubs/warfare/naval-and-air-power-theory]], [[actors/mahan-alfred-thayer]], [[periods/world-wars-era]].

- **Nuclear age impact on maritime strategy (Ch. 11):** Surface ships "too good a target"; focus shifted to tech (subs, missiles, refuelling); major fleet battles between nuclear powers "difficult to envisage" (Bull); "slow-moving struggle for command of the seas... rendered pointless"; navies in support roles (Corbett "maritime strategy"); NATO incoherence (long war vs. strategic bombing; "broken-backed wars"); second-tier powers retain coercion/gunboat roles, resource protection (fishing, minerals), island disputes (Falklands "last nineteenth-century-style naval war"). Grounding: "Edward Teller... ‘I would not put anything on the surface of the ocean – it’s too good a target’." Bull: "between nuclear powers a major war at sea is difficult to envisage... strategic nuclear threshold would not be crossed." "British historians... emphasised... interconnectedness... under Corbett’s term of ‘maritime strategy’." (pp. 269–272; Teller q.i. Till 1982; Bull 1980). Targets: [[concepts/maritime-strategy]], [[hubs/warfare/strategy]], [[periods/cold-war]], [[concepts/grand-strategy]].

- **Continuities and dialectics across naval thought:** Concepts (deterrence, gunboat coercion, blockade hitting "soft underbelly," fleet in being) influenced later air/nuclear; ideological/practical mixes (Jeune École in "liberal" France, Tirpitz fleet-in-being in Germany); no linear progress; interservice rivalry key; "within each... arguments of practicality might contend with ethical concerns." Grounding: "Crucial concepts were developed here which would influence air power and nuclear thinking... deterrence, coercive Strategies... gunboat diplomacy... blockades... ideological element... reality is more complex..." (pp. 247–248, conclusions of Ch. 9). Targets: [[hubs/warfare/strategy]], [[hubs/warfare/naval-and-air-power-theory]], [[concepts/strategy]].

- **Overall for wiki synthesis (from range conclusions):** Mahan remained influential (esp. US post-WWII education) but faith shaken elsewhere; Corbett/maritime/joint emphasis gained; nuclear + air imposed new constraints; second-tier and post-empire roles persist via limited/coercive tools. Grounding: "While the USA came out of the Second World War still believing in naval battles, the faith in Mahanian ideas had been shaken in all other major players... the nuclear revolution, imposed new considerations..." "in the nuclear age, the ‘decisive battle’ school among naval thinkers was soon dying out... navies relegated to support roles... best subsumed under Corbett’s term." (pp. 267–272). Targets: [[hubs/warfare/strategy]], [[hubs/warfare/naval-and-air-power-theory]], [[actors/mahan-alfred-thayer]], [[actors/corbett-julian]], [[periods/world-wars-era]], [[periods/cold-war]].

**Claims (verbatim-grounded only from range_3; listed with key pre-established links where applicable):**

- **Colonial "air control" as direct precursor to morale/city bombing doctrine (Trenchard school):** "Villages will be razed to the ground and all woodwork removed. Pressure will be brought on the inhabitants by cutting off water power and by destroying water lifts... the area being cleared of the necessities of life." (1920 British Army Directive re Mesopotamia, q.i. Meilinger 2003: 50). RAF officer: "One objective must be selected – preferably the most inaccessible village... The attack with bombs and machine guns must be relentless and unremitting... This sounds brutal, I know, but it must be made brutal to start with." (q.i. Budiansky 2003: 143; emphasis in range). Applied in Iraq 1922 under John Salmond to teach "natives" a "tradition". Links to [[air-power]], [[strategic-bombardment]], [[periods/world-wars-era]].

- **Trenchard on moral vs material effect and extension of air power:** "At present the moral effect of bombing stands undoubtedly to the material effect in a proportion of 20 to 1." (Trenchard 1 Jan 1919 official record, q.i. Budiansky 2003: 103, 131). In 1922 paper Trenchard proposed air power to quell "industrial disturbances" in "settled countries" including England (Churchill forbade further mention of home use). Reflects overlap of class thinking, Social Darwinism, and racist distinctions (treatment of "coloured" peoples vs whites). Grounds [[strategic-bombardment]] roots in [[hubs/warfare/naval-and-air-power-theory]].

- **Mitchell on air power as first punitive element and decisive:** Billy Mitchell prophesied (1925) states would use air power as "first punitive element" in any conflict "because it was so easy to call upon" (Mitchell 1925: 131). "The air forces will strike immediately at the enemy’s manufacturing and food centers, railways, bridges, canals and harbors. The saving of lives... will be tremendous... He therefore thought victories might be ‘sharp and decisive’." (Mitchell 1925: xv f., 15). "Nothing can stop the attack of aircraft except other aircraft." Links to established [[mitchell-william]], [[air-power]], [[command-of-the-air]].

- **Douhet on terror shortening war and erasure of belligerent/non-belligerent distinction:** "‘Mercifully’, he wrote, the decision will be quick in this kind of war, since the decisive blows will be directed at civilians... Later: "Any distinction between belligerents and nonbelligerents is no longer admissible today either in fact or theory... the safest place may be the trenches." (Douhet 1928/1983: 195f.). Ties to [[douhet-giulio]], [[total-war]], [[strategic-bombardment]].

- **Four schools of air power (core taxonomy, repeated section heading):** "Air Strategy was dominated by four schools, the strategic or city bombing school, the military target school and the two less important schools advocating leadership targeting and political signalling." Contrasted with Pape: conventional "punishment" rarely succeeds; "denial strategies work best" (Pape 1996: 10). Grounds [[strategic-bombardment]], [[deterrence]] pre-history.

- **Military targets / denial school (battlefield, lines of communication, panacea/vital points):** French 1921 instruction: reconnaissance, bombers for battlefield/artillery support and "installations of the enemy"; fighters for freedom of action. German Luftwaffe emphasized full spectrum including CAS and military installations (kept within international law per range). Soviet shift to tactical orientation. Slessor (1936): main offensive against enemy lines of communication and own air force; "air forces are less able to decline actual combat". Links [[strategic-bombardment]] (limited), [[hubs/warfare/strategy]].

- **Leadership targeting / decapitation school:** "The ‘leadership targeting school’, which aimed to inflict punishment on the chief evil doers... a sub-category of panacea targeting". Mix in Desert Storm Phase I (leadership + electricity + oil + air defences). GWAPS: inability to "land a bomb on Saddam’s lap" due to intelligence, not tech. - **Political signalling / games theory school (Osgood/Schelling influence):** "The political signalling school of air targeting... had its roots... in games theory derived from economics. It dominated the early air power application by the USA in the Vietnam War." GRIT/"graduated and reciprocated initiatives"; "there is always a prospect of worse to come"; pauses to induce negotiation. Rolling Thunder (1965–68) as punitive bargaining failure (Hanoi gained kudos, repaired during pauses; Tet showed no break in will). Linebacker I/II (1972) with PGMs as closer to compellence success per some (Shultz). "one ‘player’ was trying to fight an economic signalling ‘game’... while the other was fighting an irregular war". Links [[schelling-thomas]], [[deterrence]].

Pape (30+ cases): "conventional punishment... rarely succeeds"; "it is the threat of military failure, which I call denial, and not threats to civilians... which provides the critical leverage". - **Nuclear Strategy as direct child of air Strategy:** "Nuclear Strategy is the child of air Strategy, in that the concepts governing it were mostly derived from air power theory... the strategic or city bombing school of the pre-nuclear age became the nuclear deterrence school, while the military targeting preferences of conventional air strategy underpinned nuclear war-fighting strategy." - **Brodie and origins of nuclear deterrence (verbatim):** Brodie (1946): "the first and most vital step in any American security program for the age of atomic bombs is to take measures to guarantee to ourselves in case of attack the possibility of retaliation in kind... Thus far the chief purpose of our military establishment has been to win wars. From now on its chief purpose must be to avert them. It can have almost no other useful purpose." (Brodie 1946: 76). - **Santa Cruz de Marcenado’s comprehensive counterinsurgency (p. 429–431):** "insurgencies are unlikely to succeed or are less dangerous if they are not brought about by poor government; therefore, government should be ‘gentle and just’"; act quickly "lest it spread" or "the tide would turn in favour of the rebels"; "respect for traditional customs, laws, religion and privileges"; no punishment without regular trial (undermines rule of law); change garrisons/commanders if complaints; economic welfare ("trade, manufacture ... protected and encouraged"); "establishment of schools and universities"; "today’s rebels would ideally be tomorrow’s productive citizens"; "avoiding bloody reprisals, as they merely served to create martyrs ... and increased support for the insurgency"; "giving the population of the area concerned a stake in a reformed state which the counterinsurgency tried to establish (state-building!)". "This is perhaps the most important line of thinking on counterinsurgency." Major enrichment for [[counterinsurgency]] (prefigures modern doctrine; notes no English translation).
- **French colonial COIN (Duchemin, Lyautey; p. 432–433):** Duchemin: "The pirate [insurgent] is a plant which grows only on certain soil. ... armed occupation ... ploughs it; ... the reconstitution and equipment of the population, the installation of markets and cultures, the construction of roads, sow the good grain." Lyautey: "combined application of force and politics"; "turn to destruction only as a last resort, and only as a preliminary to better reconstruction"; "reconstruction of the village, creation of a market, and establishment of a school"; "Political action is by far the most important." Grounds [[counterinsurgency]] precursors alongside Santa Cruz.
- **Hearts and minds and Thompson (p. 433–434):** Term associated with Templer in Malaya ("win the hearts and minds of the population"); Thompson and O’Neill criticised US Vietnam failure on population support; "the government ... had to stay on the side of legality at all times"; "No government ... could impose itself and win the sympathy of the local population if it engaged in torture or execution of terrorists"; "comprehensive master-plan"; "you could never ... merely go back to the status quo ante (Thompson 1970)."
- **Galula/Trinquier and asymmetry (p. 434):** In "revolutionary war" population "would more often than not be terrorised by the partisans into giving their support"; need to "protect the population from the bullying and terror of the insurgent leaders"; French in Indochina/Algeria turned "terror, illegitimacy, torture" against insurgents (Trinquier 1961/2006: 20, 27, 68ff.); "Galula strongly condemned, pointing at the asymmetry of moral judgement regarding insurgent and counterinsurgency actions."
- **Petraeus/FM 3-24 principles (p. 435–436):** "The center of gravity in counterinsurgency operations is the population. Therefore, understanding the local society and gaining its support is critical to success." 14 recommendations incl.: "Delegate as much as possible to the locals, so that they feel ownership ... (traceable to T.E. Lawrence)"; "Visible improvements ... quickly"; financial investment; increase stakeholders; "political (and psychological) costs ... do not outweigh the military gains"; "Intelligence ... crucial ... targeted individually and discriminately"; "state-building (‘nation-building’) or civic action"; "ministries of justice, the interior ... have to be reformed"; "Cultural awareness is crucial"; "Counterinsurgency is only successful when economic and social recovery ... go along with military success"; "ultimate success depends on the behaviour and acceptance of the local leaders"; "junior commanders" need "initiative, flexibility, imagination"; "A leader’s most important task is to set the right tone" (Petraeus 2006; FM 3-24 2006).
- **Consensus on asymmetric warfare (p. 437, also p. 435):** "only the side that puts the population’s interests first will ultimately win them over – terror will bring about temporary conformity and obedience, but not long-term success." "When COIN forces are seen as occupiers, or when insurgents are seen as brutal oppressors, people may be cowed ... but it will not become a self-sustaining force." "Persuasion, not the imposition of one’s will ... is at the heart of success in asymmetric warfare." "In the context of small wars, writers realised early on that the key to lasting success had to be the pacification of a country or region, not by the brute imposition of force, but by holding out a better deal to the population." Core for [[counterinsurgency]], [[guerrilla-warfare]], [[concepts/irregular-warfare]], and [[hubs/warfare/strategy]].

**Range summary paragraph:** Heuser shows asymmetric/'small' wars invert regular assumptions: insurgents exploit illegality, population blending, and rule-breaking for survival and exhaustion (Mao fish/sea metaphor echoed even by repressors); regulars face protracted disadvantage (Callwell) and temptation to mirror brutality (Vendée to Keitel to "draining the swamp"), yet effective COIN requires rapid grievance redress, legality, economic/political incentives, discriminate intelligence, cultural awareness, and giving the population a stake in a reformed order (Santa Cruz as key early theorist; Lyautey/Galliéni; Templer/Thompson hearts-and-minds; Galula/Trinquier protection from insurgent terror; Petraeus 14 points centering population as center of gravity with state-building and junior-leader flexibility). Persuasion over pure imposition of will is the recurring lesson; terror yields only temporary results. ## Consolidated Volume Synthesis

Beatrice Heuser's *The Evolution of Strategy* (2010) provides the most comprehensive modern intellectual history of Western strategic thought surveyed in the wiki. It demonstrates that warfare and strategic thinking have fluctuated wildly rather than progressing linearly, challenging notions of a continuous "Western way of war" (battles of annihilation) or inevitable escalation to total destructiveness. Core dialectic: tension between Napoleonic paradigm (total victory, decisive battle, annihilation, cult of the offensive, mass mobilisation) and alternatives (limited war, indirect approach, coercion, defensive defence, asymmetric/people's war, nuclear deterrence).

Key contributions:
- **Definitions and dimensions**: Strategy as political instrument (link to policy, dialectics of wills, interdependent variables including Clausewitz trinity); grand strategy as integration of all power elements.
- **Long-term constants vs. change**: Technology, ethics (just war), war aims (limited/unlimited), recruitment (feudal/mercenary/militia/professional), battle avoidance vs. decisive, quest for eternal principles (from Vegetius to Fuller/Liddell Hart).
- **Napoleonic/Total War paradigm and challenges**: 1792–1945 shift via nationalism, Social Darwinism, total mobilisation, genocide (Ludendorff to Nazis); WWI/II lessons leading to relinquishment (Maginot, Liddell Hart indirect, population targeting).
- **Domain-specific evolutions**: Naval/maritime (Mahan vs. Corbett command-of-communications, Jeune École, fleet-in-being, nuclear impact on surface fleets); Air power (Trenchard morale bombing from colonial "air control," four schools: city, military, leadership, signalling; nuclear inheritance); Asymmetric/small wars (Callwell disadvantages of regulars; brutal repression continuity; Santa Cruz/Lyautey/Galula/Thompson/Petraeus COIN emphasizing hearts/minds, legality, population as center, state-building).
- **Post-WWII search for new paradigms**: Spectator warfare, limited/coercive wars (Beaufre), defensive defence, Summers Clausewitz critique, ongoing small wars, bureaucratic politics vs. strategy, human frailty.
- **Historiography**: Western-centric but self-aware; draws on vast primary strategists (Vegetius to modern); complements operational histories and other surveys (Paret, Freedman, Brands); notes ideological/practical mixes and non-linear dialectics.
