# Verbruggen, *The Art of Warfare in Western Europe During the Middle Ages* (1997 trans.)

J. F. Verbruggen's study (1954/2nd ed. rev.) is the foundational modern monograph on the conduct of war in western Europe from the Carolingians to the eve of the Hundred Years' War. The English translation (Willard & Southern) made his source-critical reconstructions of knightly and infantry tactics widely available. It remains the single most cited operational study for the period in staff-college and academic contexts.

## Verbruggen's Core Arguments (for this ingest)

- Medieval commanders **did** practice a coherent art of war: campaigns were planned, battles had tactical schemes (flank attacks, reserves, combined arms), and units (conrois) existed with recognised leaders.
- The basic tactical unit of the knight was the small conroi; larger "batailles" were formed from them.
- Infantry (urban militias, peasants, mercenaries) played a decisive and often undervalued role; Courtrai and Bouvines are case studies of foot defeating or heavily contesting heavy cavalry on favourable ground.
- Source numbers must be criticised (echoing but independently of Delbrück's *Sachkritik*); vernacular sources and logistical realities yield lower, more credible figures.
- Knighthood was both a social and a professional military class; solidarity, training, and equipment evolved in response to opponents (including foot and Muslim light cavalry in the East).
- The period shows gradual professionalisation and the increasing importance of fortifications and control of territory rather than constant pitched battle.

### Extracted Claims — Infantry Revival / Foot-Soldiers Section (this range, ~pp. 111–128+)

**Core framing of the revival**:
- "The splendid victory of the Flemish foot-soldiers in the battle of Courtrai, 11 July 1302, won them a place in history as the first to defeat a great knightly army since Roman times. But these foot-soldiers already had a considerable past history by 1302..."
- Courtrai "was followed in 1314 by Bannockburn... in 1315 by Morgarten... and in 1319 by the victory of the peasants of Dithmarschen over the knights of Holstein."
- "How did these great victories of foot-soldiers... come about at the beginning of the fourteenth century?"
- "Something is known about the communal armies of Flemish cities from the year 1127... yet the foot-soldiers remained scarcely more than a secondary arm, an auxiliary weapon at best, often put to no use at all because the count and his nobles underrated their possibilities. Then suddenly these same foot-soldiers scored a brilliant victory, and became conscious of their own strength."

**Causal factors (social, economic, psychological)**:
- "Medieval foot-soldiers were apt to suffer from an inferiority complex and to lack confidence in their own ability, which was to be blamed on their relatively lowly social status. They also lacked that inner cohesiveness so characteristic of the knights..."
- "Fighting men had to be comparatively numerous, and sufficiently well-to-do to equip themselves well, which in turn depended on demographic and economic development, and they needed capable leaders..."
- "It is not surprising that the Flemings, Scots, Swiss and Dithmarschen fought for freedom and independence. They all had a keen national feeling, permeating wide social strata; the struggle for a better life in the Flemish cities increased their sense of fellow-feeling and made them stubborn and tenacious in war. After several centuries of social and economic development these main factors matured simultaneously, and the situation of the foot-soldiers was helped by the fact that their chief rivals, the knights, had then passed their peak as a social class."

**Peasant foot-soldiers (Swiss, Dithmarschen examples)**:
- Swiss (Schwyz, Uri, Unterwalden): free and non-free formed "Mark-association"; allmende (common meadow-land) as economic/political bond; population ~1,530 families in Schwyz ~1281; able-bodied men "could be assembled in a few hours"; St Gotthard pass traffic brought prosperity and autonomy; Uri under direct royal power 1231 then self-rule.
- Dithmarschen: protected by sea, Elbe, marshes, dikes; "very favourable for the defenders, and extremely difficult for armies of knights"; Geschlechter (clans) central to vendetta, peace, land reclamation; self-government via guardians and parishes.

**Knights fighting with/on foot alongside infantry**:
- "As leaders of the foot-soldiers, the knights fought on foot in the defence of fortified towns and castles. On a battlefield they gave strength to the formation, by taking place in the first rank of the foot-soldiers. They gave moral support, for they could not abandon the foot. In Flanders some hundreds of knights fought with the foot-soldiers at Courtrai, Arques, and Mons-en-Pévèle."

**Tactics and formations (comparanda for Courtrai-style infantry)**:
- Scottish schiltrons (Falkirk 1298): "pikemen were drawn up in four schiltrons, circular formations which were supported by reserve armoured cavalry"; "the Scottish pikemen in their tightly packed formation offered very stiff resistance: the men in the front rank were kneeling, with the butt end of their pikes planted in the ground, while the next rows held their weapons above the heads of their comrades. Many English riders lost their horses during the charge against the dense enemy mass."
- Close-order discipline stressed throughout: disorder frequently cited as cause of defeat.

**English longbow evolution (context for massed missile infantry)**:
- Longbow could shoot "ten to twelve arrows a minute as against the crossbow's two, with an effective range of up to two hundred yards"; cheaper, locally made.
- Edward I used archers "en masse, thousands at a time"; "The tactical effectiveness of such weapons increases as they are used on a massive scale."

**Source criticism (applicable to Courtrai reconstruction)**:
- "what is good in poetry does not necessarily mean good tactics, and historical accounts sometimes have to stress that, contrary to what the poets say, the fight was started in a proper and orderly fashion; often also they attribute a defeat to the fact that the ranks were thrown into disorder."
- Distinguishes chansons de geste (individual heroics) from actual ordered charges followed by penetration phase.

**General army organisation and discipline (applicable to both sides at Courtrai)**:
- French royal army example (1328 at Cassel): advanced in multiple "batailles" (e.g., first under marshals + master of crossbowmen with 6 banners; royal formation with oriflamme wing, etc.); total 196 banners in one account; marshals directed camp layout.
- On the march: marshals with banners ahead; units not to leave ranks; trumpets for signals (saddle horses, arm, mount, advance, rally).
- Templar rules as model of extreme discipline: eschieles (units); no leaving formation without permission; squires handling lances/spare horses; test rides only with permission; severe but not expulsion-level punishments for breaches.
- General knightly armies: each knight "remained in his lord's formation and might not leave the ranks nor ride in front of the banner" (exceptions for necessities); at alarm, units hastened to help.

## Extracts: The Knight, Cavalry Organisation and Tactics (Range 2)

**Exclusive to this range** (approx. lines 8001–18000 of the source extraction; material on knightly equipment, training, solidarity, conrois / batailles, and mounted combat principles). All claims grounded with short verbatim quote + immediate context. No outside knowledge used.

### Tournaments as Tactical Training for Conrois

Tournaments between 1150–1250 served as direct preparation for battle tactics. Knights fought in organised units on agreed terrain, advancing from their "ground" in close order.

- "The knights were organised in conrois or units of varying strength, according to the power of the lord under whose banner they were fighting, or according to the extent of the participation of the nobility of a certain area. These units were drawn up in very close formation, the horsemen side by side, horse beside horse, and they had to advance and charge in an orderly manner. Such units were so obviously superior to those not drawn up in an orderly way that they were able to turn an unfavourable balance of strength to their own advantage."

- Individual action was condemned: "one of the greatest stupidities that could be committed was the separate individual charge made by knights who abandoned the protective ranks of the conroi in order to rush ahead into battle, for in so doing they destroyed the cohesion of the unit. If on the other hand they attacked in close order, there was no risk of the enemy breaking through."

- Philip of Alsace (praised as one of the best knights) used sophisticated reserve tactics in tournaments: kept powerful contingents (including well-equipped foot) "skilfully behind the scenes," waited for opposing units to lose cohesion through prolonged fighting, then charged the flank ("lor moveit a la traverse"). He taught these methods to Prince Henry. The *Histoire de Guillaume le Maréchal* contrasts "dense conrois (seréement)" advancing "without disorder (disrei)" and "serried ranks (errèrent sagement et rangié e seréement) so that no one could get through them (onques nuls n' en trespassa outre)" with disordered units ("a grant disrei") where knights "recklessly broke rank" ("poindre as premiers de la rote"); the latter were "severely censured (fols est qui trop tost se desrote)."

- "Just as in real wars, tournaments served to foster local pride and increased moral solidarity in military units."

### Psychology of Knights and the Protective Value of Armour and Units

Poetic glorification (Bertrand de Born, *Girart de Vienne*, etc.) of the "joy" of battle and "lust for fighting" must be balanced against evidence that knights remained human and feared death, wounds, mutilation and captivity. Courage was shown in how they overcame fear.

- Armour (1150–1250) was effective: "This was anyway not necessary between 1150 and 1250, when the defensive equipment of the knights was strong enough to prevent fatal accidents." Risk of being unhorsed existed but "the danger was not much greater in the real battles of that time when few men were killed."

- Contemporary external observers:
  - Anna Comnena on western ("Celtic") knights: "The Celtic race is independent and does not like asking for advice; they have no military discipline nor strategic skill, but as soon as they have to fight and do battle, a raging fury seizes their hearts and they become irresistible... They hurl themselves with invincible impetus into the midst of the enemy ranks... If, however, the enemy goes on laying ambushes... then their courage collapses into despair... the Celts are invincible in the first onslaught, but after that they are easy to overcome because their arms and equipment are very heavy, and they behave recklessly because of their impulsive nature."
  - Ousama ibn Munquidh: Franks seen "as beasts, outdoing all others in courage and warlike spirit." Bedouin contempt: "li Frans qui s'arme pour poour de mort!" (The Franks wear armour because they fear death!).
  - After Hattin (1187): Muslim observers described exhausted French knights as "an iron mass, off which blows simply glanced" while fighting; "These Christians were lions at the start of the battle; by the end they looked more like scattered sheep."

- Western sources also noted fewer casualties than in antiquity: "Formerly, many thousands perished in battle, but now, because of increasing calamities the means of protection have also been improved, and new defence has been found against new weapons." (William the Breton).

### Solidarity in Knightly Clans (Lignage) and Lord's Retinue

Clan and household bonds supplied the cohesion that compensated for lack of Roman drill.

- "The knightly clan was smaller, making for greater solidarity. It was provided with better weapons and had much more efficient equipment... The knights were also conscious of belonging to the ruling class..."

- "Neither chance nor a haphazard grouping makes up the unit of cavalry or foot-soldiers, but families or clans." Men advanced "under a sign or banner, the fano, gundfano, or bandwa."

- Joinville on Mansurah (1250): Guy de Mauvoisin's *bataille* "achieved splendid results... for this formation consisted entirely of members of this clan and vassals of this lord."

- Ambroise treats clans and serried units as synonymous: "The clans advanced together / And regrouped themselves together / This made the army so tightly packed / That it could scarcely be harmed."

- At Arsuf: James of Avesnes "fought with his clan and perished with three members of his family." Templars, when surprised, dismounted and formed "crown formation" "as if they all sprang from one father" (poet notes this was still not as tight as true family units).

- Epic evidence: Aymeri de Narbonne cycle shows clan members rush to each other's aid without rivalry; "the heroic deeds of Aymeri are kept in heredity"; fear of family dishonour ("Come and help me, that I may not be guilty of cowardice, which would be a reproach to my family!").

- Retinues: Ancient comitatus pattern continued. "The commander fought for victory, his men fought for their leader. He maintained them out of the booty... and he provided them with their costly charger, their arms and their board at his table."

- Concrete examples of household scale: Louis the Fat (1108) raised army "exclusively of knights belonging to his household"; Richard I's retinue "formed one of the main units in the battle"; Edward I's household in Flanders 1297 reached 475–550 armoured cavalry (400–420 permanent); French royal hostel 1317 had 235 armoured cavalrymen.

### Tactical Units: Conrois and Batailles in Battle

The practical solution for reducing danger and increasing effectiveness was the conroi / bataille system (detailed in dedicated [[concepts/conroi]] page; see also [[concepts/knightly-cavalry-organisation-and-tactics]]).

- "Together with the individual and collective training, the tightly knit social structure, noble conceptions of honour and duty, there was still another means of reducing danger, overcoming fear, preventing capture, and increasing the effectiveness of fighting units – this was to go into battle in small tactical units, conrois, which in turn formed larger units, conrois, which in turn formed larger units, batailles or 'battles', in order to be able to give more effective support."

- Dense formation repeated in eyewitness and near-eyewitness sources (Ambroise, chansons de geste): units "serried in ranks / As if they were people forged in iron"; "the wind could not blow between their lances"; glove or prune could not be thrown into the ranks without striking mail.

- "Allowing for poetic exaggeration, the fact remains that the units were so closely packed that the horses were touching each other in formation."

- At Darum (1180): army compressed "so closely that they could scarcely carry out an attack because of the mass" yet "our men were too tightly packed. They fought off the enemy attack and continued their advance deliberately." Density enabled resistance even when it hindered the charge.

- Banners, devices and liveries identified units on the field. "In small units the vassals wore the insignia of their lord... At Bouvines the emperor Otto was recognised by the device of his conroi."

- Scale and combination: conrois of 12–24 (often 6–8 in front rank); bannerets commanded ~20. Batailles = series of adjacent conrois producing fronts of 50–60+. French army to Cassel 1328: 10 *batailles*, 177–196 banners. In Latin Empire under Henry: 15 *battles* mostly of 20 knights (emperor's 50), used in staggered lines with named sub-units assigned specific roles in the attack.

- "Here we are clearly dealing with tactical units. What would be the point of carrying out their charges in the manner described if there were no tactical units executing the commands of the leader as one man?"

## Extracts from General Preface, Sources Introduction, and Knight Material (Range 1)

**Note:** All claims grounded exclusively in the text of this range (General Preface by Matthew Bennett + Verbruggen's historiographical introduction, source discussion, and early sections on the knight). Verbatim quotes provided for grounding. Page references from the 1997 Boydell edition.

### Verbruggen's Critique of Prior Historians (Methodological Statements)

Verbruggen opens by diagnosing the "unfortunate treatment" medieval warfare has received. He systematically dismantles earlier scholars for lack of source criticism, acceptance of inflated numbers, neglect of contemporary texts, and invention of manoeuvres.

- On H. Delpech (La tactique au XIIIe siècle, 1886): "He accepted all estimates of the number of troops at their face value... He described battles without carefully sifting accounts of them, and texts from the period itself were often neglected in favour of later versions... Misled by exaggerated estimates of army strengths, he concluded that tactics were further advanced than they actually were. His work has been completely discredited by historians." (p. 1)

- On the Prussian general Köhler (Die Entwickelung des Kriegswesens..., 1886-93): "he nowhere offered any proof of this hypothesis [Byzantine influence]. He was totally lacking in a critical sense and, like Delpech, was not above inventing manoeuvres. In general he accepted uncritically the data of the sources regarding army strengths, and made no distinction between contemporary and later sources. He visualized triangular knightly formations..." Valuable for material but "must be consulted with due caution." (pp. 1-2)

- On Sir Charles Oman (A History of the Art of War in the Middle Ages, 2nd edn 1924): "It is more accurate than the first... thanks to the use of Delbrück's work... His work is interesting, partly on account of his very full descriptions of battles... But Oman was too facile. He did not get to the essentials of combat tactics, and his work lacks both synthesis and critical insight. Although the book is useful, it must be carefully checked against source materials." (p. 2)

- On Hans Delbrück (Geschichte der Kriegskunst..., 2nd edn 1923): Delbrück correctly showed small medieval army numbers, applied healthy criticism, stressed state/society structure, and prefaced knighthood as military class with social survey. However: "it is difficult to agree with Delbrück in his views on knightly warfare. He had read too few sources personally; his critique of the sources was biased; he depended too much on his students' work for analyses of battles. Moreover, Delbrück's choice of significant battles was arbitrary and he did not succeed in giving a trustworthy picture of knightly tactics. In fact he did not go much beyond providing a general sketch, altogether lacking in life." (pp. 2-3)

- Lot (1946) advanced on Delbrück via "first-rate knowledge of general history", "acutely critical mind", and "merciless scrutiny" of numbers (sharper even than predecessors), plus influence of Lefebvre des Noëttes on cavalry technology. Yet "this fine, carefully written work is not wholly satisfactory. Lot went from general to military history. His synthesis is based largely on secondary sources... In dealing with tactics... he did not pursue his investigation far enough. Yet it is these very details that illuminate our knowledge of combat technique; if they are carefully sifted and then collected into a coherent system, they give a much more accurate picture. It is from these details that one should proceed to generalities and not the other way around, as Lot did." Lot still accepted the individual combatant concept. (pp. 3-4)

Verbruggen's corrective method: "it is absolutely necessary to check the military value of each account. Even the best narratives may include inventions and legends, which can be spotted solely through the comparison of many sources." Proceed from "small details of fighting and tactics" upward. (p. 3, p. 9)

### Army Strengths and Logistical Realities

Knightly armies were small due to "relatively small states", professional class composition, "underdeveloped" economic life, "limited financial resources", scanty transport, and agriculture insufficient for large forces. (p. 5)

Reliable figures come from well-placed chroniclers (Gilbert of Mons for Hainault: 80-700 knights; figures for Flanders ~1,000 knights, Brabant 700, Liège bishop 700) and archival sources (Normandy 1172: 581 knights from 1,500+ fiefs; Brittany 1294: 166 knights + 16 squires; English armies "seldom exceeded 10,000 men"). (pp. 6-7)

Battlefield terrain checks: at Courtrai or Mons-en-Pévèle front "little over 1,000 yards". On such a plain "it is extremely difficult to manoeuvre 20,000 men" except in very deep frontal formations. (p. 7)

March column and gate/bridge calculations disprove huge figures (e.g., Orderic Vitalis on Antioch 1098). (pp. 7-8)

Examples of credible small forces: Brémule 1119 (Louis VI ~400 knights vs Henry I ~500); Lincoln 1217 (English king 400 knights + 347 crossbowmen vs barons ~611 knights + 1,000 foot). (p. 9)

### Source Quality and Historiographical Problems

**Clerical/monastic sources limitations:** Often incomplete ("in order not to bore the reader"), clerics "ignorant of military matters" give "fabricated accounts, or add imaginary details." Tendency to ascribe victory to miracles, God, saints, relics (e.g., Albert of Aix's dramatized Ramla 1101 account with five successive crusader contingents, the last saved by the Holy Cross, vs. Fulcher of Chartres' more sober six-contingent version with reserves; Albert inflates enemy to 200,000 and claims 20,000 slain next day despite only 40 knights + 200 foot left). (pp. 10-11)

Clergy sometimes partisan against knights. Latin terminology often anachronistic/classical and inaccurate. (p. 11)

**Reliable secular and eye-witness sources:** Chronicles/biographies by knights or confidants. Key early: Nithard (Carolingian cavalry games as training/mock battles; 834 discipline failure of Wido's army despite superior numbers; 841 defensive deployment by Charles the Bald with sentries/signals and central reserve at St Cloud). (pp. 12-13)

First Crusade: Anonymous (Gesta Francorum, "ordinary knight"), Raymond of Aguilers, Ralph of Caen, Albert of Aix (detailed but credulous), Fulcher of Chartres (better on Ramla). Later: William of Tyre (excellent), Ambroise (Norman jongleur, Third Crusade, vernacular clearer than Itinerarium Latin), Histoire de Guillaume le Maréchal, Villehardouin & Robert de Clari (Fourth Crusade), Henry of Valenciennes, Jean de Joinville (psychology of knights), Jan van Heelu (detailed Worringen, >4,000 lines), Guillaume Guiart (1304 Flemish wars), Jean le Bel (1327 Scotland campaign, Crécy). (pp. 11-12, 14, 17-18)

Vernacular superior for precise terminology: scaren/scharen/eschielle/schieri; bataelgen/batailles/battaglie; conroten/conrois; banieren/bannières. These Germanic-derived terms (scara, bandwa, roi=order) indicate troop divisions already in use 6th-9th centuries. Latin equivalents (legiones, cohortes, acies, cunei, turmae, ordines) vary by chronicler and obscure distinctions. (pp. 13-14)

Eye-witness competence: Exaggerated modern scepticism (Fris on Guiart, citing Stendhal/Erasmus) refuted. Best battle accounts come from participants or those on the spot (Bouvines: William the Breton; Worringen: Jan van Heelu; Arsuf: Ambroise; Mansurah: Joinville; Ramla: Fulcher). Limitations are opportunity for observation, not inherent incompetence. Jean le Bel's 1327 account masterfully conveys "the state of mind of soldiers... hunger, thirst... fear, sense of honour and discipline—the whole psychology of the soldier". (pp. 16-18)

### The Knight and His Equipment

Medieval warfare "characterised by the dominant role of the heavy cavalry" from barbarian invasions (4th c.). Technical innovations: "high saddle, with supports behind and before, and stirrups"; horseshoes; "long leather cuirasses"; long lances or bows. Steppe nomads' natural advantages ended Roman legion supremacy. Among Franks, shift from foot to armoured cavalry between early 8th and mid-9th century. (pp. 19-20)

Carolingian expansion under Charles Martel, Pepin III, Charlemagne required permanent professional troops and armoured horsemen (brunia = tunic with metal rings/plates, metal helmet, leg guards, high-quality long swords, lances, bows). Vassals installed on church lands; scara units provided rapid mobile force. (pp. 20-21)

**The scara:** Elite rapid-response armoured cavalry. Examples: Pepin 766 garrison at Bourges; 768 pursuit of Aquitaine duke in four directions; Charlemagne 773 sent scara around Mount Cenis fortifications in Lombardy campaign; 774 four scarae vs Saxons; 782 disastrous disarray attack by three leaders' scara on Saxons (routed because "not as if they were attacking an enemy in battle order, but as if they were about to pursue a beaten enemy"); 784 equestri proelio victory; used for raids, garrisons, frontier marches, and parallel invasions. Evolved from royal bodyguard (replacing Merovingian antrustiones) to technical term for armoured cavalry formation by later 9th c. (pp. 21-22)

**Marchfield to Mayfield:** Muster shifted from March to May (by Pepin III ~755/756) for grass availability for horses; campaigns timed for ripe crops. (pp. 22-23)

**Cost of equipment (Ripuarian Law):** Helmet 6 solidi; brunia/byrny 12; sword+scabbard 7 (sword alone 3); leggings 6; lance+shield 2; horse 12. Total for fully armoured horseman equivalent to ~15 mares or nearly 23 oxen. Small landowners sold inheritances for horse+sword. Capitulary of Thionville 805 required brunia only for those with 12 mansi. (pp. 23-24)

Evolution of armour: brunia → hauberk (mail-shirt) → great hauberk (long) from late 12th c., reinforced with plates mid-13th c. Horses increasingly barded (armoured) from mid-12th c. (examples: 1187 Hainault unit >109/190 barded; pay differentials for squires). (pp. 24-25)

Multiple horses per knight (Robert II of Flanders 1101 promised 1,000 knights each with three horses). Expensive destriers/chargers documented in retinues (Geraard de Moor 1297: seven horses valued at £1,200 tournois total; 1302 Bruges purchases for William of Jülich included chargers £180, £179 etc.). (pp. 25-27)

By 13th c. full equipment (mail reinforced with plates, helmet, sword, lance, pennon, tent, kitchen gear, pack animal) so costly that number of true knights dwindled; squires more numerous (chose to avoid dubbing costs). Knights + squires = "armures de fer". (pp. 26-27)

### The Knight's Training

Began in earliest youth. Young boy (son of knight) entrusted to another knight (relative, friend, or monarch) for militia secularis training + court duties. Learned to withstand fatigue/hunger/thirst/heat/cold and master arms technique. Riding, javelin-throwing, fencing. Quintaine (charge with couched lance at shield on stake or dummy). Heavy equipment demanded fully-grown adults. (pp. 27-28)

Chrétien de Troyes (Roman de Perceval): training to "guide the horse during the charge, and how to stop it in time... put the lance on the felt of the saddle, spur the horse, and couch the lance under his upper arm... handle the shield... swordsmanship." (p. 28)

Roger of Hoveden on Henry II's sons: emphasis on practice to endure blows, see blood flow, fight when thrown down, gain strength through sweat. (pp. 28-29, quoting at length)

Individual training produced formidable warrior: mail-shirt, high saddle, stirrups, couched lance. From late 11th c. geared to "mighty attack". Supplemented by collective exercises/private wars (mid-12th c. in Flanders until ~1127 princes suppressed). (p. 29)

### Collective Training: Private Wars and Solidarity

Private wars/vendettas/feuds common 11th-early 12th c.; "savage fighting within the families and retinues"; "feroces socii". Families/clans showed strong solidarity; heavy casualties in clashes. Examples: Burgundy quarrel 30 years, one party lost >11 in one clash; Lambert of Wattrelos' ten paternal uncles killed near Tournai; Erembald family 1127 siege at Bruges (28 defenders hurled from towers, innocent Robert the Child beheaded); Bonifacius driven out/killed brothers who murdered his wife/son. (pp. 29-30)

Clergy opposed (Urban II at Clermont 1095 urged redirect energy vs. Moslems); chansons de geste celebrated (Raoul de Cambrai). Princes suppressed where strong (Normandy, Flanders post-William Clito/Thierry struggle); replaced with tournaments for group training. (pp. 30-31)

### Collective Training: Tournaments

Of ancient origin (primitive collective games, classical references in Tacitus on Tencteri, Visigoths "sham battles" daily, Charlemagne's sons' riding/weapon practice). Classic 12th c. form possibly 11th c. Geoffrey of Preuilly (d. 1066) legend. Military leaders reproduced war conditions in training. (pp. 31-32)

### Bouvines as Demonstration of Professional Foot-Soldiers' Value

- "The value of the mercenaries as foot-soldiers became evident in the battle of Bouvines in 1214."
- Brabancons: "The Brabancons who fought there were probably from Brabant and the Low Countries. Under the able leadership of Reginald of Boulogne they distinguished themselves so greatly that they are mentioned by all the chroniclers. They put up a splendid defence, and were the last troops to make a stand on the allied side."
- Formation and effect: "The mercenaries were drawn up in a circle only two ranks deep, and the knights' attack was smashed on their grounded pikes."
- Numbers and quality: "The Brabancons were not numerous, for by the end of the battle there were only 400, or at the most 700 of them left. But they were an example to any foot-soldiers of the time, since they fought as bravely as the knights, or even more so, as true 'preudomes'."
- Explanation: "Such excellence was of course the result of long experience in the practice of their profession on battlefields and in sieges, and of the esprit de corps which they had developed."

**Sources cited by Verbruggen for Bouvines infantry stand:** Genealogiae comitum Ft. Cont, Clarom., c. 22, p. 333; William the Breton, Chronicon, c. 197, p. 289; Ph. Mousket, Chronique rimée, II, vv. 221279, pp. 3723. (Also [G. Duby, *Le dimanche de Bouvines*, Paris, 1973.])

### French Communal Infantry at Bouvines

- Philip Augustus "made this militia march to the battlefield at Bouvines in 1214, and here it was obvious that they were properly more useful in the defence of the city fortifications."
- Placement and result: "The men of the communes were placed in the centre of the French battle order, but they were driven back by the enemy and played no vital part in the battle."
- "In France the kings had so many knights that there was no need to pay attention to the foot-soldiers."
- Numbers: "Several communes fought in the battle of Bouvines with a strength of 3,160 foot-soldiers." (In 1202: "the thirty communes gave a total of 5,140 men" or variant 5,410; royal domain post-conquests 7,695–8,054 or total 8,069 men.)

### Royal / Permanent Forces Context (1202–3, illustrative capacity)

- "In 1202–3, Philip Augustus maintained 257 knights, 267 mounted sergeants, 80 mounted crossbowmen, 133 unmounted crossbowmen and about 2,000 sergeants on foot on the Norman border, not counting the 300 mercenaries under Cadoc."
- Cadoc: "Cadoc's band was 300 men strong." (Earlier: defended Gaillon with 300 foot-soldiers 1196; penetrated Château-Gaillard 1204 with his men; took Angers; expensive upkeep — William the Breton exaggerates £1,000/day; one payment £4,400.)

### Carroccio / Banner Waggon at Bouvines

- "This means of regrouping troops on the battlefield was mentioned for the first time in 1039 at Milan. It was quickly adopted in the rest of Europe: in the twelfth century it was used in the Netherlands, including Flanders, in England at Northallerton in 1138, in the third Crusade, at Bouvines in 1214, and at Worringen in 1288."
- Description: "The Lombard foot-soldiers went out to battle in closely packed formations, behind which came a great waggon, the carroccio, with a banner on a tall pole. On this flagstaff was often also a monstrance, with the consecrated Host, and there were priests on the cart, around which troops who were driven back, or were fleeing, could [regroup and re-form in battle order, and to which the wounded were brought]."
- Flemish parallel: Philip of Alsace "excelled with his hand-picked stoutly armed foot-soldiers, who were equipped with a waggon with a banner on it, in other words a kind of carroccio."

### Additional Bouvines / Coalition Context

- Hugues de Boves "had recruited mercenaries before the battle of Bouvines and had fled precipitately there."
- After defeat: "the emperor Otto was advised to continue the struggle with the communal army of Ghent."
- Otto IV context: "Otto IV lacked the means to recruit such an army" (of mercenaries) as his rivals.

### Cadoc Career (for context to Bouvines presence)

- Cadoc "remained permanently in the royal service and became as famous as Mercadier." Defended Gaillon (300 foot) 1196; first to penetrate Château-Gaillard (6-month siege 1204); took Angers; burgrave of Gaillon, bailiff of Pont-Audemer; 1210 Auvergne; 1213 Flanders invasion; imprisoned 1219 for misdeeds (released 1227); reappeared with Louis VIII at Avignon but "had no mercenary band with him."

### Verbruggen's Broader Interpretation (Bouvines as Exemplum)

- Professional mercenaries (Brabancons, from regions like Brabant, Flanders, Hainault not directly involved in Anglo-French or imperial-Lombard conflicts) "played a prominent part, but did not fully replace knightly armies, and they never became the more important arm."
- "They fought mostly on foot." (Bouvines Brabancons; Henry II's 1173; archbishop of Cologne's rotten.)
- Excellence of such troops "an example to any foot-soldiers of the time."
