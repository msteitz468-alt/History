# India: The Ancient Past — Avari (2007)

Burjor Avari's undergraduate survey covers Indian history from early pre-history (c. 7000 BCE) through the beginning of Turco-Afghan incursions into north India (c. CE 1200). The book's critical thrust is politico-economic, socio-cultural, and intellectual development rather than purely dynastic narrative. It engages recent revisionist scholarship while remaining accessible. The treatment of the Aryan question as migration (not invasion), the four-era framework, and the synthesis of Vedic/Buddhist/Jain intellectual history are the key interpretive contributions.

## Volume Synthesis Note

Avari's *India: The Ancient Past* fills a significant gap in the wiki: a chronological political-cultural spine for the Indian subcontinent from Neolithic origins through the eve of the Delhi Sultanate, synthesising recent revisionist scholarship in accessible undergraduate form. Its three most important interpretive contributions: (1) the **Aryan migration** position (not invasion) now treated as consensus — slow, peaceful infiltration from Afghanistan c. 1700–1400 BCE, not conquest; (2) the **paradox of Mauryan imperialism** — Ashoka's Dhamma as a political-religious programme distinct from Buddhism proper, which may have humanised but also weakened the empire's coercive capacity; and (3) the **feudal era** framing of 500–1200 CE — the samanta land-grant system as the mechanism of political decentralisation, with active debate over whether "feudalism" applies and whether it caused or accompanied commercial decline.

Cross-volume tensions with prior ingests: the Kushan-era material in [[sources/benjamin-empires-ancient-eurasia-2018|Benjamin (2018)]] provides deeper treatment of the Kushan-Gupta interface than Avari, who is stronger on internal Indian dynamics. The South/Southeast Asia material from [[sources/gap3-south-asia-progress|prior Gap 3 ingests]] (Thapar, CHSEA) is largely complementary, with Avari adding the **Tripartite Struggle** framing for 9th–10th century north India and the clearest treatment of the **disappearance of Buddhism from India** as a gradual bhakti-absorption process rather than simple Turkish destruction.
