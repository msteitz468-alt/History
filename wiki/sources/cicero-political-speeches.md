# Cicero, *Political Speeches*

Eight of the published speeches of **[[cicero|Cicero]]**, "the greatest orator of the ancient world,"
spanning his career from the prosecution that made his name to the invective that cost him his life.
With his letters, the chief contemporary source for the **fall of the Roman Republic**. The eighth
source of this Phase-4 batch. For the author see **[[cicero|Cicero]]**; the philosophical companion is
**[[cicero-republic-and-laws|*The Republic and The Laws*]]**.

## The Selection

- ***In Verrem* I and II.5 (Against Verres, 70 BCE)** — the prosecution of Gaius Verres for his
  rapacious governorship of Sicily. The case that established Cicero as Rome's leading advocate (over
  the aristocrat Hortensius) and a classic exposure of **provincial misgovernment** and senatorial-court
  corruption; II.5 is the famous catalogue of Verres's cruelties (the crucifixion of the Roman citizen
  Gavius — *"civis Romanus sum"*).
- ***In Catilinam* I–IV (Against Catiline, 63 BCE)** — the four orations of Cicero's consulship
  denouncing **[[catilinarian-conspiracy-63bce|Catiline's conspiracy]]**, from the thunderous opening
  *"Quo usque tandem abutere, Catilina, patientia nostra?"* to the Senate debate on the conspirators'
  execution. Cicero's own, triumphant version of the events Sallust narrates.
- ***Pro Marcello* (For Marcellus, 46 BCE)** — a speech of thanks to the dictator **Julius Caesar** for
  pardoning a Pompeian, delicately probing the question of one-man rule and the restoration of the
  Republic.
- ***Philippic II* (44 BCE)** — the most ferocious invective in Latin, a written pamphlet (never
  delivered) destroying the character of **Mark Antony** after Caesar's assassination. The Philippics led
  directly to Cicero's proscription and **murder in 43**.

## Cicero the Orator

Berry's introduction sets Cicero against the **competitive, collapsing Republic** in which "the power
of persuading others through speech became as important as it has ever been." A *novus homo* from
Arpinum who rose by ability rather than birth, Cicero excelled in both **forensic** (court) and
**deliberative** (assembly) oratory, published his speeches "for posterity to admire and imitate," and
became the single most influential prose stylist in the Western tradition — the model of Latin
eloquence from Quintilian through the Renaissance humanists to modern rhetoric.

## Significance

These speeches are at once **literary masterpieces**, the **template of Western rhetorical education**,
and a **primary source** for the institutions and crises of the late Republic — the courts and
provincial administration (Verres), the politics of 63 and the *senatus consultum ultimum* (Catiline),
and the death-struggle of Republic and Caesarianism (Marcellus, Philippics). They must always be read
as **advocacy**, against Sallust, Cicero's own letters, and Plutarch.
