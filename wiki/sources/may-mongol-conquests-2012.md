# Timothy May, *The Mongol Conquests in World History* (2012)

A short, argument-driven world history of the **[[mongol-empire|Mongol Empire]]**,
opening Phase E (the Mongols + India depth). Where the wiki's existing Mongol coverage
came from the Cambridge volumes ([[kedar-cwh-v5-2015|CWH V]], [[abulafia-ncmh-v5-2000|NCMH V]])
as one civilization among many, May's book is dedicated to a single claim: that the
Mongol conquests were **the** integrating event of Eurasian — and ultimately world —
history.

## Argument

May frames the empire in two movements. **Part One ("The Mongol Conquests as
Catalyst")** narrates the rise of [[genghis-khan|Chinggis Khan]], the expansion, the
**dissolution into the four khanates** (Yuan, [[ilkhanate|Ilkhanate]],
[[chaghatayid-khanate|Chaghatayid Khanate]], [[golden-horde|Jochid Khanate/Golden Horde]]),
and "the world of 1350" — a Eurasia whose political map had been irrevocably redrawn,
with more than twenty states erased. **Part Two ("The Chinggis Exchange")** is the
heart of the book: the systematic, often unintended **transfers** that the empire set
in motion — the Pax Mongolica and its trade; new forms of warfare
(gunpowder's westward diffusion); a portable administrative repertoire (the *yam* postal
relay, the *ortagh* merchant partnerships, the census and tax system); the spread of
world religions and the khanates' conversions; the **[[black-death|Black Death]]**; mass
migrations and Turkicization; and the cross-pollination of art, food, and technology
(blue-and-white porcelain, the printing press, *nasij* gold brocade, noodles).

The pivotal concept is the **[[chinggis-exchange|Chinggis Exchange]]** — May's coinage
on the model of the **[[columbian-exchange-1492|Columbian Exchange]]** — which he treats
as the *precursor and enabler* of the Columbian one ("without the Mongol Empire would
Columbus have sailed?"). He is careful to credit the Mongols as *facilitators* rather
than sole agents, and devotes his introduction's "theoretical concerns" to mocking the
"Mongol mania" that would trace everything from the Bolshevik Revolution to disco back
to Chinggis Khan.

## Reliability

Secondary, recent (2012), and authored by a specialist working from the integrative
("Allsen school") historiography. It gives **Cambridge volumes presumptive weight**
where they overlap; no contradictions arose. May's tone is informal (digressions on
Olivia Newton-John's *Xanadu* and Chinggis Khan vodka), but the scholarship is sound
and the source criticism (the catalogue of Chinese, Persian, Arabic, Mongolian, and
Latin sources in the introduction) is a useful primary-source map in itself.
