# The Oxford History of Ancient Egypt

Ian Shaw (ed.), *The Oxford History of Ancient Egypt* (Oxford: Oxford University
Press, 2000; paperback 2002, new edition 2003). A multi-author narrative history
running from the Lower Palaeolithic occupation of the Nile valley (c.700,000 BC) to
the Christianization of Roman Egypt (AD 395), with each major period written by a
specialist. It is the natural narrative complement to
[[sources/kemp-ancient-egypt-anatomy-2018|Kemp's thematic *Anatomy*]]: where Kemp
asks *what held Egypt together*, this volume narrates *what happened*, dynasty by
dynasty, and is unusually strong on the intermediate periods of fragmentation.

## The Volume's Argument

Shaw's framing chapter sets the interpretive key for the whole book: Egyptian
"history" as conventionally written is a **political artefact of Manetho's dynastic
scheme**, and that scheme is increasingly hard to reconcile with the social and
material changes archaeology reveals. The contributors accordingly treat the
canonical division into kingdoms and intermediate periods as a useful but
provisional scaffold, repeatedly showing that the "intermediate periods" were not
simply dark ages of collapse but episodes of regional vitality, and that the
boundaries between dynasties were drawn by later record-keepers as much as by
events on the ground. The cumulative thesis is that Egyptian civilization was both
remarkably continuous in its forms and far more dynamic, contested, and
externally entangled than the monumental record alone suggests.
