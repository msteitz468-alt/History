# Graffiti in Antiquity

Peter Keegan, *Graffiti in Antiquity* (London/New York: Routledge, 2014).
Senior Lecturer in Roman History, Macquarie University; author of *Gender,
Social Identity, and Cultural Practices in Private Latin Inscriptions*.

## Overview

A thematic study of informal inscriptions — cut, painted, inked or charcoal-traced
texts and images — across the ancient Mediterranean, from pharaonic Egypt (c. 3100
BCE) to late antiquity (c. 600 CE), drawing chiefly on Egyptian, Greek and Roman
material. Keegan's central argument is that graffiti, precisely because they lie
"free of the everyday social restraints" governing polished literary and monumental
inscription, open a layer of lived experience — the voices of ordinary free and
enslaved men and women — unavailable through canonical sources. He reads each
graffito as a social and cognitive "script" (a sequence of perception, evaluation
and response), asking what it *does* socially and psychologically rather than merely
what it says.

The book is organized in four thematic parts framing eleven chapters, bracketed by
an introduction on modern approaches and a conclusion.

## Volume Synthesis Note

Keegan's contribution to the wiki is not a new narrative of events but a new
*class of evidence* and a method for reading it. Where the collection's
Greco-Roman coverage rests overwhelmingly on literary and monumental sources —
elite, formal, and self-conscious — *Graffiti in Antiquity* systematically
mobilizes the informal epigraphic record (scratched, painted, inked marks) as
testimony from and about ordinary people, free and enslaved, across 3,000 years.
Its analytical move is to read each graffito as a social "script" embedded in a
material context, rather than as an isolated curiosity, and to treat the volume
and diversity of the record as an argument about the breadth of ancient
functional literacy.

The book slots cleanly beside existing pages rather than contradicting them. It
enriches [[roman-literacy]] (the functional/scribal distinction), reframes
[[history-of-sexuality]] (the active/passive "principle of superiority" as read
off erotic graffiti), gives [[ostracism]] and [[athens]] their material ballot
evidence, and supplies the graffiti dimension of well-covered sites
([[pompeii]], [[deir-el-medina]], [[dura-europos]], [[naucratis]],
[[pithecusae]]). Its one genuinely new structural contribution is the
[[defixiones]] concept — curse tablets as a cross-regional magical practice with
a clear typology (erotic, legal, competitive, justice), a topic the wiki had not
previously isolated.

A methodological tension worth flagging for future ingests: Keegan's cognitive
"script" framework and his confident readings of fragmentary, often undated
texts are his own interpretive stance. The wiki records his factual claims
(corpus statistics, named graffiti, sites, dates) as attributed to him and keeps
the interpretive frame identified as his, consistent with the collection's
survival-bias and attribution caveats noted on [[ancient-graffiti]].
