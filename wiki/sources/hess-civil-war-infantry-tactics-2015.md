# Civil War Infantry Tactics: Training, Combat, and Small-Unit Effectiveness (Earl J. Hess, 2015)

**Source page for Earl J. Hess's detailed study of infantry formations, maneuvers, training, and actual battlefield practice during the American Civil War.** Hess argues that the linear tactical system was not rendered obsolete by the rifle musket. Combat occurred overwhelmingly at short ranges comparable to the smoothbore era; Union and Confederate units demonstrated high proficiency in executing complex drill under fire; lines were preferred for combat while columns served movement and support roles within multiple-line formations. The work supplies hundreds of concrete examples from the Official Records and is essential for any analysis of Civil War battle mechanics.

**Hess-CWI-R1-01: LinearTacticsDefinitionAndPurpose**

**Grounding quote:** "Civil War officers relied on a system of linear tactics to form and maneuver their units on the battlefield. Developed in Europe during the late 1600s and early 1700s, this system reached its height in limited wars fought by professional armies of the eighteenth century. The system involved shoulder-to-shoulder lines, columns, and a range of complicated maneuvers to take a unit from one formation to another. The idea was to wed tactics to the capabilities of the single-shot, smoothbore weapon that all major European armies used by the early 1700s. It had a range of about one hundred yards..."

**Locator:** "Preface"

**Analysis:** Hess defines the core linear system as a method of massing men for control of both fire and movement with short-range single-shot weapons, reaching peak unitary-army form by the mid-1700s. This directly frames why the system was inherited by Civil War armies and why its relevance hinges on actual combat ranges rather than weapon potential alone. The account revises views that treated linear tactics as inherently obsolete by tying their design explicitly to smoothbore limitations that persisted in practice.

**Hess-CWI-R1-02: RifleMusketPredictionsVsReality**

**Grounding quote:** "The biggest difference between the Civil War and previous conflicts was the introduction of the rifle musket. The only technical change, however, was the rifle’s effective range, five hundred yards compared to the one-hundred-yard range of the smoothbore. This increased range led contemporaries to predict revolutionary changes in warfare—higher casualties, greater power added to the side fighting on the defensive, and cavalry and field artillery becoming ineffective when confronting infantry armed with rifles."

**Locator:** "Preface"

**Analysis:** The text isolates the rifle musket's sole change as extended range while documenting contemporary predictions of transformed warfare that later historians adopted wholesale. Hess sets up the central revision by contrasting predicted effects with the reality that effective ranges remained close to smoothbore distances. This grounds the argument that tactics required no fundamental overhaul.

**Hess-CWI-R1-04: PriorRifleMusketStudyFindings**

**Grounding quote:** "My study of the issue in The Rifle Musket in Civil War Combat—Reality and Myth (2008) thoroughly criticizes the old interpretation and offers a new one—that the rifle musket did not revolutionize Civil War military operations. Its only advantage over the smoothbore was longer range, but the great majority of soldiers did not use it at that distance. The rifle’s parabolic trajectory made hitting targets at long range a technical problem that even rifle enthusiasts of the day admitted was difficult to solve."

**Locator:** "Preface"

**Analysis:** Hess summarizes his 2008 work as rejecting revolutionary claims by showing that range advantages were rarely exploited due to trajectory, visibility, terrain, and lack of training. Combat evidence instead confirms short-range fighting (often 100 yards or less) remained decisive, matching smoothbore-era patterns. This foundational revision frees the current study to treat linear tactics as still appropriate rather than obsolete.

**Grounding quote:** "This book is a study of infantry tactics in the Civil War, crafted within the context of the new interpretation of the rifle musket and its effect on military operations. **Hess-CWI-R1-07: LinearTacticsDevelopmentFromTerciosToFlintlock**

**Grounding quote:** "The matchlock was in the hands of about two-thirds of all infantrymen in European armies by 1600, a time when the usual tactical formation was the Spanish tercio. This huge formation was many ranks deep and quite broad as well, more akin to the old Greek phalanx than to modern battle lines. ... Maurice of Nassau experimented with smaller formations that were wider than they were deep to allow more of his musket men to fire during the campaigns of the Dutch Revolt (1566–1609). This was the precursor to the development of linear formations."

**Locator:** "Ch. 1 The European Tactical Heritage"

**Analysis:** Hess traces linear tactics' emergence from unwieldy deep tercios and matchlocks (high misfire rates, loose formations for safety) through reductions to three ranks with flintlocks and cadenced marching. The shift enabled tighter control and massed fire suited to short-range weapons. This long-term evolution shows linear tactics as a pragmatic response to weapon and command realities that persisted into the nineteenth century.

**Hess-CWI-R1-08: FrederickTheGreatInnovations**

**Grounding quote:** "The older parallel or “processional” method of deploying from marching column to battle line called for the entire column to turn right or left and move with its flank exposed to the enemy. ... The “perpendicular” method, perfected by the Prussians under Frederick, allowed individual battalions in the column to deploy either right or left from the column itself. It was faster (when employing well-trained units), did not expose any flanks, and provided the army with maneuvers that could be used to avoid obstacles on the battlefield after they deployed into line."

**Locator:** "Ch. 1 The European Tactical Heritage"

**Analysis:** Frederick's perpendicular deployment, echelon attacks, oblique order, and cadenced marching increased speed, articulation, and control over large formations compared to earlier processional methods. These refinements addressed the need for greater flexibility while preserving the core linear emphasis on disciplined mass. The text presents them as key steps toward the more articulated system later used in North America.

**Hess-CWI-R1-09: FlexibleLinearSystemInFrenchRevolutionaryAndNapoleonicWars**

**Grounding quote:** "The more flexible linear system that replaced the unitary army concept came to full fruition in this period, some historians calling it the impulse system. This allowed a good deal of latitude to battalion, brigade, division, and corps commanders to determine how to achieve their part of a larger plan for each battle. ... There developed a greater emphasis on skirmishing and on organizing corps to operate as semi-independent units of a larger field army."

**Locator:** "Ch. 1 The European Tactical Heritage"

**Analysis:** Hess shows how enlarged armies prompted adoption of divisions, corps, mixed line-and-column formations, and extensive skirmishing, replacing rigid unitary control with subordinate initiative within a framework. The 1791 Ordinance and Napoleonic practice institutionalized these changes while retaining shoulder-to-shoulder lines as the basic combat formation. Civil War armies directly inherited this modified, more flexible version of the linear system.

**Hess-CWI-R1-10: SteubenBlueBookNorthAmericanAdaptation**

**Grounding quote:** "Steuben’s manual, which became known as the “Blue Book” after the Revolution, drew material from several sources. ... A page-by-page examination of the Blue Book shows it to be the most watered-down version of European linear tactics ever published, yet Steuben managed to convey to his untutored charges the essence of that system. It was a triumph of concise and clear exposition geared to the level of his audience."

**Locator:** "Ch. 2 The North American Tactical Heritage"

**Analysis:** Steuben simplified complex European drills (British 1764 base plus French/Prussian elements) for inexperienced American citizen-soldiers while preserving core column-to-line transitions, wheeling, and two-rank lines. The result transmitted the essential linear system to North America despite militia limitations. This adaptation explains the direct continuity of European-derived tactics into the early national period and Civil War.

**Hess-CWI-R1-11: HardeeManualAndRifleMusketLimitations**

**Grounding quote:** "Lt. Col. William J. Hardee authored a new book, Rifle and Light Infantry Tactics, which was adopted in 1855 to replace Scott’s manual, now two decades old. It was hardly a revolutionary book, for Hardee essentially changed only one small aspect of American tactics by increasing the rate of the double quick to 165 steps per minute... The Americans were not as enamored of the new weapon as were the French... Lt. Cadmus M. Wilcox... warned that to truly exploit the increased range of the weapon, all soldiers had to be carefully trained to gauge distance and operate the sighting mechanism. The Minié ball traced a curved trajectory..."

**Locator:** "Ch. 2 The North American Tactical Heritage"

**Analysis:** Hardee's 1855 manual made only incremental speed adjustments rather than systemic changes to accommodate the rifle musket. Wilcox explicitly highlighted the curved trajectory's practical limits, noting that the effective 100-yard killing zone remained similar to the smoothbore and that longer-range sighting created dangerous gaps. This underscores Hess's broader point that weapon enthusiasts overstated revolutionary potential while actual tactical continuity with prior linear practice persisted.

**Hess-CWI-R2-04: CaseyManualImprovementsAdoption**

**Grounding quote:** "Casey did a far better job than Hardee in explaining complicated maneuvers, inserting suggestions on how and when to use particular movements and defining many technical terms. **Hess-CWI-R2-07: MarchingCadencesWheeling**

**Grounding quote:** "Scott indicated that common-time marching consisted of taking a step of twenty-eight inches (measured from heel to heel) 90 times per minute. Quick time involved taking that twenty-eight-inch step 110 times per minute. ... Hardee agreed on the length of the step and the speed of common and quick time, but he indicated that double quick (which Scott did not address) involved a step of thirty-three inches at the rate of 160 steps per minute. ... 'No infantry movement is more difficult than the wheel,' commented Wilbur F. Hinman of the 65th Ohio after the war. **Analysis:** Standardized cadences and the difficulty of wheeling (fixed pivot or on the move) illustrate the premium placed on collective precision to enable controlled movement across terrain. Hardee's addition of double-quick time increased tactical flexibility within the linear framework. Long practice was required because misalignment could destroy unit effectiveness.

**Hess-CWI-R2-08: DoubleColumnsSquares**

**Grounding quote:** "The manuals also allowed for the use of a double column, a formation that tended to be the most common column actually employed on Civil War battlefields. A regimental column doubled on the center consisted of two stacks of company battle lines, one next to the other. ... 'The formation of the square being often necessary in war,' wrote Casey, 'and being the most complicated of the manoeuvres, it will be as frequently repeated as the supposed necessity may require.' Despite this opinion, forming squares was seldom practiced by volunteer regiments in the Civil War and rarely used on the battlefield. ... Another reason was that cavalry rarely attacked infantry, at least on major Civil War battlefields."

**Locator:** "Ch. 3 Tactical Manuals and the Management of Men, COLUMNS AND SQUARES"

**Analysis:** Double columns offered a practical compromise between depth for movement and width for deployment, explaining their prevalence over simple or division columns. Squares, though detailed in the manuals, saw minimal use because of their complexity and the rarity of cavalry threats. The text shows selective adoption of formations based on battlefield realities.

This range from Hess's Chapters 5–6 provides granular battlefield evidence that forward movement under linear tactics was far more difficult and improvisational than drill manuals suggested, yet the system proved robust rather than fragile. Hess's core conclusion is that the linear system adapted to American conditions through training, experience, and on-the-spot judgment; obstacles and rifle fire challenged but did not invalidate it.

**Hess-CWI-R6-01:** After active service, William Hopkins Morris published *Field Tactics for Infantry* (1864) proposing shortened commands, greater emphasis on flank movement to negotiate wooded terrain, and simplified maneuvers so new troops could master the subject in half the time; [[actors/grant-ulysses-s]] read it carefully but rejected adoption in October 1865, concluding that Casey’s manual, “tho’ perhaps defective, ha[d] been sufficient during the maintenance of a large army.”

**Hess-CWI-R6-02:** Lewis Wallace advocated conducting basic maneuvers by a unit as small as six soldiers rather than companies, fighting in one rank (with a system to shift to two or three), and providing a “complete and perfect system” for skirmishing; a board found “little to recommend” in the deviations from the authorized system, and [[actors/grant-ulysses-s]] endorsed its rejection in April 1866.

**Hess-CWI-R6-03:** Emory Upton developed a system using a four-man maneuver unit for all movements (including wheeling and flanking), flexible single- or double-rank formations, and reduced commands; after board review it was adopted in August 1867 as *A New System of Infantry Tactics Double and Single Rank, Adapted to American Topography and Improved Fire-Arms*, with [[actors/grant-ulysses-s]] stating he was “fully satisfied of its superior merits and adaptability to our service.”

**Hess-CWI-R6-04:** Upton’s 1867 manual retained the linear system largely intact, including regimental organization into ten companies, placement by height, and multiple lines, while dispensing with rear-rank maneuvering, inversion, and the countermarch and substituting rapid conversions of front; it was still fundamentally linear, as Upton himself admitted, and later historians’ assumptions of revolutionary modernization have been overstated.

**Hess-CWI-R6-05:** Reports of devastating long-range fire at Plevna in the Russo-Turkish War prompted Upton (completed posthumously) to revise toward loose-order skirmishing as the primary emphasis and to delete the main battle line altogether, with two companies per battalion deploying in loose order and the remainder as reserve; neither this nor Morris’s parallel revisions were adopted.

**Hess-CWI-R6-06:** The 1891 *Infantry Drill Regulations* (Leavenworth manuals) formalized a three-line formation (skirmish line, support, reserve), allowed commanders to choose loose or close order, made the squad (two groups of four) the unit of maneuver, dispensed with the shoulder-to-shoulder battle line, and required skirmishers to advance in short rushes while seeking cover; it continued Upton’s fours concept but went further toward dispersal.

**Hess-CWI-R6-07:** In World War I the British and Germans developed infiltration tactics with the platoon or squad as the key independent tactical unit, creeping barrages, heavily armed small combined-arms groups, and marked decentralization of command down to squad leaders; the AEF lagged, initially employing denser formations, and never fully developed true infiltration tactics despite some experimentation.

**Hess-CWI-R6-08:** Norman Hitchman’s research for the U.S. Army confirmed that infantry combat remained short-range: nearly all hits on Bougainville were under 75 yards, mean range in Korea was a little over 100 yards, and 80–90 percent of surveyed soldiers reported at least 95 percent of their fire under 300 yards; casualty rates per men engaged per unit of time remained roughly constant from ancient sword combat to the twentieth century, validating Civil War officers’ insistence on waiting for close range.

**Hess-CWI-R6-09:** The Civil War was the only conflict in which the nation relied so heavily on state volunteers (more than 2.7 million versus 67,000 regulars); these citizen-soldiers achieved impressive proficiency through a sound linear tactical system, intensive training, and repeated combat experience, rising gradually rather than through a single pivot event, in contrast to the generally poor performance of state volunteers in the Mexican War.

**Hess-CWI-R6-10:** The linear system and 1791 French manual (basis for Scott, Hardee, and Casey) used by [[events/american-civil-war-1861-1865]] volunteer armies closely paralleled the experience of the French Revolutionary Armée du Nord: citizen volunteers drawn from all society, rigorous continuous training regimens, multiple lines separated by about two hundred yards, combination of lines and columns (though Americans used columns of waiting and maneuver more than the full ordre mixte), and effectiveness achieved through training plus battlefield experience.

**Hess-CWI-R6-11:** In the tactical summary, officers much preferred lines over columns for engaging the enemy because lines allowed maximum firepower and were in most ways easier to maintain; columns played a large role for holding units ready or maneuvering before contact, while successive (multiple) lines spaced two hundred or three hundred yards apart appeared on almost every battlefield.

**Hess-CWI-R6-12:** Civil War units showed a strong tendency to advance within close range, halt, and engage in prolonged firefights rather than press home the assault (with prominent exceptions such as Hood at Gaines’s Mill and Pickett at Gettysburg); frontal attacks were the easiest and most common method, moving by the flank was the most common battlefield maneuver for its speed and flexibility through vegetation or obstacles, and solid consistent performance in formations and maneuvers was evident only at the regimental and brigade levels.

Range 6 Summary  
This range extracts Hess’s postwar evolution of American tactics (Morris/Wallace rejections, Upton’s 1867 adoption and its limits, 1891 dispersal reforms), comparisons of Civil War citizen-soldier achievement and linear heritage to earlier U.S. volunteer forces, the 1790s French Revolutionary armies, and 18th-century British experience, plus the culminating tactical synthesis: preference for lines with successive-line support, dominance of frontal movement and flank maneuvers at small-unit level, persistent tendency toward close-range firefights over assaults, the unmatched effectiveness of mid-war skirmishing, and the fundamental adequacy of the inherited linear system for the rifle musket—contrasted with higher-command articulation limits at corps level that inhibited decisive exploitation. Short-range combat realities and continuity across eras are emphasized throughout.

## Volume Synthesis Note

Hess provides the most granular, evidence-rich account of how the linear tactical system actually functioned on Civil War battlefields. By grounding analysis in the drill manuals (Scott 1835, Hardee 1855, Casey 1862) and more than four hundred after-action reports, he demonstrates continuity with the flexible linear system of the French Revolutionary/Napoleonic era rather than a rifle-musket-driven rupture. Combat almost always occurred at short ranges (majority of decisive fire inside 100–150 yards), lines were strongly preferred for engagement (maximum firepower + easier maintenance), columns served movement/waiting/support within multiple-line deployments, skirmishing reached its historical peak in Western armies, and experienced regiments routinely executed complex “multiple maneuvers” in single fights—proof of high small-unit articulation. Higher-level (division/corps) articulation remained uneven, with the Army of the Potomac achieving the greatest late-war proficiency; this gap, not obsolescence of linear tactics, limited decisive exploitation.

The book is the essential companion for interpreting the tactical mechanics described in the American Civil War battle analyses in the warfare hub (Antietam, Gettysburg, Shiloh, Chancellorsville, Cold Harbor, Five Forks, etc.). It supplies concrete data on formation preferences, maneuver frequency, skirmish employment, and the gap between theoretical capability and battlefield execution. Cross-links heavily with Napoleonic-era tactics sources (Muir, Lynn Giant, Duffy Military Experience) and places the Civil War firmly inside the longer Western continuum of short-range linear combat rather than an American aberration.

Key historiographical intervention: rejects the “Attack and Die” / rifle-musket revolution thesis (McWhiney & Jamieson) and places Civil War infantry combat within a longer continuum of short-range, line-based fighting that persisted into the 20th century.
