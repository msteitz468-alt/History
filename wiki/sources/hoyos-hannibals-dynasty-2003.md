# Hannibal's Dynasty: Power and Politics in the Western Mediterranean, 247–183 BC

Dexter (B. D.) Hoyos, *Hannibal's Dynasty: Power and Politics in the Western
Mediterranean, 247–183 BC* (London/New York: Routledge, 2003). ISBN 0-415-29911-X.

## Overview

A political and strategic study of the **Barcid family** — [[hamilcar-barca|Hamilcar
Barca]], his son-in-law [[hasdrubal-the-fair|Hasdrubal the Fair]], and his sons
[[hannibal|Hannibal]], [[hasdrubal-barca|Hasdrubal]] and [[mago-barca|Mago]] — as the
effective rulers of [[carthage|Carthage]] across two generations (247–183 BC). Hoyos's
governing thesis is that the Barcids constituted a **republican ruling dynasty**: three
successive leaders who were both Carthage's de facto heads of government *and* her
elected commanders-in-chief, sustaining decades of dominance through a network of
kinsmen, friends and supporters, and — uniquely in Punic history — exercising that
dominance from hundreds of miles away, first in Spain and then in Italy.

The book reframes the familiar military narrative of the [[first-punic-war-264-241bce|First]]
and [[second-punic-war-218-201bce|Second Punic Wars]] as the story of one family's rise,
supremacy, collapse and brief revival. Hoyos argues that Barcid power was set up through
military success, exploited the charisma of victory and conquest and the wealth flowing
from it, and rested at home on the support of citizens as a whole as well as allied or
opportunistic fellow-aristocrats — a form of personal ascendancy within a republic that
"in some ways prefigured the supremacy established two centuries later by Julius Caesar
and Augustus over Rome."

## Argument and Distinctive Contributions

- **The Barcids as a dynasty, not three generals.** The family's dominance was
  institutionally continuous: each leader was *elected* to command by citizens and army
  alike, not a military dictator, and each relied on kinship and marriage alliances (in
  Africa with Numidian princes, in Spain with local lordly families) to bind the empire.
- **Empire built by treaty as much as by conquest.** Against the conventional split
  (Hamilcar the fighter, Hasdrubal the negotiator), Hoyos stresses that both extended
  Punic Spain through alliances, conciliation and diplomacy; Hasdrubal was even *chosen
  supreme leader* by the Spanish peoples — a Hellenistic-style device for building
  loyalty.
- **Carthaginian empire served the state, not just the family.** The new cities (capped
  by [[hasdrubal-the-fair|Hasdrubal's]] New Carthage) and the silver wealth benefited the
  Punic state generally; the Barcids could have set up a breakaway Spanish kingdom and
  did not.
- **Revisionist readings** (flagged on the relevant pages): the causation of the Second
  Punic War and the Saguntum affair; the degree to which Carthage's home government
  actually "abandoned" Hannibal in Italy; and Barcid *miscalculation and overconfidence*,
  not merely Roman perseverance, as a cause of the reversal after 216.
- **The Hellenistic parallel and the un-Hellenistic republic.** Barcid personal
  leadership fitted the third-century Hellenistic pattern of charismatic war-leaders, yet
  Carthage remained a republic with a home-grown civilization, never a monarchy.

## Historiography and Sources (per Hoyos's ch. XVIII)

Hoyos is explicit that Barcid history must be reconstructed by "careful detective work."
Almost nothing of Carthaginian record survives — a few quotations and references in later
authors. The narrative depends on Greek and Roman writers, most of them fragmentary or
late: **Polybius** (earliest surviving, consciously striving for objectivity but not
invariably impartial), **Livy**, **Diodorus**, **Cassius Dio** (via Byzantine epitomes),
**Cornelius Nepos**'s brief lives of Hamilcar and Hannibal, **Plutarch** (Fabius,
Marcellus), **Appian**'s regional war narratives, and the epitomators **Eutropius**,
**Justin** and **Orosius**. Nearly all carry a pro-Roman tint — inherited from their
sources or from a teleology of Roman triumph — and were written to illuminate Roman
rather than Punic history, so Punic government, society and economy surface only
incidentally. Evidence is thinnest for Hasdrubal the Fair and for the empire-building
decades in Spain; richest, though Roman-filtered, for Hannibal's war.
