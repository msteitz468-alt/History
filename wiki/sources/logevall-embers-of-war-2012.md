# Logevall, *Embers of War* (2012)

The definitive international history of how France lost Indochina and the United States stepped into its place (1940–1959). Logevall argues that the French defeat at Dien Bien Phu and the Geneva partition were not inevitable; multiple contingent choices by Paris, Washington, London, Beijing, Moscow, and the Viet Minh shaped the outcome. The book traces the "embers" from Japanese occupation and the 1945 August Revolution through the Franco-Viet Minh War to the fateful U.S. decision to build an anti-communist state in southern Vietnam under Ngo Dinh Diem. Strong emphasis on diplomacy, alliance politics, and the limits of military solutions in a nationalist war.

- **Haiphong trigger (Nov 1946):** French patrol seizes Chinese junk with gasoline; shots; 240 Vietnamese + 7 French dead. Valluy orders “take complete control of Haiphong and force the Vietnamese government and army into submission.” Nov 23 bombardment: prolonged naval/aerial attack reduces Vietnamese/Chinese quarters to rubble, civilians strafed; “thousands” dead (exact figure never firmly established). French control Haiphong + Lang Son by Nov 28. Quote: “almost the entire Vietnamese part of the city, had been destroyed.”
- **Battle of Hanoi outbreak (Dec 19, 1946):** Attack on French installations after power cut; Sainteny injured. Fighting spreads across Tonkin, Annam, Cochin China. Viet Minh plan: house-to-house defense to cover evacuation of government and main army to mountains (Viet Bac). French expected quick victory; took two months for Viet Minh withdrawal. Author: “if it’s true that the Vietnamese fired the first shots on December 19, ultimately France bears primary responsibility”; d’Argenlieu (“Bloody Monk”) “seemed determined to provoke” full war.
- **French military overreach and stalemate 1947–48:** Valluy launches Operation Léa (Oct 1947, 17 battalions + paratroops) to capture Ho/Giap at Bac Kan and cut China routes. Paratroop drop loses surprise; Ho/Giap escape by minutes; pincer bogged by ambushes, blown bridges, “piano-key” ditches. Operation Ceinture clears territory but Giap avoids decisive battle; French withdraw to lowlands. Viet Minh reoccupy immediately. By end 1948 Giap’s chu luc regulars ~30k early, total forces ~250k; controls >half population/territory.
- **Viet Minh strategy and organization:** Truong Chinh *The Resistance Will Win* (Feb 1947) + DRV proclamation elaborate Maoist three-phase (withdrawal/equilibrium/general offensive); emphasis on French public opinion + international powers. Village committees, literacy, taxes, porters; terror used selectively against unpopular notables. Intelligence superiority, terrain mastery (monsoon, mountains), population support (women/children/elderly contribute). “The soldier is the inhabitant, and the inhabitant is the soldier.”
- **Ho Chi Minh leadership 1946–49:** Rejects Paul Mus terms May 1947 (“In the French Union there is no place for cowards. If I accepted these conditions I would be one.”); Mus privately concludes French “lost the battle that counted most: the battle for the support of the local population.” Assures US visitor Moffat (Dec 1946) independence first priority, offers Cam Ranh Bay base. Late 1947 reshuffles cabinet (replaces Communist ministers including Giap as defense minister) to appear moderate. Issues Dec 20 1946 national resistance appeal. Diplomatic isolation persists (PCF, Soviets, US unhelpful).
- **Bao Dai solution evolution:** French court ex-emperor (abdicated 1945, briefly supreme counselor to Ho) as anti-Viet Minh figurehead. Bao Dai demands full independence then accepts hedged Baie d’Along protocol (Dec 1947) and Élysée Accords (March 8, 1949): “autonomy” within French Union but France retains control of army, foreign relations, defense. DRV issues treason warrant; Vietnamese see “sham.” French use “Bao Dai solution” to reframe war as anti-Communism and secure US support.
- **US policy shift:** 1946–47: sympathy for nationalists but no role; views French presence “imperative” against Soviet/Chinese influence. Acheson (1949+): “whether Ho Chi Minh was as much a ‘nationalist as a Commie is irrelevant. All Stalinists in colonial areas are nationalists… outright Commie.” Commitment deepens after Soviet bomb + Mao victory (1949); “Having put our hand to the plow, we would not look back.”
- **Key personalities (French side):** High Commissioner d’Argenlieu (“Bloody Monk”) pushes decisive action, thwarts negotiations. Valluy exploits Haiphong and launches 1947 offensives. Bollaert (replaces d’Argenlieu) pursues Bao Dai talks. Paul Mus mission detailed. Pignon advocates Bao Dai to split nationalists and attract US.

## Historiography Note (on source page)

Logevall writes against both French colonial nostalgia and American "quagmire" inevitability narratives. He restores British, Chinese, and Soviet agency and highlights moments when different choices (U.S. non-intervention at Dien Bien Phu, different Geneva terms, earlier negotiations) were available. Strengths: multi-archival, balanced on all major actors, excellent on alliance friction. Limitations for wiki: less granular on DRV internal politics or Viet Minh social base than Goscha/Duiker; relies on published Vietnamese sources more than archives for the "other side." Complements the more Vietnam-centred recent works.

## Volume Synthesis Note

Logevall’s central contribution is the demonstration that the French defeat and the American inheritance were not foreordained. Multiple turning points (1945–46 negotiations, 1950 Chinese intervention and U.S. recognition, 1951 delta battles, 1953–54 Navarre Plan and U.S. non-intervention at Dien Bien Phu, Geneva partition) were shaped by contingent decisions in Paris, Washington, London, Beijing, Moscow, and the Viet Minh leadership. The book is strongest on the diplomatic and alliance dimensions and on the French domestic and international constraints. It is deliberately less detailed on post-1954 RVN politics and the Second Indochina War (covered by later sources in the collection). Its contingency argument is a necessary corrective to both teleological nationalist histories and later American “quagmire” narratives that downplay the 1940–1954 phase. All claims in this source page are grounded exclusively in the ranges cited; cross-references to Goscha, Duiker, and military sources are required for a complete picture.
