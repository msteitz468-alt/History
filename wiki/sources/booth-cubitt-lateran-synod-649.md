# The Acts of the Lateran Synod of 649

The record of the western synod convened by **Pope Martin I** in **649** to condemn **Monotheletism** —
a decisive moment in the last great Christological controversy of antiquity and in the widening breach
between **Rome** and the **emperor**. The eleventh source of this Phase-5 batch.

## Background: the Monothelete Controversy

After the failure of earlier formulas to heal the [[council-of-chalcedon-451|Chalcedonian]] schism, the
emperor **[[heraclius|Heraclius]]** and Patriarch Sergius promoted a new compromise to win back the
Miaphysites: Christ has **one operation** (*monenergism*), then **one will** (*monotheletism*) — the
*Ecthesis* of 638, reinforced by the *Typos* of **Constans II** (648), which forbade further debate.
Dyothelete opponents — who insisted that Christ, being fully human, must have a **human will** as well as
a divine — resisted, led by the monk **[[maximus-the-confessor|Maximus the Confessor]]**.

## The Synod

In **649** **Pope Martin I** held a synod at the **Lateran** in Rome, its theology shaped by Maximus and
his Greek-speaking circle. The synod **condemned Monotheletism** and its imperial documents (the
*Ecthesis* and *Typos*) and defined the orthodox **dyothelete** doctrine of **two wills and two
operations** in Christ. As the editors show, the proceedings were substantially **prepared in advance**,
a carefully constructed dossier presenting a predetermined result as conciliar debate.

## Aftermath

The synod was an act of open defiance of imperial religious authority. **Constans II** responded with
force: **Pope Martin I** was arrested, tried at Constantinople, and died in exile in the Crimea (655);
**Maximus the Confessor** was tried, his tongue and right hand reportedly cut off, and he died in exile
(662). Both were later venerated as martyrs. The dyothelete cause they died for was finally vindicated at
the **Third Council of Constantinople (the Sixth Ecumenical Council, 680–681)**, which affirmed two wills
in Christ.

## Significance

The Lateran Synod is a landmark of the **assertion of papal doctrinal authority against the emperor**,
the climax of the **Monothelete** crisis, and a key witness to the theology of Maximus the Confessor —
one of the most important thinkers of the Greek Church. The editors' demonstration that its "acts" were
in good part a **literary construction** is also a significant case-study in how conciliar records were
made.
