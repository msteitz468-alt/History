# Blood and Mistletoe: The History of the Druids in Britain

Ronald Hutton (Professor of History, University of Bristol), *Blood and Mistletoe: The History of the Druids in Britain* (New Haven & London: Yale University Press, 2009; pbk 2011). The major published outcome of a 2000–2007 research project; the heavyweight successor to his shorter, thematic *The Druids* (2007).

## Scope and Argument

The book is **not** about the ancient Iron Age Druids but about the **British relationship with the idea of the Druid** from roughly 1500 to the present — a reception history, or "history of ideas," tracing how successive generations of Britons (Scots, English, Welsh) imagined, claimed, deployed, and reinvented Druids for their own cultural, religious, national, and political purposes. Hutton confines the study to the three major peoples of the island of Britain, though Druid-enthusiasm also spread to the Irish, Germans, French, and the English-speaking New World.

Hutton draws four organizing conclusions:

1. **Interest in Druids is intrinsic to modernity.** The ancient world barely noticed them (all surviving Greek and Roman references fit in about a dozen printed pages); the Middle Ages ignored them outside Ireland. The preoccupation boomed across north-western Europe precisely as the region took on Renaissance features around 1500, when emergent nations sought distinguished ancestors and Druids were "almost the only impressive figures" in their remote past.
2. **The ancient sources are few, contradictory, and unreliable** — presenting Druids as variously bloodthirsty barbarians, learned philosophers, or both — so there was never a single authoritative portrait. Later ages could therefore choose among competing images according to taste, making Druids a perpetually **contested** subject ("Thinking with Druids").
3. **Interest peaked in a "Druidic century" (and a bit), c. 1740–1860**, driven by the new British superstate's need for a shared pre-national past; their displacement followed the triumph of the [[concepts/three-age-system|three-age system]] and evolutionary models of prehistory.
4. **The relationship differed sharply among the British peoples**: the Scots took Druids up first and tied them to megaliths; the English Anglicized and celebrated them under the Hanoverian state, then turned increasingly hostile; the Welsh, latest to embrace them, made Druids central to a reinvented national identity ([[actors/morganwg-iolo|Iolo Morganwg]] and the Gorsedd).

Three individuals are argued to have been pivotal catalysts: **[[actors/stukeley-william|William Stukeley]]** (an Englishman, who persuaded the English that Druids were ancestors worth embracing and the builders of Stonehenge), **[[actors/morganwg-iolo|Edward Williams / Iolo Morganwg]]** (a Welshman, who placed Druids at the centre of Welsh identity), and **[[actors/reid-george-watson|George Watson MacGregor Reid]]** (a Scot, who made "Druid" a vehicle for English cultural radicalism). Each was, in some measure, a romancer, crank, or charlatan — a recurring observation that disreputability and charisma drove the story forward.

## Volume Synthesis Note

*Blood and Mistletoe* is a reception history, and its central methodological lesson reshapes how the wiki should treat the Druids: the figure that dominated British imaginations for over three centuries is overwhelmingly a **modern construction**, not a recovered antiquity. The book therefore sits in deliberate tension with the wiki's existing [[concepts/druids|Druids and Celtic Religion]] page (built from Cunliffe and Iron Age archaeology): where that page asks what the ancient Druids *were*, Hutton asks what later Britons *needed them to be*, and shows that the gap between the two is unbridgeable because the ancient sources are too few and too contradictory to constrain the imagination. The new [[controversies/ancient-druids-source-reliability|source-reliability controversy]] page formalises that tension.

The volume's deepest argument is that interest in Druids is intrinsic to **modernity** and to **cultural nationalism**: it booms with the Renaissance, peaks in a "Druidic century" (c. 1740–1860) when the new British superstate needed a shared pre-national past, and fades as professional, evolutionary prehistory (the [[concepts/three-age-system|three-age system]]) supplies a different account of human origins. This connects the page network to [[concepts/antiquarianism|antiquarianism]], [[concepts/nationalism-and-archaeology|nationalism and archaeology]], and the historiography of [[places/stonehenge|Stonehenge]] and [[places/avebury|Avebury]] — monuments whose meaning was repeatedly rewritten to suit the needs of each generation.

Hutton structures the whole through three charismatic, semi-disreputable catalysts — an Englishman ([[actors/stukeley-william|Stukeley]]), a Welshman ([[actors/morganwg-iolo|Iolo Morganwg]]), and a Scot ([[actors/reid-george-watson|Reid]]) — and a recurrent national divergence (Scots first adopt then drop the Druids; the English appropriate then demonise them; the Welsh, latest of all, make them the core of a living national institution via the [[actors/gorsedd-of-the-bards|Gorsedd]]). A striking sub-theme, traced across [[actors/toland-john|Toland]], [[actors/blake-william|Blake]], and the [[actors/universal-bond|Universal Bond]], is how modern Druid orders manufactured spurious genealogies for themselves — the Toland-1717 foundation legend being a splice of the 1717 Masonic Grand Lodge and Iolo's 1792 Gorsedd — illustrating Hutton's larger point that the history of Druidry is, repeatedly, the history of creative forgery proving more culturally durable than honest scholarship.

No direct contradiction with previously ingested volumes arises (the wiki had no prior reception-history of British antiquarianism); rather, the volume supplies a new analytical layer connecting the Iron Age [[actors/celts|Celtic]] material to the modern construction of British and Welsh identity.
