# Military Effectiveness, Volume 2: The Interwar Period (Millett & Murray, eds., new ed. 2010)

This is Volume 2 of the three-volume comparative study examining why some military forces succeed while others fail. The project analyzes the military institutions of seven major powers (United States, Great Britain, France, Germany, Italy, Russia/Soviet Union, Japan) across WWI (Vol. 1), the interwar period (this volume), and WWII (Vol. 3). The common analytical framework evaluates performance at political, strategic, operational, and tactical levels.

Volume 2 covers the interwar years (roughly 1918/1919 to 1939/1941), focusing on how the powers prepared (or failed to prepare) for the next war, innovation, doctrine development, force structure, and adaptation to technological and political changes after the trauma of WWI.

## Framework (repeated from Vol. 1, applied here)
The 4-level framework:
- Political: securing resources, civil-military relations, goal setting.
- Strategic: war aims, resource allocation, coordination with politics.
- Operational: campaign planning, linking tactics to strategy.
- Tactical: battlefield employment, doctrine, training.

Excellence does not automatically translate across levels. The interwar period highlights peacetime challenges: uncertainty about future war, technological change, cultural/institutional barriers to innovation.

## New Edition Introduction
The editors reflect on the study's relevance 20+ years later. Key points:
- Military institutions still struggle with innovation under uncertainty.
- Adaptation in war is often too slow and costly.
- Cultural factors are crucial.
- Lessons from Iraq 2003 (failure to recognize insurgency, lack of long-term strategy) echo WWI/Vietnam mistakes.
- The volumes provide a baseline for understanding military performance.

## Consolidated Volume Synthesis (Vol. 2 Interwar)
This volume applies the 4-level framework (political/strategic/operational/tactical) established in Vol. 1 across the interwar preparation of seven powers. Key cross-cutting findings from all ranges:

- **Non-transfer across levels is the rule**: Soviet deep-battle theory and early mechanized experiments (range1) remained largely theoretical or were reversed (disbanded corps, return to mass/attrition post-Finland); French methodical battle and total-war mobilization consensus (range1/2) produced impressive paper strength but collapsed under German operational tempo; British committee machinery strong at strategic analysis but weak at exploiting tank/air tech or timely rearmament (range2); Japanese tactical/shipbuilding excellence (carriers, Long Lance, zaibatsu integration) undermined by army strategic overreach post-1931 (range2/3); Italian resource allocation captured by army and diverted to colonial adventures, with interservice aviation rivalry nullifying Mediterranean geography (range3/4); German tactical/operational modernization ran ahead of political/strategic judgment and economic sustainability (range4/5).

- **Political level is decisive and often defective**: Civil-military relations varied (French military influence high but doctrine rigid; Italian Mussolini partial control created fissures without unity; German army progressively lost advisory role to Hitler; US isolationism + budget politics starved army while navy lobbied better). No power achieved seamless political-strategic integration under uncertainty.

- **Peacetime innovation and adaptation challenges**: Budget constraints, WWI trauma (British "muddling through", French methodical emphasis), cultural/institutional inertia (regimental parochialism, perfectionism in US R&D), and mismatched visions of future war (German short-campaign vs attrition) repeatedly prevented timely exploitation of new technologies or doctrines. The review (Coox) emphasizes rearmament becoming self-driving.

- **Cross to Vol. 1 and Vol. 3**: Interwar choices directly preconditioned WWII outcomes (e.g., French 1940 collapse roots in interwar doctrine/budget priorities; Japanese China overcommitment from 1931; German strategic myopia despite operational brilliance foreshadowed in Vol. 1 German chapter). Framework remains the analytical spine.

- **Historiography value**: Landmark comparative institutional history; new edition intro stresses enduring relevance to innovation under uncertainty and adaptation costs (echoing Iraq 2003 reflections also in Vol. 1). Strong on non-operational factors; complements Murray/Millett *Military Innovation in the Interwar Period* (already ingested).
