# Mencius (*Mengzi*)

The second of the Confucian classics, recording the teachings of **[[mencius|Mencius (Mengzi)]]**, the
"Second Sage" — the great defence of the **innate goodness of human nature** and of **benevolent
government**. The second source of the Phase-6 East Asian Classics; translated by Irene Bloom, edited by
P. J. Ivanhoe.

## The Work

Seven books of **dialogues and debates**, fuller and more argumentative than the
[[confucius-analects-watson|Analects]]. Many are conversations with the rulers of the
**[[warring-states-period-475-bce|Warring States]]** — **King Hui of Liang**, **King Xuan of Qi** —
urging humane rule; others are sharp exchanges with rival philosophers, above all **Gaozi**, over the
nature of the human heart.

## Key Arguments

- **The goodness of human nature** — the book's central thesis: all people possess the **"four sprouts"**
  of virtue (compassion, shame, deference, the sense of right and wrong), which mature into *ren*, *yi*,
  *li* and *zhi*. The thought-experiment of the **child at the well** shows that moral feeling is innate;
  evil results from neglect and bad environment, not from nature.
- **Benevolent government (*ren zheng*)** — kingship is won by **caring for the people**, not by force;
  "the people are the most precious… the ruler counts for least."
- **The conditional Mandate** — a tyrant who loses the people **forfeits the [[mandate-of-heaven|Mandate
  of Heaven]]** and may be deposed: a foundational statement of the **right of rebellion**.
- The cultivation of the **"flood-like *qi*"** through accumulated righteousness.

## Significance

The *Mencius* gave Confucianism its enduring **optimistic anthropology** and its most radical **political
ethic** — the welfare and ultimate sovereignty of the people. As one of the Song **Four Books** it stood
at the centre of Chinese education and Neo-Confucian thought for centuries, and remains the classic
counter-statement to Xunzi's "nature is bad" and to **[[han-feizi-watson|Legalism]]**.
