# The Travels of Ibn Battuta in the Near East, Asia and Africa 1325–1354 (trans. Samuel Lee, 1829)

**Abu Abd Allah Muhammad ibn Battuta** (1304–1377), a Maliki jurist of Berber origin from Tangier, dictated one of the most important travel accounts of the pre-modern world. Between 1325 and 1354 he traversed the equivalent of roughly fifty modern countries, performing the hajj multiple times, serving as qadi in the Delhi Sultanate and the Maldives, and reaching Yuan China. The text we have is an abridgment of the *Rihla* ("journey") prepared by the Granadan scholar **Muhammad ibn Juzayy** (who also added literary framing and some material) at the request of the Marinid sultan Abu Inan.

This 1829 translation by the Rev. Samuel Lee is based on the abridged Arabic manuscripts in Cambridge. It is the version present in the collection and provides the primary eyewitness testimony for large stretches of the 14th-century *dar al-Islam* and its maritime and steppe connections.

## Itinerary Summary (from the Lee text)

- Chapters I–V: Departure from Tangier (725 AH / 1325 CE); North Africa (Tlemcen, Algiers, Tunis, Tripoli); Alexandria and Cairo; failed Upper Egypt route to the Red Sea; entry into Syria; Jerusalem, coastal Levant, and Damascus.
- Chapters VI–VIII: Pilgrimage caravan to the Hijaz and Medina; Iraq (Basra, Kufa, Karbala, Baghdad); Persia (Shiraz and environs); second hajj; return via Mosul.
- Chapter IX: Yemen (Zabid, Aden); East African coast (Zaila, Mogadishu, Mombasa, Kilwa); return via Oman and the Gulf.
- Chapter X: Hormuz; return across Arabia to Egypt and Syria; entry into Anatolia.
- Chapter XI: Anatolia (Burdur to Kastamonu); the Akhi brotherhoods (futuwwa organizations); meeting local rulers.
- Chapters XII–XIII: Black Sea voyage to Crimea (Kaffa); the Golden Horde under Özbeg Khan (Mohammed Uzbek); camp life at Bish Tag; permission for a voyage toward Constantinople; return via the steppe, Transoxiana (Bukhara, Samarkand), Afghanistan, and the Hindu Kush into India.
- Chapters XIV–XVI: Sind and Multan; arrival in Delhi; appointment as qadi; detailed portrait of Sultan **Muhammad bin Tughluq**; court ceremonial, the capital transfer to Dawlatabad, and the sultan's severity.
- Chapters XVII–XVIII: Failed embassy to Yuan China; travels through central India (Gwalior, Chanderi, Daulatabad); Malabar coast (Hinaur, Calicut/Kālikūt, etc.); shipwreck of the embassy fleet; local politics and a coastal campaign.
- Chapters XIX–XX: The Maldives (as chief qadi under the queen); marriage and local customs; Ceylon (Adam's Peak pilgrimage).
- Chapters XXI–XXII: Return to the Coromandel and Bengal coasts; Sumatra (under al-Malik al-Zahir); Java; legendary Tawalisi.
- Chapter XXIII: China (arrival at Zaytun/Quanzhou; paper money, porcelain manufacture, urban life, mosques for Muslims, the great river, Khansa/Hangzhou); audience arrangements.
- Chapters XXIV–XXV: Return voyage via Sumatra; India ports; Arabian coast; second Persian tour; Syria and Egypt; final return to Fez (754 AH / 1353–54); side journey to Andalusia (Gibraltar, Granada); the West African crossing to Mali (Taghaza, Walata, the Mali court under Mansa Sulayman, Timbuktu, Gao, Takadda).

## Historiography Note

The *Rihla* stands as the pre-eminent Muslim travel narrative of the medieval period, comparable in scope (if not in literary polish) to Marco Polo. Its value lies in the breadth of direct observation at a moment of unusual connectivity. Limitations are well-known: composition from memory after return; Ibn Juzayy's editorial hand (some passages parallel earlier writers such as Ibn Jubayr); the author's Maliki-jurist perspective that judges frontier societies by heartland standards (especially visible in gender and ritual commentary in Africa and India); occasional credulity toward marvels. Lee himself affirmed the core reliability of the geographical and historical notices while noting the abridged state of the surviving Arabic text. ### Volume Synthesis Note (main-thread)

Ibn Battuta’s *Rihla* (as preserved in the Lee abridgment) is the single richest primary eyewitness document for the 14th-century connected Islamic world and the tail end of the polycentric Afro-Eurasian system. It supplies granular texture—court ceremony, urban infrastructure, trade mechanics (cowries, pepper, pearls, porcelain, paper money), religious practice, gender norms, and traveler hospitality networks—across North Africa, the Levant, the Indian Ocean, the Delhi Sultanate, the Maldives, Yuan China, the Golden Horde, and the Sahel that no other source in the collection matches for breadth or immediacy. 

Strengths: direct observation of functioning institutions (Akhi cells, Malabar rest-house system, Chinese traveler security, Maldives cowrie export economy, Mamluk and Tughluq courts); confirmation of long-distance mobility of ulema and merchants; vivid portraits of rulers (Tughluq’s contradictory brilliance and cruelty; Uzbek’s mobile court; Zaytun’s Muslim quarter). 

Limitations (explicit in text and Lee’s notes): memory-based composition years later; Ibn Juzayy’s literary shaping and occasional borrowings; the author’s heartland Maliki-patriarchal lens that repeatedly registers shock at frontier customs (unveiled women, dust-prostration, nudity before marriage, matriliny); abridgment losses; occasional marvels (Rokh, shape-shifting Jogees) reported without strong skepticism. The 1829 Lee translation itself is a further abridgment of the epitome.

For the wiki this source dramatically enriches the transregional picture of Late Middle Ages connectivity (linking [[afro-eurasian-world-system]], [[indian-ocean-trade]], [[late-middle-ages]]), supplies primary grounding for the Delhi Sultanate peak/decline under Muhammad bin Tughluq, the Maldives, and Chinese ports beyond secondary syntheses, and humanizes the dar al-Islam’s internal diversity. It complements (and sometimes corrects or nuances) the Cambridge histories, Thapar, Abu-Lughod, and the prior Africa selections. Cross-volume tensions (e.g., scale of Zaytun vs. later Ming retraction; Tughluq as rational experimenter vs. tyrant) are preserved rather than adjudicated.
