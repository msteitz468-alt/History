# Custer's Trials: A Life on the Frontier of a New America (Stiles, 2015)

**Source page for T. J. Stiles, *Custer's Trials: A Life on the Frontier of a New America* (Alfred A. Knopf, 2015).**

A major revisionist biography that reframes George Armstrong Custer (1839–1876) not primarily as the doomed Indian fighter of myth, but as a man whose life embodied the transition from the personal, heroic, individualistic America of the antebellum and Civil War era to the corporate, bureaucratic, national-market order of the Gilded Age. Stiles argues Custer "lived on a chronological frontier even more than a geographical one." His celebrity, contradictions, court-martials, political intrigues, authorship, and spectacular death at the Little Bighorn all illustrate a personality and career out of step with the emerging modern United States.

The book deliberately decenters the Little Bighorn as the sole lens, giving roughly equal weight to Custer's Civil War rise (boy general, Michigan Brigade, Gettysburg cavalry action, Shenandoah, Appomattox) and his postwar "fall" (Texas occupation near-mutiny and court-martial, Kentucky, New York political dabbling, 7th Cavalry command, Washita, Black Hills expedition, Belknap scandal testimony, final campaign). Custer is presented as a bundle of roles—protégé, prodigy, husband, general, hero, executioner, politician, financier, writer, enemy, accuser—none of which fully contained him.

## Methodological Notes

- Heavy reliance on Custer family papers (GAC and EBC correspondence at LBH and Beinecke/Yale), official military records, court-martial transcripts (1861 and 1867), the 1879 Reno Court of Inquiry records.
- Contemporary journalism (New York Herald, Chicago Tribune, etc.) and Custer's own articles (Galaxy, Turf, Field and Farm).
- Cross-checked against Libbie Custer's published memoirs while noting her myth-making role.
- Contextualizes via broader Gilded Age histories (Reconstruction, Grant scandals, corporate rise, Indian policy shifts).
- Not a campaign tactical study; battle mechanics of Little Bighorn are summarized with emphasis on command decisions, disobedience debates, and the Reno inquiry politics rather than exhaustive terrain/order-of-battle reconstruction.

## Key Contributions

- The strongest single-volume account of Custer's formation (Ohio/Michigan upbringing, West Point struggles and near-expulsion, early Civil War staff work under McClellan/Pleasonton).
- Detailed treatment of his Civil War celebrity and the "boy general" persona (Michigan Brigade at Gettysburg vs. Stuart, Falling Waters, Shenandoah under Sheridan).
- Fresh analysis of the two court-martials (1861 West Point-related; 1867 Texas for harsh discipline and unauthorized absence) and their meaning for Custer's character and the postwar army.
- Illuminates Custer's postwar political and financial activities (Democratic flirtations, railroad speculation, Black Hills gold promotion, Belknap impeachment testimony) as attempts to navigate the new order.
- Treats the Little Bighorn as the climax of a life already in tension with modernity, not its sole explanation; critiques both heroic "Last Stand" myth and simple "arrogant fool" reduction.
- Strong portraits of Libbie Custer's agency and the marriage as both partnership and performance; also of the army's institutional culture under Sheridan and Sherman.

## Range 05 Claims

**Extracted from exclusive cache range_05_9501-11500.txt (postwar restoration, return to 7th Cavalry, early frontier service, transition to Indian fighter years). All claims grounded exclusively in verbatim quotes + locators from this range. Prefix: Stiles-Custer-R05-**

- **Stiles-Custer-R05-001**: The 1867 court-martial charges against Custer centered on absence without leave and conduct prejudicial to good order. *Charge:* “Absence without leave from his command.” *Specification:* “That he, Brevet Major General G.A. Custer, Lieut. Col. 7th U.S. Cavalry, did at or near Fort Wallace, Kansas, on or about the 15th day of July 1867, absent himself from his command without proper authority, and proceed to Fort Harker, Kansas, a distance of about 275 miles, this at a time when his command was expected to be actively engaged against hostile Indians.” Additional specifications included marching without rest harming horses, seizing ambulances/mules for private use, and neglecting to pursue Indians or recover/ bury bodies after an ambush at Downer’s Station. An additional charge by Capt. Robert M. West alleged orders to shoot deserters without trial. (Stiles, Custer's Trials, notes [4][5]; court transcript context ~Sept 17 1867)

- **Stiles-Custer-R05-002**: Custer retained counsel Capt. Charles C. Parsons and devoted himself to the case. “At his court-martial in 1861, he had admitted his guilt. Now he acted like a man on trial for his life.” The court president was Col. William Hoffman (who had served on Custer’s 1861 court-martial); another member was Col. Benjamin Grierson. Custer successfully objected to Lt. Col. John W. Davidson as both witness and judge, who had prejudged: “he did not see ‘how General Custer expected to get out of these charges.’” (Stiles, Custer's Trials, [3][4])

- **Stiles-Custer-R05-003**: 7th Cavalry regimental organization in 1866-1867: twelve companies, each with no more than 100 enlisted men (usually far fewer). By start of 1867 the regiment counted fifteen officers and 963 troopers. “Each company rated a captain, a first lieutenant, and a second lieutenant, nearly always reduced to two or even one [officer] by details to detached service,” per historian Robert Utley (quoted). Field grades: colonel, lieutenant colonel (Custer), three majors. Peacetime regiments had extraordinarily stable officer rosters year after year. (Stiles, Custer's Trials, [18])

- **Stiles-Custer-R05-004**: Key officers of the 7th Cavalry at Fort Riley included Maj. Alfred Gibbs (West Point 1846, served with Custer in Army of the Potomac), Capt. Albert Barnitz (Civil War veteran of Custer’s 3rd Division), Lt. Thomas B. Weir (subordinate in Texas), Maj. Joel Elliott, Maj. Wyckliffe Cooper, Capt. Louis Hamilton (only twenty-two), Capt. Robert M. West, Capt. Frederick W. Benteen, Irish-born Capt. Myles Keogh, and Lt. William W. Cooke (just twenty). “Many of them were quite young... fully 42 percent of commissions in that period were issued within two years of Appomattox.” Benteen immediately resented Custer. (Stiles, Custer's Trials, [19][20][21])

- **Stiles-Custer-R05-005**: Custer assumed command of the 7th Cavalry on or about 26 February 1867 at Fort Riley while Col. Andrew Jackson Smith (West Point 1838) retained senior rank but delegated heavily, including to Custer; Smith later took Lt. Weir as adjutant when assuming District of the Upper Arkansas command. “On the day she [Jennie Barnitz] wrote this letter, Custer assumed command of the 7th Cavalry.” (Stiles, Custer's Trials, [22][23])

- **Stiles-Custer-R05-006**: Fort Riley (arrived mid-October 1866) was “not a *fort*, tho’ called so... For there are no walls enclosing it.” It was “a little city” of limestone buildings: three two-story barracks facing three across a large square parade ground, matched officers’ quarters (two attached houses each), stables, sutler store, express/post offices, chapel, etc. The Custer household settled into a permanent home “with a large porch, a room in the back for Brown, and a sizable kitchen.” Supplies from nearby Junction City. (Stiles, Custer's Trials, [17])

- **Stiles-Custer-R05-007**: Garrison life at Fort Riley included constant social calls. Jennie Barnitz wrote 26 Feb 1867: “We were at once invited to Gen. Custer’s. ... Mrs. Custer is a charming woman, and very gay. We were constantly receiving calls from officers, some times a half dozen at a time. They are splendid—I should think all of them from the best class of society.” Custer’s theatrical and display tendencies noted; he was “impossible ... to appear otherwise than himself.” (Stiles, Custer's Trials, [22][7])

- **Stiles-Custer-R05-008**: Libbie Custer’s memoir *Tenting on the Plains* described selection of companion Anna Darrah for Kansas posting: she and Armstrong “devoted ‘most of our attention…to the selection of a pretty girl.’ ... ‘A pretty girl…it was held by both of us, would do more toward furnishing and beautifying our army quarters than any amount of speechless bric-á-brac.’” Libbie disguised her as “Diana” and portrayed her as a remorseless flirt. The account reflects discomfort with proximity of her attention-loving husband and the young friend who had confessed “jealousy & envy” at Libbie’s marriage. (Stiles, Custer's Trials, [9])

- **Stiles-Custer-R05-009**: Libbie’s letters and memoirs reveal marriage strains in 1866-67, including Armstrong’s letters from New York professing sexual need while tempted by city women, and later guilt: “You know that I promised never to give you *fresh* cause for regret by attentions paid to other girls.” He referred to at least two such. Libbie’s alienation forced self-examination; she accumulated evidence of trouble (e.g., via Darrah). After Custer’s unauthorized absence to reach her, she wrote of “one long, perfect day” in summer 1867: “It was mine, and…it is still mine, for time and for eternity.” (Stiles, Custer's Trials, [8][71][72][104])

- **Stiles-Custer-R05-010**: At Fort Riley after Hancock Expedition start, black 38th Infantry troops replaced cavalry, terrifying Libbie who assumed risk of rape in “lonely, unprotected homes.” She used terms “darkies,” “apes,” “monkey acrobats,” complained of “slack and careless” men and “disconnected minstrel show.” Eliza Brown (“Lord, Miss Libbie, they won’t tech you”) and her own reliance on Brown as “worth a corporal’s guard” and “defender, solacer, and general manager” contradicted the vulnerability pose. Brown profited socially from the black troops. (Stiles, Custer's Trials, [65][66][67][68][69])

- **Stiles-Custer-R05-011**: Hancock Expedition (marched 27 March 1867 from Fort Riley): 1,400 men (seven cos 37th Inf, Battery B 4th Art, eight cos 7th Cav), wagons, pontoon bridge, agents, interpreter Edmund Guerrier, Delaware scouts, Wild Bill Hickok. Custer commanded the cavalry under Smith under Hancock. Parley 12 April at Pawnee Fork: Hancock threatened war if not peaceful; Tall Bull responded on buffalo decline and firing incidents. Indians fled village night of 14 April; Custer’s cordon found it abandoned. (Stiles, Custer's Trials, [28][29][35][36][55][56])

- **Stiles-Custer-R05-012**: During pursuit of fleeing village (15 April 1867), Custer rode ahead, killed wife’s horse Custis Lee while chasing bison at full gallop (pistol discharged into horse’s brain); he was thrown but unhurt. He later killed a bison successfully. Column exhausted; “As soon as we halted every man threw himself on the ground & fell asleep.” Custer later discovered Lookout Station burned with three mutilated bodies. (Stiles, Custer's Trials, [60][61][62][63])

- **Stiles-Custer-R05-013**: On 7 July 1867, after mass desertions (34 men 6-8 July; 30-40 previous night), Custer ordered pursuit and shooting of deserters. Maj. Joel Elliott’s party overtook four; Pvt. Charles Johnson wounded (possibly while reaching for carbine); three wounded total hauled 18 miles in wagon without initial treatment per orders; Johnson died. Tom Custer testified his brother said: “‘I want you to get on your horse and go after those deserters and shoot them down.’” Tom affirmed the wounded resulted from the order. Dr. Coates testified Custer wanted “to make an impression on the men.” (Stiles, Custer's Trials, [89][90][91][101][102])

- **Stiles-Custer-R05-014**: Custer’s unauthorized ride from Fort Wallace to Fort Harker (and on to Riley) 16-19 July 1867 to reach Libbie: escort of 75 men + 3 officers under Capt. Hamilton; hard pace exhausted horses. At Harker ~2:30 a.m. 19 July; Col. Smith (groggy) initially assumed stage arrival and later ordered return by telegraph upon learning of escort/ambulance. Custer arrested upon return to Wallace area. Smith testified: “He had no orders or authority from me.” (Stiles, Custer's Trials, [92][93][94][95][100])

- **Stiles-Custer-R05-015**: Trial witnesses (Sept-Oct 1867, Fort Leavenworth): Capt. L. M. Hamilton described escort detail and pace to Harker/Hays; Lt. Cooke on ambulance ride 67 miles in 13 hrs; Capt. Weir on arrival and social interaction at Harker (“Give my respects to the ladies” from Smith); Capt. Cox delivered Smith’s dispatch ordering stay at Wallace; Capt. Carpenter outraged at neglect of Downer’s casualties (“Custar remained unconcerned—finished his dinner, and moved on”); Sgt. Connelly: detail sent back specifically for Custer’s personal mare when attacked. (Stiles, Custer's Trials, [93][94][95][97][98][99])

- **Stiles-Custer-R05-016**: Verdict and sentence (early Oct 1867, confirmed by Grant 25 Nov 1867): Guilty on AWOL charge; guilty on conduct charge (no criminality on wagons); guilty on West’s additional charge (no criminality on medical refusal). Sentence: “to be suspended from rank and command for one year, and forfeit his pay proper for the same time.” Grant publicly deemed the court’s decision “altogether too lenient.” Custer was “morally if not legally... guilty of murder” per Grant’s view (per Leavenworth Bulletin). (Stiles, Custer's Trials, [5][7])

- **Stiles-Custer-R05-017**: Custer published public defense (Sandusky Register, 26 Dec 1867) attacking court composition (“four of them were his inferior in rank, three had never commanded troops in the field”), Hancock, and West (“a confirmed inebriate, a victim of *delirium tremens*”). This led Judge Advocate General Holt to prepare new specifications for “conduct to the prejudice of good order and military discipline,” submitted to Grant for possible second court-martial. (Stiles, Custer's Trials, [14][15])

- **Stiles-Custer-R05-018**: Sheridan (new Dept of Missouri commander) lobbied Grant 15 April 1868 for clemency: “I feel a great sympathy for General G. A. Custer... He held high command during the Rebellion and had difficulty in adapting himself to his altered position. There was no one with me whom I more highly appreciated than General Custer. He never failed me...” Custer during suspension wrote articles as “Nomad” for *Turf, Field and Farm*, began crafting plainsman/writer identity, supported Democrats (Tammany speech 1868), lived quietly in Monroe after controversies. (Stiles, Custer's Trials, [20][12][13][27][28])

- **Stiles-Custer-R05-019**: Sheridan wired Custer 24 Sept 1868 at Monroe: “Gen Sherman and myself and nearly all of the officers of your regiment have asked for you and hope the application will be successful. Can you come at once. Eleven (11) companies of your regiment will move about the first of October against the hostile Indians...” Grant approved reinstatement. Custer arrived Fort Hays 4 Oct, took command of strike force after Sheridan relieved Sully (Nov 1868). (Stiles, Custer's Trials, [31][55])

- **Stiles-Custer-R05-020**: Upon return, Custer reorganized/prepared 7th Cavalry at Camp Sandy Forsyth (near Fort Dodge): mounted drills, target practice, elite sharpshooters (Lt. Cooke), signaling classes, fresh horses/mules/wagons, requested/received 550 .44 revolvers and winter gear. “Coloring the horses” (uniform color per company, 3 Nov 1868) was a command-and-control measure for massed combat (Custer: “bordering on the ornamental perhaps, although in itself useful”). He wore custom fringed buckskin suit, hunted daily with hounds. (Stiles, Custer's Trials, [47][49][50][51])

- **Stiles-Custer-R05-021**: Broader context of “trials” with the new order: Custer’s rapid Civil War rise left him ill-adapted to Regular Army peacetime bureaucracy, stable hierarchies, and limited advancement. Sheridan diagnosed “difficulty in adapting himself to his altered position.” The 1867 court-martial and suspension exemplified clash between heroic individualism (direct action for personal/family reasons, theatrical discipline) and institutional/professional norms amid Reconstruction political crises (Tenure of Office Act, Johnson impeachment, Sheridan-Hancock command swap). Custer’s writing, politics, and quest for redemption via Indian campaigning were attempts to navigate it. (Stiles, Custer's Trials, [7][16][17][19][20][69][70])

- **Stiles-Custer-R08-001**: Custer carried massive secret debt into the 1876 campaign. On February 10, 1876, he issued a promissory note (IOU) for $8,500 to Justh (disguised, dated six months ahead at 7% interest). Justh demanded a cosigner; Custer obtained endorsement from Ben Holladay (the steamship king with Black Hills interests). "Financially, even morally, Custer had brought disaster down upon himself over the past year. Things would get worse." (Stiles, Custer's Trials (2015), pre-Epilogue section on debts and congressional investigations; notes [65])

- **Stiles-Custer-R08-002**: Context of Grant administration scandals and congressional investigations (44th Congress, Democratic House) set the stage for Custer's testimony. Democrats used probes of War Department (Belknap sutler payoffs) as partisan weapon. Custer was called by Rep. Hiester Clymer's committee on March 29, 1876. He described post tradership changes at Fort Abraham Lincoln, price rises, confrontations with trader Robert Seip, meeting with Belknap, and Seip's admission of payoffs to "an intimate friend of the Secretary of War here in Washington." Much testimony was hearsay ("It was a matter of common report..."). He also implicated President Grant's brother Orvil in questionable Interior dealings. (Stiles, Custer's Trials (2015); notes [66]–[77])

- **Stiles-Custer-R08-003**: Custer's political maneuvering in Washington (March–April 1876) included writing letters on House stationery, dining with Clymer ("an exceedingly cultivated gentleman"), attending dinners with Democratic Senator Thomas F. Bayard and Confederate generals. He attacked Maj. Lewis Merrill (7th Cavalry) before the Military Affairs Committee on hearsay, feeding stories to the *New York Herald*. "The men who in Army circles had influence a few days ago now hide away." He cultivated Sherman as potential new patron. (Stiles, Custer's Trials (2015); notes [82]–[85])

- **Stiles-Custer-R08-004**: Grant relieved Custer of command of the 1876 expedition in retaliation for testimony and perceived betrayal. Sherman telegrammed Sheridan: "The President has just sent me instructions through the Secretary of War, to send someone else than General Custer in command..." Custer appealed desperately: "I respectfully but most earnestly request that while not allowed to go in command of the expedition I may be permitted to serve with my regiment in the field. I appeal to you as a soldier to spare me the humiliation..." Sheridan noted prior clemency in 1868 and hoped this would be the last. Sherman advised: "Advise Custer to be prudent, not to take along any newspaper men... confine his whole mind to his legitimate office..." Grant relented; Custer rejoined his regiment. (Stiles, Custer's Trials (2015); notes [88]–[92])

- **Stiles-Custer-R08-005**: Reno Court of Inquiry (January 13–February 1879, Palmer House, Chicago) was convened at Reno's request to counter Frederick Whittaker's public accusations blaming him for the Little Bighorn disaster. Court: Col. John H. King (presiding), Col. Wesley Merritt, Lt. Col. William B. Royall. Recorder: 1st Lt. Jesse M. Lee. Reno's counsel: Lyman D. Gilbert. The proceedings became the closest official investigation of Custer's fate. "There is a man by the name of Whittaker whom I understand has made certain accusations against Major Reno." (Stiles, Custer's Trials (2015), Epilogue; notes [1]–[3])

- **Stiles-Custer-R08-006**: Lt. George D. Wallace (acting engineer with Custer's column) testified on Reno's orders and actions: Custer gave Reno three companies (~110 men) and ordered "The Indians are about two miles and a half ahead, on the jump. Follow them as fast as you can and charge them wherever you find them and we will support you." Reno charged, dismounted in the river bottom, was outflanked, retreated in disorder ("There seemed to be no order at all. Every man was for himself" per later testimony). Wallace last saw Custer moving to the right at a slow trot; claimed no uneasiness about Custer's fate until Terry arrived. He approved Reno's decision to retreat to the hill. (Stiles, Custer's Trials (2015), Epilogue; notes [7]–[10])

- **Stiles-Custer-R08-007**: Interpreter Frederic F. Gerard (with Arikara scouts) provided key survivor account. Custer instructed scouts to follow even small trails to catch all camps. Gerard warned Reno of Indians advancing up the valley (not fleeing). In the timber: Bloody Knife killed in front of Reno; Charley Reynolds (Gerard's friend, who had presentiment of death) shot down crossing the river. Gerard hid until dark, hearing "continuous firing" across the river move downstream toward Custer's battlefield for ~2 hours. He testified Reno's men "could have held out against the whole number of Indians as long as their ammunition and provisions would have lasted—that is, resolute, determined men." (Stiles, Custer's Trials (2015), Epilogue; notes [11]–[15])

- **Stiles-Custer-R08-008**: Capt. Frederick Benteen's testimony at the inquiry revealed deep resentment of Custer and contradictory excuses for not reinforcing him. Orders: "Benteen. Come on. Big Village. Be Quick. Bring Packs." Benteen moved without urgency; claimed "valley hunting ad infinitum," that Martin said Indians "skedaddling," that he saw Reno's retreat and "thought the whole command was thrashed," and later that he believed Custer's command already dead. Stiles notes Benteen (who criticized Custer for allegedly abandoning Elliott at Washita) "never tried" to aid Custer despite clear trail and orders. Benteen later wrote he was "close mouthed" to avoid seeming self-promoting. (Stiles, Custer's Trials (2015), Epilogue; notes [17]–[20])

- **Stiles-Custer-R08-009**: The Court of Inquiry exonerated Reno after 26 days. Judge Advocate General William M. Dunn's review: "The object of Gen. Custer in detaching Maj. Reno is shown to have been to attack the Indians simultaneously on opposite sides of their encampment or village. Their number appears to have been far greater than Gen. Custer imagined, and very far in excess of the force under his command." Stiles concurs that superior Lakota/Cheyenne numbers and willingness to fight (galvanized by Black Hills invasion) were decisive; Custer's division of force was a grave but understandable error given expectations. (Stiles, Custer's Trials (2015), Epilogue; notes [20]–[23])

- **Stiles-Custer-R08-010**: Battlefield details from Terry's arrival (June 27, 1876) per inquiry witnesses: Custer's body found atop a hill with brother Tom, George Yates, Cooke and others, amid 39 shot horses used as barricades. Custer shot in chest and left temple; body nearly intact (scalp preserved), stripped naked, thigh cut per Lakota custom, penis stabbed with arrow. Tom Custer's body heavily mutilated. Forty-nine troops killed nearby; Boston Custer and Autie Reed ~100 yards away. (Stiles, Custer's Trials (2015), Epilogue; notes [16][17])

- **Stiles-Custer-R08-011**: Immediate public and press reaction split along partisan lines. Republicans (e.g. *Chicago Daily News*) blamed "DISOBEDIENCE OF ORDERS—A FATAL BLUNDER." Southern and Democratic papers praised Custer as hero victim of Grant policies. The "glory-obsessed, arrogant fool" narrative predominated. Emmanuel Custer protested: "They should not have said so... He fought to whip and not for praise. He was not reckless... They wrong my dead boy." (Stiles, Custer's Trials (2015), citing James E. Mueller; notes [23][24])

- **Stiles-Custer-R08-012**: Stiles' central historiographic synthesis on Custer's life and legacy: the popular narrative errs on battle performance. "The popular narrative contains some truth about every aspect of Custer’s life *except* his performance in battle—the one field in which he displayed consistent good judgment and self-possession. From the Civil War through his two battles on the Yellowstone, he proved decisive, not reckless; shrewd, not foolish. In every other regard, he danced along the emerging modern world, unable to adapt to it. ... Again and again he saved himself through his ability to fight. And yet, ironically, we now remember him as a bad commander." Custer embodied clash with modernity (finance, bureaucracy, equality, politics). (Stiles, Custer's Trials (2015), Epilogue)

- **Stiles-Custer-R08-013**: Stiles cites Robert Utley as providing the last word: “The simplest answer, usually overlooked, is that the army lost largely because the Indians won... To ascribe defeat entirely to military failings is to devalue Indian strength and leadership.” The huge village on the Little Bighorn (inspired by Sitting Bull and Crazy Horse) was temporary; Sheridan had feared dispersal. Post-victory, bands broke up; military pressure (Crook, Miles) led to surrenders. Crazy Horse killed 1877; Sitting Bull fled to Canada, returned 1881, killed 1890. (Stiles, Custer's Trials (2015), Epilogue; notes [24][25])

- **Stiles-Custer-R08-014**: Libbie Custer's immediate response involved compounded grief (Armstrong's violent death, brothers and nephew killed, officer blame including Grant) plus financial ruin. Estate ~$2,140 assets vs. $4,372+ liabilities (including secret $8,500+ Justh debt settled at 10 cents/dollar). Pension initially $30/month. She refused federal post, worked as secretary, then wrote memoirs. (Stiles, Custer's Trials (2015), Epilogue; notes [28][29])

- **Stiles-Custer-R08-015**: Libbie's long-term life and response: She published *"Boots and Saddles"* (1885) and *Tenting on the Plains* (1887, followed by *Following the Guidon* 1890). She cultivated idealized image of husband while building independent career as author/lecturer on frontier military life. Key episode: 1885–86 reunion with former Black servant Eliza Brown Davison (now wife of prominent Ohio attorney). Verbatim letters from Eliza ("I receive your kind and welcom card and my heart leaps for joy...") and joint visits to Fifth Avenue Hotel, Brooklyn Bridge, Statue of Liberty, elevated railroad; Eliza attended Buffalo Bill's show. Libbie described the outings with condescension yet "delight." Never remarried; died April 4, 1933, Park Avenue. (Stiles, Custer's Trials (2015), Epilogue; notes [26]–[32])

- **Stiles-Custer-R08-016**: Stiles' final framing of Custer's significance: "It’s fitting that she [Libbie] lived out her life in New York. When given a choice, Custer had always picked the footlights over a campfire, Broadway over the open plains... Young men might go West, but Manhattan forged the corporate, technological future. ... His sudden, offstage ending left him suspended forever between East and West, past and future, to be misremembered as needed by each new generation." Reinforces the book's overarching argument of Custer as "chronological frontier" figure. (Stiles, Custer's Trials (2015), Epilogue)
