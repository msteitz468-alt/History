# The New Cambridge Medieval History, Volume 4: c. 1024–c. 1198 (2004)

*The New Cambridge Medieval History, Volume 4: c. 1024–c. 1198*, edited by David Luscombe and Jonathan Riley-Smith. Cambridge University Press, 2004. Two parts.

This volume is the primary academic backbone for the **central Middle Ages** — the eleventh and twelfth centuries, c. 1024–c. 1198, which the editors describe as "a unique fulcrum in the development of the medieval world." Chronologically it picks up where [[reuter-ncmh-v3-1999|NCMH III]] (c. 900–c. 1024) closes — at the death of Basil II, with Byzantium at its territorial height — and runs to the eve of the Fourth Crusade and Innocent III, where [[abulafia-ncmh-v5-2000|NCMH V]] (c. 1198–c. 1300) begins. It is the reference volume for the institutions and events that define the **High Middle Ages**: the [[gregorian-reform]] and the [[investiture-contest]]; the [[first-crusade-1095|First]] and Second Crusades and the [[crusader-states|crusader states]]; the [[twelfth-century-renaissance]] and the emergence of the [[medieval-university|universities]]; the [[norman-conquest-of-england-1066|Norman Conquest of England (1066)]] and the Norman conquest of [[kingdom-of-sicily|Sicily]]; the rise of the north-Italian [[commune-movement|communes]]; the great phase of the [[reconquista|Reconquista]] after the fall of Toledo (1085); and the [[komnenian-restoration|Komnenian restoration]] of the Byzantine empire after [[battle-of-manzikert-1071|Manzikert (1071)]].

The editors' organising thesis is **expansion and consolidation**: the expansion of population, agriculture, trade, towns and the territorial frontiers of Latin society; the radical reform of the structure and institutions of the western church; fundamental changes in the relationship of Latin Christendom with Byzantium, Islam and the Jews; the appearance of durable new kingdoms and states on the periphery; and the development of crusading, knighthood, written law, vernacular literature, Romanesque and Gothic art, heresy and the scholastic movement. At the opening of the period most of western Europe was "distinctly backward" by comparison with the urban, commercial Islamic world of Spain, Tunisia, Sicily and Egypt and with Byzantium; by its close the balance had begun decisively to shift, and the aggressive, "developmental mentality" of north-west Europe — reclaiming land, founding towns, building castles, exporting knightly violence into Spain, Sicily, the Baltic and the Holy Land — had inverted the eleventh-century relationship.

The volume's most important structural feature is that **its two parts are complementary, not sequential**. Part 1 treats themes (economic, social, governmental, ecclesiastical, cultural); Part 2 treats government on a territorial basis, running through the regions twice — once for the eleventh century, once for the twelfth. The synthesis pass must reconnect the thematic and the narrative treatments of the same developments (above all the church-reform chapters of Part 1 with the papal–imperial narrative of Part 2).

**Note on cycle structure.** NCMH protocol treats each of the volume's parts as a section cycle, but here both parts are very large (19 and 23 chapters) and Part 2 deliberately doubles back chronologically. The thematic Part 1 is therefore split into a secular cycle (1), a church-reform cycle (2), a learning/crusading cycle (3) and a frontier/cultural cycle (4); the narrative Part 2 is split into the eleventh-century waves (5–7) and the twelfth-century waves (8–9), keeping Byzantium and the Islamic Near East together where the chapters themselves do.
