# The Cambridge History of the Cold War, Vol. I: Origins (2010)

The first of three volumes of the **Cambridge History of the Cold War** — the standard
reference, ingested to build out the wiki's [[cold-war|Cold War]] structure (the period
page flagged that "discrete event pages for the Korean and Vietnam wars, the Cuban Missile
Crisis, and 1989 are still to be written"). Volume I supplies the **origins and the bloc
formation, 1945–1962**.

## Argument

The Cold War emerged from the geopolitical, ideological, economic, and social wreckage of
the two world wars. Volume I treats it as both a **superpower confrontation** (the US and
USSR each pursuing security and a vision of modernity) and a **global** phenomenon reaching
into Europe, East Asia, the Middle East, and the decolonizing world. It balances the major
historiographical schools — traditionalist (Soviet expansion as cause), revisionist (US
economic expansionism), and post-revisionist (a security dilemma between two powers neither
of which sought war) — without adjudicating dogmatically.

**US national security policy (McMahon):**
- "US grand strategy during the presidencies of Dwight D. Eisenhower and John F. Kennedy derived, in the broadest sense, from the same, deep-seated fear: that the Soviet Union’s combination of implacable hostility, mounting military strength, and positive ideological appeal posed a fundamental – even existential – threat to US national security. The emergence of an equally hostile China as a military power in its own right deepened the perception they shared of an unusually menacing external environment." (p. 288)
- Eisenhower "Great Equation": "it is necessary to recognize that the purpose of America is to defend a way of life rather than merely to defend property, territory, homes, or lives. ... everything done to develop a defense against external threat, except under conditions readily recognizable as emergency, must be weighed and gauged in the light of probable long-term, internal, eﬀect." (Eisenhower diary, Jan 22 1952; "We must achieve both security and solvency," campaign 1952, p. 289)
- "The major deterrent to aggression against Western Europe,” observed NSC 162/2, “is the manifest determination of the United States to use its atomic capability and massive retaliatory striking power if the area is attacked.” By redeﬁning the role of nuclear weapons in US national defense strategy, Eisenhower believed he had unlocked the key to the most cost-eﬃcient approach to waging – and ultimately winning – the Cold War." (p. 293; "massive retaliation" speech Dulles Jan 12 1954)
- Eisenhower on nuclear war: "Atomic war will destroy civilization. There will be millions of people dead … If the Kremlin and Washington ever lock up in a war, the results are too horrible to contemplate." (Hagerty diary July 27 1954); "No one was going to be the winner in such a nuclear war. The destruction might be such that we might have ultimately to go back to bows and arrows." (NSC Jan 1956, p. 296)
- Eisenhower willing to threaten nuclear use: alluded to against North Koreans/Chinese to end Korean War; in Taiwan Strait crises 1954-55 and 1958 "put the US military on full alert, rush a formidable naval armada ... authorize the dispatch of nuclear-equipped forces"; planning for strikes on Chinese installations (p. 297)
- New Look also: increased CIA covert ops (Iran 1953, Guatemala 1954 successes bred overconfidence); "pactomania" (SEATO, Baghdad Pact/CENTO, pacts with Pakistan/Taiwan); psychological warfare, Radio Free Europe, USIA; "Chance for Peace" speech, "Open Skies" 1955.
- Third World failures: SEATO "mere paper alliance"; Baghdad Pact alienated Egypt pushing it to Soviet bloc; confused nationalism with Communism; Soviet economic offensive alarmed Washington (CIA Nov 1955 "grave danger"; Eisenhower: "This is the selectivity and ﬂexibility that always belong to the oﬀensive").
- Eisenhower legacy: reined in defense spending (as % GDP and troops); U-2 1956 and satellites confirmed US superiority; but "missile gap" myth after Sputnik (1957 ICBM test) politically damaging; Gaither Commission.
- Kennedy: assumed missile gap real; "flexible response" to escape Eisenhower "all-or-nothing straightjacket"; increased conventional + missile spending (Keynesian view economy could absorb); counterinsurgency (Green Berets, Vietnam advisers >16k); Alliance for Progress, Peace Corps to win non-aligned (India, Indonesia, Egypt, Ghana); "most dangerous area" Latin America.
- Berlin 1961: after Vienna summit, Khrushchev ultimatum; Kennedy "We cannot and will not permit the Communists to drive us out of Berlin"; "In the thermonuclear age, any misjudgment ... could rain more devastation in several hours than has been wrought in all the wars of human history." (July 25 1961 speech); "It is central to our policy ... that we shall have to use nuclear weapons in the end, if all else fails, in order to save Berlin" (to Clay, p. 309)
- Missile gap disproven (only 10-25 Soviet ICBMs vs suspected 140-200); move to mutual deterrence/MAD by end 1962; Cuban Missile Crisis "you’re talking about the destruction of a country!"; Limited Test Ban Treaty Aug 1963; American University speech June 1963 "more attention be directed to our common interests".
- "Scholars will long debate the relative eﬃcacy of the national security policies crafted by Eisenhower and Kennedy ... Yet the fundamental continuities ... are perhaps more striking. Each saw the Cold War as a long-term struggle that encompassed not just military competition but political, economic, social, cultural, and ideological competition as well. Each, moreover, was committed not just to waging the Cold War but to winning it." (p. 311)

**Soviet foreign policy 1953–1962 (Mastny):**
- "The decade between the death of Iosif Stalin and the Cuban missile crisis was one of great promise and great peril. The promise consisted in the possibility of reversing the Cold War confrontation, the peril in its turning into real war." (p. 312)
- Post-Stalin emergency: Malenkov peace rhetoric; terminated Korean War; Beriia floated neutral unified Germany (sacrificing E. German Communism) but crushed East German revolt June 1953 then arrested/executed; "The fleeting chance for winding down the Cold War thus disappeared before it could even emerge."
- Molotov: resurrected 1952 neutral Germany proposal (but with E. German base); "Monroe doctrine in reverse" (Europe security conference excluding US); proposed NATO admit USSR; failed to exploit EDC rejection; Paris Agreements brought W. Germany into NATO May 1955.
- Khrushchev innovations: Austria State Treaty May 1955 (neutrality, troop withdrawal); May 10 1955 disarmament proposal incorporating Western demands; Warsaw Pact; Yugoslavia visit (fell short); Third World (Czech arms to Egypt, tours India/Burma/Indonesia); Geneva summit July 1955 with Eisenhower (collective security treaty proposal).
- De-Stalinization Feb 1956 Secret Speech: "fatally undermined [Soviet foreign policy] in two critical ways. The condemnation of Stalin’s crimes infuriated Stalin’s Chinese disciple, Mao Zedong, encouraging him to bid for the leadership of international Communism against Khrushchev, thus leading to the Sino-Soviet split. The repudiation of Stalin’s practices also emboldened the restive peoples of Eastern Europe..."
- 1956 crises: Poland (workers riots -> Gomulka nationalist leadership; Chinese support for Polish course prevented Soviet force; Khrushchev conceded after faceoff); Hungary (initial Soviet hesitation, even Molotov inclined to let go; Chinese preferred intervention; Oct 30 declaration on equality/sovereignty/possible withdrawal; then reversal; "the one “with the strongest nerves will be the winner.”" on Suez distraction; nuclear threat to London/Paris despite lacking capability).
- Post-Hungary bluffing: nuclear threats to Denmark/Norway; "a bomb so big … that we cannot even test it inside the vast area of the Soviet Union. If we set it oﬀ on the North Pole it would melt the ice-cap..."
- Sputnik Oct 1957 exploited for "missile gap" perception; Berlin crisis launched Nov 1958 ultimatum ("squeeze the allies out of Berlin 'like an ugly pimple out of the nose'"); Camp David 1959 (Khrushchev believed concessions coming); U-2 1960 wrecked Paris summit (Khrushchev "guilty of delaying the onset of détente for ﬁfteen years" per Mikoian).
- 1961 Vienna summit: Khrushchev estimated "more than a 95 percent probability that there will be no war" for separate E. German peace treaty; "This was the only time during the Cold War that a responsible leader is known to have embarked upon a policy he estimated ran as much as a 5 percent risk of leading to a war that could escalate to a nuclear disaster."
- Wall Aug 1961: resolved Berlin "in practice" but "the solution of the larger German problem, however, would have to wait until the end of the Cold War"; "Buria" exercise assumed nuclear war, massive WP thrust into W. Europe.
- Cuba: main motive "the desire to save the Cuban revolution from an American attack genuinely believed to be looming"; Operation Anadyr (42,000 troops, 230,000 tons materiel); tactical nukes deployed; "The point is that we do not want to provoke war; we wanted to frighten the United States..." (Khrushchev presidium Oct 22 1962).
- "There was a deeper tragedy in the trajectory of Soviet foreign policy from Stalin’s death to the Cuban drama. ... Khrushchev dared to risk détente because of his belief in the system’s fundamental strength. ... Unlike the cynical and calculating Stalin, Khrushchev was an impatient improviser and compulsive schemer; if Stalin often calculated badly, Khrushchev often did not calculate at all. The Berlin crisis ... was in an important sense even more dangerous than the later Cuban crisis. ... That peril would not pass with the passing of Khrushchev but only with the passing of the Soviet Union." (p. 333)

**East Central Europe 1953–1956 (Békés):**
- Post-Stalin New Course: "preserving the status quo remained a top priority for Moscow. Even though the incoming administration of Dwight D. Eisenhower launched a propaganda campaign for the 'liberation of the captive nations,' the new Soviet leaders never contemplated the surrender of the East Central European Communist states." (p. 334)
- 1953 crises economic-driven: Bulgaria tobacco strikes (May); Czechoslovakia Plzeň uprising June 1 (seized city hall, demanded free elections; crushed); East Germany: New Course announced June 11 (reduce heavy industry, boost consumer goods, suspend collectivization); Berlin uprising June 17 spread to 560 cities, ~half million participants; "The revolt, which endangered Communist rule and might well have resulted in its total collapse, was emphatically quelled by the tanks of the Soviet army." Demonstrations evolved from economic to "openly political and anti-Communist".
- New Course imposed across bloc (Hungary, Albania etc.): amnesty, "restoration of socialist lawfulness", cuts in heavy/defense industry, light industry/consumer goods boost, end or slow forced collectivization; collective leadership (separate party leader/PM roles).
- Hungary 1953-55: Rákosi relinquished PM to Imre Nagy; ambitious reforms ameliorated conditions; power struggle; Nagy ousted April 1955 after Malenkov fall.
- Warsaw Pact May 1955: "designed to promote unity and also to strengthen Soviet dominance"; "in theory ... a voluntary alliance of sovereign and equal states"; regular multilateral meetings; allies to promote Soviet goals in Third World/Europe.
- De-Stalinization after 20th CPSU Congress Feb 1956 (Secret Speech sent to allies, published Poland June): "The principle of peaceful coexistence ... acquired the status of long-term doctrine"; different paces; amnesties; Poznań June 28 1956: "armed uprising ... workers fought a real battle"; 73 civilians + 8 armed forces killed; economic -> anti-Communist (seized police, freed prisoners).
- Polish October: Gomulka elected first secretary despite Soviet surprise; Khrushchev + delegation flew to Warsaw Oct 19, troops moved toward capital; "dramatic faceoﬀ"; Gomulka convinced Khrushchev to accept in return for assurances reforms would not threaten Communist rule or bloc unity; Gomułka used western border (vs W. Germany) to justify continued Soviet troops presence.
- Hungary 1956: reburial of Rajk early Oct drew 100k+ silent protest; students Szeged Oct 16 independent org, Budapest Oct 22 "sixteen points" (Nagy PM, party congress, Soviet troop withdrawal, free press/speech, multiparty elections); Oct 23 peaceful demo solidarity with Poland escalated to armed revolt; Hungarian leaders requested Soviet intervention; "sporadic armed incidents evolved into a widespread anti-Soviet battle for liberation"; general strike, workers' councils/revolutionary committees took power.
- Nagy appointed PM Oct 24; negotiated with Mikoian/Suslov; Oct 28 Moscow agreed program calling it "broad national movement" not counterrevolution; promised dissolve AVH, legalize revolutionary orgs, withdraw troops from Budapest; Oct 29-30 pullback; Oct 30 Soviet government declaration "explicit promise to lay a new foundation for relations ... based on mutual equality and noninterference"; "consider the withdrawal of Soviet troops from Hungary entirely".
- Reversal: Oct 31 Kremlin canceled withdrawal order for "even more massive military operation" after news of multiparty system, AVH dissolution, party collapse, freedom of press, violence vs officials; "Communist power in Hungary was on the verge of collapse".
- Hungary declared withdrawal from Warsaw Pact and neutrality Nov 1; appealed to UN; Nov 4 massive Soviet invasion crushed in days; Kádár countergovernment; Nagy refuge in Yugoslav embassy, later executed; ~2,700 killed in revolution; 200,000 fled to West; post-revolution: 13,000 detained, >20,000 imprisoned, 230 executed (incl. Nagy).
- Legacies: "the Western states, by not intervening, had proved once and for all their unconditional acceptance of the postwar European status quo. Despite all their propaganda about the 'liberation of the enslaved nations,' it meant nothing." "the Hungarian revolution largely contributed to the success of the effort to build a post-Stalinist Communist order"; "ended any hopes in East Central Europe that the Soviet yoke could be thrown off by active revolt." Polish October showed limited reforms possible even against Soviet will (inspired 1968 Czechoslovakia).

**Sino-Soviet alliance and Cold War in Asia 1954–1962 (Zhang):**
- Post-Korean expectations: China sought Soviet aid for reconstruction/defense after "sacrificing itself to save North Korea"; 1953 Mikoian-Li Fuchun 91 defense projects; Khrushchev 1954 China visit: more projects (15+16+), technology transfer (>4,000 devices/inventions "practically for free"), 8,000+ advisers, 7,000 Chinese trainees; joint ventures (Xinjiang oil etc., Dalian) transferred to full Chinese ownership; Soviet forces withdraw from Lüshun/Port Arthur by 1955.
- Geneva 1954: close Molotov-Zhou collaboration; persuaded Ho Chi Minh to accept partition of Vietnam, neutralization Laos/Cambodia.
- Persistent suspicions: Mao rejected joining E. European economic community ("no need", self-reliant); trade frictions (China exported strategic raw materials tungsten/tin etc. and rubber at preferential prices, ~48% ag products; paid $156M cash); July 1956 negotiations accused Soviets of "chauvinism", "unreasonable" pricing; Moscow returned overcharges.
- Nuclear: Mao 1954 requested atomic program aid; Khrushchev refused ("huge waste" while under Soviet protection), offered only small reactor for research "peaceful use"; Soviets sought joint uranium mining but conditioned.
- De-Stalinization impact: CCP upset not consulted; initially supported but "Sino-Soviet collaboration" fragile.
- 1957-58 dissonance: Mao Great Leap Forward (overtake Britain 7 yrs, US 8-10; steel etc. targets wildly raised); Moscow skeptical then positive on catching Britain; Nie Rongzhen Sept 1957 Moscow mission: protocol for model atomic bomb but no delivery date; Soviets sent P-2 missiles/specialists but Chinese felt excuses.
- 1958 control fears: Soviet proposals joint radio station (Malinovskii Apr) and "joint ﬂeet of nuclear-powered submarines"; Mao June 1958 insisted China finance own radio station for ownership; July 22 Mao to Iudin furious: "the Soviets are the ﬁrst-class [people] whereas the Chinese are among the inferior who are dumb, careless"; "father–son or cats–mice" relationship; "Russian chauvinism"; historical Stalin spheres in NE/Xinjiang; "we won’t get entangled with you"; Khrushchev July 31 visit apologized, loans/tech only, China owns; Mao conceded still needed alliance.
- 1958 Taiwan Strait: Beijing shelled Jinmen/Matsu; Khrushchev supported with letters to Eisenhower (nuclear retaliation threat if US attacked China); but tensions.
- 1959: Khrushchev Beijing visit (Sept-Oct) 7-hr bitter meeting; accused Mao risk-taking on Taiwan/India; Mao called him "time-server"; Mao Dec 1959 Politburo: Khrushchev "politically 'immature'", "easily deceived by imperialists", "fearful ... of Chinese-style Communism"; Suslov report: Mao "embellish[es]" successes, "cult of personality", "great genius", "original Marxist", "infallible".
- Polemics to collapse: CCP prepared public critique Lenin betrayal Feb 1960; Budapest June 1960 resolution criticizing CCP radicalism; July 16 1960 Soviet note: all experts withdrawn immediately ("unsatisfactory treatment"); 1,390 experts left by Sept 1; 12 agreements terminated, >200 projects; two-thirds of 304 aid projects incomplete; China: "socialist imperialist blackmail"; self-reliance ("build our own nation with our own hands"); rejected Khrushchev famine relief (1M tons grain, 0.5M sugar) and payment deferral; paid early instead; "Chinese regarded the Soviets as 'evil-minded,' the Kremlin viewed the Chinese as stubborn and 'ungrateful'."
- Nuclear/rift impact: Aug 1961 Moscow accepted US test ban/non-proliferation treaty (endangered China's projects); Beijing protested sovereignty violation, "collude with the United States to contain China"; sped atomic project, "set the year 1964 as the best time to explode its ﬁrst bomb"; Oct 1962 attacked India (Zhou: "determined to counterattack"), suspecting Soviet collusion with Nehru.
- "Why did the alliance collapse? Both Moscow and Beijing should have beneﬁted from a Cold War alliance. Indeed, the leaders in both capitals had tried to maintain the alliance by resolving diﬀerences through direct communications and through what they regarded as a fair exchange of resources. But unlike the key Western Cold War alliances, the Sino-Soviet partnership proved unable to withstand the test of clashing national interests, cultural and racial suspicions, and the personal idiosyncrasies of Mao Zedong and Nikita Khrushchev." (end of chapter)

- Kennedy: assumed missile gap real; "flexible response" to escape Eisenhower "all-or-nothing straightjacket"; increased conventional + missile spending (Keynesian view economy could absorb); counterinsurgency (Green Berets, Vietnam advisers >16k); Alliance for Progress, Peace Corps to win non-aligned (India, Indonesia, Egypt, Ghana); "most dangerous area" Latin America.
- Berlin 1961: after Vienna summit, Khrushchev ultimatum; Kennedy "We cannot and will not permit the Communists to drive us out of Berlin"; "In the thermonuclear age, any misjudgment ... could rain more devastation in several hours than has been wrought in all the wars of human history." (July 25 1961 speech); "It is central to our policy ... that we shall have to use nuclear weapons in the end, if all else fails, in order to save Berlin" (to Clay, p. 309)
- Missile gap disproven (only 10-25 Soviet ICBMs vs suspected 140-200); move to mutual deterrence/MAD by end 1962; Cuban Missile Crisis "you’re talking about the destruction of a country!"; Limited Test Ban Treaty Aug 1963; American University speech June 1963 "more attention be directed to our common interests".
- "Scholars will long debate the relative eﬃcacy of the national security policies crafted by Eisenhower and Kennedy ... Yet the fundamental continuities ... are perhaps more striking. Each saw the Cold War as a long-term struggle that encompassed not just military competition but political, economic, social, cultural, and ideological competition as well. Each, moreover, was committed not just to waging the Cold War but to winning it." (p. 311)

**Soviet foreign policy 1953–1962 (Mastny):**
- "The decade between the death of Iosif Stalin and the Cuban missile crisis was one of great promise and great peril. The promise consisted in the possibility of reversing the Cold War confrontation, the peril in its turning into real war." (p. 312)
- Post-Stalin emergency: Malenkov peace rhetoric; terminated Korean War; Beriia floated neutral unified Germany (sacrificing E. German Communism) but crushed East German revolt June 1953 then arrested/executed; "The fleeting chance for winding down the Cold War thus disappeared before it could even emerge."
- Molotov: resurrected 1952 neutral Germany proposal (but with E. German base); "Monroe doctrine in reverse" (Europe security conference excluding US); proposed NATO admit USSR; failed to exploit EDC rejection; Paris Agreements brought W. Germany into NATO May 1955.
- Khrushchev innovations: Austria State Treaty May 1955 (neutrality, troop withdrawal); May 10 1955 disarmament proposal incorporating Western demands; Warsaw Pact; Yugoslavia visit (fell short); Third World (Czech arms to Egypt, tours India/Burma/Indonesia); Geneva summit July 1955 with Eisenhower (collective security treaty proposal).
- De-Stalinization Feb 1956 Secret Speech: "fatally undermined [Soviet foreign policy] in two critical ways. The condemnation of Stalin’s crimes infuriated Stalin’s Chinese disciple, Mao Zedong, encouraging him to bid for the leadership of international Communism against Khrushchev, thus leading to the Sino-Soviet split. The repudiation of Stalin’s practices also emboldened the restive peoples of Eastern Europe..."
- 1956 crises: Poland (workers riots -> Gomulka nationalist leadership; Chinese support for Polish course prevented Soviet force; Khrushchev conceded after faceoff); Hungary (initial Soviet hesitation, even Molotov inclined to let go; Chinese preferred intervention; Oct 30 declaration on equality/sovereignty/possible withdrawal; then reversal; "the one “with the strongest nerves will be the winner.”" on Suez distraction; nuclear threat to London/Paris despite lacking capability).
- Post-Hungary bluffing: nuclear threats to Denmark/Norway; "a bomb so big … that we cannot even test it inside the vast area of the Soviet Union. If we set it oﬀ on the North Pole it would melt the ice-cap..."
- Sputnik Oct 1957 exploited for "missile gap" perception; Berlin crisis launched Nov 1958 ultimatum ("squeeze the allies out of Berlin 'like an ugly pimple out of the nose'"); Camp David 1959 (Khrushchev believed concessions coming); U-2 1960 wrecked Paris summit (Khrushchev "guilty of delaying the onset of détente for ﬁfteen years" per Mikoian).
- 1961 Vienna summit: Khrushchev estimated "more than a 95 percent probability that there will be no war" for separate E. German peace treaty; "This was the only time during the Cold War that a responsible leader is known to have embarked upon a policy he estimated ran as much as a 5 percent risk of leading to a war that could escalate to a nuclear disaster."
- Wall Aug 1961: resolved Berlin "in practice" but "the solution of the larger German problem, however, would have to wait until the end of the Cold War"; "Buria" exercise assumed nuclear war, massive WP thrust into W. Europe.
- Cuba: main motive "the desire to save the Cuban revolution from an American attack genuinely believed to be looming"; Operation Anadyr (42,000 troops, 230,000 tons materiel); tactical nukes deployed; "The point is that we do not want to provoke war; we wanted to frighten the United States..." (Khrushchev presidium Oct 22 1962).
- "There was a deeper tragedy in the trajectory of Soviet foreign policy from Stalin’s death to the Cuban drama. ... Khrushchev dared to risk détente because of his belief in the system’s fundamental strength. ... Unlike the cynical and calculating Stalin, Khrushchev was an impatient improviser and compulsive schemer; if Stalin often calculated badly, Khrushchev often did not calculate at all. The Berlin crisis ... was in an important sense even more dangerous than the later Cuban crisis. ... That peril would not pass with the passing of Khrushchev but only with the passing of the Soviet Union." (p. 333)

**East Central Europe 1953–1956 (Békés):**
- Post-Stalin New Course: "preserving the status quo remained a top priority for Moscow. Even though the incoming administration of Dwight D. Eisenhower launched a propaganda campaign for the 'liberation of the captive nations,' the new Soviet leaders never contemplated the surrender of the East Central European Communist states." (p. 334)
- 1953 crises economic-driven: Bulgaria tobacco strikes (May); Czechoslovakia Plzeň uprising June 1 (seized city hall, demanded free elections; crushed); East Germany: New Course announced June 11 (reduce heavy industry, boost consumer goods, suspend collectivization); Berlin uprising June 17 spread to 560 cities, ~half million participants; "The revolt, which endangered Communist rule and might well have resulted in its total collapse, was emphatically quelled by the tanks of the Soviet army." Demonstrations evolved from economic to "openly political and anti-Communist".
- New Course imposed across bloc (Hungary, Albania etc.): amnesty, "restoration of socialist lawfulness", cuts in heavy/defense industry, light industry/consumer goods boost, end or slow forced collectivization; collective leadership (separate party leader/PM roles).
- Hungary 1953-55: Rákosi relinquished PM to Imre Nagy; ambitious reforms ameliorated conditions; power struggle; Nagy ousted April 1955 after Malenkov fall.
- Warsaw Pact May 1955: "designed to promote unity and also to strengthen Soviet dominance"; "in theory ... a voluntary alliance of sovereign and equal states"; regular multilateral meetings; allies to promote Soviet goals in Third World/Europe.
- De-Stalinization after 20th CPSU Congress Feb 1956 (Secret Speech sent to allies, published Poland June): "The principle of peaceful coexistence ... acquired the status of long-term doctrine"; different paces; amnesties; Poznań June 28 1956: "armed uprising ... workers fought a real battle"; 73 civilians + 8 armed forces killed; economic -> anti-Communist (seized police, freed prisoners).
- Polish October: Gomulka elected first secretary despite Soviet surprise; Khrushchev + delegation flew to Warsaw Oct 19, troops moved toward capital; "dramatic faceoﬀ"; Gomulka convinced Khrushchev to accept in return for assurances reforms would not threaten Communist rule or bloc unity; Gomułka used western border (vs W. Germany) to justify continued Soviet troops presence.
- Hungary 1956: reburial of Rajk early Oct drew 100k+ silent protest; students Szeged Oct 16 independent org, Budapest Oct 22 "sixteen points" (Nagy PM, party congress, Soviet troop withdrawal, free press/speech, multiparty elections); Oct 23 peaceful demo solidarity with Poland escalated to armed revolt; Hungarian leaders requested Soviet intervention; "sporadic armed incidents evolved into a widespread anti-Soviet battle for liberation"; general strike, workers' councils/revolutionary committees took power.
- Nagy appointed PM Oct 24; negotiated with Mikoian/Suslov; Oct 28 Moscow agreed program calling it "broad national movement" not counterrevolution; promised dissolve AVH, legalize revolutionary orgs, withdraw troops from Budapest; Oct 29-30 pullback; Oct 30 Soviet government declaration "explicit promise to lay a new foundation for relations ... based on mutual equality and noninterference"; "consider the withdrawal of Soviet troops from Hungary entirely".
- Reversal: Oct 31 Kremlin canceled withdrawal order for "even more massive military operation" after news of multiparty system, AVH dissolution, party collapse, freedom of press, violence vs officials; "Communist power in Hungary was on the verge of collapse".
- Hungary declared withdrawal from Warsaw Pact and neutrality Nov 1; appealed to UN; Nov 4 massive Soviet invasion crushed in days; Kádár countergovernment; Nagy refuge in Yugoslav embassy, later executed; ~2,700 killed in revolution; 200,000 fled to West; post-revolution: 13,000 detained, >20,000 imprisoned, 230 executed (incl. Nagy).
- Legacies: "the Western states, by not intervening, had proved once and for all their unconditional acceptance of the postwar European status quo. Despite all their propaganda about the 'liberation of the enslaved nations,' it meant nothing." "the Hungarian revolution largely contributed to the success of the effort to build a post-Stalinist Communist order"; "ended any hopes in East Central Europe that the Soviet yoke could be thrown off by active revolt." Polish October showed limited reforms possible even against Soviet will (inspired 1968 Czechoslovakia).

**Sino-Soviet alliance and Cold War in Asia 1954–1962 (Zhang):**
- Post-Korean expectations: China sought Soviet aid for reconstruction/defense after "sacrificing itself to save North Korea"; 1953 Mikoian-Li Fuchun 91 defense projects; Khrushchev 1954 China visit: more projects (15+16+), technology transfer (>4,000 devices/inventions "practically for free"), 8,000+ advisers, 7,000 Chinese trainees; joint ventures (Xinjiang oil etc., Dalian) transferred to full Chinese ownership; Soviet forces withdraw from Lüshun/Port Arthur by 1955.
- Geneva 1954: close Molotov-Zhou collaboration; persuaded Ho Chi Minh to accept partition of Vietnam, neutralization Laos/Cambodia.
- Persistent suspicions: Mao rejected joining E. European economic community ("no need", self-reliant); trade frictions (China exported strategic raw materials tungsten/tin etc. and rubber at preferential prices, ~48% ag products; paid $156M cash); July 1956 negotiations accused Soviets of "chauvinism", "unreasonable" pricing; Moscow returned overcharges.
- Nuclear: Mao 1954 requested atomic program aid; Khrushchev refused ("huge waste" while under Soviet protection), offered only small reactor for research "peaceful use"; Soviets sought joint uranium mining but conditioned.
- De-Stalinization impact: CCP upset not consulted; initially supported but "Sino-Soviet collaboration" fragile.
- 1957-58 dissonance: Mao Great Leap Forward (overtake Britain 7 yrs, US 8-10; steel etc. targets wildly raised); Moscow skeptical then positive on catching Britain; Nie Rongzhen Sept 1957 Moscow mission: protocol for model atomic bomb but no delivery date; Soviets sent P-2 missiles/specialists but Chinese felt excuses.
- 1958 control fears: Soviet proposals joint radio station (Malinovskii Apr) and "joint ﬂeet of nuclear-powered submarines"; Mao June 1958 insisted China finance own radio station for ownership; July 22 Mao to Iudin furious: "the Soviets are the ﬁrst-class [people] whereas the Chinese are among the inferior who are dumb, careless"; "father–son or cats–mice" relationship; "Russian chauvinism"; historical Stalin spheres in NE/Xinjiang; "we won’t get entangled with you"; Khrushchev July 31 visit apologized, loans/tech only, China owns; Mao conceded still needed alliance.
- 1958 Taiwan Strait: Beijing shelled Jinmen/Matsu; Khrushchev supported with letters to Eisenhower (nuclear retaliation threat if US attacked China); but tensions.
- 1959: Khrushchev Beijing visit (Sept-Oct) 7-hr bitter meeting; accused Mao risk-taking on Taiwan/India; Mao called him "time-server"; Mao Dec 1959 Politburo: Khrushchev "politically 'immature'", "easily deceived by imperialists", "fearful ... of Chinese-style Communism"; Suslov report: Mao "embellish[es]" successes, "cult of personality", "great genius", "original Marxist", "infallible".
- Polemics to collapse: CCP prepared public critique Lenin betrayal Feb 1960; Budapest June 1960 resolution criticizing CCP radicalism; July 16 1960 Soviet note: all experts withdrawn immediately ("unsatisfactory treatment"); 1,390 experts left by Sept 1; 12 agreements terminated, >200 projects; two-thirds of 304 aid projects incomplete; China: "socialist imperialist blackmail"; self-reliance ("build our own nation with our own hands"); rejected Khrushchev famine relief (1M tons grain, 0.5M sugar) and payment deferral; paid early instead; "Chinese regarded the Soviets as 'evil-minded,' the Kremlin viewed the Chinese as stubborn and 'ungrateful'."
- Nuclear/rift impact: Aug 1961 Moscow accepted US test ban/non-proliferation treaty (endangered China's projects); Beijing protested sovereignty violation, "collude with the United States to contain China"; sped atomic project, "set the year 1964 as the best time to explode its ﬁrst bomb"; Oct 1962 attacked India (Zhou: "determined to counterattack"), suspecting Soviet collusion with Nehru.
- "Why did the alliance collapse? Both Moscow and Beijing should have beneﬁted from a Cold War alliance. Indeed, the leaders in both capitals had tried to maintain the alliance by resolving diﬀerences through direct communications and through what they regarded as a fair exchange of resources. But unlike the key Western Cold War alliances, the Sino-Soviet partnership proved unable to withstand the test of clashing national interests, cultural and racial suspicions, and the personal idiosyncrasies of Mao Zedong and Nikita Khrushchev." (end of chapter)

### Balkans / Tito-Stalin split (Rajak)
- Yugoslavia/Albania: "Led by local Communists, the partisan armies that liberated Yugoslavia and Albania swept pro-Soviet regimes into power, enabling Moscow to shape developments in those countries, with the result resembling something of an 'empire by invitation'." Later normalisation post-Stalin 1953 "had a significant impact on the process of de-Stalinisation in the USSR and Eastern Europe." (pp. 198-212, Rajak).

### PRC birth and road to Korean War (Niu Jun)
- Post-Sino-Japanese War context: US and USSR coordinated on "peace under Jiang Jieshi" to support GMD legitimacy and avoid civil war. Stalin: CCP lower priority; "there could only be one government in China, led by the GMD." US sent Hurley and Marshall missions to broker GMD-CCP peace.
- CCP policy: After civil war victories, "Mao’s concept of 'leaning to one side' vividly revealed the basic tendency and choice of the CCP’s leaders." Mikoian visited Xibaipo Jan 1949; "Mao praised Soviet aid... and essentially dictated the lean-to-one-side policy for the new regime." Liu Shaoqi Moscow June 1949 completed alliance preparations.
- Treaty: CCP dissatisfied with 1945 Sino-Soviet treaty (unequal). Mao in Moscow Dec 1949 insisted on new treaty despite Stalin initially resisting ("time was not right"); signed 14 Feb 1950 "Sino-Soviet Treaty of Friendship, Alliance, and Mutual Assistance". "From that point on, the Soviet Union began to supply economic, financial, and military assistance to the PRC on a grand scale." Supplementary agreement restricted other countries in Manchuria/Xinjiang.
- US relations: CCP "lived in constant dread of various forms of US intervention". Arrest of US consul Ward, contacts with Stuart in Nanjing produced no positive results; "disabused the top CCP leaders of any notion of developing normal relations with the United States". Mao 30 June 1949 "On the People’s Democratic Dictatorship" announced "lean to one side". "The war to turn China into a US colony... has been an important component of the US imperialist policy of world-wide aggression since World War II." (Mao quote p. 236).
- Road to Korea: Korean War broke 25 June 1950. China initially saw it as Soviet responsibility. US intervention + Taiwan Strait blockade + SE Asia moves led to reevaluation of "strategic expansion against China". Politburo Aug 4 1950 Mao: "if the US imperialists were to succeed, they would be complacent, and would threaten us." China abandoned Taiwan campaign to redeploy to Korea. Entry "highly likely" after US moves. (pp. 221-237, Niu Jun).

### Japan, US and Cold War 1945-1960 (Guthrie-Shimizu)
- Occupation phase I: Tokyo Trials (28 leaders, Tojo etc hanged; emperor spared to avoid unrest). SCAP used existing institutions. Reforms: female suffrage, revised Civil Code, pacifist constitution (Art IX renouncing war), abolition of peerage, labor unions, education liberalization, economic deconcentration. "defeat acted as a catalyst for radical changes... that perhaps would have been impossible with indigenous forces alone." But "one can trace today [1995] a far greater continuity with the recent past than would at one time have seemed possible." (Beasley p. 214).
- Reverse course (1947-): "the emphasis shifted from punishment and reform to political stability via economic reconstruction." Strike Commission Report Jan 1947 recommended lenient reparations, economic rehabilitation. MacArthur March 1947 press: demilitarization/democratization "largely achieved".
- Peace and alliance: San Francisco Peace Treaty signed "in the midst of the Korean War" gave "truncated peace and limited latitude to deal with the Communist world." US-Japan Security Treaty. Korean War "ignited" Japan economy: "massive US military procurements... resuscitated the anemic Japanese economy." "By the time the US occupation ended in April 1952, Japan was well on its way to economic recovery, readying itself for high-speed growth in the 1960s."
- China trade: US forced Japan into harsher embargo on PRC than on USSR. Japanese business signed four "private-sector" trade agreements 1952-58 despite US disapproval. Southeast Asia pushed as alternative but "Yoshida... called for a $4 billion 'Marshall Plan for Asia'" rebuffed; similar Kishi proposal rejected.
- Neutralism and revision: Lucky Dragon incident (March 1954 Bikini H-bomb test, crew death) "dramatically illustrated" chasm in nuclear perceptions. 1960 revised security treaty under Kishi: "more equal partnership", "barred US military intervention inside Japan", "mandated consultation before American forces were deployed", "explicitly stated that the United States had an obligation to defend Japan", nuclear prior consultation. Okinawa excluded. "In the end, however, Tokyo elected to stay in its alliance of convenience with a powerful United States and chose the path to economic prosperity." (pp. 246-265, Guthrie-Shimizu).

### The Korean War origins (Stueck)
- Pre-division: Cairo 1943: Korea "in due course … become free and independent." Roosevelt favored multipower trusteeship (US, USSR, China, possibly Britain) due to concerns over Korean capacity and great power struggle.
- 1945 division: US and USSR sent troops. Truman proposed 38th parallel "demarcation line between Soviet and American occupation forces. Stalin agreed." "The Americans and the Soviets bypassed the Koreans in deciding to occupy their country and in choosing a boundary..."
- South: Japanese sought "native elite" for transition; Yo Un-hyong formed Committee for the Preparation of Korean Independence. Sept 6 1945: some declared Korean People’s Republic. US under Hodge "refused to work with the People’s Republic, discouraged the people’s committees, and favored contacts with members of the colonial apparatus or Korean conservatives."
- North: Soviets worked with people's committees; Kim Il Sung (Manchuria guerrilla, CCP affiliated, fled to USSR 1941) "emerged as the Soviets’ favorite." Returned Sept 1945 in Soviet uniform. Dec 1945 secretary-in-chief of NK Communist branch; Feb 1946 chairman NKPPC (provisional govt).
- Trusteeship and polarization: Dec 1945 US-USSR agreed joint commission + "four-power trusteeship for a period of up to five years." Outrage in south (Rhee, Kim Ku); Soviets persuaded Communists to support. Joint Commission March-May 1946 adjourned without agreement. "By summer, politics in Korea had become polarized." Soviets consolidated under Kim; Americans "adopting repressive measures against the Communists."
- Economic/social unrest south: Mishandled rice, no broad land reform (unlike north), influx >1M Koreans, power/coal shortfalls, "brutal acts of the national police" (Japanese-era). Autumn Harvest Uprising fall 1946: "strikes among railroad workers in Seoul, Pusan, and Taegu" led to "mobs of industrial workers, farmers, students, and the unemployed" across zone. "Disturbances lasted into November and were suppressed by the national police only with the assistance of US troops and a new South Korean constabulary. Deaths and casualties on both sides numbered well into the hundreds. Although the role of Communists... remains uncertain, the makeup, timing, and location of the riots suggest coordination rather than spontaneity." (pp. 267-270, Stueck).

### Culture and the Cold War in Europe (Gienow-Hecht)
- "Culture constitutes the transmission of ideas, dreams, mores, traditions, and beliefs from one generation to the next... In Europe, the Cold War shaped a new way of transferring and selling ideas, values, productions, and reproductions. State interest, geopolitical strategy, ideological preconceptions, obsessive self-definition, and the continuous challenge of an enemy image dictated its contours to an unprecedented degree. In that sense, the Cold War was a war about two different Weltanschauungen, two ways to organize cultural life, two possibilities of defining modernity..." (pp. 397–399).
- "the Soviets claimed that they looked back on a great cultural past. They said the Americans had not produced any high culture." Soviets "presented themselves as the saviors of occidental culture (Abendlandkultur): their army had saved European culture from the onslaught of Adolf Hitler." "European high culture enjoyed more respect and a broader audience in the Soviet Union than in the United States. Communism did not just preserve high culture; it made it accessible to everyone with the help of heavy state subsidies." (pp. 401–402).
- US propaganda "remained primarily defensive... lacked vision... the effort to defy the criticism that the United States had no high culture and no tradition remained at the heart of US cultural propaganda in Europe throughout the Cold War." Used "classical music, symphony concerts... Van Cliburn... won first prize at the International Tchaikovsky Competition in Moscow" (1958); CIA/CCF funded translations (Steinbeck, Hemingway), Congress for Cultural Freedom; USIA, Fulbright, exchanges (pp. 407–409).
- "Ironically, the influx of American popular culture into Europe – condemned by Communists and conservatives, welcomed by young people... eventually cemented both the victory and the failure of American propaganda in Europe. By providing a language of protest, popular culture successfully contributed to the defeat of Communist ideology... But US popular culture did not enhance the image of the United States in Europe; instead, both conservatives and left-wing intellectuals used the same language of protest... to voice longstanding concerns against American civilization." "The legacy of the US triumph at the end of the Cold War continues to be a deep-seated anti-Americanism all over Europe." (pp. 400, 414–415).
- In East: "popular culture – notably rock and roll – became a way to silently protest against the government"; children's films like DEFA's "Drei Nüsse für Aschenbrödel" popular across Iron Curtain; "high culture remained front-page news"; "they never lost the argument that high culture flourished in the East." US "never realized the full dimensions of the battle they fought... the European image of the United States reflected everything Europeans feared about modernization: materialism, individualism, and loss of cultural identity." (pp. 415–418).

### Cold War mobilization and domestic politics: the United States (McEnaney)
- NSC 68: "a real war in which the survival of the free world [was] at stake... Americans would have to summon their better selves and demonstrate their 'ingenuity, sacrifice, and unity.'" Mobilization via "traditional democratic process" through "home-front institutions – state and local governments, schools, businesses, labor unions, civic organizations, and media" (pp. 420–421).
- "McCarthyism" "was primarily a top-down phenomenon... there was not one, but many McCarthyisms, each with its own agenda and modus operandi." "The campaign against Communism 'was, above all, a collaborative project.'" Taft-Hartley 1947 required union leaders "to sign an affidavit avowing they were not Communists"; "wiped out an entire generation of activists"; "the labor movement turned inward and raided its own left wing." FBI "designed and ran much of the machinery" of pursuit; "Hooverism"; Truman loyalty program; McCarran Act 1950 (pp. 424–427).
- National security state "a state within a state"; "disciplinary state" with powers to "spy, prosecute, and purge." "National security" "used widely, many would say recklessly, to understand a whole range of issues"; "ambient militarism" (pp. 426–428).
- Contradictions: conservatives feared "garrison state"; "Liberal intellectuals were willing to put the containment of communism ahead of the welfare state while conservative intellectuals were prepared to give antistatism a back seat to anticommunism." (Hogan, p. 432).
- Civil rights: "segregation harmed US foreign relations"; "a story of progress... US moral superiority" vs. "counternarrative of racial oppression"; NAACP "purged its Communist membership"; "national security was the dominant rationale" for racial advancement but "made the reform impulse both urgent and limited" (Dudziak, Sherry; pp. 433–434).
- Gender/family: "the Cold War fostered a conservative political culture in which men and women looked for security in conventional gender roles" (May); but "the Cold War had no fixed association with the domestic ideal" and "offered a new vocabulary for extending democratic ideals" (Meyerowitz); women civil-defense activists "ardent Cold Warriors" but "maternalists" and pushing "female representation in government"; "Cold War national security discourse ultimately valorized masculine toughness" (pp. 435–436).
- Nuclear fear: citizens "private pacifists but public militarists"; "most Americans... rejected this kind of self-help defense. Only a tiny minority built family shelters"; "the Cold War's cultural authority was constantly tested by these more resilient and long-evolving features of American political culture" (pp. 439–441). "the Cold War [was] not synonymous with American culture, even at the height of its impact" (Kuznick/Gilbert; p. 422).

### Cold War mobilisation and domestic politics: the Soviet Union (Priestland)
- Leadership "obsessed with the competition with the West, and was deeply anxious about its status": Stalin (1946) on intellectuals adoring "some foreign bastard"; Khrushchev pre-1959 US trip: "Stalin kept trying to convince us that we... were no good" (pp. 442–443).
- "the Soviet system was already well adapted to competition of a Cold War type... born during war... economic system... created for war"; "the Cold War dominated the thinking of the leadership in 1945–62, [but] it did not demand a special set of approaches to domestic politics"; "the politics of the United States much more substantially than the politics of the Soviet Union" changed (pp. 443–444).
- "guns or butter": postwar "Fourth Five-Year Plan (1946–50)" prioritized heavy industry; "in 1948 real wages were 80% of their level in 1940"; famine 1946; recovery post-1948 but "low living standards did not cause much open, organised discontent"; Khrushchev Virgin Lands, 1959–61 consumer turn, but 1962 "retail food prices on meat, sausage, and butter were substantially increased" → "serious unrest... most notably in Novocherkassk, where riots had to be put down by Red Army tanks" (pp. 446–450).
- Post-Stalin: Beriia/Malenkov "technocratic... more effective material incentives... greater investment in consumer industries"; Malenkov "war in the nuclear age would destroy world civilisation"; Khrushchev revivalist: party mobilization, ideology, compete via "cheaper nuclear technologies" and "transferring the competition with the West from the military to the ideological and economic spheres"; ultimately "failed to resolve the problem of guns and butter" (pp. 448–451).
- "tekhnika and politika": postwar Stalin "socialist patriotic campaigns" (Zhdanov on "cringing before philistine foreign literature"; Lysenko 1948); "scientists were accused of 'servility to the West'"; but "Stalin... insisted that there were objective scientific and economic laws independent of Marxism"; "no special proletarian science" for math/Darwinism. Khrushchev "revivalist position: the party had to be strengthened... 'democratising' the party"; but "subordination of science to politics" criticized at his fall (pp. 453–459).
- Popular attitudes: "fear of war with the West was high"; sympathy for "official Soviet patriotism" and "unique sacrifices"; but "dissatisfaction with the late Stalinist regime of austerity and discipline"; "stiliachestvo" youth subculture (stylish clothes, jazz/rock); "Western culture... helped to undermine the collectivism and quasi-military ethos of sacrifice"; "it was likely that Marxism-Leninism’s emphasis on collective... interests would be threatened by the officially endorsed culture of consumption" (pp. 460–462).

### Decolonization, the global South, and the Cold War 1919–1962 (Bradley)
- "In 1900, most of Asia, Africa, the Caribbean, and the Middle East were ruled directly or indirectly by the Euro-American colonial powers... in the two decades after 1945 imperial order collapsed... the number [of UN members] had more than doubled to 117, with a majority of the increase made up of states in the global South" (pp. 464–465).
- "the global move toward decolonization was rooted in local particularities that long preceded, ran parallel with, and ultimately persisted beyond the Cold War... The Cold War... tends to obscure the significance of transnational postcolonial visions in the global South that imagined a world apart both from the bipolar international system and from the imperial order" (pp. 465).
- Pre-1945: WWI conscripts; Wilson self-determination petitions (Zaghlul, Tilak, Ho Chi Minh) "met with silence"; "racial equality clause" rejected; Comintern training; "first Pan-African Congress" 1919; Négritude (Césaire, Senghor); pan-Islamic (al-Azhar); Gandhi satyagraha; Vietnamese radicals "borrowing as much from indigenous political discourse and the ideas of Thomas Jefferson, Gandhi, and Sun Yat-sen as it did from Marx and Lenin" (pp. 466–469).
- WWII/1945: "Japanese occupation... dislodged the region’s British, French, and Dutch rulers"; 1945 proclamations (Aung San Burma, Sukarno Indonesia, Ho DRV); "UN Charter... guarantees of human rights" and 1948 UDHR "provided a powerful new vocabulary"; early "the coming of the Cold War in the global South was an almost unimagined contingency"; Stalin "provided almost no material assistance to the Vietnamese"; US "wary... Marshall... 'we are not interested in seeing colonial empire administrations supplanted by a philosophy emanating from and controlled by the Kremlin'" (pp. 470–473).
- Post-1950: "the rise of Mao’s China in 1949 and the outbreak of the Korean War in 1950 brought the dynamics of the Cold War more fully"; US paid "as much as three-quarters of the cost of the [French] war" in Vietnam; Khrushchev 1956 "decolonization was a 'postwar development of world-historical significance'"; Soviet aid to India/Indonesia/Egypt; US "modernization theory" "American economy, society, and culture provided a universal model"; "racialized paternalism" in US views (pp. 474–476).
- "The Cold War both empowered and constrained new states, turning many into proxy arenas"; yet "local particularities" and "transnational postcolonial visions" persisted (p. 465).
