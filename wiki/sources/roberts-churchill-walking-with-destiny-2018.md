# Churchill: Walking with Destiny (Andrew Roberts, 2018)

**Source page for Andrew Roberts’ *Churchill: Walking with Destiny* (2018).**

Roberts’s biography is a comprehensive life that explicitly tests Churchill’s own claim that all his past had been preparation for the hour of 1940. It uses the richest post-2010s archival releases to examine how lessons (and scars) from a long career in war, politics, and journalism equipped him — uniquely among contemporaries — to lead Britain through its “finest hour” and the global coalition that followed. The book balances admiration for his strategic vision, rhetorical power, and indomitability with candid analysis of errors of judgment, temperament, and policy.

## Approach and Method

The work is grounded in primary sources with particular strength in the newly accessible materials that allow fresh insight into Churchill’s inner circle and decision processes (War Cabinet verbatim records, royal and Soviet ambassador diaries). Roberts emphasizes the long “Preparation” (Part One) as the true key to understanding the “Trial” (Part Two). He treats Churchill as a self-conscious student of history who deliberately modeled himself on Marlborough and Napoleon while learning from personal failures. The portrait is of a man whose passions, emotions, and historical imagination were central to his leadership style, not incidental to it.

### From Range 01 (Introduction + Part One early Preparation; lines 424–8000)
**R01-Claim-1: Belief in Personal Destiny and Life as Preparation for Crisis Leadership**

Churchill held a longstanding conviction, dating to at least age sixteen, that he was a man of destiny fated to play a decisive role in saving Britain from foreign invasion or catastrophe. The text explicitly frames his entire early life (errors, military service, political lessons) as conscious preparation for high command, with the 1940 premiership as the fulfillment. This self-mythology, coloured by admiration for his ancestor the 1st Duke of Marlborough and Napoleon, provided psychological resilience and purpose.

Verbatim (Introduction): ‘I felt as if I were walking with destiny, and that all my past life had been but a preparation for this hour and for this trial … I could not be reproached either for making the war or with want of preparation for it. I thought I knew a good deal about it all, and I was sure I should not fail.’ (Recalling 10 May 1940, when he became prime minister hours after [[actors/hitler-adolf]] unleashed his Blitzkrieg on the West.)

Verbatim (Introduction): ‘He had believed in his own destiny since at least the age of sixteen, when he told a friend that he would save Britain from a foreign invasion. His lifelong admiration of Napoleon and his own ancestor, John Churchill, 1st Duke of Marlborough, coloured his belief that he too was a man of destiny.’

**R01-Claim-2: Blenheim Palace and Marlborough Heritage as Formative Influences on Identity and Worldview**

Birth and holidays at Blenheim Palace instilled a profound sense of historic continuity, martial glory, and aristocratic responsibility. The Palace—described as commemorating the 1st Duke of Marlborough’s victory that saved Britain from European domination—shaped Churchill’s self-conception; he absorbed its magnificence and the spirit of his ancestor, the greatest soldier-statesman in British history.

Verbatim (“A Famous Name, November 1874–January 1895”): ‘We shape our buildings,’ Winston Churchill was later to say, ‘and afterwards our buildings shape us.’ Although he never lived at Blenheim, he was profoundly influenced by the splendour of the Palace’s 500-foot frontage, its 7 acres of rooms and its 2,700-acre estate. ... The Palace was – still is – pervaded with the spirit of the 1st Duke, the greatest soldier-statesman in British history, who, as Churchill was to describe him in his biography of his ancestor, was a duke ‘in days when dukes were dukes’.

**R01-Claim-3: Aristocratic Birth, Self-Confidence, and “Rhinocerine Hide”**

Churchill’s sublime self-confidence and self-reliance stemmed directly from his birth into one of the families that had guided the nation for centuries, as holder of the Spencer-Churchill name. This shielded him from self-distrust and enabled him to endure controversy and criticism without personal hurt, caring more for the imagined opinion of fallen Great War comrades than living parliamentary colleagues. He was no snob despite entitlement and an aristocratic way of life.

Verbatim (“A Famous Name...”): ‘Churchill’s sublime self-confidence and self-reliance stemmed directly from the assurance he instinctively felt in who he was and where he came from. ... He knew he came from the apex of the social pyramid, and one of the key attributes of that class at that time was not to care overmuch what people further down it thought of them. As his greatest friend ... F. E. Smith ... was to write of him, “He was shielded in his own mind from self-distrust.”’

Verbatim: ‘Many are the memoirs that condemn Churchill for his insensitivity to other people and their views, but they fail to appreciate that just such a rhinocerine hide was essential for someone who was to become as addicted to controversy as he was.’

Verbatim: ‘What is the sense of being against a man simply because of his birth?’ he wanted to ask [[actors/hitler-adolf]] of the Jews.

**R01-Claim-4: Parental Emotional Neglect and Its Role in Driving Ambition and Attention-Seeking**

Physical and emotional distance from Lord Randolph and Jennie (few visits or letters; mother’s active social life) fostered Churchill’s determination to be seen and heard. Naughtiness at school stemmed from a desire for attention; the neglect instead produced an unquenchable drive to succeed in his father’s profession of politics. Jennie’s famous retrospective: “She shone for me like the evening star. I loved her dearly – but at a distance.”

Verbatim (“A Famous Name...”): ‘Lord Randolph Churchill’s political career and Jennie’s active social life meant that they had relatively little time for their son. ... Winston was later famously to write of his mother, “She shone for me like the evening star. I loved her dearly – but at a distance.”’

Verbatim: ‘After a dinner in the late 1930s, Winston was to say to his own son, “We have this evening had a longer period of continuous conversation together than the total which I ever had with my father in the whole course of his life.”’

**R01-Claim-5: Nanny Elizabeth Everest as Primary Maternal Surrogate and Source of Unquestioning Love**

Mrs Everest (“Woom”) was by far the closest figure to the young Churchill, serving as confidante during troubles at St George’s and providing the affection absent from his parents. He paid for her nursing and grave upkeep lifelong; her death left him describing her as “my dearest and most intimate friend during the whole of the twenty years I had lived.”

Verbatim (“A Famous Name...”): ‘The person who had first spotted the lashes wrought on the young boy by Sneyd-Kinnersley was Churchill’s fifty-two-year-old spinster nanny, Elizabeth Everest. “My nurse was my confidante,” Churchill later recalled. “It was to her I poured out my many troubles.” ... “She had lived such an innocent and loving life of service to others and held such a simple faith”, he later wrote of her death, “that she had no fears at all, and did not seem to mind very much. She had been my dearest and most intimate friend during the whole of the twenty years I had lived.”’

**R01-Claim-6: Education, Academic Reality vs. Myth, Development of Memory, and Early Oratory/Repartee**

Despite later self-mythologizing in *My Early Life* as an academic dunce, school reports show strong performance in History, Classics, etc. Beatings at sadistic St George’s fostered a photographic/phonographic memory (learning by heart to avoid flogging). At Harrow he won prizes for reciting 1,200 lines of Macaulay, showed repartee, fenced successfully, and absorbed patriotic school songs that instilled a sense of obligation to become a great man and serve the country.

Verbatim (“A Famous Name...”): ‘His school reports utterly belie his claims to have been an academic dunce. ... Churchill was regularly beaten at St George’s, but this was not because of his work – his History results were always “good”, “very good” or “exceedingly good” ... Aside from a lacerated bottom, Churchill took away from St George’s a photographic and phonographic memory ... He claimed in his autobiography that, because he could not master the first declension of Latin, “there was one thing I could do: I could learn by heart.”’

Verbatim: ‘At fourteen he won a prize for reciting no fewer than 1,200 lines of Macaulay’s *Lays of Ancient Rome* without error ... “If I had to make my literary will and my literary acknowledgements,” he told an acquaintance in 1946, “I should have to own that I owe more to Macaulay than to any other English writer.”’

Verbatim (on school songs and patriotism): ‘Listening to those boys singing all those well-remembered songs,’ Churchill told his son after he had visited the school during the London Blitz in 1940, ‘I could see myself fifty years before singing with them those tales of great deeds and of great men and wondering with intensity how I could ever do something glorious for my country.’

**R01-Claim-7: Lessons from Father’s Political Career, Rise, Resignation, and Death; Adoption of Tory Democracy**

Lord Randolph’s brilliant oratory, adoption of Disraeli’s imperialism + social reform (“Tory Democracy”), and spectacular self-inflicted fall (threatening resignation over Estimates, accepted by Salisbury) taught Winston key lessons: never threaten resignation unless prepared to go into the wilderness (and only with allies capable of bringing down the government). Father’s death at 45 (Jan 1895) left profound grief and a vow to pursue his aims, vindicate his memory, and act with urgency.

Verbatim (“A Famous Name...”): ‘The most important was not to threaten to resign unless one is prepared to go into the wilderness. If one is not so prepared, then only threaten to resign along with several other people capable of bringing down the Government.’

Verbatim: ‘“My father died on January 24 in the early morning,” Churchill recalled thirty-five years later. “Summoned from a neighbouring house where I was sleeping, I ran in the darkness across Grosvenor Square, then lapped in snow. ... All my dreams of comradeship with him, of entering Parliament at his side and in his support, were ended. There remained for me only to pursue his aims and vindicate his memory.”’

Verbatim: ‘“I took my politics almost unquestioningly from him,” he wrote in 1931...’

**R01-Claim-8: Omdurman (1898) – Cavalry Charge, Personal Bravery, Lessons on War, and Modern Weaponry’s Impact**

As a 21st Lancers troop commander, Churchill participated in the last significant British cavalry charge. He showed coolness under fire, killed multiple Dervishes at close range with a Mauser pistol (due to prior shoulder injury preventing sword use), and later reflected on the horror of industrialized killing versus “brave men ... destroyed, not conquered, by machinery.” He denounced aspects of Kitchener’s conduct post-battle.

Verbatim (“From Omdurman to Oldham via Pretoria”): ‘I found myself surrounded by what seemed to be dozens of men. ... I had room and time enough to turn my pony out of his reach, and leaning over the offside I fired two shots into him at about three yards. ... In the close and confused fighting he killed four men with the ten-shot Mauser automatic pistol...’

Verbatim: ‘“There was nothing dulce et decorum about the Dervish dead,” Churchill later wrote; “nothing of the dignity of unconquerable manhood; all was filthy corruption. Yet these were as brave men as ever walked the earth … destroyed, not conquered, by machinery.”’

**R01-Claim-9: Armoured Train Ambush, Capture, and Daring Escape from Pretoria (1899) – Initiative, Resourcefulness, and Ordeal**

Churchill showed bravery and leadership in the 15 Nov 1899 armoured train ambush near Estcourt (helped clear derailed trucks off the line so engine could escape, rallied troops; ~90 minutes under fire). Captured, imprisoned in State Model School, Pretoria; on 12 Dec escaped over the wall after waiting in a garden, walked through the city, hid in coal truck/jumped, took refuge, and reached Portuguese East Africa with help (using Orion for navigation, as in prior desert experience). He viewed captivity as hateful and acted with urgency due to limited time.

Verbatim (“From Omdurman to Oldham via Pretoria”): ‘Churchill displayed great bravery and initiative in leading some survivors out on to the track and then spending half an hour heaving the two overturned trucks off the line... “Keep cool, men,” ... “This will be interesting for my paper!”’

Verbatim (escape): ‘“I had come to the conclusion that we should waste the whole night in hesitations unless the matter were clinched once and for all,” ... “as the sentry turned to light his pipe, I jumped on to the ledge of the wall and in a few seconds had dropped into the garden safely on the other side.” ... “Orion shone brightly. ... Scarcely a year before he had guided me when lost in the desert to the banks of the Nile. He had given me water. Now he should lead me to freedom.”’

**R01-Claim-10: Early Political Lessons, Oratory, and Ambition in Action (Oldham By-Election and Aftermath, ~1899)**

Churchill fought the Oldham by-election (trading on his father’s name, speaking multiple times nightly), lost narrowly (opportunism on Clerical Tithes Bill backfired), but viewed it as non-disgracing and resolved to try again (hoped to be Prime Minister). He recognized strategic lessons (e.g., defend the worst attacks on one’s side) and saw the defeat as fortunate (freed him for South Africa fame).

Verbatim (“From Omdurman to Oldham via Pretoria”): ‘“No doubt the Radicals will say I am trading on my father’s name,” he conceded ... “Well, and why should I not? Did not you think it is a good name to trade with?”’

Verbatim: ‘“Everyone threw the blame on me,” he later wrote wryly. ... He returned to London ... “with those feelings of deflation...”’

Verbatim: ‘Had he squeaked into the House of Commons in 1899, he would not have gone to South Africa and would not have had the opportunity to make not just a local or national reputation for himself, but a truly international one.’

### From Range 02 (Part One mid Preparation; lines 8001–14000)
**R02-Claim-1: Fall from the Admiralty in May 1915 due to Dardanelles/Gallipoli failure and coalition formation**

As First Lord of the Admiralty, Churchill faced intense political pressure after the initial naval attack on the Dardanelles failed to force the Narrows and the subsequent land campaign stalled, leading colleagues including Asquith to view him as expendable in forming a coalition government. He fought to retain the post but was removed on 21 May 1915, retaining only a Cabinet seat without executive power as Chancellor of the Duchy of Lancaster. This demotion from commanding the Royal Navy was a direct result of the Gallipoli operation's early setbacks and inter-service tensions, especially with Fisher.

Verbatim: “‘Winston is far the most disliked man in my Cabinet by his colleagues,’ Asquith told his wife. ‘Oh! he is intolerable! Noisy, long-winded and full of perorations.’” (text describing the May 1915 ministerial crisis). “I am finished!” Churchill told Riddell... ‘Yes, finished in respect of all I care for – waging war, the defeat of the Germans.’” (same section). “Asquith merely replied, ‘You must take it as settled that you are not to remain at the Admiralty... I hope to retain your services as a member of the new Cabinet...’” (letter exchange in the crisis account).

**R02-Claim-2: Clementine Churchill’s letter to Asquith pleading to keep Winston at the Admiralty**

Clementine wrote a passionate letter to Asquith on 20 May 1915 arguing that removing Churchill would injure the war effort because of his unique knowledge, capacity, vigour, and ‘power, the imagination, the deadliness to fight Germany.’ The letter was shown to Margot Asquith, who dismissed it harshly, and Asquith did not reply, illustrating the personal and political isolation around the First Lord.

If he goes, the injury to Admiralty business will not be reparable for many months... He has the supreme quality which I venture to say very few of your present or future Cabinet possess – the power, the imagination, the deadliness to fight Germany.” (Clementine’s letter quoted in full in the May 1915 crisis section). Margot: “‘It shows the soul of a servant... That touch of blackmail and insolence...’” (diary reaction in same passage).

**R02-Claim-3: Churchill’s appeal to Asquith to retain control of the Dardanelles operation**

On or around 21 May 1915, Churchill wrote a lengthy letter to Asquith asserting his responsibility was ‘terrible’ but sustainable, claiming he and Admiral Wilson could ‘bring this vast Dardanelles business safely through’ and pleading not to have the intricate affair taken from his hands on ‘mere uninformed newspaper hostility.’ Asquith rejected the plea, confirming the removal.

Verbatim: “My responsibility is terrible. But I know I could sustain it, and without the slightest impairment... could bring this vast Dardanelles business safely through... Let me stand or fall by the Dardanelles – but do not take it from my hands.” (Churchill’s letter to Asquith in the crisis account). Asquith’s reply: “You must take it as settled that you are not to remain at the Admiralty...” (immediate follow-up).

**R02-Claim-4: Gallipoli campaign consequences and Churchill’s continued advocacy while in reduced role**

After demotion, Churchill remained on the War Council and Dardanelles Committee, urging reinforcements (six more divisions after Third Krithia) and arguing in a 5 June 1915 Dundee speech that success through the Narrows offered ‘the downfall of a hostile empire, the destruction of an enemy’s fleet and army, the fall of a world-famous capital.’ The Suvla Bay offensive in August 1915 failed due to poor maps, leadership errors (e.g., Stopford), and Turkish reinforcements, leading to stalemate and eventual evacuation recommendations.

Verbatim: “Through the Narrows of the Dardanelles and along the ridges of the Gallipoli Peninsula, he told a Dundee audience on 5 June, ‘lie some of the shortest paths to a triumphant peace... Beyond those few miles of ridge and scrub... lie the downfall of a hostile empire...’” (June 1915 advocacy). On Suvla: “Allied units were landed in the wrong place, faced sheer cliffs and deep gullies and got lost because of bad maps... Stopford was relieved of his command on 16 August, but by then it was too late.” (account of the August offensive).

**R02-Claim-5: Resignation from the government on 11 November 1915 and Commons resignation speech**

Churchill resigned after exclusion from the new small War Council, stating he could not accept general responsibility without an effective share in guidance and control, and placing himself at the disposal of the military authorities (his regiment was in France). In the 15 November 1915 Commons speech he took responsibility for the naval plan while criticizing Fisher’s lack of support, describing it as ‘a legitimate war gamble, with stakes that we could afford to lose for a prize of inestimable value.’

Verbatim: “I am in cordial agreement with the decision to form a small War Council... Knowing what I do about the present situation... I could not accept a position of general responsibility for war policy without an effective share in its guidance and control... I therefore ask you to submit my resignation to the King.” (resignation letter to Asquith). Speech: “Not a line, not a word... but I approved of the plan; I backed the plan... War is a hard and brutal job...” (defence of the decision and Fisher criticism).

**R02-Claim-6: Lessons from the Dardanelles/Gallipoli for future conduct of war and grand strategy**

The debacle taught Churchill the value of prolonged inter-allied conferences, never overruling unanimous Chiefs of Staff advice, cutting losses rather than increasing stakes (avoiding prestige-driven mission creep as in Norway/Dakar/Greece/1940 France), and ensuring elected politicians retained supremacy over ‘brass-hats.’ He later identified his post-18 March 1915 error as trying to carry out a major operation from a subordinate position, leading him in 1940 to combine the premiership with the new post of Minister of Defence.

Verbatim: “‘A single, prolonged conference, between the allied chiefs, civil and martial, in January 1915, might have saved us from inestimable misfortune,’ he wrote in The World Crisis...” (lessons section). “In his war memoirs after the Second World War he declared that after the failure of the naval attack of 18 March 1915 he had been wrong in ‘trying to carry out a major and cardinal operation of war from a subordinate position.’ So on becoming prime minister in 1940 he appointed himself to the new position of minister of defence as well.” (explicit forward linkage). “He learned that it was sometimes better to cut one’s losses than massively to increase the stakes. So in Norway, Dakar, Greece and elsewhere – and especially with RAF fighter squadrons over France in mid-May 1940 – he vigilantly guarded against mission-creep...” (same passage).

Verbatim: “However tempting it might be to some when much trouble lies ahead… to step aside adroitly and put someone else up to take the blows… I do not intend to adopt that cowardly course, but, on the contrary, to stand to my post and persevere in accordance with my duty as I see it.” He told the King the country was behind him “because there was no one else.” (Post-Singapore debate and reshuffle, February 1942, “Desert Victory” section.)

**R05-Claim-3: Alliance management with Stalin and direct confrontation over Second Front (Moscow visit)**

Churchill flew to Moscow in August 1942 to explain personally why no full Second Front was possible in 1942 and to soften the blow of Torch by delivering the news face-to-face. Stalin delivered a harsh memorandum and accused the British Navy of cowardice over PQ17; Churchill kept his temper but returned describing Stalin as “evil and dreadful.”

Verbatim: “I have just had a most terrible meeting with this terrible man Stalin… evil and dreadful.” After frank exchanges, the two men reached a working understanding. Churchill later reported “the greatest goodwill prevailed, and for the first time we got on to easy and friendly terms.” (First and subsequent Kremlin meetings, August 1942, “Desert Victory” section.)

**R05-Claim-4: Grand strategy debates and preference for Mediterranean/Italy over premature Overlord or Anvil (1943–1944)**

Churchill consistently argued against scattering resources and for concentrating on Italy to draw German forces from the eventual cross-Channel attack, while accepting Overlord as the principal 1944 operation once conditions (air superiority, sufficient divisions, landing craft) were met. He fought hard against Anvil/Dragoon at the expense of Alexander’s Po Valley/Vienna options, viewing the latter as politically and militarily superior.

Verbatim: “There is of course no question of abandoning ‘Overlord’… The retention of landing craft in the Mediterranean… may cause a slight delay, perhaps till July.” Later: “The splitting up of the campaign in the Mediterranean into two operations neither of which can do anything decisive is… the first major strategic and political error for which we two have to be responsible.” (Cairo/Teheran aftermath and Anvil controversy, “The Hard Underbelly” and “Liberation” sections.)

**R05-Claim-5: Churchill’s direct personal leadership and exposure on D-Day and immediate aftermath**

Churchill insisted on witnessing the Normandy landings and crossed to the beachhead on 12 June 1944 in HMS Kelvin, going ashore in a DUKW, visiting Montgomery, and cruising the coast under fire. He was prevented only by the King from being present on D-Day itself.

Verbatim: Churchill fired at German positions “at a range of only 6,000 yards.” On the front he noted the countryside “surrounded by fat cattle lying in luscious pastures with their paws crossed!” (Normandy visit, 12 June 1944, “Liberation” section.).

**R05-Claim-6: Percentages Agreement and blunt management of the Stalin alliance (Moscow, October 1944)**

At the Tolstoy Conference Churchill presented Stalin with a handwritten “naughty document” proposing spheres of influence percentages for Romania, Greece, Yugoslavia, Hungary and Bulgaria. Stalin ticked it; the two men later agreed Greece would stay in the Western sphere.

Verbatim (the document itself): “Greece: Great Britain in accord with U.S.A. 90%: … Russia 10%.” “After this there was a long silence. … ‘Let us burn the paper.’ ‘No, you keep it,’ said Stalin.” (Tolstoy Conference, 9 October 1944, “Liberation” section).

**R05-Claim-7: Leadership style, fatigue and resolve under extreme pressure (1944–early 1945)**

Churchill showed physical and mental strain (pneumonia, long hours, occasional intemperance at late meetings, family stresses) yet repeatedly drew on reserves of energy for oratory, travel (Yalta, Athens, Normandy) and decisive action (Greek intervention, refusal to yield on core principles). He refused honours while the European war continued and continued to intervene directly in operations and diplomacy.

Verbatim: “I am tired out in body, spirit and soul… All is planned and ready, in what better place could I die than here – in the ruins of Carthage.” (Carthage illness, December 1943.) On Greece: “This brand I snatched from the burning on Christmas Day.” (Post-Yalta reflections and Athens intervention, “Liberation” and “Victory and Defeat” sections).

**R05-Claim-8: Overlord launch, personal anxiety, and post-D-Day strategic oversight**

Churchill described pre-D-Day anxiety as supreme yet remained publicly and privately optimistic once preparations were complete. He immediately visited the beachhead and monitored the breakout, while continuing to press for maximum exploitation of success in Italy and elsewhere.

Verbatim: “Before going to sleep, he said to Clementine, ‘Do you realise that by the time you wake up in the morning twenty thousand men may have been killed?’” (Eve of D-Day.) “I had hoped we were hurling a wildcat on to the shore, but all we had got was a stranded whale.” (Anzio/Overlord period.)

## Volume Synthesis Note

Andrew Roberts’ *Churchill: Walking with Destiny* (2018) provides the wiki’s first comprehensive dedicated biographical treatment of Winston Churchill, drawing on post-2010 archival releases (unexpurgated George VI and Maisky diaries, Lawrence Burgis verbatim War Cabinet records, private family papers). Its central thesis — that Churchill’s entire life from 1874 was conscious preparation for the supreme test of 1940 — is substantiated across the full arc: Blenheim/Marlborough heritage and belief in personal destiny (age 16 onward); military journalism (Omdurman, South Africa escape); political lessons (father’s fall, Gallipoli humiliation and redemption in the trenches); oratory and historical imagination (Macaulay influence, *The World Crisis* vindication); wilderness warnings against Hitler and appeasement (1930s air parity, Munich as “total and unmitigated defeat”); and the 1940–45 leadership that refused negotiation, championed Fighter Command, forged the Grand Alliance (with Roosevelt and Stalin), and insisted on Britain’s voice at the table.

Key contributions: rich verbatim primary texture from speeches, minutes, and letters; balanced, discriminating criticism of errors (Gallipoli, Norway, Gold Standard, Greece 1941, underestimation of Japan, excessive Mediterranean focus, “Gestapo” speech) alongside triumphs; character portrait of a man whose passions, emotions, and “rhinocerine hide” enabled survival of controversy and the “black dog”; explicit linkage of pre-1940 experience to wartime decisions (e.g., civilian supremacy over “brass-hats,” cutting losses vs. prestige mission-creep, historical study as school of statesmanship). Roberts rejects gush while affirming Churchill’s unique fitness for the hour — “he had the lion heart and also gave the roar.”
