# Flynn, *The Roosevelt Myth* (1948)

John T. Flynn's *The Roosevelt Myth* (Devin-Adair, 1948) is the founding text of
what became "Roosevelt revisionism" on the American Old Right: a book-length
argument that the public image of [[roosevelt-franklin-d|Franklin D. Roosevelt]]
was "a fiction... fabricated out of pure propaganda," and that the actual record
of 1932–1945 was one of improvisation, broken promises, bureaucratic empire-
building, and diplomatic capitulation. Flynn had been a celebrated liberal
financial journalist in the 1920s–30s (the *New Republic*'s economics columnist)
who broke with Roosevelt over the NRA and turned into the New Deal's most
relentless journalistic adversary and a leading America First anti-
interventionist. The book was a runaway bestseller — twelve printings in its
first fourteen months — while being frozen out of academic historiography, a
split that itself became a datum in the debate recorded at
[[roosevelt-and-new-deal-revisionism]].

Structure: **Book One, "Trial—and Error"** (1932–36: the Hundred Days, banking
crisis, NRA/AAA, gold policy, "the dance of the crackpots"); **Book Two,
"Confusion"** (1937–40: the court-packing fight, the 1937–38 recession, Hopkins
and the WPA, the third term, [[wallace-henry-a|Henry Wallace]]); **Book Three,
"Betrayal"** (1941–45: the war economy, the Atlantic Charter, wartime
censorship, the great conferences, Teheran and Yalta, Roosevelt's death, and the
closing essay "The Roosevelt Myth" on image-manufacture).

## Evidentiary character

Unusually well-documented for a polemic: 32+ numbered references per book,
built overwhelmingly on insider memoirs and diaries of Roosevelt's own circle —
Moley (*After Seven Years*), Perkins (*The Roosevelt I Knew*), Farley, Hull,
Ed Flynn (*You're the Boss* — note: the Bronx boss Edward J. Flynn, no
relation to the author), the Morgenthau diaries as excerpted in *Collier's*,
Stimson's diary, Byrnes, Elliott Roosevelt's *As He Saw It*, and General
Deane. The signature method is "hoist by their own petard": letting New Deal
insiders' own words carry the indictment, then extrapolating interpretive
conclusions beyond what the sources state (e.g., Moley documents FDR's
inattention during the banking crisis; Flynn infers a deliberate strategy of
letting it worsen). Two load-bearing passages rest on Flynn's **own
first-person testimony**, uncorroborated: his claim (as a Nye Committee
adviser) that Roosevelt personally proposed the 1935 Neutrality Act framework,
and a January 1938 conversation with an unnamed FDR intimate predicting
war-spending on "battleships." Secondary techniques worth weighing when citing:
guilt-by-association narratives closed with formal disclaimers (Hillman–Lepke;
the Hitler-propaganda comparison "I do not compare Roosevelt to Hitler");
character material used against ideas (Hopkins, Veblen); reliance on Pegler
for the Wallace "guru letters."

A historiographically significant capture: Flynn quotes pro-Roosevelt scholars
Thomas A. Bailey ("Roosevelt repeatedly deceived the American people during
the period before Pearl Harbor") and Arthur M. Schlesinger Jr. ("he had no
choice but to trick them into acting for what he conceived to be their best
interests") conceding the deception while defending it — the point where the
revisionist and mainstream factual records overlap.
