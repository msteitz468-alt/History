# Prehistoric Europe

**Authors:** Timothy Champion, Clive Gamble, Stephen Shennan, Alasdair Whittle. Academic Press, 1984 (Left Coast Press reprint 2009). ~350 pages. Collaborative textbook.

## Book Note

A foundational post-radiocarbon textbook that organizes European prehistory thematically around settlement, subsistence, technology, exchange, and social organization instead of the traditional Three Age System. It covers from the earliest Lower Palaeolithic evidence for humans in Europe through the emergence of towns and states in the later first millennium BCE (ending coverage at the threshold of the historic period for northern Europe). 

The authors explicitly position the work after the "radiocarbon revolution" (Renfrew), which lengthened chronologies, destroyed many diffusionist links to the Near East, and forced rethinking of Childe's models. They advocate reconstructing adaptive behavior and systems rather than tracing "influences" or equating pottery styles directly with ethnic groups. The book is deliberately pitched for students; it does not attempt exhaustive regional sequences.

This source is the primary structural baseline for the wiki's treatment of European prehistory (Mesolithic through Early Iron Age), complementing the global Cambridge World History volumes and supplying Europe-specific depth on forager adaptations, the spread and character of farming, Bronze Age social differentiation (prestige goods, hierarchies, Wessex, Tumulus, Urnfield), and the rise of Mediterranean states alongside temperate European competition and early urbanism.

## Volume Synthesis Note

**Core contribution (full 6-range Deployed Subagent extraction + main reconciliation, 2026-06-26):** Champion, Gamble, Shennan & Whittle 1984 is the single best thematic, explicitly post-radiocarbon textbook synthesis of European prehistory in the collection. It organizes the material around settlement, subsistence, technology, exchange, and social organization rather than the Three Age System or regional culture sequences, and it supplies Europe-specific depth that complements the global framing of CWH Vol. 1–2. The authors position their work after the radiocarbon revolution that "greatly lengthened the time-span of European prehistory" and destroyed many of the cross-datings on which Childe's diffusionist model rested. They replace culture-as-people and "where did this come from?" questions with adaptive behavior, systems thinking (Flannery: "not only looking for the Indian behind the artifact but also for the system behind the Indian"), site-catchment analysis, home-base vs. logistical mobility, and the inseparability of economy and society.

**Key regularities across the volume:**
- Palaeolithic (Ch. 2–3): flexible, locally adapted subsistence within site-catchment territories (10 km / 2-hour radius); collector strategies with storage in last-glacial Europe; Upper Palaeolithic "package" (burials with ornaments, Venus figurines with stylistic rules, cave art, long-distance raw material transfers) as restructuring of alliance networks and information systems enabling resilience in extreme environments. Lower/Middle Palaeolithic show continuity in meat-focused flexible strategies rather than big-game revolutions.
- Post-glacial / Mesolithic to Early Farming (Ch. 4–5): parallel adaptations in post-glacial Europe and the Near East; mixed colonization + indigenous contribution model for neolithization; primary settlements on optimal soils (loess LBK longhouses, Balkan tells on fertile valleys) followed by secondary expansion onto marginal soils; high-yielding diverse cereal + livestock economies with plough, manuring, storage, and forest clearance (long-lived up to 400 years); wide exchange networks (Spondylus prestige, obsidian, flint axes); mostly egalitarian family units with communal foci (megaliths, cemeteries) but emerging signals of differentiation by late 5th–4th millennia (Varna gold/copper, Dhimini acropoleis, Millaran).
- Chalcolithic / Early–Middle Bronze social differentiation (Ch. 6–7): Beaker phenomenon and preceding Corded Ware as variable status/ideology expressions (extensive material package only in NW; elsewhere limited to fine vessels + metal items in burials); population growth, clearance, and risk in small dispersed communities drive exogamy networks and prestige competition; control of metal sources (Bohemian tin) and long-distance exotica (Baltic amber to Mycenae) underpin chiefly societies; Wessex, Unetice (Leubingen/Helmsdorf), Argaric, and Aegean (Shaft Graves, Linear B palace admin) as convergent local trajectories rather than simple diffusion from east (explicit revision of Childe); Tumulus and Urnfield as further expressions of hierarchy, uniformity in symbols, and defended sites.
- Late Bronze / Early Iron states and towns (Ch. 8–10): Mediterranean models (Etruscan vehicle/chamber rites, central Italian assimilation of Greek colonies) imitated in temperate Europe; Hallstatt elite vehicle burials at Heuneburg (Mediterranean mud-brick, first silk/chicken in temperate Europe) and Vix; La Tène art/expansion; oppida (Manching 200 ha planned, zoned production, minting, walls) as early towns; Viereckschanzen rural sanctuaries; "early forms of the state had emerged"; coinage, writing, craft centralization; "transformations of society and the economy similar to those that had occurred in the Mediterranean region 500 years earlier." Northern Europe follows separate trajectory of farmstead intensification.

**Major historiographic contribution:**
- Explicit post-radiocarbon, post-Childean stance: interregional links retained but "dependency model no longer tenable"; "convergence of local trajectories."
- Methodological honesty about patchy data and the limits of 1984 research; deliberate choice of thematic organization over exhaustive sequences.
- Strong grounding in environmental reconstruction, scientific sourcing, and social inference from settlement and burial patterning.

### R01 (lines 1–4200 cache: Front matter + Ch. 1 "The Study of European Prehistory" complete + opening Ch. 2 "Earliest Humans in Europe")

**Historiographic framing and disciplinary development (Ch. 1):**
- Foundations: antiquity of man (Lyell, Darwin); Three Age System (Thomsen, Worsaae) as first chronological method. "It was rapidly confirmed by observation of archaeological finds in the field, and thus became the first method for ordering discoveries chronologically." (Champion et al. 1984, Ch.1 / range lines ~260-280)
- Childe's synthesis: "the remarkable Australian prehistorian, Gordon Childe, who laid the groundwork for future studies" via *Dawn of European Civilization* (1925) and *Danube in Prehistory* (1929). Culture = "constantly recurring assemblage of types such as houses, pottery and burial rites" treated as "physical manifestation of a particular social or ethnic group." (Champion et al. 1984, Ch.1 / range lines ~310-335)
- Diffusionism dominant: major innovations (farming, metallurgy, architecture) attributed to Near Eastern centre; chronological dependence on East "tended to be confused with the dependence of prehistoric Europe on the East for all its advances." (Champion et al. 1984, Ch.1 / range lines ~360-380)
- Radiocarbon revolution (Renfrew 1973): independent absolute chronology; calibration (bristlecone pine) lengthens Neolithic to "millennia rather than just centuries"; many traditional links "no longer tenable." Detailed calibration error ~350 yrs at 2000 BC, ~800 yrs at 5000 BC. (Champion et al. 1984, Ch.1 / range lines ~380-410, calibration box ~460-495)

(pp. 211-212). **preceded_by** early BA undifferentiated phases.
- Metal/fortified centres link: "the growth of the copper and bronze industry was almost certainly involved... by c. 1800 BC over much of temperate Europe from Romania to western Germany a pattern of fortified regional centres was in existence... Vesele... Male Kosihy... Nitriansky Hradok... amber..." (pp. 212-214). **produced** craft specialization.
- Tin-bronze and two-piece moulds: "the beginning of copper-tin alloying to produce bronze seems to have depended on the supplies available in the Bohemian Ore Mountains... the two-piece mould came into extensive use, for the solid casting of ornaments and weapons of a previously unknown complexity." (pp. 215-216).
- Amber exotica: "Amber... Baltic sea... found in the greatest quantities in graves which are also very rich in other materials, such as the Shaft Graves of Mycenae in Greece." (p. 219, 545).
- Prestige model vs Childe: "the archaeological record of the later early Bronze Age and Middle Bronze Age of temperate Europe provides ample evidence for the operation of processes such as those outlined... The model outlined emphasizes the importance of interregional connections in the earlier Bronze Age and has that in common with Childe's view. We have seen, however, that Childe placed the motor... in the civilizations of the eastern Mediterranean, and that such a dependency model is no longer tenable... a convergence of local trajectories and an interaction between them..." (pp. 220-221). **contrasts_with** Childe diffusionism.
- Argaric: "The most spectacular Iberian Bronze Age material is undoubtedly that of the El Argar, or Argaric culture... individual burial, very often in large storage jars... much greater emphasis on metal items... the Bronze Age ones are clustered in the south, where the rich copper sources lie." (pp. 223, 881ff). **analogous_to** Wessex/central Europe.
- Aegean/Mycenaean and collapse: "Then the Shaft Graves of Mycenae... rich grave goods... Cretan influence... Linear B... form of Greek... palace of Knossos directly controlled enormous flocks of sheep... textiles... Over a period of about 100 years beginning at the end of the thirteenth century BC the mainland centres were destroyed and there was a breakdown of the cultural uniformity in the pottery... evidence of Mycenaean contact with the eastern Mediterranean also ceases." (pp. 231-236, 1693ff). **produced** Urnfield transition in west.
- Urnfield: "The most characteristic feature was a new burial rite of urned cremation, and the discovery of many cemeteries of this sort has given the name 'Urnfield' to this cultural tradition and this period... Hallstatt A and B belong to the late Bronze Age Urnfield period... considerable homogeneity of bronze types... great uniformity in the use of symbols... Sun symbols and birds' heads... spread as far as Belgium, southern and western France and north-eastern Spain." (pp. 270-271). **part_of** [[concepts/urnfield-culture]].

### R06 (lines 18501-22500 cache: Ch. 9 "Competition and Hierarchy in Temperate Europe" mid-text onward + Ch. 10 "Towns and the State in Temperate Europe" full + chapter conclusions/reading suggestions)

**Late Bronze Age / Urnfield competition, technology, hierarchy (Ch. 9):**
- Urnfield cremation rite and associated prestige objects (swords, armour, bronze vessels) adopted widely; "the close association of Urnfield arms, technology and symbolism with burial rites and defended sites" (Ch. 9, p. 291, cache ~1090s). Rich burials (e.g. Caka Slovakia corslet; Hart an der Alz vehicle + vessels) mark phases of extreme differentiation (13th-11th c and 8th c BCE).
- Proliferation of defended sites end 2nd millennium; "from the end of the second millennium they became much more common". Varied functions: storage (Britain granaries), metal production (central Europe sites like Glauberg), dense occupation (Lausitz Biskupin/Senftenberg). "The emphasis placed on the sword, spear and armour... and the proliferation of defended sites all point to the increasing importance of armed raiding" (Ch. 9, p. 292, cache ~1117s-1120s).
- Technological advances: sheet bronze vessels (cups, buckets, cauldrons) and armour (helmets, shields) via hammering/ribbing/rivetting; hollow casting, leaded bronze (~7-10%) for higher output; iron known/worked 2nd millennium but widespread replacement of bronze for tools/weapons by 7th c BCE. "the eventual mastery of the potential of iron was one of the most important elements in the growth of a more complex society in later prehistory" (Ch. 9, p. 290, cache ~920s). Salt extraction scaled enormously (Hallstatt, Dürrnberg, coastal France/England); glass, coral, lignite ornaments expand.
- Social organization: hierarchical, competitive for prestige/status via arms success, gift exchange, lavish entertaining. "Later Bronze Age social organization was, therefore, markedly hierarchical and characterized by competition for prestige and status" (Ch. 9, p. 292, cache ~1188s). Elite controlled surplus indirectly; positions unstable. "Such a system of social organization was inherently expansive" (Ch. 9, p. 292, cache ~1209s).
- Exchange networks: prestige objects (amber Baltic to Ireland/Italy; bronze vessels) circulated far; deposition (graves, hoards, rivers/bogs) to maintain value/restrict access. "At the highest level of the elite, these exchange networks... extended over most of Europe" (Ch. 9, p. 294, cache ~1390s).

**Mediterranean impact and transition ~700 BCE (Ch. 9):**
- "About 700 BC the cultural homogeneity of the Urnfield region was disturbed. The widespread adoption of iron at this time... can be used to define the beginning of an Iron Age" (Ch. 9, p. 273, cache ~78s). Bronze supply control undermined ("with the switch to iron the significance of bronze was greatly reduced"); new Mediterranean demand (slaves, raw materials, foodstuffs) reorients surplus south. "Those areas nearest to the colonies of the central and western Mediterranean and the Black Sea were most able to capitalize" (Ch. 9, p. 293, cache ~1419s).
- New burial rites: inhumation under barrow with four-wheeled vehicle + horse/chamber for elites (Bohemia, S Germany, Switzerland, E France, Alpine Italy); "a new set of burial rites may have been appropriate to symbolize a new basis for social organization" (Ch. 9, p. 292, cache ~1269s). "This new prestige burial rite... was also practised, as we have already seen in the previous chapter, in Etruscan Italy" (Ch. 9, p. 273, cache ~99s).
- Imitation: "Central European society was not just influenced by the Mediterranean world, but it seems actually to have tried to imitate it, even if somewhat palely" (adoption of Mediterranean materials, architecture for defences, pottery styles, fashions) (Ch. 9, p. 292, cache ~1318s). Heuneburg Period IV mud-brick bastioned circuit "of Mediterranean inspiration".
- Heuneburg exemplar: 3 ha promontory; workshops (bronze brooches, iron, coral/amber/lignite ornaments); "fine pottery from Athens and amphorae... from the south of France"; silk, chicken first in temperate Europe. "The Heuneburg was clearly the residence of an elite who were able to mobilize local resources to connect themselves to very far-reaching exchange networks" (Ch. 9 box, p. 288, cache ~782s). Associated Hohmichele (80m diam. barrow, vehicle burials).

**Early La Tène, oppida, towns and states (Ch. 10):**
- La Tène cultural group (c. 450 BCE+): homogeneity in weapon/jewellery forms and "art developed from that initiated in fifth century Germany"; expansion east to Austria/Czech/Hungary (burial rite diffusion more than mass migration). Brooch styles key chronological marker; "by the end of the millennium brooch styles derived from La Tène originals were found throughout northern Europe" (Ch. 10, p. 300, cache ~1630s). Rich burials (chariots, Mediterranean imports) in early phases (Champagne, Rhineland); decline by 2nd c BCE; cremation in NW.
- Production/exchange revolution: wheel-thrown pottery common (kilns e.g. 12 at Sissach, complexes at Gellerthegy-Taban, Stare Hradisko); iron tools specialized (hammers, files, saws etc.); centralized smelting near ores (Manching/Kelheim, Noricum/Magdalensberg famed); graphite clay from Passau/Ceske Budejovice; standardized iron ingots, weights systems (309g/638g "pounds"); coinage (high value gold/silver copying Philip II/Alexander; later small denom. bronze/potin for local/temple use). "increasingly the products became standardized... By the first century BC there was a marked pattern of standardization in certain products" (Ch. 10, p. 306, cache ~1970s).
- Oppida/towns: "In the final phases of the Iron Age... major changes... The settlement pattern was transformed by the growth of large sites which functioned as towns, and new centres of industrial production distributed standardized wares over larger distances. At the same time, political power was becoming increasingly centralized, and early forms of the state had emerged. Coinage was introduced... The use of writing was known, at least for keeping official records. Thus even before the Roman conquest, large parts of 'barbarian' Europe were occupied by literate societies with a high degree of social, economic and political development" (Ch. 10 opening, p. 298, cache ~1515-1530s).
- Manching key: 200 ha at river junction; "planned and well-maintained internal layout, standardized types of buildings and zoning of activities; an economic function as a centre of non-agricultural production; and a role as a focus of local and distant exchange" (Ch. 10 box, p. 313, cache ~2260s). 7 km walls/gates (massive timber/nails/stone); 10m streets; craft zoning (iron, wood/leather); minting; large cattle-based provisioning. Similar open then fortified or hilltop sites (Breisach, Basel, Bern, Aulnat → oppida; Stare Hradisko, Stradonice, Hrazany in east; British Colchester, Maiden Castle, Danebury).
- Social/political: Celtic society (nobles, druids, commons with patron-client); nobles competitive via generosity/force. By 2nd c BCE monarchies (Arverni central France; SE England dynasties styling "king"; Noricum, Thracian/Dacian states). Later western civitas (e.g. Aedui Vergobretos elected 1 year, limited power, council/magistrates for stability). "By the first century, a network of societies with elements of such a centralized and institutionalized system of government existed from western France to Switzerland" (Ch. 10, p. 317, cache ~2690s). Coinage and writing (tablets, papyrus) for state functions (tax, military pay, records); "Caesar's discovery in the baggage of the defeated Helvetii in 59 BC of written records of their population" (Ch. 10, p. 318, cache ~2830s).
- Ideology/ritual: Viereckschanzen "a new form of rural sanctuary, comprising a square ditched enclosure (Viereckschanze) with shafts containing votive deposits... appeared in the first century BC at the same time as the early states were developing" (Ch. 10, p. 319, cache ~2890s). Eastern monumental building (Seuthopolis, Sarmizegethusa sanctuaries); new temples at British centres. Burial rites change/abandon (no regular rite in many areas by 1st c BCE); cremation in some NW; rich graves persist on northern fringes.
- Northern Europe separate: farmsteads/villages, uniform cremations, no oppida; pressure from climate/soil exhaustion → intensification (rye, manuring, fields, stalled animals, new tools); later signs of stratification (large farms, defended sites, waggon/horse graves, bog deposits). "Society and economy in this area were not totally static, but the changes that did take place were more akin to those described in the previous chapter" (Ch. 10, p. 322, cache ~3010s).
- End of prehistory framing: "In the last centuries of the first millennium BC transformations of society and the economy similar to those that had occurred in the Mediterranean region 500 years earlier now took [place] in much of central and western Europe. Only northern and far north-western Europe remained outside the scope of these developments" (Ch. 10, p. 298, cache ~1530s). Roman expansion transformed or incorporated; "conquest was easiest where these developments were furthest advanced at the time of invasion, as in central France or south-eastern England" (Ch. 10, p. 324, cache ~3100s).

**Mediterranean state/Etruscan context (referenced from Ch. 8 in Ch. 9-10):**
- Etruscan Italy practised the new vehicle/chamber burial rites seen in temperate (as previous chapter). "Central European society... tried to imitate it [Mediterranean]" in architecture, styles (Heuneburg explicit). Transformations in temperate parallel those in Mediterranean "500 years earlier". Trade/contact effects visible "from Russia to Spain"; first Greek colonies assimilated central Italy, then S France/NE Spain.

### R03 (cache lines 8501–11800: Ch. 4 "Post-glacial Developments: Hunters, Gatherers and Beyond" late references + early Ch. 5 "Early Farming Societies: Seventh to Fourth Millennia BCE")

**Early Neolithic farming spread and colonization (Ch. 5):**
- Role of direct colonization on the SE–NW axis with possible indigenous contribution: "the apparent role played by direct colonization in many areas (Fig.5.7) notably on the south-east/north-west axis as well as in the central Mediterranean and to the immediate east of the Carpathians. Colonization is difficult to prove directly, though in Greece and the southern Balkans, for example, similarities in painted pottery, clay figurines and stamp-seals and other material equipment to that in the adjacent Near East are cited as supporting evidence for the hypothesis. Even with colonization there may regularly have been an indigenous contribution of population and skills; cattle for example seem to have been domesticated (at least on morphological criteria) earlier in Greece (Protsch and Berger, 1973; Evans, 1971) than in the Near East..." (Champion et al. 1984, Ch.5 pp.120–121 / cache ~270-290)
- Primary settlement on optimal soils: "In the southern Balkans, as in the Maritsa valley of Bulgaria, substantial and long-lived settlements (forming 'tells' or settlement mounds), such as Karanovo, were sited on light and workable fertile valley soils... In central and western Europe primary agricultural settlements (of the so-called Linienbandkeramik or Linear Pottery culture), again often substantial, were regularly sited on deposits of loess, a well drained and easily tillable soil." (Champion et al. 1984, Ch.5 p.122 / cache ~340-378)
- Later secondary colonization onto poorer soils and interfluves (e.g. southern Poland, Alpine foreland). (Champion et al. 1984, Ch.5 pp.123–124 / cache ~390-410)

**Subsistence: cereals, animals, technology (Ch. 5):**
- Mixed farming as secure blend with cereals dominant: "Together both cereals and animals surely presented a high-yielding, diverse and above all secure blend of resources. Their relative contributions must have varied from region to region, but overall it seems that cereals were the dominant partner of a successful marriage and represent the ultimate post-glacial triumph of the plant." (Champion et al. 1984, Ch.5 p.126 / cache ~250-260)
- Plough evidence from early 4th millennium: "the existence of a variety of ploughs, light and heavy, is seen in marks scored in the subsoil and preserved under barrows dating from the earlier fourth millennium, from southern England to Poland, on a variety of soils, chalk and sandy (Fig.5.11). Possible wooden examples are preserved beside the Sweet Track, England (Coles et al., 1973) and at Seeberg Burglischisee-Stid in Switzerland..." (Champion et al. 1984, Ch.5 pp.127–128 / cache ~540-560)
- Animal management skills, meat focus, limited early secondary products: "High proportions of juveniles are a regular feature, however, suggesting a widespread concentration on meat... It is probably too early for wider generalization but milk and wool, though valuable, may most likely have been of less significance at the outset..." Castrated cattle from 6th millennium central Europe. (Champion et al. 1984, Ch.5 pp.128–130 / cache ~920-950)
- Local environmental management and effects: clearances "both long-lived—up to 400 years in northern Irish examples... and perhaps extensive"; permanent fields likely widespread; side-effects include "soil erosion and redeposition", "heath followed arable land by about 3000 BC" on sandy soils, truncation on chalk. (Champion et al. 1984, Ch.5 pp.126–127, 139–140 / cache ~640-680, 1790-1820)

**Settlement forms and variation:**
- Balkan tells vs. temperate longhouses vs. Alpine foreland lake settlements (nucleated rows at Niederwil vs. shorter rows at Egolzwil). Site duration estimates via dendro and C14; continuity despite individual site abandonment. (Champion et al. 1984, Ch.5 pp.122–125, 152–154 / cache ~340-400, 1510-1570)
- Family as basic unit; some larger communal buildings (e.g. Nea Nikomedeia central structure). "a model of internally undifferentiated or more or less egalitarian communities has generally been proposed." (Champion et al. 1984, Ch.5 pp.141–142 / cache ~1850-1980)

**Exchange networks:**
- Spondylus gaederopus from Aegean/Black Sea as prestige chain: widely distributed as bracelets etc. from late 6th millennium on SE–NW axis. Obsidian from Melos and central Mediterranean islands; flint/stone axes with fall-off from sources (Breton, Polish, Danish, British highland); jadeite axe example on Sweet Track. (Champion et al. 1984, Ch.5 pp.144–146 / cache ~2170-2220)
- "balanced reciprocity" (Sahlins) for much utilitarian exchange; prestige networks for rarer items. (Champion et al. 1984, Ch.5 p.147 / cache ~2300-2320)

**Social organization and differentiation:**
- Early emphasis on communal identity and interdependence; burials generally show "little differentiation in goods" with all ages/sexes represented. Cemeteries and barrows/megaliths as foci for dispersed communities. (Champion et al. 1984, Ch.5 pp.142–143 / cache ~2020-2120)
- Signs of change by end of period (late 5th–4th millennia): Dhimini/Sesklo acropolis layouts with megaron and walls suggesting hierarchy; Varna cemetery (late Chalcolithic) with abundant gold, cast copper, rich vs. poor graves as "the most spectacular recent example of high-quality craft products"; Millaran fortified sites and passage graves in Iberia with ranking in tombs; large Irish passage graves (Newgrange, Knowth) with specialized goods and imposing scale possibly indicating centralization. (Champion et al. 1984, Ch.5 pp.147–149 / cache ~2400-2620)
- Early copper metallurgy in Balkans (5th millennium, Aibunar near Karanovo, Rudna Glava; Karbuna hoard) and Iberia (Millaran); emphasis on social/prestige uses (ornaments, copies of battle-axes) alongside functional; shaft mines impressive. (Champion et al. 1984, Ch.5 pp.146–147 / cache ~2230-2290)

**Megalithic monuments (Atlantic/western Europe):**
- Chambered tombs and barrows (West Kennet, Quanterness, Barnenez, Irish Boyne group) as communal/territorial foci with variable burial numbers; "attention-seeking devices, imposing architecture with the social purpose of providing a focus for surrounding communities." Later examples show possible ranking via size, goods, nucleation. (Champion et al. 1984, Ch.5 pp.143–144, 148–149 / cache ~2050-2120, 2570-2620)

**Mesolithic/post-glacial references and forager-farmer context (Ch. 4/5 transition):**
- Maps label "Mesolithic site", "Line of mesolithic sites" alongside Neolithic settlements (e.g. Alpine foreland, Britain). (Champion et al. 1984, Ch.5 fig.5.16 / cache ~1674-1713)
- Post-glacial adaptation parallel in Near East and Europe: "the emergence of new resources and novel methods of exploiting these resources in the period 9000-7000 BC... can be seen from the European perspective not just as a foreign, isolated process but as a parallel one of adaptation to changing conditions." (Champion et al. 1984, Ch.5 p.121 / cache ~290-300)
- Motive for agriculture possibly social (Ch. 4 suggestion): "It was suggested in Chapter 4 that one motive for a shift to agricultural production in the first place may have come from social conditions of emulation or status-seeking through the provision of food." Fields/pasture more easily property; surpluses buttress differentiation. (Champion et al. 1984, Ch.5 p.147 / cache ~2380-2390)

**Environmental and demographic consequences:**
- Population growth as "one dynamic source of change"; rate "surely increased" with new areas opened. Irreversible consequences. (Champion et al. 1984, Ch.5 p.140 / cache ~1760-1780)
- By ~3000 BCE widespread transitions (end of Michelsberg-TRB, replacement of cultivated areas by grassland/secondary forest in Britain, tell abandonments in SE Europe). (Champion et al. 1984, Ch.5 pp.140–141 / cache ~1820-1840)

### R02 (lines 4201–8500; core Ch. 3 "Subsistence and Society in Palaeolithic Europe")

- Site catchment analysis (Vita-Finzi & Higgs 1970): 10 km / 2-hour walking radius as critical cost threshold for site exploitation territory and home-base settlement systems. Grounded: "The model is based on the ethnographic observation that foraging radius of two hours walking time, about 10 km, represents a critical cost threshold. Beyond such distances returns diminish rapidly..." (Champion et al. 1984, Ch. 3 / range lines ~60-70). Applied to Mt. Carmel caves and Combe Grenal.
- Faunal shifts at Combe Grenal and Swabian Alb sites (middle/upper palaeolithic) reflect climate-driven changes in prey availability within local territories rather than major shifts in human strategy. Detailed tables and graphs of species percentages (mammoth, woolly rhino, bison/aurochs, horse, red deer, reindeer etc.) across periods. Grounded examples throughout Ch. 3.
- Collector (logistical) vs forager settlement systems (Binford 1980): collectors use planning, caches, field camps and stations (animals staple); foragers move consumers daily to resources (plants staple). Last-glacial Europe leaned collector end; storage critical adaptation affecting seasonality interpretations from bones (re: Bouchud SW France reindeer mandibles).
- Site organization examples: Abri Pataud (upper Perigordian, 23,010 ± 170 BP) row of five hearths; Molodova I level 4 Mousterian mammoth-bone oval 8×6 m with 15 hearths; Pincevent Magdalenian hearth arcs and seats; Mezhirich circular ~5 m² mammoth-bone structure with external hearths. Often practical windbreaks/re-use rather than complex huts.
- Specific hunting episodes: La Cotte (penultimate glaciation) layer 3/6 mammoth/rhino bone piles from stampeding and caching; Weinberg caves (Mauern) cached butchered mammoth backbone segments.
- Upper palaeolithic changes post-35k BP: increase in burials with ornaments (Sunghir), Venus figurines with shared stylistic rules across Europe (25–23k BP, Pyrenees to Don), portable art, engraved slabs, cave art (Pech Merle sanctuaries per Leroi-Gourhan). Extensive raw-material networks (Holy Cross flint up to 400 km; obsidian; shells). Alliance networks as "safety net" and information system for high-risk environments.
- Social evolution: Upper palaeolithic populations maintained occupation of central/eastern Europe during extreme glaciation where middle palaeolithic groups had withdrawn; not explained by intelligence or single tech innovations but complex feedback and restructuring of alliances/symbolic behaviour. Grounded: "The appearance of these elements—burials, display, painted caves, raw material transfers—after 35 000 BP all strongly suggest some major changes in palaeolithic adaptations." (Champion et al. 1984, Ch. 3 / range lines ~1710-1720)
- Lower palaeolithic: associations at Hoxne, Lehringen (spear), Bilzingsleben, Swanscombe, Torralba; use-wear confirms meat/marrow; depositional biases noted; flexible strategies likely similar to later groups.

### Settlement Hierarchies, Expansion, and Population Dynamics (Ch. 6)
- Expansion and clearance in central Europe produced differentiated then dispersed settlement: "in central Europe there developed a more differentiated settlement pattern than earlier, including both nucleated settlements, sometimes quite large, and hamlets (e.g. Kruk, 1980). ... in the course of the continuing clearance and expansion of the Corded Ware phase, the settlement pattern became much more dispersed and settlement units smaller, so that a pattern of small hamlets, often of quite ephemeral construction, became generally prevalent and continued until the beginning of the local early Bronze Age c. 2200 BC" (p. 162).
- SE Europe: "the pattern seems to have become more nucleated: in Bulgaria, for example, although many tells were abandoned, a small number continued in occupation during the third millennium and some, such as Ezero, were fortified (Dennell, 1978)" (p. 162).
- Britain: "the settlement pattern always seems to have been a small-scale dispersed one so there was no major change here" (p. 162).
- Corded Ware changes not primarily invasion: "the changes are much more likely to have been the result of local settlement and subsistence developments involving expansion and the adoption of new agricultural strategies" (p. 163; cf. Hausler 1981).

### Inter-Community Relations, Exogamy, and Exchange
- Small Corded Ware/Beaker communities (c. 2900–2300 BC) depended on exogamy and kinship for marriage pools and "mutual subsistence support at times of localized disaster": "the two elements forming a cohesive system of inter-community relations at the local scale which was especially important at this time" (pp. 163–164).
- Flint mining intensified: major sites Grimes Graves, Grand Pressigny, Krzemionki; "The material obtained was highly prized and consequently travelled long distances from its sources" (p. 164). Axes for clearance; large blades (Grand Pressigny) complementary to copper daggers.
- "Of all these different types of materials and artifacts which were the object of exchange, it seems that only a relatively small proportion were immediately utilitarian. The rest were of social rather than practical significance. It is likely that metal too falls into the non-utilitarian category at this stage" (p. 166).

### Metallurgy Beginnings and Adoption
- Shift from SE oxide/carbonate ores (Rudna Glava, Ai Bunar) to sulphide ores (widespread in central/western Europe, requires roasting): "some of the rare copper finds of this period... have proved on analysis to be made from the sulphide ores which formed the basis of the European Bronze industry (Novotna, 1973)" (p. 165).
- Early 3rd millennium: local sources exploited north/west of Carpathians; copper battle-axes, sheet ornaments. "A mould for one of these from a site near Salzburg, Austria (Müller-Karpe, 1974) suggests that the ore sources of this area... were already being exploited" (p. 166).
- Beaker phase increase: "the Bell Beaker phase, however, c. 2500-2200 BC, sees a marked increase in the quantity of metal in the archaeological record of temperate Europe west of the Carpathian Basin. Copper daggers were regularly deposited in graves, as well as various copper and silver ornaments (Fig. 6.14). It was not until this time that local copper metallurgy was introduced to northern France, coastal north-west Europe and the British Isles" (pp. 167–168).

### Beaker Phenomenon (and Corded Ware Precursor)
- "The Bell Beaker distribution, which stretches from Ireland to Hungary and Norway to North Africa, in fact proves to be a very variable phenomenon (Lanting and van der Waals, 1976; Harrison, 1980). Only in north-west Europe does there seem to be an extensive Bell Beaker material assemblage, although even here much of the settlement material belongs to the later phases of local sequences and not to the beginning (Whittle, 1981). Elsewhere the Bell Beaker 'culture' is limited to the occurrence of finely decorated Bell Beaker vessels, together with a small range of metal and other decorative items, generally in burials, in the context of local assemblages" (p. 168).
- Status/ideology: "On the evidence of the grave associations in many parts of Europe, it seems likely that it is part of a new form of the expression of male status (Shennan, 1976, 1977a), but in western Europe it may conceivably mark a more radical break and the introduction of a more inegalitarian ideology than had previously existed in this area (S. J. Shennan, 1982b; cf. Whittle, 1981; but see also Burgess, 1980). The widespread Corded Ware assemblage, with its burial associations of cord-decorated beaker and battle-axe, which precedes the Bell Beaker in much of central and north-west Europe, seems to be a similar status-related phenomenon" (p. 169).
- "This discussion of the Bell Beakers brings us back again to the general question of inter-regional interaction and material culture similarity in the third millennium BC" (p. 167). Distributions do not map directly onto exchange of sourced materials.

### Early Inequality Markers and Social Organization
- Neo-evolutionary frames (Service 1962 chiefdoms via redistribution; Fried 1967 rank societies) used but critiqued for masking variation; "hierarchies may be of a variety of different types which it is not satisfactory simply to place at different points on a single unilinear scale" (p. 170).
- Fortified sites common but small and localized: "It has also been suggested that there is a tendency for indications of craft activities and items obtained through long-distance exchange to occur more frequently at such sites... but the evidence for this is rather more dubious. What is certainly true is that many of the defended sites... are extremely small and could not have contained large populations" (p. 171). Suggest "institutionalized hierarchy" at lineage-head level in many cases.
- Burial change marks differentiation: collective tombs earlier (not undifferentiated); "the change in settlement pattern associated with the appearance of the Corded Ware... one aspect of it in central Europe is the disappearance of any sites which might be regarded as central places. Corded Ware settlements seem to be very small in scale and undifferentiated" (p. 172). "the cemeteries and graves of this period... provide evidence for the representation of inter-individual distinctions by means of grave goods, and associated indications of some degree of ranking (Shennan, 1977b)" (p. 172).
- In east: varied practices continued; "the evidence from both burials and settlements... suggests the existence of at least some degree of social ranking to which the control and consumption of prestige items was relevant" (p. 172). E.g. Vucedol.
- "the tendency towards a mutual exclusion of settlement and burial evidence... is much less marked in eastern Europe" (p. 172).

### Wessex, Stonehenge Phases, British Monuments
- "The third millennium BC ceremonial enclosures of southern England are some of the most remarkable monuments from this period in the whole of Europe. By analogy with the most famous of all these sites, Stonehenge (Atkinson, 1956), they have been called 'henge monuments'" (p. 172). Durrington Walls, Avebury, Mount Pleasant; Silbury Hill of "similar scale."
- Labor scale implies centralization: "the monuments of the late neolithic, and the final phase of Stonehenge in the early Bronze Age, when the trilithons were erected, were several orders of magnitude greater in scale and must have involved some kind of centralized planning and control as well as a call on the manpower resources of a relatively wide area" (p. 173; cf. Startin and Bradley 1981 for lower but still differentiated figures).
- "In Britain... the appearance of the Bell Beaker burials which represents the introduction of inter-individual differentiation in terms of the differential consumption of goods at burial" (p. 172). "Subsequently there was a drastic decline in the scale of monument building since the new form of social system operated in a very different fashion from the one which preceded it. Society became re-oriented around the control and consumption of prestige goods" (p. 174).

### Vila Nova / Millaran (Western Med) and Social Complexity
- "In the area around the Tagus estuary in Portugal, where exactly the same phenomena occur in the Vila Nova de São Pedro culture at the beginning of the third millennium as in the Millaran culture of Almeria. Fortified settlements are known (Fig.6.24), such as Zambujal... and Vila Nova de São Pedro itself... as well as small hamlets, and there are the same elaborate collective tombs with rich grave goods including copper, ivory and ostrich egg shell" (pp. 188–189).
- "the richer ones [tombs] were located closer to the settlement. The grave goods included items of copper, ivory and ostrich egg shell, materials which must have been obtained by exchange... the quality of workmanship demonstrated by many of the objects is indicative of craft specialization" (p. 188, re Los Millares).
- Similar trends (fortified centres vs hamlets, burial differentiation) in southern France (Lebous); peninsular Italy more variable by region (Gaudo more differentiated with metal).
- Obsidian and north African exotica concentrated in the complex areas; "Such a pattern... is indicative... of a greater ability to realize it, and is associated with societies of some degree of complexity" (p. 190).
- "the end of the fourth and the early part of the third millennium is, in fact, the time of the most intensive flint exploitation in the western Mediterranean" (p. 193).

### Aegean Developments and Models
- Southern Greece/Aegean islands: marked population increase and dispersed settlement expansion linked to olive, vine, sheep (wool, manure, mobile reserve): "the domestication and adoption of the olive and the vine played an important role in this process" (p. 179).
- Some central places: "at third millennium BC Lerna... a large building called by the excavators the 'House of Tiles'... contained numbers of clay seal impressions" (p. 180); fortifications at Lerna, Troy II etc.
- "it is difficult to avoid the conclusion that in many parts of the Aegean at this time regionally centralized forms of organization were prevalent, with a corresponding social hierarchy. In Service's (1962) terms we are already dealing with complex chiefdoms" (p. 181).
- Competing explanations: Renfrew subsistence/redistribution model; craft/wealth model; Gilman exploitation via capital investment (olive trees); Halstead social storage model (food exchange against prestige items as indirect storage; scale and localization favor centralization in southern Aegean) (pp. 182–183).

### Mediterranean Contrast and Broader Conclusion
- "the ecological differences between Mediterranean and temperate Europe... As we have seen, the adaptations to them of agriculturally-based societies differed from an early date. However, in the course of the period from the late fourth to the late third millennium BC these differences increased" (p. 177).
- "Before going on to consider developments in the Mediterranean area in the third millennium it is necessary to indicate some of the interrelationships between the different changes and trends which have been outlined" (p. 175). Gilman's model has "problems... when it is brought up against the archaeological evidence" (expansion continued; light plough not major capital).
- "leap-frogging" migration postulated for Netherlands/north-west: "by the time of the Corded Ware period it may no longer have been possible for daughter settlements to be established close to their parent; relatively long-distance 'leap-frogging' migration may have become necessary, resulting in an unprecedented break-up of previously close networks of communities related by kinship" (p. 176). Favored ideology of incorporation and status symbols.
