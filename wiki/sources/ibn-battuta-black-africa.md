# Ibn Battuta in Black Africa (Hamdun & King, trans., 1975/1994)

Translated selections from the **Rihla** of **[[ibn-battuta|Ibn Battuta]]** (Abu Abdallah ibn
Battuta, 1304–1377), the Tangier-born Maliki jurist who travelled the equivalent of nearly fifty
modern countries across the 14th-century **dar al-Islam**. This edition gathers the two passages on
**sub-Saharan Africa** — among the most historically valuable in the whole *Rihla* precisely because
they are the **only eyewitness accounts** of these regions for the 13th–14th centuries.

## The East African coast (c. 1329 or 1331)

Sailing from Aden on the monsoon, Ibn Battuta describes **Zaila** (squalid, fish-and-camel-blood
stinking), **Mogadishu** (a great merchant city famous for its cloths; ruled by a "shaikh," Abu
Bakr, of Barbara origin, with an elaborate Friday/Saturday court protocol and a council where the
*qadi* judged *sharia* matters and the ministers the rest), **Mombasa** (a pious Shafi'i island of
wooden mosques living on bananas and fish), and **Kilwa** ("amongst the most beautiful of cities,"
its sultan **al-Hasan Abu al-Mawahib**, "father of gifts," a generous warrior who raided the
unbelieving Zunuj for booty and ivory). The account documents a thoroughly **Muslim, Arabic-literate
yet fundamentally African** urban world tied into the Indian Ocean trade in ivory, gold (from Sofala
and the inland "Yufi"), and slaves. See [[swahili-coast]].

## The Mali empire and the Sahara (1352–54)

The longer and richer narrative: the camel crossing from **Sijilmasa** via the **Taghaza** rock-salt
mine and the **takshif** guide system; **Walata** (Iwalatan), the first Sudanese town, and the
matrilineal, strikingly un-secluded **Massufa** Berbers whose gender customs scandalised him; the
forest route (shea/*gharti*, calabashes, fonio); and the capital of **Malli** under **[[mali-empire|Mansa
Sulaiman]]** — the **banbi** dais, the gold-and-silver court regalia, the interpreter-bard **Dugha**,
the **bird-costumed poets** (a pre-Islamic survival — the earliest external notice of the
[[griot-oral-tradition|griot]]), the **dusting/self-abasement** before the king, and the powerful
**queen Qasa** whose name was spoken in the pulpit. He closes with **Timbuktu** (al-Sahili's grave),
**Gao** (cowrie currency), and **Takadda** (the copper mines whose rods were exchange-money, traded to
Gobir and to **Bornu** under its veiled king Idris).

## Significance for the wiki

Ibn Battuta supplies the **inside, contemporary view** that the Arabic geographers (who wrote at
second hand) and the later chronicles could not: the texture of court ceremony, trade, religion, and
daily life across Muslim Africa at its medieval height. His famous, ambivalent verdict on Mali —
praising the **justice, security, and devotion to prayer and the Qur'an** of "the blacks" while
deploring their gender customs and "self-abasement" — is itself a prime document of how the Islamic
heartland *saw* the Sudanic world. He cross-checks and humanises the picture built from
[[ibn-battuta-black-africa|this source]] against [[niane-sundiata|*Sundiata*]] (the Mande inside view)
and the Arabic-textual reconstruction in [[ghana-empire|Levtzion's chapter]].
