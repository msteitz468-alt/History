# Homer, *The Iliad*

The foundational poem of Greek — and Western — literature: the monumental hexameter epic of the
**[[trojan-war-1250bce|Trojan War]]**, attributed to **[[homer|Homer]]** and crystallizing a
centuries-old oral tradition around the year 700 BCE. The first of the "Epic and Myth" group of
Phase-4 sources, treated as a **cultural primary source**, not history. For the poet, the oral tradition
and the Homeric Question see **[[homer|Homer]]** and **[[homeric-poems-as-history|the Homeric poems as
history]]**.

## Subject and Structure

The *Iliad* does not narrate the whole war but a single theme over some fifty days in its tenth year:
the ***mēnis* (wrath) of Achilles**. Its opening word — *mēnin* — announces it. Insulted by the
overlord **Agamemnon**, who seizes his prize Briseis, **Achilles** withdraws from the fighting; the
Greeks are driven back to their ships; Achilles' companion **Patroclus** enters the battle in his
armour and is killed by **Hector**, prince of Troy; Achilles returns in terrible grief and rage, kills
Hector, and abuses his corpse. The poem ends not with Troy's fall but with the night-time visit of the
aged king **Priam** to ransom his son's body, and the funeral of Hector — a scene of shared mortality
and pity across the lines of war. Twenty-four books, composed for performance.

## Themes

- **Mortality and *kleos*.** The heroes act under the shadow of certain death; what they can win is
  *kleos*, undying glory in song. Achilles' tragic knowledge that he has chosen a short, glorious life
  gives the poem its depth.
- **Wrath, honour and pity.** The plot turns on affronts to honour (*timē*) and the destructive anger
  they unleash, resolved at last by Achilles' recovery of human pity in the meeting with Priam.
- **The gods.** The Olympians intervene constantly, partisan and quarrelsome, their deathless ease set
  against the gravity of human suffering — a decisive shaping of **[[greek-religion-and-mythology|Greek
  religion]]**.
- **War itself** — its glory and its horror, rendered with unflinching detail and with the famous
  **similes** that open windows onto the peacetime world of farmers, shepherds and craftsmen.

## The Oral Tradition Behind the Text

As Graziosi's introduction stresses, the poem's **repetitiveness is the fingerprint of oral
composition**: the fixed epithets ("swift-footed Achilles," "rosy-fingered Dawn") are metrical
**formulae** that let bards compose in performance, as **Milman Parry and Albert Lord** demonstrated by
recording illiterate South Slavic singers in the 1930s. The Homeric language is an artificial amalgam
never spoken by any community, layering archaic (even Mycenaean) and recent forms — the linguistic
sediment of "many centuries" of tradition (fuller treatment on **[[homer|Homer]]**).

## Significance

The *Iliad* gave the Greeks their **shared heroic past, their gods, and a common canon of language and
value** underlying Panhellenic identity; it stood at the center of Greek education and was the model and
measure for all later epic (Apollonius, **Virgil**, and beyond). With the *Odyssey* it is the
fountainhead of the Western literary tradition.
