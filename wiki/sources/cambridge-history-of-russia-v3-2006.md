# The Cambridge History of Russia, Volume III: The Twentieth Century — Ronald Grigor Suny (ed.)

## Source Summary

The third volume covers the twentieth century in Russia and the Soviet Union: political, intellectual, social, and cultural history of trials and triumphs. It encompasses the ethnically Russian core and the non-Russian peoples of the tsarist/Soviet states and post-Soviet republics. Begins with early 20th c. revolutions, through 1920s, Stalinist 1930s, WWII, post-Stalin years, to decline and collapse of the USSR.

Contributors use new primary/archival sources post-USSR breakup. Aims to go beyond Cold War historiography divisions for new syntheses. Editor's intro surveys Western historiography of the USSR: from state/elite focus to social history (workers, peasants), cultural/linguistic turn, and access to archives enabling fresh readings. Notes shifts from totalitarian model (challenged by revisionists) to more empirical, and tensions between Left/Right interpretations. Unifying themes: tensions between nations and empire; oscillation between reform and revolution (from above/below); state building and collapse; modernisation and modernity.

The volume provides chronological narratives (Part I) and thematic depth (Part II), covering Russians and non-Russians/post-Soviet. Editor stresses that understanding present/future requires exploring experiences of Russians, Soviet Union, and non-Russian peoples.

## Key Themes and Historiography

Editor surveys historiography: from state/elite/intellectual focus, to social structures/class/revolution (1920s focus then Stalin 1930s), to post-WWII and cultural turn (rituals, memory, subjectivity). Totalitarian model challenged by social historians 1970s; neo-totalitarian reappears via Enlightenment/modernity critiques. Cold War divided East/West scholarship; post-1991 archives enable new syntheses, closer East-West collaboration, but polemics persist.

Key themes: tensions nations vs empire (Russians + non-Russians/post-Soviet); reform/revolution (from above/below); state building/collapse; modernisation/modernity. Covers violence in WWI/Civil War leading to authoritarian habits; Stalin's "second revolution" from above (collectivisation, industrialisation, terror, cultural control); contradictions of Stalinism (progress + devastation; police + enthusiasm; "most democratic constitution" + no freedoms); evolution to more benign society post-1953; Gorbachev's perestroika/glasnost as potential democratic evolution, but failure leading to conservative/fatalist views.

New sources allow empirical work on leadership, non-Russians, everyday life, sexuality, violence. Volume provides variety of interpretations for controversies.

## Volume Synthesis Note

The Cambridge History of Russia, Volume III: The Twentieth Century (Suny ed., 2006) is the collection's premier multi-author scholarly synthesis for the Soviet and immediate post-Soviet eras, drawing heavily on archives opened after 1991. It deliberately seeks to transcend the Cold War-era historiographical divide between "totalitarian" models (emphasising state terror and ideology) and revisionist/social-history approaches (stressing society, contingency, and modernisation). Editor Ronald Grigor Suny frames the volume around enduring tensions: nations versus empire (Russians and non-Russians alike), oscillation between reform and revolution (from above or below), state-building and state collapse, and the promises and costs of modernisation.

Subagent extraction across the full text (narrative Part I + thematic Part II + index) grounds several high-value additions to the wiki:

- Early 20th c. pressures and 1917 revolutions receive nuanced treatment linking WWI violence, fin-de-siècle social anxieties, peasant disillusionment, and worker radicalisation directly to Bolshevik success (Steinberg, von Hagen, Smith).
- Stalinism and the Great Patriotic War are presented with stark contradictions: rapid industrial mobilisation (three-fifths of output by 1943) alongside devastating agricultural stagnation, Red Army command purges that produced obedience at the cost of quality and 1941 disasters, ~25 million total deaths (with precise military/occupation/Leningrad breakdowns), and the GKO as the high-water mark of personal dictatorship (Shearer, Barber/Harrison, Gorlizki/Khlevniuk).
- Post-Stalin evolution and the road to 1991 are illuminated through both leadership transitions (Khrushchev's partial "secret speech" and rural reforms producing 311% real income gains for collective farmers yet undermined by Virgin Lands and Novocherkassk; Brezhnev's "social contract" of job security, subsidies, and controlled nationalities expression that masked stagnation and the disastrous Afghanistan intervention) and structural themes (Gatrell on economy, Kingston-Mann on peasants, Siegelbaum on workers, Engel on women, Smith on non-Russians).
- Foreign policy and identity receive sophisticated analysis: the Comintern's dual revolutionary/security role, the post-Stalin "discourse of difference," Gorbachev's abandonment of the vanguard identity that enabled 1989 Eastern European autonomy and withdrawal from Afghanistan, and the kto-kogo logic of NEP as class leadership of the peasantry (Haslam, Hopf, Lih). The index supplies precise citation anchors for verification (Land Decree, Leningrad Affair, Belovezhskaya Accord, revisionist historiography of Stalinism, etc.).

**What it adds**: Deep, archive-grounded coverage of non-Russian peoples, social and cultural history, foreign policy evolution, and the contingency/structural drivers of Soviet collapse — areas where earlier narrative sources (Service, Freeze, Riasanovsky) were lighter or more Russia-centric. It supplies excellent historiography sections and causal nuance for existing period (world-wars-era, cold-war, contemporary), actor (Stalin, Khrushchev, Brezhnev, Gorbachev, Soviet Union), event, and process pages (perestroika, dissolution-of-the-soviet-union, etc.).

**Tensions and context with other sources**: It complements Vol. II's imperial focus by tracing the persistence of empire/nations dilemmas into the Soviet period. Compared with Service's emphasis on personality and contingency, this volume stresses institutional legacies (nationalities structures, economic planning, foreign policy identity) and historiographical maturation. It aligns with the collection's known strength in post-1991 scholarship while underscoring remaining gaps for sub-Saharan Africa, South/Southeast Asia, and some post-Soviet specifics. Claims are treated as high-authority but attributed by range and cross-checked against other ingested works.

The volume's structure and source base make it ideal for ongoing maintenance: index citations allow quick verification of specific assertions on existing pages, and its thematic chapters provide ready material for future controversy or comparison pages (e.g., Great Man vs structural causation, or empire-to-nation transitions).
