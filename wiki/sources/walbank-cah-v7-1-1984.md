## Overview

CAH² VII.1 is the **structural backbone for the Hellenistic age** in this wiki. It
picks up exactly where [[lewis-cah-v6-1994|CAH VI]] handed off — the death of
[[alexander-the-great|Alexander]] at Babylon in 323 — and carries the eastern
Mediterranean and Near East down to 217 BCE, the year [[polybius|Polybius]]
singled out as the beginning of *symploke*, the interweaving of Mediterranean
affairs that would culminate in Roman domination (the subject of VII.2 and VIII).

The volume's design is deliberate: after a sources chapter and a narrative of the
Diadochi (Chs 1–2), it treats **monarchy** as the defining new institution (Ch 3),
then surveys the three great kingdoms one at a time — Ptolemaic Egypt (Ch 5),
Seleucid Asia (Ch 6), Antigonid Macedonia and Greece (Ch 7) — frames them with a
central analytical chapter on the **transformed polis** and the Hellenistic economy
(Ch 8) and on **science, war, agriculture and townplanning** (Ch 9), and closes
with three narrative chapters: Agathocles' Sicily (Ch 10), the Syrian–Egyptian Wars
and the new kingdoms of Asia Minor (Ch 11), and Macedonia and the Greek leagues to
the Social War (Ch 12). Roman affairs and Pyrrhus' western campaigns are reserved
for VII.2.

## Volume Synthesis Note

CAH² VII.1 gives the wiki its **Hellenistic spine** — sixty-four new pages turning a single
forward-link (`[[hellenistic-world]]`) into a dense, interconnected account of the century
from Babylon (323) to Naupactus (217). The volume's overarching argument, traceable across
its very different contributors, is **Édouard Will's structural thesis**: Alexander's empire
was "not a state but an artificial aggregate of at least three states," held together only by
his person, so that his death *necessarily* produced fragmentation. The two narrative
chapters (Will, 2 & 4) and the two analytical ones (Walbank on monarchy, 3; Davies on
society, 8) together show how that fragmentation crystallised into a stable **system of
territorial monarchies** governed by a new institution — spear-won, personal, charismatic
[[hellenistic-monarchy|kingship]], legitimated by victory and [[ruler-cult]] rather than
descent. The wiki now holds this as a connected story: from [[wars-of-the-diadochi-323|the
Diadochi]] through [[battle-of-ipsus-301|Ipsus]] and [[battle-of-corupedium-281|Corupedium]]
to the three dynasties — the [[ptolemaic-egypt|Ptolemies]], [[seleucid-empire|Seleucids]],
and [[antigonid-dynasty|Antigonids]] — and out to their rivals and successors
([[pergamon|Pergamum]], [[rhodes|Rhodes]], [[greco-bactrian-kingdom|Bactria]],
[[parthian-empire|Parthia]], [[agathocles-of-syracuse|Agathocles']] Sicily).

The volume's deepest **historiographical signature** is its source-poverty and its
consequent **revisionism**. Walbank's opening chapter (1) and the wiki's
[[hellenistic-historiography]] page establish the condition: no contemporary narrative
survives intact (the great [[hieronymus-of-cardia|Hieronymus]] is lost; [[polybius|Polybius]]
begins only at 220), so the period is reconstructed from inscriptions, papyri, and coins —
which gives the documentary disciplines, and the historians who master them, decisive
authority. Three of the volume's specialists wield that authority to overturn older
syntheses: **Turner** dismantles Rostovtzeff's "planned [[ptolemaic-economy|Ptolemaic
economy]]" and indicts [[ptolemy-ii-philadelphus|Philadelphus]] ("It was Philadelphus, not
Philopator, who bankrupted Egypt"); **Musti** softens the picture of
[[seleucid-hellenization|Seleucid hellenization]] (temple-states conserved, not secularised;
a society still "ancient oriental" at base); and **Davies** rejects the very idea of a
unified "Hellenistic civilisation" in favour of a [[hellenistic-polis|plural, layered
society]] under a thin [[koine-greek|koine]] top-layer. Préaux's verdict — *interpenetration
without fusion* — recurs from Egypt ([[sarapis]]) to Syria, and is now a cross-cutting theme
of the wiki's Hellenistic pages.

Three **threads bind the volume across its chapters**, and the synthesis pass has linked
them. (1) *The "freedom of the Greeks"* runs from [[antigonus-i-monophthalmus|Antigonus']]
Tyre manifesto (315) through the propaganda of every king to the war-aims of the
[[social-war-220|Social War]] — a slogan Will and Walbank read not as a doctrine but as a
*balance of forces* (the [[siege-of-rhodes-305|Rhodian]] case is the proof). (2) *The
transformed [[hellenistic-polis|polis]]* — its democracy hollowed into
[[euergetism|elite benefaction]], its identity fixed in the gymnasium, its kings worshipped —
is the social counterpart of the monarchy, and the Greek federal leagues
([[achaean-league|Achaean]], [[aetolian-league|Aetolian]]) are its political
[[greek-federalism|scaling-up]]. (3) *The westward magnet*: both eastern kingdoms are drawn
toward the Aegean, so the [[syrian-wars|Syrian Wars]] reshape Asia Minor, and Macedon's
contest with the leagues ([[cleomenean-war-229|Cleomenean]] and Social Wars) ends, at
[[battle-of-raphia-217|Raphia]] and Naupactus (217), exactly where Polybius said the world
became one — at the threshold of **Rome**.

**Joins with the rest of the wiki.** The volume is the seamless continuation of
[[lewis-cah-v6-1994|CAH VI]]: it opens on the day [[alexander-the-great|Alexander]] dies,
where VI closed, and the [[hellenistic-monarchy|spear-won kingship]] it analyses is the
fulfilment of the monarchical "alternative to the polis" that VI's Rhodes-chapter foresaw.
It deepens existing pages from other volumes — [[parthian-empire|Parthia]] and
[[bactria|Bactria]] (created from CWH IV) now have their third-century *origins*;
[[babylon]] gains its Seleucid afterlife; [[sparta]] its revolutionary endgame. And it hands
forward cleanly: [[antiochus-iii-the-great|Antiochus III's]] collision with Rome, the
[[philip-v|Philip V]] who turns west after Naupactus, and the [[ptolemaic-egypt|Ptolemaic
decline]] all point to **CAH VII.2 and VIII** — the rise of Rome and the absorption of the
Hellenistic world. The next backbone volume in Processing-List order is **CAH VIII**
(Rome and the Mediterranean to 133 BC), but the immediate sequel in series order is **CAH
VII.2 — The Rise of Rome to 220 B.C.** (already converted in `raw/Markdown/`, un-ingested).
