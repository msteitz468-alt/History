# The First World War, Volume I: To Arms — Hew Strachan (2001)

**Oxford University Press.** First volume of planned trilogy (Vol. I: 1914 focus; later volumes *No Quarter* for 1915–16 and *Fall Out*). Commissioned as modern replacement for C. R. M. F. Cruttwell’s 1934 one-volume history. Grew substantially in scope.

## Overview

Strachan argues the First World War was global from its first weeks, not a purely European civil war. The volume integrates a chronological military narrative of the major 1914 campaigns with thematic chapters (finance, industry, ideas) treated comparatively across belligerents and for longer periods where coherence demands. It deliberately reduces the Western Front’s dominance relative to traditional Anglophone accounts and treats Germany, France, Britain, Russia, Austria-Hungary, the Ottoman Empire, Japan, and colonial theatres on more equal terms.

Core themes: the contingency and incremental nature of pre-war planning; the failure of the short-war assumption; the global reach of even the opening phase (colonies, cruisers, jihad calls, finance); the “ideas of 1914” (holy war rhetoric, Kultur vs. civilization, national regeneration) that framed purpose more than precise territorial war aims.

## Historiography Notes (from the source itself)

- Critiques inter-war German memoirs and Liddell Hart for creating the myth of a pure “Schlieffen plan” betrayed by Moltke the Younger. Actual planning was incremental, contingent, multi-option, and heavily shaped by railway timetables and annual staff rides.
- Stresses that the “short war” assumption was widely held but not the sole driver; societies remained committed far longer.
- Global and economic dimensions were present from August 1914; colonial campaigns and cruiser warfare mattered strategically even if secondary in manpower.
- Ideas and moral framing (“holy war”, Kultur, regeneration) were as important to contemporary purpose as territorial war aims developed later.

## Volume Synthesis Note (added after full subagent reconciliation)

Strachan’s Vol. I establishes 1914 as the year the war became global, attritional, and economically total — not because leaders planned a long war, but because short-war assumptions collapsed under the weight of actual consumption rates, logistics limits, and command frictions. The volume’s core contribution is its demonstration that operational plans (incremental, contingent, mythologised after the fact) were disconnected from grand-strategic, financial, and industrial reality from the first weeks. The “ideas of 1914” (holy war rhetoric, national regeneration, moral framing) provided the emotional and ideological fuel that turned initial mobilisation into sustained commitment. By treating the Western, Eastern, naval, colonial, Ottoman, and economic fronts in parallel rather than as afterthoughts, the book supplies the operational and structural spine for the warfare hub’s 1914 coverage and the period page’s global character. It complements the Cambridge History of the First World War (already held) by supplying the detailed campaign mechanics and planning critique the thematic volumes deliberately de-emphasise. No major internal contradictions with other ingested WWI sources (Millett/Murray military effectiveness framework, Lupfer tactical learning); instead supplies the narrative foundation those works analyse at higher levels of abstraction. ## Range 02 Extraction

**Chapter 2 "Willingly to War" (lines ~5065–7656).** Focus: war enthusiasm and popular responses; the collapse of the Second International and socialist parties' turn to national defence; intellectuals' imaginings of war as personal test, regeneration, and moral necessity; variation by class, region, and country. ### War Enthusiasm (core claims)

- Popular enthusiasm did not cause the war but was required to condone it once the statesmen had decided: 'Popular enthusiasm might not cause war, but it certainly needed to condone it.' (early in 'War Enthusiasm')
- The distinction between pre-outbreak feelings and post-outbreak reactions was crucial in 1914; hindsight risks making analysis mawkish or reducing enthusiasm to a 'myth' of posed photographs.
- Many contemporaries experienced the outbreak as an epochal break: Friedrich Meinecke described 1 August 1914 as 'a new historical epoch for the world'; writing in 1944 he still called 3 August 'one of the most beautiful moments of my life, which even now pours into my soul with a surprising suddenness the deepest trust in our nation and the highest joy.' (in 'War Enthusiasm')
- Adolf Hitler later recalled sinking 'to my knees and thanked heaven from an overflowing heart that it had granted me the good fortune to be alive at such a time.'
- The response generated a sense of fraternity that temporarily erased internal divisions. Stefan Zweig (Vienna): 'a rushing feeling of fraternity. Strangers spoke to one another in the streets, people who had avoided each other for years shook hands, everywhere one saw excited faces. Each individual experienced an exaltation of his ego, he was no longer the isolated person of former times, he had been incorporated into the mass, he was part of the people, and his person, his hitherto unnoticed person, had been given meaning.' (in 'War Enthusiasm')
- The same pattern appeared outside intellectual and capital-city circles. Louis Barthas (cooper, socialist, reservist, Peyriac-Minervois): brothers who had fallen out were reconciled; mothers-in-law and sons and daughters-in-law 'exchanged kisses'; neighbours became 'the best of friends.' 'The first effect of the war was, paradoxically, to bring peace.' (in 'War Enthusiasm')
- Initial responses were not primarily driven by hatred of the enemy. 'The general feeling in Paris was that the Germans were mad, not bad.' First French POWs in Germany were 'warmly received by German women, and plied with wine and chocolate.' Britons in Leipzig experienced 'no personal unpleasantness.' Official policy in Berlin censored films that might incite violence against foreign residents. (in 'War Enthusiasm')
- Xenophobia and spy hysteria rapidly appeared as the obverse of solidarity. In Germany the scare was officially promoted; 28 people were killed in patrols and roadblocks chasing alleged French gold couriers; Stuttgart police complained 'Our streets are filled with old women of both sexes in pursuit of unworthy activities.' In France economic self-interest fused with xenophobia (attacks on the Maggi dairy firm as a supposed German spy front). (in 'War Enthusiasm')
- Contemporary explanations invoked crowd theory (Gustav Le Bon) and the release of unconscious sadism or sexual potency. The nurse's role was to be patriotic and obedient, returning men 'fit to fight' rather than undermining resolve; mothers were praised for willingness 'to sacrifice their sons for their country.' (in 'War Enthusiasm')

### Socialism and the International

- By 1914 socialist parties had grown rapidly (2.4 million members in 1910 to 4.2 million in 1914); the SPD was the largest single party in the German Reichstag. A major plank for many was pacifism, but the Second International's record was one of repeated compromise. (in 'Socialism and the International')
- The 1907 Stuttgart resolution was a compromise: workers should hinder war by the most effective means; if it came nonetheless, work for its rapid end. A revolutionary rider (Luxemburg, Lenin, Martov) called for using war to hasten the end of class rule. The 1910 Copenhagen effort to specify a general strike was postponed; the 1912 Basle emergency meeting appealed broadly to pacifist elements with 'moral and even religious fervour' but lacked precision. (in 'Socialism and the International')
- On 29 July 1914 the International Socialist Bureau met in Brussels. Viktor Adler (Austrian) was defeatist: 'the ideas of a strike and so on are only fantasies.' The meeting remained optimistic; Jaurès compared it to Agadir: 'There will be ups and downs. But it is impossible that things won’t turn out all right.' Jaurès was assassinated the following evening. (in 'Socialism and the International')
- The International's analysis treated war as imperialist acquisitiveness, not self-defence, and underestimated the splits within its own ranks. Revolutionary left welcomed war as capitalism's final crisis; most reformist socialists had come to believe capitalism itself would moderate to avoid war. (in 'Socialism and the International')
- German SPD leadership was revisionist in practice. The party had grown through parliamentary and trade-union work; many deputies came via the unions. It was anti-militarist but not anti-military and favoured universal service in a democratic 'nation in arms.' The 1907 'Hottentot' election loss and later army law debates showed the difficulty of challenging nationalism. (in 'Socialism and the International')
- In late July the SPD initially condemned Austria-Hungary and opposed German support; mass demonstrations (up to ¾ million participants by 31 July) occurred. Haase and others still believed Germany was restraining its ally. By 30 July the leadership accepted war as inevitable and justified as defence against Russia. (in 'Socialism and the International')
- On 3 August the SPD parliamentary party met; 78 voted to approve war credits, 14 against. On 4 August the party voted as a bloc in favour (led by Haase). The decision was unconditional and not altered by emerging awareness of German strategy or possible guilt. 'The socialist party, reflecting its inner discipline and led by Haase, voted as a bloc in favour of war credits.' (in 'Socialism and the International')
- The Kaiser declared: 'I no longer recognize parties; I recognize only Germans.' Socialists who embraced the Burgfrieden often did so expecting constitutional and social reform (democratization of Prussian suffrage, state economic intervention) as the reward. (in 'Socialism and the International')
- In France the CGT and socialists coalesced around defencism. Jaurès wanted a preventive international general strike before war but insisted that if it failed the proletariat must defend the nation. The Paris conference approved his motion 1,690 to 1,174 (14–16 July). The CGT abandoned the strike call on 29 July. (in 'Socialism and the International')
- After Jaurès's death and mobilization, the CGT appealed for co-operation with the government. On 4 August Jouhaux affirmed faith in the justice of the French cause before Jaurès's coffin. All 98 socialist deputies supported war credits. Poincaré formalized the union sacrée. (in 'Socialism and the International')
- In Russia the three socialist groupings in the Duma abstained on war credits (8 August) but lacked local organization and power. Lenin (from exile) called for converting the imperialist war into civil war. In Britain Labour and trades unions offered little resistance once war was declared; the party executive later supported recruiting. (in 'Socialism and the International')
- 'Popular enthusiasm played no part in causing the First World War. And yet without a popular willingness to go to war the world war could not have taken place.' (closing of 'Popular Responses')

### The Imaginings of Intellectuals

- Intellectuals often framed war as a personal rather than purely national test. Rupert Brooke: 'Now God be thanked... who has matched us with this hour.' Soldiers were 'going into the totality of life.' War offered escape from 'opulence of peace' and bourgeois calculation. (in 'The Imaginings of Intellectuals')
- Nietzsche was widely invoked: 'it is the good war that hallows every cause.' War would drive out decadence: 'Let the storm come and sweep out our salons.' (Kosztolányi). Franz Marc called it 'a European civil war, a war against the inner invisible enemy of the European spirit.' (in 'The Imaginings of Intellectuals')
- Brooke's recruits were 'as swimmers with cleanness leaping, glad from a world grown old and cold and weary.' War was 'a deadly enlivening' (Rilke); 'a purging and a liberation' and 'a moral necessity' (Thomas Mann, October 1914). (in 'The Imaginings of Intellectuals')
- Avant-garde movements (Futurists, Vorticists, Expressionists) had already glorified violence and the machine before 1914: 'There is no more beauty except in strife'; 'a roaring motor car... is more beautiful than the Victory of Samothrace.' (Marinetti). War ended 'art for art’s sake.' (in 'The Imaginings of Intellectuals')
- Pre-1914 culture was saturated with apocalyptic imagery (Kandinsky, Meidner, Holst's Mars, Spengler). Many were surprised when the anticipated catastrophe arrived. (in 'The Imaginings of Intellectuals')

### Popular Responses

- Enthusiasm was more frequent in towns and among white-collar workers. 'The largest single occupational group in most armies was the peasantry, and the reactions of agricultural communities to mobilization were less positive.' Crowds were phenomena of capitals; hinterlands showed calm and quietness. (in 'Popular Responses')
- Crowds formed for news as much as enthusiasm; press and public had a symbiotic relationship that amplified the perception of enthusiasm. Demonstrations in Berlin 25 July were largely students and young middle class, drunk, singing patriotic songs. By 31 July they were older, from all classes, more serious. (in 'Popular Responses')
- In France rural departments showed only ~16% receiving mobilization news with favour, 23% with sang froid, 61% with reserve. In towns the favour figure was higher. The French army expected 13% defaulters; actual was 1.5% (many vagrants or Bretons). (in 'Popular Responses')
- Prior military service and conscription experience created acceptance: 'The widespread experience of military service already possessed by those mobilized in 1914 is perhaps the single best explanation for the mood of acceptance which predominated throughout Europe.' Veterans' organizations (2.83 million members in Germany) kept alive crown-and-nation values. (in 'Popular Responses')
- In Britain recruiting was initially slow then surged (174,901 in first week of September). Professional and commercial classes responded disproportionately (>40%); agriculture only 22%. 'Pals' battalions embodied urban civic pride and bonds of friendship. Gwynedd in north Wales showed low rates due to distance from press and parade excitement. (in 'Popular Responses')
- The rush of German volunteers was real but lower than propaganda claimed (143,922 accepted by 11 August; 308,000 by early 1915). Many were under-age students or older men. The positive reaction often cited duty rather than 'war enthusiasm.' (in 'Popular Responses')
- In Austria-Hungary enthusiasm (largely Viennese) ebbed once the war generalized to include Russia. In Russia 96% reported for duty despite rural riots; mood hardened later with victories. Belgium's outrage at the German ultimatum produced integration around the king and produced ~20,000 volunteers. (in 'Popular Responses')
- The common denominator was 'passive acceptance, a willingness to do one’s duty; enthusiasm was the conspicuous froth, the surface element only.' Ignorance of modern war's lethality (popular literature still used chivalric imagery) aided acceptance. (in 'Popular Responses')

Locator: Ch. 1, final paragraphs of The July Crisis.

Air assets poorly allocated; Moltke lacked direct reconnaissance. Locator: Ch. 3, intelligence/cavalry section.

4. **Communications and command structure left Moltke unable to control events.** "the information which Moltke did receive was frequently twenty-four hours old... Moltke conveyed his wishes by directives rather than by orders... each army, and in particular at various points Kluck’s, Bülow’s, and Crown Prince Rupprecht’s, was left free to act in a way that did not conform with the general situation or with Moltke’s own intentions." OHL hampered by Kaiser entourage too. Locator: Ch. 3, communications and OHL structure.

5. **Logistics of Schlieffen plan were fundamentally unsound; rail and horse limits exhausted the right wing.** "By the time of the Marne 60 per cent of lorries had broken down... Kluck’s 1st army had 84,000 horses. Nearly 2 million pounds of fodder a day was needed... When the battle of the Marne began on 6 September the key railheads for the 1st, 2nd, and 3rd armies were 136 to 168 kilometres behind their front." Horses and reservists worn down. Locator: Ch. 3, logistics and transport analysis.

6. **Manpower attrition and detached corps fatally weakened German right wing before Marne.** "By 6 September the German army’s field strength had fallen by about 265,000 men... two German corps had therefore been detached to cover Belgium... Moltke ordered these two corps to the aid of the 8th army [East Prussia]. ... a stage would be reached when the Germans had too few men." Locator: Ch. 3, manpower and detachments.

7. **Joffre's rail redeployment created numerical superiority on the left for the Marne counter.** "Maunoury’s army of Lorraine and part of Pau’s army of Alsace were brought west to form a new 6th army... A total of 163 trains were required... By 10 September a total of twenty infantry divisions and three cavalry divisions had been moved by rail from the French right to the centre or left. On 23 August the three armies constituting the German right wing could muster 24.5 divisions against 17.5 allied divisions: on 6 September they confronted forty-one allied divisions." Locator: Ch. 3, THE BATTLE OF THE MARNE (Joffre plan and redeployment).

8. **Kluck's independent decision to turn south-east after Guise opened the fatal gap.** "Rather than allowing gaps to appear in their line in their efforts to achieve a full envelopment, the Germans were opting to close up... Kluck was understandably confused... He decided to go on. On 3 September his advanced units began to cross the River Marne." Gap between 1st and 2nd armies exploited. Locator: Ch. 3, Kluck decisions post-Guise.

9. **Joffre and Gallieni converged on flank attack plan; BEF cooperation secured by Kitchener and d’Esperey replacement.** "Independently but simultaneously, Joffre at GQG had come to the same conclusion... Joffre poured oil on these wounds by replacing Lanrezac with the ebullient Franchet d’Esperey... At 10 p.m. that evening Joffre issued his orders. The 6th army was to cross the Ourcq... the BEF was to advance east towards Montmirail, the 5th army was to turn north..." Locator: Ch. 3, planning 3–5 September.

10. **Kluck's shift of two corps north to the Ourcq against Maunoury opened the gap the BEF entered.** "The effect of Kluck’s move was to open a gap between the 1st and 2nd German armies... The BEF’s advance had begun to threaten the 1st army as well as the 2nd... Joffre’s line was echeloned back from the left." Taxis legend minor; real pressure from Maunoury. Locator: Ch. 3, Ourcq fighting and gap.

11. **Hentsch's mission authorised retreat; Bülow's decision and Kluck's compliance ended the advance.** "Hentsch told von Kuhl that the 2nd army was defeated and being driven back. He therefore ordered the 1st army to retreat... Moltke decided to order a general retreat along the whole front. The German armies would both contract and fall back to the Aisne." By 13 September positions prepared. Locator: Ch. 3, Hentsch mission and retreat decision 8–11 September.

12. **Moltke relieved; Falkenhayn continued offensive spirit but faced same constraints.** "On the evening of 14 September von Lyncker called on Moltke and conveyed to him the Kaiser’s command that he report himself sick... Falkenhayn’s inclination... was now no longer to use the 6th army to achieve a breakthrough round Verdun but to resurrect Schlieffen’s plan for envelopment by pushing it wide, to the right wing." Locator: Ch. 3, FROM THE AISNE TO THE YSER (succession).

13. **"Race to the sea" was mutual outflanking attempts; Belgians held Yser by inundation.** "On 27 October, the lock gates at Nieuport were opened at high tide, so that the sea flooded the area between the Yser and the embankment... Thereafter the floodwater blocked the Germans’ path; the allied left flank was secure." Belgians suffered heavily but held with French/ British aid. Locator: Ch. 3, Yser battle and inundation.

14. **First Ypres exhausted both sides and fixed the line; transition to position warfare.** "Total German losses at Ypres were in the region of 80,000... The BEF’s losses for the war up to 30 November, 89,964, exceeded the establishment of the original seven divisions, and 54,105 of them had fallen around Ypres... On 25 November he [Falkenhayn] ordered the German armies in the west to form defensive positions, and to hold the ground which they had already conquered." Trenches adopted to enable future mobile ops, not as end in themselves. Locator: Ch. 3, Ypres and conclusion of mobile phase.

15. **The Marne was a decisive French victory because the Germans had neither fixed nor destroyed their opponents.** "The Marne was a French victory. The French had to manoeuvre in order to win; they had to fight in order to check the Germans... Strategically and operationally the Marne was a truly decisive battle in the Napoleonic sense... Germany had failed to secure the quick victory on which its war plan rested. From now on it was committed to a war on two fronts." Locator: Ch. 3, THE BATTLE OF THE MARNE and overall assessment.

## Range 03 Extraction

Extracted exclusively from cache `/tmp/strachan-ranges/range-03-ch3a.txt` (lines ~7657–10050 — Chapter 3 "The Western Front in 1914": War Plans section + Battle of the Frontiers). All claims grounded with direct verbatim quotes (or extremely close paraphrase) + locator from this range only. Focus: German war planning reality (Schlieffen myth vs incremental evolution), Moltke the Younger modifications, elder Moltke thinking, French Plan XVII and Joffre's decisions, Belgian neutrality dilemmas, opening battles of the Frontiers (Alsace-Lorraine, Ardennes, Sambre/Charleroi, Mons, Le Cateau).

1. **No single fixed 'Schlieffen plan'; planning was incremental, contingent on staff rides/war games/railways/diplomacy, with multiple options.** "Whatever was planned for 1905 was specific to that year; it was an incremental evolution from the plans of previous years, and it was not a definitive statement... There was no single solution, no one plan." The December 1905 memorandum was a private post-retirement paper, partly for army expansion advocacy, not an operations order. Locator: Ch. 3, WAR PLANS (early section on historiographical myth and planning process).

2. **Elder Moltke anticipated long wars and favoured counter-offensive or east-first options; not quick west-first decisive victory.** "The protracted French resistance of 1871 suggested to him that a victorious campaign in the west could not be settled sufficiently quickly... he ominously referred to the historical analogies of a war lasting seven or even thirty years." Preferred riding French offensive then counter-attack or defend west while offensive east. Locator: Ch. 3, WAR PLANS (Moltke the elder section).

3. **Schlieffen shifted to west-first concentration and flank envelopment (via Belgium) to avoid French fixed defences, but staff rides repeatedly failed to produce decisive victory.** "A general staff study of 1895 concluded that a German attack in the direction of Nancy had no prospect of achieving a quick, decisive victory. Schlieffen’s answer was not to confront France’s fixed defences, but to go round them." 1904 rides produced French victories; right wing often insufficient. "Nothing in these exercises suggested that Schlieffen had devised a solution to Germany’s two-front dilemma." Locator: Ch. 3, WAR PLANS (Schlieffen evolution 1890s-1905).

4. **Schlieffen's 1905 memorandum took risks to extreme (massive right wing through Belgium) and was untested in games; relied on assumptions of French passivity and Belgian alignment with Germany.** "Such an action requires a focused leader, who has an iron character and who possesses a determined will to win." In last 1905 war game focused on Russia first. Locator: Ch. 3, WAR PLANS (1905 memorandum and final war game).

5. **Schlieffen exploited selective military history (Cannae over Leuthen) to justify envelopment; ignored that Cannae was tactical and Hannibal lost the war overall.** "At Cannae Hannibal had left his centre weak... But there were methodological problems here too. Hannibal’s success lay in the realm of battlefield tactics; the fire-effect of modern armies might prevent the closing on the centre..." "Schlieffen’s use of history was a bogus intellectualism." Locator: Ch. 3, WAR PLANS (Schlieffen and military history).

6. **Moltke the Younger was fatalistic and pessimistic, saw limits of plans surviving contact, anticipated long war; not dogmatic like Schlieffen.** "In my opinion... it is in any case very difficult, if not impossible, to picture now what form a modern war in Europe would take... no man, I think, can know in advance." Told Conrad in May 1914: "I will do what I can. We are not superior to the French." Locator: Ch. 3, WAR PLANS (Moltke character and views).

7. **Moltke modified deployment: strengthened left wing (added 7th army for Alsace/Lorraine), narrowed right (respected Dutch neutrality, targeted Liège immediately), incorporated French offensive possibilities; created multiple options rather than one categorical plan.** Balance shifted from 7:1 to 3:1 right:left. "Moltke’s plan had at least two different options—the one adumbrated by Schlieffen, and the other in which an advance through Belgium could create the conditions for success elsewhere, probably in Lorraine." Liège seizure by day 3 was critical due to no Maastricht appendix. Locator: Ch. 3, WAR PLANS (Moltke modifications 1906-1914).

8. **French Plan XVII was incremental mobilization/deployment plan, not purely offensive doctrine; Joffre flexible on timing but ultimately committed to attacks in Lorraine/Ardennes despite Belgian intelligence.** "plan XVII... It was a plan for the mobilization, concentration, and deployment of the French armies." Joffre ruled out pre-emptive Belgian breach; focused on eastern frontier passes. Underestimated German use of reserves and full Belgian swing. "Joffre’s neglect of this conclusion [2eme bureau on reserves] ... was only in part a consequence of the deep division between the two departments." Locator: Ch. 3, WAR PLANS (French plans XV-XVII, Joffre).

9. **Belgian neutrality policy led to military unpreparedness and confusion; field army improvised, poorly officered, under-equipped; concentration debates between king/staff delayed effective defence.** "the view of the French military attache on 1 May 1914 was that 'the Belgian army could not be mobilized in 1914, neither in its new form which lacks men, cadres, staffs, material, nor its old form which has been smashed; it will have to improvise a temporary organization'." Liège garrison (reinforced by 3rd div) delayed Germans 11 days instead of 2. Locator: Ch. 3, WAR PLANS + BATTLE OF THE FRONTIERS (Belgium section).

10. **Battle of the Frontiers opened with French offensive in Alsace-Lorraine (Mulhouse/Morhange): initial gains reversed by prepared German counter-attacks; French 1st/2nd armies retreated to Meurthe/Grand Couronné with heavy losses (e.g. Castelnau's son killed).** "The French 1st and 2nd armies advanced into devastating fire." Germans had superiority in battalions/guns on that sector. Locator: Ch. 3, THE BATTLE OF THE FRONTIERS (Alsace-Lorraine/Morhange).

11. **In Ardennes, French 3rd/4th armies attacked in columns/march formation with poor reconnaissance (mist, bad cavalry/air); Germans used howitzers effectively in broken terrain; French suffered catastrophic one-day losses (e.g. 3rd Colonial Division ~10,500 casualties at Bertrix).** "the 3rd colonial division... advanced in columns of four... caught in a devastating fire from its front and flank, and lost 228 officers and 10,275 other ranks..." French planned renewal but unsustainable. Locator: Ch. 3, THE BATTLE OF THE FRONTIERS (Ardennes fighting 21-23 Aug).

12. **At Sambre/Charleroi, Lanrezac's 5th army dithered on attack vs defence; Germans (Bülow 2nd army) secured crossings; Hausen 3rd army threatened right flank; 5th army forced into unplanned defensive battle without full entrenchment or Namur reinforcement.** "Lanrezac... wisely decided to fall back south of the Sambre to fight a defensive battle. But he had dithered too long." Locator: Ch. 3, THE BATTLE OF THE FRONTIERS (Sambre/Charleroi).

13. **Mons: BEF fought delaying action from canal line; Germans attacked uncoordinated in dense formations; British used prepared positions/firepower advantage initially but salient abandoned; Sir John French ordered retreat after Lanrezac's pullback and Joffre recognition.** "the BEF had the advantage of prepared positions... the Germans broke into smaller groups and advanced by rushes..." Germans ignorant of exact BEF location/strength. Locator: Ch. 3, THE BATTLE OF THE FRONTIERS (Mons).

14. **Le Cateau: II Corps (Smith-Dorrien) turned to fight exhausted/deliberate delaying battle after separation from I Corps (Mormal forest); 7,812 casualties; British artillery forward for direct support; German attack uncoordinated due to poor intelligence; allowed withdrawal south.** "Le Cateau cost II corps 7,812 casualties." "poor German intelligence allowed inferior British forces to fight a successful delaying battle." Locator: Ch. 3, THE BATTLE OF THE FRONTIERS (Le Cateau 26 Aug).

15. **By evening 23 August Battle of the Frontiers over; all Allied armies retreating; Joffre shifted to defensive attrition: 'Our object must be to last out as long as possible, trying to wear the enemy out, and to resume the offensive when the time comes.'** French/ British had been forced back from frontiers across Alsace-Lorraine, Ardennes, to Mons/Charleroi. German right (Kluck) continued south-west push. Locator: Ch. 3, THE BATTLE OF THE FRONTIERS (conclusion of opening battles).

16. **Retreat risked rout but French/British cohesion held despite disorder, rumours, and heavy officer losses; Joffre imposed harsh discipline (executions without trial).** "Retreat did not turn into rout. The resilience of the French army’s morale... argues for the positive functions of punitive and repressive discipline." BEF marched 320 km in 13 days. Locator: Ch. 3, PROBLEMS, AND SOME SOLUTIONS (opening of retreat phase).

17. **German war plans and French plans both suffered from intelligence failures, underestimation of enemy reserves/mobilization speed, and over-optimism about enemy conformity to preconceptions.** "The French got it wrong. They failed to realize the extent of Germany’s proposed swing through Belgium." Germans surprised by British entry over Belgium and by Belgian resistance at Liège. Locator: Ch. 3, WAR PLANS (French intelligence/plan failures) and BATTLE sections.

18. **Railway timetables and mobilization schedules locked early operational choices; pre-war planning focused on deployment more than tactics or final battle mechanics.** "the initial phases of a war, those of mobilization, concentration, and deployment, attracted the bulk of the planners’ attentions." "No order... that would have been given in combat was ever discussed, hardly even a real order for combat." Locator: Ch. 3, WAR PLANS (general staff focus and Schlieffen).

Extracted exclusively from cache `/tmp/strachan-ranges/range-05-ch4a.txt` (Ch. 4 "THE EASTERN FRONT IN 1914", lines ~12438–14400: War Plans + East Prussia operations, focus on Tannenberg and related). ### Austro-Hungarian Army and Galician Plans (WAR PLANS)

- The Austro-Hungarian army’s reputation was “honourable but ambiguous”: “Although, of course, the Austrians had been victorious in all the wars in their history,” wrote Robert Musil, “after most of these wars they had had to surrender something.” Conrad von Hötzendorff (chief of staff from 1906) “had never been under fire.” Peacetime training “had elevated knowledge over leadership.” Locator: Ch. 4, opening of WAR PLANS.
- Ethnic composition: 25% Germans, 20% Magyars, 13% Czechs, 9% Serbo-Croats, 8% Poles, 8% Ukrainians, 7% Romanians etc. Over three-quarters of regular officers were Germans. Regimentsprache embraced native tongues; Conrad spoke seven languages. “Such multiculturalism aroused distrust when fashion favoured nationalism.” Locator: Ch. 4, WAR PLANS (army composition).
- Manpower and training deficits: Law of 1889 fixed annual recruit contingent at 135,670; by 1900 Austria-Hungary trained 0.29% of population (vs 0.47% Germany, 0.75% France). 1912 increase to +42,000 per year came too late; “the decade lost could not be made up by 1914.” On mobilization ~2 million total but “only a quarter or a fifth of an infantry company... was composed of active troops; units had only one regular NCO for seventeen men.” Field strength ~48 infantry divisions — “approximately half the size of that of France or Germany; and not only was it smaller, it was also qualitatively inferior.” Locator: Ch. 4, WAR PLANS (manpower, reserves).
- Artillery weakness extreme: Common army division 42 field guns (vs 54 German); Landwehr/Honved only 24 (no howitzers for Honved). Field howitzer still 1899 model without recoil mechanism. “The Austro-Hungarian army was the most under-gunned in relation to its (already inferior) strength of any army in Europe.” Doctrine (1911) emphasized infantry acting “independently of other arms; the foot-soldier’s principal resource was his own determination.” Locator: Ch. 4, WAR PLANS (artillery).
- Conrad’s offensive doctrine and limits: Social Darwinist who believed “victory would fall to him who seized the initiative, even if his forces were inferior.” Yet army size “suitable only for a Balkan war.” Preventive wars against Italy/Serbia reflected both traits. “Austria-Hungary could only undertake war with Russia in conjunction with Germany.” Locator: Ch. 4, WAR PLANS (Conrad).
- Galician deployment evolution and reliance on Germany: Plans from 1908 northward thrust between Vistula and Bug to meet Russians piecemeal; later versions more east or west-focused. By 1914, after Redl treason and Romanian unreliability fears, concentration pulled west to San (left wing up to 30 divisions) with right on Dniester (10 divisions). 2nd Army reserve to arrive 21–25 days. “Austria-Hungary could not begin active operations in Galicia until after then.” Moltke warned of faster Russian mobilization (two-thirds by day 18). Locator: Ch. 4, WAR PLANS (Galician plans 1908–1914).
- Austro-German illusions and staff talks: Elder Moltke’s defensive-west + limited-east evolved under Schlieffen to west-first concentration. Moltke 1909 promised 8th Army in East Prussia would draw Russians; Conrad promised attack by day 22. Moltke’s 10 Feb 1913 statement: “the fate of Austria will not be decided on the Bug, but definitely on the Seine.” Conrad “elected not to hear him” and continued to expect support and a “super-Cannae” envelopment in Poland. “Each had formed a mental picture of the other which conformed to its own wishes rather than to the other’s intentions.” By 1 Aug 1914: “a German attack in East Prussia was most unlikely, there would be no offensive in Poland, and thus Austria-Hungary was the mainstay of the Triple Alliance in the east.” Locator: Ch. 4, WAR PLANS (staff talks, 1909 exchanges, July 1914 communications failure).

### Russian Planning, Command, and East Prussia Theatre (WAR PLANS + EAST PRUSSIA)

- Russian Plan 19 evolution and Cases A/G: 1910 version abandoned Poland for interior concentration (53 divs vs Germany, 19 vs Austria-Hungary). Revised 1912: Case A (Alekseev influence) major offensive vs Austria-Hungary (1st/2nd Armies vs Germany; 3rd/4th/5th south-west); Case G (Danilov) major vs Germany (1st/2nd/4th to East Prussia). Decision left to day 7; assumption Case A unless countermanded. French pressure (1911–13) for attack by day 15 drove offensive commitment even under defensive deployment logic. Locator: Ch. 4, WAR PLANS (plan 19, 1910–1912 revisions).
- Sukhomlinov and offensive spirit vs structural weaknesses: Sukhomlinov (war minister 1909) imbued army with attack (Suvorov/bayonet legacy via Dragomirov); rejected fortification upgrades on Narew/Vistula/Warsaw. But railways short (system single-track, short 2,000 locos/80,000 wagons); mobilization improved but staggered (27 divs west by day 15, 52 by 23, pause for Siberia). “Russia had a mobilization plan focused on Austria-Hungary, but a grand strategy directed towards Germany. However, it lacked the railways to enable this flexibility.” Stavka/Front split divorced supply from operations. Locator: Ch. 4, WAR PLANS (Sukhomlinov, railways, command structure).
- East Prussia geography favoured German interior lines: “To the north, Königsberg was a heavily fortified zone... The Insterburg gap extended 69 kilometres... The Masurian lakes presented a formidable barrier.” South front (Johannisburg–Soldau) neglected by Russians. “Schlieffen had recognized that the Masurian lakes created the opportunity for operations on interior lines, and that that possibility was supported by the availability of adequate road and rail links, in particular the line from Insterburg to Allenstein and Osterode.” East Prussia “was the exercise area for the German army.” Locator: Ch. 4, EAST PRUSSIA (geography and pre-war German familiarity).
- Russian 1st and 2nd Armies command and deployment: Zhilinskii (North-West Front) “unpopular and ‘an official of the cut-and-dried type’.” Rennenkampf (1st Army) experienced on German front. Samsonov (2nd Army) “physically unfit and professionally out of touch” after seven years as governor of Turkestan; best staff officers taken by Front HQ; chief of staff Postovskii nicknamed “Mad Mullah.” 1st and 2nd Armies: 30 inf + 8 cav divs (Russian div = 16 bns). Zhilinskii war game (May) stressed logistic order and completion of concentration before advance; 2nd Army slower via unroaded sector. Locator: Ch. 4, EAST PRUSSIA (command appointments and initial plans).
- Russian plan compromise and dispersion: Zhilinskii aimed to envelop Germans west of lakes (Rennenkampf north, Samsonov south). Stavka (10 Aug) directed 1st Army to draw Germans, 2nd to cut Vistula line. Consequence: “the sacrifice of the Russians’ numerical advantage.” 2nd Army five-to-six days behind; up to a third of its infantry/cavalry and two-fifths artillery lost to flank protection. “Stavka was effectively stretching its resources to implement both case A and case G, not one or the other.” Locator: Ch. 4, EAST PRUSSIA (Zhilinskii plan vs Stavka additions).

### Battle of Gumbinnen and Command Crisis (EAST PRUSSIA)

- François’ independence and Prittwitz’s response: I Corps forward at Goldap-Stallupönen; disregarded orders to fall back. 17–19 Aug contact; Prittwitz agreed to attack. 8th Army concentrated east of Gumbinnen, forward of Angerapp. Locator: Ch. 4, EAST PRUSSIA (François and decision to fight at Gumbinnen).
- 20 August fighting: I Corps attacked 3.30 a.m.; Russians surprised but German flanks vulnerable. XVII Corps (Mackensen) arrived 8 a.m. after refugee-clogged night march; “the infantry came straight off the line of march into action without artillery preparation.” Russians entrenched with telephone-linked artillery/infantry: “One Russian regiment fired 800,000 rounds from its 3,000 rifles and eight machine-guns.” Germans in close order broke; XVII Corps ~8,000 casualties out of 30,000 by 5 p.m. I Reserve Corps achieved local success but missed intended flank. “Three separate corps battles had resulted in incomplete victories on the flanks and defeat in the centre.” Locator: Ch. 4, EAST PRUSSIA (battle of Gumbinnen details).
- Prittwitz’s retreat order and Hoffmann’s counter-proposal: Aerial report placed Samsonov at Mlawa. Prittwitz ordered break-off and fall back to Vistula. Hoffmann argued it was “too late to retreat to the Vistula. Samsonov would get there first.” Proposed: rail François’ I Corps to XX Corps’ right to strike Samsonov’s left; XVII and I Reserve march to support on right (Osterode concentration screened by lakes). “In Hoffman’s advice, given and adopted on the evening of 20 August, lay the manoeuvre which enabled the victory at Tannenberg.” Hoffmann acknowledged: the battle developed “entirely by itself, not as a result of a master plan.” Locator: Ch. 4, EAST PRUSSIA (20 Aug evening decisions, Hoffmann quote).

### Hindenburg/Ludendorff Assumption and Russian 2nd Army Collapse (EAST PRUSSIA)

- Command change: Prittwitz’s Vistula retreat impression (via bad line) caused Moltke loss of faith. Ludendorff (ex-operations section) appointed chief of staff 22 Aug; Hindenburg (retired 1911) confirmed commander. They met 4 a.m. 23 Aug at Hannover. “Because the outline of the Tannenberg manoeuvre was already in place, it is easy to diminish the role played... But they were its executors.” Ludendorff issued orders directly to corps; confirmed I Corps move, rested eastern corps 23 Aug to clarify Rennenkampf. Hindenburg “to steady the nerves of his chief of staff.” Locator: Ch. 4, EAST PRUSSIA (22–23 Aug appointments and Ludendorff/Hindenburg contributions).
- Russian 2nd Army (Samsonov) advance failures: Supply penalty heavier than for Rennenkampf. Lateral railway behind front; single-track crossing lines; roads sandy. Units incomplete; four corps from different districts; “shared lines of communication, meaning that one corps tended to receive too much and another too little.” By 22 Aug “all units had marched for ten to twelve hours each day for the past week, and yet had only just crossed the frontier.” Requisition failed. Comms: two radios (clear text); 25 telephones, 130 km wire; “the army’s internal communications had collapsed by 20 August.” No direct contact with Rennenkampf; messages to Zhilinskii via Warsaw car. No advance guard; cavalry “failed miserably.” False assumptions persisted: Germans fleeing to Vistula; Rennenkampf still advancing. On 23 Aug front widened to 96 km while shedding II Corps and leaving I Corps at Soldau — “strength fell to just over three corps.” Locator: Ch. 4, EAST PRUSSIA (Samsonov logistics, communications, intelligence, 23 Aug decisions).
- Rennenkampf halt and German clarity: Consistent with May 1914 war game (danger of too-fast advance). Rennenkampf army low on ammunition after Gumbinnen. By 23 Aug “all the Germans facing 1st army, with the exception of a single cavalry division, had gone.” Locator: Ch. 4, EAST PRUSSIA (aerial reconnaissance 21–23 Aug, Rennenkampf passivity).

### Initial Contact and Encirclement Setup (24 August onward)

- 23–24 August: XV Corps (Samsonov centre) contacted German XX Corps (Scholtz) on Orlau-Frankenau. “A fierce frontal battle developed on 24 August, with Hindenburg and Ludendorff telling Scholtz to hold well...” I Corps en route by rail to XX’s right (Samsonov left); XVII and I Reserve directed south-west (to Samsonov right). 8th Army now clear its objective was Samsonov. “The 8th army was able to meet first Rennenkampf and then Samsonov, and to have a local superiority in manpower on both occasions.” Locator: Ch. 4, EAST PRUSSIA (23–24 Aug contact; force concentration on 2nd Army).

### Naval Preparations and the Shift to Distant Blockade

- The cabinet in late July/early August 1914 opted for what it thought was a naval war; naval and military strategies were complementary even if independent. Hankey to Esher 31 July: ‘The great question as to whether we shall do what our War Office friends want or not is, I believe, quite undecided, and it must be settled at the Cabinet and not here.’ Locator: PREPARING FOR WAR.
- By 1914 annual naval spending stood at £48.7 million (rising to £51.5 million in 1914–15); the navy consumed a quarter of the total tax yield. One-sixth of the total British workforce (per exaggerated calculation) dependent on naval contracts. Locator: PREPARING FOR WAR.
- Fisher’s 1904–5 redistribution concentrated the fleet in home waters against Germany as the only potential foe; by 1909 Britain was on a one-power standard (Germany plus 60 per cent). The Entente allowed halving the Mediterranean Fleet. Locator: PREPARING FOR WAR.
- The navy’s operational planning was confused by personal feuds (Fisher vs Beresford), frequent changes of leadership (Wilson, Bridgeman, Battenberg, Churchill as First Lord), and lack of a proper staff. Fisher treated the navy’s justification as deterrence by existence rather than battle planning. Locator: PREPARING FOR WAR.
- Close blockade plans (seizure of Heligoland as forward base, observational line, etc.) were rejected due to submarine, mine, and torpedo threats; by 1913–14 distant blockade (exploiting Britain’s position athwart German exits) had become the formal plan, though still seen as offensive means to bring the German fleet to action. Locator: planning sections (e.g. around 1912–1913 discussions, Callaghan, war staff).
- Distant blockade was endorsed by default after December 1913 conference fluctuations; bases at Cromarty/Rosyth inadequate for Grand Fleet in bad weather. Blockade remained primarily a defensive means to an offensive end. Locator: planning sections.

### Heligoland Bight Action (28 August 1914)

- Keyes and Tyrwhitt’s Harwich Force (light cruisers, destroyers, submarines) planned dawn attack on German patrols in the Bight to drive them out for larger units. Churchill adopted it; Sturdee retimed to target only day patrols; Beatty’s battle cruisers and Goodenough’s light cruisers added but coordination poor (Tyrwhitt’s ships told only his cruisers would be big units present). Locator: THE WAR IN NORTHERN WATERS (Heligoland Bight section).
- Action: German patrols weak due to tide; British confusion (Keyes mistook Goodenough for enemy, submarine attack on Southampton, wireless issues on Arethusa). Beatty entered at high speed. German light cruisers uncoordinated; Mainz sunk (hit 200–300 times), Köln and Ariadne sunk by Lion. Moltke and Von der Tann sortied but ordered back by Ingenohl/Hipper. Three German light cruisers and one torpedo boat sunk; no British losses. Locator: 28 August action narrative.
- Significance: Timely fillip contrasting land retreats; established moral superiority at sea. Exposed British command/staff failures (signalling, coordination) and gunnery/shell defects (many British shells failed to explode or penetrate). Beatty closed ranges contrary to Fisher long-range theory. Neither side’s submarines performed as hoped. Locator: post-action analysis.
- German response reinforced caution (Ingenohl’s indecisiveness); British euphoria reduced urgency to fix faults. Locator: consequences.

### Raids and Coastal Operations (Yarmouth, Scarborough/Hartlepool)

- After Heligoland, Ingenohl pressured for offensive use of Hipper’s battle cruisers to draw fractions of Grand Fleet. On 3 November raid on Yarmouth (cover for minelaying); lack of British response encouraged further. Locator: October–November planning and Yarmouth.
- 16 December Scarborough/Hartlepool bombardment: Hipper’s force bombarded coast (first successful attack on English shores since 1667); covered minelaying. Ingenohl took High Seas Fleet out in support despite Kaiser’s restrictive orders. Room 40 intelligence gave warning but incomplete (assumed no battleships); British trap (Beatty, Warrender, Goodenough, Harwich Force) failed due to patchy reporting, bad weather/visibility, ambiguous signals (Goodenough misread order to resume position), poor initiative by commanders. Hipper escaped through gaps. British losses: 122 killed, 443 wounded (mostly civilians). Locator: 16 December raid detailed narrative.
- Both sides berated missed opportunities; Germans succeeded in raid without loss. British official historian condemned failure to bring enemy to action despite forewarning and contact. Intelligence integration poor (delays up to two hours); signalling ambiguous; Jellicoe tightened control rather than loosened. Resulted in moving Beatty’s battle cruisers to Rosyth. Locator: assessment of Scarborough raid.
- Christmas Day seaplane raid on Cuxhaven by Tyrwhitt further frustrated Germans. Zenker report (early Jan 1915) pushed for greater fleet activity. Locator: follow-on.

### Oceanic Security, the Cruiser Threat, and Pre-War Global Naval Policy

- British empire bonded by maritime communications; homeland dependent on oceanic trade (58% calories by sea pre-war). Fisher’s battle cruisers designed with global service in mind (speed, range over heavy armour for North Sea). Concentration in home waters aimed to prevent German cruisers breaking out to trade routes. Locator: OCEANIC SECURITY AND THE CRUISER THREAT.
- Anglo-Japanese alliance (renewed 1911) was primarily naval for Britain: allowed withdrawal from Pacific/Far East; Japan neutralized as threat. But Dominions (Australia, New Zealand) feared Japan more than Germany; Australia created own fleet unit (battle cruiser + cruisers etc.). Fisher fleet unit scheme for interoperability unravelled under Churchill’s focus on Germany. Locator: alliance and Dominion navalism sections.
- Germany’s East Asiatic Squadron (Scharnhorst, Gneisenau armoured cruisers + light cruisers) based at Tsingtao (acquired 1898) was the main overseas cruiser grouping; qualitatively superior to British China station. Cruiser warfare (guerre de course) seen as tool for inferior power to raise insurance/freight rates, disrupt trade, draw enemy forces away from decisive theatre. Germans prepared with wireless network (Nauen, Yap etc.), Etappen supply system, auxiliary cruisers, but no integrated colonial defence plan. Locator: cruiser warfare and German preparations.
- Cruiser warfare weakened by steam (fuel dependence), Hague rules limiting neutral ports, lack of bases. Yet valuable strategically for inferior power: one warship in Pacific could draw many. True prop was the High Seas Fleet itself. Locator: theory and limitations.

### Japan Enters the War

- On 1–4 August 1914 Japan initially affirmed neutrality per British request (limited to defending Asiatic possessions). Britain shifted 6 August seeking Japanese naval aid against German armed merchantmen/cruisers due to panic over Far East inferiority (only Triumph available). Grey sought to restrict Japanese action to China Seas/Asiatic waters to appease US and Dominions. Locator: JAPAN ENTERS THE WAR (opening).
- Kato Takaaki (Foreign Minister) saw opportunity to eliminate German influence in China and advance Japanese position. Overcame genro caution and domestic politics (navy bribery scandal fallout); Japan issued ultimatum 15 August (without reference to Britain/alliance), declared war 23 August. Claimed Germany threatened Far East peace; used alliance as legitimizing cloak for unilateral aims. Grey accepted despite misgivings. Locator: Kato policy, cabinet/genro, ultimatum.
- China declared neutrality 6 August but could not enforce; Japanese landings (Lungkow 2 Sept, Laoshan Bay) violated Chinese territory; Yuan powerless, declared Shantung war zone. British/Indian battalion participated in siege. Locator: China neutrality and landings.
- Siege of Tsingtao: Meyer-Waldeck garrison swelled to ~4,500 +90 guns; Japanese 60,000 men + heavy artillery + fleet support. Slow advance due to rain, deliberate siege methods (trench consolidation, counter-battery, night shrapnel, air bombing); Prince Heinrich Hill taken 17 Oct; artillery opened 31 Oct; armistice sought 7 Nov 1914. Locator: siege narrative and tactics.

### Seizures of German Pacific Colonies

- New Zealand force (1,383 men) occupied Samoa 30 August 1914 without fighting, screened by Australian squadron. Australia landed at Rabaul (German New Guinea) 11 Sept; wireless station at Bitapaka captured 12 Sept after minor opposition; governor surrendered 15 Sept (New Guinea + Solomons). Isolated resistance (Detzner) continued. Locator: south Pacific seizures.
- Japan’s 1st South Seas Squadron occupied Jaluit (Marshalls) 30 Sept (later abandoned temporarily); by mid-Oct naval staff/ministry subverted restrictions to occupy Micronesia (Marshalls, Carolines) for future US war bases and phosphates. Equator became de facto division with Australian/New Zealand claims. Britain could not protest effectively. Locator: Micronesia occupation and Japanese internal politics.
- British/Dominion focus initially on wireless stations (Yap, Nauru) and trade protection; territorial conquest secondary to European effort and local forces only. Locator: CID/Colonial Office principles.

### The Cruise of the German East Asiatic Squadron (von Spee)

- Von Spee (Graf Maximilian von Spee) kept squadron (Scharnhorst, Gneisenau + lights) out of Tsingtao to preserve mobility; assembled at Pagan Island (Marianas) 12 Aug after leaving Ponape. Emden detached (per Müller) for classical guerre de course in Indian Ocean. Spee chose south-east to South America for coal (Chile), space to evade, possible union with Dresden/Leipzig, chance to draw British forces. Locator: THE CRUISE OF THE GERMAN EAST ASIATIC SQUADRON (assembly and decision).
- Jerram (China station) lost track; British/Australian/Japanese efforts divided between Tsingtao blockade, wireless destruction, commerce protection. Spee’s concentration preserved fighting power but strained coal (18 days economical; vulnerable coaling). Emden achieved major raiding success. Locator: British response and supply issues.
- Coronel (1 Nov 1914): von Spee defeated Cradock’s squadron off Chile. Prompted Admiralty to dispatch three battle cruisers from home waters. Locator: context and outcome reference.
- Falklands (8 Dec 1914): von Spee’s squadron destroyed by Sturdee’s battle cruisers (Invincible, Inflexible etc.). News returned balance to North Sea temporarily. Locator: outcome reference (prompted by Coronel dispatch; destruction ended immediate threat).
- Overall: cruiser operations secondary in tonnage sunk but strategically valuable in forcing dispersion and raising costs; von Spee’s cruise exemplified the global reach and the limits (bases, coal, enemy reaction). Never again could German surface threat draw capital ships so favorably. Locator: strategic assessment.

### War in Africa – General Character (Strachan Ch. 7 opening)
- The war in Africa began and ended the global conflict: "On 12 August 1914, in Togoland, Regimental Sergeant-Major Alhaji Grunshi of the West African Frontier Force became the first soldier in British service to fire a round in the Great War. On 25 November 1918, two weeks after the signature of the armistice in Europe, at Abercorn in Northern Rhodesia Colonel Paul von Lettow-Vorbeck surrendered, the last German commander of the war to do so." Locator: WAR IN AFRICA.
- "As much from its outset as beyond its formal conclusion, therefore, the First World War was far more than just a European conflict." In August 1914 the belligerence "embraced the entire continent of Africa with the exception of Liberia, Ethiopia, and the relatively smaller colonies of Spain, Italy, and Portugal." Locator: WAR IN AFRICA.
- Flow of war reversed from 18th century: "in 1914 the flow of major war was the reverse of that in the eighteenth century—from Europe to the colonies, rather than vice versa." Locator: WAR IN AFRICA.
- "For the Great War in Africa, although the product of European devices and desires, was fought principally by the Africans themselves. In all, somewhere over 2 million Africans served in the First World War as soldiers or labourers, and upwards of 200,000 of them died or were killed in action." "never before in the history of Africa had manpower been mobilized on such a scale." Locator: WAR IN AFRICA.
- "Disease, not battle, disabled armies in Africa." "In East Africa 3,156 whites in British service died; 2,225 of these were victims of disease." Porters suffered ~20% death rate "similar to that of an army on a so-called major front." "The conventional wisdom argued that not only was the European more reliant on the maintenance of the lines of communication, he was also less immune to local diseases." Locator: WAR IN AFRICA.
- "The difficulties of supply, rather than the experiences of battle, did most to disseminate the impact of the Great War throughout the African continent." "a company with its attendant porters mustered about 300 men and on the tracks of the equatorial rain forests of Central Africa constituted a column 1,500 to 2,000 yards long." "Fighting in Africa was therefore predominantly an infantry affair, the machine-gun being the heaviest and most significant weapon regularly deployed." "Although fought between European powers for objectives that were also European, the African campaigns of the First World War bore more relationship to the nineteenth-century campaigns of colonial conquest than they did to the Great War itself." Locator: WAR IN AFRICA.

### Togoland
- "The first Entente victory of the war was the fruit, not of central staff planning, but of improvised action at the local level." Captain F.C. Bryant of the Gold Coast Regiment seized Lome and advanced on Kamina wireless station. "The railway and the road which linked Lome and Kamina were ‘parallel but not always adjacent’." Germans blew the station night of 24/5 August; von Doering surrendered 25 August. "The war in the German colonies continued for over four years, but its principal strategic objective was accomplished in its first month." Locator: TOGOLAND.
- German weakness and preference for colonial over imperial interests: von Doering "opted for the latter. No effort was made to protract resistance. Six out of the seven provinces of Togoland were abandoned without a fight." "Von Doering’s less-than-vigorous defence and his expeditious surrender were thus of a piece with initial hopes for neutrality." Locator: TOGOLAND.

### The Cameroons
- German plan (24 Nov 1913): defend from interior (Ngaundere plateau) not coast; "the stubborn resistance of the Cameroons was motivated not by any German desire to draw Entente troops from Europe... but by the fact that colonization mattered as an end in itself." Ebermaier (civilian governor) centralised economy and supply. Locator: THE CAMEROONS.
- "In the Cameroons both the British and the Germans reckoned they needed between two and three porters for each soldier." "In East Africa the distances were greater... The British recruited over a million labourers for the campaign." Death and invaliding rates enormous; "dysentery was responsible for half the porters’ hospital admissions." Locator: WAR IN AFRICA (cross-applied).
- Allied discoordination: British maritime focus on Duala wireless/port; French territorial recovery of 1911 cessions and more. Multiple conferences (Brazzaville Feb 1915, Duala Mar/Aug 1915) produced optimistic plans never executed on ground. "Command was divided over three governments... four governors-general, six independent commanders-in-chief, and eight column commanders." Dobell and Aymérich advances uncoordinated; Germans evacuated Jaunde before fall, retreated to Spanish Muni 15,000 people (incl. 1,000 Germans, 6,000 askaris). "Mora, undefeated but apprised of the retreat into Spanish territory, surrendered on 18 February." Locator: THE CAMEROONS.
- "The failure of the Campo and Gabon columns to cut off the German retreat was complemented by the delay in the allied pursuit from Jaunde." Provisional partition (Picot-Strachey line) split traditional lands; "The casualties were the tribes of the Cameroons." Final settlement "the product more of affairs in 1918 Europe than of the outcome of the campaign." Locator: THE CAMEROONS.

### South–West Africa
- Union divided: "South Africa was an exception" to domestic unity; Afrikaner nationalists (Hertzog) opposed fighting for empire; "for Boers to become the agents of British imperialism... was to the Nationalists both morally unacceptable and politically inexpedient." Maritz rising + De Wet, Beyers, Kemp rebellion; "De Wet’s personal dominance... gave his rising the greatest significance." Crushed by Botha (Mushroom Valley, etc.). "Only one rebel was executed." Locator: SOUTH–WEST AFRICA.
- German strength ~5,000 (2,000 regular + reservists); "the Germans suffered from a lack of tactical and operational cohesion." Naulila incident triggered Portuguese campaign in Angola. Botha took northern command; "Botha’s concept of operations rested on the mobility and horsemanship of the Boer commandos. Manoeuvre, envelopment, and speed were the essentials." Landings at Swakopmund, Lüderitz, Kalahari thrust; Gibeon, Karibib, Otavi. "The South-West African campaign was characterized by a maximum of movement and a minimum of casualties." Surrender 9 July 1915: "4,740 men... had agreed terms without a climactic battle." Union losses higher in rebellion than campaign. Locator: SOUTH–WEST AFRICA.
- "Botha agreed to terms which allowed the German reservists to return to their homes, German schools to continue to function, and the German civilian administration to remain in place." "By biding his time in 1915, Botha laid the foundations for South Africa’s own brand of colonialism in 1918." Locator: SOUTH–WEST AFRICA.

### East Africa, 1914–1915 and 1916–1918
- "Lettow-Vorbeck was indubitably a fine commander... But his reputation has rested not simply on... Beyond his own country, the Schütztruppen commander came to be venerated as a master of guerrilla war." Yet "Lettow was never consistently a practitioner of guerrilla warfare." "His memoirs contain no theory relevant to the guerrilla; instead, they again and again bear testimony to his desire for envelopment, encirclement, and the decisive battle." "contemporary theories of guerrilla war are grounded in ideas of national liberation; nothing could have been further from Lettow’s mind." Locator: EAST AFRICA, 1914–1915.
- Tanga (2–5 Nov 1914): IEF B under Aitken landed; "the first battalions began to come ashore; the unfamiliar process of disembarkation, carried out in the dark, left the Indian states forces exhausted." Aitken's attack 4 Nov disintegrated on left; Lettow committed reserves; British bugle confusion + loss of confidence led to re-embarkation. "Aitken had handed his adversary a major victory." Germans ~7 companies vs larger British force. Locator: EAST AFRICA, 1914–1915 (Tanga section).
- "The victory at Tanga made Lettow a hero. It gave him the authority to deal with Schnee." But Jasin (Jan 1915) "gross error of strategic judgement"; 15% losses. "In reality, East Africa was—particularly thanks to the tsetse fly—ideal country for the operations of neither." Locator: EAST AFRICA, 1914–1915.
- 1916: Smuts command (political priorities over military); "Smuts’s aim was to manoeuvre rather than to fight." "By the end of 1916 the conduct of the campaign would be the focus of public outcry in South Africa. Smuts was constantly reminded from Pretoria of the difficulties of raising men; he dreaded returning to the Union dubbed a butcher." Advances from north (Kilimanjaro, Kondoa Irangi, Pangani, Morogoro, Ulugurus, Mgeta/Rufiji), west (Belgians Tombeur to Tabora), SW (Northey); "the British crossed the Rufiji on 17 January [1917]... But German resistance was now hardening." Rains halted. "what Smuts saves on the battlefield he loses in hospital, for it is Africa and its climate we are really fighting not the Germans." Locator: EAST AFRICA, 1916–1918.
- Portuguese and other: "Looff’s resolute action secured the food-producing areas of the south." Wahle’s epic march from Tabora joined Lettow; Wintgens/Naumann long raids drew thousands of British/Belgians. "Naumann... had conducted a classic guerrilla operation." "His men had marched almost 3,200 kilometres since February." Locator: EAST AFRICA, 1916–1918.
- End: Lettow crossed Ruvuma into Portuguese East Africa/Mozambique 1917–18, evaded, sustained force until "On 25 November 1918... at Abercorn in Northern Rhodesia Colonel Paul von Lettow-Vorbeck surrendered." "the last German commander of the war to do so." Locator: opening + EAST AFRICA close.

### Africanization and logistics
- "The Africanization of the campaign began with the completion of the conquest of the Cameroons." Gold Coast Regiment at Kibata; Nigeria Regiment 5,000; KAR expanded to 22 battalions/35k by 1918. "Henceforth, Lettow was not tying down white troops that could be deployed in France and Flanders." Locator: EAST AFRICA, 1916–1918.
- Porters remained decisive burden: "12,000 porters were needed for every 3,000 soldiers." "The British forces on the Rufiji required 135,000, and eventually 175,000." German and British alike faced "trade-off between the porter’s own nutrition and the needs of the soldier." Locator: WAR IN AFRICA + EAST AFRICA sections.

**Extracted exclusively from cache `/tmp/strachan-ranges/range-10-ch10-ch11.txt` (Ch. 10 "Financing the War" full + Ch. 11 "Industrial Mobilization" to 1916 + lead-in material, lines ~34681–47776).** All claims grounded in verbatim quotes or close paraphrase + section locators from this range only. Focus: pre-war financial and munitions assumptions; 1914 gold standard crisis and national responses; financial mobilization mechanisms (Britain, Germany, Austria-Hungary, Russia, France); taxation vs. borrowing trade-offs; domestic/foreign borrowing; the shell shortage crisis and its relationship to the "short-war illusion"; creation and role of the German KRA; raw-materials and production challenges for Central Powers and Entente 1914–1916; persistence of short-war thinking; comparative industrial adaptation and its strategic consequences. Thematic claims emphasized per task.

### Gold Standard and 1914 Financial Crisis

- Pre-war gold standard regulated money supply and trade balances but was "shot through with national considerations"; 59 countries on it by July 1914; cooperation between central banks essential. "The system was therefore deeply inimical to the requirements of a war economy." Britain uniquely did not aggressively build reserves; Bank of England gold rarely >£40m vs France £120m. Locator: THE GOLD STANDARD.
- July 1914 run triggered by continental powers calling in assets via London; stock exchanges closed (London 31 July first in history); £120m of £350m outstanding bills owed by Central Powers or Russia unlikely recoverable. "Catastrophe confronted the London commercial banks." Bank rate hiked 3%→4%→8%→10%. Clearing banks squeezed credit and hoarded; "our system was endangered, not by the public running on the banks, but by the banks running on the Bank of England." Locator: THE GOLD STANDARD.
- Britain stayed on gold via Treasury notes (internal), moratoriums, Bank guarantee of pre-moratorium bills, and gold inflows from Empire (South Africa fixed price £3.17s.9d/oz) and US. Gold reserves recovered to £51.8m by year end; overall war increase 109% (210% incl. currency notes). "Britain remained on the gold standard." France and Russia suspended convertibility immediately. Locator: THE GOLD STANDARD; FINANCIAL MOBILIZATION.
- US: European withdrawals +$105m gold exports by end 1914; New York stayed on gold despite exchange pressure to ~$7/£ (par $4.87); clearing houses gold fund; dollar back to par by Nov on export revival. "New York sustained its financial prestige." Locator: THE GOLD STANDARD.

### Financial Mobilization Mechanisms

- **Germany:** Reich war chest (Julius Tower) doubled to 240m gold marks but "doubly absurd" (unproductive in peace; too small for war). Reichsbank suspended convertibility; 5bn marks short-term credit on treasury bills; Darlehenskassen created for liquidity and small change (1.5bn+ marks capacity, low-denom notes). "The process of German mobilization had worked a more fundamental revolution in the mechanism of German credit than was realized at the time." Note circulation rose sharply; inflation denied as commodity-driven only. No general moratorium. Locator: FINANCIAL MOBILIZATION.
- **Austria-Hungary:** Bosnian crisis had already eroded preparedness (gold cover to 45% avg 1912). Smallest note 50 crowns; rushed low-denom issues flawed. Bank act suspended 5 Aug; moratoriums extended repeatedly to end-1916 (25% tranches). Kriegsdarlehenskassen (max 790m crowns total) for business credit but limited inflationary effect; circulation growth +91% by year end. "Inflation over this period meant that many debts were effectively wiped out." Locator: FINANCIAL MOBILIZATION.
- **Russia:** Strong initial cover (~100%); suspended specie payments 5 Aug but gold reserves held ~1.6bn roubles. State Bank funded Treasury and commercial banks; deposit runs followed by recovery. "The determination with which Russia cleaved to the gold standard displayed a greater awareness... of its two distinct functions." Commercial lending slow to revive. Locator: FINANCIAL MOBILIZATION.
- **France:** Pre-positioned Banque de France advance of 2.9bn francs on treasury bills (post-1911 planning). Sweeping moratoriums (trade, banks, rents, insurance) from 1 Aug, many persisting to 1920 or late war; "the French economy carried an immense burden for the rest of the war." Banque carried deferred bills and advances; gold cover high but convertibility suspended. Locator: FINANCIAL MOBILIZATION.

### Taxation versus Borrowing; Domestic and Foreign Borrowing

- "The choice between taxation and borrowing... was at bottom a choice between taxation now or taxation in the future." Loans required future tax for interest/principal; equity argument favoured shifting burden to post-war generations. But heavy wartime taxation risked eroding savings and capital investment; also served price/inflation control ("an effective system of wartime taxation which reduced the purchasing power of the consumer"). Locator: TAXATION.
- Direct taxation inverted peacetime progressivity: protected wealth of rich for long-term investment; reduced disposable income of less affluent to curb inflation. Inheritance/death duties yields stagnant or marginal (Britain 13.8%→3.4% of direct tax; France down ~100m francs/yr). Excess profits duty became major revenue (nearly half Britain's direct tax by 1918; almost all Germany's). "The declining significance of inheritance tax was, above all, relative." Locator: TAXATION.
- Domestic borrowing: war loans/bonds across powers; Britain Treasury control over new issues from 1915. Foreign borrowing critical for Entente: Britain channelled US orders and credit for allies; gold shipments to support sterling-dollar rate (Britain sent £305m gold to US by 1917). "The Entente’s readier access to foreign credits is crucial to explaining the proposition that the war cost twice as much to win as to lose, $147,000 million as against $61,500 million." Central Powers denied markets; resorted to disinvestment ("falling real incomes and declining output"). Locator: FOREIGN BORROWING.
- US role: supplied goods and later credit; McAdoo and Strong policies; 1917 advances saved Britain from suspending convertibility. "The financial collapse of the Entente would have triggered economic crisis in the United States." Balfour mission 1917 stressed financial peril > submarine threat. Locator: FOREIGN BORROWING.

### Shell Shortage, Short-War Illusion Collapse, and Industrial Mobilization Onset

- Shell crisis universal by autumn 1914 but earliest/deepest for France (post-Marne). "Even as the shell crisis bit, a palliative was at hand. The shorter days... of Europe’s winter months curtailed the opportunities to fire." Pre-war stocks not neglected (France 1,397 rds/75mm; Germany 750–987 effective; Britain 1,500; Russia ~1,000 target) but "the manner of fighting was so different from pre-war expectation." "The shells crisis of the autumn of 1914 was emphatically not a manifestation of a belief that the war would be short." Locator: SHELL SHORTAGE.
- "Short-war illusion" widely held (3–9 months typical official; public even shorter: "back home before the leaves fell") but persisted post-Marne as "series of short episodes." "Not until 1917 would it become common to predict the war’s continuation in terms of years." Kitchener wobbled; Bethmann 3–4 months. "The short-war illusion therefore persisted throughout the war." It reflected wishful thinking more than coordinated analysis. Locator: SHELL SHORTAGE.
- Germany: Rathenau (AEG) proposed KRA 8–13 Aug 1914 after Moellendorff memo on blockade/raw materials (esp. copper); Falkenhayn approved. Centralized appropriation/allocation of raw materials for war industry—"deutscher Gemeinwirtschaft". Pre-war planning focused more on food/liquidity than raw materials; institutional barriers (liberalism, Interior Ministry). "The story of the establishment of the KRA is bewitching in its suddenness and in its completeness." Moltke had stressed sustained production over stockpiling. Locator: RAW MATERIALS, MUNITIONS PRODUCTION, AND THE CENTRAL POWERS, 1914–1916.
- Central Powers production limits: Austria-Hungary reached capacity by 1916; increasing German subsidy; shell output rose but heavy guns short (only ~5,000 of 13,300 needed). Germany protected until allies' demands grew 1916. Locator: RAW MATERIALS...
- Entente (esp. France): France lost NE industrial heartland (74% coal, 81% pig iron, 63% steel) but offset by expansion elsewhere (Rhône workforce surges; Loire steel +60–80%); steel imports matched then exceeded domestic; labour returned from army; Comité des Forges and regional structures. Output ramped (75mm shells from ~6–7k/day Aug to targets of 100k/day); new furnaces added. Britain and US supplied rifles/guns. Russia: War Industries Committees (fragmented, 7.6% orders by 1916); GAU and large firms (Putilov) drove 15x shell growth; rifles and heavy artillery chronic shortfalls. Locator: THE MUNITIONS CRISIS AND THE ENTENTE, 1914–1916.

**Chapter 8 "Turkey’s Entry" (lines ~27613–29704) + Chapter 9 "Germany’s Global Strategy" (lines ~29705–34680).** Focus: escape of Goeben and Breslau; Ottoman decision to join Central Powers; Turkey’s military capacity; German global strategy pivoting on Ottoman alliance, Holy War (jihad) proclamation, Caucasus, Suez/Egypt, Persia, Afghanistan, India. ### The Escape of the Goeben and the Breslau (Ch. 8 opening)

- "Shortly after 10.30 on the morning of 4 August 1914 Captain Francis Kennedy, commanding the British battle cruisers Indomitable and Indefatigable, sighted the two German cruisers, the Goeben and the Breslau." Goeben had bombarded Philippeville and Breslau Bône earlier that day; ships passed at 8,000 yards without exchange. (cache ll. 5-19)
- Goeben speed limited by boiler problems (three of twenty-four failed; average 22 knots); stokers died from scalding. British failed to intercept despite theoretical superiority. (ll. 22-38)
- Troubridge abandoned chase at 3.47 a.m. on 7 August: "he turned away. He was in tears as he did so." He had interpreted 'superior force' orders conservatively given Goeben’s 11-inch guns outranging his armoured cruisers. (ll. 86-89)
- Milne delayed; "the Germans’ escape rendered the actions of every British naval commander, with the distinguished exception of Kelly of the Gloucester, not prudent but incompetent." (ll. 134-137)
- "Souchon’s orders up until 2 August were exactly those reflected in Entente dispositions... But on that day Turkey and Germany formed a secret alliance." Souchon received orders for Constantinople even before attacks on North African ports. (ll. 218-223)
- "The significance of the Goeben and Breslau to Turkey’s decision to enter the war on the side of the Central Powers is perhaps more debatable than tradition allows. But what the episode does reveal is the difference in attitude to Turkey, and Turkey’s role in their respective strategies, enjoyed by Britain and Germany." Britain sought to restrict war to Europe; Germany to widen it. (ll. 253-273)

### Turkey’s Decision to Join the Central Powers

- Young Turk background: 1908 revolution restored constitution; 1909 counter-revolution led to deposition of Abdul Hamid; army influence dominant. Triumvirate Enver (war minister), Talaat (interior), Djemal consolidated power by 1914 after Balkan wars losses and Adrianople recovery. (ll. 403-572 approx)
- "In none of the key areas, therefore, had the revolution of 1908 reversed the decline." Economic subservience via capitulations, public debt (French/British control), trade deficit persisted; army politicized. (ll. 487-500+)
- "With hindsight, Turkey’s entry to the war was ordained from the moment it admitted the Goeben and the Breslau to the Dardanelles. The subsequent delay had been a product of Turkey’s lack of war-readiness and of Bulgarian neutrality, not of uncertainty in regard to its eventual policy." (ll. 1423-1427)
- "The idea that a foray by the Goeben and the Breslau into the Black Sea would mark the initiation of Turkish belligerence is the clearest indication of the significance of the two cruisers’ escape..." On 29 October Turkish fleet (under Souchon) raided Sevastopol, Odessa, Theodosia, Novorossisk. (ll. 1371-1421)
- Britain impounded Turkish dreadnoughts 29 July; this "was a gift to Young Turk propaganda." Turkey abrogated capitulations 8 Sept, raised duties 1 Oct. German loan of £T5m gold negotiated; first instalment secured Souchon’s Black Sea action. (ll. 1208-1369)
- "The Ottoman empire therefore entered the First World War through its own devices, and in pursuit of its own interests. It was neither the innocent pawn in Germany’s more ambitious concepts, nor the victim of Russian and British imperialism." Rational (Djemal) and emotional/pan-Turanian (Enver) factors combined; Russia declared war 2 Nov, Britain/France 5 Nov after Dardanelles bombardment. (ll. 1459-1516)

### Turkey’s Capacity for War

- Moltke March 1914: "'Turkey is militarily a nonentity!... If Turkey was described before as a sick man, it must now be described as a dying man.'" Yet army would tie down Entente forces; Dardanelles closed. But "the Turkish army was not a finely honed instrument well adapted for modern war, and nor was the Turkish economy sufficiently advanced to support it." (ll. 1520-1538)
- Officer corps split generational/political; conscription limited (Anatolian peasants burdened; Kurds/Arabs/Constantinople exempt); no common language (only 40% spoke Turkish); logistics/rail inadequate. (ll. 1559-1599+)
- "The army to which Liman von Sanders was appointed in January 1914 was a bewildering blend of the new and the unreformed." German mission focused on education; training theoretical. (ll. 1540-1557)

### Germany’s Global Strategy and Holy War (Ch. 9)

- "It was not the Entente but the Central Powers that broadened the war in 1914; it was not Britain but Germany that pursued a peripheral strategy..." Der Weltkrieg title reflected pre-war Weltpolitik and Moltke’s 30/31 July "This war will grow into a world war". (ll. 2113-2135)
- Von der Goltz vision: Turkish army threaten Caucasus to divert Russians from East Prussia; or seize Suez Canal from southern Syria to cut Britain-India. (ll. 2143-2151)
- Max von Oppenheim pre-war: in war with Turkey vs England "one may certainly expect an overall revolt of the Muslims in the British colonies..." Kaiser 30 July: "Our consuls in Turkey and India, our agents, etc., must rouse the whole Moslem world into wild rebellion against this hateful, mendacious, unprincipled nation of shopkeepers; if we are going to shed our blood, then England must at least lose India." (ll. 2157-2191)
- Moltke 2 Aug demanded revolution in India/Egypt, pressure on Persia; added Caucasus 5 Aug, Morocco/Tunisia/Algeria/Afghanistan 20 Aug; Zionism too. "In just over two weeks Moltke had drawn up a shopping list that embraced... Asia, India, Egypt, and Africa." But no men/munitions/logistics for execution; friction OHL vs foreign office. (ll. 2196-2224)
- "The confirmation of this policy came on 14 November, with the declaration of an Islamic holy war by the Sheikh-ul-Islam in the presence of the Sultan. All believers throughout the world were called upon to fight Britain, France, Russia, Serbia, and Montenegro." Translated into Arabic, Persian, Urdu, Tatar; extended to Shi’ites. Up to 270m Muslims; ~170m Asia, 50m Africa. (ll. 2434-2474)
- Turkish war plan 22 Oct included fleet attack on Russians, holy war, hold Caucasus, attack on Egypt (VIII corps, 6 weeks), possible Odessa or Balkan thrust. "Enver’s plan was, in truth, not a plan... Enver gave no attention to either of the major areas of vulnerability, the Dardanelles and Mesopotamia." (ll. 2392-2432)
- "Technically jihad concerned the spiritual striving... But the fatwas of November took 'the form of general mobilization'." Secular Young Turk agenda grafted onto Islam; Caliphate claim used for temporal authority. (ll. 2456-2466)

### Caucasus, Suez/Egypt, Persia/Afghanistan, India theatres (Ch. 9)

- Caucasus: Turks' 3rd army offensive; Sarikamish (Dec 1914-Jan 1915) disaster for Ottomans ("Turks lost 30,000 men to frostbite alone... total losses... 75,000"; Russians ~20k). "Sarikamish confirmed the image of Ottoman decline." (ll. ~2889-3503)
- Suez/Egypt/Libya: "In Germany the planned attack on Egypt was a means to an end; for Turkey it was an end in itself." Turkish force 19,000 men vs ~70,000 British in Egypt; "logistical constraints... Not until 1918... would Turkey possess the railway communications..." Kress von Kressenstein reconnoitred; Bedouin volunteers; Egyptian nationalists fractured (Watanists vs Umma). Britain declared protectorate 18 Dec 1914, deposed Abbas Hilmi. Attack launched early 1915 failed due to numbers/logistics. (ll. 3505-3702+)
- Persia and Afghanistan: Enver claimed Habibullah (Emir of Afghanistan) ready; German mission (disguised as circus; Niedermayer, Wassmuss, later Hentig/Pratap Indians) sent to Kabul via Persia to foment invasion of India + revolution. Persia weak post-1906 revolution, divided into Russian (north) / British (south) spheres; "Persia was a country 'without patriotic energy and for the moment powerless'." Missions fractured by command disputes (army vs foreign office). (ll. 5158-5399+)
- India: "In 1914 India took 16.1 per cent of all British exports... India paid for this... financing two-fifths of Britain’s deficits." Indian army ~239k + 77k British; cost on Indian taxpayers; garrisons empire-wide (Egypt, Singapore, China). By late 1914 British garrison in India down to 15,000. Bengal terrorism (post-Curzon partition) intensified (36 incidents 1915); Defence of India Act 1915 enabled internments. Revolutionaries abroad (Paris, US, then Kabul provisional govt under Barkatullah/Pratap). (ll. 6089-6229+)
