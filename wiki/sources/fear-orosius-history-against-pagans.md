# Orosius, *Seven Books of History against the Pagans* (*Historiae adversus paganos*)

The **first Christian universal history** (c. 417), written by **[[orosius|Paulus Orosius]]** at the
prompting of **Augustine** as a documentary companion to the *City of God*. The eighth source of this
Phase-5 batch, and the fountainhead of medieval **[[christian-providential-historiography|providential
historiography]]**.

## Purpose and Argument

Orosius wrote to answer the **pagan charge** — sharpened by the trauma of the
**[[sack-of-rome-410|sack of Rome in 410]]** — that the abandonment of the old gods for Christianity had
brought disaster upon the empire. His reply, across **seven books** from the Flood to AD 417, is to
**catalogue the catastrophes of the pre-Christian past** — wars, massacres, plagues, tyrannies — in such
relentless detail as to show that earlier, pagan ages were **far more miserable** than the Christian
present. History, for Orosius, is **providentially ordered**: it moves through the **four world-empires**
(Babylon, Macedon, Carthage, Rome) toward the divinely favoured **Christian Roman empire**, in which the
birth of Christ and the *Pax Romana* of Augustus providentially coincided.

## Method

Orosius is a **compiler and apologist**, not a researcher: for the distant past he draws on Livy,
Eutropius, Justin, Tacitus and Jerome's *Chronicle*, often carelessly, and he bends his material to his
thesis. The work also contains an influential **geographical survey** of the known world. For his **own
times** — the barbarian invasions of Spain and Gaul, the Gothic sack of Rome, the settlement of the
Goths — he is a valuable near-contemporary witness, if a partisan one.

## Significance

However flawed as history, the *Historiae* became one of the **most widely read books of the Middle
Ages** — surviving in some 250 manuscripts, translated into **Old English** (under King Alfred) and into
**Arabic**, and supplying medieval Europe with much of its knowledge of antiquity and its very framework
for understanding the past. Through Orosius the **providential, universal** shape of history passed to the
medieval chronicle tradition and, secularized, to the modern philosophy of history.
