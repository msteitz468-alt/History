# David Morgan, *The Mongols* (1986)

The classic single-volume scholarly introduction to the [[mongol-empire|Mongol Empire]],
ingested for Phase E as the **source-critical complement** to May's world-historical
synthesis. Where [[may-mongol-conquests-2012|May]] argues the empire's world-historical
*impact*, Morgan disciplines the picture: what the sources actually allow us to say, and
how the empire actually worked.

## Structure

Eight chapters: (1) **The Study of Mongol History** — the *Secret History of the
Mongols*, Chinese, Persian, and European sources, and the modern field; (2) **Nomads of
the Steppe** — steppe society and religion before Chingiz; (3) **Chingiz Khan and the
Founding of the Empire**; (4) **Nature and Institutions** — the army, law, taxation,
communications (the *yam*), and the Mongol approach to government; (5) **The Mongols in
China** (the Yuan); (6) **Expansion to the West** — Russia (the Golden Horde) and Persia
(the Ilkhanate); (7) **The Mongols and Europe**; (8) **What Became of the Mongols?**

## What it contributes to the wiki

- The controversy **[[the-mongol-yasa]]** — Morgan's signature argument that the "Great
  Yasa" of Chingiz Khan, long treated as a foundational written law code, very probably
  never existed as a single text; what existed was unwritten customary law, *ad hoc*
  case law, and administrative decrees, later misremembered as a code.
- The actor **[[mongol-army]]** — the decimal military system, the great hunt (*nerge*)
  as training, the whole-population army, the size debate, and Morgan's thesis that the
  conquests turned on the army's **character, not its size** (and were limited by
  **pasture**).
- Enrichment of [[mongol-empire]] (institutions and historiography), [[golden-horde]]
  (the *basqaq*, Alexander Nevsky, the "Tatar Yoke" revision, Russia as peripheral),
  [[genghis-khan]] (the *yasa* and the army), and [[pax-mongolica]] (a skeptical note).

## Reliability and stance

Authoritative, source-critical, and deliberately **deflationary** where the evidence is
thin: Morgan questions the Great Yasa, treats inflated army figures with caution, and
quotes Franke's quip that the "Pax Mongolica is no more than one of those brilliant
simplifications that can serve as chapter titles for world history books." It thus sits
in productive tension with [[may-mongol-conquests-2012|May's]] more expansive thesis —
the wiki keeps both, using Morgan to discipline May's claims and May to give Morgan's
institutions their world-historical stakes. A specialist secondary work given full
presumptive weight on Mongol institutions and Ilkhanid Persia.
