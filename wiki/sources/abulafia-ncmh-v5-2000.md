# The New Cambridge Medieval History, Vol. 5, c. 1198–c. 1300

Fifth volume of the NCMH, covering the long thirteenth century (c. 1198–c. 1300).
Successor in the backbone to [[reuter-ncmh-v3-1999|NCMH Vol. 3 (c. 900–c. 1024)]]
and [[luscombe-ncmh-v4-2004|NCMH Vol. 4 (c. 1024–c. 1198)]]; precedes NCMH Vols 6–7.

## Editorial Framing

Abulafia's introduction sets **expansion** as the dominant theme of the thirteenth
century: the expansion of Latin Christendom into Orthodox, Muslim and pagan lands;
of the economy (Italian, German, Catalan merchants penetrating the Mediterranean,
Baltic and the European interior); of population (halted only c. 1300); and of
government (consolidating monarchies and a papacy claiming authority over secular
rulers). The volume deliberately rejects the older *Cambridge Medieval History* Vol.
VI subtitle "The Victory of the Papacy" and its pope-versus-emperor framing. Instead
it adopts a **"horizontal" rather than "vertical" perspective** — treating the regions
and topics that mattered to the thirteenth century itself rather than only the Latin
Christian legacy to modernity. The organizing analytic is the **frontier**: the
interaction between frontier societies (Spain, the Baltic, the Mediterranean islands,
the Celtic lands, the Slavonic east) and the old heart of western Europe. Byzantium,
Mamluk Egypt, the Maghrib and the Mongol empire are treated as integral, not adjacent.

## Volume Synthesis Note

David Abulafia's **NCMH Vol. 5** reframes the long thirteenth century (c. 1198–c. 1300) around
**expansion** — of Latin Christendom, of the economy, of population (halted only c. 1300), and
of government — and around the **frontier** as its organising analytic. Its deliberate rejection
of the old *Cambridge Medieval History* "Victory of the Papacy" framing, in favour of a
"horizontal" perspective that treats Byzantium, the Mamluks, the Maghrib and the Mongols as
integral rather than adjacent, is borne out across the volume and now structures the wiki's
thirteenth-century backbone.

Four cross-cutting arguments emerge from the whole. **First, the divergence of the European
state.** The volume's central political story is the parting of ways between the intensive,
tax-and-law **bureaucratic monarchies** of [[capetian-dynasty|France]] and
[[angevin-empire|England]] — England's path running through [[magna-carta-1215|Magna Carta]] and
the birth of [[english-parliament|parliament]], France's through a legist crown that humbled the
[[papacy|papacy]] — and the **devolution** of the [[frederick-ii-hohenstaufen|Empire]] into
territorial princes and elective [[rudolf-of-habsburg|Hausmacht]] kingship after the
[[german-interregnum-1250|Interregnum]]. The same fiscal-military pressures produced the Hungarian
Golden Bull (1222) and the [[central-european-kingdoms|baronial constitutionalism]] of central
Europe, and the [[commune-movement|communal]] republics and [[signoria|signorie]] of Italy: a
continent-wide experiment in the relation of ruler, law and consent.

**Second, the centrality of the Mediterranean.** Abulafia's own chapters bind the volume
together: the outward thrust of [[charles-of-anjou|Charles of Anjou]] (a Capetian who became an
Italian, Mediterranean and would-be Byzantine sovereign), the [[sicilian-vespers-1282|Sicilian
Vespers]] that wrecked his empire and split the [[kingdom-of-sicily|kingdom of Sicily]], and the
rise of the [[crown-of-aragon|Crown of Aragon]] turn the western Mediterranean into a single
arena of Angevin–Aragonese conflict. The [[commercial-revolution|commercial revolution]] —
[[republic-of-genoa|Genoa]], [[republic-of-venice|Venice]] and [[florence|Florentine]] banking,
the Black Sea colonies, the [[fourth-crusade-1204|1204]] partition of Byzantium, and the opening
of the Atlantic sea route (c. 1277) — is the economic substrate of this expansion, and the gold
of the [[maghrib-successor-states|Maghrib]] its monetary fuel.

**Third, the frontier as the engine of the century.** The volume's "horizontal" perspective is
vindicated by the way its frontiers move together: the [[reconquista|Reconquista]] climaxes
(Córdoba 1236, Valencia 1238, Seville 1248) as the [[almohads|Almohad]] empire collapses into the
[[maghrib-successor-states|tripartite Maghrib]], leaving only [[nasrid-granada|Nasrid Granada]];
the [[teutonic-knights|Baltic Crusade]] and the German *Ostsiedlung* build a conquest-state on
the pagan north-east; the [[crusades|crusader states]] are extinguished by the new
[[mamluk-sultanate|Mamluk]] power (the [[fall-of-acre-1291|fall of Acre]], 1291); and the
[[mongol-empire|Mongols]] burst across both the eastern ([[mongol-invasion-of-europe-1241|1241]],
the [[rus-principalities|Rus′]]) and the south-eastern ([[battle-of-ain-jalut-1260|Ain Jalut]],
1260) frontiers at once. [[edward-i-plantagenet|Edward I's]] conquest of Wales and the
[[comparisons/celtic-lands-and-english-expansion|Celtic lands]] is the British instance of the
same pattern of consolidating cores pressing on peripheral societies.

**Fourth, the turning of two tides.** The volume captures a century that **peaks and turns**:
demographic and economic expansion halts c. 1300; the [[papacy|papal monarchy]] reaches its
zenith at Lateran IV and is "spectacularly rebuffed" by [[philip-iv-capetian|Philip IV]] at
Anagni; the crusading enterprise in the Levant ends at Acre; and [[byzantine-empire|Byzantium]],
recovered at [[empire-of-nicaea|1261]], is left "a husk." The thirteenth century is thus both the
apogee of medieval Latin civilisation and the threshold of the fourteenth-century crisis.

**Cross-volume tensions.** NCMH V hands forward directly from
[[luscombe-ncmh-v4-2004|NCMH IV]] (the take-off, the Gregorian reform, the early crusades) and
sharpens, rather than contradicts, the wiki's existing accounts: it deepens the
[[papacy|papacy]], [[byzantine-empire|Byzantium]], the [[reconquista|Reconquista]], the
[[commercial-revolution|commercial revolution]], the [[mongol-empire|Mongols]] and the
[[commune-movement|Italian communes]]. The one **flagged thinness** is the volume's lighter
wiki coverage (this ingest) of the **Scandinavian kingdoms** and the **thirteenth-century Balkan
Orthodox states** (Second Bulgarian Empire, Nemanjić Serbia, Albania), noted for future work.
