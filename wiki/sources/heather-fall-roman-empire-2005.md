# The Fall of the Roman Empire: A New History of Rome and the Barbarians

Peter Heather's 2005 narrative is the leading modern **externalist** account of the
end of the western [[late-antiquity|Roman Empire]] — a deliberate, evidence-driven
answer to **Gibbon** that reaches the opposite verdict on causation. Where Gibbon and
the long "internal decline" tradition located the cause in Rome's own enervation,
Heather argues that the fourth-century western state was **fundamentally sound** — its
army, taxation, cities and aristocratic culture functioning — and that it was destroyed
by an **exogenous shock**: the arrival of the [[huns|Huns]] on the fringes of Europe,
which dislodged the Germanic peoples and drove them across the frontier in unprecedented
numbers and cohesion.

## Core Argument

1. **The empire was not in terminal internal decline.** The detailed portrait of the
   fourth-century West (Symmachus' senatorial world, a still-effective fiscal-military
   machine, a vibrant provincial civilization) is meant to refute the Gibbonian premise.
   Strains existed, but nothing inevitable.

2. **The Huns were the prime mover.** Their westward pressure from c. 350–375 set the
   Goths in motion (the Danube crossing of 376 and [[battle-of-adrianople-378|Adrianople,
   378]]), and a second Hunnic surge helped trigger the [[sack-of-rome-410|Rhine crossing
   of 406]]. Paradoxically the Huns were also, for a generation, **Rome's indispensable
   mercenaries** (under [[stilicho|Stilicho]] and Aetius) before [[attila|Attila]] turned
   them into a predatory empire.

3. **"Supergroups" and the Roman periphery.** Centuries of contact with Roman wealth,
   subsidy, weaponry and aggression had transformed Germanic society beyond the frontier —
   consolidating small war-bands into larger, more durable confederations (the
   [[visigoths|Visigoths]], [[vandal-kingdom|Vandals]], [[ostrogoths|Ostrogoths]]). When
   this long amalgamation collided with the Hunnic shock, the **supergroups that tore the
   West apart came into being**.

4. **The fiscal spiral did the killing.** The loss of revenue-rich provinces — above all
   [[vandal-kingdom|Vandal Africa]] in 439 — shrank the tax base that funded the army, so
   each barbarian settlement made the next harder to resist. 476 is an anticlimax; the
   substance was lost in 405–439.

5. **Rome's imperialism caused its own destruction.** Heather's closing reversal of
   Gibbon: the empire "had sown the seeds of its own destruction… not because of internal
   weaknesses… but as a consequence of its relationship with the Germanic world." Roman
   dominance generated the inverse reaction that overthrew it — "By virtue of its unbounded
   aggression, Roman imperialism was ultimately responsible for its own destruction."
