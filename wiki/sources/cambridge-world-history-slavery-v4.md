## Volume Synthesis Note — completing the Cambridge World History of Slavery, Vols 1–4

The four-volume *Cambridge World History of Slavery* now sits across the wiki as a continuous,
global account of human bondage from antiquity to the present, deliberately set against the older
Greco-Roman-and-Atlantic frame. Its through-arguments:

1. **Slavery is near-universal and ancient** — a "society with slaves" was the norm of complex
   civilisation; genuine [[slave-society|slave societies]] were the rare exception (Vol. 1).
2. **The medieval millennium is the missing middle** — [[medieval-slavery]] (Vol. 2) bridges
   ancient and Atlantic slavery, dominated by *military* and *domestic* slavery, the saqaliba and
   Indian Ocean trades, and a religiously-zoned supply, while resisting the slave-society binary
   in favour of a **continuum of dependency**.
3. **The Atlantic system was the largest node in a global early-modern web of coerced labour**
   ([[atlantic-slave-trade]], [[plantation-economy]], Vol. 3) — set beside white servitude,
   [[second-serfdom]], and indigenous bondage, and culturally productive ([[creolization]]).
4. **Abolition was a genuine moral revolution** ([[abolition-of-slavery]], Vol. 4) — a sudden
   nineteenth-century inversion of millennia of acceptance — but coerced labour persists, so the
   story has no clean end.

The series synthesises with the wiki's African coverage (the [[cambridge-history-of-africa-v4|CHA]]
and GHA volumes on the Atlantic and trans-Saharan trades), its [[great-divergence|economic
history]], and its [[dependency-theory|dependency]] debates. No major contradictions arose;
Drescher's Vol. 4 framing of abolition will be cross-checked against his standalone *Abolition*
monograph (still in the ingest queue).
