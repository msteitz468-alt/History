# World Prehistory: A Brief Introduction (10th Edition)

Brian M. Fagan and Nadia Durrani, *World Prehistory: A Brief Introduction*, 10th
edition (New York and Abingdon: Routledge, 2020). A single-volume global survey of
more than three million years of human prehistory, from hominin origins in tropical
Africa to the preindustrial states of the Old World and the Americas on the eve of
literate history. First published 2005 (Pearson); a long-lived classroom text now in
its tenth edition.

## Role in the Wiki

The wiki already holds dense, specialist-grade coverage of nearly every topic Fagan
and Durrani survey (CAH Vol. I on prehistory; Champion et al. *Prehistoric Europe*;
Rhodes *Origins*; Bailey *Balkan Prehistory*; Mann, D'Altroy, and Berdan on the
Americas; the GHA series on Africa; the Cambridge histories of Japan and India on
early East and South Asia). This source therefore functions primarily as a
**global-synthesis backbone** — it supplies (1) an up-to-date (2020) consensus
narrative that ties the regional pages together into a single human story, (2) current
dates and genetic findings on human origins and the great diasporas, and (3) an
explicit comparative framework for the independent emergence of food production and
state-organized societies across the Old and New Worlds. Most pages are UPDATEs;
new pages were created only where the survey covers ground genuinely thin elsewhere
in the wiki.

## Structure

- **Part I — Prehistory** (ch. 1): archaeology, world prehistory, culture, dating,
  cyclical vs. linear time, the critique of pseudoarchaeology.
- **Part II — The World of the First Humans** (ch. 2–3): human origins; the African
  exodus (archaic to modern humans, Neanderthals, out-of-Africa).
- **Part III — The Birth of the Modern World** (ch. 4–7): the diaspora of modern
  humans (Europe, Asia, Australia, the Pacific, the Americas); origins of food
  production; the earliest farmers; chiefs and chiefdoms.
- **Part IV — Early Civilizations** (ch. 8–11): state-organized societies;
  Mesopotamia and the eastern Mediterranean; Egypt and Africa; South, Southeast,
  and East Asia.
- **Part V — Preindustrial States in the Americas** (ch. 12–14): lowland
  Mesoamerica; highland Mesoamerica; Andean civilizations.

## Volume Synthesis Note

*World Prehistory* is best understood in this wiki not as a source of new facts but as a
**global connective tissue**. Its distinctive value is fourfold. First, it supplies a
current (2020) consensus on the deep-time backbone: the Lomekwi 3 tools (3.3 Ma) that sever
the old Homo–toolmaking equation, the Jebel Irhoud reanalysis (~315 kya) that pushes *Homo
sapiens* origins back a hundred millennia and westward to Morocco, and the explicit
retirement of the Mount Toba "volcanic winter" bottleneck — each of which updates or
contradicts older framings that had entered the wiki through mid-century syntheses.

Second, it is the wiki's most sustained statement of the **comparative method** for the two
great transitions of prehistory. On the origins of food production it refuses a single
cause, staging Childe's oasis thesis against Braidwood's hilly-flanks rebuttal and
Flannery's ecological-systems model before settling on multicausal "preadaptation" — farming
as the intensification of already-sedentary Holocene foragers, arising independently and
asynchronously worldwide. On the origins of the state it runs the same play at greater
length (Childe, Wittfogel and Robert Adams's field-survey debunking of the hydraulic thesis,
Carneiro's coercion theory and its Santa Valley falsification, Yoffee's "power in three
domains," Jennings's "unintended by-product"), and its signature analytic move — that
long-distance trade, large-scale irrigation, and endemic warfare are *consequences* rather
than *causes* of civilization — is now folded into [[state-formation]].

Third, it materially strengthens the wiki's acknowledged thin regions. The peopling of the
Americas (Beringian Standstill, the contested ~23 kya DNA date, Monte Verde), the offshore
Pacific, and the Andean sequence below the Inca horizon (Caral, Chavín, Moche, Tiwanaku,
Wari, Chimú) all gained attributed detail or, in five cases, their first dedicated pages —
directly against the collection's Americas and Southeast-Asia gaps. Fauvelle's 9th–15th c.
sub-Saharan "golden age" framing similarly counters the isolation narrative for Africa.

Finally, it sits in productive tension with the wiki's specialist sources. Where a Cambridge
volume or a regional monograph (D'Altroy on the Inca, the GHA on Africa) carries the fine
grain, Fagan and Durrani supply the cross-regional scaffolding that lets those pages speak to
one another — the recurring pairing of climate whiplash and political collapse (Akkad, the
Old Kingdom, the Classic Maya, Angkor, Moche) being the clearest example of a pattern visible
only at the whole-volume scale. Its processual, neo-evolutionary voice (the bands–tribes–
chiefdoms–states vocabulary) is retained here as an explicitly contested heuristic, not
settled taxonomy, consistent with the wiki's existing [[chiefdom]] and
[[band-tribe-chiefdom-state]] pages.
