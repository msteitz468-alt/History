# Colin Imber, *The Ottoman Empire, 1300–1650: The Structure of Power* (3rd ed., 2019)

The standard structural account of the Ottoman Empire to 1650 — the foundational source for
the wiki's Ottoman coverage (**Phase C**). Imber's distinctive method is to give a compact
**chronological spine** (Chapter 1) and then anatomize the empire's **institutions** theme by
theme. Ingested single-pass.

This ingest **opened Phase C (Ottoman / post-1500 Islamic world)**, a critical gap. It
deepened the existing [[ottoman-empire]] page (extending it from the rise-to-1402 framing to
the full 1300–1650 arc) and created six discrete pages:

- [[selim-i]] — the conqueror of the Arab lands (Çaldıran 1514; the Mamluk conquest 1516–17;
  the Holy Cities and the claim to defend Sunni Islam).
- [[suleiman-the-magnificent]] — the apogee: Belgrade, Rhodes, Mohács, the siege of Vienna,
  the Mediterranean and Iranian wars, and the legislator-sultan.
- [[devshirme]] — the child-levy and the slave household (*kapıkulu*) that staffed the army
  and government.
- [[janissaries]] — the slave-infantry corps, its growth, and its role in the Ottoman
  "military revolution."
- [[timar-system]] — the cavalry-fief land-and-tax order and its seventeenth-century
  transformation.
- [[ottoman-law-kanun]] — the dual legal order of sacred law (*shari'a*) and sultanic secular
  law (*kanun*).

## Imber's framing of the empire

Imber stresses that the Ottoman Empire was **dynastic, multinational, and sultan-centered**,
not simply "Turkish" or "Islamic." By 1650 it spanned Europe, Asia, and Africa; Islam was the
ruling faith and Turkish the language of government, but Christians outnumbered Muslims in many
areas, Thessaloniki was the most Jewish city in the world (after the 1492 Spanish expulsion),
and the political élite was largely of convert and Balkan/Caucasian origin. "It was ultimately
the person of the sultan and not religious, ethnic or other identity that held the Empire
together," the only loyalty demanded of subjects being "not rebelling and paying taxes."

Its origins lay in the **Turkish and Muslim transformation of Anatolia** after the Seljuk
victory at Manzikert (1071) and the fragmentation that followed the Mongol defeat of the
Seljuks (Kösedağ, 1243) and the Fourth Crusade's shattering of Byzantium (1204) — out of which
the *beylik* of **Osman** emerged on the Byzantine Bithynian frontier c. 1300. The structural
chapters then trace how a frontier principality became a centralized empire resting on the
**slave household, the *timar* cavalry, and the dual *shari'a*/*kanun* legal order** — and how
that order entered crisis after 1590 (the "military revolution," treasury deficits, and the
Jelali revolts).
