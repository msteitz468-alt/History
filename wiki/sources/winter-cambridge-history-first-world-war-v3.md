# The Cambridge History of the First World War, Vol. III: Civil Society (2014)

The third volume covers the war's **social and cultural** history — how civil society
experienced, endured, and remembered the conflict, the domain of Winter's own influential
scholarship.

## What it contributes

- **[[war-memory-and-mourning]]** — the concept/process page: the cultural history of
  bereavement, commemoration, and the memory of the Great War.
- Enriches **[[first-world-war-1914]]** (the home front and human cost) and the
  **[[world-wars-era]]** period page.

## Argument

The volume insists that the war's meaning is inseparable from its **cultural** dimension —
"how contemporaries understood the violent world in which they lived framed what they did,"
mediated by art, faith, and "practices of faith, commemoration and mourning which continued
long after the Armistice." It documents the staggering scale of loss (2+ million war widows,
~6 million orphans), the gendered experience of women's war work and nursing, children's
war, and above all the **work of mourning** — the memorials, rituals, and "sites of memory"
through which bereaved societies tried to make sense of mass death. The Great War emerges as
"the ur-catastrophe of the twentieth century" and the wellspring of a still-growing **memory
boom**.

(Completes Winter's three volumes with I [[winter-cambridge-history-first-world-war-v1|Global
War]] and II [[winter-cambridge-history-first-world-war-v2|The State]].)

## Volume Synthesis Note (the three-volume set)

Read as a whole, Winter's *Cambridge History of the First World War* gives the wiki the war's
three great dimensions beyond the origins-and-narrative account already on
[[first-world-war-1914]]. Its organizing thesis across the volumes is that 1914–18 was the
first **[[total-war|total war]]** — and the three volumes anatomize totality at three scales.
**Vol. I (Global War)** shows it spatially: a genuinely *world* event that mobilized colonial
soldiers and labourers (the Chinese Labour Corps; Indian and African troops), spread combat
across Africa, Asia, and the oceans, and — by abolishing the soldier/civilian line — produced
mass atrocity and the Armenian genocide. **Vol. II (The State)** shows it institutionally: the
**[[war-economy|command war economy]]** and the leap in state power (government's share of GDP
from ~10% to ~50%) as "the market became the enemy of national survival." **Vol. III (Civil
Society)** shows it intimately and enduringly: the war as lived and grieved by families, women,
and children, and the vast cultural **[[war-memory-and-mourning|work of mourning]]** —
memorials, the unknown soldier, Armistice Day — that made the Great War "the ur-catastrophe of
the twentieth century."

Together the volumes reposition the war in the wiki from a diplomatic-military event into the
**hinge of the [[world-wars-era|World Wars Era]]**: the moment when industrial economy, the
nation-state, and modern ideology fused into total war. The set converges with the wiki's
existing origins coverage ([[causes-of-the-first-world-war]], [[joll-origins-first-world-war]])
and points forward — the command economy, total mobilization, and commemorative forms all
recurring, at greater scale, in the [[second-world-war-1939|Second World War]]. No
contradictions arose; Winter's cultural-history emphasis complements rather than displaces the
political-military narrative.
