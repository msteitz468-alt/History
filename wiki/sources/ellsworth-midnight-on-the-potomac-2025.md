# Midnight on the Potomac (Scott Ellsworth, 2025)

Scott Ellsworth's narrative of the United States' most precarious final Civil War year (spring 1864–spring 1865): Grant's Overland bloodletting, Jubal Early's near-run at Washington, the 1864 election, John Wilkes Booth's evolving conspiracy, Black political assertion (Henry Highland Garnet), the Second Inaugural, City Point and Appomattox, and the assassination at Ford's Theatre — framed as a fight for the "soul of the nation" that nearly failed.

Fills a wiki gap at the **last-year synthesis** level: existing coverage is strong on campaigns (warfare hub), Lincoln biography (Donald), and international pariah diplomacy (Bonner), but thin on a single integrated narrative of military crisis + home front + conspiracy + Black civic politics + assassination as one arc.

## Argument in Brief

- For twelve months in 1864–65 the Union's survival was genuinely contingent, not teleological.
- The war was fought by armies *and* by spies, actors, contrabands, nurses, preachers, and conspirators; Washington, D.C. is the stage as much as Virginia's battlefields.
- Booth's plot was networked (Confederate agents, Surratt circle, Powell, Atzerodt) and evolved from kidnap-to-Richmond toward multi-target assassination.
- Black Americans were not only freed labor (contrabands) but political actors — Garnet's February 1865 House address is a centerpiece.
- Military turning points (Wilderness/Spotsylvania, Monocacy, Atlanta, Mobile Bay, Petersburg breakthrough) and political ones (reelection, Second Inaugural) interlock; neither alone explains victory or its cost.
- Afterword extends the freedom/illusion dialectic into Reconstruction and long memory (including twentieth-century echoes).
