# The Sage of Monticello — Dumas Malone (1981)

**Jefferson and His Time, Volume 6.** This is the final volume of Malone's monumental six-volume biography. It follows Jefferson from the end of his presidency and return to Monticello in 1809 through his death on the fiftieth anniversary of the Declaration of Independence, July 4, 1826. The retirement years are presented as a period of "mixed blessings": release from the burdens of office and the joy of private pursuits (gardening, architecture, reading, grandchildren, the great correspondence with John Adams) alongside the accumulating cares of debt, the management of a large plantation and enslaved workforce in declining economic times, the intense labor of creating a university from scratch, and the sectional crisis over Missouri that Jefferson interpreted as a mortal threat to the union he had helped create.

## Overview / Thesis

Malone portrays the retired Jefferson as the "Sage of Monticello" — a revered figure whose home became a pilgrimage site, yet a man still energetically engaged in the life of the mind and the republic. The central achievement of these years, and the volume's dominant narrative, is the conception, chartering, design, funding, construction, and opening of the University of Virginia — Jefferson's "academical village," his architectural masterpiece, and his last great institutional contribution to the cause of republican government through education. Parallel to this is the extraordinary late-life friendship and correspondence with John Adams, one of the treasures of American letters. Counterbalancing these high points are the "harassments of debt," the personal and familial strains of slavery and plantation management, the "firebell in the night" of the Missouri question, and a troubled twilight marked by failing health and the recognition that many hopes for the new nation remained unfulfilled. The volume closes with Jefferson's death on the same day as Adams, his self-written epitaph (Declaration, Virginia Statute for Religious Freedom, and founder of the University of Virginia), and a quiet assessment of a life whose public career had ended but whose influence continued through ideas, institutions, and descendants.

## Methodological Notes

- Strict chronological and documentary method continuing the series: heavy use of Jefferson's letters (especially the Adams exchange), family papers, University of Virginia records and correspondence with Joseph C. Cabell and others, contemporary accounts by visitors (Ticknor, Francis Walker Gilmer, Lafayette's tour), and Jefferson's own account books and drawings.
- Detailed attention to the practical mechanics of the University project (site selection, pavilion and hotel designs, serpentine walls, recruitment of faculty in Europe and America, the Rockfish Gap report, legislative battles).
- The Adams correspondence is treated at length as both personal reconciliation and intellectual exchange on history, politics, religion, and old age.
- Appendices supply genealogical, financial, and Hemings family data available to Malone in the late 1970s.
- Malone maintains a sympathetic but not uncritical stance toward Jefferson's retirement conduct and views on slavery, union, and education.

## Key Contributions

- The most detailed scholarly reconstruction of the University of Virginia's founding available at the time of publication — from educational aspirations in the 1779 revisal bill through the 1818 Rockfish Gap report, the 1819 charter, construction of the Lawn and Rotunda, faculty hiring (including the controversial Thomas Cooper episode), and the first session in 1825.
- Full presentation of the Jefferson-Adams correspondence as the emotional and intellectual centerpiece of the retirement years.
- Candid treatment of Jefferson's financial affairs, including the sale of his library to the Library of Congress after the British burning of Washington, mounting debts, and the decision not to free more than a handful of the enslaved people he held.
- Valuable primary-source perspective on the Missouri crisis from Jefferson's Virginia plantation viewpoint and his fears of sectional "consolidation."
- Rich portraits of the "Monticello circle" — family (especially Martha Randolph and grandchildren), visitors, and the community of Albemarle County.

## Volume Synthesis Note

This final volume completes Malone's life work and supplies the richest available mid-century account of Jefferson in retirement. Its greatest permanent contribution is the granular, document-driven history of the University of Virginia's creation — an institutional capstone that embodies Jefferson's lifelong commitment to education as the "only sure foundation" of republican government. The Adams correspondence receives its full due as both personal healing and a profound late-life dialogue on the meaning of the American founding. The volume is frank about the financial and human costs of Virginia plantation life in Jefferson's final years and includes the Hemings appendix as then-available evidence. While later scholarship (especially on slavery, economics, and the Adams letters' full publication) has added layers and corrections, Malone's narrative remains the essential reference for the operational and personal texture of these years. Cross-volume tension with earlier volumes is minimal; the retirement years are presented as consistent with Jefferson's long-term principles even as circumstances (debt, age, sectionalism) limited their full realization.
