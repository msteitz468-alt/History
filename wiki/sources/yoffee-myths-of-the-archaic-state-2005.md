# Myths of the Archaic State: Evolution of the Earliest Cities, States, and Civilizations

**Norman Yoffee (2005, Cambridge University Press)**

## Overview

Yoffee challenges prevailing "myths" in the study of the earliest cities, states, and civilizations. He argues against the traditional neo-evolutionist image of early states as large, territorially integrated polities ruled by totalitarian despots who monopolized power, goods, and information. Instead, he emphasizes:

- The variety of power dimensions and their limits in early states.
- City-states as the typical early form rather than expansive empires.
- The importance of everyday social roles (slaves, soldiers, priests, merchants, women, peasants) beyond heroic male rulers.
- "Evolution of simplicity" alongside complexity.
- Collapse as drastic restructuring of social institutions and mutation of identity/memory rather than total end.
- Multiple evolutionary trajectories, not a single ladder of progress.
- The creation of ideologies that made centralized leadership seem natural and inevitable.

Primary case material from Mesopotamia (with detailed treatment of city-states, law, Old Babylonian society, collapse of Akkad/Ur III/Old Babylonian). Comparative examples from Egypt, South Asia (Harappa), China, Mesoamerica (Teotihuacan), South America, and the American Southwest (Chaco).

The book is simultaneously a critique of theory (Chapters 1–2, 8) and positive historical reconstruction (especially Chapter 9 on Mesopotamia).

## Extracted Claims by Range (from Deployed Subagent Strategy)

**Range 01 (Introduction + Ch. 1 "The evolution of a factoid" + early Ch. 2):**
- Central myth: earliest states were uniform large territorial despotisms controlling all goods/information/law. Factoids (Mailer term) repeated until taken as fact.
- Neo-evolutionism history: White (universal evolution via energy), Steward (multilinear, hydraulic hypothesis for Mesopotamia), Sahlins/Service (general/specific, law of evolutionary potential).
- Yoffee's Rule: "If you can argue whether a society is a state or isn’t, then it isn’t."
- Early power dimensions: economic (estates, trade), social (segmentation, symbols), political (admin, legitimation). States as "states of mind" via ideology and social memory.
- Ubaid vs Uruk contrast: potential inequality vs real stratification. No ethnographic chiefdom models the formative stage.
- Links established: [[neo-evolutionism]], [[archaic-state]], [[city-state]], [[state-formation]], [[mesopotamia]], [[uruk]], [[yoffee-cwh-v3-2015]].

**Range 02 (late Ch. 2 + Ch. 3 "meaning of cities" + early Ch. 4 "when complexity was simplified"):**
- City-states typical form: central place + hinterland in peer-polity clusters sharing civilization (not large empires).
- Urbanization paired with ruralization (80% urban in S. Mesopotamia; countryside depopulated but linked via estates, ceremonies, conflict).
- Scale examples: Uruk ~250 ha/20k; Lagash >3000 km²; comparisons to Teotihuacan, Harappa, Maya, China.
- "Evolution of simplicity": states make society legible (writing, weights, rations, law codes as propaganda not practice; Hammurabi context: conquests, stela as political tool).
- Local assemblies/elders retained power; law codes did not supplant them.
- Links: [[city-state]], [[urbanization]], [[mesopotamia]], [[uruk]], [[teotihuacan]], [[harappa]], [[china]].

**Range 03 (late Ch. 4 + Ch. 5 identity/agency + early Ch. 6):**
- Naditu (Sippar): elite non-marrying priestesses with business agency, bequests, litigation; kept family property intact post-Ur III.
- Kezertu: assigned performers in Ishtar rites (not anachronistic "prostitutes"); migrated from Uruk with cult amid abandonment/resistance.
- Agency in structures: choices within norms; microhistory of exceptional data reveals lives.
- Collapse as restructuring: political centers fall but institutions (temples, memory) persist or mutate.
- Links: [[identity]], [[agency]], [[collapse]], [[mesopotamia]], [[babylon]], [[uruk]].

**Range 04 (Ch. 6 collapse + Ch. 7 trajectories + early Ch. 8):**
- Collapse as "species of cultural transformation" not total end; re-made per formation ideologies.
- Chaco rituality: pilgrimage center with great houses, roads, exotic goods; "big bang" ~1050; collapse ~1135-80 drought + elite ritual competition + theological crisis; memory in "White House".
- Neo-evolutionism critiqued: assumes modern traditional = stages; ignores variability, rituality, heterarchy.
- Heterarchy: multiple hierarchies; Chaco/Cahokia more complex in some ways than pre-state.
- Links: [[collapse]], [[chaco-canyon]], [[neo-evolutionism]], [[heterarchy]], [[power]].

**Range 05 (late Ch. 8 + Ch. 9 "altered states" + back):**
- Growth model (Bronson/Childe): post-Pleistocene irreversible demographic/social growth via surpluses; villages modest for millennia then phase transitions (CAS: boiling/avalanche).
- Interaction spheres → cities crystallize rapidly in favorable locations; generative of new roles/identities.
- Mesopotamia: no single early "state" but city-states in civilizational boundary; Sargon first regional; persistent cultural vs political identity (Sumerian King List as ideal).
- Non-state trajectories (Chaco, Cahokia, Harappa per Possehl): different rules of power; no cumulative surplus memory; collapsed without becoming states.
- Palaces hard to find (mobile); temples visible due to continuity.
- Final myth rebuttal: states expected products of post-Pleistocene conditions, not rare; non-state histories need equal explanation.
- New rules: broad schemes ok but resist reductionism; focus on how people understood lives; "distressing openendedness" welcomed.
