# Polybius, *The Histories*

The principal contemporary account of **Rome's rise to Mediterranean dominion** — and, after
[[herodotus-histories|Herodotus]] and [[thucydides-peloponnesian-war|Thucydides]], the third great
work of Greek historiography. Polybius poses the question his whole work answers: "how and thanks to
what kind of political system almost the entire known world was conquered… under a single empire, the
empire of the Romans, in less than fifty-three years — an unprecedented event?" (1.1). The third
Phase-4 primary source (OWC selection: Books 1–6, 12).

## The Work

A **universal history** in forty books (only **Books 1–5 fully extant**, with large parts of 6 and 12
and Byzantine excerpts of the rest), originally covering **220–167 BCE**, later extended to **146** to
judge Roman rule "something to be admired or condemned":

- **Books 1–2** — introduction (the [[first-punic-war-264-241bce|First Punic War]], the Mercenary
  War, Rome's conquest of north Italy, the Achaean League).
- **Book 3** — the causes and first years of the **[[second-punic-war-218-201bce|Hannibalic War]]**
  to Cannae (216); Polybius personally retraced [[hannibal|Hannibal's]] route over the Alps.
- **Books 4–5** — the simultaneous wars in Greece and the east (the Social War, the Fourth Syrian
  War) — the strands of Mediterranean history beginning to "flow into a single story."
- **Book 6** — the **[[mixed-constitution|analysis of the Roman constitution]]**, the most famous and
  influential part: the cycle of constitutions (*anacyclosis*) and the "mixed" constitution that
  explains Rome's stability and success.
- **Book 12** — a treatise on historical method, framed as a sustained **polemic against Timaeus** of
  Tauromenium, the "armchair historian."

## Polybius's Theory of History

McGing draws out the principles that make Polybius "the most self-conscious methodologist of ancient
historians":

- **"Pragmatic" history** — political and military affairs ("the high affairs of state… of war and
  peace"), the only kind that is genuinely **useful**: "there is no more authentic way to prepare and
  train oneself for political life than by studying history" (1.1).
- **Practical experience required.** Only the experienced soldier-statesman can judge evidence rightly
  (the medicine/history analogy, 12.25). Timaeus failed because he "sat in a library for fifty years."
- **Autopsy, fieldwork, and the questioning of informants** — "the most important aspect of a
  historian's work" (12.4c) — plus the geography indispensable to understanding campaigns.
- **Universal history.** Since "Fortune… redirected history" so that c. 220 BCE "the affairs of the
  world" became "an organic whole" under Rome, only a world history can capture it; partial
  monographs are like trying to grasp a living body "by studying the dissected parts of its corpse"
  (1.4).
- **Truth over partisanship** (against the biased Philinus and Fabius Pictor); **causation** — the
  famous triad of **cause, pretext, and beginning** of wars (3.6–7, developing Thucydides) — and
  **perspective** (events shown through the actors' eyes, to explain why they acted).
- **Against sensationalism.** History is not tragedy: the historian must "focus exclusively on what
  was actually done and said" (the attack on Phylarchus, 2.56) — though Polybius allows drama in its
  proper place and composes speeches selectively, in his own style.
- **Tyche (Fortune).** Despite explaining Rome's success in wholly human terms (institutions, courage,
  training — explicitly denying Tyche a role, 1.63), Polybius calls the unification of the world
  Tyche's "finest achievement." McGing resolves the tension: Tyche names only the **inexplicable
  larger design** (why Rome's rise came *now*), not the humanly-understandable mechanics of power.

## Reception

Read but not canonical in antiquity (his plain Greek was thought unreadable cover-to-cover); his most
important user was **Livy**. Recovered in Renaissance Italy (Bruni 1419; Perotti's Latin 1454), he
became a pillar of **constitutional theory** through **Machiavelli's** use of Book 6 in the
*Discourses*, then **Montesquieu** and, warmest of all, **John Adams**, whose *Defence of the
Constitutions* (1787) drew on Polybius's mixed government at the founding of the United States.
