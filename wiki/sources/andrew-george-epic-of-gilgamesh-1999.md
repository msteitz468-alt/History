# The Epic of Gilgamesh — Andrew George translation

## Source Summary

*The Epic of Gilgamesh* is the earliest known masterpiece of world literature, a Babylonian epic poem in Akkadian that tells the story of Gilgamesh, king of Uruk, his friendship with the wild man Enkidu, their heroic exploits, Enkidu's death, and Gilgamesh's desperate quest for immortality. It survives primarily in the "Standard Version" (twelve tablets, "He who saw the Deep"), which circulated in the first millennium BCE, but draws on much older Sumerian poems and intermediate Old Babylonian versions.

Andrew George's Penguin Classics edition (1999; revised 2019) is the standard modern English translation. It presents the Standard Version in full, followed by the five surviving Sumerian poems of Gilgamesh, and selected Old Babylonian and other fragments. The introduction masterfully explains the textual history, the relationship between Sumerian and Babylonian traditions, and the poem's enduring themes.

The epic is not "history" in the modern sense but legendary literature rooted in the historical memory of a powerful ruler of Uruk during the Early Dynastic period. It offers an insider's (or at least elite Mesopotamian) view of power, mortality, friendship, sexuality, violence, and the boundaries between human, animal, and divine.

The narrative core is the Standard Version's 12 tablets. The Sumerian poems are shorter, more episodic, and closer in time to the historical Uruk period. Fragments show the poem's development over centuries.

The poem is structured around Gilgamesh's transformation from arrogant, tyrannical king to a man who has confronted death and learned wisdom. The flood story (Tablet XI) is the emotional and narrative climax, where Uta-napishti recounts the deluge and why only he (and his wife) were granted eternal life.

## Key Themes and Historiography

Major themes:
- **Mortality and the human condition**: "When the gods created man, they gave him death but kept life for themselves." Gilgamesh's quest fails; he returns with the knowledge that immortality lies in deeds, city, and story.
- **Friendship and civilization**: Enkidu, created by the gods as a counterpart, is civilized by the prostitute Shamhat. Their bond tames the wild and challenges the king.
- **Kingship and hubris**: Gilgamesh as 2/3 divine, 1/3 human; oppressive ruler who builds Uruk's walls but learns limits.
- **Sexuality, gender, nature vs. culture**: Enkidu's transformation from animal-like to human via sex; Ishtar's destructive sexuality (Bull of Heaven episode).
- **Flood myth**: One of the oldest versions; direct ancestor or parallel to the Biblical Noah story and Atrahasis.

As historiography: Valuable for *mentalités* of ancient Mesopotamia — views of death, friendship, power, and the past. Not reliable for specific "events" of c. 2600 BCE (Gilgamesh was likely a real ruler of Uruk). The Sumerian poems are closer in time to the historical kernel. Andrew George is meticulous on variants and philology; the translation aims for readability while staying close to the Akkadian/Sumerian.

## Volume Synthesis Note

The *Epic of Gilgamesh* (Andrew George trans., 1999/2019) is the oldest surviving masterpiece of world literature and the essential literary primary for legendary Early Dynastic Sumer and the city of Uruk. Drawing on Sumerian poems (Ur III core, c. 2100 BCE) and Old Babylonian traditions, the Standard Version (c. 1200–1000 BCE, attributed to Sîn-leqi-unninni) tells of semi-divine King Gilgamesh of Uruk (2/3 god, 1/3 human), his tyrannical rule, the gods' creation of the wild man Enkidu as counterpart, their transformative friendship (wrestling bond, shared adventures), the Cedar Forest expedition against Humbaba (with divine winds, despite pleas and curse), the Bull of Heaven episode (Ishtar's rejection and rage), Enkidu's death and Gilgamesh's shattering grief leading to a desperate quest for immortality, the meeting with flood survivor Uta-napishti (deluge narrative underscoring human limits), and return to Uruk with acceptance that legacy lies in walls, deeds, statues, and offspring rather than eternal life.

Subagent extractions across the full text supply:

- Detailed narrative of tyranny-to-wisdom arc, Enkidu's civilizing (Shamhat's taming, loss of animal nature, gain of reason), joint heroism, raw mourning (laments "like a lioness deprived of her cubs," funeral rites/offerings to netherworld gods), and philosophical dialogues on mortality.

- Sumerian variants (political assembly in "Gilgamesh and Akka," aura-stripping trickery and Enlil's redistribution in Huwawa, Inanna's tantrum and Bull in "Bull of Heaven," detailed Netherworld fates Q&A in "Netherworld," gods' grant of Netherworld governorship in "Death of Gilgamesh").

- Textual history and evolution (OB fragments showing name variants/substitutions, prologue summaries, creation parallels; no flood in some early versions).
