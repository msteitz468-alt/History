# Whitman, *Hitler's American Model* (2017)

James Q. Whitman (Yale Law School), *Hitler's American Model: The United States and the Making of Nazi Race Law* (Princeton University Press, 2017), ~135 pp. main text + notes.

**Thesis.** In the early 1930s, as Nazi lawyers drafted the Nuremberg Laws (Reich Flag Law, Reich Citizenship Law, and the Law for the Protection of German Blood and German Honour), they took a sustained, significant, and sometimes eager interest in American race law as a model and source of precedent. Nazi Germany looked to the United States — not merely the Jim Crow South, but federal immigration/naturalization law, state second-class citizenship regimes for Blacks and others, and especially state anti-miscegenation statutes criminalizing interracial marriage — as the "classic example" and innovative world leader in explicitly racist legislation. The most radical Nazis (notably Roland Freisler) championed American approaches most vigorously; moderates (Gürtner, Lösener) were more cautious. Direct influence is visible in the Prussian Memorandum (1933), the June 5, 1934 planning meeting transcript (where a Ministry memo on US law was presented and discussed at length), and the final form of the 1935 Blood Law. The Nazis were aware of, and sometimes rejected, aspects of American practice they viewed as too harsh (e.g., the one-drop rule) or constrained by liberal constitutionalism. This history does not explain the genesis of Nazism or the Holocaust; American liberal and democratic traditions were also repellent to the Nazis, and responsibility remains German. It does, however, show that when the Nazis set out to build a "race state," they turned first to American precedents for techniques of racist lawmaking.

The book is a focused comparative legal history, not a general account of Nazi racism or US–German relations. It engages and revises the prior consensus (Rethmeier, Bernstein, Guettel, Hanke) that Nazi references to US law were insignificant, fleeting, or mere propaganda.

## Historiography and Source Notes (preliminary)

Primary sources: verbatim transcript of the June 5, 1934 Strafrechtskommission meeting (two versions in Regge/Schubert, *Quellen zur Reform des Straf- und Strafprozeßrechts*); Prussian Memorandum 1933; Nazi legal handbooks and journals (*Nationalsozialistische Monatshefte*, etc.); US state anti-miscegenation statutes, Immigration Act 1924, case law (*Plessy*). Whitman supplies his own translations. The book is explicitly revisionist against a reassuring post-war consensus that minimized foreign models for Nazi crimes. Recent scholarship (post-2017) reception not yet tracked in wiki; flag for update. Collection bias note: excellent for the German legal-bureaucratic side; thinner on US domestic politics and African-American responses to the 1935 laws (those belong to other sources).

## Key Claims Extracted (reconciled; grounded in source)

- June 5, 1934 meeting transcript: Gürtner presented US race-law memo; radicals (Freisler) championed it as model for criminalization and "primitive" categories ("would suit us perfectly"); moderates noted US law targeted "coloreds," classified Jews Caucasian, and was sometimes "too harsh." (Primary evidence for influence debate.)
- Specific domains of interest: US immigration/naturalization (1790–1924 quotas), second-class citizenship (disfranchisement devices, Insular Cases non-citizen nationals), anti-miscegenation statutes (~30 states, criminal penalties, classification rules). Cited repeatedly by Hitler, Krieger, Kier, Koellreutter et al.
- Prussian Memorandum (1933): explicit Jim Crow citation; proposed "Race Treason" crimes.
- Limits and selectivity: one-drop and some US practices rejected as excessively severe; no literal copy of statutes; influence conceptual (criminalization of mixing, open racist categories vs. US subterfuge).
- Broader: US as "leading racist jurisdiction" and "classic example" in Nazi eyes; parallels noted to New Deal Legal Realism and "bold experimentation" vs. formalist constraints. Qualifications preserved: does not explain genesis of Nazism; US had liberal counter-traditions; Nazis brought centralized fascist state power and mercilessness absent in US.
