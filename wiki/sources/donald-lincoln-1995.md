# Lincoln (David Herbert Donald, 1995)

**Source page for David Herbert Donald’s *Lincoln* (1995), the premier modern one-volume biography.**

Donald’s work is a political and personal biography centered on Lincoln’s own perceptions and information environment. It traces his rise from frontier poverty and self-education through law and Whig/Republican politics to the presidency, with deep attention to how an inexperienced leader grew into command of a civil war that abolished slavery and preserved the Union. The preface explicitly frames the method: biography “written from Lincoln’s point of view,” using what he knew when critical actions were required.

### From Range 01 (Front matter + Preface + early narrative / Ch. 1–2; lines ~1–7000)

**R01-01-Lincoln-Passivity-Doctrine-of-Necessity**  
Donald frames his biography around Lincoln’s own perspective on decisions—what he knew at the time—and stresses Lincoln’s lifelong sense that larger forces shaped his life rather than his own initiative. Lincoln explicitly recognized this trait in an 1864 letter and traced it to an early acceptance of the Doctrine of Necessity.  
Grounding: “I claim not to have controlled events, but confess plainly that events have controlled me.” “From his earliest days Lincoln had a sense that his destiny was controlled by some larger force... the Doctrine of Necessity, which he defined as the belief ‘that the human mind is impelled to action, or held in rest by some power, over which the mind itself has no control.’”

**R01-02-Lincoln-Ancestry-Self-Made-Man**  
Lincoln presented himself as a self-made man of humble origins with almost no interest in or knowledge of his ancestry, offering only the barest outline when asked for biographical information in 1859–1860. He believed his mother was the illegitimate daughter of Lucy Hanks and an unnamed “well-bred Virginia farmer or planter.”  
Grounding: “My parents were both born in Virginia, of undistinguished families—second families, perhaps I should say.” “it is a great piece of folly to attempt to make anything out of my early life. It can all be condensed into a single sentence, and that sentence you will find in Gray’s Elegy, The short and simple annals of the poor.”

**R01-04-Nancy-Hanks-Lincoln-Death-Milk-Sickness**  
Nancy Hanks Lincoln died on October 5, 1818 (when Abraham was nine), of milk sickness. She called her children to her bedside and exhorted them “to be good and kind to their father—to one an other and to the world.”  
Grounding: “Nancy fell ill. She struggled on, day after day, for a week... She died on October 5...” “Deeper consequences of the loss of his mother before he was ten years old... gave a gloomy cast to his memories of his Indiana home.”

**R05-Rise-1858-National-Visibility**  
The 1858 Lincoln-Douglas debates and “House Divided” speech gave Lincoln a national hearing on the great question of the age. Herndon predicted after hearing the draft: “Lincoln, deliver that speech as read and it will make you President.” Lincoln carefully oversaw the printed version in the Illinois State Journal, with paragraphing and italics exactly as he wanted.  
Grounding: “By this point Herndon had reconsidered his objections and was the only member of this little group to urge Lincoln to make the speech, predicting—if belated memories can be trusted—'Lincoln, deliver that speech as read and it will make you President.' *Herndon’s Lincoln*, 2:400.” (Note 209, WHH to Jesse W. Weik, Oct. 29, 1885, HWC.) “A house divided against itself cannot stand. I believe this government cannot endure permanently half slave and half free.” (CW, 2:461–462, quoted in note 206; Lincoln oversaw the *Illinois State Journal* printing.)

**R05-SignatureDecisions-1858-Challenge-and-Speech**  
Lincoln actively sought the hearing against Douglas despite being the lesser-known candidate. Friends urged him not to deliver the House Divided speech (too radical or conservative); Lincoln proceeded anyway and controlled the printed text. The challenge itself and the speech’s moral framing (“slavery... a political wrong,” “of ultimate extinction,” “the whole earth”) were calculated positioning.  
Grounding: “make himself available”; “thick and thin”; “than anything else” (CW, 2:472). Friends’ objections: Joseph Gillespie statement; “not do that” (Herndon’s Lincoln, 2:398). Lincoln told Douglas the speech contained “no acceptance in it” (CW, 3:120). “A house divided... all the other.” (CW, 2:461–462, with Lincoln overseeing printing.)

**R05-SignatureDecisions-Freeport-and-1858-Legend-Debunk**  
At Freeport Lincoln asked whether the people of a Territory could exclude slavery prior to statehood (Freeport Doctrine). Douglas answered that they could by “unfriendly legislation.” The range debunks the later legend that Lincoln deliberately asked this in 1858 to deprive Douglas of Southern support in the 1860 election.  
Grounding: “the slavery question?” (Johannsen ed., p. 79). Douglas: “against unfriendly legislation” (p. 146–147). “Explaining the true intent of the Freeport question, Fehrenbacher (*Prelude to Greatness*, pp. 122–128) demolishes the legend that Lincoln, against the warnings of his advisers, asked the question in order to deprive Douglas of Southern support in the 1860 presidential election.” (Note 219.)

**R05-Historiography-Debates-Text-and-Scholarship**  
There is no wholly reliable text of the debates. Lincoln’s scrapbook (basis for 1860 Follett edition) corrected only his own typographical errors and removed applause/laughter brackets. Basler (CW) and Angle restored interruptions. Holzer used hostile press for Lincoln and friendly for Douglas; Donald prefers Johannsen but checked Holzer. Scholarly views shifted from Beveridge/Randall (“deserve little notice”) to Jaffa, Zarefsky, Ericson, Fehrenbacher (“by far the best analysis”).  
Grounding: “There is no wholly reliable text of the debates.” Lincoln scrapbook details; “I am not persuaded that this tactic necessarily gives a more authentic version...” (Holzer discussion). Fehrenbacher on Freeport legend and election mechanics (*Prelude to Greatness*, pp. 118–120, 122–128).

**R05-ElectionMechanics-1858-Illinois**  
Republicans won the popular vote but lost the Senate due to malapportionment. Republican districts averaged 19,655 inhabitants; Democratic only 15,675. “Douglas... ‘was elected for the reason that 750 voters in ‘Egypt’ are an offset to 1000 in Canaan.’”  
Grounding: “to their numbers” (*Illinois State Journal*, Nov. 10, 1858; Rockford Register, Nov. 13, 1858, note 228); “were closely divided” as a whole.

**Main-thread synthesis note (reconciled across ranges):** Donald presents Lincoln as a self-made man whose early fatalism and personal losses (mother, Ann Rutledge) produced both melancholy and extraordinary steadiness under pressure. His political genius lay in timing and language: the House Divided speech, Cooper Union research, Gettysburg Address, and Second Inaugural reframed the war’s meaning around the Declaration’s promise of equality. As president he grew from inexperience (Sumter crisis, cabinet management, early generals) into mastery, using emancipation as a military and moral instrument, elevating Grant, and insisting on generous terms at Appomattox while reserving ultimate peace authority. The 1864 election tested and affirmed democratic self-government. The assassination cut short his reconstruction vision of magnanimity, black suffrage for the deserving, and rapid restoration of Southern states under loyal (if imperfect) governments. Claims emphasize Lincoln’s growth, realism about slavery’s offense, and refusal to shed first blood while refusing to surrender the Union or its moral fruits.
