# The Fall of Carthage — Adrian Goldsworthy (2000)

(Published in the US as *The Punic Wars*.) A single-volume operational-military
history of the whole century-long struggle between Rome and Carthage (264–146 BC),
written by a specialist in the Roman army. Goldsworthy's distinctive contribution is
to treat **all three wars as one connected struggle** — each war's causes and aims
determined by the outcome of the last — and to analyse the campaigns **within the
military culture of their own period** rather than as proto-modern operations. He is
emphatic that "warfare is affected as much by culture as any other human pursuit":
Rome and Carthage fielded armies at the same technological level but organized,
commanded, and conceived of war very differently, and **those differences ultimately
decided the outcome**.

This source supplies the warfare hub's backbone for the Punic Wars: it creates the
First and Third Punic War hubs (previously absent), deepens the Second Punic War hub
beyond the existing Cannae exemplar, and underwrites template-grade analyses of ten
battles and the siege of Carthage.

## Argument in brief

- **Roman war-making was structurally decisive.** Rome's manpower (the Italian
  allied system / *socii*), its political-military fusion (senators were both
  politicians and generals), and above all its refusal to accept that a war was lost
  — "the deep-seated determination with which the Romans waged war" — let it absorb
  catastrophes (Trebia, Trasimene, Cannae; the loss of whole fleets) that would have
  ended any Hellenistic state.
- **Carthage fought a different kind of war.** Reliant on mercenary and allied
  contingents and a narrower citizen base, Carthage waged wars of limited commitment
  and was repeatedly willing to negotiate; even Hannibal's genius could not convert
  battlefield annihilation into political victory because Rome would not concede.
- **The wars are continuous.** The Second War grew from Carthaginian resentment of
  the unjust peace of 241 (and the seizure of Sardinia); the Third from Rome's
  decision, once Carthage revived, that the city must be destroyed (*Carthago
  delenda est*).
- **Anti-anachronism.** Goldsworthy resists the 19th–20th-c. tradition (Schlieffen,
  Liddell Hart, Fuller) of mining the wars for timeless tactical lessons; he asks
  *why* a commander decided as he did, not how he might have done "better."

## Historiography & reliability

Goldsworthy's source-criticism (Introduction) frames the whole wiki's Punic-War
historiography:

- **Polybius** (2nd c. BC, Greek) — eyewitness to the fall of Carthage, intimate of
  Scipio Aemilianus, the most reliable narrator; "usually to be preferred" where
  accounts differ, but his text survives complete only to 216 and in fragments
  thereafter, and he is partial to the Scipios/Aemilii.
- **Livy** (Augustan) — the fullest continuous account of the Second War (Books
  20–30) but uneven, patriotic, and capable of real error; to be used with caution.
- **Appian** (2nd c. AD) — the indispensable narrative of the **Third** War, drawing
  on Polybius' lost books.
- **The Punic side is gone.** No Carthaginian account survives; even the
  sympathetic Greek narratives (Philinus, Sosylus) are lost. The First War is thinly
  recorded; numbers are corrupt; this is irreducibly "the story of Rome's wars."

This makes the source authoritative for operational narrative while honest about
where the wiki must hedge — see [[controversies/roman-grand-strategy]] and the
per-battle Historiography sections for the live debates (e.g. troop numbers at
Cannae and Zama, the reality of the *corvus*, Hannibal's Alpine route).

## Cross-source position

Goldsworthy complements [[jones-the-art-of-war-in-the-western-world-1987|Archer Jones]]
(who reads the Punic Wars through his persisting-vs-raiding and weapon-systems
framework) with a tighter operational lens, and it supplies the campaign substrate
that the strategic-theory canon ([[clausewitz-on-war-1832|Clausewitz]] on Hannibal as genius;
[[sources/paret-makers-of-modern-strategy-1986|Makers of Modern Strategy]] on
Schlieffen's Cannae cult) presupposes. Goldsworthy's companion volume [[goldsworthy-cannae-2001|*Cannae* (2001)]],
already ingested, drills into the single battle that the wiki's exemplar analysis covers.
