# Open Veins of Latin America (Galeano, 1971)

The single most widely read statement of the **dependency** reading of Latin American
history: that the region's underdevelopment is not a failure or a backwardness-to-be-overcome
but the **active product** of its incorporation, as a plundered periphery, into the world
capitalist economy. "Latin America is the region of open veins" — its silver, gold, sugar,
rubber, nitrate, copper, oil, and labor "transmuted into European — or later United States —
capital… accumulated in distant centers of power."

## Argument

Galeano's thesis, applied relentlessly across every commodity and century:

- **"Our wealth has always generated our poverty by nourishing the prosperity of others."**
  The same places that produced the greatest riches — [[potosi-and-colonial-silver|Potosí]],
  the Brazilian Northeast, Amazonian rubber, Chilean nitrate — became the poorest, left as
  "desolate warrens" once the wealth was drained.
- **The colonial division of labor.** [[global-silver-trade|American silver]] financed
  European (not Spanish) capitalism — "Spain owned the cow, others drank the milk" — as
  Marx's *primitive accumulation* (Galeano cites *Capital* and Ernest Mandel): the loot of
  the Americas, the Indies, and the slave trade exceeded all European industrial capital
  c. 1800 and "directly financed" the Industrial Revolution, while *preventing* accumulation
  in the plundered lands.
- **King Sugar and the monocultures.** The [[plantation-economy|plantation]]→*latifundio*
  is "a sieve for the draining-off of natural wealth": each export boom (sugar, cacao,
  cotton, coffee, rubber, henequen, quebracho) ravages soil and society, then collapses,
  leaving "a culture of poverty." "The more a product is desired by the world market, the
  greater the misery it brings to the Latin American peoples whose sacrifice creates it."
- **The contemporary structure of plunder.** The same logic continues through nineteenth-
  century free trade (railways built only to drain exports), the latifundio, and twentieth-
  century **imperialism** — US Marines, multinationals (United Fruit, General Motors), the
  IMF and World Bank, foreign loans and investment, and a comprador "commission-agent
  bourgeoisie." Dependent industrialization spreads unemployment rather than relieving it.

The 1978 **"Seven Years After"** postscript revisits the argument amid the era's military
dictatorships (Chile 1973, Argentina, Uruguay, Brazil), reading them as the violent defense
of the dependent order against redistribution.

## Place in the wiki

Galeano supplies the **dependency-theory counterpoint** to the Americas-gap colonial spine.
Where [[burkholder-johnson-colonial-latin-america|Burkholder & Johnson]] describe a colonial
order of negotiated institutions and stress that much survived independence, Galeano reads the
*entire* five centuries as one continuous act of extraction — the most forceful articulation
of the [[causes-of-latin-american-underdevelopment|underdevelopment controversy]]. It anchors
the new [[dependency-theory]] concept page and enriches [[colonial-latin-america]],
[[potosi-and-colonial-silver]], [[plantation-economy]], and [[latin-america-modern]].
