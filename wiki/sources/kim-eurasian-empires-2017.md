# Eurasian Empires in Antiquity and the Early Middle Ages

Edited volume (Cambridge University Press, 2017; 354 pp.) edited by Hyun Jin Kim
(Melbourne), Frederik Juliaan Vervaet (Melbourne) and Selim Ferruh Adalı (Ankara).
Studies the great pre-industrial empires of Eurasia — Rome, Han and Tang China,
Assyria, Persia, the Huns, Kushans, Parthians and Franks — holistically, as a single
interconnected field, stressing the role of **Inner Asian empires and peoples in
facilitating contact and exchange across Eurasia** in antiquity and the early Middle
Ages. Four thematic parts: political organization, socio-institutional aspects,
cultural legacies, and archaeology.

## Volume Synthesis Note

The volume's controlling argument, restated in its conclusion, is that **Inner Asia was the
geographical pivot of Eurasia** in antiquity and the early Middle Ages, and that the great
sedentary empires — Rome, the Franks, Assyria, Persia, Tang China, the Kushans — are best
understood as a single interconnected system rather than as separate national histories. Its
distinctive contributions to the wiki are: (a) a coherent, if **contested**, thesis that
steppe political organization propagated westward into European state formation (Kim); (b) a
set of rigorous **Rome–Han comparisons** on honour, slavery/forced labour, and canonical
texts, which map onto the wiki's `comparisons/` section and complement the existing
[[unfree-labor-systems-compared]] page for a later era; and (c) fresh **archaeological**
material for under-covered ground — the Caucasus frontier ([[samtavro-cemetery]]) and Central
Asian [[khorezm|Khorezm]].

Two tensions with existing wiki content are worth flagging. Kim's steppe→Frankish transfer
thesis sits against the Roman-continuity account on [[merovingian-franks]] (recorded there as
an attributed, contestable claim, not a silent overwrite). Negus Cleary's "mobile capital"
reading of Khorezm explicitly overturns the older Tolstov/Wittfogel *hydraulic-state* model.
Both are handled as attributed interpretive positions per the wiki's no-wiki-voice-
adjudication rule. Scheidel's and Lieu's chapters are the most heavily documented and travel
best; the two editors' programmatic framing (chs. 1 and the conclusion) is the most
interpretive and is labelled as such throughout.
