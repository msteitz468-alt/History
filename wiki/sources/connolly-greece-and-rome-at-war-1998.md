# Peter Connolly, *Greece and Rome at War* (new and revised edition, 1998)

A lavishly illustrated reference work on the military systems, equipment, formations, and selected campaigns of Greece and Rome from the emergence of the city-states (c. 800 BCE) through the later Roman Empire. Connolly emphasises practical reconstructions based on surviving artifacts, experimental archaeology, and close analysis of how equipment and formations actually functioned in battle. The book provides both broad historical surveys and highly detailed treatments of arms, armour, phalanx/legion organisation, and tactics, with notable depth on the Persian Wars, the Hannibalic War, and the Battle of Pydna.

This is a Tier 4 reference source in the Outstanding War Strategy Sources roadmap specifically flagged for kit, formations, and reconstruction detail to support the classical-antiquity section of the [[hubs/warfare/classical-antiquity|warfare hub]]. It supplies the material and visual layer that pairs with more narrative/operational works (Goldsworthy, Hanson, Kagan) and cultural analyses (Lendon). Essential for accurate order-of-battle tables, equipment descriptions, and "how it worked" explanations in battle pages.

### Foreword and Overall Approach
- **Connolly’s contribution via practical reconstruction and experimentation**: Connolly copied and tested a hoplite shield from the Vatican Museum, revealing advantages of the sharp curve on the inside rim and armourer’s problems. Experiments showed it was impossible to use a two-handed pike with a standard hoplite shield but possible with the notched Boeotian rim. “It is perhaps in his treatment of arms and armour that Peter Connolly makes his greatest contribution.” (Foreword, H.H. Scullard).
- The book surveys military systems, equipment, and selected campaigns from 8th c. BC Greek city-states through the later Roman Empire, with emphasis on practical function of kit and formations.

### Greek Phalanx and City-State Warfare (City States 800–360 BC)
- **Phalanx revolution (8th c. BC)**: Abandoned “free for all” heroic fighting for disciplined phalanx — orderly battle line several ranks deep, divided into files (short files, not long ranks). Each file a unit; fallen man replaced from behind. Open order (two paces/man) or close order. “The new formation was strengthened by the introduction of the Argive shield probably early in the 7th century. This was a round buckler which did away with the age-old central handgrip and was fastened to the left forearm; it was held across the chest covering the warrior from chin to knees. In close order... the shield was wide enough to offer protection to the unguarded side of the man on the left.”
- **Equipment evolution**: Bronze cuirass → lighter/cheaper linen corselet (layers glued to ~0.5 cm stiff shirt, wrapped/tied, shoulder flaps, often scale-reinforced; author’s copy 3.6 kg). Greaves (full lower leg 7th c., later shorter/muscled). Helmets: Kegel → Illyrian → Corinthian (flexible cheeks, lining essential) → Chalcidian/Attic (no nose guard) → Thracian. Spears ~2–3 m (ash, leaf blade, butt spike); swords evolved from longer slashing to shorter leaf-shaped cut-and-thrust (~60 cm); kopis (curved Etruscan-origin). Chigi vase shows transitional throwable spears + adopted bronze armour.
- **Spartan organization and drill**: Enomotia (basic unit, e.g. 36 men) in files → deploy 3 abreast then 6. Lochos = 4 enomotiai (~144 in Xenophon’s day). Mora = 6 lochoi. Deployment from column (three abreast) to phalanx by wheeling/closing files; rear half moves up. Flanking “horns”: wings turn outward into column, advance, wheel inward. Pre-battle: garlands, nanny-goat sacrifice, paean to flutes, level spears, run/charge on trumpet. Clash: othismos (rear ranks press and strike over shoulder). Spartan discipline emphasized holding formation over individual heroism (Tyrtaeus; criticised Aristodemus at Plataea for endangering comrades). Uniform scarlet cloaks. Hippeis = 300 elite infantry bodyguard. Training: harsh from age 7; “return with your shield or on it.”
- **Athenian structure**: Citizens 17–59 liable (~30,000 hoplites). Ten taxeis (one per tribe) commanded by taxiarchs; subdivided into lochoi. Ten strategoi elected annually. Fought eight deep.
- **Theban innovations**: Deeper formations (Delium 25 deep; Leuctra 50 deep). Sacred Band (300 picked male lovers, trained elite). Echelon tactics + massed left wing (Epaminondas at Leuctra/Mantinea). Connolly proposes two-handed pike + notched Boeotian shield rim for the phalanx.
- **Light troops**: Peltasts (Thracian origin, pelta rimless skin shield, javelins, speed-based; used run-in/throw/run-out). Archers/slingers (Rhodians best, >350 m). Protected wings or threw from behind phalanx (sloping ground ideal).

### Macedonian Developments
- **Philip II reforms (359 BC onward)**: Infantry reorganized into phalanx (Theban model); austere training (forced marches with full kit); baggage limits (one servant per 10 men, carry 30 days’ rations); “foot companions.” Units based on multiples of 8 (dekas/file of 16 by Alexander’s time).
- **Sarissa phalanx**: Two-handed pike (Theophrastus 12 cubits/~5.4 m; Polybius 14 cubits originally 16). Possibly two-part (iron sleeve join, Vergina finds). Weight ~6.5–8 kg. Round shield ~60 cm. Front ranks metal cuirass/greaves; others linen or none. Equipment possibly state-supplied (Amphipolis inscription fines for loss).
- **Organization (later Hellenistic per Asclepiodotus/Polybius)**: Syntagma basic unit 256 men (16 files × 16) + supernumeraries (ouragos, hyperetes, stratokeryx, semeiophoros, salpingktes). Full hierarchy down to files (protostates front/epistates rear). Officers in front rank. Larger units (taxeis, etc.) hypothetical. Phalanx heavier/less mobile than Alexander’s; centre more important than wings; reduced cavalry emphasis in Europe (1:20 inf:cav vs. Alexander’s higher ratio).
- **Alexander’s army and campaigns**: Invasion force 30,000 inf + 5,000 cav (later ~44k/6.5k); breakdown (Macedonian Companions, Thessalians, allies, Agrianian light). Cavalry reorganized (ilai/hipparchies). Use of elephants (post-Alexander introduction at Gaugamela/Hydaspes; towers by Pyrrhus; Indian vs. African forest types; countermeasures spikes/caltrops). Reconstructions of array, sarissa use, and battle mechanics (e.g., Granicus, Issus, Hydaspes).

### Early Roman and Italian Systems (Rise of Rome 800–275 BC)
- **Etrusco-Roman / 6th c. BC**: Etruscans adopted Greek hoplite phalanx (archaic lochos 12×8 + ouragoi); mixed with Italian elements (Negau helmets, round breastplates, feathers). Rome under Tarquins: Etruscans formed phalanx core; Romans/Latins on wings with freer style (spears, axes, javelins). Mixed panoplies (Argive shield + Italian helmet/greaves).
- **Servius Tullius reform (mid-6th c.)**: Reorganized by wealth into 6 classes (not race). Class I (80 centuries): helmet, Argive clipeus, greaves, cuirass, spear, sword + 2 engineer centuries. Lower classes progressively lighter (scutum instead of clipeus; no cuirass/greaves; javelins/slings). Poorest exempt. Split by age (veterans home guard). Army: 2×40 lochoi phalanx + lighter support. Centuries provided enomotiai for field use. Political + military purpose.
- **Other Italian systems (for context/adaptation)**: Aequi/Volsci (Capestrano type: pot helmet, disc breast/back plates, throwable javelins, axes, Greek-style swords, no shield or non-metallic scutum). Samnites (loose formation; Argive or scutum/thureos; javelins + kopis; broad decorated belts; triple-disc cuirass; Samnite Attic helmets with feathers). Celts/Gauls (long slashing swords evolving; mail or naked early; long oval shields; heroic single combat emphasis; chariots; weak discipline per classical authors but could form lines).
- **Transition to manipular (4th c. per Livy)**: Phalanx abandoned; legion split into three lines with gaps (quincunx). Rear: 15 ordines (triarii veterans + rorarii + accensi). Middle: 15 maniples principes. Front: 15 maniples hastati + 20 leves each. ~5,000 total (double centuries). Tactics: leves skirmish → withdraw; hastati charge (pila + swords) → principes if needed; triarii kneel as “palisade” last resort. Retreat through lanes if needed.

### Republican Legion c. 160 BC (Polybius) and Punic Wars Reconstructions
- **Structure**: 4,200 inf + 300 cav (5,000 in danger). Velites (1,200 youngest/poorest): small javelins (hasta velitaris), sword, parma shield. Hastati (1,200): 10 maniples. Principes (1,200). Triarii (600, always 600): 10 maniples (hastae). Each maniple (hastati/principes) 120 heavy + 40 velites; triarii 60 heavy + 40 velites. 2 centuries per maniple (prior/posterior centurions + optio + signifer) but tactical unit = maniple. Cavalry 10 turmae of 30. Allies alae on wings; extraordinarii (1/3 best cav + 1/5 inf). Pay/rations deducted.
- **Camp and marching (Polybius)**: ~800 m square on raised ground. Groma used for rectangular grid (via principalis, via praetoria). Ditch + turf-faced rampart + stakes (40–50k for perimeter; 13–16 per metre, intertwined). Internal: legions beyond via principalis on either side of via praetoria; cavalry facing it; triarii back-to-back with others. 50 ft space for via quintana. Praetorium/quaestorium/forum. Four gates. Velites guard rampart. Pioneers with vanguard. Average 30 km/day.
- **Battle procedures**: Velites harass and retire through gaps. Hastati close gaps, charge (pila volley then sword work leaning on grounded shields). If stalled: trumpet recall, posterior centuries disengage, line retreats through principes gaps. Principes repeat. If needed: triarii close to solid phalanx. Cavalry/velites pursue. Pila designed to pierce shield and buckle (prevent reuse).
- **Cannae (216 BC) reconstruction (topography and phases)**: Roman camp north bank (spur of San Ferdinando di Puglia); smaller south to deny foraging. Selected old river bed for ~2 km plain. Romans right on river, left on hills; infantry close-packed, many ranks deep; cavalry max depth 10. Carthaginians: Spanish/Celtic cavalry left (~6,000 total cav left); Numidians right; infantry crescent (Spanish/Celtic swordsmen alternate speirai; centre dense, flanks thinner); Africans in column behind cavalry (turn to phalanx). Phases: light troops; heavy infantry clash (Romans push crescent flat); Spanish/Celtic cav break through and attack allied horse rear; centre trapped between turning African phalanxes + cavalry return. Bloodiest day; heavy Roman losses (debated figures ~50k+ dead). Ground chosen by Romans; no ambush opportunity for Hannibal.
- **Other Punic reconstructions**: Trasimene ambush (lake level higher; Montegeto spur + hills deployment; 3-sided attack). Ilipa (Scipio conditioned enemy by reversed order days prior; then early deployment on wings + column turn/wheel to outflank). Zama (three lines + elephants; Romans filled gaps with velites; cavalry stripped wings; regrouped principes/triarii on flanks).
- **Siege and naval**: Casilinum (blockade + floating supplies countered by hurdles). Syracuse (Archimedes machines). Taranto (land/sea siege; ships dragged across peninsula). Quinqueremes, corvus (boarding plank with spike; model from captured ship). Blockades (e.g., Philip V fleet burned).

### Transition to Empire and Imperial Organisation
- **Marius reforms**: Property qualification dropped; volunteers (regardless of wealth). Legions carried own gear (“Marius’ mules”). Eagle standard. Professionalization.
- **Cohort legion**: Maniples of triarii raised to same strength and coupled with hastati/principes to form 10 cohorts per legion (instead of 30 maniples). Maniple lost tactical significance (names survived for centurion identification). First cohort later expanded.
- **Early Empire legion (~5,500 total)**: 10 cohorts (1–9 ~500 each = 6 centuries ~80 men; 1st cohort ~800 = 5 centuries). +120 horsemen (scouts/dispatch). All armed alike (sword + pilum). Auxilia: infantry cohorts ~500 (6 centuries) or 1,000; alae cavalry 500 (16 turmae) or 1,000. Cohortes equitatae (mixed). ~25 years service → citizenship (diploma). Local recruiting (except some archers). Pay ~1/3 legionary infantry.
- **Training**: 30 km route marches 3×/month. “Stake” drill with double-weight wicker shield/wooden sword (thrust not slash). Mock battles. Stone slinging, swimming, vaulting on/off horse fully armed.
- **Rome garrison**: Praetorian Guard (9–10 cohorts; higher pay 3½×, 16 yrs; traditional republican dress for palace duty; standards with emperor images). Urban Cohorts (police; traditional republican armour e.g. Montefortino helmet). Vigiles (fire/night police; originally freed slaves).
- **Officers/staff**: Legatus legionis (senatorial); camp prefect (ex-primus pilus, 3rd in command); tribunes (laticlavius young senatorial track; angusticlavii ex-auxiliary); centurions (primus pilus top promotion); immunes (trades/posts with fatigues exemption, 1½ or double pay). Beneficiarii, speculatores, frumentarii, etc.
- **Pay/service**: Deductions for food/clothing/equipment/savings/burial. Donatives after victories/new emperor. 25–26 years service. Unofficial marriages accepted; children later citizenship.
- **Equipment reconstructions (Empire)**: Mail (lorica hamata); Montefortino helmets (urban cohorts confirmed); muscled cuirasses/greaves for officers/tribunes; oval scuta (Praetorians on reliefs); pila (tanged/flat-tang, barbed); standardised auxilia equipment. Trajan’s Column shows details (entrenching, parade, auxiliaries).

### Historiography and Source Notes
- **Value of archaeology and experiment**: Dedications at Olympia (discarded armour in streams/wells); graves (Vulci, Orvieto, Paestum, Capestrano, La Tène finds); situla art; Trajan’s Column; Fayum shield example (~10+ kg laminated); author’s practical copies/tests (shields, cuirasses, pike grip).
- **Literary sources (as presented)**: Herodotus/Thucydides/Xenophon for Greeks; Polybius primary for Roman Republican but Scipionic bias; Livy unreliable (patriotic inventions, modernizes terms, poor topography/tactics, chronology errors — e.g., weather excuses at Cannae/Trasimene); Diodorus, Dionysius, Appian supplementary. For Empire: archaeology/inscriptions/papyri/Trajan’s Column compensate for weak literary record.
- **Adoption and adaptation**: “The organisation and tactics of the Roman army, unlike those of the Greeks, were not an invention of the Romans but the result of a process of adoption and adaptation” (from Italians, Greeks, Celts, Spaniards, etc.).
