# Leonardo da Vinci (Walter Isaacson, 2017)

**Source page for Walter Isaacson, *Leonardo da Vinci* (Simon & Schuster, 2017).**

Isaacson’s biography presents Leonardo da Vinci as the archetype of the Renaissance polymath — the “Renaissance Man” whose art, science, engineering, and imagination were inseparable. The book argues that Leonardo’s genius was human and learnable: driven by relentless curiosity, acute observation, a childlike sense of wonder, and the ability to make connections across disciplines. It covers his entire life chronologically while devoting substantial thematic attention to the notebooks, the science of art (optics, anatomy, perspective), major commissions (Virgin of the Rocks, Last Supper, Mona Lisa, Saint Anne), court service under Ludovico Sforza, Cesare Borgia, the Medici, and Francis I, and his many unfinished projects.

The work fits Isaacson’s ongoing interest in innovators who bridge the arts and sciences (cf. his Einstein, Franklin, Jobs, and Innovators books).

## Approach and Method

Isaacson combines narrative biography with close reading of primary materials. He had the notebooks and drawings read and translated with the help of leading specialists. Structure is largely chronological (following the “Primary Periods of Leonardo’s Life” the book itself provides: Vinci, Florence, Milan 1482–99, Florence 1500–06, Milan 1506–13, Rome, France) with dedicated chapters on the notebooks, specific scientific inquiries (birds/flight, mechanical arts, math, nature of man, anatomy), and individual masterpieces. Emphasis on character: Leonardo as playful, obsessive, easily distracted, left-handed mirror-writer, vegetarian, perfectionist who valued conception and process over completion. Historiographically careful: distinguishes Vasari’s 16th-c. hagiography from notebook evidence; notes mysteries and gaps (illegitimate birth, sexuality, exact fate of some works and remains).

## Range 01 Claims (Formation: early life & training)

- **Illegitimate birth 15 Apr 1452 (Vinci/Anchiano area):** Grandfather Antonio recorded verbatim: “1452: There was born to me a grandson, the son of Ser Piero my son, on the 15th day of April, a Saturday, at the third hour of the night [about 10 p.m.]. He bears the name Leonardo.” Mother Caterina Lippi (16yo orphaned peasant) omitted from birth note and baptism (large public event with 10 godparents incl. Piero di Malvolto). Piero arranged her marriage to Accattabriga shortly after. (Ground: Ch. 1)
- **"Non legittimo" status and enabling effect:** Tax census 1457: “Leonardo, son of the said Ser Piero, non legittimo, born of him and of Caterina, who is now the woman of Achattabriga.” Barred notary guild (Arte dei Giuduci e Notai) and Latin schools but “freed” him for “disciple of experience.” “A GOLDEN AGE FOR BASTARDS” (Burckhardt); “The problem with bastards was that they were part of the family, but not totally” (Kuehn) fostered “adventurous, improvisational creativity” linked to Florence’s artistic flourishing. Raised primarily with grandfather Antonio and uncle Francesco (surrogate father, 15 yrs older). (Ground: Ch. 1)
- **Disciple of experience and mirror writing:** Signed “Leonardo da Vinci, disscepolo della sperientia.” Only abacus school (practical math/analogies). Full verbatim: “I am fully aware that my not being a man of letters may cause certain presumptuous people to think that they may with reason blame me, alleging that I am a man without learning... my subjects require experience rather than the words of others.” Left-handed mirror script practical (“They are not to be read save with a mirror”); distinctive \\ hatching. Never mastered Latin/equations. (Ground: Ch. 1–2)
- **Childhood memories (kite, cave, fossil, deluge):** Kite: “among the first recollections of my infancy, it seemed to me that, as I was in my cradle, a kite came to me and opened my mouth with its tail and struck me several times with its tail inside my lips.” Cave: “Having wandered some distance among gloomy rocks, I came to the mouth of a great cavern... two contrary emotions, fear and desire... Desire won.” Discovered fossil whale; triggered apocalyptic vision (“You lashed with swift, branching fins... the fertile and fruitful earth will be forced to end with the element of fire”). (Ground: Ch. 1)
- **Move to Florence 1464 and stone fable:** After Albiera’s death in childbirth + grandfather’s death, father brought ~12yo Leonardo to Florence (never legitimated). Stone fable verbatim: left “place of solitude and tranquil peace” for road “continual distress from the wheels of the carts... covered with mud or the dung of some animal”; moral “This is what happens to those who leave a life of solitary contemplation and choose to come to dwell in cities among people full of infinite evil.” Yet praised company drawing and thrived in urban centers. (Ground: Ch. 2)
- **Florence stimulation:** “There was no place then, and few places ever, that offered a more stimulating environment for creativity than Florence in the 1400s.” ~40k pop, 1/3 literate (highest Europe); Medici (Cosimo d. 1464); Brunelleschi dome (art+engineering); interdisciplinary botteghe (silk/goldbeaters, perspective, 108 churches). (Ground: Ch. 2)
- **Verrocchio apprenticeship (c. 1466–1477):** Father (client) secured place on merit; Verrocchio “astonished.” Bottega commercial/collaborative (open to street; no signatures; dining table/beds/globe/books Petrarch/Ovid/Sacchetti); topics math/anatomy/dissection/music/philosophy; “particularly geometry.” Likely modeled for bronze David (luxurious curls, flexed veins, motion). 1471 Duomo ball: “loving, meticulous drawings” of hoists/gears; soldered with “fire mirrors”; “mesmerized—at times obsessed”; decades later “Remember how the ball of Santa Maria del Fiore was soldered together in sections?” (Ground: Ch. 2)
- **Shield fantasy+observation:** Assembled real lizards/cricket/snakes/butterflies/grasshoppers/bats into dragon-monster; “labored over it so long that the stench... was past bearing, but Leonardo did not notice it, so great was the love that he bore towards art.” Sold 100/300 ducats. Later rule: “If you wish to make an imaginary animal... take for the head that of a mastiff...” (Ground: Ch. 2)
- **Drapery, chiaroscuro, sfumato invention:** Clay models + plaster-dipped cloth; black/white brush on cambric. Manifesto: “The first intention of the painter... is to make a flat surface display a body as if modeled... arises from light and shade, or we may say chiaroscuro.” Sfumato: “Your shadows and lights should be blended without lines or borders in the manner of smoke losing itself in the air.” “Paint so that a smoky finish can be seen, rather than contours and profiles that are distinct and crude...” (Ground: Ch. 2)
- **Baptism of Christ (mid-1470s):** Painted radiant turning angel (left), Jesus legs, landscape. Oil thin layers/fingertip dabs vs Verrocchio tempera. Dynamic twist: “Always set your figures so that the side to which the head turns is not the side to which the breast faces...” Verrocchio “resolved never again to touch a brush” (Vasari). River narrative + vortices/eddies as “favorite pattern: nature’s spirals.” Clark: Leonardo’s angel “belongs to a world of the imagination which Verrocchio’s never penetrated.” (Ground: Ch. 2)
