# The Years of Lyndon Johnson, Volume 2: Means of Ascent (Robert A. Caro)

**Source page for Robert A. Caro's *Means of Ascent* (1990), the second volume of his definitive biography of Lyndon Baines Johnson.**

This volume covers the period from the 1941 Senate defeat through the 1948 special Senate primary and general election. It examines Johnson's "inactive" Navy service, Lady Bird Johnson's business success (KTBC radio), the building of a financial base, and above all the epic and deeply controversial 1948 campaign against former Governor Coke Stevenson — a campaign that featured helicopter innovation, unprecedented spending, modern media, and ultimately the theft of votes in Box 13 in Jim Wells County that gave Johnson an 87-vote margin.

Caro uses the 1948 race as a case study in the transformation of American politics and in the moral question of "ends and means."

**Claim R01-01-LBJ-1948-Election-Stealing**  
It wasn't eighty-seven votes that Lyndon Johnson stole to win in 1948, but thousands of votes—many thousands, in fact. Thanks in part to a manuscript relating the details of the theft given to the author by the man responsible; interviews with key figures who had never spoken in detail; and court transcripts combined with interviews, a previously bewildering picture comes blindingly clear.  
Grounding: Introduction and related early text.  
Significance: The 1948 election is framed as decisive; Johnson's character—pragmatism shading into amorality—is most visible. Illustrates clash of new vs. old politics.

**Claim R01-02-Coke-Stevenson-Old-Politics-Exemplar**  
Coke Stevenson was not just the old politics; he was the perfect exemplar of that old politics. The absolutism of his campaigning—the rigidity of his refusal to employ modern political techniques—made the 1948 campaign as complete a contrast between the new and the old as can be imagined. It was the last campaign of its type ever waged by a major candidate for statewide office in Texas.  
Grounding: Early framing and Coke biography sections.  
Significance: Illuminates transformation of American politics (electronic/media/manipulation vs. direct contact) and ethical issues of means.  
Links: contrasts_with [[actors/johnson-lyndon-baines]]; part_of election analysis in [[sources/caro-years-of-lyndon-johnson-vol2-1990]].

**Claim R01-11-Means-vs-Ends-Theme**  
The two threads do not run side by side in this volume. The bright one is missing. These seven years are years in which Johnson was all but totally consumed by his need for power. So in this volume can be seen—stark and unadorned—the traits which were later to divide a nation. That campaign raises, in fact, one of the greatest issues invoked by the life of Lyndon Baines Johnson: the relationship between means and ends. To what extent are ends inseparable from means? Of all the questions raised by the life of Lyndon Baines Johnson, no question is more important than that.  
Grounding: Introduction "Ends and Means".  
Significance: Central analytical frame of the volume; 1948 "new politics" vs. Stevenson's idealism makes the moral issues vivid; later civil rights/Vietnam/Credibility Gap threads are introduced as contrast.

**Claim R01-03-LBJ-Campaign-Pledges-vs-Actual-Service**  
In the 1941 Senate campaign and repeatedly thereafter, LBJ pledged to serve "in the front line, in the trenches, in the mud and blood" and to "tear up my draft number and join the boys" if war came, but after Pearl Harbor he sought Washington assignments and spent initial war months on West Coast inspection tours, partying, and political maneuvering rather than combat.  
Grounding: Direct quotes on pledges vs. actual conduct.  
Significance: Reveals contradictions in character (ambition overriding pledges; avoidance of danger until politically necessary).  
Links: part_of [[actors/johnson-lyndon-baines]] via [[hubs/biographies/world-wars-era/johnson-lyndon-baines]]; concurrent_with [[periods/world-wars-era]]; documented in [[sources/caro-years-of-lyndon-johnson-vol2-1990]].

**Claim R01-08-LBJ-War-Story-Exaggeration-and-Self-Conviction**

### From Range 02 (Part I: Navy years, Lady Bird office takeover, KTBC acquisition and early business, House "marking time", loss of fundraising power, postwar depression, decision to run 1948)

**Claim R02-01-Lady-Bird-Self-Discipline**  
Lady Bird Johnson displayed remarkable self-discipline and graciousness in Washington social circles despite her husband's public rudeness and absences.  
Grounding: "To the regulars' condescension, Lady Bird Johnson responded with an unshakable graciousness... 'If everyone was just trying to be nice to her,' she would be nice right back, calm and gracious—'self-contained.'... 'She was forever working,' not only on her reading, but on her figure... And as for her husband's affair with the salon's mistress, 'Oh, of course,' Lady Bird must have known... 'I could never understand how she stood it,' Mary Louise says. 'Lyndon would leave her on weekends, weekend after weekend, just leave her home.' But stand it she did... When, at Texas State Society parties or other Washington social functions, her husband bellowed orders at her across the room, or insulted her, she never showed anything, either."  
Significance: Establishes character evidence of Lady Bird's poise and emotional control under humiliation; contrasts with LBJ's behavior and foreshadows her later public persona.

**Claim R02-03-Lady-Bird-Office-Takeover-1942**  
In early 1942, while LBJ was away on Navy duty, Lady Bird Johnson was placed in charge of his congressional office and gradually asserted authority despite staff resistance and her inexperience.  
Grounding: "On January 29, 1942, he and Connally left for the Coast again, Lady Bird Johnson went to Suite 1320... At first, she didn't behave as though she was in charge... But that changed. Things weren't being done the way Lyndon would have wanted, she felt. She was signing all the letters... 'There was no doubt about it: O.J. and Mary knew more than I ever would,' she recalls, 'but I had one advantage. I had Lyndon's name, and he had a network of friends in the departments... They knew more, but perhaps I cared a bit more.'... 'I was determined, and I wanted to learn. And I was scared.'"  
Significance: Core claim on Lady Bird's emergence; shows causal drivers (staff shortages, LBJ's instructions, her sense of duty) and her learning curve in handling bureaucracy.

**Claim R02-04-Lady-Bird-Gains-Confidence-1942**  
Running the office transformed Lady Bird Johnson's self-perception, giving her confidence she could make her own living and greater understanding of her husband's pressures.  
Grounding: "'After a few months,' she says, 'I really felt that if it was ever necessary, I could make my own living—and that's a good feeling to have... I didn't have a home. I didn't have any children, and although I had a tremendously exciting, vital life, I didn't have any home base, so to speak, except for Lyndon... 'The very best part of it,' she would say, 'was that it gave me a lot more understanding of Lyndon. By the time the end of the day came... I could know what Lyndon had been through.'... '1942 was really quite a great year!'"  
Significance: Analytical point on personal growth during "marking time"/Navy years; character evidence of capability emerging under pressure.

**Claim R02-05-LBJ-Dismisses-Lady-Bird-Post-Return**  
Upon returning from the Pacific, LBJ immediately reasserted pre-war patterns, excluding Lady Bird from political discussions and treating her as before.  
Grounding: "After Johnson returned from the war... Mrs. Johnson says that after her husband's return, 'I did not go into the office regularly.'... Any illusions Mrs. Johnson may have held about now being included in her husband's political discussions were shattered at one of the first of those discussions, when she ventured to stay in the room after it began. 'We'll see you later, Bird,' her husband said, dismissing her. ... 'I talk everything over with her.' Then Lyndon Johnson paused. 'Of course,' he said, 'I talk my problems over with a lot of people. I have a nigger maid, and I talk my problems over with her, too.'"  
Significance: Decision mechanics showing LBJ's control over family/political boundaries; character contrast with Lady Bird's growth.

**Claim R02-07-LBJ-Loses-Fundraising-Power**  
LBJ lost his key source of independent power—the ability to direct Texas oil/construction contributions—when Edwin Pauley and the DNC took over in 1942; Roosevelt provided no intervention.  
Grounding: "Johnson's possession of a measure of political influence... was based on his fund-raising efforts during the 1940 elections... He had expected to perform the same role in 1942... Edwin W. Pauley... had emerged as a Democratic fund-raising force... Because of Pauley's emergence, Johnson was no longer needed... 'these $200 droplets will not get the job done.'... In 1944... Johnson had to sit watching as State Treasurer Harry L. Seay presented the Californian with a $50,000 check—a check that represented the combined contributions of men who had once channeled their checks through him."  
Significance: Critical turning point in LBJ's independent power base; causal link to shift toward business.  
Links: [[actors/johnson-lyndon-baines]] lost fundraising role caused_by Pauley's rise and Roosevelt's priorities; contributed_to marking time.

**Claim R02-08-LBJ-House-Inactivity**  
During his House years (especially wartime and immediate postwar), LBJ introduced almost no national legislation, delivered almost no speeches, avoided taking public stands, and did not fight for causes on the floor or in private.  
Grounding: "In the House, as well as in the war effort, he wasn't being allowed to lead... His record on the introduction of national legislation... had been striking. During his first six years in Congress, he introduced exactly one such bill... During his first months in Congress, in 1937, he delivered a brief memorial tribute... In 1941, he gave a speech advocating the extension of the Selective Service Act—the only other speech he had given in six years... 'He just simply was not especially interested in general legislation that came to the floor of the House... he stayed away from the floor, and while he was there, he was very, very silent.'... Entire years had passed during which he had not employed this device even once... liberal congressmen believe that Lyndon Johnson was a liberal; conservative congressmen believe he was a conservative."  
Significance: Defines "marking time" in Congress—lack of substantive output despite earlier dynamism; character evidence of caution and avoidance of commitment.  
Links: [[actors/johnson-lyndon-baines]]'s House record part_of marking time.

**Claim R02-10-KTBC-Acquisition-Political-Influence**  
LBJ used political leverage (including Alvin Wirtz, prior favors like Naval Academy appointment, and connections) to obtain the option on failing KTBC radio station for Lady Bird and secure unusually rapid FCC approval in 1943.  
Grounding: "Kingsbery... 'I'll recommend that they give it to you, and then we'll be even.'... Johnson wanted someone more influential... George Brown... The Wests agreed to surrender their option on one condition: that the FCC approve the sale... On January 23, 1943, Mrs. Johnson filed an application... On February 16, the Secretary of the FCC wrote, 'The consent of the Federal Communications Commission is hereby granted.' The Wests—first the father, then the sons—had been attempting for three years to obtain such a consent. Mrs. Lyndon Johnson had obtained it in twenty-four days."  
Significance: Core business claim; decision mechanics of using congressional influence for private gain; rapid shift from prior bureaucratic delays.  
Links: [[actors/johnson-lyndon-baines]] orchestrated KTBC purchase via influence; produced [[actors/lady-bird-johnson]] ownership.

**Claim R02-11-FCC-Rapid-Changes-and-Behind-Scenes-Help**  
After Lady Bird's purchase, FCC speedily granted KTBC full-time hours and a desirable new frequency (590); LBJ actively aided the FCC politically (via coded info to Red James, pressing Rayburn against Cox Committee) during its 1943 existential crisis while the application was pending.  
Grounding: "In June, 1943, she applied to the FCC for permission to operate twenty-four hours a day—at a new frequency, 590... Her application... was filed with the FCC on June 25. 'This voice says, 'All right, no names. But today the bald-headed fellow met with the Irishman and the chairman. Significance: Evidence of reciprocal influence trading; primary texture on covert operations (coded calls, Rayburn interventions).  
Links: [[actors/johnson-lyndon-baines]] provided FCC assistance contributed_to rapid approvals for KTBC.

**Claim R02-12-KTBC-Advertisers-and-Revenues**  
Political backers (Herman Brown, Ed Clark, Alvin Wirtz clients, etc.) directed advertising to KTBC; revenues rose sharply after CBS affiliation and influence-based deals (e.g., extra grapefruit allocation for H.E. Butt).  
Grounding: "Herman Brown gave him some advertisers... 'We twisted his arm,' George Brown was to recall... Ed Clark... advised Mr. Butt to advertise on KTBC... In 1944, when the OPA was limiting... Significance: Shows the fusion of political power and business revenue; decision mechanics of "buying" influence via ads; early growth of LBJ financial empire.

**Claim R02-16-Postwar-Marking-Time-Depression-and-Health**  
After the war, LBJ experienced deepening depression, physical symptoms (eczema, smoking, hospitalizations), and anxiety over stalled career, family health legacy, and lack of progress in the House.

'I'm not gonna live to be but sixty. My Daddy died at sixty. My uncle...'... 'Too slow. Too slow.'... 'He was thirty-nine years old... when a man reached forty, it was all over.'"  
Significance: Analytical synthesis of "marking time" period; character evidence of ambition thwarted; causal links to health, family fears, and political stagnation.

**Claim R02-17-Family-and-1948-Decision**  
The Johnsons had daughters (Lynda 1944, Luci 1947) amid health complications; by late 1947 LBJ decided to risk his House seat for a 1948 Senate run against long odds, viewing it as his last real chance.

'FDR—LBJ, FDR—LBJ... What I want is for them to start thinking of me in terms of initials.'... 'I just could not bear the thought of losing everything.'... ### From Range 03 (Coke Stevenson biography + 1948 campaign launch: Valley head start, helicopter, illness crisis, attacks, early erosion)

**Claim R03-01-Stevenson-Road-Building-Persuasion**  
As Kimble County Judge, Coke Stevenson persuaded residents to donate right-of-way and personally engineered low-water road crossings using cardboard models of Model T wheels to determine proper "roll" of dips; the Junction-Kerrville Road remained in use more than twenty years later.  
Grounding: "One was an unusual ability to persuade men to sacrifice for the common weal. ... Stevenson knew nothing about engineering and little about mathematics. But he cut miniature car wheels out of cardboard... and tested the miniature wheels on various concave surfaces to determine the proper 'roll' of the dips. It was... 'testimony of the character of the road he built' that the Junction-Kerrville Road ... was still in use more than twenty years later."  
Significance: Demonstrates Stevenson's practical self-education, persuasive leadership for public good, and lasting impact.  
Links: [[actors/stevenson-coke]] preceded his higher offices through hands-on public service; part of [[places/texas-hill-country]] development.

**Claim R03-02-Stevenson-Law-Ethics**  
Stevenson built a reputation as a premier self-taught trial lawyer who would never defend livestock theft cases and would only represent clients he believed innocent, yet handled the majority of cases in Junction court terms.  
Grounding: "He would never defend a man charged with livestock theft... and he would never accept a client, no matter how large the offered fee, in whose innocence he did not believe. Yet the docket for a single court term at Junction lists 'C. R. Stevenson' as defense attorney in twenty-seven out of thirty-two cases. ... 'one of the greatest trial lawyers in the history of Texas.'"  
Significance: Evidence of strict personal ethics and professional integrity that underpinned his later political reputation for incorruptibility.  
Links: [[actors/stevenson-coke]] maintained these standards from Junction practice into statewide roles; contrasted later with campaign tactics by [[actors/johnson-lyndon-baines]].

**Claim R03-04-Stevenson-Constitutional-Philosophy**  
Stevenson read the Texas and U.S. Constitutions literally "as literally as he read his Bible," embracing their anti-government spirit, limits on power, thrift/frugality, and emphasis on individual liberty over bureaucratic expansion or debt.  
Grounding: "he buried himself in ... the history of the law. ... he became lastingly inspired with the principles set forth in two documents: the constitutions of his country and his state. He read the two constitutions---and took their words---as literally as he read his Bible. ... The Constitution of Texas ... was ... 'an anti-government instrument.' ... 'in him ... there is an ingrained hatred of debt of all kinds.'"  
Significance: Core worldview driving his resistance to bond issues, New Deal expansion, and preference for pay-as-you-go government.  
Links: [[actors/stevenson-coke]] applied this in Austin and as Governor; contrasted with [[actors/johnson-lyndon-baines]]'s approach.

**Claim R03-06-Stevenson-Governorship-Record**  
As Governor (installed 1941 after O'Daniel's Senate move; elected in his own right with record margins including 85% and carrying all 254 counties in 1944), Stevenson turned a $34M deficit into a $35M surplus through "economy" (no dramatic programs), improved education ranking (38th to 24th), tripled pensions, and strengthened unemployment compensation while avoiding overall legislative programs.  
Grounding: "The $34,000,000 state deficit he inherited at his inauguration had become a surplus of $35,000,000 by the time he left office. ... Texas ranked 38th among the states in spending on education when Stevenson came to office; it ranked 24th when he left. ... pensions were tripled. ... 'Mr. Stevenson has given Texas an economy administration,' said one newspaper, and 'that's what the people want.'"  
Significance: Demonstrated strengths of his frontier individualism philosophy in fiscal management.  
Links: [[actors/stevenson-coke]] longest-serving Governor with unique Speaker/Lt. Gov/Governor record.

**Claim R03-09-Johnson-Valley-Head-Start**  
Before the 1948 campaign, Johnson secured a ~25,000-vote head start via the South Texas Valley's controlled "bloc vote" (patrón system under George Parr), shifting from Stevenson's traditional support through prior personal/financial ties (Wirtz negotiations, Brown & Root money, pardon help).  
Grounding: "In 1948, however, the patrónes were not going to be behind Stevenson. ... 'George and Lyndon were very close' ... 'George told me to give our candidates 80 percent of the total votes' ... 'Be sure we elect Johnson.' ... Coke Stevenson was going into the campaign with his great popularity. But before the campaign began, Johnson had a 25,000-vote head start."  
Significance: Machine politics gave Johnson structural advantage in a one-party state.  
Links: [[actors/johnson-lyndon-baines]] leveraged [[actors/stevenson-coke]]'s prior independence from bosses.

**Claim R03-12-Johnson-Kidney-Stone-Illness**  
During campaign kickoff (May 1948), Johnson suffered severe kidney colic with fever/infection (waves of "agonizing" pain radiating to groin/testicles, nausea, chills); he refused surgery (would end campaign), performed at Wooldridge Park rally despite painkillers/morphine, endured Panhandle trip changing shirts 6-7x/day while sweating, and was hospitalized in Dallas before flying to Mayo Clinic.  
Grounding: "the ache changed into the sharp, gripping, radiating cramps ... 'kidney colic,' a pain that ... doctors class kidney colic as one of the three or four most intense pains ... he was running a fever ... 'several shots of pain killer that day and that he didn't know how in the world a man could keep functioning in the pain' ... 'I'm freezing, Woody! I'm freezing!' ... temperature was over 104 degrees."  
Significance: Extreme demonstration of Johnson's willpower and refusal to yield in crisis.  
Links: [[actors/johnson-lyndon-baines]] with support from [[actors/lady-bird-johnson]].

**Claim R03-13-Johnson-Near-Withdrawal-And-Recovery**  
In Dallas hospital (fever/delirium), Johnson dictated a withdrawal statement and ordered it issued immediately; aide Warren Woodward delayed by invoking Lady Bird's imminent arrival; at Mayo's, after successful cystoscopic stone removal (high in ureter, barely possible), Johnson returned after ~2 weeks lost, with little ground gained.  
Grounding: "Dictating a statement irrevocably withdrawing from the campaign ... 'Do it right now' ... 'Why don't you wait until Mrs. Johnson gets here.' ...
