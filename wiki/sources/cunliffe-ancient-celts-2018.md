# The Ancient Celts

**Author:** Barry Cunliffe. Oxford University Press, Second Edition 2018 (first 1997). Major archaeological-historical synthesis.

## Book Note

Barry Cunliffe's *The Ancient Celts* provides the leading modern synthesis of the Iron Age societies of temperate and Atlantic Europe referred to as Celts (or Gauls, Galatae, etc.) by classical authors. It spans from the Late Bronze Age roots through the Hallstatt and La Tène periods, the migrations of the 4th–1st centuries BCE, the developed 'Celtic' world of oppida and emerging polities, Roman interactions and conquest, and the survival of Celtic languages and identities into the early medieval period.

The 2nd edition (2018) is a substantial revision reflecting two decades of debate. It moves away from 19th/early-20th-century models that tied 'Celtic' material culture (Hallstatt/La Tène) directly to a linguistic homeland in west-central Europe with subsequent migrations. Instead, it seriously engages the alternative view that Celtic languages may have developed earlier in the Atlantic zone (as a lingua franca) and spread eastward, and it anticipates the impact of ancient DNA studies for resolving questions of mobility vs. cultural transmission.

Thematically rich: visions and constructions of 'the Celts'; early Atlantic and Mediterranean systems; migrations and their causes; regional communities; warfare, art, and religious systems; the developed world (oppida, social complexity); retreat under Rome; and long-term survival. Cunliffe emphasizes connectivity (trade, mercenary service, elite exchange) alongside regional diversity, and treats the classical sources critically while giving archaeology primacy for social and economic reconstruction.

This is the key structural source for the wiki's treatment of the European Iron Age (especially Early Iron Age onward), Celtic identity debates, oppida urbanization, La Tène art and society, and the transition to Roman and early medieval Europe in western regions.

- **Core historiographic framing**: "'Celtic' of any sort is . . . a magic bag, into which anything may be put, and out of which almost anything may come" (Tolkien, Ch. 1, p. 1, cache ~205–215). Concept built from classical sources, linguistics, archaeology, art, vernacular lit, and nationalism/wishful thinking.
- **Classical vision (Hecataeus to Caesar/Poseidonius)**: Hecataeus (late 6th c. BCE): Massalia near Celts, Narbo Celtic (Ch. 1, p. 4, cache ~315). Herodotus: Danube source in land of the Celts; near Pyrene; Celts west of Pillars next to Cynetoi (Ch. 1, pp. 4–5, cache ~320–330). Ephorus: one of four great barbarians (Ch. 1, p. 5). Aristotle/Aristotelian: hardy, warlike, Druids mentioned (Ch. 1, pp. 5–6). Poseidonius: brave/hospitable but impetuous "boisterous schoolchildren"; Druids "most just of men"; soft primitivist (Ch. 1, pp. 9–11, cache ~490–530). Caesar: "We call [them] Gauls, though in their own language they are called Celts" (Ch. 1, p. 4). Keltoi older term than Galli (Pausanias). Stereotype: fearless in onset but despairing, headhunters, heavy drinkers, barbarians (Ch. 1, p. 8, cache ~390–405). Visualized in Pergamon Dying Gaul and monuments (Ch. 1, pp. 7–9, cache ~420–470).
- **Celtomania to archaeology (1700–1970)**: Stukeley (Druids on megaliths/hillforts); Pezron, Macpherson Ossian fabrication; Iolo Morganwg Gorsedd invention; La Villemarqué *Barzaz-Breiz* (Ch. 1, pp. 16–17). Napoleon III excavations at Alesia/Bibracte for national morale and *Histoire de Jules César* (Ch. 1, pp. 18–20, cache ~770–800). Ramsauer Hallstatt (1846–63, called Celtic); La Tène (1857); 1871 Bologna links Marzabotto to historical Celts → La Tène = Celts (Ch. 2, pp. 40–44, cache ~1450–1560). Chronology: Hildebrand, Tischler, Reinecke (Hallstatt C–D, La Tène A–D); Jacobsthal *Early Celtic Art* (1944) defines original styles (Early, Waldalgesheim, Plastic, Sword) as "first great contribution by the barbarians to European arts" (Ch. 2, pp. 49–51, cache ~1780–1800).
- **Language**: Lhuyd *Archaeologia Britannica* (1707); Continental (Celtiberian 8th c. BCE SW inscriptions Phoenician script; Gaulish; Lepontic 6th c. BCE) vs Insular (Goidelic Q vs Brythonic P). Sims-Williams place names: heavy Atlantic west concentration. Koch: Celtic in SW Iberia by 8th c. BCE. Phylogenetic: distinguishable ~4000 BCE (Ch. 2, pp. 31–37; Ch. 3, pp. 54–55, cache ~1070–1230, ~1870–1890).
- **Definition and origins debate**: "a Celt will be considered to be a person who spoke the Celtic language" (Ch. 3, p. 53, cache ~1840). Celtic already in Atlantic Late Bronze Age; uncoupled from La Tène package. Atlantic lingua franca + Beaker spread east hypothesis vs traditional west-central European homeland/migration model. Kurgan/Anatolian IE debate; compromise possible (Ch. 3, pp. 55–65, cache ~1910–2340).
- **Atlantic Late Bronze Age networks (Ch. 4 opening)**: Shared swords, shields, feasting gear, hillforts, watery votives across façade. Phoenician impact severs full system ~9th c. BCE (Ch. 4, pp. 67–75, cache ~2350–2590).

### R03 Detailed Claims (Range Only, Verbatim Grounding)

**Migrations 450–50 BCE (causes/mechanics/Italy/Greece/Asia Minor Galatians/Egypt):**

- Egypt from Asia Minor horde: "to learn of Celts in Egypt as early as 274 bc serving in the army of Ptolemy II Philadelphos. They were most likely recruited from the horde that entered Asia Minor in 278" (celts and others on the eastern fringes 208). Rebellion and fate: "driving some 4,000 of the mercenaries onto an island in the Sebennytic branch of the Nile, where, Pausanias tells us, ‘they perished by hunger and each other’s swords’." Fayum cleruchs (wooden shield); Raphia 217 BC: "Among the 50,000 or so troops who were eventually amassed there were 6,000 ‘Gauls and Thracians’"; "Gauls still retained their identity throughout the second century bc" (Abydos, Hermopolis, Alexandrian stelae). Delphi memory: "camped in its shadow waiting to pillage Delphi" (celts and others on the eastern fringes 208–209).

- Po valley/Italy: "the continuous threat created by the Celtic settlement in the Po valley were instrumental in the honing of Rome’s military competence." Celts "volatile and unpredictable" vs. Romans (warfare and society 212). 4th-c single combat in Italy (Manlius 361 BC; Valerius 348 BC).

- Galatia: "in Galatia in 189 bc, when the Tolistobogii and Trocmi took off their clothes before battle with the Romans" (warfare and society 223). Brennos/failed leaders include "Brennos fleeing from Delphi" (warfare and society 230).

- Drivers (social/warrior): Feast for proposing raids; potlatch (gifts, ritual death); clientage/hostages; "parasites" praise-singers; torcs, nakedness (Gaesatae, Galatians), carnyces, belief in afterlife enabling ferocity. "The feast may well have been the moment when a warrior would propose to lead a raid" (warfare and society 226–234).

**Atlantic façade communities (Britain, Gaul, Iberia):**

- Britain/Ireland: Sling "stockpiles ... Maiden Castle and Danebury"; chariots in Britain per Caesar ("combined the staying power of infantry with the mobility of cavalry"; Cassivellaunus 4,000); "insular Britons"; "doubtful if the war chariot was ever seen in Ireland". Power figures: Cartimandua, Boudica (gold torc). Artifacts: Witham/Battersea shields, Welwyn, Newbridge (Scotland), Lough Crew (Ireland), carnyces (warfare and society 217–232; arts).

- Gaul/France: Caesar on "Gaulish warfare", cavalry, classes ("Druids and the knights"); clientage (Aedui etc.); sites: Saint-Pol-de-Léon (Finistère), Tintignac (Corrèze), Euffigneix, Marne, Garonne (warfare and society; arts 239+).

- Iberia: "brooch, possibly of Iberian origin"; Celtiberians/Lusitanians (warfare and society 215, 233).

**Art of the Migration Period (Po Valley migration links):**

- "broad cultural similarity that can be seen over vast tracts of country and the dazzling inventiveness of what can broadly be called La Tène (or Celtic) art." Symbolism (boar/bird as war gods; "Cheshire Cat Style" faces; torcs); techniques (cire perdue, repoussé, enamelling, iron swords/ingots). (the arts of the migration period 239–247).

- Early Style (5th c): Etruscan-inspired flagons (Kleinaspergle, Basse-Yutz, Dürrnberg) + eastern/Scythian animals + Hallstatt echoes; gold openwork (Schwarzenbach, Rodenbach, Erstfeld). (the arts ... 247–252).

- 4th c Vegetal Style & Po Valley: Innovation centres "the Marne, Switzerland, Hungary, and the Po valley". "It is now widely believed that the Celtic groups in the Cisalpine region, notably the Boii and the Senones, were responsible for integrating Graeco-Italic ideas with Celtic ones and transmitting the result back to the innovating centres north of the Alps." "close relationship between the craftsmen of the Po valley and Marne in the fourth century." Jockey-cap helmets evidence. "Celtic Art in the Fourth Century: Influences from the Po Valley". (the arts of the migration period 252–254).

**Source critique (range):** "The Graeco-Roman world had many opportunities to observe Celtic armies in action." Biases detailed for Polybius (Rome vs Celt contrast), Poseidonius (Noble Savage), Caesar (prejudice for justification), Pausanias (formulaic), Tacitus. **R02 claims (lines 3201–6800; Ch. 3–5 focus: Late Bronze Age roots, Atlantic connectivity, Hallstatt developments, Mediterranean interactions; grounded exclusively in this range):**

- **European re-formation after Aegean collapse, 1000–800 BCE:** "The first two centuries of the first millennium bc constituted a crucial period in the formation of Europe. During this time the principal social and economic groupings that were to dominate the scene for over a millennium began to crystallize." The impact of the Aegean systems-collapse on the European hinterland was considerable. Existing exchange systems broke down or were transformed. (west central europe and the mediterranean 96; range ~74–78). Urnfield cultural continuum emerged: "extending from eastern France to Hungary and from Italy to Poland, there emerged in the period from about 1300 to 800 bc a considerable degree of cultural uniformity... the burial rite, which involves the interment of the cremated remains of the dead in cinerary urns... cast and beaten bronze-work... shows considerable stylistic similarities over large areas." Initial development "in the period 1300–1150 bc in the zone north of the Alps responding to the dislocations... In the Middle and Lower Danube region long-established tell settlements were widely abandoned... By 1000 bc Italy, the Alps, and Transalpine Europe from eastern France to Slovakia were closely interlinked." (96–98).

- **Atlantic system (Bronze–Iron Age connectivity):** "The western fringe of Europe, facing the Atlantic, was altogether different. Here, in a broad arc reaching from Scotland to south-western Iberia, the maritime communities were linked in a complex of exchange networks probably stretching back in time at least to the Neolithic period. By the Bronze Age metals were among the more important commodities shipped: copper from Ireland, Wales, and western Iberia; tin from Cornwall, Brittany, and Galicia; and gold from Ireland, Wales, and north-western Iberia. This metal-rich arc will have provided much of the bronze and gold circulating in western Europe, and the enhanced consumption of metal in the Aegean may well have provided a market eager for gold and tin from the Atlantic zone." With collapse "demand would have slackened, but there is ample evidence for a still-vigorous zone of contact along the seaways... intensification of activity in the ninth century, occasioned... by developing Phoenician interests in the south-west of Iberia." (96; range ~142–155; cross-ref Ch. 4). Phoenician disruption: "the effect of the developing Phoenician interest in Iberia was... to draw the Atlantic coast of the peninsula and of North Africa into the Mediterranean sphere, dislocating the old connectivities of the Atlantic Bronze Age." (100).

- **Hallstatt C (c.800–600 BCE) elites and vehicle burials:** "Within the old Urnfield zone of west central Europe... significant changes can be detected after the beginning of the eighth century bc. This period... is referred to as the Hallstatt C culture... It is at the aristocratic level that the culture is most universal, being reflected in the widespread distribution of warrior gear, in particular the Gundlingen long sword, a type originating in the Atlantic zone... The highest social level was probably that represented by vehicle burials, which in this period were restricted to a zone stretching from the Middle Rhine to Bohemia." Vehicles: "four-wheeled and comparatively heavy... spoked wheels, strengthened by iron tyres... wooden yokes studded with decorative bronze nails." "The long slashing sword suitable as a cavalry weapon, horses, ceremonial vehicles, the rite of inhumation, and the widespread use of iron for weapons mark a distinct break with traditional Urnfield culture." Influences: "The horses and the style of trappings are essentially eastern in origin... The inspiration for the vehicle... transmitted from the east via the Mediterranean... via Etruria." "an elite eagerly absorbing cultural influences from the east via the mid-Danube, from the south through the east Alpine passes, and from the Atlantic zone to the west." (101–103; range ~313–365).

- **Salt/mining economies:** "From about 1000 bc salt, mined at Hallstatt, would have contributed to the flow of commodities passing along the east Alpine corridor. About 600 bc the rich salt deposits at Dürrnberg (near Salzburg) began to be worked in parallel with the Hallstatt mines. Both ceased production about 400 bc." (128; range ~1050s).

- **Mediterranean trade (wine, luxury goods) and West Hallstatt chiefdoms (Hallstatt D, c.600–450 BCE):** Foundation of Massalia c.600 BCE by Phokaians: "the first Phokaian colony was founded at Massalia (Marseilles)... lay close to the mouth of the Rhône, which provided access into the barbarian hinterland." "That the colony of Massalia was now contributing to the aristocratic culture of the West Hallstatt zone is clear... some exotic products, such as the hydria found at Grächwil, ‘Rhodian’ flagons... and bead-edged bronze basins... may well have been transported via Massalia and the Rhône corridor to add a touch of the exotic to the Hallstatt feast enhanced by Massiliot wine." Heuneburg: "During Hallstatt D1 the defences of the plateau enclosure were completely remodelled in a style unique in Transalpine Europe: stone wall footings were laid with hollow, forward-projecting bastions... the wall was built of carefully squared mud-bricks... This alien structure, so clearly the concept of a Greek mind, and presumably overseen by a Greek architect." Lower settlement ~100 ha, ~5,000 people. Vix/Mont Lassois: "The rich female burial at Vix neatly exemplifies the range of imports with its huge krater for wine-mixing, jugs for pouring, basins, and Attic cups for drinking." "A geophysical survey has shown that the area within the fortifications was systematically laid out... One dominant enclosure contained a large timber building with an apsidal end. The scale of the structure and the associated Greek pottery suggests that it may have been a palace." Concentration of power in Hallstatt D2–3: "a concentration of power in fewer centres... the increasing exclusivity of the elite burial rite and by the abandonment of a number of hillforts."
