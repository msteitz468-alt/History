# Ancient Teotihuacan: Early Urbanism in Central Mexico

**Author:** George L. Cowgill (Emeritus Professor, Arizona State University; director ASU lab at Teotihuacan)
**Publisher:** Cambridge University Press, 2015
**Series:** Case Studies in Early Societies (ed. Rita P. Wright)

## Scope and Argument

The first comprehensive English-language synthesis of Teotihuacan — the largest city of the
ancient Americas before the 1400s, a UNESCO World Heritage Site located ~40 km from Mexico
City. Cowgill traces the city from its regional predecessor [[cuicuilco]] (~400 BCE) through
Teotihuacan's founding (~150/100 BCE), its rise to hemispheric dominance (~250–550 CE), and
its violent internal collapse (~550–650 CE). The book covers politics, economics, technology,
religion, architecture, craft production, and social life at all status levels.

Cowgill's central interpretive claims:
- Teotihuacan's early institutions may have been **collective/corporate**, but by the first
  century CE strong individual leaders likely **subverted** this into more autocratic rule
  (evidenced by massive pyramid construction and human sacrifice scale)
- Around the 300s CE, desecration of the Feathered Serpent Pyramid may mark a **ideological
  reversal** back toward more collective governance
- Collapse resulted from multiple interacting causes: growing intermediate-elite power
  siphoning surpluses, environmental degradation, rising external competition — NOT a single
  cause
- Teotihuacan fits neither "territorial state" nor "city-state" neatly; it was a distinctive
  urban form best understood on its own terms

## Relationship to Existing Wiki Content

Substantially expands [[teotihuacan]] (place) and [[teotihuacan]] (actor) pages previously
based on Cambridge World History Vol. III and IV chapters and Snow et al. 2020. Cowgill
provides far greater chronological granularity (ten ceramic phases), architectural detail,
and interpretive depth on political organization, religion, and collapse than the CWH
syntheses. Key tensions to watch: CWH IV emphasized the **trade-network/obsidian monopoly**
explanation for collapse; Cowgill emphasizes **internal political dynamics** (intermediate
elites, ideological reversal) as primary, with trade disruption as a secondary factor.
