# Alexander of Macedon, 356–323 B.C. (Peter Green)

Peter Green's *Alexander of Macedon, 356–323 B.C.: A Historical Biography* (Hellenistic Culture and Society 11). Originally a shorter 1970 version; expanded 1974 UK edition; substantially revised 1991 UC Press edition with documentation; 2013 reprint with important new preface restating Green's final interpretive stance. Foreword by Eugene N. Borza.

This is a full-scale **historical biography** (Plutarchan in intent) rather than pure military narrative. Green reads the ancient sources for political, familial, and personal motives, heavily informed by Ernst Badian's work on the Hobbesian realities of power. The book is notable for its willingness to treat Alexander's character as central and to draw unsentimental conclusions.

## The Approach (from Prefaces)

Green positions the work against both romantic idealists (W.W. Tarn's vision of Alexander as proponent of the "Brotherhood of Man") and pure reductionists. He accepts that Alexander was a military genius of the first order but argues his obsession with conquest, combined with success, produced megalomania, god-claims, and a widening gulf between king and Macedonian rank-and-file. By the end, Alexander's plans (Arabian expedition, further fusion, continued expansion) were abandoned immediately upon his death.

Key methodological points emphasized by Green:
- Use of "vulgate" sources (Diodorus, Curtius, Justin, Plutarch) is not automatically inferior; many lurid details have parallels in well-documented later tyrannies.
- Badian's influence on the analysis of conspiracy, purge, and realpolitik is pervasive.
- Alexander's goals diverged from those of the men who followed him.
- Contemporary political experience (including the Greek Colonels) informed Green's reading of autocracy and violence.

## The Granicus Propaganda Thesis (Appendix)

Green's appendix on the **Granicus** (334 BCE) is his most
sustained piece of source criticism and a model of the method, so it is recorded here in
full since no dedicated **battle-of-the-Granicus (334 BCE)** event page yet exists. The
puzzle: **Arrian/Plutarch** (drawing on
Ptolemy and Aristobulus) describe a *late-afternoon frontal assault* across a steep river
against a Persian line whose dispositions — cavalry forward on the bank, the first-class
Greek mercenary infantry held uselessly in the rear — most scholars (Tarn, Wilcken,
Fuller, Schachermeyr) concede are tactically "insane." **Diodorus** (with hints in Justin
and Polyaenus) instead reports a *dawn* engagement in which Alexander crossed unopposed
and fought a normal pitched battle on the plain — an account that is militarily sane but
which scholarship dismissed out of "Arrian-Kultus" (Beloch's phrase) and the conviction
that "Alexander was a Homeric hero," not a cautious Parmenio.

Green's resolution is the **two-battles hypothesis**: there were *two* engagements. An
**abortive afternoon assault** (the impetuous frontal charge Ptolemy describes, with its
circumstantial detail — the Macedonian panic, the calendar intercalation, the argument
with Parmenio) ended in a **humiliating repulse** by Memnon's mercenaries; Alexander then
swallowed his pride, marched downstream overnight, forded the river, and won the **decisive
dawn victory** (Diodorus' account). The official record fused the two — keeping the
afternoon drama but transferring the *victory's* setting back to the river-bank — to
preserve Alexander's indispensable charisma of being ἀνίκητος ("unconquerable," per
Delphi). Supporting indicators Green marshals: the absurd inflation of Persian infantry
numbers and losses against a *minuscule* Macedonian casualty list (which makes sense only
as the losses of the **thirteen squadrons** that charged with Alexander, down to the "nine
foot-soldiers" of the single infantry file); the unique statues at Dium to the 25
Companions killed "in the first assault"; and Alexander's **uniquely savage vengeance** on
Memnon's mercenaries (massacre + enslavement) despite normally recruiting such men — read
by Green, via Aristobulus' θυμῷ μᾶλλον ἤ λογισμῷ ("anger rather than reason"), as personal
revenge for the humiliation, dressed up as Hellenic-League justice. Green concedes the
thesis is not provable ("the enigma must be pronounced insoluble") but argues it "answers
more questions than it raises."

This is a clean instance of two standing wiki themes: **source-reliability** (the vulgate
vs. the Ptolemy/Aristobulus "official" tradition) and the **propaganda mechanics**
(*suppressio veri* / *suggestio falsi*) by which Alexander's court — Eumenes, Callisthenes
— shaped the record. It sits alongside the **Great-Man vs. structural causation**
debate as a caution against taking the heroic Alexander tradition at face value.

## Collection Context

Part of the strong **Hellenistic Culture and Society** series (Brill/UC). Complements already-ingested Fox (narrative mechanics) and Thomas (context). Should be cross-referenced heavily with CAH VI (Bosworth/Hornblower) and CWH Vol. IV.

Primary value: character, motive, and the political sociology of the Macedonian court and the post-conquest army. Battle tactics and topography are solid but secondary to Fox/Arrian reconstructions.
