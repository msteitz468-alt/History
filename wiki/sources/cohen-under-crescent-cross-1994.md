# Under Crescent and Cross: The Jews in the Middle Ages

Mark R. Cohen's *Under Crescent and Cross* (Princeton University Press, 1994;
revised edition 2008) is a systematic **comparative** history of Jewish–gentile
relations in the two dominant medieval civilizations — the Arab-Islamic world
and Latin Christendom. Its governing question is not *whether* Jews fared better
under Islam than under Christendom during the classical centuries (Cohen takes
that as established fact) but **how and why** — and why the Islamic–Jewish
relationship "bred so much less violence and persecution than relations between
Christians and Jews."

## Core Argument

Cohen frames the field as trapped between two distorting narratives, both of
which he rejects:

- The **"myth of the interfaith utopia"** — a nineteenth-century construction of
  the Central European fathers of scientific Jewish historiography (Graetz and
  others), who idealized the Andalusian "Golden Age" (940–1040) as a foil to the
  "[[lachrymose-conception-of-jewish-history|lachrymose conception]]" of Jewish
  life under Christendom. Later adopted by Arab anti-Zionist propaganda, which
  held that Muslim–Jewish harmony existed until shattered by Zionism.
- The **"countermyth" / "neo-lachrymose conception of Jewish-Arab history"** —
  the post-1967 revisionist claim (popularizers such as Bat Ye'or, and in oblique
  scholarly forms) that Islam persecuted Jews innately and continuously from
  Muhammad onward, the doleful equal of Christendom. Cohen argues this arose
  dialectically, in reaction to Arab antisemitism and the misuse of the utopia
  myth, and equally distorts the past.

Against both, Cohen builds a **structural comparison** across parallel domains —
religion and law, economy, the social order, and polemic/persecution — arguing
that a *combination* of factors (Islam's non-theological hostility to Jews as
one of several tolerated minorities; the legally embedded [[dhimmi|dhimma]]
system; the Jews' diversified, non-pariah economic role; the pluralistic social
hierarchy of "essential equality among inferiors") produced the relative
security of Jews under classical Islam — while their theological centrality,
economic isolation into the moneylending niche, and demonization in a religiously
homogeneous Christendom produced escalating persecution in the medieval West.
See [[jews-under-islam-myth-and-countermyth]] for the historiographical debate.

## Scope and Method

- **Islam** here means Sunni Islam (the orthodox, dominant form and source of
  policy toward infidels); Shiite regimes (Buyids, and the notably tolerant
  Fatimids, 969–1171) are treated as exceptions.
- **Christendom**: focus on the Latin West, mainly the northern lands, where the
  contrasts are sharpest; Mediterranean France (the Midi), Christian Spain before
  1391, and Poland are noted as instructive intermediate cases showing that
  political/economic/social realities could temper religious intolerance.
- **Classical centuries**: the argument holds most strongly up to the thirteenth
  century, after which conditions for Jews deteriorated in *both* worlds.

## Volume Synthesis Note

*Under Crescent and Cross* is the single most important source in the wiki for
the comparative dimension of medieval Jewish history and for the historiography
of Islamic–Jewish relations. Its central methodological contribution is to move
the debate off the sterile axis of "tolerance vs. persecution" — a category Cohen
argues is anachronistic before Locke — and onto a structural comparison of how two
hierarchical, monotheistic majority societies incorporated the same subordinate
minority. It complements the wiki's [[dhimmi]] concept page (which it substantially
deepens) and the [[managing-religious-pluralism-compared]] comparison, and it
supplies the historiographical frame — myth, countermyth, lachrymose and
neo-lachrymose — needed to read every other source touching Jews under Islam. Its
one built-in limitation, which Cohen concedes, is asymmetry of expertise: the
Islamic side rests on his own Geniza scholarship, the Christian side on synthesis
of others' work.
