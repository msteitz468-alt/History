# Conquerors and Chroniclers of Early Medieval Spain

The core Latin chronicles of **Visigothic Spain and the Arab conquest** — and, as Wolf stresses, the
**earliest Latin Christian writing about Islam**, encountered first not as a religion but as a
conquering political and military power. The second source of this Phase-5 batch (the first of the
Translated Texts for Historians series).

## The Texts

- **John of Biclaro, *Chronicle*** (c. 590) — a Catholic Goth educated at Constantinople, continuing
  the chronicle tradition of Eusebius–Jerome–Victor of Tunnuna down to 590. Our key narrative for
  **[[leovigild|Leovigild's]]** reunification of Spain and the pivotal reign of **Reccared**.
- **Isidore of Seville, *History of the Kings of the Goths*** (c. 625) — the great encyclopedist's
  patriotic history, framed by the famous *Laus Spaniae* ("hail, Spain, sacred and ever-blessed mother")
  and casting the Catholic Visigoths as the legitimate, providential heirs of Rome in Spain.
- **The *Chronicle of 754*** (the "Mozarabic Chronicle") and the related **Chronicle of 741** — written
  by Christians living **under Muslim rule** in al-Andalus, these supply the fullest near-contemporary
  Latin account of the **[[umayyad-conquest-of-hispania-711|conquest of 711]]** and the early emirate,
  reporting it relatively soberly as a transfer of power rather than apocalyptically.

## Significance

The collection documents the **two great transitions of early medieval Spain**: the consolidation of
the Catholic Visigothic kingdom (Leovigild and Reccared's conversion from Arianism at the
**[[third-council-of-toledo-589|Third Council of Toledo, 589]]**, the *Hispania* of Isidore's learning),
and its sudden destruction by the **Umayyad conquest of 711**. Wolf's central argument is that the
**first Latin Christian "views of Islam"** are found precisely here — in chronicles preoccupied with
Islam's political and military reality rather than its theology — and that the Christians *inside*
al-Andalus saw the conquest very differently from the Asturians who later mythologized it as a scourge
to be thrown off (the seed of the **[[reconquista|Reconquista]]** ideology).
