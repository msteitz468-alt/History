# The Egyptian Book of the Dead — Budge (Romer intro) edition

## Source Summary

This is the classic English translation (by E. A. Wallis Budge) of the ancient Egyptian funerary corpus known as the *Book of the Dead* or, more accurately in Egyptian terms, the *Book of Coming Forth by Day* (prt m hrw). It consists of spells, hymns, litanies, and vignette descriptions intended to empower the deceased to navigate the perils of the afterlife, achieve resurrection, and live eternally among the gods.

The texts originate in the Pyramid Texts of the Old Kingdom (c. 2400–2300 BCE) for royal use, evolve into Coffin Texts for elites in the Middle Kingdom, and reach their "classic" form in the New Kingdom (c. 1550–1070 BCE) as illustrated papyri rolls placed in tombs. The most famous exemplar here is the Papyrus of Ani (Theban, c. 1250 BCE), a high-quality scroll for a royal scribe and his wife.

John Romer's introduction (2008) frames it as a product of New Kingdom Theban religion: a "passport" and toolkit for the dead, reflecting beliefs in a moral judgment before Osiris, the solar cycle of Ra, protective magic (heka), and the ideal of eternal life in a perfected "Field of Reeds" or union with Osiris. Budge's original prefaces (1899/1909) provide Victorian-era overviews of Osiris as god of resurrection/judgment, the "object and contents" of the spells, and historical development.

The work is not "scripture" in a biblical sense but practical religious technology: spells recited or inscribed to make the dead "effective" (akh), avoid second death, transform into birds/lotus/etc., and pass tests. It reveals Egyptian ethics via the "negative confessions" (Chapter 125), where the deceased denies sins before 42 assessor gods.

## Key Themes and Historiography

- **Afterlife as perilous journey**: Duat filled with monsters, gates, tests; spells provide passwords, weapons, knowledge.

- **Judgment and morality**: Heart weighed against Ma'at's feather (Ch. 125); negative confessions ("I have not stolen... lied... killed...") reveal a developed ethical code tied to cosmic order (Ma'at).

- **Resurrection and union with gods**: Identification with Osiris (dead king reborn), Ra (solar rebirth), transformations into divine forms.

- **Magic and efficacy**: Heka (magic) as real force; words/images = power. Personalized for the owner (Ani in main papyrus).

- **Social/elite function**: Expensive papyri for officials/priests; democratized afterlife ideals from royal Pyramid Texts.

Budge's translation: Influential but superseded (modern: Faulkner, Allen, Quirke). Literal, with 19th-c. assumptions; Romer notes its value as accessible entry while critiquing Victorian Egyptology's "mummified" view of the culture. Strong on Theban New Kingdom context; collection note that this fills gap in funerary religion beyond Old Kingdom pyramids/mummification.

## Volume Synthesis Note

The *Egyptian Book of the Dead* (more precisely the *Book of Coming Forth by Day*, prt m hrw) in the Budge translation with Romer 2008 introduction is a modular anthology of roughly 190 spells, hymns, declarations, and vignette rubrics rather than a single narrative scripture. New Kingdom Egyptians—especially Theban elites, as exemplified by the Papyrus of Ani (c. 1250 BCE)—selected subsets of these texts to equip the deceased for the perils of the Duat, secure a favorable verdict in the Hall of Judgment, achieve resurrection, and attain eternal life among the gods.

The theological and ethical centerpiece is Chapter CXXV together with its vignettes: the deceased's heart is weighed against the feather of Maāt by Anubis while Thoth records the result; the deceased recites the "Negative Confession" or Declaration of Innocence before the Forty-Two Assessor Gods of the Hall of Double Maāti, repeatedly affirms "I am pure, I am pure, I am pure, I am pure," and—if the balance remains at rest—is declared "true of voice" (maā kheru), escaping the monster Ām-mit and joining Osiris in the Field of Reeds (Sekhet-Åaru). Parallel spells grant the power to transform into divine forms (hawk, lotus, Bennu bird, crocodile), to command air, water, food, and light in the underworld, to pass the gates and pylons of the Duat, and to identify with both the solar cycle of Ra and the resurrection of Osiris. The corpus itself evolved from the royal Pyramid Texts of the Old Kingdom through the Coffin Texts of the Middle Kingdom into the illustrated papyri of the New Kingdom Theban Recension.

Budge's literal rendering preserves the direct, performative quality of the incantations, while Romer's introduction reframes them as functional religious technology and elite status symbols that extended royal afterlife ideals more widely. For the wiki this supplies the single richest primary source on Late Bronze Age Egyptian religion, ethics (Maāt as both cosmic order and personal morality), and the Osirian judgment complex—material that complements the institutional and material-culture focus of the Cambridge Ancient History volumes and directly populates the dedicated actor pages for Osiris, Anubis, Thoth, and Maāt as well as the Thebes and late-bronze-age entries.

Principal limitations are philological (Budge's grammar and interpretation are Victorian and have been superseded by later editions) and recension-specific (strong Theban focus; earlier Pyramid/Coffin traditions and later variants receive lighter treatment). Historiographically the edition bridges 19th-century comparative religion with more recent emphasis on the spells' this-worldly magical efficacy and social role. In the collection it significantly strengthens coverage of pre-classical Egyptian thought while underscoring the relative thinness on other regions for this period.
