# Edbury, *The Kingdom of Cyprus and the Crusades, 1191–1374* (1991)

Peter W. Edbury's Cambridge monograph is the collection's **anchor secondary source on
Lusignan Cyprus** as a crusading kingdom. It fills the gap left by Sir George Hill's
*History of Cyprus* (vols II–III) with a reappraisal grounded in the post-1950s revival of
crusade scholarship (Riley-Smith, Hamilton, Richard, and others). The governing thesis is
that Cyprus, though conquered from **Byzantine Christians** rather than Muslims, was
**intimately bound to the Latin East by dynasty, war, and commerce**, and that the island's
strategic role in the confrontation with Islam **grew as the mainland states contracted** —
culminating in Peter I's independent crusading initiative (1365) and collapsing into the
Genoese catastrophe of 1373–74.

Edbury's emphasis is **political history within the crusading system**. He deliberately
omitted free-standing social and economic chapters (preface), though institutional material
on kingship, the High Court, and offices fills Ch. 8.

## Volume Synthesis Note

Edbury's monograph is a single sustained argument in three movements. **First**, Cyprus
entered the Latin world as a Third Crusade accident — revenge landing, supply base, cash
sale — not as a planned crusading colony, yet differed structurally from First Crusade
conquests because it was taken from **Christian Greeks**. **Second**, the island's fate was
inseparable from the mainland Latin East: Aimery's dual monarchy, Ibelin regency and the
War of the Lombards, Hugh III's failed dual kingship, and Cypriot manpower at Acre all show
that "it is impossible… to treat either kingdom in isolation." **Third**, after 1291 Cyprus
became the sole major outpost, peaked commercially under Henry II–Hugh IV, briefly set the
crusading agenda under Peter I (Alexandria 1365 — mainly Cypriot forces, commercial aims
under Holy Land rhetoric), and lost that capacity in the Genoese climacteric of 1373–74.
The closing judgment — almost 200 years of stability and prosperity making Cyprus one of
the most successful crusader-founded western regimes in Levant or Aegean — is the book's
load-bearing thesis and the wiki's anchor for Lusignan Cyprus against Hill's ageing survey
and Ghazarian's Cilicia-centred treatment of the same dynasty.
