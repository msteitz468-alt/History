# The Cambridge History of Greek and Roman Warfare (Sabin, van Wees, Whitby, 2007)

The primary multi-author reference for **Greek and Roman warfare** after the late-twentieth-century broadening of military history beyond Delbrück/Kromayer battle studies. Two volumes, four chronological parts, six recurring themes — designed so readers can pursue a single theme (e.g. battle experience, war and the state) across antiquity or compare systems within a period.

## Editors' design (from prefaces)

- **Not a narrative of wars** — thematic analysis of warfare as institution, practice, and social fact across ~15 centuries.
- **Four chronological parts** (Archaic–Classical Greece; Hellenistic world + mid-Republican Rome treated *in parallel*; Late Republic–Principate; Later Roman Empire).
- **Six themes per part:** (1) international relations / war and peace; (2) military forces; (3) conduct of wars and campaigns; (4) battle (land; naval and sieges); (5) warfare and the state (politics, finance); (6) war and society.
- Later Empire framed against older "Decline and Fall" prism: robust protracted defence amid Christianization and the Hunnic shock.
- Polybius epigraph: war is terrible, but not so terrible that one should put up with anything to avoid it — volumes examine both the terror and the forces that made resort to violence seem necessary.

## Volume Synthesis Note

CHGRW is the multi-author Cambridge consolidation of post-1970s ancient military history: thematic, comparative, and deliberately *not* a war-by-war narrative. Its unique wiki value is threefold.

1. **Historiographical frame.** Hanson–Hornblower–Whitby supply the field’s missing handbook-level map: from Delbrück/Kromayer *Sachkritik* through Keegan face-of-battle and social/institutional expansion, plus reconstruction rules that keep every battle narrative provisional. That frame now anchors [[historiography-of-ancient-warfare]] and tightens [[face-of-battle-method]] and [[sabin-lost-battles-model]].

2. **Six-theme comparative grid.** The same six questions (IR, forces, war, battle, state, society) across four chronological blocks force structural comparison — classical polis vs Hellenistic monarchy vs mid-Republican Rome vs Principate vs late Empire — rather than isolated national military histories. [[hellenistic-warfare]], [[greek-warfare]], [[roman-imperial-army]], and [[late-roman-army]] now share that grid; [[processes/war-and-society-greek-roman]] holds the social synthesis (van Wees’s limited-impact thesis; Lendon’s *technē* vs *virtus*; Adams/Fear on soldiers as society).

3. **Revisionist load-bearing claims.** Highest-yield for wiki orthodoxy checks: Krentz demolition of the agonal annual-battle model; Hunt on mixed force composition and slave labour; Gabrielsen/van Wees against hoplite-middle-class and navy-makes-democracy simplifications; Sekunda’s Iphicratean reading of Philip’s phalanx; Sabin’s dual-level battle model and Cannae “battlefield clock”; Rankov/Alston on Marian reforms as partial myth and Augustan standing army as the real break; Sidebottom/Goldsworthy/Whitby on grand strategy (debate useful, modern GS not recoverable from *consilium*); Rathbone fiscal accounting vs Adams provincial-income share (denominators distinguished on [[roman-military-finance]]); Elton/Rance/Lee against combat-quality explanations of western collapse (Africa 439 as fiscal hinge) — folded as **position 8** on [[causes-of-the-fall-of-the-western-roman-empire]].

**Source grain for the Warfare Hub:** institutional, social, and combat-experience chapters are richest; individual battles appear as *types* and case studies. Staff-ride pages (Cannae, Zama, etc.) keep operational spine from dedicated monographs and take CHGRW as comparative/historiographical reinforcement only.

**Relation to same-day Goldsworthy *Roman Warfare* ingest:** Goldsworthy supplies a single-author narrative spine; CHGRW supplies multi-author depth, Greek half, late-antique institutional detail, and higher presumptive weight as Cambridge reference. Where they agree (aggressive Roman way of war; anti-Luttwak frontiers; continuity of late army performance), wiki voice can state the consensus with dual attribution; where CHGRW is denser (finance, society, Hellenistic parallel), prefer CHGRW.
