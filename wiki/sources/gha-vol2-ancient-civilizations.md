# UNESCO General History of Africa, Vol. II: Ancient Civilizations of Africa

The second volume of the **UNESCO *General History of Africa*** (ed. **G. Mokhtar**, 1981) covers the
continent's **ancient civilizations** — Pharaonic Egypt, Nubia and Kush, Aksum, Carthaginian and Roman
North Africa, the Sahara, and the early Iron Age societies of sub-Saharan Africa — from roughly the third
millennium BCE to the seventh century CE. Written largely by **African scholars** as a conscious
**post-colonial reclamation** of African history, it is the authoritative reference for the
**[[sub-saharan-africa|Gap 2]]** ancient period, deepening the state-level detail that
[[reader-africa-biography|Reader]] treats lightly.

This is a **Large-Volume Protocol** ingest. The **Section Plan** below governs it; each section is processed
(read → pages written → committed to disk) before the next is read, with a single push on completion.

## The volume's argument

Editor Mokhtar frames ancient Africa around the **Nile valley as the great corridor** of the continent —
after the post-2400 BCE desiccation of the Sahara, the privileged route by which goods and ideas moved
between Mediterranean and sub-Saharan Africa. The volume's deeper purpose is **historiographical**: to
treat Egypt, Nubia/Kush and Aksum **as African civilizations**, against the colonial habit of detaching
them from the continent — most provocatively in **Cheikh Anta Diop's** opening chapter arguing the ancient
Egyptians were a **Black African** people (with a UNESCO symposium annex recording the scholarly dispute).

## Volume Synthesis Note

GHA Vol. II gave the wiki its first **authoritative, African-authored reference** for the continent's
ancient civilizations, deepening the [[sub-saharan-africa|Gap 2]] backbone that [[reader-africa-biography|
Reader]] had sketched. Across six section cycles it created **3 pages** and enriched **9**.

**The volume's unifying move is to repatriate ancient Africa to Africa.** Egypt is treated as an African
civilization in continuous exchange with Nubia and the south (most provocatively in **Diop's** Black-Egypt
chapter, now a [[peopling-of-ancient-egypt|controversy page]]); Nubia/Kush is followed past Meroë into the
thousand-year world of the **[[christian-nubia|Christian Nubian kingdoms]]** — a "missing millennium" the
wiki had entirely lacked; **[[aksum|Aksum]]** and Christian Nubia emerge as the **two enduring African
Christian civilizations**; the **[[berbers|Libyco-Berbers]]** are restored as agents (Masinissa's Numidia,
the Garamantes) rather than backdrop to Carthage and Rome; and the **early Iron Age** subcontinent — Nok,
the Bantu/Urewe spread, Azania, Austronesian Madagascar — is set out as indigenous achievement.

**Cross-volume relationships.** The volume sits well with the wiki's Cambridge backbone (CAH/CWH) on Egypt,
Kush and Carthage, which it supplements rather than contradicts; its distinctive additions are the African
framing and the post-Meroitic/Christian-Nubian and Berber material. One genuine **historiographical
tension** is recorded — the **race of the ancient Egyptians** (Diop vs. the modern indigenous-Northeast-
African consensus), promoted to its own controversy page and cross-linked to the **Hamitic-hypothesis**
discussion on [[bantu-expansion]]. As a 1981 reference it is now dated in archaeological detail (later
work has refined Bantu, iron-origin and dating questions), so its strongest specific claims are flagged as
positions, not settled fact. The political detail of the **medieval** West African and Sahelian states
(Ghana, Mali, Songhai, Kanem) belongs to GHA Vols III–IV, which follow.
