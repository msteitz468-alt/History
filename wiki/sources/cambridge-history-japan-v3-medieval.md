# The Cambridge History of Japan, Vol. 3: Medieval Japan (Yamamura ed., 1990)

The deep, interdisciplinary authority on **medieval Japan (1185–1603)** — the warrior age from
the Kamakura bakufu through the Muromachi/Ashikaga order, the Warring States, and the
Azuchi–Momoyama reunification. Its task here is to **deepen and refine** the existing
medieval-Japan pages ([[kamakura-period]], [[muromachi-period]], [[samurai]],
[[shogunate-bakufu]], [[minamoto-no-yoritomo]], [[mongol-invasions-of-japan-1274-1281]],
[[zen-buddhism]], [[japanese-buddhism]]) with current scholarship, and to add discrete-topic
pages.

## Volume Synthesis Note

CHJ Vol. 3 did for the wiki's **medieval Japan** what Vol. 1 did for the ancient period:
it put scholarly authority and analytical depth behind the spine that
[[mason-caiger-history-of-japan|Mason & Caiger]] and [[sansom-short-cultural-history-japan|Sansom]]
had sketched — creating four discrete-topic pages ([[medieval-shoen]], [[shugo-daimyo]],
[[medieval-japanese-commerce]], and the controversy [[zen-and-medieval-japanese-aesthetics]])
and deepening eight existing ones. Its through-line is a single long process viewed from many
angles: the **transfer of real power over the land from the capital to the warriors**.

Politically, that process runs from [[minamoto-no-yoritomo|Yoritomo's]] creation of the
**shugo and jitō** offices, through the [[kamakura-period|Kamakura bakufu's]] maturation into
a **sophisticated court of justice** under the Hōjō (Mass's revisionism: the *hyōjōshū* was a
vehicle of the regent, not a power-sharing council; the **Jōkyū War of 1221** the true
turning point), to the [[muromachi-period|Muromachi]] order — which John W. Hall
**rehabilitates** against the "weak shogunate" verdict (Yoshimitsu and Yoshinori held all
secular authority; strong [[shugo-daimyo|shugo]] *advanced* bakufu rule under shogunal
primacy). Economically, the same shift is the slow dissolution of the **[[medieval-shoen|shōen]]**:
jitō encroaching from below via *ukesho* and *shitaji chūbun* into **kokujin** overlords;
shugo absorbing estate income from above via **hanzei**; and **[[medieval-japanese-commerce|commerce
and monetization]]** (Chinese coin, *za* guilds, the Ming tribute trade, autonomous *sō*
villages and **ikki**) eroding the estate economy until the *sengoku* daimyo's territorial
domains replaced it — the institutional bridge to the early-modern *baku-han* order.

The volume's **diplomatic** thread (Kawazoe) ties domestic and foreign politics together: the
[[mongol-invasions-of-japan-1274-1281|Mongol invasions]] both consolidated and ultimately
**broke** the Kamakura bakufu (a defensive victory left no reward land, impoverishing the
gokenin and forcing the *tokusei* debt-cancellations), while the **Ming tribute system** gave
Yoshimitsu income, legitimacy, and security. The **cultural** chapters (Varley, Ruch, Ōsumi)
are the volume's most revisionist: Ōsumi explains *why* the **Kamakura Buddhist schools** were
revolutionary (popular salvation, rooted among commoners), while Varley and Ruch mount a
sustained challenge to the older **Zen-centric** reading of medieval aesthetics — Varley
grounding the culture in *mappō* pessimism and Heian nostalgia (with Zen merely *coinciding*),
Ruch recovering the nonelite "common culture" the elite frame ignored. This produced the
wiki's first medieval-Japan **controversy** ([[zen-and-medieval-japanese-aesthetics]]),
flagged against the [[sansom-short-cultural-history-japan|Sansom]] and Mason readings.

Where it touches other volumes it reinforces and refines: it gives the Mason political spine
its deep institutional and legal underpinning, supplies the *shōen* / *shugo* / commercial
concepts that Mason and Sansom assumed, and qualifies Sansom's confident Zen-saturation
thesis. It is the authoritative base for medieval Japan; the early-modern and modern
Cambridge volumes (4–6) remain to give the later periods the same depth.
