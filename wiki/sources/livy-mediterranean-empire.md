# Livy, *Rome's Mediterranean Empire* (*Ab Urbe Condita*, Books 41–45) and the *Periochae*

The **last surviving stretch of Livy's continuous narrative** (Books 41–45, covering 178–167 BCE),
together with the **Periochae** — the ancient summaries of almost all 142 books. The second Livy
selection in this Phase-4 batch, after **[[livy-hannibals-war|*Hannibal's War*]]**. For the author and
his method see **[[livy|Livy]]**.

## Books 41–45: The End of Antigonid Macedonia

The surviving books centre on the **[[third-macedonian-war-171-168bce|Third Macedonian War
(171–168)]]**, Rome's war against **[[perseus-of-macedon|Perseus]]**, the last Antigonid king. After
years of inconclusive campaigning, the consul **[[aemilius-paullus|Lucius Aemilius Paullus]]**
destroyed Perseus's phalanx at the **battle of Pydna (168 BCE)** — the decisive demonstration that the
Roman legion had mastered the Macedonian phalanx. Perseus was captured and paraded in Paullus's
spectacular triumph; the **Antigonid monarchy was abolished** and Macedonia broken into four tributary
republics. The settlement marks the moment Rome became the arbiter of the entire eastern Mediterranean
— the brutal sack of Epirus, the humbling of Rhodes, and the famous "day of Eleusis" (the legate
Popillius Laenas drawing a circle in the sand around Antiochus IV) all belong to this watershed.

## The Annalistic Form

Chaplin's introduction uses these books to explain Livy's **annalistic** method to modern readers: the
narrative is built on the recurring rhythm of the Roman public year — the election of consuls,
praetors and (every fifth year) censors; the assignment of *provinciae* by lot; levies, embassies, and
above all **religious business** — the Latin Festival, priestly colleges, and the **prodigy-lists**
(rocks falling from the sky, deformed births, a wolf running through the city) that the Senate
expiated. This *basso continuo* of civic and sacral routine, alien to modern expectation, frames the
military narrative and orients the reader in time.

## The *Periochae*

Because Books 1–10 and 21–45 are all that survive of the original 142, the **Periochae** — brief
book-by-book summaries compiled within a century or so of Livy's death — are indispensable: they are
the **only continuous thread through the lost three-quarters of the history**, from the aftermath of
Pydna down through the Gracchi, Marius and Sulla, the fall of the Republic, and Augustus (the
narrative ran at least to 9 BCE). Terse and occasionally garbled, they preserve the structure,
chronology and headline events of the lost books and are themselves a key witness for late-Republican
history.

## Significance

These books document the **decisive phase of Rome's conquest of the Hellenistic world** — the question
Polybius set out to explain (how Rome subjected nearly the whole inhabited world in under fifty-three
years) — from the Roman side, in the annalistic idiom. With the breaking-off of Livy's continuous text
at 167, the **quality of our knowledge of Roman history sharply declines**, making both these books and
the Periochae especially precious.
