# Jefferson the President: First Term, 1801-1805 — Dumas Malone (1970)

**Jefferson and His Time, Volume 4.** Comprehensive narrative biography covering Thomas Jefferson's first presidential term (March 1801–March 1805): the "revolution of 1800" and inauguration; formation of the cabinet and initial patronage/economy program; the bitter fight over repeal of the Judiciary Act of 1801 and the broader struggle with the Federalist judiciary; the Tripolitan War and early assertion of naval power; the Louisiana crisis, secret negotiations, purchase treaty, constitutional debates, ratification, and territorial organization; domestic politics including the emerging split with John Randolph; family and personal life at Monticello and the President's House; and the 1804 election and transition.

## Overview / Thesis

This volume treats the first term as the practical test of Jefferson's long-held republican principles in the office of President. The "revolution of 1800" is presented not merely as a change of party but as a deliberate effort to restore the "spirit of 1776" — economy in government, strict construction, protection of individual liberties, and a foreign policy of neutrality and peace while advancing vital American interests (especially navigation of the Mississippi and control of the West). Jefferson is shown as both theorist and administrator: deeply concerned with constitutional limits (especially the Louisiana Purchase) yet willing to stretch or improvise when national interest and opportunity aligned, while always documenting his scruples.

The narrative balances the President as central actor with the larger forces (European war, French and Spanish diplomacy, domestic Federalist resistance, Republican factionalism). Malone emphasizes that the presidency merged Jefferson's story with that of the country more than any previous phase. Key achievements (Louisiana, reduction of debt and taxes, Barbary success) are weighed against political costs (judiciary repeal backlash, the Randolph tertium quid split, the personal and partisan costs of the Walker affair revival). The volume portrays a thoughtful, moderate, and effective executive who nonetheless operated within real constraints of information, cabinet talent, congressional independence, and a hostile judiciary.

## Methodological Notes

- Strict chronological narrative to show the development of decisions in real time without hindsight.
- Heavy reliance on Jefferson's own letters and memoranda (the Anas), cabinet papers, congressional debates, contemporary newspapers (National Intelligencer, Federalist press), and diplomatic dispatches available to mid-century scholars.
- Extensive use of date-specific reconstruction: what Jefferson knew and when he knew it is repeatedly stressed.
- Balances official acts, political management, constitutional reasoning, and private life (Monticello visits, family, inventions, the resurfacing Walker scandal).
- Acknowledges the vastly larger documentary base for the presidential years and the difficulty of separating Jefferson's personal agency from structural forces and the actions of others (Livingston, Monroe, Talleyrand, Gallatin, Randolph).

## Key Contributions

- The most detailed scholarly reconstruction of the day-to-day conduct of the first term, especially the Louisiana Purchase negotiations, constitutional debate, and implementation.
- Full anatomy of the judiciary battle (repeal of the 1801 Act, the political context of Marbury, Stuart v. Laird) from the administration's perspective.
- Clear treatment of the Tripolitan War as an early exercise of presidential war powers and naval projection without formal declaration.
- Insightful analysis of Jefferson's relations with his cabinet (especially the talented but difficult Gallatin and the Smith brothers) and with John Randolph of Roanoke.
- Careful weighing of Jefferson's "revolution of 1800" rhetoric against actual policy continuities and pragmatic adjustments.

## Range 01 Claims

- **"Revolution of 1800" characterization**: “although effected by the suffrage of the people and not by the sword, it was 'as real a revolution in the principles of our government as that of 1776 was in its form.'” (in the section discussing the first inaugural address and its philosophy, referencing TJ's private letter to Spencer Roane, Sept. 6, 1819). Jefferson viewed the 1800 election outcome as restoring popular sovereignty and checking a counter-revolution on the political front. [[actors/jefferson-thomas]] [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-first-term-1970]]

- **Inaugural minimization of party differences**: “But every difference of opinion is not a difference of principle. We have called by different names brethren of the same principle. We are all republicans: we are all federalists.” (March 4, 1801, first inaugural address, "Profession and Promise: The First Inaugural"). Jefferson deliberately did not capitalize the key words; this conciliatory language pointed away from proscription and was more agreeable to Federalists of all shades than to many in his own party. preceded_by the contested 1800-1801 election tie and obstructionism. [[actors/jefferson-thomas]] [[actors/adams-john]] [[actors/burr-aaron]] [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-first-term-1970]]

- **"World's best hope" description of government**: “the world's best hope” (March 4, 1801, in one of the most memorable phrases of the inaugural). Jefferson referred to the government in this way while noting fears that a republican government was not strong enough; he believed it the strongest on earth because of citizens' personal concern for it. [[actors/jefferson-thomas]] [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-first-term-1970]]

- **Core definition of good government**: “a wise and frugal government, which shall restrain men from injuring one another, shall leave them otherwise free to regulate their own pursuits of industry and improvement, and shall not take from the mouth of labor the bread it has earned. This is the sum of good government, and this is necessary to close the circle of our felicities.” (March 4, 1801, first inaugural). This implied a minimal federal government functioning essentially as a disinterested policeman and unexacting landlord. [[actors/jefferson-thomas]] [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-first-term-1970]]

- **Gradualist approach to change**: “the chief thing was 'to reform the waste of public money, and thus drive away the vultures who prey upon it, and improve some little on old routines.'” (TJ to Dr. Walter Jones, Mar. 31, 1801; also referenced Solon's remark “that no more good must be attempted than the nation can bear”). At the dawn of the new era Jefferson was promising very little and appeared content to let nature take its course after removing the danger of tyranny. [[actors/jefferson-thomas]] [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-first-term-1970]]

- **Initial lodging and move after inauguration**: “FOR two weeks after his inauguration Jefferson remained at the boarding-house near the Capitol; not until March 19 did he remove to the big box of a President's House a mile away. He still ate at the common table.” (opening of "From Capitol Hill to the President's House"). The chief magistrate attended to business in the parlor adjoining his bedroom at Conrad and McMunn's. [[actors/jefferson-thomas]] [[places/washington-dc]] [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-first-term-1970]]

- **Private secretary appointment**: “He had already invited Captain Meriwether Lewis to be his private secretary, but did not learn of his acceptance until he himself moved to the President's House. At first, therefore, he even lacked a messenger.” (early March 1801, "From Capitol Hill to the President's House"). Lewis later served as aide and courier rather than clerk or amanuensis; Jefferson wrote his own numerous letters. [[actors/jefferson-thomas]] [[actors/lewis-meriwether]] [[places/washington-dc]] [[sources/malone-jefferson-the-president-first-term-1970]]

- **Core cabinet nominations submitted day after inauguration**: “His entire list consisted of Madison, Dearborn, Lincoln, and Livingston.” (March 5, 1801; Madison for secretary of state, Dearborn for war, Lincoln for attorney general, Livingston as minister to France). Jefferson could do nothing specific about organizing an administration until assured he would head it; the Senate promptly consented and adjourned. - **Early practice of "revolution of 1800" as limited change in spirit**: “He was saying essentially what he had said in letters in the last few years... He was listing the maxims of republicanism... The major change that he anticipated was in the spirit of the government.” (analysis of inaugural and immediate aftermath). Jefferson's negativeness on the side of government was matched by positiveness on the side of persons; he sought to make the federal government more nearly a government of the people while making it work through conciliatory manners and gradualism. ## Volume Synthesis Note

This volume of Malone’s *Jefferson and His Time* presents Jefferson’s first presidential term as the decisive practical test of the “revolution of 1800.” The election itself is framed as a genuine (if bloodless) restoration of the “spirit of 1776” after a decade in which Federalists had seemed to Jefferson to be steering the country toward monarchy and aristocracy. Once in office, Jefferson pursued a program of economy, reduction of the military and internal taxes, and limited government, while confronting the realities of power: a hostile Federalist judiciary, a declared war with Tripoli, and the sudden opportunity (and constitutional crisis) of the Louisiana Purchase.

The judiciary repeal (1802) and the Tripolitan operations demonstrate Jefferson’s willingness to use executive and legislative tools aggressively against entrenched opposition, yet at measurable political cost. The Louisiana Purchase is the volume’s centerpiece — an act of statecraft in which Jefferson stretched his own strict-construction principles in the face of an extraordinary opportunity, justified it on grounds of national necessity and popular will, and secured Senate ratification and implementation legislation with remarkable speed. The diplomatic and constitutional debates are reconstructed in detail, showing both Jefferson’s private qualms and his ultimate acquiescence once the treaty was a fait accompli.

Domestically, the volume charts the emergence of intra-Republican fissures (notably John Randolph of Roanoke’s break over the Yazoo claims) and the personal strains of the office, including the revival of the old Walker scandal for partisan purposes. The 1804 landslide is presented as vindication, yet the narrative closes with reflections on the limits of what even a principled executive could achieve and the enduring tension between republican theory and the necessities of governing a continental republic.

Malone’s account remains the most granular scholarly reconstruction of the day-to-day conduct of the first term, grounded in the documentary record available at mid-century. It underscores Jefferson’s combination of high principle, administrative attention to detail, and pragmatic flexibility when vital interests (Mississippi navigation, western expansion) were at stake. Later scholarship has added depth on the Haitian dimension of Napoleon’s decision and on the precise constitutional implications, but Malone’s narrative of what Jefferson knew, when he knew it, and how he decided stands as the foundational synthesis.

Cross-volume continuity with Vols. 1–3 is strong: the same emphasis on character through action, the same scrupulous attention to chronology, and the same authorial conviction that Jefferson’s central project was the preservation and extension of the revolutionary inheritance. The first term marks the moment when that inheritance moved from opposition and theory into the exercise of national power.
