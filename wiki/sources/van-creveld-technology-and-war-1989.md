# Technology and War: From 2000 B.C. to the Present (Martin van Creveld, 1989; rev. 1991)

This book examines the role of technology in the development and transformation of war from antiquity to the present. Van Creveld's central premise is that war is completely permeated by technology and governed by it—not only weapons and weapon systems, but technology in the broader sense of know-how, infrastructure, organization, and a way of looking at the world.

Unlike most works that focus narrowly on weapons, this study considers the full spectrum: roads, vehicles, communications, timekeepers, maps, energy sources, management techniques, and conceptual frameworks. It is structured around four technological "ages" rather than traditional weapon eras, showing how shifts in the nature of technology itself reshape warfare across all domains (land, sea, air, nuclear) and functions (tactics, strategy, logistics, command, organization).

The work provides a powerful analytical lens for the warfare hub, emphasizing continuity and transformation in how technology interacts with human, organizational, and societal factors.

## Framework
- Technology affects war like ripples from a stone: strongest at the point of impact (combat/weapons), but spreading to operations, strategy, logistics, intelligence, organization, command, and even the conceptual frameworks used to think about war.
- Four Ages:
  - **Age of Tools** (earliest times to ~1500): Muscle-powered (human/animal). Field, siege, naval, infrastructure.
  - **Age of Machines** (1500–1830): Non-organic energy (wind, water, gunpowder). Rise of professionalism.
  - **Age of Systems** (1830–1945): Integrated machines (rail, telegraph, industrial mobilization). "Invention of invention."
  - **Age of Automation** (1945–present): Cybernetics, feedback, computers, nuclear, integrated and "make-believe" war.
- Key themes: the "logic" of technology vs. the "logic" of war; how technology both enables and constrains; the interplay with organization, society, and human factors.

## Historiography Note
Van Creveld's work is highly regarded for its broad sweep and for moving beyond narrow focus on weapons to technology as a system of knowledge and organization. It is essential for the warfare hub because it provides a consistent framework for analyzing continuity and change across 4,000 years. Some critics note that its thematic structure can blur chronological nuances or that it sometimes underplays non-technological factors (culture, politics, economics). Nevertheless, it remains a foundational text for operational and strategic analysis of technology's role. Complements more narrative or battle-focused sources.

## Range 1 Extraction (Part II: The Age of Machines, 1500–1830)

**Source file slice:** range1.txt (Field Warfare, Siege Warfare, The Infrastructure of War; file cut mid-sentence at end of Infrastructure).

**Machine transition:** "After 1500, however, the most important weapons employed derived their energy not from biological sources, but from sources that were inanimate, and specifically chemical. It is in this sense that we may speak of them as machines. Indeed a gun of any size may well be understood simply as an internal-combustion engine acting in one direction instead of two." (Field Warfare). Revolutionary in power and "the ability to kill an opponent was no longer directly related to an individual’s physical prowess, but tended to become a question of trained, professional skill."

- Firearms evolution: arquebus (~1500) → musket + bayonet (~1660, homogeneous infantry). Cannon: cast iron/bronze, corned powder, horse artillery (Gustavus, Gribeauval/Frederick). Ammo: iron, grape/canister, chain shot.
- Combined arms and linear tactics: pikes + shot "sleeves"; ranks reduced 8-10 → 3-2 for volley fire. "The best armies of the period were those which were most successful in turning their men into soulless robots goose-stepping forward at exactly 90 paces the minute." (Martinet / Prussian drill). Edged weapons largely replaced by bayonet (1 bayonet wound per ~100 small-arms per Larrey).
- "Where arms and equipment on both sides were approximately the same... the factor which decided the issue was not technology, but the ability to combine hardware, training, doctrine, and organization into a single decisive whole." (Field Warfare).
- Siege: cannon breached medieval walls (tall targets); response *trace italienne* / star forts (low profile, bastions for raking fire, Vauban systematized parallels/zigzags, 40-60 day sieges typical). "the principal effect ... was to make both the attack and the defense of fortresses very much more complicated and expensive." Led to state centralization (monarchs only could afford cannon trains); "minimum size necessary to make political units militarily viable." No decisive attacker win: "the two progressed very much together." Fortresses key to strategy 1560-1700 (belts, front/rear).
- Infrastructure (non-military tech dominant): "THE IMPACT OF GUNPOWDER on warfare made itself felt in the field of tactics above all. Its effect on organization, logistics, intelligence, command and control, and on strategy itself, was much less, and for the most part indirect. To understand the technological reality underlying the evolution of warfare in these fields, it is necessary to turn mainly to nonmilitary technology."
  - Printing, paper, double-entry bookkeeping, Arabic numerals/logarithms/decimals (Stevin) "critical to the rise of military bureaucracies and of modern armed forces."
  - Army scale: 100k+ (late 16th) → Gustavus 200k → France 400k → Napoleon 1M+ (levée en masse). Standing forces / long-service regulars.
  - Command: rulers in palaces; royal mail 60-90 km/day (2 weeks for reply from Germany to Versailles). Battlefield C3 still oral/visual (no major advance); commanders on hills with telescopes.
  - Maps: printing + triangulation (Snellius, Cassini) improved but no contours, inconsistent miles, spotty; often local guides/captured maps (Frederick 1740, Napoleon marshals "marched into the unknown").
  - "the revolution in strategy usually associated with the name of Napoleon Bonaparte" ultimately from nonmilitary tech developments.
- Professionalism: paid regulars, state monopoly on expensive cannon, trained skill (firearms decoupled from personal prowess), bureaucracy.
- "War is too complex a tapestry to be dominated by a single thread, however thick and however brilliant."

## Range 2 Extraction (Part II late + Part III early: Age of Systems onset, Mobilization, Land Warfare start)

**Slice:** range2.txt (Mobilization Warfare core; Land Warfare to mid; some pre-text and transitional professionalism/sea notes).

**Systems definition:** "UNTIL ABOUT 1830, warfare was very largely an affair of using individual tools and machines. ... they were not integrated into complexes, or systems. ... After 1830, for the first time, not only men but technological devices as well were integrated into systems." (Mobilization Warfare).

- Chappé optical telegraph (1794): revolutionary as *system* (hardware + precise software/coordination of dozens of towers; not the semaphore arm but the integrated operation). Military lines (Napoleon 1809, British Admiralty, Prussian).
- Railway + electric telegraph (Morse): interdependent systems. Timetables, fixed speeds, clock synchronization (end of local times). "the two technologies not only developed simultaneously but often did so at the hands of the same people." Rail for deployment but "remarkably inefficient when it comes to transporting large forces over distances smaller than 100 km"; more for national strategy.
- Prussian mastery (Moltke, Wartensleben on railway committee): 1866/1870-71 mobilizations "proceeded with a clockwork precision." "so superior were the Prussians in utilizing telegraphs and rails that the outcome of the conflict was decided almost before the first shot was fired." Post-1870: every general staff added railway departments; "the officers in charge were military technicians pure and simple. For the first time, they not only commanded specialized units and offered advice, but actually took over the conduct of war."
- Tightly coupled systems → 1914 chain reaction: "if country A took the first step towards mobilization, countries B and C and D were compelled to follow suit. ... In 1914, with the would-be commanders standing helplessly by, it played a major role in dragging the world into the largest war in history to that point."
- Total mobilization enabled: "By 1914, telegraphs and railways for the first time had made the military populations of entire countries and continents both readily accessible and easily deployable. Total mobilization for war was turned into a practicable proposition." Armies in millions; "countries such as France and Germany became capable of putting up to 10 percent of their populations into uniform."
- Total war / grand strategy: "strategy now acquired the added dimension of an exercise in correctly distributing one’s total resources." "the systems approach by permitting unprecedently massive forces to be created and deployed ultimately dominated the Second World War." Ludendorff: "instead of war being the continuation of politics, in a very real sense politics became a mere appendage of war." Nuclear (1945) "practically abolished" total war.
- Land Warfare impact (from 1830 watershed): rail/telegraph immobile/inflexible → external lines, dispersed fronts (hundreds km), telegraph for control. Firepower revolution (percussion, rifling, breech, magazine, machine gun, smokeless, recoil artillery, TNT shells). "the volume of fire... the range... and the accuracy... square meter by square meter, the battlefield became a more deadly place than ever before. ... warfare entered a new medium and increasingly took place in a storm of steel."
- Responses: entrenchment, dispersion (phalanx 1:1 → WWI 1:250+), camouflage (drab "horizon blue, earth brown, field gray"; helmets from miners). Cavalry obsolescent on high-firepower fronts. Logistics explosion (ammo 2/3 of div supplies); field rail/horses insufficient; breakthroughs halted by supply. Mines, barbed wire, gas.
- "although every major war between 1861 and 1905 was dotted with battles in which firepower brought tactical mobility to a halt, all of them were ultimately decided by offensives moving on external lines." Stagnation 1914-17; counters: German infiltration/flamethrower/SMG/light artillery; Allied tank.

## Range 3 Extraction (Part III mid-late: Systems maturation, Air/Sea, Invention of Invention)

**Slice:** range3.txt (Land Warfare cont.; full Command of the Air; full Sea Warfare; The Invention of Invention).

**Armored as system:** "Add antitank artillery to enable one’s own infantry to resist enemy tanks, add engineering, maintenance, logistic services, and a signals detachment to permit control of the lot, and the armored division was born." Logistics: Panzer ~300 tons/day, US armored 1000-1500+; "vicious cycle" of protection vs. air vulnerability; ops "as cumbersome as any infantry battle."

**C3 radio core of Blitzkrieg:** Germans "insisted that every Panzer should come equipped with two-way radio. Their armored divisions thus acquired very great tactical and operational flexibility such as has rarely been equaled before or since. This flexibility, possibly even more than the tanks themselves, constituted the true core of the new style in warfare." Allowed "take the line of least resistance much as water flows down a slope."

**Air systems vs flexibility:** Balloons/aircraft "fully integrated into the military organization down to corps level." Strategic bombing "itself assumed all the characteristics of such a system. The price to be paid for “mechanical” efficiency was, as usual, a loss of flexibility." Tactical fighter-bombers "kept their operational flexibility much longer" and "devastating." Airborne surprise-capable but vulnerable. Air "ended the immunity of those economies and those societies." Radar "had to be coordinated and integrated with other devices" → networks.

**Sea: battleship system → carrier/sub flexibility:** Fleet "integrated into a system which would take no action except at the orders of the flag admiral. The result... extreme prudence, not to say timidity." Aircraft/subs "neither formed the core of a large, integrated system. This left them much greater flexibility and, when properly employed, that flexibility was decisive." (Midway, wolf packs). Postwar cost explosion threatens carriers like old battleships.

**Invention of Invention:** "A transition took place from a situation in which inventions were for the most part not only exceptional but accidental and unexpected, to one in which technological change—and the anticipation of technological change—became the normal state of affairs." "technological progress also became deliberate and therefore, up to a point, predictable." "vast human and economic resources were expended."
- Military poor for invention (hierarchy, secrecy, rigidity): "all the most important nineteenth century military devices originated in the minds of civilians" (Dreyse, Krupp/Armstrong, Ericsson, Maxim, Holland; 20th c. airplane, tank, jet, radar, helicopter, atom bomb, "the electronic computer").
- Military excellent for development: resources "regardless of... financial cost" (Manhattan). "armed forces in many ways represent an ideal environment for undertaking technological development and forcing it through, they hardly offer a climate that is favorable for invention, and should therefore pay others to do their inventing for them."
- Problems: lead times up to 15 years; heterogeneous inventories ("logistic nightmare"); "growth of “tails” (service) over “teeth”"; "turbulence in doctrine, training, organization"; "loss of institutional memory and of cohesion."
- Computers/C3I precursors: mechanical computers (naval fire control); "considerations involved could be quantified and entrusted to automatic devices, from which the first computers were later to evolve." Radio/radar integration.
- Nuclear: "in 1945 the dropping of two nuclear bombs... offered a dramatic demonstration of the advantages of technological superiority..." "With the exception of nuclear devices, the effect of which is to suspend war rather than contribute to its conduct..." Seesaw; complex environments favor superior tactician. "technology does not just represent an assemblage of hardware but a philosophical system. As such, technology affects not only the way war is conducted and victory is sought, but the very framework that we use for thinking about it..."

## Range 4 Extraction (Part IV: Integrated War, Make-Believe War)

**Slice:** range4.txt (Integrated War, Make-Believe War).

**Integrated warfare (electronics):** Dispersion problem solved by wireless "in what was later to become known as ‘real time.’" "electronics realized the ancient dream of building guided weapons." Generations every ~10 years. "on the modern battlefield, a blizzard of electromagnetic blips is increasingly being superimposed on—and to some extent substituted for—the storm of steel." ELINT, jamming, frequency hopping, anti-radiation missiles. Platforms linked by "intricate networks"; AWACS; computers to "fuse" info; officers "at one remove from reality… by sitting in front of multiple television screens, talking newspeak into microphones, and pressing buttons."

**Platform continuity + electronics overlay:** "the majority of the principal systems in use today are linear descendants of those fielded during World War II." Tank still "the most important single weapon." Jets: maneuver > top speed; "flying complexes made up of missiles and computers." Helicopters: airmobile (Vietnam); attack role (missiles, pop-up). Carriers task forces + nuclear subs (SLBM security). Guided missiles on escorts. Gulf 1991: C2 suppressed electronically; 90% smart bombs hit (but vs. exposed Western-equipped force in flat terrain).

**Paradoxes/limits of integration:**
- "there exists a point beyond which integration... will lead to diminishing returns. ... Since war is characterized precisely by a very high level of uncertainty… that point is reached much sooner."
- Loss of autonomy: new weapons most effective *before* full integration ("preserves a greater degree of autonomy and flexibility"). Tank example: early Panzer balance lost to over-integration + antitank pressure. "Simplicity and flexibility, both of them vital principles of war, were lost."
- "tactical aircraft became increasingly dependent on cooperation with electronic navigation and communication systems, electronic warfare aircraft, and unmanned platforms known as RPVs." Pilots "imprisoned in... inconceivably complicated machines."
- "much, if not most, current modern military activity takes place below the sophistication threshold, and conversely that many kinds of military technology have become too sophisticated, too powerful, and too indiscriminate for use in real-life warfare. Partly as a result, such technology seems to be on its way to turning war itself into an empty, hollow game."
- EMP or low-tech ballistic weapons can neutralize high-tech side.

**Make-Believe War:** In homogeneous tech/culture, war develops rules (Hellenistic chess-like battles; medieval chivalry; 18th c. game of kings). Post-1945: "a common technology tends to overshadow these differences." "modern military technology tends towards homogeneity." "the very fact that conventional war takes place already points to the existence of something like a technological balance between the belligerents." → gamelike, predictable patterns. Cultural revulsion/pacifism (esp. West) reinforces. High-tech risks making "real-life warfare" rare; activity below threshold common.

## Range 5 Extraction (Part IV late + Conclusions)

**Slice:** range5.txt (Integrated/Make-Believe/Real War + full Conclusions: The Logic of Technology and of War).

**Real War:** "THOUGH ADVANCING military technology has caused the conduct of war to change and change again, it does not seem to have affected the causes of armed conflict." Nuclear "put the lid on global strategic warfare, and on much conventional warfare as well. Underneath, however, the pot continues to boil." Subconventional (guerrilla/terrorism) use dispersion, concealment, mobility, outside aid, political warfare + everyday tech (phones, cars, media); advanced forces may need to shed "favorite gadgets." "War will remain what it has always been, namely a horrible, messy affair in which some people are killed and others mutilated."

**Premise return:** "LIKE A WALK in a garden where every path returns to the entry gate... war is permeated by technology to the point that every single element is either governed by or at least linked to it." Non-military infrastructure ("roads, vehicles, communications, and timekeepers") "govern, and may indeed constitute, what we have called the infrastructure of war" and "dictate the character of organization, logistics, intelligence, strategy, even the concept of battle itself." Weapons "limited mainly to tactics"; nuclear = "too powerful to use" + "exercise in make-believe, a game." "Technology is perhaps best understood as an abstract system of knowledge, an attitude towards life and a method for solving its problems." The "war is primarily a question of technology" idea "neither self-evident, nor necessarily correct, nor even very old" and "dangerous" at extremes. Includes "nonutilitarian and nonfunctional" psychological aspects "paramount" in war. "the interaction of technology and war at any given time has been as much the product of the arbitrary and the accidental as it was of the inevitable and the necessary."

**Immutable elements:** War "an act of violence where each side is out to destroy the other... province of hardship and of suffering, of stress and fear and pain and death... primarily an affair of the heart. It is dominated by such irrational factors as resolution and courage, honor and duty and loyalty and sacrifice of self... none of these have anything to do with technology." Functions "rooted in the very nature of war and thus immune to technology" (supplying, communicating, intelligence/securing, fixing, maneuvering, protecting, striking). "the logic of conflict... is likewise immutable and immune to any amount of technology."

**Linear vs. Paradoxical (core):** "Technology, then, to the extent that it is rooted in physical nature, might be described as linear. ... two plus two are four and results are directly proportional... the world of again and again and again... specialization in turn led to integration... efficiency." "War, far from being an exercise in technology, is primarily a contest between two belligerents... The underlying logic of war is, therefore, not linear but paradoxical. The same action will not always lead to the same result. The opposite, indeed, is closer to the truth... uncertainty inherent in war... forms a necessary part of war and can be eliminated only at the cost of turning conflict into a charade... no success is possible—or even conceivable—which is not grounded in an ability to tolerate uncertainty, cope with it, and make use of it." Logistics example: pure tech-efficient (centralized, no redundancy, specialized) "would be useless or worse" (paralysis on loss of brain; cascade on single failure; no substitution). "To explain the way technology relates to the logic of war, and hence should be used in it, is the last task of the present volume; and, quite possibly, it is the most important one." (Calls Luttwak "brilliant.")

**Future:** Advancing tech "will continue to help alter the shape of war, there unfortunately appears to be little prospect that it will bring war to an end. Although the power of modern weapons may cause some forms of war to become extinct, others will take their place. ... sooner or later war will break through the rules."

## Consolidated Volume Synthesis

Van Creveld's *Technology and War* provides the wiki's foundational framework for technology's role across the four ages, emphasizing that technology (broadly: know-how, infrastructure, organization, energy, conceptual tools—not just weapons) permeates and governs war in every dimension, yet interacts with immutable "logic of war" (paradoxical, heart-driven, uncertainty-tolerant) that linear technological efficiency cannot supplant.

**Key through-lines (synthesized, cross-range):**
- Infrastructure and non-military tech often more decisive for strategy, organization, logistics, and command than weapon hardware (writing → permanent armies; printing/paper/bookkeeping → bureaucracies/professionalism; rail/telegraph → total mobilization/systems; electronics → real-time integration).
- Ages mark shifts in energy/organization: Tools (muscle, limited C3/strategy); Machines (chemical + professionalism + trace italienne/state scale); Systems (integrated complexes + deliberate R&D + total war/grand strategy); Automation (C3I/guided + integration-flexibility paradox + make-believe vs. real war).
- Recurring tradeoff: integration enables power/scale but erodes flexibility, autonomy, simplicity (key principles of war); new weapons often strongest pre-integration. Systems tightly coupled → fragility (1914 mobilization, modern networks).
- Military vs. society: civilians drive most invention; military excels at development/deployment at any cost. Continuous innovation brings lead times, logistic nightmares, doctrine turbulence, tails growth.
- Nuclear and high-tech limits: suspend rather than enable decisive conventional use; sophisticated systems vulnerable to low-tech or EMP below "sophistication threshold"; much activity stays low-tech.
- Philosophical: technology shapes not only conduct but the very frameworks for thinking about war. The belief in technological superiority as primary path to victory is recent (post-Industrial), not self-evident, and dangerous in extremes. War's core (violence, suffering, courage, uncertainty) and functions remain immune.
- Complements other van Creveld (Supplying War: logistics constraints; Command in War: C3 evolution) and Jones/Heuser/Luttwak/Military Effectiveness: technology changes shape and enables scale, but does not abolish friction, paradox, or the need for judgment, organization, and will.
