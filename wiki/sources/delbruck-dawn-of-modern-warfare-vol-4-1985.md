# Hans Delbrück, *History of the Art of War, Volume IV: The Dawn of Modern Warfare* (trans. 1985)

The final volume of Delbrück's four-volume critical history. It analyzes the re-emergence of disciplined, permanently embodied infantry and cavalry from the late medieval Swiss and the Renaissance experiments, through the Dutch and Swedish tactical revolutions, the fiscal-administrative creation of standing armies under absolutism, the drill-and-maneuver systems of the eighteenth century, and the transformation into national armies and Napoleonic operational art (with the Prussian reformers' response). The constant theme is the interdependence of political constitution, military organization, tactics, and strategy.

This volume supplies the primary Tier 3 backbone for the early-modern and Napoleonic wings of the [[hubs/warfare/warfare-hub|warfare hub]] and the [[hubs/warfare/strategy|strategy]] sub-hub.

## Historiography / impact note
Delbrück's Vol. IV is the capstone of the modern critical-operational school. His treatment of Frederick the Great as a limited-war practitioner using exhaustion more than annihilation remains controversial and directly challenged the Prussian General Staff narrative that fed into 1914 planning. His insistence that political constitution and fiscal base determine what tactics and strategy are possible is the thread connecting all four volumes and directly informs the wiki's link taxonomy (causation vs. sequence, actor vs. structure). For the warfare hub it supplies the institutional and theoretical bridge between late-medieval/early-modern infantry revival and the Napoleonic synthesis.

## Primary Claims from the Text (Grounded Extractions)

All claims below are verbatim-grounded in the 1862 Renfroe translation text (Bison/Nebraska ed.), extracted via 5 disjoint ranges and reconciled on the main thread. Locators use the source’s internal chapter/section and visible page references. Delbrück’s recurring political-framework thesis, *Sachkritik* method, and the attrition/annihilation polarity are highlighted.

### Preface & Method (Ranges 0 / front)
- **Core thesis**: “Recognition of the mutual interaction between tactics, strategy, national organization, and politics throws light on the relationship of these subjects to universal history... This work has been written not for the sake of the art of war but in the interest of world history... ‘The History of the Art of War Within the Framework of Political History,’ as the title indicates.”
- **Strategy terminology (translator note, carried throughout)**: “Ermattungs-Strategie as ‘strategy of attrition,’ and Niederwerfungs-Strategie as ‘strategy of annihilation’.”
- **Scope and stance**: Work ends with Napoleon; technical details de-emphasized in favor of the basic theme; acknowledges prior struggles with Ranke, Mommsen, General Staff, and misunderstandings. Credits Hobohm’s *Machiavellis Renaissance der Kriegskunst* as “indispensable prelude and the most valuable supplement.”

### Book I — Renaissance Infantry Rebirth (Range 0)
- **Swiss squares as origin point**: “The surprising power of the Swiss military system was based on the mass effect of the large, closed squares... With the battle of Nancy, this Swiss warfare by masses had come down out of the mountains...”
- **Guinegate 1479 turning point**: Flemish pikemen “in the Swiss manner” responsible for victory; “Militarily, however, it was a critical turning point... the precursors of the lansquenets.”
- **Lansquenets as first trained tactical body**: “For the first time in world military history we encountered a tactical body...” (analogy to Spartan phalanx); Maximilian personally drilled; systematic training (Bruges 1488 “snail,” drums, wheeling).
- **Firearms loading as true invention**: “The real invention that led from gunpowder to shooting was the invention of the loading process. The ball had to be pressed so firmly into the tube... The concentration of power due to the cramming of the tube also caused the sharp crack.”
- **Pressure from the rear in pike squares**: “the second and third ranks decided the victory, since they were pushed forward by the following ranks... ‘pressure from the rear was not for the best’.”
- **Machiavelli’s militia failure (Prato 1512)**: Lacked rooted captain authority and Roman-style centurion discipline; “the drilling of the centurion with his grapevine staff gave the Roman troops the steadfastness... a steadfastness that Machiavelli’s militiamen were sadly missing in the breach of Prato.”
- **Delbrück on Machiavelli**: “In Machiavelli’s writings we find the principles... side by side but unbalanced.” He saw the problem “prophetically but did not yet create a well-rounded theory.”

### Book II — Wars of Religion (Range 1)
- **Knighthood vs. cavalry distinction**: “knighthood was based on qualified individual warriors, whereas cavalry consists of tactical bodies composed of horsemen.”
- **Reîtres as fathers of European cavalry**: “They are to be regarded as the fathers of European cavalry in the same sense that the German Swiss are considered the fathers of European infantry... It was here on French soil that they developed the new method of mounted combat.”
- **Caracole significance primarily drill/discipline**: “the significance of the often mentioned caracole is to be sought less in its direct, practical use than in the drill itself, that is, in the development of discipline...”
- **Cavalry not progressive from knighthood**: “Cavalry was no progressive development of the knightly method but a new system that replaced the knightly one.”
- **Maurice of Orange**: Drill and discipline as decisive; small units + many officers + punctual pay created the modern officer; “with a regiment of his soldiers no stronger than 1,000 men he can stand up to a regiment of his enemy of 3,000.”
- **Sweden first national army**: “The Swedes were the first people to form a national army... The Swedish army, however, was a trained military organization serving the defense, the greatness, and the fame of the fatherland.”
- **Breitenfeld 1631**: Swedish “close cooperation with the musketeers”; light artillery delivered “hail of shots against the thick masses”; “only three of his seven infantry brigades had really fought.”
- **Lützen 1632**: “governed very strongly by chance” (fog, timing, death of the king); Wallenstein shifted to ten ranks + light pieces.
- **Cromwell/Ironsides**: Discipline “had its footing in the passion and truth of belief”; red tunic established as standard uniform.
- **White Mountain / Wittstock source criticism**: Thin echelons and large intervals; questionable strength rosters; leadership failures (Anhalt, etc.).

### Book III — Standing Armies (Ranges 2–3)
- **Standing army by permanence, not new class**: “The mercenary bands were not replaced by a warriorhood of different origin; instead, they changed their character by remaining permanently under the colors and becoming standing armies.”
- **Military organization as basic national factor**: “The military organization is always the most basic factor in the existence of a nation. The entire socio-political situation of Europe was transformed with the new military organization. The standing army was the point of contention in the struggle between the princes and their Estates of the Realm...”
- **Le Tellier / Louvois (France)**: Le Tellier created intendants, regular pay in presence of officials, retained regimental cadres on reduction; Louvois perfected the system. “the idea of a standing army was really brought to fulfillment.”
- **Prussia (Great Elector to Frederick William I)**: *miles perpetuus* post-1660; canton regulation 1733 with height standard and exemptions; levy as “precursor of the general military obligation” but conceived as profession, not national duty.
- **Prussia vs. France contrasts**: Daily drill vs. limited; unified officer corps vs. cleavage; beating discipline vs. French; higher population burden in Prussia (~3–4% vs. ~1%).
- **Tactics evolution**: Pikes phased out; bayonet + flintlock (socket + cross-arm) enabled uniform firearm; Prussian 3-rank close formation, rolling platoon salvo (no aiming); real rates ~2.5–3 rounds/min (Seven Years’ War), ~4 later (Delbrück’s *sachkritik* on inflated contemporary claims).
- **Oblique / echelon**: Frederick refined prior ideas (Montecuccoli, Folard, Puységur); at Leuthen narrow-front echelons turned the Austrian left; at Torgau divided the attack (Napoleon later criticized the loss of cohesion; Delbrück reverses the judgment).
- **Attrition/annihilation polarity (core)**: “We have already seen in the chapter on Machiavelli that the nature of strategy leads to a central problem, the problem of the double form of strategy, the strategy of annihilation and the strategy of attrition... In the strategy of attrition, however, one’s own damages must be taken very carefully into account... This is the same polarity which resides in the fact that the instrument through which the political goal is to be reached, the army, itself is always the stake...”
- **Frederick as attrition practitioner**: “our wars must be short and active... it is better that one person die than the whole people be ruined.” Battles as high-risk last resorts; “most generals who let themselves be easily drawn into a battle resort to this solution only because they do not know what else to do... the sign of a lack of genius.”
- **Frederick vs. General Staff historiography**: Official works read 1741–44 plans as annihilation spirit; Frederick “still fell far short. Never was the enemy army designated as the specific objective... he was not at all anxious to have that country broken up.” (Delbrück *sachkritik*).

### Book IV — National Armies & Prussian Reform (Range 4)
- **Revolutionary transformation in three points**: “the new military system that was created in and by the revolution differed from that of the ancien régime in three ways: the army was much larger, it fought as skirmishers, and it requisitioned.”
- **Levée en masse**: Theoretically universal (unmarried 18–25); estimates ~770k total by early 1794 (Aumale); ~500k facing external enemies.
- **New tactics not conscious invention**: Columns + marksmen/skirmishers emerged pragmatically because linear salvo discipline was impossible with the new material; continued under 1791 regulations into Napoleonic era.
- **Napoleonic annihilation principle**: “The natural principle of strategy is... assembling one’s forces, seeking out the enemy’s main force, defeating it, and following up the victory until the loser subjects himself to the will of the victor... ‘The destruction of the enemy armed forces is, among all the purposes that can be pursued in war, always the one that dominates all others’ (Clausewitz).”
- **Napoleon vs. Frederick plans**: Napoleon’s possessed “inherent objective necessity” and grew in execution; Frederick’s far-reaching plans shrank under political and resource limits.
- **Battle contrast (Frederican vs. Napoleonic schematic)**: unified body / small or no reserves / first blow strongest vs. corps system / very strong reserves / last blow strongest; “On s’engage partout et après on voit.”
- **Prussian reformers**: Scharnhorst recognized skirmishers early (1794 diary, 1797 essays); proposed third rank for skirmishing while preserving cohesive front; post-1806 introduced “column toward the middle,” universal service (1813 wartime, permanent via Boyen). Gneisenau executed bold operational marches sacrificing communications (1813 Saale junction; 1815 Wavre to La Belle-Alliance). “These animals have learned something” (Napoleon).
- **Clausewitz synthesis**: “War, however, is an action of politics, and strategy can therefore in no way be isolated but must always be considered only in its relationship to politics... In the most critical decisive moments, politics and strategy are not to be distinguished from one another.” Double-poled art of war (overthrow vs. limited). “The great strategists in the full sense of the word begin only with Gustavus Adolphus. ... This period finds its summit and its conclusion in Frederick the Great.”
- **Method note**: Contemporary observers (Hoyer 1800 etc.) missed the strategic shift, seeing only technical details. “reports can always be considered as confirmed... only when it can be established that they are objectively in agreement with the overall direction of the development.”

(Additional battle-specific and numbers claims—Höchstädt, Turin, Malplaquet, Mollwitz, Hohenfriedberg, Leuthen, Kunersdorf, Torgau, “potato war,” 1812 losses, etc.—are recorded in the range extracts and available for event-page enrichment.)
