# Rome in the East: The Transformation of an Empire

Warwick Ball, *Rome in the East: The Transformation of an Empire* (London/New York:
Routledge, 2000). A synthesis of the history, architecture and archaeology of the Near
Eastern provinces of the Roman Empire, of Roman penetration beyond the frontiers, and of
the eastern influences that Ball argues transformed Rome itself. Period covered: from
Pompey's conquest (63 BC) to Heraclius' loss of the East (AD 636).

## Central Argument

Ball inverts the conventional Romanization narrative. Where Classicists (he singles out
Fergus Millar's *The Roman Near East*) read the Roman East as a Graeco-Roman world with a
thin native substrate, Ball argues the opposite: Near Eastern civilisation — older and more
continuous than Rome's — lived on under a Macedonian then Roman "veneer" and ultimately
absorbed and transformed its conqueror. The evidence he privileges is **material remains**
(the Roman East has more than any province save North Africa) and **native-language
epigraphy** (Safaitic, Palmyrene, Nabataean, Aramaic — vastly outnumbering the over-read
Greek inscriptions), against what he treats as an uncritically accepted Graeco-Roman
literary bias descending from Gibbon.

The "oriental revolution" he traces has several strands: proxy rule through Near Eastern
client kingdoms (Emesa, Judaea, Nabataea, Palmyra, Edessa, later the Tanukhid and Ghassanid
confederations); the rise of Syrian/Arab emperors of Rome (the Severans via Julia Domna's
Emesene family; Elagabalus and the cult of Sol Invictus Elagabal; Philip the Arab); the
eastern derivation of much "imperial Roman" architecture; the movement of eastern peoples,
cults and ideas westward; and finally the triumph of an eastern religion (Christianity) and
the move of the capital east to Constantinople — Rome becoming "more of an Asian than a
European power."

## Terminology (Ball's usages, adopted where it aids linking)

- Prefers **"Seleucid"/"Macedonian"** over "Greek"/"Hellenistic" for the post-Alexander Near East.
- Prefers **"Iran"/"Eranshahr"** over "Parthia"/"Persia" (both western appellations; stresses
  Parthian–Sasanian continuity, the Shahinshah title, Achaemenid claims).
- Rejects the **"Byzantine"** category as a late coinage that disinherited the eastern Roman
  Empire from Rome; uses "Roman Empire" throughout to 1453.
- Uses native personal-name forms (Udaynath, Wahballath, Maliku, ‘Obaydath, Mawiyya,
  al-Mundhir) but retains familiar Latin forms where entrenched (Zenobia, Julia Domna, Herod).
- Geographic scope: the Near East ≈ Roman Syria, Palestine, Arabia and (briefly) Mesopotamia;
  Anatolia and Egypt deliberately excluded.

## Volume Synthesis Note

*Rome in the East* is the most sustained single-volume statement of the "Near East as agent, not
object" reading of Roman history, and it enters a wiki already strong on the Graeco-Roman East
(CAH XII, Millar-derived framing on [[roman-syria]], [[palmyra]], [[antioch]]). Its value here is
precisely as a **counterweight**: where the existing pages narrate the Roman East from the imperial
centre, Ball supplies the archaeological, epigraphic and Arab-dynastic material to argue the
opposite direction of influence. The ingest treats his descriptive archaeology as reliable and his
interpretive inversions as *positions* — hence the dedicated [[orientalization-of-rome]] process
page and the neutral [[orientalization-of-rome-thesis]] controversy, which name Fergus Millar's
*The Roman Near East* (1993) as the mainstream counter-view.

The volume's densest contribution is the **Arab dimension of Roman history**, thinly covered
before this ingest: the client kingdoms ([[emesene-dynasty]], [[nabataean-kingdom]],
[[kingdom-of-edessa]]) and the foederati confederations ([[tanukhids]], [[ghassanids]],
[[queen-mawiyya]]) now have pages, and the Arab thread running through the Roman throne
([[julia-domna]], [[elagabalus]], [[philip-the-arab]]) is drawn out. A second cluster concerns
**material culture and architecture** — the eastern derivation of the colonnaded street, the
tetrapylon (from the Persian *chahartaq*), and the temenos-temple, staged across [[petra]],
[[baalbek]], [[dura-europos]], [[gerasa]], [[bosra]] and the [[dead-cities]], and exported west at
[[lepcis-magna]].

Two of Ball's larger theses create productive tension with existing pages and are flagged as his,
not adopted: his dismissal of the organised **"Silk Road"** as a modern myth sits directly against
the wiki's Benjamin-derived [[silk-roads]] and caravan-city material (noted on [[palmyra]] and
[[roman-eastern-trade]]); and his claim that **[[philip-the-arab|Philip the Arab]]**, not
Constantine, was Rome's first Christian emperor qualifies the [[christianization-of-the-roman-empire]]
narrative. The [[gandharan-art]] "Romano-Buddhist" material connects the volume, unexpectedly, to
the wiki's South Asian pages ([[gandhara]]). Throughout, Ball's own first-person archaeological
observations and his sharpest interpretive leaps were flagged during extraction and attributed in
wiki text.
