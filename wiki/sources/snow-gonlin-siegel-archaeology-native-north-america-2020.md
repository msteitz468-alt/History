## Overview

The standard graduate/upper-undergraduate survey of Native North American archaeology, covering the initial peopling of the continent through European contact and colonial-period archaeology. Uniquely includes Mesoamerica and the Caribbean within its scope. Organized thematically within regional chapters — each covers Environment and Adaptation, Demography and Conflict, Subsistence and Economy, Architecture and Technology, Culture/Language/Identity, Art and Symbolism, and Resilience and Collapse.

The book's central argument: the independent cultural evolution of the Americas paralleled developments elsewhere in the world, demonstrating universal human potential. Agricultural intensification, urban centers, and complex political organization all emerged in North America independently of Old World trajectories.

## Volume Synthesis Note

The book's governing thesis — that Native North American cultural evolution independently paralleled Old World trajectories — is borne out across the chapters: mound-building, urban centers (Cahokia), complex political hierarchies (Mississippian chiefdoms), long-distance exchange networks (Hopewell Interaction Sphere), and intensive agriculture all emerge independently on the continent, demonstrating universal human potential rather than diffusion from the Old World.

The single most structurally significant argument for the wiki is the multi-center nature of North American agricultural origins: Eastern Woodlands domestication (squash, sumpweed, sunflower, goosefoot, c. 3000 BCE) is independent from and preceded Mesoamerican crop diffusion northward. The Eastern Agricultural Complex underpins Adena-Hopewell; maize adoption c. 400–800 CE enables Mississippian chiefdoms. This corrects any tendency to treat North American complexity as derivative of Mesoamerica.

The Clovis-first controversy receives thorough treatment: Monte Verde, Paisley Caves, Meadowcroft, and Gault establish pre-Clovis occupation is now accepted, while the exact timing, entry routes, and number of founding populations remain contested. The book accepts a primary entry from Beringia ~14,500+ BP with coastal route as plausible co-route.

The megafaunal extinction debate is presented as unresolved (2020): combination hypothesis (hunting + climate) is now dominant over either pure overkill or pure climate, but the thin kill-site evidence for most extinct species keeps the question open. The absence of horses and other draft animals from the Americas until European reintroduction is correctly identified as a structural constraint shaping all pre-Columbian technology and economy.

A notable tension with other ingested sources: the scope of Cahokia's population and political complexity remains debated; the book treats it as a Mississippian chiefdom without pressing the question of whether it exceeded chiefdom-level complexity. This aligns with mainstream North American archaeology but may require a controversy note relative to more maximal interpretations.
