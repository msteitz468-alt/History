# Paddy Griffith, *Battle Tactics of the Western Front: The British Army’s Art of Attack, 1916–18* (1994)

Paddy Griffith’s study is the key Tier-3 source for the **British** side of the Western Front tactical “learning curve” in the second half of the First World War. It directly complements the German-focused [[sources/lupfer-dynamics-of-doctrine-1981]] and provides the detailed British material needed to flesh out the [[hubs/warfare/world-wars-era/first-world-war/first-world-war]] hub and related battle analyses beyond the 1 July 1916 baseline established by [[sources/keegan-face-of-battle-1976]].

Griffith positions the work against two problems: the popular image of the Western Front as pure futile slaughter (especially 1 July 1916 and Passchendaele) and the historiographical tendency to award tactical modernity only to a narrow set of “innovators” (German stormtroopers, tanks, ANZACs/Canadians) while portraying the broader BEF and its high command as congenitally rigid. His central claim is that tactical innovation was widespread within the BEF; that by early 1917 the essentials of modern small-unit all-arms tactics had been worked out and made available to the entire force; and that artillery, automatic weapons, and training systems evolved in parallel and mutually supporting ways.

## Historiography / impact note
Griffith’s book is a deliberate corrective within the “new military history” tradition. It insists on technical military analysis (what weapons actually did, how formations were trained and employed, the evolution of fire support) rather than moral or social commentary alone. It directly feeds the warfare hub’s requirement for 9-principles analysis, OOB detail, critical decisions, and modern doctrine parallels. The author himself flags his own limitations of coverage and source base.

The work is frequently cited in debates over the British “learning curve” and the relative sophistication of 1917–18 BEF methods versus popular memory. It pairs especially well with Lupfer for balanced Allied/Central Powers tactical comparison on the Western Front.

## Primary Claims from the Text (Grounded Extractions)

### Range 1 (Front matter + Preface + Introduction + Ch. 2 "The Tactical Dilemma") — lines ~1–2563
**Historiographical purpose and corrective focus:** Griffith constructs the volume around the technical/tactical achievements of the infantry (and supporting arms) in the second half of the war, countering historiography that dismisses them in favour of horror and high-command incompetence.

Quote: "much Great War historiography tended to be dismissive of the many technical achievements of the infantry... it seems to be the infantry itself, which so horrifically bore so much more than the lion’s share of the war, that has most seriously been denied a proper account of its tactical methods in modern times. In the present book I therefore hope to offer something of a corrective to this trend." (Preface).

**Neglect of obvious tactical primary sources:** Papers of Maxse, Lindsay, Partridge, and Liddell Hart’s 1916–18 scribblings had not been systematically exploited.

Quote: "It seems incredible that such a major war should have been so badly served by previous historians..." (Preface).

**BEF pre-Somme exposure limited; Somme as pivot:** "the BEF was heavily engaged in major battle for little more than thirty days between the Christmas fraternisations of 1914, and 30 June 1916." The Somme was the biggest single BEF battle, roughly equalling all prior action; the following 23 months contained roughly three times as much.

**Lions led by Donkeys narrative and material vs command debate:** Majority view since late 1920s blamed high-command stupidity/inflexibility (cavalrymen wanting mounted breakout) rather than new material/technological conditions. By 1917 platoon tactics and artillery cooperation were solving problems; "it had been repeatedly proved that carefully prepared assaults could be very successful even against strong defences."

### Range 2 (Ch. 3–4: Infantry first two years + Lessons of the Somme) — ~2570–4208
**Prewar doctrine and ambiguities:** Fire-and-movement (firing line ~200 yds then bayonet charge). Ambiguity between "full" (dense waves) and "empty" battlefield. Maxse advocated multiple successive waves (four lines usually succeed). 

**Old Contemptibles and New Armies:** High-volume "mad minute" musketry; limited offensive opportunity in 1914 (Mons/Le Cateau defensive). 1915 muddle; early creeping barrages and machine-gun barrages at Loos/Neuve Chapelle.

**9th Division examples (Longueval 14 July 1916):** Wave/column variants; densities as light as 1 man per 5.5 yards in places.

**Laffargue influence:** French pamphlet (May 1915) promoted successive waves for infiltration; distributed to BEF; hailed as "father of infiltration tactics" but required backing waves.

**Fourth Army Red Book (May 1916):** Platoons as tactical unit; specialised fighting/mopping/support/carrying platoons; "all rising... as one man".

**Somme lessons — creeping barrage and anti-bomb reaction:** Necessity of hugging the barrage closely. Reaction against "cult of the bomb"; rifle and bayonet reasserted as main weapon.

**SS 143 (Feb 1917):** Platoon as self-contained all-arms unit (Lewis, bombers, rifle-grenadiers, riflemen); two-wave structure. "In essence a stormtrooper’s handbook" applied army-wide.

**Assault spearhead divisions and reformers:** Informal GHQ "premier league" (9th Scottish most innovative; 18th under Maxse; others + Dominion corps). Maxse, Lindsay, Jacob drove analysis and preparation. "There is really no substitute for careful preparation."

### Range 3 (Ch. 5–6: Final eighteen months + Search for New Weapons) — ~4211–5733
**1917 formal set-pieces vs 1918 chaotic mobile:** Arras (2,817 guns + Livens + tanks; spectacular Vimy penetration by 9th/4th Divs); Messines (20 mines + 2,266 guns); Third Ypres planned as repeated bite-and-holds (Davidson GHQ 22 June memo); Haig switched from Gough to more methodical Plumer after early failures/mud.

**Flexible formations and initiative:** Platoon sections evolved; Maxse standardised sections (1 NCO + 6 men); official choice of waves/worms/blobs/diamonds by Oct 1918 IT pamphlet. "When in doubt go ahead... identify soft spots."

**Weapons mobilisation:** Bottom-up improvisation to central Ministry of Munitions. Stokes mortar saga; gas (Foulkes Special Brigade, 110 major cloud attacks in H2 1916, Livens projectors for sudden mass delivery); institutional "cap-badge clannishness" hindered all-arms coherence.

**Infantry tactics continuity:** Still "charging forward as closely as possible behind a creeping barrage" + rifle/bayonet; "To advance is to win."

### Range 4 (Ch. 7–8: Automatic Weapons + Artillery) — ~5736–7678
**Lindsay and MGC:** Centralisation battle vs infantry diffusion; Haig initially opposed independent corps. MGC warrant Oct 1915; effective 1916 (Grantham/Camiers). Lindsay's "strategic" manpower-saving argument; overhead/long-range barrage fire using artillery methods (Brutinel practical pioneer; British ahead by 1917).

**Lewis gun integration:** Early adoption; 1 per platoon (SS 143) with dedicated section; often 2 by 1918. Initial infantry suspicion (displacing Vickers); proved key for covering fire, pillbox suppression, hip fire in mobile ops.

**Artillery revolution and neutralising fire:** Predicted fire (survey, air photos, flash/sound ranging, weather, calibration) by 1917 (80 yds accuracy at 4,000 yds for 18-pdr). Shift from destructive to neutralising (pin/suppress so infantry can close). Creeping barrage maturation (multiple lines, HE/shrapnel/smoke mixes; 106 fuse; shorter preps; surprise at Cambrai via unregistered predicted fire).

**Deep battle:** Long-range CB + air interdiction/ground attack; attrition extended behind front lines. Gun densities and shell-per-yard formulae developed and applied.

**Command:** BGRA/GOCRA authority increased; enabled flexible 1918 planning.

### Range 5 (Ch. 9–11 + apps: Controlling the Mobile Battle + Doctrine/Training + Conclusion) — ~7681–10150
**Cavalry, tanks, signals in mobile battle:** Cavalry modernised (wireless, motor MGs, all-arms battlegroups) and used in four actual mobile episodes; tanks auxiliary (penny-packet justified; limited breakout role pre-cruiser). Signals evolved dramatically for 1918 leapfrogging/forward command.

**Partridge and doctrine machine:** Army Printing and Stationery Depot (Aug 1914) under Partridge produced massive, iterative SS-series manuals (SS 135 five versions; SS 214 etc.). GHQ already systematic before 1918 Inspectorate.

**Training schools:** "University of higher tactics" disseminating complex all-arms methods amid high turnover. Post-battle analysis and questionnaires.

**Synthesis and comparisons:** SS 143 "stormtrooper’s handbook" applied to whole BEF; British often ahead or parallel. German 1918 successes against straw-man (unprepared/overextended) positions; poor German tactics sometimes ("cricket match crowds"). Historiography overstates charmed circle of innovators; GHQ drove broad innovation. BEF a highly effective tactical machine whose full stature appeared in the Hundred Days.

**Appendices:** Critique of university "politics and society" marginalisation of pure tactical history; ACW parallel (rapid learning but slower tech/structural change + weaker regular continuity).

**Volume Synthesis Note:** Griffith demonstrates that British tactical adaptation 1916–18 was systematic, army-wide, and often in advance of or parallel to German efforts. The "learning curve" was real and institutionally driven (manuals, schools, reformers like Maxse/Lindsay, artillery technical revolution). Popular and some scholarly narratives that confine modernity to a narrow set of actors distort the record. The BEF entered the Hundred Days with proven methods that could be applied flexibly in semi-open warfare. This source supplies the British counterpart to Lupfer and the technical depth required for West Point-grade analysis of 1917–18 Western Front battles in the warfare hub. Cross-volume tension: complements Strachan/Doughty operational layers while challenging any residual "lions led by donkeys" or "only Germans innovated" simplifications.
