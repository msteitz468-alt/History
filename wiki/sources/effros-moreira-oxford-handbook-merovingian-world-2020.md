# The Oxford Handbook of the Merovingian World — Effros & Moreira (eds.), 2020

**The reference survey of Merovingian studies in English.** Forty-six essays gather
the present state of the field across history, archaeology, and the sciences, covering
Gaul and its wider world from the fifth through the eighth centuries. The editors'
governing argument (Ch. 1, "Pushing the Boundaries of the Merovingian World"): the
Merovingian centuries were **not a "Dark Age"** but a vital, decentralized yet deeply
**connected** world — linked to the Mediterranean, northern and central Europe, and
Byzantium through diplomacy, religion, war, and trade — in which "much of Rome still
survived," ready for the Carolingians to put to practical and symbolic use.

## Argument and approach

- **Against decline and teleology.** The volume rejects both the nineteenth-century
  image of the [[rois-faineants|rois fainéants]] ("do-nothing kings") and the
  Carolingian-driven story of Merovingian decay. It reads the period "on its own terms,"
  resisting the discursive imperatives of *stagnation, decline, transition,
  transformation, rise*.
- **Interdisciplinarity as method.** Its defining move is to set **material evidence
  alongside written sources** — and to let archaeology, osteology, isotope and DNA
  analysis, archaeozoology, archaeobotany, numismatics, and gemmology *question* the
  texts rather than merely illustrate them. New scientific techniques (fission-track
  dating of glass, garnet microspectrometry) reveal trade reaching Sri Lanka, the Red
  Sea, and the Indian Ocean.
- **Pushing the geographic boundaries.** Following Ian Wood's *The Merovingian North
  Sea* (1983), the volume treats Merovingian Gaul as the center of a far-flung network
  rather than an isolated Franco-Gallo-Roman dynastic story — connected to Byzantium,
  Britain, Ireland, Italy (Ostrogoths and Lombards), the Avars, Slavs, Bavarians,
  Thuringians, Frisians, and Anglo-Saxons.
- **Religion on its own terms.** Merovingian Christianity is recovered from the charge
  of theological poverty: a "mosaic of local traditions" in liturgy, the cult of saints,
  hagiography, penance, and lay devotion, distinct from Carolingian uniformity.
