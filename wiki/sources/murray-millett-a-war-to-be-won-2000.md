# A War to Be Won: Fighting the Second World War (Murray & Millett, 2000)

Comprehensive operational history of World War II emphasizing the actual fighting: campaigns, battles, command decisions, logistics, technology, and adaptation at the operational and tactical levels. The authors argue that while moral and ideological factors mattered, the war had to be won on the battlefield through superior strategy, resources, and the ability to learn and adapt. It covers the interwar "revolution in military operations," Axis early successes, turning points, the combined bomber offensive, Battle of the Atlantic, invasions, and final campaigns in Europe and the Pacific, plus a chapter on "Peoples at War."

The book stresses the importance of combined arms (air-ground-sea integration), the decisive role of American and Soviet production and manpower, Allied coalition (despite frictions), and Axis failures in grand strategy and adaptation.

## Key Themes and Claims (for Extraction)
- Interwar: Revolution in military affairs (blitzkrieg concepts, but incomplete implementation by most).
- Why Axis early success: German combined arms, Japanese surprise and initial superiority; but overextension and poor grand strategy.
- Turning points 1942: Production shift to Allies, Stalingrad, El Alamein, Midway, Guadalcanal; Soviet recovery.
- Allied strengths: US industrial might ("arsenal of democracy"), British sea/air power, Soviet manpower/resilience, coalition (Tehran, etc.).
- Air power: Bomber offensive's mixed results (moral effect, industry damage, but high cost; LeMay firebombing Japan decisive in end).
- Pacific: Island hopping, carrier battles, submarine campaign strangling Japan.
- Final campaigns: Overlord logistics (Mulberries), air superiority, Soviet steamroller; Japan: Iwo/Okinawa as bases, atomic + Soviet entry forcing surrender.
- "Peoples at War": Total war's impact on civilians, economies, societies; atrocities on both sides.
- Conclusion: Allies won because they fought better in the end – learned from mistakes, mobilized resources more effectively, maintained coalition, exploited Axis mistakes. Moral cause alone insufficient; had to win battles.

## Historiography Note
Murray and Millett (authors of the Military Effectiveness series) provide a clear-eyed operational account stressing friction, logistics, command, and adaptation. Strong on Eastern Front revisionism post-Soviet archives and Pacific naval/air war. Less emphasis on political/ social than some (e.g., Overy), but excellent for "how the war was won" at campaign level. Complements West Point History's map/graphic focus with narrative depth. Potential US/UK-centric bias on coalition success noted; good on Soviet contributions.
