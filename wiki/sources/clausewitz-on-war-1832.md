# On War (Vom Kriege) — Carl von Clausewitz (Howard & Paret trans., 1984)

**Primary source.** The foundational theoretical work of Western strategic thought. Written
by Prussian general Carl von Clausewitz (1780–1831) across roughly fifteen years
(c. 1816–1831) and published posthumously by his widow Marie. Clausewitz died of cholera
before completing his planned revision; only Book One Chapter One was considered finished
by the author. The Howard & Paret Princeton translation (1976, indexed ed. 1984) is the
standard English scholarly text.

This edition also includes:
- **Peter Paret**, "The Genesis of On War" and "The Continuing Relevance of On War"
  (introductory essays)
- **Michael Howard**, "The Influence of Clausewitz" (introductory essay)
- **Bernard Brodie**, book-by-book analytical commentary (the "indexed" portion)

## Editors' Introductory Essays

Peter Paret's essay ("The Genesis of On War") traces Clausewitz's intellectual development under Gerhard von Scharnhorst and his experience of Prussia's catastrophic defeat by Napoleon in 1806. Paret outlines how Clausewitz developed his core concepts—specifically [[friction-in-war]]—as early as 1812 in his tutorial essay to the Prussian Crown Prince. Paret emphasizes Clausewitz’s use of Kantian philosophical framework to establish "absolute war" as a pure logical construct, which is then modified in reality by political aims and physical friction. 

Michael Howard's essay ("The Influence of Clausewitz") documents the historical reception of *On War*. He details how Helmuth von Moltke the Elder successfully integrated Clausewitz's emphasis on resolution, flexibility, and *coup d'oeil* into Prussian general staff doctrine. Howard also traces the Marxist-Leninist reception (via Lenin's intensive reading of the political sections) and the post-World War I British critique led by B. H. Liddell Hart, who accused Clausewitz of being the "apostle of total war" and bringing about the bloody attrition of the Western Front.

## Consolidated Volume Synthesis (Brodie + Editors)

*On War* presents a unified, dialectical theory of war that seeks to explain both its violent nature and its subordination to human reason. The work’s central thesis is that war is not an autonomous activity but a political instrument—"a continuation of political intercourse, with the addition of other means" (Book Eight, Chapter Six, B). 

The core challenge of the work is resolving the tension between the logical drive of war toward absolute violence (reciprocal escalation) and the modifications it undergoes in the real world. Clausewitz resolves this through the **Paradoxical Trinity**: war is a dynamic suspension between popular passion (violence), military chance and talent, and political reason (government). The physical and psychological impediments of **friction** ensure that real war always falls short of the absolute, resulting in a spectrum of conflict ranging from unlimited wars of overthrow to limited wars of limited aims.

The central methodological contribution of the volume is Clausewitz's rejection of prescriptive, geometric rules of strategy (such as Jomini's). Theory cannot provide a manual for action; its purpose is to analyze the constituent elements of war, train the commander's judgment, and guide his self-education. 

By ingesting the primary text, the wiki shifts from relying on secondary summaries (such as Freedman and Paret) to direct textual authority. It introduces detailed operational concepts from Books Three through Eight—including the primacy of moral forces, the defensive counteroffensive, the theater of operations, the logistics of supply, and the culminating point of victory.

## Range 0 Extraction (Book 1 or equivalent)

- **Strategic Plans as Inherently Political** (ll. 962-962): "War is not an independent phenomenon, but the continuation of politics by different means. - **Critique of Logistical and Supply-Centric Theories** (ll. 974-974): "Bülow attributed dominant roles to geographic features and the appropriate arrangement of the supply system, while largely ignoring the physical and psychological effects that might result from unexpected movements of the opponent, from violence, from the fortuitous." — Clausewitz critiques rigid mathematical and supply-oriented systems of warfare, asserting that strategy must account for psychological factors and chance rather than just material constraints. - **The Concept of Friction** (ll. 1010-1010): "Friction, he was to conclude in On War, is the only notion that more or less comprises those matters that distinguish the real war from war on paper." — This concept encapsulates the countless, unpredictable physical and psychological difficulties that degrade military performance, linking directly to [[concepts/friction-in-war]].
- **Subordination of the Military Aim to Political Objectives** (ll. 1178-1178): "The soldier trained to revere offensive spirit does not feel comfortable with the argument that the defensive is obviously the stronger form of war, and he especially does not like being told that the military aim must always be subordinated to the political objectives laid down by the civilian leaders." — Bernard Brodie notes military resistance to Clausewitz's assertions of defensive superiority and political subordination, linking to [[concepts/brodie-on-clausewitz]], [[concepts/defense-vs-attack-clausewitz]], and [[concepts/war-as-continuation-of-policy]].
- **The Unique Timelessness of Clausewitz** (ll. 1204-1204): "His is not simply the greatest but the only truly great book on war." — Brodie argues that Clausewitz's insights are unique and have not been fully superseded or integrated into later literature, linking to [[concepts/brodie-on-clausewitz]] and the legacy of [[actors/clausewitz-carl-von]].
- **Theory Must Remain Anchored in Experience** (ll. 1271-1271): "just as some plants bear fruit only if they don’t shoot up too high, so in the practical arts the leaves and flowers of theory must be pruned and the plant kept close to its proper soil—experience." — Clausewitz argues that military theory must not become an overly abstract logical system but must remain close to practical, empirical experience, linking to [[concepts/clausewitz-on-theory]] and the methodology of [[actors/clausewitz-carl-von]].
- **The Dual Aims of War** (ll. 1374-1374): "War can be of two kinds, in the sense that either the objective is to overthrow the enemy—to render him politically helpless or militarily impotent, thus forcing him to sign whatever peace we please; or merely to occupy some of his frontier-districts so that we can annex them or use them for bargaining at the peace negotiations." — This distinction introduces the two fundamental types of conflict (total vs. limited), linking to [[concepts/absolute-vs-real-war]] and [[concepts/war-aims-and-limited-war-clausewitz]].
- **The Continuation of Policy by Other Means** (ll. 1376-1376): "war is nothing but the continuation of policy with other means." — Clausewitz summarizes the relationship between political intercourse and military force, linking directly to [[concepts/war-as-continuation-of-policy]].
- **Instinct and Genius in Great Commanders** (ll. 1407-1407): "All great commanders have acted on instinct, and the fact that their instinct was always sound is partly the measure of their innate greatness and genius." — Command under stress relies on intuitive, rapid decisions rather than deliberate calculations, linking to [[concepts/military-genius]] and [[actors/clausewitz-carl-von]].
- **The Fundamental Asymmetry of Attack and Defense** (ll. 1411-1411): "defense is the stronger form of fighting with the negative purpose, attack the weaker form with the positive purpose" — This establishes the structural inequality between offensive and defensive modes of combat, linking to [[concepts/defense-vs-attack-clausewitz]] and strategic planning in [[concepts/strategy]].
- **The True Nature of Strategic Victory** (ll. 1411-1411): "victory consists not only in the occupation of the battlefield, but in the destruction of the enemy’s physical and psychic forces, which is usually not attained until the enemy is pursued after a victorious battle" — True victory requires breaking both the material and psychological capacity of the enemy, linking to [[concepts/battle-decision-and-exploitation]] and [[concepts/moral-forces-in-war]].
- **The Law of Diminishing Impetus in Attack** (ll. 1411-1411): "every attack loses impetus as it progresses." — Offensive operations naturally exhaust their momentum over time and space, linking to [[concepts/culminating-point-of-victory]] and [[concepts/defense-vs-attack-clausewitz]].
- **The Core Definition of War** (ll. 1455-1455): "War is thus an act of force to compels our enemy to do our will." — Clausewitz defines war by its primary means (force) and ultimate object (compelling the enemy), establishing the foundation for the [[hubs/warfare/warfare-hub]] and the study of [[hubs/warfare/strategy]].
- **The Fallacy of Bloodless Victory** (ll. 1468-1468): "Pleasant as it sounds, it is a fallacy that must be exposed: war is such a dangerous business that the mistakes which come from kindness are the very worst." — He warns against the dangerous assumption that war can be civilized or fought without maximum violence, linking to [[concepts/absolute-vs-real-war]] and the violent nature of the [[hubs/warfare/warfare-hub]].
- **The Theoretical Law of Extremes** (ll. 1484-1484): "war is an act of force, and there is no logical limit to the application of that force." — In abstract theory, reciprocal interactions drive both sides to apply maximum force without limit, linking to [[concepts/absolute-vs-real-war]].
- **Disarming the Enemy as the True Aim of Warfare** (ll. 1495-1495): "to overcome the enemy, or disarm him—call it what you will—must always be the aim of warfare." — In pure concept, war requires rendering the enemy defenseless to prevent counteraction, linking to [[concepts/absolute-vs-real-war]] and [[concepts/war-aims-and-limited-war-clausewitz]].
- **War is a Collision of Living Forces** (ll. 1497-1497): "War, however, is not the action of a living force upon a lifeless mass (total nonresistance would be no war at all) but always the collision of two living forces." — Because war involves two interacting active wills, it cannot be planned like a one-sided equation, linking to [[concepts/absolute-vs-real-war]].
- **The Two Inseparable Components of Resistance** (ll. 1508-1508): "If you want to overcome your enemy you must match your effort against his power of resistance, which can be expressed as the product of two inseparable factors, viz. the total means at his disposal and the strength of his will." — Resistance is calculated by combining material resources with psychological willpower, linking [[concepts/absolute-vs-real-war]] and [[concepts/moral-forces-in-war]].
- **Why Real War Falls Short of the Absolute** (ll. 1523-1523): "But move from the abstract to the real world, and the whole thing looks quite different." — Real-world factors moderate the theoretical drive to extremes, introducing the distinction between [[concepts/absolute-vs-real-war]].
- **Real-World Gauging vs. Abstract Perfection** (ll. 1534-1534): "Each side can therefore gauge the other to a large extent by what he is and does, instead of judging him by what he, strictly speaking, ought to be or do." — In practice, opponents assess each other based on real-world actions rather than theoretical maximums, linking [[concepts/absolute-vs-real-war]] and [[concepts/clausewitz-on-theory]].
- **Imperfect Concentration of Forces Moderates War** (ll. 1559-1559): "the very nature of war impedes the simultaneous concentration of all forces." — The physical impossibility of deploying all national resources instantly moderates the speed and violence of conflict, linking to [[concepts/absolute-vs-real-war]] and the application of [[concepts/strategy]].
- **The Transitory Nature of Strategic Outcomes** (ll. 1570-1570): "The defeated state often considers the outcome merely as a transitory evil, for which a remedy may still be found in political conditions at some later date." — Political realities ensure that military victory is rarely absolute or permanent, affecting [[concepts/war-aims-and-limited-war-clausewitz]].
- **Real War Governed by Probabilities** (ll. 1581-1581): "Once the antagonists have ceased to be mere figments of a theory and become actual states and governments, when war is no longer a theoretical affair but a series of actions obeying its own peculiar laws, reality supplies the data from which we can deduce the unknown that lies ahead." — Real war operates in the realm of probability and judgment rather than absolute logic, linking [[concepts/clausewitz-on-theory]] and [[concepts/absolute-vs-real-war]].
- **The Political Object as the Standard of Effort** (ll. 1596-1596): "The political object—the original motive for the war—will thus determine both the military objective to be reached and the amount of effort it requires." — The political purpose of the war sets the scale of both military goals and resource commitment, linking to [[concepts/war-as-continuation-of-policy]] and [[concepts/war-aims-and-limited-war-clausewitz]].
- **The Unequal Strength of Attack and Defense** (ll. 1671-1671): "But there are two distinct forms of action in war: attack and defense. As will be shown in detail later, the two are very different and unequal in strength." — Polarity does not apply directly to attack and defense because they are fundamentally asymmetric, forming the basis of [[concepts/defense-vs-attack-clausewitz]].
- **Superiority of the Defensive Explains Inaction** (ll. 1684-1684): "I am convinced that the superiority of the defensive (if rightly understood) is very great, far greater than appears at first sight. It is this which explains without any inconsistency most periods of inaction that occur in war." — The inherent strength of defensive combat allows weaker forces to wait, leading to periods of operational suspension, linking to [[concepts/defense-vs-attack-clausewitz]] and strategic planning in [[concepts/strategy]].
- **Imperfect Knowledge Delays Operations** (ll. 1695-1695): "partial ignorance of the situation is, generally speaking, a major factor in delaying the progress of military action and in moderating the principle that underlies it." — Ignorance of the enemy's situation slows down operations, demonstrating how [[concepts/friction-in-war]] moderates the absolute tendencies of [[concepts/absolute-vs-real-war]].
- **War as a Gamble of Probabilities** (ll. 1719-1719): "No other human activity is so continuously or universally bound up with chance. And through the element of chance, guesswork and luck come to play a great part in war." — Chance makes war a highly unpredictable activity, linking to [[concepts/friction-in-war]] and the central realities of the [[hubs/warfare/warfare-hub]].
- **The Card Game Metaphor of Combat** (ll. 1732-1732): "In the whole range of human activities, war most closely resembles a game of cards." — The subjective and objective uncertainty of war makes it a gamble where courage must balance risk, linking [[concepts/friction-in-war]], [[concepts/military-genius]], and [[concepts/moral-forces-in-war]].
- **The Dominance of the Political Purpose** (ll. 1758-1758): "If we keep in mind that war springs from some political purpose, it is natural that the prime cause of its existence will remain the supreme consideration in conducting it." — War must always remain subordinate to the political objective that created it, linking to [[concepts/war-as-continuation-of-policy]].
- **War is a Continuation of Politics with Other Means** (ll. 1769-1769): "We see, therefore, that war is not merely an act of policy but a true political instrument, a continuation of political intercourse, carried on with other means." — War is a distinct tool of statecraft that uses violence as its specific medium, linking directly to [[concepts/war-as-continuation-of-policy]].
- **The Cardinal Question of Strategy** (ll. 1806-1806): "The first, the supreme, the most far-reaching act of judgment that the statesman and commander have to make is to establish by that test the kind of war on which they are embarking; neither mistaking it for, nor trying to turn it into, something that is alien to its nature." — Aligning the nature of the conflict with political realities is the most critical judgment in [[concepts/strategy]] and [[concepts/war-aims-and-limited-war-clausewitz]].
- **The Paradoxical Trinity** (ll. 1819-1819): "As a total phenomenon its dominant tendencies always make war a paradoxical trinity—composed of primordial violence, hatred, and enmity, which are to be regarded as a blind natural force; of the play of chance and probability within which the creative spirit is free to roam; and of its element of subordination, as an instrument of policy, which makes it subject to reason alone." — Clausewitz defines the three aspects of war (emotion/people, chance/army, reason/government) as a dynamic system, linking to [[concepts/remarkable-trinity]] and [[concepts/war-as-continuation-of-policy]].
- **Three Broad Objectives in Warfare** (ll. 1847-1847): "But we should at once distinguish between three things, three broad objectives, which between them cover everything: the armed forces, the country, and the enemy’s will." — Strategic goals focus on these three elements, where physical actions are aimed at breaking the political will, linking to [[concepts/war-aims-and-limited-war-clausewitz]] and identifying the [[concepts/center-of-gravity]].
- **Alternatives to Disarming the Enemy** (ll. 1861-1861): "Inability to carry on the struggle can, in practice, be replaced by two other grounds for making peace: the first is the improbability of victory; the second is its unacceptable cost." — Peace is often negotiated when victory becomes unlikely or too expensive, rather than waiting for complete collapse, linking to [[concepts/war-aims-and-limited-war-clausewitz]] and [[concepts/absolute-vs-real-war]].
- **The Principle of Proportional Sacrifice** (ll. 1865-1865): "Since war is not an act of senseless passion but is controlled by its political object, the value of this object must determine the sacrifices to be made for it in magnitude and also in duration." — The value of the political goal limits the resources and time that can be rationally spent on it, linking [[concepts/war-as-continuation-of-policy]] and [[concepts/war-aims-and-limited-war-clausewitz]].
- **Strategic Disruption of Alliances** (ll. 1873-1873): "I refer to operations that have direct political repercussions, that are designed in the first place to disrupt the opposing alliance, or to paralyze it, that gain us new allies, favorably affect the political scene, etc." — Political maneuvers can achieve strategic objectives more efficiently than direct combat, linking [[concepts/strategy]] and [[concepts/war-as-continuation-of-policy]].
- **Wearing Down the Enemy** (ll. 1885-1885): "Thus the negative aim, which lies at the heart of pure resistance, is also the natural formula for outlasting the enemy, for wearing him down." — Defensive operations leverage time to gradually exhaust the enemy's resources and will, linking [[concepts/defense-vs-attack-clausewitz]] and [[concepts/strategy]].
- **Combat as the Sole Means** (ll. 1899-1899): "There is only one: combat. However many forms combat takes, however far it may be removed from the brute discharge of hatred and enmity of a physical encounter, however many forces may intrude which themselves are not part of fighting, it is inherent in the very concept of war that everything that occurs must originally derive from combat." — The threat or reality of physical combat is the basis of all military actions, linking [[concepts/clausewitz-on-theory]] and [[concepts/battle-decision-and-exploitation]].
- **The Cash Payment Metaphor of Battle** (ll. 1935-1935): "The decision by arms is for all major and minor operations in war what cash payment is in commerce. Regardless how complex the relationship between the two parties, regardless how rarely settlements actually occur, they can never be entirely absent." — Just as commerce relies on cash settlements, strategy ultimately depends on the ability to win battles, linking [[concepts/battle-decision-and-exploitation]] and [[concepts/clausewitz-on-theory]].
- **Destruction of Armed Forces as the Supreme Aim** (ll. 1961-1961): "of all the possible aims in war, the destruction of the enemy’s armed forces always appears as the highest." — Disarming the enemy's main army is the most decisive and effective option in combat, linking to [[concepts/battle-decision-and-exploitation]].
- **The Definition of Military Genius** (ll. 1983-1983): "Genius consists in a harmonious combination of elements, in which one or the other ability may predominate, but none may be in conflict with the rest." — Command talent requires a balanced integration of both mental and moral strengths, defining the concept of [[concepts/military-genius]].
- **Coup d'oeil and Determination** (ll. 2007-2007): "first, an intellect that, even in the darkest hour, retains some glimmerings of the inner light which leads to truth; and second, the courage to follow this faint light wherever it may lead. The first of these qualities is described by the French term, coup d’oeil; the second is determination." — Intuitive insight and the courage to make decisions under pressure are the two pillars of [[concepts/military-genius]] needed to overcome [[concepts/friction-in-war]].
- **The Coalescence of Strategy and Policy** (ll. 2109-2109): "To bring a war, or one of its campaigns, to a successful close requires a thorough grasp of national policy. On that level strategy and policy coalesce: the commander-in-chief is simultaneously a statesman." — High command requires a comprehensive understanding of state policy, linking [[concepts/strategy]], [[concepts/war-as-continuation-of-policy]], and [[concepts/military-genius]].
- **Danger as a Source of Friction** (ll. 2145-2145): "Danger is part of the friction of war. Without an accurate conception of danger we cannot understand war. That is why I have dealt with it here." — The physical threats of the battlefield distort rational thought, serving as a primary component of [[concepts/friction-in-war]] and testing [[concepts/moral-forces-in-war]].
- **Physical Exertion as a Source of Friction** (ll. 2167-2167): "Our reason for dealing with physical effort here is that like danger it is one of the great sources of friction in war." — Physical exhaustion degrades execution, acting as a major component of [[concepts/friction-in-war]] and demanding strong willpower, a key [[concepts/moral-forces-in-war]].
- **Contradictory and False Intelligence** (ll. 2185-2185): "Many intelligence reports in war are contradictory; even more are false, and most are uncertain." — The unreliability of information creates a fog of uncertainty, acting as a key element of [[concepts/friction-in-war]] and a challenge for [[concepts/clausewitz-on-theory]].
- **The Definition of Friction** (ll. 2205-2205): "Friction is the only concept that more or less corresponds to the factors that distinguish real war from war on paper." — Friction defines the real-world impediments that differentiate theoretical war from reality, capturing the essence of [[concepts/friction-in-war]].
- **Movement in a Resistant Element** (ll. 2211-2211): "Action in war is like movement in a resistant element. Just as the simplest and most natural of movements, walking, cannot easily be performed in water, so in war it is difficult for normal efforts to achieve even moderate results." — Clausewitz uses the water resistance metaphor to describe how [[concepts/friction-in-war]] retards all military movement.
- **Combat Experience as the Sole Lubricant** (ll. 2229-2229): "Is there any lubricant that will reduce this abrasion? Only one, and a commander and his army will not always have it readily available: combat experience." — Actual combat experience is the only force that can prepare troops to navigate [[concepts/friction-in-war]] and build necessary [[concepts/moral-forces-in-war]].


## Range 1 Extraction (Book 2 or equivalent)

- **Distinction Between Tactics and Strategy** (ll. 2285-2285): "tactics teaches the use of armed forces in the engagement; strategy, the use of engagements for the object of the war." — This fundamental claim by [[actors/clausewitz-carl-von]] establishes the dividing line between tactics and strategy. Tactics is localized, limited to the form and conduct of the engagement itself. Strategy, however, is the higher-level coordination that employs these engagements to achieve the political goals of the war, aligning with [[concepts/strategy]] and [[hubs/warfare/strategy]].
- **Inadequacy of Positive and Geometrical Theories in War** (ll. 2491-2497): "It is only analytically that these attempts at theory can be called advances in the realm of truth; synthetically, in the rules and regulations they offer, they are absolutely useless. They aim at fixed values; but in war everything is uncertain, and calculations have to be made with variable quantities. They direct the inquiry exclusively toward physical quantities, whereas all military action is intertwined with psychological forces and effects. They consider only unilateral action, whereas war consists of a continuous interaction of opposites." — This claim highlights the failure of early military theorists who attempted to reduce the art of war to mathematical or geometrical formulas. Clausewitz argues that war is dominated by uncertainty, psychological forces, and the continuous interaction of two active wills, which cannot be captured by rigid, unilateral models, linking to [[concepts/clausewitz-on-theory]] and [[concepts/friction-in-war]].
- **Theory as Analytical Study rather than Prescriptive Doctrine** (ll. 2706-2708): "a theory need not be a positive doctrine, a sort of manual for action. ... Theory exists so that one need not start afresh each time sorting out the material and plowing through it, but will find it ready to hand and in good order. It is meant to educate the mind of the future commander, or, more accurately, to guide him in his self-education, not to accompany him to the battlefield" — Clausewitz argues that theory should serve as an analytical investigation to train a commander's mind and judgment rather than a set of rules to be blindly followed on the battlefield. A commander must identify this point of density in the enemy's alignment to deliver a decisive blow, linking to [[concepts/center-of-gravity]] and [[concepts/strategy]].
- **Political Conditions as the Root Cause of Offensive Failure** (ll. 6445-6447): "The reason for the ineffectiveness of most attacks lies in the general, the political conditions of war. ... - **Political Intentions as Hidden Strategic Dampeners** (ll. 6461-6461): "The counterweights that weaken the elemental force of war, and particularly the attack, are primarily located in the political relations and intentions of the government, which are concealed from the rest of the world, the people at home, the army, and in some cases even from the commander." — The political motives behind a war act as hidden dampeners (friction) on military operations, often preventing commanders from taking decisive actions, linking to [[concepts/war-as-continuation-of-policy]], [[concepts/friction-in-war]], and [[concepts/war-aims-and-limited-war-clausewitz]].
- **Misjudging the Scale of War and Popular Passion in 1792** (ll. 8491-8491): "We will hardly find a more erroneous standard of measurement in history than that applied in 1792. ## Range 4 Extraction (Book 5 or equivalent)

- **Defense as an Inherent and Impeding Burden on the Attack** (ll. 8543-8545): "The act of attack, particularly in strategy, is thus a constant alternation and combination of attack and defense. It is its original sin, its mortal disease." — This claim outlines the dialectical nature of offensive operations, which cannot exist without integrating elements of defense to protect lines of communication and secure areas of halt. Clausewitz notes that defense acts as a dead weight ("original sin") that slows down the offensive's momentum, highlighting the inherent friction that attackers must overcome. While the defender benefits naturally from the support of the local population and home territory, the attacker operates in a hostile environment where such popular forces are rarely available and, if they do occur, are merely fortuitous. - **The Diminishing Force of the Attack** (ll. 8587-8587): "The diminishing force of the attack is one of the strategist’s main concerns. Strategists must calculate this natural decay of offensive power to avoid making decisions that exceed their resources. This links to [[concepts/defense-vs-attack-clausewitz]], [[concepts/logistics-and-supply-clausewitz]], and [[concepts/strategy]].
- **The Culminating Point of the Attack** (ll. 8633-8633): "Most of them only lead up to the point where their remaining strength is just enough to maintain a defense and wait for peace. This is what we mean by the culminating point of the attack." — This claim defines the critical threshold of offensive campaigns. Once the attacker's relative superiority is exhausted without securing a peaceful settlement, the balance of power shifts, forcing the attacker into a vulnerable defensive posture that invites a powerful counterattack from the defender. - **The Culminating Point of Victory** (ll. 9236-9236): "It is not possible in every war for the victor to overthrow his enemy completely. A victorious commander must recognize the threshold where their relative superiority peaks and begins to decline due to the friction of occupying hostile territory, extended supply lines, and the defender's heightened resistance. This links to [[concepts/culminating-point-of-victory]], [[concepts/defense-vs-attack-clausewitz]], and [[concepts/battle-decision-and-exploitation]].
- **The Necessity of Defining War's Political Purpose and Operational Objective** (ll. 9456-9456): "No one starts a war—or rather, no one in his senses ought to do so—without first being clear in his mind what he intends to achieve by that war and how he intends to conduct it. The former is its political purpose; the latter its operational objective." — This claim is a foundational tenant of Clausewitzian theory, establishing that all military plans and actions must be governed by a clear political purpose. The political goal sets the scope of the war and directs the military operations toward a unified operational objective. This links to [[concepts/war-as-continuation-of-policy]], [[concepts/absolute-vs-real-war]], and [[concepts/strategy]].
- **Theory Must Account for Real-World Friction and Human Nature** (ll. 9472-9472): "We must, therefore, be prepared to develop our concept of war as it ought to be fought, not on the basis of its pure definition, but by leaving room for every sort of extraneous matter. We must allow for natural inertia, for all the friction of its parts, for all the inconsistency, imprecision, and timidity of man; and finally we must face the fact that war and its forms result from ideas, emotions, and conditions prevailing at the time..." — Clausewitz argues that military theory cannot rely solely on abstract, logical deductions of absolute war. It must be flexible enough to accommodate real-world friction, human psychology, political contexts, and unpredictable occurrences. This links to [[concepts/friction-in-war]], [[concepts/clausewitz-on-theory]], and [[concepts/absolute-vs-real-war]].
- **Absolute War as the Essential Theoretical Point of Reference** (ll. 9476-9476): "Theory must concede all this; but it has the duty to give priority to the absolute form of war and to make that form a general point of reference, so that he who wants to learn from theory becomes accustomed to keeping that point in view constantly, to measuring all his hopes and fears by it, and to approximating it when he can or when he must." — While acknowledging that real-world wars rarely reach absolute violence due to political limitations and friction, Clausewitz maintains that "absolute war" remains the necessary logical benchmark. Theory must prioritize this absolute form so commanders can measure real campaigns against it and strive for decisive action. This links to [[concepts/clausewitz-on-theory]], [[concepts/absolute-vs-real-war]], and [[actors/clausewitz-carl-von]].
- **Intuition and Genius as Necessary for Assessing Strategic Resources** (ll. 9548-9548): "To assess these things in all their ramifications and diversity is plainly a colossal task. Rapid and correct appraisal of them clearly calls for the intuition of a genius; to master all this complex mass by sheer methodical examination is obviously impossible." — Clausewitz asserts that estimating the resources needed for war and evaluating political factors is an art rather than a mathematical science. Because of the complexity and uncertainty of these elements, a commander must rely on the intuitive coup d'oeil of military genius rather than methodical calculations. This links to [[concepts/military-genius]], [[concepts/clausewitz-on-theory]], and [[concepts/war-as-continuation-of-policy]].
- **Identifying and Targetting the Center of Gravity** (ll. 9654-9654): "What the theorist has to say here is this: one must keep the dominant characteristics of both belligerents in mind. That is the point against which all our energies should be directed." — Clausewitz introduces the center of gravity as the focal source of an enemy's power (e.g., their army, capital, or alliances). To achieve a decisive victory, all strategic planning must concentrate force directly against this hub rather than wasting resources on minor, peripheral operations. This links to [[concepts/center-of-gravity]], [[concepts/strategy]], and [[hubs/warfare/strategy]].
- **Two Core Principles of Strategic Planning: Concentration and Speed** (ll. 10018-10020): "The first principle is that the ultimate substance of enemy strength must be traced back to the fewest possible sources, and ideally to one alone. In short the first principle is: act with the utmost concentration. ## Range 5 Extraction (Book 6 or equivalent)

- **Logical Absurdity of Moderation in War** (ll. 10351-10351): "To introduce the principle of moderation into the theory of war itself would always lead to logical absurdity" — This core claim, highlighted in [[concepts/brodie-on-clausewitz]], targets the ideal concept of war where force is pushed to its absolute limit. In the theory of [[concepts/absolute-vs-real-war]], introducing moderation is a logical contradiction because war is an act of force to overcome the opponent, who would otherwise gain the upper hand. Thus, [[actors/clausewitz-carl-von]] argues that theory must start from the absolute nature of war before examining the modifications that occur in practice.
- **War is Never an Isolated Act** (ll. 10359-10359): "war is never an isolated act." — In real-world conflict, war does not break out suddenly without connection to preceding events. Instead, it arises from political relations and is influenced by previous history and preparation. This claim is crucial to [[concepts/absolute-vs-real-war]] and [[concepts/war-as-continuation-of-policy]], as it explains why real war deviates from the abstract concept of absolute violence, where war would occur in a vacuum.
- **Defense as the Stronger Form of War** (ll. 10363-10363): "defense is a stronger form of fighting than attack." — This claim introduces the core Clausewitzian paradox that defense is inherently stronger than attack. The defender benefits from waiting, terrain, shorter lines of supply, and the support of the populace, which helps offset numerical or physical weakness. This is a foundational concept in [[concepts/defense-vs-attack-clausewitz]] and strategic planning.
- **Continuous Influence of Policy on Military Operations** (ll. 10365-10365): "If we keep in mind that war springs from some political purpose, it is natural that the prime cause of its existence will remain the supreme consideration in conducting it.... It must adapt itself to its chosen means, a process which can radically change it; yet the political aim remains the first consideration. Policy, then, will permeate all military operations and, in so far as their violent nature will admit, it will have a continuous influence on them." — This is a key articulation of [[concepts/war-as-continuation-of-policy]]. Since war is initialized by political goals, military operations cannot act as an independent entity. While the violent means of war may modify or shape policy, the political purpose must remain the primary guide throughout the conflict. This claim establishes the integration of politics and [[concepts/strategy]] under the supervision of [[actors/clausewitz-carl-von]].
- **Coincidence of Political and Military Aims in Total War** (ll. 10371-10371): "The more powerful and inspiring the motives for war ... the closer war will approach its abstract concept ... the more closely will the military aims and the political objects of war coincide, and the more military and less political will war appear to be." — This claim explains the sliding scale between limited war and total/absolute war. When the political motives are intense, the real war closely resembles [[concepts/absolute-vs-real-war]], making the political purpose seem to disappear behind the violent military actions. Conversely, when motives are weak, the war appears highly political. This dynamic is central to [[concepts/war-aims-and-limited-war-clausewitz]] and is analyzed closely in [[concepts/brodie-on-clausewitz]].
- **Renunciation of Purpose when Effort Exceeds Value** (ll. 10385-10385): "Since war is not an act of senseless passion but is controlled by its political purpose, the value of this purpose must determine the sacrifices to be made for it in magnitude and also in duration. Once the expenditure of effort exceeds the value of the political purpose, the purpose must be renounced and peace must follow." — This claim establishes a rational limit on the conduct of war, directly linking [[concepts/war-aims-and-limited-war-clausewitz]] with [[concepts/war-as-continuation-of-policy]]. Because war is a tool of policy, the costs (in terms of lives, resources, and time) must be balanced against the value of the political objective. When the price of victory becomes too high, the rational actor must seek peace rather than continue a pointless struggle.
- **The Definition of Tactics and Strategy** (ll. 10438-10438): "tactics 'the study of the employment of fighting forces in battle,' and strategy he describes as 'the study of the employment of battles for the object of the war'" — This claim provides the classic Clausewitzian definitions of the two distinct levels of warfare: tactics and strategy. Tactics deals with the immediate, physical clash of arms in an engagement, whereas [[concepts/strategy]] represents the higher-level art of using these engagements collectively to achieve the broader object of the war, a distinction key to [[hubs/warfare/strategy]] and [[concepts/clausewitz-on-theory]].
- **Theory as Training of the Mind Rather than Prescriptive Manual** (ll. 10446-10446): "Theory exists, he says, to 'distinguish precisely what at first sight seems fused.' With good theory one absorbs new material and experience without having to start afresh each time. ... This kind of theory serves the future commander by guiding him in his self-education but it is not suitable for accompanying him to the battlefield." — This is a fundamental assertion in [[concepts/clausewitz-on-theory]]. Theory is not meant to provide a dogmatic set of rules or a prescriptive manual for the battlefield. Instead, it serves an educational purpose: to clarify concepts, organize empirical historical experience, and train the mind of the future [[concepts/military-genius]] so that they can act intuitively under pressure.
- **War as a Clash of Animate Wills** (ll. 10452-10452): "war is not an exercise of will directed at inanimate matter ... but rather will directed 'at an animate object which reacts.'" — This claim distinguishes the conduct of war from typical arts or sciences, aligning it with social interaction and conflict. Because the enemy is an active, reacting agent rather than passive material, war is characterized by continuous interaction and reciprocal action. This concept is fundamental to [[concepts/clausewitz-on-theory]], [[concepts/strategy]], and the execution of military plans.
- **The Ultimate Objective of Decisive Actions is Peace** (ll. 10464-10464): "those involving great and decisive actions, the analysis must extend to the ultimate objective, which is to bring about peace." — This claim reinforces [[concepts/war-as-continuation-of-policy]] by stating that even the most massive and violent military operations must be analyzed in relation to their final political goal—establishing peace. A battle or campaign is not an end in itself but must serve to build the political conditions for a favorable peace, a connection emphasized in [[concepts/clausewitz-on-theory]] and [[hubs/warfare/strategy]].
- **Scientific Guidelines Cannot Function as a Truth Machine** (ll. 10472-10472): "One should never elaborate scientific guidelines as though they were a kind of truth machine." — This claim warns against the misuse of military theory to create rigid formulas that promise certain victory. Because war is filled with chance, human passion, and friction, it cannot be solved like an arithmetic problem. [[concepts/clausewitz-on-theory]] must remain flexible and avoid the jargon-laden, prescriptive approach criticized by [[concepts/brodie-on-clausewitz]].
- **Empirical Nature of the Art of War** (ll. 10482-10482): "The knowledge which is basic to the art of war,' he says, 'is empirical." — [[actors/clausewitz-carl-von]] claims that military theory cannot be deduced purely from abstract principles; it must be built upon empirical historical experience. History reveals the psychological realities of combat—such as how green versus veteran troops respond to danger—which cannot be calculated mathematically, linking to [[concepts/clausewitz-on-theory]] and [[concepts/moral-forces-in-war]].
- **The Simplicity and Difficulty of Strategy** (ll. 10503-10503): "Everything in strategy,' Clausewitz says again, 'is very simple; but that does not mean that everything is very easy. Once it has been determined, from the political conditions, what a war is meant to achieve and what it can achieve, it is easy to chart the course. But great strength of character, as well as great lucidity and firmness of mind, is required in order to follow through steadily, to carry out the plan, and not to be thrown off course by thousands of diversions." — This claim highlights the role of [[concepts/friction-in-war]] and [[concepts/military-genius]] in [[concepts/strategy]]. While the basic concepts of strategy are straightforward, execution is made extremely difficult by unforeseen events, danger, and administrative friction. Overcoming these "diversions" requires exceptional qualities of character and mind from the commander, a theme explored in [[hubs/warfare/strategy]].
- **Measureable and Non-Absolute Nature of Army Spirit** (ll. 10515-10515): "The spirit of an army ... is a tool whose power is measurable" — While recognizing the significance of morale, [[actors/clausewitz-carl-von]] avoids romanticizing it as all-important. This balanced view of moral factors is key to the study of [[concepts/moral-forces-in-war]].
- **Boldness Governed by Intellect** (ll. 10521-10521): "Boldness governed by superior intellect is the mark of a hero. When combined with high intellect, it enables a commander to make rapid, intuitive judgments and act decisively despite uncertainty, linking to [[concepts/moral-forces-in-war]].
- **Limit of Talent Against Superior Numbers** (ll. 10529-10529): "even the most talented of commanders will find it difficult to defeat an opponent twice his strength." — While military genius can compensate for some deficiencies, there is a physical limit to what leadership can achieve against raw numbers. - **Surprise is Frequently Thwarted by Friction** (ll. 10537-10537): "The principle is highly attractive in theory, but in practice it is often held up by the friction of the whole machine." — Although surprise is universally desired, Clausewitz cautions that it is difficult to achieve in practice due to the administrative, physical, and environmental friction of moving large forces. - **Strategic vs. Tactical Surprise** (ll. 10539-10539): "In strategy surprise becomes more feasible the closer it occurs in the tactical realm, and more difficult the more it approaches the higher levels of policy." — This claim establishes that surprise is highly dependent on scale. Tactical surprise (in local, immediate engagements) is relatively easy to achieve, whereas strategic surprise (at the level of theater plans and national policy) is extremely difficult because major preparations are hard to hide and slow to execute, a key element in [[concepts/strategy]] and [[hubs/warfare/strategy]].
- **The Law of Keeping Forces Concentrated** (ll. 10565-10565): "there is no higher and simpler law of strategy than that of keeping one’s forces concentrated" — This claim highlights concentration as the primary rule of [[concepts/strategy]]. - **The Principle of Economy of Force** (ll. 10579-10579): "make sure that all forces are involved ... that no part of the whole force is idle." — Economy of force is defined not as hoarding or minimizing force, but as ensuring that all available resources are actively contributing to the military goal. Idle forces are wasted forces, as even inappropriately used forces occupy the enemy, which is central to [[concepts/strategy]] and [[concepts/battle-decision-and-exploitation]].
- **The Real Nature of War is Crisis** (ll. 10597-10597): "The state of crisis ... is the real war; the equilibrium is nothing but the reflex." — In this claim, Clausewitz asserts that the true nature of war is found in periods of intense tension and decision rather than long periods of inactivity or equilibrium. - **The Inevitability of Pursuit to Secure Victory** (ll. 10618-10618): "the winner’s casualties ... seldom show much difference from the loser’s ... the really crippling losses which the vanquished does not share with the victor only start with his retreat." — Clausewitz explains that the physical destruction of the enemy is not fully accomplished during the battle itself, where casualties are often relatively even. If the attacker fails to secure peace before passing this culminating point, they will be forced into an awkward defensive position, linking to [[concepts/defense-vs-attack-clausewitz]] and [[concepts/war-aims-and-limited-war-clausewitz]].
- **Requirement of Clear Aims Before Commencing War** (ll. 10936-10936): "No one starts a war—or rather, no one in his senses ought to do so—without first being clear in his mind what he intends to achieve by that war and how he intends to conduct it." — This is a fundamental axiom of strategic planning.
