# On War (Vom Kriege) — Carl von Clausewitz (Howard & Paret trans., 1984)

**Primary source.** The foundational theoretical work of Western strategic thought. Written
by Prussian general Carl von Clausewitz (1780–1831) across roughly fifteen years
(c. 1816–1831) and published posthumously by his widow Marie. Clausewitz died of cholera
before completing his planned revision; only Book One Chapter One was considered finished
by the author. The Howard & Paret Princeton translation (1976, indexed ed. 1984) is the
standard English scholarly text.

This edition also includes:
- **Peter Paret**, "The Genesis of On War" and "The Continuing Relevance of On War"
  (introductory essays)
- **Michael Howard**, "The Influence of Clausewitz" (introductory essay)
- **Bernard Brodie**, book-by-book analytical commentary (the "indexed" portion)

## Editors' Introductory Essays

Peter Paret's essay ("The Genesis of On War") traces Clausewitz's intellectual development under Gerhard von Scharnhorst and his experience of Prussia's catastrophic defeat by Napoleon in 1806. Paret outlines how Clausewitz developed his core concepts—specifically [[friction-in-war]]—as early as 1812 in his tutorial essay to the Prussian Crown Prince. Paret emphasizes Clausewitz’s use of Kantian philosophical framework to establish "absolute war" as a pure logical construct, which is then modified in reality by political aims and physical friction. 

Michael Howard's essay ("The Influence of Clausewitz") documents the historical reception of *On War*. He details how Helmuth von Moltke the Elder successfully integrated Clausewitz's emphasis on resolution, flexibility, and *coup d'oeil* into Prussian general staff doctrine. Howard also traces the Marxist-Leninist reception (via Lenin's intensive reading of the political sections) and the post-World War I British critique led by B. H. Liddell Hart, who accused Clausewitz of being the "apostle of total war" and bringing about the bloody attrition of the Western Front.

## Consolidated Volume Synthesis (Brodie + Editors)

*On War* presents a unified, dialectical theory of war that seeks to explain both its violent nature and its subordination to human reason. The work’s central thesis is that war is not an autonomous activity but a political instrument—"a continuation of political intercourse, with the addition of other means" (Book Eight, Chapter Six, B). 

The core challenge of the work is resolving the tension between the logical drive of war toward absolute violence (reciprocal escalation) and the modifications it undergoes in the real world. Clausewitz resolves this through the **Paradoxical Trinity**: war is a dynamic suspension between popular passion (violence), military chance and talent, and political reason (government). The physical and psychological impediments of **friction** ensure that real war always falls short of the absolute, resulting in a spectrum of conflict ranging from unlimited wars of overthrow to limited wars of limited aims.

The central methodological contribution of the volume is Clausewitz's rejection of prescriptive, geometric rules of strategy (such as Jomini's). Theory cannot provide a manual for action; its purpose is to analyze the constituent elements of war, train the commander's judgment, and guide his self-education. 

By ingesting the primary text, the wiki shifts from relying on secondary summaries (such as Freedman and Paret) to direct textual authority. It introduces detailed operational concepts from Books Three through Eight—including the primacy of moral forces, the defensive counteroffensive, the theater of operations, the logistics of supply, and the culminating point of victory.
