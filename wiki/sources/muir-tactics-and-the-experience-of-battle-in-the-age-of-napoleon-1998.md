# Rory Muir, *Tactics and the Experience of Battle in the Age of Napoleon* (1998)

Muir provides a detailed study of what actually happened on Napoleonic battlefields at the level of the soldier, the company, the battalion, and the regiment. Drawing on hundreds of eyewitness accounts, the book examines preparation for battle, the roles and interactions of artillery, light infantry, line infantry and cavalry, the realities of command and control, the psychological strains of combat, and the aftermath of victory or defeat.

The work sits in the "face of battle" tradition pioneered by John Keegan and developed for the Napoleonic period by Paddy Griffith, while expanding the scope beyond single armies or single battles. It deliberately privileges practice over theory and the testimony of participants over later reconstructions.

## Historiography

Muir positions his study against both traditional tactical manuals (often arid and theoretical) and the narrative focus of campaign histories (Oman, Chandler), which devoted only limited space to "what actually happened" on the battlefield. He builds on Keegan's *Face of Battle* (soldier experience) and Griffith's psychological emphasis on infantry, while providing broader coverage across all arms and the full range of command levels. 

The source base is acknowledged as heavily British (with French supplementation). Muir argues that at the level of the common soldier under fire, national differences were less decisive than training, recent experience, leadership, and unit cohesion. He treats variations (e.g. Austerlitz vs Leipzig, or Peninsula vs central Europe) explicitly where evidence warrants, without forcing a single "Napoleonic tactic."

Later scholarship has continued to mine memoirs and to test drill-book prescriptions against practice; Muir's collection of quotations remains a primary reference point for the human and mechanical realities of Napoleonic combat.

### Range 1 Extraction (lines 50-1410)

- Prefatory historiographical gap in Napoleonic battle analysis. "despite all that has been written about Napoleonic warfare, there has been little serious analysis of what actually happened on the battlefield. Some of the leading historians in the field, including Sir Charles Oman and David Chandler, paused in their main work to devote a chapter or two, or an essay, to the tactics employed..." (Preface). contrasts_with: [[chandler-the-campaigns-of-napoleon-1966]]; contributed_to: [[napoleonic-tactics]]; part_of: [[sources/muir-tactics-and-the-experience-of-battle-in-the-age-of-napoleon-1998]].

- Shift toward soldier experience pioneered by Keegan and Griffith. "In 1976 John Keegan, in The Face of Battle, pioneered an original approach to the subject by concentrating on the experience of individual soldiers in battle, while in 1981 Paddy Griffith analysed infantry combat with a new and convincing emphasis on psychological factors." (Preface). analogous_to: [[face-of-battle-method]]; contributed_to: [[morale-and-cohesion-napoleonic]]; part_of: [[sources/muir-tactics-and-the-experience-of-battle-in-the-age-of-napoleon-1998]].

- British first-hand accounts unusually rich and central to analysis, though supplemented by French. "for the period of the Napoleonic Wars there is an extraordinarily rich collection of first-hand British accounts of combat, which appears to be unmatched in any other language." (Preface). contributed_to: [[sources/muir-tactics-and-the-experience-of-battle-in-the-age-of-napoleon-1998]]; part_of: [[napoleonic-warfare]].

- National differences mattered comparatively little at the level of actual combat. "at this level of combat national differences mattered comparatively little: Russian, Scot or Portuguese, a line of horsemen galloping straight at you looks much the same, and the steadiness of your unit will depend on its confidence and training..." (Preface). contrasts_with: [[napoleonic-wars]]; part_of: [[combined-arms]]; contributed_to: [[napoleonic-tactics]].

- Napoleon's personal visit to outposts on the eve of Austerlitz produced a spontaneous torch-lit demonstration of loyalty. "The troops, dozing in front of their camp-fires on the chill December night, recognized the party and, twisting the straw which was their only bedding into thousands more torches, illuminated Napoleon's path... saying as he returned to his bivouac, ‘This is the finest evening of my life.’" (Ch. 1). preceded_by: [[hubs/warfare/long-19th-century/napoleonic-wars/battle-of-austerlitz]]; contributed_to: [[napoleon]]; part_of: [[napoleonic-wars]].

- Pre-battle mood at Borodino included dread and fatalism among officers. "‘I could not escape the feeling that something huge and destructive was hanging over all of us... many of these brave troops would not survive until tomorrow evening, but would be lying torn and bloody on the field of battle.’" (Ch. 1, von Linsingen). preceded_by: [[hubs/warfare/long-19th-century/napoleonic-wars/russian-campaign-1812]]; contributed_to: [[morale-and-cohesion-napoleonic]].

- Soldiers in Moore's army at Sahagun expressed eagerness for battle as preferable to campaigning hardships. "‘On the 24th of December [1808] our headquarters were at Sahagun. Every heart beat with joy... “We will beat them to pieces and have our ease and enjoy ourselves”, said my comrades. I even preferred any short struggle, however severe, to the dreadful way of life we were, at this time, pursuing.’" (Ch. 1). contributed_to: [[morale-and-cohesion-napoleonic]]; part_of: [[napoleonic-wars]].

- Young Guard soldier Barrès began the 1805 campaign enthusiastic but rapidly encountered severe hardships. "We left Paris quite content to go campaigning... I was young, full of health and courage... The march to Strasbourg ‘was beautiful, but long’... I was so weary that I could neither eat nor sleep." (Ch. 1). preceded_by: [[hubs/warfare/long-19th-century/napoleonic-wars/battle-of-austerlitz]]; contributed_to: [[morale-and-cohesion-napoleonic]].

- Barrès witnessed plundering and the destructiveness of war en route to Ulm/Austerlitz. "Soon Barrès was shocked at his first sight of the destructiveness of war when he saw a farm thoroughly plundered and half demolished for firewood... ‘I shed tears over the fate of these poor villagers...’" (Ch. 1). part_of: [[napoleonic-wars]]; contributed_to: [[actors/muir-rory]] analysis of experience.

- Battle provided excitement, glamour and purpose that campaigning lacked; confidence in the commander was vital. "Battle added an element of excitement, glamour and purpose to a soldier's life... There assuredly never was an army so anxious as ours was to be brought into action on this occasion [Salamanca]... with the most devoted confidence in their leader, and an invincible confidence in themselves." (Ch. 1). contributed_to: [[morale-and-cohesion-napoleonic]]; enabled: [[wellington-arthur-wellesley]]; part_of: [[napoleonic-wars]].

- Pre-battle apprehension was intense and nearly universal, even among the brave. "time appears to move upon leaden wings; every minute seems an hour... On the whole, it is a situation of higher excitement, and darker and deeper feeling, than any other in human life..." (Ch. 1). contributed_to: [[morale-and-cohesion-napoleonic]]; part_of: [[face-of-battle-method]].

- At Austerlitz overall French losses were relatively low but unevenly distributed. "At Austerlitz the French army as a whole lost about 8,800 casualties... 1,305 were killed. In other words forty-nine of every fifty French soldiers present at Austerlitz survived the battle... the 24th Léger... lost no fewer than 126 men killed and 364 wounded." (Ch. 1). part_of: [[hubs/warfare/long-19th-century/napoleonic-wars/battle-of-austerlitz]]; contributed_to: [[napoleonic-tactics]].

- Salamanca produced roughly 10% British casualties; Albuera was far bloodier for the British infantry. "At Salamanca, in 1812, the 30,562 British and German troops in Wellington's army lost 3,129 casualties (just over one in ten)... At the terrible, bungled Battle of Albuera no fewer than four in every ten of the British and German infantry... were killed, wounded and missing." (Ch. 1). part_of: [[hubs/warfare/long-19th-century/napoleonic-wars/battle-of-waterloo]] (contextual); contributed_to: [[napoleonic-wars]] casualty patterns.

- French losses at Waterloo were heavy, especially in the Guard and d'Erlon's corps. "At Waterloo the British contingent... lost 30 per cent... Defeat at Waterloo cost the French dearly: 40 per cent of the officers in the Imperial Guard were casualties... d'Erlon's corps suffered even more, with over half of its officers being casualties (395 of 788)." (Ch. 1). part_of: [[hubs/warfare/long-19th-century/napoleonic-wars/battle-of-waterloo]].

- Disease and privation killed far more soldiers than battle across the period. "it is not surprising that the greatest killer of soldiers in the age of Napoleon was not the enemy, but disease and privation... of every twelve soldiers who crossed the Niemen [Russia], two returned alive, one fell in action or died from wounds, two were taken prisoner, and the remaining seven succumbed to the rigours of the campaign." (Ch. 1). part_of: [[hubs/warfare/long-19th-century/napoleonic-wars/russian-campaign-1812]]; contributed_to: [[napoleonic-wars]].

- Most major campaigns were decided by one or more decisive battles. "Marengo, Austerlitz, Jena-Auerstädt, Friedland, Wagram, Leipzig and Waterloo all transformed the strategic balance and led to diplomatic negotiations... an initial victory such as Marengo, Ulm, Eckmühl, or even Jena-Auerstädt would give one side an advantage, but did not deprive the other of all hope of victory." (Ch. 1). produced: [[napoleonic-wars]] outcomes; part_of: [[hubs/warfare/long-19th-century/napoleonic-wars/napoleonic-wars]].

- Leipzig was by far the largest battle of the period. "Leipzig was the largest battle of the Napoleonic Wars with more than half a million combatants and fighting on four successive days (16–19 October 1813), although the heaviest fighting was limited to two days (16 and 18 October)." (Ch. 2). part_of: [[hubs/warfare/long-19th-century/napoleonic-wars/battle-of-leipzig]]; contributed_to: [[napoleonic-warfare]].

- Typical Napoleonic field armies were 50,000–100,000 per side; Napoleon's greatest victories used fewer than 100,000 on the field. "A number of Napoleon's battles saw one or both armies bring 100,000 men or more into action, but a figure of between 50,000 and 100,000 on each side was more common. Significantly, all Napoleon's greatest victories – Marengo, Austerlitz, Jena and Friedland – were won with armies of fewer than 100,000 men on the field of battle." (Ch. 2). part_of: [[napoleonic-tactics]]; contributed_to: [[napoleon]] command style.

- Most battles were fought and decided in a single day. "The great majority of battles were completed in a single day of fighting, sometimes just an afternoon... Only very rarely did full-scale fighting extend over more than one day – as at Wagram and Leipzig." (Ch. 2). part_of: [[napoleonic-warfare]]; contrasts_with: [[hubs/warfare/long-19th-century/napoleonic-wars/battle-of-leipzig]].

- Weather strongly shaped battles; fine conditions favoured the most celebrated actions. "The finest battles of the period were all fought in good weather and ‘the sun of Austerlitz’ became part of the Napoleonic legend." Rain or snow produced attrition or disasters (Albuera lancers, Eylau Augereau). (Ch. 2). contributed_to: [[hubs/warfare/long-19th-century/napoleonic-wars/battle-of-austerlitz]]; part_of: [[combined-arms]].

- Battle forms varied widely, including pitched, encounter, and spontaneous actions. "Auerstädt... the most obvious example [encounter]. More common were battles where one general attacked what he believed was a detached body... as at Friedland, Lützen and Dresden. Then there was Salamanca, where... Wellington saw that the French were over-extending their forces, and... suddenly launched his army into an attack." (Ch. 2). part_of: [[hubs/warfare/long-19th-century/napoleonic-wars/battle-of-jena-auerstedt]]; contributed_to: [[napoleonic-tactics]].

- Infantry formed the backbone; cavalry and artillery were auxiliaries with specialized roles. "Most fighting in almost every battle was between the infantry of the two armies... Light cavalry could also be used for a shock role in battle, but this was best performed by medium and heavy cavalry... whose primary function was to break the enemy line once it had been shaken... Artillery by contrast was best used in defence where it could fire steadily at an advancing enemy... The way these three arms combined and interacted on the battlefield constituted the elements of combat." (Ch. 2). part_of: [[infantry-tactics-napoleonic]]; part_of: [[combined-arms]]; analogous_to: [[shock-vs-fire]].

- Open, gentle country predominated, but hills conferred major defensive advantages, especially with reverse-slope tactics. "This was the key to Wellington's defensive tactics, but he added to the advantage by not deploying his troops on the actual crest of the hill... but slightly on the reverse slope where they were safer and where their counter-attack would benefit from an element of surprise." (Ch. 2). enabled: [[wellington-arthur-wellesley]]; part_of: [[hubs/warfare/long-19th-century/napoleonic-wars/battle-of-waterloo]]; contributed_to: [[napoleonic-tactics]].

- Villages became focal points of heavy, often savage house-to-house fighting. "A village was a tangible, recognizable prize... The fighting in the streets and houses was normally the preserve of infantry... General Langeron... describes how at Leipzig he captured the village of Schönefeld for the second time" followed by repeated attacks, reserves, burning, and chaos. (Ch. 2). part_of: [[hubs/warfare/long-19th-century/napoleonic-wars/battle-of-leipzig]]; contributed_to: [[napoleonic-warfare]].

- Isolated farmhouses and châteaux could decisively strengthen or threaten a position (e.g., Waterloo). "Wellington's line at Waterloo was immensely strengthened by his two outposts... Indeed probably Napoleon's greatest tactical mistake at Waterloo was to permit so much of Reille's corps to be committed to the struggle for Hougoumont... the fall of La Haye Sainte posed a serious threat." (Ch. 2). part_of: [[hubs/warfare/long-19th-century/napoleonic-wars/battle-of-waterloo]]; contributed_to: [[combined-arms]].

- Field fortifications such as the Raevsky Redoubt at Borodino were heavily contested, sometimes with costly cavalry assaults. "the first French assault which reached the redoubt was driven back... This was followed by a prolonged artillery bombardment before the French mounted a second attack, this time, most unusually, led by two corps of heavy cavalry... the cramped interior space... was [soon] filled with a frightful press of murderously-intentioned cavalry and Russian infantry." (Ch. 2). part_of: [[hubs/warfare/long-19th-century/napoleonic-wars/russian-campaign-1812]]; contributed_to: [[napoleonic-tactics]].

- Battlefield smoke and noise varied but could severely impair visibility, command, and cohesion. "‘the smoke hung so thick about, that, although not more than eighty yards asunder, we could only distinguish each other by the flashes of the pieces’... at Busaco, ‘Orders … could only be communicated by sound of bugle, or by the stentorian voice of a company officer.’" (Ch. 2, Waterloo and other examples). contributed_to: [[morale-and-cohesion-napoleonic]]; part_of: [[hubs/warfare/long-19th-century/napoleonic-wars/battle-of-waterloo]].

- The battlefield was a place of extreme sensory horror that tested but did not always destroy unit cohesion. "As well as the smoke and noise of weapons, there were the screams of wounded men and horses... riderless and sometimes horribly injured horses galloping about... the smell of gunpowder, blood, vomit and human excreta. All this acting on men raised to a high pitch of excitement... was enough to overwhelm some soldiers... but most remained, consoled by the reassuring presence of their comrades." (Ch. 2). contributed_to: [[morale-and-cohesion-napoleonic]]; part_of: [[face-of-battle-method]].

**Range summary: main contributions of this chunk to understanding Napoleonic tactics and battle experience.** This introductory section establishes the source's core method (first-hand accounts emphasizing experience, morale, and arm interaction over drill manuals or grand narratives), documents the rarity of actual battle amid campaign hardships and disease, quantifies variable casualty rates and the decisive role of set-piece battles in campaigns, and surveys the physical and human geography of battlefields (terrain, villages, farms, fortifications, smoke/noise) with concrete examples from Austerlitz, Salamanca, Waterloo, Leipzig, Borodino, Auerstädt, and others, laying essential groundwork for later analysis of combined-arms tactics, command, and cohesion.

### Range 2 Extraction (lines 1411-3270)

- Artillery formed the smallest arm in Napoleonic armies with highly variable proportions across forces. "Wellington's armies in the Peninsula suffered from a shortage of artillery: at Salamanca he had only fifty-four guns ... a ratio of just over one gun per 1,000 men. This compared to the French at Wagram and Waterloo who had a ratio of about 3.3 guns per 1,000" (Ch. 3 / artillery proportions). part_of: [[napoleonic-tactics]] contributed_to: [[hubs/warfare/long-19th-century/napoleonic-wars/napoleonic-wars]]

- Horse artillery was distinguished by greater mobility and elite panache while foot artillery provided the bulk of firepower. "its uniforms were modelled on those of hussars and other light cavalry, while the gunners ‘rapidly developed something of the panache and élitism of the cavalry, and would regard their companions of the foot artillery as boring stick-in-the-muds’" (Ch. 3 / horse vs foot artillery). analogous_to: [[combined-arms]] part_of: [[napoleonic-tactics]]

- Field artillery batteries were typically organized as the smallest tactical units of six or eight guns (mostly cannon with one or two howitzers). "All artillery, horse and foot, was organized into batteries (called troops, companies or brigades), most commonly of six or eight guns" (Ch. 3 / battery organization). part_of: [[napoleonic-tactics]] contributed_to: [[hubs/warfare/tactics-and-battle-strategies]]

- Battery commanders in Wellington's army exercised substantial tactical independence and often resisted interference from divisional or senior officers. "‘I should use very plain words to any Commanding Officer of Artillery who would venture to interrupt or hinder the arrangements I had concerted with the Commanding Officer of my Artillery’" (Ch. 3 / Cairnes on Dickson at Vitoria). enabled: [[napoleonic-tactics]] contrasts_with: [[napoleonic-warfare]]

- Grand batteries became more common from 1809 onward as armies concentrated reserve artillery (including Guard guns) to bombard a chosen sector of the enemy line. "the corps, reserve and Guard guns could be collected into a grand battery which would bombard a selected part of the enemy line in preparation for the decisive attack" (Ch. 3 / grand batteries). part_of: [[napoleonic-tactics]] contributed_to: [[combined-arms]]

- Gunners worked in harsh conditions with a realistic sustained rate of fire far below theoretical maxima; 20–30 rounds per hour was typical, with Napoleon considering 150 rounds per gun ample for a major battle. "Between twenty and thirty rounds per hour was probably the norm for sustained fire, with the tempo increasing before an attack was launched" (Ch. 3 / gunners' conditions and fire rate). part_of: [[morale-and-cohesion-napoleonic]] contributed_to: [[sources/muir-tactics-and-the-experience-of-battle-in-the-age-of-napoleon-1998]]

- Artillery provided mutual close support with infantry; friendly guns gave soldiers confidence even when their material effect was limited. "soldiers like the noise of Artillery; it gives them confidence when employed in their support, with however little effect" (Ch. 3 / Dickson on mountain guns and morale). enabled: [[combined-arms]] part_of: [[morale-and-cohesion-napoleonic]]

- Napoleon abolished regimental guns in the French army during the Peace of Amiens; attempts to restore them later had limited impact. "Napoleon abolished them in the French army during the Peace of Amiens (1802–3); he attempted to restore them in 1809–10 in response to the declining quality of French infantry, but few if any saw service in Spain" (Ch. 3 / regimental guns). preceded_by: [[infantry-tactics-napoleonic]] part_of: [[napoleonic-tactics]]

- At Salamanca, Dyneley's battery occupied the Lesser Arapile, bombarded French positions on the Greater Arapile at 700–800 yards, then used canister at 300 yards to check a French counter-attack. "his task was to bombard French troops on the Greater Arapile 700 or 800 yards away ... Dyneley's guns redoubled their efforts, opening with canister at 300 yards which effectively checked the French advance" (Ch. 3 / Dyneley at Salamanca). contributed_to: [[napoleonic-tactics]] part_of: [[hubs/warfare/long-19th-century/napoleonic-wars/napoleonic-wars]]

- In the rearguard action at the River Huebra (17 Nov. 1812), Swabey and Dyneley's horse artillery endured hours of French fire from a height while under strict orders from Wellington to reserve fire for formed bodies only. "We remained four hours calmly receiving the enemy's fire ... Lord Wellington gave no orders but to reserve our fire for formed bodies, none of which appeared" (Ch. 3 / Swabey and Dyneley at Huebra). contributed_to: [[morale-and-cohesion-napoleonic]] part_of: [[wellington-arthur-wellesley]]

- Senarmont at Friedland advanced his guns aggressively to very short ranges (traditional account: 450, 250, then 150 yards) after collecting ~30 guns, repulsing Russian cavalry and breaking the left wing, though actual ranges and details are likely exaggerated. "Again the French guns advanced until they were only 150 yards from the Russian line; the Russian cavalry attempted to overrun them but were repulsed with terrible losses" (Ch. 3 / Senarmont at Friedland). part_of: [[napoleonic-tactics]] contributed_to: [[napoleon]]

- Artillery doctrine (Adye) strongly discouraged counter-battery fire in favor of targeting enemy troops, though the convention broke down in practice. "‘Artillery should never fire against artillery’, although he went on to add the qualification, ‘unless the enemy's troops are covered, and his artillery exposed’" (Ch. 3 / counter-battery fire). contrasts_with: [[combined-arms]] part_of: [[napoleonic-tactics]]

- Despite massive ammunition expenditure, artillery inflicted far fewer casualties than theoretical estimates suggested because much fire occurred at long range or against covered troops. "In the two days of fighting at Wagram the 617 guns in Napoleon's army fired a total of 96,000 rounds: considerably more than double the highest estimate of Austrian casualties" (Ch. 3 / effectiveness and Wagram). contributed_to: [[morale-and-cohesion-napoleonic]] part_of: [[sources/muir-tactics-and-the-experience-of-battle-in-the-age-of-napoleon-1998]]

- Effective battlefield range for field artillery was limited; even twelve-pounders were not very effective beyond ~1,000 yards, with canister generally reserved for under 450–500 yards. "the maximum effective range in battle conditions of even the heaviest field pieces (twelve-pounders) was not much more than 1,000 yards" (Ch. 3 / ranges and ammunition). part_of: [[napoleonic-tactics]] contrasts_with: [[shock-vs-fire]]

- Artillery tended to withdraw rather than stand at close quarters, reflected in consistently low gunner casualty rates compared to the army as a whole (e.g., ~13.5% for listed British batteries at Waterloo vs. 30% overall). "it does seem reasonable to conclude that artillery in general was inclined to withdraw rather than let the enemy come to close quarters" (Ch. 3 / gunner casualties and withdrawal tendency). contrasts_with: [[infantry-tactics-napoleonic]] part_of: [[morale-and-cohesion-napoleonic]]

- Artillery's primary battlefield contribution was often psychological: wearing down morale and cohesion through prolonged cannonading even when physical damage was limited. "‘Several of our officers remarked, & I think it just, that cannon make more noise and alarm than they do mischief’" (Ch. 3 / Hennell at Vitoria). part_of: [[morale-and-cohesion-napoleonic]] contributed_to: [[face-of-battle-method]]

- Soldiers across armies found passive exposure to artillery fire among the most unpleasant experiences; there was nothing to occupy the mind and no means of reply. "‘The standing to be cannonaded, and having nothing else to do, is about the most unpleasant thing that can happen to soldiers in an engagement’ (Ensign Leeke on Waterloo)" (Ch. 3 / psychological effects of cannonading). contributed_to: [[morale-and-cohesion-napoleonic]] part_of: [[hubs/warfare/long-19th-century/napoleonic-wars/battle-of-waterloo]]

- "Bobbing" or ducking under cannon fire was near-universal; officers' attempts to suppress it often backfired dramatically, as in Landmann's Vimeiro anecdote where a reprimanding officer himself bobbed after a near-miss. "‘Who is that I see bobbing there? What are you bobbing about, sir? Let me see you bob again, sir, and I'll—’ ... so down went my officer a yard at least" (Ch. 3 / bobbing and Landmann at Vimeiro). part_of: [[morale-and-cohesion-napoleonic]] contributed_to: [[sources/muir-tactics-and-the-experience-of-battle-in-the-age-of-napoleon-1998]]

- Skirmishers in loose order suffered least from artillery while cavalry presented large targets; columns and squares were more vulnerable at close range than lines because men had less chance to dodge and awareness of hits spread more readily. ## Volume Synthesis Note

Muir's *Tactics and the Experience of Battle in the Age of Napoleon* supplies the missing human and mechanical layer to the operational narrative provided by Chandler's *The Campaigns of Napoleon* and similar works. While operational studies reconstruct marches, concentrations, and high-level decisions, Muir reconstructs what happened once the armies closed: how artillery and light troops wore down enemy cohesion before the decisive moment; the practical trade-offs of line versus column and the primacy of the first volley and controlled fire; the reality that cavalry rarely crashed home against steady troops and that true bayonet melees were exceptional; the overwhelming importance of morale, group loyalty, colours, and regimental leadership in determining whether units held or broke; and the grim, unheroic aftermath of plundering, suffering wounded, and disease.

The book is grounded in an exceptionally rich (though acknowledged Anglo-French biased) body of first-person testimony. It demonstrates that at the level of the soldier and small unit, national differences were less significant than training, recent experience, and the quality of immediate leadership. It repeatedly shows friction, psychological shock, and the difficulty of maintaining order and control under fire — themes that enrich the existing Napoleonic battle analyses in the warfare hub (Austerlitz, Jena-Auerstedt, Salamanca, Leipzig, Waterloo, Russian Campaign) with concrete primary quotes and analysis of why certain attacks succeeded or failed.

In the wiki, this source directly strengthens [[napoleonic-tactics]], [[infantry-tactics-napoleonic]], [[morale-and-cohesion-napoleonic]], the combined-arms and shock-vs-fire discussions, and the high-resolution hub pages. It complements rather than duplicates the operational bible (Chandler) and the broader "face of battle" tradition (Keegan). It is the key reference for the lived experience of Napoleonic combat and should be cited on any page treating small-unit mechanics or soldier psychology in the period 1792–1815.
