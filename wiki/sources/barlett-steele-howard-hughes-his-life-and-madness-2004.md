# Howard Hughes: His Life and Madness (Barlett & Steele, 2004)

Originally published 1979 as *Empire: The Life, Legend, and Madness of Howard Hughes*. Norton paperback reissue 2004.

## Overview / Thesis

The definitive investigative biography of Howard Robard Hughes Jr. (1905–1976), the reclusive American aviator, film producer, industrialist, and billionaire whose life embodied both the glamour and the pathology of 20th-century American wealth and power. Barlett and Steele systematically dismantle the myth of Hughes as a Midas-touch genius and corporate superman, showing instead a man driven by an overpowering urge to become a legend in his own lifetime, whose actual record was one of repeated technical brilliance undermined by perfectionism, secrecy, mismanagement, and eventual physical and mental collapse.

Core thesis: Hughes always sought to make the world think he was making money and doing the impossible; image mattered more than results. He succeeded wildly at projecting the image (record flights, Hell's Angels, the Flying Boat, Las Vegas empire) while presiding over tens of millions in losses over decades, empire decay, and personal self-destruction. The book traces the arc from early inheritance of the Hughes Tool fortune, through Hollywood and heroic aviation (1930s), wartime contracts and Senate scrutiny, the RKO takeover and political entanglements, the Mormon inner circle, the Las Vegas acquisition and "Lord of the Desert" phase, the bizarre exile and decline, Watergate/Hughesgate connections, the CIA's Glomar Explorer project, to the sordid fight over his estate after death in Acapulco (1976).

The authors emphasize that Hughes was remarkable in memory, technical reading ability, and ambition, but never learned to convert knowledge into sustainable application; he sought a perfection that assured failure. Secrecy was both weapon and prison.

## Methodological Notes

- Four years of research by Donald L. Barlett and James B. Steele, building on their 1975 Philadelphia Inquirer series ("The Hughes Empire") that ran ~20,000 words.
- Primary materials: quarter-million+ pages of public and corporate records (court filings, SEC reports, congressional hearings/transcripts, state corporate records, IRS, aviation and film archives, Hughes internal memos and logs); no central Hughes archive existed.
- Post-1976 interviews with former aides, executives, pilots, Mormon handlers, attorneys, and associates once loyalty constraints lifted.
- Reconstruction of financial flows, political contributions (e.g., to Nixon/Rebozo, Democratic figures), CIA ties, and daily operations from indirect but voluminous paper trail.
- Emphasis on verifiable documents over anecdote; repeated cross-checking of legend against records.
- Authors acknowledge errors possible but stress the scale of the documentary foundation.

## Key Contributions

- The single most comprehensive document-based corrective to the Hughes legend and PR machine.
- Detailed anatomy of the decline: how secrecy, the Mormon "palace guard," mismanagement of TWA and other assets, and personal obsessions (codeine, Kleenex boxes, fear of germs) led to loss of control.
- Exposure of Hughes' political connections across parties (contributions, influence in Nevada, Watergate-era ties via intermediaries) and the CIA's use of Hughes companies for Project Jennifer (Glomar Explorer recovery of Soviet sub).
- Full treatment of the Las Vegas "empire" phase: the $178k/day spending, PR blitz, master plan, and ultimate loss of the casinos/hotels.
- The post-death will fight, the "Mormon Will," estate battles, and questions around the circumstances of death (malnutrition, dehydration, possible codeine overdose or neglect).
- Demonstration that Hughes repeatedly lost money in core businesses for 30+ years yet maintained a "ten greatest businessmen" reputation through image control.
- Rich primary texture from logs, memoranda, testimony, and aide recollections.

- **Death of Howard Hughes in Acapulco (April 5, 1976)**: In the final days of his life, [[actors/hughes-howard|Howard Hughes]] lay emaciated and delirious in a penthouse at the Acapulco Princess Hotel, refusing substantial food for weeks, repeatedly requesting water he barely drank, and self-administering codeine via syringe while aides monitored his decline. “Pour me some water,” Hughes said; later aides noted he “had not eaten any substantial food in nearly two weeks”; a local doctor found him “a long skeleton clad in wrinkled, wasted flesh… less than a hundred pounds” with a “tumor gaped open on the left side of his scalp,” bedsores, needle tracks, and “enough codeine… to kill an ordinary man.” (Prologue; [[actors/hughes-howard|Howard Hughes]])
- **Aides’ management of Hughes’ final decline (early April 1976)**: Hughes’ aides (Chuck Waldron, George Francom, Jim Rickard, John Holmes) handled constant water requests, food trays, and medical logistics while Los Angeles executives delayed decisions on hospitalization; they observed him mumbling about an “insurance policy,” twitching, and slipping into coma. “Hughes had stopped eating… It was not the first time he had refused to eat. They had seen him go without food for days at a time.” Dr. Montemayor later told aides “This man should be in a good hospital… Take him back to the States.” (Prologue; [[actors/hughes-howard|Howard Hughes]])
- **Final medical interventions and transport decision (April 5, 1976)**: After bloodwork showed “exceptionally high urea level—104.5 milligrams” indicating critical dehydration, aides summoned Dr. Wilbur Thain; Thain arrived and spent time shredding documents before examining Hughes and giving injections. “It was after 7 A.M. that Monday morning before Dr. Thain finally arrived… [he] began rummaging through the filing cabinets, pulling out papers… feeding them into a shredder.” The decision was made to fly Hughes to Houston. (Prologue; [[actors/hughes-howard|Howard Hughes]])
- **Allene Gano Hughes’ obsessive care of young Howard (1905–1922)**: Allene smothered her only child with health monitoring, nightly mineral oil, frequent doctor visits for feet/teeth/digestion, and removals from Houston during disease outbreaks; relatives noted Howard “idolized” her and behaved with unusual formality. “His mother… made it a full-time job… If she detected any abnormality… she whisked him off to a doctor… ‘Howard and his mother were very close.’” (Ch. 1 Mother; [[actors/hughes-howard|Howard Hughes]])
- **Mother’s influence on lifelong health phobias and attention-seeking (Ch. 1)**: Allene’s anxiety over Howard’s “supersensitiveness,” nervousness, and physical condition instilled in him the belief that illness was a tool to gain attention or escape situations; she wrote camp officials detailed instructions about his feet, bowels, digestion, and emotional fragility. “Howard also learned from her that the best way to attract attention or to escape unpleasant situations was to complain of illness.” (Ch. 1 Mother/Tragedy; [[actors/hughes-howard|Howard Hughes]])
- **Invention and commercialization of the Hughes rock bit (1908–1909)**: Hughes Sr. and partner Walter Sharp developed the “rock eater” bit after buying a crude wooden-spool model from Granville Humason for $150; Hughes Sr. sketched the design on a breadboard, patented it, and tested it successfully at Goose Creek. “Hughes pulled his partner aside… Sharp handed over $1,500… On August 10, 1909, the Patent Office granted Hughes patents 930,758 and 930,759.” The bit revolutionized drilling and founded the family fortune. (Ch. 1 The Rock Eater; [[actors/hughes-howard|Howard Hughes]])
- **Death of Allene Hughes (March 29–30, 1922)**: Allene entered the hospital for a curettement and never regained consciousness; the sudden loss at age 39 devastated Hughes Sr. and left 16-year-old Howard to be told the truth by his uncle Rupert in Los Angeles. “Allene… had died suddenly… Young Howard, then just sixteen, arrived in great anxiety and suspense. I steeled myself, told young Howard the truth…” (Ch. 1 Tragedy; [[actors/hughes-howard|Howard Hughes]])
- **Death of Howard Hughes Sr. and immediate aftermath (January 14, 1924)**: Hughes Sr. suffered a fatal heart attack at age 54 during a business meeting; 18-year-old Howard received the “horrible blow” while the family buried him next to Allene. Hughes Sr.’s death triggered Howard’s obsession with his own health and early death. “At the time of his father’s death, Howard did not have the slightest notion of what to do in life.” (Ch. 1 Tragedy; [[actors/hughes-howard|Howard Hughes]])
- **Power play to gain sole control of Hughes Tool Company (1924)**: Less than a month after his father’s death, Howard dropped out of Rice, bought out his grandparents’ and uncle Felix’s 25% interest for $325,000 (using company reserves), then petitioned the court at age 19 to remove his “disabilities of minority.” “The thing I knew was that I would never be able to get along with my relations… I was determined to buy them out and go it alone.” Judge Montieth granted full adulthood on December 26, 1924. (Ch. 1 Power Play; [[actors/hughes-howard|Howard Hughes]])
- **Early Hollywood ventures and Hell’s Angels (1925–1930)**: After modest successes, Hughes conceived an epic WWI aviation film; he assembled the world’s largest private air force (87 planes), fired directors, and took over direction himself. Production cost $3.8 million for a 15,000-foot movie; he used extravagant promotion. “It had become the movie colony’s favorite joke… The cost was no joke—$3.8 million.” Hughes later admitted: “Making Hell’s Angels by myself was my biggest mistake.” (Ch. 2 Hollywood / Hell’s Angels; [[actors/hughes-howard|Howard Hughes]])
- **Jean Harlow casting and launch (late 1929)**: After Greta Nissen proved unsuitable for sound, Hughes tested many; Jean Harlow’s screen test was initially poor but agent convinced him. Hughes paid her $1,500 for six weeks. Her line “Do you mind if I slip into something more comfortable?” helped make the film a hit and launched her as a sex symbol. (Ch. 2 Hell’s Angels; [[actors/hughes-howard|Howard Hughes]])
- **H-1 Racer design and 1935 land-speed record (September 13, 1935)**: Hughes hired Richard Palmer and Glenn Odekirk to build the fastest land plane with innovations including flush rivets and fully retractable landing gear. He flew four passes averaging 352 mph, breaking the French record. “It’ll go faster,” he muttered inspecting the damage. “I only flew a perfect machine.” (Ch. 3 Record Breaker; [[actors/hughes-howard|Howard Hughes]])
- **1938 around-the-world flight execution (July 10–14, 1938)**: With crew Edward Lund, Richard Stoddart, Lt. Thomas Thurlow, and Harry Connor, Hughes flew the heavily loaded Lockheed from New York via Paris, Moscow, Omsk, Yakutsk, Fairbanks, and Minneapolis in 3 days 19 hours 17 minutes, beating Lindbergh’s time in half. “I hope we get to Paris before we run out of gas…” (Ch. 3 Race Around the World; [[actors/hughes-howard|Howard Hughes]])
- **Public reaction and Houston homecoming (1938)**: Record flights brought ticker-tape parades and hero status, yet Hughes remained shy. Houston renamed its airport and turned out 250,000 people; this was his last visit to his hometown. (Ch. 3; [[actors/hughes-howard|Howard Hughes]])

## Range 07 Claims

- **Mell Stewart enema standby crisis (Feb–March 1975)**: Hughes kept aide Mell Stewart waiting weeks in Freeport for an enema he kept postponing, first demanding immediate return then offering a $500-per-day premium; Eckersley wrote: “I am very much concerned with the large amount of money that is accruing each day to Mell Stewart” and warned of a “revolution”. (Mell Stewart problem; [[actors/hughes-howard|Howard Hughes]])
- **CIA media briefing and Jack Anderson leak (March 18, 1975)**: At the height of the Mell Stewart crisis, CIA Director Colby briefed media on Project Jennifer; Anderson concluded on air it was “a cover-up of a $350 million failure”. (COVER STORY; [[actors/hughes-howard|Howard Hughes]])
- **Hughes himself deceived by the cover story**: Hughes and his penthouse staff believed the ship bearing his name would launch a trillion-dollar ocean-mining industry; “We thought we were going to get rich from minerals”. (Project Jennifer section; [[actors/hughes-howard|Howard Hughes]])
- **Hughes Aircraft vs. Summa empire contrast (1975)**: The autonomous Hughes Aircraft Company had paid off debts, rolled up profits, reached over $1 billion in annual sales; Summa posted $79.6 million losses 1971–1974 (over $100 million by end of 1975) plus disappearance of half a billion dollars in cash and securities since 1966 at $137,000 a day. (THE LEGACY; [[places/las-vegas|Las Vegas]])
- **Hughes’s physical and mental state in last year**: Hughes weighed slightly more than 100 pounds (later 93 at death), had shrunk nearly three inches, had a quarter-sized malignant tumor on his scalp, rotting teeth, needle marks carpeting arms and thighs, chronic peptic ulcer, kidneys two-thirds normal size; he had bowel movements only once or twice a month after enemas and often defecated on his bed. (THE LAST YEAR; [[actors/hughes-howard|Howard Hughes]])
- **Desert Inn decay and futile planning (1974–1975)**: 90 rooms were condemned by the Department of Health by end of October 1974; repeated memos while Hughes changed plans from 65-story high-rise to cheapest short-term fix, but “there would be no construction at the Desert Inn until after Hughes’s death.” (THE LAST YEAR; [[places/las-vegas|Las Vegas]])
- **Four-physician medical team and unorthodox practice**: Hughes had an internist, general practitioner (Thain, Bill Gay’s brother-in-law), pathologist, and surgeon drawing combined salaries/expenses >$250,000/year; none gave complete physicals; records were not shared. (THE CANDYMAN; [[actors/hughes-howard|Howard Hughes]])
- **“Candyman” drug procurement system**: For two decades Crane and then Thain supplied tens of thousands of codeine, Empirin, and Valium tablets; prescriptions written in aides’ names; Thain switched to direct shipments “to reduce the risk of detection”. (THE CANDYMAN; [[actors/hughes-howard|Howard Hughes]])
- **Final move and death (Feb–April 1976)**: Hughes was persuaded to move from Freeport to Acapulco Princess on Feb 10, 1976, by the false claim codeine was unavailable in the Bahamas; he stopped eating and drinking, became a “ninety-three-pound skeleton,” and died aboard the flight to Houston on April 5, 1976. (WHERE IS THE WILL? / CHAPTER 25; [[actors/hughes-howard|Howard Hughes]])
- **Autopsy findings and deliberate cover-up (April 5–6, 1976)**: Preliminary findings announced as death from “chronic renal disease”; details withheld included bed sores, open tumor sore on scalp, X-ray fragments of hypodermic needles broken off in both arms, extraordinarily high codeine level; burial private in Glenwood Cemetery less than 48 hours later. (COVER-UP; [[actors/hughes-howard|Howard Hughes]])
- **Mormon will discovery and text (April 27, 1976)**: A sealed envelope arrived at LDS headquarters; the three-page yellow legal-pad will (dated “19 day of March 1968”) contained 16+ misspellings and left shares to the Medical Institute, universities, LDS Church, “Melvin DuMar of Gabbs, Nevada,” Jean Peters, Ella Rice, etc. (THE MORMON WILL; [[actors/hughes-howard|Howard Hughes]])
- **Melvin Dummar’s desert pickup story**: Dummar claimed he picked up a tall, skinny, scarred, bleeding man lying in the road near midnight on Route 95 between Tonopah and Beatty in January 1968, drove him to the Sands in Las Vegas, the man identified as Hughes, and Dummar gave him a quarter. (THE MORMON WILL; [[places/las-vegas|Las Vegas]])
- **Mormon will declared forgery by experts**: Leading handwriting analysts found it an imitation; errors in spelling, grammar... were “completely at odds with Hughes’s precise nature”; use of hated term “spruce goose” and misspelling of cousin’s name were decisive. (THE MORMON WILL; [[actors/hughes-howard|Howard Hughes]])
- **Lummis takeover and Summa financial disaster (1976–1977)**: After appointment as administrator, Lummis discovered Summa sustained >$131 million net operating losses 1971 through Sept 1976; liquid assets fell from ~$715 million to $94 million by March 1977. (FALLOUT; [[places/las-vegas|Las Vegas]])
- **Death-by-negligence questions (1976–1978)**: Autopsy codeine blood level 1.96 mcg/ml (above 1.4 lethal threshold); expert concluded Hughes took 5–9 grains (possibly far more) within 6–8 hours of death; he had been unconscious ~24 hours; neither state nor federal authorities investigated negligence or neglect. (DEATH BY NEGLIGENCE?; [[actors/hughes-howard|Howard Hughes]])
- **Epilogue core thesis on image vs. reality**: “It was money that etched Howard Hughes into the public mind... That he lacked this gift is now evident. If Hughes had invested the profits of the tool company... in a passbook savings account, he would have died a much richer man”; Hughes sought to become “a legend in his own lifetime” through glamorous fields and PR mastery so that “history is made by reputation, not reality”. (EPILOGUE A SUMMING UP; [[actors/hughes-howard|Howard Hughes]])
- **CIA exploitation of Hughes’s condition as “best-kept secret”**: “Not only had Hughes surrendered control of his empire to others, but the CIA—aware of his narcotics addiction and mental state—took advantage of the situation by making use of his organization, with the cooperation of a few friendly executives, to serve its own needs”. (Project Jennifer section; [[actors/hughes-howard|Howard Hughes]])
