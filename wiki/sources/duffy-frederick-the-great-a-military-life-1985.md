# Frederick the Great: A Military Life (Christopher Duffy, 1985)

**Source page for Christopher Duffy's military biography of Frederick the Great.** This is a detailed narrative of Frederick II's career as soldier and war leader, from his troubled youth and accession, through the conquest and defense of Silesia, the desperate campaigns of the Seven Years War, to his final years and the construction of the "Old Fritz" legend. Duffy emphasizes Frederick's personal direction of strategy and operations, the strengths and limitations of the Prussian military system he inherited and refined, and the gap between the king's Enlightenment ideals and the bloody reality of his wars.

**Duffy-FG-R1-PrimarySourcesPCOeuvresBattlefields**

**Grounding quote:** "In the matter of source material, historians suffered an undeniable loss when the Prussian military archives were destroyed in 1945. By then, however, the publication of Frederick's Politische Correspondenz had been completed, and this material, together with the king's printed works, makes up no less than seventy substantial volumes. No other monarch has ever written at such length about his doings, or (with the possible exception of Louis XIV) has been observed so closely over such a long period of time. ... Another form of evidence, which has survived mostly intact, is the physical setting of Frederick's battles and campaigns. With a certain amount of persistence it is possible to tour the scenes of all of the more important field headquarters and encampments, and every battlefield except the two (Mollwitz and Chotusitz) which lie under aircraft runways."

**Locator:** "Preface"

**Analysis:** Duffy presents the *Politische Correspondenz* (PC, 46 vols) and *Oeuvres* (30 vols) as the core post-1945 source base that preserves the detail of Frederick's actions and self-justifications. Battlefield topography supplies independent tactical and strategic texture that printed accounts alone cannot provide. This combination allows a narrative military biography that establishes continuities and contradictions without relying on destroyed archives or armchair speculation.

**Duffy-FG-R1-GreatElectorCreatesStandingArmy**

**Grounding quote:** "On this reckoning the first 'Prussian' was certainly Frederick William, 'The Great Elector', who ruled Brandenburg-Prussia from 1640 to 1688. He crushed the local feudal assemblies, and won the freedom to set up a standing army of 30,000 troops. Independent military force was now to form the base of Hohenzollern power, and not the shifts of alliance which had served Brandenburg so badly in the Thirty Years War. ... The new regular army won its spurs in campaigns against the Swedes, and episodes like the Great Elector's victory at Fehrbellin (1675) became treasured memories in the developing military consciousness of the Brandenburgers."

**Locator:** "Ch. 1 Origins"

**Analysis:** Duffy identifies the Great Elector as the true founder who replaced unreliable alliances with a permanent standing force of 30,000 as the foundation of Hohenzollern power. Fehrbellin entered the collective memory as a formative victory. Dutch models and later Huguenot French influences shaped the early force that later rulers would expand.

**Duffy-FG-R1-FrederickWilliamIArmyReforms**

**Grounding quote:** "In point of size, the Prussian army grew in Frederick William's reign to no less than 83,000 men, which was a remarkably high figure for a population base of about 2,250,000 souls. ... The Old Dessauer effectively invented the practice of troops marching in step (the 'cadenced step'). ... 1718 saw the introduction of the celebrated Prussian iron ramrod, a device which could be wielded with speed and force, permitting muskets to be loaded much more quickly than with the wooden ramrods of the other services of Europe. In the same year Frederick William accomplished what Hans Bleckwenn has termed the Stilbruch in the Prussian officers' uniforms, a deliberate turning-aside from the richly embroidered fashions of western Europe, and the imposition of sober coats of dark indigo blue. Frederick the Great was to retain this weaponry and clothing, and by implication to uphold the values enshrined in them."

**Locator:** "Ch. 1 Origins"

**Analysis:** Under Frederick William I the army reached 83,000 through foreign recruitment and tight General-Directorium administration. Cadenced step, the iron ramrod, and the indigo-blue Stilbruch created a distinctive, austere, Pietist-influenced force optimized for parade-ground precision and battlefield compactness. Duffy stresses that Frederick retained these exact material and symbolic elements.

**Duffy-FG-R1-TutorsImbueSwordAsGlory**

**Grounding quote:** "His French style was formed by his teacher and true friend, the Huguenot Jacques Duhan de Jandun. His corresponding military development lay in the hands of two soldiers from East Prussia: Colonel Christoph Wilhelm von Kalckstein, and the widely travelled and fine-mannered Lieutenant-General Count Albrecht Konrad von Finckenstein. These officers were told to imbue Frederick with the conviction 'that nothing in the world can endow a prince with more honour and glory than the sword' (Koser, 1921, I, 8)."

**Locator:** "Ch. 1 Origins"

**Analysis:** Duffy shows Frederick's education deliberately balanced French literary culture (Duhan) with martial values instilled by Kalckstein and Finckenstein. The explicit doctrine that the sword alone confers supreme honour and glory on a prince shaped the crown prince's priorities. This early formation explains the continuity between the Rheinsberg philosopher-prince and the later aggressor.

**Duffy-FG-R1-RheinsbergAntimachiavelWarJustifications**

**Grounding quote:** "Frederick's tract took its name from his desire to take Machiavelli to task for maintaining that a prince must adopt different standards for his public and private conduct. ... In the interest of his subjects, a prince might be justified in going to war in any one of three main eventualities - to fight off an actual invasion, to maintain his legitimate rights, or (most illuminating of all) to anticipate a threatening danger. In the event, Frederick invoked the second argument when he went to war in December 1740, and the third when he attacked Saxony in 1756."

**Locator:** "Ch. 1 Origins"

**Analysis:** At Rheinsberg Frederick completed the *Antimachiavel*, which asserted the unity of public and private morality while still enumerating three legitimate causes for war. Duffy notes the strong continuity: the 1740 Silesian invasion was framed as defence of legitimate rights, while the 1756 Saxon attack used the "anticipate a threatening danger" rationale. The tract did not prevent aggressive action; it supplied a vocabulary for it.

**Duffy-FG-R1-HerstalAffairEncouragesPresumption**

**Grounding quote:** "The Prussian cabinet minister Podewils, accustomed to normal diplomatic usage, declared, 'That's strong, that's lively - that's the language of Louis XIV!' ... Borcke seized the Meuse town of Maseyck on 14 September, and the Prussians released the barony to the bishop only after he made over 180,000 thaler in compensation... 'It is impossible to exaggerate the influence which this small episode had on the violent measures which ensued, and how it encouraged the presumption of the king of Prussia' (Valori, 1820, I, 93-4)."

**Locator:** "Ch. 2 The Silesian Wars, 1740-5"

**Analysis:** The Herstal ultimatum and swift armed execution in September 1740 demonstrated Frederick's readiness to use force for minor gains and extract disproportionate compensation. Contemporary observers immediately linked the episode to the larger presumption displayed weeks later in Silesia. Duffy presents Herstal as the practical dress-rehearsal that confirmed Frederick in the efficacy of rapid, forceful action.

**Duffy-FG-R1-RheinsbergConferenceDecisionSilesia**

**Grounding quote:** "It was evident on 28 October, the first day of the talks, that Frederick had already made up his mind to seize the Austrian province of Silesia. ... First of all Silesia was 'the part of the Imperial inheritance to which we have the strongest right'. Next the king drew Podewils's attention to the preparedness of the Prussian army, 'which gives us a vast superiority over all the other powers of Europe, in an unexpected eventuality like this'. Lastly the state of international affairs argued for immediate action, to anticipate Frederick's fellow-robbers (though he did not use that term) (PC 140)."

**Locator:** "Ch. 2 The Silesian Wars, 1740-5"

**Analysis:** Within days of Charles VI's death Frederick had resolved on the conquest and used the Rheinsberg meetings only to settle execution and justification. The three arguments—legal right (however weak), unique army readiness, and the window of opportunity—reveal a calculated, opportunistic decision rather than a response to immediate threat. Duffy stresses that Frederick's mind was made up before the formal discussions began.

**Duffy-FG-R1-CrossedRubiconInvasionBreslau**

**Grounding quote:** "he wrote to Berlin: 'My dear Podewils, I have crossed the Rubicon with flying colours and beating drums. My troops are full of enthusiasts the officers are fired by ambition, and our generals are avid for glory' (PC 208). ... Just before noon on the same 3 January the royal train entered by way of the Schweidnitzer Tor. Frederick's table silver was first through the gate. It was borne on pack-horses, which were draped with hangings of blue silk, all a-dangle with gold tassels and little bells. Frederick himself was mounted on a mettlesome steed. ... In retrospect the beginning of the Prussian presence in Breslau may be seen as inaugurating the first of the sequence of modern wars."

**Locator:** "Ch. 2 The Silesian Wars, 1740-5"

**Analysis:** Frederick's 16 December 1740 crossing and the theatrical entry into Breslau on 3 January 1741 combined military momentum with deliberate ceremonial display. The "Rubicon" letter and the silver-laden procession signalled both personal commitment and the transformation of a provincial electorate into a conquering power. Duffy notes the medieval joyeuse entrée symbolism ironically marking the start of modern-style wars.

**Duffy-FG-R1-MollwitzInfantryVictoryFrederickFlight**

**Grounding quote:** "A captured Austrian lieutenant-colonel had to admit . . . that it did not appear to be infantry that was marching towards them, but moving walls' (Captain von Thile, in Geuder, 1902, 115). ... Frederick's tactical summary was forceful and accurate: 'It is to our incomparable infantry alone that we are indebted for the continuation of my good fortune, the preservation of our valiant army, and the welfare of the state . . . but the cavalry is damnably awful - none of the officers can do anything with it' (Gr. Gstb., 1890-3, I, 419). ... 'My God', he cried, 'this is too much! Why are You so intent on punishing me?' (Valori, 1820, 104-5). ... It is agreeable to record that Frederick, so ungrateful to humankind, never forgot his debt to the long-striding animal which had carried him to safety. The 'Mollwitz Grey' (Mollwitzer Schimmel) was put into retirement..."

**Locator:** "Ch. 2 The Silesian Wars, 1740-5 (Mollwitz)"

**Analysis:** At Mollwitz the Prussian infantry's drilled solidity ("moving walls") won the battle after the cavalry collapsed and Frederick himself fled the field. Duffy records Frederick's frank admission of cavalry failure and his later reverence for the horse that saved him, while noting Schwerin's decisive role. The experience taught Frederick never again to defer lightly to another's strategic judgment and exposed the limits of his personal battlefield control as a novice.

**Duffy-FG-R1-ChotusitzInfantryFlankAttack**

**Grounding quote:** "It was at about 10.30 a.m. that the Prussian lines began to press forward. They marched some six hundred paces onto the plateau, then executed a giant wheel to left, and opened a long-range fire of musketry against the left flank of the Austrian forces around Chotusitz. The Austrians immediately sensed the danger to the path of their retreat to Tschaslau, and within minutes their regiments dissolved over the fields. ... Frederick was delighted at the figure he was sure he must be cutting in European opinion."

**Locator:** "Ch. 2 The Silesian Wars, 1740-5 (Chotusitz)"

**Analysis:** Chotusitz was an encounter battle in which Frederick's concealed right-wing infantry executed a decisive left-wheel flank attack after cavalry actions on both wings produced mixed results. The infantry's discipline again proved decisive. Duffy shows Frederick immediately framing the victory in terms of personal European reputation, while privately acknowledging that his own cavalry still lacked ensemble.

## Volume Synthesis Note

Duffy's military life is the premier English operational biography. It uses Frederick's 76-volume printed corpus + topography + participant testimony (Mitchell, Catt, Warnery, Archenholtz, Gaudi) to demonstrate both the power of the inherited Prussian machine and its ultimate brittleness when commanded personally at scale against coalitions. The work excels at showing the theory-practice dialectic: oblique order, short wars, concentration, and personal oversight were real advantages in 1740-45 and 1757 but repeatedly strained or contradicted by logistics, enemy adaptation, and the limits of unitary command. Duffy is critical of myth-making (Old Fritz cult) while giving full credit to Frederick's resilience, intelligence work, and capacity to learn (or at least adapt) under extreme pressure. The "Miracle" and allied frictions are presented as co-equal with Prussian arms in the survival outcome. Ch. 9 is the analytical crown: Frederick as deist soldier-intellectual whose writings are both indispensable and self-serving. The biography supplies the spine for all detailed treatment of Frederick's generalship in the wiki.
