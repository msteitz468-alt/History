# Evans — The Third Reich in History and Memory (2015)

Richard J. Evans's collection of twenty-eight essays — mostly long review
essays from the *London Review of Books*, *New York Review of Books* and
similar venues, 2003–2014 — surveying how the historiography of Nazi
Germany changed in the generation after reunification. Its unifying theme
is announced in the preface: the Third Reich is increasingly seen **in a
longer historical perspective** (colonialism, WWI, Weimar) and **in
comparative context**, while its **memory politics** (memorials,
corporate histories, ministerial self-investigations) has become a
research field in its own right. The book functions for this wiki as a
historiographical audit layer over the events/actors backbone built from
Evans's trilogy.

## Preface theses (Evans's own framing)

1. **History and memory have become intertwined** — writing on 1933–45 now
   inescapably involves how its memory was shaped, suppressed or
   transformed (corporate histories, ministerial self-investigations, the
   Beetle's postwar image).
2. A danger flag: guilt-confrontation pressure sometimes produces "crude
   and sweeping condemnation where historians should be making careful
   distinctions."
3. The war is now understood as **one globally interconnected conflict**,
   with military history set in economic and cultural context.
4. Research has shifted to **postwar "subterranean continuities"** —
   expulsions, planning utopias, looted-art restitution.

## The Toepfer case (ch. 14, "The Fellow Traveller")

Evans's personal historiographical essay on tainted funding: Alfred
Toepfer (1894–1993), whose foundation funded the Hanseatic Scholarships
Evans himself held, was shown by the foundation's own 2000 Mommsen-chaired
commission to have been far more Nazi-entangled than admitted (estates
lent to Nazi plotters incl. Henlein; SS sponsoring member; Abwehr and
Economics Ministry work) — yet Evans rebuts Pinto-Duschinsky's escalation
(the slaked-lime and Veesenmayer-release allegations fail on evidence):
Toepfer was "a fellow traveller whose contribution to the Third Reich and
its crimes was at most minor and marginal," unlike Krupp, Flick, IG
Farben or Degussa, whose 1990s commissioned histories addressed "far, far
more murderous" records. The essay's method point: funding source did not
determine scholarly conclusions — but commissioned histories (James's
Krupp) show sponsorship shaping emphasis.

## Volume Synthesis Note

The book functions as a **historiographical audit of the post-1990
transformation of Third Reich studies**, and its ingestion layered
attributed state-of-the-field sections over the events/actors backbone
already built from Evans's trilogy. Three arguments recur. First, the
**longer-perspective turn**: Nazism is now framed against colonialism
(chs. 1–2), the First World War (ch. 3) and Weimar (chs. 4–6) — Evans
consistently endorses the widened frame while resisting strong-continuity
causal claims (Windhoek→Auschwitz fails on evidence; WWI brutalization is
his preferred bridge; the domestic Sonderweg is dead but its successor
question is colonial/global). Second, the **coercion/consent
intervention** (chs. 7–8), the book's analytical core: against Gellately,
Wehler, Aly, Fritzsche and the *Zustimmungsdiktatur* school, Evans
argues for "the absolute centrality of violence, coercion and terror...
from the very outset," with the law rather than the camps as the
principal instrument and the Volksgemeinschaft "more propaganda myth than
social reality." Third, **memory politics as a research field**: doctored
diaries (Prüfer), ministerial self-histories (*Das Amt*), commissioned
corporate pasts (Krupp, Toepfer, VW), the uniqueness debate's public
stakes (Holodomor recognition, *Bloodlands*), expulsion memory, and
looted-art restitution — throughout, Evans's characteristic move is to
credit the reviewed author's research while dismantling the interpretive
frame, and the wiki records both sides as attributed positions.

Cross-volume tension worth flagging: Evans's harsh review of Snyder's
*Bloodlands* (ch. 25) and his anti-uniqueness-dogma-but-anti-equivalence
position (ch. 24) sit in the same interpretive space as the wiki's
[[communism-and-fascism]] comparative pages — the controversy pages
record Evans as one position, not as adjudication.
