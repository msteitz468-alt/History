### Parker-Roberts-Military-Revolution-Thesis-Core-Definition
**Grounding quote:** "Four changes in the art of war during the period were singled out as critical. First came a 'revolution in tactics': the replacement of the lance and pike by the arrow and musket, as the feudal knights fell before the firepower of massed archers or gunners. Associated with this development were a marked growth in army size right across Europe (with the armed forces of several states increasing tenfold between 1500 and 1700), and the adoption of more ambitious and complex strategies designed to bring these larger armies into action. Fourth and finally, Roberts's military revolution dramatically accentuated the impact of war on society: the greater costs incurred, the greater damage inflicted, and the greater administrative challenges posed by the augmented armies made waging war far more of a burden and far more of a problem than ever previously, both for the civilian population and for their rulers."
**Locator:** Introduction (discussion of Michael Roberts' 1955 lecture)
**Analysis:** Parker restates Roberts' four-part thesis (tactics, army size, strategy, societal impact) as the starting point for the book, while noting subsequent criticisms and positioning his own analysis.

### Parker-Chinese-Warring-States-Military-Revolution-Parallel
**Grounding quote:** "The resemblance between this sequence of events and the European military revolution is striking. Both involved a massive manpower growth, a profound change in tactics and strategy, and an intensified impact of war on society. Both, too, required equally profound changes in the structure and philosophy of government. If one be deemed to have constituted a revolution, then so must the other."
**Locator:** Introduction (Warring States comparison)
**Analysis:** Parker uses the Ch'in unification via the Warring States military revolution (army growth to ~1 million, tactical shift to mass infantry, bureaucratic centralization, enduring institutions) as an independent historical benchmark to validate calling early modern European changes a true "revolution," despite differences in duration and global effects.

### Parker-1494-French-Invasion-Artillery-Shock-Watershed
**Grounding quote:** "Before the year 1494, wars were protracted, battles bloodless, the methods followed in besieging towns slow and uncertain; and although artillery was already in use, it was managed with such lack of skill that it caused little hurt. Hence it came about that the ruler of a state could hardly be dispossessed. But the French, in their invasion of Italy, infused so much liveliness into our wars that, up to the [present], . . . whenever the open country was lost, the state was lost with it."
**Locator:** Ch. 1 (Guicciardini on the French invasion of 1494-5)
**Analysis:** Parker presents the 1494 French campaign (18,000 men + 40+ horse-drawn guns) as the decisive catalyst that ended the medieval stalemate of vertical defenses and slow sieges, forcing Italian states to innovate rapidly.

### Parker-Alberti-Bastion-Response-To-Artillery
**Grounding quote:** "The Italian architect and humanist, Leon Battista Alberti, was the first to divine the correct response to the bombard. His treatise De re aedificatoria, composed in the 1440s, argued that defensive fortifications would be more effective if they were 'built in uneven lines, like the teeth of a saw', and even speculated that a star-shaped configuration might be best."

**Analysis:** Parker stresses that the intellectual and engineering solution to gunpowder artillery predated widespread adoption but required the 1494 shock for rulers to implement angled, low, thick defenses with flanking fire.

### Parker-Siena-Fortification-Costs-Extinction
**Grounding quote:** "In 1553, faced with the threat of imminent attack by its enemies, it was decided that seventeen towns, including Siena, would be equipped with new bastions and ramparts. But labour, funds and building materials for such a major project were all so hard to come by that, when the invasion occurred in 1554, few of the projected defence works were complete; and yet the Republic had spent so much on fortification that it had no resources left either to raise a relief army or even to hire and man a fleet to succour its coastal fortresses. So in April 1555, after a gruelling ten-month siege, Siena surrendered unconditionally and was, after a short period of occupation, annexed by her neighbour, Florence. The military revolution had led directly to extinction."
**Locator:** Ch. 1 (costs of the trace italienne)
**Analysis:** Parker uses the Republic of Siena as a stark case of how the fiscal and resource demands of modern fortifications could destroy a state, illustrating the societal and political consequences of the military revolution.

### Parker-Dutch-Volley-Fire-Countermarch-Tactics
**Grounding quote:** "We can date the Dutch discovery of the 'volley' technique very precisely: it first appeared, in diagrammatic form, in a letter from William Louis to his cousin Maurice dated 8 December 1594... if their men were drawn up in a series of long lines, the first rank firing together and then retiring to reload while the following ranks came forward and did the same, a continuous hail of fire would be maintained to keep the enemy at bay."
**Locator:** Ch. 1 (Maurice of Nassau reforms; plate 5 reference)
**Analysis:** Parker highlights the Nassau cousins' (Maurice and William Louis) precise invention of the countermarch/volley system—drawing on Roman precedent and Lipsius—as a key tactical adaptation to slow-firing muskets, requiring smaller units, constant drill, and standardized weapons.

### Parker-Army-Size-Growth-Early-Modern-Europe
**Grounding quote:** "Whereas Ferdinand and Isabella of Spain conquered Granada in 1492 with no more than 20,000 men, their grandson Charles V commanded perhaps 100,000 against the Turks in Hungary in 1532, and almost 150,000 overall at the time of his unsuccessful siege of Metz in 1552. ### Parker-Swedish-Indelningsverk-and-Demographic-Devastation
**Grounding quote:** "The parish of Bygdea in northern Sweden, for example, provided 230 young men for service in Poland and Germany between 1621 and 1639, and saw 215 of them die there, while a further five returned home crippled. ... ### Parker-Plunder-Contributions-System-and-Army-Sustenance
**Grounding quote:** "from there it was but a short step to the fully fledged 'contributions system': a permanent military tax levied by the army from all the communities within a certain radius of its presence. Under the direction of a skilled and ruthless commander, such as Parma or Wallenstein, 'contributions' could be made to supply the troops' every need - food, clothes, lodgings, munitions, transport —the exact mechanics and amounts of the goods and services supplied being worked out between the regimental and company clerks on the one hand, and the local magistrates on the other. ... By the 1640s, most military administrators reckoned to supply two-thirds of their troops' wages in kind."
**Locator:** Ch. 2
**Analysis:** Parker shows how armies systematized extraction from civilians (Brandschatzung to contributions) and in-kind payments to keep forces operational when state treasuries could not provide cash, turning occupation into a fiscal tool.

### Parker-Dutch-Financial-Revolution-and-Sustained-Borrowing

### Parker-Military-Enterprisers-and-Contractor-Devolution
**Grounding quote:** "By the end of the sixteenth century, several states had begun to recruit and supply their armies — particularly units required to serve abroad — through private contractors; but during the Thirty Years War the system reached its apogee, with some 1,500 individuals raising troops all over Europe, under contract, for one or more warlords. Between 1630 and 1635, perhaps 400 military enterprisers were active, raising and maintaining fully equipped regiments, brigades and (in the case of Wallenstein and Bernard of Saxe-Weimar) even whole armies on behalf of governments which lacked either the financial or human resources to do the job themselves. ... Wallenstein advanced over six million thalers (£1.25 million) to the emperor between 1621 and 1628."
**Locator:** Ch. 2
**Analysis:** Parker details the shift to private military entrepreneurs who used their own credit and organizational skill to raise and maintain forces when states could not, with economic power (not prior military success) as the core qualification.

### ming-china-rejects-heavy-shipborne-artillery
**Grounding quote:** "Eventually, in the 1550s, the Chinese Imperial authorities decided that naval artillery was of little value against the pirate bands from Japan, known as wako (literally: dwarf pirates), who terrorized the coasts of China. According to an illlustrated treatise of naval warfare, published in 1564 by a protege of the Chinese Grand Admiral, the pirates had been defeated, not by naval means, but by a combination of three things: intrigue designed to divide the Chinese from the Japanese groups among the invaders; diplomatic pressure on the pirates' supporters in China and on their bases in Japan; and the overwhelming military assaults on their land bases. And even the latter were conducted without firearms: the Ming commanders did not place their trust in muskets or cannon, whether by land or at sea, for they found them too difficult to supply with shot of standard calibre, and too prone to explode when fired. They also dismissed proposals for reinforced, shot-resistant junks on the grounds that they would be too cumbersome; and they deprecated ship-borne artillery as too inaccurate. Instead, they defeated the wako in the traditional Chinese way: by concentrating vastly superior numbers of troops armed with bows, lances and swords. And although Chinese war-junks continued to carry some guns, they were only light anti-personnel pieces (plate 20). The principal weapon of the Imperial navy remained, even in the 1630s, the fireship."
**Locator:** Ch. 3
**Analysis:** Despite centuries of ship-borne artillery (including on Cheng Ho's Indian Ocean expeditions and a 1522 victory over Portuguese), Ming authorities explicitly rejected heavy naval guns as unreliable, inaccurate, and logistically burdensome. They retained only light anti-personnel pieces and fireships while defeating threats through numbers and land-based operations. This demonstrates that technological possession alone did not produce revolutionary change.

### european-naval-guns-adopted-despite-ramming-boarding-tradition
**Grounding quote:** "So although ship-borne artillery was readily compatible with their naval traditions, the Chinese deliberately rejected it. In Europe, by contrast, the naval gun was adopted in spite of the fact that the standard technique of warfare at sea was ramming and boarding. But it was slow to make its mark. ... And when (after the 1360s) credible reports of the use of artillery at sea appear, they clearly refer to anti-personnel rather than to ship-sinking weapons. ... these can have been little more than light breech-loaders ... And the recoil from even a modest cannon mounted in the main top would inevitably have produced catastrophe!"
**Locator:** Ch. 3
**Analysis:** Europeans integrated guns into a tradition centered on close combat and boarding, beginning with limited anti-personnel weapons on galleys and roundships. Early use was slow and ineffective for sinking ships, highlighting the technical and tactical barriers that had to be overcome before broadside tactics emerged.

### galeasses-deliver-decisive-firepower-at-lepanto
**Grounding quote:** "At the battle of Lepanto on 7 October 1571, for example, the Turkish fleet mistook six Venetian galeasses for merchant supply vessels and attacked them in force: they paid a high price for their error - no less than seventy galleys were said to have been sunk by the new warships, 'an incredible thing that only six galeasses should have caused such great destruction, for they had not [hitherto] been tried in the forefront of a naval battle'. ... It has been calculated that they deployed 1,815 guns against only 750 on the Turkish side, and in the end the Turks ran out of ammunition even for those. ... In all, the Ottoman fleet lost 200 galleys, together with their artillery, stores, and some 30,000 men."
**Locator:** Ch. 3
**Analysis:** The galeass combined sail and oar with heavy broadside-capable armament (eight heavy guns fore and aft plus side anti-personnel weapons). Its firepower advantage at Lepanto proved devastating against the Ottoman galley fleet, though the Ottomans rapidly replaced losses through existing shipyard capacity (while facing acute manning problems). This illustrates firepower's growing importance even within the galley paradigm.

### gunports-and-muzzle-loaders-transform-sailing-ships
**Grounding quote:** "About the year 1500, however, two important inventions were made which eventually transformed the nature of sea warfare. First, the wrought-iron breech-loaders were joined by various types of muzzle-loading bronze artillery. ... Here was a gun that could shoot straight through a ship's timbers. But the new artillery was too heavy to be carried safely in either the castles or along the main-deck: it had to be positioned lower down. Starting (according to tradition) around 1500 in the port of Brest in Brittany, and quickly spreading all over Atlantic Europe, hinged gun-ports were cut into the sides of the larger ships, making it possible to deploy artillery along their entire length on several levels."
**Locator:** Ch. 3
**Analysis:** The combination of stronger muzzle-loading bronze guns and hinged gunports allowed heavy artillery to be mounted low along the sides of Atlantic roundships. This shifted warfare from castles and end-on attacks to broadside capability, fundamentally altering ship design and tactics.

### early-overlarge-warships-prove-impractical
**Grounding quote:** "For example, the 1,000-ton Henry Grace a Dieu ... carried 43 heavy and 141 light guns, with a combined weight of over 100 tons ... But, as contemporary illustrations show, most of these weapons were still used from the castles, to fire down on potential boarders, rather than near the waterline to sink ships. The Great Harry, like the Venetian quinquereme, was too cumbrous to be effective ... The Mary Rose, another unwieldy 600-ton, 78-gun warship launched in 1511, suddenly keeled over and sank with all hands in 1545 ... The history of Scotland's Great Michael was little better: although built in 1511 at the king's express request, she proved simply too expensive to operate - perhaps as much as one-half of James IV's total yearly receipts went on her construction and 10 per cent of his entire annual budget on wages for her crew."
**Locator:** Ch. 3
**Analysis:** Initial attempts at heavily armed sailing ships suffered from excessive size, superstructure, cost, and instability (exemplified by the Mary Rose disaster and Great Michael's expense). Practical effective designs required slimming down, reducing castles, and better integration of guns amidships.

### english-truck-carriages-enable-sustained-broaside-fire
**Grounding quote:** "But the English navy did not suffer from this handicap because, for at least half a century, its guns had been mounted on a compact, four-wheeled truck carriage. ... The design of the English truck carriages meant that the gun muzzles could protrude much further through the gun-ports, and ensured that there was no awkward trail or wide wheels to obstruct the sides and rear. Adequate working space was thus available for the crews to haul their guns back with tackles after firing, and reload inboard before heaving them into place for another round. The truck carriage also allowed guns to be traversed and aimed far more easily and accurately in action. Broadsides could therefore be delivered consecutively during the course of a fight, the range being dictated by the superior sailing qualities of the English ships."
**Locator:** Ch. 3
**Analysis:** The four-wheeled truck carriage (in use by the Mary Rose era) permitted rapid inboard reloading, aiming, and repeated broadsides. This contrasted with heavier Spanish two-wheeled carriages that made reloading at sea difficult or impossible under fire, giving English ships a decisive tactical edge in sustained gunnery.

### armada-campaign-exposes-galley-tactics-failure-on-sailing-ships
**Grounding quote:** "What, then, went wrong on the Spanish gundecks in the Channel? ... the bigger, muzzle-loading pieces, however, could only be recharged at sea in one of two ways. ... All the available evidence suggests that the Armada's ship-killing guns were never able to apply a continuous close-range cannonade against the enemy, although this was the only way in which they might have achieved success. ... According to Sir Arthur Gorgas, who had been there, the Queen's ships were able to weave in and out among the Spanish fleet 'discharging our broadsides of ordnance double for their single, we carrying as good and great artillery as they do and to better proof.' A captured Spanish colonel, Don Diego Pimentel, agreed: ... 'to be able to tack four or five times in the time it took us to go about once'..."
**Locator:** Ch. 3
**Analysis:** The Spanish Armada relied on a single loaded salvo followed by boarding (galley-style doctrine), but English refusal to close and superior reload/maneuver capabilities prevented this. Archaeological and administrative records show Spanish ships fired far fewer rounds than supplied, confirming the tactical mismatch.

### portuguese-line-ahead-and-stand-off-gunnery-in-indian-ocean
**Grounding quote:** "The Portuguese and Castilian galleons had long been familiar with — and had often successfully employed - the tactic of stand-off gunnery. For instance, the Instructions given by King Manuel of Portugal to Pedro Alvares Cabral in 1500 for his voyage to India specified that, if he met any Muslim ships, 'you are not to come to close quarters with them if you can avoid it, but you are to compel them with your artillery alone to strike sail' ... The next fleet, sent under the command of Vasco da Gama in 1502, defeated a large Muslim fleet in pitched battle off the Malabar coast by sailing 'one astern of the other in a line', and keeping up rapid artillery fire, the gunners making 'such haste to load again that they recharged the guns with bags of powder which they had ready for this purpose, made to measure so that they could load again very speedily.'"
**Locator:** Ch. 3
**Analysis:** Line-ahead formation and sustained stand-off artillery were practiced by Portuguese fleets in Asian waters from at least 1500–1502, decades before the Armada. This shows the tactics had Atlantic/Indian Ocean origins and were not invented in 1588, though European adoption remained uneven.

### dutch-and-republican-frigates-and-line-ahead-create-permanent-high-seas-fleets
**Grounding quote:** "It was in fact the Dutch, not the English, who first created a high-seas fleet capable of operating at long range. ... Shortly after 1600, eight new 300-ton capital ships were built at Hoorn ... They came to be called frigates ... It was at the Downs that the attack in line ahead was apparently first performed in European waters ... the Dutch admiral, Maarten Harpertszoon Tromp, led his squadron in among the Spaniards ... and sank forty of their fifty-three ships. ... The Republic's fleet, numbering 157 warships, was now expressly ordered to adopt in action the line-ahead formation favoured by the Dutch. ... Here indeed was a high-seas fleet capable of operating at long-range, on a permanent basis, as an ocean-going force: it was arguably the first in European history. ... The critical element was the change in ship-design, for only frigates could operate effectively for long periods at long range."
**Locator:** Ch. 3
**Analysis:** Dutch frigates (long, low, shallow draft, ~40 guns) and line-ahead tactics enabled effective long-range operations, demonstrated at the Downs (1639). Post-Civil War England adopted the design and formation, producing (with superior administration and finance) the first sustained European high-seas battlefleet able to station forces in the Caribbean and Mediterranean.

### korean-turtle-ships-and-japanese-red-seal-policy
**Grounding quote:** "on 8 July, in a decisive battle, Admiral Yi destroyed the main enemy fleet in Hansan Bay; and on the following day he defeated a relief expedition sailing up from Japan. ... the second decisive factor in Admiral Yi's victory was his use of the famous 'turtle ships', about 33 metres long and 8 metres broad, entirely encased in hexagonal metal plates so that they could neither be boarded nor holed. ... They were armed with twelve gunports and twenty-two loopholes per side (for small-arms), plus four more ports at each end, together with fire-pots and toxic smoke. Sometimes the turtle-ships came up close, just like a modern torpedo boat, and fired broadsides; sometimes they used their metal ram to hole the enemy ... Their armament outweighed that of the Japanese by about 40 to 1. ... a new form of maritime enterprise grew up in Japan: a fleet of large unarmed merchantmen, on average 300 tons each, was built locally and entrusted by their Japanese merchant owners to European pilots and (often) European crews. ... They carried no guns at all. ... The Japanese government, like the Mughals, considered it cheaper and more effective to pay protection money to the Europeans than to invest in new ship-designs and massive armament..."
**Locator:** Ch. 3
**Analysis:** Korean turtle ships combined armor, heavy broadside/ram capability, and aggressive tactics to destroy Japanese invasion fleets in 1592 (including Hansan Bay) despite Japanese numerical superiority. Japan later abandoned armed long-distance shipping for unarmed "Red-seal" merchant vessels protected by shogunal passports, mirroring Mughal reliance on permits and non-naval countermeasures rather than matching European naval investment.

### naval-revolution-enables-global-european-projection-with-asian-limits
**Grounding quote:** "The savage and prolonged naval rivalry of the Atlantic states of Europe in the century after 1588 had thus created battlefleets capable, both in number and design, of pursuing strategic objectives far from home. The new ships-of-the-line were able to operate by 1688 in the Caribbean, in the Indian Ocean, and in the Pacific, in order to gain both tactical and strategic superiority. ... In America, for example, seapower had been acquired by the Westerners from the days of Columbus, since the Amerindian peoples seem to have lacked any specialized warships of their own ... the arrival in the Indian Ocean of first Dutch and then English ships in the 1590s presented the Estado da India with a new and intractable problem. ... For as long as the European powers lacked a military presence in Asia, the native rulers were able to counter the power of Western artillery with the permit and the prison."
**Locator:** Ch. 3
**Analysis:** The post-1588 development of versatile, long-endurance battlefleets and frigates allowed European powers to pursue sustained operations across oceans, securing dominance in the Americas and initial advantages in Asia. In Asia, however, armed local resurgence (e.g., Achinese, Malabar) and non-military responses (Mughal permits, Japanese unarmed trade) limited full control, underscoring that naval revolution produced asymmetric global reach rather than universal supremacy.

### By 1650 European military mastery confined to four distinct regions
**Grounding quote:** "By 1650 the West had already achieved military mastery in four separate areas: Central and Northeast America; Siberia; some coastal areas of sub-Saharan Africa; and the islands of Southeast Asia."
**Locator:** Ch. 4
**Analysis:** Parker stresses that these early gains were geographically and chronologically specific, not a uniform global triumph. By 1800 Europeans still had "scarcely made any impact" in East Asia. The "initial 35 per cent" of global land control (by 1800) predated the Industrial Revolution and cannot be attributed to it or to innate moral superiority.

### Distinctive European style of warfare: fought to kill rather than enslave
**Grounding quote:** "the white men, they found, fought dirty and (what was worse) fought to kill. Thus the Narragansett Indians of New England strongly disapproved of the colonists' way of making war. 'It was too furious,' one brave told an English captain in 1638, 'and [it] slays too many men'. ... Roger Williams, a colonial governor, likewise admitted that the Indians' fighting 'was farre lesse bloudy and devouring than the cruell warres of Europe'."
**Locator:** Ch. 4
**Analysis:** This pattern (also noted among Javanese) distinguished European expansion from local norms and facilitated rapid territorial gains where opponents avoided total destruction.
**Links/taxonomy:** contrasts_with: native warfare practices in [[americas]], sub-Saharan Africa and Southeast Asia; contributed_to: initial conquest successes.

### Native wars in Americas, Africa and Southeast Asia primarily for slaves, not territory
**Grounding quote:** "Mr Scott had noted a vital and unusual feature of military organization in Southeast Asia which was shared (though he probably did not know it) with America and sub-Saharan Africa: native wars in these areas were almost always fought to enslave enemies rather than to exterminate them. ... 'that the sole object of their wars was to procure slaves, as they could not obtain European goods without slaves, and they could not get slaves without fighting for them'."
**Locator:** Ch. 4
**Analysis:** This objective made Western firearms and tactics (designed for decisive killing or territorial seizure) a poor cultural fit in many regions; smoothbore inaccuracy and the risk of gangrenous wounds further limited adoption for slave-raiding.
**Links/taxonomy:** contrasts_with: European objectives; contributed_to: limited long-term impact of gun imports in Africa and Indonesia.

### Absence of walled cities facilitated initial conquests but required European adaptation to guerrilla methods
**Grounding quote:** "In America, although the Aztec and Inca empires possessed some walled cities, the less civilized peoples to the north and south of them did not. This dearth, of course, facilitated the initial conquest, because the natives lacked defensible bases to fall back on; but it complicated consolidation. ... Only gradually did the Europeans recognize that the only way to beat the Indians was to adopt those same guerilla methods."
**Locator:** Ch. 4
**Analysis:** Examples include King Philip's War (1675) and Vargas Machuca's 1599 manual advocating commando-style search-and-destroy in Chile rather than European linear formations or permanent garrisons.
**Links/taxonomy:** enabled: early [[americas]] conquests; produced: European adoption of native tactics in certain theaters.

### Tiny European forces toppled the Aztec and Inca empires
**Grounding quote:** "the vast, centrally organized empires ruled by the Aztecs and the Incas collapsed before the Iberian onslaught with a speed and finality that has few parallels: the former in 1519-21, to a force of some 500 Spaniards (together with 14 cannons and only 16 horses) under Hernan Cortes; the latter in 1531-3, to an even smaller group of 168 Europeans (together with only 4 guns and 67 horses) under Francisco Pizarro."
**Locator:** Ch. 4
**Analysis:** Parker notes these were relatively large concentrations by early colonial standards; smaller "bands" of 20-100 mounted warriors sufficed elsewhere.
**Links/taxonomy:** produced: rapid Iberian control in [[americas]]; contrasts_with: much larger forces required later in [[india]] or against organized resistance.

### Native Americans acquired firearms but lost primarily due to demographic factors
**Grounding quote:** "From the 1640s they managed to acquire an adequate supply of guns from the French, the English and ... the Dutch; and they used them to deadly effect ... In the end the 'Red Indians' lost ground not so much through any technical inferiority as because their numbers dwindled throughout the seventeenth century (largely thanks to the inroads of European diseases), while those of the Westerners (largely thanks to immigration) relentlessly increased."
**Locator:** Ch. 4
**Analysis:** Narragansetts even built a European-style fortress in the Great Swamp during King Philip's War. Guns were adopted effectively for their superior force and penetration.

### Extensive firearms trade to Africa produced virtually no change in military techniques
**Grounding quote:** "Some 8,000 muskets were sent to the Gold Coast for trade in the three years following July 1658 ... whilst a century later, the total number of firearms exported every year was around 400,000 ... And yet, in most areas, this inflow of Western technology had virtually no impact on African military techniques. ... As late as 1861 an English officer in Nigeria ... noted that the native troops in battle still 'spread themselves out anyhow into open order, and skirmish away until their ammunition is exhausted'."
**Locator:** Ch. 4
**Analysis:** Guns were obtained mainly for slave-raiding (exchanged at rates such as 4-12 per slave); their inaccuracy and wounding characteristics did not suit the goal of capturing fit slaves. Europeans remained confined to coastal forts until the 19th century.
**Links/taxonomy:** enabled: trade but not conquest in sub-Saharan Africa; contrasts_with: decisive advantage claimed in other regions.

### Acheh accumulated massive artillery yet failed to displace tradition or defeat Europeans
**Grounding quote:** "Sultan Iskandar Muda of Acheh, for example, had by 1620 accumulated some 2,000 artillery pieces from various Ottoman and European sources. But it led nowhere: the guns proved inferior in action against the Portuguese and almost all were lost at the unsuccessful siege of Malacca in 1629. In reality, firearms never fully replaced Acheh's 900 war-elephants as the front line of defence."
**Locator:** Ch. 4
**Analysis:** Similar patterns at Mataram's siege of Batavia (1629). Few walled towns existed; surrender or flight had been standard before European-style permanent fortifications.

### European trace italienne forts in Southeast Asia proved decisive for maritime hegemony
**Grounding quote:** "the Europeans erected many other fortifications in Southeast Asia ... But there was only one other fully fortified city to compare with Batavia and Malacca: Manila in the Philippines. ... the Spaniards built massive defences that defied all assaults for over two centuries. ... with the wealth conferred by their possession, the Europeans could exercise a maritime hegemony over all other major ports in the region."
**Locator:** Ch. 4
**Analysis:** Portuguese Malacca (A Famosa), Dutch Batavia, and Spanish Intramuros Manila withstood enormous native siege efforts (e.g., Acheh's 20,000 men + 236 boats in 1629). Some local rulers (Banten, Makassar) attempted imitation but unsuccessfully for control of long-distance trade.

### Ottomans adopted Western guns and siege techniques rapidly but practised the military revolution imperfectly
**Grounding quote:** "At first sight, the Ottoman army appears to have adopted and mastered Western military technology with remarkable speed and thoroughness. ... And yet there were three important respects in which the military revolution was imperfectly practised by Europe's most dangerous neighbour. First ... the Ottoman decision to build their artillery big, whereas the Western powers concentrated on increasing the mobility and numbers of their guns. ... the failure to fortify their siege-camp. ... metallurgical inferiority."
**Locator:** Ch. 4
**Analysis:** Heavy guns could not be redeployed in time at the relief of Vienna (1683); no countervallation built around the siege works. Contemporary and modern analyses showed Ottoman metal was brittle; troops remained in thick columns and traditional frameworks.

### Islamic slave-soldier systems (Mamluks, Janissaries) structurally resistant to tactical innovation
**Grounding quote:** "Mamluks were not supposed to think, but to ride horses; they were designed to be not a military elite, but military automata. ... In 1517 the imperious knights of the Mamluk army — who tolerated the use of firearms for sieges but refused to deploy them in battle — were overthrown by the slave soldiers of the Ottoman Turks, whose commanders lacked such high principles."
**Locator:** Ch. 4
**Analysis:** Core armies composed of men lacking local ties fought "in the traditional manner." This system "was certainly not a system that favoured strategic or tactical innovation."

### Mughal and other Indian armies remained aggregations of heroic individual warriors
**Grounding quote:** "Be it known to the reader that these two armies were not ordered in the disposition obtaining in Europe. But one division was close to another, as the trees of a pinewood ... I saw in this action ... that the only soldiers who fought were those well to the front. ... If those in front advanced, those behind followed the example; and if the former retired, the others fled - a custom of Hindustan quite contrary to that of Europe."
**Locator:** Ch. 4 (Manucci on 1658 battle)
**Analysis:** Even with European experts, renegades, and gifts of guns/artillery, native armies did not adopt linear volley tactics or disciplined formations. "Indian armies may have been huge but they remained, essentially, aggregations of individual heroic warriors."

### Traditional Indian fortifications too massive for early modern European artillery to decide sieges
**Grounding quote:** "many of the major Indian fortresses were already so huge that even the heaviest early modern artillery bombardment could make little impression on them: thus the fourteenth-century walls of Gulbarga in the Deccan were seventeen metres thick ... the walls of Agra ... consisted of two revetments of dressed red sandstone blocks ten metres apart, filled in with sand and rubble. ... sieges in early modern India were decided by blockade and mines rather than by cannonade."
**Locator:** Ch. 4
**Analysis:** European strongholds resisted native sieges but fell to other Europeans; locals had little incentive or need to copy the trace italienne until later pressures.

### Turning points in India driven by European-trained sepoys and company financial resources, not pre-existing superiority
**Grounding quote:** "at the battle of Adyar river with the classic European technique of the musketry salvo: 300 Europeans and 700 native troops, drawn up in three ranks, moved forward against their 10,000 adversaries firing successive volleys of shot. ... The battle of Adyar river was a turning-point in Indian history. ... By 1782 the British were able to maintain 115,000 men in India (90 per cent of them sepoys) and reduce the odds against them in battle from the 10 to 1 of Plassey to only 2 to 1."
**Locator:** Ch. 4
**Analysis:** French pioneered systematic European drill, weapons, officers and NCOs for sepoys; British followed using superior local deposits and revenues after Plassey (1757). Earlier British policy explicitly opposed heavy military spending ("Our business is trade, not war").
**Links/taxonomy:** produced: shift from coastal factories to territorial control in [[india]]; part_of: [[state-formation]] / fiscal-military dynamics exported; contrasts_with: earlier Portuguese and British restraint.

### East Asian states already possessed gunpowder traditions and "knew the rules of the game," limiting Western impact
**Grounding quote:** "the peoples of East Asia, by contrast, were able to keep the West at bay throughout the early modern period because, as it were, they already knew the rules of the game. Firearms, fortresses, standing armies and warships had long been part of the military tradition of China, Korea and Japan. ... The distinctive 'world order' of both China and Japan endured intact until the Industrial nations of the West deployed steamships, steel artillery and sepoys against them in the mid-nineteenth century. They did not fall before the military revolution."
**Locator:** Ch. 4
**Analysis:** Parker explicitly qualifies any simple "rise of the West" narrative: prior familiarity with guns and fortification allowed selective adaptation or restriction (e.g., Japan reducing licensed gun production and limiting daimyo to one castle after 1615).

### Selective adoption in China: Western-style guns used on northern frontier but remained marginal overall
**Grounding quote:** "under the direction of Ch'i Chi-kuang ... the Great Wall was equipped with pill-boxes to shelter musketeers, and the reserve units of the northern army were strengthened with small carts (known as 'battle wagons') each carrying breech-loading light artillery ... But firearms remained only a minor part of the armament of Chinese armies. ... the Ming supported (in theory at least) some 500,000 men and 100,000 horses on the northern frontier ... So the soldiers of the new dynasty continued to fight in the traditional manner until the nineteenth century."
**Locator:** Ch. 4
**Analysis:** Qing captured Portuguese-style casters and guns (1630s) and used them; massive scale made universal re-equipment impractical. Heavy artillery mainly defensive or for specific campaigns.
**Links/taxonomy:** analogous_to: limited European-style adoption; contrasts_with: full transformation in some Western contexts; part_of: [[gunpowder-empires]] / East Asian military continuity.

### Japan rapidly integrated volley fire and artillery-resistant castles but later restricted firearms
**Grounding quote:** "The battle of Nagashino, 1575, marked a decisive stage ... The troops of Oda Nobunaga ... used volley-fire from their Western-style muskets to destroy the charges of the Takeda cavalry. ... A new sort of defensive fortification therefore emerged ... Quite possibly ... these Japanese castles had 'no peers in terms of size and impregnability' anywhere else in the early modern world."
**Locator:** Ch. 4
**Analysis:** Nobunaga/Hideyoshi/Tokugawa built massive hilltop or plain citadels (Azuchi, Odawara, Himeji, Osaka with walls up to 19m thick). Yet after unification, guns were controlled, production reduced, and most castles destroyed or limited to one per lord.
**Links/taxonomy:** produced: effective adaptation of volley and [[trace-italienne]] elements; contrasts_with: later "demilitarization"; analogous_to: European fortress responses but on larger scale.

**Range 4 Notes for reconciliation:** This range provides Parker's core qualifications on the military revolution's uneven global export: decisive "slight edge" advantages in guns, discipline, and permanent trace italienne fortifications in some theaters (Americas, coastal SE Asia, specific sieges) were real but geographically bounded and often required local adaptation or demographic luck. Gunpowder empires (Ottomans, Mughals) and East Asian states showed rapid initial uptake followed by imperfect integration or deliberate restriction due to scale, cultural fit, existing traditions, and institutional conservatism. Internal European changes (linear tactics, mobile artillery, counter-siege works) did not translate uniformly abroad; many non-Western actors (Indians with huge armies, Chinese with frontier wagons, Japanese with castles) demonstrated agency and limits to diffusion. No claims here rely on material outside this slice. Strong coverage of "rise of the West" caveats, internal vs. external firearm effects, and counter-examples in [[india]], [[ottoman-empire]], [[china]], [[japan]], and [[americas]]. ### Range 5 Extraction
**Source grounding note:** Lines 5651-6200 (Chapter 5 "Beyond the revolution" — chronology debate, Frederick the Great as culmination of old system, light troops/division/artillery innovations as transition, final assessment of when the military revolution ended). All claims verbatim from this slice only.

### Frederick the Great's military system culminated the ancien regime and the military revolution
**Grounding quote:** "Just as the middle decades of the eighteenth century represented the apogee of the ancien regime, so the same period saw the culmination of 'the military revolution'."
**Locator:** Ch. 5
**Analysis:** Parker dates the plateau and effective end of the core military revolution to the middle decades of the eighteenth century, with Frederick's Prussia as its highest expression. Frederick's Prussia (and its enemies) retained the social hierarchy, aristocratic officer corps, commoner rank-and-file (often foreign), financing methods, and limited transport of seventeenth-century states; only marginal improvements separated it from the era of Philip II or Charles V.
**Links/taxonomy:** [[frederick-the-great]] part_of [[military-revolution]]; [[frederick-the-great]] preceded_by [[early-modern]]; [[military-revolution]] contrasts_with earlier periods via [[controversies/military-revolution-thesis]].

### Frederick's originality lay in supply improvements and superior discipline, yet produced only limited war
**Grounding quote:** "The originality of Frederick's military system thus lay principally in his improvements in supply, which allowed him to move his armies relatively swiftly and in relatively good order (provided they did not stray too far from Prussian territory); and in the superior discipline of his troops, which allowed the king to direct surprise attacks under the noses of his enemies (manoeuvres which, in other armies, would have produced chaos). But it was still not enough to secure decisive victories."
**Locator:** Ch. 5
**Analysis:** Supply (standard uniforms, magazines holding two years' grain for 60,000 men) and drill enabled mobility and surprise within Prussian territory, but Frederick achieved only the capture of Silesia in 1741 before becoming a defender of the status quo. Wars remained attritional and low-gain.
**Links/taxonomy:** [[frederick-the-great]] produced limited outcomes in [[seven-years-war-1756]]; [[frederick-the-great]] contrasts_with decisive victory models associated with [[gustavus-adolphus]].

### Frederick explicitly described the limited character of eighteenth-century warfare
**Grounding quote:** "The ambitious should consider above all that armaments and military discipline being much the same throughout Europe, and alliances as a rule producing an equality of force between belligerent parties, all that princes can expect from the greatest advantages at present is to acquire, by accumulation of successes, either some small city on the frontier, or some territory which will not pay interest on the expenses of the war, and whose population does not even approach the number of citizens who perished in the campaigns."
**Locator:** Ch. 5
**Analysis:** Frederick's 1775 statement (quoted verbatim) shows the mid-eighteenth-century system had stabilized into predictable, costly, limited conflicts where major decisive results were unattainable due to parity in arms, discipline, and alliances.
**Links/taxonomy:** [[frederick-the-great]] contributed_to stabilization of [[early-modern]] military system; [[seven-years-war-1756]] part_of this limited-war equilibrium.

### Light infantry and light cavalry emerged as a major post-revolution innovation during the War of the Austrian Succession
**Grounding quote:** "By the time of Frederick's death in 1786, however, the military system of early modern Europe was clearly changing. In the first place, new varieties of regular troops appeared: the light infantry and light cavalry. They first achieved prominence in 1740—1, when Frederick of Prussia's surprise attack on the Habsburg empire was halted with the aid of some 20,000 veterans from the military border with the Turks, in Hungary and Croatia."
**Locator:** Ch. 5
**Analysis:** These lightly armed, irregular-style skirmishers (described as "fierce, undisciplined and subject to scarcely any military laws") from the Habsburg-Ottoman border surprised regular forces and prompted adoption by Savoy (1742-3), France under Saxe (1743), and later British forces in America. Frederick rejected them due to desertion fears.
**Links/taxonomy:** light troops preceded_by [[habsburg-monarchy]] border forces; contributed_to transition out of the old system; [[france]] adopted them; contrasts_with Frederick's conservative stance.

### Divisional organization and improved infrastructure enabled more mobile strategy
**Grounding quote:** "Another innovation of the War of the Austrian Succession (1740—8) was the organization of large armies into a number of self-sufficient units called divisions. Pierre de Bourcet's Principles of mountain warfare... proposed that the ideal army should be made up of three separate columns... Only in 1787—8 did the French adopt the division as the basic administrative unit... Divisional organization in the field, however, was harder to achieve (and did not become standard in France until 1796) because of the relative scarcity of the roads and maps..."
**Locator:** Ch. 5
**Analysis:** Divisions (up to 12,000 mixed arms under one staff) plus major surveys (Josephine 5,400 sheets for Habsburgs; Cassini for France) and new roads/canals allowed dispersal, coordination, and rapid reassembly. These changes remained constrained until better infrastructure arrived.
**Links/taxonomy:** divisions enabled by infrastructure in [[france]]; preceded_by War of Austrian Succession innovations; part_of transition toward [[age-of-expansion]] mobility.

### Gribeauval's reforms created the first powerful, fully mobile field artillery
**Grounding quote:** "These changes in the art of war in the later eighteenth century were accompanied by the creation, for the first time, of a powerful yet fully mobile field artillery... Under the enlightened direction of Jean Baptiste de Gribeauval, the calibres, carriages and equipment of the French artillery were standardized... the weight of a French four-pounder field gun dropped from 1,300 pounds to only 600. At this weight, it could easily be drawn by only three horses (and handled by a mere eight men) and so could keep up even with the more mobile armies and divisions of the day."
**Locator:** Ch. 5
**Analysis:** Standardization, interchangeable parts, reduced powder charges, and lighter barrels (enabled by accurate casting) produced mobile field guns that could accompany divisions—distinct from the heavier, dual-purpose siege/field guns of Louis XIV's era.
**Links/taxonomy:** Gribeauval artillery produced by French technical advances in [[france]]; contributed_to more mobile strategy; preceded_by earlier siege-oriented artillery.

### Revolutionary France's mass armies constituted a further military revolution by breaking the trace italienne's stranglehold
**Grounding quote:** "Here at last was an army large enough to break the stranglehold of the trace italienne... This was clearly warfare on a totally different plane to anything previously seen in Europe... the scale of warfare was by then so totally transformed that it might be said that another 'military revolution' had occurred."
**Locator:** Ch. 5
**Analysis:** From ~150,000 royal troops (1788-9) to paper strengths over 1 million by 1794 via levee en masse (actual ~730,000 in 1794), France fielded forces (e.g., 600,000 with 1,146 guns invading Russia in 1812) that could encircle fortresses while still operating huge field armies. Napoleonic armies fought tactically like earlier ones but at unprecedented scale.
**Links/taxonomy:** [[france]] produced mass armies that followed_by Revolutionary/Napoleonic era; [[trace-italienne]] contrasts_with new scale (broke its previous dominance); [[military-revolution]] contains this second phase.

### Naval developments paralleled land changes and produced British global supremacy
**Grounding quote:** "The evolution of naval warfare was roughly similar... In 1789, there were perhaps 440 ships-of-the-line in Europe's navies, of which almost one-third (153) were British... But by 1810... the Royal Navy comprised over 1,000 purpose-built warships (243 of them ships-of-the-line) with a total displacement of 861,000 tons and a complement of 142,000 men. These, too, represented an almost irresistible concentration of force, which could be applied anywhere in the world."
**Locator:** Ch. 5
**Analysis:** Britain's lead shattered the late-seventeenth-century north-west European naval equilibrium, enabling worldwide power projection.
**Links/taxonomy:** British naval growth part_of transition to [[age-of-expansion]]; enabled Western hegemony.

### The sixteenth-seventeenth century military revolution ultimately produced Western global hegemony
**Grounding quote:** "Thanks above all to their military superiority, founded upon the military revolution of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, the Western nations had managed to create the first global hegemony in History."
**Locator:** Ch. 5
**Analysis:** Sustained European interstate competition, amplified by the revolution and its later extensions (light troops, divisions, artillery, mass armies, naval dominance), produced overwhelming force applied globally (examples include Opium War, Shimonoseki, American plains, African interior). Further technical thresholds (telegraph, railways, breech-loaders, ironclads) were required before even larger forces could operate effectively.
**Links/taxonomy:** [[military-revolution]] produced Western hegemony; [[age-of-expansion]] followed_by this outcome; enabled global projection against [[china]], [[japan]], [[americas]], [[ottoman-empire]].

**Range 5 Notes for reconciliation:** This range supplies Parker's explicit chronological verdict (culmination mid-18th c. with Frederick, not Roberts 1660) and frames post-1780s changes (light troops, divisions, Gribeauval, mass armies) as a distinct further threshold/bridge rather than the core revolution itself. It directly links back to [[trace-italienne]], [[frederick-the-great]], [[france]], and [[seven-years-war-1756]] while showing how the sixteenth-seventeenth century foundation enabled later global outcomes. ## Volume Synthesis Note

Parker's 1988 work is the single most cited statement of the "military revolution" as an explanation for both the rise of the fiscal-military state in Europe and the initial phase of European global dominance. By relocating the causal center from Roberts's tactical/ infantry reforms to the *trace italienne* and the resulting siege-logistics-army size spiral, Parker made the argument more robust against "tactics-only" critiques while simultaneously testing it worldwide.

The book is deliberately balanced: it asserts a real European transformation with global consequences, yet repeatedly documents the slowness, contingency, and limits of Western military superiority before the industrial era. The non-European chapters (Japan's rapid adoption and later rejection of firearms; Ottoman and Mughal gunpowder use without full European organizational package; Chinese scale overwhelming early European advantages) serve as built-in qualifications to any crude "guns = empire" narrative.

For the wiki, this source supplies the authoritative articulation of the Parker variant of the thesis (fortification + scale + state capacity + selective global projection) and the primary evidence base for the standing controversy page. It directly informs the early-modern section of the warfare hub and will be cited on any future hub analyses of 16th–18th century European campaigns and colonial expansion. Cross-references to Delbrück Vol. IV, Duffy *Siege Warfare*, and the West Point volumes are natural.

**Key enduring claims (synthesized):** 
- The bastioned fortress turned war into a contest of resources and engineering more than decisive battles.
- Army size increases (tenfold in many states) were the most measurable index and the heaviest burden.
- Naval broadside + line tactics gave Europeans a clearer edge at sea than on land in Asia.
- The "revolution" reached a plateau around the mid-eighteenth century; the next true rupture came with the French Revolutionary/Napoleonic armies and the industrial age.
