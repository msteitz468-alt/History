# Warfare and Society in the Barbarian West, 450–900

Guy Halsall's *Warfare and Society in the Barbarian West, 450–900* (London/New York:
Routledge, 2003) is the standard scholarly synthesis of war in western Europe between the
end of the Western [[late-antiquity|Roman Empire]] and the break-up of the
[[carolingian-empire|Carolingian Empire]]. It deliberately fills the gap between two
well-studied fields — the late-Roman army and the tenth-/eleventh-century world of knights
and castles — that the classic histories of medieval warfare (Oman, Delbrück, Verbruggen,
Contamine, Beeler) had passed over or treated as an undifferentiated "Dark Age."

## Author's Argument

Halsall advances two linked theses. The first is **methodological**: early medieval warfare
must be studied **"substantively"** — within the norms, values and mentalities of the
society practising it — not through the **"normalist"** assumption of timeless principles of
strategy, tactics and "inherent military probability" imported from other periods. The
sources are sparse and shaped by genre: early medieval writers, unlike classical ones, "did
not write in detail about warfare," because authority rested on moral and exegetical learning,
not empirical report, and because battles were "supposed to be won by divine favour, not human
agency." Reconstruction therefore demands a **multidisciplinary method** (written, archaeological
and pictorial evidence used critically and on its own terms) and constant **contextualisation by
date and place** — against the long tradition of treating the whole 450-year period as a static
whole.

The second thesis is **substantive and social**: warfare was central to early medieval society,
politics and identity, and its forms **changed dynamically over time** — Halsall locates major
transformations **around 600** and again **in the ninth century**. War is best understood through
its broad social and economic context, because that context is far better evidenced than military
specifics. The way armies were **raised** is the hinge linking war to state power: a state that
extracted **tax** could pay a standing army and dominate its aristocracy; a state that raised
troops by **obligation** from landholders and their followings depended on aristocratic consent
and was correspondingly weaker. The shift from the former (late Rome) toward the latter is the
master-process of the period.

Other distinctive arguments: much warfare was **endemic, small-scale, ritualised and limited** —
a "grammar of warfare" through which polities communicated and maintained a status quo (see
[[grammar-of-warfare]]); armies were **small** (hundreds to low thousands, not the tens of
thousands of the sources); **ravaging and devastation**, not pitched battle, were the normal
strategic instruments, and battle was often sought or avoided for reasons (ordeal, honour, divine
judgement) alien to modern strategic "rationality"; and the old **Brunner/Lynn White "stirrup
thesis"** — that Poitiers/Tours (732) and the stirrup created battle-winning shock cavalry — is
rejected.

## Scope and Coverage

- **Chronology.** c. 450 (the messy end of the Western Empire — Visigothic forces in Gaul from
  ~418, Frankish polities from the 450s) to c. 900 (deposition of Charles the Fat; death of
  Alfred; the threshold of the age of knights and castles).
- **Geography.** Post-Roman Britain, Anglo-Saxon and "Celtic" kingdoms, Ireland, Visigothic and
  post-conquest Christian Spain, Merovingian and Carolingian Gaul, Ostrogothic/Lombard/Carolingian
  Italy, Germany and Scandinavia. Frankocentric by the author's own admission; Muslim Spain and
  Byzantium largely excluded.
- **Structure (thematic, not battle-by-battle).** Ch. 1 Warfare and violence; Ch. 2 Warfare and
  society; Ch. 3–6 Raising an army (post-Roman; Carolingian; allies/mercenaries/training; the size
  of armies); Ch. 7 Campaigning; Ch. 8 Weaponry and equipment; Ch. 9 Battle; Ch. 10 Fortification
  and siege warfare; Epilogue; Appendix (Frankish military activity 581–90).

## Volume Synthesis Note

Halsall's volume is the wiki's anchor for **how war was actually organised and fought** in the
post-Roman West, and it slots into three existing debates. First, on the **fall of Rome**: the book
is the military companion to Halsall's "migrations as result, not cause" position (NCMH I) —
warfare and the means of raising armies are the mechanism by which the dissolving Roman
fiscal-military state remade frontier and provincial society. Second, against the **continuity
optimists** (notably Bernard Bachrach), Halsall argues for small armies, limited siegecraft, and
the rejection of a smooth Roman-to-Carolingian military professionalism — a tension the wiki should
preserve rather than resolve. Third, he **overturns the textbook "stirrup → shock cavalry →
feudalism" causal chain** (Brunner / Lynn White Jr.), realigning the [[carolingian-empire|Carolingian]]
military story with the rise of aristocratic followings and *beneficia* around 700 rather than a
technological revolution at Poitiers.

The volume's deepest contribution to the wiki is **methodological**: its insistence that early
medieval warfare changed over time (transformations ~600 and in the ninth century), that it must be
read "substantively" from a multidisciplinary evidence base, and that the way an army was raised is
the single most revealing index of how power worked — see [[grammar-of-warfare]]. Its acknowledged
limit is its Frankocentrism and its deliberate exclusion of Byzantium (a companion volume) and
Muslim Spain, which the wiki notes where Halsall's generalisations reach those regions.
