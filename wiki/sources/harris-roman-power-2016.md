# Roman Power: A Thousand Years of Empire

W. V. Harris, *Roman Power: A Thousand Years of Empire* (Cambridge: Cambridge
University Press, 2016). A single-author analytical history of the waxing and
waning of Roman imperial power, integrating **external** domination (over
neighbours and subjects) with **internal** power structures (political, social,
gender, economic, ideological) across roughly a thousand years — from the
mid-Republic (from Camillus / Veii, c.390s BC) to the failure of the eastern
empire as an empire after the Battle of the Yarmuk and the reigns of Heraclius
and Constans II (AD 636–68).

## Central Argument

Harris contends that Roman external power and internal power must be studied
together, and that **cross-period comparison within Roman history** (mid-Republic
vs high Empire vs late antiquity) illuminates both the growth and the shrinkage
of Roman power more clearly than premature period cut-offs (Constantine, 410,
476, or 602). He periodizes the long arc at **AD 16** (Tiberius slows expansion
and finishes the absolute monarchy) and **AD 337** (Constantine's death; internal
cohesion begins to fray; factors of western collapse become visible in the next
generations). The Roman state remains an empire through Justinian; it ceases to
be one, in Harris's definition, after the Muslim conquest of the Syrian provinces
and the disasters of 636–42.

Three analytical dimensions run through the book: (1) national / imperial power
over non-Romans and along the frontiers; (2) social differentiation (class,
slavery, gender, family) and political institutions; (3) time and discontinuity
across the millennium. Continuities he stresses include **slavery** (Webster's
guesstimate of >100 million enslaved across the millennium is quoted) and elite
co-optation of local men of property. He criticizes modern "power-worship" and
"vicarious identification" with Justinian's empire (after Brent Shaw) as obstacles
to clear analysis of both republican imperialism and late-antique failure.

## Distinctive Positions (Harris, not wiki voice)

- Republican expansion **is** imperialism; reluctance to use the word is evasion.
- Mid-republican Rome: warlike, aggressive, high citizen military participation;
  organizational techniques (colonies, roads, citizenship, elite co-optation)
  sustained control of Italy and then the Mediterranean.
- High Empire: expansion decelerates largely because emperors fear successful
  commanders and the fiscal burden of large armies; military service falls to
  ~4% of citizen males (vs ≥50% mid-second century BC guesstimate); durability
  and docility are the historical puzzles (co-optation + idea of Rome + economic
  interests of many under the peace).
- Western contraction (c.370s–430s/455) and eastern contraction (c.560s–636/41)
  are **separate chains of events** that must be analysed separately.
- Justinian's reconquests made an over-extended empire that later rulers could not
  defend ("Justinian's Folly"); Muslim militarization, Christian doctrinal
  division, poor generalship/discipline, and fiscal–military vicious circles all
  figure in the seventh-century failure.
- Gender power, social power, and the power of ideas (including Christianity after
  Constantine's privileges) are first-class analytical categories, not appendices.

## Volume Synthesis Note

Harris supplies the wiki’s first full-arc, single-author **power analysis** linking
external domination to internal structures from the mid-Republic through Heraclius. The
ingest is deliberately **interpretive-layer**: the Roman backbone was already dense
(Heather, Scheidel CEHGRW, Ball, Luttwak, CAH, fall controversies). Distinctive
payoffs are (1) the periodization pair **AD 16 / AD 337** and the claim that the empire
as empire ends after **Yarmuk 636–42**; (2) restatement of aggressive republican
imperialism inside formal/informal empire and organizational-technique language;
(3) the high-empire **durability and docility** problem (co-optation + idea of Rome +
economic interests under the peace, against short-lived emperors); (4) **MPR collapse**
(~50% mid-second century BC → ~4% mid-second century AD) as a measure of
demilitarization; (5) two **separate** late-antique contractions, with Justinian’s
reconquests as over-extension (“Justinian’s Folly”) rather than heroic restoration.
Harris sits as a multi-factor internal-plus-external position on western fall (contra
pure Heather exogenous trigger; overlapping fiscal spiral and army-quality critiques)
and as a seventh-century end-point that partly converges with Pirenne’s chronology
while rejecting Gibbonian continuity to 1453 as *empire*. Cross-check against Heather
on barbarian agency, Wickham on fiscal-aristocratic aftermath, and Byzantine specialists
on whether post-636 Romania remains an empire.
