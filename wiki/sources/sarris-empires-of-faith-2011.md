# Empires of Faith: The Fall of Rome to the Rise of Islam, 500–700

Peter Sarris's volume in the **Oxford History of Medieval Europe** (series editors
Henrietta Leyser and Jinty Nelson) is a single-author synthesis of the two centuries
that "did most to define the medieval world as commonly understood" — the demise of the
western Roman state and the rise of a culture of **military lordship**; the recasting of
the surviving eastern empire into **[[byzantine-empire|Byzantium]]**; and the formation
across the southern and eastern Mediterranean of a **sophisticated Islamic rival** that
gave sharper definition to an embryonic Christendom. The dates 500 and 700 are, Sarris
concedes, not in themselves significant — the book is an argument about a *transformation*,
not a chronicle bounded by events.

It is one of the collection's anchor secondary works for **[[late-antiquity|Late
Antiquity]]** and the **[[early-middle-ages|Early Middle Ages]]**, and (per CLAUDE.md's
hybrid-depth rule) was ingested with **deep section reads of Sarris's distinctive
arguments** and lighter touch on well-trodden narrative already richly covered in the
wiki. Top_100 Structural Sources **#74**.

## What this source adds to the wiki

**1. A fiscal-materialist account of Rome's fall and of post-Roman economic change.**
Following Jairus Banaji, Sarris makes the **late-Roman monetary fiscal system** the
motor of the period's "economic sophistication": by minting vast quantities of gold and
copper, paying army and bureaucracy in coin, and demanding taxes in coin, the state
catalysed a deep monetisation that gave the late-Roman world urban and commercial levels
"not seen again in parts of western Europe until the seventeenth century." The corollary
is his distinctive thesis of **de-monetisation**: as militarised landowners came to
equate tax-exemption with elite status and provincial defence devolved onto barbarian
war-bands, the costly fiscal machinery was abandoned, coinage drained away, urban life
contracted, and aristocrats retreated from *civitas* to *villa*. → new page
[[demonetisation-of-the-post-roman-west]]; updates to [[fall-of-rome-causes]].

**2. A "maximalist" reading of the [[justinianic-plague-541|Justinianic plague]].**
Sarris (a leading maximalist) makes the bubonic plague of 541 a structural turning point
that *simultaneously* shattered the aristocracy's grip on labour (wages rose; the
gold:copper ratio moved in the poor's favour; tenants won better leases) and gutted the
state's fiscal base — forcing debasement and the retrenchment of military pay. → updates
to the plague event page; new [[justinianic-plague-scale|controversy on plague scale]].

**3. The Eurasian/steppe frame.** Rome and Persia are read as two sedentary empires
whose fates were repeatedly set by the **west Eurasian steppe** — the Huns, the
**invitation of the Avars by Justinian (558)**, the rise of the **Turk Khaganate**
(which, by threatening Persia's eastern flank, handed [[justinian|Justinian]] his
Caucasian settlement and later gave [[heraclius|Heraclius]] the alliance that won the
Persian war). → updates to Justinian, Khusro I, Heraclius.

**4. Religion as state power — and the convergence of holy war.** The title thesis: from
Aurelian's Sol Invictus through Constantine to Heraclius' *basileus pistos en Christô*,
imperial power and confessional faith fuse; and the **Christian holy war** Heraclius
enunciated against Persia in the 620s appears to have **percolated into Arabia** at the
very moment Muḥammad preached *jihād* and martyrdom. Sarris stresses an early **umma that
was neither purely Muslim nor purely Arab** — the Constitution of Medina as a coalition
of monotheist fighting men — read through the **contemporary non-Arabic witnesses**
(the *Doctrina Iacobi*, the Syriac chronicles, the Armenian *History* of Sebeos). →
updates to [[muhammad-and-the-rise-of-islam]], [[heraclius]].

## Volume Synthesis Note

The book's organising argument is **comparative and structural**: a single set of
forces — warfare, fiscal pressure, plague, the rise of military lordship — works itself
out differently across the post-Roman space. Its sharpest formulation comes in Chapter 8:
in the fifth-century West (and again in the seventh-century Near East) **the late-Roman
aristocracy survived the military crisis largely at the expense of the late-Roman
state**, whereas in seventh-century Anatolia **the state survived largely at the expense
of the aristocracy** — the great commercialised estates shattered by Arab raiding, their
lands recycled into military holdings, leaving the most tightly governed territory "west
of China." From the same processes flow both the slave's shackle and the coastal *wic*:
the Epilogue closes on a north-western Europe "piecing itself back together" around a
landed military nobility, an expanding silver economy, and the first economic growth
since the fourth century — the world of [[charlemagne|Carolingian]] Europe in embryo.

Against the wiki's existing poles, Sarris is a **materialist transformationist who keeps
the violence**: with Brown and Wickham he sees deep continuities and a *transformation*
rather than a morality-tale "fall," but with Ward-Perkins and Heather he insists the
economic regression was real, brutal, and in lowland Britain near-total. He gives
**Banaji-style fiscal mechanics** the explanatory weight that Halsall gives state-
collapse and Harper gives climate and disease — complementary rather than contradictory
accounts that the [[fall-of-rome-causes]] controversy page now holds side by side. The
one direct tension with already-ingested material is emphasis, not fact: where
[[cameron-cah-v14-2000|CAH XIV]] (Conrad) relocates the explanation of Islam's rise to
Mecca's shrine-and-pilgrimage function and treats the Arabic tradition cautiously,
Sarris foregrounds the **external, imperial, holy-war context** and the ecumenical early
umma — two angles on the same methodologically contested problem.
