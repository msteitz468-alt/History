# Cannae: Hannibal's Greatest Victory (Adrian Goldsworthy, 2001)

This is a focused modern study of one of the most famous and tactically perfect battles in history: Hannibal's double envelopment of a much larger Roman army at Cannae in 216 BCE. Goldsworthy places the battle firmly in the context of the Second Punic War, ancient warfare practices, and the fundamental asymmetry between Hannibal's operational brilliance and his strategic limitations.

The book provides exceptionally clear reconstructions of army organisation, the campaign leading to the battle, the precise mechanics of the engagement, the numbers debate, and why the greatest tactical victory of the ancient world did not win the war for Carthage.

## Framework and Value

- Primary modern tactical reference for the canonical example of **double envelopment / battle of annihilation**.
- Strong emphasis on the **limits of tactics**: Cannae demonstrates both the possibility of near-perfect execution and the reality that even total battlefield destruction may not achieve political/strategic ends.
- Detailed source criticism: Polybius as the best narrative (analytical, with military experience context); Livy for Roman political reaction and aftermath but more dramatic/patriotic.
- Valuable appendices on army strengths, losses on the march to Italy, and casualty figures.
- Complements the existing high-resolution battle analysis in the warfare hub and the broader Second Punic War coverage.

## Consolidated Volume Synthesis

Adrian Goldsworthy’s *Cannae* is the single best modern tactical and operational study of the battle. It excels at:

- Rigorous source criticism (Polybius as sober analyst preferred for the fighting itself; Livy for Roman political/moral reaction and full aftermath).
- Transparent numbers debate (Polybius’ post-Alps inscription figure most reliable for Hannibal; Roman strength variants explained; casualty totals compared with practical assessment).
- Precise mechanics of the double envelopment: the bulged centre as deliberate bait, Libyan reserves held fresh for the wheel, Hasdrubal’s exceptional discipline in re-forming and shifting to the rear (the decisive non-pursuit), Roman self-compression from their own deep formation choices.
- Emphasis on contingency and friction: even “perfect” tactics required many things to go exactly right (timing of centre collapse, cavalry control, Roman commitment to the trap).
- Strategic context: why the greatest battlefield annihilation in the ancient world did not win the war — Roman manpower depth, refusal to follow classical “rules” of war termination, Hannibal’s lack of siege capability and reinforcement, and the difference between destroying field armies and breaking a confederation.
- The human and organisational realities of ancient combat (tentative front-rank duels more than Hollywood masses; officer leadership critical; fatigue and unit disintegration decisive in the end).
