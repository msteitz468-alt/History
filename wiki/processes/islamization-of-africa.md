# The Islamization of Africa

The spread of **Islam across Africa** — beginning with the Arab conquest of **Egypt (639–642)** and, over
the following centuries, transforming North Africa, the Sahara, the West African Sahel, and the East
African coast — was, in the **UNESCO [[gha-vol3-7th-11th-century|General History of Africa, Vol. III]]**,
**"the most consequential development of these centuries from the African perspective."** This page is the
wiki's anchor for that process; it is the African dimension of the wider spread of [[islam|Islam]].

## Not by the sword: how Islam actually spread

GHA III (El Fasi, Hrbek, Dramani-Issifou) stresses that Islamization was **overwhelmingly gradual and
non-coercive**. Islamic law discouraged forced conversion of "Peoples of the Book," and the early
**Umayyad** state actively *discouraged* mass conversion (converts stopped paying the *jizya* poll-tax,
eroding revenue). Conversion came instead from a **mix of motives** — sincere belief, the **social and
fiscal advantages** of joining the dominant community, intermarriage, Bedouin immigration, the decay of
existing churches, and the patient work of merchant and Sufi missionaries. The long survival of **Christian
Berber and Coptic communities** for centuries after the conquest is, GHA argues, a "strong argument against
forced conversion."

## Three great channels

The volume distinguishes the routes by which Islam entered the continent:

1. **North Africa — conquest then slow conversion.** Egypt fell quickly (639–642), but its **Coptic**
   majority persisted for centuries, becoming Muslim only gradually (and never wholly). In the **Maghrib**,
   fierce **[[berbers|Berber]]** resistance (and the foundation of **Kayrawan**, 670, as a radiating
   centre) gave way to an uneven Islamization — Christian enclaves lingered into the later Middle Ages — and
   the Berbers' independent streak (earlier expressed in **Donatism**) reasserted itself in their own
   **Kharijite** and then reforming Islamic movements (the [[almoravids|Almoravids]]).
2. **The Sudan — conversion through the [[trans-saharan-trade|gold trade]].** Islam crossed the Sahara not
   with armies but with **merchants**: the Maghrib's hunger for West African gold drew Muslim traders to the
   Sahel, where ruling elites of states like **[[ghana-empire|Ghana]]** and Kanem adopted Islam (or
   tolerated Muslim merchant quarters) for the commercial, diplomatic, and literate-administrative
   advantages it brought — long before their general populations converted.
3. **The East African coast — the Indian Ocean.** Muslim traders from Arabia and the Gulf settling along
   the **[[swahili-coast|Swahili coast]]** and the Comoros seeded the Islamic, Kiswahili-speaking merchant
   civilization that would define the coast.

## Consequences

- A **Muslim North Africa** integrated into the Islamic world-economy; the Sahel states and the Swahili
  coast tied into the same ecumene of trade, law (the *sharia*), pilgrimage, and **Arabic literacy** — which
  gave Africa some of its first written documentary history.
- A durable **religious frontier**: Christian **[[christian-nubia|Nubia]]** and **[[aksum|Ethiopia]]**
  survived as Christian states amid the Islamic expansion (Nubia via the Baqt treaty), while the desert and
  Sahel became lastingly Muslim.
- The foundations of the later great Muslim empires of **[[mali-empire|Mali]]** and Songhai.
