## Definition and Scope

**Domestication** is the process by which plants or animals are separated from
their wild populations and genetically altered — through human protection,
selection, and manipulation — to the point that they lose the ability to survive
and reproduce without human intervention (Barker & Goucher, after the OED). It
is the biological engine of [[agricultural-origins]]: the slow, often
unintentional reshaping of the genetic make-up of target species toward traits
that were more productive, more storable, or more pleasing.

Markers of plant domestication include loss of natural seed dispersal (e.g.
non-shattering cereal rachis), larger seeds, loss of dormancy, and tougher
attachment — changes that make a plant dependent on humans to reproduce. Animal
domestication markers include body-size reduction, herd demographic profiles
skewed by human culling, and (later) breed diversification.

## Mutual / two-way domestication

A central theme of this volume: **domestication is not a one-way exercise of
human control.** When plants, animals, and humans are brought into sustained
close proximity, all three are altered. Humans were "domesticated" too — in
diet, disease load, body, settlement pattern, labour regime, and social
structure (the seedbed of inequality). Barker & Goucher call this the "mutual
domestication process," and the case study of 'Ain Ghazal (Cycle 3) reads
changes in the built environment as evidence of humans being reshaped by the
relationship as much as reshaping it. This reframes domestication from a
technological achievement into a *coevolutionary* one.

## Pre-domestication cultivation

Domestication was preceded, often by centuries or millennia, by
**pre-domestication cultivation**: foragers sowing, tending, weeding, and
harvesting still-*wild* plants. This is documented for wheat and barley in
Southwest Asia and for millet and rice in China. It means the morphological
"moment" of domestication lags well behind the *behavioural* commitment to
managing plants — one reason the foraging/farming binary breaks down.

## "Failed" and reversible domestications

Not every experiment succeeded. The volume notes hunter-gatherers whose
management of plants and animals was an "unsuccessful moment of
intensification," creating temporary farmers/herders who later reverted to
foraging; and species once managed that are today regarded as weeds or wild
game. Domestication was a field of experiment, not a one-way ratchet.

## Geographic and taxonomic spread

Independent domestications produced distinct packages: wheat/barley/sheep/goat
(SW Asia); millet, rice, pig, soybean (China); maize/teosinte, beans, squash
(Mesoamerica); manioc (Amazonia); quinoa + llama/alpaca (Andes);
squash/chenopod/sunflower (eastern North America); sorghum, African rice, tef,
cowpea, oil palm, Sahelian cattle (Africa); banana, taro, sugarcane (New
Guinea); the zebu (South Asia). Domesticates then travelled far beyond their
hearths via [[agricultural-origins|diffusion and exchange]], often decoupled
from the people who first bred them — a process the editors call "food
globalization."

## Southwest Asian founder domesticates (Fagan & Durrani 2020)

DNA evidence points to the **Karacadağ Mountains** of southeastern Turkey as the
likely origin area of the earliest cereal domesticate, **einkorn wheat**;
rachis-toughening (the key non-shattering trait) could occur within as little as
**20–30 generations** once human selection began — a strikingly rapid
morphological shift. **Goats and sheep** were the first Southwest Asian animal
domesticates, by ~9500 BCE; **cattle** (*Bos primigenius*) were domesticated from
small local female stock in the Near East/southeastern Anatolia (per mtDNA
evidence) by ~8000 BCE, consistent with Andrew Smith's arid-herding model.
(Fagan & Durrani 2020)

## End Conditions

Ongoing. Domestication did not stop in the Neolithic — new species, breeds, and
cultivars continue to be created — but the foundational crop and livestock
packages on which world agriculture still depends were largely established in
the periods this volume covers.

## Mesoamerican animal domestication late; plant focus (CHNPA II.1)

Zeitlin & Zeitlin (CHNPA Vol. II Part 1, Ch. 2) stress that turkey, muscovy duck,
honeybee, and dog are not represented in significant numbers archaeologically
before full-time farming villages. Dog — the earliest New World animal domesticate —
appears only in small numbers in Late Archaic Tehuacan fauna; later abundance
suggests dogs were kept primarily as **food** after agricultural dependence, not as
hunter-gatherer companions. Early Mesoamerican domestication history thus focuses on
**plant production** by Archaic cultivators (see [[maize-domestication]],
[[early-agriculture-americas]], [[paleoindian-and-archaic-mesoamerica]]). This
reinforces the page-level contrast with Old World herd-animal packages and the limited
[[secondary-products-revolution]] in the Americas outside Andean camelids.
