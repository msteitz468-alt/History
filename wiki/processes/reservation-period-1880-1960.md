# Reservation Period, 1880–1960

Allotment, boarding schools, the Indian Reorganization Act, termination, and pan-Indian politics across continental United States and southern Canada. Principal synthesis: Frederick E. Hoxie, CHNPA I.2 ch. 12.

## Definition and Scope

Hoxie frames 1880–1960 as the reservation/reserve era: Native homelands defined as “reserves” (Canada) or “reservations” (United States). Whether reserved by Native peoples in formal negotiations or assigned by national authorities, they normally represented what remained of the original Native domain and contained religious and historic sites, community resources, and institutions. Borders between reserves and surrounding nations were cultural and political frontiers of exchange, negotiation, and conflict.

By 1900 most Indians in both countries were surrounded by outsiders: Canada ~100,000 Natives (1.3% of national population) on 5 million acres in 1,500 reserves; United States ~250,000 Natives among nearly 76 million others on 200 reservations totaling nearly 72 million acres.

## Causal Drivers

- **No remaining “beyond the settlement line.”** For the first time, neither country could remove Natives to unsettled zones. Assault on lands and resources therefore combined with an unprecedented campaign to “civilize” Indian nations; extension of “civilized” settlement was linked to national identities that excluded Indian cultures (contributed_to: [[allotment-policy]]; contributed_to: [[us-civilization-program]]).
- **Industrial transport and communication.** In 1880 most Native communities (Oklahoma, New Mexico, Arizona; border west of Lake Superior to Puget Sound) lay beyond steel rails. By 1960 most Native people outside the Far North could not avoid regular non-Indian contact via rail, highways, and air. Transport enabled non-Indian intrusion (missionaries, business, uniform policies) *and* unprecedented Native-to-Native communication that enabled: [[pan-indian-movement]].
- **Rail-driven land pressure.** During the 1880s Colorado Utes, Minnesota Ojibwas, Lakota Sioux, Montana Crows/Blackfeet/Assiniboines, interior Salishes, Bannocks and Shoshones of Idaho, and several Oklahoma peoples lost over **70 million acres** through imposed land-sale agreements. Southern Pacific (1881), Northern Pacific (1883), and Great Northern (1892) spelled end of buffalo then arrival of non-Indian cattlemen and farmers. Canadian Plains: by 1880 over 25 million acres of former Indian land ceded to the CPR.

## Major Phases

### 1. The Assault, 1880–1900
- Massive US land cessions and allotment; Canada smaller reserves with less “surplus” sale but potlatch ban (1884), North-West Mounted Police, Indian Act control.
- Courts of Indian Offenses (US, 1883) and reservation police (from 1878) enforced work, school attendance, and abandonment of tradition.
- [[dawes-act-1887]] as principal vehicle for non-Indian access to Indian lands until 1934.
- Armed and religious crises: Northwest Rebellion 1885; Apache POW exile; [[ghost-dance-1890]] and [[wounded-knee-1890]] at [[pine-ridge-reservation]]; California dispossession (Warner’s Ranch 1903 march to Pala).
- Oklahoma: 1890 Territory organized from Creek/Seminole purchases; 1907 statehood with no reservations and non-Indians outnumbering Natives seven to one (Curtis Act, not Dawes, for Indian Territory).

### 2. Re-creations of Native life, 1900–1920
- Religious and cultural innovation (peyote / Native American Church; continued Ghost Dance among some groups; Grass Dance at multigroup gatherings).
- Early intertribal political organizations (Alaska Native Brotherhood 1912; Black Hills Council 1911; All Pueblo Council 1922; Society of American Indians 1911).
- Economic subordination: fee patents peak 1915–20 (>20,000 forced fee patents); leasing of fertile Crow, Yakima, Papago lands; Canadian Commissioner Hayter Reed’s peasant-farming program devastated Prairie agriculture (1889–97).
- *Lone Wolf v. Hitchcock* (1903) held Congress could abrogate treaty provisions—enabling unconsented disposals (related: [[treaty-sovereignty-native-north-america]]).

### 3. Age of public reorganization, 1920–1940
- Meriam Report (1928) documented health, income, and boarding-school food crisis; blamed allotment as origin of bleakness; contributed_to: [[indian-reorganization-act-1934]].
- Indian Citizenship Act 1924 (dual citizenship reading; some Haudenosaunee and Pueblo resistance).
- Canadian 1920 compulsory enfranchisement bill (Scott: absorb “until there is not a single Indian”) passed then repealed late 1921—analogous_to: [[termination-policy]] absorption logic; 1927 statute criminalized fundraising for Indian rights litigation.
- Collier first-year reforms then [[indian-reorganization-act-1934]]: 164 communities organized, 94 rejected; land restoration modest (2.75M acres 1934–40, mostly public domain/submarginal; only 20% purchase/exchange).
- Navajo stock reduction and IRA rejection; New Deal day-school shift from boarding model.

### 4. Survival in an urban world, 1940–1960
- WWII: ~25,000 US Indians in forces; perhaps 50,000 left home for defense work; Canada up to 6,000 in forces.
- Dams: Grand Coulee destroyed Columbia fishing sites (Yakima, Colville); Pick-Sloan Missouri dams destroyed over 550 square miles of Indian land.
- [[termination-era-1953]] / [[termination-policy]]: HCR 108, PL 280; >13,000 members and 1.3 million acres “freed” by Nixon’s 1970 renunciation.
- Urban relocation: BIA branch 1954; >30,000 supervised moves by 1961; of ~122,000 urban migrants 1940–60 government assisted ≤¼; pan-Indian urban centers.
- Indian Claims Commission (1946–78): 370 petitions / 615 dockets; awards >$818 million.
- NCAI (1944) and homeland-nationalism language defeated termination as “in Indians’ best interests.”

## Geographic Spread

Continental United States and southern Canada (Hoxie defers Arctic/Subarctic). Regional patterns varied sharply:

| Region | Pattern |
|---|---|
| Great Lakes / Ojibwa | 80-acre allotment; surplus sale; timber contracts (1888: 731 contracts, >190 million board feet); up to half reservation land to Euro-Americans |
| Plains US | 70+ million acres 1880s cessions; Ghost Dance / Wounded Knee; ranch leases (e.g. 865,000 acres on Standing Rock 1902) |
| Canadian Plains | CPR cessions; buffalo collapse; starvation; Reed anti-machinery farming policy |
| Southwest | Navajo rebound, little major cession; Hopi–Navajo grazing contest; later stock reduction |
| Northwest Coast | Potlatch ban Canada; US allotment on many interior WA/OR reserves (~3M acres lost in Northwest over ~30 years post-Dawes) |
| Oklahoma / Indian Territory | Curtis Act breakup; statehood 1907 without reservations |
| California | Title-less communities; Warner’s Ranch eviction 1903 |
| East | Mashpee land opening 1869; Lumbee state recognition 1885 |

## End Conditions

Hoxie’s thesis: despite domination instruments of two modern nation-states bent on cultural destruction, inventiveness and endurance preserved communities “altered but whole.” Early twentieth-century religious, cultural, and political reorganization laid foundations of modern Indian nationalism even as economics remained peripheral and dependent. By 1960 Native communities had legal and political capacity unimaginable in 1880; termination’s defeat by Native-led organizations closed the era’s policy apex and opened the [[native-american-renaissance-1960-1995]].

## Historiography

Hoxie (1996) is a synthetic chapter, not a primary-source monograph: he integrates US–Canada comparison, stresses dual character of industrial modernity (conquest *and* Native-to-Native communication), and treats reserves as cultural/political frontiers rather than mere cages. Allotment acreage figures (~90 million acres Dawes-era loss; 70+ million 1880s cessions) rest on 1930s federal accounting and contemporary administrative record as he reports them. Wounded Knee first-shot dispute is left open. Meriam statistics are report figures, not re-audited. Arctic/Subarctic deliberately deferred. Later Washburn (ch. 15) frames post-1960 “renaissance” as the sequel; termination restoration (Menominee 1973, Klamath later) completes the narrative beyond Hoxie’s close.
