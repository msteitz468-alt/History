## Definition and Scope

Southeast Asian nationalism as a modernist reform process refers to the organized, ideologically self-conscious movements that emerged from approximately the 1880s and sought to transform or end colonial rule, primarily on the basis of the territorial units defined by colonial administrations. Chapter 5 of [[tarling-cambridge-history-southeast-asia-v2-1992]] distinguishes this from both religious anti-colonial resistance (see [[religion-and-anticolonialism-southeast-asia]]) and from ethnic nationalism based on cultural community. The chapter's analytical frame draws explicitly on Benedict Anderson's *Imagined Communities* (1983) and Anthony D. Smith's *Theories of Nationalism* (1971).

Three types of political movement emerged in the period 1900–1940:

1. **Territorial nationalism**: based on the colonial administrative units, directed at taking control of a colonial state — the dominant form in the Philippines, Indonesia, Vietnam, and Burma
2. **Ethnic nationalism**: based on shared cultural features, emerging in Siam (as state-sponsored official nationalism), among Malays, Karen, Moros, and other minorities
3. **The loyalist option**: communities that perceived advantages in the colonial relationship — Eurasians, Ambonese, Chinese, and minority ethnic groups fearful of majority domination

The process was "modernist" in that it accepted Western bureaucratic and organizational forms even while opposing Western rule, and drew its leadership overwhelmingly from the tiny educated elite produced by colonial schools.

## Causal Drivers

The common causal conditions across the region were:

- **Expansion of education**: colonial schools produced a small but politically consequential educated stratum whose aspirations outran the opportunities the colonial system offered
- **Print media**: indigenous-language newspapers and pamphlets disseminated ideas across linguistic communities (La Solidaridad in the Philippines; the Malay press in Malaya; Indonesian periodicals)
- **External models**: Japan's victory over Russia (1904–05), the Chinese Revolution of 1911, Indian nationalism (especially Gandhi and the Congress), and Marxist-Leninist theory via the Comintern all furnished organizational and ideological templates
- **Economic grievances**: the Great Depression of the 1930s accelerated radicalization across the region by destroying agricultural export economies on which colonial subjects depended
- **Colonial unresponsiveness**: the failure of colonial regimes to make meaningful concessions drove reform-oriented movements toward non-cooperation and confrontation by the 1920s and 1930s

## Major Phases

### The Philippines: First and Most Distinctive Case

The Philippines produced the first major territorial nationalist movement in Southeast Asia, with structural peculiarities that set it apart. The *ilustrado* (educated elite) Propaganda Movement of 1880–95 — centered on the fortnightly newspaper *La Solidaridad* and on the novels of [[jose-rizal]] (*Noli Me Tangere* and *El Filibusterismo*) — initially sought representation for the Philippines in the Spanish Cortes rather than independence, reflecting nineteenth-century liberal rather than nationalist goals. Rizal famously wrote in 1887: "in the present circumstances we want no separation from Spain; all we demand is more care, better instruction, better officials, one or two representatives, and more security for ourselves and our property."

The execution of three reformist Filipino priests in 1872 (Burgos, Gomez, Zamora) shifted the focus from clerical to colonial grievances and radicalized subsequent generations. Rizal's own trajectory — from liberal reformism to founding the Liga Filipina (1892) — was cut short by his arrest and exile to Mindanao; the subsequent founding of the [[katipunan-1892|Katipunan]] by Andres Bonifacio produced the 1896 insurrection.

Under American rule (from 1899), the Philippines diverged sharply from the rest of the region: the US gave Filipinos substantial and increasing administrative participation, stated independence as the explicit long-term goal, and made education widely available. The Partido Nacionalista dominated Philippine politics throughout the American period but, as Theodore Friend observed, "the Philippine political party was unideological and only loosely institutionalized, tending to form around charismatic persons rather than special programs." Independence was achieved through the Tydings-McDuffie Act (1934), establishing the Philippine Commonwealth in 1935 under Manuel Quezon. Historiography has disputed whether the elite leadership (Quezon, Osmeña, Roxas) genuinely pursued independence or exploited it for class advantage — Norman Owen argued that "wealthy agriculturalists succeeded in defining their own interests as those of the Philippines."

### Netherlands East Indies (Indonesia): Mass Movements and Repression

Indonesian nationalism developed in four phases after 1900, shaped by the Dutch Ethical Policy of 1901 (which briefly liberalized indigenous participation) and then by successive rounds of repression.

**Phase 1 (1900–18): co-operative activity.** The STOVIA-trained founders of Budi Utomo (1908) launched the first indigenous political organization, though it quickly became conservative. Sarekat Islam (1912), with a claimed membership exceeding two million, was the first mass organization, combining Islamic identity with economic grievance; its charismatic leader Tjokroaminoto was identified with the messianic *ratu adil* tradition. Muhammadiyah (1912) pursued Islamic modernization through education rather than politics. The Nationale Indische Partij (1912) called for racial equality and independence and was suppressed within a year.

**Phase 2 (1918–26): radicalization.** The Marxist ISDV (later PKI, Indonesian Communist Party), founded by the Dutch Hendricus Sneevliet, infiltrated Sarekat Islam, producing a "Section B" secret revolutionary apparatus. After its expulsion from Sarekat Islam (1921), the PKI launched abortive uprisings in Banten (1926) and west Sumatra (1927). These were quickly suppressed; the Dutch effectively destroyed the PKI as a pre-war force.

**Phase 3 (1927–31): PNI and non-cooperation.** Students in the Netherlands organized the Perhimpunan Indonesia (Indonesian Association) under Mohammad Hatta, which coined the term "Indonesia" and developed a non-cooperative independence ideology. Within Indonesia, the General Study Club of Bandung — which included [[sukarno]] — formed the Perserikatan Nasional Indonesia (PNI, 1927). Sukarno argued for a synthesis of nationalism, Islam, and Marxism, and used mass oratory to build a following. The Dutch arrested and convicted Sukarno in 1929, sentencing him to four years (served two).

**Phase 4 (1931–40): grudging co-operation.** After Sukarno's conviction and the dissolution of the PNI, moderate co-operative groups (Parindra, Gerindo) dominated. The umbrella GAPI federation (1939) offered Dutch-Indonesian co-operation against fascism in exchange for a promise of autonomy, but the Netherlands repeatedly rebuffed these proposals. By the eve of the Japanese invasion, most prominent nationalist leaders had been detained or banished.

### French Indochina (Vietnam): Confucian Transition to Communist Leadership

Vietnamese anti-colonialism had deep roots in the Can Vuong monarchist resistance of 1885–95 (see [[religion-and-anticolonialism-southeast-asia]]), but the development of modernist nationalism was complicated by the difficulty of drawing a line between "traditional patriotism" and modern nationalism in a country with centuries of resistance to Chinese hegemony.

The early twentieth century saw Confucian scholar-activists — Phan Boi Chau (1867–1940) and Phan Chu Trinh (1871–1926) — who disagreed fundamentally on tactics (Chau favored retaining the monarchy as a symbol; Trinh was a republican who believed the French presence could be reforming) and inspired by Japanese victories and the late Qing reform movement. Japan lost its appeal as a model after 1910 when it annexed Korea and made the Twenty-One Demands on China.

The 1927-founded Vietnam Quoc Dan Dang (VNQDD), modelled on the Kuomintang, attempted insurrection at Yen Bai in February 1930 and was effectively destroyed by French reprisals. [[ho-chi-minh]] had organized the Vietnamese Revolutionary Youth League from 1925 and in 1930 consolidated several factions into the Indochina Communist Party (ICP). The ICP-linked uprisings in Nghe An and Ha Tinh provinces in 1930–31 demonstrated rural revolutionary potential. By the late 1930s, the ICP had adopted a rural strategy and established the Vietminh front (1941) as a broad anti-colonial, anti-Japanese coalition. The source notes the structural reason for communist success in Vietnam: "it is largely in areas of traditional Confucian influence that popular communist revolutions have, to the present, succeeded in Asia," owing to structural similarities between Confucianism and Marxism as comprehensive political philosophies.

Cambodia and Laos produced minimal pre-1941 nationalism, attributed to lighter French rule, French "protection" from Thai and Vietnamese encroachment, and the persisting legitimacy of the Cambodian monarchy. The French virtually created Laos in its modern form.

### Burma: Buddhist-Secular Division and British Contrast

Burmese nationalism was shaped by four distinctive factors: ethnic heterogeneity (Burmans, Mon, Arakanese, Karen, Shan, Kachin plus Indians and Chinese), the British abolition of the monarchy in 1885, administrative linkage to India, and the resulting influence of Indian nationalist models.

The Young Men's Buddhist Association (YMBA, 1906) was initially cultural, becoming political through the "shoe controversy" — whether foreigners had to remove shoes in Buddhist temple precincts — which the nationalists won. The General Council of Burmese Associations (GCBA, 1921) broadened this into a more secular umbrella organization, but factionalism was endemic. The question of separation from India (and thus independence from Indian-derived constitutional reforms) divided nationalists through the 1920s and 1930s.

The Saya San rebellion (1930–32) — a millenarian peasant uprising by a former monk invoking traditional amulets and royal imagery — overlapped in time with the emergence of urban, secular, socialist nationalism among university students. The Dobama Asiayone ("We Burmese Association"), taken over by young *thakin* activists in the mid-1930s including [[aung-san]] and U Nu, adopted a slogan of cultural Burman supremacy that alienated non-Burman minorities. The *thakin* ultimately turned to Japan for support; the "Thirty Comrades" including Aung San went for Japanese military training in Hainan and returned with the invading Japanese in 1942. Three themes characterize Burmese pre-war nationalism: recurrent factionalism, the rise of secular radicalism over religious nationalism, and relatively moderate British policy that encouraged working within the system.

### Siam/Thailand: Ethnic Nationalism Without Colonialism

Siam occupied a unique position as the only Southeast Asian state to retain formal independence. Thai "nationalism" accordingly took distinctive forms. Under King Chulalongkorn (1868–1910), a "self-strengthening" programme of modernization was accompanied by an emerging "official nationalism" focused on loyalty to throne and the concept of *chat* (nation). King Vajiravudh (r. 1910–25) formalized this into an ideology of "King, Nation, Religion" (*chat, saat, phramahakasat*), promoted through the paramilitary Wild Tiger Corps, extensive personal writings, and public addresses — accompanied by a strident anti-Chinese ethnicism. The source describes him as "the 'father of Thai nationalism'" in official historiography while critics note his nationalism was "strongly elitist" and "ethnically and religiously exclusive."

The 1932 coup by the People's Party — organizationally deriving from Thai students in France who had encountered republican and socialist ideas — ended royal absolutism. After factional struggles, Luang Phibun Songkhram (Phibun) became prime minister in 1938. Phibun revived and intensified Vajiravudh's cultural nationalism: a series of twelve *ratthaniyom* (state conventions) mandated Western dress codes, the Thai language was "modernized," Chinese-language media was suppressed, and a "pan-Thai" irredentism was formulated that led to war against weakened French Indochina in 1940–41 and recovery of Cambodian and Lao territories. The 1939 change of name from "Siam" to "Thailand" was explicitly an ethnic nationalist declaration. Phibun's ideology combined fascist-style state enterprise with Buddhist patronage and military glorification, modelled on contemporary trends in Germany and Japan.

### Malay Nationalism: Late, Ethnically Defensive

Malay nationalism before 1940 was limited in scope, reflecting the British administration's relatively pro-Malay policies, the late economic development of northern Malay states, and the absence of a large indigenous educated elite. The Sultan Idris Training College (1922) produced teacher graduates who played a prominent role in political critique. A reformist *kaum muda* (young group) pressed for Islamic purification against the conservative *kaum tua* (elder group). The Kesatuan Melayu Singapura (Singapore Malay Union, 1926) provided the first political forum. The dominant grievance was economic displacement by Chinese and Indian immigrant communities who had come to outnumber Malays in some states. Malay nationalism "did not become a major political force until after 1945."

## The Loyalist Option

Across the region, certain communities found advantage in the colonial relationship and resisted nationalism accordingly:

- **Netherlands East Indies**: Chinese, Eurasians, Ambonese, and priyayi aristocrats all had grounds to fear Indonesian territorial nationalism
- **Philippines**: Muslims in the south adopted a loyalist stance as Filipinization placed Catholic Filipinos in authority over them
- **Malaya**: The "King's Chinese" Straits Chinese community, the Malay aristocracy, and Anglicized Indians all had loyalist tendencies
- **Vietnam**: The Constitutionalist Party, the Catholic community, and Vietnamese holders of French citizenship formed a loyalist element
- **Burma**: Shan, Karen, and other ethnic minorities turned to the British for protection against Burman nationalist pressure

## End Conditions

The Japanese occupation of Southeast Asia from 1941–42 transformed the political landscape entirely. Colonial regimes collapsed; Japanese military administrators variously encouraged, manipulated, or suppressed nationalist movements. The post-war period brought independence to the Philippines (1946), Burma (1948), Indonesia (1949), Vietnam (divided, 1954), Malaya (1957), and Singapore (1965). In each case the organizations built in the pre-war period provided the framework for the successor state, but the ideological character of those states reflected the specific character of the dominant nationalist movement.

## Historiography

The standard narratives of Southeast Asian nationalism — particularly George McTurnan Kahin's *Nationalism and Revolution in Indonesia* (1952) — traced a chronological progression of organizations representing distinct ideologies. Subsequent scholarship (Takashi Shiraishi's *An Age in Motion*, 1990; Reynaldo Ileto's work on the Philippines) questioned whether Western ideological categories adequately describe these movements. The chapter in [[tarling-cambridge-history-southeast-asia-v2-1992]] notes that Mohammad Hatta considered Indonesian Marxists to be nationalists at heart, and that Sukarno "glossed over the differences between nationalism, Marxism and Islam" — which "might be seen as reflecting a deep understanding of Indonesian society." Debates about elite versus mass participation, collaboration versus non-cooperation, and the relationship between religious and secular nationalism remain active in the scholarship.
