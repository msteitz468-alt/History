## Definition and Scope

The Meiji reform program refers to the comprehensive reorganization of Japanese political, military, fiscal, and social institutions carried out by the Meiji oligarchs in the period from the Restoration (1868) to the promulgation of the Meiji Constitution (1889) and the Rescript on Education (1890). Its core impulse was defensive modernization: Japan had to become strong enough to resist Western imperialism and negotiate revision of the unequal treaties imposed under the Tokugawa.

This page focuses on the early phase (1868–1874), when the structural foundations were laid. Later developments (the constitutional movement, treaty revision, the Sino-Japanese and Russo-Japanese wars) are covered in linked pages.

The program was not ideologically unified. It combined Western institutional imports (conscript armies, prefectural administration, national education, Western legal codes) with the deliberate reinforcement of traditional authority symbols (the emperor's public visibility, Shintō as state ritual, Confucian ethics in education). The guiding formula — articulated by Motoda Nagazane and others — was "Eastern ethics, Western science."

## Causal Drivers

**The unequal treaties** — extraterritoriality and fixed import tariffs signed under Tokugawa duress — created the specific external pressure. Revision required convincing Western powers that Japan operated under recognizable institutions of law, finance, and public order. Every structural reform was in part a performance for a Western audience.

**The fiscal crisis** — domain debts, samurai stipend obligations, and the costs of suppressing pro-Tokugawa holdouts meant the new government faced immediate fiscal emergency. Haihan chiken, the land-tax reform, and the dissolution of samurai stipends were all partly driven by the need to establish a solvent national fiscal system.

**Military vulnerability** — the experience of Western gunboat diplomacy and observations of European armies (especially the Prussian army's performance in 1870–71) made the case for a national conscript army self-evident to those who had witnessed Western military power.

**The emperor as symbol** — Ōkubo Toshimichi, drawing on European models (particularly Louis XIV as analyzed by historians), insisted on making the emperor visible to his subjects and to the world. The emperor's public life — reviews of troops, domestic tours, Western dress — was a deliberate construction designed to project legitimacy both domestically and internationally.

## Major Phases

### Phase 1: Constitutional Demolition (1868–1871)

Return of domain registers (*hanseki hōkan*, 1869); gradual erosion of domain autonomy; abolition of the domains and establishment of prefectures (*haihan chiken*, August 29, 1871) — the decisive act that made centralized administration possible.

See: [[abolition-of-han-1871]]

### Phase 2: Institution Building (1871–1873)

The [[iwakura-mission-1871]] (December 1871 – September 1873) sent Japan's senior leaders abroad to observe Western institutions while a caretaker government implemented reforms at home. Key measures of this phase:

- **National education system** (September 1872): 53,760 elementary schools planned; all children from age six to attend; French system as model. See: [[meiji-compulsory-education-1872]]
- **Solar calendar adoption** (December 1872): Japan joined the Western temporal frame. See: [[meiji-calendar-reform-1872]]
- **Conscription law** (January 10, 1873): universal male conscription at age twenty, ending the samurai military monopoly. See: [[meiji-conscription-law-1873]]
- **Abolition of sale of persons** (November 1872): prostitutes and indentured workers legally freed, partly prompted by the *Maria Luz* incident exposing Japanese practices to foreign diplomatic scrutiny.
- **Permission for mixed-religion marriage** and release of "hidden Christians" (1873): concessions demanded by Western powers as evidence of religious tolerance.

### Phase 3: Crisis and Consolidation (1873–1877)

The return of the Iwakura mission precipitated a political crisis over Korea. Saigō Takamori's proposal to send a suicidal envoy to provoke war was defeated by Iwakura and Ōkubo in October 1873 (emperor's decision: October 24, 1873). Saigō and four allied councillors resigned.

The political fallout generated the Saga Rebellion (February 1874) and the Taiwan expedition (May 1874), the former a samurai revolt against modernization, the latter an assertion of Japanese authority over Ryūkyū/Okinawa territory. The Satsuma Rebellion (1877) was the final major armed resistance to the new order.

Land-tax reform (1873, not covered in Keene's chapters 18–25) systematized agricultural taxation on a cash basis tied to assessed land value rather than crop yields, providing the state with predictable revenue independent of harvest conditions.

## Geographic Spread

The reforms were administered through the new prefectural system centered on Tokyo. The emperor's domestic tours (western Japan, 1872; subsequent circuits) served partly to carry the reform program's legitimating symbolism to remote regions where government authority was weakest. The tour's specific emphasis on schools, local industries, and troop reviews embodied the three priorities Keene identifies: industry, education, military.

## Interaction with Other Processes

The reform program ran in direct tension with samurai class identity. The [[abolition-of-han-1871]], conscription, and the eventual abolition of stipends dismantled the economic and social foundations of the samurai order. The armed rebellions of 1874–77 were the political expression of this conflict.

The program also ran in creative tension with Confucian conservatism represented by Motoda Nagazane (the emperor's tutor from 1871). Motoda was not opposed to Western science but insisted that "with respect to human relations, the West had nothing to offer." His influence would culminate in the Rescript on Education of 1890, which inscribed Confucian ethics at the center of the national school curriculum.

## End Conditions

The Meiji reform program as an emergency restructuring project reached its formal completion with the Meiji Constitution (1889) and the Rescript on Education (1890). These documents codified the political and educational order the oligarchs had been improvising since 1868. The program did not end change — modernization continued through the Meiji period and beyond — but the constitutional moment marked the transition from emergency reformation to institutionalized order.

The unequal treaties, the original external driver, were not revised until 1894 (extraterritoriality) and 1911 (tariff autonomy). The reform program had taken over two decades to produce the conditions for treaty revision.
