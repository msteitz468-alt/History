# Political Surveillance (US Federal, 20th Century)

**Political surveillance** — the systematic federal monitoring, cataloguing, and covert
disruption of domestic political groups and individuals deemed subversive — grew from
modest origins in the [[events/palmer-raids-1919|1919–1920 Palmer Raids]], where a young
[[hoover-j-edgar|J. Edgar Hoover]] built an index-card system tracking suspected radicals,
into a durable institutional practice at the FBI that persisted, in varying forms and
intensity, for the rest of the twentieth century.

## Development

Hoover, who led the FBI from 1924 until his death in 1972, repeatedly revived and expanded
domestic intelligence-gathering during periods of perceived crisis — a General
Intelligence Division reestablished in 1939 ahead of US entry into WWII, wartime custodial
detention indices, and, during the Cold War, extensive monitoring of the Communist Party
USA, civil rights organizations, and antiwar movements, culminating in the covert
COINTELPRO program (1956–1971), which extended beyond passive surveillance to active
disruption and discrediting of targeted groups and leaders. Congressional investigations in
the 1970s (notably the Church Committee) exposed the scale of these programs and produced
lasting legal and institutional reforms intended to constrain domestic intelligence
activity.
