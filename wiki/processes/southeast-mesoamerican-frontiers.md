# Southeast Mesoamerican Frontiers

## Definition and Scope

Payson Sheets (CHNPA II.1 Ch. 9) models El Salvador and Honduras as a **frontier zone** (not a mere periphery) where Mesoamerican and Intermediate Area societies interacted. A **boundary/border** is a line; a **frontier** is a dynamic **zone** of contact between at least two societies, often with enclaves of both expanding and autochthonous groups, facilitating social differentiation as elites exploit resources or labor. The plural **“frontiers”** is deliberate: political, economic, ethnic, and religious frontiers differ and shift. Sheets avoids “periphery” as pejorative and discounts autochthonous achievements. Serious SE investigation began only decades before his writing; data suffice to treat the countries as a changing prehistoric frontier.

The conventional southeastern Mesoamerican line follows the **Ulua and Lempa** rivers (or somewhat east along Ulua–Comayagua–Choluteca). Beyond, the Intermediate Area emphasizes manioc + maize, simpler societies, smaller polities, South American ceramic affiliations, metallurgy, and fewer pan-area integrative traits (Willey). Costa Rica lay effectively beyond the frontier (minimal artifacts, local reuse of glyphic objects as raw material — Lange).

## Causal Drivers

Contact among societies of unequal complexity; trade (Ixtepeque obsidian, jade, cacao, salt); elite emulation of power iconography; local autochthonous traditions; catastrophic volcanism (**Ilopango** ~A.D. 200–250; Laguna Caldera/[[ceren-salvador|Cerén]] ~A.D. 600); multiethnic composition of “Maya” centers such as [[copan|Copán]].

Theoretical toolkit Sheets reviews: against simple diffusionism/age-area “poor cousins”; Turner-style expanding frontier; Lange lower Central America; interaction spheres; asymmetrical acculturation; world-systems (Olmec and Classic Maya obtaining SE obsidian).

## Major Phases

### Paleoindian–Archaic

Very sparse evidence (unprovenanced fluted points in Salvadoran collections; La Esperanza fluted bifaces in Honduras). Archaic data scant; Henderson’s **2500 B.C.** used as Archaic–Formative boundary. Absence is research bias plus vegetation, ash, alluviation.

### Formative / Preclassic

Mesoamerica as culture area emerges toward end of Archaic. Early sedentary societies in westernmost El Salvador may date mid-2nd millennium B.C. (Ocos-related ceramics averaging 1590 ± 150 B.C.). Marcus: Pacific coast Chiapas–El Salvador may have developed complex society coeval with or prior to the Olmec.

**Chalchuapa** (~1200 B.C.): agriculturally based village; principal workshop/provisioning for Pacific–Tehuantepec–Olmec Ixtepeque obsidian route beginning a system lasting to Conquest. Middle Formative chiefdom with ~20 m conical pyramid; Olmec-style presence; headed own polity ~600 B.C.–A.D. 200. **Los Naranjos** Jaral phase (800–400 B.C.): 6 m earthen structure; defensive ditch 7×20 m × 1,300 m; elite jade burial. **Yarumela**: stronger South American ceramic affiliations in 2nd millennium B.C. Honduras and El Salvador more uniform and “Mesoamerican” ~1000–300 B.C. than later.

Late Formative: dense Salvadoran landscape of large chiefdoms; [[quelepa|Quelepa]] founded ~400 B.C. with Jaguar Altar (Izapan/Kaminaljuyú links) and Uapala ceramic sphere (possible Lenca). Los Naranjos defensive earthwork lengthened to 5 km; pyramids to 18 m. NE Honduras loses Mesoamerican indicators and may shift toward Intermediate Area by Classic.

### Ilopango and Classic reorganization

**Ilopango** (~A.D. 200 / Sharer ~A.D. 250): >20 km³ acidic ash; agriculture impossible for generations; central-western El Salvador abandoned; Pacific coastal Guatemala sites disrupted. Eastern El Salvador spared direct tephra but **isolated** — [[quelepa|Quelepa]] Shila phase shows maximum Intermediate Area traits. Dual reoccupation: NE Honduras largely self-sufficient non-Mesoamerican peoples; **central El Salvador** by thoroughly Mesoamerican (likely Ch’orti’-related) peoples — [[ceren-salvador|Cerén]] domestic window; Zapotitán Valley hierarchy ≥40,000 Late Classic.

**[[copan|Copán]]** stimulated trade, elites, and civic construction across western/central Honduras but Mesoamericanization minimally involved commoners/rural households; multiethnic (probable Lenca enclave). Gualjoquito fortunes intertwined with Copán. Ulúa–Yojoa polychromes widely traded. Quelepa Lepa phase (A.D. 625–1000): Gulf Coast–related **site-unit intrusion**; frontier shifts east toward Choluteca. Chalchuapa Late Classic resurgence with Copán ties and lower Central American gold ornaments at Tazumal.

### Postclassic

Central/western El Salvador avoided the worst demographic declines; Chalchuapa continuity with **Pipil**-associated central Mexican traits (talud-tablero, circular pyramid, Xipe Totec, chacmools, Plumbate). **Cihuatán** and Santa María short-lived Pipil centers, possibly deliberately burned at abandonment. Pipil–Nicarao multiple migrations over centuries (Fowler); Chorotegans preceded. Honduras: primary-center declines ~A.D. 1000; Classic Maya expansion ended; frontier receded to Motagua; **Naco** Postclassic trading enclave (~10,000 people at contact) beyond the frontier on circum-Yucatán networks. Greater Nicoya influence as weaker counter-frontier after Maya demise. Parts of north/extreme NE/E/SE Honduras outside Mesoamerica all prehistory.

## Geographic Spread

Western Honduras, El Salvador, adjacent Guatemala — the southeastern edge of the [[mesoamerica-culture-area|Mesoamerican]] culture area, expanding and contracting into the Intermediate Area several times.

## Interaction

- Classic Maya lowlands and [[copan|Copán]]/Quiriguá multiethnic nodes to the north and west.
- Intermediate Area / Greater Nicoya to the east and south.
- Olmec Formative resource networks; Postclassic Pipil–Nicarao from central Mexico; Aztec-era coastal trade farther north (cross-ref [[maya-civilization]] Putun/Xicalango).
- Elite package for identification: large pyramids, palaces, economic centralization, specialized crafts, monumental sculpture — context and frequency matter; isolated distant jades may be curiosities (Sheets).

## End Conditions

Colonial reorganization; ethnohistoric Lenca, Pipil, Maya, and other groups; analytical frontier problem continues. Sheets’s summary: **four waves** of foreign influence — Olmec Formative; Maya Classic; Postclassic Pipil–Nicarao; Greater Nicoya groups — while autochthonous trajectories and research bias (Copán research exceeds all SE research outside Copán) must not be erased.

## From Evans & Webster 2001 (Southeast Mesoamerica entry)

Encyclopedia **Southeast Mesoamerica** synthesis (broad transect from Honduras’s Caribbean coast south to El Salvador’s Pacific littoral) complements Sheets ([[evans-webster-archaeology-ancient-mexico-central-america-2001]]).

### Formative complexity

Little known before **1200 BCE**; La Esperanza obsidian flows probably visited **4000–1200 BCE**. Earliest clearly identified occupations near end of that span at Chalchuapa (migrants from Pacific coast Guatemala/Chiapas) and possibly Naco Valley. By **1000 BCE** two-tier hierarchies and differential burial appear — clearest at Chalchuapa (Structure E3-1-2nd, conical platform **≥20 m**), Yarumela, Los Naranjos (possible **1,300 m** defensive ditch). Playa de los Muertos child burial with shell/jadeite belts/necklaces suggests inherited status. Olmec-horizon motifs especially at Chalchuapa. Cuyamel Caves (NE Honduras) held Early–Middle Formative ceramics (**c. 1200–400 B.C.**) with Olmec-related motifs and Tlatilco parallels — Honduran participation in Formative Mesoamerican networks possibly related to cacao.

**Late Formative:** continued growth in productive valleys; Chalchuapa’s El Trapiche complex (~0.5 km²) after hiatus **c. 500–400 BCE**; Usulután ware originated in western El Salvador and was traded/imitated northward; closest material culture ties with southeast Highland Maya ([[kaminaljuyu|Kaminaljuyú]] ideas via Chalchuapa); **few similarities** with contemporary Lowland Maya. Motagua Maya region: Early/Middle Formative [[copan|Copán]] ceramics fit SE Mesoamerican traditions but differ from neighboring Lowlands; Late Formative Copán developmental anomaly (apparent **decline** while Lowlands centralized).

### Classic peak and limited Copán hegemony

**Early Classic:** population growth unevenly matched by political centralization; third-century **Ilopango** truncated western El Salvador (Chalchuapa); Quelepa grew with terraces atypical of Mesoamerican patio focus (closer lower Central American ties); Salitrón Viejo **>500** jade/jadeite pieces; some green obsidian and talud-tablero suggest Central Mexican contacts possibly via Kaminaljuyú.

**Late Classic** = maximum population density and political complexity in most studied areas (except lower Aguan and Comayagua): **3–5** tier hierarchies; capitals with up to ~**500** structures; elite monopolies of long-distance jadeite/obsidian/shell. **Southern La Entrada may have been incorporated within the Copán state from A.D. 628 to 738**, but **no evidence that this hegemony extended farther** into Southeast Mesoamerica. Ulúa polychromes widely distributed; Copador from Copán especially in western El Salvador; Ulúa marble vessels traded to Maya Lowlands. Outside southern La Entrada, southeastern rulers largely **impervious** to Lowland Maya elite culture though some adopted ball courts. Gualjoquito (8 ha, 48 buildings) compact non-Maya civic center: Classic prosperity correlates with Copán florescence; plan mimics Copán core; authority from Ulúa–Jicatuyo crossroads.

Sulaco–Humuya corridor: first occupied ~**400 B.C.** by non-Maya groups; peak **A.D. 600–800** with at least one small chiefdom centered on Salitron Viejo; population declined after **800**, abandoned after **1000**.

### Collapse-era and Postclassic

Terminal Classic–Early Postclassic: population drops almost everywhere except lower Aguan and western El Salvador; abandonment by **A.D. 1000** in middle Humuya/Sulaco and La Entrada; brief reversals Cerro Palenque (Sula Plain, **A.D. 850–1000**), fortified Tenampua (Comayagua, **900–1100**), Cihuatán (violent end early 11th c.). Early Postclassic El Salvador shows chac mool, Tlaloc braziers, talud-tablero partly from migrations; **Pipiles** (Nahua) enter El Salvador sometime **A.D. 800–1350**. Changes generally contemporary with [[maya-classic-collapse-800ce|Maya Collapse]]; **no clear evidence** that human-caused environmental degradation drove SE declines (contrasts with Copán).

**Zapotitán Valley** (346 km², El Salvador): ~**A.D. 200** Ilopango deposited **1–2 m** acidic ash → abandonment ~2 centuries; Late Classic dense hierarchical system with San Andrés primary center; imported Ixtepeque obsidian; Copador shared with Copán; Loma Caldera buried [[ceren-salvador|Cerén]].

**Late Postclassic:** Honduran landscape of competitive ranked chiefdoms; Naco and Viejo Brisas del Valle as late centralization nodes and trade emporia (obsidian, copper, circular architecture possibly Central Mexican–inspired). El Salvador divided among small competitive states, many Pipil-descended, growing cacao. Spanish conquest protracted through early–mid 16th c.; disease, slaving, and warfare nearly destroyed SE populations — essentially disappeared by early 19th c. in Honduras and ~a century later in El Salvador.
