# Second Urbanisation of India (Gangetic Plain)

## Definition and Scope

The **Second Urbanisation** refers to the emergence of a new phase of urban life in
northern India — centred on the **Ganga valley** — from approximately **600 BCE**, which
was distinct from, and arose nearly a thousand years after, the earlier **first urbanisation**
of the Indus valley ([[indus-valley-civilization|Harappan civilization]], c. 2600–1900 BCE).
The term distinguishes the Gangetic urban phenomenon from its Harappan predecessor and
signals the qualitatively different social, economic, and political conditions that produced it.

As Avari explains: "The agricultural surplus had a positive effect on the growth of towns during
this period... This development was most marked in the Ganga valley, and hence historians have
described this area as the home of the second phase of urbanisation in Indian history. The first
phase had come to an end nearly a thousand years before in the Indus valley with the collapse of
such cities as Mohenjo Daro and Harappa."

## Causal Drivers

### Iron Technology

The decisive enabling technology was **iron**, which began to be widely used across communities
of northern India during c. 1000–600 BCE. The earliest Iron Age sites of India were located in
three main regions: the Ganga plains, the central regions of Malwa and the Tapti valley, and
the megalithic sites of south India. Iron was not brought into India by the Aryans; "it developed
gradually from about 1000 bc onwards, long after the Aryans had metamorphosed into Indo-Aryans."
The transition was also uneven: "the Iron Age did not succeed the Chalcolithic Age uniformly
throughout India at a particular point of time; the progression and transition between the two
ages remained extremely uneven in different parts of India."

Iron's crucial contribution was to forest clearance: "the first iron tools were not axes and
ploughshares, but weapons; and so the clearing of forests in the Ganga basin is more likely to
have taken place in the later centuries of this period, that is after 1000 bc, than before." As
iron axes, hoes, and ploughshares spread after 1000 BCE, "large-scale clearances of forests
were taking place in the upper and middle Ganga valley" and "large areas of marshland were also
being drained through inundation channels."

### Agricultural Surplus and Political Power

The forest clearances produced arable land; the arable land produced agricultural surplus; the
surplus was captured by the rulers of the emerging states through taxation. Tax collectors
(*gramabhojaka*) collected revenues in the form of "either voluntary offerings or royal
entitlements," greatly augmenting royal treasuries. The rulers and nobles "acquired enormous
power over labour through such means as the caste system, the clan organisation and, in the case
of the kingdoms, the political legitimacy acquired through the alliance between the royal and
Brahmanic authorities."

This surplus `enabled` the transition from village-based subsistence to urban specialization: "It
was this surplus that enabled the rulers of states to encourage relatively large numbers of
non-agricultural people to live in towns and pursue their crafts and skills."

### Mahajanapada State Formation

The formation of the **sixteen mahajanapadas** (great states) from the numerous smaller
*janapadas* (territories of the *janas*) was both a driver and a product of the urbanisation
process. Panini's grammatical compendium *Ashtadhyayi* (c. 500 BCE) names sixty-nine polities
of various sizes. The competitive warfare among these states concentrated power in fewer, larger
centres, each requiring fortified capital cities. Two types of state emerged: clan states
(Mallas, Vriji) on the periphery, with corporate governance and anti-Brahmanical tendencies;
and kingdoms in the heartland, with hereditary monarchs sustained by brahmanical ritual authority.

## Major Phases

### Phase 1: The Vedic-Period Transition (c. 1000–600 BCE)

The preconditions for urbanisation were laid in the later Vedic period (c. 1000–600 BCE), with
the eastward expansion of Indo-Aryan settlement from Punjab into the Ganga basin, the spread of
iron technology, and the beginning of systematic forest clearance. Village settlements of the
PGW (Painted Grey Ware) culture expanded. Political organisation moved from clan chieftainship
to hereditary monarchy. The population at the end of the Vedic age was "probably about 20 million"
(Avari, citing Thapar).

### Phase 2: The Urban Florescence (c. 600–300 BCE)

From 600 BCE onward, urban centres multiplied throughout the Ganga valley. Avari identifies
six sites with archaeological evidence of walls, gateways, and ramparts datable to around 600 BCE:
**Taxila, Rupar, Rajghat, Rajagriha, Kausambi, and Ujjain**. These six "were either the capitals
of their mahajanapadas or else they straddled one of the earliest well-defined trade routes of
India." The material hallmark of this urban prosperity was **Northern Black Polished Ware (NBPW)**:
a highly burnished, wheel-made pottery found at these urban sites, replacing the earlier Painted
Grey Ware.

Towns concentrated craftsmen of many kinds: "masons, bricklayers, plumbers, carpenters and general
labourers for building work; metal workers for producing weapons and tools from copper and iron;
and a host of specialist craftsmen who manufactured trade commodities, such as glass, ivory objects,
ceramics, beads and textile fabrics." The towns were the principal centres of trade and exchange,
housing treasuries "of grain, silver and gold." Certain towns also became centres of religious
activity — Kashi (Varanasi) and Vaishali developed strong Buddhist followings.

## Geographic Spread

The Second Urbanisation was concentrated in the **Ganga valley** — particularly the upper and
middle Ganga plain, the region that Avari describes as **Pracya** (the east: "all lands between
Prayag and the Ganga delta"). This is where "the ascendancy of the Magadhan state was established"
and "the east that would decide the destiny of India for the next thousand years." The Madhyadesha
(Middle Country, the Ganga-Yamuna doab) was a secondary centre. The northwest (Gandhara, Taxila)
was important but more exposed to Persian and then Macedonian disruption.

## Settlement Hierarchy

Archaeology has clarified the hierarchy of settlements characteristic of the Second Urbanisation:
*grama* (village), *nagara* (town), *pura* (defensive-cum-administrative structure), *rajdhani*
(capital city), and *mahanagara* (metropolitan city). Archaeological work at **Kausambi**, capital
of the Vatsa mahajanapada (modern Allahabad district), has provided the best documented evidence
of how this hierarchy can be determined at a historic site. Towns were defined by their defensive
structures — gateways, bastions, moats, and ramparts — and by the concentration of power and
wealth in the form of treasuries.

## Interaction

The Second Urbanisation and the political competition it fuelled were intimately connected to
the rise of the heterodox religious movements. Urban people "were less receptive to the Brahmanic
hegemony, and many came to be influenced by various dissenting traditions, particularly Jainism
and Buddhism." The merchant class (vaishya) — who controlled trade and converted agricultural
surplus into mobile wealth — were particularly receptive. The sixth century BCE was thus "a
watershed in the history of speculative ideas," with the growth of urban centres feeding
philosophical ferment and the rise of the Shramanist movement opposing Brahmanism.

The process `produced` [[magadha]] as the dominant mahajanapada and ultimately the heartland of
the [[mauryan-empire|Mauryan Empire]].

## End Conditions

The Second Urbanisation was not a process that ended abruptly; it `produced` the conditions for
the Mauryan Empire (321 BCE), which consolidated and expanded the urban network across the
subcontinent. The urban centre of gravity shifted to [[taxila|Taxila]] in the northwest and
**Pataliputra** (on the Ganges near modern Patna) in the east — both massive fortress-cities,
the latter eventually the Mauryan capital.

## Historiography

The term "Second Urbanisation" was developed by Indian historians — most prominently Romila
Thapar — to distinguish the Gangetic urban phase from its Harappan predecessor and to challenge
earlier frameworks that treated the Aryans as simply destroying civilisation before the Mauryas
rebuilt it. Avari draws on both Thapar's textual reconstruction and on archaeological work,
particularly the excavations at Kausambi. The dependence on Buddhist literary sources (the Jataka
stories) for descriptions of urban life is a recognised limitation. Avari's assessment is balanced:
archaeological confirmation for most towns has not yet been established for the pre-Mauryan period,
but "further advances in archaeological methods and techniques will confirm, not dispute, the
contents of the texts regarding the towns of the sub-continent."
