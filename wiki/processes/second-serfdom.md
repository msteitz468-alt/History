# The Second Serfdom (East-Elbian Enserfment)

The **"second serfdom"** (*Gutsherrschaft*; German *zweite Leibeigenschaft*) is the divergent path
of east-central and north-eastern European rural society in the later Middle Ages and early modern
period: as serfdom **decayed in the west**, peasants in Poland, the Baltic lands, Brandenburg,
Pomerania, Mecklenburg, Bohemia and Hungary were progressively **tied to the soil** and burdened
with heavy labour services on enlarged noble demesnes. Christopher Dyer (Rural Europe,
[[allmand-ncmh-v7-1998|NCMH VII]] Ch. 5) treats it as the great regional exception to the
fifteenth-century improvement in peasant conditions.

## Definition and Scope

The label is partly a misnomer: Dyer stresses that "the peasants of eastern Europe were being
brought under serfdom **for the first time**." They had been recruited to settle the eastern lands
in earlier centuries "with privileges and easy terms"; only now were their movement restricted and
heavy *Robot* labour services imposed. The process was **slow** — "beginning in the later years of
the fifteenth century, and... not completed until well after 1500" — and so cannot be read as an
immediate, mechanical response to the post-plague fall in population (which would have predicted
*improved* peasant bargaining power, as in the west).

## Causal Drivers

- **Weak states and weak towns.** East of the Elbe the territorial state was feeble and the urban
  sector underdeveloped, so peasants lacked the alternative employers, market outlets and royal
  protection that strengthened their western counterparts.
- **A powerful, numerous nobility.** Where the nobility was both numerous and politically dominant
  (the Polish *szlachta*, which "reduced the power of urban societies" and made itself "the sole
  political group"), it could enforce its will on the land directly rather than retreating from
  agriculture as western lords did.
- **Western demand and the export economy.** The growth of [[urbanization|north-western towns]]
  created a large market for **Baltic rye** (shipped via Danzig) and for **cattle** droved from
  Denmark, Poland and Hungary. Lords reorganised their estates as export-oriented grain farms
  worked by compelled peasant labour.

## Interaction

The second serfdom is the **eastern mirror** of the western rural transformation traced in the
[[late-medieval-crisis|late-medieval crisis]]: the same demographic conjuncture produced freedom
and rising wages in England, France and the Low Countries, but bondage in the east — a divergence
that would shape European economic and social history for three centuries. It depended on, and fed,
the [[european-nobility-late-medieval|consolidation of the eastern nobility]] and the long-distance
[[hanseatic-league|Baltic]] grain and cattle trade.

## End Conditions

Outside this volume's scope: the system hardened through the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries
(the classic *Gutsherrschaft* of Brandenburg-Prussia and Poland) and was dismantled only by the
agrarian reforms of the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries.

## Historiography

The "second serfdom" is an etic model (with Marxist genealogy) for the long east–west divergence of
European agrarian regimes. Dyer's contribution is to **decouple it from demographic determinism**:
because enserfment lagged the population fall by generations and ran *against* the predicted
direction, the cause must lie in the regional balance of power between lords, peasants, towns and
the state — not in plague mortality alone. This is part of his broader critique of the
"demographic crisis" as a sufficient explanation of fifteenth-century rural change.

## As a coerced-labour regime (CWHS Vol. 3 & 4)

The [[cambridge-world-history-slavery-v3|Cambridge World History of Slavery]] treats East
Central European **manorialism (1500–1800)** as one regime on the early-modern continuum of
coerced labour, alongside [[atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic slavery]], white servitude, and
indigenous bondage — the unfree tied to the land rather than owned as chattel. Vol. 4 then takes
up the **[[abolition-of-slavery|emancipation of the serfs]]** (Habsburg lands 1781/1848,
Russia 1861) as part of the same nineteenth-century wave of emancipation that ended New World
slavery — linking the end of European serfdom to global abolition.
