## Definition and Scope

Patrick Manning (Ch. 12 of CWH Vol. I) argues that migration is not merely
important to world history but is a *natural, even inevitable* theme for it,
because world history is by definition about *connections*. Where conventional
history studies communities as discrete units, world history stresses the
permeability of communities and the many differences that encourage migratory
flows.

Crucially, Manning observes that **most human migration is cross-community
migration** — taking people (generally young people) from one already-existing
human community to another — not migration into empty land. Even in the
Paleolithic, most migrations were from one community to another, not into
unoccupied regions. This reframes migration as a fundamentally *social* process,
not merely a matter of filling empty space.

It is not only people who migrate but also species, religions, ideas, and
technologies — so the study of migration touches many sub-fields of world history.

## Causal Drivers

- **Environmental/climatic change**: the earliest human migrations were probably
  driven by environmental change. Paleolithic migrations took ancestors around
  the world into environments that changed their technologies and even their
  body types and skin colors. See [[paleolithic-global-dispersal]].
- **Social evolution**: the permeability of communities and the social structures
  (kinship, marriage, trade) that connect them.
- **Economic pressure and opportunity**: peasants moving to cities; labor seeking
  work.
- **Coercion**: the Atlantic slave trade and other forced migrations.
- **Technology**: horses enabled new long-distance and annual migrations in
  Eurasia; ships and railways accelerated and extended migration in the modern era.

## Major Phases / Turning Points

| Phase | Period | Character |
|---|---|---|
| Paleolithic dispersals | Early Prehistory–Behavioral Modernity | Out of Africa 1 and 2; global colonization; small-group, community-to-community movement |
| Agricultural migrations | Neolithic onward | Larger communities; new motivations; trackable by genetic evidence |
| Horse-based migrations | Bronze Age onward | Eurasian steppe; long-distance and annual movements |
| Language-family expansions | Holocene | Austronesian and Bantu speakers spread across vast areas, carrying language and culture |
| Urban migrations | with cities | Villages/small towns to cities, in search of work and opportunity |
| Trade diasporas | ancient–modern | Land and sea networks foster diaspora communities |
| First global migrations | from ~1500 CE | The slave trade (coercive) and voluntary migrations |
| Industrial-era mass migration | Long 19th Century | Steamships and railways: ~50 million Europeans crossed the Atlantic 1840–1940; ~80 million migrants from India and China; ~10 million enslaved Africans crossed 1550–1850 |
| Contemporary urbanization | Contemporary | By the early 21st century, most humans live in urban areas |

## Geographic Spread

Migration is, by definition, the process that produced the global distribution of
humanity. The two foundational events are [[out-of-africa-1-1800kbp]] (archaic
*Homo*) and [[out-of-africa-2-60kbp]] (modern humans), the latter giving rise to
the [[paleolithic-global-dispersal]] that populated every habitable continent.

In the modern era, two regions of sparse population — North America and Southeast
Asia — each absorbed over thirty million immigrants, creating entirely new
composite communities and increasing the salience of racial and ethnic identities.

## Interaction with Other Processes

- **Language divergence and convergence**: migration both spreads and fragments
  languages; central to the [[divergence-convergence]] framework.
- **Disease**: migrating people carry pathogens; epidemiological exchange is a
  migration effect (cf. the Columbian Exchange, later volumes).
- **Gender**: migration is gendered — who moves, and how, depends on gender
  relations (see [[gender-history]]).
- **Urbanization**: rural-to-urban migration is a defining demographic process of
  the modern era.

## End Conditions

Migration is ongoing and, if anything, intensifying. Manning notes that despite
distinctively modern patterns (mass, long-distance, technology-enabled), ancient
patterns persist: today's cross-community educational migrations (young people
leaving their home community to study) echo the most ancient migratory pattern of
all — the movement of the young away from their natal community.

## The Early Modern Inflection (CWH VI)

Dirk Hoerder (CWH VI) develops the "first global migrations" row of the table
above into a full account of how the *character* of migration changed after 1400 —
treated in detail at [[early-modern-migrations]]. His key correction to any
heroic narrative parallels Manning's: the European-centered story of white settler
mass migration obscures both the far larger forced migration out of Africa and the
vast internal migrations within all the world's societies. The decisive early
modern novelty was the fusion of state power with mercantile investment ("armed
trade"), which induced or enforced labor, refugee, and settler migrations on a
global scale, and produced the Atlantic slave system, settler-colonial
"repeopling," and new métis and creole populations. This is the demographic engine
of the [[early-modern|construction of a global world]].

## Fagan & Durrani (2020): "two great radiations"

Fagan & Durrani (2020) frame the whole span of Paleolithic dispersal as **"two great radiations of humanity."** The first is the Out of Africa dispersal itself; the second — the subject of their chapter on the "Diaspora" — is the settlement of the Americas, which they treat as the event that completes the global diaspora of *Homo sapiens*, rendering the human radiation "nearly complete" and setting the stage for the subsequent, qualitatively different processes of food production, village life, urban civilization, and the (much later) settlement of the remote Pacific. This is consistent with, and reinforces, the two-event structure ([[out-of-africa-1-1800kbp]] and [[out-of-africa-2-60kbp]]) already used above, while adding the explicit framing device of "two radiations" and identifying the peopling of the Americas as the closing chapter of the second.

## Note on Method

Migration history draws on an exceptionally wide range of sources: archaeology,
genetics (increasingly precise for tracking population movements), historical
linguistics, and conventional written records. This methodological breadth makes
migration a model theme for the multi-disciplinary ambitions of world history.
