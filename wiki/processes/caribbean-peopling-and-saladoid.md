## Definition and Scope

The Caribbean peopling and Saladoid expansion encompasses the entire multi-millennium sequence by which the Caribbean islands moved from complete absence of human population to the complex, diverse societies encountered by Columbus in 1492. It has three analytically separable phases: (1) the **initial peopling** of the West Indies by Archaic (pre-ceramic, pre-agricultural) peoples probably before 3600 BCE; (2) the **Saladoid horticultural colonization** of the Lesser Antilles and Puerto Rico beginning c. 250 BCE; and (3) the **proto-historic millennium** (C.E. 500–1500) in which local processes in the Greater Antilles produced the [[taino]] chiefdoms and the Barrancoid-derived descendants in the Lesser Antilles who eventually became — or were replaced by — the [[island-caribs]].

The process is grounded in Louis Allaire's chapter in the *Cambridge History of the Native Peoples of the Americas*, Vol. III Part 1 (see [[cambridge-history-native-peoples-americas-v3-1]]). It must be read alongside the continental Amazonian background, because "the important Amazonian element in the development of cultures in the eastern parts of the Caribbean region must never be ignored."

## Causal Drivers

### Environmental and Geographic Frame
The Caribbean Sea is a unifying basin — "the world's second largest sea" (c. 1,900,000 km²) — whose easterly trade winds and warm currents produced predictable westward flow. This made coasting navigation feasible; direct open-ocean crossings were apparently not practiced ("There are no recorded historic trans-Caribbean voyages"). Allaire's framing is explicitly non-determinist: "This is not a matter of environmental determinism but more properly of opportunistic circumstances."

Island environments constrained colonization biology. Beyond Trinidad, the islands "only supported an impoverished fauna that never allowed the dispersal of large animals"; large game (deer, peccary) were absent. Marine and shore resources (fish, shellfish, crab) compensated, and manioc horticulture transferred readily from the mainland. The Caribbean islands thus rewarded horticulturalists more than hunters.

### Amazonian-Orinoco Agricultural Base
The process originated not in the Caribbean but in the **Amazonian tropical lowlands** and their northern extension, the middle Orinoco. The La Gruta culture at the Ronquin locality on the middle Orinoco — dated to "the very beginnings of the second millennium B.C.E." (with a disputed earlier calibrated date of 2750 BCE) — marks the earliest firm association of pottery-making with **bitter manioc cultivation** (*Manihot esculenta*), evidenced by abundant clay *budares* (griddles) and stone chips from manioc grating boards. "The origins of La Gruta suggest a homeland in the Amazon Basin, where related ceramic styles are widely distributed after 2000 B.C.E. within the so-called Zoned Hachured Horizon."

La Gruta initiated the **Saladoid series** (named from the later Saladero site on the lower Orinoco), characterized by:
- Composite or bell-shaped vessels
- White-on-red painted decoration — "the hallmark of early ceramic styles in the eastern Caribbean"
- Elaborate modeled-incised adornos (small anthropomorphic/zoomorphic heads attached to vessel rims)
- Bitter manioc cultivation (attested by budares)

By c. 1500 BCE, the Ronquinan Saladoid spread down the Orinoco, establishing permanent floodplain villages where the levees "became small islands in flood season and received annual deposits of fertile alluvium, not unlike the situation, as Rouse has observed, in the Nile Valley."

### Barrancoid Pressure and Displacement
From c. 1500 BCE, the Barrancoid culture developed on the lower Orinoco — distinctive for bucket-shaped vessels with elaborate flanged rims and broad curvilinear incised designs, and expanding rapidly after C.E. 500. The relationship between Saladoid and Barrancoid peoples remains disputed. What is clear is that Barrancoid expansion along the lower Orinoco, Trinidad, and eventually the eastern Venezuela coast created competitive pressure on Saladoid groups, plausibly contributing to their colonization push into the island arc.

### Maritime Technology
Island colonization required canoe navigation across open water. The Lesser Antilles form "stepping stones between Trinidad and the Greater Antilles," and "all the Caribbean islands, including the Bahamas, maintained continuous and regular contacts with simple watercraft." The process took approximately 200 years to colonize all islands from Grenada to Puerto Rico — an indication of either a substantial founding population or high inter-island mobility.

## Major Phases

### Phase 1 — Initial Peopling: Archaic Age (c. 4000–500 BCE)

**Chronology**: The earliest radiocarbon date for the West Indies (excluding Trinidad) is c. 3600 BCE from Haiti, supporting Rouse's long-hypothesized date of 4000 BCE. Eastern Cuba has a date of 3100 BCE at Levisa.

**Peoples and technology**: The **Casimiroid** series, centered on Hispaniola and Cuba, represents the primary Archaic colonists. Their technology was lithic — large blade and flake tools ("reminiscent of Old World Palaeolithic archaeology"), with no pottery and no agriculture. Subsistence relied on small terrestrial fauna (rodents, iguanas, an extinct giant ground sloth possibly hunted into the last millennia BCE) and marine resources. Populations spread slowly; Puerto Rico had a simple Coroso culture by c. 1000 BCE; some Lesser Antilles (Antigua's Jolly Beach culture, c. 2000 BCE) showed a related blade tradition.

**Origins problem**: The homeland remains unresolved. Computer simulation studies (Callaghan) show that the most likely successful accidental drift would originate from the Colombian/Venezuelan coast aimed westward toward Cuba, Jamaica, and Hispaniola — but no archaeological homeland with compatible technology and dating has been identified. The possibility of accidental drift (rather than deliberate colonization) is taken seriously: "On the basis of current models of biological dispersals in the Caribbean region, it appears that the West Indies flora and fauna are essentially South American in origin. Biologists explain the introduction of these species as a series of accidental drifts mainly from South America."

**The Ortoiroid**: Trinidad was colonized very early — the Banwari Trace culture dates to 5230 BCE — with a simple Archaic toolkit (ground stone pestles, crude flakes, edge grinders, shellfish collecting). Ortoiroid peoples may represent a second mainland wave through the Lesser Antilles, but evidence from the island corridor is fragmentary.

### Phase 2 — Saladoid Colonization and the Formative Millennium (c. 500 BCE–C.E. 500)

**The Saladoid migration (c. 250 BCE–C.E. 100)**: Cedrosan Saladoid peoples from the lower Orinoco colonized all islands between eastern Puerto Rico and Trinidad "sometime before 100 B.C.E." The process was rapid — "hardly 200 years" — covering the chain from Grenada (Pearl style) to Puerto Rico (Hacienda Grande). Allaire notes the colonists "apparently pushed out into the islands from their lower Orinoco homeland, likely under ongoing Barrancoid pressure, practically at the same time they were establishing themselves on the coast ... Trinidad, and the Guianas."

**Subsistence adaptation**: The earliest Saladoid sites show dense remains of land crab (the culture was originally called the "Crab Culture"); manioc cultivation is attested by clay griddles from the beginning. Other artifacts are spare: petaloid celts, stone chips from local jaspers, and conch-shell axe blades (from *Strombus gigas*) for land clearing. The "impoverished fauna" of the islands forced reliance on marine resources alongside manioc.

**Material culture**: Saladoid pottery is distinctive: "fine incisions, modelings such as adornos, and the diagnostic white-on-red painted designs." Elaborate incense burners (hollow cylinders, open at both ends) may have been an island invention. Small carved pendants in semiprecious stones (amethyst, carnelian, greenstone) hint at inter-island trade networks reaching potentially to the Amazon.

**The La Hueca controversy**: At eastern Puerto Rico and Vieques, an aberrant early style (La Hueca, c. 200 BCE) lacks the white-on-red decoration universal elsewhere and may represent a separate migration or source population. It is "a subject of much bitter controversy among specialists." La Hueca is associated with unusually fine lapidary work, including carved bird-head pendants.

**Barrancoid interaction (C.E. 350–500)**: After C.E. 350, strong Barrancoid influences from eastern Venezuela affected the Saladoid ceramic styles of the Lesser Antilles, producing more baroque decoration and early polychrome painting, and the first appearance of three-pointed stones (trigonoliths) — objects that would become central to Taíno religion.

### Phase 3 — Proto-Historic Millennium: Divergence and Chiefdom Formation (C.E. 500–1500)

**Collapse of Saladoid uniformity**: By C.E. 500–650, Saladoid hallmarks — especially white-on-red painted decoration — disappeared across the islands. In the Lesser Antilles, a new Troumassoid series replaced Saladoid, "entirely indigenous to the islands." No mainland migration accounts for the change.

**Suazey and the Lesser Antilles (C.E. 1100–1500)**: The Troumassoid eventually gave rise to the Suazey culture in the Windward Islands and Barbados — coarse, plain cooking pots with finger indentations, contrasting sharply with earlier ceramic artistry. Suazey subsistence was coastal: shellfish, fish, and turtle hunting. Large three-legged clay griddles (unique to the Lesser Antilles) attest continued manioc cultivation.

**Island Carib late migration**: The historic [[island-caribs]], "relentlessly raiding Puerto Rico as well as the mainland when encountered by the Spaniards," left no identified Suazoid pottery: "Island Carib pottery was entirely different from Suazoid ceramics; their cooking pots and pans instead belonged to a widespread tradition of the Guianas from where the Caribs claimed to have recently migrated to the Lesser Antilles." The Suazey culture thus pre-dates the Caribs and probably belonged to a different ethnic group.

**Taíno emergence in the Greater Antilles (C.E. 500–1500)**: In the Greater Antilles, the process was entirely distinct. From late Saladoid beginnings (Cuevas culture, eastern Hispaniola), a local evolution produced the Ostiones culture, which:
1. Colonized Jamaica and introduced pottery/agriculture to Cuba.
2. Colonized the forested interiors of the large islands for the first time.

From Ostiones, centered on the Mona Passage between Hispaniola and Puerto Rico, a fully Taíno culture emerged just before C.E. 1000 (initial Atajadizo style) and spread westward to eastern Cuba (c. C.E. 1200) and eastward to Puerto Rico and the Virgin Islands. By the fifteenth century, Taíno chiefdom societies controlled the entire Greater Antilles and Bahamas.

**Taíno material markers**:
- Chican Ostionoid ceramics — elaborate modeled and incised vessels with adorno decoration; no painted designs.
- Unique stone portable carvings: trigonoliths (three-pointed stones), stone collars, and elbow stones — "unique to the Greater Antilles" — representing deities (*zemies*).
- Ball courts (bateys): vast open spaces lined with standing stones or embankments, associated with both a ball game and religious ceremonies. "Usually located in the hilly interior valleys of the islands, these ball court sites may have been the seat of major chiefdoms."
- Carved wooden duho seats (hammock-shaped), symbols of chiefly rank.

**Chiefdom formation**: "Population increase and the rise of a complex of the chiefdom type, as encountered by Columbus, as well as diversified art forms of a complexity unknown elsewhere in the islands and eastern Venezuela, are everywhere associated with the phenomenon." The Taíno world Columbus described thus represented "the full realization of many of the developments previously ongoing in the Caribbean region."

**A world past its peak**: Allaire's concluding observation is significant: "the world of 1492 was not the time of the climax of any of the most developed cultures or the height of these chiefdom societies." Ball courts are rarely mentioned in European descriptions; elaborate ceramic traditions had simplified; "Almost everywhere on the Caribbean coast of South America by the end of the fifteenth century, the sophisticated and highly decorated pottery styles had given way to plain, coarse, and poorly decorated wares." The Europeans encountered a Caribbean in partial cultural decline, having missed "by a few centuries a Caribbean world at its most populous and powerful."

## Geographic Spread

The process moved in a consistent directionality:
- **Amazonia → middle Orinoco**: origin of manioc cultivation and the Saladoid tradition.
- **Lower Orinoco → Trinidad**: Saladoid and then Barrancoid expansion along river levees.
- **Trinidad → Lesser Antilles → Puerto Rico**: Saladoid colonization of the island chain, c. 250–100 BCE.
- **Eastern Hispaniola (Mona Passage) → full Greater Antilles**: Ostiones and then Taíno development, C.E. 500–1300.
- **Guianas → Lesser Antilles**: Island Carib late migration, pre-contact but recent.
- **Continental Venezuela coast → offshore islands (Curaçao, Bonaire, Aruba)**: Macro-Dabajuroid expansion, C.E. 1000–1500, linguistically associated with spread of Arawakan languages.

Also operating as influence vectors rather than migrations:
- **Colombia/Magdalena Basin**: the earliest pottery in the Americas (Puerto Hormiga, c. 3000 BCE or earlier); fiber-tempered tecomate tradition; possible ancestor to Caribbean and even North American early ceramics.
- **Mesoamerica → western Caribbean (Central America, Greater Nicoya)**: Olmec and later Maya trading networks, especially C.E. 500–1000 (Code culture of Panama; Tairona affinities debated).

## Interaction with Other Processes

- **[[andean-state-formation]]**: The Andes remained geographically peripheral to the Caribbean archaeological sequence — "The Andean area, a center of high powerful civilizations, is the most remote, and it seems to have had little direct effect on the Caribbean region." The older circum-Caribbean hypothesis overstated Andean derivation.
- **Amazonian baseline** (see [[amazonia]]): The Amazonian contribution is fundamental: Saladoid origins in the Amazon Basin, Barrancoid-Saladoid interaction through the Orinoco-Casiquiare-Rio Negro corridor, Arauquinoid connections to the pan-Amazonian Incised and Punctate Horizon.
- **[[atlantic-slave-trade]]**: Contact-period collapse was swift: "disease, displacement, forced labor, failure to reproduce, slave raiding, and warfare, all contributed to a collapse everywhere of the native societies and cultures." The Taíno experienced "practically total depopulation within a single generation of initial European contact."
- **[[columbian-exchange-1492]]**: The process ended when European contact triggered this catastrophe.

## End Conditions

The process was terminated by European colonization after 1492. The [[taino]] of the Greater Antilles were demographically destroyed within a generation. The [[island-caribs]] survived longer in the Lesser Antilles due to greater resistance capacity and European reluctance to colonize the smaller islands initially, but faced sustained pressure through the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. The archaeological ceramic traditions, elaborate stone carving, and ball court ritual complexes vanished. No internal dynamic accounts for the end — the process was externally terminated.

## Historiography

The chapter engages the "circum-Caribbean hypothesis" introduced by Kirchhoff and elaborated by Steward in the *Handbook of South American Indians* (1940s–1950s), which attributed Caribbean cultural similarities to Andean derivation. Allaire accepts the geographical unity of the Caribbean as a real phenomenon but rejects the Andean-diffusionist model as "oversimplified": "Amazonian contribution to the Caribbean area proper is fundamental and may be the source of many features once attributed to the Andes." The emphasis on ceramics as evidence reflects their status as the only reliable archaeological record — "mute messages from the past" that "have left their very own images and symbols."

Key ongoing debates include:
1. **Origins of the initial Archaic colonists**: No definitive homeland identified; accidental drift from the Colombian/Venezuelan coast most plausible by simulation (Callaghan).
2. **The La Hueca controversy**: Whether the aberrant early Puerto Rican style represents a separate migration wave or a local variation.
3. **Barrancoid origins**: Whether Barrancoids developed in situ from Saladoid antecedents (Rouse, Roosevelt) or derived from Colombia/the Andes (Meggers, Evans).
4. **Meillac origins**: Whether this important Hispaniolan culture derived from Guyana, the Venezuelan llanos, or local preceramic peoples.
5. **Island Carib pre-history**: The Suazey culture does not match historic Carib pottery; Carib migration timing and mainland origin remain contested.
