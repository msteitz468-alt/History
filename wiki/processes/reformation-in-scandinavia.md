# Reformation in Scandinavia

The introduction of Lutheranism as the established religion of the Nordic
monarchies in the sixteenth century, simultaneously a religious transformation
and an instrument of state-building. In Denmark–Norway the Reformation was
imposed by royal ordinance under Christian III (1536–37), with church property
passing to the crown; in Sweden–Finland it advanced under
[[gustav-i-vasa|Gustav Vasa]] from the 1520s (Västerås, 1527) and was confessionally
fixed at the Uppsala Synod (1593). The process dissolved the medieval church
(the disintegration of the medieval church), redistributed wealth and power to
the monarchy, reshaped culture and literacy, and set the religious-territorial
pattern of the [[denmark-norway|Danish]] and [[swedish-empire|Swedish]] realms.

## Definition and Scope

The Reformation was "in the first place the great revolution in religion and
church. With its by-products in thought and learning, in constitutional changes,
in national and international affairs and … in economic, social and cultural
factors in general, it changed history" (L1187–1194). In Scandinavia it was
inseparable from state-building: doctrinal change was often "minimal," while the
decisive measures concerned "the church's economic position" — the transfer of
church property to the crown and the curtailment of clerical jurisdiction
(L1171–1174). The differing political and national conditions produced **two main
types**: "one Danish-Norwegian and another Swedish-Finnish" (L4600–4601), each
ending in a national Lutheran church to which "almost the entire population
belongs" (L4603). See [[lutheran-state-church]] for the resulting institution.

## Causal Drivers

A pre-existing popular evangelical movement in the towns (especially in Denmark),
the immense wealth of the Catholic church (which held roughly one-third of
cultivated land in Denmark — see [[denmark]]), the financial needs of the crown,
and the example of the German princely reformation all converged. In Denmark Grell
argues for "two reformations": a popular urban evangelical movement from the mid
1520s onto which Christian III's princely reformation was "engrafted"
(L2838–2839), correcting the older "solely princely" view (L2759–2761).

## Major Phases

- **Denmark (1520s–1537):** Lutheran preaching under Hans Tausen, protected by the
  Viborg magistracy (1526) and king Frederik I; the *Confessio Hafniensis* (1530);
  victory of [[christian-iii-of-denmark|Christian III]] in the Count's War (1536);
  the Church Ordinance drawn up by Johannes Bugenhagen (1537), "the common root of
  the churches in Norway, Iceland and the Føroyar" (L1089–1090).
- **Sweden (1527–1593):** Västerås 1527 transferred church property to the crown
  and curtailed clerical jurisdiction under [[gustav-i-vasa|Gustav Vasa]]; doctrinal
  fixity came only with the **Uppsala Resolution of 1593**, which condemned
  Catholicism, sacramentarians, Calvinists, and Anabaptists and set the
  confessional basis for Sweden–Finland until 1869 (L4039–4061).
- **Peripheries:** Norway received Lutheranism slowly (its own Church Order only in
  1607); Iceland's reform was imposed by force after the execution of bishop Jón
  Arason and his two sons in 1550, with real progress only from the 1570s under
  Guðbrandur Þorláksson; Finland's reform was carried by Wittenberg-trained clergy,
  above all [[mikael-agricola|Mikael Agricola]].

## Geographic Spread

From the Danish and Swedish urban cores outward to [[norway]], [[iceland]], and
[[finland]]. The reform "emphasised the national languages and was important for
the formation of a nationality and culture" in Finland and Norway — though in
Finland the parallel "Swedishing" of administration caused a decline in written
Finnish (L4564–4572).

## Interaction

The Reformation was the engine of the early-modern Nordic state: by enriching and
empowering the crown it laid the groundwork for the later turn to
[[scandinavian-absolutism|absolutism]] (Denmark 1660, Sweden 1680) and for the
[[denmark-norway|Danish]] and [[swedish-empire|Swedish]] realms' capacity to wage
the [[struggle-for-baltic-supremacy|struggle for Baltic supremacy]]. It built on
the dissolution of the [[kalmar-union-1397|Kalmar Union]] (1523). It paralleled
the wider [[protestant-reformation-1517|Protestant Reformation]] and stood against
the [[catholic-reformation|Catholic Reformation]].

## End Conditions

The process closed with the consolidation of Lutheran orthodoxy: the Danish refusal
of the Formula of Concord under Frederik II (who banned the Book of Concord on pain
of death, L3218–3221) fixed a distinct Danish-Norwegian confession, while the 1593
Uppsala Resolution fixed the Swedish-Finnish one. By c. 1600 both realms had
national territorial Lutheran churches as instruments of royal power.
