# The Transpacific Economy

## Definition and Scope

The **transpacific economy** is the trade and production system spanning the Pacific
basin, linking East Asia (above all China and Japan) with the Americas. Lionel Frost's
chapter in [[mcneill-cwh-v7-2015|CWH VII]] traces its growth from the early-modern
European trading enclaves to its post-1945 emergence as the most dynamic axis of the
world economy. Frost organizes the story around **demand and supply shifts** and the
**removal of obstructions to trade**, focused on three countries: China, Japan, and
the United States.

## The long-run datum

World trade grew ~**1% per year from 1499 to 1800** (despite monopolies and political
barriers), then **3.7% per year from 1800 to 1992** as transport costs fell and force
opened markets. The signal twentieth-century fact: **transpacific trade overtook
transatlantic.** In 1965 transpacific trade was 59% of transatlantic; by 1985 it was
**124%** — the quantitative mark of the world economy's recentering on the Pacific
(cf. [[east-asia-modern]], [[great-acceleration]]).

## Major Phases

1. **Early-modern enclaves (1535–1800).** In 1750 China and Japan together held ~244
   million people — almost double Europe's — and ~86% of the Pacific region's
   population; "High Qing" prosperity rested on American silver and crops, frontier
   settlement, a market-oriented peasantry, and low-tax government. Europeans operated
   from constrained enclaves: Portuguese **Macao** (1535) and **Dejima** (Nagasaki);
   the Spanish **Manila galleon** (from 1571) carrying Acapulco silver to silver-hungry
   China; Dutch **Batavia** and Fort Zeelandia; and the **Canton system** (1757),
   which funneled Western traders through a single window port (see
   [[global-silver-trade]], [[early-modern-commerce]]).
2. **Forced opening (19th c.).** Steam riverboats and improved guns let Europeans wage
   war in coastal and river waters: the **Opium Wars (1839–42)** forced open five
   Chinese ports and ceded Hong Kong; **Perry's "Black Ships" (1853)** opened Japan.
3. **American entry and the Pacific world.** After Cook's voyages, British and American
   whalers and fur hunters converged on the Pacific, disrupting island communities and
   spreading disease; Hawai'i became the hub of American Pacific whaling and trade. The
   US gained Pacific shores (Oregon Treaty, 1846).
4. **The post-1945 transpacific boom.** The rise of East Asian manufacturing (Japan,
   the tigers, then China) made transpacific trade the world economy's leading axis —
   the labor-intensive [[industrialization]] path Sugihara describes (see
   [[east-asia-modern]], [[contemporary]]).

## Interaction

The transpacific economy is the maritime dimension of [[east-asia-modern|East Asia's
resurgence]] and a key thread of the [[great-divergence|Great Divergence]] and its
reversal; it connects to [[global-silver-trade]] (the Manila galleon as the first
genuinely global trade route) and to [[imperialism]] (the forced openings).

## Geographic Spread

Centered on the China–Japan–US triangle, with the Pacific coast of Latin America,
Southeast Asia, Pacific Russia, Canada, Australia, and New Zealand drawn in by forces
emanating from the basin's major powers.

## Historiography

Frost adopts an explicitly **anti-[[eurocentrism|Eurocentric]]** stance, rejecting the
"colonizer's model of the world" (Blaut) that casts Asia as a passive "Outside"
awaiting European diffusion. Following John Wills's "interactive emergence of European
domination," he argues Asian traders, navigators, and merchant-princes remained
effective competitors far longer than older scholarship allowed, and that European
intrusion is best seen as an **exogenous shock provoking diverse local responses** —
not a one-way "intrusion and response."
