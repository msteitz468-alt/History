# Tang Borderland Horse-Breeding Ranch System

The state-run system of frontier horse ranches through which the [[tang-dynasty|Tang
dynasty]] (618–907 CE) bred its cavalry mounts, analysed by Jonathan Karam Skaff (Kim et
al. 2017, ch. 2). Skaff argues it was the **primary** source of Tang military horsepower —
not a supplement to trade with independent steppe polities — and that its fortunes turned
on control of the China–Inner Asia borderlands, on weather, and on the multiethnic
personnel who ran it.

## Definition and Scope

Administered by the **Court of Imperial Stud** (*taipu si* 太僕寺) — a chief minister, two
vice-ministers, ~50 clerks, 600 veterinarians, and veterinary erudites teaching 100
students — the system comprised **48 ranches in the mid-7th century, perhaps 65 by the
early 8th**, in the northern and north-western frontier prefectures. Each ranch (under a
*mujian*, "director of herds") held 3,000–5,000+ horses in herds (*qun*) of 120 under a
chief herdsman; from the mid-7th century regional supervisors (*jianmu shi*) oversaw
clusters of ranches.

## Causal Drivers and Mechanics

- **Breeding regime**: stallions dispatched to frontier armies after the season; a quota of
  **60 foals per 100 mares**, scheduled to the third lunar month; silk rewards for exceeding
  it, escalating corporal and penal-servitude punishments (up to three years) for shortfalls.
- **Branding and allocation**: foals triple-branded (official mark, year, ranch); the finest
  juveniles and strongest geldings ("three flowers" brand) went to the emperor's stables,
  the rest down a hierarchy of military, postal-relay and bureaucratic use.
- **Fodder economy**: under Wang Maozhong (by 725), 31,000 farming households cultivated
  ~1,900 *qing* (~24,700 acres) of barley and **alfalfa** (*musu*); an edict of 753 banned
  private ranches within 500 *li* of the twin capitals to protect fodder supply.

| 9th c. | a few thousand | collapse after loss of borderlands |

## Interaction and End Conditions

Skaff **revises Ma Junmin and Wang Shiping**, who blamed Türk raids and corruption: he shows
weather disasters (which struck Türk herds too) and swings in the balance of power — war
booty and diplomatic gifts when the Tang was strong (e.g. 70,000 head from the Chuyue in
652), plunder losses when weak (100,000 sheep and horses from the Ordos in 702) — drove the
fluctuations. Personnel at every level were disproportionately **borderland and multiethnic**
(Sogdians, Türks, Koreans): Zhang Wansui was a borderland defector; **Wang Maozhong** was the
son of an enslaved Koguryŏ aristocrat, executed on probably false charges after a 12-year
tenure. The system was permanently crippled when the [[an-lushan-rebellion|An Lushan
Rebellion]] (755–763) cost the Tang the borderland pastures, forcing reliance on horse
imports from the Uighurs.

## Historiography

Skaff builds on Paul Smith's "geographical thesis" (horse self-sufficiency depends on
borderland pasture) but argues it cannot alone explain the herd swings. The reframing fits
the volume's programme (Kim et al. 2017): steppe and sedentary China as a **symbiosis**
rather than a binary opposition.
