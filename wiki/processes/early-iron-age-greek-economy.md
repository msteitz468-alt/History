# The Early Iron Age Greek Economy

## Definition and Scope

Ian Morris's chapter in the *Cambridge Economic History of the Greco-Roman World*
([[sources/scheidel-cambridge-economic-history-greco-roman-world-2007]]) frames Greek
economic history 1200–700 BCE via Douglass North's distinction between economic
**performance** (total/per-capita output, income distribution) and **structure**
(institutions, technology, demography, ideology). This page treats the Early Iron Age
economy specifically as the successor system to the collapsed
[[processes/mycenaean-palace-economy|Mycenaean palace economy]]; it is not simply a
"Dark Age" absence of activity but a distinct, lower-performance economic equilibrium.

## Causal Drivers

- **The Late Bronze Age palace collapse (c. 1200 BCE).** No 12th-century Linear B
  documents survive anywhere; the wanax title lost its function by c. 1100 BCE, leaving
  the lesser office of pasireu (cf. Homeric basileus) as the effective top authority.
- **Catastrophic demographic contraction.** Snodgrass's raw site counts implied population
  decline of up to 95% (1200–1000 BCE), which Morris judges implausible at face value but
  still confirms "a catastrophic population collapse" — population by 1000 BCE was "no
  more than half," "probably more like a quarter," of two centuries earlier. Settlement
  sizes collapsed correspondingly: 13th-century sites of 5,000–10,000 shrank to largest
  11th–10th-century sites (Athens, Knossos, Argos, Lefkandi, Karphi) below 1,500.
- **The basileus/oikos household economy.** Nobles (basileis) headed large households
  (oikoi) using chattel slaves, landless/poor thetes (casual wage labor), and dependents;
  land was held as elite temenos or partible-inheritance kleroi. Three exchange mechanisms
  operated: peripheral ad hoc markets, Phoenician-intermediated "ports of trade" (Al Mina,
  Kommos), and ritualized gift-exchange among basileis (a low-trust-world transaction-cost
  reducer; violating gift norms was seen as loss of wits, e.g. Glaukos/Diomedes).

## Major Phases

1. **Collapse, 1200–1000 BCE.** Economic collapse in Aegean Greece following the palace
   destructions.
2. **Stagnation, 1000–800 BCE.** By 1000 BCE Greece had settled into "a new economic
   equilibrium, at a lower level of performance than the LBA equilibrium" — small
   population, weak political leadership, minimal external contact, lost advanced skills
   (e.g. literacy).
3. **8th-century acceleration.** Driven by external pressure (Neo-Assyrian tribute demands
   on Phoenicia — Ashurnasirpal II from the 870s BCE, Tiglath-Pileser III's annexation of
   Phoenicia except Tyre c. 740 BCE — pushed Phoenician traders to intensify
   Mediterranean-wide exchange, including with the Aegean) and internal Mediterranean-wide
   population growth. Morris rejects Snodgrass's claimed demographically implausible
   population "explosion" (would require female fertility incompatible with skeletal-data
   life expectancy of only 20–22 years); Morris argues instead for a roughly **doubling**
   of Greek population across the 8th century, partly via emigration (20,000–40,000 adult
   male Greeks to Italy/Sicily, 750–600 BCE).
4. **Genuine economic "take-off," c. 550–500 BCE.** Morris identifies this later moment,
   not the 8th century itself, as the period of the most important economic acceleration.

## Geographic Spread

Centered on mainland Greece, Crete (least affected by the population collapse, along with
Macedonia), and the Aegean islands, with external contact channeled through Phoenician
intermediaries at sites like Al Mina and Kommos (Crete), and later through Greek
colonization of Sicily and southern Italy.

## Interaction

- **Living-standards evidence.** Skeletal data (Angel) show life expectancy at birth
  falling from 23.1 years (LBA) to 22.3 (EIA); porotic hyperostosis and vertebral arthritis
  actually declined somewhat, suggesting a partially better diet (more meat) even amid
  shorter lifespans. Median house size fell to 45–55 m² (9th–7th c.) from a 13th-century
  peak of 76 m², not recovering until the "take-off" after 550 BCE (4th-century houses
  reaching 150–200+ m², an estimated 5–10x the consumption level of an 8th-century house).
- **Morris's central thesis: the "optimality band."** The decisive 8th/7th-century
  development was political, not economic per se — "middling" aristocrats formed
  oligarchies strong enough to block would-be tyrants, generalizing equality among adult
  male citizens and creating a state "strong enough to guarantee property, but not strong
  enough to act as predators," fitting E. L. Jones's "optimality band" for pre-modern
  growth. This political structure (rather than Near-Eastern-style strong monarchy) is
  credited as the structural precondition for later [[processes/archaic-greek-economy|
  archaic-classical Greek economic growth]]. Sparta, with military/serf-based wealth but
  narrow citizenship, is cited as a partial counter-case, ultimately less successful
  long-run than broad-citizenship poleis like Athens.
- **Slavery and citizenship.** The development of chattel slavery is linked (following
  Finley) to the strengthening of free citizenship: citizens working their own land had
  little incentive to labor for wages, generating demand for slave labor — best documented
  via Solon's reforms at Athens (594 BCE).
- **Colonization as mutual-gains trade.** De Angelis's evidence of grain silos at Megara
  Hyblaea shows Greek Sicilian colonists generating agricultural surpluses for Aegean trade
  by 700 BCE; conquest of Sicily's plains roughly doubled arable land under Greek control
  by 500 BCE, read by Morris as specialization-driven mutual gain (Aegean wine/oil/pottery
  for Sicilian grain) rather than a core-periphery relationship.

## End Conditions

The Early Iron Age economy gives way to the [[processes/archaic-greek-economy|Archaic
Greek economic transformation]] as the "structural revolution" (Snodgrass's term for
population growth, state formation, colonization, and the return of literacy, art, and
monumental architecture) matures across the 8th–7th centuries, culminating in Morris's
identified take-off point of c. 550–500 BCE.
