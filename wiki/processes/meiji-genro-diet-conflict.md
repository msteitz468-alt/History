# The Genro-Diet Conflict in Meiji Japan (1890–1903)

The structural political conflict of the Meiji period between the **genrō** (the oligarchic
elder statesmen who controlled cabinet formation and the military) and the **elected political
parties** that dominated the House of Representatives from the Diet's opening in 1890. The
conflict shaped all domestic politics of the 1890s–1900s, repeatedly bringing government to a
crisis and driving the evolution toward party cabinet government. From Donald Keene's
[[keene-emperor-of-japan-meiji-2002|*Emperor of Japan: Meiji and His World*]] (chapters 48–53).

## Definition and Scope

The conflict was structural, not personal. The [[meiji-constitution-1889|Meiji Constitution]]
of 1889 created a bicameral Diet with budgetary powers but placed the executive firmly in the
hands of cabinet ministers appointed by and responsible to the emperor — not to the Diet. The
prime minister was selected by the genrō (senior statesmen, principally from Satsuma and
Chōshū), who also controlled the non-partisan army and navy ministers, whose presence in the
cabinet was legally mandatory. A cabinet could thus govern against a hostile Diet majority
(dissolving it repeatedly if needed), but the Diet could block budgets and tax bills. The
result was chronic deadlock.

## Causal Drivers

- **Constitutional design**: the Bismarckian model, which placed sovereignty in the emperor
  rather than the legislature, meant cabinets need not command parliamentary majorities.
- **Domain-clique monopoly** (*hanbatsu*): appointments rotated between Satsuma men
  (Matsukata, Saigō) and Chōshū men (Itō, Yamagata, Katsura). Party affiliation did not
  enter into selection logic.
- **Genrō ideology**: Yamagata Aritomo, the most powerful genrō through the 1890s–1900s,
  regarded party cabinets as a violation of the national polity. "To allow a political party
  to form a cabinet," he told Inoue Kaoru, "would be to destroy the history of the Meiji
  government and to violate the imperial constitution."
- **Military veto**: army and navy ministers could resign at will, collapsing any cabinet. The
  military used this power instrumentally — Katsura deliberately inflated the Ozaki scandal of
  1898 to destroy the Kenseito cabinet.

## Major Phases

### Opening confrontations (1890–1896)
The Diet was dissolved five times in its first eight years. Tax and budget conflicts recurred
in every session; the government used bribery and police pressure in elections; opposition
parties organised motions of no confidence. Through this period the government relied on
cooperation bought from the Jiyūtō or Shimpōtō in exchange for individual concessions.

### Cabinet crises (1896–1898)
After the Sino-Japanese War, foreign minister Mutsu's illness and cabinet reshuffling triggered
the resignation of the second Itō cabinet (August 1896); the Matsukata cabinet that followed
(1896–98) was equally unstable. Matsukata openly told the Privy Council that he was following
imperial instructions to ignore party reactions — and was defeated by a no-confidence motion.
The repeated inability of non-party cabinets to manage the Diet escalated pressure for change.

### The Kenseito experiment (1898)
The merger of Jiyūtō and Shimpōtō into the [[kensei-to|Kenseito]] in June 1898 created
a parliamentary majority that could not be ignored. Itō recommended the first **party cabinet**
to the emperor; Ōkuma became prime minister. The cabinet collapsed within four months through
internal Jiyū–Shimpō factionalism and army manipulation, but the precedent had been set: a
majority party could form a government.

### Seiyūkai consolidation (1900–1903)
Itō's founding of the [[rikken-seiyukai-1900|Rikken Seiyūkai]] (September 1900) with imperial
backing represented an attempt to reconcile party government with genrō authority by placing
a genrō *at the head of* the leading party. The Seiyūkai's first cabinet (fourth Itō cabinet,
1900–01) faced continued House of Peers obstruction. By 1903 the conflict had shifted: the
Katsura cabinet (a non-party government) relied on Seiyūkai toleration in the Diet while
managing the building crisis with Russia.

## Interaction with External Events

The conflict intensified during foreign crises (the Sino-Japanese War, the Boxer Rebellion)
because the government sought extraordinary appropriations and demanded party cooperation in
exchange for access it could not indefinitely deny. The [[boxer-rebellion|Boxer Rebellion]]
in particular accelerated the evolution by demonstrating that Japan's international position
required stable, efficient government — argument used by all sides.

## End Conditions

The Meiji-era conflict did not resolve; it evolved. The Taishō era (from 1912) saw the gradual
establishment of party cabinets as the norm. The genrō's power shrank as the founding generation
died. By the 1920s full "Taishō democracy" — party government, universal male suffrage — had
been achieved, only to be reversed by military dominance in the 1930s.
