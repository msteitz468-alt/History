# Merovingian–Byzantine Relations

**Two centuries of diplomacy, subsidy, religion, and rivalry binding Frankish Gaul to Constantinople —
and the strongest single case against the Pirenne thesis.** Stefan Esders's chapter (Oxford Handbook,
Ch. 16) argues that "from the intense, Mediterranean-wide ecclesiastical and diplomatic interaction
centered around [the great church councils], a very different picture of Merovingian connectivity
emerges" than Pirenne's image of a seventh-century rupture. The [[byzantine-empire|Eastern Empire]]
was, by turns, model, paymaster, ally, and wary rival of the Merovingians.

## Definition and Scope

The relationship operated across four registers: **ceremonial/legitimacy**, **subsidy and proxy war**,
**religion (the Christological controversies)**, and **coinage**. It is documented in Procopius,
Gregory of Tours, Fredegar, papal registers, and conciliar acta.

## Major Phases

- **Clovis and imperial legitimacy (c. 508).** After [[battle-of-vouille-507|Vouillé]], [[clovis|
  Clovis]] paraded at Tours "wearing a purple tunic and Roman cloak (*chlamys*)… distributing coins in
  the fashion of a reception ceremony (*adventus*)," receiving the patriciate and honorary consulate
  from Anastasius. His Catholic baptism "certainly must also be seen as a symbolic concession to
  Constantinople." Roman troops in NW Gaul settled with the Franks "on the basis of sharing the
  Christian religion."
- **Justinian's wars (535–562).** A Byzantine embassy approached the three Frankish kings in 535
  before the Ostrogothic War; in 536 Justinian ceded Ostrogothic [[provence|Provence]] to them.
  **Theudebert I** then claimed imperial prerogatives — circus games, his image on coins, the title
  *Augustus* on a Metz coin (see [[ostrogoths]]). Justinian's 537 indiction reform was rapidly adopted
  (Gallic inscriptions date by indiction from 540).
- **Subsidy and proxy war (570s–590).** Tiberius II and Maurice paid for Frankish armies against the
  [[lombards|Lombards]]; the **Gundovald affair (582–585)** was a Byzantine-funded intervention in
  Frankish succession politics (see [[merovingian-episcopate]]). Maurice "sent 50,000 solidi… recalled
  the money when… Frankish military engagement was deemed insufficient." Childebert II's 590 peace
  with the Lombards "marked the end of eastern Rome's policy of sending huge sums of money west" — the
  likely context for the pseudo-imperial gold coinage of [[provence|Provence]] (see
  [[merovingian-coinage]]).
- **Rupture and renewal (602–630).** After Phocas's usurpation (602) and the dynastic turnover of 613
  — which exterminated the Austrasian branch with the closest Byzantine links — the sources fall
  silent; Frankish mints replaced Heraclius's image with [[chlothar-ii|Chlothar II]]'s c. 615.
  [[dagobert-i|Dagobert I]] and Heraclius then concluded a *pax perpetua* (c. 629/630), Dagobert even
  adopting Heraclius's policy of forced Jewish conversion (see
  [[migrants-and-minorities-in-merovingian-gaul]]).

## Religion: From the Three Chapters to Monotheletism

The Christological controversies repeatedly entangled Gaul. The **Three Chapters** crisis (548–553)
saw Byzantine envoys pressing Gallic bishops; the **Monothelite** controversy (from 638) split the
kingdoms — Austrasia declined to convoke an anti-Monothelite synod for fear of "a political break with
Byzantium," while Neustro-Burgundy's bishops at Chalon (c. 650) affirmed Nicaea and Chalcedon but
pointedly omitted the Fifth Council. At the **Sixth Ecumenical Council (680/681)** three Gallic bishops
subscribed "as legates for the provinces of Gaul," their signatures again documenting "the split of the
Merovingian episcopate of… Austrasia and Neustro-Burgundy."

## Interaction and End Conditions

Esders's thesis directly contests the [[merovingian-ceramics-and-mediterranean-trade|ceramic-based]]
"Pirennian rupture": ecclesiastical and diplomatic connectivity persisted through the seventh century
even as bulk Mediterranean trade thinned. As late as c. 690 the Pippinid mayor Pippin received embassies
from "Greeks, Romans, Lombards, Avars, Slavs, [and] Saracens," showing Frankish Mediterranean
connectivity outlasting the Carolingian transition.
