## Definition and Scope

The Araucania frontier was the long process by which the [[mapuche]] and related Araucanian peoples defended, adapted, and eventually lost political sovereignty over the territory south of the Bío-Bío River in Chile and — in its later phase — the Argentine pampas. Running from the Spanish Crown's abandonment of conquest ambitions in the late sixteenth century to the Argentine "Conquest of the Desert" (1878–1879) and Chilean "Pacification of the Frontier" (1881–1883), it represents one of the longest-lasting successful indigenous resistances to European colonization in the Americas.

Jones argues that "native peoples numbering at least between 100,000 to 300,000 individuals were able to resist conquest and colonization for nearly four centuries deserves our attention when considering the history of South America" (*CHNPA* Vol. III, Part 2, Ch. 16). The frontier was emphatically not simply a military stalemate; it was a dynamic zone of exchange, reorganization, and mutual transformation.

## Causal Drivers

**Spanish structural weakness in the south.** After the failed campaigns of Pedro de Valdivia and the general Mapuche uprising of 1598 — which expelled Spaniards from every settlement south of the Maule River and resulted in Governor Loyola's capture and decapitation — the Spanish could not sustain offensive operations. Most resources were redirected northward to support the Andean mining economy. A royal cedula of 1622 formally declared the frontier Mapuche "royal patrimonial Indians" who could not be called into encomienda mita service.

**Mapuche socio-military resilience.** Mapuche society was organized around kinship units (lef) headed by lonkos, with a tradition of consensus decision-making and individual autonomy hostile to centralized authority. This decentralization made the society difficult to decapitate. The 1554–1557 uprising produced an emergent military hierarchy — as Gerónimo de Vivar described, leaders gathered at Tocape to elect a paramount war chief (toqui) — and subsequent generations institutionalized regional toquis ranking over local ulmenes, mirroring but adapting Spanish military structures.

**Material adaptability.** The Mapuche incorporated European crops, sheep, horses, and iron technology almost immediately. By the late sixteenth century, wheat, sheep, and horses appeared in Mapuche territory. The horse extended raid and communication range; sheep wool fueled a textile export to Chiloe and the pampas; captured Spanish women and livestock were integrated into the household economy. "The material basis of Mapuche society appears to have improved as an indirect result of the encounter" (Jones).

**Parlamento system.** After the general uprisings of 1723, colonial Chile institutionalized the parlamento — formal treaty-negotiation assemblies costing 2,000–10,000 pesos and involving up to 4,000 participants — as the primary mechanism for frontier management. These assemblies, held regularly between 1726 and 1803, formalized Mapuche territorial sovereignty west of the Andes while leaving the eastern pampas beyond treaty constraints.

## Major Phases

**Phase 1 — First encounters and permanent war (1540–1650).** Spanish attempts to impose the [[encomienda]] in Araucania from 1550 provoked cycles of warfare. The uprising of 1554–1557, a larger revolt in 1598, and continuous localized resistance established the Bío-Bío River as the effective southern limit of Spanish authority. After 1598, Jesuit missions operated behind fortified lines.

**Phase 2 — Frontier equilibrium and expansion east (1650–1780).** The parlamento system stabilized the western (Chilean) frontier while Araucanian warrior parties — the maloqueros — pushed east across the Andes into the Argentine pampas, hunting cimarrones (feral cattle and horses) and raiding Creole frontier settlements. This period saw "Araucanization of the desert": expansion of Mapundungun as a pampas lingua franca, political marriages linking Mapuche lineages to Pampas and Tehuelche bands, and rising social stratification within Araucanian society.

**Phase 3 — Bourbon reforms and independence struggles (1780–1820).** As the feral cimarron herds were exhausted by intensive hunting, competition over livestock and salt intensified. Araucanians and Pampas bands largely took the royalist side in the independence wars, calculating that the Crown offered better protection for their territorial rights than Creole liberals.

**Phase 4 — Araucanian diaspora and confederation (1820–1870).** Following Chilean independence (1818), hundreds of Araucanian families crossed the cordillera and established permanent encampments in the Argentine pampas. [[calfucura]] consolidated control of the Salinas Grandes salt sources and built a dynastic intertribal confederation. In Chile the state progressively carved up Mapuche community lands for European colonists, especially German settlers from 1858. The malón (large coordinated raid) became the primary Araucanian instrument of political pressure in Argentina.

**Phase 5 — Military defeat and reduction (1870–1882).** Argentina deployed a national army with Remington rifles, telegraph lines, and a 610-kilometer defensive ditch (Alsina's Folly, 1876). General Roca's offensive of 1878–1879 broke the Araucanian confederation. In Chile the Pacification of the Frontier (1881–1883) drove the final Mapuche resistance to defeat. Araucania was formally partitioned by the Chile-Argentina border, and survivors were settled in reducciones (Chile) and reservas (Argentina).

## Geographic Spread

The process unfolded in three overlapping geographic zones:

- **Western Araucania** (Chilean cordillera and coastal plains, south of the Bío-Bío): the Mapuche heartland and primary parlamento zone.
- **Andean passes** (Pehuenche territory): controlled the critical trans-Andean routes and served as buffer and intermediary between Mapuche and Pampas worlds.
- **Argentine pampas** (east of the Andes, south of Mendoza-Buenos Aires line): arena of the malón economy, Araucanian diaspora settlement, and the final military campaigns.

## Interaction

The frontier process was intimately linked to [[pampas-horse-cultures]]: the adoption of the horse and feral cattle gave Araucanian society the mobility and surplus wealth to project power east of the Andes. It also produced the "Araucanization" — the spread of Mapuche linguistic, kinship, and military forms across Pampas and Tehuelche groups — which the chapter frames as one of the most remarkable indigenous political expansions of the colonial era.

The frontier interacted structurally with [[reduccion]] and [[encomienda]] dynamics on its margins: those Mapuche who approached colonial settlements fell under extractive labor regimes; those who maintained distance did not.

The process is analogous_to the long resistance of the Comanche and other Plains nations in North America, as Jones explicitly notes in the bibliographic essay, comparing the eventual incorporation into reservations and the ideology of "civilization and barbarism" deployed by both Argentine and U.S. governments.

For a comparative perspective see [[cambridge-history-native-peoples-americas-v3-2]].

## End Conditions

The process ended through a combination of factors: the exhaustion of the feral livestock resource base that had sustained Araucanian political economy; the consolidation of Argentine and Chilean national armies armed with modern weaponry (Remington rifles) and logistics (telegraph, railways); the closing of frontier markets to Indian traders; and the ideological framing of indigenous peoples as racially "inferior" obstacles to civilizational progress. The 1880s defeats were final: "State policies for the 'Conquest of the Desert' in Argentina and the 'Pacification of the Frontier' in Chile ultimately ended the political sovereignty and autonomy of the Indian world in the Southern Cone" (Jones).

The legal and cultural partition of Araucania by the Argentina-Chile border created the artificial division between "Argentine Mapuche" and "Chilean Mapuche" that has shaped twentieth-century indigenous politics. In 1992, dozens of caciques from both sides of the border met for the first time in over a century to claim continued cultural sovereignty.

## Historiography

National historiographic traditions in Argentina and Chile have long distorted the history of the frontier. Argentine historiography (exemplified by Walther's *La Conquista del Desierto*, 1970) framed the wars as the civilization of an empty desert; Chilean historiography turned Mapuche into passive "peasants" by the twentieth century. Both erasures, as Jones argues, leave fundamental gaps in understanding how Chile and Argentina were actually formed.

More recent ethnohistorical scholarship — notably Leonardo León Solis's *Maloqueros y conchavadores* (1990) — has challenged the image of an "ahistorical" Mapuche culture and documented the dynamic, agency-driven character of trans-Andean trading and raiding. Jones's own work reconstructs frontier commercial relations from the Indian side.
