## Definition and Scope

The Northwest Coast maritime cultures are the long-duration economic and social complex developed by Indigenous peoples occupying the Pacific coastline of North America from southeast Alaska through northern California. The region is defined archaeologically as a cultural area and is characterised by dependence on marine and estuarine resources — above all fish, marine mammals, and shellfish — combined with intensive management of terrestrial and intertidal environments. Unlike most complex societies elsewhere in the world, Northwest Coast groups achieved social stratification, surplus accumulation, and elaborate ceremonial life without agriculture in the conventional sense: "The people of the Northwest Coast were fishers and food producers first and foremost" (Moss, ch. 26, [[jones-matsuda-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v1-2022]]).

This process encompasses the full arc from initial human settlement (~15,000–13,000 BP, following the post-LGM opening of the Pacific coastal corridor) through sustained intensification across the Holocene, producing societies documented ethnographically in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. The chapter's author cautions explicitly that those contact-era societies "are not simulacra of pre-contact societies" — colonial disruption transformed population size, distribution, and the scale of social networks.

The scope is regional (Alaska to Oregon coast) but internally diverse: ecological variability in latitude, wave exposure, precipitation, and glacial history drove divergent local trajectories. "Diversity in cultural trajectories for the ancestors of different groups tracks ecological variability at local, sub-regional, and regional scales" (Moss).

## Causal Drivers

**Ecological abundance and patchiness.** The Northwest Coast is one of the world's most productive coastal environments. Salmon, Pacific cod, herring, rockfish, lingcod, flatfish, and dozens of other species contributed to an economic base in which "fish typically account for 80–85 per cent of the number of bones in the region's faunal assemblages" (Moss). Spatial and seasonal patterning in resource distribution drove the development of territorial management and storage technologies.

**Mass-capture technology and organised labour.** Fishing weirs, traps, gill nets, reef nets, and trawl nets enabled group harvests on a scale impossible through individual effort. More than 1,300 wood-stake and stone weirs and traps have been identified in Alaska, British Columbia, Washington, and Oregon, ranging in age from ~6,000 cal BP to the twentieth century. At Little Salt Lake Weir near Klawock, Alaska, features covering 75 hectares with an estimated 100,000 stakes could yield 75,000–80,000 fish annually.

**Food storage as economic infrastructure.** The ability to process fish — drying, smoking, oil rendering — into storable and tradeable commodities was as important as the catch itself. "Capturing large numbers of fish is pointless without the technological knowledge and skilled organized labour to process them for storage" (Moss). Storage created the material base for surplus, wealth accumulation, and ceremonial redistribution.

**Resource ownership and territorial systems.** Salmon streams and spawning areas were owned by particular social groups — matrilineages in the north, bilateral kin groups on the central coast, households in the south. The antiquity of this ownership extends possibly to 7,000 BP at Namu (British Columbia), where evidence of reliance on pink salmon exists for that duration. Corporate group ownership structured investment in costly infrastructure (clam gardens, fish traps, root gardens) whose payoffs justified the labour cost only if ownership could be defended across generations.

**Ecological stewardship as production strategy.** Northwest Coast peoples "managed harvests as trustees who had established long-term relationships not only with resource territories but with the plant and animal persons with whom they shared the world" (Moss). This included herring cultivation, salmon ranching, prescribed burning for berry production, and estuarine root gardening — practices that enhanced the biological productivity of key species.

## Major Phases

### Initial Settlement and Coastal Corridor (~15,000–12,000 BP)

Early human presence on the Northwest Coast follows the opening of the Pacific coastal route as Cordilleran and Laurentide ice retreated. Sea level was more than 125 metres lower than today during the Last Glacial Maximum; the earliest coastal sites are likely drowned. Evidence from Paisley Caves in interior Oregon (coprolites dated 14,340–12,000 cal BP) indirectly supports coastal migration by 15,000 BP, since the site predates the viable ice-free interior corridor. The oldest reliably dated coastal evidence includes human footprints on Calvert Island (British Columbia) at 12,600 BP and bear hunting on Haida Gwaii dating to approximately the same period.

The individual known as Shuká Kaa — represented by skeletal elements from a cave on Prince of Wales Island, Alaska, dated ~11,150–11,230 cal BP — provides the earliest direct evidence of a maritime-adapted individual: "isotopic studies show that he had a diet rich in seafood" and he "and his people were skilled seafarers who used boats to travel the waters of southeast Alaska" (Moss). He is a member of haplogroup D and the Q-M3 Y-chromosome subhaplogroup, connecting him genetically to other Native American populations.

### Diversified Marine and Estuarine Economies (9,000–4,000 BP)

By 9,300 cal BP, vast numbers of salmon were caught at The Dalles on the Columbia River. At Namu (central British Columbia) people relied on pink salmon for at least 7,000 years. Harbour seal and northern fur seal hunting is documented at Kilgii Gwaay (Haida Gwaii) by 10,700 BP. Clam gardens on Quadra Island (British Columbia) have been dated to 3,500 cal BP, illustrating investments in mariculture infrastructure. Katzie ancestors built a wetland wapato garden near Pitt Meadows at ~3,800 BP, an "anthropogenic feature engineered to increase wild food plant production" (Moss).

During the Neoglacial (~3,800–2,600 cal BP), changing sea ice conditions may have shifted northern fur seal breeding grounds southward, as evidenced by pup bones found in southeast Alaska, British Columbia, Washington, and Oregon archaeological sites from this period.

### Intensification and Complexity (4,000–1,500 BP)

The period from roughly 4,000 to 1,500 BP sees intensification of all major subsistence strategies: elaboration of trap and weir systems, corporate-group herring cultivation, whaling traditions, and estuarine root gardening. Nuu-chah-nulth whale hunting has an antiquity of 2,500–4,000 years, with whale bones in their archaeological sites dating to nearly 5,000 BP (though whether these represent drift whales or active hunting is uncertain). Corporate group structures capable of building and maintaining major fishing infrastructure were in place in the Broughton archipelago at least 2,500 years ago.

Steller sea lion hunting by Northwest Coast First Nations is documented for at least 5,000 years. The Deep Bay fish trap on Vancouver Island, used for capturing herring and their predators during spring spawning, required "tremendous labour to build and maintain" and was supported by a corporate group structure in place for at least 2,500 years (Monks, cited in Moss).

### Elaboration of Trade, Ceremony, and Plant Management (1,500 BP–Contact)

Introduction of Haida tobacco (*Nicotiana quadrivalvis*) from the dry interior or further south is estimated at 1,000–500 years ago. The Chinook of the Lower Columbia intensified wapato production, owning fields, developing specialised harvest canoes, and controlling competing animals — producing "large quantities of wapato for trade, bringing them great wealth" (Moss). Wood-stake weirs at Grays Harbor include aboriginal traps dating ~1,000 years ago alongside later commercial traps.

## Geographic Spread

The cultural complex spans approximately 3,000 km of coastline from southeast Alaska south through British Columbia, Washington, and Oregon to northern California. Internal diversity is substantial: the Tlingit, Haida, and Tsimshian in the north; Nuu-chah-nulth and Kwakwaka'wakw on the central coast; Coast Salish in the south; Makah, Chinook, and Oregon coast groups further south. Each group managed distinct constellations of resources within its territory. The region's ecological diversity — created by latitudinal extent, wave exposure, precipitation, and complex glacial and sea level history — explains much of the archaeological variability observed across space and time.

Estuarine root cultivation practices of the Northwest Coast have been characterised as "remarkably similar to the 'wetland cultivation' methods used by Indigenous peoples in Oceania and the American tropics" (Deur, cited in Moss), suggesting convergent intensification strategies across Pacific-rim coastal environments.

## End Conditions

The Northwest Coast maritime cultural complex was not terminated but severely disrupted by European contact from the 1770s onward. The maritime fur trade, beginning in the late eighteenth century, drove sea otters "to near extinction" (Moss) and restructured exchange networks; epidemic disease collapsed Indigenous populations; and colonial policies dismantled territorial ownership systems. The processes described here — fishing, mariculture, plant management — were ongoing Indigenous practices at contact and persist in modified forms today. Several groups are actively restoring clam gardens, herring cultivation, and salmon management.

The chapter's archaeological record ends effectively at contact (~1750–1800 CE for most areas), but as Moss notes, Northwest Coast environments "are in a sense adapted to the sociocultural and political systems . . . that have coexisted with them . . . for long periods of time" (Balée and Erickson, cited in Moss), indicating a deep co-evolutionary relationship between these societies and the ecosystems they managed.

## Historiography

**Sources.** The Northwest Coast has an unusually rich archaeological record for a non-agricultural society, owing to waterlogged intertidal sites that preserve organic materials (wooden stakes, netting, basketry) rarely found elsewhere. Shell middens are ubiquitous and allow zooarchaeological analysis of resource use over millennia. Genetic and isotopic analysis of human and faunal remains has expanded the evidence base since the 1990s.

**Scholarly debates.** The chapter engages several active debates: whether the region was settled by coastal migration before or alongside the overland Clovis route; whether Northwest Coast peoples were "complex hunter-gatherers" or whether terms like "salmon rancher," "horticulturalist," and "mariculturalist" more accurately capture their economies; and the degree to which ethnographically documented societies reflect pre-contact conditions given 250 years of colonial transformation.

Moss argues explicitly against the "complex hunter-gatherer" framing, preferring "fishers and food producers" to highlight active resource management rather than passive foraging. She also argues the rigid division of labour often attributed to Northwest Coast fishing "breaks down under a close review of ethnographic sources."

**Dating.** The earliest Northwest Coast occupation dates depend heavily on sites away from the coast itself (Paisley Caves) and on submerged or drowned coastal sites that remain archaeologically inaccessible. Sea levels ~125 m lower than today during the LGM mean the earliest coastal evidence is likely lost. The post-LGM dates from Meadowcroft (13,400 cal BP) and Monte Verde (14,000 cal BP) are now broadly accepted, whereas the deeper pre-LGM dates (22,000 and 33,000 BP respectively) are not.

**Collection coverage.** [[jones-matsuda-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v1-2022]] provides strong archaeological coverage through ch. 26 (Moss), which draws extensively on zooarchaeology, palaeobotany, and genetic studies. Pre-contact social organisation (potlatch, ranking, ceremonialism) is referenced but not the chapter's focus; those dimensions are better covered by ethnographic and ethnohistorical sources not in this collection. The chapter is explicit that it cannot "adequately describe the incredible resource variability across the Northwest Coast."

## Collection Coverage Note

The Cambridge History of the Pacific Ocean, Vol. 1 (ch. 26, Moss) provides good archaeological depth for Northwest Coast subsistence and technology. Social complexity, ceremonialism, and post-contact history are underrepresented in this chapter. No dedicated North American Pacific coast volume is currently ingested; coverage of contact-era societies and the maritime fur trade remains a gap.
