# African Environmental History

## Definition and Scope

**African environmental history** is the study of how Africa's diverse ecosystems have changed over time through the interaction of physical geography, human agency, long-distance trade, colonial rule, and global commodity markets. The field was given its foundational statement in John Iliffe's *Africans: The History of a Continent* (1995), which proposed "the human struggle against nature" as "the central text of Africa's prehistory." The key analytical question for the modern period, as historian James McCann frames it in [[parker-reid-oxford-modern-african-history-2013]], is whether Africa's modern ecological patterns are still distinctive or exceptional in comparison with Asia, Europe, or the Americas under the pressures of imperialism, industrialization, and global trade — or whether colonial-era change was continuous with earlier dynamics.

## Causal Drivers

### Geological and climatic foundations

Africa is the oldest continent, the largest fragment of Gondwanaland, and its soils and geomorphology reflect immense geological age combined with more recent volcanic upheaval. The continent ranges from the Danakil Depression (120 m below sea level) to Mount Kilimanjaro (5,895 m). Forty per cent of Africa's land has a slope of more than eight degrees, producing chronic erosion by rain and river systems. The Great Rift Valley formed c. 750,000 years ago. These deep structural features set the envelope within which human environmental history unfolded.

Climatic variability — especially the two-season rainfall pattern of West Africa (with the harmattan dry wind), the highland-lowland contrast in East Africa, and the arid Sahelian zone — created the foundations for distinctive agricultural and pastoral adaptations.

### Domesticate introduction via global ecological networks

Africa's environmental history is not one of isolation but of **absorption and adaptation** of genetic stock from global ecological networks. In the early Holocene (c. 7,000 BP), domestic cattle (*Bos taurus*, descended from the wild ox *Bos primigenius*) entered via the Nile Valley. The humped zebu (*Bos indicus*) arrived from South Asia c. 1500 BCE and in larger numbers after the seventh century CE, spreading across the Sahelian zone and eastern and southern Africa due to its tolerance for arid conditions. Camels, introduced in the third century CE, were particularly transformative: the milch camel lactated for eleven months of twelve, could survive without water or fresh grazing for up to ten days, and dramatically reduced long-distance transport costs, enabling the expansion of trans-Saharan trade and human settlement in arid ecologies. As environmental historian James Webb has emphasized, "camels were particularly important for human adaptation to Africa's dry ecologies."

### New World crops and the Columbian Exchange

The introduction of American staple crops — cassava, maize, and sweet potato — was a potential driver of population growth in ecologically favoured areas. The spread of these crops after 1500 created new agricultural possibilities but also new vulnerabilities: crop disease and monoculture exposure grew alongside food security gains. See [[bantu-expansion]] for earlier agricultural transitions.

### The West African forest-fallow system

By the early nineteenth century, when European observers first encountered it, West Africa's **forest-fallow agriculture** presented as a fully developed system. Its rhythms derived from two seasonal rainfall periods and the dry harmattan wind. Farmers managed fallow land not as abandoned plots but as a "careful and conscious selection process" of pioneer forest species known to have nutrient-recycling properties. Geographer Kojo Amanor documented eight tree species and nine shrubs and herbs that local farmers recognized as soil-enhancing — a body of accumulated biodiversity knowledge built up over generations of forest occupation.

### Colonial-era commodity frontiers

The colonial period introduced capitalist commodity markets that reshaped African landscapes at speed:

- **Cocoa in the Gold Coast**: Ghanaian legend attributes the first cocoa farm to blacksmith Tetteh Quashie, who brought seedlings from Fernando Pó in the 1870s. By 1890, cocoa had secured a place in the Gold Coast forest economy; railway construction from Accra through the cocoa zone in the 1910s and motorized lorry transport in the 1920s allowed world markets to fully penetrate the forest biome. By the 1940s, swollen shoot disease had destroyed 105 million trees (by 1961), and the ecology of the eastern cocoa zones had fundamentally shifted. The loss of shade cover from dying cocoa trees disrupted the symbiosis between tree crops and food crop cultivation that had previously maintained forest soils.

- **East African savanna dynamics**: Holly Dublin's work on the Serengeti–Mara ecosystem demonstrated that the woodland composition visible in the late twentieth century was not primordial but rather the product of specific twentieth-century dynamics — including the rinderpest panzootic of the 1890s, subsequent changes in elephant populations, and fire management regimes. African landscapes change over time in complex, historically contingent ways that resist static characterization.

## Major Phases

| Phase | Approximate dates | Key dynamic |
|---|---|---|
| Early Holocene adaptation | 7000–1500 BCE | Domesticate introduction via Nile corridor; early African food production |
| Pastoralist spread | 1500 BCE–700 CE | Zebu diffusion across Sahelian zone; camel introduction |
| Forest-fallow consolidation | ~1000–1800 CE | Development of West African forest-fallow system as sophisticated adaptive technology |
| Columbian exchange integration | 1500–1800 | New World crops introduced, potentials and vulnerabilities created |
| Colonial commodity frontiers | 1870–1960 | Export crop cultivation (cocoa, groundnuts, palm oil) restructures forest and savanna ecologies |
| Postcolonial urban footprint | 1960–present | Rapid urbanization creates new forms of environmental stress and land-use change |

## Geographic Spread

The process operates differently across Africa's distinct ecological zones:

- **West Africa**: The forest-fallow system; the cocoa and oil palm commodity frontiers; the harmattan's influence on seasonal patterns.
- **East Africa**: The highland-lowland contrast; Great Rift Valley lake ecosystems; savanna-woodland dynamics shaped by elephant populations, fire, and colonial wildlife management.
- **Sahelian zone**: Camel pastoralism; the interaction of drought, overgrazing, and desertification.
- **Equatorial Africa**: Rainforest resilience and fragility; the Congo basin as a major carbon sink.

## Interaction with Other Processes

African environmental history interacts closely with:
- [[early-african-food-production]] — the deep-time foundation of the agricultural systems that colonial commodity production disrupted
- [[bantu-expansion]] — the primary vehicle for spreading agricultural and metallurgical technologies across sub-Saharan Africa
- [[colonial-rule-in-africa]] — colonial administrators, dams, railways, and forced resettlement created new disease environments and disrupted local systems of environmental management
- [[disease-and-demography-in-africa]] — ecological disruption (rinderpest, drought, sleeping sickness) was directly implicated in the demographic catastrophes of the late nineteenth century
- [[trans-saharan-trade]] — camel introduction transformed both the ecology of arid zones and the economics of long-distance commerce

## End Conditions

This is an ongoing process. The urban footprint of postcolonial African cities (among the fastest-growing in the world) has created new forms of environmental pressure. The anthropocene intersects specifically with Africa's environmental history through climate change, which is expected to affect the continent's arid and semi-arid zones disproportionately. See [[anthropocene]].

## Historiography

African environmental history as a **field** emerged in the 1970s–80s, influenced by broader environmental history (William Cronon, Alfred Crosby) and by the Africanist tradition's turn to rural, agrarian, and ecological subjects. Key works:

- **John Iliffe**, *Africans: The History of a Continent* (1995): established human-environment struggle as the master theme of African prehistory; ecological determinism critiqued as too sweeping by Thomas Spear and others.
- **James McCann**: specialist in West African and Ethiopian agricultural history; authored *Maize and Grace* on the ecological history of New World crops in Africa.
- **James Webb**: work on camel introduction and human adaptation to arid ecologies.
- **Kojo Amanor**: Ghanaian geographer documenting the sophisticated ethnobotanical knowledge embedded in forest-fallow management.
- **Holly Dublin**: ecological work on Serengeti-Mara dynamics showing the historically contingent character of East African savanna landscapes.

A recurring debate concerns whether colonial rule constituted a **distinctive environmental rupture** or whether it accelerated pre-existing dynamics. The weight of current scholarship suggests both: the colonial commodity economy did qualitatively transform certain ecological zones, but its effects were mediated by and layered onto African environmental practices of great depth and sophistication.
