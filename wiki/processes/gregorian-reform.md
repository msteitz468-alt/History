## Definition and Scope

The **Gregorian Reform** (named for [[gregory-vii|Pope Gregory VII]], r. 1073–85,
but broader than his pontificate) is the movement to purify, free, and reorganise
the Latin Church that ran from the imperial reform of the papacy in the 1040s to the
Concordat of Worms in 1122. [[luscombe-ncmh-v4-2004|NCMH IV]] (I. S. Robinson, Ch. 9)
stresses that contemporaries understood reform (*reformare*, *renovare*, *reparare*,
*restaurare*) as a **backward-looking renewal** — the restoration of the Church to an
idealised "golden age" of apostolic purity from which a corrupt "iron age" had
fallen — to be accomplished "with the hammer of just government." Its targets were
three abuses cast as diseases infecting the body of the Church:

- **Simony** — the buying and selling of church offices and sacraments (named for
  Simon Magus), treated by the reformers as heresy;
- **Nicolaitism / clerical marriage** — the "unchastity of clerks," against which the
  reformers imposed clerical celibacy;
- **Lay investiture** — the conferring of ecclesiastical office (with ring and staff)
  by lay rulers, and the homage of prelates to kings — the practice whose prohibition
  precipitated the [[investiture-contest|Investiture Contest]].

The unifying principle was the ***libertas ecclesiae*** — the freedom of the Church
from lay control — and, with it, a sharp new insistence on separating spiritual
authority (*sacerdotium*) from royal power (*regnum*), with the former supreme.

## Causal Drivers

- **Monastic reform.** The reforming energies of [[cluny|Cluny]], Lorraine (Gorze),
  and (in Germany) Hirsau supplied the personnel, ideals, and vocabulary of renewal;
  all the popes from 1073 to 1119 had been monks.
- **The imperial reform of the papacy.** [[luscombe-ncmh-v4-2004|NCMH IV]] (Cowdrey,
  Ch. 8) dates the turning point to **1046**, when Emperor **Henry III** crossed the
  Alps, set aside three rival claimants at the **Synod of Sutri**, and installed a
  series of reforming popes — above all **Leo IX** (1048–54), who took reform on the
  road through councils in Italy, France, and Germany. Paradoxically, the reform that
  emperors began would turn against the empire.
- **Canon law.** The recovery and reorganisation of [[ius-commune|canon law]] gave
  the reformers an arsenal of ancient authority and a programme of jurisdiction.

## Major Phases

1. **Imperial reform (1046–1057).** Sutri, Leo IX's councils, the gathering of
   reform-minded clergy (Humbert of Silva Candida, Peter Damian, Hildebrand) at Rome.
2. **Papal emancipation (1059–1073).** The **Papal Election Decree of 1059** vested
   election in the cardinals, freeing the papacy from both Roman factions and imperial
   control; the alliance with the Normans of southern Italy.
3. **The Gregorian confrontation (1073–1085).** Gregory VII's radical assertion of
   papal supremacy (the *Dictatus Papae*, 1075) and the decrees against lay
   investiture ignite the [[investiture-contest|Investiture Contest]] with
   [[henry-iv-holy-roman-emperor|Henry IV]] — excommunication, Canossa (1077), the
   schism of the antipope Wibert of Ravenna ("Clement III"), and Gregory's death in
   exile at Salerno (1085).
4. **Continuation and settlement (1085–1122).** Urban II (who launched the
   [[crusades|First Crusade]] in 1095) and Paschal II carry the reform forward; the
   conflict is resolved by the **Concordat of Worms (1122)**.

## Interaction

The reform reshaped almost everything in the volume: it produced the
[[papal-monarchy|papal monarchy]] and the institutional Church of cardinals, legates,
and councils; it drove the [[investiture-contest]] and the long papal–imperial
struggle; it fed the explosion of [[medieval-monastic-revival|new religious orders]];
its demand for an ordered law of the Church drove the systematisation of
[[ius-commune|canon law]]; and its sanctification of war in the Church's service
helped generate the [[crusades|Crusade]].

## End Conditions

The reform did not end so much as institutionalise itself. The **Concordat of Worms
(1122)** settled the investiture question by compromise — the king surrendered
investiture with ring and staff (the spiritual symbols) but retained a role in
episcopal elections and granted the temporal *regalia* — and the reforming impulse
passed into the permanent machinery of the papal monarchy, confirmed by the First
Lateran Council (1123).

## From Logan, *A History of the Church in the Middle Ages* (2013)

[[logan-history-of-the-church-in-the-middle-ages-2013|Logan]] **rejects** the traditional labels “Gregorian Reform” and “Hildebrandine Reform” as inappropriate: they wrongly imply a papally led movement from the start and give Gregory VII a central role hard to sustain. He prefers **“Eleventh-century Reform”** for variegated forces into the first quarter of the twelfth century.

**Two phases:** reform **before 1049** was local (bishops, abbots, secular rulers) while the papacy was corrupt; from **[[leo-ix|Leo IX]] (1049)** the papacy took control; under **Gregory VII** reform was used to **enhance papal power**, leading to conflict with German rulers not settled until the **[[concordat-of-worms-1122|Concordat of Worms]] (1122)**, and then only tenuously. Non-Cluniac centres: **Gorze** near Metz (933) and Brogne; English reform under Dunstan, Ethelwold, Oswald with kings Edmund, Edred, Edgar; Capetian and imperial support — **Henry III** set papal reform in motion via **Sutri 1046**.

**Simony debate:** Humbert’s invalidity thesis vs Damian’s validity-with-sin; Damian prevailed. **Clerical celibacy** unsettled until **Second Lateran (1139)** voided marriages of priests, deacons, and subdeacons and stripped married clergy of benefices. Reform proceeded by compiling ancient canons (**Burchard**, the **74 Titles**, **Ivo of Chartres**, **Anselm of Lucca**) rather than new decretals — feeding [[ius-commune|canon law]] as a discipline.
