# Carolingian Art and Architecture

## Definition and Scope

**Carolingian art and architecture** was the visual and built culture of the
[[carolingian-empire]] — a self-consciously **Roman and Christian** aesthetic, sponsored by the
court and the great churches, that gave material form to the imperial ideology and the
[[carolingian-renaissance]]. In [[mckitterick-ncmh-v2-1995|NCMH II]] (Nees, Ch. 30) it is set
alongside the Byzantine and Islamic worlds as one of three contemporary artistic traditions in
which "forms closely related to and expressive of their religion... were defined."

## Architecture: Aachen and the Royal Palace

The defining monument is **Charlemagne's palace complex at Aachen** — a non-urban imperial seat with
baths, a great vaulted reception hall, and the famous **octagonal palace chapel** (built by Odo of Metz),
whose form and rich marble and mosaic decoration deliberately evoked **Ravenna** (San Vitale) and imperial
Rome. Nees stresses that Aachen, though the grandest, was **not unique**: comparable palace-and-chapel
complexes existed at Ingelheim, Paderborn, Worms, and elsewhere, and Charles the Bald later built his own
Aachen-inspired palace and chapel. Such complexes asserted the **royal capital as a symbolic centre** of the
Christian empire. Church architecture developed the **westwork** and the monumental basilica (idealised in
the **Plan of St Gall**).

## The Arts of the Book and the Object

The most brilliant Carolingian achievements were in **manuscript illumination** and the luxury arts. Court
schools produced sumptuous illuminated **Gospel books** (the Ada group, the Lorsch Gospels, the Lothar Gospels
of Tours) in which books "counted as treasure" and circulated as prestige gifts among rulers. The lively,
classicising line of the **Utrecht Psalter** is among the most celebrated products of the age. Carved **ivory**
book-covers and thrones, **goldsmiths' work**, **bronze casting**, and **mosaic and wall-painting** (as at
Theodulf's oratory at Germigny-des-Prés) extended the same aesthetic across media.

## Significance

Carolingian art and architecture revived antique and Ravennate models in the service of a new Christian-imperial
ideology, established enduring building types (the westwork, the palace chapel) and the conventions of medieval
manuscript illumination, and — as the volume's Conclusion notes — helped fix "fundamental attitudes towards art"
in the Latin West, above all the accepted role of **images in religious practice**, in parallel with the
contemporary debates of [[byzantine-iconoclasm|Byzantine iconoclasm]].

## Historiography

NCMH II (Nees) situates Carolingian art comparatively (against Byzantium and Islam) and stresses the central
role of **patronage** — royal and personal — in its production. Debates concern the degree of genuine "renewal"
versus continuity with late antique and Insular traditions, the function of the Aachen chapel's borrowings from
Ravenna, and the interpretation of programmatic works like the *Libri Carolini* and the Utrecht Psalter.
