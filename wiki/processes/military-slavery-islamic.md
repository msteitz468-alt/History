# Military Slavery (Ghulam/Mamluk System)

The recruitment, training, and employment of foreign (often Turkish, Circassian, or other non-local) slaves as the core professional military and administrative elite of Islamic states, especially post-Abbasid regimes.

## Definition and Scope

"Ghulam" or "mamluk" denoted a purchased slave (often young, 10-12 years) converted to Islam, raised in barracks, trained in arms and administration, and bound by personal loyalty to master and comrades. Upon graduation/manumission, they became freedmen/clients with full legal capacity but retained surrogate kinship and patronage ties. Elite status derived from the master's rank, not servile origins. No native sons (in principle) could enter the caste.

## Causal Drivers

Response to fragmentation of Abbasid power and rise of nomadic conquerors lacking local ties. Rulers used slaves to counterbalance tribal levies or established notables. Personal household bonds (oaths, benefits, surrogate parent-child) created reliable forces. **Enabled** regimes in societies with strong parochial or lineage solidarities.

## Major Phases and Features

- Abbasid origins: Turkish guards.
- Buwayhid/Ghaznavid: regiments + iqta' support.
- Mamluk perfection (Egypt/Syria 1250–1517): entire sultanate and officer corps from slave regiments; sultan as mamluk of previous sultan.
- Social position: slave of sultan could be general/minister; status reflected master's prestige.
- Manumission + clientage preserved loyalty across generations in some cases.

## Interaction

Paired with iqta' fiscal system. Rulers maintained slave bodyguards and scribal records to enforce service. Contrasted with European feudalism (free vassals, hereditary fiefs, serf tenants). Integrated with Sufi or scholarly patronage networks for legitimacy.

## End Conditions

Declined with centralizing cash armies, European military models, and abolition of slavery in 19th-20th c. Ottoman janissaries represent a parallel/evolved form.

**Grounded in Lapidus Ch. 20 (Range 2):** "The translation of the word “ghulam” or “mamluk” into English as “slave” carries inappropriate connotations... The slave soldier was the personal property of a master... The social position of the slave, however, did not reflect his personal servitude but rather reflected the status of his master... The slave of the sultan could be a general or minister of state... military slaves were eventually manumitted and became freedmen, clients..."
