## Definition and Scope

The Chinese maritime mercantile resurgence describes the process by which ethnic Chinese merchants and settlers, after a century of reduced overseas activity caused by Ming restrictions, rebuilt their commercial dominance in maritime East Asia between roughly 1500 and 1720. The culmination was the "Chinese century" of the eighteenth century, in which "ethnic Chinese emerged as the pre-eminent diasporic community in maritime East Asia. From the East China Sea to the Strait of Melaka, Chinese junks dominated the trading lanes." (Hang, in [[jones-matsuda-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v1-2022]])

This was not a linear rise but a contingent process: "The emergence of the 'Chinese century' resulted from a contingent confluence of regional developments during the preceding sixteenth and seventeenth centuries." These included the commercialization of the Ming economy, the wokou crisis, the Zheng family's organizational genius, European cooperation with Chinese settlers, and the dispossession of Ming loyalists after 1644.

## Causal Drivers

### Ming Sea Ban and its Paradox

The Ming Dynasty's sea ban (*haijin*) of 1371 "heralded the beginning of the end of this burst of Chinese maritime activity." By restricting all foreign trade to the tributary framework, the ban paradoxically created the conditions for its own subversion. "The restrictive policy aimed to achieve a government monopoly over all foreign trade." Zheng He's seven voyages (1405–35) attempted to supplant private merchants with state trade, but their discontinuation in 1435 left a power vacuum.

The commercialization of the late Ming economy, with voracious silver demand that domestic mines could not meet, and a direct trade ban with Japan (the main silver supplier), made the ban increasingly unenforceable. By the 1540s "serious official efforts to put teeth into the prohibition" triggered "a massive outbreak of piracy" — the *wokou* (wrongly called "Japanese pirates") who "were multi-ethnic in composition" but led primarily by Chinese.

### The Wokou Networks

The wokou syndicate of Wang Zhi (d. 1559) — "a former salt merchant" operating from Hirado, Kyushu — "owned hundreds of ships and a formidable following of 2,000 men" and "worked closely with the Portuguese, Japanese daimyo, and Patani, buying and selling firearms." Wang's downfall came when he sought legitimization from the Ming court and was betrayed and executed. Subsequent pirate leaders "lacked further opportunities for reconciliation with the court." One, Limahong, fled to the Philippines and attempted to seize Manila; another, Lin Daoqian, settled in Patani (c. 1566), married a prominent woman, assumed the *syahbandar* role, and epitomized the absorption of Chinese into Southeast Asian Muslim polities.

The coexistence of the "legend of Maiden Lin" (celebrating ethnic Chinese purity) alongside Lin Daoqian's assimilation marks "the beginning stages of a larger wave of Chinese immigration overseas in numbers that would increasingly strain the capacity of many local societies to absorb them."

### The 1567 Partial Opening

When the Ming court "permitted licensed private traders to sail to Southeast Asia from the port of Haicheng in Fujian" in 1567, it "converted large numbers of smugglers into legitimate merchants overnight." This did not open Japan trade, but widened the channels enormously.

### European Colonial Nodes

The establishment of European outposts — Portuguese Macao (1557) and Nagasaki (1571 foothold), Spanish Manila (1571), Dutch Batavia (1619) and Taiwan (1624), Dutch Melaka (1641) — created new institutional environments for Chinese commercial activity. The VOC's *kapitan* (headman) system and tax-farming arrangements "drew large numbers of wealthy Chinese away from purely commercial endeavours towards participation in the governance of the colony." Tonio Andrade calls this "co-colonization." Manila's Chinese population "numbered in the tens of thousands" — overwhelming the small Spanish elite who "depended upon them not just for trade but also for essential services, such as food provision and labour" — leading to the Philippine Chinese massacres of 1603, 1639, and 1662.

## Major Phases

### Phase 1: Wokou and Consolidation (c. 1500–1621)

The wokou crisis was also a consolidation episode. Wang Zhi, Lin Daoqian, Limahong, and others built the frameworks of armed Chinese overseas enterprise. The Ming military revival under Qi Jiguang and Yu Dayou in the 1560s destroyed the wokou coastal hold, but the networks and the people who manned them did not disappear — they became the substrate of the next phase.

### Phase 2: Zheng Zhilong and State Integration (1621–1644)

Zheng Zhilong (1604–61), "a native of the cosmopolitan maritime environment of southern Fujian," synthesized all previous developments. He had been baptized Catholic in Macao, lived in Japan (where he befriended daimyo and had an audience with the Tokugawa shogun), married a Japanese woman (Lady Tagawa Matsu), served as interpreter for the Dutch in Taiwan, and then turned pirate in 1621, "leading a small band of followers under the patronage of the Dutch in Taiwan."

By 1628 he had surrendered to the Ming and was appointed military commander of Fujian. He used his position to privatize the army and bureaucracy, appointing clan relatives to key posts and funding operations with trade revenues. When the Tokugawa bakufu's 1630s sea bans removed Japanese from Southeast Asian trade, "Zheng wasted no time in shoring up his position of supremacy among Chinese traders." In 1641 he established direct Fujian–Nagasaki trade, "violating the longstanding Ming ban and ending the previous cooperative arrangement whereby he authorized merchants to sail exclusively to Taiwan, and allow the Dutch to act as intermediaries." Ships flying the Zheng flag "carried silver, tea, silk, porcelain, and spices across his trading networks from Japan to Southeast Asia." Estimates suggest Zheng earned "three to four times as much per year as the VOC in all its dominions."

### Phase 3: Koxinga's Mercantile State (1646–1683)

When Zheng Zhilong was captured by the Manchus in 1646, his son Koxinga "wrested control of his father's assets and warfleet from rival kinsmen" and rebuilt the organization into something new: a full military-commercial state that at its peak "controlled some 70–80 per cent of all Chinese shipping in maritime East Asia."

Koxinga's organizational innovations were decisive. In 1653 he "established his own central bureaucracy, consisting of offices that were equivalents of the Ming Six Boards." The Revenue Office "oversaw what later became known as the Public Firm (*gongsi*), which bought and sold directly on behalf of the Zheng family." The *gongsi*, "a term whose earliest mention in the historical records appears here in conjunction with the Zheng family, most likely served as a forerunner of the numerous joint-stock associations of Chinese merchants and immigrants bearing the same name that flourished in Southeast Asia in the eighteenth century."

After Koxinga expelled the Dutch from Taiwan in 1662 (see [[western-pacific-naval-rivalry-1600-1720]]), his son Zheng Jing (1642–81) transitioned the organization towards a territorial state — naming it Dongning ("Eastern Pacification") — while continuing to control maritime trade. Jing's merchants "conducted lucrative smuggling along the mainland Chinese coast" despite the Qing sea ban; the Zheng "consistently outperformed the VOC across maritime East Asia in revenues and trading volume."

Ideologically, the Zheng propagated Ming loyalism through official calendars bearing the Yongli reign name, distributed by merchant ships across the diaspora. As Anthony Reid argues, this made possible "the legitimation, for the first time, of a Chinese identity outside China."

### Phase 4: Ming Loyalist Diaspora and the "Chinese Century" (1683–c. 1720)

Qing forces destroyed the Zheng fleet in the Taiwan Strait in 1683 and incorporated Taiwan into the Qing Empire. Yet paradoxically this defeat triggered the final surge: "large numbers of Ming loyalists took refuge at ports across maritime East Asia, where they gradually edged out competing mercantile groups." These were not isolated migrants but "entire clans [that] uprooted themselves from their native places and emigrated in groups," bringing lineage networks, institutional knowledge, and Ming loyalist identity.

The Qing's 1684 lifting of the sea ban then opened the floodgates. "This tolerance, Zhao Gang claims, was unprecedented for any previous dynasty." Southeast Asia "with its abundant natural resources and sparse population, became a natural complement to the overcrowded Chinese coast."

## Geographic Spread

The process radiated outward from Fujian and Guangdong:
- **Japan (Nagasaki):** Japanese silver drove the first phase; Tokugawa sea bans later restricted this node but it remained a lucrative Chinese commercial channel through the 1680s.
- **Taiwan:** Dutch colony 1624–1662; Zheng statelet 1662–1683; Qing province 1683+. Critical transit entrepôt for Chinese silk and Japanese silver until ca. 1660.
- **Philippines (Manila):** Parián Chinese quarter outside city walls; periodic massacres (1603, 1639, 1662) but also indispensable Chinese population.
- **Southeast Asian mainland (Cochinchina/Hội An, Ayutthaya, Patani, Cambodia Mekong Delta):** Sites of permanent overseas communities; Japanese presence faded after Tokugawa sea bans; Chinese took over routes to China and Japan by late 17th century.
- **Batavia (Dutch capital):** VOC "co-colonization" with Chinese tax farmers; remained a major Chinese commercial hub.

## End Conditions

The process does not have a clean terminus. The Qing conquest of Taiwan (1683) and the 1684 opening of trade ended the stateless Ming loyalist period and began the "Chinese century." The dispersed Ming loyalist creoles — known as Peranakan in Batavia, mestizo in Manila, and Minh Hương in Cochinchina — became the institutional scaffolding for this expansion. "They ultimately forged the infrastructure and institutions that made possible Chinese mercantile dominance in maritime East Asian trade during the eighteenth century."

## Historiography

Xing Hang (Ch30 of [[jones-matsuda-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v1-2022]]) argues the "Chinese century" was not inevitable but the product of "a contingent confluence of regional developments." He builds on Leonard Blussé's framework of the "Chinese century" (1999) and Carl Trocki's work on Chinese pioneering in Southeast Asia, while adding the institutional analysis of the Zheng organization's ideological and commercial innovations. Anthony Reid's *Southeast Asia in the Age of Commerce* provides the regional Southeast Asian context. Tonio Andrade's *How Taiwan Became Chinese* and *Lost Colony* provide detailed Taiwan-focused accounts. The collection's coverage of this period is moderate: the Cambridge Pacific Ocean Volume 1 is the strongest source; CHSEA Vol. 1 provides complementary polity-level detail.
