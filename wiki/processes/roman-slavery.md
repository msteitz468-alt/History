## Definition and Scope

Roman slavery was the legally codified institution of chattel bondage that underpinned much of the Roman economy and household structure from the mid-Republic through Late Antiquity, distinct in scale, legal elaboration, and social integration from slavery in most other ancient Mediterranean societies. Slaves in the Roman system were legally property (*res*) without personhood, yet occupied an unusually wide range of social and economic roles — agricultural labor, mining, domestic service, skilled craftsmanship, estate management, and even (for educated slaves) tutoring and administrative work — with a correspondingly wide range of lived conditions.

## Causal Drivers

The institution expanded dramatically with Rome's wars of conquest from the third century BCE onward, which supplied enormous numbers of captives (hundreds of thousands after major campaigns) funneled through organized slave markets, most famously on Delos. Sustained demand came from the Italian *latifundia* — large slave-worked agricultural and pastoral estates that displaced smallholding peasant farmers, a process linked to the [[processes/agrarian-crisis-roman-republic|agrarian crisis of the Roman Republic]] — as well as mining operations (notably Spain's silver mines) and urban households' demand for domestic slaves as a marker of status.

## Major Phases

Slavery's scale peaked during the late Republic's era of conquest (2nd–1st centuries BCE), a period also marked by major servile uprisings — the two Sicilian Slave Wars (135–132 and 104–100 BCE) and Spartacus's revolt (73–71 BCE) — that exposed both the brutal conditions on large slave-worked estates and Rome's capacity for overwhelming military suppression of servile unrest. Under the Principate, as conquest slowed, the slave supply shifted increasingly toward natural reproduction ("home-born" slaves, *vernae*) and frontier trade rather than mass battlefield captures, and manumission (formal freeing, creating the *libertus*/freedman class) became an increasingly routine and legally regulated pathway, though freed status carried lasting social and some legal disabilities.

## Geographic Spread

The institution was heaviest in Italy itself (especially the *latifundia* regions of southern Italy and Sicily) and in provinces with major mining or plantation-style agricultural economies, while remaining a significant, if less economically dominant, feature of urban household life across the empire.

## Interaction

Roman slavery interacted closely with [[processes/agrarian-crisis-roman-republic|the agrarian crisis of the Republic]], as slave-worked estates displaced the smallholding citizen-soldiery, and with the later spread of Christianity, which — while it did not abolish slavery — introduced a body of ethical teaching (accommodating rather than directly challenging the institution) that gradually reshaped attitudes toward manumission and the treatment of slaves in Late Antiquity.

## End Conditions

Roman chattel slavery did not end with a single legal abolition; it gradually transformed and diminished through Late Antiquity and into the post-Roman West, as the imperial economy fragmented, large-scale conquest (and thus mass captive supply) ceased, and slave labor was progressively supplemented and eventually substantially replaced by various forms of tied peasant labor (coloni) that prefigured medieval serfdom.
