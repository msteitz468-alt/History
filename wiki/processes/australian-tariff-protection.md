# Australian Tariff Protection and New Protection

## Definition and Scope

Australian tariff protection was the system by which manufacturing industries were sheltered from foreign competition by customs duties, first enacted at the federal level in 1902 and progressively raised until the fully protective tariff of 1908. "New Protection" was the refinement introduced by [[deakin-alfred]] that made tariff protection conditional on manufacturers paying fair wages to their workers. The two together — protection plus arbitrated wages — constituted the economic core of what Paul Kelly later called the "[[australian-settlement]]": the policy framework that defined Australian political economy from Federation to the 1980s.

## Causal Drivers

**Colonial precedent.** Protection had been Victoria's industrial policy since the mid-nineteenth century. By Federation four of the six colonies were nominally protectionist (though at varying rates and with varying ideological commitment), leaving only New South Wales under George Reid committed to free trade. The formation of a customs union at Federation made a single national trade policy inevitable; the Protectionists held incumbent advantage as Barton's first ministry.

**Labor's alliance with manufacturing.** The Labor Party, initially divided on the tariff, was won to protection by Deakin's argument that tariffs could be made conditional on fair wages — "new protection." Labor's core commitment was to the wage standard, not to protection per se; once protection was tied to the living wage it became a Labor cause. "The discipline of the caucus came to Deakin's aid, constraining former Labor free traders to vote for the first protective tariff, which was carried in 1908."

**The Deakin-Reid contest.** Alfred Deakin "had grown up in Victoria with an alliance of workers and manufacturers in support of protection. He had managed to keep an alliance between Liberal and Labor alive just long enough to impose Victoria's economic policy on the Commonwealth." George Reid's anti-socialist crusade threatened to detach this alliance by making the tariff disappear as an issue — "if successful, [it] would kill off protectionism."

## Major Phases

**Phase 1 — First tariff (1902).** Barton's formula was that the tariff "was intended to raise revenue without damage to any existing industry." The result was "less protective and less likely to raise revenue than the government planned" — the Senate combined Free Traders and Labor free-traders to reduce it.

**Phase 2 — New Protection and Harvester (1907–08).** Deakin's New Protection linked tariff protection to excise duties on local production, with the excise lifted when "fair and reasonable wages" were paid. Justice H.B. Higgins in the Harvester judgment (1907) determined that fair wages must meet "the normal needs of an average employee regarded as a human being in a civilised country" — sufficient to maintain a man, wife, and three children. Wives of trade unionists provided evidence on household expenditure patterns. The High Court ruled the specific excise mechanism unconstitutional, but Higgins adopted the living-wage standard in all subsequent awards. "Labor became firmly attached to the system of the state ensuring that a man should be able to look after his wife and family."

**Phase 3 — Full protection (1908).** The first fully protective tariff was carried in 1908, "the lasting legacy of Deakin's outsmarting of George Reid."

**Phase 4 — Post-war consolidation (1920s).** After 1918 manufacturers sought and obtained increased protection and a Tariff Board to determine future duties. This antagonised the new Country Party, which represented rural interests hurt by the tariff's effect on their costs.

**Phase 5 — Long decline and dismantlement (1950s–1988).** The economic cost was that "inefficient industries had to be given more and more protection to enable them to pay the state-determined wage rates, a nexus not broken until the 1980s."

## Interaction with Other Processes

- part_of: [[australian-settlement]] — the tariff was one of five pillars (with arbitration, white Australia, state enterprise, and imperial defence)
- contributed_to: [[australian-labour-movement]] — high wages in protected industries sustained union membership and Labor's political base
- contrasts_with: Free trade as practised in New South Wales under Reid — the tension between open trade and protection was the defining issue of the first decade of Commonwealth politics
- produced: The wage-earners' welfare state — the preference for work and adequate wages over contributory social insurance as the mechanism of welfare

## Limitations

New Protection had a gendered cost: women's wages were set at a fraction of men's, since the living-wage standard assumed a male breadwinner with dependent wife and children. "The economic cost of the system was that inefficient industries had to be given more and more protection to enable them to pay the state-determined wage rates, a nexus not broken until the 1980s."

## Historiography

Paul Kelly's *The End of Certainty* (1992) coined "the Australian Settlement" and traced its dismantlement in the 1980s under the Hawke-Keating governments. The source (Hirst, Chapter 1) treats the tariff as central to the political economy of the early Commonwealth and traces the specific legislative and judicial steps by which Victoria's economic policy was imposed on the nation through Deakin's political maneuvering. John Lack and Charles Fahey (2008) provide the standard revisitation of the Harvester judgment.
