The post-Mongol Islamic world (Hodgson's "Later Middle Period," c. 1258–c. 1500) designates the long restructuring of [[islamicate|Islamicate]] civilization following the Mongol destruction of the Abbasid Caliphate and the central Muslim cities. It is not a period of simple decline: Hodgson argues strenuously against the historiographical convention of treating it as one of "stagnation and decadence," while acknowledging genuine economic contraction, an intensification of the conservative spirit, and a deepening militarization of political life. The period is characterized instead by continuity and elaboration of patterns established in the Earlier Middle Period, absorption and conversion of the Mongols into Islamdom, further crystallization of the [[military-patronage-state]] model, continued geographic expansion of Islam (into India, sub-Saharan Africa, and Southeast Asia), and the cultural ferment that eventually issued in the sixteenth-century florescence.

## Definition and Scope

The "Later Middle Period" as Hodgson uses it runs from the Mongol sack of Baghdad (1258) to approximately 1500, when new forces — gunpowder empires, the Safavid revolutionary movement, the Ottoman consolidation — produce a qualitative shift. It covers the political, economic, social, and cultural history of Islamdom from the Nile to the Oxus (and beyond), with particular attention to the Ilkhanate territories, Egypt and Syria under the Mamluks, India under the Delhi sultanate and its successors, and Anatolia and the Balkans under the early Ottomans.

The core question Hodgson poses is whether this is a period of "decadence." His answer: economic contraction in some central lands was real but uneven; cultural conservatism was intensified but not stagnation; genuine creativity continued, often in indirect forms; and the later fifteenth century shows an unmistakable creative ferment especially in the visual arts and in Iran. What is most original about the period is the very *fact of continuity* — the Islamicate society demonstrating its strength by absorbing rather than being transformed by the Mongol conquerors.

## Causal Drivers

### 1. The Mongol Irruption and Its Aftermath

The destruction wrought by the Mongol conquests — sack of cities, killing of artisans and scholars, disruption of irrigation works — had both direct effects in the central lands and indirect effects globally. The fall of Baghdad (1258) ended the vestige of the Abbasid caliphal state, the one institution that had provided symbolic legitimacy for the Islamicate political order. Subsequent Mongol rule (especially the [[ilkhanate]]) remoulded the political and cultural climate through the introduction of the military-patronage-state model.

### 2. Arid-Zone Agricultural Decline

Hodgson devotes extended analysis to a long-term secular process: the cumulative degradation of agricultural productivity in the mid-Arid Zone (Nile to Oxus). This was not caused by the Mongols alone but had been underway since at least the late Abbasid period, driven by:

- Deforestation and erosion reducing soil moisture retention
- Salinization and silting of irrigation canals (especially severe in Iraq/Sawâd)
- Introduction of goats into previously agricultural marginal zones, destroying tree cover
- Nomadization of marginal lands as cultivation became uneconomic
- Failure of landlord initiative under patterns of multiple inheritance and absentee landholding

The Mongols presided over the *peak* of this secular decline rather than initiating it. The result was a relative economic contraction — more pronounced in the central Islamic lands (Iraq, Iran, inner Syria) than in newly Islamized peripheral regions (sub-Saharan Africa, South and Southeast Asia). This contraction narrowed the margin for cultural experimentation and reinforced the conservative spirit.

### 3. Rise of the Turkic Military Elite

From Seljuk times and intensifying under the Mongols, Turkic soldiers became the dominant military element from the Nile valley to the Afghan mountains. This occurred through two complementary channels:
- Importation of Turkic slaves (*ghulâm*/*mamlûk*) trained as cavalry and organized in corps loyal to their individual commanders
- Entry of nomadic Turkic tribes (*Türkmen*) as free pastoralists and military forces in their own right

The Mongol conquest sent additional Turkic tribal groups into the central Islamic lands and confirmed Turkic as the language of the military throughout the region. The cultural homogeneity of the military class — Turkic, transregional, and largely alienated from the civilian urban population — deepened the structural breach between civil and military life, making integrated political development more difficult and reinforcing the *a'yân-amîr* pattern of decentralized urban autonomy capped by garrison control.

### 4. The Military-Patronage State

The Mongol ruling elites institutionalized a new form of statecraft — the [[military-patronage-state]] — which Hodgson (drawing on Martin Dixon) identifies as having three characteristic features:
1. Legitimation of independent dynastic law alongside (and eventually encapsulating) the Sharî'ah
2. Conception of the whole state as a single military force, with civilian administrators enrolled as auxiliary military personnel
3. Treatment of all economic and cultural resources as dynastic appanage, to be patronized and exploited as expressions of the ruling family's magnificence

This model proved resilient: it was adopted in modified form by successor states (Mamluks, Delhi, early Ottomans, and above all the Timurids) and eventually came to full fruition in the gunpowder empires of the sixteenth century (Ottomans, Safavids, Mughals).

## Major Phases

### Phase 1: The Mongol States (1258–c. 1335)

Three major Mongol realms in Muslim territory:
- **[[ilkhanate]]** (Hülegü's descendants): Tigris-Euphrates, Iranian plateau, nominal suzerainty over Anatolian Seljuks. Capital at Marâghah (Azerbaijan). After internal conflicts between Buddhist and Muslim factions, definitively turned Muslim under Ghazan (r. 1295–1304). The Marâghah observatory became the world's leading astronomical centre. The vizier Rashîduddîn Fażlullâh produced comprehensive historical works before his execution (1318) at age 70. The Ilkhanate dissolved after Abû-Sa'îd's death (1335) into competing successor dynasties (Jalâyirids, Muẓaffarids, Kurts, Sarbadârs).
- **Chaghatay Khanate**: Syr and Oxus basins plus Yedisu steppes. Torn by conflict between Muslim southwestern elements and pagan northeastern ones; first fully Muslim ruler achieved power only in 1326. Dissolved c. 1334.
- **[[golden-horde]]**: Volga basin, extending suzerainty over Christian Russia. First Mongol state to turn Muslim (by 1290). Entered period of factional disorder after 1357.

### Phase 2: Successor States and Fragmentation (c. 1335–1370)

The dissolution of the Ilkhanate produced a landscape of competing military principalities — Jalâyirids, Muẓaffarids, Kurts, the anomalous Sarbadâr republic at Sabzavâr — none capable of providing stable central rule. Meanwhile:
- The [[mamluk-sultanate]] consolidated as the only major power to have resisted the Mongols, becoming the cultural heart of Arabic-language Islamicate civilization at Cairo.
- The [[delhi-sultanate]] expanded aggressively into the Deccan under 'Alâuddîn Khiljî (r. 1296–1316) and Muḥammad Tughluq (r. 1325–51), bringing most of the Indian peninsula under nominal Muslim authority — before dissolution from 1334 onward.
- The early [[ottoman-empire]] crossed into Thrace (1353) and reduced the Byzantine empire to a dependent ally by 1372, reaching Kosovo (1389) and near-extinction of Ottoman power at Ankara (1402).

### Phase 3: Timur (1370–1405)

[[timur]]'s career represents the culminating and most extreme expression of the military-patronage-state principle. His campaigns reached every major Muslim power from Delhi to Cairo, destroying what remained of the Ilkhanate successor states, temporarily shattering the Ottomans, and definitively ending strong central power in north India. He rebuilt Samarqand into the most splendid city of the Islamicate world and assembled artisans and scholars from every conquered territory.

### Phase 4: Timurid Florescence and Transition (1405–c. 1500)

After Timur's death and succession wars, the Timurid state stabilized under Shâhrukh at Herat (1405–47). The period saw the most brilliant cultural production of the Later Middle Period: miniature painting, Persian poetry, historiography, and speculative thought flourished at the Timurid court in ways that Hodgson explicitly compares to Renaissance Italy. Gunpowder weapons were being patronized and developed. The stage was being set for the sixteenth-century transformation.

## Geographic Spread

The post-Mongol restructuring had highly uneven geographic effects:
- **Most severely affected**: Iraq (Sawâd), inner Iran, inner Syria — areas of maximal Mongol destruction and underlying agricultural blight
- **Relatively less affected**: Egypt (protected by Mamluk resistance), India (absorbed Mongol refugees and expanded), Anatolia and Balkans (Ottoman expansion)
- **Largely unaffected by Mongol destruction but shaped by broader trends**: sub-Saharan Africa, Southeast Asia, and the Indian Ocean world, where Islamic expansion continued and accelerated

## Interaction with Other Processes

The post-Mongol Islamic world unfolded in interaction with the wider Afro-Eurasian world:
- **The Black Death (1346–48)**: The hemisphere-wide plague struck a Muslim world already ecologically stressed by agricultural decline. Its demographic consequences were severe and long-lasting in the central lands.
- **The Oikoumenic recession**: Hodgson argues that the fourteenth- and fifteenth-century economic retardation was not limited to Islamdom but was global — detectable in Chinese iron production, European urbanization, and Indian government revenues alike. The Mongol destruction may have been a contributing cause; its own effects were amplified by this wider depression.
- **Chinese commercial connections**: The ruin of the "burgeoning Chinese industrial economy" (itself partly Mongol-caused) removed a major motor of the central Asian commercial network. The Timurid court's intense interest in Chinese aesthetics — adopting Chinese artistic motifs, attempting (unsuccessfully) to introduce paper money on Chinese models — reflects both the Mongol legacy and the continuing prestige of Chinese civilization in the Islamicate world-view.

## End Conditions

The Later Middle Period ends not with a single event but with a cluster of transformations around 1500:
- The Safavid revolution in Iran (1501) created a militantly Shî'î state on a new political-ideological basis
- Ottoman consolidation produced an effectively centralized gunpowder empire
- The Mughal founding (1526) eventually created a new order in India
- European oceanic expansion began redirecting global trade away from the central Islamic route network

These developments shifted Islamicate civilization onto new institutional and cultural foundations — what Hodgson calls the "gunpowder empires" that presided over the florescence of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries.

## Historiography

The standard European and Arabist view treated this period as one of unambiguous decadence — the decay of High Caliphal civilization under barbarian pressure and conservative spirit. Hodgson argues this view conflates two distinct phenomena: (1) a genuine economic contraction and heightened conservatism in the central lands in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, and (2) a second and more serious deterioration beginning in the seventeenth century as the result of European commercial and military expansion. The "decadence" narrative anachronistically reads the second crisis back onto the first and ignores the creative ferment visible especially in the Timurid period. Hodgson's framework has been influential in reorienting scholarship toward recognizing the Later Middle Period's genuine cultural achievements.
