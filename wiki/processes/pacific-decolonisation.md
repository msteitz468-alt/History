## Definition and Scope

Pacific decolonisation designates the political transformation of the Pacific Islands from colonial rule towards formal sovereignty, free association, or continuing territorial incorporation that began in earnest around 1960 and remained incomplete as of the source's publication in 1997. The process encompasses formal independence (Papua New Guinea 1975, Vanuatu 1980, etc.), freely associated states (Cook Islands, Niue, Federated States of Micronesia, Marshall Islands, Palau), and cases where decolonisation has been structurally foreclosed — notably Hawai'i, Guam, New Caledonia, and French Polynesia.

As [[denoon-cambridge-pacific-islanders-1997]] argues, "the dichotomy of colony-or-nation may be too rigid to capture the range of Island experiences." The process is uneven, contested, and in many places ongoing — "in the French colonies, New Zealand and Hawai'i, where indigenous people still struggle for sovereignty, the term [post-colonial] is meaningless."

## Causal Drivers

- caused_by: UN Declaration on the Granting of Independence to Colonial Countries and Peoples (1960), which "declared that all peoples have the right to self-determination"
- caused_by: UN special committee on decolonisation applying international pressure
- caused_by: Organised anti-colonial movements — the Mau in Western Samoa, FLNKS in New Caledonia, Vanua'aku Pati in the New Hebrides, Tahitian RDPT, Hawaiian sovereignty movement
- contributed_to: Global decolonisation wave boosting Islander "awareness of indigenous rights"
- contributed_to: Economic transformation (cash cropping, wage labour, migration) disrupting the social bases of colonial authority
- contributed_to: University-educated Islander elite articulating political demands

## Major Phases

**Phase 1 — First wave of independence (1960s–1980s):** Samoa (1962), Nauru (1968), Tonga (1970), Fiji (1970), PNG (1975), Kiribati (1979), Vanuatu (1980), and others. Melanesian decolonisation presented the hardest challenge — nations "encompassing many different languages and cultural groups faced perhaps the most difficult task of nation-building."

**Phase 2 — Free association and compact arrangements (1980s–1990s):** Cook Islands, Niue, Marshall Islands, FSM, Palau negotiated compacts with New Zealand or the United States creating MIRAB economies dependent on Migration, Remittances, Aid, and Bureaucracy. The MIRAB model "has been vehemently rejected by Pacific politicians, for it flies in the face of national sovereignty and self-sufficiency."

**Phase 3 — Persistence of dependencies and Fourth World struggles:** For "Fourth World peoples such as Maori and Hawaiians, colonial hegemony was inescapable. The trajectory, the rhetoric, and the dynamics of their political and cultural struggles are therefore very different and often more intense." Key markers: New Zealand's Waitangi Tribunal (1975, extended retrospectively to 1840 in 1985); Hawaiian sovereignty movement; Sealord deal (1992) and Tainui land settlement (1995) in New Zealand.

**Phase 4 — Post-independence crises:** Independence created new political violence: [[wiki/events/fiji-coups-1987|Fiji coups (1987)]], [[wiki/events/bougainville-civil-war-1988|Bougainville civil war (1988–)]], the 'Uvea massacre in New Caledonia (1988).

## Geographic Spread

Decolonisation proceeded fastest in Polynesia and those Micronesian territories where the United States sought to terminate trusteeship obligations. It proceeded more slowly and violently in Melanesia (PNG, New Caledonia, Vanuatu). It has been structurally blocked for Hawai'i, Guam, and New Caledonia, where settler populations or strategic interests precluded independence. "Some Islands have been affiliated into metropolitan nations, without plebiscite — the Torres Strait Islands to Australia; Hawai'i, American Samoa and Guam to the United States; Rapanui to Chile; Irian Jaya to Indonesia; Tokelau to New Zealand; New Caledonia, Wallis and Futuna, and Tahiti to France."

## End Conditions

Pacific decolonisation remains open-ended. "The 'End of Insularity'" framing in [[denoon-cambridge-pacific-islanders-1997]] suggests that rather than a clean transfer of power, the process is producing transnational Islander communities that render formal state sovereignty only one element of an emerging Pacific political reality. "New pan-state societies are created as Islanders tap new resources, facilitated by the ease of air transport and satellite communications."

## Historiography

The source critiques both nationalist and colonial historiographies. It notes that "for most Pacific peoples under colonial rule, the independent nation was a self-evident political goal; but the dichotomy of colony-or-nation may be too rigid." It also critiques dependency theory and the MIRAB model as frameworks that, while analytically useful, are "belittling" because they "mask relationships of dominance" and ignore that "it is not charity but the labour of family members abroad — elite and working class — that produces remittances."

The collection of sources in [[denoon-cambridge-pacific-islanders-1997]] is strong on Polynesia and Melanesia; weaker on Micronesian decolonisation politics in detail.
