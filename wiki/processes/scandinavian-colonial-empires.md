## Definition and Scope

Scandinavian colonialism encompasses the overseas territorial and commercial ventures of Denmark-Norway and Sweden-Finland from the early modern period through the nineteenth century. The scope is broader than tropical empire: it includes the inherited Norwegian crown lands of the Faroe Islands, Iceland, and Greenland, together with seventeenth-century ventures in India, West Africa, North America, and the Caribbean. "All of the Scandinavian colonies were rather small. Even though Iceland is a large island and Greenland the world's largest, only limited coastal areas were accessible to the Europeans" (L13774–13776). Relatively few Scandinavians settled in the tropical colonies, yet they mattered economically — especially to Denmark — through shipping, trade, sugar-refining, insurance, and slave-trading.

This article covers the Danish empire in detail. The Swedish effort was smaller and shorter-lived: New Sweden in Delaware lasted seventeen years (1638–55); St Bartholomew in the Caribbean was held 1784–1878. For the Swedish Baltic empire (Finland, Estonia, Livonia, Pomerania), see [[swedish-empire]] and [[struggle-for-baltic-supremacy]].

## Causal Drivers

**Mercantilist policy and chartered companies.** Both crowns used the chartered company model, heavily borrowed from the Dutch. The Danish East India Company charter of 1616 "was to a high degree simply copied from the Dutch of 1602" (L12884–12886). Companies were vehicles for mercantilist policy: controlling trade routes, channelling colonial revenues to the crown, and supporting money income through customs and employment.

**Danish Sound Due wealth.** Denmark's command of Sound revenues provided both the liquid capital and the political motivation for colonial investment. Colonial ventures extended Danish commercial reach beyond the Sound.

**Neutral-flag advantage.** Denmark-Norway's neutrality during inter-European wars (especially 1688–1713, 1775–1807) made Danish-flagged ships attractive as carriers for belligerents. "The Revolutionary and Napoleonic Wars between France, England, the Netherlands and Spain brought about an immense demand for tonnage under the neutral Danish flag" (L13057–13059). This temporarily made the India trade and Caribbean entrepôt extremely profitable.

**Transatlantic slave trade integration.** The Gold Coast forts and Caribbean colonies formed an integrated Atlantic system: "The colonies in the West Indies and on the Gold Coast were integral parts of an Atlantic system, primarily by virtue of the transatlantic slave trade which was necessary in order to produce the costly cane sugar in the plantations in the Caribbean" (L13192–13195). Over 100,000 enslaved Africans were transported on Danish ships (L13222).

## Major Phases

### Inherited Atlantic territories: Faroe Islands, Iceland, Greenland

**Faroe Islands.** Together with Norway, the Faroese came under the Danish crown in 1380 (L12537–12538). The Reformation (1540) made church land — nearly half the arable — into crown land. Chartered companies held trading rights during the sixteenth–seventeenth centuries; in 1655–1709 the islands were enfeoffed to the king's creditors. A Danish crown trade monopoly operated 1709–1856. The monopoly suppressed fisheries in favour of woollen goods (stockings and jerseys) and "hampered all initiatives as no private activity was allowed to be profitable" (L12602–12603). Monopoly abolition in 1856 unleashed a deep-sea fishing economy that transformed the islands. Population in 1860: 8,922 persons, so poor they were exempt from state taxes (L12605–12606). The Faroese became a Danish county (amt) in 1821 and part of the Kingdom of Denmark by the 1849 constitution. A national linguistic and cultural movement emerged in the 1870s.

**Iceland.** Populated ca. 870 by Norse settlers, under Danish rule from 1380. A Danish trade monopoly 1602–1787 created stagnation; Iceland became a monoculture of woollen goods and dried fish. The 1703 census found 50,358 inhabitants; a smallpox epidemic 1707–9 killed thousands, reducing population to just over 34,000 (L12674–12676). In 1783 a volcanic eruption poisoned the island: between 1783 and 1785, a third of Iceland's cows, three-quarters of its sheep, and around 10,000 people (20 per cent of the population) died from starvation (L12728–12730). Further smallpox in 1786 killed 1,500 more. Danish assistance "was very limited and arrived much too late" (L12731–12732). The monopoly was abolished in 1787. Absolutism was introduced in 1662; the ancient Althing (Alpingi) lost real power. Population reached 69,763 by 1870 (L12678).

**Greenland.** Colonisation launched in earnest from 1721 under missionary Hans Egede (founding Godthaab/Nuuk in 1728). European diseases — especially smallpox — "carried off large numbers of Eskimos" (L12785). The Royal Greenland Trading Company established in 1776 held a monopoly lasting until 1950. A local board system (forstanderskaber) gave Greenlanders some local representation. Illiteracy "had been almost eradicated by the middle of the nineteenth century" through mission schools including German Moravian Brethren (L12849–12851). Population ca. 6,000 in 1805, reaching only 9,720 in 1880. Cryolite mining from 1807 (intensified after 1859) added a new export commodity.

### Asia: The Danish India trade

**Founding of Tranquebar (1620).** The Danish East India Company (chartered 1616) sent its first expedition under nobleman Ove Gjedde. After 535 days and many dead sailors, the fleet reached the Coromandel Coast and negotiated a treaty with the Nayak of Tanjore, who granted the town of Tranquebar for a fort and factory (L12898–12904). Fort Dansborg was erected; a small garrison, traders, and a pastor were left. The Company was dissolved in 1650 after only twenty-three expeditions in its first phase (L12913–12924). A relief expedition in 1669 found Tranquebar still in Danish hands.

**Revival and expansion.** A new charter in 1670 revitalised the Danish East India Company. By 1674 a Bengal factory was re-established; around 1700 a Malabar Coast factory at Oddeway Torre. Crucially, in 1705, Moravian Brethren missionaries established the "very first Protestant mission in India" at Tranquebar, learning Tamil, translating the Bible, and founding a printing press (L12940–12942). The Company went bankrupt in 1729 after trade stopped during the Great Northern War.

**The Asiatic Company era (1732–1807).** A new Asiatic Company (1732) sent one ship per year to India and, more profitably, one ship directly to Canton. Tea from China generated "the largest returns and dividends" (L12981–12982). The Company expanded to factories at Calicut (1752) and Colachel (1755) on the Malabar Coast; Serampore (Frederiksnagore) was established in Bengal in 1755 on the River Hooghly near Calcutta (L12994–12996). From 1772 until 1807 the combined Company and private India trade produced cargoes worth 73 million Danish rix-dollars — in a state whose annual budget was 6 million rix-dollars (L13075–13080).

**Colonial character.** In Tranquebar ca. 3,000 inhabitants were Tamil Hindi with Muslim and Catholic minorities; only 100–200 Danes. The territory of ~50 sq km with population of ~20,000 paid land taxes to the Danish East India Company (L12951–12955). "It was a deliberate Danish principle to interfere as little as possible in the affairs of the local people, as it would have been economically destructive and militarily impossible to try to impose a rule which conflicted with the social and religious norms of the Indians" (L13050–13053).

**Decline and sale.** British occupation of Tranquebar (1801, 1808–15) effectively integrated Serampore into the British economy. After 1815 the colonies lost significance as trade hubs. Both were sold to the British East India Company in 1845 (L13124–13127). Denmark also attempted colonisation of the Nicobar Islands three times, but malaria made settlement impossible. Britain took sovereignty in 1868 (L13129–13131).

### West Africa: The Gold Coast

**Swedish pioneer, 1645–63.** Sweden-Finland sent expeditions to the Gold Coast around 1645. The Swedish African Company (chartered 1649) established Fort Carolusborg at Cape Coast and smaller factories at Anomabo, Osu, Butri, and Takoradi under commander Henrich Carloff. The commodities sought were gold, ivory, slaves, and sugar (L13146–13149). In 1663 the Dutch expelled the Swedes from the Gold Coast permanently (L13161–13163).

**Danish Gold Coast, 1659–1845.** The Danes established Fort Frederiksborg at Fetu and then Fort Christiansborg at Accra. In 1666 the Dutch States General recognised Danish rights to these forts and the Cape Coast factory (L13181). The West India and Guinea Company (founded 1671) integrated Gold Coast and Caribbean activities: "The colonies in the West Indies and on the Gold Coast were integral parts of an Atlantic system, primarily by virtue of the transatlantic slave trade" (L13192–13195). Enslaved Africans became the principal Danish export commodity; "almost all ships under the Danish flag returned via the West Indies" (L13210–13211). In 1693 Africans captured Fort Christiansborg and held it for nearly a year (L13204–13205).

**Slave trade scale.** Table 14.1 in the source records 344 Danish slave voyages transporting at least 85,650 Africans 1660s–1806; total estimated at "well over 100,000 slaves" (L13222). The peak decade was 1793–1802: 125 voyages transporting ~24,900 Africans (2,490/year). Denmark enacted the world's first slave-trade abolition decree in 1792, taking effect in 1803 (L13332–13339). However, the transition period paradoxically saw more slaves than ever transported: 2,500 annually 1793–1802 (L13347–13348). After abolition a "not insignificant illegal Danish slave trade was carried on" (L13349–13350). Danish forts sold to Britain in 1845 for £10,000 (L13382–13383).

### North America: New Sweden (1638–1655)

A Swedish South Sea Company (later New Sweden Company) with half Swedish, half Dutch capital sent two ships from Göteborg to the Delaware River in 1638. The colonists signed a treaty with the Lenape people and settled the west bank up to the Schuylkill River (present-day Philadelphia area), erecting Fort Christina at what is now Wilmington (L13389–13397). Twelve expeditions followed 1638–55 carrying settlers (including pardoned criminals and landless Finnish peasants), provisions, and goods. The colony expanded to cover parts of modern Delaware, New Jersey, and Pennsylvania, with scattered settlements bearing Swedish and Finnish names (L13403–13410). In August 1655, Dutch governor Stuyvesant struck with a superior fleet, forcing the Swedes' immediate capitulation. Final population: 400 Swedes and Finns, most of whom chose to stay (L13434–13436). The colony was ceded to Britain at the Peace of Breda (1667), becoming part of Delaware. Swedish pastors continued to be sent until 1783; "the Swedes set up the very first Lutheran communities in North America, and the Holy Trinity Church at Christina is today the oldest stone church building in the United States" (L13442–13444).

### Caribbean: Danish West Indies and Swedish St Bartholomew

**Danish West Indies (1672–1917).** Governor Jørgen Iversen arrived in 1672 with a small flotilla at St Thomas, building Fort Christian. Tobacco and indigo gave way to sugarcane, which required slave labour: the West India and Guinea Company formally adopted the triangular slave trade in 1674. St Thomas (1672), St John (1718), and St Croix (bought from France 1733) together comprised 330 sq km, the full extent of the Danish West Indies (L13522–13523). The state took over all Company assets in 1755 when the colony's composition was 228 whites and 3,481 slaves in St Thomas; 212 and 2,031 in St John; 1,323 and 8,897 in St Croix — "the unfree made up 90 per cent of the inhabitants" (L13534–13537). Peak slave population 35,235 in 1802 (L13575–13576).

St Thomas became a free port in 1764 and regional entrepôt; in 1782 during the American War of Independence 227 Danish ships arrived in a single year (L13568–13570). Emancipation was forced by slave riot in St Croix: Governor-General Peter von Scholten immediately emancipated all slaves on 3 July 1848 (L13613–13614). Former slave owners received 50 dollars per slave compensation in 1853 (L13635–13636). Charlotte Amalie became Denmark's second largest town in the 1830s, dominated by British, French, and German merchant houses. The islands were sold to the United States in 1917 and are now the US Virgin Islands (L13683–13685).

**Swedish St Bartholomew (1784–1878).** France ceded the island to Sweden in 1784 in exchange for staple rights in Göteborg. Sweden established Gustavia as a free port and charter hub for Caribbean transit trade. A Swedish West India Company (1787) administered jointly with the crown, "permitted to participate in the transatlantic slave trade, but apparently it did not avail itself of this opportunity" (L13703–13705). However, a "transit trade in slaves went through Gustavia" until 1813, and Gustavia "around 1805 had become an important port in this respect — maybe almost as busy as Tortola" (L13741–13743). Emancipation in 1847. Sold to France in 1878 (L13761–13763).

**Guadeloupe (1813–14).** Sweden formally held sovereignty for fifteen months before the Peace of Paris returned the island to France (L13768–13772).

## Geographic Spread

Danish colonial holdings spanned the North Atlantic (Faroe Islands, Iceland, Greenland), the Indian Ocean (Tranquebar, Serampore, Malabar and Bengal factories), West Africa (Gold Coast forts from Cape Coast to the Volta), and the Caribbean (St Thomas, St John, St Croix). Swedish colonial ambitions were geographically scattered — North America, West Africa briefly, one Caribbean island — and lacked the integrated system Denmark developed via the slave trade triangle.

## Interaction

- [[struggle-for-baltic-supremacy]]: Wars between Denmark and Sweden disrupted colonial operations (e.g. Danish seizure of Swedish Gold Coast forts, 1658; British occupation of Danish colonies during Napoleonic War).
- [[scandinavian-absolutism]]: Crown companies were instruments of mercantilist absolutist policy; state takeovers of company assets (1755 in West Indies, 1777 in India) reflected absolutist state capacity.
- [[fiscal-military-state-scandinavia]]: Colonial revenues (Sound Due, sugar refining, India trade) contributed to the fiscal base; wars financed by this base then disrupted colonial trade.
- [[militarisation-of-scandinavia]]: Colonial garrisons were small but symbolically important; naval capacity required for colonial trade routes.

## End Conditions

Danish tropical colonialism wound down piecemeal: Indian colonies sold 1845, Gold Coast forts sold 1845, Danish West Indies sold to the United States 1917. Greenland remained Danish (becoming a county in 1953). Sweden sold St Bartholomew to France in 1878. The retreat resulted from competition with larger colonial powers, declining profitability after slave-trade abolition and emancipation, and the small demographic and financial base of the Scandinavian states relative to Britain, France, and the Netherlands.

## Historiography

Erik Gøbel's chapter in [[kouri-olesen-cambridge-history-scandinavia-v2-2016]] provides the synthetic overview drawn on here. The series *Vore gamle tropekolonier* (Our Old Tropical Colonies, 1966) remains the standard Danish reference for individual territories. For the India trade, Ole Feldbæk's *India Trade under the Danish Flag 1772–1808* (1969) is foundational. For the slave trade, Gøbel's own articles on Danish shipping on the triangular route and P. O. Hernæs's *Slaves, Danes, and African Coast Society* (1995) are authoritative. The Danish slave-trade abolition decree of 1792 — first in the world — has received attention in Gøbel, *Det danske slavehandelsforbud 1792* (2008). For New Sweden, Dahlgren and Norman, *The Rise and Fall of New Sweden* (1988), and for St Bartholomew, Hildebrand's Swedish study (1951) and Holger Weiss's more recent work. Collection coverage is moderate for the Danish empire; weaker for the Swedish colonial ventures.
