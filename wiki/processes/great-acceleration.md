# The Great Acceleration

## Definition and Scope

The **Great Acceleration** is the abrupt, sustained, and roughly synchronous
post-1950 surge in nearly every measure of human activity and environmental impact —
energy use, population, economic output, water use, fertilizer use, urbanization,
transport, telecommunications — and in the Earth-system responses to them
(atmospheric CO₂ and methane, ocean acidification, biodiversity loss, tropical
deforestation). In [[mcneill-cwh-v7-2015|CWH VII]], J. R. McNeill treats it as the
**second and decisive phase of the [[anthropocene|Anthropocene]]**: the "age of oil"
and of nearly 2% annual population growth, succeeding the "turbulent" age of coal
(c. 1750–1950).

It is *not* simply "modernity" or "industrialization" in general — those began
earlier and more gradually. The Great Acceleration names the specific mid-twentieth-century
inflection at which the trends turned sharply upward and became genuinely global.

## Causal Drivers

- **The maturation of the [[fossil-fuel-energy-transition|fossil-fuel transition]]:**
  oil overtook coal as the leading energy source by 1960; between 1945 and 2015
  humanity burned roughly 50–150 million years' worth of fossilized sunshine. Our
  species probably used more energy after 1920 than in all prior history combined.
- **The post-war population spike:** global growth peaked around 1970 at just over
  2% per year. About **two-thirds of all the population growth in the history of the
  species occurred between 1945 and 2015** — within a single human lifetime.
- **Mass consumption and mass production:** the spread of the automobile,
  petrochemicals, the [[agricultural-transformation-modern|Green Revolution]], and
  consumer society, first in the North Atlantic and Japan, then far more widely.
- **The rapid post-1950 diffusion of [[industrialization]]**, especially to East
  Asia (China increased its energy use twelve-fold in the 45 years after 1965).

## Major Phases

- **c. 1950–1965:** the take-off in the rich world (North America, Western Europe,
  Japan); high fossil-energy life still confined to ~one-fifth of humanity.
- **c. 1965–1990:** popular **[[environmentalism]]** emerges in reaction; the worst
  urban air and water pollution begins to be cleaned up in the West even as it
  worsens in the industrializing South.
- **c. 1990–present:** the center of gravity shifts decisively outside Europe and
  North America, above all to East Asia; climate change moves from prediction to
  observed, possibly self-reinforcing process (see [[contemporary]]).

## Geographic Spread

Began in the rich industrial cores; became truly global late in the twentieth
century as the acceleration's engine moved to East and South Asia. The geographic
*shift* of the acceleration is one of its defining features — and one reason its
environmental burdens and benefits are so unevenly distributed.

## Interaction

The Great Acceleration is the meeting point of several processes the wiki tracks
separately: [[industrialization]], the [[demographic-transition]], the
[[fossil-fuel-energy-transition]], and [[agricultural-transformation-modern|the
modern agricultural transformation]]. It is the proximate cause of the
[[anthropocene|Anthropocene]]'s most alarming features and the trigger for organized
[[environmentalism]].

## End Conditions

**Ongoing.** McNeill notes the population-growth component has decelerated since
~1970 (UN projections put 2050 growth below the 1800 rate), but energy use,
consumption, and greenhouse-gas emissions continue to climb. The volume leaves the
acceleration's resolution — whether it proves a self-limiting or self-reinforcing
process — explicitly open; climate change, it warns, "may well have reached a point
of no return."
