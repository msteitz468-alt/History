## Definition and Scope

The decline of the ritsuryō public-land order denotes the progressive breakdown, between approximately 743 and the early 11th century, of the institutional complex through which the Japanese state claimed sovereign control over land, population, and economic production. The ritsuryō system, modeled on T'ang Chinese administrative law, presumed: (1) a nationwide census of all subjects; (2) periodic redistribution of allotment fields (kubunden/handen) to registered households; (3) collection of a field tax (so), tribute commodities (chō), and labor-commutation taxes (yō) directly from taxable subjects; (4) maintenance of provincial granaries (shōzei) under the direct custody of capital-appointed officers; and (5) suppression of private accumulation of land, labor, and stored grain outside state channels.

All five presumptions had been effectively abandoned by the second decade of the 10th century. The process was not a sudden collapse but a structural transformation — the substitution of a negotiated, patron-client fiscal order for an authority-based redistributive one. The [[cambridge-history-japan-v2-heian]], chapter 4, provides the most detailed English-language analysis of this transition.

## Causal Drivers

### Internal contradictions of the discharge (geyu) system
The 731 reform requiring incoming provincial governors to audit their predecessors, institutionalized through the Board of Discharge Examiners (Kageyushi, est. c. 795–797), paradoxically accelerated the decay it was meant to prevent. By concentrating all fiscal responsibility in a single custodial governor ([[zuryo-provincial-governors]]), it released lower-ranking commissioned officials from any accountability, eroded the collegiate corporate identity of the provincial headquarters, and turned the central accounting relationship into a bilateral dispute rather than systematic oversight. "The balance between province and gun established by the organic code was fatally upset" (CHJ vol. 2, ch. 4).

### The suiko-loan system and private accumulation
Public suiko loans — rice lent from the stored tax-grain fund at 30-percent annual interest — were the financial backbone of provincial administration. But privately held rice stores could be lent at 50 percent, giving wealthy local gentry a competitive advantage. By the late 9th century, a substantial fraction of officially registered stored tax-grain existed only as paper obligations: tax-grain was "lent back" to private custodians who paid only the interest while retaining the principal. Sugawara no Michizane described the system explicitly in 891: "If a certain province has stored tax-grain to the amount of 1 million, in actuality an amount of 500,000 will be counted as lent back. On the day for the collection of suiko loans, with respect to lent-back grain, only the interest, not the principal, will be returned." This de facto privatization of state capital was rationalized as provincial precedent (kokurei).

### Fictionalization of census and allotment registers
The land-allotment system required accurate census data; the census required effective control over population movement; control over population movement required that local gentry cooperate with surveyors. By the mid-9th century, all three conditions had broken down. Miyoshi Kiyoyuki's 914 memorial stated: "Over half of the peasants listed in the major reports from the various provinces are fictitious." Women and children vastly outnumbered taxable males in official returns, justifying occupation of extensive allotment fields with minimal tax liability. The Council of State accepted data it knew to be false because the alternative — demanding accurate returns — would have collapsed the entire accounting structure.

The last nationwide attempt at allotment redistribution was in 806. A ruling of 801 had already extended the redistribution cycle from six to twelve years. Thereafter, each province followed its own trajectory. By the 920s, Fujiwara no Tadahira's government formally acted on Kiyoyuki's recommendation, ordering abandoned kubunden to be leased out for chishi rent — acknowledging that allotment was over.

### The growth of provincial precedent (kokurei)
The most structurally important change was the recognition by the Council of State that locally established practices could override both ritsuryo law and recent imperial edicts. From 873 onward, provincial precedent was accepted as a valid legal norm in fiscal matters. The Hizen petition of 881 demonstrates the logic explicitly: the vice-governor cited the precedent of Chikugo Province to justify using migrants and former officers as land managers, notwithstanding repeated prior edicts to the contrary. By the early 10th century, the Council's orders were effectively defining minima — the floor below which central government revenue could not fall — rather than the actual structure of fiscal relations.

## Major Phases

### Phase 1: Discharge-system reforms and custodial concentration (731–879)
The geyu system transforms provincial governance. Lower-ranking officers lose accountability; all fiscal responsibility concentrates in the single custodial governor. The gun (district) offices merge into the corporate structure of the provincial headquarters. Gun magistrate appointments devolve from capital examination to provincial selection (812) to provisional three-year probationary terms (822). The number of local irregular officials (zōshiki) proliferates, occupying tokoro (functional offices) at the provincial headquarters with hereditary tendencies.

### Phase 2: The failure of land redistribution and the growth of private accumulation (801–902)
The allotment cycle extends from six to twelve years (801), nationwide redistribution effectively ceases after 806, the register of standing crops (seibyōbo) ceases to be required by the capital (845). Private suiko at 50-percent interest proliferates; private granaries become entrenched intermediaries in the official loan system. Shōen formation accelerates. The reformist edicts of 902 under Fujiwara no Tokihira represent the last sustained attempt to restore the ritsuryo distributive order: calling for nationwide field allotment, abolition of imperial grant fields (chokushiden), restriction of shōen, and reaffirmation of tribute and labor taxes. "For the last time in history the Council called for a nationwide distribution of allotment fields" (CHJ vol. 2, ch. 4).

### Phase 3: Discretionary taxation and the kokuga domain (902–c. 1000)
The Council's 902 program fails. The 920s under Tadahira shift to a damage-limitation strategy: preventing further erosion rather than restoration. The "rule of sevenths" (shichibumpo, 914) and "rule of thirds" (sambumpo, 928) replace factual field-grading with mechanical formulas, de facto making the zuryō the real object of taxation rather than the peasant household. From the mid-10th century, the stored tax-grain system is organized around "named obligees" (fumyo) — rich gentry who assume the tax liabilities of entire villages. The old distinction between public fields (chishiden) and taxable allotment fields (sozeiden) dissolves. All cultivated land not exempt becomes kōden (public field within the provincial domain), assessed through the emerging kokuga estate management system. By around 1000, allotment fields appear in provincial registers but have no real fiscal significance.

## Interaction with Other Processes

- **[[shoen]]**: Shōen formation was both symptom and accelerant of ritsuryo decline. The two processes operated in positive feedback: as public-land controls weakened, shōen multiplied; as shōen multiplied, public-land controls became harder to enforce.
- **[[zuryo-provincial-governors]]**: The zuryō was the institutional pivot of the decline. The discharge system that created the zuryō simultaneously destroyed the collegiate ritsuryo framework and generated the adversarial fiscal relations that drove the process.
- **[[tato-field-managers]]**: The tato class filled the vacuum left by the retreating state. By the late 10th century, tato were the effective tax base: "Tax revenues were no longer available except through the rich and powerful, who by the late tenth century were, as far as agriculture was concerned, the field managers" (CHJ vol. 2, ch. 4).
- **[[heian-period]]**: The ritsuryo decline is one of the defining structural characteristics of the Heian period (794–1185), differentiating Heian Japan from the Nara period that preceded it.
- **[[ritsuryo-state]]**: The parent institution whose collapse is described here.

## End Conditions

The ritsuryo public-land order was not abolished; it was gradually rendered fictional. Its vocabulary — allotment fields, tribute taxes, census registers, stored tax-grain — persisted in official documents for decades after the underlying reality had changed. The system was replaced not by a designed alternative but by the accumulated weight of provincial precedent, discretionary practice, and patron-client arrangements. The mature shōen/kokugaryō system of the 11th–12th centuries was the institutional successor. The Owari petition of 988 — in which local gentry cited provincial precedent to establish limits on gubernatorial taxation — marks the point at which "administrative regulation" was clearly being displaced by "estate-oriented customary law" (CHJ vol. 2, ch. 4).

## Historiography

The decline of the ritsuryo public-land order has been the central problem of early Japanese history for post-WWII scholarship. Japanese historians (Ishimoda Shō, Toda Yoshimi, Nakano Hideo, Murai Yasuhiko, Abe Takeshi, and Satō Shin'ichi among many others) have debated whether the process should be understood as: (1) a linear "decomposition" of the ritsuryo system; (2) a transformation from one type of state (authority-based) to another (proprietary); or (3) a structural shift in the relationship between labor control and land control. The CHJ vol. 2, ch. 4 synthesis leans toward the third interpretation, emphasizing that the key transition was not simply privatization of land but the maturation of fumyo/tato control over peasant labor into domination over land — a shift the author locates in the late 11th to early 12th centuries rather than the 10th.

The collection covers this topic primarily through CHJ vol. 2, ch. 4. Coverage is strong for the institutional and fiscal dimensions; thinner for the social history of peasant experience.
