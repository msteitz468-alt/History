## Definition and Scope

The Southern Cone — the great triangular landmass extending from the edges of the Central Andean high plateaus southward to Tierra del Fuego — is conventionally divided by Mario A. Rivera into **five component spaces**: (1) the South-Central Andes (northern Chile, northwestern Argentina, and part of southern Bolivia); (2) the Chaco; (3) the Pampas; (4) Araucania; and (5) Patagonia. The "marginality" label long applied to the region is explicitly challenged: societies here were neither deficient versions of Andean civilization nor isolated. Rivera invokes Luis Orquera's finding that Fuegian and Patagonian groups had reached "a successful equilibrium with their environments," and notes that "the high productivity of the Fuegian and southwestern Magellanic environment permitted a population density some thirty times higher than recorded on the Pampas and continental Patagonia prior to Araucanian penetration."

The process covers the emergence of socio-cultural complexity from roughly 1000 BCE (formal archaeology of the ceramic/agropastoral horizon) to the eve of European contact (~1500 CE), though deep continuities extend to Paleoindian times.

The major discontinuity within the Southern Cone is not between "civilized" and "marginal" zones, but between the **South-Central Andes** (which was drawn into Andean civilizational networks and eventually Inka rule) and the **four regions of the Southern Cone proper** (Chaco, Pampas, Araucania, Patagonia), where distinct processes operated.

## Causal Drivers

- **Ecological diversity** — from hyperarid Atacama to forested fjords to open grasslands — generated radically different adaptive strategies; no single subsistence or technology trajectory was universal.
- **Tropical forest migrations** — The earliest formative influences in the South-Central Andes came from the tropical lowlands (traceable in modeled ceramics, clay pipes, lip-plug ornaments, and a hallucinogen complex). In the Chaco, the Tupi-Guarani sphere of influence was decisive; their horticulture, ceramic urns, canoe navigation, and mace-and-bow armament spread via demographic surges from the upper Paraná and Uruguay rivers.
- **Andean altiplanic radiation** — After c. CE 500, Tiwanaku influence from the Lake Titicaca basin overrode earlier tropical-lowland cultural profiles in the South-Central Andes, bringing urban nodes, camelid pastoralism, irrigation-based agriculture, and an iconographic system centered on the sacrificer figure, winged beings, felines, and condors.
- **Resource complementarity** — Exchange between coast, oasis, precordillera, and highland produced networks that long antedated any state. "We know that a long time before the rise of Tiwanaku, networks of exchange and ecological complementarity were facilitated by a common ideological frame."
- **Climate oscillations** — Periods of lesser humidity in the Amazon Basin, drought on the high plains, and wetter Atacama episodes drove migrations and altered access to resources.
- **Inka incorporation** (from c. CE 1450) — imposed decimal administration, mit'a labor, kurakas, and mitmaq resettlement on the South-Central Andes; Araucania and regions farther south remained outside direct Inka control. See [[inca-empire]] and [[mita]].

## Major Phases

### South-Central Andes

**Early Intermediate (600 BCE – CE 500): Chinchorro transition and Formative.**
The Chinchorro coastal tradition, notable for elaborately mummified dead, transitions into complexity from about 1500–500 BCE. At Alto Ramirez, the early Intermediate brought "a new sociopolitical system, with an economy based on reciprocity and complementarity. Rationally managed surplus and the adoption of new agricultural and livestock techniques allowed the population to grow." Key cultigens: quinua, maize, chili peppers, beans, squashes, gourds. Technology included copper and gold ornaments (sumptuary, not adaptive), hallucinogenic snuff kits, tapestry and kelim weaving, and camelid-wool textiles. Exchange nodes were the urban centers around [[lake-titicaca]].

**Middle Intermediate / Middle Horizon (CE 500–900): Tiwanaku hegemony.**
Tiwanaku political unification "covered all the north of Chile, the Argentinian northwest, and southern Peru." At the Azapa valley sequence (Cabuza Phase CE 300–700; Maytas Phase CE 700–1000), luxury goods — carved wooden beakers, gold cups, engraved bones, hallucinogenic gear — carry Tiwanaku iconography. In the San Pedro de Atacama oasis, caravan traffic to the coast, highlands, Titicaca Basin, and NW Argentina intensified. Tiwanaku influence then "faded" and a "re-regionalization" followed.

**Late Intermediate (CE 900–1350): Regional Development / Balkanization.**
Post-Tiwanaku fragmentation produced local chiefdoms maximizing autonomy. Fortresses (pukaras) proliferated as highland groups moved into western valleys. Ceramic sequences (San Miguel, Gentilar, Pocoma, Chilpe) show distinct regional styles alongside shared Aymara-linked iconography. NW Argentina chiefdoms (Hualfín, Calchaquí, Santa María) display "social stratification, industrial manufacturing, and long-distance distribution, particularly of exotic or prestigious products such as those made of copper and bronze."

**Inka Horizon (c. CE 1450 onward).**
Inka hegemony overlaid regional diversity in the South-Central Andes — waystations, forts, road segments, cemeteries, and mines signal the force of state-controlled economy. Among the Diaguita of semi-arid Chile (who had "dominated the semiarid northern region for over 700 years"), Inka invasion brought "a forcible imposition of a different system": appointment of kurakas and transportation of Diaguita as mitmaq to central Chile and Argentina to fulfill mit'a labor obligations. Yet the Diaguita federation of dual lordships retained potential for resistance; they chose a single war commander in 1541 against Pedro de Valdivia.

### The Chaco

The Chaco (northeastern Argentina, eastern Bolivia, western Paraguay) is a vast alluvial plain "characterized by semisedentary lifeways." It served as a transitional corridor, deriving from both the Amazon Basin and the Andean foothills. Archaeological data remain limited; "the scarcity of archaeological information about its peoples has exaggerated images of social simplicity." Key ceramic complexes — Las Mercedes, San Francisco (dated 620 BCE and CE 300), Lomas de Olmedo, Riberenos Plasticos — show combinations of Andean and tropical-lowland traits: corrugated ceramics, modeled figurines, cord-impression, and finger-impressed techniques. Subsistence mixed wild-food gathering (carob, palms, honey), fishing, hunting (guanaco, rhea, peccary), and limited agriculture of maize, manioc, beans, tobacco, and cotton. Metallurgy was unknown before the Spanish invasion.

### The Pampas

The Pampas (Argentine plains, Uruguay, extending to latitude 43°S) shows the Parana-Pampean cultural tradition combining Andean hunting-collecting with riverine fishing in southeastern Brazilian style. The earliest known pottery resembles the Periperi phase of eastern Brazil (dated to 880 BCE). The Ribereños Plasticos ceramic tradition dominated the lower Paraná-Uruguay, dated between CE 860 and 1200 at Salto Grande. South Buenos Aires shows north-Patagonian complex triangular points and, from the early Common Era onward, ceramic association. The late Araucanian/Tehuelche expansion into the Pampas was "but the last of these waves."

### Araucania

Araucania (south of the Bío-Bío River to the Chiloé archipelago) is the heartland of the Araucanian peoples — historic [[mapuche]] — who later expanded eastward. The subsistence base combined pine-nut (piñones) gathering, horticulture (potatoes, maize, beans, quinua), coastal fishing, and some livestock. The Pitren Phase (C-14 date of CE 660 from Huimpil) represents the earliest Araucanian development, linking to the El Molle Formative of the north and connections to NW Argentina's Candelaria culture. The Neo-Araucanian (Valdivia, distinct from the Ecuadorian culture of the same name) ceramic style shows Inka influences and lasted into the historic era. The Araucanian roots lie entirely in pottery-making culture; Monte Verde and San Vicente de Tagua Tagua Paleoindian sites pre-date and are not ancestral to the Mapuche.

### Patagonia

Patagonia (south of latitude 43°S) hosted two parallel adaptive trajectories: **land hunting** on the eastern Patagonian steppe, and **maritime sea-hunting, fishing, and gathering** in the fjords and channels on the west. The Junius Bird sequence (Fell's Cave, Palli Aike, Las Buitreras) documents the long hunting tradition. The Classic Patagonian (CE 0–600) emphasizes guanaco, rhea, and smaller-bird hunting combined with rock art ("footprint engraved" and curved geometric designs). Patagonian II / Tehuelche (CE 600 to contact) sees pottery appear (site of Shequen) and abstract step-fret rock art. In the southern channels, the Tunel site sequence at Ushuaia reveals a stabilized marine adaptation by the third millennium BCE, with many-toothed harpoons, sea-lion hunting, and abundant bone industry: "a long and stable marine adaptation... about 6,000 years of continuity, ending with the historic Yahgans."

## Geographic Spread

The process spans roughly 55° of latitude (~15°S to ~55°S), across:
- **Arid coast and oasis** (Atacama, Arica, Azapa Valley)
- **Semi-arid river valleys** (Copiapó, Huasco, Elquí, Limarí)
- **High Andean plateau connections** (Puna, Altiplano around [[lake-titicaca]])
- **NW Argentine valleys** (Hualfín, Calchaquí, Santa María)
- **Chaco lowlands** (Bermejo, Pilcomayo, Paraguay rivers)
- **Pampas grasslands and Paraná-Uruguay drainage**
- **Araucanian heartland** (central valley of Chile, Bío-Bío to Chiloé)
- **Continental and maritime Patagonia** (Santa Cruz, Tierra del Fuego)

## Interaction

- **With Andean state formation**: The South-Central Andes was dynamically linked with Andean developments — first Pukina-speaking early networks, then Tiwanaku, then Inka. See [[andean-state-formation]].
- **With tropical forest cultures**: Tupi-Guarani expansion connected Chaco, Paraná-Uruguay, and the Pampas to Amazonian cultural spheres. See [[amazonia]].
- **With the [[vertical-archipelago]] system**: Caravan circuits linking coastal, valley, oasis, and highland polities were operative before Inka absorption and represent the South-Central Andean expression of multi-ecosystem access.
- **With [[inca-empire]]**: Inka expansion after CE 1450 incorporated the South-Central Andes while leaving Araucania, the Pampas, and Patagonia outside direct political control. See also [[southern-cone]] place page.
- **With the [[mapuche]]**: Araucanian/Mapuche expansion eastward into the Pampas and northern Patagonia (well into the eighteenth century) constitutes the latest in the Southern Cone's long history of population movements.

## End Conditions

The prehistoric trajectory was disrupted unevenly. European contact arrived first along rivers and coasts; the Chaco was more fully affected later. But the pace and depth of disruption varied dramatically:
- The South-Central Andes was already incorporated into the Inka administrative structure; Spanish conquest overlaid one imperial system with another (see [[collision-at-cajamarca-1532]] for the decisive rupture).
- The Mapuche/Araucanians of Araucania successfully resisted Spanish conquest and endured into the nineteenth century.
- The Chaco, Pampas, and Patagonia remained substantially outside colonial control until the nineteenth century, with some societies (Yahgans, Onas) not encountering sustained European contact until the late nineteenth century.

Rivera's conclusion is that "once equilibrium with the environment had been achieved, and because of relative protection from violent and massive changes, the material elements of cultural development remained almost unchanged for millennia" in the four Southern Cone proper regions. Disruption when it came was catastrophic and external, not the product of internal developmental failure.

## Historiography

**Source quality**: Archaeological data are uneven — richest for the South-Central Andes (especially northern Chile), thinnest for the Chaco ("almost nothing is known from the preceramic"). Ceramic sequences for Araucania "remain insufficiently defined because of scarce stratigraphic excavations." Dating methods include radiocarbon (C-14) and thermoluminescence; key absolute dates cited include 620 BCE (El Piquete, Jujuy), CE 300 (Palpalá), CE 660 (Huimpil/Pitren), and CE 1265 (Fell's Cave Period V).

**Marginality debate**: The dominant interpretive shift in this chapter is from "marginality" as social deficiency to "marginality" as a relative term used by intruders. This follows Gordon Willey's broad comparative framework and Orquera's ecological productivity argument.

**Key scholars**: Mario A. Rivera (northern Chile synthesis), Luis A. Orquera (Pampas and Patagonia), Junius Bird (Patagonian stratigraphy), Erland Nordenskiold (pioneer of Chaco archaeology), Tom Dillehay (Araucania), Alberto Rex González (NW Argentina), Gordon Willey (South Andean synthesis).

**Collection coverage**: This source — [[cambridge-history-native-peoples-americas-v3-1]] — provides the best English-language synthesis for South-Central Andean prehistory; coverage of Chaco, southern Pampas, and far Patagonia is thinner.
