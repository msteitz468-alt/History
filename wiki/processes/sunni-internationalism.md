## Definition and Scope

"Sunni internationalism" — Hodgson's preferred phrase is the "new Jama'i-Sunni internationalism" or "new Jama'i-Sunni cultural synthesis" — refers to the process by which a coherent, trans-regional Sunni Muslim identity and institutional framework consolidated itself across the fragmented post-caliphal political landscape between approximately 945 and 1258 CE. This was not a political unification but an *social* and *cultural* one: it produced a shared Islamicate civilization spanning from Morocco to Central Asia and India, capable of reproducing itself without dependence on any central political authority.

The process is Hodgson's primary framework for understanding the Earlier Middle Period (roughly 945–1258). It explains both the apparent paradox of cultural florescence amid political fragmentation and the remarkable resilience of Islamic civilization through the Mongol catastrophe.

## What "Jama'i-Sunni" Means

Hodgson uses "Jama'i-Sunni" (from Arabic *jama'a*, community) to designate not simply adherence to Sunni hadith traditions but the specific political-theological position that accepts the historical Muslim community (including its compromises) as legitimate, rejects perfectionist sectarianism, and grounds authority in the consensus (*ijma'*) of the scholarly community. This is distinguished from Shi'ism (which grounds authority in the imam's lineage) and from Khariji rigorism. The Jama'i-Sunni position was capacious: it could accommodate wide variation in legal school, theology, and piety as long as the fundamental social framework of the Shari'ah was maintained.

## Causal Drivers

**Fragmentation of caliphal power.** The buyid takeover of Baghdad in 945 and the subsequent proliferation of independent amirships across the Nile-to-Oxus region eliminated the caliphate as an effective administrative center. The 'ulama, who had always operated with substantial autonomy from the state, now had to supply the social glue that the state could no longer provide. This created both the necessity and the opportunity for the new synthesis.

**The 'ulama as independent class.** By the tenth century, the Islamic scholarly class had developed its own institutional independence: graduates moved between positions across the entire Islamic world, carrying a common culture of legal reasoning, hadith transmission, and theological training. The key insight Hodgson develops is that this class reproduced itself independently of any particular political patron — it was the *primary* bearer of trans-regional Islamic identity.

**The [[madrasa]] network.** The Nizamiyya foundations of [[nizam-al-mulk]] (from 1067) and their many imitators institutionalized the 'ulama's educational function. By providing endowed colleges with regularized curricula, resident student bodies, and waqf-funded salaries, the madrasa allowed 'ulama culture to reproduce itself across political discontinuities. This was the institutional backbone of Sunni internationalism.

**The [[tariqa-orders]].** The Sufi orders provided the complementary "inward" dimension that the [[madrasa]] alone could not supply. While the madrasa addressed outward legal conformity and scholarly transmission, the tariqas addressed personal piety, popular religion, and the Islamization of non-scholarly populations. Their indifference to political frontiers and their tolerance of local custom made them more effective vectors of Islamization than formal legal institutions. Together, madrasa and khâniqâh constituted the bifurcated institutional infrastructure of the synthesis.

**[[al-ghazali]]'s synthesis.** [[al-ghazali]]'s achievement — demonstrating philosophically that Sufi inwardness was not merely compatible with Shari'ah observance but its deepest expression — was the intellectual ratification of what was becoming institutional reality. After al-Ghazali, the Sufi-Shari'ah combination was no longer a precarious compromise but a principled synthesis. Hodgson calls this "the comprehensive Sufi spirituality" that underpinned the new Sunni cultural synthesis.

**Waqf endowment system.** The shift from state taxation to waqf private endowment as the primary funding mechanism for religious and educational institutions was structurally foundational. Waqf funds were inviolable by Shari'ah; they could not be seized by a new ruler. This meant that madrasas, khâniqâhs, mosques, and hospitals built in this period survived political upheavals. The system "expressed the apolitical mood of the times" (Hodgson) while simultaneously ensuring institutional continuity.

## Major Phases

**Phase 1 — Consolidation under Buyid and early Seljuk patronage (945–1095):** The 'ulama develop their independent institutional networks; the [[madrasa]] emerges in Khurasan; al-Ghazali's generation represents the intellectual synthesis of kalam, Falsafa, and Sufi piety.

**Phase 2 — High synthesis under Seljuk and successor states (1095–1200):** The [[tariqa-orders]] crystallize; [[nizam-al-mulk]]'s madrasa network is widely imitated; the new Sunni synthesis shapes statecraft from Morocco to Central Asia. The Crusades in the west and the Nizari challenge in the east are both defeated by powers drawing on Jama'i-Sunni legitimacy.

**Phase 3 — Geographic expansion (1100–1258):** Islam expands into Anatolia (gradual Turkic settlement after 1071), into the Ganges plain (Ghurids from 1192 onward), southward across the Sahara, and eastward into the Malaysian archipelago — all driven primarily by [[tariqa-orders]] and merchant networks rather than by formal conquest.

**Phase 4 — The Mongol catastrophe and aftermath (1220–1260):** The Mongol invasions destroy the caliphate (Baghdad falls 1258) and devastate the political structures of the central Islamic lands. But the institutional infrastructure of Sunni internationalism — madrasas, tariqas, waqf endowments — proves resilient enough that the Mongol successor states themselves convert to Sunni Islam within two or three generations. Hodgson treats this resilience as the defining proof of the process's success.

## Geographic Spread

The process originated in the Nile-to-Oxus heartland and spread outward through multiple channels: military conquest (but only as an initiating moment), trade networks, migrating scholars and Sufi teachers, and the prestige of the "lands of old Islam" as cultural models. The Indian frontier and the Anatolian frontier are the cases Hodgson analyzes in greatest depth in the range covered by this source.

## Interaction with Other Processes

**Preceded_by:** [[early-sufism]], the [[sharia]] legal tradition, early Abbasid caliphal culture.

**Concurrent_with:** The [[tariqa-orders]] process (which is the Sufi dimension of the same phenomenon), the Crusades, the Mongol conquests, the expansion of Islam into India.

**Produced:** Trans-regional Islamicate high culture (Persian literary tradition, the new Arabic sciences), the resilience of Islam through Mongol conquest, subsequent Islamization of Mongol successor states.

**Contrasts_with:** The earlier High Caliphal model, which depended on centralized political authority; the later Ottoman and Safavid imperial models, which re-centralized religious authority in different ways.

## End Conditions

The process in its classic form ended with the Mongol conquest of 1258 (fall of Baghdad) as a political moment, but its institutional products — madrasas, tariqas, waqf endowments — survived and enabled the recovery. Hodgson's larger argument is that this process created a form of Islamic civilization that was *more* resilient than the caliphal form precisely because it did not depend on political centralization. The "end" of the Abbasid caliphate was thus not the end of Sunni internationalism but rather its ultimate test and proof.

## Historiography

Hodgson's formulation of this as a distinct analytical process, centered on the period 945–1258, is his central contribution to the historiography of medieval Islam. He rejects the older paradigm of "decline" after the caliphate's fragmentation in favor of a more sociologically sophisticated reading of the period as one of cultural consolidation and expansion. Key points of scholarly debate: the degree to which the new synthesis was genuinely pluralist or whether it represented Ash'ari theological hegemony (challenged by Makdisi); the role of specifically Khurasanian versus Baghdad-centered intellectual traditions; the question of how the synthesis related to Shi'i communities that were formally outside it.
