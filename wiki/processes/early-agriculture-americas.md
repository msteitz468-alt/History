## Definition and Scope

The origins of farming in the **Americas** — among the most intensively studied of
the world's hearths for some crops, though unevenly (Deborah Pearsall, CWH Vol. II
Ch. 20; case study **Nanchoc valley**, Peru, Tom Dillehay, Ch. 21). The defining
feature is **multiple independent domestications** scattered across the
hemisphere, producing staples that now feed billions: **maize** (from teosinte) in
Mexico, **manioc/cassava** in Amazonia, **quinoa** and **camelids** in the Andes,
and an **eastern North American** complex of squash, chenopod, marsh elder, and
sunflower. (Note: the collection is otherwise weak/"archaeology only" on the
Americas per CLAUDE.md, so this is a key page.)

## The independent domestications

- **Maize** is the signature crop: domesticated from **teosinte** in **southern/
  western Mexico**. The earliest secure evidence is from **Xihuatoxtla shelter,
  central Balsas River valley** (the wild ancestor's home), where maize starch and
  phytoliths on grinding stones date to **~6700 BCE**. Maize then spread through
  the lowlands — Gulf Coast by ~5100 BCE, across southern Mexico/Guatemala/Belize/
  Honduras by ~3500 BCE, and earlier still into Panama, Colombia, and Ecuador.
- **Squash** (*Cucurbita pepo*) was among the very earliest — directly dated to
  ~8000–6000 BCE (Guilá Naquitz, Oaxaca).
- **Amazonia/northern South America:** manioc, arrowroot, llerén, peanut, cotton,
  *Phaseolus* beans, achira, chile, and **potato** — many domesticated during the
  warm, wet **thermal maximum** (~8500–3400 BCE) and the weak-ENSO window
  (~6800–3800 BCE).
- **Eastern North America:** an independent complex of **squash, chenopod, marsh
  elder, and sunflower** from ~3000 BCE — a genuine fourth+ New World hearth.
- **The Andes:** quinoa (*Chenopodium*) and camelids (llama, alpaca) domesticated
  around the same time — a rare case of plants and herd animals together.

## Climate and human agency

Early domestication tracked **increasing warmth and moisture** (the northern and
southern thermal maxima) and ENSO variability. But — a key general point — **climate
was not a simple forcing agent**. At Nanchoc, intensification occurred through
**human decision-making, risk management, communal resource use, and technological
innovation**, and agricultural populations then expanded into new environments via
**alluvial-fan gardening and floodplain exploitation** even when no environmental
crisis was present. Demographic growth and dynamic ecologies kept interacting
regardless of climatic stress.

## Case study: Nanchoc valley (northern Peru)

Dillehay's case study examines the Nanchoc valley, where between **~7,000 and
4,000 years ago** foraging and horticultural groups **intensified their adoption
of cultigens** (squash, peanut, cotton, llerén, manioc — confirmed by directly
dated remains and **starch grains on human teeth**) during a critically arid
period — yet the move toward food production was driven by social and
technological factors, not climatic compulsion alone. Nanchoc is the volume's
exhibit for the **primacy of human agency** in agricultural transitions.

## Anthropogenic landscapes

In parts of **Mesoamerica**, agricultural regimes transformed landscapes through
**irrigation, terracing, fire, modified fields, and canal building**. The
controlled use of **fire** was widespread. Pearsall's conclusion: **"By the time
of European contact anthropogenic landscapes existed throughout the Americas"** —
the New World was a humanized, managed environment, not a wilderness, a finding
with direct bearing on the [[pre-columbian-population-debate|debate over
pre-Columbian populations]] *(standing controversy, dangling)*.

## A notable absence

The Americas largely **lacked domestic herd animals** (outside Andean camelids)
and therefore the [[secondary-products-revolution]] complex (milk, wool, traction)
— a structural contrast with Eurasia (see [[neolithic-communities]]), argued by
some (Diamond) to have shaped the later asymmetry of the Columbian encounter.

## Guilá Naquitz, teosinte, and the Andes (Fagan & Durrani 2020)

Fagan & Durrani (2020) reinforce Flannery's Oaxaca evidence and add Andean
detail: **Guilá Naquitz** was occupied intermittently 8750–6670 BCE, with
AMS-dated squash cultivation to ~8000 BCE — as early as Southwest Asian cereal
domestication — Flannery's key evidence for his ecological-systems model (see
[[agricultural-origins]]). The earliest maize/teosinte samples come from the
**Río Balsas region** of SW Mexico, ~6700 BCE, with maize established by 4000
BCE and reaching a domesticated Guilá Naquitz form by ~3400 BCE. In the Andes,
squash was domesticated in the highlands by ~8000 BCE; **llama and quinoa**
domestication is dated to ~2500 BCE (Panalauca Cave); potato (*Solanum
tuberosum*, one of four domesticated strains) has tuber evidence from a Casma
Valley midden ~2000 BCE; and at coastal sites such as **Chilca** and **Paloma**,
beans and squash were cultivated by 3800 BCE alongside a fishing-dominant
economy — agriculture remaining secondary far longer on the Andean coast than in
Mesoamerica. (Fagan & Durrani 2020)

## End Conditions

By European contact, food production and anthropogenic landscapes spanned the
hemisphere (maize reached the Canadian prairies). The trajectory was cut across by
the post-1492 Columbian Exchange — a standing transition page topic.

## CHNPA I.1 — Eastern Woodlands as independent center (Cordell & Smith ch. 4)

*Source: Linda S. Cordell and Bruce D. Smith, “Indigenous Farmers,” CHNPA Vol. I Part 1.*
Within the deciduous forest region of eastern North America the transition from exclusive
reliance on wild species to crop-centered farming was **neither abrupt nor uniform**. In
contrast to the Southwest, this transition was **not based initially on tropical cultigens
introduced from Mesoamerica** — the East was an **independent center of plant domestication**.
*part_of:* [[north-american-agricultural-transition]]; *contrasts_with:*
[[southwest-agricultural-development]]; *contains:* [[eastern-seed-crop-complex]].

At least four indigenous seed plants domesticated by 2000–1500 B.C. (lamb’s quarter,
marsh elder, squash, sunflower); seven local crops cultivated before maize ~A.D. 200;
early *Cucurbita* more likely local *cf. C. texana* than Mexican introduction. Indigenous
crops became major dietary items only ~200 B.C.–start of Christian era over the interior
riverine East; maize minor until A.D. 800–1100. SW farming from ~1500 B.C. depended on a
Mexican-origin package (corn, squash, beans) plus later cotton/sieva beans/pigweed; turkeys
domesticated in the northern Southwest. See [[eastern-seed-crop-complex]] and
[[southwest-agricultural-development]] for full phase detail.

## CHNPA II.1 — Mesoamerican Archaic agricultural origins (Zeitlin & Zeitlin)

*Source: Zeitlin & Zeitlin, CHNPA Vol. II Part 1, Ch. 2.* Mesoamerican case detail
alongside the hemispheric CWH II synthesis above. See also [[maize-domestication]] and
[[paleoindian-and-archaic-mesoamerica]].

### Principal botanical sites and evidential limits

Only a handful of sites yield substantial botanical evidence of early domesticates:
highland **Tamaulipas** and **Tehuacan** caves, **Zohapilco**, **Guila Naquitz**; lowlands
mainly pollen from Yucatán/Veracruz wetlands. Only Tehuacan and Tamaulipas have long
sequences for diachronic subsistence statements — hence when/where/how/why of first
domestication remain far from conclusive.

### Guila Naquitz — Early Archaic foraging and early squash

Flannery's Guila Naquitz (Valley of Oaxaca, near Mitla): Naquitz phase **8900–6700 b.c.**;
flake industry with little standardization (152 retouched of 1,716 flaked); >20,000 plant
specimens — acorns dominant, plus maguey, mesquite, guaje, susi, nanche, prickly pear,
pine nuts, etc. **No maize/teosinte macroremains** (*Zea* pollen in one sample).
Morphologically early domesticated *Cucurbita pepo* throughout Early Archaic; **B. D. Smith
AMS confirms** early *C. pepo* domestication. Wild runner beans abundant (possible sowing).
Small family bands, fall occupations; deer, cottontail, mud turtle.

Dietary quantification (Flannery et al.): 13 most common plants needed only 5–15 ha
sustaining area for 4-month occupation, but larger territory for 6–8 white-tailed deer.
Animals 5–11% of adult daily kilocalories, 32–35% of protein by weight — **plants major
food even in earliest Archaic**. Dumond raised intrusion concerns; AMS on squash and runner
bean confirmed early-level integrity.

### Squash, bottle gourd, beans — AMS chronology

Squashes (*Cucurbita*) and bottle gourds (*Lagenaria siceraria*) remain the earliest
Mesoamerican domesticates; only Oaxaca *C. pepo* has AMS dates confirming Early Archaic
context. Ocampo *Lagenaria* and *C. pepo* now Middle Archaic ~**3700 b.c.** by direct
dating. Cultivated *C. mixta* reported El Riego Tehuacan; single cucurbit seed in Zohapilco
Playa. Early thin-rind squashes valued as containers as much as for seeds (parallel to
Eastern Woodlands *C. pepo* ~2500 b.c.).

**Beans:** Wild runner beans eaten Early Archaic in Oaxaca and SE Tamaulipas; Guila Naquitz
*Phaseolus* not of a species that was eventually domesticated; domesticated *P. coccineus*
not confirmed until Late Formative. Middle Archaic common bean (*P. vulgaris*) dates from
Tehuacan/Tamaulipas challenged by much younger AMS. Morphologically domesticated tepary
beans (*P. acutifolius*) at Coxcatlán Cave ~3000 b.c. stratum; only wild tepary at Guila
Naquitz has direct 14C as early as 5000 b.c.

### Lowland root-crop hypothesis and regional signals

Long speculation that coastal lowlands had a different agricultural history dominated by
starchy root crops complementary to high-protein aquatic wild foods. Support: Formative
lowland obsidian flakes as possible manioc-grater components (S. American ethnographic
analogy); **manioc pollen** at Cobweb and Cob swamps (Belize) by **2750 b.c.** Macroremains
of root crops not found in Mesoamerica before ~900 b.c., and then in highland Tamaulipas,
Tehuacan, and Chiapas — not the coast.

Honduras Lake Yojoa pollen: forest clearance and maize no later than **2500 b.c.** Panama
(beyond Mesoamerica): Lake La Yeguada charcoal/weeds suggest Early Archaic garden clearing;
maize pollen/phytoliths claimed as early as 5000 b.c. (debated); Lake Gatun cores show Late
Archaic slash-and-burn maize. Ground stone manos from Early Archaic; palm nuts dominate
macrofloral remains until maize becomes staple early 1st millennium b.c.

Oaxaca sedentary villages (Marcus & Flannery): maize, beans, squash already cultivated for
millennia before House 20 at San José Mogote ~1900–1400 B.C. Maya lowlands (Hammond):
Swasey Cuello maize abundant and humid-adapted from earliest layers plus manioc, squash,
beans, chile.

### Broad-spectrum readaptation, scheduling, and explanations of origins

Increasing variety of animal and plant remains points to Flannery's **"broad spectrum
readaptation"** (term from Near Eastern pre-Neolithic). Edwards challenges terminal
Pleistocene timing of broad-spectrum as stage-setting for domestication (Levant data show
varied exploitation throughout Late Pleistocene). Mesoamerican Early Archaic components
with good preservation (Guila Naquitz, Santa Marta) show mixed foraging by ≥7000 b.c.
**"Scheduling"** prioritized seasonal wild resources; highland camps seasonal, limited
storage. Macroband (e.g. rainy-season Coxcatlan, 15–25 people = 3–4 families) vs microband
camps; Oaxaca specialized site types increase as foragers shift toward Binford-style
**collecting** strategy — possibly significant for permanent residence.

**Explanatory models (CHNPA synthesis):** 1960s nuclear-area innovation models gave way to
external-cause explanations. Currently favored: subsistence technology balances population
against environmental carrying capacity; labor-intensive cultivation adopted when
equilibrium disrupted (post-Pleistocene Holocene changes paralleled worldwide). Flannery:
post-Pleistocene thorn-scrub-cactus expansion facilitated domestication; year-to-year
rainfall variability made agriculture attractive. Reynolds Guila Naquitz simulation: neither
long-term climate change nor population growth alone produced cultivation — combination of
wet-year experimentation and dry-year testing did. Population-pressure models (Cohen et al.)
poorly constrained by sparse Mesoamerican Archaic samples. Lengthy "incipiency" vs SW Asia
partly shrinks if maize domestication begins ~3000 not 5000 b.c. Older model: maize yield
had to reach 200–250 kg/ha to beat mesquite — threshold ~2000 b.c. at earliest (Flannery).
Authors emphasize Late Archaic **intergroup exchange** (Bender 1978): nonlocal obsidian
rise; *Zea mays* spread from limited highland habitat as distributive capacity of Archaic
alliances; highland–lowland exchange (e.g. Late Archaic marine shell in Mitla cave); Hayden
competitive-feasting model better for lowland maize *spread* than highland origins
(Blake et al.). Adoption of agriculture best understood as effort to manage unpredictable
climate (and perhaps population) variability by decision-making foragers actively involved
in social networks.
