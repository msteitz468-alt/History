## Definition and Scope

The origins of farming in the **Americas** — among the most intensively studied of
the world's hearths for some crops, though unevenly (Deborah Pearsall, CWH Vol. II
Ch. 20; case study **Nanchoc valley**, Peru, Tom Dillehay, Ch. 21). The defining
feature is **multiple independent domestications** scattered across the
hemisphere, producing staples that now feed billions: **maize** (from teosinte) in
Mexico, **manioc/cassava** in Amazonia, **quinoa** and **camelids** in the Andes,
and an **eastern North American** complex of squash, chenopod, marsh elder, and
sunflower. (Note: the collection is otherwise weak/"archaeology only" on the
Americas per CLAUDE.md, so this is a key page.)

## The independent domestications

- **Maize** is the signature crop: domesticated from **teosinte** in **southern/
  western Mexico**. The earliest secure evidence is from **Xihuatoxtla shelter,
  central Balsas River valley** (the wild ancestor's home), where maize starch and
  phytoliths on grinding stones date to **~6700 BCE**. Maize then spread through
  the lowlands — Gulf Coast by ~5100 BCE, across southern Mexico/Guatemala/Belize/
  Honduras by ~3500 BCE, and earlier still into Panama, Colombia, and Ecuador.
- **Squash** (*Cucurbita pepo*) was among the very earliest — directly dated to
  ~8000–6000 BCE (Guilá Naquitz, Oaxaca).
- **Amazonia/northern South America:** manioc, arrowroot, llerén, peanut, cotton,
  *Phaseolus* beans, achira, chile, and **potato** — many domesticated during the
  warm, wet **thermal maximum** (~8500–3400 BCE) and the weak-ENSO window
  (~6800–3800 BCE).
- **Eastern North America:** an independent complex of **squash, chenopod, marsh
  elder, and sunflower** from ~3000 BCE — a genuine fourth+ New World hearth.
- **The Andes:** quinoa (*Chenopodium*) and camelids (llama, alpaca) domesticated
  around the same time — a rare case of plants and herd animals together.

## Climate and human agency

Early domestication tracked **increasing warmth and moisture** (the northern and
southern thermal maxima) and ENSO variability. But — a key general point — **climate
was not a simple forcing agent**. At Nanchoc, intensification occurred through
**human decision-making, risk management, communal resource use, and technological
innovation**, and agricultural populations then expanded into new environments via
**alluvial-fan gardening and floodplain exploitation** even when no environmental
crisis was present. Demographic growth and dynamic ecologies kept interacting
regardless of climatic stress.

## Case study: Nanchoc valley (northern Peru)

Dillehay's case study examines the Nanchoc valley, where between **~7,000 and
4,000 years ago** foraging and horticultural groups **intensified their adoption
of cultigens** (squash, peanut, cotton, llerén, manioc — confirmed by directly
dated remains and **starch grains on human teeth**) during a critically arid
period — yet the move toward food production was driven by social and
technological factors, not climatic compulsion alone. Nanchoc is the volume's
exhibit for the **primacy of human agency** in agricultural transitions.

## Anthropogenic landscapes

In parts of **Mesoamerica**, agricultural regimes transformed landscapes through
**irrigation, terracing, fire, modified fields, and canal building**. The
controlled use of **fire** was widespread. Pearsall's conclusion: **"By the time
of European contact anthropogenic landscapes existed throughout the Americas"** —
the New World was a humanized, managed environment, not a wilderness, a finding
with direct bearing on the [[pre-columbian-population-debate|debate over
pre-Columbian populations]] *(standing controversy, dangling)*.

## A notable absence

The Americas largely **lacked domestic herd animals** (outside Andean camelids)
and therefore the [[secondary-products-revolution]] complex (milk, wool, traction)
— a structural contrast with Eurasia (see [[neolithic-communities]]), argued by
some (Diamond) to have shaped the later asymmetry of the Columbian encounter.

## Interaction

- A multi-centre instance of [[agricultural-origins]] and [[domestication]].
- Contrasts with Old World hearths in its lack of herd animals/secondary products.
- Underpins later Mesoamerican (Olmec, Maya) and Andean civilizations — material
  for CWH Vol. III and later Processing-List volumes.

## End Conditions

By European contact, food production and anthropogenic landscapes spanned the
hemisphere (maize reached the Canadian prairies). The trajectory was cut across by
the post-1492 Columbian Exchange — a standing transition page topic.
