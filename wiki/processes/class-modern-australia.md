## Definition and Scope

The evolution of class relations in Australia from Federation (1901) to the present, tracing three distinct periods: (1) the mobilisation of labour as an industrial and political movement with protective institutions (1901–WWII); (2) the post-war settlement of full employment, arbitrated wages, and expanded welfare (WWII–1970s); and (3) deregulation, deindustrialisation, declining union power, and widening inequality (1980s–present).

Source: [[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]], chapter by Stuart Macintyre and Sean Scalmer.

---

## Causal Drivers

**The Harvester judgment (1907)** — Justice Henry Higgins established the principle of a "fair and reasonable" wage as "a first charge on industry," calculated on "the normal needs of the average employee, regarded as a human being living in a civilized community." This became the foundation of Australia's distinctive industrial arbitration system and "the 'living wage.'"

**Democratic capitalism** — From Federation, Australia combined universal (white) male and then female suffrage with a market economy. Macintyre and Scalmer define this as involving two competing principles: property rights and social entitlement via democratic politics. The task of government has been "to reconcile these two principles."

**Tariff protection and immigration control** — The pre-WWI "settlement" combined tariffs (protecting Australian jobs from imports), immigration restriction (limiting labour supply), compulsory arbitration (settling disputes), and centralised wage determination — together "a new relationship between the state and the market."

## Major Phases

### Phase 1: Mobilisation and Regulation (1901–1939)

The ALP rose from 19 per cent of the vote in 1901 to 50 per cent in 1910, "when it became the first working-class party in the world to form a majority government." Trade union membership recovered from 6 per cent of the workforce in 1901 to 45 per cent by 1914. The Commonwealth Court of Conciliation and Arbitration provided unions with "exclusive coverage, preference in employment, the right to compel employers to attend hearings, and enforceable awards." By 1914, 84 unions with 60 per cent of total union membership had federal registration.

The "worker" in labour movement imagery was "a manual wage earner combating the Fat Man, a grotesque caricature of the capitalist employer. This worker was masculine since both the unions and the Labor Party were dominated by men...The Labor man was also white, since white Australia was a plank of Labor's original platform."

The **1917–1940 wilderness**: the conscription split over WWI "consigned [Labor] to the political wilderness." Between 1917 and 1940 the ALP won only a single federal election. Union membership remained resilient (lowest level 42.6% in 1934), but "lengthy industrial disputes" and "endemic factional conflict narrowed the labour movement's appeal."

The **Depression** exposed the limits of protection: "A small trading economy that was dependent upon commodity exports and capital inflow was powerless to withstand the onset of the world Depression." Unemployment among trade unionists peaked at 30 per cent in mid-1932. Australia had "rejected the provision of social security in favour of job protection and wage regulation," leaving minimal public assistance.

### Phase 2: The Post-War Settlement (1941–c.1975)

World War 2 enabled the ALP government under Curtin to exercise wartime powers over exports, banking, production, and labour. Women's employment expanded "from about half a million to 800,000." The government secured "fiscal supremacy over the States" by assuming control of income tax in 1942 and rebuilt the Commonwealth Bank. It introduced substantial social security and, crucially, adopted **Keynesian economic management** to maintain full employment — "a new settlement between labour and capital" in which "the organised working class accepted the operation of capitalist markets in return for full employment, social security and a rising standard of living."

The Menzies Liberal-Country coalition (1949–66) did not dismantle this settlement but "adjusted" it: shifting housing from public provision to assisting purchasers (home ownership reached 70% by 1961); maintaining full employment (annual growth 4.8% over two decades); absorbing Cold War tensions by exploiting communist influence in unions. Union coverage peaked at 63 per cent of all employees by 1953.

Macintyre and Scalmer note that by the mid-1960s "the sense of new beginnings encouraged many in the view that class divisions were no longer significant." A 1940s Melbourne survey found 55 per cent of respondents described themselves as middle class, 37 per cent as working class.

The Whitlam Labor government (1972–75) pushed towards European social democracy: "earnings grew by 43 per cent for men and 60 per cent for women" in its first two years, while prices rose 33 per cent. After "business interests and the conservative parties mobilised against the government, it was dismissed by the governor-general and then defeated at election. Australia's experiment with social democracy was short."

### Phase 3: Deregulation and Denial (1975–present)

The Fraser government (1975–83) failed to unwind the Whitlam legacy while struggling with stagflation. It was the **Hawke-Keating Labor government (1983–96)** that carried out economic liberalisation: floating the currency, abolishing capital controls, removing tariff protection — "financial deregulation was introduced in agreement with the ACTU, through a Prices and Income Accord that kept wage rises low in return for tax and social policy benefits."

Foreign debt rose from 11 per cent to 50 per cent of GDP between 1981 and 1992. With the **decline of manufacturing**, "the post-war system of production used large-scale, integrated factories... employing large numbers of workers to produce standardised goods: these secure, well-paid jobs had been the heartland of the labour movement. The new system was more specialised and flexible, outsourcing many of its operations." Union coverage dropped from half the workforce to 30 per cent by 1996.

**Enterprise bargaining** was introduced in 1993; the Howard government (1996–2007) strengthened non-union bargaining, stripped wage-fixing powers from the Industrial Relations Commission, and narrowly circumscribed the right to strike. The ACTU's "Your Rights at Work" campaign assisted Labor's 2007 election victory; the new government restored minimum protections without reversing union decline.

**Outcomes by 2000**: The economy expanded 80 per cent between 1991 and 2008. Services contributed four-fifths of GDP; manufacturing fell to 10 per cent; mining expanded to 8.3 per cent driven by Asian industrialisation. The wage share of national income fell from 62.5 per cent (1975) to 53.4 per cent; the profit share grew from 16 to 26.5 per cent. Income inequality, which had been compressed from the 1920s through the 1970s (the lowest half of earners' share of income rose from 10% in 1922 to 29% in 1972), subsequently widened: by 2002 the CEO of a large company earned 98 times the average wage, up from 27 times in 1992.

## The Middle Class as Pivot

Macintyre and Scalmer emphasise the political centrality of the middle class throughout: first as the "Protectionist" constituency Deakin mobilised against Labor; later as Menzies' "Forgotten People" (self-reliant families squeezed between big business and big labour); and ultimately as the primary audience for neoliberal appeals to consumer choice and homeownership. "Thinking about society in terms of stratification rather than class division gained plausibility from the growing importance of education as a determinant of careers."

---

## Geographic Spread

Working-class communities concentrated in inner-city areas near ports and industrial suburbs of Sydney, Melbourne, and other capitals, and in mining towns and industrial centres. The suburban dispersal from the 1950s physically broke up these communities. From the 1980s, deindustrialisation hollowed out manufacturing heartlands; mining expansion in WA and Queensland created new regional labour dynamics.

## End Conditions

The process is ongoing. The institutional framework bequeathed by Higgins — minimum wages, arbitration, social security — survives in attenuated form. Macintyre and Scalmer conclude that "expectations of protection against the market and support of social needs remain" and Australia's "progressive system of taxation and means-tested, non-contributory benefits" directs social security more to those in need than comparable Anglophone economies. But "the changes have not been felt equally": widening inequality, labour market insecurity, and a labour movement that "seemed to have exhausted its capacity" are the enduring features of the contemporary period.

## Historiography

The chapter draws on a dense Australian economic and labour history literature: N.G. Butlin on capital formation, McLean and Richardson on income distribution, R.W. Connell and T.H. Irving's *Class Structure in Australian History* (1992), and Janet McCalman's social histories. Central historiographical debate: was Australia's "social laboratory" of the early twentieth century a genuinely progressive experiment or a racialized, gendered, and class-partial settlement? Macintyre's own prior work (*The Oxford History of Australia*, vol. 4) provides the narrative framework, while the chapter frames the Higgins tradition as a long arc that was dismantled not by the expected conservative opponents but by a Labor government in the 1980s.
