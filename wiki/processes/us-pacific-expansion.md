# US Pacific Expansion

The sustained, multi-century extension of American commercial, military, and political
power across the Pacific Ocean — from the first China trade voyages of the 1780s to the
twenty-first-century "Pacific pivot" and confrontation with China. Driven initially by
commercial capitalism, the process accumulated strategic and imperial layers over time.
As David Hanlon writes, "there are several prominent themes that emerge: expansion,
territorial acquisition, the rise of an imperial presidency, the building of empire, the
deep denial of the colonization that accompanied that building of empire, and a turn to
militarization" — and the whole was "grounded in privilege and patriarchy, and … racialized,
gendered, and sexualized."

Source: [[hattori-samson-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v2-2023]], ch. 54 (David Hanlon).

## Definition and Scope

The process encompasses US commercial penetration of Pacific markets from 1784;
territorial acquisition beginning with the Guano Act claims (1856), Midway (1867), Samoa
(1878/1899), and Hawaii and Guam (1898); colonial empire in the Philippines (1899–1946);
the construction of a Pacific military basing network after 1945; and ongoing assertion of
strategic dominance framed as an "American lake." It is inseparable from continental
westward expansion: the Pacific coast of the continental US was itself acquired (Oregon
Territory, California by 1847, Alaska 1867) before the transoceanic phase accelerated.

## Causal Drivers

**Commercial capitalism and the China trade.** In 1784 the *Empress of China* was "the
first American flag vessel to dock at a Chinese port." Between 1805 and 1808 America's
China trade reached $10–15 million, 15 per cent of foreign trade. The Northwest fur trade
(1787–1830s) added $15–20 million in furs exported to China. The sandalwood trade depleted
Fiji, Hawaii, and the Marquesas; whaling and sealing dominated Pacific commerce from the
1820s to mid-century ("Between 1829 and 1845, American whaling in the Pacific accounted
for 10–20 per cent of the country's total foreign commerce tonnage").

**Manifest Destiny and ideological exceptionalism.** The conviction that God had blessed
continental westward expansion was directly translated into Pacific empire. President McKinley
declared of Hawaii: "We need Hawaii just as much and a good deal more than we did California.
It is manifest destiny." Thomas Paterson's analysis, cited by Hanlon, shows Americans
believed "they were prosperous because they were democratic, and democratic because they were
prosperous" — making the global extension of American order both virtuous and necessary.

**Strategic and naval doctrine.** Alfred Thayer Mahan's naval strategy provided the
intellectual framework for Pacific empire from 1898. The US Pacific Squadron (created 1822),
the US Exploring Expedition (1838–42), the forced opening of Japan by Commodore Perry, and
Plan Orange (1911, anticipating war with Japan) all reflected systematic strategic thinking
long before formal empire.

**Racism and labour exploitation.** The process was inseparable from racial hierarchy:
the 1882 Chinese Exclusion Act, anti-Japanese riots in California, the violent suppression
of Filipino resistance, and the sexualized commodification of Pacific Islander women.
"The racism that was a part of America's own continental expansion was inextricably linked
to and affected its empire building in the Pacific." Richard Drinnon characterised US policy
as the export of "its metaphysics of Indian-hating and empire-building to the Pacific."

## Major Phases

### Phase 1 — Commercial Penetration and Early Footprints (1784–1850)

American merchants dominated Pacific whaling from the 1820s to mid-century, with multi-ethnic
crews (Afro-American, Bengali, Chinese, Filipino, Native American, Pacific Islander) whose
"labour proved crucial to the development of capitalism and empire." Beach communities formed
across Pacific ports, initially racially mixed and socially relaxed, hardening into commercial
port towns. Protestant missionaries arrived in Hawaii in 1820 and later in Micronesia.
The US Exploring Expedition (1838–42) under Wilkes surveyed the Pacific scientifically and
militarily, advocating the harbour at Pago Pago as a naval base. The fur trade and westward
continental expansion secured 2,400 km of Pacific coastline by 1847, with Alaska added in 1867.

### Phase 2 — Sugar, Reciprocity, and Territorial Acquisition (1850–1898)

Hawaiian sugar exports exploded from 289,908 pounds (1855) to 23,129,101 pounds (1873).
A reciprocity treaty (1875, renewed 1877) gave the US Navy exclusive rights to Pearl Harbor,
which US Minister McCook predicted would prepare for Hawaii's "quiet absorption." Secretary
of State Blaine called Hawaii "key to the American dominion of the Pacific" (1881). Midway
was claimed in 1867 under the 1856 Guano Act; the right to a coaling station at Pago Pago
was obtained in 1872.

The **overthrow of Queen Lili'uokalani** (1893) combined commercial interests, the Annexation
Club, and US Minister Stevens's landing of marines from the USS *Boston*. Though the Blount
Report recommended restoration of the monarchy, annexation by joint resolution in 1898 raised
the American flag over ʻIolani Palace on 12 August 1898. Related: [[overthrow-of-hawaiian-monarchy-1893]].

### Phase 3 — Formal Empire (1898–1941)

The Spanish-American War (1898) added Guam and the Philippines to Hawaii and American Samoa.
The Philippine-American War (1899–1902) was "bloody and brutal … driven by a mix of racism,
paternalism, and capitalist desire." US territory now stretched across the Pacific — Alaska,
the Aleutians, Midway, Wake, Kingman Reef, Howland, Baker, Guam, the Philippines —
"a veritable bridge or set of stepping-stones to the other side of the Pacific."

East Asia became the prime focus. The Open Door Notes (1899–1900), Dollar Diplomacy,
Plan Orange (1911), the Root-Takahira Agreement (1908) and subsequent tensions with Japan
over Manchuria, immigration, and the 1924 Immigration Act's Asian exclusion all structured
US-Japan rivalry. By 1937 Japan had declared all-out war on China; the US responded with
embargoes and military aid to China, hardening the lines toward Pearl Harbor.

### Phase 4 — War, Occupation, and American Lake (1941–1968)

The Pacific War (1941–45) confirmed US dominance. The Truman Doctrine sought "the domination
of the Atlantic and Pacific oceans, an extensive system of bases in both oceans to project
American power, access to the markets and natural resources of Eurasia, and global nuclear
superiority. To many, the Pacific now took on the features of an American lake." Related:
[[pacific-war-1941-1945]].

Post-war military installations dotted the Pacific — Hawaii, Guam, the Philippines, Okinawa,
Australia, Japan, South Korea. The former Japanese Mandate Islands became a US strategic
trust territory (1947). Nuclear testing in the Marshall Islands (64 devices, 1946–1954)
was integral to this militarisation. Military bases were "gendered male in their practices,
policies, and priorities" and generated systematic sexual exploitation of local women.
Related: [[pacific-nuclear-testing]].

The Vietnam War revealed limits of the American lake concept. Nixon announced the "Nixon
Doctrine" on Guam (1968): the US would defend allies but could not "conceive all the plans,
design all the programs, execute all the decisions and undertake all the defence of the
free nations of the world." Ford's Pacific Doctrine (announced in Hawaii) reflected that
for the first time "US economic interests in Asia were becoming greater than those in Europe."

### Phase 5 — Post-Cold War and the China Challenge (1991–present)

The US closed Clark Air Force Base and Subic Naval Station in the Philippines (1991) but
later reopened both. Guam became the "new defence perimeter for American interests in Asia."
Obama's "Pacific pivot" (Hillary Clinton, October 2011) declared Asia-Pacific the "driver
of global politics." The concept of "strategic denial" has continued to shape US policy.

China's growing naval power and economic assistance to Pacific Island states have challenged
the American lake concept — Eric Margolis noting "the North Pacific is no longer an American
lake." Trump-era abandonment of the Trans-Pacific Partnership (TPP) and bilateralist trade
policy undermined relations with Australian and other allies.

## Geographic Spread

- **Hawaii**: commercial hub from 1820s; annexed 1898; nuclear-testing launch point
- **Philippines**: colonial 1898–1946; bases closed 1991, reopened after 2001
- **Guam / Mariana Islands**: naval administration 1898; strategic hub through Cold War and present
- **American Samoa / Pago Pago**: coaling station rights 1872; formal territory 1899
- **Micronesia (former Japanese Mandate)**: US strategic trust territory 1947–1994
- **Okinawa / Japan / South Korea**: Cold War basing network
- **Alaska / Aleutians**: purchased 1867; nuclear testing at Amchitka 1971

## End Conditions

The process is ongoing. The "Pacific pivot," military expansion on Guam, and US concerns
about Chinese influence in Pacific Island states continue its logic. Climate change and
rising sea levels — which the US has been criticised for ignoring in refusing to sign the
Kyoto Protocol and withdrawing from the Paris Agreement — now compound the legacy of
nuclear and military exploitation.

## Historiography

Hanlon identifies two analytical schools: Donald Johnson (no coherent Pacific policy 1784–1898,
only varied commercial motivations) versus Michael Green (*By More than Providence*, 2019:
early and growing strategic policy toward Asia-Pacific developing from the start). Both
agree on the steady expansion and the accumulation of commercial, strategic, and military
layers over time.

The "anarchy of empire" framework (Amy Kaplan, drawing on W.E.B. DuBois) emphasises
that imperial dynamics are bidirectional: foreign domination shapes domestic culture even
as domestic ideology drives foreign expansion. Paul Lyons's concept of "American Pacifism"
identifies a dual Pacific imagination — islands as economic stepping-stones AND as pristine
utopias, both of which serve imperial ideology.

Counter-histories foregrounded by Indigenous scholars (David Chang, Adria Imada,
Teresia Teaiwa, Jon Osorio) stress Islanders as active agents: Native Hawaiians as
"active travellers and explorers," hula performers as subversive counter-colonial voices,
Chamorro and Micronesian resistance and negotiation throughout. The collection of US
empire studies (William Appleman Williams, Walter LaFeber, Bruce Cumings) situates
Pacific expansion within the global logic of American capitalism.

Collection coverage for this process is strong for the period 1784–1945 (via [[hattori-samson-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v2-2023]] and multiple other sources) and increasingly detailed for the Cold War and contemporary period. The Islander counter-perspective remains underrepresented.
