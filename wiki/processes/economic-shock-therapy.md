# Economic Shock Therapy
## Definition and Scope
**Economic Shock Therapy** refers to the rapid, radical transition of post-Soviet Russia from a communist state-directed command economy to a capitalist free-market system. Launched in January 1992 by President Boris Yeltsin's economic team (led by Deputy Prime Minister Yegor Gaidar and privatization minister Anatoly Chubais), the process was characterized by immediate price liberalization, the lifting of trade barriers, fiscal austerity, and a sweeping, voucher-based privatization of state property.

## Causal Drivers
- **Economic Disintegration:** By late 1991, the Soviet economic system had completely collapsed, resulting in severe food shortages, hyperinflation, and a massive budget deficit.
- **Irreversibility of Reform:** Reformers like Gaidar believed that a sudden, rapid transformation was necessary to dismantle the communist command structure completely and prevent a return to the old order.
- **Western Advice and Credits:** The International Monetary Fund (IMF) and Western governments recommended "shock therapy" as a condition for providing the loans and financial assistance that Russia desperately needed to stay solvent.

## Major Phases
- **Price Liberalization (January 1992):** The government lifted price controls on 90% of consumer goods and 80% of producer goods. Prices immediately surged, leading to hyperinflation that wiped out the life savings of millions of Russian citizens. "On 2 January 1992 he permitted Gaidar... to introduce free-market prices... prices rose by 245 per cent in January 1992" (lines 3539, 3543).
- **Voucher Privatization (1992–1994):** Directed by Anatoly Chubais, the state distributed privatization vouchers to every Russian citizen. However, due to poverty and a lack of market knowledge, most citizens sold their vouchers cheap. This allowed a small group of directors and speculators to buy up factories, mines, and utilities, laying the foundations for the **oligarch** class. "Chubais introduced a system of ‘vouchers’, which would be available to the value of 10,000 roubles per citizen" but "20,000 roubles was a minuscule grant"; "facilitation of internal enterprise buy-outs virtually guaranteed that managers could assume complete authority" (line 3565).
- **Loans-for-Shares (1995–1996):** In a bid to raise cash and win the backing of wealthy financiers for the 1996 presidential election, the government auctioned off controlling stakes in major resource companies (such as oil and metal giants) to private banks in exchange for loans, creating the super-rich "oligarchs" (such as Berezovsky and Gusinsky). "He agreed a secret deal with Boris Berezovski and a handful of other exceptionally wealthy businessmen... whereby they would receive a lucrative stake in state-owned mining enterprises in return for bailing out the state budget" (line 3668).
- **Crisis and Default (1998):** The economy remained unstable, dependent on international loans (a "pyramid scheme") and oil/gas exports. The collapse of oil prices and the Asian financial crisis led to the Russian default and devaluation of the ruble in August 1998, marking the failure of the shock therapy model. "Russia unilaterally defaulted on its international loan repayments and Sergei Kirienko... stepped down" (line 3684). "The devaluation of the rouble increased the costs of imported goods and inadvertently provided a stimulus to domestic manufacturing" (line 3686). "Industrial production continued to fall. Output in the same year was down by eighteen per cent on 1991" (line 3577). "By the end of the twentieth century about two-fifths of the population lived below the poverty level" (line 3714).

## Geographic Spread
The process covered the entire Russian Federation, with its most visible effects (such as the sudden availability of luxury consumer goods and the rise of capitalist services) concentrated in Moscow and St. Petersburg.

## Interaction
- **With the Duma:** Shock therapy met with intense opposition in the Russian parliament (the Supreme Soviet), which repeatedly tried to impeach Yeltsin and block his reforms, culminating in the violent **[[russian-constitutional-crisis-1993|1993 Constitutional Crisis]]**.
- **With Society:** The process led to a sharp polarization of society. While a tiny minority became extraordinarily wealthy (the "New Russians"), the bulk of the population (especially pensioners and public sector workers) sank into poverty. Mortality rates surged, and life expectancy took a precipitous plunge.

## End Conditions
Economic shock therapy effectively ended in August 1998 with the financial collapse and devaluation of the ruble. Prime Minister Sergei Kiriyenko was dismissed, and Yeltsin appointed Evgeny Primakov as Prime Minister, signaling a retreat from radical market reforms in favor of a compromise policy aimed at stabilizing the economy and paying off wage arrears.
