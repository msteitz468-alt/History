## Definition and Scope

The arabization of the *diwan* refers to the Umayyad caliph Abd al-Malik's administrative reform mandating that the record-keeping, taxation, and bureaucratic apparatus of the Islamic state be conducted in Arabic rather than in the Greek and Persian that had governed the former Byzantine and Sasanian territories respectively. The *diwan* (plural: *dawawin*) designated the administrative offices — taxation bureau (*diwan al-kharaj*), army payroll (*diwan al-jaysh*), chancellery (*diwan al-rasa'il*) — through which the Islamic state managed its territories.

The process is distinct from, but causally connected to, [[the-translation-movement]] of the early Abbasid period. In George Saliba's account, the arabization of the *diwan* is the upstream cause that set in motion the competitive dynamics from which the systematic translation of Greek scientific and philosophical texts ultimately emerged.

The term *diwan* in this context refers not merely to institutional offices but to the technical content embedded in those offices: measurement tables, agricultural and astronomical calendars, land-survey methods, and other applied mathematical and natural-scientific knowledge that *diwan* employees required to perform their functions.

---

## Causal Drivers

- **Umayyad state-building**: As the Islamic empire stabilized and expanded, administrative coherence required a common administrative language. Arabic, as the language of the Quran and of the Arab ruling class, was the natural choice.
- **Political-religious legitimacy**: Arabic was also the sacred language of Islam; arabizing administration reinforced the religious character of the state.
- **Displacement of existing administrative classes**: The reform threatened the livelihoods of Syriac-speaking Christian and Persian-speaking Zoroastrian bureaucrats who had staffed the *diwans* from the pre-Islamic period and continued through the early Umayyad era.

---

## Major Phases

**Phase 1 — Elementary administrative translation (c. 685–720 CE)**: Translation of the technical content of the *diwans* themselves — land registers, taxation tables, measurement standards, agricultural calendars, astrological almanacs — into Arabic. This phase created a body of technical Arabic prose in applied mathematics, calendrics, and administration. Saliba identifies this as the neglected first phase of what is usually called "the translation movement," distinct in character (applied, administrative) from the later phase of philosophical and scientific translation.

**Phase 2 — Competitive specialization (c. 720–750 CE, Umayyad to early Abbasid transition)**: As Arabic-speaking competitors entered the *diwan* positions made accessible by arabization, incumbent Syriac- and Persian-origin employees responded by acquiring more advanced knowledge — reaching beyond the elementary administrative sciences to more sophisticated Greek philosophical and scientific texts. This phase straddles the Umayyad-Abbasid transition and precedes the Abbasid period that the classical narrative treats as the translation movement's origin.

**Phase 3 — Advanced translation movement (c. 750–870 CE, early Abbasid)**: The Abbasids inherited a competitive administrative class already sorted by specialization level. The most advanced scientific texts — Ptolemy's *Almagest*, Euclid's *Elements*, Galen's medical corpus, the *Arithmetica* of Diophantus — were translated during this phase, patronized primarily by bureaucrats (the Banu Musa brothers, individual *kuttab* and viziers) rather than by caliphs themselves.

---

## Geographic Spread

The process operated across the full territorial extent of the Umayyad and early Abbasid empires, but its center of gravity was Iraq (the former Sasanian heartland) and Syria (the former Byzantine frontier zone), where the *diwan* administrative apparatus was most developed and where Syriac- and Persian-speaking administrative communities were most concentrated. Baghdad, founded 762 CE, became the capital of the new competitive environment.

---

## Interaction with Other Processes

- **preceded_by**: [[the-translation-movement|earlier Syriac and Pahlavi translations]] of elementary Greek texts — though Saliba argues these were of insufficient sophistication to explain the Abbasid translation phenomenon
- **produced**: the technical Arabic vocabulary that made al-Hajjaj b. Matar's Arabic *Almagest* (829 CE) possible — without a century of prior administrative Arabic technical prose, such a linguistically accomplished translation could not have been achieved
- **concurrent_with**: the *shu'ubiyya* literary-cultural movement (anti-Arab sentiment among Persian-origin Abbasid intellectuals), which Saliba distinguishes sharply from the administrative mechanism as a causal driver
- **enabled**: [[islamic-science-origins]] — the sustained competitive acquisition of advanced knowledge that generated Islamic science's "Golden Age"

---

## End Conditions

The arabization of the *diwan* as a discrete reform process was complete within the Umayyad period. Its consequences — the competitive specialization dynamic among administrative classes — continued through the ninth century and arguably beyond, as successive generations of bureaucrat-scientists maintained and extended the advanced knowledge base that the competition had originally generated. The process did not end so much as it was absorbed into the stable institutional culture of Abbasid administration.

---

## Historiography

The arabization of the *diwan* has been documented as an administrative fact in standard Islamic history. Its significance as a **cause** of the translation movement is Saliba's contribution, derived from his reading of al-Nadim's *Fihrist* (987–988 CE) and al-Jahshiyari's *Kitab al-Wuzara' wa-l-Kuttab* (10th c.). Both sources describe the *diwan* content and arabization process in ways that al-Nadim explicitly connects to the spread of scientific and philosophical books — a connection that previous historians of Islamic science had not developed into a causal thesis.

The process is poorly documented in the Bibliotheca Alexandrina's holdings, which are strong on the translation movement's *output* (Arabic scientific texts) but thin on the administrative history of the Umayyad state. [[saliba-islamic-science-renaissance-2007]] is the primary source for the causal argument. The [[abbasid-caliphate]] page covers the state apparatus that inherited the competitive dynamic.
