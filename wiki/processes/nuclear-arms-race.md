# The Nuclear Arms Race

The **nuclear arms race** was the open-ended competition between the United States and the
Soviet Union (later joined by other powers) to build ever-larger and more sophisticated
**nuclear arsenals** during the [[cold-war|Cold War]]. It made the superpower confrontation
uniquely dangerous — for the first time in history, a great-power war could mean the
**annihilation of civilization** — and shaped the strategy, diplomacy, and culture of the
entire period. From [[leffler-westad-cold-war-v1|*The Cambridge History of the Cold War*,
Vol. I]].

## From monopoly to balance

The United States opened the atomic age with the bombing of **Hiroshima and Nagasaki**
(1945) and a brief **monopoly**; some US leaders (Byrnes) hoped the bomb would be diplomatic
leverage over Stalin. The monopoly proved short: the **Soviet atomic test (1949)**, hastened
by espionage, ended it and shocked Washington (contributing to NSC-68 and the militarization
of [[containment]]). Both powers then raced to the vastly more powerful **thermonuclear
(hydrogen) bomb** — the US in 1952, the USSR in 1953 — and to the means of delivery:
long-range bombers, then **intercontinental ballistic missiles (ICBMs)** after the Soviet
**Sputnik** launch (1957) raised fears of a "missile gap," and **submarine-launched
missiles**, completing the survivable "triad."

## The balance of terror and MAD

As both sides acquired the ability to destroy the other even after absorbing a first strike,
deterrence settled into **mutual assured destruction (MAD)**: neither could attack without
guaranteeing its own annihilation. This "balance of terror" is widely credited with keeping
the Cold War **cold** — making direct superpower war effectively unthinkable and channeling
the conflict into proxy wars, crises, and competition in the [[decolonization|Third World]].
It also produced recurring terror, above all the **[[cuban-missile-crisis-1962|Cuban Missile
Crisis]]** (1962), the closest the world came to nuclear war.

## Arms control

The danger generated its own counter-process: **arms-control diplomacy**. After the 1962
crisis came the Partial Test Ban Treaty (1963), the Non-Proliferation Treaty (1968), and the
détente-era **SALT** agreements and ABM Treaty (1972). The race nonetheless resumed in the
late 1970s–80s (the "second Cold War," the Euromissiles, Reagan's SDI), before the
Reagan–Gorbachev **INF Treaty** (1987) and the end of the Cold War brought deep reductions.

## Significance

The arms race defined the Cold War's distinctive character: a confrontation too dangerous to
resolve by war, sustained for four decades under the permanent shadow of extinction. It drove
enormous scientific and industrial mobilization, shaped alliance politics (the credibility of
the US "nuclear umbrella" over [[nato]]), spawned a global anti-nuclear movement, and left
the [[contemporary|post-Cold-War]] world the unresolved problem of proliferation.
