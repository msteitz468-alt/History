# The Nuclear Arms Race

The **nuclear arms race** was the open-ended competition between the United States and the
Soviet Union (later joined by other powers) to build ever-larger and more sophisticated
**nuclear arsenals** during the [[cold-war|Cold War]]. It made the superpower confrontation
uniquely dangerous — for the first time, a great-power war could mean the **annihilation of
civilization** — and shaped the strategy, diplomacy, and culture of the entire period. From
[[leffler-westad-cold-war-v1|*The Cambridge History of the Cold War*, Vol. I]].

## Definition and Scope

The process is the **competitive accumulation of nuclear weapons and their delivery
systems**, together with the strategic doctrines that governed them. It runs from the US
atomic monopoly of 1945 to the deep reductions at the Cold War's end, and encompasses
warhead numbers, the bomber–missile–submarine "triad," and the evolving theory of
deterrence. It is the technological-strategic core of the broader [[cold-war]] rivalry and a
limiting case of [[total-war]] (war made unwinnable by mutual annihilation).

## Causal Drivers

The race was driven by the **US–Soviet security dilemma**: each side's pursuit of security
through weapons threatened the other, spurring counter-buildup. It was reinforced by
**[[containment]]** and its militarization (NSC-68), by military-scientific-industrial
momentum, by espionage (which sped the Soviet bomb), and by the search for **deterrent
credibility** — especially the credibility of the US "nuclear umbrella" over [[nato]].

## Major Phases

1. **US monopoly (1945–49):** Hiroshima and Nagasaki; brief American sole possession.
2. **The thermonuclear leap (1949–57):** the Soviet atomic test (1949) ends the monopoly;
   both race to the **hydrogen bomb** (US 1952, USSR 1953).
3. **The missile age (1957–62):** **Sputnik** (1957) and ICBMs raise fears of a "missile
   gap"; submarine-launched missiles complete the survivable triad; the
   **[[cuban-missile-crisis-1962|Cuban Missile Crisis]]** (1962) is the terrifying climax.
4. **Parity and arms control (1963–79):** rough parity yields **MAD**, the hotline, the
   Partial Test Ban (1963), NPT (1968), and SALT/ABM (1972).
5. **The "second Cold War" and the end (1979–91):** the Euromissiles, Reagan's SDI, then the
   **INF Treaty** (1987) and Cold War's end bring deep cuts.

## Geographic Spread

Centred on the two superpowers (North America and the USSR), the race extended through
alliance systems (US warheads deployed across [[nato]] Europe; Soviet ones across the Warsaw
Pact) and through **proliferation** to other states (Britain, France, China, later Israel,
India, Pakistan), making it a global condition rather than a purely bilateral one.

## Interaction

The race shaped, and was shaped by, the whole [[cold-war]] system: it underwrote
[[containment]] and [[nato]], turned crises like [[cuban-missile-crisis-1962|1962]] into
near-catastrophes, channelled superpower conflict into **proxy wars** (since direct war was
suicidal), drove enormous scientific mobilization, and spawned a global **anti-nuclear
movement** and the counter-process of **[[detente|arms-control diplomacy]]**.

## End Conditions

The race wound down with **[[detente|détente]]**-era treaties and, decisively, the
Reagan–Gorbachev **INF Treaty (1987)** and the **end of the Cold War (1991)**, which brought
START reductions. It did not end the nuclear danger: it bequeathed the
[[contemporary|post-Cold-War]] world large surviving arsenals and the unresolved problem of
**proliferation**.
