# The Domestication of Maize (Teosinte to Corn)

The creation of **maize** (corn, *Zea mays*) was, in Charles Mann's [[mann-1491-2005|*1491*]] account, one
of the most improbable feats in the history of agriculture — a deliberate act of genetic engineering with
**no edible wild ancestor**, which the Mexican National Museum of Culture once flatly called a plant that
"was not domesticated, but *created*" almost from scratch. It was the staple that underwrote the
[[pre-columbian-americas|civilizations of Mesoamerica]] and spread across the hemisphere, and — after
1492 — one of the world's most important crops.

## The teosinte puzzle

Unlike wheat, rice, barley, and millet — whose wild ancestors **resemble their domesticated descendants**,
are edible, and can propagate themselves — maize is, in Mann's image, "the one redheaded early riser in a
family of dark-haired night owls":

- **No wild maize exists**, and none has ever been found despite decades of search.
- Maize **cannot reproduce itself**: its kernels are sealed inside a tough husk, so humans must sow every
  generation. It is a wholly **anthropogenic** species.
- Its closest genetic relative, a mountain grass called **teosinte**, looks nothing like it — teosinte
  splits into many thin stems and bears "ears" scarcely an inch long, of 7–12 hard, woody seeds; "an
  entire ear of teosinte has less nutritional value than a single kernel of modern maize." No one eats it.
- The genetic gulf is vast: where a **single-gene** mutation produced the non-shattering wheat/barley
  that triggered the Middle Eastern Neolithic, **at least sixteen genes** control shattering in
  teosinte/maize — and there is no known non-shattering teosinte variant. Geneticists, Mann notes, have
  "effectively thrown up their hands" trying to explain how the transformation could have happened
  spontaneously; it required sustained, intentional human selection over a very long time.

## Significance

Maize was the third pillar of the Mesoamerican **milpa** — intercropped with **beans and squash** (and
avocados) — giving a diet Mann judges "arguably more nutritious than its Middle Eastern or Asian
equivalent." But Mann argues its very strangeness **slowed** the spread of farming in the Americas: a crop
this hard to develop and dependent on human sowing did not propagate as easily as self-seeding Old World
grains, which (with low population densities) may help explain why the American Neolithic Revolutions
spread more slowly than Eurasia's. Maize agriculture nonetheless became the demographic foundation of the
[[olmec|Olmec]], [[maya-civilization|Maya]], [[teotihuacan|Teotihuacan]], and [[aztec-empire|Aztec]]
worlds, and spread north to the [[cahokia|Mississippian]] mound-builders. After 1492 it entered the
[[columbian-exchange-1492|Columbian Exchange]] and became a staple from Africa to China — one of the
American gifts that reshaped global population.
