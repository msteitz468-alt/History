# The Roman Agrarian Economy (the Land)

The foundation of the entire Roman economy: **land as the source of subsistence and wealth**, and
the agricultural system — its ecology, its produce, its landholding, and its labour — that fed the
empire. Treated in [[bowman-cah-v11-2000|CAH XI]] Ch. 23 (**Peter Garnsey**), the lead chapter of
the volume's economy section.

## Food, ecology, and the Mediterranean constraint

In the Graeco-Roman world land was looked to **primarily for food**, which made up the major part
of surplus production — "a much more vital concern then than it is now." Garnsey frames antiquity
(after Braudel) as a society **on the verge of famine**, its agriculture bounded by the
**Mediterranean climate**: highly **seasonal, winter-concentrated rainfall**, a fierce **summer
drought**, and — most dangerous — high **interannual variability** (the less rain, the less
regular; at Athens wheat fails ~4 years in 10). The diet rested on the **Mediterranean triad** —
**cereals** (60–75% of calories, wheat displacing barley and porridge giving way to bread),
**vines**, and **olives** (sharply restricted by the frost line) — supplemented by the often-
overlooked **dry legumes** ("the poor man's meat"), with meat scarce and a marker of the rich and
the military. Garnsey stresses (after Duby) that culture, not just ecology, shaped what was grown:
Greek and Roman regimes (the vine, the olive) were **transplanted** into temperate Europe.

## Pastoralism and transhumance

The semi-arid lowlands disfavoured **large-scale pastoralism** and cattle (a prestige food); sheep
and goats, valued also for **manure**, were near-ubiquitous on the standard farm. Garnsey
**debunks the old geographical-determinist thesis** that the summer drought forced massive
long-distance **transhumance** independent of and antagonistic to agriculture: the agronomists
show pastoralism and arable **integrated** on Italian farms, and the nomad–sedentary relationship
(North African harvesters; the *tratturi* of Abruzzo) was **symbiotic**, not hostile. The scale of
specialized transhumance "must remain conjectural."

## Expansion and cash crops in the West

The Principate saw the **expansion of agriculture, especially in the western provinces** — a
direct result of conquest, pacification, **frontier armies**, taxation, and a rising number of
city consumers. Garnsey's hypotheses: growth and innovation were **most marked in the less-
developed West**, and **change of land-use** mattered more than the extension onto virgin land.
The pattern by region:

- **Britain, the Rhineland, the Danube–Balkans** (two-thirds of the army, needing >100,000 tonnes
  of wheat a year): mostly the redirection of existing arable, plus the spread of bread-wheat and
  larger livestock — "steady growth rather than innovation."
- **Gaul**: viticulture booming from Languedoc to the Rhineland; olive oil in the Midi; the reaping
  machine (*vallus*) and the scythe in the north.
- **Spain and Africa** (light military presence, supplying Rome and their own cities): the great
  **Baetican oil** flowing to Rome (~6 million kg/year, building Monte Testaccio), **African grain**
  (two-thirds of Rome's supply, per Josephus) and the spectacular **African oil boom** pushing into
  the Tripolitanian pre-desert.
- **Italy**: no comparable expansion — small adjustments of cultivated area, provincial imports
  reshaping (not collapsing) viticulture, the steady **agglomeration of estates**.

## Landholding: inequality, scatter, and the peasantry

Property was **profoundly unequal** (the alimentary tables of **Veleia** and **Ligures Baebiani**:
a few percent of owners holding a quarter to half the land), but large estates were typically
**scattered** middle-sized and small units (marriage, inheritance, risk-spreading), not single
blocs — the true latifundia (with a pastoral/cereal sector) condemned by Columella and Pliny being
only part of the picture. Senatorial fortunes rested increasingly on **provincial land** (Trajan,
then Marcus, requiring senators to invest a third, then a quarter, of their wealth in Italy). The
**peasantry** — barely visible in the sources — survived through **risk-minimizing diversification,
property fragmentation, storage, family limitation, and "social storage"** (patronage and
neighbourly ties); state **"recycling" of peasants** (veteran/colonial allotments) replenished
their stock in the provinces, and large landlords often found it in their interest to **preserve a
symbiotic peasant labour reserve** rather than extrude them.

## Management and labour

The propertied had several options, varying widely by region: the **slave estate** (a *vilicus*
managing slave labour, with hired free/slave labour at harvest — concentrated in Italy, Sicily,
old Greece, southern Gaul); **tenancy combined with slavery**; **share-cropping (*métayage*)** (the
North African imperial estates; Pliny's experiment with defaulting tenants); the **slave-tenant**
(*servus quasi colonus*); and, most widely, **free tenants and free wage labour** (the Egyptian
Appianus estate showing no slaves and a variety of free workers). Agreements ranged from formal
*locatio–conductio* leases to clientage and *precarium*. Garnsey cautions that "scanty and
superficial" evidence forbids any single model.

## Significance

Garnsey's chapter establishes the **agrarian base of the High Empire**: an ecology of scarcity and
risk, worked by a hierarchy from the conspicuous-consuming senator to the resilient subsistence
peasant, in which the **Principate's prosperity rested on the extension and intensification of
Mediterranean agriculture into the provinces** — above all the cash-crop (oil, wine, grain) export
economies of Spain, Africa, and Gaul — driven by the demands of the army and the city of Rome.

## Cambridge Economic History of the Greco-Roman World

**Dennis Kehoe** (Ch. 20, "The Early Roman Empire: Production," in
[[scheidel-cambridge-economic-history-greco-roman-world-2007|CEHGRW]]) frames the same
agrarian base in terms of production technique, risk management, and elite investment
psychology. The empire's population grew roughly a third from Augustus to the Antonine
period (45→55–65 million), much of it urban; Rome itself held 750,000–1,000,000 under
Augustus. Kehoe's overall judgment: the economy underwent "at best a limited structural
transformation" over the early empire — agriculture remained the basis of both the economy
and the fiscal system throughout, and the elite's share of agricultural wealth only
increased over time (with very large estates emerging in Egypt and Palestine by the 3rd–4th
centuries).

**Yields and technique.** Mediterranean dry-farming (the two-field system, light plough,
fall planting/spring harvest, fallow of more than a year) produced representative yields of
~500 kg/ha, well below medieval/early modern Europe; Nile flood irrigation in Egypt reached
~1,000 kg/ha. Farmers reduced risk through **polyculture** (mixing olives, vines, cereals)
and crop rotation using lupines as green manure, prioritizing diversification over yield-
maximizing investment. Because Mediterranean pastures lay distant from cultivated land,
livestock manure was hard to integrate into arable farming — unlike the fully integrated
northern European three-field system of later centuries (Morley 1996; disputed by Kron
2000). The lever and later screw olive/wine press enabled market-oriented oil/wine
production at scale — North African lever-press installations (per Mattingly) show
deliberately excess capacity built to exploit occasional bumper harvests — while the Gallic
reaping machine (*vallus*) sped the harvest but was adopted narrowly, mainly in Gaul for the
*siligo* wheat variety. Egyptian irrigation (*shaduf*, *saqiya*) served intensive garden,
vineyard, and orchard plots; North African arid-zone farmers used terracing and wadi-bed
water capture predating Roman rule but expanded under it, especially on the Tripolitanian
frontier.

**The villa economy and its decline.** The classic Roman villa (late Republic–early empire,
coastal Campania, central Italy, Etruria) — modest estates such as Villa Settefinestre at
Cosa, combining *pars urbana* and *pars rustica*, worked by 50–100 slaves under a slave
*vilicus* — specialized in wine as its cash crop, shipped in Dressel 1 then Dressel 2–4
amphorae to Rome and Gaul from the late 2nd century BCE. Kehoe argues the villa/viticulture
system's decline by the early 2nd century CE was **not** a "crisis of the slave mode of
production" (contra older Marxisant readings) but resulted from **provincial competition**
— Spanish and Gallic wine, Spanish and African oil — eroding the comparative advantage of
concentrated slave-run production; wine production continued but shifted to less
archaeologically visible forms (wooden casks) (citing Patterson 1987, Morley 1996). By the
2nd century, Italian estates increasingly took the form of **latifundia** — non-contiguous
agglomerations of farms acquired by bequest or purchase, commonly leased to tenants —
with slavery and tenancy coexisting rather than being mutually exclusive.

**Provincial commercial agriculture** spread the villa model to Gaul (1st century CE) and
drove large oil-press installations in Africa (Kasserine plain, the Gebel region and Lepcis
Magna hinterland, among the largest such installations found in North Africa; oil-amphora
stamps of the late 2nd–early 3rd century suggest oil wealth underlay Lepcis Magna's elite).
The richly documented 3rd-century estate of **Aurelius Appianus** in Egypt's Fayyum
(Arsinoite nome) — the Heroninos archive — shows a village-based *phrontides* structure
under *phrontistai* reporting to a central nome administration, achieving economies of
scale through centralized labor and shared draft-animal management, with a small permanent
labor force supplemented by daily-hired workers. The Roman state itself was the empire's
**largest landowner** (the *Fiscus* — former royal property, *ager publicus*, and
confiscations); in Egypt, public and royal land together could comprise up to half the land
in some nomes.

**Tenancy.** Tenants ranged from large-scale estate managers to small dependent laborers.
The *lex Manciana* and *lex Hadriana de rudibus agris*, governing imperial estates in
Africa's Bagradas valley, incentivized *coloni* to plant olive trees alongside grain on
favorable terms — the imperial government relying on small-scale tenant investment in
capital-intensive perennial crops. Tenants holding legally enforceable leases had real
bargaining power, and imperial-estate tenants in Africa and Asia Minor petitioned
successfully against abuses by large tenant-collectors, tax officials, and soldiers in the
late 2nd–3rd centuries (see also
[[processes/roman-imperial-taxation-and-fiscal-system]]).

**Elite investment behavior.** Kehoe's central thesis: the Roman elite's real economic
achievement was not more efficient production but extending landholding to draw modest
income from many scattered sources, while urban demand for food "probably never fully met"
left recurrent hardship and intermittent starvation. He applies the neoinstitutional
concept of a "conceptual framework" together with Herbert Simon's **bounded
rationality/satisficing** to explain elite investment psychology: landowners were
risk-averse, prioritizing economic security and social standing over profit maximization —
land was security, not investment in the modern sense — though Rathbone's study of the
Appianus estate shows sophisticated accounting and profitability assessment did occur
within these constraints. Public/monumental construction (e.g. the imperially controlled
Mons Claudianus and Mons Porphyrites granite/marble quarries in Egypt) is read as
conspicuous consumption/redistribution of wealth rather than productive investment, even
though it employed large numbers and injected cash into local economies through wages for
requisitioned labor and animals.

### Manufacturing and mining

Roman **manufacturing** (ceramics, textiles, bricks) was organized overwhelmingly as small,
independent workshops run by artisans of modest, freed, or slave status — not by the
landowning elite, whose involvement was largely confined to supplying raw materials (clay,
wool, flax, timber) by leasing land and resource rights to independent *officinatores*. The
Ostia brick-stamp study (Helen 1975, a sample of 9,000 stamped bricks, including "binomial"
stamps naming both the *officinator*/producer and the *dominus*/clay-pit owner), the
pottery workshops of Arretium and La Graufesenque (Gaul, roughly ten kilns producing
hundreds of thousands of pieces per season), and Egyptian textile-workshop papyri all
document this pattern. Despite modest growth and rising demand for manufactured goods, the
empire never developed a substantial manufacturing entrepreneurial class able to rival the
landowning elite politically or socially — even wealthy manufacturing families like the
3rd-century Secundinii of Trier (Gallo-Roman textile merchants, per Drinkwater 2001) never
entered the elite; for elite Romans, land remained a more stable long-term asset than
manufacturing profit dependent on organizing skilled labor.

Roman **mining** employed two main methods: surface mining (panning, "hushing" using
reservoir water released in torrents, "ground-sluicing," collection in riffle boxes) and
shaft mining (vertical shafts and horizontal galleries, typically 1–2m in diameter, reaching
depths of roughly 200m below the water table, drained with Archimedes' screws and
water-wheels). Extraction was extremely labor-intensive hand work with iron and copper
tools, consuming enormous quantities of wood for tunnel supports and smelting fuel; products
included gold, silver, lead, copper, tin, and iron. Atmospheric lead pollution recorded in
Greenland ice cores (a proxy for silver smelting) rose sharply from ~500 BCE, peaked in the
1st century CE, declined in late antiquity, and was matched again only around 1000 CE
(central European mining) before being exceeded during the Industrial Revolution — evidence
that Roman silver production rivaled the scale of medieval European mining. Imperial
administration directly controlled major mining districts (e.g. Vipasca, Lusitania,
documented on two bronze tablets from Aljustrel, Portugal): the *Fiscus* leased mining
rights to contractor-*coloni* for half the ore produced, dug exploratory shafts itself, and
sold off exhausted mine rights for cash — a gradual shift over the empire from
Republican-era private/*societates publicanorum* leasing toward direct Fiscus ownership.

## Woolf, *Rome: An Empire's Story* (2012)

Source: [[woolf-rome-empires-story-2012]].

- **Scarcity and risk.** Holocene Mediterranean climate never supported dense forest or cattle grassland; droughts could fail wheat ~1 year in 4—“Classical civilization was built in the shadow of scarcity and risk.”
- **Intensification package.** Translated Mago after 146; eastern fruit/nut crops moved west; arboriculture north of Alps; drainage, mills, roads; larger cattle breeds—sustaining consumer/producer ratios up to three in ten in some early-third-century regions. Lasting environmental traces few except mining heavy-metal pollution; deforestation/soil-erosion theses “have failed to convince.”
- **Slavery and peasant displacement.** Agricultural slavery expanded with intensive agriculture; free peasant displacement pace debated since antiquity; freeholders/tenants persist into Principate; citizen-farmer/soldier-citizen link broken in Italy.
- **Growth then contraction.** Intensification-powered growth ~2nd c. BCE to some point in 3rd c. AD then reverse—cities shrink (NW cities to ~quarter 2nd-c. max by end 3rd; Rome to third; almost no new urban monuments after 230s); trade patterns regionalize. Explanations contested (plague 160s; climate; carrying capacity). For tributary empire: indirect taxes fall with trade/manumissions; land tax limited if land less profitable.

## From Mitchell, Later Roman Empire (2015)

Stable imperial conditions encouraged consolidation of landownership into large/middle estates, reinforcing provincial aristocracies and conservative elite mentality that underpinned city life; emperors amassed huge property (especially Spain, Italy, Africa, Asia Minor, Egypt), leased to long-term tenants and managed by freedmen agents, forming a major supply base for armies and capitals. “Imperial estates, which were generally divided up and leased out to long-term tenants, played a large part in the supply system of the armies and the imperial capital cities.” `part_of:` [[agrarian-citied-society]]; `concurrent_with:` [[colonatus]].
