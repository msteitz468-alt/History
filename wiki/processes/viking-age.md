# The Viking Age

## Definition and Scope

The **Viking Age** (conventionally 793–1066) was the era of Scandinavian raiding,
trading, and settlement across the seas of northern and western Europe and the North
Atlantic. In [[mckitterick-ncmh-v2-1995|NCMH II]] (Coupland, Ch. 7; Lund, Ch. 8) the
Vikings are treated not as an undifferentiated horde but as varied, often small bands
whose activities — and the responses to them — became a central force in ninth-century
European politics.

## Raids on the Frankish and English Coasts

The first shocks were the sacks of **Lindisfarne (793)** and Monkwearmouth (794). Raiding
intensified from the 830s, falling hardest on the trading emporia: the great Frisian market
of **Dorestad** was sacked in 834 and repeatedly thereafter. NCMH II notes the political
timing — the Dorestad raids coincided with [[lothar-i|Lothar I's]] revolt against
[[louis-the-pious]] (834–839), and West Frankish writers accused Lothar of *encouraging*
Danish leaders to harm his father, rewarding them with land grants. Whether or not true, the
return of loot-laden raiders encouraged imitation. Frankish and English defensive efforts —
coast guards, fleets, ring fortresses — were "largely ineffectual," partly because local
counts were unwilling to obey royal orders.

## The "Great Army" and Settlement

A decisive shift came with the **"Great Army"** (*micel here*) that landed in England in 865:
larger, semi-permanent forces that wintered in fortified camps and conquered territory rather
than merely raiding. It overran Northumbria, East Anglia, and eastern [[mercia|Mercia]],
creating the **Danelaw**. Resistance varied sharply by ruler: in England, **Alfred of Wessex**
needed years to find an effective strategy but, after his decisive victory at **Edington (878)**,
built a chain of fortresses (**burhs**) and a reorganised army. In Francia, the defence of
**Paris** during the Viking siege of 885–886 made the reputation of Count Odo. Where they were
met with resolute opposition (England 878) or famine, the great armies dispersed.

## Settlement and the North Atlantic

Beyond raiding, the Vikings settled: the **Danelaw** in England, the lands granted to the Viking
leader **Rollo** around Rouen (911, by Charles the Simple) that became the **Duchy of Normandy**,
and the colonisation of the previously uninhabited North Atlantic, above all **Iceland** — whose
king-less society of *goðar* (chieftains) and *thing* (assembly) NCMH II uses as the best window
onto Scandinavian social organisation before kingship.

## End Conditions

The Viking Age waned as Scandinavian kingdoms Christianised and consolidated and as the raided
societies built durable defences; it is conventionally closed by the failed invasions of 1066.

## Interaction

The Viking impact accelerated the militarisation and fortification of the post-Carolingian West,
contributed to the fragmentation of the [[carolingian-empire]], and reshaped the
[[post-roman-transformation-of-the-west|North Sea economy]]. See also [[norse-scandinavia]].

## Historiography

NCMH II (Coupland) stresses the **imbalance of sources** — a rich Continental record (annals,
charters, capitularies, miracle texts, even hymns) versus the near-sole reliance on the
*Anglo-Saxon Chronicle* in England — and warns against the loaded vocabulary of "the Great Army,"
which often denotes a specific force rather than a single unified host. Modern scholarship has
shrunk older estimates of fleet and army sizes and emphasised the Vikings' integration into,
rather than mere disruption of, European political and commercial life.
