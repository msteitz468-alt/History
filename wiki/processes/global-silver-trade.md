# The Global Silver Trade (1400–1800)

## Definition and Scope

Silver was the paramount monetary substance of the early modern world, and its
global flows were the **financial circulatory system of the first world economy**
(Dennis O. Flynn, CWH VI). The defining fact of the period is that **China became
the world's great silver "suction pump"**: for roughly three centuries, the
white metal flowed relentlessly toward China, paid for by equally prodigious
Chinese exports of silk, porcelain, and (later) tea. Flynn dates the **birth of
genuinely global trade to 1571**, when Spain founded **Manila** as an entrepôt,
permanently linking the New World and Asia across the Pacific (the
Acapulco–Manila galleon) for the first time.

## The Mechanism: Arbitrage, Not Trade Deficit

Flynn argues forcefully **against** the conventional "trade deficit" explanation
(that silver merely flowed east to balance Europe's deficit in spices and silks).
Instead, silver moved by **arbitrage** — buy where cheap, sell where dear — driven
by silver's own supply and demand. China's fiscal **"silverization"** (after
hyperinflation destroyed its paper-money/silver-exchange standard in the 1430s)
created enormous demand to *hold* silver, raising its purchasing power in China to
**double** its value in the Mediterranean (a ~100 percent price premium). With
silver worth twice as much in China as in Europe — and the gold:silver ratio
running 1:5–6 in China versus 1:12 in Europe — it was hugely profitable to ship
silver east. Because the four main monetary substances (silver, gold, copper,
cowries) flowed to *different* end-markets, often in opposite directions, Flynn
holds that money cannot be treated as a passive residual of "real" trade
imbalances.

## The Two Great Cycles

- **The Potosí–Japan Cycle (1540s–1640).** New World silver — from **Potosí**
  (Upper Peru, possibly half of world output at its peak) and Mexico — and
  prodigious **Japanese** mine output both flowed to China. About three-quarters
  of Spanish-American silver crossed the **Atlantic** (via Panama and Havana to
  Seville, the redistribution fountainhead); the rest went west across the
  **Pacific** on the Manila galleon. Japanese silver reached China via Nagasaki
  (Portuguese, then Dutch, middlemen).
- **End of arbitrage (~1640).** As mining flooded global stocks, silver's value
  fell worldwide at about 1 percent per year — the monetary engine of the
  **Price Revolution** — until it reached production cost. Chinese silver prices
  fell to world levels by 1640, closing the cycle. (A later eighteenth-century
  Mexican cycle followed.)

## Political Consequences

Silver underwrote empires. The **Spanish empire** "could not have existed" without
the profits of American silver and its Chinese markets (the royal *quinto* taxed
the flows effectively despite smuggling down the Andean "Back Door"). In Japan,
the Shogun's **monopoly of the silver mines** let him subdue some 250 *daimyō* and
unify the country in 1600, inaugurating the **Pax Tokugawa**. The
[[dutch-east-india-company|VOC]] and [[english-east-india-company|EIC]] were
likewise driven by silver: James Tracy (CWH VI) notes the balance of trade ran
overwhelmingly in **Asia's favor**, so the companies paid for their European
imports by **shipping bullion east** — the VOC covering up to 80 percent of its
European exports with silver, even with its lucrative intra-Asian "country trade."

## Interaction

The silver trade is the monetary face of [[early-modern-commerce]] and
[[indian-ocean-trade]], and it physically carried the [[columbian-exchange-1492|
Columbian Exchange]]: the same ships bore American maize, sweet potato, and
peanut to China, crops that helped double China's cultivable land and population.
It is the integrative core of the [[afro-eurasian-world-system|planetary world
system]] and a central exhibit in the [[great-divergence|Great Divergence]] debate
(China's silver demand, not European institutional superiority, organized much of
world trade).

## End Conditions

Around 1800 the British began **substituting Indian opium for silver** in the
China trade and **import-substituting** Indian cotton textiles with domestic
machine production — moves that reversed the silver flow and opened the
industrial era (see [[industrious-revolution]], [[great-divergence]]).
