# Revolutionary Syndicalism

## Definition and Scope

The movement and doctrine holding the syndicate (trade union) — not the
parliamentary party — to be the vehicle of proletarian revolution, with the
general strike as its instrument and mobilizing myth. It is distinct from
anarchism ("a real abyss" divided them, per [[berth-edouard|Berth]]): the
syndicalists cultivated elites and wanted to transfer state authority to the
syndicates ("The Least State Possible!"), not abolish authority. Sternhell's
three-element characterization: dependence on a market economy, Sorelian
social myths, and the destruction of liberal democracy.

## Causal Drivers and Major Phases

**France.** The Sorelian "New School" formed around Hubert Lagardelle's *Le
Mouvement socialiste* (founded 15 January 1899), converted to Sorelianism by
[[berth-edouard|Édouard Berth]]'s articles of 1903. The Charter of Amiens
(1906) proclaimed the CGT's revolutionary independence from party politics.
The paradox that fueled theory: a massive strike wave (peak 1906: 1,039
strikes, 438,000 strikers; shootings at Longwy 1905 and Raon-l'Étape 1907)
coincided with reformist electoral triumph — the SFIO doubled its membership
and vote — convincing [[sorel-georges|Sorel]] that the proletariat would not
play its assigned revolutionary role. Sorel and Berth left *Le Mouvement
socialiste* in 1908–09, "the moment when the socialist-national synthesis
began to mature," heading toward the [[cercle-proudhon|Cercle Proudhon]].

**Italy.** [[labriola-arturo|Arturo Labriola]] founded *Avanguardia
socialista* (Milan, December 1902) as the PSI's revolutionary wing, allied
with Enrico Leone's Naples circle. Key tests of the general-strike myth:
the **1904 general strike** (triggered by the Buggerru and Castelluzzo
shootings; ~5 million workers; *Avanguardia socialista* hailed "the first
experiment in the dictatorship of the proletariat," but the Labor Chamber,
not the party, proved the real organ); marginalization from the PSI
(Orano and Leone dismissed from *Avanti!* 1905; Ferrara decision to leave,
July 1907; formal expulsion at the Florence congress, September 1908); the
**1908 Parma agrarian strike** (>33,000 workers, >8 weeks, broken by the
army's occupation of the Labor Chamber); the founding of the **USI** (Modena,
November 1912, >100,000 members); and **Red Week** (June 1914). Journals:
*Il divenire sociale* (Leone/Mantica, 1905), *Pagine libere* (Olivetti,
Lugano, 1906), *La lupa* (Orano, October 1910 — the first venue uniting
syndicalists with [[corradini-enrico|Corradini]]'s nationalists).

**The turn to the nation.** Disillusion with the proletariat, the
producer/parasite revision of Marxist economics (Labriola, Leone), the
Libyan War split (1911), and the interventionist crisis
([[italian-intervention-debate-1914]]) carried the movement's intellectual
wing from class to nation — producing [[national-syndicalism]]. Sternhell's
epigram for the substitution of war for the general strike: "a class could
go on strike, but a nation went to war."

## Interaction and End Conditions

Sternhell distinguishes the two professional economists (Labriola, Leone),
influenced by early Sorel but not the myth-and-violence doctrine, from the
"pure Sorelians" (Panunzio, Olivetti, Orano, Lanzillo) who took the myth
whole: "These pure Sorelians also were among the founding fathers of
fascism, whereas the two economists prudently retired from the scene." The
movement dissolved into [[national-syndicalism]] and Fascist syndicalism
(Rossoni) on one side and anti-Fascist opposition
([[de-ambris-alceste|De Ambris]], Labriola, Leone) on the other; the USI's
anarchist rump (Borghi) stayed antimilitarist. See
[[fascism-and-the-political-left]] for the interpretive stakes.
