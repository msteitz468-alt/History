# De-monetisation of the Post-Roman West

The long contraction of the **monetary economy** in the former western Roman provinces
between the fifth and seventh centuries — the disappearance first of small-denomination
copper coinage, then of the land tax and of much gold circulation — and the
consequences that followed from it: de-urbanisation, the retreat of landowners from
city to estate, and the emergence of the closed, self-sufficient early-medieval estate.
This is **Peter Sarris's** distinctive fiscal-materialist account (in the tradition of
Jairus Banaji) of *how* the post-Roman world became "medieval" at the level of everyday
economic life. It is the mirror image of the monetisation that the late-Roman
[[diocletian|Diocletianic]]–[[constantine-the-great|Constantinian]] tax-state had earlier
catalysed.

## Definition and Scope

**What it is:** the unwinding of the deep monetisation that the late-Roman state had
generated by minting vast quantities of gold (the *solidus*) and copper, paying its army
and bureaucracy in coin, and demanding that taxes be paid in coin. By forcing curial
landowners *and* peasants to convert produce into cash, that fiscal system had sustained
commercialised agriculture, urban artisanship, and inter-regional trade — economic
sophistication "not seen again in parts of western Europe until the seventeenth century."
De-monetisation is the reversal of that engine.

**What it is *not*:** a simple synonym for "the fall of Rome," nor a uniform collapse. It
was a *developmental trajectory* that ran at very different speeds — near-total in
lowland Britain, partial and slow in Ostrogothic Italy or Frankish Gaul, and largely
arrested in zones under Constantinopolitan control (Sicily, Rome, the Byzantine East),
where copper coinage and the land tax persisted into the seventh century.

## Causal Drivers

- **`caused_by:`** the breakdown of the Roman **fiscal-military machine**. Once provincial
  defence devolved onto barbarian war-bands settled in return for land, the costly
  apparatus of assessment, census, and coin-collection lost its rationale.
- **`contributed_to:`** **aristocratic tax-evasion**, a grievance running from the fourth-
  century *patrocinium* legislation through Salvian of Marseilles; militarised landowners
  increasingly equated **tax-exemption with elite status**, and resisted the fisc. (When
  the Frankish king Chilperic tried to revive the land tax in 579, the people of Limoges
  burned the registers and hanged his collector.)
- **`enabled:`** the **eastward drainage of gold**: surviving Mediterranean commerce was
  driven by western elite demand for eastern goods, pulling the remaining gold stock
  toward Constantinople.

## Major Phases

1. **Early fifth century** — copper coinage ceases to be minted in quantity in the West;
   the last ruler to strike it in volume is Theodosius I (r. 379–95).
2. **Fifth–sixth centuries** — the Romano-Germanic kingdoms (Vandal Africa, Ostrogothic
   Italy, Burgundy) mint gold on the Constantinopolitan model and some copper, but in
   sharply reduced volume; the **land tax** fades from western sources except under
   Byzantine control.
3. **Seventh century** — outside the imperial enclaves, small-denomination coinage and the
   commercialised exchange it supported have largely gone; aristocrats live directly off
   estate produce.
4. **Later seventh–eighth centuries (recovery)** — as the [[justinianic-plague-541|plague]]
   fades and aristocratic accumulation resumes, an expansion of the monetary economy
   returns, marked by the **shift from gold to silver** in Francia (670s) and renewed
   minting in lowland Britain (from the 630s), and by new coastal *wic* trading centres
   (Quentovic, Hamwic).

## Geographic Spread

A gradient by **dependence on the trans-regional imperial system**. Net recipients of
imperial subsidy and investment (lowland Britain, northern Gaul) suffered the steepest
collapse — Britain returning, in material terms, to near-prehistoric levels (loss of the
wheel-thrown pot, of mortar, brick and tile, and a reversion to pastoralism and barter).
Regions with strong *local* exchange networks (the Paris basin) or high internal
development (Africa) weathered fragmentation better; coastal Mediterranean zones, buoyed
by contact with the East, retained the most.

## Interaction

- **`part_of:`** the broader [[fall-of-the-western-roman-empire-476|collapse of the western
  Roman state]] and its [[fall-of-rome-causes|contested causes]].
- **`produced:`** the **autarkic estate** and, via the survival of late-Roman *direct
  management* of a bipartite demesne, the structural ancestor of the **manor** — the
  estate *vici* and allotments shared out among barbarian settlers, the labour-hungry
  demesne kept by the Roman owner, the *coloni* and slaves merging into an undifferentiated
  servile *mancipia*.
- **`concurrent_with:`** the [[justinianic-plague-541|Justinianic plague]] and (in Sarris's
  reading) a cooling, wetter climate that already nudged northern agriculture toward
  pastoralism — warfare providing the sharper incentive.
- **`contrasts_with:`** the eastern trajectory, where the fiscal state survived; in
  seventh-century Anatolia, by contrast, it was the *aristocracy* that was shattered and
  the **state** that endured (see [[late-antiquity]], and Sarris's Ch. 8 thesis).

## End Conditions

The process bottoms out and reverses in the later seventh and eighth centuries, when
renewed aristocratic accumulation, the receding plague, rising population, and a more
plentiful silver coinage generate the first sustained western economic growth since the
fourth century — the material substrate of the [[carolingian-empire|Carolingian]] world.
