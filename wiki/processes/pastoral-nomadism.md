# Pastoral Nomadism (Steppe Empires and the Eurasian Frontier)

## Definition and Scope

**Pastoral nomadism** is the mobile herding economy of the Central Eurasian
steppe — and, in [[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|CWH IV]] (Timothy May, Ch 9), one of the
period's defining *political-military* forces. Where [[pastoralism]] (from
[[barker-cwh-v2-2015|CWH II]]) treats the origins of herding as a Neolithic–Iron Age
economic adaptation, this page treats the **mounted steppe nomads of 1200 BCE–900
CE as historical agents**: the Scythians, Sarmatians, Xiongnu, Huns, and Türks
whose confederations raided, taxed, traded with, migrated into, and at times
dominated the great agrarian [[empire|empires]] — and who, crucially, **carried
and protected the [[silk-roads|Silk Roads]]**.

The steppe stretched from Manchuria to the Hungarian Alföld, broken by rivers,
hills, mountains, and deserts. Nomads subsisted on the **dairy** of their herds
(horses, sheep, goats, cattle/yaks, camels — kumiss, fermented mare's milk, a
staple), supplemented by meat and hunting; herds were carefully managed as wealth,
and camps (felt **yurts**, easily struck and loaded) rotated pastures seasonally.

## The Defining Dynamic: Non-Self-Sufficiency

May's analytical key (after Khazanov): **pastoral nomadism was not self-sufficient.**
Nomads needed grain, textiles, metal, and luxuries from sedentary societies. This
produced a permanent choice between **trade** (a symbiotic exchange of horses and
animal products for silk, grain, and weapons) and **raiding/warfare** when trade was
denied. The whole history of the steppe–sown frontier turns on this dynamic — and
on the fact that a nomad ruler, once he had subdued all rival nomads, could no
longer reward his followers from them and so was driven to extract wealth from the
settled world (the Xiongnu pattern under Maodun).

The **tribe** (after Rudi Lindner) was a socio-political group built on real or
fictive kinship, its identity that of the dominant clan; membership was fluid, and
leadership quasi-elective — a leader had to prove both military competence *and*
fulfilment of social obligations, or be replaced.

## Major Phases

**The Scythian era (c. 8th–3rd c. BCE).** Iranian-speaking horse-archers of the
Pontic-Caspian steppe (the Saka/Sai of Persian and Chinese sources), known from
[[herodotus|Herodotus]] and thousands of excavated *kurgan* tombs. Mobile (wagon-
homes, not yet yurts) yet building hill-forts (*gorodische*) and, under King Atheas
(4th c.), minting coins and a city — a trajectory toward sedentarization driven by
the Greek grain trade and Siberian fur tribute. They drove the **Cimmerians** into
the Near East, helped exhaust Assyria, and famously frustrated [[cyrus-the-great|Cyrus]]
(killed by the Massagetae queen) and Darius I (whose 513 BCE invasion they defeated
by retreat — "they would not stand and fight").

**The Sarmatians (c. 5th c. BCE–4th c. CE).** Related Iranian nomads east of
Scythia; notable for relatively **greater gender egalitarianism** (women in
political, governance, and military roles — the root of the Amazon legend) and for
**heavy cataphract cavalry** (lance, scale armor — "dressed in dragon scales") that
influenced the Goths and the later European mounted tradition.

**The Xiongnu (3rd c. BCE–1st c. CE).** The first great *eastern* steppe empire, an
Altaic confederation (forerunners of Türks and Mongols) led by the **shanyu**.
Under **Maodun** (r. from 209 BCE) the Xiongnu built a disciplined cavalry empire
from Mongolia to Xinjiang, shattering the Yuezhi (driving the group that became the
[[kushan-empire|Kushans]] west) and forcing the [[han-dynasty|Han]] into the
**Heqin treaties** (from 198 BCE): regular Han "gifts" of silk, grain, and a royal
bride in exchange for peace — a tributary relationship that lasted sixty years
until Emperor Wu's offensives (and the [[silk-roads|Zhang Qian]] mission of 138 BCE)
reversed it. The Xiongnu used the **stirrup** (mounting and accurate mounted
archery) and iron, and ruled subject agriculturalists and oasis traders — a "shadow
empire" mirroring and feeding on Han China.

**Rouran and the Türk khaganates (4th–8th c. CE).** After the Huns (in the west) and
the Rouran (in Mongolia), the **Türks** (Göktürk / "Kök Türk") under **Bumin** and
his brother **Ishtemi** overthrew the Rouran (552 CE) and built the first steppe
empire spanning **Manchuria to the Black Sea** — astride and profiting directly from
the Silk Roads, allied at times with the [[sasanian-empire|Sasanians]] (against the
[[white-huns|Hephthalites]]) and Byzantium, and dominating the northern Chinese
states until the empire split (east/west) and was pressed by [[tang-dynasty|Tang]]
China. Migrating Türks would reshape the Islamic, Byzantine, and Indian worlds.

## Geographic Spread and the Silk Roads

The steppe was the **connective tissue of Eurasia**. May and the volume's editor
stress (with [[silk-roads|Liu, Ch 17]]) that nomads were not merely raiders but the
*facilitators and protectors* of long-distance trade — the only people who could
cross the deserts and mountains between civilizations, taxing, guiding, and guarding
the caravans. Steppe power and the [[trade-network-formation|trans-regional network]]
rose and fell together.

## Interaction

Pastoral nomadism is the permanent counterpart to sedentary [[state-formation]] and
[[empire]]: the nomad–sown dialectic (trade vs. raid; tribute vs. conquest;
sinicization vs. resistance) structured the politics of China, Iran, and Europe
throughout the period. It drove the [[hunnic-migrations|migrations]] that pressed on
Rome, Persia, India, and China in [[late-antiquity|Late Antiquity]]. It fed and
protected the [[silk-roads]]. Compare the economic substrate in [[pastoralism]].

## End Conditions

Within the period nomadic power did not end but cycled — confederations formed
around charismatic militarized leaders, expanded by extracting from the sown world,
and fragmented at succession. The Türk collapse (and Uyghur successor) closes CWH
IV's frame; the pattern culminates beyond it in the Mongol empire ([[kedar-cwh-v5-2015|CWH
V]]).

## Nomadic Migrations and Conquests in the Middle Millennium (CWH V)

[[kedar-cwh-v5-2015|CWH V]] (Anatoly M. Khazanov, Ch 14) carries the analysis through
the period of the steppe's "historical climax." His core argument: the **relative
economic and social "backwardness" of the nomads, paired with their mobility, was
itself a military advantage** — year-round grazing gave a steppe warrior a string of
horses where sedentary states (China, India) chronically lacked cavalry mounts — and
this military superiority gave nomads the leverage to convert economic and cultural
relations with the sown world into **political domination**.

Khazanov distinguishes **migrations** (mostly westward within the steppe, driven by
inter-nomadic conflict and the greater fertility of the Ponto-Caspian end) from
**subjugations and conquests** (directed at the settled societies — China, Central
Asia, the Middle East, the Slavic lands), which did not always require mass migration
into the conquered territory. He treats three theatres: the **Eurasian steppe**, the
**Near and Middle East and North Africa**, and **India**.

His key qualification — important against triumphalist or catastrophist readings —
is that nomadic conquests brought real change (spread of **Turkic languages**, new
trade and the flow of goods and ideas) but **did not ultimately transform
sociopolitical structures drastically or permanently**, because nomad rulers had to
**adopt or adjust to the pre-existing institutional, administrative, and religious
infrastructure** of the states they conquered. This frames the volume's treatment of
the [[mongol-empire|Mongols]] (Ch 20) and the gunpowder reversal of the steppe
advantage analyzed in [[military-history-middle-millennium|Rogers's warfare chapter]].

## Archaeological Origins and the Inner Asian Context (Di Cosmo 2002)

Nicola Di Cosmo's *Ancient China and Its Enemies* (2002) provides the most detailed
archaeological synthesis of pastoral nomadism's origins as they bear specifically on
China's northern frontier. Di Cosmo situates the emergence of nomadic cultures within
a continent-wide process structured by what he calls the [[steppe-highway|steppe
highway]] — the Eurasian grassland belt as a conduit for technological and cultural
diffusion from the Pontic steppe to Manchuria.

### The Debate on Origins

Di Cosmo surveys five competing theories for why pastoral nomadism crystallized from
Bronze Age agro-pastoralism:

1. **Evolutionary (Morgan / positivist)**: Nomadism is a stage between hunting and
   agriculture — more mobile than hunters, less settled than farmers. This nineteenth-
   century model is rejected by modern scholarship.

2. **Agricultural precondition theory**: Domestication of animals required a sedentary
   base first. Sedentary plant cultivation probably preceded animal domestication:
   "plant domestication with primitive farming was probably a precondition for the
   domestication of animals" (Di Cosmo 2002, p. 21). The earliest animal domestications
   (7500–6000 BCE in the Fertile Crescent) occurred within agricultural communities.

3. **Lattimore's displacement theory**: Pastoral nomadism emerged from oasis margins
   as sedentary agriculturalists expanded, "pushing" marginal populations into the steppe.
   Di Cosmo notes this is "not supported by current archaeological research" (p. 34).

4. **Gryaznov's competition model**: In the eighth century BCE some tribes adopted
   nomadism as it became a more economically rewarding activity. Generalized aggressive
   warfare followed, aimed at securing pasture territory.

5. **Gradual transition (Rudenko)**: A slow, multi-century transition to "true" pastoral
   nomadic status, spanning several centuries.

The current scholarly consensus holds that "mature pastoral nomadic cultures were built
on the achievements in both technology and social and political organization of previous
agro-pastoral peoples" — a synthesis rejecting sharp rupture theories (Di Cosmo 2002,
p. 34).

### Khazanov's Four-Phase Evolutionary Model

A. Khazanov's influential model (adopted and contextualized by Di Cosmo) traces four
stages in the formation of pastoral nomads:

| Phase | Character | Archaeological Correlate |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Sedentary animal husbandry | Animals kept alongside agriculture | Early horse breeders, Dereivka site (4200–3700 BCE, south Russian steppe) |
| 2. Semi-sedentary pastoralism | Primitive horseback riding increases mobility | Steppe communities between Ural and Volga, mid-third millennium BCE |
| 3. Herdsman / distant pastures husbandry | Bronze Age steppe culture; wheeled vehicles | Andronovo culture, second millennium BCE; chariots |
| 4. Pastoral nomadism proper | Seasonal cyclical migration as primary economy | Early Iron Age; Scythian-type cultures, 1st millennium BCE |

Di Cosmo notes that "true" pastoral nomads — defined as pastoralists moving with
their herds according to a **fixed seasonal cycle** — appear only in the late Bronze
and early Iron Age. Earlier communities practiced "herder husbandry" or semi-nomadism,
retaining agricultural settlements and experiencing permanent dislocations rather than
seasonal cycling (Di Cosmo 2002, p. 26).

New evidence has revised some aspects of this model: horseback riding may have begun
as early as the late fourth millennium BCE (earlier than Phase 2 originally implied),
and the saddle and stirrup, which enabled accurate mounted archery, diffused through
Inner Asia only in the first half of the first millennium CE.

### The Horse: Domestication and Riding

The horse's role in the transition to mounted pastoral nomadism is central to Di Cosmo's
analysis. Key evidence:

- **Dereivka site** (south Russian steppe, 4200–3700 BCE): 99 percent of recovered
  animal remains are horses. Evidence of bit wear caused by a hard bit — predating
  the wheel — indicates the Dereivka horses were ridden, not only bred. Cheek pieces
  of deer antler with drilled holes support this conclusion. The Dereivka horses are
  morphologically similar to those from Pazyryk (Altai Mountains, 3,500 years later),
  demonstrating sustained human-controlled selection.

- **Northern Kazakhstan** (fourth–third millennium BCE): A settlement excavated where
  99 percent of animal remains are horses; the inhabitants specialized in horse breeding
  but did not yet lead a mobile lifestyle.

- The chariot horse's training techniques may have been developed by Indo-European
  steppe peoples: "The Aryan terminology that appears in a fourteenth-century b.c.
  Hittite treatise on horsemanship illustrating the training of the chariot horse
  suggests that such training may have been developed by steppe Indo-European peoples"
  (Di Cosmo 2002, p. 28).

- The transition to **mature mounted pastoral nomadism** was probably not complete
  until the beginning of the first millennium BCE; "the first Scythian mounted archers
  appear on the scene only in the tenth or ninth century b.c." (p. 28).

Horseback riding allowed "fewer people to control larger herds, and, by allowing
increased mobility, leading to expansion of the political and cultural horizons of early
pastoralists" (Di Cosmo 2002, p. 25, citing David Anthony).

### The Andronovo Culture, Bronze Metallurgy, and the Chariot

The **Andronovo culture** (c. 1900–1200 BCE) represents the critical Bronze Age
phase of the steppe highway. Its practitioners:

- Were skilled bronze metallurgists, capable of manipulating alloys for specific
  weapons and tool functions.
- May have played a role in the development of oasis economy (Bactria-Margiana
  Archaeological Complex) through long-distance migration contacts.
- Developed or disseminated the **war chariot** — distinguished from heavy wagons
  and carts by spoked wheels and a rear-axle box for two warriors. Fully formed
  chariots with spoked wheels (Sintashta-Petrovka culture) may date as early as
  2026 BCE.

The chariot was subsequently transmitted eastward along the steppe highway:
"numerous studies indicate that the chariot was imported into China from the west,
through Central Asia, possibly around the thirteenth century b.c." (Di Cosmo 2002,
p. 29). The earliest Chinese chariots found — in Shang dynasty burials at An-yang —
appear already fully formed, with no prior local tradition of wheeled vehicles.

### Scythian-Type Nomads and the "Scythian Triad"

"True" early pastoral nomads recognizable as "Scythian-type" appear across Central
Eurasia in the early first millennium BCE, documented in tenth-century BCE Iranian
iconography and ninth-century BCE Assyrian and Greek sources (Cimmerians, Scythians,
Sakas). The consensus (2002) locates the origin and dispersal zone in the **Altai and
T'ien-shan regions of Central Asia**, with Gryaznov's Altaic-Scythian periodization
(early stage 8th–6th c. BCE; middle 5th–3rd c. BCE; late 2nd c. BCE–1st c. CE) widely
accepted.

Archaeologists identify this horizon by the **Scythian triad**:
1. Bronze and iron **weapons** (bow and arrow, dagger, sword, spearhead)
2. **Horse gear** (bits, cheek pieces, bridle cockades, girt buckles)
3. **Animal-style art** (metalwork depicting mountain goats, elk, birds of prey,
   boars, large felines)

plus the **kurgan** (burial mound) in which horse and horseman were interred together.
Di Cosmo cautions that funerary rites varied widely and the triad is an etic scholarly
construct, not a marker of ethnic or linguistic unity (Di Cosmo 2002, p. 32n.).

The shared cultural horizon was spread via the steppe highway:
> "The material culture and social organization of the early pastoral nomads of Eurasia
> have impressed researchers for their high degree of similarity across an immense
> territory." (Di Cosmo 2002, p. 42)

C14-calibrated dendrochronological analysis of wooden remains from early nomadic sites
(Arzhan, Tuetka, Pazyryk, Shibe) confirms that the animal-style art was "probably
developed as a native tradition, and not as an adaptation of Near Eastern motifs"
(Di Cosmo 2002, p. 36).

### Social Stratification and Economic Diversification

Di Cosmo's analysis of early nomadic social structure refutes the image of the "pure"
nomad. The mature Scythian-type societies had internally differentiated economies:

> "The image of the 'pure' nomad is often misleading when applied to groups in this
> early period; thus the cattle breeders of the Altai appear to have had some semi-
> permanent settlements where they engaged in primitive agriculture." (Di Cosmo 2002,
> p. 41)

The economic base included: pastoralism (primary), hunting, hoe farming at winter
camps, leather processing, bone and horn craftsmanship, and metallurgy. Metallurgical
centers in sedentary communities (Itkil culture in the trans-Ural forest-steppe,
7th–3rd c. BCE) produced nomadic-style weapons, harnesses, cauldrons, and mirrors for
nomadic markets — evidence of deep sedentary-nomadic economic integration.

Social evolution proceeded in two stages:
1. Early stage: prestige goods dominated by weapons; military leadership primary.
2. Later stage (from c. 5th–4th c. BCE): prestige goods increasingly decorative —
   golden plaques, inlaid ornaments — signifying wealth and commercial/political
   functions rather than military prowess alone. The aristocracy evolved from purely
   military leaders toward commercial and political brokers controlling trade revenues.

> "The formation of a leadership that controlled the sources of wealth, such as trade
> and production, was arguably a necessary condition for the centralization of power
> and military expansion on nomadic polities." (Di Cosmo 2002, p. 43)

This commercial aristocratic evolution is the precondition for the formation of the
Xiongnu confederation and other large nomadic empires.

### The Northern Zone as Eastern Node

The Northern Zone of China was integrated into the steppe highway from an early date.
The Karasuk culture (12th–8th c. BCE, South Siberia and Yenisei–Minusinsk Basin,
extending to western Mongolia) produced bronze bits, knife types analogous to objects
found at Shang An-yang, and pick-axes with Northern Zone parallels — all indicating
that the Northern Zone "possibly functioned as a clearinghouse for new technical
developments into and out of China" (Di Cosmo 2002, p. 32). Chinese silk found in
Pazyryk burials (5th–3rd c. BCE) further confirms this long-range connectivity.

## Historiography

The steppe is largely a **text-poor** world known through hostile sedentary sources
(Herodotus for the Scythians; Sima Qian for the Xiongnu) plus rich archaeology
(*kurgans*, deer stones, frozen Pazyryk tombs). Major debates: whether "Scythian"
or "Xiongnu" denotes a single people or a cultural-political label for many groups;
the degree of nomad state-formation (David Sneath's "headless state" critique of
imposing kinship-tribe models); and — the volume's revisionist thrust — the
reframing of nomads from "barbarian" destroyers to indispensable economic and
cultural **intermediaries** of Afro-Eurasian integration.

Di Cosmo's specific contribution (2002) is to situate the Inner Asian archaeological
record in a methodological framework that combines textual and archaeological evidence
rather than privileging either alone. He critiques both the purely text-based tradition
(Prušek) and purely archaeological approaches (Watson) as insufficient in isolation,
and argues for comparative analysis that can establish "similarities, analogies, and
points of contact" across disparate evidence types (Di Cosmo 2002, pp. 4–5). His key
revisionist claim is that the northern frontier of China was **not** characterized by
an immemorial binary opposition between settled civilization and barbaric nomads, but
rather by a complex, permeable zone in which cultural diffusion, economic symbiosis,
and political interaction preceded and shaped the later sharp dichotomy constructed
by Han-dynasty historiography (especially Sima Qian's *Shiji*).
