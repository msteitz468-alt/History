# The Commercial Growth of Medieval Japan

The steady **commercialization** of [[japan|Japanese]] society between the thirteenth and
sixteenth centuries — a transformation that quietly reshaped medieval Japan as profoundly as
the wars of the [[shugo-daimyo|shugo and daimyo]] did, dissolving the
[[medieval-shoen|estate economy]] from within and laying the base for the dynamic
early-modern economy. From **Kozo Yamamura's** chapter "The Growth of Medieval Commerce" in
the [[cambridge-history-japan-v3-medieval|Cambridge History of Japan, Vol. 3]].

## Definition and scope

Yamamura traces a growth that **continued steadily through the [[kamakura-period|Kamakura
period]] and accelerated in the Nambokuchō and [[muromachi-period|Muromachi]] eras**. It is
not a single event but a cluster of interlocking changes — in agriculture, money, urban
life, guild organization, and transport — whose cumulative effect was to turn a largely
in-kind agrarian order into a society penetrated by markets and money, "even by cultivators
in the countryside."

## Causal drivers

- **Rising agricultural productivity** — double-cropping, better tools, and improved
  irrigation produced the surpluses that made exchange possible: the foundation of the whole
  process.
- **The increase in the size and number of urban centers** — markets, port towns, and (by
  the late medieval period) the "free cities" of trade such as Sakai and Hakata.
- **Monetization** — the spreading use of **imported Chinese copper coins** as the medium
  of exchange, and the **commutation** of estate dues from kind into cash.
- **Institutional innovation** — the rise of **guilds (*za*)**, the use of **bills of
  exchange**, and growing **specialization** among artisans, merchants, and transport
  operators, served by a developing land-and-water transport network.

## Money without a mint

A puzzle Yamamura highlights: the **Ashikaga bakufu and the [[shugo-daimyo|shugo daimyo]]
did not mint their own coins**, relying instead on **Chinese coin** despite real
disadvantages. He reads this (with the tools of economic theory) as a rational outcome given
the costs and credibility problems of a domestic coinage amid fragmented authority — a money
economy running on someone else's currency. Likewise **commutation** of dues into cash was
adopted not only at proprietors' demand but at times **at the request of the cultivators
themselves**, who found cash payment advantageous.

## Interaction and conflict

The commercial surge sharpened struggles among three groups: the **estate elite** (nobles,
temples, shrines who received [[medieval-shoen|shōen]] dues), the **warrior class** (the
bakufu, its retainers, and regional powers), and the **commoners** (merchants, artisans,
moneylenders). The flashpoints:

- **over the *za*** — between guilds and their competitors, and between their old patrons
  (nobles, temples) and new ones (the bakufu and daimyo);
- **over the *tokuseirei* (debt-abrogation decrees)** — pitting moneylenders against the
  warrior and commoner borrowers they had financed (the same decrees that, as
  [[mongol-invasions-of-japan-1274-1281|gokenin relief]], destabilized credit);
- **over dues** — the shifting mix of in-kind versus cash payment among estate recipients
  and between recipients and peasants.

## Interaction with other processes

Commercial growth was both cause and symptom of the [[medieval-shoen|shōen system's
dissolution]]: monetized dues, rural markets, and indebted warriors all eroded the
proprietor's grip. It fed the autonomy of the self-governing ***sō*** villages and the
**[[muromachi-period|ikki]]** leagues, and it was amplified by the **[[muromachi-period|Ming
tribute trade]]** that pumped Chinese coin and goods into the islands.

## End conditions

The medieval commercial economy did not end so much as **mature** into the early-modern one:
the *sengoku* daimyo's castle-towns (*jōkamachi*), the consolidation of domains, and
eventually the [[edo-period|Tokugawa]] *baku-han* order inherited and vastly extended this
money-and-market economy.
