## Definition and Scope

The Chaco Frontier process describes three centuries of armed interaction, ecological transformation, selective adoption of missions, and ultimate demographic collapse along the contested margins where the Gran Chaco met Spanish colonial settlement in present-day Paraguay, Argentina, and southern Brazil. Its central actors were the Guaycuruan-speaking peoples — principally the Abipón, Mocoví, Toba, Mbaya, and Payaguá — whose adoption of the horse after 1600 gave them military parity with and often superiority over Spanish frontier society until the 1730s–1740s. The process ends with the Argentine military conquest of the Chaco in the 1870s and 1880s, which subjugated the last independent Toba and Mocoví bands.

The Chaco itself was a flat alluvial plain sloping west to east, lying north of the Río Salado between the Río Paraná–Río Paraguay system to the east and the Andean foothills to the west. In the 1500s it was dominated by nonsedentary band-level peoples speaking Guaycuruan languages. Alongside the dominant Guaycuruans lived the sedentary Arawakian Guana (Chané), the semisedentary Vilelas, the Tonocote-speaking Lules, the Maskoy (Machicui), and Mataco-speaking groups along the Río Pilcomayo.

## Causal Drivers

**The horse revolution (post-1600).** Guaycuruan groups acquired iron weapons and horses after 1600 through barter and raids. As Saeger summarizes a key scholarly formulation: "The adoption of the horse meant a profound sociocultural change, but was closely tied to the way of life of the colonial Creole environment." Horses extended Guaycuruan military reach, secured raiding escape routes, and broadened hunting range. Men hunted from horseback and raided for cattle, trading hides, captives, and honey with Spaniards for iron, knives, and cloth.

**Guaycuruan warfare culture.** Pre-contact Guaycuruan religion "sanctioned the complementary skills of warfare and hunting. It glorified predatory military behavior, violent economic acquisition, and extreme ethnocentrism." War and hunting were interconnected economically and spiritually; raiding supplied captives for barter and service as well as livestock and iron goods.

**Spanish colonial encroachment.** After 1700 Spaniards increasingly protected livestock, established ranches with armed cowboys, and sent punitive expeditions deep into the Chaco. The expansion of lumber interests and Lule-Vilela missions restricted Guaycuruan autonomy and intensified resource competition. Serious epidemics from the 1730s unbalanced native life and, combined with iron-tool overexploitation of palm groves and algarroba stands, impoverished the Chaco environment.

**Ecological degradation.** Guaycuruan use of iron hatchets and knives between 1541 and the 1740s devastated palm groves and algarroba stands — critical food sources — also exploited by Spaniards for furniture timber.

## Major Phases

**Phase 1 — Pre-equestrian contact and early resistance (1540s–1600).** Spanish entrada forces encountered the Guaycuruans as pedestrian warriors. Toba and Mocoví attacks drove Spaniards from Concepción del Bermejo (founded 1585). The Mbaya in the 1680s pushed the Paraguayan frontier southward. Initial Spanish–Guaraní alliances directed combined military force against the Mbaya ("allied forces like the expedition of several thousand Guaraní and two hundred Spaniards under Cabeza de Vaca in 1542 aimed to annihilate the southern Mbaya").

**Phase 2 — Equestrian hegemony (1600–1740).** The fully mounted Guaycuruans dominated the southern Chaco. The Payaguá conducted waterborne guerrilla warfare, winning stunning victories in 1725, 1730, and 1739, "annihilating large expeditions heading for the mines." The Abipón and Mocoví "halted the commerce of Santa Fe in 1732 and threatened the city's existence." The Mocoví, probably allied with Tobas, laid siege to Salta in January 1735, "killing 100 people, capturing 200 more, and disrupting the city's public life for the next year." Guaycuruan commerce with Spaniards — captives, skins, honey for iron goods — ran alongside continuous raiding.

**Phase 3 — The mission experiment (1740s–1810).** Loss of females to Spanish raiders, epidemics, over-hunting, and increasing penetration by Spanish ranches eroded Guaycuruan strength. In the 1740s Mocoví leaders Ariacaiquin, Citaalin, and Aletin, and Abipón chief Ychamenraiquin led their bands into the San Javier and San Jerónimo reductions. By 1800 at least seventeen more missions had been established. Missions that received cattle endowments, Spanish forbearance, and adequate time — San Jerónimo, San Javier, San Ignacio de Tobas — survived and grew; others, like Timbó, where the Abipón were required to acculturate immediately after Jesuit departure, were short-lived. In missions, Guaycuruan men became cowboys, artisans, and military auxiliaries; women wove blankets and ponchos for Spanish markets. Mission Guaycuruans fought enemies from other missions and served as auxiliary troops — Toba from San Ignacio de Ledesma participated in the anti-Spanish uprising in Jujuy in 1781.

**Phase 4 — Post-independence dispersal and conquest (1810–1882).** After 1810 the collapse of colonial institutions interrupted the mission experiment. Abipón soldiers joined the armies of Artigas; families dispersed to ranches and farms. In the Mato Grosso, Mbaya bands continued raiding into the 1830s, provoking bloody reprisals from the Francia government. Most Toba retreated further from Spanish power, maintaining political independence until Argentine military expeditions in the 1870s and 1880s "subdued, incorporated, unhorsed, and subjugated the resisting Toba and Mocoví, many of whom were living in bands of 50 to 60 families."

## Geographic Spread

The Chaco frontier ran from Asunción and Santa Fe in the east to Salta and Santiago del Estero in the west, and from the Río Pilcomayo north into the Mato Grosso. Guaycuruan mobility — especially of the mounted Mbaya and Toba — drew the frontier into adjacent Spanish provinces: Corrientes, Córdoba, Jujuy. The Payaguá's riverine range extended from Corrientes north to Cuiabá, interdicting Mato Grosso mining traffic throughout the early eighteenth century. After independence the Mbaya split between Brazilian and Paraguayan jurisdictions; their descendants the Kadiwéu (Caduveós) retained identity into the twentieth century in Brazilian Mato Grosso.

## End Conditions

The Chaco frontier process ended through the Argentine military conquest of the Chaco in the 1870s and 1880s, which broke the last independent Toba and Mocoví bands. By this point most surviving Guaycuruans were "biological mestizos, and most were significantly hispanized, the outcome of centuries of contact." In Paraguay, the Guana integrated into the Guaraní-speaking rural proletariat. In Brazil, the Kadiwéu retained their territory partly through service in the War of the Triple Alliance, receiving a land-rights document in exchange for their military participation, but were subsequently restricted as cattle ranchers settled the region. The Payaguá, who had numbered about 6,000 in 1600, dwindled to a handful in Asunción; the last Payaguá woman died there in 1942.
