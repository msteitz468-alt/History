# Pacific Navigation Revival

## Definition and Scope

The **Pacific Navigation Revival** is the 20th–21st century movement to recover, practise, teach, and institutionalize Indigenous Pacific sailing and wayfinding traditions that were suppressed or destroyed during four centuries of European colonialism. It spans the full range of Pacific navigation traditions — Polynesian, Micronesian, and Melanesian — and involves a linked set of institutions: voyaging societies, cultural foundations, university programmes, and community organizations. The revival has both **cultural** aims (recovering identity, reconnecting to ancestral knowledge) and **instrumental** aims (providing sustainable inter-island transport in a climate-threatened ocean, and demonstrating the viability of decarbonized shipping).

It is the continuation of [[concepts/pacific-traditional-navigation]] in a contemporary setting, and part of the broader [[processes/pacific-cultural-renaissance]].

## Causal Drivers

Colonial suppression was systematic. "The arrival of Europeans as explorers and whalers, and subsequently as colonists, missionaries, and traders, led to the (often violent) suppression of Indigenous seafaring practices. Colonial governments — initially Spanish, British, Portuguese, and French, and more recently including Russians, Germans, Japanese, and Americans — often suppressed Indigenous seafaring by creating new and artificial political barriers between countries and communities, and promoting and protecting their own trading interests over those of local populations" ([[jones-matsuda-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v1-2022]], ch. 14, Nuttall et al.). The German colonial administration put a stop to the 'Saile Hapilmohol' — the fleet of canoes that voyaged over 1,600 km from the central Caroline Islands to Yap to trade — in the late 19th century; Japanese colonizers "further discouraged Islanders from sailing their canoes, effectively putting an end to most inter-island voyages" ([[jones-matsuda-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v1-2022]], ch. 15, Raigetal).

By the mid-20th century, "most of the Pacific Islands had all but lost their traditional seafaring practices." In Micronesia alone, where there had been "more than ten schools of traditional navigation and canoe carving throughout the northwestern Pacific," only "two navigation and four canoe carving schools have survived today" in the central Caroline Islands (ch. 15). "A living culture of sail can be lost in a generation, and much has already been lost" (ch. 14).

## Major Phases

### Phase 1 — The Hōkūleʻa Founding Voyage (1970s)

The revitalization crystallized in Hawai'i in the early 1970s, when a group of Hawaiians set out to build a Polynesian double-hulled canoe and sail it to Tahiti using traditional navigation — thereby proving that deliberate, skilled voyaging (not random drift) had settled the Pacific. They quickly realized that the navigational knowledge had been lost in Hawai'i and required a living practitioner.

Their search led to **Satawal atoll**, Yap State, Federated States of Micronesia, and to **Pius Piailug, known as Mau** — a master navigator (pwo) of the Weriyeng school, one of the last holders of intact Caroline Islands star-compass navigation. Mau's participation was itself culturally fraught: sharing proprietary clan knowledge with outsiders "would be frowned upon by members of his clan and those families in the circle who are the 'rightful' owners in line to inherit the knowledge" ([[jones-matsuda-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v1-2022]], ch. 15, Raigetal). Mau proceeded regardless, judging that the greater risk was the knowledge dying out entirely.

Mau could not simply transpose his home-island star-compass to the Hawai'i–Tahiti route: "Mau's home was over 3,000 nm away, on a completely different latitude. So islands, wind and swell patterns, and starpaths were different on a route to Tahiti in the southern hemisphere. Mau learned the rises and sets of celestial bodies for Hawai'i by observing them and consulting with David Lewis about how the winds, seas, currents, and stars would change along the route to Tahiti" ([[jones-matsuda-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v1-2022]], ch. 14, Nuttall et al.). The adaptability itself demonstrated the depth of the navigational system.

In 1976 Mau guided the **Hōkūleʻa** — a 19-metre Hawaiian double-hulled canoe — from Hawai'i to Tahiti in **thirty-one days**, without compass, chart, or sextant, "using only learned oral knowledge." The return voyage took twenty-two days with favourable winds. "This journey reopened a traditional seaway of 2,500 nm that had been used between 400 BCE and 1400 CE" (ch. 14).

The Hōkūleʻa voyage "spurred on a renaissance of Polynesian seafaring that has since actively spread across the Pacific." Mau continued teaching Hawaiian apprentices, most notably **Nainoa Thompson**, who became a pwo and carried the lineage forward through the **Polynesian Voyaging Society** (ch. 15).

### Phase 2 — Regional Spread and Institutionalization (1980s–2000s)

The Hōkūleʻa success demonstrated that traditional navigation was not extinct but recoverable, and inspired analogous efforts across the Pacific. In the Santa Cruz Islands of the Solomon Islands, **Te Aliki Kaveia** led his Taumako community through the thirty-step construction process for the **TePuke** in 1996–7 — the first to be built in sixteen years. Another was built by his grandchildren in 2016–17. At Taumako, "there is an unbroken chain of practice of voyaging skills that Lata pioneered" — a lineage of active practice, not merely revival, preserving methods including submarine hull trim and shunting proa rigs (ch. 14).

Meanwhile, surviving Caroline Islands schools — Weriyeng and Geshi on Lamotrek and neighbouring atolls — continued transmission on a diminished scale. The outer islands of Yap and western Chuuk maintained inter-island voyaging into the contemporary period: "The outer Islanders of Yap still build their traditional canoes and carry out inter-island voyages today. One of the main reasons these islands have kept their Indigenous knowledge is because their choices are limited. They are so remote that, for them to sustain their livelihoods, seafaring and the affiliated skills of this complex system are, by necessity, applied daily" ([[jones-matsuda-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v1-2022]], ch. 15, Raigetal).

### Phase 3 — Waa'gey and Community Adaptation (2010–present)

The displacement of outer-island Micronesian communities to Yap Proper by climate change and economic migration created a new challenge: young people of Caroline Islands descent were growing up without access to the canoe house, the atolls, or the elders who held the knowledge. In 2010, **Waa'gey** (a word meaning "future" in Ulithian) was established in Gargey, one of the outer island settlements on Yap, "chartered by the state of Yap," with the mission of integrating "traditional knowledge and systems to address contemporary issues of concern" (ch. 15). Waa'gey's vision: *Using Traditional Skills to Confront Tomorrow's Challenges*.

Waa'gey's method involves the full ten-domain curriculum of the traditional navigator: building canoe houses, carving canoes, sailing, celestial navigation, herbal medicine, weather prediction, and the spiritual/ritual dimensions (ch. 15). It deliberately opened the traditionally proprietary knowledge to those outside clan circles — a decision framed as urgency: "those who hold these vital skills, including people like Mau, are now continually having to reassess the best course to take in order to preserve their knowledge" (ch. 15). Raigetal himself was initiated as master canoe carver (Geshi school) and then pwo navigator (Weriyeng school) in 2016 — after more than twenty years of study.

A landmark demonstration: in 2016, Waa'gey carved a sailing canoe and **sailed it with traditional celestial navigation** more than 800 km from Yap to the **Festival of Pacific Arts in Guam**. Three canoes made the voyage, sailed by Raigetal and young apprentices. The sail — woven pandanus, autographed by the entire community — carried a message on the impact of climate change on small islands (ch. 15).

## The Climate Change Dimension: Instrumental Stakes

The revival is no longer merely cultural; it is operational. The existing shipping infrastructure that replaced traditional voyaging is "characterized by an aged fleet of mainly imported and fossil-fuel powered ships that service and underpin micro-economies at the end of long and narrow operating routes" — "the most expensive per unit distance and per capita in the world" ([[jones-matsuda-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v1-2022]], ch. 14). As climate change raises sea levels and intensifies cyclones, remote communities face increasing isolation. "When sea transport by unaffordable, unreliable, and ecocidal ships breaks down completely, the ingenious and efficient designs of the ancients may be appreciated and put into service, but only by people who remember enough of what the seafaring ancestors knew" (ch. 14).

Nuttall et al. argue that Indigenous vessel traditions offer: **ecological integrity** (craft made entirely from locally renewable materials); **shallow-draught access** to beaches and reef-lagoon systems inaccessible to modern ships; **community ownership and control** (a village with its own vessel controls its connectivity and thus its decisions); and proven **performance** (Drua/Kalia at 15 knots; TePuke at 10 knots cruising). The 2018 International Maritime Organization decarbonization target (50% GHG reduction by 2050) makes wind-powered hybrid shipping technically and commercially relevant for the first time since the 19th century, and Pacific Indigenous designs "offer a solution to the stability-under-sail problem that is utterly different from the Western solution" — one with millennia of refinement in exactly the operating environment (shallow tropical oceans, reef and lagoon coasts) that modern shipping serves poorly (ch. 14).

## End Conditions

The process is **ongoing**. The window for transmission is narrowing rapidly: knowledge held by specific elders cannot be recovered once those elders die. Waa'gey's pandanus-sail weaving project nearly failed when Maria Labucheilam, 95 years old, "was perhaps the last person on Lamotrek who knew how to weave this pandanus sail" — she died weeks before the sail was completed, but only "after she was satisfied that the knowledge had been reclaimed by her siblings and other apprentices" ([[jones-matsuda-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v1-2022]], ch. 15). Parallel to transmission, documentation (designs, dimensions, performance data, stories, weather systems) is urgently needed to support syncretic design processes that draw on traditional knowledge while leveraging contemporary materials science.

## Historiography and Source Coverage

The scholarship on Pacific navigation revival is led by practitioners and advocates: Ben Finney (Polynesian Voyaging Society), David Lewis (*We, the Navigators*; *The Voyaging Stars*), and the Hōkūleʻa programme's extensive documentation. Nuttall et al. (ch. 14) and Raigetal (ch. 15) in [[jones-matsuda-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v1-2022]] represent the current state of both scholarly and practitioner knowledge. Raigetal's chapter is itself an example of Indigenous knowledge transferred to text "as closely as possible as a way of demonstrating Pacific ways of learning" — a methodological statement about the form of historical record, not just its content (ch. 15).

Collection strength: moderate. This source provides strong coverage of Micronesian (Caroline Islands) and Melanesian (Taumako/Santa Cruz) traditions and the Hōkūleʻa founding voyage. Broader coverage of Polynesian revival institutions (Polynesian Voyaging Society, Samoa, Tonga, Aotearoa) is not yet ingested.

## Phase 4 — Post-Independence Migration and New Voyages (1960s–present)

Ch. 62 of [[hattori-samson-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v2-2023]] (Ng Shiu and Bailey) situates the navigation revival within the broader process of Pacific transnational mobility since the independence era. Their argument parallels the physical voyaging revival: 'The new economic reality made nonsense of artificial boundaries, enabling people to shake off their confinement and they have since moved, by the tens of thousands, doing what their ancestors had done before them: enlarging their world as they go, but on a scale not possible before' (Hau'ofa, cited in ch. 62). Contemporary Pacific migration — to Australia, New Zealand, the United States, and France — is framed through Hau'ofa's concept of **world enlargement** rather than as 'outward migration' or 'brain drain.' Communities 'expand across multiple nation-states' and flows are 'multidirectional' and 'involving multiple generations.'

The **MIRAB model** (Migration, Remittances, Aid, Bureaucracy — Bertram and Watters, 1985) designated most Pacific nations as small, non-viable economies dependent on former colonial powers. Hau'ofa challenged this: the MIRAB 'basket cases' were 'the result of barriers created by colonial boundaries and policies that imposed an artificial sense of isolation and separation upon Islanders. They must now decolonize their minds, and recast their sense of identity by rediscovering the vision of their ancestors for whom the Pacific was a boundless sea of possibilities and opportunities' (ch. 62).

**Labour migration patterns** since independence track colonial legacies: New Zealand's Samoan Quota (1970); the US Compact of Free Association (1986, FSM/Palau/Marshall Islands); Australia's Seasonal Worker Program (SWP, 2012) and Pacific Labour Scheme (PLS, 2018). By 1997 over 400,000 Pacific people lived overseas; by the 2010s over 1.2 million in the United States alone. Cook Islanders, Niueans, and Tokelauans living in New Zealand outnumber those in their home islands (ch. 62). These trans-oceanic flows are structurally continuous with pre-colonial inter-island exchange: 'The settlement and colonization of the Pacific Islands by Austronesian-speaking peoples was the greatest nautical diaspora in world history in the pre-modern era, stretching from Madagascar in the west to Rapa Nui in the southeast Pacific' (ch. 62).

**Sports migration** represents a new voyaging pathway: Pacific male athletes in American football, rugby union, and rugby league constitute a contemporary movement across ocean borders. Almost half of all players in the National Rugby League (NRL) in Australasia are of Pacific heritage (ch. 62). These migrations reveal 'deeper identity, citizenship, and mobility complexities for Pacific migrants' and are 'enmeshed within the broader arena of sports politics.'

**Climate migration** is framed as the next wave: sea level rise, water insecurity, and coral degradation are producing environmental migration. The World Bank *Pacific Possible* report recommended open-access migration from Tuvalu and Kiribati to Australia and New Zealand. The chapter calls for comprehensive climate migration policies (ch. 62).

The common thread is Hau'ofa's 'Oceanian' identity as the conceptual frame: 'Pacific migration, mobility, and movement is best described by Epeli Hau'ofa's process of world enlargement' (ch. 62).
