# The Struggle of the Orders

## Definition and Scope

The Struggle of the Orders denotes the political conflict, extending from the
foundation of the Roman Republic (traditionally 509 BCE) through the passage of
the Lex Hortensia (c. 287 BCE), by which Rome's non-patrician majority — the
**plebs** — progressively dismantled the hereditary monopoly of office,
priesthood, and legal knowledge held by the **[[roman-patriciate|patriciate]]**.

The process is not a simple two-sided class conflict. The term "plebs" denotes
all non-patricians — a heterogeneous mass including wealthy land-owning families
barely distinguishable in economic status from the patriciate, independent peasant
proprietors who formed the "hoplite" infantry, and the landless poor. The
leadership of the movement came overwhelmingly from the first group; the
military leverage that gave it teeth came from the second. The grievances of the
poorest citizens (debt, bondage, land shortage) were real but received only
marginal institutional attention until the fourth century.

**Emic vs. etic.** "Struggle of the Orders" is an etic term coined by modern
historians (Niebuhr, Mommsen) to denote what Roman sources describe piecemeal as
the history of the tribunate, the Twelve Tables, and a series of specific conflicts
over magistracies and land. Roman sources themselves presented it as an
accumulation of constitutionally significant moments rather than a sustained
structural conflict. The modern framing captures a real dynamic but risks
overstating the coherence and continuity of plebeian organization.

## Causal Drivers

**The patrician monopoly.** By the start of the Republic the patriciate had
monopolized the consulship, the major priesthoods (the pontificate and augurate
that controlled religious law), the senate, and through its control of legal
procedure — kept secret by the pontifices — the entire machinery of civil
justice. This was not merely political exclusion but a monopoly of expertise: a
non-patrician litigant depended on patrician willingness to reveal the forms of
legal action. The ban on patrician-plebeian intermarriage (codified in the Twelve
Tables) reinforced social exclusivity.

**Hoplite warfare as the enabling condition.** The sixth-century adoption of
hoplite tactics created a heavy infantry based on a substantial independent
peasantry — men who owned enough property to furnish their own armour but who
lacked patrician status. This class provided the muscle behind plebeian self-
assertion. A "military strike" — the secession, in which the plebs withdrew its
military service — was a weapon of enormous coercive power in a city-state whose
survival depended on its infantry.

**Economic pressure.** Subsistence agriculture, fragmented holdings, the system
of equal inheritance among all heirs, fluctuating yields, and the demands of
military service combined to make debt endemic. The threat of debt-bondage (*nexum*)
— by which a debtor surrendered himself as a bondsman to a creditor — was a
constant. This economic vulnerability channelled into political agitation,
especially when the patriciate appeared indifferent or actively exploitative.
However, CAH VII.2 (Drummond) emphasizes that the fifth-century plebeian
movement's concrete economic achievements were minimal; debt bondage was only
formally abolished in 326/313 BCE.

**Plebeian elite ambitions.** The process was led by wealthy plebeian families
of essentially the same economic standing as the lesser patrician houses. Their
grievance was political exclusion from the offices that conferred status, military
command, and a place in the Senate. The Twelve Tables ban on intermarriage, if
anything, sharpened their sense of a manufactured distinction between families of
similar substance. It was these families — the Licinii, Sextii, Genucii, Publilii
— who eventually broke the patrician monopoly of the consulship in 367 BCE.

## Major Phases

**Phase 1: Creation of the tribunate (c. 494/3 BCE).** The First Secession —
a military strike in which the plebs withdrew to the Aventine or the Sacred Mount —
ended with the creation of the **tribunes of the plebs** (*tribuni plebis*). The
precise date is uncertain (the link to the dedication of the temple of Ceres may be
an aetiological construct). The initial function of the tribunate was *auxilium*:
physical intervention by the tribune on behalf of an individual plebeian threatened
with oppressive magisterial action, particularly at the levy and in civil
jurisdiction. Tribunician inviolability (*sacrosanctitas*) — secured by a plebeian
oath — protected the tribune's person when he intervened. Two additional plebeian
officers, the **aediles**, were probably created at the same time, as guardians of
the Ceres precinct and its market.

The broader veto powers, the right to summon the senate, to propose plebiscites
of general validity, and to prosecute magistrates before the centuriate assembly
are all later developments — most cannot be placed before the mid-fourth century.
The Fifth-century tribunate was a precarious, usurpative office dependent on mass
popular support rather than recognized constitutional rights. See [[tribunate-of-the-plebs]].

**Phase 2: The Twelve Tables (451–449 BCE).** Years of plebeian agitation for
the publication of the law — ending the patrician/pontifical monopoly of legal
knowledge — culminated in the appointment of the First Decemvirate (451 BCE), an
exclusively patrician ten-man commission that replaced the consulship and produced
ten Tables of law. A Second Decemvirate followed (450 BCE), adding two more Tables.
The Twelve Tables remained the foundational text of Roman private law into the
second century BCE. See [[twelve-tables-450bce]].

The Twelve Tables were primarily the work of patrician legislators, designed to
clarify and publish existing law rather than reform the political order. They
reinforced certain patrician prerogatives (the ban on intermarriage, restrictions
on tribunician bills) while also fixing the rules governing debt execution, family
law, and property in ways that gave the substantial peasantry clearer legal
standing. The "Second Secession" narrative associated with the fall of the
Decemvirate (the Verginia legend) is largely fictional — a pale imitation of the
Lucretia story — though it may preserve a kernel of reassertion of tribunician
rights.

**Phase 3: Minor gains (449–368 BCE).** The Canuleian law (traditionally 445 BCE)
rescinded the Twelve Tables ban on patrician-plebeian intermarriage. The
quaestorship opened to plebeians (421 BCE). Three brief plebeian entries into the
consular tribunate occurred (400–396 BCE) during the war with Veii, followed by
reassertion of patrician control. The annalistic tradition vastly exaggerates the
legislative activity of this period; the Licinian-Sextian laws of 367 BCE are the
first secure major constitutional success.

**Phase 4: The Licinian-Sextian settlement (367 BCE).** The tribunes C. Licinius
and L. Sextius proposed over a decade of agitation that one of the two annual
consuls must always be plebeian; alongside debt relief measures. The **Licinian-
Sextian laws** (367 BCE), passed after the restructuring of the chief magistracy
that also created the praetorship and curule aedileship, established the plebeian
share of the consulship and effectively ended the patrician monopoly of the chief
executive. This is Section 3's territory; see [[licinian-sextian-laws-367bce]].

**Phase 5: Access to priesthoods and final validation (300–287 BCE).** The Ogulnian
law (300 BCE) admitted plebeians to the pontificate and augurate — the last bastion
of patrician religious monopoly. The **Lex Hortensia** (c. 287 BCE) gave plebiscites
(decrees of the plebeian tribal assembly) automatic binding force on the entire
Roman people without requiring senatorial ratification. This is conventionally
treated as the end of the Struggle of the Orders: the plebeian assembly was now
formally equal to the centuriate assembly in legislative authority.

## Interaction with Other Processes

The Struggle of the Orders was not isolated from military history. Roman expansion
in the later fifth century (capture of Fidenae and Veii) opened territory for
distribution that periodically defused economic pressure. The Gallic Sack of 390
BCE temporarily united patricians and plebeians against an external threat.
Conversely, the continuous demands of the Samnite Wars (from the 340s) made
patrician military commanders dependent on plebeian cooperation and created the
conditions for the Licinian-Sextian settlement's success. Roman expansion was
simultaneously the cause of the political tensions (producing war profits
concentrated in aristocratic hands, slavery displacing free labor) and the material
that made settlements achievable (distributable conquered land).

## End Conditions

The Lex Hortensia (c. 287 BCE) is the conventional terminal point. It completed
the formal legal architecture of the Roman mixed constitution: the Senate as the
senior deliberative body, annually elected magistracies open to all, plebiscites
with universal validity. The result was not "democracy" but an oligarchy shared
between an increasingly merged patrician-plebeian nobility (*nobilitas*: families
with a consul among their ancestors). Wealthy plebeian families had been absorbed
into the ruling class; the landless poor and those in debt bondage had gained
little.

The deeper structural tensions — land concentration, mass slavery from conquest,
armies loyal to commanders rather than the state — persisted and intensified,
eventually producing the crisis that destroyed the Republic in the first century BCE.

## Historiography

The Struggle of the Orders is one of the best-attested processes in Roman history
by ancient standards (Livy, Dionysius, Cicero, Diodorus) yet one of the most
methodologically fraught. The primary problem is chronological compression and
retrojection: the annalists of the first century BCE, writing under the influence
of the Gracchan crisis and Sullan constitutional experiments, projected back onto
the fifth-century plebeian agitation the vocabulary, procedures, and institutional
concerns of their own time. Specific fifth-century comitial prosecutions recorded
by Livy and Dionysius are largely fictitious. The Valerian-Horatian laws of 449
duplicate known third-century legislation.

CAH VII.2 (Drummond, Chs 4–5) represents the current scholarly consensus: accept
the broad constitutional framework (the sequence of offices, the fasti as broadly
reliable from c. 509 BCE), be deeply skeptical of specific narrative detail before
c. 400 BCE, and focus on institutional survivals and the logic of what was
structurally possible at each stage. The key scholarly divide is between skeptics
(the tribunate itself may date from 449 rather than 494, most fifth-century
legislation is invented) and moderates (the framework is genuinely early even if
the narrative is elaborated). See [[reliability-of-early-roman-tradition]].

## Flower, *Roman Republics* (2010) — attributed early-republics map

Flower does not treat the Struggle of the Orders as a frozen baseline that a single later “Republic” simply inherited and then lost. Instead she offers an early “Time Map 2” (attributed positions in [[roman-republics-periodization]]): a pre-republican transition (~509–494); a **proto-republic** (~494–451/0); **Republic 1** beginning with the **Twelve Tables** (451/0) as the actual start of the first republican period (*libertas* / equality before the law as foundation), including the experimental consular-tribune boards; **Republic 2** from the **367/6** reform (two consuls shared between patricians and plebeians) through the emergence of the *nobiles*.

She marks **~300** (sharing of the major priesthoods — last major barrier to plebeian emancipation), **not 287 (*lex Hortensia*)**, as the start of the first *nobiles* republic (her Republic 3), though she notes 287 remains arguable. After the Twelve Tables she sees patricians dominating office for a generation or two — written law initially producing *less* sharing of executive office; the final major late-fourth-century struggle was over **priesthoods** more than magistracies. She cautions against accepting wholesale the traditional hereditary-patrician story (plebeian names in early *fasti*; doubts on a closed caste) and against classifying competing early plebeian aims (separate community vs integration) as one single republican system. For the second century she also rejects the model of a “fundamental Republic” frozen since the Conflict of the Orders and merely weakened by late-Republican “revolutionary” behavior.
