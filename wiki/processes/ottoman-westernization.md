## Definition and Scope

Ottoman Westernization designates the century-long sequence of reforming initiatives by which the Ottoman empire — and its Turkish nationalist successor state — attempted to assimilate European military, administrative, legal, and cultural forms in order to survive under conditions of European hegemony. It encompasses the reforms of Mahmud II (1808–39), the [[tanzimat-reforms]] period (1839–76), the constitutional episode of 1876, the Hamidian despotism (1876–1908), the Young Turk interlude (1908–18), and the Kemalist republic (1923–38). Each phase represents a different answer to a constant question: how much Westernization is sufficient, and what of the Ottoman-Islamic heritage must be preserved or discarded?

Hodgson presents the Ottoman case as the paradigmatic instance of "Modernization through Westernization" and devotes more analytical attention to it than to any other Muslim society, because "the possibilities and limitations of Westernization as a means of Modernization come out more clearly through the Turkish example than through any other."

## Causal Drivers

**Military necessity** was the original driver. Keeping Ottoman independence against Russian pressure (which had displaced the Safavids permanently from the Caucasus and was now advancing on Ottoman territory) required armies organized on European lines, which in turn required reorganized administration, new schools, and eventually a different social fabric. "Effective military reform required more and more a general Westernization of the armed forces and even … of the civilian governmental régime … and of the social habits of important strata."

**The particular exposure of the Ottoman Turks**: unlike any other Muslim people, the Ottomans were located *within* Europe geographically, with extensive Christian subject populations who were themselves Westernizing and demanding independence — Greeks, Serbs, Romanians, Bulgarians. Their subject peoples' cultural advantage over them sharpened the urgency and the shame of Westernization. "In Europe yet not of it, they were being out-distanced by their own subject peoples as well as by the independent Russians in coming to terms with the momentous forces of Modernity."

**Debt and European financial control** punctuated the process with crises — the Ottoman bankruptcy of the 1870s led to the Public Debt Administration (1881), which gave European creditors effective control of a substantial portion of state revenues and provided the indirect support that sustained Abdülhamid II's despotism.

## Major Phases

### Mahmud II and the Foundation (1808–39)

Mahmud II destroyed the Janissaries (1826) after years of patient preparation, having used them against other obstacles to central power. He introduced conscription, European-style military schools, the fez (replacing the miscellaneous headgear that had distinguished status groups), and Western-trained bureaucrats. These were changes from the top designed to restore Ottoman military competitiveness, not to transform society at large. They evoked bitter resistance from the ulama and the Istanbul populace.

### The Tanzimat (1839–76)

Under the reign of Abdülmecid (1839–61) the "Great Minister" Reshid Pasha formalized Westernization as state policy. The [[tanzimat-reforms]] promised civil equality for all confessions, an end to arbitrary punishment, regular law and courts, and eventually a public secular university. Acceptance into the Concert of Europe after the Crimean War (1853–56) represented "full-fledged membership in Europe itself, a logical culmination of the Tanzimat." In practice the reforms were partially implemented: courts functioned imperfectly, the university remained largely on paper, but the spirit of modernization pervaded the educated classes and proliferated Western-style schools. The crucial long-term effect was a generation of Ottoman officials and writers formed by French education rather than the Islamic scholarly tradition.

### Young Ottomans and the First Constitution (1860s–1876)

The Young Ottomans — educated in the reformed schools and diplomatic service, influenced by Italian liberal nationalism — articulated a constitutionalist vision in which Islam, properly understood, contained the principles of Western liberalism. The most prominent, Namık Kemal, dramatized the new conception of national patriotism in Turkish literature, writing in a simplified Turkish modeled on French rather than Persian. When the minister Midhat Pasha succeeded in promulgating a constitution (1876), it embodied the ideal of a transcommunal "Ottoman nationality" — but it was suspended within two years by Abdülhamid II.

### Abdülhamid II: Despotism, Pan-Islam, and Modernity (1876–1908)

Abdülhamid's solution was paradoxical: deploy modern police methods, the telegraph, the railroad, and the modern bureaucracy — while ideologically anchoring the regime in conservative Islam and pan-Islamic sentiment. He claimed the title of universal caliph of all Muslims, built the Hijaz Railway as a symbolic statement of that claim, and invited Jamal al-Din [[al-afghani]] to Istanbul (though he kept him under control). His despotism "depended, itself, not only on the technique which only the presence of men trained in Modern ways could provide, but on more subtle institutional changes" — the breakdown of old autonomous local solidarities and their replacement by an impersonal national army and professional schools. Modern schools multiplied under him even as constitutional liberty was suppressed. Ultimately his regime was "shot through with discontent" among the officer corps and in 1908 the Salonika mutiny forced restoration of the constitution.

### Young Turks (1908–18)

The Committee of Union and Progress (Young Turks) faced the inherited contradictions at full intensity. The old question — Ottoman, Turkish, or pan-Islamic nationality? — could no longer be deferred. Officially Ottomanist, in practice they pressed Turkish cultural and linguistic primacy; Albanian and Arab restiveness followed. Meanwhile the empire was almost continuously at war from 1911 (Italian invasion of Libya, Balkan Wars 1912–13, World War I). The thinker Ziya Gök-Alp provided the most articulate theoretical foundation: nations are ethnically and linguistically defined; a nation may change civilizations (as the Turks had from Perso-Arabic to Western) without losing its national identity. This framework authorized radical Westernization in the name of Turkish nationality rather than Ottoman or Islamic continuity.

### Kemalist Republic: Full Westernization (1923–38)

Mustafa Kemal (Atatürk) — having defended Gallipoli, held the northeastern front against Russia, and then led the nationalist resistance that expelled the Greek forces from Anatolia — was undisputed master of the new state. He adopted Gök-Alp's Turkist program uncompromisingly. See [[ataturk-mustafa-kemal]] for his person. The republic's principles were formalized as six: Republicanism, Nationalism, Populism, Étatism, Laicism, and Revolutionism.

**Disestablishment of Islam**: The caliphate was abolished (1924). The tariqa orders were suppressed, their properties seized. Madrasas closed. Sharia law replaced entirely by European codes (primarily Swiss civil law). The call to prayer was required in Turkish rather than Arabic.

**Hat law (1925)**: The brimmed hat replaced all Ottoman headgear, including turbans and fez. Because Islamic prayer requires bowing until the forehead touches the ground — which a brimmed hat prevents — the law symbolically but substantively severed the tie between public identity and Muslim practice. It also eliminated the visible distinctions of religious status groups. Those who resisted were crushed; thereafter the population was "implicitly committed to whatever more might be implied in Westernization."

**Alphabet reform (1928)**: Latin letters replaced the Perso-Arabic script. Beyond phonetic convenience, this severed the younger generation from the Ottoman literary heritage and made the reform "psychologically complete and irreversible."

**Language reform**: Systematic Turkification of vocabulary, purging Persianisms and Arabisms. French and Gallicized Latin replaced them as sources of technical vocabulary, completing the civilizational shift.

## What the Process Produced

Kemal's Turkey answered definitively the question "what does coming to terms with Modernity mean?" by identifying Modernization and Westernization entirely: "to become Modern was to join the West as one nation among other Western nations, sharing unreservedly in whatever was Western, for better or for worse."

But Islam was not extinguished by legal disestablishment. The Sufi orders survived underground; the Mevlevis performed their ceremonies in private; popular piety attached itself to imams however poorly trained. The Sharia-minded tradition withered (as the madrasa system collapsed), but Sufi Islam "could be reaffirmed and perhaps even strengthened spiritually." After World War II, when greater freedom was allowed, "the vitality of Islam seemed to lie chiefly on the Sufi side."

## Historiography

Hodgson's treatment avoids two standard errors: he neither credits Kemalism with having "solved" the modernization problem nor condemns it as cultural betrayal. He notes that the question of what to do with Islam remained the central unresolved political issue of the Turkish republic well after Atatürk's death (1938), and that the legal suppression of Islamic institutional life produced unexpected outcomes — Sharia-minded Islam withered, popular Islam became less disciplined and more superstitious, and Sufi Islam proved resilient precisely because it required only informal transmission.

The Turkish case is placed in explicit contrast with the Arab path (revival of classical heritage rather than abandonment of it) — a contrast that shaped the entire trajectory of Middle Eastern modernism in the twentieth century.
