## Definition and Scope

This page covers the history of Islam in Africa during the modern era — from the colonial encounter (beginning with the French conquest of Algiers in 1830) through the postcolonial period. It is **distinct from** [[islamization-of-africa]], which covers the longue-durée spread of Islam across the continent from the seventh century onward; the present page focuses on how colonial rule transformed existing Muslim societies and thought, how Islam expanded dramatically under colonialism (often paradoxically), and how Muslim communities in Africa have navigated modernity.

Roughly half of Africa is Muslim, and approximately one in four Muslims worldwide is African (Miran-Guyon and Triaud, in [[parker-reid-oxford-modern-african-history-2013]]). Unity of creed does not mean uniformity of practice: Muslim life in cosmopolitan Cairo or Zanzibar contrasted sharply with that in Berber villages or among new converts in coastal West Africa.

## Causal Drivers

**Colonial encounter as religious transformation.** The entry of European powers into Muslim Africa was experienced as much as a spiritual catastrophe as a political one — conquest by Christian *kuffār* (infidels) over the *dār al-Islām*. Muslim responses were complex, shaped by Islamic legal casuistry: armed *jihād*; physical emigration (*hijra*) on the model of the Prophet's withdrawal to Medina; *taqiyya* (dissimulation in the face of repression); or accommodation under the canonical principle of *maslaha* (the common good of the community).

**Trade and migration networks.** Long before colonialism, Muslim merchants, clerical lineages, and Sufi masters moved Islam southward and eastward through trade corridors. The colonial economy deepened these axes: plantation agriculture, labour migration, and new urban centres in the forest belt opened pathways for Muslim merchants and proselytizers. Mossi migrants became Muslim in southern Côte d'Ivoire; the Yao of the Tanzania–Mozambique–Nyasaland frontier converted en masse; freed slaves across both West and East Africa embraced Islam as a new identity.

**Sufi brotherhoods (*turuq*).** The eighteenth and nineteenth centuries saw major transformation of the *tariqa* landscape. The Moroccan mystic Ahmad b. Idrīs inspired new brotherhoods including the Sanūsiyya and the Khatmiyya. In Senegal, Amadu Bamba Mbacké (1853–1927) founded the **Muridiyya** — the most important brotherhood founded in sub-Saharan Africa — around the concept of *jihād al-nafs* (the greater jihad of spiritual discipline and work) rather than armed struggle. The *dara* (rural Sufi lodge) and later the *daira* (urban equivalent) structured a vast socio-religious community centered on Touba, today Senegal's second largest city. The Tijāniyya also ramified in West Africa, producing Shaykh Hamallah's 'eleven-beads' Hamawiyya and Ibrahima Niasse's Fayda (*spiritual flood*), which spread cosmopolitanly across the Gold Coast and Nigeria.

**Reformist and Salafi currents.** The Salafiyya intellectual tradition — associated with Jamal al-Din al-Afghani, Muhammad Abduh, and Rashid Rida — called for return to the Quran and Sunna and critique of *bid'a* (innovation). In Algeria, Shaykh Abdelhamid Ben Badis founded the Association of Muslim Algerian Ulama (1931), forging a link between Islam, the Arabic language, and Algerian nationalism. In West Africa from the 1950s, graduates returning from al-Azhar and other Arab universities challenged the authority of hereditary marabouts, provoking violent clashes in cities like Bamako, Bouaké, and Kankan. Colonial authorities labelled these young reformers 'Wahhabis'.

## Major Phases

### Colonial Period (c. 1830–1960)

**Resistance and accommodation.** The diversity of Muslim responses to colonial conquest defies simple narratives. Armed *jihād* leaders — al-Hājj 'Umar in Senegal (d. 1864), Samori Touré in Upper Guinea (d. 1900), the Sudanese Mahdī (d. 1885), Muhammad 'Abdallah Hasan ('the Mad Mullah', d. 1920) in Somalia, the Sanūsiyya in Libya — have been celebrated by nationalist historiography as resistance heroes. But their careers were complex: 'Umar fought both 'pagan' populations and a rival Muslim power; Samori destroyed the Islamic scholarly centre of Kong and imposed forced Islamization. When armed resistance failed, *hijra* and accommodation became the dominant strategies. 

French colonialism developed a systematic framework for classifying 'good' and 'bad' brotherhoods and imposed the ideology of *Islam noir* ('Black Islam') — the claim that sub-Saharan Islam was a mild, animism-infused variant, manageable and non-threatening — which persisted in paternalistic Western opinion long after decolonization. The colonial state also inflated the figure of the grand *marabout* as a key interlocutor, creating a system of religious clientelism. In British Nigeria, the [[sokoto-caliphate]] was incorporated as a 'Colonial Caliphate': Islamic institutions preserved under discreet British oversight via indirect rule.

**Paradoxical conversion.** One of the greatest and most paradoxical consequences of European imperial conquest was the dramatic acceleration of Islamization. The Muslim population from Senegal to Cameroon and from Sudan to Mozambique probably at least doubled in the colonial era. Colonial economic development — new communication axes, labour migration, monetization — created conditions in which Muslim merchants and clerical families prospered; many converts saw Islam as access to a moral 'macrocosm' that transcended the shattered local 'microcosm'. Some new converts (Methodist church members in Accra in the 1930s) even used their Bibles to preach in the name of Muhammad.

**Islamic thought in colonial context.** Despite the conventional view that colonialism represented a dark age for Muslim intellectual life, recent scholarship emphasizes renewal (*tajdid*) and reform (*islah*). Traditional Quranic schools operated within an 'esoteric episteme' of hierarchical transmission; reformers introduced *madrasa* with classrooms and textbooks — an 'epistemic shift towards a rationalistic episteme' (Louis Brenner, cited in [[parker-reid-oxford-modern-african-history-2013]]).

### Postcolonial Period (1960–present)

Independence did not represent a watershed for Islamic societies. Postcolonial state power fell into secular hands; Islam continued to evolve outside the public sphere. The Iranian Revolution of 1979 was the decisive turning point that propelled Islam to the centre of scholarly and political attention. In the 1980s, Pentecostalism and Islamic reform ('Salafism') both expanded rapidly, both challenging the routinization of spiritual guidance and older elites' monopoly on religious authority. By the late 1990s, many intra-Islamic conflicts had subsided as states exercised greater control over national religious organizations and as popular fatigue forced community rapprochement.

In Senegal, the close alignment between Sufi *shaykhs* and government ministers proved resilient, recovering after a 1990s weakening. Da'wa movements (outreach preaching) became major features of the social landscape, their structure and goals resembling those of development NGOs. The global Islamic development industry — Saudi Arabia, Libya, Iran directing funding to mosques, schools, and health centres — both enabled and constrained African Muslim communities' self-definition.

## Geographic Spread

- **North Africa and the Maghrib:** French colonialism began with Algeria (1830); Islam provided a framework for national resistance. Ben Badis's Association of Muslim Algerian Ulama fused Islamic and nationalist sentiment. Libya's Sanūsiyya resisted Italian conquest into the 1930s; its leader became Libya's first king.
- **West Africa (Sahel and forest belt):** The most studied zone. Major Sufi brotherhoods (Qādiriyya, Tijāniyya, Muridiyya) navigated colonial politics. Reformist/'Wahhabi' movements challenged marabout authority from the 1950s. Nigeria became a site of both Yoruba Islamic plurality and Sufi–reformist conflicts in the north.
- **East Africa:** Swahili coastal Islam extended inland; the Yao converted en masse. East African Islamic studies remain underdeveloped relative to West Africa. The Zanzibar-based Shaykh al-Amîn al-Mazrû'î and 'Abdallâh al-Farsy led reformist movements from the 1890s–1980s.
- **Horn of Africa:** Mahdi-led resistance in Sudan; complex interactions between Islam, Ethiopian Orthodoxy, and colonial powers.

## End Conditions

Ongoing. In the contemporary period, African Islam is marked by diversification: old Sufi orders persisting and adapting, reformist and Salafi movements expanding, da'wa NGOs proliferating, and radical movements in Somalia, Nigeria, Mali, and elsewhere confronting state authority. The 'southerning' of Christianity runs parallel to Islam's own demographic expansion; African Muslim voices increasingly shape the global *umma*'s debates about poverty and racism.

## Historiography

The study of Islam in Africa began within the colonial sphere. The French tradition — beginning with the *Description de l'Egypte* (1809–28) and developed through Delafosse, Paul Marty, and Alfred Le Châtelier — generated the ideology of *Islam noir* and the 'brotherhood plot' framework, which cast Sufi orders as either useful conduits for colonial governance or dangerous subversive networks. British scholarship (Herbert Richmond Palmer on Northern Nigeria; J. S. Trimingham's seven books from 1949–1971) was less ideologically driven but similarly text-centred. Both traditions left a lasting bias toward elite Muslim actors and a text-based representation of African Islam.

From the 1960s, decolonization encouraged return to precolonial Islamic history via Arabic manuscript sources; Nehemia Levtzion was a pioneer. The Iranian Revolution (1979) propelled African Islamic studies to scholarly prominence. Since then the field has undergone a 'postcolonial turn': emphasis on Muslim agency, diversity of practice, avoidance of essentializing Islam as a monolith, attention to women and ordinary believers, and critique of both the 'Sufi versus Wahhabi' binary and the 'moderate versus radical' discourse inherited from colonial surveillance. The journals *Islam et sociétés au Sud du Sahara* and *Islamic Africa* (formerly *Sudanic Africa*) have been key venues.

A significant deficit remains: East African Islam is understudied relative to West Africa and the Maghrib; studies of women's Islamic practice have grown but remain disproportionately thin.
