# The Early Bronze Age Levant (Canaanite Urbanism and Its Collapse)

The first urbanization of Palestine and the Lebanese coast — the rise of
**fortified Canaanite cities** across the third millennium BCE and their collapse
around 2200 BCE. It is the Levantine parallel to the urban revolutions of
[[early-dynastic-sumer|Sumer]] and [[old-kingdom-egypt|Egypt]], and the deep
substrate of later Canaan. Treated in [[edwards-cah-v1p2-1971|CAH Vol. I, Part 2]]
(Ch. XV, R. de Vaux, on Palestine; Ch. XVII, on Syria).

## Definition and Scope

This page covers the **Early Bronze Age (EB I–IV)** Levant — the era de Vaux
reconstructs almost entirely from **pottery seriation** across well-stratified
tells (Jericho, 'Ai, Megiddo, Beth-shan, Tell el-Far'ah, Khirbet Karak, Tell
'Arad) — not the preceding [[chalcolithic|Chalcolithic]] village world or the
later Middle Bronze city-states.

## The decisive shift: from villages to walled cities

De Vaux's central point: the **Chalcolithic was the age of villages** — open,
unwalled, scattered. The Early Bronze Age was **the age of cities**: dwellings
concentrated and, from phase I*b*, enclosed within **massive ramparts**. The
fortifications are the signature of the period — the brick-and-stone walls of
**[[jericho|Jericho]]** (rebuilt seventeen times on the west), the angled walls and
"palace" of **'Ai**, the towered gate of **Tell el-Far'ah**, the semicircular
towers of **Tell 'Arad**. The first **bamah** ("high place," an open-air altar)
appears at Megiddo — a cult form with a long Canaanite and Biblical future.

## Causal Drivers

De Vaux argues the EB civilization did **not** evolve from the local Chalcolithic
(Ghassul–Beersheba) cultures but arose from the **in-migration of a new
population** that brought an "established tradition of architecture and urban
life" (the heavy use of brick even in stone-rich uplands points to an alluvial
homeland). For want of a better name he calls them, conventionally, the
**"Canaanites"** — the Semitic population the Bible distinguishes from the later
Amorites. Settlement was by peaceful infiltration, not conquest.

## Major Phases

- **EB I (a/b)** (~3200–2900) — the new culture forms in central Palestine; the
  first fortified towns appear.
- **EB II** (~2900–2700) — the **apogee**: dense, numerous fortified cities,
  implying many small warring states; First/Second-Dynasty Egyptian synchronisms.
- **EB III** — the **Khirbet Karak ware** horizon: a distinctive red-and-black
  burnished pottery intruding from the north (Anatolia/Transcaucasia via Syria),
  evidence of a new population wave; contemporary with the Egyptian Pyramid Age.
- **EB IV** — a poor, transitional, **de-urbanizing** phase.

## Interaction (Egypt)

Egypt's interest was real but limited: trade for **olive oil** and access to the
Sinai turquoise mines; the **Narmer Palette** appears to depict a Palestinian
**turreted city** among Narmer's enemies, and Egyptian vessels reached
First-Dynasty tombs. The deepest tie was by sea to **[[byblos|Byblos]]** for
cedar. Mesopotamian contact was slight (a Mesopotamian-style ivory bull's head at
Jericho echoing the [[early-dynastic-sumer|Tell Asmar]] statuary).

## End Conditions — the ~2200 BCE collapse

Around the end of EB III the cities were **abandoned more or less simultaneously**
('Ai, Megiddo, Beth-shan, Khirbet Karak, Jericho; Byblos burned), beginning a
centuries-long **eclipse of urban life** (the EB–MB Intermediate Period). De Vaux
links this to the **"great thrust from the desert"** that struck the whole Fertile
Crescent at once — the **[[unknown|Amorites]]**, the same nomadic pressure
associated with the [[fall-of-old-kingdom-2160bce|fall of the Egyptian Old
Kingdom]] and the troubles that would later bring down the Third Dynasty of
[[ur|Ur]]. He flags an open question: whether the Amorites caused the collapse or
merely entered a land already devastated by other invaders.
