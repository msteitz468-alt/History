# Trade Network Formation (Networks of Exchange)

## Definition and Scope

**Trade network formation** is the long-duration process by which separate
societies were progressively linked into **trans-regional systems of exchange** —
of goods, but also of people, ideas, religions, technologies, genes, and pathogens.
The third term in [[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|CWH IV]]'s title (*states, empires, and
**networks***) names what the editor regards as the period's most genuinely
world-historical novelty: by the late first millennium BCE the major states of
Afro-Eurasia were, for the first time, joined end-to-end into a single interacting
web — the [[silk-roads|first Silk Roads era]] — and after a contraction this
re-formed, larger, as the [[silk-roads|second Silk Roads era]] of the
[[early-middle-ages|Tang–Abbasid]] age.

This is the connective counterpart to [[state-formation]] and [[empire]]: networks
both depended on and partly drove the rise of large states. Specific networks have
their own pages — [[silk-roads]], [[indian-ocean-trade]], [[trans-saharan-trade]].

## Causal Drivers

- **Specialization and surplus** — ecological and craft complementarities (silk and
  lacquer from China; spices from India and Southeast Asia; horses and furs from
  the steppe; gold and salt across the Sahara; glass, wine, and metalwork from the
  Mediterranean) created durable incentives to exchange.
- **[[Empire|Imperial]] demand and imperial peace** — large states generated elite
  demand for prestige goods and provided the roads, garrisons, currency, and
  relative security that made long-distance trade viable. The first Silk Roads era
  rested on the simultaneous strength of Han, Kushan, Parthian, and Roman power.
- **[[pastoral-nomadism|Pastoral nomads]] as intermediaries** — CWH IV (May, Ch 9;
  Liu, Ch 17) stresses that it was steppe nomads — able to survive the deserts and
  mountains between civilizations — who physically *carried* and *protected* much of
  the overland trade. Networks were as much a nomad achievement as a sedentary one.
- **Money, credit, and maritime/caravan technology** — [[coinage]], the camel
  saddle, the monsoon-sailing dhow, and oasis/port infrastructure lowered
  transaction costs.

## Major Phases

1. **Pre-integration** (Early Iron Age): regional networks — [[phoenicians|Phoenician]]
   and Greek Mediterranean trade, Mesopotamian and Indian Ocean coasting trade —
   flourishing but not yet joined across Eurasia.
2. **First trans-Eurasian integration** (Classical–Late Antiquity): the
   [[silk-roads|first Silk Roads era]], with [[han-dynasty|Han]] China linked to
   Rome via the [[kushan-empire|Kushans]] and [[parthian-empire|Parthia]];
   simultaneous growth of [[indian-ocean-trade|Indian Ocean]] monsoon trade
   (the *Periplus of the Erythraean Sea*, c. 40–50 CE).
3. **Contraction** (3rd c. CE): the near-simultaneous withdrawal of Han, Kushan,
   Parthian, and Roman power thinned the overland network.
4. **Second integration** (Early Middle Ages): the [[silk-roads|second Silk Roads
   era]] under [[tang-dynasty|Tang]], [[abbasid-caliphate|Abbasid]], and
   [[byzantine-empire|Byzantine]] sponsorship; intensified Indian Ocean and newly
   vigorous [[trans-saharan-trade|trans-Saharan]] trade, the latter binding West
   Africa ([[ghana-empire|Ghana]]) into the Mediterranean economy.

## The Archaic Greek and Phoenician Mediterranean (CAH IV)

[[boardman-cah-v4-1988|CAH Vol. IV]] (Ch. 7e, C. Roebuck) gives the close view of the
**pre-integration** regional network in the Mediterranean on the eve of the Persian Wars —
the moment when "the slender threads of commerce... had been woven into a complex net."

- **A shared but contested sea.** Greeks and **[[phoenicians|Phoenicians]]** divided the
  Mediterranean: Carthage jealously guarded the **far west** (the route by Malta–Sicily–
  Sardinia–Spain), closing direct Greek access to Spanish tin, copper and silver after c. 540
  (the Etruscan–Carthaginian expulsion of the Phocaeans from Alalia), while the **Levant and
  Egypt** remained "primarily a Phoenician area." Greeks dominated the **Aegean, Adriatic and
  Black Seas** they had colonized, with chartered footholds at **[[naucratis]]** (Egypt) and
  the Black Sea *emporia*.
- **Commodities.** Beyond the old trade in **luxuries and scarce metals**, the sixth century
  added **bulk cargoes** — Egyptian and Black Sea **grain**, **olive oil**, **wine**, salt
  fish, **timber** for ships and temples, and the **silver** (Laurium, Thrace) and Lydian
  **electrum** demanded by the new [[coinage|coinages]]. Pottery (Corinthian, then Athenian
  after c. 575) traces production but **not carriage** — Athenian vases reached Italy in the
  ships of **Ionians and Aeginetans** (the merchants' marks of **Sostratus of [[aegina|Aegina]]**).
- **Traders, not states.** Roebuck insists the pattern was set by **producers and carriers**,
  not government policy: famous professionals (the Samian **Colaeus**, who opened the
  Tartessus metal trade c. 640; **Sostratus**, "richest of all traders") were owner-captain-
  merchant in one, and by c. 500 the **Corcyra lead tablets** show voyages financed by
  **maritime loans** — an early credit economy. Physical limits were severe: small (70–80 ton),
  slow, square-rigged ships, seasonal April–October navigation, coasting within sight of land,
  and the constant threat of **piracy** (whose suppression Thucydides ties to the rise of sea
  powers like Corinth and [[polycrates|Polycrates']] Samos).
- **Persia and trade.** Persian conquest of Ionia (after 540) and Thrace (after 512) was "a
  mixture of benefit and harm" — satrapal patronage replaced Lydian, grain transports kept
  flowing, and Athenian silver poured into Egypt — refuting any picture of empire strangling
  commerce.

This regional Mediterranean web is the western anchor of the longer Afro-Eurasian system
below; "freedom of navigation and trade" would, within a generation, become an issue
dividing the [[athenian-democracy|Athenian Empire]] and the [[peloponnesian-league|
Peloponnesian League]].

## Geographic Spread

The networks knit together a continuous Afro-Eurasian zone from Atlantic Iberia and
West Africa to Pacific China and Japan, with three great arteries — the overland
[[silk-roads|Silk Roads]], the [[indian-ocean-trade|Indian Ocean]], and the
[[trans-saharan-trade|Sahara]]. The Americas, Australasia, and most of Oceania
remained outside this system (their own internal exchange networks aside) until
1492 — a separation CWH IV treats as one of the period's defining structural facts.

## Interaction

Networks were the principal medium for the spread of the [[universal-religions|world
religions]] (Buddhism, Christianity, Manichaeism, then Islam), of technologies
(papermaking, crops, metallurgy), and of **disease** (the Antonine and Justinianic
plagues moved along the same routes as silk). They fed [[urbanization]] at nodal
points (Palmyra, [[baghdad|Baghdad]], the Swahili ports, the oasis cities) and were
both cause and consequence of [[empire]]. They are the mechanism behind the
[[divergence-convergence|convergence]] of the Afro-Eurasian world zone.

## End Conditions

The process does not end — it intensifies toward the maritime "expanding webs" of
[[kedar-cwh-v5-2015|CWH V]] and ultimately the post-1500 global system. Within the
period, network *intensity* fluctuated with the rise and fall of the empires that
underwrote it, contracting sharply in the 3rd century and reviving in the Tang–
Abbasid era.

## Historiography

The "Silk Roads" framing — once an exotic specialism — has become a central
organizing device of world history, and CWH IV (edited by a Silk Roads scholar) is
a prime exhibit. Recent scholarship cautions against romanticizing a single
continuous "road," emphasizing instead a shifting mesh of relays, the centrality of
the [[indian-ocean-trade|maritime]] routes (often carrying far more bulk than the
overland ones), the agency of [[pastoral-nomadism|nomadic]] and African
intermediaries, and the co-movement of disease and religion with goods. See
[[silk-roads]], [[indian-ocean-trade]], and [[trans-saharan-trade]].
