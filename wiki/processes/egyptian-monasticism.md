# Egyptian Monasticism

The birth, in the deserts of [[roman-egypt|Roman Egypt]] in the late third century CE, of
**Christian monasticism** — the withdrawal of ascetics into the wilderness that became, in the
[[sources/shaw-oxford-history-ancient-egypt-2000|Oxford History's]] words, the root "from which
all others derived."

## Origins in the desert

"In the late third century, according to tradition, two young rich men, **Paul the first
hermit** and **Anthony the first monk**, each separately left their homes in the Nile Valley to
live in the solitude of the wilderness." Anthony famously **began in an old tomb** near his
village on the Nile, where "he wrestled with demons and wild animals," before going deeper into
the desert. His fame spread across the empire — even **Constantine wrote to him asking for
prayers** — and the community of disciples that gathered around him "eventually developed into a
monastery — the most significant monastery in Christendom, from which all others derived."

## Phases and spread

Anthony's **eremitic** (solitary hermit) model was complemented by the **cenobitic**
(communal) monasticism organized in Egypt soon after, and from Egypt the institution radiated
across the Christian world. It grew on the foundation of an Egyptian Christianity that "took
root in [[alexandria|Alexandria]]" by the mid-first century CE and spread up the Nile, surviving
the **great persecutions of Diocletian (from 303 CE)** to become, after Constantine, the
dominant faith — and ultimately the **Coptic Church**.

## Pharaonic undertow

The volume notes a continuity even here: "Monasticism had its roots in the Egyptian desert …
Even here pharaonic culture was not without its influence" — Anthony's first cell was a
**pharaonic tomb**, and the desert asceticism grew in a landscape saturated with the older
religion (mummification persisted "in some cases as late as the fourth century AD"). It is a
characteristic instance of the [[roman-egypt|Roman period's]] deep **continuity** with the
Egyptian past.
