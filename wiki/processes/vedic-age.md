# The Vedic Age and the Second Urbanization

## Definition and Scope

This is the long arc of state formation in early South Asia, from the composition of the
**Rig Veda** (c. 1500 BCE) to the rise of the **[[mauryan-empire|Mauryan Empire]]** (c. 321
BCE). In [[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|CWH IV]] (Kaul, Ch 18) it is the foundation of "civilization
in mainland South Asia as we know it today" — the transition from semi-nomadic pastoral
tribes to agrarian states, cities, money, writing, and the **caste** order. (It is distinct
from, and conventionally traced separately after the decline of, the earlier Bronze Age
**Harappan/Indus** civilization, c. 1800 BCE.)

## Early and Later Vedic

- **Early Vedic** (c. 1500–1000 BCE): **Indo-Aryan**-speaking (archaic Sanskrit,
  Indo-European), pastoral and semi-nomadic, cattle-centered. Tribes (*jana*) under a chief
  (*rajan*) who received voluntary offerings (*bali*); a kin-based, pre-state, pre-class
  society with reciprocal gift-exchange and no clear money. Reconstructed almost entirely
  from the **sacred texts** themselves.
- **Later Vedic** (c. 1000–500 BCE): eastward expansion into the fertile **Ganga basin**;
  the decisive shift from pastoralism to **agriculture** (wheat, rice, lentils) with the
  ox-plough and (debated) iron. Tribes became **janapadas** ("footprints of the *jana*") —
  fixed territories under *kshatriya* ruling clans; *bali* became compulsory **tribute**;
  lavish royal sacrifices (*ashvamedha*) enacted a "prestige economy" served by *brahmana*
  priests rewarded with land.

## State Formation and the Second Urbanization

By **500–300 BCE** these processes culminated in the **sixteen great states**
(*mahajanapadas*) — monarchies (*rajya*) and oligarchies (*gana-sangha*) in perpetual war —
from which **Magadha** rose supreme (its capital eventually [[pataliputra|Pataliputra]]).
This is the **Second Urbanization**: new cities (Varanasi, Rajagriha, Kaushambi, Mathura),
massive ramparts, the **Northern Black Polished Ware**, the first metal **[[coinage|coins]]**
(*karshapanas*), the **Brahmi** script (late 4th c. BCE), guilds, and long-distance trade
along the **Uttarapatha** and **Dakshinapatha** routes.

## Interaction

The urban ferment of the sixth–fifth centuries BCE produced the **[[axial-age|Axial Age]]**
faiths of South Asia — **[[buddhism|Buddhism]]** and **[[jainism|Jainism]]** — which rejected
Vedic ritual authority and won merchant and royal patronage. The fourfold **[[caste-varna|varna]]**
order crystallized in the Later Vedic period as occupational stratification fused with ritual
hierarchy.

## End Conditions

The arc culminates in the **[[mauryan-empire|Mauryan Empire]]** (321 BCE), South Asia's
first and largest ancient empire — the political consummation of Vedic-to-state
development.

## Historiography

The Early Vedic is reconstructed **almost entirely from sacred oral texts** (the Rig Veda's
earliest manuscript is no older than the 12th c. CE), with thin and uncertain archaeology
(Painted Grey Ware). Two debates are central: the **Indo-Aryan migration** question
(immigration from outside vs. indigenous origin — "heated and racially tinted," majority view
favoring migration; see [[indo-european-origins]]), and the **role of iron** as prime mover
of Gangetic state formation (challenged in favor of socio-political drivers). The "varna"
system's divine self-account (the *Purusha Sukta*) is read by historians as a conscious
structure for controlling access to resources.

## Avari (2007) — Aryan Migration, the Vedas, and Vedic Society

[[avari-india-ancient-past-2007|Avari]] (*India: The Ancient Past*, Routledge 2007, Ch. 4–5)
provides the fullest treatment in the wiki of the Vedic Age as a sequence from pastoral
migration to agrarian state. Key contributions:

### The Aryan Migration: Not Invasion

Avari is explicit that the Aryans were **migrants, not invaders**. Groups of nomadic tribal
people from eastern and southern Afghanistan began migrating to India around **1700 BCE**; a
particular group calling itself *Arya* (noble) arrived around **1400 BCE**. These Rig-Vedic
Aryans carried the earliest portion of the Rig-Veda, composed by bards in an early form of
Sanskrit. "Migrations in ancient times were always slow, gradual and mostly peaceful."

The Aryan migration was part of a larger Indo-European dispersal from the Eurasian steppe
lands north and east of the Black Sea during the third millennium BCE. The Rig-Vedic Aryans
were closely related to the **Avestan** people of Iran — both called themselves Aryan, shared
mythology, ritual, and the soma/haoma plant (identified as an ephedra species from eastern Iran
and Afghanistan). The Avestan and Rig-Vedic traditions "have emerged from a single common source."

**Against Hindutva revisionism**: Avari systematically refutes the revisionist claim that the
Indus Valley Culture and the Vedic Aryan culture are identical. The decisive evidence is that
Vedic texts never mention the cities of the Indus by name; the Rig-Veda was composed in
Afghanistan and reflects Afghan/northwest geography in its early parts; and Aryan names appear
in Mesopotamian documents (Kassite rulers of Babylon, Mittani kingdom of Syria, Boghazköy
tablets recording a 1350 BCE treaty that lists Rig-Vedic gods — Indra, Nasatya, Varuna, Mitra)
predating any Indian textual evidence.

### The Swat Valley and the Gandhara Grave Culture

The **Swat valley** of northern Pakistan is identified as possibly the first area of Aryan
intrusion into the subcontinent (c. 1700 BCE). Archaeological evidence: from 1700 BCE a
change occurred in burial rites (both flexed inhumation in a pit and cremation burial in an
urn — a dual practice not common in contemporary cultures), and a new handmade grey ware with
incised decoration appeared. This is the **Gandhara Grave Culture**, whose geographical features
match the natural scenes depicted in the Rig-Vedic hymns.

### The Four Vedas and Vedic Literature

Avari describes the Vedic literary corpus in detail:
- **Rig-Veda**: 10 books, 1,028 hymns, 10,600 verses. Composed 1400–900 BCE. The oldest
  significant extant Indian text; also the first major composition in any Indo-European tongue.
  Composed by sages from different priestly families over at least 500 years. Written down only
  after 500 BCE. Gives more references to pastoralism than to agriculture.
- **Sama-Veda**: Rearranged hymns of the Rig-Veda, set to music; the first Indian treatise on
  music.
- **Yajur-Veda**: The priest's manual, in two versions (Black and White). The attached *Sulvasutra*
  texts deal with the geometry of fire altars — an early mathematical tradition.
- **Atharva-Veda**: Popular character; plants, herbs, diseases; the first Indian treatise on
  medicine.

Attached to these are the **Brahmanas** (interpret the rituals, emphasize the priest's role in
sacrifices), the **Aranyakas** (forest-books; mysticism rather than ritual), and the
**[[upanishads|Upanishads]]** ("the last stage or the end of Veda, Vedanta"). The Upanishads
raise deeper cosmological and personal questions and "foreshadowed the dissenting tradition that
emerged after 600 bc."

### Vedic Society

- **Pastoralism to agriculture**: Cattle-rearing was central throughout; cattle were "almost a
  religious duty" to protect. Farming was learned from indigenous peoples. Rice replaced wheat
  as the main crop as the Ganga plains were cleared.
- **Patriarchal family (kula)**: Extended three-generation households; senior male as head;
  position of women deteriorated in the later Vedic period; the custom of *sati* had origins
  in the later Vedic period.
- **Political structure**: early Vedic — clan chieftains, no standing state, voluntary offerings
  (*bali*), no regularized taxation; assemblies (*vidatha, parishad, sabha, samiti*). Later Vedic:
  *janapadas* (regions/states), hereditary kings (*rajas*), formal tax collection, court offices
  (purohit, senani, samagrahitri, bhagadugha, kshata). The alliance between priest and king
  "became a key element in the maintenance of the hierarchical balance within the caste system."
- **Sacrifice (yajna)**: Household sacrifice transformed in the later Vedic period into a
  celebration of royal power. The horse sacrifice (*ashvamedha*) marked territorial claims. One
  of the vedas can be understood as "a vast and complex instruction manual for the sacrifice."
- **Varnashrama Dharma**: The fourfold varna system (brahman, kshatriya, vaishya, shudra) was
  "something quite novel, extraordinary and most negative in its social impact" when it developed
  during the Vedic period. The Purusha hymn (Rig-Veda Book X, 90.11–12) gave it divine sanction.

### Pottery and Iron Archaeology

- **OCP** (Ochre-Coloured Pottery): Pre-900 BCE; "could therefore have belonged to the early
  Rig-Vedic Aryans."
- **PGW** (Painted Grey Ware): 900–600 BCE; wheel-made, well-fired grey pottery; associated with
  Aryan expansion into the Ganga basin. Most likely produced by indigenous pre-Aryan craftsmen.
- **NBPW** (Northern Black Polished Ware): After 600 BCE; the hallmark of urban prosperity.
- Iron technology: developed independently within India from c. 1000 BCE (not imported by Aryans).
  First iron tools were weapons, not agricultural implements.

### The Sixteen Mahajanapadas and the Pre-Mauryan Era

By 600 BCE, the political landscape consisted of five large regions (Madhyadesha, Praticya,
Pracya, Uttarapatha, Dakshinapatha) and sixteen mahajanapadas. Two types: clan states (Mallas,
Vriji; anti-Brahmanical, corporate governance, assemblies/sangha, oligarchic) and kingdoms
(hereditary monarchs, brahmanical legitimation via sacrifice). The **three dynasties of Magadha**:
Haryanka (Bimbisara 544–493 BCE; Ajatsatru 492–462 BCE), Shishunaga (413–364 BCE; annexed
Avanti), Nandas (364–321 BCE; founder Mahapadma of shudra origin).

The **Shramanist** heterodoxies challenged Brahmanical orthodoxy: Ajivakas (predetermination),
Charavakas (pure materialism, no afterlife), sceptics (Sanjaya Belatthiputa). Jainism and
Buddhism arose in the eastern clan states, where Brahmanical authority was weaker. See
[[second-urbanisation-india]], [[jainism]], [[spread-of-buddhism]].
