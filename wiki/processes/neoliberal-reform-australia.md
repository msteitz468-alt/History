## Definition and Scope

Neoliberal economic reform in Australia refers to the sustained dismantling of the post-war "[[australian-settlement]]" — the protectionist, arbitration-based, state-managed order — in favour of market liberalisation, privatisation, deregulation, and fiscal discipline. The process began in earnest with the election of the Hawke Labor government in March 1983 and continued, with ideological variations, through the Keating Labor government (1991–96), the Howard Coalition government (1996–2007), and the Rudd–Gillard Labor governments (2007–13). The term "neoliberalism" captures the Australian variant of what was simultaneously occurring across western democracies, but the precise Australian trajectory — driven partly by Labor rather than a purely conservative coalition — is distinctive.

The process is distinct from the earlier postwar "managed prosperity" (see [[australian-settlement]]) that it replaced, and must not be conflated with the 1970s stagflation crisis that provided its political opening.

## Causal Drivers

The immediate impetus was the economic crisis of the early 1980s: between 1981–82 and 1983–84 unemployment rose from 6.2 to 9.6 per cent, and 1982 recorded "the worst Australian economic performance since the Depression" ([[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]], ch. 7). The Fraser government's failure to deliver on reform rhetoric, combined with Keynesian stagflation internationally, discredited the post-war policy consensus.

A generational shift was decisive: "A cohort of activists who had come of age in the 1960s and experienced the bitter reverses of politics in the 1970s was ready to take over from the old hands whose formative ideas derived from the 1940s and 1950s. Within this emerging cohort it was agreed that the Keynesian model of state intervention to ensure prosperity had failed" ([[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]], ch. 7). On the Labor side, this expressed itself as the need to avoid the economic mismanagement associated with the Whitlam period; among Liberals, as rejection of the Deakinite ameliorative liberalism of Menzies and Fraser.

International intellectual currents — particularly Thatcherite Britain and Reaganite America — reinforced the domestic momentum. Globalisation rhetoric (the term itself barely used before the mid-1980s) provided the framework: "governments must be willing to compete in global markets" or face ruin.

## Major Phases

### Phase 1 — Labor Liberalisation under Hawke (1983–1991)
The Hawke government floated the dollar in December 1983 and deregulated capital markets. It progressively reduced tariff protection for Australian industries (manufacturing and rural production), reviewed and began privatising public utilities, streamlined regulation and competition policy, and pressured states to dismantle their own monopolies. Central to the early phase was the **Accord** with the trade unions: "its purpose was to couple wage restraint with the delivery of social benefits, securing cooperation from business and unions in the pursuit of reform" ([[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]], ch. 7). The Commonwealth public service shed more than 76,000 staff in the decade from 1986 through contracting-out and privatisation.

University tuition fees (abolished by Whitlam in 1974) were reintroduced as income-contingent loans; the unified national system of higher education was created; the proportion of public funding for universities fell from 85 per cent in 1987 to 65 per cent in 1992. By 1990 media concentration had reached the point where two corporations (Murdoch's News Corp and Fairfax) controlled 88 per cent of the press.

### Phase 2 — Keating's Social-Democratic Neoliberalism (1991–1996)
Keating, who displaced Hawke in December 1991, deepened the reform trajectory while attempting to graft onto it a social agenda: engagement with Asia, Indigenous reconciliation, and republicanism. His signature economic reform was the introduction of **compulsory superannuation** payments by employers in 1992. Winning the 1993 election unexpectedly against John Hewson's even-more-radical "Fightback!" platform, Keating was forced to revisit the role of the state in providing public goods, producing the "Working Nation" jobs compact and "Creative Nation" cultural package. His 1992 Redfern speech, acknowledging "it was we who did the dispossessing," was the high-water mark of reconciliation politics within the neoliberal framework.

### Phase 3 — Howard's Conservative Neoliberalism (1996–2007)
Howard, elected promising that Australians could feel "comfortable and relaxed," maintained market reform while rejecting Labor's redistributive framing. The Howard government's largesse included over $61 billion from privatisation — Telstra, airports, the Commonwealth Bank, Qantas — while running budget surpluses and cutting personal income tax. The **goods and services tax** (GST) was introduced in 2000, having regressive effects on low-income families. Howard redirected welfare outlays toward middle-class families through private health insurance rebates (non-means-tested 30 per cent rebate, increasing private coverage from 34 to 44 per cent), private school funding (for every dollar on government schools, two on private schools), and concessional superannuation taxes.

The most controversial measure was **WorkChoices** (legislated December 2005): it removed unfair dismissal protections for workers in firms with up to 100 employees, limited union officials' right to enter workplaces, and allowed individual contracts to override minimum conditions. "Within the OECD, the extent of the intervention on the side of employers was unprecedented" ([[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]], ch. 8). The ACTU's "Your Rights at Work" campaign (costing around $30 million) directly countered WorkChoices and shifted votes at the 2007 election.

### Phase 4 — Rudd–Gillard and the GFC Response (2007–2013)
Kevin Rudd's government (from November 2007) maintained the structural framework of market reform but deployed a major fiscal stimulus to shield Australia from the Global Financial Crisis (GFC): $14.7 billion for school buildings, $12.7 billion in cash handouts, $6.6 billion for public housing, $2.7 billion for home insulation, plus a first home buyers' grant. GDP growth dropped from nearly 5 per cent in 2006 to about 1 per cent in 2008 but Australia avoided recession. The Resources Super Profits Tax proposed by Rudd in 2010 triggered a mining-industry advertising campaign that contributed to his removal by the Labor caucus in June 2010. Julia Gillard's Clean Energy Bill (2011) introduced carbon pricing, fulfilling a commitment both major parties had made in the 2007 election.

## Geographic Spread

The reform was national in scope but uneven in impact. The mining-rich states of Western Australia and Queensland benefited most from the commodities boom. Energy and mining accounted for 35 per cent of Australia's export earnings in 2000 and 55 per cent in 2010. Western Australia's inequality would have been "almost as unequal as the United States" while Tasmania and the ACT would have ranked among the most equal in the OECD ([[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]], ch. 8). A "three-speed economy" was identified: mining in the fast lane, manufacturing in the slow lane, the non-tradable sector in the middle.

## End Conditions

The process had not ended by 2013 (the scope of the source). The structural framework — floating dollar, open capital account, low tariffs, privatised utilities, income-contingent university fees, compulsory superannuation — remained in place across successive governments. The political controversy shifted to distribution (who bears the costs and captures the gains) rather than the market-orientation itself. The GFC of 2007–08 demonstrated both the resilience of the reform framework (Australia avoided recession, uniquely among OECD countries) and its limits (the Rudd government's stimulus package was explicitly Keynesian). The bipartisan consensus on market reform was "the common ground" of Australian politics after 1996; what divided the parties was how to distribute its proceeds.

## Historiography

The canonical account is Paul Kelly, *The End of Certainty: The Story of the 1980s* (1992), which frames the reform as a heroic dismantling of the "Australian Settlement." Kelly's later *The March of Patriots* (2009) extends this narrative through the Howard years. Critical responses include Michael Pusey's survey work showing that "reformers had not won consensus for 'the new consensus'" and that middle Australians consistently preferred old economic institutions to new market-based arrangements. The chapter (James Walter in [[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]]) offers a qualified assessment: the reforms produced strong productivity and growth outcomes in the 1990s, but at the cost of rising inequality, precarious employment, weakened social solidarity, and declining trust in government (74 per cent of survey respondents having little confidence in federal government by the late 1990s). The collection coverage on post-1983 Australia is strong via this volume.
