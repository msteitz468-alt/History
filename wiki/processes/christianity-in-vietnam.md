## Definition and Scope

This page covers the establishment and growth of Roman Catholic Christianity among the Vietnamese during the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, under both the [[nguyen-lords|Nguyen Lords]] in the south and the [[trinh-lords|Trinh Lords]] in the north, as documented in Jesuit and Paris Foreign Missions Society accounts. It covers missionary activity, the development of the romanized Vietnamese script associated with (but not invented by) Alexandre de Rhodes, the parallel and much larger body of vernacular Nom Christian literature produced for use in Vietnamese congregations, and the ambivalent political toleration Christian missions received from both Vietnamese courts, who valued missionaries' astronomical, medical, and diplomatic utility as much or more than their religious message. It does not cover nineteenth-century French colonial-era Christianity or missions after 1800.

## Causal Drivers

Jesuit missionaries first entered the Vietnamese-speaking world at Hoi An in the 1610s, `enabled` by existing Portuguese trade networks based at Macau and by the presence of Japanese Christian refugees fleeing persecution in Japan who assisted early proselytizing. The Paris Society of Foreign Missions was founded in the 1650s with Papal backing as a French-led, secular-clergy alternative to the Portuguese-linked Jesuits, and began operating clandestinely in the north in the late 1660s in collaboration with the newly formed French East India Company. Vietnamese court toleration of missionaries was `contributed_to` by their non-religious utility: Jesuit skill at predicting eclipses and keeping accurate calendars (an important marker of a ruler's competence to govern) and Jesuit medical knowledge won them access to both the Trinh and Nguyen courts even amid periodic persecution.

## Major Phases

- **1615–1630s: initial establishment.** Alexandre de Rhodes and other early Jesuits worked first in the south (Hoi An, Qui Nhon) before Rhodes entered the north in 1627, remained three years, and was expelled; he returned to the south 1640–1645. Geronimo Maiorica (1589–1656), less remembered outside Vietnam than Rhodes but arguably more consequential locally, spent eighteen years (1632–1650) building one of the most successful early missions in Nghe An, reporting 26 churches and over 4,000 Christians by 1634, rising to an estimated 40,000 Christians in the province by 1650. He produced a large body of Nom-language devotional literature (saints' lives, biblical stories, liturgical aids) still used by Vietnamese Christian communities in the twentieth century.
- **1651: Rhodes's dictionary.** Rhodes published a Vietnamese-Portuguese-Latin dictionary in Rome (1651) using a romanized transcription of Vietnamese — the ancestor of modern quoc ngu — though the alphabet itself had been devised earlier by Portuguese missionaries as a language-learning aid, not by Rhodes, and was not the primary script used in Vietnamese Christian community life (which relied on Nom).
- **1660s–1710s: expansion under the Paris Society and renewed Jesuit activity.** By Trinh Can's death in 1709, forty-seven European missionaries (eighteen Jesuits, fourteen Paris Society, ten Dominicans, one Augustinian, four others) had served in the north, ordaining twenty-eight native priests (Paris Society) and four (Jesuits). A Jesuit estimate placed northern Christians at 250,000 by 1655, part of a broader religious ferment of the era also expressed in Buddhist, Daoist, and spirit-cult activity (the "holy mothers" pantheon emerging alongside Marian veneration).
- **1660s–1760s: persistence under proscription.** Christianity was periodically banned by the Trinh (reaffirmed in 1696, noted even then to have had no effect over the preceding 33 years) and occasionally by the Nguyen (a 1699 order to expel Christians from Gia Dinh went apparently unenforced). Despite this, from 1709–1760s, eighty-six more European missionaries served the north and 111 more northern Vietnamese were ordained priests. Major community concentrations developed in Nghe An, the Hai Duong plain (Ke Sat), and south of Ke Cho at modern Phat Diem.
- **Gia Dinh/Mekong frontier, 1690s–1700s.** Christianity took particular root among Vietnamese settlers on the Gia Dinh frontier: missionary accounts estimate roughly 10 percent of the Vietnamese population of Gia Dinh was Christian by the early eighteenth century, drawn by the greater freedom of the multi-ethnic frontier.

## Geographic Spread

Concentrated initially at Hoi An and in Nghe An Province (both north and south of the Trinh-Nguyen border), later spreading through the Red River plain (Ke Cho, Hai Duong, Phat Diem) under the Trinh, and southward into Gia Dinh and the Mekong delta frontier under the Nguyen, where Christian communities coexisted with Chinese, Khmer, and Cham settlers.

## Interaction

Christianity developed alongside, and arguably stimulated, a broader wave of religious activity in seventeenth-century Vietnam: Buddhist temple-building and Nom sutra publication, Daoist claims on popular spirit cults, and the elaboration of spirit-medium pantheons (including the "holy mothers," emerging in apparent parallel with Marian devotion). The proscription-yet-persistence pattern paralleled Nguyen and Trinh courts' selective use of Christian missionaries' scientific and diplomatic utility even while banning the religion itself — a pattern of state pragmatism outweighing doctrinal concern until periodic local persecutions.

## End Conditions

This process did not "end" within the period covered by this ingest; Christian communities persisted, sometimes clandestinely, through the eighteenth century and into the Tay Son and early Nguyen dynasty periods, expanding further under nineteenth-century French colonial influence (outside this page's scope). Vietnamese Christians and European missionaries continued to suffer periodic localized persecution throughout, without the state ever succeeding in suppressing the religion outright.

## Historiography

Sourced from K. W. Taylor, *A History of the Vietnamese* (Cambridge, 2013), who draws on published Jesuit and Paris Foreign Missions accounts (Alexandre de Rhodes's own writings; Joseph Tissanier; Giovanni Filippo de Marini; Christoforo Borri; Samuel Baron's later "Description of the Kingdom of Tonqueen"). Taylor is explicit in correcting a "common misperception" that Rhodes invented the Vietnamese alphabet or that missionaries proselytized primarily through it — the alphabet was a Portuguese-devised study aid, while the actual devotional literature used in Vietnamese congregations (via Geronimo Maiorica and others) was overwhelmingly in Nom, a point Taylor notes has received little attention from modern scholars of Nom literary history, though Vietnamese Christian lexicographers (Vu Van Kinh, Father Tran Van Kiem) pioneered modern Nom dictionary studies using Maiorica-attributed texts.
