# Greater Southwest and California Colonial Encounter to the 1880s

Spanish, Mexican, and U.S. regimes over [[pueblo-peoples|Pueblo]], [[apache|Apache]], [[navajo-dine|Navajo (Diné)]], and [[california-native-peoples|California Native]] peoples from first sustained European contact to the reservation threshold of the 1880s. Primary synthesis: Howard R. Lamar and Sam Truett, “The Greater Southwest and California from the Beginning of European Settlement to the 1880s,” [[sources/cambridge-history-native-peoples-americas-v1-2|CHNPA I.2]] ch. 10.

## Definition and Scope

Not a full aboriginal ethnography of the Southwest and California, but a map of **broader interactions** between Native and European-American cultures from first mutual discovery through New Spain, Mexico, and the United States to ~1888. The chapter’s geographic frame runs from Texas through New Mexico and Arizona into Alta California.

Coronado’s 1540s reports that **no gold and no fabulous cities of wealth** existed north of New Spain produced the first realistic Spanish image of the Greater Southwest: **village-dwelling agricultural Indians** surrounded by **nomadic hunting and raiding** peoples across a vast arid belt from present-day Texas to Arizona (`preceded_by:` [[coronado-expedition-1540]]). Diversity among Native peoples of this region was, and remains, greater than in any other part of the continental United States—from Rio Grande irrigators to Coahuiltecan foragers and Chumash maritime economies (`contains:` [[pueblo-peoples]], [[california-native-peoples]], [[apache]]).

Early Spanish–Indian relations differed structurally from later Anglo–Indian relations: Spanish policy was **generally not genocidal** and did **not require complete cultural assimilation**, so many groups survived centuries of encroachment before U.S. control in the 1850s (`contrasts_with:` later Anglo removal/extermination assumptions). The Crown aimed to conquer, convert, and dominate under elaborate legal and missionary codes and argued for Native–Spanish alliance in production, defense, even intermarriage—but intentions were not always translated into actions, and radical demographic, economic, and cultural changes were not “just and fair” from Indian perspectives on the far northern frontier.

## Causal Drivers

- **Imperial mining and frontier strategy** — Zacatecas silver (1546) kept northern dreams alive despite Coronado’s discouragement; Oñate’s 1598 patent mixed mining, Franciscan conversion, and fear of English Pacific forays (Drake) (`followed_by:` [[onate-new-mexico-1598]]).
- **Colonial extraction on sedentary farmers** — tribute, encomienda, mission labor, and Athapaskan slave export strained Pueblo resource margins and intensified raiding.
- **Religious confrontation** — Franciscan kiva raids, burning of sacred objects, witchcraft hangings as precipitants of 1680.
- **Horse–livestock–raiding complex** — Spanish animals remade Apache and Navajo economies; Comanche displacement of Eastern Apaches with French guns reorganized the Texas–Plains edge.
- **Disease** — bibliographic and narrative consensus treats epidemic disease as “perhaps the most deadly element of the European conquest” (`concurrent_with:` [[virgin-soil-epidemics]]); load-bearing collapses among Pueblos and California peoples.
- **Mexican fiscal/political breakdown** — cut Gálvez-style Apache subsidies; secularization and ranch peonage in California; open northern raiding corridors to 1845.
- **U.S. conquest and settler democracy** — annexations 1845–48; California gold 1849+; local militia/miner violence where federal protection failed; Carleton/Crook/Miles military reduction where the Army concentrated force.

## Major Phases

### 1. Entradas and image-making (1540–1598)
[[coronado-expedition-1540]] (1540–42) and subsequent prospectors; Cabeza de Vaca among Karankawas (1528); de Soto among Caddos (1541); Cabrillo (1542) and Vizcaíno (1602) on the California coast without lasting occupation. Pre- and early-contact trade already linked coast, Coahuiltecans, Caddos, and Rio Grande Pueblos (cornmeal, cotton blankets, turquoise); the horse later amplified these networks.

### 2. New Mexico colony and crisis (1598–1696)
[[onate-new-mexico-1598]]: first permanent colony; first-year food seizure, rape, and murder; Acoma siege, killings, and maiming of captives; governors’ tributes; mission and encomienda labor; export of Apache/Navajo/Ute captives to mines; [[pueblo-revolt-1680]] (August 1680) killing 400+ colonists and 21 Franciscans; de Vargas reconquest 1692–94 without full restoration of the antebellum regime (encomienda abolished; ceremonial eradication largely abandoned; Spanish–Pueblo cooperation against Apache/Comanche/Navajo raids).

### 3. Spanish borderlands equilibrium (c. 1690s–1821)
- **Pima/Papago and Arizona:** Kino (Mission Dolores 1687) with cattle, food, non-interference in Pima–Apache war; undone in part by Apache raids and 1751 Pima revolt; Yuman destruction of Colorado River mission (1780s) breaks overland trail to California; Arizona becomes a “no-man’s-land.”
- **Apache corridor (~250 miles, SE Arizona to NM–Texas border):** by 1685 presidios (Janos, Altar); by 1760 a century of Spanish control efforts had failed—blocking settlement north of the Gila, middle Rio Grande (Albuquerque–El Paso), and Laguna/Conchos.
- **Eastern Apache collapse vs Comanche ascent:** San Saba destruction 1757 by ~2,000 Comanches; Comanche–European warfare in Texas until [[red-river-war-1874]].
- **Bourbon Apache peace (late 18th c.):** José and Bernardo de Gálvez—separate treaties per band, food, alcohol, inferior firearms, trade to fracture inter-Apache hostility; by 1800 many Western Apaches near presidios; Mexican independence cuts funds → new devastation (~5,000 Mexican dead; comparable flight from NW settlements).
- **California mission frontier (1769–1830s):** Serra–Portolá 1769; 21 coastal missions to Sonoma; concentration, forced labor, disease, and sanitary despair; ~54,000 baptisms; population ~300,000 → 98,000 by 1832 (end of mission period).
- **Eighteenth-century trade:** captives south; Indian children as household servants; Pecos and Taos fairs; after 1780s Comanche peace, Comancheros into Colorado, western Kansas, west Texas; Crown shift toward trade/treaties and Provincias Internas.

### 4. Mexican period (1821–1846/48)
Mission influence collapses in New Mexico (priests from as many as sixty to five by 1832); Pueblo numbers fall further; Pecos abandoned under Plains raids. Western/Eastern Coyotero and other Apache groups raid deep into Sonora and Chihuahua still in 1845—no funds for gifts, unpaid presidials, American arms trade. Navajo raiding dominates society 1800–1850 (goods/captives more than killing); reciprocal war with Mexican settlers from 1818 to U.S. conquest. Texas: Jumanos/Coahuiltecans exterminated or absorbed; Republic after San Jacinto 1836 rejects accommodation—conquest, treaty, removal, or extermination; Rangers and Colt arms; war into the 1870s until buffalo slaughter ends Comanche food base. California: 1834 Secularization Act promises neophytes half of mission lands; rancheros take virtually all → peonage; Central Valley slave-raids for labor; Sutter’s Fort (1839) as life-and-death labor regime—prelude to gold-rush hostility.

### 5. U.S. conquest to closed “frontier” (1846–1888)
Framing statistic: **~¾ of Greater Southwest Indian populations still not under Mexican control in 1845**; **all under some form of U.S. control/restraint by 1887–88**—the “traumatic saga” of that transformation.

- **New Mexico:** Kearny 1846; Taos rebellion 1847 (Hispanic + Taos Indians kill Gov. Charles Bent); Calhoun seeks special Pueblo status (property rights confirmed, not voting); Sumner’s Fort Union perimeter (Fort Defiance vs Navajos, etc.); 1855–56 reservation treaties unratified.
- **Navajo Long Walk:** Carleton / Kit Carson winter campaigns—“entire subjugation or destruction of all the men”; cornfields and food caches destroyed; ~8,000 marched to Bosque Redondo; drought 1864 and freeze 1865; by 1868 return, population reduced by **perhaps half** of 1863 by disease and starvation; military power broken though raids continue to ~1885 at waning force.
- **Arizona Apache wars:** Territory 1863 (~5,000 Anglo/Mexican vs ~30,000 Indians); Cochise/Mangas Coloradas crises; Gov. Goodwin’s extinction ideology vs Spanish-Mexican incorporation ideal; Camp Grant massacre 1871 (115 Arivaipa killed by Tucson citizens and Papagos); Crook/Miles; Geronimo 1886; Chiricahua to Fort Marion then Fort Sill—“incomplete conquest.”
- **California demographic collapse:** still ~150,000 in 1845; 100,000+ gold-seekers 1849–51; 18 unratified 1851 treaties; militia campaigns; disease, starvation, malnutrition, and **simple homicide** → 35,000 by 1860, ~10% of contact numbers by the twentieth century.
- **Southern Plains:** [[medicine-lodge-treaty-1867]]; [[red-river-war-1874]]; Quanah Parker’s Quahadi surrender; Utley’s “convergence” of command analogous to Carleton/Crook/Miles.

**Endpoint (1888):** southwestern Indian “frontier” as zone of autonomous raiding/warfare gone under U.S. military and reservation control; cultures persist in Pueblo/Pima/Papago villages, Navajo canyonlands, Apache reservations, “almost invisible pockets in California,” and Indian Territory.

## End Conditions

By **1888** the autonomous southwestern Indian “frontier” ends under U.S. military and reservation restraint—not cultural extinction. Lamar & Truett stress **mutual survival over three centuries** and persistence of distinctive if evolving cultures, against older historiography of constant confrontation alone. The process closes when free-ranging raiding polities are broken or confined, while Pueblo villages, Navajo canyonland society, Apache reservation communities, and California remnant groups continue into the multiethnic present—“still learning to share the same southwestern home.”

## Historiography

Lamar & Truett frame the chapter as interaction history, not aboriginal reconstruction. Bibliographic pillars include Spicer *Cycles of Conquest* (seminal Spanish–Mexican–U.S. impact synthesis); *Handbook of North American Indians* vols. 8–10 (Heizer California; Ortiz Southwest); post-Bolton ethnohistory (Forbes, Jones, John, Weber) critical of Spanish borderlands boosterism; Crosby and Reff on disease; Cook, Heizer *Destruction of California Indians*, Hurtado *Indian Survival*, Rawls, Phillips on California; McNitt, Moore, Thompson, Richard White *Roots of Dependency* on Navajo; Cordell *Prehistory of the Southwest* and Bandelier’s Pecos sequence for precontact continuity (see [[ancestral-pueblo]], [[chaco-canyon]]). Older literature stressed permanent confrontation; recent work stresses survival, negotiation, and evolving distinctiveness.
