# Early Bronze Age Aegean

The roughly two-millennium span (~3200–2000 BCE) during which the lands around the
**Aegean Sea** — mainland Greece (**Helladic**), the Cyclades (**Cycladic**), and
Crete (**Minoan**) — passed from Neolithic village life to the threshold of the
**Minoan palaces** and the coming of the Greeks. It is a prehistory *without
written records*, reconstructed entirely from **stratification and the comparison
of objects**, and it is the seedbed of the later Aegean civilizations. Treated in
[[edwards-cah-v1p2-1971|CAH Vol. I, Part 2]] (Ch. XXVI(a), J. L. Caskey; Cyprus in
Ch. XXVI(b)).

## Definition and Scope

Caskey's chapter is a deliberately **cautious** survey: written sources are
lacking, much of the excavation was done by underfunded pioneers, and "objects of
similar appearance are not necessarily contemporary." The conventional scheme
divides each region's Bronze Age into **Early, Middle, Late**, and the Early phase
into I–III. The three cultural provinces — **Helladic** (mainland), **Cycladic**
(islands), **Minoan** (Crete) — developed in parallel and in contact, but were not
a single people or homogeneous culture. This is *not* a tree but a network of
trading communities around a shared sea.

## Causal Drivers

- **The sea and trade.** The Aegean is a web of islands and harbours; "fishing and
  trading were always the natural pursuits of men." Free movement is proven by the
  wide distribution of **Melian obsidian** (from the Dhemenagaki ridge on Melos),
  found all over Greece, the islands, and western Anatolia.
- **Metallurgy.** Copper (at first unalloyed) and then tin-bronze; crucibles and
  moulds at island sites; gold and silver in tombs. The Aegean shared in the
  metal-hungry economy of [[early-bronze-age-anatolia|EBA Anatolia]] and
  [[troy|Troy]].
- **Island craft.** The Cyclades produced the famous **marble figurines** —
  austere, stylised folded-arm figures (and the joyous **harpist and flute-player**
  of Keros and Naxos) — and incised "frying pans," a craft tradition with no
  mainland parallel at the time.

## Major Phases

- **Early phase (EH/EC/EM I, ~3200–2700 BCE).** Red burnished pottery; first
  settlements; round huts giving way to rectangular houses (Eutresis in Boeotia is
  the key mainland sequence; C-14 dates ~2670 BCE).
- **The florescence (EH/EC II, ~2700–2300 BCE).** The richest phase: the
  **sauceboat** is its hallmark vessel. Fortified citadels with monumental
  buildings — above all **[[lerna|Lerna]]'s "House of the Tiles"** in the Argolid
  and the round building at **Tiryns** — point to ranked societies under local
  "princes." Cycladic **Chalandriani** (Syros), a fortified town with U-shaped
  bastions, is the island counterpart. Seafarers coasted the eastern Mediterranean
  and perhaps crossed to Africa.
- **The catastrophe and EH III (~2300–2000 BCE).** A wave of **violent
  destruction** ended EH II in the Argolid — Lerna's House of the Tiles, Tiryns,
  Asine, Zygouries burnt; Corinth abandoned. A **new people** of different
  antecedents settled, building **apsidal houses**, making new pottery, and using
  the **potter's wheel** and the first **grey "Minyan"-like ware**. This horizon
  feeds directly into the [[coming-of-the-greeks-1900bce|coming of the Greeks]]
  debate.

## Crete: the Prepalatial Foundation

Crete (Evans's **E.M. I–III**, now also called **Prepalatial**) followed its own,
**uninterrupted** course: no EH II-style catastrophe struck the island. The east
(Mochlos, Vasiliki) and the Messara plain (with its great circular **tholos
tombs**) led; stone vases imitating **Egyptian** prototypes and Old Kingdom
contacts appear in E.M. II; E.M. III shades into M.M. Ia and the **age of the
palaces**. Refugees from the mainland catastrophe may have joined this development.
The continuity of Crete against the disruption of the mainland is one of the
chapter's structural contrasts.

## Geographic Spread: Cyprus

[[edwards-cah-v1p2-1971|CAH]] Ch. XXVI(b) treats **Early Cypriot** separately. The
EBA on Cyprus **begins ~2300 BCE, synchronising with the end-EB2 catastrophe in
[[early-bronze-age-anatolia|Anatolia]]** — and may, the chapter suggests, have been
a **direct outcome** of it (refugees or trade-disruption from the Anatolian
mainland). Its diagnostic is **Red Polished ware**; its beginnings are disputed
between the **Philia culture** of the central plain and the classic **Vounous**
(Bellapais) cemetery sequence. Cyprus's copper would make it pivotal later; in the
EBA it is known almost entirely from cemeteries.

## Interaction

The Aegean EBA interlocks with [[troy|Troy I–II]] and [[early-bronze-age-anatolia|EBA
Anatolia]] (the depas cup, grey wares, the metal trade) on one side and with
[[old-kingdom-egypt|Old Kingdom Egypt]] (stone-vase forms, sealings) on the other.
Its end-EH II catastrophe is roughly contemporary with the **~2200 BCE crisis
horizon** that also saw the fall of [[gutian-invasion|Akkad]] and the Egyptian
[[first-intermediate-period|First Intermediate Period]] — though the Aegean
evidence is for invasion, not climate, and the synchronism should not be
over-read.

## End Conditions

The Early Bronze Age closed ~2000 BCE with another change: the **Middle Helladic**
mainland (apsidal houses, **Minyan ware**, cist graves) and, in Crete, the first
**Minoan palaces**. The mainland transition is bound up with the arrival of
**Indo-European Greek-speakers** (see [[coming-of-the-greeks-1900bce]] and
[[indo-european-migrations]]); Crete's is a peaceful internal florescence.

## Historiography

A model of **disciplined inference from material culture alone**. Caskey's caution
about equating destruction-layers with invasions, and his refusal to fix the origin
of the EH III newcomers ("who came we know not whence"), is exactly the
methodological restraint that the [[indo-european-origins|Indo-European
migration debate]] demands. Absolute dates rest on **early radiocarbon** ("still in
experimental stages") and on a few synchronisms with Egypt and the Cyclades. The
EH II/III destruction was established by the **Lerna** excavations of the 1950s,
which overturned the older picture of a peaceful EBA ended only by a Middle Helladic
incursion. See [[cambridge-ancient-history-methodology]].
