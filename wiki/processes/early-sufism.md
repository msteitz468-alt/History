## Definition and Scope

**Early Sufism** refers to the development of organized Muslim mysticism from the ascetic movements of the Marwani period through the ecstatic and theoretical flowering of the early 'Abbasid era, c. 750–945 CE. "Sufism" (*tasawwuf*) takes its name, by one tradition, from the coarse woolen garment (*suf*) worn by early ascetics as a sign of renunciation. The movement began as a minority current within [[sunni-islam|Jama'i-Sunni]] Islam but would, in later centuries, come to "dominate the whole inner life of Islam, Sunni and even to a degree Shi'i" (Hodgson). This page covers the formative period only; the mature Sufi order system belongs to the High Middle Ages.

## Causal Drivers

### 1. The Tension between Prosperity and Spiritual Depth

The dazzling prosperity of the 'Abbasid court created conditions for spiritual discontent. "The glitter of the court and its refinement were founded at last upon pride and greed, upon torture and murder, upon innumerable falsehoods of word and deed. Nor could any privileged circles in Islamdom fully escape a like indictment" (Hodgson). For those who felt the Qur'anic challenge most acutely, the very success of the Muslim state seemed to mock the ideal of individual responsibility before God.

### 2. Ascetic Roots in the Piety-Minded Movement

From the earliest generations, Muslims noted for their *zuhd* (pious world-renunciation amounting to practical asceticism) had existed within the Piety-minded circles. Islam formally rejected monastic celibacy — "the Islamic tradition set its head against monastic celibacy" (Hodgson) — but individuals who might in other traditions have become monks found equivalent forms for their devotion. **Hasan al-Basri** (d. 728), the austere saint of Basra in Marwani times, was claimed as a source by both the Mu'tazili school and the early Sufi movement; his sermons on death, accountability, and divine transcendence set a model of inward personal concern.

### 3. Encounter with Christian Monasticism

"Much was learned by the mystically-inclined from Christian monks, and no doubt from other sources too, of the inward life of the soul that would detach itself from the world and love God only" (Hodgson). The 'Abbasid expansion over formerly Christian and Mazdean populations brought Muslims into contact with traditions of monastic discipline, mystical theology, and spiritual direction that had no equivalent in the garrison-town Islam of Marwani times. "At least occasionally, men who might otherwise have become Christian monks were converted to Islam in its Sufi form when they felt the call to a more reflective life" (Hodgson).

### 4. The Kerygmatic and Mystical Modes of Piety

Hodgson distinguishes three components of devotional experience: the **paradigm-tracing** (ritual conformity to cosmic patterns), the **kerygmatic** (response to irreversible historical revelation), and the **mystical** (inward exploration of selfhood and its relation to the divine). Early Sufism represented the mystical mode asserting itself within an Islamic tradition dominated by the kerygmatic: "Less historically-oriented Muslim movement was called Sufism at Kufah, and this name came to prevail elsewhere also" (Hodgson). While the Hadith folk, the Mu'tazilis, and the Shi'i groups all worked primarily within the kerygmatic mode, the Sufis turned inward.

## Major Phases

### Phase 1: Asceticism and World-Renunciation (c. 720–800)

The earliest Sufi precursors were marked above all by *zuhd* — rejection of worldly goods, pleasures, and social position. Their mood was one of fear before divine judgment and sorrow at the distance between the Qur'anic ideal and actual Muslim society. No distinctive mystical doctrine separated them clearly from the more austere Hadith folk; "in some cases it is hard to draw a line between what was Sufi mystical self-examination and what was Hadithi moralism" (Hodgson).

### Phase 2: Emergence of Distinctively Mystical Experience (c. 800–870)

"With 'Abbasi times such tendencies ceased to be merely ascetic" (Hodgson). Great ecstatic saints arose — figures whose experiences of divine nearness (or union) went beyond the moral framework of Shari'ah-mindedness. Circles of devoted admirers gathered around them to record their words and experiences and to attempt to imitate their state of detachment from all worldly things.

The Sufis "normally accepted the Shar'i approach in its Jama'i-Sunni forms as valid in its own realm. But they tended to call the Shari'ah law merely external, a matter of mere outward actions only incidentally relevant to the soul" (Hodgson). This position created permanent tension: the Shari'ah-minded could always accuse the Sufis of antinomianism; the Sufis could always argue they were transcending rather than violating the law.

A critical conceptual development was the distinction between spiritual **stations** (*maqamat* — achieved through sustained discipline: repentance, renunciation, patience, trust in God) and **states** (*ahwal* — gifts of divine grace beyond the aspirant's control: intimacy, awe, longing, love). This two-tier structure allowed Sufism to maintain that mystical experience was both a discipline and a gift.

The **Karramis** of Khurasan — founded by Ibn-Karram (d. 869) — occupied an ambiguous position close to both Sufis and Hadith folk. They were concerned with law and hadith but contributed significantly to Sufi analysis of inward experience; their founder's work fed into later Sufi theory.

### Phase 3: Theoretical Articulation and the Crisis of Ecstasy (c. 870–945)

The most creative and most contested period of early Sufism produced both sophisticated theoretical accounts of the soul's journey toward God and dramatic individual figures whose ecstatic utterances (shatahat) pushed against the limits of Shari'ah propriety.

Hodgson's account reaches the threshold of the mature period (c. 945) but gestures toward it: "the Sufi tradition was later, in a developed form, to dominate the whole inner life of Islam" — implying that by 945 the major conceptual structures were in place even if the Sufi order (tariqa) system had not yet crystallized.

## Sufism and the Hadith Folk

The early Sufis were "closely associated with the Hadith folk" (Hodgson) and found the intellectual Mu'tazilis "uncongenial." This association was not incidental: both movements were rooted in the personal, immediate encounter with the Qur'an and with the divine presence they believed its words conveyed. The Hadith folk's insistence that God's own speech was present on the tongue of the Qur'an reciter — "not Creator but not created" — provided a theological basis for the Sufi experience of divine nearness. Both movements rejected mediation through priests, sacraments, or saints in the Catholic sense; both insisted on the direct confrontation of the individual soul with God.

At the same time, the Sufis went beyond the Hadith folk's textualism. Where the Hadith folk "wanted to hear [God] directly" through the literal words of the Qur'an, the Sufis hoped "in some measure to repeat in their own lives something of the experiences which presumably Muhammad must have gone through in receiving the various portions of the words of God" (Hodgson). This experiential aspiration could not be reduced to isnad criticism or fiqh.

## Sufism and the Shi'i Batiniyyah

Within the [[shia-islam|Shi'i]] milieu, the esoteric Batiniyyah movements (especially the Isma'ilis) occupied a structurally parallel role to that of Sufism among Jama'i-Sunnis: "In some ways, but not all, the Sufis represented in a Jama'i-Sunni milieu what Batini piety represented in a Shi'i milieu" (Hodgson). Both movements stressed inward depth beyond the external Shari'ah. But Sufi esotericism was "personal and experiential" rather than historical and conspiratorial; the Sufis explored "hidden meanings and personal resonances not allowed for by the soberly public Shari'ah," while the Batinis sought a privileged vision of history encoded in the Qur'an's inner meaning.

## Social Conditions

Early Sufism was concentrated in the cities — Kufah, Basra, Baghdad, and the Khurasan towns. "Islam under the 'Abbasids came to include a general cross-section of society and no longer merely a ruling minority; concurrently, the mysticism which had long been a part of life between Nile and Oxus, particularly among Christians, began to flower among Muslims" (Hodgson). The broad social base of the 'Abbasid Muslim community, including converts from Christian and Zoroastrian traditions with their own mystical resources, was a critical enabling condition.

## End Conditions

The process described here — the formation of early Sufism as a recognizable movement — concludes by c. 945 CE with the disintegration of the High Caliphal order. The Sufi movement thereafter entered its classical phase of order formation (the tariqa system), hagiographical literature, and theoretical synthesis. The works of figures like al-Junayd of Baghdad (d. 910) and later al-Ghazali (d. 1111) bridged early and classical Sufism.

## Historiography

**Source quality**: Hodgson's treatment is analytically sophisticated but limited in detail for individual Sufi figures. The collection's coverage of early Islamic mysticism is thin overall; specialist works on early Sufism (Massignon's *La Passion d'al-Hallaj*, Sviri on the early period) are not ingested.

**Scholarly debates**: The question of non-Islamic influences on early Sufism — Christian, Neoplatonist, Buddhist — has been extensively debated. Hodgson acknowledges Christian influence without overstating it; more recent scholarship (Lewinstein, Melchert) has emphasized the indigenous Islamic roots in Qur'anic meditation and the Hadith folk tradition.

**Collection coverage**: Weak for this specific topic. Hodgson provides structural analysis; the depth of individual Sufi biography and doctrine requires supplementary sources not yet ingested.
