# The Indian Independence Movement

The **Indian independence movement** was the long campaign — from elite petitioning to Gandhian mass
struggle — that ended the [[british-raj|British Raj]] and won independence (and
[[partition-of-india-1947|Partition]]) in 1947. Documented for the wiki through Percival Spear's
[[spear-history-of-india-v2-1965|*A History of India*, Vol. II]] (1965).

## Phases

Spear traces the movement through distinct phases:
1. **Cultural-intellectual roots (19th c.).** The English-educated class created by the post-1857 Raj,
   energised by the religious renaissance (Ram Mohan Roy and the **Brahmo Samaj**, the **Arya Samaj**,
   Vivekananda), which gave Indians confidence to reclaim their civilisation.
2. **Moderate Congress (1885–1905).** The **[[indian-national-congress|Indian National Congress]]** as a
   loyal, petitioning body seeking a larger Indian share in administration (Gokhale's liberalism).
3. **The extremist turn (1905–1919).** **Curzon's Partition of Bengal (1905)** and the **Swadeshi**
   boycott radicalised politics; **Tilak** gave nationalism a popular, Hindu-revivalist, direct-action
   idiom; the Muslim League (1906) and separate electorates planted the communal division.
4. **The Gandhian mass movement (1919–1947).** **[[mahatma-gandhi|Gandhi]]** transformed the Congress
   into a mass organisation with a **peasant base** and the method of **satyagraha** — Non-cooperation
   (1920–22), Civil Disobedience and the **Salt March (1930)**, and Quit India (1942).
5. **Endgame and Partition (1942–1947).** [[muhammad-ali-jinnah|Jinnah's]] Muslim League and the
   two-nation demand, the Cabinet Mission's failure, communal civil war, and the Mountbatten settlement
   — **independence with Partition** on 15 August 1947.

## Significance

The movement is one of the modern world's great **anti-colonial struggles** and the model of mass
**non-violent resistance**. Spear's framing stresses its **irony and continuity**: the very
English-educated class and liberal-constitutional ideas the Raj fostered turned against it, while the
movement inherited and reworked the Raj's (and the Mughals') unifying imperial framework into a modern
nation-state. Its tragedy was that the unity it achieved came **fractured by Partition** and the
communal violence that accompanied it.
