## Definition and Scope

The **Medieval Agricultural Revolution** is the sustained expansion of European
agricultural output and rural population between roughly 1000 and 1300, which
[[luscombe-ncmh-v4-2004|NCMH IV]] (Robert Fossier, Ch. 2) and an older economic
literature call the **"take-off" of European history** — the moment when "the
soldiers, priests, farmers and merchants of the west," previously confined to the
Carolingian heartland, spilled outward to northern Scandinavia, the Slav lands,
southern Spain, Sicily and the Near East. It is the demographic and productive
foundation on which the [[commercial-revolution|commercial revolution]], the
[[urbanization|urban revival]], the [[crusades|Crusades]], and the cultural
flowering of the [[high-middle-ages|High Middle Ages]] all rest. Fossier dates the
decisive acceleration to **1050–1080** in central France and northern Spain, later
(1110–1120) in England and the Rhineland, and only to the mid-twelfth century in
Brittany and much of Germany.

## Causal Drivers

- **The "human flood."** The population of western Europe **more than doubled**
  between 1000 and 1300, and locally quadrupled — a burst of growth not repeated
  until the late eighteenth century. Estimates run from c. 40–55 million toward
  60–65 million across a century and a half: the British Isles from ~1.5 to over 2
  million, France from ~6 to ~9 million, Germany and Italy from ~5 to ~7 million.
  Fossier attributes this above all to a "natalist" marriage pattern (5–7 surviving
  boys per fertile couple), working against still-devastating infant mortality
  (25–30% under age two) and a large celibate/sterile share.
- **The single great source, [[domesday-book|Domesday Book]] (1086).** William the
  Conqueror's inquest — 276,000 informants on ~1.5 million subjects — is unmatched in
  Europe before the fourteenth century and anchors the demographic argument.
- **Land clearance (assarting) and colonisation.** New villages, reclaimed forest,
  marsh and (in the Low Countries) sea, and the eastward movement onto Slav lands
  (the *Ostsiedlung*) expanded the cultivated area enormously.
- **Technical change.** The spread of the heavy mouldboard plough, the three-field
  rotation, the horse-collar and shoulder harness, watermills and (later) windmills,
  and improved iron tools raised yields — though Fossier stresses production grew
  more sluggishly than population, leaving recurrent local famine (1095, 1125, 1144,
  1160, 1172) and a last general dearth in 1195–7.

## Major Phases

1. **Recovery and acceleration (c. 950–1080).** Growth discernible from the tenth
   century gathers pace after 1040–60 in the most advanced regions.
2. **High expansion (c. 1080–1180).** The "age of great progress": new towns, fairs,
   clearances, the Cistercian grange economy, and a measurable improvement in diet
   (a shift toward animal protein — "the twelfth century was weighed down by sides of
   beef") and in longevity (the powerful routinely reaching 50–55; the abbots of
   Cluny past 80).
3. **Approaching the ceiling (c. 1180–1300).** Continued growth but with mounting
   strain between population and food supply — the prelude to the fourteenth-century
   crisis that lies beyond this volume.

## Interaction

The revolution is inseparable from the [[commercial-revolution]] and
[[urbanization|urban revival]] (surplus population and food fed the towns), from the
consolidation of seigneurial lordship (see [[feudalism]], [[bipartite-manor]]), and
from the outward [[reconquista|Reconquista]], crusading and colonising frontier
movements that exported surplus warriors and settlers. It is the material substrate
of the whole period: Fossier's "take-off" is what made possible the cathedrals, the
universities, and the wars.

## End Conditions

The process did not end within this volume's window; it ran into the demographic
ceiling and climatic deterioration of the early fourteenth century, when famine
(1315–22) and the [[black-death|Black Death]] (1347–) reversed three centuries of
growth — events treated in the [[late-middle-ages|Late Middle Ages]].
