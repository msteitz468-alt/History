# Soviet Collectivization
## Definition and Scope
**Soviet Collectivization** was the rapid and coercive consolidation of millions of individual peasant farms into large, state-controlled agricultural units: collective farms (**kolkhozes**) and state farms (**sovkhozes**). Launched by Joseph Stalin in late 1929 alongside the First Five-Year Plan, this process was designed to integrate the peasantry into the socialist state, modernize farming through mechanization, and, most importantly, extract grain and labor to fund the rapid industrialization of the Soviet Union. As described by Robert Service, the Politburo majority came to the opinion that compulsory collectivization should be initiated (although the fiction was maintained in public that coercion would not be used). By January 1930 the Politburo was insisting that a quarter of the sown area should be held by collective farms within two years. An agricultural revolution was heralded.

## Causal Drivers
- **Financing Industrialization:** The Soviet state needed a reliable and cheap source of grain to feed the growing cities, export for foreign currency, and obtain capital for industrial investments. Stalin was still more delighted with the record of industry. From collectivization he acquired a reservoir of terrified peasants who would supply him with cheap industrial labour. To some extent, too, he secured his ability to export Soviet raw materials in order to pay for imports of industrial machinery.
- **Ideological Control:** Individual farming was viewed by the Bolsheviks as inherently bourgeois, capitalistic, and counterrevolutionary. Collectivization was intended to socialize the countryside. Stalin the Man of Steel boasted that he had introduced ‘socialism’ to the villages.
- **Grain Procurement Crisis:** In the late 1920s, peasants refused to sell grain at the low prices set by the government. After two successive winters of grain seizures, the peasants would not voluntarily maintain their sown area. Stalin could draw up a balance sheet that, from his standpoint, was favourable: after collectivization it was the countryside, not the towns, which went hungry if the harvest was bad. State’s grain collections rose from 10.8 million tons in 1928–9 to 22.8 million tons in 1931–2 (Service, lines 1472, 1456).

## Major Phases
- **The "Great Turn" and Dekulakization (1929–1930):** Stalin ordered all-out collectivization. Among Stalin’s several motives in supporting the international turn to the left was a wish to cause maximum discomfort to Bukharin (line 1452). In spring 1929 Stalin... appointed a Central Committee commission under N. Yanson to explore opportunities for convicts to work on projects in the USSR’s less hospitable regions... Among the first results was the formation of the ‘Dalstroi’ trust in the Far East which ran the notorious gold mines of Kolyma (lines 1454). To Molotov was entrusted the job of explaining this to the Central Committee in November 1929. Bukharin was sacked from the Politburo at the same meeting. Kulaks were to be disbarred from joining collective farms and divided into three categories. Those in category one were to be dispatched to forced-labour settlements or shot. Category two... to distant provinces. Category three... allowed to stay... on a smaller patch of land. Between five and seven million persons were treated as belonging to kulak families (line 1460). About 25,000 of them [‘25,000-ers’] rallied to the Politburo’s summons... The central party apparatus imaginatively introduced a special category of ‘sub-kulaks’ who were poor but yet opposed the government. Sub-kulaks were to be treated as if they were kulaks (lines 1462, 1464).
- **Peasant Resistance and "Dizzy with Success" (1930):** Peasant resistance was fierce... 700,000 peasants were involved in disturbances at the beginning of 1930. With monumental insincerity he wrote an article for Pravda in March 1930, ‘Dizzy with Success’, in which he called local functionaries to task for abusing their authority (line 1464). But this was a temporizing posture. For Stalin, the priority remained mass collectivization. By the time of the harvest of 1931, collective farms held practically all the land traditionally given over to cereal crops.
- **Famine and Consolidation (1931–1933+):** The price was awful. Probably four to five million people perished in 1932–3 from ‘de-kulakization’ and from grain seizures. The dead and the dying were piled on to carts by the urban detachments and pitched into common graves without further ceremony. Pits were dug on the outskirts of villages... Child survivors, their stomachs swollen through hunger, gnawed grass and tree-bark (line 1466). While the government’s policies were killing peasants, peasants were killing their livestock... Even some of Stalin’s colleagues blanched... For instance, Ordzhonikidze was aghast at the behaviour of officials in eastern Ukraine (lines 1466). With the exception of 1930, mass collectivization meant that not until the mid-1950s did agriculture regain the level of output achieved in the last years before the Great War (line 1470). The state had to pump additional resources... Machine-tractor stations had to be built from 1929... No major state has inflicted such grievous economic damage on itself in peacetime (line 1470).
- **Kolkhoz vs Sovkhoz Definition:** The idea of peasants taking most of their own decisions was anathema to Stalin. The government, he insisted, should own the land, appoint the farm chairmen and set the grain-delivery quotas. His ideal organization was the sovkhoz... Yet Stalin recognized that most peasants were ill-disposed to becoming wage labourers, and he yielded to the extent of permitting most farms to be of the kolkhoz type. In a kolkhoz the members were rewarded by results... each peasant was paid a fraction of the farm wage-fund strictly in accordance with the number of ‘labour days’... And so the kolkhoz was defined as occupying a lower level of socialist attainment than the sovkhoz (lines 1478).

## Geographic Spread
The process covered all major agricultural regions of the USSR, encountering the strongest resistance in Ukraine, the Don and Kuban cossack lands, and the North Caucasus, where individual peasant landownership had deep historical roots. Resistance was confined to a particular village or group of villages. Fewer large revolts broke out in Russia than in areas where non-Russians were in the majority: Kazakhstan, the North Caucasus, Ukraine and parts of Siberia (Service, line 1482).

## Interaction
- **With the MTS:** The Machine Tractor Stations had to be built from 1929 to provide equipment and personnel for the introduction of technology (as well as to provide yet another agency to control the peasantry).
- **With Private Plots and Rural Society:** In reality, most kolkhozniki... could no more make a profit in the early 1930s than fly to Mars. An entire way of life, too, was being pummelled out of existence. The peasant household was no longer the basic social unit recognized by the authorities. Grain quotas were imposed on the collective farm as a whole, and peasants were given their instructions as individuals rather than as members of households (line 1484).
- **With Industrial Labor Supply:** Cheap labour was made available by peasants fleeing the villages. They came for work and for ration-cards, and their arrival permitted a lowering of the wages of labourers (line 1454).

## End Conditions
Collectivization established the permanent structure of Soviet agriculture, replacing 25 million individual farms with slightly less than 250,000 kolkhozes. The system persisted until the collapse of the USSR, although Nikita Khrushchev disbanded the MTS in 1958 and allowed collective farms to buy the machinery directly. Despite the massive social and human costs (millions of deaths), the system never achieved satisfactory agricultural productivity, forcing the Soviet Union to repeatedly buy grain from the West in its later decades. Stalin and the Politburo had won the agrarian war (line 1464), but at the cost of conditions in the countryside so dire that the state had to pump additional resources... to maintain the new agrarian order (line 1470).
