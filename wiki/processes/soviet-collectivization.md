# Soviet Collectivization
## Definition and Scope
**Soviet Collectivization** was the rapid and coercive consolidation of millions of individual peasant farms into large, state-controlled agricultural units: collective farms (**kolkhozes**) and state farms (**sovkhozes**). Launched by Joseph Stalin in late 1929 alongside the First Five-Year Plan, this process was designed to integrate the peasantry into the socialist state, modernize farming through mechanization, and, most importantly, extract grain and labor to fund the rapid industrialization of the Soviet Union.

## Causal Drivers
- **Financing Industrialization:** The Soviet state needed a reliable and cheap source of grain to feed the growing cities, export for foreign currency, and obtain capital for industrial investments.
- **Ideological Control:** Individual farming was viewed by the Bolsheviks as inherently bourgeois, capitalistic, and counterrevolutionary. Collectivization was intended to socialize the countryside.
- **Grain Procurement Crisis:** In the late 1920s, peasants refused to sell grain at the low prices set by the government (the "scissors crisis"), threatening the industrialization program.

## Major Phases
- **The "Great Turn" and Dekulakization (1929–1930):** Stalin ordered all-out collectivization. Tens of thousands of urban worker activists (the "twenty-five thousanders") were sent to the countryside to establish kolkhozes. The state declared the "liquidation of the kulaks as a class," leading to the arrest, execution, or deportation of about five million people.
- **Peasant Resistance and "Dizzy with Success" (1930):** Peasant resistance was fierce, manifesting in armed revolts, grain hoarding, and the mass slaughter of livestock (the number of horses fell from 34 million to 16.6 million, and cattle from 68 million to 38 million; Line 5949). In March 1930, Stalin temporarily retreated, writing the article "Dizzy with Success," which blamed local cadres and allowed peasants to leave the collectives temporarily.
- **Re-collectivization and Famine (1931–1933):** The government resumed pressure using tax exemptions and incentives. Famine struck bounteous regions like Ukraine, the Kuban, and Kazakhstan due to droughts and excessive grain requisitions. By the end of 1932, over 14 million peasant households had been collectivized.
- **Consolidation (1933–1937):** The Second Five-Year Plan completed the process. The state established the **Machine Tractor Stations (MTS)** to provide mechanized harvesting machinery and maintain political control over the collectives.

## Geographic Spread
The process covered all major agricultural regions of the USSR, encountering the strongest resistance in Ukraine, the Don and Kuban cossack lands, and the North Caucasus, where individual peasant landownership had deep historical roots.

## Interaction
- **With the MTS:** The Machine Tractor Stations held a state monopoly on heavy machinery (tractors, combines) and served as political police organs in the countryside, monitoring kolkhoz compliance.
- **With Private Plots:** To appease the peasantry, Stalin permitted each collective farmer to maintain a small private plot of land (around half an acre) and a limited number of livestock. These private plots proved to be highly productive, supplying a large percentage of the country's vegetables, eggs, and milk.

## End Conditions
Collectivization established the permanent structure of Soviet agriculture, replacing 25 million individual farms with slightly less than 250,000 kolkhozes. The system persisted until the collapse of the USSR, although Nikita Khrushchev disbanded the MTS in 1958 and allowed collective farms to buy the machinery directly. Despite the massive social and human costs (millions of deaths), the system never achieved satisfactory agricultural productivity, forcing the Soviet Union to repeatedly buy grain from the West in its later decades.
