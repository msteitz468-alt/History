# Raskol (Schism)

## Definition and Scope
The **Raskol** (Schism) refers to the mid-17th century rupture within the Russian Orthodox Church, triggered by Patriarch Nikon's liturgical and textual reforms (L2458-2460, L2648-2650). It resulted in the emergence of the **Old Believers** (*starovery* or *staroobriadtsy*), who rejected the revised books and rituals and broke away from the state-backed Orthodox Church, initiating centuries of religious dissent and state persecution (L2656-2658).

## Causal Drivers
* **Accumulated Textual Errors:** Over centuries, transcription errors had crept into Russian liturgical texts and practices (L2648-2650). Visiting Greek and Ukrainian church scholars urged Moscow to correct these discrepancies to align with the wider Orthodox world (L2648-2650).
* **Nikon's Authoritarian Reforms:** Upon becoming patriarch in 1652, Nikon pushed reforms with extreme zeal and rigidity, demanding absolute conformity to contemporary Greek practices and refusing to tolerate local variations (L2650, L2666-2670).
* **Church-State Alliance:** The state, seeking to integrate newly acquired Ukrainian territories (which followed Greek-aligned texts) and harboring ambitions of leadership over the Balkan Orthodox, threw its absolute weight behind Nikon's corrections (L2664-2666).

## Major Phases
1. **Introduction of Reforms (1652–1653):** Nikon introduced the corrected books and changed the sign of the cross from two fingers to three (L2650, L2654). Leaders of a previous religious revival, including Archpriest Avvakum, denounced Nikon as a heretic (L2650).
2. **Ecclesiastical Councils (1654–1656):** Russian and Greek-endorsed councils approved the textual corrections, and Mount Athos manuscripts were brought to Moscow for collation (L2650-2652).
3. **The Great Moscow Councils (1666–1667):** Attended by the patriarchs of Alexandria and Antioch, these councils resolved the political dispute by defrocking Nikon for his political papist ambitions, but fully validated his liturgical reforms and excommunicated all who refused to accept them, finalizing the Schism (L2462-2464, L2654-2656).
4. **Active Persecution and Resistance (Late 17th Century):** The state enforced the council's decrees with violence. The Solovetskii Monastery in the north resisted in a siege from 1668 to 1676 (L2656-2658). Dissenters embraced apocalyptic views, leading to mass self-immolations; over 20,000 burned themselves alive between 1672 and 1691 (L2658). Archpriest Avvakum was burned at the stake in 1682 (L2658).

## Geographic Spread
The Schism was concentrated primarily among the Great Russian population of Muscovy (L2666). The Old Believers fled state persecution, establishing remote communities in the Russian North, the Volga basin, the Urals, Siberia, and southern borderlands (L2656-2658).

## Interaction
* **Socio-Political Dissent:** The Schism was not merely theological; it acted as a channel for socio-economic protest. Dissenters were often well-established peasants and traders protesting against gentry dominance, fiscal centralization, and the loss of local parish autonomy (L2664-2666).
* **Calvinist Parallel:** Denied access to traditional state paths, Old Believers developed strong communal networks and business acumen, playing a prominent role in the development of Russian commerce and industry in the 18th and 19th centuries (L2664-2666).
* **Sectarianism:** Without bishops to ordain new priests, the movement split into the *popovtsy* (who actively enticed priests from the official Church) and the *bespopovtsy* (priestless, who reorganized their religious life without the liturgy), giving rise to various Russian sects (L2658-2662).

## End Conditions
The Schism was never resolved. While Peter the Great replaced violent persecution with double taxation and practical tolerance, the legal divide between the official Orthodox Church and the millions of Old Believers lasted until the Russian Revolution of 1917, and communities of Old Believers persist to this day (L2658-2660, L2980-2982).
