## Definition and Scope

"Pacific first contacts" refers to the extended process — spanning the sixteenth to mid-twentieth centuries — by which European explorers, traders, missionaries, and colonial officials encountered Pacific Islander societies for the first time, and by which those societies encountered and interpreted Europeans. Chapter 4 of [[denoon-cambridge-pacific-islanders-1997]], "Discovering Outsiders," takes the Islander perspective as its primary focus.

The chapter warns against treating first contact as a discrete incident: "'contact' was an extended process rather than a discrete incident … 'First-contact' encounters are so called not because they were certainly the first cross-cultural meetings, but because they occurred in an era of purposeful European exploration by navigators, philosophers and scientists who defined the events as historic." Prior castaways, South-East Asian traders, and inter-island drifting may have produced earlier encounters outside the European documentary record.

## Causal Drivers

**Spanish empire**: Spain extended its conquest from the Americas to the Pacific in 1567 to find "rich islands" — the Spaniards had "in mind the Biblical King Solomon's mines of Ophir." The Treaty of Tordesillas (1494) blocked westward Spanish expansion into the Spice Islands (claimed by Portugal), turning ambitions toward Terra Australis.

**Portuguese trade**: From East Indian bases at Ternate and Tidore, Portuguese navigators explored the westernmost Pacific seeking gold. A 1525 expedition blown to Ulithi in the western Carolines found Islanders who "openly approached the strangers … a people who had never received any harsh treatment."

**Dutch commerce**: From the early seventeenth century, Dutch interests shifted from gold-and-souls to trade and strategic position. "They wanted neither to evangelise nor to colonise islands with scant resources" — resulting in briefer, less interfering contacts than the Spanish, though violent incidents still occurred (LeMaire/Schouten attacked in the Tuamotus and by Tongans of Niuatoputapu).

**English and French science/strategy**: Cook's three voyages (1768–1780), Bougainville (1766–69), Vancouver (1791–95), and La Pérouse (1786–88) combined geographic, botanical, and strategic objectives. These voyages generated the primary documentary record that shaped European conceptions of Islanders.

**Commodity trade**: Whalers from the Atlantic and Sydney sought provisions; sandalwood and bêche-de-mer traders entered Fiji, Vanuatu, New Caledonia, and Hawai'i from the early nineteenth century; missionaries arrived from 1797.

## Major Phases

### Phase 1 — Spanish Encounters (1521–c. 1700)

Magellan reached Guam in 1521. The dominant pattern of early Spanish encounters: "a 'sickening cycle of friendly welcome, misunderstanding, sullen retreats, occasional reconciliations, robberies and killings'" (Oskar Spate). The issue was food — Spanish crews demanded it "insatiably, taxing resources to the limit."

**Mendaña 1567–68** (Solomon Islands): Two ships, c. 100 men. Sighted islands of Tuvalu, then Santa Isabel, Guadalcanal, Florida, New Georgia, San Cristobal — named "Solomon Islands." Initial politeness (exchange of music, name-exchange with a chief) collapsed over food demands. On Guadalcanal, ransacking a village for pigs led to Islanders killing nine Spaniards; Spaniards counter-massacred and mutilated Islander corpses. Expedition departed after five months, famished.

**Mendaña and Quirós 1595** (Marquesas, Santa Cruz): Four ships, 378 people including six friars. First recorded Polynesian-European confrontation. Marquesans welcomed the fleet but "rushed about grabbing everything they could lift" — the commander's lady "fondled the heads of children," the most sacred part of the Polynesian body. Guns fired; within two weeks "about 200 Islanders had been killed." At Santa Cruz, friendly relations disintegrated when "disillusioned colonists began to kill Islanders." Mendaña died; survivors withdrew to Manila.

**Quirós 1605–06** (Tuamotus, Vanuatu): Quirós named a Vanuatu island "La Australia del Espiritu Santo" and attempted his New Jerusalem. "The people resisted from the first moment" — a reconnaissance party was met by a crowd who "drew a line on the ground and indicated that both sides should lay down their arms," but settlers advanced; battle ensued. Quirós withdrew.

**Torres 1606** (Mailu, southern New Guinea): Separated from Quirós, Torres's ship paused at Mailu, performed a perfunctory prayer, and attacked: "Cierra Espana … we gave them a Santiago and in that skirmish some fell dead." Fourteen youngsters were captured, transported to Manila, and baptised. "Spaniards compounded senseless violence by an equally vacuous assumption of sovereignty over an island which they would never again locate."

**Outcome of Spanish Melanesia**: "No doubt the apparent absence of gold, the prevalence of malaria, the belligerence of the people and their resistance to evangelism convinced the Spaniards to look elsewhere." Melanesians were "delivered (by the anopheles mosquito) from a particularly rapacious era of colonialism."

**Marianas (Guam from 1668)**: Spanish Jesuit missionaries allied with soldiers and Chamorro chiefs created an "oppressive hierarchy, which then provoked wars between sections of Chamorro and Spanish soldiers throughout the 1670s and 1680s." By 1695, resistance was crushed; population "greatly reduced by 'pacification' campaigns, resettlement from scattered settlements to crowded villages where they were vulnerable to new infections — including smallpox."

### Phase 2 — Dutch and English Encounters (1615–1767)

LeMaire and Schouten (1615–16): Tuamotus, northern Tonga — LeMaire offended Tongans by refusing kava. Abel Tasman (1642): First European contact with New Zealand (Taitapu/Golden Bay). Maori aboard canoes "probably some of their bravest warriors" challenged with incantations and shell trumpets. Dutch cannon fire drove them off; the next morning a Maori canoe rammed a Dutch boat, killing four. Tasman "was welcomed by Tongans on Tongatapu," where "no one carried weapons." Jacob Roggeveen (1722): Civil first contact with western Samoans, then his men were ambushed at Tutuila — apparently "acts of revenge for some earlier insult."

### Phase 3 — Enlightenment Voyages and Tahiti (1767–1800)

Samuel Wallis (1767) at Matavai Bay, [[tahiti-society-islands]]: greeted by "a hundred canoes." Tahitians boarded and helped themselves to everything — understood by Howe as conforming to Islander protocol for receiving castaways (visitors surrendered possessions as acknowledgment of hosts' authority). Wallis's crew repelled them with gunfire. After cannon fire caused many deaths, "Tahitian chiefs, intimidated by British firepower, had discovered an effective way of placating the strangers … some women of low birth were ordered to prostitute themselves as a political strategy."

Bougainville (1766–69): His "rhapsodic depiction of an island paradise" and "Tahiti" as the South Seas paradise entered European philosophical discourse, influencing ideas about "the condition of humankind in a 'state of nature'." Bougainville likened Tahitians to Greek gods.

Cook's three voyages (1768–80): See detailed treatment under the Cook-as-Lono controversy below.

### Phase 4 — Material Relations and Beachcombers (c. 1800–1870)

Whalers, sandalwood and bêche-de-mer traders created sustained contact. "The veneration or fear with which Europeans were first greeted was soon displaced by hard-headed attitudes based on the calculations of material advantage." Islanders "bargained acutely, being eager for trade and foreign goods." Chiefs recruited outsiders — mainly sailors who had jumped ship, escaped convicts, and some educated men — as firearms specialists and technology brokers. "Foreigners who chose to live among Islanders or trade with them did so on terms dictated by Islanders, and at their own risk." British and US warships began patrolling from the 1830s; the Wilkes expedition (1839–40) sought legal frameworks to protect American commercial interests — "the first step towards changing the balance of power."

### Phase 5 — Twentieth-Century First Contacts in New Guinea (1930s–1960s)

The New Guinea Highlands were penetrated by Australian patrols only in the 1930s. The Hides-O'Malley expedition (1935) traversed 1,800 kilometres of interior country; "about fifty people were shot, fending off aggression, real or perceived." The Leahy brothers (gold prospectors, 1933) filmed Highlanders' responses for Australian audiences. Dutch scientific expeditions (1907–13) measured skulls, prompting "the older men to tremble so violently during the process that they were hardly capable of remaining on their feet." Last uncontacted populations in Irian Jaya/West New Guinea remained outside administration when the Dutch departed in 1962. These late contacts allow reconstruction of Islander perceptions through oral testimony, unavailable for sixteenth-century encounters.

## Islander Perceptions and Cosmological Frameworks

The chapter's most original contribution is its reconstruction of how Islanders made sense of Europeans within their existing categories:

**Samoans and Tongans** called the strangers papalagi — "sky bursters": "when the strange ships sailed across the horizon, their utter unfamiliarity caused Islanders to suppose that they must have burst through the dome of heaven."

**New Guinea Highlands (1930)**: Gold prospectors in the Goroka valley were identified with recently dead ancestors; villagers "named one of our people who had died before" for each stranger.

**Gorohonota villager (Gopie)**: Recognised a New Guinean carrier as his dead cousin "because half his finger was missing" — exactly as it had been cut in punishment when alive.

**Huli people (1935)**: Classified the Hides-O'Malley expedition as dama spirits. "The most frightening feature of the group was the two creatures who stood at the front. Their skins were so pale they seemed to glow." One man hid his weapons — "Dama should not be provoked."

**Onabasulu (New Guinea)**: Had already received steel tools via trading networks. Feared these were things of "Malaiya [a legendary Origin place] … things that should not be touched or used by mortal men." A leader proposed gathering all axes and bushknives to return to their "parents" when the patrol arrived, lest the owners come looking.

**Wopkaimin (1957)**: Reached by kiap (patrol officer) accompanied by a Telefolmin interpreter who ordered construction of a rest house, demanded food, and killed the kamokim's dog. Men secretly armed and surrounded the village but held back because of the Telefolmin precedent of harsh government retaliation.

**Maori (1769, Whitianga)**: Horeta Te Taniwha described Cook's crew as tupua — "visible beings or objects of supernatural origin, regarded with a mixture of terror and awe." Children and women fled initially; warriors stayed. The Maori elder correctly decoded Cook's charcoal map-sketch as a request for the outline of the land. "In every corner of the earth there are wayside shrines to Captain Cook" — but whether Hawaiians saw him in the same light is the subject of a major scholarly controversy (see below).

**Tahitians** incorporated foreign symbols of authority. The feather girdle of Pomare contained a British red pennant from Wallis's 1767 act of possession — "the Tahitians had taken down the symbol of English sovereignty and incorporated it into a symbol of sovereignty of their own." Skinner the barber's auburn hair was sewn into the girdle because red was tapu and he "had a special power to touch tapu places."

## The Cook-as-Lono Controversy

Cook's identity as the god Lono (Hawaiian makahiki festival deity) is the defining controversy of Pacific ethnohistory. The chapter summarises three positions:

**Sahlins/Daws**: Cook's two arrivals (1778, 1779) both coincided with the makahiki festival. "Cook came from the sea, as Lono had promised he would, and Cook's ships had tall masts and white sails, shaped very like the upright sticks and swaths of kapa cloth that were carried in the makahiki procession." His death at Kealakekua in 1779 followed the climactic phase of the festival in which Lono's warriors ritually challenge the ruling chief's warriors — a predictable outcome given prevailing beliefs.

**Howard and Borofsky**: Cook's "reluctance to make liberal use of firearms contributed to his death. A less humanistic man, a person more concerned in demonstrating Western weaponry, might have left the island alive, independent of whatever ritual identity Hawaiians sought to place on him."

**Obeyesekere**: Hawaiians "could not have mistaken Cook for a god, in view of the many human failings exhibited by him and his crew," and his apotheosis was "an intrinsic element of the justification of European imperialism." Hawaiian scholar Kame'eleihiwa endorsed Obeyesekere's critique politically: "The best part about Cook's visit is that we killed him, as the mana of his death accrues to us."

**Sahlins's response** (*How 'Natives' Think*): Trenchant critique of "bourgeois practical rationality" applied retrospectively to eighteenth-century Hawaiian world-views. The controversy remains open; resolution_status: open.

## "Fatal Impact" vs. Islander Agency

The chapter frames a historiographical debate between two poles:

**Fatal impact thesis** (Moorehead): Europeans arrogantly brought disease, violence, and destruction; Islanders were passive victims. A secularised social Darwinism underlying this view assumed that "native cultures — and the natives themselves — must inevitably perish in the path of Western culture and technology."

**Dening's sophisticated version**: Focuses on the Marquesas, where "the culture and most of the populace were obliterated between 1774 and 1880." "There was no conscious conspiracy … the discrepancy between cost and consequence in 'a life for a nail' in Cook's day was constant." The beach as "a savage place, made so by the mutual contempt of those who stood across it from one another."

**Denoon's counter-emphasis on Islander agency**: "This was seldom the case. Some Islanders (assisted by the anopheles mosquito and rugged terrain) terrified and repelled foreigners." Niueans "sent men aboard visiting ships with their faces blackened, their bodies smeared with ash … shouting and gesticulating wildly" — bringing speedy departures. Tonga's Taufa'ahau Tupou I "used the ideological confusion and political instability of the era to conquer the archipelago and establish himself as its monarch." "In most Island groups the populace not only survived, but responded in markedly different ways, and elaborated their cultures into the robust and unique forms which they retain to the present day."

The fundamental structural contrast: "In Island societies trade — the mundane exchange of useful commodities — always took place in the guise of social relations … This trade was never conducted in the impersonal terms of the marketplace." European commercial exchange did "not require kinship, nor fraternal partnerships between men, nor did their material interests require ceremonies of affinity and friendship. These radically different understandings of how material interests should be mediated underlay the bizarre and tragic character of many early encounters."
