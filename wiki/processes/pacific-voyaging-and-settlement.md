# Pacific Voyaging and the Settlement of Remote Oceania

## Definition and Scope

This page tracks the **maritime colonization of the Pacific** — "the last great chapter in the
human colonisation of the globe" — from the Lapita expansion into Near and Remote Oceania to
the final settlement of New Zealand, as set out in [[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|CWH IV]] (McNiven,
Ch 22). For nearly all of human history **Remote Oceania** (12,600 km east–west from Palau to
Rapa Nui) was **empty of people**; its peopling in barely three millennia is the volume's
clearest case of history made by **voyaging and inter-community networks** rather than empire.
It complements the agricultural/linguistic account in
[[early-agriculture-southeast-asia-pacific]] and the debates in [[austronesian-dispersal]].

## The Lapita Cultural Complex

Around **3,500 years ago** (c. 1500 BCE), systems of exchange on the coastal lowlands of
northeast New Guinea intensified with the emergence of **Lapita** maritime peoples —
identified archaeologically by **finely dentate-stamped earthenware pottery**, and linked to
the movement of **Austronesian-speakers** into the Bismarck Archipelago from island Southeast
Asia. Lapita peoples then voyaged into the previously uninhabited islands of **Remote
Oceania**: the eastern Solomons (~3,200 BP), Vanuatu, New Caledonia, and Fiji (~3,000 BP), and
on to **Tonga (~2,900 BP)** and **Samoa (~2,700 BP)**. They carried a "transported landscape"
— pig, dog, chicken, the Pacific rat, and at least fifteen plant species (taro, yams,
bananas) — plus obsidian tools, shell adzes, and fishhooks. Obsidian sourcing reveals the
staggering reach of these networks: Bismarck obsidian travelled to Fiji (3,300 km) and as far
as **Borneo (3,500 km west)**, a span of nearly 7,000 km, demonstrating sustained connection
between colonizers and homeland communities.

## Polynesian Voyaging

After settling Tonga and Samoa (the ancestral homeland **Hawaiki**) for some 1,500 years,
descendants of the Lapita peoples — now **aceramic** — launched the colonization of East
Polynesia in **purpose-built double-hulled canoes**. A recent radiocarbon synthesis dates this
dispersal late and fast: the **Society Islands ~1000–900 BP**, then the "near-instantaneous"
settlement of the **Marquesas, Hawaii, and Rapa Nui ~800–700 BP**, and finally **New Zealand
(Aotearoa) by ~700 BP** — the Maori landing, in McNiven's phrase, "perhaps the final great act
of human global colonisation." Geochemical sourcing of basalt **adzes** moved 1,400–4,000 km
between archipelagos confirms **inter-archipelago interaction spheres** down to ~500 BP, after
which island communities became more self-contained. Sweet potato in East Polynesia and
Polynesian chickens in Chile hint at voyages reaching **South America** (see
[[pre-columbian-population-debate]]).

## Ecological Transformation

Colonization remade island ecosystems. The introduced **Pacific rat** and human hunting and
**landscape-firing** drove the extinction of perhaps **2,000 bird species** (~20% of the
world's birds). In New Zealand, ancestral Maori extinguished all **moa** species within ~300
years and burned nearly half the forests to grassland; on **[[rapa-nui|Rapa Nui]]** some twenty
tree species vanished. CWH IV cautions (with Hunt & Lipo) that the link between deforestation
and **societal collapse** has been "overstated," and the impact of European contact
underestimated.

## Interaction and Significance

The Pacific networks were sustained by **skilled leaders** who managed voyaging, ceremonial
exchange, and distant kinship — the basis of status and ranking that later elaborated into the
hereditary **[[polynesian-chiefdoms|chiefdoms and archaic states]]** of Tonga, Hawaii, and
elsewhere. The process exemplifies CWH IV's thesis that **connection across distance**, not
territorial empire, was a primary engine of world history — here achieved entirely by
indigenous **seafaring**.
