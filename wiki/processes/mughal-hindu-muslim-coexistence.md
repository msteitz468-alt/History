## Definition and Scope

This page covers the structural process by which Muslim ruling elites and Hindu populations (primarily upper-caste administrators, Rajput warriors, and merchant communities) developed shared institutional, cultural, and to some degree spiritual frameworks within the Mughal empire, 1526–1707. It is distinct from:

- **Mere tolerance** (passive non-persecution), which predated Akbar and was never complete
- **Syncretism** (blending of doctrines), which occurred in specific intellectual and devotional circles but did not characterize the population as a whole
- **Assimilation** (Hinduization or Islamicization of the other), which was explicitly resisted by both communities

The process was driven primarily from the top down — from the court — and functioned most robustly among the thin privileged stratum of nobles, officials, and artists. As Hodgson observes, "the genteel courtly universalism, satisfactory for allowing privileged groups of diverse traditions and persuasions to share in a common brilliant culture, seems to have done little for humane relations beyond the thin layers of the highly privileged classes."

## Causal Drivers

### Administrative necessity
The sheer scale of a subcontinent-wide empire made reliance on Muslim administrators alone impossible. Under [[akbar]], Hindus (especially Rajputs and the clerical Kayastha caste) rose to high fiscal, administrative, and military positions. The mansabdari system — a graded hierarchy of ranked officers — was deliberately multi-confessional: "though Akbar's empire was Muslim in its foundation and in the ultimate locus of its power, yet Hindus and Muslims co-operated effectively in its actual management, and jointly reaped its benefits in wealth and splendour."

### Persian as neutral medium
Persian, the language of Islamicate high culture, served as a trans-religious medium of civilization. Many Hindus, particularly of clerical backgrounds, read Persian fluently; Muslims and Hindus shared "the same arts and learning" in Persian-medium poetry, painting, and architecture. This created a cultural space that was Islamicate in form but not exclusively Muslim in participation.

### Rajput military integration
The alliance between the Mughal throne and most Rajput ruling houses — cemented by intermarriage (on what Hodgson calls "an unprecedentedly voluntary basis") — gave the Rajputs a structural stake in Mughal power. The later Mughals were "chiefly of Rajput ancestry save in the direct male line." This integration made Hindu and Muslim aristocracies not merely coexisting but genealogically entangled.

### Sufi universalism
The Chishti Sufi order, noted for "its relatively universalist tendencies," had deep roots in India. Sufi pirs sometimes accepted Hindu disciples, and Sufi shrines attracted non-Muslim visitors. The devotional Bhakti movements among Hindus (Kabir, Mirabai, Tukaram) shared enough structural similarity with Sufi *tawhid* to enable a common discourse of inward piety across confessional lines.

### Pre-existing Indian pluralism
India's unusually strong tradition of religious diversity — maintained through caste insulation but always presenting "the potentiality, to alert minds, for mutual confrontation and even understanding" — provided fertile ground. Jains, Zoroastrians, Christians, and multiple Islamic sects had already established recognized niches within Indian society before Akbar.

## Major Phases

### Phase 1 (1526–1556): Foundation under Babur and Humayun
[[babur]] established Timurid power while inheriting the Afghan-Hindu administrative accommodation of the Delhi Sultanate. [[mughal-empire]] foundational period, with interconfessional administration already practiced at a basic level but without explicit policy.

### Phase 2 (1556–1605): Akbar's systematic construction
The most creative and deliberate phase. See [[akbar-religious-policy]] for detailed treatment. Key developments:
- Abolition of jizyah tax on Hindus (1562–63); abolition of Hindu pilgrimage taxes
- Integration of Rajput chieftains through military alliance and intermarriage
- Institution of the *ibadat-khana* (house of worship) from 1575
- Formalization of the *tawhid-e ilahi* / [[din-i-ilahi]] circle c.1582
- Legal declaration (1579) allowing the emperor to adjudicate between differing *fiqh* opinions
- Abolition of the death penalty for apostasy from Islam
- Promotion of Sanskrit-to-Persian translation projects at court
- Construction of Fatehpur Sikri: architectural synthesis of Hindu and Islamicate forms

### Phase 3 (1605–1658): Maintenance under Jahangir and Shah Jahan
Both successors maintained the broad framework while modifying details. Jahangir "maintained, on the whole, his father's religious and cultural policy, though with distinctly more caution." Shah Jahan "maintained the same tradition on the whole, both politically and culturally," while showing "marked leanings toward a more severe Shari'ah-mindedness in expression" — encouraging conversions and destroying some temples while continuing Hindu patronage at court. The Taj Mahal, built under Shah Jahan, represents the aesthetic peak of Indo-Islamicate synthesis.

Dara Shikoh (d. 1659), Shah Jahan's favored son, wrote *Majma al-Bahrayn* ("Confluence of Two Seas," 1655) demonstrating that the Upanishads expressed the same truth as Sufi teachings — the most explicit intellectual synthesis of the period.

### Phase 4 (1658–1707): Aurangzeb's systematic reversal
[[aurangzeb]] progressively dismantled the institutional framework of coexistence:
- Reduced attendance at Hindu festivals; replaced Hindu court astrologers with Muslims
- Imposed double taxation on Hindu merchants (versus Muslim rate)
- Forbade Hindu festivals within the army camp
- Reimposed the jizyah tax on Hindu *dhimmis* from 1679, "exacted in a humiliating manner"
- Widespread destruction of Hindu temples "on various pretexts"
- Hostile measures against Shia Muslims and Shia mourning ceremonies

These policies provoked or intensified multiple revolts: the Jat rebellion (c.1669–1689), the Satnami peasant rebellion (1670s), Sikh resistance, and Rajput insurrection. Hodgson's assessment: "Any opportunity to hold together the empire on the basis of common privileged interests, despite economic difficulties, was scuttled; the economic strains were allowed to have their full effect."

## Geographic Spread

The process operated most intensively in the Gangetic plain, centered on Agra, Delhi, and Lahore. It extended to Rajputana through military alliance. It reached the Deccan primarily as a result of Mughal conquest (completed 1686–87) but was less institutionally embedded there. Bengal and Kashmir represented different configurations. The phenomenon was quintessentially north Indian, with the Persianate cultural medium serving as its vehicle.

## Interaction with Other Processes

- **[[sufism]]**: Sufi universalism provided the intellectual and devotional infrastructure for coexistence; conversely, Aurangzeb's alignment with Naqshbandi Mujaddidi Sufism shows that Sufi orders could also drive communalism.
- **[[military-patronage-state]]**: The military-administrative logic of integrating locally powerful groups made Hindu integration structurally necessary.
- **[[islamicate]]**: The broader Islamicate world provided cultural prestige and personnel (Iranian artists, scholars) that shaped court culture, but the Indian adaptation was distinctively pluralistic.
- **[[bhakti-movement]]**: The devotional movements provided a Hindu-side openness to universalist discourse, especially through Kabir, Mirabai, and the Sikhs.
- **[[tariqa-orders]]**: The Naqshbandi Mujaddidi order served as the organized opposition to coexistence under Sirhindi and his followers.

## End Conditions

The process effectively ended institutionally with Aurangzeb's policies from 1679. The empire never fully recovered after his death in 1707 — not because of coexistence per se, but because the removal of the integrative framework exacerbated fiscal and military stresses that were already acute. As Hodgson notes, "By his own confession, Awrangzêb had neglected that side of the Sharî'ah that called for protection and justice for the peasantry."

The deeper structural conditions — Hindu majority population, Rajput military power, necessity of multi-confessional administration — persisted after 1707 and continued to shape Mughal successor states (Hyderabad, Awadh, Maratha confederacy), as well as the later colonial-era debates about Hindu-Muslim relations.

## Historiography

This process has been heavily contested by twentieth-century communalist and anti-communalist historians, particularly in connection with Pakistani national historiography. Hodgson (in [[hodgson-venture-of-islam-v3-1974]]) identifies two historiographical positions: (1) British liberal historians who credited Akbar's tolerance and blamed Aurangzeb's communalism for imperial decline; (2) Muslim communalist historians who reversed the moral valuation — praising Aurangzeb for maintaining Islamic purity and critiquing Akbar as a Muslim apostate. "Anti-communalist historians (Muslim and non-Muslim) answered by depreciating the influence of these heroes and even of Awrangzêb himself, in part by insisting on the uncontrollable economics of the decline."

Hodgson's own position is nuanced: coexistence was real and significant, but it was a phenomenon of a thin courtly stratum and did not generate deeper interconfessional social bonds capable of surviving fiscal and political breakdown.
