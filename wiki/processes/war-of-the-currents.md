# War of the Currents

The **War of the Currents** (c. 1887–1893) was the commercial, technical, and propaganda struggle over whether the United States — and then the world — would electrify on **direct current (DC)** or **[[concepts/alternating-current|alternating current (AC)]]**. [[actors/edison-thomas|Thomas Edison]] and his Edison General Electric backed DC, which his existing central stations and patents depended on; [[actors/westinghouse-george|George Westinghouse]], having licensed [[actors/tesla-nikola|Nikola Tesla]]'s polyphase AC patents in 1888, backed AC.

## Definition and Scope

AC's transformer-based ability to transmit power at high voltage over long distances gave it a decisive economic advantage over DC, which could only serve customers within about a mile of a generating station. The contest was fought in the marketplace (utility contracts, equipment sales), in the courts (patent litigation), and in public opinion.

## Major Phases

- **Patent and licensing (1887–88):** Tesla's polyphase patents; the Westinghouse license.
- **Propaganda war (1888–90):** Edison's campaign emphasizing AC's lethality — staged public electrocutions of animals (December 1888) coined the verb "to Westinghouse" — and the promotion of AC for the first electric-chair execution, the botched 1890 killing of William Kemmler, to brand it "the executioner's current."
- **Decisive victories (1893–95):** Westinghouse's AC system lit the [[events/worlds-columbian-exposition-1893|World's Columbian Exposition]] (1893) and was chosen for the [[events/niagara-falls-power-project-1895|Niagara Falls power project]], settling the standards question in AC's favor.

## End Conditions

By the mid-1890s AC had won as the standard for generation and long-distance transmission; the consolidation of Edison General Electric into General Electric (which itself adopted AC) ended the contest. AC became the basis of modern [[processes/electrification|electrification]].
