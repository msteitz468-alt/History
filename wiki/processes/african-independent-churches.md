## Definition and Scope

'African Independent Churches' (also called African Initiated Churches, African Instituted Churches — all abbreviated AIC) refers to the broad category of Christian churches in Africa founded by Africans and independent of white missionary control. Also known historically as the 'Ethiopian Movement' (a term taken from Psalm 68:31, 'Ethiopia shall stretch out her hands unto God'), the phenomenon encompasses institutions ranging from Protestant breakaway churches in late-nineteenth-century South Africa, to the mass prophetic movement of Simon Kimbangu in the Belgian Congo, to Aladura and Apostolic/Zionist churches, to the wider Pentecostal and Charismatic explosion of the late twentieth century.

AICs are examined here as a sub-process of [[african-christianity]] and as the primary subject of Ch. 17 ('New Religious Movements') by Sean Hanretta in [[parker-reid-oxford-modern-african-history-2013]]. They are distinct from but intertwined with the broader mission Christianity from which they broke or emerged.

## Causal Drivers

**Racial exclusion and wounded respectability.** The first generation of mission-educated Christian elites in southern Africa encountered the colour bar: despite their Christian 'respectability', they were denied equality with whites. Mangena M. Mokone split from the Wesleyan Church in 1892, drawing inspiration from the ancient traditions of Christian Ethiopia, and founded the first 'Ethiopian Church' — inaugurating the Ethiopian Movement.

**Vernacular scriptures.** The translation of the Bible into African languages was the critical technical precondition: once vernacular scriptures existed, Africans found in them healing miracles, visions, exorcisms, food taboos, and polygamy that missionaries had not emphasized. The Gikuyu New Testament (1926) spurred the Kikuyu revival and inspired the *akurinu* (praying) church. Isaiah Shembe's Nazaretha Church drew its imagery of power and prophecy directly from the Zulu scriptures.

**Prophetic charisma.** Many of the most significant AICs emerged from individual prophets: Simon Kimbangu (Belgian Congo, 1920s); Isaiah Shembe (Nazaretha Church, Zulu); Johanna Maranke and Johanna Masowe (Vapostori / Apostles, Shona Zimbabwe); the Prophet William Wadé Harris (Côte d'Ivoire and Gold Coast, c.1910). These leaders elevated Christ's healing ministry over his atonement, founded centres of healing and prayer as their headquarters, and provided personal security through prayer, exorcism, and community solidarity.

**Colonial crisis.** The 'successive crises of the Great Depression and the Second World War' between 1929 and 1945 severely tested mission Christianity. As the colonial state consolidated its alliance with traditional leaders, and economic conditions worsened, resistance found expression in religious rather than secular forms. Southern African prophetic leaders emerged from traditions of Pentecostalism and revivalism. The Zionist and Apostolic churches were 'not explicitly proto-nationalist; rather, they saw themselves as providing personal security through prayer, healing, and exorcism' (David Maxwell, in [[parker-reid-oxford-modern-african-history-2013]]).

**Critique of missionary and customary authority.** AICs challenged both 'worldly missionaries' and 'pagan customary rulers' — the two key pillars of indirect rule. Their deep criticism of the colonial order explains why they unsettled imperial officials even when they were not explicitly anti-colonial. Young men in conversion movements 'burnt "fetishes", destroyed sacred sites, and challenged traditional hierarchies' while carrying out AICs' message.

## Major Forms

### Ethiopian Movement (southern Africa, 1890s–)

Named after ancient Ethiopian Christianity as a symbol of African self-determination. Split from white-controlled mission churches; drew on African theology and rejected racial hierarchy in the church. The movement's theology emphasized African dignity, African-controlled institutions, and sometimes Black Zionism (connections to the idea of return to Zion as Africa). Samuel Crowther, the first African Anglican bishop (1864), can be read as a precursor to this aspiration even within mission structures.

### Prophetic / Healing Churches

- **Kimbangu movement (Belgian Congo, 1921–):** Simon Kimbangu, a former Baptist catechist, led a mass movement among the Bakongo. Suppressed and imprisoned for life by Belgian authorities, Kimbangu became a martyr figure; the church survived underground and emerged legally after independence as the Kimbanguist Church, one of the largest in Africa
- **Harrist Church (Côte d'Ivoire, c.1913–):** William Wadé Harris, a Liberian Grebo, baptized tens of thousands while traveling with a cross, calabash, and Bible; channelled converts into Methodism and Catholicism where available, but where they were not, the distinctive Harris Church developed
- **Nazaretha Church (Nazarites, South Africa, 1910s–):** Isaiah Shembe elevated Christ's healing ministry and combined Christian elements with Zulu spiritual practices; his hymns and meditations drew heavily from Zulu vernacular scripture
- **Vapostori (Apostles, Zimbabwe, 1930s–):** Johanna Maranke and Johanna Masowe founded Shona Apostolic churches emphasizing spirit possession, prophecy, and healing

### Aladura Movement (Yoruba Nigeria, 1920s–)

The Aladura ('praying') churches — including the Cherubim and Seraphim, the Christ Apostolic Church, and others — arose among the Yoruba in response to the influenza pandemic and the failures of missionary medicine. They combined Protestant Christianity with Yoruba healing practices, prophecy, and spiritual power; women played significant roles in early Aladura movements.

### Balokole Revival (East Africa, from 1939)

The Balokole ('saved people' in Luganda) began in Rwanda in 1939 and spread through East African Protestant churches. Drawing on the biblical language of renewal, revivalists preached against the institutionalization of mission Christianity and called for public confession of personal sin. Women and youth were central to the revival's energy.

### Zionist and Apostolic Churches (southern Africa)

Connected to North American Pentecostal antecedents but rapidly indigenized. These churches proliferated across South Africa and Zimbabwe from the 1920s–1930s, stressing faith healing, spirit baptism, and purification rituals adapted from indigenous practice (wearing white, worshipping outdoors, using holy water and staffs). They provided belonging and therapy in the dislocation of migrant labour economies.

### Roho Movement (Kenya)

Cynthia Hoehler-Fatton's work on the Roho ('Spirit') movement in western Kenya traces women's roles in a spirit-centred church that developed from the Luo community's encounter with mission Christianity; women of fire and spirit were central to early leadership, a pattern common to insurgent AICs.

## Process Dynamics and Later Phases

By the 1980s–2000s the distinction between AICs and mainstream Pentecostalism had become blurred. Pentecostal and charismatic churches expanded explosively, sharing AICs' emphasis on conversion experience, gifts of the Spirit, faith healing, and personal transformation. What Sean Hanretta calls the 'prosperity gospel' built on older AIC ideas of rupture with the past while shifting focus toward individual material well-being. Women remained over-represented in membership but were usually excluded from top leadership; gender and age dynamics within AICs tracked broader social transformations — 'Movements among Christians and Muslims alike have seen women play important leadership roles during early insurgent phases only to find their options narrow as institutionalization proceeds' (Hanretta, in [[parker-reid-oxford-modern-african-history-2013]]).

The Brotherhood of the Cross and Star (Nigeria, 1950s–) illustrates the complexity of contemporary new religious movements: drawing on Protestant terminology, local spiritualities, and transnational influences, while attacking 'traditional' forms of social authority. Alice Lakwena's Holy Spirit Movement in Uganda (1980s) — which drew on Acholi spirit possession, a global array of spirits, and Christian healing traditions — blurred AIC, new religious movement, and armed insurrection, ultimately prefiguring the Lord's Resistance Army.

## Historiography

Early scholarly attention to AICs came from missionaries (Bengt Sundkler's *Bantu Prophets in South Africa*, 1948 — later revised as *Zulu Zion*) and colonial officials worried by their political implications. Terence Ranger's early work sought to identify AICs as 'proto-nationalist'; he later abandoned this, finding the political implications too varied and the movements too diverse. Literary scholars (Liz Gunner) have read AIC canonical histories and spiritual autobiographies as legitimating charters. Matthew Engelke's anthropological study of the Friday Apostles in Zimbabwe (*A Problem of Presence*, 2007) showed how new media and objects transform relationships between practitioners and the divine. Joel Cabrita's work on the Nazaretha Church (*Texts and Authority*, 2012) traced how Zulu Christian nationalism was imagined through scripture and sacred text.

The term 'African Independent Churches' has given way in much recent scholarship to 'African Initiated Churches' or 'African Instituted Churches' to emphasize African agency rather than separation from European institutions. The field's centre of gravity has moved from counting and classifying these movements to understanding what Christianity meant for ordinary adherents — the cognitive, aesthetic, and social transformations involved in conversion and worship.
