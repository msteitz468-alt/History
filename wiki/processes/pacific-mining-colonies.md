## Definition and Scope

Pacific mining colonies were a distinctive sub-form of the colonial economic process, characterised by resource extraction (phosphates, nickel, gold) that generated enclave economies detached from Islander welfare. Mining preceded plantation agriculture in some cases (New Caledonia nickel from the 1870s) and rose to prominence alongside or above it in others (gold overtook sugar in Fiji's export value during World War II). Mining differed from plantations in several respects: it did not require Islander participation even in principle; it frequently required Islander displacement; and its profits were almost entirely repatriated. As [[denoon-cambridge-pacific-islanders-1997]] states: "Of all the catastrophes which befell Islanders, phosphates were the worst. Rights were quickly and cheaply alienated. There was no need to involve them in production, nor even to provide space."

## Phosphate Mining

### Banaba (Ocean Island) and Nauru
The Pacific Islands Company (incorporated 1897) combined J. T. Arundel's guano interests with Auckland traders Henderson and MacFarlane, chaired by Lord Stanmore (formerly Sir Arthur Gordon, Governor of Fiji). "The company languished until Albert Ellis identified phosphates on Banaba (Ocean Island) and in 1900 induced two Banabans to sign an exceptionally generous 999-year concession."

The company was restructured in 1902 as the **Pacific Phosphate Company**. It achieved "economies of scale (and market leverage) in 1907 by securing similar mining rights in German Nauru." The result was near-total Pacific phosphate monopoly. Banabans' dispossession was complete: they "not only lost their chance of royalties: in 1927 they were exiled to Rabi Island in Fiji."

Nauruans "were involved only to reinforce German authority over mutinous Chinese workers, and were marginalised until they had the good fortune to gain independence before mining had finished. The present distinction between affluent Nauruans and homeless Banabans seems mainly a matter of luck."

### Makatea
An islet 200 kilometres north-east of Tahiti, "inhabited by 160 to 200 Polynesians who had the misfortune to live above phosphates." The **Compagnie française des phosphates de l'Océanie** (1908) joined with the Pacific Phosphate Company. "During years of litigation and negotiation, the inhabitants were almost forgotten and, deprived of their land, drifted away, making room for labourers." Japanese and Chinese workers were introduced.

### Angaur (Palau)
The island "was bought for £60 (from two men, when women refused to negotiate), restricting the inhabitants to a 150-hectare reservation." Chinese labourers were recruited, then Pohnpeian prisoners of war, then "nearly a thousand other Islanders, mainly atoll men 'Volunteered' by Yapese patrons who confiscated their earnings." By 1914: "ten Germans, 100 Chinese and 500 Islanders, paid in proportion to civil status." After 1914 the Japanese continued with similar racially stratified labour practices. The island had "an unhappy reputation among Micronesians . . . known as the 'Village of the Dead.'"

### Common Features of Phosphate Mining
- Rights acquired "quickly and cheaply" through perfunctory or coerced negotiation
- Asian (Chinese, Japanese) and Islander labour, racially stratified; white managers only
- Colonial state provided annexation on demand (Royal Navy annexed Banaba 1901) and legal frameworks
- "The inter-changeability of prisoners and 'free' labourers speaks volumes for industrial relations"

## Nickel and Metal Mining (New Caledonia)

Australians discovered gold in New Caledonia in 1870; copper was found nearby; "the most significant discovery was nickel." Rothschilds put **Societe le Nickel** on sound footing and "during the 1890s when European armies hardened their steel, nickel production boomed." New Caledonia's colonial economy exhibited "the paradox of a large population, convicts—and a chronic labour shortage." The gap was filled by New Hebridean labourers (from 1850s) and later Vietnamese.

Workers suffered "from pneumonia in underground mining"; employers reckoned Vietnamese were hardier, "but Islanders were employed in surface work: one nickel mine was known as 'La Mine des Sans-Culottes' on account of its under-dressed Islanders."

## Gold Mining (Papua New Guinea)

### Milne Bay Islands (from 1888)
Prospectors focused first on Tagula (Sudest): "400 miners descended on a population of about 1000." Islanders washed gold themselves for several years, earning more than formal employment. But "increased mortality from the unsanitary practices of prospectors and labourers, dysentery and other infections" must be reckoned against those benefits.

Misima and Murua (Woodlark) attracted Australian capital. Kulumadau (on Murua) was "promoted by stock market manipulation, insider trading, exaggeration (if not falsification) of assays" — a lesson in how mining capital operated. Broken Hill Proprietary took over Misima from 1914, employing men from other Islands "at 10 shillings per month — but few from Misima."

### New Guinea Mainland (1920s–30s)
The 1923 Koranga find and the 1926 Edie Creek find (on the shoulder of Mount Kaindi) triggered a gold rush: "more than 200 miners and 1300 labourers had endured the six-day climb to Edie Creek, and in 1927 more than 110,000 ounces of gold were declared."

The **Leahy brothers** (Mick, Dan, Jim) from Queensland arrived in 1932, famously "discovering" the million-soul Highlands. They found gold, "though not the colossal deposits which they expected."

Labour conditions in the rush were extractive in the literal sense: "You bought, as we used to call them, slaves . . . at about ten pounds a head . . . some of the more unscrupulous recruiters used dog chains and padlocks. Every night they'd chain the boys up because the further they got from home, the more homesick they'd get."

**Bulolo Gold Dredging Ltd** was "most spectacular of the mining giants": built an airstrip, flew in dredge components, made Lae "the busiest airport in the world." Employed 350 foreigners. "Such companies might have trained New Guineans and reduced their wage bills, but Australian trade unions and the colonial administration barred this approach, so New Guineans worked with picks and shovels for 5 to 10 shillings per month, alongside high-technology machines and unionised artisans." Bulolo was "a classic enclave development . . . remitting most earnings abroad . . . Apart from royalty payments and import taxes, little money stayed in the country."

### Gold in Fiji (1932)
Gold identified in Tavua district (Vanua Levu) in 1932 during the Great Depression. "Small prospectors and speculators were deterred by high fees on claims; Fijian claims to ownership were finessed by legislation." Frank Packer, Edward Theodore, and John Wren created a mining complex. CSR was "conciliated by agreeing that few Indians would be employed in mining." By 1938 the mines produced "more than a quarter of the colony's exports." Rotumans were employed at intermediate wages — "too far from home to abscond easily — relished an intermediate status." Fijians were sent by their *bull* (chief) with "little choice but to endure the heat and dust, wretched barracks, and miserable rations."

## Colonial State and Mining

Officials "assumed that any production was desirable. To promote phosphate mining, therefore, they allowed companies to acquire rights cheaply and import Asian labour, and waived regulations which protected workers. To promote gold-mining, Papuan officials exempted machinery from import duties, and set royalties and licence fees at risible levels. Fijian companies routinely manipulated working costs to minimise taxes." The state's role was to "regulate labour (sometimes providing convicts), keep the peace, build infrastructure, and provide transport."

The Rabaul Strike (1929) organisers were "condemned to work in the Highland mines and some men were convicted on slender evidence merely to conscript them" — the colonial administration using mining conscription as judicial punishment.

## Historiography

Williams and Macdonald, *The Phosphateers*, is the essential source on nitrate/phosphate mining. Nelson, *Taim Bilong Masta* and *Black, White and Gold*, covers Papua New Guinea conditions. Emberson-Bain, *Labour and Gold in Fiji*, is the thorough analysis of gold mining in Fiji. Hezel and Berg (eds), *Micronesia: Winds of Change*, covers Angaur. McGee and Henning, "Investment in Lode Mining, Papua 1878–1920," analyses promoter corruption.

**Collection coverage**: Moderate for New Guinea and Nauru/Banaba. Thin for Angaur, Makatea, and Fijian gold.
