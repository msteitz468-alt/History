# The Christianization of the Roman Empire

The long process by which Christianity went from a persecuted minority to the **dominant and then official
religion** of the Roman world — and, more elusively, by which the *population* came to be Christian.
Treated for the fourth and early fifth centuries in [[cameron-cah-v13-1998|CAH XIII]] Ch. 21 (**Peter
Brown**), whose account is a powerful **revision of the triumphalist narrative**.

## Definition and scope: against the "death of paganism"

Brown's central move is to **dismantle the Christian "representation"** of the age — the story (running
from the church fathers through Gibbon and Burckhardt to modern textbooks) that the fourth century
witnessed the rapid, inevitable **"death of paganism,"** as Christian emperors from Constantine to
Theodosius II abolished "the error of the Greeks" amid a few "forlorn pagan revivals." This was a
**facilitating narrative** — the *strepitus mundi*, the reassuring "roar" of a world believed to be rushing
into the church — that imposed "a firm narrative closure" on what was in reality a **"wavering century."**
In Christian eyes the decisive victory had already been won by Christ on the cross; the imperial
suppression of cult was a mere "mopping-up." Narratives of temple destruction (Zeus at Apamea 386, the
**Serapeum of Alexandria** c. 392) moved at a "brisk rhythm" of instant, supernatural triumph — a
stylization both sides used to "defuse recrimination" (the priests of Serapis claiming he had simply
**withdrawn to heaven**).

## What Christianization actually meant

Behind the "roar," Brown argues, the reality was an **"infinitely diverse religious climate"**: into 425, a
large part of the population at all social levels remained **polytheist**, and even committed Christians
shared a **compartmentalized model of the universe** "rustling with invisible presences" — a High God in
the heavens communicating through hosts of lower spirits open to human appeal. At the shrine of St Thecla
or the spring of Sulis Minerva at Bath (the curse-tablet dividing humanity "whether gentile or
Christian"), Christian and non-Christian powers **jostled for recognition**. For hardliners like
**Augustine, Shenoute and Ephraim the Syrian**, "Christianization" therefore meant **staking a claim to a
monopoly** — asserting the lordship of Christ over *all* invisible powers, declaring the old gods bankrupt
and outside the church, and channelling spiritual power through the clergy. But this monopolizing claim was
"held in check" throughout the period by the older, more tolerant, compartmentalized worldview that gave
ordinary Christians "freedom of manoeuvre."

## The drivers: patronage, the church, and the law

- **Imperial patronage** from Constantine (the wealth, buildings and privilege of the
  [[late-roman-church-institution|church as a public institution]]); the "Age of Authority" in which
  imperial self-assertion (save under [[julian|Julian]]) backed the church.
- **The law codes**: by 425 the laws against polytheists, Jews and heretics had "congealed into a system"
  that, **for the first time in Roman history, made correct religious adherence a requirement for the full
  enjoyment of Roman society.** The laws were "frankly intended to terrorize" (capital punishment for
  sacrifice; lethal leaden whips) — though local authorities applied them laxly and **bishops often
  obstructed them**, claiming the prestige of intercession. From c. 370 the chancery adopted ***paganus***
  ("pagan") — a Christian "lump word" (originally "civilian"/"rustic") — as the official term for
  polytheists.
- **The episcopate and the ascetic/monastic movement** (see [[late-antique-asceticism]]), which gave
  Christianity its new heroes and its penetration of society.

## Significance

Brown's chapter reframes Christianization as a **gradual, ambiguous, contested process** rather than a
sudden triumph — the "silent working-out of a complex 'logic of the possible.'" Its lasting outcome was
not the instant abolition of polytheism but the creation of a **religious ordering of society** — an
"official map" drawn in Christian categories, an "inseparable adjunct of imperial rule" in the empire and
its successor states. This is the deep process underlying the volume's narrative: the conversion of the
empire was real and decisive in law and public life by 425, but the conversion of the *cognitive majority*
was the work of centuries.

## Causal Drivers / Major Phases / End Conditions

- **Drivers**: imperial patronage (Constantine onward), the wealth and legal privilege of the episcopate,
  the terror and incentive of the law codes, the ascetic movement.
- **Phases**: Constantine's conversion (312) → the contested fourth century (the [[arian-controversy|Arian
  disputes]], [[julian|Julian's]] reaction) → [[theodosius-i|Theodosius I's]] Nicene state church and
  anti-pagan laws (380–392) → the legal "system" of c. 425.
- **End conditions**: formally dominant by 425; the deeper conversion of the population continued for
  centuries (and the process is incomplete in Brown's "wavering" sense).

## The Egyptian case (Bagnall & Rathbone)

Egypt is among the best-documented arenas of the process. [[sources/bagnall-rathbone-egypt-alexander-copts-2004|Bagnall
and Rathbone]] trace it through the [[papyrology|papyri]]: a Christian presence by the third
century, but serious penetration of the countryside only in the **fourth**, visible in the
appearance of churches and an ecclesiastical hierarchy and in the **shift of personal names from
pagan to biblical**. By the fifth century "Egypt had become a Christian land," its villages full of
churches and its deserts full of monasteries. Its landmarks recur in this wiki: the
[[destruction-of-the-serapeum-391|destruction of the Serapeum (391)]] and the killing of Hypatia
(415); the birth of [[egyptian-monasticism|monasticism]] under [[antony-of-egypt|Antony]] and
[[pachomius|Pachomios]]; the doctrinal leadership of [[athanasius-of-alexandria|Athanasius]]; and
the emergence, after [[council-of-chalcedon-451|Chalcedon]], of the
[[coptic-church|Coptic Church]]. Bagnall's own work cautions that the dramatic temple-destruction
set-pieces can overstate how *sudden* conversion was — the documents show a gradual, uneven
process.

## Ball: Christianity as the eastern religion (2000)

Warwick Ball's *[[ball-rome-in-the-east-2000|Rome in the East]]* reads Christianisation as the
culminating act of his [[orientalization-of-rome|orientalization]] thesis — Rome's adoption of "a
completely alien" eastern religion (see the [[orientalization-of-rome-thesis|debate]]). His claims
here are interpretive and, in places, sharply revisionist; they are recorded as **Ball's argument**,
not as settled history:

- **Eastern roots.** Ball traces core Christian ideas to **Zoroastrianism** (afterlife/judgment,
  good–evil dualism, an abstract wholly-good creator, a virgin-born redeemer, the Magi) — "the
  Christian god owes more to Zoroastrianism than to Judaism" — and to Semitic congregational
  temple-cult, aniconic Semitic deity-representation, Anatolian mother-goddess cults (the Virgin at
  Ephesus), and Phoenician resurrection cults. He argues Kartir's Sasanian state church
  "foreshadowed the Papacy."
- **Antioch the hinge.** [[antioch|Antioch]] — where followers were first called "Christians," and
  which claimed Petrine primacy over Rome — is his crucial east-to-west relay.
- **Class and gender.** Ball frames the faith's spread from underclass to aristocracy as running
  through household slaves converting elite women — "Christianity's first great triumph represents a
  triumph of feminism."
- **The Severan / Sun-cult bridge.** He credits the Emesene [[severan-dynasty|Severans]] as
  accelerators (not converts) and draws a direct line from [[elagabalus|Elagabalus]]'s sun-god
  through Aurelian's Sol Invictus to Constantine — east-facing churches, the Sunday Sabbath, and
  25 December as accommodations to the pre-existing cult: "Constantine's revolution... was a true
  orientalisation of Rome."
- **Contrarian points.** Ball downplays the missionary effect of Peter and Paul as "greatly
  exaggerated," argues persecution actually suppressed the faith, holds that
  [[kingdom-of-edessa|Edessa]] under Abgar the Great was the first Christian state, and contends
  ([[philip-the-arab|Philip the Arab]]) was Rome's first Christian emperor before Constantine.

## OHCW (Matthews; Russell; Chadwick)

**Matthews** (Ch. 31): Eusebius of Caesarea saw *pax Augusta* as providential for Christian expansion — Roman power peaked with Jesus’ sojourn and Augustus’ defeat of Cleopatra; nations under Rome reachable without war barriers — yet Eusebius barely mentions western/northern regions (Greek reserve toward the Latin West). Younger Pliny’s Bithynia correspondence: executed obstinate low-status Christians; noted contagion had reached countryside; temples and sacrificial meat markets revived under his intervention.

**Russell** (Ch. 27): dual grounding of Pliny–Trajan Christian letters within high-imperial prose culture.

**Chadwick** (Envoi): Christian mission met persecution and philosophic criticism; early apologists claimed compatibility with Stoic ethics and Platonic metaphysics (Justin, Clement, Origen); “Seneca saepe noster” (Tertullian); tensions over incarnation vs immutability, eternity of world, reincarnation; monasticism from c.320 (Pachomius; Athanasius’ *Life of Antony*; Basil’s rules) shocked bishops by draining dedicated urban members. Capture of society largely by ~400 raised social questions: poor-register alms inadequate (Augustine wanted redistributive state subsistence); church landownership and episcopal community leadership; deep disapproval of capital punishment and torture (modified only gradually); Donatist schism 311–Islam split N. Africa; Monophysite alienation after Chalcedon 451 weakened Byzantine resistance to Arabs from 634. Church as alternative career structure is a more plausible contribution to the end of antiquity than crude “Christian pacifism.” Source: [[boardman-oxford-history-classical-world-1986]].

## From Logan, History of the Church in the Middle Ages (2013)

- **Two factors in growth (R1-010):** Logan (following a “factors” not “causes” approach) singles out the existence of the Roman Empire (roads, *mare nostrum*, Pax Romana, Greek/Latin) and a “spiritual vacuum” left by formalized state religion.
  - Quote: "what factors contributed to the growth of Christianity? Two will be singled out here: the existence of the Roman Empire and a spiritual vacuum in the Roman world."
- **Scale by Constantine (R1-011):** By Constantine’s conversion (312), perhaps six or seven million Christians within the empire; about one-tenth of the population at the Edict of Milan, denser in the East.
  - Quote: "by the conversion of Constantine in 312, perhaps as many as six or seven million Christians lived within the empire"
- **Character of persecutions (R1-012):** Nero’s local persecution set a precedent for persecuting Christians *as* Christians; thereafter for ~200 years persecutions were sporadic and local. Great Persecution under Diocletian (303–311) was last and largest. Popular “underground church / catacombs / fed to lions” picture is not borne out; overemphasis of persecution distorts realities, though martyrs could attract admirers (Tertullian’s *semen est sanguis Christianorum*).
  - Quote: "the picture of Christians in an 'underground' church, worshipping by stealth in the catacombs, being ruthlessly sought out by a uniformly hostile state and fed to lions is a picture hardly borne out by the facts."
- **Fourth-century process (R1-017):** From a small minority in 312, Christians formed the majority by at least the 380s; Christianization proceeded within decades after Constantine’s toleration rather than centuries.
  - Quote: "within decades after its toleration by Constantine, rather than centuries, Christianity had a predominant place in the religious life of the Roman world."
- See also [[constantine-the-great]] (R1-013) and [[arianism]] (R1-015).

## Gibbon, *Decline and Fall* (1776–1788) — Chs. XV–XVI

[[sources/gibbon-decline-and-fall-1776|Gibbon]] makes the progress of Christianity “a very essential part of
the history of the Roman empire” and the most contested hinge of the
[[concepts/gibbonian-decline-thesis|Gibbonian decline thesis]]. All of the following is **Gibbon’s framing**,
not wiki adjudication.

- **Five secondary causes of growth** (Ch. XV set-piece): (1) inflexible, “intolerant” zeal (Mosaic
  exclusiveness without the ceremonial fetters); (2) the doctrine of a future life made weighty for the
  multitude; (3) miraculous powers ascribed to the primitive church; (4) pure and austere morals; (5) union
  and discipline of the Christian republic as an independent state within the empire. Recap metaphor: zeal,
  future life, miracles, and virtue as arms; constitution as trained volunteers vs an undisciplined multitude.
- **Scale before Constantine (Gibbon’s numbers):** Christians perhaps ~1/20 of the empire; Rome ~50,000 of
  ~1,000,000; Antioch ~100,000 of ~500,000 — large enough to matter, far from majority.
- **Persecutions (Ch. XVI):** not continuous empire-wide slaughter; Gibbon filters martyr literature harshly
  and estimates **fewer than 2,000** capital sentences in the Great Persecution — a deliberate deflation of
  ecclesiastical legend (compare Logan’s modern “sporadic then Diocletian” line above, which converges on
  scale without Gibbon’s irony).
- **Causal fear and civic withdrawal:** Pagans reproached Christian refusal of war and magistracy; Gibbon
  records the charge that if all followed, the empire would fall to barbarians — fuel for his later claim
  that Christianity hastened fall by discouraging active civic virtue while also mollifying conquerors.
- **Tone:** sustained irony toward miracles, monks, and clerical power; “Who can refute a sneer?” became
  the standard clerical critique of his method. Use as **historiography of Christianization**, not as
  demographic or theological authority against CAH XIII / Brown / Harris.

## Harris, *Roman Power* (2016)

Harris treats Christianization as first a minor nuisance, then a structural power problem. At some point (perhaps Decius 249–51) the church was seen as a general nuisance though still a small segment; Diocletian failed to eliminate Christians; Constantine joined the cause near capturing Rome. After 312 the new religion mattered mainly by empowering an ecclesiastical hierarchy indifferent to state interests and by creating potential **divided religious loyalty in the army**. CTh 16.1.2 (380) ordered all peoples into Peter’s religion as followed by Damasus and Peter of Alexandria—non-Catholics “demented and insane”—a program of forced orthodoxy later substantially repeated by Justinian; not mass death for nonconformists (emperors knew they could not) but step-by-step marginalization (Jews barred from imperial service from 404; heretic property attacks 407; expulsions aspired 425).

Religious developments weakened Roman power two ways beyond this-worldly contempt: (1) divided military morale; (2) troops frequently diverted into religious conflicts—“religion occasioned the most frequent [internal] disruptions to require military intervention” (Whitby, cited). Temple destruction with imperial support (Cynegius PPO east 384–8; complete ban 391 *CTh* 16.10.11; western delay, Carthage 399). Harris argues late-fourth/early-fifth centuries saw important **social disintegration** from breaking bonds and internal hatreds—not sole-caused by Christianity, and Christianity had some positive social effects, but he rejects Heather’s “trickle-down more than confrontation” account. The spread of Christianity might have been only marginal for defence **if it had not brought Christian intolerance**—loathing for pagans, Jews, and differently-opinioned Christians. Seventh-century parallel: Chalcedonian–Monophysite mutual hatred a serious factor (Greatrex’s “fatal blow” slightly exaggerated); Egyptian Monophysites made little effort against Muslim attackers; Ghassanid devotion at Yarmuk doubtful. Source: [[harris-roman-power-2016]].

## Woolf, *Rome: An Empire's Story* (2012)

Woolf’s Ch. XVI frames Christianization as the rise of **religions-as-bounded-entities** that eventually overwrote the empire’s own grand narrative. Source: [[woolf-rome-empires-story-2012]].

- **Rise of “religions.”** Traditional ritual was constrained within family, city, army unit, or empire; Christian communities were of believers who might share little except belief and be separated by it from family/city. Manichaeans, Jews, and others similarly treated religious identity as separable. “The great history of the Roman Empire … had been taken over by another even grander narrative, the rise of religions.” No Greek/Roman words match the modern exclusive “religion”; conversion is more or less meaningless in antiquity unless as a component of changing citizenship.
- **Proto-religious pluralism.** First signs of religion as a distinct sphere: Euripides’ *Bacchae*; Bacchanalian panic 186 BCE. Migration cults (Isis, Syrian Baalim, Magna Mater, Jewish god), mysteries (Eleusis, Samothrace, Cybele, Mithras), philosophical sects; Mithraism and Manichaeism “created from scratch.” Early Christianity’s exclusive claim was perhaps its strongest selling point among proto-religions—“a new social identity based solely on religious membership … that cuts across the most fundamental social boundaries of antiquity.”
- **Scale to Constantine.** First generation: low thousands, mostly eastern cities, no scriptures at start. House churches under one bishop per city; absolute numbers remained small—non-Christian texts hardly remark on Christians before 200. “Few scholars believe more than 10 per cent of the empire’s subjects were Christians in Constantine’s day and the figures could easily be even smaller.” Very few high-status Christians before the third century; persecution probably less common than unpopularity until early third century (Pliny–Trajan shows some ban existed but emperors not keen to enforce).
- **General persecutions as accident.** Decius 250: first general persecution during military crisis—response to refusal of some Christian leaders to join empire-wide *supplicatio* of citizens (after Caracalla’s expanded citizen body). “General persecution, in other words, was an accident. Caracalla had inadvertently created … a form of religious authority that might be applied to the empire as a whole.” Great Persecution 303–11 (Galerius/Diocletian): military purges, text confiscation/destruction, attacks on churches. Year before: Manichaeism edict (Persian origin; texts burned; high-status converts to mines). Implementation poorly known; martyrdom rare in many parts; Donatist schism after 311 over *traditores*.
- **Why persecution stopped.** Comparative evidence (Manichaeism later eradicated in west; Buddhism largely extinguished in medieval India; Christian fragments under caliphates) suggests near-eradication was not unrealistic—Diocletian *might* have reversed higher-status gains. Possible reasons for failure: insufficient hatred; enemies poorly organized; cost without local zealots. Edicts of Toleration: Galerius 311; Constantine and Licinius at Milan 313. Constantine’s active patronage from ~312 was more remarkable than cessation of persecution.
- **Limits of Christian emperors.** Traditional cults survived long after Julian’s failed restoration: funded city cults into late fourth century; senatorial tenacity into the 380s; temple attacks rare before end of fourth century; Athens schools open until Justinian 529; isolated old-god communities in Asia Minor to late sixth. Constantine banned blood sacrifice, oracles, statues to old gods, and imposed harsh Jewish legal restrictions (converting Christians, owning Christian slaves, circumcising them)—“no great vision of a multi-faith imperial society.” Bans not universally accepted; controversy to century’s end.
- **Incomplete unity; Arianism.** Nicaea 324 aimed at unity (Christology; Easter; Nicene Creed); unity not achieved—“The Arian heresy remained a central source of division through the fourth century”; barbarian kings (Goths, Vandals) largely Arian, divided from eastern empire and own subjects. On Constantine’s death 337 church position unassailable; all subsequent emperors except Julian Christian; fusion of Christian universalism with imperial ideology came only gradually and remained far from unified.
- **Ideology less effective than state cults.** In Woolf’s fall analysis: “Christianity was a less effective imperial ideology than had been the traditional state cults, partly because of the chronic tendency to schism and heresy, partly because it conferred an independent authority on religious leaders, such as the bishops of Rome.” Sixth century: one could be Christian without being emperor’s subject, and Roman under a barbarian king. “Christianity and Islam did not destroy the Roman Empire, but the world they introduced was one less friendly to the great political empires of antiquity.”
- **Christian pasts flexible.** Melito, Orosius (providential empire) vs Augustine (Earthly City convulsions little relevance for Heavenly City); Gregory of Tours can write Frankish history with western empire falling mid-book 2 without mention. “Nothing could catch them out, not the fall of the western empire, the thousand-year succession of Byzantium, or the terrible events of the Arab conquests.”

## From Mitchell, Later Roman Empire (2015)

Mitchell’s Chs. 7–8 frame Christianization as rapid but incomplete, ideologically central to imperial politics, and systematically misrepresented by triumphalist sources.

### Pace, character, and source bias

- Change was rapid by any standard but not instantaneous; significant pagan groups persisted into the fifth and even sixth centuries; impact was empire-wide yet hard to explain. Pagans and Christians were not homogeneous; conversion was individual and collective, voluntary and coerced; traffic was not one-way; some “Christian” communities retained pre-Christian traditions. Open questions: how large a place religion occupied in public/private life, and how far religious preference determined social/political behavior versus secular space for fraternizing, intermarriage, and business.
- **Literary dominance:** Most new late antique literary genres are Christian and unabashedly partisan; pagan writings survive more sparsely because Christians disseminated their own literature and had an interest in suppressing pagan cultural products — visible in full survival of mid-fifth-century church histories versus selective excerpts of secular historians.
- **Eusebius** created church history as “national” Christian narrative whose revealed truth coincided with Augustus’s empire; he incorporated verbatim documents (sometimes independently confirmed by papyri/inscriptions). Dating of books 1–7 (Barnes: before 300 vs later) bears on whether the church already had a confident social foothold before the [[great-persecution-303|Great Persecution]] and Constantine’s conversion.

### Constantine to state religion

- Constantine’s conversion transformed Christianity more than it merely favored an existing cult: illegal cult became most favored and by **325** religion of the Roman state; clergy privileged; bishops gained legal powers and the emperor’s ear; pagan cults restricted/undermined without full suppression; imperial example prompted mass conversions (Eusebius commissioned 50 luxury scripture copies for Constantinople’s swelling congregations). Pre-300 minority strictness vs post-persecution mass entry: Chadwick’s “society of saints” vs “school for sinners.” Early councils (Arles 314, Elvira, Ancyra 313–16, Neocaesarea) regulated office-holding, trade, usury, sexual discipline. Barnes argues Christians already majority among top posts under Constantine/Constantius; regardless, Christians held high office from conversion onward and worked with pagans — religious affiliation often a neutral factor in state service. Army “made Christian” by Chi-Rho on shields yet soldiers retained pagan traditionalism and eastern cults. Baptism marked elect vs compromised everyday Christians; Constantine baptized only on deathbed.
- At or around [[council-of-nicaea-325|Nicaea]], Constantine’s saying (Eusebius) that he is *episkopos* “of those outside” acknowledged responsibility for non-Christian subjects: passionate Christian promotion coexisted with commitment to the empire as a whole.

### Political ideology and unity project

- Political objective: a unified Roman state co-extensive with a unified Church; **Orthodox Catholic Christianity** became central to imperial political ideology and the main symbolic identifier of Roman inhabitants.
- Three emblematic conversions (Constantine; Julian reverse; Augustine) structure religious identity, but imperial policy’s continuous project was **political-religious unity** — from Constantine’s “one faith” through Theodosius’s imposed Nicene settlement to Justinian’s Chalcedonian-Roman equation — repeatedly producing schism as the price of definition (Donatists, Melitians, Novatians, Arians/homoeans, Nestorians, Monophysites, Acacian East–West split). Religious identity formation worked at regional (African Christianity), empire-wide doctrinal, and ethnic-barbarian (Arian foederati) scales. Mitchell’s standing judgment: legislation’s intolerant surface vs practical balancing until Justinian; Christian triumphalist sources systematically misrepresent diversity and pagan residual strength.

### Court piety and Justinianic repression

- **Pulcheria** and Theodosius II’s court: palace as monastic *asketerion*; Pulcheria’s virginity (not legal *tutela*) drove religious policy; she and her sisters promoted the Virgin as **Theotokos**, elevating Marian theology; entangled in Ephesus 431/449 leading to [[council-of-chalcedon-451|Chalcedon 451]] and two-natures doctrine against Cyril, contributing to Nestorius’ deposition after Ephesus 431.
- From **527** Justinian outlawed Manichees and Samaritans; Samaritan revolt **529** crushed with claimed 100,000 dead and crushing tax aftermath (again 556); Montanist repression from 529 culminating ~550 pogrom at Pepuza; **529** law aimed to abolish pagan practice and force baptism, closing Athens’ Neoplatonic school and sending staff fleeing to Persia — missionary fervor paired with foreign-policy Christianization from Crimea to Ethiopia.
