## Definition and Scope

From 1946 to 1996 the United States, Britain, and France tested nuclear weapons in Pacific Island territories, almost continuously across five decades. The process was defined by two structural conditions: "The test sites were on Islands remote from Western population, and Islanders were politically subordinated to the nuclear powers." The Americans conducted atmospheric tests of atomic and hydrogen bombs in the Marshall Islands between 1946 and 1958, with a final series at Johnston Atoll and Christmas Island in 1962. The British tested in the Australian atmosphere 1952–1957 (minor trials to 1963) and detonated hydrogen bombs at Christmas and Maiden Islands in 1957 and 1958. The French tested at Moruroa and Fangataufa from 1966 to 1992, atmospherically until 1975 then underground, conducting a final series in 1995–1996. Nuclear devices were exploded "almost continuously from 1946 to 1996."

This process is distinct from the [[nuclear-free-pacific-movement]], which was its political counter-response.

## Causal Drivers

**Great-power competition**: The US–Soviet arms race was the primary driver of American testing; "driven by the arms race with the Soviet Union, the pace of American nuclear testing quickened during the 1950s." Britain tested because its political leadership feared Britain would "sink to the rank of a second-class nation" without an independent deterrent. France tested because it would not be dependent on the United States and because loss of Algeria's Sahara test sites after 1962 left the Tuamotu Archipelago as the only option.

**Colonial geography**: Each power chose remote colonial territories where "populations were sparse and the political costs minimal." The Marshall Islands, held under American strategic trusteeship since 1947 under a provision "in which the administering authority could conduct military experiments," were the primary American site. Britain used Australian desert country (Maralinga) and the Gilbert and Ellice Islands Colony (Christmas and Maiden Islands). France used French Polynesia (Moruroa and Fangataufa in the Tuamotu Archipelago).

**Economic leverage**: In French Polynesia, France combined testing with massive economic transfers: the Centre d'Expérimentation du Pacifique (CEP) constructed an "economy of transfer, where money comes from the other side of the world in return for the provision of strategic services," turning French Polynesia into a nuclear dependency where the CEP contributed 76 per cent of GDP at its 1960s peak.

## Major Phases

### Phase 1 — US Testing in the Marshall Islands (1946–1962)

The US exploded 66 nuclear weapons in the northern Marshall Islands between 1946 and 1958. The 166 people of Bikini Atoll were evacuated in 1946 to Rongerik Atoll, "asking to go home within months, only to be told that repatriation was out of the question." The 142 people of Enewetak were moved to Ujelang Atoll on 21 December 1946, recorded in an Enewetakese song: *"On the day of the twenty-first all of the people came to this atoll. / But we do not worry for it is the will of the Lord. / We are very sad, we miss the islands from which we are separated."*

In 1952 the US detonated MIKE, its first thermonuclear device, with force equivalent to 500 Hiroshimas—it "completely removed an island from Enewetak Atoll and left a hole more than a kilometre wide in the fringing reef."

The defining catastrophe was the BRAVO shot of 1 March 1954: the largest American test, it created a mushroom cloud "of unprecedented magnitude and spread radioactive fallout over a vast area." Rongelap, 300 kilometres from the test site, received heavy fallout. John Anjain described it: *"I thought I saw what appeared to be the sunrise, but it was in the west . . . Then all of the people heard the great sound of the explosion . . . Then all of the people heard the great sound of the explosion. Some people began to cry with fright. Several hours later the powder began to fall on Rongelap, which covered our island and stuck to our bodies."* The Americans did not evacuate Rongelap until the third day; the people of Utrik Atoll, also exposed, waited a further day.

A 1954 petition to the UN Trusteeship Council from Marshallese leaders (including Kabua Kabua and Dwight Heine) documented burns, nausea, hair loss, and the centrality of land loss: *"Land . . . means more than just a place where you can plant your food crops and build your houses . . . It is the very life of the people. Take away their land and their spirits go also."* The Trusteeship Council "merely expressed regret." A 1960 civil suit for $8.5 million was dismissed by the American Chief Justice of the Trust Territory on sovereign immunity grounds.

Long-term health effects became undeniable. By 1979, thyroid growths had been found in 77 per cent of Rongelapese who had been under ten at the time of BRAVO. The people of Rongelap told a 1961 UN Visiting Mission of miscarriages, abnormal births, stomach disorders, and fish from the lagoon giving them "boils in the mouth." *"Whenever we have a cold, or some other kind of sickness, we think of the bomb,"* they said in 1972. By 1992 the four recognized "radiation atolls" had received $67 million in direct compensation and a further $21 million for healthcare, with a Nuclear Claims Tribunal recognising 25 medical conditions presumed to result from radiation exposure.

Enewetak was partially decontaminated at a cost of $100 million in the late 1970s, with radioactive soil capped under a concrete dome on Runit Island; 300 Islanders returned in 1980 but could live on only 3 of the atoll's 40 islands. Bikini resettlement in the 1970s failed when scientists discovered residents were absorbing unsafe levels of strontium and cesium. The Rongelap people were evacuated to Kwajalein Atoll by Greenpeace's Rainbow Warrior in 1985, fearing residual radioactivity.

### Phase 2 — British Testing in Australia and the Pacific (1952–1963)

Britain tested weapons at Monte Bello Islands, Emu Field, and Maralinga in South Australia, and H-bombs at Christmas Island and Maiden Island. The Maralinga tests—kept secret until the 1980s—dispersed approximately 22 kg of plutonium-239 and a similar amount of uranium-235, both radioactive for thousands of years.

Aboriginal Australians—the Yankunytjatjara and Pitjantjatjara—were "constantly forming and re-forming" across the Maralinga Prohibited Zone. Ground patrols were inadequate: one pilot admitted that the plane "really just flew up the road and not over the country." One government officer, Walter MacDougall, who tried to warn the authorities of inadequate safety searches, was accused of "apparently placing the affairs of a handful of natives above those of the British Commonwealth of Nations." After a 1953 test, a "black mist" enveloped people at Wallatinna 173 km away; people vomited and developed sore eyes, skin rashes, and fevers. The 1985 Australian Royal Commission confirmed contamination but could not "quantify radiation injuries nor say who had suffered them." Britain agreed in 1993 to pay £20 million—less than half the sum required for clean-up.

At Christmas Island (now Kiritimati), a few hundred Gilbertese copra workers were present during both British tests (1957–1958) and American tests (1962). They were temporarily taken offshore on vessels and shown cartoons below decks during blasts; as tests continued, "fewer people bothered to leave."

### Phase 3 — French Testing in French Polynesia (1966–1996)

France conducted atmospheric tests at Moruroa and Fangataufa from 1966 to 1975, then underground from 1975, finally offshore (beneath the lagoon) from the 1980s. A final series was conducted in 1995–1996 despite massive regional protest, before France joined the South Pacific Nuclear-Free Zone Treaty.

French testing turned [[french-polynesia]] into a "nuclear dependency." CEP expenditure peaked at 76 per cent of territorial GDP in the mid-1960s construction period. By 1990 the territory depended on France for 75 per cent of its external income. The trade balance collapsed: exports had covered 90 per cent of imports in the 1950s but fell to 9 per cent by 1965 and never recovered. By the early 1990s the territory was producing merely a quarter of its food. As French Prime Minister Rocard stated in 1989: *"Who pays, controls."*

France suppressed the independence movement to protect its testing program—jailing nationalist leader Pouvanaa a Oopa in 1959 on dubious arson charges, dissolving the RDPT in 1963, and blocking independent monitoring of contamination. Deaths of workers from an explosion and fire at the test site, a 1979 bomb detonated half-way down a shaft that produced a subterranean landslide and tidal wave, and 1981 storms dispersing plutonium residues across the lagoon, all led the Territorial Assembly to demand independent inquiry—demands France rejected. Three invited expert missions (Tazieff 1982, Atkinson 1983, Cousteau 1987) were "restricted in their sampling, and—denied access to the most heavily contaminated sections of the atoll—produced no long-term conclusions."

When President Chirac announced resumption of testing in 1995, Pape'ete "erupted in a major riot": demonstrators stormed the international airport terminal with bricks and a bulldozer, rampaged through the city, and set part of the town ablaze. France completed its final series of eight tests and then, in 1996, joined the South Pacific Nuclear-Free Zone Treaty.

## Geographic Spread

- **Marshall Islands**: Bikini, Enewetak, Rongelap, Utrik (radiation atolls); Kwajalein (military base and missile testing continuing post-1958)
- **South Australia and Western Australia**: Monte Bello Islands, Emu Field, Maralinga
- **Kiribati**: Christmas Island (Kiritimati), Maiden Island
- **French Polynesia**: Moruroa and Fangataufa atolls in the Tuamotu Archipelago

## End Conditions

The process ended in 1996 when France completed its final test series and signed the South Pacific Nuclear-Free Zone Treaty, and the United States and Britain followed in signing. "All five nuclear powers had agreed that the days of testing in the Pacific were over." However, "in another sense the nuclear era would not be closed now, or for centuries to come. The legacy of half a century of nuclear blasts remained in the bodies of people exposed to radiation, in the natural environment of the test atolls, and in the risk of future contamination."

The decontamination of Enewetak and Bikini remains incomplete; the Runit dome on Enewetak encases radioactive material that will remain hazardous for thousands of years. American physicians still visit the Marshall Islands twice yearly to examine approximately 160 remaining fallout victims "at increased risk for malignant diseases as late complications of radiation exposure." Moruroa atoll is monitored but has not been fully assessed for contamination.

## Historiography

Per the chapter's bibliographic essay, leading sources include Firth's *Nuclear Playground*; Weisgall's *Operation Crossroads*; the Australian Royal Commission report (1985); and Danielsson and Danielsson, *Poisoned Reign* (for French Polynesia). Security restrictions—especially on French testing—mean the contamination record is incomplete. The 1985 Australian inquiry found contamination but "could not quantify radiation injuries nor say who had suffered them." For French testing, "no comprehensive and independent study of contamination has ever taken place." The US record is better documented but contentious: Marshallese themselves came to believe they were "deliberately exposed to fallout so that the United States would have a group of irradiated human beings for long-term study." American authorities dismissed Islander health complaints as "more psychological than real," a characterisation the text explicitly rejects: "The distinction between 'psychological' and 'real' was false."

## Fischer (2002): the half-century of testing, 1946–1996

[[fischer-history-pacific-islands-2002|Fischer (2002)]] totals "more than 250 nuclear devices … detonated at Bikini, Enewetak, Johnston Atoll, Christmas Island, Malden, Moruroa and Fangataufa" over 1946–1996 (L8031–8034). The US 1954 **Bravo** thermonuclear test irradiated the Rongelap and Utrik peoples, evacuated only days later (L8055–8066); in 1958 alone the US detonated as many devices over Bikini and Enewetak as in all prior Pacific tests combined (L8063–8066); Kennedy's 1962 halt concealed the secret post-Cuba deal with Khrushchev (L8067–8073). Britain's Christmas Island/Malden H-bomb tests (1957–58) added "only" ~4% of global fallout to 1980 (L8111–8116). **France** relocated testing from newly independent Algeria to Moruroa in 1963 without consulting Polynesian representatives (L8153–8160); the first atmospheric shot (2 July 1966) registered contamination in Peru and New Zealand (L8165–8169). France sank the *Rainbow Warrior* in Auckland on 10 July 1985 (L8202–8205); Chirac's 1995 resumption sparked the first modern Polynesian civil insurrection in Papeete (L8247–8260), and only after the final Fangataufa test (January 1996) did the US, UK and France sign the South Pacific Nuclear-Free Zone Treaty protocols (L8268–8270). Enewetak's people returned in 1980 to only 3 of 40 islands (L8086–8094).
