## Definition and Scope

The reconstruction of Italy after the fall of Mussolini and the end of the Italian campaign (1943–1945): the political contest between resistance-derived and prefascist forces for control of the new state; the drafting of a republican constitution; the consolidation of Christian Democratic power under [[de-gasperi-alcide]]; and the economic stabilization and neocorporatist rebuilding of an economy devastated more severely than any Western belligerent except Germany. It is not identical with the broader "Italian economic miracle" of the 1950s–60s, which built on this foundation but belongs to a later phase.

## Causal Drivers

- Italy's status as both battleground (the 1943–1945 campaign) and defeated Axis power, formalized in the Peace Treaty of 1947, which stripped Italy of its African and Aegean empires and transferred Fiume to Yugoslavia.
- The parallel existence of two antifascist claimants to legitimacy: the Allied-backed monarchist government under Marshal Badoglio in the south, and Communist-led resistance committees of liberation exercising de facto control in the north in spring 1945.
- Palmiro Togliatti's decision, on returning from Moscow exile in 1944, to order Italian Communists to cooperate with the provisional government rather than pursue revolution — foreclosing the option of contested power by force.
- U.S. political and economic backing for the Christian Democrats as the anti-Communist alternative, delivered through Marshall Plan aid.

## Major Phases

1. **1943–1945**: dual antifascist authority (south vs. north); resistance committees disarm as directed by the provisional government at war's end.
2. **June 1946**: constituent assembly elections (Christian Democrats 207 seats, Socialists 115, Communists 104); referendum abolishes the monarchy (54 percent) and establishes the republic.
3. **1947**: Communists expelled from government, part of the general Western European Cold War realignment also seen in France; De Gasperi emerges as dominant power.
4. **April 1948**: Christian Democrats win an outright parliamentary majority — the only single-party majority in modern Italian history — under the new republican constitution.
5. **1947–1951**: currency and price stabilization after severe inflation and black-marketeering; use of Marshall Plan aid; limited southern land redistribution (1.75 million acres, ~85,000 families).

## Geographic Spread

Concentrated in the Italian peninsula; the north/south divide (industrial north vs. impoverished south) remained the central structural fault line, with mass southern-to-northern migration deferred until the 1960s boom.

## Interaction

Closely parallel to, and causally linked with, the broader Western European pattern captured in [[french-fourth-republic]] and the British Labour welfare state: a wartime resistance coalition of Communists, Socialists, and Christian Democrats governs briefly, then splits along Cold War lines by 1947, with Christian Democratic or center-right parties emerging dominant under U.S. patronage. Italy's version was distinguished by the unusual strength of its Communist Party (regularly a third of the vote, governing major cities into the late 1980s) and by heavier institutional continuity with Fascist-era corporatist economic structures (the IRI state holding company).

## End Conditions

The process of reconstruction proper gives way to the sustained high-growth "economic miracle" of the later 1950s–60s; politically, Christian Democratic dominance established in this period persisted, with De Gasperi's party in every governing coalition, until 1993.

## Historiography

Paul Ginsborg, *A History of Contemporary Italy, 1943–1988* (2003), is the standard scholarly account; Donald Sassoon, *Contemporary Italy* (1997), supplements it. See [[paxton-europe-twentieth-century-2011]].
