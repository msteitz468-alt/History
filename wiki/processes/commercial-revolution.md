## Definition and Scope

The **Commercial Revolution** is the transformation of the European and
Mediterranean economy between roughly 950 and 1500 — the term is Robert S. Lopez's
(*The Commercial Revolution of the Middle Ages, 950–1350*) — by which a backward,
land-based, weakly monetized western Europe became a densely commercialized region
tied, by the fifteenth century, into a Mediterranean-to-Baltic system that
[[kedar-cwh-v5-2015|CWH V]] (Michel Balard, Ch 10) calls a **"first globalization"
of trade**. It is the European/Mediterranean segment of the broader
[[afro-eurasian-world-system|Afro-Eurasian world system]] — the "northwestern
portion" of the Afro-Eurasian zone, and in Richard Smith's typology a former
**cul-de-sac** that grew into a genuine commercial center.

[[luscombe-ncmh-v4-2004|NCMH IV]] (Derek Keene, Ch. 3, "Towns and the growth of
trade") frames the two centuries "that opened with the decline of Córdoba and closed
with the sack of Constantinople in 1204" as ones of vigorous commercial and urban
development across the whole 4,000-km span from the Volga to Ireland — while stressing
that for most of the period **western Europe remained backward and peripheral** by
comparison with the highly commercialised, urbanised Islamic Mediterranean and Middle
East, a relationship not conclusively reversed for half a millennium. The European
revival was thus in part stimulated *from outside*, by trade with the richer Muslim
and Byzantine south.

## Causal Drivers

- **Urban and demographic growth** from the eleventh century — itself driven by the
  [[medieval-agricultural-revolution|agricultural "take-off"]] — generating demand,
  labor, and markets.
- **The Italian maritime republics** — Venice, Genoa, Pisa, Amalfi — which seized
  the carrying trade of the Mediterranean, secured quarters in Byzantine and
  Crusader Levantine ports, and linked Europe to the Islamic and Asian luxury trades
  (silk, spices, alum, slaves).
- **Institutional and financial innovation**: the *commenda* and partnership
  contracts, bills of exchange, double-entry bookkeeping, marine insurance, branch
  companies (the Datini, the Medici, the Fuggers), and revived **gold coinage** (the
  Genoese genovino and Florentine florin, 1252; the Venetian ducat).
- **The Crusades**, which opened Levantine markets to Italian shipping.

## Major Phases and Networks

- **The Champagne fairs** (12th–13th c.) — the great overland clearing-house linking
  the Italian/Mediterranean trades to Flemish and northern cloth, with cycles of
  fairs structuring credit and settlement.
- **The Flemish cloth industry** (Arras, Ghent, Ypres, Bruges, Douai) — the
  manufacturing heart of the north, drawing English wool and Mediterranean dyestuffs.
- **The Hanseatic League** — German merchants of Cologne, Lübeck, and Hamburg formed
  Hanses (from c. 1130s) and merged (1281) into a league that by c. 1250 monopolized
  London–Novgorod traffic and bound the North and Baltic Seas (the Peterhof at
  Novgorod, the Steelyard at London, Visby on Gotland); later a confederation of ~150
  cities.
- **National variation**: France's merchants stayed largely provisioning-focused (the
  exceptional Jacques Cœur excepted); England lacked a strong merchant oligarchy
  until the later organization of the wool trade.
- **The fifteenth-century synthesis**: despite the [[black-death|Black Death]] and
  the fourteenth-century depression, sea and land routes knit the Mediterranean and
  the north into one growing system — Balard's "first globalization."

## The Thirteenth-Century Apogee (NCMH V)

[[abulafia-ncmh-v5-2000|NCMH V]] (Kathryn Reyerson, Ch. 3) treats the thirteenth century
as "the apogee of medieval economic expansion" — the brilliant "autumn of the Middle
Ages" before the famine of 1315–17 and the late-medieval crises. Her chapter adds
concrete texture to the Lopez thesis on three fronts:

- **Communications and roads.** A dense network of revived Roman roads, secondary
  routes, river systems (Rhine, Rhône, Po, Seine), and Alpine passes (Mont Cenis, the
  St Gotthard opened c. 1230) carried trade; travel from the Mediterranean coast to
  Paris took ~20–24 days by merchant caravan. The decisive innovation was the **opening
  of the Atlantic sea route** (the first Genoese galleys reaching Bruges, 1277, followed
  by the Majorcans), which slashed transport costs (London–Libourne by sea cost a
  seventh of the overland equivalent) and began to **bypass the Champagne fairs**.
- **The Champagne fairs.** Reyerson makes the six fairs of Troyes, Provins, Lagny, and
  Bar-sur-Aube — patronized by the counts of Champagne, later the kings of France — the
  "best laboratory" of medieval commerce: an annual ~50-day cycle that drew Flemish/
  northern cloth exporters, Italian caravan-merchants, and Genoese capitalists, with
  their own *lettres de foires*, wardens' courts, and law of *marque*. Their decline
  (the last Italians, the Piacenzans, leaving by 1320) came from royal fiscality, the
  Atlantic route, the rise of an integrated Italian cloth industry (Florence's *Arte
  della Lana* displacing the *Calimala*), and the Italians' shift to **permanent branch
  offices** in Bruges.
- **Financial instruments and mentality.** Reyerson catalogues the *commenda* and
  *societas* partnerships, the *cambium*/bill of exchange, sea loans, deposit banking,
  marine insurance (a Palermo contract of 1350), double-entry bookkeeping, and the
  merchant manuals (Pegolotti's *Pratica della mercatura*, c. 1340) — alongside the
  risk-embracing **merchant mentality** ("buy cheap and sell dear") embodied by figures
  like Benedetto Zaccaria (admiral, merchant, and pirate by turns) and the
  unlimited-liability super-companies of the **Bardi, Peruzzi, and Tolomei** whose
  bankruptcies close the period.

Reyerson's chapter also reinforces the volume's frontier theme: the Genoese reached the
Black Sea (Caffa, Trebizond) and the Venetians Alexandria and Tana, while Italian
merchants traded in **Mongol-era China** (Marco Polo) — an "ephemeral European access"
dependent on the unity of the Mongol empire and lost when it fragmented.

## Interaction

The Commercial Revolution was the European end of the [[afro-eurasian-world-system|
world system]], feeding off the [[silk-roads|Silk Roads]] and (via the Italians and
Egypt) the [[indian-ocean-trade|Indian Ocean]] luxury trades. Its credit techniques
and capital accumulation — together with the [[military-history-middle-millennium|
gunpowder]] and navigational advances of the period — formed the commercial
preconditions for the European oceanic expansion that opens the [[early-modern|Early
Modern]] era. It also reshaped European society and [[gender-history|gender roles]]
(the shift from bride-gift to dowry, the rise of mercantile patriciates).

## End Conditions

The process did not end but mutated: by 1500 the Mediterranean-centered system began
to be overtaken by the **Atlantic**, as Portuguese and Spanish navigation extended
European commerce around Africa and across the ocean — folding the medieval
commercial revolution into the genuinely global economy of
[[bentley-cwh-v6-2015|CWH VI]]. The early modern continuation — its business forms,
trade diasporas, finance, chartered companies, and the militarization of maritime
commerce — is treated at [[early-modern-commerce]]. Notably, CWH VI's contributors
(Trivellato, Parker, after Pomeranz) stress that the medieval commercial techniques
this page describes were **not uniquely European**: comparable institutions
(contracts, credit, brokerage, deposit banking) existed across Afro-Eurasia, so
Europe's later advantage owed more to New World resources and armed trade than to
superior commercial institutions (see [[great-divergence]]).

## The Fourteenth-Century Culmination and Contraction (NCMH VI)

[[jones-ncmh-v6-2000|NCMH VI]] (Peter Spufford, Ch. 7) treats the fourteenth century as a hinge:
the first half was "the fruition and culmination of the so-called 'commercial revolution' of the
long thirteenth century," with the European **money supply reaching its medieval maximum** around
mid-century; the second half saw "the beginnings of many of the changes which accelerated in the
fifteenth century." Two structural shifts stand out. First, the **decline of the Champagne fairs**:
by 1300 they were "largely places for settling accounts," as Italian and Tuscan merchants
established **permanent resident agents** in **Bruges, Paris and London** and transacted business
"more efficiently" than the periodic fairs allowed (successor fairs grew up on the navigable rivers
to serve the German interior the new system bypassed). Second, the [[late-medieval-crisis|crisis]]:
plague, war, and the failures of the great Florentine super-companies (the **Bardi and Peruzzi**
crashes of the 1340s, after Edward III's default) shook international finance, and a later
**bullion famine** contracted the money supply — the monetary underside of the late-medieval
downturn that Spufford made his special study.
