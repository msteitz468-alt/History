# The Spanish Economic Miracle (el desarrollo), 1959–1973

The fastest economic growth in Spanish history — roughly 7 percent annually through the 1960s, second in the world after Japan's ([[processes/japanese-economic-miracle|analogous_to]]) — triggered by the 1959 Stabilization Plan, which dismantled the autarkic controls of the 1940s–50s in favor of orthodox stabilization, trade opening, and convertibility, under the [[actors/opus-dei|Opus Dei]] technocrat ministers sponsored by [[actors/carrero-blanco-luis|Carrero Blanco]].

## Definition and Scope

Covers the policy shift (1957–59), the boom (1961–73), and its social transformation: mass rural exodus, industrialization, mass tourism (the single largest earner), emigrant remittances from workers in France/Germany/Switzerland, and foreign investment — the three external props Payne (1987) identifies as financing the chronic trade deficit. Ends with the 1973–75 oil crisis, which the terminal regime refused to confront.

## Causal Drivers and Phases

- Preceded and necessitated by the failure of autarky: the 1940s were "the years of hunger," with living standards not recovering 1935 levels until circa 1951–54; near-bankruptcy of reserves in 1957–59 forced the turn.
- Enabled by: European boom demand, OEEC/IMF sponsorship, US relationship post-1953.
- produced: a new urban middle-class consumer society, secularization, labor migration and a new (technically illegal but tolerated) workers' commissions movement — the social base that made the post-1975 democratic transition rapid; Payne treats this as the regime's central unintended consequence ([[events/death-of-franco-1975]]).

## Detail (Payne 1987)

**From autarky to stabilization.** Real GNP already grew 7.9%/year in 1951–58 on the shallow autarkic base, but by July 1959 four years of payments deficits brought the state to the edge of default. The Stabilization Plan (decreed 22 July 1959, backed by a $544m Washington loan): peseta devalued 42→60/$, 18 control agencies abolished, import licensing ended for ~half of imports, foreign-investment ceiling raised to 50%. Franco had "not the slightest confidence" in it and Carrero Blanco had urged "almost Stalinesque" intensified autarky in 1957; Navarro Rubio's technical and patriotic arguments won Franco over in days. Results were immediate: a $100m exchange surplus within five months; foreign investment $12m (1958) → $82.6m (1960); tourists doubled to six million by 1960.

**The boom.** Growth averaged 8.7%/year in 1961–64 and 7.2%/year over 1960–75 — highest in Europe, second worldwide to Japan; Spain reached 11th–12th industrial power, momentarily the world's fourth shipbuilder (1971). Tourism reached 34.6 million visitors and $3.09bn (1973), at times ≥9% of GNP; foreign investment 1960–74 exceeded $7.6bn (40%+ American); net emigration to northern Europe absorbed nearly a million workers, reversing to net return (−84,473) by 1974. Social transformation: agriculture fell from 40% of output (1951) to 13% (1970) and 22% of employment; half a million minifarms disappeared in a decade; by 1970, 40% of Spaniards lived in cities over 100,000. Per-capita income passed $2,000 by end-1973 (Japan's level of four years earlier); refrigerator ownership went from 4% of families (1960) to 87% (1975), TVs 1%→89%, cars 4%→48%; life expectancy 62.1 (1950) → 73.3 (1975); infant mortality fell below the Soviet Union's; social-security coverage rose from 29% to 79% of the population; after 1970 Spain for the first time spent more on education than on the armed forces. Persistent underside: the wealthiest 1.23% held 22.4% of national income (1970), more than the poorest 52% combined; INI became a bloated safety net (half its 4,000 executive posts political sinecures); featherbedded labor rules; strike-hours lost rose to 18.2 million (1974).

**Payne's causal framing (via Bernecker).** Distinguish developments the regime planned, those it merely exploited once underway, and those unforeseen and counterproductive to it: the 1959 turn belongs to the second category — liberal market economics "was not the type of development Franco preferred," and the growth of 1948–58 is often overlooked. The end came with the 1973 oil shock (69% oil-dependent, ~2% domestic), cushioned by price caps that delayed adjustment; inflation hit 18% by 1974.

## Interaction and End Conditions

Interacts with the regime's shift to performance legitimacy (Development Plans, "25 Years of Peace" campaign, 1964). Halted by the 1973 oil shock amid political paralysis; the transition governments inherited the resulting stagflation.
