# African Ironworking

The development and spread of iron-smelting technology in sub-Saharan Africa from roughly the early first millennium BCE, most famously associated with the [[nok-culture|Nok culture]] of central Nigeria (with iron-smelting evidence dated as early as c. 900–800 BCE at some sites), among the earliest well-documented iron industries in Africa outside contact with the Mediterranean or Near Eastern iron traditions.

## Definition and Scope

Whether African ironworking developed independently in one or more sub-Saharan centers, diffused from North Africa or the Nile Valley (Meroë), or reflects some combination of the two remains a genuinely open question in African archaeology, complicated by the still-incomplete radiocarbon chronology for early African smelting sites and the methodological difficulty of dating smelting furnaces reliably.

## Causal Drivers and Impact

Iron tools (axes, hoes) improved agricultural clearance and productivity in forest and savanna zones, supporting denser settled populations, while iron weapons altered the military balance among African societies. Most significantly, the spread of iron-working knowledge is closely tied to the [[bantu-expansion|Bantu expansion]], the demographic and linguistic spread of Bantu-speaking agricultural populations across Central, East, and Southern Africa over the following two millennia.

## Geographic Spread

From early centers in West Africa (Nok) and possibly independently in the African Great Lakes region and Nubia/Meroë, ironworking knowledge spread across the sub-Saharan interior largely in tandem with Bantu-speaking agricultural migration.

## Interaction with Other Processes

Tightly interwoven with the Bantu expansion and with the broader spread of settled agriculture across sub-Saharan Africa; iron tools and the Bantu demographic expansion are generally treated as mutually reinforcing rather than as a simple cause-and-effect sequence.
