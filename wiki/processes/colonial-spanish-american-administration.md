# Colonial Spanish American Administration

The structure of government through which the Castilian Crown ruled
[[colonial-latin-america|Spanish America]] for nearly three centuries — and, in
Burkholder and Johnson's [[burkholder-johnson-colonial-latin-america|account]], a
system whose defining trait was **not centralized absolutism but negotiated,
decentralized, "rooted" rule**. The Crowns opposed the rise of a hereditary
conquistador aristocracy and expected revenue; to that end they planted a
bureaucracy that, over time, became deeply enmeshed in local elite society.

## Definition and Scope

The Spanish imperial system is best pictured not as a pyramid but as **a set of
wheels** — each *audiencia* capital a hub, the Spanish court the hub of a wheel
whose spokes were the audiencias. Two structural facts shaped everything:

- **Time and distance.** A round-trip fleet took 14–15 months; word from Potosí
  to the Caribbean took weeks. This gave New World officials wide latitude — the
  famous formula **"*obedezco pero no cumplo*" ("I obey but I do not execute")** —
  and made overseeing them the Crown's central problem.
- **The refusal of the *cortes*.** The one peninsular institution the Crown would
  *not* transplant was a representative assembly; there was no colonial parliament
  to check royal authority.

## The institutions

- **The Council of the Indies** (1524) — the supreme governing body at court:
  legislative, judicial, financial, military, and ecclesiastical authority over the
  colonies, arranging the *residencia* (end-of-term judicial review) and *visita*
  (inspection), and exercising the [[spiritual-conquest-and-colonial-church|royal
  patronage]]. The **House of Trade (*Casa de Contratación*)** at Seville (1503)
  ran commerce and emigration.
- **The viceroyalties** — **New Spain** (created 1535, capital Mexico City; included
  the Caribbean and, after the 1570s, the Philippines) and **Peru** (early 1540s,
  capital Lima). No further viceroyalties until the eighteenth century. The
  **viceroy** (90+ in all, mostly high Spanish nobles, ~6–7 year terms) was the
  king's "living image."
- **The audiencias** — high courts that were also the **most important standing
  civil institution**: their lifetime-appointed *oidores* combined judicial,
  administrative, and legislative roles and resided for decades.
- **Provincial administrators** (*corregidores*, *alcaldes mayores*, *gobernadores*) —
  term appointees who extended royal authority into the countryside and over
  Indians; notorious for the **[[encomienda|*repartimiento de bienes*]]** (forced
  sale of goods to Indian communities on credit).
- **The municipality (*cabildo*)** — the cornerstone of settlement: the town council
  that distributed lots, ran public works and markets, and gave local elites a
  permanent base.

## The sale of offices

The single most consequential dynamic. After the Crown's **bankruptcy of 1557**,
Philip II extended the **sale of offices** from Castile to the Indies — first
municipal posts (saleable and inheritable from 1606), then treasury appointments
(1633), provincial posts (1677), *audiencia* seats (1687), and by 1700 even the
office of viceroy. The effects:

- It **raised the proportion of native sons (*criollos*) and *radicados*** (outsiders
  who had "put down roots") in office, since people preferred to serve at home.
- It **diminished royal control** — purchasers needed to recoup their investment,
  inviting extralegal income, and officials enmeshed with the local elite would not
  vigorously enforce unwelcome laws.
- Royal authority over colonial government fell to its **nadir around 1750** — the
  condition the [[bourbon-reforms|Bourbon reforms]] would try to reverse.

The case of the *corregidor* of Chancay (Peru) shows the logic: a five-year term
cost ~16,000 pesos for the office plus huge sums for fees and the *repartimiento*
of 1,900 mules — all ultimately recouped from the labor of ~1,125 Indian
households. Office was an investment paid for by the governed.

## Brazilian Counterpoint

Portuguese administration in Brazil developed **later, more slowly, and more
decentralized**. There was no Council-of-the-Indies equivalent until the
**Overseas Council (1642)**. The early **donatary captaincies** (hereditary grants
to twelve courtiers, 1530s) mostly failed, prompting the Crown to buy back **Bahia
(1549)** and appoint a **governor-general**. A high court (*relação*) came only in
**1609** (vs. the first Spanish audiencia of 1511). Crucially, **governorships were
not sold**, and Brazilian-born men rarely served in their home province — so the
officialdom was less "rooted" than its Spanish counterpart, though corruption was
equally typical.

## Interaction

Administration was inseparable from the [[spiritual-conquest-and-colonial-church|colonial
Church]] (which the Crown governed through the *patronato*), from the
[[global-silver-trade|silver economy]] (the fleet system, the *consulados*), and
from the labor regime (the *corregidor*'s *repartimiento de bienes*). Its
late-colonial dysfunction is the premise of the [[bourbon-reforms]].

## End Conditions

The Habsburg system "underwent little structural modification until the eighteenth
century." Its drift toward local capture — the sold, rooted, creolized bureaucracy
of c. 1700–1750 — was precisely what the [[bourbon-reforms|Bourbon reformers]] set
out to recentralize, with consequences for Creole loyalty that fed into
[[spanish-american-independence|independence]].
