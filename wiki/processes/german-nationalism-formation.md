## Definition and Scope

The formation of German nationalism covers the emergence of national consciousness, nationalist ideology, and organized nationalist movements in the German-speaking territories between approximately 1740 and 1850. Per Christian Jansen (ch. 11 of [[smith-oxford-modern-german-history-2011]]), this process followed a "discontinuous" trajectory: it moved "not … in a straight line, but in different waves marking temporary peaks and deep ruptures." The formation was complicated by structural ambiguity about which geographic or ideational entity nationalism should apply to (all of Germany? Prussia? Austria? the Confederation states?) and by intense political repression between peaks of mobilization.

The chapter's key analytical move is to distinguish three **ideal-type currents** of early German nationalism, and then three analytical categories running through to 1848:
- **Republican nationalism** (Zimmermann's Swiss-influenced tradition): citizenship, equality, law
- **Prussian / Kleindeutsch nationalism** (Abbt's tradition): dynastic loyalty fused with nationalist sentiment
- **Reich / Großdeutsch nationalism** (Moser's tradition): ethnic and cultural unity of all German-speakers

These map onto three later categories: **political** (constitution-focused), **ethnic** (descent and language-focused), and **historical** (territorial-continuity-focused) nationalism.

## Causal Drivers

### Structural Prerequisites
Jansen follows Benedict Anderson, Ernest Gellner, and Eric Hobsbawm in treating nations and nationalism as modern phenomena requiring specific structural conditions: modern statehood, demands for political participation, a public sphere, and widespread literacy. Pre-modern sentiment of "national" affiliation existed but lacked these foundations: "allegiances towards the ruler of a duchy or principality … were more important than national affiliations." The critical additional condition was "the inclusion offered by the concept of nationalism and nation states as a specifically modern feature based on civic equality" — impossible in feudal estate societies.

### Seven Years War (1756–1763) and Its Aftermath
Three texts published during and immediately after the Seven Years War mark the inauguration of modern German nationalist discourse:

1. **Johann Georg Zimmermann**, "On National Pride" (1758): Swiss-republican position. National pride as love of fatherland possible only in free republics where "only the law is sovereign." Acknowledged nationalism's dark side: "national hatred arises from the very same sources which create national pride … and it is based very often on equally imagined rights." Zimmermann represents what Jansen calls a "civil religion" version of nationalism.

2. **Thomas Abbt**, "On Death for the Fatherland" (1761): Prussian-monarchical counterpart. Fatherland as "ultimate value" overcoming fear of death. Abbt coined "topoi which have subsequently turned up time and again in radical nationalist texts," including metaphorical language of "blood" and the heroicization of the soldier's death ("a paradigm shift … one of the conditions for the militarization of civil societies in the nineteenth century"). The fatherland in Abbt's text was Prussia, not Germany — this is "one of the first examples of a current of nationalism in Germany that supported a Greater Prussia."

3. **Friedrich Karl von Moser**, "On the German National Spirit" (1765): Reich-nationalist position. Moser's opening sentence captures the program: "We are one people with one name and language … and yet … we still remain a puzzle as a political system, the prey of our neighbours." Moser anticipated concepts of the Sprachnation (language-unified people), Kulturnation (culture-unified people), and nation as Erinnerungsgemeinschaft (community of remembrance) — while remaining moderate in political recommendations (rulers should simply serve the common good). This represents the beginning of "constitutional patriotism."

### Herder and the Discovery of the Volk
Johann Gottfried Herder's reconceptualization of "Volk" in the 1770s–1780s is the most consequential intellectual development for the ethnic turn in German nationalism. Key elements:

- Herder elevated "Volk" from a pejorative term for lower classes to an emphatic positive concept
- He identified authentic national spirit in folksongs, dances, traditions, and customs — the ordinary people rather than court culture
- He equated language acquisition with natural affiliation: "individuals acquire their affiliation with a 'Volk' by means of the native language … during an early phase of their development"
- He characterized the German language as "pure and unmixed" since antiquity (ignoring actual migration history) — a claim later weaponized by Fichte
- He applied organological imagery to peoples, implying biological rather than political identity

Herder's influence spread beyond Germany: "nationalists from southern and eastern Europe consistently referred to Herder." Yet Jansen emphasizes that "many ideas of Herder and the Romantics were politically enhanced by the later nationalist discourses in a manner that did not accord with their original intentions."

The Romantic movement amplified Herder: collections of fairy tales (Grimm brothers, 1812–1814) and folk songs (Arnim and Brentano, Des Knaben Wunderhorn, 1805–1808) claimed to demonstrate "the innate creativity of the German people." The Grimms revised tales in successive editions, introduced harsh moral messages, promoted conservative family values and a "creative and enduring German people." Their claims to document authentic oral tradition have not worn well: at least one informant was of Huguenot descent; many sources were printed; texts were repeatedly revised.

### Anti-Napoleonic Wars (1813–1815)
The wars against Napoleon were the decisive catalyst for organized German nationalism as a social movement. Key developments:
- Ernst Moritz Arndt stylized the struggle as "wars of the people" — in practice exaggerating popular nationalist motivation (most opposition was material: Continental Blockade, taxation, looting, conscription)
- Friedrich Ludwig Jahn's gymnastic associations (Turnbewegung) provided organizational infrastructure and paramilitary training: 150 associations with 12,000 active members by 1818
- Student fraternities (Burschenschaften): first founded at Jena in June 1815 with 150 members; by 1818 the Allgemeine Deutsche Burschenschaft had ~4,000 members at 14 universities
- The Wartburg Festival (October 1817): 1,000+ organized nationalists staged symbolic book-burning of "un-German" texts; "explicitly nationalist organizations" staging the nation publicly

The content of this nationalism was already ethnically exclusive: Arndt published "anti-French hate lectures and anti-Semitic tirades." Jews, Catholics, and "cosmopolitans" were excluded from the community of good Germans. Jahn's key term Volksthum defined national character in quasi-biological terms: "the individualized collective notion of 'Volk' was meant in a concrete physical sense."

Two additional elements made this nationalism "more aggressive and also more appealing to the masses":
1. **Claims of German superiority**: Fichte ("Addresses to the German Nation," 1807–1808) characterized Germans as "primordial people" (Urvolk) barely corrupted by civilization; Hegel suggested the "coming era of world history would be the time of the Germanic peoples"
2. **Religious character**: Nationalism as ersatz religion — Körner called the anti-Napoleon war a "crusade"; choral and gymnastic festivals mimicked church liturgy; nationalist texts followed catechism structure

## Major Phases of Organized Nationalism

### Wave 1: 1811–1819
Gymnastic associations and fraternities reach peak membership; Wartburg Festival (1817); assassination of Kotzebue by a fraternity member (1819) triggers Carlsbad Decrees: strict censorship, ban on gymnastic associations and fraternities. "The (preliminary) end for organized nationalism after just a couple of years."

### Interlude: Crypto-Political Associations (1819–1830)
Repression forces nationalists into singing societies and gymnastic clubs operating under the guise of sociability. These spread the concept of the nation but with "vague political objectives."

### Wave 2: 1830–1832
July Revolution in France reinvigorates German liberalism and nationalism; Hambacher Fest (May 1832): "the biggest national festival before the Revolution with about 25,000 people participating" in southwest Germany. Again followed by strict persecution.

### Interlude: Expansion of Civil Society (1832–1848)
By the 1840s: almost 300 gymnastic associations (80,000–90,000 members); over 1,100 male choral societies (100,000+ members). Core area: southwest Germany (Württemberg, Baden, Rhenish Palatinate, Hesse). National festivals remained the key form of public expression. No permanent nationwide organization achieved; no confirmed reference to the nation-state before 1848.

### Wave 3: Revolutions of 1848–1849
[[revolutions-of-1848]] mobilize up to 1.5 million men in liberal and democratic societies (within German Confederation excluding Austria). The **Frankfurt Paulskirche Assembly** debates the German question publicly:
- Territorial maximalism: majority wanted German Confederation + Schleswig + East and West Prussia + most of Posen
- Three-power conception: France, Germany, Russia as the three great European civilizational blocs
- Ethnic vs. historical vs. political nationalism debated explicitly
- Poland debate (24–27 July 1848): motion for peaceful co-operation between nations defeated 342–31; majority accepted the inevitability of war to resolve national conflicts

**Ludwig Bamberger** (political-republican position): rejected both historical and ethnic justifications; defined national unity as "not an issue of nationality" but "our first revolutionary task … a political and administrative reform." Germany has "no history" as a political unit; unification must be justified politically, not mystically.

**Jakob Venedey** (ethnic position): advocated ethnic and linguistic borders, accepting loss of non-German territories; but his argument nonetheless invoked "blood baptism of Germanness" — showing the difficulty of maintaining a purely civic nationalism within ethnic discourse.

The revolution's failure left the ethnic-nationalist majority's program intact in aspiration but without institutional means of realization. The 1871 unification satisfied key ethnic-nationalist demands (Alsace-Lorraine recovered) but was achieved through Prussian military power rather than popular sovereignty.

## Analytical Conclusions (Jansen's Summary)

Six key conclusions:

1. German nationalism's origins should be located around **1740**, not 1800; but it became a social movement only around 1810, and achieved mass resonance in only three brief periods before 1850.

2. Romantic literary culture contributed "latent völkisch content" to nationalist discourse. Early nationalism was **anti-southern** rather than anti-western: enemies were France, Catholics, and Jews — all associated with Mediterranean/Roman civilization. Greeks (federalism, republicanism) were seen as a kindred model.

3. Political role models came from **western Europe**: Britain, Switzerland, USA, sometimes France and (from 1830) Belgium.

4. In 1848 it was **not yet decided** whether Germany would become one or several nation-states; political and pan-German ethnic nationalism frequently overlapped.

5. Three ideal types of nationalism (republican/political, ethnic/Großdeutsch, historical/Kleindeutsch) structured all subsequent debates.

6. The radicalism of German nationalism was not new after 1871; what changed was its **position within the system of rule** — from opposition movement to state-sponsored ideology operating through schools, military training, and government historical policy.

## Historiography

Jansen's chapter follows constructivist theories of nationalism (Anderson, Gellner, Hobsbawm) against primordialist accounts. It rejects the older historiographical division between "good" early liberal patriotism and "bad" later chauvinistic nationalism, arguing instead that all nationalism is "Janus-faced" — pursuing political emancipation while seeking power at others' expense. The chapter is notable for its emphasis on the early establishment of antisemitic and anti-Catholic exclusion within nationalist discourse — a corrective to narratives that situate these as later perversions of an originally liberal movement. The continuities between 1810s territorial fantasy and 1848 Paulskirche debates and 1870s Pan-German fantasies require "further research" per Jansen.
