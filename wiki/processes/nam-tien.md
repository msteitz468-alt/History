## Definition and Scope

Nam Tien ("southward advance") is the multi-century process by which Vietnamese-speaking polities expanded political and demographic control down the coast of what is now central and southern Vietnam, chiefly at the expense of [[champa|Champa]] and, in later centuries, Khmer Cambodia. This page covers the process through roughly 1500, its most decisive fifteenth-century phase; the process continued for another two-plus centuries under the autonomous Nguyen lords and the Nguyen dynasty (to be extended by later ingests covering the Trinh-Nguyen period and the Nguyen dynasty proper). Nam Tien is not a single planned campaign or policy but a long-run structural tendency, punctuated by decisive military episodes, sustained migration, and deliberate frontier settlement policy. It is not identical to any single dynasty's foreign policy — it recurred under the Ho, the early Le, and (as this page's temporal scope ends) was about to become the defining project of the autonomous southern Nguyen regime.

## Causal Drivers

- **Demographic and land pressure** in the Red River plain and Thanh Hoa, driving successive regimes to see southern territory as a release valve and a reward for military service.
- **Relative Cham weakness relative to Dai Viet**, especially after Champa's own internal fragmentation and repeated defeats (by Ho Quy Ly in the 1390s–1400s, by Le forces in 1446, decisively by Le Thanh Tong in 1471).
- **State strategy of channeling potentially destabilizing military manpower** — particularly the martial Thanh Hoa elite — into frontier campaigns rather than domestic factional competition; explicit in Le Thanh Tong's reign.
- **Deliberate settlement policy**: newly conquered territory was populated with categories of people the state wished to remove from the center — former Ming collaborators, unreliable upland chieftains, criminals, unsatisfactory royal slaves, resident Chams and Laotians, and officials being punished by reassignment.

## Major Phases (through c. 1500)

1. **Ho Quy Ly's annexation (1402)** — forced the Cham king to cede what are now Quang Nam and Quang Ngai provinces; Vietnamese officials, settlers, and Cham militia units under Vietnamese officers were installed. Reversed during the Ming occupation, when Champa reoccupied the territory.
2. **Post-Ming reassertion (1446)** — an early Le expedition (with generals Le Kha, Le Thu, and Le Khac Phuc) took the Cham capital of Vijaya and installed a vassal king, but the arrangement collapsed within a few years as relations deteriorated.
3. **The 1471 conquest of Vijaya** — see [[conquest-of-vijaya-1471|Conquest of Vijaya]]. The decisive turning point: permanent annexation of Quang Nam, Quang Ngai, and Binh Dinh as Quang Nam Province, and the fragmentation of Champa into weaker vassal successor states (Phan Rang, the Central Highlands, and the coast near modern Phu Yen/Khanh Hoa).
4. **Consolidation and settlement (1471–1474 and after)** — systematic population of the new province with marginal groups and punished officials; regulation of Cham-Vietnamese naming practices (1472) and a schedule of banishment for criminals to the frontier (1474), integrating the new territory into normal Le administrative categories (unhealthy/frontier assignment, alongside the northern border provinces).

5. **The Nguyen lords into the Mekong (17th–18th c.)** — under the [[nguyen-lords|Nguyen lords]], expansion
   shifted from Cham conquest to the Khmer Mekong delta, and from plunder-raids to **marriage alliance,
   settler colonization, and client-prince patronage** (the delta had no natural defensive terrain). A 1620
   royal marriage secured tax rights at Prei Nokor and Kampong Krabei — the origin of Vietnamese **Saigon**;
   **Gia Dinh province** was formally established in 1698; Ming Chinese refugee fleets were settled as
   military auxiliaries at Bien Hoa, My Tho, and Ha Tien (from 1679). Champa was formally annexed as Binh
   Thuan in 1693. Repeated interventions in Khmer succession disputes partitioned Cambodia into
   Vietnamese- and [[siam-chakri-dynasty|Siamese]]-backed factions (see [[khmer-empire]]). By Nguyen Cu
   Trinh's campaigns of 1754–1758 "the Khmer-Viet border was drawn more or less as it exists today."
6. **Nguyen dynasty capstone (1802–1840)** — [[gia-long|Gia Long]]'s **Vinh Te Canal** (1819–1824), dug by
   tens of thousands of Khmer and Vietnamese labourers, linked Ha Tien to Chau Doc and fixed what is today
   the Cambodia–Vietnam border. [[minh-mang|Minh Mang]]'s **Six Provinces (Luc Tinh) reorganization of
   1832** ended the semi-autonomous Saigon viceroyalty and imposed full provincial administration over the
   delta; his **1832 abolition of the Cham tributary kingdom** in Binh Thuan closed out the last nominally
   autonomous Cham polity.
7. **Overreach into Cambodia and reversal (1836–1841)** — Minh Mang's effective annexation of Cambodia
   behind the figurehead "Queen" Ang Mei (1836) pushed *nam tien* beyond ethnic-Vietnamese territory into a
   non-Vietnamese vassal kingdom. Assimilationist pressure and a Siamese offensive provoked Khmer uprisings
   that forced Vietnamese withdrawal by 1841 — the furthest and least durable extension of the process.

## Geographic Spread

By the end of this phase, Vietnamese state control extended from the Red River plain through Thanh Hoa, Nghe An, and the former Cham heartland down to and including Binh Dinh — roughly the central Vietnamese coast as far as the Hai Van Pass and beyond it to Quang Nam/Quang Ngai/Binh Dinh. Cham successor polities persisted further south (Phan Rang and beyond) and in the Central Highlands, not to be absorbed until later centuries under the Nguyen lords.

## Interaction

Nam Tien interacted directly with internal Vietnamese dynastic politics: it functioned as a safety valve for the ambitions of the Thanh Hoa military elite whose domestic factionalism repeatedly destabilized the Le court (see [[le-dynasty-vietnam|Le dynasty]]). It also set the stage geographically and demographically for the later autonomous southern regime of the Nguyen lords, whose base of power (from Nguyen Hoang's 1558 governorship of Thuan Hoa) lay precisely in territory secured by this fifteenth-century phase of expansion, and whose accumulating regional distinctiveness fed directly into the [[seventy-years-war-vietnam-1533-1592|Seventy Years War]] and the later Trinh-Nguyen division of the country.

## End Conditions

The fifteenth-century phase reached a stable equilibrium — administered Vietnamese provincial territory
(Quang Nam) bordering fragmented Cham rump states — that held for roughly a century before renewed southward
pressure resumed under the [[nguyen-lords|Nguyen lords]]. The process as a whole terminated in the early
nineteenth century: with the Mekong delta fully absorbed and reorganized into [[minh-mang|Minh Mang]]'s Six
Provinces (1832) and the last Cham kingdom abolished (1832), Vietnamese settlement had reached the natural
limit of the delta. The attempt to carry expansion further — the direct annexation of Cambodia (1836) —
overreached into non-Vietnamese territory and was reversed by 1841 amid Khmer revolt and
[[siam-chakri-dynasty|Siamese]] war. The Cambodia–Vietnam border fixed by the Vinh Te Canal and the 1847
peace has essentially held since.
