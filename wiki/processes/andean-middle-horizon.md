## Definition and Scope

The Middle Horizon (c. CE 550–1000) is the term archaeologists use for a phase of interregional integration across the Central Andes during which two dominant polities — [[wari]] (centred at Ayacucho, modern Peru) and [[tiwanaku]] (centred at the southern basin of [[lake-titicaca]]) — spread characteristic urban forms, iconographic canons, and administrative practices across much of the Andean world. Lumbreras defines it precisely: "The first part of this process is known as the Middle Horizon (c. C.E. 560–1000); the term 'horizon' signals the spread of a typical culture and social organizations across multiple regions."

The Middle Horizon is bounded at the start by the sixth-century environmental crisis and the near-simultaneous rise of Wari and Tiwanaku, and at the end by the collapse of Wari's imperial apparatus and the decline of Tiwanaku (tenth to eleventh centuries CE), which ushered in the [[post-wari-reorganization]].

The two components are qualitatively different:
- **[[wari]]** was a militaristic, manufacturing-and-redistribution empire operating through conquest, road networks, and planned administrative cities imposed on the sierra and coast of Peru from Cajamarca in the north to Cusco in the south.
- **[[tiwanaku]]** was a pastoral-agricultural polity whose expansion followed a logic of "interzonal complementarity" — colonising lower-altitude valleys (Moquegua, Cochabamba) for maize and coca, and linking desert oases (San Pedro de Atacama) through llama-caravan exchange rather than direct military occupation.

Scope: primarily the Central and South-Central [[andes]], covering modern Peru, Bolivia, northern Chile, and northwestern Argentina.

## Causal Drivers

**Environmental crisis.** "The mid-sixth century witnessed an era of serious climatic alterations in the Andes … rains fell almost 30 percent below normal levels for about 30 years, between the years C.E. 562 and 594." This stress disrupted existing polities and created conditions for new political formations, though Lumbreras is explicit that the rise of Wari and Tiwanaku were "not direct consequences of such natural events."

**Structural economic pressures in Ayacucho (Wari's origin zone).** The pre-Wari Huarpa subsistence relied on "polycyclical" dry-land farming of limited productive yield. This "structural insufficiency" made autarchic community life precarious. Wari's solution was diversified manufacturing (ceramics, textiles, lapidary work) and redistributive imperialism: "A guarantee of sustained stability … was only possible through a redistributive system, managed from the promoting center … legitimated by a state, and reinforced by a powerful military apparatus and a solid religious base."

**Tiwanaku's lake-basin agricultural logic.** The altiplano around [[lake-titicaca]] required management of floods and droughts through raised-field agriculture (*camellones* or *waru waru*), centralized calendric planning, and access to ecological zones lacking on the high plain. Tiwanaku's temple-like urban centres served agricultural-planning functions: "The religious success of such installations … derived more from their effectiveness as guides to production than from myth or popular devotion."

**Ideological syncretism.** Both polities drew on a shared iconographic heritage (the staff-bearing deity traceable to [[chavin]] and Pukara) to legitimise power. Wari actively imported Tiwanaku craftsmen and iconography early in its formation, then developed its own "lay" (palace-centred rather than temple-centred) urban model.

## Major Phases

**Phase 1 — Precursor formation (fourth–sixth centuries CE).** Both Wari and Tiwanaku develop from pre-existing formations: Huarpa villages in Ayacucho; Pukara and Chiripa on the Titicaca basin. Polychrome ceramic contacts between Ayacucho and Nasca are noted as an early marker.

**Phase 2 — Middle Horizon 1A–1B: Wari expansion (c. 560–c. 800 CE).** Wari constructs Tiwanaku-style buildings at its capital, initiates conquest southward to Cusco and Cotahuasi, northward to Cajamarca. Planned orthogonal cities are built: Pikillaqta near Cusco, Viracochapampa in Huamachuco, Cerro Baúl in Moquegua. Simultaneously Tiwanaku reaches its "classic" and "decadent" phases (IV–V), expanding via colonisation into Moquegua, Cochabamba, and Atacama. "A separate though culturally related and simultaneous [expansion] moved from Wari south toward Cusco and also north through various territories including parts of the coast."

**Phase 3 — Middle Horizon 2: Consolidation and regional differentiation (c. 800–c. 900 CE).** Post-Wari coastal polities (Sican/Lambayeque, the "White-Black-Red State" between Chicama and Casma) grow independently, using Wari urban models without direct Wari control. Tiwanaku continues at its apogee. The road network and khipu system are now operative tools of the Wari state.

**Phase 4 — Middle Horizon 3–4: Epigonal and decline (c. 900–c. 1000 CE).** "The third and fourth phases showed local offshoots … but all associated with the Wari expansion." Post-Wari "epigonal" tricolor ceramics spread; polychrome techniques degrade as access to Wari pigment supply chains collapses. The city of Wari "stopped functioning in the 2B period, leaving large new buildings that remained partially completed." Tiwanaku declines from the tenth century onward, possibly exacerbated by renewed drought (Ortloff and Kolata).

## Geographic Spread

**Wari zone of direct control:** the sierra from Cajamarca south to Cusco; the Pacific coast valleys from Lima (Cajamarquilla) to Nasca (Pacheco) and Ica; intrusions at Moquegua (Cerro Baúl) on the western Andean slope. "Forcible Wari expansion throughout a vast space ranging from Cusco to Cajamarca."

**Tiwanaku zone:** "the extreme southern coast of Peru, from Arequipa to Tacna, northern Chile from Arica to Atacama, the valleys of Cochabamba and the southern altiplano, including Oruro and part of Potosí, with less defined connections toward Chuquisaca, Tarija, and the Quebrada de Humahuaca and the Calchaquí valleys in Argentina."

**Overlap and interface:** Moquegua was contested — Tiwanakan farmers occupied the valley before Wari arrived; "Cerro Baúl is a strongly protected place, fortified with ramparts … Relations with surrounding Tiwanakan populations must not have been peaceful."

## Interaction with Other Processes

The Middle Horizon directly precedes and shapes [[post-wari-reorganization]]: the urban models, road infrastructure, khipu record-keeping, and administrative vocabulary (mita, yana) that Wari pioneered were inherited and amplified by the [[inca-empire]] in the Late Horizon. Lumbreras states: "Many of its elements were later taken up by Tawantinsuyu, the Inka state."

The process interacts with [[andean-state-formation]] as its pivotal chapter: the Middle Horizon is the first moment at which genuinely state-level institutions (planned administrative cities, road networks, standardised iconography serving imperial ideology, coercive labour mobilisation) operate at interregional scale in the Andes.

Tiwanaku's model of "vertical archipelago" complementarity — establishing productive "islands" at lower altitudes, connected by llama caravans — was preserved through the Late Intermediate by the [[aymara-kingdoms]] and was directly institutionalised by the Inka.

## End Conditions

Wari collapsed from within around the tenth century CE; its capital was abandoned with large buildings left uncompleted, suggesting sudden disruption rather than gradual decline. "The Wari empire began to decline around the tenth century C.E. … many groups seem to have fought their way loose of domination." The coastal polities it had stimulated continued to grow; the sierra reverted to village-level communal life.

Tiwanaku declined from the tenth to eleventh centuries, likely compounded by prolonged drought (abandonment of raised-field agriculture beginning in the twelfth or thirteenth century). The altiplano Aymara-speaking señoríos that emerged (Qulla, Lupaqa; see [[aymara-kingdoms]]) represented a pastoral adaptation, inheriting Tiwanaku territorial networks while abandoning monumental urbanism.

The end of the Middle Horizon inaugurated the [[post-wari-reorganization]] and set the regional conditions from which the [[inca-empire]] eventually emerged.

## Historiography

The history of scholarship on the Middle Horizon begins with Max Uhle's misidentification of Wari-style coastal artefacts as "Coastal Tiahuanaco" (early twentieth century). Bennett's 1932 excavations at Tiwanaku and his later work established a ceramic-based four-phase chronology. The fundamental reinterpretation — separating Wari and Tiwanaku as two distinct but simultaneous expansions — was achieved progressively from the 1930s–1960s (Bennett, Menzel's "Style and time in the Middle Horizon," 1964). Scholars debated whether the Middle Horizon represented (1) commercial networks, (2) a proselytising religious movement, or (3) direct political empire. Lumbreras argues for the third, citing "extensive information that has come to light in the past two decades" supporting "forcible Wari expansion." The Tiwanaku component has been reassessed by Kolata (*The Tiwanaku*, 1993) and Goldstein's Moquegua studies as a different kind of polity — colonial-agricultural rather than militaristic.

Primary evidence is almost entirely archaeological; documentary sources (sixteenth-century Spanish records) bear on the Late Intermediate that followed, not on the Middle Horizon itself. Dating is based on ceramic seriation and radiocarbon; chronological boundaries carry uncertainty of decades to a century.
