# Group Feeling and Power: The Asabiyyah–Mulk Dynamic

## Definition and Scope

The **asabiyyah–mulk dynamic** is the core political process in [[ibn-khaldun|Ibn Khaldun's]] science of civilization: the mechanism by which group solidarity ([[asabiyyah]]) among desert or tribal peoples generates leadership, then royal authority ([[royal-authority-mulk-ibn-khaldun|mulk]]), then the conditions that destroy that authority, preparing the way for the next cycle. This process is analyzed in depth in [[the-muqaddimah|the Muqaddimah]], particularly in Chapters II and III, where Ibn Khaldun demonstrates it through dozens of examples drawn from North African, Andalusian, and Near Eastern history.

This is not an abstract theory but a process Ibn Khaldun derived from direct observation of political cycles across the Islamic West and from his reading of Islamic historiography. He explicitly states that "the inhabitants of Spain especially have forgotten group feeling and its influence" because so long a time has passed — and this observation drives him to theorize why and how group feeling is generated, deployed, and lost.

## Causal Drivers

### Desert Life and Shared Hardship

Group feeling is not primarily a matter of blood kinship, though common descent reinforces it. Its deepest source is shared material conditions: "long familiarity, and the companionship that results from growing up together, having the same wet nurse, and sharing the other circumstances of death and life." Pastoral and desert life produces these conditions in their most intense form, because survival depends on mutual defense.

"Desert people need each other for their defense and for the repulsion of the enemy raids. Their solidarity is necessary in their homeland, too, since it is a place of danger and fear." This necessity makes asabiyyah a natural product of environment.

### Genealogical Reinforcement

Common descent strengthens the bonds of group feeling because "the consequences of common descent, though natural, still are something imaginary." The psychological reality of solidarity based on supposed blood ties is functionally equivalent to the real solidarity based on shared experience — in both cases, what matters is the *belief* in common cause and the willingness to act on it. When client relationships predate a dynasty's rise to power, they acquire the same emotional force as kinship.

### Religious Calling as Amplifier

Ibn Khaldun identifies religious propaganda (*da'wa*) as a *multiplier* of existing group feeling, not a substitute for it. A religious call can transform a merely strong group into an irresistible one, because it eliminates mutual jealousy within the group and produces willingness to die without hesitation:

> "Religious propaganda gives a dynasty at its beginning another power in addition to that of the group feeling it possessed as the result of the number of its supporters... Religious coloring does away with mutual jealousy and envy among people who share in a group feeling, and causes concentration upon the truth. When people who have a religious coloring come to have the right insight into their affairs, nothing can withstand them, because their outlook is one and their object one of common accord. They are willing to die for their objectives." (*Muqaddimah*, Ch. III, §5)

The early Muslim conquests are the paradigm: 30,000 Muslim soldiers at Qadisiyyah defeated 120,000 Persians and 400,000 Byzantines at the Yarmuk not through numbers but through the supercharged group feeling that Islam provided to Arab tribal solidarity. Similarly, the Almohads defeated the Almoravids because their religious calling (the Mahdist movement of Ibn Tumart) doubled the effective strength of their Masmudah group feeling.

However — and this is critical to Ibn Khaldun's argument — religious propaganda **cannot** materialize without prior group feeling: "God sent no prophet who did not enjoy the protection of his people." Religious reformers who attempt to establish truth without group feeling behind them perish: they are "either treated as insane, or punished by execution or beatings when they cause trouble, or ridiculed and treated as buffoons." Ibn Khaldun catalogs several such failed reformers in Spain and the Maghrib as illustrations.

## Major Phases

### Phase 1: Formation of Group Feeling (Desert / Pastoral Stage)

A group living under conditions of shared danger — desert, steppe, mountain, or frontier — develops mutual affection and willingness to fight and die for each other. This is the raw material. At this stage, glory is *shared*: all members of the group participate equally in defense, raiding, and collective decision-making. Frugality and toughness characterize the group's life.

Within the group, one lineage with the highest claim to descent or the greatest demonstrated leadership begins to emerge as *primus inter pares*. The group feeling of this lineage is superior to the others, and it gradually draws the other group feelings under its umbrella: "One of the various tribal group feelings must be superior to all others, in order to be able to bring them together, to unite them, and to weld them into one group feeling comprising all the various groups."

### Phase 2: Achievement of Leadership (Pre-Royal Stage)

The dominant lineage produces a leader who embodies and focuses the collective group feeling. At this stage he still shares glory with his group — he serves as a "model to his people by the manner in which he acquires glory, collects taxes, defends property, and provides military protection. He does not claim anything exclusively for himself to the exclusion of his people, because such an attitude is what is required by group feeling."

This pre-royal leadership is the most politically effective stage. The group fighting as a unit for shared glory under a recognized leader is nearly unstoppable against defenders of an existing polity who have lost their own group feeling through luxury. Numerical disadvantages are overcome by cohesion and willingness to die.

### Phase 3: Conquest and Royal Authority

When the group defeats an existing polity and takes possession of its territory, royal authority crystallizes. The dominant leader now concentrates glory — he "develops the quality of egotism (*ta'alluh*), which is innate in human beings," excludes rivals, and takes full control. The shared group feeling that produced the conquest begins to be replaced by the ruler's monopoly.

The territory of a new dynasty is proportionate to the numerical strength of its group: "The more numerous the tribes and groups of a large dynasty are, the stronger and larger are its provinces and lands." A small group can only fill a small territory; when its members are distributed across border regions as garrisons, their numbers are exhausted, and expansion stops. "The dynasty's strength is at its center; at its border regions it is like light rays that spread from their centers."

### Phase 4: Sedentary Transition and Luxury

Victory brings prosperity. Prosperity brings sedentary life. Sedentary life brings luxury. This transition is *structurally necessary*, not a moral failure: "the sedentary stage of royal authority follows the stage of desert life of necessity, as a result of the fact that royal authority is of necessity accompanied by a life of ease."

The dynasty inherits and adopts the cultural apparatus of the polity it has replaced — food, clothing, building, music, court ritual. Each generation surpasses the previous one in refinement. Meanwhile, the desert toughness and shared frugality that made the original group formidable erodes: "They forget the customs of desert life that enabled them to achieve royal authority, such as great energy, the habit of rapacity, and the ability to travel in the wilderness and find one's way in waste regions."

### Phase 5: Erosion and Dissolution

By the third generation, group feeling has dissolved. The ruler now:
- Relies on clients, mercenaries, and followers rather than on the original tribe
- Faces increasing demands from luxury-habituated subjects that drain the treasury
- Loses military effectiveness as soldiers grow soft and unwilling to sacrifice themselves
- Excludes his own group from power, driving them into enmity

The ruler's increasing reliance on foreign followers (clients, mercenaries, slave soldiers) to suppress the now-hostile members of his original tribe is the signature symptom of dissolution. The Abbasid reliance on Turkish guards, the Fatimid use of Armenian and Sudanese troops, the use of Zanatah by Andalusian rulers — all follow this pattern.

At this point, the dynasty is vulnerable. A fresh group from the desert or frontier, carrying strong new group feeling, can conquer it despite being far less numerous. The cycle begins again with new actors.

## Geographic Spread

Ibn Khaldun presents the dynamic as universal — applicable to all dynasties he knows. He explicitly tests it against: Arab conquests of Persia and Byzantium; Berber dynasties in the Maghrib (Sinhajah, Almohads, Almoravids, Zanatah); Andalusian political history from the Umayyads through the reyes de taifas and Ibn al-Ahmar; Abbasid decline; Turkish military power; Mongol conquests. He regards it as a general law of civilization, not a peculiarity of any one region.

However, he notes that the cycle operates more visibly in areas with strong tribal geography — North Africa, Arabia, the steppe — than in long-settled urban societies like Egypt, where the absence of competing group feelings makes dynasties more stable: "It is easy to establish a dynasty in lands that are free from group feelings. Government there will be a tranquil affair, because seditions and rebellions are few."

## End Conditions

The asabiyyah–mulk dynamic does not end — it is cyclical. Each dynasty's dissolution creates the conditions for the next group-feeling mobilization. Ibn Khaldun explicitly denies that this cycle can be broken from within: "There can be no hope it will recover from that illness." A dynasty cannot re-grow the group feeling it has lost through luxury.

The only partial remedy Ibn Khaldun acknowledges is the deliberate recruitment of fresh "tough" groups as military slaves or mercenaries (the Mamluk model), which can extend a dynasty's life without reversing the underlying erosion. "This could prove a cure for the senility of the dynasty when it comes, until God permits His command regarding the dynasty to be executed."

## Historiography

Ibn Khaldun's theory was largely ignored in the Arabic scholarly tradition after his death but was rediscovered by European Orientalists in the nineteenth century, particularly through Silvestre de Sacy and later the translation project of Franz Rosenthal (1958). Modern social scientists have variously described it as an anticipation of Ibn Khaldun as the first sociologist, as a proto-Marxist analysis of class conflict between pastoral and urban modes of production, or as a cyclical theory comparable to Giambattista Vico.

The Rosenthal translation — used here — renders *asabiyyah* as "group feeling," which captures the social-structural rather than purely ethnic dimension. Alternative renderings include "group solidarity" (Hodgson), "esprit de corps," and simply the untranslated Arabic term.

Historians of the Islamic world have found the framework most useful for the Maghrib and the steppe, less applicable to settled agrarian empires where group feeling plays a smaller role in political transitions.
