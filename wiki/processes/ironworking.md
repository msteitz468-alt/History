# Ironworking (Iron Metallurgy)

**Ironworking** — the smelting and forging of iron into tools and weapons — spread across
Afro-Eurasia beginning around 1200 BCE, displacing bronze as the dominant metal for
utilitarian objects over the following centuries and giving the [[periods/early-iron-age|
Early Iron Age]] its name. Iron ore is far more widely available than the tin needed for
bronze (tin sources were geographically scarce and dependent on long-distance trade
networks disrupted by the [[events/bronze-age-collapse-1200bce|Bronze Age Collapse]]),
making iron tools and weapons cheaper and more accessible once smelting and carburizing
(steeling) techniques matured — though early iron implements were often inferior to
well-made bronze until smithing technique improved.

## Spread and Effects

Iron technology likely originated or was refined among Anatolian/Hittite smiths before
diffusing outward through the eastern Mediterranean and Near East after the Bronze Age
collapse, while sub-Saharan Africa developed independent iron-smelting traditions from an
early date, debated among archaeologists as to whether wholly independent or influenced by
external contact. Cheaper, more widely producible iron tools are frequently linked by
historians to agricultural intensification (heavier ploughs, more efficient clearance of
forest for farmland) and to changes in warfare — larger armies could be equipped with iron
weapons than the bronze supply chain had ever allowed (see
[[concepts/iron-age-warfare]]) — contributing to the wider social, demographic, and urban
transformations associated with the [[axial-age|Axial Age]].
