# Convict Transportation to Australia

Between **1788 and 1868**, Britain transported roughly **160,000 convicts** to its
Australian colonies, beginning with the First Fleet to [[botany-bay|Botany Bay]] /
[[sydney|Sydney]] and ending with the last shipment to Western Australia. Transportation
supplied the coerced labour that built [[new-south-wales-colony|New South Wales]] and
[[van-diemens-land|Van Diemen's Land]], and shaped the social character of colonial
Australia.

[[bashford-macintyre-cha-v1-2013|CHA Vol. 1]] sets the system in **global context**: the
Botany Bay decision of 1786–88 followed the loss of the American colonies (which had
absorbed British convicts), and was a node in a wider eighteenth-century world of penal
labour, empire, and global strategy rather than a purely punitive expedient.

## Definition and Scope

Transportation was both a punishment and a labour and settlement system: convicts were
assigned to government works or to private masters, could earn tickets of leave and
pardons, and many became emancipist settlers. It is **not** synonymous with slavery, but
shared coercion, unfreedom, and forced migration.

## From CHA Vol. 1 (Ch 3–4): Global Context, Assignment System, and the Convict Economy

### Global Context to 1788 (Ch. 3, Christopher and Maxwell-Stewart)

Transportation was not invented for Australia. British courts had sentenced convicts to
transportation since **1615**; roughly **50,000 felons** were shipped to Virginia and
Maryland between 1718 and 1775, sold into colonial servitude for the length of their
sentence. The system was a variant of indenture: the state disposed of the convict's body
by transferring property rights to a contractor. Sentence lengths (minimum seven years)
were fixed "not for legal reasons, but in order to position convicts competitively within
the transatlantic market in unfree labour" — longer terms offsetting convicts' lower
attractiveness relative to indentured servants.

The **American Revolution** (1776–83) closed this market abruptly. The hulks
(dismasted warships, authorised by the Hulk Act 1776) became the stopgap. After
failure with west Africa (1775–86, catastrophic mortality; 344 of 746 convict soldiers
died) and Das Voltas Bay (declared "doomed to everlasting sterility"), the Pitt
administration announced Botany Bay in August 1786. See [[botany-bay-decision]] for the
full historiographical debate.

By the time Australia was selected, the structural context made it near-inevitable:
racialisation of unfreedom meant British/Irish convicts could not be sold into plantation
labour (the Caribbean was "amply supplied with slaves"); India had its own convict supply;
and the Atlantic indenture market was collapsing anyway with rising free-migrant wages.

### The Assignment System (Ch. 4, Karskens)

The assignment system — the most important institution of convict labour management — was
born in **1792** when Acting Governor Grose began granting land to fellow officers and
assigning ten convicts each, on full government rations, to work those grants. From this
improvisation arose the dominant mode of convict labour: convicts still under sentence
were assigned to private masters who provided their keep in exchange for their labour,
while the government retained ultimate disciplinary authority.

The system had profound social effects. Officers acquiring 100-acre grants (later 1,000–
1,500 acres for men and women "of substance") used assigned convicts as their workforce.
By 1821, free settlers and officers (17 per cent of landholders) held **57 per cent** of
alienated land; ex-convicts held the remaining 43 per cent, farming smaller plots.

Convicts in assignment were not uniform victims. Those with skills were "treated not
according to their crimes, but according to their abilities and usefulness." Those in
government work gangs insisted on **task work**: once government tasks were complete they
could work for private masters at market wages — three shillings/day for unskilled, five
shillings for skilled labour (women paid much less). This created a vigorous private
convict economy.

### Emancipist Society and the Convict Economy 1788–1822 (Ch. 4)

The colony the British government planned — an isolated subsistence agricultural penal
camp — did not materialise. Within years, [[sydney]] had become "a thriving, polyglot
port town, alive with people, ships and goods from all over the globe." Key features:

**Emancipist traders:** Ex-convicts like **James Underwood**, **Simeon Lord**, and
**Mary Reibey** built trading networks that outscaled the government economy. Simeon Lord
alone had running accounts with London brokers of £102,120 between 1804 and 1810,
compared to Governor King's total treasury drawings of £87,447 over his entire
governorship (1800–06).

**Legal status:** Convicts in New South Wales were not legally attainted (as they would
have been in England), so they could use civil courts to enforce contracts, defend
property, and establish businesses. Courts also upheld women's property rights
independent of husbands. This enabled an unusually capable female commercial class
among emancipists.

**Monetary system:** No money was sent with the First Fleet; a sophisticated monetary
system emerged from "mutual obligations…book accounts, bills and notes," including
promissory notes and, from 1809–10, small merchant's notes.

**Export industries:** The first successful exports came from the sea, not the land.
Sealing at Bass Strait (from 1798) and whaling (from 1791) were the founding export
industries; only later did wool emerge under pastoralists like Macarthur and Marsden.

**Grain and the agricultural decades:** Cereal crops constituted an estimated 40 per cent
of the colonies' gross domestic product to c.1822, while pastoralism contributed only
14 per cent in NSW and 19 per cent in Van Diemen's Land. By 1804, the colony was
broadly self-sufficient in grain, though floods, droughts, and gluts created recurring
instability.

### The Bigge Report and the End of the Convicts' Colony (1823)

Commissioner John Thomas Bigge, sent by the British government in 1819 to review
transportation's effectiveness, concluded the colony's future lay not with small farming
by ex-felons but with large-estate grazing worked by assigned convicts, granted free to
men of fortune. His 1823 report marked the effective end of the founding vision, though
the practice of granting land to ex-convicts ended formally only in 1825.
