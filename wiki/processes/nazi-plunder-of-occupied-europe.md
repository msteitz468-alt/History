# Nazi Plunder of Occupied Europe

## Definition and Scope

The systematic economic spoliation of German-occupied Europe, 1939–45: seizure of national-bank gold and foreign holdings, punitive "occupation costs," forced credits, levies in kind, confiscation of land and enterprises, and organized art looting. It is distinct from (but interlocked with) the [[forced-labor-nazi-germany|slave-labor program]] — the plunder of goods versus the plunder of people — and from battlefield requisition: this was policy, ordered from the top. Göring to his subordinates: "Whenever you come across anything that may be needed by the German people, you must be after it like a bloodhound"; and to the eastern commissioners (6 Aug 1942): "It used to be called plundering… I intend to plunder and to do it thoroughly" (Shirer 1960, ch. 27).

## Causal Drivers

- War-economy necessity: financing total war without domestic austerity on the WWI pattern.
- Ideology: [[lebensraum]] framed the East as colonial extraction zone; Hans Frank on Poland (3 Oct 1939 directive): "Poland shall be treated as a colony. The Poles shall be the slaves of the Greater German Reich."
- Personal avarice at the top: Hitler's planned Linz museum and Göring's private collection (self-valued at 50 million RM) drove the art-looting apparatus — spoliation with no war-economic rationale.

## Major Phases

1. **1939–40, Poland**: immediate colony-style extraction; confiscation decrees covering Polish as well as Jewish property; by 31 May 1943, ~700,000 estates (15m acres) "seized" plus 9,500 (6.5m acres) "confiscated" in the annexed districts.
2. **1940–42, the West**: occupation assessments — France paid 31.5 billion RM (annual tribute over four times Weimar's Dawes/Young reparations payments), plus 4.5bn in forced Bank of France "credits"; an estimated two-thirds of Belgian and Dutch national income extracted. Levies in kind from France: 9m tons of cereals, 75% of oat production, 80% of oil, 74% of steel (184.5bn francs total). Art: the Einsatzstab Rosenberg; Göring's 5 Nov 1940 Louvre distribution order (Führer's choice first, his own collection second); 137 freight cars with 21,903 objects including 10,890 paintings shipped west-to-Reich by July 1944; Rosenberg valued the French art loot at 1bn RM as early as January 1941. Göring, when a staff expert cited the Hague Convention: "I am the highest jurist in the state."
3. **1941–44, the USSR**: plunder married to deliberate starvation — Göring's Economic Staff East directive (23 May 1941) ordered industrial regions left to starve and prohibited food relief from the black-soil zone (see [[hunger-plan-1941]]). Yet extraction disappointed: net Soviet "deliveries" were reckoned at only ~4bn RM; Alexander Dallin's judgment (endorsed by Shirer) is that normal trade would have yielded Germany more.
4. **1943–45, intensification and collapse**: Frank's "squeeze out of this province everything that is still possible to squeeze out" (late 1942) while warning of famine in Warsaw; extraction increasingly cannibalized by military collapse.

**Totals**: occupation levies ~48bn RM by Feb 1944 (Finance Minister Schwerin von Krosigk's own figure), ~60bn by war's end; the U.S. Strategic Bombing Survey put total tribute at 104 billion RM (~$26bn) (Shirer 1960, ch. 27).

## Geographic Spread

Universal across occupied Europe but sharply graded: the West was milked financially under legal forms; the East was stripped as conquered colonial space, with the population's subsistence explicitly subordinated.

## Interaction

Interlocks with [[forced-labor-nazi-germany]] (labor as plundered commodity), [[hunger-plan-1941]] (food extraction as starvation policy), [[the-holocaust]] (victims' property — see the "Max Heiliger" Reichsbank account for dental gold and valuables under the Himmler–Funk agreement), and Vichy collaboration economics ([[paxton-vichy-france-1972]] coverage).

## End Conditions

Ended by military defeat, 1944–45. Aftermath: Nuremberg documentation (the totals above were established there), Allied restitution programs, and decades of art-provenance litigation that continue into the present.

## Historiography

The Nuremberg record made the plunder unusually well documented from perpetrator files; Shirer's 1960 synthesis remains a useful compendium of the raw figures, which he notes "overwhelmed" the tribunal's capacity to total precisely. Later scholarship (Dallin on Russia; Götz Aly's *Hitlers Volksstaat* debate on how far plunder subsidized German living standards) has reframed the data; Aly's strong thesis is contested. Collection coverage: strong via Nuremberg-based sources; no dedicated economic-history monograph of the occupation economy yet ingested.
