# Paleoindian and Archaic North America

## Definition and Scope

The long process of human peopling, Paleoindian colonization, and Holocene Archaic differentiation across North America — from first entry debates through regional hunter-gatherer cultures preceding widespread farming. Distinct from [[paleoindian-and-archaic-mesoamerica]].

Dean R. Snow’s synthesis for the *Cambridge History of the Native Peoples of the Americas* (Vol. I Part 1, ch. 3) frames the process as evidence for “the arrival of human beings in the Americas and the subsequent development of American Indian and Inuit hunter-gatherer societies in North America,” ending with horticulture where it arose but continuing to first European contact for much of the continent. All American Indians descend from full-time hunter-gatherers; later horticulturalists retained hunting/gathering as a supplement; after European contact many North American horticulturalists reverted to full-time hunting and gathering as firearms and the reintroduced horse made Plains bison more accessible.

## Causal Drivers

- Entry timing and routes (ice-free corridor vs coastal; pre-Clovis debate — see [[clovis-first-vs-pre-clovis]])
- Post-glacial environmental mosaics
- Technological traditions ([[clovis-culture]], [[folsom-culture]], regional Archaic point styles)
- Local resource intensification (e.g. [[poverty-point]])
- **Adaptive radiation** (Snow’s preferred model over freewheeling “migration”): cultures expand rapidly when environment meets technological capability, settle to environmental limits, then differentiate internally; expansions typically involve a single language that later fragments
- Rouse’s five-criteria burden of proof for archaeological migrations: intrusive identification, plausible source, contemporaneity, motive, no simpler alternative

## Major Phases

### 1. Peopling and Paleo-Indian (to ~8000 B.C.)

**Beringia.** Snow states there is “no longer any doubt” that North America was initially populated by hunter-gatherers expanding eastward into Alaska and the submerged continental shelf; Bering Strait was a ~1,000 km-wide isthmus continuous with NE Asian hunting grounds. All American human remains are biologically modern; necessary conditions after ~40,000 years ago with Upper Paleolithic technology. Significant movements through NE Asia only after peak last glaciation ~16,000 B.C. Siberian analogs: Kamchatka bifacial leaf-shaped points ~12,300–11,600 B.C. (Dikov argues up to 2,000 years older); Dyuktai (Lena) similar points ≤13,000 B.C.; Chukchi complexes judged as evidence of dispersal into Alaska ~11,000 B.C.

**Dental and blood-group waves (Turner et al.).** American Indians resemble NE Asians; split not much before 13,000 B.C.; no European genetic mixture until after 1492. Ancestors of most Indians through Alaska 18,000–10,000 B.C.; Na-Dene after 10,000 but before 6500 B.C.; Inuit/Aleut same period or slightly later. ABO blood group B present in Inuits/Aleuts, not Na-Dene or most Indians.

**Snow’s preferred scenario.** Ancestral Paleo-Indians into Alaska after 12,000 B.C., Plains by 10,000 B.C.; earlier failed migrations left few undisputable traces. American Paleo-Arctic tradition (Gallagher Flint Station etc.) ~8500 B.C.; Anangula maritime; sometimes lumped as “Beringian.” Well-dated sites prove Paleo-Indians widespread by 10,000 B.C.; earlier presence remains controversial (see [[clovis-first-vs-pre-clovis]]).

**Ice sheets.** Laurentide (NE Canada) and Cordilleran (Rockies) in collision by 18,000 B.C. → solid ice Atlantic to Pacific; earlier/later gaps still blocked by proglacial lakes making transit difficult or impossible for long periods. Ice-Age vegetational zones compressed; middle-latitude tundra richer than modern northern tundras (more intense, seasonally even sunlight), supporting surviving game and doomed megafauna. By ~8000 B.C. spruce/pine/fir establishing where ice/tundra had been.

**[[clovis-culture|Clovis]]** (oldest generally accepted Paleo-Indian complex; hallmark fluted Clovis point; many sites 10,000–9000 B.C.) and **[[folsom-culture|Folsom]]** (later Plains fluted derivative). Unfluted leaf-shaped Paleo-Indian points in Rockies and along the spine of the Americas to Argentina; fish-tailed types in South America. Toolkit: portable big-game kit; for a millennium hunters took living game plus extinct horses, mammoths, mastodons, camels, *Bison antiquus*.

**Society and demography.** Three site types: full-kit band camps; special-purpose kill/hunting sites; large multiband encampments. Density under 1 per 100 km²; habitable North America ~10 million km² → ≤100,000 Paleo-Indians. Band of ~25 needs ~2,500 km²; network of 500 ~50,000 km² (Wobst long-term survival threshold). Egalitarian; little food storage (rare Vail site Maine stone-slab cist); fluted points of exotic materials far from quarries indicate gift/trade networks without strong ethnicity.

**Megafaunal extinctions.** Role of Paleo-Indians remains open: against (hard to credit 100,000 people with spears; extinctions extended to songbirds; environment as prime mover); for (selective hunting of young; naive prey; cascading vegetation change). By 8000 B.C. ice nearly gone; mammoths and four-horned antelopes gone quickly; horses, ground sloths, camels soon after; mastodons and some mountain goat/bison hung on longer. Strategy shifted from specialized large mobile resources to broader exploitation of smaller/immobile ones → Archaic.

### 2. Archaic as culture type (~8000 B.C. onward)

Snow uses “Archaic” mainly as **culture type** (not only period): hunter-gatherers adapting to post-Pleistocene environments via specialization and technological complexity. Eastern Woodlands: to third millennium B.C. in some areas, much later elsewhere; plant cultivation and pottery mark local “ends” but transitions are gradual. West and North retained Archaic-type adaptations until the nineteenth century.

**Eleven long-term Eastern trends** (after Hayden; 5,000 years after 8000 B.C.): seasonal scheduling/sedentism/middens; complex technology; smaller game; rising density; restricted ranges; marginal environments; environmental modification (esp. fire for deer meadows); mortuary ceremonialism; competition/ranking; food storage; cultural introspection/language differentiation.

**Eastern point sequence.** By ~8500 B.C. fluted forms replaced: west/north of Eastern Woodlands by unfluted lanceolate **Piano** tradition (bison west; caribou north; some to Maritimes/Maine to ~6000 B.C.). Early Archaic 8000–6000 B.C.: stemmed/notched/barbed (Dalton, Quad, Hardaway, Kirk; bifurcate base farther north). Middle Archaic 6000–4000 B.C.: Stanley, Neville, Stark, Merrimack. Late Archaic after 4000 B.C.: broad side-notched; Otter Creek with **Laurentian** Archaic (lower Great Lakes–St. Lawrence); related **Maritime Archaic** around Gulf of St. Lawrence. Caution: point types can have long lives; 14C outside expected ranges often wrongly dismissed.

**Technology.** Ground stone adzes/celts/gouges; **Old Copper** (Keweenaw Peninsula — beaten copper annealed by heat + cold plunge); specialized fishing; stone boiling (fired clay balls at [[poverty-point]]); fiber-tempered pottery SE third millennium B.C.; grit-tempered pottery NE by 1000 B.C.; domesticated dog. Principal weapon: spear thrower (atlatl) inherited from Paleo-Indians; Eastern flexible-shaft throwers (green ironwood) later enhanced with ground stone weights — eased eventual bow-and-arrow adoption.

**Population.** Density rose from Paleo-Indian <1/100 km² into 1–100 per 100 km²; West Coast eventually >1 per km² without farming; elsewhere farming prerequisite for ≥1/km². Rough continental Archaic total “not greater than a million.”

**Social ranking and language.** Differential adult-male burials → ranking beyond age/sex; food storage becomes practical once outsiders defined. Paleo-Indian languages replaced by mutually unintelligible Archaic languages → protolanguages of historic families (≥21 families; many isolates; 50–100 Archaic protolanguages may have expired). Reconstructed protolanguages (proto-Algonquian, proto-Iroquoian) mostly no older than Late Archaic after ~4000 B.C. Historic expansions as adaptive radiations: Iroquoian from Allegheny Plateau; Eastern Algonquians south along coast; Uto-Aztecans N/E through Desert West; Siouans west onto Plains after Europeans; last Eskimo expansion left little linguistic variation Alaska–Greenland. Glottochronology disfavored (rate too variable); internal ordering with archaeology still useful. West Coast language diversity more likely recent niche fragmentation than 12,000-year-old first-occupation traces.

**Early Eastern plant cultivation.** By third millennium B.C.: sunflower, goosefoot, pigweed, knotweed, maygrass, marsh elder; first North American cucurbits from Eastern wild ancestors (not Mexico). “Cultivating ecosystem” ~2500 B.C. in few Mississippi drainage places lasting to ≥A.D. 400 some places — low-yield buffers, not staples. See [[early-agriculture-americas]], [[north-american-agricultural-transition]].

### 4. European impact and horse redistribution

Spanish enduringly in Southwest by 1598 reintroduced the horse. Shoshones carried horses N/E to Plains; many nations became mounted nomads ~1700 under opportunity and eastern pressure. European stereotype of all Indians as hunters aided removal policy; without rapid expansion and epidemics, historic cultural dynamics would have differed. *`caused_by:`* [[sixteenth-century-north-american-contact]]; *`concurrent_with:`* [[virgin-soil-epidemics]].

## Geographic Spread

Continent-wide with sharp regional trajectories (Eastern Woodlands, Plains, Desert West, California, Northwest, Subarctic, Arctic). Linguistic map (≥375 languages in 1492; ≥21 families + 32 poorly known languages) is largely a product of the last ~4,000 years of Archaic/post-Archaic expansions, while archaeological chronology is at least three times longer — pre-4000 B.P. populations may never get clear linguistic identities. Three late Asian-related linguistic strata: Eskimo-Aleut (recent, Asian relatives clear); Na-Dene/Athapaskan (second-to-last from Asia); remainder (Greenberg’s single “Amerind” stock simultaneously plausible as a given and unprovable).

## Interaction

Feeds [[north-american-agricultural-transition]], [[north-american-mound-building]], and later agricultural chiefdoms. Contrasts with Mesoamerican sequence timing ([[paleoindian-and-archaic-mesoamerica]]). Regional processes: [[desert-archaic]], [[plateau-archaic]], [[california-archaic]], [[northwest-coast-archaic]], [[subarctic-archaic]].

## End Conditions

Transforming into agricultural and ranked societies in the East and Southwest rather than a single "end"; Archaic lifeways persist in many regions into contact. Mortuary ceremonialism (L’Anse Amour mound 7,500 ya → Maritime Archaic “Red Paint”; Glacial Kame; Old Copper) and ranking *enabled* later [[adena-culture]] and Hopewell elaborations.

## Historiography

Snow’s bibliographic essay frames peopling as the most controversial topic: Shutler *Early Man in the New World*, Bryan *New Evidence for Pleistocene Peopling* vs Owen against Ice-Age population; *Natural History* 14-article series 1986–88; Martin & Klein *Quaternary Extinctions*; Fagan *Great Journey*; Campbell & Mithun languages vs controversial Greenberg *Language in the Americas* linked to Turner/Zegura dental-genetic model; Hayden and Bruce Smith on Archaic tech/early agriculture. Dates below are **as Snow states** (B.C./A.D. calendar years); radiocarbon for pre-8000 B.C. requires skepticism (calibration limits, half-life standard error, material differences, technical errors that tend to increase ages).
