## Definition and Scope

Southeast Asian regionalism refers to the process by which the non-communist states of the region constructed shared political institutions and diplomatic norms from the late 1950s onward, culminating in the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN), founded 1967. This process is not to be conflated with regional identity or cultural solidarity (which predated ASEAN and took quite different forms); it is specifically the political-diplomatic project of building inter-state co-operation among states that shared anti-communist orientation and fear of external interference rather than cultural commonality.

The concept of "Southeast Asia" as a political unit was itself an accident of World War II, emerging from the creation of the South East Asia Command (SEAC) at the Quebec Conference in August 1943. "No single empire had dominated the whole region in pre-colonial times." The post-independence states were successor states to different colonial systems (British, Dutch, French, American, Thai) and inherited competitive and sometimes hostile relationships with each other. That they eventually built a functioning regional organization was not predetermined.

## Causal Drivers

**Anti-communist solidarity**: "The ASEAN countries were all anti-communist, bound together by fear of an aggressive North Vietnam and the perceived threat from communist China. Their governments tended towards authoritarianism, but with open economies." The founding moment of ASEAN (1967) occurred against the backdrop of the escalating Vietnam War and anxiety about communist subversion internally and from Hanoi and Beijing externally.

**Post-confrontation reconciliation**: The armed confrontation between Indonesia and Malaysia (1963–6) had destroyed its predecessor organization, ASA. When General Suharto's New Order came to power in Indonesia after 1966 and ended confrontation, the opportunity arose for a genuinely inclusive regional organization. Indonesia's foreign minister Adam Malik "was prepared to exercise restraint" despite Indonesia's size and ambitions. The reconciliation between Indonesia and Malaysia, and the restoration of Philippines-Malaysia relations, was the precondition for ASEAN.

**Economic development imperatives**: Member states saw regional stability as a prerequisite for the economic development that was their primary goal. ASEAN's stated primary objective was "to accelerate the economic growth, social progress and cultural development in the region," with security as the means rather than the end.

**Vietnamese regional ambitions**: The Vietnamese invasion of Cambodia in December 1978 — "the first sign of any substance to the domino theory" — was the catalyst that transformed ASEAN from a slow-developing institution into a diplomatically effective bloc. For the first time ASEAN adopted a common stance, rejecting Hanoi's intervention as "an affront to national sovereignty." This galvanized ASEAN's international standing and produced a decade of sustained diplomatic pressure.

## Major Phases

**Pre-ASEAN attempts (1959–1966)**: Tunku Abdul Rahman proposed regional co-operation to combat communist subversion as early as 1959. The Association of Southeast Asia (ASA, 1961) brought together Thailand, the Philippines, and Malaya, but Indonesia's opposition under Sukarno and the Malaysia-Philippines dispute over Sabah destroyed it. The Maphilindo concept (Malaysia, Philippines, Indonesia — a "Greater Malay Confederation") was stillborn. These failures were nonetheless significant as locally generated initiatives.

**ASEAN formation and slow development (1967–1975)**: The Bangkok Declaration (August 1967) created ASEAN with five founding members — Indonesia, Malaysia, the Philippines, Singapore, and Thailand. It was explicitly not a military alliance: ASEAN relied on "collective political defence," a term coined by Thailand's foreign minister Thanat Khoman. Practical co-operation was limited: "For many years ASEAN developed slowly. Its member states were a motley collection of diverse cultures and competing economies." Bilateral tensions persisted: Singapore's execution of Indonesian saboteurs in 1968 provoked Jakarta mob violence; the Philippines' Sabah claim rankled Malaysia throughout.

**ZOPFAN and early consolidation (1970–1975)**: Malaysia's prime minister Tun Abdul Razak proposed making Southeast Asia a Zone of Peace, Friendship and Neutrality (ZOPFAN) at the Lusaka non-aligned summit in 1970. ASEAN endorsed ZOPFAN in 1971, though members attached "a variety of meanings to this." Malaysia was the first ASEAN country to establish diplomatic relations with the People's Republic of China (1974). These moves reflected growing ASEAN confidence and a more nuanced approach to the Cold War, especially as Nixon's China rapprochement and the beginning of American military withdrawal transformed the regional strategic environment.

**Bali summit and Vietnamese challenge (1975–1978)**: The communist victories in Indochina in 1975 alarmed ASEAN. The first ASEAN summit, held at Bali in February 1976, produced the Declaration of ASEAN Concord and the Treaty of Amity and Cooperation — the institutional consolidation that gave ASEAN its political character. An ASEAN Secretariat was established in Jakarta. But it was Vietnam's invasion of Cambodia (December 1978) that forced ASEAN to become genuinely effective: for ten years ASEAN led the effort to bar the Heng Samrin regime from the United Nations and sustain a government-in-exile.

**ASEAN as diplomatic power (1979–1991)**: The Cambodian crisis "impelled the ASEAN countries for the first time to adopt a common stance" and earned ASEAN "enhanced international respect as a body with political and diplomatic influence." The internal dynamics remained complex: Thailand faced Vietnam directly and aligned with China; Indonesia retained "considerable respect for Vietnam's militant nationalist record and saw Beijing as a more ominous long-term threat." Despite these differences, ASEAN maintained unified public positions. Brunei joined in 1984 (the first expansion). By the late 1980s ASEAN was "probably the most effective of Third World organizations."

## Geographic Spread

ASEAN remained geographically incomplete throughout this period. Burma refused to join, preferring non-alignment; Cambodia, Laos, and Vietnam were either communist or in active conflict. Indonesia's initial exclusion of itself from ASA meant that the largest state in the region entered regional co-operation only in 1967. Papua New Guinea had observer status from 1976 but was conventionally linked with Australasia.

## Actors

- [[asean]] (organization, f. 1967)
- Indonesia under Suharto: the largest member, "leading the way in encouraging the development of ASEAN" while being the most cautious about China; key broker role throughout
- Singapore under Lee Kuan Yew: most dependent on external relationships; most vocal on economic principles; insisted on executing Indonesian saboteurs 1968 despite Suharto's personal pleas
- Thailand: "frontline" state directly facing Vietnam; relied most on China as counterweight
- Malaysia under Tun Abdul Razak: originated ZOPFAN; first to normalize with China; proposed New Economic Policy internally
- Philippines under Marcos and then Aquino: the "weak link" — internal instability, economic chaos under Marcos, Aquino's precarious democracy; Sabah claim troubled regional solidarity
- Brunei: admitted 1984; stabilized by oil wealth; used ASEAN membership to normalize relations with Malaysia and Indonesia
- Vietnam: the key external challenger; invasion of Cambodia transformed ASEAN from inside

## End Conditions

The Cambodian peace agreement, finally signed in October 1991, removed the immediate catalyst for ASEAN's effectiveness. The Cold War's end also removed the ideological glue of anti-communism. ASEAN faced the question of whether it could sustain its diplomatic momentum and eventually expand to include Vietnam, Laos, and Cambodia. The trajectory remained open as of 1992.

## Historiography

Chapter 10 of CHSEA v.2 (by Lau Teik Soon and Leo Suryadinata) provides a diplomatic history perspective, drawing on ASEAN's own documentary record (ASEAN Documents Series, 1967–1985) and regional political science (Jorgensen-Dahl on regional organization; Justus Van der Kroef on SEATO). Key interpretive points:

1. **Minimalist functionalism**: ASEAN succeeded precisely because it did not attempt too much. It was not a military alliance, not a common market, and not a supranational body. Its "consensus and not confrontation" norm allowed states with divergent interests to co-operate without requiring ideological uniformity.

2. **Political over economic**: "While only limited progress had been made in the economic, social and cultural fields, ASEAN was more successful in political and diplomatic co-operation." The economic rationale was secondary to the security rationale throughout the Cold War period.

3. **The Cambodia test**: The unified response to Vietnam's Cambodia invasion is treated as the definitive proof of ASEAN's value — showing "what could be achieved by diplomatic and political pressure, without a parallel military pact."

4. **Indonesia's central role**: Indonesia's restraint under Malik and Suharto, despite being the obvious regional hegemon, was decisive for ASEAN's viability. Without Indonesian buy-in, no regional organization could function.

The chapter's coverage of Vietnam, Laos, and Cambodia after 1975 is comprehensive and balanced. The treatment of Burma is thinner, reflecting that country's withdrawal from regional engagement.
