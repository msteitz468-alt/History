## Definition and Scope

The Pacific transport and communications revolution designates the multi-decade
process by which the Pacific Ocean was "shrunk" — meaning that the unit costs and
elapsed times of moving goods, people, and information across the ocean fell
dramatically — transforming the Pacific from a barrier into the core of the world
economy. Rimmer and Dick in [[hattori-samson-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v2-2023]]
(ch. 57) frame it as "a two-stage process, first reformation and densification of
pre-war networks until the end of the 1960s, then transformation through the new
technologies of container shipping, jet aircraft, and the Internet."

The three revolutions constitute a "network of networks" in which container
shipping, jet air transport, and telecommunications carry goods, people, and
information respectively — each with distinct time-distance characteristics (goods
still take weeks; persons take hours; information now moves in milliseconds).

## Causal Drivers

- caused_by: post-war US-backed reconstruction of Japan, which revived the Asian Rim of the Pacific from the 1950s onwards
- caused_by: containerisation — Malcolm McLean's Sea-Land organisation demonstrated the feasibility of steel-box shipping in 1956; Matson Navigation carried containers from San Francisco to Honolulu in 1958; full trans-Pacific containerisation followed by 1967–68
- caused_by: jet aircraft — the Boeing 707 entered Qantas service in 1959, "reducing flying time between Sydney and San Francisco from 27.30 hours to 16.10 hours"; wide-bodied jets (747, A380) subsequently opened mass tourism
- caused_by: Internet — the World Wide Web from 1989 "reincarnated" ARPANET into a worldwide interactive information exchange, replacing telephony by VoIP and enabling cloud-based services
- contributed_to: US airline deregulation (1978 abolition of the Civil Aeronautics Board) and subsequent deregulation in Canada, Australia, and New Zealand, halving inflation-adjusted air fares between 1995 and 2015
- contributed_to: ISO container standardisation (1970) enabling intermodal door-to-door logistics
- contributed_to: proliferation of fibre-optic submarine cables carrying 99 per cent of all trans-oceanic data traffic

## Major Phases

**Phase 1 — Reformation, 1945–1969.** Restoration of pre-war shipping and air
networks within two years of 1945 using surplus war-built tonnage. The critical
difference from the pre-war situation was "the absence of Japanese-flag shipping" until
1952. Three changes marked the era: marginalisation of the China trade after Communist
capture of Shanghai in 1949; rapid Southeast Asian decolonisation introducing political
instability that suppressed shipping demand; and marginalisation of deep-sea passenger
shipping as air travel became faster and cheaper. A trans-Pacific propeller-driven air
network — San Francisco–Honolulu–Tokyo route (Japan Airlines from 1954), Sydney–North
America route (Qantas from 1954) — was established before the jet era.

**Phase 2 — Transformation, 1970–1990.** Container shipping: by 1983 twenty-six Pacific
countries had adopted containerisation. Container flows in the 1970s were dominated by
bilateral North America–Japan trade, but diffused rapidly to ASEAN nations by 1990.
"Reliable door-to-door container logistics allowed hitherto vertically integrated
manufacturing corporations … to unbundle their production system and relocate the
making of parts and their assembly to lower-cost countries around the Pacific Rim and
create global supply chains." Jet travel: the Boeing 747 (1972) opened air travel to a
mass market; the 747SP (1984) first achieved non-stop Sydney–Los Angeles; the 747-400
(1989) was "dubbed 'the Pacific Airliner' because it drew a wider range of airports on
the Pacific Rim closer to each other and the rest of the world." Super-hub airports
opened: Singapore Changi (1981), Kansai (1994), Hong Kong Lantau (1998), Shanghai
Pudong (1999), Seoul-Incheon (2001).

**Phase 3 — Internet and China's ascent, 1990s–present.** By 1995 trans-Pacific and
trans-Suez container traffic surpassed trans-Atlantic for the first time; by 2015 the
flows were 24 million and 22 million TEUs respectively against only 7 million for the
North Atlantic. China moved ahead of the USA as the world's leading generator of
container traffic by the mid-1990s. Shanghai's Yangshan deep-water port (built from 2004)
enabled Shanghai to supplant Singapore as the world's leading container port by 2015. In
telecommunications, East Asian economies — notably South Korea, Taiwan, and China —
became prominent in fixed and mobile broadband; internet exchanges in Tokyo, Seoul,
Hong Kong, Los Angeles, Singapore, and Shanghai became primary carrier hubs.

## Geographic Spread

Rimmer and Dick propose a fundamental distinction between contiguous **continental**
economies (China, North America), fragmented **coastal** economies (Japan, Korea,
Taiwan, Southeast Asia, Chile, Peru, Australia's east-coast corridor), and dispersed
**archipelagic** economies (Pacific Islands, the Philippines, Indonesia). The transport
revolution has been radically uneven: "whereas the technological advances of
containerization, jet aircraft, and IT have wrought a total logistics revolution in the
continental economies, there has been only a partial revolution in Paciﬁca." Increasing
container-ship size means many island ports are bypassed, while Papua New Guinea's
internet penetration was only 15 per cent in 2021.

Ten **gateway** nodes dominate all three networks simultaneously: Los Angeles, San
Francisco, and Seattle on the west coast of North America; Hong Kong, Seoul, Shanghai,
Singapore, Taipei, and Tokyo in East Asia; and Sydney in the southwest Pacific.

## End Conditions

The process is ongoing. Container ship sizes continue to grow (exceeding 20,000 TEUs by
2017), creating route-concentration pressures. The Internet is the largest consumer of
Pacific submarine cable capacity. Geopolitical tensions — notably the US–China contest
over the North Pacific described as "a geopolitical contest between two continental
powers with seemingly incompatible aspirations" — may reshape trade and data-routing
patterns. COVID-19 disrupted the trans-Pacific air network from 2020. The authors
conclude that the transport revolution is not producing a uniform "Pacific Century": "the
North Pacific has become the world's leading trade route in terms of value and frequency
in all three modes" while southern hemisphere connections remain far thinner, and "slow
and costly inter-island logistics continue to be stubborn obstacles to raising common
welfare" throughout Paciﬁca.

## Historiography

Rimmer and Dick employ a "geologistics" framework — originally a US military term — to
map flows of goods, people, and information as complementary networks with shared hub
structures rather than as separate transport sectors. This allows them to reveal structural
geographic patterns (gateway nodes, continental vs. archipelagic economies) invisible in
purely nation-state or trade-route analyses. Their coverage is strong on quantitative data
(TEU tables, airport-pair statistics, bandwidth pricing) and on East Asia and North
America; thinner on Pacific Island perspectives and on the environmental costs of
containerisation. The chapter's concentration on infrastructure and flows means that
labour relations, regulatory politics, and ecological externalities receive limited
treatment.
