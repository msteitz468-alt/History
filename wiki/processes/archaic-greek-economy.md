# The Archaic Greek Economic Transformation

Between **800 and 500 BCE** the economic and social structure of the Greek world
"underwent massive alterations which set the framework for the Classic age" — the
shift from the simple, near-static economy of the Dark Age to the "relatively advanced
commercial system" Cyrus mocked, where men "come together [in the agora] to cheat each
other." [[boardman-cah-v3p3-1982|CAH Vol. III, Part 3]] (Ch. 45a, C. G. Starr) analyses
it.

## Drivers: the real forces

Starr argues that the usual explanations — cities, coinage, tyrants, colonization,
trade — are **consequences or attendant circumstances**, not causes. The deeper forces
were:

- **The Near Eastern stimulus.** The most significant factor "lay entirely outside the
  Aegean": the rapid recovery of the Near East after the Bronze Age collapse, which by
  800 (under Assyrian mastery) protected trade routes and generated **demand for luxury
  goods and raw materials** — drawing the Greeks into exchange and obliging them to find
  exportable cargoes.
- **A slowly rising population** — Starr is sceptical of the "great eighth-century jump"
  (pre-industrial demography rules out sudden surges; much apparent growth was a shift
  from semi-nomadic life), but accepts a gentle recovery and expansion.
- **The upper classes' "eager, ruthless drive for wealth."** With no powerful
  priesthoods and the kings set aside, the **galvanizing factor was the aristocracy's
  intensified desire for *chremata*** ("useful things") — driving overseas contact,
  colonization, mercenary service, and the exploitation of their own countryside. "No
  poor man is noble" (Alcaeus).

## The agricultural base and peasantization

Almost all Greeks lived in **villages on a level of *penia*** (the need to work with
one's hands); only a few were *plousioi*. The base was agriculture (Solon's classes
were defined by income in grain, oil and wine), with no major technical advance —
just more land tilled and harder work, and some shift toward specialized olive and
vine. The central development, Starr argues, was **the reduction of smaller farmers to
"peasants"** — no longer self-sufficient, but dependent producers yielding their tiny
surpluses to a non-farming group, through rural **debt**, the demands of the rising
**[[city-state|polis]]** (temple-building, cult, markets), and above all the "incessant
pressure of the upper classes." In **Thessaly, Sparta and Crete** the rural population
was legally bound (serfdom/helotry); elsewhere the dynamic of progress kept farmers
**peasants but still citizens** of the polis. The result was the accumulation of
**disposable wealth (capital)** — food, metals, animals — in a few hands, "adequate to
fuel continuing economic growth."

## Trade, industry, and cities

The outward marks of the process were the **rise of trade and specialized industry**.
Starr cautions against overstating early "trade": commercial motives were *not* primary
in colonization, and the specialized merchant/artisan classes arose later than often
claimed. But movement of goods expanded steadily from 800 — metals, **textiles**
(Milesian wool to Sybaris), timber, slaves, and (the most visible evidence) pottery,
which "underwent a great jump" with Protocorinthian (7th c.) and then Attic
black-/red-figure (6th c.). The network was begun and long sustained by **adventurous
upper-class men** (Sappho's brother, Solon, Colaeus of Samos) before passing, by the
sixth century, to **independent shipowners**. Specialized crafts — autonomous workshops,
not aristocratic household production — flourished through **borrowed Near Eastern
technique** plus a uniquely **competitive, experimental spirit** ("potter is angry with
potter," Hesiod; "as never Euphronios," the painter's boast). These artisans and
traders clustered in the **emerging cities** — true cities by the sixth century, though
their primary cause was social/religious (the temple, the agora), not economic.

## Coinage and economic tensions

[[coinage|Coinage]] (from Lydia) appeared "as a tangible economic force" alongside the
cities — but Starr stresses it was **not in itself a potent economic force until well
after 600**, issued for **state purposes** (navies, mercenaries, fines, tolls, prestige)
rather than retail trade, and **not a cause of tyranny or unrest**. The era's **tensions
were mainly rural and aristocratic**: contention over land, debt and enslavement (the
crisis [[solon|Solon]] confronted), and the rivalry between aristocrats and the
"self-assertive *kakoi*" (ambitious non-noble freeholders, Theognis's despised
goatskin-wearers). Starr rejects reading these as modern class war or as economic
rivalry between "trade leagues" (the [[lelantine-war-c700bce|Lelantine War]] was not such): the
tensions were "a reflection of growth, not of decline," and the polis's communal ethos
and the safety-valve of expansion kept Greece from the bondage that overtook the
peasantries of the Near East.

## Why it mattered

- The making of the **commercial, monetized, city-based economy** of Classical Greece —
  and of the surplus that funded the temples, sanctuaries, and the leisure of poets and
  philosophers.
- The **economic engine behind the Archaic social crises** — peasantization, debt, and
  aristocratic faction — that produced [[greek-tyranny|tyranny]] and the reforms of
  Solon, and so the Greek constitutional experiments.
- A distinctively Greek outcome — "carried on by free men," its difference from the
  Near East and early-modern Europe rooted in "the tiny scale of Greek political
  organization and its inheritance of a sense of communal unity."

## Hall (2014) — Archaic Greek World

[[sources/hall-history-archaic-greek-world-2014|Hall (2014)]] reframes Starr’s CAH synthesis with Finley-era debates, survey archaeology, and a stronger sixth-century economic pivot.

### Finley and the ancient-economy debates

Recent scholarship rejects an autonomous “economic sphere.” Finley’s *Ancient Economy* (1973) is the seminal intervention against the older primitivist–modernist (Bücher–Meyer) and formalist–substantivist (Polanyi) polarities. On Hall’s reading of Finley (via Weber/Hasebroek), the ancient economy aimed at agricultural self-sufficiency; trade was tiny; the *polis* was a **consumer center** extracting rents/taxes/tributes from its hinterland. Few now hold extreme modernist/formalist views, but many suspect Finley **underestimated complexity**; New Institutional Economics (transaction costs) has been applied to Archaic regional monetary currencies. Hall’s own position is post-Finley without full modernism: agriculture dominant, markets real, professional profit trade late.

### Agriculture dominant; peasant vs yeoman

Nobody disputes agriculture’s dominance: *Works and Days* and Phocylides treat farming as the basis of subsistence/wealth; property qualifications for citizenship reinforce cultivation’s centrality; commercial/industrial classes remained negligible even later (e.g. only ~10–15 vase-painters active at Athens at once). **Peasants** (vs slaves/serfs below; vs profit-maximizing farmers above) produce some surplus for rents/taxes but gear production primarily to subsistence and risk-minimization. Hall **rejects** an egalitarian “yeoman farmer” model of most Archaic rural residents as market-oriented intensive homesteaders—that model rides an early egalitarianism ideology he has already questioned and unrepresentative literary examples (Laertes; Hesiod). Hesiod’s mixed farming, slaves, wage labor, and relatively large farm (~6–8 ha) look resource-rich, yet debt/hunger motives for seaborne trade and fallowing/village residence look risk-minimizing; the persona is inconsistent and problematic evidence. Survey data: isolated rural farmsteads uncommon until later sixth century; intensive rural settlement peaks in fifth–fourth centuries; Metapontum hinterland farmsteads jump from 5 to 66 in the second half of the sixth century with strip division and increased olive pollen. “Peasant societies” fit the Archaic world better than a “slave economy” (propertied surplus from unfree labor), which better describes Classical Greece; sharp chattel slavery is poorly defined for much of the Archaic, and at Athens becomes more important after Solon’s legislation. Freeholders of modest properties still meet the peasant definition when subsistence remains primary even with occasional unfree labor; Pisistratus allegedly levied 10% (Aristotle *AC* 16.4) or 5% (Thuc. 6.54) on agricultural produce.

### Division of labor, workshops, sanctuary markets

Agriculture dominated, but Hesiod, Solon, and Homer attest craft specialization and markets (Achaean camp market for wine vs bronze, iron, hides, cattle, slaves). Eighth-century metal workshops inside urban centers (Zagora, Argos, Athens); late eighth-century Eretria goldsmith’s house with skyphos of gold/electrum/jewelry (510 g). Earliest explicit *agora* commercial function: early fifth-century Gortyn Law Code; earlier markets may have been seasonal at sanctuaries (bronze casting at Olympia from ≥seventh century; Athena Alea Tegea workshop from the eighth). Peasant households still exchanged modest surpluses; elites engaged markets more; ~1/3 of land estimated in the richest 10%; Solonian ban on exports except oil (if genuine) implies surplus-oriented Attic landowners.

### Trade: necessity and desire; Naucratis; elite shipping

**Aegina** (~40,000 people) could support only ~4,000 on local land, forcing import-based subsistence and early long-distance trade. **Naucratis**: Herodotus’s Amasis charter (Hellenion; Aeginetan Zeus, Samian Hera, Milesian Apollo) vs archaeology pushing Greek presence to late seventh century under Psammetichus I; classified as an administered **port of trade** (prostatai; Egyptian customs control; silver the Egyptian priority; grain crucial for Aegina and Miletus). Desire as well as necessity drove trade: Levant imported Corinthian aryballoi and Greek wine/oil despite local alternatives; brand cachet in poetry; luxury desire especially a propertied-class/symposium preoccupation. Until late sixth century warships and merchant ships are not pictorially distinct; dual-purpose **pentekonters** carried Phocaean voyages; high cost and luxury/ore cargoes imply **elite initiative** (*naukleros* vs *emporos* distinction is later). Significant rise of **professional, profit-driven trade** in the second half of the sixth century (e.g. Nikosthenic workshop targeting Etruria; sail-driven round ships on late-sixth Athenian pottery), concurrent with rural intensification and accelerated per capita growth.

### Coinage late

Numismatics plays a minor role in Archaic political narrative because coinage appears fairly late—around the **mid-sixth century** in a few mainland states—and early issues are longer-lived standards rather than event propaganda (weight-standards and distribution still inform trade history). This aligns with Starr’s caution that coinage was not a potent retail force until well after 600, while sharpening the chronological point: monetary facilitation belongs to Hall’s sixth-century pivot, not an eighth-century “renaissance.”

### Sixth-century economic shift (Hall’s “shape”)

In Hall’s concluding reconstruction, mid/late sixth century marks the shift from elite surplus **prestige trade** to professionalized **profit commerce**, facilitated by coinage and *emporia* (Naucratis, west, Black Sea). Theognidean anxiety over *nouveaux riches* signals challenge to landed aristocracy as the *dēmos* (esp. Athens) questioned aristocratic rule—full realization Classical, linked also to ethnic self-conception of Greekness vs barbarian. Land tenure remains among the most contentious Archaic subjects: private ownership assumed in Hesiod; communal property co-existed; evolutionist inalienable kinship-land theories lack modern credence; citizenship originally likely depended on property in most poleis even where Classical Athens did not require land for citizens.

## Cambridge Economic History of the Greco-Roman World (Osborne, ch. 10)

[[sources/scheidel-cambridge-economic-history-greco-roman-world-2007|Scheidel, Morris &
Saller (eds.)]] (2007), Robin Osborne's chapter, provides an independent, quantified
account of the 700–500 BCE transformation that corroborates Starr's and Hall's framing
above with harder archaeological/economic detail.

**Population and colonization.** Osborne estimates population growth of roughly 0.5%/year
across the period (enough to double the population over its span), with per-capita
consumption and production also increasing (evidenced by fine pottery and housing).
Agricultural production per capita likely rose through colonization into more favorable
environments rather than technological advance: roughly **30 cities were founded in the
50 years from the 730s to 680s BCE** alone (Italy, Sicily, southern France, northwest
Spain, North Africa, the northern Aegean, the Hellespont, the Black Sea), mostly as
single-center urban settlements rather than the mainland's village clusters.

**Coinage.** The earliest coinage was electrum, struck in late-7th-century Lydia (a
deposit survives at the Temple of Artemis, Ephesus); Herodotus (1.94.1) credits the
Lydians as the first to strike coins and the first retail traders (*kapeloi*). Silver
coinage begins around 550 BCE; by 500 BCE over 40 Greek cities were minting, from Cyprus
to Libya to Sicily and southern Italy. Osborne argues coinage **followed rather than
caused** the commoditization of the Greek city economy, though it became its symbol;
electrum coinage's variable metal content and localized weight standards suggest it was
as much a political marker of civic authority as an economic tool, and cities lacking
native silver/electrum still minted by importing metal — a political rather than purely
economic motive.

**Land, investment, and trade infrastructure.** At Metapontum, roughly 20,000 ha were
divided into a regular grid in the second half of the 6th century BCE; surveyed farm
sites in a 31.5 km² area rose from 5 (early 6th c.) to 66 (late 6th c.) to 116 (early 5th
c.), alongside rising olive pollen and grain becoming Metapontum's coin type. Selinus
undertook a massive temple-building program (9 temples, 560–480 BCE, one measuring
50×110m); Snodgrass estimates ~270 tons of stone were transported by sea per year from the
mid-7th century onward for sculpture alone — the largest capital investment most archaic
cities made. Trade evidence includes SOS amphoras (probably Attic, carrying olive oil)
reaching Crete, the Ionian Sea, southern Italy, Etruria, Sicily, Cyprus, Al Mina, and
Spain by the end of the 8th century BCE, with Corinthian and then Athenian fine pottery
dominating export markets in succession. **Naucratis** in Egypt functioned as a regulated
Greek "port of trade," with Greek contact funneled through this one site by Egyptian
authorities; the **Berezan lead letter** (Achillodoros to his son) is the earliest known
Greek commercial private letter, showing a regular personal trading network among Black
Sea Greeks by the 6th century BCE.

**Gift to commodity, and Solon.** Osborne frames the period through an archaic "transition
from gift to commodity" (citing Morris 1986b): the Homeric/Hesiodic economy was still
substantially organized around gift exchange and dependent personal relations, while
Solon's Athens (early 6th c.) shows wealth and land determining formal political status
via census classes — an economic relationship becoming politicized and partly
commodified. Solon's reforms banned the enslavement of Athenians for debt, ended the
*hektemoroi* ("sixth-parters") sharecropping underclass, and (per his own fr. 36) freed
debt-slaves and returned exiles sold abroad; the subsequent expansion of chattel slavery
at Athens is read as a 6th-century phenomenon filling the resulting demand for dependent
labor. Hesiod's *Works and Days* already shows awareness of profitable long-distance
grain/goods trade as an alternative to poverty, alongside a "peasant" ideal of small-scale
household self-sufficiency.
