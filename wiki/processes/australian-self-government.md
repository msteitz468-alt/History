## Definition and Scope

The advent of self-government in the Australian colonies (1840s–1890) was the process by which Britain's settler colonies in Australia gained first representative and then responsible government — a system of bicameral parliaments in which ministers were answerable to the elected legislature rather than the imperial-appointed governor. The process was compressed but uneven: five of the six colonies achieved responsible government between 1855 and 1859; Western Australia waited until 1890 because its small settler population and continuing frontier violence delayed British approval.

Responsible government meant more than a constitutional transfer of power. It entailed the simultaneous abandonment of direct British oversight of Aboriginal affairs — a point the chapter (Curthoys and Mitchell) frames as the defining shadow-side of the process: 'with the decision in 1850 to grant the colonies of eastern Australia self-government, the Colonial Office prepared to surrender responsibility for the Aborigines to the very colonists whom they had frequently accused of trying to exterminate the tribes they encountered' (Henry Reynolds, quoted by Curthoys and Mitchell).

The process is distinct from the earlier granting of representative councils (the 1823 NSW Act established an appointed legislative council; the 1843 NSW Act created a partly elected council) and from Federation (1901). Its core moment was the 1852–55 period in which the British government authorised each colony to draft its own constitution along Canadian lines.

## Causal Drivers

**Canadian precedent and Durham Report (1839):** Armed rebellions in Upper and Lower Canada in 1837–38 forced a British rethink. The Durham Report recommended responsible government to retain North American colonies within the Empire. Earl Grey adopted these principles in 1846 and implemented them in Canada. Australian colonists then demanded the 'Canadian system'.

**British fiscal imperatives:** Britain wanted to transfer colonial administration costs to the colonists themselves, keep domestic taxes low, and rely on free-trade commercial ties rather than administrative controls to maintain imperial connections.

**Anti-transportation movement:** Opposition to convict transportation united diverse colonial constituencies — moralists, free labourers, middle classes — across the colonies. The Anti-Transportation League, formed in Van Diemen's Land in January 1849 under John West and the *Launceston Examiner*, spread to the mainland after 'exile' convict ships arrived in Melbourne and Sydney. Mass meetings in Sydney attracted up to 10,000 people. The *Hashemy*'s arrival in Sydney in June 1849 provoked the last convict landing in New South Wales.

**'Popular constitutionalism':** From the 1820s, colonists invoked 'Anglo-Saxon' liberties and the rights of Britons, appealing to the history of English representative government going back to the seventeenth century. This argument, characterised by historian Paul Pickering as 'popular constitutionalism', was not republican — it was characterised by declarations of loyalty to the crown, particularly Queen Victoria — but it demanded political rights equal to those of Britons at home.

**Gold rushes:** The British Colonial Secretary Pakington cited 'those extraordinary discoveries of Gold which have lately taken place' as a reason for the change in British policy in 1852, noting that they had 'imparted new and unforeseen features to their political and social condition'. Gold brought educated middle classes, urban radicalism, and political literacy — especially in Victoria, where the [[eureka-stockade-1854]] rebellion (December 1854) generated a democratic surge. See [[australian-gold-rushes-1851]].

**Squatter political organisation:** Squatters had won a dominant role in the partly elected 1843 legislature and used it to protect pastoral interests. Their influence shaped the franchise distribution in early constitutions toward rural seats. See [[australian-pastoral-expansion]].

## Major Phases

**Phase 1 — Representative councils (1823–50):** The 1823 NSW Act established the first appointed legislative council; the 1843 Act created a partly elected body (two-thirds elected on a limited franchise). By 1843 similar institutions existed or were being established across the eastern colonies. This was 'representative government' but not 'responsible government': the governor, not the elected parliament, held executive power.

**Phase 2 — Australian Colonies Government Act 1850:** This Act separated Port Phillip District into the new colony of Victoria, extended the partly elected legislative council model to Van Diemen's Land, South Australia and Victoria, widened the franchise (owners of freehold worth £100, or occupiers paying £10 annual rent, could vote), but did not grant responsible government. Reformers were disappointed; Van Diemen's Land was still receiving large convict shipments.

**Phase 3 — The breakthrough (1852–53):** In December 1852 Secretary of State Pakington announced transportation to Van Diemen's Land would end and that the four eastern colonies could draft constitutions 'similar to that of the United Canadas'. A further dispatch from the Duke of Newcastle in 1853 removed the requirement for a nominated upper house, giving each colony choice on its upper chamber form. Colonists were 'overjoyed'.

**Phase 4 — Constitutional debates and drafting (1853–55):** Each colony debated the form of its constitution, producing distinct results shaped by local conditions:
- **New South Wales**: Wentworth's proposal for a hereditary upper house was ridiculed as a 'bunyip aristocracy' by Daniel Deniehy; the council settled on a governor-nominated upper house and a lower house with a franchise skewed toward rural pastoralist seats.
- **Victoria**: After the Eureka rebellion, a proposed constitution balancing conservative and democratic interests was adopted — Assembly on wide franchise, Council elected by those with property worth £1,000; both favoured rural seats; 'strong bicameralism'.
- **South Australia**: The most democratic constitution. After Governor Young deceived the council into passing a conservative constitution and was exposed, new elections were held in 1855; the result provided universal male suffrage for the lower house, banned multiple voting. 
- **Van Diemen's Land/Tasmania**: The least liberal constitution, reflecting a population three-quarters composed of current or former convicts by 1851, economic stagnation, and conservative governance. Responsible government celebrated in 1856 alongside the colony's name change to Tasmania.

**Phase 5 — Implementation and democratic extension (1856–72):** The secret ballot was adopted by Tasmania, Victoria and South Australia in 1856, NSW in 1858, Queensland and Western Australia by the early 1860s–77. Manhood suffrage was introduced in South Australia (already held), Victoria (1857), and NSW (1858). Queensland, whose conservative legislature retained pastoral dominance through malapportionment (30 of 42 electorates dominated by pastoral interests by 1872), restored universal male suffrage in 1872, but removed the right from Aboriginal people in 1885 — the only colony to make this exclusion explicit by legislation.

**Phase 6 — Western Australia (1870–90):** Western Australia's tiny settler population (only 8,000 non-Aboriginal adults in 1874, two-thirds with convict antecedents) and continuing frontier violence delayed self-government thirty years. The Legislative Council rejected responsible government in 1878. Gold discoveries from 1885 and growing economic diversification shifted elite opinion; a bipartisan movement emerged. The Aboriginal question shaped the constitutional terms: Governor Broome's proposal that the Aborigines Protection Board remain responsible to the governor, funded by a reserved annual sum of £5,000, passed as the price for responsible government in 1890. The 1893 gold rush at Coolgardie and Kalgoorlie, bringing over 100,000 people, prompted the Constitution Act Amendment Act 1893, which removed property qualification for white male voters while retaining it for Aboriginal Australians, Asians, Africans, and people of mixed descent.

## Geographic Spread

The process affected all six Australian colonies but on different timescales and with different constitutional outcomes:
- Tasmania, Victoria, NSW, South Australia: responsible government 1855–56
- Queensland: separation and responsible government together 1859
- Western Australia: responsible government 1890

The process also shaped New Zealand (a separate constitution 1852) and was influenced by Canadian, Cape Colony, and British Chartist precedents — an imperial rather than merely colonial phenomenon.

**concurrent_with**: [[australian-gold-rushes-1851]] — gold accelerated the democratic politics that fed the constitutional movement, particularly in Victoria; gold discoveries were explicitly cited by the British government as justifying self-government.

**produced**: Abandonment of humanitarian Aboriginal protection by British authorities; devolution of Aboriginal policy to colonial governments that used it to entrench dispossession through protection boards and coercive regimes from the 1860s onward.

**part_of**: [[british-empire]] restructuring of settler-colony governance worldwide (same period: Canada, Cape Colony, New Zealand).

The Curthoys and Mitchell chapter argues against the existing historiography's separation of responsible government history from Aboriginal history: 'Australian historians of the origins of responsible government have not typically seen it as having any connection to the contemporaneous displacement and replacement of Aboriginal societies.' The chapter insists the two were structurally connected — colonists demanded political rights precisely in the context of an economy built on Aboriginal dispossession, and self-government transferred Aboriginal policy to the colonists who had the most to gain from continued dispossession.

## End Conditions

The process concluded with Western Australia's responsible government in 1890. Federation (1901) subsequently unified the six colonial parliaments into the Commonwealth, though the colonial constitutions survived as state constitutions. The exclusion of Aboriginal Australians from the constitutional polity — reinforced rather than resolved in each colonial constitution — persisted until the 1967 referendum and remains partially contested.

## Historiography

Two main historiographical debates are identified by Curthoys and Mitchell:

1. **British initiative vs. colonial initiative**: Some historians emphasise the role of British fiscal and strategic calculations (relief from administrative costs, maintaining empire without direct rule) in driving self-government; others emphasise colonial pressure, democratic agitation, and the anti-transportation movement. The chapter treats both as genuine drivers.

2. **The "struggle" narrative**: Terry Irving has argued there was 'no anti-imperial "struggle for responsible government" in the Australian colonies'; conflict was rather about who should rule once responsible government was achieved. The chapter accepts this nuance while insisting the process was nonetheless significant.

3. **The Aboriginal absence**: Most histories of responsible government ignore Aboriginal dispossession; most histories of Aboriginal-settler relations ignore the constitutional transition. The chapter argues this is a historiographical error: the two processes were structurally linked, and 'the process and memory of dispossession did shape colonists' sense of what it meant to be self-governing Britons'.
