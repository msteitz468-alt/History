# Pacific Whaling and Cetaceans

The long history of human relationships with whales in the Pacific — spanning
from the earliest arrival of *Homo sapiens* on Pacific shores to the present — is,
in Ryan Tucker Jones's framing, the single best lens for understanding the Pacific
as a unified historical space. Whale migrations integrate the ocean across thousands
of miles; Indigenous cultures built around cetaceans stretched from Siberia to New
Zealand; and commercial whaling after 1800 connected those traditions with
industrial capitalism in ways that reshaped Pacific societies, ecologies, and politics.

## Definition and Scope

This process encompasses: (1) Indigenous subsistence and ritual whaling cultures
around the North and South Pacific; (2) commercial sail whaling, principally Yankee
and European, from roughly 1800 to the 1870s; (3) industrial steam-powered factory
whaling from the 1860s to 1986; (4) the cetacean rights and anti-whaling movement
from the 1960s; and (5) the recovery of some species and the emergence of
whale-watching economies after the 1982 IWC moratorium.

What binds these phases is the argument Jones advances: that "stories of
inter-species relationships, illustrated especially well with whales and humans,
bring the history of the entire Pacific Ocean together" — cutting across Western
divisions between nations and between species.

> "The history and biogeography both suggest that the notoriously misnamed Pacific
> Ocean might instead be termed the Cetaceous, or the Kohola, or the Kondoli, or
> the Ecola Sea."
> (Jones, ch. 3, in Jones & Matsuda eds., *Cambridge History of the Pacific Ocean*,
> Vol. 1, 2022)

## Causal Drivers

- **Biological**: Modern whales evolved ~40 million years ago; distinct cetacean
  cultures appeared ~30 million years ago, linked to large brains, high sociability,
  and the need to share knowledge of distant feeding grounds over long migrations.
- **Human co-evolution**: Around 50,000 years ago, *Homo sapiens* arrived on Pacific
  shores. Both whales and humans are K-selected mammals with long childhoods,
  complex cultures, and deep reliance on kinship for cultural transmission; their
  co-evolutionary paths "mirrored each other."
- **Ecology**: Krill-rich cold polar waters in both hemispheres concentrated baleen
  whales; sperm whales gathered at upwelling zones (e.g., New Zealand's Canterbury
  Coast). Humans at high latitudes could not rely on agriculture, making whales
  crucial caloric sources.
- **Genealogical frameworks**: Indigenous Pacific cultures overwhelmingly treated
  whales as kin — Hawaiians with sharks, Rarotongans and Marquesans with whales,
  Ngarrindjeri (southern Australia) with *Kondoli* (southern right whales), Māori
  with *tohorā* (sperm whales). These frameworks simultaneously enabled and
  regulated hunting.
- **Commercial capitalism**: After 1800, Euro-American exclusion of cetaceans from
  community meant no limits beyond profitability; this enabled industrial-scale slaughter.

## Major Phases

### Phase 1: Indigenous North Pacific Whaling Cultures (pre-contact)

The earliest Pacific whaling cultures developed on the Siberian coast, where cold
oceans "foreclosed the agricultural ecologies humans to the south often made."
The practice spread in an arc from Siberia across Japan, Alaska, British Columbia,
Washington State, and Oregon. American anthropologist Margaret Lantis termed
the coherent body of rituals and beliefs the "Alaskan Whale Cult" (1938), unifying
practices across vast stretches.

Key features:
- Whales were understood to possess minds, intentions, and culture. The Iñupiat
  said: "The whales, they give themselves" — a compact in which a whale's voluntary
  sacrifice sustained human communities; failure to give oneself would "condemn
  humans to die."
- Ritual preparation: Nuu-chah-nulth whalers anointed themselves with the
  decomposing fat of legendary dead whalers, imitated whale surfacing and diving,
  while the whaler's wife "became the whale's soul" — the marriage bond and the
  harpoon line expressed as one metaphor.
- Iñupiat whalers gently struck their wives with the harpoon before a bowhead hunt;
  during the hunt she sat immobile while "the whaler's wife 'was the whale's soul'."
  Whale bones then contributed to building the iglu's entryway.
- Aleuts who hunted successfully "were expected later to be driven insane by the
  secret evil they had committed" — an honest moral reckoning with the killing of
  "a creature so obviously intelligent."

### Phase 2: Indigenous South Pacific Frameworks

Whaling was not generally practised as a hunt in the South Pacific, but genealogical
kinship with cetaceans was equally present:
- **Ngarrindjeri** (southern Australia) sang *Kondoli* (southern right whales) to
  shore; the power to do so "had been gained through a genealogy that included
  whale ancestors."
- **Fiji's *tabua*** (sperm whale tooth) encoded chiefly power, the marriage bond,
  and female reproductive symbolism simultaneously.
- Chukchi oral tradition told of a human woman who married a whale-man; their
  first children were whale calves; later human children who "forgot their kinship"
  and hunted whales brought grief and death to their mother.
- Māori genealogies placed *tohorā* within the origin pantheon: in Ihimaera's telling,
  sharks and whales originally lived in lakes and were granted the liberty of the ocean
  by petition to Tangaroa. Māori followed whale roads (*hwaelweg*), and in one version
  of the Paikea story the ancestor Kahutia Te Rangi shapeshifted into a whale.
  In 1970, fifty-nine stranded sperm whales on an Aotearoan beach were declared
  *tangata* — human.

### Phase 3: Commercial Shore Whaling and Colonial Entanglement (c. 1800–1870)

British and French whalers entered the South Pacific after 1800, targeting right
whales in the Antarctic convergence. Shore-whaling stations appeared in Tasmania
(the Danish adventurer Jørgen Jørgensen killed the first right whale in Tasmania
in 1805), then South Australia, then Aotearoa.

Key dynamics:
- In Tasmania, "only 6 per cent of right whales pursued escaped death" because
  the whales' defensive tactic (stopping and fighting) was suited to killer whales
  but made them more vulnerable to harpoons.
- In New Zealand, European whalers had to marry into Māori *iwi* to receive
  permission to use shore land and protection. Kāi Tahu women "domesticated"
  European men into iwi genealogies; women actively assisted shore whaling
  through maritime labour and sometimes harpooned small porpoises.
- Cross-cultural kinship produced mixed-race shore communities; their
  collapse followed the collapse of the whale populations, to be replaced by
  settler-colonial agriculture.
- In Aotearoa's South Auckland Islands in the 1840s, right whales that had been
  hunted *learned* to flee rather than fight: "97 per cent" survived. A mother who
  fled, however, abandoned her calf, severing the transmission of cultural
  knowledge. Calving ground memory — "a type of cultural memory" — was lost,
  and right whales "no longer use many of the Australian and Aotearoan lagoons
  their ancestors did, even after human persecution ceased."
- Japanese coastal stations that had "diminished local whale populations for
  centuries" closed completely when Yankee pelagic whalers decimated right whales
  offshore. Whalers from Taiji violated a longstanding taboo against killing mothers
  with calves; 111 men drowned in a subsequent accident.

### Phase 4: Yankee Pelagic Whaling and the Industrial Transition (c. 1820–1880)

Euro-American pelagic whalers crewed ships manned by racially stratified,
itinerant communities — Polynesians, Portuguese, African Americans, Azoreans
forming the largest groups after white Americans. Life aboard was structured by
professional, racial, and class hierarchies, yet ships created their own trans-Pacific
kinship networks.

Herman Melville's *Moby-Dick* (1851) centred on familial formations: Ishmael and
Queequeg as "a joint-stock company of two," Captain Ahab as "abusive father."
Melville got sperm whale social structure wrong (they do not form harems): female
sperm whales form tight-knit matrilineal groups in tropical waters, with grandmothers
and aunts taking turns guarding calves while others dive for squid. Post-menopausal
female sperm whales — like killer whales and humans — survive long past reproductive
years, likely guiding pods with long memories of El Niño patterns.

### Phase 5: Industrial Steam Whaling and Near-Extinction (c. 1870–1982)

Norwegian environmental imperialism opened factory stations from Kamchatka to
Queensland. Grenade-tipped harpoon guns and stern slipways now targeted previously
inaccessible species (blues, fins, seis). Soviet and Japanese factory whaling reached
peak destruction in the 1960s after Antarctic stocks collapsed. Illegal Soviet whaling
in Alaskan waters secretly killed hundreds of blue whales and nearly extinct North
Pacific right whales. One Soviet station in the Kuril Islands recorded 95 per cent of
catch as nursing or pregnant sperm whale mothers.

Whale stress hormones preserved in annual earwax layers showed cortisol "jumped
in the 1930s, fell during the war years of the 1940s, and then rose dramatically
through about 1963, the peak year of post-war whaling."

By the 1980s: only several hundred blue whales remained in either hemisphere;
perhaps a few thousand sei whales; humpbacks "disappeared from Hawaiian waters
almost entirely" in the late 1970s. In New Zealand, only about 25 breeding female
southern right whales remained.

> "The Pacific, for the first time in 40 million years, had ceased to be the
> cetacean-rich sea that had drawn so many humans in search of energy and kin."
> (Jones, ch. 3)

### Phase 6: Anti-Whaling Movement, Moratorium, and Recovery (1960s–present)

The live-capture orca industry centred on Puget Sound in the 1960s revealed that
killer whales rarely attacked humans and maintained lifelong mother–offspring bonds.
By the 1980s "each orca in the Pacific Northwest now had an alphanumeric label,
a family tree, and even affectionate nicknames."

In 1975, Greenpeace piloted zodiacs between Soviet whalers and sperm whale females
and calves off California, broadcasting footage worldwide. Paul Watson stood on the
dying body of a sperm whale calf to demonstrate Soviet targeting of "whole pods en
masse, right down to the children." US financial pressure on Japan and the Soviet
Union combined with global activism to produce the 1982 IWC moratorium on
commercial whaling.

Since the moratorium, humpbacks have rebounded impressively; grey whales in Baja
California "friendly" lagoons now actively approach whale-watching boats and push
their calves toward them — a shift in grey whale culture that has spread to Bering
Sea populations. Whale-watching in Kaikoura, New Zealand, is run by Ngāti Porou
*iwi* since 1987.

## End Conditions / Ongoing Nature

The process is ongoing. The 1982 IWC moratorium halted commercial whaling
globally, though Japan, Norway, and Iceland have maintained limited hunts.
Indigenous subsistence whaling continues (Iñupiat bowhead; Makah after legal
recognition of treaty rights; Chukchi). Some species (humpback, grey) have
recovered substantially; others (blue, North Pacific right) remain far below
pre-whaling numbers. The cultural dimensions — kinship frameworks, whale-watching,
legal personhood debates, Lummi Nation's 2019 suit for return of a captive orca —
continue to evolve, recapitulating debates about community, kinship, and power that
have structured Pacific whale history across millennia.

## Historiography and Primary Sources

Jones acknowledges the chapter draws on his own prior work — "Running into Whales:
The History of the North Paciﬁc from Below the Waves" (*American Historical Review*
118:2, 2013) and *Red Leviathan: The Secret History of Soviet Whaling* (2022) — as
well as Bathsheba Demuth's *Floating Coast* (2019) on the Bering Strait, and an
edited volume *Across Species and Cultures: Whales, Humans and Pacific Worlds*
(Jones & Wanhalla eds., 2022).

Key methodological argument: inter-species history is not a "boggy, soggy, squitchy
picture" of mystical harmony (Melville's phrase) but a rigorous examination of
power, coercion, genealogy, and kinship operating between species as much as
within them. Indigenous genealogical frameworks that include non-humans challenge
Western assumptions about the boundaries of community and historical agency.
