# Indian Feudalism (the Samanta System)

**"Indian feudalism"** is the model — most influentially advanced by Romila Thapar and D. D.
Kosambi/R. S. Sharma — that the political economy of early-medieval India (c. 800–1200) was
structured by a **feudatory (*samanta*) hierarchy** resting on **land/revenue grants**, analogous to
(though not identical with) European feudalism. Documented for the wiki through Thapar's
[[thapar-history-of-india-v1-1966|*A History of India*, Vol. I]] (1966).

## Origins: the land grant

The seed, Thapar argues, was the practice — spreading from the **[[gupta-empire|Gupta]]** period — of
granting **tax-free land** to **brahmans and religious establishments** (who owed neither tax nor
military service). From **c. 1000 CE** grants increasingly went to **officials and military vassals**
in return for service. In theory only the **revenue** (not the land) was granted, for the grantee's
lifetime, revocable on failure; in practice grants became **hereditary** (one brahman ministerial
family is recorded holding a benefice through five generations), and from hereditary revenue it was
"a simple step" to assume disposal of the land itself.

## The samanta hierarchy

The result was a graded order of **feudatories** bound to the king by obligations — attendance at
court, permission for administrative changes, and above all the duty to **supply troops** — and
rewarded with titles and dignities (*mahasamanta*, *mahamandaleshvara* for the great; *raja*,
*samanta*, *ranaka*, *thakura* for the lesser) and symbols (throne, fly-whisk, palanquin, the "five
instruments"). The system "lent itself admirably to the rise of the **[[rajputs|Rajput]] clans**,"
whose estates were reckoned in standard village-units (the Pratihara group of **eighty-four
villages**). **Sub-infeudation** multiplied intermediaries, diffusing the income of the land away
from **both ends of the scale — the king and the cultivator**.

## Economic and political consequences

Thapar links the system to a **closed, low-trade economy**: self-sufficient villages producing at
**subsistence level** (no incentive to a surplus that the landowner would simply claim), a **decline
in coinage** and in long-distance trade (worsened by proliferating local weights and measures), and
the investment of surplus not in trade or craft but in **conspicuous consumption** — the lavish
temples that, in a grim irony, drew the plundering [[mahmud-of-ghazni|raids of Mahmud]]. The
peasantry was **depressed** by a high land tax (often one-sixth, sometimes one-third), additional
dues levied by intermediaries and temples, forced labour, and the appropriation of common pasture;
the **king was politically weakened**, dependent on vassals who could withhold their levies. This
fragmentation — not "fatalism" or any racial trait, Thapar insists — explains the **disunity** that
left north India unable to resist the Turkish conquest.

## End conditions and debate

The system was **transformed** (not abolished) by the [[delhi-sultanate|Delhi Sultanate]], which
overlaid it with the **iqta** (revenue-assignment) administration. The "Indian feudalism" model is
**contested**: critics argue the European analogy is misleading (no manorialism, serfdom, or vassalic
contract of the Western type; Thapar herself notes "feudatory" in the southern context meant
*political vassalage*, not feudal tenure), and that the picture of a collapsed trade economy is
overdrawn. It remains, nonetheless, the dominant materialist framework for the period and a structural
explanation Thapar uses throughout the volume.
