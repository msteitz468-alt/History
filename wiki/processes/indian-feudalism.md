# Indian Feudalism (the Samanta System)

**"Indian feudalism"** is the model — most influentially advanced by Romila Thapar and D. D.
Kosambi/R. S. Sharma — that the political economy of early-medieval India (c. 800–1200) was
structured by a **feudatory (*samanta*) hierarchy** resting on **land/revenue grants**, analogous to
(though not identical with) European feudalism. Documented for the wiki through Thapar's
[[thapar-history-of-india-v1-1966|*A History of India*, Vol. I]] (1966).

## Origins: the land grant

The seed, Thapar argues, was the practice — spreading from the **[[gupta-empire|Gupta]]** period — of
granting **tax-free land** to **brahmans and religious establishments** (who owed neither tax nor
military service). From **c. 1000 CE** grants increasingly went to **officials and military vassals**
in return for service. In theory only the **revenue** (not the land) was granted, for the grantee's
lifetime, revocable on failure; in practice grants became **hereditary** (one brahman ministerial
family is recorded holding a benefice through five generations), and from hereditary revenue it was
"a simple step" to assume disposal of the land itself.

## The samanta hierarchy

The result was a graded order of **feudatories** bound to the king by obligations — attendance at
court, permission for administrative changes, and above all the duty to **supply troops** — and
rewarded with titles and dignities (*mahasamanta*, *mahamandaleshvara* for the great; *raja*,
*samanta*, *ranaka*, *thakura* for the lesser) and symbols (throne, fly-whisk, palanquin, the "five
instruments"). The system "lent itself admirably to the rise of the **[[rajputs|Rajput]] clans**,"
whose estates were reckoned in standard village-units (the Pratihara group of **eighty-four
villages**). **Sub-infeudation** multiplied intermediaries, diffusing the income of the land away
from **both ends of the scale — the king and the cultivator**.

## Economic and political consequences

Thapar links the system to a **closed, low-trade economy**: self-sufficient villages producing at
**subsistence level** (no incentive to a surplus that the landowner would simply claim), a **decline
in coinage** and in long-distance trade (worsened by proliferating local weights and measures), and
the investment of surplus not in trade or craft but in **conspicuous consumption** — the lavish
temples that, in a grim irony, drew the plundering [[mahmud-of-ghazni|raids of Mahmud]]. The
peasantry was **depressed** by a high land tax (often one-sixth, sometimes one-third), additional
dues levied by intermediaries and temples, forced labour, and the appropriation of common pasture;
the **king was politically weakened**, dependent on vassals who could withhold their levies. This
fragmentation — not "fatalism" or any racial trait, Thapar insists — explains the **disunity** that
left north India unable to resist the Turkish conquest.

## End conditions and debate

The system was **transformed** (not abolished) by the [[delhi-sultanate|Delhi Sultanate]], which
overlaid it with the **iqta** (revenue-assignment) administration. The "Indian feudalism" model is
**contested**: critics argue the European analogy is misleading (no manorialism, serfdom, or vassalic
contract of the Western type; Thapar herself notes "feudatory" in the southern context meant
*political vassalage*, not feudal tenure), and that the picture of a collapsed trade economy is
overdrawn. It remains, nonetheless, the dominant materialist framework for the period and a structural
explanation Thapar uses throughout the volume.

## Avari's Account (Chapter 10): The Feudalism Debate in Detail

Avari's *India: The Ancient Past* (2007) provides the most extended treatment of the feudalism question yet encountered in the wiki, engaging directly with the historiographical debate.

**The European concept**: Avari opens the discussion by defining feudalism in its European context — the pre-capitalist mode of production based on lord-peasant relations, labour service, and judicial power — as articulated by Marx and summarised by Frank Williamson: "essentially 'a system based primarily on the exploitation of the peasantry.'"

**R.S. Sharma's thesis**: "The distinguished twentieth century historian **R.S. Sharma** has argued that the political, social and economic development of India during the period [c. 750–1000] can be characterised as feudal." Sharma's evidence: "widespread disruption in the post-Gupta period, such as the loss of public revenues through the decline of trade and debased coinage, the issuing of **land grants (fiefs)** by monarchs to their subordinates, the subjugation of the peasantry by landed intermediaries, and the rise of **religious devotional movements** emphasising loyalty and reverence in general." The Puranas' descriptions of social disorientation and breakdown of loyalties are also invoked.

**The counter-arguments**: Against Sharma, it is contended that the post-Gupta period "witnessed a growth in state power through a continual series of state formations in the form of new regional kingdoms." Land grants "were not a sign of the sovereign's weakness, but a method of legitimising his authority." A key empirical objection: "peasant subjugations increased from the thirteenth century onwards under the **Delhi Sultanate**, which made comparatively few land concessions" — so land grants cannot explain peasant oppression if the period of maximum subjugation postdates land-grant feudalism. The decline of trade and coinage is also "fiercely contested."

**Avari's position**: While acknowledging the counter-arguments, Avari maintains that "there is still a core of evidence that lends support to the essentials of Sharma's analysis." The inscriptions provide strong evidence: "The king parcelled out lands to groups of people for different reasons — either to the brahmans or to royal officers and bureaucrats — and with the land concessions went the attachment of the rights of **land revenue**." Grantees acquired "a range of fiscal and judicial rights over taxes and rents" and were "not obliged to pay taxes themselves." Large grants to dynastic collaterals created feudatory princes who raised levies for the king's armies, consented to marriage alliances, and attended court. These princes "became all-powerful in their own domains, enjoying luxurious lifestyles in their palaces and castles. They, too, in turn, created a subordinate layer of lesser feudatories; and in this way the feudal structure and power spread."

**Mechanisms of kingdom formation**: Avari adds detail on how new kingdoms arose within the feudal system: (1) recipients of earlier land grants "broke loose from the existing kingdom and formed new polities"; (2) as "utility lands were being increasingly wrested from forested terrain, new territories emerged under new names, with local chieftains sometimes assuming a dominant role"; (3) "forest dwellers, who normally lived in a pre-state situation, were being increasingly drawn into the processes of state formation through interaction with the sedentary peasant societies." The caste system reinforced all of this: "the kingdom model, based on the girder of the **brahman-kshatriya alliance**, became the standard for all states."

**Commerce and the feudalism question**: Avari challenges the classic "commercial decline" narrative associated with the feudal thesis. He argues commerce "never really died out; it merely changed its orientation." The Islamic commercial empire absorbed Indian exports; the Chinese market remained buoyant; "agricultural production generally expanded as a result of the opening up of new lands, partly through land grants." The third phase of Indian urbanisation was underway — older imperial cities like [[pataliputra|Pataliputra]] declined while new regional centres arose. Market exchanges (*hatta* in north India, *nagaram* in the south), guild activity, fairs, and pilgrimage centres all sustained an urban-commercial life through the "feudal" period.

**Caste and feudal exploitation**: Avari shows how the two systems reinforced each other. In the north, "various categories of merchants, artisans or even semi-professional people such as scribes" sought higher ritual status. Non-caste peoples absorbed into Brahmanisation "swelled the ranks of the shudra caste." Those performing the most menial tasks "became part of the **untouchable groups**. Their large numbers ensured the relative comfort of all those above them, and this helped to give feudalism a strong lease of life. In the final analysis, feudalism was, and remains, an oppressive system of exploitation."
