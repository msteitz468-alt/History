## Definition and Scope

**Complementary dependence** is Hodgson's term for the characteristic economic and political order that the [[great-western-transmutation|Great Western Transmutation]] imposed on non-technicalized societies integrated into the world market of the Technical Age. He defines it as "a régime in polity and economy of complementary dependence on one or more Western lands": the dependent land produced raw materials for Western industrial processes; in return it received cheap finished goods from Western industries, displacing local craftsmen and turning the land "more solidly to agriculture than before."

The term appears as part of Hodgson's analysis of the [[generation-of-1789-islam|generation of 1789]] and its impact on [[islamicate|Islamicate]] societies, but he presents it as a structural feature of the Technical Age affecting all non-technicalized regions, not specifically Islamic ones.

## Structural Features

**Economic.** The dependent land's economy was redirected from internal food and craft production toward export commodities keyed to Western industrial demand. Hodgson's primary examples: Bengal redirected toward jute and indigo; Egypt redirected toward long-staple cotton for British textile mills; Turkistan toward raw cotton for the Russian market. In each case, local craft industries (particularly textiles) were destroyed by competition from machine-produced Western goods. Agriculture became "increasingly turned not to local food production but to raw materials for the industrialized sector in the world market."

**Political.** The complementary dependent order required "some sort of absolutism in which the various alternative loci of power typical of pre-Technical times were eliminated" — a polity that could guarantee the "kind of order necessary to carry out such a trade and prevent local attempts at deviation from the pattern." This absolutism could be established either by Western rulers (Bengal/India) or by local reformers responding autonomously to Western pressure (Memed-Ali's Egypt, Ottoman reform). The outcome was structurally similar regardless.

**Social.** The destruction of craft industries weakened the urban artisan-merchant class that had been a foundation of Islamicate social and political life. Population increased (modern medicine suppressing epidemic disease) without corresponding economic development, producing rural crowding and declining social mobility. The new ruling class — whether British colonial administrators or Westernizing local reformers — was technicalistic in character, approachable only "through a thoroughgoing redesigning of one's personality in a complete educational experience." This created sharp cultural bifurcation between a Westernized elite and a population still operating within traditional frameworks but now dependent on an alien market structure.

## Mechanism — Myrdal's Backwash Effects

Hodgson cites Gunnar Myrdal's *Rich Lands and Poor* (1957) as the best theoretical account of the economic mechanism. Myrdal showed that unhampered market operation "tends to exaggerate any initial inequalities through the cumulative effects of even slight differences, and that in particular it intensifies differences between regions." Any advantage a more developed region holds is reinforced by market integration; the less developed region suffers "backwash effects" — its own capacity for independent production is undermined — while "spread effects" (stimulus from proximity to the developed region) are normally much weaker. The nineteenth-century world market "illustrated this principle excellently."

Hodgson adds a note distinguishing the situation of long-urbanized, highly cultivated societies (the Islamicate lands) from remoter non-citied areas: in the former, "the population explosion came before industrialization, and so made the creation of an agricultural excess for the transition period into industrialization internally very difficult."

## Cases

| Region | Form of dependence | Primary export | Established by |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bengal / India | Direct British rule | Jute, indigo, raw cotton | British colonial administration (1772–1818) |
| Egypt | Autonomous local reform + market integration | Long-staple cotton | Memed-Ali (from c. 1820) |
| Ottoman empire | Nominal independence + forced commercial openness | Various raw materials | European treaty pressure; internal reform (Tanzimat) |
| Turkistan | Integration into Russian market via Volga Tatar merchants | Raw cotton | Tatar commercial expansion (generation of 1789 onward) |

## Distinction from "Colonialism"

Hodgson notes explicitly that "it made no decisive difference in this respect whether there was actual Western rule or not." Both outright colonies and formally "independent" states were caught in the complementary dependent order if they could not industrialize pari passu with the West. The political distinction between colony and nominally independent state mattered for local power relations, cultural identity, and the character of reforming movements, but not for the fundamental economic structure.

This is an important analytical point against both colonial apologists (who argued that formal independence showed no Western imposition) and some nationalist narratives (which located the problem primarily in the fact of colonial rule rather than in the structural features of market integration).

## End Conditions

Hodgson presents complementary dependence as a feature of the Technical Age that persists as long as the development gap persists. Whether and how non-technicalized societies could escape it was the central political and intellectual question of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries for Islamicate and other non-Western societies.
