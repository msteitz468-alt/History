## Definition and Scope

The post-war Australian mass immigration program was a state-directed demographic expansion launched under Labor's Arthur Calwell with the slogan "populate or perish." Beginning in 1945–47, it drew settlers first from Britain and Ireland, then from Europe's refugee camps, then more broadly from European nations (Australia signed immigration agreements with seven European countries including Italy and Greece). The program was explicitly premised on cultural selectivity — the [[white-australia-policy]] set the racial framework — but the need for sheer numbers pushed the boundaries progressively wider. By the early 1980s the program had transformed Australia from a population of 8 million in 1949 to nearly 15 million in 1981, with profound effects on culture, economy, and politics.

## Causal Drivers

The program was driven by a strategic fear: that Australia's small population left it unable to defend a continent. "Populate or perish" expressed a security logic as much as an economic one. Secondary drivers included the post-war labour shortage (a booming economy, large infrastructure projects, pent-up consumer demand), the availability of European refugees, and — under the Liberal governments — the political calculation that new migrants would be socially conservative and supportive of the Coalition.

Economic incentives structured the scheme: those on assisted passages were bound to designated jobs for a specified period, many working on large public infrastructure projects such as the Snowy Mountains hydro-electric scheme. Critically, migrants "were included in the award system of wages, which had been a condition of union support for government-sponsored migration. Thus whatever cultural marginalisation they experienced was not compounded by economic exploitation."

## Major Phases

**Phase 1 (1945–early 1950s): UK and European refugees.** Australian population grew; Britain and Ireland were the preferred sources but European displaced persons quickly became a major stream.

**Phase 2 (mid-1950s–1966): Broadening European intake and incremental reform.** European migrants (Italians, Greeks prominently) transformed inner-city working-class neighbourhoods. Suburban expansion absorbed migrant families alongside post-war newlyweds. Incremental reforms to the White Australia policy: in 1958 the dictation test was dropped from the new Migration Act; conditions of citizenship were relaxed; Japanese war brides admitted from 1952. Publicly, governments opposed easing restrictions on non-European migration while quietly making small accommodations.

**Phase 3 (1966–72): Holt's liberalisation.** Harold Holt "resurrected proposals for liberalising Australia's immigration policy that Menzies had blocked." Reform was in *sotto voce* — rhetorically conservative but practically increasing non-European settlement. The Colombo Plan brought Asian students to Australia from 1950; diplomatic pressure from newly independent Asian nations (especially India) was persistent.

**Phase 4 (1972–75): Whitlam and the formal end of White Australia.** The Whitlam government "erased the last vestiges of the white Australia policy." Immigration Minister Al Grassby in the Philippines in 1973: "give me a shovel and I will bury it." Australia formally transitioned from Anglo-monoculturalism to racial pluralism.

**Phase 5 (1975–83): Fraser and multiculturalism.** After immigration fell to a post-war low under Whitlam, the Coalition substantially boosted intake. In the second half of the 1970s Asians made up around one-third of the immigration intake. A substantial number were Indochinese refugees fleeing after the fall of Saigon in 1975. Fraser "insisted on accepting these refugees, some of whom journeyed to Australia in rickety vessels across treacherous seas." The Special Broadcasting Service (SBS), established 1978, served non-English-speaking communities. By the early 1980s 14.1 per cent of the population had been born outside Australia or the United Kingdom, including a nearly fourfold rise in Australians born in Asia over the preceding fifteen years.

## Geographic Spread

Post-war migration was concentrated in capital cities. New migrants replaced existing working-class residents in inner-city areas as those residents moved to new suburban houses, and migrant men took jobs in car and whitegoods factories. Some regional towns (Griffith and Mildura were examples, with Italian prominence in horticulture) also developed distinctive ethnic character.

## Interaction with Other Processes

The immigration program was the most significant driver of Australia's population growth and cultural transformation in the period. It interacted with:
- The suburban building boom (home ownership reached 70 per cent by the 1960s, partly driven by migrant demand)
- [[australian-cold-war-politics]] (the Colombo Plan as Cold War diplomacy; migrants from Eastern Europe and Baltic states supporting DLP anti-communism)
- The [[white-australia-policy]] (the immigration program progressively eroded from within)
- The 1970s social movements — the growing non-Anglo population contributed to multiculturalism as a political program

## End Conditions

The White Australia policy was formally ended in the Whitlam period (1972–75) and multiculturalism was embraced as official policy under Fraser. The mass program as a demographic-strategic project moderated in the 1970s as economic conditions tightened, though immigration (now non-discriminatory) remained substantial. The process did not end so much as transform: from demographic-security strategy under racially exclusive terms to multicultural nation-building from the mid-1970s.
