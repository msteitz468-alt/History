# The Japanese Economic Miracle (1945–1973)

The era of **rapid, sustained economic growth** that transformed defeated, devastated
[[japan|Japan]] into the world's second-largest economy in a single generation — averaging
near **10% annual GNP growth** at its peak and turning "Imperial Japan" into "Japan
Incorporated." From the [[cambridge-history-japan-v6-twentieth-century|Cambridge History of
Japan Vol. 6]] (Kosai Yutaka, ch. 10 — the postwar economy; with Nakamura Takafusa, ch. 9,
and Crawcour, ch. 8, on the prewar foundations).

## Definition and Scope

Editor Peter Duus's economic theme for the century is **"from success to success"** —
continuous modern growth "interrupted but not broken" by the mid-century upheavals. The
postwar "miracle" runs from recovery in the late 1940s through the high-growth decades
(c. 1955–1973), ending with the **1972–73 "shocks"** (the yen revaluation and the oil crisis)
that closed the era of rapid growth. Crawcour and Nakamura stress the **prewar foundations**:
modern growth from the 1880s, the build-out of heavy industry, and the 1930s
**recovery and wartime** expansion of the heavy-and-chemical sector that — for all the
destruction — left technical and organizational capacity in place.

## Causal Drivers

- **The Occupation reforms.** Kosai foregrounds three "democratization" reforms with lasting
  economic effect: **land reform** (1946–47, "almost equivalent to confiscation" — tenanted
  land fell from ~46% to ~10%, creating a stable class of owner-farmers); the **dissolution of
  the zaibatsu** (breaking up the old family combines, though successor *keiretsu* groups
  re-formed); and **labor reform** (legalizing unions). Together these broadened the home
  market and defused prewar social tensions.
- **High savings and investment.** Exceptional household savings funneled through banks into
  heavy industrial investment.
- **Labor and demography.** A skilled, educated, and (into the 1960s) growing labor force — a
  **demographic dividend** as the high-birthrate 1930s cohorts reached working age and surplus
  agricultural labor shifted to industry.
- **Technology and policy.** Large-scale **import of foreign technology**; an
  export-oriented **industrial policy** (MITI guidance, undervalued yen under Bretton Woods);
  and — crucially — the **Cold War context**, which gave Japan a US security guarantee, a
  **light defense burden**, and the stimulus of the Korean War "procurement boom."

## Major Phases

- **Reform and recovery (1945–55):** rebuilding from devastation; the **Dodge Line**
  stabilization; the **Korean War (1950)** boom.
- **High growth (1955–73):** double-digit growth, the radical **shift of the industrial
  structure** from agriculture and light industry to heavy-and-chemical and then consumer
  durables and electronics; mass migration to the cities (employment in agriculture fell from
  ~16 million in 1950 to ~7 million by 1975).

## Characteristics: the Japanese enterprise system

Kosai analyzes the distinctive **Japanese enterprise** institutions that crystallized in this
era: long-term (often "lifetime") employment and seniority wages in the large firms; enterprise
unions; close bank–firm and inter-firm (*keiretsu*) ties; and a persistent **"dual structure"**
— large modern firms alongside a low-wage small-enterprise sector, the wage gap a structural
feature of the labor market.

## Interaction

The miracle is the economic dimension of postwar Japan's broader passage **"from instability
to stability"** ([[japanese-militarism|Fukui's]] political stabilization under the LDP). It
extended the long arc of [[industrialization|Japanese industrialization]] begun in
[[meiji-period|Meiji]], and became the **model for later East Asian growth** (Korea, Taiwan,
then China).

## End Conditions

The high-growth era ended with the **1973 oil shock** and the end of the Bretton Woods fixed
exchange rate; Japan thereafter shifted to slower, more mature growth — the boundary at which
CHJ Vol. 6 closes its account.
