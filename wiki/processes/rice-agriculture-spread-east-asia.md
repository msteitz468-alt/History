# The Spread of Rice Agriculture in East Asia

The long transmission of domesticated rice (*Oryza sativa*, from wild *O. rufipogon*) from its Yangzi
Basin hearth across the [[yellow-sea-interaction-sphere|Yellow Sea sphere]] into the Korean Peninsula
and the Japanese Islands — one of [[barnes-archaeology-east-asia-2015|Barnes's]] clearest cases of the
**Pen/Insular developmental lag** and of technology moving by sea.

## Hearth and Tempo on the Mainland

Collection or incipient cultivation of wild rice in the Yangzi drainage began **9000–8000 BC** among
non-sedentary groups; sedentary "in-between" communities developed after 7000 BC, but **morphological
domestication only fixed around 4000 BC** — late by older claims. At [[early-agriculture-china|Jiahu]],
on the northern edge of wild-rice range, domestication ran faster (less back-crossing with wild forms).
The earliest **paddy fields** (Chodun, Caoxieshan, ~4300–4000 BC) were tiny plots (1–15 m²) cut into
subsoil beside canals — Barnes's "puddle-field agriculture." Even after rice entered the diet, gathered
plants long dominated: acorns were still 68% of plant remains at Tianluoshan in the mid-5th millennium.
Rice and northern millet were divided by the Qinling–Huai line; the **Shandong peninsula** is the
acknowledged route by which rice moved northeast.

## Across the Yellow Sea to Korea

Dryland rice is postulated to have reached **Liaodong from Shandong ~2500 BC**, irrigated wet-rice
following ~1500 BC by the same route. Rice appears at **Dazuizi** (Liaodong, 1300–1100 BC); the
earliest Korean Peninsula grains cluster **1100–900 BC**, with permanent-field production after ~950 BC
— the agricultural marker of the [[mumun-culture|Mumun]] period. Paddy with bunds and irrigation canals
is known from ten southern sites (e.g. the 7th-century-BC Okhyon fields beside a 60-house village).
Barnes notes the live debate over **migration vs. adoption**, and the newer hypothesis that the
**Japonic** (not Tungusic) language family was carried from Shandong through Liaodong with the rice
farmers.

## Across the Korea Strait to Japan

The southwestern Peninsula was the springboard for wet rice into Final [[jomon-culture|Jomon]] North
Kyushu. The earliest Japanese paddy fields date to **970 cal. BC**; rice arrived not alone but as a
**Mumun "package"** — field systems, polished stone reapers, grooved axes, spindle whorls, dolmen
burial, ditched settlements and Songguk-ri pit-buildings — initiating the [[yayoi-culture|Yayoi]] by
"bilateral acculturation" (the Etsuji and Shinmachi cemeteries show locals and immigrants side by side).
The **triangular reaping knife**, which harvested only grain-heads, selected for early-ripening,
**cold-resistant** varieties, enabling rice to spread east through the Inland Sea to the waist of Honshu
by ~400 cal. BC and, haltingly, toward Tohoku.

## Where It Stopped

Rice farming was **not** adopted everywhere. Northeastern (Tohoku) Jomon, secure in deciduous forest,
salmon runs and marine resources, **resisted wet rice** even when "offered on their doorstep,"
intensifying community ritual instead; only from Middle Yayoi did Tohoku "Yayoinise," and Hokkaido
(Epi-Jomon) and the Ryukyus stayed outside the agricultural sphere altogether though linked by trade.
The selectivity is central to Barnes's argument that the Pen/Insular societies *chose* among Mainland
offerings rather than passively receiving them.
