# Sufism in South Asia

## Definition and Scope

Sufism in South Asia designates the transplantation and transformation of Islamic mysticism across the Indo-Pakistani Subcontinent from the Arab conquest of Sind (711 CE) to the present. It is distinct from the broader [[sufism]] process in that it developed its own orders (principally the [[chishtiyya]]), a rich vernacular literary tradition, and a characteristic mode of popular piety centred on the dargāh (shrine of a saint) that has no close analogue elsewhere in the Muslim world. Schimmel characterises it as the primary vehicle of Islamization: "the Islamization of the country was achieved largely by the preaching of the dervishes, not by the sword" ([[schimmel-mystical-dimensions-of-islam-1975]]).

## Causal Drivers

### First wave: Arab conquest and early contacts (711–1000)

The Arab conquest of Sind (711) introduced Muslim piety into the Subcontinent, though early Muslim activity centred on ḥadīth collection and scientific transmission rather than mysticism. The mystic Ḥallāj travelled in Sind in 905, probably engaging Hindu and Buddhist sages; early "European theories that tried to explain Sufism as an Islamized form of Vedanta philosophy or of Yoga have now been discarded" (Schimmel).

### Second wave: Ghaznawid and Delhi Sultanate (1000–1200)

The Ghaznawid invasions from ca. 1000 brought Persian Muslim culture, including Hujwīrī (whose *Kashf al-maḥjūb*, the first Persian treatise on Sufism, was composed in Lahore), and established Lahore as the first centre of Persian-inspired Muslim culture. His tomb remains a Punjabi pilgrimage site.

### Third wave: Classical orders settle India (1193–ca. 1400)

The decisive moment was the late twelfth and early thirteenth centuries, when the main Sufi orders consolidated in the central Muslim world and sent masters eastward. Muʿīnuddīn Chishtī reached Delhi in 1193 and settled at Ajmer — "his dwelling place soon became a nucleus for the Islamization of the central and southern parts of India." The [[chishtiyya]] spread rapidly, its appeal rooted in three principles attributed to Muʿīnuddīn: "a generosity like that of the ocean, a mildness like that of the sun, and a modesty like that of the earth." The Chishtī khānqāh practiced a classless society, attracting lower-caste converts. The suhrawardiyya simultaneously took root in Multan under Bahāʾuddīn Zakariya (d. ca. 1262), offering a contrasting model: land-holding, formally structured, willing to deal with rulers.

## Major Phases

### High tide of Delhi Sufism (1200–1350)

Niẓāmuddīn Auliyā (d. 1325) at Delhi represented "the high tide of mystical life in Delhi." Under his influence, "Sufism became a mass movement in northwestern India." His circle included the poet Amīr Khosrau (1254–1325), founder of the Indo-Muslim musical tradition. Chishtī khānqāhs generated the *malfūẓāt* genre (recorded conversations of masters), the first example being Niẓāmuddīn's *Fawāʾid al-fuʾād* (compiled 1307–1322). See [[nizamuddin-auliya]].

The Suhrawardiyya spread northward and eastward: Jalāluddīn Tabrīzī reached Bengal (d. 1244); from Ucch (northeast of Multan) the [[qadiriyya]] later spread across the Subcontinent and into Indonesia.

### Deccan expansion and Ibn ʿArabī's influence (1350–1500)

The Chishtī chain extended southward with Muhammad Gīsūdarāz (d. 1422) at Gulbarga under Bahmani patronage. Gīsūdarāz was the first to use Dakhni (southern Urdu) for Sufi instruction and introduced Ibn ʿArabī's *Fuṣūṣ al-ḥikam* systematically to South Asian audiences, "making accessible both the refined love mysticism of ʿAynuʾl-Quḍāt's *Tamhīdāt* and the fundamental work of Ibn ʿArabī." The [[qadiriyya]] arrived in Ucch in the late fifteenth century.

### Mughal synthesis and Akbar's eclecticism (1526–1605)

Babur deferred to Sufi leaders, affiliated with the [[naqshbandiyya]] in Central Asia, then turned to the Chishtīyya in India. Akbar built a Chishtī dargāh at Fathpur Sikri in gratitude for the blessing of a Chishtī saint at the birth of his heir (Salīm/Jahangir). His attempt at the *dīn-i ilāhī* ("Divine Religion") drew on the mystical current but was viewed with deep suspicion by orthodox Muslims. See [[akbar]].

The tension between *waḥdat al-wujūd* (unity of being — Ibn ʿArabī's legacy) and the orthodox tradition found in the 16th–17th centuries an increasing number of defenders of a more sober mysticism.

### Dārā Shikūh's Hindu–Muslim synthesis (1640s–1659)

Dārā Shikūh (1615–59), Mughal heir apparent, pursued the most ambitious attempt at Hindu–Muslim religious synthesis, translating the Upanishads into Persian (*Sirr-i akbar*) and arguing they were the "hidden book" referenced in the Quran. His circle included the naked Persian-Jewish convert Sarmad (executed 1661). His work introduced Hindu mysticism to Europe via Anquetil Duperron's Latin translation (1801). He was executed by his brother Aurangzeb in 1659. See [[dara-shikuh]].

### Naqshbandī reaction (1600–1740)

The [[naqshbandiyya]] entered India shortly before 1600 under Khwāja Bāqī Billāh. His disciple Aḥmad Sirhindī (1564–1624) — later titled *mujaddid-i alf-i thānī* ("Renovator of the Second Millennium") — mounted a sustained critique of Akbar's syncretism and of *waḥdat al-wujūd*, proposing instead *waḥdat al-shuhūd* ("unity of vision" or "testimonial monism"): the mystic experiences union as a subjective state, not as an ontological identity with God. See [[sirhindi-ahmad]]. The Naqshbandiyya combined sober dhikr (silent recollection) with political engagement and strict adherence to the sharīʿa.

### 18th-century Delhi convergence and reform (1700–1800)

Shāh Walīullāh (1703–62), initiated into both [[qadiriyya]] and [[naqshbandiyya]], attempted to bridge the gap between *waḥdat al-wujūd* and *waḥdat al-shuhūd* and to translate the Quran into Persian. He was politically active, inviting Afghan king Aḥmad Shāh Durrānī to defend Muslims against Sikh and Mahratta expansion. See [[shah-waliullah]].

Two of the "pillars of Urdu literature" were Naqshbandī disciples in Delhi: Maẓhar Jānjānān (killed 1782) and Khwāja Mīr Dard (1721–85), who founded the *ṭarīqa muḥammadiyya* ("Muhammadan Path") and wrote the first truly mystical poetry in Urdu. See [[mir-dard-khwaja]].

### Regional vernacular Sufism (1200–present)

Parallel to Persian-language Sufism in Delhi, a robust vernacular tradition grew across the Subcontinent. Sindhi, Punjabi, Pashto, and Bengali each developed distinctive mystical poetry employing folk tales, local musical modes, and imagery from village life. The leading figures were Shāh ʿAbduʾl-Laṭīf of Bhit (1689–1752) in Sind and Bullhē Shāh (1680–1752) in Punjab. See [[indo-muslim-mystical-poetry]] and [[shah-abdul-latif]], [[bullhe-shah]].

## Geographic Spread

- **Sind and Multan** (7th–13th centuries): first entry point; Suhrawardiyya and Chishti settlements; Lāl Shahbāz Qalandar at Sehwan
- **Rajputana / Delhi** (late 12th–14th centuries): Chishtī heartland (Ajmer, Delhi, Pakpattan)
- **Deccan / Gulbarga** (14th–15th centuries): Gīsūdarāz and southern Chishtī branch
- **Bengal** (13th century onward): Suhrawardiyya, Bengali folk mysticism (*marifati* songs)
- **Kashmir** (14th century): Kubrāwī presence (Sayyid ʿAlī Hamadhānī, 1371)
- **Northwest Frontier / Pashto belt** (16th–18th centuries): Roshaniyya movement, Raḥmān Bābā

The [[qadiriyya]] expanded from Ucch to Indonesia and Malaysia, carrying the Indo-Muslim Sufi model beyond the Subcontinent.

## Interaction with Other Processes

- **[[bhakti-movement]]**: Overlapping emphasis on vernacular devotion, love of God, rejection of brahmanical ritual; influenced Sufi vocabulary and imagery in Punjabi and Sindhi poetry; syncretistic figures like Kabīr (15th century) operated at the intersection. Schimmel is careful, however, to note that "the boundaries between Muslim and Hindu saints remained quite well defined during the first five centuries of Muslim rule."
- **[[philosophical-sufism]]** (Ibn ʿArabī's *waḥdat al-wujūd*): Became dominant in 14th-century India via Gīsūdarāz; produced the central theological tension between ontological union and testimonial monism that shaped South Asian Sufi discourse for four centuries.
- **[[naqshbandiyya]]**: The major reform current that repeatedly challenged emotional, ecstatic, and syncretistic Sufism while paradoxically contributing to Urdu literature.
- **[[mughal-empire]]**: Mughal patronage sustained Chishti shrines and elite Persian Sufism; Aurangzeb's orthodoxy curtailed the ecstatic current without ending it.

## End Conditions

The process is ongoing. Colonial period (British occupation of Sind 1843; Partition 1947) reorganised but did not end dargāh culture. The Naqshbandī *madrasa* tradition (Deoband school) perpetuated Shah Walīullāh's reformist impulse in attenuated form, while popular saint veneration persists across Pakistan, Bangladesh, and India.

## Formichi: The Naqshbandi-Haramayn Network and Trans-Asian Renewal (tajdid)

Chiara Formichi ([[formichi-islam-and-asia-2020]]) situates Baqi Billah's Delhi khanaqa and Ahmad
Sirhindi's Mujaddidi reform within a wider trans-Asian Naqshbandi network rather than as a purely
South Asian development: Sirhindi — "renewer of the second millennium" (*mujaddid alf saani*) —
drew on a chain of transmission that had passed through Bukhara and would, in turn, be claimed by
later Central Asian reformers. Formichi traces one such line through **Ishan Niyazquli
at-Turkmani** to **'Abd al-Nasir al-Qursawi** of Bukhara (1776–1812), whose ijtihad-focused
reformism fed the late-19th-century **Jadidist** educational reform movement among Tatar and
Bukharan scholars responding to Russian rule — a genealogy connecting Delhi's Naqshbandi
revivalism to Central Asian modernism independent of Wahhabi influence. She also credits **Shah
Waliullah** (1703–1762; see [[shah-waliullah]]) as "the intellectual ancestor of virtually every
Islamic movement in modern India," his descendants splitting into the moderate juridical path of
Shah Abdul Aziz and the militant path of Shah Isma'il and Sayyed Ahmad Barelvi (whose 1826 jihad
against the British and Sikhs anticipates the Deobandi current this page already traces to
Waliullah). Formichi's frame reinforces this page's account of a South Asian Naqshbandi reaction
while situating Baqi Billah's Delhi khanaqa as a node fed directly from Medina-based teachers
(Ahmad al-Qushashi, Ibrahim al-Kurani) who also trained the Sufi returnees who carried Naqshbandi
teaching into [[islamization-of-southeast-asia|maritime Southeast Asia]] — evidence, per Formichi,
of a genuinely **multidirectional** rather than one-way Islamic scholarly network across Asia.

## Historiography

The primary account drawn on here is Schimmel, *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* (1975), Chapter 8, the most comprehensive English-language survey of South Asian Sufism to its date of publication. Schimmel wrote with deep personal familiarity with Sindhi and Urdu literatures. Her treatment reflects 1970s scholarly consensus: it emphasises peaceable conversion over coercive Islamization, aligns with Khaliq Aḥmad Nizami's positive reassessment of Chishtī social function, and is cautious about Hindu–Muslim "syncretism," resisting the tendency of Hindu scholars (and some Western ones) to dissolve Islamic distinctives into an undifferentiated South Asian mysticism.

Key debates not resolved by Schimmel: (1) the actual extent of Aḥmad Sirhindī's political influence on Mughal orthodoxy (Yohanan Friedmann argued the heroic image was partly post-1919 construction); (2) whether Shāh ʿInāyat's 1718 movement at Jhok constituted proto-agrarian reform or was simply pious charity; (3) the degree to which lower-caste conversion was driven by Sufi egalitarianism vs. other political-economic factors.

**Collection coverage**: The Bibliotheca Alexandrina collection is moderate-to-strong on South Asian Sufism through Schimmel's synthesis and the Translations from the Asian Classics series, but lacks dedicated monographs on regional Sindhi/Punjabi poetry and on the post-1800 period.
