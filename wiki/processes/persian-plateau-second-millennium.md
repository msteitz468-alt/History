# The Persian Plateau in the Second Millennium BCE (archaeology)

The archaeology of highland **Iran** between the Bronze Age and the Iron Age — one
of the **most poorly known periods** anywhere in the Near East, reconstructed almost
entirely from **pottery styles and burial practices** rather than texts. Beyond
lowland **[[elam|Elam]]** (the only literate plateau society), the highlands are
"prehistoric" in this period and must be read through ceramics. Treated in
[[edwards-cah-v2p1-1973|CAH Vol. II, Part 1]] (Ch. XVI, Robert H. Dyson, Jr.).

## Definition and Scope

Because the evidence is so largely ceramic, Dyson deliberately speaks of "**ceramic
traditions**," not "cultures." In the late third and early second millennia the
plateau is divided into **distinct regional ceramic provinces**, most of them rooted
in the third millennium and most fading or transformed by the end of the first
quarter of the second millennium, before a new horizon — itself ended ~1350–1150 BCE
by the ethnic movements of the early Iron Age.

## The regional ceramic traditions

- **Gurgan Grey Ware (north-east):** a fine pattern-burnished grey pottery (Shah
  Tepe, Tureng Tepe, Yarim Tepe, **Tepe Hisar**) of the later 3rd millennium, rich
  in metal, with trade reaching the **Indus** and perhaps China eastward and
  Mesopotamia and Anatolia westward. It spread east into Turkmenistan (Namazga V).
- **Giyan IV–III Painted Ware (Luristan / the Zagros):** the painted-pottery
  tradition between Mesopotamia and the plateau (Tepe Giyan, Tepe Jamshidi, Godin
  Tepe), originating under Elamite **Susa D** influence and running from the mid-3rd
  into the early 1st millennium — the most **stable, continuous** sequence.
- **Yanik Early Bronze Age Ware (north-west Azarbayjan):** a black-burnished,
  often incised handmade ware intruding from **eastern Anatolia and Transcaucasia**
  — the eastern counterpart of the **Khirbet Karak** ware that moved west into
  Syria-Palestine; it faded by ~2050–2000 BCE.
- **Northern polychrome wares (Geoy Tepe, Dinkha Tepe, Sakkizabad):** mid-2nd-
  millennium painted wares derived from Anatolian **"Cappadocian"** and Trialeti
  styles; the **Dinkha** painted ware is the eastern end of the north-Mesopotamian
  **Khabur ware** province.
- **Southern painted wares (Qal'eh / Shoga, in Fars):** local painted traditions of
  the far south, evolving gradually like the Giyan sequence.

## The "hiatus problem" of the north

Dyson's central puzzle is the **Gurgan hiatus**: the grey-ware towns end abruptly
~1900/1800 BCE, yet the **grey ware of Iron Age I** (after ~1350) shows real
continuities (pattern-burnishing, shapes, metal types) — across a gap of 300–700
years (or none, depending on dates). He weighs two explanations: **continuous
internal development** (the Gurgan people simply displaced westward), or a series of
**intruding but related foreign groups** (Caucasus / Anatolia / Central Asia),
"some of the culture-bearers... Indo-European." The resolution he leaves **open**.

## Interaction and dating

The plateau is dated almost wholly by **cross-links** and early radiocarbon: a
Giyan III cylinder-seal of **Old Elamite/Old Babylonian** type matched to a sealing
of **[[hammurabi|Samsuiluna]]'s** reign (1749–1712) — the very reign that gives the
first mention of the **[[kassites|Kassites]]** — and an axe like one inscribed for
the Elamite ruler **Attahushu** (see [[elam]]). Strikingly, the imported fine grey
ware of the Dinkha horizon resembles **both** the Aegean **[[mycenaean-civilization|
Minyan]]** ware and the Hisar grey ware — part of the broad grey-ware/Indo-European
question.

## End Conditions

The Bronze Age plateau cultures end **~1350–1150 BCE** (Hasanlu V marks the new
Iron Age I grey-ware horizon in the north-west), amid the "new ethnic movements" —
plausibly including the arrival of **Iranian-speaking (Indo-European) peoples**,
the ancestors of the Medes and Persians (see [[indo-european-migrations]]).

## Significance

This is the **prehistoric backdrop to later Iranian history**: the ceramic horizons
of the second millennium frame the question of **when Iranian-speakers reached the
plateau**, and the Hasanlu/Dinkha sequence sets up the highland Iron Age out of
which the historical **Medes and Persians** emerge. It is also a methodological
showcase — a whole regional history written from **potsherds, graves and
radiocarbon** in the near-total absence of texts.
