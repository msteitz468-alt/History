## Definition and Scope

Cargo and millenarian movements were a cluster of religious, prophetic, and political responses by Melanesian communities to the social and material disruptions of colonialism and, most acutely, World War II. The term "cargo movements" covers a range of phenomena—from prophecy and ritual directed at obtaining material goods from the spirit world, to organised political resistance and socioeconomic reorganisation—united by the belief that a fundamental transformation was imminent that would overturn the existing colonial order.

"'Cargoism,' drawing on Melanesian notions of religious ritual as a technology for producing wealth, was the intellectual framework within which many villagers sought to explain the extraordinary gap between their poverty and the wealth of the whites, and from which they drew hope of improvement."

The term is contested and to some extent pejorative; scholars have debated whether it usefully captures these movements or imposes a Western category on diverse phenomena.

## Causal Drivers

The movements arose from the collision of Melanesian cosmologies—in which ritual action controls material outcomes, and the dead communicate with the living—with colonial structures of extreme material inequality. The war was the catalytic event: "The experience of war, and of vast wealth suddenly on their shores, became part of the cargoists' explanation." Key drivers:

- **Material gap**: Villagers observed vast quantities of American goods (tins of food, vehicles, aircraft) arriving and being freely shared, then disappearing when colonial authorities returned. The contrast with their own poverty was inexplicable within existing frameworks.
- **Collapse of colonial order**: Europeans had been seen as invincible; the war showed "Europeans afraid, fleeing in panic." This disruption made alternative cosmologies more credible.
- **Prophecy confirmed**: Some prophets had correctly predicted the coming of foreigners and foreign goods. Tannese on Vanuatu said of John Frum: *"We were ignorant of planes, but he was the first to say that planes would arrive."* A Malaitan prophet correctly predicted the Americans' coming in 1939.
- **American model**: The Americans—wealthy, generous, racially egalitarian in Islander perception—seemed to validate the idea that different social arrangements could produce vastly different material conditions.

## Major Phases and Cases

### Pre-War Anticipation (1930s–1941)

Pre-war prophets were already making magic "so that another country would replace Australia." A villager from the Yangoru area of New Guinea recalled a prophet who said: *"Why are we wild, raw bushmen (buskanaka) while the waitman has a comfortable life (sindaun gutpela)? You come with me friend, we will work some magic and you will become the same as the waitman."*

### War-Era Movements (1942–1945)

**Madang area, New Guinea**: Cult leaders Tagarab and Kaut believed the Japanese were "spirits of the dead or special people who had come to open the road of the cargo." Tagarab was shot dead by the Japanese after turning against them; Kaut was arrested as a collaborator by the Allies.

**Kaum's rebellion (1944)**: After Tagarab's death, a man called Kaum in the Bagasin area west of Madang seized weapons from retreating Japanese and assembled perhaps 2,000 people in a stockaded settlement. Kaum taught that the guns had come directly from a deity called God-Kilibob; once followers abandoned sorcery and conflict over women, God-Kilibob "would turn them white and send cargo directly by aircraft so that Europeans could not intercept it." His movement was crushed by troops in November 1944.

**Misima Island (1942)**: When Europeans suddenly departed in 1942, Bulega from Siagara village prophesied that St James and Ezekiel had come to his house, sent by Adolf Hitler, ordering the cleaning of the ancestral mound. "That accomplished, a new world could come, they would be rich and would not have to work." A police party sent to arrest him was killed; Bulega fled, was arrested, and committed suicide. The Australians hanged eight men at Misima in February 1944; "the event left a very bad effect on the people and brought us as a race into contempt."

**Tanna/John Frum (Vanuatu)**: Tannese followers of John Frum "gathered on Tanna in 1943 to build an airstrip for American planes and offer America and its wealth a direct link with their island." They had identified the Americans as the fulfilment of John Frum's prophecies.

**Madang — Yali's Rehabilitation Scheme**: After the war, the former police sergeant Yali, "proclaimed by many as supernatural," inaugurated a Rehabilitation Scheme in which followers modelled their lives on army camp routines.

### Post-War Reconstruction Movements (1945–1950s)

**Maasina Rule (Solomons, 1943–1953)**: Not classically "cargo" in the ritual sense, but born of the same wartime experience of American generosity and colonial repression. The movement centred on 'Are'are in south Malaita, collected a head tax, and reinstituted customary law. It was boosted by British confiscation of goods Islanders had acquired from Americans. Two years after the war, "as rumours spread that the Americans were coming back to replace the British, villagers piled beacons to burn for the ships, and built stores for the cargo." See maasina-rule-movement.

**Tommy Kabu, Purari delta (Papua)**: Attempted to reorganise village economies without awaiting magical transformation, "simply called upon people to abandon old ways in favour of doing business in the European fashion." Failed primarily due to lack of technical and accounting knowledge.

**Paliau Maloat, Manus**: Ex-soldier who "combined a new cosmology with a program of social transformation, which has survived for nearly fifty years, despite the suspicion of the colonial and the independent governments."

## Geographic Spread

Cargo and millenarian movements occurred most intensively in:
- **New Guinea and Papua**: Madang, Sepik, Manus, Purari delta, Misima, and Highland fringes—wherever colonial disruption was acute and Melanesian cosmological frameworks remained strong.
- **Solomon Islands**: Maasina Rule (south Malaita) and related expectations of American return; Santa Cruz Islands where isolation briefly "reinvigorated . . . custom."
- **Vanuatu**: Tanna's John Frum movement, one of the most persistent and studied of all Melanesian millenarian movements.

## End Conditions

Individual cargo movements typically collapsed when prophesied events did not occur, when leaders were killed or arrested, or when the colonial authorities suppressed them. However, as a broader process, millenarian and neo-traditional movements did not end—they transformed. Paliau Maloat's movement survived for decades. The John Frum movement on Tanna persisted into the independence era and beyond. The underlying conditions—inequality, cultural disruption, the problem of "explaining the extraordinary gap between their poverty and the wealth of the whites"—were not resolved by decolonisation, ensuring the relevance of these frameworks persisted.

## Historiography

Cargo movements became one of the most debated topics in Pacific anthropology. The text notes: "Anthropologists have disagreed in interpreting them, some discerning forerunners of anti-colonial nationalism, others claiming that they can be understood only in local terms, still others saying the term 'cargo movements' is misleading and demeaning." Key interpretive positions:

1. **Proto-nationalist**: Cargo movements were inchoate expressions of anti-colonial resistance that would crystallise into formal political movements (associated with scholars like Peter Worsley, *The Trumpet Shall Sound*).
2. **Local cosmological**: The movements were expressions of Melanesian religious logic about the relationship between ritual, the dead, and material wealth, and should not be read through Western political categories.
3. **Terminological critique**: The label "cargo cult" homogenises radically different movements and imposes a demeaning colonial gaze.

The colonial administration and missionaries consistently suppressed the movements: "missionaries condemned such movements, which competed with their own message of salvation. Colonial administrators suppressed them as threats to government authority." This means the primary sources on these movements are overwhelmingly colonial and missionary, with Islander voices filtered through hostile or condescending observers.
