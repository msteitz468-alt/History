## Definition and Scope

This process page traces the development of film, radio, and television in Ireland from the early twentieth century, drawing on Robert J. Savage's chapter in [[jackson-oxford-modern-irish-history-2014]]. Ireland's media development was shaped by its position as a small English-speaking nation on the periphery of Britain, receiving Anglo-American cultural output while attempting to construct indigenous institutions. The result was persistent tension between cultural protectionism (especially the Irish-language lobby and Catholic Church) and audience preference for American westerns, British comedies, and imported programming.

Ireland's first cinema, the Volta (Dublin, December 1909), was managed by James Joyce. It failed as a commercial venture; within twelve years Dublin had thirty-seven cinemas. By 1954, cinema admissions had reached 54.1 million annually — among the highest rates in the world relative to population.

## Radio

### Establishment of 2RN (1926)

The Irish Free State's first radio station, 2RN, began broadcasting 1 January 1926 via a low-power Dublin transmitter. The establishment process was contentious. Postmaster-General James Joseph Walsh circulated a white paper proposing a private Irish Broadcasting Company loosely modelled on the recently established BBC, explicitly rejecting the 'chaos' of the American commercial model. A Special Parliamentary Committee rejected Walsh's proposal and endorsed state ownership, to Walsh's fury. Walsh warned that the Dáil had made 'a serious mistake' and instructed the first director to 'steer clear as far as possible of religion and politics.'

Douglas Hyde opened the station with an address on Irish-language revival — framing broadcasting as an instrument of cultural restoration ('The fine, Gaelic water had ebbed away and was replaced by the mud, slime and filth of English'). The service became Raidió Éireann in 1932.

### Programming and Constraints

Radio operated under severe financial constraints through the 1920s and 30s; the Minister of Finance (Ernest Blythe, 1927–32) was 'notoriously frugal.' Programming supported conservative national ethos: Gaelic culture, Irish language content, and Catholic religious broadcasts. Archbishop John Charles McQuaid of Dublin successfully arranged the introduction of the Angelus on radio (inaugurated 15 August 1950), exemplifying the 'comfortable unofficial relationship between Radio Éireann and the Catholic Church.' Irish-language programmes, despite their ideological priority, had embarrassingly low listenership; a 1953 survey showing this was suppressed from publication. Irish found its radio niche only with the establishment of Raidió na Gaeltachta (County Galway, 1972).

Taoiseach Éamon de Valera used radio effectively: his 1943 St Patrick's Day 'Ireland that we dreamed of' address and his 1945 reply to Churchill's attack on Irish neutrality remain iconic broadcasts. The 1960 Broadcasting Act created an independent public authority (RTÉ) overseeing both radio and television.

### BBC Northern Ireland (from 1924)

Northern Ireland's first station, 2BE, went on air in 1924 and became BBC Northern Ireland when the BBC received its Royal Charter (1927). The Unionist establishment came to value the BBC as 'a critical link to a British culture they considered their own.' When Director George Marshall arrived in 1932, cooperation with Dublin declined; Marshall was under pressure from the Unionist press after being seen in Dublin, and he denied any collaboration was planned. BBC Northern Ireland acquiesced to Lord Craigavon's demand that it stop reporting GAA match results in the province, since Sunday broadcasts were 'hurting the feelings of the large majority.' The station employed few Catholics well into the 1950s.

## Film

### Early and Foreign-Dominated Production (1910–1950)

The American Kalem Film Company made some of the first films in Ireland (1910–14), including adaptations of Boucicault plays and sympathetic nationalist films (*Bold Emmet, Ireland's Martyr*, 1914). The short-lived Film Company of Ireland (1917–c.1920) produced *Knocknagow* and *Willy Reilly and His Dear Colleen Bawn*. Indigenous post-independence film was financed on small budgets and focused on recent national history (*Irish Destiny*, 1926; *The Dawn*, 1936). Foreign films generated the most critical acclaim: Robert Flaherty's *The Man of Aran* (1934), Carol Reed's *Odd Man Out* (1947), John Ford's *The Quiet Man* (1952). These tended to project Ireland as 'either mired in a pre-modern rural idyll or a nation challenged by a legacy of political violence.'

### Indigenous Film Culture (1960s–2000s)

Improving economic conditions from the 1960s enabled young filmmakers — Joe Comerford, Thaddeus O'Sullivan, Pat Murphy, Bob Quinn — to develop short fiction and documentary films, often with British funding (BFI, Channel 4). The Irish Film Board, established 1980, financed Neil Jordan's *Angel* (1982) before being suspended in 1987 due to government cutbacks. The Irish Film Centre (Dublin, 1992) provided critical archival and educational infrastructure. Minister Michael D. Higgins (from 1992) restarted the Film Board and revised tax codes to encourage small-scale production investment. Higgins was subsequently elected President of Ireland in 2011.

The Irish Screen Commission (1998) encouraged foreign production in Ireland. A defining feature of recent 'Irish' film is multi-national co-production: Ken Loach's *The Wind that Shakes the Barley* (2006) was backed by Irish, British, French, and German companies.

Northern Ireland accessed British lottery funds (1995) and European funding (1997); the Northern Ireland Film Commission (from 1998) promoted co-productions with the Irish Film Board.

## Television

### Establishment of RTÉ

Television debates in the Republic replicated the radio debates: a parliamentary committee examined British and American models. Léon Ó Broin (Secretary, Department of Posts and Telegraphs from 1940) emerged as the 'architect' of Irish television, advocating a public service model on BBC lines. The Department of Finance resisted the cost; British TV signals were already reaching Irish viewers from the early 1950s (Divis Mountain transmitter, 1955 — coverage for ~80% of Northern Ireland and many southern viewers). This 'external creep' of programming British authorities considered inappropriate (adultery, single mothers, semi-nudity, royal family coverage) alarmed political, religious, and cultural elites.

A Television Commission (1958) heard proposals; the 1959 Lemass government chose a hybrid: state-owned, commercially funded by advertising and licence fees, overseen by a public authority. Telefís Éireann launched in 1961 (later merged with radio as RTÉ). American Edward Roth was first Director-General; the station featured 'countless American Westerns' to generate advertising revenue. Taoiseach Lemass urged the station to project 'a vigorous progressive nation, seeking efficiency' while avoiding 'stage-Irishisms' — and to exercise extreme caution on Northern Ireland coverage so as not to upset Stormont.

### State-RTÉ Tensions

Political interference in broadcasting was immediate. In 1966, Agriculture Minister Charles Haughey called RTÉ's newsroom directly to complain about a news report; a subsequent broadcast was edited to satisfy him, generating press controversy. Lemass declared in the Dáil that RTÉ 'was set up by legislation as an instrument of public policy and as such is responsible to the government' — a statement widely cited as exemplifying government hostility to press freedom. In 1972, the Lynch government fired the entire RTÉ Authority for broadcasting material it defined as seditious, following escalating violence in Northern Ireland.

Gay Byrne's *Late Late Show* (from 1962) was among the most significant social forces: Byrne's discussion of previously taboo subjects including unmarried mothers, infanticide, sexuality, and clerical celibacy helped open Irish public discourse. The 1966 'bishop and the nightie' incident (in which Bishop Thomas Ryan of Clonfert condemned a contestant's remark about her wedding night) illustrated both the Church's residual sensitivity and the public's growing willingness to ridicule clerical intervention.

### Irish Language and Religious Broadcasting

Irish-language advocates consistently demanded more airtime; the 1960 Broadcasting Act disappointed them. Irish-language television found resolution only with the establishment of TG4 (an Irish language television service in the west of Ireland) in 1996. Catholic priests found their way into broadcasting as the Church grew more comfortable with television: the documentary series *Radharc* (from 1962), produced by Dublin priests as an independent company, became internationally regarded. The 1967 broadcast by Dominican Austin Flannery on Dublin poverty, featuring a Jesuit, two Sinn Féin members, and the General Secretary of the Irish Communist Party, illustrated the pace of change.

### Television in Northern Ireland and the Troubles

BBC Northern Ireland's Unionist era ended violently when television covered the civil rights movement. On 5 October 1968, RTÉ cameraman Gay O'Brien captured RUC violence against civil rights marchers in Derry on film; the footage broadcast on BBC's *Twenty-Four Hours* caused international uproar. The juxtaposition of the footage with a government statement supporting the police 'heavily damaged its credibility.' Terence O'Neill's 'Ulster at the Crossroads' television address (1968) was perhaps the only time the Stormont government used television effectively; it was the last. Television coverage — bombs, shootings, internment, Bloody Sunday, hunger strikes — chronicled thirty years of the Troubles. Governments in Dublin, Belfast, and London all attempted to control or censor broadcasting, raising serious questions about democratic media in conflict zones.

## Conclusion

By the close of the twentieth century, Ireland moved from radio scarcity to a competitive multi-channel television and radio environment (TV3, TG4, multiple commercial radio stations, satellite television). Christopher Morash's *History of the Media in Ireland* (2010) frames this as a transformation from a country with no printing presses in 1500 to one ranked most globalized in the world in 2004. The outstanding scholarly lacunae include: women in shaping radio and television; the representation of women in programming; the history of children's, educational, and sports broadcasting; and the relationship between advertising and audience formation.

## Historiography

Irish media history has been defined by John Horgan, John Bowman, Christopher Morash, Richard Pine, and Rex Cathcart (BBC Northern Ireland). Irish film studies was established by Kevin Rockett, Luke Gibbons, John Hill, Lance Pettitt, and Martin McLoone. These fields have developed since the 1980s and remain productive areas of interdisciplinary scholarship.
