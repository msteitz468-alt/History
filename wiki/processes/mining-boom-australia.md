## Definition and Scope

The Australian mining boom of the 2000s refers to the sustained commodity price increases driven primarily by Chinese (and secondarily Indian) demand for iron ore, coal, oil, gas, and gold, which generated exceptional export revenues, supported fiscal surpluses, and underpinned two decades of uninterrupted Australian economic growth. The boom is distinct from the earlier (early 1980s) mining upturn that was cut short by the global recession of 1982. It is also distinct from the structural [[neoliberal-reform-australia]] that provided the open-investment framework within which the boom operated.

## Causal Drivers

The primary driver was Chinese industrialisation and infrastructure expansion from the late 1990s onward. "Energy and mining — especially coal and iron ore — accounted for 35 per cent of Australia's export earnings in 2000, but 55 per cent in 2010 as a result of big price increases" ([[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]], ch. 8). By 2005, China displaced the United States as Australia's second-biggest export market after Japan; "by 2009–10 China was Australia's biggest trading partner in goods, especially iron ore and coal, as well as in services, especially education and tourism" ([[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]], ch. 8).

The Foreign direct investment framework established under [[neoliberal-reform-australia]] made Australia accessible to overseas capital. From 1991 to 2005 foreign investment in Australia more than doubled; the value of Australian investment abroad increased fivefold.

## Major Phases

### Build-up (1990s–2003)
Australia experienced sustained growth from the early 1990s despite the global slowdown, supported by domestic structural reforms and moderate resource demand. Between 1990 and 2005 house values more than doubled, the average price of Australian shares trebled, and the value of Australian companies listed on the stock exchange increased sixfold.

### Boom Peak (2003–2008)
GDP grew at nearly 5 per cent in 2006 and about 3 per cent in 2007. "With over 2 million jobs created between 1991 and the GFC, the number of employees had grown by over a quarter; unemployment had dropped to a level not seen since the 1970s" ([[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]], ch. 8). The Howard government ran budget surpluses in almost every year, funded the Future Fund, cut personal income taxes, and increased transfer payments. Over $61 billion from privatisation supplemented revenues.

The boom produced concentrated geographic effects: Western Australia, Queensland, and the Northern Territory grew more rapidly than the rest. Max Corden identified a "three-speed economy": mining in the fast lane, manufacturing plus other trade-exposed industries in the slow lane, and the non-tradable sector in the middle ([[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]], ch. 8). The rising dollar made Australian exports outside mining more expensive, disadvantaging manufacturers and rural producers (already suffering drought 2003–2009).

Mining was nevertheless "one of the smallest job creators, generating just 1.7 per cent of total employment. Most of the after-tax profits from the mining industry went to shareholders outside Australia; in 2005 profits were four times greater than the wages paid" ([[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]], ch. 8).

### GFC Shock and Recovery (2008–2011)
In 2008 GDP growth dropped to about 1 per cent. The Rudd government deployed a major stimulus package — including $14.7 billion for school buildings, $12.7 billion in cash handouts, $6.6 billion for public housing — to prevent recession. Rising commodity prices and a declining dollar assisted recovery; from 2009 annual growth averaged about 2 per cent. Australia was the only one of the world's 34 rich countries "to have experienced 20 years of uninterrupted economic growth" ([[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]], ch. 8).

### Political Consequences: Resources Super Profits Tax (2010)
The Rudd government proposed a Resources Super Profits Tax in 2010. "Exaggerating their contribution helped the mining companies mobilise public opinion against Labor's proposed Resources Super Profits Tax in 2010 and sealed Rudd's fate" ([[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]], ch. 8). The mining industry's advertising campaign contributed directly to Rudd's removal by the Labor caucus in June 2010. The tax was subsequently modified and legislated under Gillard as the Minerals Resource Rent Tax.

## Geographic Spread

Western Australia (iron ore, petroleum), Queensland (coal, gas), and the Northern Territory (uranium, gas) were the primary loci. NSW and Victoria, as manufacturing-dependent states, were relatively disadvantaged. The rural sector was doubly disadvantaged by drought and the high dollar.

China and Japan were the primary destination markets for minerals exports. The boom was thus as much a story of Australian integration into the Asia-Pacific economy as of domestic resource exploitation.

## End Conditions

The boom peaked around 2011–12. Iron ore and coal prices fell sharply from 2012 onward as Chinese demand growth moderated and new supply came online. The Minerals Resource Rent Tax was repealed by the Abbott government in 2014. The legacy included sustained infrastructure in mining states, a reshaped export composition, and a more concentrated economy. By the end of the source period (2013), Australia ranked first on the OECD Better Life Index but poorly on inequality, part-time employment, and home affordability — partly consequences of the boom's uneven distribution.

## Historiography

John Edwards, *Quiet Boom: How the Long Economic Upswing Is Changing Australia and Its Place in the World* (Lowy Institute, 2006) coined the defining framing. George Megalogenis, *The Australian Moment* (2012), and Peter Hartcher, *The Sweet Spot* (2011), provided broadly celebratory accounts. Critical assessments (source: [[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]], ch. 8) note that Australia ranked poorly in trade union membership, public university funding, child care costs, and ecological footprint despite its economic record — suggesting the boom did not resolve distributional tensions. Collection coverage on the 2000s mining boom is strong via this volume.
