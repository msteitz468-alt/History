# Conversion of Britain and Ireland to Christianity

The Christianisation of the [[british-isles|Atlantic Archipelago]] was not a single
event but a centuries-long, multi-stranded process running from the late Roman period to
c.700, after which an "ecclesiastical framework" of bishops, minsters, and monasteries
made deepening **Christianization** and individual **spiritual conversion** possible.
Following Thomas Pickles (in [[sources/stafford-companion-early-middle-ages-britain-ireland-2009|Stafford's *Companion*]]),
three strands are distinguished: **adoption** (outward acceptance), **Christianization**
(investment in Christian culture and institutions), and **spiritual conversion** (inward
transformation).

## Definition and Scope

By 500, Christianity was the norm only among the **Britons**, who had received it inside
the Roman Empire (Patrick's *Confession*; Gildas). The process then proceeded along
distinct regional tracks — Irish, Pictish, and Anglo-Saxon — each shaping the church to
local conditions. Crucially, transplanted into societies "in which there were virtually
no towns and no *civitas* structure," the Mediterranean model of the town-based bishop
gave way to a church where **monasticism became more prominent** and bishops were
relatively lesser figures: in Ireland each *túath* had its own bishop, "meaning that
there were over a hundred dioceses."

## Causal Drivers

Christianity offered kings and elites tangible advantages quite apart from salvation: new
models of royal authority, association with Rome and the Franks, access to **literacy and
stone-building**, and new non-military routes to power (ascetic and institutional
authority — abbots, abbesses, saints). Religious communities paralleled existing
kin-and-household structures and, through **[[concepts/bookland|bookland]]**, let elites
convert family land into permanent, partible-inheritance-proof, service-free endowments
under their own control. Pre-Christian monumental burial (Sutton Hoo, Prittlewell) shows
rulers already "seeking to reinforce their position through the construction of
monuments" — something religious communities could do "in a more permanent and
prestigious way."

## Major Phases

- **Ireland (5th–6th c.).** Palladius "sent by Pope Celestine … in 431"; **Patrick**, a
  Briton, active later in the 5th c. Conversion drew on both the continent and Britain.
- **The Britons and Picts.** Whithorn, dedicated to St Martin of Gaul, was an early
  Christian centre (Ninian); Columba's Iona (563) evangelised among the northern Picts.
- **The Anglo-Saxons (597–c.660s).** The **Gregorian/Roman mission** of Augustine to
  Æthelberht of Kent (597), the **Irish/Columban mission** from Iona (Aidan in
  Northumbria), and Frankish and British contributions converged. Conversion tracked the
  rise of kingship; pre-Christian cult had narrowed onto **Woden**, a favoured royal
  progenitor.
- **Resolution and consolidation.** The [[events/synod-of-whitby-664|Synod of Whitby]]
  (664), where Oswiu chose the Roman Easter, was "a royal as much as an ecclesiastical
  decision"; Theodore of Tarsus and Hadrian (669–70) reorganised the English church and
  raised the number of sees.

## Geographic Spread

A network of bishops and dioceses formed everywhere, dense in Wales and Ireland, sparse
in England; the **minster/monasterium** (Old Welsh *clas*, Irish foundations serving
their *túath* and its *manaig*) became the backbone of pastoral care. The same Irish
*peregrini* who travelled the continent — **Columbanus** at Luxeuil, the brothers Fursey,
Foillan, and Ultan — exported a monasticism that reshaped Frankish religious life, while
**English missionaries** (Willibrord, Boniface) carried their devotion to Rome back to
the continent.

## Interaction

Conversion was inseparable from [[concepts/insular-art|Insular art]] and the Latin
golden age (Aldhelm, Bede), from state formation (charters and larger kingdoms appearing
together — see [[periods/early-middle-ages-britain-ireland]]), and from the Frankish
world (see [[processes/christianization-of-the-roman-empire]] for the wider frame).
Pagan practice persisted in domestic "deeply profane" forms, but lost its status as an
official, royally endorsed religion.

## End Conditions

By c.700 the archipelago was institutionally Christian; the process transmutes into the
ongoing histories of [[processes/scandinavian-settlement-of-britain|Scandinavian
conversion]] in the Danelaw and the tenth-century [[actors/benedictine-reform|Benedictine
reform]]. The "Celtic church" as a unified counter-tradition is now rejected by scholars
as a modern myth — the regional churches were variants within a shared Latin Christianity.
