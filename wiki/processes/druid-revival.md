# The Druid Revival (British Druidry, 1500–present)

## Definition and Scope

The **Druid Revival** is the long cultural process by which the ancient Iron Age Druids — known only through a handful of contradictory Greek and Roman texts — were rediscovered, reimagined, appropriated, and reinvented by Britons (Scots, English, and Welsh) from the Renaissance to the present. It is a history of *reception and imagination*, not of the [[concepts/druids|ancient Druids]] themselves: as Ronald Hutton argues in *[[sources/hutton-blood-and-mistletoe-2009|Blood and Mistletoe]]* (2009), almost no genuine information about the original Druids was recovered, so the modern figure is overwhelmingly a construct shaped by the needs and tastes of those who deployed it.

What it **is not**: a continuous survival of ancient Druidic tradition. Hutton finds no evidence of unbroken transmission; modern Druidry is a creation of the modern period, built on classical texts, antiquarian speculation, and creative forgery rather than living memory.

## Causal Drivers

- The **Renaissance** quest for distinguished national ancestors among peoples (between the Rhine and the Atlantic) whose remote past the Bible and the classics had ignored — leaving Druids "almost the only impressive figures" available.
- **Cultural nationalism** and, after the 1707 Union, the new British superstate's need for a *shared* pre-national heritage older than the mutually antagonistic medieval nations.
- The unreliability and contradictory character of the [[controversies/ancient-druids-source-reliability|ancient sources]], which made Druids endlessly available for opposing purposes.
- **Antiquarianism** ([[concepts/antiquarianism|the antiquarian movement]]) and the speculative association of Druids with megalithic monuments.
- Religious and political **dissent**: Druids as forerunners of preferred religions, as nature-priests, and as emblems of cultural radicalism.

## Major Phases

1. **Continental and 16th–17th c. reinvention** — The modern image of the Druid was first assembled on the Continent: the German humanists Johannes Trithemius (1492) and Conrad Celtis (1508) claimed them as learned monotheist ancestors who fled Roman persecution across the Rhine, and the French (Symphorien Champier, 1508; Sébastian Rouillard, 1609) made them the noble philosophers of an ancestral civilisation. The forged "ancient" fragments of Annius of Viterbo (1498) supplied a pseudo-genealogy (Samothes → Druiyus → Bardus) that nationalist historians cited into the nineteenth century. In Britain it was the **Scots** who took Druids up first: Hector Boece (1526) wove them into Scottish history and tied them to the recumbent-stone circles near Aberdeen decades before the same association was made in England. [[actors/camden-william|William Camden]] (*Britannia*, 1586) and [[actors/bale-john|John Bale]] set the ambivalent Protestant pattern; field antiquarians — above all [[actors/aubrey-john|John Aubrey]], reacting against Inigo Jones's Roman attribution of Stonehenge (1655) — began systematically associating Druids with stone circles.
2. **The English apogee, c. 1740–1860** — [[actors/toland-john|John Toland]] and above all [[actors/stukeley-william|William Stukeley]] make Druids ancestral heroes and the builders of Stonehenge and Avebury; the "Druidic century."
3. **Welsh appropriation and convivial orders** — [[actors/morganwg-iolo|Iolo Morganwg]] invents the [[actors/gorsedd-of-the-bards|Gorsedd of the Bards]] (1792) and places Druids at the centre of Welsh national identity; his rival Edward "Celtic" Davies popularised an alternative Druidic mythology (Hu, Ceridwen) in works that were among the most influential of the nineteenth century. Alongside the bardic revival ran a wave of convivial and charitable "Druid" societies — the Anglesey Druidical Society (1772), William Jones's short-lived Druids of Cardigan (c. 1779), and above all the Freemasonry-modelled [[actors/ancient-order-of-druids|Ancient Order of Druids]] (London, 1781) and its friendly-society offshoots, which spread to hundreds of thousands of members across Britain and the English-speaking world.
4. **Downfall and afterglow** — the [[concepts/three-age-system|three-age system]] and evolutionary prehistory strip Druids of scholarly credibility from the mid-19th century; popular and fraternal enthusiasm persists. John Lubbock's *Pre-Historic Times* (1865) ignored the Druids entirely; Daniel Wilson (1851) relegated them to the final stages of the Iron Age with no connection to megaliths; by the 1880s William Boyd Dawkins's racial model of successive prehistoric invasions omitted Druids altogether. Yet popular belief was tenacious: the *Daily News* still called Stonehenge 'the Druid's temple' without hesitation in 1899.
5. **The twentieth century** — [[actors/reid-george-watson|George Watson MacGregor Reid]]'s [[actors/universal-bond|Universal Bond]] brings robed Druids to Stonehenge (from 1912); the 1924 ash-interment controversy crystallises professional archaeological authority over prehistoric monuments; the Bond's schism on Reid's death (1946) produces rival successor groups; [[actors/piggott-stuart|Stuart Piggott]]'s *The Druids* (1968) is the definitive scholarly dismissal; English Heritage bans solstice access 1985; Philip Carr-Gomm revives Ross Nichols's **Order of Bards, Ovates and Druids** in 1988, the largest Druid order of the contemporary period.

## Geographic Spread

Centred on Britain, with sharply divergent national trajectories (Scottish, English, Welsh). Parallel enthusiasms developed in Ireland, France, Germany, and the English-speaking New World (America, Canada, Australia), beyond this book's scope.

## Interaction

Closely bound up with [[concepts/antiquarianism|antiquarianism]], [[concepts/nationalism-and-archaeology|nationalism and archaeology]], [[concepts/celticism|Celticism]], and the development of [[concepts/three-age-system|prehistoric periodization]]. Intertwined with the modern interpretation of [[places/stonehenge|Stonehenge]] and [[places/avebury|Avebury]].

## End Conditions

Ongoing. As a scholarly framework it was displaced by professional prehistory in the later 19th century; as a cultural and spiritual phenomenon it continues, transformed into modern Druidry and revived again in the 1990s.
