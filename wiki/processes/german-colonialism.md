## Definition and Scope

German colonialism encompasses Germany's formal overseas empire (1884–1919) and the broader pattern of German economic, missionary, and political engagement with Africa, the Pacific, East Asia, and the Ottoman world dating from earlier in the nineteenth century. Smith's chapter 16 (Andrew Zimmerman) places German colonialism within a transnational framework of imperial competition and racial ideology rather than treating it as an exceptional national phenomenon.

Germany was "a very significant third" colonial power in terms of territory acquired between 1876 and 1913: behind Britain (4 million square miles) and France (3.5 million), but ahead of Belgium, Italy, the United States, Japan, Portugal, and Spain. "With more than a million square miles of new territory, Imperial Germany outstripped Belgium and Italy, the United States and Japan, and Portugal and Spain."

Yet German colonialism was economically marginal to the metropolitan economy: "By 1914, investment was meager, imports were minor, and exports — mainly of gunpowder, weapons, and alcohol — remained insignificant." Emigration to German colonies was tiny compared with emigration to the United States.

## Causal Drivers

**[[Kulturkampf]] ideology**: The most important conceptual driver was the extension of the domestic *Kulturkampf* framework overseas. German anthropologists and colonial officials conceived of non-European peoples not as potentially educable or civilizable, but as *Naturvölker* (natural peoples) existing in a static realm outside history — opposite the European *Kulturvölker*. This distinction was not primarily biological in the early phase but cultural: "Culture was not a universal human property, but rather the exclusive possession of Europeans and other 'historical peoples' or 'cultural peoples.'"

**Berlin Congo Congress (1884–1885)**: The formal acquisition of territories was coordinated at the West Africa Conference held in Bismarck's Berlin residence (November 1884 – February 1885). By the time the conference closed, Germany had protectorates in Southwest Africa (Namibia), Togo, Cameroon, and East Africa (Tanzania, Rwanda, Burundi). The conference rules for great power partition of Africa were "established" in Berlin.

**[[Weltpolitik]]** from 1897: Naval Secretary Tirpitz and Foreign Minister (later Chancellor) Bülow articulated a vision of Germany as a world power requiring a colonial empire commensurate with its industrial strength and population. Colonial enthusiasm was partly manufactured by nationalist organizations — the German Colonial Society (43,152 members at peak), Pan-German League, Navy League — and reached "far into the social hierarchy."

## Major Phases

**1884–1890 — Bismarckian acquisition**: Bismarck was personally reluctant about colonial commitments ("a map of Africa in Germany is of no use," he reportedly said) but recognized the domestic political value of colonial ventures. Formal protectorates were established in all major German colonial territories during this phase.

**1890–1905 — Consolidation and settler colonialism**: The transition from coastal trade and sovereignty assertion to capitalist organization of colonial labor in the hinterland required new racial ideologies. German authorities borrowed from American New South models. Colonial Secretary Bernhard Dernburg and other officials visited Tuskegee Institute; from 1901–1909 Tuskegee graduates ran a cotton-growing school in Togo. "German colonial luminaries including Frederick Lugard and E.D. Morel, head of the Congo Reform Association, applauded Germany as a model colonizer."

**1904–1908 — Colonial wars and genocide**: The Herero–Nama War (German Southwest Africa, 1904–1907) resulted in the first genocide of the twentieth century. General Lothar von Trotha's October 1904 "extermination order" called for shooting every Herero man and driving women and children into the desert. By 1907 fewer than 20,000 Herero survived from a pre-war population of 60,000–80,000; more than half the 20,000 Nama were killed. Simultaneously, the Maji Maji War (German East Africa, 1905–1907) involved scorched-earth tactics that killed up to 200,000 Africans.

**1906–1907 — Colonial election**: The Catholic Center Party's opposition to colonial abuses brought down the government and triggered elections in which the government branded critics "enemies of the Reich" and liberals re-deployed *Kulturkampf* rhetoric of civilization. The "Hottentot election" demonstrated that colonial politics had become a domestic battleground.

**1919 — Loss of colonies**: The Versailles Treaty stripped Germany of all colonial possessions; they were redistributed as League of Nations mandates to Britain, France, Belgium, Japan, South Africa, and Australia.

## Racial Systems

Chapter 16 (Zimmerman) provides a systematic analysis of the racial categories employed in different colonial contexts:

**'Naturvölker' framework (early phase)**: The distinction between natural peoples and cultural peoples justified colonial extraction without any civilizing pretense. Anthropological collections focused on bones and artifacts rather than living cultural systems.

**'Negro' and the American New South model**: As German colonial economies moved from coastal trade to plantation agriculture, German officials adopted the American New South's model of racialized free labor — emancipated but controlled through debt peonage, contract labor, and Jim Crow segregation. The concept of "educating the Negro to work" (*Erziehung des Negers zur Arbeit*) became central colonial policy language.

**'Coolie' labor**: German Pacific colonies relied on Chinese contract laborers (*coolies*), who were regarded as superior in discipline to Pacific Islanders. In German New Guinea between 1899 and 1914 approximately 85,000 Papuans worked as indentured laborers; historian Stewart Firth estimated indigenous laborer death rates at 20%. The New Guinea Company tolerated flogging of Chinese workers until 1907, when Chinese diplomatic pressure forced a temporary suspension.

**Anti-Polish 'internal colonization'**: The Settlement Commission (1886) pursued what officials explicitly called *innere Kolonisation* (internal colonization) of the Prussian East, purchasing Polish estates for German settlers. Zimmerman argues this was continuous with overseas colonialism and drew on the same racial-ideological toolkit.

## Interaction with Other Processes

German colonialism was embedded in and produced by several intersecting processes:
- **[[weltpolitik]]**: Colonial ambition was the overseas expression of Weltpolitik; failure to secure adequate colonies despite naval build-up was a source of nationalist frustration
- **[[kulturkampf]]**: The domestic campaign provided the vocabulary (*Kulturkampf*, *Kulturvolk*, *Naturvolk*) that shaped colonial ideology
- **[[german-industrialization]]**: Industrial products (weapons, alcohol, manufactured goods) were the chief German exports to colonies; colonial raw materials were economically marginal but symbolically important
- **International racism**: German colonial racism was embedded in and contributed to transnational racial politics — Jim Crow America, British settler colonialism, Belgian Congo, French empire

## Historiography

German colonialism was largely invisible in both German national memory and historiography until the 1990s–2000s. The Herero genocide became a major site of "memory politics" after Namibian independence (1990) and the German government's formal acknowledgment (2021). Key debates include:

1. **Continuity thesis**: Does a line run from German colonial genocide (1904–1908) through Nazi genocide (1941–1945)? Jürgen Zimmerer and Isabel V. Hull have argued yes; critics (Benjamin Madley, others) see the connections as partial rather than determinative.

2. **Transnational framing**: Sebastian Conrad (*Globalisation and Nation in Imperial Germany*, 2010) and Zimmerman (*Alabama in Africa*, 2010) have argued for understanding German colonialism within an international framework of racial knowledge production rather than as a specifically German pathology — useful for understanding comparative colonialisms, though potentially diminishing German agency and responsibility.

3. **Liberal complicity**: The Social Democratic Party was the only political force that "wholesale rejected" imperialism; liberals were deeply implicated in colonial racial ideology. This complicates Whiggish liberal historiography.

The genocide scholarship (Hull, Gewald, Zimmerer) is referenced but not directly held.
