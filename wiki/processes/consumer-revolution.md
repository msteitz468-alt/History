# The Consumer Revolution (Early Modern Europe)

## Definition and Scope

The **consumer revolution** is the marked expansion and transformation of consumption in early
modern Europe — especially north-western Europe between roughly 1600 and 1800 — in which a widening
range of people acquired more, and more varied, **goods**: new groceries (sugar, tobacco, tea,
coffee, chocolate), textiles (Indian calicoes, lighter "new draperies"), household furnishings,
ceramics, clocks, books, and fashion. As treated in the [[oxford-handbook-early-modern-europe-v1|
Oxford Handbook of Early Modern European History]] (Maegraith & Muldrew on "Consumption and
Material Life"), it marks a shift from a world of scarce, durable possessions toward one of
**comfort, fashion, and discretionary buying** well below the elite — a key part of the social and
economic history of the period and a contested prelude to industrialization.

## Causal Drivers

- **The "industrious revolution"** (Jan de Vries): households reallocated labour from
  self-provisioning toward **market-oriented work** in order to earn cash for the new goods —
  working harder and longer to consume more — raising effective demand ahead of, and helping to
  cause, the [[industrial-revolution|Industrial Revolution]].
- **Global trade and empire**: the [[atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic]] [[plantation-economy|plantation
  complex]] (sugar, tobacco, coffee) and [[indian-ocean-trade|Asian trade]] (tea, calicoes,
  porcelain) flooded Europe with desirable exotics, the dark underside of the consumer boom being
  enslaved and coerced labour.
- **Urbanization and retailing**: the growth of [[urbanization|towns]], fixed shops, advertising,
  newspapers, and credit created the infrastructure of consumption.
- **Emulation and fashion**: social competition (Veblen-style emulation, or McKendrick's "trickle-
  down" of elite fashion) spread new tastes across the [[social order]], though historians dispute
  how far imitation versus autonomous popular demand drove it.

## Major Phases

1. **Seventeenth-century groceries**: the spread of colonial groceries and the Dutch and English
   "golden age" of comfortable middling material life.
2. **The eighteenth-century boom**: the maturing of fashion, shops, and "populuxe" goods (cheap
   versions of luxury items) — most intense in Britain and the Low Countries, and in the
   [[thirteen colonies|North American]] colonies.
3. **Toward industrialization**: demand for cottons and manufactures feeding directly into the
   factory production of the later eighteenth century.

## Geographic Spread

Concentrated in the **North Sea/Atlantic** economies (the Dutch Republic, England, and their
colonies), more muted in southern and eastern Europe, where the consumer transformation came later
and more unevenly — a regional divergence that tracks the wider early-modern "little divergence"
within Europe.

## Interaction

The consumer revolution is entangled with [[early-modern-commerce]], the [[price-revolution]], the
[[atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic]] and [[indian-ocean-trade|Asian]] trades, [[urbanization]], and —
as both cause and effect — the [[industrial-revolution|Industrial Revolution]] and the
[[great-divergence]] debate.

## End Conditions

It does not end so much as **merge into industrial mass consumption**: the discretionary demand and
"industrious" work habits of the eighteenth century flow directly into the industrializing economy
of the nineteenth.

## Historiography

The concept was popularised by McKendrick, Brewer & Plumb (*The Birth of a Consumer Society*, 1982)
for eighteenth-century England, and reframed by **Jan de Vries's "industrious revolution"** (*The
Industrious Revolution*, 2008), which links consumption to household labour decisions. Debates
concern its **chronology** (a seventeenth- vs. eighteenth-century phenomenon), its **drivers**
(emulation vs. autonomous demand vs. household strategy), and its **geography** (how far beyond the
Atlantic core it reached) — and its moral underside, since much of the new consumption rested on
[[atlantic-slave-trade|slave-produced]] commodities.
