## Definition and Scope

The "Great Transformation" — borrowing Karl Polanyi's term — refers to the total social transformation Germany underwent between 1850 and 1914, encompassing economic, demographic, geographic, and political change so fundamental that "within only two generations, the part of Central Europe which became Germany in 1871 underwent a fundamental and unprecedented social transformation." Chapter 15 (Cornelius Torp) in the Oxford Handbook provides the systematic economic analysis; Chapter 14 (Helmut Walser Smith) situates the social consequences in political context.

The transformation had two distinguishable but inseparable dimensions:
1. **Industrialization** — the shift from agrarian to industrial capitalism, with all its technological, organizational, and financial features. See [[german-industrialization]] for the economic analysis.
2. **Globalization** — Germany's integration into an expanding world economy that by 1913 had reached a level of trade intensity comparable to the 1970s–1980s.

This page focuses on the *social* consequences and political implications rather than the economic mechanics (covered in [[german-industrialization]]).

## Demographic Transformation

Germany's population grew from 35.3 million (1850) to 67.0 million (1913) — nearly doubling in two generations. The driving force was not increased fertility (birth rates had actually been declining since the 1880s) but dramatically falling infant and child mortality. Germany became "a country of large families, on average four to five children, with children under fifteen more than a third of the population, and Germany one of the youngest countries in Europe."

This demographic explosion produced immense human movement:
- **Emigration**: In the 1880s, nearly 2 million Germans — mainly from Pomerania, East and West Prussia — emigrated to the United States, "the largest wave of emigration in German history"
- **Transit migration**: Roughly 5 million emigrants from Russia and Austria-Hungary crossed Germany
- **Internal migration**: An estimated 8.7% of the population were long-distance internal migrants in 1907; about half of all Germans no longer lived at their birthplace. The flow ran from agrarian east to industrial centers (Ruhr, Berlin, Hamburg)
- **Immigration**: By 1914 there were 1.2 million foreign workers in Germany (nearly 2% of the population) — mainly Polish agricultural laborers from Russian and Austro-Hungarian Poland, plus Italians, Dutch, and Swiss

The demographer Steve Hochstadt calculated that "the years immediately before the outbreak of World War I represented a high water mark of peacetime internal movement." Imperial Germany was more internally mobile than the Federal Republic of Germany of the 1950s–1980s.

## Urbanization

Germany transformed from a predominantly rural to an urban society:
- 1871: Urban population 36.1% of total
- 1910: Urban population 60% of total
- Cities of over 100,000: increased six-fold
- Berlin and Hamburg emerged as genuine world cities

The industrial zones produced novel urban forms: the Ruhr with Duisburg, Essen, Bochum, and Dortmund had a combined population over 1 million by 1910. Mining districts along the Emscher produced "densely populated, never-ending, makeshift industrial towns." In Berlin in 1900, 43% of households had only one heated room; half of working-class flats changed hands every year — producing what contemporaries called "modern nomads."

## Class Formation

The most politically significant consequence was the "advent of class society." Smith's Chapter 14 provides the social stratification picture for 1878–1914:

**Industrial and agricultural workers** (the wide base): More than 18 million workers counted in the 1907 census. Agricultural workers — roughly 40% of the workforce at 1900 — were "the least protected workers"; the right of the lord of the manor to physically punish farm hands was not abolished until 1900. More than a third of industrial workers were women.

**Bourgeoisie** (~15% of population total): Included both *Wirtschaftsbürgertum* (economic bourgeoisie) and *Bildungsbürgertum* (educated bourgeoisie) (~6% combined), plus *Mittelstand* (old: shopkeepers, artisans; new: white-collar workers, subaltern officials). The urban Bürgertum built an "immense" world of cultural and economic power — "from steel factories to opera houses, from department stores to museums."

**Prussian Junkers** (0.3% of Prussian population, ~85,000 people in 20,000 families): Despite their tiny numbers, they held disproportionate state power — 92% of provincial governors, 64% of district governors, 57% of county prefects, 84% of army generals were Prussian nobles.

The *elevator effect* (Werner Sombart's term) — rising real wages for all — coexisted with widening income inequality: the taxable income of the top 5% in Prussia went from 21% of total (1854) to 32.6% (1913). "The differences between rich and poor widened, rather than narrowed, in this period."

## Living Standards and Material Life

Despite persistent inequality, the transformation brought genuine improvement in material conditions:
- Net domestic product per person nearly trebled between 1850 and 1914
- Consumption of meat doubled 1870–1910
- Average working day declined from fourteen hours to ten hours by 1910
- Child labor in factories was "largely, though not completely, a thing of the past"
- Real wages for industrial workers nearly doubled 1871–1913

"Even the poor no longer lived constantly on the edge of sustained hunger and malnourishment, as had been the case in the previous century." The welfare state foundations — health insurance (1883), accident insurance (1884), invalidity and old-age insurance (1889) — created by Bismarck to undercut Socialist appeal provided genuine social protection.

## Political Consequences

The Great Transformation's most important political consequence was the creation of a massive, organized working class whose political representative (SPD) was by 1890 the largest vote-getter in Reichstag elections and by 1912 had the largest parliamentary delegation.

"No working class, save for perhaps in Australia, was as completely organized as in Germany." Free trade unions had over 2 million members by 1912. Yet the unions exercised "a profound brake on the possibility of radical political action" — their organizational investments made a general strike too risky. Newer research (Welskopp) sees the SPD less as a revolutionary party than as "one of the most active proponents of democratic reform, insisting, at every turn, on democratic reform and a more just society."

The combination of Socialist growth, Catholic confessional bloc, and ethnic minority parties meant that from 1898 onward, the "enemies of the Reich" commanded a majority of the popular vote. This structural democratic pressure drove the [[weltpolitik]] strategy as an attempt to create a nationalist majority that could override these forces.

## Stability Amid Tension

Despite deep socioeconomic cleavages, Germany in 1914 was, in Torp's assessment, "a fractured, but stable society." Two factors explain this stability: (1) the trickle-down effect of economic growth giving workers reason to expect continuing improvement within the existing system; (2) the active state — municipal infrastructure, welfare state, agricultural protectionism — cushioning the worst blows of capitalist transformation.

The Association Law of 1908 broadened political participation for women and youth, intensifying gendered public sphere debates. Women made up more than a third of the workforce but remained excluded from political rights; female suffrage had been achieved in New Zealand, Australia, Finland, and Norway by 1913 while Germany's parties "demurred" across the political spectrum.

## Historiography

The social history of the Great Transformation was central to the Bielefeld School's project (Wehler, Kocka): *Deutsche Gesellschaftsgeschichte* integrated economic and social history to explain the origins of National Socialism through structural features of Wilhelmine society. The Sonderweg thesis held that the [[german-sonderweg|structural gap]] between rapid socioeconomic modernization and political authoritarianism was the fatal flaw. 

Blackbourn and Eley's *The Peculiarities of German History* (1984) challenged the comparative framework, arguing that German bourgeois culture was not "feudalized" and that the Sonderweg relied on an idealized British norm. Subsequent scholarship has accepted many of these revisions while maintaining that Germany's constitutional structure created distinctive political impediments to democratic development.

Torp's contribution in this volume adds the globalization dimension: the Great Transformation cannot be understood as a purely domestic German process but only as Germany's integration into a world economy whose pace of integration from 1850 to 1913 rivaled anything since.
