# Law, Crime, and Punishment in Colonial Africa

## Definition and Scope

Law lay at the heart of the colonial encounter in Africa. Colonial officials used law to promote both the legibility and the stability of African societies, but in practice colonial legal systems promoted conflict by imposing rules and expectations that were not widely shared or deeply embedded in African discourses of political and social authority. As Margery Perham noted in 1947, the colonial encounter produced "deep effects" and "problems and ambiguities" in need of study (Richard Roberts, [[parker-reid-oxford-modern-african-history-2013]], ch. 10).

The result was profound litigiousness: Max Gluckman's 1955 observation that "The Lozi, like all Africans, appear to be very litigious" captures how African agency expressed itself through — and was shaped by — the new legal arenas created by colonial rule.

## Legal Pluralism

All African societies possessed some form of legal pluralism — more than one system of normative beliefs and practices coexisting. The classic colonial form was the dual legal system: "native" law and the received metropolitan law existing alongside one another in a hierarchy the colonial state claimed to dominate.

Lauren Benton's analysis shows that in early phases of colonial encounter, "rampant boundary crossing" and "jurisdictional jockeying" characterised the multicentric legal systems that emerged. Increased interaction led to cultural convergence. The colonial state gradually became strong enough to structure these legal spheres hierarchically — but never comprehensively.

The predominant international legal instrument of late nineteenth-century imperial expansion was the **protectorate**: a treaty ceding some degree of sovereignty to the superior power. The protectorate:
- Divided internal from external sovereignty
- Reserved capital crimes and threats to public order for the colonial state
- Ceded "internal sovereignty" to "native authorities"
- Gave rise to indirect rule by guaranteeing legitimacy to the domain of "custom"
- Enabled the state to determine whether customs were "contrary to civilization" (French) or "repugnant to justice, equity, and good conscience" (British)

## The Invention of Customary Law

To exert control over native courts and deal with appeals, colonial states sought to make indigenous law "legible." This was a key dimension of what Terence Ranger called the "invention of tradition" — collaboration between indigenous authorities (typically male elders) and colonial officials to produce handbooks of customary law.

**What this produced**: indigenous law became "customary law" through this process. Male household heads used the opportunity to consolidate their power; colonial magistrates shaped customary law according to their perceptions of African societies. Despite incentives for invented custom to stray far from practice, Thomas Spear argues that "popular consensus" limited how far-reaching such inventions could be.

**Gendered consequences**: the invention of tradition gave rise to "an era of intensified contestations over custom, power, and property" (Sara Berry). A central debate was the appropriate level of subservience that wives and junior males owed to household heads. Women and men brought conflicts before colonial courts to gain control over their lives during a period of rapid change — but these struggles "did not necessarily challenge the gendered nature of power in the household."

## Shari'a and Muslim Law

In Muslim communities, the protectorate model recognised shari'a as a moral and ethical code. Colonial states incorporated Muslim family law and qadis into their legal systems, though often "colonising" Muslim family law in ways that generated disputes over jurisdiction and further controlled the application of shari'a.

The entire Maliki legal code was translated into French in 1854 and condensed for use by French district officers; a British translation served similarly in British West Africa. This reification of a "messier corpus of judicial commentary and debate" into a code conforming to Western categories of "family law" had profound consequences for how Islamic law was applied.

The provision for appeals also profoundly challenged Muslim jurists: it put into question the standing of the qadi, who was thought to judge cases based on infallible divine revelation.

## Metropolitan Legal Traditions: Common Law vs. Civil Law

**Common law** (British colonies): significance of legal precedent developed by judges; the appeals judge plays a prominent role in making the law. Supreme Courts also heard appeals from native courts, blurring the boundary between metropolitan law and African customs.

**Civil law** (French, Belgian, German, Portuguese, Italian, Spanish colonies): law legislated by government and taking the form of a code; the judge applies the code. France imposed a three-tiered system for African subjects: village tribunals (reconciliation), provincial tribunals (family law and misdemeanours), and district tribunals (felonies and appeals), with a cour d'homologation at the government-general level.

Both systems attempted to "codify" custom. Both systems found this far harder in practice than they imagined.

## African Use of Colonial Courts

Africans became energetic and strategic litigants — not passive subjects. Key findings:

- Many Africans avoided village reconciliation processes and brought domestic disputes directly before higher provincial or district courts
- Litigants "jockeyed for the venue they thought would provide the best outcome"
- The creation of multiple legal spheres enabled women, younger adults, and others of low status to confront higher-status men even in courts designed to uphold custom
- Courts became "sites where those least entrenched in the social hierarchy could challenge the status quo and occasionally win" (Bernard Cohn)

Susan Hirsch's analysis of Swahili women's disputes in Mombasa qadi courts shows that, by narrating their troubles, women were "transforming gender through speech and attempting to transform their lives through action" — courts functioned as "sites of resistance."

## African Lawyers

West African coastal elites began to be sent to England for legal training from the 1880s. These African lawyers played crucial roles in transforming grievances into complaints hearable by British judges and were prominent in early constitutionalism. Pixley ka Isaka Seme, a British-trained lawyer, was prominent in founding the South African Native National Congress (1912); Mohandas Gandhi tested early legal and political skills in South Africa; Nelson Mandela and Oliver Tambo were lawyers. Colonial officials worried about the "influx" of African lawyers and passed ordinances limiting their roles in district courts.

## Colonial Anthropology and the Study of Law

British colonial anthropologists — R. S. Rattray, C. K. Meek, Isaac Schapera — were strongly influenced by ideas of homoestasis and evolutionism, producing digests of "rule-oriented customs" based largely on conversations with male elders.

A major shift came with the case method: Max Gluckman examined actual disputes to develop theories about how African judges adjudicated them; A. L. Epstein examined urban Africans' legal behaviour. Laura Nader shifted emphasis toward the "dispute process" as revealing conflict management strategies. June Starr and Jane Collier placed unequal power relations at the centre of their analysis.

Sally Falk Moore proposed moving "back and forth between the small-scale events documented in the court cases and the large-scale social processes that are not visible through the court records themselves."

## Crimes and Punishments

**Precolonial**: prisons were rare; crimes were primarily victim-focused, with compensation to kin rather than incarceration. Some crimes resulted in execution, enslavement, or expulsion.

**Colonial transformation**: Elizabeth Colson's analysis of the Tonga showed how British imprisonment of a manslaughter defendant robbed the victim's kin of both acceptable compensation and the right to armed vengeance — tensions that festered until a new solution was negotiated.

**Whipping**: probably the most ubiquitous form of colonial punishment across the continent, diminishing over time as exposés forced authorities to rein in abuses.

**The indigénat** (French): an administrative law from 1887 giving summary powers of punishment to French administrators — arrest and imprisonment without formal court proceedings or right of appeal for petty infractions. Described by Gregory Mann as not a code but "a grab bag of sanctions and punishments." Algeria alone listed 33 infractions including "speaking disrespectfully to or about a French official" and "failing to register a death."

**Prison expansion**: prisons spread with colonialism, appearing as early as 1812 in Sierra Leone. Carceral regimes were most developed in white settler societies where they were employed to control labour, linked to pass laws and labour contracts. In Kenya, during the Mau Mau emergency (mid-1950s), the colony housed over 86,000 prisoners in 176 prisons and camps. Justice C. Clifton Roberts noted in 1935 that "by multiplying the number of offences which an African is able to commit, often unwittingly, the law in Africa is frequently creating criminals."

Prisons also fed demand for cheap agricultural labour through recruitment of forced labour from prison populations — despite international pressure from the International Prison Commission (1929) and the ILO (1932).

## Conclusion

Debates about the meaning of law, legal statuses, and rights and expectations predated colonialism, were accelerated by the colonial encounter, and persist today. The law was only part of wider and intersecting processes of change; students of colonial law, crime, and punishment must situate legal history within the contexts of labour migration, urbanisation, the restructuring of family and gender relations, and the politics of decolonization.
