# Highland Maya under Colonial and National Rule

## Definition and Scope

Long-duration history of **highland Maya** peoples of **Guatemala** and the Mexican state of **Chiapas** from Spanish invasion (from **1524**) through colonial rule, nineteenth-century Liberal export regimes, and late-twentieth-century violence, reform, and diaspora. Primary synthesis: **W. George Lovell**, “The Highland Maya,” *Cambridge History of the Native Peoples of the Americas* Vol. II Part 2, Ch. 21 (2000) — [[cambridge-history-native-peoples-americas-v2-2|CHNPA II.2]]. Overview framing from **Murdo MacLeod** (Ch. 12) supports the regional extractive pattern and national-era arcs.

This process is the colonial–modern continuation of prehistoric southern highland–Pacific development ([[maya-highland-pacific-development]]) and of the civilizational actor page [[maya-civilization]]. It is the highland counterpart to [[lowland-maya-colonial-modern]] and a regional strand of [[colonial-mesoamerica-native-peoples]].

**Framing (Lovell):** Survival is the key issue but must not be romanticized. National Geographic–style “timeless relics” and Marxist “inert victims of colonial/neocolonial exploitation” both fail; experiences varied regionally. Treat Mayas as **subjects and social agents** who mold culture under invasion and domination. Colonial inequality (**1524–1821**) cast the mold for later iniquities. International native-rights debate runs from Bartolomé de Las Casas to Rigoberta Menchú (Nobel Peace Prize **1992**) and Subcomandante Marcos’s communiqués after the **1994** Zapatista uprising.

**Not this page:** Preclassic–Postclassic highland archaeology and early writing (Sharer) — see [[maya-highland-pacific-development]] and [[kaminaljuyu]]. Lowland Yucatán/Petén colonial–modern trajectory — [[lowland-maya-colonial-modern]].

## Causal Drivers

- **Dispersed polities at contact:** After [[spanish-conquest-of-mexico-1519|Tenochtitlan]], Spaniards pushed south/east into lands where Aztec influence reached but authority generally did not. Chiapas and Guatemala were known at Tenochtitlan for cacao, cochineal, and quetzal feathers. Spaniards faced quarrelsome, disparate polities — conquest neither sudden nor sure ([[conquest-of-guatemala-1524]]).
- **Disease:** Among factors of Indian demise, epidemic disease was most crucial — smallpox, measles, mumps, plague into virgin-soil environments ([[virgin-soil-epidemics]]); up to eight pandemics in Guatemala c. **1519–1632**, often triggering crop failure and famine (Lovell).
- **Institutions of rule:** [[encomienda]] and [[congregacion|congregación]] after warfare and collapse; Crown curtailment of encomendero feudalization; missionary territorialization (Dominican NW/Chiapas; Franciscan center; secular Oriente).
- **Economic geography (MacLeod):** Spanish attention on cacao-rich Pacific Coast and temperate SE of the capital vs less attractive tierra fría NW — produces a **ladinoization gradient** and a NW **culture of refuge**.
- **National projects after independence:** Conservatives ≈ colonial culture of refuge; Liberals ≈ assimilative ladino state and irrevocable land-relation change. Liberal victory 1860s–70s; coffee as principal export driving coerced highland labor.
- **Twentieth-century bifurcation:** Mexico’s (partial) agrarian reform and PRI clientelism vs Guatemala’s counterinsurgency peak **1978–83** and peace without land reform (**1996**).

## Major Phases

### 1. Conquest and demographic catastrophe (c. 1519–c. 1650)

**Chiapas:** Luis Marín’s February **1524** expedition was strategic reconnaissance (Zoque country, Chiapanecos at present Chiapa de Corzo, Zinacantán, Tzotzil at Chamula; return toward Tabasco rather than Tzeltal/Coxoh country). Effective Spanish domination of the central highlands came ~four years later under Pedro Portocarrero and Diego de Mazariegos. Territory initially under New Spain, then (**1530–1821**) Guatemala, except self-government of “Chiapa” **1540–44**. Colonial Spanish center: **Ciudad Real** (modern San Cristóbal de las Casas).

**Guatemala:** Shortly before Marín from the north, **Pedro de Alvarado** crossed Soconusco into Guatemala; little Pacific Coast resistance, battles after highland ascent. Main opponents the **K’iche’s**; then Mam, Ixil, Ch’orti’ and others one by one. **Kaqchikel** sometimes fought with Spaniards (e.g. against Tz’utujil of Atitlán); allegiance withered after ~six months under excessive tribute, sparking a rebellion lasting almost four years. Q’eqchi’ and Uspanteko temporarily defeated invaders before later conquest. Subjugation took ~**ten years or more** in places. See [[conquest-of-guatemala-1524]].

**Demography — Guatemala contact estimates (Table 21.5, Lovell):** Denevan and Lovell & Lutz ~**2,000,000** (all/substantial national territory); Sanders & Murdy **500–800,000** (highlands only); Zamora **315,000** (western half); Solano **300,000**. Lovell & Lutz series: **1520** 2M → **1550** 427,850 → **1575** 236,540 → **1600** 133,280. Depopulation lasted well into the seventeenth century.

**Demography — Chiapas/Soconusco (Gerhard, Table 21.6):** Chiapas Indian population ~**275,000** at contact (**1511**) → 125,000 (**1550**) → 85,000 (**1600**) → 70,000 (**1650**) → 72,000 (**1700**) → 65,000 (**1750**) → 53,000 (**1800**) → ~**58,000** at independence (**1821**). Soconusco: **80,000** (**1511**) → **7,000** (**1550**) → **4,000** (**1650**) → **4,000** (**1821**).

**Pre-Spanish plague:** *Annals of the Cakchiquels* describe pestilence reaching Guatemala before Spaniards themselves (year 25 = **1519–20**): cough, nosebleeds, bladder illness; massive mortality; half the people fled to the fields.

### 2. Colonial institutions: encomienda and congregación (16th–18th c.)

**Encomienda** awarded privileged Spaniards/Creoles tribute in labor, goods, or cash — **not land grants** but rights to products (gold, silver, salt, cacao; or corn, beans, cloth, chickens), with nominal Catholic-instruction obligation few lay Spaniards fulfilled. Crown strategically curtailed inheritance and labor privileges; Bourbons abolished the institution in the eighteenth century as modest pension. Wendy **Kramer** stresses the pre-Cerrato decades (first ~20 years) as most remunerative for Spaniards and most exploitative for Maya; Alonso López de Cerrato’s presidency of the Audiencia de Guatemala (**1548–55**; jurisdiction Chiapas to Costa Rica) is often wrongly treated as the starting point rather than a mid-stream assessment.

**Case — Huehuetenango (Juan de Espinar):** Held Mam Huehuetenango **1525–1560s** (hiatus **1530–31**). Claimed ~**9,000 pesos**/year mining + **3,000** agriculture in heyday; gold placers ~10 km south on Río Malacatán. Tributary base: Huehuetenango **3,000–3,500** tributaries **1530–31** → **500** in **1549**. **1530–31** labor: 40 men year-round twenty-day shifts to Santiago; 120–200 men + 30 women daily to gold mines; 80 male + 40 female mine slaves — representing **43,200–72,000** male and **10,800** female work-days/year. Post-Cerrato **1549** obligations drastically reduced; still 11th-largest private encomienda in Guatemala at mid-century.

**Congregación:** Highland Maya settlements were dispersed; limited urbanization on defensive hilltops unfit for Spanish administration. Policy forced scattered rural Indians into nucleated **pueblos de indios** (municipal center + countryside; theoretically segregated from non-Indians) for conversion and “orderly” life. Dominicans and Franciscans led resettlement around Catholic churches. Royal order **21 March 1551** mandated reduction to villages. *Annals* record Dominican instruction and founding of Sololá on **7 Coak = 30 Oct 1547**.

**Legal country vs real country (Lovell, after Carlos Fuentes):** Sixteenth-century pueblos (Table 21.10: **95** by **1555**, **336** by **1600** by Dominicans/Franciscans/Mercedarians/seculars) persist as modern municipios — key units of Maya community life — but Indians soon drifted back to the mountains: force of resettlement; escape from tribute, labor, road/church work, human-carrier service; disease worse in crowded towns; highland farming better from small dispersed groups. Pedro Ramírez de Quiñones (**20 May 1556**): audiencia cannot vouch for one-tenth of its territory. Tomás de Cárdenas and Juan de Torres (Sacapulas, **6 Dec 1555**): groups of 4–8 houses in gullies Spaniards never reached; ancestral attachment to fathers’ huts and bones of forefathers.

**Missionary map:** Dominicans far north and west (Verapaz across Chuacús and Cuchumatanes to Chiapas); Franciscans ~50 km radius of Lake Atitlán; Mercedarians took Huehuetenango–Soconusco border strip ceded by Dominicans; Oriente left to less experienced secular clergy → “secular east / regular west” divide (van Oss). **<1,000** missionaries for Guatemala Maya entire colonial period. Dominican hegemony in Chiapas.

**Parcialidades and cofradías:** Even in towns, Maya regrouped as **parcialidades** — pre-conquest patrilineal clans/kin affiliates tied to land tracts. Missionaries often threw several into one pueblo; parcialidades kept separate social/economic operation — mosaic not homogeneous community. Totonicapán province c. **1683**: nine pueblos comprised **>30** parcialidades, each assessed separately for tribute. Parcialidades correlated with **cofradías** (saint sodalities) that over time covered more “suspect” worship.

**Fugitivism and resistance evidence (1560s–1680s):** Santiago Atitlán leaders (**1561**) complained of outlying “rebellious Indians” dodging tribute; Verapaz ~**1579** parcialidades left for mountains; Santa Catalina and Zulbén almost abandoned five years after bishop-led congregación; late-17th-c. Bishop Andrés de las Navas dossier: fugitives, day-keepers, San Mateo Ixtatán hilltop shrine on pre-Hispanic altar site (incense, turkey sacrifice) — Fray Alonso de León fled for his life.

**Repartimientos and clerical exactions:** Corregidores/alcaldes mayores who bought office forced commodity sales (or reverse arbitrage); rampant eighteenth-century Chiapas among Tzeltal, Tzotzil, Zoque — raw cotton forced-spun/woven into *mantas*. Alcalde mayor of Ciudad Real **1760–65** windfalls (e.g. spinning 500,000 lb cotton → 100,000 lb thread: **27,500** pesos). Clergy: unpaid personal service, unauthorized livestock sales, overcharging for mass/confession, embezzling cofradía assets.

**Tzeltal Revolt 1712–13:** Only full-scale native colonial uprising in the region. Cancuc visionary María de la Candelaria/María de la Cruz (influence of Tzotzil prophet Sebastián Gómez de la Gloria); manifesto that God and King were dead. **>20** towns rose with armies of **3,000–6,000**; failed to spread far beyond Tzeltal–Tzotzil heartland; crushed by militia from Tabasco and Guatemala; ringleaders garrotted or shot; communities suffered years after. Century later Totonicapán tribute riots carefully stopped short of full rebellion. Late colonial order: Maya subordination taken as natural right; Martínez Peláez: “The colonial regime was a regime of terror.”

### 3. MacLeod regionalization — culture of refuge vs ladinoization

South and east of Santiago de Guatemala: stronger encroachment, faster cultural/biological assimilation; Oriente and El Salvador became predominantly mestizo/ladino (Pipil and displaced highland Maya pockets as cheap labor for boom–bust cash crops: cacao, indigo → cochineal, sarsaparilla → coffee, bananas). **North and west:** Maya retained more land, community organization, identity, languages, worship, seasonal rhythms, and calendrical time — a **culture of refuge** hybridizing Hispanic and native traits that baffled imperial authority. Hybrid mores clear by the seventeenth century, trend earlier. This geography structures the entire later national divergence (MacLeod via Lovell).

### 4. Independence partition and Conservative/Liberal struggle (1821–c. 1870)

After Iturbide’s **1821** accord, Central American Creoles briefly joined Mexico (~2 years); then United Provinces of Central America, later dismembered. **Chiapas stayed Mexican** — only unit of the former Audiencia de Guatemala (since **1561**) to do so. Conservatives preferred Hispanic institutions preserving colonial status quo; Liberals sought capitalist external ties and assimilation into a modern ladino state. Neither side hegemonic until Liberals prevailed in the **1860s–70s**. Chiapas: **>25** government transfers before **1850**; constant native welfare deterioration, especially land loss.

**Guatemala:** Liberals dominated **1823–39**; **Rafael Carrera** then led Conservatives after popular uprising (MacLeod overview: Maya village/rural components; halt to Liberal confiscations). Woodward: pro-Indian protection of land/labor in the **1840s** lessened after **1850** as Carrera tied more to elite — still a temporary shield vs Chiapas trajectory. Liberals returned **1871** under **Justo Rufino Barrios**.

### 5. Liberal reconquest of land and labor (c. 1860s–1944)

**Chiapas land expropriation:** “Vacant/unused” Maya land seized for export crops; many Soconusco lowland communities disappeared; highland settlements severely affected. Jan Rus: all **25** intact Tzotzil and Tzeltal townships at independence suffered. Larraínzar family took **3/4** of Chamula community land — **476 of 636** caballerías (~**20,000** ha) — plus land from two other townships → Nuevo Edén estate ~**874** caballerías; by mid-century **>700** families, males **3 days** labor/month for subsistence plot. In ~two decades **>1/4** of Chiapas Indians transformed from free villagers to permanently obligated peons. Juárez Reform Laws in effect **1863** accelerated ladino acquisition (e.g. Zinacantán: half of residents tenants **1838–75** per Wasserstrom). On the eve of the Mexican Revolution after Porfirio Díaz (**1876–1910**), **>10 million pesos** foreign capital in Chiapas (Soconusco coffee; Usumacinta mahogany/cedar) worked by indentured highland Maya labor. MacLeod overview also notes highland Tzeltal/Tzotzil deportations to Tabasco lowlands under the Porfiriato (B. Traven novels).

**Guatemala coffee and labor law:** Liberal law demanded formal/individual land title; “unclaimed” tracts to Creoles/ladinos. Carol Smith asserts communities lost ~**half** of colonial-era claimed lands (Naylor sees little discernible change — scholarly range). Coffee principal export from mid/late nineteenth century (Verapaz highlands; Pacific boca costa/piedmont), needing seasonal harvest labor from highland Maya for **>100 years**.

- **Mandamiento** draft authorized by Barrios **November 1876** reinforced debt peonage into the twentieth century.
- Then **vagrancy law**: farmers with **<10 cuerdas** owed **150** days wage labor/year; **10+ cuerdas** but less than **3–4 manzanas** exemption owed **100** days; **libreta** ID book inspected for fulfillment.
- McCreery: aggravated intra-community differentiation, broke corporate self-protection, underwrote export profits, impoverished rural population, contributed to preconditions of present-day violence.
- Reglamento de Jornaleros **1877** (MacLeod overview) reconﬁrmed colonial-era mandamiento; Estrada Cabrera (**1898–1920**) continued policies with *habilitadores* recruiting highland workers via advances.

**Case — Santa Eulalia (Shelton Davis):** **1880–1920** ~**70%** of communal holdings to ladinos, including Ixcán tierra caliente. Of **55** titled lots Indians got **9**; of **1,520** caballerías Indians got **183**. Municipio of **Barillas** created **17 Oct 1888** after Huehuetenango ladino militia claimed ~**200** caballerías (President Manuel Lisandro Barillas, **22 May 1888**) — name linked state action to Maya dispossession. Classic dichotomy: large ladino lowland estates vs small highland Indian fields.

**Case — Momostenango (Carmack):** Lost best agricultural lands under Liberals (Buenabaj; piedmont El Palmar and Samalá). Population doubling → average family holding **<0.5 ha**. **1876** full-scale guerrilla war brutally suppressed: Barrios ordered militia to burn houses/crops in rebel zones and forcibly resettle suspect families in town (strategies later used by twentieth-century army). Final fifty years of Liberal rule: **1,000–2,000** Momostecos channeled yearly to coffee piedmont plus local public service = **>336,000** coerced labor-days/year (**16%** of total available); ladino rule by “terror and paternalism.”

**Habilitación and colonos:** Robert Burkitt at Nebaj **1913**: plantation advances + rum sellers create automatic rum–coffee cycle. Advance pay = **habilitación**, hard to refuse in lean July–August corn months; some short contracts led to permanent **colono** status. Dessaint: **1894–1930** Nebaj area sent **6,000** laborers/year to piedmont fincas, not all returned. Maud Oakes (Todos Santos, **Jan 1946**): neighbor Domingo and 14-year-old son contracted **64** days coffee picking for **$16** total after illness prevented corn planting; 4–5 day walk each way; mosquito huts, unground corn only, no medical care — less than **$1**/week each.

**Structural inequality after formal coercion fades:** ~**90%** of Indian minifundistas on plots too small for year-round subsistence. Land concentration: **2%** of farms hold **65%** of farm area; **90%** of farms hold **16%**. Best land grows coffee, cotton, bananas, sugarcane for export while ~**70%** of population in U.N.-described “extreme” poverty (Lovell, late-20th-c. figures).

### 6. Mid-twentieth-century reform bifurcation

**Guatemala 1944–54:** Only serious modern attempt to redress injustices; overthrow of democratically elected **Jacobo Arbenz Guzmán** by foreign interests + domestic opposition. Wasserstrom (vs Handy/Gleijeses): Arbenz sought “mitigation, not metamorphosis” — modest program misreading problem as Indian “ignorance and isolation” rather than commercial agriculture as special capitalism that itself spread subsistence minifundia; failed to see symbiotic interdependence of highland Maya communities and piedmont fincas (if former endure, latter get labor). Benefits short-lived after overthrow. MacLeod overview: Ubico (**1931–44**) vagrancy laws; Decree 900 (**1952**); U.S.-sponsored overthrow; reforms canceled.

**Chiapas after Mexican Revolution:** Late **1930s** Oficina de Contrataciones and Sindicato de Trabajadores Indígenas required plantation contracts and union membership under Cárdenas labor codes. Erasto Urbina organized labor then, as director of Department of Indian Protection, land restitution returning holdings as **ejidos**. Caveats (Wasserstrom): estate owners kept buildings, capital improvements, choicest/irrigated land; of **62,000** beneficiary families ~**1/3** insufficient resources; Zinacantán nearly half eligible excluded; **60%** of final grant forests/hillsides, **40%** seasonable arable. Still, Collier: central highlands transformed from patchwork Indian hamlets among ladino properties into consolidated continuous Indian control — **no Guatemalan government ever contemplated equivalent reparation** (Lovell).

### 7. Demographic rebound; Chiapas path to Zapatistas; Guatemalan civil war

**Long-view demography:** After conquest collapse, **>4 centuries** to regain contact numbers, then doubling in a generation — unprecedented growth taxing multiethnic Mexico and Guatemala (Lovell on Tables 21.1–21.6).

Even undercounting censuses: **>1 million** Maya in Chiapas; **5–6 million** in Guatemala; Maya-speaking peoples about **half** Guatemala’s population. Major language groups (Tables 21.3–21.4): Chiapas — Tzeltal, Tzotzil, Tojolabal (Zoques more Mixe-linked); Guatemala — K’iche’, Mam, Kaqchikel, Q’eqchi’ and many smaller highland languages.

**Chiapas → Zapatistas (1 Jan 1994):** Mexican countryside peace via Article 27 land reform rhetoric, credit, food subsidies → peasant PRI support (Chiapas **89.9%** for Salinas **1988**). De la Madrid austerity after **1972–82** oil-boom debt; Salinas structural adjustment and NAFTA; **1992** redraft of Article 27 ended land-reform commitment — Collier & Quaratiello: robbed peasants of land hope where claims languished. Uprising primarily a **peasant** (not exclusively Indian) rebellion as protest against broken social contract, though begun in Chiapas, involving Maya, and addressing native rights — national symbolic impact (MacLeod overview: EZLN; Salinas/NAFTA frame).

**Guatemala civil war:** Cold War politics after **1954** Arbenz overthrow entrenched. Six years later junior officers’ abortive coup vs Gen. Ydígoras Fuentes began brutal civil war lasting **36 years** on/off. **1960s** fighting mostly Oriente (ladino-majority); **1970s** guerrilla shift west/north into highland Maya country.

**Counterinsurgency peak 1978–83 (full fidelity, Lovell):** Military governments framed war as counterinsurgency vs “communist subversion.” Between **1978 and 1983**, violence claimed the lives of **tens of thousands** of Maya Indians, most unlikely to have known Marx. Generals bearing most responsibility: **Romeo Lucas García, Efraín Ríos Montt, Oscar Mejía Víctores**. Guerrillas not blameless: especially Huehuetenango and El Quiché, when Army of the Poor retreated under government offensives, unarmed villagers suffered horrific reprisals for food/shelter/moral support. Hundreds of Maya communities paid for real or perceived proximity to insurrection. Scorched-earth tools: aerial bombardment, destruction of property, burning crops/supplies, livestock killing, regrouping into **model villages** and **development poles** under army and army-organized **civil defense patrols**.

**Exemplar — Finca San Francisco, Huehuetenango, 17 July 1982:** Remote finca near Chiapas border. Perpetrators: Guatemalan soldiers. Corroborated survivor testimony (Cultural Survival / Anthropology Resource Center **1983**): soldiers removed wives from the church in groups of 10–20, raped them in houses, then shot them and burned houses; children locked in church then killed by stabbing; elders stabbed/hacked with dull machetes; working-age men taken in groups of ten, shot in the courthouse patio, bodies piled and carried into the church. Among scores of incidents.

**Peace without land reform:** Civilian Vinicio Cerezo **1986**; Jorge Serrano **1991** (abolished constitutional guarantees **25 May 1993**; removed after resistance); Ramiro de León Carpio **6 June 1993**; Álvaro Arzú from **Jan 1996** signed “firm and lasting” peace with guerrillas **29 December 1996**. Land reform not in the accord nor Arzú priority. Lovell: Guatemala not resource-poor but made poor by unequal land access; without confronting land and Maya dignity, root of unrest unaddressed.

### 8. Diaspora and transnational survival (1980s–present)

Highland Maya no longer only mountain village-bound; live/work far from Mesoamerican origins — numerous in California, Texas, Florida (flight during early **1980s** violence), also Chicago, Boston, Providence, parts of Canada. Political refugees joined by economic migrants. As many as **1 million Guatemalans** (many Maya; most without legal status) in US/Canada; ~**$500 million**/year remittances. Santa Eulalia alone (**1995**): **$3 million** from **>6,000** Q’anjob’al residents in US (mostly California) — *Prensa Libre* **13 Nov 1996**. Transnational migration networks as much a reality as colonial encomienda/mandamiento demands; cultural equipment for endurance (Lovell close).

## End Conditions

**Ongoing.** Independent highland Maya states ended in the sixteenth century; colonial “culture of refuge,” Liberal land/labor extraction, Mexican partial reform, Guatemalan civil-war trauma, Zapatista and Maya cultural-political movements, and transnational remittance economies reshape but do not terminate highland Maya demographic and cultural presence. Lovell’s survival thesis: despite conquest, disease, congregación, encomienda, Liberal dispossession, plantation labor, civil war, and structural inequality, highland Maya sustain presence through adaptive agency — hybrid refuge culture, resistance short of suicidal revolt when possible, labor migration, diaspora, and political voice (Menchú; Maya movement; Zapatistas).

## Historiography

Highland Maya colonial studies long overshadowed by central Mexico/Yucatán (*Mesoamérica* **14**, **1987** roundtable). **Murdo J. MacLeod**, *Spanish Central America* (**1973**), is the landmark for a quarter-century; MacLeod & Wasserstrom *Spaniards and Indians* (**1983**) extends ethnic relations into the 19th–20th c. Chiapas: Gerhard; Gasco (Soconusco cacao); García de León *Resistencia y utopía*; Gosner and Viqueira on Tzeltal Revolt **1712** (moral economy); de Vos (Lacandón; *Vivir en frontera*); Lenkersdorf on Portocarrero–Mazariegos. Guatemala more advanced: Carmack *Quichean Civilization*; Sherman forced labor; van Oss *Catholic Colonialism* (Indian west / ladino east); Kramer pre-Cerrato encomienda; Lovell on Cuchumatanes/congregación “real vs legal country”; Lovell & Lutz demography; disease least nuanced factor for colonial Spanish America generally.

Postcolonial irony (Woodward **1987**): often better knowledge of Spanish colonial than postcolonial Central America. Key modern works: Woodward on Carrera; McCreery *Rural Guatemala 1760–1940*; Smith ed. *Guatemalan Indians and the State*; Benjamin, Collier, Rus, de Vos on modern Chiapas. Arbenz debate: Wasserstrom (little Maya gain) vs Handy (more); CIA: Immerman, Gleijeses. Violence literature **1978–83** dense: Montejo, Carmack *Harvest of Violence*, Manz, Stoll Ixil, Falla Ixcán massacres, Lovell *A Beauty That Hurts*, Le Bot, Schirmer military project; monumental *Guatemala: Nunca Más* (**1998**) — cost Bishop Juan José Gerardi his life. Rigoberta Menchú *I, Rigoberta Menchú* (details disputed by Stoll **1998**); Maya renaissance/cultural activism (Cojtí, Warren, Wilson, Fisher & Brown); diaspora studies (Vlach, Burns, Hagan, Jonas). Early English entry points: Thomas Gage; John Lloyd Stephens *Incidents of Travel* (**1841**); La Farge **1940** on sequence of cultures.

**Collection coverage:** Strong for colonial demography, congregación, and Guatemalan highland labor via Lovell’s synthesis; thinner on Zoque/Mixe west and on post-1996 peace implementation beyond Lovell’s closing note.
