## Definition and Scope

Religious change in contemporary Southeast Asia refers to the complex, multi-layered transformation of the region's religious life from the end of World War II through the late twentieth century. The process cannot be reduced to secularization (the region remains profoundly religious), Westernization (most change was indigenously driven), or simple revivalism (the forms adopted were novel). The defining characteristic is creative appropriation and reinvention: "Recent reformulations of religion are evident through representations in politics as well as through participation in institutions which are conventionally recognized as religious."

At the outset of the period, Kenneth Landon (1949) aptly characterized the region as a "crossroad of religion," noting that imported faiths — Hinduism, Buddhism, Islam, Christianity — had historically been subordinated to ancestral spirit cults grounded in relatively autonomous villages. The half-century after 1945 saw this village substratum progressively challenged without being destroyed. The diversity that resulted — "magical animism, esoteric mysticism, traditional piety, scriptural literalism and modern scepticism" intersecting in villages, markets and offices — made the region "an especially rich laboratory for the exploration of religion."

This process is closely related to but distinct from the earlier [[islamization-of-southeast-asia]] (which treats the medieval spread of Islam) and the colonial-period religious transformations embedded in [[colonialism-in-southeast-asia]].

## Causal Drivers

**Urbanization and demographic revolution**: Cities grew explosively from the 1970s. Singapore, Bangkok, Manila, Kuala Lumpur, and Jakarta were unrecognizable from their 1950 selves. "An increasing majority has grown up in a postwar world dominated by modern states rather than ethnicity, by education in schools rather than village ritual religion, and by monetarized economies rather than communal co-operation." Urban migration disrupted the village ritual communities that had sustained ancestral spirit cults; Javanese transmigrants in Kalimantan or factory women in Malaysia could not sustain the cosmological realities of their grandparents.

**State formation and national ideology**: Independent states required national ideologies, and every government in the region engaged with religion in defining national identity. This produced "neo-traditionalism": "whenever contemporary elites invoke indigenous spiritual cultures it is within new frameworks which make the process one of reinvention rather than strictly of preservation." Buddhism as state religion in Burma and Thailand, Pancasila (including belief in God) in Indonesia, Islam as the official religion of Malays in Malaysia — each was a political construction as much as a spiritual inheritance.

**Print and electronic media**: The extension of print, radio, film, and television into village societies was "remarkable." These media spread national languages, scriptural literalism, and standardized religious forms at the expense of performance modes and syncretic styles. "As literatures and religions have been articulated increasingly through print, they also become defined as text. This has militated against the performance modes and syncretic styles which were predominant in earlier local practices."

**Green Revolution disruption**: Agricultural modernization undermined the cosmological framework of rice-farming villages. In Java: "some villagers report that only the older strains of rice are connected to spirits; the realms of the devas become distant and the connections loose as new miracle strains and chemical fertilizers move in."

**Cold War ideological competition**: Governments facing communist insurgencies promoted anti-communist religious identities. U Nu in Burma used Buddhism as a "bulwark against communism." Islam was used across Malay-world contexts both to mobilize and to contain political energy.

## Major Phases

**Revolution, independence, and spiritual expectation (1940s–1950s)**: The Japanese occupation had "punctured the myth of white supremacy" and created millenarian expectations of a new cosmological order. For many Southeast Asians, independence "seemed both imminent and cosmologically destined." Early independence was thus spiritually charged; religious organizations competed to define the national identity. In Indonesia, the struggle over an Islamic state versus Pancasila was fought out in this period; the compromise of Pancasila (belief in God without specifying Islam) contained Islamic state aspirations without defeating them.

**State-sponsored religious institutionalization (1950s–1960s)**: Governments created official religious bureaucracies, sponsored religious education, registered and subsidized religious organizations. In Burma, U Nu's government sponsored 60,000 sand pagodas and eventually declared Buddhism the state religion (1961), triggering conflict. In Indonesia, the Ministry of Religion administered recognized faiths; kebatinan (Javanese mystical movements) were pushed to seek recognition or remain unofficial.

**Islamic revival and dakwah (1970s–1980s)**: Across the Malay-Islamic world, urban educated youth embraced a more scripturalist, orthodox Islam as a vehicle for both spiritual authenticity and political identity. In Malaysia the dakwah (Islamic proselytization) movement represented "a reinvigoration of Malay identity through strengthening of a pure Islamic commitment." Partai Islam (PAS) in Malaysia drew on similar energies. The underlying dynamic was the convergence of Islamist ideology (increasingly shaped by global currents from the Middle East) with post-independence disillusionment: political independence had "not remove[d] economic dependence on the industrialized countries."

**Buddhist political engagement (1960s–1980s)**: In Vietnam, Buddhist monks' self-immolation (1963) was the dramatic symbol of religious resistance to authoritarian secular nationalism (the Diem regime). In Burma, U Nu's promotion of Buddhism and later Ne Win's suppression of it bracketed the period. In Thailand, the forest meditation tradition of Achaan Mun and his disciples — spreading from northeastern Thailand to urban Bangkok and then internationally — represented a different kind of engagement: inner renewal with social-ethical implications. Bhikkhu Buddhadasa at Suan Mokh articulated a "Buddhist socialism" rejecting both communism and capitalism.

**Christian expansion (throughout)**: Christian missionaries and indigenous church communities expanded significantly among upland minority groups across mainland Southeast Asia. The Pau Chin Hau movement among Chin in Burma adopted Christian elements as an indigenous democratization movement opposing both traditional chiefly authority and Burman state control. In the Philippines, Catholic Basic Christian Communities organized politically against Marcos; the church and middle classes were pivotal in the 1986 revolution.

## Geographic Scope

Religious change was pan-regional but took distinctive national forms:

- **Indonesia**: The Darul Islam movement (1948–62) sought an Islamic state; suppressed but not resolved. Kebatinan movements proliferated — Subagya listed 288 in 1973. The tension between scriptural Islam, syncretic Javanism, and secular Pancasila is the structuring axis of Indonesian religious politics.
- **Malaysia**: Islam as the religion of Malays institutionalized; dakwah revival from the 1970s; Chinese and Indian communities maintained Buddhist, Hindu, and Christian traditions. Thaipusam festival (trance ritualism) coexisted with orthodox Islam in the same national space.
- **Thailand**: Theravada Buddhism state-sponsored but internally diverse; forest meditation revival had international reach; Buddhist socialism as an intellectual current.
- **Burma**: U Nu's Buddhist politics gave way to Ne Win's secular socialism; ongoing monastic and popular resistance; vipassana meditation internationally disseminated from Burmese teachers, notably Mahasi Sayadaw.
- **Vietnam**: Mahayana Buddhism compromised with socialist state; Hoa Hao (founded 1939, ~2 million members by 1964) and Cao Dai (founded 1925, 1–2 million members) syncretic sects persisted through the socialist period. About 10% Catholic in southern Vietnam.
- **Philippines**: Catholic majority but internally diverse; Basic Christian Communities as vehicle of social-justice organizing; Iglesia ni Kristo as an indigenous non-Catholic Christian sect.

## End Conditions

The process was ongoing at the time of writing (1992). No resolution of the tension between scriptural revival and syncretic tradition was in sight. "Despite the range of changes since then, the region remains a site of encounter between deeply held and widely divergent world-views." The author (Jan Watt) notes that "revivalist religion calls into question the assumption that industrialization necessarily erodes spiritual commitment" — the expected secularization had not occurred.

## Historiography

Chapter 9 of CHSEA v.2 (by Jan Watt) takes an unusually phenomenological approach to religious history, attending to the experiential and consciousness dimensions of religious change as well as its institutional and political expressions. This distinguishes it from straightforwardly sociological accounts.

Key interpretive positions:
1. **Against secularization thesis**: The chapter explicitly rejects the 1950s–60s social-science assumption that modernization would produce secularism. "Revivalist religion calls into question the assumption that industrialization necessarily erodes spiritual commitment" (line 2989 in source text).
2. **Against simple Westernization**: Religious change was driven by Southeast Asians appropriating and adapting global religious tools, not passively receiving them.
3. **Neo-traditionalism as reinvention**: State-sponsored invocations of traditional religion are always new: "Neo-traditionalism must be considered a theme of cultural politics in the independent states of the region" — a creative political act, not preservation.
4. **Village substratum persistence**: Despite dramatic urban change, "beneath surface transitions the structures of popular perception and belief remain remarkably cohesive." Magic, millenarianism, and mysticism remain among Western-educated middle-class Southeast Asians, not just villagers.

Collection coverage: The CHSEA v.2 is the primary source. The collection is generally weak on post-1945 religious history and has no dedicated monographic sources on dakwah or Buddhist modernism in this period.
