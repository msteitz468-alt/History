## Definition and Scope

Early farming society in **Europe** — by far the **best-documented** region for the
*social* interpretation of the Neolithic, with "more than a century of research"
producing data rich enough to glimpse the range of individual and community
decision-making (Alasdair Whittle, CWH Vol. II Ch. 22; case study **Brześć
Kujawski**, Poland, Bogucki & Grygiel, Ch. 23). Farming was **introduced** to
Europe from [[early-agriculture-southwest-asia|Southwest Asia]] — Europe is not a
domestication hearth — but how it spread and what kind of society it produced are
exceptionally well studied here.

## How farming arrived: leapfrog colonization + assimilation

Europe is the type case of the [[demic-vs-cultural-diffusion]] debate, and the
[[archaeogenetics|ancient-DNA]] evidence (LBK haplogroup N1a; Mesolithic
haplogroup-U foragers) supports the model Whittle endorses: **leapfrog pioneer
colonization** from Anatolia into southeastern Europe — with an archaeologically
attested **pause** to adapt farming technology — followed by **rapid expansion**
into central Europe as the **Linearbandkeramik (LBK)**, accompanied throughout by
**assimilation of indigenous foragers**. Foragers and farmers coexisted as
"parallel societies" for centuries. Imported cattle and pigs were later partly
replaced by local lineages.

## Regional diversity, not a single "Europe"

Whittle stresses that "Europe" as a unitary region is questionable: early Neolithic
farming was **vastly different** in central, southeastern, and western Europe, in
the Alpine settlements, and in the Mediterranean — yet these were **not
disconnected** worlds. He calls for "complex, detailed, precise, and regionalized
narratives," abandoning the old expectation of a uniform, linear march toward
greater complexity and inequality.

## Household production and a critique of "intensification"

Whittle is **skeptical of simple trends toward intensification and increasing
scale**. His view: agricultural production remained largely **household-based**,
its goals being survival plus meeting the **social obligations of household and
community** — not surplus accumulation. Crucially, he doubts that any households,
lineages, or groups managed to so **control the means of production** that land or
surplus became owned by a minority — i.e. early Neolithic Europe did *not* slide
inevitably toward entrenched inequality.

He also **qualifies the [[secondary-products-revolution]]**: milk came *earlier*
than Sherratt's 4th-millennium model (lipid residues — see
[[secondary-products-revolution]]), wool probably *later*, and wheeled vehicles do
appear in the 4th millennium (Carpathian basin, Alpine foreland) — but the package
was not a single synchronized "revolution."

## Materiality and worldview

Whittle's distinctive contribution: Europe is the one region where we can recover
early farmers' **worldview and sense of time** ("temporalities of futures,
presents, and pasts"). The **materiality** of Neolithic life — houses, monuments,
pottery, performances, rituals — "bound people together more than it set them
apart," providing "checks and balances against tendencies in the direction of
competition, acquisitiveness, and inequality."

## Case study: Brześć Kujawski (Poland)

Bogucki & Grygiel's case study is the cluster of early Neolithic villages at
**Brześć Kujawski and Osłonki** in northern Poland (6th millennium BCE), of the
**Brześć Kujawski Group** within the post-LBK **Danubian/Lengyel** world. It
exemplifies the richness of European evidence:
- **trapezoidal longhouses** with associated **burials** (dozens, contemporaneous
  with the houses — allowing households to be linked to graves);
- **social differentiation** in grave goods — including imported **copper**
  ornaments — and specialized activities (flint manufacture, ground stone, bone
  tools, jewellery);
- a mixed **farming + herding** economy with poor diets, hard labour, and evidence
  of **violence**;
- farmers living only a few days' travel from **forager neighbours** — the
  forager–farmer frontier was **porous and long-lived**.

## Interaction

- A diffused (not indigenous) instance of [[agricultural-origins]], derived from
  [[early-agriculture-southwest-asia]].
- The empirical heart of [[demic-vs-cultural-diffusion]] and a key source for
  [[archaeogenetics]] (LBK ancient DNA).
- Its household forms anchor [[neolithic-communities]]; Whittle's critique tempers
  the [[secondary-products-revolution]] and "wealth on the hoof"
  ([[pastoralism]]) narratives.

## End Conditions

Transitions into the later Neolithic and [[chalcolithic]] (copper, then the
secondary-products complex and growing differentiation) — the agrarian foundation
of European later prehistory.

## Historiography note

The richest data set in the volume for *social* and *ideological* interpretation
— the standard Whittle holds out as the goal for less-studied regions. It is also
where the demic-vs-cultural debate is closest to resolution (via ancient DNA).
