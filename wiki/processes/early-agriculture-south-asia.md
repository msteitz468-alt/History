## Definition and Scope

The origins of farming in the **Indian subcontinent** — a region long assumed to
have received agriculture wholesale from West Asia, but now understood (CWH Vol.
II Ch. 10, Kingwell-Banham, Petrie & Fuller; case study Mehrgarh, Ch. 11, Petrie)
to have **at least three distinct pathways**, combining diffusion with
substantial indigenous domestication. South Asia is otherwise a thin spot in the
collection (per CLAUDE.md), so this volume is a key source for the region.

## The three pathways

**1. The northwest (Baluchistan) — the SW Asia-like package.** Early sedentism,
**cereal farming, and livestock pastoralism** evolved here on the model of
[[early-agriculture-southwest-asia|Southwest Asia]]: grain-based breads central
to foodways, substantial **mud-brick** architecture, with **ceramics added
later**. Anchored by Mehrgarh (below).

**2. The Ganges plains — slow indigenous rice.** Wetland foragers used **very
early ceramics** (akin to East Asian late-Pleistocene pottery traditions),
coexisting with persistent aceramic "Mesolithic" hunters. **Rice cultivation
emerged slowly** — full commitment to domesticated rice, livestock, and
sedentism developed perhaps **5,000 years after** the first Neolithic elements
(ceramics, ground stone) appeared. The pigeon pea (*Cajanus cajan*) was
domesticated here. This is one of the world's clearest cases of
*primary* foragers becoming farmers in situ.

**3. The southern Deccan savanna — pastoralism + local plants.** Mobile
savanna/woodland seed-gatherers incorporated **sheep, goats, and cattle**
(adopted from across the Thar desert — Fuller's "savanna corridor" hypothesis),
while **potting, local plant domestications, and the distinctive ashmound
tradition** were largely indigenous innovations. Another primary forager-to-farmer
pathway.

(Eastern/northeastern India remain too poorly documented to characterize.)

## Case study: Mehrgarh (Baluchistan, Pakistan)

Mehrgarh, at the foot of the **Bolan Pass** on the Kacchi plain, is the
**best-known early village in South Asia** and the type site for pathway 1.
- **Period I (aceramic Neolithic, from ~7000 BCE):** mud-brick architecture,
  cereal farming, herding — but **no pottery at first**. Wheat, barley, sheep,
  and goats here were largely **imported domesticates** from the west; long-range
  contacts are evident.
- **The zebu (humped cattle, *Bos indicus*)** was very likely **domesticated
  locally** from wild stock — a genuinely indigenous South Asian domestication,
  and Mehrgarh also lies at the easternmost range of **wild barley** (possible
  local barley domestication).
- **Period IIA:** earliest fired ceramics appear (containers more efficient than
  earlier).
- **Period III (Togau phase, ~4000–3500 BCE):** craft florescence — pottery,
  **copper metallurgy**, baskets — embedding Mehrgarh in far-flung economic and
  social networks. It is a direct antecedent of the Indus (Harappan) civilization.

## Causal Drivers

A genuine mix of **diffusion** (the western package into the northwest) and
**indigenous innovation** (rice in the Ganges; zebu, millets, pulses, ashmounds
in the south). The relative weight of migrating farmers vs. local foragers
adopting domesticates — the [[demic-vs-cultural-diffusion]] question — varies by
pathway and is hard to resolve, but "more frequently than not, agriculturalists
moved into a new area or local foragers adopted elements of the Neolithic from
elsewhere."

## Interaction

- A regional instance of [[agricultural-origins]] feeding the global picture.
- Northwest pathway derives from [[early-agriculture-southwest-asia]].
- Marks a major **culinary divide** (grinding/baking breads in the NW vs. the
  boiling/steaming traditions to the east — see [[early-agriculture-china]]
  *(Cycle 4)*).
- Antecedent to the Indus civilization (later Processing-List volumes / CWH Vol. III).

## End Conditions

The South Asian Neolithic is best seen as a **"protracted transformation
episode"** in which farming long coexisted with foraging. Its diversity
ultimately supported one of the densest human populations of any world region
(~15–20% of humanity today on ~2.7% of global land).
