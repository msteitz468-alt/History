## Definition and Scope

This page describes the set of commercial, demographic, political, and cultural connections between Ireland and the Atlantic world — principally British North America and the United States — from the Williamite settlement (1690) through the era of Daniel O'Connell and Catholic Emancipation (to c. 1840). It covers three interrelated dimensions: the Atlantic commercial economy that integrated Ireland into transatlantic flows of linen, flaxseed, beef, and emigrants; the political and ideological transfers that moved American and French revolutionary ideas to Ireland; and the diaspora networks that linked Irish emigrants to their homeland. See [[jackson-oxford-modern-irish-history-2014]], Ch. 23 (Maurice J. Bric).

## Causal Drivers

### The Imperial Commercial Framework

By the mid-seventeenth century, Britain's first Atlantic empire was structured around commercial monopoly enforced through the Navigation Acts and managed by the Board of Trade. Ireland occupied an anomalous position within this framework: legally subordinate to Westminster through Poynings' Law (1494–5) and the Declaratory Act (1720), but in practice increasingly integrated into Atlantic commercial circuits.

The penal laws enacted in Ireland from 1695 onwards — designed, as Archbishop Edward Synge of Tuam put it in 1731, "to lessen their [Catholic] number or break their power" — structured Irish Atlantic participation along confessional lines. Similar penal laws existed in the American colonies, but operated less pervasively outside Maryland (founded by the Catholic Calvert family in the 1630s). The result was that Irish Catholics were discouraged from viewing colonial America as a congenial destination, while Ulster Presbyterians — subject to their own penal disabilities — emigrated in substantial numbers.

### The Linen and Flaxseed Nexus

The direct export of Irish linen (from 1705) and the direct importation of American flaxseed (from 1731) created an interlocking transatlantic trade that was the structural backbone of Irish-American connections throughout the eighteenth century. The Linen Board's bounties further stimulated this trade. Because the linen industry was concentrated in Ulster, the flaxseed ships concentrated on Ulster ports, and the return voyages — loaded with emigrants — made Philadelphia and New York (through which most flaxseed had been processed) the primary landing places for Irish emigrants.

Merchants managed both ends of this nexus. Figures like Blair McClenachan — Derry-born, established in Philadelphia — operated as agents for both the flaxseed and emigrant trades, maintaining networks of local agents in Ulster towns and villages. The commercial incentive to fill return voyages profitably aligned merchant interest with emigrant aspiration. As Bric notes, "the merchants of the later eighteenth century encouraged a positive view of America" — acting as transmission belts for the idea of America as a land of opportunity.

## Major Phases

### Pre-Revolutionary Emigration (1718–1775)

Ulster Presbyterian emigration to colonial America began as early as the late 1710s, triggered by episodes of depression in the linen trade. The latest scholarly estimates suggest that between the 1710s and 1775, Ulster emigration to the American colonies "may have ranged up to 250,000 or more" (Kerby Miller et al., quoted in Dickson, Ch. 21). These emigrants settled disproportionately in Pennsylvania, New York, and the Carolinas, forming distinctive Scots-Irish communities whose political culture would contribute to American frontier radicalism.

Catholic emigration before the Revolution was comparatively limited, partly because penal law conditions made Catholic America uncongenial, and partly because the linen-flaxseed commercial nexus was concentrated in Protestant Ulster.

### Revolution and Radicalization (1775–1800)

The American Revolution transformed the Atlantic connection from a commercial into a political relationship. In Ireland, the Volunteer movement of 1778–83 drew directly on American example; the campaign for "Free Trade" (1779) and the "constitution of 1782" that produced the short-lived legislative independence of Grattan's Parliament both invoked the precedent of colonial resistance. The newspapers and pamphlets of the American Revolution circulated in Ireland, and Irish Protestant patriots saw themselves as participating in a common Anglo-Atlantic struggle for constitutional liberty.

The French Revolution deepened and radicalized this Atlantic ideological current. For the united irishmen, France offered both an ideological template (the replacement of corporate privilege by popular sovereignty) and a practical military ally. Tone's mission to France (1796–8) was inseparable from his Atlantic vision: he proposed to use Ireland as a base for an invasion of Britain, knocking the primary Atlantic imperial power out of the war.

### Post-Union Diaspora and O'Connell's Atlantic Politics (1800–1840)

After the [[act-of-union-1801]], emigration accelerated and the Irish-American community became a political actor in its own right. Irish works in New York, Philadelphia, and Baltimore "continued to see themselves as the anchors of the Irish presence in the cities" (Bric, Ch. 23). O'Connell mobilized these diasporic networks in support of Catholic Emancipation, and after 1829 attempted to engage them in the Repeal campaign. However, the political demands of the Catholic Emancipation campaign also created a transatlantic controversy: O'Connell's uncompromising opposition to American slavery alienated many Irish-American supporters who held or accommodated to slaveholding. O'Connell "put his case for [his cause] as had been the case with Catholic emancipation" but found that the diaspora's political priorities had diverged from his own. See [[daniel-o-connell]].

## Geographic Spread

The Irish Atlantic connection was never uniformly distributed. Its primary commercial axis ran from Ulster linen ports (Derry, Belfast, Newry) to Philadelphia and New York. South Munster (Cork) participated primarily through the cattle and butter trades, which connected Ireland to the Caribbean provisioning economy rather than to the settler colonies. The Catholic diaspora of the eighteenth century was concentrated in France and the Spanish Empire (through the Irish Brigades and the Wild Geese networks) rather than in British America.

After 1800, the geographic spread broadened: emigrants from Connacht and Munster — increasingly Catholic, increasingly landless — moved toward Liverpool, and then to North American port cities. The character of the Irish-American community shifted accordingly: from relatively prosperous Ulster Presbyterian artisans and farmers to a more economically precarious Catholic population concentrated in eastern seaboard cities.

## End Conditions

The Atlantic commercial nexus that had structured Irish emigration — the linen-flaxseed trade — was disrupted by industrialization. Mechanized spinning and weaving, concentrated in Belfast after c. 1820, broke the household linen economy that had sustained Ulster's proto-industrial demographic regime. Emigration continued but was increasingly driven by agrarian crisis and clearance rather than commercial cycles. The [[great-famine-ireland-1845]] marked the catastrophic terminus of this phase: emigration became a mass phenomenon of flight rather than a commercially managed flow.

## Historiography

Maurice J. Bric's chapter in the Jackson *Oxford Handbook* (Ch. 23) provides the most synthetic recent treatment of the Irish Atlantic connection. Earlier foundational work includes Kerby Miller et al., *Irish Immigrants in the Land of Canaan* (2003), and R. C. Nash's work on Irish Atlantic trade. The political dimension — especially the transfer of revolutionary ideas — is covered in Marianne Elliott, *Partners in Revolution* (1982). O'Connell's transatlantic politics and the slavery controversy are treated in Bric's own earlier monograph and in the broader literature on the Irish-American community.
