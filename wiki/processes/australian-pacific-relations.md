## Definition and Scope

This process covers colonial Australia's multi-dimensional engagement with the Asia-Pacific region from 1788 to federation in 1901: as a recipient of Chinese and Pacific Islander labour, as a sub-imperial power projecting influence into Pacific island groups, as a racialised political entity legislating racial exclusion in response to Asian demographic and geopolitical pressure, and as a commercial actor integrated into trans-Pacific trade networks. The process was shaped simultaneously by proximity to Asia and distance from Europe — 'as much the perceived peril of proximity to Asia as the imagined tyranny of distance from Europe' defined Australia's colonial experience (Marilyn Lake in [[bashford-macintyre-cha-v1-2013]]).

Australian historians traditionally wrote as though Australia's 'geographical position in the south-west Pacific, at the edge of Asia and adjacent to Oceania, were largely irrelevant to its historical experience'. Recent scholarship has transformed this by showing that 'Australasian nation-building was shaped in a series of critical encounters with the empires of China and Japan'.

## Causal Drivers

**Geographic proximity and demographic pressure**: China's population doubled during the eighteenth century from 150 to 300 million and reached over 400 million by mid-nineteenth century. 'Demographic revolution fuelled territorial expansion.' Chinese settlers transformed Singapore from a tiny settlement (a few thousand in 1871) into 86,000 Chinese, 20,000 Malays and 100 white men by 1884. This regional Chinese expansion generated Australian fears of what the *Sydney Morning Herald* framed as a choice between 'Greater Britain or Greater China'.

**Gold and labour demand**: Chinese migration to the Australian goldfields peaked at 42,000 in Victoria by the late 1850s — one in five men in that colony. On the goldfields, Chinese comprised 25 per cent of diggers in Victoria and up to 60 per cent in New South Wales. Pacific Islander labourers were recruited for Queensland sugar plantations from 1863.

**Commercial interests**: Trading companies such as Burns Philp were eager to exploit Pacific island resources — copra, pearling, sandalwood, bêche de mer, sugar. Sydney became an entrepôt for the South Pacific. From the 1830s Australian missionaries established a presence in Tahiti; 'the Missionary was the merchant's Pioneer' (*Sydney Gazette*).

**Great-power rivalry**: French annexation of New Caledonia (1853) and activities in the New Hebrides, German annexation of north-east New Guinea (1884), and Japanese military victory over China (1895) all drove Australian demands for pre-emptive annexation of Pacific island groups.

**Racial ideology**: Charles Pearson's *National Life and Character: A Forecast* (1893), predicting ultimately dominant oriental societies, 'by pointing to the insecurity of the white man's place in the world...energised a defensive race nationalism'. The US Congressional Joint Committee report on California (1876), widely circulated in Australia, provided a template for anti-Chinese rhetoric about 'servile' coolie labour depressing wages.

## Major Phases

### 1788–1850: Foundational Contacts
Early contacts with Indigenous networks in the north; Chinese arrivals in commerce; Australian-based whalers, sealers, and sandalwood traders operating across the South Pacific; Sydney as embryonic entrepôt; missionary expeditions to Tahiti and island groups.

### 1850s–1870s: Gold Rush and the Chinese Question
Mass Chinese immigration to Victoria and New South Wales goldfields. Victoria passed the first race-based immigration restriction legislation in Australia — 'perhaps in the world' — in 1855 (repealed under British pressure 1863). Labour movement agitation against Chinese employment intensified. The Queensland government began recruiting Pacific Islander ('Kanaka') labour for sugar plantations from 1863. Australian newspapers ran correspondents in San Francisco, tracking Californian anti-Chinese politics: 'Australian newspapers employed their own correspondents in San Francisco and regularly received American files and personal accounts from travellers taking the popular San Francisco shipping route.'

### 1870s–1883: Pacific Sub-Imperialism and Mounting Anxiety
Australian colonial governments began active lobbying for British annexation of Pacific islands. Victorian Premier Graham Berry asserted 'a kind of Monroe Doctrine should be established that all the islands in this part of the world should be held by the Anglo Saxon race' (1877). The *Melbourne Polynesia Company*, envisaged as 'the East India Company of the Pacific', investigated Fijian cotton and sugar. Fiji incorporated into the British Empire via 'cession' in 1874, presided over by NSW Governor Sir Hercules Robinson. Around 60,000 Pacific Islander indentured labourers transported to Queensland sugar plantations over four decades. The Federal Council of Australasia constitution (1885) included New Zealand and Fiji, 'reflecting the more regional and less continental sense of identity'. In 1883 the Queensland government dramatically and unilaterally claimed all of Eastern New Guinea on behalf of the British Empire — disallowed by London, but Germany's subsequent move forced Britain to claim south-east New Guinea. Colonial leaders presented the Colonial Secretary with 'a gigantic scheme of annexation, including not only New Guinea, but the New Hebrides, Samoa & in fact all the South Pacific within about 1,000 to 1,500 miles from the Australian coast'.

### 1880s–1901: Racialisation, White Australia, and the Monroe Doctrine
New immigration restriction laws against Chinese passed in 1881 and 1888 (extended to all Chinese regardless of British subject status). Death rates among Islander labourers were catastrophic: in 1883, one in twelve died; in 1884, one in seven; on the CSR estate at Goondi, up to 60 per cent died. Chinese imperial commissioners visited in 1887 to demand 'recognition of the equality of national sovereignties'. The 1897 Colonial Conference in London produced the 'Natal formula' — a literacy test device to achieve racial exclusion while preserving imperial unity. Japan's victory over China (1895) shifted anxiety from Chinese to Japanese power; colonial leaders resolved in 1896 to extend exclusion to all 'Asiatics'. At federation (1901), the new Commonwealth passed the *Immigration Restriction Act* (dictation test), the *Pacific Islands Labourers Act* (deportation of Pacific Islanders), and asserted constitutional authority over 'the relations of the Commonwealth with the Islands of the Pacific'. By 1907 all but a small proportion of Pacific Islander workers were forcibly deported.

## Geographic Spread

- **China and Southeast Asia**: Chinese migrants came primarily from Guangdong province, part of broader networks reaching Peru, Chicago, Hawaii, California, Southeast Asia, and Australasia. Singapore became the pivot: its transformation into a predominantly Chinese city influenced the labour and social relations of Darwin.
- **Melanesia and Polynesia**: Queensland labour recruitment extended across the New Hebrides (Vanuatu), Solomon Islands, New Caledonia, Fiji, Gilbert Islands, and New Guinea. Australian missionaries operated across Samoa, Tonga, Fiji, New Britain, and Papua.
- **Northern Australia**: In Broome, Cooktown, Townsville, and Darwin, 'multi-national, multi-racial and multi-religious communities — Indigenous, Malay, Indian, European, Chinese and Japanese — were established in which non-whites often composed the majority of the population'. The pearling industry drew workers from the southern Philippines, eastern Indonesia, and Japan.
- **New Zealand**: Australian and New Zealand colonial politics were closely intertwined — sharing immigration restriction models, arbitration schemes, women's suffrage experiments, and Pacific ambitions. 'One New Zealander in twenty was Australian-born.'

## End Conditions

The process underwent a decisive institutional transformation at federation in 1901, when White Australia was constitutionalised as national policy and Pacific Islander deportation was mandated. The labour trade's maritime phase effectively ended by 1907. The pearling industry's Asian labour base survived until a 1916 Royal Commission ruling, but was ultimately 'killed off not by White Australia but by the plastic button'. The process produced a paradox: Australia had expelled its Pacific Islander workers while simultaneously asserting the right to administer Pacific territories — 'White Australia was an imperial project as well as a national one, its vision of "Greater Australasia" the logical expression of its long-held ambition to be "masters of the Pacific".'

## Historiography

The chapter by Marilyn Lake (Ch. 22, [[bashford-macintyre-cha-v1-2013]]) represents a historiographical turn away from 'insiderist' British-world frameworks toward a regional and transnational perspective. Early nationalist historiography (Myra Willard's *History of the White Australia Policy*, 1923) presented racial exclusion as progressive labour protection. Later sociological frameworks emphasised racial ideology ('social Darwinism'). More recent scholarship (Lake and Reynolds, *Drawing the Global Colour Line*, 2008; Tracey Banivanua Mar, *Violence and Colonial Dialogue*, 2007) has foregrounded the agency of Chinese, Pacific Islander, and Indigenous peoples, the violence of the labour trade, and the transnational connections that shaped Australian racial politics. A key insight is the triangular dynamic (Regina Ganter's 'triangulated' race relations) joining Indigenous, European, and Asian people in northern Australia — far more complex than a simple settler/native binary.

Open controversy: the degree to which Pacific Islander labour recruitment was voluntary or coercive remains contested between labour historians who emphasise Islander agency and those who foreground systematic colonial violence.
