## Definition and Scope

**Merchant brokerage in Mughal [[surat|Surat]]** was the system of Indian
intermediaries — brokers (*dalals*), money-changers/bankers (*shroffs*, *sarrafs*),
and agents (*marfatias*) — through whom the [[english-east-india-company|EIC]] and
[[dutch-east-india-company|VOC]] actually conducted business in western India across
the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. Ghulam Nadri's chapter in
[[clulow-mostert-dutch-english-east-india-companies-2018|Clulow & Mostert (2018)]]
makes it a case study in **interdependence, not domination**: against the older
"European domination / Indian subordination" model (Moreland, Panikkar), it shows a
relationship "underpinned by interdependence and reciprocity" yet "highly
competitive and at times sharply contested."

## Causal Drivers

The companies needed brokers because they lacked local knowledge, languages, and —
critically — **credit**. In the seventeenth century Indian merchants were often
better positioned than the companies: they lent the money the companies needed and
exercised **monopsonistic control** over company sales. **Virji Vora**, the dominant
Bania merchant of mid-century, lent to both companies and bought their imports almost
single-handedly; by 1634 the EIC council called him "the sole monopolist of all
Europian commodities," and in 1643 conceded he "must not bee displeased in any case."
Brokers, in turn, needed company **protection** in a dangerous ocean — low customs
duties, pass rights, and armed convoy — making the bargain genuinely reciprocal.

## Major Phases

1. **Merchant-princes (early–mid 17th c.):** individuals like Virji Vora wielding
   near-monopsony power; companies heavily indebted.
2. **Broker dynasties (mid–late 17th c.):** the **Parekh family** (EIC: Tapidas
   1609–1660, Tulsidas 1636–1667, Bhimji to 1686 — fortune over £1 million sterling
   by 1682) and the **Kishandas family** (VOC broker 1659–1795, 136 years).
3. **Parsi challenge (c. 1700):** Zoroastrian merchants — Rustamji Manikji,
   Mancherji Khurshedji (VOC, ~6 ocean-going ships), Dhanjishah Manjishah — broke
   Bania monopoly, enlarging the broker pool.
4. **Portfolio-splitting (18th c.):** companies divided the broker's role into
   distinct functions (import-disposal broker, export-goods supplier, local agent;
   VOC *makelaars* vs. *leveranciers*); the EIC relabelled its broker a *marfatia*
   by 1740 to curtail his authority.
5. **Political ascendancy (post-1759):** the EIC's takeover of Surat castle gave it
   leverage — yet "did not free the company from its dependence on brokers."

## Interaction and Significance

Protection functioned as a form of **extraterritorial jurisdiction**: in the 1771
capture of Dhanjishah Manjishah's ship by Portugal, the EIC declared "our broker
cannot be permitted to apply to your tribunal for justice," asserting sovereignty
over an Indian merchant and pressuring the nawab to detain Portuguese goods in
retaliation. The decline of single-family power tracked the decline of Mughal
patronage and the diffusion of mercantile wealth (Abdul Ghafur, d. 1718 with ~17
ships and over 8 million rupees, "was perhaps the last of this generation of merchant
princes"). The EIC succeeded more than the VOC in limiting broker authority — but
"the companies and the Indian merchants continued to play complementary roles and
their relationship remained mutually beneficial."

## End Conditions

VOC commercial activity collapsed after the Fourth Anglo-Dutch War (1781–83); the
broker system persisted under the EIC into the colonial period in attenuated form,
the intermediaries never wholly displaced even as the company became a territorial
power.
