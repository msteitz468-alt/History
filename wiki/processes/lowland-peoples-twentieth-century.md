## Definition and Scope

The twentieth-century history of lowland South American indigenous peoples is a process of progressive incorporation — largely coercive — into national states whose governments, economies, and development programmes had previously overlooked or only marginally engaged them. David Maybury-Lewis, the author of Ch. 26 of the *Cambridge History of Native Peoples of the Americas* Vol. III Part 2 (1999), organises this history around seven countries: Venezuela, Colombia, Ecuador, Peru, Bolivia, Paraguay, and Brazil. All contain portions of the Amazon Basin or its northern and southern flanks; all have indigenous populations whose demographic and legal precariousness reflected centuries of frontier marginalisation.

The process has two dominant phases: a first phase of destructive incorporation (rubber boom, ranching, missionary reduction, state colonisation programmes) and a second of organised resistance via indigenous federations, NGO partnerships, and international law — the latter phase accelerating from the 1960s onward. Maybury-Lewis notes the chapter carries an advocacy dimension, writing from the standpoint of indigenous rights rather than state interests.

**Definitional problem.** Accurate population counts did not exist; Table 26.1 in the source presents low, high, and average estimates by country. Bolivia's lowland Indian population ranged from 80,000 to 187,000; Brazil's from 200,000 to 300,000; Venezuela's from 105,000 to 400,000. Governments frequently minimised figures, while indigenous organisations alleged undercounting. As Maybury-Lewis notes, "the numbers of Indians in any area fluctuate according to people's feelings about Indianness" — itself a function of political context.

## Causal Drivers

- **Rubber Boom (c. 1880–1920).** The demand for latex for the automobile industry drew settlers and labour-recruiters deep into [[amazonia]], causing "grave disruptions for several Amazonian ethnic groups." The Andoke people of Colombia testified: "They killed and killed and killed and killed everyone. . . . They shot them and our leaders died of hunger." Roger Casement's reports prompted British parliamentary debate about slavery.
- **Road-building and colonisation programmes (1950s–1990s).** State-funded roads opened formerly isolated forest territories to highland migrants, ranchers, and logging concerns. The Shuar Federation of Ecuador was founded in 1961, the same year the highway cut into the jungle.
- **Oil extraction.** Venezuela, Colombia, Ecuador, and Peru all experienced oil booms that brought infrastructure, settlers, and resource-extraction enterprises into lowland territories. Ecuador's Amazon oil boom from 1972 generated new threats to peoples already present.
- **State agricultural policy.** "Empty lands" legislation across multiple countries ignored swidden cultivators already in residence, legalising colonisation over indigenous occupation. In Ecuador, Law 2171 (1964) regulated "empty lands," ignoring existing populations.
- **Missionary activity.** Catholic and Protestant missions had complex effects: they disrupted lifeways (Salesian boarding schools among the Shuar) but also, when reform-minded, catalysed indigenous self-organisation. The Summer Institute of Linguistics (US-based Protestant mission) began operations in Colombia in 1962 and was eventually expelled from several areas by indigenous organisations.
- **Demographic recovery.** After catastrophic losses during the colonial and early-republican periods, lowland groups experienced population recovery across the twentieth century, providing an organisational base that had not previously existed.

## Major Phases

### Phase 1: Rubber Boom and Early Frontier Incorporation (1880–1945)

The rubber economy's labour demands generated mass atrocities across the upper Amazon. States used frontier expansion to assert sovereignty over territories previously inhabited autonomously. In Venezuela, successive constitutions and laws (1821, 1836, 1838, 1885, 1904, 1936) sought to abolish indigenous group rights entirely, treating all resguardos as national land — described by Nelly Arvelo-Jiménez as "legalized theft." South of the Orinoco, remote peoples were placed under Catholic Church tutelage by the 1915 Mission Law, which declared their territories tierras baldías (vacant lands).

In Paraguay, German Mennonite colonists moved into the Gran Chaco from 1927 onward, establishing farms on land the indigenous Enxet and neighbouring peoples depended on for hunting and gathering. Guaraní speakers of the Chaco experienced similar dispossession.

### Phase 2: Post-War Development and Missionary Reduction (1945–1970)

State-led development programmes promoted road-building, colonisation, and resource extraction as the path to national modernity. Lowland peoples were either invisible in planning processes or treated as a problem to be "civilised." In Brazil, the Indian Protection Service (SPI), created in 1910 with the famous humane pacification principle of Rondon, was riven by corruption and was eventually replaced by FUNAI in 1967. A 1967 inquiry revealed widespread abuses: "summary executions, torture, biological warfare (introducing smallpox, flu, and TB), theft, forced labour, and the outright expropriation of Indian lands." In Venezuela, Catholic orders controlled large areas in the south under mission laws that provided schooling in exchange for cultural assimilation.

### Phase 3: Organised Indigenous Response (1960–1995)

The decisive rupture was the formation of self-governing indigenous federations, beginning in Ecuador. The **Shuar Federation** (1961, formalised 1964) organised 40,000 inhabitants in 240 centres by 1987, pioneering a model of territorial consolidation and bilingual education within indigenous control. "The Shuar stimulus was fundamental to the organization of other Amazonian groups, especially after 1972, when the oil boom began."

In Bolivia, the **CIDOB** (Confederation of Peoples and Indigenous Communities of Eastern Bolivia) formed in 1982 as a top-down coordinating body, stimulating organisation among lowland nationalities. The Assembly of the Guaraní People (from 1987) united 60,000 Guaraní-Chiriguano people in three departments. In August 1990 the **"march for territory and dignity"** brought 800 men, women, and children from twelve ethnic groups on a 34-day walk to La Paz; on the Andean Cordillera they were received by Aymaras "amidst a sea of waving wiphala flags." The march won government decrees recognising five indigenous territories covering 1.5 million hectares.

In Brazil, the 1988 constitution was a landmark: it recognised indigenous peoples' rights to their traditional lands and required the state to demarcate them. The Kayapó, among others, organised spectacular protests that attracted international attention. In Colombia, the Amazonian groups organised under regional federations and eventually through ONIC and COICA.

| Brazil | SPI/FUNAI, rubber-tapper movement, Kayapó media campaigns, 1988 constitution |

## End Conditions

As of 1995 the process remained open. Brazil's 1988 constitution was the most advanced legal framework; Bolivia's 1990 march had won territorial recognition in decree form; Ecuador's CONAIE had moved from Amazonia into a highland-lowland synthesis. However, resource-extraction pressure from oil, ranching, and logging continued to grow; guerrilla and state violence in Colombia and Peru continued to trap lowland communities between opposing armed forces; and no country had translated territorial recognition decrees into fully implemented land demarcation.

The 1990s international context added new enablers: the rise of environmental NGOs, the "green lung" discourse around Amazonia, Vatican II liberation theology, and post-Soviet opening within the Latin American left all gave indigenous territorial claims greater visibility — though Maybury-Lewis notes these audiences were attracted as much by ecological motives as by indigenous rights proper.
