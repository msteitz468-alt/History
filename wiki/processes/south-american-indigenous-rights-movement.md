## Definition and Scope

The South American indigenous rights movement is the collective process by which indigenous peoples of South America — Andean highlanders, Amazonian lowlanders, and peoples of the Caribbean coast, Patagonia, and the Gran Chaco — built organisations to defend land, language, and self-government against states, extractive industries, and colonisation fronts. It spans the 1960s to the present, with decisive institutional acceleration in the 1970s–1990s.

The movement is characterised by three simultaneous demands that distinguish it from earlier peasant movements:

1. **Territory**, not merely land redistribution. The old slogan "the land to those who work it" gave way to "our own territory," implying "the right to all the natural resources of the territory and a certain leeway to develop lifeways and forms of government autonomously."
2. **Cultural recognition**: education in indigenous languages, legal recognition of customary governance structures, official status for indigenous law.
3. **Political participation** within — but not only within — existing state frameworks; the concept of the **plurinational state** (Bolivia, 1979–2009) represents the most radical expression.

**Historiographical note.** Both Albó (Ch. 25) and Maybury-Lewis (Ch. 26) in the *Cambridge History of Native Peoples of the Americas* Vol. III Part 2 (1999) write from an explicitly pro-indigenous standpoint and acknowledge this. Their accounts should be read with awareness that they were composed in the mid-1990s, before the most recent phase of constitutional transformation (Bolivia 2009, Ecuador 2008).

## Causal Drivers

- **Failure of "peasantist" agrarian reforms.** Across the Andes, the 1950s–1970s reforms dismantled the hacienda but replaced ethnic community structures with state-controlled unions, erasing indigenous identity without resolving poverty. The frustrations of this period fed ethnic resurgence.
- **Amazonian development frontier.** Road-building, oil prospecting, ranching, and colonisation programmes from the 1960s displaced peoples previously autonomous from state authority, creating urgent defensive imperatives.
- **ILO Convention 169 (1989).** The International Labour Organization's revision of earlier assimilationist Convention 107 created new international legal language for indigenous peoples' rights, particularly over territory and consultation. This gave transnational legitimacy to movement demands.
- **NGOs and liberation theology.** From the 1960s "the presence of NGOs has become increasingly important." Bishops like Leónidas Proaño (Ecuador), Samuel Ruiz (Mexico), and Pedro Casaldáliga (Brazilian Amazon) became symbols of church solidarity with indigenous peoples.
- **Environmental discourse.** The "green lung" framing of Amazonia from the late 1980s made lowland indigenous territorial claims media-visible internationally and introduced new allies (environmental organisations, World Bank) to movements previously confined to domestic politics.
- **1992 quincentennial.** Five hundred years since Columbus, the year generated continent-wide indigenous gatherings and produced the shared motto "500 years of resistance," adopted at the Quito meeting of 1990.
- **Post-Soviet left.** The collapse of statist Marxism created space within Latin American left parties and intellectual circles for indigenous proposals previously marginalised as "pre-political."

## Major Phases

### Foundation Phase (1961–1975): Defensive Federations

The Shuar Federation (Ecuador, 1961/1964) pioneered the model: a self-governing federation of indigenous communities managing territory, bilingual education, and economic development without state mediation. Its founding was directly triggered by highway construction that threatened colonisation.

In Colombia, the Regional Indigenous Council of Cauca (CRIC, 1971) was the first explicitly ethnic political organisation in the Andean highlands, born from a meeting of 2,000 Paez and Guambiano Indians in Toribio in February 1971. Its foundational demands: exemption from labour obligations, recovery of usurped *resguardo* lands, strengthening of *cabildos*, defence of language and culture.

In Bolivia, [[katarismo]] crystallised as an Aymara political movement combining class and ethnic analysis.

### Expansion and National Confederation (1975–1990)

National-level umbrella organisations emerged:

### High Mobilisation (1990–1995)

- **June 1990, Ecuador.** CONAIE led a simultaneous national uprising. Demonstrators occupied Quito's Santo Domingo Church; rural sympathisers blockaded roads. The event, called "The Uprising" (*levantamiento*), was described by commentators as "the Ethnic Earthquake." Nothing comparable had occurred since the 1870 rebellion of Fernando Daquilema.
- **August 1990, Bolivia.** 800 lowland indigenous marchers from twelve ethnic groups walked to La Paz in a 34-day "march for territory and dignity." On the Andean Cordillera "they were received by Aymaras amidst a sea of waving wiphala flags." Won recognition of five territories, 1.5 million hectares.
- **June 1994, Ecuador.** Second national uprising paralysed the country for ten days, forcing the government to negotiate with a commission led by Luis Macas and Nina Pacari.
- **1993, Bolivia.** Victor Hugo Cárdenas, Katarista Aymara, elected vice-president.
- **1991, Colombia.** New constitution included indigenous rights: ethnic and cultural diversity recognised (Art. 7); indigenous territorial entities (resguardos) given right to self-governance (Arts. 329–330); two indigenous senators mandated (Art. 171).

## Geographic Spread

The movement is continental but uneven:

- **Most advanced institutionally**: Bolivia (plurinational concept, CSUTCB, Cárdenas), Ecuador (CONAIE, two uprisings, bilingual education policy)
- **Most constrained by violence**: Colombia (400+ indigenous leaders murdered in Cauca alone by 1994), Peru (Shining Path and army counter-insurgency killed 20,000+ mostly indigenous peasants in the 1980s)
- **Latest to join**: Chile (Mapuche reorganisation after 1990 Pinochet plebiscite; new indigenous law 1993; north Chilean Aymara revival)
- **Most advanced international framework**: Brazil (1988 constitution mandating demarcation of indigenous lands)

Cross-border attempts at coordination included meetings in Cusco, Tiwanaku, and Quito, and bodies like CISA (Coordination of Indians of South America) — which failed to consolidate — and COICA (Amazon lowlands) — which proved more durable.

A notable instance of cross-border solidarity: when Peru and Ecuador fought a border war in 1995 in Shuar territory, CONAIE (Ecuador) and AIDESEP (Peru) issued a joint statement "exhorting their respective governments to rise above exaggerated nationalisms."

## End Conditions

As of 1995 the movement was ascending, not concluded. The most significant legal achievements (Bolivia's 2009 constitution, Ecuador's 2008 constitution) lay ahead of the source's composition date. Structural constraints persisted: state resistance to "nation" as an indigenous category, resource-extraction pressure, internal organisational fragmentation, violence, and neoliberal economic policies that undermined community livelihoods. The movement's own intellectual framework — "as Indians they exploited us, as Indians we liberate ourselves" — indicated the extent to which the process had moved from defensive to proactive.
