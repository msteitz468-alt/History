# The Organization of Early Modern Trade (1400–1800)

## Definition and Scope

This is the analytical frame for **how long-distance trade was organized**
between 1400 and 1800 — its technology, institutions, business forms, finance,
and relation to states — drawn from Francesca Trivellato and Charles H. Parker in
[[bentley-cwh-v6-2015|CWH VI]]. The central practical problem everywhere was the
**"problem of distance" (or agency)**: how to ensure that delegated agents in
faraway places provided reliable service. Some solutions were universal; others
varied with ecology, technology, and institutions. The story extends the
[[commercial-revolution|medieval commercial revolution]] into the era when trade
became genuinely global.

Both authors write against the old Marx/Weber "rise of the West" framing
(extractive Asian agrarian empires versus commercial Europe) and emphasize a
**"rough symmetry"** of commercial development across Afro-Eurasia. The chapter
is explicitly situated within the **Great Divergence** debate (Pomeranz):
advanced regions of China resembled England in markets, productivity, and wages
until the late eighteenth century, so any European edge came less from superior
institutions than from New World resources, plantation slavery, and the
**militarization** of maritime commerce. (See the dispute at
[[great-divergence]].)

## Causal Drivers and Conditions

- **Technology and infrastructure.** No transport revolution before railways and
  steamships, but cumulative "micro-innovations": the caravel, the Dutch
  *fluitship*, the full-rigged "Atlantic ship," wind charts, and marine
  insurance. Asian (Chinese, Southeast Asian) maritime technology was advanced at
  the period's start; the sharpest gains in shipping came in Europe, and falling
  freight costs (e.g. Chesapeake tobacco, −1.4%/year 1619–1775) owed more to
  **institutional** support than to dramatic invention.
- **Information and markets.** Merchant manuals, printed price-currents (from the
  late sixteenth century), periodic fairs, and designated market quarters
  reduced transaction costs. Foreign merchants were typically segregated
  (*funduq*, Macao, Deshima, Manila's Parián); Amsterdam's openness was the
  exception. Cross-cultural trade did **not** dissolve cultural barriers or
  produce impersonal markets — a host of local solutions (brokers, Hong
  merchants, licensed linguists) managed the risk of dealing with strangers.

## Business Forms

- **Family firms and sole proprietorships** were the most prevalent form; kinship
  offered partial solutions to capital-raising and agent-trust everywhere
  (Portuguese nephews and godchildren; Chinese lineages; Fujianese foster sons),
  with **women's dowries** an important capital source and widows often taking
  over firms.
- **Contracts**: the *commenda* (Arabic *qirad/mudaraba*) of the medieval
  Mediterranean — resident partner supplies capital and bears risk, traveling
  agent supplies labor — persisted and spread through Central Asia (but not China
  or England); alongside it, commission agency, salaried factors, and the
  *accomandita* and *compagnia*. Trivellato cautions that the presence or absence
  of formal contracts is **not** good evidence for the impersonality of markets;
  Chinese merchants used contracts and third-party arbitration, while European
  merchants relied heavily on informal reputation.
- **[[trade-diaspora|Trade diasporas]]** (Curtin's concept): geographically
  dispersed, stateless merchant communities bound by profession, religion,
  language, or origin — Armenians of New Julfa (monocentric), Sephardic Jews
  (polycentric), Hokkien Chinese, Hausa and Wangara, Gujaratis. See the dedicated
  page.

## Finance and the State

Credit ran on partnerships, **bills of exchange** (and Islamic *suftaja*, Indian
*hundi*, Japanese equivalents), and public banks (Amsterdam's *Wisselbank*,
1609). England's **financial revolution** (parliamentary control of the public
debt, 1688; Bank of England, 1694) may have lowered private interest rates; the
speculative crashes of 1720 (South Sea, Mississippi) showed the dangers. The
decisive European difference was **military protection of maritime trade**:
Portuguese *cartazes* and the Dutch system turned the open Indian Ocean into a
"militarized system of extraction," though this never amounted to a single-handed
European triumph — Asian powers hired, challenged, or routed around the
Europeans.

The **chartered joint-stock company** (the [[dutch-east-india-company|VOC]],
1602; the [[english-east-india-company|EIC]], 1600) was the era's most dramatic
organizational innovation: permanent tradable capital, separation of ownership
and management, and limited liability — but also a **quasi-state**, the military
and administrative arm of its sponsor, governing territory and waging war. Its
supremacy "derived at least as much from military force as from managerial and
financial organization."

## Changing Patterns of Eurasian Trade

Trivellato marks five discontinuities: Zheng He's first voyage (1405) and
Southeast Asia's "age of commerce"; da Gama's Cape route (1497); the
Acapulco–Manila galleon (after 1571) joining American silver to Asian markets;
the seventeenth-century shift of Europe's economic center from the Mediterranean
to the Atlantic; and the changing composition of Asian imports (spices giving
way to Indian cottons). Silver flowed east toward China's favorable gold:silver
ratio (1:5–6 versus 1:12 in Europe), the engine of [[global-silver-trade|the
global silver economy]].

## The Fifteenth-Century European Recovery (NCMH VII)

[[allmand-ncmh-v7-1998|NCMH VII]] (Wendy Childs, Commerce and Trade, Ch. 7) supplies the European
opening of this period: a century "generally seen as one of economic **contraction until the late
1460s and then of expansion**," resting on a "tightly integrated" and "sophisticated" trade network
flexible enough to specialise and adapt.

- **A "world economy" in embryo.** Routes linked the Baltic/North Sea (the Hanse *kontors* at
  Novgorod, Bergen, Bruges, London), the Atlantic, the Mediterranean and Black Sea, and the
  east–west land roads via Prague. Their epitome was **Bruges** (later Antwerp), where "Baltic furs,
  Icelandic stockfish, English cloth, Polish grain, Iberian wine, Florentine silk and Indian spices
  could be purchased."
- **Capitalist organisation.** Italians led with company forms, branches, bills of exchange,
  double-entry bookkeeping and premium insurance — the **Medici** company (eight branches, ~60 staff)
  rising fastest in the *hardest* years before its decline after Cosimo (d. 1464). But Catalans,
  Germans (the **Fugger** of Augsburg, rising on mining and finance) and Englishmen (the **Staple
  Company**; the Cely and Veckinchusen firms) were equally capable; the most spectacular career was
  **Jacques Coeur** of Bourges (ennobled 1440, crashed for political reasons 1451).
- **The three problems of the contraction.** Childs identifies three largely "external" pressures,
  varying by region and date: **demographic decline** (worst early in the century, sparing the
  east); the **"bullion famine"** (most acute in the 1450s–60s, as silver drained east for spices,
  relieved only when new Saxon/Tyrolean/Bohemian mines came on stream from the 1460s); and
  **war** (intermittent — the end of the Hundred Years War, Italian and Hanse–English conflicts, the
  Ottoman disruption of the Black Sea and Levant after 1453). Crucially, **falling *volume* often
  meant much less-fallen *value***, as trade shifted toward luxuries and higher-value cloth.
- **The late-century expansion.** From the late 1460s population and bullion both grew; English
  cloth exports climbed from ~40,000 to ~90,000 cloths a year; Bordeaux's wine fleet and Baltic
  Sound traffic swelled; the new **printing/book trade** and the [[age-of-discovery|Atlantic
  voyages]] opened. Winners and losers shifted — the Hanse lost the Baltic carrying trade to the
  Dutch and English, **Bruges finally lost to Antwerp**, and the **commercial centre of gravity
  began moving westward** toward the Atlantic and a "truly world economy," built on sea-going skills
  "already familiar, honed and refined" across the century.

## Interaction and End Conditions

The long-run trajectory ran (unevenly) from the generalist entrepreneur toward
complex organizations and more impersonal markets; from general to limited
partnerships; from kin-based to non-familial firms. The process feeds
[[indian-ocean-trade]], [[global-silver-trade]], [[plantation-economy]], and the
[[afro-eurasian-world-system]], and underlies the
[[early-modern|construction of a global world]].
