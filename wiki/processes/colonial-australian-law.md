## Definition and Scope

The process by which [[australia|colonial Australia]] received, adapted, and expanded English law from 1788 to Federation in 1901. This encompassed: the reception of English common law and statute; the construction of a court hierarchy in each colony; the development of centralised colonial police forces; an expanding regulatory state managing "populations defined by boundaries of exclusion"; and the legal architecture of land dispossession and Aboriginal control rooted in [[terra-nullius]]. Law was simultaneously "the twin of government" and an instrument of [[settler-colonialism]]. "In the first century of Australian settlement law was the twin of government; sometimes its critic, at others its instrument."

Source: Mark Finnane, ch. 16 in [[bashford-macintyre-cha-v1-2013]].

---

## Causal Drivers

- **[[terra-nullius]] and the taking of land**: "Legal institutions were scarcely visible at the founding of New South Wales. It was nearly half a century before the scope of the legal authority claimed by the British settlers over the country's Indigenous inhabitants was determined." Dispossession proceeded with military force and the warrant of martial law; the legal non-recognition of Aboriginal sovereignty was foundational.
- **Reception of English law**: The **Australian Courts Act of 1828** declared that the law was "what the common and statute law provided at that date in England," giving the colonies a common-law baseline while colonial parliaments added statutes.
- **Convict society**: The penal foundations required "a precise and comprehensive bureaucratic control system" — a precursor of later population surveillance and regulatory regimes.
- **Self-government**: From the 1850s colonial parliaments legislated extensively without Colonial Office oversight, enabling coercive as well as liberal reforms.
- **Utilitarian political culture**: Australia was characterised as a "'Benthamite society', utilitarian and legalist in its political culture, a place where there was less interest in the language of rights than in what governments might deliver to the people, through bureaucratic institutions."
- enabled_by: [[british-empire]] (imperial statutes framing each colony; Colonial Office oversight of colonial legislation; model of Irish Constabulary for rural policing)

## Major Phases

### 1788–1823 — Military jurisdiction and gubernatorial law
Initial courts were military, sitting under a civilian judge advocate with jury of military officers. The governor was the final court of appeal; "the authority of law in the early colonial period was therefore a gubernatorial authority." Judges served on executive councils and drafted statutes. Governor Macquarie established the Sydney police in 1810, appointing D'Arcy Wentworth a salaried superintendent.

**Martial law and Indigenous resistance**: "Dispossession proceeded with the aid of military force, and occasionally under the warrant of that emergency act of executive government, martial law." Key episodes: Castle Hill Irish convict rebellion (1804); Macquarie's 1816 proclamation of ten named Aboriginal men as outlaws; Governor Brisbane's 1824 martial law against the Wiradjuri at Bathurst; Governor Arthur's proclamation of martial law in Van Diemen's Land (1828, extended to the whole island 1830), enabling the **Black Line** operation.

### 1823–1850s — Supreme Courts; legal institutionalisation
The **NSW Act 1823** established Supreme Courts in Sydney and Hobart. South Australia (1837), Victoria and Queensland (1852/1861), Western Australia (1861) followed. "In comparison with England, the courts of the colonies were unified, with the Supreme Court jurisdiction encompassing civil and criminal matters as well as equity and chancery." More than 300 courthouses were built in NSW in the nineteenth century; judicial ceremony (robes, the black cap, the Queen's Proclamation against vice) performed authority. Trial reporting saturated colonial newspapers: "Law and litigation saturated colonial society, providing entertainment, instruction and moral tales that defined identities, boundaries and norms."

The **Colonial Laws Validity Act of 1865** resolved the "repugnancy" crisis triggered by Justice Boothby in South Australia (who invalidated successive SA statutes as repugnant to English law until removed from the bench), clarifying the scope of colonial self-government.

### 1820s–1860s — Police formation; bushranger suppression
Governor Macquarie established the Sydney police (1810); Governor Stirling established police at Swan River (1830). In Van Diemen's Land, Lieutenant-Governor Arthur created a "highly centralised policing, with two-thirds of police also being convicts." Two imperial models shaped Australian policing: the **London Metropolitan Police (1829)** for urban policing; the **Irish Constabulary (1836)** for rural. "The weakness of local government in Australia meant that at an early point police were controlled from the colonial capitals" — highly centralised, unlike England's county-based system.

Bushranger crises drove police reform. In NSW in the 1850s–60s (Ben Hall, Frank Gardiner), and Victoria in the late 1870s (Ned Kelly), police were found inadequate. The **NSW Felons Apprehension Act 1865** and the post-Kelly **Longmore Commission** (recommending promotion by examination, new police code, detective integration) were outcomes. "The Bushranging Act" (NSW, *Robbery and Firearms Act* 1830) reversed onus of proof and gave broad detention powers; its eventual repeal in 1853 was a liberal cause célèbre.

### 1830s–1900 — Population regulation; the expanding regulatory state
The nineteenth century was "a period of enormous expansion in law's capacity to shape populations, define the boundaries of acceptable behaviour and express preferences for a certain style of urban order." Key regulatory domains:

- **Criminal law codification**: Police Offences Acts from 1820s; codification of serious offences from 1850s. Prosecution patterns shifted from victim-led to police/public-prosecutor-led.
- **Lunacy law**: NSW Lunacy Act 1843 empowered police to channel people from cells into asylums — "in effect a branch of penal legislation." Asylum populations grew steadily.
- **Child welfare**: 1860s legislation (NSW, Victoria) for neglected children and juvenile reformatories. Henry Parkes's 1860 select committee established state institutional provision for poverty, distress, and crime. Fostering/boarding-out systems developed as alternatives, shaped by women's philanthropic activism and globe-trotting reformers like Florence and Rosamond Hill.
- **Contagious diseases regulation**: Queensland's *Act for the Prevention of Contagious Diseases* (1868) — originating in an 1857 Indian ordinance protecting British soldiers — allowed detention and medical inspection of women identified as prostitutes. Only Tasmania followed Queensland's full model; Victoria's 1878 Act was never enacted; NSW and SA attempts failed. Vagrancy law provided an alternative coercive tool.
- **Immigration and quarantine**: "The racial discriminations of Chinese-restriction legislation were complemented by the refinement of quarantine controls to prevent the spread of infectious diseases, and draconian segregations targeting leprosy."
- **Capital punishment**: Colonies abolished capital punishment for rape (unlike England); the politics of mercy mitigated the death penalty for women; interracial offences retained exemplary use of hanging reflecting anxiety about "the threat of black men, especially to white women."

### 1838–1900 — Aboriginal policing and the protection regime
The **Queensland Native Police** (1849–) was the archetype of paramilitary frontier policing: troopers recruited from districts foreign to those they policed, on a principle derived from the Irish Constabulary; operating "under limited supervision" with "estimates of fatalities remain highly speculative." The earliest Native Police Corps (Port Phillip District, 1837) was conceived as integrative — Captain Lonsdale thought enlistment would foster "gradual weaning from their native habits." Later colonials "were less sanguine, and more brutal."

By the 1880s a new policing model of Aboriginal "protection" emerged in north Queensland. "A later review of legal definitions of 'Aboriginal identity' found no less than 67 distinct definitions, reaching back into the 1830s." Vagrancy law was adapted to control Aboriginal movement and limit "miscegenation." Governor Bourke's 1835 NSW law penalised "idle associations between the settlers and 'black natives'"; Victoria adapted it in 1852.

The Kimberley resistance of Jandamarra (Pigeon, Bunuba; 1894–97) — himself a former police tracker — required large-scale policing operations to suppress, occurring simultaneously with the formal shift from Native Police to protection in Queensland, "underscoring the ineradicable foundation of colonial law in the use of force."

## Geographic Spread

- **NSW**: Epicentre of legal institutionalisation; Supreme Court 1823; largest courthouse building programme; 1880 Public Instruction Act overlap with regulatory state.
- **Van Diemen's Land / Tasmania**: Arthur's authoritarian centralised policing was rejected after self-government (1856) in favour of municipal police — the only colony to maintain local control until 1898.
- **Victoria**: Shaped by gold-rush demographics; Kelly outbreak drove major police reform; corporate schools and policing both shaped by Irish immigration (80% of Victorian constabulary Irish-born by 1870s, half ex–Irish Constabulary).
- **Queensland**: Most extensive Native Police operation; contagious diseases legislation enacted; distinctive frontier policing context.
- **South Australia**: Colonial Laws Validity Act crisis (Boothby); no lunacy-law/vagrancy excesses; Nonconformist influence moderated some regulatory impulses.
- **Western Australia**: Supreme Court 1861 (in a leaking converted gaol); police commissioner in Perth controlled vast Northern Territory and Kimberley regions.

## Interaction

- caused_by: [[terra-nullius]] — the foundational legal fiction enabling British sovereignty claims and land alienation without treaty.
- part_of: [[settler-colonialism]] — law was "the twin of government" in the dispossession process.
- concurrent_with: [[colonial-australian-education]] — the Public Schools Acts were major colonial legislation; the regulatory state and education state developed in parallel.
- concurrent_with: [[religion-colonial-australia]] — Church Acts (1836–37) were state legislation allocating funds to denominations; divorce law reform provoked Colonial Office objection; Sabbatarian legislation shaped policing of public order.
- analogous_to: Irish Constabulary model of centralised rural policing — explicitly adopted as the template for Australian rural police forces.
- contrasts_with: English county magistracy system — Australian colonies developed paid stipendiary magistrates from Macquarie's era, given the "frequently chaotic conditions of colonial expansion."
- produced: Aboriginal Protection Acts of the 1890s–1900s — direct outgrowth of the protection policing model developed in Queensland in the 1880s.

## End Conditions

Federation (1901) unified the colonies under a Commonwealth but left most law (criminal, civil, property, police) to the states, which retained their distinct colonial legal inheritances. The **Australia Act 1986** finally ended appeals to the Privy Council in London — a line of authority that had "endured for almost two centuries." The Aboriginal protection regime, entrenched in colonial law by 1901, was intensified federally after the exclusion of Aboriginal people from the franchise and from Commonwealth welfare. The regulatory architecture — vagrancy law, lunacy law, child-welfare institutions, police centralisation — was carried forward into the twentieth century largely intact.
