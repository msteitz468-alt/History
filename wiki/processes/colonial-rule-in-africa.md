# Colonial Rule and Society in Africa

Beyond conquest and extraction, colonialism **reordered African societies** — through systems of
administration, the creation of new social classes, and a religious transformation. Documented for the wiki
through the **UNESCO [[gha-vol7-colonial-1880-1935|General History of Africa, Vol. VII]]** (chs. 16–18),
which weighs the social "balance sheet" of colonial rule.

## Systems of administration

European powers governed on the cheap, through contrasting (but overlapping) models:

- **Indirect rule** (British, theorized by **Lugard** from the [[sokoto-caliphate|Sokoto]] model): governing
  through existing African rulers and "native authorities" — preserving (and often **inventing**) chiefs
  and "customary law" as instruments of colonial control.
- **Direct rule / assimilation** (French, Portuguese): a centralized administration with the promise (rarely
  honoured) that a small elite of *évolués*/*assimilados* could become citizens of the metropole.
- **Settler rule** (Kenya, the Rhodesias, Algeria, South Africa): where European settlers seized land and
  political power, producing the harshest racial hierarchies.

All shared a foundation of **racial subordination**, the alienation of land and labour, and the redrawing
of identities — colonial administration **hardening fluid ethnicities into bounded "tribes"** (the African
counterpart to the [[colonial-knowledge|colonial production of knowledge]] Cohn traced in India).

## Demographic shock and new social structures

GHA VII stresses the **demographic catastrophe of early colonialism**: conquest, forced labour, famine, and
above all introduced and spread **epidemic disease** (sleeping sickness, influenza, the 1918 pandemic)
caused population decline in many regions before later recovery. Colonialism also forged **new social
classes** — a Western-educated elite (clerks, teachers, professionals), urban workers, migrant labourers,
and cash-crop farmers — whose aspirations and grievances would feed **[[african-nationalism|nationalism]]**.

## Religion in the colonial era

Religion was transformed: **Christian missions** spread schooling and literacy (and a Western cultural
frame) and won mass conversions, while **Islam** also expanded rapidly under colonial peace and trade.
Crucially, Africans **Africanized** these faiths — founding **independent ("Ethiopian" and Zionist/Aladura)
churches** and Islamic brotherhoods that fused new religion with African forms and often became vehicles of
**anti-colonial protest** (the prophet movements of Kimbangu, Harris, and others).

## Significance

Colonial rule was, in GHA VII's judgement, a **brief but profoundly disruptive episode** whose "balance
sheet" was negative — but one that, through the new classes, ideas, churches, and grievances it created,
generated the very forces that would end it. Its administrative legacy (artificial borders, "tribalized"
identities, weak extractive states) shaped post-colonial Africa.
