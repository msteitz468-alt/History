## Definition and Scope

The evolution of Australian government, law and citizenship from the Federation of 1901 to the early twenty-first century constitutes a long-duration process of centralisation, legal reinterpretation, and contested social identity. The central dynamic is the tension between the founding federal compact — in which States retained residual sovereignty and equal Senate representation — and the steady fiscal and administrative expansion of Commonwealth power. Alongside constitutional change runs the transformation of the Australian 'subject' into an 'Australian citizen' whose rights, duties and social entitlements have been contested, extended and contracted across successive governing regimes. Source: [[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]].

## Causal Drivers

**The constitutional design** was a 'Washminster' hybrid: the US model of a state-based Senate and enumerated federal powers, combined with British responsible government and Westminster conventions. The Constitution "carefully enumerated the fields 'surrendered' to national government, including defence, taxation, external affairs, and postal services and telecommunications" while preserving state powers (Brown, citing Parkinson). The provision that referendums required majority approval in a majority of States as well as by a majority of voters entrenched conservatism — only 8 of 44 referendums passed.

**Fiscal centralisation** drove de facto Commonwealth expansion regardless of the original compact. By 1901–02 States received 93 per cent of government revenue; by 1909–19 only 55 per cent. The 1942 *Uniform Tax* case transferred all income taxation to the Commonwealth for the duration of World War 2 — and was never reversed. The States were left in "a remarkably weak position in comparison to other federal systems."

**The High Court** served as the third arm, formally protecting the federal balance but in practice legitimising expansion. The 1920 *Engineers' Case* overturned State immunity doctrine and "legitimised the expansion of federal powers at the expense of 'States' rights'." Chief Justice Owen Dixon's resolute legalism (1929–1964) entrenched deference to British precedent and avoided popular constitutionalism.

**The 'Australian Settlement'** of the first decade — a compact linking wages, tariffs, protectionism, racial exclusion under [[white-australia-policy]], and public investment — created a model W.K. Hancock described in 1930 as treating "the State as a vast public utility, whose duty it is to provide the greatest happiness for the greatest number."

**Keynesian economics**, arriving in Australia in the mid-1930s through figures such as H.C. Coombs, shifted government's role from regulating enterprise to managing macroeconomic aggregates. The 1945 White Paper on full employment formalised this commitment. Commonwealth employees rose from 68,000 in 1939 to 182,000 by 1949.

## Major Phases

### Phase 1: Federal Compact and the Australian Settlement (1901–1929)
The first decade established the key institutions: the Commonwealth Public Service (1902), the Court of Conciliation and Arbitration (1904), and the landmark 1907 *Harvester judgment* establishing a 'basic wage' sufficient for "the normal needs of the average employee living in a civilised community." The Tariff Board (1921) became "almost transcendent" authority in industry assistance. Full adult suffrage was granted in 1902 — adding ~750,000 voters — but women were not elected to Commonwealth parliament for nearly 40 years. Political polarisation sharpened: in 1910 Labor formed the world's first national majority labour government; the Nationalist–Country Party coalition (1923) represented capital and rural interests.

### Phase 2: Depression, War, and the Keynesian Turn (1929–1949)
Financial crisis and the rise of Keynesianism reshaped the state's role. War centralisation was authorised by the *National Security Act* (1939); the 1942 *Uniform Tax* case entrenched Commonwealth fiscal dominance. In 1948 Minister Arthur Calwell inaugurated 'Australian citizenship' as an independent status (following Canada and New Zealand), though "it brought with it no new rights" and Australians remained simultaneously 'British subjects' until 1984.

### Phase 3: Post-War Boom and the Managed Economy (1949–1972)
The Liberal–Country Party coalition (1949) inherited Keynesian instruments and used them without dismantling them. Commonwealth scholarships, the Murray Report (1957) on universities, and the managed 'mixed economy' characterised this era. ASIO surveillance of political radicalism ran in parallel with civil quiescence. From the late 1960s pressure built on multiple fronts: Indigenous rights (1967 referendum extending Commonwealth power), equal pay, decriminalisation of homosexuality (South Australia under Don Dunstan, 1970–79), and Senate revival via proportional representation.

### Phase 4: Whitlam and Social Citizenship (1972–1975)
Gough Whitlam's Labor government (1972–75) pledged "equality of opportunity" and acted on it: universal health care (Medibank), abolition of university fees, creation of a Department of Aboriginal Affairs, multiculturalism, Schools Commission per-capita grants, Family Court, Racial Discrimination Act (1975), and invocation of international conventions on human rights. Attorney-General Lionel Murphy introduced a Human Rights Bill and an Administrative Appeals Tribunal. The 1975 dismissal of the Whitlam government by the Governor-General remains constitutionally contested and central to republican debate.

### Phase 5: Economic Rationalism and Neoliberal Reform (1983–2000)
The Hawke Labor government's Accord with the ACTU embraced a 'social wage' and counter-inflationary wage restraint. The concept of an 'active society' — citizens gaining skills through education rather than remaining dependent — replaced the older welfare model. Protection was abolished. The 1987 Dawkins university reforms, privatisation of the Commonwealth Bank, and introduction of the national competition policy (1992, COAG) marked the peak of 'economic rationalism', described by Michael Pusey as a "locust strike" on the nation-building ethos. The High Court in *Mabo* (1992) and *Wik* (1996) recognised native title, triggering further tension between Indigenous rights and pastoral property claims. By 2002 both parties agreed the prohibition on dual citizenship had become untenable given the "international mobility of labour and capital."

## Geographic Spread

Federal–State tensions played out differently across the continent: Western Australia voted 68 per cent for secession in a 1933 referendum; Queensland, South Australia, Tasmania and Western Australia have consistently been beneficiaries of Grants Commission 'equalisation.' Indigenous land rights processes concentrated most acutely in the Northern Territory (nearly 50 per cent land held under native title by the early 2000s) and remote Queensland. Local government remained constitutionally weak, excluded from the federal compact, with 104 local councils established after the 1970s specifically to serve [[aboriginal-australians]] communities.

## End Conditions

The process is ongoing. The chapter author (Nicholas Brown) concludes that future historians are unlikely to find at the beginning of the twenty-first century "the vibrant civic culture rediscovered at the beginning of the twentieth," but may remark on the "persistence of federalism" despite centralising pressures, and on the "emergent creativity of law" in the High Court of the 1990s. The Australian Settlement's distinctive features — arbitrated wages, White Australia, protectionism, state socialism — were largely dismantled by the 1990s. Citizenship "continued to be seen as deficient, passive rather than active," reflecting the "utilitarianism, legalism and positivism inherent in Australian political culture."

## Historiography

The chapter by Nicholas Brown in [[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]] traces government–law–citizenship as an integrated field rather than three separate histories, following W.K. Hancock's 1930 *Australia* as the foundational synthesis and building on Francis Castles's concept of the 'wage earners' welfare state.' Key interpretive frameworks include: Brian Galligan on the High Court as political institution; Alastair Davidson on the transition from subject to citizen; and Michael Pusey's contentious 'economic rationalism' thesis. The chapter does not adjudicate whether the Whitlam dismissal was constitutional. It frankly notes the limited attention to local government as a structural weakness in the federal design. Indigenous citizenship remains a running thread — excluded from census counts at Federation, granted the right to vote federally in 1962, recognised as native title holders from 1992, but persistently underserved by Commonwealth–State divisions over responsibility. The collection coverage on twentieth-century Australian governance is moderate-to-strong through this volume.
