# Pacific Anthropocene Sustainability — Lessons of Pacific History

## Definition and Scope

This page covers the contemporary process by which Pacific Island communities are drawing on deep historical environmental management traditions to build climate resilience — and the tensions generated when those traditions encounter colonial land/marine tenure, international conservation initiatives, and the Anthropocene's pace of change. Two case studies ground the analysis: the **Federated States of Micronesia** (FSM), focusing on traditional zonal and clan-based resource management in the Mortlock Islands (Chuuk State); and **French Polynesia**, focusing on the institution of *rāhui* in the Society Islands (particularly Teahupoo, Tahiti) and its historical suppression and revival.

The chapter by Bambridge and Puas argues that 'Pacific Island nations are some of the most vulnerable nations on Earth to the negative consequences of global warming' and that their responses — 'emphasizing successful ancestral ways of resource management in partnership with Western science and technology' — deserve global recognition not as 'reluctant victims' but as 'proactive and successful climate mitigators' ([[hattori-samson-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v2-2023]], ch. 63).

## The Climate Threat

The threat profile faced by Pacific Island nations:

- Ocean acidification in the Pacific already 52 parts per thousand beyond the safe 350 ppm maximum.
- Impact of climate change on Pacific Islands is three times the global average.
- Pacific fisheries are shifting in response to water temperature changes; 'fish such as tuna and corals are sensitive to even one degree of variation in average water temperature.'
- Low-lying atoll nations (Kiribati, Marshall Islands, Tuvalu) face inundation; still consider mass relocation 'a last resort.'
- Mountainous island nations (PNG, Solomon Islands, Fiji) face mounting population pressure and crop/marine threats.
- Variable rainfall and intensification of natural hazards threatens food and water security on land.

International frameworks (2015 Paris Agreement, Sustainable Development Goals) are broadly supported by Pacific nations but 'the general failure of most nations to achieve the carbon emission reduction targets' has 'serious implications for Pacific Island nations' (ch. 63).

## FSM Case Study: Integrating lang, fanou, and saat

### Environmental Ontology

The Chuukese-Mortlockese people of the FSM low-lying atolls (the *shon matau* — 'people of the deep seas') understand long-term sustainability through three epistemological doctrines:

- **lang** — the sky/heaven and all within it; dictates daily activities through celestial movements of wind, stars, moon, sun.
- **fanou** — any island, any land above water; the physical soil that produces agricultural yield (*mongo*).
- **saat** — anywhere one can see or feel the sea; the ocean as provider of protein sustenance (*salei*).

A complete meal must have both *mongo* and *salei*; an incomplete meal signals ignorance of the environment. The interaction of lang, fanou, and saat influences *nonon aramas* — 'how people live with the environment historically' (ch. 63). 'Environmental care is essentially about the total knowledge of the unity between lang, fanou, and saat working together in harmony' (ch. 63).

### Zonal Management

Mortlock Islanders divided atoll space into approximately seventeen named environmental zones from the deep ocean to the lagoon centre, each specifying permitted activities, species targeted, and fishing/agricultural techniques. The zones also serve as communication system (locating persons through zones), ritual system (island priests direct *waitawa* — communication with ancestors — by zone), and resource training system (specialist fishers, canoe builders, traditional doctors locate resources by zone). Knowledge of the thirty stages of the moon — each with different implications for species behaviour — is integral to the system (ch. 63).

| Key Zones | Activity |
|---|---|
| Lematau (deep ocean) | Deep-water fishing and trawling |
| Likin ounou (exposed reef) | Reef fish source |
| On alang (shellfish area) | Shellfish and sea crab gathering |
| Lepwel (taro farms) | Taro farming |
| Lenomw (lagoon centre) | Big fish trapping |

### Clan-Based Resource Management and Pwau

In pre-colonial Micronesia, resources were 'community owned and jointly managed by members of the extended *ainang* [clan].' The social order — *inepwine* (immediate family), *mwalo* (maternal extended family), *ainang* (largest clan unit, thousands strong) — assigned different parts of the environment to different groups as custodians. The paramount *samol* (chief) controlled resource distribution and conservation decisions (ch. 63).

The key conservation instrument is **pwau**: public restriction on access to a portion of reef, island shore, or land — either to honour the death of an important clan member, or to allow depletion areas to replenish. A large tree branch (called *shell*) is planted to warn the public off designated areas. Violation could historically lead to 'severe consequences, including violence or even death' (ch. 63). Analogous restrictions: *otoul* (coconuts), *oneisat* (fish), *mwenmai* (breadfruit), *unupwel* (taro) — first-harvest offerings to the *samol* before the community consumes.

Seasonal storage (*maar* — underground storage of breadfruit during abundance) and fish weirs (*maii*, *laleo*) using tidal and current knowledge round out the management toolkit. 'Fishing and agricultural restrictive practices are common in the low-lying islands where customs and traditions remain the essence of life' (ch. 63). FSM's national and state constitutions now formally recognise clan reef ownership — Yap and Chuuk constitutions acknowledge this — and the national government holds conservation jurisdiction from 12 to 200 miles (EEZ), states from 12 miles to the barrier reef drop, municipalities within (ch. 63).

### Colonial Disruption

Successive colonial powers (Spanish, German, Japanese, American) 'permanently altered the land- and seascape.' Colonists built structures, dug trenches, introduced inappropriate fertilizers, imposed restrictive regimes 'contrary to the traditional conservation system.' The Japanese era saw punishment for opposition to colonial environmental practices (oral testimony records 'body scars left by Japanese punishment,' fn. 37). 'A consultative approach with the Indigenous experts was not part of the colonial environmental policy' (ch. 63). Post-colonial problems inherit these distortions.

Climate change is understood by Puas as 'caused by the external industrialized countries and yet impacts tremendously on the island environment.' The response is the **re-invitation** of traditional methods: 'Re-imagining the past traditional methods has been re-invited into the present to strengthen adaptation strategies to mitigate the impact of climate change on the environment' (ch. 63).

## French Polynesia Case Study: Rāhui and Mā'ohi Habitus

### The Rāhui Institution

The **rāhui** — a temporary restriction in time or space over a resource or territory — was the central institution of Polynesian resource management across eastern Polynesia. The Polynesian Lexicon gives the protoform *raafui* for East Polynesia with the meaning 'prohibit'; in practice, rāhui governed access to lagoons, lands, fish stocks, agricultural produce, and ceremonial resources (ch. 63).

The rāhui was embedded in the *ramage* system of Tahitian political economy: every senior member of an extended family was chief of his own ramage on its own territory, with rights to implement rāhui on land and marine territory within that territory. The *ari'i* (chief of the ramage) could hold rāhui rights on territories not directly controlled (through first-fruits and first-fish obligations). Rights were 'more relative than absolute,' involving 'access rights, extractive rights, penalties for violations, and relevant jurisdictions' (ch. 63, citing Bambridge, *The Rāhui*, ANU Press, 2016).

### Mā'ohi Habitus

Bambridge develops the concept of **Mā'ohi habitus** (in Bourdieu's sense — deep-seated, socially ingrained habits, skills, and dispositions) as the practical mechanism through which Islanders have continuously transmitted environmental relationship across generations, even through the disruptions of Christianisation, French colonisation, nuclear testing, and international conservation:

> 'Rooted in the relational, reciprocal bonds that link Islanders to resources and each other, Mā'ohi habitus dissolves classic Western distinctions between human and non-human, nature and culture, as objects, environment, and individuals respond to the continual flow of life and materials.' (ch. 63)

Fisherman Patrick Rochette from Teahupoo exemplifies this: fishing in a lagoon historically containing taro plantations, fish nurseries, and coral sites, he feels and responds to ancestral spirits through *fa'atura* (respect). His ancestor's *puna i'a* (stone fish — a large stone shaped like a fish and oriented toward the mountain) 'was already used by Patrick's ancestors as a means to ask favours and generously give back to the spirit of nature dedicated to the fishers and their resources' (ch. 63). He notes: 'the first rāhui ever known in Taiarapu date back from the thirteenth century and a more recent one put in place by the ari'i Teauroa at the end of the nineteenth century' — memory recalled with deep respect as part of contemporary identity (ch. 63).

### Colonial Destruction of Rāhui

The history of rāhui under colonialism is a layered process of appropriation, abolition, and paradoxical reintroduction:

- Christianisation sought to abolish the rāhui as a form of maintaining links to ancestors and old deities — 'inconceivable at a time when the political objective was to impose a new Judeo-Christian god' (ch. 63).
- Pomare II's Code of 1824 made rāhui the monopoly of the *ari'i nui* (great chief), centralising what had been a ramified system.
- French annexation (1853) and the 1887 land tenure decree prompted 60,000 land claims that also encompassed reef, lagoon, and fish-hole areas. Most Mā'ohi refused to divide these lands; most land in French Polynesia remains undivided across four to seven generations.
- Colonial coconut monoculture (for copra) destroyed forest cover, reduced soil fertility, and eliminated many of the Pisonia grandis forests that had sheltered birds, maintained humidity, and built humus on atolls. This 'reduced their resilience in the case of extreme climate change' (ch. 63).
- The nuclear economy from the 1960s — centred on the French nuclear experimentation centre — provoked exodus from outer islands, land price inflation in Papeete, and dependency on French transfers. Public resources represented 71% of GDP in 2015; Bambridge characterises the 'race for political competences' as having 'obliterated any other economic or social policy' and generated 'an economic model unsuited to development challenges' (ch. 63).
- The French colonial state paradoxically reintroduced rāhui in the Leeward Islands after the 1897 annexation — but only for land resources (not lagoon), administered by district chiefs, stripped of ancestral spiritual content and incorporated into the coercive state apparatus.

### The Teahupoo Rāhui Revival

Since 2010, fishers at Teahupoo have maintained a community rāhui on their lagoon: 'this place is rāhui meaning not only fishing is forbidden but also passing along, stopping by, etc.' The *tāvana* (mayor) supports it. The rāhui is upheld 'by discussions, exchanges, and surveillance by the fishers of Teahupoo and all the population living nearby.' The result: 'incredible biomass increase' in the protected lagoon, interpreted through Mā'ohi habitus as the ancestors' satisfaction — evidenced by unusual apparitions of turtles and whales (ch. 63).

### Dissonance with International Conservation

UNESCO, WWF, National Geographic, and BirdLife International now operate major conservation initiatives in French Polynesia: the Marquesas Protected Marine and Terrestrial Area, UNESCO World Heritage designation for Taputapuātea Marae (2019), 'the world's largest Ocean MPA,' and various lagoon reef management projects since 1990. These create fundamental tensions:

- Top-down governance culture of official conservation bodies conflicts with *rāhui* rules embedded in Mā'ohi habitus.
- French law (Civil Code 1866) separates sea from land, while Mā'ohi habitus integrates them into 'a meaningful, holistic appreciation of the relation between nature and culture.'
- 'Privatization of land law conflicts with extended families' lands still apprehended as a common heritage' (ch. 63).
- Tutelar spirits personalising extended families become 'symbols of endangered species for humankind' — a translation that may serve conservation but strips ancestral meaning.

Hau'ofa is cited as anticipating this tension: 'It is one of the great ironies of the Law of the Sea Convention, which enlarged our national boundaries, that it also extended the territorial instinct to where there was none before' (Hau'ofa, 'The ocean in us,' p. 39, cited in ch. 63). Hau'ofa proposed 'a new paradigm of an open sovereignty whose nature is fundamentally relational' — what he called Oceanian, not state-based, because 'the alternative term for the Pacific Islands, Oceania, refers to a world of people connected to each other' (ch. 63).

## Historical Pre-Colonial Resilience

Archaeological evidence from the Tuamotu atolls shows pre-European atoll ecosystems as 'self-sustaining.' Extensive Pisonia grandis forests sheltered seabird colonies whose droppings created phosphate-enriched humus, retaining humidity. Taro pits (some over 100 metres long, excavated several metres deep) allowed cultivation down to the underground fresh water lens using picks of *miki miki* (Pemphis acidula). Pandanus (Pandanus tectorius) was exploited for food, construction, rope, and leaf covers. 'By this means, the Pa'umotu were able to cultivate and water a varied range of plants and develop relative self-sufficiency in food which they lost with the establishment of the coconut monoculture' (ch. 63). Island communities also managed climate risk through maritime mobility: matrimonial networks across atolls allowed temporary resettlement when hazards threatened, enabled by the collective (rather than private) land tenure system (ch. 63).

## Conclusion and Global Relevance

Bambridge and Puas argue for reversing the framing of Pacific peoples as 'reluctant victims' to recognise them as 'proactive and successful climate mitigators.' Dame Meg Taylor, Secretary-General of the Pacific Islands Forum 2014–2021, was the world's first Ocean Commissioner. Healthy Pacific marine ecosystems are 'vital if the Pacific is to continue its vital global role as a carbon sink and major component of the planet's thermal regulation' (ch. 63). Pacific models of customary environmental management 'arose to manage one of the most resource-poor environments on Earth where colonists literally had to bring most of their food crops with them and practise sophisticated agriculture, making extensive use of irrigation, mulching, and complementary farming, as well as fish farming.' This inherited sophistication has direct relevance for Anthropocene climate adaptation globally.

## End Conditions

This process is **ongoing** and existentially urgent. The Anthropocene pace of change — ocean acidification, coral bleaching, sea level rise, intensified typhoons — may overwhelm traditional management systems that evolved to handle natural climate variability. Bambridge and Puas are cautiously optimistic: 'the ultimate deciding factor on the continuation of the practices outlined in this chapter may be their ability to ameliorate the consequences of the Anthropocene rather than their cultural value.' If traditional practices can demonstrate measurable ecological effectiveness (Teahupoo rāhui biomass recovery), they acquire instrumental as well as cultural legitimacy — the strongest basis for continuation (ch. 63).

## Historiography

Bambridge (French Polynesia) and Puas (FSM/Mortlocks) write from mixed insider/outsider positions: Puas is an Indigenous person of Lukunor Island in the Mortlocks; Bambridge is a French/Pacific scholar who has worked with the Teahupoo MPA since its inception. The chapter grounds quantitative claims (71% GDP from public transfers, 52 ppm ocean acidification overshoot) in cited data, while oral history, personal communication, and ethnographic observation supply the management detail. Analytical framework draws on Bourdieu's *habitus* concept and Hau'ofa's ocean-centred Oceanian identity theory. The chapter does not adjudicate between international conservation and Indigenous management as competing systems — it maps the tensions. Collection coverage on this topic: **moderate**. Strong on French Polynesia and FSM; thin on Papua New Guinea, Fiji, Vanuatu, and other island states facing similar challenges.
