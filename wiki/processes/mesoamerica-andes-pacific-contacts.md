## Definition and Scope

Mesoamerica–Andes Pacific contacts refers to the long-duration process of long-distance exchange and cultural transmission operating along the Pacific coast between western Mesoamerica (primarily Colima, Jalisco, Michoacan, Nayarit, Guerrero, and Oaxaca in Mexico) and northern South America (principally Ecuador and coastal Peru). The process spans approximately 1500 BCE to the Spanish conquest of Peru in 1532 CE, organised by Ballesteros Danel and Jaramillo Arango (ch. 27, [[jones-matsuda-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v1-2022]]) into two archaeologically distinct periods.

This is not a single event or migration but a "prolonged and constant flow of contact and trade prior to the Spanish conquest" (Danel and Arango). The core material evidence comprises: shared funerary traditions (shaft tombs), analogous ceramic types, comparable textile imagery, iridescent paint technology, the Spondylus shell trade, diving weights for shell collection, and the introduction of Ecuadorian metallurgical techniques and axe-money currency to Mesoamerica.

The process is explicitly distinguished from trans-Pacific contact with Polynesia or East Asia: all well-documented exchange is coastal, south-to-north and north-to-south along the Pacific littoral of the Americas.

## Causal Drivers

**Sailing technology.** The northern South American balsa-raft tradition provided the maritime infrastructure for long-distance Pacific coastal exchange. Rafts of *Ochroma* (balsa) logs with quadrangular sails and *guares* (centreboards) could reach "up to 25 metres in length and 7 metres in width" (Juan and Ulloa, 18th c., cited in Danel and Arango). Computer simulation (Callaghan) demonstrated that a voyage from Ecuador to Mesoamerica was feasible in as few as 97 days; engineering analysis (Dewan and Hosler) confirmed that pre-Hispanic raft technology was "wholly equipped to conduct return voyages between Ecuador and Mexico." A structural constraint operated: *Teredo navalis* wood-boring organisms gave rafts a commercial lifespan of approximately eight months in tropical waters, making fast return voyages and monetised exchange systems (axe-monies) economically necessary.

**Spondylus shell demand.** *Spondylus* (thorny oyster) was among the most highly valued ritual objects in both regions. "In Peru spondylus was used as a means of communication between the human and divine worlds, allowing the existence of both planes" (Danel and Arango). In Mesoamerica "practically all elites from this cultural area manufactured and displayed spondylus objects and ornaments as a sign of their wealth and power." Objects manufactured from Spondylus have been found at Teotihuacan, Tula, and as far as Oxkantah on the Caribbean coast. The bivalve's restricted Pacific coastal habitat (concentrated in Ecuador and Tumbes/northern Peru) made exchange structurally necessary for Mesoamerican demand.

**Ecuadorian metallurgical surplus and coastal specialisation.** Copper and arsenic bronze metallurgy developed earlier and more elaborately on the Ecuadorian coast than in Mesoamerica, creating a south-to-north technology gradient that drove transfer. Coastal societies in both regions "valued marine resources for trading and subsistence, hence choosing to settle in coastal environments" (Danel and Arango), creating the shared orientation toward maritime exchange.

## Major Phases

### First Period: Shared Material and Funerary Traditions (1500 BCE–200 CE)

A geographically distributed set of shared features appears across western Mesoamerica and the Andean Pacific coast during this long period. The mechanisms of transmission are less clear than for the second period, and some evidence is found inland, suggesting that maritime trade influenced societies beyond direct coastal contact.

**Shaft tombs.** A funerary tradition of burying bodies in chambers accessed via a vertical shaft appears in western Mexico (second century BCE–third century CE) and in Colombia, Venezuela, Ecuador, Peru, and Panama (300 BCE to the Inca period). Mexican examples occur in Colima, Jalisco, and Nayarit. "Some authors see a similarity in the morphology of the tombs as potentially revealing an ideology shared by their respective societies regarding death" (Oliveros Morales, Gárate, cited in Danel and Arango). The temporal overlap is substantial.

**Hairless dogs.** Dogs with the genetic trait for hairlessness (which also produces simplified dentition) appear in both Mesoamerica and Andean countries. The Old Temple of Chavín de Huantar (Peru) yields hairless dog bones dated 1500–500 BCE; Mesoamerican examples appear in Guadalupe, Michoacan from the sixth–tenth centuries CE; figurines of hairless dogs dated from 50 BCE appear in both western Mesoamerica and Ecuador and Peru. The genetic marker makes independent invention an unlikely explanation.

**Capacha pottery and the stirrup motif.** Kelly documented systematic stylistic and technological similarities between Capacha pottery from Colima (1870–1200 BCE) and Machalilla pottery from the Ecuadorian coast (1500–100 BCE), including shared cranial deformation practices. The stirrup-handle motif found in Tlatilco (1500–500 BCE) and Chupicuaro (600–100 BCE) through the Purepecha period is "a defining motif of Andean pottery in all the periods of its development" (Danel and Arango).

**Textile representations.** Figurines from Ixtlan del Rio, Mexico (400 BCE–400 CE) and from the Guangala period in Ecuador (200 BCE–800 CE) display comparable complex geometric textile designs: "the designs appearing in these figurines in Mexico and Ecuador are fairly complex and correspond in the forms of use, colour combinations, and detail layout" (Danel and Arango), arguing against independent invention.

**Iridescent paint.** A technically complex paint producing "bright gray and pink tones" when exposed to sunlight appears in Ecuador (Chorrera period, 1200 BCE–500 CE), in Guatemala (Ocos period, 1500–800 BCE), and in Nayarit, Mexico (200–700 CE). Due to "complex mixtures of various chemical components" required for manufacture, Coe argued it represents a sole technique shared between two distant regions rather than independent invention.

### Second Period: Spondylus Diving, Metallurgy, and Axe-Money Exchange (600–1532 CE)

The second period is more precisely documented than the first. Its core elements are shared diving technology, the transfer of Ecuadorian metallurgy northward, and a standardised exchange currency.

**Diving weights for Spondylus.** Stone artefacts shaped like torpedoes with cavities for rope attachment, interpreted as diving weights for underwater Spondylus collection, appear in Punta Mita (Nayarit, Mexico), dated 900–1521 CE, and on the Ecuadorian coast from the Machalilla period (1500–100 BCE) through the Manteño period (650–1532 CE). "The appearance of these stone objects shaped like torpedoes shows the expansion of a diving technique, with a century of development in and around the Ecuadorian coast, before reaching western Mesoamerica during the Postclassic period" (Danel and Arango).

**Metallurgical transfer.** Hosler's landmark study documented two sub-periods of Ecuadorian metallurgical influence on western Mesoamerica: (1) 600/800–1200/1300 CE, during which basic metal objects and techniques reached and were adopted in western Mexico; (2) 1200/1300 CE until European arrival, during which more complex alloys (copper-arsenic) arrived, "triggering the independent refinement of Mesoamerican metallurgy, with innovative uses, motifs, and designs being developed." South American tweezers (hair-removal instruments) were imported to Michoacan and repurposed as Purepecha authority pendants — illustrating how transferred objects were adapted to new cultural contexts. Mesoamerican metallurgy ultimately "reached a high level of specialization with a high degree of detail, dominating complex techniques such as wax casting and cold-hammering" (Danel and Arango).

**Axe-monies.** Copper axe-shaped objects found in standardised sizes (miniature, small, regular, large), grouped in packs of five, ten, fifteen, or twenty units, appear on both the Ecuadorian coast/northern Peru and in Oaxaca, Guerrero, and western Mexico. Sixteenth-century ethnohistoric documents from both Ecuador and Oaxaca "portray the populations native to these regions using 'axe-monies' in transactions at markets and as payment of tribute and to extinguish debt" (Danel and Arango). An exchange rate between axe-monies and Spanish reals is attested. Critically, axe-monies in both regions are made of arsenic bronze with less than 10 per cent arsenic — a composition traceable to South American enargite mines — requiring deliberate alloy replication in Mesoamerican contexts where naturally occurring enargite was not exploited. The standardised currency enabled fast exchanges during time-constrained voyages and the creation of inter-expedition debts, solving the structural problem posed by the eight-month raft lifespan.

## Geographic Spread

The southern pole of contact is the Ecuadorian coast and adjacent northern Peru (Tumbes, Lambayeque). The northern pole is western Mesoamerica: Colima, Jalisco, Michoacan, Nayarit, Guerrero, and Oaxaca. Secondary nodes include the Guatemalan Pacific coast (iridescent paint) and Colombian/Panamanian coast (intermediate contacts). The evidence is predominantly coastal and maritime in the second period; in the first period, inland distribution in Mexico and in Andean highlands suggests that maritime-mediated influences diffused beyond coastal communities.

The chapter explicitly limits its well-documented evidence to these regions: "the evidence of these pre-Hispanic trading encounters, though, is limited to northern South America and parts of western Mesoamerica" (Danel and Arango). Contacts with the Caribbean, Atlantic, or trans-Pacific (to Polynesia) are not established by the archaeological evidence reviewed here.

## End Conditions

The Spanish conquest of Peru in 1532 CE terminated the sailing tradition that had sustained long-distance Pacific exchange. Pizarro's navigator Bartolomé Ruiz encountered a large Ecuadorian trading raft in 1526, carrying cargo of approximately 40 tons with triangular cotton sails — evidence that the tradition remained vigorous on the eve of conquest. The nautical tradition itself "did not disappear with the conquest": "it has been actively used until today in towns from Ecuador and northern Peru" (Danel and Arango), preserved in colonial and republican-era descriptions and in the annual Balsa Manteña festival at Salango since 1992.

The exchange network's end as a mechanism of inter-civilisational contact, however, is definitive: Spanish colonial trade systems replaced pre-Columbian coastal exchange routes, and the axe-money system was supplanted by Spanish currency.

## Oral Traditions and Documentary Evidence

Sixteenth-century Spanish chronicles preserve oral traditions corroborating the archaeological evidence. A 1525 letter from the royal accountant Albornoz to Charles V records Zacatula coast natives reporting that "from time to time Indians from certain islands toward the south, which they point to, would come to this coast in large canoes, and they brought there exquisite things which they would trade for local products." Acosta concluded: "So as there wants no witnesses to prove that they sailed in the South Sea before the Spaniards came thither." Three Spanish chronicles (Sarmiento de Gamboa, 1572; Cabello de Balboa, 1576–86; Murúa, 1590) describe the Inca ruler Túpac Yupanqui sailing to two Pacific islands (Ninachumbi and Auachumbi), and Sarmiento records merchants arriving at Tumbez "navigating in balsas with sails" from these lands. The Mixe oral tradition from the Isthmus of Tehuantepec places their origin in Huanchaco, Peru, with migration to Oaxaca beginning in 1294 and continuing as a "constant migration flow for trading purposes" until the sixteenth century (Sánchez Castro, cited in Danel and Arango).

The chapter's authors note that these chronicles blend factual observation, oral tradition, colonial interests, and supernatural elements (including "giants" arriving from the sea), requiring source-critical reading. Nevertheless, "the archaeological evidence for these trading contacts not only matches the hints of long-distance voyages described in a number of sixteenth-century chronicles; it also demonstrates the Amerindian sailing capacity of the populations of northern South America and Mesoamerica" (Danel and Arango).

## Historiography

**Scholarly trajectory.** Long-distance pre-Columbian Pacific coastal exchange was historically sceptical terrain. The chapter positions itself within a growing academic consensus: contacts "are increasingly believed in academia to have taken place during pre-Hispanic times." The key modern treatment of metallurgical transfer is Hosler's *The Sounds and Colors of Power* (1994), which remains the foundation for the second-period evidence. Hosler, Lechtman, and Holm's *Axe-monies and Their Relatives* (1990) established the currency framework. Geurds (2017) explicitly frames the 600–1200 CE intensification as a "globalization" phenomenon.

**Debates.** The chapter distinguishes carefully between the two periods: the first (1500 BCE–200 CE) has less precise evidence of mechanism and route; the second (600–1532 CE) has more direct documentation via metallurgical diffusion, diving-weight distribution, and axe-money standardisation. The question of whether first-period shared traits represent direct maritime contact, overland transmission, independent invention, or some combination remains open. The authors argue that the complexity of iridescent paint, the genetic specificity of hairless dogs, and the ceramic stylistic parallels make independent invention unlikely but acknowledge the evidence is not conclusive.

**Experimental archaeology.** Heyerdahl's 1947 Kon-Tiki expedition (Callao to Raroia, French Polynesia) and subsequent replications (Seven Little Sisters, Las Balsas, Tangaroa, Kon-Tiki II) demonstrated long-distance balsa-raft feasibility. Haslett's Salango-to-Acapulco attempt (not completed but technically instructive) and Callaghan's computer simulation specifically modelled Ecuador–Mexico coastal voyages.

**Collection coverage.** [[jones-matsuda-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v1-2022]] ch. 27 provides the most detailed recent treatment of the archaeological and documentary evidence for this exchange process. The chapter is explicitly focused on the Pacific-coast corridor; Andean highland and Mesoamerican interior contexts are referenced only as destinations for traded objects. A dedicated Andean prehistory source (e.g. a Cambridge History of the Andean World volume) would substantially enrich coverage of the Spondylus economy and Chimú/Inca maritime contexts in the wiki.
