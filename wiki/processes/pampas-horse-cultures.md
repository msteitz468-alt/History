## Definition and Scope

The "Pampas horse cultures" process describes the transformation of indigenous societies of the Argentine pampas, Patagonia, and the eastern slopes of the Andes between roughly 1580 and 1879 by the adoption of the horse and the exploitation of vast feral livestock herds (cimarrones). It encompasses the Pampas peoples (speakers of Gennaken, variously called Querandie, Puelche, Serrano), the Tehuelche of Patagonia, and the [[mapuche]]/Araucanians who crossed the Andes in increasing numbers from the seventeenth century onward.

The process was not simply ecological adaptation but a profound social and political reorganization: "by the middle of the eighteenth century, the shape of intercultural relations in the Southern Cone had been radically transformed" as cimarrones attracted "Araucanian hunting and raiding parties and Chilean conchavadores east from the cordillera, salt caravans, gauchos, and pulperos south from the littoral, and Tehuelche trading delegations north from Patagonia" (*CHNPA* Vol. III, Part 2, Ch. 16, Jones).

## Causal Drivers

**Ecological windfall.** Horses and cattle left behind by early Spanish expeditions on the Parana River found a perfect ecological niche in the pampas grasslands. Within decades, herds numbered in the thousands. By the early seventeenth century, feral horses were available to native groups even before direct encounters with Spaniards.

**Demographic collapse on the littoral.** The Querandie, who had initially resisted Spanish settlement at Buenos Aires (1536) and maintained advantageous intermediary positions in north-south trade along the Parana, were nearly extinguished by epidemics before Juan de Garay's 1580 refoundation of Buenos Aires. Their near-disappearance opened the pampas grasslands to southerly Pampas and Tehuelche bands.

**Araucanian expansion.** [[araucania-frontier-1573-1882]] stabilized the Chilean (western) frontier through parlamentos, freeing Araucanian warrior parties to project eastward. "The largesse of the cimarrones (herds of wild cattle and horses) and greater opportunities for intertribal trade attracted more and more Araucanian hunting parties into the grasslands east of the cordillera" (Jones). By the eighteenth century Mapuche settlements in the eastern pampas became permanent.

**Trade-addiction cycle.** Colonial pulperos (itinerant traders) and conchavadores (Chilean traders) channeled aguardiente, tobacco, yerba mate, and sugar into the pampas in exchange for hides, ponchos, rhea feathers, and salt. Over time indigenous bands grew dependent on these stimulants, which became instruments of political control.

## Major Phases

**Phase 1 — Horse adoption and hunting florescence (c. 1580–1680).** Pampas groups north of the Salado River adopted horses for hunting and trading within decades of encountering feral herds. The Tehuelche of Patagonia, constrained by water scarcity, relied on trade rather than full equestrian adaptation. Occasional hostile and peaceful exchanges with Buenos Aires settlers established a pattern of truce trade.

**Phase 2 — The malón economy (c. 1680–1780).** Araucanian hunting and war parties (maloqueros, or aucas) pushed deep into Pampas territory. The cimarrones attracted all parties — Araucanian, Pampas, Tehuelche, gaucho, Creole — into a common competitive arena. Raids against Creole frontier settlements, called malocas in Chile and malones in Argentina, became regularized as an economy: raids supplied livestock for pampas consumption and for export west to Chile. The 1740 raid by Pampas chief Cangapol (Bravo) against the partido of Magdalena killed over 200 colonists, took captives, and carried off over 20,000 livestock in a single action — a demonstration of the scale these operations reached.

"This 'Araucanization of the desert' included the expansion of Mapundungun — the common language of Araucania — as a kind of lingua franca adopted by the Pampas and even Tehuelche groups for trading purposes" (Jones). Araucanian bilateral preferential-cousin marriage customs merged with Pampas bilateral kinship systems, creating an incipient intertribal patrilineal network that raised the political complexity of Pampas and Tehuelche societies.

**Phase 3 — Cimarron exhaustion and market integration (c. 1780–1820).** By mid-century, intensive hunting by all parties had devastated the feral herds. Colonial authorities imposed licensing for the hunt; surviving livestock was branded as property. As free hunting became impossible, both gauchos and indigenous bands were pushed toward dependency on Creole market circuits — gauchos as coerced ranch hands, Indian bands through the "peaceful trade" allotment system. Pulperías (frontier trading posts) grew sixfold between 1781 and 1816. The transition from subsistence hunting to market-driven livestock raiding was complete.

**Phase 4 — Confederation and final wars (c. 1820–1879).** Following Argentine independence, the intertribal alliance system built around Rosas's treaty network partially collapsed. Araucanian diaspora families — including the Vorogano followers of [[calfucura]], who settled the Salinas Grandes and established dynastic control of salt sources — created permanent pampas confederations. The malón became a political weapon as well as an economic one. The 1875 offensive by a confederation under Namuncura raided five Buenos Aires partidos, killing over 300, taking 500 captive, and carrying off over 300,000 head of livestock. Argentine General Roca's 1878–1879 campaign ended the horse cultures militarily.

## Geographic Spread

- **Northern pampas (Rio de la Plata delta and Salado zone)**: earliest horse adoption; Querandie territory, then Pampas bands after collapse
- **Central pampas (Tandil–Salinas Grandes–Rio Quinto)**: core arena of the malón economy and intertribal fairs; Ranquel and Calfucura confederations
- **Andean piedmont (Mendoza–Pehuenche territory)**: trans-Andean corridor for horses, cattle, and textiles
- **Patagonia (south of Rio Negro)**: Tehuelche territory; partial horse adoption only in the north

## Interaction

The process was closely entangled with [[araucania-frontier-1573-1882]]: horse cultures on the eastern slopes were both driven by and sustained the Araucanian resistance on the west. The two were different faces of the same political economy.

The process was concurrent_with and interacted with [[encomienda]] and [[reduccion]] dynamics: those indigenous bands that accepted treaty allotments and entered the Buenos Aires allotment system were gradually converted from raiders into pastoral laborers; those remaining outside the system sustained the horse culture into the 1870s.

The sexual division of labor within horse-culture societies was profoundly shaped by the economy: men led raids and herded; captive Creole women and Mapuche wives produced ponchos for trade, cured charqui, and cared for livestock. As the early nineteenth-century English observer Maria Calcott Graham noted, "their flocks are entirely managed by their women and slaves [Christian women], who watch alternately during the night, mounted on horseback" (quoted in Jones).

The process is analogous_to the Plains Indian horse cultures of North America, as Jones explicitly compares (referencing the Comanche economy and the role of alcohol dependency).

## End Conditions

Three factors ended the pampas horse cultures: (1) military defeat — the Argentine national army with Remington rifles and telegraph communications was qualitatively superior to mounted raiders; (2) ecological closure — by the 1870s the open cattle economy of the pampas had been converted into a system of titled landholdings controlled by the Sociedad Rural Argentina, which excluded Indian and gaucho traders alike; (3) political atomization — the allotment system, paradoxically, had simultaneously strengthened confederation leadership (Calfucura, Namuncura) and created dependency on state goods ("vices": aguardiente, tobacco, yerba mate) that sapped long-term capacity for independent action.

Surviving members of pampas horse-culture societies were settled as paisanos (rural laborers) on the sheep and cattle estancias that proliferated after the Conquista del Desierto.

## Historiography

Argentine national historiography largely erased the indigenous peoples of the pampas, treating Argentina as a country "without an Indian presence." Revisionist ethnohistory — led by Leonardo León Solis, Raul Mandrini, Miguel Angel Palermo, and Kristine Jones — has reconstructed the horse-culture economy from archival records, travel accounts, and military documents. Rodolfo Casamiquela's linguistic and archaeological synthesis (*Un nuevo panorama etnológico*, 1969) remains foundational for understanding pre-contact and early colonial Pampas identity.
