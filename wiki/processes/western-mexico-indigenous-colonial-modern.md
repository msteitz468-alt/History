# Western Mexico Indigenous History (Colonial–Modern)

## Definition and Scope

Regional indigenous history of the Mexican **Center-West** from the Spanish invasion to the late twentieth century, as synthesized by **Eric Van Young** in [[cambridge-history-native-peoples-americas-v2-2|CHNPA II.2]] Ch. 15. Part of [[colonial-mesoamerica-native-peoples]].

Van Young locates the Center-West as a roughly pie-shaped macroregion with apex in the eastern Bajío and outer arc along the Pacific from near Mazatlán to the Balsas mouth (Michoacán–Guerrero abuttal): all of modern Jalisco, Michoacán, Colima, Nayarit, Aguascalientes, and parts of Zacatecas and Guanajuato — about **220,000 km²**, over a tenth of national territory. Alpine ranges, lacustrine basins, arid canyons, and Pacific fringes still structure movement and farming; densest aboriginal settlement and post-contact political/economic gravity lay in well-watered ancient lake basins (Bajío, Morelia, Guadalajara/Chapala, Ameca, Sayula, Tepic, Autlán, Colima, Lake Pátzcuaro). All three Mesoamerican climate zones (*fría* / *templada* / *caliente*) apply.

Indigenous cultural heritage remains strongest where precontact density and state polities were high (Michoacán highlands) or isolation preserved culture (Nayarit mountains); thinly settled or quickly depopulated zones (Pacific littoral, Altos de Jalisco) more closely resemble Crosby-style “neo-Europes.”

**Interpretive frame (Van Young):** Mexican national mythology casts the Center-West as homeland of the sturdy mestizo **ranchero**, apotheosized in the **Cristero War** (1926–29). That obscures large-scale landholdings dominating much colonial and postcolonial countryside and endemic tension with **identifiably indigenous communities** — a bi-modal villager/estate distribution. Proliferation of ranchos (except Altos de Jalisco, already by ~1800) was largely mid- to late-nineteenth-century; agrarian transitions centrally involved indigenous groups until comparatively recent times. Deep mestizaje after the colonial close intertwines “native” history with non-natives, making “Indian” vs “peasant” analytically difficult. Categories “native/indigenous/Indian” are themselves colonial artifacts still active in public discourse.

## Causal Drivers

- **Military and administrative conquest** — early probes (Cristóbal de Olid 1522; Francisco Cortés coastal 1524–25) then the destructive energy of **Nuño Beltrán de Guzmán**, who carved **Nueva Galicia** over ~6–7 years, founded cities (Culiacán, Guadalajara), killed or enslaved thousands, and in February **1530** tried and brutally executed the Tarascan *cazonci* Tzintzicha Tangaxoan (*preceded_by* [[spanish-conquest-of-mexico-1519]]; *contributed_to* lasting native ill will).
- **[[virgin-soil-epidemics|Disease]]** — bacterial phalanx effects **paled Spanish military technology**; overall mortality analogous to the Basin of Mexico: drop of **~90% or more** contact–~1650. Pátzcuaro Basin ~100,000–200,000 (1520) → **<10,000** natives by 1650; Peter Gerhard: Nueva Galicia ~**850,000 → ~70,000**. Coastal lowlands near-total depopulation within few years.
- **Land-labor extractive institutions** — [[encomienda]], chattel slavery (early Pacific lowland mining), shift to repartimiento and free/debt wage labor, and above all the mixed livestock-farming **[[hacienda]]** spreading onto lands where indigenous population retreated or died (*produced* bi-modal agrarian structure).
- **Nucleation and frontier administration** — [[congregacion|congregación]] (and related [[reduccion|reducción]] practices) to nucleate villages; military frontier province of **Colotlán** after ~1590; late pacification of mountainous Nayarit (Cora chieftain meets viceroy 1721; expeditionary subjugation **1722**).
- **[[spiritual-conquest-and-colonial-church|Spiritual conquest]]** — overwhelmingly **Franciscan** field in Michoacán and Nueva Galicia (millenarian/apocalyptic stamp on post-conquest native Christianity, cryptopaganism, and resistance ideology); hospital-village regime (Santa Fé de la Laguna, 1533, Vasco de Quiroga, More’s *Utopia*); later Jesuit concentration of Coras.
- **Late-colonial demographic recovery and commodification** — recovery to ~30–40% of precontact levels region-wide by late colonial; European legal fictions commodifying land and labor; intra-community differentiation (kulak-like village notables); inter-pueblo land litigation flooding audiencias.
- **Independence and liberal citizenship projects** — Hidalgo insurgency 1810–21 (~50–60% of ordinary insurgents native villagers); post-independence dismantling of community land ownership; **Ley Lerdo 1856** and state repartitional laws; midcentury risings under **“Religión y Tierras!”**.
- **Twentieth-century state and market** — Revolution and Cristero violence; official [[indigenismo]], socialist education, anticlericalism; Cárdenas land/ejido and infrastructure; railroads, highways, tourism, out-migration.

## Major Phases

### 1. Contact ethnography and conquest (to ~1540s)

At European contact the Center-West held: (1) one great complex state-level society, the **[[purepecha|Tarascans / Purépecha]]**; (2) smaller city-state polities of the core tradition (e.g. upper Chapala Basin — “Chimalhuacán Confederation” overstates unity); (3) hunter-gatherers and semi-sedentary farmers mainly north/northeast, labeled pejoratively **Chichimecas**. A cultural transition zone roughly SE–NW bisected the macroregion. Tarascan and Mexica ([[aztec-empire|Aztec]]) states abutted tensely along a militarized SE frontier. Post-conquest Nahuatl lingua franca + missionary activity extinguished many local languages.

Olid met no Tarascan resistance at Taximaroa and reached [[tzintzuntzan|Tzintzuntzan]] unopposed (1522). Guzmán’s satrapy and the *cazonci*’s judicial murder (1530) stamped the region more than many Mesoamerican zones. Mid-1540s silver (major **Zacatecas**) drew settlers who subjugated polities and compressed native economies.

### 2. Demographic collapse, congregación, encomienda, and Mixtón (1540s–c. 1600)

Epidemic collapse restructured settlement; into the vacuum came Otomí, Tarascan, Tecuexe and other indigenous colonists, black slaves, free mixed-blood. Congregación nucleated survivors with uneven results (Cora/Huichol mission villages throve under discipline then reverted to dispersed settlement). Encomienda longer-lived and more economically important in the **Tarascan zone** than in wilder Nueva Galicia (low density, flight/evasion; >half communities escheated to Crown before 1600).

The **[[mixton-war-1540|Mixtón War (1540–41)]]** — Caxcan (Cazcán) uprising that threatened for a time to extinguish Spanish influence in western Mexico — was the most spectacular large-scale resistance episode of the first colonial generation (*caused_by* encomendero/missionary pressure, Guzmán bitterness, and Coronado expedition draw-down of Nueva Galicia forces; millenarian ideology: war of extermination, rejection of Christianity, return of old gods). Related risings and the larger **Chichimeca War** racked the near north until nearly 1600.

Franciscans founded houses across Michoacán (1525–31 “favorite land of the early apostolate”) and Nueva Galicia; secularization followed bishoprics Michoacán (1536) and Nueva Galicia (1548). Indigenous responses ranged from foot-dragging and clandestine ritual through litigation to riot/rebellion; *Relación de Michoacán* records conversion-pressure rumors (Franciscans as dead men in shrouds killing infants in baptism); late-century *pastorelas* could encode counternarratives.

### 3. Seventeenth–early eighteenth-century consolidation

Structures of domination/accommodation set before 1600 consolidated. Mixed livestock-farming [[hacienda|haciendas]] spread; after Chichimeca War close (~1600) Spanish occupation advanced toward Zacatecas; coastal cattle estancias worked by some Indians and black slaves. Smaller compact estates (wheat, sugar, European crops) jostled denser Indian villages recovering demographically around Guadalajara and Valladolid. Fringe indigenous population/culture virtually disappeared or submerged by late colonial period. Christianity took firm root → conservative popular Catholicism, cult of saints, cofradías. Locus of specifically Indian **rebellion shifted north** with the military/mining/land frontier (exception: late-pacified Nayarit sierra, 1721–22).

### 4. Late-colonial agrarian structure and the cusp of modernity (~1750–1810)

Indigenous landholding villages had diverse origins: precolonial continuity; Spanish congregación of fragments; late-colonial demographic cloning. Beyond *fundos legales*, many held large common farming/grazing/reserve lands worked by families as **tierras de reparto**. Despite cargo-system equilibrating mechanisms, considerable **intra-community social differentiation** — kulak-like village notables dominating political/economic life (Tlajomulco ~1800: ~5% of adult land/livestock owners held highly skewed wealth; similar concentration in Tarascan zone). Vast majority of formal land litigation was **inter-pueblo**, not internal (Van Young: litigiousness partly elite deflection of internal differentiation strains). Mid-eighteenth demographic pressure made once-adequate holdings inadequate; elites often rented communal property to non-Indians for appropriated cash.

Rural collective resistance rooted not only in material agrarian pressure but in a still vital if uneven **substrate of indigenous ethnicity**. In predominantly indigenous pueblos, personal identity, group culture, and livelihood conflated → Indian/non-Indian resource conflict took character of **class and ethnic/“racial” struggle** (“caste war”). Political legitimacy anchored in sacral realm → cultural conflict and popular protest deployed **religious discourse** from the colonial period through the Cristero rebellion.

**El Indio Mariano** (Tepic area, 1800–1802): abortive uprising around an almost certainly apocryphal messianic figure (“el de la máscara de oro”); program of community land restoration and abatement/elimination of royal tribute; chiliastic crowning with thorny crown of Jesus the Nazarene; alleged reach to Huicholes, Tepehuanes, even Yaquis; failed for lack of coordination and vigorous viceregal reaction — ideological kinship with José Bernardo Herrada messianism in Durango, foreshadowing popular indigenous ideology in the 1810s.

### 5. Independence wars (1810–1821)

Center-West was fertile ground for Hidalgo’s anti-Spanish insurgency begun Bajío September **1810** — abortive peasant insurrection with racial overtones within national liberation war. Many Indian communities split; active/passive indigenous support deep especially central Nueva Galicia, adjacent Michoacán, parts of highland Nayarit. Despite conventional wisdom of Creole/mestizo leadership and rank-and-file, **~50–60% of ordinary insurgents were native villagers**. Indian peasants sought to expand farming onto never-legally-claimed lands *and* fought to preserve communal cultural way of life. Endemic focus: Lake Chapala region and Nueva Galicia capital; Nayarit sierra (Cora/Huichol); Michoacán coastal hot country (mulato Gordiano Guzmán; native cabecilla “El Indio Candelario”).

**Hidalgo decree 5 Dec 1810** (rebel government Guadalajara): renters of Indian pueblo lands to pay back rents into “national treasury”; communities to reassume and work lands only themselves — largely rhetorical, but foreshadowed post-independence parcelization/privatization and **Ley Lerdo 1856**. *contributed_to* [[spanish-american-independence]] from below; *preceded_by* El Indio Mariano ideology.

### 6. Liberal reform, desamortización, Lozada (1820s–c. 1880)

Early project: Guadalajara provincial government **1822** to divide Indian corporate holdings in name of development/individualism. Official language shifted to “those previously referred to as Indians” / “extinguished indigenous communities” with theoretical equal citizenship. Most important identity-eroding reforms: dismantling **community land ownership** for fee-simple household redistribution. State laws (Michoacán 1827–28, Zacatecas 1829, Guanajuato 1829) repeatedly amplified; **Ley Lerdo 1856** confirmed and helped plunge the nation into Reform War and French intervention (1857–67). Conflation of attacks on indigenous community lands with Church lands → midcentury rural uprisings under **“Religión y Tierras!”**. Effects generally negative for cohesion/identity; many communities effectively extinguished; land concentrated in non-Indian hands; partitioned holdings became minifundia.

Center-West had disproportionate peasant revolt **1855–~1880**. Lake Chapala 1855–57: ≥2,000 armed Indians (La Barca, Zacoalco) sought lands lost to haciendas. Tarascan-area unrest 1869–70, 1871, 1878 (Valle de Taretán “great agricultural community,” crushed early 1879). **Manuel Lozada**, “Tigre de Alica”: mestizo peon-turned-agrarian guerrilla with largely Indian (Cora and Huichol) supporters; high-water **1868–73** nearly all Nayarit and adjacent sierra/plateau; advanced on Guadalajara with army reputed **~11,000**; captured, tried, executed **July 1873**. Díaz regime later combined military action with deportation of dissidents to Yucatán henequen plantations.

### 7. Modern Huichol, Cora, and Purépecha trajectories (Porfiriato–late 20th c.)

By mid-twentieth century only the largest groups repay focused attention. **Huichol and Cora** show more marked ethnic survival (language, folkways, art) than many western Nahuas or Tarascans who became *indios civilizados* blending into mestizo hacienda labor. Porfirian *terrenos baldíos* surveying (1883, 1894) wrested huge tracts; by ~1925 foreigners owned **>50%** of Nayarit rural area. Revolution: sierra adherence to Villista Rafael Buelna (Huicholes) / Carranza (many Coras); Nayarit ejido carving pre-Cárdenas → by ~1970 nearly **80%** rural land collective. **Cristero War**: about **two-thirds of Huichol** indigenous people active cristeros. 1990 census: Huichol speakers something under **20,000**.

**[[purepecha|Tarascans / Purépecha]]** (see actor page for full colonial–modern arc): by mid-nineteenth century speech limited to NW Michoacán heartland (~20% of state / ~125,000); [[tzintzuntzan|Tzintzuntzan]] ~50% Tarascan speakers 1850 → ~**10%** a century later. Revolution + Cristero “catastrophic”; Tarascan peasant Cristero support **virtually universal**. **Lázaro Cárdenas** (governor then president): ~150,000 ha as governor; schools, infrastructure, forestry coops; socialist education met hostile response. Mid-1980s Tarascan-speech population of Michoacán as high as **~90,000**, with relative weight declining under mestizaje.

## Geographic Spread

- **Core sedentary/state zone:** Lake Pátzcuaro / Michoacán highlands ([[purepecha]], [[tzintzuntzan]]); Guadalajara–Chapala basins; denser plateau north of Lake Chapala.
- **Pacific littoral and lowlands:** early near-total depopulation; cattle estancias, African and mixed-blood auxiliaries.
- **Northern fringe / Chichimeca and sierra:** Colotlán frontier; eastern Nayarit sierra; Cora/Huichol/Tepehuan/Tepecano zones; Aguascalientes/Altos Guachichil hunter-gatherers displaced by cattle estates.
- **Bajío and silver corridor:** presidios/garrison towns (San Miguel el Grande 1555, Lagos 1563, Jérez 1570, Celaya 1575, Aguascalientes 1576); Christianized Valley of Mexico and Tlaxcala colonists.

## End Conditions

Ongoing. Van Young’s 1990s assessment: Center-West barely figures in national indigenous discourse (~90% of Mexico’s then ~5.3 million indigenous-language speakers live Center-South), yet a discernible tradition endures, stewarded chiefly by **Tarascan, Huichol, and Cora**. His arc is **failed redemptions imposed by others** — colonial violence and disease; demographic recovery with miscegenation; evangelization and land greed; liberal “citizenship”/yeoman projects that exposed communities to markets; twentieth-century [[indigenismo]], socialist education, anticlerical enlightenment, developmental modernization; internal war and thickening capitalism wearing peoples “to a nubbin.” Yet some endure with demographic vitality, institutional/economic adaptivity, and cultural self-consciousness. Culture is “group process and daily negotiation,” not a fixed consensus preserved “like a fly in amber.” Arms and millenarian revival now unlikely; long-term forces of dissolution (numbers, state power, markets, commodification, technology) dominate without canceling survival.

## Historiography

Van Young (CHNPA II.2 bibliographical essay) judges indigenous Center-West past better served by **anthropologists and archaeologists** than historians and ethnohistorians; Tarascans, Huicholes, and Coras best covered; postcolonial ethnohistory often embedded in ethnography, demography, or politics, or inferred from silences. Structural anchors he privileges: **Peter Gerhard** historical geography; Berkeley demographers (Sauer, Borah & Cook); **J. Benedict Warren** *Conquest of Michoacán* (definitive Tarascan conquest); Philip Powell on Chichimeca/north frontier; Robert Ricard *Spiritual Conquest*; large Quiroga literature; López Portillo y Weber / Ahumada on Mixtón; Castro Gutiérrez / Archer / López on Indio Mariano; **Jean Meyer** on Lozada and Cristero; Helen Pollard (and Gorenstein & Pollard) on prehispanic Tarascan state; Phil Weigand on Huichol/West Mexico; HMAI Beals (Tarascans) and Grimes & Hinton (Huichol/Cora). **Colonial rebellions “still hardly studied.”** Indigenous role in Independence generally not singled out, still less for Center-West.
