# Australia's Engagement with the Asia-Pacific Region

## Definition and Scope

The long and contested process by which Australia moved from seeing Asia primarily as a threat to be excluded or contained, to seeing it as the fundamental context of Australian economic life, security, and identity. The transformation was neither linear nor complete. As Tomoko Akami and Anthony Milner argue in [[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]], "such is the dominant narrative of Australia's regional relations in the twentieth century" — a story of persistent anxiety gradually overcome.

The process spans three recognisable periods of heightened regional consciousness: the mid-1930s (world economic crisis and Japanese expansion); the years immediately after World War II; and the late 1980s when "the scale of Northeast Asia's economic potential became apparent." For most of the century, however, "Australians who were concerned about the wider world concentrated on their imperial relationships."

## Causal Drivers

**Geography**: Australia's location on the edge of Asia's most populous region made engagement ultimately unavoidable, even when ideologically resisted.

**The white Australia policy**: The 1901 Immigration Restriction Act and Pacific Island Labourers Act — "commonly referred to as the white Australia policy" — actively excluded Asian peoples and defined the region as a source of threat. This policy "characterised Australia in the rest of the region even after it had been overturned in the 1960s and 1970s."

**Britain's strategic failure**: The fall of Singapore (1942) shattered confidence in British imperial protection and forced a direct reckoning with Australia's neighbourhood. Yet the reorientation was slow: the British imperial framework was replaced not by Asian engagement but by American alliance.

**Japan's economic transformation**: Japan became Australia's largest export market in 1967, overtaking Britain. "By the early 1970s Japan had emerged as Australia's most important trading partner." This economic fact preceded political engagement.

**Vietnam and its aftermath**: The war's end (1972–75) and the influx of 130,000 Indochinese refugees confronted Australians with Asia as a human reality rather than an abstraction.

**Northeast Asian boom (1980s–90s)**: China, South Korea, Hong Kong, and Taiwan's rapid economic growth created the "Asia-Pacific century" narrative that Hawke and Keating translated into policy.

## Major Phases

### Phase 1: Exclusion and Anxious Imperialism (1901–1941)
Australia's approach was defined by racial exclusion combined with a paradoxical regional ambition: to exercise imperial influence over the Pacific Islands while excluding Asian peoples from Australia proper.

Alfred Deakin was pivotal: "a regionalist in the sense that his policies were strongly influenced by Australia's geographical location" but "his nationalism was 'conceptualised and structured...in racialised terms', with citizens' rights extending only to the British race." He pursued an "Australasian Monroe Doctrine" for the South-West Pacific, sought American counter-weight to Japan (the Great White Fleet visit, 1908), and established the Royal Australian Navy (1911).

PM Hughes at the 1919 Paris Peace Conference opposed a racial equality clause in the League of Nations Covenant (while the US secretly shared his opposition) and secured Australian mandate over former German Pacific territories south of the equator.

Knowledge of the region was thin. Richard Casey observed in 1928 that information about Asia was "vague, muddled and defective." Australia had no diplomatic missions in Asia until 1940. Intelligence about the region was filtered through London.

Trade with Asia did grow: exports to Asia rose from 5.6% of total Australian exports in 1913 to 9.2% in 1918–19; imports from 3.7% to 12.9%. New South Wales sent a trade commissioner to Kobe in 1903; other states sent agents to Shanghai, Hong Kong, Manila, Singapore, and Java. But business commitment was shallow and government appointments were "soon abandoned."

In the 1920s, the League of Nations and US presence created impetus for a "more independent Australian contribution to regional affairs." John Latham's diplomacy and the Institute of Pacific Relations represented early attempts at multilateral Pacific engagement.

### Phase 2: War, Alliance, and Anxious Anti-Communism (1941–1972)
World War II's Pacific theatre forced immediate, violent engagement with the region. The Kokoda campaign (1942) in Papua New Guinea entered the national mythology alongside Gallipoli. But the strategic response was to substitute American alliance for British protection, not genuine Asian engagement.

Key post-war dynamics:
- **Indonesia's independence (1945–49)**: Australia initially supported Indonesian independence, then grew alarmed as Indonesia's direction became uncertain. "The form that the new Indonesian republic, so strategically vital to Australia, would take had been of grave concern." By the 1950s Menzies supported Dutch retention of West Papua; after Indonesian incorporation (1961–62) Australian anxiety peaked.
- **Colombo Plan (1950)**: Launched by External Affairs Minister Percy Spender, designed "to foster closer relations with newly independent Asian countries." Provided scholarship aid and technical assistance, particularly education in Australia for Asian students — the "human bridge" approach to engagement.
- **SEATO (1954)**: Followed ANZUS as a regional security framework explicitly directed at containing communist expansion. Australia joined; ASEAN members did not.
- **Korean War (1950–53)**: Australia contributed within the UN/Commonwealth framework, operating alongside South Korea and the US.
- **Indonesian Confrontation (1963–66)**: Australian troops in Malaysian Borneo; relations with Sukarno's Indonesia at nadir.
- **Vietnam War (1965–72)**: "More than 50,000 Australians served in Vietnam." The war locked Australia into a US-led Cold War framework that saw the region through an anti-communist lens.

Throughout, "defence thinking" cast Asia as "a threat, and the Pacific Islands a possible sphere of Australian influence; but very few members of the governing class sought a genuine accommodation with the societies of Asia and the Pacific."

### Phase 3: Opening and Reorientation (1972–2000)
The Whitlam government (1972–75) enacted a series of pivotal shifts:
- Established official diplomatic relations with the People's Republic of China (1972)
- Completed dismantling of the white Australia policy
- Recognised East Timor's incorporation into Indonesia (pragmatic engagement with Indonesia despite moral complications)
- Became ASEAN's "first dialogue partner" (1974)

Vietnam refugees: "After the end of the war in Vietnam, Australia accepted 130,000 refugees, and by the early 1980s over one-third of the migrant flow was from Asia." The demographic transformation of Australia's population was the most lasting legacy of the reorientation.

Hawke government (1983–91): "Prime Minister Hawke successfully championed the formation of the Asia Pacific Economic Cooperation (APEC) group in 1989." Simultaneously: floated the Australian dollar, liberalised foreign investment, reduced tariffs. Japan-Australia trade was already the dominant bilateral relationship; China, South Korea, and Taiwan grew rapidly.

Keating government (1991–96): Articulated the most explicit "Australia in Asia" vision. Keating declared that Australia needed to see the Asian region "through the eyes and traditions" of Asian peoples. But Malaysia's PM Mahathir called Keating "recalcitrant" for boycotting an APEC summit, and ASEAN consistently resisted Australian membership.

The period also saw Australian engagement with Pacific Island security: cooperation with New Zealand in Bougainville (1997–98) and the Solomon Islands (from 2003), framed as stabilising a "near neighbourhood" but sometimes resented as neo-colonial.

### Phase 4: Hedging and the China Challenge (2000–present)
Three-quarters of Australia's trade by the early 2000s was in the Asia-Pacific region. China rapidly became Australia's largest trading partner. Yet "investment levels — which represent a deeper engagement — remained strikingly low." Australia hedged: deepening economic integration with China while reinforcing its US security alliance.

Australia "is still kept out of the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN)" despite long links through the Five Power Defence Arrangement (with Malaysia and Singapore), the Colombo Plan, and ASEAN dialogue partnership.

## End Conditions

The process is ongoing. Australia is economically integrated into the Asia-Pacific but politically and strategically still partly outside it. The China-US strategic competition (2010s–) has sharpened the contradiction between economic dependence on China and security alignment with the US.

## Historiography

The chapter by Tomoko Akami and Anthony Milner in [[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]] draws on:
- David Walker, *Anxious Nation: Australia and the Rise of Asia 1850–1939* (1999) — fear as the dominant register
- David Goldsworthy (ed.), *Facing North: A Century of Australian Engagement with Asia*, 2 vols (2001, 2003) — standard diplomatic history
- Sandra Tweedie, *Trading Partners: Australia and Asia 1790–1993* (1994) — economic dimension
- Michael Wesley, *There Goes the Neighbourhood: Australia and the Rise of Asia* (2011)

The dominant narrative is "anxiety gradually overcome" — but Akami and Milner stress that deep cultural and institutional engagement lagged far behind commercial interdependence throughout the century.
