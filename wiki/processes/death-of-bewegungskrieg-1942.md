# Death of Bewegungskrieg

## Definition and Scope

The "death of Bewegungskrieg" (war of movement) refers to the terminal collapse in 1942 of the traditional Prussian-German operational style centered on rapid maneuver, encirclement (Kesselschlacht), and the independence of the subordinate commander (Auftragstaktik or "the independence of the subordinate commander within his own sphere of competency"). This was not merely defeat in specific campaigns but the end of a way of war dating back centuries, from the Great Elector to Moltke and beyond. The 1942 campaigns—beginning with spectacular victories at Kerch, Kharkov, Gazala, and Sevastopol and ending in the twin disasters of El Alamein and Stalingrad within weeks—demonstrated that the old methods no longer corresponded to modern conditions of mass armies, vast spaces, industrial production, and modern communications.

As Citino concludes: "In those brief six months, an entire way of war that dated back centuries had come to an end." The notion that "war is an art, a free and creative activity" and belief in subordinate independence "had revealed themselves to be no longer valid." (Ch. 9, "The Death of the Wehrmacht")

## Causal Drivers

**Overextension and culmination point.** The Wehrmacht's advances in 1942 repeated the classic pattern of attacker loss of strength identified by Clausewitz. Advances of hundreds of miles (e.g., XXXX Panzer Corps crossing Don, Manych, Kuban, Kuma, Terek) created extended, vulnerable LOCs, supply drying up, loss of air support, and "nervous" command. General Carl Wagener (XXXX Panzer Corps) observed the signs: "An ever sparser flow of supply... increased jumbling of formations; their dispersion over a wide area; the immobilization of entire units; ever growing enemy resistance, including the assumption of air superiority; diminished fighting strength, equipment breakdowns, a loss of combat spirit... the command becomes nervous and makes mistakes..." "If the leadership can only count on the valor of the troops, then it has done something wrong." The army continued "long beyond the point when it should have stopped." (Ch. 8, opening on Clausewitz)

**Material superiority and industrial warfare.** In both theaters, enemies countered maneuver with overwhelming materiel: "hordes of tanks, skies filled with aircraft, seventy artillery gun tubes per kilometer." At El Alamein, British 8th Army employed a "1,000 gun barrage" (actually 892 guns) plus massive air power; tanks acted as infantry support in a battle of position. Rommel noted the British "military machine was well-suited to the task, for its entire program of training rested on the battles of materiel (Materialschlachten) of the First World War." German defeat "looked far less like an art than an exercise in an industrial shop class." (Ch. 8, "The British Way of War"; Ch. 9)

**Radio and the death of independent command.** "The new communications technology... now showed its negative face. Radio gave the high command a precise, real-time picture... It also allowed staff and political leaders alike to intervene in the most detailed and... most obnoxious way possible." A German staff officer: "Thanks to modern means of communication and information, an exchange of ideas between the highest command echelons of the army and the lower ranking commanders was possible on an almost uninterrupted basis. This technical advantage had psychological disadvantages, however. Specifically, it caused the supreme command to interfere too often with the leadership of the armies under it. It also caused many army commanders to substitute the opinions of the high command for their own decisions. This was not a plus for the art of war." (Ch. 9)

Historical contrast: At Zorndorf, Seydlitz to Frederick's messenger: "Tell the king that after the battle my head is at his disposal, but meantime, I hope he will permit me to exercise it in his service." By 1942 such independence was gone. Hitler issued the absurd Haltbefehl to Rommel ("stand fast, to take not one step backwards... victory or death") and effectively commanded the Stalingrad pocket from November 22 orders ("6th Army will hedgehog itself and await further orders") to the January 24 refusal of surrender. "Hitler did not kill the concept of flexible command... Radio did." (Ch. 9)

**Coalition weaknesses and satellite armies.** Flanks held by Romanians, Italians, Hungarians proved brittle: threadbare, broad fronts (up to 50-100 miles), few antitank guns, poor supply. Targeted precisely in Uranus. German mobile reserves (e.g., XXXXVIII Panzer Corps) were fuel-starved, low tank counts, and split by contradictory orders. (Ch. 8, "The Soviet Way of War"; "Operation Uranus")

## Major Phases

1. **Spring-summer 1942 victories (illusory last hurrah):** Kerch, Kharkov, Gazala, Sevastopol produced small encirclements (20-30k prisoners) that seemed disappointing compared to earlier war but were in fact the last classic Kesselschlachten.

2. **Culmination and stasis (fall 1942):** Advances to Ordzhonikidze (1 mile), Volga suburbs (400 yards), Alexandria (15 miles) halted by fuel, supply, air inferiority. "The Wehrmacht stuttered and even stopped a half-dozen times, mainly because of fuel shortages."

3. **El Alamein (23 Oct–4 Nov 1942):** British methodical "crumbling" attack (Lightfoot/Supercharge). Mine boxes, deep defense, massive firepower destroyed Panzerarmee cohesion. Hitler's stand-fast order marked the moment the army "fell apart." Rommel: "It was like we had all been hit over the head." Marked first encounter of traditional German craft with "the Machine." (Ch. 8, full El Alamein sections)

4. **Operation Uranus and Stalingrad Kessel (19 Nov 1942 onward):** Soviet deep battle / consecutive operations doctrine executed with echeloned shock groups against Romanian flanks. Mobile corps linked at Kalach/Sovietsky, encircling 6th Army (~246k ration strength). Winter Storm relief reached only ~35 miles short before 6th Panzer diverted; airlift averaged ~117 tons/day vs. required 500-750. (Ch. 8, "Operation Uranus" through "The End: Winter Storm")

## Geographic Spread

Primarily eastern front (southern sector: Don, Caucasus, Stalingrad) and North Africa (El Alamein position). Demonstrated failure in "wide open spaces of the Soviet Union" and bottleneck terrain of Egypt where maneuver was impossible.

## Interaction

- Interacted with (and was defeated by) British return to WWI-style set-piece battles of attrition and Soviet resurrection of "deep battle" + "consecutive operations."
- Radio technology, once enabler of Auftragstaktik, now enabler of centralization.
- Clausewitzian culmination point manifested in both theaters.

## End Conditions

1942 marked the end. The Wehrmacht shifted permanently to defensive posture; the journal Militärwissenschaftliche Rundschau retreated to history in 1944, debating "independence of the subordinate commander" and culmination point as safe topics while current operations were too troubling. Rommel himself recognized the shift: "The time of a Seydlitz and Ziethen has returned." The old way of war, grounded in "handiwork, tradition, and old-world craftsmanship," was trapped "in the grip of the Machine." (Ch. 9)

**Historiography note (per Citino):** The El Alamein–Stalingrad confluence was more than a "turning point." It ended an era. Small 1942 Kessels were the last; future operations would be of a different character.
