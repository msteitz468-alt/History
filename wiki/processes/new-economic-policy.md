# New Economic Policy (NEP)
## Definition and Scope
The **New Economic Policy (NEP)** was a compromise economic system introduced in Soviet Russia by Vladimir Lenin in March 1921, lasting until the launch of the First Five-Year Plan in 1928. It represented a strategic retreat from the radical socialization of War Communism, reintroducing elements of capitalism to revive a ruined, starving nation. Under the NEP, the state retained control of the "commanding heights" of the economy (finance, large-scale industry, transportation, foreign trade, and wholesale commerce), but permitted private enterprise in small-scale manufacturing, retail trade, and agriculture.

## Causal Drivers
- **Economic Ruin:** In 1921, Russia's industrial output was only 20% of pre-war levels, cultivated land had shrunk to 62%, and a massive famine (along with disease and war) had claimed millions of lives (Line 5827).
- **Popular Rebellion:** Peasant uprisings in the provinces and the Kronstadt naval rebellion (March 1921) demonstrated that the population was no longer willing to tolerate the harsh requisitioning of War Communism.
- **Postponed World Revolution:** Lenin realized that socialist revolutions in the industrialized West were not imminent, forcing the Soviet state to survive in isolation.

## Major Phases
- **Introduction (1921):** Promulgated at the Tenth Party Congress. Replaced grain requisitions with a fixed **tax in kind** (later a money tax), giving peasants an incentive to produce since they could sell surpluses on the free market.
- **Recovery and Prosperity (1922–1926):** Backed by aid from the American Relief Administration and other groups, the economy recovered rapidly. Cultivated land and industrial output regained pre-war levels by 1928 (Line 5839). Small private merchants (**Nepmen**) came to control 75% of retail trade, while prosperous peasants (**kulaks**) flourished in the villages.
- **The "Scissors Crisis" and Restrictions (1923–1927):** A pricing crisis emerged due to the wide gap between low agricultural prices and high industrial goods prices, making peasants reluctant to supply grain. The government began introducing measures to restrict Nepmen (1924–1925) and kulaks (1927).

## Geographic Spread
The NEP covered the entire territory of the newly formed Union of Soviet Socialist Republics (USSR), which was established in December 1922 as a federation of Russia, Ukraine, Belarus, and Transcaucasia.

## Interaction
- **With the Party:** The NEP caused severe ideological anxiety among Bolsheviks, who feared the restoration of capitalism and the rise of a new bourgeoisie.
- **With the Succession Struggle:** The future of the NEP was the central issue in the struggle for power after Lenin's death, pitting the Right (Bukharin, who wanted to continue the NEP) against the Left (Trotsky, who criticized it) and the Center (Stalin, who eventually opted to dismantle it).

## End Conditions
The NEP was dismantled in 1928. Following Stalin's victory over the Left and Right factions, the Fifteenth Party Congress (December 1927) adopted measures that ended the NEP. The state launched the **[[soviet-industrialization|First Five-Year Plan]]** and **[[soviet-collectivization|forced collectivization]]**, returning to a fully centralized, command economy.
