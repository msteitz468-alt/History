## Definition and Scope

Japan's engagement with Oceania spans roughly 140 years, from commercial and exploratory ventures of the 1880s through the formal colonial administration of Micronesia (1914–1945), wartime occupation of much of the western and central Pacific (1941–1945), forced withdrawal and repatriation after defeat, and a sustained post-war re-engagement through fisheries, tourism, Official Development Assistance, and multilateral diplomacy. Dvorak (ch. 35) uses the term **"phantom empire"** to capture this arc: "like the uncanny phenomenon of the 'phantom limb' experienced by amputees, who feel their missing appendage hurt even though it has long been removed, many Pacific Islanders — especially in northern Oceania — viscerally sense the presence and sometimes the pain of Japan's enduring influence as if it were yesterday."

The process does not include Japan's continental empire in Korea, Taiwan, Manchuria, or China, though that larger empire shaped policy and resources directed at the Pacific. It is also not reducible to militarism: "The majority of those who settled the Nanyō Guntō, aside from the elite business owners and officials, were the poorest of the poor from Japan, including colonized people from the overthrown Ryukyu Kingdom and the Korean peninsula. Japan's engagement was mainly a peacetime, civilian exercise in out-migration, development, and colonialism."

## Causal Drivers

**Nanshin ideology and early exploration.** The concept of *nanshin* (southward advance) crystallized in the 1880s around the vaguely bounded geographic idea of *Nanyō* (South Seas). Anti-Western intellectual Shiga Shigetaka "was among the first Japanese writers to appropriate the concept of Nanyō as a term that corresponded more to regional/cultural spheres like 'the West' (*Seiyō*) or 'the East' (*Tōyō*)." Early voyages by Suzuki Keikun and Gotō Taketarō in 1884 — nominally to investigate the murder of Japanese pearl divers in the Marshall Islands — produced colourful maps and ethnographic accounts that "piqued the imaginations of elite readers back in Japan."

**Commercial migration.** Under official emigration policy from the late nineteenth century, Japanese and Okinawans began settling wherever labour was needed: Hawaii from 1868, the pearl-diving grounds of Western Australia and Papua, and Rabaul in German New Guinea. Trading companies such as Nanyō Bōeki Kaisha (NBK), formed by the 1908 merger of the Hiki and Murayama companies, established a commercial network across the islands. NBK "still endures to the present day."

**World War I and the League of Nations mandate.** Japan occupied German Micronesia (Marshall Islands, Pohnpei, Kosrae, Chuuk, Yap, Palau, Saipan) in late 1914 with minimal resistance, offering assistance to its British ally. "In 1922, under a League of Nations Class C Mandate, Japanese rule in these islands — which had been administered by the occupying Imperial Navy for eight years — was transferred to a civilian government called the Nanyō-chō, headquartered in Koror, Palau."

**Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere rhetoric.** By the late 1930s, pan-Asian anti-Western ideology fused with economic expansion: "the emerging ideology of the Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere in the late 1930s as an Asian-controlled, Japanese-led bloc that would resist Western colonialism — albeit by promoting the notion of Japanese racial superiority over all other Asians and Pacific Islanders — also made the Nanyō Guntō a peculiar space of resistance."

## Major Phases

### Phase 1 — Pre-Colonial Commercial Presence (1885–1914)

Japanese merchants and settlers filtered into the Pacific through pearl diving, trading, and informal settlement. Pioneer Mori Koben — from a samurai family, sent by a trading company — reached the Marianas and Pohnpei in 1891 before settling permanently in Chuuk, marrying a local chiefly woman and fathering children. His descendants "now number in the thousands." NBK and related firms won commercial footholds even under Spanish and German colonial rule; in Rabaul the German colonial government "gave them the legal status of Europeans, unlike other Asian migrants."

Early explorers produced a "Nanyō Orientalism" — travel accounts and artistic works imagining Oceania as a Japanese frontier. Suzuki Keikun's *Nanyō Tanken Jikki* (1884) "embellished and conflated ethnography from various Pacific locales," while watercolour sketches "inscribe Japanese ukiyo-e style flourishes on Micronesian landscapes, early artistic colonizations that imagine Oceania as a Japanese frontier waiting to be settled."

### Phase 2 — Nanyō-chō Administration (1914–1941)

The civilian Nanyō-chō administered what it renamed *Nanyō Guntō* (South Seas Islands) as a showcase colonial project. Infrastructure — hospitals, postal services, schools, power plants — was built in district centres. Companies such as NKK (sugar, especially in Saipan) and Nantaku (phosphate mining) drove economic development. A "steady flow of people, including thousands of Okinawans, began to emigrate to the Nanyō Guntō through the 1920s to 1930s, reaching over 70,000 people in 1938."

A racialized social hierarchy emerged. Japanese *naichi* (mainland Japanese) held top administrative and corporate positions; below them were "Chamoro" (Japanese administrators' term for Islanders with European ancestry) and Okinawans; then Koreans; at the base, Islanders designated *Kanaka* — "a term appropriated from Polynesian languages and converted into derogatory slang." Japanese colonial ideology "discursively erased all of the distinctions between the incredibly diverse societies and communities that stretched across nearly all of northern Oceania," flattening them into two racialized categories.

Islanders attended segregated *kōgakkō* schools (usually two to three years) while Japanese and Okinawan children attended full eight-year *shōgakkō*. Traditional chiefly leadership was co-opted: "Traditional leaders were recruited into serving as largely symbolic *sonchō*, or village mayors, a way for Japanese to exploit the pre-existing power structure, while pulling chiefs away from their constituents."

At the same time, lived experience was more complex. Okinawans occupied an ambiguous position — discriminated against by *naichi* Japanese yet wielding more status than Islanders in urban centres. In remote atolls "Japanese or Okinawans were the minority, and often had to adapt by learning to speak the local language." Japanese settlers who married into local families built genuine trust. Many Micronesians who experienced the Japanese education system recall it "with a mix of ambivalent bitterness and nostalgia."

**Nanyōism in popular culture.** The serialized manga *Bōken Dankichi* (Boys' Club, 1933–1939) depicted a Japanese boy civilizing "cannibalistic dark-skinned" Islanders, "fuelling the dreams of young men in Japan to cultivate their own Pacific fantasies of life in Paradise." The *Nanyō Odori* (South Seas dance) genre, originating from a racially mocking school skit and commercialized in a 1931 Polydor recording, popularized the image that Islanders and their lands "were alluring, available, and eager for Japanese men to conquer." Even after the war, "variations of Nanyō Odori have been danced throughout Japan by the descendants of returnees from Micronesia, but less as parody than as a form of nostalgic cultural memory."

Japan submitted annual reports to the League of Nations on "developing" the mandate, emphasizing "responsibility to the colonized Islanders in its custody as a 'mandatory power'" even after its formal withdrawal in 1935, up to 1938.

### Phase 3 — Pacific War Militarization and Occupation (1937–1945)

From the late 1930s, Japan fortified Micronesia, constructing major naval bases at Chuuk and Kwajalein with the labour of "hundreds of Japanese political prisoners, as well as over 10,000 Korean forced labourers." The Pearl Harbor attack was "launched from its bases in the Marshall Islands." Japan subsequently occupied Wake, Kiribati, New Guinea, Guam (renamed Ōmiya Island), East Timor, Nauru, parts of the Solomon Islands, and Bougainville. Japanese forces also conducted air attacks on Darwin, Broome, and Townsville and a submarine attack on Sydney.

US forces began island-hopping from 1944, from the Marshall Islands westward to Palau, Saipan, and Guam, "practically bulldozing the islands, leaving a wake of complete destruction and death." Bypassed garrisons experienced starvation; the Mili Atoll Rebellion of 1943, triggered by "starvation and cannibalism by Japanese captors," ended in the massacre of 170 Koreans and 84 Marshallese. "Over 500,000 enlisted Japanese were killed in Pacific Island places during the war." Islanders were drafted to bury the dead; many Japanese settlers were repatriated.

### Phase 4 — Post-War Amnesia and Selective Re-engagement (1945–present)

After defeat, Japan was effectively excluded from formal re-engagement: "travel to the US Trust Territory that replaced the Japanese mandate was off limits for the first decades after the war." Deep-freeze refrigeration enabled long-distance tuna fishing in former Nanyō Guntō waters from the 1950s. Japanese tourists eventually returned for scuba diving and golf in Guam, Palau, and Saipan, "mostly for scuba diving and golf, and rarely with much awareness of the pre-war era."

The Nihon Izokukai (Japan Bereaved Families Association) organised pilgrimage tours to former Pacific battlefields. The *Daigo Fukuryūmaru* (Lucky Dragon No. 5) incident of 1954 — when a tuna fishing vessel was irradiated by the Castle Bravo hydrogen bomb test at Bikini — "sparked massive protests throughout Japan against nuclear weapons and led to a sense of solidarity... between Pacific nuclear testing victims... and Japanese *hibakusha* (radiation victims)." The Lucky Dragon inspired Godzilla; historian Igarashi Yoshikuni reads Mothra's "coconut-shaped egg" washing ashore in 1950s–1960s films as Japan's "post-war nightmares" replacing pre-war dreams of southern expansion.

From 1997, Japan has hosted **Pacific Islands Leaders Meetings (PALM)** every three years. These have been "widely criticized for lumping all Pacific nations into one without consideration for local contexts, and they have tended to function less as spaces of mutual dialogue than as opportunities for the Japanese government to promote its latest plans in the region." Japanese Official Development Assistance (ODA) via JICA and JOCV programmes funds fishing bases, hospitals, and schools throughout independent Pacific Island nations, though "some scholars have suggested that Japan's sole purpose in engaging in aid diplomacy is to garner better access to dwindling fisheries."

Historical revisionism has intensified as the wartime generation disappears: "right-leaning memorialist groups travel to sites of former Japanese occupation... building shrines and memorials to war heroes" and "hijacking Islanders' narratives of nostalgia in the service of Japanese right-wing nationalism."

Meanwhile, recent decades have seen the emergence of meaningful alliances: "Indigenous Ainu, Ryukyuans (Okinawans), and Pacific Islanders... have begun to redefine and subvert the Japanese government's pattern of paternalism in Oceania."

## Geographic Spread

Core zone of formal colonial presence: the former German Micronesia — Mariana Islands (except Guam), Caroline Islands, Marshall Islands — administered as *Nanyō Guntō* 1922–1945. Broader zone of economic and demographic presence: Hawaii (from 1868), Philippines, Western Australia and Papua (pearl diving), Rabaul/New Guinea (trading communities), and by the late 1930s, survey operations extending to Melanesia and Southeast Asia. Wartime occupation temporarily extended to Solomon Islands, Bougainville, Kiribati, Nauru, Guam, New Guinea, and parts of Southeast Asia. Post-war presence dispersed via fishing fleets, tourism infrastructure, and ODA programmes across all independent Pacific Island states.

## End Conditions

The Japanese empire in Oceania was administratively ended by defeat in August 1945 and the formal surrender terms, but the *phantom* empire persists: Japanese loanwords dominate Palauan, thousands of Islanders of Japanese and Okinawan heritage seek family connections in Japan, and the remains of Japanese soldiers "still sleep in the coral sediment of many Pacific places." As Dvorak asks: "How can Japan transcend its phantom empire to pursue an 'Oceanian' worldview — as a more conscious, equal partner in the perpetuation of a healthy, peaceful, and safe environment for the people and ecosystems of all who share these waters?"

## Historiography

**Foundational scholarship.** Mark Peattie's *Nan'yō: The Rise and Fall of the Japanese in Micronesia, 1885–1945* (1988) remains "the most significant" English-language history of Japanese colonialism in northern Oceania, focused on political history. Japanese-language scholarship by Kawamura, Yano, Kobayashi, and Imaizumi provides nuanced considerations of Japanese empire in the Pacific Islands, but "further work is needed to compile and thread together these rich and complex narratives."

**Recent critical perspectives.** Dvorak's framework of the "phantom empire" builds on Merleau-Ponty's phenomenology of the phantom limb to theorize how both Japanese and Islanders experience the spectral presence of a vanished imperial system. Post-colonial critics Kawamura Minato and Sudō Naoto analyse pre-war Japanese popular culture as "Tropicalism" or "Nanyō Orientalism." Tomiyama Ichiro examines the ambiguous position of Okinawans in the Nanyō Guntō. Dvorak's own *Coral and Concrete: Remembering Kwajalein Atoll between Japan, America, and the Marshall Islands* (2018) provides the deepest case study.

**Memory and revisionism.** Keith Camacho's *Cultures of Commemoration* (2011) and Dvorak's "Who closed the sea?" (2017) document contested politics of war memory in the Mariana Islands and beyond. Iitaka Shingo warns against reading Micronesians' ambivalent nostalgia for Japanese rule as straightforward approval, pointing out how these memories "have been recontextualized in the post-war era under a different government."

**Collection coverage note.** [[hattori-samson-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v2-2023]] (ch. 35, Dvorak) provides the most recent synthesis available here. The collection has moderate coverage of Japan's Pacific empire through this Cambridge volume but is thin on Japanese-language scholarship and on post-war fisheries and ODA dimensions.
