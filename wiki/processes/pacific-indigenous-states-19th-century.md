## Definition and Scope

The nineteenth century was the pivot of Pacific political history: simultaneously an era of Indigenous state modernization and an era of colonial conquest. The conventional narrative focuses on the *outcome* — colonial partition — but Gonschor (in [[hattori-samson-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v2-2023]]) insists the more important story is transformation. As he frames it: "the century was first and foremost one of transformation, modernization, and hybridization of Indigenous systems of governance." At the century's opening, European colonial presence was minimal (only the Spanish Marianas and Norfolk Island). By 1901, every archipelago had been claimed. Yet for most of the century this outcome was not foreordained; everything was, in Andrew Robson's phrase, "in a state of flux [and n]o one knew what the ultimate political fate of the islands would be."

This process page covers: (1) the development of Indigenous hybrid states; (2) the networks of institutional transfer that spread constitutional models; (3) pan-Oceanian regionalism centred on [[hawaii]]; (4) European imperial encroachment and the "scramble for the Pacific" from the 1870s onward.

## Causal Drivers

**Pre-existing state structures.** Three Pacific societies — Hawai'i, Tonga, and Kosrae — had already developed "primary states" before Western contact, comparable (argues archaeologist Robert Hommon) to ancient Egypt, Mesopotamia, and Mesoamerica. These stratified polities held a structural advantage in adapting to modernity over more politically fragmented neighbours.

**Christian conversion and literacy.** Missionaries introduced both Christianity and written vernacular languages. Benedict Anderson's argument about print capitalism and "imagined communities" applies directly: standardized Bible translations in Hawaiian, Tahitian, Tongan, Samoan, and Fijian created the linguistic-cultural foundations for national identities. Oceania's first newspaper, the missionary-edited *Ka Lama Hawaii*, appeared in 1834; by the 1860s Hawai'i had up to five concurrent Hawaiian-language newspapers.

**Hybrid statecraft strategies.** Three interlocking concepts explain Indigenous state-builders' approach: *parity* (being accepted as equals under international law), *similitude* (conscious self-presentation stressing likeliness to Western states), and *hybridity* (selective appropriation of Western forms combined with Indigenous structures). Hawaiian rulers, in Beamer's formulation, "used traditional structures and systems of knowledge in an attempt to construct a modern nation-state" while creating "something new, neither completely Anglo American nor traditionally Hawaiian."

**Networked institutional transfer.** State-building did not proceed in isolation. Eleanor Westney's concept of "institutional transfer" — selective appropriation of Western organizational patterns — was at work across Polynesia, but crucially mediated through *Oceanian* channels, not just European ones. Tahiti first; then Hawai'i; then Tonga and Fiji copying the Hawaiian model.

**European imperial competition.** From the 1870s, competition among Britain, France, Germany, and the United States accelerated partition. Colonial claims were used to pre-empt rivals as much as to administer territory.

## Major Phases

### Phase 1: Tahitian Initiative and Early Constitutional Monarchies (1800s–1840s)

Despite lacking Hawai'i's and Tonga's pre-contact centralized structures, Tahiti moved first on written constitutions. High chief Pomare II converted to the Protestantism of the London Missionary Society, defeated traditionalist rivals in 1815, and created a centralized Christian monarchy. His legal code of 1819 — Oceania's first written legal code, containing Old Testament-inspired prohibitions alongside classical Tahitian regulations — was the template for constitutional development across central eastern Polynesia. Amended in 1825 to include a partly elected legislative assembly, the Tahitian kingdom "actually preceded Hawai'i in having a constitutional monarchy." The law code's key vocabulary (ture for law, tāvana for district chief) spread to the Society, Austral, Cook, Samoan, and eventually Tongan islands as institutional transfer.

### Phase 2: Hawaiian Kingdom Consolidation and Leadership (1820s–1880s)

In Hawai'i, the Kamehameha dynasty oversaw a long arc of modernization. Kamehameha III (1814–54) converted to Christianity in the mid-1820s and initiated a written constitution in 1840 — "one of the earlier ones in the world, when many European nations were still absolute monarchies" — compulsory education (producing one of the world's highest literacy rates), and a land reform protecting both private ownership and native tenants' traditional rights. In 1843 Hawai'i was "recognized as an independent state in a joint declaration by Great Britain and France, the first, and for almost the entire century the only, non-Western nation to achieve such a status."

Under Kamehameha IV and V and then King Kalākaua (1836–91), the monarchy moved away from missionary influence toward secularism and a culturally assertive national programme. At its peak in 1887, Hawai'i maintained 103 embassies and consulates worldwide, concluded over twenty international treaties, and joined the International Postal Union (1882).

### Phase 3: Networked Institutional Transfer (1850s–1880s)

Three nodes of political diffusion:
- **Tahiti** influenced the Society, Austral, and Cook Islands in the 1820s–1840s, and indirectly Hawai'i.
- **Tonga** became a model for western Polynesia. Tāufa'āhau (George Tupou I, 1797–1893) reunified the Tongan islands through war and Christian alliance, then adopted the Hawaiian model: Tonga's 1875 constitution was "a slightly modified version" of Hawai'i's 1864 constitution, obtained through Hawaiian diplomat Charles St. Julian and his successor Edward Reeve. That document "in amended form is still the basic legal document of Tonga today."
- **Hawai'i** was the dominant hub. Hawaiian diplomat Charles St. Julian promoted Hawaiian models across western Polynesia from the 1850s. The Fijian constitutional kingdom (1867–71) under Cakobau was explicitly based on the Hawaiian constitution; the Samoan constitution of 1873 was partly influenced by St. Julian, then amended in 1875 under advice from American diplomat Albert Steinberger, who had studied Hawai'i's model directly from Kalākaua.

Western advisors who worked with Indigenous rulers against settler interests were systematically removed: "Both Steinberger and Baker ended up being removed from office and deported by invading British navy ships, in 1876 and 1890 respectively."

### Phase 4: Pan-Oceanian Regionalism (1857–1887)

The most ambitious dimension of Hawaiian foreign policy. St. Julian first articulated pan-Oceanian union in 1857 — a confederation that would be "a power in the world" capable of resisting colonialism. Kalākaua revived and enlarged this vision. Following his 1881 world circumnavigation (the first by any head of state), he issued a 1883 proclamation appealing for recognition of "the inalienable right of the several native communities of Polynesia to enjoy opportunities for progress and self-government." In 1887 a Hawaiian diplomatic delegation convinced Samoa's Malietoa Laupepa to sign a confederation treaty; the HHMS *Kaimiloa* was dispatched to project this pan-Pacific hybrid modernity. The project collapsed simultaneously with a domestic coup against the Hawaiian government by American missionary descendants and a German naval invasion of Samoa.

Kalākaua also pursued pan-Asian solidarity during his 1881 circumnavigation, meeting rulers of Japan, China, Thailand, and Johor. Hawai'i played a role in revising Japan's unequal treaties in the 1890s, and the Chinese revolutionary Sun Yat-sen, educated in Hawai'i during Kalākaua's reign, "repeatedly referred to Hawai'i as one of the conceptual models for his own political reforms."

### Phase 5: The Scramble for the Pacific (1874–1901)

Colonial encroachment was neither inevitable nor always desired by Islanders:

**British actions.** New Zealand claimed by the Treaty of Waitangi in 1840 — contested from the start due to divergent translations of *rangatiratanga* (rendered as "independence" in Māori but "governance" in the English text). Māori resistance throughout the century included the election of Pōtatau Te Wherowhero as Māori King in 1858, founding a movement still extant today. Britain acquired Fiji in 1874 through a "deed of cession" that ignored the existing constitutional kingdom — Cakobau and his allies "had virtually no other choice" after British officials actively sabotaged the Fijian government and Hawaiian support lapsed. Subsequent British acquisitions: Cook Islands (1888), Tokelau (1889), Gilberts (1892), Solomons (1893), and a de facto protectorate over Tonga (1900).

**French actions.** France proclaimed the Marquesas a colony in 1842, forced a protectorate over Pomare IV's Tahitian kingdom in 1842–47 after fierce resistance, annexed the Tahitian protectorate in 1880, and extended control over the Leeward Islands (Raiatea resisting until the mid-1890s under Teraupoo), the Austral Islands, New Caledonia (1853, established as a penal colony and settler colony displacing Kanak people to reserves), and the Marquesas more fully in the 1880s. The 1878 Kanak pan-tribal uprising against land dispossession in New Caledonia required "massive French military force to be suppressed."

**German actions.** Newly unified Germany became aggressive from the 1880s. With Britain it divided Melanesia and Micronesia in 1885 (Bismarck Archipelago, northern Solomons, Marshall Islands under Germany). Germany invaded Samoa in 1887, destroying Kalākaua's confederation; after tripartite negotiations failed, Samoa was divided between Germany and the USA in 1900.

**American actions.** In Hawai'i, the Hawaiian Kingdom's unique recognized standing meant direct colonial seizure was impossible. Instead, missionary-descent families staged a domestic coup against Kalākaua in 1887, then conspired with the US diplomatic representative to overthrow Queen Lili'uokalani in 1893. The US government disavowed the invasion and negotiated to restore her constitutional government — "that promise was never kept." The 1898 Congressional resolution annexing Hawai'i bypassed treaty requirements, creating what Beamer calls a "faux-colonial" occupation. The USA also acquired Guam (1898) and Samoa's eastern islands (1900).

**Chilean actions.** Chile claimed Rapa Nui (Easter Island) in 1888 via a controversial bilingual document "similar to the treaty of Waitangi," outsourcing actual colonization to a company.

## Geographic Spread

The process was uneven by subregion:
- **Polynesia** was the centre of Indigenous state formation — Hawai'i, Tonga, Tahiti, Samoa, and Fiji developed the fullest constitutional structures. Māori in Aotearoa/New Zealand mounted decades of armed and political resistance.
- **Micronesia** saw smaller-scale parallels. On Kosrae — the only other Oceanian primary state — monarchy eroded under missionary Christianity rather than transforming into a constitutional state. On Kiribati (Abemama), chief Binoka modelled himself on Kamehameha. Hawaiian and US Congregationalist missionaries shaped emerging identities across Kiribati, the Marshalls, and Pohnpei.
- **Melanesia** was the exception: high linguistic diversity (dozens of mutually unintelligible languages) and political fragmentation prevented archipelago-scale Indigenous state formation. Colonial power eventually imposed political unity by force.

## End Conditions

The process of Indigenous state formation was arrested (not ended) by colonial conquest. The "long interlude of colonial rule interrupted these processes," but Gonschor argues the same features reappear in post-colonial constitutionalism and Pacific regionalism. Hawai'i remained in a "state of constitutional limbo" — an unresolved situation that "would resurface in the late twentieth and early twenty-first century." The vision of pan-Oceanian confederation, first articulated by St. Julian in 1857 and revived by Kalākaua, "first arose a century earlier" than Epeli Hau'ofa's influential "Our Sea of Islands" essay, and has had a "cyclical reassertion" in the 1970s–1980s and again in the 21st century.

## Historiography

Gonschor's chapter represents a significant historiographical revision: it explicitly rejects the teleological narrative that reads the 19th century as "simply a period of transition towards colonial rule." The older framework treated colonialism as the dominant story; the revised framework centres Indigenous political agency, hybrid statecraft, and transoceanic networks of constitutional diffusion. Key theoretical frameworks deployed: Niklaus Schweizer's concept of *parity*, Jeremy Prestholdt's *similitude*, Kamanamaikalani Beamer's *hybridity*, and Eleanor Westney's *institutional transfer*. Benedict Anderson's *Imagined Communities* is applied to Pacific print cultures and literary nationalism.

A persistent historiographical blind spot has been the treatment of Melanesia as passive: Gonschor acknowledges that transformation there was "more local in scale" but notes that settler and trader adventurism produced unusual entities (Franceville in New Hebrides; Samoan-American Emma Coe's business empire in the Bismarck Archipelago) that deserve more attention.

Collection coverage through [[hattori-samson-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v2-2023]] is strong for Polynesia and moderate for Micronesia; Melanesian political history remains underrepresented.
