# Rural Settlement in Northern Gaul

**The ruralization of the post-Roman countryside, from dispersed timber farms to the first nucleated
villages — and the parallel spectrum of hilltop and elite residences.** Edith Peytremann's chapter
(Oxford Handbook, Ch. 31), with Luc Bourgeois's on hilltop sites and elite residences (Ch. 28),
reconstructs Merovingian rural life from settlement archaeology rather than texts, documenting both the
small family farm and the elite places that towered over it.

## Definition and Scope

Peytremann identifies **three coexisting settlement morphologies** in northern Gaul — dispersed isolated
farms (*exploitations isolées*), hamlets of 2–5 farm units, and villages of more than 5 — "with no single
form dominant everywhere." The diagnostic building is the **sunken-feature building** (*Grubenhaus*/SFB):
a rectangular pit under a light superstructure, used for weaving, craft, or storage, "regionally
variable" and not a uniform replacement for Roman stone construction.

## Major Phases and Features

- **Small-scale family farming.** Individual farm units "rarely exceeded 1.5 hectares"; the productive
  unit was the family farm, not the slave-worked villa (see [[end-of-the-roman-villa]]).
- **Continued colonization.** About **25% of sixth-century sites** were founded *ex nihilo* on previously
  unoccupied land — "continued landscape colonization rather than mere continuity from Roman sites."
- **Timber over stone.** Rural buildings "overwhelmingly used timber frame construction"; stone was rare
  outside elite or ecclesiastical contexts.
- **Polyculture and craft.** Sites show polyculture (wheat, barley, rye, oats — see
  [[farming-and-diet-in-merovingian-gaul]]), small-scale metallurgy, ceramic production, weaving (loom
  weights in SFBs), and riverine exploitation.
- **The mid-seventh-century transition.** Around the mid-seventh century settlements "became more
  grouped/nucleated; cemeteries and churches were incorporated within settlement boundaries… production
  centers grew in scale" — possibly "the early emergence of the village as an institutionalized form."

## Hilltop Sites and Elite Residences

Bourgeois documents a spectrum of high-status places. **Hilltop settlements** ranged "from 0.5 to over 100
hectares," from temporary refuge to permanent elite residence: Roc de Pampelune (peak c. 540), **Larina**
(Isère, 21 ha, the most fully excavated), Mont-Sainte-Odile (Alsace, 120 ha, c. 670, the largest), and La
Malène. Alongside them stood **open (unfortified) lowland elite residences** — Pouthumé, Serris-Les-
Ruelles, Saint-Romain-de-Jalionas — yielding high-status material without defensive architecture. A
gold-*tremissis* study of Poitou identified some **45 minor "central places"** (*lieux centraux*) below
the level of the *civitas* city.

## Interaction and Significance

This settlement record underpins Frans Theuws's "bottom-up" reading of the
[[merovingian-economy|Merovingian economy]]: a dense, productive, broadly-furnished rural population with
few aristocratic residential sites — the very pattern that lets him argue for rural ritual demand, rather
than elite extraction, as the motor of exchange. It also concretizes the ruralization that reshaped diet,
livestock, and the city (see [[merovingian-cities]]).
