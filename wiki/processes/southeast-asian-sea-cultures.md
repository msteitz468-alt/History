## Definition and Scope

The enduring sea cultures of Southeast Asia denotes the continuous, internally driven maritime networks, polities, and cultural practices that characterised the region from the seventh through seventeenth centuries — and which predate, outlast, and substantially exceed in scope any European influence on the region. Gaynor (Ch31 of [[jones-matsuda-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v1-2022]]) argues that "political and ideological legacies have led some historians of the maritime world to misrepresent people from beyond the region as the principal drivers of change, even when they were not, rather than come to grips with the enduring sea cultures at the heart of regional dynamics."

Southeast Asia's "vast archipelagoes and extensive mainland coastlines have nurtured sea-focused cultures for millennia." The Pacific, Indian Ocean, and South China Sea "all flow through Southeast Asia," whose "patterned currents in wind and water spawned interconnected sea-focused communities and shaped complexes of cultural, commercial, and political interaction."

## Causal Drivers

### Prehistoric Maritime Networks

Linguistics documents "the basic dispersion of Austronesian languages, thought to have spread from around Taiwan into Southeast Asia, and reaching across the Indian Ocean to Madagascar in the west, as well as eastward across the Pacific." The Sa Huynh-Kalanay Interaction Sphere — operating between "about 500 BCE and 100 CE, though some objects, such as nephrite, suggest it lasted into later centuries" — linked mainland and island Southeast Asia via jar burial, distinctive pottery, and exchange of green nephrite jade chemically traced to a single Taiwanese mine. The network's distribution "reveals one of the most extensive sea-based networks of a single geological material in the prehistoric world" and "pre-dates South Asia's religious, philosophical, and architectural influence in Southeast Asia."

After 800 CE, the flow of Chinese ceramic, metal, and textile manufactures "flooded Southeast Asian markets." Southeast Asian exports to China and westward were primarily raw commodities: metals, spices, and "highly prized sappan wood ... exported to India and China from Timor."

### Indigenous Shipbuilding

Southeast Asian shipbuilding was "distinct from Indian Ocean and Chinese shipbuilding technology." The "stitched plank and lashed-lug technique" — identified in "third-century Chinese historical records" and attributed to Southeast Asian (Kunlun) people — produced vessels "up to 45–50 metres long, with multiple masts and sails, and able to carry hundreds of passengers as well as a substantial cargo." These vessels conveyed seventh-century Buddhist monks to and from Śrīvijaya and Bay of Bengal harbours. By the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, Javanese *jong* (from which "junk" derives) averaged "350–500 tons deadweight, while exceptional examples would have reached a thousand tons and carried aboard as many men," sailing to southern China, Maluku, and "as far west as the Maldives, Calicut, Oman, Aden, and the Red Sea."

### The Shipmaster (*puhawang*)

Central to all Southeast Asian maritime societies was the *nakhoda* (medieval/Islamic) or *puhawang* (Old Malay vernacular) — the shipmaster who owned the ship and part of her cargo. The earliest Southeast Asian mention appears in a sixth-century Sanskrit inscription; the 680s Śrīvijayan inscriptions "use a term of foreign origin for the sea merchant ... Yet they render 'shipmaster' as *puhawang* in the vernacular Old Malay." This role "reappears, with the same name, in the many foundation myths of insular Southeast Asia's trading polities." During the early modern period "coastal polities came to share a similar organization, with shipmasters, admirals, and harbourmasters playing important roles in society and politics."

## Major Phases and Polities

### Champa (7th–17th c.)

Champa, founded "by Austronesian-speaking people who arrived by sea and established ports on river deltas," produced "the earliest extant inscription in any Southeast Asian vernacular." Champa centres shifted in relative importance (Mỹ Sơn and Trà Kiệu dominant until mid-8th c.; then Nha Trang and Phan Rang; then Thu Bồn valley from mid-9th c.) according to "the vicissitudes of trade that linked Cham ports to China, archipelagic Southeast Asia, and India."

Champa served as intermediary for Philippine trade with China: "Before the mid-fourteenth century, Philippine trade and tribute to China apparently travelled via Champa." Cham influence extended "as far afield as Java, Sulawesi, and the Philippines." From the eighth to tenth centuries, Cham inscriptions mention "ferocious, pitiless, dark-skinned sea raiders" attacking Cham towns, illustrating the violence and connectivity of the maritime world. During the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries "Cham Muslims formed a diaspora of traders, warriors, and refugees in the region and its seas."

### Śrīvijaya (7th–13th c.)

Founded "in the late seventh century, with its centre in present-day Palembang," Śrīvijaya expanded to "establish a strategically dominant position in relation to the Melaka Straits." Archaeological work has overturned the idea of sudden emergence: "Long before Śrīvijaya's founding, long-distance trade networks facilitated long-term processes of urbanization and state formation." The famous Chinese Buddhist monk Yijing "stayed at Śrīvijaya for nearly eight years between 671 and 695, translating hundreds of Sanskrit manuscripts," reporting over a thousand priests engaged in study.

Śrīvijaya's control mechanism was characteristically maritime: "This country, lying in the ocean and controlling the straits through which the foreigners' sea and land traffic in either direction must pass, in olden times used iron chains as a barrier to keep the pirates of other countries in check" (Zhao Rugua, 13th c.). Ships that passed without calling could expect armed attack: a system that "prefigures the Portuguese *cartaz* system, which required all passing ships to present these passes."

Śrīvijaya's decline came under Chola attacks (1017, 1025) and Song Chinese maritime commercial expansion. Its centre shifted from Palembang to Jambi in the late eleventh century. The eventual fall cleared the way for Majapahit expansion from Java and for the founding of Melaka.

### Brunei/Boni (10th–16th c.)

Chinese sources noted Boni as "newly entered" in 977 CE. Scholars debate precise locations, but from the mid-fourteenth century onwards, "archaeology provides more solid evidence that Chinese references to 'Boni' then referred to Brunei," located at Kota Batu on Borneo's northwest coast. Antonio Pigafetta's account of Brunei (c. 1521) describes a city "entirely built in salt water ... the houses are all of wood, built over large piles, high off the ground. When the tide rises, women go through the city in boats, selling things necessary for life."

### Melaka (15th c.) as Institutional Model

The port of Melaka, founded by the fugitive Paramesvara (followed by sea people from Palembang) around 1400, became the premier Southeast Asian entrepôt of the fifteenth century. The *syahbandar* system — a headman from each major merchant community who "levied customs duties, provided warehouse space and transportation services, and generally represented the community in its dealings with the state" — became the institutional model replicated by the Dutch VOC's *kapitan* system and adapted across maritime Southeast Asia.

Tomé Pires noted that rulers in this region were measured by "strength in boats"; traders had recourse to temporary fairs "so did not need to rely on large urban markets."

### Makassar and the Sama (17th c.)

In the seventeenth century, "Southeast Asian states also pursued imperial ambitions of their own. For instance, Makassar, with its naval resources and nautical partners, opposed the VOC and its local allies, and well into the early eighteenth century continued to launch expansionary naval campaigns." 

The maritime-oriented Sama people "played important social, political, and military roles in early modern Makassar": selecting community leaders, holding the office of harbourmaster, co-commanding naval expeditions, and manning naval forces. Makassar's close ally Tiworo — described by VOC as "a nasty pirates' nest" — was "an autonomous amphibious polity." Rather than destroy Tiworo's irreplaceable boats or kill its mariners, VOC's local ally "armed and elevated sixty of Tiworo's men to form half of his elite guard." Even after the VOC defeated Makassar in 1667, "the Sama remained Makassar's 'muscles and sinews' in its eighteenth-century naval expeditions across the Flores Sea."

The *kora-kora* boat federations (*hongi*) of Moluccan villages — used first by the Portuguese and then by the VOC (first Dutch *hongi* in 1607) — illustrate how European colonizers built on "and took advantage of existing nautical practices."

## Geographic Spread

The process encompasses:
- **Central Vietnam coast:** Champa, from Mỹ Sơn south to Phan Rang.
- **Sumatra:** Śrīvijaya (Palembang–Jambi); later Aceh in the north.
- **Malay Peninsula:** Kedah, Melaka, Patani.
- **Borneo:** Brunei/Boni on northwest coast.
- **Eastern archipelago:** Makassar (Sulawesi), Banda Islands, Ternate, Tidore (Maluku), Ambon.
- **Philippines:** Butuan (Mindanao) maintaining 11th–12th c. connections via Champa.

## Historiography

Gaynor (Ch31 of [[jones-matsuda-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v1-2022]]) builds on Pierre-Yves Manguin's extensive nautical archaeological work on Southeast Asian ships and harbours; Anthony Reid's foundational *Southeast Asia in the Age of Commerce* (1988, 1993); and her own *Intertidal History in Island Southeast Asia* (2016) on the Sama and coastal capture. The chapter explicitly frames itself against Eurocentric "colonial wars" narratives and toward an "integral part of world history" approach that foregrounds indigenous maritime polity agency.

Key debates include: whether Champa was a unitary kingdom or a federation of separate entities; the precise identity of Boni/Brunei in Chinese sources; and the degree to which the *puhawang* / *nakhoda* role was an indigenous Southeast Asian institution or a hybrid of Indian Ocean and local practices.

Linguistic evidence (Austronesian dispersal), archaeology (Sa Huynh-Kalanay sphere, nephrite distribution, sewn-plank boats, Palembang tidal settlements), and indigenous epigraphy (Śrīvijayan Old Malay inscriptions from the 680s) provide the core primary evidence. Chinese sources (Zhao Rugua, Ma Huan, dynastic records) are valuable but reflect the perspective of a foreign observer/tributary power. Portuguese sources from the early 16th century (Tomé Pires, Antonio de Paiva) are the first extensive European accounts.

**Collection coverage note:** Sub-Saharan Africa and Southeast Asia are listed as gaps in the collection; this chapter partially closes the Southeast Asian sea-cultures gap for the 7th–17th c. period.
