## Definition and Scope

Brazilian Indian slavery was the systematic coerced appropriation of indigenous labor in Portuguese colonial Brazil, operating from the first permanent settlements of the 1530s through the mid-eighteenth century, though its coastal phase was substantially replaced by African slave labor by the mid-seventeenth century. It was not a single institution but a set of overlapping legal categories, informal practices, and raiding economies, all oriented toward supplying plantation and household labor to Portuguese colonists.

John Monteiro's analysis (Ch. 13, *Cambridge History of the Native Peoples of the Americas*, Vol. III, Part 1) situates Brazilian Indian slavery as structurally distinct from the [[atlantic-slave-trade]] that would ultimately replace it: it was rooted in the internal dynamics of Tupi warfare (vengeance cycles, captive-taking), was managed through indigenous alliance networks, and was legally justified through categories alien to African chattel slavery.

The process must be distinguished from the precolonial Tupi practice of taking war captives for ritual sacrifice. The Portuguese misread Tupi captives as equivalent to slaves ("colonial observers, for obvious reasons, sought to equate captives to slaves"), but in precolonial context "these captives were neither exploited nor treated as slaves, even though they performed certain productive tasks." Colonial Indian slavery transformed this ritual institution into a labor institution, destroying its cultural logic in the process.

## Causal Drivers

**Sugar economy.** The establishment of permanent sugar plantations, concentrated especially in Pernambuco and Bahia from the 1540s onward, created demand for permanent, coerced agricultural labor. Most sixteenth-century sugar planters "turned exclusively to the local Indian population." Barter and alliance-based food and labor supply proved inadequate: Tupi surpluses "usually were limited to short-term storage strategies related to military expeditions," and "barter failed to develop more fully because each party attached radically different meanings to it."

**Exploitation of inter-Tupi warfare.** The Portuguese "perceived that the permanent state of war between Tupi groups offered a potentially unlimited source of Indian captives." By supplying firearms and organizing allied Tupi warriors in raids against traditional enemies, colonists escalated indigenous conflict into *saltos* (slave raids). By the end of the sixteenth century, "colonial writers began to assert that the allies eagerly waged battles against their enemies only to produce captives for sale to the whites."

**Legal architecture: Just War and Resgate.** Two legal categories legitimated enslavement under Portuguese law:
1. *Guerra justa* ("Just War"): Wars authorized by the king or governor against indigenous peoples who attacked colonists, killed clergy, or otherwise resisted colonial authority.
2. *Resgate* ("ransom"): The "rescue" of indigenous captives (*indios da corda*, or bound Indians) who had been taken by enemy groups for sacrifice and cannibalism. Their ransomers were legally permitted to keep them as slaves in exchange for saving them from death.

The Law of 20 March 1570 codified both categories while declaring all other Indians free. In practice both categories were abused: colonists invented pretexts for Just Wars (the Caete campaign of 1562 served as template), and *resgate* expeditions used the term "to mean not only ransom but also any other sort of trade with the Indians."

**Mamaluco intermediaries.** Mixed-descent (*mamaluco*) backwoodsmen fluent in Tupi-Guarani became the leading agents of interior slave-raiding from the 1570s onward. Anchieta described how these men "venture two or three hundred leagues into the sertao, serving the devil with such amazing martyrdom, just to barter or steal slaves." The documented case of Domingos Fernandes Nobre (*Tomacauna*, Pernambuco, deposed before the Inquisition in 1591) illustrates their methods: he penetrated indigenous societies "at times persuading them to migrate to the coast or enticing them to attack other enemies in order to produce captives."

## Major Phases

**Phase 1 — Early barter and informal captive trade (c. 1500–1540).** Sporadic Portuguese and French traders extracted brazilwood and small numbers of slaves. Indigenous warfare patterns provided captives; the Portuguese fit within existing Tupi alliance logic. No systematic slave economy yet existed.

**Phase 2 — Sugar economy and the war for slaves (1540–1562).** Permanent plantations created structural demand. The Portuguese "managed to incite their allies to intensify ongoing conflicts with traditional rivals." Indigenous warfare "gradually assumed the character of *saltos*, or slave raids." The Tamoio War (c. 1548–1567) was partly a resistance to this escalating predation. Jesuit arrival (1549) and establishment of aldeias created institutional competition for Indian labor.

**Phase 3 — Just War, epidemic crisis, and legal codification (1562–1580).** The Caete "Just War" (1562) set a precedent: "within a few months over 50,000 captives were taken in the war, but only 10,000 lived on to serve as slaves in the Salvador area, the others falling victim either to ruthless massacres or uncompromising disease." Simultaneous smallpox (1559) and measles (1562–1563) epidemics killed tens of thousands in the Bahia aldeias, driving demand for new slave sources. The Law of 1570 attempted regulation without abolition. Between 1570 and 1600 the number of sugar mills on the northeastern coast tripled.

**Phase 4 — Interior raiding and the transition to African slavery (1580–c. 1640).** As coastal Tupi populations collapsed, slave-raiding expeditions penetrated ever-deeper into the backlands — precursors to the seventeenth-century *bandeiras* of Sao Paulo. Anchieta's 1583 account: "Go now to the mills and plantations of Bahia and you will find them filled with Guinea blacks and very few native blacks." Stuart Schwartz's analysis documents the gradual shift to African labor on Bahian plantations between 1570 and 1620. Indian slavery on the coast became economically unviable as populations were exhausted.

**Phase 5 — Interior continuation (c. 1640–c. 1750).** The slave-raiding cycle "lasted well into the eighteenth century" in Sao Paulo, Goias, Mato Grosso, Maranhao, Para, and Rio Negro, where the sugar economy had not yet made the transition to African labor and interior indigenous populations remained accessible.

## Geographic Spread

The process began on the northeast and southeast coasts — Pernambuco, Bahia, Espirito Santo, Sao Vicente — where the sugar economy was concentrated. Interior slave-raiding spread along the major river valleys and the Sao Francisco River corridor as coastal populations were depleted. By the late sixteenth century, slaving expeditions reached "two or three hundred leagues into the sertao." The interior colonies of Sao Paulo, Goias, Mato Grosso, Maranhao, Para, and Rio Negro sustained Indian slavery into the eighteenth century.

## Interaction

**Conflict with [[jesuit-aldeias]].** The slave economy and the Jesuit mission system were in permanent structural conflict. Colonists wanted access to aldeia residents as labor; Jesuits sought to shield them under crown protection. In the Caete Just War (1562), colonists "attacked and enslaving any Caete to be found, including those living in the Jesuit villages." The aldeia system's failure to supply adequate labor to settlers drove intensified slaving.

**Relationship to [[atlantic-slave-trade]].** Indian slavery was the economic foundation of the early sugar colony; African slavery replaced it as Indian populations collapsed. The two systems overlapped (some planters sought West African labor from early dates; the Schetz family of Sao Vicente is noted as an early case). The epidemiological mechanism was direct: "the introduction of large numbers of captives with little or no immunity to a new disease environment kept mortality rates always at high levels," accelerating the collapse that made African slavery economically necessary.

**Continuity with Andean [[mita]] and [[encomienda]]**: Brazilian Indian slavery operated without the formal encomienda institution of Spanish America, relying instead on Just War capture and resgate. However both systems extracted coerced indigenous labor for colonial commodity production. See also [[spiritual-conquest-and-colonial-church]] for the Jesuit dimension.

## End Conditions

Indian slavery ceased to be the primary labor system on the Brazilian coast during the first half of the seventeenth century, driven by the near-total demographic collapse of coastal Tupi peoples. Anchieta's grim accounting was already visible by 1583: "if you ask what happened to so many people, they will tell you they have perished." African slave imports through the [[atlantic-slave-trade]] filled the gap. Interior Indian slavery persisted where African labor was not yet economically viable, and was not fully suppressed until Pombal's reforms in the 1750s abolished the legal categories that had sustained it.
