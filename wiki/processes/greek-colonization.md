# Greek Colonization (Archaic)

The **Archaic Greek colonization movement** (c. 800–500 BCE) planted Greek city-states
on or off the coasts of the modern Spain, France, Italy, Sicily, Albania, Greece,
Bulgaria, Romania, Ukraine, Russia, Turkey, Egypt and Libya — "a new epoch in the
history of western man." It is the demographic and political counterpart of the
[[orientalizing-revolution|orientalizing revolution]], and the process by which the
Greek world expanded from the Aegean into a Mediterranean-and-Black-Sea-wide
civilization. [[boardman-cah-v3p3-1982|CAH Vol. III, Part 3]] (Ch. 37, A. J. Graham)
gives the synthetic treatment.

## Definition and scope

Colonization was practised in every period of Greek history; what distinguishes the
Archaic movement is **its scale** (rivalled only by Alexander's Hellenistic
foundations) and **its character — it was a product of the world of the independent
city-state, the [[city-state|polis]]**. The Archaic colony was not a "colony" in the
Roman or British imperial sense but a **new, fully independent polis** (*apoikia*,
"home away from home"): the citizen "shed the citizenship of his homeland from the day
he set sail." This is the crucial distinction from the *emporion* (trading post, such
as [[al-mina|Al Mina]] or chartered [[naucratis|Naucratis]]) — and Graham is sharp
that **Al Mina and Tell Sukas were not colonies at all** (no Greek graves, writing,
cults or architecture), only commercial contact-points.

## Sources and the chronology problem

The literary evidence (Herodotus, Thucydides, Strabo, Ps.-Scymnus, and the
chronographer **Eusebius**) is all post-Archaic; only the early poets (Homer,
Archilochus of Thasos) are contemporary. The great question is whether the precise
**foundation dates** are genuine records or artificial **generation-counts** (30–35
years/generation back-projected from king- and victor-lists). Graham defends the
dates: Thucydides' meticulous chronology of the Sicilian colonies (VI.3–5) resists
every attempt (van Compernolle's especially) to derive it from genealogy, and the
**archaeology of colonial cemeteries** — Greek painted pottery now independently dated
to the quarter-century — has repeatedly **vindicated the literary dates**, increasing
confidence in the whole tradition. Archaeology is decisive for chronology, town-plans
and material life, but treacherous for the human questions (who is buried here — Greek,
Hellenized native, or native buyer of Greek goods?), where the explicit literary
sources must guide interpretation.

## The act of foundation

Graham's reconstruction of the foundation process is the page's analytical core:

- **The decision.** Usually a public act of state by the mother city (a few
  **foundation decrees** survive on stone — Cyrene/Thera, Naupactus, Brea, Black
  Corcyra); sometimes a private venture (Miltiades the Elder to the Chersonese) or the
  flight of a defeated faction (Sinope).
- **The oikist.** The *oikistes* (founder) — normally a noble (Archias of Syracuse and
  Chersicrates of Corcyra were both Corinthian Bacchiads) — held complete authority,
  named the city, and led the venture; when a colony itself colonized, custom required
  it to fetch an oikist from its *own* mother city.
- **Delphi.** Approval of [[delphi|Apollo at Delphi]] was the indispensable preliminary
  — the god "directed" colonization in every foundation story (most of the oracles are
  later forgeries, but Apollo Archegetes' altar at Naxos shows the practice was early).
  The sanction gave a **moral title to other people's land**; failure was explained by
  a missing or misread oracle.
- **Recruitment and the voyage.** Settlers went as volunteers or, when the community
  decided it could not feed them, as **conscripts on pain of death** (Thera→Cyrene).
  Attested numbers are strikingly small (200 at Cyrene, 200 at Apollonia, 1,000 at
  Leucas); the voyage was in **penteconters (warships)** — colonization was "virtually
  a military undertaking." Rapid later growth came from **further drafts of settlers**
  from the mother city, not (as once thought) wholesale native admission.
- **The women question.** Herodotus's tale that the Ionian settlers of Miletus took
  no women but married murdered Carians' daughters is the standard "model" for
  men-only colonization with native wives — but Graham doubts it was the rule: where
  women's graves and names survive in colonies (Syracuse's Fusco cemetery) they are
  "just as clearly Greek as the men," and Greek priestesses are attested at Thasos and
  Massalia. Women probably followed once the colony was established.
- **Religion and layout.** The oikist took **fire from the mother city's sacred hearth
  (Hestia)** to kindle the colony's — symbolizing the colony as a continuation of the
  metropolis's life. He chose the site (offshore islands, peninsulas, headlands,
  river-mouths; water supply decisive), set aside sanctuaries, and — strikingly —
  **orthogonal town-planning appears as early as the seventh century** (Megara
  Hyblaea), long before Hippodamus. The land was divided **by lot** into allotments
  (*klēroi*); whether the shares were **equal** is debated (Classical practice and
  Megara Hyblaea suggest equality; the princely tombs of Cyme and Pithecusa show early
  nobility and inequality).
- **The oikist's hero-cult.** At his death the oikist became a **hero**, worshipped at
  his tomb as the immortal guardian of the foundation — attested early for Battus of
  Cyrene and archaeologically for Antiphemus of Gela.

## Relations with the mother city

The colony normally reproduced the metropolis's cults, calendar, dialect, script and
institutions, and was **from the first a separate, independent state**. The tie was
one of shared cult, ancestors and sentiment (the ancestral graves lay in the mother
city), making metropolis and colony **natural allies** — war between them was held
shameful. But the relationship had no fixed legal content: where a strong sea-power
(Corinth, later Athens) chose, it founded **dependent "imperial" colonies** with
metropolitan officials and obligations; Corcyra's bitter wars with Corinth show the
other pole. *Isopolity* (shared citizenship) and easy movement of domicile were common
between related communities.

## Relations with the natives

Outcomes ranged across a spectrum: foundation after **expelling** the natives
(Syracuse), or **by invitation** of a local ruler (Megara Hyblaea, perhaps Massalia);
reduction of natives to **serfdom** (the Cyllyrii at Syracuse, the Maryandini at
Heraclea Pontica); or Greek **political subordination** to a stronger native power
([[naucratis|Naucratis]] under Egypt, the [[scythians|Scythian]]-zone Pontic colonies).
Politically variable, the Greeks were almost everywhere **culturally dominant** —
"Hellenization was a concomitant of colonization" (only Egypt resisted), while
"barbarization" of the Greek communities did not occur. Whether mixed settlements were
common is the most contested question; the cities themselves tended to stay
thoroughly Greek, with mixture appearing in peripheral zones.

## Causation

Graham's analysis distinguishes **passive causes** (the opportunities — geography,
weak or welcoming natives, Greek seamanship) from the **active cause**, which must lie
in Greece itself. His argument is that the basic active cause was **overpopulation /
land-hunger**: people for whom conditions at home were intolerable (sometimes
conscripted because the community could not feed them); the Greeks themselves compared
colonization to the swarming of bees (Plato, Thucydides); and archaeology shows a
**marked population rise in later-eighth-century Greece**, exactly when the movement
begins. Most colonies and colonists therefore lived **predominantly by agriculture**
(Aristotle's primary modes of life; Gwynn's classic thesis). **Trade** was real and
sometimes decisive — a rare colony where it was negligible, many where it mattered, a
few where it was all-important — but proof that commerce was the *raison d'être* of a
given foundation can rarely be had. The right conclusion rejects **unitary
explanations**: livelihood was sought in various ways, agriculture for the majority.

## Why it succeeded

Graham closes by attributing Greek success to practical skill and superior seamanship
and soldiering, but **above all** to two things: the **polis** — a highly effective,
easily transplantable and adaptable social-political organization, more cohesive than
native rivals — and a strong **"culture pattern,"** the religiously grounded morale
that let men "believe in their gods and hence in themselves" enough to build permanent
new communities far from home.

## Interaction and legacy

Greek colonization runs **concurrent with** the [[orientalizing-revolution|
orientalizing revolution]] and the maturation of the [[city-state|polis]], and
**produces** the network of daughter-cities that carried Hellenic culture, the
[[alphabet]] and (via [[coinage]] and the silver-and-grain trade) the monetized
economy across the Mediterranean and Black Sea — the world the Persian Wars and the
Classical age inherited. Its great theatres are treated separately: the **western
Greeks** (Sicily and Magna Graecia), the **eastern Greeks** of Ionia, and the colonial
fringes of the [[thracians|north Aegean]], the Pontus, the Adriatic, and
[[cyrene|North Africa]].

## Hall (2014) — Archaic Greek World

[[sources/hall-history-archaic-greek-world-2014|Hall (2014)]] Ch. 5 (“New Homes Across the Seas”) revises Graham-style orthodoxy on foundation reliability, pots-and-peoples, and land-hunger monocausality without denying scale or agricultural motives.

### Foundation-story skepticism

Modern scholarship distinguishes post-Mycenaean “migrations” from eighth-century+ organized “colonization,” but ancient authors did not (Thucydides 1.12 uses *apoikia* for both western foundations and Ionian settlement within post-Trojan upheaval). Hall doubts that colonial traditions are qualitatively more trustworthy than migration legends. Accurate 300-year oral preservation of eighth/seventh-century foundations would uniquely distinguish western Greeks from Aegean Greeks who could not remember events so early. Sequential office lists are insecure before late-fifth-century antiquarian reconstruction; oikist hero-cult + annual foundation-recitation arguments are weak (hero honors often late or thin; Cyrene’s Battos platform ca. 600 is the best early case). Foundation dates often look **generational** (Thucydides’s 245 and 70 years ≈ 35-year generations), projected backward—interest in civic foundations is largely fifth-century and present-justifying. Archaeology shows **gradual footholds**, not instantaneous Classical-style foundations: Megara Hyblaea’s bulk Greek ceramics sit near Thucydides’s 728, but buildings rarely predate the seventh century and earliest burials slightly postdate first imports—small initial footholds, then a generation-plus trickle (analogous to early British New World settlement). Reading Megara Hyblaea’s grid/agora as planned “from day one” is teleological: only a handful of eighth-century houses survive; agora and road-grid responses likely early seventh-century growth.

### Pots ≠ peoples; multi-origin settlers

Early colonial imports vary little by colony: Corinthian wares dominate almost everywhere (Corinthian Gulf route preference). “Achaean”/NW Peloponnesian pottery appears in Achaean foundations *and* Locri, Siris, Taras, Otranto, Pithecusae, Cumae, and many Sicilian sites; Euboean and East Greek wares spread beyond named mother-cities. Predominant Corinthian pottery does not prove Corinthian commercial monopoly; diverse assemblages do not automatically prove mixed ethnicity—yet colonial diversity exceeds Old Greece and makes human movement along trade routes highly likely. Classical foundation stories simplify complex multi-group processes toward later dominant groups. Methone (Macedonia) hypogeum inscriptions (730–690) show multi-script, multi-origin settlers (Euboean, Boeotian/Argive/Ithacan/Cretan, almost certainly Attic letter forms; local Thermaic manufacture plus Euboean, Attic, Lesbian, Chian, Samian imports)—if typical, literary colonial accounts are not vastly different in kind from earlier migration traditions.

### Taras / Partheniai (Antiochus vs Ephorus)

Strabo preserves Antiochus and Ephorus on Taras with almost **no narrative overlap** beyond foundation timing (First Messenian War era) and settlers as Laconian **Partheniai**. Phalanthos dominates Antiochus and is absent from Ephorus (helot alliance; failed agora revolt; overseas colony). Later tomb traditions displace Phalanthos (Brentesion burial; bones scattered on the agora)—an embarrassed acknowledgment that the supposed oikist lacked physical presence in “his” city. The divergence is a case study in foundation-story unreliability, not a problem solvable by picking the “true” version.

### Al Mina and Pithecusae

**Al Mina** (Orontes): disputed pottery origins, dates, and meaning amid disturbed stratigraphy. Pendent semicircle skyphoi ca. 770–750; some central Euboean fabric. “No permanent Greeks” vs “Euboean enclave” extremes both overreach; temporary Greek-speakers including Euboeans are plausible, but not all Euboean wares arrived via Euboeans, and Al Mina should not be treated as uniquely Euboean. **Pithecusae** (Ischia) among the earliest permanent western settlements is also among the most distant—Mezzavia industrial complex processing Elba iron for Levantine luxury exchange; western contacts before permanent colonies (Scoglio del Tonno; early ninth-century Corinthian sherds at Otranto; pendent semicircle skyphoi in Italy/Sicily) show longer pre-apoikia interaction.

### Hunger, greed, inheritance, and empire — motives plural

Hall challenges **land-hunger orthodoxy** as monocausal: fertile colonial sites, Theran drought/Cyrene oracles, and eighth-century growth matter, but emigration is not the only or most obvious response to land shortage (birth control, delayed marriage, agricultural intensification); clear eighth-century overpopulation is hard to demonstrate; Corinth’s large fertile territory shows little intensive rural habitation; Achaea’s Dyme zone was only sporadically settled until the sixth century despite Achaean Italian foundations; Aegina lacked capacity yet first colony only after 520 (Kydonia), long supported by piracy; Megara Hyblaea’s early scale was no mass exodus relieving home overcrowding. **Partible inheritance** and sibling resentment (Hesiod *WD* 37–9, 376–7) help explain distant relocation; foundation traditions encode illegitimacy/marginal status (Partheniai; Battos; Myskellos) but formulaic oracles reduce independent credibility. **Commercial opportunity** is rehabilitated without reverting to “trade before the flag”: Hesiod’s father left Cyme “desirous of a good life”; Naucratis as trade port; choke-point sites (Zancle/Rhegium; Chalcedon/Byzantium); Phocaean far west; Sinope with little hinterland exploitation for two centuries; northern Black Sea grain export for Athens is Classical, not early Archaic. Underplaying commerce reflects Finley-style primitivism and anti-imperial trade historiography; Hall: no early official “flag,” not no trade. **Eastern imperial pressure** drives some waves (Milesian Black Sea under Lydian then Achaemenid pressure; Phocaeans under Harpagos ca. 540 → Elea; Pentathlos under Asian kings → Lipara). Land remained *one* motive among several; the first eighth-century wave was probably **not official state action**, and much later Archaic “colonizing” is similar (Dorieus private; Miltiades’ Thracian fiefdom; Athenian *klēroukhiai* only late sixth century, Chalcis ~506 as defensive outcome not pure expansionism). Overall picture: less official, less formal, more haphazard movement for various reasons over generations—not so qualitatively different from earlier post-Mycenaean mobility.

### Violence then integration

Initial foundations often involved violence/displacement (Syracuse, Sybaris, Taras, Locri), sometimes later serfdom (Syracuse; Heraclea Pontica), but within a couple of generations many contexts stabilize and integrate (Black Sea hinterlands; L’Amastuola mixed settlement ca. 675–fifth century with coexisting local and Greek ceramics/rituals)—refining Graham’s spectrum of expulsion / invitation / serfdom / subordination.

## From the Oxford History of the Classical World (1986)

**Forrest (Ch.1, [[sources/boardman-oxford-history-classical-world-1986|OHCW 1986]]).** What we “rather misleadingly call colonization” was often a state-organized enterprise sent in a direction that would further the mother-state’s interests, yet the *apoikia* **became an independent unit**, normally keeping no more than sentimental and religious ties. Motives must not be generalized: overpopulation, famine, politics, trade, farming, chance—“we must argue for confusion.” By c.600 Greeks were established in southern France, northern Africa, Egypt, the Black Sea and its approaches, the north Aegean coast, and above all Sicily and southern Italy. Corinth’s foundations of Corcyra and Syracuse (733 BCE) sit amid westward riches. Earlier Euboean metal-seeking and settlement (N Syrian coast before 800; Italy later) and the Lelantine War’s strains form the immediate prelude to denser late-eighth-century foundation activity.

**Murray (Ch.9).** By the classical fifth–fourth centuries the result was hundreds of Greek communities around Mediterranean shores “like frogs around a pond” (Plato), regarding the *polis* as the only truly civilized life despite huge economic variation (agriculture; trade in corn, oil, wine, slaves, metals; mercenaries; fortresses; shrines).

**Crawford (Ch.16).** From the eighth century southern Italy hosted Greek colonies from Cumae to Tarentum; Etruria developed advanced urban civilization by internal evolution plus largely **Greek** influence (language neither Greek nor Italic; cities remained separate polities)—colonization as a cultural force beyond Greek political borders.
