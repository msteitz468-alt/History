## Definition and Scope

The twentieth-century history of Andean indigenous peoples — Quechua and [[aymara-kingdoms|Aymara]] speakers in Bolivia, Peru, and Ecuador, and Mapuche, Paez, Guambiano, and others in Chile, Colombia, and Argentina — is a sustained process of resistance, coerced transformation, and eventual political mobilization. It encompasses the final phase of hacienda-era land expropriation, a peasantist interlude of agrarian reform in which indigenous identity was officially erased in favour of the class category "peasant," and then an unexpected resurgence of ethnic politics from the late 1960s onward that the chapter's author, Xavier Albó, calls "the return of the Indian."

As Albó notes at the outset, "the indigenous peoples of Colombia and Chile are also discussed here, although their situations are somewhat distinct," and intensifying cross-border Amazonian networks further complicate any purely national framing. The process spans rural haciendas, highland communities, Amazonian frontier zones, and growing urban migrant populations.

**Historiographical note.** Ch. 25 of the *Cambridge History of Native Peoples of the Americas* Vol. III Part 2 (Albó, 1999) carries an explicit advocacy dimension, written from the perspective of indigenous communities themselves rather than of states or elite society. Population statistics are acknowledged as unreliable; census data systematically undercount indigenous people because "many census respondents tend to hide information that could cause them problems."

## Causal Drivers

- **Hacienda expropriation (1880s–1930s).** Following the War of the Pacific, new railroad networks raised the value of Andean lands, intensifying land grabs from indigenous communities. In Peru's Puno region, haciendas quadrupled between 1879 and 1915. Systems of bonded labour — *yanaconaje* (Peru), *pongueaje* (Bolivia), *huasipungo* (Ecuador), *terraje* (Colombia), *inquilinaje* (Chile) — tied indigenous workers to estates.
- **Commodity booms and crises.** Tin (Bolivia), wool (Peru), cacao then banana (Ecuador), coffee (Colombia), nitrate and copper (Chile) each in turn restructured rural labour markets and indigenous land tenure, often adversely.
- **Chaco War (1932–1935).** Bolivia's defeat catalysed new political consciousness among indigenous soldiers forced into combat. An "undeclared parallel war" of community land defence ran concurrently with the front.
- **1952 Bolivian Revolution and agrarian reform.** The MNR government's nationalization of mines and [[agrarian-reform-bolivia-1953|Agrarian Reform Decree of 2 August 1953]] (signed at Ucureña, Cochabamba) dismantled the hacienda system but reframed indigenous identity as peasant identity, suppressing ethnic categories in official discourse.
- **Cuban Revolution (1959) and Alliance for Progress.** External pressure from the United States and domestic guerrilla unrest drove Ecuador, Peru, Chile, and Colombia to introduce agrarian reforms of varying depth between 1964 and 1973.
- **Urban migration.** Lima grew from under 700,000 inhabitants in 1940 to some 7 million by 1990. Migrants carried highland identity into coastal cities while remaining connected to their communities of origin, creating an urban indigenous middle class that became a major vector of ethnic politics.
- **Failure of agrarian reforms.** State-led reforms were widely perceived as substituting one boss for another. In Peru, Velasco Alvarado's SAIS and CAPS model excluded free-standing native communities and collapsed under the weight of political rejection, opening the door to Shining Path violence.

## Major Phases

### Phase 1: Resistance to Expropriation (1900–1930)

Communities organised through traditional authority networks to defend communal lands. In Bolivia a "cacique movement" — led by figures like Santos Marka T'ula and Eduardo Leandro Nina Qhispi — combined legal petitions (appealing to colonial land titles), ritual, and insurrection. Peru saw indigenist intellectuals (Valcárcel, Mariátegui) converge with highland rebellions. In Ecuador the hacienda system was embedded enough to forestall coordinated mobilisation. In Chile the Mapuche responded to post-1881 pauperisation through a new class of urban-educated leaders forming "societies" (Caupolican, Araucanian Federation, Araucanian Union).

In Colombia, Manuel Quintín Lame led the "Quintinada" from 1914 — a multi-ethnic, multi-decade insurgency in Cauca and Tolima that by 1939 had Lame claiming he had been imprisoned 108 times. He described himself as "the bola that spun and passed through the center of the wild herd."

### Phase 2: Unions and Agrarian Reforms (1930–1973)

The dominant framework shifted from ethnic community to peasant class. Unions (*sindicatos campesinos*) replaced [[ayllu|ayllus]] as the official unit of rural organisation. In Ecuador the Ecuadorian Federation of Indians (FEI, 1945) was linked to the Communist-led CTE labour federation. Indigenous identity was officially suppressed: "the terms 'Indian' and 'indigenous' came to sound passé and discriminatory and were replaced in all would-be progressive discourse by 'peasant'."

Bolivia's 1952 Revolution produced the most radical reform: peasants in the Cochabamba valleys independently seized haciendas in 1952, forcing the government's hand, and the Agrarian Reform Decree of August 1953 legalised mass land takeover. In some localities "peasant super-states" briefly displaced government authority. The MNR coopted unions through a "Military–Peasant Pact" from 1964, which eventually alienated Aymara communities.

### Phase 3: The Return of the Indian (1973–1995)

From the late 1960s onward, the collapse of the "peasantist" project and the partial failure of agrarian reforms created conditions for ethnic resurgence. Key institutions:

- **Bolivia — Katarismo (1969–1993).** The Aymara political movement that recovered "long-term memory" of colonial resistance; see [[katarismo]].
- **Ecuador — ECUARUNARI (1972) and CONAIE (1986).** ECUARUNARI ("awakening of the Indians of Ecuador") organised Quichua speakers. The Shuar Federation (1961) organised Amazonian groups. Both merged in CONAIE (1986), which mounted mass uprisings in June 1990 and June 1994, eventually forcing the government to negotiate in the presidential palace.
- **Colombia — CRIC (1971) and ONIC (1982).** The Regional Indigenous Council of the Cauca, born from a meeting of 2,000 Paez and Guambiano Indians in February 1971, combined land demands with defence of cabildos and indigenous language. ONIC united practically all Colombian indigenous peoples by 1982.
- **Chile — Mapuche explosion (1967–1973), then suppression.** The Allende government returned 68,381 hectares to Mapuche communities in 1971, passed the Ley de Indígenas (1972); Pinochet's 1979 Decree-Law 2568 reversed these gains, dividing 60 percent of undivided communities by 1986.
- **Pan-Andean symbolism.** The wiphala (rainbow flag) representing Qullasuyu/Tawantinsuyu became the movement's banner. The quincentennial of 1992 produced the shared motto "500 years of resistance."

## Geographic Spread

- **Bolivia and Peru** — the core of highland Aymara and Quechua mobilisation. Bolivia was furthest advanced in ethnic politics; Peru was paralysed by Shining Path violence (over 20,000 dead in the 1980s, mostly indigenous peasants).
- **Ecuador** — unique success of CONAIE in building a highlands–lowlands coalition; CONFENIAE joined highland ECUARUNARI in 1980.
- **Colombia** — CRIC and ONIC operated under extreme violence; more than 400 indigenous leaders murdered in Cauca by 1994.
- **Chile** — the Mapuche case in Araucanía; Aymara communities in the north (formerly Peruvian and Bolivian territory) also revived after 1990.
- **Urban diaspora** — La Paz, Quito, Lima, and foreign cities (New York, Amsterdam) became key nodes of the movement via migrant networks.

## End Conditions

As of 1995 (the writing of Albó's chapter), the process was ongoing rather than concluded. Bolivia had elected Aymara Katarista leader Victor Hugo Cárdenas as vice-president (1993); Ecuador's CONAIE had won bilingual education policy and forced presidential-palace negotiations; Colombia's 1991 constitution recognised ethnic and cultural diversity and reserved two Senate seats for indigenous peoples; Chile's post-Pinochet transition produced a new indigenous law (1993). But the fundamental asymmetry between state sovereignty and indigenous territorial demands remained unresolved across all countries.

The parallel economy of coca production (primarily Bolivia and Peru) had by the 1990s generated both powerful new indigenous organisations (coca-grower federations in Chapare) and fresh sources of state repression.
