# Egyptian Imperialism and Foreign Relations

The long development of Egyptian power beyond the Nile valley — the conquest and management
of Nubia and Syria-Palestine, and the diplomacy and trade that linked Egypt to the wider
ancient world. Ian Shaw's "Egypt and the Outside World" chapter in the
[[sources/shaw-oxford-history-ancient-egypt-2000|Oxford History]] is its synthetic statement.

## Definition and scope

Shaw groups Egyptian foreign contact into three arenas: **Africa** (Nubia, Libya,
[[places/punt|Punt]]), **Asia** (Syria-Palestine, Mesopotamia, Arabia, Anatolia), and the
**northern/eastern Mediterranean** (Cyprus, Crete, the Sea Peoples, the Greeks). The
ideology was framed by two words for "border": *djer* (the eternal, universal limit the king
was entitled to claim) and *tash* (the actual, movable geographical frontier). Every pharaoh
was in theory charged with "extending the borders."

## Two models: Nubia vs. the Levant

The volume stresses that Egypt ruled its two great spheres differently:

- **Nubia** was held by **direct colonial occupation**. The
  [[actors/middle-kingdom-egypt|Middle Kingdom]] fortress chain ([[places/buhen|Buhen]],
  Semna, Uronarti, Askut) gave Egypt "a stranglehold over Nile commerce" and doubled as
  granaried springboards into Africa; the [[new-kingdom-egypt|New Kingdom]] governed Nubia
  through a **Viceroy of Kush** and settled it with Egyptian temples and towns.
- **Syria-Palestine** was held more loosely, through a **vassal system**. Established by
  [[thutmose-iii|Thutmose III]] after [[events/battle-of-megiddo-1457bce|Megiddo]], it took
  the children of local rulers to Egypt "to be Egyptianized" and propped client kings on
  their thrones ("whoever died … his Majesty caused that his son stand in his place"). The
  [[concepts/amarna-letters|Amarna Letters]] reveal Egyptian intervention there as "limited
  police actions" to stop volatile vassals switching to Mitanni or Hatti. Whether this
  amounted to a residential occupation (governors and garrisons in three zones — Amurru, Upe,
  Canaan) or merely periodic pressure is **debated**, since the material culture of Palestine
  stays distinct from Egypt's (unlike Nubia's).

## Mechanisms

The empire ran on **tribute** (*inu*, recurring deliveries) and one-time **booty**, on a
royal bureaucracy "obtaining taxes and conscripted labour from the provinces of Egypt" and
"exacting tribute and spoils from the lands outside Egypt's borders," and on a deliberate
policy of **acculturation** — local elites encouraged (or compelled) to adopt Egyptian
customs and names. As Shaw concludes, "the overall image of Egyptian 'imperialism' … is
multi-faceted, the economic and political pragmatism of the pharaohs often being cloaked in
the hyperbole of royal rhetoric and piety."

## Interaction and end conditions

Imperial Egypt was one pole of the Late Bronze **great-power system** documented in the
Amarna Letters and the [[events/egyptian-hittite-treaty-1259bce|Egyptian–Hittite treaty]].
That system, and Egypt's Levantine empire, unravelled in the
[[events/bronze-age-collapse-1200bce|Bronze Age Collapse]]: after the [[actors/sea-peoples|Sea
Peoples]] and the fall of Hatti, "Egypt finally lost their provinces in Palestine and Syria,"
and the 20th Dynasty could no longer even reach the Nubian gold mines.
