## Definition and Scope

The twentieth-century transformation of the Australian environment constitutes a process of cumulative ecological damage — from deforestation, hydrological disruption, salinity, overfishing and invasive species — followed in its second half by a contested and partially successful conservation and environmental movement. This page covers the period from Federation to the present; for colonial-era environmental history see [[environmental-transformation-colonial-australia]], which this page succeeds.

The core dynamic is that "most Australians only discovered the fragility and uniqueness of the country's natural systems after agriculture, pastoralism, mining and fishing had transformed them" (Barton and Bennett, in [[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]]). White Australians had to learn what [[aboriginal-australians]] already knew: "that much of Australia is prone to drought, flood and fire."

## Causal Drivers

**Climatic volatility**: Australia is governed by the El Niño Southern Oscillation (ENSO). In an El Niño phase, warm Pacific water drifts east, bringing drought; in a La Niña phase warm water moves toward Australia, bringing rain. Major droughts affected large parts of the continent in 1895–1903, 1914–15, 1918–20, 1937–45, 1965–68, 1982–83 and 1991–95. The Federation Drought alone halved the national sheep population from 100 million to 50 million. Droughts "exacerbated economic slowdowns and recessions at the turn of the century, the early 1980s and early 1990s."

**Agricultural intensification**: Dam construction, irrigation networks, and soldier-settlement schemes (post-WWI and post-WWII) modified hydrological systems without understanding the salinity consequences of raised water tables. The Murray–Darling Basin — covering watersheds running from Queensland through NSW, Victoria and South Australia — was the primary site of these interventions. Clearing of Mallee eucalypts across north-west Victoria and South Australia (covering some 4.4 million hectares, a fifth of Victoria) for wheat farming removed the vegetation acting as a "reservoir and water pump that kept the water table from rising and mixing with the salt." Western Australia replicated this with its 'million acres a year' settlement plan from the 1950s to the 1970s.

**Invasive species**: European settlers introduced cane toads (1935, to control cane beetles in Queensland — they failed that purpose and spread into the Northern Territory by 1984, toxic to native snakes, lizards and crocodiles), rabbits, camels, foxes, myna birds, carp, and numerous grass and tree species. Rabbits were "protected even after it was widely known that they had become invasive." Altered ecologies also caused native species (cockatoos, lorikeets, galahs, noisy miners) to expand aggressively.

**Fire**: European immigrants and early foresters "did not at first recognise fire's ecological significance for forests and savannah." Mountain ash forests of Victoria (Eucalyptus regnans, growing to 90 metres) regenerate from fires every 60–100 years. The 1939 'Black Friday' fires burned 1.4 million hectares and killed 71 people; Judge Leonard Stretton's royal commission concluded the dead "had not lived long enough," meaning they had insufficient experience of the continent's natural rhythms.

**Urbanisation**: By 1900 Australia was already one of the most urbanised societies in the world, and continued to urbanise. This created the social conditions for conservation: "most activists in these movements lived in cities while most of their activities were concerned with non-urban environments," generating class and urban–rural tensions in environmental politics.

## Major Phases

### Phase 1: Damage and Utilitarian Conservation (1901–1945)
The first half of the century combined extensive ecological damage with a technocratic, utilitarian conservation response led by forestry professionals. The Australian Forestry School opened in Canberra in 1927. National parks were created in "iconic or desirable landscapes, particularly mountainous and forested regions" fitting North American or European aesthetics — Cradle Mountain–Lake St Clair (Tasmania), Kosciusko (NSW) — not desert or Mallee environments. The Quarantine Act (1908) built a fortress-like biological border. Commercial and recreational overfishing depleted tiger flathead off NSW (1915–1961), Murray cod in the Murray–Darling Basin (collapse of large-scale commercial fishing by the 1930s), and humpback and sperm whales from Albany, Western Australia post-WWII.

Australia's flora colonised other parts of the world: from the 1860s *Eucalyptus* and *Acacia* species were planted globally for timber; by the 1990s *Eucalyptus* composed approximately 20 per cent of the world's new plantations, challenging the view that "New biota did not colonise the 'Old World'."

### Phase 2: Biological Control and Continued Transformation (1945–1969)
The CSIRO released Myxomatosis in 1950, killing 99.8 per cent of the feral rabbit population — described as "the most spectacular magic bullet in pest control history" — increasing the wool clip by ~18 million kilograms by 1953. Rabbits recovered by the 1980s; a second control, RHDV, was released in 1995. Prickly pear (*Opuntia* spp.), which had colonised approximately 58 million hectares of Queensland and NSW by the 1920s, was brought under control by biological intervention.

Western Australia's whaling station at Albany targeted humpback and sperm whales. An international ban on humpback whaling (1963), local protests and economic losses eventually ended all whaling there in 1978 — Australia was the last English-speaking country to ban the practice.

Australian perceptions of the central deserts shifted: from J.W. Gregory's 'dead heart' (1906) to Henry Finlayson's 'red centre' (1935) to a spiritual landscape as tourism to Ayers Rock (Uluru) grew from the 1930s. Ayers Rock National Park was established in 1950; in 1985 the federal government returned Uluru to its Pitjantjatjara owners and signed a 99-year joint management lease.

### Phase 3: Environmental Movement and Legislative Response (1969–1992)
The conservation movement "realigned itself into a newly styled environmental movement" in the late 1960s–70s, "a popular movement that advocated a wider and more ambitious set of goals" — protecting nature for its own sake, not merely for human benefit. Key battles:

- **Little Desert (1969)**: Victorian government plan to develop scrubland for agriculture was defeated by a press campaign; the first major public environmental campaign purely on ecological grounds.
- **Lake Pedder (1972–74)**: Tasmanian government dammed the lake despite seven years of protest; the United Tasmania Group became Australia's first political party with an express environmental platform.
- **Fraser Island sand mining and Great Barrier Reef oil drilling**: both halted by environmental protest in the early 1970s.
- **Franklin River campaign (1978–83)**: plans by the Tasmanian Hydro-Electric Commission to dam the Gordon River generated national and international protest. The Gordon River was listed as a World Heritage Site in 1982. The incoming federal Labor government (1983) passed the *World Heritage Properties Conservation Act*, prohibiting construction — upheld in the High Court in *Commonwealth of Australia v State of Tasmania*, establishing federal power under the 'external affairs' clause of the Constitution.
- **Daintree rainforest**: World Heritage listed and protected 1988.
- **Antarctic minerals convention (1989–90)**: Australia successfully lobbied foreign governments to reject a proposed convention regulating Antarctic mining, instead sponsoring the Protocol on Environmental Protection to the Antarctic Treaty.

Environmental legislation multiplied from the 1970s; the High Court in *Commonwealth v Tasmania* (1983) upheld the Hawke government's blocking of Tasmanian plans to dam the Franklin River, Chief Justice Mason declaring in 1986 that "'ultimate sovereignty' resided in the Australian people."

### Phase 4: Native Title, Co-Management and Climate Change (1992–present)
*Mabo v Queensland (No 2)* (1992) and *Wik Peoples v Queensland* (1996) recognised native title, enabling [[aboriginal-australians]] to negotiate with mining companies and co-manage national parks. Kakadu National Park and Uluru–Kata Tjuta National Park are located on native title lands; the Northern Territory has nearly 50 per cent of its land under native title. Aboriginal people now "work as guides, help determine conservation strategies and receive park revenue."

Climate change became the dominant issue in Australian environmental politics from the late 1990s. Anthropogenic climate change "entered into national debates in the late 1990s, partly as a result of chronic declines in rainfall in south-west Western Australia." Prime Minister Howard refused to ratify the Kyoto Protocol (1997); Prime Minister Rudd ratified it as his first act in December 2007, then failed to pass a Carbon Pollution Reduction Scheme — a major reason for his removal in 2010. Prime Minister Gillard introduced a carbon tax and emissions trading scheme. Balancing coal export revenues against domestic emissions abatement "will continue to be a major challenge for the next decades of the twenty-first century."

## Geographic Spread

Concentrated in: the Murray–Darling Basin (salinity, water regulation); the Mallee regions of Victoria and South Australia (deforestation, salinity); south-west Western Australia (salinity, deforestation, rainfall decline); northern Queensland and Northern Territory (cane toads, invasive species, fire, pastoral disruption); Tasmanian wilderness (Franklin River, Lake Pedder); Great Barrier Reef (oil drilling, bleaching).

## End Conditions

The process is ongoing. Forest cover declined through the first half of the century but "turned around slightly towards the end of the century" due to timber plantations, reduced native logging, and expansion of woody vegetation in pastures. The environmental movement has achieved significant legislative and judicial victories. Climate change represents the unresolved, dominant challenge: Australia's per capita domestic carbon emissions are high, while coal exports to China and other countries create a far larger externally generated footprint that current policy does not seek to regulate.

## Historiography

The chapter by Gregory Barton and Brett Bennett in [[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]] deploys the American conservation/environmentalism distinction but notes that Australian historians challenge its dichotomies. Key interpretive works cited: Tom Griffiths (*Forests of Ash*, 2001; 'Environmental History' in Oxford Companion); Libby Robin (*How a Continent Created a Nation*, 2007; *Defending the Little Desert*, 1998); Drew Hutton and Libby Connors (*A History of the Australian Environmental Movement*, 1999). The 'fire-stick farming' debate — between Rhys Jones (1969), Eric Rolls, Tim Flannery and Bill Gammage arguing for intentional Aboriginal fire management versus researchers using palynology, archaeology and palaeoclimatology who find less frequency and intentionality — is presented as actively open. Indigenous environmental knowledge was "not incorporated into state environmental management models or popular interpretations of nature until the last decades of the twentieth century."
