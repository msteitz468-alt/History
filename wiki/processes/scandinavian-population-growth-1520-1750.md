## Definition and Scope

This process describes the demographic expansion of the Scandinavian kingdoms — Denmark-Norway and Sweden-Finland — from roughly 1520, when recovery from the late-medieval catastrophe was well under way, to the mid-eighteenth century, when a new sustained growth phase began after several generations of stagnation and crisis. The process encompasses rural settlement expansion, urbanisation, Finnish frontier colonisation, and the population crises caused by famine, war, and epidemic. Source: [[kouri-olesen-cambridge-history-scandinavia-v2-2016]], ch. 7 (Eljas Orrman).

## Causal Drivers

The primary driver was immunological: "the most devastating epidemic diseases towards the end of the Middle Ages and during the first centuries of the modern era became endemic in Europe" (L7610), reducing both the frequency and lethality of outbreaks. Scandinavia benefited additionally from Danish quarantine measures against disease spread from the south after the 1570s (L7614–7616).

Secondary drivers included:
- **Land availability**: the late-medieval demographic collapse had left enormous numbers of deserted farmsteads available for reoccupation (L6459–6462).
- **Crown colonisation policy**: Gustav Vasa declared wilderness lands crown property and granted them to settlers; Duke Karl (later Karl IX) offered tax exemptions to Finnish incomers in Sweden from c. 1580 (L6753–6756).
- **Improved living standards**: "successive improvements in living standards even in rural communities in the Scandinavian countries from the sixteenth century led to certain improvements in hygiene, which seem to have reduced infant mortality" (L7616–7619).

Limiting and reversing factors included climate deterioration ("a definite deterioration of the climate in Sweden around 1570, when the average temperature decreased by half a degree" — L7650–7652), recurring crop failures, heavy war conscription, and epidemic clustering.

## Major Phases

### Phase 1: Recovery and Growth (c. 1520–c. 1620)
All three major regions — Norway, Denmark, and Sweden — experienced strong net growth. Norway grew fastest: from c. 155,000 in 1520 to close to 440,000 by 1665, with annual rates of 0.7–0.9% overall and peaks of 1.0–1.2% in the southern Agder region (L6504–6511). Denmark's rate was roughly 0.5% p.a., reaching c. 820,000 (including the south Swedish provinces) by the mid-seventeenth century (L6579–6581). Sweden within medieval borders grew from 500,000–600,000 in the 1570s to c. 900,000 by the early seventeenth century (L6683–6686).

### Phase 2: Stagnation and Crisis (c. 1620–c. 1720)
"The whole of the seventeenth century in Europe was a period of stagnation and decline from the population perspective" (L6467–6468) and Scandinavia conformed to this pattern, though with regional variation. Denmark suffered a population decline of close to 20% in the 1650s as a result of war, famine, and epidemic (L6595–6596). During the latter half of the seventeenth century Denmark grew by only c. 40,000 (0.14% p.a.) (L6593–6594). Finland was catastrophically struck by the famine of 1695–7, which caused the deaths of c. 138,000 — 31–2% of a population of c. 450,000 — and was followed immediately by the Great Northern War and Russian occupation (L7700–7703, L6830–6834). Iceland experienced similar disaster: after peaking close to 55,000 in the mid-seventeenth century, a smallpox epidemic of 1707–9 reduced the population to 34,000 (L6670–6672).

The Swedish war machine exacted a separate demographic toll. "Half a million Swedish and Finnish men perished in military service under the Swedish banners between 1620 and 1719, corresponding to an annual loss of a third of the annual cohort of 20-year-old men" (L7773–7776). A case study of the north Swedish parish of Bygdeå illustrates the effect: of 236 men conscripted 1621–39, 215 died, the adult male population aged 15–60 fell by 40%, and women rose to 1.5 for every man (L7793–7799).

### Phase 3: Recovery (c. 1720 onwards)
Sweden's population within present borders reached c. 1.5 million around 1700 (L6702–6704). By the early eighteenth century Norway's population was around 500,000 (L8571–8572). Sustained recovery followed the end of the Great Northern War (1721).

## Geographic Spread

**Norway** (L6498–6563): Recovery began earliest in the fjord and inland districts of western Norway and Trøndelag, hardest hit by late-medieval depopulation. Outer coastal districts and south-east Norway (Østlandet) began growing later. North Norway fluctuated, with a quick sixteenth-century rise, early-seventeenth-century stagnation, then renewed growth from the 1650s.

**Denmark** (L6571–6661): Growth was strongest in northern Jylland and the southern Swedish provinces. In the agricultural core — the larger islands and western Skåne — an "unambiguous if moderate growth" in the sixteenth century was followed by stagnation by 1600. Noble manors in the east depressed settlement counts there.

**Iceland** (L6662–6673): Growth peaked after the mid-seventeenth century at c. 55,000 before the smallpox collapse of 1707–9.

**Sweden proper** (L6674–6731): Forested tracts expanded significantly; the south-western provinces (cattle-rearing dominant) grew at 0.8% p.a. 1560–1600. Northern marginal areas along the Gulf of Bothnia declined c. 1590 before partial recovery.

**Finland** (L6798–6965): Western arable Finland showed incipient stagnation by the mid-sixteenth century and then contraction from the 1570s (rural holdings fell from c. 36,000 to c. 28,000 between the 1570s and 1630s), owing partly to the Swedish-Russian war of 1571–95 (L6804–6816). The eastern burn-beating zone (Savo/Karelia) simultaneously entered "an extremely expansive phase" (L6847), colonising interior forests under the *huuhta* (slash-and-burn) system, and migrants spread from Savo across central Finland to the sixty-fifth parallel by 1580, covering c. 60,000 sq km with barely 1,000 holdings (L6903–6905).

## Interaction

**Finnish colonisation of Sweden and Norway**: From c. 1580, Savo settlers colonised the vacant coniferous forests of central Sweden, eventually reaching Värmland (c. 5,000 by 1700) and crossing into Norway (c. 1,000). Their *huuhta* burn-beating technique allowed rye cultivation on moraine soils unsuited to indigenous Swedish farming (L6752–6776). The crown later imposed restrictive decrees in 1647 fearing depletion of timber for the iron industry (L6768–6771). Some forest Finns emigrated to the Swedish colony of Delaware (1630–1650) (L6775–6776).

**Urbanisation**: Settlement was overwhelmingly rural. The urban share of Sweden's population rose from 4–5% around 1620 to 10% in the 1690s following mercantilist town-founding policies (29 new towns to the 1690s) (L7248–7278). Denmark's urbanisation was higher at 13.9% by 1645, dominated by København (60,000–70,000 by 1700, about half of Denmark's urban population) (L7201–7202, L7333–7335). Stockholm grew from c. 6,000 in the late sixteenth century to 57,000 around 1690 (L7337).

**Social differentiation**: Population pressure produced new landless or near-landless classes — cottars (*husmen* in Norway and Denmark) — who by the mid-1660s numbered 17,000 households in Norway (L6546). In Denmark by the 1680s cottars reached 40% of all households in northern Sjælland (L6649–6650).

**Demographic potential and military power**: The total population of the two kingdoms in the 1640s–1660s was: Danish monarchy c. 1.8 million, Swedish kingdom c. 1.4 million. At Sweden's greatest extent (post-1658), its population stood at c. 2.8 million, but "the country's medieval core area accounted for barely half of the population" (L7141–7143).

## End Conditions

Stagnation and crisis ended unevenly. The Peace of Nystad (1721) ended the Great Northern War and Russian occupation of Finland, allowing demographic recovery to resume. In Denmark-Norway a new growth phase opened around 1700–1720 after recovery from the 1650s wars. The mid-eighteenth century agricultural recovery and improved international markets eventually ended the long stagnation. The 1695–7 famine represented the last great pre-industrial mortality crisis for most of the region; the plague visited Scandinavia for the last time in 1709–11, entering from Russia (L7735–7737).
