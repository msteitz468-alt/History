## Definition and Scope

The **Secondary Products Revolution** is Andrew Sherratt's hypothesis (1981) that
a transformation in animal exploitation occurred in later Old World prehistory:
whereas Neolithic herding had focused on **primary products** (meat, hide, bone —
obtained by killing the animal), later societies turned to **secondary products**
obtainable from the *living* animal — **milk and dairy, wool, traction (plough,
cart), and riding**, plus manure. Sherratt placed this shift in the
Chalcolithic/Early Bronze Age, coincident with copper and bronze metallurgy, and
argued it underpinned population growth, new patterns of wealth and power, and
the move toward more complex societies. Its geographic focus was the eastern
Mediterranean and Near East. It is discussed in Ch. 6 of CWH Vol. II (Outram).

Sherratt did **not** claim milking, wool, or traction were *invented* then —
rather that economies *shifted emphasis* toward intensive secondary-product
exploitation at that time.

## How it is detected

- **Zooarchaeology** (Payne, Legge): herd age/sex "kill-off" profiles reveal
  husbandry goals — dairying culls most males young; meat husbandry culls males
  at full size; wool husbandry keeps animals alive longest.
- **Lipid- and protein-residue analysis** (Evershed's team, from the late 1990s):
  fats absorbed into unglazed pottery survive for millennia and can distinguish
  ruminant *milk* from *carcass* fat — direct chemical evidence of dairying.

## The major revision (why `disputed`)

Outram's key point: while zooarchaeology broadly supported Sherratt's Bronze-Age
timing in **southeastern Europe, Anatolia, and the Near East**, newer
**lipid-residue** data show **substantial dairying already in the early
Neolithic** in temperate zones — e.g. early Neolithic Britain (milk residues
dominant on causewayed-enclosure pottery, mature dairy cows) and northwestern
Anatolia (a very high proportion of Neolithic pots with milk residues). Dairying
clusters where cattle thrive ecologically, not on a single chronological horizon.

Consequences:
1. The "revolution" remains "reasonably valid" as an *intensification* for some
   regions (Greece, parts of the Near East) but was **not** a single, synchronous
   Old-World event.
2. Dairying needed a **shorter lead-in** than thought, so the
   [[secondary-products-revolution]] can **no longer be treated as a
   prerequisite** for the great steppe/plains [[pastoralism|pastoral cultures]] —
   their genesis must be explained otherwise.

Barriers that did have to be overcome included sustaining milk let-down in
primitive breeds and human **lactose intolerance** (lactase-persistence alleles
were absent in early Neolithic Europeans — see [[archaeogenetics]]).

**European archaeological perspective from Bogucki & Crabtree (eds.) 2004 (Range 01 extraction):** In central Europe the process began in the Final Neolithic with innovations enabling greater diversification: exploitation of animals for secondary products (milk/dairy, power for traction, wool) and introduction of plowing with wooden ards. "These innovations made possible a greater diversification of subsistence strategies reflected by changes in land use, occupying a wider range of locations. In many areas pastoralism and transhumance seem to have gained greater importance, with possibly larger numbers of animals kept for their primary and secondary products. This tendency seems to be even more pronounced in the Middle Bronze Age, as reflected by a much more dispersed settlement pattern." Crops show continuity + intensification (emmer/einkorn/barley dominant; increase in barley possibly for beer/rituals; new spelt/millet/oats/broad beans). "One major change was an increase in the exploitation of the horse—which remained fairly rare after its introduction in the Final Neolithic—suggesting an increase in its use as a traction animal and for riding." Evidence from sites like Feudvar (MBA) includes carbonized grains, sickles, loom weights, grinding stones. Ritual/ideological dimensions tied to feasting (drinking cups in hoards/pits) and ancestor places. Cross-links to Bronze Age prestige economies and social differentiation. (verbatim: "The Bronze Age witnessed an intensification in the agricultural practices carried down from the Neolithic, a process that began in the Final Neolithic with the introduction of a number of important innovations sometimes termed the “Secondary Products Revolution”" from Range 01 Central Europe article.) Complements lipid data revisions; emphasizes regional intensification in temperate Europe rather than single horizon. ## Interaction

- Builds on [[domestication]] and feeds [[pastoralism]].
- The traction component (plough, cart) intensified cereal [[agricultural-origins|agriculture]]
  and fed into [[agriculture-and-urbanism|urban]] surplus economies.
- Bridges the [[neolithic]] and the [[chalcolithic]]/[[early-bronze-age]] *(period pages forthcoming)*.

## End Conditions

Merges into the established mixed-farming and pastoral economies of the Bronze
Age; the secondary-products complex (dairy, wool, traction, riding) becomes a
permanent feature of Old World agriculture.
