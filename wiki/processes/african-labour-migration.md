# African Labour Migration

## Definition and Scope

African labour migration refers to the large-scale movement of workers — primarily but not exclusively male — within and beyond Africa, driven by the demands of extractive industries, colonial cash-tax regimes, and commercial agriculture from the late nineteenth century onward. It encompasses both forced and voluntary migration, seasonal and permanent relocation, and encompasses the entire spectrum from compound mine labour in South Africa to small-scale artisanal migration in West Africa.

The historiography of work and migration in Africa has dominated research since the early twentieth century but is "riddled with blind spots" — above all, the equation of work with wage labour in industry, mines, and railways, which has created a bias towards male workers and excluded women, small-scale entrepreneurs, and the vast majority of Africans who never entered formal waged employment (Emily Lynn Osborn, [[parker-reid-oxford-modern-african-history-2013]], ch. 11).

## Historical Roots and Scale

Pre-colonial and nineteenth-century roots of migration were more extensive than the "primitivist" colonial paradigm acknowledged. Patrick Harries showed that Mozambican men travelled great distances to access markets and opportunities in hunting and trading before the South African mines opened — making them ready recruits rather than immobile populations. François Manchuelle demonstrated that Soninke peoples in present-day Mali migrated to coastal areas for work and commerce well before French colonisation.

By the early twentieth century, migration to the mines had become a dominant feature of the southern African economy. In some regions it was not uncommon for 80 per cent of all adult men to leave for over a year at a time. Urban populations grew dramatically: Lagos went from 99,700 (1921) to 230,000 (1950); Dakar from 30,000 (1926) to 305,000 (1953).

## Phases of Scholarship

### The 'Primitivist' Interpretation (early 20th century)

The earliest writing on migration — concentrated on South Africa following the diamond (1867) and gold (1886) discoveries — promoted what Emily Osborn calls the "primitivist" interpretation. Three tenets anchored it:

1. Africans lived a static, unchanging existence prior to European contact — neither capable of nor interested in progress
2. Fixed gender norms (men herd cattle; women cultivate) liberated men to work in mines while sustaining rural households
3. Males migrated only temporarily — tribal bonds meant Africans could not or would not survive long in town or mine

This interpretation justified colonial indirect rule (Africans are essentially rural and tribal people directed by chiefs), and reached its most virulent form in South African Apartheid, which from 1948 designated all Africans to tribal "homelands" ruled by chiefs.

### Modernization Theory (1940s–1960s)

Modernization theorists — including French and British colonial officials and the anthropologists of the Rhodes-Livingstone Institute — refuted the primitivist myth while still seeing capitalism and industrial development as a universal, progressive pathway.

Max Gluckman argued that "detribalization" was instantaneous: as soon as a migrant "crosses his tribal boundary to go to town," he is an urbanite, not a tribesmen. This helped change mining company policy in Northern Rhodesia in the 1930s, moving from short-term contracts and compound housing towards "stabilization programmes" permanently settling miners and their families.

Modernization theory emphasised a Western-educated middle class as the key to political development, neglecting small-scale entrepreneurial activities and women.

### The Marxist Interpretation (1970s–1980s)

Marxist historians argued that capitalism was the motor of economic, social, and political change. Gutkind, Cohen, and Copans (1978): "all over the continent, the general laws of capitalist development were set in motion by the colonial process." Africans were "wrenched from their previous modes of production and reproduction and constrained to place their labor power at the disposal of alien forces of production."

Key studies:
- Charles van Onselen's *Chibaro* (1976) on Southern Rhodesian gold miners: brutal compound conditions gave rise to workers' associations, dance groups, burial societies, and mutual aid societies — indirect forms of solidarity and class consciousness. "Market intelligence" shared among miners exercised a significant check on mine owners.
- Ibrahim Abdullah's study of Sierra Leonean iron ore miners: connections forged in palm-wine bars and off-site housing enabled strikes in 1935 and 1938.

Labour activism, in this interpretation, planted seeds for nationalist movements — confirmed by the fact that some African independence leaders (e.g. Sékou Touré of Guinea) entered politics through trade unions.

**Critique**: capitalism often emerges in this literature as a "monolithic power that often acts in its own right, independently of human action and initiative." The framework is more applicable to South Africa's industrialised mining economy than to the rest of the continent where wage labour remained the exception.

### Social History (1980s–present)

Social historians broadened the frame beyond wage labour:

**Migration**: pre-colonial traditions of mobility undercut the primitivist premise. Internal dynamics as important as external pressures.

**Agriculture**: Polly Hill's 1963 study of Ghanaian cocoa farmers refuted the conceit that African farmers lacked "economic foresight" or were incapable of accumulating or expanding. Farmers acted on capitalist principles of profit motive and market forces while deploying local resources such as patron-client networks and kinship ties.

Sara Berry's study of Yoruba farmers showed that the Marxist principle — accumulation requires ownership of the means of production — did not explain Yoruba commercialisation. Farmers who did not own land or have access to capital still grew cocoa by drawing on kinship networks and compensating help with foodstuffs or future harvests.

**Slavery and its demise**: the tepid colonial commitment to abolition meant slavery declined erratically from the early 1900s to the 1930s, as slaves abandoned masters, renegotiated servitude, or died, leaving behind a younger generation who refused to shoulder parental obligations. Colonial forced labour simultaneously undermined abolitionist rhetoric.

**Women**: feminist historians from the 1970s used life histories and oral interviews to recover women's labour. Luise White's study of prostitution in colonial Nairobi showed female sex workers reproducing "the comforts of home" — domestic services for male migrants — and investing earnings in property to become landlords. Gracia Clark documented women traders in Kumasi's central market. Women perform many kinds of work both inside and outside the household; their domestic labour constitutes "work."

**The "informal sector"**: Keith Hart coined the term in 1973. Historians have questioned the sharp formality/informality distinction, arguing it obscures "the structured systems of organization and apprenticeship that govern many of these activities." Trevor Marchand's study of masons in Djenné (Mali) shows highly skilled workers whose practice is informed by deeply rooted systems of knowledge and production as well as new technologies and shifting market demands.

## Labour and Decolonisation

Frederick Cooper (*Decolonization and African Society*, 1996): African workers were not an undifferentiated mass drawn together by uniform grievances, but a diverse group. Labour movements did play an important role in the road to independence — not because of strong ties between trade unions and nationalists, but because "union demands and effective strike action helped expose the competing claims that the colonial state made to development, progress, and civilization." The colonial state could not respond to demands that would put workers on par with metropolitan workers "without dismantling the justification of colonialism and its fundamental distinction between 'subjects' and 'citizens'."

Yet only 1–2% of the population worked for wages in the 1930s–1940s.

## Late Twentieth and Twenty-First Centuries

James Ferguson's *Expectations of Modernity* (1999): on the Zambian Copperbelt, the pathway to working-class stability and respectability promised by mining employment was shattered by structural adjustment programmes and fluctuating global copper prices from the 1970s. "Progress," once achieved, did not simply give way to more advanced stages of development. Workers on the Copperbelt have "a clear sense of what it means to be 'modern' and 'developed'" — but for most it "remains elusive, a moving target that is ever-distant from their precarious daily lives."

Job insecurity is more the norm than the exception. Many African countries have become increasingly dependent on remittances from citizens living abroad. Chinese investment in African mines and factories raises old debates in new form — viewed either as modernisation bringing industrialisation benefits, or as self-interested exploitation of labour.

## Future Directions

- The history of Africa's professional middle classes — at the intersection of competing values, intellectual traditions, and modalities of rule — demands more historical analysis
- Connections and disjunctures between contemporary human trafficking and older forms of slavery, pawning, and captivity
- Forms of productive activity that have not made a firm imprint on colonial or postcolonial archives: tailors, mechanics, hairstylists, aluminium casters, basket weavers
