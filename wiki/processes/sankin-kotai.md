# Sankin-kōtai (Alternate Attendance)

*Sankin-kōtai* ("alternate attendance") was the requirement that every daimyo in [[japan|Japan]]
maintain a residence in Edo, spend alternating years attending the Shogun in the capital and
residing in his domain, and leave his wife and children as permanent hostages in Edo. Generalized
to all daimyo by the third Shogun Tokugawa Iemitsu in 1635 (formalized in the expanded
[[buke-shohatto|Buke Shohatto]] of that year), it was the single most effective mechanism of
Tokugawa control over the daimyo, described by Cambridge History of Japan scholar John Hall as
"the most effective mechanism developed by the Tokugawa shoguns." It defined the political economy
and urban geography of the [[edo-period|Edo period]] for over two centuries.

## Definition and Scope

The system required each daimyo to:
- **Maintain two residences** — one in his domain, one in Edo — stocked, staffed, and ceremonially
  maintained according to his rank.
- **Alternate annually** — spending one year in Edo in attendance on the Shogun, then returning to
  his domain for one year. The precise schedule varied by daimyo category and region.
- **Leave family as hostages** — wives and heirs remained in Edo permanently, never accompanying
  the lord to his domain. This was the decisive coercive element.
- **Travel with appropriate retinue** — processions between domain and capital were elaborate and
  expensive status displays; the [[buke-shohatto|Buke Shohatto]] regulated maximum retinue sizes
  to prevent intimidating concentrations of armed force.

## Origins and Development

The practice of requiring powerful lords to attend a hegemon's capital — and leave family as
security — had deep roots in Japanese feudal practice. Ieyasu had employed hostage-holding within
his own vassal band from his earliest days as lord of Mikawa. He reportedly told Hidetada before
his death in 1616 to "keep the daimyos in Edo for three years" after his death to prevent any
disorder — a temporary version of the logic that would become permanent policy.

The 1611 daimyo oath bound lords to obey Shogunal ordinances; the 1615 Buke Shohatto (article 9)
regulated the size of retinues when daimyo came to Edo "to render service," citing the *Shoku
Nihongi*: "no one is to assemble his clan within the capital or go about attended by more than
twenty horsemen." The full *sankin-kōtai* obligation — annual alternation with permanent family
hostages — was made universal by Iemitsu in 1635, twenty years after Ieyasu's initial legislation,
confirming that the mature Tokugawa system was built incrementally rather than designed whole.

## Mechanisms of Control

**Hostage logic:** With wife and children permanently in Edo, any daimyo contemplating rebellion
faced the immediate destruction of his family. This made the system self-enforcing without
continuous policing.

**Financial drain:** The cost of maintaining two full establishments — Edo residence (mansion,
staff, guards) and domain headquarters — plus the biennial procession with hundreds or thousands of
retainers was enormous. Major *tozama* daimyo might spend thirty to eighty percent of domain revenue
on *sankin-kōtai* obligations. This structural impoverishment was not an accidental side effect: a
daimyo too poor to stockpile weapons, build fortifications, or hire armies could not threaten the
Shogunate even if he wished to.

**Affirmation of hierarchy:** The alternate attendance system put every lord in personal contact
with the Shogun and his court on a regular basis, "affirming Edo's centrality, enabling constant
personal communication" (Hall). The processions themselves were public performances of Tokugawa
hegemony, with precise rules about who could cross whose path, who must dismount, and how many
men could accompany a lord of each rank.

**Intelligence:** The permanent presence of daimyo and their retainers in Edo gave the bakufu
continuous access to information about domain affairs; the families left in Edo were both hostages
and inadvertent informants.

## Economic and Urban Consequences

The *sankin-kōtai* was the primary driver of Edo's growth into one of the world's largest cities
by the eighteenth century. The Edo residences (*yashiki*) of hundreds of daimyo, each maintaining
large staffs, created enormous demand for goods and services. The biennial processions sustained
the great highway network (Tokaido, Nakasendo, and other routes), generating post-town (*shukuba*)
economies along their length. The need for cash to meet Edo expenses drew domain economies into the
monetized market, accelerating the growth of commercial activity documented in the [[tokugawa-economy|Tokugawa
economy]]. The *chonin* (townspeople) culture of [[chonin-culture|Edo, Osaka, and Kyoto]] was
partly a product of the spending power generated by the daimyo attendance system.

## End Conditions

The *sankin-kōtai* obligation was suspended in 1862, in the final years of the Tokugawa Shogunate,
as part of a series of concessions made under the pressure of the *sonnō jōi* ("Revere the Emperor,
Expel the Barbarians") movement. The suspension freed domain resources and military capacity that
had been locked into Edo attendance — a critical precondition for the domain coalitions that
overthrew the Shogunate in the Meiji Restoration of 1868.

## Historiography

The *sankin-kōtai* is treated in the Cambridge History of Japan (Hall) as the most important
single mechanism of the [[bakuhan-system|bakuhan system]], superior even to castle-building
controls and the [[buke-shohatto|Buke Shohatto]] prohibitions in practical effect. The
[[sadler-shogun-tokugawa-ieyasu-1937]] treats it as an outgrowth of Ieyasu's deathbed advice to
Hidetada (to keep daimyo in Edo after his death), though the formal system was implemented by
Iemitsu. The economic consequences — daimyo impoverishment, Edo growth, road development — have
been extensively studied by Japanese economic historians since the postwar period.
