# Industrialization

## Definition and Scope

**Industrialization** is the transformation of economies from organic, muscle- and
biomass-powered production toward mechanized, fossil-fuel-powered, factory-based
production, accompanied by sustained per-capita growth, urbanization, and rising
material throughput. Beginning in Britain in the later eighteenth century, it spread
over two centuries to become the dominant mode of production for most of humanity.
This page treats industrialization as a **global, multipolar process** — the framing
Kaoru Sugihara gives it in [[mcneill-cwh-v7-2015|CWH VII]] — rather than a single
European event diffusing outward.

## Causal Drivers

Sugihara's organizing argument: industrialization occurred wherever the interaction
of **environment, technology, and institutions** successively **released local and
regional resource constraints**. Before 1800 most agrarian societies in Eurasia
lived within nature-imposed limits (the "Malthusian trap"); the question is how
those limits were broken, and the answer differed region by region.

- **The Western path** ("coal and North America," after Pomeranz): the early
  mechanization of cotton textiles (the 1750s–1770s innovations; labor cost of
  spinning fell 97% by 1836), the steam engine pumping water from coal mines, the
  fiscal-military state, and the resource bonanza of New World land and Old World
  coal. Embedded in capital-intensive **mixed farming**, Europe leaned toward a
  **capital- and resource-intensive, labor-saving** technological path — encouraged
  by high wages and integrated capital markets (cf. [[great-divergence]],
  [[technology-and-innovation]]).
- **Contingency of coal:** Wrigley and Allen stress that early steam engines were so
  fuel-wasteful they made sense only where coal was extremely cheap at the pithead;
  the local availability of coal "was largely a matter of chance."

## Major Phases

1. **British industrial revolution (c. 1750–1830):** cotton, coal, steam — but as
   late as 1830 Britain used as much waterwheel as steam power. The "first act."
2. **Diffusion in the long nineteenth century:** to continental Europe, the
   [[united-states|United States]], and Meiji Japan; the **second industrial
   revolution** (c. 1870–1914) of steel, chemicals, and electricity.
3. **Interwar and state-led industrialization (1914–1945):** socialist and
   developmental models of varying success (see [[world-wars-era]]).
4. **Post-1950 acceleration and the Asian path:** capital-intensive heavy industry
   in Asia was increasingly absorbed into a more **labor-intensive, employment-
   generating** industrialization that spread fastest of all, making most of
   humanity industrial or industrializing by 2000 (see [[great-acceleration]],
   [[cold-war]], [[contemporary]]).

## Sugihara's labor-intensive path

CWH VII's signature contribution is the **labor-intensive industrialization** thesis.
In land-scarce, labor-rich East Asia (the Lower Yangtze, Tokugawa Japan), the peasant
family economy developed a multi-occupational, skill- and labor-absorbing pattern —
intensive rice farming plus household proto-industry — analogous to the European
[[industrious-revolution|industrious revolution]] but more thoroughgoing. When modern
industry arrived, this region adapted it along a **labor-intensive, resource- and
energy-saving** route distinct from the Western capital-intensive one. Western
technology became global "not because [it was] universally applicable, but because
local and regional efforts neutralized [its] cultural and environmental specificity":
industrial diffusion was **the result of multipolar agency**, not one-way Western
impact. Asian merchants, Sugihara notes, probably handled the majority of regional
trade worldwide even in 1840.

## Geographic Spread

Britain → northwestern Europe and the northeastern US → Japan → (post-1950) East
Asia broadly, then South and Southeast Asia and beyond. The reversal this implies —
the return of Asia, which held ~50% of world population and output before 1800, to
the center of global production — is the empirical core of the revisionist
[[great-divergence|Great Divergence]] account.

## Interaction

Industrialization is inseparable from the [[fossil-fuel-energy-transition]] (its
energy basis), the [[great-acceleration]] (its post-1950 climax), the
[[anthropocene]] (its environmental signature), [[urbanization]] (its spatial form),
the [[demographic-transition]], and [[agricultural-transformation-modern|the modern
agricultural transformation]] that released farm labor to industry.

## End Conditions

Ongoing and still spreading. Sugihara closes by asking whether globally diffused,
resource-intensive industrialization is compatible with environmental sustainability
— linking the process directly to the [[anthropocene|Anthropocene]] predicament.
