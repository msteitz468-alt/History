# Nazification of German Education (1933–1939)

The remaking of German schools, teachers, youth organizations, and
universities in the service of race and war. Based on Evans, *The Third Reich
in Power* (2005), ch. 3.

## Definition and Scope

Hitler's educational theory (*Mein Kampf*): physical hardening and character
over "mere knowledge," culminating in military service — canonized in the 1935
Nuremberg formula every Hitler Youth had to memorize: "slender and supple,
swift as greyhounds, tough as leather and hard as Krupp steel."

## Major Phases

**Purge and coordination of teachers (1933–36).** The 7 April 1933 civil
service law removed politically and racially unwanted teachers (Prussia: 157
of 1,065 male secondary heads; 32% of women secondary heads; Berlin's Karl
Marx School lost 43 of 74 teachers). The National Socialist Teachers' League
grew from 12,000 (Jan 1933) to 97% of Germany's ~300,000 teachers by 1936,
running mandatory political-education camps (215,000 trained by 1939). The
leadership principle made heads "Leaders" and staff their "retinue";
"school assistants" served as classroom political spies ("like the appendix:
useless and easily inflamed").

**Curriculum.** History rebuilt around "heroism in its Germanic form"
(guidelines 30 July 1933) — objectivity dismissed as "a fallacy of
liberalism"; biology reoriented to heredity, "racial teaching," and
population policy; arithmetic textbooks set problems on the cost of
maintaining asylum patients; documented pupil essays parroted *Stormer*
antisemitism. Physical education rose to 5 hours/week by 1938; employers
complained of collapsing literacy and numeracy by 1939.

**The Hitler Youth vs. school and family.** From 18,000 members (1930) to 2.3
million (end 1933), ~4m (1935), 8.7m (early 1939 — near the whole 10–18
cohort); legally compulsory from 25 March 1939. Direct paramilitary training
(1938: 45,000 Naval-HY, 74,000 in Flying Units, 1.2 million in small-calibre
shooting instruction). Its indiscipline undermined the schools it was meant to
complement — uniforms in class, denunciation of teachers, brutal camp
punishments (documented cases include a 1935 sexual assault and killing of a
boy at camp, hushed up by arresting his mother). Evans's verdict: ideological
indoctrination often superficial; the deeper effect was the erosion of every
authority except the regime's — a Social Democratic observer summarized the
whole apparatus as serving "one will... the will to war."

**Elite schools.** Napolas (from 20 April 1933, SS-run in practice, 16 by
1939); Adolf Hitler Schools (from 1937, party-run, no exams); Order Castles
(Falkenburg, Vogelsang, Sonthofen) for the party's future "Junkers"; the Reich
School Feldafing. All together they educated only ~6,173 pupils by September
1939 — an elite project more symbolic than demographic, with documented low
intellectual standards.

**Universities.** Enrollment collapsed from ~104,000 (1931) to ~41,000 (1939)
— Evans's multi-causal analysis: the WWI birth-trough, the pull of the
expanding officer corps (2% of male graduates joined the army in 1933, 28% by
1937), labour-service waiting periods, Jewish exclusion (~3–4% of the
decline), and the deliberate restriction of women (10% admission cap from
January 1934; girls' Gymnasium education abolished 1937; female students fell
from ~17,000 to under 6,000). Overtly Nazi content stayed thin (<5% of
lectures, ≤15% of dissertations), but new racial-studies institutes spread to
12 of 23 universities; "Aryan physics" (Lenard, Stark) failed to capture the
discipline (6 of 81 chairs by 1939) yet destroyed theoretical physics at
Munich by blocking Heisenberg; the historians' "unpolitical" nationalism
(war-innocence 1914, eastward colonization as civilizing mission) dovetailed
with Nazi revisionism without needing coordination. Medicine became the
dominant faculty (49% of students by 1939) as racial hygiene corroded medical
ethics — Evans draws the explicit line from 1930s "preventive medicine"
rhetoric to sterilization and, eventually, murder.

## End Conditions

Ongoing at the outbreak of war; the war economy then cannibalized the system
it had built (shortened courses, teacher shortage of ~3,000 unfilled posts by
1938 against 2,500 annual graduates vs. 8,000 needed).
