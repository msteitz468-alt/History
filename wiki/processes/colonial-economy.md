# The Colonial Economy in Africa

The **colonial economy** was the system by which European powers reorganized African economies to serve
**metropolitan needs** — extracting raw materials and minerals, growing export cash crops, and mobilizing
African land and labour by force. Documented for the wiki through the **UNESCO
[[gha-vol7-colonial-1880-1935|General History of Africa, Vol. VII]]** (chs. 12–15), which treats it as the
core mechanism of colonial **exploitation** and a root of Africa's later underdevelopment.

## The logic: colonies must pay

The cardinal rule of colonial economics was that **each colony should be financially self-supporting** and
yield raw materials and markets to the metropole. This dictated an economy built **outward** — railways and
ports oriented from the interior to the coast (to export, not to integrate African regions), a cash economy
imposed through **taxation** (the hut and poll taxes that forced Africans to earn money), and the
subordination of African production to European demand.

## Cash crops, mines, and forced labour

GHA VII surveys the variants across the empires:

- **Cash-crop economies** — peasant-grown export crops (cocoa in the Gold Coast, groundnuts in Senegal,
  cotton, palm oil) and **settler/plantation** crops (coffee, sisal, tea in East and Central Africa) that
  often displaced food production and tied farmers to volatile world prices.
- **Extractive/mineral economies** — the gold and diamonds of Southern Africa, the **copper** of Katanga
  and the Rhodesian Copperbelt, built on the **migrant-labour and compound system** (see
  [[southern-african-mineral-revolution]]).
- **Forced and coerced labour** — from the atrocities of the **[[congo-free-state|Congo Free State]]** and
  Portuguese *chibalo* to corvée labour on roads and railways and the contract-labour systems that
  underpinned settler agriculture and the mines.

## Consequences

The colonial economy left Africa with **export-dependent, externally-oriented economies** — producing a
narrow range of primary commodities for world markets they did not control, with little industry, and with
infrastructure designed for extraction rather than development. GHA VII (and the dependency tradition, cf.
[[impact-of-atlantic-slave-trade-on-africa|Rodney/Inikori]]) reads this as the **"development of
underdevelopment"** — the structural foundation of post-colonial economic dependency. Against this,
historians also note African **agency** within the colonial economy (peasant cash-croppers, migrant
workers, traders) and real, if uneven, growth.

## Continuity into independence: neo-colonialism (GHA VIII)

The **UNESCO [[gha-vol8-since-1935|General History of Africa, Vol. VIII]]** (Sections III–IV) shows the
colonial economy's **structures outliving colonial rule**: the new states inherited export-dependent,
externally-controlled economies, so that political independence did not bring economic independence —
**Nkrumah's "neo-colonialism"** (see [[post-colonial-africa]]). The "development of underdevelopment"
thesis (Coquery-Vidrovitch in the volume; Rodney) treats the colonial economy as the direct origin of
Africa's post-colonial dependence and the 1980s debt crisis.
