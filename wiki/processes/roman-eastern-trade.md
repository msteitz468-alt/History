## Definition and Scope

Roman eastern trade denotes the flow of luxury goods — spices, incense, textiles, gems,
ivory, and (to a lesser extent) silk — between the Roman Mediterranean and Arabia, India,
and (indirectly) China, from roughly the 1st century BCE through the 6th century CE. It
covers both the Red Sea/Indian Ocean sea route (Egypt–Arabia–India) and the Persian
Gulf/overland route (Mesopotamia–[[palmyra|Palmyra]]–Gulf), and the much thinner, largely
indirect contact with China and Central Asia. It does *not* include the movement of Roman
military or diplomatic personnel eastward, nor intra-Mediterranean trade.

This page draws on John Ball's *Rome in the East* (2000), whose account of this trade is
explicitly revisionist on several points (flagged as **THESIS** below) and departs from
older, more triumphalist narratives of direct Roman commercial penetration of Asia.

## Causal Drivers

Roman demand for eastern luxuries — especially pepper and other spices, frankincense and
myrrh, Chinese silk, Indian textiles and gems, and African ivory — was met primarily
through **existing** Arabian, Iranian, and Indian networks that predated significant Roman
involvement, rather than being created by Roman initiative. Knowledge of the Indian Ocean
monsoon cycle allowed direct-crossing voyages from Red Sea ports to India (documented in
the *Periplus of the Erythraean Sea*, a mid-1st-century CE Egyptian-Greek merchant's
navigational manual), but Ball argues the seafaring itself was disproportionately conducted
by Arab, Ethiopian, and Iranian sailors and shipwrights, with Rome largely a purchasing
market rather than a maritime participant. **(THESIS, Ball)**

## Major Phases

**Literary and archaeological evidence (Red Sea/India route).** The Periplus describes the
route from Myos Hormos and Berenice via south Arabian ports (Mouza, Ocelis, Qana) to
Barbaricon/Barygaza in northwest India or Muziris in the south, a voyage averaging roughly
six months. Pliny the Elder (*Natural History*, Book 6) estimated some 100 million
sesterces flowed east annually under Nero to pay for the India trade; a single documented
Muziris–Alexandria cargo (recorded on the "Muziris papyrus," referenced via the Peutinger
Table's "Temple of Augustus" at Muziris) comprised 700–1,700 lbs of nard, over 4,700 lbs of
ivory, and roughly 790 lbs of textiles, valued at 131 talents — enough to purchase some
2,400 acres of prime Egyptian farmland. An average Roman cargo ship reportedly carried
roughly 150 such consignments, which Ball treats as lending Pliny's aggregate figure some
plausibility. Rome levied a 25% tax on Indian goods entering at the Red Sea port of Leuce
Come.

**The coin-finds question.** Roman coin finds in southern India total roughly 5,400 denarii
and 800 gold aurei (plus some 700 more from the Laccadive Islands), overwhelmingly
Julio-Claudian (especially Augustus and Tiberius), reserved for trade because of their
higher gold/silver fineness; the coin supply drops sharply after Nero's coinage reform and
again in the 3rd century, a decline sometimes — though not certainly — linked to Emperor
Valerian's capture at [[capture-of-valerian-260|Edessa in 260]]. **Ball argues (THESIS)** that
the total value of these coin finds (roughly 100,000 sesterces equivalent) is a
"negligible" fraction — about 0.01% — of Pliny's claimed annual trade value, and that
coin-hoard clustering near the Coimbatore beryl mines is misleading, since beryl was
actually a minor export; he uses this to downplay the scale of direct Roman-Indian
exchange implied by older scholarship.

**Arikamedu and the "Roman colony" question.** The site of Arikamedu (excavated by
Mortimer Wheeler in the 1940s), identified with the Periplus's port of Poduke, yielded
Roman and Roman-imitation amphorae, Arretine terra sigillata (precisely dated 10–30 CE,
the only site in India with genuine Arretine ware), and Roman glass, active from roughly
the early 1st century CE to around 200 CE. Whether Arikamedu represents an actual resident
Roman trading colony is disputed: the archaeologist David Whitehouse argued the total finds
(some 150 amphora fragments, 50 Arretine fragments, and a handful of glass finds spread
over more than two centuries) are comparable in scale to finds at pre-Claudian Colchester
in England, and do not by themselves prove a resident foreign community absent distinctive
colonial architecture or inscriptions — a view Ball reports approvingly alongside an
unnamed Indian historian making a similar argument. Other Indo-Roman sites include Nasik,
Ter, Paithan, Nevasa, Kanchipuram, Kolhapur (a hoard of 102 bronzes, mostly Indian
imitations), and Karaikadu, the second most important Indo-Roman site after Arikamedu.

**The "Yavana" caution.** The term "Yavana" (from "Ionia," ultimately the root of the Old
Persian/Hebrew/Arabic/Chinese words for "Greek") originally meant specifically "Greek"
following Alexander's conquests, but broadened over time to mean "westerner" generally,
much as "Indian" or "Feringhi"/"Frank" later became generic terms. Ball argues that
"Yavana" traders named in Tamil and Sanskrit sources associated with Roman-era trade need
not literally have been Roman — they could equally have been Arab, Ethiopian, or Iranian
middlemen. **(THESIS)**

**The Persian Gulf route and the caravan cities.** Ball argues that scholarly focus on the
Red Sea/southern India route (centered on Roman Egypt) has understated the Persian Gulf
route, which was dominated by Palmyrenes, Arabs, and Iranians and predated significant
Roman involvement; he cites Chinese sources reportedly describing Indian trade with Rome as
routed via the Gulf rather than the Red Sea, and 1st-century BCE–1st-century CE Roman
glassware finds in the Gulf emirates as supporting evidence. **(THESIS)** Consistent with
this, [[palmyra|Palmyra]]'s own trade — attested by a Palmyrene tariff stele of 137 CE
recording slaves, salt, dried foods, dyed purple cloth, perfumes, and other goods, and by
excavated finds of silk, jade, muslin, spices, ebony, incense, ivory, and glass — is argued
by Ball not to represent a "trans-Asian caravan network" but rather an extension of the
maritime trade network to India, with Palmyrene merchant-house evidence found from Bahrain
and the Indus Delta to Merv, Rome, and even Newcastle-upon-Tyne. **(THESIS)**

**Roman ignorance of trade-good origins.** Ball notes that Roman knowledge of the actual
geographic origins of eastern trade goods was poor — Romans reportedly believed cardamom
came from Armenia, Media, or Assyria (rather than Ceylon), cinnamon from south Arabia or
northeast Africa (rather than Malaya), and rice from Ethiopia (rather than the Far East).
He reads this as evidence that Arabian middlemen deliberately spread disinformation about
sourcing and dominated the actual trade routes, undercutting claims of direct Roman
commercial control of the eastern trade. **(THESIS)**

**Shipping technology.** Ball argues Roman Mediterranean bulk cargo vessels, built
primarily for grain transport, were poorly suited to open Indian Ocean sailing, whereas
Indian ocean-going ships (built in Gujarat from the 2nd century BCE, per Indian sources
carrying 500–700 passengers, or around 75 tons per Pliny) and Arab stitched vessels
(sambuq/dhow) were better adapted — implying a larger Arab and Indian role in the actual
seafaring than a "Roman trade" narrative suggests. **(THESIS)** By the 6th century,
Sasanian Iran dominated Indian Ocean trade via naval bases across the Gulf, south Arabia,
the Indus mouth, and possibly Ceylon, with no Roman ships mentioned in the sources at all;
much of the actual seafaring was conducted by Nestorian Christian merchants and sailors
(originally Syrian, having fled persecution by the Orthodox Byzantine court) operating
under Sasanian protection, with Nestorian bishoprics across southern Iran, southern
Mesopotamia, northern Arabia, and western/southern India (Kerala's Christian community
traces descent from these communities). Emperors Anastasius and Justinian both attempted,
unsuccessfully, to bypass Iranian control of the Asian trade via Arabia and the Red Sea.

**Indian embassies westward.** Ball notes that Indian influence on Rome — though less
discussed than Roman-Indian trade — is attested by more numerous literary and
archaeological records of Indian delegations traveling west than the reverse: recorded
embassies include King Poros's mission to Augustus (including a Brahman priest who
self-immolated at Athens, per Strabo), embassies to Hadrian and Antoninus Pius, an Indian
embassy passing through Edessa to Elagabalus around 218, Indian and Bactrian ambassadors
attending Aurelian's triumph over [[zenobia|Palmyra]], and a Ceylon (Taprobane) embassy
received by [[julian|Julian]]. Ball also notes that no evidence survives of Rome ever
sending official embassies to India or China — unlike the Parthians, who maintained
official relations with China — so the "ambassadors" named in Roman sources were probably
merchants rather than diplomats.

**China and Central Asia — the "Silk Road" thesis.** Ball's most sweeping claim
**(THESIS — major, contested)** is that the popular and much of the scholarly notion of an
organized overland "Silk Road" linking Rome and China is a modern historiographical
construction with no ancient basis: no ancient Chinese or Classical source describes such
a road; no continuous overland Roman-Chinese trade existed before the Mongol Empire; silk
was never the primary commodity Rome imported from the East (spices dominated), and what
silk did reach Rome arrived mainly indirectly via India rather than overland. Ball notes
the term "Silk Road" (*Seidenstrasse*) was coined only in 1877 by the geographer Baron
Ferdinand von Richthofen, popularized by Sven Hedin's 1938 book of that title, and became
academic orthodoxy chiefly after 1989 amid a wave of "Silk Road mania" following the fall
of the Iron Curtain. He points out that Edward Gibbon, writing well before this modern
interest, describes Roman eastern trade solely in terms of Arabia and India and does not
mention China or a "Silk Road" at all. As literary evidence for any Roman land route to
Asia, Ball cites only Isidore of Charax's *Parthian Stations* (a 1st-century CE handbook
describing one official Parthian administrative route from Mesopotamia to Arachosia/
Kandahar) — stressing this is an internal Parthian administrative record making no
reference to Rome or China — plus reports that agents of the Syrian merchant Maes
Titianus reached a "Stone Tower" (possibly Tashkurgan) near Chinese territory in Hadrian's
reign, and Ptolemy's mention of 2nd–3rd-century trade reaching "Cattigora" (possibly
Canton). Later Han Chinese annals record a Roman "envoy" from "the King of Da-tsin, An-tun"
(i.e., Marcus Aurelius Antoninus) arriving in 166 CE — probably, per Ball, a private
Syrian or Palmyrene merchant rather than an official legation — with further merchant
contacts in 226 and 284. Archaeological evidence for Roman presence in China and Southeast
Asia is very sparse (Antonine intaglios at Oc-Eo, a Roman bronze lamp at P'ong Tuk, a coin
of Maximian in Tonkin, a small hoard at Xi'an, some Byzantine gold coins in Xinjiang),
which Ball reads as indicating that China's Rome trade was predominantly indirect, via
India, rather than overland.

**Mainstream contrast.** Mainstream scholarship on the ancient Near East (e.g. Fergus
Millar, *The Roman Near East*, 1993) has generally treated the region's trade networks as
substantially integrated into a Graeco-Roman commercial system; Ball's insistence on
indirect, middleman-dominated Roman participation and his rejection of the "Silk Road" as
a meaningful ancient category are part of his broader [[orientalization-of-rome-thesis|
orientalization thesis]] and should be read as a distinct, revisionist position within that
debate rather than settled consensus.

## Geographic Spread

Red Sea Egyptian ports (Myos Hormos, Berenice) to south Arabia (Mouza, Ocelis, Qana) to
northwest India (Barbaricon, Barygaza) and south India (Muziris, Arikamedu, and other Tamil
coast sites); the Persian Gulf route from Mesopotamia through [[palmyra|Palmyra]] and Gulf
ports to the same Indian destinations; sparse indirect extensions into Central Asia
(Begram, see [[gandharan-art]]) and China.

## Interaction

Closely bound up with [[palmyra|Palmyra's]] caravan economy and rise, with the broader
[[orientalization-of-rome|orientalization]] debate over the character of Rome's eastern
provinces, and with the artistic exchange documented at [[gandharan-art|Gandhara]] and the
[[gandharan-art|Begram treasure]]. Also intersects with the spread of Nestorian
Christianity into the Indian Ocean world.

## End Conditions

The sea trade did not end abruptly but was progressively reoriented under Sasanian and
later Islamic naval dominance of the Indian Ocean by the 6th century; Byzantine attempts
under Anastasius and Justinian to bypass Iranian middlemen failed, leaving the eastern
trade under Sasanian- and Arab-mediated control on the eve of the Islamic conquests.
