## Definition and Scope

The **communal movement** is the emergence, from the late eleventh century, of
self-governing **city-communes** in northern and central Italy (and, in different forms,
elsewhere in Europe) — sworn associations of citizens that took over the government of
their cities from bishops and counts. [[luscombe-ncmh-v4-2004|NCMH IV]] (Giovanni
Tabacco, Chs 4a and 15a, on northern and central Italy) treats the commune as the
characteristic Italian political creation of the period, growing out of the **decline of
local public power** and the **commercial revival** of the towns.

## Causal Drivers

- **Urban and commercial growth.** The [[commercial-revolution|commercial revolution]]
  produced wealthy, populous cities (Milan, Genoa, Pisa, Florence, Bologna) with
  elites — merchants and lesser nobles (the *boni homines*) — capable of self-rule.
- **The collapse of intermediate public authority.** As comital and margravial power
  dissolved in eleventh-century Italy and bishops' temporal lordship weakened (partly
  through the [[investiture-contest|Investiture Contest]], which discredited
  bishop-rulers), cities filled the vacuum.
- **The sworn association (*coniuratio*).** The commune was constituted by a collective
  oath binding citizens to mutual aid and self-government, electing **consuls** (and
  later a *podestà*) to administer justice, war, and finance.
- **The distance and weakness of imperial authority**, which left the cities room to
  act — until an emperor tried to reassert it.

## Major Phases

1. **Emergence (c. 1080–1150).** Consular communes appear and take over urban
   government, extending control over their rural *contado*.
2. **The struggle with the empire (1150s–1180s).** **[[frederick-i-barbarossa|Frederick
   Barbarossa's]]** attempt to reimpose imperial *regalia* (Roncaglia, 1158; the
   destruction of Milan, 1162) provoked the cities to form the **[[lombard-league|Lombard
   League]]**, whose victory at **[[battle-of-legnano-1176|Legnano (1176)]]** and the
   **Peace of Constance (1183)** secured communal autonomy under nominal imperial
   overlordship.
3. **Internal conflict and transformation (13th c.).** [[abulafia-ncmh-v5-2000|NCMH V]]
   (Trevor Dean, Louis Green) details how factional strife — the
   [[guelphs-and-ghibellines|Guelf–Ghibelline]] division and the struggle of **magnates**
   and ***popolo*** — fractured the communes, most of which were "weak, divided and unable to
   command adequate political consent." Two divergent outcomes followed. In much of Lombardy
   and the Veneto, single lords (***[[signoria|signori]]***) — Ezzelino da Romano, then the
   Visconti, Este and della Scala — recreated **monarchical power at the local level**, the
   seedbed of the Renaissance principalities. Elsewhere the **guild republic** survived and
   matured: [[florence|Florence]] built the priorate and the Ordinances of Justice (1293),
   and [[republic-of-venice|Venice]] its stable patriciate.

## Interaction

The communes are the political face of the Italian [[commercial-revolution|commercial
revolution]], the great obstacle to imperial power in Italy
([[frederick-i-barbarossa|Barbarossa]]), and a beneficiary of the [[papacy|papacy's]]
willingness to ally against the empire. Their distinctive **republican self-government**
is a defining feature of medieval and Renaissance Italy.
