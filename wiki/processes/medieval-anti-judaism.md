# The Church and the Jews (Medieval Anti-Judaism)

## Definition and Scope

This is the long-duration dynamic of the Latin Church's relationship with the Jews of
Christendom — a relationship of **toleration conditioned on subservience** that hardened,
across the thirteenth century, into containment, segregation, and (driven increasingly by
secular rulers) expulsion. [[abulafia-ncmh-v5-2000|NCMH V]] (Kenneth Stow, Ch. 2d)
argues against the common view that the thirteenth century **invented** a new
anti-Jewish program: rather, it "brought out the implications of long-standing policies
and heightened their definition," with grievous consequences for the Jews.

## Causal Drivers

- **The canon-law settlement.** Stow stresses deep continuity. The governing principle —
  that Jews were to be **protected and tolerated so long as they did not threaten
  Christianity** but assisted its ends — runs from Gregory I through Alexander II's letters
  to Spain (1063) into Gratian's *Decretum* (c. 1140) and the bull *Sicut Iudaeis non*.
  Jews were never legally equated with heretics; their conversion was reserved (following
  Paul) for the End of Days, so their presence was theologically **necessary** as a
  witness to unbelief and its punishment.
- **Perpetual Servitude.** Innocent III's bull ***Etsi Iudaeos* (1205)** enunciated the
  doctrine of *Perpetua servitudo* — not a real enslavement, Stow insists, but a "mnemonic
  device" emblematizing correct (subservient) Jewish behaviour, provoked by an incident
  over Christian wet-nurses at Eastertime.
- **Social vulnerability.** By 1200 Jews had lost their status as a distinct *genus* and
  become **directly dependent on feudal suzerains**, prey to arbitrary rule, and (in
  northern Europe) identified with **lending at interest**.
- **Myth and libel.** From the First Crusade (1096) onward, Jews were increasingly cast as
  the **mythical enemy of the Christian polity** — the ritual-murder and host-desecration
  libels, projection of doubts about the real presence of Christ in the Eucharist.

## Major Phases

- **Lateran IV (1215).** Stow argues that, apart from the **distinguishing dress** (the
  badge), every conciliar decree on Jews can be found in earlier collections (Burchard of
  Worms, Ivo of Chartres). The Council barred Jews from public office, regulated the
  interest they took from crusaders, and ordered distinctive clothing — which Innocent III
  justified (Stow says sincerely) as preventing inadvertent Christian–Jewish sexual
  contact. The **badge** rapidly came to signify inferior status; it was often kings
  (Henry III of England, 1218; Frederick II) who first enforced and degraded it.
- **The Talmud campaign.** From c. 1239 the convert **Nicholas Donin's** 35 charges led
  Gregory IX to order the **Talmud confiscated and burned at Paris (1240, 1242)**. The
  **Disputation of Barcelona (1263)** pitted the Dominican convert **Paul the Christian**
  against **Nahmanides** (Moses ben Nahman). Stow notes the popes simultaneously
  **restrained** excesses: Innocent IV (a canon lawyer) insisted Jews must keep the books
  "without which they cannot understand the Bible," and Clement IV (1267) warned that
  action against the Talmud must not violate the Jews' canonical privileges.
- **Expulsion.** The trajectory ended, under **royal** rather than papal initiative, in
  the great expulsions — **England (1290)**, **France (1306)** — and the forcible
  conversion of nearly all southern Italian Jewry (c. 1290).

## Interaction

The process intertwines with the rise of the [[mendicant-orders|mendicant orders]] (the
Dominicans led the Hebrew-learning, forced-sermon, and Talmud campaigns), the
[[medieval-inquisition|Inquisition]] (inquisitorial procedure touched the Talmud trials),
and the [[albigensian-crusade-1209|broader thirteenth-century drive against deviance]].
Stow situates it within the Church's wider **failure to reform society into a submissive
body** — heresy, contested clerical taxation, internal disputes — which made the visible
**subjection of the Jews** a symbolic stage in achieving the desired Christian order.

## End Conditions

The medieval canon-law framework of conditional toleration persisted, but its balance
shifted decisively from protection toward marginalization and expulsion; the genuinely
murderous, racialized phase (pollution of the Christian "body," charges of magic) Stow
locates in the later Middle Ages and the sixteenth century.
