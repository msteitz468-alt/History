# Egyptian Mummification and Burial Practice

**Mummification** was the ancient Egyptian process of preserving the body after death —
removal of internal organs (stored separately in canopic jars, protected by
[[anubis|Anubis]]-associated deities), desiccation of the body using natron salt, and
wrapping in resin-soaked linen — developed and refined over roughly three millennia from
simple natural desiccation in the desert sand to the elaborate embalming techniques of the
New Kingdom and later periods. The practice rested on core Egyptian afterlife beliefs:
that the preserved body was necessary as a physical anchor
for the *ka* (life-force) and *ba* (personality-soul) to recognize and inhabit in the
afterlife, and that rituals invoking Osiris (god of the dead and resurrection) and Anubis
(god of embalming) were essential to a successful transition.

## Social Scope and Decline

Initially an elite and royal prerogative — most elaborately expressed in monumental tomb
architecture such as the pyramids — mummification gradually extended to broader segments
of Egyptian society able to afford it, particularly in the Late and Ptolemaic periods, when
commercial embalming workshops served a wider clientele. The practice declined with the
spread of Christianity in Egypt from the 4th century CE onward, as Christian doctrine
rejected the underlying theology while retaining, in modified form, some burial customs.
