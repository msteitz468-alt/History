## Definition and Scope

Shimada's chapter in the *Cambridge History of the Native Peoples of the Americas*, vol. III part 1 (hereafter [[cambridge-history-native-peoples-americas-v3-1]]) adopts the term **Early Regional Development (ERD)** cultures for the complex polities that flourished in coastal and highland northwestern South America from roughly 500 BCE to 600 CE. The name deliberately avoids loaded older labels such as "Mastercraftsmen," "Florescent," or "Classic":

> "We adopt the designation 'Early Regional Development' (hereafter ERD) culture, as it describes the regional character of the cultures under study without attaching undue subjective value or evolutionary implications to them."

The process is best understood as the *second phase of an oscillating pattern* in Andean prehistory: the spread of the [[chavin]] "Early Horizon" style (c. 400–200 BCE) had temporarily homogenised material culture across much of Peru, integrating otherwise separate coastal and highland polities around a shared religious iconography centred on the Staff God and the "Smiling God" at Chavín de Huántar. When the Chavin cult dissolved around 200 BCE, "it was followed by a period of assimilation that involved selective integration of new and old cultural features into new overall configurations. It was a time of reasserting regional identity and setting new values and orders." The resulting florescence of *distinct* regional cultures — each with its own ceramic, textile, metallurgical, and architectural tradition — lasted until the expansion of the [[wari]] empire (the "Middle Horizon") re-integrated much of Andean Peru after c. 600 CE.

Cultures covered by this process include (in Peru): [[chavin]] (transition), [[paracas]] and [[nasca]] (south coast), [[lima-culture]] (central coast), Vicús/Sechura, Salinar, Gallinazo/Virú, [[moche]] (north coast), Layzon/[[recuay]]/Cajamarca (north highlands); and outside Peru: La Tolita-Tumaco, Jama-Coaque, Bahía, Guangala (coastal Ecuador), Pukara and early [[tiwanaku]] (altiplano).

## Causal Drivers

**Environmental foundation.** The extreme ecological diversity of the Andean west slope — from hyperarid Pacific coast near sea level to 6,000-metre glaciated peaks within a few dozen horizontal kilometres — created distinct resource bases that encouraged both specialisation and exchange. Coastal valleys with perennial rivers (especially in the north) supported large-scale gravity-fed irrigation; the altiplano required ridged-field cultivation and agropastoralism with llama-alpaca herds; the yunka inter-valley zones held copper deposits, coca, and canal intake points that all parties contested.

**Water management and agricultural intensification.** The slice documents a suite of innovations: filtration galleries (*puqyu*) to tap groundwater on the Nasca south coast (attested by Nasca Phase 5, c. CE 300–400); sunken fields (*mahamaes*) excavated down toward the water table (documented at Salinar sites, c. 250 BCE–CE 1); ridged/raised fields around Lake Titicaca (begun c. 1000 BCE, well-established by 500 BCE); and the Mochica's massive inter-valley gravity canals — by c. CE 500 their irrigated acreage exceeded modern levels by 30–50 % in some valleys.

**Craft specialisation and cross-craft interaction.** The period formalised macroregional **aesthetic loci**: precious metals (gold and gilded copper) for the Northern Mochica; polychrome embroidered textiles for the south-coast Paracas; polychrome ceramics for Nasca. Once a medium achieved prestige status it stimulated adjacent crafts by analogy and competition. Mochica press-mould ceramic technology probably derived from and fed back into copper casting. Paracas weaving techniques (tapestry, discontinuous warp-and-weft, block-colour embroidery) spread south-coast dyeing and spinning skills to unprecedented complexity.

**Long-distance exchange networks.** The Spondylus (spiny oyster), Strombus (conch), and Conus shells — tropical species available only in warm Ecuadorian waters — were ritually indispensable to Andean highland and coastal elites alike, driving maritime exchange from at least the Chavin period. Ecuadorian traders likely acted as intermediaries between Mesoamerica (western Mexico shaft-tomb cultures) and Peru. The Mochica established a trading foothold in the upper Piura Valley precisely to access this network.

**Dissolution of the Chavin horizon.** The structural driver is the Chavin cult's collapse. Having homogenised regional expression, its dissolution created a creative vacuum that local traditions filled by "reclaiming local identity visually, which involved processes such as syncretism and archaic revival."

## Major Phases

### Phase 1 — Late Horizon Overlap and Transition (c. 500–200 BCE)
The Chavin cult (associated with the Early Horizon) was still spreading during this phase, carried by branch oracles, pilgrims, and painted cotton textiles. The Janabarriu ceramic style (c. 400–200 BCE) reached as far south as the Ayacucho and Ica regions and north to the Lambayeque coast. Contemporaneous Paracas Cavernas burials on the south coast already incorporated Chavin iconography through the Karwa painted-cloth cache (c. 300–200 BCE); Salinar culture on the north coast (c. 200 BCE–CE 1) shows Chavin-derived forms transitioning toward what would become Mochica. In the altiplano, the Chiripa and Qaluyu cultures were already practising ridged-field agriculture.

### Phase 2 — Regional Crystallisation (c. 200 BCE–CE 100)
The Chavin cult dissolved and regional styles sharpened. On the north coast the Gallinazo polity emerged, building monumental adobe platforms across the Moche-Virú-Santa sector. Salinar sites on the north coast were abandoned c. CE 1. The Initial Cajamarca kaolin-paste ceramic tradition crystallised in the northern highlands. The Paracas Necropolis mortuary complex reached its apogee (c. 300 BCE–CE 200), with 429 funerary bundles bearing up to sixty layers of embroidered textiles. Vicús-related populations (possibly migrants from the Colombia-Ecuador border) established middlemen networks in the upper Piura.

### Phase 3 — ERD Florescence (c. CE 100–400)
The period of greatest regional diversity and artistic achievement. On the north coast, early Mochica "royal" tombs at Sipán (mid-Lambayeque, c. CE 100–300) demonstrate extraordinary metallurgical skill in gold, gilded copper, and granulation — disproving the earlier assumption that Lambayeque was a marginal region. Mochica Phases I–III saw significant territorial expansion; by Phase III the Southern Mochica polity controlled much of the north coast. On the south coast, Nasca polychrome ceramics replaced Paracas embroidery as the aesthetic locus; the Cahuachi pilgrimage centre attracted regional gatherings. On the central coast, the Lima (Maranga) culture erected substantial adobe-and-fill platform mounds, including a mound measuring approximately "270 × 100 m and 15 m high." In the north highlands, Recuay stone sculpture and tricolour ceramics flourished in the Callejón de Huaylas. Early Cajamarca ceramics spread across the North Highlands and into both yunka zones.

### Phase 4 — Late ERD and Integration Pressures (c. CE 400–600)
A severe multi-decade drought (c. CE 562–594) disrupted the Mochica polity, triggering unprecedented Spondylus ritual use and coinciding with the rise of the Wari empire in the Ayacucho highlands. Mochica Phase V capital shifted to the large urban site of Pampa Grande. The altiplano saw Tiwanaku IV colonial expansion (Omo Phase settlements in the Moquegua region, c. CE 550–600). Ridged fields on the northern Titicaca shore were abandoned c. CE 300–350 following inferred Tiwanaku expansion and demise of the Pukara polity. The process ends with Wari "Middle Horizon" expansion overwriting regional corporate styles across much of Peru.

## Geographic Spread

The process operated across three interacting macrozones:

- **Peruvian north coast** (Piura to Huarmey valleys): Mochica and Gallinazo; horizontality strategy of coastal territorial expansion.
- **Peruvian south coast** (Chincha, Pisco, Ica, Nazca drainages): Paracas and Nasca; emphasis on textiles, ceramics, and geoglyphs; verticality limited; irrigation supplemented by filtration galleries.
- **Peruvian central coast** (Rimac/Lima valleys): Lima/Maranga adobe mound complex, poorly documented.
- **North Peruvian highlands** (Cajamarca basin, Callejón de Huaylas): Cajamarca kaolin-paste ceramics (autonomy from c. 200 BCE to Spanish conquest); Recuay stone sculpture and tricolour ceramics, competing with Mochica for the yunka zone.
- **Southern altiplano** (Lake Titicaca basin): Pukara and early Tiwanaku; raised-field agriculture, agropastoralism, and the Yaya-Mama Religious Tradition ancestral to Wari/Tiwanaku ideology.
- **Coastal Ecuador and southern Colombia**: Jama-Coaque, Bahía, La Tolita-Tumaco, and others; chiefdom-level societies in hollow figurine and mound-building tradition; middlemen in Spondylus/Strombus trade.

## Interaction with Other Processes

- **[[andean-state-formation]]** — the ERD period represents the crucial intermediate phase: post-Chavin but pre-Wari. Only the Mochica clearly achieved state-level organisation (Phases III–V), with a four-tier settlement hierarchy, labour taxation for mound construction using marked-adobe accounting, and redistributive urban economy at Pampa Grande. Most other ERD cultures remained at the chiefdom level.
- **[[vertical-archipelago]]** concept (Murra) — direct vertical control by altiplano polities is attested by the Tiwanaku Omo Phase colony in Moquegua (c. CE 550–600); the Recuay downward thrust into the western yunka is an analogous northern highland pattern; north-coast Mochica instead practised "horizontality," lateral coastal expansion rather than vertical zonation.
- Link to [[tiwanaku]] — Pukara and Tiwanaku shared major mythical themes and deities; Tiwanaku's ridged-field expansion on the southern Titicaca shore (c. CE 400–600) directly followed Pukara's collapse.
- Link to [[wari]] — Wari expansion c. CE 600 constitutes the end condition for most ERD cultures; Middle Cajamarca B ceramics subsequently enjoyed widest distribution *during* Wari dominance, suggesting selective elite incorporation rather than erasure.

## End Conditions

The process ended through two overlapping mechanisms:

1. **Wari Middle Horizon expansion** (c. CE 600 onwards) — the Wari empire from its Ayacucho heartland re-homogenised ceramic and iconographic styles across much of Peru, overwriting north-coast Mochica, south-coast Nasca, and central-coast Lima corporate styles. The terminal Nasca workshop at Maymi (Pisco Valley) shows Wari-style ceramics produced with indigenous techniques, documenting the transition.
2. **Environmental stress** — the prolonged drought c. CE 562–594 disrupted Mochica Phase IV–V stability and may have accelerated Wari intrusion.

The Cajamarca tradition uniquely persisted, showing "unbroken stylistic continuity (i.e., autonomy)… from its inception around 200–100 BCE up to the Spanish conquest," attesting that Wari and later Inca expansion did not erase all ERD-descended identities.
