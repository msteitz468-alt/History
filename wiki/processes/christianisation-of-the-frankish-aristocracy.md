# Christianisation of the Frankish Aristocracy

**The process by which the Frankish lay aristocracy was drawn into the Church on its own terms** in the
seventh century — founding family monasteries, producing a new kind of saint, and ultimately making
control of religion the basis of political power. It is, in Geary's argument, the deep social transformation
underneath the more visible dynastic politics.

## Definition and the gap it filled

After [[clovis|Clovis]]'s conversion "the Frankish aristocracy had no religious role in society" — unlike
the Gallo-Roman aristocracy, which monopolised the [[merovingian-episcopate|episcopate]]. This gap was
filled by [[merovingian-monasticism|Columbanian monasticism]]. The list of aristocrats drawn to
[[columbanus|Columbanus]] "reads like a Who's Who of the Frankish aristocracy": Audoenus (Dado), bishop
of Rouen; Romaricus, founder of Remiremont; Bertulf of Luxeuil, kinsman of Arnulf of Metz.

## Mechanisms

- **Family monasteries.** Aristocrats founded monasteries on **family property** under the mixed
  Iro-Frankish rule; these became "spiritual centers for the small political units of family control" —
  and family **necropolises** (the church of St-Paul at Jouarre still holds the tombs of founders such as
  Agilbert, bishop of Wessex then of Paris, all venerated as saints).
- **A new model of sanctity.** Seventh-century hagiography celebrated "the aristocrat who served actively
  in the royal court before going on to found monasteries" — a saint who never abandoned family or social
  rank, so that "his sanctity was reflected back on them." This sanctified the aristocracy itself, in
  sharp contrast to the earlier model of the bishop who renounced the world.
- **The royal court as engine.** [[dagobert-i|Dagobert I]] gathered regional elites at the Neustrian
  court, educated them, and returned them as bishops and officials — royal bishops such as **Amandus,
  Eligius, and Audomar** then undertook missions in Flanders and Frisia with massive royal land grants.
- **Burial shift.** From the second half of the sixth century, aristocratic burial moved from rural
  row-cemeteries (weapons, jewellery) to **church burial near saints' tombs** — a material index of the
  Christianisation of elite culture.

## Mission, commerce, and Rome

Evangelisation was inseparable from Frankish political and commercial expansion: Dagobert's church at
Utrecht under Bishop Cunibert coincided with moving royal coiners to **Duurstede**, the centre of Frisian
trade. The later **Anglo-Saxon missionaries** (Willibrord from 690, Boniface from 719) differed from the
Irish: they brought a papally-imposed hierarchy, close cooperation with kings, and Benedictine monasticism,
and they turned to **Rome** for sanction — Willibrord travelling to Rome on arrival, "unthinkable for a
Frankish clergyman." Conversion of the Frisians meant "a radical break with their own autonomous social and
political past," which Duke Radbod refused rather than be parted from his pagan ancestors. Pope Gregory III's
gift to [[charles-martel|Charles Martel]] of the keys and chain of St Peter (739) began the importation of
**Roman relics**, shifting the sacred geography of the West from local Gallic saints toward Rome.

## Outcome

The process culminated paradoxically. The aristocracy's capture of religion gave the [[pippinids|Pippinids]]
their decisive weapon: Charles Martel and his kin took over monasteries and bishoprics wholesale, and the
synods of the 740s issued their decrees in the name of **Carlomann**, not the bishops. Control of the Church
had become the foundation of sovereign power — and the road to [[pippin-iii|Pippin III]]'s anointing in 751.
