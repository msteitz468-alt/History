# Sixteenth-Century North American Contact

## Definition and Scope

The century of intermittent European–Native encounter in North America before enduring settlement outside Florida and (from 1598) New Mexico — fisheries, whaling, Spanish *entradas*, French and English reconnaissance, gift-trade, kidnapping, and colonial experiments — and the associated disease, trade, and political shocks. Bruce G. Trigger and William R. Swagerty (“Entertaining Strangers,” [[sources/cambridge-history-native-peoples-americas-v1-1|CHNPA I.1]] ch. 6) treat the century as a first-class period of change, overturning the older assumption that Native cultures remained static until seventeenth-century colonization.

**What it is not:** continuous colonial occupation of most of the continent; full Columbian Exchange transformation of every region; or a single “conquest” narrative. Contacts were geographically patchy, source-poor, and highly uneven in intensity.

## Causal Drivers

- Centralizing Western European monarchies seeking wealth for armies and administrations, and coping with a precious-metals drain to Asia; Portuguese and Spanish long-distance voyages for gold, silk, spices, slaves, and sugar islands, backed (for Spanish routes) by Italian merchants threatened by Ottoman Mediterranean control and Portuguese gold-trade domination (`caused_by` broader [[columbian-exchange-1492]] expansion)
- Failure of a navigable Northwest Passage south of the Arctic (coastal charting 1524–27; Cartier’s 1535 demonstration that the St. Lawrence was a river estuary — [[cartier-expeditions-1534]])
- Seasonal dry fisheries and Basque whaling landing thousands of Europeans on northeastern shores
- Spanish *entradas* and failed/successful colonies: Narváez, [[de-soto-expedition-1539]], [[coronado-expedition-1540]], Luna, Ayllón, Pardo, Menéndez (Florida), [[onate-new-mexico-1598]]
- [[virgin-soil-epidemics]] (scale and inland reach disputed — see regional sections)
- Trade goods (“gift kit” of beads, bells, knives, hatchets) and slave-raiding / salvage economies

## Major Phases

### 1. Historiographic baseline and source problem

Until the 1960s, anthropologists and ethnohistorians paid little attention to North America in the sixteenth century. Aware of European exploration, they generally assumed Native cultures had remained static until Europeans established direct and lasting contact, and that seventeenth-century descriptions recorded ways of life essentially unchanged from earlier times. Even in the Southeast, where *entradas* and disease were long recognized as possibly major, systematic investigation was scarce.

Today it is generally acknowledged that the sixteenth century was a time of major changes over large areas of North America — yet scholars remain far from agreed how geographically extensive or far-reaching European-contact transformations were. Written documentation is mostly maps and place-name lists; only a small corpus of detailed eyewitness accounts exists. Whole classes of encounters that must have been very common — e.g. between European whalers and fishermen along the east coast of Canada — went unchronicled, though commercial archives attest large scale. Written records cover only eastern and southern fringes. Archaeology of the “protohistorical period” (earliest European goods/diseases to continuous written records) expanded only after the 1970s; oral traditions purporting to refer to the century are few and often reworked. Trigger & Swagerty still call the sixteenth the “unknown century” because the prior assumption of little change inhibited archaeology; current interpretations remain provisional.

### 2. North America in 1492 — already in flux

At Columbian contact North America was “a continent in flux,” filled with diverse peoples whose social, political, and economic relations were complex. From the Caribbean to Hudson Bay more cultural change was probably occurring than at any previous time — some transitions centuries in the making, others recent but uninfluenced by European invasion.

- **Iroquoian lower Great Lakes:** After A.D. 1300, increasing corn agriculture and rapid population expansion; some settlements up to ~2,000 people; communities uniting into small nations; leagues/confederacies may already have been developing in the fifteenth century ([[haudenosaunee-confederacy]], [[huron-wendat]]).
- **Southeast:** Parts becoming politically/residentially decentralized as Late Mississippian ceremonial centers were abandoned before contact, yet evidence also shows chiefdom formation and persistence (e.g. Coosas of Georgia, Powhatans of Virginia consolidating — influence of European intrusion vs indigenous trajectory unclear) ([[mississippian-culture]], [[powhatan-confederacy]]).
- **Southwest:** Redistribution into large communities in formerly scarcely inhabited regions; florescence of ceremonialism and art (Katcina cult, ceramics, kiva murals); Hawikuh (Zuni) and Pecos expanded; Rio Grande Valley filled while Western Ancestral Pueblo sector saw many abandonments ([[ancestral-pueblo]]).
- **Interregional trade:** Southwest hubs at Zuni and Pecos linking Plains, Great Basin, California, and northern Mexico; California trade hubs rather than major centers; The Dalles on the Columbia “the largest trade center in the Northwest and possibly all of North America,” with massive summer salmon processing. Eastern networks moved corn, pelts, marine shells, Lake Superior copper, catlinite, mica, galena, buffalo hides.
- **Florida–Caribbean:** Long debate on Southeast–Caribbean interaction. Negative archaeological evidence and ethnobotanical dissimilarities (especially lack of manioc in the Southeast) support little if any significant pre-Columbian Florida–Caribbean contact; Europeans later witnessed Native fishermen/traders crossing the Gulf Stream.

### 3. Nature of European contact — intensity and practices

Sixteenth-century European ships generally sailed west to Newfoundland or via Canaries/Caribbean to Florida; Spanish returned via the Gulf Stream. Apart from Florida, the U.S. east coast was visited mainly by explorers, privateers, and fur traders between Newfoundland and the Caribbean. Native people from Maine to North Carolina had much less regular contact than those in Florida or Atlantic Canada. Most prolonged regular contact: Spanish settlements in the southeastern United States and summer fishermen on the Northeast coast. Except far south near Spanish Mexico/West Indies, contacts were restricted to coastal areas including the St. Lawrence Valley.

**Gift kit.** Portuguese graded-presents practice from West Africa was applied in the Americas. Glass beads (first doled by Columbus 1492), metal bells, hatchets, knives, rattles, feathers, and cloth formed a “gift kit” carried by Columbus, Cortés, Narváez, Coronado, Luna, and many other expeditions. Cartier’s 1534 Gaspé encounter: initially reluctant St. Lawrence Iroquoians rowed out after realizing the French would give knives, glass beads, and combs; women received comb and tin bell/ring each; Cartier distributed knives, hatchets, beads, and tin trinkets at Hochelaga 1535.

**Pacific coast.** Within a decade of Mexico’s conquest Spaniards explored Pacific shores; Cortés’s Baja beachheads in the 1530s failed; 1540 Alarcón reached the Colorado River delta; 1542 Cabrillo’s ships met Alta California Indians and wintered among Island Chumashes at San Miguel; 1579 Drake reached northern California. Drake’s Bay/Coast Miwok sites yield 800+ sixteenth-century exotic items (mostly Chinese porcelain) attributed to Drake 1579 and the wreck of the Manila galleon *San Agustín* 1595.

**Shipwreck salvage and ransom (Florida).** Calusas gained a reputation for recovering goods and live “Christians” (sometimes killed, usually adopted then ransomed) from Florida Keys wrecks (“Los Mártires”); Spanish items including coins traded interior or exchanged after 1565. Mound Key (Calos, Calusa central town) yields many sixteenth-century Spanish pottery examples. Ais, Jeaga, and Tequesta on the SE Florida coast had especially good salvage/enslavement/ransom years in the 1550s–1570s amid French/Spanish/English corsair competition.

**Padre Island 1554.** Spanish salvage with Native divers from Mexico and primitive diving bells retrieved about half of 1.5 million pesos from three Havana-bound ships off Padre Island, Texas. Local Indians did not recover a wreck share but stalked and killed all but a few of ~250 survivors walking back to Mexico.

**Kidnapping for trophies and interpreters.** Common practice: Sebastian Cabot’s three Indians at Henry VII’s court 1502; Frobisher’s Inuit and kayak 1577; Verrazzano kidnapped a boy and tried to take a woman; Francisco de Chicora (Ayllón 1521, SC coast) lived five years among captors; Don Luis de Velasco (Chesapeake 1561) educated by Spanish; Cartier seized Donnacona’s relatives 1534 and in 1536 entrapped Donnacona and ten Iroquoians — all but one dead before a conquest expedition could launch; Weymouth seized five New England Indians 1605. News of kidnappings spread along the eastern seaboard. Returned captives became intelligence brokers — Chicora and others eluded Ayllón’s colonists; Donnacona’s sons advised charging the French more for food because exchange goods were worth little in France; Don Luis de Velasco played a leading role murdering Jesuit priests he accompanied home to Ajacán 1570–71. Familiarity and outward adoption of European beliefs did not secure captives’ loyalty.

**Slave raids (Caribbean and SE coasts).** Early Spanish penetration as state-supported plundering then tribute. Columbus’s second voyage brought 30 Indian prisoners sold as slaves in Seville; hundreds sold yearly thereafter. Corte-Real 1501 and Gomes 1525 abducted large numbers for European markets. By 1509 Lucayans of the Bahamas bore first large-scale enslavements; chain virtually depopulated by 1514. With slaves fetching 150 pesos after 1509 (vs 5 pesos 1508), Lesser Antilles largely emptied by 1520 for mines/plantations of Española, Puerto Rico, Jamaica, Cuba. In a quarter century the Native population of the Spanish main became a “sorry shell.” A 1550 ordinance forbade bringing captive American Indians to Spain; high mortality and opposition helped curb mass kidnappings — no evidence of such piratical raids on east coasts of North America after the 1520s. From 1513 Ponce de León briefly landed on Florida looking for slaves and loot; Ais and Calusas resisted. Second attempt 1521 among Calusas: forced retreat with mortal wound. Pedro de Salazar (1514–16) captured boatloads for labor; somewhere on the Carolina coast, hospitable Indians who traded with Lucayans opened talks — 500 landed on Española in chains.

**Newfoundland fisheries and Basque whaling.** Earliest record of Newfoundland fish cargo to England 1502; Portugal levying duties by 1506; Normans active by 1504; Bretons by 1511; French Basques after 1512; Bordeaux fleet 1517. Dry fishery (shore processing) involved contact with Natives; wet/green fishery (offshore heavy salting) did not. From ~1540 Spanish/French Basques controlled the Strait of Belle Isle, hunted whales on the Labrador coast with up to 1,000 Basques; ships to 600 tons. By the late sixteenth century more ships and men crossed the North Atlantic each year to fish and hunt whales than sailed between Spain and its New World colonies; Parkhurst 1578 estimated 350–380 vessels in the Newfoundland fishery. Dry fisheries/whaling landed thousands of Europeans on NE shores — perhaps 20,000 by 1580 — in contact with Beothuks, Montagnais (Innu), and Micmacs (Mi’kmaq). Basque–Montagnais relations often amicable (shallops left in Labrador over winter); Beothuks, more dependent on coastal resources and with fewer furs, abandoned southern fishing sites and retreated north by 1583. Annual contacts restricted to Newfoundland, Nova Scotia, Labrador, and the Gulf of St. Lawrence. (`enabled` [[eastern-north-american-fur-trade]])

### 4. Six contact types, 1526–1560s (and after)

Beyond fisheries meetings, six main types of direct contact from 1526 to first successful colonization in the 1560s:

1. Coastal exploratory/trader landings (Verrazzano, Cartier, Díaz, Cabrillo)
2. Interior meetings with explorers/would-be colonists who wintered under formal colony charters that failed — Narváez 1528–33 (Cabeza de Vaca account 1542); [[de-soto-expedition-1539]] into the Mississippi heartland and Texas; [[coronado-expedition-1540]] into the Southwest; Luna 1559–61 Southeast
3. Mesoamerican/Caribbean Native allies with *entradas*
4. Missionaries
5. Fur trade
6. Colonial ventures

**Missions.** Priests accompanied many expeditions but proselytization was often low priority (e.g. de Soto: 12 priests, 4 died first year; remaining lost clothes/sacramentals at Mabila; few individual “Christians,” no large-scale conversions; “dry mass” held). Pre-permanent settlement experiments: Dominicans under Luis de Cancer 1549 Florida — four priests including Cancer killed almost immediately among Timucuans near Tampa (memory of 1539 de Soto landing influenced hostile reception); Franciscans under Rodríguez New Mexico 1581. 1573 ordinances favored ecclesiastics in new discoveries (max 20 soldiers).

**Failed and lasting colonies (Southeast).** Ayllón’s ~500-man party founded San Miguel de Gualdape (Spain’s first municipality north of Mexico); goods filtered to Indians; de Soto later found Biscayan axes, Spanish dagger, and European beads at Cofitachequi two days from San Miguel. Juan Pardo from Santa Elena built six Carolina interior forts 1566–67, distributed hardware/buttons/cloth/necklaces; forts overrun within 1–2 years — all tools became Native possessions. French Charlesfort among Guales 1562 (abandoned within a year); Fort Caroline among Saturiwan Timucuans 1564; expelled by Menéndez 1565. French became middlemen/political brokers among competing Timucuan polities; after capture many French harbored by Guale/Timucuans; Gourgues leveled Fort San Mateo 1567–68.

**Menéndez Florida.** Permanent SE settlement: St. Augustine 1565, Santa Elena 1566; Spain held the province until 1763 at high cost to resident Indians. Menéndez married the sister of Calusa chief Carlos 1566; sought end to inter-Indian warfare via peace pledges and exchanging Native women/boys for Jesuits. Unruly soldiers, colonists, intolerant priests, and deceitful officials alienated polities. 1572 Jesuits left; Franciscans few. Menéndez later proposed war of “blood and fire” and enslavement of survivors for Caribbean plantations — request denied. Assassination of Carlos 1573; Drake sacked St. Augustine 1586; Guale rebellions forced Santa Elena abandonment. 1595: five Timucuan + four Guale missions, 1,500 “Christian” Indians. 1597 Guale revolt over Franciscan interference in chiefdom succession/multiple marriages: five of six priests died; province not reassigned until 1603. St. Augustine became a North American “melting pot” via mestizaje; poorest known Spanish Florida/Caribbean site, highly dependent on Timucuan foods, ceramics, architecture; majority of recorded marriages with local women.

**New Mexico.** [[onate-new-mexico-1598]] — colonization of the Española Valley and the Acoma war of 1598–99, planting seeds for the 1680 Pueblo Revolt.

### 5. Native responses

**Perception of Europeans.** Overt reactions ranged from terror/flight to fascination/reverence to suspicion/hostility. Compelling evidence that some early-sixteenth-century Europeans were regarded as powerful shamans or spirits — but documentary evidence for widespread religious determination of Native behavior is “very limited and contradictory”; chronicles generally show exchange, defense, and hierarchical impressing of outsiders from the start. Regular contact quickly established Europeans as mortal humans (Stadaconans vs Hochelagans contrast). Hochelagans brought sick to Cartier 1535; Cabeza de Vaca was welcomed as healer across Texas; sick were brought to de Soto. Coast Miwoks lacerated faces and offered sacrifices to Drake 1579; Virginia 1587 English were seen as spirits of the dead because Indians died where they passed while English did not.

**Hamell cosmology (goods symbolism).** George Hamell (controversial in detail) argues Algonquian/Iroquoian/Siouan cosmologies associated marine shells, native copper/silver, and rock crystals with physical/spiritual/social well-being; Europeans’ copper/brass/tin equated with native metals and glass beads with crystals; ocean origin placed goods at mythical time/space convergence — hence supernatural status. Explains cutting copper/brass kettles into tiny fragments for exchange and burial (not living sites); arrival of goods stimulated revival of burial ceremonialism and marine-shell exchange after a low ebb of exotic burial ritualism. Marine shell remained irreplaceable by Europeans.

**Alliances.** Donnacona sought French ties for iron weapons vs Micmacs and better standing vs Hochelagans; soured when Cartier visited Hochelaga without alliance and wintered without permission. Late-century St. Malo traders formalized alliance with Montagnais of Tadoussac — yearly weapons vs Mohawk raiders. De Soto exploited inter-Indian rivalries; Espejo: Zunis joined Spanish against Hopis. French supplied harquebusiers in Timucuan wars; Menéndez recruited anti-French guides to surprise Fort Caroline. French policy of *douceur* (realistic assessment of Native capacity to promote or thwart French goals) became a cornerstone of later New France colonization (Washburn & Trigger, CHNPA I.1 ch. 2).

**Armed resistance.** Pre-1560 Florida a battle zone of slavers, privateers, and would-be colonists among Natives prepared to resist. Appalachees abandoned central towns/croplands and waged guerrilla ambushes vs de Soto. **Mabila** (Oct 1540): Tuscaluza’s feigned friendship; estimated 5,000 Mobilian, Choctaw, and other warriors; battle >7 hours; Native casualties estimated 2,500–3,000 including women and children — critical turning point signaling Spanish weakness (detail on [[de-soto-expedition-1539]]). **Tiguex** winter 1540–41 under Coronado: people of a large settlement forced out with only clothes; ~50 Spaniards/Mexican allies wounded or killed; 600 Tiguan males reported perished, many by post-surrender execution; wives/children enslaved — largest sixteenth-century Southwest battle; Tiguans never recovered (detail on [[coronado-expedition-1540]]). Timucuans fought Menéndez for a decade from 1566; Tocobaga garrison killed winter 1567–68; Guale rebellions 1576–82 and major 1597 revolt. Cartier’s 1541–42 Cap Rouge colony besieged — 35+ colonists killed after failure to return kidnapped people and unauthorized fortification. **Acoma** 1598–99 under Oñate: full atrocity record on [[onate-new-mexico-1598]].

**Interethnic coalitions.** Outstanding interethnic/intervillage cooperation against Europeans: Appalachees 1528–1561; Mobile–Choctaw alliance at Mabila 1540; Chickasaws and Coosas (historic Creeks) showing confederated systems in archaeology and documents. Coronado resistance by six Zuni settlements and Tiguex indicates strategies beyond village boundaries, but no pan-Pueblo or pan-Indian organization comparable to the seventeenth-century Great Pueblo Revolt of 1680.

**Interior political reorganization for goods access.** European presence caused political changes/relocations far from explorers. Micmacs used metal weapons to expand hunting territories; war with Stadaconans for Gaspé by the 1530s; early seventeenth-century westward expansion forced some Maliseets to Saguenay Montagnais. Second half of the sixteenth century: remaining St. Lawrence Iroquoians dispersed; by ~1570 Mohawks and Oneidas at war with Montagnais and Algonquin north of the St. Lawrence — possibly Iroquois seeking direct access to European goods. The Iroquois confederacy may have been strengthened by the need to compete with northern/eastern groups already armed with wooden-armor-piercing iron axes and metal arrowheads. Huron confederacy concentration at southern Georgian Bay partly for access to Lake Nipissing–St. Lawrence trade routes. Susquehannock southward relocation: either toward Chesapeake traders/shell monopoly or to avoid better-armed Iroquois. Powhatan chiefdom origins debated as precontact vs European-response ([[powhatan-confederacy]]).

### 6. Conclusion patterns (Trigger & Swagerty)

Most widespread impact: European diseases and dramatic population fall — clearest in the Southeast (simplification of hierarchical Mississippian societies); dramatic decline in Atlantic Canada and probably the Southwest; not clear how far inland north and west; Great Lakes evidence may show few if any major sixteenth-century epidemics → lower precontact populations than revisionist highs → less effective transmission (see [[virgin-soil-epidemics]], [[pre-columbian-american-population]]). European goods circulated far inland along trade routes but significant technological impact only near the end of the century; in the Northeast mainly metal-kettle scraps valued for religious significance, stimulating burial ceremonialism and marine-shell demand. European conquest/settlement efforts were second only to pathogens as destabilizers; early-century Florida Indians expelled successive invaders before major disease reduction; later mixture of resistance and accommodation; few European settlements withstood growing Native resistance unless more accommodating. The century ended with only two small European settlement areas, but many thousands of Europeans had visited; few Indians visited Europe and returned. Initial supernatural interpretations gave way to pragmatic modes of interaction that shaped responses to growing intrusion.

## Geographic Spread

Atlantic and Gulf coasts, Southeast interior (de Soto corridor), St. Lawrence approaches and fisheries coasts, Pacific California reconnaissance, Southwest *entradas* and 1598 colonization; Mid-Atlantic and Maine–North Carolina coasts lightly and irregularly touched; Great Lakes interior mainly via goods and rumor until late century.

## End Conditions

Transitions as permanent colonies take root after c. 1600 (St. Augustine 1565 and New Mexico 1598 as earlier exceptions). Jamestown 1607, Quebec 1608, Santa Fe 1610 open the next process phase. Jennings’s “widowed landscape” — a continent already veteran of more than a century of unintentional but highly lethal germ warfare — frames the demographic baseline for seventeenth-century colonization.

## Historiography

Trigger & Swagerty’s chapter is the CHNPA I.1 synthesis for the century. Pre-1960s static-culture assumption; post-1970s protohistoric archaeology; standing debates on pandemic horizons (Dobyns vs Snow/Warrick/Miller), fur-trade volume before the 1580s, Florida–Caribbean precontact, Powhatan origins, and Southwest Athapaskan-vs-disease abandonment. Bibliographic anchors they privilege include Morison; Quinn; Sauer *Sixteenth Century North America*; Crosby *Columbian Exchange* and *Ecological Imperialism*; Dobyns and Thornton; Ramenofsky; Marvin T. Smith; Snow & Starna; Warrick; Miller; Weber *Spanish Frontier*; Spicer *Cycles of Conquest*; Swanton De Soto Commission; Trigger *Natives and Newcomers*; Turgeon Basque-Amerindian trade; Fitzgerald; Bradley; Handbook of North American Indians regional volumes. Most published works from the late fifteenth century to the present have focused on European images and perceptions of the environment and Native cultures at “first contact” rather than Native-centered process.
