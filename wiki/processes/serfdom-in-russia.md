# Serfdom in Russia

## Definition and Scope
**Serfdom in Russia** was the legal, economic, and social system of peasant bondage that tied the majority of the Russian agrarian population to private lands owned by the gentry, or to state-administered lands. Unlike Western European feudalism, Russian serfdom did not emerge from political decentralization; instead, it developed concurrently with the rise of the centralized Muscovite autocracy and became the fiscal and military foundation of the imperial state.
From Wirtschafter (Cambridge History of Russia Vol. II, ll. 9299–9323): Pre-1861, "the pursuit of profit, the satisfaction of greed and the struggle to subsist frequently took the form of forbidden economic activity that the government sought to eradicate or co-opt." Raznochintsy category "at once facilitated and undermined governmental control." Serf entrepreneurs like Nikolai Shipov operated illicitly until emancipation. Military conscription "produced a floating population" of soldatki (soldiers’ wives) freed from seignorial control but marginalised (no land share, communities intolerant, many to urban trades/prostitution). "The Russian government’s insistence that economic functions be based on social origin inevitably led ambitious and talented individuals to violate the law." `produced:` evasion + raznochintsy; `contributed_to:` instability of fortunes.

From Cambridge Vol. II (Moon ch.18, ll. 12301–12351, 12489+ exclusive range): “For much of the period, moreover, between a third and a half were the serfs of noble landowners”; “it is likely that Russian peasants were compelled to hand over around half of the product of their labour.” “The ways of life of much of the Russian peasantry had what are often assumed to be their ‘classic’... forms: the three-field system of arable farming; large, extended households; and communal land tenure.” “It seems likely that peasants in central Russia developed and adopted these practices in response to the growing restrictions and demands made on them as a result of the development of serfdom and the state’s increasing demands, which Peter the Great consolidated into the poll tax and regular levies of conscripts.” “The key to understanding the new system seems to be taxation.” Post-1861: reforms led to “a reduction in the burden of exploitation”; “slow improvement in peasant standards of living”; yet 1917 land seizures. `caused_by:` state service + poll tax + population; `produced:` classic institutions + resources for empire; `followed_by:` Great Reforms transition + 1917.

In the period 1462–1613 "the vast majority of the population in the years 1462–1613 were peasants who were becoming serfs, perhaps 85 per cent. Of the rest, perhaps 5 to 15 per cent were slaves." "Even townsmen, most clergymen and even many servicemen raised much of their own food." Peasants were treated as "tenants of the land, rather like immovable structures left by one holder of the land for the next one." This was ‘legalised’ by the state in two forms of state charters (vvoznaia gramota and poslushnaia gramota). (Cambridge History of Russia Vol. I, lines ~7564, 7643-7645 from exclusive range 7501-11000). "The Ulozhenie of 1649 also bound townsmen to their posady in parallel fashion."

## Causal Drivers
* **State Service and the Gentry:** To defend and expand its territory, Moscow relied on a service class of gentry warriors supported by land grants (*pomestie*). These landlords could only perform their military duties if their estates possessed an assured, stable labor force. "the purpose was to stabilise the labour force of the provincial middle service-class cavalry, who could not render military service in the absence of peasant rent-payers."
  From Lieven (Cambridge Vol. II ch.1, ll. 4335, 4429, 4449): “The tsarist autocracy and its alliance with a serf-owning nobility was an exceptionally effective (and ruthless) mechanism for mobilising resources...” “the Russian alliance between autocratic tsar and serf-owning mini-autocratic noble landlord allowed far greater exploitation of the peasantry than existed when the Ottoman system worked properly.” “the wide cultural gap between European elites and Russian masses” key factor in 1917 bitterness. From Weeks ch.2: serfdom context for nationalities policy (e.g. post-1863 reforms amid Great Reforms). From ch.7 Martin (ll. 7461+): 1812 fears of peasant revolt (Pugachev memory); serfs petitioned state for redress post-invasion; contributed to social brittleness and later revolutionary pressures.
* **Peasant Indebtedness and Precedent of Slavery:** "Bonds of dependence often began with individual contracts." More fundamentally, "Enserfment, especially as it descended into a slave-like condition, unquestionably would have been impossible without the fact that the Russians were accustomed to enslaving their own people." The major slavery institution by this time was "limited service contract slavery (kabal’noe kholopstvo). A Russian – typically a low-energy, low-initiative down-and-outer – approached another Russian and asked him to buy him. The transaction was phrased in terms of a loan: the ‘borrower’ took a sum (perhaps 1, 2 or 3 roubles) from the ‘lender’ and agreed to work for him for a year in lieu of paying interest on the loan... Almost no such ‘loans’ were ever repaid, and both parties realised from the start that the transaction was in reality a self-sale into perpetual slavery." Over the sixteenth century this replaced full slavery as the major relief institution. (l7560-7623 from range)
* **State Legislation and 'Agapetus state' intervention:** The Russian state actively intervened. "Then in the 1580s the government began to repeal the right of peasants to move on St George’s Day who lived on the lands of selected landholders. In 1592 this prohibition was extended ‘until further notice’ to all peasants." Boris Godunov added "another provision to the enserfment decree, a statute of limitations on the recovery of fugitive serfs... Five years seems like a long time, but Russia is a big country, and was getting bigger all the time." "Ivan IV during his mad oprichnina introduced a dramatic change into the ‘obedience charter’: instead of ordering the peasants to pay traditional rent, they were ordered to ‘obey their landholder in everything’. This gave the landholders complete control over their peasants." "The 1607 Shuiskii decree enhanced this abasement." "The period 1462–1613 witnessed intervention by the ‘Agapetus state’ ... in the lives of its subjects unparalleled in previous history." (l7628-7677 from range)
* **Three-field agriculture shift and depopulation:** Movement from slash-burn to three-field system expressed in Law Code (Sudebnik). "By 1550 the movement from slash-burn agriculture to the more intensive three-field system had progressed to the point that it was expressed in the Law Code (Sudebnik)." But depopulation after policies led to abandonment: "ensuing censuses found up to 85 per cent of the heartland of Muscovy, especially around Moscow and Novgorod, abandoned and the right of peasants to move on St George’s Day was gradually abolished. Also often abandoned was the three-field system of agriculture, which was not to become widely used again until the second half of the eighteenth century." (l7507-7556)

## Major Phases
1. **Initial Restrictions (Late 15th - 16th Century):** The legal right of peasants to leave their landlords was gradually restricted to St. George’s Day (26 November), after the winter crop had been sown. "Article 88 of the Sudebnik of 1550 permitted peasants who had moved on St George’s Day ... to return in the following summer to harvest that crop." Historians assume the three-field system was fairly widespread by 1550. Along with this went a system of strip-farming in which fields were divided into long, narrow strips allotted to spread risks equally. (l7533-7546 from range)
2. **"Forbidden Years", Oprichnina effects and Censuses (Late 16th Century):** Under Ivan IV and Boris Godunov, government began repealing rights and declaring forbidden years. Ivan's oprichnina and policies caused massive flight north, east and south. "ensuing censuses found up to 85 per cent of the heartland of Muscovy, especially around Moscow and Novgorod, abandoned and the right of peasants to move on St George’s Day was gradually abolished."
3. **Boris Godunov's 1590s Decrees (from range):** "Boris Godunov, acting in the name of the mentally challenged Tsar Fedor Ivanovich, tried to stabilise the situation by history-making measures enacted in the 1590s involving both slaves and peasants." In 1592 prohibition extended "until further notice" to all peasants; added 5-year statute for recovery of serfs. "with a flourish of the pen Boris Godunov’s hypertrophic government changed the legal status of more than nine-tenths of the Russian population."
4. **Ulozhenie of 1649 and later codification:** Built directly on 1590s foundations by removing statute of limitations (as prior details).
5. **Later phases:** (Petrine merging, Catherine peak, as prior).

## Geographic Spread
Serfdom predominated in the fertile black-earth regions of central, southern, and western Russia, where it supported gentry estates (L2520-2522, L3552). It did not take root in the northern territories or in Siberia, where a lack of military frontiers eliminated the need for a service gentry, leaving the population composed primarily of state peasants (L2230, L2522, L2602-2604). Under Catherine the Great, serfdom was legally extended to Ukraine (1763–1783), and Paul I extended it to the New Russia territories in the south in 1797 (L3388, L3500).

## Interaction
* **Economic Systems:** Serfdom in the south was tied to the *barshchina* (corvée labor) system, where serfs worked three to five days a week on the landlord's fields. In the less fertile north, the *obrok* (quitrent) system prevailed.
* **With Slavery:** "the simultaneity of the 1592 and 1597 decrees changing the status of the slaves and the peasants."
* **With State, Towns and Commerce:** Strict control advantages for "hard-pressed and financially constrained Muscovite state". Towns had 'black' posad people (liable to tiaglo) and 'white' suburbs (owned by nobility, Church etc.). "Members of the posad, and the land that they occupied, were designated ‘black’". The state relocated wealthy merchants to Moscow.
* **With Agriculture:** Three-field system adopted then often abandoned in crisis zones: "Also often abandoned was the three-field system of agriculture..."
* **Social Unrest:** "This was responsible for much of the peasant chaos that led to the repeal of the right to move on St George’s Day." Contributed to flight and Bolotnikov revolt. (Prior unrest details remain.)

## End Conditions
The binding was consolidated in 1649 and later; state regulation attempts came later (Paul I Three-Day Law etc.). The system persisted until 1861.
