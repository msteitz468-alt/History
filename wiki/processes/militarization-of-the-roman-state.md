# Militarization of the Roman State

The progressive shift, over the third century CE, of political power and
state resources toward the army and away from the civilian senatorial
order, beginning under the Severan dynasty and culminating in the endemic
military usurpations of the Crisis of the Third Century.

## Definition and Scope

The process describes a structural change in the relationship between
emperor, army, and civilian institutions — not any single reform, but the
cumulative effect of dynasties (especially the Severans under
[[septimius-severus|Septimius Severus]] and [[caracalla|Caracalla]]) buying
army loyalty through pay increases and expanded privileges, appointing
equestrian officers to posts once reserved for senators, and increasingly
depending on soldiers' direct political support to secure and hold power.

## Causal Drivers

Severus's own path to power through civil war (193 CE) taught him that
senatorial favor mattered far less than army loyalty; his deathbed advice to
his sons — "enrich the soldiers and scorn all other men" — became emblematic
of the dynasty's priorities. Sustained frontier pressure from a resurgent
Sasanian Persia and increasingly organized Germanic confederations further
required larger, better-paid, and more politically indulged forces.

## Major Phases

Severan pay raises and privilege expansion (193–235) gave way, after the
dynasty's collapse, to the Crisis of the Third Century (235–284), a period
of near-continuous army-driven imperial turnover in which legions and
frontier armies repeatedly proclaimed and deposed emperors, most without
senatorial involvement.

## Geographic Spread

The process was empire-wide but concentrated where large standing armies
were based — the eastern frontier facing Persia and the Rhine-Danube
frontier facing Germanic peoples — as regional armies increasingly acted as
independent political constituencies.

## Interaction

The trend fed directly into the [[civitas-sine-suffragio|broader
transformation]] of Roman civic and administrative structures across the
third century, and set the institutional preconditions that Diocletian's
later Dominate-era reforms (a further-restructured, more bureaucratized
military establishment) were designed to remedy.

## End Conditions

Diocletian's accession (284) and subsequent reforms — restructuring army
command, expanding the officer corps' professionalization, and separating
civil from military authority in the provinces — brought a measure of
stability, though the underlying dependence of imperial legitimacy on
army support remained a defining feature of the later Empire.
