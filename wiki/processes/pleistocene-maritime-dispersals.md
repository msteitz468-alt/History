## Definition and Scope

Pleistocene maritime dispersals encompass all sea-crossings by hominins — archaic and modern — between approximately 1 million and 12,000 years before present, from the first archaic crossings in Island Southeast Asia (ISEA) to the terminal-Pleistocene peopling of the Americas via the Pacific Rim coast. The process predates any surviving physical evidence of watercraft; "no boat remains, boat effigies, or boats depicted in rock art of Pleistocene age have so far been found" (Erlandson, in [[jones-matsuda-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v1-2022]]). Evidence is therefore indirect: the presence of human remains and artefacts on islands that were never connected to continental landmasses during any Pleistocene sea-level low.

The term "maritime dispersal" is used here to include simple raft or flotation-device crossings by archaic hominins as well as deliberate open-water voyaging by early modern humans (EMH). Erlandson defines boats as "virtually any type of watercraft more complex than a log," seafaring as "taking to sea in such boats," and voyaging as "any purposeful travel in boats."

Distinct from the later [[austronesian-expansion]], which was a Holocene agricultural-maritime expansion using sophisticated outrigger sailing craft, Pleistocene dispersals were hunter-gatherer colonization events using technology that left no surviving trace.

## Causal Drivers

**Southern Dispersal Route and mangrove habitats.** After EMH populations left Africa, "probably between ~80,000 and 60,000 years ago," one population may have followed the southern coast of Asia — Arabian Peninsula → ISEA → Pacific shores. Dense mangrove forests at major river mouths (e.g., the Ganges) have been argued to be barriers to overland movement but "would have offered a wealth of food resources to people in small boats. The resource diversity and richness of mangrove forests may have been major attractions for coastal peoples, and their protected waters could have been ideal habitat for the development of seaworthy boats" (Erlandson and Braje, cited in [[jones-matsuda-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v1-2022]]). Mangrove forests extend discontinuously from East Africa to ISEA, providing a broadly consistent coastal ecological corridor.

**Lowered sea levels in Wallacea.** During glacial periods much of western ISEA merged into Sundaland; eastern ISEA and Australia/New Guinea formed Sahul. The intervening island chain, Wallacea, remained separated at all sea-level stands. "During glacial periods and low sea-stands, much of what is now western ISEA was united into a subcontinental land mass known as Sunda (or Sundaland) that could be settled without boats." Crossing distances in Wallacea — while reduced from modern values — still required water transport.

**Kelp highway productivity (for Americas colonization).** Nearshore Pacific Rim kelp forests "shelter a diverse array of marine organisms — fish, shellfish, seaweeds, seabirds, marine mammals, and more — providing high nearshore marine productivity." Erlandson et al.'s kelp highway hypothesis proposes this productivity, combined with the ecological homogeneity of Pacific Rim kelp communities over vast distances, "facilitated a rapid coastal dispersal" from Northeast Asia into the Americas, perhaps as early as 17,000–18,000 years ago.

## Major Phases

### Phase 1 — Archaic hominin crossings in Wallacea (~1,000,000–200,000 BP)

Archaic members of the genus Homo reached the shores of the northwest Pacific "nearly 2 million years ago." Within Wallacea, archaeological evidence confirms sea-crossings to:
- **Flores**: occupation by at least 1 million BP (H. erectus → H. floresiensis)
- **Philippines**: at ~709,000 BP
- **Sulawesi**: >200,000 BP (possibly Denisovans)

No credible evidence exists that any archaic hominin reached Sahul (greater Australia/New Guinea), Near Oceania, the Ryukyus, or Japan. "The fact that only Homo sapiens reached greater Australia and Near Oceania, and not until after ~50,000–60,000 years ago, suggests that archaic seafaring was limited in nature, both technologically and geographically." Genomic data from Aboriginal Australians and Papuans suggest EMH interbred with archaic humans in Wallacea before reaching Sahul.

### Phase 2 — EMH colonization of Sahul and Near Oceania (~60,000–40,000 BP)

"By about 60,000–50,000 years ago, early modern humans reached ISEA and Wallacea, and soon reached greater Australia and several islands of Near Oceania." The colonization of Sahul required "multiple sea-crossings ranging up to 80–100 kilometres in length," some of them blue-water crossings with no land visible on the horizon. Archaeological evidence now places EMH in Australia by "55,000 ± 5,000 years ago."

By approximately 40,000 BP, descendants of the Sahul colonists reached New Ireland, New Britain, and the Solomon Islands — requiring "several additional voyages of equal or even greater distances." Coastal subsistence is documented: sites in ISEA and Near Oceania contain "marine shellfish and fish being heavily represented." On New Britain, obsidian moved between islands from at least the Pleistocene. At Buang Merabak (New Ireland), a perforated tiger shark tooth — "the earliest evidence so far recorded for personal adornment during the Pleistocene in Papua New Guinea" — was recovered from levels dated to ~44,890–43,100 BP.

### Phase 3 — Northwest Pacific dispersal: Ryukyus and Japan (~35,000–37,000 BP)

"By at least 35,000 years ago, long-distance voyages by Upper Palaeolithic peoples led to the colonization of Okinawa and several other islands within the ~1,000 kilometres long Ryukyu archipelago, probably via a maritime dispersal following the Kuroshio Current from the Taiwan area." These maritime peoples — probably ancestors of the Ainu — may also have settled the Japanese main islands. In Japan, "by at least 37,000 years ago, seaworthy boats were used to obtain and transport obsidian from Kozushima Island, involving voyages of up to 50 kilometres each way." The Kuroshio route was reconstructed in a 2019 experimental voyage in a Jomon-style cedar dugout.

### Phase 4 — Coastal migration to the Americas (~18,000–13,000 BP)

As the Last Glacial Maximum waned after ~18,000 BP, no major geographic barriers would have blocked maritime peoples already present in Northeast Asia from moving eastward along the Beringian and Northwest North American coasts. The coastal route is argued to have become passable and ecologically productive "perhaps as much as 17,000–18,000 years ago and two to three millennia before the ice-free corridor became passable and ecologically productive."

Key evidence:
- **Monte Verde II (Chile)**: at least eight types of edible kelps and seaweeds, AMS-dated to "~14,000–14,500 years ago — a millennium before Clovis"
- **Huaca Prieta (Peru)**: ~14,200–13,300 BP, heavy reliance on marine resources including pinnipeds, fish, seabirds, shellfish
- **British Columbia archipelagoes**: stone artefacts on ancient beach ~13,800 BP; human footprints and artefacts in intertidal muds ~13,000 BP; artefact dredged from submerged delta ~11,500 BP
- **Northern Channel Islands, Alta California**: human presence by at least 13,000 BP; kelp forest resources (abalone, shellfish) harvested by 12,000 BP; "these Palaeocoastal peoples had some sort of seafaring capabilities . . . at the same time that Clovis hunters were roaming the interior"
- **Cedros Island, Baja California**: shell middens dated to ~12,000 BP with diversified coastal harvest

The central archaeological limitation is that "most Late Pleistocene coastlines associated with a Pacific Rim route have been submerged by rising post-glacial seas," placing the primary evidence zone under modern ocean.

## Geographic Spread

The process spans the full Pacific Rim: East Africa → Arabia → South Asia → ISEA → Wallacea → Sahul → Near Oceania → Japan/Ryukyus → Northeast Asia → Beringia → North American Pacific Coast → South American Pacific Coast. Its total latitudinal range extends from roughly 60°N (Beringia) to 42°S (Monte Verde), encompassing tropical, subtropical, temperate, and subarctic maritime environments.

## End Conditions

Pleistocene maritime dispersals as a process effectively end with the close of the Pleistocene (~12,000 BP) and the commencement of the Holocene. By this date, EMH had occupied the entire Pacific Rim coastal zone. The process did not stop — maritime movement continued — but the Holocene transition introduced agriculture, intensified sedentism, and eventually the outrigger/sail complex that enabled the qualitatively distinct [[austronesian-expansion]] into Remote Oceania.

The Americas were the last major landmasses reached, and even within this terminal phase there is ongoing debate about precise dates, routes (single vs. multiple dispersal events, coastal vs. interior), and the relative chronology of coastal and interior colonization.

## Historiography

**Source quality.** Erlandson's chapter in [[jones-matsuda-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v1-2022]] provides a 2022 synthesis. The primary evidence base is archaeological (island assemblages, coastal sites, obsidian sourcing) and genomic (ancient and modern DNA tracing population movement and archaic admixture). No physical watercraft survive from the Pleistocene anywhere in the world.

**Scholarly debates.** Three major disputes remain active:
1. *Archaic seafaring capability*: Did H. erectus use intentional sophisticated watercraft (Bednarik's position, citing Flores) or was archaic crossing accidental/opportunistic? Current consensus leans toward the latter given the absence of archaic presence in Sahul.
2. *Kelp highway vs. ice-free corridor*: The coastal route hypothesis displacing Clovis First has gained majority support since Monte Verde's acceptance in the late 1990s, but precise chronology, number of founding populations, and relationship to interior dispersals remain contested.
3. *Australia/Sahul dating*: Claims for occupation at 65,000 BP (optically stimulated luminescence, Madjedbebe) remain disputed against a more conservative ~50,000–55,000 BP consensus.
