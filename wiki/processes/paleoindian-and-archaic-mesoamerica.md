# Paleoindian and Archaic Mesoamerica

## Definition and Scope

The deep prehistory of [[mesoamerica-culture-area|Mesoamerica]] from first human entry
through the end of the Archaic / Lithic–Preceramic era, principally as synthesized by
Robert N. Zeitlin and Judith Francis Zeitlin in *The Cambridge History of the Native
Peoples of the Americas*, Vol. II Part 1, Ch. 2 (CHNPA II.1). The stage covers mobile
foragers, the Pleistocene–Holocene transition, and the long shift toward food production
that enables later [[mesoamerican-preclassic|Preclassic]] villages.

Zeitlin & Zeitlin open with two bookends: Mesoamerican prehistory begins **more than
12,500 years ago, and perhaps as early as 35,000 years ago**, with small hunter-gatherer
bands from northeast Asia; by **9000 b.c.** such foragers extended coast-to-coast and
from Alaska to Tierra del Fuego. Post-Pleistocene megafaunal extinctions and population
pressure drove new subsistence methods culminating in agriculture (maize, beans, squash,
and **more than forty other cultigens**); by **1500 b.c.** much of Mesoamerica was
dotted with small permanent farming villages — the platform for later civilization.

Over 5–7 millennia of the Archaic the package of change is profound but regionally
uneven: hunting/wild gathering → agriculture; itinerant life → permanent villages;
egalitarian achievement-based bands → ranked chiefdoms by ancestry.

## Causal Drivers

- **Colonization** via Beringian and/or Pacific coastal routes (timing contested; CHNPA
  allows ≥12,500 BP and canvasses claims into the 30,000s BP) — concurrent with
  [[clovis-first-vs-pre-clovis]].
- **Environmental reorganization** after the Ice Age: Late Pleistocene Mesoamerica was
  generally drier and cooler than today; glaciers confined to mountain ice fields (not
  continental sheets); megafauna present (mastodon, mammoth, horse, camelids, etc.).
- **Extinction and dietary breadth**: Paleoindians are often identified with megafauna
  hunting, but smaller animals and wild plants played a prominent subsistence role.
  P. S. Martin's "Pleistocene overkill" (~32 genera extinct with human assistance) is
  challenged in Mesoamerica: no Great Plains-style multi-animal slaughter; evidence
  favors a more varied diet outside the SW United States.
- **Plant management and domestication** — see [[maize-domestication]],
  [[early-agriculture-americas]], [[domestication]].
- **Interregional exchange** by Late Archaic (obsidian flakes/blades widespread; seeds
  and plants likely moved in the same networks).

## Major Phases

### Paleoindian

**Research bias.** Reconstruction leans on interior highlands. Coastal lowlands lost much
Paleoindian record to rising post-Pleistocene sea levels, land subsidence, and poor
organic preservation. Consistent research accelerated only from ~1960; early sites are
mostly campsites or single-event kill sites. Aveleyra de Anda's work on the Iztapan
mammoth kills is still cited as unmatched for technique.

**Routes and biological markers.** Consensus first Americans: anatomically modern
hunter-gatherers who crossed **Beringia** from NE Asia during the final Wisconsin
Pleistocene (~70,000–10,000 ya; sea levels down up to 150 m; grassy plain >1,500 km
wide), possibly in several waves. Inland route: unglaciated interior Alaska then south
via the Yukon ice-free corridor between Laurentide and Cordilleran ice — but geology
suggests the McKenzie Basin corridor opened only ~11,500 ya with limited resources
afterward. Scarcity of early sites and corridor doubts revive Fladmark's **maritime
hypothesis** (boats via Aleutians/Pacific littoral); Peru coastal camps ~13,000 B.P.
and west-coast language diversity estimates (~143 languages; some estimated ~35,000-year
depth) support coastal entry. Shared **Sinodont** dental traits link historic NE Asians
and Native Americans (separation >15,000 ya); mtDNA analyses favor multiple Asian waves
beginning as early as 40,000 ya.

**Very early Mesoamerican candidates (contested).** Valsequillo Reservoir cluster near
Puebla (Hueyatlaco, Tecacaxco, El Mirador, El Horno, Cualapan; Armenta Camacho &
Irwin-Williams 1962–66): lithics with Pleistocene fauna; experimental tephra hydration /
U-series ~250,000 BP largely disparaged; Irwin-Williams preferred ~21,850±850 b.p.
radiocarbon from Cuilapan barranca. Other candidates in the 14,000–35,000 ya bracket:
Tlapacoya (Lake Chalco; hearth 21,700±506 b.p. accepted by excavators but contemporaneity
of tools and dates criticized); Rancho La Amapola / El Cedral (hearth 31,850±600 b.p.;
only three definite artifacts); El Bosque (Nicaragua; dates and tool authenticity
doubted); Loltún Cave (Yucatán Puuc) — only lowland candidate, no 14C, typology/fauna
only. None universally accepted; full debate on [[clovis-first-vs-pre-clovis]].

**By 12,000 ya** Paleoindian settlement was unequivocally widespread; well-dated Clovis
(first defined on the New Mexico high plains) set the basemark since radiocarbon's
1950s introduction. Clovis/Clovis-like fluted points (mostly surface finds) run from
northern Mexican states through highland central Mexico (Tlaxcala, Puebla, Oaxaca) south
to Belize, Guatemala, Costa Rica; Ladyville (coastal Belize) extends the range to
lowlands. MacNeish's Lowe-Ha complex co-occurrence of Clovis with "fishtail" points
(9000–7500 b.c.) would indicate contact of two Late Paleoindian traditions (fishtails
at Fell's Cave, Chile; El Inga, Ecuador; Madden Lake, Panama; Quiché Basin, Guatemala);
Kelly questions Belize fishtail classification. Later unfluted Planoid types (Lerma,
Angostura, Dalton, Plainview, Scottsbluff) date ~9000–7000 B.C.

**Santa Isabel Iztapan** (ancient Lake Texcoco, Basin of Mexico): two dismembered mammoth
skeletons with Lerma, Scottsbluff, and Angostura points; cut marks and associated
chert/obsidian tools indicate on-site butchery; some lithics from hundreds of km away
(mobility or early exchange). Adams (CHNPA intro) notes thirty-two genera disappeared
by 9000 B.C. with assistance from human hunting — counter to neo-Rousseauian harmony
tropes.

**Broad-spectrum Late Paleoindian subsistence.** Tehuacan Archaeological-Botanical
Project (MacNeish): **Ajuereado** phase 12,000–9,000 ya — Lerma and Plainview-like
points, atlatl; fossil horse and antelope present, but many small animals (jackrabbit,
gopher, rat, turtle, birds) plus avocado, agave, opuntia. Coxcatlan Cave yielded nearly
400 jackrabbit bones in one lower zone (communal drives). Flannery: climatic faunal
shift from herd species (pronghorn, jackrabbit) to solitary (white-tailed deer,
cottontail) forced hunting-strategy change. Social unit: mobile **microband** of ~4–8
related individuals; density <1 person per 5–70 sq mi of 1,400 sq mi survey. Cueva
Blanca Zone F (Oaxaca) is contemporary with Ajuereado.

**Early art and burial.** Tequixquiac (Hidalgo): camelid sacrum carved as animal head —
if Late Paleoindian, among earliest indigenous New World art (Aveleyra reanalysis
supports antiquity). Tepexpan skeleton (1947, de Terra): adult female flexed facedown
burial, roughly contemporary with Iztapan mammoths — earliest known formal burial in
Mesoamerica, implying spirit/afterlife concepts.

**Early unifacial tradition hypothesis.** Absence of bifacial flaking at El Horno and
lower Hueyatlaco is taken by some (Krieger; Bryan, MacNeish, Willey) as Early Paleoindian
characteristic extending to ~40,000 ya — simple unifacial tools ancestral to later
bifacial points, paralleling NE Asian Upper Paleolithic; early migrants may have been
opportunistic foragers with perishable specialized tools (Monte Verde reminder).

### Evans & Webster 2001 — Introduction frame

The encyclopedia Introduction (Webster & Evans) restates the consensus that New World
populations came from northeast Asia via Beringian land bridge and/or shoreline boats.
**Clovis** peoples with distinctive projectile points were widespread in the western
United States by **11,500–11,000 B.P.**; similar artifacts appear in northern Mexico with
surface finds as far south as Panama. **Santa Isabel Iztapan** (Basin of Mexico): mammoth
kill/butchery ~**11,000 B.P.** Northern Yucatán carried temperate thorn-scrub vegetation
before ~14,000 years ago rather than tropical forest.

On **pre-13,500 B.P.** claims: 1997 Paleoindian consensus accepted human settlement in
central Chile by at least 13,500 B.P. (possibly much earlier), implying earlier passage
through Middle America, but **solid local evidence predating the very end of the
Pleistocene remains lacking**. Valsequillo and Tlapacoya (Central Highlands) and El
Bosque (Nicaragua) yield possible tools with extinct fauna and radiocarbon >21,000 B.P.,
but associations and artifact status remain questionable — more *plausible* after South
American redating, still not *secure*.

**Archaic (8000–2000 B.C.)** is framed as the interval of agricultural origins: Mesoamerica
as a world domestication hearth with sparse animal domesticates (dogs, turkeys, ducks)
but rich plant package (maize, beans, squash, chocolate, tomatoes, chile, cotton,
avocado, gourds, tobacco, etc.). Maize from **teosinte** in west-central Mexican uplands;
directly dated samples ≤~4,700 years; likely first domestication **4000–3000 B.C.**;
wide spread 4,000–3,500 years ago as adjunct to wild diets; after **2000 B.C.** many
Mesoamericans were settled pottery-using agriculturalists. Key dry-cave evidence:
Tehuacán, Oaxaca, Tamaulipas (southern fringe of the Northern Arid Zone).

Source: [[evans-webster-archaeology-ancient-mexico-central-america-2001]].

### Archaic

**Start date.** Radiocarbon dates from two stratified Oaxaca Valley sites establish the
Mesoamerican Archaic beginning at **8750 b.c.** (though many still use traditional
7000 b.c. tied to horse extinction); by the earlier date southern Mesoamerica already
used fully modern flora and fauna.

**Tehuacan type sequence** remains the principal characterization: three Archaic phases —
**El Riego** (7000–5000 b.c.), **Coxcatlan** (5000–3400 b.c.), **Abejas** (3400–2000
b.c.); 35 components from nine sites; **Coxcatlan Cave** alone provided ~75% of
classified excavated stone tools and the richest plant floors — the most extensive
diachronic plant-domestication record in Mesoamerica (subject to AMS revision on
[[maize-domestication]]). Lithic industry is a gradual continuum; phases are heuristic
Early/Middle/Late segments. Manos and metates mark growing plant importance.
MacNeish's Tamaulipas projects (1948–55) and Tehuacan (1960–63) produced the prodigious
data base; Callen's fecal-material method assessed animal vs vegetable food proportions.

**Desert Culture linkage.** Northern Mexico (just beyond Mesoamerican boundaries) links
highland central Mexico Archaic to SW United States Desert Culture via shared points
and continuous environments. Coahuila caves (Frightful, Fat Burro, Nopal Shelter) from
~7600 b.c. with little change into historic times: plant-heavy diet, storage pits,
fiber/wood technology; most NE Mexican sites lack cultigens.

**Regional highland and lowland sequences (selected).**

| Region / site | Archaic signal (CHNPA II.1 Ch. 2) |

| Gheo-Shih (Oaxaca) | ~5000–4000 b.c. "dance ground" boulder lines; possible macroband aggregation |
| Blanca / Martinez (Oaxaca) | Blanca ~3300–2800 b.c.; Martinez eve of sedentism ~2100–2000 b.c. |
| Zohapilco (Lake Chalco) | Playa 5059–4250 b.c.; multi-season lacustrine economy; possible preagricultural sedentism (no architecture); late Archaic cultivation by ~3000 b.c. |
| Santa Marta (Chiapas) | Only well-dated southern highland sequence; **no domesticates** even in Early Formative Santa Marta V (1300–1000 b.c.) |

**Broad-spectrum readaptation and scheduling.** Increasing variety of remains points to
Flannery's "broad spectrum readaptation" (term borrowed from Near Eastern pre-Neolithic;
Edwards challenges terminal-Pleistocene timing of broad-spectrum as stage-setting).
Early Archaic components with good preservation (Guila Naquitz, Santa Marta) show mixed
foraging by ≥7000 b.c. **Scheduling** prioritized seasonal wild resources; highland
camps seasonal, limited storage. **Macroband** (e.g. rainy-season Coxcatlan, 15–25 people
= 3–4 families) vs microband camps; Oaxaca specialized site types (chert quarrying,
maguey roasting, hunting) increase as foragers shift toward Binford-style collecting —
possibly significant for permanent residence.

**Sedentism still poorly evidenced.** Permanent-residence architecture not confirmed
until Late Archaic and remains ambiguous (Abejas "pit house" 3500–2300 b.c. may be
temporary). Lacustrine/coastal habitats *could* have supported preagricultural sedentism
(cf. coastal Peru), but Chantuto residential claims have been disclaimed; Santa Luisa and
Zohapilco offer multi-season economies without permanent structures. **Village life not
widely established until after 2000 b.c.** Greater settlement stability from collecting
may have incentivized plant domestication rather than vice versa.

**Lithics and integration.** Archaic lithic inventories are mainly casual flakes from
local materials; standardized tools unusual except shared projectile-point styles, the
Belize macroblade industry, and Middle Archaic ground stone. By Late Archaic, highland
obsidian is common at most highland *and* lowland sites — well-developed interregional
interchange relevant to agricultural spread.

## Geographic Spread

Coast to coast within later Mesoamerica; strongest botanical and diachronic sequences
in highland dry caves (Tehuacan, Tamaulipas, Oaxaca); lacustrine Basin of Mexico
(Zohapilco); Pacific and Gulf coastal shell/midden economies; lowland pollen sequences
in Belize/Yucatán/Veracruz wetlands. Northern Mexico Archaic links to the SW Desert
Culture beyond the culture-area boundary.

## End Conditions

Not a collapse. Archaic lifeways grade into Formative agricultural villages at different
times by region — typically second millennium BCE for permanent farming villages across
much of Mesoamerica (Adams: agricultural villages established all over by 1500 B.C.;
Archaic stage 8/7000–1500 B.C.). CHNPA notes the database remains provisional: AMS and
pollen refine results, but fundamental revision of initial settlement and agriculture is
under way; aesthetics, ideology, and religion of Paleoindian–Archaic research are still
in their infancy.

## Historiography (CHNPA II.1)

Zeitlin & Zeitlin (Ch. 2) is the volume's systematic synthesis of Paleoindian–Archaic
Mesoamerica. MacNeish's Tamaulipas and Tehuacan projects founded modern plant-domestication
chronologies later revised by B. D. Smith's AMS work and Long et al. on Tehuacan
corncobs. Flannery's Oaxaca projects (Guila Naquitz, Cueva Blanca, Gheo-Shih) supply the
best Early Archaic foraging models. Collection coverage for the Americas remains
archaeology-heavy; this chapter is a primary structural source for the wiki's Mesoamerican
deep prehistory.

## From Evans & Webster 2001 — Paleoindian and Archaic entries

### Paleoindian (Thomas R. Hester)

Conservatively, first people in Mesoamerica are placed at **11,500 years ago** because Clovis
points of early Paleoindian occur across Mexico, Guatemala, Belize, and into Central America,
contemporary with the Llano Complex (11,200–11,500 ya) of the American Southwest. Most
Mesoamerican Clovis points are **surface finds**; none has been found in stratigraphic context,
and none is associated with mammoth kills. Fell’s Cave/fish-tail fluted points known in Belize
and Panama—South American fluting tradition extended north; age relative to Clovis unclear.
Late Paleoindian (8200–6800 B.C. Southern Plains forms) hard to recognize. Golondrina Complex
(~9,000 ya) at San Isidro (Nuevo León); La Calsada Rockshelter bipointed “Lerma” points in
clear early context 9550–10,600 ya. Hester urges regional Mesoamerican Paleoindian patterns
rather than forcing North American typology—“materials may prove much different.” See
[[clovis-first-vs-pre-clovis]] for Pre-Clovis claims assessment.

### Archaic overview (Richard S. MacNeish)

Archaic follows Paleoindian hunting of extinct Pleistocene fauna and precedes Formative
(~2000 B.C.) agriculture, village life, and pottery. Three substages: (1) Early Archaic/First
Forager—seasonal scheduling, grinding stones, weaving, storage; (2) Middle Archaic/Incipient
Agriculture—agriculture beginnings, stone bowls, complex storage, notched atlatl points;
(3) Late Archaic “semisedentary”—expanded agriculture, more domesticates, pit houses,
mano-metate complex. Limited remains indicate ≥3 highland and 3–5 lowland traditions + northern
periphery. **El Riego tradition** c. 7600–5000 B.C. from Tecolote Cave (Hidalgo) to El Riego
in the [[tehuacan-region|Tehuacán Valley]]. Late Archaic highland/lowland dichotomy foreshadows
Formative Mesoamerica; Zohapilco levels may be permanent settlements; Tehuacán may include
pit-house hamlets + macroband camps linked to surplus hybrid maize. Gulf Coast Palo Hueco at
Santa Luisa (Veracruz) c. 3250–2410 B.C. may be large preceramic village. “The Archaic is the
base on which the Formative stage of Mesoamerican culture was built.”

### Domestication trajectory (Scott L. Fedick)

Highland valleys of Oaxaca, Tehuacán, and to some degree Basin of Mexico: hunting-gathering
~10,000–7000 B.C. with shift from large game to smaller game and plants; plant collection
increases 7000–5000 B.C.; small domesticated maize ~5000 B.C.; by ~1500 B.C. (or earlier)
permanent settlements with full-time agriculture. Pollen: maize into humid southern tropics
~3000 B.C. with Archaic slash-and-burn as supplement; sedentary villages in lowlands
~2000–1000 B.C. with coastal/estuarine resources + incipient agriculture. Domestic dogs not
evidenced before ~2000 B.C.; Tehuacán caves ~1900–1400 B.C. (Flannery).

### Sites

**Gheo-Shih** (Oaxaca): 1.5-ha open-air hunting/gathering/incipient cultivation site with
boulder-lined cleared ritual/dance area (Flannery). **La Conchita** (Veracruz): 7600 B.P. hearth
with pebble chopper—oldest confirmed human presence in eastern lowland Mexico (Wilkerson).
**Cueva Blanca** (Oaxaca): Late Ice Age fauna then 3300–2800 B.C. levels with gendered activity
areas. Tehuacán Ajuereado–Abejas phases: see [[tehuacan-region]].
