## Definition and Scope

The eleventh and twelfth centuries saw the **greatest expansion and diversification
of the religious life in European history**. [[luscombe-ncmh-v4-2004|NCMH IV]] (Giles
Constable, Ch. 10, "Religious communities, 1024–1215") judges that "more people —
probably in absolute as well as relative terms — were attracted to the life of
religion in the eleventh and twelfth centuries than at any other time in the history
of Europe." The number of monks, nuns, and canons in England grew from fewer than
1,000 to over 10,000 between the Norman Conquest and the thirteenth century; German
nunneries rose from ~70 (900) to ~500 (1250); the abbey of [[cluny|Cluny]] itself
quadrupled. Contemporaries spoke of "the confusion growing with the crowd of
religious" (*cum turba crevit turbatio*).

## Causal Drivers

- **The spiritual energies of the [[gregorian-reform|reform]]**, which raised the
  prestige of the religious life and the demand for clerical and monastic purity.
- **The ideal of the *vita apostolica*** — the apostolic life of the primitive Church,
  which now expanded from a model of contemplative withdrawal to embrace **preaching
  and pastoral work**, fuelling both the regular canons and (later) the friars.
- **Material and demographic growth**, which supplied recruits, patrons, and the
  endowments for thousands of new foundations.

## Major Phases and Forms

1. **The Benedictine apogee (11th c.).** [[cluny|Cluny]] reached its height under
   Abbots Odilo and Hugh, the head of a vast congregation and "the treasury of the
   entire Christian republic," with reform offshoots such as **Hirsau** spreading the
   Cluniac model in Germany.
2. **The eremitical reaction (late 11th c.).** A search for greater austerity produced
   the **Carthusians** (Bruno of Cologne, La Grande Chartreuse, 1084) and a wave of
   hermits, recluses, and anchorites — at least 200 anchorites in thirteenth-century
   England, over half of them women.
3. **The "new monasticism" (12th c.).** The **[[cistercian-order|Cistercians]]**
   (Cîteaux, 1098; transformed by **Bernard of Clairvaux**) rejected Cluniac wealth and
   liturgical elaboration for manual labour, austerity, and grange farming, becoming
   the dominant order of the century. The **regular canons** (following the Rule of St
   Augustine) and the **Premonstratensians** (Norbert of Xanten, Prémontré, 1120)
   combined community life with pastoral care.
4. **Toward the friars.** The expansion of the *vita apostolica* toward preaching and
   poverty set the stage for the [[mendicant-orders|mendicant orders]] (Franciscans,
   Dominicans) of the early thirteenth century, beyond this volume's close.

## Interaction

Constable stresses that religious communities were **not a counter-society** but
"embodied the highest ideals of medieval society" and performed functions later
assumed by public institutions: education, charity, hospitality, care of the poor,
pilgrims, and the sick, the keeping of roads, and counsel to rulers (Suger of St
Denis, Bernard of Clairvaux, Hildegard of Bingen). All popes from 1073 to 1119, and a
quarter of the cardinals, had been monks; the canonist Gratian came from the monastic
order. Monastic values "permeated both lay and ecclesiastical society."

## End Conditions

The proliferation provoked controversy (the Cluniac–Cistercian polemics; criticism of
"too many monks") and was eventually regulated: the Fourth Lateran Council (1215)
forbade the foundation of new orders with new rules, channelling future religious
enthusiasm into the existing forms and the coming mendicants.
