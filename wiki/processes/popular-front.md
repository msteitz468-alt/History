## Definition and Scope

The Popular Front was a broad electoral and political alliance — from middle-class
democrats through Social Democrats to Communists — formed to oppose fascism and
authoritarianism in the mid-1930s. It is not a single organization but a strategy and,
where successful, a governing coalition. It explicitly *postponed* social revolution and
even most social reform in order to keep the alliance as broad as possible; Léon Blum
described his own government's mandate as "exercise of power within the framework of
existing institutions" rather than "conquest of power." It achieved parliamentary
majorities only in France and Spain (1936); elsewhere it remained an aspiration or a
street-level phenomenon that party leaderships never formalized.

Popular Front is distinct from a "united front" (the earlier, narrower Communist tactic
of recruiting rank-and-file socialist workers away from their own leadership) and from
wartime "national unity" governments, which cut across the Left/Right axis entirely
rather than uniting the Left and center against a common enemy.

## Causal Drivers

- **contributed_to by**: the Comintern's reversal, in 1934–1935, of Stalin's 1928 "class
  against class" line, which had branded Social Democrats "social fascists" and produced
  occasional Communist–Nazi tactical cooperation in Germany (the August 1931 Prussian
  recall referendum, the November 1932 Berlin transport strike).
- **caused_by**: Stalin's changed calculation in spring 1934 — fear of a two-front war
  with Germany and Japan — which led the USSR to join the League of Nations (September
  1934) and pursue collective security (Litvinov's "eastern Locarno," the May 1935
  Franco-Soviet pact).
- **contributed_to by**: the shock of the French right-wing riots of February 6, 1934,
  which convinced ordinary workers (ahead of their party leaderships) of the need for
  unity — the spontaneous fusion of Communist and Socialist/Radical marches on February
  12, 1934, when Maurice Thorez, Léon Blum, and Édouard Daladier linked arms.
- **enabled by**: middle-class Radical parties in Catholic countries (strong only in
  France and Spain), which were anticlerical and civil-libertarian enough to ally with
  the Left while remaining conservative on property — this is why Popular Fronts achieved
  power only in France and Spain and nowhere else in Europe.

## Major Phases

1. **1934 — street-level fusion, elite hesitation.** The Paris demonstrations of
   February 12, 1934 preceded any formal party agreement by over a year.
2. **July 14, 1935 — formal proclamation.** Thorez, Blum, and Daladier publicly declared
   the Popular Front at the Bastille Day parade, following the growth of the Croix de feu
   past one million members.
3. **February–June 1936 — Spanish and French electoral victories.** The Spanish Popular
   Front won in February 1936; the French Popular Front won 386 of 608 seats in May–June
   1936 (57% of the vote, up only modestly from 54% in 1932 — the two-round system and
   non-competition between Communists and Socialists in runoffs drove the seat swing).
4. **June 1936–June 1937 — Blum's government ("the French New Deal").** Paid vacations,
   collective bargaining law, cautious rearmament; undermined by financial strain, the
   Spanish nonintervention controversy, and Senate refusal of full powers (June 1937).
5. **July 1936–March 1939 — Spain moves from Popular Front government to civil war**
   after Franco's revolt; see [[spanish-civil-war-1936]].
6. **1937–November 1938 — disintegration.** The Communist-led suppression of the
   syndicalist POUM in Catalonia (June 1937) split the Spanish Left; in France, Radical
   support for strikebreaking measures in November 1938 formally ended the alliance.

## Geographic Spread

Formal parliamentary Popular Fronts existed only in **France** and **Spain**. Popular
Front-style antifascist sentiment was widespread among European intellectuals (Picasso's
*Guernica*, the ~40,000 International Brigade volunteers in Spain) but did not translate
into governing coalitions elsewhere — Britain, the Low Countries, and Scandinavia
retained stable parliamentary majorities that made a Popular Front unnecessary;
authoritarian or fascist regimes elsewhere (Portugal, Austria, most of Eastern Europe)
pre-empted it by suppressing the Left before 1934.

## Interaction

- Directly produced the French government's response to the [[spanish-civil-war-1936]]
  — the Non-Intervention Commission and covert, limited aid — which in turn discredited
  the Popular Front with its own Left.
- Intersects with [[appeasement]]: Popular Front sympathizers (pro-Soviet-alliance,
  pro-intervention in Spain) and appeasers were often mirror-image camps in French and
  British politics, with conservative fear of "Stalin's War" driving opposition to both
  the Franco-Soviet pact's ratification and to firmer resistance to Hitler.
- Its failure in France (Radical defection, financial crisis, Senate veto) left France
  "more polarized than ever" on the eve of the German remilitarization crises — see
  [[remilitarization-of-the-rhineland-1936]].

## End Conditions

The Popular Front ended for different reasons in each country: in Spain, military defeat
in the [[spanish-civil-war-1936]] (Madrid fell March 28, 1939); in France, internal
collapse — Radical defection, financial retreat under Blum, the Senate's refusal of full
powers in June 1937, and the formal dissolution of the coalition when Radicals backed
strikebreaking in November 1938. In neither country did it survive to contest the next
scheduled elections (Spain's civil war, France's 1940 elections were both overtaken by
war). Paxton and Hessler judge that "nowhere did the Popular Front recipe manage to unite
free Europe against fascist and authoritarian regimes" — it left Britain, France, and the
Soviet Union mutually distrustful on the eve of World War II.
