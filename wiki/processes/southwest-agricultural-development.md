# Southwest Agricultural Development

## Definition and Scope

The development of mixed farming–gathering–hunting economies in the North American Southwest culture area — modern Arizona and New Mexico, SE Utah, SW Colorado, and (beyond the U.S.–Mexico border of the CHNPA series) Chihuahua, Sonora, and Sinaloa. *part_of:* [[north-american-agricultural-transition]]. Aridity is the primary climatic unifier across diverse topography. Spanish explorers from 1539 found peoples linked by trade and some shared religion, not by a single polity or language.

Unlike the Eastern Woodlands, SW farming was **not** an independent domestication center: principal crops were Mexican-origin *Zea mays*, *Cucurbita pepo*, and beans (*Phaseolus vulgaris*, *P. acutifolius*, *Canavalia ensiformis*), later joined by cotton, sieva beans, and pigweed; turkeys were domesticated in the northern Southwest. Cordell & Smith (CHNPA I.1 ch. 4) treat SW trajectories alongside Eastern ones as parallel but non-convergent historical paths.

## Causal Drivers

- **Crop package introduction** from Mexico via lowland (west of Sierra Madre Occidental) and highland routes along it — *caused_by:* [[maize-domestication]] as introduced package; *part_of:* [[early-agriculture-americas]].
- **Environmental constraints:** low deserts <20 cm annual precipitation require irrigation; higher mesas/mountains get ~35–40 cm unreliably; May–June virtually rainless so germination depends on winter moisture or hand watering; frost-free season adequate in low deserts but often insufficient for reliable corn in mountains and narrow canyons.
- **Dean’s Colorado Plateaus model:** low-frequency processes (LFP; >25-year periodicities: arroyo cutting/deposition, water-table change) vs high-frequency processes (HFP; annual precipitation/growing-season variation). Farmers buffer HFP via storage, water-control, and wild foods; HFP become critical as population nears long-term carrying capacity; major adaptive change including regional abandonment when LFP reductions coincide with HFP shortfalls.
- Settlement aggregation, labor coordination for canals, and ritual centers ([[chaco-canyon]], Hohokam ball courts/platform mounds).

## Major Phases

Cordell & Gumerman period scheme (correlated with paleoenvironment):

| Phase | Approximate dates (A.D.) |
|---|---|
| Initiation | 200/500–750/800 |
| Expansion | 770/800–1000/1050 |
| Differentiation | 1000/1050–1130/1150 |
| Reorganization | 1130/1150–1275/1300 |
| Aggregation | 1275/1300–1540 |

**Late Archaic (~1500 B.C.–A.D. 200):** introduction of corn, beans, and squash throughout most of the Southwest; ends with painted ceramics. Horticulture appears at roughly the same time in low Sonoran basins, mountains, and plateaus. Early house sites sit near floodwater-watered drainages (low desert) or benches overlooking fields (mountains/plateaus); multi-house sites are hamlets/household compounds with reoccupation, not simultaneous villages. Wills: maize implies seasonally repetitive local mobility, not full sedentism.

Maize-introduction dating remains debated: ambiguous Bat Cave radiocarbon once suggested ~3500 B.C.; Cordell & Smith’s Late Archaic frame and Snow (ch. 3) favor a more conservative **~1500 B.C.** consistent with other early domesticates (esp. beans); some archaeologists prefer only ~750 B.C.

**Pottery:** polished red/brown ware in southern SW by about the first century A.D.; brown and gray wares in northern mountains/plateaus by ~A.D. 300; pottery ubiquitous across the Southwest by ~A.D. 300, favoring storage and cooking among more sedentary groups.

**A.D. 600/700–900:** agriculture, small permanent/semipermanent hamlets, and pottery spread throughout the Southwest; by mid/end of period, local adaptation supports talking of separate cultural traditions. Three major traditions emerge by 700–900: [[hohokam]], [[mogollon]], and [[ancestral-pueblo]] (plus peripheral Patayan, Sinagua, Fremont, Salado). Snow places horticultural Hohokam as early as 300 B.C. or as late as A.D. 500; Mogollon by A.D. 700 (some put four centuries earlier); Ancestral Pueblo pit-house villages by 185 B.C., functioning by A.D. 500; Yuman Colorado Valley Patayan farming by A.D. 500.

**By 900:** horticulture and small-village settlement had spread throughout the Southwest; most settlements small and short-lived (perhaps ≤7–10 years); mountains/plateaus relied on storage and exchange; lower deserts used irrigation canals plus ball courts/platform mounds as ritual/community foci.

**900–1500:** in each southwestern region, systems of regionally organized communities developed, flourished for a time, and then collapsed — Hohokam canal hierarchies and ball-court networks, the [[chaco-canyon]] town–outlier system, late Mogollon/Mimbres and [[casas-grandes-paquime]], post-1300 Pueblo aggregation and alliance networks.

**After ~A.D. 1200:** mechanisms integrating Hohokam ball-court and Chaco outlier communities ceased operating without immediate population decline — distribution shifts and aggregation into larger settlements; regional abandonments by A.D. 1300 correlated with formation of very large aggregated settlements nearby. After 1425 the cultural map more closely resembles first European observations.

Southern Athapaskan speakers (Navajo and Apache) entered much later, probably late fifteenth or sixteenth century; they practiced some cultivation learned from neighbors, but their most distinctive post-contact economy was livestock herding adopted from Europeans.

## Geographic Spread

Arizona, New Mexico, southern Utah/Colorado; interaction south into northern Sonora/Chihuahua ([[casas-grandes-paquime]]). Water-control technologies (canals, diversion ditches, mulched/terraced gardens, check dams, reservoirs) constructed as landscape required, not restricted to one subtradition. Hohokam irrigation canals were certainly in place by A.D. 200–300; irrigation may have been necessary and present in the lower deserts by the start of the Christian era.

Comparative nutrition (Cordell & Smith): the East derived good calories/nutrients from domestic plants; SW most nutritious foods were often wild plants encouraged around fields/settlements — arid SW required technological innovations for reliable agriculture.

## Interaction

*part_of:* [[north-american-agricultural-transition]]; *contrasts_with:* [[eastern-seed-crop-complex]] (independent Eastern domestication vs Mexican-origin SW package). East and Southwest independently developed farming supporting complex varied social systems with different timing and forms; no impenetrable barriers — bow and arrow and perhaps maize exchanged — but knowledge of other regions was insufficient to cause increased interaction or parallel development. Initial crop experimentation ~1500 B.C. in both regions; farming way of life had primacy in the East; SW commitment to farming comparable to East somewhat later, ~A.D. 500–700.

On complexity: Cordell & Smith state **no one maintains state-level organizations occurred in the Southwest**, nor that hierarchical regional systems were stable; options included mix of hunting/gathering vs horticulture, formalizing/disrupting trade, occasional redistribution, and population movement — until Europeans and livestock closed many responses. See [[north-american-states-debate]] and [[chiefdom]].

## End Conditions

Reorganization after 1130–1300; aggregation 1275–1540; continuity into historic Pueblo, O'odham (Pima/Papago), and related peoples, with scholarly debate on degree of Hohokam–O'odham continuity (most investigators see some continuities, but not marked). Contact-period disruption of katcina-trade alliances means archaeology is vital to recovering precontact systems.
