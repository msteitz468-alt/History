# Biological Expansion of Europe, 900–1900

The **biological expansion of Europe** (Crosby’s framing) was the long-term process by which European humans and their associated Old World organisms (the portmanteau biota) spread beyond the continent’s historic range and achieved dominance in selected temperate zones worldwide. It was not merely human migration or political empire but an ecological and demographic transformation in which plants, animals, and microbes played leading roles.

## Definition and Scope

The process encompasses roughly a millennium: early medieval Norse experiments in the North Atlantic, medieval outreach via the "Fortunate Isles," the post-1492 surge across the Atlantic and into the Pacific, and the culminating 19th-century wave of mass migration that filled the newly prepared "Neo-Europes."

It is distinct from (but causally linked to) political imperialism, the Columbian Exchange as exchange per se, and purely economic or technological accounts of European hegemony. Its signature outcome is the creation of Neo-Europes whose populations, agriculture, and ecologies became extensions of Europe’s.

## Causal Drivers

- **Biogeographic preparation**: The breakup of Pangaea and subsequent evolution produced temperate zones in the Americas and Australasia with climates broadly similar to Europe’s but with biotas that proved less competitive against the introduced European suite under conditions of heavy grazing, clearance, and disturbance.
- **Maritime and navigational advances**: Development of ships capable of long ocean crossings and mastery of wind systems (trade winds, westerlies, volta do mar) that turned the Atlantic and later the Pacific into highways.
- **Stepping-stone colonization**: Norse Greenland/Vinland; Iberian and other European occupation of the Azores, Madeira, Canaries, and Cape Verdes as laboratories for the full Neo-European pattern.
- **The portmanteau biota itself**: Weeds that pioneered and stabilized disturbed ground; livestock that multiplied explosively; crops suited to the new soils; diseases that reduced competition.
- **European demographic and economic push**: Population growth, land hunger, and (later) industrial-era surplus labor seeking better conditions.
- **Pull of prepared lands**: Once the biota had begun transforming landscapes, the prospect of abundant food, land, and familiar (or improved) living standards drew further migrants.

## Major Phases

1. **Medieval outreach (c. 900–1500)**: Norse ventures; Crusader-era contacts; settlement of Atlantic islands; gradual accumulation of knowledge and adapted biota.
2. **Early modern surge (1492–c. 1800)**: Post-Columbian biological invasion of the Americas; rapid spread of weeds and livestock; disease-assisted weakening of indigenous societies; establishment of beachheads and ranching/planting economies in temperate zones.
3. **19th-century filling (c. 1820–1930)**: Steam-powered mass migration of >50 million Europeans; conversion of prepared lands into high-output agricultural exporters; peak rates of natural increase in settler populations.

## Geographic Spread

Success was strongly concentrated in temperate latitudes with climates analogous to Europe’s and with indigenous human populations that had not co-evolved with the full suite of Old World crowd diseases and domesticates. 

- Strongest outcomes: eastern North America, pampa (Argentina/Uruguay), southeastern Australia + Tasmania, New Zealand.
- More limited or different: tropical zones, highland or arid interiors, regions with dense, immunologically experienced populations (much of Asia, parts of Africa).
- Reverse flow was far weaker: few New World or Australian organisms naturalized successfully in Europe without human assistance.

## Interaction with Other Processes

- Closely intertwined with the **Columbian Exchange** (the initial reconnection and asymmetric exchange) and **virgin-soil epidemics** (a key early mechanism).
- Enabled and was enabled by **imperialism** and **colonial economy** (political structures and markets followed and reinforced the biological foundation).
- Contributed to the **Great Divergence** and industrialization by supplying food, raw materials, markets, and population outlets that allowed Europe (and later the Neo-Europes) to escape Malthusian constraints.
- Produced long-term **environmental change** (erosion, species loss, homogenization) alongside caloric gains.

## End Conditions / Legacy

By the early 20th century the major temperate Neo-Europes were demographically and agriculturally "Europeanized." Rates of natural increase later declined as populations urbanized and aged. The lands became central to global food security, creating new dependencies and vulnerabilities (weather, pests, politics, war) for importing regions. Ecological costs (erosion, biodiversity loss, invasive species) accumulated and continue.

The process did not end abruptly in 1900; ongoing exchange, globalization of agriculture, and continued human movement have extended aspects of it, but the distinctive phase of rapid, large-scale creation of Neo-Europes through Old World biological expansion was largely complete by the early 20th century.
