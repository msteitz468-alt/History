# Barbarization of the Roman Army

**The progressive incorporation of barbarians — individuals, units, and finally whole
peoples — into the Roman military between the second and fifth centuries**, and the parallel
militarization of Roman society at large. In Patrick Geary's argument it is the master process
by which Rome itself "created" the barbarian world: the army was the great machine of
assimilation that turned outsiders into Romans and Romans into a soldiery increasingly
indistinguishable from the enemies it was raised to fight.

## Definition and Scope

By the early fourth century "military organization and structure, along with the soldier's
cultural and political values, had become the primary model along which Roman society was
ordered. But these soldiers were no longer the Italian peasants of an earlier age — increasingly
they were the very barbarians they were enlisted to oppose." The process is therefore two things
at once: the entry of barbarians into Roman service, and the reshaping of Roman society on a
military template.

## Major Phases

**1. Individual recruitment (from Marcus Aurelius).** Faced with plague, falling birthrates, and
frontier pressure, Marcus Aurelius incorporated Germanic warrior groups along the *limes*. Only
citizens could serve in the legions and praetorian cohorts; foreigners served in *auxilia* — but
by the third and fourth centuries the ranks of all units filled with barbarians. Roman policy
bribed frontier leaders with citizenship, gifts, gold, and grain in return for troops, who served
a term and went home: "the migrant labor force of the ancient world."

**2. The laeti and the comitatenses.** The first barbarians *settled* inside the empire — the
**laeti** — were refugees or prisoners of war assigned to prefects or landowners in depopulated
regions of Gaul and Italy, their communities obligated to supply recruits. Constantine (c. 300)
created mobile elite units, the **comitatenses**, stationed within or near major provincial cities;
their proximity to civilian communities accelerated assimilation. Since Hadrian, recruits served in
their native provinces, and by the fourth century military service was a hereditary obligation,
making legions locally rooted and self-perpetuating.

**3. The foederati and "imperial Germans."** Unlike laeti, **foederati** kept their own chiefs and
stayed in contact both with the Roman population they dominated and with tribes across the Rhine and
Danube. Germanic commanders rose to the top: the Franks **Arbogast, Bauto, and Richomer** served as
*magistri militum* and consuls under Gratian and Valentinian II — "virtual rulers who could make and
break emperors." These men were culturally Roman: Bauto's consular panegyric (385) was written by the
future St Augustine; Richomer was an acquaintance of Symmachus. See [[frankish-ethnogenesis]].

**4. Settlement of whole peoples (gentes).** The decisive, most misunderstood phase was the entry of
entire peoples. Precipitated by the Huns' arrival in 376 and the destruction of the Gothic
confederation, it ran through the catastrophe of **Adrianople** (378), where the Goths killed the
emperor Valens, to **Theodosius's treaty of 382**, which settled the [[visigoths|Visigoths]] in Thrace
under their own chiefs as foederati — the template for all subsequent incorporations.

## The mechanism of settlement (debated)

How barbarian armies were quartered on Roman soil is contested: the traditional view (*hospitalitas*
assigning one-third of estates) versus a revisionist view (assigning shares of tax income, leaving
proprietors undisturbed). The reality varied by region, but warriors in Gaul and Italy "did not
usually physically occupy the lands to which they were assigned. They tended to remain in cities or at
strategic points," collecting the rents or taxes of allotted properties.

## Interaction

The process is inseparable from the [[late-antiquity|late-Roman transformation]] of [[roman-gaul|Gaul]]
(militarization, taxation, the rise of the landowning aristocracy), from [[ethnogenesis]] (army service
became a primary marker of tribal membership), and from the [[comitatus]] tradition the recruits carried.
It produced the [[merovingian-franks|Frankish]], [[visigoths|Visigothic]], and [[ostrogoths|Ostrogothic]]
kingdoms — barbarian armies that had become Roman armies, ruling provincials.

## Did barbarization degrade the army? (Goldsworthy's bounded thesis)

[[sources/goldsworthy-roman-warfare-2000|Adrian Goldsworthy, *Roman Warfare* (2000)]] cautions against
reading "barbarization" as a straightforward story of decline. Through most of the period, he argues,
there is **little evidence that recruiting barbarians reduced the army's fighting ability**: individual
recruits and even Germanic officers (the Alamannic king Vadomarius became a Roman *dux*) were absorbed
into standard Roman service patterns and fought reliably, "even against their own people" — continuous
with the army's long history of foreign recruitment rather than a new degrading trend. He locates the
**turning point** precisely where the page's Phase 4 does: when barbarians began to serve in **distinct
units under their own leaders**, the arrangement increasingly denoted by *foederati* after the treaty of
**418** that settled the Visigoths in Aquitaine — the first time a western emperor acknowledged an
autonomous king inside the provinces. This later stage, for Goldsworthy, "promoted the final collapse of
central authority": such groups bolstered individual emperors in the short term but the infrastructure to
raise, pay, supply, and control a large trained standing army no longer existed in the West. (He notes,
without resolving, the "fierce debate" over whether the Danube crossing *created* or merely *accelerated*
the Visigoths' unusual tribal unity.) The distinction thus turns less on ethnicity than on **command
structure and the state's fiscal capacity** — dovetailing with his reading of the [[late-roman-army]].

## End Conditions

The process did not "end" so much as **complete itself**: when whole barbarian armies became the effective
government of the western provinces, the distinction between a barbarized empire and a Romanized barbarian
kingdom dissolved. [[clovis|Clovis]]'s kingdom is its endpoint — an imperial-German military commander who
absorbed Roman provincial administration to make himself a legitimate king.

## OHCW (Chadwick Envoi)

**Chadwick**: barbarians had long supplied the army before 476; Odovacer’s assumption of regal insignia was real power taking ceremonial power, not a sudden collapse of Roman resistance. Even before Constantine, Germanic tribes provided some of the best legionary soldiers; when the Goths shattered Valens’ army at Adrianople (378), the military incorporation of outsiders was already structural. In 476 “no one at the time much noticed” — the process of [[barbarization-of-the-roman-army|barbarization]] had already made the commander of a barbarian army the natural holder of western power. Source: [[boardman-oxford-history-classical-world-1986]].

## Harris, *Roman Power* (2016)

Harris places “barbarian” recruitment inside the broader problem of **military loyalty and identity**, not as an isolated ethnic pathology. A complex of factors (money above all, but not only money) kept soldiers loyal or failed: prompt pay, donatives, accession-anniversary oaths before the emperor’s portrait, victory leadership, dynastic play. Constantine created a substantially new problem by recruiting much of the army with which he warred against Maxentius from among “barbarians.” Tetrarchic–Constantinian army seeds of future trouble: huge expense plus imperfect identification of “barbarian” troops with Rome. Second/third-century soldiers mostly provincials with no reason to doubt Roman identity—or proof unconditional; they seldom supported anti-Roman causes. Late fourth century: boundary between legitimate forces and marauders thinner (Tribigild, Gainas); Synesius cast Gothic troops as wolves. At Yarmuk high commands were often non-Roman (Armenian Vahan, Persian Nicetas, Ghassanid Jabalah)—desertions made recovery significantly harder; commanders carried soldiers with them. Western fall synthesis: forces too much in conflict among themselves **ethnically and religiously**—the latter conflict mattered. Source: [[harris-roman-power-2016]].

## From Gibbon, Decline and Fall (1776–1788)

[[actors/gibbon-edward|Gibbon]] makes barbarization of arms a master chain in the [[concepts/gibbonian-decline-thesis|decline thesis]] — “more universal, more necessary, and more fatal.” **Gibbon’s views.**

- **Settlements after Claudius/Aurelian/Diocletian:** Aurelian’s ~2,000 Gothic cavalry auxiliaries; hostages educated/married to officers; Diocletian distributed captive barbarians as shepherds in depopulated Gaul and granted Carpi/Bastarnae/Sarmatians lands with dangerous retention of national manners — “multitudes of secret enemies, insolent from favor, or desperate from oppression, were introduced into the heart of the empire.”
- **Carausius and Frankish naval arts:** Menapian admiral’s British usurpation “communicated to the barbarians the dangerous knowledge of military and naval arts.”
- **Core thesis:** “The introduction of Barbarians into the Roman armies became every day more universal, more necessary, and more fatal”; they learned to despise Roman manners while mastering Roman arts; commands filled with foreign names; Constantine set example of Barbarian consulships.
- **Leakage of secrets:** Lentiensian guardsman of Gratian revealed western march plans, triggering Alemannic invasion that delayed aid to Valens — “One of the most dangerous inconveniences of the introduction of the Barbarians into the army and the palace.”
- **Gothic admission as whole nation:** Valens’s ministers, flattering pride/indolence/avarice, saw free invincible army and saved recruiting gold — an unprecedented statecraft problem of admitting “an innumerable multitude.”
- **Theodosian civil wars / Frigidus:** Goths and Alani in imperial armies; pagan standards vs cross; Honorius’s Catholic-only office purge disqualified brave pagan/Arian officers.

## CHGRW: Elton, Rance, Lee, Fear on “barbarization”

[[sabin-van-wees-whitby-cambridge-history-greek-roman-warfare-2007|CHGRW]] Vol. II (Elton Ch. 8; Rance Ch. 10; Lee Ch. 11; Fear Ch. 12; Humphries Ch. 7) supplies the strongest multi-author pushback against treating “barbarization” as a simple degradation thesis — while still registering real structural change in the fifth-century West.

### Scale and recruiting (Elton)

**Elton** rejects the severe manpower-shortage thesis (Boak) as over-read from legal evidence also found in earlier periods. Conscripts came from soldiers’ sons, *laeti/gentiles* settlers, defeated enemies, and volunteers from in and beyond the empire; regional recruitment reputations shifted (Danubians, Gauls, Isaurians, Goths, Armenians). Non-Roman recruiting was substantial but **exaggerated as “barbarization”**: names are unreliable for ethnicity; a crude estimate based solely on names suggests **~1 in 4 field-army soldiers of non-imperial origin**, a proportion that seems **stable across late antiquity**; among border troops almost all recruits had Roman names. Ethnic character of new *foederati/auxilia palatina* diluted over time; majority of regular regiments remained mostly non-barbarian. Large prisoner drafts: Stilicho after Radagaisus 406 took **12,000** barbarians into service; Justinian formed units from defeated Vandals (*Justiniani Vandali*); individual captive elites could be integrated (Vadomarius Alamannic king → *dux Phoenices*). Cultural prejudice could be overridden for good relations and manpower: Julian objected to transferring Rhine German recruits to Persia because enlistment had promised they would not fight far from home.

### Combat performance (Rance; Lee)

**Rance** rejects the traditional model that ethnically heterogeneous “barbarian” troops corrupted discipline and morale: rural recruiting had long mixed ethnicities; allies often increased operational options (e.g. Hunnic horse-archers); **unit cohesion** (*contubernium*, regimental colours) mattered more than army-wide ethnic homogeneity; Aëtius at Châlons 451 fought similarly to Julian at Strasbourg 357. The “swine’s head”/*caput porcinum*/*cuneus* is often assumed Germanic (cf. later *svínfylking*), but may be a barbarian popular name for an **existing Roman formation** — not proof of tactical Germanization. (On the *fulcum* shield-wall and related “barbarization” claims about infantry tactics, see [[late-roman-army]].)

**Lee**: “barbarization” via non-Roman recruits/commanders did **not seriously compromise fourth-century effectiveness**; tribal fragmentation and professional socialization undercut dual-loyalty fears; Ammianus shows no inferior performance by non-Roman soldiers. He separates a more serious issue — **fifth-century western reliance on federate blocs under non-Roman commanders** — as a structural problem of state capacity and politics rather than ethnic unreliability as such (see [[causes-of-the-fall-of-the-western-roman-empire]]).

### Settlement, ideology, and reverse cultural flow (Fear; Humphries)

**Fear**: barbarian recruits sometimes served under co-ethnics retaining customs (e.g. Hnaufridus unit’s Germanic deities on Hadrian’s Wall); the army as vector of reverse “barbarization” of civilian fashion (trousers ban in Rome); *laeti* settlements (Gaul/N. Italy) and other controlled barbarian military colonies on vacant land. The 376 Gothic admission was framed as a recruit source allowing cash taxes rather than men from the provinces (Ammianus), ending in [[battle-of-adrianople-378|Adrianople]] then the 382 autonomous federate status under Theodosius. **Humphries** notes Pacatus already celebrated Theodosius’s barbarian recruits who “followed standards which they had once opposed” and filled Pannonian cities they had emptied — propaganda of successful integration that Humphries labels hollow given subsequent conflicts those troops joined.

These CHGRW positions largely **converge with Goldsworthy’s bounded thesis** above (individual/non-Roman recruitment ≠ decline; autonomous federate blocs + fiscal collapse = the dangerous phase), while adding quantitative discipline (Elton’s ~1/4 estimate; name-unreliability caveat) and tactical scepticism (Rance on *cuneus*/Germanization). They diverge from Gibbon’s “more universal, more necessary, and more fatal” chain by refusing ethnic composition as the master causal variable.

Source: [[sabin-van-wees-whitby-cambridge-history-greek-roman-warfare-2007]] (Elton II.8; Rance II.10; Lee II.11; Fear II.12; Humphries II.7).

## From Mitchell, Later Roman Empire (2015)

### Why accept the 382 Gothic settlement?

Mitchell lists four reasons Theodosius and Gratian accepted Gothic settlement inside the empire after [[battle-of-adrianople-378|Adrianople]]: (1) major defeat at Adrianople; (2) inability to regain the upper hand decisively 379–81; (3) thin Danube defense vs Huns, Alans, Sarmatians, Lentienses; (4) critically, **manpower** — every soldier was one less taxpayer and *colonus* — so better to enlist Goths to defend the frontier than fight them as internal enemies.

### Pre-375 officers and terms of enlistment

German recruits longstanding; third–fourth centuries drastically raised barbarian proportion and officers of Frankish/Alemannic/Vandal origin (Bauto, Arbogast, Stilicho at apex; Sulpicius Severus on 394 Frankish control of western military/civil affairs). They took Roman titles, used Latin, and show little evidence of fifth-column tribal loyalty; imperial family intermarriage was interest-based. “These barbarians, however, had been enlisted into the Roman Empire on Roman terms.”

### Laeti, foederati, and western warlords

Romans used barbarians as *laeti* (resettled captives), *foederati* (esp. Goths 382 retaining tribal leadership), and large personal followings of western warlords (Stilicho’s **35,000** federates at death 408; Constantius’s barbarian retinue; Bonifatius; Aetius’s Hunnic mercenaries; Marcellinus; Ricimer). Such warlords shaped western collapse; **eastern** generals with private armies never seized the civil hierarchy even under Leo/Zeno; Belisarius’s 7,000 horsemen remained subject to Justinian. `contributed_to:` [[causes-of-the-fall-of-the-western-roman-empire]] / [[fall-of-the-western-roman-empire-476]].

### Sixth-century label shift

By the sixth century Procopius reports *foederati* no longer only free barbarians under treaty equality but an open category anyone might claim — illustrating fluid recruitment labels (*Bell. Vand.* 3.11.3–4).
