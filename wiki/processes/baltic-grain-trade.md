## Definition and Scope

The Baltic grain trade — and the broader process of Baltic market internationalisation — describes the progressive integration of the Baltic Sea region into wider European and eventually global commercial networks, from Hanseatic dominance through Dutch hegemony to English ascendancy. The process was centred on bulk commodities (grain, timber, naval stores, copper) flowing westward and salt, wine, cloth, and manufactured goods flowing eastward, with the Danish Sound as the compulsory bottleneck. The timeframe covered here is c. 1350–c. 1800, with the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries the decisive period. Sources: [[kouri-olesen-cambridge-history-scandinavia-v2-2016]], ch. 10 (Åke Sandström) and ch. 11 (Ole Peter Grell).

## Causal Drivers

**Structural Dutch demand**: "In the century between 1350 and 1450 a fundamental change took place in the economic structure of the Netherlands. During this period the Dutch gradually ceased to be self-sufficient in grain and grew more and more dependent on import" (L9851–9853). From the 1470s Baltic grain importance rose; "from the 1520s the increase was very sharp" (L9855–9856).

**The price revolution**: "The 'price revolution' of the sixteenth century created a considerable gap in grain prices in particular between the Baltic and Western Europe, making it worthwhile for merchants to sustain all the expense involved in bulk trade over long distances" (L10354–10357). This price differential underwrote the entire logistics investment.

**Technological advantage — the fluyt**: Dutch shipbuilders "focused on low-cost vessels with large hulls for maximum cargos while simultaneously dropping armaments and simplifying rigging." The fluyt, emerging from the 1590s, "reduced the size of the crew needed by around 30 per cent" reducing freight rates dramatically (L10432–10438). Between 1562 and 1657 over 60% of ships passing through the Sound sailed under the Dutch flag (L10439–10440).

**Geographic advantage**: The Dutch position "halfway between most markets" allowed integration of Iberian salt (outbound to Baltic) with Baltic grain (inbound to Western Europe), and their ports became ice-free earlier than Hanseatic rivals, giving Dutch ships a seasonal head start (L10380–10401).

**Sound Toll leverage**: Denmark's control of the only viable water route in and out of the Baltic — "a narrow passage ... controlled on both sides by Danish fortifications" — gave the Danish crown both revenue and geopolitical leverage (L10636–10641). Duties were levied on virtually all commodities from 1561 (L10641).

## Major Phases

### Phase 1: Hanseatic Dominance (to c. 1520)
"Up to the sixteenth century the Baltic market was mainly controlled by German merchants ... The formation of the Hanseatic League from the thirteenth century confirmed and consolidated the domination of German-speaking tradesmen" (L9725–9733). The Peace of Stralsund (1370) "confirmed and strengthened the position of the Hansa as the major economic power within the Nordic realms" (L9739–9741). Domestic Nordic merchants lacked capital and business networks to displace them. In Stockholm's port in the first decade of the 1600s, Lübeck and Danzig still supplied "more than 50 per cent of the value of imported goods" (L9989–9990).

### Phase 2: Dutch Entry and Expansion (c. 1520–c. 1645)
Dutch merchants did not displace the Hansa abruptly: "It is more correct to view the Dutch trade in the early modern period as a new layer of networks over a still-existing older pattern of trade" (L9870–9872). High-volume Stockholm merchants in the 1640s sourced 80% of imports by value from the North Sea and Atlantic; low- and medium-volume merchants still sourced 96% from within the Baltic (L9911–9925). The Dutch brought new commodity diversity: by the mid-seventeenth century a Dutch fluyt cargo included "lemons and oranges, cheap jewellery, mirrors, hats and dresses for all levels of society, wallpaper, children's toys, different sorts of candies, oysters and pastries" (L9999–10001) — a transformation from the medieval cargo of salt, wine, beer, and cloth.

The Twelve Years Truce (1609–21) between Spain and the Dutch Republic restored Iberian access for Dutch ships, "reducing freight and insurance costs considerably," boosting the Dutch share of Baltic trade from 60% to 70% while the Hanseatic, English, and Danish shares contracted (L10630–10634).

Dutch dominance peaked with the Treaty of Christianopel (1645), forced on Denmark-Norway after Dutch Admiral Witte de With passed through the Sound with 300 merchant vessels without paying any toll (L10792–10804). The treaty gave the Dutch preferential treatment, exemption from boarding by Danish customs, and cancellation of the Elbe toll. "The subsequent treaty ... offered the Dutch a number of concessions which came to form the basis for their maritime dominance of the Baltic for the rest of the century" (L10798–10801).

**The herring trade**: The Dutch North Sea herring fishery — known as the 'great fishery' — emerged when the Skåne herring stocks collapsed in the late fifteenth century and large schools moved to the North Sea Dogger Bank area. Dutch herring busses, large factory ships, could stay at sea five to eight weeks curing fish on board. By 1560 the northern Dutch provinces mustered "more than 500 herring busses, manned by 7,000 men" (L10502–10503). From 1600–1650 the Dutch supplied 80–90% of herring passing through the Sound; this share fell to 30% in 1710–40 and c. 10–15% a generation later (L10510–10513).

### Phase 3: Dutch Decline and English Ascendancy (c. 1645–c. 1800)
Dutch Baltic trade contracted by 20–30% after 1660, declining faster after 1672 when France's war repeatedly tied Dutch naval forces to the Western Front and French privateers from Dunkirk struck Dutch merchant vessels. Swedish mercantilist anti-Dutch tolls (1667) and the Skåne War (1675–9) further damaged Dutch commerce. A Dutch-Swedish treaty in 1679 cancelled those anti-Dutch regulations, producing a brief Baltic recovery in the 1680s, but "the Dutch share of the Baltic trade contracted from 50 per cent of the total in 1720 to less than 30 per cent by 1770" (L10981–10982).

English trade grew through the Navigation Acts era, the Anglo-Dutch Wars, and technological catch-up in ship design. "Around 1720 England's shipping tonnage probably exceeded that of the United Provinces. Twenty years later English merchants had become the dominant force in the Baltic even circumventing the Dutch Republic when dealing with colonial goods, delivering them directly to Hamburg and the Baltic" (L11004–11008). The centre of gravity shifted from Amsterdam to London.

**St Petersburg (1703)**: Russia had long sought direct Baltic access to avoid Western middlemen. Sweden controlled both shores of the Gulf of Finland from 1617 to the early 1700s (L10202–10206). Peter the Great founded St Petersburg in the Neva estuary — while the Great Northern War was still in progress — as Russia's definitive breakthrough to direct Baltic commerce (L10213–10216). By 1802 about 70% of Russian foreign trade still passed through Baltic ports; by 1890 this had fallen to 35% as Ukrainian grain exports shifted to Black Sea ports (L10319–10320).

### Phase 4: Baltic Peripheralisation (nineteenth century)
"In 1802 about 70 per cent of Russian foreign trade still passed through the ports of the Baltic. In 1890 this share was reduced to 35 per cent" (L10319–10320). The shift of Russian export gravity to Ukraine and the Black Sea, combined with North American competition in timber and grain, gradually marginalised the Baltic. "The Baltic region without doubt lost much of its former international position during the nineteenth century" (L10329–10331).

## Geographic Spread

The trade centred on a triangle linking: (1) Baltic grain ports — Danzig (Gdańsk), Riga, Königsberg — exporting Polish, Prussian, and Russian grain; (2) Swedish and Finnish ports exporting copper, iron, tar, and timber; (3) Western entrepôts — first Amsterdam, later London and Hamburg — redistributing goods to Mediterranean, Iberian, and Atlantic markets. The Sound at Helsingør/Helsingborg was the compulsory transit point for all trade between the Baltic and the North Sea. The Arctic dimension (White Sea/Archangel route) served as an alternative when the Sound was blocked or when Baltic grain prices rose sharply; Dutch Arctic trade peaked c. 1630 at around one hundred ships sailing to Archangel (L10725–10726).

Norwegian and Swedish timber exports fed English and Dutch shipbuilding. Swedish copper flowed through Amsterdam houses and was often "used as security for loans taken by the cash-starved Swedish government" (L9862–9864). "The lion's share of [Sweden's] dominant production of copper was sold through business houses in Amsterdam" (L9862–9863).

## Interaction

**Sound Toll diplomacy**: Denmark's toll was a constant flashpoint. Christian IV raised it in 1611 during the Kalmar War, causing a severe shipping slump in Holland and Friesland (L10684–10686), which forced Dutch diplomatic intervention (treaties with Lübeck and Sweden) that eventually compelled Denmark to cancel the additional toll (L10688–10695). In 1638 Christian IV again raised the toll and added a new Elbe toll at Glückstadt; repeated Dutch diplomatic pressure, then the dispatch of a fleet in 1645, forced a definitive reduction (L10757–10804).

**Swedish Great Power expansion as commercial strategy**: "It is quite reasonable to look at Swedish military expansion around the Baltic not merely from the viewpoint of political security, but also as an appropriate way of breaking the country's economic isolation by pushing its borders nearer to the main European markets" (L8754–8758). Sweden's control of Baltic coastal territories gave it leverage over both commodity flows and customs revenues.

**Scandinavian trading companies**: Both Denmark and Sweden launched East India-style companies to bypass Dutch intermediaries: the Danish *Ostindisk Kompagni* (1616), closely modelled on the VOC, and Sweden's *New Sweden Company* which established a colony in the Delaware River estuary (1638), fort named after Queen Kristina, hoping to link Swedish copper exports to a North American hub. "In 1655 New Sweden ceased to exist after an attack carried out by the Dutch in the Delaware River" (L10075–10077). Capital shortage doomed all these enterprises: "it was and is often necessary to have money to earn money" (L10079–10080).

**Dutch-Swedish commercial symbiosis and conflict**: Sweden largely escaped the seventeenth-century European economic crisis through its connection to Amsterdam. "It has been claimed that Sweden avoided most of the seventeenth-century economic crisis as a result of the close connection to the expansive market controlled by Amsterdam" (L9896–9898). But Sweden increasingly chafed: from the 1650s it adopted "a robust mercantilist stance ... which eventually broke Dutch control over the carrying trade to and from the country" (L10817–10819). The Skåne War (1675–9) — Denmark and the Dutch Republic versus Sweden — ended with a Dutch-Swedish reconciliation treaty in 1679 that cancelled anti-Dutch shipping tolls (L10975–10977).

**European great-power supervision**: "It was not in the interest of the European Great Powers to let any of the Scandinavian states grow too mighty ... The inlets to the Baltic in the sole hands of either Danes or Swedes was not acceptable" (L10268–10272). When Karl X attempted to eliminate Denmark as an independent state in 1658, a joint European intervention ended the attempt. The Scandinavian powers were in this sense operating within limits set by Amsterdam, London, and Versailles.

## End Conditions

The process of Dutch Baltic hegemony ended through a combination of war attrition, English commercial competition, and Russian geopolitical restructuring. The Baltic remained a significant European trade zone through the eighteenth century but lost its earlier position as "the mother of all trades." Industrialisation in Britain and the opening of Ukrainian grain lands via Black Sea ports shifted the centre of gravity definitively away from the Baltic in the nineteenth century.
