## Definition and Scope

The Australian pastoral expansion (c. 1820–1850) was the rapid, largely unsanctioned spread of sheep and cattle grazing across the eastern half of the continent, driven by the British wool market and enabled by cheap convict labour and weak frontier governance. The process transformed two small coastal settlements around Sydney and Hobart into a continental colonial enterprise: by the late 1840s, 'some 70 million acres (28 million hectares) covering more or less the entire eastern third of the continent had been commandeered by fewer than 2,000 squatters' (Ford and Roberts, ch. 5). This process is distinct from the colonisation of individual settlements (New South Wales, Van Diemen's Land, South Australia, Swan River Colony) but overlaps all of them, unifying them economically through wool exports to Britain.

The term "squatter" describes the key agent of this process: persons who occupied Crown land without legal title. Until the mid-1830s squatters were 'a disreputable class; those without the means or respectability to acquire sufficient property through legal means' — often emancipists and ticket-of-leave men who ran cattle to raise capital for sheep. As the wool boom intensified, moneyed free settlers joined them, and the squatting class became politically dominant.

This process is not simply agricultural settlement: it was the primary vehicle of [[settler-colonialism]] in this period, spreading dispossession of [[aboriginal-australians]] across the continent. See also [[australian-frontier-conflict]].

## Causal Drivers

**British wool market demand:** A reduction of wool tariffs in Britain in 1823 immediately caused Australian wool exports to surge from 175,400 to 400,000 pounds per year. British industrial production required longer, stronger wool than English growers could supply; after the Napoleonic Wars, stable colonial supply replaced German imports. By 1850, Australian colonies provided 47 per cent of Britain's wool imports, compared to Germany's 10.6 per cent (down from 75.8 per cent in 1830).

**The Bigge Report (1822–23):** Commissioner John Thomas Bigge, sent by the imperial government to make transportation 'once again an object of real terror', forged a nexus between 'punishment and profit' by endorsing large-scale pastoralism. He advocated generous land grants to free settlers with capital, redistribution of convict labour from towns to the countryside, and the political and legal subordination of emancipists. These recommendations reshaped colonial policy toward rural pastoral enterprise.

**Convict labour redistribution:** Under Governors Brisbane and Darling, convicts were redirected from urban work to rural assignment and road gangs — building the arteries connecting Sydney to the Hunter Valley, Goulburn and Bathurst. Road construction opened previously inaccessible grazing country. Van Diemen's Land under George Arthur (1824–36) built an elaborate surveillance and assignment system.

**Crown land licensing:** Successive governors issued Tickets of Occupation (Brisbane) and later formal licensing systems (Bourke 1836, Gipps 1839) that allowed pastoralists to graze Crown land for an annual fee. The 1836 Act let squatters 'run as many animals as they desired across an unlimited extent of terrain for an annual fee of £10'. Though insecure, these arrangements regularised the squatters' presence on the frontier.

**Wool technology:** New packing technologies in the 1840s Depression enabled wool exports to grow even when prices fell, underpinning eventual recovery.

## Major Phases

**Phase 1 — Restricted expansion (1820–28):** Governor Macquarie concentrated settlement within the Cumberland Plain; expansion beyond the Blue Mountains was controlled by temporary permits. The Bigge Report shifted policy toward pastoralism and large-scale rural grants, but the Hunter Valley became the primary zone of legal settlement.

**Phase 2 — First boom and squatting age (1828–36):** The 1828 parliamentary endorsement of Australian fleece precipitated the colonies' first export boom. By 1831 NSW and Van Diemen's Land were exporting 2.5 million pounds of wool; by 1834 all colonies together exported 13.5 million pounds. Squatting spread beyond the nineteen counties. Governor Darling drew the 'limits of location' in 1829, but these could not contain the pastoral frontier. The Crown Lands Encroachment Act 1833 affirmed Crown ownership but failed to prevent occupation. Port Phillip District was occupied by 1835.

**Phase 3 — Concession and crisis (1836–46):** As squatters became politically organised and economically essential, governments made incremental concessions. Bourke's 1836 licensing scheme, Gipps's 1839 Act (establishing squatting districts, Border Police, and commissioner jurisdiction), and squatter dominance in the partly elected 1843 legislature marked their political rise. The global economic crisis of 1841–44 crashed colonial economies but pastoralism led recovery. Squatters lobbied London through Pastoral Association networks.

**Phase 4 — Legal consolidation (1846–50):** The Waste Lands Occupation Act 1846 (UK) and the Order in Council of March 1847 allowed leases of 8 years (intermediate districts) or 14 years (unsettled districts) at £10 per 4,000 sheep, converting squatters into leaseholders. This 'brought squatters decisively back into the fold of the law' while consolidating the pastoral monopoly. By the end of the 1840s, 'five million sheep and nearly four hundred thousand head of cattle' grazed Port Phillip alone.

## Geographic Spread

Expansion proceeded in multiple directions from the Sydney and Hobart cores:

- **West/north-west (NSW):** Across the Blue Mountains to Bathurst, then along Cudgegong and Talbragar rivers to the Warrumbungle Ranges; north to Liverpool Plains and Darling Downs via Pandora's Pass and Cunningham's Pass
- **South (NSW):** To Breadalbane, Goulburn Plains, Lakes Bathurst and George, the 'Limestone Plains' (ACT), and on to the Murrumbidgee
- **Port Phillip District (Victoria):** Settled initially by Van Diemen's Land pastoralists from 1835; the Port Phillip Association signed its abortive Batman's Treaty with Kulin people; overlanders arrived from Sydney from 1837; by 1848 five million sheep grazed the district
- **Van Diemen's Land:** 1.5 million acres granted and sold 1823–31; pastoralists crossed to Port Phillip by 1835
- **Swan River Colony:** Modest pastoral expansion, constrained by aridity and labour shortage; only 40,000 sheep by 1841
- **South Australia:** Agricultural mixed with pastoral; sheep numbers exceeded one million by 1850; copper mining added a mineral dimension
- **New Zealand:** Described as 'an Australian frontier'; served as an extension of Australian pastoral and maritime enterprise

## Interaction

The pastoral expansion was inseparable from:
- **[[australian-frontier-conflict]]**: Squatting onto Aboriginal land required and produced violence; the Aboriginal Protectorate was established in Port Phillip specifically to police frontier violence
- **[[terra-nullius]] doctrine**: Legal opinions from the 1820s onward denied Aboriginal proprietary and political rights; the 1836 *Murrell* case formalised British sovereignty over all Aboriginal persons, facilitating dispossession without treaty
- **[[australian-self-government]]**: Squatters' political organisation — their role in the 1843 legislature, their London lobbying networks — made them a key constituency in the movement toward responsible government, and their security of tenure demands shaped the constitutional debates
- **[[australian-gold-rushes-1851]]**: The pastoral economy temporarily disrupted by gold, which drew labour away; in the longer term the population increase gold brought enriched pastoralists through higher land and livestock values

## End Conditions

The Waste Lands Occupation Act 1846 marked the effective end of the "squatting age" as a legal grey zone, converting squatters to leaseholders. However, the struggle between pastoral monopoly and smallholder claims for land (the "unlocking the land" movement) continued through the Robertson Land Acts 1861 (NSW) and Nicholson Land Act 1860 (Victoria), which opened former pastoral runs to small farming selection. The gold rushes of the 1850s partially disrupted pastoral dominance by depopulating some stations and shifting the colony's economic centre of gravity, but pastoralism recovered and remained central to Australian exports until the twentieth century.

## Historiography

The key historiographical framework is provided by Stephen H. Roberts's *The Squatting Age in Australia, 1835–1847* (1935), which remains foundational but is now read alongside social and legal histories attentive to the colonial violence the expansion entailed. Lisa Ford's *Settler Sovereignty* (2010) traces how legal doctrines — including the emerging concept of terra nullius — were articulated in tandem with geographical expansion, not as a prior ideological program but as a series of ad hoc legal opinions responding to frontier contingencies. Philip McMichael's *Settlers and the Agrarian Question* (1984) situates Australian pastoral capitalism within a global settler-colonial framework. James Belich's *Replenishing the Earth* (2009) situates the pastoral boom within the wider Anglo-settler revolution.

The chapter's argument (Ford and Roberts) is that expansion was 'as multifaceted as the urban, rural and pastoral expansions that occasioned it' and rested on both overt violence and 'the quiet encroachment of legal institutions and legal ideologies'. This historiographical position — treating law and violence as co-constitutive tools of dispossession rather than alternatives — distinguishes recent from earlier scholarship that emphasised economic development narratives.
