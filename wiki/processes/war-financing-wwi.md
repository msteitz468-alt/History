# War Financing in the First World War

The **war financing** of 1914–1918 was the process by which the belligerent states raised, allocated, and managed the enormous sums required for industrialized warfare once the pre-war assumptions of short conflicts funded from current revenue or victory-secured credit collapsed. It encompassed the management of the gold standard, emergency credit creation, taxation versus borrowing choices, domestic and foreign loans, and the subordination of peacetime fiscal norms to military necessity.

## Definition and Scope

War financing differed fundamentally from peacetime debt (which funded capital investment) and from pre-war expectations. "The contrast in financial management between the United States and, say, Austria-Hungary was therefore much less striking than the difference in their political structures might have suggested." All major powers ended up borrowing heavily; "Britain’s wartime budget deficit... was 60.6 per cent of its pre-war national income; Germany’s, at 64 per cent, was little higher." The process involved suspension of convertibility, creation of new credit instruments (treasury notes, Darlehenskassen, Kriegsdarlehenskassen), and eventual loss of control to external creditors (especially the United States). Locator: TAXATION and FOREIGN BORROWING (Ch. 10).

It is distinct from industrial mobilization (raw materials, production) though intertwined: money enabled procurement of goods, but goods shortages ultimately determined sustainability.

## Causal Drivers

- Gold standard's incompatibility with war economy: regulated money supply and pivoted on international commerce; "the system was therefore deeply inimical to the requirements of a war economy." National hoarding and central-bank cooperation broke down into "a series of lesser financial units."
- Pre-war fiscal orthodoxy and complacency: Britain intolerant of debt post-Napoleonic and South African wars; Germany relied on 240 million mark war chest (Julius Tower) and Reichsbank treasury bills; Austria-Hungary weakened by Bosnian crisis liquidity drain; Russia maintained high gold cover but limited credit duration.
- Immediate 1914 crisis: runs on banks, stock exchange closures (London 31 July), foreign withdrawals, army cash demands for horses/fodder. "The pressure was, however, almost entirely domestic" in Germany; Britain faced international bill market collapse (~£120 million owed by Central Powers/Russia).
- Alliance dynamics and blockade: Central Powers isolated from foreign markets; Entente could draw on US and each other but at cost of exchange-rate management and gold shipments to London.

## Major Phases

**1914 Financial Mobilization (Aug–Dec):** Emergency measures dominated. Britain raised Bank rate to 10%, suspended Bank Act, issued Treasury currency notes, guaranteed pre-moratorium bills, stayed on gold; gold reserves actually rose. Germany suspended convertibility 4 Aug, issued 5 billion marks short-term credit on treasury bills, created Darlehenskassen (1.5+ billion marks capacity) for liquidity and small change; Reichsbank note circulation covered increasingly by bills not gold. Austria-Hungary suspended Bank act 5 Aug, massive note issue (circulation +91% by year end), Kriegsdarlehenskassen; moratoriums extended repeatedly. Russia suspended specie payments 5 Aug but maintained high gold reserves internally; France suspended convertibility, imposed sweeping moratoriums (rents, trade, banks) that persisted to 1920, Banque de France advanced 2.9+ billion francs. "The process of German mobilization had worked a more fundamental revolution in the mechanism of German credit than was realized at the time." Locator: FINANCIAL MOBILIZATION and GOLD STANDARD.

**1915 Adaptation and Exchange Management:** Britain used gold exports and US coordination to peg sterling; allies (France, Russia) pressured to ship gold. France reluctant ("the gold was effectively being used twice over"). Keynes arguments on using gold rather than hoarding. US remained on gold but accumulated reserves. Initial taxation experiments (excess profits duties delayed). Locator: GOLD STANDARD, FINANCIAL MOBILIZATION.

**1916–1918 Borrowing Dominance and External Discipline:** Heavy domestic loans (war bonds) across powers; foreign borrowing for Entente from US (Britain as intermediary until 1917). US entry and McAdoo conditions imposed coordination and discipline. Central Powers shifted to disinvestment ("the residue of war expenditure was funded through disinvestment... falling real incomes and declining output"). "The war cost twice as much to win as to lose, $147,000 million as against $61,500 million." Britain and France faced near-bankruptcy crises in 1917 despite American support. "Britain’s gloom about its financial position in 1918 was less a realistic response... than a continuing coda on the prevalence of pre-war views." Locator: FOREIGN BORROWING, TAXATION.

## Geographic Spread and Comparative Dynamics

- **Britain:** Stayed on gold longest in form; used Empire gold (South Africa fixed price), US market, and control of exchange to sustain liquidity and credit. City of London influence initially strong but Treasury gained control.
- **Germany:** Internal credit revolution via Reichsbank + Darlehenskassen; no general moratorium but delayed payments; price controls attempted alongside inflation denial ("inflation... could only be the consequence of a shortage of commodities").
- **Austria-Hungary:** Weakest position pre-war (gold cover fell sharply 1912); persistent small-change crisis; heavy reliance on Austro-Hungarian Bank advances; moratoriums damaged private credit and savings banks.
- **Russia:** Strong initial gold position but fiduciary reputation slender; State Bank funded both state and commercial needs; rapid deposit withdrawals followed by stabilization; transport and capital issues hampered industry.
- **France:** Sweeping moratoriums stifled industry/commerce long-term; Banque de France carried much of the burden (deferred bills, advances); used gold less reluctantly than Britain expected.
- **United States (neutral then belligerent):** Gold influx from Europe; Federal Reserve policies; later creditor role imposed discipline on Entente.

## End Conditions

The process ended with the Armistice and transition to peace settlements. Central Powers exhausted assets and faced defeat; Entente shifted burden via American credits and victory. Peacetime gold standard theory survived in rhetoric ("countries went ‘off’ gold in 1914 precisely in order to be able to go ‘on’ it again") but practice had transformed state-economy relations permanently. "The United States thus imposed some discipline on its allies."
