# State, Lordship and Community in the Post-Roman West

The structural transformation by which the **Roman superstate of the West gave way to the successor
kingdoms** and a more **localized, land-based society** — the deep social change underlying the political
events of the fifth and sixth centuries, and the starting point of medieval Europe. Treated in
[[cameron-cah-v14-2000|CAH XIV]] Ch. 16 (**Chris Wickham**), an analytical essay on the overlapping
"communities" — vertical (lord and follower) and horizontal (peer groups) — in which post-Roman people
acted. Wickham focuses on the **West** because that is where continuity of imperial institutions (army,
administration) was least and "the greatest changes in our period took place."

## Definition and scope

The process is the passage, c. 400–600, from a **European superstate** — Britain south of the Wall, Gaul,
Iberia, Italy, much of Germany under direct Roman rule — to a "**series of far from stable successor
states**": [[clovis|Frankish]] and Burgundian kingdoms, Visigoths and Sueves in Iberia,
[[theoderic-the-great|Ostrogoths]] then Lombards in Italy, Anglo-Saxons in Britain. The question is how
these kingdoms compared **as states** to Rome, and how they generated new political boundaries "in the minds
of their subjects, where previously there had been none."

## Causal drivers — from tax-state to land

- **The Roman fiscal-bureaucratic machine.** Late imperial power rested on a vast **bureaucracy** (~30,000
  posts, ~6,000 leading to senatorial rank) that gathered information, wrote law, **redistributed wealth**,
  and bound court, region and *civitas* into a unity — and worked as a **patronage machine**. Local
  pre-eminence required becoming an *honoratus* (a retired imperial servant), so aristocratic standing
  depended on **participation in the central state** and on the classical literary education that was its
  entry ticket.
- **The decline of the curiae.** As decurions fled into imperial service (echoing the
  [[late-roman-cities|cities]] chapter), local self-government withered and authority concentrated centrally
  — then, when the central state dissolved, devolved onto **local magnates and bishops**.
- **The decisive shift.** With the western tax-state gone, aristocratic power ceased to derive from **office
  and tax** and came instead to rest on **landownership and personal lordship** — the *vertical* ties of
  patronage and the warband. Kings, lacking the Roman fiscal base, ruled through **counts** and through
  their personal followings, rewarding followers with land rather than salaries (cf. the
  [[late-roman-army|army's]] fiscal collapse).

## Major phases and geographic spread

The transformation ran **unevenly**: fastest and furthest in **Britain** (near-total rupture) and northern
Gaul; more gradual where Roman structures persisted longest — **Ostrogothic Italy** (senate and
administration intact under [[theoderic-the-great|Theoderic]]), Visigothic and Burgundian Gaul/Iberia, and
above all the **surviving [[eastern-roman-empire-425-518|eastern empire]]**, where the tax-state endured.

## Significance

Wickham's essay reframes the "fall of Rome" as a **social transformation, not merely a political collapse**:
the disappearance of the **fiscally based aristocracy of service** and its replacement by an aristocracy of
**land and lordship** is the foundation of the early medieval order — the seedbed from which feudal lordship
and the localized politics of the post-Roman West would grow. It links the volume's economic argument
(Ward-Perkins's [[late-roman-economy|decline of complexity]]) to its political narrative (the
[[fall-of-the-western-roman-empire-476|fall of the West]]).
