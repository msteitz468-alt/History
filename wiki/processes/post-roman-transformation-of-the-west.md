# State, Lordship and Community in the Post-Roman West

The structural transformation by which the **Roman superstate of the West gave way to the successor kingdoms** and a more **localized, land-based society** — the deep social change underlying the political events of the fifth through seventh centuries, and the starting point of medieval Europe. Treated in [[cameron-cah-v14-2000|CAH XIV]] Ch. 16 (**Chris Wickham**) and [[fouracre-ncmh-v1-2005|NCMH I]], this process traces how post-Roman people re-established political authority, military structures, and economic networks following the collapse of imperial institutions (army, administration).

## Definition and scope

The process is the passage, c. 400–700, from a **European superstate** — Britain south of the Wall, Gaul, Iberia, Italy under direct Roman rule — to a series of successor states (such as the Frankish Merovingian kingdoms, the Visigothic Catholic kingdom in Iberia, and the Lombard kingdom in Italy). The question is how these kingdoms compared as states to Rome, and how they consolidated power and identity among their populations.

## Causal drivers — from tax-state to land

- **The Roman fiscal-bureaucratic machine.** Late imperial power rested on a vast **bureaucracy** (~30,000 posts, ~6,000 leading to senatorial rank) that gathered information, wrote law, redistributed wealth, and bound court, region and *civitas* into a unity. Standings depended on participation in the central state and classical literary education.
- **The decline of the curiae.** As decurions fled into imperial service, local self-government withered and authority concentrated centrally — then, when the central state dissolved, devolved onto **local magnates and bishops**.
- **The decisive shift.** With the western tax-state gone, aristocratic power ceased to derive from office and tax and came instead to rest on **landownership and personal lordship** — the *vertical* ties of patronage and the warband. Kings, lacking the Roman fiscal base, ruled through **counts** and through their personal followings, rewarding followers with land rather than salaries (cf. the [[late-roman-army|army's]] fiscal collapse).

## Major phases and geographic spread

The transformation ran **unevenly**:
- **Britain**: Near-total rupture in the fifth century, leading to fragmented, illiterate Anglo-Saxon kingdoms where social stratification only slowly re-emerged through weapon burials, barrows, and coastal trading centers (**emporia**) in the late sixth and seventh centuries (see [[coventry|Anglo-Saxon migration archaeology]]).
- **Italy**: The gradual Ostrogothic transition (dualism under [[theoderic-the-great|Theoderic]]) was shattered by the destructive **[[gothic-war-535-554|Gothic War (535–554)]]** and the subsequent **[[lombard-invasion-568|Lombard Invasion (568)]]**, permanently fragmenting the peninsula into Lombard duchies and Byzantine enclaves.
- **Iberia**: Gradual consolidation under the Visigoths, culminating in King Leovigild's unification and Reccared's conversion to Catholicism. Legal personality was officially replaced by a single, territorial law code, the **[[liber-iudiciorum|Liber Iudiciorum]]** (654), integrating the Roman and Gothic populations.
- **Gaul**: Frankish expansion under the Merovingians. Central royal power devolved to regional Mayors of the Palace, leading to the victory of Pippin II of Herstal at the **[[battle-of-tertry-687|Battle of Tertry]]** (687), establishing Austrasian hegemony. The Pippinid mayors converted this into kingship (**[[charles-martel]]**, then **[[pippin-iii|Pippin III]]**'s [[pippinid-coup-751|coup of 751]]) and then empire under **[[charlemagne|Charlemagne]]**. NCMH II (Fouracre) reads the [[carolingian-empire|Carolingian state]] as the culmination — yet largely the continuation — of this transformation: "in 814 as in 714, power on the ground lay in the hands of counts and bishops," the land-based aristocracy this process had created (see [[nature-of-carolingian-government]]).
- **Byzantine East**: Faced with the same loss of tax-base due to Sasanian and Arab conquests, the eastern empire underwent its own military-administrative transformation in the seventh century, replacing civil provinces with military districts under the **[[theme-system|Theme System]]**.

## Significance

Wickham and the NCMH contributors reframe the "fall of Rome" as a **social transformation, not merely a political collapse**: the disappearance of the fiscally based aristocracy of service and its replacement by an aristocracy of land and lordship is the foundation of the early medieval order — the seedbed from which feudal lordship and the localized politics of the post-Roman West would grow.

## Economic Transformation: From Mediterranean to North Sea

The post-Roman transformation was not only political but economic. The unified late Roman Mediterranean commercial network — anchored on African grain, Egyptian papyrus, Levantine oil and spice — contracted progressively through the fifth and sixth centuries as the fiscal-military machine that had underpinned it dissolved. By the seventh century, with the Arab conquests removing the southern Mediterranean from the Christian sphere, the commercial center of gravity in northwestern Europe had shifted decisively northward:

*   **The wic network**: A series of planned, royal-controlled trading settlements — **[[wic|wics]]** or *emporia* (Hamwic/Southampton, Lundenwic/London, Ipswich, Quentovic, Dorestad) — emerged on the North Sea and Channel coastlines from the late sixth century. These were not organic market-towns but royal fiscal instruments, generating toll revenue and concentrating artisanal production for export.
*   **The monetary shift**: The Merovingian gold coinage (*tremissis*), inherited from Rome and suited to elite exchange and tax collection, was progressively replaced from the 670s–680s by a low-denomination silver coinage — the **[[sceatta]]** — suited to everyday market transactions. This monetary transformation both reflected and enabled the expansion of commercial activity at the wic level.
*   **The Pirenne debate**: Whether the Arab conquests *caused* this northern reorientation (the **[[pirenne-thesis|Pirenne thesis]]**) or whether it had independent developmental roots in the Germanic world is the central controversy. Current consensus: both factors were at work — the Germanic invasions damaged Mediterranean commerce; the Arab conquests accelerated a process already underway.

## Legal and Ecclesiastical Transformation

The post-Roman transformation also produced a distinctively early medieval legal and ecclesiastical structure:

*   **The leges nationum**: Written law codes — the Salic Law, the Lombard *Edictus Rothari*, the Visigothic **[[liber-iudiciorum|Liber Iudiciorum]]** — encoded social hierarchies through **[[wergild]]** (monetized blood-price tables) and served as acts of royal legitimacy as much as practical administrative instruments (Wormald, *NCMH* Ch. 21).
*   **The Eigenkirche**: The ecclesiastical parallel to the land-based aristocracy was the **[[eigenkirche|Eigenkirche]]** (proprietary church) — churches treated as private property by lay magnates who appointed their priests and collected their tithes. The bishop's theoretical authority over his diocese was typically overridden in practice by the lord's *ius patronatus*. The royal synod, not the canonical council, was the effective instrument of church governance.
