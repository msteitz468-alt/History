# The Age of Discovery (European Oceanic Exploration, c. 1290–c. 1500)

The opening of long-range ocean routes by explorers from Latin Christendom, traced by **Felipe
Fernández-Armesto** in [[allmand-ncmh-v7-1998|NCMH VII]] (Ch. 9). Here "exploration" means "the
identification, investigation and recording" of practical routes between previously isolated
peoples. The fifteenth century turned the Atlantic from a barrier into "a highway to the rest of
the world," and the voyages of the 1490s — Columbus to the New World (1492), da Gama to India
(1497–8) — stand among the most consequential events in world history.

## Definition and Scope

Fernández-Armesto frames the process **comparatively and against expectation**. A "cosmic observer"
in 1400 would not have predicted Latin Christendom's pre-eminence: compared with China and Islam it
was "underequipped in the technical resources" for long journeys. Yet the better-equipped
civilisations turned inward. **China's** great tribute fleets under the eunuch-admiral **Zheng He**
(1405–33) reached Jiddah and Malindi, then were abruptly abandoned (mandarin hostility, factional
conflict, cost), the shipbuilding discontinued and records destroyed. **Islamic** navigation,
master of the monsoon Indian Ocean, never attempted the Atlantic. The decisive European advantage
was geographic: a **starting-place on the Atlantic rim**, whose wind systems offered routes to the
Pacific and Indian Oceans and between the Old and New Worlds — whereas the ways out of the Indian
Ocean were "laborious and dangerous" and the Pacific an effective dead end.

## Causal Drivers

- **The "culture of explorers."** A shared ethos, "unique to Latin Christendom," discerned "in
  patterns of behaviour rather than of thought." Explorers came mainly from **Genoa, Majorca**, and
  later **Portugal and Andalusia**; many had crusading or piratical backgrounds; most were penurious
  noblemen or would-be noblemen "escaping from a society of restricted opportunity," steeped in the
  **chivalric** romance of the footloose prince who wins an island-kingdom (Amadís, "Brutus,"
  Pero Niño's *El Victorial*). Columbus's "life's trajectory startlingly resembled the plot of a
  chivalric romance of the sea."
- **Patronage and finance.** Expeditions were small (1–3 ships, 20–50 men each) but few explorers
  could self-fund or protect a discovery. The crucial roles were the patron (conferring legitimate
  title — **Dom Henrique** "the Navigator," **Dom João II**, Henry VII for Cabot, the Catholic
  Monarchs for Columbus) and merchant backers (Genoese, Florentine, Bristol consortia). Royal cash
  was generally modest outside Portugal.
- **Cumulative technique.** The cog, compass, marine chart and primitive celestial navigation
  sufficed for the first phase; later came the manoeuvrable **lateen-rigged caravel**, long-keeping
  water casks, Azores victualling way-stations (from 1439), and — crucially — empirical mastery of
  the **Atlantic wind-and-current system**. Fernández-Armesto stresses that "rudimentary and
  antiquated technology was adequate for revolutionary tasks": *solvitur navigando* — it is solved by
  sailing.
- **Cartography.** Maps shifted from devotional *mappae mundi* to instruments of geographical
  realism, collating navigators' reports; rediscovered **Ptolemy** (Latin, early 15th c.) and Strabo
  fed speculation about the Antipodes and a "navigably narrow Atlantic" (Toscanelli, 1474).

## Major Phases (the Five Routes)

1. **The eastern Atlantic archipelagoes** (late 13th c.–mid-15th c.): rediscovery and settlement of
   the **Canaries, Madeira and Azores** (the Vivaldi brothers lost off Africa, 1291; records
   proliferate from the 1330s–40s; Flores and Corvo by 1452).
2. **Around and across the Sahara → the West African coast** (15th c.): the search to outflank the
   trans-Saharan gold trade. Under Dom Henrique, Genoese-led voyages reached the Cape Verde Islands
   and the Senegambia (Cadamosto) by the 1450s; **Fernão Gomes** added 1,200 miles of coast; under
   Dom João II, **Diogo Cão** reached the Kongo (1482–5) and **Bartolomeu Dias** rounded the **Cape
   of Good Hope (1488)**, finding the south-Atlantic westerlies.
3. **The north Atlantic** (from the 1450s): the old Norse Iceland–Greenland–Markland route had
   lapsed; Bristol's hunt for the "Isle of Brasil" culminated in **John Cabot's** crossing to
   **Newfoundland (1497)**.
4. **The central transatlantic route** (1492–3): **Columbus**, daring "to sail with the wind at his
   back" along the trade-wind diagonal from the Canaries, made landfall in the Antilles and
   established the enduring outbound/return routes of the age of sail.
5. **The south Atlantic → the Indian Ocean** (1497–1500): **Vasco da Gama's** great open-sea arc to
   India (1497–8); **Cabral** touched Brazil en route (1500); **Pinzón** and **Vespucci** traced the
   Brazilian coast — the continent eventually taking Vespucci's name.

## Interaction

The exploration process is the proximate cause of the **[[columbian-exchange-1492|Columbian
Exchange]]** (its biological and demographic consequences) and of European overseas empire and the
[[atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic slave trade]] (begun in Henrican West Africa). It both drew on and
redirected the [[early-modern-commerce|commercial recovery]] (the Portuguese pepper route, the
shift of the [[urbanization|commercial centre of gravity]] from the Mediterranean toward the
Atlantic — Bruges→Antwerp, Burgos→Seville). It pushed celestial navigation into the southern
hemisphere, stimulating the science of navigation, and rebuilt the world map on empirical
foundations.

## End Conditions

The fifteenth-century achievement opened the routes that the sixteenth century would exploit:
sustained transatlantic colonisation, the *Carreira da Índia*, and circumnavigation. The process
does not so much end as transform into the [[age-of-expansion|age of overseas empire]].

## Historiography

Fernández-Armesto writes against both the heroic "cape-by-cape" nationalist narrative (the
chronicler Zurara's glorification of Henry the Navigator's circle — he downplays the supposed
1434 "breakthrough" at Cape Bojador as an artefact of that narrative) and an over-gradualist
flattening of events: while change came through "long, grinding processes," "the concentrated speed
and power of the episodes of the 1490s... still demand to be acknowledged." He stresses the
**contingency and modest means** of the explorers, the prominence of the **chivalric and crusading
imagination**, and the paradox that this "great leap forward... followed a period of demographic
decline and... contracting frontiers" — it may have "helped to come from behind."
