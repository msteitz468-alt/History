## Definition and Scope

The Pacific cultural renaissance designates the broad revitalisation of Pacific Islander cultural production, language, performance, and self-representation that accelerated from the 1960s and intensified through the 1980s and 1990s. It is intertwined with — though distinct from — the processes of [[wiki/processes/pacific-decolonisation|decolonisation]] and [[wiki/processes/pacific-migration-and-diaspora|diaspora]]. The renaissance encompasses performing arts, visual arts, oral literature, written literature in English, language revival, and the contemporary revival of traditional voyaging.

As [[denoon-cambridge-pacific-islanders-1997]] frames it: "The era of decolonisation saw a cultural reawakening in literature and art. In their attempts to mould nation-states, Island leaders organised national art and craft festivals to revive suppressed art forms."

## Hawaiian Renaissance

The Hawaiian case is treated as paradigmatic of a Fourth World cultural renaissance. After annexation (1900) and statehood (1959), systematic assimilation had "driven Hawaiian arts and sciences near to extinction." From the 1960s onwards, in response to the civil rights movement, land rights protests (Kalama Valley, Kaho'olawe), and sovereignty challenges: "Hawaiian music was taking on new vigour. Hula halau (training schools and repositories of Hawaiian dance) gained prestige and members, canoe clubs flourished, Hawaiian language-classes burgeoned, as did interest in the natural medicines of Hawai'i and Hawai'i's history."

A defining victory: in February 1992 "the Board of Education approved 'a policy allowing public school students to be taught almost entirely in the Hawaiian language through high school'." The *punana leo* (language nest) model for pre-school immersion was adopted from the Maori example and applied in Hawai'i.

## Maori Renaissance

In New Zealand, Maori language and culture experienced revival linked to the political mobilisation around the Treaty of Waitangi. Maori *punana leo* (language nests) for pre-school language immersion are mentioned as pioneering the model later adopted in Hawai'i. The rebuilding of Maori meeting houses and assertion of *tangi* (funeral ceremonies) in public contexts are cited as expressions of cultural identity and political resistance.

## Festival of Pacific Arts

"The culmination of this renaissance was the first South Pacific Festival of Arts (now the Festival of Pacific Arts) in Suva in 1972. The Suva festival was a turning point in restoring dignity and pride to all Pacific countries, except Guam and Tahiti which did not participate until the second festival four years later in Rotorua, New Zealand." The festival has brought together artists every four years since then, producing "a cultural explosion on a scale previously unheard of in the Pacific."

The festival raised questions about authenticity and representation: representations of identity "tend to project images of a static, 'traditional' culture." At the 1980 Port Moresby festival, other Islander participants initially had difficulty crediting Chamorro modern dance as authentically "Pacific"; by 1988 "other nations started moving beyond the strict constraints of traditional pasts."

## University of Papua New Guinea and Pacific Literature

"The University of Papua New Guinea opened its doors in 1966, and its students started writing imaginative literature with the encouragement of Ulli and Georgina Beier. Soon poems, stories, plays and novels were being written in English." This moment marks the beginning of Anglophone Pacific literature as a recognised form. Albert Wendt, Epeli Hau'ofa, Witi Ihimaera are among the writers whose work is woven into the chapter's analysis.

## Contemporary Voyaging Revival

The revival of traditional voyaging — canoe-building and ocean navigation by traditional methods — is an important dimension of the renaissance, concretising the [[wiki/concepts/sea-of-islands|sea-of-islands]] concept. "Vigorous attempts to revive canoe-building skills in preparation for the 1992 Festival of Pacific Arts constitute one such example." Canoe clubs flourished in Hawai'i as part of the Hawaiian renaissance from the 1960s. The source frames contemporary voyaging as continuous with — not a break from — pre-colonial patterns: "The histories of Islanders are histories of movement — into the Pacific to settle; sailing between islands to maintain links."

## The Syncretism Problem

A key analytical issue is that revived cultural forms are necessarily syncretised with colonial and Christian elements. "Christianity has shed its 'light' on the Islands for more than a hundred years; cultural elements which are revived are therefore usually those which can be reconciled with Christian beliefs." In Tonga, Christian hymns sung in native languages are regarded by participants as "keeping the culture alive." "In the matter of religion, however, most Islanders have no intention of resurrecting their ancestors' gods. Instead, Christianity and its practice are regarded as traditional."

This creates a contradiction: "Attempts to preserve traditional cultures in a context of profound social change have led to charges of 'invented traditions' and 'inauthenticity' by Western scholars." The source does not dismiss these charges but insists "these identities are valid and necessary, as Islanders struggle towards self-determination in all spheres of their lives."

## Education as Limit

The renaissance has been constrained by educational systems still oriented to metropolitan culture. "In its early stages the University of the South Pacific showed great promise in restoring Island pride. Yet a generation later its Literature and Language Department has yet to teach any Pacific language." "Unless politicians and teachers introduce sweeping changes in curricula, true decolonisation of the mind will remain a dream."

## Historiography

[[denoon-cambridge-pacific-islanders-1997]] treats the cultural renaissance primarily through the framework of Hereniko (Rotuman) and Hau'ofa (Tongan), both insider scholars. The source is attentive to the danger of elite capture: cultural identity in public discourse is shaped disproportionately by "national ruling elites" and the "educated elite in urban areas or in metropolitan countries." The source is also attentive to gender: the renaissance has been male-dominated in many settings, though women are making claims to cultural production and leadership.
