## Definition and Scope

German industrialization refers to the transformation of the German economy from agrarian to industrial-capitalist, concentrated in the period 1848–1914 (with momentum beginning in the late 1840s and accelerating dramatically after 1871). Chapter 15 (Cornelius Torp) of the Oxford Handbook situates it within dual processes of *industrialization* and *globalization* that together constituted what Karl Polanyi would call "the Great Transformation."

The process was specifically regional rather than national. "Even if the nation states with their institutions and legal frameworks played an important role, regional factors, such as the existence of raw materials, the population structure, and transportation and communication channels, are decisive." The key industrial zones — the Ruhr, the Saar, Upper Silesia — were determined by coal deposits. By 1914, industrial regions still coexisted with pre-industrial rural areas, and the Ruhr–Pas-de-Calais industrial macro-region straddled national borders.

Some scholars compare the significance of industrialization with the Neolithic agricultural revolution: Torp notes that "some scholars consider industrialization, the first of the processes mentioned above, as one of the two fundamental revolutions of mankind, comparable only with the 'neolithic revolution.'"

## Causal Drivers

**Phase One (1848–1890): The Leading Sector Complex**

Germany's industrialization was driven by four interlocking "leading sectors": railroads, iron and steel, coal mining, and engineering. These formed a complex of mutual backward and forward linkages:
- Railroads required steel and coal; they produced it at scale and distributed it cheaply
- Coal mines and steel works used large machines and transported by train
- Track length grew from 469 km (1840) to 30,125 km (1880); railroad freight from 3 million to 13,039 million ton-km

The state played a crucial enabling role: Prussia liberalized coal mining law (enabling Prussian coal production to more than double between 1850 and 1860, and again in the following decade), reformed joint-stock company law in 1870 (triggering a boom of 928 new corporations in 1871–1873 versus 295 in the previous two decades), and invested massively in railroad construction, with Prussian national debt rising 100% between 1848 and 1865.

*Banks* were essential. Unlike Britain's light-industrial path, Germany's heavy-industrial path was "extremely capital intensive from the outset." The great universal banks (*Universalbanken*) — Deutsche Bank, Darmstädter, Dresdner, Disconto-Gesellschaft — were founded in two waves (1853–1856, 1869–1873) and combined investment and commercial banking. They "played a crucial role in the German industrialization process" as shareholders, lenders, and directors of industrial enterprises.

By 1893, German steel production overtook Britain's; by 1903, pig iron production followed. Germany also surpassed Britain as leading iron and steel exporter.

**Phase Two (1890–1914): Chemical and Electrical Industries**

The second phase was driven by "new industries" in chemicals and electrical goods — industries "which depended much more than earlier industries on systematic scientific research."

*Chemicals*: German companies (BASF, Bayer, Agfa, Hoechst) had covered half of world demand for synthetic dyestuffs by the late 1870s; by 1914 their share was nearly 90%. German dyestuff exports accounted for 82% of these firms' turnover in 1913. Germany became "the pharmacy of the world" in pharmaceuticals. The competitive advantage derived from university and *Technische Hochschule* training of scientific personnel — a systematic linkage between academic science and industrial production.

*Electrical industry*: Dominated by Siemens-Schuckert (merger 1903) and AEG (founded 1883 by Emil Rathenau). Together they controlled three-quarters of German electrical output. Siemens employed 81,745 people in 1913 (18,348 outside Germany), making it the world's largest electrical employer. German electrical exports were 46% of world total in 1913 — twice Britain's share and three times the United States'.

## Major Phases

| Phase | Years | Key Features |
|---|---|---|
| Take-off | 1848–1873 | Railroad boom, iron/steel/coal leading sectors, *Gründerboom* |
| Great Deflation | 1873–1895 | Retarded growth, declining prices, cartelization, 1879 tariff shift |
| Second surge | 1895–1914 | Chemical/electrical leadership, globalization peak, real NDP +3.41%/yr |

**Quantitative indicators** (Torp, Table 15.1):
- Net domestic product (1913 marks): 9.4 billion (1850) → 48.5 billion (1913)
- NDP per capita: 265 marks (1850) → 728 marks (1914)
- Investment rate: exceeded Rostow's "take-off" threshold of 10% consistently after 1870
- Industrial sector overtook primary sector by early 1890s

## Geographic Spread

Industrialization was deliberately concentrated by the railroad network and natural resources:
- **Ruhr**: Coal and iron; Dortmund, Essen, Bochum, Duisburg combined population over 1 million by 1910
- **Saar**: Iron and coal industry center
- **Upper Silesia**: Mining and metallurgy triangle (Beuthen, Tarnowitz, Kattowitz)
- **Berlin and Hamburg**: World cities, financial and commercial centers
- **Electro-chemical belt**: Rhine-Main, Munich, Siemens headquarters Berlin

By 1910, one in five Germans lived in cities of over 100,000; the number of such cities had increased six-fold. Population grew from 35.3 million (1850) to 67.0 million (1913). The great emigration of the 1880s (nearly 2 million, mainly to the United States) gave way to immigration of over 1.2 million foreign workers by 1914 — mostly Polish agricultural laborers from Russian and Austro-Hungarian Poland.

## Interaction with Other Processes

Industrialization was inseparable from:
- **[[german-unification]]**: Railroads integrated the national market; the Zollverein (customs union) created the economic preconditions for political unification
- **[[great-transformation-germany-1850-1914]]**: The social consequences — class formation, urbanization, mass migration — were the immediate outputs of industrialization
- **[[german-colonialism|German colonialism]]**: Colonial acquisitions were partly driven by search for markets, raw materials, and investment outlets, though in practice colonial trade and investment remained marginal to the German economy
- **[[weltpolitik]]**: The naval expansion program was partly a Keynesian measure to smooth cyclical fluctuations in heavy industry through guaranteed naval procurement
- **Globalization**: By 1913, German export ratios approached 16% of GNP; imports nearly 20%. Germany was the world's third-largest exporter, with the United States and United Kingdom

## End Conditions

German industrialization was not "completed" in 1914; it was interrupted by World War I, which destroyed the globalized world economy that had sustained the second phase. The institutional patterns established in this period — universal banking, coordinated market capitalism, cartelization, welfare state foundations (Bismarck's social insurance 1883–1889) — persisted and became the basis of the post-1945 "German model" of capitalism. Werner Abelshauser identifies "strong continuities of German capitalism" linking the Kaiserreich to the Federal Republic's *Wirtschaftswunder*.

## Historiography

The principal historiographical debates concern: (1) the role of the state versus markets in German industrialization (Gerschenkron's "relative backwardness" thesis emphasized state substitution for missing capital markets; recent work has modified this); (2) whether there was a distinctive "German model of capitalism" (*koordinierte Marktwirtschaft*) or simply a regional variant of general capitalist development; (3) the relationship between industrial growth and political authoritarianism — the core of the [[german-sonderweg]] debate.

Torp's chapter explicitly addresses the second debate, concluding that while "organized capitalism" characteristics did mark important sectors, "there are as many particular ways of industrialization or evolving models of capitalism as there are industrializing countries."
