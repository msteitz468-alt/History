# Ottoman Reform (the New Order and the Tanzimat)

The long effort to **reform and recentralize** the Ottoman state in response to military
defeat and European power — from Selim III's "New Order" through the Tanzimat ("Reordering")
of the mid-nineteenth century to the 1876 constitution. From Caroline Finkel's
[[finkel-osmans-dream|*Osman's Dream*]] (chs 13–15).

## The "New Order" (Nizâm-ı Cedîd) and its destruction

The first sustained reform was **Selim III's** ***Nizâm-ı Cedîd*** ("New Order") from the
1790s: a European-style **new army** (uniformed, drilled), new fiscal measures, and Ottoman
permanent embassies in European capitals. It collided with entrenched interests — the
**[[janissaries]]**, the provincial notables (*ayan*), and conservative clerics — who opposed
conscription and "infidel" innovation. In **1807** a revolt of Bosphorus militia and
Janissaries (the Kabakçı Mustafa uprising) destroyed the New Order and **deposed Selim**, who
was later murdered: Finkel notes the parallel with Osman II (1622) — sultans brought down for
trying to reorganize military manpower.

## Mahmud II: clearing the ground

**Mahmud II** (1808–39) revived reform by force. His decisive act was the violent **abolition
of the Janissaries in 1826** (the "Auspicious Incident"), removing the chief obstacle to a
modern army, followed by the curbing of the provincial notables and the *waqf* (pious
foundations), the creation of new ministries and a European-style central bureaucracy, and the
beginnings of modern schooling and a postal and census apparatus — the groundwork for
systematic reform.

## The Tanzimat ("Reordering," 1839–76)

The **Tanzimat** proper was inaugurated by the **Gülhane Rescript (1839)** and the **Reform
Edict (1856)**, which promised security of life, honour, and property for **all subjects
regardless of religion**, regular taxation and conscription, and equality before the law. It
built a centralized, codified state: new **secular law codes** (commercial, penal, and the
*Mecelle* civil code), **secular schools and the bureaucracy** that produced a new reforming
elite, provincial reorganization, and the ideology of ***Ottomanism*** — a common Ottoman
citizenship transcending religious community (the *millet* system). The movement culminated in
the **first Ottoman constitution and parliament (1876)** under the reformers led by Midhat
Pasha.

## Tensions and outcomes

Reform was double-edged. Centralization and equality alienated both conservative Muslims (who
saw privilege eroded) and the empowered Christian communities (whose nationalisms it could not
contain); European powers used the reform edicts as levers of intervention (the
[[eastern-question|Eastern Question]]); and the costs drove the state into **European debt and,
after the 1875 default, the foreign-controlled Public Debt Administration**. The new sultan
**Abdülhamid II** suspended the constitution (1878) and ruled autocratically for three decades,
turning from Ottomanism toward **pan-Islamism** while continuing administrative and educational
modernization. The constitution's suspension and the contradictions of reform fed the
opposition that became the **Young Turk revolution of 1908** — which restored the constitution
and opened the empire's final phase ([[fall-of-the-ottoman-empire-1922]]).

## Interaction

Ottoman reform is the eastern counterpart of the era's other [[meiji-modernization|defensive
modernizations]]; it shaped, and was shaped by, the [[eastern-question|Eastern Question]] and
left deep legacies to the **Turkish Republic** (the centralized, secularizing, bureaucratic
state Atatürk inherited and radicalized).
