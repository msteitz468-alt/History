## Definition and Scope

**Pastoralism** is the practice of keeping grazing animals — sheep, goats,
cattle, horses, camelids — as the primary subsistence base, especially in mobile,
non-industrial societies that rely *mostly* on their livestock for food. It is
the subject of Ch. 6 of CWH Vol. II (Alan Outram). It is distinguished from
**mixed farming** (where stock-keeping is integrated with, and often ancillary
to, crop cultivation).

**Revised status.** Once thought a *primitive* stage *preceding* plant
cultivation, pastoralism is now recognized as usually a **sophisticated,
specialized economy on the edge of — and intimately linked to — state-level
agricultural societies** (Barker & Goucher). It is generally *later* than and
*derived from* mixed farming, not ancestral to it. (Africa is a partial
exception, where animal domestication often preceded crops — see
[[early-african-food-production]].)

## The key constraints

The limiting factors a pastoralist must solve are **access to grazing land** and
**fodder for the lean season**. The universal response is **mobility**, which
takes graded forms:
- **Nomadism** — the whole community moves with the herds, without fixed base.
- **Transhumance** — seasonal movement of animals (often with part of the
  community) between, e.g., winter lowland and summer upland pasture
  (vertical) or between regions (horizontal).

Tim Ingold's ethnographic typology (cited by Outram): **meat pastoralists**
(exploit animals only for carcass products, like hunters); **milch pastoralists**
(also exploit live-animal "secondary products" — milk, wool, traction, riding);
and **ranchers** (operating in a cash economy). Outram cautions these cannot be
mapped onto ancient archaeology simplistically: "activities in the past do not
always have modern or recent analogues."

## "Wealth on the hoof" and inequality

A central theme of the volume: livestock is **mobile, accumulable wealth**.
Herds can be counted, banked, lent, and used to host feasts or pay bride-wealth,
making pastoralism a powerful engine of **social and economic inequality** — one
of agriculture's two great inequality-generating complexes (the other being
fixed land and urbanism — see [[agriculture-and-urbanism]]). The
boundary between controlled herds and surrounding wild populations could be
fluid (e.g. the modern and ancient DNA of Eurasian-steppe horses).

## Relation to the secondary-products revolution

Specialized milch pastoralism depends on exploiting *live* animals — the
[[secondary-products-revolution]]. The volume's important revision (Outram):
because **lipid-residue analysis** now shows substantial **dairying already in
the early Neolithic** (e.g. Britain, NW Anatolia), the capacity for
milk-based pastoral economies existed *much earlier* than the Bronze-Age
"secondary products revolution" once posited — so that revolution can no longer
be treated as a *prerequisite* for steppe and plains pastoral cultures, and their
genesis must be explained another way.

## Geographic Spread

Specialized pastoralism flourished above all on the **Eurasian steppes** and
**Central Asian** plains (often in the absence of cereal agriculture) and across
the **Saharan/Sahelian** and **Near Eastern** arid zones. Saharan cattle were
herded by ~6000 BCE (see [[early-african-food-production]]). Steppe pastoralism
later fed into horse-riding, chariotry, and the mobile societies that recur
throughout Eurasian history.

## Interaction

- Derives from [[domestication]] and the [[secondary-products-revolution]].
- A specialized branch of [[agricultural-origins]].
- Co-generates inequality alongside [[agriculture-and-urbanism]] and
  [[neolithic-communities]] (where herding added risk-spreading resilience to
  household economies).

## End Conditions

Ongoing. Mobile pastoralism persists across Eurasia and Africa, though
increasingly marginalized by states, enclosure, and sedentarization.
