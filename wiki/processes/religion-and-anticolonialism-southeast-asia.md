## Definition and Scope

Religion and anti-colonialism in Southeast Asia from the mid-nineteenth to the early twentieth centuries constituted an intertwined religio-political phenomenon in which established faiths — Theravada Buddhism, Islam, and Catholicism — furnished both the language for articulating discontent and the social forms for mobilizing adherents against colonial rule. This process is distinct from secular nationalist movements (see [[southeast-asian-nationalism]]): it preceded and partially overlapped with nationalism, was primarily rural-based, and drew on millennial and messianic traditions rather than liberal or Marxist ideologies. The chapter on religion and anti-colonial movements in [[tarling-cambridge-history-southeast-asia-v2-1992]] treats these movements "not as momentary interruptions of the grand sagas of colonial conquest, nationalism, modernization or state construction" but in their own terms, as expressions of indigenous thought.

## Causal Drivers

### The Religio-Political Landscape

Every major Southeast Asian civilization possessed a religio-political structure in which sacred authority and temporal power were mutually constituting. Buddhist polities in Burma, Siam, and Cambodia organized themselves around the concept of the *cakkavatti* (world-conquering righteous king) and the anticipated coming of the Maitreya (Future Buddha). Kings maintained legitimacy by patronizing the *sangha* (monkhood) and embodying accumulated merit (*phumibun*). The corollary of this was that individuals demonstrating exceptional merit — forest-dwelling ascetics, *weikza* (magicians), *saya* (esoteric experts) — could challenge or rival royal authority, especially when the monarchy was seen to have lost its efficacy.

In the Javanese and Malay Islamic world, the *kyai* (Islamic teacher) and the *ulama* based in *pesantren* (Islamic schools) occupied an analogous position: outside state control, yet commanding popular loyalty. The Sufi *tarekat* brotherhoods offered networks through which reformist ideas — including Wahhabi-influenced purification from Mecca — could circulate and radicalize village-level religion. The *ratu adil* (just king) tradition in Java paralleled Buddhist millenarianism, and the figure of the Mahdi gave Islamic anti-colonialism a messianic cast.

In the Christianized Philippines, the *Pasyon* (the Gospel passion narrative, widely performed in vernacular drama) provided a popular cosmology in which holy men and prophets could be identified with Christ's redemptive suffering, and colonial oppression could be framed as the prelude to apocalyptic renewal. Pre-Spanish animist practitioners — *babaylan* (sorcerers and healers) — persisted alongside Catholic forms, providing an alternative religious idiom that colonial authorities dismissed as mere superstition.

### Colonial Disruption

The acceleration of European penetration from the 1850s destabilized all of these structures. The British abolition of the Burmese monarchy in 1885 — removing the king who was the cosmological anchor of the Buddhist state — opened the field to any claimant capable of articulating millennial expectations. The French gradual subordination of the Vietnamese emperor at Hue spiritually emptied the centre and prompted peasant rebels to look to alternative messianic figures. Dutch expansion in Java displaced the *priyayi* (Javanese aristocracy) from their traditional role, pushing independent *kyai* into a more explicitly anti-colonial position. In the Philippines, the Spanish policy of replacing Filipino secular priests with Spanish friars from the 1830s onward generated racial tensions that fed directly into the 1896 Katipunan rebellion.

Economic disruptions compounded ideological ones: the cholera epidemics of the 1880s, the depression of that decade, the opening of the Suez Canal (1869) that intensified the Mecca pilgrimage and the circulation of reformist Islamic ideas, and later the Great Depression of the 1930s — all triggered the appearance of prophets and holy men who promised relief from suffering through cosmic renewal.

## Major Phases

### Pre-1850: Religio-Political Landscape and Early Movements

The Dipanagara rebellion in Java (1825–30), though led by a prince of the Yogyakarta royal family, drew its mass support from popular millennial expectations: Dipanagara assumed the title Sultan Erucakra (a name for the *ratu adil*) and framed the struggle as a jihad against the Dutch and their collaborators. After its defeat, *priyayi* identification with Dutch rule deepened, leaving independent *kyai* and *ulama* as the primary organizers of rural resistance.

In Vietnam, the Buu Son Ky Hong ("Strange Fragrance from Precious Mountain") tradition, founded by the "Buddha Master" Doan Min Huyen in the south, provided a syncretic millenarian framework combining Buddhist, Taoist, and Khmer elements. Its slogan — "The Low Era is at an end" — addressed populations living beyond effective Confucian or later French control.

In Cambodia, *nak sel* (holy men with magical powers) led anti-Vietnamese rebellions in the 1820s and the great onkya-led revolt of 1840–47 that the source describes as similar to Burmese guerrilla resistance against the British after 1885: fragmented bands armed with primitive weapons, animated by Buddhist millennial expectations, defending the kingdom against alien encroachment.

### 1850–1900: Anti-colonial Movements Under Intensified Colonial Pressure

This phase saw an acceleration of religio-political resistance across the region. Key instances:

**Burma**: After the British annexation of lower Burma (1852) and upper Burma (1885), a succession of *Setkya-min* (messianic king) claimants emerged. Ex-monks like U Ottama (who established a small principality in 1886–88) and U Kelatha led armed uprisings. The British deployment of 32,000 troops and 8,500 military police to suppress resistance between 1886 and 1891 indicates the scale of this resistance. The source notes: "It took another five years of campaigning from 1886 to subdue the country."

**Vietnam**: The Can Vuong ("Loyalty to the King") movement from 1885, launched by edict of the boy-emperor Ham Nghi, combined Confucian scholar-gentry resistance with popular millenarianism. In the Mekong delta, the Dao Lanh sect under Tran van Thanh and the Buu Son Ky Hong tradition produced repeated uprisings through the 1860s–1880s, culminating in Nam Thiep's 1878 rebellion and his proclamation of himself as a living Buddha on Elephant Mountain.

**Java/Indonesia**: The Banten uprising of 1888 exemplified the role of *hajji*-mediated reformist Islam: returning pilgrims from Mecca distributed amulets and preached jihad through the Kadiriah *tarekat* network. The rebels marched "convinced that they were invulnerable in waging the holy war." Similar movements followed in 1903 (Kasan Mukmin) and 1907 (Kyai Dermadjaja's *ratu adil* claim).

**Philippines**: The Cofradia de San Jose (founded 1832 by Apolinario de la Cruz as an approved Catholic sodality) transformed into an independent commune on Mount Banahao that "attracted thousands of lowlanders" before being violently suppressed in 1841. The subsequent proliferation of *babaylan*-led movements in the Visayas through the 1880s, promising millennial renewal in the wake of cholera, comets, and falling sugar prices, established the popular religious terrain that the 1896 Katipunan rebellion would enter.

### 1900–1940: Persistence Alongside and Within Nationalism

Colonial authorities expected that military victory and co-optation of educated elites would terminate religio-political resistance, but it persisted well into the twentieth century — both in older forms and in new hybrid combinations with nationalist idioms.

**Philippines**: After the Philippine-American War (1899–1902), Macario Sakay continued Katipunan resistance until 1906. Felipe Salvador — hailed as king, pope, or Christ by peasant followers — proselytized in central Luzon until his capture in 1910 after occupying a town upon the appearance of Halley's Comet. Sects descended from the Cofradia continued operating on Mount Banahao. The Sakdal movement of 1934–35 blended peasant grievances, Filipino nationalist rhetoric, and messianic expectations.

**Burma**: *Min-laung* pretenders and Setkya-min claimants continued to appear through the 1910s. The Saya San rebellion (1930–32) combined explicit millenarian traditionalism — amulets, a makeshift royal palace and regalia, claims to restore the Buddhist monarchy — with genuine peasant grievances against colonial taxation and land rents exacerbated by the Great Depression. Even while the urban Dobama Asiayone (*thakin*) movement emerged in the 1930s, the idiom of Buddhist messialism remained potent in rural areas.

**Vietnam**: Phan Xich Long's 1913 attack on Saigon with white-garbed followers claiming descent from deposed emperor Ham Nghi, and the 1916 rebellion centered on the monk Bay Do on Forbidden Mountain, extended the millennial tradition. The Cao Dai sect from the mid-1920s combined syncretic theology with conservative anti-colonialism, eventually veering toward pro-Japanese sentiments.

**Siam/Thailand**: Phumibun-led movements continued in the northeast and in southern Laos, where the movements of 1901–02 led by Ong Keo and Ong Man claimed Maitreya authority and were suppressed by combined Thai and French forces. Rural millenarianism in the northeast (the uprisings of 1924, 1933, 1936) envisioned the coming of the Maitreya as a prelude to restoring a Lao kingdom — a sub-territorial ethnic nationalism in millennial idiom.

**Malaya**: The 1915 Kelantan uprising and the 1928 Terengganu rebellion illustrate how *hajji*-derived holy men — operating on the fringes of sultanate authority — framed anti-colonial resistance as jihad against the *kafir*, even where formal independence rhetoric was absent.

## Geographic Spread

The process operated across the entire region but with locally distinctive religious idioms:

- **Buddhist mainland** (Burma, Siam, Cambodia, Laos): Setkya-min, phumibun, *min-laung*, Maitreya imagery
- **Confucian Vietnam**: Can Vuong loyalism, Buu Son Ky Hong sectarianism, scholar-gentry martyrdom traditions
- **Javanese and Sumatran Islam**: ratu adil, Mahdi, *jihad*, *pesantren* and *tarekat* networks, Wahhabi reformism via Mecca pilgrimage
- **Malay coastal Islam**: *keramat* holy men, *hajji*-led anti-tax rebellions
- **Christian Philippines**: pasyon-inflected messiahs, *babaylan* sorcerers, Cofradia lineages, Rizal-as-Christ

The source emphasizes "striking regularities in the style of anti-colonial resistance throughout Southeast Asia" despite these doctrinal differences — in particular the centrality of the charismatic holy man, the promise of invulnerability, the distribution of amulets, the prediction of imminent cosmic change, and the formation of communities set apart from colonial society.

## End Conditions

This process did not terminate sharply. The Japanese occupation (1941–45) disrupted the colonial order so completely that many of these movements were absorbed into or outcompeted by fully organized nationalist parties. However, the source insists that these traditions "continued to thrive in their rural environments during the period of high colonialism" and that "the course of 'modern' movements was profoundly shaped by older terrains and discourses." Post-independence states in Burma (Buddhist socialism under U Nu), the Philippines (folk-Catholic political culture), and Indonesia (the continued political role of NU and Muhammadiyah) all bore marks of this religio-political substrate.

## Historiography

The historiography of these movements has been contentious. Colonial records dismissed them as "disturbances" led by "dacoits or fanatics" — a labelling strategy, as the chapter notes, that placed them outside the realm of legitimate politics. Post-colonial nationalist historiography appropriated them as proto-nationalist resistance. The influential framework of Harry Benda established a hierarchy from "primitive" peasant movements to "modern" urban nationalism, which later scholars criticized for imposing a teleology that obscured what these movements actually meant on their own terms. Reynaldo Ileto's *Pasyon and Revolution* (1979) on the Philippines and James Scott's work on peasant consciousness have been key in reading these movements "from below." The Tarling volume explicitly follows this approach, treating religious idiom not as superstition to be explained away but as the matrix through which peasants organized their experience of colonial disruption.
