## Definition and Scope

This process covers the systematic destruction of indigenous societies across southern, coastal, and northeastern Brazil and the Amazon Valley from 1580 to 1890, the forms of resistance and adaptation indigenous peoples deployed, and the transformations in ethnic identity that resulted. Robin M. Wright with Manuela Carneiro da Cunha identify five intersecting themes: diversity of aboriginal sociocultural patterns; patterns of penetration and conquest; directions in Indian policy; transformations in interethnic relations; and forms of adaptation and strategies of resistance.

The geographic scope is vast: the southern states (São Paulo, Paraná, Santa Catarina, Rio Grande do Sul) inhabited by Guaraní, Guaycurú, Kaingang, and Xokleng; the central coast and northeastern sertão (Botocudo, Kariri, Potiguar); and the Amazon Valley (Omagua, Yoriman, Manao, Mura, and dozens of others). Though the specific dynamics differ by region, Wright and Carneiro da Cunha argue the master process was consistent: slaving, missionization, epidemic disease, and forced relocation combined to "transform an ethnically diverse population into a homogeneous mass of either nominally free or slave laborers."

## Causal Drivers

**The slave economy.** The principal agents of southern expansion were the [[bandeirantes]] "whose constant expeditions served to supply an ever-growing demand for Indian labor." In the Amazon, official and private slave troops operated from the early seventeenth century; various religious orders collaborated with slaving expeditions. "At least 1,000 slaves per year at the height of official slaving in the eighteenth century" were relocated to coastal plantations. The systemic depopulation of principal rivers forced slavers to venture ever-greater distances, creating a self-amplifying cycle of geographic expansion and demographic destruction.

**Indian policy oscillations.** Portuguese Indian law followed two coexisting principles: a law of peace governing settled, allied Indians and a law of war permitting enslavement. The crown repeatedly declared Indian freedom (1570s–1609, 1680, mid-18th century) and repeatedly reintroduced exceptions under colonist pressure. As late as 1808 Dom João VI "waged an anachronistic war against the Botocudo in Minas Gerais, Espirito Santo, and present-day Paraná, in which slavery was once again introduced." Slavery was formally abolished in 1833 but "Indians were still sold as slaves until well into the middle of the nineteenth century."

**Mission-aldeia system.** Religious orders (Jesuit, Franciscan, Carmelite, Mercedarian) established [[jesuit-aldeias]] and mission villages, nominally protecting Indian freedom but in practice supplying labor to crown administration and colonists. Free Indians in aldeias had a "triple obligation: to sustain the villages, to take part as troops and rowers in further expeditions to bring more Indians down from the backlands, and to serve crown officials and private parties mostly in agricultural work, fishing, and gathering." Aldeias were multiethnic — decimated populations constantly replenished by new forced descents — a mechanism for dissolving distinct identities into a generic "Tapuya" or "Carijó" neo-indigenous stratum.

**Pombaline secularization and its aftermath.** The mid-eighteenth-century reforms of the Marquis of Pombal expelled the Jesuits from Brazil in 1759, abolished mission segregation, and mandated cultural assimilation including Portuguese-language use and intermarriage. The Pombaline Directorate (1757–1798) placed lay administrators in villages, granting them 6 percent of village revenue — incentivizing exploitation. Indians deserted villages en masse; by 1798 the directorate was officially abolished. In 1845 the Regulation of the Missions revived mission administration under Italian Franciscans, but the system never recovered coherence.

**Land and capital accumulation (19th century).** After 1822 the dominant question shifted from labor to land. By claiming extinction of indigenous populations, provinces and municipalities could legally extinguish village lands and auction them to ranchers. The Land Law of 1850 and expansion of coffee, cattle, and rubber economies accelerated this process on all fronts simultaneously.

## Major Phases

**Phase 1 — Trade to conquest (mid-16th to early 17th century).** Initial Portuguese contact was based on barter. By the seventeenth century this "had been superseded by a colonization agenda" requiring direct labor control. Both the bandeirante raids in the south and the Amazon ransom troops (tropas de resgate) emerged in this phase.

**Phase 2 — High slavery and systematic depopulation (1620s–1750s).** Paulista slaving expeditions destroyed the Guairá Guaraní (1628–1632), introducing "certainly more than 50,000 Guaraní captives" to São Paulo between 1600 and 1640. The Amazon mission economy "dissolved ethnic diversity into the homogeneity of the 'Tapuya,' generic Indians." Manao resistance on the Rio Negro under chief Ajuricaba (1720s) — "the ad hoc confederation of Arawak-speaking peoples of the middle Rio Negro . . . against Portuguese expansion" — was crushed in 1727; Ajuricaba's suicide became a rallying point for generations of Rio Negro indigenous resistance.

**Phase 3 — Pombaline reform and directorate (1750s–1798).** Jesuit expulsion and mandatory assimilation dismantled mission segregation but intensified labor exploitation under lay directors. Peoples who had accepted reduction withdrew to reconstitute societies in new territories; others maintained "superficial acceptance of 'reduction' but actively maintained their posture of nonparticipation in the construction of the colony."

**Phase 4 — Imperial period: land, identity, rebellion (1822–1889).** In the northeast, the "War of the Barbarians" — the Confederation of the Kariri uprising (1687–c.1720) — was followed by a long period of settlement and identity negotiation. From the 1830s on, settled Indians redefined their ethnic identity collectively, using selectively revived cultural elements to affirm Indian-ness and defend land rights against forced assimilation claims. The Kaingang of southern Brazil resisted from the dense forests, conducting raids against the cattle frontier; Brazilian authorities declared war on them in 1808, legalizing enslavement of captives for up to 15 years. Professional "bugreiros" (Indian bounty hunters) massacred Kaingang and Xokleng throughout the mid-to-late nineteenth century; the leader Joaquim Bueno reportedly poisoned the drinking water of over 2,000 Kaingang in western São Paulo.

**Phase 5 — Republican conquest and the emergence of protection (1889–1910).** Rubber extraction, coffee expansion, railroad construction, and telegraph lines reached the most remote indigenous territories. The atrocities against Kaingang and Xokleng became an international cause célèbre, with sharp accusations at the 1908 International Americanists' Congress in Vienna. The government created the SPILTN (Indian Protection Service) in 1910 under General Cândido Rondon, a Positivist who advocated peaceful contact but whose program of "attracting to pacify" differed little in structure from eighteenth-century settlement policies.

## Geographic Spread

- **South (São Paulo, Paraná, Santa Catarina, Rio Grande do Sul):** Guaraní, Kaingang, Xokleng, Charrúa — earliest and most intense slavers; scene of the Guaraní War (1750–56) and nineteenth-century extermination campaigns.
- **Northeast (Bahia, Pernambuco, Ceará, Piauí, Rio Grande do Norte):** Kariri confederation, Potiguar, Botocudo, Pataxó — cattle ranching frontier, Dutch rivalries, the Guerra dos Bárbaros (1687–c.1720); nineteenth-century land expropriation of settled villages.
- **Central coast (Espírito Santo, Minas Gerais):** Botocudo — resisted colonization until early nineteenth century; targeted by royal war declarations (1801, 1808) and by bugreiros through the cacao boom.
- **Amazon Valley:** Omagua, Yoriman, Manao, Mura, Munduruku — slavers reached the Tapajós in 1626, the Negro in 1656, upper Solimões in 1690; "the Amazon várzea was practically depopulated" by end of seventeenth century.

## End Conditions

The formal end of [[brazilian-indian-slavery]] (1833) and the Pombaline Directorate (1798) removed the legal frameworks but not the practice. The process did not end in 1890 but transformed: "the dynamics of change in indigenous societies were based on an entirely different set of factors" in the early twentieth century — no longer primarily slaving and resettlement but territorial occupation, rubber extraction, railway construction, and explicit extermination. The Indian Protection Service (1910) marked a new institutional phase, not a resolution. Many southern indigenous groups — Kaingang, Xokleng, Guaraní Mbya — survived on reservations or in small toldos (independent villages with insecure land tenure) into the twentieth century and beyond, maintaining ethnic identity under continued pressure.

### Regional Case Summaries

**Guaraní (South):** Over 50,000 captives taken to São Paulo 1600–1640; Guaraní became the dominant "Carijó" ethnic category of Paulista slave society. Post-1640, Paulistas turned to Kaingang and other groups. Guaraní War 1750–56. By 1821 only 3,000 of 30,000 remained in the Seven Missions. Independent Guaraní groups (Kaiowá, Nandeva, Mbya, Apapokuva) persisted in remote areas; prophetic movements drove long-distance migrations to the Atlantic coast in the nineteenth century.

**Guaycurú / Kadiwéu:** Resisted conquest until the second half of the eighteenth century through combined Mbaya-Payaguá land-and-river alliance. The Kadiwéu (Mbaya subgroup) received official land-rights guarantees after service in the War of the Triple Alliance, but were subsequently confined as ranchers occupied their territory. See [[chaco-frontier-1573-1882]].

**Kaingang and Xokleng (South):** Kaingang resisted slavery, escaped Portuguese control through mobility, and mounted persistent frontier raids. Royal War of 1808 legalized enslavement of captives. Nineteenth-century professional bugreiros massacred populations; Vitorino Conda's career illustrates the complexity of indigenous leadership under colonial pressure — mediating between groups, soliciting imperial protection, retreating when dispossessed. Final Kaingang "pacification" in São Paulo came in 1912; over half died of measles in the aftermath.

**Kariri Confederation (Northeast):** Guerra dos Bárbaros (1687–c.1720), a pan-Indian uprising against Portuguese cattle-ranching expansion following Dutch expulsion, united Jandui, Paiaku, Kraticé, Iká, Xukuru, and others across Ceará, Rio Grande, Paraíba, Pernambuco, and Piauí. A 1692 peace treaty promised the Jandui 10 square leagues and freedom for 5,000 warriors' service; noncompliance prolonged the conflict. After defeat, settlement Indians forged a new collective identity to resist forced extinction and defend land titles — a historically significant example of ethnic reinvention as political strategy.

**Botocudo (Central Coast):** Near-total economic collapse of Ilhéus, Porto Seguro, and Espírito Santo captaincies through Botocudo resistance; crown turned the region into a buffer zone. Nineteenth-century war declarations, "Indian hunters," child trafficking (kurukas sold to ranchers and even to Dom Pedro II), and railway construction destroyed most bands. Final survivors confined to settlements on Doce, Mucuri, and Suaçui rivers by the early twentieth century.

**Amazon Valley (Omagua, Manao, Mura):** The várzea was depopulated within a century of sustained contact. The Manao confederation under Ajuricaba (1720s) was the most significant organized resistance; its defeat prompted genocidal counter-campaigns. The Mura, who occupied spaces vacated by destroyed peoples, were declared enemies requiring extermination. Tupinambá of Tupinambarana Island were refugees from the northeastern coast who had migrated across Amazonia from the second quarter of the sixteenth century — "children and grandchildren of the first settlers" who "had dominated the inhabitants of the region and with time married and established kinship amongst themselves."
