# The Bhakti Movement

The **Bhakti movement** was the great current of **popular, personal devotion** (*bhakti*) in Indian
religion — emphasising a direct, loving relationship between the devotee and God over priestly ritual,
caste, and image-worship. Documented for the wiki through Romila Thapar's
[[thapar-history-of-india-v1-1966|*A History of India*, Vol. I]] (1966), which traces it from its
Tamil roots to the Sultanate-era figures of Kabir and Nanak.

## Roots: the Tamil devotional cult

Bhakti's deep origin lies in the **Tamil devotional cult** of the
[[pallava-chalukya-rashtrakuta|Pallava-era]] south (from c. 6th c.) — the Shaiva and Vaishnava
hymnist-"saints" whose ecstatic vernacular devotion spread northward and reshaped pan-Indian
[[hinduism|Hinduism]], later given philosophical form by **Ramanuja's** theistic Vaishnavism (against
Shankara's austere Advaita). Bhakti made *prasada* (God's grace) and personal love, not sacrifice,
the heart of religion.

## The coalescence with Sufism

Under the [[delhi-sultanate|Delhi Sultanate]] the movement was transformed by contact with **Sufism**.
The **Sufis** — organised in orders (the **Chishti** most prominent in India), revering the **pir**
(master), stressing love and union with God, and dissociating themselves from the orthodox **ulema**
— moved among the **artisans and cultivators** and carried an Islamic stress on **social equality**.
Sufi and Bhakti thought "coalesced at various points": the common belief in union with God through
love, and the need for a *guru/pir*. From this fusion came a Bhakti that **attacked institutionalised
religion and objects of worship, disregarded caste, welcomed women, and taught entirely in the
vernacular** — drawing its adherents above all from the **lower castes and the urban artisan class**.

## Kabir and Nanak

Thapar singles out **Kabir** and **Nanak** as the movement's most historically significant figures,
precisely because they drew on **both Hindu and Islamic traditions** and gave voice to the **urban
artisan** world of the Sultanate towns:
- **Kabir** (1440–1518), a weaver and disciple of the Vaishnava reformer Ramananda, "wished to change
  society," not merely reform worship. In simple, memorable couplets he **denied or equated** the
  Hindu and Muslim conceptions of God — "I am neither in temple nor in mosque… Hari is in the East,
  Allah is in the West; look within your heart" — rejecting both ritual systems.
- **Nanak** (1469–1530), of a rural Punjabi family, went further, describing **God without reference
  to either Hindu or Muslim conceptions** (the formless **True One** of the *Adi Granth*). His
  teaching and community became the foundation of **Sikhism**.

## The Mughal-era flowering and the vernaculars (Spear)

[[spear-history-of-india-v2-1965|Spear, Vol. II]] traces the movement's continued vitality under the
[[mughal-empire|Mughals]] and its **decisive role in shaping the modern Indian languages**. The bhakti
"religion of the heart" spread from western India (the Maratha *sant* poets) into the Punjab, where
**Guru Nanak** founded what became the **Sikhs**; **Chaitanya** (16th c.) carried Krishna-devotion
through Bengal and restored Krishna's holy city of Brindaban; and **Tulsidas** of Benares (1534–1623)
wrote his Hindi *Ramayana* (the *Ramcharitmanas*), "almost the Bible of the north Indian people,"
laying the foundation of modern **Hindi** — as the movement did for **Marathi** and **Bengali**. By
using the vernacular and loosening (even as they sometimes recrystallised) caste bonds, the bhakti
cults gave India's regional languages their literary "personality."

The Sikh case shows bhakti's political edge under Mughal pressure: Nanak's syncretist, peaceful,
monotheist sect was **militarised** after [[aurangzeb|Aurangzeb]] executed **Guru Tegh Bahadur (1675)**;
**Guru Gobind Singh** completed its transformation into a distinct community (the *Khalsa*, the *Granth
Sahib* as scripture, holy warriors and martyrdom) that would "alternately disrupt and rule the Punjab"
for three centuries.

## Significance

The Bhakti movement was the chief popular response to the **"assimilation on trial"** of the
Hindu–Muslim encounter — a partial religious rapprochement from below, an enduring **anti-caste,
vernacular, devotional** tradition, and (in Nanak) the seed of a new religion. Thapar reads it
**sociologically**: its anti-caste, anti-ritual, egalitarian character "expressed the sentiments" of
the lower castes and the rising urban artisan class of the Sultanate, who found the brahmanical and
ulema establishments alike unresponsive to their position.

## Avari's Treatment (Chapters 9 and 11): Tamil Origins and the Decline of Buddhism

Avari's *India: The Ancient Past* (2007) provides rich detail on the bhakti movement's Tamil origins and its relationship to the disappearance of Buddhism from India — the two aspects least covered in the Thapar and Spear accounts above.

**Tamil origins — Nayanars and Alvars**: Avari traces the bhakti movement explicitly to the Pallava era (c. 6th–8th centuries). "The 'simple-hearted' devotees, or *bhaktas*, sometimes known as saints, were the **Shaiva Nayanars** or **Vaishnava Alvars**. Their songs, poems and hymns, all glorifying either Vishnu or Shiva, were utilised principally in the process of weaning the south Indian masses away from the heterodoxies." These heterodoxies were Buddhism and Jainism. The bhakti hymns and devotional songs of the Nayanars and Alvars were "collected into a volume which came to constitute a major branch of Tamil literature." An example is preserved in the poem of Appar, a seventh-century Tamil poet-saint: "We are not subject to any; we are not afraid of death; we will not suffer in hell; we live in no illusion... there is no sorrow, for we have become servants, once for all, of the independent Lord."

**Social dimensions — reaction against power structures**: Avari frames bhakti not only as religious devotionalism but as a **social-political reaction**: "The Bhakti Movement can also be seen, in that sense, as a reaction against the power structures and elites within the south Indian kingdoms, such as those of the Pallavas." This anticipates the later cross-caste, anti-ritual character of the northern bhakti movement.

**The temple as bhakti infrastructure**: Under the [[chola-empire|Cholas]], the temple institutionalised bhakti. "Chola centralism also effectively institutionalised the various religious cults associated with the popular bhakti movement in south India, and **temple patronage** was a key element in this aspect of the imperium. The temple proclaimed to the world the Chola state's ideology." The temple was not merely a spiritual institution but the mechanism through which the Chola state absorbed popular devotionalism and converted it into a source of royal legitimacy.

**Bhakti and the absorption of Buddhism**: Avari's most historically significant contribution on the bhakti movement is his account of its role in the disappearance of Buddhism from India (Chapter 11). He offers four reasons for Buddhism's decline; the bhakti dimension is central to two of them:

1. The Buddhist laity's loss of passion: "It was not easy advice to follow [the Buddha's individual path]; but its simplicity had made Buddhism popular both among the poor and the socially and commercially mobile people during the earlier centuries. The laity, however, could hardly be expected to retain that sense of loyalty to the Buddha's pristine message in an age when the **ideologically revived Brahmanism**, with its myriad rituals and caste etiquettes, could more easily draw the Indian masses into its embrace."

2. "Tolerant broad church that was Hinduism offered a real home for the assimilation of Buddhists... the Buddha himself was incorporated into the Hindu pantheon of gods." The bhakti movement's capacity to absorb and adapt, rather than reject, meant Buddhism's distinctive claims became progressively indistinct.

The bhakti movement, in Avari's view, represents **Hinduism's most powerful instrument** for competitive religious absorption — capable of appealing across caste lines, offering ecstatic personal devotion comparable to Buddhist or Jain spiritual experience, while remaining within the Hindu social frame. Buddhism offered no comparable rival devotionalism in the critical period of the 9th–12th centuries. "Buddhism had been dying in India long before the advent of the Turks."

**Three-strand model of Hinduism**: Avari places bhakti within a three-strand model of Hinduism as it existed by the 11th–12th centuries: (1) Vedic Brahmanism (oldest); (2) "the bhakti movement, springing from the **Puranic religion** formed around the Vaishnava and Shaivite traditions, which demanded from their followers intense devotion to personal gods such as Vishnu or Shiva... swept through India like a whirlwind in the later centuries of the first millennium ad"; and (3) local cults (goddess worship, animism, tribal practices) absorbed into the Puranic frame. Philosophers Shankaracharya (788–822), Ramanuja (1017–1137), and Madhava (1197–1276) "attempted to bridge the differences between the two strands [Vedic and Puranic/bhakti] and not precipitate a destructive separation between them."
