# Turco-Afghan Incursions into North India (c. 1000–1206 CE)

The **Turco-Afghan incursions** refer to the wave of armed intrusions into the Indian subcontinent launched from Ghazni and later Ghur between c. 1000 and 1206 CE, beginning with **Mahmud of Ghazni's** seventeen lightning raids and culminating in **Muhammad Ghuri's** conquest of north India and the establishment of the **[[delhi-sultanate|Delhi Sultanate]]** in 1206. Avari describes the period as "a massive ransacking of north Indian wealth by Turco-Afghan warriors and marauders." Documented through Burjor Avari, *India: The Ancient Past* (Routledge, 2007), Chapters 11.

## Background: The Turkic Slave-Soldier System

The Central Asian origins of the Turco-Afghan warriors lay in "nomadic Turkic-speaking tribes of Central Asia, which had, throughout their history, faced constant harrying over land and land rights from their various neighbours and enemy confederations." After these territories came under Abbasid control from the mid-eighth century, the Abbasid rulers and their client governors "saw in them a ready supply of 'slave' soldiers for their armies. Bought as children, they were given military training" in archery and horsemanship. "Although remaining slaves, they could rapidly rise within the ranks of the army, suffering little in the way of racial discrimination." The slave-soldier system created a military class with unusual social mobility but remained potentially destabilising — "there was the danger that the various groups of these soldiers would complot in rebellions against the Abbasid caliphs."

## The Ghaznavid Dynasty and Mahmud's Raids

"A hundred miles southwest of Kabul there lie today the ruins of **Ghazni**, which, in the tenth century, had been a part of the Samanid Empire of Iran." In 933 a Turkish slave named Alptigin seized the territory. Another risen slave, **Sabuktigin**, married Alptigin's daughter and inherited Ghazni on his death in 977, "while continuing to offer nominal allegiance to the Samanids. Thus was established the **Ghaznavid dynasty**, which lasted from 977 to 1186." Sabuktigin's key achievement was to "weaken the Indian kingdom of the **Hindu Shahis**," the Buddhist/Hindu rulers of the Punjab frontier area — "the first of the Indian rulers to be destroyed by the Gaznavid conquerors."

**Mahmud of Ghazni** (r. 997–1030) pressed the advantage further, first destroying the Hindu Shahis: their ruler Jayapala "preferred suicide by self-immolation rather than humiliating subjection by paying a ransom to Mahmud." "For the next quarter century [Mahmud] launched **seventeen separate raids** deep into northwestern and northern India." His strategy was deliberate: "simply to carry out **lightning raids** on cities, temples and monasteries rather than engage the Indian forces in set-piece battles." The "bulky Indian armies, more accustomed to conventional static warfare, were no match for Mahmud's mobile horsemen recruited from among the varied Turkic and Afghan ethnic groups." He took Kanauj in 1019 but "avoided the temptation of permanent conquests."

Mahmud's **primary motive** was fiscal: "The transfer of Indian wealth to the treasury of Ghazni was Mahmud's key motive, because only with money could he continue to secure the support for the Ghaznavids among the chiefs and warlords." "Much of the bullion was minted down for the purpose of financing more trade." Avari contextualises his Islamic iconoclasm — the destruction of the Somanatha Temple on the Gujarat coast being the most famous act — as a mix of genuine zealotry and desire to please the Islamic clerical establishment, but cautions against the Hindu nationalist narrative that makes Mahmud the origin of all Indian misfortunes: "Indian kings themselves drained away much of their wealth and resources in many a pointless struggle against one another; that they too destroyed temples and monasteries during their own bouts of sectarian hatreds."

The scholar **Al-Biruni**, who accompanied Mahmud and spent ten years in India, was bold enough to write "that Mahmud's raids were nothing short of barbaric." Al-Biruni's *Kitab fi Tahqiqi ma li-l Hind* provides the single most penetrating contemporary account of India at the moment of these incursions.

## The Ghurid Conquest

"The Ghaznavid raids did not cease after Mahmud's death in 1030. The economy of Ghazni depended upon the continuous supply of funds from India." In the mid-twelfth century, the province of **Ghur** (between Ghazni and Herat) began to displace Ghaznavid power. The Ghur territory "was ruled by a local Iranian/Tajik family of Shansabanis." By 1186 Ghur controlled Ghazni entirely, with two brothers — Ghiyasuddin Muhammad and **Muzuddin Muhammad** (known in Indian sources as **Muhammad Ghuri**) — sharing power. Muhammad Ghuri's ambition went beyond raiding: "his ambition was not just to raid, but also to annex lands."

He introduced military innovations: "The cavalry, with their well-mounted archers, was widely introduced. The army recruited from a broader range of ethnic groups with military skills, such as the Damghanis of northern Iran, the Tajiks, and the Seljuq and Ghuzz Turks. Two separate units, the advance guards and the reserve forces, were formed in every Ghurid army." He also attached "officially recognised grain dealers and pastoralists, later known as *banjaras*, who, with their massive bullock carts, ensured steady supplies for his mobile forces."

## The Battles of Tarain and Their Outcome

Muhammad Ghuri launched "a massive attack, through the Punjab, into the territories of the Chahamana kingdom in 1191, but was repulsed with great losses by its ruler, **Prithviraja Chauhan**. It is remembered as the **first battle of Tarain**." The **second battle of Tarain** (1192) was decisive: Prithviraja "and his army were completely overwhelmed by the Ghurid forces. Prithviraja was taken prisoner and executed." (See [[battle-of-tarain-1192]] for the detailed analysis.)

After Tarain, "the Ganga valley and Madhyadesha fell into Turco-Afghan hands within a few years." Between 1197 and 1205 Bakhtiyar Khalji conquered Bihar and Bengal, sacking the great Buddhist monastic universities of Nalanda and Vikramashila. Muhammad Ghuri was assassinated in 1206, but "the Turco-Afghan hold over north India could not be shaken." That year his slave general **Qutb-ud-din Aybak** "became the first king of the slave dynasty and inaugurated the **Delhi Sultanate** (1206–1526)."

## Why India Was Vulnerable

Avari identifies several structural factors explaining north Indian vulnerability:

1. **Feudal disunity**: "Prithviraja was a traditional Rajput ruler, steeped in the heroic traditions of his people and dependent upon the **feudal levies**; but these were notoriously unreliable in comparison with the permanently committed centralised Turkish standing armies." The [[indian-feudalism|samanta system]] diffused military strength.

2. **Infantry weakness**: "The Indian forces had huge numbers of poorly armed and poorly positioned infantrymen who just could not withstand the cavalry charges of the Turco-Afghans."

3. **Rajput political rivalries**: Prithviraja asked other Rajput rulers for help, "but none of them felt particularly threatened from across the border at the time." At least one rival — Jayachandra of Kanauj — "is even supposed to have invited and encouraged Muhammad Ghuri."

4. **Complacency after Tarain I**: "If Prithviraja had followed up his first victory by pursuing Muhammad, the latter might have been sufficiently intimidated not to return; but Prithviraja's complacency and inaction led to his undoing at the second crucial battle."

## Consequences

The Turkish conquest inaugurated "nearly 600 years of Muslim political authority and influence over a large part of India." The destruction of the north Indian Buddhist monasteries effectively ended Buddhism as a living presence in India (see [[spread-of-buddhism]] and [[pala-empire]]). The great north Indian temples of Mathura, Kanauj, and Benares were destroyed. "After the consolidation of Turco-Afghan rule in 1206 there were hardly any great Hindu temples built in north India; most of the temple building was carried on in the south." The [[chola-empire|Chola south]] remained Hindu and outside Turkish control during this entire period, flourishing commercially while the north was plundered.

Avari notes, however, that Turco-Afghan settlement transformed over time: "militarily triumphant, the Turco-Afghans nevertheless had to make compromises with Indian realities, and over time they became integrated in Indian society. Through their Delhi Sultanate they were eventually to become the greatest bulwark in the defence of India against the Mongols." And as for religious conversion: "in those two centuries there was hardly any conversion by the Turco-Afghans" — conversion of the Indian population was a slower, later process.

## Historiography

The key contemporary source for the Ghaznavid incursions is **Al-Biruni's** *Kitab fi Tahqiqi ma li-l Hind* (completed c. 1030), an unusually dispassionate and intellectually serious account that Avari rates as superior to many nineteenth-century British commentaries for its impartiality. Arabic and Persian chronicles provide the Ghaznavid and Ghurid perspectives. Kalhana's *Rajatarangini* provides an Indian perspective on Kashmir at the same period. Modern historiography has focused on: the degree of deliberate religious iconoclasm vs. economic opportunism in Mahmud's raids; the question of how far the raids (rather than internal processes) caused Indian "decline"; and the nationalist-communalist uses of Mahmud's memory in modern India, which Avari explicitly critiques. The debate over Indian vulnerability — feudal structure, military technology, political disunity — parallels scholarship on the fall of other complex societies to mobile pastoral-military forces.
