# Islamic conquests (seventh–eighth centuries)

The rapid military and political expansion of the early Islamic community after the death of [[muhammad|Muhammad]] (632), which within roughly a century established rule from the Atlantic to the Indian Ocean and deep into Asia—setting the southern and much of the eastern boundary of medieval Christian Europe.

## Definition and Scope

Logan treats the conquests as unexpected and unmatched in speed: Christianity was still a minority centuries after its founder’s death, whereas Islam’s political reach exploded within decades. This page covers the **seventh–early eighth-century** expansion (Orthodox Caliphs through Umayyad Spain), not later medieval expansions ([[expansion-of-islam-middle-period]]).

> "Mohammed died in 632, and within 100 years his followers had conquered a wide swathe from the Atlantic to the Indian Oceans and far into the Asian interior."

## Causal Drivers

- **Religious-political mobilization** of the Arabian peninsula under the first caliphs after succession crisis.
- **Byzantine and Sasanian exhaustion** and, in Byzantine provinces, poor administration, heavy taxation, local loyalties, and theological differences (including [[monophysitism|Monophysite]] alienation) that drained allegiance to Constantinople.
- **Not primarily forced conversion:** jihad developed as struggle on God’s path, later nearly synonymous with holy war; consensus that jihad is by the *umma* not private individuals; forced conversion was not Islamic practice, though life for non-Muslims was not universally benign.

> "although life for non-Muslims in Muslim lands was not universally benign, it should be remembered that forced conversion was not the Islamic practice."

## Major Phases

1. **Abu-Bakr (632–34):** first of the four Orthodox Caliphs; recovered apostate tribes and controlled the peninsula.
2. **Umar (634–44) and successors (Uthman 644–56, Ali 656–61):** Fertile Crescent and Egypt—Damascus 635, Jerusalem 637; Persian Ctesiphon 637, Sasanian empire fallen 642; Egypt by 642; Cyprus 649.
3. **Umayyads (to 750):** capital at Damascus (Baghdad later, 762, under Abbasids). Maghreb with Berber support; **Spain 711–719**—Tariq Ibn Ziyad landed at Gibraltar spring 711; 19 July 711 defeated Roderick; half of Spain by end of summer; Seville and Saragossa 713; conquest complete 719 save Asturias and mountain Christian enclaves (al-Andalus; last Muslim rule in Iberia ended 1492 at Granada).
4. **North of the Pyrenees:** a small Muslim splinter force defeated by [[charles-martel|Charles Martel]] near Tours (732)—Logan: **a minor incident**, not the turning point of European history, because supply lines made major assault north of the Pyrenees impossible.

> "At a crucial battle on 19 July 711, Tariq and his Berber warriors defeated the Visigothic forces of King Roderick."

## Geographic Spread

Arabian peninsula → Syria/Palestine → Iraq/Iran → Egypt → North Africa → Iberia; further into Central Asia. Christian pockets and slow conversion over centuries under tributary (*dhimmi*-style) arrangements; Logan contrasts Arab tolerance of subject religions for tribute (often less than Byzantine taxes) with [[charlemagne|Charlemagne]]’s forced baptism-or-death policy toward Saxons.

## Interaction

- concurrent_with: end of Visigothic Catholic Spain; Justinianic/Byzantine overstretch aftermath; Lombard Italy’s separation of East and West.
- produced: southern boundary of medieval Christian Europe; conditions for Pirenne’s and Logan’s theses linking Muhammad to the medieval papacy and empire.
- enabled (Logan/Pirenne frame): a medieval Latin West whose Mediterranean south was no longer a Roman lake.

## End Conditions

The first great wave closed with Umayyad collapse (750) and the stabilization of frontiers (Pyrenees, Anatolia, Central Asia). Conquest as explosive expansion gave way to consolidation, conversion over generations, and later medieval expansions under other dynasties.

## From Tannous (2018) — world found and world made

Tannous ranks the mid-seventh-century Arab conquests with the rise of Christianity as the first millennium’s most consequential events, but shifts attention from campaign narrative to the **religious-social world conquered and created**: a Middle East of simple Christians already fractured by post-Chalcedonian competition; non-Muslim demographic majorities for centuries after conquest; early Muslim ranks filled in part by late mass converts with limited formation; and (Appendix II) Syriac *ṭayyāyē* for conquerors read as “Arab”/Arabic-speaker usage continuous with sixth-century sources, not merely “nomad.” Conquest enables but does not equal [[islamization-of-the-middle-east|Islamization]].

## Gibbon, *Decline and Fall* (1776–1788) — Chs. L–LII (historiography only)

[[sources/gibbon-decline-and-fall-1776|Gibbon]]’s conquest narrative is **18th-c. orientalizing** (fanaticism, desert freedom, “Saracen” valor). Attribute heavily; do not use as modern causal authority against Sarris, Harris, Tannous, or NCMH.

- **Ridda crisis:** “the death of Mahomet was the signal of independence; and the hasty structure of his power and religion tottered to its foundations” until Abubeker/Caled restored it.
- **Yermuk hinge:** Hardest, most doubtful day for Syrian veterans yet most decisive; “After the battle of Yermuk, the Roman army no longer appeared in the field” (see [[battle-of-yarmuk-636]]).
- **Opinion as power:** Heraclius’s Antioch confession — “The Saracens were invincible in fact, since they were invincible in opinion.”
- **Confessional enablers:** Copts/Jacobites alienated from Chalcedonian masters; later still “the Nestorians and Jacobites preferred a Saracen to an orthodox master.”
- **Dual exhaustion:** Sassanid Persia collapses into multi-year anarchy after Chosroes; Rome and Persia both wounded by their mutual war before Arab success; first-wave Arabs “surprised at the ease and rapidity of their own success.”
- **Alexandria library:** Gibbon doubts Omar’s burning as decisive cultural catastrophe — “our treasures, rather than our losses, are the objects of my surprise.”

## From Mitchell, Later Roman Empire (2015)

### Mutual exhaustion and the hinge 636–641

Mitchell’s synthesis: the last great ancient conflict between Rome and Persia “brought both contestants to the verge of collapse,” coinciding with Islam’s rise — so Arab success is inseparable from prior bipolar total war (602–30), demographic/fiscal plague damage, Balkan hemorrhage, and ideological militarization that still could not match *umma*/jihad fusion. Eastern empire survives as Anatolian–Constantinopolitan rump; Sassanians extinguish. In **636**, forces of Islam under Omar (second caliph in Mitchell’s framing) won [[battle-of-yarmuk-636|Yarmuk]] for Syria; two years later Qadisiya overwhelmed a Sassanian army — “decisively establishing a new era in the history of the Near East.”

### Demographic precursor: Arabization of the Levant

Arabs long military factors via Lakhmids (Sassanian) and Ghassanids (Roman) between Syria and Euphrates; built settlements on desert margins; flourishing frontier cities attracted Arab settlers — steady **demographic Arabization of the Levant as vital precursor** to Muhammad’s followers’ military onslaught (parallel to Slavic Balkan infiltration). `enabled:` conquest success; `concurrent_with:` Ghassanid/Lakhmid frontier system.

### Why Arabs won (Mitchell’s two factors)

Narrative of early conquests is hard (Islamic tradition ~150 years after *hijra*; non-Islamic sources crucial — West Syrian chronicles, Sebeos). Two factors above all for Arab superiority in the Levant:

1. **Military organization** and aggressive capacity comparable to Huns/Avars/Turks — clans fused into large warrior nations.
2. Unlike steppe charismatic personal leadership, **unity by a compelling religious idea**: Islamic state on *umma* (community of believers) transformed tribal society; only intermediate authority the Prophet then caliphs; unconditional monotheism; leaving = apostasy. Complete fusion of political and religious loyalty independent of Constantinopolitan Christianity or Sassanian Mazdaism. Christian holy war of Heraclius “outmatched by a jihad.” Neither Rome nor Persia had military capacity to resist after mutual exhaustion.

### Earliest Greek notice: *Doctrina Iacobi* (~634)

Possibly earliest Greek mention of Arab invasions of southern Syria: *Doctrina Iacobi* (Teachings of Jacob), perhaps 634, Palestine setting — also prime evidence for Heraclius’ militant Christian politics (forced Jewish conversion at Carthage, Pentecost 632). Dialogue quotes letter of Abraham: *candidatus* killed by Saracens at Caesarea; Jews overjoyed; rumor of prophet with Saracens proclaiming coming anointed one; old man: false prophet (prophets not sword-armed); Abraham hears no truth in so-called prophet, only bloodshed; claim to keys of paradise “incredible.” Useful as source-critical window on early non-Muslim perception of Muhammad and the first wave.
