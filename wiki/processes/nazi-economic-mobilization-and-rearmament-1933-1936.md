# Nazi Economic Mobilization and Rearmament (1933–1936)

## Definition and Scope

The process by which the Nazi regime, from the moment of taking power, directed the German economy toward rapid, large-scale rearmament and military-economic preparation for expansion, while maintaining public propaganda of recovery and peace. Within six years Hitler boasted of 65 billion Reichsmarks spent on rearmament. The 'civilian recovery' phase (1933–36) was a myth; military spending was the priority from the outset. The process was characterized by Hitler's anti-bureaucratic style, competing agencies, and 'working towards the Führer' pressures rather than unified central planning.

## Causal Drivers

- Hitler's fixed worldview and conviction that Germany must prepare for inevitable struggle; rearmament as the core of national revival and power restoration.
- Shared revisionist aims among military, Foreign Office, and economic élites (full equality in armaments, removal of restrictions including Rhineland demilitarization).
- Structural incentives: 'working towards the Führer' rewarded those who advanced presumed goals of strength and rearmament without waiting for detailed orders.
- Economic context: Depression unemployment provided labour reservoir; initial Schacht mechanisms (MEFO bills, etc.) enabled deficit financing of arms.
- Hitler's personal interventions: overriding objections (e.g. to Darré for seed-oil imports despite Schacht protests); setting scales (36 divisions immediate in March 1935).

## Major Phases (to 1936)

- **1933–early 1934 (secret buildup under caution):** Public conciliatory stance while rearming in secret. Foreign Office memoranda (Bülow March 1933) stressed avoiding conflict 'until we are strong again.'
- **1934 (post-consolidation acceleration):** SA purge removed obstacle to rearmament plans. Army planning for 21–36 division force.
- **1935 (open breach):** Conscription announcement (16 March) — 36 divisions immediately. Göring air-force announcement (10 March). Saar plebiscite (Jan) provided propaganda windfall and indirect boost.
- **1935–1936 (strains and Four Year Plan):** Severe food crisis and raw-material shortages (by early 1936 only 1–2 months' supplies left for rearmament). Schacht demanded slowdown; Hitler and Darré pushed imports. Hitler intervened personally for scarce foreign currency. Corruption and competing claims (Ley Labour Front vs. employers; party Gauleiter on wages). Creation of Four Year Plan (1936) as plenipotentiary institution directly under Hitler to manage raw materials and rearmament drive, bypassing normal ministries.

## Geographic and Institutional Spread

Nationwide. Overlapping bureaucracies: Reichswehr/Wehrmacht planning, Schacht's Economics Ministry and Reichsbank, Labour Front (Ley), SS-police economic interests, new special agencies (Four Year Plan under Göring from 1936). Regional Gauleiter intervened on local impacts (e.g. wage structures). International ripple: provoked British/French responses that in turn provided pretexts for German moves.

## Interaction with Other Processes

- Interacted with 'working towards the Führer': subordinates competed to deliver rearmament results; radical initiatives in resource allocation advanced without central coordination.
- Fueled and was fueled by foreign-policy coups (conscription, Rhineland): successes loosened constraints and increased domestic support for further mobilization.
- Clashed with food/agricultural priorities (Darré) and created social tensions that Hitler sought to offset with propaganda triumphs.
- Undermined coherent government: cabinet meetings declined; legislation by circulation; special agencies proliferated.

## End Conditions / Transformation

By 1936 the process had produced visible rearmament gains and popular identification with the regime but also acute bottlenecks. The Four Year Plan institutionalized the drive under direct Führer authority. The dynamic of rapid, uncoordinated expansion continued and intensified, contributing directly to the decision for war in 1939 when resource asymmetries became unsustainable for a long conflict. No 'normalization' occurred; the personalized, competitive style ensured continuing radicalization of economic policy in service of expansion.

## Historiography

Kershaw integrates economic pressures into the broader analysis of Hitler's personalized rule and the 'working towards' mechanism. Stresses that rearmament was never decoupled from strategy in Hitler's mind (contra some structural accounts); timing of open steps was his. Notes the role of food crisis and raw-material shortages in 1935–36 as both brake and spur to foreign-policy risk (Rhineland partly to offset domestic discontent). Cross-reference to detailed economic studies (Tooze) for technical quantification; here the focus is political/structural interaction with charismatic authority and radicalization. Primary evidence from army memoranda (Beck), cabinet exchanges, Goebbels diary, Rosenberg, Hassell reports, Schacht interventions.

**Collection coverage note:** Grounded in Kershaw *Hubris* range material on 1933–1936 interactions of rearmament, food crisis, Four Year Plan origins, and Hitler's interventions. Strong on political drivers and consequences for power consolidation.

## From Evans, *The Third Reich in Power* (2005), ch. 4

The economic mechanics in quantity (ingested 2026-07-02):

- **Disguised rearmament from day one.** Hitler, 8 February 1933: every job
  scheme judged "by the criterion of whether it is necessary from the point
  of view of the rearmament of the German people"; 9 February on the
  Schleicher works program: "particular stress must be laid on this
  concealment." Krupp's "agricultural tractors" (tanks) from July 1933; a
  1934 air plan for 17,000 aircraft by 1939 with bombers listed as trainers.
  Industrial investment: 17% of the 1928 level (1932) → 63% (1935);
  rearmament 1.5% of national income (1933) → 21% (1938); army spending
  478m RM (1933) → 9,137m (1938).
- **Financing:** [[schacht-hjalmar|Schacht]]'s Mefo bills covered ~50% of
  arms purchases 1934–36 (~12bn RM by 1938); the "New Plan" barter bloc tied
  South-eastern Europe to Germany. The Reichsbank board's 7 January 1939
  warning of "the edge of ruin" got the whole board sacked; the 15 June 1939
  law removed all limits on money-printing.
- **The propaganda economy deflated:** the "battle for work" rested partly on
  statistical manipulation (real unemployment "much nearer four" million than
  the official ~2m in 1935; ~3m "invisible" unemployed, mostly women, in
  1933); marriage loans removed women from the registers; the Weimar
  Republic's 1929 housing output (317,682 dwellings) was never surpassed;
  motorway Germany remained one of Europe's least motorized societies (1.6%
  vehicle ownership vs. 4.9% France) and the KdF-car scheme delivered 110m RM
  of workers' savings and not one car. The demographic trough of the missing
  WWI birth cohorts eased the labour market invisibly.
- **Agriculture and consumption:** the Reich Food Estate never approached
  autarky (fats ~50% short); rationing of butter and fats from January 1937,
  scrap-metal drives tearing up railings and grave fences from 1937 — a
  peacetime economy already at war-economy strains, which Evans (with the
  1936–39 labour shortages, >1m conscripted workers, and Gestapo on the
  factory floor) reads as the material logic pushing toward *Blitzkrieg*
  conquest ([[four-year-plan-1936]]).

## Tooze via Evans (2015)

Evans's review of Adam Tooze, *The Wages of Destruction* (2006), in
[[evans-third-reich-history-memory-2015]] (ch. 11) — Evans ranks it "the
leading account of the economic history of Nazi Germany in any language":

- **Rearmament, not job creation, drove recovery** — Tooze's data "finally
  puts these arguments beyond dispute" (Evans notes the recovery itself
  began in late summer 1932, before Hitler; the Autobahnen served military
  logistics and created few jobs; unemployment only cleared with mass
  conscription from 1935). Rearmament absorbed over a fifth of state
  spending by 1939; consumers paid in cotton shortages, ersatz fibers, and
  mid-1930s butter rationing (food rationing of fats began January 1937,
  pre-war).
- **Foreign-exchange crises (1934, 1939)** repeatedly forced arms
  cutbacks; Tooze traces them to Hitler's refusal to devalue the
  Reichsmark. The currency shortage drove the ban on emigrating Jews
  taking assets — suppressing emigration until [[kristallnacht-1938]].
- **Against Aly**: Germans were Europe's most heavily taxed population;
  Tooze "completely explodes" the claim the regime cushioned civilians,
  and puts the wartime occupied-territory revenue share at ~25%, not 70%.
- **Against Mason**: the 1939 armaments crisis was real but not a general
  economic crisis with worker unrest; still severe enough that Hitler told
  Mussolini (March 1940) he had had to strike "even at the risk of thereby
  precipitating the war" 2–3 years early.
- **Blitzkrieg as improvisation**, not economic grand strategy; the
  Barbarossa gamble rested on the racist assumption of Soviet collapse.
  Exploitation of France (nearly half of German artillery by March 1944
  was non-German) could not close the gap: Soviet production ran 4:1 in
  tanks and 3:1 in guns by 1942.
- **Speer demolished**: Tooze "comprehensively demolishes the myth so
  carefully crafted by [[speer-albert|Albert Speer]]" — fixed statistics,
  credit for pre-tenure improvements (Milch, Kehrl), and 1943–44 output
  gains riding on lethal forced labor.
- Evans's caveats (attributed): Tooze overstresses "medium-sized European
  power"; risks making extermination "too instrumental" (Western European
  Jews were already being deported before the spring 1942 food crisis);
  and underplays the ideological bar on mobilizing women.

## Shirer (1960) Additions

- Army ordered trebled from 100,000 to 300,000 by 1 October 1934; General Ludwig Beck (Chief of General Staff) was told in April 1934 that conscription would be openly decreed by 1 April 1935 — dates the pre-1935 secret buildup phase more precisely than the page's existing narrative.
- Documentary — General Keitel, chairman of the Working Committee of the Reich Defense Council, admonished aides as early as 22 May 1933: "No document must be lost, since otherwise enemy propaganda will make use of it. Matters communicated orally cannot be proven; they can be denied."
- Documentary — Raeder's June 1934 notes on Hitler's instructions for naval rearmament: "No mention must be made of a displacement of 25–26,000 tons, but only of improved 10,000-ton ships... The Fuehrer demands complete secrecy on the construction of the U-boats" (referring to the Scharnhorst and Gneisenau, built 16,000 tons above the Versailles limit; submarines had been secretly built in Finland, Holland, and Spain during the Weimar years, with frames stored at Kiel by Raeder).
- Hard data — Krupp boasted in 1942 that "the basic principle of armament and turret design for tanks had already been worked out in 1926... Of the guns being used in 1939–41, the most important ones were already fully complete in 1933," despite the Allied post-1919 ban on Krupp armaments production. I.G. Farben was ordered to raise synthetic oil production to 300,000 tons/year by 1937; by early 1934 the Reich Defense Council's Working Committee had approved mobilization plans for roughly 240,000 plants for war orders.
- 16 March 1935 (a Saturday — Wilhelmstrasse officials joked that "most of Hitler's surprises were reserved for Saturdays," since British officials took weekends off), Hitler decreed universal military service and a peacetime army of 12 corps/36 divisions (~500,000 men), unilaterally ending the Versailles military restrictions; Britain and France protested but took no action.
- Manstein's Nuremberg testimony: the General Staff, if consulted, "would have proposed twenty-one divisions... The figure of thirty-six divisions was due to a spontaneous decision of Hitler" — generals including Manstein and von Witzleben learned of the conscription decree over the radio, not having been consulted.
- Economic recovery data for 1932–37, consistent with Evans/Tooze above but with slightly different framing: unemployment fell from 6 million (1932) to under 1 million (spring 1937); national production rose 102% and national income doubled over the same period. General Georg Thomas (Military Economic Staff chief), quoted: "History will know only a few examples of cases where a country has directed, even in peacetime, all its economic forces deliberately and systematically toward the requirements of war" — the term he used was "Wehrwirtschaft" (war economy).
- Wage/labor data: skilled-worker wages fell from 20.4¢/hr (1932) to 19.5¢/hr (mid-1936); unskilled fell from 16.1¢ to 13¢/hr. Labor's share of national income fell from 56.9% (1932) to 53.6% (1938) while capital/business income share rose from 17.4% to 26.6%.
- Labor conscription mechanism: the "workbook" (February 1935), required for employment, could be withheld by an employer to prevent a worker leaving; a 22 June 1938 decree instituted full labor conscription, with fines or imprisonment for unauthorized absence. The Hereditary Farm Law (29 September 1933) similarly bound farmers (up to 308 acres) to inherited, non-mortgageable, non-divisible land, restricted to "Aryan" ownership provable to 1800.
- Volkswagen "swindle": Hitler-conceived "People's Car" at 990 marks ($396), engineered by Ferdinand Porsche; the Labor Front (Ley) ran a "pay-before-you-get-it" installment scheme (5–15 marks/week; 750 marks bought an order number) — not a single car was ever delivered to a paying customer before the war, and the factory converted to military production. This corroborates and dates precisely the "KdF-car scheme delivered 110m RM of workers' savings and not one car" line already on this page from Evans.
