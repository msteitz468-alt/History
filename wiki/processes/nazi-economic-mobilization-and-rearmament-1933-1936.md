# Nazi Economic Mobilization and Rearmament (1933–1936)

## Definition and Scope

The process by which the Nazi regime, from the moment of taking power, directed the German economy toward rapid, large-scale rearmament and military-economic preparation for expansion, while maintaining public propaganda of recovery and peace. Within six years Hitler boasted of 65 billion Reichsmarks spent on rearmament. The 'civilian recovery' phase (1933–36) was a myth; military spending was the priority from the outset. The process was characterized by Hitler's anti-bureaucratic style, competing agencies, and 'working towards the Führer' pressures rather than unified central planning.

## Causal Drivers

- Hitler's fixed worldview and conviction that Germany must prepare for inevitable struggle; rearmament as the core of national revival and power restoration.
- Shared revisionist aims among military, Foreign Office, and economic élites (full equality in armaments, removal of restrictions including Rhineland demilitarization).
- Structural incentives: 'working towards the Führer' rewarded those who advanced presumed goals of strength and rearmament without waiting for detailed orders.
- Economic context: Depression unemployment provided labour reservoir; initial Schacht mechanisms (MEFO bills, etc.) enabled deficit financing of arms.
- Hitler's personal interventions: overriding objections (e.g. to Darré for seed-oil imports despite Schacht protests); setting scales (36 divisions immediate in March 1935).

## Major Phases (to 1936)

- **1933–early 1934 (secret buildup under caution):** Public conciliatory stance while rearming in secret. Foreign Office memoranda (Bülow March 1933) stressed avoiding conflict 'until we are strong again.'
- **1934 (post-consolidation acceleration):** SA purge removed obstacle to rearmament plans. Army planning for 21–36 division force.
- **1935 (open breach):** Conscription announcement (16 March) — 36 divisions immediately. Göring air-force announcement (10 March). Saar plebiscite (Jan) provided propaganda windfall and indirect boost.
- **1935–1936 (strains and Four Year Plan):** Severe food crisis and raw-material shortages (by early 1936 only 1–2 months' supplies left for rearmament). Schacht demanded slowdown; Hitler and Darré pushed imports. Hitler intervened personally for scarce foreign currency. Corruption and competing claims (Ley Labour Front vs. employers; party Gauleiter on wages). Creation of Four Year Plan (1936) as plenipotentiary institution directly under Hitler to manage raw materials and rearmament drive, bypassing normal ministries.

## Geographic and Institutional Spread

Nationwide. Overlapping bureaucracies: Reichswehr/Wehrmacht planning, Schacht's Economics Ministry and Reichsbank, Labour Front (Ley), SS-police economic interests, new special agencies (Four Year Plan under Göring from 1936). Regional Gauleiter intervened on local impacts (e.g. wage structures). International ripple: provoked British/French responses that in turn provided pretexts for German moves.

## Interaction with Other Processes

- Interacted with 'working towards the Führer': subordinates competed to deliver rearmament results; radical initiatives in resource allocation advanced without central coordination.
- Fueled and was fueled by foreign-policy coups (conscription, Rhineland): successes loosened constraints and increased domestic support for further mobilization.
- Clashed with food/agricultural priorities (Darré) and created social tensions that Hitler sought to offset with propaganda triumphs.
- Undermined coherent government: cabinet meetings declined; legislation by circulation; special agencies proliferated.

## End Conditions / Transformation

By 1936 the process had produced visible rearmament gains and popular identification with the regime but also acute bottlenecks. The Four Year Plan institutionalized the drive under direct Führer authority. The dynamic of rapid, uncoordinated expansion continued and intensified, contributing directly to the decision for war in 1939 when resource asymmetries became unsustainable for a long conflict. No 'normalization' occurred; the personalized, competitive style ensured continuing radicalization of economic policy in service of expansion.

## Historiography

Kershaw integrates economic pressures into the broader analysis of Hitler's personalized rule and the 'working towards' mechanism. Stresses that rearmament was never decoupled from strategy in Hitler's mind (contra some structural accounts); timing of open steps was his. Notes the role of food crisis and raw-material shortages in 1935–36 as both brake and spur to foreign-policy risk (Rhineland partly to offset domestic discontent). Cross-reference to detailed economic studies (Tooze) for technical quantification; here the focus is political/structural interaction with charismatic authority and radicalization. Primary evidence from army memoranda (Beck), cabinet exchanges, Goebbels diary, Rosenberg, Hassell reports, Schacht interventions.

**Collection coverage note:** Grounded in Kershaw *Hubris* range material on 1933–1936 interactions of rearmament, food crisis, Four Year Plan origins, and Hitler's interventions. Strong on political drivers and consequences for power consolidation.
