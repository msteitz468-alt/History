## Definition and Scope

The wave of conspiratorial, avowedly revolutionary violence conducted by Far Left organizations in Western Europe from roughly 1969 to the early 1980s, concentrated above all in West Germany (the Red Army Faction, or Baader-Meinhof group) and Italy (the Red Brigades, *Brigate Rosse*), with related but distinct ethno-nationalist insurgencies in the Basque Country (ETA) and Northern Ireland (the Provisional IRA — see [[northern-ireland]]) that combined Leninist/Maoist revolutionary ideology with nationalist grievance. This page covers the Marxist-Leninist/Maoist revolutionary strand proper (Red Brigades, RAF); it is not a synonym for all Western European political violence of the period, which also included Far Right/neofascist terrorism (e.g., the December 1969 Piazza Fontana bombing in Milan) running in parallel and sometimes in a cycle of mutual escalation with the Left.

## Causal Drivers

`caused_by` in part the radicalization of a fringe of the 1968 student movement (see [[may-1968-france]], [[the-1968-generation-germany]]) that concluded, after the movement's political failure to unseat established governments, that only armed struggle could overthrow capitalism. `contributed_to` by: anti-Americanism sharpened by the Vietnam War and hostility to "consumption terror" (the perceived cultural imperialism of American-dominated consumer capitalism); in Italy specifically, the "hot autumn" of 1969 industrial unrest (Pirelli tire-factory strikes, wildcat actions) which connected student radicals to disaffected factory workers; and in both West Germany and Italy, the presence of unresolved, aggravating neofascist/neo-Nazi subcultures and terror (Piazza Fontana, 1969) that fed a cycle of escalation.

## Major Phases

**West Germany:** The Red Army Faction (RAF), led by Andreas Baader and Ulrike Meinhof, drew ideologically on Mao, Che Guevara, and the Vietcong rather than Marx proper, targeting American military bases, West German government offices (viewed as an "American puppet regime"), department stores, and the Axel Springer press empire. A May 1972 bombing campaign led to the arrest of its core leadership; most subsequently died in prison (hunger strikes, apparent suicides). A second generation continued sporadic violence into the 1980s, increasingly divorced from coherent ideology. The kidnapping of Israeli athletes by Palestinian militants at the 1972 Munich Olympics, while not an RAF operation, exposed West German security failures in the same period.

**Italy:** The Red Brigades, based on underground cells of radical factory workers and former students, deployed kidnapping, "kneecapping" (crippling by gunshot to the knee), firebombing, and bank robbery against corporate leaders and pro-business officials. The campaign's climax was the March 1978 kidnapping and, fifty-five days later, murder of former prime minister Aldo Moro in Rome. Italian police, aided by informers, dismantled the organization in 1980–1982.

## Geographic Spread

Centered in West Germany and Italy; related but organizationally and ideologically distinct movements operated in the Basque Country (ETA, killing some 450 people by the end of the 1970s) and Northern Ireland (the Provisional IRA, far more lethal — over 1,800 killed over three decades). Belgian anarchists' 1967 arson of a Brussels department store (253 dead) is an early precursor cited by Paxton and Hessler as inspiration for German revolutionary rhetoric.

## Interaction

`contrasts_with` the much larger current of the post-1968 Far Left that channeled into nonviolent "new social movements" — feminism, environmentalism — and eventually the Green parties (see [[greens-parties-western-europe]]); terrorism was a minority strategy even among radicalized 1968 veterans. `contributed_to`, in Italy, the "historic compromise" between the Christian Democrats and Berlinguer's Eurocommunist Party (see [[eurocommunism]]), partly motivated by the shared imperative of combating terrorism from both the Red Brigades and the neofascist Right.

## End Conditions

Ended by police success (Italy, 1980–1982, aided by informers and public revulsion after the Moro murder) and by the collapse of internal support and leadership attrition (West Germany, through the 1970s–80s). The ETA and IRA insurgencies, rooted in distinct territorial/nationalist grievances rather than generic Marxist revolution, persisted far longer (ETA into the 2000s–2010s; the IRA until the 1998 Good Friday Agreement) — see [[northern-ireland]] for that separate trajectory.

## Historiography

Paxton and Hessler situate 1970s terrorism as a direct, if minority, legacy of 1968, drawing an explicit — though qualified — comparison to the political violence of 1918–1921 (both generations, they note, shared "impatience with party politics" and "affinity for political extremes" despite the earlier generation having fought a war and the later one not having done so). Primary interview material (e.g., French student Julien G.'s account of the "friendship... where there was really a sense of one for all" in street violence) is used to distinguish the mass of participants swept up in the moment from the conscious revolutionary leadership cadre.
