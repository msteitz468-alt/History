## Definition and Scope

Pacific Island exchange systems encompass the full range of pre-contact mechanisms by which goods, labour, knowledge, and social obligations circulated within and between island communities. These are not reducible to "trade" in the Western market sense. As [[denoon-cambridge-pacific-islanders-1997]] states: "it is tempting — but misleading — to interpret trade, bride price, mortuary payments and compensation simply as ecological adaptations to maximise material benefits." People did not foster relations merely to facilitate exchanges, nor exchange merely to build alliances — they "drew no such distinctions between 'economic' and 'social', profane and sacred."

The process spans specialist local production, inter-village exchange, inter-island maritime networks, tributary relations between high and low islands, and the great ceremonial exchange cycles of the New Guinea Highlands.

## Causal Drivers

**Ecological complementarity**: Most families needed access to multiple ecological zones — reef, lagoon, coast, lowland garden, and upland or forest — for a viable diet. Where such variety was unavailable on one plot, exchange partnerships supplied it. The inhabitants of resource-poor islands "often enjoyed higher standards of living than their better-resourced neighbours, so long as their trading partners maintained the flow of food and material in a system of 'trading subsistence'."

**Surplus agriculture**: Intensification of production (sweet potato in the Highlands, continuous taro cultivation in river deltas) generated surpluses "with which to sustain exchange partnerships." Pigs, the primary store of agricultural surplus, became the medium of Highlands ceremonial exchange.

**Cosmological obligation**: Among the Huli, "exchange is so fundamental a feature of Huli cosmology and society that it resists abstraction. The Huli universe is itself constructed upon a notion of exchange between living humans and ancestral and non-ancestral spirits." Mineral oil seeps were "critical nodes within Huli sacred geography." Exchange was not separable from religion.

**Political competition**: "These relationships were intensely competitive: gifts imposed obligations, which must be acknowledged at almost any cost." Exchange was a form of warfare by other means — "the rhetoric of giving was often profoundly aggressive": Michael Young describes exchange contests as "fighting with food."

## Major Phases

### Phase 1 — Foundation: Lapita Networks (c. 3500–500 BCE)
Lapita colonists carried pigs, dogs, chickens, taro, breadfruit, bananas, and yams across the western Pacific, establishing agricultural systems that produced sustainable surpluses on the larger islands. The [[pacific-voyaging-and-settlement]] process created the demographic and ecological substrate for exchange. Irrigated taro fields on the larger islands and taro pits on atolls supported dense populations, while inter-island contact maintained network ties.

### Phase 2 — New Guinea Highland Intensification (c. 5000 BCE–1800 CE)
Systematic drainage, mounding, and continuous cultivation in the Highlands produced massive surpluses sustained by feral pig domestication. From the late nineteenth century at latest, sweet potato "sustained massive production of pigs, the main exchange items" for moka and tee ceremonies. The Melpa Big Man Ongka's account of his last moka describes the full logic: through years of soliciting gifts, receiving, redistributing, and giving "pigs, cassowaries, shells, decorating oil, cattle, a motorbike, a car, and money," he consolidated political authority. Mervyn Meggitt observed that Big Men kept the system going "by paying off those supporters whose aid is essential to them" while "retaining for themselves whatever resources they can abstract at the expense of the weaker and poorer members."

### Phase 3 — Maritime Specialist Networks (c. 1000 BCE–1800 CE)
Coastal and island specialists developed maritime exchange routes of remarkable reach:

- **Hiri network**: Motu villages on the Papuan coast despatched "twenty multi-hulled lakatoi on each year's hiri expedition to the Gulf of Papua, carrying about 26,000 pots and returning with perhaps 500 tons of sago."
- **Torres Strait network**: Canoe-borne trade in stone and bone tools, bows, arrows, and canoes — "the most important items" — with some tribes not even on friendly terms with their trading partners.
- **Kula ring**: Trobriand men "travelled long distances in elaborately decorated canoes to exchange armshells and necklaces with their trading partners" — a non-utilitarian exchange that "ceremoniously masked the exchange of essential things such as pottery, salt and adze blades."
- **Yap-Palau stone money circuit**: From about the seventeenth century, Yapese sailed to Koror in Palau to quarry raay (calcium carbonate discs), paying for quarrying rights by chiefly arrangement and transporting discs across hundreds of kilometres on frail rafts. Raay were "a store of value" whose ownership "passed from village to village even when raay were irretrievably sunk."

### Phase 4 — Polynesian Long-Distance Exchange Communities
Hau'ofa's model: "Fiji, Samoa, Tonga, Niue, Rotuma, Tokelau, Tuvalu, Futuna and 'Uvea formed a large exchange community in which wealth and people with their skills and arts circulated endlessly." Tonga, with few natural resources, "acted as a 'middleman' between Samoa and Fiji, the sources of red feathers, timber for boat building, adze blades and fine pandanus cloth." "The highest chiefs of Fiji, Samoa and Tonga still maintain kin connections that were forged centuries before Europeans entered the Pacific."

### Phase 5 — Atoll-High Island Clientage Systems
Atoll populations, "having few resources except shell," depended on high islands for daily necessities. High island patrons "could succour their clients in the event of drought, cyclones or other calamities." The most elaborate case: Yapese priests exercised hegemony over atolls in the central Carolines — "each year dozens of canoes streamed into Yap from other islands to present what looked like tributary gifts." Women's banana-cloth textiles were "the most important form of tribute." Carolinian ocean-going canoes "travelled along named sea-lanes, relying on sailing instructions memorised by one generation after another."

## Geographic Spread

The process operated across all of Oceania but with distinct regional characteristics:

- **New Guinea Highlands**: Moka (Melpa) and tee (Enga) — ceremonial pig exchange sustaining political competition among Big Men
- **Papua coast and islands**: Hiri (Motu-Gulf sago/pottery), Kula (Trobriand armbands/necklaces), Mailu pottery monopoly
- **Micronesia**: Yap-centred stone money and tribute network; atoll-high island clientage across the Carolines
- **Western Polynesia**: Fiji-Samoa-Tonga exchange community; Samoan le toga fine mats and 'oloa complementarity
- **Eastern Polynesia and Hawai'i**: Tributary cloth and food exchanges; Hawaiian chiefs' extraction of tapa, mats, and food from commoners
- **Solomon Islands**: Langalanga shell money manufacture (mass-produced by women); tambu shell wealth of the Tolai (New Britain)

## Interaction with Other Processes

- **Land tenure** (concepts/vanua-and-land-cosmology — link when created)): Exchange and land were interlocked — chiefs' authority over land underpinned their ability to extract surplus for ceremonial exchange.
- **Gender division of labour** ([[gender-division-of-labour-pacific]]): Women's production (tapa, mats, pots, fine mats) provided the material substance of most ceremonial exchanges; men's control over the public stage obscured women's structural role.
- **European contact** ([[pacific-first-contacts-process]]): Exchange systems quickly accommodated European commodities — moka embraced manufactured imports including cattle, motorcycles, and cars; Polynesian exchanges quickly incorporated iron tools, cloth, and firearms. Chiefs' access to whale's teeth (tabua) via Europeans transformed Fijian politics (Bau's rise).

## End Conditions

The process was not ended but transformed by European commercial contact. "These exchanges are often described as 'traditional', but they were not as inflexible as that term implies. As demand changed, so did production and the exchange networks." Tambu shell wealth became "capital" only when commercialised. Sandalwood and bêche-de-mer trade inserted commodity relations into networks previously couched in social idiom. British and American naval patrols from the 1830s established legal frameworks protecting foreign traders, shifting the balance of power away from Islander-dictated terms of exchange. Formal colonisation from the 1870s subordinated exchange systems to colonial political economies but did not eliminate them; moka ceremonies continued through the colonial era and adapted further imports as exchange goods.

## Historiography

The foundational ethnographic text for Highlands exchange is Andrew Strathern's account of Ongka's moka. For kula: Malinowski, *Argonauts of the Western Pacific* (1922; criticised for ignoring women's mortuary exchanges — see Annette Weiner, *Women of Value, Men of Renown*). For the Torres Strait: Colonial reports in British New Guinea Annual Reports. For the Yap-Palau system: Hezel, *First Taint of Civilization*. For Hau'ofa's "sea of islands" reframing: "Our Sea of Islands" (1993). For the Motu hiri: Martha Macintyre and Jim Allen, "Trading for Subsistence." Meggitt's "Pigs Are Our Hearts" analyses the tee exchange system. The dominant interpretive shift since the 1970s has been from purely ecological-functional explanations toward analyses that foreground cosmological, political, and gender dimensions.
