# The African Diaspora

## Definition and Scope

The African diaspora refers to those tens of millions of Africans and their descendants forcibly removed from the continent by the various branches of the slave trade, together with later voluntary migrants. As generally understood, the diaspora is united by a consciousness of a common origin, by the struggle to secure freedom in the face of chattel slavery and subsequent injustice, and by ongoing efforts to maintain or forge links with the African homeland.

The term gained wide scholarly currency after 1965, when it was introduced by George Shepperson and Joseph E. Harris at a conference of African historians in Tanzania, though the intellectual tradition reaches back to W. E. B. Du Bois, Carter G. Woodson, and nineteenth-century redemptionist discourse.

Recent decades have dramatically expanded the field's geographic scope beyond the Anglophone North Atlantic to include Latin America, the Indian Ocean, the Middle East, and the African continent itself.

## Major Phases and Geographies

### The Atlantic Diaspora (c.1500–1900)

The forced transportation of enslaved Africans across the Atlantic constituted the largest involuntary human migration in history. The trans-Atlantic slave trade database (Voyages, 1999) has allowed much more precise analysis of the shifting composition of slave populations across time and space.

Three broad cultural zones shaped the African impact on the Americas (John Thornton): the coasts of Upper Guinea, Lower Guinea, and West Central Africa from Loango to Angola. West Central Africa — including Kongo and Angola — provided more enslaved people than any other region across four centuries of the trade.

Key diaspora heartlands:
- **Brazil (Salvador da Bahia)**: largest enslaved African population in the Americas; majority Nagô (Yoruba) by the 1840s following the collapse of the Oyo Empire
- **Cuba (Havana)**: the cabildos de nación formed from the 1870s as sites of Afro-Cuban religious and cultural life
- **Haiti**: the Haitian Revolution (C. L. R. James, *The Black Jacobins*, 1938) demonstrated the agency of enslaved Africans in the Atlantic revolutionary age
- **Suriname**: maroon communities studied by Richard Price as the locus classicus of creolized culture

### The Indian Ocean and Islamic Diasporas

A similar number of Africans may have been sold into slavery via the trans-Saharan and Indian Ocean trades as those who crossed the Atlantic, yet this diaspora has received far less scholarly attention. John Hunwick identifies three reasons: reluctance of Arab and Muslim scholars to research slavery; lack of active diasporic consciousness among individuals with African ancestry; and linguistic barriers facing Western historians.

Spirit possession and healing cults — bori, zar, tumbura — proved resilient in North Africa and Ottoman cities (Tunis, Algiers, Istanbul, Izmir), often taking hybrid forms in dialogue with local Sufi practices and pre-Islamic beliefs.

The western Indian Ocean diaspora — Madagascar, Mauritius, Réunion — has been explored by Pier Larson (*Ocean of Letters*, 2009), who shows that hybrid Francophone créolité and vernacular Afro-Malagasy identity were "closely imbricated with each other," rather than alternatives.

## The Continuity vs. Creolization Debate

The central historiographical tension in diaspora studies since the 1930s:

**Melville Herskovits** (*The Myth of the Negro Past*, 1941): argued for the survival of African cultural "retentions" in the Americas, particularly in religion and music.

**E. Franklin Frazier**: argued the link with Africa was ruptured by the Middle Passage and plantation slavery, producing a new African American identity.

**Sidney Mintz and Richard Price** (*The Birth of African-American Culture*, 1976): the creolization model argued for the creative rebuilding of cultures by Africans from diverse ethnic and linguistic backgrounds — a process likely already underway during the Middle Passage.

**Robert Farris Thompson** (*Flash of the Spirit*, 1983): reinserted Africa into the picture by tracing continuities in Yoruba, Kongo, Dahomean, Mande, and Ejagham visual cultures between Africa and their respective traditions in the Americas.

**John Thornton** (*Africa and the Africans in the Making of the Atlantic World*, 1992): portrayed Africans as active agents in Atlantic commerce and argued for the transplantation and survival of particular African ethnicities and cultures in specific American locations.

**Paul Gilroy** (*The Black Atlantic*, 1993): explored the role of black thinkers and activists in the creation of a "counterculture of modernity" — a more fluid, transformative culture shaped by efforts to overcome racial exclusion from Enlightenment modernity.

Recent scholarship (J. Lorand Matory, Stefania Capone, Stephan Palmié, David Brown) has moved beyond the search for origins, showing how diasporic cultures were created through active, historically situated processes of reinvention — not simple retention or wholesale rupture.

## African American Religions

The Afro-Atlantic religions are the most studied strongholds of African cultural survival and transformation:

**Candomblé (Brazil)**: dominated by the Nagô (Yoruba) "nation" through a contested process of anagônização ("Yorubaization") driven by the strategic assertion of African purity by leading practitioners, local intellectuals, and scholarly interlocutors (Matory; Capone). The process accelerated under the Vargas regime in the 1930s when African culture was valorised as Brazilian patrimony.

**Santería / Regla de Ocha (Cuba)**: emerged through the fusion of mutual-aid societies and African ethnic formulations into the cabildos de nación from the 1870s. Fernando Ortiz (1881–1969) moved from hostile analysis to active sponsorship of Afro-Cuban religion and developed theories of "transculturation."

**Vodou (Haiti)**: the Rada and Petwo "nations" associated with deities of Dahomey and Kongo/Angola respectively. "Guinea" functions as a "cosmographic metaphor" (Karen Richman) rather than a primordial place of origin.

The Yoruba òrìsà diaspora has grown into a transnational religious movement with the International Congress of Orisha Tradition and Culture (founded 1981) and claims to worldwide religious status.

## Atlantic Creoles and Africa's Role

Ira Berlin's concept of the "Atlantic creole" — members of the Charter Generation of slaves for whom contact with cosmopolitan Atlantic cultures gave rise to a hybrid identity, sometimes before arrival in the Americas — has been extended by Linda Heywood and John Thornton. They argue that the long-standing engagement of Kongo and Angola with European-influenced traits created an already-creolized culture that was transplanted to the Americas.

J. Lorand Matory's analysis of the dialogue between Brazil and West Africa beginning in the 1830s (including the deportation of rebellious slaves from Salvador to Lagos) shows that "homelands" are not simply sources of diaspora; diasporas also create and transform their homelands.

## The Global Luo Diaspora and Indigeneity

Modern African diasporic identity increasingly intersects with global discourses: marginalised groups in Tanzania have positioned themselves within neo-liberal frameworks of indigeneity; Luo ethnic imagination now "stretches from Kisumu to the White House via the virtual 'Little Kisumus' of a world-wide diaspora."

## Historiography

The bifurcated historiography that kept African and African American history separate has been transformed since the 1990s by transnational and diasporic approaches. The "tidal wave of scholarly, funding, and political interest in the African diaspora" (Judith Byfield, 2000) has produced large collected volumes as well as increasingly sophisticated monographic studies.

Key methodological shifts: from comparative ethnography of African "survivals" to historically situated analysis of reinvention; from Africa-centred origin-seeking to circum-Atlantic exchange; from the creolization vs. continuity binary to attention to the creativity and agency of practitioners themselves.
