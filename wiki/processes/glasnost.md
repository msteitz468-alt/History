# Glasnost
## Definition and Scope
**Glasnost** (Russian for "openness" or "transparency") was the policy of cultural and political liberalization introduced in the Soviet Union by Mikhail Gorbachev in the mid-1980s. Designed to allow free discussion of economic, social, and historical issues in speech and print, glasnost aimed to revitalize the Soviet system by exposing bureaucratic corruption and inefficiency. Instead, the policy unchained long-suppressed criticisms of the Soviet regime, political censorship collapsed, and nationalist movements arose that fractured the Union.

## Causal Drivers
- **Support for Perestroika:** Gorbachev needed the support of the educated public and intellectuals to overcome the passive resistance of conservative Party bureaucrats who were blocking economic restructuring (**[[perestroika|Perestroika]]**).
- **The Chernobyl Disaster (1986):** The initial cover-up of the nuclear reactor explosion at Chernobyl highlighted the danger of the traditional Stalinist culture of secrecy, forcing the government to allow more honest reporting in the news media.
- **Historical Reckoning:** The need to confront the systemic rot of the stagnation era required an open discussion of the country's past mistakes, including the legacy of Stalinist terror.

## Major Phases
- **Introduction and Thaw (1986–1987):** The relaxation of censorship allowed the publication of previously banned literary works (such as Pasternak's *Doctor Zhivago*) and the return of exiled figures like Andrei Sakharov. In July 1987, Gorbachev resumed de-Stalinization, permitting criticism of Stalin's terror.
- **Explosive Openness (1988–1989):** The media began publishing details about historical atrocities, including the Katyn Forest massacre and the mass graves of Stalin's victims (such as Kurapaty near Minsk, Line 7165). Public discussion expanded to current issues, such as the failing economy and the war in Afghanistan.
- **Political Pluralism and Nationalisms (1990–1991):** Glasnost led to the legal recognition of diverse political groups and political parties. Crucially, it allowed non-Russian ethnic groups to openly express their grievances against Moscow, leading to the rise of popular fronts and nationalist independence movements in the Baltic and Transcaucasian republics.

## Geographic Spread
While starting as a top-down policy in Moscow, glasnost spread rapidly across all fifteen republics of the USSR, finding its most radical expression in the Baltic states (Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania), Ukraine, and Georgia.

## Interaction
- **With the Church:** Glasnost permitted the return of religious buildings to the Russian Orthodox Church, the broadcast of religious services, and a general religious revival.
- **With Soviet History:** It led to the rehabilitation of millions of purge victims, including early Bolshevik leaders like Bukharin, Rykov, and Kamenev who had been executed in the show trials.

## End Conditions
Glasnost ended as a policy with the **[[dissolution-of-the-soviet-union|dissolution of the Soviet Union]]** in December 1991. The free press and intellectual pluralism established under glasnost survived the collapse, becoming the foundation for the freedom of speech and press in independent Russia during the Yeltsin presidency.
