## Definition and Scope

Paleolithic art and symbolic culture is the body of representational and symbolic
production by behaviorally modern *Homo sapiens* (and the debate over its archaic
precursors): cave paintings, engravings, portable carvings ("art mobilier"),
figurines, body ornament, and the symbolic practices they imply. It is the most
direct surviving evidence of the Paleolithic *mind* — of imagination, cosmology,
and religion.

Its appearance is a key marker in the [[homo-sapiens-emergence-date]] debate: the
proliferation of art and personal ornament is among the strongest evidence for
fully modern cognition.

## Earliest Evidence (Africa)

- **Blombos Cave, South Africa** (~100,000–75,000 BP): shell jewellery, incised
  slabs of ochre, shell "crucibles" and spatulas for mixing pigments — an ochre-
  processing workshop. Evidence that ancestors had "a theory of mind."
- **Sibudu, South Africa**: ochre-powder production ~58,000 BP.
- **Apollo 11 Cave, Namibia**: painted rock slabs dated to before ~29,000 BCE —
  among the oldest dated art in the world, almost as old as any in Europe.

## European Florescence

- **Chauvet** (France): earliest art-filled caves of southern France, ~30,000 BP.
- **Lascaux** (France): ~17,000 BP (10,000 years later than Chauvet, yet subjects
  and techniques "hardly changed in all that time").
- **Venus figurines**: Venus of Willendorf (~30,000 BP, Germany; a "plump little
  carving of a fat female," interpreted as goddess, ruler, or fertility object);
  Venus of Laussel (carved on a cave wall in France, raising a horn). Female
  portraits from Brassempouy (France) and Dolní Věstonice (Moravia, >20,000 BP).
- **Ivory carvings**: arched-necked horses from Vogelherd (~30,000 BP, S. Germany);
  the Löwenmensch (lion-human) of ~40,000 BP. Clay models of bears, dogs, and women
  fired ~27,000 BP at Dolní Věstonice and Maininskaya (Russia).
- About fifty European cave complexes contain thousands of paintings (mostly of
  animals) and hundreds of smaller works. Sculptures and carvings are scattered
  from Britain and the Atlantic to the Oder, Carpathians, Ukraine, and the Urals.

## Other Regions

- **Namibia** (southern Africa): four painted rock slabs ~26,000 BP.
- **Arnhem Land, Australia**: earliest rock paintings show faint traces of extinct
  giant kangaroos and scary snakes. Stencils of human hands and tools at Kenniff,
  Australia, ~20,000 BP. (See [[colonization-australasia-50kbp]].)
- Rare finds occur across the whole range of human habitats, suggesting general
  conclusions from concentrated evidence apply more widely.

## Interpretation: What Was the Art For?

- **Hunting magic / sympathetic ritual**: Some animal images are slashed or
  punctured as if in symbolic sacrifice; stencilled handprints and footprints may
  derive from prey-tracking. Cave art "depicts an imagined world, full of the
  spirits of the animals people needed and admired: a magical world, accessed in
  mystical trances."
- **Shamanism**: The dominant interpretation (Lewis-Williams, Clottes) links cave
  and rock art to shamanic trance experience. See [[shamanism-paleolithic-religion]].
- **Storytelling and cosmology**: The art "surely told stories and had magical,
  ritual uses."

## "Ice Age Globalization" (a key argument)

Fernández-Armesto argues that the comparable art across widely separated regions
points to "the last great era of what we would now call a kind of globalization."
Key elements of culture were similar all over the inhabited world: hunter-gatherer
economies with similar technology, food, levels of material culture, and — as far
as can be told — similar religious practices. Social and intellectual change was
slow compared to later periods (Chauvet to Lascaux spans 10,000 years with little
change). This convergence of Paleolithic culture is a deep-time instance of the
divergence/convergence dynamic (see [[divergence-convergence]]); here, shared
heritage and parallel adaptation produced striking *uniformity* before the great
post-Ice-Age divergence.

## Technology of the Art

The cave-art palette was simple: mineral ochre in three colours (red, brown,
yellow) mixed with animal fat, applied with wood, bone, and animal hair. The
elegance of the animal portraits reflects "practised, specialized hands and
learning accumulated over generations."

## End Conditions

Paleolithic symbolic culture transitions into Neolithic symbolic systems (the
burials, figurines, and cosmologies of early farming villages such as Çatalhöyük;
see [[family-and-biopolitics]]). The tradition does not end so much as transform
as lifeways change.

## Note on Preservation Bias

We know most about European cave art because it survived in deep, inaccessible
caves. Most Paleolithic art elsewhere — painted on exposed rock faces, bodies, or
hides — has been lost. The European concentration is therefore partly an artifact
of preservation, not of where symbolic culture flourished (a caution against
[[eurocentrism]]).
