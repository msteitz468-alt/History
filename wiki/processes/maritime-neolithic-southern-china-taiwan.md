## Definition and Scope

The Maritime Neolithic of Southern China and Taiwan denotes the emergence, between roughly 6000 and 500 BCE, of interconnected farming-fishing communities along the coastlines of the lower Yangtze, southeastern China, and Taiwan that developed the maritime capabilities and cultural toolkit from which the Austronesian diaspora would later spring. It encompasses: early coastal rice-farming villages in the Yangtze delta; coastal hunter-gatherer-fisher settlements in Fujian, Guangdong, and Guangxi; the Neolithic occupation of Taiwan from ~3000 BCE; the first migration across the Taiwan Strait; and the initial expansion of Taiwan-related populations into the Philippines (~2200–2000 BCE), from which later Austronesian movements into ISEA, Micronesia, Melanesia, and Polynesia would flow.

"Austronesian was the most widespread language family in the world prior to 1500 CE, representing one of the most impressive records of human dispersal prior to the large-scale European expansions of the last few centuries" (Hung, in [[jones-matsuda-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v1-2022]]). Today Austronesian languages are spoken by over 380 million people across Taiwan, the Philippines, maritime Southeast Asia, Madagascar, Melanesia, Micronesia, and Polynesia.

This process is distinct from the later [[austronesian-expansion]] in that it covers the *foundation* period — the homeland communities and first wave of migration — not the full trans-Pacific dispersal.

## Causal Drivers

**Rice agriculture.** As early as 6000 BCE, rice-farming groups had established coastal villages in the lower Yangtze. Increasing agricultural land-use drove demographic expansion southward, eventually reaching coastal Fujian and Guangdong (by 3000 BCE) and crossing into Taiwan (by 3000 BCE). In Taiwan itself, rice and millet cultivation "appears to have been the mainstay of subsistence security" by 2800 BCE, with the period 2500–1500 BCE witnessing a "seven times" increase in documented archaeological sites.

**Watercraft development.** The oldest log canoe in China, dated to 6000 BCE, was excavated from the Kuahuqiao site in Zhejiang: a lacquered pine dugout 560 cm long, repaired with Chinese lacquer tree adhesive ~8,000 years ago. Five paddles and probable sail matting were associated. By the Baojinwan rock carvings (Pearl River delta, ~2300–2000 BCE), plank-built sailing boats with masts and sails, "similar to the plank-built boats traditionally used in Lanyu (Botel Tobago)," are depicted. At Dapinshi one carving shows "a large boat with more than twenty people and a few animals . . . a tall mast on the centre for hanging a large sail." The ~1000-year pause between initial Taiwan Neolithic (3000 BCE) and the first Taiwan-to-Philippines migration (2200–2000 BCE) "may have been due to the limits of sailing technology, and this obstacle was overcome by the invention of the outrigger canoe complex."

**Coastal fishing ecology.** Multi-generational accumulation of marine knowledge was crucial: "people of Taiwan's coastal communities were familiar with their seashores and nearby marine environments, and multi-generational knowledge probably contributed to the success of later seafaring traditions." At Nanguanli (2800–2200 BCE) more than 50% of animal bones were fish; by ~2000 BCE at Eluanbi, stone net-sinkers and bones of dolphinfish and marlin imply "trolling capture from a canoe moving quickly over a deep-sea zone." Shark-hunting (species >2 metres, speed >50 km/h) is documented by 2200–2000 BCE.

**Inter-island exchange.** Stone tool workshops on Penghu (Pescadores) producing olivine basalt adzes from 2500–2000 BCE served as staging points; Fengtian nephrite from eastern Taiwan moved to Penghu and on to Luzon. Shell bead manufacture at Ludao (Youzihu, ~1636–1440 cal. BCE) used techniques identical to those at contemporary Mariana Islands sites, demonstrating wide-ranging network connections.

## Major Phases

### Phase 1 — Early farming communities, Lower Yangtze (6000–3000 BCE)

The Kuahuqiao site in Zhejiang (6000–5000 BCE) represents the oldest documented coastal rice-farming community in southern China with preserved watercraft. It yielded domesticated rice (*Oryza sativa japonica*), pigs, and dogs alongside marine taxa including dolphin. The Hemudu group (5000–3300 BCE) — notably Hemudu and Tianluoshan — produced large pile-raised stilt villages with sophisticated mortise-tenon-dowel carpentry, twelve paddles, and abundant bones of pelagic fish (sharks, mackerel, tuna, bonito) alongside cultivated rice. Stable isotopes from Tianluoshan human bones indicate, paradoxically, only low levels of marine food consumption, suggesting pelagic fish were caught rather than as dietary staple.

Contemporaneously, coastal southeastern China (Fujian, Guangdong, Guangxi) supported shell-midden communities (6000–3000 BCE) sustaining themselves through fishing and foraging without rice or millet farming. The oldest, Daowei I and II on Liangdao in the Mazu Islands (Taiwan Strait), dates to 6300–5500 BCE. High marine-diet stable isotope ratios confirm a primarily coastal subsistence; dietary starch came partly from sago palms. These communities lived on newly emerged offshore islands following post-glacial sea-level stabilization.

"Two internally diverse populations met here in coastal southeast China around 3000 BCE. These included the long-established hunter-gatherer-fishers, and the newly arriving groups of rice farmers from the Yangtze valley. With this meeting of cultural groups, events and processes were set into motion that would develop eventually into the long chronological narrative of Austronesian migration."

### Phase 2 — Taiwan Strait crossing and early Taiwan Neolithic (3000–2200 BCE)

Around 3000 BCE, farming populations crossed the 130–180 km Taiwan Strait, bringing rice, millet, pigs, and dogs to Taiwan. The pre-Neolithic occupation of Taiwan dates to at least 30,000 BCE (stone tools in caves; one flexed skeleton at Xiaoma Cave ~4000 BCE, craniometrically affiliated with Agta Negrito people of Luzon). The incoming Neolithic horizon is marked by red-slipped cord-marked earthenware by ~3500–3000 BCE, followed by rice and millet confirmed by 2800 BCE at Nanguanli and Nanguanlidong.

"According to Blust, Taiwan was the home of nine of the ten primary subgroups of the Austronesian language family. This island was therefore a centre of diversity at the deepest level of Austronesian language history." Nine Formosan subgroups (Atayal, Paiwan, Tsou, etc.) still survive. DNA studies confirm that "diagnostic genetic markers of the modern-day Austronesian people of Taiwan were already identifiable in burials dating at 2700–500 BCE."

Maritime trade networks appeared by 2500 BCE: basalt adze workshops on Penghu, Fengtian nephrite exchange back to Taiwan. People's navigational knowledge may have been connected to astronomical observation encoded in the Liangzhu cultural group of the lower Yangtze (3300 BCE).

### Phase 3 — Expansion to Philippines and intervening islands (2200–1500 BCE)

Around 2200–2000 BCE, Taiwan-related Neolithic settlers moved south to Ludao, Lanyu (Botel Tobago), the Batanes Islands, and northern Luzon, somehow circumventing the northward-flowing Kuroshio Current. The Batanes Islands (190 km south of Taiwan, 160 km north of Luzon) served as a staging ground, with pottery-bearing layers from 2200–2000 BCE and rich artefact assemblages by 1200 BCE including Fengtian jade adzes, Taiwan slate points, and Taiwan-style net-sinkers. The Indigenous Tao people of Lanyu today still produce lashed-lug plank-built boats (the *cinedkeran*) traceable to ancient Austronesian boatbuilding traditions, closely comparable to the Maluku *orembai* and Solomon Islands *mon* type.

In northern Luzon (Cagayan valley), the same material complex — red-slipped pottery, polished stone tools, Fengtian jade ornaments, domesticated pigs, and eventually rice by 1200 BCE — appeared by 2200–2000 BCE. Population growth in the Cagayan valley as agricultural terraces developed after 1500 BCE drove further outward expansion.

### Phase 4 — ISEA colonization and Mariana Islands (1500–500 BCE)

By 1500 BCE, red-slipped earthenware with dentate-stamped and circle-impressed designs was spreading through the Philippines, Sulawesi, and farther ISEA. "An exceptional ocean-crossing event occurred when this distinctive pottery tradition was brought to the Mariana Islands in western Micronesia, this perhaps being the world's longest ocean crossing at the time, more than 2,500 kilometres from the Philippines." The Chamorro language derives from an early Malayo-Polynesian source in the northern or central Philippines. The outrigger canoe — "probably the major form of watercraft" — enabled this crossing, operating with a reversible prow/stern design marvelled at by Magellan's chronicler Alvo (6 March 1521): "they had mat sails of a triangular shape, and they went both ways, for they made of the poop the prow."

Bukit Tengkorak (NE Borneo, ~1300–1000 BCE) produced obsidian chips from two Melanesian sources — Admiralty Islands (north of New Guinea) and Kutau/Bao Peninsula (northern New Britain) — alongside green mica from Mindoro Island (Philippines), demonstrating trans-regional exchange networks spanning >3,000 km by the Late Bronze Age.

"Other than the settlement in the Mariana Islands at 1500 BCE, the remainder of Remote Oceania beyond New Guinea and the Solomons was uninhabited until close to 1000 BCE."

### Phase 5 — Iron Age South China Sea networks (500 BCE–500 CE)

After 500 BCE, iron and copper-bronze technologies entered the maritime Southeast Asian cultural inventory, accelerating boatbuilding. Chinese records describe ships by 200–300 CE capable of carrying 600–700 people or eighty horses. Sewn-plank (lashed-lug) boatbuilding traditions are attested archaeologically at Pontian, Malaysia (~260–430 CE). Locked mortise-and-tenon plank construction found at Dong Son sites (Red River delta, Vietnam; ~400 BCE–200 CE) parallels Egyptian Middle Kingdom boats (from ~2000 BCE) and Han Dynasty boats, including a vessel excavated at Wei Bridge near Chang'an — "a remarkable sharing of boatbuilding technology from Rome to Chang'an and northern Vietnam."

## Geographic Spread

The process begins in the lower Yangtze delta (eastern China), spreads south along the Chinese and Taiwanese coastlines, leaps the Taiwan Strait, ramifies through the Philippines, and reaches the Mariana Islands (western Micronesia) by 1500 BCE and Borneo by 1300 BCE. It is the westernmost foundation for the broader [[austronesian-expansion]] that eventually encompassed Madagascar in the west and Easter Island in the east.

## End Conditions

This process ends as a distinct phase around 500–1000 CE when the fully developed Austronesian maritime network — outriggers, sophisticated navigation, widespread iron technology — enabled the settlement of Remote Oceania's easternmost islands (Hawai'i, New Zealand, Easter Island). The foundation-building phase described here is complete by ~500 BCE, when the South China Sea networks shift to a qualitatively new Iron Age character with pan-Asian commodity exchange connecting the maritime Silk Road.

## Historiography

**Source quality.** Hung's chapter in [[jones-matsuda-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v1-2022]] draws on convergent archaeological, linguistic, and genetic evidence. The "Out of Taiwan" model for Austronesian origins has achieved near-consensus (Blust, Bellwood, Pawley), supported by: (a) the distribution of primary Austronesian subgroups (nine of ten in Taiwan); (b) the southward sequence of pottery and jade artefact horizons; (c) genetic studies confirming Taiwanese markers in early Philippine burials. A minority "Nusantao" hypothesis (Solheim) proposed Southeast Asian origins south of Taiwan but has not gained archaeological support.

**Scholarly debates.** Active debates include: (1) the role and timing of the "long pause" between Taiwan Neolithic (~3000 BCE) and Philippine settlement (~2200 BCE) — attributable to technological limits or demographic thresholds; (2) whether bamboo rafts or plank-built canoes were used for Taiwan Strait crossings (experimental evidence suggests bamboo is insufficient against the Kuroshio); (3) precise mechanisms of Mariana Islands settlement and whether it predates some Philippine dispersals.
