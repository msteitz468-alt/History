# Public Authorities as Power Instrument (New York)

The public authority (or public benefit corporation) as an institutional device that allowed unelected officials—most dramatically Robert Moses—to exercise sovereign-like powers (eminent domain, rule-making, police) with independent revenue streams from bonds and user fees, largely insulated from legislatures, governors, mayors, and voters.

## Definition and Scope

Unlike traditional government agencies funded by appropriations and subject to annual budgets and civil service, public authorities could issue revenue bonds, set their own tolls/fees, retain surpluses (after the "gimmick" amendments), and operate with minimal oversight. Early U.S. examples were narrow (small water systems, the Port of New York Authority as a multi-state exception). Moses scaled and weaponized the form for parks, parkways, bridges, tunnels, expressways, and related development across New York State and City.

## Causal Drivers

- Progressive-era faith in expert administration (Moses' early civil-service work and Oxford thesis).
- Al Smith's governorship and Moses' drafting skill: creation of Long Island State Park Commission and early authorities with "appropriation" powers (walk on land and notify owner) and broad definitions of "parks" and "real estate."
- Moses' realization (mid-1930s) that high revenues could retire bonds early (dissolving authorities and surrendering control to the city); decision to amend statutes and contracts to keep money and power indefinitely.
- Bond contract irrevocability under the U.S. Constitution: powers written into contracts could not be impaired by later legislatures or executives.
- Political realities: elected officials needed the projects, jobs, and visible accomplishments; Moses delivered scandal-free (in the public eye) and on time while cultivating the press and "Moses myth."

## Major Phases

- 1920s: Discovery and first authorities under Smith; Jones Beach and Long Island parkway system.
- 1930s–1940s: Expansion to NYC (Triborough complex, Henry Hudson, West Side); multiple simultaneous titles; Depression-era federal funds routed through Moses-controlled planning capacity.
- 1930s–1950s: "Gimmick" and revenue retention; monopoly over multiple authorities and later the Tunnel Authority; control of surpluses for new projects.
- 1950s–1960s: Joint Program (auto-only commitment with Port Authority); Title I slum clearance powers; peak of highway program.
- 1960s onward: Erosion of independent power (Rockefeller, Lindsay fights) but the model and many authorities persisted.

## Geographic Spread

Primarily New York metropolitan area and Long Island, with influence on other cities and states that studied or adopted similar devices. Moses was consulted by planners from other U.S. cities and countries.

## Interaction

- Enabled the highway program and urban renewal at scale while bypassing normal democratic checks.
- Interacted with federal New Deal and interstate highway funding (Moses controlled the local planning and matching capacity).
- Produced both major public amenities (parks, beaches, bridges) and destructive outcomes (neighborhood clearance, auto dependence, low bridge clearances excluding buses from 200+ miles of parkways).
- Contrasted with traditional city departments and with reformers' attempts to reassert planning commission or elected control.

## End Conditions

Moses' personal empire was dismantled piecemeal in the 1960s–70s (loss of key posts, TBTA merger fights, retirement disputes), but the authority form and many specific entities continued. The model influenced later infrastructure financing nationwide. Later reforms attempted greater transparency and accountability, but revenue-bond authorities remain common.
