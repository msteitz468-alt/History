## Definition and Scope

The channeling of post-1968 Far Left energy — rejected by most participants in favor of terrorism (see [[left-wing-terrorism-western-europe-1970s]]) — into "new social movements" of the 1970s: environmentalism, the peace movement, the gay and lesbian movement, and feminism (the last treated at length in Chapter 20 of Paxton and Hessler, not covered by this range). These movements shared a rejection of capitalism, consumerism, conventional party politics, and the traditional family in favor of communalism, citizen involvement, and lifestyle experimentation, especially strong in northern Europe (Scandinavia, West Germany, the Netherlands). By the early 1980s they had coalesced, in most of continental Europe, into a new electoral party family: the Greens.

## Causal Drivers

`caused_by` the dissipation of the 1968 student movement (see [[may-1968-france]], [[the-1968-generation-germany]]) after its failure to achieve revolutionary aims: most of its leaders did not join Communist parties but sought new outlets. `contributed_to` by rising public awareness of industrial pollution and ecological risk — citizen initiatives (some 20,000 in West Germany alone by the mid-1970s), influential texts (Herbert Gruhl's *Ein Planet wird geplündert*, André Gorz's *Ecologie et politique*, translations of Rachel Carson's *Silent Spring* and Ivan Illich's *Tools for Conviviality*) — and by opposition to nuclear power plant construction specifically.

## Major Phases

Early-to-mid 1970s: countercultural, lifestyle-oriented activism (communes, health-food stores, alternative publishing) combined with single-issue campaigns (wilderness preservation, anti-highway construction, anti-nuclear-power). Individual "green" candidates begin standing in local elections in the early 1970s. Early 1980s: national Green parties form (West Germany's is the most consequential and best documented, with figures such as Petra Kelly and Joschka Fischer), explicitly structured as "antiparties" — rotating leadership after one term, no permanent campaign committee, refusal to join government coalitions — reflecting continued suspicion of conventional politics even while contesting elections. Through the 1980s the Greens function primarily as a protest party, their radical-democratic internal structure (the rotation principle above all) limiting legislative effectiveness even as it reinforced their claim to represent a genuine alternative.

## Geographic Spread

Strongest in West Germany, the Netherlands, and Scandinavia; present but less institutionally developed elsewhere in Western Europe during this period.

## Interaction

`analogous_to` the American and broader Western youth-culture currents of the same period. `contrasts_with` [[left-wing-terrorism-western-europe-1970s]] as the much larger, nonviolent channel for the same generational discontent. `contributed_to`, well beyond the Greens' own electoral results, a gradual mainstreaming of environmental consciousness across the Western European political spectrum — by the 1980s, factories faced sustained pressure to cut pollution and municipalities to manage waste more responsibly, and by the century's end Denmark led the world in wind-generated electricity (20% of national supply).

## End Conditions

Not a completed process as of the Paxton and Hessler narrative's end point (1989) — the Greens remain a protest party through the 1980s rather than a governing one in most countries; the process's institutionalization (e.g., German Green participation in national government) postdates this range and is covered by later chapters/sources.

## Historiography

Paxton and Hessler frame environmentalism as displacing Marxism as "the core philosophy of the European Left" by the 1970s–80s — a significant interpretive claim (see also [[eurocommunism]] for the parallel, related decline of Marxist orthodoxy among Western Communist parties in the same period). Key cited works: Michael Bess, *The Light-Green Society: Ecology and Technological Modernity in France, 1960–2000* (2003); Gerd Langguth, *The Green Factor in German Politics* (1986); Thomas Scharf, *The German Greens: Challenging the Consensus* (1994); Andrei Markovits, *The German Left: Red, Green, and Beyond* (1993).
