# War and Society in the Greek and Roman Worlds

The long-duration relationship between **organized violence and social structure** across Greek and Roman antiquity — who fought, who paid, who suffered, how military service shaped citizenship and status, and how conquest redistributed land and wealth. [[sabin-van-wees-whitby-cambridge-history-greek-roman-warfare-2007|CHGRW]]'s four "War and society" chapters (**van Wees**; **Lendon**; **Adams**; **Fear**) plus related state and historiographical chapters (**Hornblower**; **Hanson**; **Hunt**) are the primary spine; this process page holds the comparative synthesis.

## Definition and Scope

- **Is:** social recruitment, training norms, gender and war, veterans, civilian–military relations, cultural valorization or critique of war, demographic and agrarian consequences of campaigning and conquest.
- **Is not:** a narrative of wars; pure tactical history; modern "militarism" projected uncritically onto antiquity.

**Hornblower** (CHGRW I.2) defines a “militaristic” society as one geared virtually exclusively for war, with male citizens enjoying war for its own sake and military institutions determining civic ones; he argues that **neither the Greeks (not even Spartans/Macedonians) nor the Romans were militaristic**, though Romans sit further toward that end of the scale. Against Vernant, he follows van Wees: Greeks did not regard war as normal/natural. “Peace-loving cultured Greeks versus gory regimented Romans is a simplification. But it is a simplification rather than an outright lie.”

## Causal Drivers

- Polis and republican **citizen-soldier** ideologies and their limits.
- **Manpower regimes** (Spartan *agoge*; Roman *assidui* and allies; Hellenistic mercenary markets; imperial professional enlistment; late *foederati*).
- **Economic feedback:** booty, land settlement, taxation, and recruitment pressure under unequal spoils.
- **Honour and profit** as twin recognized causes of Greek war (van Wees); elite competition for glory and office at Rome (Lendon; Adams).

## Major Phases

### Archaic and classical Greece

**Van Wees** (CHGRW I.9) advances a core thesis that runs against war-totalizing readings: despite art, literature, booty dedications, and monuments, **war’s impact on Greek society was rather limited**. Social, political, and economic demands shaped warfare more than warfare shaped daily life, structures, or the economy. Outside Sparta and Crete, elites were proud amateurs of war; leisure-class ideal was a structural cause of war — plunder, conquest, and imperialism alone could extend leisure beyond a narrow non-productive elite (Plato *Resp.* 373). Pervasive competitiveness structured society; competition for wealth intensified resource pressure. Greeks framed unprovoked humiliation as *hybris* requiring revenge; **honour (*timē*) and profit** carried equal weight as causes, with honour the more respectable motive. Power (*hēgemonia/archē*) was a means to prestige and wealth, not the ultimate end. *Pleonexia* (“desire for more”) among the already prosperous, not absolute scarcity, was the central problem Greeks saw.

Most Greek warfare was short seasonal general levies (days/week in summer), with familiar companions and low danger — not WWI-style transformative total war. Military service did not automatically politicize: working-class Athenian hoplites lacked office rights until (and partly after) reforms; perioikoi, metic hoplites, and slave/serf rowers served without full rights. War’s ideological role justified elite power, subject ambitions, Greek superiority over “barbarians,” and male rule over women — explaining cultural prominence without remaking social structure. Hesiod’s peaceful farmers balance Homeric warrior ideology (*Certamen* awards Hesiod over Homer).

**Hornblower** challenges the orthodoxy that war was omnipresent (Athens at war two years in three from 490–338): epigraphic institutions of conflict-avoidance — foreign judges, arbitration, kinship diplomacy, federalism — are nearly silent in literature. Foreign judges (panels from polis C settling A–B disputes) are a classic case of an extremely common institution nearly invisible in literary texts (Robert). Arbitration decrees are better read as conflict-avoidance, often brokered by kings (with Ager), not as proof of endemic hostility (contra Ma). Kinship diplomacy exploiting mythical founder–daughter ties created peaceful relations across distinct poleis already in Herodotus’ and Thucydides’ time.

**Gender, slaves, prestige.** Male literary bias suppresses women’s active military role: set-piece phalanx battles exclude them, but in *Kleinkrieg*, sieges, and improvised defence (tiles, walls, hair for torsion engines; workshops at Carthage — App. *Pun.* 93) women’s participation was likely much commoner. **Hunt**: much more slave military participation than literary texts show — unconscious “censorship” because slave recruitment was ideologically awkward under *andres gar polis* (military service ≈ citizenship). Three modes: emergency freed infantry; naval rowers (Corinth, Corcyra, Chios, Syracuse, Athens — standard vs exceptional disputed); helots/*neodamodeis* as frequent Spartan infantry. Prestige hierarchy of arms did not track pure effectiveness: classical Athens valorised the hoplite above cavalry despite the navy’s greater strategic weight — mixed origins of naval crews prevented the navy from upstaging hoplite primacy. Elite units had political stakes (Argive 1,000 overthrowing democracy; Arcadian *eparitoi* class composition flipped with pay cuts).

### Hellenistic kingdoms and mid-Republican Rome

**Lendon** (CHGRW I.15) contrasts Hellenistic ubiquity of mercenaries (Taenarum as hiring fair) with mid-Republican Rome’s rare use of mercenaries — signaling different military cultures: Greeks more inclined to soldiering as **learned craft (*technē*)**; Romans more to **inherited virtue (*virtus*)**. If war prowess was learnable, birth/wealth lost claim to natural superiority — reducing deference and raising economic class conflict in fourth-century/Hellenistic *stasis*. Mercenaries reduced elite dependence on citizen shield-fellows; the rich could buy defence and demand power — helping oligarchic drift of Hellenistic democracies. Mid-Republic went to war nearly every year; *comitia centuriata* never refused a Senate-wanted war; 200–168 nearly **1/6 adult male citizens under arms yearly** without security threat; Second Punic War higher than 1/4 — so the average citizen spent ~1/6 of adult life in the army (campaign season). When/why Rome became singularly warlike remains open (pre-338?; fifth/early-fourth alarms?; conflict of orders?; alliance system?). Lendon’s metaphor: Romans shark, Greeks dolphin — both predators; Greek war as craft/choice vs Roman war as ground of masculine self-respect; Greeks hired mercenaries, Romans did not — *virtus* manned the legions.

**Sekunda**: Macedonian and Roman solutions to city-state military limits both extended citizenship/allied incorporation into military structures (vs exclusive classical Greek/Italian citizenship). Off-battlefield human costs rose with starvation sieges, assault casualties, and naval disasters (255: ~350 ships hit off Sicily, only 80 survived — tens of thousands dead). Hellenistic conventions of war expanded (Polybius: no Greek city destroyed for a century after Alexander), yet massacres still occurred especially in civil/rebellion contexts and against non-Greeks; Polybius was horrified by Roman cruelty. Traditional “manpower crisis”/disappearance of small farmers (Toynbee’s *Hannibal’s Legacy*) is rejected by Brunt and much survey archaeology; a clearer reason for recruit dearth is that Roman wars became **visibly less profitable** (Spain 151 vs Carthage 149 volunteers).

### Late Republic and Principate

**Adams** (CHGRW II.6) uses Sallust (*Iug.* 41) programmatically: before Carthage’s destruction, fear of external enemies kept Senate and People cooperative; after *metus hostilis* vanished came arrogance, split between nobility and people, spoils seized by generals, soldiers’ families driven from homes. Late-Republican warfare differed: multiple theatres; after 146 BC less glamorous wars; Social War, Marius–Sulla, Spartacus, Catiline, Caesar–Pompey, and post-Caesar civil wars had deep Italian roots in political competition that became armed struggle. Second-century wealth influx: temple-building mostly *ex manubiis*; after 167 BC *tributum* no longer collected in Italy. Personal fortunes (Crassus 200 million HS; Caesar’s Gallic income) dwarfed state management; governors expected profits (Verres; Cicero).

**Alston** (via CHGRW II.5): soldiers were political agents, not mere playthings — mutinous when rewards unpaid; unwilling to back Octavian’s coup against Antony (44) or fight fellow Caesarians at Brundisium (40). By the 40s BC military service meant a particular status — epigraphic self-identification as soldiers/veterans. If soldiering was survival for an extremely poor Italian stratum, land after discharge was meal-ticket and political power; mobilization of those interests was crucial to Republican destabilization. Roman assemblies required physical presence and could not represent 300,000–1,000,000 citizens legitimately; soldiers of a recently and bloodily unified Italy were unlikely to defer to a remote senatorial elite.

**Principate social engine.** Army <1% of population (>50 million); Rome’s million inhabitants (2× army size) were a harder logistical challenge; urbanization was the main provincial economic motor. Yet soldiers as the largest pre-industrial salaried labour force diffused monetized, credit-using economic behaviour — Tacitus’s Syrian legionaries as “sleek businessmen.” Provincial soldiers remained a distinct epigraphic social group; the bullying/rapacious soldier is not mere literary topos — governors’ edicts, Digest (Ulpian), New Testament, and Egyptian papyri (2,200 drachmas “for extortion”) document it. East vs west pattern: east — units in existing cities (Dura), less rural impact; west — *canabae* and *vici* at camps becoming markets and administrative centres, central to provincial urbanization (Bonn, Vienna, Budapest, Belgrade). Karanis archive shows soldiers/veterans integrated via family and village recruitment yet with natural barriers and tension. Citizenship via *diplomata*; military *officia* as template for late empire’s uniformed civilian bureaucracy — “even the civilian bureaucrats of the late Empire were militarily organized and wore military uniform.” Adams’s conclusion: Republic — citizens/allies benefited materially, aristocrats pursued prestige, competition collapsed Republic into autocracy; Principate standing army made war’s social effect more profound — from cities to villages the army was visible Roman power, policing and enabling provincial economies.

### Later Empire

**Fear** (CHGRW II.12): early imperial army was socially distant (frontier fortresses, volunteers, amateurs as commanders, low political weight); late empire transforms this — more numerous but smaller units (~1,100-man post-Diocletianic legions; smaller forts), deeper interior defences, billets and urban forts, making the army a **constant civilian presence** and psychologically a force for defence rather than guaranteed initiative. The state became “para-military”: civil service as *militia*, officials as “soldiers,” *cingulum* badge; yet army’s social esteem fell amid failures and costs — soldiers as “wolves not watchdogs” (Chrysostom/Themistius). Recruitment crisis was not purely demographic: even at high estimate ~550,000 men needing ~24,000 recruits/year (~1.5–2% of eligible males), burden was not intolerable; likelier causes are **status/pay collapse** (real pay maybe down ~80%) and higher chance of prolonged hard fighting. State responses: hereditary service for veterans’ sons; annual free-born conscription; barbarian recruiting; self-mutilation laws escalating after Adrianople (381: two mutilated = one whole recruit); *aurum tironicum* cash in lieu (Valens standardizes 30 solidi + 6 equipment); tattooing on enrollment; desertion laws escalating then effectively accommodating multi-year AWOL by 413.

Field-army billeting highly unpopular (house thirds; *cenaticum* extortion; sexual abuse); billeting abuses helped alienate Lazi and Abasgi from Rome toward Persia. Army as tax collector of last resort; Libanius alleges local troops protect villages from collectors for wine/meat — Fear reads advanced local integration rather than pure protection racket. By sixth century *numeri* commanders at Nessana/Syene act as local legal authorities. Elite hostility to Germans (Synesius; anti-Stilicho politics) coexists with lower-class ambivalence and cultural interchange. Final western blending: cash substitution for *annona* + unpaid units dissolve into communities, join *bucellarii*, or form local post-Roman forces (Olibriones; Aegidius–Syagrius; Birdoswald hall) — “ironically… the army emerged as a better defender of *Romanitas* after the collapse of Rome than it had while it survived.”

**Whitby** on religion: from tetrarchic *pax deorum* through Constantine, imperial security and victory remained gifts of correctly observed religion; Christianization did **not** produce pacifism but new pretexts (421 Persian war; Heraclius as holy war; Justinian *Nov.* 30). Correct worship = victory; triumphs shifted into church rites; bishops as urban war leaders; pacifist strand existed (Martin; Basil) but just-war arguments (Augustine) and crisis impressment of clergy prevailed — “Christianity was predominantly seen as a religion of war, led by a God of Battles.”

## Geographic Spread

Mediterranean core with continuous extension into temperate Europe, North Africa, and the Near East as Roman power expanded; local military societies (Thracian peltasts, Numidian cavalry, Gothic war-bands, Isaurians, Armenians) repeatedly incorporated.

## End Conditions

No single end — transforms with the disintegration of western imperial structures and the reconfiguration of eastern Roman military society; elements persist in early medieval and Byzantine regimes (Fear’s forward look: post-imperial local soldiers as paradoxical defenders of *Romanitas*).

## Woolf, *Rome: An Empire's Story* (2012)

[[woolf-rome-empires-story-2012|Greg Woolf]] supplies quantitative and institutional social-military claims for mid/late Republican Rome. Manpower intensity: between 10 and 25 percent of the male population under arms in any campaigning season (comparable to WWI European participation); in the worst Hannibalic years (218–215), one in six adult males died on the battlefield; political careers required military command; booty, triumphs, games, and temples involved the urban population. Institutional transformation of command/tactics: distant multi-year wars broke short summer campaigning; core shifted from phalanx spearmen to javelin-and-sword troops in maniples (~120) and cohorts (~400).

Late-Republic lethal innovation: increased recruitment of landless citizens made resettlement depend on generals—“Sulla’s army marched against Rome on the first occasion because they wanted eastern booty, and on the second in order to win land”; no Republican reform solved demobilization; Augustus later bound a standing army to emperors via a military treasury and fixed discharge bonuses. Slave-trade demand could drain allied manpower (Nicomedes of Bithynia refusing troops because subjects had been captured by traders).
