## Definition and Scope

The ***Reconquista*** ("reconquest") is the centuries-long, intermittent expansion of
the Christian kingdoms of northern Iberia southward at the expense of Muslim
[[al-andalus|al-Andalus]], conventionally dated from the eighth century
to the fall of Granada in 1492. [[luscombe-ncmh-v4-2004|NCMH IV]] (Hugh Kennedy,
Ch. 16) treats its decisive **eleventh- and twelfth-century phase**, when the collapse
of the [[caliphate-of-cordoba|Caliphate of Córdoba]] transformed the balance of power
in the peninsula. The term itself is partly an anachronistic, ideologically loaded
construct (it implies a continuous national-religious project that contemporaries did
not always share), but it names a real and consequential process.

## Causal Drivers

- **The fragmentation of al-Andalus.** After the Caliphate of Córdoba collapsed into
  the **taifa kingdoms** (c. 1010–1031), the Muslim south was divided into small,
  competing, and largely **demilitarised** polities — Seville, Toledo, Saragossa,
  Granada, Badajoz, and others — unable to resist the northern kingdoms.
- **The parias system.** From the mid-eleventh century the taifa kings bought immunity
  from attack by paying **parias** (tribute) to Christian rulers — an enormous transfer
  of Andalusi wealth north that funded Christian expansion, while the fiscal burden bred
  discontent in the taifas. As the deposed king Abd Allah of Granada saw, "the
  extraction of parias was the prelude to conquest."
- **Christian demographic and military growth**, frontier settlement (*repoblación* of
  the Duero and, later, the Tagus and Ebro valleys), and the importation of
  trans-Pyrenean [[crusades|crusading]] ideology and knights.

## Major Phases (11th–12th c.)

1. **The taifa exploitation and the fall of Toledo (to 1085).** Alfonso VI of
   León-Castile took **Toledo in 1085** — the first great Christian capture of a major
   Andalusi city and a watershed that "precipitated a general crisis in al-Andalus."
2. **The Almoravid reaction (1086–c. 1145).** The terrified taifa kings summoned the
   Berber **[[almoravids|Almoravids]]** from Morocco; Yusuf b. Tashfin crushed Alfonso
   VI at **Sagrajas/Zallaqa (1086)** and then annexed the taifas, halting the
   reconquest and re-uniting Muslim Spain under North African rule. This is the era of
   **[[el-cid|El Cid]]**, who carved out an independent Valencia (1094–1102).
3. **The Almohad period and the turning point (c. 1147–1212).** As Almoravid power
   decayed, a second taifa period gave way to the **[[almohads|Almohads]]**, who
   defeated Castile at **Alarcos (1195)** — but were decisively broken at **Las Navas
   de Tolosa (1212)**, opening the great thirteenth-century conquests (Córdoba 1236,
   Seville 1248) treated in [[abulafia-ncmh-v5-2000|NCMH V]].

## The Thirteenth-Century Climax (NCMH V)

[[abulafia-ncmh-v5-2000|NCMH V]] (David Abulafia, Chs. 17a–b) treats the long thirteenth
century as the **decisive phase**. The Christian victory at **Las Navas de Tolosa (1212)** broke
the [[almohads|Almohad]] army and exposed al-Andalus to "a process of fission" into weak taifa
statelets. The Christian kingdoms then made their greatest gains:

- **Castile** under **[[christian-kingdoms-of-spain|Ferdinand III]]** took **Córdoba (1236)**,
  **Jaén (1246)** and **Seville (1248)**, and reduced **Murcia** to a tributary (annexed
  1243–1266); **Alfonso X** consolidated and settled the conquests.
- The **[[crown-of-aragon|Crown of Aragon]]** under **James I** took the **Balearics
  (1229–1235)** and **Valencia (1238)**, turning Reconquista into Mediterranean empire.

The conquered Muslims sank into the degraded status of ***mudéjares***. Crucially, the advance
**stopped short of [[nasrid-granada|Granada]]**: protected by its sierras and by the lack of
outside support that doomed its neighbours, the Nasrid emirate survived as a Castilian tributary
— the great exception that would endure until 1492. Abulafia stresses that the collapse of the
unitary [[almohads|Almohad]] empire into the [[maghrib-successor-states|tripartite Maghrib]]
removed the North African counterweight that had twice (Almoravid, Almohad) reversed the
reconquest.

## Interaction

The Reconquista was assimilated to the [[crusades|crusade]] (papal indulgences for
Iberian fighters from the early twelfth century; the founding of peninsular
military orders — Santiago, Calatrava, Alcántara), drove the
expansion of [[christian-kingdoms-of-spain|León-Castile, Aragon, Navarre, and
Portugal]], and produced a distinctive **frontier society** of mixed conquest,
settlement, tribute, and the contested coexistence (*convivencia*) of Christians,
Muslims, and Jews.

## End Conditions

The process continued past this volume into the great thirteenth-century advance and
the long final phase ending with the conquest of **Granada (1492)** — the same year as
Columbus's first voyage, folding the Iberian frontier experience into Atlantic
expansion.

## The Completion: Granada 1492 (NCMH VII)

[[allmand-ncmh-v7-1998|NCMH VII]] (Angus MacKay, Ch. 24b) supplies the final act. After two and a
half centuries in which the [[nasrid-granada|Nasrid emirate of Granada]] survived as a tributary
client, the consolidated Castile of the **[[catholic-monarchs|Catholic Monarchs]]** waged the
systematic war of 1482–1492 that ended in the **[[fall-of-granada-1492|surrender of Granada]]**
(1 January 1492). MacKay stresses the conquest's **medieval, crusading and messianic** character
(Ferdinand cast as the prophesied *Encubierto* destined for Granada, all Islam and Jerusalem) — even
as it folded, in the same year, into the **expulsion of the Jews** and the launch of
[[age-of-discovery|Atlantic expansion]]. The Reconquista thus closed exactly as the European frontier
turned outward across the ocean — and as the [[ottoman-empire|Ottoman]] frontier advanced into the
Balkans at the Mediterranean's other end.

Alan Mikhail's [[mikhail-gods-shadow|*God's Shadow*]] builds on this conjunction a **contested**
argument: that the **crusading, "Moor-slaying" (*Matamoros*) mentality** of the Reconquista was
carried directly across the Atlantic — Columbus (a veteran of the Granada war) sailing as a
holy warrior against Islam, and the conquistadors recasting Native Americans through the same
anti-Muslim frame. For Mikhail the global rivalry with the [[ottoman-empire|Ottomans]] is the
key context of 1492; critics judge the causal claim overstated (see
[[ottoman-impact-on-european-expansion]]).

## From F. Donald Logan, *A History of the Church in the Middle Ages* (2013)

### Completion save Granada; contested framework

In the second half of the thirteenth century the Christian recovery of Muslim Iberian lands was complete save for Granada; Castile, Aragon, and Portugal took their place among the Christian kingdoms of medieval Europe. By Ferdinand III’s death (1252) Christian kings ruled almost all Iberia; Seville fell 1248; from the late thirteenth century only the emirate of Granada remained Muslim until 1492.

Logan stresses that use of “Reconquest of Spain” is long disputed and entangled with modern Spanish politics (Civil War 1936–39); exclusive emphasis distorts 711–1492 by sidelining social, economic, political, and ecclesiastical history. He denies reconquest was always all Christian rulers’ goal, notes frequent Christian–Muslim alliances against other Christians and impure motives, while affirming reconquest as a goal articulated soon after 711 (722 cave speech; chronicle ~883) though often vague and low priority. Reconquest is **one strand** among several: Cluny, papal legates from 1067, Cistercians/Augustinians/Premonstratensians, Dominicans (Spanish founder) and Franciscans, council attendance, Marian devotion, universities—the great accomplishment was reorienting Iberia into Christian western Europe (“without a Toledo there would not have been a Paris”).

### Dhimmi conditions; taifas; major milestones

After Muslim conquest, Christians in al-Andalus could practice religion without forced conversion but faced restrictions (no public Masses/processions or bells; higher taxes); many adopted Arabic ways (Mozarabs); reliable estimates suggest majority or near majority converted to Islam by the eleventh century. Fall of Cordova caliphate (1031) produced ~20 taifa states. Alexander II–encouraged Barbastro (1064): French “crusaders” distinguished by unspeakable barbarities after broken safe-conduct; Alfonso VI entered Toledo 25 May 1085 promising Muslim tolerance. Almoravids by 1094; El Cid’s Valencia until 1102; Almohads from 1146; Lisbon 1147 with ~10,000 crusaders; Las Navas de Tolosa 1212; conversions of Muslims apparently no more forced than earlier Christian conversions to Islam. Toledo after 1085 became the pre-eminent Arabic-to-Latin translation center enabling scholastic achievement.

## From Cobb, *The Race for Paradise* (2014)

[[cobb-race-for-paradise-2014|Cobb]] embeds the Iberian advance in a Mediterranean-wide Muslim reading of Frankish aggression (Sicily → al-Andalus → Syria), with Toledo **1085** as a civilizational-scale shock.

**Taifa collapse and the protection racket.** Umayyad caliphal unity disintegrated in the early eleventh century; civil war to **1031**; Hisham III expelled from Córdoba; dynasty ended, succeeded by taifa (“party”) kingdoms (Seville, Granada, Badajoz, Valencia, Balearics, Zaragoza, Toledo, etc.). Taifa culture flourished (Ibn Hazm; Saʿid al-Andalusi at Toledo, whose *Categories of Nations* specially damned Berbers and Spanish Christians—“Galicians”—who would transform al-Andalus after his death). Eleventh-century practice: taifa rulers bought Christian military aid with ruinous tribute (cash, land, plunder, Muslim slaves)—“no more and no less than a protection racket”; Christian kings competed for richest tributaries and squeezed with raids; Ibn Hazm denounced rulers for allying with Christians against Muslims.

**Fernando I and Frankish holy-war infusion.** Fernando I of León-Castile restored al-Maʾmun of Toledo for annual tribute (**1043**), raided when unpaid, took Zaragoza castles ~**1060**, squeezed Badajoz/Seville, seized Lamego **1057**, Viseu **1058**, Coimbra **1064**, Coria **1065**; died **1066** campaigning vs Valencia. Contemporaries: El Cid (Rodrigo Díaz) and Sisnando Davídiz freelanced across confessional lines. Parallel to Norman Sicily, Spanish lords conquered piecemeal with game-changing help from Frankish clergy/warriors carrying ideas of Church power and Christian holy war—via Santiago pilgrimage, dynastic marriage, Cluniac/monastic mobility.

**Barbastro 1064.** **1063**: Ramiro I of Aragon killed at Graus by Fernando’s army (including El Cid) called in by al-Muqtadir of Zaragoza. **1064** Barbastro: Franks (Thibaut of Châlon, Aquitaine, Catalans, Normans) with Sancho Ramírez; Pope Alexander II’s **1063** bull allegedly promised penance commutation for fighting in Spain (Cobb hedges “it seems”); a Muslim chronicler styles a leader “commander of the cavalry of Rome.” After ~month siege and water cut-off, terms of life/property for cash/slaves were accepted then broken—massacre, enslavement, plunder; Ibn Hayyan: king ordered general slaughter, **6,000+** Muslims killed (source figure); slaves to France and reportedly Constantinople. Short-term: al-Muqtadir reconquered months later with jihad volunteers. Long-term: deeper papal involvement drawing the Christian North into the Latin Frankish mainstream; returning Franks’ tales spurred more campaigns; locally shifted priorities from tribute to outright conquest—setting the stage for Alfonso VI and Toledo.

**Fall of Toledo, May 1085.** Under Dhu al-Nun (al-Maʾmun) Toledo was a major taifa; after **1075** weak al-Qadir lost Córdoba/Valencia, fled **1079**; Alfonso VI (“Emperor of All Spain”) restored him then, by treaty, was to receive Toledo with Valencia as al-Qadir’s compensation; the city resisted; Alfonso besieged from autumn **1084** (supplied by southern tributary taifas); **May 1085** Toledo surrendered; main mosque converted to cathedral against Alfonso’s will. Cobb: shock across the Muslim world—not a frontier port like Palermo but a major heartland kingdom, “the greatest city that the Christians had yet conquered from the Muslims anywhere”; strategically doomed remaining al-Andalus unless rolled back (Tagus settlement, free raiding); poet: garment of the peninsula fraying at the center. Packaged with Sicily’s fall as prelude to Frankish armies in Anatolia **1096**.

**Almoravid and Almohad reactions.** Taifa lords of Seville, Granada, Badajoz debated inviting the Almoravids; al-Muʿtamid of Seville: “Better to pasture camels than to herd swine.” **23 October 1086** Zallaqa (Sagrajas) defeat of Alfonso VI; from **1090** Almoravids deposed taifas (Granada/Malaga **1090**; Badajoz **1094**; Valencia **1102**; Zaragoza **1110**). Cobb’s thesis: Almoravids did little against the long-term Frankish threat—frontier essentially unchanged; last Muslim armies near the Pyrenees **1112** around Huesca; Alfonso el Batallador took Zaragoza **1118**, Tudela **1119**; benefits were Muslim unity veneer and end of *parias*. Almohads took Fez then Marrakesh **1147**, crossed to al-Andalus; Second Crusade–linked Iberian operations took Lisbon (**1147**, English-route crusader fleet), Almería (**17 Oct 1147**; Almohads retook **1157**), Tortosa (**Dec 1148**), Lérida (**Nov 1149**). Mid-12th c.: Christian advance stalled ~**1150** with Tagus as effective boundary for the next century—inter-Christian conflict as much as Muslim resilience—yet Lisbon/Tortosa showed Iberian kings integrated into Christendom’s mainstream with European resources, while Almohads were isolated from the broader *dar al-islam*; “the Franks truly had put nearly the entire Abode of Islam, from Spain to Syria, under siege.” Almohad high-water Alarcos **1195**; **16 July 1212** Las Navas de Tolosa (al-ʿIqab) ended effective Almohad power (chronicler blames unpaid troops’ intentional non-fight).

**Subject Muslims; later phases.** Spain developed Mudejar (*al-mudajjan*, “domesticated”) vs Mozarab terminology (Andalusi-specific); no consistent conversion policy. **1264–66** mudejar revolt crushed—expulsion/killing; by late **1260s** few Muslims remained in Christian territory; refugees crowded into Granada. **1340** Rio Salado crushed joint Nasrid–Merinid army; Merinid withdrawal permanent; Algeciras fell **1344** (first recorded cannon use on the peninsula by Muslim defenders). Final act: Zahara **Dec 1481** (last Muslim victory); internal Nasrid split (Boabdil vs al-Zaghal) met Fernando and Isabella’s planned war; Granada surrendered **25 November 1491** at Santa Fe (formal Alhambra handover followed); Ferdinand broke capitulations, forced conversions → revolts and emigrations to North Africa and the Ottoman Empire.
