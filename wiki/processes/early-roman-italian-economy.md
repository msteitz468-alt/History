# The Early Roman and Italian Economy

## Definition and Scope

The economic development of Italy from the arrival of Greek colonists in the eighth
century BCE through the crisis year of 133 BCE, drawing on **Jean-Paul Morel**'s chapter
in Scheidel, Morris & Saller, *The Cambridge Economic History of the Greco-Roman World*
(2007). Morel deliberately avoids the "primitivism vs. modernism" historiographical
framing as "old, schematic, and endless," preferring to trace concrete archaeological
developments — colonization patterns, amphora typology, coinage, infrastructure — rather
than adjudicate the ancient-economy debate (see [[concepts/ancient-economy]]). This page
covers Magna Graecia, Etruria, and Rome's own rise to Italian hegemony and the beginnings
of the slave-based economy; its direct sequel is [[concepts/roman-economy-late-republic]].

## Causal Drivers

- **Greek colonization in three networks.** Euboean/Chalcidian, Corinthian, and Achaean
  colonizing networks settled Magna Graecia, with colonies classifiable by function:
  commercial (Pithekoussai, Himera), agrarian (Cumae, Catania, Metapontum, Sybaris,
  Kroton, Poseidonia), defensive (Parthenope), and port/route-control (Zancle, Rhegium,
  Naxos). Pithekoussai (Ischia), founded c. 770 BCE — the first western Greek colony —
  was long read as a purely trading/ironworking settlement, but excavation at Punta
  Chiarito now shows agrarian activity too. Sybaris and Poseidonia, similarly long assumed
  purely agrarian "commercial dwarfs," are now argued (via amphora studies showing
  "Corinthian B" amphoras were actually local production) to have exported wine at scale.
- **Etruscan city-differentiated economies.** Caere and Vulci specialized in
  export-oriented agriculture (Etruscan wine dominated the Marseille market through the
  first half of the sixth century BCE); Vetulonia and Populonia specialized in mining and
  metallurgy; Tarquinia and Pisa functioned as trade-network cities. Etruscans built the
  *cuniculi* subterranean drainage tunnels later adopted by Romans for land improvement in
  the Pontine plain from the fourth century BCE, but were slow, limited coin users —
  relying on pre-monetary bronze ingots (*aes rude*, "ramo secco" ingots) from perhaps the
  sixth century, with the first "regular" gold/silver coinage only from Populonia in the
  mid-fifth century BCE.
- **Rome's rise to Italian hegemony.** Traditional founding (753 BCE) coincides with the
  first Greek Italian settlements and the Etruscan "orientalizing" period; Rome was
  initially just one Latinized, Etruscan-influenced city ("the great Rome of the
  Tarquins") among many. A fifth-century crisis of impoverishment via continuous warfare
  gave way to fourth-century expansion, land access, and rising living standards.

## Major Phases

- **Chronic grain weakness (5th century BCE).** Rome imported grain (*frumentationes*)
  from Etruscans, the Volsci, Cumae, and Syracuse well before its territorial expansion;
  early agrarian legislation (486 BCE, and the *lex Licinia Sextia* of 367 BCE limiting
  per-citizen land and cattle holdings) responded to the fact that the standard *heredium*
  land grant (2 *iugera*, roughly 0.5 ha) could not feed a family alone.
- **Coinage's slow arrival.** Sequence runs from barter to cattle-money (*pecunia*) to
  bronze ingots (*aes rude*, *aes formatum*, *aes signatum*), possibly stimulated by the
  introduction of military pay (*stipendium*) in 406 BCE; genuinely Roman minted coinage,
  and Rome's first mint, date to 269 BCE (the *triumviri monetales* were created earlier,
  290/289 BCE); the denarius's own date remains disputed, proposed anywhere from 269–187 BCE.
- **The Great Latin Colonization (from mid-4th century BCE).** Ostia (mid-4th c.), Antium
  (338), Cales (334), Cosa and Paestum (273), Placentia and Cremona (219). By 200 BCE Rome
  and its allies controlled roughly 130,000 km² with about 3 million free inhabitants
  (Rome proper: ~30,000 km², ~1 million citizens). Rome's wars of hegemony in this period —
  the First (343–341) and Second (327–304) Samnite Wars, dissolution of the Latin League
  (338 BCE), the Pyrrhic War's aftermath (decline/desertification in Bruttium and
  Lucania), the Gallic Wars in the Po valley (236–218), and war with the Ligures
  (238–233, ending with the founding of Genoa) — mark the military side of this expansion.
  The Via Appia (312 BCE) opened a deliberate Roman road-building program, though roads
  served administrative/strategic more than economic ends; river transport (Tiber, Anio,
  Arno, Po) remained highly important.
- **First Punic War (264–241 BCE) — stagnation, not growth.** Despite victory and the
  annexation of Sicily and Sardinia, Morel argues the war produced economic stagnation in
  Italy — a sharp contrast with the transformation triggered by the Second Punic War.
- **Second Punic War aftermath (post-201 BCE) — infrastructural and social
  transformation.** Puteoli founded as a colony in 194 BCE, becoming Rome's chief port
  until Ostia's first-century-CE expansion; a new *emporium* river harbor on the Tiber's
  left bank (192 BCE) replaced the old Forum Boarium harbor; the Porticus Aemilia
  warehouse (193–174 BCE, 487m × 60m, ~30,000 m²) was built using *opus caementicium*
  (Roman concrete) — cheap materials and low-skill labor under skilled supervision — with
  Monte Testaccio beginning to form nearby from discarded amphora shards; a new *macellum*
  market was established by the censors of 179 BCE. Greco-Italic amphoras (from the mid-4th
  century) had already marked a shift of Italy's economic core from Magna
  Graecia/Sicily toward Rome-centered Tyrrhenian Italy; they were later replaced by Dressel
  1A amphoras (mid-2nd century BCE), exported "in the tens of millions" by the late
  Republic. The *lex Claudia* (219/218 BCE) barred senators and their sons from owning
  ships over 300-amphorae capacity (~80 hectoliters) — possibly to confine the senatorial
  class to a landed-aristocracy role; Morel argues against reading this as evidence of a
  broader Republican "colonial preference" in trade patterns, finding none.
- **Emergence of the "slave mode of production" (from the early 2nd century BCE).**
  Driven by conquest, piracy, and slave-breeding, and concentrated in central Tyrrhenian
  Italy (Etruria–Campania, centered on Rome); associated with standardized mass production
  (Campanian A pottery, most widely distributed Mediterranean terracotta ware at its peak,
  c. 250–50 BCE, with an estimated 9 million pieces exported to Gaul alone over 150 years)
  and *opus caementicium* construction. Delos was a hub of the slave trade — Strabo (14.5.2)
  reports "myriad" (10,000) slaves sold there per day, with the "agora of the Italians"
  likely its slave market; Polybius (via Strabo) reports 40,000 slaves working Cartagena's
  silver mines. First Roman villas, precursors to the second-century Catonian slave villa,
  appeared in central Italy by at least the third century BCE; Cato's *De agri cultura*
  reflects the shift toward cash-crop (olive, wine) agriculture, reinforced by the
  Senate's decision to have the Carthaginian Mago's agronomic treatise translated into
  Latin as the standard authority on olive and vine cultivation after Carthage's defeat.
  The Elogium of Polla inscription, boasting that a magistrate "made shepherds give way to
  farmers" on *ager publicus*, evidences contested land use (pastoralism vs. arable) at the
  end of the period.
- **The pre-Gracchan crisis (down to 133 BCE).** Adult male citizen counts rose from
  258,000 (188 BCE) to 337,000 (163 BCE), then dropped to 318,000 by 135 BCE — read by
  Morel as a symptom of the social/agrarian crisis that produced Tiberius Gracchus's
  tribunate and assassination (133 BCE).

## Geographic Spread

Magna Graecia and Sicily; Etruria; Latium and Campania as the emerging Tyrrhenian core;
extending via colonization across the Italian peninsula and, through trade (Marseille,
Delos), into the wider Mediterranean.

## Interaction with Other Processes

Feeds directly into [[concepts/roman-economy-late-republic]] (Harris's chapter on the
post-133 BCE economy) and the broader [[processes/slavery]] process, of which the "slave
mode of production" here is the formative Republican-Italian phase. Contemporaneous with,
but structurally distinct from, the [[hellenistic-economy]] to the east.

## End Conditions

The period closes not with a clean institutional break but with the social and
demographic strain — falling citizen numbers, land concentration — that culminates in
Tiberius Gracchus's 133 BCE tribunate, conventionally read (including by Morel) as the
watershed between an early-Republican economy of colonization and consolidation and the
imperial, slave-intensive late-Republican economy analyzed in
[[concepts/roman-economy-late-republic]].
