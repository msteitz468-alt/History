## Definition and Scope

African urbanization designates the historical process by which sub-Saharan and North African populations shifted from predominantly rural to increasingly urban settlement patterns, producing new built environments, social institutions, economic structures, and cultural forms. This page is distinct from [[urbanization]] (the global-level concept page). It focuses on Africa's distinctive urban history from the nineteenth century to the present, including the nineteenth-century cosmopolitan port towns, the colonial city, postcolonial urban growth, and the emergence of African urban studies as a field.

In 1960, more than four out of five Africans lived in the countryside. Africa remains the world's least urbanized continent but has experienced explosive urbanization rates since the 1950s: by 2020, it was projected to contain an absolute majority of urban dwellers. Cairo and Lagos are the continent's only megacities (over 10 million), with Kinshasa projected to be the largest African city by 2025. This dramatic transformation — "one lifetime" from rural to majority-urban — is the demographic framework for the entire modern history of African urban culture.

## Causal Drivers

**Long-distance trade networks** (Indian Ocean, Atlantic, trans-Saharan) were the primary engines of pre-colonial African urbanism. Swahili coast towns (Mombasa, Zanzibar, Kilwa), West African forest-zone city-states (Kumasi, Abomey, Ibadan), and Sudanic Islamic cities (Kano, Khartoum, Timbuktu) existed as nodes in trade-driven urban systems long before European conquest.

**The nineteenth century** reconfigured established networks and created new urban forms. The Sokoto Caliphate (founded 1804) strengthened Islam as urban glue across Hausaland. Yorubaland's civil wars gave rise to Ibadan — the great city of warriors and refugees. Khartoum (founded 1820s), Kampala, and Addis Ababa (founded 1886) were new capitals of indigenous or Egyptian imperial projects. Zanzibar grew dramatically as a hub of the Omani commercial empire and slave-worked clove plantations, reaching a population of c. 50,000 by the 1870s with its characteristic spatial division between Stone Town and the poorer Ng'ambo district.

**Nineteenth-century cosmopolitanism** was the characteristic mode of major port towns. Alexandria was refounded by Muhammad Ali in the 1820s as the gateway of modernising Egypt, attracting Greek, Italian, Jewish, Levantine, and indigenous Egyptian migrants and reaching 500,000 by World War I. Cape Town developed contrasting urban cultures across Dutch burghers, Coloured free labourers, and slaves, with Islam taking hold as a community force after emancipation in the 1830s. West African coastal ports (Accra, Ouidah, Lagos, Freetown) were eclectic outposts where local African rulers, European trading forts, Euro-African mixed communities, and liberated slave settlers all competed over the terms of urban life. Jeremy Prestholdt's *Domesticating the World* traces restless consumerism in Mombasa and Zanzibar as a form of African cosmopolitan agency: Sultan Barghash's "House of Wonders" in Zanzibar's waterfront (1880s) displayed exotic manufactures and scientific instruments but set its four large clocks to Zanzibari time — "daybreak as one o'clock" — a telling emblem of customized cosmopolitanism.

**Colonial urbanisation** from the 1880s imposed a more ideologically structured urbanism: the "colonial city" as a projection of European power. Colonial capitals originated in three ways: (1) planted on older African towns (Lagos, Accra, Kampala); (2) grown from existing colonial outposts (Dakar, Luanda, Cape Town); (3) entirely new creations from military camps or communication nodes (Nairobi, Salisbury/Harare, Brazzaville, Léopoldville/Kinshasa). Racial segregation, sanitation laws, labour controls, and pass systems structured colonial urban space. Yet demographic reality repeatedly defeated planners: Casablanca's Muslim population grew from half of 160,000 in 1921 to 69% of 473,000 by independence, with spontaneous *bidonvilles* ("tin-can towns," a term coined in Casablanca in the early 1920s) encircling the planned French city.

**The Rhodes-Livingstone Institute** anthropologists (1940s) were the first social scientists to systematically study the new urban life of Copperbelt mining towns — the "social situation" of migrant workers creating ethnic associations, dance societies, burial clubs, and new forms of sociability. Georges Balandier's *Sociologie des Brazzavilles noires* (1955) documented the restless, rapidly changing indigenous life in the Equatorial African capital, at odds with the orderly colonial vision.

**Postcolonial urbanisation** accelerated dramatically after independence. Colonial capitals became undisputed primate cities. New capitals were built (Dodoma in Tanzania, Abuja in Nigeria, Yamoussoukro in Côte d'Ivoire) partly as acts of anti-urban ideology or personal prestige. Socialist-oriented regimes in Ghana and Burkina Faso razed central markets to root out women traders deemed "profiteers." Urban growth created the contemporary crisis of megacity poverty, infrastructure collapse, and slum expansion (Mike Davis's *Planet of Slums* framing) alongside celebrated resilience and inventiveness.

## Major Phases

**Phase 1 — Pre-Colonial Urban Systems (before 1880).** Sub-Saharan cities as nodes of trade, religion, and political power. Cosmopolitan port towns, Islamic Sudanic cities, forest-zone Yoruba and Akan urban centres. Limited in demographic scale (15,000–50,000) but rich in cultural complexity.

**Phase 2 — The Colonial City (1880–1945).** European conquest reorganises urban hierarchies. New primate capitals rise; older cities often decline. Built-environment studies, racial segregation, and the emergence of African urban studies in its social-science form. The first generation of urban migrants creates new institutions: ethnic associations, dance societies, syncretic music styles (highlife in Gold Coast; marabi in South Africa; taarab on the Swahili coast).

**Phase 3 — Decolonisation and the Urban Nationalist Moment (1945–1970).** Urban space as the principal arena of mass nationalism. "It was in the towns that Europeans first lost control of Africa" (John Iliffe on Dar es Salaam). 1948 Accra riots; Apartheid election in South Africa that same year. Workers' strikes, political mobilisation, and the birth of nationalist parties in urban contexts.

**Phase 4 — Postcolonial Urban Explosion (1970–present).** Rural-urban migration accelerates. Cities grow faster than infrastructure or formal employment can absorb. Urban dystopia becomes a dominant frame (Lagos, Kinshasa, Luanda as emblems of crisis). But counter-frames emerge: AbdouMaliq Simone on "apparent constraints" pointing to "unforeseen possibilities in livelihood and in the elaboration of vibrant urban cultures." Dar es Salaam, neglected in the historiography until recently, emerges as East Africa's largest city (c. 3 million) and a site of research on beni ngoma, dansi, and bongo flava music, as well as contested national cultures.

## Geographic Spread

Urban growth is uneven across regions:
- **North Africa**: Cairo and Alexandria have multi-millennial urban histories; Casablanca's explosive twentieth-century growth is the paradigmatic colonial-to-postcolonial story.
- **West Africa**: Yoruba towns (Lagos, Ibadan, Kumasi) and Hausa cities (Kano) were pre-colonial urban centres that were incorporated into and transformed by colonialism. The railway's arrival in Kumasi (1903) enabled its regeneration; the French deliberately sited Dahomey's railway at Bohicon rather than royal Abomey — producing Kumasi's megacity status and Abomey's relative stagnation.
- **East Africa**: Zanzibar, Mombasa, Dar es Salaam, Nairobi, and Kampala all emerged in distinct ways. Swahili coastal urbanism has deep pre-colonial roots; Nairobi and Dar es Salaam are colonial creations.
- **Central Africa**: Brazzaville and Léopoldville/Kinshasa — the "twinned capitals" eying each other across the Congo River — are the cities whose postcolonial trajectories have attracted the most extreme utopia/dystopia readings.
- **Southern Africa**: Johannesburg (founded 1886) outstripped Cape Town within years of its founding on mineral wealth; both display extreme racial-spatial legacies of colonial and Apartheid planning.

## Interaction

- **[[urbanization]]**: Africa's trajectory is both continuous with (rural migrants → urban explosion → infrastructure stress) and divergent from (colonial racial segregation, postcolonial patrimonialism, climate vulnerability) global patterns.
- **[[education-and-literacy-in-africa]]**: Urban space generated literacy institutions — mission schools, literary clubs, vernacular presses — that in turn accelerated urbanisation. The urban-educated elite shaped anti-colonial nationalism.
- **[[colonial-rule-in-africa]]**: Colonial urban planning was an exercise of power, but its hegemonic designs were consistently subverted by African urban practices.
- **[[youth-in-african-history]]**: Urban compounds and neighbourhoods were where rural migrants' youth identity was forged anew — in dance societies, gangs, football clubs, and labour associations.
- **[[women-and-gender-in-african-history]]**: The colonial city was overwhelmingly male in its formal institutions, yet women created economic niches in food trade, beer brewing, domestic services, and entertainment.
- **[[health-and-healing-in-africa]]**: Urban sanitation crises, epidemics, and the concentration of migrant workers drove early colonial public health interventions; the postcolonial city is central to the AIDS epidemic.
- **[[indian-ocean-trade]]**: The pre-colonial cosmopolitanism of Swahili coast towns is inseparable from this long-distance trade system.
- **[[trans-saharan-trade]]**: North African and Sudanic urbanism was structured around trans-Saharan commercial flows.

## End Conditions

Ongoing. Africa's urban transition is the largest and fastest in history. The challenge of the coming decades is to write African urban history with the sophistication of European or American urban historiography — a literature in which, as John Parker notes in [[parker-reid-oxford-modern-african-history-2013]], "more has probably still been written on the history of Paris, London, or New York than on all of Africa's cities combined."

## Historiography

**Historiographical status**: Urban history is widely considered underdeveloped within African historiography. Historians of Africa long wrote about things that happened in cities rather than about cities themselves. The decade since David Anderson and Richard Rathbone's landmark 2000 volume *Africa's Urban Past* has seen significant advances: Bill Freund's *The African City: A History* (2007) is the standard overview; Brennan, Burton, and Lawi, eds., *Dar es Salaam* (2007) exemplifies the new city-specific scholarship.

**Key debates**: (1) Were pre-colonial African towns genuinely "urban" or merely large villages? (Lonsdale argues many interior towns lacked institutions distinct from rural governance; Macola's analysis of Kazembe's *musumba* suggests meaningful urban differentiation.) (2) Did colonial built environments primarily serve hegemonic control? (Some scholars yes; others — Cohen and Eleb on Casablanca — argue hybrid appropriations worked in both directions.) (3) Is the contemporary African city best read as dystopia or as space of resilience and invention? (4) How to integrate economic and cultural approaches — economic history of African cities has receded since the 1990s cultural turn.

**Urban culture and arts**: The turn to "urban imaginaries" (Mamadou Diouf's term: "beliefs, practices, histories, fears, phantasms, and readings of the world") draws on Benjamin's *Arcades Project* and Henri Lefebvre but risks neglecting economic and historical analysis in favour of the spectacular contemporary. Music is a central theme: photography from the 1870s; "highlife," marabi, taarab in the interwar period; postcolonial Congolese pop painting (Chéri Samba's canvasses on the "vulgarity" of Kinshasa).

**Collection coverage**: Moderate for North Africa and West Africa; improving for East Africa (Dar es Salaam) and southern Africa (Johannesburg, Cape Town). Weakest for Francophone Central Africa.
