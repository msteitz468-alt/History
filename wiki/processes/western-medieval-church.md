# Western Medieval Church

## Definition and Scope

The **western medieval church** is the long process by which Latin Christianity became the dominant religious institution of western Europe from the end of antiquity to c.1500: converting new peoples, building a papal and episcopal hierarchy, generating monastic and mendicant forms of life, defining orthodoxy against dissent, and binding disparate societies through shared rite and Latin learning. This page is the process spine for [[logan-history-of-the-church-in-the-middle-ages-2013|Logan's survey]]; it is **not** a history of Eastern Orthodoxy or of Islam except where they shape the Latin story.

## Causal Drivers

- Mission from Rome and the Irish/English monasteries
- Alliance and conflict with kings and emperors ([[gregorian-reform]], [[investiture-contest]])
- Urban growth, schools, and [[medieval-university|universities]]
- Lay demand for apostolic poverty ([[waldensians]], [[francis-of-assisi]], [[mendicant-orders]])
- Catastrophe and consolation in the fourteenth century ([[black-death]], [[purgatory]])

## Major Phases

1. **Late antique foundations** — Constantine to Gregory I
2. **Conversion and Carolingian order** — missions; Franco-papal alliance; Viking disruption
3. **Reform, schism, crusade** — eleventh–twelfth centuries
4. **Papal high tide and mendicants** — Innocent III; friars; Lateran IV
5. **Crisis and redefinition** — Avignon; Great Schism; Constance; fifteenth-century piety

## Geographic Spread

From Mediterranean core to Ireland, Anglo-Saxon England, Germany, Scandinavia, and the Iberian Reconquista frontier; always with local custom under Latin norms.

## Interaction

Interacts with [[crusades]], [[reconquista]], state formation, and [[conciliarism]]; contrasts with Byzantine Christianity after [[east-west-schism-1054]].

## End Conditions

No sharp end: Logan closes pedagogically at **1492** inside a longer transition toward Reformation and global mission.

## From Logan, History of the Church in the Middle Ages (2013) — survey synthesis

Logan’s second edition is the narrative map behind this process page:

1. **Latin West unit; shared Christendom.** One Creed, Latin learned culture, and comparable liturgy from Iceland to Sicily; local custom as flavour. Judaism and Islam appear as interacting monotheisms (persecution, crusade, coexistence), not as full parallel histories.
2. **Benchmarks after c.1050.** Strong papacy often in struggle with rulers; separation from Greek-speaking Christians ([[east-west-schism-1054]]); ideal of war against “infidels” ([[crusades]], [[first-crusade-1095]]).
3. **Two sides of the church.** Institutional (papal-imperial politics, [[papal-monarchy]]) and devotional (eucharistic seeing of the host; [[medieval-marian-devotion]]; village churches as community centres) are both essential.
4. **Apostolic poverty fork.** Lay hunger for evangelical simplicity produces both condemned dissent ([[waldensians]], [[catharism]]) and approved [[mendicant-orders]] ([[francis-of-assisi]], [[dominic-de-guzman]]); [[fourth-lateran-council-1215]] legislates annual confession and orthodoxy.
5. **Legacies still standing.** [[medieval-university|Universities]] and Gothic cathedrals ([[gothic-architecture]], [[chartres-cathedral]]) as durable institutional and physical inheritances.
6. **Fourteenth-century crucible.** [[great-famine-1315|Great Famine]], [[black-death]], [[purgatory]] as pastoral answer, [[avignon-papacy]], then [[great-schism-1378]] and [[council-of-constance-1414]].
7. **Fifteenth century not a “waning.”** Vibrant lay piety ([[devotio-moderna]], English parish calendar, Italian civic charity); popes recover constitutional supremacy amid princely Italian politics; Epilogue **1492** (Granada, Jewish expulsion, Columbus) as pedagogical close.
