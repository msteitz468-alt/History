## Definition and Scope

This process covers the transformation of religious life, church-state relations, and the conceptual boundaries of the "secular" and the "religious" in Germany between the founding of the Second Empire (1871) and the collapse of the Third Reich (1945). It encompasses the three major confessional communities — Protestant (approximately two-thirds of the population), Catholic (approximately one-third), and Jewish (one to two percent) — as well as dissenting movements, quasi-religious volkisch groups, and the theological crisis provoked by National Socialism. It does not treat religion as simply declining under modernity; a central finding of recent scholarship is that the presumed "secularization" of this period was much more complex and contested than contemporary or early sociological observers recognized.

Source: [[smith-oxford-modern-german-history-2011]], Chapter 20 (Rebekka Habermas, "Piety, Power, and Powerlessness: Religion and Religious Groups in Germany, 1870–1945").

## Causal Drivers

**Wilhelmine reconfiguration of secular and religious**: The founding of the Second Empire in 1871 entangled Protestantism with national identity in ways that repositioned mainstream Lutheranism within the "secular" realm of legitimate public life. Simultaneously, Catholicism, Orthodox Judaism, Pietists, and Awakened Protestants were coded as belonging to a "religious" sphere carrying negative connotations of irrationalism, superstition, and foreign loyalty. This rearrangement was not a theological verdict but a political one, driven by liberal Protestant assumptions baked into Bismarckian governance.

**The Kulturkampf (1871–1887)**: A cluster of Prussian laws expelled or curtailed Jesuit, Redemptorist, and other religious orders; placed seminary education under state oversight; and introduced civil marriage (1874). The Kulturkampf "in nuce" revealed the reconceptualization at work: Catholicism was to be marginalized, associated with the irrational-religious, while Protestantism was divested of its explicitly religious character and absorbed into the national-secular order. Most Kulturkampf laws were subsequently repealed, but the symbolic damage to Catholic standing within the national community persisted.

**Liberal anti-Catholicism**: Following Michael Gross, liberalism's self-definition depended on the construction of Catholicism as anti-modern, anti-rational, and antithetical to bourgeois individualism. Liberals associated Catholicism with "superstition, obscure practices, and militant subordination" — the same charges placed against Orthodox Jews and Catholic-adjacent groups.

**Associational explosion**: Catholic associational life grew dramatically after 1848, with between one-quarter and one-third of all Catholics belonging to at least one association by 1914, and a "springtime of religious orders" — especially for women — producing Catholic social infrastructure in schools, hospitals, soup kitchens, and media. Protestants saw a parallel but smaller-scale wave. Jewish associations also multiplied.

**Crisis of classical modernity (Weimar)**: The Republic (1919–1933) produced three important religious shifts: (1) large-scale exits from the Protestant church (almost three million by 1932), primarily by urban men; (2) pluralization through dissenting sects and quasi-religious movements; (3) intensified anti-Semitic conflict linked to economic catastrophe and the arrival of Eastern European Jewish refugees.

## Major Phases

### Phase 1: Wilhelmine reconfiguration (1870–1918)

Contrary to secularization theory, the evidence shows not a general decline in piety but a selective and gendered pattern. Catholic mass attendance held steady or increased. Protestant attendance declined among men but increased among women, especially in deaconesses' houses and female associations. The much-discussed "decline of faith" was primarily a public discourse projecting middle-class anxieties about the urban working class onto religion in general.

Missions became a second major vector: Protestant missionary societies founded from the 1820s onward, joined by Catholic missionary orders after the Kulturkampf's waning, structured German perceptions of non-European peoples — projecting an image of African and Asian "heathendom" that reinforced both colonial legitimacy and racialized hierarchies at home.

The anti-Semitism dispute (*Antisemitismusstreit*) of 1879–1881, sparked by historian Heinrich von Treitschke's characterization of Jews as "German-speaking Orientals," crystallized the conditional terms on which Jewish belonging in the nation was negotiated: assimilation into Protestant bourgeois culture was the price of entry into the "secular" realm; Ostjuden (*Treitschke*: "ambitious trouser-selling youths … forcing their way across our eastern border from the inexhaustible Polish cradle") were coded as belonging to the negatively-valued religious sphere.

### Phase 2: Weimar pluralization and crisis (1919–1933)

The Republic's constitution for the first time contained no explicit Christian reference and formally separated church from state, ending the traditional patronage of Landeskirchen by Protestant princes. This produced a "massive loss of power" for Protestantism felt especially acutely among educated middle-class men. The response: a proliferation of quasi-religious alternatives — Salvation Army, Baptists, Mennonites, New Apostolic Church, Wandervogel, spiritualistic séances, nudist and vegetarian communes, and on the radical fringe, avowedly völkisch and anti-Semitic esoteric groups that drew directly on heathen and race-mystical elements. The publisher Eugen Diederichs wrote in 1929 of "an instinctive, unexpressed yearning for religious renewal, since ecclesiastical Protestantism in its present form has become barren for the life of the people (Volksleben) and, hence, also barren for the coming people's community (Volksgemeinschaft)."

The Catholic Church's position improved relatively: its wartime enthusiasm (1914) had brought it within the national-secular orbit, and its dense associational milieu offered cohesion unavailable to fragmented Protestantism.

Jewish life became increasingly precarious. Growing economic discrimination, anti-Semitic riots, and the influx of Ostjuden fleeing pogroms strained the delicate coexistence of integration and separate community. Zionism emerged partly as a response to the contradiction between liberal promises of inclusion and the daily experience of exclusion.

### Phase 3: Churches under National Socialism (1933–1945)

**Catholics**: The Center Party voted for the Enabling Act; Cardinal Faulhaber of Munich welcomed the Reichskonkordat (July 1933) with relief. Catholic membership remained stable throughout the Third Reich. The Church's self-understanding as victim of persecution (a legacy of Kulturkampf martyrology) blinded it to the far more serious persecution of Jews; Catholic anti-Judaism reinforced this blindness (a Bishop of Limburg wrote in 1939: "The Jewish people is guilty of the death of God"). The Church protested effectively against the T-4 euthanasia program (1939–1941) but remained largely silent about anti-Semitic legislation, Kristallnacht (1938), and the Holocaust. Pope Pius XI's encyclical *Mit brennender Sorge* (1937) defended the Church's autonomy and implicitly criticized race-worship but was concerned with Catholic institutional survival.

**Protestants**: The "Faith Movement of German Christians" (*Glaubensbewegung Deutscher Christen*, founded 1932) declared goals of "racial purity," anti-Marxism, and "dejudaization" of the church. Ludwig Müller was appointed *Reichsbischof* in late 1933. In response, Martin Niemöller's "Young Reformist Movement" became the *Bekennende Kirche* (Confessing Church), which invoked the Gospel against any "other masters" beyond Christ and published a 1936 memorandum denouncing de-Christianization, anti-Semitism, concentration camps, and Gestapo despotism. Neither church constituted a systematic resistance; both showed extensive conformity. The *Bekennende Kirche*'s struggle was primarily identity-defensive, not broadly anti-fascist.

**Post-1945 self-presentation**: Both churches issued statements in 1945 that generated misleading images of active resistance. The Lutheran Stuttgart Declaration acknowledged failing to "profess more courageously" while implying sustained combat against Nazism. The Catholic hierarchy immediately instructed dioceses to document priests' resistance. These narratives — still contested by historians — inaugurated a final reconceptualization in which the "religious" sphere became positively valued as a domain beyond worldly corruption, and Judaism was for the first time recognized as an equally legitimate part of German religious heritage.

## Geographic Spread

The process was primarily national in scope but differentiated sharply by region. Catholic regions (Bavaria, Rhineland, Münster) showed higher and more stable church attendance and institutional cohesion. Protestant regions showed greater variation: some areas (Franconia, Württemberg) retained high attendance while Berlin and northern industrial cities saw the steepest decline. The völkisch quasi-religious movements were concentrated in urban Protestant milieus and in the post-1918 crisis zones.

## End Conditions

The process as described ends with the collapse of the Third Reich (1945) and the postwar reconstitution of both churches on terms that falsified their wartime record. German religious life after 1945 entered a new phase of the secular-religious reconceptualization in which Islam, not Catholicism or Judaism, occupies the position of stigmatized "irrationalism" that Catholicism held in the Wilhelmine era.

## Historiography

The central historiographical move of recent decades has been the dismantling of the secularization thesis as a master narrative. Sociologists of religion (American more than European) forced recognition that modernity and religiosity are not inversely correlated. Emmet Larkin's 1972 identification of a nineteenth-century "devotional revolution" in Ireland was an early harbinger. In the German debate, Olaf Blaschke's "reconfessionalization thesis" argued that the period 1818–1968 saw rising denominational consciousness; this in turn attracted criticism for its periodization. Talal Asad's work on the constructedness of "secular" and "religious" categories has been particularly influential in the German literature (Rebekka Habermas explicitly draws on Asad). The history of women's religiosity has been substantially revised from the 1970s onward (Barbara Welter, Hugh McLeod) to show that gendered patterns of practice do not straightforwardly confirm secularization. The most active current debates concern the Catholic Church's behavior under National Socialism and the nature of Nazi quasi-religious ideology.

## The churches at war — Evans, *The Third Reich at War* (2008)

In 1939, 95% of Germans were still church members (3.5% *gottgläubig*, mostly Party activists). Hitler privately promised to abrogate the Concordat after victory ("black bugs," "abortions in cassocks"; Christianity "a prototype of Bolshevism" that would "die a natural death" before science), but tactically suspended the church struggle — until Bormann's June 1941 circular resumed pressure: monastery expropriations (130 by May 1941), bell-melting, chaplaincy bans. The **Bavarian crucifix protests** (1941) forced a rare full public retreat — Wagner's school-crucifix decree beaten by mothers' protests and Faulhaber's pastoral letter, Hitler threatening Wagner with Dachau — teaching the leadership that the "final solution of the Church question" must wait for victory (cf. the Galen euthanasia victory, [[aktion-t4-1939]]). On the genocide the record is bleak: the bishops discussed it privately, Bertram suppressed the planned November 1941 protest, Preysing's 1943 draft was diluted, and the Nuncio told him "the greatest neighbourly love consists in avoiding making any difficulties for the Church." Pius XII — no antisemite on Evans's reading, but fixed on Communism, neutrality, and fear that intervention would speed the killing — confined himself to veiled formulas; Protestant Bishop Wurm's letters to Hitler and Lammers (1943) condemning the "measures of annihilation" earned him a speaking ban. The German Christians' and Confessing Church's 1939 racial declarations, Evans notes, differed little. Source: [[evans-third-reich-at-war-2008]]
