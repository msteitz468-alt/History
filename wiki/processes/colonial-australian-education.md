## Definition and Scope

The process by which [[australia|colonial Australia]] built a system of schooling, higher education, and cultural institutions from the founding of [[new-south-wales-colony]] in 1788 to Federation in 1901. The central tension was between denominational (church-run, state-funded) schooling and a secular, state-provided "national" model. By the 1870s–1890s, colonial parliaments had imposed "free, compulsory and secular" Education Acts across all colonies, entrenching a state/Catholic divide that persisted for nearly a century. The process was inseparable from [[settler-colonialism]]: Aboriginal children were progressively excluded from public schools by the century's end.

Source: Horne and Sherington, ch. 15 in [[bashford-macintyre-cha-v1-2013]].

## Causal Drivers

- **Evangelical Christianity and literacy**: "The governors brought with them Christianity as the foundation to education…education had become closely associated with words and numbers and the culture of the book, particularly the Bible." The Society for the Propagation of the Gospel saw universal literacy as conversion strategy.
- **Colonial state formation**: Governors and their wives (Elizabeth Paterson, Anna King, Elizabeth Macquarie) moved early to establish institutions for convicts' orphaned children.
- **Denominational competition**: Multiple churches competed for colonial populations and for government education funds, producing duplicated school systems on neighbouring street corners.
- **The secular liberal turn**: Growing political ideology of secularism—rooted in the separation of church and state and Enlightenment reason—drove demands for state schools open to "all faiths and with preference for none."
- **Gold-rush demographics**: Gold-rush wealth funded the Universities of Sydney (1850) and Melbourne (1853) and reinforced private and corporate schooling in Victoria.
- enabled_by: [[british-empire]] (imperial charters; London selection committees for professors; Westminster model legislation)

## Major Phases

### 1788–1826 — Convict foundations and mission schooling
The first known teacher in New South Wales was the convict Isabella Rosson (1789). Governor Macquarie supported missionaries William and Elizabeth Shelley in establishing a **Native Institution** at Parramatta (later Blacktown) for Aboriginal children, but "removed from their families and kin, Aboriginal children soon left the institution." Initial state involvement was through charitable provision for convicts' children, not a system.

### 1826–c.1850 — Church and Schools Corporation; denominational era
The **Church and Schools Corporation** (1826) attempted a comprehensive Anglican system in NSW—infant schools, grammar schools, orphan schools, and a "native school" at Blacktown for children under five. It "foundered, in part on its ambitions but principally on the differences and jealousies between the emerging colonial churches." By the late 1830s government accepted that state funds must be distributed equitably across denominations. Governor Bourke's proposal for a "national" Irish-model school (mixed faith, shared curriculum) was rejected by Anglican and Catholic bishops alike; instead state aid was extended to all religious schools.

### c.1848–1872 — National schools movement
A **National Board of Education** was established in NSW (1848). Inspector George William Rusden travelled 10,000 miles in 18 months surveying remote districts. The "national schools" attracted mixed-denomination enrolments; superintendent William Wilkins reported that in Sydney's national schools "they are of all denominations, and among them their parents may be found representatives of all classes of society." The **1866 Public Schools Act** (NSW) set standards that denominational schools found hard to meet; Catholic parents began drifting to public schools.

### 1872–1895 — The secular Acts
"Beginning with Victoria in 1872 and ending with Western Australia in 1895, the parliaments of all the Australian colonies enacted the 'free, compulsory and secular' Acts." The Victorian Act (1872) endorsed "non-sectarian" instruction. NSW Premier Henry Parkes promoted the **Public Instruction Act of 1880** as a system "making no distinction in faith, asking no question where the child has been born." Protestant churches accepted the loss of elementary schools; the **Catholic Church refused**. Catholic bishops condemned secular schools as "seed plots of future immorality, infidelity, and lawlessness." The result was a sectarian bifurcation: one generation of non-Indigenous Australians schooled in state secular schools, another in Catholic parish schools staffed by religious orders of nuns and brothers.

### 1850–1901 — Universities and higher education
Four universities founded by 1900: Sydney (1850), Melbourne (1853), Tasmania (from the Tasmanian Council of Education, 1858; university 1890), Adelaide (1874). R.J.W. Selleck's characterisation: "a state university: urban, secular, professional, non-residential and non-collegiate, centralised in government, controlled by a laity." Women admitted: Adelaide from 1876, Melbourne from 1880, Sydney from 1882. The University of Sydney Chancellor's reasoning: "the sexes have an equal right to participate…seeing that this University has been founded for the general benefit of the Public."

### 1850s–1901 — Corporate (elite private) schools
All major churches established "public school"-style boys' colleges from the 1850s to 1870s (Scotch, Geelong Grammar, Melbourne Grammar, Wesley, Xavier in Victoria; equivalent GPS schools in NSW and Queensland). These were "part of a common British imperial culture that crossed ethnic and religious lines, uniting Protestants and Catholics." Girls' corporate schools followed in the last quarter of the century, enabled by university admission of women.

## Geographic Spread

- **New South Wales**: Epicentre of the national schools debate; strongest state-endowed secondary schooling (Sydney Grammar 1857; Sydney Boys/Girls High 1880s).
- **Victoria**: Corporate schools dominant; state education stopped at elementary level; gold-rush wealth drove church building and private ventures.
- **South Australia**: Most progressive in technical and agricultural education (Roseworthy Agricultural College 1883; SA School of Mines 1889); Nonconformist influence led to early withdrawal of church-school aid.
- **Queensland**: Distinctive system of state-endowed local grammar schools (Ipswich 1863 to Rockhampton Girls 1892) co-existing with Catholic boarding colleges.
- **Western Australia**: Catholic Church often "the pace setter, setting up the first schools and forcing the state to compete."
- **Tasmania**: More effective rural reach through early examination network; Associate of Arts scholarship pathway to Oxford/Cambridge.

Mechanics' institutes and schools of arts spread from the 1820s into urban and rural areas across all colonies, providing libraries, lectures, and adult education—though "out of 51 mechanics' institutes [in Victoria in 1869], only two offered instruction useful to trade skills."

## Interaction

- part_of: [[settler-colonialism]] — Aboriginal education was consistently marginalised; by 1900 NSW allowed exclusion of Aboriginal children from public schools on the demand of a single white parent.
- concurrent_with: [[religion-colonial-australia]] — the secular Acts were primarily driven by Protestant–Catholic conflict; the school question was "one of the clearest expressions of Irish-Australian Catholicism."
- concurrent_with: [[colonial-australian-law]] — the 1866 Public Schools Act (NSW) used state inspection and enrolment standards as regulatory tools; the Education Acts were colonial parliament legislation.
- analogous_to: Comparable denominational/secular struggles in England, Canada, and Ireland — but Australian colonies chose a harder secular line, unlike Britain and Canada where churches retained state-aided schools.
- contrasts_with: United States — Australian universities were secular from the outset; religious tests abandoned immediately; no land-grant tradition but philanthropic endowment was significant.

## End Conditions

The secular Education Acts (1872–1895) effectively ended the denominational era for Protestant schooling, though Catholic parish schools — funded by communities and religious orders rather than the state — remained and expanded. Federation (1901) left education as a state power. The state/Catholic divide endured until state aid was restored to Catholic schools in the 1960s–70s. The exclusion of Aboriginal children from public schooling, formalised in NSW after the 1900 Gulgong incident, locked in a racialised educational gap that would persist deep into the twentieth century. The four colonial universities became the foundation of the Australian higher education system post-Federation.
