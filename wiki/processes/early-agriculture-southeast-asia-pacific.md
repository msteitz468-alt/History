## Definition and Scope

The origins of food production in **mainland Southeast Asia (MSEA)**, **island
Southeast Asia (ISEA)**, and the **Pacific** — a region that "defies a simple
summary" (Huw Barton, CWH Vol. II Ch. 16; case study **Kuk Swamp**, New Guinea,
Tim Denham, Ch. 17). Critically important because the collection is otherwise
**absent** on Southeast Asia (per CLAUDE.md), so this is the wiki's primary source.

The region overturns the cereal-centric, package-diffusion model of agriculture:
here the foundational practices were **vegeculture** (cultivating roots and tubers
— taro, yam, sago) and **arboriculture** (managing tree crops — banana,
breadfruit, sugarcane), which resemble hunter-gatherer in-situ plant management
more than cleared-field cereal farming, and which permit **multiple, diffuse
domestication events**.

## Indigenous vegeculture and arboriculture

Long before (and independent of) rice, the region's peoples managed root, tuber,
tree, and palm crops:
- Late-Pleistocene/early-Holocene "arboricultural" and "vegecultural" practices
  are evidenced at sites like **Niah Great Cave** (Sarawak) and **Kosipe** (New
  Guinea highlands) — recovered largely thanks to **starch-residue analysis**
  (a Barton speciality), which finally made tropical root crops archaeologically
  visible.
- **Pottery, ground stone axes, and sedentism appear without agriculture** in
  parts of southern China and northern Vietnam — the "Neolithic package" decouples
  even more thoroughly here than in Southwest Asia. Pottery played a major role in
  **burial ritual**.
- Even rice, when it came, was apparently **adopted into indigenous vegecultural
  systems** rather than arriving as a ready-made farming package from China; in
  central Thailand (Khao Wang Prachan valley), **millets, not rice**, were the key
  early crop, rice becoming important only in the mid-2nd millennium BCE.

## Case study: Kuk Swamp (highlands of New Guinea)

Kuk, in the upper Wahgi valley, is the volume's key evidence for **independent
agricultural origins in New Guinea**. Excavated since Jack Golson's work in the
1970s, this waterlogged site preserves **drainage and cultivation features from
~10,000 years ago** — among the world's earliest — documenting indigenous
domestication of **banana, taro, sugarcane, breadfruit, and sago** within local
**vegeculture/arboriculture**, entirely separate from the Eurasian cereal world.
Banana phytoliths first domesticated in New Guinea later turn up in Sri Lanka and
West Africa "at bafflingly early dates," illustrating early **"food
globalization."**

## The Austronesian question

The dominant model (Peter Bellwood) holds that **Neolithic farmers speaking
proto-Austronesian** spread from China/Taiwan through ISEA into the Pacific
(~3000–1500 BCE), carrying rice and pigs — the "express-train"/farming-language
dispersal. The volume strongly qualifies this: see the dedicated controversy page
[[austronesian-dispersal]]. Archaeogenetics shows most Pacific ancestry (including
the mtDNA "Polynesian motif") has **deep island roots** shaped by post-glacial
sea-level rise, with eastern Indonesia/Near Oceania as a key source; domestic
**pigs did not spread with rice** (and some island pigs were domesticated
separately). One proposal is that Neolithic material culture spread via **social
networks and a rise of elites/belief systems**, largely *without* the
domesticates.

## Causal Drivers

- **Indigenous experimentation** with roots, tubers, and trees (vegeculture).
- **Post-glacial sea-level rise** (an area the size of western Europe drowned at
  the start of the Holocene), shaping maritime networks and island isolation.
- **Selective adoption** of rice and pigs from [[early-agriculture-china]], on
  local terms, often after long resistance.

## Interaction

## New Guinea agriculture and Melanesian society (CWH IV)

[[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|CWH IV]] (McNiven, Ch 22) extends the New Guinea story into its
**social** consequences. Highland cultivation of yams, taro, and bananas began ~10,000 BP and
**intensified by ~7,000 BP** (mounds, drainage channels, forest clearance), driving new
**leadership hierarchies** to manage production. The later introduction of the **pig** and,
~500 BP, the South American **sweet potato** (grown as pig fodder) underwrote the surplus,
competitive **feasting** and prestige-exchange economy of the ethnographic **"Big Man"
societies** — densely populated (up to 300 people/km²), territorial, and warring. Highland
stone-axe quarries produced up to 1,000 axes/year for exchange networks reaching 350 km. This
is the agricultural-Melanesian counterpart to the maritime story told under
[[pacific-voyaging-and-settlement]].

## End Conditions

Austronesian-speaking voyagers settled Remote Oceania (Polynesia, Micronesia)
through the 1st millennium BCE–CE, completing the human settlement of the planet's
last habitable lands (the detailed colonization sequence is traced in
[[pacific-voyaging-and-settlement]]). Barton's conclusion: abandon the "Mesolithic/Neolithic"
straitjacket and "explore the diversity for what it is."

## From Miksic & Goh, *Ancient Southeast Asia* (2017)

[[miksic-goh-ancient-southeast-asia-2017|Miksic & Goh]] reinforce the **vegeculture/tree-crop** and **gradual-transition** readings already on this page, with mainland Thai and western Indonesian pollen/site detail.

**Historiography.** Carl Sauer (1952) hypothesized agriculture began in Seasia from bananas, coconuts, sugar cane (wild varieties only in Seasia); tree-crop adaptation differs from grain domestication and leaves few archaeological traces. Domestication of rice and animals (pigs, cattle) first began in the Yangzi Valley (then northern border of tropical Seasia linguistically/ecologically); earliest food production in politically defined Seasia debated ~3000–2000 BCE (Bellwood ~3000; Higham ~2000). Historiographic split: Bellwood/Diamond propose rapid farmer expansion with a "Friction Zone"; Oppenheimer and Higham favor gradual, differentiated advance; Higham holds agriculture did not end hunting-gathering. Early plant manipulation may have focused on long-lived trees and vegetative propagation (taro, yams) rather than annual seed crops — harder to detect archaeologically.

**Pollen and sites.** Sumatran highland pollen: forest clearance ~6,500 BP at Pea Sim-Sim; Danau Diatas disturbance before 7,500 BP; Lake Kumphawapi (NE Thailand) ~6500 BP; Java large-scale clearance ~5500 BP. Authors infer dryland crops possibly from ~7,500 BP and wider cultivation ~3,000 BP in western Indonesia (data sparse); oldest island Seasia rice samples from **Sireh Cave** (Sarawak) — grains/husks in pottery ~4300 BP. New Guinea highland swamp water-management systems resemble western Pacific taro gardens without proving taro domestication at that date. **Khok Phanom Di** (~2000–1500 BCE): marine hunter-gatherers then agricultural tools, domesticated rice in faeces, isotopic evidence women from farming communities moved in — **symbiosis not friction** with foragers. **Ban Non Wat** (settled mid-17th c. BCE): domestic pigs/cattle/dogs/rice plus wild resources; hunter-gatherers and rice farmers shared burial sites; later sea-level change returned the community to marine H&G and rice cultivation ceased — farming not irreversible replacement. Contrasts_with Bellwood/Diamond friction-zone model; concurrent_with [[austronesian-expansion]] debates.
