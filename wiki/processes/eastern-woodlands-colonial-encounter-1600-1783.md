# Eastern Woodlands Colonial Encounter, 1600–1783

## Definition and Scope

The two-century process by which European permanent colonies expanded across eastern North America while Native nations pursued alliance, trade, warfare, accommodation, ethnogenesis, and avoidance. Primary analytic frame: Neal Salisbury, “Native People and European Settlers in Eastern North America, 1600–1783,” CHNPA I.1 ch. 7.

**Not a simple conquest narrative.** By c. 1600, Europeans, goods, and microbes had already affected many eastern seaboard and southeastern-interior peoples—depopulation, migration, European goods in material culture, altered subsistence and alliance—yet most still occupied familiar territories and understood newcomers largely through precontact lifeways; Europeans remained outsiders almost everywhere except around St. Augustine. After 1600, “explosive and historically unprecedented” European spread challenged continuity of lifeways, homelands, and identities—but Indians remained central to regional power balances through the American Revolution.

**Regional matrix (Salisbury’s integrated system, not colony silos):** (1) Northeast—St. Lawrence, New England, Maritimes ([[micmac-mikmaq]], [[abenaki]], [[wampanoag]], [[narragansett]], [[pequot]], Mahican); (2) Mid-Atlantic—Hudson, Delaware, Susquehanna, Chesapeake ([[lenape-delaware]], Susquehannock, [[powhatan-confederacy]]); (3) Southeast—Florida missions, Carolina slave complex, Creek/Cherokee/[[catawba]]/Choctaw/Chickasaw/Natchez/[[tuscarora]]/Yamasee; (4) Great Lakes/Ohio—[[huron-wendat]], Ottawa, [[ojibwe]], Illinois, Miami, Fox, [[shawnee]], Delaware, Mingo “republicans”; (5) lower Mississippi/Louisiana.

## Causal Drivers

- [[eastern-north-american-fur-trade]] (and deerskin/slave complexes in the Southeast)
- Capitalism and mercantile nation-state competition: England, France, Netherlands challenging Spain after c. 1600; earliest colonial successes still built on earlier Indian–European exchange/alliance patterns
- Anglo-French-Dutch (later British) imperial rivalry and settler demographic expansion
- [[virgin-soil-epidemics]] and warfare mortality; alcohol; dietary change from trade and land loss
- Native alliance strategies, captive adoption (mourning wars), multipolar “play-off” neutrality

## Major Phases

### 1. 1600–1660 — Colony nodes, epidemics, concentration of power

European outposts remained few but consequential as nodes for microbes, goods, people, and ideas. Over four decades epidemics, intensive hunting, exchange, war, and English immigration transformed demography and politics. Political power concentrated in several European colonies **plus the Five Nations** ([[haudenosaunee-confederacy]]), each dependent on ties with other Native groups.

Key strands: Quebec 1608 and Champlain’s routing of [[mohawk]] with Montagnais/Algonquin/[[huron-wendat]] (1609–10); Dutch Fort Orange and Mohawk victory over Mahicans by 1630; [[micmac-mikmaq]] coastal military dominance to Cape Cod until after 1629; 1616–19 New England epidemics (~90% mortality) and Massasoit–Plymouth alliance 1621; [[powhatan-confederacy]] wars and tobacco expansion; Spanish Florida mission revival and 1613–17 epidemics killing half of ~16,000 mission-linked Indians; wampum commercialization; [[pequot-war-1637]]; [[beaver-wars-17th-century]] climax (Huron destruction 1648–49); Munsee wars in New Netherland; Susquehannock anti-Iroquois power; Powhatan uprisings 1622 and 1644–46 destroying the confederacy as a political entity.

### 2. 1660–1701 — English replace Dutch; French western alliance; slave trade; Covenant Chain

English supplanted Dutch on the Hudson; France introduced military force and Great Lakes/Ohio alliances; Iroquois sought accommodations with both great powers. Southeast: Spanish decline hastened by South Carolina and an Indian slave trade that engulfed most regional Natives by century’s end. [[king-philips-war-1675]] devastated southern New England; Eastern [[abenaki]] fought to a 1677–78 standoff; Bacon’s Rebellion attacked tributary Indians; Covenant Chain (1677) made Iroquois intercolonial brokers. Late-century Iroquois western wars met French-allied crushing defeats; **Grand Settlement of 1701** secured Iroquois neutrality in future Anglo-French wars.

### 3. 1700–1744 — Neutrality and play-off under Anglo-French balance

Successful Indian groups reconciled need for European trade with desire to avoid Europe’s conflicts—neutrality possible from relative Anglo-French balance (weakened Spain bolstering France). English commercial/demographic advantages already threatened that balance. Fox Wars from 1712; [[tuscarora]] War 1711–13 and Sixth Nation 1722; Yamasee War 1715 (pan-Indian uprising saved only by Cherokee alliance with Carolina); Creek “play-off” under Brims after 1717; [[catawba]] ethnogenesis; Dummer’s War 1722–25 in the far Northeast; Walking Purchase fraud and Ohio “republican” multi-ethnic independence from Six Nations, French, and English control.

### 4. 1744–1763 — Seven Years’ War ends multipolar Indian diplomacy

[[seven-years-war-1756]]: most Indians sought in vain to maintain European balance; French vanquished. Ohio “republicans” turned anti-French mid-1740s then reassessed English land threat; Braddock routed 1755 by French + ~1,000 Native allies; Treaty of Easton 1758; Canada fell 1760; Cherokee War 1759–61; Treaty of Paris 1763 expelled France from mainland North America.

### 5. 1763–1783 — British monopoly; Pontiac; Revolution; end of Indian multipolar diplomacy

Amherst’s cessation of presents/ammunition and captive-return demands; [[pontiacs-war-1763]] (Neolin, Pontiac, Fort Pitt smallpox blankets); Proclamation of 1763 weakly enforced; Fort Stanwix 1768 Iroquois cession of others’ Ohio lands; Dunmore’s War 1774; Revolution—most threatened Natives sided against secessionists, recognizing rebels sought end to Indian sovereignty. Sullivan–Clinton 1779 destroyed dozens of Six Nations villages; >5,000 Iroquois fled to Canada. Peace of Paris 1783 transferred English title without Native consultation—opening [[indian-removal-eastern-dispossession-1780-1880]].

## Geographic Spread

Atlantic seaboard to Mississippi; Great Lakes and Ohio Valley as hinge of imperial and Indian diplomacy. Spanish Florida missions and Louisiana lower Mississippi form southern poles; Maritimes and St. Lawrence the northern.

## End Conditions

Collapse of France (1763) then of British imperial restraint after the Revolution ended structural conditions for multipolar play-off diplomacy. Demographic collapse (Thornton ~5+ million → 600,000 lower-48; Ubelaker more conservative), trade dependence, and loss of European multipolarity confined many East Natives to small tracts or emigration—yet **persistence of Indian community life and cultural identity** remains Salisbury’s closing emphasis.

## Native Strategies (inventory)

(a) Building precontact alliance/rivalry networks onto European posts (Huron–French; Mohawk–Dutch); (b) multipolar play-off neutrality (Creeks under Brims; Iroquois Grand Settlement; Abenaki/Micmac attempts); (c) military resistance (Powhatan 1622/44; King Philip; Yamasee; Pontiac; Cherokee 1776); (d) ethnogenesis/confederation under pressure (Creeks, Catawbas, Six Nations + Tuscarora); (e) refugee multi-ethnic coalescence and “republican” independence (Green Bay; Ohio); (f) religious syncretism/nativism (Druillettes; Neolin; Christian Indian towns); (g) post-1763/1783 confederation projects (Shawnee anti-Stanwix; McGillivray–Spanish).
