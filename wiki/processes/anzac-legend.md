# The Anzac Legend

## Definition and Scope

The Anzac legend is the cluster of beliefs, stories, values, and commemorative practices that emerged from Australia's experience of the First World War and that have constituted the dominant framework of Australian national identity ever since. It defines how Australian and New Zealand men "ought to respond to the challenges of war" and, more broadly, frames national character in terms of virtues supposedly displayed in extremis: courage, resilience, laconic humour, contempt for authority, disregard for social hierarchy, and fierce loyalty to one's mates. The legend is grounded in real experience but was substantially constructed by journalists, official historians, and political actors, often in tension with the actual diversity of Anzac experience.

The legend is distinct from (though related to) Anzac commemoration: it is a set of cultural claims about national character, not merely a calendar event. It produced Anzac Day, war memorials, pilgrimage tourism to Gallipoli and the Western Front, and a political vocabulary that has shaped every subsequent Australian engagement with war.

## Origins: Construction at Gallipoli

The legend began forming on 25 April 1915. "Initial reports, especially by the celebrated British journalist Ellis Ashmead-Bartlett, represented the landings as a triumph of colonial manhood, stimulating a further burst of volunteering." Australia's official correspondent Charles Bean "likewise blended reportage and propaganda, building an impression of the qualities displayed by the troops." The source states that "Bean's words endured as the basis of the influential 'Anzac legend', which defined how Australian and New Zealand men ought to respond to the challenges of war."

The caption "Here Australia Became a Nation" appeared on a photograph of Gallipoli in 1915. The phrase expressed the central claim: that national identity was forged in military sacrifice rather than through political federation.

The evacuation of Gallipoli (December 1915 – January 1916) — a military humiliation — was propagandistically converted into a triumph. "The combination of 'nationhood, brotherhood and sacrifice', as Bill Gammage wrote, proved to be a powerful and enduring mix."

## Major Phases

**Wartime construction (1915–18).** Government, press, and official correspondents built the legend during the war itself, partly to maintain enlistment and war enthusiasm. The Department of Defence commissioned war artists: Arthur Streeton, Will Longstaff, Will Dyson. C.J. Dennis' *Ginger Mick* brought the idiom of the Digger to popular literature. The "Anzac legend…produced a rich mix of beliefs, ideas, stories and practices that a century on retain a power in Australian life."

**Commemoration and institutionalisation (1916–1920s).** War memorials began appearing from 1916, "represent[ing] a popular collective commemorative impulse." Uniquely in Australia, many memorials "record the names of all those who served, not just those who died" — attesting to "the widespread sense that the Great War did not merely represent loss but something more: a national 'becoming' made by all those who served." The Returned Sailors' and Soldiers' Imperial League of Australia (later RSL) emerged as the dominant returned-soldier organisation; "by the mid-1920s almost one in three returned men were members of the RSL, a much higher rate than in other Dominions."

Anzac Day was institutionalised as a public holiday distinct from the Empire-wide Remembrance Day, "more important" for Australia and New Zealand. In Australia it combined "solemn commemorative rituals" with "public marches of returned men, cheered by large crowds."

**Conscription politics and the volunteer myth.** The defeat of both conscription referendums (1916, 1917) contributed to the Anzac legend by making the AIF's all-volunteer status a point of distinction and pride. The source notes this was "misleading": Australia was not the only all-volunteer force, and the AIF's high casualty rate reflected its front-line concentration, not unique bravery.

**Post-war repatriation and resentment.** Returned men who found reintegration difficult channelled frustration through the legend — the "girl who wouldn't wait," the "fat profiteer," the "foreign immigrant." Loyalist riots in 1919–20 were partly driven by returned-soldier discontents. The RSL became the political vehicle for asserting that Anzacs had earned a special standing as citizens.

**Resurrection and pilgrimage (late 20th–21st c.).** The source notes that "Gallipoli and the Western Front have become popular sites of pilgrimage to celebrate those who served and the national traits they embodied," and that the legend retains its power "a century on."

## Geographic Spread

The legend is primarily Australian and to a lesser extent New Zealand, with Gallipoli as its sacred site and the Western Front (especially Pozières and Mont St Quentin) as secondary sites. Anzac Day differs in emphasis between the two countries: in New Zealand it is more purely solemn; in Australia it combines mourning with celebration of national founding.

## End Conditions

The Anzac legend has not ended. The source treats it as an ongoing process. However, revisionist historiography since the 1970s has complicated the legend's foundations: L.L. Robson showed the Anzacs were more likely city-born and British-born than rural-Australian; Peter Stanley documented mutiny, desertion, and self-harm; feminist historians (Marilyn Lake) critiqued its masculine nationalism. The legend has proven resilient against these critiques, incorporating complexity without collapsing.

## Historiography

The historiography divides into three positions:

1. **Nationalist tradition**: Charles Bean's official history and popular successors (Patsy Adam-Smith, Les Carlyon) treat the legend as substantially accurate — the Anzacs did embody distinctive virtues derived from bush culture.
2. **Revisionism**: L.L. Robson, Peter Stanley, and others document the gap between the legend and demographic and disciplinary reality.
3. **Cultural-constructionist**: Graham Seal (*Inventing ANZAC*, 2004), George Mosse (*Fallen Soldiers*), and the source's own framework treat the legend as a "myth of war experience" constructed by specific agents (Bean, government, RSL) for specific purposes.

The source (Garton and Stanley) occupies the third position while acknowledging the legend's genuine emotional grounding in loss: "If Australia became a nation at Gallipoli, the cost of that making was a heavy one."
