## Definition and Scope

The process by which Christianity was established, contested, diversified, and partially secularised in [[australia|colonial Australia]] from 1788 to Federation in 1901. Unlike Massachusetts, "religion was not foundational to the formation of New South Wales. This colony was a government outpost, a repository for criminals and a potential base for trade with Asia." Its first chaplains were evangelicals embedded in the military apparatus. The dominant dynamic was not simple secularisation but denominational rivalry — principally between Anglicanism, Irish Catholicism, Presbyterianism, and Methodism — shaped by the state's changing posture toward church funding. By 1901, 96 per cent of the population identified as Christian but less than half attended worship regularly.

Source: Anne O'Brien, ch. 17 in [[bashford-macintyre-cha-v1-2013]].

---

## Causal Drivers

- **[[settler-colonialism]]**: Christianity arrived as part of the colonial apparatus; chaplains were magistrates; missions were used to pacify Indigenous resistance; the Dreaming — "the Ancestral Beings, whose travels had created land forms, people, animals, plants, sea and stars" — provided both cosmology and law to some 250 language groups for ~50,000 years before 1788.
- **[[british-empire]]**: Imperial politics drove church–state arrangements — Tory ascendancy in the 1820s produced Anglican quasi-establishment; liberal reform in the 1830s dismantled it.
- **Irish Catholic convicts and immigration**: Irish Catholics "comprised about a quarter of all convicts transported" and were forced to attend Anglican services before 1820. Successive Irish bishops appointed from the 1830s–1860s envisaged Australia as "a new and better Irish spiritual empire."
- **Evangelical revivalism**: Periodic revivals (estimated ~70 localised, mostly Methodist, 1834–1894) and transatlantic evangelicalism (the Salvation Army, Keswick missions) drove membership and social activism.
- **Gold-rush demographics**: The influx of wealth and population in the 1850s "strengthened religious practice" — 36 churches built in Ballarat in 20 years; Jewish community in Victoria increased tenfold; Chinese temples erected; Muslim cameleers present in the interior.
- **Secularising pressures**: "Slow processes of secularisation, growing out of improved patterns of consumption and medical advances"; scientific challenges (Darwin; biblical higher criticism); the Australian Secular Association (1880s); growth of alternative religions (Theosophy, spiritualism, Christian Science).

## Major Phases

### 1788–1820 — The "peculiar colony": contested foundations
The narrative of early religious failure is partly mythological but partly real. First chaplain Richard Johnson built his own church (1796) — "the convicts burned it down." Governors disagreed with evangelical strictures; most "took convict mistresses, drank alcohol, allowed blood sports and showed so little respect for the Sabbath that they allowed the theatre to open on Easter Sunday." Yet between a quarter and a half of convicts attended Johnson's voluntary Sunday services. Anti-clericalism coexisted with folk belief (crucifixes tattooed on bodies, almanacs, wakes for the Irish). The Church of England was the only denomination with official recognition before 1820; Irish Catholics repeatedly petitioned for a priest. Two brief Catholic ministerial periods ended abruptly, feeding "the sense of persecution that characterises much of Catholic historiography in Australia."

The Rev. Samuel Marsden exemplified an alternative evangelical adaptation: "He saw no clash between public order and mission; indeed, order was the 'fixed point' in his life" — which underpinned "his ruthless treatment of Irish convicts and his responses in the wars with Aboriginal people." His failed experiment with Gadigal children "discouraged British missionary societies from working in Australia for the first two decades of the nineteenth century." Marsden redirected to New Zealand (1814), establishing Sydney as a base for Pacific missions.

### 1820–1836 — Anglican quasi-establishment and its dismantlement
Governor Macquarie used religion instrumentally for moral reform: compulsory Sabbath observance, church-building programme (architect Francis Greenway). The **Bigge Report** (1819) shaped the 1820s Tory response: NSW made an archdeaconry under the Bishop of Calcutta (1824); Archdeacon Thomas H. Scott ranked second in authority to the governor. The **Church and Schools Corporation** received one-seventh of crown land for Anglican clergy and schools.

By the 1830s liberal reform reversed this. Governor Bourke — who had supported repeal of civil disabilities for Dissenters (1828) and Catholics (1829) in England — wound back the Corporation and introduced the **Church Acts (1836–37)**, making financial aid available to the four largest denominations (Anglican, Catholic, Presbyterian, and eventually Methodist) in proportion to congregation size. The Acts "reconfigured the status hierarchy of British religion, removing the privileges of the Church of England and putting old enemies on an equal fiscal footing." They were rescinded in NSW in 1862.

### 1836–1860s — Sectarian crystallisation; Irish Catholic entrenchment
The Church Acts' material effects were profound: steady growth in churches and clergy, funded denominational competition, and heightened sectarian animosity. "Funding made the Catholic Church a more formidable enemy than it was in the United States, where the Catholic Irish were poorer and more 'alien'." The appointment of English Benedictine John Bede Polding as Catholic Bishop was intended to assuage fears of "priestcraft and popery"; instead, the 1830s–40s influx of Irish convicts, immigrants, and priests, and the creation of an Australian Catholic hierarchy in 1842, intensified them. Presbyterian John Dunmore Lang denounced the prospect of the colony being "transformed into a Province of Popedom."

The 1868 attempted assassination of the Duke of Edinburgh by a Fenian-connected Irishman in Sydney, coinciding with Fenian transportation to Western Australia and surging Irish immigration (75% of arrivals 1863–65), inflamed sectarianism. Rome's **Syllabus of Errors** (1864) and declaration of **papal infallibility** (1870) heightened Protestant fears. Six Irish bishops appointed to NSW and Brisbane dioceses (1859–69) — all protégés of Cardinal Paul Cullen — envisaged "a new and better Irish spiritual empire."

The adherents of the four largest denominations — Anglican, Catholic, Methodist, Presbyterian — "remained remarkably stable: 80 per cent of the population identified with these faiths until the 1960s."

### 1850s — Gold rush and religious diversification
The gold rushes stimulated the growth of religion, particularly in Victoria. Methodist growth was spectacular: "between 1852 and 1867 those colonists affiliated with the Methodist church increased by 300 per cent, nearly twice the population increase." Methodism's "simple doctrines had direct emotional appeal…Evangelical religion offered a personal relationship with Jesus that brought consolation to lonely immigrants." Half of Ballarat's 36 post-gold churches were Methodist (Wesleyan, Primitive, Bible Christian).

Gold also brought religious diversity: Jewish communities built synagogues in major cities; Chinese miners erected temples in South Melbourne, Bendigo, Ballarat, Castlemaine; Muslim cameleers ("generally temporary residents") were essential to interior transport; millenarian sects and Cornish Methodist revival at Moonta (SA, 1875) echoed the American "Burned Over District."

### 1872–1901 — Secular Acts; the school divide; secularisation pressures
When government funding to church schools was removed in all colonies (1872–96), "the Catholic bishops expanded and entrenched their alternative education system, staffed largely by orders of religious women." concurrent_with: [[colonial-australian-education]]. The school question became the sharpest edge of sectarian conflict.

Between 30–40% of the population were not regular church attenders by 1871. Bush missions and city missions addressed non-attendance; the **Salvation Army** (1880) brought military-style populist religion, pressuring churches to open formal roles to women. The **Australian Secular Association** (1880s) challenged literal biblical interpretation; Anglican Bishop Moorhouse accommodated science while Melbourne Presbyterians forced Charles Strong to resign (1883). Alternative religions (Theosophy, spiritualism, Christian Science) gained followers. At the 1901 census, 2% of NSW answered "other persuasion" or "no religion" (vs 0.4% in 1851).

**Women in religion**: Observers "reckoned that women outnumbered men in the pews by two or three to one" yet women were largely excluded from formal ministry. The Bible Christian preacher Serena Thorne (Adelaide, 1870) filled the town hall but was framed as an "exceptional call." Melbourne's Unitarian Church elected Martha Turner as minister (1873), the only church to do so. The Salvation Army — co-founded by Catherine Booth — pressured churches toward formal women's roles; deaconesses and sisters worked mainly with women and children. Catholic women's work was channelled through religious orders: the **Sisters of Charity** opened St Vincent's Hospital, Sydney (1857); **Mary MacKillop** founded the **Sisters of St Joseph** (Penola SA, 1867) to teach isolated settlers' children.

### Mission to Aboriginal people (throughout)
Foreign missions were not a high priority. From the 1820s governors supported missions instrumentally: Governor Brisbane granted Lancelot Threlkeld's mission 10,000 acres at Port Macquarie (1825); Threlkeld (with Awakabal mentor Biraban) compiled "the first study of Aboriginal language." In Van Diemen's Land, Arthur commissioned George Augustus Robinson to "pacify" and convert Aboriginal people after the "black war" of 1828. The **Native Institution** (Parramatta/Blacktown, 1820s) failed; the **Wellington Valley Mission** closed 1844; the **Maloga Mission** (Murray River, 1874) recorded "a revival in 1883–84" — under Indigenous agency, in the absence of a white missionary, on land of ancestral significance.

"Missionaries wanted to stop Indigenous people from 'wandering', encourage them to work, increase their desire for consumer goods and save their souls." Most "treated Aboriginal people as members of a 'child-race'." Some Aboriginal people engaged selectively: Fanny Cochrane Smith maintained Methodist community while continuing traditional ceremonies; Nathanael Pepper's conversion at Ebenezer integrated shared understandings of blood, animals, and kinship with Christian imagery. John Green's Coranderrk mission (1863–74) was exceptional in treating Aboriginal people "as friends and 'equals', encouraging their self-governance."

By 1880 missions multiplied in north Queensland amid hardening racial determinism; the missions were "under-funded, had no security of tenure on their lands, and were on the periphery of the vision of the Australian churches."

## Geographic Spread

- **NSW**: Epicentre of anti-Catholic sectarianism (Castle Hill 1804; Fenian crisis 1868; 1879 Pastoral Letter of Bishops condemning secular schools); strong Anglican presence via Oxbridge-connected Sydney Diocese.
- **Victoria**: Most diverse gold-rush religious landscape; Methodism dominant in Ballarat and regional centres; corporate Catholic boys' colleges (Xavier 1878, Riverview 1880); Sabbatarian legislation; Salvation Army from 1880.
- **South Australia**: Founded by Nonconformist Dissenters; most hospitable to unorthodox belief (Unitarians, New Church, German Lutherans at Klemzig/Bethany); state aid for churches introduced late (1840s depression) and for only five years; Cornish Methodist revival (Moonta 1875).
- **Van Diemen's Land / Tasmania**: Wesleyan missionary Schofield at Macquarie Harbour ("Hell's Gates"); Arthur's authoritarian use of missions for pacification; Fanny Cochrane Smith (Oyster Cove Methodist community).
- **Queensland**: Six Irish bishops 1859–69; most aggressive Catholic entrenchment; missions to Aboriginal people in northern Queensland under hardening racial context; Anglican Bush Brotherhoods from 1897.
- **Western Australia**: Sisters of Mercy first school (Mercedes, Perth, 1846); Spanish Benedictine New Norcia mission (1846); John Gribble expelled from community for opposing frontier violence (1887); Muslim community of ~300 in Coolgardie by end of century.

## End Conditions

Federation (1901) produced a constitutional compromise: the Preamble invoked God; Section 116 barred any establishment or religious test. State aid to churches had already ended in all colonies. The Catholic Church entered the twentieth century with a self-funded, self-staffed parallel school system, an Irish-Australian ethnic base, and a defensive-oppositional posture toward secular culture that persisted until the 1960s. The large four denominations (Anglican, Catholic, Methodist, Presbyterian) retained ~80% denominational identification until the 1960s. Aboriginal traditional religion had been suppressed, fragmented, and driven underground across most of colonial Australia; missionary "conversion" was marginal, partial, and syncretic where it occurred at all. The secular trajectory — institutionally embedded in state schools, universities, and the Constitution — provided the framework within which Australian religious life would be negotiated through the twentieth century.
