## Definition and Scope

The quarter-century of nearly uninterrupted economic growth in Western Europe from postwar reconstruction to the 1973 oil crisis, conventionally dated 1950–1973 (French historians call it *les trente glorieuses*, "the thirty glorious years," dating from 1945). It is not simply postwar reconstruction — growth continued long after reconstruction's immediate stimulus was exhausted — and it is broader than any single national "miracle" (the West German *Wirtschaftswunder* is its most-studied national instance; see also [[spanish-economic-miracle]] and [[japanese-economic-miracle]] for parallel but distinct national processes). It is a Western European phenomenon; Eastern Europe under planned economies is explicitly excluded, and Southern Europe (southern Italy, Spain until the late 1950s, Portugal, the Balkans) participated only partially and late.

## Causal Drivers

Three factors, per Paxton and Hessler: (1) a backlog of interwar and wartime technologies (nuclear power, transistors, integrated circuits) ready for rapid commercial exploitation, aided by Europe's high levels of technical education; (2) government policy — the removal of internal European tariffs (see European economic integration), corporatist bargains giving labor a role in governance in exchange for wage restraint, and (more controversially) Keynesian demand management; (3) a shift in consumer psychology, documented anthropologically by Lawrence Wylie's study of a Provençal village (*Village in the Vaucluse*), from the parsimonious, war-scarred caution of 1951 to the confident borrowing and installment buying of the early 1960s.

## Major Phases

Early 1950s: postwar reconstruction proper. Mid-1950s–1973: sustained growth beyond reconstruction's stimulus, interrupted only by minor downturns (1958–1959, 1966–1967 in Britain and Germany). By the early 1970s the boom had transformed consumer expenditure patterns (food fell below 50% of household spending for the first time in most Western European countries), driven mass automobile and television ownership, and financed the expansion of secondary and university education. The process ends abruptly with the October 1973 OPEC oil-price shock and Arab oil embargo, which exposes underlying structural weaknesses (aging industry, rising Asian competition) masked by two decades of growth.

## Geographic Spread

Most complete in West Germany, France, Britain, Benelux, and above all Scandinavia (where poverty was "nearly unknown" by the 1960s and inequality was largely erased). Weakest in southern Italy, Spain, Portugal, Greece, and the Balkans, which remained impoverished through the 1950s and only began catching up via tourism revenue and labor emigration (some 6 million southern Italians moved to northern Italian industrial cities in the 1960s).

## Interaction

Enabled the political consensus described under "the politics of prosperity" — conservative dominance in West Germany, Italy, and Britain through the late 1950s/early 1960s, and the moderation of socialist parties (the SPD's 1959 Bad Godesberg renunciation of Marxism is the paradigm case). It also `produced` the youth culture and generational affluence that in turn destabilized that same consensus by the later 1960s (see [[may-1968-france]], [[the-1968-generation-germany]]). `contrasts_with` the 1970s–1980s recession and "stagflation" that followed it.

## End Conditions

Ended by the October 1973 oil crisis and the accompanying structural crisis of European heavy industry (steel, automobiles) in the face of new competition from Japan and, later, Taiwan, South Korea, Brazil, and Mexico. Unlike the 1930s Depression, the end of the miracle produced not collapse but "stagflation" — simultaneous stagnation and inflation — and a prolonged period (into the 1980s) of welfare-state retrenchment and conservative political revival across northern and central Europe.

## Historiography

Paxton and Hessler treat the technological, policy, and psychological drivers as complementary rather than competing explanations, while flagging Keynesian demand management specifically as "more controversial" — West Germany's Social Market Economy succeeded despite comparatively modest direct state intervention, which undercuts a purely Keynesian account. The concept overlaps with, but should not be conflated with, national "economic miracle" pages already in the wiki ([[spanish-economic-miracle]], [[japanese-economic-miracle]]); this page is the general Western European process those are instances of.
