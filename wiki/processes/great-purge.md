# The Great Purge
## Definition and Scope
The **Great Purge**, also known as the Great Terror or the **Ezhovshchina** (after NKVD chief Nikolai Ezhov), was a massive campaign of political repression and terror in the Soviet Union from late 1936 to late 1938. Directed by Joseph Stalin, the purge targeted the Communist Party leadership, government bureaucrats, the Red Army officer corps, and the general population. It resulted in the arrest of at least eight million people, widespread executions, and the expansion of the forced-labor camp system (the Gulag), establishing Stalin's absolute personal dictatorship.

## Causal Drivers
- **Stalin's Paranoia:** Stalin lived in a paranoiac world of constant threat and wholesale conspiracy, blending real political struggles with imaginary plots (Line 5917).
- **Consolidation of Power:** Stalin sought to eliminate the "Old Bolsheviks"—party members who had joined before 1917 and did not owe their careers directly to him—and replace them with a new, younger generation of loyal functionaries (the nomenklatura).
- **Preparing for War:** With the rise of Nazi Germany and Japanese expansion, Stalin wanted to eliminate any potential "fifth column" of dissenters, ethnic minorities, or former oppositionists.

## Major Phases
- **Prelude (1934–1935):** The assassination of Leningrad Party boss Sergei Kirov in December 1934 served as the pretext for enacting harsh emergency laws and executing early purges of suspected Zinovievites.
- **The Show Trials (1936–1938):** Three public trials in Moscow targeted prominent Old Bolsheviks:
  1. *August 1936:* Trial of Zinoviev and Kamenev.
  2. *January 1937:* Trial of Radek, Pyatakov, and others.
  3. *March 1938:* Trial of Bukharin, Rykov, and Yagoda.
  All defendants were forced to confess to absurd, imaginary crimes (espionage, terrorism, sabotage) and were executed.
- **The Military Purge (1937):** In June 1937, Marshal Mikhail Tukhachevsky and seven other top Red Army commanders were tried in secret and executed. The purge swept through the armed forces, claiming three of five marshals, thirteen of fifteen army commanders, and the majority of divisional and regimental commanders (Line 6003).
- **The Ezhovshchina (1937–1938):** Under Nikolai Ezhov's zealous direction, the terror expanded into the general population. The NKVD received quotas to arrest a certain percentage of the population. False confessions were systematically extracted using physical torture.
- **De-escalation (Late 1938):** Ezhov was replaced by Lavrentii Beria, and Ezhov himself was subsequently arrested and executed. Many of Ezhov's collaborators were also purged.

## Geographic Spread
The purge affected every union republic and administrative region of the USSR, with particularly devastating consequences for the leadership apparatus of Ukraine, Belarus, Georgia, and the Central Asian republics, as well as the major cities of Moscow and Leningrad.

## Interaction
- **With the Nomenklatura:** The elimination of the Old Bolsheviks allowed a new generation of younger Stalinist cadres (including Khrushchev and Brezhnev) to rapidly rise to high offices.
- **With Soviet Culture:** Writers, scientists, actors, and scholars were heavily purged, leading to the destruction of Yiddish intellectuals and the enforcement of rigid "socialist realism."

## End Conditions
The active phase of the Great Purge quieted down by late 1938 as the administrative and economic machinery began to collapse under the weight of arrests. When the Eighteenth Party Congress met in 1939, Old Bolsheviks composed only about 20% of its membership compared to 80% in 1934 (Line 6127). The purge succeeded in creating a highly centralized, terrorized state structure that obeyed Stalin implicitly.
