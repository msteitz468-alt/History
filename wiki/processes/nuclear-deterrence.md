# Nuclear Deterrence

The long-term historical process by which the United States, the Soviet Union (later Russia), and other nuclear powers have attempted to prevent major war through the threat of nuclear retaliation, while simultaneously managing the risks and instabilities created by the weapons themselves.

## Definition and Scope

Nuclear deterrence is the practice of discouraging aggression by threatening unacceptable retaliatory damage. In the nuclear age it has involved not only the threat of massive retaliation but also efforts to make threats credible across a spectrum of contingencies (conventional attack in Europe, limited probes, etc.), to stabilize the arms competition, and to reassure allies. It is distinct from (but interacts with) actual war-fighting plans.

## Causal Drivers

- Bipolar US–Soviet rivalry and ideological antagonism after 1945.
- Technological competition (bombers → missiles → MIRVs → accuracy improvements) creating reciprocal fears of surprise attack and first-strike vulnerability.
- Alliance commitments (NATO extended deterrence; Soviet guarantees to Warsaw Pact and clients).
- Domestic bureaucratic and military-industrial pressures (see Freedman on the "military-industrial complex" and procurement momentum).
- Moral and political constraints making actual use politically and ethically problematic, forcing reliance on the threat itself.

## Major Phases

- **1945–early 1950s (Monopoly and Retaliation Logic)**: US atomic monopoly; immediate recognition that retaliation (even after suffering the first blow) was the only practical deterrent. Attlee to Truman: "The only deterrent is the possibility of the victim of such an attack being able to retort to the victor." General Arnold: "our first line of defense is the ability to retaliate even after receiving the hardest blow the enemy can deliver."
- **1950s (Massive Retaliation)**: Eisenhower "New Look" and Dulles doctrine of responding to aggression "at places and with means of our own choosing." Relied on nuclear superiority to deter at lower budgetary cost.
- **Late 1950s–1960s (Formal Strategists and Stable Deterrence)**: Schelling, Kahn, Brodie, Wohlstetter on second-strike forces, bargaining, escalation, "threat that leaves something to chance." Shift from disarmament to arms control. McNamara's city-avoidance then assured destruction.
- **1970s (Parity, Erosion of Consensus, Selective Options)**: Soviet build-up to numerical parity (and beyond in land-based missiles); Wohlstetter attacks on arms-race myths; moral critiques of MAD (Ikle, Brennan); Schlesinger doctrine seeking essential equivalence plus limited options; ICBM vulnerability scenarios and escalation dominance concerns; PD59 for prolonged limited nuclear war. European theatre nuclear modernization debates and 1979 missile decision.
- **1980s–1991 and beyond (Renewed Competition and Arms Control)**: SDI, INF Treaty, START process; end of Cold War bipolar structure but persistence of deterrence logic.

## Geographic Spread

Primarily US–Soviet (later US–Russia) central strategic balance, with critical extension to Europe (NATO flexible response, British/French independent forces, German views on coupling). Also influenced Chinese "paper tiger" doctrine and minimum deterrent postures of second-tier powers. Transregional in its implications (global destruction risk) and in SALT/START negotiations.

## End Conditions

No clean end. The process continues in the Contemporary period under multipolar conditions, with new actors (North Korea, potentially others), technological change (hypersonics, cyber, space), and renewed great-power rivalry, although the post-1991 reduction in deployed strategic forces and the absence of direct US–Russia nuclear confrontation mark a partial transformation. The core dilemma identified by Freedman persists: stability may rest more on the shared fear that events could get out of control than on any rationally controllable threat.

## Historiography Note from Freedman (Range 08 / Conclusion)

Freedman concludes that after 35 years, "one is forced to the conclusion that there has been a move to the analysis of second- and third-order issues." "Stability depends on something that is more the antithesis of strategy than its apotheosis — on threats that things will get out of hand, that we might act irrationally, that possibly through inadvertence we could set in motion a process that in its development and conclusion would be beyond human control and comprehension." "The Emperor Deterrence may have no clothes, but he is still Emperor." Europe has remained at peace since 1945; whether this is because deterrence is credible or because no one can confidently forecast the consequences of breaking it remains unresolved. "C’est magnifique, mais ce n’est pas la stratégie." (Freedman 1981, CONCLUSION, grounded exclusively in Range 08).
