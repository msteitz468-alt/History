# The End of the Roman Villa in Gaul

**The disappearance of the villa as a building and an institution — while large aristocratic landholding
survived, transformed, through the Church.** Alexandra Chavarría Arnau's chapter (Oxford Handbook, Ch. 29)
tracks the fate of the late-Roman villa across the fifth–seventh centuries, with sharply divergent
regional trajectories, and argues that the *form* vanished while the *underlying pattern of large estates*
endured.

## Definition and Scope

The villa was both a built complex and a social-economic institution of slave-worked agrarian production.
Its end was uneven: "northern Gaul villa complexes largely declined from the 3rd century onward;
Mediterranean (southern) Gaul villas continued to function into the 5th–6th century." By the seventh
century the very word had shifted: documentary *villa* "referred to a rural property or landholding unit
(an economic/legal entity), not… a Roman building complex" — confirmed in the charters of Desiderius of
Cahors and Bertrand of Le Mans.

## Causal Drivers and Evidence

- **Regional divergence.** The northern decline (from the third century) and southern persistence (into
  the fifth–sixth) reflect different trajectories of aristocratic landownership and economic continuity.
- **Documentary richness amid archaeological thinning.** A survey of seventh-century sources found **178
  references to rural properties**, even as the archaeology of standing villa buildings thins out — the
  texts describe estates, not architecture.
- **Aristocratic survival through the Church.** "A key mechanism by which late Roman landowning
  aristocracy preserved their wealth and status… was entry into the church — as bishops, abbots, or donors
  of estates to monasteries" (see [[merovingian-episcopate]], [[merovingian-monasticism]]). Churches
  built on former villa sites "are generally ecclesiastical foundations… rather than private oratories."

## The Central Thesis

"The Roman villa as a built form and as a productive/social institution disappeared, but the underlying
pattern of large-scale aristocratic landholding persisted… in different institutional (mainly
ecclesiastical) guises." The chapter engages the **accommodation debate** — Goffart's model of barbarian
elites receiving tax revenues versus direct land expropriation — since villa survival or abandonment by
region bears on how barbarian settlement is reconstructed (see [[ethnogenesis]], [[roman-gaul]]).

## End Conditions and Interaction

The villa's disappearance is the negative image of the new [[merovingian-rural-settlement|rural
settlement]] of timber farms and (eventually) nucleated villages, and it underlies the weak-aristocracy
picture of northern Gaul that Theuws builds into his account of the [[merovingian-economy]].
