# Macassan Trepang Trade with Northern Aboriginal Australia

## Definition and Scope

From at least the mid-1600s CE until 1907, **fishing fleets from present-day Indonesia**
sailed seasonally to the northern coast of Australia to harvest **trepang** (sea cucumber
/ *bêche de mer*), which commanded high value in Chinese markets. The trade represents
the most sustained and consequential pre-colonial external contact for northern Aboriginal
Australians, documented in archaeology, rock art, oral traditions, ceremonies and
historical records.

The term **"Macassan"** in Australian scholarship refers primarily to crews from
**Makassar in south Sulawesi** (the main port of origin and return), but by extension
describes crews of diverse ethnic composition including people from Timor, Roti, Aru and
other parts of the Indonesian archipelago. As Campbell Macknight explains, "Macassan"
was not what the people called themselves; their own term was "Mangkasara" (Bugis:
*Mangkasa'*; Malay/Indonesian: *Makassar*).

This process is distinct from — and importantly contrasts_with — [[settler-colonialism]]
and [[first-european-encounters-australia]]: the Macassans were seasonal traders without
territorial ambitions, not colonisers.

---

## Causal Drivers

1. **Chinese demand for trepang**: Dried and smoked trepang was a high-value commodity
   in Chinese markets. Increased quantities entered Asian markets in the 1770s–1780s,
   with northern Australia identified as the most likely source.

2. **Monsoonal wind system**: Monsoonal winds reliably carried fleets from Makassar
   c. 2,000 km to the coast of Arnhem Land (known as *Marege'* to the trepangers) or
   the Kimberley coast (*Kai Jawa*) in November–December each year, with south-east
   trade winds enabling the return trip in April.

3. **VOC framework**: Dutch East India Company operations in the region created the
   commercial infrastructure and regional knowledge within which Macassar-based merchant
   activity operated; the earliest Dutch record of the trade dates to 1754.

4. **Aboriginal resource abundance**: The northern Australian continental shelf offered
   abundant trepang, Hawksbill turtle shell, pearl shell, pearls, sandalwood and
   minerals — "other products of value" gathered alongside trepang.

## Major Phases

### Phase 1 — Early Contact and Intermittent Visits (pre-1650 to c. 1780)

Initial studies suggested the trade began between 1650 and 1750, with evidence for
intermittent contact in this period. The earliest confirmed rock art evidence is an image
of a Southeast Asian *prau* at the **Djulirri rock art site in western Arnhem Land**,
recently dated to before 1664 CE. The earliest Dutch documentary reference dates to
1754.

### Phase 2 — Regular Seasonal Trade (c. 1780 to mid-19th century)

Campbell Macknight proposes c. **1780** as the probable date for a formalised regular
seasonal trade along the Kimberley and Arnhem Land coasts, based on increased trepang
quantities entering Asian markets in the 1770s–1780s. This makes Macassan activity
concurrent with British and French exploration on the east coast and with British
colonisation at Port Jackson in 1788 — though when Flinders interviewed the trepang
fleet captain Pobassoo in 1803, "they had no knowledge of any European settlement in
this country."

At its height, fleets of 50 or more vessels worked the coast. When Matthew Flinders
encountered six *praus* in the Gulf of Carpentaria in 1803, their captain Pobassoo
told him **60 praus** were working the coast that season, each with c. 30 crew. Fleets
broke into small groups working 4–6 locations before the return journey.

The trepangers made large onshore camps with **tripots, smokehouses and living quarters**
in protected coastal areas. Their season could last up to six months. Unlike European
colonisers, they brought their own staples (rice and other provisions) and were not
dependent on Aboriginal resources or labour.

### Phase 3 — Intensification and Disruption (mid-19th century to 1907)

By the time of the British northward expansion from the 1820s, the trepang industry was
still in operation. Colonial pressure eventually resulted in an Australian government ban
in 1907, ending regular Macassan visits.

## Geographic Spread

The Macassan trepang industry operated along Australia's northern coastline from the
**Kimberley coast** (*Kai Jawa*) in Western Australia to the coasts of **Arnhem Land**
(*Marege'*) in the Northern Territory, with evidence also from the **Edward Pellew
Islands** (Queensland). Rock art depicting *praus* and Macassan material culture is
concentrated at:
- Groote Eylandt
- The Arnhem Land coastline (including Djulirri rock art shelter, western Arnhem Land)
- Edward Pellew Islands (Sir Edward Pellew Group, Queensland)
- The Cobourg Peninsula

The fleets originating from **Makassar (south Sulawesi)** were the main point of
departure, with crews also from Timor, Roti, Aru, and other parts of the Indonesian
archipelago. part_of: [[indian-ocean]] trade world.

## Interaction with Aboriginal Communities

The regularity and duration of Macassan visits enabled the establishment of sustained
social and trade relationships. Key dynamics (sourced from Konishi and Nugent, Ch 2,
[[bashford-macintyre-cha-v1-2013]]):

**Technology transfer**: Aboriginal groups acquired **dugout canoes** and **detachable
metal harpoon heads**, which transformed marine hunting capability. Post-contact middens
on the Cobourg Peninsula were substantially larger and contained more diverse exotic
artefacts and large marine fauna (turtle and dugong) — attributed to adoption of
Macassan technology. Metal harpoon heads also appear to have enabled the permanent
occupation of very small islands (less than 1 km²) for the first time.

**Trade goods**: Aboriginal groups received pipes, cloth, tobacco, rice and other goods
in exchange for pearls, pearl shell and tortoise-shell. Local Aboriginal groups
stockpiled these products in anticipation of the Macassans' seasonal return.

**Cultural impact**: The trade is "recorded in Indigenous oral traditions, ceremonies,
stone arrangements and rock and bark paintings." Many hundreds of images of *praus* and
scenes relating to Macassan visitation survive in rock shelters along the northern coast.
Exchange networks, mobility and settlement patterns were affected.

**Violence and negotiation**: Interactions were multifaceted. Flinders' 1803 account
records that trepangers "sometimes had skirmishes with the native inhabitants." The Tiwi
of Bathurst and Melville Islands were reportedly persistently hostile. However, "in
general, the trepangers had an interest in developing and maintaining reasonably good
relations with Indigenous people." Occasional individuals operated as 'go-betweens',
making payments of goods for access rights and labour. Some Indigenous groups
"assimilated" Macassans into their seasonal and social calendars: "they were regular and
expected traders, not occasional visitors, like European explorers."

---

## Contrasts with European Contact

The Macassans differed critically from European explorers and colonisers:
- "Neither future designs on territory, nor ethnological interest in the local population"
- Did not form permanent settlements, claim possession, or attempt religious conversion
- Brought their own provisions; were not dependent on Aboriginal resources or land
- Maintained a seasonal, cyclical relationship compatible with existing Aboriginal
  calendrical patterns
- Had no knowledge of British colonisation at Port Jackson until Flinders told Pobassoo
  in 1803

The encounter between Flinders' British survey expedition and Pobassoo's fleet in 1803
has been described as "a symbolic moment in Australian history, because 'for centuries
European and Asian nations had been potential arbiters of the fate of Australia's
Indigenous population'. It would be British colonisation that ultimately prevailed."

---

## End Conditions

The Australian colonial government banned Macassan trepang fishing in **1907**, ending
a trade that had lasted at least 250 years in its intermittent phase and approximately
125 years in regular seasonal form. The ban severed trade networks, technological
relationships and social ties that had become deeply embedded in northern Aboriginal
communities' cultural life.

## Historiography

Veth and O'Connor (Ch 1) and Konishi and Nugent (Ch 2) draw primarily on C.C. Macknight,
*The Voyage to Marege': Macassan Trepangers in Northern Australia* (Melbourne University
Press, 1976) and Macknight's more recent 2011 article "The View from Marege'" (*Aboriginal
History*, 35), which revised the start date for regular formalised trade to c. 1780. Rock
art dating by Taçon et al. (2010) established the pre-1664 anchor for the earliest known
prau depiction. Scott Mitchell's unpublished 1994 thesis on Cobourg Peninsula is the
primary archaeological source for post-contact midden change. The trade's full impact on
Aboriginal exchange networks and settlement remains an active area of research.
