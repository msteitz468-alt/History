## Definition and Scope

The history of Christianity in Africa from the earliest missionary encounters to the contemporary period, covering both the long precolonial presence of Christianity in North Africa, Nubia, Ethiopia, and the Kingdom of Kongo, and the modern era of mission Christianity, mass conversion, African Independent Churches, and Pentecostalism. The focus here is on the social and cultural history of African Christianity — what Christianity meant for ordinary adherents — rather than a history of missionary organizations per se. From approximately 4 million Christians in 1900, adherence grew to 34 million by 1950, 75 million by 1965, and 351 million by 2000, making sub-Saharan Africa the world's fastest-growing centre of Christian faith (David Maxwell, in [[parker-reid-oxford-modern-african-history-2013]]).

This process is distinct from and interacts with [[african-independent-churches]], which covers the specific phenomenon of churches founded by Africans free from missionary control (Ethiopian Movement, Kimbanguism, Aladura, Zionists, etc.).

## Causal Drivers

**Deep historical roots.** Christianity was established in North Africa before reaching most of Europe. Tertullian (c.160–240 CE), from Carthage, was the first to write theology in Latin; Augustine was Bishop of Hippo (396–430 CE). Nubia adopted Christianity early; Ethiopia's church connected to the Coptic Patriarch of Alexandria for over a thousand years. The first gentile convert in the Acts of the Apostles was an 'Ethiopian' from Nubia. This deep history challenges the still-widespread misconception that Christianity was diffused from an imperial European centre into a passive Africa (Maxwell, [[parker-reid-oxford-modern-african-history-2013]]).

**Portuguese contact and the Kingdom of Kongo.** The first European missionary encounter in sub-Saharan Africa began in 1483 in the [[kingdom-of-kongo]]. King Afonso's genuine religious enthusiasm, combined with African catechists (*maestri*), sustained a laicized, indigenized popular Christianity with little missionary oversight. This early case demonstrates that Africans actively sought out Christianity's material, spiritual, and intellectual resources rather than passively receiving it.

**Late-nineteenth-century imperial context.** Christianization in earnest began in the late nineteenth century when the infrastructure of empire made missionaries' penetration of remote environments possible. But the most important agents of Christianization were Africans themselves: former slaves freed by the Royal Navy in Sierra Leone, returning along the West African coast with their new faith; catechists and evangelists in mission bush schools; labour migrants carrying Christianity southward and homeward.

**Mass conversion movements.** Major waves of mass conversion:
- Kingdom of Buganda (1880s–1900): court pages' conversion, martyrdom, civil conflict, then full Christianization, spreading through Ganda missionaries to surrounding states
- West African coast (from c.1910): the Liberian Prophet William Wadé Harris baptized tens of thousands in Côte d'Ivoire, channelling converts into Catholicism and Methodism
- Belgian Congo (1920s): Simon Kimbangu's prophetic movement among the Bakongo; suppression by Belgian colonial authorities only deepened its significance
- East African Revival (Balokole, 'saved people'): beginning in Rwanda 1939, spreading through Protestant churches across the region

**Conversion's social logic.** J. D. Y. Peel's model of 'opportunity costs' (for the Yoruba) has broad applicability: initial converts were often the socially peripheral — outsiders, ex-slaves, young men seeking exit from gerontocratic power. Christianity offered 'a ready legitimation to the new cultural choices which now beckoned to the young' (Peel, cited in [[parker-reid-oxford-modern-african-history-2013]]). Women fled to mission stations from domestic slavery, widowhood, and unhappy marriages; in southern Africa, Christianity was often 'a woman's movement' in the early twentieth century. A new class of 'respectable' Christians — clerks, teachers, translators — emerged from mission education.

**Bible translation.** Missionaries' most important contribution was the translation, printing, and distribution of vernacular scriptures. Once in African hands, the Bible was seized for its resonance with local cultures: healing miracles, visions, food taboos, polygamy. The Gikuyu New Testament (1926) spurred revival and inspired the Kikuyu's reading of their own history as analogous to Israel's — 'a story of servitude and salvation, exile and return' (Lonsdale, cited in Maxwell, [[parker-reid-oxford-modern-african-history-2013]]). The Bible also shaped ethnicities: Anglican, Jesuit, and American Methodist missionaries elevated the Manyika dialect in Zimbabwe, creating a new ethnic identity in the 1920s.

## Major Phases

### Mission Era (c. 1880s–1945)

Missionaries were diverse in national origin, theology, and practice. By the interwar period many had shed ethnocentrism and turned to ethnography; Diedrich Westermann (director of the International African Institute) exemplified the age in which missionaries studied African languages and cultures. After 1918, old British agencies faced decline while North American Evangelical, Pentecostal, and Faith Mission groups expanded. Catholics, increasingly Ultramontane, fought vigorously with Protestants for territory. Mission Christianity was tested by the Great Depression and Second World War, but saved by revivalist women and youth.

### African Independent Churches (1890s–present)

The first wave of independence was the Ethiopian Movement: Mangena Mokone split from the Wesleyan Church in 1892, drawing on ancient Ethiopian Christian traditions. This gave its name to a broader movement of churches free from missionary control. Subsequent forms — Zionist and Apostolic churches in southern Africa (Isaiah Shembe's Nazaretha Church; the Vapostori of Johanna Maranke and Johanna Masowe), Kimbanguism in the Congo, Aladura churches among the Yoruba of Nigeria — elevated Christ's healing ministry and provided personal security through prayer and exorcism. These churches were not explicitly proto-nationalist but unsettled colonial officials through their challenge to missionary and customary authority alike. See [[african-independent-churches]].

### Post-1945 and Decolonization (1945–1980)

Christianity was closely enmeshed in African nationalism; mission education produced the first generation of independent states' leaders. Adrian Hastings argued that the Bible provided the original model of the nation as the God-given unit of political action: Harry Nkumbula publicly burned Northern Rhodesia's White Paper in 1953 while singing 'O God our Help in Ages Past'; Hastings Banda told crowds in Nyasaland to fill colonial prisons 'shouting Hallelujah'. Between 1900 and 1950, Christian adherence increased from 4 million to 34 million; by 1965, approximately 75 million Africans identified as Christian. With independence, any formal connection between Christianity and empire diminished; Africa's Christian future proved robust.

### Pentecostalism and the Contemporary Church (1980s–present)

From the 1980s, Pentecostalism expanded rapidly, emphasizing conversion experience, faith healing, gifts of the Spirit, and prosperity. Pentecostal churches skew heavily toward young women; women play important leadership roles, though rarely at the very top levels. The 'prosperity gospel' built on older ideas of radical rupture, shifting the target toward personal suffering and individual evil. By 2010, Africa accounted for around 20 per cent of the world's Christian population — a decisive 'southerning' of global Christianity. Africa-oriented theologians now have profound (if contested) consequences for the worldwide church.

## Geographic Spread

Christianity's geography of growth was uneven: it spread rapidly in Buganda, coastal West Africa (Yoruba Nigeria, Gold Coast), and southern Africa, while advancing more slowly in the forest belt, pastoralist zones, and strongly Muslim areas. Mission stations were the nodes; labour migration routes were the arteries. The translation of scripture into hundreds of African vernaculars was the key mechanism of grassroots spread, creating localised Christianities beyond missionary control.

## End Conditions

Ongoing. The process of Christianization remains active; its contemporary forms are overwhelmingly African-driven, from megachurches in Lagos to Pentecostal movements in Zimbabwe and Congo. The centre of gravity of world Christianity has decisively shifted to the Global South.

## Historiography

Until the 1980s, African Christianity was largely written from the perspective of missionaries and their institutions. A major shift brought grass-roots religiosity to the centre, interpreting religion as intrinsic to culture. Key moves:

- **Jean and John Comaroff's** concept of the 'long conversation' between missionary and African captured bidirectional change, but was critiqued for overstating missionary agency and neglecting African initiatives
- **J. D. Y. Peel's** model of religious encounter (Yoruba) showed Africans selecting Christianity instrumentally while reshaping it
- **Adrian Hastings** offered the classic survey *The Church in Africa, 1450–1950* and connected Christianity to African nationalism and national identity
- Feminist historians showed women were a central creative force (Geiger on TANU women in Tanzania; women's prayer bands saving mission Christianity in the 1930s)
- The 'linguistic turn' generated studies of Bible translation, literary production by African Independent Churches (Liz Gunner), and the transnational reception of texts (Isabel Hofmeyr on *The Pilgrim's Progress* translated into 80 African languages)
- The 'material turn' — photographs (Paul Jenkins), objects, and media — has enriched understanding of how new media transform relations between practitioners and the divine
