# The Romano-Punic Treaty Series (508–241 BCE)

## Definition and Scope

For over 250 years — from the traditional founding of the Roman Republic to the peace
that ended the First Punic War — Rome and Carthage maintained a series of formal
bilateral agreements governing commercial access, political spheres, and military
cooperation. **Polybius** (III.22–27) is the primary source, having personally examined
(or had examined) bronze copies in the Roman treasury of the aediles. He quotes three
treaties directly and discusses a fourth of disputed existence (the [[philinus-treaty-controversy|
Philinus treaty]]). The Livian tradition adds references suggesting more renewals.

These treaties constitute the longest sustained diplomatic record between any two
Mediterranean powers in antiquity before the Hellenistic period, and their progressive
evolution traces the rise of Roman Italian power as seen through Carthaginian eyes.

## The First Treaty (508/7 BCE)

**Source**: Polybius III.22–23; Livy does not mention it.

The first treaty's archaic Latin was so difficult that Polybius could barely understand
it — itself evidence for early date. Key terms:

*For the Romans:*
- No Roman shall sail beyond the **Fair Promontory** (Cape Farina, north of Carthage)
  for trade or plunder; if driven there by storm, he shall have 5 days to depart
- At Carthage and in the Punic province of Sicily: trade is permitted under Punic
  official supervision (no private contract without Punic herald/scribe)
- No Roman shall build fort or stay overnight in Libya

*For the Carthaginians:*
- Shall not harm the coastal cities of Latium that are subjects of Rome
- Cities of Latium not subject to Rome may not be settled by Carthage; if taken by
  force, they must be restored
- May not build fort in Latium
- In Sicily, Punic and Roman rights are equal

**Significance**: Polybius says the Fair Promontory means the geographical limits of
Carthaginian power: Romans may not trade to the west of it (i.e., in Spain or West
Africa). The treaty treats Rome as a minor Latium power; it confirms Carthage as the
dominant western Mediterranean sea-power.

**Date controversy**: Polybius places this in the first year of the Republic (509/8 or
508/7 BCE). Many modern scholars accept this date based on the archaic Latin and
Polybius's explicit statement; some have argued for a later date, but the archaic
language, the absence of Campanian cities (acquired only in the 340s), and Polybius's
explicit "first year of the Republic" statement are strong evidence for authenticity.

## The Second Treaty (348 BCE)

**Source**: Polybius III.24; Livy VII.27.2 (confirming date: Carthaginian envoys to
Rome *amicitiam ac societatem petentes*).

Terms show a radically different balance from the first treaty:

*For the Romans:*
- May not sail beyond the **Fair Promontory** for trade or plunder (now extended westward:
  Mastia/Tarseum = modern Cartagena area; the entire western Mediterranean is now closed)
- May not trade or colonize in **Sardinia** or **Libya** (previously permitted under
  controlled conditions — now completely excluded)
- In Punic Sicily and at Carthage: normal access permitted

*For the Carthaginians:*
- Shall not harm Roman allies in Latium (named cities: Ardea, Antium, Circeii,
  Tarracina; Polybius may omit Lavinium or add it to the first treaty)
- Latin foederati (treaty-allies of Rome, not subject) also protected
- At Rome: normal access permitted

**Significance**: The geographical restriction of Roman commercial access has grown
enormously. Previously admitted to Sardinia and Libya under supervision, Romans are
now wholly excluded. Mastia/Tarseum extends the prohibition to the Spanish coast.
Scullard dates this to 348 BCE on the basis of Livy's notice, the political context
(Carthage rebuilding after Timoleon), and the absence of Campanian references (which
would appear in any post-343 BCE treaty).

## Possible 343 BCE Renewal

Livy (VII.38.2) records Carthaginian envoys in Rome in 343 BCE offering a gold crown
weighing 25 lbs to Jupiter Capitolinus to congratulate Rome on its Samnite victory.
Whether this was accompanied by a formal treaty renewal or only a gift is uncertain.
If there was a formal renewal in 343, it may account for one of Livy's numbered
renewals without requiring any new substantive terms.

## Treaty of 306 BCE

Livy (IX.43.26) records a treaty renewed "for the third time" (*tertio*) in 306.
Content is unknown. Context: Carthage was near victory over Agathocles; Rome was
encircling Samnium. Both powers were "negotiating from growing strength." The treaty
probably recognized Rome's expanded Italian position more fully than the 348 treaty's
explicit reference to Latium only.

Whether the 306 treaty also contained the "spheres of influence" clause (Italy for
Rome / Sicily for Carthage) attributed to Philinus is the central question of the
[[philinus-treaty-controversy]]. Scullard finds this "probable but uncertain."

## Third Treaty / Pyrrhic War Treaty (279/8 BCE)

**Source**: Polybius III.25.1–6.

This is the treaty made when Carthaginian admiral Mago appeared off the Tiber with
120 ships, fearing Pyrrhus might conquer Rome and then cross to Sicily. Terms:

- Both parties affirm "all the previous agreements"
- Permissive mutual-aid clause: either party may make a formal military alliance
  (symmachia) against Pyrrhus; each may help the other in their territory if attacked
  (not obligatory)
- Carthage provides transport ships; each side pays its own men
- No Carthaginian sailor to be compelled to land against his will

**Significance**: The confirmation of "all previous agreements" presumably covered
whatever definition of Roman Italian interests had been agreed in 306. Scullard and
others argue that if the 279/8 treaty included a permissive clause allowing troop
movement into Italy or Sicily, it implies that a **prior prohibition** on such movement
existed — the strongest indirect evidence for the Philinus sphere-of-influence treaty.

## Peace of Lutatius (241 BCE)

The treaty ending the First Punic War (negotiated by Hamilcar Barca and C. Lutatius
Catulus, stiffened by Roman commissioners) terminated the treaty series as a
diplomacy between equals. Terms:

- Friendship between Rome and Carthage
- Carthage evacuates Sicily; no war on Hiero II or his allies
- Return all Roman prisoners without ransom
- 3,200 Euboeic talents total (Polybius's Book III account): 2,200 over 10 years +
  1,000 immediately (commissioners' addition to Hamilcar's draft)
- No fortification, recruitment, or public buildings in the other's territory

This treaty was embodied in an *epigraphe* (codicil) to which the Roman seizure of
Sardinia in 238 BCE was later unilaterally appended — Polybius's "contrary to all
justice." The subsequent embittering of relations effectively ended the treaty series
as a functioning diplomatic framework.

## Major Phases

| Treaty | Date | Driving Context | Change in Scope |
|---|---|---|---|
| First | 508/7 BCE | Republic's founding; Rome minor Latium power | Rome acknowledges Punic commercial dominance |
| Second | 348 BCE | After Gallic Sack; Rome recovering; Timoleon | Romans excluded from Sardinia/Libya; Spanish coast added |
| Possible | c. 343 BCE | Timoleon's Sicily; First Samnite War | Possible renewal, minimal new terms |
| Third | 306 BCE | Agathocles crisis; Third Samnite War | Probably expands recognition of Roman Italian interests |
| [Philinus] | 306? | Unknown | Possible spheres-of-influence clause — disputed |
| Pyrrhic | 279/8 BCE | Pyrrhus in Italy; Mago's fleet | Mutual-aid clauses; confirms previous agreements |
| Lutatius | 241 BCE | End of First Punic War | Subordinates Carthage; not a treaty between equals |

## Geographic Spread

The progression of the treaty series maps precisely onto **Roman expansion in Italy**:
the first treaty knows only Latium; the second adds Campanian names (Polybius's gloss);
the 306 and 279/8 treaties presumably encompass all Italy. As Rome grew, the zone of
Roman interests recognized by Carthage grew proportionally. The treaties are thus a
mirror of Roman Italian consolidation seen from the west.

## End Conditions

The treaty series as a framework of mutual accommodation was **effectively destroyed**
by the Roman seizure of Sardinia in 238 BCE and the humiliating terms imposed on
exhausted Carthage. Hamilcar Barca reportedly made his son Hannibal swear eternal
enmity to Rome (Polybius). The Second Punic War (218–201 BCE) was the consequence.
