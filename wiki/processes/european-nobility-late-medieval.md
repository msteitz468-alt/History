# The European Nobility in the Fifteenth Century

The transformation of the European nobility across the fifteenth century, as analysed
comparatively by **Philippe Contamine** in [[allmand-ncmh-v7-1998|NCMH VII]] (Ch. 4). Against the
old image of a decaying feudal order, Contamine argues that "the nobility of the *ancien régime*
dates from the fifteenth century" — a class that adapted, absorbed newcomers, and re-grounded
itself on service, surviving in France until 1789 and elsewhere "at least until the First World
War."

## Definition and Scope

The nobility is defined as the class which "perceived itself and was perceived by others as
occupying the apex of the social pyramid" within societies that wished to be hierarchical — a
hereditary order, recruited "by birth and legitimate male lineage," but in practice graded into
higher and lower strata (in Castile: *títulos*, *caballeros*, *hidalgos*, *escuderos*). Contamine's
method is deliberately **pan-European and comparative**: contemporaries themselves compared
nobilities (Louis XI wished to remodel the French nobility on English and Italian lines; Diego de
Valera's *Espejo de verdadera nobleza*, 1441, surveyed the differing rules of nobility country by
country).

A **European nobility** existed in a real sense — bound by intermarriage (Amadeus VIII of Savoy's
ancestry spanned France, Italy and the Empire), by international **chivalric orders** (the
Burgundian [[order-of-the-golden-fleece|Golden Fleece]], the English Garter), by heralds whose
"vocation and outlook were international," and by a shared Latin-derived vocabulary of rank (duke,
marquis, count, viscount, baron, knight, squire) and a common code of honour and *jus armorum*. Yet
each society had a distinct nobility: the Polish *szlachta*, the English peerage-and-gentry, the
Castilian *hidalguía*, and the Imperial *Adel* obeyed different rules of inheritance, derogation
and number.

## Causal Drivers

- **Wildly varying numbers.** The noble share of population ranged from ~0.25% (Denmark) and ~0.5%
  (Norway) through ~1.2% (England, ~60 peers + gentry) and ~1.5–2% (France, Aragon) to ~5%
  (Hungary, Scotland) and perhaps ~10% (Castile). A small nobility meant weak noble dominance; a
  superabundant one (Castile, Poland) meant widespread noble poverty.
- **Lineage extinction.** As a hereditary class the nobility suffered constant biological
  extinction — ~27% of English lordly lines per quarter-century; of 136 barons summoned in 1299,
  only 16 had male descendants in 1500; 68 of 138 Bavarian tournament families survived 1440–1500.
  This forced a continuous **influx of new members**, fuelling anxiety in a culture obsessed with
  ancestry.
- **The economic squeeze.** The fifteenth century was "in no way the golden age of landed revenues,"
  which often "fell catastrophically" (the [[late-medieval-crisis|agrarian depression]]). Nobles
  felt threatened by new legal, financial and administrative elites and turned increasingly to
  income from **office-holding** and state service.
- **The prestige of the noble ideal.** Despite talk of "crisis," the noble order was the most
  envied in society; candidates "jostled forward" for ennoblement, and rulers controlled this
  pressure through letters of ennoblement, grants of arms and the creation of knights.

## Major Phases / Comparative Structures

- **Derogation (*dérogeance*).** Whether nobility was compatible with trade and manual work varied
  sharply: forbidden and fiscally policed in France (with the Breton exception of "dormant"
  nobility), but happily combined with commerce in Venice and Genoa, and with humble trades among
  the Castilian *caballeros* and impoverished rural *hidalgos*. Bartolus of Sassoferrato's maxim —
  "whoever engages in the mechanical arts cannot be noble" — was the touchstone, increasingly
  qualified.
- **Bastard feudalism and clientage.** Classic fief-and-vassal [[feudalism|feudalism]] gave way to
  new bonds: English **indentures** (retainers wearing a magnate's livery for fees/annuities),
  Hungarian *familiaritates*, Spanish *criados*, French household service. These clienteles could
  congeal into "bands" and "factions" active in politics (the league of the Public Weal, 1465). But
  kings and princes had their own, usually wealthier, noble clients — so politics was never a simple
  king-versus-magnates struggle.
- **The titled high nobility** broke away as a magnate class of a few dozen families (49 Castilian
  *títulos/grandes* in 1474; ~60 English and ~60 Scottish peers; ~60 Hungarian barons holding 40%
  of the land). Poland was exceptional: the *szlachta* blocked hereditary titles and built a "noble
  democracy."
- **Toward the nobility of the *ancien régime*.** Service crystallised the future **noblesse de
  robe** beside the traditional **noblesse d'épée** in France; in the Empire the *Herren* and
  *Ritter* fused into the *Adel*. The Renaissance court "witnessed the flowering... of the
  courtier."

## Interaction

Bound up with the [[late-medieval-crisis|agrarian depression]] (which strained noble revenues and
drove some nobles to war and others to office), with the rise of the [[new-monarchy|new monarchies]]
(which both threatened and re-employed the nobility), with [[chivalry|chivalry]] and the chivalric
orders, and with the eastern divergence of [[second-serfdom|second serfdom]], where a powerful
nobility tightened its grip on the peasantry rather than losing it.

## End Conditions

The fifteenth-century reconfiguration did not end the nobility but founded its early-modern form:
a service-and-honour aristocracy, recruited through ennoblement as much as descent, that endured
across Europe into the modern era.

## Historiography

Contamine sets two portraits against each other: the nobility as a class in "serious crisis" —
demographically and economically weakened, intellectually underdeveloped, "on the verge of
forfeiting... its historic role" — versus the nobility as a resilient, adaptive order that
"transcending obvious differences... considered itself a single order, *una et eadem nobilitas*."
He prefers the second, treating the demographic "crisis" evidence (high extinction rates) as
partly an artefact of method (omitting daughters doubles the apparent rate) and noting that the
class replaced its short-fall "without difficulty." The "bastard feudalism" label is itself a
modern coinage describing the new man-to-man bond that replaced classic feudalism.
