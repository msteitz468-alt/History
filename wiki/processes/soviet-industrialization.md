# Soviet Industrialization
## Definition and Scope
**Soviet Industrialization** refers to the rapid, state-directed transformation of the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics (USSR) from a backward agrarian country into a major industrial superpower. Initiated under Joseph Stalin's First Three Five-Year Plans (1928–1941), the process was characterized by a massive concentration of national investment into heavy industry, machine-building, energy production, and military manufacturing. This crash program was financed internally by squeezing the agricultural sector, suppressing real wages, and enforcing strict labor discipline.

## Causal Drivers
- **Ideological Imperative:** Classical Marxism assumed that a socialist society could only exist on the basis of an advanced industrial economy. A dictatorship of the proletariat in a country where peasants formed over 80% of the population was considered an ideological anomaly (Line 5929).
- **National Security:** Stalin and his associates believed the USSR was surrounded by hostile capitalist states. In a famous speech, Stalin warned that the USSR had to catch up to the West in ten years or be crushed.
- **Economic Impasse of the NEP:** The mixed economy of the New Economic Policy struggled to generate the large-scale investments needed to build new factories, transport infrastructure, and power grids.

## Major Phases
- **The First Five-Year Plan (1928–1932):** Focused on heavy industry, which absorbed 86% of all industrial investment (Line 5941). Created whole new branches of industry (chemical, automobile, agricultural machinery, aviation) and built giant complexes like Magnitogorsk in the Urals and Kuznetsk in western Siberia.
- **The Second Five-Year Plan (1933–1937):** Placed greater emphasis on "mastering the technique" of production, including precision instruments and machine tools, while attempting to introduce better quality control and transport networks.
- **The Third Five-Year Plan (1938–1941):** Heavily militarized and shifted resources toward arms production, aircraft manufacturing, and the development of backup industrial bases east of the Volga (such as the Urals and Siberia) in anticipation of a German invasion.

## Geographic Spread
The process transformed the geography of the Soviet Union. While traditional centers like Ukraine, Moscow, and Leningrad were heavily expanded, massive new industrial zones were created in the interior: the Urals (Magnitogorsk), western Siberia (Kuznetsk), and later Central Asia, ensuring the country's strategic survival during World War II.

## Interaction
- **With Collectivization:** Industrialization was funded by the coercive collectivization of agriculture. The state bought grain from collective farms at low prices and sold it to consumers (or exported it) at a premium, utilizing the difference (along with a turnover tax) to fund heavy industry.
- **With Society:** The process generated a massive influx of peasants into the cities, creating an enormous urban proletariat. It also saw the rise of a new technical elite (engineers, managers) and led to chronic housing shortages and rationing.

## End Conditions
The initial phase of rapid industrialization was interrupted by the Nazi invasion in June 1941. During the war, the industrial infrastructure of the western USSR was either destroyed or evacuated eastward. Post-war reconstruction under the Fourth and Fifth Five-Year Plans resumed the same heavy-industry-focused model, which continued until Andropov and Gorbachev recognized the stagnation of this traditional "coal and iron" model in the computer age.
