## Definition and Scope

The economy of [[heian-kyo]] from its founding in 794 to the political collapse of the late twelfth century was an economy of consumption, patronage, and in-kind distribution rather than production or commercial exchange. In its early form, the capital was "almost exclusively a city of consumers, drawing into itself the produce, manufactures, and labor of the countryside; sending back little in return except orders, officials, and superfluous members of its own society; and producing solely (or virtually so) for its own uses" ([[cambridge-history-japan-v2-heian]], ch. 2). Over the course of the Heian period the economy transformed from near-complete dependence on statutory governmental revenues into a more complex system in which noble household economies, private landholding, and provincial patronage played central roles.

## Phase 1: The Statutory Economy (794–c. 900)

The city's founding economy was organized almost entirely around the distribution of governmental tax revenues in kind and labor levies. Under the statutory (*ritsuryo*) system, all holders of rank and office received payments in three forms:

1. **Land revenue assignments**: Tax revenues from specified amounts of land or households were assigned to rank- and office-holders. Revenues consisted of handicraft items (especially cloth) and agricultural products (rice and food).
2. **Labor assignments**: Noble and princely ranks received specified numbers of household officials, servants, and guards, provided partly through government labor levies.
3. **Stipends**: All officials received direct stipends from government treasuries in cloth, iron implements, salt, rice, other foods, and occasionally cash.

Most residents received the necessities of life directly through their incomes rather than through commerce. Those at lower levels supplemented income with small vegetable gardens, fishing, and hunting on the capital's outskirts, and with cultivation of rice and barley on vacant city land or adjacent fields.

Commerce was formally confined to two official markets (the East and West Markets), closely supervised by the Market Office, which fixed prices, weights, and measures; controlled merchant registration; and specified the types of goods to be sold. The markets alternated at half-monthly intervals so that one was always open. Both were subdivided into shop areas (*ichikura*), each devoted to a single kind of merchandise. A century after the capital's founding, sixty-seven kinds of goods were regulated; the total shop areas across both markets numbered eighty-four (fifty-one in the East, thirty-three in the West).

The goods sold ranged from staples (rice, barley, salt, bean paste, fruits, seaweed, cloth, clothing, dyestuffs, combs, tools) to luxury items (pearls, jade, weapons and armor, medicines, aromatics). The chief means of trade was barter; cash (Japanese copper coins) circulated only partially and with difficulty.

**Currency**: Japan minted twelve series of copper coins between 708 and 958 (eight during the Heian years), supplemented by one silver and one gold issue. Coins were legally overvalued relative to metallic content; new mintings exchanged at ten old coins per new coin. This caused hoarding, private minting, and eventual debasement. The coins fell out of use after the last minting in 958, replaced in the late Heian period by imported Chinese copper coins of the Northern Song.

## Phase 2: Contraction and Privatization (c. 900–c. 1050)

The deterioration of the statutory regime's revenue capacity produced fundamental changes in the capital's economy. Governmental revenues shrank; many offices ceased to exist or became paper organizations; palace workshops curtailed operations. The official class was drastically reduced, leading to the departure from the capital of large numbers of labor-levy workers and their descendants.

The great nobles and especially the Fujiwara regents absorbed a growing share of available income through:
- **Private landholdings** acquired directly
- **Commended *shoen***: estates commended to powerful noble houses in exchange for tax protection (see [[medieval-shoen]])
- **Gifts from provincial governors** (*zuryo*): wealthy provincial administrators who owed their appointments to senior nobles returned much of their profits to the capital as gifts
- **Patronage income**: the obligation of great nobles to receive household officials, attendants, and professionals in return for regular rewards in cloth, robes, and horses

The provincial governorship (*zuryo*) system became the chief engine by which new wealth entered the capital. Competition for rich governorships drove much of the political maneuvering within the [[kugyo-aristocracy]].

The official market system was in trouble by 835. By the 860s, merchants were placing themselves under the protection of noble houses, forming "gangs" (*shiirui*, possibly precursors of guilds) and overriding market regulations. By the early tenth century attempts to restrict trade to the official markets had become largely futile; private need and convenience determined commerce. The physical area of the markets tripled in the tenth century, but their regulatory character had dissolved.

## Phase 3: Household Economy and Urban Diversification (c. 1050–1185)

By the eleventh century the senior-noble household had become the primary economic unit of the capital. The great Fujiwara mansions functioned as command economies: they employed artisans (mostly migrants from contracting palace workshops), monks, scholars, warriors, and administrative officials; they held storehouses of luxury goods, cloth, foodstuffs, and craft materials; and they redistributed income to their clients and dependents in a patronage system that structured the entire court.

As palace artisans migrated to noble and princely households, they retained some formal obligation to the palace while working primarily for their new patrons. A few operated quasi-autonomously in the market. The weavers of fancy silks were among the last to leave the palace, maintaining a monopoly until the mid-eleventh century; by 1013 one was already working concurrently in a near-prince's household.

Commerce expanded despite — or because of — the collapse of official regulation. New commercial quarters emerged in the southeastern part of the city by the mid-eleventh century, generating street names derived from local concentrations of merchants and artisans: Salt Street, Oil Street, Needle Street, Brocade Street, Damask Street. The official markets eventually converted to private ownership and disappeared in name by the late twelfth century; the commercial center shifted to a north-south street near the center of the eastern half of the city (*Machi*/modern Shimmachi) and its intersections with Sanjo, Shijo, and Shichijo Avenues.

The urban population diversified significantly. New or expanded groups included:
- Provincial warriors serving in the Kebiishi office or regimental forces
- Transport workers handling the growing private flow of provincial goods
- Buddhist monks (likely in the thousands), supported by noble patronage and sustaining a wide range of artisans
- Peddlers, entertainers, gamblers, and a flourishing beggar society
- Rich former provincial administrators, sometimes targets of popular resentment
- Merchants no longer tied to official market registration

## Income Distribution and Wealth Concentration

The statutory code specified income by rank; the lowest noble (Fifth Rank) received approximately ten times the income of the highest non-noble official (Sixth Rank), and the Third-Rank noble received approximately ten times that of the highest Fourth-Rank noble. As revenues contracted, wealth concentrated further at the top: by the eleventh century the ordinary nobility was largely dependent on senior-noble patronage, and the regental Fujiwara houses controlled the greatest share of disposable capital in the city.

Great noble incomes were used substantially to employ large numbers of servants, laborers, nurses, guards, estate managers, and workshop artisans. Pay at these levels was almost entirely in goods rather than cash. The economy thus functioned as a cascade of in-kind redistribution from the top of the court hierarchy downward, with commercial exchange filling the gaps at each level.

## End Conditions

The Heian capital economy was structurally disrupted by the rise of warrior power in the late twelfth century. The Taira dominance of the 1160s–1180s shifted court patronage toward Taira military interests; Taira no Kiyomori's active development of trade at Owada-no-tomari (near modern Kobe) and his direct dealings with Song Chinese merchants represented a departure from the court's traditional passive attitude toward foreign commerce. The Genpei War (1180–85) and the establishment of the Kamakura shogunate transferred the economic and political center of the country away from [[heian-kyo]], beginning the transformation of the capital from a political command city into a commercial and religious city.

## Historiography

The classic study of the early Heian market economy is Abe Takashi, "Heiankyo no keizai kozo," in Ito Tasaburo, ed., *Kokumin seikatsu shi kenkyu*, vol. 2 (1959). The social history of the capital's economy from the noble household perspective draws on Yoshioka Saneyuki, "Kizoku shakai no seijuku," in Hashimoto Yoshihiko, ed., *Komonjo no kataru Nihon shi*, vol. 2: Heian (1991). The [[cambridge-history-japan-v2-heian]] synthesizes these and other Japanese scholarship for an English-language audience.

The chief historiographical question is whether the mid-Heian shift from statutory to household economy represents a fundamental transformation of political economy or merely a reorganization of the same elite's control over essentially unchanged flows of provincial surplus. The [[medieval-shoen]] system is central to this debate.
