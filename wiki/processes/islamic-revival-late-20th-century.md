## Definition and Scope

The Islamic Revival (also called the global Islamic resurgence or the *da'wa*
movement) denotes the wave of intensified public and personal Islamic piety that
spread across the Muslim world, and especially across Asia, from the 1960s
onward. It is not a single organization or doctrine but a cluster of parallel
phenomena: campus-based *da'wa* (call/invitation) activism modeled on the Muslim
Brotherhood's *tarbiya* (education) method; new visibility of veiling and modest
dress; growth of Islamic banking, education, and media; state-sponsored "official"
Islamization alongside grassroots and sometimes illegal movements; and the
religious effects of labor migration to the Gulf and elsewhere. It is distinct
from — though it overlaps and interacts with — [[political-islam]], which
concerns the pursuit of state power in Islam's name; the revival is as much about
personal and communal piety as about politics. Per Chiara Formichi (*Islam and
Asia*, ch. 6), the phenomenon is best understood comparatively across Egypt,
Pakistan, Malaysia, Indonesia, Central Asia, and China rather than as a
homogeneous "return to religion."

## Causal Drivers

- **Disillusionment with secular nationalism.** Post-WWII secular-nationalist
  development projects (from Morocco to Indonesia) had, by the 1960s–70s, failed
  to deliver promised prosperity and dignity, fueling a search for an Islamic
  alternative to failed developmentalism.
- **1967 Six-Day War.** Egypt's defeat was widely read as evidence of religious
  insufficiency rather than mere military failure, becoming a rallying point for
  political Islam across the Muslim world.
- **1973 oil boycott and Saudi wealth.** The Arab oil boycott failed as a direct
  political weapon but quadrupled oil prices, making Saudi Arabia the world's
  largest oil producer by 1976 and funding the global export of Wahhabi
  religious ideology.
- **1979 as a hinge year.** Within a single year: Zia ul-Haqq's Hudud
  Ordinances in Pakistan, Khomeini's return and the proclamation of the Islamic
  Republic of Iran, Saddam Hussein's accession in Iraq, and the
  [[soviet-war-in-afghanistan-1979|Soviet invasion of Afghanistan]] together
  marked a global turning point in the relationship between Islam and politics.
- **Campus-based organizing.** The *da'wa* method — gender-segregated study
  circles (*halaqa*) forming small "family" units (*usra*) — devised by Hasan
  al-Banna's Muslim Brotherhood (1928), was exported and adapted across Asia as
  a low-cost, replicable organizing technology.
- **Labor migration.** Migration to the Gulf (from the 1970s oil boom) and later
  to East Asia exposed millions of Asian Muslim workers to Gulf religious norms
  and, on return or from abroad, made visible piety (veiling, prayer) a marker
  of identity in new social settings.

## Major Phases

1. **1960s–70s — Ideological formation.** Disillusionment with secularism;
   founding of campus and youth movements (e.g., Malaysia's ABIM, Indonesia's
   Dewan Dakwah Islamiyah Indonesia); Tablighi Jama'at's early spread to Central
   Asia via Soviet students in India.
2. **1979 — Global rupture.** The Iranian Revolution, Pakistani Islamization,
   and the Afghan war reframed Islamic revival as a plausible political as well
   as personal project; Deng Xiaoping's China simultaneously reopened religious
   space after the Cultural Revolution (Bureau of Religious Affairs re-established
   1979), enabling a parallel Chinese Islamic revival.
3. **1980s — Institutionalization.** State co-optation and containment occur
   together: Malaysia's state sponsors "official" Islam (Islamic university,
   shari'a-compliant banks) under Mahathir while banning groups deemed
   threatening (Darul Arqam); Indonesia's usra/tarbiya study-circle movement
   spreads nationwide, engaging even with Iranian Shi'i thought despite
   Sunni-majority hostility; unregistered study circles in Soviet Central Asia
   study al-Banna, Qutb, and Mawdudi underground.
4. **Post-Soviet 1990s–2000s — Central Asian divergence.** Post-independence
   Central Asian states diverge sharply in tolerance: Kyrgyzstan accommodates
   [[tablighi-jamaat|Tablighi Jama'at]], while Uzbekistan under Islam Karimov
   tolerates only private, depoliticized piety and violently suppresses
   organized groups; Hizb ut-Tahrir and the Islamic Movement of Uzbekistan
   emerge as militant/political responses.
5. **1990s–present — Migration and consumer piety.** Indonesian domestic
   workers in Hong Kong and elsewhere become visible carriers of the revival;
   a Muslim middle class in Indonesia and Malaysia drives Islamic fashion,
   halal consumption, and Islamic finance as mass markets.

## Geographic Spread

The revival's Asian expressions varied by political context: top-down and
constitutionally entrenched in Pakistan (1973 constitution, 1979 Hudud
Ordinances); state-managed in Malaysia (ethnic politics after the 1969 Kuala
Lumpur race riots left Islam as Malay identity's primary marker); grassroots and
pluralistic in Indonesia (Suharto's New Order tolerated personal piety as an
anti-communist bulwark while suppressing organized political Islam); repressed
and clandestine under Soviet/post-Soviet rule in Central Asia; and state-managed
alongside genuine revival in China, where mosque-building and Islamic
associations expanded rapidly after 1979 despite continued Communist Party
control.

## Interaction

The Islamic Revival interacts closely with but is not identical to
[[political-islam]] — the Muslim Brotherhood-derived *da'wa* method underlies
both apolitical piety movements and explicitly political parties (e.g.,
Malaysia's PAS). It also intersects with [[islamic-modernism]] as a rival and
sometime ally: modernist reformers and revivalist activists both critique
"traditional" religious authority but differ on method and on the desirability
of state power. The revival's spread into Central Asia and China connects it to
the longer history of [[sufism]] and reformist networks documented for earlier
centuries.

## End Conditions

The process has no clear end point and is treated in the source as ongoing into
the 21st century, evolving into new forms (transnational Islamic feminism,
new-media religious authority, halal consumer markets) rather than concluding.
State responses continue to shape its trajectory unevenly across Asia — from
active promotion (Malaysia's "state Islam") to active suppression (Uzbekistan,
Xinjiang) — with no single regional resolution in view.
