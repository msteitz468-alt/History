## Definition and Scope

The Whitlam reform program was a concentrated burst of social-democratic legislation and executive action by the Whitlam Labor government (December 1972 – November 1975). The source characterises it as an attempt "to complete Labor's interrupted post-war social reconstruction project" — legislation that comparable advanced democracies (Western Europe especially) had enacted in the late 1940s and 1950s when left-of-centre governments built welfare states, but which Australia had delayed through 23 years of conservative government. The program ranged across education, health, welfare, law reform, foreign policy, urban development, Indigenous affairs, arts, and women's policy. By the time of the 1972 election, "Whitlamism had ballooned into a phantasmagoric manifesto comprising some 200 specific promises."

## Causal Drivers

- Twenty-three years of Labor opposition during which detailed reform programs had been developed
- The expanding post-war suburbs had outrun States' capacity to provide services (schools, sewerage, roads) — creating demand for Commonwealth intervention
- The baby-boom generation's entry into adulthood and the university-educated "new middle class" as new Labor constituents
- Whitlam's personal conviction that Commonwealth expansion was necessary and that "Labor had a plan for catching up with government provision in this late blooming social democracy"
- The momentum of the anti-Vietnam War movement and associated social movements (women's liberation, Aboriginal rights, environmentalism) which augmented Labor's promises between 1969 and 1972

## Major Phases

**Duumvirate fortnight (December 1972).** Rather than wait for results to be finalised, Whitlam and his deputy were sworn in as a two-man government. An avalanche of decisions followed: diplomatic recognition of Communist China; withdrawal of Vietnam advisers; abolition of conscription and release of draft resisters; ratification of UN human rights conventions; equal pay reopened; wine excise and contraceptive pill sales tax abolished; Aboriginal reserve mining leases suspended.

**Education (1973–75).** "Occupying pride of place in the program" — Whitlam called education "the great instrument for the promotion of equality." A needs-based formula for school funding "marked a watershed in the Commonwealth's movement into school education funding"; Commonwealth expenditure on schools trebled in two years to mid-1975. From 1 January 1974: Commonwealth assumed full responsibility for university funding, abolished tuition fees, introduced means-based living allowances for students.

**Health (1973–75).** Medibank — a national health insurance scheme — "weathered fierce resistance from the Coalition parties in the Senate, doctors, private hospitals and private health insurance funds, before finally coming into operation in September 1975." The government also boosted public hospital funding, instigated a community health program, and pioneered women's health services.

**Welfare (1973–74).** Benefits increased across the board; means test on aged pensions abolished; supporting mothers' allowance introduced.

**Law reform.** Attorney-General Lionel Murphy rushed through divorce regulation changes as a precursor to overhaul of family law; abolished the federal death penalty; introduced trade practices and racial discrimination legislation.

**Urban and regional development.** A new ministry invested heavily in rehabilitating inner-city areas and regional growth centres.

**Foreign policy.** Diplomatic recognition of China; assertion of autonomy within the US alliance; replacement of 'God Save the Queen' with 'Advance Australia Fair'; Order of Australia replacing imperial honours; UN multilateralism prioritised; Asia-Pacific engagement deepened.

**Aboriginal affairs.** Spending increased; National Aboriginal Consultative Committee created; royal commission into land rights; land title legislation drafted for NT reserves (the Bill was on the Senate noticeboard when the government was dismissed). In August 1975 Whitlam solemnised a Gurindji land transfer.

**Arts and culture.** Australia Council upgraded; Australian Film and Television School created; Sydney Opera House completed; national art gallery commenced; purchase of Jackson Pollock's *Blue Poles* for $1.3 million (became a symbol of Labor extravagance in opponents' attacks).

**White Australia policy ended.** Immigration Minister Al Grassby in 1973: "give me a shovel and I will bury it."

## End Conditions

The reform program was terminated by the [[dismissal-1975]] on 11 November 1975. The Fraser government dismantled some elements (Medibank was gradually wound back; university funding reduced; urban development ministry abolished) while maintaining others (Aboriginal Land Rights Act 1976 enacted the principle if not the specific legislation; multicultural policy continued). The overall effect was that social spending could not be reduced to pre-1972 levels and many Whitlam-era institutions persisted.
