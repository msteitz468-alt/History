## Definition and Scope

The *Kulturkampf* (literally "culture struggle" or "struggle for civilization") was a campaign of legislation conducted primarily by the Prussian state and then the [[german-empire]] against the Catholic Church and its institutional presence in German public life, running from the early 1870s to the late 1880s when Bismarck negotiated its end. The term was coined by the liberal parliamentarian, professor of medicine, and anthropologist Rudolf Virchow, who declared the campaign "a struggle for civilization" — positioning Catholicism as anti-modern and subversive of rational statecraft.

The term has two analytical dimensions that Smith's volume takes care to distinguish:
1. The *confessional* Kulturkampf: legislation targeting Catholic institutional power (seminaries, Jesuit order, school inspection, clerical appointments)
2. The *racial* Kulturkampf: anti-Polish policies in Posen and West Prussia that Bismarck himself described as the real heart of the campaign

Chapter 16 (Zimmerman) argues these cannot be separated: "The legal campaign that the Kingdom of Prussia undertook in the 1870s against its Catholic subjects constituted the first major instance in which German policy followed the racist logic of specifying and subordinating elements of a population as minorities."

## Causal Drivers

**Liberal ideology**: German liberals of the *Bildungsbürgertum* conceived of their project as assimilation to a universal standard of rational modernity. The Catholic Church — with its Papal Infallibility doctrine (1870), its rejection of the Enlightenment heritage, its Marian devotions, and its *Syllabus Errorum* (1864) condemning liberalism as error #80 — stood as the obvious obstacle. As Virchow put it, Catholicism had become "absolutely incompatible with the Kultur whose bearers we believe ourselves to be."

**Bismarck's calculations**: Bismarck's primary motive was less religious than political. He sought to destroy the institutional infrastructure of the Catholic Center Party, which had formed in 1871 as a confessional defense organization and quickly emerged as a major Reichstag force. He later acknowledged the campaign was driven "overwhelmingly" by "its Polish aspect" — the desire to suppress Polish national consciousness in the eastern provinces.

**Ultramontane consolidation**: The proclamation of Papal Infallibility in July 1870, immediately before the [[franco-prussian-war-1870]], "sharpened pointed differences" by making Catholic institutional loyalty to Rome a doctrinal matter. Lower-class Catholics in the Rhineland, many in employer-employee relations with Protestant industrialists, found the Marian devotions and Sacred Heart cult a form of communal solidarity against both liberal culture and economic subordination.

## Major Phases

**1871–1875 — Legislative assault**: May Laws (1873) gave the Prussian state authority over clerical appointments, required clergy to pass a "culture exam" in history, philosophy, and German literature, and subordinated church institutions to state inspection. The Jesuit order was expelled. Dozens of bishops were imprisoned or went into exile.

**1875–1878 — Stalemate and hardening**: The campaign intensified Catholic organizational solidarity rather than breaking it. "The 'culture war' had enabled this development. It emotionally anchored the German party system in the minds of the Catholic electorate." Between 1874 and 1884, the Center Party mobilized nearly 80% of all eligible Catholic voters. Catholic voluntary associations, media, and *Katholikentage* continually "restaged" the original sense of threat.

**1878–1887 — Retreat**: The death of Pope Pius IX (1878) allowed Bismarck to negotiate without appearing to capitulate. Anti-Socialist legislation (1878) provided a new enemy that allowed the government to begin dismantling Kulturkampf laws without liberal opposition, since liberals increasingly feared Social Democracy more than Catholicism. The formal end came gradually through negotiation with Leo XIII.

**Parallel anti-Polish measures (continuing beyond 1887)**: Between 1883 and 1885 Bismarck expelled approximately 32,000 Poles without Prussian citizenship. In 1886 the Royal Prussian Settlement Committee was established to purchase estates and place German settlers in West Prussia and Posen — described explicitly as "internal colonization."

## Geographic Spread

The Kulturkampf was primarily a Prussian phenomenon, though associated legislation extended to the Reich level. Its effects were geographically concentrated in:
- The Rhineland and Westphalia (industrial Catholic working class)
- Bavaria (rural and urban Catholicism in tension with liberal Munich)
- Posen and West Prussia (Polish Catholic population — the anti-Polish dimension)
- Berlin and the Protestant industrial north (the liberal protagonists)

The chapter emphasizes the explicitly European comparative dimension: "There were 'culture wars' in other European nation states too. Sometimes these were between Catholics and Protestants, and sometimes between secular liberals and Catholics." France, Italy, Belgium, and Switzerland saw parallel struggles. What was distinctive in Germany was the racial-ethnic dimension targeting Poles specifically.

## Interaction with Other Processes

The Kulturkampf was inseparable from [[german-unification]] and the [[franco-prussian-war-1870]]. The military victory over Catholic France provided the nationalist and ideological framework within which the domestic campaign was prosecuted. The Kulturkampf in turn shaped [[german-colonialism|German colonialism]]: "Many Germans applied the ideological concepts of the Kulturkampf, the 'struggle for civilization,' to their overseas colonial efforts." The concept of *Kulturvölker* (cultural peoples) versus *Naturvölker* (natural peoples) carried a direct genealogy from the domestic *Kulturkampf*.

The [[kulturkampf]] also established the conceptual apparatus that the Nazi regime would later adapt for racial policy — the legal specification and subordination of a population minority, the apparatus of "internal colonization."

## End Conditions

The Kulturkampf ended through negotiation rather than clear victory or defeat. The confessional legislative apparatus was largely dismantled; the ban on Jesuits persisted until 1917. But its political consequences endured:
- The Catholic Center Party's confessional identity remained robust until 1928
- The Settlement Commission continued purchasing Polish land until 1918
- Anti-Polish racial policy became a permanent feature of Prussian/German eastern policy
- The concept of *Reichsfeinde* (enemies of the Reich) — Catholics, Socialists, national minorities — established a political template that Bismarck subsequently applied to Social Democrats (Anti-Socialist Laws, 1878) and remained available for later appropriation

The Catholic social milieu created in the 1870s through the Kulturkampf's threat "shaped the German party landscape until 1928 when it ultimately eroded."

## Historiography

The principal historiographical question is whether the Kulturkampf was primarily a religious, a racial, or a political phenomenon — and who was responsible. An older liberal tradition presented it as Bismarck's cynical manipulation of liberal principles. Michael Gross (*The War Against Catholicism*, 2004) argued it was a "realization of liberal principles" rather than a distortion of them — the liberals genuinely believed in a civilization war against Catholicism. Zimmerman extends this to argue that liberal racism (cultural rather than biological) was the driving force, and that the anti-Polish dimension was not incidental but central. Helmut Walser Smith (*German Nationalism and Religious Conflict*, 1995, also cited in this volume) provided the foundational work on the Protestant-Catholic dynamic.

The comparative European dimension has been illuminated by Clark and Kaiser (*Culture Wars: Secular-Catholic Conflict in Nineteenth-Century Europe*, 2003), which situates Germany in a wider pattern of liberal-Catholic conflict across the continent.
