## Definition and Scope

The "early Muslim community" names the social formation that emerged from Muhammad's proclamation of Islam c. 610 CE and that, through successive transformations, became the caliphal state by the reign of 'Abd al-Malik (685–705 CE). This process encompasses five distinguishable phases: (1) the Meccan preaching period (c. 610–622); (2) the Medinan community-building period (622–632); (3) the conquest of Arabia and the Ridda Wars (632–634); (4) the great external conquests under the first four caliphs (634–656); and (5) the First and Second Fitnas (656–692). Throughout, a community defined by religious adherence rather than tribal kinship was constructing — and repeatedly contesting — its own form.

Hodgson's frame is essential: he treats the early Muslim community not as the automatic unfolding of a religious program but as a historically contingent social formation whose character at any moment reflected the political and material pressures acting on it. The community was not the same thing in 615 (a persecuted minority in Mecca), 628 (a regional state centered on Medina), 645 (a conquering empire), and 685 (a polity in civil war). Tracking its transformations is tracking the formation of a civilization.

## Phase 1: The Meccan Preaching (c. 610–622)

Muhammad began receiving revelations c. 610 — auditory experiences he interpreted as messages from the one God (Allah). "For a time, only his wife Khadijah and a few close intimates" acknowledged his mission. The early community was small, socially diverse (including both well-connected Qurayshi figures and dependent clients and slaves), and socially fragile.

The Quraysh resisted not only because Islam rejected their polytheism but because the Meccan political-religious system depended on religious pluralism: the Ka'ba's value as a pan-tribal shrine required neutrality. Muhammad's monotheistic exclusivism, and particularly "his challenge to existing cults in which the Quraysh took part," threatened this directly. Persecution followed, though Hodgson stresses it was modulated by tribal protection obligations — families could not simply kill members of other clans without risking blood feud.

The community during this phase was constituted by personal conversion and discipleship. "The first step in joining the Muslim community came to be abandonment of idolatry" — a social rupture, not merely an intellectual assent. Some Muslims were sent to Abyssinia (c. 615) to escape persecution, an early experiment with community in exile.

The Meccan community developed its devotional practices — collective prayer (*salat*), recitation of the [[quran]] — and its internal solidarity, but had no territory, no military capacity, and no governmental structure. It was a religious movement within an existing society, unable to realize the social vision its scripture articulated.

## Phase 2: The Medinan Community (622–632)

The [[the-hijra-622|Hijra]] of 622 transformed the community's social form. In Medina, Muhammad became not merely a prophet but an arbiter and commander. The "constitution of Medina" established Muhammad's authority over a multi-tribal, multi-religious polity; the *umma* was defined as a community of shared obligation cutting across tribal identity.

**The umma and its internal structure.** The new community divided into: (a) the **Muhajirun** ("emigrants," Meccan followers who migrated with Muhammad), who had high prestige but no economic base; (b) the **Ansar** ("helpers," Medinan converts), who hosted the emigrants; (c) Jewish clans of Medina, initially included in the constitutional framework but subsequently expelled (Banu Qaynuqa' in 624, Banu Nadir in 625, Banu Qurayza in 627); (d) pagan Arab groups in various degrees of alliance. The community was "becoming a complex and extensive society of heterogeneous elements."

**Military raiding as constitutive.** "Almost immediately, he began sending the Muhajirun out to raid." This was structurally necessary: the emigrants had no income, and the community needed to establish its economic and military credibility. The raid at Nakhla (January 624) — breaking a sacred month, shocking Meccan opinion — and the **Battle of Badr** (March 624, a decisive Muslim victory over a Meccan caravan escort) were pivotal. Badr "was certainly won" and gave the community its first major military victory, confirming its viability and greatly strengthening Muhammad's authority.

**Jewish expulsion and the redefinition of the umma.** The progressive expulsion of Medina's Jewish clans was both politically and religiously significant. Muhammad had initially oriented Islam toward Jerusalem in prayer and observed Jewish fasts; he had hoped for Jewish recognition of his prophethood. When this recognition was withheld — "Muhammad found that the Jews of Medina denied his prophethood" — the community's ritual independence was asserted: the *qibla* shifted to Mecca, the Ramadan fast was instituted, Islam "became ritually independent of previous monotheisms." The expulsions removed an internal challenge to Muhammad's authority and concentrated the *umma*'s Muslim identity.

**Law and community regulation.** The Medinan period saw the elaboration of Islamic law in practice. Key developments included: formalization of the *zakat* (wealth tax for community purposes); regulation of marriage and family law ("perhaps at the heart of any social structure is its family law"); military command structure; and the collection and distribution of booty. "The month of Ramadan, in which the battle of Badr was certainly won, was likewise instituted as a time of fasting." Each institution was both pragmatic and sacrally grounded in Quranic revelation.

**Conquest of Arabia.** By 628, Muhammad commanded enough force to negotiate directly with Mecca. The **Treaty of Hudaybiyya** (628) was a strategic concession (Muslims would not make the pilgrimage that year) that implicitly acknowledged Muslim power. In 630, Mecca fell with minimal resistance; the Ka'ba was purified of idols and rededicated to Allah. By 632, most of Arabia was either converted or subordinate. "In 632 Muhammad made the pilgrimage in person, establishing the forms of the pilgrimage which were to hold" — the *hajj* became an Islamic institution.

## Phase 3: The Ridda and Consolidation (632–634)

Muhammad died in June 632 "in the arms of 'A'ishah" without designating a clear successor. The community faced its first existential crisis: would it hold together? Many Arabian tribes had joined Muhammad through personal allegiance or political calculation rather than deep conviction; his death triggered widespread withdrawal of allegiance ("apostasy" or *ridda* in Islamic terms).

**Abu Bakr as first caliph.** The resolution — improvised, not planned — was the acclamation of **Abu Bakr** as *khalifa* ("successor," "caliph") to the Prophet. Hodgson explains: "Abu-Bakr and 'Umar are credited with persuading the Muslims of Medina" to accept unity under Abu Bakr rather than split; 'Umar "promised his loyalty to Abu-Bakr, and the Ansar yielded." The decision was taken at a gathering (*Saqifa*) of Medinan leaders, with Meccan emigrants arriving to assert their claim. This moment — unplanned, contested, made under pressure — became the foundational precedent for all subsequent debates about legitimate succession to Muslim leadership.

The **Ridda Wars** (632–634) suppressed tribal rebellions throughout Arabia. Abu Bakr "was able to impose his authority" through military campaigns that also served to redirect Arabian military energy outward. These wars were not purely about religious apostasy — some rebels accepted monotheism but refused to pay *zakat* to Medina — but became framed in Islamic terms as defense of the community's integrity.

## Phase 4: The Great Conquests (634–656)

Under 'Umar ibn al-Khattab (r. 634–644), the caliphal state launched the most consequential military expansion in late antique history. Within two decades, Muslim Arab armies conquered:

- **Syria and Palestine** (636 — Byzantine army defeated at the Yarmuk river)
- **Iraq** (637 — Sasanian army defeated at Qadisiyya)
- **Persia** (641–651 — progressive Sasanian collapse)
- **Egypt** (639–642 — Byzantine Egypt falls)
- Initial raids into **North Africa** and **Central Asia**

Hodgson's structural analysis of why this happened is important. The Byzantine and Sasanian empires had "lost all morale" after the exhausting wars of 602–628. In Syria, non-Chalcedonian Christians were "persecuted by the Christian empire" and did not fight hard for Byzantium; in Iraq, Sasanian administrative breakdown had already occurred. The Arab armies were not overwhelming in size — they prevailed through superior morale, unity, and the paralysis of their opponents.

The conquests transformed the community's character. From an Arabian commonwealth, the caliphal state became a trans-regional empire governing millions of non-Muslim subjects. 'Umar's response was to create a system of **garrison cities** (*amsār*) — Kufa and Basra in Iraq, Fustat in Egypt, later others — designed to keep Arab Muslim fighters physically separated from the conquered populations and economically dependent on state pensions rather than local agriculture. "He distributed the revenues from the conquest individually in pensions to the men," creating a warrior aristocracy with a financial interest in continued conquest. The system recognized "that conquest was the keynote of the Muslim state."

Under **'Uthman ibn 'Affan** (r. 644–656), the community's internal tensions deepened. 'Uthman, of the Umayyad clan, favored relatives in provincial governorships. The garrison soldiers in Iraq and Egypt resented what they saw as favoritism and deteriorating pay as conquests slowed. The compilation and standardization of the [[quran]] under 'Uthman (suppressing variant recensions) was a major achievement but also a source of controversy. By 655, dissatisfied soldiers had converged on Medina; in 656, 'Uthman was murdered.

## Phase 5: The First and Second Fitnas (656–692)

The murder of 'Uthman opened the **First Fitna** (civil war, 656–661): see [[first-fitna-656]]. Its core issue was the legitimacy of succession. **'Ali ibn Abi Talib**, Muhammad's cousin and son-in-law, was acclaimed caliph at Medina but faced opposition from 'A'ishah (Muhammad's widow) and her allies at the Battle of the Camel (656), and then from **Mu'awiya ibn Abi Sufyan**, the Umayyad governor of Syria, at the inconclusive Battle of Siffin (657). 'Ali's acceptance of arbitration alienated his most extreme supporters, the **Kharijites**, who seceded and assassinated him in 661.

Mu'awiya then united the empire as the first Umayyad caliph, ruling from Damascus (661–680). His system was pragmatic — relying on Syrian Arab tribal support, acknowledging the role of the Quraysh, and building a hereditary principle. When he designated his son **Yazid** as successor, the dynastic principle came into explicit conflict with the elective/consultative ideal.

The **Second Fitna** (680–692): see [[second-fitna-680]]. It began with **Husayn ibn 'Ali's** attempt to raise Kufa against Yazid; his killing at **Karbala** (680) became the founding martyrdom of Shi'i Islam. The fitna widened: **Ibn al-Zubayr** claimed the caliphate from Mecca; Kharijite rebellions spread; the Shi'i movement at Kufa produced **Mukhtar al-Thaqafi**'s revolt (685–687) in the name of 'Ali's son. The Umayyad general Hajjaj put down Ibn al-Zubayr at Mecca in 692, ending the fitna and establishing the Umayyad caliphate on a more coercive, imperial basis.

By 692, the community that had formed around Muhammad's preaching had become a state apparatus governing the former territories of two great empires. Its internal diversity — Arab tribal identities, differing claims about legitimate rule, emerging regional interests — had been exposed by the fitnas; their suppression had not resolved these tensions but institutionalized them.

## Causal Drivers

**Prophetic charisma and institutional gap.** Muhammad combined religious and political authority in his own person. No institutional mechanism for transferring this authority existed; its absence drove the First Fitna.

**Material interests of the conquest class.** The garrison soldiers who formed the backbone of the state had economic claims on the state (pensions, booty shares) that became politically explosive when conquests slowed and distribution was perceived as unfair.

**Tribal identity under Islamic framing.** Tribal allegiances persisted beneath the Islamic universalism of the *umma*. The division between early Meccan converts, Medinan Ansar, and later converts from conquered peoples created status hierarchies that generated grievance.

**The structural tension between empire and community.** The caliphal state was simultaneously a religious community (requiring moral legitimacy and consultative governance) and a conquest empire (requiring military discipline and centralized authority). These demands repeatedly conflicted.

## End Conditions

The process ends with the consolidation of the Umayyad caliphate under 'Abd al-Malik (r. 685–705), who stabilized the empire, Arabized the administration, and established the caliphate on explicitly imperial — rather than prophetic-communal — foundations. This marks the transition from the "early Muslim community" as a formative process to the Umayyad caliphate as an established state. The religious and political questions left unresolved — who may legitimately govern? by what principle? — continued to drive Islamic history in the forms of Sunnism, Shi'ism, and Kharijism.

## Umma as a Contested Textual Category (Formichi)

Chiara Formichi's [[formichi-islam-and-asia-2020|*Islam and Asia: A History*]] (2020) adds a
complementary reading of the *umma* concept sketched above: she stresses that the Qur'an's own
boundaries for "community of believers" are not fixed across the text — ranging from encompassing
all humankind (Q 10:19) to marking Muslims specifically as "the best community" (Q 3:110) — which
Formichi (2020) reads as a textual trace of the very transformation this page narrates, from the
Meccan "persecuted minority" community (Phase 1) to the Medinan "self-ruling community" with Muhammad
as political leader, military commander, spiritual guide, and religious teacher (Phase 2). She frames
this as the Qur'an's own record of "becoming Muslim" as an evolving, rather than static, category — a
useful gloss on this page's broader argument that the *umma*'s character differed sharply at each
phase of its formation. See [[muhammad-and-the-rise-of-islam]] for the fuller Formichi discussion of
the 'Uthmanic codex and the Meccan/Medinan periodization.

## Historiography

**Source problems.** The early Muslim community is documented by the *sira* (biographical) literature, *hadith* (traditions attributed to Muhammad and Companions), and early Islamic chronicles — all compiled generations after the events and shaped by the controversies of the Umayyad and Abbasid periods. The Quran is the only contemporary primary source. Hodgson is explicit about these limits.

**Scholarly debates.** Major disputes: the authenticity of *hadith* and *sira* as historical sources (the tradition from Ignaz Goldziher through Joseph Schacht to Patricia Crone and Michael Cook that skeptics have built); the original character of the "constitution of Medina"; the causes and conduct of the great conquests; the nature of the Ridda Wars; the degree to which early Islam represented genuine religious transformation versus tribal/political movement in Islamic dress.
