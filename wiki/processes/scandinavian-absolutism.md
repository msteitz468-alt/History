# Scandinavian Absolutism

The consolidation of royal absolute power in the Nordic monarchies in the later
seventeenth century, a regional variant of European [[absolutism]]. In
[[denmark-norway|Denmark–Norway]] absolutism was instituted abruptly in 1660–61
after defeat by Sweden and the bankruptcy of the aristocratic regime, formalised
in the *Kongelov* (Royal Law, 1665) — the most thoroughgoing absolutist
constitution in Europe. In [[swedish-empire|Sweden]] Karl XI established royal
absolutism in 1680 through the *reduktion* (resumption of crown lands from the
nobility) and the consent of the Riksdag. The systems rested on fiscal-military
needs, professional bureaucracies, and the regimentation of society and church.

## Definition and Scope

Royal absolute power in the two Nordic monarchies in the later seventeenth century.
"Absolutism was introduced in Denmark in 1660 and in Sweden in 1680 at the latest …
due, among other things, to growing tensions between the estates and the growth of
bureaucracy" (L1385–1387). The two cases share a structure but differ in ideological
basis: Denmark grounded absolutism in a written **[[kongelov|constitution]]** and a
*translatio* of power from the estates (soon acquiring theocratic colour), while
Sweden framed Karl XI's autocracy as the *restoration* of pre-aristocratic royal
power (L16804–16806).

## Causal Drivers

"In both Scandinavian kingdoms, absolutism arose after a war which had operated as a
catalyst for structural problems. During the war, power had in real terms shifted to
the monarch, and after the war the estates had had to solve the problems of the peace
… the king was able selfishly to exploit differences between the estates"
(L16442–16450). In Denmark the trigger was the crushing debt (~5 million rigsdaler)
left by the Karl X Gustav wars; in Sweden the long minorities of 1632–72 and the
fiscal crisis of crown-land donations. The weakening of the aristocratic
"community of interest" with the crown under the cost of standing armies was the
deeper cause.

## Major Phases

- **The 1660 Danish coup.** The Copenhagen estates meeting tasked with financing the
  war debt deadlocked over noble tax exemption; the lower estates proposed converting
  the elective monarchy to a hereditary one and "under military pressure the council
  was forced to accept" (L15880–15882). [[denmark-norway|Frederik III]] had the
  estates cancel the coronation charter; the 1661 *Act of Hereditary Autocratic
  Government* "gave the king not only the right of inheritance but also absolute
  power" (L15899–15901). Contemporaries and historians read "the acceptance, under
  intimidation … [as] a coup d'état" (L16900–16901).
- **The Kongelov (1665).** "Uniquely in Europe, an actual constitutional basis for
  absolutism and for succession" (L15902–15903) — see [[kongelov]].
- **Karl XI's Swedish absolutism (1680).** The Riksdag of 1680 declared the king
  "bound only by the law of the land" and reduced the council to discretionary
  advisers — "simply the king's true men and subjects" (L16407–16412): a
  *reinterpretation* of law, not a new constitution. In 1682 the king won rights to
  conscript, repossess crown lands, and frame legislation; the 1693 sovereignty
  declaration held him "not bound to answer to any earthly power" (L16440–16441).
- **The reduktion.** The Swedish nobility's share of royal estates had risen from a
  third to two-thirds via donations; the *reduktion* reversed this (in Finland the
  noble share fell from ~60% back to 5%), ruining magnates like Magnus Gabriel de la
  Gardie and funding the self-financing **indelningsverk** allotment army
  (L16299–16303, L17739–17749, L17061–17063).

## Geographic Spread

Denmark–Norway (and its North Atlantic dependencies) and Sweden–Finland (and the
Baltic provinces). The Danish state, administratively too thin to operate alone,
co-opted the manorial nobility as local tax assessors and collectors (L17334–17337);
a full register of Danish landed property (1688) underpinned both taxation and the
1701 conscription militia.

## Interaction

Absolutism was the political settlement of the
[[struggle-for-baltic-supremacy|Baltic wars]] and rested on the
[[fiscal-military-state-scandinavia|fiscal-military state]] and
[[militarisation-of-scandinavia|militarisation]]. It reorganised the nobility:
Denmark's 1671 introduction of counts and barons and a table of ranks made
office, not birth, the basis of rank (L16983–16986). It accompanied intensified
religious and social regimentation (confessional discipline).

## End Conditions

Opposition was mostly crushed (Oluf Rosenkrantz in Denmark, 1681; Johann Reinhold
Patkul in Swedish Livonia, executed — and a later architect of Sweden's downfall).
In Sweden absolutism ended after Karl XII's death, when "based on the theory of
natural law, in 1719 the estates took back their legitimate power" (L17097–17100),
inaugurating the Age of Liberty. Danish absolutism endured until the constitution
of 1849.
