## Definition and Scope

Media in African history refers to the process by which successive communications technologies — print, telegraph, film, radio, television, and internet — have been adopted, adapted, and contested across the African continent from the nineteenth century to the present. As James Brennan argues in [[parker-reid-oxford-modern-african-history-2013]], African media "do not represent a singular advance towards a universal modernity but rather the particular adaptation of globalized technologies to fit historically specific social formations."

The central analytical question is the transformation of African public spheres: from "segmented knowledge" in precolonial societies, where "knowledge is by definition unequally distributed," to the emergence of publics in which texts are addressed simultaneously to anonymous, co-present collectivities. Drawing on Karin Barber's theoretical framework, Brennan defines a "public" as an audience whose members "are not known to the speaker/composer of the text, and not necessarily present, but still addressed simultaneously, and imagined as a collectivity." This transformation is driven not merely by new media technologies but by a fundamental shift in the relationship between power and knowledge.

The process is inseparable from colonial history: African media were "exogenous and often 'colonial' in origin" — the printing press, telegraph, radio, and film were all introduced under colonial auspices. Yet "African actors played a determinative role" in appropriating and redirecting these technologies for nationalist, religious, and social purposes.

## Causal Drivers

**Colonial communications infrastructure**: European conquest was accompanied by incorporation into global telegraph networks — North Africa (1868), East and South Africa (1879), West Africa (1886). Colonial railways, roads, telegraph lines, and radio networks all linked provincial centres but were "systematically extraverted through capital cities to metropolitan hubs," making communication between neighbouring African countries often more difficult than between Africa and European metropoles.

**Missionary print culture**: The first African newspapers appeared in Egypt (1797), South Africa (1800), Sierra Leone (1801), and Liberia (1826). Missions drove the initial spread of print, deploying literacy as a tool of religious and social transformation. The Anglican Henry Townsend published Nigeria's first newspaper, Iwe Irohin (1859), in Yoruba — likely the first vernacular newspaper in West Africa. According to J. D. Y. Peel, its purpose was not merely to promote literacy but "that sense of individual inwardness, of solitary self-motivated quest, so integral to the evangelical sensibility."

**African appropriation**: Diaspora editors and independent African newspapers challenged missionary and colonial print from the mid-nineteenth century. Figures including Edward Wilmot Blyden (Liberia, Sierra Leone), Charles Bannerman (Gold Coast), and later Mohandas Gandhi (South Africa), Nnamdi Azikiwe, and Kwame Nkrumah built newspapers as nationalist political organs. "Nationalism was also a literary undertaking" (Heather Sharkey on Sudan) — the press was central to anti-colonial politics across British colonies.

**State monopolization after independence**: Between the mid-1960s and early 1990s, most postcolonial states converted newspapers, radio, and television into instruments of nation-building and party loyalty. Nkrumah articulated the ideal: "The true African journalist...works for the organ of the political party to which he himself belongs." Many independent newspapers "disappeared along with political oppositions." Cold War powers — primarily Britain (Reuters) and France (AFP) — dominated international news content for postcolonial African media.

**Technology waves**: Each successive global communications technology arrived in Africa and was adapted to local conditions. Radio became dominant from the 1950s–60s; television expanded rapidly after 1975; mobile phones and internet from the late 1990s.

## Major Phases

**Pre-colonial communications**: Oral transmission dominated. The drum (ngoma) functioned as a media technology — transmitting information across communities — but was limited by language difference. Knowledge was "segmented" among precolonial elites who controlled access. Arab script and manuscript culture thrived in North Africa and along trans-Saharan trade routes; Swahili literate culture existed on the East African coast; Islamic education spread Arabic literacy across West Africa.

**Missionary and coastal print culture (1797–1880s)**: First newspapers established in European-controlled coastal cities. Content was largely in European languages or mission-supervised vernaculars. Print culture was geographically confined to ports and mission stations. Pioneering editors from the African diaspora (Blyden, Russwurm) initiated pan-Africanist intellectual traditions.

**Interwar print expansion and nationalism (1920s–1940s)**: Print culture expanded rapidly beyond coastal towns. Post volumes tripled between 1920 and 1938 in the Belgian Congo, Kenya, Nigeria, Tanganyika, and Uganda. Reading groups in Tanganyika in the 1930s often had 100+ listeners per newspaper copy — actual readership far exceeded subscription figures. Literary clubs and fraternal lodges created distribution networks for independent nationalist newspapers. This was "the golden age of independent African newspapers."

**Radio and the colonial public sphere (1927–1960)**: The first radio station opened in South Africa (1924), followed by Algeria (1926) and Kenya (1927). Short-wave radio was adopted quickly by French and more slowly by British colonies. Before independence, radio listening was predominantly a public rather than domestic experience — crowded around shared loudspeakers. Northern Rhodesia's "Saucepan Special" battery-operated radio (1949) was a turning point for African listenership. Numbers of sub-Saharan receivers grew from 460,000 in 1955 to 4.8 million in 1965.

**Decolonization and state media (1960–1990)**: Radio was the primary medium of anti-colonial liberation movements — Nasser's Egypt broadcast in Swahili, Somali, and Hausa to challenge colonial states; Brazzaville's high-wattage station was deployed by de Gaulle and then by leftist Congolese governments. After independence, state monopolies over broadcasting were nearly universal. Censorship was systematic. Television arrived in Nigeria in 1959 and spread across the continent through the 1960s–70s, but access remained overwhelmingly urban. Television's Africa-wide share of global receivers was 0.3 per cent in 1965, rising to 2.6 per cent by 1989.

**Deregulation and digital media (1990–present)**: Media liberalization from the early 1990s produced commercial FM stations, community radio, and independent newspapers across most of Africa. Rwanda's Radio-Télévision Libre des Mille Collines (RTLM, 1993) entered world-historical infamy for fomenting and coordinating genocide in 1994 — the most destructive deployment of African radio's capacity to incite violence. Social media played "an unquestionably important role in the popular overthrow of Egypt's President Hosni Mubarak" (2011). Mobile phone and internet penetration accelerated rapidly from the early 2000s.

## Geographic Spread

Media penetration followed colonial infrastructure and urbanization patterns. West African coastal cities (Lagos, Accra, Freetown, Dakar) were the earliest centres of African print culture. South Africa had the most developed media infrastructure on the continent throughout the twentieth century. North Africa (Egypt, Algeria) was incorporated earliest into global telegraph networks. Sub-Saharan interior territories received radio only in the 1950s–60s; television reached rural areas only from the 1990s–2000s with cheap Chinese-made sets.

Language was a critical variable. In East Africa, Swahili served as a regional lingua franca enabling vernacular radio to create trans-ethnic publics. In most of Africa, colonial languages (English, French, Portuguese) were the languages of nationalism, not vernacular languages, which meant that Anderson's "print capitalism" model for imagined national communities required modification for multilingual African contexts.

## End Conditions

The process is ongoing. The internet and mobile phone revolution since the late 1990s has accelerated media transformation at a pace exceeding all previous communications revolutions combined. State monopolies over information have weakened but not disappeared; concentration of global media ownership (Western news agencies, streaming platforms) persists. The challenge Brennan identifies for future research is integrating the "political economies in which media are embedded" with the better-studied intellectual and ideological histories of African public spheres.

## Historiography

**Source quality**: Pre-1950 African media history is poorly documented — few broadcasts survive, newspaper archives are incomplete, and quantitative data on distribution and readership are thin. Colonial administrative files and audience letters supplement what survives. After 1950, quantitative surveys exist. The field has been dominated by synchronic social-science media studies; historical approaches are a minority strand.

**Scholarly debates**: (1) Whether African media follow Benedict Anderson's print capitalism model for nation formation — the consensus is that they do not, for linguistic and structural reasons. (2) The degree of postcolonial press freedom vs. state control and how to measure it. (3) "Hate radio" — the degree to which RTLM caused Rwanda's genocide vs. reflecting existing polarization. (4) Whether digital media empowers African civil society or primarily benefits authoritarian surveillance.

**Recent revisionism**: The shift from normative press-freedom frameworks to political economy analysis; the ethnographic turn (Debra Spitulnik on Zambia, Brian Larkin on Nigeria, Harri Englund on Malawi) studying how media are actually received; the reintegration of oral media (radio trottoir, "pavement radio") into the history of African communications.

**Collection coverage**: The Bibliotheca Alexandrina is weak on post-1750 Africa generally and on media studies. The Parker–Reid Oxford Handbook chapter by Brennan provides the primary framework. Deep engagement requires Brian Larkin's Signal and Noise, Karin Barber's The Anthropology of Texts, Persons and Publics, and specialist country studies.
