# Byzantine Iconoclasm

## Definition and Scope

**Iconoclasm** ("image-breaking") was the imperial Byzantine policy, pursued in two phases
(c. 726–787 and 815–843), of prohibiting and destroying the veneration of religious images
(icons), and the bitter controversy it provoked. In [[mckitterick-ncmh-v2-1995|NCMH II]]
(McCormick, Ch. 14; Brown, Ch. 13) iconoclasm is treated above all for its impact on the
**relations between Byzantium and the West** and on Byzantine Italy.

## The Controversy

Beginning under the emperor **Leo III** (c. 726) and intensified by **Constantine V**, iconoclasm
set the iconoclast emperors and much of the army and episcopate against the **iconophiles** —
especially the monasteries and the populace of regions such as Italy and Greece. The first phase
was ended by the **Second Council of Nicaea (787)** under the empress Irene, which restored image
veneration; a **second wave (815–843)** followed before icons were finally and permanently restored
in 843 — the **"Triumph of Orthodoxy,"** still commemorated in the Orthodox church.

## Western Reception

Iconoclasm sharpened the **estrangement of eastern and western Christendom**. In Byzantine Italy the
imperial decrees were widely resented (NCMH II, Brown), contributing to the alienation of the papacy
and population that helped doom the [[exarchate-of-ravenna|Exarchate]]. In the Frankish world,
[[charlemagne|Charlemagne]] commissioned a distinctive Frankish position — the **_Libri Carolini_** —
that rejected *both* the destruction *and* the (as the Franks understood it) "adoration" of images
endorsed at Nicaea II, asserting Frankish theological independence and putting Charlemagne forward as a
leader of Christendom. Iconoclasm thus fed the wider **"two emperors" tension** and the divergence of
Latin and Greek Christianity, of which the later **Photian schism** was another symptom.

## Significance

Iconoclasm shaped Byzantine identity (the eventual, definitive defence of images became central to
Orthodox theology and art) and deepened the cultural and ecclesiastical separation between Byzantium and
the Latin West that runs through the whole of NCMH II's treatment of East–West relations.

## Historiography

The sources are overwhelmingly **iconophile** (written by the victors after 843), making the iconoclasts'
own theology hard to recover and prone to caricature. The depth of popular support for iconoclasm, its
relationship to the contemporary rise of Islam (with its own aniconism), and its economic and political
(versus purely theological) motives are all debated. NCMH II uses it chiefly as a lens on East–West
relations rather than as an internal Byzantine narrative.

## Gibbon, *Decline and Fall* (1776–1788) — Chs. XLIX–LIV

[[sources/gibbon-decline-and-fall-1776|Gibbon]] folds iconoclasm into East–West schism and a long chain from Paulicians to Reformation — Enlightenment Protestant teleology, not modern Byzantinist consensus.

- **Image-worship “stole into” the church:** By insensible degrees; Jews and Muslims charge Christians with idolatry from Law and Koran; Leo’s reform resists popular deities.
- **Greek fire vs. iconodule fleet:** Archipelago islands arm against the emperor; “their miracles were inefficient against the Greek fire.”
- **Italy and the papacy:** Italians swear to die for pope and images; withhold tribute; destroy statues of Leo; liberty of Rome rescued after 750 years (see [[papacy]]).
- **Paulicians (Gibbon’s reformation seedbed):** Anti-image “Manichaean” branch; exile scatters “seeds of reformation” in the West; creed reduces images, relics, cross, eucharist to mortal workmanship; persecution produces armed revolt with Saracen alliance (Tephrice, Chrysocheir).
- **Thrace transplant → Albigensians → Luther:** Constantine Copronymus and Tzimiskes transplant Paulicians to Thrace/Philippopolis; Western diffusion yields Albigensians; spirit reappears in Wycliffe, Huss, Zwingli, Luther, Calvin — Gibbon’s teleology of private judgment (philosophic balance-sheet of Reformation ends the chapter).
