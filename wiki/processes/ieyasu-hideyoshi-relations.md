## Definition and Scope

The relationship between [[tokugawa-ieyasu]] and [[toyotomi-hideyoshi]] from the death of Oda Nobunaga (1582) to Hideyoshi's death (1598) is one of the defining dynamics of Japanese political history. It shaped the terms of Japan's unification, determined the allocation of power that would eventually produce the Tokugawa shogunate, and illustrates the particular logic of competitive submission that characterized Sengoku-period politics at its highest level.

This process is distinct from a simple conflict or alliance. It was a sustained, evolving relationship of mutual assessment, strategic manipulation, and calibrated concession — in which both parties correctly understood that the other was irreplaceable and dangerous, and in which the outcome was permanently undecided until Hideyoshi's death left Ieyasu as the dominant surviving force.

## Causal Drivers

**Structural asymmetry**: After Nobunaga's assassination, Hideyoshi moved faster and more decisively to secure Nobunaga's legacy than any other successor candidate. By the time Ieyasu absorbed Kai and Shinano, Hideyoshi had already defeated Shibata Katsuie and positioned himself as the preeminent Sengoku warlord. Ieyasu was always playing catch-up, though he was far more powerful than any other potential challenger.

**Mutual indispensability**: Hideyoshi needed Ieyasu's acquiescence because military conquest of the Tokugawa five-province base would be expensive and uncertain — as the Komaki-Nagakute campaign (1584) demonstrated. Ieyasu needed to avoid direct confrontation with Hideyoshi's larger coalition. Both had stronger incentives to manage the relationship than to destroy it.

**Coalition management**: Hideyoshi's power depended on maintaining an ever-larger coalition of daimyo. Having Ieyasu as a subordinate ally, rather than an enemy, dramatically increased Hideyoshi's capacity to project power eastward. Ieyasu as an ally could be used to pacify the Kantō; Ieyasu as an enemy was a permanent threat at Hideyoshi's rear.

## Major Phases

**Phase 1: Parallelism and Implicit Rivalry (1582–1584).** After Nobunaga's death both men operated independently. Ieyasu seized Kai and Shinano; Hideyoshi defeated Shibata. They exchanged ceremonial gifts and congratulations — Ieyasu sent the tea jar Hatsubana; Hideyoshi responded with a fine sword — while maneuvering for position. Hideyoshi arranged court promotions for Ieyasu, probably to create obligation.

**Phase 2: Open Confrontation (1584).** The [[komaki-nagakute-campaign-1584]] was the only period of direct armed conflict. The campaign ended in tactical stalemate: Nagakute was an Ieyasu victory, but Hideyoshi's strategic position was unassailable. Peace was made through Oda Nobuo's independent settlement with Hideyoshi, leaving Ieyasu without a pretext for continued hostilities but with his military reputation enhanced.

**Phase 3: Diplomatic Maneuvering and Ieyasu's Isolation (1584–1586).** Hideyoshi used military success in Shikoku and Kyushu to complete the encirclement of the Tokugawa. He subverted Ieyasu's retainers (Ishikawa Kazumasa defected in 1585; Sanada Masayuki shifted allegiance) and sent repeated diplomatic missions. Ieyasu refused to come to the capital, brushed off emissaries, and sought to strengthen his eastern flank by visiting the Hōjō at the Kisegawa frontier (1586), performing elaborate deference to maintain that alliance.

**Phase 4: Submission on Terms (1586).** The marriage of Hideyoshi's half-sister Asahi-hime to Ieyasu, combined with the extraordinary gesture of sending Hideyoshi's mother to Okazaki as effective hostage, enabled Ieyasu's visit to Osaka and formal acknowledgment of Hideyoshi's supremacy. Ieyasu secured three key conditions: his chosen heir Hidetada would succeed regardless of any son born of the marriage; Hidetada need not go as a hostage; and the four provinces would be inherited intact. The relationship became formally hierarchical, with Ieyasu as a powerful subordinate daimyo rather than an equal.

**Phase 5: Joint Operations and Transfer (1587–1590).** Ieyasu participated in Hideyoshi's Kantō campaign against the Hōjō (1590) as a loyal subordinate, bearing the heaviest mobilization burden. His reward — and strategic removal — was the exchange of his ancestral five provinces for the eight Kantō provinces and the new capital of Edo.

**Phase 6: Kantō Development and Korean Campaign (1590–1598).** Ieyasu spent the early 1590s consolidating the Kantō base. He participated marginally in the Korean campaigns but was not assigned a major command, remaining in Japan as a power-in-reserve. Hideyoshi's death in 1598 left Ieyasu as the dominant figure among the five regents (Go-Tairō) appointed to govern until Hideyoshi's son Hideyori came of age.

## Geographic Spread

The relationship operated across the full arc of Honshu: the theater of confrontation (Owari/Mikawa, 1584), the diplomatic axis (Okazaki/Osaka), and the eventual Kantō base. It was always fundamentally about control of the central highway corridor linking the capital with eastern Japan.

## End Conditions

The process ended with Hideyoshi's death on the eighteenth day of the eighth month of 1598. Sadler records his final instructions to the regents: "I think it unlikely I shall last out today. When I am gone hold services for me as a Deity for five days, but for certain reasons I think it would be well to conceal my death as long as possible. And don't forget all I have told you about Hideyori's succession." Hideyoshi died at sixty-three. The proverb that Sadler cites is apt: "Ieyasu won the Empire by retreating."

## Historiography

Sadler follows Tokutomi Soho's analysis throughout, treating both men as rational strategic actors. Tokutomi's interpretation — that Ieyasu's patience and calibrated submission was a form of strategic genius, not weakness — became the dominant frame in twentieth-century Japanese historiography. Western historians have generally accepted this reading while noting its circularity (the outcome makes the strategy look brilliant). The Taikoki (pro-Toyotomi) and the Mikawa Fudoki (pro-Tokugawa) provide competing versions of nearly every episode; Sadler negotiates between them without fully declaring a methodology. The "Ieyasu won the Empire by yielding" formulation appears in multiple chronicles and is probably a retrospective construction, but it captures a structural truth about the period.
