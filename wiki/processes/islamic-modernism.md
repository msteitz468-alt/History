## Definition and Scope

Islamic Modernism designates the intellectual and reformist movement — emerging primarily in Egypt and India in the second half of the nineteenth century, spreading across the Arab world and beyond in the early twentieth — that sought to reconcile Islam with modern science, rationalism, and the social demands of the [[great-western-transmutation]], while rejecting both simple Westernization and conservative quietism. It is distinct from the [[salafiyya]] (which it overlapped but from which it differed in emphasis), from Wahhabism (which it opposed on theological grounds), and from Kemalist-style laicism (which it regarded as capitulation).

The defining intellectual commitment was the reopening of *ijtihad* — independent legal and theological reasoning — against the prevailing norm of *taqlid* (following established precedent without re-examination). As Hodgson frames it, Muhammad Abduh "rejected [taqlid] not in favour of Westernization ad libitum, but of Muslim ijtihad — again taken in a correspondingly broad sense: the free exploration, within the originally established rules of legal inquiry and the moral norms of Islam, of what was best here and now."

## Causal Drivers

**The crisis of legitimacy produced by European hegemony.** Conservative Islam appeared to offer no path forward; outright Westernization required abandoning the community's identity. Islamic Modernism proposed a third way: return to the sources of Islamic civilization, purged of later accretions and re-read in the light of modern knowledge. This was not mere apologetics — it was a genuine reckoning with the structural challenge.

**The collapse of the taqlid consensus.** By the nineteenth century, *taqlid* had expanded (in popular understanding, if not in formal theology) beyond its technical meaning in jurisprudence to become a general disposition rejecting all things Western as alien. Islamic Modernism attacked this conflation directly, insisting that the rejection of new knowledge was itself a later accretion, not an Islamic imperative.

**The Arabic literary and intellectual revival.** Especially in Egypt and Syria, the revival of classical *Fus̝ā* Arabic and the recovery of the 'Abbasid intellectual heritage created an intellectual environment in which the Modernists could argue that classical Islamic rationalism (Mu'tazilism, the Faylasuf tradition) was as intellectually rigorous as European positivism. 'Abduh acknowledged the influence of Auguste Comte's positivism while arguing that Islam rather than Comte's invented religion could meet the persistent human need.

**The Sufi and guild collapse.** The disintegration of the Sufi tariqa orders (undermined by the failing of guilds and government seizure of waqfs) and the decline of the Persianate cultural world left a vacuum. Islamic Modernism, in both its Abduh-style and its Salafi variants, stepped into this vacuum, offering a reformed Shari-minded Islam in place of what it characterized as Sufi quietism and superstition.

## Key Figures and Institutions

### Jamal al-Din al-Afghani (c. 1839–1897)

The catalyzing figure. See [[al-afghani]]. His pan-Islamic vision — that each Muslim people must revitalize itself and then co-operate in resisting Western domination — provided the political framework within which Egyptian, Iranian, and Indian Modernists operated. He denounced Sufi quietism and ulama obscurantism; called for a "Muslim Luther"; urged reception of modern science combined with Islamic *asabiyya* (group solidarity). He was willing to operate as an instrument of 'Abdülhamid's pan-Islamism and simultaneously encourage forces hostile to it — a contradiction that ultimately imprisoned him.

### Muhammad Abduh (1849–1905)

See [[muhammad-abduh]]. The systematic theologian of Islamic Modernism. Afghani's most influential Arab disciple. Where Afghani was peripatetic and political, Abduh was institutional and pedagogical. As mufti of Egypt he reintroduced ijtihad into Islamic law; his Quranic commentary demonstrated the possibility of re-reading sacred texts within modern frameworks without introducing "radically new principles." He fought superstition and corruption; reformed institutional charity; attempted (imperfectly) to modernize al-Azhar. His influence was "in part a personally moral one" and in part the intellectual legitimation of the classical Muslim-Arab heritage as a valid foundation for modern society.

### Rashid Rida (1865–1935)

See [[rashid-rida]]. Abduh's Syrian disciple and editor of *al-Manar* (The Lighthouse), the most widely read Arabic journal of religious reform. Where Abduh remained flexible and contextual, Rida moved in a more strictly Salafi direction, eventually linking the movement to the Wahhabi–Saudi alliance. This trajectory illustrates the internal tension within Islamic Modernism between liberal reinterpretation and Salafi purification.

### Aligarh Movement (India)

Founded by Sayyid Ahmad Khan (by 1875). Sought to demonstrate that Islam was compatible with Western science and education, enabling Indian Muslims to enter the colonial educational and administrative system. Parallel in intent to Abduh's project but working in a directly colonial rather than nominally sovereign context.

## Major Phases

### First Generation: Political Awakening (1860s–1890s)

Afghani's campaigns across India, Egypt, Iran, and the Ottoman empire; the journal *al-'Urwa al-wuthqa* published in Paris (1884) with Abduh; the tobacco boycott in Iran (1891). This phase was primarily political — arousing Muslim nations to resist European dominance through internal renewal.

### Second Generation: Institutional Reform (1890s–1920s)

Abduh's work as mufti and reformer of al-Azhar; the Aligarh movement in India; proliferation of the Arabic reformist press. This phase was primarily educational and legal — demonstrating that ijtihad could produce outcomes compatible with modern life without abandoning Islamic identity.

### Third Generation: Divergence (1900s–1930s)

The movement split into several trajectories:
- The [[salafiyya]] proper (Rashid Rida and successors): Arabist, Shari-minded, increasingly aligned with Wahhabism, hostile to Sufism and popular Islam.
- Arab liberal nationalism: Abduh's followers like Qasim Amin and Taha Husayn who prioritized social reform (women's emancipation, Fus̝ā Arabic as national medium) over specifically religious concerns.
- Political Islam: movements like the Muslim Brotherhood (founded 1928, outside this source's range) that drew on Modernist vocabulary but rejected the accommodation with Western political forms.

## Geographic Spread

The process was centered in Egypt and Syria, spread to India and Malaysia, and had secondary effects in Iran (via Afghani). Cairo served as the hub: al-Azhar (however imperfectly reformed) was the premier Sunni educational institution; the Arabic press (partly staffed by Syrian Christian intellectuals committed to Arabic revival) reached the Maghrib, West Africa, and Southeast Asia. As Hodgson notes: "Cairo, as the most active centre of the Jamā'ī-Sunnī eastern Arabs, finally attracted into its orbit in greater or less degree the Maghrib and West Africa and most of the Southern Seas region."

## End Conditions / Ongoing Character

Islamic Modernism did not end but transformed. It was partially absorbed into nationalist movements (Egyptian nationalism, Arab nationalism) and partially radicalized into Islamist political movements. The core tension it identified — between the need to engage modern knowledge and the need to maintain Islamic identity — remains structurally unresolved and continues to generate reformist movements.

## Historiography

Hodgson's framing insists that Islamic Modernism was not mere apologetics or superficial accommodation. He stresses the seriousness of Abduh's theological engagement and the genuine intellectual achievement represented by his Quranic commentary. He also notes the limits: Abduh's solution "found it unnecessary to introduce any radically new principles, such as a general principle of historical cultural relativity which would put in question all the imagery of the texts." This preserved Islamic Modernism's Islamic character but also its ceiling.
