# Early Modern Migrations (1400–1800)

## Definition and Scope

The early modern centuries saw a transformation in the **character**, not merely
the volume, of human migration (Dirk Hoerder, CWH VI). The deep human story of
movement (see [[migration-in-human-history]]) continued, but it was reshaped by
the fusion of **state power with mercantile investment** — armed trade backed by
public-funded soldiery — which replaced older protocols of unarmed trade and
induced or enforced labor, refugee, and settler migrations across the globe. The
process is best grasped as the interaction of a few "armed and numerically few"
European men with the far larger movements of forced and free labor they set in
motion, against the backdrop of migrations within all the world's societies that
the European-centered story tends to obscure.

Hoerder insists this was **not** the spread of a developed core to a backward
periphery: capitalist development and producer migrations had emerged
independently across Asia and the eastern Mediterranean. What changed was the
imposition of armed European power on less well-armed peoples — the so-called
"age of discovery" reread as an age of imposition.

## Causal Drivers

- **Armed state-commercial enterprise.** Iberian and then Dutch circum-African
  and oceanic outreach combined state revenue and soldiery with private
  investment, monetizing and militarizing long-distance exchange.
- **Demand for unfree labor.** Plantation and mining production for European
  markets required mass labor where wages would not attract free migrants,
  producing several forced-labor regimes (see below).
- **Demographic and subsistence pressure.** In rural regions worldwide only two
  of a family's surviving children could remain on the land; the rest migrated.
  The Little Ice Age (c.1550–1800) intensified rural displacement.
- **Religion and war.** Expulsions (Jews and Muslims from Iberia, 1492–8), the
  Reformation's religious wars and the Thirty Years' War, and Habsburg–Ottoman
  contests generated refugee generations.
- **Conquest and settler colonialism.** State-backed settlers dislodged resident
  ("First") peoples — the "peopling" of the Americas reread as a *re*-peopling.

## Major Phases / Forms

- **Six post-1450 transformations** (Hoerder): the Chinese diaspora in Southeast
  Asia; new Iberian–Asian mobility; bound Africans to Iberia; chattel slavery and
  the plantation belt; American population collapse and resettlement; and
  armed circum-African outreach.
- **The Atlantic slave trade.** Roughly twelve million enslaved Africans
  force-migrated c.1500–1870s (the great majority after 1700), into the
  Caribbean, Brazil, Spanish America, and — a small share — British North
  America. See [[atlantic-slave-trade]].
- **Mining and the Andean mita.** From 1574 the Potosí *mita* forced Andean
  people across a 1,400-km belt to deadly silver labor; labor-reservoir
  populations collapsed. Contrast the free Chinese credit-ticket miners of the
  Malay Peninsula.
- **The fur belt.** Company men from Moscow to Montréal formed unions with
  resident women "according to the customs of the country," producing Métis
  populations and reorganizing Indigenous economies.
- **European settler and indentured migration.** Up to one-half to two-thirds of
  European migrants to the Americas before 1800 came under **indenture**;
  deportation peopled Australia; the Spanish, French, Dutch, Russian, and
  British settlements were internally polyglot.

## Geographic Spread

Global, but uneven: the Atlantic became a single migratory arena (Africa →
Americas forced; Europe → Americas settler/indentured); the Pacific saw a brief
Manila trans-Pacific system; the Indian Ocean and Southeast Asian seas absorbed
European armed traders and a growing Chinese diaspora; and vast internal
migrations continued within China, the Islamic world, and the Americas.

## Interaction

The intermediaries and "go-betweens" of migration (transactional, physical, and
"representational" brokers; landlord–stranger reciprocity) are the human basis of
[[intercultural-relations-early-modern|cross-cultural encounter]] and
[[interpolity-law|interpolity law]]. The process is the demographic engine of the
[[early-modern|construction of a global world]] and of [[proto-globalization]],
and it overlaps with the [[atlantic-slave-trade]], the
[[columbian-exchange-1492|Columbian Exchange]], and
[[mesoamerican-state-formation|the collapse of American polities]].

## End Conditions

The Enlightenment and the Atlantic Revolutions (1776–1821), together with
economic recalculation, began to transform forced-labor regimes and migration in
the nineteenth century, when the patterns described here gave way to the mass
free and indentured migrations of the industrial age.

## Historiography

Hoerder's "bird's-eye view" deliberately foregrounds the breadth of human
agency — people moving to feed themselves, escape violence, or evade exactions —
against heroic "pioneer" narratives and the racialized lore of white settler
mass migration. He stresses how the nineteenth-century "national identity"
paradigm of ethnicity distorts the actual craft- and religion-based,
trans-regional logic of migrant decisions.
