# Interregional Interactions in Mesoamerica

## Definition and Scope

Long-distance exchange, horizon styles, political contacts, and migration narratives across [[mesoamerica-culture-area|Mesoamerican]] regions and their frontiers ([[northern-arid-zone|Northern Arid Zone]], [[intermediate-area|Intermediate Area]], South America). Mesoamerica’s original culture-area designation rested on shared traits (maize/beans/squash + *coa*; 260-day calendar; Rain God and Feathered Serpent; autosacrificial bloodletting, etc.) once explained as **diffusion** (passage of ideas/goods with few carriers) and **migration** (permanent transfer of sizable populations with cultural baggage). Early–mid twentieth-century models presupposed unilinear elaboration via successive innovating cores radiating outward (Edward Schortman & Patricia Urban, EW2001).

The central issue is not whether diffusion and migration occurred, but that “a coherent body of archaeological theory specifying the behavioral significance of intersocietal interaction has not yet been articulated” (Schortman & Urban). Recent approaches include world-systems applications (Aztec, Teotihuacan, Monte Albán), trade-sourcing science, and elite monopolies of exotic goods; migration remains undertheorized.

## Causal Drivers

- **Elite status-goods exchange** from the advent of sedentary villages (c. 2000–1500 B.C.); by 1100 B.C. shared symbols/iconography on crafted items indicate ideas as well as finished goods transmitted via elite down-the-line networks (Gary M. Feinman; Jeremy A. Sabloff).
- **Obsidian** as a pan-regional industrial commodity (Pastrana & Athie): sources cluster in the Mexican Neovolcanic Axis + Sierra Madre Occidental and ~900 km south from W Honduras to Guatemala/El Salvador Pacific. Pico de Orizaba, Zaragoza, Altotonga, El Chayal, San Martín Jilotepeque, and Ixtepeque dominated successive Formative–Postclassic circuits.
- **Transport constraints** (Ross Hassig): land limited to foot traffic—no wheels (except figurines) or draft animals; professional *tlamemes* stage ~5 leagues/day, load ~23 kg; canoes ~40× more efficient than porters where usable—so cities controlled relatively small economic hinterlands unless water-linked.
- **South American maritime links** for metallurgy into West Mexico (Hosler; Pollard); Motagua jadeite for greenstone networks into Intermediate Area and West Mexico.

## Major Phases

### Early Formative village networks

Early Formative villages exchanged obsidian, iron-ore mirrors, jade/greenstone, feathers, marine shells, and probably cacao. Similarities in decorated pottery and figurines suggest highland societies formed one major interaction zone and lowland societies another (John E. Clark). Craft specialization focused on status goods (mirrors, shell, jade) sometimes “attached” to emergent chiefs.

### Classic Teotihuacan-centered sphere

Middle Classic [[teotihuacan|Teotihuacan]] influence structured pan-Mesoamerican interaction via enclaves, formal relationships, and commodity exchange. Linkages visible at Cholula, Monte Albán, Kaminaljuyú, Matacapan, Tikal; *talud-tablero* and slab-footed tripod cylinders spread widely. Coexisting more local cores: Monte Albán and Tikal. **Cacaxtla** shows contacts with Maya area, Oaxaca, Xochicalco, and Basin of Mexico and mural syncretism of Maya and central Mexican traditions (Santley; Parsons; García Cook). “It is clear that Teotihuacan played a major political, economic, and ideological role in pan-Mesoamerican affairs” (Santley).

### Epiclassic–Postclassic migrations and world system

Standard migration narratives include Gulf Coast Nonoalca + Chichimec → Toltec; Olmeca-Xicalanca; Chontal-Putun “mexicanized” Maya to Seibal, Altar de Sacrificios, Becan, Chichén; Pipil-Nicarao Nahua trek; Quiché/Cakchiquel/Tzutujil warrior bands. Diagnostic trait laundry list (*talud-tablero*, Quetzalcoatl/Tlaloc/Xipe, captive sacrifice, I-shaped ball courts, *tzompantlis*, fine-paste/Tohil Plumbate, Pachuca green obsidian, Chac Mools, etc.) is eclectic, not a required package.

Postclassic sea trade around Yucatán (Chontal) increased volume of long-distance exchange after decline of Teotihuacan, Monte Albán, and major Classic Maya centers c. A.D. 650–950. Three major Postclassic social trends—city-state growth, trade/markets, empire expansion—were interconnected within a **preindustrial world system**: a geographically extensive network of independent polities linked by a common division of labor and by stylistic interaction; cut short by Spanish conquest starting 1519 (Michael E. Smith).

### Mesoamerica–South America

Contact after initial settlement is rarely demonstrated archaeologically. Overland down-the-line trade from early Formative is earliest; eastern coastal canoe trade grows Late Classic; Pacific deep-sea raft models lack Galapagos pre-Columbian presence. No materials of Mesoamerican manufacture have been found in South America; southern gold at Copán, Altun Ha, and Chichén Itzá is small/portable. Metallurgy’s appearance in Mesoamerica Late Classic–Early Postclassic may reflect contact or value-system change; independent invention of West Mexican metallurgy remains possible. “On the basis of current archaeological and historical evidence, it appears that contact between Mesoamerica and South America was indirect” (Karen Olsen Bruhns). West Mexican copper-bronze techniques show Ecuadorian/Colombian styles via maritime trade; Michoacán early Postclassic copper via coastal trade (port of Zacatula at Balsas mouth ethnohistorically used for six-month stays).

## Geographic Spread

Core highland–lowland Mesoamerica; Gulf–Isthmus–Pacific corridors; Yucatán maritime routes; West Mexico–Ecuador/Colombia metallurgy corridor; Motagua jade into Costa Rica; Plumbate from [[soconusco|Soconusco]] across Postclassic networks; turquoise and macaws on the northern frontier.

## Interaction

Related to [[mesoamerican-state-formation]], [[teotihuacan-trade-network]], [[mesoamerican-preclassic]], [[greater-mesoamerica]], and regional culture histories. Interaction both **produced** the culture-area package and was **constrained** by transport technology and elite monopolies of exotics.

## End Conditions

Spanish conquest reconfigured but did not erase interaction networks; colonial and modern indigenous exchange continued under new constraints. The preindustrial world-system of competing city-states and tribute empires was cut short starting 1519 (Smith).
