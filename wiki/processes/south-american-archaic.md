## Definition and Scope

The Archaic stage in South America refers to the long transitional era between the initial Paleoindian colonisation (~12,000–10,000 BP) and the full establishment of food-producing, ceramics-using societies. It is characterised by broad-spectrum foraging — intensive exploitation of diverse plant, aquatic, and terrestrial resources — as opposed to the Paleoindian emphasis on large gregarious prey animals. Lynch notes the fundamental logic: "The Paleoindians were relatively highly specialized hunters and gatherers, particularly in the beginning, as they entered this New World; from specialized and focused extraction, they turned, in the Archaic stage, to very generalized hunting and gathering; finally, with agriculture, American peoples focused again on a narrow range of especially productive herd animals and concentrated plants."

The Archaic is not a discrete horizon but a continent-wide process that played out at different speeds and through different pathways in the coast, highlands, and forests. It was "continuous and progressive," with no sharp boundary separating it from either the Paleoindian or the subsequent Formative/ceramic stages.

## Causal Drivers

1. **Megafaunal extinction:** The loss of horses, mastodons, ground sloths, and glyptodonts — whether through Paleoindian over-hunting, postglacial climate change, or both — removed the primary prey of the focal Paleoindian economy. Peoples were "forced" into broader exploitation strategies.
2. **Postglacial environmental restructuring:** Rising sea levels (flooding former continental shelves and destroying coastal Archaic sites), expanding forests, and changes in vegetation zonation opened new ecological niches — the maritime fringe, tropical forest interiors, and high Andean grasslands — that had not been heavily exploited.
3. **Demographic pressure:** "Population rose rapidly during the Archaic stage, to judge from the far greater number of sites, found not only in the open and thinly forested habitats suitable for Paleoindian group hunting, but also in the closed forest, along the seashore, and even in extremely arid zones."

## Major Phases

### Early Archaic (~10,000–7,000 BP)

Continuation of broadly Paleoindian subsistence patterns but with diversifying stone tool assemblages (smaller projectile points, more grinding stones) and early use of maritime and forest resources. In the Peruvian highlands, the Puente culture (~11,000–9,000 BP, Ayacucho Basin) and the Jaywa culture (~9,000–7,800 BP) typify the transition — continuing camelid and deer hunting but adding triangular, leaf-shaped points and evidence of broader plant consumption. On the Peruvian coast, the **La Paloma** site (fog forest near the Pacific; ~8,000–5,000 BP) documents semi-sedentary fishing-collecting communities without agriculture: "Subsistence focused on marine resources: fish, sea mammals, and invertebrates."

In the far south, epi-Paleoindian adaptations based on surviving camelids (guanaco, vicuña), deer, rhea, and rodents persisted on the Patagonian steppe and high Andean punas throughout and beyond the early Archaic.

### Maritime Fringe Adaptation (~10,000–3,000 BP)

One of the most archaeologically visible Archaic trajectories. On the Peruvian coast, the Paijanense (coastal, ~10,000–7,500 BP) combined marine and terrestrial resources. In Chile, the littoral Archaic is dated as early as 9,680 BP at Quebrada Las Conchas near Antofagasta, with camelid, sea lion, dolphin, fish, and shellfish. Lynch emphasises that this maritime adaptation "seems more characteristic of the Postpaleoindian Archaic stage" — the large-scale exploitation of the sea was not typical of Paleoindians.

Brazilian coastal **sambaquis** (shell middens) begin accumulating no earlier than ~6,000 BP (accessible sites), though earlier ones were probably destroyed by postglacial sea-level rise. These shell-mound peoples had diverse tool kits in stone, bone, shell, wood, and other materials and consumed a mixture of marine and terrestrial resources. Dental wear and caries patterns in some sambaqui populations suggest a diet high in carbohydrates, possibly indicating incipient cultivation.

### Forest Adaptation (~8,000–3,500 BP)

With postglacial warming and humidity expansion (~6,500–4,000 BP), broad-leaved forests extended through the Paraná, Uruguay, and Jacuí valleys of southern Brazil. The **Humaita tradition** (dense forest zones) and **Umbu tradition** (open savannah with Araucaria forest) diverged as distinct adaptations: Umbu maintained bifacially worked projectile points and long-distance hunting over open country; Humaita used robust, primarily unifacial tools for digging and woodworking, possibly reflecting early horticulture. The **Itaparica tradition** (upland tropical parkland, ~11,000–8,000 BP) used unifacial scrapers and very rare projectile points, exploiting deer, rodents, birds, fish, and molluscs across a broad spectrum.

In [[amazonia]], the process took a different form. Early Archaic preceramic cultures (Itaparica continuation, Caverna do Gavião) maintained broad-spectrum economies. But strikingly, floodplain and estuarine zones of the Amazon/Orinoco basin witnessed the **earliest pottery in the Americas** (Taperinha ~7,000–6,000 BP, Alaka ~6,000–4,500 BP) associated not with agriculture but with specialised aquatic foraging — "the sedentism achieved by aquatic foraging, in turn, may have stimulated the initiation of plant cultivation."

### Agriculture and Sedentism (~7,000–3,000 BP)

The transition to food production was "gradual... different in all regions of South America, insofar as the record is known." Key pathways:

- **Highland Andean pastoralism/horticulture:** Camelid domestication (llama, alpaca) probably begins in the high puna by ~6,000 BP; guinea-pig keeping is attested earlier. Root crops (potato, oca, ulluco), tubers, and industrial plants (cotton, gourd) precede staple maize, which does not become important in Peru until the Formative (~3,000 BP).
- **Coastal Peru sedentism:** Late Archaic coastal sites (Aspero, El Paraiso, La Galgada) ~5,000–3,000 BP show permanent nucleated settlements with monumental architecture built primarily on marine protein. Roosevelt (Ch. 4) summarises: "the first cultures with populous settlements, monumental ceremonial constructions, and fine art seem to have combined foraging and incipient farming rather than practiced intensive agriculture."
- **Lowland root-crop horticulture:** Manioc (*Manihot esculenta*), bitter and sweet varieties, appears to have been the first staple crop of Amazonian horticulturalists, inferred from ceramic griddles in Formative-period sites (~4,000–2,000 BP). Maize does not become a staple food in most of Greater Amazonia until ~AD 1000.

## Geographic Spread

| Region | Archaic Trajectory |
|---|---|
| Pacific Coast (Peru/Chile) | Maritime foraging → Late Archaic sedentary settlements → monumental architecture |
| Central Andean Highlands | Broad-spectrum hunting (camelids/deer) → pastoralism → incipient cultivation |
| Southern Cone (Patagonia/pampas) | Epi-Paleoindian hunting persists longest; Umbu/Humaita forest/savannah split |
| Brazilian planalto | Itaparica → diversified hunting-gathering → maize cultivation ~3,500 BP (Minas Gerais) |
| Atlantic coast (Brazil) | Sambaqui maritime cultures → incipient coastal agriculture |
| Amazonian floodplains | Early pottery fishermen-foragers → manioc horticulture → late prehistoric chiefdoms |

## End Conditions

The Archaic does not end sharply but dissolves into the **Formative** (ceramic-using, horticultural village) stage at different times in different regions: ~4,000 BP in Amazonia and Ecuador; ~3,000 BP in coastal and highland Peru; later in Patagonia and the far south where agriculture never became important. The process of "agriculturalisation" was gradual. Lynch concludes: "the Archaic stage represented a major change in the quality, orientation, and style of life. Broadly based subsistence systems were accompanied by experimental horticulture, varying degrees of sedentism... and technological proliferation."
