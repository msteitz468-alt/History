# The Bourbon Reforms (and the Pombaline Reforms)

The **Bourbon Reforms** were the eighteenth-century drive by Spain's new Bourbon
dynasty to **recentralize, tax, defend, and rationalize** its American empire —
reversing the decentralized, locally captured order of the late Habsburg period
(see [[colonial-spanish-american-administration]]). Their Portuguese counterpart was
the **Pombaline reforms** in Brazil. Together they form the climactic, contradictory
phase of [[colonial-latin-america|colonial Latin America]]: they extracted
unprecedented revenue and revitalized the peripheries, but in alienating the Creole
elite they helped prepare the empire's collapse into
[[spanish-american-independence|independence]].

## Causal Drivers

The **Treaty of Utrecht (1713)** that ended the War of the Spanish Succession
confirmed the Bourbon Philip V but hobbled Spain, forcing trade concessions (the
English *asiento* and annual ship) that legalized rivals' access to colonial markets.
A century of contraband and military defeat had left foreigners dominating the
Indies trade. The **British capture of Havana (1762)** was the great shock that
accelerated reform. The animating ideologies were **mercantilism** (foster colonial
production for the metropolis; exclude rivals) and **regalism** (assert royal
authority over all rival institutions, above all the Church) — sharpened by the
**Enlightenment**.

## Major Phases

- **Early Bourbon (Philip V – Ferdinand VI, 1713–59).** Administrative recentralization:
  the **secretary of state for the Indies** stripped the Council of the Indies of
  power after 1717; the House of Trade moved to **Cádiz** (1717); tax farming gave
  way to direct collection; **register ships** progressively replaced the moribund
  fleet system; trading companies (the Caracas/Guipuzcoana Company, 1728) and the
  **Cuban tobacco monopoly** (1717) boosted revenue.
- **The Caroline Reforms (Charles III, 1759–88; continued under Charles IV).** The
  intense phase. Its instruments:
  - **The intendant system** — championed by **José de Gálvez**, rolled out from the
    1760s–80s, replacing the corrupt *corregidores*/*alcaldes mayores* with
    salaried, peninsular, merit-appointed **intendants** to tighten revenue
    collection and administration (it raised costs more than it improved rural
    justice, since underpaid *subdelegates* drifted back to the *reparto*).
  - **New viceroyalties and territorial reorganization** — **New Granada** (1739),
    **Río de la Plata** (1776), new captaincies-general (Venezuela 1777, Cuba 1764),
    and new audiencias, redistributing power and wealth toward the **peripheries**.
  - **Military reform** — expanded standing armies and disciplined **militias** (from
    ~8,000 to ~29,000+ troops), funded by surplus colonies subsidizing strategic
    ones (Mexican silver bankrolling Cuba).
  - **Comercio libre ("free trade within the empire," 1778)** — opening many
    Spanish and American ports to one another, ending the Seville/Cádiz monopoly;
    it boomed peripheral exports (Buenos Aires, Caracas, Havana) and integrated the
    empire economically.
  - **Regalism and the [[spiritual-conquest-and-colonial-church|Jesuit expulsion
    (1767)]]** — the assault on Church autonomy: parishes transferred from regulars
    to seculars, ecclesiastical immunity curtailed, and the **Society of Jesus
    expelled** (2,100+ from the mainland), its vast estates confiscated and sold —
    devastating the Paraguay Guaraní missions and feeding market expansion.

## The Pombaline Counterpart (Brazil)

In Portugal the **Marquis of Pombal** (chief minister, 1750–77) pursued parallel
ends: he expelled the **Jesuits from Brazil (1759)**, broke the equal partnership of
Church and state, created **monopoly trading companies** to develop the north,
reformed Coimbra's university, and centralized overseas administration under the
Ministry of the Navy and Overseas Territories.

## Interaction and Backlash

The reforms had a **contradictory legacy**. They sharply raised revenue (New Spain
remitted 90+ million pesos, 1761–1800) and revitalized the peripheries — but at a
price. The deliberate **discrimination against Creoles** (peninsulars favored for
audiencias, intendancies, bishoprics, and high army ranks) bred resentment among
the very elites whose loyalty the empire needed; native-born ambition redirected
itself to the **municipal councils** that would become the seedbeds of autonomy.
New taxes and the *reparto* provoked the great revolts of the 1780s — the
**Túpac Amaru II rebellion** in Peru (1780–81) and the **Comunero revolt** in New
Granada (1781). And the [[atlantic-slave-trade|expansion of slavery]] (Cuban sugar)
and Enlightenment economic societies and Freemasonry seeded both prosperity and
subversion.

## End Conditions

The reforms could not be completed: the system fell deeper into debt even as
revenue rose, and the **Napoleonic crisis of 1808** detonated the accumulated
tensions. The Bourbon project's recentralizing ambition and Creole alienation are
the proximate background to the
[[spanish-american-independence|wars of independence]].
