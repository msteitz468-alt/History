## Definition and Scope

The yerba mate forced labour system was the colonial mechanism through which indigenous Paraguayan communities were compelled to harvest and process wild *yerba mate* (*Ilex paraguariensis*) for export to Andean markets. It took two institutionalized forms: the *beneficio yerbatero* (a grant of forced labour for yerba harvest) and the *mandamiento* (a state labour decree assigning Indians to non-encomendero entrepreneurs). Together they made yerba mate "the most important mechanism for recruiting an indigenous labor force to produce the most important trade commodity connecting Paraguay and the Andean market."

## Causal Drivers

- **Andean market demand.** Yerba mate, drunk as an infusion, spread from ritual Guaraní use to widespread consumption across the Peruvian viceroyalty by the late sixteenth century. Demand from mining centres (especially Potosí) and urban markets created a large, monetized commodity trade.
- **Ecological constraints.** Wild yerba mate grew in specific forest zones (Mbaracayú, Guayrá, the Jejuy region) requiring seasonal harvest and processing. The labour-intensive nature of collection and porterage to river ports made coerced indigenous labour the only viable supply mechanism in a colony without a free labour market.
- **Encomienda personal service structure.** Paraguay's exclusively personal-service encomienda (labour rent, not goods tribute) was ideally suited to labour-intensive seasonal extraction but created structural pressure for extending service beyond legal limits. Yerba mate was explicitly exempted from the time limits Hernandarias (1603) placed on other mita service.

## Major Phases

**Emergence alongside encomienda (1555–1597).** As yerba mate export expanded in the late sixteenth century, encomenderos began directing mitayo labour toward harvest without specific legal authorization. Governor Hernandarias's 1603 ordinances acknowledged "a catastrophic situation in the villages" arising from excessive demands connected to yerba mate expansion, and newly shortened turn limits — but exempted yerba from those limits "because the productive demands of yerba mate made it exempt from these time limits."

**Alfaro's failed prohibition (1611–1618).** Francisco de Alfaro's inspection tour of 1611 proposed to "prohibit forced labor absolutely in the gathering of yerba mate," influenced by Jesuit lobbying. But the reforms were largely stripped away when confirmed in 1618: "neither was personal service eliminated, nor was length of service limited." The Mbaracayú yerba zone was specifically identified as one where forced labour remained legal after all other reforms.

**Cespedes Xería ordinance (1629).** A 1629 ordinance governing the Mbaracayú *beneficio yerbatero* — "one of the oldest testimonies we have about the early period of the beneficios yerbateros" — specified that an Indian's term of service lasted 6 months: 4 months working in the yerba mate fields and 2 months hauling the product to river ports. Mitayos from encomiendas worked alongside *enganchados* — workers "hooked" by credit advances into a form of semi-voluntary peonage. The governor's lieutenant controlled the opening and closing of the *beneficio* season and the distribution of labour among encomendero and non-encomendero enterprises.

**Bandeirante disruption and aftermath (1632–1676).** Portuguese [[bandeirantes]] destroyed the main yerba-producing zones of Guayrá (fall of the Guayrá province, 1632) and Mbaracayú (fall of the Mbaracayú province, 1676), eliminating most of the northern forest zones and their Guaraní labour pools. The Jesuit reductions increasingly controlled access to remaining Guaraní communities, limiting encomendero labour supply. This created chronic tension between Jesuit autonomy and encomendero demands.

**Persistence through the Comuneros rebellion (1720–1730).** The *beneficios yerbateros* "structured more or less as we have described" survived until the Comuneros rebellion of 1720–1730, when the social contradictions of the system — including encomendero resentment of Jesuit control over indigenous labour and the yerba trade — contributed to colonial political crisis.

## Geographic Spread

The system was concentrated in three forest zones:

- **Mbaracayú** (northeast Paraguay, near the Jejuy River): the principal wild yerba zone, last major production area surviving bandeirante raids
- **Guayrá** (present-day Paraná state, Brazil): destroyed by bandeirantes by 1632
- **Tape** (between Uruguay River and Serra do Mar): partially affected

River porterage linked these zones to [[asuncion]] and downstream trading centres via the Paraná-Paraguay river system. The entire colonial transportation infrastructure — by land and river — depended on state *mandamientos* assigning indigenous labourers.

## End Conditions

The *beneficio yerbatero* system was not formally abolished during the period covered by this chapter. Its crisis came in the eighteenth century: bandeirante raids had destroyed the primary forest zones; Jesuit communities had insulated the remaining Guaraní population from encomendero demands; and the Comuneros rebellion (1720–1730) reflected the political explosion of accumulated tensions. The system's end, when it came, was as much ecological (destruction of wild groves) as political.
