# Indian Boarding Schools

U.S. and Canadian off-reservation industrial/boarding and residential school systems aimed at cultural assimilation. Principal CHNPA I.2 synthesis: Hoxie ch. 12 (system scale, compulsion, curriculum, Meriam food figures, Collier reforms, mission infrastructure). **Full fidelity to what Hoxie documents; no outside atrocity detail invented.**

## Definition and Scope

part_of: [[us-civilization-program]]; part_of: [[reservation-period-1880-1960]]

Boarding and industrial schools removed or concentrated Native children for uniform, government-mandated curricula intended to suppress traditional lifeways and instill private property, capitalism, and Victorian (and Christian) morality. They operated alongside day schools at agencies, reservation police/Courts of Indian Offenses enforcement of attendance, and mission establishments. Canada and the United States ran parallel systems with mutual observation (Canadian officials visited Carlisle-type industrial schools).

**Source-boundary note (Hoxie ch. 12):** The chapter documents system scale, compulsory enrollment (parents hid children), assimilation curriculum, Meriam Report food/starvation (11¢ per child per day), mandatory Christian services (abolished under Collier), mission-school infrastructure, and the shift from boarding to day/public schools. It is **not** a detailed physical/sexual-abuse exposé. Claims below stay within that evidence.

## Causal Drivers

- Common front of government and Christian churches in both countries (produced_by: [[us-civilization-program]]).
- Belief that “civilizing” children was cheaper and more permanent than military conquest once removal beyond the settlement line was impossible.
- Industrial-training model (Carlisle, Pennsylvania) as prestige export; Canadian appropriations from 1884.
- Compulsory school enrollment enforced by reservation policemen and related authorities.

## Major Phases

### Build-out, 1880–1900
- Between **1880 and 1895** the U.S. Office of Indian Affairs started **twenty off-reservation boarding schools** and opened or expanded day schools at every agency; annual Indian education appropriations rose from **$75,000 to over $2 million**.
- Canadian boarding-school appropriations entered the Indian Department budget in **1884**; by **1896** there were **twenty boarding schools** (largely missionary-operated) in Manitoba, British Columbia, and the Northwest Territories.
- By the turn of the century a **uniform, government-mandated curriculum** was imposed on **more than 30,000 Indian children** across the continent.

### Coercion and resistance
- Compulsory enrollment met resistance: **parents hid their children** from reservation policemen and others who came to enroll them; other noncooperation included removing allotment boundary markers ([[nez-perce]] under Alice Fletcher 1889) and boycotting missionary services.
- Control institutions concurrent with schooling: US Courts of Indian Offenses (1883) and reservation police (first authorized 1878) enforced work, school attendance, and abandonment of tradition; Canada used North-West Mounted Police and Indian Act (1930 statute even permitted arrest for “misspending or wasting his time” in a poolroom).

### Mission infrastructure into the 1920s–30s
- Large Catholic boarding establishments remained major: St. Labre (Northern Cheyenne); St. Francis (Rosebud).
- Protestant Bacone College (Muscogee, Oklahoma) and Cook Christian Training School (Phoenix) trained Indian preachers/church workers.
- 1921 interdenominational survey: nearly **one-half of 400,000** Native people in North America were nominal Christians—often at odds with elders over tradition, peyote, and schooling.

### Meriam Report exposure (1928)
- *The Problem of Indian Administration* documented that the government’s highly publicized boarding schools spent **eleven cents per child per day for food**; nearly half of all Indians had annual income under $200; amid the “Roaring Twenties,” thousands of Native Americans knew hunger as a daily reality.
- Broader Meriam findings (infant mortality >190 per 1,000 births vs Whites 70.8 / Blacks 114.1; TB, pneumonia, measles, trachoma; ~50¢ per person government health spending) framed education deprivation within allotment-era impoverishment (contributed_to: Meriam Report reform climate → [[indian-reorganization-act-1934]]).

### Collier and New Deal education reform
- Collier’s first year: religious freedom at agencies; **abolished mandatory Christian services at boarding schools**—reversing church–state assimilation practice in federal schools (contrasts_with earlier civilization program).
- W. Carson Ryan then Willard W. Beatty: day schools rose from **132 to 226**; boarding enrollment dropped from **22,000 to less than 15,000**.
- Johnson-O’Malley Act authorized contracts with public school districts; by 1938 in California, Washington, Minnesota, and Arizona.

### Termination-era public-school shift
- 1950s: attendance at federally funded schools stayed steady while **public-school enrollment of Indians rose more than 60%**—administrative assimilation parallel to [[termination-policy]] (related displacement of federal school monopoly).
- Washburn notes last federal residential school in Canada closed **1988** (forward pointer beyond Hoxie’s main education narrative).

## Geographic Spread

US off-reservation industrial schools nationwide; agency day schools at every reservation; Canadian boarding concentration in Manitoba, British Columbia, and Northwest Territories (1890s); Catholic mission schools on Northern Cheyenne and Rosebud; Protestant training schools in Oklahoma and Arizona; later public-school contracts in CA, WA, MN, AZ.

## Interaction

- Instrument of [[us-civilization-program]] alongside [[allotment-policy]] and Courts of Indian Offenses.
- Unintentionally enabled: English-literate cadres for [[pan-indian-movement]] (Society of American Indians; later NCAI).
- Meriam food/health findings contributed_to New Deal reform climate for [[indian-reorganization-act-1934]].
- Termination-era public-school push part_of [[termination-policy]] institutional dismantling.
- Rosebud IRA vote pitted boarding-school “progressives” against Treaty Council claims advocates (local politics absorbing federal education products).

## End Conditions

Boarding as **dominant** model was deliberately reduced under New Deal day-school policy; federal monopoly further eroded by 1950s public-school enrollment growth and Canadian residential-system wind-down (last federal school 1988 per Washburn). Cultural and intergenerational effects, day schools, remaining boarding/mission institutions, and public-school Indian education continued—process transforms rather than cleanly ends. Ongoing status reflects residual institutions and historical trauma beyond Hoxie’s 1960 close.
