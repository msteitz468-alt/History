## Definition and Scope

The formative period of Islamic law is the process by which a full-fledged legal system — the [[sharia|Sharīʿa]] — emerged from the social and religious environment of the early Muslim community between the Prophet's death (11/632) and approximately the middle of the fourth/tenth century (c. 960 CE). Hallaq identifies four concurrent developments that together produced the Sharīʿa's mature form: (1) the judiciary and the office of the qāḍī; (2) the legal schools ([[madhhab]]); (3) substantive law ([[fiqh]]); and (4) legal theory ([[usul-al-fiqh]]). Chapters 1–2 of Hallaq's *Sharīʿa: Theory, Practice, Transformations* (2009) address the first two and the fourth of these.

## Major Phases

### Phase 1: Near Eastern Background and Quranic Legality (pre-632 to c. 660)

The emergence of Islamic law did not occur in a cultural vacuum. The Arabian Peninsula was thoroughly connected to the legal and commercial cultures of the Fertile Crescent, Yemen, and the Sasanid and Byzantine empires. Meccan traders, including the Prophet, were familiar with Nabatean commercial practices, Mesopotamian contract law, and Near Eastern customary norms: "the Peninsular Arabs maintained extensive relations with their neighbors to the south and north, with whom they shared ethnic, linguistic and cultural grounds" (Hallaq, ch. 1, §1).

The Prophet's mission in Mecca was primarily ethical and religious. Upon migrating to Medina, the new community (Umma) required its own law, distinct from Jewish and Christian legal systems. Quranic revelation in Medina introduced substantive legislation — inheritance, marriage, divorce, commercial contracts, criminal penalties — though not as a comprehensive code. "Although these rules surely did not constitute a system, their fairly wide coverage and the rapidity with which they appeared suggest a conscious effort toward building a new legal system" (Hallaq, ch. 1, §2). Pre-Islamic Arabian customary law was largely retained, with modification, wherever the Prophet did not explicitly abrogate it.

### Phase 2: Conquest, Garrison Towns, and the Proto-Qāḍī (c. 632–c. 700)

The rapid Arab conquests produced garrison towns (Kūfa, Baṣra, Fusṭāṭ, Damascus) where Muslim communities required legal regulation. Early judges (*proto-qāḍīs*) were appointed from the ranks of pre-Islamic arbitrators (*ḥakams*). Their jurisdiction was initially limited to the Muslim garrison population; conquered communities retained their own laws.

The early legal order rested on three sources: the Qurʾān, a plurality of *sunan* (exemplary practices — Prophetic, caliphal, and Companion), and *raʾy* (considered opinion). The Prophet's Sunna in this period was not yet distinguished from other authoritative sunan: "throughout most of the first century, the scheme of authoritative sources was: the Qurʾān, the sunan (including a thin layer of caliphal law) and considered opinion (raʾy)" (Hallaq, ch. 1, §4).

Caliphal authority was conceived as parallel to, not derivative of, Prophetic authority: caliphs saw themselves as direct agents of God's law, not merely deputies of the Prophet.

### Phase 3: Emergence of Legal Specialists and Prophetic Authority (c. 680–c. 780)

The most transformative development of the late first and second Islamic centuries was the emergence of two interrelated phenomena: (1) a class of *legal specialists* (fuqahāʾ) whose authority rested entirely on epistemic competence, not political office; and (2) the progressive elevation of Prophetic Sunna as the authoritative sunna *par excellence*, distinct from all others.

Legal study circles (*ḥalaqas*) formed in the major centers — Medina, Mecca, Kūfa, Baṣra, Damascus, Fusṭāṭ — where piety and juristic knowledge were cultivated together. The scholars who led these circles acquired "epistemic and moral authority" as the decisive qualification for legal influence: "those men in possession of a greater store of knowledge grew more influential than others less learned, gaining in the process — by the sheer virtuousness of their knowledge — exclusive authority as legists" (Hallaq, ch. 1, §4). This made Islamic legal authority fundamentally *private* and *personal*, residing in individual scholars rather than political institutions — a feature that would shape the entire subsequent history of the Sharīʿa.

Simultaneously, the traditionist movement began the systematic collection and transmission of formal Prophetic hadith, progressively displacing the practice-based *sunnaic practice* of the regional schools. By the end of the second/eighth century, hadith had positioned itself as the decisive expression of Prophetic Sunna.

### Phase 4: Competition Between Sunnaic Practice and Hadith (c. 750–c. 850)

The core tension of the second/eighth century was between *sunnaic practice* (the living legal traditions of Medina, Kūfa, and other centers, understood as continuous transmission of the Prophetic past) and the *formal hadith* tradition (literary reports equipped with isnād chains, transmitted by a mobile class of traditionists):

- **Medinese position** ([[malik-ibn-anas|Mālik b. Anas]]): The community's continuous practice is itself proof of Prophetic Sunna; formal hadith that contradicts this practice is suspect.
- **Iraqian position** ([[abu-hanifa|Abū Ḥanīfa]] and successors): Sunnaic practice rooted in Companion precedent, supplemented by *raʾy* and istiḥsān.
- **Traditionist position** ([[shafii-muhammad-ibn-idris|al-Shāfiʿī]], d. 820; then the hadith movement): Only formal Prophetic hadith with verified isnād chains can constitute authoritative Sunna; regional sunnaic practice is mere custom.

Al-Shāfiʿī's attack on both raʾy and sunnaic practice was decisive in the long run, though it "did not gain immediate acceptance" in his lifetime. The victory of formal hadith over sunnaic practice was complete approximately half a century after his death, when hadith became (with the Qurʾān) "the exclusive material source of the law" (Hallaq, ch. 1, §4).

### Phase 5: The Great Synthesis (c. 820–c. 960)

The intellectual and legal history between 150–350 H (c. 770–960 CE) is dominated by the competition between *rationalism* (ahl al-raʾy, including many Ḥanafites) and *traditionalism* (ahl al-ḥadīth, including [[ibn-hanbal-ahmad|Ibn Ḥanbal]]).

The Inquisition (*Miḥna*, 218–234/833–848), initiated by the caliphs and rationalist Muʿtazilite scholars, was about the createdness of the Qurʾān but more fundamentally about the role of human reason in interpreting divine texts. The traditionalists won, but their victory was relative: "the limited success of the traditionalists was largely a function of the weakness of the rationalists" (Hallaq, ch. 1, §6). The Miḥna produced not traditionalist dominance but the recognition that neither extreme — pure rationalism nor austere traditionalism — had majority support.

The Great Synthesis emerged from this: reason was disciplined and tethered to revelation; hadith was accepted as the dominant material source; rationalist methods (including [[qiyas-analogy|qiyās]]) were retained but subordinated to textual authority. The first comprehensive articulation of this synthesis as [[usul-al-fiqh|legal theory]] was the work of the Baghdadian Shāfiʿite circle of Ibn Surayj (d. 306/918) and his students. "By the middle of the fourth/tenth century, the Synthesis was fully in place" (Hallaq, ch. 1, §6).

### Phase 6: Formation of the Doctrinal Legal Schools (c. 850–c. 960)

Concurrent with the Synthesis, the four surviving *doctrinal madhhabs* took final shape (Ḥanafite, Mālikite, Shāfiʿite, Ḥanbalite). The process moved from *study circles* → *personal schools* → *doctrinal schools*. The defining features of the doctrinal school were: cumulative doctrine (not just the founder's opinions), methodological identity, bounded substantive doctrine, doctrinal loyalty, and an *axis of authority* constructed around a founding imam. See [[madhhab]] for the full account.

## Geographic Scope

The process was concentrated in the cities of the Islamic heartland — Medina, Mecca, Kūfa, Baṣra, Damascus, Fusṭāṭ — during phases 1–3, generating approximately 70% of early legal scholarship. By phases 4–6, the geographic spread of Islamic scholarship to Khurāsān, Transoxiana, Egypt, and North Africa both diversified and universalized legal culture, undermining the authority of regional sunnaic practice in favor of formally transmitted Prophetic hadith: "Prophetic hadith was free of these peculiarities, and was, as a textual entity, more amenable to use in new environments" (Hallaq, ch. 1, §6).

## End Conditions

The formative period closed approximately in the mid-fourth/tenth century when: (1) uṣūl al-fiqh achieved its first complete systematic articulation; (2) the doctrinal madhhabs achieved full institutional form; (3) the Great Synthesis established the normative Sunnite position on the relationship between reason and revelation. What followed was not stagnation but the post-formative period of juristic elaboration within the schools — a highly productive period of accumulated doctrine that Hallaq's later chapters address.

## Historiography

**The Schacht thesis**: Joseph Schacht argued that Islamic law originated in secular "administrative" and "popular" practices of the Umayyad period, becoming "Islamicized" only through the later fabrication of Prophetic hadith. Hallaq directly refutes this: the *sunan* that preceded formal hadith were already religious in nature, inspired by the early Muslims' interpretation of Islam; the process of subsuming them under Prophetic authority attests to their Islamic content, not to a transition from secular to religious law (Hallaq, ch. 1, §4).

**The "closure of the gate of ijtihād"**: Hallaq's broader work has challenged the notion that the formative period ended with a definitive "closure" of independent legal reasoning. The schools continued to exercise qualified forms of ijtihād through the post-formative period. See [[taqlid-and-ijtihad]].

**Authority-construction**: The attribution of systematic legal methodology to the four founding imams is largely a retrospective process, as Hallaq demonstrates through the case of al-Shāfiʿī and, more dramatically, Ibn Ḥanbal: "Shāfiʿī had little to do with the elaboration of uṣūl al-fiqh, since he advocated the Synthesis in a rudimentary and incomplete form" (Hallaq, ch. 1, §6). The "founders" did not found; they provided the axes of authority around which later jurists organized collective doctrinal projects.
