## Definition and Scope

This process covers the sequential introduction and entanglement of three industrial technologies — steamships, undersea telegraph cables, and wireless telegraphy — that networked the [[pacific-ocean]] between roughly 1820 and 1930. As Frances Steel argues in [[hattori-samson-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v2-2023]], these were "interdependent and entangled" systems celebrated in their own time as "annihilating time and space, accelerating exchange, and binding distant peoples and places more tightly together." Yet their impact was deeply uneven: strategically critical islands rose to new prominence while many communities remained poorly connected. The process did not simply globalize the Pacific; it reconfigured space, "regulating, directing, and selectively intensifying activity within and across the Pacific."

## Causal Drivers

driven_by: [[transpacific-economy]] — demand for faster, more reliable connections between Atlantic commercial centres and Pacific colonies and markets

The Pacific's reputation as a "slow" or belated ocean — "the Atlantic is crossed daily by steamers, the Pacific not once a year" (M.F. Maury, 1861, cited in Steel) — drove sustained investment to overcome its vast distances. Key drivers included:

- **Imperial extension**: Steam promised to "enhance the stability, speed, and regularity of sea transport" and gave colonial administrators reach over distant territories. The British Naval surveyor in New Zealand equated one steamship with one regiment of troops.
- **Railroad-shipping symbiosis**: "In the Pacific, unlike other oceans, the construction of railroads repeatedly provided impetus for the introduction of steamship routes." The completion of the transcontinental railroad to San Francisco (1869) shifted trans-Pacific exchange north from Panama; Canadian Pacific Railroad to Vancouver (1885) spawned the Empress fleet to Asia; Great Northern Railway's 1896 service from Seattle drove Japanese entry.
- **Resource extraction**: Burns Philp (est. 1883) and the Matson Navigation Company (est. 1882) both invested in plantations to generate cargo for their vessels. Matson was described as "a seafarer that has dug itself in on the land, intermingling with the sources of the commodities that keep its ships breathing."
- **Strategic communications rivalry**: Britain's fear of being displaced as global communications hub, American ambitions after seizing Guam and the Philippines in 1898, and Japanese state subsidies for shipping all drove investment in both shipping and cables.

## Major Phases

### Phase 1 — Coastal Steam, 1820s–1860s

Steam was first trialled in the Pacific from the 1820s along the South American coastline. William Wheelwright, an American merchant-mariner and consul at Guayaquil, proposed regular connections from Peru to Chile and Panama, eventually securing London backers to establish the **Pacific Steam Navigation Company in 1838**. Its first vessel's launch was "rhetorically tied to the restoration of good government and the 'civilizing' of native peoples" — an ideological conflation of steam with Western progress that "would reverberate down the decades."

The **Pacific Mail Steamship Company** was established in 1848 in New York, securing a Congressional subsidy for a mail route to Oregon, opening coastal service between Panama and Astoria via San Francisco. Steam entered deep-ocean crossings more slowly, hampered by the expense of coaling and the inefficiency of early engines.

### Phase 2 — Trans-Pacific Integration, 1867–1900

Pacific Mail opened services to **Yokohama and Hong Kong in 1867**, the first regular trans-Pacific steamship routes. The principal interchange shifted north from Panama to San Francisco after the railroad's completion in 1869. The Occidental and Oriental Steamship Company (O&O) was established in 1874 by the Central Pacific and Union Pacific railroads to compete with Pacific Mail on the Asia run.

Pacific Mail's attempt in 1870 to build a coaling station at Midway Island (claimed by the USA in 1867) had to be abandoned as "too costly and difficult." A ten-year Sydney–San Francisco contract was signed with Pacific Mail in 1875, with the route advertised as the fastest way home to Britain for Australian settlers — crossing the tropics "almost at right angles."

The **Oceanic Steamship Company of San Francisco**, originating in Claus Spreckels' Hawaiian sugar empire and partnering with the Union Steam Ship Company of New Zealand (USSCo.), took over trans-Pacific Australia/New Zealand connections from 1885. American annexation of Hawaiʻi (1898) barred foreign shipping from the profitable Honolulu–mainland leg; USSCo. rerouted via Rarotonga and Tahiti.

The **"All Red Route"** from 1893 linked Sydney, Brisbane, Suva, Honolulu, Victoria, and Vancouver via subsidized British and Canadian steamships, restoring Fiji's position as a South Pacific crossroads. The Canadian Pacific Railroad's fleet of "Empress" ships served Japan, Shanghai, Hong Kong, and Manila. Japan's **Nippon Yusen Kaisha (NYK)** entered Seattle–Asia service in 1896 in consort with Great Northern Railway; **Toyo Kisen Kaisha (TKK)** launched Japan–San Francisco service in 1899. Mexico and Asian lines eventually broke San Francisco's intermediary monopoly, with TKK opening direct Mexico–Asia services by 1910.

### Phase 3 — Undersea Telegraph Cables, 1893–1910

Submarine cables used gutta percha insulation and copper core wire (~2.5 cm diameter). Imagined "insuperable physical difficulties" delayed Pacific cable-laying until surveys in the mid-1880s demonstrated feasibility; nearly 600 seabed samples were obtained during the survey for the British "all red" cable.

Two trans-Pacific cables opened in quick succession:

**British "all red" cable (1902)**: Vancouver (Bamfield) — Fanning Island — Suva — Norfolk Island, bifurcating to Auckland (Doubtless Bay) and Queensland (Southport). The Vancouver–Fanning leg was the longest cable section yet laid. The Pacific Cable Board was formed in 1901 with members from Britain, Canada, Australia, and New Zealand — the first state-run cable — with Sir Sandford Fleming, Scottish inventor and railroad pioneer, as a key promoter. Its political aim was to reduce dependence on the Eastern Extension Telegraph Company, whose cables to Australia ran through Darwin–Java–Singapore–London.

**American cable (1904)**: San Francisco — Honolulu — Midway — Guam, with branches to Manila, Shanghai, and Yokohama/Bonin Islands. Managed by the Commercial Pacific Cable Company, presumed American but with Eastern (British) and Northern (Danish) interests holding 75 per cent of its stock, "not made public until 1921." Guam, the cable's vice-president asserted, was a "valueless" atoll now elevated to "one of the most important islands in the Pacific Ocean."

French interests (Société Française des Télégraphes Sous-Marins) had opened a Queensland–New Caledonia cable in 1893. Dutch-German cables from Yap to Menado, Shanghai, and Guam were laid in 1905; Japan's WWI seizure of Yap raised American fears about interference with Philippines communications, resolved in 1920–1 with Yap–Guam going to the USA, Yap–Manado to the Dutch, and Yap–Shanghai to Japan.

Cable stations depended on racially stratified labour. Fanning Island (Tabuaeran) hosted ~20 European cable board operators, Indian men from Suva recruited as domestic servants, and ~30 Gilbertese labourers as carpenters and general workers, competing with up to 150 indentured men on the island's copra plantation.

**Impact and limits**: High tariff rates limited the cable's utility. "Neither trans-Pacific cable became a means of mass communication." North Pacific rates were so high that China–USA press messages were sent via London. Introduction of cheaper "social rates" in 1913 expanded use; Indian settlers in Fiji used the Pacific Cable to communicate with India. Eight cable breaks near Guam occurred in 1918 alone, caused by submarine volcanic activity. The "all red" cable accumulated surplus; the American cable consistently underperformed.

### Phase 4 — Wireless Telegraphy and the End of "Pacific Silence," 1900–1930

The **Marconi Wireless Telegraph Company** (est. 1900) dominated early radiotelegraphy. Shipping companies were "compelled by law to install wireless sets, rented from Marconi and operated by its own personnel." Wireless offered connections to islands off the main shipping routes or excluded from cable networks, appealing particularly to missionaries, traders, and plantation operators.

A **Pacific Radio Telegraph Company** formed in London in 1909 proposed high-powered stations at Suva and Ocean Island (Banaba), creating "overlapping circles" across Papua, New Hebrides, Solomons, Tonga, Rarotonga, Tahiti, Nauru, and Sāmoa. It pitched wireless as solving the "Pacific silence": the trader "who roams further afield, vanishes from ken altogether for weeks or months at a time. Rapid wireless communication was essential for trade." The 1909 Radio Telegraphic Conference in Melbourne endorsed the concept but insisted on state rather than private control, effectively defeating the company.

**Amalgamated Wireless (Australasia) Limited (AWA)**, formed in Sydney in 1913 from the merger of Marconi and Telefunken Australian interests, assumed control of all wireless stations in the British Pacific from 1922, taking New Guinea/Papua in 1922 and Fiji in 1928. Fiji became the "'nerve-centre of the South Seas'" — in touch with every ship in the southern Pacific and interconnecting subsidiary island stations with the trans-Pacific cable. By 1930 a resident of almost any island "could send a message to Europe, or America or Australia, and receive a reply within twelve hours."

The **US Navy** opened an effective trans-Pacific wireless system from 1913: stations at Pearl Harbor, American Sāmoa, Guam, Philippines, and Beijing. The **Radio Corporation of America (RCA)**, established 1919, handled 50 per cent of international Pacific traffic by 1922. America's swift embrace of wireless was celebrated as achieving in six years what the British cable system took "a half century or more of constant effort."

The beam (shortwave) wireless of the mid-1920s — five times faster than longwave, bouncing off the atmosphere — threatened cable's commercial viability. Canada resisted expensive cable duplication; the duplicate northern section (Fanning–Bamfield) opened 1926, but cheaper beam rates undercut it. In 1929, British imperial wireless and cable communications were amalgamated into **Cable and Wireless, Ltd.**, subsidizing cable from wireless profits to shore up imperial security. For Canada, the merger ended an integrated imperial service.

## Geographic Spread

The process originated on the South American Pacific coast, transferred north to Panama and San Francisco, and expanded across the North Pacific to Japan and China, while a separate axis ran south from San Francisco and Sydney through Fiji and Polynesia. Cable geography created new hubs: Fanning Island, Midway, Guam, and Yap became strategically vital despite carrying little previous shipping traffic. AWA's Fiji hub anchored the SW Pacific wireless network; US naval stations at Pearl Harbor and Guam anchored the northern arc.

Island communities experienced this geography paradoxically. Trans-Pacific steamship routes "bypassed most islands," spending "only a handful of hours in the small number of ports en route." Many island groups — Marquesas, Cook, Society, Gilbert, Ellice — had "little or nothing to do with steam-age maritime industry." Colonial governments concentrated foreign ship calls at designated ports, "further diminishing visits to outlying islands."

The process intersected with racial exclusion regimes: "Britain's settler colonies waged battles from the 1870s to uphold white labour supremacy in the mercantile marine," ousting Chinese and Pacific Islander crews. The USSCo. by 1885 employed only British and white colonial crews. US legislation in 1915 attempted to reduce Asian crews through English-language requirements; the "Cantonese Pacific" nevertheless maintained two-way flows of letters, money, goods, and the bones of dead emigrants.

Non-white labor was indispensable at lower tiers: coaling, cargo handling, lightering, pilotage, and laundry work in ports of call. Hawaiian maritime livelihoods were disrupted by US regulations after 1901 requiring licensed officers, which mainlanders with no local knowledge filled, causing accidents and raising costs.

Steam inaugurated mass tourism and new modes of representing the Pacific; shipping companies became "publicists and tour operators" and hotel proprietors in Suva and Honolulu. Route networks also allowed Indigenous Polynesians to reassert deep genealogical ties — Māori in Auckland anticipated Hawaiian visitors, "dramatically different ocean-spanning affinities than those recently set in motion by Anglo imperial expansion." Fijian, Tongan, and Sāmoan communities maintained the encompassing "native sea" of Vasa Loloa through inter-island steamer travel.

## End Conditions

The period 1860–1930 saw the Pacific transformed from the "last" ocean criss-crossed by steamships into a networked space. Yet the process did not reach a clear terminus; it transitioned into aviation (anticipated in the 1920s discourse on wireless as the precursor to "America's probable future leadership in aviation") and eventually satellite communications. The 1929 Cable and Wireless merger consolidated British imperial control while acknowledging wireless primacy for commercial mass communications. The [[world-wars-era]] accelerated wireless infrastructure across many island territories previously marginal to the cable network.

## Historiography

Steel (in [[hattori-samson-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v2-2023]]) synthesizes a cross-disciplinary literature spanning transportation history, imperial history, Pacific studies, and communications history. She argues against two tendencies: studying shipping and telegraphy separately (as if one displaced the other), and treating infrastructure as mere background. Her key interpretive claim is that the technologies fashioned "a Pacific networked at many scales and to different speeds and rhythms" — not a uniformly shrunken ocean, but one whose space was "reconfigured, regulating, directing, and selectively intensifying activity."

The chapter draws on: archival sources in Fiji (NAF), New Zealand, and Australia; period newspapers and company prospectuses; and a substantial secondary literature including Starosielski's *The Undersea Network* (2015), Winseck and Pike's *Communication and Empire* (2007), Tribolet's *International Aspects of Electrical Communications in the Pacific Area* (1929), and Steel's own *Oceania under Steam* (2011).

The chapter's limitation is its relative thinness on Asian agency (Japan's NYK and the China Commercial Steamship Company appear but receive minimal analytical depth) and on Indigenous Pacific responses beyond brief examples. The "white labor" sections are the strongest treatment of labor politics; cable finance receives thorough treatment.
