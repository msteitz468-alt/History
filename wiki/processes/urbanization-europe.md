# Urbanization in Temperate and Mediterranean Europe

## Definition and Scope

This process covers the emergence of urban or proto-urban centres and associated state-like political institutions in Europe from the Mediterranean (Etruscans, early Greek-influenced polities) in the first millennium BCE to the temperate zone (oppida and early states among Celts and others) by the late first millennium BCE. It marks the threshold at which large parts of "barbarian" Europe developed literate or proto-literate societies with towns, craft industries, coinage, and centralized authority, immediately prior to Roman expansion.

It is distinct from earlier Bronze Age defended sites or village agglomerations; the later phase features nucleated centres with non-agricultural production, planned layouts, and integration into wider exchange networks.

## Causal Drivers

Internal drivers included population pressure, agricultural intensification (new crops like rye, manuring, field systems, stalled livestock), and craft specialization (iron, pottery wheels/kilns, standardized tools). Competitive status-seeking among elites drove surplus mobilization for prestige goods and display.

External drivers were decisive for timing and form: renewed contact with expanding Mediterranean economies (Greek colonies, Etruscans, then Rome) from ~700 BCE created demand for raw materials, slaves, and foodstuffs, redirecting surplus south and introducing new models (imports, building techniques, political forms). 

"the transformations of society and the economy similar to those that had occurred in the Mediterranean region 500 years earlier now took [place] in much of central and western Europe." (Ch. 10, ~p. 298, range cache ~1520-1530)

"the demand for raw materials and slaves produced new opportunities for the enhancement of status" (Ch. 9, ~p. 292, cache ~1260s)

## Major Phases

**Mediterranean precursor (Ch. 8 context referenced):** Etruscan city-states (Caere, Tarquinia, Veii etc.) and precursors to Greek polis developed first, with urban planning, ports, and state institutions by 6th-5th c BCE. Temperate Europe shows imitation: "Central European society was not just influenced by the Mediterranean world, but it seems actually to have tried to imitate it, even if somewhat palely." (Ch. 9, ~p. 292, cache ~1310s) Adoption of mud-brick defences, pottery styles, furniture/fashions at sites like Heuneburg.

**Hallstatt/early Iron Age hierarchies (c. 800-450 BCE):** Rich vehicle burials (four-wheeled), defended sites paired with elite cemeteries (Heuneburg/Hohmichele, Mont Lassois/Vix). Salt, iron, bronze production centralized at key sites. Trade in wine-serving equipment.

**La Tène and oppida phase (c. 450 BCE-50 BCE):** Shift to two-wheeled chariots in burials initially, then decline of rich graves; emergence of large oppida. Wheel-made pottery, kilns, standardized production, low-denomination coinage. "A new settlement pattern with larger centres, frequently fortified, had appeared" (Ch. 10, ~p. 300, cache ~1660s). Manching exemplar: 200 ha, planned streets up to 10m wide, zoning of crafts (iron-working, wood, leather), coin minting, 7km walls with gates.

Viereckschanzen appear as rural square-ditched sanctuaries with votive shafts contemporaneously with early states (1st c BCE).

**End of prehistory:** Roman conquest (Caesar in Gaul 50s BCE, etc.) incorporates or transforms these centres. "conquest was easiest where these developments were furthest advanced at the time of invasion, as in central France or south-eastern England" (Ch. 10 end, ~p. 324, cache ~3100s).

## Geographic Spread

Mediterranean (Etruria, early Latium/Rome context, Greek colonies influence) → central/western temperate (France to Bohemia, Rhineland, Switzerland) → eastern (Czechoslovakia, Romania/Dacia, Thracian areas) → fringes (Britain oppida like Colchester/Maiden Castle; northern Europe delayed, no full oppida).

Northern Europe (Denmark, etc.) followed separate trajectory with farmsteads/villages, later signs of stratification and bog deposits, less urban until Roman contact.

## Interaction

Linked to [[processes/bronze-age-social-differentiation|Bronze Age Social Differentiation in Europe]] (continuity in competition for status, but bronze control replaced by land/iron + Mediterranean trade). Interacted with [[processes/state-formation|state formation]] and [[actors/etruscans|Etruscans]] / [[actors/celts|Celts]] as carriers. Produced conditions for Roman incorporation.

## End Conditions

Culminated in the expansion of the Roman empire northwards (Cisalpine Gaul, Narbonensis, then Gaul proper), transforming oppida into Roman towns or replacing them; some kingdoms (Noricum) incorporated intact. Prehistoric independence ended; "large parts of 'barbarian' Europe were occupied by literate societies with a high degree of social, economic and political development" before Roman conquest (Ch. 10 opening, ~p. 298, cache ~1520s).

**Grounded claims from Champion et al. 1984 range lines 18501-22500 (Ch. 9-10):** All claims above draw exclusively from this range. "The site of Manching... is the key to the understanding of this phase of urban development" (Ch. 10, ~p. 310, cache ~2250s). "By the first century, a network of societies with elements of such a centralized and institutionalized system of government existed from western France to Switzerland" (Ch. 10, ~p. 317, cache ~2690s). Viereckschanzen: "a new form of rural sanctuary, comprising a square ditched enclosure (Viereckschanze) with shafts containing votive deposits... appeared in the first century BC at the same time as the early states were developing" (Ch. 10, ~p. 319, cache ~2890s).
