## Definition and Scope

The economies of the Roman provinces outside Italy and Egypt (Egypt is treated separately,
see [[roman-egyptian-economy]]), synthesizing three zones: the western provinces (Leveau),
the eastern Mediterranean (Alcock), and the frontier zones (Cherry) — chs. 24, 25, and 27 of
Scheidel, Morris & Saller (eds.), *The Cambridge Economic History of the Greco-Roman World*
(2007). `part_of: [[roman-imperial-trade]]`; `contrasts_with: [[roman-egyptian-economy]]`
(Egypt's distinct closed-currency, papyrus-documented case).

## The Western Provinces (Leveau, ch. 24)

The Libyan, Iberian, Celtic, and Germanic linguistic zones had no prior experience of state
organization before Rome — administrative practice there is "recent and Roman in origin,"
unlike the east, where "exploitation had started earlier." Combined population of north
Africa, Spain, Gaul, Germany, and Britain is estimated at ~25–30 million. Carthage was the
only western "megapolis" (60,000–300,000); regional capitals rarely exceeded 10,000–20,000,
and most Iberian cities held only 1,000–2,000 inhabitants.

**Villas and the Settefinestre model.** Villa counts, once put at ~1,000 for Gaul, the
Germanies, Spain, and Britain (Smith 1997), have been revised sharply upward by recent
surveys. The "Settefinestre model" (Carandini 1985) — a tripartite residential/production/
slave-quarter villa tied to slave-run market production — describes Italian/late-Republican
villas but is hard to observe in the western provinces, where the producer-villa form does not
appear before the mid-1st century CE. Debate persists over whether villa construction a
century after conquest reflects Roman-brought prosperity ("discontinuity") or investment of
pre-existing indigenous aristocratic wealth expressed architecturally ("continuity").

**Land and infrastructure.** Centuriation accompanied colony foundation from the end of the
civil wars; recent scholarship (since the 1990s) has scaled back the older view of
centuriation as uniform landscape "domestication," and many "pseudo-centuriations" in Gallia
Narbonensis have been debunked (Chouquer and Favory 2001). The *vicus* functioned as a
craft-production node alongside the villa (agriculture) and the city (political function);
Whittaker (1990) argues vici fostered a free "middle class" more open to innovation than
slave-worked Italy.

**Trade reversal.** Amphora studies show Gaul and Iberia shifting from importing Italian
(Dressel-type) wine to exporting provincial wine to Italy. Olive oil expansion (south Spain,
Tripolitania, Tunisia) was driven by army/elite demand (Mattingly 1988; Hitchner 1993), though
Ørsted (2000) and pollen evidence caution against reading it as monoculture. Terra sigillata
production migrated from Lyon's suburbium to Montans/La Graufesenque, then Lezoux, then the
Argonne/Rhine — its presence at Pompeii by 79 CE marks "the reversal of the relationship
between the Gauls and Italy." Mining is best documented in Spain (Carthago Nova, reportedly
40,000 workers per Polybius/Strabo), a rare case of durable regional development from mining
wealth.

**Regional-development typology.** Leveau applies Friedmann's (1973) fourfold typology to the
Roman west: only Carthage approaches a "core region"; the northwest provinces and Iberia are
"upward-transition regions"; most of Gaul passed through "resources frontier region" status;
parts of Gallia Narbonensis after Marcus Aurelius may be "downward-transition regions." His
conclusion frames but does not adjudicate two competing readings: an "evolutionist" view of
genuine regional development and demographic growth, versus a "dependency/center-periphery"
(Marxist) view of urbanization as a mechanism of wealth transfer to Rome, linked to the
[[consumer-city|consumer-city model]].

## The Eastern Mediterranean (Alcock, ch. 25)

Covers c. 200 BCE – 300 CE: Achaea, Macedonia, Asia, Bithynia-Pontus, Galatia, Cappadocia,
Lycia-Pamphylia, Cilicia, Syria, Judaea/Palaestina, and Arabia. Alcock explicitly challenges
the standard assumption (Garnsey and Saller 1987) that the Roman east was "less radically
transformed" than the west, arguing this partly reflects evidentiary bias (weaker
archaeological synthesis in the east) rather than economic reality.

**Demography and settlement.** Population density in the east exceeded the west under
Augustus; Frier's (2000) simulation projects western overtake by 164 CE, though he calls this
"very tentative" and Scheidel is more optimistic about eastern growth. The east was far more
urbanized than the west, with "super-cities" (Antioch, Pergamum, Ephesus) approaching 100,000,
though villages (*komai*/*katoikiai*) remained vital, especially in Anatolia and Syria. Field
survey shows widespread 1st–3rd century CE expansion into marginal land across the Aegean,
Asia Minor, and Syria (the "Dead Cities" of the northern limestone massif) — though Achaea
shows the opposite trend and Judaea/Palaestina's rural peak comes later, in the Byzantine
period.

**Land and labor.** Land ownership was highly fragmented among the emperor, the Roman state,
cities, *negotiatores*, private citizens, and temples; Herodes Atticus's dispersed holdings
across ≥3 provinces exemplify large-scale ownership. Servile agricultural labor was relatively
rare in Asia; tenancy and hired free labor dominated production.

**Craft and trade.** Non-agrarian production included fine ironwork (Cibyra), glass (Sidon),
and textiles (Cos silk, Tarsus linen, Palmyra's reworking of imported cashmere/cotton/silk).
Eastern Sigillata wares boomed under Augustus. Marble quarrying was a distinct eastern
"growth industry" — ~90% of marble at the Trajanic Portus yards came from the eastern
provinces, with Docimium (Phrygia) a key site. Rural working villas are notably rarer in the
east than the west, though this is partly an excavation-bias artifact; recent excavations
(Sagalassos, Corinth, Antioch) push back against a purely non-productive "consumer city"
reading of eastern urbanism. Long-distance luxury trade (spices, silk, cotton) moved via
Red Sea ports (Berenice) and overland via Petra and Palmyra — Rostovtzeff's "caravan city"
label for Palmyra has been "greatly deflated" but its mediatory role remains significant.

**Alcock's conclusion.** The east is best understood through the "plurality of the ancient
economy" — a "loosely articulated mélange of separate systems" (Davies 1998) rather than a
single "monocolore" framework. Aggregate growth (urban hierarchy, population, rural
settlement, consumption) is real for the early imperial east, with the 3rd century a
temporary "hiccup" followed by later recovery; whether the east grew "less" than the west is,
for Alcock, a question that has mainly served to justify the region's historiographical
neglect.

## The Frontier Zones (Cherry, ch. 27)

Cherry frames Roman frontiers not as defensive lines but, following Whittaker, as "zones or
borderlands" reflecting the empire's inability to find "the optimum balance between its range
of conquest... and the economy of its rule." Frontiers did not track ethnic/cultural
boundaries — the German frontier, for instance, cut across a zone of Celtic-Germanic cultural
homogeneity. The "least effort subsistence model" (Green 1979) predicts frontiers form at the
ecological transition from intensive agriculture to extensive pastoralism.

**Agricultural intensification** is attested in the Rhineland; Britain's case is more
ambiguous, with revised pollen dating attributing highland cultivation increases mainly to the
pre-Roman Iron Age and later Roman period rather than early occupation. In North Africa,
UNESCO's Libyan Valleys Survey documents dramatic olive-driven agricultural expansion,
especially in the 2nd century, with marketable surpluses reaching coastal cities and inland
military bases. Cherry argues against attributing this mainly to Roman technique — dry-farming
methods were largely indigenous (Hitchner) — concluding Rome's main contribution was opening
new markets, not superior technology; Duncan-Jones's data on elite wealth concentration
suggests growth widened inequality rather than raising general per capita wealth.

**Army demand as a growth driver.** Several scholars (Whittaker, MacMullen) argue army food
demand was "the fundamental factor" in frontier agricultural development; population-based
estimates (soldiers as % of provincial population: Britain 2–5%, Danube ~3%, north Africa
<0.5%) suggest a modest aggregate footprint, though Cherry cautions this likely understates
concentrated local impact near garrisons. The army generally paid cash for supplies rather
than relying on tax in kind before the 3rd century (per Vindolanda accounts); individual
soldier spending — an estimated ~150 sesterces/year discretionary income per soldier —
plausibly drove the rapid growth (and equally rapid collapse) of *canabae*/*vici* around
legionary bases.

**Monetization asymmetry.** Annual North African military pay (>10 million sesterces) likely
exceeded the entire pre-Roman regional money supply, diffusing a money economy there; Britain
shows the opposite pattern, with little Roman coin circulating outside major towns and forts
until the late 3rd century. Cross-frontier trade is attested (a Danube fort literally named
"Commercium"; Roman goods in Germany, some restricted by treaty — Tacitus records only the
Hermunduri admitted to trade); a "200km buffer zone" pattern shows simple goods near the
frontier and higher-quality goods further out, while some Gallo-Belgic pottery types
circulated within the frontier provinces but rarely beyond — the frontier sometimes acted as a
barrier, not just a conduit. Rome's east-frontier trade with India involved mostly luxury
goods; Roman coin finds in India are almost all pre-Tiberian, suggesting sharp decline in that
trade from the mid-1st century CE.

## Causal Drivers

Provincial integration into an empire-wide market (roads, army logistics, tax-driven
production incentives), regional agro-ecological specialization, and — at the frontiers —
concentrated military demand and soldier spending.

## From Mitchell, Later Roman Empire (2015)

### Asia Minor: civic transformation and southern coastal boom

- Secure eastern heartland (except Isauria) shows late-antique civic transformation via epigraphy and archaeology: no new temples after late third century; church conversion of city-center temples clustered in early **390s** and **440s** (Ephesus St Mary; Sagalassus temples; Aphrodisias temple of Aphrodite) — organized civic undertakings with systematic *spolia* reuse, not random pillaging. Benefaction shifted to imperial officials (governors); urban prosperity focused on provincial capitals (Ephesus, Sardis, Ancyra, Aphrodisias, Side). Secular amenity (baths, aqueducts, theaters, racing) continued under Christian monotheism. `part_of:` [[late-roman-cities]].
- Major regional economic shift toward the **south coast**: new stone-built harbor settlements on the Lycian shore (Kekova, Tristomon, Andriake) on shipping routes Alexandria/Syria/Cyprus–Aegean/Constantinople; Aperlae specialized in imperial-monopoly purple dye; Rugged Cilicia (especially Corycus) boomed fourth–sixth centuries as commercial entrepôt (~600 occupational funerary inscriptions: olive/wine merchants, ship owners/builders, guilds) exporting timber, wine, cheese, and especially olive oil into the Constantinople annona (Cilician shipowners named at Abydus). `part_of:` [[byzantine-economy]]; `contributed_to:` [[roman-imperial-trade]].

### Balkans/Danube: least “classical” zone

Sparse municipal aristocracy (≤69 municipalities/colonies across Rhine–Danube frontier provinces in 1st–3rd c.), few large estates, few senators, military officers dominating society. Fourth-century fort-like enclosures in Pannonia/Valeria reflect insecurity (refuges, interior line, or annona collection points). Civil wars along the Constantinople–Aquileia trunk road had larger regional impact than barbarian confrontations until later; *via Egnatia* rose in importance as central Balkans became insecure. Hunnic period devastated key cities (Priscus on deserted Naissus; fall of Sirmium, Viminacium, Ratiaria); St Severinus’ *Life* shows late-fifth Noricum held by isolated *limitanei*/*foederati* pockets until Odoacar’s **488** evacuation — “a haunting picture of Roman impotence and the utter collapse of the former frontier.” `concurrent_with:` [[post-roman-transformation-of-the-west]], [[hunnic-migrations]].
