# Central Mexico Indigenous Peoples since Independence

## Definition and Scope

Regional history of **native peoples of central Mexico** from Mexican political independence (1821) through the late twentieth century, as treated by Frans J. Schryer, “Native Peoples of Central Mexico since Independence,” *Cambridge History of the Native Peoples of the Americas* Vol. II Mesoamerica Part 2 (2000) ([[cambridge-history-native-peoples-americas-v2-2|CHNPA II.2]] Ch. 17). Continues and reworks the colonial corporate world analyzed in [[central-mexico-indigenous-colonial]] (Cline); part of the broader [[colonial-mesoamerica-native-peoples]] arc into the national period.

**Heterogeneity first.** Schryer opens by rejecting treatment of central Mexican native peoples as a homogeneous group: under a single national state they vary by class, social organization, discourse, local administration, economy, and relations with non-natives — even within the same language/identity group. Shared elements are **dispersion, survival, and renewal**, plus the analytical questions of why some communities retained distinct identity while others were absorbed, and how selective reinterpretation of dominant culture shaped the region (Schryer).

**Region (Carrasco / HMAI).** Central Mexico = southeastern Mesa Central plus eastern slopes with part of the coastal plain and parts of the Balsas Basin — roughly the colonial bishoprics of Mexico and Puebla. Once Aztec imperial core and hub of New Spain; natives still a sizable majority on the eve of independence. Today roughly 6.5 Mexican states; groups include Nahuatl, Nähñu/Otomí, Mazahua, Popoloca, Huastec enclaves, with Zapotec/Mixtec spillover from Oaxaca on the southern fringe and Huasteca merge on the north (Schryer). Socioeconomic unity: intermingling among native groups and common links to Mexico City and highland urban centers (Pachuca, Cuernavaca, Puebla, Toluca); pre-motorized interethnic trade (Otomí ambulant traders to Nahua Huasteca; Popoloca–Mixtec trade to Tehuacán; Spanish as weekly *tianguis* lingua franca in multi-language Guerrero localities) (Schryer).

## Causal Drivers

- **Independence as legal–cultural rupture.** 1821 marked the start of systematic **cultural genocide** policy and accelerating loss of native languages (including Nahuatl/*mexicano*, formerly a regional second lingua franca). Before independence many legal/historical documents were still written in Nahuatl and litigants could use native languages; independence gave an Amerindian word (*Meshico*) to the new country while rulers refused to recognize languages, social fabric, or cultural values of descendants of ancient Mexico (Schryer). Complements Cline’s observation of the abrupt end of Nahuatl court documentation.
- **Semantic shift of *indio*.** Non-Spanish-speaking natives continued as *naturales* (commoner connotation), separate from *gente de razón*. *Indio*, officially abolished, remained in daily discourse but changed meaning: once a recognized legal status with rights and obligations under the Crown, by end of nineteenth century upper-class Mexicans used it for all poor, illiterate country people — motivating many natives to shed the stigma (Schryer).
- **Municipios + dissolution of communal tenure.** Setting up municipios (usually larger than former native jurisdictions) combined with state policy dissolving communal land were major factors in loss of ethnic solidarity — yet many communities of peasants, merchants, and artisans continued former republic organization with reduced land (Schryer). Bourbon late-eighteenth-century reforms and Liberal governments had already altered especially religious institutions (bridge from colonial).
- **Ley Lerdo / desamortización (1856) and Porfirian full implementation.** Liberal legislation to abolish collectively owned rural property threatened native communities’ existence; full implementation delayed until after ~1880 (often 1890s) under stronger Porfirio Díaz state and capitalist expansion (Schryer).
- **Hacienda and ranchero economies.** Estate encroachment (especially Morelos/Puebla sugar lowlands and central-plateau grain valleys) and parallel montaña ranchero economies structured labor, isolation, and sometimes language retention (Schryer; institutional detail on [[hacienda]]).
- **Mexican Revolution and ejido.** New legal land tenure and official ideology from the 1920s; central state expansion and land reform challenged corporate villages and former hacienda settlements turned into *ejidos* (Schryer; native participation in the Mexican Revolution).
- **State [[indigenismo]] and schools.** Mestizo-controlled forced acculturation via bilingual teachers and community development, late to arrive in central Mexico; later native critique and pluriculturalism discourse (Schryer).
- **Native agency.** Communities joined broader political movements; military brokerage and regional nationalism; creative legal defense (*sociedades agrícolas* / *condueñazgo*); late-twentieth-century ethnic revival (Schryer).

## Major Phases

### Demographic and linguistic inversion (1821–mid-20th c.)

Schryer’s estimates (he flags scarcity of direct sources): in **1821** natives still probably **>70%** of central Mexico’s population; around the Revolution **<50%** if native-language knowledge is the criterion; by **1980 ~12%** *indígenas*. Unlike much of North America, Mexico had high genetic continuity, high miscegenation, and low foreign immigration — most inhabitants are mestizos (cultural more than phenotypic) rather than Euro-Americans; yet majority worldview/values are predominantly European (increasingly North American), with prejudicial/paternalistic attitudes toward those classed as Indians (*inditos*, *compadritos*, *compas*) (Schryer).

Around 1821 almost all of central Mexico had some native-language speakers. Rapid linguistic assimilation on the central plateau **1840–50**; by late nineteenth century large monolingual Spanish zones (N half of Federal District, S tip of Hidalgo; sections of Tlaxcala–Puebla valley; SW Mexico state; lower S Puebla). By mid-twentieth century the map **reversed**: native speakers were islands/clusters maintained by daily social intercourse in towns, villages, neighborhoods (Schryer).

**Critique of “regions of refuge.”** Anthropologists’ model (inaccessible places, subsistence + crafts vs mestizo commercial lowlands) is too simplistic; no clear correspondence between native proportion and terrain, climate, or urban proximity. Examples: Morelos Nahuatl in only some highland sections plus lowland Temixco/Cuautla; Nähñu peons on Temoayan hacienda lands preserved speech/identity as much as upland communal-land barrios; Mazahuas of Ixtlahuaca–Toluca meseta (former hacienda peons) retained language within commuting distance of Mexico City; NE Hidalgo lowland Nahuas retained language/culture while Sierra Alta de Hidalgo Nahuas largely lost both decades earlier (Schryer). **“Being ‘Indian’ is not the same thing as being poor”** — 1970 Morelos census and 1890–1910 southern Puebla literacy variation (e.g. Zoquititlán 83% Nahuatl / 3% literate vs Cohueacan 93% indigenous-language / 29% men and 15% women literate) (Schryer).

Multi-factor retention: past autonomy/land struggles, economic interaction with other natives, estate owners’ ability to isolate native workers, village-level class tensions. **Tlaxcala Malinche paradox:** rapid industrialization helps explain Nahuatl loss 1870–1920 between Tlaxcala and Puebla cities, but not survival on Malinche volcano slopes; northern greater loss of daily Nahuatl with higher Mexico City migrant proletarianization correlated with **greater** ethnic/indigenista solidarity; throughout Tlaxcala “Indianness” is expressed via agricultural work and conservative dress rather than Nahuatl (Schryer).

Ethnic boundaries are not reducible to language or “authentic” culture content: rural natives and non-natives share many traits (compadrazgo, folk customs); some scholars argue many such traits are medieval European rather than prehispanic, while material culture (foodways, agricultural technology) is authentically native even among rural mestizos. Boundaries depend on interpretation of real/imputed differences. Future survival depends on land/resource access, local/regional political autonomy, and control over formal education (Schryer).

### Continuity inside Mexico City: parcialidades

Andrés Lira documented strong community sense and de facto parallel native administration for many neighborhoods/villages inside today’s Federal District. Politics of land tenure and local government through the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries in two former native administrative units (**parcialidades** — San Juan Tenochtitlan and Santiago Tlatelolco) illustrate continuity of colonial institutions, separate ethnic identity, and struggle against non-native city government long after formal abolition of local native self-government and of the legal category *indio* (Schryer).

**Differential survival within San Juan Tenochtitlan:** humid southern section — market gardening on fertile [[chinampa-agriculture|chinampas]], fodder cutting/selling — kept language and customs well into the twentieth century via strict endogamy, outsider restriction, resource control, and an intellectual elite. Northern section along avenues to Villa de Guadalupe (marginal fishing, salt-making on desiccating lakes) — communal pastures of Santiago Tlatelolco rented to mestizo businessmen with rents appropriated by non-native leaders — first lost ethnic cohesion and separate culture (Schryer).

### Changing social structure: from Indian republics to municipios and haciendas

At independence many central/southern natives belonged to former “Indian republics”/*pueblos* with land bases and separate administration. New ayuntamientos usually fell to Spanish-speaking mestizos, but sometimes former native notables used town councils to assert authority over local non-Indians; high colonial self-regulation survived despite republican national government. Local autonomy depended on isolation and pre-Liberal economic prosperity (Schryer).

Loss of land and self-government did **not** always dissolve ethnic solidarity. Natives settled permanently inside hacienda boundaries did not invariably lose culture: Mazahua hacienda communities (Mexico state); 1900 Tehuacán district (Puebla) — 23 haciendas, almost completely native rural population; Hidalgo Huejutla district Nahua peon settlements (Chalahuiyapan, Huitzachahual, Ecuatitla). Landless natives were legally subordinate to non-native owners but could manipulate paternalistic symbols and forge their own social patterns (Schryer).

### Closed corporate community and civil-religious hierarchy

Mesoamerican native pueblos are classically depicted (Eric Wolf) as **closed corporate communities**: highly endogamous, restricting outsiders from residence/property. Key feature: civil-religious hierarchy (**cargo** / *mayordomía*) — adult men rotate alternating civil and religious posts of rising rank to elder/principal; households sponsor saint-feasts; women do most food prep/marketing and activate networks, with informal authority. Central Mexico rarely preserved as elaborate a system as elsewhere in Mesoamerica, but modified versions persisted in parts of Puebla, Tlaxcala, Hidalgo, Guerrero; one full hierarchy still reported in the 1970s for a Nahua village near Texcoco (Schryer).

**Nineteenth-century origins debate:** several scholars argue the hierarchy emerged in the nineteenth century after independence pressures on colonial *cofradías*. Colonial communal land/cattle had funded public feasts; competition over proceeds among officials, native elites, Church, and peasantry dissolved many cofradías; feast sponsorship shifted to individual households; religious posts intertwined with civil posts in a “ladder” less dependent on direct state/church control (Schryer).

**Function controversies:** (1) defensive strategy vs further exploitation of native peasants — evidence supports both; local/regional variation in redistribution. (2) whether it leveled internal wealth differences and prevented permanent classes — hierarchy probably leveled effectively until ~1870s in communities blocking outsiders; abundant evidence of re-emergence of internal class differences in last two nineteenth-century decades even where classic cargo form remained intact. After Independence War, noble/commoner status distinctions in larger former republics disappeared though *principales* retained influence (Schryer).

**Post-1920s truncation:** more efficient centralized bureaucracy and de facto one-party system brought local civil posts under government control (mestizo state representatives approved municipal elections; sometimes intervened in religious ceremonial system). Result: reduced local autonomy and **separation of civil from religious posts** — villagers left with predominantly religious hierarchy. Dissolution began earlier in central than southern Mexico; by 1950s truncated system in all but fringe regions. Surviving religious hierarchy still marks some village autonomy but is also found in mestizo communities that were once Indian republics (Schryer).

### Land struggle continuum: Independence War to Ley Lerdo to Porfiriato

Native land struggle long predated independence: court battles, ideological debates, armed rebellion when favorable. **Hacienda encroachment** intensified before Liberal reform, especially sugar lowlands of Morelos and Puebla and grain valleys of the central plateau (Mexico, Tlaxcala, Puebla). Methods: appropriating surplus communal land already rented for nominal fees in low-population periods; taking land used as collateral for cattle/financial “assistance” for ceremonies. Expansion slowed in first half of nineteenth century amid weak state and elite conflict. During the Independence War, native communities in the Valley of Mezquital and plains of Apan attacked estates after joining Hidalgo and Morelos insurrections — region of rising tension between communities and absentee pulque/livestock landowners. Per Tutino, grain-plateau natives did not revolt then because estate–village symbiosis compensated for declining autonomy (Schryer).

Post-independence equilibrium did not last: haciendas took over neighbor lands both in expanding Morelos sugar zones and on declining central-plateau estates; natives fought via courts and land invasions; in stable periods national government restored most land won by peasants to usurpers (Schryer).

**Ley Lerdo implementation stages:** first *denuncia* of rented sections (loss of ceremonial/school/hospital/charity rental income); then distribution of remaining commons to native occupants for high fees, who often sold to richer natives or outsiders. Partitioning first hit communities with good communications near towns; Mexico City parcialidades remained corporate exceptions (benefiting mestizo/native elites siphoning income — poor natives sometimes petitioned for distribution). Vehement pueblo opposition, measurement costs, and lip service by *jefes políticos* delayed implementation for decades; full implementation after 1880 under Díaz — then courts no longer viable and open rebellion too risky (Schryer).

**Porfirian modern hacienda boom:** commercial estates diversified into food processing, railroad finance, and mechanization, yet used labor-intensive techniques for cheap native labor and revived paternalistic discourse. Modern hacienda expanded into eastern Mexico state (Mazahua communities): lumber (railroad ties), grain, animals, and *zacatón* grass cultivation transformed a prehispanic native broom-fiber craft into semimechanized capitalist brush production for European/North American horse grooming. Unlike Yucatán henequen’s forced Mayan labor bordering on slavery, NW Mexico state hacendados used deceptive paternalism and patron–client bonds to secure Mazahua/Otomí labor (Schryer).

### Ranchero economy and *sociedades agrícolas*

Parallel to commercial haciendas on the plateau, mountainous foothills between plateau and Gulf/Pacific coasts developed mixed tenure: communal land, tiny private plots, and medium *ranchos*. Huasteca of Hidalgo and Sierra Norte de Puebla predominantly native with few small cattle haciendas, land still communal; influx of highland mestizos and some European immigrants. Haciendas in intermontane valleys generally did not encroach on ranchero-region villages; wealthy natives and mestizo rancheros both benefited from Liberal reforms; mestizos learned local languages and accommodated native institutions; outsiders became commercial middlemen in long-distance trade with reciprocal ties rather than destroying village economy (Schryer).

In Huejutla (NW Hidalgo) wealthier Nahuas became traders, moneylenders, and small commercial rancheros (“Indian rancheros”); communities kept much communal land via **sociedades agrícolas** — private co-ownership (**condueñazgo**) under Mexican law. Same option used elsewhere (Popoloca San Felipe sociedad from 1830s controlled most communal land into 1960s; Totonacos of Papantla, Veracruz). Sociedades dominated by village elites who set aside sections for the poor; sometimes individual titles held in trust by one representative. Mestizos sometimes joined sociedades with Nahua representatives (Schryer; Escobar Ohmstede and Schryer debunk the view that Liberal land law only usurped native land).

In Huejutla, mestizo politicians still recognized original Nahua pueblo communal boundaries even when portions became private or sold; communal boundaries concerned **judicial authority** as much as access rights. Nahua authorities continued *faena* (communal labor) for own benefit or mestizo counterparts — as colonial caciques/*principales* had. Mid-twentieth-century Cárdenas reform barely touched most montaña small properties (below expropriation limits); Huasteca of Hidalgo exception — Nahua and mestizo caciques created huge **fictitious ejidos** coinciding with corporate village boundaries while buying/selling continued and wealthy Nahua cattle ranchers monopolized pastures. Colonial-era tenure/admin structures survived because they served both wealthy Nahua and mestizo ranchero class interests; passive resistance of poor peasants also conserved subsistence access and legal bases for future claims (Schryer).

### Mexican Revolution, ejidos, and factionalism

Revisionists treat the Revolution as an interlude in industrial capitalist development and state expansion begun in the late nineteenth century, but it produced a new legal land-tenure system and official ideology. From the 1920s, stronger central state power and another land reform challenged corporate peasant villages and former hacienda settlements turned into **ejidos** (usufruct in perpetuity, cannot sell/rent — modeled on colonial communal forms). Contrasts between independent native pueblos and native hacienda villages diminished as both faced new mestizo politicians (Schryer).

Post-Revolution **ejido committees** (*comisariados*) gave former hacienda-subject natives first chance to manage own affairs (not designed only for natives). Rare exceptions aside, hacienda workers had been barred from organizing religious fiestas and lacked civil-religious hierarchy; *mayordomo* meant labor supervisor/intermediary, not feast sponsor. After expropriation, day laborers/sharecroppers gained land titles and free religious practice; for former Indian peons without independent village status, ejido status enabled new autonomy/cultural expression (e.g. Chalahuiyapan built chapel, joined Huejutla cathedral pilgrimage) (Schryer).

In stable times natives still needed external brokers; post-Revolution ejidos became prone to internal conflict, often created/exacerbated by non-natives; factionalism over ejido committees and peasant leagues produced native strongmen/**caciques** — ambivalent brokers between community and national state. Mazahua Valley of Toluca ejidos 1920s–30s superficially like mestizo ones — both used declining hacienda power for milpa and *zacatón* crafts — but when land shortages returned and *zacatón* demand fell, mestizos switched economic activities via language/political contacts; more Mazahuas remained poor, reinforcing “poor Indian” vs “progressive mestizo” stereotype (Schryer).

**1970s–80s Huejutla land struggle:** caused by breakdown of modus vivendi after lopsided development (cattle expansion, demographic growth, subsistence erosion, loss of outside part-time work). Wealthy Nahuas refused peasant movement; open class conflict sometimes within Nahua communities; poor mestizos even invaded against Nahua ranchers. Poor Nahuas unlike mestizos refused to divorce land rights from village residence eligibility — insisted non-supporters leave; where radicals won, communities approximated Wolf’s closed corporate community. Many Nahua ejidos set aside special “communal land” reserves for members without parcels (contrary to standard Land Reform procedure) (Schryer).

### Native presence in national politics and military service

In first two decades after independence, a small elite of educated natives in Mexico City protested abolition of native corporate rights/special institutions: fought to preserve parcialidad corporate landholding and the **Colegio de San Gregorio**. Native national politics was not always cultural preservation — sometimes individual upward mobility; politicians of native descent sometimes acted against their ethnic group’s interests (Schryer).

**Benito Juárez** — “full-blooded Zapotec” from Oaxaca, first liberal national president, leader against French invasion of 1861 — hero of official mestizo mythology according natives equal place in national history. Raised in a native community but highly acculturated and self-identified as Europeanized Mexican; his **desamortización** law set legal mechanisms that disintegrated/destroyed many native communities in central Mexico and elsewhere; writings showed disdain and paternalism toward “his own people” (Schryer).

Abolition of Indian republics left no official native representation; national politicians saw natives as “uncivilized” or “poor, exploited Indians,” explaining reported nineteenth-century peace-time disinterest — yet on issues that mattered, natives were as active as non-natives. Strong ethnic cohesion and autonomy struggles created need for **brokers**. Example: **Totonacos of Papantla** violent rebellion from **1836** owed as much to a Church edict prohibiting native religious celebrations (syncretic, distinct from Spanish-speaking practice) as to landowner encroachment and attempts to cut traditional “contraband” trade (Schryer).

Liberals and Conservatives learned forced conscription failed among native peasants; had to promise protection, tax reduction, or land defense — especially in montaña. In northern Puebla, Nahua military leader **Juan Francisco Lucas** (“patriarch of the Sierra”) built power via indigenous National Guard citizen-army of peasant volunteers; success partly because Liberal reforms had not adversely affected those communities. Variation within sierra: Totonacos in warmer N preferred supplies and military tax rather than full militarization like land-short Nahuas; armed Nahuas (*cuatecomacos*) under Lucas said to resemble Aztec warrior behavior (Guy Thompson) (Schryer).

Guerrero native communities gained tax reduction and local-government voice via 1840s rebellions and federalist alliance. **Juan Álvarez** as broker; native support was a principal reason he and other mestizo politicians won creation of the **state of Guerrero itself in 1849**. Liberal (briefly president 1856), Álvarez was ally of Juan de Dios Rodríguez Puebla, radical native Mexico City leader advocating preservation of capital’s native community lands and schools. French invaders and Maximilian also depended on native support in strategic areas and publicly proclaimed pro-native policy (Schryer).

Apparent “fickleness” of prominent native politicians reflects consistent commitment to a Mexico with a place for “Indians,” not lack of loyalty: **Juan Galicia Chimalpopoca** (administrator of formerly native Mexico City communal lands after Ayutla; under Maximilian president of Junta Protectora de Clases Menesterosas, 1863–67); similar pattern for José Calixto Vidal and Juan Rodríguez Acatlan; Juan Francisco Lucas maintained Sierra de Puebla control across regimes and transitioned from ally of Porfirio Díaz to supporter of Madero (1910) then Carranza representative (Schryer).

### Revolution: Zapatismo and non-Zapatista native leaders

Non-native leaders generally exploited native clients without furthering long-term aboriginal interests; Revolution (primarily mestizo factional war) similar. **Emiliano Zapata** (mestizo) most identified with “fighting for the Indians”; part of his army/officers likely from native communities (large Morelos Nahuatl-speaking population); gained more native adherents expanding into Puebla and Tlaxcala. Only late, when losing, did Zapatistas broaden respect for native culture: circulated handwritten **Nahuatl letters** to be read aloud to woo Cirilo Arenas’s Tlaxcala agrarians; only then deliberately translated national symbols into native categories — no other such official circular found. Ironically, native revolutionary leaders elsewhere often not Zapata/Villa radicals: in Huasteca of Hidalgo, Nahuatl-speaking **Nicolás Portes** (“El Indio Portes”) and Lara family of Yahualica affiliated with conservative Carranza; forays vs Villista rivals seen by Atlapexco mestizo rancheros as “Indian uprising” due to local enmity. With possible Zapata exception, revolutionary leaders rarely took account of natives’ broader long-term concerns and alternative worldviews (Schryer).

### State indigenismo and late-twentieth-century revival

Regional underdevelopment unrest motivated special native institutions drawing on Mexican anthropologists’ theories: **[[indigenismo]]** controlled by mestizo urban intellectuals. Paid lip-service to cultural difference while introducing forced acculturation to eliminate “exploitation and marginality”; bilingual teachers used as more efficient means to replace native languages with Spanish; education and community development created new native brokers. Impact **late in central Mexico**: conceived in Cárdenas era; large coordinating centers early 1950s; first central Mexico center only **1963** in remote Guerrero; Mazahuas (Mexico state) and Nähñus/Nahuas (Hidalgo) not exposed until **1970s**. 1970 Echeverría National Councils of Indian Nations (*Consejos Supremos*) gave formal national representation but PRI carefully selected representatives — Huejutla initially included radical Nahua agrarians (some later assassinated); after 1980 more conservative professionals and small businessmen appointed (Schryer).

After 1970s Huejutla Nahua revolt (land wins ongoing less dramatically), issues shifted toward middleman exploitation and state credit/employment failure, plus educational struggle by radical bilingual teachers against mestizo ideological hegemony for self-worth and ethnic pride. Until ~1970s educated natives left for capitals and lost language/identity; from early 1980s professionals returned to home-region towns, joining large bilingual-teacher stratum; use native languages and patron–client ties while living in Spanish mestizo world — new educated elite increasingly self-identify as *indígenas*. Independent native intellectuals criticize official indigenista institutions; pressure forced López Portillo **pluriculturalism** program — largely paper but opened discourse tool for cultural/political autonomy. Huasteca Nahua intellectuals (Hidalgo and Veracruz) lead; some identify with pan-Indian Latin American movements (Schryer).

Beyond intellectuals: ~8,000 Nahua and Totonaco in Puebla organized rural cooperative **Tosepan Titataniske** (“we will overcome together”). In Mexico City, low-status migrant native women increasingly open about minority identity — “**Marías**” carved ethnic niches (Mazahua fruit vendors Merced; Nähñus of Hidalgo/Querétaro selling *pepitas* north city; Amealco Nähñu gum vendors/beggars downtown). Unlike rural mestizo neighbors, tend not to emigrate permanently; Mexico City becoming more “Indian” again as a century earlier. Parallel temporary migration: unskilled Nahua miners in Pachuca mostly returned to Tetla (Yahualica, Hidalgo) late 1980s (Schryer).

**Schryer’s conclusion:** ethnic survival among bilingual and monolingual natives — and separate identity even in Spanish-speaking corporate communities — is not solely landowner/government divide-and-rule but **creative response** to uneven third-world development and impoverishment. Universal education and accelerating national economic/political penetration will increase assimilative pressure; land and village government will no longer suffice — survival depends on educated natives winning control of own institutions, especially **schools** (regardless of language of instruction). Example of mother-tongue writing: Jesús Salinas Pedraza’s 250,000-word Nähñu (Hidalgo) cultural account on microcomputer (Schryer).

## Geographic Spread

- **Core plateau and valleys:** Mexico, Tlaxcala, Puebla, Morelos, parts of Hidalgo and Guerrero; Federal District parcialidades.
- **Montaña / Huasteca / Sierra Norte de Puebla:** ranchero–communal mixed tenure; strong Nahua (and Totonac) military and agrarian politics.
- **Mexico state:** Mazahua/Otomí hacienda peon communities and later ejidos; commuting to Mexico City.
- **Urban Mexico City:** colonial-derived parcialidades → modern re-Indianization via temporary/permanent migrants.
- **Spillover:** Zapotec/Mixtec on southern fringe; Huastec enclaves; Papantla Totonac on Veracruz edge of the regional story.

Postrevolutionary artistic/political development drew on native traditions adopted by a state that also tried to destroy separate native discourse; partly acculturated native intellectuals in Mexico City sometimes opposed non-native/pseudo-native interpretations and forged new ethnicity (Schryer). ## End Conditions

**Ongoing.** No terminal date: indigenous presence, bilingual education struggles, cooperatives, urban ethnic niches, and debates over pluriculturalism continue beyond Schryer’s late-1980s/1990s horizon. The process’s character shifts from majority native society under liberal assault → minority islands under revolutionary state and indigenismo → selective ethnic revival and institutional control contests. End of the *colonial* legal regime in 1821 is the start condition, not the end of indigenous history.

## Historiography

Schryer’s bibliographical essay stresses **scarcity of publications specifically on central Mexican natives after independence**; scholars of the period often fail to distinguish mestizos from natives among the peasantry (Schryer). Key works he draws on: Schryer *Ethnicity and Class Conflict in Rural Mexico* (1990); Florencia Mallon on gender, ethnicity, racism, native discourse, and nation-building (esp. *Peasant and Nation*, 1995 — Morelos and Sierra Norte de Puebla); Redfield/Lewis Tepoztlán ethnographies focus on mestizo/acculturated cabeceras, under-attend native hamlets; Nutini/Bell compadrazgo stresses acculturative homogeneity; Guardino on Guerrero 1800–55 (natives establishing authority over non-Indians after republic abolition); Thompson and LaFrance on Juan Francisco Lucas; Escobar Ohmstede and Schryer on Huasteca sociedades debunking view that Liberal land law only usurped native land; Andrés Lira on Tenochtitlan/Tlatelolco communities 1812–1919; Tutino, Reina, Van Young on rebellions/Independence millenarianism; Powell, Hale on liberalism and peasantry; Weeks on Juárez myth; Molina Enríquez *Los grandes problemas nacionales* (1909) on class/ethnicity with residual racial stereotypes; language stats from Slicher van Bath, Beaucage, Carrasco HMAI map ~1940 (Schryer).
