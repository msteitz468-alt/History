# The Atlantic Slave Trade and the African Diaspora

## Definition and Scope

The transatlantic slave trade was the **largest forced migration in history** in
both duration and numbers (John Thornton, CWH VI) — and a "Crime Against
Humanity" (Durban, 2001). Over roughly four centuries (c.1450–1870s), an
estimated **thirteen to fifteen million** Africans were embarked for the
Americas in European ships; because of catastrophic shipboard mortality, only
about **eleven to twelve million** arrived alive. African forced migrants
outnumbered European migrants — often many times over — in every American region
except North America. Beyond demography, the trade was a vast **cultural
movement**, carrying a large share of Africa's diversity into the visual,
musical, religious, and verbal cultures of the Americas (the **African
Diaspora**).

This is the demographic and economic counterpart of the
[[columbian-exchange-1492|Columbian Exchange]] and a central strand of
[[early-modern-migrations|early modern migration]]; it is the human foundation of
the [[plantation-economy|plantation complex]].

## Causal Drivers

- **Plantation labor demand.** The decisive pull: nearly two-thirds of all
  enslaved Africans went to the **sugar** regions of Brazil and the Caribbean,
  and another ~15 percent to **mining** (mostly southeastern Brazil) — together
  about three-quarters of the total, in labor regimes Thornton calls "nothing
  short of murderous."
- **American demographic collapse.** The destruction of Indigenous labor forces
  (see [[pre-columbian-american-population]]) drove the substitution of enslaved
  African labor, beginning with Caribbean sugar in the early sixteenth century.
- **Pre-existing African slavery.** West and West Central Africa already had
  legal institutions of slavery (a class of persons who could be sold) and, in
  the savanna and Sahel, centuries of [[trans-saharan-trade|trans-Saharan slave
  export]]. The feature that enabled the Atlantic trade was not African labor
  conditions but the **legality of transfer** of enslaved persons to foreigners.
- **European armed maritime commerce.** After brief early raiding, the
  Portuguese shifted (from the 1450s–60s) to **peaceful trade** with African
  rulers, which yielded far more captives than raiding ever had.

## Mechanisms and Control

Thornton stresses that, with the notable exception of Portuguese **Angola**
(founded 1575, where European-led armies captured a substantial share of
exports), Europeans **bought** rather than seized their slaves. The trade was
controlled by **African authorities**: not every region participated (the Ivory
and Gabon coasts rarely sold slaves; the Kingdom of Benin entered, exited, and
re-entered the trade), which strongly implies **no African power could be
compelled** to sell slaves by economic or military pressure alone. He is
skeptical of the **"gun–slave cycle"** thesis (that European arms sales forced
otherwise reluctant rulers to enslave), finding no evidence of weapons-trade
manipulation in the commercial records and noting that African textile and metal
producers were not dependent on European goods. **[CONTRADICTION]** — Reader
(1997) takes the opposite view on both the gun–slave cycle and the demographic
impact; see the "Reader" section below and the controversy page
[[impact-of-atlantic-slave-trade-on-africa]].

Enslavement occurred chiefly through **warfare**, then **banditry**, then
**judicial action** (with proportions varying by region and political
conditions). The two purchase modes were **shipboard trade** and **factory
trade** (fortified coastal posts). Almost half of all captives left from West
Central Africa; Lower Guinea supplied about a third, Upper Guinea about
15 percent, with Mozambique a late entrant.

## Major Phases

- **To mid-17th century**: ~1 million exported; Iberian-dominated; Senegambian
  and Angolan "waves."
- **Mid-17th century to 1807**: ~9 million — the peak, driven by Caribbean and
  Brazilian sugar and the rise of the Dutch, English, and French.
- **1807 to 1870s**: despite British abolition and suppression, **more than 3
  million** more were carried (chiefly to Brazil and Cuba), at nearly the peak
  annual rate; children made up a rising share.

## The Middle Passage and Demographic Impact

The **Middle Passage** (three months West Africa–Caribbean; about one month to
Brazil) routinely killed part of every cargo — crews dying at nearly the same
rate as captives — with mortality driven by voyage length, dehydration, and
fecal-borne disease. Cargoes were 60–65 percent male and 25–30 percent children.
In Africa, the sex-skewed removal of adults distorted populations: an Angolan
census of 1776 records a male-to-female ratio of 43:100, raising dependency
burdens and disrupting gendered divisions of labor. Total population likely
declined where export was heavy and concentrated, especially in later centuries.

## Diaspora and Culture

Because heavy-labor regions never achieved self-sustaining populations, a large
share of the enslaved were African-born and -socialized, making **African
cultural elements** especially strong during slavery itself. Slaves organized by
**"nation"** (broad cultural-linguistic designations, not polities), and
national funerals and "country plays" preserved language and practice. Multi-
national residential communities eroded individual African **languages** within
a generation or two (yielding **creole** colonial languages) but **enriched
music, dance, and religion**: talented musicians blended forms to please diverse
audiences, seeding much of modern world music. African **continuous revelation**
shaped how Christianity (Kongo) and Islam (the Sahel) were received and
re-created in the Americas — from Vodou and Calinda to Muslim communities in
Bahia (the 1835 revolt). North America, with lighter labor regimes, became
self-sustaining and creole-dominated by the mid-eighteenth century.

## End Conditions

British abolition (1807) and the long nineteenth-century suppression campaign,
together with the Atlantic Revolutions' new concepts of rights, gradually ended
the trade, though it persisted to the 1870s and slavery itself to 1863–5 (US),
the 1880s (Brazil, Cuba). See [[slavery]] for the longer institution.

## Reader: African agency, the gun–slave cycle, and "Africa Transformed"

**[[reader-africa-biography|Reader]]** (1997) supplies a complementary and, in places, **contradictory**
account that the wiki preserves alongside Thornton's:

- **The granular African supply side.** Reader confirms (and enriches) the point that Africans, not
  Europeans, controlled supply — but stresses that the suppliers were **often small-scale**, not only great
  states. The **barter ledgers of Richard Miles** (Gold Coast, 1772–80) record 2,352 slaves bought in 1,308
  separate deals from **295 different individuals**, most selling just one or two; the **diary of Antera
  Duke** (an Efik trader of Old Calabar, 1785–88) shows the same petty, pervasive commerce — and the
  horrifying domestic counterpart, ritual human sacrifice of unsold captives. Reader's verdict is harsher
  than Thornton's: African elites "plundered the accumulating human resources... like asset-strippers,"
  selling "their brothers, their cousins, their neighbours."
- **The cowrie currency.** The trade ran on **cowrie shells** (*Cypraea moneta*) shipped from the
  **Maldives** via Europe — billions of them — the near-ideal commodity money that priced the slaves of the
  "Slave Coast" (Ouidah, Lagos, Benin); elsewhere the units were iron "bars," copper "manillas," or the
  Gold Coast "ounce trade."
- **The gun–slave cycle (contra Thornton).** Reader argues firearms made the transformation
  **irreversible**: ~**20 million guns** were sold to African merchants over the era; British traders alone
  shipped ~283,000–394,000 guns *per year* to West Africa, 1750–1807. A 1705 Dutch agent reported the Gold
  Coast had "completely changed into a Slave Coast... the natives no longer occupy themselves with gold,
  but make war on each other to furnish slaves." When the export trade ended, the guns let strongmen keep
  the power they had bought.
- **Demographic catastrophe (the transformation thesis).** Following **Lovejoy** and **Patrick Manning's**
  population simulations, Reader holds that the trade caused **sub-Saharan Africa to experience virtually no
  population growth between c. 1750 and 1850** — a population of ~50 million where, absent the trade, it
  might have been **70–100 million**. Manning's model implies that shipping ~9 million across the Atlantic
  (1700–1850) entailed capturing ~21 million, of whom ~7 million were kept in domestic slavery and ~12
  million died within a year of capture. The deepest damage, Reader argues (with Miller and Lovejoy), was
  not the aggregate loss but the **transformation** of African political economies into systems run by
  warlords and merchants — a view **disputed** by Eltis and Thornton (see
  [[impact-of-atlantic-slave-trade-on-africa]]).
- **One mitigation: American crops.** The Portuguese introduced **maize and cassava** from the Americas;
  cassava especially (storable underground for years, beyond raiders' reach) spread food production into
  new land and may have driven Africa's first real population spurt — much of it then consumed by the
  trade's own growth.

## Historiography

The modern field began with **Philip Curtin's** census (1969) and matured with
the **Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade Database** (1999; expanded 2010) and Eltis &
Richardson's *Atlas* (2010), the standard quantitative foundation. Successive
themes: volume and direction; the trade's workings and impact within Africa; and
— now predominant — the role of African culture in forming African-American
societies (the Herskovits–Frazier debate, revived by Mintz & Price versus
Thornton; Ira Berlin's "Atlantic Creoles"; Heywood & Thornton on West Central
Africa; Diouf and Gomez on Muslim slaves).
