# Phoenician Colonization of the Western Mediterranean

The **westward expansion of the [[phoenicians|Phoenicians]]** (eighth–sixth centuries
BCE) — the planting of Tyrian trading colonies across the western Mediterranean from
North Africa to the Atlantic — was one of the great movements of the early first
millennium, the maritime counterpart of [[neo-assyrian-empire|Assyrian]] land empire
and the rival and contemporary of [[greek-colonization|Greek colonization]]. It made
the inner sea a Phoenician as much as a Greek lake and produced **[[carthage|Carthage]]**,
the western superpower that would fight Rome. [[boardman-cah-v3p2-1991|CAH Vol. III,
Part 2]] (Ch. 32, W. Culican) is the authority.

## Definition and Scope

Phoenician colonization was **not the Greek model of founding self-sufficient
*poleis*** with a large agricultural *chora*. Drawing on Near Eastern practice of
trading-colonies in foreign cities, the Phoenicians sought **security and sea access** —
typically a promontory tip or small offshore island near a delta or lagoon, easily
defended and evacuated, good for anchorage, fishing, and salt. Culican stresses,
though, that the old image of a purely "flimsy commercial network of *comptoirs*" is
too narrow: **Carthage, Cagliari, Malta, Ibiza, and the Spanish and Moroccan towns
commanded real agricultural and forest hinterlands**, and the need for timber,
charcoal, and pasturage soon led to substantial land control.

## Causal Drivers

- **Metals above all** — especially the **silver of southern Spain** ([[gades-tartessos|
  Tartessos]] and the Huelva mines), plus copper, tin, and lead; the distribution of
  Phoenician metalwork tracks the Atlantic river-routes.
- **Assyrian pressure** — Esarhaddon's policies toward Phoenicia (and Assyrian control
  of inland markets) plausibly drove the **great expansion of the first half of the
  seventh century**; though, paradoxically, Assyrian demand may also have *stimulated*
  the metals market.
- **Population, harvests, and unrest** — Culican lists population growth, bad harvests,
  and political instability as unmeasurable but real factors; a *rising* standard of
  living at home may have sent the first pioneers out to satisfy demand for raw materials.

Apart from Cyprus ([[cyprus|Kition]]), there is **little firm evidence of overseas
colonies before 700 BCE** — the **Nora Stone** of Sardinia (eighth century at the
latest) is the earliest western Phoenician inscription, and the excavated record at
Carthage begins c. 750–720, a century after the traditional 814 foundation date.

## The colonial geography

Culican surveys the network region by region:

- **North Africa** — **[[carthage|Carthage]]** (Qart-hadasht, "New Town," trad. founded
  814/813 by Elissa/Dido of Tyre) and **Utica**, plus a chain of harbours along Tunisia,
  Algeria (Tipasa, Rachgoun), and Tripolitania (Leptis, Sabratha). The native **Libyans**
  were drawn in by symbiosis (labour, mercenaries, intermarriage), not pure enslavement.
- **Sicily** — the Phoenicians withdrew before the Greeks to the west of the island:
  **Motya** (the island fortress finally taken by Dionysius in 398), **Panormus**
  (Palermo), and **Solus**, in alliance with the native **Elymians**.
- **Malta and Pantelleria** — settled for the route to Spain; the inland capital at
  **Mdina-Rabat** rather than the harbour.
- **Sardinia** — a unique encounter with the wealthy Bronze Age **nuraghic** culture:
  colonies at **Sulcis, Nora, Tharros** (the richest), **Cagliari**, and **Bithia**, with
  inland forts (Monte Sirai) policing the metalliferous south-west.
- **Spain** — the goal of the silver trade: the **Málaga colonies** (Toscanos, Morro de
  Mezquitilla, Trayamar, some founded before 700), the **Huelva** mining region, and at
  the far west **[[gades-tartessos|Gades (Cadiz)]]** with its great **temple of Melqart**,
  beside the native silver kingdom of **[[gades-tartessos|Tartessos]]**.

## Religion: Melqart, the tophet, and Tanit

Culican insists "no account of colonization can ignore the religious dimension." The
process **duplicated the iconography of Tyre's Melqart temple** — the twin pillars (the
"Pillars of Hercules"), the perpetual flame, the tomb of the god — across the west to
Cadiz; **Melqart of Tyre**, conceived as a sea-god who preceded the pioneers, was the
divine *archēgetēs* (founder). A distinctively **western** religion emerged: the cults of
**Ba'al Hammon** and **Tanit** (eclipsing Astarte), large temples that doubled as asylums
and centres of commercial oath-taking, and above all the **tophet** — open-air precincts
where **incinerated children were buried** as *molk* sacrifices. New evidence from the
Tanit Precinct at Carthage shows **child sacrifice genuinely predominated** down to the
Punic Wars; Culican links its intensity to colonial insecurity and possible Libyan
influence.

## Major Phases

1. **Precursor trade and Cyprus** (before 700) — [[cyprus|Kition]] the only firm early
   colony; scattered pre-colonial contacts (the Nora Stone).
2. **The great seventh-century expansion** — the bulk of the western foundations, plausibly
   accelerated by Esarhaddon's pressure on the homeland.
3. **Carthaginian hegemony** (from c. 550) — as the homeland fell to Babylon and Persia,
   **[[carthage|Carthage]]** assumed leadership of the western colonies: Malchus's
   Sardinian campaign (c. 550), the rise of the Magonids, and the treaties with **Rome
   (509, 348)** delimiting trade in Sardinia, Sicily, and Spain.

## Miles: "Survival, Not Glory" and the Temple as Guarantor

Richard Miles ([[miles-carthage-must-be-destroyed-2010]]) sharpens the causal account:
it was **"survival, rather than glory,"** that drove the expansion. Intensifying
[[neo-assyrian-empire|Assyrian]] tribute demands — above all for **silver and iron**,
heightened after Adad-Ninari III's conquest of northern Syria — forced the Phoenicians to
prospect new metal sources, largely by **inserting themselves into pre-existing trade
networks** (Sardinia's, active since the 12th c.) rather than founding fresh ones. He
stresses early **Phoenician–Greek cooperation** as much as rivalry: at Pithecusa (Ischia)
Euboeans and Phoenicians worked together because their objectives were complementary (a
commercial "middle ground"), and Phoenicians helped reconnect post-Mycenaean Greece to the
Near East.

On Miles's reading the colonies strung along the trade arteries "like links in a giant
chain" formed a **defensive line locking competitors out of the metal-ore market**, while
the [[gades-tartessos|Gades]] temple of [[carthaginian-religion|Melqart]] became the
institutional anchor of the far west: the embodiment of Tyrian royal authority at a
distance, a **financial guarantor** (oaths sworn to the god; temple hallmarks certifying
ingot weight and purity), and the recipient of an annual **tithe of one-tenth** of Gades'
public treasury sent to Tyre. The same tithe-relationship bound [[carthage|Carthage]] to
Tyre. See [[heracles-melqart-syncretism]].

## Interaction and End Conditions

The colonization ran **parallel and competitive with [[greek-colonization|Greek
colonization]]** (Thucydides describes the Phoenicians withdrawing in Sicily as the
Greeks "began to arrive in number"), and it carried the **alphabet** and **eastern
("orientalizing") art** into the central and western Mediterranean. The homeland phase
ended as Tyre and Sidon passed under Babylon and Persia; the western network did not end
but **transformed into the Carthaginian (Punic) world**, which carried Phoenician
civilization down to **146 BCE**.
