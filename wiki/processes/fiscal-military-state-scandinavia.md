## Definition and Scope

The fiscal-military state in Scandinavia denotes the process by which Denmark-Norway and Sweden-Finland transformed their financial and administrative structures to sustain permanent armed forces at unprecedented scale. In both kingdoms, the medieval domain state — where the crown lived off estate income and feudal dues — proved incapable of funding the military demands of the dominium maris Baltici rivalry. Over the course of the seventeenth century, both states transitioned to predominantly tax-based financing. Sweden did so through the indelningsverk (allotment system), a domain-based assignment of military costs to agricultural units; Denmark through a shift first to extraordinary taxes and then after 1660 to universal direct and indirect taxation under absolutism. "Posterity has marvelled at the fact that the Swedish kingdom, with its limited population and resource base, could in the course of the first half of the seventeenth century elevate itself to the status of a European Great Power" (L14481–14483). That rise was made possible by the fiscal-military apparatus described here. See also [[militarisation-of-scandinavia]] for the social and institutional dimensions.

## Causal Drivers

**Permanent warfare.** From 1560 onwards Sweden "experienced almost constant war with its neighbours, and in some years it fought several neighbours at the same time" (L14627–14629). Denmark entered the Thirty Years War in 1625 and fought Sweden three times between 1643 and 1660. Military expenditure far outstripped domain income, forcing fiscal innovation.

**Gustavus Adolphus and Axel Oxenstierna.** Sweden's rise "was due, among other things, to the fact that Sweden developed an effective administrative and military apparatus, and that the country was led by two talented politicians. The Swedish King Gustav II Adolf demonstrated his talents as a politician, but also as a military commander who employed new military technology and tactics. However, Sweden's expansionary policies would not have been possible without the king's close co-operation with the chancellor Axel Oxenstierna who succeeded in persuading the nobility to make sacrifices and made it possible to mobilise the relative prosperity of the Swedish peasant economy" (L14489–14495). See [[gustavus-adolphus]] and [[axel-oxenstierna]].

**Military imperative as self-sustaining dynamic.** The doctrine that war should "feed itself" — be conducted on foreign territory and move to new areas to extract contributions — gave warfare "a momentum of its own" (L14087). Sweden extracted licenter (customs duties) from Prussian ports 1627–35, demanded contributions from occupied territories, and financed operations through French and Dutch subsidies and complex bill-of-exchange credit systems (L14718–14724). Yet the war never actually financed itself, and demobilisation debts created "the problem of peace" (L14748).

**Noble donations and the crisis of the domain state.** As Gustav II Adolf rewarded the nobility for military and administrative service with donations of crown estates, "while the nobility at the beginning of the seventeenth century had possessed one-third of the land, by the middle of the century their share had grown to two-thirds" (L14658–14659). This stripped the crown of the domain income needed to finance its own system, creating fiscal crisis and the demand for reduktion.

**Sound Due and Danish fiscal peculiarity.** The Danish crown had access to liquid, internationally-valid currency from the Sound Due — "by virtue of the Sound Due the Danish monarch also had at his disposal liquid capital in internationally valid currency" (L14915–14916). This gave Denmark access to international capital markets (Kielerumschlag) and meant the king could personally finance the first phase of a war. But dependence on the Sound Due created political constraints and international tensions.

## Major Phases

### Sweden: the indelningsverk and military-domain state

**Older indelningsverk (1620s).** Gustav II Adolf put the allotment principle into practice in the 1620s, initially for the cavalry. Farms were assigned to produce and maintain a trooper; officers were allotted farms (boställen) and income sources; up to eight infantrymen were allotted the income from one farm (L14614–14626). This exploited natural-economy resources without requiring liquid capital but depended on sufficient crown estates.

**Conscription and demography.** National conscription of every tenth man was formalised in 1619; from the 1640s, conscription was assessed on farms rather than individuals (L14539–14541). Between 1630 and 1648 about 70,000 Swedish soldiers died; of those sent to Germany, "60 per cent were killed, wounded or taken prisoner. The army's huge losses were caused more by epidemics than by warfare" (L14565–14568). In Bygdeå parish, of 255 soldiers conscripted 1620–40, 215 died (L14569–14570). Such losses depopulated communities and deserted farms.

**Donations crisis and reduktion.** By mid-century the nobility held two-thirds of Swedish land as donations. Growing tax pressure on a shrinking group of peasants led demands for reduktion (crown repossession of donated estates). A limited reduktion in 1655 proved insufficient. The full reduktion came in the 1680s with absolutism, reversing the ratio so the crown held two-thirds and the nobility one-third (L14793–14796).

**Younger indelningsverk (1680s).** With sufficient crown estates restored, the later indelningsverk created a domain-based military finance system. Officers received farms; rusthållet farms maintained mounted troopers; infantry roter (two to five farms) maintained one infantryman or boatswain (L14804–14810). This system produced "a professional army which was not mobilised in peacetime" (L14812–14813) suited to defence but proved "insufficient during the offensive warfare of the Great Northern War (1700–20/21), where, among other things, it was difficult to replace the soldiers who were killed" (L14813–14815).

**Great Northern War collapse.** After Poltava (1709), Sweden lost 23,000 men at a stroke; a further 10,000 surrendered at Tønning in 1713. Sweden "lost 100,000 men during the Great Northern War" (L14840), causing dramatic farm desertion in Sweden and Finland. Post-1709 the war had to be financed exclusively from Swedish resources through direct and indirect taxes, culminating in a capital tax in 1713 and "a state-led economy, a state capitalism, which in real terms distanced Sweden from the domain-based economy of the indelningsverk to an economy based on money" (L14851–14853). Sweden's exhaustion forced acceptance of harsh peace terms in 1720–1.

**Arms-industry component.** Sweden's copper exports, iron bar exports (replacing osmund iron), and tar production from Pohjanmaa (Österbotten) provided internationally valid currency to lease mines and develop a Walloon-staffed arms industry and export trade (L14670–14696). Town-founding concentrated trade, especially in Gulf of Bothnia mining regions, to increase money income.

### Denmark-Norway: from domain state to tax state

**Pre-absolutist structure.** In the mid-sixteenth century Denmark-Norway maintained "no standing forces, apart from minor garrisons in its fortresses" (L14868–14869). The mounted cavalry service (rostjeneste) of crown and nobility could be mobilised, but offensive war required expensive mercenary recruitment followed by rapid disbanding (L14890–14896). The Sound Due provided liquid capital but created international friction when raised.

**Domain expansion through Reformation and fief reform.** Church confiscation in 1536 trebled crown domains. Fief rationalisation in the later sixteenth century extracted greater crown shares, and Sound Due conversion to a cargo-based toll in the 1570s increased yield. These measures balanced the budget in the second half of the sixteenth century (L15012–15016).

**Transition to tax state.** The Kalmar War (1611–13) marked the turning point: after it, "Denmark-Norway changed from a domain- to a tax-based state" (L14979–14980). Permanent mercenary forces established in 1637 required systematic funding. The Sound Due became less significant as taxation grew. Christian IV was politically constrained: the nobility's tax exemption and the council's consent requirement forced use of alternative revenues (Sound Due increases, Norwegian timber dues, Elbe tolls), while extraordinary taxes fell on an ever-narrower taxpayer base.

**After the Karl X Gustav Wars (1657–60).** The domain state "fell apart once and for all" (L15087). The national debt of 4–5 million rigsdaler exhausted credit capacity. With this fiscal crisis coinciding with noble privilege resentment, the absolutist revolution of 1660–1 occurred. Thereafter the Danish state sold off royal estates for around 8 million rigsdaler and rebuilt finances on direct and indirect taxation affecting all estates including nobles (L15093–15096).

**Absolutist tax state.** Post-1660, ordinary domain income fell from about half of crown income to 3–12 per cent; ordinary taxes rose to about 40 per cent; combined direct and indirect taxes (including dues) made up about 85 per cent of the national budget (L15102–15108). A copyholder on a noble estate "whose (monetary) tax burden had increased sixfold in the first half of the seventeenth century, was paying four to five times as much in the second half of the century" (L15128–15130). Tax income was split roughly half from Denmark, one-third from Norway (falling to one-fifth after the Scanian War of 1675–9), with the remainder from the duchies.

**Military scale post-1660.** Absolutism brought large-scale fleet rebuilding and fortress construction. A national militia was formed (1661–2), setting five farms responsible per soldier. By the end of the seventeenth century total armed forces numbered 30,000–35,000 (one per fifty inhabitants). "At the beginning of the eighteenth century the armed forces were doubled to 70,000 men, equivalent to one soldier per twenty-five inhabitants. Thus, the Danish state was highly militarised, only surpassed by Sweden and Brandenburg-Prussia" (L15174–15178). British envoy Robert Molesworth wrote in 1692 that Denmark "resembles . . . a Monster that is all Head and no Body, all Soldiers and no Subjects" (L15178–15181).

## Geographic Spread

Fiscal extraction covered all territories of both composite states: Denmark, Norway, Holstein, Schleswig (Denmark-Norway); Sweden and Finland (Sweden-Finland). Tax burdens fell most heavily on the rural populations. In Sweden, Finland bore especially severe conscription burdens. In Denmark-Norway, Norway contributed about one-third of ordinary taxes in the early period, falling thereafter. Urban burghers — especially in Stockholm and Copenhagen — bore disproportionate burdens among non-nobles given noble exemptions.

## End Conditions

The Swedish fiscal-military state effectively ended with defeat in the Great Northern War (1720/21): Sweden lost its Baltic empire and reverted to a middle-ranking power whose fiscal capacity matched a greatly reduced military ambition. The indelningsverk persisted in form into the nineteenth century but was no longer the engine of great-power expansion. In Denmark-Norway, the absolutist tax-state created after 1660 proved durable as an institutional form, continuing well past the period of intense military competition. The ending of the Sound Due in 1857 and the transformation toward liberal constitutional government in both states completed the transition away from the fiscal-military state model.

## Historiography

The concept of the "fiscal-military state" as applied to early modern Europe derives from John Brewer's work on Britain and was extended to Scandinavia by Jan Glete, *War and the State in Early Modern Europe: Spain, the Dutch Republic and Sweden as Fiscal-Military States, 1500–1660* (2002), the foundational work (cited at L12145). For Sweden, the "military state" thesis of Sven A. Nilsson (*De stora krigens tid*, 1990; *Den karolinska militärstaten*, 1984) is the classic reference. E. Ladewig Petersen's "From Domain State to Tax State" (1975) established the interpretive framework for Denmark. The indelningsverk is analysed in Lars Erik Wolke, *Svenska knektar* (1995). For conscription's demographic impact, Jan Lindegren's Bygdeå study (*Utskrivning och utsugning*, 1980) is the model. Leon Jespersen's chapter in [[kouri-olesen-cambridge-history-scandinavia-v2-2016]] synthesises both states' trajectories comparatively.
