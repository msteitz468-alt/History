## Definition and Scope

The Paleolithic global dispersal describes the process by which behaviorally
modern *Homo sapiens* spread from an African origin to occupy every habitable
continent on Earth between approximately 60,000 and 14,500 BP. It encompasses
[[out-of-africa-2-60kbp]] and its downstream events ([[colonization-australasia-50kbp]],
[[colonization-americas-14500bp]]), as well as the colonization of all major
Eurasian regions.

This is distinct from Out of Africa 1 (the dispersal of archaic *Homo* species
beginning ~1.8 million BP; see [[out-of-africa-1-1800kbp]]) in that the
Paleolithic global dispersal involved fully modern humans and was ultimately
globally complete.

This process is the foundational demographic event of all human history.
Every regional history traces back to the initial colonization of its region
during this process. Every subsequent divergence of cultures, languages,
genetic lineages, and civilizations builds on the foundations laid here.

## Causal Drivers

**Behavioral modernity** was the enabling condition. The acquisition of full
modern language and cumulative cultural learning (~70,000–50,000 BP) allowed
humans to accumulate and transmit increasingly sophisticated technologies,
plan long-range migrations, build social networks that could sustain dispersing
groups, and adapt to novel environments faster than previous hominins. Without
this cognitive shift, the dispersal could not have succeeded — as demonstrated
by the failure of the earlier Levant "false start" (~125,000–70,000 BP).

**Climate** shaped the timing and routes. The Milankovic cycles drove glacial-
interglacial alternations. During warmer phases, corridors opened (through the
Near East, across Beringia, etc.) and habitable zones expanded. During cold
phases, populations retreated, regions were abandoned, and new adaptations
were required. Heinrich events caused abrupt disruptions. The process was
therefore a series of pulses — not a steady advance.

**Technology** enabled entry into new niches. Composite tools, projectile
weapons, fire management, cold-weather clothing, and watercraft all expanded
the range of colonizable environments. The Sahul crossing required seafaring
technology; Siberian colonization required cold-weather survival skills not
available to earlier hominins.

**Demographic pressure** after the ~70,000 BP population bottleneck may have
driven outward expansion as populations recovered.

## Major Phases

| Phase | Period | Key developments |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-dispersal | ~200,000–60,000 BP | Anatomically modern humans in Africa; behavioral modernity emerging; failed Levant dispersal |
| Main dispersal from Africa | ~60,000–50,000 BP | Exit via Near Eastern bottleneck or southern coastal route |
| Sahul colonization | ~67,000–50,000 BP | Seafaring; first open-ocean crossing |
| Eurasian spread | ~50,000–40,000 BP | Modern humans across South/East Asia; in Europe ~42,000 BP |
| Far north and extreme environments | ~40,000–20,000 BP | Japan, Siberia; adaptations for cold |
| Last Glacial Maximum contraction | ~25,000–18,000 BP | Retreats from many northern sites; ice sheets maximal |
| Post-LGM recolonization | ~18,000–14,500 BP | Renewed expansion; Beringia crossing; coastal Pacific route |
| Americas spread | ~14,500–8,000 BP | Rapid colonization of both Americas; Clovis tradition; megafaunal extinctions |

## Geographic Spread

The dispersal proceeded along two broad axes:
1. **Coastal/tropical axis**: The "southern dispersal route" from East Africa
   along the Indian Ocean coast to South Asia, Southeast Asia, and Sahul.
   Evidence: early dates in Arabia, South Asia, and Australia; genetic
   evidence for a coastal migration.
2. **Northern/continental axis**: Through the Near East, Central Asia, and
   northward into Europe and Siberia. This route required cold-weather
   adaptations developed progressively.

The Americas were colonized last, requiring the prior colonization of Siberia
and the opening of routes across Beringia.

## Interaction with Other Processes

The global dispersal interacted profoundly with:
- **Megafaunal extinctions**: on each newly colonized continent, large-bodied
  animals unfamiliar with human hunters were disproportionately vulnerable.
  Australia (~50,000 BP), Americas (~13,000 BP), and island extinctions in
  later millennia all follow this pattern.
- **Language divergence** (the divergence dimension of [[divergence-convergence]]):
  as populations separated, languages, cultures, and genetic lineages diverged.
  The dispersal seeded the extraordinary linguistic and cultural diversity of
  the modern world.
- **Neanderthal and Denisovan extinction**: the advance of modern humans
  across Eurasia was accompanied by the disappearance of all other hominin
  species, through some combination of competition, disease, and interbreeding.

## End Conditions

The process effectively concludes with the colonization of the Americas by
~14,500 BP (possibly earlier at disputed sites). After this, all habitable
continents are occupied. The process does not fully "end" — migrations and
colonizations continue throughout the Holocene — but the phase of initial
continental settlement is complete.

The Paleolithic dispersal transitions into the [[neolithic]] and subsequent
demographic processes, which reorganize already-settled populations rather
than colonizing new continents.
