## Definition and Scope

The dissident movement was the loose, largely intellectual network of samizdat
publishers, human-rights monitors, and legal-aid activists that arose across the Soviet
bloc from the mid-1960s, after the reversal of de-Stalinizing reforms (Khrushchev's Thaw
in the USSR, the Prague Spring in Czechoslovakia) foreclosed the option of working
within official structures. It is not identical with mass working-class protest
([[solidarity-poland]] fused the two in Poland) and, outside Poland, remained small in
numbers and limited in immediate political impact. Czech playwright and dissident
Václav Havel called it a "specter" haunting Eastern Europe in his 1978 essay "The Power
of the Powerless" — a natural and inevitable consequence of a system that relied on
co-opting conformity through material security rather than outright terror, and whose
suppression of authentic personal expression, he argued, made revolt inevitable.

## Causal Drivers

- The reimposition of censorship after 1968 in Czechoslovakia (following the [[prague-spring-1968]] invasion) and the narrower opening-and-closing of the Khrushchev Thaw in the USSR, which foreclosed the option of publishing criticism through official channels.
- Show trials of cultural figures — the 1965 Moscow trial of writers Andrei Siniavsky and Iulii Daniel, the 1964 trial of poet Joseph Brodsky for "parasitism," the 1977 trial of the Czech rock group Plastic People of the Universe — which scared some intellectuals into silence but galvanized others toward a wider defense of human rights beyond censorship alone.
- The 1975 Helsinki Accords, signed by the USSR and all its allies, which committed signatories on paper to human rights and gave dissident "Helsinki Watch" groups (Moscow Helsinki Watch, Czechoslovakia's Charter 77) a legalistic lever against their own governments.

## Major Phases

**Samizdat origins (mid-1960s).** Dissidents began circulating critical poetry, fiction,
histories, and political commentary in typewritten, carbon-copied form ("samizdat," or
self-publishing) once official publication was foreclosed. Unofficial transcripts of
political trials — such as the Brodsky trial, in which Brodsky told his judge that his
vocation as a poet came "from God" — circulated widely and exposed the moral bankruptcy
of Soviet justice.

**Legal-aid organizing (1970s).** Czechoslovakia's Committee for the Defense of the
Unjustly Persecuted (VONS) and Poland's Committee for the Defense of Workers (KOR), both
founded to provide legal and material aid to political prisoners and their families,
were among the most active dissident organizations of the era. KOR's work opened a
dialogue between labor organizers and intellectuals that would prove decisive in Poland
in 1980.

**Helsinki-era human-rights framing (1975–onward).** Charter 77 (Czechoslovakia, founded
1977 partly in response to the Plastic People trial) and Moscow's Helsinki Watch group
(cofounded by physicist Andrei Sakharov) shifted dissident strategy
toward documenting specific violations against a signed international standard, and
publicizing them through underground channels and foreign journalists.

**Three ideological currents.** By the late 1970s the movement had diversified beyond a
single "reform communist" position (weakened badly after 1968) into: **humanistic
Marxism** (strongest among reform-minded intellectuals in Kádár's Hungary, Gomułka's
Poland, and Khrushchev's USSR, but discredited by Prague Spring's failure);
**nationalism** (strongest where communist rule coincided with a sense of ethnic
subordination — Croatia, Slovakia, the Baltic republics, Ukraine, Poland, Hungary —
often carrying a religious inflection, exemplified by Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn's Orthodox,
anti-Western turn after his 1974 deportation); and **liberal pluralism**, epitomized by
Havel in Czechoslovakia and Sakharov in the USSR.

## Geographic Spread

Present across the Warsaw Pact but concentrated most heavily in the Soviet Union and
Czechoslovakia (where the reversal of a genuinely liberalizing reform created the
sharpest sense of betrayal among intellectuals) and reaching mass scale only in Poland
via its fusion with [[solidarity-poland]]'s labor base and the Catholic Church.

## Interaction

The dissident movement is best understood as a **symptom rather than cause** of
communism's decay in the 1970s–80s — a consequence of the economic and social
stagnation traced in [[brezhnev-stagnation]] — but it built organizational and
intellectual infrastructure (self-organization outside official power, articulated
alternative political visions) that dissident-movement veterans (Havel, Dubček, KOR
alumni) drew on directly when communist power began to erode in [[revolutions-of-1989]].

## End Conditions

The movement did not so much end as convert directly into governing power during 1989:
Havel became president of Czechoslovakia in December 1989 as leader of the dissident-led
Civic Forum; Solidarity, the movement's mass exception, negotiated Poland's transition
from within government beginning in 1989.

## Historiography

H. Gordon Skilling's *Samizdat and an Independent Society in Central and Eastern Europe*
(1989) and his *Charter 77 and Human Rights in Czechoslovakia* (1981) remain standard
references; Liudmilla Alexeyeva's *Soviet Dissent* (1985) catalogs Soviet-specific
movements. Havel's own essays, collected in *Open Letters: Selected Writings,
1965–1990*, are the primary-source touchstone for the movement's self-understanding —
his theory that authentic personal living ("living in truth") was itself a political act
against a system dependent on universal, low-level complicity.
