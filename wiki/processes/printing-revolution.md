# The Printing Revolution (Gutenberg and the Spread of Print, c. 1450–c. 1500)

The invention and rapid diffusion of printing with movable type, treated by **David McKitterick** in
[[allmand-ncmh-v7-1998|NCMH VII]] (Ch. 14). McKitterick judges that "of all the changes witnessed by
the fifteenth century... none has had so profound an effect as the invention of printing" — "the
first agent of mass communication" — while insisting the change was **gradual**: print and
manuscript marched side by side for generations.

## Definition and Scope

The *concept* of identical multiple copies was old (the Carolingian Tours Bibles; the thirteenth-
century university **pecia** system; mass-produced Paris pocket Bibles; the *devotio moderna*
devotional books of the Brethren of the Common Life; humanist stationers' organised scribal
workforces). Block-printed woodcut **images** had circulated since the late fourteenth century. What
**Gutenberg** added at Mainz around 1450 was the technology of **movable metal type**, and a market
logic: that books "could, if produced in sufficient quantity, be manufactured more cheaply."

## Causal Drivers / The Invention

Gutenberg's invention united three crafts:
- **Type.** Each character engraved on a steel **punch**, struck into a copper **matrix**, cast in an
  adjustable **type-mould** (the technical heart of the invention) from a lead–tin alloy. The
  42-line Bible's fount used ~270 sorts (German script's many ligatures/contractions) — a feat of
  analysis, with the professional scribe **Peter Schoeffer** likely crucial to its legibility.
- **Ink.** A new oil-based, high-metal-content ink that would adhere to non-porous type.
- **Press.** Adapted from the wine/olive press — cheap; the real capital cost was **type** and, above
  all, **paper** (most of a book's cost), drawn from a paper trade established across Europe since the
  twelfth century, though Germany's first mill (Nuremberg) dated only to c. 1390.

The first products served an **institutional market**: the **42-line ("Gutenberg") Bible** (c. 1454,
perhaps prompted by monastic reform), the Fust–Schoeffer Psalters (1457, 1459), nine folio Vulgates
by 1470 — large books for churches and libraries, which "do not need to buy the same text very
often," pushing printers toward shorter, cheaper texts for individuals (Zel at Cologne: Latin
quartos of Cicero, Gerson, Augustine).

## Geographic Spread

Print spread along a **north–south corridor** from the Low Countries through the Rhine and upper
Danube to northern Italy, carried by **peripatetic German printers**. Presses reached Strasbourg
(1460), Cologne (1464), Basel (c. 1467), Augsburg (1468), Rome/Subiaco (Sweynheim & Pannartz, after
the 1462 sack of Mainz), Venice (the de Spira brothers, 1469; **Nicolas Jenson** 1470), Paris (1470,
Fichet's humanist press — "the first secular learned press"), and within a decade Cracow to Seville,
Lübeck to Naples, and **Westminster (William Caxton, 1476**, a native who learned the craft in
Cologne and printed at Bruges). Only by 1500 did print's distribution seriously match population.

## Major Phases / The Trade

After an artisanal start, the trade was reshaped by **overproduction crises** in the 1470s–80s into
domination by a few **internationally organised firms**: Jenson's German-capitalised Venetian
syndicate; above all the **Koberger** house of Nuremberg (Anton Koberger: 24 presses, ~100
apprentices, branches at Lyons, Paris, Toulouse, agents across Europe; the **Nuremberg Chronicle**,
1493). Type-founding became a separate business in the 1470s, so different printers could share type
designs.

## Interaction

The printing revolution amplified everything around it: it disseminated **[[renaissance-humanism|
humanist]]** classical texts (while sometimes perpetuating corrupt *editio princeps* readings), the
**[[late-medieval-lay-religion|devotional and vernacular religious]]** literature of the age (the
indulgences Gutenberg printed "only hinting at the change of scale"), and the science and maps of the
**[[age-of-discovery|age of discovery]]**. It accelerated **linguistic standardisation** of the
vernaculars (Caxton's Chaucer and Malory) and the urban literacy stressed in [[urbanization|Dobson's
fifteenth-century town]]. McKitterick stresses it could also **degrade** knowledge (coarsened copied
woodcuts, frozen cartographic errors). Its full consequence — the transformation of religious debate
— lay just ahead, in the **[[protestant-reformation-1517|Reformation]]**.

## End Conditions

Print did not displace manuscript until well after 1500: the wealthiest (Federigo da Montefeltro,
Matthias Corvinus) still preferred manuscripts; illuminators found work decorating printed books;
the two media were often bound together. The "revolution" was real but cumulative — "the printing
revolution took shape only gradually."

## Historiography

McKitterick writes against a crude technological-determinist "print revolution," emphasising
**market, patronage and continuity** over the moment of invention, and the long coexistence of
script and print. The page complements the strong-revolution framing associated with Elizabeth
Eisenstein by stressing how unevenly and slowly print's effects actually arrived.
