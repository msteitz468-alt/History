## Definition and Scope

The Pacific plantation economy was the dominant form of colonial economic organisation in the Pacific Islands from roughly the 1840s to 1945. It combined large-scale monoculture or resource extraction (sugar, copra, phosphate, gold, nickel) with coerced or indentured labour, alienated land, and monopoly corporations underwritten by colonial states. The process was not uniform: it ranged from the sophisticated CSR sugar complex in Fiji (with Indian indentured labour) to German copra estates in Samoa and New Guinea, French plantations and mining in New Caledonia, and the haole sugar oligarchy in Hawai'i. What united them was the structural formula: secure land, import or coerce labour, generate exports, and rely on the colonial state as guarantor of last resort.

As [[denoon-cambridge-pacific-islanders-1997]] summarises: "Almost any concession would be made to attract a major investor — 'in Fiji the CSR, in German Samoa the DHPG, in the British Solomons Lever Brothers and Burns Philp, in the Marshalls the Jaluit Company, in German New Guinea the New Guinea Company, in New Caledonia Societe le Nickel.'"

## Causal Drivers

**Land alienation** was the prerequisite. Strategies differed: New Caledonia appropriated as "Vacant" all uncultivated land (1855 precedent), dispossessing Kanaks and confining them to reserves. The Solomon Islands Protectorate issued 999-year leases and Certificates of Occupation. In Hawai'i the Mahele privatised land; haole developers accumulated most of what followed. In Fiji, Gordon's approach — preserving 83 per cent of land in Fijian hands through the mataqali system while importing Indian labour — was anomalous and generated a distinct social structure.

**Labour shortages** were endemic because "in most places labour was scarce because land was plentiful and remained in the hands of its owners." Three solutions operated across the Pacific:
1. **Indentured Asian labour** — Indian girmitiyas in Fiji (60,000 recruited 1879–1916 under five-year *girmit* contracts); Chinese in New Guinea, Nauru, Banaba, and Samoa; Okinawan workers in Japanese Micronesia; Vietnamese in New Caledonia.
2. **Recruited Islander labour** — I-Kiribati in DHPG Samoa; New Hebrideans in New Caledonia; inter-island indentures in New Guinea and the Solomons ("an estimated 280,000 indentures being signed in New Guinea alone between the world wars").
3. **Coercion** — head taxes payable only in cash, forcing wage labour; punitive expeditions to enforce compliance; legal regulations favouring employers.

**Capital and corporate dominance**: "Their ventures could not be allowed to fail. The French government rescued Higginson's companies . . . just as the New Guinea Company's liabilities were cancelled by the German government." Colonial governments consistently deferred to large corporations over settler or Islander interests, because only corporations could generate reliable tax revenue: "only a vigorous export trade [offered] a dependable source of taxation."

## Major Phases

### 1. Commercial Expansion and Informal Empire (1840s–1884)
German traders dominated: Godeffroy, based in Samoa, "acquired plantations, bought Samoan-grown copra, and handled most business." Godeffroy went bankrupt in 1879; DHPG replaced it. British planters in Fiji, French in New Caledonia (penal colony from 1863). Tahiti limited by Pomare-era land protections. Labour recruitment from Melanesia (the "blackbirding" era) supplied Queensland, Fiji, and New Caledonia. Three sources of instability: competing colonial powers, settler pressure for annexation, and Islander resistance.

### 2. Formal Colonialism and Chartered Companies (1884–1914)
Bismarck's 1884 protectorates (German New Guinea, Marshall Islands, Samoa) attempted to govern through chartered companies (New Guinea Company, Jaluit Company). New Guinea Company failed catastrophically: "massive loss of life, as well as the dissipation of capital." British protectorates over Papua and the Solomons; Anglo-French Condominium in the New Hebrides (1906). In Fiji, CSR switched from plantation labour to Indian tenant farming (10-acre leased plots), transforming the labour relation while maintaining corporate control over cane prices. In Hawai'i, the overthrow of the monarchy (1893) and annexation (1898) locked in haole control.

### 3. Interwar Consolidation and Depression (1919–1939)
Post-WWI expropriation of German estates: Australian ex-servicemen replaced Germans in New Guinea; New Zealand retained Samoan estates as "Reparation Estates." Copra remained king for most colonies. Mining (phosphate, gold, nickel) rose in importance — gold overtook sugar in Fiji by WWII. Great Depression (1929–) collapsed copra prices; Islanders reverted to subsistence, demonstrating the resilience of village economies. Japanese Micronesia developed the most intensive plantation system under Matsue Haruji's Nan'yo Kohatsu (Nanko): sugar on Saipan and Tinian "produced almost two-thirds as much sugar as Fiji in the late 1930s," but at the cost of Chamorro and Carolinian displacement (from two-thirds to less than 10 per cent of the population by the late 1930s).

## Working Conditions and Labour Resistance

The plantation became, in [[denoon-cambridge-pacific-islanders-1997]]'s framing, "a self-contained autocracy. Work was governed by the clock and the siren . . . Overseers often imposed a reign of terror."

**Fiji (girmitiyas)**: Indian minimum wages were prescribed but CSR insisted on piecework, "and a generation passed before men and women attained the legal minima." Women recruited under a 100:40 sex ratio requirement, but in practice "women were harassed by supervisors and other men, and prostitution or concubinage were the only survival strategies available to many." When contracts expired, girmitiyas who remained found themselves as CSR tenant farmers, still subject to company dictation of "what crops they grew . . . and set the price for their cane."

**New Caledonia (New Hebridean labour)**: A visitor in the 1880s observed recruits "marched in gangs to the verandah of the owner's store, and there left for the inspection of intending purchasers . . . the transaction was as openly and avowedly a sale as anything to be seen in the old days 'down South.'" Girls "paraded in the yards" were sold for 500 francs (young ones, fifteen years), 400 francs (approaching twenty), 300 for those older. Women comprised 10–20 per cent of passenger lists; many became concubines or domestic workers.

**DHPG Samoa (Gilbertese)**: Workers walked 50 kilometres to petition the government in 1875, complaining of "recruited by misrepresentation, corporal punishment, Sunday labour, the arbitrary reduction of wages, withholding of wages, inadequate rations and miserable medical treatment."

**Solomon Islands**: "The law favoured employers, and the government had few supervisors and little incentive to harass employers. Violence was common. Black perpetrators were severely punished, whereas whites were quietly deported." Wages were "depressed artificially" through employer collusion: "All employers conspired to offer comparable wages and refrained from bidding them up."

**Labour responses**: Formal strikes were structurally impractical. The 1875 DHPG petition was suppressed; the Rabaul Strike (1929) — the most significant collective action — "expressed a simmering rage against paternalism" rather than wage demands alone, and its organisers were imprisoned and sentenced to forced labour on goldfield tracks. Pidgins evolved as both practical communication and resistance: "elusive enough to baffle overseers." Physical resistance — testing new overseers — functioned as a "moral economy" that gave workers "some marginal influence."

**Tonga Ma'a Tonga Kautaha**: Islanders formed cooperatives to bypass trader price discrimination. One — "Tonga for the Tongans Association" (1909) with "several thousand members" — was wound up by the British Consul at trader pressure despite assets "massively outweighing its debts."

## Geographic Spread

- **Fiji**: CSR sugar dominant; Indian indenture system; Fijian communal system partially insulated from full proletarianisation; gold from 1932 (Vanua Levu).
- **Samoa**: DHPG copra monopoly; German then New Zealand administration; Reparation Estates interwar.
- **New Caledonia**: Nickel mining; Kanak dispossession and confinement to reserves; New Hebridean indentured labour; Vietnamese and other Asian workers.
- **Solomon Islands**: Lever Brothers (400,000 acres), Burns Philp; copra dominant; chronic labour shortage.
- **New Guinea / Papua**: New Guinea Company failure; gold (Bulolo, Edie Creek from 1926) transformed economy; White Australia policies barred Asian labour.
- **Hawai'i**: Haole sugar oligarchy; post-annexation multiethnic labour force (Japanese, Chinese, Portuguese, Filipino); full incorporation into US economy.
- **Japanese Micronesia**: Sugar (Saipan, Tinian); phosphate (Angaur, Nauru); intensive state-private partnership under Nanko.

## End Conditions

The plantation economy was disrupted but not ended by World War II. Post-war decolonisation movements, the end of indenture (Indian indenture ended 1920), and rising Islander political power gradually transformed the structural conditions. In Fiji the ethnic stratification produced by the CSR-girmit system persisted as a structural legacy into independence (1970) and beyond (Fiji coups 1987). The process did not produce a "politics of class" because circular labour migration preserved village subsistence as a hidden subsidy: "the village remained intact, a hidden subsidy for wages which were far too meagre to maintain labourers' wives and children."
