# Garnet and Bead Trade (Merovingian Indian-Ocean Connections)

**Scientific provenancing that places Frankish Gaul inside trade networks reaching the Indian Ocean.** The
chapter by Constantin Pion, Bernard Gratuze, Patrick Périn, and Thomas Calligaro (Oxford Handbook, Ch. 36)
uses chemical fingerprinting (PIXE; LA-ICP-MS) to trace the raw materials of two prestige object classes —
garnet cloisonné and glass beads — proving that Merovingian Gaul "was not an isolated post-Roman backwater
but was embedded in wide-ranging exchange networks connecting it to the Byzantine world, the Near East,
South Asia, the Baltic, and sub-Saharan Africa." It is the Handbook's signature demonstration of the
"connected world."

## Definition and Scope

The Rhine–Meuse valley was the main hub for working imported materials into the jewellery deposited in
furnished graves — a demand sustained by the burial rite itself (see [[merovingian-economy]]).

## Garnet Cloisonné and the "Garnet Horizon"

The period **c. 480–600** saw a florescence of garnet cloisonné — sword fittings, brooches, buckles — a
distinct **"garnet horizon"** before its seventh-century decline. Trace-element analysis distinguishes
several provenances:

- **Bohemian/Central European almandine** (Type I) dominated the early phase.
- **Pyrope-almandite (pyralspite) garnet from Sri Lanka / South India** (Type II) is the headline finding:
  chemical signatures match South Asian deposits, implying "supply chains crossing the Persian Gulf or Red
  Sea and Indian Ocean, connecting Frankish jewelers to South Asian mining regions" — "a testimony to the
  global connections of the Merovingian world."

Garnet cloisonné essentially vanishes from the burial record by the mid-seventh century, with possible
causes including supply disruption from Persian/Islamic expansion across the trade routes, changing burial
customs, and fashion change.

## Glass Beads

LA-ICP-MS distinguishes glass recipes by origin: **Levantine** (natron-based) raw glass and beads were
imported and re-melted in Frankish workshops; polychrome millefiori (Eastern Mediterranean), amethyst
(Mediterranean/Red Sea), **Baltic amber**, and **Indian Ocean / Red Sea cowries** all appear. Beads were
"overwhelmingly found in female graves," confirming a gendered prestige economy (high-status women's graves
held 100–400 beads; see [[gender-in-merovingian-gaul]]), and local Frankish bead production grew in the
seventh century.

## Significance and Historiography

The science-based method is "methodologically distinctive within the handbook" (PIXE at the C2RMF, LA-ICP-MS
at Orléans), giving chemical certainty to the "global connections" interpretation and "challeng[ing] earlier
scholarship that treated Merovingian Gaul as economically and culturally isolated." It dovetails with the
maritime networks of [[merovingian-emporia]] and the diplomatic connectivity of
[[merovingian-byzantine-relations]].
