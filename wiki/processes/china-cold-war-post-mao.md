# China and the Cold War after Mao

After Mao Zedong’s death on 9 September 1976, Deng Xiaoping emerged as paramount leader and initiated “reform and opening.” China experienced a profound derevolutionization, changing from an “outsider” in the US-dominated international system to an “insider.”

Mao’s own late pivot (Three Worlds theory, publicly presented by Deng April 1974) emphasized development over continuous revolution: “The [United States] and the Soviet Union belong to the First World... We are the Third World.” The 1969 Zhenbao island clashes created the “worst war scare in the history of the People’s Republic of China,” spurring rapprochement with the United States (Nixon February 1972 visit — “the week that changed the world”).

Beijing reduced support for revolutions (urged Hanoi to strike a deal in Paris talks; sharply cut aid to Vietnam post-Accords; showed little interest in Kim Il Sung’s 1975 unification plans). In 1972–73 Beijing approved twenty-six major import projects from the West and Japan amounting to $4.3 billion.

Deng’s victory in the 1978 “Practice Is the Sole Criteria” debate and the December 1978 Third Plenum launched the new course. Household responsibility system (adopted 1980) undermined the People’s Communes. Special economic zones; price reforms so “the market rather than state plans would determine prices”; private and joint-venture business explicitly encouraged (Twelfth and Thirteenth Party Congresses).

Strategic partnership with Washington: diplomatic relations 1 January 1979; Deng’s US visit January–February 1979; February 1979 Chinese invasion of Vietnam “to teach the Vietnamese a lesson.” Military-technical cooperation (Harold Brown 1980, Geng Biao 1980, 400 export licenses). August 1982 US-PRC communiqué on gradual reduction of arms sales to Taiwan.

By the mid-1980s the Soviet Union was a superpower in decline and China contributed in crucial ways: partnership with Washington + continued confrontation with Moscow “completely altered the balance of power”; market-oriented reforms “destroyed Moscow’s claims that Communism remained a viable alternative to capitalism”; “Beijing’s repudiation of the Soviet model discouraged other Third World countries”; “China played a crucial — indeed, at times even central — role in bringing the Cold War to its conclusion in the late 1980s and early 1990s.”
