# The Spartan Hegemony (the "Second Spartan Empire")

## Definition and Scope

The **Spartan hegemony** is the period from the surrender of [[athens]] in **404** to
the catastrophe at **Leuctra** in **371**, during which [[sparta]] was the dominant
power of the Greek world — what the sources sometimes call the "**second Spartan
empire**." [[lewis-cah-v6-1994|CAH Vol. VI]] (Ch. 2, D. M. Lewis, "Sparta as Victor")
treats it as a hegemony Sparta was **structurally unfit to hold**: the very victory in
the [[peloponnesian-war|Peloponnesian War]], won "for the freedom of Greece," delivered
her a leadership role her institutions could not sustain.

## Sparta's disqualifications

Lewis stresses the paradox at the outset. Sparta had "**serious disqualifications for
the role of a leading power, even more for that of an imperial power**":
- A **tiny and declining citizen body** (*oliganthropia*) — a few thousand Spartiates,
  already supplemented by *perioikoi* hoplites and freed-helot **neodamodeis**.
- A military training "**calculated to produce obedience and conformity rather than
  independence of thought**," which left individual Spartans abroad unable to observe
  others' conventions or improvise their own (the stereotype created by
  [[pausanias-regent|Pausanias the Regent]] in the 470s).
- A constitution that "**had little place for successful individuals who were not
  kings**" — making the post-war prominence of [[lysander]] a structural problem.
- A **haphazard system of state finance**, now partly dependent on tribute and on
  Persian goodwill.

## Lysander's system: dekarchiai and harmosts

In the immediate aftermath, **[[lysander]]** shaped policy by action while commanding
the fleet. Where Athenian control collapsed, he installed narrow boards of ten —
**dekarchiai (decarchies)** — backed by a Spartan governor, the **harmost**, and (where
he could) a garrison. Lewis sees these not as a grand personal plan for power "exercised
beyond the state" (a later distortion in Plutarch's *Lysander*) but as the natural
outcome of the fall of Athenian-backed regimes: the factions that "won the ear of the
Spartan commander" came out on top. Samos — refounded for the returning exiles under the
harmost Thorax — is "the only place where we actually see a decarchy being appointed,"
and the site of Lysander's near-divine honours (the renaming of the Heraia as the
**Lysandreia**). At Athens the parallel imposition was the **[[thirty-tyrants|Thirty]]**
— "a larger decarchy for a larger state." Diodorus reports that Sparta then levied
**tribute of over 1,000 talents a year** on the conquered cities, the financial spine of
the new empire.

## Major Phases

- **404–400: imposition.** Decarchies and harmosts across the Aegean; the King's Peace
  commitments to Persia leave the Greeks of Asia Minor "in some sense abandoned." The
  watchword of **autonomy** proves "capable of considerable manipulation."
- **400–395: Agesilaus and Asia.** The accession of **[[agesilaus-ii|Agesilaus II]]**,
  the exposure of the **[[cinadon-conspiracy-399bce|Cinadon conspiracy]]** (c. 399)
  revealing internal helot/sub-citizen discontent, and Agesilaus' campaign in Asia Minor
  against Persia.
- **395–386: the [[corinthian-war-395bce|Corinthian War]].** A coalition of Thebes,
  Athens, Corinth and Argos, financed by Persia, checks Sparta on land and sea (Conon's
  victory at Cnidus 394 destroys the Spartan fleet).
- **386–371: the [[kings-peace-386bce|King's Peace]] and supremacy.** Sparta, as Persia's
  enforcer of the "autonomy" clause, breaks up rival leagues and reaches the height of
  her power under Agesilaus — overreaching at Thebes (the seizure of the Cadmea, 382) and
  provoking the **[[second-athenian-confederacy|Second Athenian Confederacy]]** (378/7).

## End Conditions

The hegemony ended abruptly at **Leuctra (371)**, where [[epaminondas|Epaminondas]] and
the Thebans destroyed the myth of Spartan invincibility (see
[[thebes-hegemony-371bce|the Theban hegemony]]). The deeper cause was the one Lewis
identifies at the start: a polis of a few thousand citizens, with a brittle military
culture and no institutional home for talent, could not garrison and govern the Greek
world. The Theban liberation of **Messenia** then stripped away the helot base that had
underwritten Spartan power for three centuries.
