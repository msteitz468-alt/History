## Definition and Scope

Irish patriot politics designates the organized political culture of the Protestant settler elite in the Irish House of Commons and in extra-parliamentary agitation between c. 1750 and 1791, in which constitutional grievances against British executive control and economic restriction were articulated in the language of Irish parliamentary rights and Protestant liberties. It is analytically distinct from [[irish-nationalism]], which succeeded it on different premises of popular sovereignty and Catholic inclusion; and from the concept of [[irish-patriotism]], which covers the longer ideological tradition from which patriot politics drew.

As James Kelly shows in [[jackson-oxford-modern-irish-history-2014]], Ch. 24, modern historians emphasize the "contingent, discontinuous, and individualistic character of patriot politics" — rejecting the older Grattanite narrative of a seamless tradition from Molyneux (1698) to the Union (1800). The process described here is specifically the *activation* of that tradition as organized parliamentary and extra-parliamentary politics in the second half of the eighteenth century.

## Causal Drivers

The foundational text of Protestant Irish patriotism was William Molyneux's *The Case of Ireland* (1698), arguing that the Irish Protestant community had the same constitutional rights as Englishmen and was not subject to Westminster legislation. Jonathan Swift had inflamed this tradition in the 1720s during the Wood's halfpence controversy. But for the quarter-century after the Wood's halfpence crisis (1724–5), the "undertaker system" — through which powerful parliamentary managers brokered between Dublin Castle and local political interests — dominated Irish politics and marginalized constitutional questions.

The decisive shift came with the Money Bill dispute of 1753–56, in which Henry Boyle and his allies challenged the Crown's right to spend a surplus in the Irish exchequer without parliamentary approval. This "classic whig/county/patriot issue" deprived the Irish administration of a parliamentary majority and created the conditions for an identifiable patriot party to emerge, with Edmond Sexton Pery at the fore. The dispute demonstrated that constitutional grievances could mobilize significant political support and could inflict real costs on Dublin Castle.

The parallel Dublin agitation of the late 1740s, led by the "ebullient Charles Lucas," also contributed: Lucas appealed to patriot rhetoric in challenging the oligarchical practices of Dublin Corporation, encouraging younger MPs to take a more principled stance. Though Lucas's direct impact was limited, his campaign extended the language of patriotism from Westminster-facing constitutional argument to urban civic politics.

## Major Phases

### The Patriot Party in Parliament (1750s–1770s)

After the Money Bill crisis, an identifiable patriot grouping in the Commons pressed for pension reform, shorter parliaments, and limits on Crown patronage. Figures like Henry Flood (a key personality studied by James Kelly) gave intellectual coherence to the movement. The undertaker system gradually broke down as successive chief secretaries, particularly Townshend (1767–72), created a resident lord lieutenancy that changed the political dynamics. Townshend's new system removed the local brokers and made confrontation with the patriot opposition more direct.

### The Volunteer Movement and the Constitution of 1782 (1778–83)

The American revolutionary crisis supplied the external catalyst that transformed parliamentary patriotism into a mass political movement. When Britain withdrew regular troops from Ireland to fight in America, the Volunteer movement — an armed Protestant militia organized to defend Ireland — provided both a vehicle for political mobilization and a powerful lever for constitutional demands. By 1779, the Volunteers were pressing for "Free Trade" (the removal of commercial restrictions on Irish exports), and by 1782 for legislative independence.

Henry Grattan's famous apostrophe of 16 April 1782 — "Spirit of Swift, spirit of Molyneux, your genius has prevailed, Ireland is now a nation" — captured the self-understanding of this moment. In practical terms, the "constitution of 1782–3" repealed the Declaratory Act of 1720 and modified Poynings' Law, giving the Irish Parliament formal legislative independence. However, the Irish Parliament remained deeply unaccountable — it was elected under restricted franchises, controlled by borough proprietors, and permeated by Crown patronage. The apparent victory was real but limited.

### Stagnation and the Emergence of the United Irishmen (1783–91)

The parliamentary reform campaign of 1783–5 failed. The Volunteer movement fragmented. The question of Catholic political rights — which would have required extending patriot rhetoric to include the Catholic majority — was shelved. Into this political stagnation came the impact of the French Revolution and Theobald Wolfe Tone's *Argument on Behalf of the Catholics of Ireland* (1791). The founding of the united irishmen in October 1791 represented the decisive supersession of patriotism by a different political project: one that proposed to "substitute the common name of Irishman in place of the denominations of Protestant, Catholic, and Dissenter" and demanded "an equal representation of all the people in parliament."

This was a conceptual revolution. Where patriotism had defended the corporate rights of one confession, the new politics invoked a popular nation that included all confessions. The transformation was rapid but not total: some elements of the patriot tradition flowed into [[act-of-union-1801|unionism]] (through its Whig constitutionalist strand), while others fed into the revolutionary nationalism of 1798.

## End Conditions

The process ended with the founding of the United Irishmen in 1791 — the point at which the patriot tradition was superseded by a politics that incorporated Catholics and sought republican separation rather than legislative independence within the empire. The [[act-of-union-1801]] then dissolved the constitutional framework (the Irish Parliament) within which patriot politics had operated, making a return to the original patriot project impossible.

## Historiography

Modern scholarship, guided by developments in the history of political thought, has moved away from the older Grattanite narrative of a continuous patriot tradition. Joep Leerssen's work identifies patriotism as a specifically early-modern constitutional phenomenon, not a proto-nationalist one. Ian McBride established that the ideological foundation was provided by "polity (the myth of an ancestral constitution) and religion (a sense of chosenness)" — making clear that Protestant patriotism was simultaneously constitutional and confessional. Jacqueline Hill's *From Patriots to Unionists* (1997) traced how the patriot tradition eventually fed into unionism for a significant portion of the Protestant elite. James Kelly's work on individual patriot politicians (Henry Flood; Henry Grattan) has reconstructed the political sociology of the movement.

The scholarly consensus rejects the term "colonial nationalism" (J. G. Simms) as anachronistic, and is cautious about the "seamless tradition" narrative promoted by Grattan himself for self-interested reasons (Koebner's identification that Grattan's published speeches do not always match contemporary reports is a standing historiographical caution).
