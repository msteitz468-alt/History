# Southern Plains Indian Wars

## Definition and Scope

Long-duration armed conflict among [[actors/comanches|Comanches]], Kiowas, and allies on one side and Spanish/Mexican, Texan, and U.S. polities (plus rival indigenous groups) on the other, centered on the southern Great Plains and Texas frontier. Gwynne’s longue-durée frame starts with Spanish–Comanche war from **1706**, not merely the Anglo Texas period. Distinct from the better-mythologized northern plains wars (Lakota/Cheyenne vs. U.S., culminating at [[events/battle-of-little-bighorn-1876|Little Bighorn]]) though concurrent in the 1860s–70s. By six years after the Civil War Gwynne called the western frontier “an open and bleeding wound”; hostiles included Comanches, Kiowas, Arapahoes, Cheyennes, and Western Sioux — but for [[actors/mackenzie-ranald-s|Mackenzie]] on the southern plains, Comanches were the obvious target as having caused the most havoc under every colonial occupation of the land.

## Causal Drivers

- Settler and ranching expansion into hunting ranges (Texas ~15,000 in 1836 → 604,215 by 1860; 1850s leapfrog past Cross Timbers into ancient buffalo ground)
- Comanche imperial defense and raid economy ([[processes/comanche-empire]]) — including raiding as political terrorism to roll the frontier east of the 1860 line
- State military learning (Texas Rangers copying Comanche methods; U.S. cavalry under Mackenzie; indigenous scouts)
- Market slaughter of buffalo (hide industry + explicit Army political logic)
- Treaty systems that neither side could or would enforce (Gwynne cites ~378 U.S. treaties made and broken as context; failed/fraudulent Comanche treaties 1847 Penateka, 1850 unratified, 1853 northern “representatives” without tribal power)

## Major Phases

### 1. Spanish–Comanche wars and New Mexican borderlands (1706–1821)

Raid/counterraid warfare for 150+ years; presidios/missions failed to protect converts or colonists. San Saba mission destruction (1758) and Parrilla’s failed 1759 punitive expedition ended deep Texas Spanish projection. Anza’s 1779 campaign and equal-status peace stabilized New Mexico but not Texas/northern Mexico. Spanish who hunted Comanches onto the [[places/llano-estacado|Llano Estacado]] often found themselves hunted.

### 2. Texas Republic and early statehood blood feuds (1836–1848)

- **Parker’s Fort (1836):** first hard U.S.–Comanche imperial contact; captivity wars begin ([[events/fort-parker-raid-1836|Fort Parker raid]]).
- **Lamar policy:** capital moved to Austin against the edge of Comanche country; 1839 east-Texas Cherokee/Delaware/Shawnee/Caddoan expulsions (“trumped-up” Mexican-plot charge; Chief Bowles killed) preceded western focus.
- **Early Republic militias mostly lost** to superior Comanche plains warfare; Lipan and Tonkawa trackers essential — policy and practice for the whole conflict; cinematic cavalry image often omits the scout.
- **Moore 1839:** 63 volunteers + 14 Lipans struck a Penateka village of 500+ on Colorado/San Saba; left horses unguarded; Comanches stampeded Texan remounts; Moore retreated on foot ~150 miles to Austin.
- **Council House Fight (San Antonio, 19 Mar 1840):** ~35 Penateka warriors + 32 noncombatants brought only Matilda Lockhart (nose burned off, body fire-scarred); Texans seized chiefs as hostages; melee killed 30 warriors, 3 women, 2 children vs 7 Texans dead / 10 wounded; aftermath revenge tortures of remaining white captives; Isimanica’s ~300-warrior demonstration at San Jose Mission. Gwynne notes major chiefs (Buffalo Hump, Pah-hah-yuco, Old Owl et al.) were absent — not a “nation” leadership strike.
- **Great Linnville Raid (1840):** ~1,000-rider trail; torture-killing of Tucker Foley (feet cut off, forced walk on burned prairie, then shot and scalped); McCulloch could only shadow; Plum Creek fight followed.
- **Moore’s Colorado massacre (Oct 1840):** 90 whites + 12 Lipans, dawn horseback attack on ~60-lodge camp (some accounts Buffalo Hump’s); Moore claimed ~130 dead in ~30 minutes, 34 prisoners, 500 horses, village burned; 2 soldiers killed — “more butchery than battle.”
- **Ranger crystallization:** irregular “spies/mounted volunteers” only mid-1840s widely called Rangers; methods copied from Comanches. Caperton: ~half the Rangers killed yearly; life expectancy in service ~1–2 years; of ~140 young men in San Antonio 1839, ~100 killed by Indians/Mexicans. Jack Hays as exemplar (San Antonio captain at 23; Walker’s Creek 8 June 1844: 15 Rangers vs ~75 Penatekas, Colt five-shooters decisive after cylinders emptied Gillespie shot the rallying chief).
- **Colt Paterson .36 five-shot:** Lamar ordered for Texas navy/army ~1839–40; reached Hays’s Rangers ~1843 — first multishot horseback weapon that could even odds despite fragility and hard field reload. Structural result: odds evened or better against Penatekas; high-plains core still unconquered.

### 3. Federal ambivalence and 1850s intensification (1848–1861)

Defensive, ambivalent, incompetent federal Indian policy; infantry/dragoons vs horse Indians. Failed treaties. Settler violence against reservation Indians concurrent with northern-band raids (e.g. Dec 1858 attack on 17 peaceful Anadarkos/Caddoans; murderers never charged; Neighbors removal 1859). Raiding intensified after 1857 as settlement hit the plains barrier. Antelope Hills / Van Dorn / Ford campaigns showed post-1858 white firepower advantage briefly.

### 4. Civil War vacuum and post-1865 chaos (1861–1867)

U.S. troops withdrew 1861; Comanches unleashed violence on Chickasaws, Choctaws, Creeks, Wichita farmers; ~100 Tonkawas killed 1862 (cannibalism pretext; scouting for Texans as real motive). Parallel: 1862 Santee Sioux Minnesota uprising (Gwynne: up to ~800 settlers killed — highest U.S. civilian wartime toll before 9/11; 40,000 refugees; 38 hanged). By summer 1864 southern plains free tribes raided Colorado–south Texas: frontier rolled back 100–200 miles; Santa Fe Trail nearly shut; Denver flour $45/bag.

- **Elm Creek Raid (Oct 1864):** ~700 Comanche and Kiowa warriors + 300 noncombatants under Little Buffalo crossed Red above Fort Belknap; burned settlement of ~60 houses; 11 settlers + 5 soldiers killed, 7 women/children captive; no pursuit.
- **Carson Adobe Walls (1864):** federal force nearly destroyed by Comanches (≠ 1874 Second Adobe Walls).
- **Pease River / Martha Sherman context (Nov 1860):** Nocona’s raiders gang-raped and scalped alive nine-months-pregnant Martha Sherman at Stagg Prairie; she lived four days, birthed a stillborn infant; one of 23 killed in two days — Gwynne frames as territorial war, not random slaughter. [[events/battle-of-pease-river-1860|Pease River]] (Dec 1860) followed; incorrectly remembered later as ending the war.
- **Spring 1865:** post-Appomattox Texas chaos; ~4,000 Comanches still holding civilization’s advance; cattle–gun Comanchero economy peaked.

### 5. Peace Policy failure → hard war (1867–1874)

[[events/medicine-lodge-treaty-1867|Medicine Lodge]] (1867) as “thinly disguised ultimatum” based on property/cartography/Manifest Destiny (Gwynne’s interpretive frame) and as “last great betrayal” via corrupt Indian Office. Grant Peace Policy (~1869): Quaker agents (Lawrie Tatum for Comanches); farming mandate. Structural failure: policy “rewarded aggression and punished good conduct” — raids → treaties + gifts; good behavior → nothing; 1868–69 reservation Yamparikas/Nokonis forfeited annuities for depredation claims of west Texas raiders. Reservation as sanctuary: troops barred → base for Texas raids; cross Red River to keep stolen stock. Even pacifist Tatum concluded force needed. June 1869: ~916 on reservation, none self-supporting farmers.

After Salt Creek Massacre (Sherman narrowly escaped) and Kiowa chiefs’ trial, Mackenzie reported Kiowas/Comanches “entirely beyond any control”; Sherman agreed to campaign + freedom to pursue hostiles north of the border — beginning of the end of peace policy. **3 Oct 1871:** ~600 U.S. soldiers + 20–25 Tonkawa scouts under Mackenzie broke camp on the Clear Fork of the Brazos — Gwynne frames as start of the end of Indian wars in America / “end of anything like tolerance.”

- **Blanco Canyon (Oct 1871):** Quanah’s midnight stampede stole ~66–70 best horses including Mackenzie’s gray pacer; Spencer carbine firepower gap (.52-cal, seven-round magazine, ~20 rounds/min, accurate to 500 yards vs Comanche revolvers/bows effective <60 yards); village escape over ~40 miles through blue norther; Mackenzie wounded by barbed arrow. First white attempt of this kind against Quahadis on panhandle plains.
- **Tonkawa scouts (“Tonks”):** occasionally cannibalistic tribe nearly exterminated by Comanches; essential for U.S. Army tracking — without them army had “shadow of a chance” on open plains; historically always out-horsed by Comanches.
- **1872:** Mackenzie “unbound” for off-reservation scorched earth; Comanchero Ortiz revealed llano water roads; North Fork village strike; multi-band prisoners; mass horse slaughter doctrine thereafter.
- **Hide slaughter as war:** 1868–1881 ~31 million buffalo killed; new tanning tech ~1870 + Dodge City railhead; Tom Nixon 120 in 40 min / 3,200 in 35 days (1873); Brick Bond 250/day; winter 1871–72 hide $3.50; ~5 million killed in two years on Kansas plains near Dodge. By spring 1874 middle plains decimated → hunters south to Texas Panhandle; March 1874 Adobe Walls trading post. Sheridan (quoted by Gwynne): hunters “more to settle the vexed Indian question than the entire regular army… destroying the Indians’ commissary… let them kill, skin and sell until the buffaloes are exterminated.” Killing food as deliberate political act.
- **Winter 1873–74:** reservation starvation; Mackenzie patrols devastated small parties (Hudson at Kickapoo Springs: 9 of 21 Comanches + 9 Kiowas; Buell near Double Mountain Fork: 11, etc.).

### 6. Terminal [[events/red-river-war-1874|Red River War]] (1874–75)

[[events/second-adobe-walls-1874|Second Adobe Walls]] (June 1874) under Isa-tai / Quanah spiritual-military mobilization; post–Adobe Walls summer raids Colorado→Texas killed ~**190** whites; hide hunting stopped; frontier “forted up.” 26 July 1874: Grant put agencies under military control, ending five years of failed peace policy; Davidson ordered friendly Indians to register by Aug 3 + daily roll call; army free to pursue to agency porch. Five converging columns (~46 companies, ~3,000 men — largest force Gwynne says ever sent to hunt Indians): Mackenzie’s Fourth from Fort Concho/Blanco; Davidson’s Tenth from Fort Sill; Buell’s Eleventh between; Price’s Eighth from Fort Bascom NM; Miles’s Sixth Cavalry + Fifth Infantry from Fort Dodge. Sheridan doctrine: harass through four seasons, starve, burn villages, take horses. Free-band strength: ~1,000 untamed Comanches (≤300 fighters) + comparable Cheyenne/Kiowa hostiles concentrated in northern Texas Panhandle canyon lands. Palo Duro village destruction and horse slaughter; Quahadi and residual free bands surrender 1875 to [[places/fort-sill|Fort Sill]].

## End Conditions

Free bands’ surrender 1875; reservation system at Fort Sill; ecological end of the southern herd. Gwynne’s continuity thesis: Comanches stopped Spanish northward advance in the eighteenth century and, post–Civil War, rolled the Anglo frontier back on a larger scale before final military-ecological collapse. Pease River (1860) was **not** the turning point — Comanche raids in 1864 were among the worst in history; 1871 and 1872 were bad years; only the 1874–75 combination of columns + hide slaughter ended free power.

## Link Taxonomy Notes

- **caused_by** (war process): settler expansion into buffalo ground; Comanche raid-economy defense of Comanchería; post-1869 political decision for hard war after Peace Policy failure
- **preceded_by** (not itself causal): Civil War vacuum enabled peak raiding but did not create the war system; Medicine Lodge **preceded** hard war without causing peace
- **contributed_to** end: industrial hide hunt, Mackenzie logistics learning, railroad/telegraph, disease-thinned demographics
- **produced:** reservation confinement; end of free Comanchería; Texas frontier memory and Ranger myth; parallel lessons for northern plains campaigns

## Fowler, CHNPA I.2 — northern/central plains war spine concurrent with southern theater (append)

Gwynne’s narrative above is southern-plains/Texas operational history. Fowler’s chapter adds the **multi-theater** frame:

- **Sand Creek (Nov 1864):** Denver gold ~40,000 trespassers 1859–60 onto ~5,000 Arapaho/Cheyenne lands; Colorado volunteer militia (mostly miners) attacked peaceful Cheyennes and Arapahos ordered by the Army to camp on Sand Creek; aftermath major Cheyenne–Arapaho–**Comanche–Kiowa** offensive into 1865; northern bands and Teton ranks swollen by Santee refugees after 1862 ([[actors/cheyenne]], [[actors/lakota]], [[actors/comanches]]).
- **Indian scouts after mid-1860s:** 1866 Congress allowed Indian enlistment; scouts got uniform and repeating carbine, kept moccasins, furnished own horses; Indian ponies outlasted grain-fed cavalry. Motives: traditional enemies (Pawnee/Arikara/Hidatsa vs Sioux; Crows in Montana; Osages vs Cheyennes; **IT villagers vs Comanches**), rations/ammo/pay, prestige, seized property, influence against southern removal.
- **1865/1867 southern reservation agreements** (Medicine Lodge sequence in bibliography; body omits place-name): Southern Arapahos, some Cheyennes, Comanches, Kiowas accept reservations, protection from unprovoked attack, supplies for lost game—government renegotiated 1867 when it could not keep 1865 terms.
- **Confinement by 1875:** winter campaigns destroying provisions; commercial hide hunters as targets; ~2,500 Comanches and Kiowas back to Canadian–Red River reservation to stay (Fowler does not name Adobe Walls or “Red River War”—operational names from Gwynne/army historiography).
- **Commercial hide hunting** nearly extinguished buffalo U.S. and Canada by 1880s—contributed_to southern and northern terminal wars.
- Concurrent northern sequence (Powder River, Black Hills, Little Bighorn) is part of the same **1850s–70s** multi-tribal war complex though distinct theater; by **1878** all American Plains Natives on reservations.
