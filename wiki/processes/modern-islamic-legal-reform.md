## Definition and Scope

Modern Islamic legal reform designates the century-and-a-half-long process by which Muslim-majority states, jurists, intellectuals, and political movements have sought to adapt, reconstruct, or restore the [[sharia]] under the conditions of modernity. It encompasses legislative reform of family law, constitutional Islamization, neo-ijtihad methodologies, and calls for comprehensive reimplementation of the Sharīa. Wael Hallaq insists in [[hallaq-sharia-2009]] that these diverse projects share a common structural predicament: all operate within and through the [[islamic-law-nation-state|state apparatus that destroyed the original Sharīa]], making genuine restoration impossible and producing instead a fundamentally different phenomenon.

## Causal Drivers

The process was set in motion by:

- The colonial-era reduction of the Sharīa to personal status law (family, inheritance) while commercial, criminal, and administrative law was replaced by European codes. See [[colonial-transformation-of-islamic-law]].
- Destruction of the [[waqf]] financial base that sustained the independent juristic profession.
- Subordination of [[madrasa]] education to state authority, beginning with the Ottomans and accelerating under nationalist governments.
- The perceived failure of Muslim polities militarily, economically, and politically relative to European powers — producing both reformist and Islamist responses.
- Post-1967 Arab defeat and the rise of political Islam as an alternative framework for Muslim identity and governance.

## Major Phases

### Phase 1: Liberal-Nationalist Reform (c. 1870–1950)
The Ottoman *Mecelle* (1870–77) was the first attempt to codify Hanafi doctrine in European statutory form. The 1917 Ottoman Law of Family Rights inaugurated state-sponsored personal status codification. Egypt under Khedive Ismail imported wholesale French commercial, civil, penal, and procedural codes. Post-World War I nationalist governments (Turkey 1926, Egypt from the 1920s, Iran from 1927) extended and deepened this process. The dominant ideology was modernization through selective borrowing: reformers retained personal status law as a gesture toward Islamic identity while replacing the substantive law in all other domains.

Key technique: **takhayyur** (selecting across schools) and **talfīq** (combining elements from different schools) to produce new family law provisions without claiming total rupture from the fiqh tradition.

### Phase 2: Constitutional Islamization (c. 1950–1990)
Under pressure from Islamist movements (the Muslim Brothers in Egypt, Mawdūdī's Jamaat-e-Islami in Pakistan), post-independence states began inserting Sharīa provisions into their constitutions:

- **Egypt**: Article 2, 1971 Constitution ("a principal source of legislation"), amended 1980 to "the principal source." The Supreme Constitutional Court's 1993 definition reduced Sharīa to broad Quranic principles applicable by any modern-trained judge.
- **Pakistan**: 1949 Objectives Resolution; repugnancy clauses in 1956, 1962, and 1973 constitutions; Federal Shariat Court (1980) under Zia al-Ḥaqq; 1988 Enforcement of Sharīat Ordinance. But the FSC's jurisdiction excluded the constitution, fiscal law, procedural law, and personal status; Supreme Court could reverse its rulings. The 1961 Muslim Family Laws Ordinance — allowing orphaned grandchildren to inherit — persisted despite FSC objection.
- **Iran (1979)**: The Islamic Republic's constitution required all laws to comply with Islamic principles (Article 4); a supervisory council of six Sharīa jurists and six Western-trained lawyers was created. But pragmatic accommodation of the existing state apparatus dominated: Khomeini's final doctrine held the state's interests above even the pillars of Islam, making law "a tool" of state governance rather than an independent constraint on it.
- **Indonesia**: Ministry of Religion (1946) promoted Islamic courts; Law No. 7 (1989) gave sharī courts autonomous authority without secular ratification; 1991 Compilation of Islamic Law unified family law nationally in a modernized form.

### Phase 3: Methodological Reform Movements (c. 1880–present)
Parallel to legislative reform, a sustained effort to construct a new theoretical basis for Islamic law:

**Riḍā's Neo-Maṣlaḥa theory** (1920s–30s): Converted the classical concepts of *maṣlaḥa* and [[maqasid-al-sharia]] from limited secondary tools of *uṣūl al-fiqh* into overriding meta-principles that could supersede any textual ruling. Effect: "a total negation of traditional legal theory."

**Khallāf's ijtihad framework** (1940s): Argued that cases not covered by explicit texts (the bulk of law) were open to ijtihad guided by the *maqāṣid*; published widely adopted textbooks on legal theory at Cairo University.

**Fazlur Rahman's Double Movement** (d. 1988): A two-stage hermeneutic — extract general principles from specific Quranic rulings (First Movement), then apply them to contemporary conditions (Second Movement). Criticized by Hallaq for leaving the mechanics of the Second Movement undefined: "just what the criteria are for rejecting certain 'important respects' and not others is a crucial question that Rahman does not seem to answer decisively."

**Muḥammad Sharūr's Theory of Limits** (1992): Derived from engineering and physics, proposing that Quranic rulings set Upper and Lower Limits within which human legislation may operate. Hallaq notes that Sharūr "employs the techniques of the natural sciences in order to make the revealed texts come to life" but, like Rahman, does not ground his method in the Arabicate hermeneutic tradition.

**Abdolkarim Soroush (Iran)**: Distinction between the "essence" (unchanging) and "accidents" (historically conditioned forms) of religion, permitting radical reinterpretation of legal rulings. Places at the secular-rationalist end of the methodological spectrum.

**Muḥammad Saʿīd al-Ashmāwī (Egypt)**: Distinction between "God's pure Sharīa" (universal moral intention) and the historically contingent fiqh elaborations; closely parallels Rahman and Soroush.

**Muḥammad Saʿīd Ramaḍān al-Būṭī (Syria, d. 2013)**: Principal defender of traditional [[usul-al-fiqh]]; argues that the Arabicate hermeneutical rules are objective, derived from the language of revelation, and cannot be replaced by modern rationalist substitutes. The 2006 Būṭī–Marzūqī dialogue published as *Ishkāliyyat Tajdīd Uṣūl al-Fiqh* exemplifies the "utter absence of common grounds of recognition" among competing camps.

## Four Camps

Hallaq maps the contemporary Muslim intellectual landscape into four partially overlapping groups:

1. **Ulama** — trained in classical disciplines, reading classical sources with traditional hermeneutics, functioning now primarily as educators rather than legal practitioners.
2. **Secular Modernists** — from atheists to moderate believers who reject any public role for religion; range is wide.
3. **Islamists** — trained in modern technical disciplines (engineering, medicine, business), employing "complex hybridity" of modern and Islamic hermeneutics; the proliferation of internet fatwas reflects the "multifarious production of 'religious knowledge' that has consistently lacked any axis of authority."
4. **State actors** — governments that selectively invoke Sharīa for legitimacy while maintaining the structural apparatus of the modern state.

## Geographic Differentiation

- **Turkey**: Complete replacement of Sharīa with European codes (1926); Islamic practice persisted socially despite legal abolition.
- **Egypt**: Longest experiment in jural modernization; Supreme Constitutional Court as the key site of tension between secular and Islamist definitions of Sharīa.
- **Pakistan**: Strongest ulama constituency; clearest tension between "delegated sovereignty" of God and practical sovereignty of the state.
- **Iran**: Unique claim to have merged state and juristic authority (Wilāyat al-Faqīh); in practice, "the great majority of laws adopted before and after the Revolution were Western in inspiration and content, and they remain so."
- **Indonesia**: Most pluralistic trajectory; accommodation of adat alongside Sharīa; 1989 unification of Islamic courts as significant institutional gain.

## Hallaq's Assessment

The process as a whole represents, in Hallaq's analysis, failure — not in the sense that reformers did not try, but in the structural sense that the problem they faced was insoluble within the available institutional framework. The epistemic vacuum left by the destruction of [[usul-al-fiqh]] as a living practice has not been filled by any of the modern methodological proposals. The Būṭī–Marzūqī dialogue is emblematic: "the disparity in outlook is highly indicative of a larger problem within the disintegrated Muslim legal culture."

The surviving movements are "all … testimony to the dangers of classification" and simultaneously testimony to the "failure to provide indigenous solutions to the epistemic havoc wrought by modernity." See [[destruction-of-the-sharia-thesis]] for the theoretical framework.

## End Conditions

The process is ongoing. Hallaq's prognosis is that the Sharīa "has become a marker of modern identity, engulfed by modern notions of culture and politics (but, ironically, much less by law). To assert that this marker will persist for some time to come would be to understate the case." Formal legal reimplementation, however, faces structural barriers that no political will alone can overcome.
