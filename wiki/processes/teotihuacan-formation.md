# Teotihuacan Formation

## Definition and Scope

**Teotihuacan Formation** refers to the process by which the city of [[teotihuacan|Teotihuacan]]
grew from a small nucleated settlement in the Teotihuacan Valley to a major urban center
dominating the [[basin-of-mexico|Basin of Mexico]] — from the beginning of the **Patlachique
ceramic phase** (c. 100 BCE) through the end of the **Tzacualli phase** (c. 100 CE). This is
the formative period of what became the largest city in the pre-Columbian Americas.

The process is defined by four interlocking dynamics: (1) explosive demographic nucleation
driven heavily by in-migration; (2) the decline and eventual abandonment of the rival center
[[cuicuilco|Cuicuilco]]; (3) the early construction of monumental civic-ceremonial structures;
and (4) the consolidation of a regular grid and centralized political authority. It is not
simply a story of "who" built Teotihuacan — the people were descendants of already-resident
Mesoamerican populations — but of *why* growth was so rapid and *how* the city came to
dominate a region.

## Causal Drivers

### 1. Cuicuilco's Decline and Volcanic Displacement

Cuicuilco, in the southwestern Basin, had been the Basin's dominant city (at least 20,000
people, c. 400–100 BCE). Its decline opened a demographic vacuum. Key causes of the decline
were probably: competition and possibly warfare with the rising Teotihuacan; and ash fall
from a **major Popocatépetl eruption** dated by Siebe (2000) to c. 200–1 BCE, which
devastated the southeastern Basin (as documented at Tetimpa in Puebla) and likely also
affected Cuicuilco. Survivors in the Amecameca-Chalco region of the far southeastern Basin
"would have had to migrate to other places" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 3).

The famous Xitle lava eruption that buried Cuicuilco was, according to Siebe (2000), a
later event (c. 200–400 CE), long after Cuicuilco had already been abandoned. "We can no
longer look to Xitle for an easy explanation for the decline of Cuicuilco and the rise of
Teotihuacan" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 3).

Critically, Teotihuacan, "at the northeastern extreme of the Basin of Mexico, would have
been far less affected" by Popocatépetl ash. The Patlachique population decline in the
Chalco-Xochimilco region was "followed by an apparent near-depopulation of that entire
region in the Tzacualli phase" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 3). Migration from the devastated
southeast partly explains Teotihuacan's growth, though not entirely — Cuicuilco was still
occupied in the Patlachique phase and unlikely to have already sent refugees.

### 2. Trade-Route Advantages

Teotihuacan was "on an easy route to the Tlaxcala/Puebla plains and beyond to the Gulf
Lowlands and to south-Central Mexico, while Cuicuilco was less well situated for
exchange" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 3). Control of the obsidian-rich Teotihuacan Valley, with
access to the nearby Otumba gray obsidian and (about 55 km north) the highly prized
Pachuca green obsidian of the Sierra de las Navajas, gave Teotihuacan material advantages
in long-distance exchange. Already in the Patlachique phase, Maya ceramics (in the Chicanel
tradition) reached Teotihuacan — possibly through intermediaries — and Thin Orange Ware
from southern Puebla and Granular Ware from Guerrero were arriving by the Tzacualli phase.

### 3. Perennial Springs and Agricultural Potential

The springs of the Teotihuacan Valley — which in 1954 still sustained about 3,650 ha of
irrigated land at a flow of ~590 liters per second — and a small area of wetland agriculture
(~100 ha in the Puxtla district) gave the site a productive agricultural base. The city
may even have been named "Tollan" (place of reeds) in Náhuatl, referring to this reedy
wetland zone. "This small patch of highly productive land may have helped give Teotihuacan
an early edge over other settlements in the northern Basin of Mexico" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 2).

### 4. Religious Authority and Ideological Legitimation

"Perhaps the Teotihuacan leadership capitalized on the fact that the Volcano God had spared
the Teotihuacan Valley in order to bolster their claim to a special connection with that
powerful deity" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 3). The massive Sun Pyramid — constructed in a single
prodigious effort during the Tzacualli phase — was built in association with an artificial
tunnel under its base, possibly created as "the physical manifestation of a myth about a
cave of origin" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 5). The experience of shared supervised labor on such
a project "could have been a means of altering consciousnesses to create a populace more
amenable to being subordinated by a small elite" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 5).

### 5. Political Leadership (Possibly Collective)

The rapidity of Patlachique phase growth "suggests strong, although possibly collective,
leadership" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 4). Average population growth must have been at least
1.5% per year in the Patlachique phase — a rate achievable only through substantial
in-migration, not natural increase alone. Multiple civic-ceremonial zones exist with no
single center in the Patlachique phase, possibly suggesting "a coming-together of several
relatively autonomous and, in terms of politics, relatively equal groups" (Cowgill 2015,
Ch. 4). By the Tzacualli phase, the scale of the Sun Pyramid suggests "powerful and highly
centralized authority."

## Major Phases

### Patlachique Phase (c. 100–1 BCE): The Initial Urban Explosion

In "one or two centuries, Teotihuacan grew from almost nothing to become a large city"
(Cowgill 2015, Ch. 4). Dense sherd cover extended over 6–8 km²; population probably
reached at least 20,000 — comparable to Cuicuilco at its peak. The TMP identified the
highest Patlachique densities on the eastern and northern slopes of Cerros Colorado and
Malinalco, with another heavy zone centered in TMP sectors N5W2 and N4W2.

There is little evidence of monumental construction in this phase except **Stage One of
the Moon Pyramid**: a square platform 23.5 m on a side, faced with small pink cut stones
set in mud mortar, oriented 11–12° east of north (about 4° different from the later
canonical orientation). No evidence of ornamentation, burials, or offerings survives.
Several triadic groups (three-pyramid complexes) in the northwestern quadrant may have
Patlachique antecedents. Pre-pyramid features under the Sun Pyramid — including what may
have been a free-standing civic-ceremonial enclosure wall — may date to this phase or early
Tzacualli.

Residential architecture was probably of perishable materials, perhaps similar to the
adobe-and-stone structures well preserved at Tetimpa in Puebla. Obsidian workshops
were already operating on the city's western edge.

### Tzacualli Phase (c. 1–100 CE): Monumental Construction and Basin Dominance

The city expanded to cover roughly 20 km², and population rose to perhaps 60,000–80,000
— three to four times the Patlachique level. Growth rate slowed to about 0.9% per year
(still very high) as migration replaced an already-large starting population. Population
across most of the rest of the Basin declined markedly.

The defining event is the construction of the **Sun Pyramid** — at once the city's
undisputed epicenter and one of the largest structures ever built in Mesoamerica. Its
basal dimensions were 216 m on a side (later enlarged to 222.7 m), height ~63 m, and
volume approximately 1,175,000 m³ (Millon et al. 1965). Tatsuya Murakami (2010) estimates
its construction required nearly 12 million person-days; at 50 years' construction time
and the known population, this would have required roughly 15 days per year per able-bodied
adult male — a modest but not trivial per-capita burden.

The **Moon Pyramid Stage Two** (29.3 m east-west at base) was also built during this
phase, and Stage Three added a modest further enlargement. The **pre-Ciudadela complex** —
several large civic-ceremonial structures with rectangular layouts — probably originated
in the Tzacualli phase. These pre-Ciudadela structures, faced with Teotihuacan concrete
and decorated with abstract polychrome murals, yielded materials from distant regions
(greenstones, cinnabar, marine shells, Thin Orange Ware, Granular Ware, Gulf Lowlands
fine paste wares). They were "thoroughly razed when the Ciudadela itself was built"
but attest to elite access over a region of at least a few hundred kilometers.

Both the Sun Pyramid and the Moon Pyramid Stage Two share the **canonical orientation of
15.5° east of true north**, which became the defining feature of the Teotihuacan grid.
The layout of the civic-ceremonial core likely evolved rather than being planned from the
start: "the layout of the civic-ceremonial core of Teotihuacan evolved over time" (Cowgill
2015, Ch. 5). The Ciudadela was not conceived when the Sun and Moon Pyramids were built.

By the end of the Tzacualli phase, Teotihuacan "almost surely came to dominate the Basin
of Mexico politically as well as demographically" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 5).

## Geographic Spread

The process was concentrated in the **Teotihuacan Valley** and radiating outward into the
Basin of Mexico. In the Patlachique phase, the impact was mainly within the valley; by the
Tzacualli phase, population in the Texcoco region just south of the valley had significant
Tzacualli material, while the southeastern Basin depopulated almost entirely. Tzacualli-
related wedge-rim ollas spread into eastern Morelos, possibly indicating migration or trade
connections, though Morelos was probably not yet under Teotihuacan political control.

## End Conditions

The formation process ends, and the mature city begins, with the Miccaotli phase (c. 100–
170 CE), when Teotihuacan was already fully urban and regionally dominant. The
construction of the Ciudadela and Feathered Serpent Pyramid in the following Tlamimilolpa
phase (c. 170–350 CE) marks the transition to the city's mature and most politically
confident period.
