# Christianisation of Anglo-Saxon England

The **Christianisation of Anglo-Saxon England** is the process by which the pagan Anglo-Saxon kingdoms of England—formed during the fifth- and sixth-century migrations—were converted to Christianity and integrated into the organizational structure of the Roman Catholic Church. The process spanned roughly a century (c. 597–680 CE) and proceeded from two distinct missionary directions: the Roman mission of **Augustine of Canterbury** (597 CE) from the south, and the Irish monastic mission of **Aidan of Lindisfarne** (635 CE) from the north. The clash of these two traditions, resolved in favor of Rome at the **[[synod-of-whitby-664|Synod of Whitby]]** (664 CE), and the subsequent institutional consolidation by Archbishop **[[theodore-of-tarsus|Theodore of Tarsus]]** (669–690 CE), produced the organized English church that became one of the most powerful agents of literacy, learning, and missionary activity in early medieval Europe.

---

## Definition and Scope

Unlike the Christianisation of the Frankish or Visigothic kingdoms—where the ruling classes were already Christian (or Arian) and the process was one of confessional conversion and doctrinal unification—the Anglo-Saxon conversion required the introduction of Christianity to an almost entirely pagan population. The Anglo-Saxon migrations of the fifth century appear to have effectively swept away the Romano-British Christian population of lowland Britain (whether through displacement, demographic replacement, or cultural assimilation is debated), leaving the seventh-century missionaries facing a genuinely pagan religious landscape.

## Causal Drivers

### Pope Gregory I's Papal Initiative
The conversion began as a deliberate **top-down political strategy** of Pope **Gregory I** ("the Great", r. 590–604 CE). Gregory dispatched a mission of Benedictine monks from Rome under the prior **Augustine** in 596 CE, specifically targeting the kingdom of **Kent**, whose queen **Bertha** was already a Frankish Christian princess. Gregory's letters to Augustine articulate a careful policy: do not destroy pagan temples, but consecrate them as churches; do not abolish pagan festivals, but redirect them to Christian purposes (an early formulation of what modern scholars call **syncretism** or, critically, cultural accommodation). Gregory's explicit goal was the conversion of the *rex Anglorum*—ultimately, of all the English kingdoms.

### Royal Conversion as Political Instrument
The conversion of individual kingdoms followed, overwhelmingly, the decision of their kings. **Æthelberht of Kent** was baptized in 597 CE; **Rædwald of East Anglia** (the probable occupant of the Sutton Hoo ship burial) accepted baptism but maintained a dual altar (pagan and Christian) in his hall. **Edwin of Northumbria** was baptized in 627 CE after a famous council at which his councillors compared human life to a sparrow flying briefly through a lit mead-hall before returning to darkness—a passage recorded by Bede that encapsulates the appeal of a religion offering afterlife guarantees. In each case, the king's conversion triggered the nominal conversion of his nobility and thegns, as followers adopted the identity and symbolic markers of their lord.

### The Irish Mission from Iona
Simultaneously, **Iona** (the great Irish monastery founded by Columba in 563 CE off the Scottish coast) was the base for the conversion of the **Picts** and the **Northumbrians**. At the invitation of the Northumbrian king **Oswald** (r. 634–642 CE), the Irish monk **Aidan** established the monastery of **Lindisfarne** (Holy Island) in 635 CE, which became the center of Irish missionary activity across northern England. The Irish monastic model differed from the Roman diocesan model: the abbot of the monastery (not the bishop) was the primary authority; the monastery rather than the cathedral city was the center of ecclesiastical life; and specific liturgical practices (the method of calculating Easter, the form of the clerical tonsure) differed from Rome.

## Major Phases

### Phase 1: The Roman Mission to Kent and the South (597–633 CE)
Augustine arrived in Kent in 597 CE and established the see of **Canterbury** as the primary archiepiscopal center. He consecrated the cathedral of **Christ Church, Canterbury** (on the site of a Roman church), founded the monastery of **St. Augustine's**, and baptized Æthelberht. Pope Gregory envisioned two archbishoprics—Canterbury and York—each with twelve suffragans, but the northern archbishopric was not effectively established until the following century. The Roman mission spread into Essex (London) and East Anglia but suffered severe setbacks after Æthelberht's death (616 CE), when pagan reaction temporarily drove the missionaries from London and Kent.

### Phase 2: Northumbrian Florescence and Irish Competition (634–663 CE)
The Irish mission from Lindisfarne converted Northumbria more thoroughly than the Roman mission had the south, producing the remarkable cultural florescence of seventh-century Northumbria—the world of **Cuthbert** (d. 687 CE), **Caedmon** (the first named English poet), and the foundations of what would become the Northumbrian school from which **Bede** (673–735 CE) emerged.

### Phase 3: The Synod of Whitby and the Roman Decision (664 CE)
The coexistence of two missions with different liturgical practices became untenable within a single kingdom. King **Oswiu of Northumbria** convened the **[[synod-of-whitby-664|Synod of Whitby]]** in 664 CE to adjudicate between the Irish and Roman methods of computing Easter. Oswiu ruled in favor of Rome (famously swayed by the argument that St. Peter, who held the keys of Heaven, must outrank St. Columba). The decision was pivotal: it brought Northumbria into the Roman liturgical orbit and ended the organizational independence of the Irish mission in England.

### Phase 4: Theodore's Institutional Consolidation (669–690 CE)
The appointment of **[[theodore-of-tarsus|Theodore of Tarsus]]** as Archbishop of Canterbury in 669 CE—a Greek-speaking scholar from Cilicia, appointed by Pope Vitalian—was the decisive institutional turning point. Theodore:
1.  Conducted the first comprehensive **visitation** of all the English kingdoms, establishing the authority of Canterbury over the entire English church.
2.  Reorganized the **diocesan structure**, creating new sees (subdividing Northumbria, establishing sees in Mercia, etc.) and systematically placing the network of episcopal authority across the whole country.
3.  Founded the **school of Canterbury**, which attracted students from across England (including Aldhelm of Malmesbury and Bede's teachers) and introduced the study of Greek, Latin grammar, computus (Easter calculation), and music.
4.  Convened the **Synod of Hertford** (672 CE), the first synod of the entire English church—a landmark in the emergence of an ecclesia Anglicana ("church of the English") as a distinct entity.

## Christianisation and the Cult of Saints

**Ian Wood** (*NCMH*, Ch. 25) emphasizes the role of the **cult of saints** in the practical mechanics of conversion. The monastery of Lindisfarne promoted the cult of **Cuthbert** (d. 687 CE); Canterbury promoted the cults of its own archbishops and of the Kentish royal martyrs. Saints served as:
*   **Local intercessors**: Specific saints were patrons of specific localities, kingdoms, and communities, making Christianity legible in terms of the local landscape.
*   **Loci of miraculous power**: The relics of saints—physical objects (bones, clothing, associated artifacts)—were held to channel divine healing power, providing a practical religious service analogous to pagan sacred sites.
*   **Political instruments**: A king who possessed the relics of a powerful saint possessed his protection; translating relics (moving them to a new church or shrine) was a political as well as a religious act.

The syncretist dimension was also real: Bede records (and condemns) practices of wearing amulets, consulting auguries, and maintaining cattle-sacrifices that persisted alongside nominal Christian identity for generations after formal conversion.

## End Conditions and Legacy

By c. 680 CE, all the major Anglo-Saxon kingdoms had been formally converted, and the institutional church was organized under the dual hierarchy of Canterbury and (from 735 CE) a fully empowered **York**. The Christianisation of England produced three long-term consequences of enormous historical importance:

1.  **The English intellectual tradition**: The school at Canterbury, Lindisfarne, and Wearmouth-Jarrow produced the **Venerable Bede** (*Historia Ecclesiastica Gentis Anglorum*, 731 CE)—the most important historical work produced in the early medieval West—and the traditions of Latin scholarship that fed into the **Carolingian Renaissance**.
2.  **The continental mission**: English monks—principally **Willibrord** (d. 739 CE) and **Boniface** (Wynfrith, d. 754 CE)—carried Christianity to Frisia, Hesse, and Thuringia, effectively Christianising Germania. This mission was organized from England and maintained close ties to Canterbury and Rome.
3.  **[[insular-art|Insular art]]**: The fusion of Irish monastic book culture, Germanic metalwork tradition, and Mediterranean iconographic models produced the Lindisfarne Gospels, the Book of Kells, and a distinctive artistic tradition of lasting influence.
