# The Pax Mongolica

The **Pax Mongolica** ("Mongol Peace") is the period of relative security and
unprecedented connectivity across Eurasia under the [[mongol-empire|Mongol Empire]] and
its successor khanates (c. 1206–c. 1350), during which a single political order — or a
family of cooperating Chinggisid states — made it possible for merchants, missionaries,
envoys, artisans, and ideas to traverse the continent from the Carpathians to the Sea of
Japan. It is the **enabling condition of the [[chinggis-exchange|Chinggis Exchange]]**:
the infrastructure of connection on which the great Mongol-era transfers travelled.
[[may-mongol-conquests-2012|Timothy May]] treats it as the most consequential of the
empire's structural achievements.

## Definition and scope

The proverb captured the ideal — that "a virgin carrying a gold urn filled with jewels
could walk from one end of the empire to another without being molested." May treats
this as exaggeration with a real core: the Eurasian routes were **more secure than they
had ever been**, and entirely new ones opened beyond the old [[silk-roads|Silk Road]].
The Pax Mongolica was never total peace — the khanates fought near-constant civil wars
after 1260 — but trade and revenue continued *across* the front lines, because the
*Altan Urugh* (Golden Family) collected rents from each other's territories and had a
shared interest in keeping the routes open. The Polos crossed a Eurasia at war.

## Causal drivers

May, following Janet Abu-Lughod and Thomas Allsen, locates the breakthrough in a
**change of mindset**, not merely the elimination of rival toll-takers. Pre-conquest
steppe–sedentary trade was sporadic, coercive, and conducted at border towns; the
Mongols **reversed the flow** — having grown rich and remaining in the steppe, they made
the merchants come to *them*, and paid fair prices for bulk and luxury goods alike.
[[genghis-khan|Chinggis Khan]] cultivated Muslim merchants from early in his career
(the Baljuna covenant with the merchant Asan), posted route guards (*qaraqchin*) at the
1206 *quriltai*, and sponsored the international caravan whose massacre at **Otrar**
(1218) triggered the destruction of the Khwarazmian Empire — for Allsen "at bottom a
pocket book issue." Under **Ögödei** the capital **Karakorum** became a polyglot
commercial hub with royal warehouses, granaries, and walled desert wells across the Gobi.

## Instruments

- **The *yam*** — the relay postal/courier system of way stations that supplied mounts,
  food, and shelter to authorized travellers, "tying the loose ends of the empire
  together"; merchants used its routes as the fastest and safest.
- **The *ortagh*** — merchant partnerships financed by the khans and princes, who
  invested capital with (mainly Muslim) trading companies and shared the returns.
- **Security and predictability** — route guards, regularized tolls, and "calculable"
  transport costs (Abu-Lughod), making long-distance bulk trade profitable.
- **A continental silver stratum** and shared demand for luxury goods (*nasij* brocade,
  porcelain) linking markets from England to China.

## Geographic spread and phases

The unified empire (1206–1260) created the first Pax Mongolica from Mongolia outward.
After the dissolution into four khanates the peace **fragmented but did not collapse**:
trade and tribute crossed even hostile borders, and the route between the
[[ilkhanate|Ilkhanate]] and the Yuan increasingly went **by sea** around India when the
[[golden-horde|Golden Horde]]–Ilkhanate war closed the land link. Periods of renewed
peace (after Qaidu's death in 1301; under Abu Said and Üzbek Khan in the 1320s–30s)
restored overland flows.

## End conditions

The Pax Mongolica unravelled in the mid-fourteenth century through the same channels it
had opened. Its own connectivity carried the **[[black-death|Black Death]]** west out of
the steppe (Kaffa, 1346), and the plague — recurring in waves to 1396 — gutted the
armies, bureaucracies, and trading cities of the khanates (Uli Schamiloglu argues it was
the single greatest factor in the [[golden-horde|Jochid Khanate's]] decline). The fall
of the Ilkhanate (1335) and the Yuan (1368), and Timur's later devastation of the
Jochid cities, ended the integrated system — though the routes and the appetite for
their goods outlived it, feeding into the early-modern world.

## A note of caution

The term itself invites overstatement. [[morgan-the-mongols|David Morgan]] quotes Herbert
Franke's deflation — "the *Pax Mongolica* is no more than one of those brilliant
simplifications that can serve as chapter titles for world history books" — and notes the
concept is modelled on the *Pax Romana*. The peace was real but **partial and intermittent**:
punctuated by the khanates' civil wars, never extending security uniformly, and resting as
much on the threat of Mongol violence as on settled order. The wiki uses the term for the
genuine connectivity the empire created while keeping Morgan's caution against romanticizing
it.
