## Definition and Scope

Japan's transition from foraging to farming — distinctive because the
**Jomon** culture sustained dense, sedentary, pottery-using forager societies for
millennia *before* agriculture, and farming arrived comparatively late and from
outside. Covered in CWH Vol. II Ch. 14 (Simon Kaner & Ken'ichi Yano), case study
the **Nara basin paddies** in Ch. 15 (Ken'ichi Okada). The collection is
otherwise thin on Japanese narrative history, so this is a key page.

## The Jomon: affluent foragers (the long prelude)

The **Jomon** (from ~14,000 BCE) made some of the world's earliest **pottery**
(for boiling/steaming — see the East Asian culinary tradition,
[[early-agriculture-china]]) and built **relatively sedentary villages**
sustained by the rich resources of forests, rivers, lakes, and coasts. Broad-based
foraging replaced big-game hunting early; by the middle Jomon, large regional
centres (eastern/northern Japan) anchored exchange networks in greenstone and
obsidian, and Pacific-coast **shell middens** show intensive (and pressuring)
marine exploitation. The Jomon is a premier example of **[[sedentism]] without
agriculture** (cf. its appearance on the [[mesolithic]] page).

**Revised view:** Jomon subsistence sometimes included an element of *cultivation
and management* — tree fruits (chestnut), barnyard millet, soybean — so the old
sharp Jomon-forager / Yayoi-farmer binary is softening; scholars now "redefine
what is understood by cultivation among temperate foragers."

## The Yayoi transition: paddy rice arrives

Wet **paddy rice farming** became established in **northwestern Kyushu in the
first centuries of the first millennium BCE** (rice itself introduced from ~900
BCE), arriving from the **Korean peninsula or directly from southern China**.
Crucially, it was **not** a simple step-change: rice was first **incorporated
into existing Jomon foraging** and practised alongside dry-field horticulture
(barnyard/broomcorn/foxtail millet, barley, pulses, fruit) — as in China.

Whether the Yayoi represents **new people, transformed Jomon foragers, or a
mixture** is hotly debated. The evidence points to **mixing of incoming traits
with native practices** rather than wholesale replacement — rice was "not
spreading into unoccupied territories." The Yayoi "package" (rice + bronze and
iron metallurgy + weaving) spread out of Kyushu only after several centuries,
through western then eastern Honshu (not Hokkaido), and carried the **Japonic
language** (precursor of Japanese).

## Consequences: population, violence, disease, state

Once a real commitment to paddy rice developed, it **sustained rapid population
growth**: by the start of the Common Era there were villages over 100 ha with
perhaps 1,500 residents, fed by extensive canal-irrigated paddy systems managed
by elite groups in separate precincts. The Yayoi sees:
- the **first widespread evidence for inter-communal violence** in Japan;
- via waterlogged cesspit residues ("toilet archaeology"), a marked **increase in
  the diversity of disease and intestinal parasites** (cf.
  [[agriculture-and-human-health]]);
- the rise of **regional hierarchical societies** whose elites legitimated power
  through ritual drawing on agriculture, hunting, and warfare — leading toward the
  Kofun period and early Japanese state. By the 5th c. CE, central authorities
  undertook large-scale land development.

Domestic animals stayed marginal: horse and cattle were used for **traction**,
not meat or milk (no [[secondary-products-revolution]] dairying); pigs
contributed little. Hunting, gathering, and fishing continued throughout.

## Case study: Nara basin paddies (early Yayoi)

Okada's case study (Ch. 15) is the remarkable system of **early Yayoi paddy
fields** excavated ahead of motorway/development work in the **Nara basin**
(e.g. Nakanishi-Akitsu, Kawanishi-Negarigaki) — showing the physical transition
from final-Jomon settlement to early-Yayoi paddy landscapes, with plans,
sections, and calibrated radiocarbon dates documenting the wholesale
**transformation of wild landscape into engineered, irrigated paddy** at the
dawn of Japanese rice farming.

## End Conditions

By ~500 CE (Kofun period) paddy intensification and centralized land development
had reshaped much of the archipelago, feeding into the formation of the early
Japanese state. Rice remains central to Japanese identity, ritual, and the
*satoyama* landscape ideal.

## Note (Mason & Caiger)

Mason and Caiger's [[mason-caiger-history-of-japan|history]] stress that **Jōmon
pottery** (rope-marked) was made **before 10,000 BCE** — among the earliest in the world —
by gatherer-hunter-fishers who practiced rudimentary cultivation from ~5000 BCE. The
**Yayoi** transformation (wet rice, bronze and iron, weaving, the potter's wheel) spread
rapidly west-to-east after ~500 BCE, probably via continental migrants, enabling denser
populations, permanent villages, and the first **social stratification** — vividly shown
by the large defended Yayoi settlement excavated at **Yoshinogari** in northern Kyūshū.
This is the substrate from which [[yamato-japan|Yamato]] and the Japanese state arose.

## The Yayoi transition (Cambridge History of Japan)

The [[cambridge-history-japan-v1-ancient|Cambridge History]] (Kidder, ch. 1) adds
archaeological depth. **Wet-rice agriculture** is traced not merely to Korea but ultimately
to the **Yangtze delta** (the Wu/Yue region) of the temperate zone — some Japanese
vocabulary has been linked to that area's speech — entering north Kyūshū (the threshold site
of **Itazuke**) and spreading **up the Inland Sea** to the Kinki and Tōkai and on to the
Kantō/Tōhoku, reaching northern Tōhoku before the period closed; the spread is dated by
imported **Han Chinese bronze mirrors**. The hallmark of Yayoi culture was the **storage
pot** and the raised granary (against damp and field-mice). Crucially, current excavation
has **revised** the old view that Jōmon and Yayoi peoples occupied separate environments:
large sites (e.g. Kusakari, Higuchi) contain **both** Jōmon and Yayoi dwellings, indicating
coexistence and gradual transition rather than simple replacement.
