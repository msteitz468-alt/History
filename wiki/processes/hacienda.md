# The Hacienda

The **hacienda** was the great landed estate that became the characteristic rural
institution of [[colonial-latin-america|colonial Latin America]] — the agrarian
counterpart of the [[potosi-and-colonial-silver|mine]] and the
[[atlantic-slave-trade|plantation]], and the base of a **landed elite** that
dominated the countryside into the twentieth century. Unlike the
[[encomienda]] (a grant of *people*), the hacienda was a holding of **land**,
worked by a mix of resident and seasonal labor and producing food, livestock, and
cash crops for urban, mining, and (sometimes) export markets.

## Definition and Scope

The classic account is François **Chevalier**'s *Land and Society in Colonial
Mexico: The Great Hacienda* (1963), which depicted the hacienda as a vast,
semi-feudal, partly self-sufficient seigneurial domain. Later scholarship qualified
this: many haciendas were **market-oriented businesses**, their owners diversifying
across ranching, farming, and commerce to hedge the boom-and-bust cycles of the
colonial economy. The hacienda's scale and self-sufficiency varied enormously by
region (the wheat-and-livestock estates of the Mexican Bajío and the Río de la
Plata; the Andean estates absorbing *yanaconas*; the grazing latifundia of the
north).

## Origins: from encomienda to estate

The hacienda did **not** simply descend from the [[encomienda]]. As the
[[virgin-soil-epidemics|demographic collapse]] emptied the land and the encomienda
declined (after the New Laws of 1542), Spaniards acquired land through royal grants,
purchase, and the **congregación/reducción** resettlements that freed up native
fields. With the indigenous population shrinking and urban/mining demand for wheat,
meat, wine, and sugar rising, **land** — not tribute rights — became the road to
rural wealth. The great estate thus arose alongside, and partly replaced, the
encomienda as the basis of elite power.

## Labor

Haciendas drew on the full [[encomienda|colonial labor repertoire]]: early
**repartimiento/mita** drafts, then increasingly **free wage labor** bound by
**debt peonage** — advances of wages or goods that tied workers to the estate
(in Chile taking the form of ***inquilinaje***, land-loans in exchange for service;
in the Andes resident **yanaconas** transferred *with* the estate when sold).
Burkholder and Johnson stress that "wage earners may have been free, but a true
labor market seldom existed": powerful landowners held wages artificially low and
used credit to immobilize labor.

## The symbiosis with the free rural poor

A central insight: great estates "existed **symbiotically** with populations of
impoverished free farmers and tenants." Because the heavy labor of planting and
harvest was followed by slack months, it was uneconomic to own a slave or peon
workforce large enough for peak demand. So haciendas (and plantations) relied on
**poor free families** — subsistence farmers and tenants squeezed onto the worst,
surrounding land — who worked their own plots and then sold their labor for the
harvest. The harvest wage carried the poor through hard months; the estate avoided
high fixed costs. It was interdependence, but never partnership: the rural poor were
always subordinate. Small producers (the Brazilian *lavradores de cana*; uncapitalized
wheat farmers forced to sell their crop cheap before harvest; frontier cattlemen on
untitled *tierras baldías*) were routinely squeezed by capitalized neighbors with
ties to the courts.

## Interaction and End Conditions

The hacienda was the **rural face of the [[casta-system|casta hierarchy]]** — the
place where the correlation of race and class was tightest, far from the more fluid
cities. It fed the colonial cities and mines, anchored elite family fortunes (the
preferred refuge of capital when sugar or silver faltered), and shaped land tenure
for centuries. It long outlived the colonial era, becoming the target of the great
twentieth-century land reforms (Mexico 1910s, Bolivia 1952, etc.).
