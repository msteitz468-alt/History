# Unification of the Scandinavian Kingdoms

## Definition and Scope

The long transformation of fragmented chieftaincies and petty kingdoms into the
three consolidated realms of [[denmark|Denmark]], [[norway|Norway]], and
[[sweden|Sweden]], each with a single monarchy, an organised church, and
eventually a nationally framed system of government. Closely bound to
[[christianization-of-scandinavia|Christianization]].

## Causal Drivers

Military and fiscal resources released by the [[viking-age|Viking expansion]];
the *leidang* naval levy; Christian kingship as a unifying ideology; and the
gradual subordination of regional assemblies and provincial laws to royal power.

## Major Phases

- **Denmark:** earliest and most centralised; the [[harald-bluetooth|Jelling
  kings]] (10th c.) claim to have "won all Denmark"; consolidated under the
  Valdemars (12th c.).
- **Norway:** [[harald-fairhair|Harald Fairhair]]'s traditional unification
  (c. 900); contested through the 11th–12th c.; durable national monarchy and
  unified law under Hákon Hákonarson and Magnus the Lawmender (13th c.).
- **Sweden:** latest; welding of Götar and Svear; stabilised under the
  [[folkung-dynasty|Folkungs]] (Bjälbo dynasty) in the 13th c.

## Geographic Spread

From core regions (Jutland/the Danish isles; the Trøndelag and western Norway;
the Mälaren basin and Götaland) outward over provinces, the North Atlantic
tributaries, and [[finland|Finland]].

## Interaction

Mutually reinforcing with [[christianization-of-scandinavia|Christianization]],
[[scandinavian-urbanisation|urbanisation]], and the growth of royal and
ecclesiastical administration; the consolidated kingdoms made the later
[[kalmar-union-1397|Kalmar Union]] possible.

## End Conditions

By c. 1300 three stable kingdoms existed with nationally organised government;
the process transformed into the dynamics of dynastic union and aristocratic
constitutionalism. See [[helle-cambridge-history-scandinavia-v1-2003]].
