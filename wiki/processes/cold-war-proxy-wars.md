## Definition and Scope

Alongside direct superpower confrontation, the [[cold-war|Cold War]] generated a long series of secondary or "surrogate" wars fought across the decolonizing world, in which the United States and Soviet Union (and, in places, Britain, France, China, and Cuba) backed opposing local factions rather than fighting one another directly. Alfred McCoy, in *[[mccoy-cold-war-five-continents-2024|Cold War on Five Continents]]* (2024), estimates these wars killed roughly twenty million people between 1945 and 1990, and — attributed to McCoy — characterizes the Cold War on this basis as "the world's bloodiest conflict after World War II." This scale claim, along with McCoy's broader interpretive framing of these wars as expressions of a superpower "covert empire" contest, is McCoy's own synthesis rather than settled historiographical consensus; see [[mccoy-covert-empire-thesis]].

This process is distinct from, though closely linked to, [[decolonization]]: proxy conflicts frequently erupted at or after the moment of formal independence, as departing colonial or newly sovereign states became arenas for superpower competition.

## Causal Drivers

- **Nuclear stalemate** — the mutual deterrence produced by thermonuclear arsenals (US and Soviet stockpiles reaching tens of thousands of warheads by 1991) foreclosed direct superpower war, redirecting competition into peripheral, covert, and proxy arenas.
- **[[containment]] doctrine** — NSC-68 (1950) and the global US alliance/basing network (NATO, METO, SEATO, bilateral Pacific treaties) committed the US to resisting Soviet-aligned expansion by whatever local means were available short of general war.
- **Decolonization** — the collapse of European colonial empires (1946–1994) created new, often unstable, states whose internal power struggles became superpower proxy battlegrounds.
- **[[covert-action]] doctrine** — NSC 10/2 (1948) institutionalized covert operations as a standing instrument, giving the US (and comparable Soviet, British, and French services) a mechanism for intervention below the threshold of overt war.

## Major Phases

McCoy periodizes the proxy-war landscape into three broad phases:
1. **1946–1962, Europe primary** — Soviet consolidation of Eastern European satellite states against CIA covert intervention and military alliance-building in Western Europe (e.g., the 1948 Italian election).
2. **Early 1960s onward, Southeast Asia primary** — Indonesia (psychological warfare and the 1965–66 killings, see [[indonesian-mass-killings-1965]]), the conventional [[vietnam-war|Vietnam War]], and the air wars over Laos and Cambodia.
3. **1975–1991, dispersed theatres** — following the conventional US defeat in Vietnam, CIA covert operations shifted to Central America (see [[central-america-cold-war]]), Central Asia (the [[soviet-war-in-afghanistan-1979|Soviet war in Afghanistan]]), and southern Africa (see [[angolan-civil-war-1975]] and [[southern-africa-cold-war]]).

## Geographic Spread

Documented theatres include Korea, Vietnam, Laos, Cambodia, Indonesia, Guatemala, Chile, Cuba, the Congo, Angola, and Afghanistan, alongside covert political action in Western Europe and Iran. The Angolan civil war alone (1975–2002, in McCoy's account) drew in Cuban, South African, Soviet, and Chinese intervention on top of US and Portuguese involvement, killing an estimated 800,000 people and displacing four million.

## Interaction with Other Processes

Proxy warfare interacted closely with [[decolonization]] (many proxy wars began as independence struggles or immediately followed independence), with [[counterinsurgency]] doctrine (Kennedy-era programs modeled partly on French colonial counterinsurgency in Algeria), and with [[covert-action]] as the primary operational toolkit through which superpowers intervened without formal declarations of war.

## End Conditions

The process wound down with the end of the Cold War itself (1989–1991): negotiated settlements in southern Africa (the 1988 New York Accord ending South African and Cuban involvement in Angola, followed by Namibian independence in 1990), Soviet withdrawal from Afghanistan (1989), and the broader collapse of Soviet sponsorship of allied movements after the [[soviet-collapse-1990-1991|Soviet collapse]]. Several conflicts that began as Cold War proxy wars (notably Angola) continued for years afterward on local momentum, indicating the process's local dynamics had by then outlived its superpower origins.
