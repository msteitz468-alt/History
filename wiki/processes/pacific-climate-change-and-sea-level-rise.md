## Definition and Scope

Pacific climate change and sea-level rise denotes the accelerating environmental process by which anthropogenic global warming elevates ocean temperatures and sea levels, with catastrophic disproportionate impact on low-lying coral atoll nations. As Hilda Heine, former President of the Republic of the Marshall Islands, states: "Our atolls and islands total 70 square miles in land area surrounded by 750,000 square miles of the Pacific Ocean… no more than a few feet above the sea level." The process is existential for nations like the Marshall Islands (Aelon Kein Ad), Kiribati, Tuvalu, and the Maldives: states that contribute negligibly to global emissions but face total territorial submersion. The Marshall Islands case is distinctive because it compounds climate threat with a pre-existing nuclear testing legacy, making it — in Heine's words — "the only country in the world where the impacts of both the climate crises and the US nuclear weapons testing programme are intersecting to further threaten our way of life."

## Causal Drivers

Global industrial carbon emissions are the primary driver; the Marshall Islands and comparable atoll nations bear no meaningful causal responsibility for them. Contributing factors specific to the Marshall Islands include the US nuclear testing programme of 1946–1958 (sixty-seven bombs; the largest, Castle Bravo on Bikini Atoll on 1 March 1954, was "equivalent to dropping 1.6 Hiroshima bombs daily for twelve years"), which degraded atoll environments and displaced populations before climate effects became significant. The Runit Dome on Enewetak Atoll — storing approximately 111,000 cubic yards of radioactive waste, completed 1979 — is now cracking and leaching radioactive material into the ocean as sea levels rise, creating a compound hazard. A 2013 US Department of Energy report confirmed that soil around the Dome was already more contaminated than its contents.

## Major Phases

**1946–1958 — Nuclear compounding.** US atomic and thermonuclear testing at Bikini and Enewetak atolls displaces Marshallese communities and contaminates land, sea, and food chains. The Castle Bravo test (1954) vaporized entire islands and rained radioactive ash on Rongelap Atoll 100 miles away.

**1979–2000s — Recognition and early response.** Runit Dome constructed (1979); scientific consensus on anthropogenic climate change hardens; atoll nations begin international advocacy. The Convention on Biological Diversity (2004) sets a 10 percent marine conservation target; Micronesia pledged 30 percent of nearshore marine and 20 percent of terrestrial resources.

**2010s–present — Diplomatic escalation and cultural activism.** Pacific Island Forum leaders affirm climate change as "the single greatest threat to our regional security." The Marshall Islands Parliament (Nitijela) passes a national climate crisis resolution. The country develops a National Adaptation Plan — formally termed a "National Survival Plan." The Reimaanlok eight-step Conservation Area Management Planning Framework is deployed in atoll communities to fuse traditional ecological knowledge with modern science. At the international level, Heine and her daughter Kathy Jetñil-Kijiner become recognized voices: Jetñil-Kijiner performed her poem "Dear Matafele Peinam" at the opening of the 2014 United Nations Climate Summit, gaining worldwide attention. Jo-Jikum, the nonprofit Jetñil-Kijiner founded for Marshallese youth and environmental issues, and her role as Climate Envoy for the Marshall Islands Ministry of Environment represent institutionalization of grassroots activism.

## Geographic Spread

The process affects the entire low-lying tropical Pacific with particular severity: Marshall Islands, Kiribati, Tuvalu, Cook Islands, Tokelau, and Maldives. Within the Marshall Islands, the dual Ratak (Sunrise) and Ralik (Sunset) chains — comprising 1,225 individual islands and islets — are all at risk. Outer islands face king tides inundating homes and droughts threatening food and water; seawalls and water catchments have been constructed as adaptive infrastructure. The Banaba case (in neighboring Kiribati) establishes an earlier historical template: phosphate mining destruction of that island "presaging the destruction of Gilbertese spaces through global sea level rise," making Banaban historical experience "emblematic of the wider processes of environmental and sociopolitical change that have threatened and are threatening the survival of places and cultures across the Pacific" (Mayo, ch. 33).

The process interacts with the long history of Pacific Islander relationships to land and sea documented across Oceanian cultures. For Marshallese, land is not merely economic but constitutive of identity and social order: "Our identities are intricately tied to our land, so much so that the RMI Government owns less than 1 per cent of land. Our culture and livelihood as a people are intricately connected to our ancestral lands" (Heine). Loss of land therefore threatens not only physical survival but the entire Ri-Majol cultural framework — the bwebwenato (oral storytelling traditions), the navigational knowledge, the community reciprocity structures, the manit (cultural practices). Traditional ecological knowledge (reef management, seasonal fishing methods, navigation by stars) is simultaneously imperiled and invoked as adaptive resource.

## End Conditions

The process has no foreseeable end under current global emissions trajectories. Mitigation depends on global decarbonization consistent with the Paris Agreement; adaptation measures (seawalls, island-raising engineering, water catchments, managed migration) may delay but cannot permanently counter sustained sea-level rise. Potential outcomes range from partial preservation through radical engineering to the scenario Heine encapsulates as "sink or swim." The Kimberley Declaration (2002) invoked by Heine — "we walk to the future in the footprints of our ancestors" — frames the cultural stakes: "If the footprints are destroyed, then we have no future."

## Historiography

Ch. 32 of [[hattori-samson-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v2-2023]] (Heine and Jetñil-Kijiner) is unusual in the scholarly genre: it presents a keynote address by a sitting head of state and a poem by her daughter rather than conventional academic prose. This deliberately blends Indigenous political voice with academic discourse, embodying the chapter's argument that traditional Marshallese knowledge (Reimaanlok, bwebwenato) and modern science must be held together rather than subordinated one to the other. The authors explicitly critique the Western scientific framework's tendency to treat Indigenous peoples as research subjects rather than knowledge producers: "The scientific result of any research must recognize this cultural perspective, otherwise it will be purely based upon a Western, scientific view of Indigenous peoples and our traditional knowledge and will not be representative of our story." The chapter contributes to a broader Pacific studies literature that positions climate change as inseparable from colonial and nuclear legacies, rejecting frameworks that treat these as independent crises.
