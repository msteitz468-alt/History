# The National Revolution (Révolution nationale), 1940–1944

[[vichy-france]]'s program of internal regeneration — "Travail, Famille,
Patrie" replacing "Liberté, Égalité, Fraternité." Paxton's central claim:
"The National Revolution was not Hitler's project. It was not 'imported on
German tanks'… I have been unable to turn up any direct German order for
French anti-Masonic, anti-Semitic, or other legislation during the most
active period of Vichy legislation in 1940… The National Revolution tells us
about France, not Germany." It was the program of the losers of the 1930s —
"the expression of indigenous French urges for change, reform, and revenge,
nurtured in the 1930's and made urgent and possible by defeat."

## Definition and scope

Not a bloc but a coalition of competing visions — integral-Catholic vs.
pagan-nationalist moral order; federal vs. Napoleonic state; communal vs.
organized-capitalist economy; persuasion vs. coercion — whose history is "a
history of the gradual gathering around one set of poles: integral
Catholicism, Napoleonic centralism, more concentrated capitalism, and
coercion." Its common fuel was the interwar discourse of *decadence*
(Girardet: fin-de-siècle nationalism as "a meditation upon decadence";
Valéry's May 1940 "stock market report on the values of civilization") and
antibolshevism, "the nearest thing to a Vichy common denominator."

## Program areas

- **Moral order**: favors to the Church, school reform, natalist family
  policy, youth movements (Chantiers de Jeunesse), anti-alcohol laws (some
  still in force), campaigns against "swing" and Gide.
- **"France for the French"**: denaturalizations, the anti-Masonic laws
  (13 August 1940), and the anti-Jewish statutes — see
  [[vichy-anti-jewish-laws-1940]].
- **Corporatism**: the Labor Charter and Organization Committees, which
  traditionalist anticapitalists intended as artisan community and "craftier
  businessmen quickly turned to privileged cartelization."
- **Return to the soil**: failed entirely against the pull of urbanization.
- **The police state**: from persuasion to constraint after the June–August
  1941 turning point (Resistance assassinations, German hostage massacres,
  Special Sections, the Tribunal d'État) — Monzie: "The National Revolution
  has boiled down to the social advancement of the gendarmerie."

## Was it fascism?

Paxton's ideal-type analysis (the seed of his later *Anatomy of Fascism*):
fascism is "a mass antiliberal, anticommunist movement, radical in its
willingness to employ force and in its contempt for the upper-class values of
the time, sharply distinct not only from its enemies on the left but also
from its rivals on the right, traditional conservatives" — conservative
hierarchy vs. the fascist rallies' "leveling egalitarianism before the
leader." Since "no undiluted fascist regime has wielded power," he ranks
radical-right regimes on a spectrum from fascist-dominated (Nazi Germany) to
conservative-dominated (Salazar): "The Vichy National Revolution clearly
occupied a place on such a spectrum nearer the conservative than the fascist
end. Pétain felt himself closer to Franco and Salazar than to Hitler." Yet it
was no ancien-régime restoration either — "a heresy of Third Republic liberal
and progressive doctrines," Enlightenment social harmony and positivist
technocracy with the parliamentary faith burned out. His closing double
verdict: "Hard measures by a frightened middle class — that, indeed, is one
good general definition of fascism. In that broader sense, Vichy was fascist.
And in that sense, fascism has not yet run its course." Both halves of this
verdict are cited in [[fascism-and-the-political-left]].

## End conditions

The war it depended on escaping never ended. From 1941 coercion displaced
persuasion; after November 1942 the regime was hollow; by 1944 its
traditionalists had long lost to the technocrats, whose planning apparatus —
the National Revolution's most durable product — passed almost intact into
the Fourth Republic (see [[vichy-france]], Legacy).

## From Paxton and Hessler, *Europe in the Twentieth Century* (2011)

Vichy's **divided legitimacy** was unique in occupied Europe. Where the Dutch, Norwegian, and Belgian governments-in-exile preserved unified national legitimacy abroad — and Belgium's King Leopold III chose to remain in the country as a prisoner of the Germans rather than head a rival collaborationist authority, while Denmark's King Christian X made his daily rides through occupied Copenhagen a quiet, visible act of national continuity — France instead split into a legally continuous government at Vichy and a rival claim to legitimacy from de Gaulle's Free French abroad, with no clean resolution until the Liberation.

Vichy abolished parliament and independent trade unions outright, enacted the anti-Semitic legislation covered above, and built a corporatist economic order in practice run by big-company executives rather than the small-producer and artisan classes the National Revolution's rhetoric claimed to champion. The Montoire meeting of 24 October 1940, at which Pétain met Hitler and announced France's entry "into the way of collaboration," anchored this program diplomatically. Postwar polling and estimates suggest only about 2% of French adults were engaged in active resistance and roughly 10% read the clandestine press regularly — while at least as many French people remained supportive of Pétain through to the end of the occupation, complicating any simple resistance-versus-collaboration national narrative. Occupation costs consumed some 58% of French tax receipts. Source: [[paxton-europe-twentieth-century-2011]].
