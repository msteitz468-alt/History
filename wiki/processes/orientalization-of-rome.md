# The Orientalization of Rome

**"The orientalization of Rome"** is the interpretive thesis of Warwick Ball's
*[[ball-rome-in-the-east-2000|Rome in the East]]* (2000): that the deep historical
current running through Rome's seven centuries in the Near East flowed **east-to-west** —
that Rome was progressively transformed *by* the older, more continuous civilisation of the
Near East rather than transforming it, and that the Graeco-Roman cultural presence in the
region was, in Ball's word, a "veneer." This page records the process **as Ball argues it**.
It is a distinctive and contested reading; the scholarly debate it provokes is filed
separately at [[orientalization-of-rome-thesis]].

> **A note on terms.** Ball's "orientalization" is *not* [[orientalism|Edward Said's
> "Orientalism"]] (a Western discourse *about* the East). Ball uses the word for a claimed
> real historical process — the movement of eastern peoples, forms, cults and ideas into the
> Roman world and their eventual dominance within it. The label is Ball's own etic framing.

## Definition and Scope

What the thesis **is**: an argument that beneath the colonnaded streets, Corinthian orders and
Latin inscriptions of the Roman Near East, the population, cult identity, architectural logic
and post-Roman resurgence remained Semitic, Arab and Iranian; and that this native substrate
did not merely survive Roman rule but reached back out to reshape Rome itself.

What it is **not**: a claim that Rome had no impact, or that the East was culturally static.
Ball concedes deep change (Christianisation above all); his claim is about the *direction* of
the deeper influence and the *superficiality* of Romanisation as against Near Eastern continuity.

Ball's method is consistent across the book: take a city or form with the outward "trappings"
of Graeco-Roman urbanism and argue the substance is native. Applied to
[[dura-europos|Dura Europos]] (his model case — a Macedonian colony that became "practically
indistinguishable from the Near Eastern environment"), [[gerasa|Jerash]] ("a veneer that hides
an essentially oriental city"), [[bosra|Bosra]] (a "Roman veneer over Nabataean substance"),
the [[decapolis|Decapolis]], [[petra|Petra]], and [[baalbek|Baalbek]].

## Causal Drivers

- **The client-kingdom system.** Rome ruled the Near East largely through native "Friendly
  King" states — [[emesene-dynasty|Emesa]], Judaea, [[nabataean-kingdom|Nabataea]],
  [[palmyra|Palmyra]], [[kingdom-of-edessa|Edessa]], later the [[tanukhids|Tanukhid]] and
  [[ghassanids|Ghassanid]] foederati — leaving native dynasties, cults and languages intact
  beneath a thin Roman superstructure. Ball likens it to the British Raj's Princely States.
- **Trade and migration.** [[roman-eastern-trade|Eastern trade]] and the westward movement of
  Syrian, Phoenician and Arab merchant communities (attested from Ostia and Puteoli to Lyons,
  Trier and South Shields) carried eastern cults and personnel deep into the West.
- **The rise of eastern emperors.** The [[severan-dynasty|Severan dynasty]] — through
  [[julia-domna|Julia Domna]] and her Emesene family — put Syrians on the throne;
  [[elagabalus|Elagabalus]] briefly installed the Emesene sun-god at Rome; [[philip-the-arab|
  Philip the Arab]] presided over Rome's own Millennium.
- **Eastern religion.** Sun-worship (Elagabal → Aurelian's Sol Invictus), Mithraism, and finally
  [[christianization-of-the-roman-empire|Christianity]] — all, in Ball's account, eastern imports.

## Major Phases

1. **Conquest and proxy rule (64 BCE – 2nd c. CE).** Pompey annexes the Seleucid state; the
   Near East is governed through client kingdoms; native monumental building (Jerusalem,
   [[petra|Petra]], Palmyra) is the work of these states, not of Rome directly.
2. **The eastern emperors (193–249 CE).** The Severans; Elagabalus's sun-cult experiment;
   Philip the Arab. Ball's "Severan Revolution": at [[lepcis-magna|Lepcis Magna]] Septimius
   Severus builds, in Ball's reading, "a conscious oriental transplant in the West."
3. **The religious revolution (3rd–4th c. CE).** Aurelian's state Temple of the Sun; Constantine's
   Sol Invictus and then Christianity; the move of the capital to Constantinople — Rome becoming,
   in Ball's phrase, "more of an Asian than a European power."
4. **The Arab culmination (5th–7th c. CE).** The Ghassanid phylarchate and an increasingly "Arab"
   Monophysite Christianity; then the Islamic conquest, which Ball frames as the last act in a
   millennia-old tradition of Near Eastern expansion into Europe, not its first.

## Geographic Spread

From the Levantine and Mesopotamian heartland outward: into Anatolia and the Aegean (Sol/Sun,
Magi communities, the cult of the Virgin at Ephesus); into Italy and the western provinces
(merchant colonies, army cult-shrines to Jupiter Dolichenus and Heliopolitanus as far as
Hadrian's Wall); into North Africa (Lepcis Magna; the Phoenician tophet and
its claimed priming of North African Christianity); and ultimately into Spain and southern
Europe via the 8th-century Arab conquests.

## Interaction

The process runs alongside and partly drives [[christianization-of-the-roman-empire|the
Christianisation of the Roman Empire]]; it presupposes the [[hellenization-of-rome|earlier
Hellenisation of Rome]] (which Ball would say the Graeco-Roman reading over-extends into the
East); and it stands against the mainstream Romanisation/Hellenisation model of the Roman Near
East associated with Fergus Millar. It contrasts with, and should not be conflated with, the
Archaic-Greek [[orientalizing-revolution|Orientalizing Revolution]] (Near Eastern influence on
early Greece, a separate and earlier phenomenon).

## End Conditions

In Ball's narrative the process does not "end" so much as complete itself: the eastern religion
triumphs, the capital moves east, the western empire is "ditched to the barbarians," and the
Roman Empire continues as an eastern, then increasingly Greek, then finally (1453) a
Near-Eastern-conquered state. The Islamic conquest of the 7th century marks the point at which
the East's clients — in Ball's closing phrase for the Ghassanids — "had become its rivals."
