## Definition and Scope

Germany's relationship to European integration since 1945 is one of the defining threads of postwar European history. Uniquely among European states, West Germany regained sovereignty precisely by surrendering it — membership in NATO and the EEC restored international legitimacy while embedding a potentially dangerous Germany in institutional constraints. The process moved through several distinct phases: the foundational Westbindung of the Adenauer era; the more self-confident Ostpolitik-era engagement; the deepening post-1990 commitment culminating in Maastricht and monetary union; and the increasingly assertive, occasionally skeptical role of the early 21st century.

## Phase 1: Dividing Germany, Making Europe (1945–1968)

The postwar moment posed a fundamental problem: how to embed Germany in European order so it could neither launch nor be the occasion for another world war. Three early initiatives set the framework: the Brussels Treaty (1948, military co-operation), the Council of Europe (1949), and — crucially — the Marshall Plan and OEEC, which treated the western German zones as legitimate economic partners from the outset. American pressure was decisive: Washington insisted on German economic participation as essential to European recovery.

The key breakthrough was the **Schuman Plan (1950)**, which proposed pooling French and German coal and steel production under supranational authority. This led to the European Coal and Steel Community (ECSC, 1951) — the first supranational organization with binding authority, and the foundation of Franco-German reconciliation. [[konrad-adenauer]] was its critical German champion, seeing Westbindung as both the essential defense against Soviet aggression and a bulwark against Germany's own violent nationalist past. The formula: Germany recovered sovereignty by surrendering it.

The European Defence Community (EDC) failed in 1954 when the French National Assembly vetoed it. But the 1955 package deal gave West Germany NATO membership and sovereignty (General Treaty) simultaneously. The Treaties of Rome (1957) created the EEC and Euratom, extending economic integration

Domestically, European integration in the 1950s was largely an elite project. Opinion polls in 1957 showed that only 49% of West Germans had heard of "the Common Market" and only 17% could explain it. The SPD initially opposed the EEC as a kleineruopäisch (small-Europe) Catholic-conservative cartel project, only accepting the Treaties of Rome (the first major integration step the SPD supported). Most Germans prioritized unification over integration. "Europe" was built by "elderly statesmen, well-versed in power politics," not popular enthusiasm.

## Phase 2: West Germany as Post-National Democracy (1968–1990)

By the late 1960s, the FRG was more active and self-confident in European affairs. Key changes: the removal of EEC internal customs duties (1968), the Hague summit (1969) opening EC enlargement and new policies, and the transition from Adenauer's Westbindung to Brandt's more active role. The FRG pushed for EC enlargement after de Gaulle's resignation (which had blocked British entry), and took an active role in monetary policy coordination.

Chancellor Helmut Schmidt and French President Giscard d'Estaing created the **European Monetary System (EMS, 1979)**, linking European currencies to prevent large fluctuations. This was a significant German concession: the DM was the anchor currency, and the Bundesbank's concern about inflationary contagion was subordinated to political calculation.

A broader shift: West Germany increasingly identified itself as a "post-national democracy amongst nation states" (Dolf Sternberger's phrase). By the 1980s, 76% of West Germans still hoped for reunification but believed they would not see it in their lifetimes. The younger generation felt little national attachment to East Germans. "European" and "Western" identity displaced classical nationalism — not through enthusiasm for EU institutions (Euro-skepticism was already present), but through a cultural cosmopolitanism that made aggressive nationalism socially illegitimate.

The CSCE (Helsinki Accords, 1975) added a pan-European dimension beyond the EC, and the civil rights provisions proved more destabilizing to Eastern bloc regimes than the border guarantees were stabilizing — an outcome critical to understanding 1989.

The Single European Act (1986) deepened the single market — a direction more in line with West Germany's export-oriented economic interests than the original EEC's protectionist bent.

## Phase 3: Unification, Maastricht, and the Contemporary EU (1990–present)

German unification was inextricably linked to deeper European integration. France (above all) insisted on monetary union as the price of accepting a unified Germany. In February 1992, twelve member states signed the **Maastricht Treaty**, creating the European Union and committing to a common currency by 1999. Germany gave up the DM — its ersatz national symbol — as the European price of unification. Chancellor [[helmut-kohl]] supported Maastricht unambiguously, believing a united Germany must be anchored deeply in European institutions.

Reunification also expanded Germany's weight in the EC: it now accounted for 30% of EC GDP and 23% of population. The 16 million former East Germans entered the Community without special treaty — simply by virtue of GDR accession to the FRG.

Post-Maastricht, Germany's European stance became more complex. Chancellor Schröder (1998–2005) was more willing to invoke German "national interests" than his predecessors. The German Constitutional Court's 1993 ruling on Maastricht (confirmed in 2009 re: Lisbon Treaty) insisted that the EU could not extend its jurisdiction without member-state consent — increasingly asserting national sovereignty against federalist expansion. Angela Merkel's government during the 2010 Euro crisis was criticized by partners as "close-fisted, short-sighted, and egoistic."

By the late 2000s, the "permissive consensus" that had supported European integration in Germany had eroded: Eurobarometer (2008) showed 58% of Germans felt their voice did not count in Europe; only 48% had a positive image of the EU, compared to ~80% in the 1970s–1980s.

## Germany's Distinctive European Position

Several features distinguish Germany's role:
- **Sovereignty through surrender:** The paradox that Germany regained international legitimacy and influence precisely by embedding itself in multilateral structures is unique. No other major power had this incentive structure.
- **Absence of Euroskepticism:** Unlike the UK, France, and other states, Germany through 2010 lacked a significant Euroskeptic political formation — partially explained by the country's past, partially by the fact that all major European decisions passed through parliament (no referenda, which blocked populist mobilization).
- **Franco-German engine:** Germany consistently subordinated its formal economic weight to the political framework of Franco-German partnership. It was the "economic Gulliver" that "voluntarily kept its shackles."
- **Post-national identity:** The FRG developed a Verfassungspatriotismus (constitutional patriotism, Habermas) in place of ethnic nationalism — an attachment to democratic constitutional values rather than ethnic or cultural Germanness. This post-national self-understanding was distinctively pro-European.

## Historiography

Key historiographical debates: (1) Alan Milward's argument that European integration was the "rescue of the nation state" — states used integration to reinforce their domestic authority and legitimacy, not to transcend it. (2) The debate over German European hegemony — was Germany a "tamed power" (Katzenstein) or a nascent European hegemon? (3) The question of popular vs. elite integration — how genuine was public support versus elite consensus?
