## Definition and Scope

The Great Transition denotes the protracted transformation of the German-speaking territories from a regionally diverse agrarian-corporate economy into an industrial-capitalist order between approximately 1750 and 1860. The process was not a clean break: Friedrich Engels observed in 1847 that Germany presented "the simultaneity of several economies," with nobles, serfs, petty bourgeoisie, journeymen, manufactory workers, and proletarians existing "alongside one another." James M. Brophy (ch. 8 of [[smith-oxford-modern-german-history-2011]]) argues against narratives of dramatic rupture: "The period 1750–1860 presents both varied and hybrid economic worlds."

Three key premises structure the chapter's analysis:
1. Economic change remained **highly uneven** — in 1860 Germany was still more rural than urban.
2. Regional variations correspond **poorly with political frontiers** — Rhineland textiles connect to Belgian/French industrialization; East-Elbian agrarian capitalism links to East European grain markets; Hanseatic merchants are networked with Amsterdam, London, and Baltimore.
3. The emerging economy did **not prefigure** the 1871 nation-state; German economies participated as European components of world markets.

## Causal Drivers

### Agrarian Transformation
Two systems of peasant servitude defined early-modern Germany: **Gutsherrschaft** (East-Elbian manorial system, serfdom, labor services, patrimonial justice) and **Grundherrschaft** (western land-tenancy system with money rents, absent lords, greater de facto peasant autonomy). By the late eighteenth century, neither system was economically efficient by western European standards: Prussian East yields 57 bushels per acre against the Rhineland's 175.

Napoleon's defeats forced reform. Prussia's **October Edict of 1807** abolished serfdom and enabled non-aristocratic land purchase; further decrees (1811, 1816) set emancipation terms. Settlements overwhelmingly favored the Junker class, which by mid-century repossessed over three million acres of peasant land and 86 percent of all common land (fifteen million acres). Despite these inequities, peasant farmers survived and increased in number. Outside Prussia, the reform process was "dilatory and fitful": in Hesse, Württemberg, Nassau, and Baden, serfdom was not fully abolished until the [[revolutions-of-1848]].

Between 1800 and 1870, agrarian production rose 174 percent, exceeding the 66 percent population rise. Agriculture attracted 58 percent of Zollverein investment in 1830–1840, compared to industry's 3 percent — though this investment funded capital-intensive agrarian capitalism oriented toward European export markets, not subsistence.

### Proto-Industrialization
Franklin Mendels's concept of "proto-industry" (1972) identifies rural domestic manufactures as a structural precondition for industrialization: they expanded commodity markets, mobilized rural labor, and generated merchant capital. By 1800, the German territories showed 39 distinct areas of industrial concentration (Kaufhold). Key examples:
- Duchy of Berg: 40,000 metal workers producing swords, scissors, and textiles for export markets
- Silesian linen: for Atlantic and East India markets via Vienna entrepôt ("Oberlausitz linen wares … became an essential part of the flowing colonial trade" — Jean Quataert)
- Wupper valley (Elberfeld-Barmen): 33,000 workers in textiles by 1767
- Chemnitz region: 15,000 rural spinners within four miles of city by 1799

Subsequent scholars have qualified the proto-industrialization model: it does not explain all paths to industrial growth, and the link between domestic industry and family formation is "increasingly tenuous." Jan de Vries's concept of the "industrious revolution" — altered consumer behavior driving greater market participation — supplements it.

The **Silesian weavers' crisis of 1844** illustrates the fragility of proto-industrial livelihoods: when Silesian linen workers starved, "the larger explanation involved a worldwide shift toward cotton, English mechanized spinning and weaving, and the slow response of German merchant-capitalists to consumer taste."

### Guild Decline
State mercantilism had already undermined guilds before proto-industry did so. By 1783, Berlin-Brandenburg suspended guild restrictions for silk, cotton, and wool. Napoleonic law abolished guilds; even when they returned as weakened craft associations, their economic force was broken. By 1835 in Württemberg, guilds controlled only 17 percent of manufacturing, yet 80 percent of their masters and apprentices worked in non-guild enterprises. The pressure point was career advancement: Prussian masters grew 65 percent but apprentices 124 percent — desperate journeymen fought on 1848 barricades for the "right to work." Yet guild traditions persisted in Saxony (industrial rights retained to late 1850s) and provided skilled craftsmen for new industries and cross-national political networks via journeyman wandering years.

### Urbanization
Population grew from 30.7 million (1816) to 47.6 million (1865). Urban growth was modest until the 1850s: only 4.5 percent of Germans lived in cities above 20,000 in 1830. Explosive industrial-urban growth came later: Essen grew twelve-fold, Dortmund eleven-fold, Wuppertal eight-fold between 1800 and 1870. Migration was already substantial: the 1871 census found nearly half of Prussians dwelling outside their birth municipality.

New urban forms included property-based voting (Prussia's 1808 Stein'sche Städteordnung) and voluntary associations that created civil society infrastructure: choirs, symphonies, reading circles, hunger relief networks. These embodied a bourgeois-communitarian ideal, though deeply exclusionary toward the poor and toward women.

### Railroads and the Industrial Takeoff
The railroad was the decisive driver of the 1840s–1850s takeoff. German rail grew from 579 km (1840) to 7,123 km (1849) to 10,338 km (1860). Backward linkages created soaring demand for coal, iron, steel, and machinery. Forward linkages opened east-west trade networks, connected regions to continental markets, and conquered "time and space."

Key facts: In the 1840s railroads attracted 20–30 percent of all investment. By 1846, railroads employed 178,000 workers. Between 1850 and 1873, railroads absorbed one-quarter of all German investment. Coal output grew from 6,900 metric tons (1850) to 16,731 (1860), outdistancing France. By 1870, Germany exported 57 percent of its steel production of 1.3 million tons, having imported steel in 1850.

Paradoxically, **railroads were primarily private and transnational** rather than state-national: the Rhenish Railway (1837) aimed to connect the Rhine valley to Antwerp; Silesian railways depended on Austrian connections; German lines competed for Alpine freight. "Such contests between railway companies centered on time, distance, and freight rates, not on the abstraction of national economy."

### The [[zollverein]] and the State's Role
Prussia's customs union (1818–1834) minimized currency and weight confusion and created a domestic tariff-free market. Recent scholarship (Hans-Joachim Voth) has questioned its heroic narrative: excluding Austria made little economic sense; intra-German trade forced Germans to buy Prussian products rather than exploit comparative advantages. "The idea of a customs union may have excited the hearts and minds of contemporary bourgeois commentators, but did little to boost economic performance." Prussia's aims were "ultimately more political than economic."

The Prussian state played a **secondary** role in industrialization overall: it initially opposed railroads (seeing them as unprofitable threats to toll roads and state bonds); refused to charter joint-stock banks until the 1850s; and only gradually liberalized coal mining. "German states encouraged economic growth but did not supplant the more elemental criteria of international markets, natural resources, and entrepreneurial initiative."

## Major Phases

1. **1750–1807:** Hybrid old-new economy; agrarian reform pressure mounts; proto-industry expands; guild system erodes under mercantilist and market pressure; population growth accelerates.
2. **1807–1840:** Serfdom emancipation unfolds unevenly across German states; agricultural productivity rises; urbanization begins; proto-industry and guilds continue to dominate manufactures outside Saxony and Rhineland.
3. **1840–1860:** Railroad-led industrial takeoff; coal-iron-steel complex emerges in Ruhr and Upper Silesia; financial innovation (joint-stock banks after 1848); agricultural sector restructures toward export markets; the hungry forties (1845–1848) represent the last old-regime subsistence crisis; [[revolutions-of-1848]] partly driven by economic dislocation.

## Interaction with Other Processes

- **[[german-pauperism-vormarz]]:** The transitional period generated acute mass poverty as proto-industrial livelihoods were undercut and serfdom emancipation created landless laborers. See that page for detail.
- **[[german-nationalism-formation]]:** The Zollverein was politically designed to pull middle German states into Prussian orbit; but the emerging economy was not nationally bounded — it was regionally articulated and transnationally networked.
- **[[atlantic-revolutions-german-lands]]:** Napoleonic legal reforms (commercial courts, guild abolition, free labor) were prerequisites for western Germany's rapid industrialization in the 1840s.

## Historiography

The Sonderweg thesis (see [[german-sonderweg]]) attributes Germany's political disasters partly to an "exceptionally rapid" industrialization that destabilized social structures. Brophy argues against this: "the great transition … is more accurately understood as a protracted process, stretching back into the early modern period," with deep roots in social forces both domestic and external. The regional specificity of German industrialization is emphasized by Gary Herrigel (industrial constructions) and Hubert Kiesewetter (Saxon model) against older national-aggregate interpretations. Jan de Vries's industrious revolution model and Jonathan Sperber's property-and-civil-society framework both redirect attention from production to consumption and property relations.

Works on Prussian agrarian reform, Rhineland industrial history, and proto-industrialization are represented in the collection through specialist series; coverage of financial history and railroad history is thinner.
