# The Old Assyrian Trade Network

The **long-distance commercial system** (~1910–1780 BCE) by which merchant houses
of the city of **[[ashur|Ashur]]** ran caravans across the Euphrates and the Taurus
into **central Anatolia**, exchanging **tin and textiles** for Anatolian **silver
and gold** — documented in tens of thousands of clay tablets from
**[[kultepe|Kültepe (Kanesh)]]**, the richest commercial archive of the ancient
world. It is the documented descendant of the unrecorded
[[early-bronze-age-anatolia|Early Bronze Age Anatolian metal economy]], and the
moment Anatolia first enters written history. Treated in
[[edwards-cah-v1p2-1971|CAH Vol. I, Part 2]] (Ch. XXIV(b), Garelli/Orlin).

## Definition and Scope

A **private, family-firm commerce** operating under royal license rather than a
state enterprise. Ashur lay between the **tin sources** of the east (imported, then
re-exported) and the **metal-rich but tin-poor** Anatolian plateau; its merchants
carried **tin and fine woollen textiles** (some Babylonian, some Ashur-made) by
donkey caravan to Anatolia, sold them for **silver and gold**, and shipped the
precious metal home to buy more goods. King **Ilushuma** and his son **Erishum I**
(~1940–1900 BCE) — whose inscription was found at Kültepe — deliberately
**liberalized trade**, Erishum proclaiming "freedom of movement" for silver, gold,
copper, lead, wheat, and wool and relinquishing the old **state monopoly on
copper** (a monopoly that elsewhere let rulers control who could arm).

## How it worked

- **The kārum system.** In each Anatolian city that submitted to Ashur, the
  Assyrian community formed a self-governing body called a **kārum** ("quay,
  harbour" → "merchant colony"), headed at [[kultepe|Kanesh]]; smaller posts were
  *wabartum*. The kārum sat in the lower town beside (not inside) the native
  prince's citadel.
- **Family firms across generations.** Senior partners stayed in Ashur and directed
  the business; sons ran the Anatolian branches. Three-generation merchant
  dynasties are traceable — **Pushu-ken**, **Imdi-ilum**, **Enlil-bani** — their
  letters and accounts forming the archive's core.
- **Eponym (limmu) dating.** Documents were dated by the annual **eponym official
  (limmu)** at Ashur — a precise, sequential system (see [[assyrian-king-list]])
  that lets the period be reconstructed in years: the main "level II" archive spans
  ~1910–1840 BCE, a brief later "level Ib" falls in the reign of
  [[shamshi-adad-i|Shamshi-Adad I]].
- **Mixed society and temporary marriage.** Assyrian merchants abroad often
  contracted **dissoluble local marriages** with Anatolian women (paying
  divorce-money on departure) alongside their wives back home — a documented
  practice of the colonial community.

## Causal Drivers

The fundamental driver is the **complementarity** of resource zones: Ashur's access
to **tin** (essential for bronze and unavailable on the plateau) and to woollen
textiles, against Anatolia's **silver and gold**. The metal-hungry, tin-poor
Anatolian market that [[early-bronze-age-anatolia|Mellaart's "metal-wealth thesis"]]
inferred for the EBA is here visible in documents. Royal trade-liberalization at
Ashur supplied the legal framework.

## Geographic Spread and Society

The network linked Ashur to a web of Anatolian colonies — **Kanesh** (the hub),
**Purushhanda**, **Wahshushana**, **Durhumid**, **Hattum (Hattusha)**, **Ankuwa
(Alişar)**, **Zalpa** — set among a patchwork of small **city-states** ruled by
native princes (*rubā'um*) who swore vassal oaths to "the City" (Ashur) and, in
return, kept the trade flowing. The population was strikingly **mixed**:
Indo-European **Hittites and Luwians** (the Assyrians' "barbarians," *nu'ā'u*),
**Hurrians** (dealt with as equals; some, like Enishru, major merchants),
Assyrians, and Amorites. From this Anatolian princely milieu emerged
**Pitkhana and his son Anitta of Kushshara** — conquerors who took the title
"great prince" and destroyed Hattusha, remembered as forerunners of the
**Hittite** kings.

## Interaction and End Conditions

The network is the economic backbone of Middle Bronze Anatolia and the bridge
between the [[early-bronze-age-anatolia|EBA Anatolian metal economy]] and the rise
of the **Hittites**. The main phase ended ~1840 BCE — most likely because
**caravan traffic became unprofitable** rather than from any single conquest — with
a brief revival under [[shamshi-adad-i|Shamshi-Adad I]] before the consolidation of
Anatolian kingdoms (and Zimri-Lim's Mari) cut the routes.

## Historiography

A rare case where **private economic life is better documented than politics**: the
Kültepe archive is overwhelmingly commercial (promissory notes, caravan accounts,
letters, marriage and divorce deeds), so "historical data are rather scanty" and
the chronology is reconstructed from **eponym lists and merchant genealogies**
rather than narrative. Its value is precisely as a window onto **trade, law, and
daily colonial society** in the early second millennium. See
[[cambridge-ancient-history-methodology]].
