## Definition and Scope

Colonial Australian cultural formation describes how art, literature, and public institutions evolved in the Australian colonies from 1788 to Federation (1901), not as the organic expression of a nascent nation, but as "a series of attempts to establish forms of subjectivity and sociability, and the cultural institutions associated with them, deriving from late-Georgian and Victorian Britain" (Dixon & Hoorn, ch. 20). The chapter's central argument is that this culture was **cosmopolitan** rather than narrowly nationalist: before the 1890s, it oriented itself simultaneously toward the British/European cultural past, toward the United States via the gold-rush circuit, and — through Aboriginal cultural production — toward an ancient pre-colonial tradition. Nationalism, when it finally crystallised in the *Bulletin* and the Heidelberg School, was itself entangled with cosmopolitan influences. The process is concurrent_with [[gender-colonial-australia|gender and colonial society]] and [[colonial-science-technology-australia|colonial science and technology]], and part_of the [[long-19th-century|Long 19th Century]].

## Causal Drivers

**Absence of a "national literature" as the founding condition.** "The idea of the nation functioned as an absence" in colonial cultural life. Early cultural production modelled itself on British forms (poet-laureate odes, neoclassical architecture, subscription libraries) rather than originating from local conditions.

**Enlightenment civic humanism.** The belief that art elevates citizens — the duty of a "republic of equals" to educate the less fortunate — drove public patronage: mechanics' institutes, schools of arts, national galleries, university arts degrees grounded in Greek and Latin. The Hobart Mechanics' Institute lecturer Benjamin Duterrau compared it to "the School of Athens."

**Convict origins and social materiality.** Literary culture failed to take deep root early because "social institutions were too thin to support literary life, and partly because colonial society was so strongly materialistic." Writers could rarely achieve book publication; most earned livings through journalism, law, or public service. Literary circles (Stenhouse circle in Sydney, Meredith in Tasmania) were "short-lived."

**Gold rushes and the transpacific imaginary.** Discovery of gold in California (1848) and Australia (1850s) created a Pacific circuit. Up to 11,000 Australians had crossed to Californian goldfields by the 1850s. Folk songs, sheet music, periodicals, photographs, and theatrical troupes circulated freely between California and the eastern colonies. American actors Edwin Booth and Laura Keene toured Australia in 1854. The literary scholar Paul Giles identifies a "transpacific imaginary" transforming Australia's cultural orientation from the European classical past toward "a more modern connection with the United States."

**Pastoral capitalism and patronage.** Pastoralists became the most important art patrons outside the urban elite. Eugene von Guerard and Conrad Martens both travelled into pastoral districts when they could not survive on city patronage. "Of his 22 rural patrons almost all were pastoralists."

**Nationalist sentiment (1880s–1901).** From the centenary of European settlement (1888) onward, calls grew for a "national iconography" and a "national school of painting." The *Bulletin* (1880) championed Henry Lawson, Banjo Paterson, and Joseph Furphy. The Heidelberg School — Tom Roberts, Charles Conder, Arthur Streeton — applied French Impressionist techniques to Australian pastoral subjects. Yet even these nationalist projects remained cosmopolitan in practice: the *Bulletin*'s editor was a Francophile who imported American illustrators; Streeton's landscapes bore titles from Shelley and Wordsworth.

## Major Phases

**1788–1830 — Neoclassical and convict foundations.** Governor Macquarie appointed the emancipist Michael Massey Robinson to write commemorative odes (1810), modelling Sydney as "a second Rome, rising at the antipodes." Naval draftsmen — the Port Jackson Painters (William Bradley, George Raper) — recorded the settlement, Eora/Gadigal/Wangal Wangal people, and natural history. Convict artists (Thomas Watling, Joseph Lycett, Thomas Bock) pressed into service by governors. Australian Subscription Library opened in Macquarie Street, Sydney (1826). Hobart Town Mechanics' Institute and Library (1827).

**1830–1855 — Institutional consolidation and first literary aspirations.** Public libraries evolved from subscription to public institutions (Free Public Library of Sydney, 1869; Victoria's Public Library, 1856). First native-born poets: William Charles Wentworth (*Australasia*, 1823), Charles Tompson (*Wild Notes*, 1826). Frank Fowler launched *The Month* in Sydney (1857–58), seeking "to establish something like a national literature…by the publication of a free and fearless organ of literary criticism." Lady Franklin Museum, Hobart (1842–43), modelled on a Greek temple. Painters Augustus Earle and Conrad Martens arrived via Darwin's *Beagle* expedition; both painted sublime Blue Mountains landscapes.

**1855–1880 — Gold wealth, civic institutions, and the pastoral in art.** Gold transformed Melbourne into a "thriving Victorian city boasting Italianate mansions and grand terraces" — "boom style." Australia's first national gallery opened in Melbourne in 1861 (art critic James Smith a driving force). Art Gallery of New South Wales (1880), Adelaide (1881), Hobart (1885), Brisbane (1895), Perth (1895) followed. Universities of Sydney and Melbourne built in Gothic revival style. The Victorian Academy of the Arts commenced annual exhibitions from the 1870s; by 1872 two-thirds of exhibits were Australian landscapes. Serialisation of fiction in newspapers became the major outlet for novelists. Marcus Clarke's *For the Term of His Natural Life* first serialised in the *Australian Journal* (1870–72).

**1880–1901 — Nationalism and the cosmopolitan paradox.** The *Sydney Bulletin* (founded 1880) became the nexus of Australian literary nationalism — but editor J.F. Archibald was a Francophile who recruited American graphic artists and published European writing alongside Lawson, Paterson, and Furphy. The *Picturesque Atlas of Australasia* (1886–88) was produced by American illustrators using European impressionist styles. The Heidelberg School's famous *9 × 5 Impressions Exhibition* (1889) declared sympathy with French painting; James Smith called it "a pain to the eye." Tom Roberts' *Shearing the Rams* was heralded as emblematic of national identity while depicting labour in a pastoral-capitalist mode. Arthur Patchett Martin's *The Beginnings of an Australian Literature* (1898) codified the canon: Clarke, Boldrewood, Kingsley, Rosa Praed in fiction; Adam Lindsay Gordon and Banjo Paterson in verse.

## Geographic Spread

Cultural production concentrated in Sydney and Melbourne. Sydney was the early centre (Government House culture, subscription library, Australian Museum, *Sydney Morning Herald*). Melbourne became the cultural capital after the gold rush (National Gallery 1861, University of Melbourne, the *Bulletin*-era bohemian circles, Heidelberg School painting camps in Heidelberg, Box Hill, and bayside suburbs). Adelaide hosted an unusually coherent literary circle around Catherine Helen Spence. Hobart punched above its weight in the early period (Lady Franklin Museum, Mechanics' Institute). Perth remained culturally thin until the 1890s gold rush.

## Interaction

**Contains [[colonial-science-technology-australia|colonial science]]:** The observatories, botanic gardens, and museums that housed colonial science were also cultural institutions. Walter Baldwin Spencer — professor of biology, Darwinist, friend of the Heidelberg painters — was "one of the first serious collectors of Australian art, purchasing many paintings by Heidelberg School artists over a period of 40 years."

**Concurrent_with [[gender-colonial-australia|gender and colonial society]]:** The colonial cultural sphere was heavily gendered. Bohemian artistic culture "excluded women and involved rowdy nights of drinking in men's-only establishments." Women participated in amateur natural history but were marginalised from professional literary and artistic careers. Rosa Praed, "Tasma" (Jessie Couvreur), and Ada Cambridge wrote successfully but often required London publication or newspaper serialisation. The *Bulletin*'s 1887 declaration of Australian identity implicitly excluded women from the "Australian" type.

**Concurrent_with Aboriginal cultural production:** Indigenous Australians were the first and — per Sidney Nolan — "best" Australian artists: millennia of bark, stone, body, and sand art; songlines and didgeridoo. After 1788, Aboriginal people actively engaged with print culture: Bennelong's letter to the Phillipses (1796); Biraban's scriptural translations with Threlkeld at Lake Macquarie Mission (1830s); William Barak (Beruk) of the Wurundjeri at Coranderrk Reserve painted corroboree scenes in "a unique blend of pencil, gouache, charcoal and ochres" in the 1880s–90s, works now in European museums. Tommy McRae (Yakaduna) made satirical drawings of Europeans from the 1860s. This production was simultaneous with, but structurally excluded from, the dominant narrative of colonial culture.

**Produced_by [[british-empire]]:** The galleries' founding ambition was "to collect British art and copies from the classical world as well as to encourage and foster the productions of local artists." Colonial reading habits included monthly shipments of British periodicals (Edinburgh, Quarterly, Westminster, *Blackwood's*, Cornhill). Even at century's end Australians read Dickens and Trollope alongside local writers.

**Analogous_to settler culture elsewhere:** The *Picturesque Atlas* was explicitly modelled on *Picturesque Canada*. Wentworth's vision of Australia as "A new Britannia in another world" (1823) was succeeded by John Dunmore Lang's "a second America in the Southern Hemisphere" (1840) — a conceptual shift from Atlantic imperial model to Pacific republican one. Vaudeville and minstrel shows from the United States circulated "racial stereotypes — the Chinese, the Irishman, the Negro, the Aborigine, the Māori — [helping] to consolidate an infrastructure of racial prejudice in Australia, at the same time as opening up the country more generally to the dynamics of liberal individualism" (Giles, via Dixon & Hoorn).

## End Conditions

By 1901 a recognisable national literary and artistic canon existed — but it had been constructed through a decade (the 1890s) in which cosmopolitan influences were reformulated as national expression rather than displaced by them. The Heidelberg School's impressionism was French-derived; the *Bulletin*'s nationalism was indebted to American publishing models. Arthur Patchett Martin's 1898 survey codified "literature" in its modern restricted sense (poetry, novel, drama) and connected it isomorphically to land and nation — the framework that would dominate the twentieth century. Equivalent consolidation of a national art history waited until William Moore's *The Story of Australian Art* (1934).

## Historiography

Chapter by Robert Dixon and Jeanette Hoorn in [[bashford-macintyre-cha-v1-2013|Bashford & Macintyre, *Cambridge History of Australia*, vol. 1 (2013)]]. Their key intervention is the "cosmopolitan culture" thesis, directly opposing the cultural-nationalist teleology that reads colonial culture as preparation for the 1890s national moment. They draw on Philip Mead's "Nation, Literature, Location," Simon During's work on literary subjectivity in colonial Australia, and Paul Giles's concept of the "transpacific imaginary." Strong on visual art and patronage networks, on Indigenous cultural production, and on the transpacific circuit; relatively brief on music and theatre (though both are touched). The chapter situates colonial culture within [[settler-colonialism|settler-colonial]] frameworks without reducing art to ideology. Collection coverage for colonial Australian literary and art history is moderate in this wiki; specialist monographs by Bernard Smith (*European Vision and the South Pacific*), Ann-Mari Jordens (*The Stenhouse Circle*), and Sylvia Lawson (*The Archibald Paradox*) would deepen particular threads.
