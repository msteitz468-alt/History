# Pen/Insular State Formation

The formation of the first states in the [[pen-insular|Korean Peninsula and Japanese Islands]] —
[[koguryo|Koguryo]], [[paekche|Paekche]], [[silla|Silla]], the [[kaya|Kaya]] chiefdoms, and
[[yamato-japan|Yamato]] — between roughly the 1st century BC and the 7th century AD.
[[barnes-archaeology-east-asia-2015|Barnes's]] central thesis is that these were **secondary states**:
they arose in sustained contact with already-operating Mainland dynasties, which supplied ready-made
models of administration, elite display and legitimacy. This produces the **~2,000-year lag** behind
Mainland state formation that defines the [[yellow-sea-interaction-sphere|Yellow Sea sphere]].

## The Secondary-State Mechanism

State formation in the Pen/Insulae accelerated precisely as the Mainland's unifying structures
fractured. The [[han-dynasty|Han]] commandery system — above all [[lelang-commandery|Lelang]]
(108 BC–313 AD) — first drew local chiefs into a tribute order, stimulating iron production
([[samhan|Samhan]]) and structuring chiefly hierarchies (North Kyushu [[yayoi-culture|Yayoi]]). The
**fall of Wei and abandonment of Daifang (265)** then created the power vacuum that Koguryo, Paekche
and Silla filled. Barnes reads the whole sequence as a "fractalization" — collapsing hierarchies
seeding new ones at the periphery.

## The Archaeological Signature

Against the native chronicles — which back-date foundations to 57 BC (Silla), 37 BC (Koguryo), 18 BC
(Paekche) and even 660 BC (Yamato) — Barnes dates **statehood by the archaeology**, above all the
debut of **monumental mounded-tomb building** in the mid-to-late 3rd century AD ([[kofun-culture|the
Kofun MTC]] in Japan; pyramidal cobble- and chamber-tombs in Korea). Other shared markers: adoption
of **Chinese writing** (via Buddhist monks, late 3c–4c), of **Buddhism** as a tool of royal
legitimacy, of **steppe-derived horse-riding armour** (from Koguryo), and the petitioning of Chinese
courts for **titles** (five Yamato kings; Paekche from the Eastern Jin) to bolster status at home.

## Variation Across the Field

- **Koguryo** transformed in the late 4c "from a resilient regional authority… to a stable
  bureaucratic state," built on a paired mountain-fortress/plains-capital system.
- **Paekche** and Koguryo both claimed **Puyo descent**, using a Han-ally founder myth as legitimation.
- **Silla** rose from the Chinhan polity of Saro, its sudden gold-rich wood-chamber tombs perhaps the
  mark of new ruling clans (Lelang refugees after 313); it alone went on to **unify** the Peninsula
  (660–668) with Tang help.
- **Kaya** remained a cluster of up to seven chiefdoms; only Tae-Kaya is argued to have approached
  statehood before Silla absorbed it (562) — but Kaya's **iron** made it pivotal to Yamato.
- **Yamato** is Barnes's case study in **relatively peaceful** secondary-state formation: the Kofun
  Mounded Tomb Culture spread "without coercion" (the 527 Iwai Rebellion is the lone attested military
  episode), capped by the 7th-century **Century of Reform** that produced "Japan" (Nihon) as a Tang-
  equivalent state.

## Completion: "East Asian Civilization"

Barnes holds that the process completed in the **8th century**, when Tang, United Silla and Nara Japan
shared the same package — gridded capitals, Buddhism, Confucian political philosophy, and Tang-derived
**Ritsuryo**-style penal-and-administrative law. At that point the Pen/Insular states had become full
members of a common civilisation rather than peripheries of one.
