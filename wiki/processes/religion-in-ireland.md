## Definition and Scope

For four centuries Ireland has been the theatre of contesting politicized religions. Marianne Elliott, in [[jackson-oxford-modern-irish-history-2014]], opens her survey with the observation that 'it is a peculiarity known only to Ireland, perhaps of all other countries, that its inhabitants are more distinguished from each other, on account of their religious opinions, than they are by any other criterion' (George Cooper, 1799). This process page traces the development and transformation of Ireland's two major faith systems — Catholicism and Protestantism (primarily Anglicanism and Presbyterianism) — from the Reformation settlements of the seventeenth century through the decline of confessional institutions at the close of the twentieth century.

The process is distinct from the simple history of theological positions. It concerns how religious identity became inseparable from political loyalty, social organization, educational control, economic opportunity, and ultimately territorial partition. Ireland rarely experienced widespread religious conflict between ordinary people; the confrontational character was largely an elite phenomenon that was nonetheless capable of mobilizing mass communities.

## Causal Drivers

**State church failure.** The Church of Ireland (Anglican/Episcopalian) was the established state church 1537–1871 but represented only approximately 10 per cent of the population. Its failure to convert the Catholic majority has been interpreted as proving the 'organic link' between Irishness and Catholicism, though Elliott cautions this ignores the fact that every country that adopted the Reformed faith had been Catholic beforehand. The structural weakness of the established church outside towns, combined with English monarchs' reluctance to enforce conformity, made conversion campaigns marginal.

**Plantation and Presbyterian settlement.** The Ulster Plantation (1609) and Scottish migration after 1640 gave Irish Presbyterianism its distinctive Scottish Covenanting character. Presbyterians saw themselves as a covenanted people pledged to establish biblical church governance. Their democratic congregational structure, anti-episcopal theology, and hostility to uncoveanted kingship placed them in permanent tension with both the established church and with Catholic Ireland. The 1798 Rebellion included significant numbers of covenanting Presbyterians (Reformed Presbyterians and Seceders), as David Miller and Ian McBride's work has demonstrated — not merely the 'New Light' tolerant wing previously emphasized.

**Penal Laws (after 1695).** Following William III's victory, Catholic political rights, landownership, and public life were legally curtailed. Catholic bishops and regular clergy were officially banned; in practice, enforcement was patchy (strongest in Ulster and Munster). By 1703, over 1,000 secular priests had registered. After 1730, de facto toleration prevailed. The Penal Laws' long-term effect was to transfer social leadership of Catholics to the Catholic clergy (since the landed elite had largely conformed or been dispossessed) and to make loyalty synonymous with Protestantism.

**Devotional Revolution (19th century).** Archbishop Paul Cullen (effective head of the Irish Catholic Church 1849–78) drove the transformation of Irish Catholicism into a highly organized, disciplined, ultramontane institution: visible organizational structure, disciplined clergy, high levels of religious instruction and practice, Church control of Catholic education, and visible allegiance to Rome. Emmet Larkin coined the term 'devotional revolution' in 1972 to describe these changes; critics note the process began earlier, in the eighteenth century.

**Evangelical revival.** Methodism arrived in Ireland with John Wesley in 1747. By 1815 its main presence was in Ulster, where the evangelical revival 'helped reinforce the peculiar concentration of Protestantism in the north of the country and sharpened its anti-Catholic characteristics' (Hempton and Hill). The proselytizing activities of evangelicals among Catholics from the 1820s ('Second Reformation') provoked Catholic fightback and helped create the modern ultramontane Catholic Church.

**Partition (1921).** Partition dramatically separated religious geographies. In the Free State, Catholics rose from 74 per cent in the 1911 all-island census to 93 per cent of the southern population. The revolutionary generation ran Ireland in a culture of 'suffering Catholicism'; Church influence permeated state institutions without needing formal legal establishment. Archbishop John Charles McQuaid (Dublin, 1940–72) exemplified this informal but pervasive influence. In Northern Ireland, the 34 per cent (and growing) Catholic minority were poorly led by a hierarchy hostile to the state; their religion became inseparable from political aspiration for reunification.

**Vatican II (1962–5).** The Council reversed many Counter-Reformation structures, transforming the Catholic Church and opening the possibility of ecumenism. In Ireland the changes were slow (McQuaid was resistant) but ultimately effective. By the late twentieth century, clerical abuse scandals had devastated the authority of the Catholic Church in both parts of Ireland.

## Major Phases

1. **Reformation and contest (1537–1660):** Church of Ireland established; Ulster plantation; Catholic Counter-Reformation friars drive definition of Catholicism as Ireland's national religion
2. **Penal era and recovery (1660–1800):** De facto toleration after 1730; Catholic Church rebuilds; Presbyterian communities entrench in Ulster; Maynooth seminary founded 1795
3. **Mobilization and confrontation (1800–1870):** Catholic emancipation campaign (1823–29); evangelical revival and 'Second Reformation'; devotional revolution; Church of Ireland disestablishment 1871
4. **Confessional politics (1870–1921):** Ulster Covenant (1912); Catholic identity fused with nationalist politics; Protestant churches unite against Home Rule
5. **Partition and Catholic state (1921–1965):** Free State as Catholic cultural nation; Vatican II as external disruption; Northern Ireland's Catholic minority under siege
6. **Secularization and crisis (1965–2000):** Ecumenical overtures; Troubles reinvigorate politico-religious divisions; clerical abuse scandals; dramatic fall in Church authority

## Geographic Spread

Protestantism was always concentrated in Ulster and Dublin, with small clusters in Leinster and Munster. By 1861, Catholics were 78 per cent of Ireland's population, Anglicans 12 per cent, Presbyterians 9 per cent — but Presbyterianism was negligible outside Ulster. By 1961, Methodism and Presbyterianism together accounted for only 1 per cent of the southern population; the Church of Ireland represented 3.7 per cent, and by the 1970s 80 per cent of its members lived in Northern Ireland. The Catholic Church's strict rules on raising children of mixed marriages in the faith were held largely responsible for the near-disappearance of Protestantism from rural southern Ireland.

## Popular Religion vs. Institutional Religion

Elliott's chapter insists on the distinction between what churches taught and what people believed. Pre-Famine Catholic popular religion retained significant pagan survivals: mass-rocks (many post-Cromwellian impoverishment rather than persecution), holy wells with 'patterns' (patron days) attended by Protestants and Catholics alike into the nineteenth century, prophecies (Colum Cille; Pastorini in the 1820s), Marian devotion rooted in medieval spirituality. The friars were uniquely linked to these popular traditions, which is precisely why they were repeatedly targeted by reforming bishops and ultimately by Rome's closure of Irish noviciates in 1751. The 'devotional revolution' channelled this popular energy into orthodox church structures but could not entirely eliminate older layers.

## Historiography

The field has privileged institutional aspects, denominational conflict, and contribution to national cultures over spiritual and popular dimensions. Landmark syntheses by J. C. Beckett, Patrick Corish, and Finlay Holmes defined the institutional framework. David Miller and Ian McBride fundamentally revised the narrative of Presbyterian radicalism. Emmet Larkin's 'devotional revolution' thesis (1972) generated extensive debate. Recent work has moved toward the laity, internal church debates, and the spiritual dimensions of faith practice. Elliott notes there is no recent equivalent to the masterly overviews of the earlier generation. Sources include the major collections of ecclesiastical history journals, episcopal correspondence in Rome, and increasingly, literary and material sources for popular religion.

**Collection coverage:** The [[jackson-oxford-modern-irish-history-2014]] chapter by Marianne Elliott provides the foundation for this page. The Bibliotheca Alexandrina has moderate coverage of early modern religious history and stronger coverage of modern institutional church history. The collection is weak on the post-Vatican II transformation and on Northern Ireland's religious culture after 1970.
