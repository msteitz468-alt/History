## Definition and Scope

The development of Australia's media landscape from Federation to the present: the interplay between a small domestic market, imported cultural dominance (first British, then American), recurring nationalist debate over cultural authenticity, and successive waves of government intervention. The chapter by David Carter and Bridget Griffen-Foley in [[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]] identifies the core tension as "tensions between localism and national integration of production and markets; persistent calls for a national culture or cultural industries; domination by a few large national or multinational operators; and intervention by public institutions in the commercial marketplace."

## Causal Drivers

**Market size and import dependence.** "The domestic market was not large enough to sustain major production industries but it was sufficiently large to be a target for cultural exports such as Hollywood movies and British books." American films supplied more than 90 per cent of feature films screened in the 1920s; Australia imported more American films than any other country in most years. Britain controlled the book trade until the 1950s through informal Anglo-American copyright territory division.

**Media dynasty formation.** Two dynasties emerged in the 1920s: Keith Murdoch's Melbourne newspaper empire and the Packer family's Sydney press. Rupert Murdoch launched the national daily *The Australian* in 1964, acquired the Herald and Weekly Times in 1987 (with Hawke Labor government approval), and came to control two-thirds of Australia's metropolitan daily circulation through News Ltd. Australia's newspaper industry became "among the most concentrated in the developed world."

**Dual broadcasting model.** Broadcasting commenced 1923; by the end of the 1920s a hybrid emerged combining British (public service) and American (commercial) precedents. 'A' class stations nationalised into the **Australian Broadcasting Commission (ABC)** in 1932, modelled on the BBC. 'B' class stations remained commercial. The same dual model was adopted for television (1956) and FM radio (1970s).

**Government cultural policy acceleration.** The Gorton Coalition government established the Australian Council for the Arts (1968, $1.7 million budget); the Whitlam government replaced it with the Australia Council (1975, $14 million, seven boards). The Australian Film Development Corporation (1970) and Australian Film Commission (1975) funded nearly 400 films 1970–1985. Local content regulations for television introduced 1960 (40 per cent Australian); drama quota 1966 (30 min/week locally produced drama).

## Major Phases

| Print and live performance | 1901–1922 | Bulletin dominance; newspaper concentration; J.C. Williamson touring circuits; Nellie Melba |

| Television and pop music | 1956–1969 | Dual-system TV from Melbourne Olympics; *Homicide* (1964) as first major local drama hit; Top 40 radio; *Countdown* |
| National cultural revival | 1970–1985 | Australia Council; film renaissance (*Picnic at Hanging Rock*, *Gallipoli*, *Mad Max*); *Neighbours* and *Home and Away* for export |
| Concentration and digital disruption | 1987–present | Herald & Weekly Times takeover; SBS launched; satellite TV (AUSSAT 1986); digital terrestrial TV 2001; newspaper circulation collapse 2001–2011 |

**Key institutional moments:**
- **ABC** (1932): devoting half its airtime to music by mid-1930s, with orchestras in every state but Queensland; became the most innovative digital adapter.
- **Television** (1956, Sydney and Melbourne): within 13 years eight of the twelve most popular programs were Australian; *Skippy* sold to over 100 markets.
- **SBS** (radio 1978, television 1980): launched under multicultural policy; audience described as "the urban cosmopolitan of varying ethnicities" rather than the working-class migrant.
- **Local content and advertising rules**: the advertising requirement was "critical in underwriting a local production industry for television and cinema."

## Cultural Production

**Film.** Local production peaked 1910–12 (100+ features, mostly bushranger melodrama); near-collapsed 1930s–1960s under American vertical integration; revived from 1970 with government funding. *The Story of the Kelly Gang* (1906) is "often claimed as the world's first feature-length film." The 1970s–80s renaissance produced an internationally distinctive genre: "Australian film heroes differed from their Hollywood counterparts, being ordinary rather than extraordinary, survivors rather than conquerors."

**Indigenous cultural production.** Central Desert dot painting emerged at Papunya (1970s), pioneered by artists including Clifford Possum Tjapaltjarri and Emily Kame Kngwarreye; "now collected internationally." Urban Aboriginal artists (Gordon Bennett, Tracey Moffatt) brought postmodernist confrontation to questions of history and identity. Bands such as Yothu Yindi, the Bangarra Dance Theatre, and directors Ivan Sen and Rachel Perkins made Indigenous voices central to the national cultural scene.

**Literature.** The 'cultural cringe' (A.A. Phillips, 1950) named colonial deference; Patrick White's Nobel Prize (1973) was a turning point. From the mid-1970s to mid-1980s a generation of major novelists appeared: Peter Carey, David Malouf, Tim Winton, Helen Garner, Kate Grenville.

**'Oz Rock'.** After the Beatles model of performing original material, Australian bands built local fan bases before touring overseas (AC/DC, INXS). The ABC's *Countdown* (1974–87) created a national audience for local music. By the 1980s commentators talked confidently of 'Oz Rock.'

## Geographic Spread

Sydney and Melbourne were the media centres. The AUSSAT domestic satellite (1986) enabled commercial stations to complete national networking, rendering TV schedules "virtually identical" across the country. Radio Australia (launched 1939 as shortwave service) and the Australia Network television service extended reach into Asia.

## End Conditions

Ongoing. Australia has moved from client-state consumer of British/American culture to "a medium-sized player, exporter as well as importer, within globalised cultural industries." Yet "local publishing, film-making, theatre and music production, while vibrant and diverse, are still often precarious and dependent upon government subsidy or protection." The internet fragments audiences while national markets remain crucial.
