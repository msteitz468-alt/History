# The Carolingian Renaissance

## Definition and Scope

The **Carolingian Renaissance** was the revival of Latin learning, textual scholarship, education,
and the arts in the Frankish realms from the late eighth century, sponsored by the rulers of the
[[carolingian-empire]] and centred on its courts, cathedrals, and monasteries. In
[[mckitterick-ncmh-v2-1995|NCMH II]] (Contreni, Ch. 27; Ganz, Chs. 28–29; McKitterick, Ch. 25;
Banniard, Ch. 26) it is the volume's culminating theme — the enterprise that, in the words of the
Conclusion, ensured that "without the Carolingians, neither Latin script nor Latin texts would have
secured the continuities" linking antiquity to the later medieval world.

## Renovatio and Correctio

The Carolingians called their programme **renovatio** ("renewal"), but Contreni stresses that what
they really sought was **reform and correction** (*correctio*) of a Christian society — correct texts,
correct worship, correct conduct. The drive began with **Charlemagne's** mandate *Epistola de Litteris
Colendis* ("On Cultivating Letters") and the reforming legislation that urged every monastery and
bishopric to teach. Learning was harnessed to the moral and religious ordering of the realm; it was
inseparable from [[carolingian-kingship|royal government]] and [[carolingian-church-reform|church reform]].

## Schools and Scholars

The renaissance worked through a network of schools. At its heart was the **palace school**, a loosely
organised, transient gathering of scholars around the ruler — most brilliantly under Charlemagne, who drew
the Northumbrian **[[alcuin|Alcuin of York]]**, the Lombard Paul the Deacon, the Visigoth **Theodulf of
Orléans**, and others to his court. After its heyday in the 780s–790s, the court scholars dispersed to found
formal schools at the great monasteries and cathedrals — **Tours** (Alcuin), **Fulda** (**Hraban Maur**),
**Ferrières** (**Lupus**), **Reims** (**Hincmar**), **Auxerre**, **Corbie**, **St Gall**. Charles the Bald's
court later sustained a second flowering, including the daring philosopher **John Scottus Eriugena**.

## The Industry of the Scriptoria

The renaissance was above all a vast effort of **textual production and preservation**. Carolingian
scriptoria copied "perhaps as many as 50,000" books in the ninth century alone; some **7,000 manuscripts
survive** from the Carolingian period against only some 500 from all of Merovingian Gaul. Scholars revised
the biblical text, compiled patristic works, wrote commentaries and histories, exchanged letters, and
glossed the classics — and in doing so **preserved the bulk of surviving Latin literature**, which reaches
us overwhelmingly through Carolingian copies written in the new **[[caroline-minuscule|Caroline minuscule]]**.

## Language and the Liberal Arts

Banniard (Ch. 26) studies the **language** dimension: the gap opening between learned, reformed **Latin** and
the emerging spoken **Romance** vernaculars, and the problem of *communicability* — whether a corrected,
"purer" Latin could still be understood by ordinary congregations. The curriculum was built on the **seven
liberal arts** (the *trivium* of grammar, rhetoric, dialectic and the *quadrivium* of arithmetic, geometry,
music, astronomy), pursued as the path to the understanding of Scripture.

## Intellectual Disputes

A sure sign of the renaissance's vitality was serious **theological controversy** (Ganz, Ch. 28): the
condemnation of **Adoptionism** (Felix of Urgel and Elipandus of Toledo, condemned at the Council of
Frankfurt, 794, and 798); the bitter dispute over **predestination** provoked by the monk **Gottschalk**
(condemned at Mainz, 848); the **eucharistic debate** between Paschasius Radbertus and Ratramnus of Corbie;
and the Frankish position on religious images set out in the **_Libri Carolini_** (against both
[[byzantine-iconoclasm|iconoclasm]] and the image-veneration of Nicaea II). Scholars also "proved themselves
adept at forgery" (the [[donation-of-constantine|Donation of Constantine]], the Pseudo-Isidorian Decretals).

## Significance

The Carolingian Renaissance secured the **transmission of classical and Christian Latin culture** to the
later Middle Ages, created the educational institutions and the legible script of medieval Europe, and
established learning as a function of Christian rulership. It is, in a real sense, the intellectual
foundation of European civilisation.

## Historiography

NCMH II resists a top-down, monolithic picture: Contreni stresses that the reality was "less schematic and
more decentralised than official mandates suggest," and that the blending of dissonant Christian and antique
traditions made a truly "unitary culture" illusory — the harvest was "luxuriant and in some ways unintended."
The old debate over whether this was a genuine "renaissance" or merely clerical reform is reframed by the
Carolingians' own word, *renovatio*, and by the sheer scale of textual survival.

## From Logan, History of the Church in the Middle Ages (2013)

Seventh–eighth-century English scholarly pre-eminence (especially Northumbria) came from two interwoven streams—Irish and continental/Roman—best called “**insular**”; Christian missionaries brought literacy that civilized barbarian English. Theodore–Hadrian Canterbury school; Wearmouth–Jarrow and Bede; York under Egbert and Ethelbert (c.766–79) likely had western Europe’s best library and trained **Alcuin**; Charlemagne invited Alcuin to Aachen (781/783) for educational advice—“in light of these events of a century or more in England.” Gregory’s *Pastoral Care* and related works fed later medieval spiritual reading (Alfred’s English translation). Roman Rite path (Hadrianic sacramentary + Frankish supplementation) is part of the same *correctio* culture.
