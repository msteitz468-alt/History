# De-Stalinization
## Definition and Scope
**De-Stalinization** was the political and social process in the Soviet Union and its satellite states that dismantled the cult of personality, mass terror apparatus, and concentration camp system established under Joseph Stalin. Initiated primarily by Nikita Khrushchev following Stalin's death in 1953, the process included denouncing Stalin's crimes, releasing and rehabilitating millions of political prisoners, easing censorship, and introducing moderate economic and administrative reforms.

## Causal Drivers
- **Intra-Elite Power Struggle:** Khrushchev utilized the denunciation of Stalin's terror to discredit his chief rivals in the Presidium (such as Molotov and Malenkov), who had been deeply implicated in Stalin's crimes.
- **Systemic Survival:** The Soviet leadership realized that the system of permanent terror and mass slave labor was economically counterproductive and politically unsustainable, causing deep instability and anxiety among the elite.
- **Popular Pressure:** Uprisings in forced-labor camps (such as Vorkuta in 1953) and unrest in non-Russian republics (like Georgia in 1956) signaled a dangerous undercurrent of popular anger.

## Major Phases
- **The Initial Thaw (1953–1955):** Following the execution of secret police chief Lavrentii Beria in late 1953, the power of the political police was curtailed, and early releases of Gulag inmates began.
- **The Secret Speech (1956):** At the Twentieth Party Congress, Khrushchev delivered a closed-session speech denouncing Stalin as a cruel tyrant, blaming him for the purges, wartime defeats, and military unpreparedness. This speech caused a massive shock throughout the communist world.
- **The Peak and Rename Campaigns (1961):** At the Twenty-second Party Congress, Khrushchev renewed the attack on Stalinism, detailing further atrocities. Stalin's body was removed from the Red Square mausoleum, and cities named after him were renamed (most notably Stalingrad became Volgograd).
- **The Backlash and Halt (1964):** Khrushchev's arbitrary style and the instability caused by de-Stalinization led to his peaceful removal from power. Under Leonid Brezhnev, de-Stalinization was halted, and a period of partial re-Stalinization and compromise set in.

## Geographic Spread
While centered in Moscow, the process affected the entire Soviet Union and led to major political crises and rebellions in the satellite states of Eastern Europe, most notably the Poznań protests in Poland and the **[[hungarian-revolution-1956|Hungarian Revolution of 1956]]**.

## Interaction
- **With the Gulag:** The process resulted in the rapid release of millions of forced-labor camp inmates, reducing the camp system to a fraction of its former size.
- **With Soviet Culture:** It enabled the publication of previously banned literature, such as Solzhenitsyn's *One Day in the Life of Ivan Denisovich*, and fostered a vibrant "thaw" in the arts, though this remained strictly bounded by Party guidelines.

## End Conditions
De-Stalinization ended as a state-directed policy with the fall of Khrushchev in October 1964. The Brezhnev administration opted for stability, avoiding mention of Stalin's crimes at Party congresses, though it did not return to mass terror. The process was later revived and completed in its radical form during the Gorbachev years under the policy of **[[glasnost|Glasnost]]**.
