## Definition and Scope

China–Pacific relations since 1949 covers the evolution of the People's Republic of
China's engagement with the Pacific Ocean basin and its island nations — spanning
fisheries, diplomacy, aid, trade, migration, and strategic competition — from the PRC's
founding to the present. Sheng and D'Arcy in [[hattori-samson-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v2-2023]]
(ch. 58) describe "a story of two distinct eras": before 1978, China focused inward while
its Pacific diplomacy "espoused solidarity for the decolonization and sovereignty of
fellow developing nations"; from 1978, China engaged increasingly with the overseas
economy as it "rapidly rose to become a global powerhouse of manufacturing."

The chapter treats two core engagement tracks: the Pacific Ocean as an **environmental
and fishery/sea-lane resource**, and **diplomatic and strategic engagement** with the
wider Pacific community.

## Causal Drivers

- caused_by: PRC foundation in 1949 and subsequent Cold War isolation from Western-aligned Pacific nations
- caused_by: Deng Xiaoping's reform and opening-up (1978) — "PRC has been the exception, rising recently to become the second largest economy in the world … through strong state control of its economic modernization"
- caused_by: Pacific Island decolonisation in the 1970s — most nations gained independence just as China was modernising and seeking developing-nation partners
- contributed_to: China's domestic coastal fisheries decline since 1978 driving expansion to distant-water fishing in Pacific EEZs
- contributed_to: 1972 UN Stockholm Conference, which exposed Chinese delegates to the global character of industrial pollution and shifted environmental thinking
- contributed_to: Taiwan's competing diplomatic-recognition campaign, which forced China to match and outbid Taiwan's aid to Pacific Island nations
- contributed_to: Xi Jinping's designation of the marine fishery as a "state strategic industry" in China's 12th Five-Year Plan (2011)

## China's Long Pacific Maritime Engagement

Chinese maritime engagement with the Pacific predates 1949 by centuries. Coastal
south Chinese peoples traded extensively with Southeast Asia and the Indian Ocean.
Southeast Chinese diaspora communities followed gold rushes to Australia and New
Zealand in the nineteenth century. Professor Wang Gungwu distinguishes three
historical categories — *huashang* (traders), *huagong* (overseas labourers from the late
nineteenth century), and *huaqiao* (sojourners who remained connected to the mainland)
— and a contemporary fourth category: *huayi*, Chinese who no longer hold Chinese
nationality and are "globally mobile in pursuit of economic success."

Ethnic Chinese are now the largest non-Western population in Australia and New
Zealand, and the third-largest non-Indigenous group in the Pacific Islands. Graeme
Smith's detailed study of Fuqing migrants in Papua New Guinea found that most came
from Fujian province, set up small retail businesses, and "intended to settle in Fuqing
after making their money in Papua New Guinea" — illustrating how Chinese diaspora
networks in the Pacific are entrepreneurially driven rather than state-directed.

## Fisheries

China's combined fresh water and marine catch grew from 4.7 million metric tonnes in
1978 to 67 million in 2015 — one-third of the total declared global catch. By 2015 China
had the largest fishing fleet in the world with 672,416 motorised fishing vessels,
including 2,512 distant-water vessels operating globally. "In 1985, an estimated
90 per cent of China's fish production came from inshore waters"; by 2015 aquaculture
accounted for around 75 per cent of total catch and the catch from distant-water vessels
working on the high seas and in other nations' EEZs "overtook that of coastal fishery
vessels in 2002."

China's coastal fisheries are threatened by overfishing, coastal urban pollution,
nitrogen and phosphate eutrophication, and climate-driven temperature shifts. Since
President Xi, the marine fishery is a "state strategic industry" and a "key area for
international cooperation in the Belt and Road initiative." China's preferred cooperation
is bilateral rather than through regional bodies like the Pacific Islands Forum Fisheries
Agency. See also [[pacific-fisheries-and-eez]].

## Diplomatic Relations with Pacific Island Nations

The PRC was largely focused on domestic reconstruction in its first three decades. China
began diplomatic relations with Australia and New Zealand in 1972. Premier Zhou Enlai
opened relations with Fiji and Western Samoa in 1975 and announced intention to
recognise Papua New Guinea before his death in January 1976. Recognition of Kiribati
followed in 1980 and Vanuatu in 1982. "From the outset of relations, China has always
emphasized its respect for the sovereignty of Pacific states and their EEZs, and a policy
of non-interference."

China largely avoided Cold War nuclear politics in the Pacific. Unlike the USA, UK, and
France, which all conducted nuclear tests in the Pacific, China tested only within its
metropolitan territory (Gansu province). In May 1980 China conducted its first ICBM
test, landing in open ocean 1,200 km northwest of Fiji; Vice Premier Li Xiannian was on
a contemporaneous friendship tour and "noted China had no intention of conducting
nuclear bomb tests in the Pacific."

China's engagement stepped up from 2000 as its economic and strategic reach expanded.
Premier Wen Jiabao visited Fiji in April 2006 and announced an aid package of 3 billion
yuan (US$484 million), mostly as low-interest preferential loans at approximately 2 per
cent for terms of up to twenty years, with the possibility of debt cancellation. This
prompted stepped-up aid from Australia, Japan, and the United States in response.

## Strategic Framework: China's Four Pacific Interests

Chinese academic Changsen Yu (Sun Yat-sen University) provided what Sheng and D'Arcy
describe as one of the few comprehensive statements of China's Pacific priorities (2016):

1. **Sea lanes**: "90 per cent of China's imports and exports and 40 per cent of its petrol and oil travel by sea" through the western Pacific and Southeast Asia; Pacific Island locations matter for these routes.
2. **Marine defence strategy**: China increasingly seeks blue-water naval capacity "to be able to operate beyond the range of land-based air and missile defence systems." China's main focus remains coastal defence within the first island chain (Ryukyus, Senkaku, Paracels, Spratlys), but it seeks to break through the second island chain (Marianas, Guam, Micronesia). The US's upgrading of Guam and re-engagement with Micronesian compact states is described as a direct response.
3. **Taiwan recognition**: Until 2019, six Pacific Island nations (Palau, Marshall Islands, Solomon Islands, Tuvalu, Kiribati, Nauru) gave Taiwan diplomatic recognition. In September 2019 Solomon Islands and Kiribati switched recognition to China. As of ch. 58's writing, four Taiwan allies remain in the Pacific (Palau, Marshall Islands, Nauru, Tuvalu).
4. **Economic development and soft power**: China promotes itself as "a fellow developing nation willing to be a responsible international partner," imposing no aid conditionality on internal governance — explicitly contrasted with the "Washington Consensus" conditions attached by Australia and the United States.

## The Taiwan Recognition Contest

The contest for diplomatic recognition is the clearest example of Pacific Island agency
generating superpower competition. Jian Yang (*The Pacific Islands in China's Grand
Strategy*, 2011) argued that Beijing sought international peace permitting domestic
development and "lacked both the means and the desire to challenge Western powers in
the Pacific Islands." Wesley-Smith and Porter (*China in Oceania*, 2010) argued China
"has allowed Pacific Islanders a greater degree of freedom of choice over aid donors
than at any other time since independence." Both argue against a threat-framing — though
subsequent events (the 2019 Solomon Islands and Kiribati switches) gave the competition
more intensity than Yang's 2011 analysis anticipated.

Anti-Chinese riots in Papua New Guinea and the Solomon Islands reflect complex
dynamics of economic resentment rather than a single geopolitical cause.

## Climate Change and Future Relations

Pacific Island nations face the most acute climate change exposure on Earth: "ocean
acidification levels in the Pacific are already 52 parts per million (ppt) beyond the
maximum safe limit of 350 ppm." In a 2014 visit to Fiji, President Xi promised support
for climate change issues. China's climate aid focuses on solar power generators, biogas
equipment, and capacity training. China and Pacific Island nations share a Common But
Differential Responsibility (CBDR) framework for emissions. However, China's climate
investment remains "marginal compared to other major Pacific donors such as Australia,
the EU, and the Green Climate Fund," and like Australia it opposes the 1.5-degree limit
preferred by Pacific Island nations.

In September 2019, Kiribati's President Maamau — having just switched recognition from
Taiwan to China — entered discussions with Chinese officials about "using Chinese
expertise to build up, extend, and consolidate the nations' vulnerable low-lying atolls
from sea erosion and sea level rise."

## Historiography

The scholarly literature on China–Pacific relations has grown rapidly since 2010. Major
frames are the threat frame (prevalent in Australian and US policy circles), the
partnership frame (Wesley-Smith, D'Arcy), and the agency frame emphasising Pacific
Island choices. Sheng and D'Arcy lean toward the partnership frame while acknowledging
growing strategic complexity. Yang's 2011 study is the first systematic analysis of
China's grand strategy in the Pacific Islands; the 2019 recognition switches by Solomon
Islands and Kiribati post-date and partially complicate his argument. Source coverage
for Chinese domestic fisheries policy and aquaculture is strong; coverage of security
dimensions of the second island chain and of Chinese domestic debates is thinner.
