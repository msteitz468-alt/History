## Definition and Scope

This page covers Leonard Andaya's argument, in chapter 25 of the Cambridge History of the Pacific Ocean vol. 1 (2022), that eastern Indonesia — typically assigned to "Southeast Asia" in post-World War II geopolitical categories — can be profitably understood as part of the Pacific world rather than as a separate regional entity. The argument rests on shared maritime ecology, shared social constructions of ocean space as land-water continuum, shared archaeological connections (Austronesian Painting Tradition, Lapita Complex), and shared economic structures (fisher-forager-trader as primary social type).

The chapter covers the period 1400–1830s, when the spice trade made eastern Indonesia a node of global exchange, and European colonialism (Portuguese, Spanish, Dutch VOC) transformed but did not dissolve indigenous maritime networks.

## Rethinking Spatial Categories

Andaya opens by challenging the tyranny of "Southeast Asia" as a unit of analysis. The term originates in WWII's Southeast Asia Command — "the wartime division of responsibility among the allies towards the Asia-Pacific lands consolidated perceptions of two separate regions, which have been retained in the post-war period." This division is arbitrary when applied to eastern Indonesia, which shares ecological, cultural, and economic features with the Pacific far more than with the mainland Southeast Asian kingdoms that dominate regional historiography.

Drawing on E. Hau'ofa (1994), who challenged the "watery desert" image of the Pacific in favour of a "Sea of Islands" criss-crossed by kin and trade networks; on Peregrine Horden and Nicholas Purcell's advocacy for smaller "oceanic arcs" as units of study; and on Matt Matsuda's conception of the Pacific as "different seas, cultures, and people intersecting with each other," Andaya proposes eastern Indonesia as "one of the seas within the Pacific."

Wigen and Lewis's *Myth of Continents* (1997) provides the metageographical critique underpinning this move: Europe-derived continental categories obscure "true comparables" (societies sharing ecological zones or maritime environments).

## Social Construction of Eastern Indonesian Sea Space

The most prominent centres of eastern Indonesia lie on small volcanic islands off the northwest coast of Halmahera: the five Maluku islands (Ternate, Tidore, Moti, Makian, Bacan) — clove-endemic peaks of tall underwater volcanoes — and the ten tiny Banda Islands south of Ambon, native to the nutmeg tree. Eastern Indonesia sits within the Ring of Fire; volcanic eruptions and tsunamis are recurrent (the Makian people maintain a second settlement on Halmahera for evacuation purposes). This is a "Sea of Islands" in Hau'ofa's sense: islands for residence, islands for cultivation, islands with limestone caves for swiftlet nests, islands reserved for burial, refuge islands.

### The Tanah Air (Land-Water) Concept

The Malay-Indonesian *tanah air* ("land water") denotes the lived environment of land, rivers, and sea as a continuous whole. The river is an arm of the sea reaching inland (*tali harus*). Within the *tanah air*, local spirits protect inhabitants; in open sea, translocal spiritual protectors are invoked: Mazu (Chinese sea goddess), Nabi Khidr (Islamic figure), Stella Maris. In eastern Indonesia the same distinction between political entity (*negeri* or *kerajaan*) and the people's lived world (*tanah air*) appears in indigenous texts. This mirrors exactly the Pacific Island conception of land-water continuum that Hviding documented for Marovo Lagoon (Solomons), D'Arcy for Oceania generally, and Salesa for the Cook Islands, Hawai'i, and Federated States of Micronesia.

### Kin Networks and Marriage Alliance

"Land-water units form the core of the polities in eastern Indonesia and constitute extensive familial and trade relationships." Kin was created through:
- Real and fictive marriage
- Lactic (milk-sharing) bonds — among the Bugis of South Sulawesi and the ruling houses of Ternate, "the sibling relationship created by this lactic bond had its own specific term and was regarded as even stronger than those among blood siblings"
- Adoption of extraordinary skill-holders

"Family was always more trusted than strangers in any economic transaction" — so marriage alliances were deliberately established at all important trade intersections. The result was the institution of "temporary wives": local women who married foreign traders but remained at home; free to remarry if husbands did not return within a set period. Mixed offspring of these unions served as "useful intermediaries between the society of their fathers and that of their mothers" — a creole intermediary class structuring trade with Papuan groups in West New Guinea.

## Pacific Connection: Archaeology and Linguistic Evidence

Eastern Indonesia's Pacific connections extend to prehistory. The dominant Bellwood-Blust thesis posits unidirectional Austronesian expansion: from China to Taiwan (~3500 BCE), then south through Philippines and Indonesia, with a split sending one branch west and another east into the Pacific (~1500 BCE). Andaya notes this is challenged by:

- **Solheim's "Nusantao" model**: a multidirectional maritime trade network in which Austronesian lingua franca evolved; "a rake-like model" rather than a "tree-like model." Austronesian speakers were "fisher-forager-traders rather than agriculturalists."
- **Austronesian Painting Tradition (APT)**: widespread in island Southeast Asia but not found in Taiwan or the Philippines; may have "originated in island Southeast Asia somewhere in the Banda Sea" — i.e. in eastern Indonesia.
- **No genetic input from Taiwan** into Austronesian languages of Melanesia, contra the Bellwood-Blust model.
- **New archaeological evidence**: APT is found in Sulawesi, Flores, Alor, Timor Leste, the Kei Islands, Papua (eastern Indonesia) and the Bismarck Archipelago and Vanuatu (western Pacific) — "the ideological underpinnings of the Austronesian expansion, may have occurred in the eastern Indonesian islands around the Banda Sea."
- **Lapita Cultural Complex** (c. 1350–750 BCE): distinctive pottery that emerged in island Melanesia, coastal New Guinea, and eastern Indonesia — a second cross-regional connection.

These findings demonstrate "the very early links that the eastern Indonesian islands had with the Pacific, but also the elements that they had in common."

## Maritime Environment and Boat Technology

Eastern Indonesian waters contain the world's richest coral reefs (more than 350 species in eastern Indonesia alone), extensive seagrass beds, mangrove forests, and rich fisheries. Indonesia has some 18,110 islands, 75,000 km² of coral reefs, 800 species of seaweeds, 90 species of mangroves, over 25 species of whales and dolphins, and the richest fish species diversity in the world. The region forms part of the "Coral Triangle" and the "East Indies Triangle" (Malaysia, Philippines, Indonesia, Papua New Guinea, western Pacific) — "regarded as the centre of maximum marine biodiversity in the Indo-Malayan region."

### The Lashed-Lug Planked Boat

The characteristic boat technology of eastern Indonesia — identified by A. Horridge as the "lashed-lug planked boat" — was built entirely of local plant material: wooden planks, rattan lashing, palm matting. Five basic components: shell-first construction on keel or dugout foundation; hardwood edge-dowelled planking carved (not bent) to shape; lugs carved in transverse rows on the inside; flexible frames in tension; transverse thwarts lashed to lugs. Rattan lashing gave elasticity "suited to the bumping and choppiness in the sea"; quarter rudders on rattan strands and curved keel enabled manoeuvrability through shallow coral reefs. Boats lasted 30–40 years and could be "repaired indefinitely" by knocking out locking pegs and reassembling. "Such hardwood boats could be repaired indefinitely and last thirty to forty years in the tropics."

This design was "the standard construction over at least a thousand years, extending from Taiwan to Timor." In the Lamalera whaling village (Lembata), the boat is believed to have a soul; its parts carry human-body names; strict ritual governs boatbuilding. Konjo boatbuilders (South Sulawesi) observe the *semangat* (soul) of the boat through ancestor-derived ritual sequences from tree selection to launching.

The most effective variant: the *caracoa* (southern Philippines) / *kora-kora* (eastern Indonesia) — "two rows of paddlers on two levels that proved far faster than the Spanish galleys that were built to counter raids by these native boats." They were built to be carried by crew, enabling rapid disappearance into estuaries and wetlands.

**Sixteenth-century transformation**: European arrival and the spice trade required boats to carry more cargo. Outrigger removed from kora-kora; central stern rudder introduced; single mast replaced tripod; floors and stiff ribs inserted. This shifted primary function from person-transport to cargo-transport. Large Southeast Asian *jong* (400–500 tons) then carried goods to southern China and the Coromandel in India "until the second half of the sixteenth century, when the jong were displaced as long-distance carriers."

## Fisher-Forager-Trader Economy

The primary social type was the fisher-forager-trader rather than the sedentary agriculturalist. Sea commodities prized in the China market:
- Tortoiseshell (hawksbill turtle)
- Pearls and mother-of-pearl
- Edible seaweeds
- Tripang (*bêche de mer*, sea cucumber)
- Swiftlet nests (white-nest and black-nest swiftlets from limestone caves) — "a rare delicacy, like the tripang, that graced the banquet tables of the wealthy and powerful in China"

**Sama Bajau**: sea people from southern Philippines to Papua New Guinea coast, living on houseboats or stilt settlements within coral complexes. Their language contains precise terms for specific types of coral (e.g. *sapa timpusuq* — a coral rising sharply from deep water without reaching the surface, invisible and dangerous). "The naming of such dangerous reefs could serve as reminders of their location"; reefs were inscribed into the seascape as transmissible knowledge. Sama Bajau organized into small family boat communities under respected kin leaders, undertaking "long-distance and lengthy collecting and fishing expeditions."

**Forest products**: eastern Indonesia's "trinity of spices":
- **Cloves** (*Syzygium aromaticum*): indigenous to five small islands of northern Maluku; "whenever traders arrived to purchase cloves, the inhabitants would go to the forests, break off branches bearing the clove, and then exchange this prized commodity with products from the outside world"
- **Nutmeg and mace** (*Myristica fragrans*): native to Banda Islands; "the lure of these spices in preserving and providing flavour to the food and drink of medieval Europe was one of the factors that launched the great European explorations"
- **Sandalwood** (*Santalum album*): grew on Sumba, Flores, Timor, Wetar between 300–1300 metres; "the Chinese were the main purchasers," using it as incense, scented soap, ointment, and medicine

Other forest products: parrots, birds of paradise (endemic to the islands and Papua; "eagerly sought by communities in Europe, the Middle East, India, and China"). From Papua: slaves (high demand in VOC era), massoia bark (medicinal), sago (staple diet in eastern Indonesia; "Even the European employees of the VOC came to rely on sago for their diet").

### Trade Networks and Elephant Tusks

Trade networks were "extensive and complex." Elephant tusks — required as bride price in Solor archipelago and Tanimbar archipelago — had to be obtained from western Indonesia or Burma and Thailand, generating elaborate networks moving tusks eastward. "The allure of the eastern Indonesian products ensured that elephant tusks were among the items that traders, including Europeans, brought to the east."

East Seram communities traded with Papuan groups along the Papua coast for at least 500 years, establishing intermarriage settlements on small offshore islands near Teluk Berau and Onin-Kowiai coasts of West New Guinea, operating through *sosolot* (bays or anchorages under recognized trade monopoly). Mixed creole communities at these outposts "facilitated trade, which was the lifeblood of these communities."

## End Conditions (1830s)

The chapter ends the analysis in the 1830s — the period of accelerating VOC and Dutch colonial consolidation, which progressively restructured (though never eliminated) indigenous trade networks. The primary difference Andaya identifies between eastern Indonesia and the Pacific proper: "eastern Indonesia experienced far earlier and greater impact from the outside world as a result of the strong international demand for its unique fauna and flora." But the fundamental maritime-world character — islands with separate functions, reefs as food and trade resource, sea lanes linking family and partners — persisted.

## Historiography

Andaya's chapter is positioned within two revisionary scholarly movements: (1) the post-Hau'ofa reconceptualization of Pacific Islander agency and the "Sea of Islands" paradigm; (2) the Southeast Asia maritime history field associated with Anthony Reid (who is implicitly present through the period framing and sources), Leonard Andaya's own prior work (*The World of Maluku*, 1993; *Leaves of the Same Tree*, 2008), and Barbara Watson Andaya. The Solheim "Nusantao" challenge to Bellwood-Blust is noted with approval but not resolved; Andaya is sympathetic to multidirectional models but does not adjudicate the linguistic-genetic debate.

Key source limitation: this chapter's analysis relies heavily on Andaya's own previous scholarship, Horridge's boat-technology studies, and ecological survey literature; it does not engage deeply with the large VOC archive, which would be needed to trace the seventeenth-century Dutch transformation in detail.
