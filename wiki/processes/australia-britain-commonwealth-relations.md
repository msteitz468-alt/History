# Australia–Britain and British Commonwealth Relations

## Definition and Scope

The process by which Australia moved from being an integral, self-governing part of the British Empire in 1901 to a substantially independent state with residual but transformed British ties by the late twentieth century. As Carl Bridge argues in [[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]], the relationship "changed fundamentally, if gradually, across the twentieth century...In the 1960s and 1970s this relationship, which had been attenuating for decades, unravelled with astonishing speed." The transformation was asymmetric: Britain shed empire and retreated into Europe; Australia diversified economically and diplomatically into Asia while many cultural and institutional bonds endured.

The process is also the story of the [[british-commonwealth]] — the voluntary multilateral body that replaced the British Empire — and Australia's changing relationship with it.

## Causal Drivers

**Settler-colonial demography**: In 1901 the population was, as it "boasted, '98 per cent' British, most of them the children or grandchildren of British immigrants." Alfred Deakin's famous self-description as an "independent Australian Briton" captured the psychological reality. Institutions were British from top to bottom: governors, professors, archbishops, judges all routinely appointed from Britain; the highest court of appeal was the Privy Council in London.

**Material dependence**: "Over half of the country's imports and exports came from and were sent to Britain." British investment "oiled the wheels of Australia's commerce." Australia was within the sterling bloc. The Royal Navy provided the primary strategic shield.

**The British Empire's contraction**: From the 1930s the strains became visible. The Depression, German rearmament, and Japan's expansion tested imperial solidarity. Britain's entry into the EEC (1963 application; successful 1971) represented the decisive break — it reclassified Australians as "aliens" for immigration purposes and redirected British trade away from Commonwealth preferences. Britain's independent nuclear deterrent was scrapped in 1963, defence forces withdrawn from "east of Suez" after 1967, the pound devalued in 1967.

**Australian demographic change**: Post-war immigration from non-British Europe, then from Asia, diluted the British ethnic majority. The dismantling of the [[white-australia-policy]] (1960s–70s) followed.

**Economic diversification to Asia**: Japan surpassed Britain as Australia's largest export market in 1967. By the early twenty-first century "three-quarters of Australia's trade is in the region, with China, Japan and Korea the main partners; Britain accounts for just 4 per cent of imports and exports." (Bridge, in [[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]])

## Major Phases

### 1901–1919: The British World at Its Zenith
Australia federated within the Empire, not outside it. The [[commonwealth-of-australia]] was, in New Zealand historian James Belich's phrase, "a neo-Britain." Forty per cent of the first Australian Imperial Force at Gallipoli were British-born. At Versailles (1919) Hughes signed the treaty "under a British Empire indent and the legal 'High Contracting Party' was King George V, who formally agreed to the treaty on behalf of the whole British Empire." The "Britannic experiment" — Hughes' and later Curtin's vision of coordinated imperial foreign, defence and trade policy — was emerging.

Yet the seeds of divergence were present. Hughes aggressively pursued Australian interests on reparations and the New Guinea mandate ("as essential to Australia as the Channel Ports were to England"), resisted the racial equality clause despite US opposition to it also, and tried to assert a distinct Australian voice on Pacific security.

### 1919–1939: The Britannic Experiment and Its Limits
The Balfour Declaration (1926) and Statute of Westminster (1931) acknowledged Dominion equality in law. Canada and South Africa moved quickly toward independence; Australia and New Zealand refused to ratify the Statute of Westminster, remaining automatically at war when Britain declared in 1939. The Ottawa Conference (1932) bound the Empire more tightly economically through "empire free trade" preferences — "even more Australian eggs went into the imperial basket" during the Depression.

Three revealing episodes illustrated the complex relationship:
- **Niemeyer mission (1930)**: A Bank of England mandarin visiting Australia to impose financial austerity exposed the dependent colonial economy beneath the rhetoric of equality.
- **Bodyline cricket (1932–33)**: England's unsporting tactics to defeat Bradman nearly ruptured the cricketing relationship but ultimately resolved by mutual accommodation.
- **Abdication crisis (1936)**: Australian PM Lyons and High Commissioner Bruce were among those most opposed to Edward VIII's marriage, showing deep investment in the symbolic monarchy.

### 1939–1956: World War II and Qualified Continuity
Despite Curtin's dramatic "look to America" statement (December 1941), Australia's closeness with Britain "did not unravel with World War 2 or quickly thereafter." After the war, Chifley gifted £45 million to Britain and Australia remained within the sterling bloc. Britain tested its nuclear bomb at Maralinga (1952–57) and missiles at Woomera with Australian knowledge and acquiescence. The Royal Tour by Queen Elizabeth II in 1954 "saw the largest turnouts for any public events ever across the country."

H.V. Evatt's energetic External Affairs ministry (1941–49) established a proper diplomatic network, reducing reliance on British embassies — by 1949 Australia had missions in 17 countries, against only 4 before the war.

### 1956–1972: The Unravelling
The "wholesale slaughter of sacred cows" (as *The Times* editorialised) gathered pace in this period:
- Britain's EEC bids (1963, 1967, 1971 — last successful) redirected British trade and signalled European identity
- Britain's defence withdrawal from "east of Suez" confirmed after 1967
- British New Immigration Acts (1962, 1971) reclassified Australians as "aliens"
- When Britain devalued the pound in 1967, Australia did not follow — "The sterling bloc was dead; Australia was now a dollar economy"
- Japan became Australia's leading export partner in 1967; the US its major import partner

ANZUS (1951) had already been established without Britain. Australia's entry into Vietnam (1965) was not a British commitment. A visionary trade treaty with Japan (1957) had already begun trade diversification.

### 1972–1986: Post-Imperial Adjustments under Whitlam and Successors
Whitlam's Labor government actively dismantled the remaining "relics of colonialism": the national anthem was changed (1974), Australian honours replaced imperial ones (1975), the Privy Council appeals at federal level abolished (though States retained appeal rights until the Australia Acts 1986). Whitlam declared these changes "in no way anti-British…simply pro-Australian." The Australia Acts (1986) under Hawke finally removed the last constitutional anomalies.

Paradoxically, as material ties weakened, personal movement intensified. Short-term Australian visitors to Britain grew from 5,000 annually interwar to 250,000 by 1968; by the early 2000s the permanent Australian community in Britain numbered about 300,000. Australian cultural figures — Germaine Greer, Barry Humphries, Clive James, Rolf Harris — "assaulted the citadel of Britain's cultural establishment in the 1960s."

### 1986–present: The Commonwealth as One Forum Among Many
The [[british-commonwealth]] was "de-Britannicised" in 1965 when India and other republics had been accommodated. From being the primary framework of Australian diplomacy, it became "one of a number of useful platforms for dealing with global and regional issues." Australia used Commonwealth forums to oppose apartheid in South Africa (joining with India and other states against Thatcher's Britain) and to support democratic transitions in Zimbabwe and Fiji. Australia and New Zealand cooperated through Commonwealth frameworks in the Solomon Islands and Bougainville.

Relations with individual Commonwealth members diversified: the India relationship grew from near-hostility (1950s–60s) to a major partnership by the 2000s; the Pakistani relationship oscillated over terrorism and governance; Malaysia clashed with Australia on economic reform and capital punishment.

## Geographic Spread

The process was global in reach — the British Empire and then the Commonwealth spanning every continent. Its Australian dimension was most intense in the triangular relationship between Canberra, London, and Washington.

## End Conditions

The process is substantially complete in structural terms — constitutional, economic, and strategic dependence all severed. Residual bonds (shared monarchy for now, language, legal traditions, people movement, cultural affinity, intelligence sharing) persist but are qualitatively different from the colonial-era relationship.

## Historiography

Carl Bridge's chapter in [[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]] draws on:
- W.K. Hancock, *Australia* (1930) — "independent Australian Britons"
- John Darwin, *The Empire Project* (2010) — "Britannic experiment"
- James Curran and Stuart Ward, *The Unknown Nation* (2010) — Australia's disorientation after empire
- David Goldsworthy, *Losing the Blanket* (2002) — end of British Empire and Australia
- Andrea Benvenuti, *Anglo-Australian Relations and the 'Turn to Europe'* (2008)

The key historiographical shift has been away from a nationalist narrative in which Britain "abandoned" Australia in 1941–42 and toward a more nuanced view of slow, multi-decade transition in which British frameworks persisted decades longer than the rhetoric of independence implied.
