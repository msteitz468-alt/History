# Environmental Transformation in Colonial Australia

## Definition and Scope

The large-scale restructuring of Australian ecosystems between British colonisation (1788) and Federation (1901), driven by pastoral expansion, mining, agricultural selection, introduced species, and the deliberate suppression of Aboriginal land-management practices. The process encompasses the marine environment (whaling and sealing), terrestrial environments (clearing, grazing, cropping), inland waters (irrigation, artesian bore extraction), and urban environments (sanitation, water supply, suburban sprawl). It is inseparable from [[settler-colonialism]]: the ideological premise that Indigenous people did not possess the land underwrote nearly every destructive intervention.

The chapter author (Gaynor) frames the whole process around Alfred Crosby's concept of "portmanteau biota" — the suite of "germs, plants and domestic animals" that European colonisers carried and which "helps to explain why populations of colonial Europeans increased so dramatically, while those of native peoples crashed."

## Causal Drivers

**Pre-colonial baseline.** Aboriginal Australians had managed the landscape through fire for tens of thousands of years, producing the "grassy woodland landscapes" that early European settlers likened to "a nobleman's park." The collapse of this fire-management regime — caused by Aboriginal dispossession and death — was itself a primary driver of subsequent vegetation change: Thomas Mitchell noted by 1848 that "thick forests of young trees" had grown where "a man might gallop without impediment."

**Enlightenment ideology of improvement.** Colonial officials and free settlers understood antipodean nature as "raw material for improvement: a brute wilderness waiting to be transformed into congenial, 'civilised' surrounds." This drove clearing, cultivation, and suburban expansion from the outset.

**Pastoral expansion.** [[australian-pastoral-expansion|Sheep pastoralism]] was the dominant engine of early environmental change. Sheep were described as "the shocktroops of land seizure." They eroded creek banks, fouled water sources, ate out native grasses, and left soils open to weed invasion and wind erosion. Formal fencing from the 1850s reduced the mobility that had previously offered some protection against overgrazing. By the 1880s environmental degradation from overstocking was "widely acknowledged, though indebtedness saw it widely practised."

**Introduced species.** The [[acclimatisation-societies|acclimatisation movement]] deliberately introduced European flora and fauna. Thomas Austin's 1859 release of 24 wild rabbits near Geelong set off a spread "faster than any other introduced mammal anywhere in the world." By 1930 the historian W.K. Hancock judged that the rabbit had "made new deserts." Foxes, released for sport in the mid-1870s, spread across south-eastern Australia and joined rabbits and cats in devastating native fauna. Feral cats reached 90 per cent of the mainland by 1890.

**Gold mining.** The gold rushes (1850s Victoria, 1890s Western Australia) generated two waves of environmental devastation: alluvial mining annihilated stream-side vegetation and muddied waterways; deep mining introduced mercury and arsenic contamination; and the wood-fuel demands of mining stripped woodland for many miles around Kalgoorlie, causing "frequent dust storms."

**Land selection legislation.** Victorian Land Acts of the 1860s and the Land Act of 1869 (offering 320 acres at £1/acre over ten years) triggered "a frenzy of clearing, ringbarking and fencing." In South Australia, government release of land beyond "Goyder's line" (the surveyor-general's 1865 rainfall boundary) led to mass farm abandonment when dry seasons returned. In South Gippsland, over 180,000 hectares were cleared by the 1920s and no fewer than 125,450 of them were "abandoned or overgrown."

## Major Phases

**Phase 1 — Maritime extraction (1788–c.1840).** Whaling and sealing provided the first export staple industries. By 1839 alone, Van Diemen's Land whalers took more than 1,000 southern right whales in a single season. Between 1828 and 1850 more than 11,700 southern right whales were taken in eastern Australian waters. At least 317,000 fur seals were taken from southern Australia before 1825; Bass Strait seal populations were "totally annihilated" by the early 1810s. The last locally recorded right whale was caught in Western Australia in 1866. Southern right whale populations have still not re-established their pre-whaling distribution.

**Phase 2 — Pastoral frontier (c.1820–1860).** Sheep numbers in Van Diemen's Land reached 172,000 by 1819. Pastoral squatting pushed 500 km from Sydney by 1835. In Queensland, artesian bore water (reaching 512 m depth in 1886) extended grazing into arid Aboriginal country "previously considered too arid for stock."

**Phase 3 — Gold rush and mining (1851–c.1900).** Victoria by 1861 was producing one-third of the world's gold; 46 per cent of Australia's non-Indigenous population lived there. Melbourne's Yarra River became "a fetid drain, carrying sewage and industrial effluent slowly to the sea." The 1890s Western Australian rushes stripped Kalgoorlie's woodlands bare.

**Phase 4 — Agricultural selection and soil exhaustion (1860s–1900).** Small farmers practised "mining the soil" — exploiting fertility until yields declined, then moving on. By the century's end, superphosphate, new fallowing methods, and mechanisation began to stabilise agriculture, shifting it toward capital-intensive, export-oriented holdings.

**Phase 5 — Water management and irrigation (1880s–1900).** El Niño droughts of 1877 and 1880–81 sparked an irrigation movement. Alfred Deakin's 1886 Irrigation Act (modelled on California) and the Chaffey brothers' colonies at Mildura introduced large-scale river management, but early ventures ended in bankruptcy and salt emergence.

**Phase 6 — Early conservation responses (1860s–1900).** George Perkins Marsh's *Man and Nature* (1864) stimulated colonial debate on forest–climate–water linkages. Victoria called for forest reserves in 1865; South Australia created a Forest Board in 1875. Royal National Park was declared near Sydney in 1879. Despite such measures, licensing systems "failed to discourage wasteful and excessive exploitation."

## Geographic Spread

The process unfolded sequentially: coastal fisheries (Bass Strait, Tasmanian estuaries) first; then the Cumberland Plain and south-east mainland from 1813; Van Diemen's Land grasslands from the 1820s; South Australia from the 1830s; Victoria's goldfields from 1851; inland Queensland enabled by artesian water from the 1880s; and finally Western Australia's goldfields in the 1890s. Urban environmental degradation tracked the growth of Sydney, Melbourne, and colonial capitals from the 1850s. The process was essentially continent-wide by 1901.

## Interaction

- **contributed_to:** [[aboriginal-australians|Aboriginal demographic collapse]] — grazing animals destroyed native food sources (e.g. murnong/yam daisy in Port Phillip), while soil and water degradation reduced the resource base supporting surviving communities.
- **caused_by:** [[settler-colonialism]] — legal premise of terra nullius underwrote land seizure.
- **concurrent_with:** [[colonial-australian-demography]] — population growth, mining booms, and urbanisation drove demand for resources.
- **produced:** [[acclimatisation-societies]] — acclimatisation movement formalised introduced-species policy.
- **analogous_to:** Environmental transformation in other settler-colonial contexts (New Zealand, North America), sharing Crosby's "portmanteau biota" dynamic.
- **part_of:** [[bashford-macintyre-cha-v1-2013|Cambridge History of Australia Vol. 1]] analytical framework on colonial environmental history.

## End Conditions

The process did not end at Federation (1901) but underwent a structural shift: farming became more capital-intensive and export-oriented, water management was centralised, and the conservation movement — though weak — had established the legislative precedent for reserves and national parks. The author notes that "aesthetic, utilitarian and ethical arguments for conservation were articulated at every stage" but "rarely acted upon with efficacy" due to beliefs about property rights and the material benefits most non-Indigenous Australians derived from resource exploitation. The transformation continued through the twentieth century, but the foundational ecological damage — whale and seal population crashes, soil exhaustion, rabbit and fox infestation, near-elimination of Aboriginal fire management — was complete by 1901.

## Historiography

**Key interpretive frames.** The chapter draws on Alfred Crosby's ecological imperialism framework (*Ecological Imperialism*, 1986; *Germs, Seeds and Animals*, 1994) and J.M. Powell's environmental management history. Stephen Pyne's *Burning Bush* (1992) is the primary authority on fire history.

**Fire debate.** Whether Aboriginal burning primarily "made" the landscape (Bill Gammage, Eric Rolls) or whether vegetation was shaped first by climate and soil, with colonisation's clearing as the decisive event (Butzer and Helgren 2005), remains contested. The chapter acknowledges both positions without adjudicating.

**Megafauna extinction.** The causes of the c. 46,000 BP extinction of 23 of 24 large land mammals (Aboriginal hunting vs. climate change) are described as "hotly debated" with "inconclusive" evidence.

**Collection coverage note.** [[bashford-macintyre-cha-v1-2013]] provides strong secondary coverage of colonial environmental history for the long-19th-century period in Australia. Coverage of pre-colonial and post-Federation environmental change is thinner in this volume.
