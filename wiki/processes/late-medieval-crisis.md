# The Late-Medieval Crisis (the "Calamitous Fourteenth Century")

The **late-medieval crisis** is the cluster of demographic, economic and social disasters that
"brought to an end three centuries of economic growth and cultural expansion in Christian Europe."
[[jones-ncmh-v6-2000|NCMH VI]] (Michael Jones, Introduction; Christiane Klapisch-Zuber, Paul
Freedman, Jean-Pierre Leguay) takes this as the defining frame of the fourteenth century — for one
contemporary, Filippo Villani, "this shipwreck of a century which is going from bad to worse."

## Definition and Scope

This page covers the **whole conjuncture** of fourteenth-century reversal: the end of the
[[medieval-agricultural-revolution|high-medieval boom]], the demographic collapse, and the social
transformations that followed. It is distinct from its single greatest component, the
**[[black-death|Black Death]]** (1347–1351), and from the political and religious crises
(the Hundred Years War, the [[papacy|Avignon papacy]] and the Great Schism) treated elsewhere in
the volume.

## Causal Drivers

- **The demographic ceiling.** By c. 1300 Europe's population "had attained its maximum and
  reached a ceiling" after almost three centuries of growth — England perhaps 5–6 million, France
  ~17–20 million, Italy ~10 million — pressing on the land's capacity (the **Neo-Malthusian**
  reading of M.M. Postan).
- **Famine and climate.** The **Great Famine of 1315–1322**, born of catastrophic weather,
  ravaged northern Europe (Germany, the Low Countries, England, half of France); recurrent
  shortages followed. In much of France, Italy and the Mediterranean **population was already
  stagnant or falling before 1347**.
- **The Black Death and recurrent plague.** The pandemic of 1347–1351 and its repeated returns
  (1361 and after) "stimulated profound upheavals in modes of production, living and feeling," and
  prevented recovery for generations.
- **War, taxation and money.** Endemic war (above all the Hundred Years War), heavy taxation, and
  coinage debasement "hampered commercial and manufacturing activity and exacerbated social
  tensions."

## Major Phases

1. **Signs of strain (c. 1300–1347).** The end of expansion, the failure of new town foundations,
   the Great Famine, regional decline.
2. **The mortality (1347–c. 1400).** The Black Death and its recurrences; demographic collapse.
3. **Adjustment (15th c.).** A smaller population on more land, with the social re-balancing below
   — feeding into the next NCMH volume.

## Interaction

The crisis interacted with everything: the **agrarian depression** ruined much of the aristocracy
as rents and grain prices fell; **labour scarcity raised real wages**, weakening lordship and
hastening the **decline of serfdom in the west** while the "**second serfdom**" tightened in the
east; the squeeze produced the great **peasant and urban revolts** (the Jacquerie 1358, the English
Rising of 1381, the Florentine Ciompi 1378); and the towns contracted, reversing the
[[urbanization|thirteenth-century urban apogee]]. It is the economic substrate of the century's
political turbulence and of the anxious turn in [[late-medieval-lay-religion|religious life]].

## End Conditions

The crisis did not "end" so much as resolve into a new equilibrium: a much-reduced population
enjoying higher per-capita resources, a transformed rural society, and (in the west) a freer
peasantry — the demographic and social baseline of the fifteenth-century recovery. By the century's
end population was rising again and many trends reversed (arable re-expanding, wages falling, grain
prices rising), though the large commercial holdings created by the crisis survived into the
sixteenth century.

## The Fifteenth-Century Countryside (NCMH VII)

[[allmand-ncmh-v7-1998|NCMH VII]] (Christopher Dyer, Rural Europe, Ch. 5) describes the **aftermath
and adjustment**: the fifteenth-century countryside was "more sparsely populated than at any time
since about 1150," population having fallen to a half or two-thirds of pre-plague levels and reaching
its nadir c. 1420–1450 (France ~18m→<10m; England ~6m→2.5–3m; the *contado* of Florence 300,000→
104,000). The consequences were a transformed landscape and social order:

- **Desertion and a new ecological balance.** Tens of thousands of settlements were abandoned
  (>40,000 of 170,000 in Germany; half of Norway's farms); arable reverted to **pasture** and
  woodland recovered, because a single herdsman could work land that tillage needed a dozen hands
  for. Diet shifted upward — ordinary people now ate **meat** in quantities once reserved for the
  aristocracy.
- **The "price scissors."** Grain prices and rents fell while **wages rose** and the prices of
  animal and manufactured goods held up, squeezing the **nobility** (who leased out demesnes and
  turned to office-holding) and improving conditions for much of the **peasantry** (lower rents,
  hereditary tenure, the end of many servile dues; the rise of the *gros fermiers* and English
  yeomen on enlarged holdings).
- **Dyer's critique of the "demographic crisis."** Dyer argues that demography, though real, "cannot
  explain all" of the period's changes. Regional experience diverged sharply — Castile and parts of
  eastern Europe escaped the worst, recovery came at different dates, and structural change (the
  market, lord–peasant bargaining, peasant decision-making, [[second-serfdom|eastern enserfment]])
  proceeded **independently of population levels**. Productivity per worker was high and the century
  was not exceptionally hungry (the great exception being the north-European **famine of 1437–9**).
- **Peasant assertiveness.** Fewer mass rebellions than the surrounding centuries, but constant
  negotiation, litigation and local agitation, and significant revolts: the Catalan **remences**
  against the "bad customs" (civil war 1462–72; abolished 1486), the German **Bundschuh** risings
  (1439, 1493), the Galician *Irmandiño* revolt (1467), and the Norman anti-tax rising (1434–6).

## Historiography

Freedman frames the central interpretive divide: whether the late-medieval transformation should
be related **to the [[black-death|plague]]** as a great exogenous accident, or **minimised**
in favour of **systemic crisis** already underway by 1300 (population outrunning resources,
frictions in the lord–peasant relation). The demographic-versus-systemic debate determines how
"organic" the connection is between the early-century strains and the late-century upheavals —
and how much causal weight a single pandemic can bear.
