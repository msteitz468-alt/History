# The Catholic Reformation

The renewal and self-reform of the Roman Catholic Church in the sixteenth and early seventeenth
centuries — at once a **response to the [[protestant-reformation-1517|Protestant Reformation]]** (the
"Counter-Reformation") and a **deeper internal revival** with late-medieval roots. It reshaped Catholic
doctrine, discipline, piety and reach, and carried Catholicism around the globe.

## Definition and Scope

Historians distinguish, and combine, two faces of the movement:
- a **"Counter-Reformation"** — the combative reaction against Protestantism (the Roman Inquisition, the
  Index of Forbidden Books, confessional warfare);
- a broader **"Catholic Reformation"** — a renewal of spirituality, learning and church order that **drew
  on pre-Reformation reform currents** (Observant movements, Christian humanism, Spanish mysticism) and
  would have proceeded even without Luther.

## Major Developments

- **The Council of Trent (1545–1563)** — the defining event: it reaffirmed contested doctrines (justification,
  the seven sacraments, the authority of Scripture *and* tradition, transubstantiation) against Protestant
  teaching, and enacted sweeping **disciplinary reform** — residence of bishops, seminaries to train a
  reformed **clergy**, control of abuses.
- **New religious orders** — above all the **Society of Jesus (Jesuits)**, founded by Ignatius of Loyola
  (1540), masters of education, mission and confession; with the Capuchins, Theatines, Ursulines, Oratorians
  and reformed Carmelites (Teresa of Ávila, John of the Cross).
- **A reformed piety and culture** — a renewed sacramental and devotional life, the catechism, and the
  emotional, persuasive art of the **Baroque** mobilized for the faith.
- **Global mission** — Catholic missionaries followed the Iberian empires to the **Americas, Africa, India,
  China and Japan**, making the Catholic Reformation the first **worldwide** Christian expansion.

## Interaction and End Conditions

The movement was entangled with the **confessional state** and with Europe's religious wars (the French
Wars of Religion, the Dutch Revolt, the **Thirty Years' War**), as Catholic monarchies (Spain, the
Habsburgs) made the renewed Church an instrument of power. By the mid-seventeenth century the
**confessional boundaries of Europe had stabilized**, and the militant phase gave way to a consolidated,
disciplined, globally extended Catholicism.

## The mission to Japan — and its destruction (Mason & Caiger)

The Catholic Reformation's **global mission** reached its most dramatic Asian success, and
sharpest reversal, in **[[japan|Japan]]**. Jesuits (from Francis Xavier, 1549) won
hundreds of thousands of converts, concentrated in northern **Kyūshū**, where the port of
**Nagasaki** was virtually governed by the Society and several daimyo were Christian. But
the religion's strength in a region far from the center alarmed the unifiers: **Hideyoshi**
issued an expulsion edict (1587) and executed 26 Christians (1597), and the **Tokugawa**
then mounted a full persecution — torture, forced apostasy, and thousands of martyrdoms —
culminating in the suppression of the **Shimabara rebellion (1637)** and the **[[sakoku]]**
closure that expelled the Iberians and banned Christianity for over two centuries. The
Japan mission is thus both the high-water mark of the Reformation-era Catholic outreach and
the clearest case of its **limits** where it met a strong, centralizing state. (See also
the parallel mission field of [[spiritual-conquest-and-colonial-church|colonial Latin
America]].)
