## Definition and Scope

Akbar's religious policy designates the ensemble of institutional, legislative, fiscal, and personal-devotional measures by which [[akbar]] (r. 1556–1605) constructed an interconfessional framework for Mughal rule and articulated an Islamic universalism that challenged both Shari'ah-minded communalism and Hindu caste exclusivism. It is distinct from [[mughal-hindu-muslim-coexistence]] (which is the broader structural process across the full Mughal period) and from [[din-i-ilahi]] (which is the specific spiritual movement formalized c.1582). Akbar's policy is the causal driver of both.

## Causal Drivers

### Personal religious trajectory
Akbar underwent a visible personal transformation from a young man who "rather liked bloodshed" to a ruler who gave up hunting, adopted a largely fleshless diet, and explored the spiritual traditions of every religion represented at his court. This trajectory was shaped by Sufi pirs — especially of the Chishti order — and by court intellectuals including Shaykh Mubarak and his sons Abulfazl and Faizi.

### Political logic of legitimation
Akbar's early effort to legitimate Mughal power via Shari'ah-grounded authority — personally leading the *salat* prayer as caliph-imam, imposing the jizyah briefly, and (in 1579) obtaining a declaration from the ulama that the just ruler could adjudicate between conflicting *fiqh* positions — revealed its own limits. The Shari'ah establishment was too independent; and a Shari'ah-only legitimation excluded the Hindu administrative and military elites on whom the empire increasingly relied. A more neutral or universalist legitimation was structurally advantageous.

### Revolt of 1580
A Muslim officers' rebellion in the Ganges region, "not without overtones of a Sharî'ah-minded criticism of Akbar," confirmed the limits of Shari'ah-based legitimation from a different angle — it showed that the Shari'ah-minded could become a center of political opposition. The shift in Akbar's policy was in part a response to this.

### Administrative integration of Rajputs
Akbar's incorporation of Rajput chieftains — through political alliance, intermarriage (Hindu Rajput princesses married into the imperial family), and military appointments within the mansabdari system — created stakeholders in the Mughal order who were Hindu. Any coherent imperial ideology had to accommodate their dignity.

## Major Phases

### Phase 1 (1556–c.1575): Pragmatic accommodation
Akbar's early reforms were less Shari'ah-oriented than typical Muslim royal inaugurations. He abolished certain non-Shari'ah trade taxes but also "forbade the unquestionable jizyah tax on dhimmî non-Muslims." His Rajput alliance deepened. He was "very solicitous of the Shar'î 'ulamâ' scholars, giving them many grants" while simultaneously removing a powerful *sadr* (religious administrator) to reassert royal control over religious patronage. This phase combined real piety with political calculation.

### Phase 2 (c.1575–c.1582): Systematic interconfessional inquiry
Akbar organized the *ibadat-khana* (house of worship) at Fatehpur Sikri beginning in 1575, initially for Muslim scholars of different viewpoints, then expanded to "scholars representing all known religious traditions." He listened to Jesuits, Jains, Hindus, Parsis, and representatives of various Muslim schools. The sessions often became acrimonious, and Akbar "patiently continued the sessions for years, but eventually closed the 'house of worship'."

In 1579 he obtained the controversial declaration (*mahzar*) from leading ulama recognizing the emperor as the arbiter of conflicting legal opinions. Hodgson reads this as an attempt to "assimilate the Shari'ah into agrarian absolutism" — an approach Akbar soon abandoned as the Shari'ah establishment resisted.

### Phase 3 (c.1582–1605): Institutionalized universalism and the tawhid-e ilahi circle
Akbar formalized a personal spiritual movement (*tawhid-e ilahi*, later called [[din-i-ilahi]]) — structured as a Sufi *tariqa* with himself as *pir*. Initiates were few, overwhelmingly Muslim, and received no preferential treatment in imperial appointments. Simultaneously, Akbar pursued a wide range of social reforms driven by "respect for the dignity of living beings":
- Discouragement of child marriage
- Prohibition of *sati* (widow-burning) unless clearly voluntary
- Permission for Hindu widows to remarry
- Ban on forcible conversion of Hindu girls to enable marriage to Muslim men
- Discouragement of second marriages
- Hospital and caravanserai construction along imperial highways
- Animal welfare measures including restriction on slaughter on his birthday and in Hindu sacred areas

Hodgson is careful to note: "It must be noted that his moral reforms applied primarily only to city life in any case; often, in effect, only to the notables or to Delhi and Agra, the capitals."

## Intellectual Architecture: Abulfazl's Akbar-Namah

The *Akbar-Namah* by Abulfazl Allami (1551–1602) is the fullest intellectual expression of Akbar's policy. Abulfazl articulated the ideology of *sulh-e kull* ("universal conciliation"): "such generosity and forgiveness to all that no occasion was left for dissension. The great error was bigotry: to reject a human achievement because its maker was of the wrong allegiance is contrary to the good sense of the civilized man." Islam appears in the *Akbar-Namah* as "Muammad's religion" — one path among several, distinguished from *islam* in its original Quranic sense as submission to God, "which may be entered upon in every tradition."

Abulfazl presented Akbar simultaneously as the ideal philosopher-king of the Falsafah tradition and as the Sufi "perfect man" (*qutb*) — the spiritual pole around whom the world revolves. "In reverencing him, as a *murîd* reverences his Sûfî *pîr*, one is led to spiritual growth toward the perfection he embodies." This dual framing anchored a legitimation that was at once philosophical, spiritual, and political.

## Opposition: Ahmad Sirhindi

The most significant systematic opposition to Akbar's policy came from Ahmad Sirhindi (d. 1624), called "Mujaddid-e Alf-e Sani" (Renovator of the Second Muslim Millennium). Sirhindi did not attack Sufism from the outside; rather, he "waged his fight within the forms of Sufism itself." He attacked the monist doctrine of *wahdat al-wujud* ("oneness by existence") as only one stage in the mystical path, proclaiming *wahdat al-shuhud* ("oneness by witness") as the higher realization — one that restored the ultimate distinction between God and creation and thus the paramount authority of external Shari'ah over inner spiritual experience.

Akbar's successor Jahangir imprisoned Sirhindi, then released him. His influence at the time was "slight," but the Mujaddidi branch of the Naqshbandi order he founded became a major force in later Islamic revivalism across the Mughal empire and beyond.

## Relationship to Aurangzeb's Reversal

Aurangzeb explicitly "assimilated [some of Akbar's special practices] to the practices of a Sûfî pîr" when justifying their continuation — indicating that the practices were not understood even by critics as constituting a "new religion." The reversal under Aurangzeb was not doctrinal repudiation but political and communalist: from 1679, Aurangzeb systematically reimposed the dhimmi disabilities that Akbar had removed, culminating in the jizyah reimposition. The contrast between the two emperors became, as Hodgson notes, the central organizing controversy of both British-colonial and post-independence historiography of the Mughal period.

## Historiography

Hodgson (in [[hodgson-venture-of-islam-v3-1974]]) insists on reading Akbar's policy as internally intelligible within Islamicate thought — not as proto-secularism or Hindu-Muslim syncretic sentimentalism. "The universalist sort of cultural and moral life which Akbar fostered... was not in itself inconsistent with Islam. Indeed, it was cast in Islamicate terms." The critics who accused Akbar of apostasy "lumped together different sorts of acts on Akbar's part, of a more or less religious cast" without distinguishing the political (control of religious appointments), the tolerant (allowing non-Muslim practices), and the personal-spiritual (the *tawhid-e ilahi* circle).

The primary source is Abulfazl's *Akbar-Namah*; Hodgson notes that "the translations are unsatisfactory." The hostile eyewitness source is Badaoni's court history, which Hodgson characterizes as systematically distorted. Sri Ram Sharma, *The Religious Policy of the Mughal Emperors* (1940), is cited as an accessible secondary account.
