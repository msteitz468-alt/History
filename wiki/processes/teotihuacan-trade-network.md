## Definition and Scope

Teotihuacan's long-distance trade network was a complex system of exchange, political projection, and resource procurement that connected the city to regions spanning more than a thousand kilometers — from Pacific coastal Guatemala in the south to western Mexico in the northwest and the Maya Lowlands in the east. The network was neither a simple free market nor a top-down state monopoly; different commodities, directions, and partners operated under different institutional arrangements, ranging from direct state procurement to independent elite exchange to household-level craft export.

The period of maximum reach was roughly 250–550 CE (Tlamimilolpa through Xolalpan phases), coinciding with the city's demographic and political peak.

## Obsidian: Pachuca Green Obsidian

The most important export in which Teotihuacan held a distinctive position was Pachuca green obsidian from the Sierra de Las Navajas, approximately 55 km northeast of [[teotihuacan]]. Pachuca obsidian is "relatively free of imperfections and is especially good for making prismatic blades — long narrow and thin flakes whose nearly parallel edges are more than razor-sharp" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 7). Its NAA (neutron activation analysis) signature is unmistakable. "Throughout pre-Hispanic times, it was the most widely exported obsidian in Mesoamerica" (Ch. 7).

Alejandro Pastrana (2009) describes small Teotihuacan settlements near the Pachuca source, with ceramics ranging from Patlachique through Xolalpan phases, indicating long occupation. Craftsmen at the source produced both partially finished objects for export and a range of finished objects. Cowgill suspects the Teotihuacan state or a powerful group within it "controlled access to the Pachuca source and probably also supervised its mining. They may then have sold it directly to crafting households in the city, or perhaps they sold it in bulk to middlemen who then sold smaller amounts to artisans" (Ch. 7).

Crucially, Cowgill rejects Santley's (1983) claim of a Teotihuacan monopoly over Mesoamerican obsidian trade: "many obsidian source regions apparently remained politically independent of Teotihuacan." Obsidian from the Oyameles/Zaragosa source near Cantona — about 165 km east of Teotihuacan — remained outside Teotihuacan control, and in the Mixtequilla of southern Veracruz, over 90% of obsidian came from that source rather than Pachuca. "It is unlikely that obsidian export played a major commercial role for the Teotihuacan state" overall (Ch. 7).

The Teotihuacan state produced obsidian dart points and symbolic objects in precinct workshops near the Moon Pyramid for military and ideological purposes — a distinct use from commercial export.

## Thin Orange Ware: The Major Import

Thin Orange Ware was [[teotihuacan]]'s most abundant import. Rattray (1990b) identified its place of manufacture near the Río Carnero in southern Puebla, approximately 200 km from Teotihuacan. It constituted about 10% of all vessels consumed at Teotihuacan — an extraordinary volume for an imported ware. It is primarily hemispherical bowls, functionally replacing incised molcajete bowls in household cuisine.

The question of what Teotihuacan offered in exchange for Thin Orange Ware is unresolved. Rattray found no evidence of a Teotihuacan colony in the source area. Green obsidian was found at source sites but possibly not in quantities sufficient to pay for all pottery received. "Perhaps further research in the source area will discover a Teotihuacan presence, or possibly the mere threat of a punitive raid by Teotihuacanos was enough to motivate makers to export it to Teotihuacan as a kind of 'protection money'" (Ch. 7).

Thin Orange's presence at sites outside Teotihuacan has often been taken as a marker of Teotihuacan influence, but Cowgill cautions against this: the ware was probably exported directly by its makers in southern Puebla, not redistributed by Teotihuacan. "The mere presence of Thin Orange ceramics cannot be taken as a secure marker of a Teotihuacan connection" (Ch. 7).

## Other Imported Ceramics

**Granular Ware**: Imported from Guerrero/Morelos, primarily as amphoras (tall necked jars suitable for liquids), about 12,600 TMP sherds. Abundant in Miccaotli and Tlamimilolpa phases; declining through Xolalpan; possibly ceased in Metepec.

**Lustrous Ware**: Probably from several sources in the Gulf Lowlands, perhaps northern Veracruz; up to 1–2% of ceramics in TMP excavations. Fine serving vessels including cylinder vases; locally imitated.

**Foreign ceramics in small quantities** from West Mexico, Oaxaca, the Gulf Lowlands, and the Maya area are scattered throughout the city, with concentrations in ethnic enclaves.

## Presence at Maya Sites

Teotihuacan's engagement with the Classic Maya world was extensive and varied. Key sites:

**Tikal**: Ceramic evidence shows "long-lasting relations with Tikal" — 10 Maya sherds from Teotihuacan compositionally similar to Tikal specimens; figures in military garb on monuments. Rulers at Tikal "proclaimed Teotihuacan connections in their regalia and in hieroglyphic inscriptions, and they founded new dynasties that soon returned to Maya styles" (Ch. 7). Teotihuacan-related individuals apparently intervened directly in Tikal's politics in the Early Classic.

**Copán**: The Copán dynastic founder carried symbolic ties to Teotihuacan; the "Dazzler" cylinder vase from the Margarita tomb is probably an import from Teotihuacan itself. However, stable isotope analysis suggests the founder was from the central Petén region rather than Teotihuacan directly.

**Kaminaljuyú**: Elite tombs in two mounds built in Teotihuacan style contained substantial offerings in Teotihuacan style, some imported from Central Mexico.

**Montana (Pacific Guatemala)**: A large settlement of probable descendants of Teotihuacan migrants, producing locally-made Teotihuacan-style objects. Pachuca obsidian and direct imports are scarce, suggesting the colony had become self-sufficient.

Stable isotope analyses of teeth and bones in Maya area burials indicate "few, if any, known burials in the Maya area were of persons who lived for any significant time at Teotihuacan" (Ch. 7) — pointing to a model of Teotihuacan-descent or Teotihuacan-affiliate migrants rather than direct administrative deployment from the city.

## Gulf Coast Contacts

At **Matacapan** in the southern Gulf Lowlands, locally made twin-chambered candeleros (otherwise extremely rare outside Teotihuacan) indicate a Teotihuacan-related center, though its sphere of influence was limited. In the northern Gulf Lowlands, Lustrous Ware probably came from this general area. The presence of Teotihuacan-style imagery at Cerro de las Mesas (stela with possible Storm God imagery) suggests at least some Teotihuacan military presence in south-central Veracruz.

## Oaxaca

The Zapotec state centered at Monte Albán "seems to have enjoyed commercial and diplomatic relations with Teotihuacan on an equal footing" (Ch. 7) — a rare case of peer interaction rather than Teotihuacan dominance. The Oaxacan Enclave within Teotihuacan may represent the residential community of Zapotec merchants or diplomats operating under this arrangement. Teotihuacanos may also have traded more directly with people in the Río Verde area of Pacific coastal Oaxaca, bypassing Monte Albán.

## West Mexico

Signs of Teotihuacan connections in Michoacán, Querétaro, and Guanajuato include murals in Teotihuacan style at El Rosario (Querétaro), suggesting a probable Teotihuacan settlement on a northwestern trade route. The Mezcala region of Guerrero provided greenstone figurines to Teotihuacan; the Ucareo district of Michoacán provided obsidian as well as, later, probable Coyotlatelco ceramic antecedents.

## Long-Distance Prestige Items

Smaller quantities of materials from up to a thousand kilometers away were obtained, mostly through elite exchange: greenstone (fuchsite/serpentine from Guerrero), jadeite (from Motagua River sources, Guatemala), marine shells from both Pacific and Gulf coasts, mica (probably from Oaxaca), cinnabar, hematite, pyrites, and exotic bird feathers. Cacao was "likely seen as a preciosity, but sufficiently in demand that it may have led to political control of cacao-growing areas by Teotihuacanos, including Pacific coastal Chiapas and Guatemala" (Ch. 7).

## Nature of Control vs. Exchange

Cowgill's assessment is deliberately nuanced. Teotihuacan probably exercised direct state control over the Pachuca obsidian source and possibly cacao-producing coastal regions. Relations with the Zapotec state were peer commercial/diplomatic. Interactions with Tikal and Copán involved military intervention and dynastic manipulation. Relations with southern Puebla (Thin Orange Ware source) remain puzzling — possibly coercive, possibly commercial. Most long-distance prestige goods moved through elite exchange networks with minimal state involvement. "Relations abroad of both the Teotihuacan state and of individuals claiming Teotihuacan identity were quite different in different regions and in different periods" (Ch. 7).

## End Conditions

Teotihuacan's trade network contracted before and collapsed with the state. By the Metepec phase (550–650 CE), locally-made substitutes for Thin Orange Ware appeared — "poor makeshifts for the genuine article" — suggesting foreign goods were becoming harder to obtain. The destruction of the city around 600–650 CE ended the institutional framework that had organized exchange, though some patterns of Mesoamerican trade continued in altered form in the Epiclassic period.
