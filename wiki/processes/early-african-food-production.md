## Definition and Scope

This process page documents the **independent African origins of food production**,
as reconstructed by Ehret (CWH Vol. I, Ch. 15). It is a direct rebuttal of the
older view that agriculture diffused into Africa from the Near East. Ehret's
closing thesis: "Africa at the beginning of the Holocene was not a place apart.
The same trajectories of human change were emerging in Africa as in several other
parts of the world — toward agricultural ways of life and, much farther off in
time, toward more complex and more unequal societies."

This is foundational for filling the collection's flagged critical gap on
Sub-Saharan Africa. It will be substantially expanded when CWH Vol. 2 (*A World
with Agriculture*) is ingested.

## Three Independent Pathways

### 1. Afrasian wild-grain collecting (Horn of Africa → Egypt, from ~15,000 BCE)
Wild grains were "an old feature specifically of the earliest Afrasian economies."
The reconstructed proto-Afrasian subsistence lexicon reveals reliance on wild
grains as a key food source from the family's beginning, including ancient root
words for grains, for *grinding grain into flour*, and for *making flat breads*
from it. (Crucially, the linguistic evidence shows the proto-Afrasians were
*collectors* of wild grains, **not** farmers or herders — cultivation and herding
lexicons developed only later and independently in each sub-branch.) The ability
to exploit expanding Ethiopian grasslands during the LGM may explain the growth
and spread of early Afrasian communities. Afrasian (North Erythraic) speakers
carried wild-grain collecting north to Upper Egypt ~15,000 BCE (the Afian and
Qadan cultures), corroborated by Y-chromosome evidence of movement from the Horn
into Egypt in the same time frame.

### 2. Nilo-Saharan cattle herding (eastern Sahara, 9th–8th millennium BCE)
In the eastern Sahara, **Nilo-Saharan peoples initiated the earliest herding of
cattle in world history** in the 9th–8th millennium BCE — and probably not much
later began pre-domestication cultivation (likely gourds and melons first, then
grains). This is among the most significant claims in the volume for world
history: a major domestication event of fully independent African origin,
predating or paralleling Near Eastern developments.

### 3. Niger-Congo pre-domestication cultivation (West Africa, before ~9500 BCE)
Western Niger-Kordofanian (proto-Niger-Congo) communities, settled in well-watered
savanna/woodland zones, faced the drying of the Younger Dryas (~10,800 BCE). One
group responded with two mutually reinforcing innovations:
- Harvesting wild-grain species such as **fonio** that flourished in steppe
  grasslands after the rains
- **Independently inventing ceramic technology**, fashioning pots in which to cook
  their grains whole

Several verb roots for the *deliberate tending and protecting of food plants*
reconstruct to early Niger-Congo (one possibly to proto-Niger-Congo itself),
indicating **pre-domestication cultivation** was underway by the 10th–9th
millennium BCE. The earliest direct testimony comes from the **Bandiagara
escarpment (Mali)**, sites dating to before 9400 BCE, with phytoliths in a
multiproxy context.

## Expansion and Crop Domestication to 500 CE (CWH Vol. II — Lane, Ch. 18)

The archaeological view (Paul Lane) complements Ehret's linguistic reconstruction
and confirms the headline pattern: in sub-Saharan Africa, **the adoption of
domestic animals typically *preceded* the adoption of food crops** — the reverse
of most other world regions. The development and spread of farming/herding were
"long drawn-out and uneven processes spanning up to five millennia with many
stops and starts."

- **Multiple independent crop domestications**, adapted to distinctively African
  ecologies: **pearl millet** and **sorghum** in the Sahel; **African rice**
  (*Oryza glaberrima*), **cowpea**, **groundnut**, and **fonio** in West Africa;
  **tef** in the Ethiopian highlands; **oil palm** and the incense tree
  **Canarium** managed through long Holocene arboriculture (echoing island
  Southeast Asian vegeculture — see [[early-agriculture-southeast-asia-pacific]]).
- **Pearl millet** was cultivated widely across the Sahel by ~1500 BCE; it appears
  in **Kintampo** (Ghana) contexts and across northeastern Nigeria.
- **Northeastern Africa as crossroads**: the Nile valley's proximity to Saharan
  and West Asian domesticates made it a conduit; Sahelian cattle were herded by
  ~6000 BCE (see pathway 2 above).
- **Crossbred cattle** (African taurine × South Asian zebu) and the two-way
  **exchange of domesticates** across the Indian Ocean and (later, via slavery)
  the Atlantic mark Africa as both recipient and donor in global crop history.
- By **500–700 CE** hunter-gatherers, farmers, and pastoralists coexisted across
  Central Africa; domesticates reached the **Southern Cape ~2,000 years ago**.

Lane stresses that Africa's low standing in origins research reflects a *presumed*
(and increasingly questioned) absence of "primary" domestication centres south of
the Sahara — a bias the volume actively corrects, and one the wiki flags per
CLAUDE.md's collection-bias mandate.

## The Bantu question

A central debate: was farming carried south by migrating **[[bantu-expansion|Bantu]]**-speaking
farmer-herders in a relatively straightforward migration, or by more complex
processes of assimilation and exchange (paralleling the
[[austronesian-dispersal]] debate for the Pacific)? Linguistics, archaeology, and
[[archaeogenetics]] now point to **assimilation-heavy** spread rather than simple
replacement. See [[bantu-expansion]].

## Case study: the Tichitt tradition (Dhar Tichitt, Mauritania — MacDonald, Ch. 19)

Long held up as a *pristine* locus of Sahelian millet domestication, **Dhar
Tichitt** has been **reinterpreted**: domestic pearl millet is present from the
earliest inhabitants (and earlier still at sites to the south in Mali/Ghana,
~2500–1900 BCE), and the faunal record shows dispersed hunter-gatherers plus
**pastoralists** rather than a single domestication event driven by drying
climate. The old "decreasing-rainfall-forced-intensification" story does not hold.

At its apogee (~1600–1000 BCE), Tichitt is identified as **West Africa's first
large-scale complex society** — though MacDonald, following Africanist scepticism
of imported "chiefdom"/"state" labels, prefers to describe its features: a strong
**settlement hierarchy** along the Tichitt–Walata escarpments; massive
**stone-walled settlements** (apparently demarcating *lineage* space, not for
defence) with large cattle **kraals**; evidence of **competition for cattle
wealth and territory**; and ritual concentration (hundreds of tumuli at Dhar
Tichitt). The Soninke founders of the later empire of **Ghana/Wagadu** are
plausibly descendants of the **Tichitt diaspora**, linked by distinctive
cord-roulette ("Faïta") ceramics reaching the middle Niger — making Tichitt an
ancestor of West African urbanism and statehood.

## Causal Drivers

- **Climate**: The Younger Dryas (~10,800 BCE) drove Western Niger-Congo peoples to
  adapt to drier conditions; the Holocene warming (~9600 BCE) then advanced steppe
  and dry savanna northward, and these grain-collecting peoples followed.
- **Wild-grass abundance**: expanding savanna/steppe grasslands offered exploitable
  wild grains (fonio, wild wheat/barley relatives, sorghum ancestors).
- **Linguistic-cultural inheritance**: each language family carried distinct
  subsistence knowledge (see [[african-language-family-origins]]).

## Significance

- Establishes Africa as one of the **independent centres of food-production
  origins**, alongside Southwest Asia, China, Mesoamerica, and the Andes.
- The eastern Saharan cattle claim makes Africa a candidate for the *earliest*
  pastoralism globally.
- Demonstrates that African deep history is recoverable and globally significant —
  directly countering [[eurocentrism]] and the collection's African gap.

## Limitations

- Much rests on Ehret's historical-linguistic reconstructions, which are contested
  (see [[african-language-family-origins]]).
- Direct archaeological evidence for the earliest cattle herding and pre-
  domestication cultivation is limited and its dating debated.
- The independence of African cattle domestication from Near Eastern cattle (vs.
  introgression of Near Eastern taurine cattle) is an active research question not
  fully resolved here.
