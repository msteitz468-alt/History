## Definition and Scope

The mobilization of an entire society's manpower, industry, and civil institutions in support of a war effort, blurring the traditional distinction between combatant and civilian and between the front line and the home economy. Distinct from earlier limited warfare in which fighting was conducted by relatively small professional or conscript forces with comparatively modest disruption to civilian economic life.

## Causal Drivers

The unprecedented scale and duration of the First World War forced belligerent states to convert civilian industry to munitions production, ration consumer goods, and mobilize female labor on a mass scale to replace conscripted men; the Second World War extended these practices further, adding large-scale strategic bombing of enemy industrial capacity as a deliberate instrument of total war.

## Major Phases

First World War mobilization (1914–1918), pioneering state economic planning (e.g., Germany's Hindenburg Program, British Ministry of Munitions); interwar retrenchment; and Second World War mobilization (1939–1945), which reached even higher levels of societal commitment, exemplified by German, Soviet, British, and American war-production economies and by the deliberate targeting of enemy [[concepts/total-war-economy|war economies]] through strategic bombing.

## Geographic Spread

Most fully realized among the major industrial belligerents of both world wars (Germany, Britain, the Soviet Union, the United States, Japan); less completely achieved in less industrialized combatants.

## Interaction

Closely linked to the rise of [[concepts/totalitarianism|totalitarian]] state structures, which found total-war mobilization congenial to their existing emphasis on state control of economy and society, though democracies also mobilized totally during the world wars without adopting totalitarian governance.

## End Conditions

Total-war mobilization wound down rapidly after 1945 demobilization in the Western democracies, though Cold War-era defense-industrial complexes retained some of its institutional legacy (permanent large defense industries, continued state-economy coordination).
