# The Southern African Mineral Revolution

The **discovery of diamonds at Kimberley (1867–71)** and of **gold on the Witwatersrand (1885)** transformed
southern Africa from a pastoral backwater into the industrial heart of the continent — and, in
**[[reader-africa-biography|Reader's]]** argument, **built the institutional machinery of racial
domination** that would culminate in apartheid. It inaugurated the modern era of African history.

## Diamonds: Kimberley

A child's plaything on a Vaal-river farm (1867) and the 83-carat "Star of Africa" (1869) set off a rush; by
1871 the dry "diggings" had revealed the world's first known **diamond pipes** (kimberlite). Within a year
**50,000 people** lived at Kimberley — more than Cape Town. The British promptly **annexed** the
diamond-fields (Griqualand West, 1871). Reader traces how the early egalitarian claim-system gave way, after
the 1876 repeal of the claim limit, to **amalgamation and monopoly**: by 1888 **Cecil Rhodes's De Beers
Consolidated** controlled Kimberley production — a company whose trust deed empowered it to annex land and
maintain armies anywhere in Africa.

## The labour system — Reader's central argument

Reader's emphasis falls on **how the mines organized African labour**, which he treats as the template for
the whole later system of segregation:

- **Migrant labour.** The mines "prised a workforce out of a pre-capitalist rural hinterland"; men walked up
  to 1,600 km to work for months, then returned. Mine labour was the "barometer of diamond wealth."
- **Pass laws (1872).** Registration depots, compulsory passes and contracts, and floggings for desertion
  enshrined, in carefully "colour-blind" language (workers were "servants," never "natives"), a precedent of
  racial control. **"The origins of apartheid as a legally defined policy lay here."**
- **The closed compound (from 1885).** Modelled partly on **convict labour** (De Beers used convicts to
  1932) and on a mining inspector's admiring report of **Brazilian slave barracks**, the closed compound
  sealed workers inside wire-mesh enclosures for the duration of their contracts — controlling theft,
  suppressing wages, and creating a captive labour reserve.
- **Land and taxation as labour coercion.** "Locations" deliberately too small for self-sufficiency, plus
  **hut taxes**, forced Africans onto the labour market.

## Gold: the Witwatersrand

The 1885 Rand discovery dwarfed Kimberley. Capital (much of it from diamond fortunes) poured into deep-level
mining; by 1897 the Rand produced a quarter of the world's gold, employing 69,000 Africans (190,000 by
1912). The Kimberley compound and pass systems were carried over wholesale, and recruitment was centralized
under **Wenela** (the Witwatersrand Native Labour Association), which drove wages *down* even as
productivity rose. Mine labour was never "employment... but always the **exploitation** of an indispensable
resource."

## Consequences

- **Imperial ambition.** The mineral wealth fired the expansionism of **Rhodes** and Britain, leading toward
  the **[[scramble-for-africa|Scramble]]** and the Anglo-Boer War (1899–1902).
- **The migrant-labour society.** A continent-wide pattern of male migrant labour, compounds, and
  segregation — by 1936, 23% of South African men aged 15–50 worked in the mines and allied industries.
- **The road to apartheid.** Reader's thesis: the racialized labour controls invented on the diamond and
  gold fields were the **direct institutional ancestors** of twentieth-century apartheid.
