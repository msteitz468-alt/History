## Definition and Scope

The Ikkō-ikki (一向一揆, "Single-minded leagues") were a series of armed uprisings by adherents of the Jōdo Shinshū (True Pure Land) Buddhism, particularly the Monto or Ikkōshū sect organized under the Hongwanji institution, against secular feudal authority in Japan. The uprisings span roughly a century from the Kaga revolt of 1488 to the fall of the Ishiyama Honganji in 1580. They represent one of the most sustained examples of religious-military confrontation in Japanese history, comparable in some respects to European conflicts between ecclesiastical and secular power, though Sadler notes that Japanese historians themselves have sometimes under-recognized this parallel.

The Monto sect "was particularly popular because it made no great demands on its believers either ethically or intellectually, while its priests were permitted to marry and hand down their temples and eat fish and live just like any layman" (Sadler). Its accessibility produced a mass following especially among rural farmers, who "have always been far more susceptible to the propaganda of the Amida sect than any other class" (Sadler).

## Causal Drivers

**Institutional accumulation**: Japanese Buddhist temples had long enjoyed tax exemptions and rights of sanctuary that enabled the accumulation of landed wealth and dependents. The military were "not much inclined to be frightened by the ghostly terrors with which the monks tried to threaten them," which produced the evolution of the soldier-monk (sōhei). Temples had both the wealth to hire fighters and the ideological resources to motivate them.

**Hongwanji organizational genius**: The founder Shinran (1174–1268) and especially the reformer Rennyo (1415–1499) built the Hongwanji into the most powerful branch of Pure Land Buddhism. Rennyo compiled vernacular scripture, traveled incessantly, and proved a brilliant organizer. He had twenty-eight children and built a network of branch temples across the country. The Hongwanji's control of Ishiyama (later Osaka) — "the greatest trading center of the country" at "a position extremely easy to defend on the one hand, and equally easy to provision on the other" (Sadler) — gave it formidable strategic assets.

**Political vacuum of the Sengoku period**: The weakness of Emperor and Shogun allowed the Hongwanji to strengthen itself at their expense. By 1488 the monks had taken the entire province of Kaga and expelled its governor. Their strong places at Nagashima (near Kuwana), Tomita (in Owari), and three temples in Mikawa (Toro, Harisaki, and Sasaki) represented a parallel power structure that acknowledged "no authority but that of the chief of the Hongwanji" (Sadler).

**Alliance-building**: The Hongwanji leadership proved adept at aligning with whatever secular lords opposed their enemies. [[takeda-shingen]] — himself a Zen monk — was the brother-in-law of Lord Abbot Kennyo Kosa of the Hongwanji, and used this connection to direct Monto energies against [[oda-nobunaga]] and [[tokugawa-ieyasu]]'s ally Uesugi Kenshin. The monks wore helmets inscribed with the legend: "He who advances is sure of Heaven; but he who retreats of eternal damnation" (Sadler).

## Major Phases

**Kaga Province (1488)**: The Monto successfully expelled the provincial governor Togashi and governed Kaga for roughly a century, making Kanazawa their headquarters.

**Mikawa Revolt (1563–1564)**: The most significant episode in [[tokugawa-ieyasu]]'s early career. The monks of three Mikawa temples (Toro, Harisaki, and Sasaki) refused to allow Ieyasu's forces to requisition supplies from their precincts, then sought to exploit the discontent of older clan retainers who opposed the new Oda alliance. Key retainer Honda Masanobu was among the leaders of the ecclesiastical party. The revolt was also supported by commanders who held castles near Ieyasu's capital.

The revolt was suppressed by 1564, chiefly because the majority of military leaders remained loyal to Ieyasu and the Monto forces consisted largely of untrained farmers, however numerous. Ieyasu also secured the assistance of his own sect (Jōdō, a different Pure Land branch) whose ancestral temple, the Daijuji, sent a thousand men to fight for him. On suppressing the revolt he accepted terms guaranteeing the temples would be left "as they originally were" — then immediately ordered them demolished, explaining when challenged that by "originally" he meant the bare ground before any building stood on it. This piece of legalistic sharp dealing was noted by later commentators as characteristic.

**Integration with anti-Nobunaga coalition (1570–1580)**: The Ishiyama Honganji and its network of fortified temple-towns became the centerpiece of the broad coalition that opposed Nobunaga throughout the 1570s. The monks at Nagashima in Ise and Tomita in Owari continued military operations after the Mikawa revolt was suppressed. Nobunaga's destruction of the Tendai monasteries on Mount Hiei in October 1571 — burning all the buildings, killing several thousand monks, women, and children — was partly motivated by their support for Asai and Asakura. Sadler quotes the *Record of Nobunaga* justifying the destruction on grounds of monastic corruption.

## Geographic Spread

The Ikkō-ikki operated across a wide swathe of central and northern Honshu: Kaga and Noto (north coast), Echizen, Owari, Mikawa, Ise (Nagashima), and above all the Kinai heartland (Ishiyama/Osaka). Their geographic concentration in commercially and agriculturally dense provinces made them economically formidable.

## Interaction with Other Processes

The Ikkō-ikki intersected with the [[sengoku-period]] warlord conflicts at multiple points, providing military forces to various secular lords while pursuing their own institutional survival. They represent a distinctive Japanese variant of the broader late medieval pattern in which religious institutions functioned as territorial powers. The parallel with European ecclesiastical politics is suggestive but imperfect: Japanese warrior monks fought for material institutional interests with little doctrinal content, unlike, for example, the Hussites or Calvinist militias.

The introduction of Portuguese firearms (matchlocks) by mid-century complicated matters: firearms lowered the skill threshold for infantry, making large farmer levies somewhat more militarily viable. However, Sadler notes that the matchlock's limitations — poor performance in wet weather, slower loading than a bow — limited its advantage over trained archer-samurai.

## End Conditions

The Ikkō-ikki as an independent armed force effectively ended with Nobunaga's decade-long siege of the Ishiyama Honganji, which surrendered in 1580 after the emperor mediated a peace. By this point Nobunaga had suppressed the Tendai and Shingon establishments, eliminated or co-opted most of the Monto strongholds, and broken the political-military power of the Hongwanji. The religious institutions survived but as subordinate bodies under secular authority — a transformation consolidated under the Tokugawa Shogunate.

## Historiography

Sadler's account is sympathetic to Ieyasu's suppression of the Mikawa revolt while acknowledging the institutional grievance that motivated it. The source material (largely Tokugawa-period chronicles) reflects the perspective of the secular victors. Modern Japanese scholarship on the Ikkō-ikki is substantial and revisionist, emphasizing the genuine popular religious energy of the movement and the economic grievances of its farmer adherents, aspects that Sadler treats as secondary to the political-military narrative. The collection's coverage of this process is thin beyond Sadler's account; specialist monographs on the Hongwanji and the Kaga theocracy are not represented.
