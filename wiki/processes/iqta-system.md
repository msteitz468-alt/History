# Iqta' System

The iqta' was the principal post-Abbasid mechanism for assigning state tax revenues from designated lands or districts to military officers and officials in lieu of cash salaries or direct bureaucratic payment.

## Definition and Scope

An iqta' granted the holder (muqta') the right to collect and retain taxes from a specified area as payment for service (primarily military). In principle it was revocable, non-hereditary, and conferred no ownership of land, judicial powers over peasants (who remained state subjects), or personal servile rights. It differed from European feudal fiefs, which fused economic, jurisdictional, and personal authority in a hereditary, contractual vassal-lord relation.

## Causal Drivers

Adopted widely from Buwayhid times (945) onward as central Abbasid tax collection and payment systems collapsed. Regimes (Buwayhid, Ghaznavid, Saljuq, Ayyubid, Mamluk, later variants) used it to support slave regiments and nomadic/tribal levies without maintaining large cash treasuries or full bureaucracies in the countryside. It **enabled** the survival of militarized regimes amid fiscal decentralization.

## Major Phases

- Early (Buwayhid/Ghaznavid/Saljuq): strong central registers, rotation, no hereditary rights.
- Later (post-Malik Shah Saljuqs onward): increasing hereditary usurpation, merger with private property, emergence of semi-independent principalities in Mesopotamia/Syria.
- Mamluk/Egyptian variant: central bureaucracy retained tighter control over surveys and revenues; amirs received assignments but state monitored.
- Later empires (Ottoman timar, Safavid tuyul, Mughal jagir): analogous but regionally named systems of prebendal revenue assignment.

## Geographic Spread

From Iraq/western Iran (Buwayhids) to Khurasan, Syria, Egypt (Saljuqs, Ayyubids, Mamluks), and adapted across later Islamic empires.

## Interaction

Closely tied to **military slavery** (ghulam/mamluk) systems and the rise of sultanates over the caliphate. Interacted with awqaf endowments (which could shield property) and local notable families (who sometimes mediated or were displaced). Contributed to but did not fully produce "feudal" conditions; rulers retained slave bodyguards and records to enforce obligations.

## End Conditions

Reversible under stronger central regimes (Mamluks, Ottomans). Transformed or replaced by cash salaries, tax farming, or modern land systems in 19th-20th c. Never evolved into a full recognized feudal order with serfdom and private jurisdictions.

**Grounded in Lapidus Ch. 20 (Range 2):** "In principle, an iqtaʿ was a grant of the right to collect taxes and to keep them in lieu of salary... An iqtaʿ was not a personal property; it could not be sold, leased, or willed... In the Middle East, then, decentralization entailed no fundamental institutional changes and could, in changed circumstances, be reversed."
