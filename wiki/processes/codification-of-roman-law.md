# The Codification of Roman Law

The great late-antique enterprise of **gathering, pruning and authoritatively republishing** the vast,
unwieldy mass of Roman law — from the private collections of the Diocletianic age to the official **Codex
Theodosianus (438)** and the monumental ***Corpus Iuris Civilis*** of **[[justinian|Justinian]]** (529–534).
It is the channel through which classical Roman jurisprudence reached the medieval and modern world, and the
foundation of the **civil-law tradition**. Treated in [[cameron-cah-v14-2000|CAH XIV]] Ch. 9 (**Detlef
Liebs**).

## Why codification was needed

Law was, Liebs stresses, **more central to the late Roman social structure** than under the Principate or in
the barbarian kingdoms — a source of Roman pride, and one barbarian nation after another (the Vandals
excepted) sought to emulate it. But the system had become a burden. The old **jurists who gave *responsa***
had given way to **career bureaucrats** managing an "ever more unwieldy body of enacted law." The legislative
machine — now centred on the imperial *consistorium* at Constantinople and Ravenna rather than the senate —
poured out enactments (especially heavy AD 425–450 and 525–550). The sheer flood of imperial constitutions,
scattered and often contradictory, made the law nearly unusable without consolidation.

## The early codices (private enterprise)

The first collections were **private works** of the Diocletianic period: the **Codex Gregorianus**
(c. 291) and **Codex Hermogenianus** (c. 295), gathering rescripts. They demonstrated the method but had no
official sanction.

## The Codex Theodosianus (438)

**Theodosius II's** code was the first **official, state-sponsored** collection: sixteen books of imperial
constitutions since Constantine, arranged by subject, promulgated for use throughout the empire in **438**
(and recognized by the western senate). Unlike the Diocletianic codices it was an act of government, and it
is an **invaluable historical source** — its sixteenth book on religion the chief monument of the
Christianized state's legislation. It did not stop the flow: both halves of the empire continued to issue
**Novels** (Valentinian III's batch of 438, etc.), which the Theodosian Code's successors had again to
absorb.

## The Corpus Iuris Civilis (Justinian, 529–534)

Justinian's commissioners, led by the quaestor **Tribonian**, produced the supreme codification, later
called the ***Corpus Iuris Civilis***:

- the **Codex Justinianus** (first edition 529; surviving **second edition 534**) — twelve books
  consolidating the Gregorian, Hermogenian and Theodosian codes and all subsequent Novels into a single
  authoritative collection of imperial constitutions;
- the **Digest** (or *Pandects*, **533**) — fifty books distilling the writings of the classical jurists
  (Justinian's preface boasts of reducing "three million lines" of juristic literature), each fragment
  preceded by an "inscription" naming its author and work — the **rescue of classical jurisprudence**;
- the **Institutes** (533) — an official students' textbook with the force of law;
- the **Novels** — Justinian's own later legislation, mostly in Greek, never officially collected by him.

## Vulgar law and the classicizing revival

A central theme is the tension between **"vulgar law"** — the simplified, practice-driven law of the western
provinces, where classical subtleties had decayed — and the **classicizing revival** centred on the great
law schools, above all **Berytus (Beirut)** and Constantinople, whose professors ("the *antecessores*")
furnished Justinian's compilers. The Digest is the high point of this learned revival; yet much everyday
late-Roman law (serfdom of the *coloni*, harsher penalties, the erosion of free status' immunity from
torture, the hereditary binding of *curiales* and trades) reflects the vulgarizing, bureaucratic reality.

## The barbarian codes

The Roman model propagated into the successor kingdoms. The **leges** of the barbarian kings — the Visigothic
*Code of Euric* and *Lex Romana Visigothorum (Breviary of Alaric*, 506), the Burgundian and Ostrogothic
codes — drew directly on Roman law (especially the Theodosian Code and the Sententiae of Paul), preserving
Roman law for the Romans living under barbarian rule (CAH XIV Ch. 10, on **law in the western kingdoms**).

## Significance

The codification is the **single most consequential legal act in European history.** The Theodosian Code
preserved the legislation of the Christian empire; Justinian's *Corpus* preserved both imperial law and
classical jurisprudence, and — rediscovered in eleventh-century **Bologna** — became the basis of the
**medieval and modern civil-law tradition** across continental Europe. It is the most enduring product of
Justinian's reign and the clearest case of late antiquity transmitting a fully "ancient" achievement to
the post-Roman world.
