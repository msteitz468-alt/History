# Deep Aboriginal History (50,000-year Archaeological View)

## Definition and Scope

This process page covers the 50,000-year archaeological record of Aboriginal Australia as
synthesised by Peter Veth and Sue O'Connor in Chapter 1 of the
[[bashford-macintyre-cha-v1-2013|Cambridge History of Australia, Vol. 1]] (2013). It
encompasses the initial peopling of **Sahul** (the joined landmass of Australia, New
Guinea and the Aru Islands), continental spread, economic and social development across
the Pleistocene and Holocene, the megafaunal extinction debate, the effects of the Last
Glacial Maximum, post-glacial coastal reorganisation, late-Holocene social intensification,
and the pre-contact period of northern Australian contacts with Southeast Asian voyagers.

The process begins with arrival on Sahul's northern coastline (now submerged) and ends
with the era of sustained European and Macassan external contact. The authors frame the
story explicitly as one of adaptability, dynamism, and cultural resilience — "one of the
most resilient cultures, precisely because of its adaptability, dynamism and the inherent
competencies held by people from the very beginnings of Australian history."

This is distinct from the discrete migration event at [[colonization-australasia-50kbp]],
which covers the founding crossing itself.

---

## Causal Drivers

- **Out-of-Africa dispersal**: Modern humans left Africa c. 70,000 BP and reached Sahul
  before 50,000 BP via the Wallacean island chain, requiring multiple open-water crossings
  out of sight of land — "an impressive accomplishment."
- **Sea-going technology**: Watercraft capable of island-chain crossings through Wallacea
  (Lesser Sundas: Flores, Timor) were prerequisite for arrival.
- **Pleistocene environmental conditions**: Environmental reconstructions suggest that
  interior freshwater availability may have been equal to or better than in the Holocene
  up to 45,000 BP in many regions, facilitating early inland spread.
- **Holocene climate change**: Sea-level rise after ~19,000 BP reshaped coastlines
  dramatically; ENSO intensification c. 4,000 BP reorganised subsistence economies;
  new wetland formation in Arnhem Land within the past 2,000 years opened new
  ecological niches.
- **Human agency and innovation**: Edge-ground axes appear by 35,000 BP; long-distance
  exchange networks for ochre, shell and other materials are documented from >40,000 BP;
  fire management of landscapes is inferred from the earliest occupation.

## Major Phases

### Phase 1 — Initial Colonisation and Rapid Continental Spread (c. 60,000–30,000 BP)

The oldest sites are in **Arnhem Land**, dated by optically stimulated luminescence (OSL)
to between 65,000 and 50,000 BP. Sites in the inland arid zone (Parnkupirti, Lake
Gregory) date to 50,000–45,000 BP. Devil's Lair in south-west Western Australia has the
oldest radiocarbon date at c. 47,000 BP. Alpine Tasmania was occupied by 39,000–37,000 BP.

"Sites of human occupation in the order of 40,000 BP…are now well established in
Australia" — refuting older models that treated the interior as uninhabitable during
the Pleistocene.

Signature behaviours in this phase:
- **Regional tool traditions**: Edge-ground axes (north, >35,000 BP); thumbnail scrapers
  (Tasmania); seed-grinding implements in the arid zone.
- **Long-distance exchange**: Ochre transported 250 km from the Barrier Range to Willandra
  Lakes burial site (>40,000 BP); pearl, baler and tusk shell moved up to 500 km from
  as early as 30,000 BP.
- **Personal adornment**: Strands of cone shells (>32,000 BP) and tusk shells (~30,000 BP)
  from north-west Australian desert-edge sites — paralleling African and Levantine shell
  bead traditions.
- **Ochre use**: Faceted ochre lumps and ground-ochre slabs dated to c. 42,000 BP from
  the Kimberley, Arnhem Land, Lake Mungo and central Australia — "assumed that ochres were
  used in the earliest pigment art."
- **Rock art**: At least four regional art traditions plausibly dated to the Pleistocene;
  some art may predate the Last Glacial Maximum, though reliable dating "is still in
  its infancy."

### Phase 2 — Last Glacial Maximum and Desert Refugia (c. 30,000–17,000 BP)

From c. 30,000 BP global glaciation intensified: sea levels fell ~50 metres in 1,000
years, the monsoon weakened to its lowest effectiveness in 100,000 years, and south-east
temperatures dropped by up to 9°C. Interior dunefield environments were devegetated;
lakes dried; surface water became scarce.

Eight sites are documented within what would become the expanded arid core during the
Last Glacial Maximum (peaking 22,000–18,000 BP). Groups consolidated around "productive
and well-watered refuges." The "desert transformation model" proposes that groups first
adapted to more benign conditions, then modified technology, diet and mobility strategies
to accommodate landscape change.

### Phase 3 — Post-Glacial Transgression and Coastal Reorganisation (c. 17,000–6,000 BP)

From c. 19,000 BP global temperatures rose and sea levels climbed toward modern levels
(stabilising c. 6,500 BP). The sea had been 123 metres lower at the glacial maximum.
Australia was connected to New Guinea across Torres Strait; Tasmania was joined to the
mainland. Bass Strait flooded c. 14,000 BP, isolating Tasmania.

An enormous freshwater **Lake Carpentaria** (c. 190,000 km² at its maximum, 14,000 BP)
dominated the low-lying country between New Guinea and north-west Queensland before
being flooded by rising seas by 10,000 BP. The thousands of offshore islands visible
today were formed between 8,000 and 6,000 BP.

Between approximately 8,000 and 6,000 BP, widespread use of offshore islands began, as
evidenced in coastal shell middens. New maritime technologies appeared: fish traps,
specialised hooks, and watercraft serving extended seasonal rounds.

**Tasmanian isolation and the 'devolution' debate**: After Bass Strait formed, earlier
interpretations posited social and economic decline in the isolated Tasmanian population.
Archaeological reassessment showed that the cessation of fishing and bone-tool use
(4,000–3,700 BP) represented a *switch* in marine species choice, not maladaptation;
abalone and crayfish replaced fish as coastal staples; shell midden sites increased over
the past 3,000 years; and population probably grew before contact. "This pattern profiles
the efflorescence of Tasmanian Aboriginal societies over the past several millennia — the
very opposite of the previous 'devolution' argument."

### Phase 4 — Holocene Social and Economic Elaboration (c. 6,000–1,500 BP)

Sea-level stabilisation, ENSO intensification (~4,000 BP), and new wetland formation
generated conditions for significant change:

- **Large shell mounds**: Appearing from c. 4,000 BP along the northern Australian
  coastline, with highest densities 2,500–1,000 BP; linked by some scholars to elaboration
  of social complexity and ceremony.
- **Earth mounds**: Appearing in south-east Victoria c. 4,000 BP, proliferating after
  2,000 BP along margins of new freshwater wetlands; associated with more sedentary
  occupation and wetland exploitation.
- **Lake Condah fish-trap complex** (western Victoria): Earliest channel phase dated to
  c. 6,600 cal BP — "one of the world's oldest known fish traps." Multi-tiered basalt
  walls added within the past 600–800 years. Stone-walled fish traps up to 6 m high,
  with channels over 3 km long, exploited eel and fish runs.
- **Australian small-tool tradition**: Backed artefacts dated to c. 10,000 BP (New South
  Wales) and as early as 16,000 BP (Gulf of Carpentaria); bifacial points to ~8,000 BP
  in Arnhem Land; tula adzes to <4,000 BP. The proposition that these arrived as a
  Southeast Asian package c. 5,000–4,000 BP is "not consistent with present
  archaeological evidence" — they are uniquely Australian developments, distributed
  regionally, not as a single horizon.
- **The dingo**: The one confirmed introduction, arriving from outside Australia between
  4,500 and 4,000 BP. Its arrival coincided with the extinction of the Tasmanian Tiger
  (Thylacine) on the mainland, thought to reflect competition.

### Phase 5 — Late-Holocene Northern Contacts (c. 1,200 BP to 1606 CE)

Shell fish hooks appeared in northern Australia c. 1,200 BP and were in widespread use
along northern, north-eastern and south-eastern coasts by 1,000 BP — likely introduced
from Torres Strait or via Polynesian contacts. Their appearance at Bass Point (NSW)
coincided with changes in fish species and shellfish in the midden, associated with a
shift in gendered labour division.

From at least the mid-1600s CE, Macassan trepang fleets from Sulawesi sailed seasonally
to Arnhem Land and the Kimberley coast (see [[macassan-australian-contact]]). Rock art
depicts Southeast Asian praus; artefact assemblages include pottery and metal; post-contact
middens are substantially larger and contain a wider variety of exotic artefacts and large
marine fauna, enabled by adoption of dugout canoes and detachable metal harpoon heads.

## Geographic Spread

The process encompasses the entire Australian continent and, in the Pleistocene, the
greater Sahul landmass (including New Guinea and Tasmania). The northern coasts (Arnhem
Land, Kimberley, Cape York) show the earliest occupation and the most intensive late-
Holocene external contacts. The inland arid zone was occupied from the earliest phases
(Parnkupirti, 50,000–45,000 BP; Puritjarra, 40,000 BP). Tasmania represents the
southernmost Pleistocene human occupation globally (39,000 BP, 42° south latitude).

## End Conditions

The process of autonomous Aboriginal cultural development was radically disrupted by
British colonisation from 1788 CE. The text notes: "the interval between the first
fleeting visits of European voyagers in the 1770s and the establishment of a British
colonial settlement was devastatingly brief" — particularly for south-eastern peoples
who lacked the prior exposure to outsiders that northern groups had developed through
Macassan contact.

The process did not end — Aboriginal cultural continuity persists — but the trajectory of
autonomous development was interrupted and transformed by [[settler-colonialism]].

## Historiography

### Dating Methods and Confidence

Veth and O'Connor employ two primary dating techniques:

1. **Optically Stimulated Luminescence (OSL)**: Measures when sediments were last
   exposed to light. Used for the oldest Arnhem Land sites (Malakunanja, Nauwalabila 1:
   65,000–50,000 BP) and arid-zone sites (Parnkupirti: 50,000–45,000 BP). Dates given
   as cal BP (calendar years before 1950).

2. **Radiocarbon (¹⁴C)**: Dates charcoal and organic remains; used for Devil's Lair
   (~47,000 BP), Lake Mungo (50,000–46,000 BP by OSL; older radiocarbon dates also
   exist), and many Holocene sites. Radiocarbon dates are expressed as "BP" (before
   present = before 1950) and converted to cal BP via calibration curves.

The text notes that "the place of first occupation on the expanded northern coastline of
Sahul is now submerged and thus the precise time of human arrival will never be known."
General consensus places initial arrival between 60,000 and 50,000 BP, with continental
spread complete by ~40,000 BP, based on sites in all regions of Sahul in the 50,000–
40,000 BP range.

Rock art dating "is still in its infancy"; some Pleistocene-era art is plausibly dated
but few precise ages are confirmed.

### Theoretical Debates

**Megafaunal extinction**: Hotly debated. Three positions: (1) rapid human-caused
extinction following arrival and dispersal ('blitzkrieg'); (2) gradual extinction over
10,000–20,000 years from long-term aridity and climate change; (3) synergistic model
combining climate stress, human hunting, and fire management. Over 60 species of large
animals did not survive the Pleistocene–Holocene transition in Australia. See
[[pleistocene-megafaunal-extinction]] for a dedicated controversy treatment.

**Routes of entry**: Southern route (Lesser Sunda islands → north-west Australia shelf)
is generally preferred for involving shorter open-water crossings. A northern route via
Borneo → Sulawesi → New Guinea has also been proposed (Morwood and van Oosterzee), but
is "incompatible with the overwhelming evidence for human presence in this region
42,000 years ago."

**Coastal vs. inland colonisation models**: Early models stressed maritime-then-coastal
dispersion; others argue the Pleistocene coastline was inhospitable and groups quickly
moved inland — now supported by early arid-zone sites.

**Social intensification**: The dominant narrative of cumulative increase in social
complexity over the Holocene (Lourandos) is critiqued: many supposedly 'late'
developments have earlier precedents (Cycad seed preparation at 15,680 BP; Lake Condah
fish traps at 6,600 BP; backed artefacts at 10,000–16,000 BP). The authors argue changes
were responses to environmental conditions as much as autonomous cultural trajectories.

**Tasmanian isolation**: Rhys Jones' 1970s thesis of post-isolation 'devolution' is now
substantially rejected; the Tasmanian case is reframed as cultural innovation and
adaptation.

The [[bashford-macintyre-cha-v1-2013|Cambridge History of Australia, Vol. 1 (Ch 1)]]
