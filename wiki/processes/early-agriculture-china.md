## Definition and Scope

The origins of farming in **China** — a genuinely independent primary hearth with
**two centres**: the **Yellow River** north (broomcorn and foxtail millet) and the
**Yangtze** basin (rice). Covered in CWH Vol. II Ch. 12 (Xinyi Liu, Dorian Fuller
& Martin Jones), with case study **Xinglonggou** in Ch. 13. A key strength of
this volume for the collection, which is otherwise moderate on early China.

## The deep-seated East Asian culinary tradition: boiling and steaming

A striking, consequential contrast with [[early-agriculture-southwest-asia|Southwest
Asia]]. **Pottery is extraordinarily early in East Asia** — ~18,000 years ago in
the Yangtze region, ~12,000 years ago in northern China — made by *foragers* for
**boiling** fish and plant foods, *millennia before* agriculture. SW Asia, by
contrast, ground grain into flour for **bread** in clay ovens, and got pottery
late (~8,500 BP). This produced a lasting **culinary divide**: a West/South Asian
grinding-and-baking tradition vs. an East Asian **boiling-and-steaming**
tradition — with downstream effects on which grain traits were selected (gluten
for bread in the west; starch/stickiness in the east). The "glutinous endosperm
starch" culture area (sticky rice, sticky millets) reflects a deep, parallel
cultural preference.

## The northern millet centre

Broomcorn (*Panicum miliaceum*) and foxtail (*Setaria italica*) millet originated
in northern China and were the staples of the developed Neolithic (Yangshao,
Dawenkou, Longshan) and of the Shang/Zhou. **Millet domestication was a
protracted process** — grain size increased and shape changed gradually across
the Neolithic (the millet from early sites like Xinglonggou is intermediate
between wild and modern domesticated forms). Soybeans and hemp were added as
secondary domesticates; pig isotope chemistry shows pigs were fed on millet
after ~4500 BCE (indirect evidence of human commitment to millet).

## The Yangtze rice centre

Domesticated rice (*Oryza sativa* japonica) emerged in the Yangtze basin. Against
claims of late-Pleistocene domestic rice, systematic flotation shows
**morphological domestication only by ~4000 BCE** (with the first paddy-field
technologies); before that, wild rice was just one element of a broad Holocene
foraging economy (acorns, water chestnuts). Rice replaced these as a staple only
by ~3000 BCE. Early Yangtze agriculture was **"single-mindedly about rice"** —
unlike the diversified north, it added few secondary crops (pigs, gourds, plus
probable fruit trees, ramie, and silk-mulberry; the earliest textiles are
Liangzhu ramie and silk).

## Spread and "Bronze Age food globalization"

- **Rice spread south** to Fujian/Guangdong/Guangxi ~5000–4500 BP, and on to
  **mainland Southeast Asia by ~4000 BP** (see [[early-agriculture-southeast-asia-pacific]]
  *(Cycle 5)*).
- **Millet spread west** across the Eurasian steppe to Europe (Begash, Kazakhstan,
  ~2450–2150 BCE).
- In return, **Southwest Asian domesticates moved east into China**: bread wheat
  by ~2500–2270 BCE (oldest at Zhaojiazhuang, Shandong); sheep, goats, cattle
  ~4500 BP; **bronze metallurgy** by ~2000 BCE; **horses, wheels, and chariots**
  by ~1200 BCE. This two-way exchange is termed **"Bronze Age (food)
  globalization."** Notably, central China — unlike west/Central/South Asia —
  developed **no major dairying tradition** (no [[secondary-products-revolution]]
  milch complex), using cattle/horses for traction, not milk.

## Case study: Xinglonggou (northeast China)

Xinglonggou (discovered 1982; excavated from 1998) is an early Neolithic site of
the Xinglongwa culture whose inhabitants relied on **broomcorn and foxtail millet**
as staples — carbonized millet here dates to **~7700 cal BCE**, among the earliest
in China and showing intermediate (early-domestication) grain morphology. The site
also yielded some of the **oldest jade objects in East Asia** (slit rings),
rows of semi-subterranean "pit structures," and (in Xinglonggou II–III)
evidence of ordinary domestic life — a window onto an early northern millet
village.

## The "five grains" and Chinese identity

By ~2000 BCE all five legendary "five grains" of the Shang oracle bones (broomcorn
and foxtail millet, soybeans, wheat/barley, hemp) were grown — the Yellow River
region developing an **ideology of crop diversity**. The Chinese word for "meal"
(*fan*) denotes cooked cereal; agriculture today feeds ~20% of humanity.

## Subsistence on the Eve of the State (Liu 2004)

[[liu-chinese-neolithic-2004|Li Liu's]] household-archaeology of the late
[[longshan-culture|Longshan]] adds texture to the millet base. At the Kangjia household
([[yiluo-basin|Wei River valley]], ~2500–2000 BC) subsistence was **mixed and self-sufficient
within a ~20 km radius** — millet agriculture plus animal husbandry, hunting, and gathering.
Two long-run shifts stand out: **pig husbandry declined** through the Neolithic (pig bone falls
from ~34% of fauna at pre-Yangshao Baijia to ~6% in the late Longshan at Jiangzhai as deer
rises), and **domestic sheep/goat were newly introduced** to the middle/lower Yellow River only
in the early Longshan (~2800 BC at Miaodigou), spreading from the northwest (Hongshan, Gansu
Majiayao) — possibly via population movement, diffusion, or elite demand for exotic/prestige
food. Wild animals actually **dominated** the Kangjia assemblage (72%, rising over time), much
of it consumed in **ritual feasting** rather than daily diet — tying subsistence to the
emerging social hierarchy of [[early-china-state-formation]].

## End Conditions

Diversifies after ~2000 BCE as the Yangtze is drawn into the orbit of Yellow
River states and Bronze Age exchange networks; transitions into the agrarian base
of early Chinese civilization.
