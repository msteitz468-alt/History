## Definition and Scope

This process covers the transformation of the western fringe of the [[amazonia|Amazon Basin]] — the montaña (eastern slopes and piedmont of the Andes) from the Colombian Caquetá headwaters to the Bolivian Mamoré — through Spanish colonization, missionary activity, epidemic disease, and indigenous responses between roughly 1540 and 1820. The geographic zone encompasses present-day Ecuadorian Oriente, the upper Peruvian Amazon (Marañón, Huallaga, Ucayali headwaters), and the Bolivian piedmont.

Anne Christine Taylor argues that the process entailed "an overall trend toward increased cultural homogeneity, a breakdown of macrosystems and regional networks to the benefit of intermediate or purely local levels of organization, and a growing spatial, sociological, and symbolic distancing between upland and lowland societies" (*CHNPA* Vol. III, Part 2, Ch. 17). The standard picture of the western Amazon as a uniform zone of "tropical forest cultures" is, she demonstrates, itself a product of this colonial transformation rather than a description of pre-Columbian reality.

## Causal Drivers

**Pre-Columbian baseline: complexity and connectivity.** At first contact, the western Amazon was far from marginal. Between 1538 and 1560, the southern part of the equatorial piedmont was "inhabited, urbanized, and administered to a greater degree than the corresponding Andean and coastal zones" (Taylor). Large riverside societies (Omagua, Conibo) had elaborate material cultures; the [[andes|Andes]]-Amazon interface was crossed by dense trade networks linking highland chiefdoms to piedmont and lowland groups. The Jívaro-related Palta had occupied parts of the sierra since the seventh century CE. Macro-regional trade circuits — mediated by the Piro, Conibo, Omagua, and Kallawaya lords — connected Cuzco silver to eastern forest goods.

**Spanish entrada and gold rush (1538–1580).** Spanish penetration was fueled by gold: panning in the Zamora Valley and the Condor massif. Between 1540 and 1580, more towns were founded in what became Ecuadorian Amazonia than in the equatorial sierra or coast. Around 1560, five gobiernos (Mocoa, Quijos, Macas, Yaguarzongo, Jaén) covered the piedmont. Towns were squalid ephemeral boomtowns resembling contemporary Brazilian garimpos rather than planned settlements. Colonists "routinely launched plundering, slaving, or prospecting expeditions very distant from their settled bases" (Taylor).

**Encomienda as labor-extraction cover.** The lowland [[encomienda]] functioned differently from its highland equivalent: because native production was too meager or costly to transport, encomiendas served primarily as frameworks for capturing and controlling native labor. By the early seventeenth century, the claim that a group harbored cimarrones (escaped encomienda workers) sufficed to justify a slave raid. "By contemporary accounts, only one in ten captives remained at a year's end; the rest fled or died of epidemic disease, ill treatment, and despair" (Taylor). The encomienda was officially suppressed in 1721 but titles were still honored in the lowlands until 1730.

**Epidemic mortality.** Colonists' extraordinary mobility and their role as disease vectors produced epidemics disproportionate to their numbers. "The population in the mission region fell from some 200,000 in 1550 to 20,000 or 30,000 in 1730" — a 90% reduction in the Mainas mission zone alone (Taylor).

**Mission system (1630–1768/1808).** After the frontier contracted, missions became the primary colonial institution managing the lowlands. Franciscans and Jesuits pursued different strategies with different effects (see below), but both shared obsession with congregating dispersed populations into riverside reductions — thereby maximizing epidemic exposure — and both accepted "terrible mortality in their reductions with the same resignation and often indifference" (Taylor).

## Major Phases

**Phase 1 — Entrada and collapse (1538–1600).** The initial Spanish penetration was the most intense and destructive. It was followed by widespread indigenous uprisings and flight: the Quijos shamans' revolt of 1579 briefly united Omagua and Quijos and destroyed Avila and Archidona; the Jívaro revolt of 1590 (see [[jivaro]]) destroyed Logrono and Sevilla de Oro and drove colonists to the highlands. By 1600, only three maintained tracks crossed the northern montaña; many "cities" had simply disappeared.

**Phase 2 — Frontier fossilization and mission establishment (1600–1700).** The colonization frontier shriveled and fossilized. Indigenous peoples abandoned the eastern slopes and river valleys closest to Andean towns. Many highland Indians forced into eastern gold mines abandoned their identities and merged into forest populations. The Jesuits established the Mainas mission on the Marañón from 1634 (Borja), beginning intensive reduction of Mayna, Xebero, and Cocama populations. The Franciscans pushed into the upper Huallaga and Putumayo-Caquetá.

**Phase 3 — Mission expansion and demographic scar (1660–1742).** A great wave of Jesuit expansion in the Curaray-Tigre sector (1660–1700) contacted most Zaparoan and Candoan tribes. Nominally 160,000 Indians were within the Mainas mission by 1700, though real control was far thinner. Epidemic mortality was endemic; the grim hunts for converts using armed native recruits exposed populations to epidemics "at least once a generation." The Mainas mission peaked at some forty reductions stretching from the Andes to the Rio Negro.

**Phase 4 — Revolt, closure, and recomposition (1742–1820).** The messianic revolt of Juan Santos Atahuallpa (1742), a half-caste Andean who united Campa Arawak and piedmont groups, routed two Lima-sent generals, destroyed Moyobamba, Tarma, and Andamarca, captured all forts overlooking the montaña, and closed the central selva to whites for over 50 years. The Jesuit expulsion (1768) transferred the Mainas mission to the Franciscans and initiated a second round of reorganization. This phase also saw Portuguese slaving raids penetrate from the east (from 1730), driving groups like the Tukano northeastward and contributing to the formation of the Vaupés multiethnic culture.

## Geographic Spread

- **Northern montaña** (upper Caquetá-Putumayo to upper Napo, present-day Colombia/Ecuador): Franciscan territory; Cofan, western Tukano, Quijos affected
- **Central piedmont** (upper Marañón basin, Ecuador-Peru): Jesuit Mainas mission core; Jívaro, Zaparo, Candoa, Cocama, Xebero affected
- **Central selva** (Ucayali, Campa-Arawak territory, Peru): Franciscan missions; Shipibo, Conibo, Piro, Amuesha, Campa (Ashaninka) affected; closed by Juan Santos revolt 1742–1790s
- **Bolivian piedmont** (upper Beni, Mojos llanos): Franciscan Apolobamba missions; Jesuit Mojos missions (1675–1768); Arawak Mojo, Araona, Leko affected

## Interaction

This process is closely linked to [[reduccion]]: the reduction model — concentrating dispersed populations into riverside mission villages — was the primary mechanism of both colonial control and epidemic mortality. Concentration on major rivers maximized epidemic spread.

The process produced what Taylor calls "colonial tribes" — new ethnic formations like the Lamista, Canelos, Andoas, and Quijos (post-eighteenth century) — that served as brokers between autonomous selva populations and the colonial world. These groups developed a structural duality: an outward "manso" (tame) identity visible to colonists, and a closed "sacha runa" (wild) sphere maintaining indigenous knowledge and shamanic practice. They are part_of the broader process of [[ethnogenesis]] in colonial contexts.

The process interacted with [[brazilian-indian-slavery]] through Portuguese slaving raids from the east (Rio Negro direction, from c. 1730), which pushed western Tukanoan groups northeastward and contributed to the distinctive multilinguistic Vaupés culture.

## End Conditions

By 1820 the western Amazonian societies had been fundamentally reorganized:

1. **Demographic**: catastrophic reduction of riverside and piedmont populations; interfluvial groups had retreated into relative isolation.
2. **Social**: stratified riverside chiefdoms (Omagua, Conibo, Napo Encabellados) dissolved; "Jívaroan-style settlements in isolated domestic units" had replaced multifamily longhouses; political hierarchies gave way to more egalitarian, acephalous local autonomy.
3. **Ethnic**: a triadic structure — whites / "tame" colonial Indians (mansos, Christianized brokers) / "wild" autonomous aucas — replaced the pre-Columbian mosaic of varied polities. "Zaparoization," "Mojoization," and the creation of colonial tribes became survival strategies.
4. **Cultural**: Quichua spread as a mission lingua franca across the upper Amazon (paralleling Mapundungun in the southern pampas); clothing, settlement patterns, kinship terminology, and ritual were reshaped by mission contact.

The process created the conditions for the nineteenth-century rubber boom's even greater violence on already-reduced populations.

## Historiography

Taylor challenges both the "environmental determinism" that treated western Amazonian societies as inevitably simple forest cultures and the nationalist historiographies that excluded them from Andean or national historical traditions. She argues that the mosaic of apparently primitive small groups depicted in Steward and Métraux's canonical *Handbook* is itself a product of colonial transformation, not a pre-Columbian baseline. Archaeological work following Donald Lathrap has demonstrated Amazon-origin populations moving *upward* into the Andes, reversing the assumed direction of cultural influence.

The Mainas mission is the most extensively documented case, through Jesuit diaries including M. Uriarte's detailed account of daily life in the Omagua reduction San Joaquín (c. 1750–1768). For the southern frontier of this zone, comparative analysis with [[araucania-frontier-1573-1882]] reveals contrasting indigenous responses: Mapuche successful resistance through social adaptability; Amazonian groups less able to maintain territorial sovereignty against the combined pressures of disease, slavery, and mission reduction.
