## Definition and Scope

Second-wave feminism denotes the feminist movement that developed across every
Western European country in the late 1960s and early 1970s, distinguished from the
"old feminism" of 1890–1930 (chiefly focused on suffrage) by roughly forty years.
It is not a single organization but a loosely connected set of national movements
sharing methods (consciousness-raising, grassroots organizing, direct action) and
two overlapping policy currents: **women's liberation** (autonomy over the intimate
sphere — sexuality, reproduction, culture) and **equal rights** (civil, economic, and
legal equality in the public sphere). It is not the same as the long-run rise of
women's paid labor force participation, which had independent economic drivers,
though the two reinforced each other.

## Causal Drivers

- **Post-1945 social change** — rising education for girls, the spread of the
  contraceptive pill, and the growth of white-collar and welfare-state employment
  eroded the material basis of the housewife model even before organized feminism
  emerged.
- **The New Left and 1960s cultural revolution** — many founding feminists began as
  activists in Campaign for Nuclear Disarmament, Northern Ireland's civil rights
  movement, or continental student movements, and reacted against the sexual
  division of labor within those movements (women relegated to clerical/support
  roles while men held political authority).
- **Consciousness-raising as method** — small discussion groups, feminist bookshops,
  journals (Britain's *Spare Rib*, France's Éditions des Femmes), and services
  (shelters, rape crisis centers) built an organizational base independent of
  formal party politics.

## Major Phases

1. **Emergence (late 1960s–early 1970s)** — formation out of New Left disillusionment;
   consciousness-raising groups proliferate in northern Europe.
2. **Abortion and bodily autonomy campaigns (1970s)** — the most energizing cause
   of the decade. France's "Manifesto of the 343" (1971), in which prominent women
   publicly declared "I have aborted," swung public opinion toward legalization and
   was imitated (less successfully) in West Germany.
3. **Equal-rights legislative campaigns (1970s)** — reform of inheritance, property
   management, and employment-consent laws that had disadvantaged married women
   (e.g., West Germany's 1957 Equal Rights Statute only partially addressed these);
   most legal disadvantages were erased through legislation over the course of the
   1970s.
4. **Internal fracture (mid-1970s)** — disagreement over lesbian separatism and
   whether men were intrinsically violent split feminist organizations; Italy saw the
   most extreme "gender warfare," including neofascist violence against feminist
   institutions (the 1979 attack on Radio Donna).
5. **Welfare-state and representation phase (1980s–1990s)** — shift toward childcare
   funding, family allowances, pay equity, and equal political representation.

## Geographic Spread

Developed independently in every major Western European country but with markedly
different intensities and outcomes: strongest and most institutionally successful
in Scandinavia (already high female labor-force participation via the welfare
state); most ideologically radicalized and violently contested in Italy; centered
on church-state conflict in Catholic France, Spain, and Portugal, where Roman
Catholicism combined with cultural emphasis on family honor to reinforce women's
legal subordination longer than in the Protestant north.

## Interaction

Directly entangled with [[european-secularization-process]] (the abortion and divorce
campaigns directly confronted Catholic and Orthodox church authority) and with
women's rising wage labor and the transformation of the European family (see
Chapter 20 discussion in [[paxton-europe-twentieth-century-2011]]). Distinct from,
but politically allied with, the broader New Left and Green movements of the 1970s–80s.

## End Conditions

Second-wave feminism did not end so much as diffuse: by the 1980s–1990s its central
legal demands (contraception, abortion access in most countries, equal-pay and
anti-discrimination law) had been substantially achieved in northern and western
Europe, and activist energy shifted to informal discrimination, equal political
representation, and — by the 1990s — third-wave and postmodern feminist critiques
not covered by this source range. Structural continuities remain: unequal division
of household labor persisted across Europe, including egalitarian Scandinavia,
through the end of the twentieth century.

## Historiography

Gisela Kaplan's *Contemporary Western European Feminism* (1992), based on primary
research in seven languages, is the standard comparative survey and the principal
source underlying this page. Claire Duchen's *Feminism in France: From May '68 to
Mitterrand* (1986) offers an insider account of the French case. Paxton and Hessler
(*Europe in the Twentieth Century*, 5th ed., ch. 20) synthesize these alongside
Eurostat demographic data on divorce, cohabitation, and out-of-wedlock birth rates
to link feminist activism to measurable family-structure change. Collection coverage
of this topic is otherwise essentially absent — Age of Expansion/Cold War-era social
history is a thin region overall for this wiki.
