# Colonial and Postcolonial Native Peoples of Mesoamerica

## Definition and Scope

The long arc of **indigenous Mesoamerican history since the Spanish invasion** (1519–present),
as framed by Murdo J. MacLeod’s overview chapter in
[[cambridge-history-native-peoples-americas-v2-2|CHNPA II Part 2]] and the regional chapters that
follow it. MacLeod presents the chapter as a compressed general introduction to **some three
centuries of colonial and about 175 years of national** history of Native American peoples in
Mesoamerica after the Spanish invasion; national-period emphasis falls on the nation-states of
**Mexico and Guatemala** (other Central American states appear occasionally). The nine
subsequent Part 2 chapters survey regional histories by mid-career and younger scholars.

This process page is the **overview synthesis**: it is not a substitute for regional process pages
([[central-mexico-indigenous-colonial]], [[northwest-mexico-indigenous-colonial-modern]],
[[northeastern-mexico-indigenous-colonial-modern]], [[western-mexico-indigenous-colonial-modern]],
[[gulf-coast-indigenous-colonial-modern]], [[oaxaca-indigenous-colonial-modern]],
[[lowland-maya-colonial-modern]], [[highland-maya-colonial-modern]]) but the shared
multi-century frame they sit under. It complements the prehistoric sequence in
[[cambridge-history-native-peoples-americas-v2-1|Part 1]].

**What it is not.** Operational campaign narrative of the conquest itself (see
[[spanish-conquest-of-mexico-1519]]); full institutional treatises on single colonial devices
([[encomienda]], [[congregacion|congregación]], [[hacienda]], [[bourbon-reforms]]); or Andean
parallels ([[cambridge-history-native-peoples-americas-v3-1|CHNPA III]]).

## Causal Drivers

- **Spanish invasion and legal-political incorporation** — naming of “Indians” as a single
  category; recognition of *señores naturales* in law while destroying overarching imperial
  nobilities; tribute and labor regimes ([[encomienda]], draft labor, later hacienda peonage).
- **[[virgin-soil-epidemics|Catastrophic demographic collapse]]** and slow mid/late colonial
  recovery, reshaping labor systems, conversion methods, village government, and land tenure.
- **Evangelization and colonial Church institutions** — incomplete “spiritual conquest,”
  cofradías, *cajas de comunidad* ([[spiritual-conquest-and-colonial-church]]).
- **Resettlement and administrative reorganization** — [[congregacion|congregación]], *cabildo*
  / cabecera–sujeto, *pueblo de indios* corporate structure.
- **Commercialization and state fiscal pressure** — late-colonial hacienda expansion, Bourbon
  interventionism, then Liberal and Porfirian land assault; coffee/henequen export regimes;
  Revolution/ejido and indigenismo; late-twentieth-century neoliberal rollback.

## Major Phases

### 1. Invasion, classification, and early colonial incorporation (16th c.)

Naming all varied American peoples “**Indians**” was, for MacLeod, the first classificatory
imposition of the European invasion — as imprecise as “Asians,” reductionist and unimaginable as a
collective label to pre-contact peoples before Europeans came ashore in the Antilles. Once
lumped together, debate over the “true nature” of this one people could begin: some thinkers and
settlers assigned subhuman status; [[las-casas-bartolome|Bartolomé de Las Casas]] proclaimed
“All Mankind is One” and prevailed among thinkers if not among exploiters of native labor.
Indians were fitted into Christian-political schemes as **free but lowly Crown vassals** to be
protected by Crown and Church.

Because natives had created intricate polities meeting Catholic natural-law criteria of *vida
política*, Spanish legal minds accorded Native American nobilities a place as ***señores
naturales*** (natural lords). On the ground, Spaniards killed overarching or imperial nobilities
that could reunite regions in revolt; remaining colonial Indian nobility was mostly local or
intermarried with conquerors. Pre-conquest law books, royal histories, and creation myths were
commonly destroyed as unchristian/devil’s work (Bishop Diego de Landa of Yucatan the most
notorious book-burner); survival owed to native elite concealment, later incorporation into
colonial compositions, and Spanish trophy-collecting (e.g. Dresden Codex; Moctezuma’s treasure
sent by Cortés as a bribe for approval of his “illegal” conquest of central Mexico from Cuba).

### 2. Demographic catastrophe and source baseline (16th–17th c.)

From Spanish arrival native populations declined catastrophically: Caribbean islands and many
humid tropical Mesoamerican coasts saw virtual disappearance; highlands and plateaus may have
fallen by **90 percent or more**. By mid-seventeenth century (earlier in cores, much later in far
peripheries and unconquered areas) native populations had fallen to a remnant. Spanish
immigration, African slave imports, and miscegenation changed proportionate ethnic distribution
and forced readjustments in labor, conversion, village government, and land tenure (see
[[virgin-soil-epidemics]], [[pre-columbian-american-population]], [[black-legend]]).

Baseline knowledge of Mesoamerican society around 1500 is relatively plentiful for Aztec
tributary centers, the Tarascan state, highland Guatemala petty states, and Yucatan city-states;
**minimal for huge peripheries invaded late**. MacLeod inventories source categories with
biases: archaeology; post-conquest native/mestizo authors in Roman script (Chimalpahin, Alva
Ixtlilxochitl, Muñoz Camargo, Tezozomoc; Códice Pérez, Books of Chilam Balam, Annals of the
Cakchiquels); Spanish ethnographers ([[spiritual-conquest-and-colonial-church|Sahagún]] foremost);
conquerors’ letters; early chroniclers; Crown tribute counts and 1570s *relaciones geográficas*;
native petitions and *títulos* often in Nahuatl (colonial lingua franca).

### 3. First two colonial centuries — continuity, extractive neglect, and northern wars

For most of Mesoamerica the first two colonial centuries set the scene for debates about **nature,
periodization, pace, and regional variation of change**, without agreement on what change or
stasis means. Recent trends try to see change/stasis “from the inside out,” breaking with earlier
models of oppression on a mostly passive mass.

- **Central Mexico (Nahuatl school).** Continuity and slow change in the first colonial century
  or so: few Spaniards; tribute/forced-labor economy relied on native elite administration;
  Spanish language/culture penetrated slowly; natives adopted Spanish tools, crops, and animals
  but clung to [[altepetl]], *calpulli*/*tlaxilacalli*, and *tlatoque*/*pipiltin* under Spanish
  guises (*cabildo*, cabecera, sujeto, [[encomienda]], *gobernador*). Religious change was
  likewise slow: mass baptisms, but many unbaptized/uncatechized for decades after 1520; formal
  outward Christianity with folk syncretism or rare outright opposition. The school tends to
  treat population collapse, new tributes, Spanish officials, [[congregacion|congregación]], and
  Indian slave migrations as **important but secondary** to preconquest cultural persistence —
  critics reply that ~90% population loss in an agrarian society must have been transformative.
  (See [[central-mexico-indigenous-colonial]].)
- **Yucatan.** Relative cohesion ascribed either to local political elites plus Spanish neglect
  (no silver/plantations until nineteenth-century henequen) or to assigned extractive roles
  (tributary labor and capitation tax) that slowed cultural change until mature capitalism
  ([[lowland-maya-colonial-modern]]).
- **Oaxaca.** Natives retained lands and languages more than elsewhere: few Spaniards, and
  sought-after luxuries (silk; later especially **cochineal**) required specialized manual labor
  unattractive to elites — indigenous production with surpluses extracted by petty merchants and
  *alcaldes mayores* as agents of Mexico City–Veracruz–Spain interests
  ([[oaxaca-indigenous-colonial-modern]]).
- **Highland Maya / Chiapas–Guatemala.** “Only more so”: large native numbers, few Spaniards,
  lack of desired export product made indigenous production and labor the main wealth; heavy
  seasonal migrant labor (forced and semi-voluntary) to Creole–ladino intensive zones, with
  surprisingly little impact on local cultures ([[highland-maya-colonial-modern]]).
- **Northern frontier.** Later conquests, vast often-desert spaces, nomadic/semi-nomadic warfare
  cultures; murderous wars of attrition; Spanish scorched-earth and search-and-destroy; northwest
  silver lure; Jesuit/Franciscan missions; mines and subsidiary haciendas using dislocation,
  encomienda, draft labor, and introduced southerners (e.g. Tlaxcalans). Best Spanish general
  tactic became extermination and demographic obliteration by weight of numbers. Stubborn
  survivals (Tarahumaras, Tepehuanes, Sonora peoples, Yaquis) remain research problems
  ([[northwest-mexico-indigenous-colonial-modern]], [[northeastern-mexico-indigenous-colonial-modern]]).
- **Zones of refuge.** Porous unconquered deserts/mountains/jungles (Nayarit highlands, Sierra
  Gorda, Petén, and lowland Maya forests) mixed refugees who reformulated ways of life; reduced
  pressure on subjugated peoples and may have made local Spaniards more cautious about onerous
  labor or taxes (Aguirre Beltrán’s *regiones de refugio*).

Some parts of Mesoamerica remained outside Spanish control until late in the colonial era (e.g.
highlands of Nayarit, Sierra Gorda, Petén).

### 4. Mid–late colonial commercial, Malthusian, and Bourbon pressures (18th c.)

From mid-eighteenth century significant changes pressed native populations: demographic growth
met little available land in western Mexico and the Bajío because village lands rented out or
lost to commercial expansionist [[hacienda|haciendas]] → Malthusian pressures; market-oriented
estates near cities drew migration and eroded ethnic identity; plantation industries (sugar,
indigo) attracted labor. Great epidemics returned: *matlazáhuatl* (probably typhus) of 1737–38
erased some villages and helped turn parts of the center-west definitively mestizo; *año de
hambre* of 1786 remembered for accompanying dearth; maize-price spikes in the last ~20 years
before independence, especially 1808–1811.

The [[bourbon-reforms|Bourbon regime]] (via Mexico, Guadalajara, Puebla, Guatemala), despite
half-starts and failures, was far more interventionist than the Habsburgs: more efficient
tax/tribute collection; attack on clergy power and especially **cofradías** (Indian cultural
redoubts) and some *cajas de comunidad*. Combined with commercialization and hacienda
encroachment: disappearance of ethnic distinctiveness and proletarianization in some regions; in
the indigenous south (Oaxaca, Yucatan, Chiapas, Guatemala) populations grew but living levels and
community coherence probably declined.

### 5. Independence and Liberal assault (early–mid 19th c.)

Received knowledge (challenged by volume chapters showing earlier state-level attacks) holds that
the **great assault on Native American society—especially communal lands—came not primarily in
the colonial period** but during Mexico’s Liberal Reforma, with focal villain the **Ley Lerdo of
1856** prohibiting corporate-owned lands. Spanish paternalism had treated natives as a protected
caste of free but lowly vassals; litigious indigenous villages and institutions (cofradía, *caja
de comunidad*) provided partial shelter. After independence, ambitious regional elites structured
government for profit and land access.

Liberals in Mexico and Guatemala rejected colonial corporate structure for a society of free
individuals under law; rejected protectionist legislation and corporate ownership — adversely
affecting communal villages. State laws breaking up landholdings often led not to redistribution
among occupants but purchase by the powerful. Loss of land worse in the west (especially Jalisco),
minor in the center; attacks began earlier than Ley Lerdo in several new Mexican states.
Guatemala under Mariano Gálvez (1831–38): two-peso peasant head tax with many confiscations.
Native responses: legal proceedings, switch to smallholdings, rural revolts (Sierra Gorda
multi-year).

**Caste War of Yucatan (1847).** Embittered by land loss to henequen plantations, Maya revolted
seeking to drive all others from the land; years of warfare reduced population by perhaps half;
autonomous regime mostly in today’s Quintana Roo; independence until 1901 or beyond in isolated
areas. Paradox: Yucatan elites, effectively independent of Mexico after 1839, had to call in
national troops or lose the peninsula — war brought Yucatan definitively into the Mexican state
while giving local Maya long autonomy ([[caste-war-yucatan-1847]]).

In Guatemala, native peoples and peasants rallied to caudillo **Rafael Carrera** (direct/indirect
rule 1837–1865), who halted and sometimes reversed Liberal land confiscations; armies had large
Mayan village and rural components.

Independence-era indigenous participation was uneven: center-west rural proletariat with
messianic, anti-Spanish beliefs supported stages of the Hidalgo revolt; Morelos region more
clearly indigenous; north little information; Guatemala saw rising village unrest half a century
before Central American independence.

### 6. Porfirian land assault and forced labor (late 19th–early 20th c.)

After his first term, Porfirio Díaz proved a greater enemy of Indian landholdings than
predecessors. Decrees of 1875, 1883, 1894 extended baldío alienation: estimated ~**96 million
acres** of vacant state lands turned over to private ownership; 1883 law let land companies
survey public lands and take up to one-third plus buy the rest cheaply. By early twentieth century
land companies held about one-fifth of Mexico’s land mass; most villages lost all or part of
ejidos. By 1900 peons including hacienda workers lived poorer than in the late colonial period.
Científico positivism cast the Indian as a drag on development — mixture of paternalism and
oppression. Yaquis of Sonora suffered deportations mostly 1900–1910 to Yucatan henequen
plantations (estimates up to 15,000 ejected/enslaved; many authorities ~half that); highland
Tzeltals and Tzotzils of Chiapas suffered deportations to Tabasco lowlands on foreign-owned
rubber/lumber projects.

Nineteenth-century demography is impressionistic because “Indian” classification (language,
lifestyle, phenotype; incentive to escape head tax and labor drafts) is unstable. Mexico around
independence: slightly over 6 million, perhaps half indigenous; by Reforma total ~7.8 million but
indigenous ~one-third; thereafter total grew to just over 15 million by 1910 while native
component grew slowly or not at all, remaining under 3 million. Guatemala retained a clear rural
Indian majority outside cities and ladino villages.

Guatemalan **Liberal Revolution of 1871** and Justo Rufino Barrios built export-led coffee
development, encroachment on Indian lands, and *Reglamento de Jornaleros* (1877) reconfirming
colonial-era *mandamiento* forced-labor drafts; Estrada Cabrera continued similar policies.

### 7. Revolution, indigenismo, and late twentieth century

Indigenous roles in the Mexican Revolution (1910–29+) are hard to analyze amid disagreement over
the Revolution’s meaning. Zapata fought for return of alienated village lands and expropriation of
one-third of hacienda lands as ejidos. Constitution of Querétaro 1917 **Article 27** enabled
common landholding → ejido emphasis. Redistribution sped under Obregón and Calles (~11 million
acres); Cárdenas (1934–38) redistributed ~49 million acres in four years (~12,000 villages); by
1940 one-third of Mexicans had received land, much as ejidos. Ejido often judged economic failure
but social/political success.

**[[indigenismo]]** became government policy under Cárdenas; cultural by-products (archaeology,
muralists Rivera/Orozco/Dr. Atl, novels) often judged more important than the political program.
By the 1960s critics debated incorporation versus autonomy; development/assimilation accused of
paternalism. After Cárdenas, land reform lost impetus; post-1940 peasantry and native peoples lost
relative income/health/education share; Alemán favored commercial agro-business; by 1970s–80s
indigenous states (Oaxaca, Yucatan, Chiapas) saw falling living conditions.

Late 1980s–1990s under Carlos Salinas: Article 27 altered, ending the ejido system based on it;
individual owners could rent, mortgage, sell shares. Salinas’s NAFTA support preceded the
**Chiapas uprising of January 1994** under the EZLN (most participants Chiapas peasants of
indigenous background). In Guatemala, Ubico’s vagrancy laws, the 1944 Revolution and Arbenz
Decree 900, U.S.-sponsored overthrow, military rule, and 1980s–90s massacres of entire Indian
communities devastated the countryside; after 1996 peace prospects some refugees returned;
autonomous organizations (Mexico FIPI; Guatemala COMG) push native-language education, political
participation, and pan-Indian exchanges after disillusionment with indigenismo paternalism and
neoliberal rollback.

## End Conditions

No single end: indigenous peoples remain demographic and political forces across Mexico and
Guatemala. Late-twentieth-century neoliberal land reform and free-trade integration transformed
but did not erase corporate community claims; EZLN, COMG, FIPI and related organizations mark a
shift from elite-mediated indigenismo toward indigenous self-representation. The process
continues under Contemporary-period national and international indigenous-rights regimes.

## Colonial resistance historiography (MacLeod)

Research boom on colonial Indian resistance/rebellion has been driven by history-from-below,
anti-colonial mood, and rich Spanish documentation of crises (authorities often overestimating
importance). Naive approaches label any native violence as anti-colonial revolt — from village
fisticuffs through *corregidor* riots to rare pan-regional rebellions (rare except on frontiers).
Counter-view: few uprisings, few inland city fortifications, weak militias until eighteenth-century
reforms imply a *pax hispanica*. Middle position (James Scott everyday resistance; E. P. Thompson
moral economy; Ranajit Guha subaltern studies) widens analysis to passive and active
accommodation and reformulation of daily colonialism. Caveats: failed revolts rarely brought
lasting improvement; winners were Spanish colonialism; peasant survival struggles involve many
non-colonial hostilities (credit, soils, plagues, climate).

## Historiography

MacLeod stresses how much native peoples have been **written about by others** and how little
comes from natives about themselves — a situation already changing at the volume’s publication
(2000). No Native American contributors among the nine chapter authors may reflect educational
and institutional failures. Source categories for the contact baseline are listed above; each
carries bias (Christianized native chroniclers proclaiming Spanish monarchy’s benefits; conquerors’
self-justification; archaeology’s underrepresentation of peripheries and non-monumental peasant
tropics).

Standing interpretive axes for this process:
- Continuity (Nahuatl school / Lockhart) vs. transformative impact of collapse and colonial
  institutions.
- Black Legend vs. White Legend on demographic catastrophe ([[black-legend]]); scholarly
  consensus that preinvasion populations were large and pandemics the main proximate cause
  ([[pre-columbian-american-population]], [[virgin-soil-epidemics]]).
- Spiritual conquest extremes vs. multiple ordinary-people Catholicisms
  ([[spiritual-conquest-and-colonial-church]]).
- Colonial paternalism as partial land protection vs. Liberal/Porfirian corporate dismantling.
- Indigenismo as cultural-political incorporation vs. later indigenous autonomy critique
  ([[indigenismo]]).

Bibliographical anchors in MacLeod’s essay include the *Handbook of Middle American Indians*;
*Historia de los pueblos indígenas de México* (Rojas Rabiela & Ruz); Cook & Borah population
history; Lockhart *Nahuas After the Conquest*; Gibson *Aztecs Under Spanish Rule*; Farriss and
Patch on Yucatan; Aguirre Beltrán on refuge zones; Ricard and Taylor on spiritual conquest;
Van Young on late colonial crisis; Reed on the Caste War; Markiewicz and others on Revolution
and agrarian reform. Regional chapter authors (Deeds, Frye, Van Young, Cline/Schryer, Romero
Frizzi, Jones, Lovell, and others) supply the monographic depth this overview compresses.
