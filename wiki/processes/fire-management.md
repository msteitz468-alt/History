## Definition and Scope

Fire management is the human capacity to control fire — to create it at will,
maintain it, and harness it to practical purposes — using accumulated cultural
information. Johan Goudsblom (Ch. 8 of CWH Vol. I) argues that fire management is
a **"species monopoly"**: all human societies control fire, and no other species
does. It is therefore a defining feature of humanity and a clear product of
cultural accumulation and collective learning.

There is a strong case for counting fire management as the **first technological
threshold** in human history (see [[technology-and-innovation]]). Goudsblom frames
fire through the conceptual triad of **energy, matter, and information**: fire is
a form of energy used to manipulate matter, but managing it requires information
(cultural knowledge transmitted across generations).

## Definition and Scope — What It Is Not

Fire itself is ancient and natural. Fires require oxygen (rare before the oxygen-
rich atmosphere of 2–3 billion years ago), fuel (rare before land plants spread
in the last 400 million years), and ignition (lightning and other natural sources).
Hominin *familiarity* with fire is therefore very old — as old as the species'
presence in fire-prone environments. Fire *management* — the deliberate creation
and control of fire — is the distinctively human development, and it is this that
the process page tracks.

## Causal Drivers

- **Cognitive and cultural capacity**: managing fire requires the ability to
  accumulate and transmit knowledge — collective learning.
- **Dietary advantage (Wrangham hypothesis)**: Richard Wrangham argues cooking
  was critical in human evolution, allowing ancestors to "pre-digest" high-value
  foods (meat, otherwise-toxic or indigestible plants). Cooking provided high-
  energy foods using little metabolic energy. The shortening of the human gut
  within the last ~2 million years may be an indirect sign of the importance of
  cooking and therefore of fire management.
- **Norbert Elias's insight**: controlling fire also meant increasing control by
  humans over their own behavior (a step in the "civilizing process").

## Major Phases / Turning Points

| Phase | Approximate date | Development |
|---|---|---|
| Long familiarity | before ~2 million BP | Hominins familiar with natural fire, as all animals are |
| Earliest claimed management | ~800,000 BP (contested) | Some archaeologists argue for fire management in Israel and South Africa |
| Firm controlled use | ~250,000 BP | Earliest firm dates for controlled use by hominins |
| Cooking revolution | across Early/Late Prehistory | High-energy foods; gut shortening; defensive weapon; focus for sociability |
| Fire-stick farming | Behavioral Modernity onward | Aboriginal Australians and others use fire to increase land productivity — a foretaste of agriculture |
| Agrarianization | Neolithic | Slash-and-burn clears land; responsible for much Neolithic deforestation; new division of labor (blacksmithing, etc.) |
| Industrialization | Long 19th Century+ | Fossil fuels (coal, oil, gas) — the "subterranean forest" (Sieferle) — drive manufacturing and transport |
| Electrification & nuclear | 20th century | Indirect consumption of fire's energy; fire as weapon (most nuclear victims died from fire) |

## Geographic Spread

Fire management spread with the human lineage out of Africa (see
[[out-of-africa-1-1800kbp]] and [[out-of-africa-2-60kbp]]). The dating of the
earliest controlled use is geographically contested, with the strongest early
claims from Israel (Levant) and South Africa. Notably, **Neanderthals could
maintain fires but apparently could not make fire from scratch and left no
evidence of sewn clothing** — a striking limitation given their cold-climate
habitat (see [[late-prehistory]]).

## Interaction with Other Processes

- **Cooking and human evolution**: fire management is implicated in brain-size
  increase and gut reduction.
- **Agriculture**: fire-stick farming and slash-and-burn link fire management
  to the [[neolithic]] transition.
- **Industrialization**: fossil-fuel combustion is the third great fire-driven
  revolution, connecting this process to the modern energy economy.
- **Technology generally**: see [[technology-and-innovation]]; fire is the
  archetypal first technology.

## End Conditions

Fire management is ongoing and indeed central to the modern energy crisis.
Goudsblom notes that today fire is often invisible (electrification moves
combustion far from the point of consumption), which may be why we speak of
"energy" rather than fire. The process may eventually require new forms — e.g.,
tapping the energy of the sun (the "largest source of fire in our solar system")
directly. The process has no end; it has transformed continuously since the
Paleolithic.

## Mythological and Religious Dimension

Fire's necessity and danger meant it loomed large in myth and religion — as a
god-like force or a power to be seized and used, as in the Prometheus legend.
This is a recurring emic theme in the cultural record of fire.
