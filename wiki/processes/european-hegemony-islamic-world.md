## Definition and Scope

European hegemony over the Islamic world designates the condition — established by the generation of 1789 and persisting through roughly 1945 — in which European powers could, when agreed, enforce their will in virtually all Muslim lands regardless of whether direct colonial rule obtained. As Hodgson frames it: "whether a European power ruled directly or whether there was merely a generalized dependent relation to the European social order as a whole, it was in the power of the Europeans, if they were agreed on a policy, to enforce their will in most Muslim lands" (Book VI, Ch. II).

The process was not reducible to outright military conquest. It operated through an interlocking system of international law, property-rights norms, extra-territoriality, debt leverage, dynastic legitimism imposed from outside, and the self-perpetuating development gap between the [[great-western-transmutation]] zone and the rest of the world.

## Causal Drivers

**The primacy of property rights as the criterion of sovereignty.** European international law recognized a government as legitimately sovereign only if it could guarantee the security of property — especially European property. Failure to do so authorized intervention, which might escalate from forcing a change of officials to full occupation. Extra-territoriality extended European legal jurisdiction into nominally sovereign Muslim territory, subordinating local courts to European consular jurisdiction. This was "generalized from the case of Islamicate dhimmî courts … but under European hegemony it was extended so that the foreign jurisdiction claimed superiority to any local one."

**Dynastic legitimism and the freezing of boundaries.** Europeans insisted on defining fixed frontiers and hereditary successions, eliminating internal competition that had previously been a mechanism of renewal. Protected dynasties that degenerated — as several princely states of India did — were replaced by direct European administration. The Russian manipulation of the Qajar succession in 1834 exemplifies this freezing dynamic.

**Debt as instrument of penetration.** The cycle of Westernizing expenditure followed by financial crisis followed by European financial control played out in Egypt (Khedive Ismail, 1875–82), the Ottoman empire (1870s–1880s), and the Qajar state. Once European controllers managed a state's finances, effective sovereignty had already passed.

**The self-perpetuating development gap.** The dependent economic position proved self-reinforcing: old industries were destroyed by Western competition, local capital was exported, railroad and export enclaves did not diffuse development, and population growth eroded per capita gains. "The very existence of the development gap tended to undermine whatever might have made it easier to bridge it, and even to reinforce whatever could serve to widen it."

## Major Phases

### Phase 1: Initial Resistance (c. 1800–1840)

Rulers in still-independent areas led resistance while recognizing the necessity of modernizing armies. Muhammad Ali in Egypt and Mahmud II in the Ottoman empire represent this phase — modernization from above that simultaneously evoked bitter resistance from those displaced. In India, the 1857 uprising ("Sepoy Mutiny" or the first Indian national rebellion) marked the last major armed attempt to restore the pre-British order; its failure placed Muslims under particular suspicion.

### Phase 2: Upper-Class Accommodation (c. 1840–1870)

Moneyed classes began Westernizing projects voluntarily. The [[tanzimat-reforms]] (from 1839 under Abdülmecid) formalized this for the Ottoman empire: civil equality, European-style law, secular schools, and acceptance into the Concert of Europe after the Crimean War (1856). Egyptian khedives competed in Europeanizing Cairo. The Qajar Shah toured Europe and granted concessions to European entrepreneurs. Muslim elites "so learned to see themselves through European eyes that they accepted identification as 'Eastern'."

### Phase 3: Rising Nationalism (c. 1870 onward)

The British occupation of Egypt (1882), the French seizure of Tunisia (1881), the Russian absorption of Turkistan, and the Ottoman constitutional crisis of 1876–78 shattered the accommodationist moment. "In this phase, much of such goodwill as existed toward things Western was breaking down." Nationalism emerged as the organizing framework: Mustafa Kamil in Egypt, Young Ottomans, pan-Islamic sentiment under Abdülhamid II, and ultimately the Young Turks (1908) and the Arab Revolt (1916).

### Phase 4: Completion of Conquest and Contested Settlement (1880–1920)

After 1880 the "new imperialism" absorbed the sub-Saharan interior and other remaining territories. By 1905 all of sub-Saharan Africa was partitioned. The dissolution of the Ottoman empire after World War I replaced direct Muslim territorial sovereignty with League of Nations mandates in the Arab east and eliminated the last significant Islamic great power — except for Kemalist Turkey, which survived by carving out a defensible national-territorial core.

## Geographic Spread

The process unfolded unevenly:

- **India and Southeast Asia**: under direct European rule (British, Dutch) earliest, from the eighteenth century.
- **Ottoman empire**: formally sovereign but systematically penetrated through debt, Capitulations, extra-territoriality, and national minority agitation backed by European powers.
- **Egypt**: nominally Ottoman, effectively independent under Muhammad Ali's dynasty, then under British occupation from 1882.
- **North Africa (Maghrib)**: French conquest of Algeria from 1830; Tunisia 1881; Morocco 1912; Ottoman Tripolitania seized by Italy 1911.
- **Iran (Qajar state)**: formally independent but divided into British and Russian spheres; dynastically managed by Russia; sovereignty de facto hollow.
- **Central Asia**: Russian absorption of the Uzbek khanates from the 1860s; the Kazakh steppe colonized by Russian settlers.
- **Sub-Saharan Africa**: Muslim territories divided at the Berlin Conference and in subsequent agreements by 1905.

The disruption of the Islamicate commercial and cultural network was as significant as political conquest. "The primacy of the old-Islamic area from Nile to Oxus … the cultural interdependence of the central Eurasian commercial region … the interrelations of the different coastlands of the Southern Seas — such lines of contact and development of the Islamicate society were weakened or even disrupted and replaced by such common ties as those which arose through being dominated by a single European power."

## End Conditions

The process did not simply end but underwent structural transformation after 1945. Formal decolonization removed direct colonial rule without eliminating economic dependence, which Hodgson treats as the deeper mechanism. Arab defeat in the 1948 Palestine war demonstrated to many that the hegemony persisted in a new form even after nominal independence.

## Historiography

Hodgson's analysis departs from both Eurocentric liberal historiography (which credited European hegemony with "development") and simple anti-imperialist narratives. His emphasis falls on the structural self-perpetuation of the gap and the cultural dislocation suffered by Muslim elites forced to absorb technicalistic forms developed for an alien heritage. Lord Cromer's position (that Islam was "in its nature opposed to progress" and that "reformed Islam is Islam no longer") is presented as simultaneously influential and historically naïve — founded on ignorance of the speed of the Great Western Transmutation itself.

The collection's coverage of this process is uneven: strong for the Ottoman empire and Egypt, moderate for India and Iran, weak for Central Asia and Southeast Asia in this period.
