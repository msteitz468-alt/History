## Definition and Scope

The North American Agricultural Transition encompasses the multiple, regionally variable processes by which hunter-gatherers in North America shifted from foraging to food production over a period of approximately 10,000 years. Unlike a single-origin diffusion, North American agriculture developed in at least two independent centers — the Eastern Woodlands and Mesoamerica — with domestication events in each producing distinct crop assemblages that later diffused across the continent. The transition was not a single event but a gradient from intensive foraging through cultivation to full domestication, unfolding at different rates in different regions from at least 6000 BCE forward.

The transition stands as one of North America's most consequential long-term processes, ultimately underpinning the mound-building cultures ([[adena-culture]], [[hopewell-culture]]) of the Eastern Woodlands and the later Mississippian civilizations.

## Causal Drivers

**Holocene climate stabilization**: The Younger Dryas ended abruptly around 9550 BCE. The Holocene that followed was less variable than previous interglacial periods, making dependence on cultivation "possible, if still sometimes episodic and precarious, and a viable solution to population growth." Relative climatic stability was a necessary precondition.

**Population growth**: As Archaic populations grew and bands settled into predictable seasonal rounds, they exceeded the critical threshold size (approximately 500 people) above which self-reliance replaced long-distance sharing networks. Growing populations increased pressure on local resources, incentivizing more intensive extraction and eventually cultivation.

**Niche construction through intensive foraging**: Hunter-gatherers were already modifying their environments — using fire to clear forest patches, selectively harvesting plant stands — before conscious cultivation began. This intensive interaction with plants produced conditions favorable to domestication through unconscious artificial selection: gatherers repeatedly harvested larger, tastier, or more tightly seed-bearing plants, inadvertently selecting for traits that reduced wild fitness but increased food value.

**Environmental opportunity**: Domestication arose "first where conditions were demonstrably most favorable, later where conditions were more difficult." Two regions of North America had the right combination of suitable plants and favorable climate: the Eastern Woodlands (initial domestication c. 3000 BCE) and Mesoamerica (initial domestication c. 8000 BCE). Other parts of North America either lacked suitable domesticable species or faced environmental constraints.

**Animal domestication constraints**: Of 148 mammalian candidates for domestication worldwide, only 24 occurred in the Americas (vs. 72 in Eurasia). North America had no naturally occurring animals suitable for domestication apart from the turkey. This constraint shaped the trajectory: North American agriculture was plant-based, without the animal-power advantage that drove Old World intensification.

## Major Phases

**Intensive foraging (6000–3000 BCE, Eastern Woodlands)**: Archaic people foraging with "considerable skill and a sophisticated knowledge of the native plant species" used hundreds of plants to varying degrees. Fire cleared patches favoring desired species. Processing and storage of acorns, hickory nuts, and other mast extended resource use. This phase involved what Smith (2007) calls "niche construction" — modifying local environments to enhance productivity without yet domesticating plants.

**Eastern Woodlands domestication (3000–1000 BCE)**: Four species evolved through human selection into true domesticates:
- *Cucurbita texana* (squash) — domesticate
- *Iva annua* (sumpweed/marshelder) — domesticate
- *Helianthus annuus* (sunflower) — domesticate
- *Chenopodium berlandieri* (goosefoot) — domesticate

Four more species — bottle gourd (*Lagenaria siceraria*), erect knotweed (*Polygonum erectum*), maygrass (*Phalaris caroliniana*), and little barley (*Hordeum pusillum*) — were intensively managed but lack sufficient archaeological evidence to confirm full domestication status. This assemblage constituted the Eastern Agricultural Complex, centered in the Ohio River Valley.

**Mesoamerican domestication (8000–5000 BCE)**: Wild teosinte evolved into maize (*Zea mays*) through artificial selection in central Mexico, with the earliest complex societies developing in the hot, wet coastal lowlands where highland domesticates flourished in cleared tropical forest, yielding two crops a year. Beans and squash were also domesticated in Mesoamerica.

**Diffusion of Mesoamerican crops northward (post-500 BCE–1200 CE)**: Maize, beans, and squash — the "Three Sisters" — were carried northward and eventually became staples in diets as far north as the Great Lakes. Maize became a staple crop in the Eastern Woodlands only after 400 CE, post-dating [[hopewell-culture]]'s decline. Its adoption enabled the rise of the large permanent towns and chiefly societies of the Mississippian period (c. 800–1600 CE).

**Adena-Hopewell florescence (1000 BCE–500 CE)**: The Eastern Agricultural Complex provided the subsistence base for the moundbuilder cultures. The domestication of native plants "underpinned the elaboration of mound building and burial ceremonialism after 1000 BCE." [[hopewell-culture]] "featured a more intensive use of the Eastern Agricultural Complex" than [[adena-culture]].

## Geographic Spread

Two major centers of domestication emerged in the Americas: Eastern North America (intensive foraging by 6000 BCE, initial plant domestication by 3000 BCE) and Mesoamerica/Central Mexico (intensive foraging by 10,000 BCE, initial domestication by 8000 BCE). Comparable dates from other world regions:

| Region | Intensive Foraging | Initial Domestication |

Native Americans were "completely isolated from Eurasian and African peoples while they were adopting horticulture," making North American domestication independent of Old World processes.

## Interaction with Other Processes

The agricultural transition enabled and interacted with [[hopewell-interaction-sphere]]: surplus production from Eastern Agricultural Complex cultivation supported the specialized craft production and periodic ceremonial gatherings that drove the exchange network.

The transition was deeply shaped by Archaic fire management — "the landscape of America has been in large part anthropogenic for thousands of years." Fire management was not merely a precursor to agriculture but a form of environmental manipulation that shaded into cultivation.

The failure of this transition in some regions (Great Basin, Arctic) reflects both environmental limits and the principle that "horticulture did not arise at all" where "local conditions" precluded it, not cultural conservatism or lack of intelligence.

## End Conditions

The transition did not "end" in a conventional sense — rather, it reached a stable state of mixed agriculture and foraging in most regions. In the Eastern Woodlands, maize adoption after 400 CE intensified agricultural production and enabled population growth that transformed social organization, culminating in the Mississippian period. In the Great Basin and other arid regions, Archaic adaptations persisted until European contact. The disruption of indigenous agricultural systems by European colonization after 1492 constitutes the true endpoint of this process as an autonomous indigenous development.

## Historiography

The study of North American agricultural origins has been transformed by multiple revolutions since the 1960s. Flotation techniques recovered plant remains from sites where older excavation methods missed them. Ancient DNA analysis has directly traced the genetic histories of domesticated species. Radiocarbon dating has permitted precise dating of first appearances. Bruce Smith's work (1989, 2001, 2007) has been particularly influential in documenting the Eastern Woodlands domestication center and articulating the "niche construction" framework for understanding how foragers became farmers. Key debates include the degree to which domestication was conscious vs. unconscious, the role of climatic stress vs. opportunity in triggering intensification, and the relationship between Eastern Woodlands domesticates and the later arrival of Mesoamerican crops. The source base for Mesoamerican domestication is strong; the Eastern Woodlands center is increasingly well documented; other potential North American domestication centers remain poorly understood.
