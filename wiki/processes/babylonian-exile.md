# The Babylonian Exile and the Restoration

The **Babylonian Exile** (conventionally 586–538 BCE) was the deportation of the elite
of [[kingdom-of-judah|Judah]] to Babylonia after [[nebuchadnezzar-ii|Nebuchadnezzar II's]]
destruction of [[fall-of-jerusalem-587bce|Jerusalem]], the formative crisis of Israelite
religion, and the origin of the **Jewish diaspora**. Unlike the [[fall-of-samaria-722bce|
Assyrian deportation of the northern kingdom (722)]], whose exiles vanished into the
local population, the Judaean exiles **kept their identity and returned** — making the
Exile not an ending but a transformation. [[boardman-cah-v3p2-1991|CAH Vol. III, Part 2]]
(Ch. 31, T. C. Mitchell) treats it; the Babylonian social setting is in
[[neo-babylonian-civilization]].

## Definition and Scope

The "Exile" spans the deportations of **597** (Jehoiachin and the elite), **587/586**
(after the destruction of Jerusalem), and **582** (after Gedaliah's murder), to the
**decree of [[cyrus-the-great|Cyrus]] (538)** permitting return, and beyond it to the
**rebuilding of the Temple (516)**. It is the heir-practice of
[[assyrian-mass-deportation|Assyrian mass deportation]] applied by the Neo-Babylonians —
with the crucial difference that the exiles' descendants returned.

## The three communities

Mitchell traces three Judaean communities after 586:

- **Palestine under Babylonian rule.** Nebuchadnezzar left **Gedaliah ben Ahikam**, of a
  distinguished official family (not the Davidic line), as governor at **Mizpah** (Jerusalem
  being destroyed). Soldiers and refugees gathered to him, but he was **assassinated**, and
  in the aftermath **Johanan ben Kareah took the survivors — including the prophet Jeremiah —
  to Egypt**. Judah was left depleted, with Edomites and others pressing into the south.
- **The exiles in Babylonia.** Settled in communities on canals near **Nippur** (Ezekiel's
  *nəhar kəbar*, the Kabaru canal; place-names like *tēl abīb*), the deportees were **not
  enslaved but settled as taxpaying farmers**, keeping their own elders and identity (see
  [[neo-babylonian-civilization]]). **Jehoiachin** received rations attested on Babylonian
  tablets ("Ya'ukin king of Judah") and was released from prison by **Amel-Marduk (562)**.
  By the fifth century (the **Murashu archive**) Jews were active in commerce. This community
  produced the prophet **Ezekiel** and, in much scholarship, the **synagogue** and the
  editing of the scriptures.
- **The diaspora in Egypt.** Judaean settlement in Egypt — older than 586 (mercenaries) but
  swollen by the refugees with Jeremiah — is vividly attested by the fifth-century **Aramaic
  papyri of the [[neo-babylonian-civilization|Elephantine]] colony**, which kept an
  unorthodox temple to Yahweh and "knew nothing of synagogues."

## Religious transformation

The Exile reoriented Israelite religion away from **temple and land** toward **text, law,
and covenant**. The prophet **Ezekiel** (exiled 597) moved from warnings of Jerusalem's
fall to visions of restoration — the **valley of dry bones** and the ideal future Temple.
The **book of Kings** was compiled in this period (586–539) as a theological explanation of
the catastrophe; the exilic and post-exilic prophets (Ezekiel, Second Isaiah) reframed
disaster as purification. See [[hebrew-prophets]].

## The Restoration

In **539** [[cyrus-the-great|Cyrus]] took [[fall-of-babylon-539bce|Babylon]], and his
policy of restoring deported gods and peoples (the **Cyrus Cylinder**) extended to the
Jews: a **decree (538/537)** — preserved in two forms in Ezra — authorized the **rebuilding
of the Temple** on its old foundations and the return of its vessels. **Sheshbazzar** (the
*nasi'*) and then **Zerubbabel** (a Davidic descendant) led returning exiles; after delays,
the **Second Temple was completed in 516** under Darius I. Many Jews, however, **remained in
Babylonia and Egypt** — the permanent diaspora.

## End Conditions

The Exile "ended" politically with Cyrus's decree and the rebuilt Temple, but its great
product — a **diaspora people defined by scripture and covenant rather than by a state** —
was permanent. The returned community became the **Second Temple Judaism** from which both
later Judaism and Christianity descend.

## Interaction

- Method inherited from [[assyrian-mass-deportation|Assyrian deportation]]; agent the
  [[neo-babylonian-empire|Neo-Babylonian Empire]] under [[nebuchadnezzar-ii|Nebuchadnezzar II]].
- Triggering event: [[fall-of-jerusalem-587bce|the fall of Jerusalem]]; ending event:
  [[fall-of-babylon-539bce|the fall of Babylon to Cyrus]].
- Contrast: the [[fall-of-samaria-722bce|deportation of Israel (722)]], whose exiles did
  **not** return — the "ten lost tribes."
