## Definition and Scope

The Austronesian expansion is the dispersal of Austronesian-speaking peoples — and the languages, agricultural package, and maritime technology associated with them — from an origin in Taiwan across island Southeast Asia and the Pacific over roughly four millennia (~5000–1000 BP). It is one of the most geographically extensive demographic and linguistic expansions in human prehistory, ultimately placing Austronesian speakers from Madagascar in the west to Rapanui (Easter Island) in the east.

This page treats the process as evidenced in the Pacific Islands specifically, drawing on linguistic analysis (Darrell Tryon and collaborators), archaeological evidence from the Lapita cultural complex, and genetic data, as synthesised in [[denoon-cambridge-pacific-islanders-1997]] chapter 2. For the maritime technology and navigational practices, see also [[pacific-voyaging-and-settlement]].

The Austronesian expansion is distinct from the earlier (Pleistocene) settlement of Near Oceania, which was accomplished by fully modern humans (~40,000 BP) using watercraft and without the Austronesian agricultural package. The Austronesian process overlaid that earlier substrate and enabled the crossing of the Remote Oceania barrier, where naturally occurring foods were insufficient to support hunter-gatherer colonisation.

## Causal Drivers

**Linguistic foundation.** Austronesian languages "are so closely related that linguists choose to represent them as members of a family, whose relationships can be expressed like a genealogy in which recent languages are the offspring of older ones, no longer spoken." The sequencing of language splits provides the chronological skeleton, calibrated by archaeological rather than purely linguistic evidence (since "languages do not change at a regular or predictable pace"). Proto-Austronesian split into Formosan and Malayo-Polynesian groups "around 5000 BP when some people moved south to the Philippines and Sulawesi."

**Agricultural package.** "The combined package of crops and the three Pacific domesticates — pigs, dogs and chickens — formed a systematic agricultural complex. Perhaps this is what enabled the first permanent settlers to cross the Remote Oceania barrier." The crops included taro, yam, breadfruit, and other species; "many crops which were carried into the Pacific during the Lapita period have also been found in pre-Lapita South-East Asia, and the words which describe many of them can be traced to Malayo-Polynesian (spoken about 5000 years ago)."

**Maritime technology.** The familiar "seagoing outrigger and dugout canoes of the Pacific were only developed much later, allowing the Austronesians to disperse." Reconstructions of Malayo-Polynesian terms include those for "boat, sail, outrigger, rollers to beach a canoe, cross-seat or boat ribs, paddle, to steer or rudder, and boat/sea travel." A critical addition in Lapita times was the double-hulled canoe. Roger Green argues that "an effective voyaging system based on dugout canoes or rafts was already in place in Near Oceania, but to it was added the outrigger canoe, the double canoe, new developments in 2-boom triangular sail technology as well as an ability to navigate these improved sailing vessels in return voyages over distances independent of having land in sight."

## Major Phases

### Phase 1: Taiwan to Island Southeast Asia (~5000–4000 BP)
Proto-Austronesian split from Formosan languages as groups moved south into the Philippines and Sulawesi (~5000 BP). Malayo-Polynesian "broke up when people moved from Sulawesi to north Maluku at about 4500 BP." A further split occurred "with a move to the east, probably centred in Cenderawasih Bay in north-western Irian Jaya, perhaps around 4000 BP." "Island South-East Asian radiocarbon chronology shows the spread of Neolithic cultures from Taiwan through the Philippines to eastern Indonesia."

Dating method: radiocarbon; calibrated against language-family sequencing. Confidence: century-level.

### Phase 2: Bismarcks and Lapita Emergence (~3500–3000 BP)
"A further spread east resulted in Austronesian and Lapita settlement in the Bismarcks by 3500 BP." The Lapita cultural complex — first defined by "its distinctive decorated pottery, which used toothed stamps to impress designs on the pot before firing" but also including "stone adze forms, ornament types, rectangular stilt houses in sedentary villages, and introduced domestic animals" — became the material signature of the expansion. Its "immediate homeland lay in the Bismarck Archipelago."

The Lapita homeland shows signs of integration between intrusive Austronesian elements and pre-existing Melanesian material culture: "In brief, Lapita is basically of South-East Asian Austronesian origin, although some elements were invented and added in the Bismarcks, and some pre-Lapita Melanesian elements were integrated into it." Elements of clear Southeast Asian origin include "particular kinds of pottery, pigs, dogs and chickens, quadrangular stone adzes, polished stone chisels, various shell ornament types, rectangular houses (some on stilts), large villages, language, and probably aspects of boat technology."

The "complementary distribution of Austronesian languages and the Island South-East Asian Neolithic and Lapita cultural complexes suggests dates for the spread of both." Archaeological evidence indicates Lapita was carried east as Oceanic Austronesian languages "fragmented as Lapita settlements spread south and east through the main Solomons and into Vanuatu and New Caledonia at about 3200 BP."

Dating method: radiocarbon; pottery typology. Confidence: century-level. Source: Lapita pottery design evidence from Spriggs and Kirch cited in [[denoon-cambridge-pacific-islanders-1997]].

### Phase 3: Colonisation of Remote Oceania / West Polynesia (~3200–3000 BP)
"The next move was from northern Vanuatu to Fiji, Tonga and Samoa by 3000 BP." The span of Lapita stretches from "Manus and the Vitiaz Straits (between New Guinea and New Britain) in the west to Tonga and Samoa in the east." The crossing of the Remote Oceania barrier — where "thirty genera of land birds and 162 genera of seed plants find their eastern limits" beyond the main Solomons — was made possible by the agricultural package: it overcame the "paucity of naturally occurring foods" that had limited earlier Pleistocene colonists.

"First settlement of Polynesia by the Lapita culture and the absence of any but Polynesian languages there suggest that Polynesians are direct descendants of the bearers of Lapita culture." An admixture event occurred: "Genetic evidence suggests that Fijians mixed with Island Melanesians after first settlement by Lapita groups. The original Fijian population would have been more Polynesian in appearance."

Simultaneously, the first colonisers reached the Mariana Islands in northern Remote Oceania "about 3200–3000 BP. There are specific parallels in pottery style with the Philippines and Sulawesi at this time. The languages of the Marianas and Palau are related most closely to those of the Philippines and eastern Indonesia in a separate Austronesian migration contemporary with Lapita."

### Phase 4: Post-Lapita Divergence and Micronesian Settlement (~2500–2000 BP)
"The Lapita complex ended about 2500–2000 BP. In Polynesia the dentate-stamped pottery was succeeded by plain-wares and a distinctively Polynesian material culture." Exchange systems contracted sharply: "Obsidian was never again carried so far." "After Lapita, exchange systems contracted sharply ... from about 2000 BP diversification seems stronger than cultural unity in Island Melanesia."

"Several Micronesian archipelagos were settled at about 2000 BP." Nuclear Micronesian languages are a "late offshoot from Lapita, while West Micronesia seems to have been settled by different colonists from island South-East Asia."

### Phase 5: East Polynesia (~1600–1000 BP) and Final Settlement
"At least a thousand years elapsed after 3000 BP, between Lapita settlement in Tonga and Samoa, and the colonisation of East Polynesia. Perhaps this interval was needed to develop a new voyaging strategy which operated mainly upwind across the great distances of East Polynesia."

"Most of East Polynesia was first settled between 2000 and 1000 BP: the Marquesas in AD 300–600, Hawai'i at about AD 650, the Cooks and Tahiti at about AD 750–800 and Rapanui (Easter Island) towards the end of the first millennium. New Zealand was first settled even later, in the period AD 1000–1200." "Occupation of the more remote Chatham Islands at about 500 BP completed the settlement of the Pacific Islands."

Dating method: radiocarbon dating of earliest settlement layers. Confidence: century-level; some individual island dates are decade-level. The earliest radiocarbon date for East Polynesia is "AD 300 for a site on Nuku Hiva, Marquesas Islands."

## Geographic Spread

The expansion proceeds in a documented arc: Taiwan → Philippines → Sulawesi → Maluku/Cenderawasih Bay → Bismarck Archipelago → Solomon Islands → Vanuatu / New Caledonia → Fiji / Tonga / Samoa (West Polynesia) → Marquesas / Hawai'i / Cooks / Tahiti / Rapanui (East Polynesia) → New Zealand / Chatham Islands. Simultaneously: Philippines/eastern Indonesia → Mariana Islands (West Micronesia); Bismarck/Solomon area → Nuclear Micronesia.

The Talasea obsidian trade reached "its greatest extension at the same time [as Lapita peak settlement], from Borneo to Fiji, a span of 7000 kilometres." This long-range exchange network is a material proxy for the integration of the Lapita sphere before it contracted post-2000 BP.

## Interaction with Pre-Existing Populations

Near Oceania was inhabited for ~35,000–40,000 years before Austronesian arrival. The interaction between incoming Lapita populations and pre-existing Non-Austronesian ("Papuan") speakers was complex: "Lapita lost its distinctiveness and a variety of Creole cultures developed." There was likely marital and cultural exchange: "The need for Lapita groups to establish peaceful relations with earlier settlers probably eroded preference for marrying their own kin, and technological transfer allowed these other groups to 'catch up'." In northern New Guinea, "the word for 'pig' in many Non-Austronesian languages is an Austronesian loan-word even in inland areas, a hint of the late introduction of this animal."

Polynesia itself, settled by Lapita-derived groups in Remote Oceania where there were no prior populations, shows fewer traces of this interaction: "The Lapita move into Remote Oceania... brought colonists into an uninhabited area, where local recruitment was not possible." Genetic evidence "down-plays any direct link between Polynesians and Micronesians." However, the 500-year intermediate settlement in Tonga and Samoa before East Polynesian dispersal produced "a distinctively Polynesian material culture" distinct from Island Melanesian trajectories.

## End Conditions

The process reached effective completion with the settlement of New Zealand (~AD 1000–1200) and the Chatham Islands (~AD 1500). "Settlement stopped when there were no more islands to find and the eastern edge, the Americas, had been located and briefly contacted." A South American contact episode brought the sweet potato (*Ipomoea batatas*) and its Quechua term *kumar* into East Polynesian economies: "it is more likely that the contact was made by Polynesians seeking new islands to colonise, than by South Americans."

The Austronesian expansion did not create a unified or integrated Pacific world — it produced instead a diversity of cultures shaped by isolation, local environment, and the post-Lapita contraction of inter-island exchange networks. "The region's famous [Melanesian] diversity may be a product of the immediate post-Lapita era."

## Historiography and Dating

The chapter in [[denoon-cambridge-pacific-islanders-1997]] is explicit about the provisional nature of all dates: "Every sentence should include 'probably': but most have been omitted because their reiteration would be tedious." Radiocarbon dating "only covers the last 40,000 or 50,000 years" and standard deviations can embody "7000 years of uncertainty." Thermoluminescence dating is discussed as an expanding but still controversial supplement.

The "continuity argument" in Lapita studies — that Lapita was a purely local development from pre-Lapita Bismarck cultures, without western input — is not supported by the balance of evidence: "the evidence supports a South-East Asian origin." The linguistic evidence of Tryon's *Comparative Austronesian Dictionary* and Pawley and Ross's "Austronesian Historical Linguistics and Culture History" provides the genealogical sequencing; the archaeological evidence (Kirch, Spriggs, Allen) provides the spatial grounding.

Key unresolved gap: a "gap in sites of the relevant period in northern New Guinea and islands to the west" means "the connection between South-East Asia and Melanesia is not absolutely certain." Post-Lapita back-migration of Oceanic Austronesian speakers from east to west has obscured the signal in northern New Guinea.
