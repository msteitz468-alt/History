# Pan-Indian Movement

Cross-tribal political, religious, and cultural organizing that forged shared Native identity and institutions under reservation, boarding-school, and urban experience—and that later defeated termination as a defendable policy.

## Definition and Scope

“Pan-Indian” here means organizations, rituals, and political languages that address *Indians as Indians* across tribal lines, not only single-nation politics. Hoxie (CHNPA I.2 ch. 12) stresses that industrial transport made unprecedented Native-to-Native communication possible; Washburn (ch. 15) tracks mid–late twentieth-century national organizations (NCAI vs AIM) and Canadian counterparts. The process is not monolithic: cultural pan-Indianism (dances, churches), educated-elite associations, tribal-government federations, urban centers, and radical protest coexist and often conflict.

## Causal Drivers

- enabled_by industrial rail/auto networks that linked distant agencies (Hoxie intro).
- Reservation confinement and [[allotment-policy]] land loss created shared legal and economic grievances (Black Hills claim; Oklahoma dissolution resistance).
- [[indian-boarding-schools]] produced English-literate cadres who could lobby Washington and publish journals.
- Religious innovation (peyote / Native American Church; residual Ghost Dance) crossed tribal boundaries.
- Postwar [[termination-policy]] and urban relocation injected fear and concentrated multi-tribal populations in cities—caused_by termination as stimulus (Hoxie).
- WWII service and wage work broadened contact with non-Indians and among tribes.

## Major Phases

### Cultural and religious pan-Indianism (c. 1890–1920)
- Grass Dance (originally Pawnee/Omaha warriors’) appropriated by Teton Sioux and other Plains groups as mark of “traditional” dancer at multigroup gatherings; performed at government-sponsored July 4 / Dominion Day celebrations as cover for distributions to the needy, competitions, intertribal visits, women’s and children’s dances, and crafts—amplified by rail and automobile.
- Peyote religion: rail to Laredo (1881) enabled bulk transport; Lipan Apache teachers among Oklahoma Comanches and Kiowas in the 1880s; spread 1900–1920 to Winnebago, Ojibwa, Taos, Sioux, Cheyenne, Crow, Shoshone, Arapaho, Ute; likely Canadian Plains by mid-1920s. Quanah Parker defended peyote in constitutional terms. Native American Church of Oklahoma 1918; national umbrella 1944 (officers from Cheyenne, Kiowa, Crow, Oto)—part_of pan-Indian institutionalization; contrasts_with government/missionary prohibition.
- Ghost Dance continued after Wounded Knee among Kiowas, Comanches, Pawnees, Saskatchewan Sioux, Wyoming Shoshones (Wovoka d. 1932).

### Early intertribal political organizations (c. 1911–1924)
- **Black Hills Council (1911):** nine Sioux communities plus Cheyennes; planned litigation over 1876 Black Hills acquisition as fraudulent.
- **Alaska Native Brotherhood (1912):** Tlingit/Haida/Tsimshian; English-language; anti-segregation.
- **Society of American Indians (1911):** Ohio State; membership limited to Natives (Euro-Americans as “associates”); journals and Washington office; weakened by peyote and BIA-abolition disputes; empty treasury by 1920; assembled cadre (Eastman, Montezuma, Bonnin, Coolidge, Cornelius, Parker) and enabled later national organization.
- **All Pueblo Council path:** 1913 legal victory → 1922 Santo Domingo meeting against Bursum bill validating squatter claims; press appeal; Collier/Atwood coalition.
- Four Mothers Society (Redbird Smith) and Creek “Snake Uprising” (Chitto Harjo) against Oklahoma allotment/statehood.

### Institutional federation and termination struggle (1940s–1960s)
- **National Congress of American Indians (NCAI):** 1942 discussions among BIA Indian employees (D’Arcy McNickle et al.); first convention Denver Nov 1944 (Mark L. Burns; 27 groups; Napoleon B. Johnson president); constitution to “preserve Indian cultural values” and treaty rights. 1953 Joseph R. Garry president: tribes as “nations within a nation.” Feb 1954 emergency conference of 43 groups (over one-third of US Indian population) issued “Declaration of Indian Rights”: “Reservations do not imprison us… they are our ancestral homelands.”
- Hoxie: by declaring reservations ancestral homelands, NCAI forged pan-Indian identity on **shared separate national heritage** (not merely race/culture)—once articulate Indians rejected termination, it could not be justified as “in the best interests” of Indians. opposed: [[termination-policy]]; produced: [[tribal-self-determination]] language/trajectory.
- Urban Indian centers (Chicago, Los Angeles, Denver, San Francisco, etc.) were “by definition pan-Indian” (Hoxie on relocation).

### Radical and national protest (1960s–1970s) and conventionalization
- Washburn: NCAI as loose organization of tribes/individuals closest to official tribal-government spokesman; **American Indian Movement (AIM)** as radical organization of individuals often challenging existing tribal governments—exemplified by the 1973 siege at Oglala Sioux village of Wounded Knee (contains: [[wounded-knee-occupation-1973]]); Canada: Assembly of First Nations, Métis National Council, Inuit Committee on National Issues.
- Trail of Broken Treaties (1972), Longest Walk (1978); anthropologists drawn into activism not always welcomed by established tribal governments.
- AUTHOR POSITION (Washburn): 1960s–70s radical challenge gradually replaced by conventional debate; AIM leaders Dennis Banks and Russell Means sought to influence rather than abolish tribal governments; “John Collier was, in effect, vindicated.”
- Canadian White Paper 1969 (termination analogue) provoked Red Paper *Citizens Plus* and pan-Indian mobilization; contributed_to movement intensity (analogous_to: [[termination-era-1953]]).
- Oka crisis 1990 (78-day Mohawk armed defence) as high-visibility Canadian flashpoint concurrent_with [[haudenosaunee-confederacy]]; analogous_to Wounded Knee 1973 occupation.

## Geographic Spread

Plains ceremonial circuits; Oklahoma as peyote and allotment-resistance hub; Alaska Native Brotherhood coastal Tlingit/Haida/Tsimshian; Pueblo Southwest inter-village council; national headquarters politics in Denver/Washington; postwar multi-tribal cities (Chicago, LA, Denver, Bay Area, Dallas, Cleveland); Canadian national organizations and Quebec/British Columbia flashpoints.

## Interaction

- Concurrent religious infrastructure: peyote religion / Native American Church; residual Ghost Dance.
- Builds on and sometimes conflicts with IRA-era tribal governments ([[indian-reorganization-act-1934]]).
- Direct antagonist of [[termination-policy]]; ally of claims litigation ([[black-hills]], Indian Claims Commission).
- Urban relocation under termination-era administration enabled multi-tribal organizing.
- Forward into [[native-american-renaissance-1960-1995]] and government-to-government rhetoric of [[tribal-self-determination]].

## End Conditions

No single end: cultural pan-Indianism continues in powwow circuits and churches; NCAI-style federation remains institutional core; radical AIM-style protest peaked and partially conventionalized per Washburn’s reading. The durable product is permanent-homeland / nations-within-a-nation language that made assimilationist “freedom from supervision” politically untenable after the 1950s–70s.
