## Definition and Scope

This process traces Latin American states' and societies' long engagement with the [[pacific-ocean]] as "simultaneously a conduit for material exchanges and as a laboratory for ideological experiments" — in the formulation of Edward Melillo in [[hattori-samson-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v2-2023]]. The arc runs from the Manila Galleon system (1571–1815), through guano and nitrate extraction and the War of the Pacific (1879–84), trans-Pacific labor migration, twentieth-century Maoist and neoliberal ideological circuits, Chile's copper-for-Chinese-goods economy, the contested annexation of Rapa Nui, and finally Chile's twenty-first-century "tricontinental" strategic posture. Throughout, as Melillo frames it, the Pacifico functions as the site where "material and ideological flows have mingled in the tides of history."

The process is distinct from, though connected to, the general [[transpacific-economy]] entry, which addresses the broader economic geography of the Pacific; this page focuses on the specifically Latin American political and social dimensions of Pacific engagement. See also [[mesoamerica-andes-pacific-contacts]] for pre-colonial antecedents.

## Causal Drivers

The Manila Galleon Trade was driven by Ming China's shift from rice to silver as the unit of taxation across the empire between 1520 and 1619, creating insatiable demand for silver coin. Potosí's mines (discovered 1545) provided the supply through the mita forced-labor system. Commercial asymmetry — Latin America as raw-material exporter to wealthier cores — persisted structurally. In the words of Eduardo Galeano, cited in the source: "Development is a voyage with more shipwrecks than navigators."

## Major Phases

### Phase 1 — Manila Galleon System, 1571–1815

Following Spain's conquest of Manila in 1571, the Pacific served as a thoroughfare for Spanish galleons carrying American silver to Asian entrepôts. "Spanish merchants and their Asian counterparts developed the world's first truly global trade circuit. Social, economic, and biological exchanges enlivened this network, and for the first time in history, people, commodities, and organisms circulated among Asia, Europe, the Americas, and Africa."

The focal point was the **Cerro Rico (Rich Mountain) of Potosí**, Bolivia, at 5,000 meters elevation in the Viceroyalty of Peru. The Spanish viceroy Don Francisco de Toledo organized the **mita** system in 1575, forcing each of sixteen Indian provinces to send annually one-seventh of their adult male population to the mines. The mita lasted from 1575 to 1825. In 1669, Count Lemos, the Peruvian viceroy, wrote: "There is no people in the world so exhausted. I unburden my conscience to inform your Majesty with due clarity. It is not silver that is brought to Spain, but the blood and sweat of Indians." The phrase *plata es sangre* (silver is blood) acknowledged but did not alter the system.

Silver traveled by donkey train and coastal vessels to **Acapulco**, where West African slaves minted it into pesos and loaded it onto galleons — which "frequently exceeded 1,000 tons and took as many as 2,000 trees to build" — for the trans-Pacific passage to Manila. Chinese merchants exchanged silk, jade, tea, porcelain, and lacquerware for the precious metal. The Mexican peso became "the most widely circulated coin in history" (historian William Schell Jr). Spain's King Philip II attempted in 1593 to cap the trade at 250,000 pesos annually; by the end of the sixteenth century, ships were smuggling an annual average of 5 million pesos from Mexico to the Philippines.

Throughout the seventeenth century, silver reaching China averaged **50 tons per year**; Ming Emperor Wanli's reign (1572–1620) "relied rather precariously on the shifting fortunes of the Spanish silver trade." Silver inflow eventually caused uncontrollable inflation, "which hastened the downfall of the Ming Dynasty in 1644."

**Botanical transfers**: The Manila Galleons (along with Portuguese ships at Macau) introduced the **peanut, maize, and sweet potato** to China, transforming Chinese agriculture and demography under the Ming and Qing (1644–1911) dynasties. "No large group of the human race in the Old World was quicker to adopt American food plants than the Chinese" (Alfred Crosby, *The Columbian Exchange*, 1972). These three crops enabled Qing-sponsored settlement of western China, becoming "staples of settler colonial agriculture" in territories historically inhabited by Tibetans, Uighurs, and Miao people.

The Manila Galleon Trade lasted 1571–1815, "making it one of the world's longest-lived, continuous trade routes."

### Phase 2 — Guano, Nitrates, and the War of the Pacific, 1840s–1884

Chilean sodium nitrate and Peruvian guano drove "The First Green Revolution in global agricultural history" (Melillo, *American Historical Review*, 2012). Between 1875 and 1929, Chile exported **nearly 80 million metric tons** of sodium nitrate from Atacama Desert mines. A "relentless procession of clippers and steamships docked at Chile's northern ports, filled their holds with burlap bags of the powdery fertilizer, and conveyed these sacks from Latin America's Pacific coast to North American and European ports."

The **War of the Pacific (1879–84)** determined which state would control this wealth. The decisive naval engagement was the **Battle of Angamos** (8 October 1879), in the Pacific waters off the Mejillones Peninsula (22S, 70W): "two armoured Chilean frigates bombarded and captured Peru's ironclad, steam-propelled warship Huáscar." Chilean naval blockade of Callao followed, setting the Peruvian fleet on fire and shattering port defences.

In 1881, Colombian writer Adriano Páez warned: "Chile will have a coastline more extensive than that of Brazil on the Atlantic. And since neither Ecuador nor Colombia has a fleet, Chile will dominate from the Strait [of Magellan] to the Panamanian Isthmus." Other observers derided Chile as "the Prussia of the Pacific."

The **Treaty of Ancón (1883)** gave Chile the provinces of Tarapacá, Tacna, and Arica. **Bolivia forfeited coastal access** when it ceded the Pacific seaport of Antofagasta to Chile in 1884, resulting in Bolivia's landlocked status that persists to the present (the International Criminal Court reaffirmed in 2018 that Chile had no obligation to grant Bolivia sea access). Chile secured dominion over a 644-kilometre expanse of sodium nitrate mines.

### Phase 3 — Trans-Pacific Labor Migration: The Coolie Trade, 1847–1874

"Just as the trans-Atlantic slave trade was losing its battle with trans-Atlantic Abolitionism, a new trans-Pacific trade in unfree labour arose." From 1847 to 1874, "upwards of 100,000 'coolies' from China came to Peru aboard an estimated 276 ships." A New York Daily Times correspondent in 1853 reported: "Vessels, it appears, are equipped for the business upon the model of slave ships... The victims – men, and even children – are kidnapped."

No fewer than **sixty-eight "coolie ships" experienced some sort of uprising** during their trans-Pacific passage. Maritime writer Basil Lubbock noted that "a rising of the coolies was the one terror that ever stalked behind the captain of a Chinese coolie ship." Those who reached Peru worked on cotton and sugar plantations, as domestic servants, on Andean railroad construction, or on the **Chincha Islands** digging seabird guano — "three granite outcroppings located 21 kilometres off Peru's southwest coast." Employers promised freedom after 3–5 years; timely release was rare. Peru had abolished slavery in 1854 but willingly substituted *la trata amarilla* (the "yellow trade").

Global shipping firms including **W.R. Grace and Company** profited from the trade. International outrage eventually closed it; on 26 July 1874, representatives of the Qing government and Peru signed the *Tratado de paz, amistad, comercio y navegación* (Treaty of Peace, Friendship, Commerce and Navigation), formally ending the coolie trade. Mexico and China signed a similar treaty on 14 December 1899.

Former coolies settled in Lima's **Barrio Chino** (Chinatown), a few blocks from the Plaza de Armas. In the kitchens there arose the spontaneous fusion of Peruvian and Cantonese food traditions known as **chifa** (from the Cantonese *chī fàn*, "to eat"): arroz chaufa, tallarines saltado, wantán frito. By the 1920s, chifa had become the most popular cultural expression of Peru's Chinese population.

### Phase 4 — Twentieth-Century Ideological Trans-Pacific Circuits

**Peru and Maoist China**: Abimael Guzmán Reynoso, founder of the **Sendero Luminoso (Shining Path)**, made extended visits to China in the 1960s during the Cultural Revolution (1966–76). He visited a cadre school in 1965 and trained at a military school in Nanjing. His fraternal relations with the Chinese Communist Party enhanced his prestige; his followers regarded him as the "Fourth Sword" of communist thought after Marx, Lenin, and Mao. The guerrilla war of the 1980s, "costing 20,000 lives," drew on this Maoist trans-Pacific ideological pipeline. Guzmán was captured in September 1992.

**Peru and Fujimori**: The campaign against the Shining Path was orchestrated by **Alberto Fujimori**, born in 1938 to parents who had immigrated from Kumamoto, Japan. Fujimori — dubbed *El Chino* by the media — defeated writer Mario Vargas Llosa in the 1990 election. His authoritarian neoliberal programme "Fujishock" sent food prices up 500 per cent and gasoline prices up 3,000 per cent; he dissolved congress in an *autogolpe* and promulgated a new constitution (1993). After fleeing to Japan in 2000, he was extradited from Chile and sentenced to multiple prison terms for human rights violations, bribery, and money laundering.

**Chile's Pinochet Era and the Chicago Boys**: A US-backed coup on 11 September 1973 overthrew the democratically elected socialist President Salvador Allende, who had won a three-way election in 1970. Allende's programme — *la vía chilena al socialismo* — included land reform, nationalizations, minimum wage increases, and health care expansion. From 1970 to 1973, the CIA spent US$8 million in covert action to undermine it. General Augusto Pinochet's dictatorship murdered thousands, jailed and tortured tens of thousands, and "disappeared several hundred opponents in the Pacific Ocean, which became a watery grave for dissenters." From 1975, a cohort of Chilean economists known as the **"Chicago Boys"** implemented sweeping tariff reductions, austerity, and privatization. Chilean economist Pablo Barahona (a Friedman student who became Pinochet's economy minister) declared in 1980 that "an authoritarian government — absolutely authoritarian — that could implement reform despite the interests of any group, no matter how important it was, was needed." Milton Friedman in a 1982 Newsweek editorial called Chile "an economic miracle."

The Pinochet dictatorship ended after a popular referendum in February 1988. The Concertación governments of Aylwin, Frei, Lagos, and Bachelet (1990–2010) instituted truth-and-reconciliation processes "yet none of these governments posed major challenges to the neoliberal economic policies that had been hallmarks of the Pinochet era."

### Phase 5 — Chile, China, and Copper, 1952–Present

Copper has been central to Chile's Pacific position for most of the twentieth century; mines at Chuquicamata, Escondida, Collahuasi, and El Teniente rank among the planet's most extensive copper reserves — referred to as *el sueldo de Chile* ("Chile's salary") or *oro rojo* ("red gold").

In 1952, Chile became "the first Latin American nation to sign a trade agreement with the People's Republic of China." The same year, the two countries inaugurated the Instituto Chileno Chino de Cultura. In 2006, China concluded a free trade agreement with Chile — the first it forged with any Latin American nation. By 2012, "China was consuming 80 per cent (US$ 14 billion) of Chile's copper exports."

During the 1980s–90s, neoliberal advocates positioned the Pacific as the geographical stage for their success story. Ronald Reagan declared in May 1984, "The great Pacific Basin... that is the future." Chilean political pundits began calling their country a "jaguar" — South America's counterpart to the Asian Tiger Economies. Chile hosted the **Sixteenth APEC Leaders' Meeting in 2004**, the first Latin American nation to do so. Chilean politician Ignacio Walker wrote in 2006: "Chile, with 4,000 miles of coasts, looks face to face with Asia and the Asia-Pacific."

The **Asian Financial Crisis of 1997** disrupted "Pacific Century" triumphalism; investor panic and currency crises engulfed much of Southeast Asia, exposing overreach.

### Phase 6 — Tricontinental Doctrine and Rapa Nui, 1888–Present

**Chile's annexation of Rapa Nui (Easter Island)**: Chile annexed the 170-square-kilometre island on 9 September 1888; it is located 3,600 kilometres west of the mainland port of Caldera and is "the world's most remote, continuously inhabited island." Polynesian canoe voyagers settled it around 1200 CE and created more than 900 mo'ai statues from compressed volcanic tuff (the largest possibly weighing 165 tons). Dutch explorer Jacob Roggeveen was the first European to land, on Easter Sunday 5 April 1722. Peruvian slave raids in the late 1800s, combined with smallpox and tuberculosis, reduced the Native population from over 6,000 in 1862 to approximately 600 by 1869.

Following annexation, the Chilean government confined the surviving Rapa Nui to the town of Hanga Roa and rented the rest of the island to the Anglo-Chilean Williamson-Balfour Company for use as a sheep farm for more than half a century. When the lease expired in 1953, the Chilean Navy assumed control. In 1964, schoolteacher **Alfonso Rapu** led an uprising forcing the Chilean government to return land rights and cultural patrimony; he became the first Rapa Nui person elected mayor. On 22 February 1966, President Eduardo Frei signed the "Easter Island Law" creating the Departamento de Isla de Pascua, ending military rule and granting full Chilean citizenship. Since 2010, ongoing disputes over land rights, healthcare, garbage from tourism, and fisheries management have continued to plague the Rapa Nui; the Rapanui language (East Polynesian, related to Māori) is spoken by fewer than 2,000 people.

**Chile tricontinental**: The geopolitical doctrine declaring Chile *un país tricontinental* (a tricontinental country) spanning South America, Oceania, and Antarctica was first conceived by geographer Hernán Santis Arenas in 1990 and has since been incorporated into official government publications and grade-school curricula. Chile's Antarctic Province (Provincia de la Antártica Chilena) encompasses southern Tierra del Fuego and Chile's Antarctic zone from 53W to 90W, registered under the Antarctic Treaty of 23 June 1961. This westward and southward expansion is framed as parallel to Chile's nineteenth-century northward expansion into the Atacama during the War of the Pacific.

Historian Arif Dirlik and theorist Rob Wilson noted "the glibly economistic habit by which Latin America is reified as an impoverished 'South' and thus excluded from contemporary Pacific Rim Discourse, even though Chile has the longest coastline of any country in the Pacific."

## Geographic Spread

The process began in the Andean altiplano (Potosí) and the colonial Mexican Pacific coast (Acapulco), with its commercial node in Manila. It then concentrated along the South American Pacific seaboard (Callao, Valparaíso, Antofagasta, Antofagasta's Atacama hinterland) before the twentieth century added East Asian capitals (Beijing, Tokyo) and the Pacific Rim ideological discourse centered the entire rim. Rapa Nui extends Chilean presence deep into Polynesia.

## End Conditions

This process has no terminal date; it continues into the contemporary period with China as Chile's largest trading partner and ongoing Rapa Nui sovereignty disputes. The terminal year of 2022 in the chapter title reflects the source's endpoint but not a historical closure. Chile's tricontinental doctrine and APEC membership mark the most recent ideological crystallization of Pacific ambitions that trace back to 1571.

## Historiography

Melillo (in [[hattori-samson-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v2-2023]]) synthesizes a wide literature from economic history, environmental history, and Latin American studies. His framing draws heavily on dependency theory (Galeano's *Open Veins of Latin America*) while insisting on agency from below: "from the Quechua Indian mitayos who mined Potosí's silver to the Chinese cooks who invented chifas, ordinary people have done as much as tyrants, traders, and technocrats to create the Pacific World." The chapter blends economic, social, environmental, and cultural history across five centuries, with explicit attention to Indigenous dispossession (Rapa Nui, Mapuche).

Key secondary sources include Melillo's own work (*American Historical Review*, 2012; *Strangers on Familiar Soil*, 2015), Giráldez (*The Age of Trade*, 2015), Flynn and Giráldez ("Born with a 'silver spoon'," 1995), Galeano (*Memory of Fire*; *Open Veins*), Crosby (*The Columbian Exchange*, 1972), and the Rettig/Valech Reports on Pinochet-era human rights violations.

**Interpretive debates**: The chapter presents the Ming collapse debate but notes "an opposing viewpoint" exists to the silver-inflation thesis (von Glahn, 1996). The coolie trade numbers are contested (Stewart: 90,000; Meagher: 109,146). The framing of Chilean neoliberalism as "economic miracle" vs. human rights catastrophe is explicitly treated as an ideological controversy, not an open interpretive question — Melillo takes a clear position.
