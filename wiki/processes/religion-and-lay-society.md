# Religion and Lay Society in the Carolingian World

## Definition and Scope

This is the study of how Christianity actually shaped the lives of ordinary lay people in the
[[carolingian-empire]] — how the faith reached, disciplined, and was appropriated by the laity. In
[[mckitterick-ncmh-v2-1995|NCMH II]] (J.M.H. Smith, Ch. 24) the central finding is that by this period
Christianity had penetrated lay life so deeply that **"its only antithesis was paganism"** — yet its forms
remained remarkably varied across the empire.

## Baptism and Belonging

**Baptism** was the universal rite of Christian belonging — so much so that the Old English and Old Welsh
words for baptism stood in effect for conversion itself. The ancient adult initiation rite had been adapted
to a world of **infant baptism**: in Wessex, infants were to be baptised within thirty days of birth on pain
of a fine. Beneath great local variety in ritual lay a common structure — instruction, exorcism, immersion or
aspersion — and reformers pressed (often unsuccessfully) to restrict baptism to **Easter and Pentecost** and
to episcopally controlled baptismal churches, asserting order over a diverse practice.

## Penance and Discipline

**Penance** — increasingly shaped by the **penitentials** (handbooks tariffing sins and their satisfactions,
of Irish and Anglo-Saxon origin) — extended the church's reach into the moral and interior life of the laity,
demanding that confessors weigh "the differences of times and persons, of places and ages." Penitential
discipline, the regulation of **marriage**, and the policing of residual **pagan and "superstitious" practices**
brought religious norms into the most intimate areas of daily life.

## The Cult of Saints

The **cult of saints** and their **relics** was the most vital form of lay devotion — saints as patrons,
healers, and intercessors, their shrines the focus of pilgrimage and local identity. The Carolingian state
sought to regulate it (the Synod of Frankfurt in 794 forbade the veneration of new, unapproved saints),
channelling popular devotion toward officially sanctioned cults.

## Significance

By 900 the framework of medieval lay religion — baptism, penance, the mass, the cult of saints, the parish —
was substantially in place, and "the reception of religious norms in every aspect of quotidian lay life" (as
the volume's Conclusion puts it) had made Christianity the universal idiom of European society. The reform of
the church was directed at the moral welfare of the subject, and thereby widened the church's and the state's
claim on everyday life.

## Historiography

NCMH II (Smith) reflects the turn toward the social and cultural history of religion — the study of "lived"
or "popular" Christianity — drawing on penitentials, liturgical books, hagiography, and conciliar legislation.
A key methodological problem is that the sources are prescriptive (what reformers wanted) rather than
descriptive (what people did), so the gap between norm and practice must always be read critically. Connects
to [[carolingian-church-reform]] and [[christianisation-of-anglo-saxon-england]].
