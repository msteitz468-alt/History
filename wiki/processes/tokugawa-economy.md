# The Tokugawa Economy (c. 1600–1800)

The transformation of [[japan|Japan]] under the [[edo-period|Tokugawa peace]] from a
war-torn agrarian society into **one of the most urbanized and commercialized economies in
the pre-industrial world** — a growth that the political order (a hereditary warrior class
living on fixed rice stipends) could neither fully control nor share in, producing the
chronic **fiscal crisis** that drove the era's reform politics. From the
[[cambridge-history-japan-v4-early-modern|Cambridge History of Japan Vol. 4]] (Furushima
Toshio, ch. 10 — village and agriculture; Nakai Nobuhiko & James L. McClain, ch. 11 —
commercial change and urban growth; Tsuji Tatsuya, ch. 9 — eighteenth-century politics).

## Definition and Scope

The Edo economy is the **commercialization of an agrarian base** within a closed country
([[sakoku]]) and a frozen four-status order. Its motor was the conjunction of **peace**, the
**[[kokudaka-system|kokudaka]] settlement** (a registered peasantry tilling its own plots,
taxed in rice), and the **withdrawal of the [[samurai]] into castle towns** — which created
huge concentrated consumer markets that the countryside had to feed and supply.

## The urban revolution

Nakai and McClain show that between 1550 and 1700 Japan "became one of the most urbanized
societies in the world." In 1550 only **Kyoto** topped 100,000; by 1700 four cities had
passed that mark and **5–7% of all Japanese lived in large cities** — against ~2% in Europe,
where only the Netherlands and England-Wales were more urban. **Edo became the largest city
in the world** by 1700 (approaching a million), while **Osaka** and **Kyoto** rivaled London
and Paris. The seed was the **castle town** (*jōkamachi*): as daimyo gathered their samurai,
then artisans, merchants, and laborers, around their fortresses and established licensed
markets, the *bushi*-consumption city replaced the medieval temple-town and free port. The
three great cities specialized — **Edo** the political consumer capital, **Osaka** the
commercial and credit "kitchen of the realm," **Kyoto** the luxury-crafts center — knit by
coastal shipping and the highways into a **single national market**.

## The agricultural revolution

Furushima documents a parallel **rural transformation**: massive **land reclamation and
irrigation** roughly doubled arable land over the seventeenth century; **double-cropping**,
selected and seed-saving sowing methods, commercial fertilizers (dried sardine, oil cake),
and printed **farm manuals** raised yields. Above all, proximity to the urban markets pulled
villages into **commercial agriculture** — vegetables, fruit, cotton, indigo, mulberry,
tobacco, and branded specialty produce (the sealed Kyoto *makuwa* melons). Yet the village
remained a **cooperative unit** (the *mura*): irrigation works, communal forest and pasture,
and shared water rights bound households together even as wealth differentiated them, and the
village collectively answered for its tax (*murauke*).

## Commerce, finance, and the merchants

Commercialization bred sophisticated institutions: wholesale houses (*ton'ya*), licensed
merchant guilds (*kabunakama*), money-changers, the great houses (the **Mitsui**, from 1673),
and the **Dōjima rice exchange** in Osaka (1730), where the price of the daimyo's tax-rice —
and so the income of the entire samurai class — was set in what was effectively a futures
market. Because samurai income was fixed in rice while their wants were met in a money
economy, the warrior class fell **chronically into debt to merchants** of low official
status — the central economic tension of the period (see [[chonin-culture]]).

## The fiscal crisis and the three reforms

The same growth undermined bakufu and *han* finances, whose **land-tax revenue could not keep
pace** with a monetized, commercializing economy. Tsuji Tatsuya's chapter ties the era's
political history to this strain through the **three great reforms**, each a conservative
attempt to restore solvency and "frugality":

- **Kyōhō Reforms** under shogun **Yoshimune** (1716–45) — retrenchment, land-tax reform,
  debt relief for vassals, currency adjustment, and the cautious lifting of the ban on
  Western books.
- **The Tanuma era** (c. 1767–86) — **Tanuma Okitsugu's** mercantilist turn: taxing and
  licensing merchant guilds, monopolies, and reclamation ventures. Reviled by contemporaries
  as corruption, he is **rehabilitated** by modern historians as a pragmatic response to
  commercial reality.
- **Kansei Reforms** under **Matsudaira Sadanobu** (1787–93) — a moralizing reaction against
  Tanuma: retrenchment, cancellation of samurai debts, ideological discipline.

Tsuji's revisionist point (echoed in Hall's Introduction): once the Confucian moral rhetoric
is stripped away, the **policy aims of the various leaders were strikingly similar**, and the
deeper development was the quiet **professionalization of the bakufu bureaucracy**,
especially the Finance Office — experienced administrators, not ideologues, increasingly
shaping policy.

## Interaction

The Tokugawa economy is the material substrate of [[chonin-culture]] and
[[genroku-culture|Genroku culture]], the strain behind the reform politics of the
[[edo-period|Edo period]], and — in the standard reading — the **foundation of Japan's rapid
post-1868 industrialization**: a commercialized countryside, a national market, a literate
mercantile class, and sophisticated financial institutions already in place.

## Rulers, producers, and consumers; the ecological base (New Cambridge History of Japan)

The [[howell-early-modern-japan-asia-world|New Cambridge History of Japan Vol. II]] adds two
fresh angles. Komuro Masamichi & Bettina Gramlich-Oka (ch. 8) reframe the economy through the
interplay of **rulers, producers, and consumers** — foregrounding consumption, household
economy, and monetary policy rather than the older ruler-centered fiscal story. And Brett
Walker's environmental chapter (ch. 9) supplies the **ecological metabolism** beneath the
commercial revolution: the cash-crop boom depended on **marine fertilizers** (herring mulch,
whale oil) and recycled urban nightsoil — a "transfer of energy from the Pacific to Japan's
fields" — while eighteenth-century **famine** (the Tenmei famine of 1782–88) marked the system's
ecological limits. The full account is on [[japanese-environmental-history]].

## End Conditions

The process did not end so much as accelerate and burst its bounds: by the nineteenth century
rural proto-industry and merchant wealth strained the *bakuhan* fiscal order past repair,
feeding the crisis that — with Western pressure — brought the [[meiji-restoration-1868|Meiji
Restoration]] (covered in CHJ Vols 5–6).
