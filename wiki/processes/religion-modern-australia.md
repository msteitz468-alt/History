## Definition and Scope

The transformation of religion in [[commonwealth-of-australia]] from 1901 to the present: the slow uncoupling of personal belief, social practice, and political order; the erosion of Protestant–Catholic sectarianism; the diversification of faiths through immigration; and the contested emergence of a post-Christian but not irreligious public culture. The process is not simply secularisation as linear decline but, in Graeme Davison's formulation in [[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]], "a gradual uncoupling of relationships between personal belief, social practice and political order that were once tightly bound together."

At Federation more than 95 per cent of Australians identified as Christians, yet the religious landscape was "almost as tribal as that of the first Australians" — with Anglicans, Catholics, Presbyterians, Methodists, and a host of smaller denominations forming tight, overlapping social worlds. By 2009 only 47 per cent professed belief in God and 43 per cent said they had 'no religion'.

## Causal Drivers

**Denominational tribalism at Federation.** Four in ten Australians were Anglican; one in five Catholic; Presbyterians c.10 per cent; Methodists 14 per cent. Class and denominational allegiance overlapped closely: Catholics were predominantly Irish-origin and working-class; Presbyterians were predominantly Scots and prosperous. The 1908 papal decree *Ne Temere* (invalid marriages outside the Catholic church) deepened the fault-line and remained a source of grievance for decades.

**World War 1 as spiritual crisis.** Protestant clergy backed the war as holy; Catholics, led by Archbishop Daniel Mannix, resisted conscription. The human cost of the conflict shook individual faith: "There were thousands of non-church members who were still in spiritual revolution as a result of the war…They had fixed their minds on the wreckage in Belgium, France, and England and had ceased to pray" (Congregationalist Sydney Cox, 1919, quoted in [[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]]). Anzac commemorations assumed a secular classical form by the 1920s, separating civil religion from British Christianity.

**Post-war suburban expansion and the 1950s peak.** After 1945 suburban church-building boomed, Sunday school enrolments swelled with the baby boom, and attendance rose modestly. The peak came in 1959 when Billy Graham's three-month crusade attracted more than 3 million Australians, with 130,000 'decisions for Christ' and a record 143,000 at the Melbourne Cricket Ground.

**Collapse from the mid-1960s.** The Sunday afternoon drive eclipsed the Sunday school; between the mid-1960s and mid-1970s Sunday school enrolments were halved. The Student Christian Movement, still drawing hundreds to annual conferences in the early 1960s, had "all but collapsed a decade later." The drivers included individualism, consumerism, flexible leisure patterns, and growing female workforce participation; church membership lost its social function as schools, universities, cafés, and sports stadia provided education, sociability, and transcendence.

**The 1960s–1970s cultural revolution.** Vatican II, the contraceptive pill, and the "Age of Authenticity" hit simultaneously. Humanae Vitae (1968) devastated many Australian Catholics: "People stopped going to confession…If you believed that taking the Pill was wrong, you couldn't go along and say 'I'm not going to do it again' when you already had the next month's supply" (quoted in [[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]]). Ordination of women and homosexuality polarised Protestant denominations.

**Immigration and pluralisation.** Post-war migration Europeanised Australian Catholicism (Poles, Italians, Croatians) and planted Orthodox churches. Buddhism and Islam were the fastest-growing faiths from the 1970s–1990s, the former "easing quietly into the interstices of Australian society," the latter arriving "more conspicuously, through the obligatory observance of distinctive traditions of public prayer, dress, diet, fasting, funerary rituals and pilgrimage."

**Clerical abuse.** By 2012 almost 150 Catholic priests had been charged with sexual offences against children. Revelations of abuse in church-run institutions for child migrants and Aboriginal 'stolen generations' children made "clerical abuse outrank the attacks of militant atheists among the reasons for disowning religion."

## Major Phases

| Phase | Dates | Key dynamics |
|---|---|---|
| Sectarian tribalism | 1901–c.1940 | Protestant–Catholic divide; Freemasonry vs. Knights of the Southern Cross; men's alienation from churches |
| Wartime disruption | 1914–1945 | WWI shatters Protestant patriotism; WWII less sectarian; civic Protestantism peaks at Coral Sea |
| Suburban prosperity | 1945–1959 | Church construction boom; Billy Graham crusade 1959 as high-water mark |
| Rapid decline | 1960–1980 | Sunday school collapse; Vatican II dislocation; Student Christian Movement implosion |
| Fragmentation and alternatives | 1980–present | Pentecostal mega-churches (Hillsong); New Age spirituality; 'believing without belonging'; clerical abuse scandals; Julia Gillard as first avowed atheist PM (2010) |

**Pentecostalism** is the counter-narrative: from ~16,000 in 1961, Australian Pentecostals grew sixfold to 72,000 by the 1980s, eventually overtaking the Uniting Church and ranking second only to Catholics in active participants. Their mega-churches "modernised the medium but not the message."

**The Uniting Church** (1977) — formed from Methodists, Presbyterians, and Congregationalists — became the third largest and most liberal mainstream denomination, but "after 25 years historians…acknowledged 'disappointments', 'mistakes' and 'some decline'."

## Geographic Spread

Religious geography reflected colonial migration: Anglicans strongest in NSW and Queensland; Presbyterians in Scottish-settled western Victoria and New England; Methodists and Baptists in South Australia ('Paradise of Dissent') and Victorian goldfields. Post-war Pentecostalism was strongest in outer suburbs and Queensland. Islam and Buddhism concentrated in metropolitan areas with large migrant populations.

## End Conditions

Not concluded. By 2012 one-sixth of Australians attended church monthly (down from one-third in the 1960s), but the country was "not yet ready to declare itself no longer religious." Belief in God (47 per cent, 2009) remained high by European standards; Anzac had been "quietly transformed…into a civil religion with an elaborate liturgy." The process is one of institutional disembedding rather than belief extinction.

## Historiography

Scholarship divides between a secularisation thesis (Patrick O'Farrell, Walter Phillips) and a "transformation not decline" reading (David Hilliard, Charles Taylor's *A Secular Age*, Graeme Davison in [[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]]). Hilliard's comparative work situates Australia as "towards secularisation and one step back," more secular than the United States but less than Western Europe. The collection note: [[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]] (Graeme Davison, ch. 9) is the authoritative synthesis; primary statistical sources are census data and surveys by the Christian Research Association.
