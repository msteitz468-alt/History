# Aksumite Trade Network

## Definition and Scope

The Aksumite trade network was the commercial and diplomatic system centred on the Red Sea port of **[[adulis|Adulis]]** through which the [[aksum|Kingdom of Aksum]] connected the resources of highland Ethiopia and sub-Saharan Africa to the Mediterranean world (Rome, then Byzantium) and the Indian Ocean economy (Arabia, Persia, India, Sri Lanka). It operated roughly from the first century BCE to the Arab conquests of the seventh century CE, and constitutes one of the major long-distance commercial networks of late antiquity.

This process is distinct from the broader [[indian-ocean-trade|Indian Ocean Trade]] — the Aksumite network was a specific node within it, with Aksum operating both as a producer/exporter of African commodities and as a transshipment point and diplomatic actor in its own right.

## Causal Drivers

**Geography**: Aksum occupied the Ethiopian highlands, giving access to ivory and gold from the sub-Saharan interior, to Red Sea coast ports, and via the Nile valley corridor to Egypt and the Mediterranean. The straight-line distance from Aksum to the Nile is less than 650 km — virtually the same as to Addis Ababa.

**Mediterranean demand**: The Roman Empire's appetite for luxury goods — ivory for furniture, diptychs, and statuary; tortoiseshell; aromatics; rhinoceros horn — made the Aksumite kingdom a favoured supplier. Ivory was the principal export, supplied from elephant populations in the Ethiopian interior and, via Aksumite raiding and trading networks, from more southerly and westerly regions. Roman gold flowed eastward to pay for these goods.

**Aksumite shipping**: By the sixth century Cosmas Indicopleustes recorded that Aksumite ships reached as far east as **Sri Lanka**; the fleet assembled for Kaleb's South Arabian campaign in the 520s CE included significant numbers of vessels built at Adulis itself. Aksumite copper coins recovered from Qana (south coast of Arabia) and pottery with possible Aksumite affinities confirm actual Aksumite commercial presence across the Red Sea.

**Political alignment**: Aksum's adoption of Christianity in the mid-fourth century was partly, as Phillipson argues, a geopolitical alignment with Rome — the king wishing to "ally himself with, while maintaining his distance from, the dominant power in the circum-Mediterranean world." This Christian alignment guaranteed diplomatic access and preferential commercial treatment.

## Major Phases

**Phase 1 — Pre-eminence established (1st c. BCE – 1st c. CE)**: The *Periplus of the Erythraean Sea* (c. AD 50) documents Adulis receiving imports of cloth, glass, brass, copper, iron, and limited wine and olive oil; exports were ivory, tortoiseshell, rhinoceros horn, slaves (from more southerly ports). The king "Zoscales" controlled both Adulis and the hinterland. The network was already integrated into the Rome–India route.

**Phase 2 — Peak florescence (3rd–5th c. CE)**: Aksumite metalworking expertise developed rapidly, replacing most metal imports with local products. Red tableware from Roman North Africa was imported and locally copied. Glass was imported extensively (and eventually produced locally). The coinage system — gold, silver, bronze — began in the late third century with very fine metal, implying substantial gold and silver inflows. Diplomatic missions to Rome are attested; Aksumite coins reached Palestine, Syria, and Berenice (Egyptian Red Sea coast). Technology transfer was substantial.

**Phase 3 — Extension and strain (6th c. CE)**: Aksumite commercial and diplomatic reach was greatest — ships to Sri Lanka, diplomats at Justinian's court in Constantinople, gold expeditions to "Sasu" (near the Sudan border southwest of Lake Tana). At the same time, gold sources near Aksum were becoming exhausted (requiring far-flung expeditions), elephant populations near the capital were being protected by c. 530 CE, and coinage debasement had begun. The political over-extension of Kaleb's South Arabian campaigns (see `[[aksumite-control-yemen-520ce]]`) imposed severe costs.

**Phase 4 — Disruption and termination (570s–7th c. CE)**: Persian seizure of Himyar (570s) ended Aksumite control of the Arabian side of the Red Sea. The Arab conquests severed the Mediterranean–Aksumite axis entirely and led to the **destruction of Adulis**. The trade network that had sustained Aksumite prosperity for six centuries effectively ceased. This was the immediate commercial cause of Aksumite decline.

## Geographic Spread

- **Exports** from Aksum to: Rome/Byzantium (ivory, aromatics); Arabia (goods, soldiers, political influence); Egypt (commercial traffic via the Red Sea and Nile)
- **Imports** to Aksum from: Roman North Africa and Constantinople (red tableware, chancel screens of Proconnesian marble, glasswares, textiles, wine in amphorae from Ayla); Persia/Persian Gulf (glazed pottery, 6th–7th c.); Byzantine Constantinople (marble church furnishings, possibly religious icons in Byzantine style)
- **Transshipment**: Adulis served the Rome–India route — the same vessels that called at Egyptian Red Sea ports (Berenice, Myos Hormos) also traded at Adulis, probably making the same run to and across the Indian Ocean

## End Conditions

The network was terminated by the Arab conquests of the seventh century, which transferred Red Sea commercial dominance to Arab merchants and polities. The destruction of Adulis removed Aksum's port infrastructure. The kingdom fell back on subsistence highland agriculture and local exchange networks. The process did not gradually decline — its commercial basis was severed relatively sharply between the 570s (Persian occupation of Himyar) and c. 640–700 CE (effective Arab control of the Red Sea littoral).
