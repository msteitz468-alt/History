## Definition and Scope

The transformation of gender relations and sexual norms in Australia from Federation (1901) to the present, encompassing women's paid labour and political participation, feminist movements (first, second, and third wave), the decriminalisation of homosexuality, shifts in marriage and reproductive practice, and the persistent limits on full equality for women, Indigenous people, and LGBTQ Australians.

Source: [[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]], chapter by Katie Holmes and Sarah Pinto.

---

## Causal Drivers

**Maternal citizenship and the racial state.** At Federation, women were constituted primarily as "citizen-mothers" within a racialised nation-building project. The 1912 maternity allowance of £5, won by Labor women, was paid to "all white women" — "Asian, Aboriginal and Islander mothers were ineligible," an extension of the [[white-australia-policy]] that some feminists condemned as "the White Australia policy gone mad."

**The Harvester judgment's gender wage gap.** The 1907 Harvester judgment enshrined the concept of the living wage calculated on "the needs of a family of five and applied only to men." Women in manufacturing received just over half the male wage — a ratio that proved structurally persistent for decades.

**Wars as gendered turning points.** World War 1 consolidated the "Anzac" as the dominant masculine ideal and reinforced domestic femininity, while World War 2 radically expanded women's employment into previously male occupations ("welders and truck drivers, farmers and mechanics, secretaries and munitions workers") and brought wages for women in war industries to between 60 and 100 per cent of the male rate.

**The contraceptive pill and second-wave feminism.** Introduction of the contraceptive pill in January 1961 decoupled fertility from sexuality. Alongside it came second-wave feminism: "frustration with workplace inequalities, outrage at the men of the New Left, demand for new models of female desire, insistence on full control over reproduction, and engagement in universities." Achievements listed by Holmes and Pinto include equal pay decisions (1969, 1972), no-fault divorce (1975), single mother benefits (1973), paid maternity leave in the public service (1973), and anti-discrimination laws (1984).

## Major Phases

### 1. Federation to World War 1 (1901–1914): Citizen-Mothers and Race

Women were newly enfranchised citizens but structured in complementary if subordinate roles to male "citizen-workers." The marriage rate was falling (12.3% of women and 22.4% of men remained single in 1911). The 1903 NSW Royal Commission blamed "selfish women" for declining birthrates that had fallen "from six children in the average family in the 1880s to three in 1910." Feminist organisations multiplied, ranging from the conservative Australian Women's National League (52,000 members by 1914) to the more radical United Associations of Women. Male homosexuality was illegal across all States; lesbian sexuality was not formally outlawed but was subject to growing harassment.

### 2. Interwar Modernity (1918–1939): Flappers, Depression, and Eugenics

The economic expansion of the 1920s brought "several versions of femininity" — the flapper, the bachelor girl, the modern woman. Factory work displaced domestic service as the dominant employment for working-class women. But during the Depression of the 1930s, "women in paid work were accused of taking men's jobs." Abortion rates were high: in 1935, Melbourne's Royal Women's Hospital estimated abortions ran "as high as one for every two births," with "poverty" the leading reason women gave. Male homosexuality remained prosecuted; lesbian activity was increasingly policed. In this period child removal from Indigenous families intensified: "the practice was designed to breed out the blackness and to purify white Australia."

### 3. World War 2 and the Post-War Domesticity Regime (1939–1960)

War expanded women's employment and independence; peace brought a determined reassertion of domesticity framed as "imagined solution" to postwar instability. The common image of the 1950s — "suburbia, nuclear families, material prosperity, social stability" — was constructed, not natural. Some 30 per cent of women worked outside the home; married women faced the marriage bar in the public service. For gay and lesbian people the 1950s were "a 'dark decade' of increased persecution": in Victoria a squad was formed with the stated aim to "get" hundreds of "perverts."

### 4. The Long Revolution (1961–1990): Pills, Liberation, and Femocrats

The pill, second-wave feminism, and gay liberation transformed the landscape. Homosexual law reform: South Australia decriminalised male homosexuality in 1972, the ACT in 1976, Victoria in 1980; all remaining States and Territories followed, with Tasmania last in 1997. Since "only 22 per cent of surveyed Australians had supported homosexual law reform in 1967, this was a swift and substantial change." The first Sydney Gay Mardi Gras parade in 1978 was initially conceived as political protest. The Affirmative Action Act of 1986 required employers with more than 100 staff to create equal opportunity programs. "Femocrats" — feminist bureaucrats in government — were instrumental in creating publicly funded refuges, rape crisis centres, and health services.

### 5. Backlash and New Inequalities (1990–present)

The 1990s saw "a backlash": women's policy units were abolished, the Women's Electoral Lobby lost funding, and pro-natalist policies returned (the Howard government's Family Tax Refund echoed the 1912 maternity allowance). By the early 1990s, "the Australian workforce was one of the most gender-segregated in the industrialised world" — women concentrated in low-paid, part-time, and casual roles. The fertility rate fell from a post-war high of 3.5 to below 2 by the 1980s. The HIV/AIDS epidemic "curtailed the possibility of radical sex." Julia Gillard's 2012 parliamentary speech attacking Tony Abbott's "sexism and misogyny" was the "most visible indication of an undercurrent of frustration."

## Geographic Spread

Primarily urban centres: Sydney and Melbourne drove feminist activism and gay liberation; rural and regional Australia retained more conservative gender norms longer. Indigenous communities, spatially and socially marginalised, experienced gendered violence and disadvantage at disproportionate rates throughout the period.

---

## Interaction with Other Processes

- **Race and the state**: Gender regulation was inseparable from racial policy; the maternity allowance, child removal, and sexual surveillance were all racialised instruments.
- **Class**: The labour movement entrenched male breadwinner norms; women's industrial activism was marginalised until the 1970s. See [[class-modern-australia]].
- **Economy**: Women's entry into paid work and rising education participation from the late 1950s were primary drivers of economic and social change. See [[australian-economy-20th-century]].
- **Indigenous Australia**: Indigenous women had reason to "resist the women's movement's critiques of motherhood and the family" because "colonisation had resulted in sustained and continuing attacks on Indigenous family life." See [[indigenous-australia-20th-century]].

---

## End Conditions

The process is ongoing. Fundamental decoupling of fertility from sexuality has occurred; legal discrimination against women and LGBTQ people has largely been dismantled. But "the changes have not been felt equally. Most notably, Indigenous communities continue to experience high levels of gendered violence and disadvantage. While women have infiltrated many areas of traditional male employment, the sexual segregation of the labour market continues."

## Historiography

Holmes and Pinto draw on a rich specialist literature: Lisa Featherstone (*Let's Talk about Sex*, 2011), Frank Bongiorno (*The Sex Lives of Australians*, 2012), Marilyn Lake (*Getting Equal*, 1999), Graham Willett (*Living Out Loud*, 2000), Robert Reynolds (*From Camp to Queer*, 2002), and Miriam Dixson (*The Real Matilda*, 1976). A recurring debate concerns how distinctively "masculinist" Australian society was; Dixson famously described Australian women as "the doormats of the western world." The chapter frames Australian gender history in relation to British and American trajectories while insisting on distinctively Australian features (femocrats, the arbitration wage system, the Anzac mythology).
