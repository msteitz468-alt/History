# Financing Greek Warfare

> **Division of labor:** this **process** page tracks the long-duration shift from private to state war finance. The denser institutional/analytic synthesis (Gabrielsen and related CHGRW chapters) lives at [[concepts/financing-greek-warfare]].

## Definition and Scope

This process traces the shift, over the Classical period, from **privately-funded** to
**state-funded** Greek warfare — how city-states paid for hoplite armies, and above all
for navies, and how the scale and duration of war came to depend on treasury capacity
rather than on private wealth alone. It does not cover the *tactics* of war ([[hoplite-warfare]],
[[greek-naval-warfare]]) but the *economics* that made those tactics sustainable.

## Causal Drivers

In the Archaic period, war costs were borne mainly by private individuals: hoplites
self-equipped (see [[hoplite-warfare]]), and even some triremes were privately owned and
crewed through the time of the Persian Wars. The turning point was Athens's decision
(483/482 BCE) to spend the revenue of the Laurium silver mines on a large state-owned
fleet of triremes rather than distributing it to citizens (Herodotus 7.144; [Aristotle],
*Constitution of the Athenians* 22.7) — creating, for the first time, a navy that was a
state asset rather than an aggregation of private ships.

## Major Phases

- **State pay for service.** Standard rates emerged for rowers and marines — two, later
  three, obols a day (subsistence level), doubling to about a drachma a day for distant
  expeditions. Hoplite pay was introduced at a comparable rate, apparently by the
  mid-fifth century. Cavalrymen received pay plus a state loan toward purchasing and
  maintaining a horse. Mercenaries and allied troops were paid at citizen rates.
- **Treasury depletion under sustained war.** The Athenian treasury, reckoned at its
  peak at roughly several thousand talents around 431 BCE, was drawn down sharply over
  the [[peloponnesian-war|Peloponnesian War]]: the nine-month siege of Samos alone cost
  over a thousand talents (Fornara 124), and the two-and-a-half-year siege of Potidaea
  cost roughly another two thousand, forcing emergency war taxes (Thucydides 2.13.3,
  3.17).
- **Supplementary funding sources.** Plunder, sold on the spot by army "booty-sellers"
  (*laphyropolai*); ransom, at a standard rate far above the market price of a slave;
  "borrowing" from temple treasuries (dedicated tithes, *dekatê*); and, decisively in the
  war's later phases, **Persian gold subsidies** to Sparta — which allowed the Spartan
  navy to grow dramatically at Athens's expense.

## Geographic Spread

Centered on Athens (best documented, owing to the [[delian-league|Delian League]]
treasury) but applicable across the major naval and hoplite powers of the Aegean;
Sparta's late-war dependence on Persian subsidy shows the same treasury logic operating
even for a state without Athens's silver revenue.

## Interaction

Feeds directly into [[greek-naval-warfare]] (state-owned fleets, paid crews) and
[[hoplite-warfare]] (the fourth-century rise of paid mercenary service, as citizen
militias proved too limited for prolonged or distant campaigns). Interacts with
[[classical-greek-economy]] broadly and with the political economy of the
[[delian-league]], whose tribute was itself a war-financing mechanism.

## End Conditions

The process does not end so much as culminate in the fourth-century emergence of
professionalized, state-financed military establishments — most strikingly Macedon under
Philip II, whose gold-mine revenue (reckoned at roughly a thousand talents a year)
funded a full-time drilled infantry and matched the combined forces of the Greek
city-states by 338 BCE. Greek treasury-based war finance was, in that sense, superseded
by a fiscal-military scale the fragmented poleis could not match.
