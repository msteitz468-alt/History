## Definition and Scope

Multiculturalism in Australia refers to the official policy framework that replaced the [[white-australia-policy]] from the 1970s onward, affirming cultural diversity as a national value, providing institutional support for ethnic communities, and framing Australia as a society enriched by immigration from varied origins. It is distinct from the mere fact of ethnic diversity (which predates official policy) and from non-discriminatory immigration policy per se (which preceded the adoption of the "multiculturalism" label). The process here tracks the rise, institutionalisation, contestation, and partial retreat of multiculturalism as an explicitly named policy framework.

## Causal Drivers

The abolition of the White Australia Policy by the Whitlam government (1972–75) and its consolidation under Fraser opened migration to Asian and non-European source countries. "The Hawke government promoted diversified immigration as means of promoting competitive advantage, while the Keating government aimed at a broad agenda of social and economic modernisation — both building upon the cultural pluralism first promoted by Whitlam and Fraser, but now in terms recognisably tied to globalisation" ([[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]], ch. 7). The economic rationale of migration for skill acquisition and demographic renewal reinforced the cultural case for pluralism.

The Special Broadcasting Service provided a distinctive institutional channel: "the latter especially in relation to ethnic communities" though its "audience shares were low" relative to commercial media ([[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]], ch. 7).

## Major Phases

### Ascent and Bipartisan Consensus (1972–1984)
The Whitlam and Fraser governments established the core institutional framework of cultural pluralism. Both major parties accepted diversity and non-discriminatory immigration as settled policy. The 1988 Australian Bicentenary included "a year-long program of celebrations, many with a determinedly multicultural flavour" ([[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]], ch. 7).

### Contestation (1984–1996)
In 1984, historian Geoffrey Blainey "suggested that the level of Asian immigration might strain public acceptance and engender national fragmentation." Opposition leader John Howard "called for 'one Australia' and suggested slowing Asian immigration in the interests of social cohesion, though his challenge to the then bipartisan policy of multiculturalism cost him his party leadership and he soon recanted" ([[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]], ch. 7). A 1988 report commissioned to advise on immigration policy "identified such levels of community concern, faltering support and lack of consensus that it recommended abandoning the term multiculturalism" ([[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]], ch. 7).

In 1992 the Keating government introduced **mandatory detention** for asylum seekers arriving without valid visas — the first major institutional expression of restrictionism within the nominally multicultural framework.

### Retreat under Howard (1996–2007)
When Howard won office in 1996 "he endorsed diversity and pluralist policies, but he abolished the Office of Multicultural Affairs and the Bureau of Immigration, Multicultural and Population Research, and stressed what were implicitly Anglo-cultural 'core' values and national homogeneity" ([[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]], ch. 7). He "abhorred the idea of 'hyphenated Australians'" and "saw 'multiculturalism' as antithetical to migrants 'assimilating' and becoming 'Australians'" ([[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]], ch. 8). Citizenship tests were introduced as checks on "the right values." The Department of Immigration, Multicultural and Indigenous Affairs was eventually replaced by the Department of Immigration and Citizenship, with "multicultural" excised from its title.

Nonetheless, immigration from South Asia rose from 8 per cent of intake in 1996–97 to 20 per cent in 2007–08. Howard maintained that Australia did not have to choose "between its history and its geography." Muslim schools "multiplied and flourished" under his schools policy even as multicultural policy was publicly downgraded.

### Pacific Solution and Asylum Politics (2001 onward)
The Tampa Affair of September 2001 (see [[tampa-affair-2001]]) crystallised the restrictionist turn. The "Pacific Solution" — processing asylum seekers on Nauru and Manus Island, removing Christmas Island and other territories from Australia's migration zone — operated in formal tension with multicultural values. "'We will decide who comes to this country and the circumstances in which they come' became the rallying call for the Liberal Party's 2001 campaign" ([[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]], ch. 8).

### Labor Partial Restoration (2007–2013)
The Rudd government abolished temporary protection visas and promised more humane practices, but retained mandatory detention, military interception, and offshore processing. Multiculturalism as a named framework was not formally restored. The Gillard government commissioned an expert panel on constitutional recognition of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples but did not commit to adopt its recommendations.

## Geographic Spread

A national policy framework applied across all states, with higher concentrations of multicultural populations in Sydney and Melbourne. The 457 visa system introduced in 1996 allowed employers to sponsor skilled migrants; by 2007–08, 110,000 workers annually entered on this basis. Working holiday and student visa workers (188,000 in 2008–09) added a further layer of diversity.

## End Conditions

Multiculturalism as official policy framework had not ended by 2013, but had been substantially hollowed out institutionally under Howard and was not fully restored under Labor. "Multiculturalism had proved a useful means of meeting the needs of ethnic communities and of managing cultural diversity relatively peacefully, but when parties and leaders began to contest its elements and bipartisan commitment eroded, it lost broad popular support" ([[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]], ch. 7). Its successor framework — a civics-based, values-testing model of integration — retained ethnic diversity as demographic fact while retreating from cultural pluralism as positive value.

## Historiography

James Jupp, *From White Australia to Woomera: The Story of Australian Immigration* (Cambridge University Press, 2002/2007) provides the primary scholarly account. The source ([[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]], ch. 7) notes that Pauline Hanson's One Nation party, which captured 22.7 per cent of the Queensland vote in 1998, embodied "anti-Indigenous and anti-immigrant sentiment" but "in essence appealed to the anxieties of all who had lost control of their economic fortunes" — suggesting the anti-multicultural vote was partly a displacement of economic anxiety rather than pure ethnic nationalism. The Cronulla riots of December 2005 (fomented partly by radio personality Alan Jones, described as a Howard favourite) point to the persistence of racial tension beneath the official discourse. Collection coverage on post-1972 multicultural policy is strong via this volume.
