# Baltic Crusades

The **Baltic Crusades** (also "Northern Crusades") were the campaigns of
Christianization and conquest waged from the mid-12th century into the early 15th
against the pagan and, in places, Orthodox peoples of the eastern and southern
Baltic — Wends, Prussians, Livs, Estonians, Letts and, longest of all, the
Lithuanians. They combined papal crusade authorization, German and Danish
territorial expansion, and the state-building of the military orders — the
**Order of the Sword-Brethren** in Livonia and, above all, the
[[teutonic-knights|Teutonic Order]] in Prussia. The lands east of the Baltic
were, until the late 14th century, literally styled the *frontaria* of
Christendom — Archbishop Frederick of Riga in 1305 described them as lying *in
paganorum frontaria*.

## Definition and Scope

Unlike the Levantine [[crusades|Crusades]], the Baltic campaigns were justified
less by recovery of holy places than by the "expansion of the boundaries of the
Christian faith" — Innocent III urged Valdemar II of Denmark toward *terminos
christiane fidei dilatandos* — and by the defence of missionaries, which licensed
permanent conquest. Two distinct frontiers run through the process: the **Danish
maritime frontier** against the Wends across the Baltic Sea (see
[[denmark-norway|Denmark]]), and the **land frontier** of the military orders
through Prussia and Livonia toward Lithuania.

## Causal Drivers

- **Latin Christianization** of the last pagan zone of Europe, framed as crusade.
- **Danish and German eastward expansion** — encastellamento, tribute extraction,
  cattle-raiding and slave-taking accompanied the campaigns; Danish provincial
  laws had to regulate the resulting slaves.
- **Military-order state-building** — the Teutonic Order converted crusade into a
  territorial *Ordensstaat*.
- **Frontier ideology** — chroniclers such as Saxo Grammaticus (for Denmark),
  Henry of Livonia, Peter of Dusburg, Wigand of Marburg and Herman of Wartberg
  constructed the moral geography that justified the wars.

## Major Phases

1. **Wendish Crusade (1147)** and the long Danish–Wendish struggle, culminating
   in the Danish conquest of pagan **Rügen (1168)** and its fortress-shrine of
   Arkona.
2. **Livonian crusade** from c. 1200: the Sword-Brethren, the founding of Riga,
   the subjection of Livs, Letts and Estonians (Danish conquest of northern
   Estonia).
3. **Prussian crusade** from 1226–31: the Teutonic Order's conquest of the
   Prussians over some fifty years, building the *Ordensstaat*.
4. **The Lithuanian wars** (14th c.): the last pagan state in Europe resisted for
   over a century until the dynastic conversion of **1386–87** (Jogaila's baptism
   and the Polish–Lithuanian union), which removed the crusade's justification.

## A Religious Frontier of Ambiguous Belief

Rasa Mažeika's study of the crusade chronicles (in *[[abulafia-berend-medieval-frontiers-2002|Medieval
Frontiers]]*) finds that, despite two centuries of near-constant war, both sides
occasionally **"granted power to enemy gods."** Chroniclers "actualized" pagan
deities as real powers: the *Livonian Rhymed Chronicle* has the god Perkūnas
freeze the sea to let a Lithuanian army cross, and pagan divination by lot
repeatedly proves accurate. A 1338 Lithuanian–Livonian treaty was sealed by
Christians kissing the cross while the pagans "did their holy rites"; the Teutonic
Knights were themselves accused — on their own chronicler's evidence — of
pagan-style divination and cremation. Grand Duke **Gediminas** told papal envoys
that Christians, Orthodox Rus' and Catholic Poles worshipped in their way and
Lithuanians in theirs, since "we all have the one God"; his predecessor
**Vytenis** both trampled a communion host and built the first Christian church
for Franciscans in Lithuania. Theologically, Latin writers filed the pagan gods
as demons (Vulgate Psalm 95:5), yet the lived frontier was one of "sullen
suspicion that the enemy can call on strange powers" — a religious permeability
comparable to the Christian–Muslim frontiers of Iberia and the Levant.

## Geographic Spread

From the Elbe and the Danish isles eastward along the southern Baltic shore
(Wendish lands, Pomerania, Prussia) and around the Gulf of Riga and Gulf of
Finland (Livonia, Estonia), reaching its final and most durable frontier against
Lithuania and Orthodox Rus'.

## Interaction

The Baltic Crusades overlap the wider [[crusades|crusade movement]] and the
[[christianization-of-scandinavia|Christianization of Scandinavia]], and produced
the [[teutonic-knights|Teutonic Order's]] Prussian state and its later collision
with Poland–Lithuania. They are a central case for the
[[medieval-frontier|medieval frontier]] as both an ideological construct and a
zone of religious ambiguity.

## End Conditions

The dynastic conversion of Lithuania (1386–87) removed the crusade's pagan
target; the Order's defeat by Poland–Lithuania at Tannenberg/Grunwald (1410) and
the Peace of Brześć (1435) ended the era of Baltic crusading, though the
*Ordensstaat* endured until the 16th century.
