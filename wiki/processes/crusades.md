## Definition and Scope

The **Crusades** were penitential holy wars proclaimed by the papacy, in which
participants took a **vow**, wore the **cross**, and received an **indulgence** (the
remission of the penance due for sin) in return for fighting the enemies of the
Church — most famously to recover and defend Jerusalem and the Holy Land, but also in
Spain, the Baltic, and against heretics and the papacy's political enemies.
[[luscombe-ncmh-v4-2004|NCMH IV]] (Jonathan Riley-Smith, Ch. 14, "The crusades,
1095–1198") defines crusading by these juridical features rather than by theatre of
war: Urban II "gave the idea of penitential warfare . . . substance by linking it" to
the traditions of war and pilgrimage, introducing the vow, the cross, and the
indulgence, and setting the tone for crusading as "always to be a penitential" act —
a meritorious pilgrimage in arms.

Riley-Smith's "pluralist" definition (a war is a crusade by virtue of papal
authorisation, vow, and indulgence, wherever fought) underlies the chapter and the
modern scholarly consensus, against an older view that confined "crusade" to the
expeditions to the Levant.

## Causal Drivers

- **The [[gregorian-reform|reform papacy]] and [[just-war|just war]].** The crusade
  grew directly out of the reformers' willingness to authorise sanctified violence:
  Gregory VII had planned to lead 50,000 men east in 1074; the network of *milites
  sancti Petri*, the Norman alliance, and the theological "research" of Anselm of
  Lucca (resolved magisterially by Gratian, *Decretum* Causa 23) prepared the ground.
  Riley-Smith judges the crusade so rooted in the reform that "it is even possible to
  imagine that the crusade would have taken place, in much the form it did, without
  the justification provided by the Turkish invasions."
- **The Byzantine appeal and the Seljuk conquest.** The Turkish overrunning of Asia
  Minor after [[battle-of-manzikert-1071|Manzikert (1071)]] and the threat to
  Constantinople drove the Byzantine emperor Alexios I to appeal west; an appeal
  reached Urban II at Piacenza in March 1095.
- **The ideology of liberation.** Urban proposed at Clermont (1095) a "war of
  liberation" with two aims — the liberation of **people** (the eastern Christians)
  and of a **place** (Jerusalem) — language already used of the wars against Muslims
  in Spain and Sicily.

## Major Phases (to 1198)

1. **The [[first-crusade-1095|First Crusade]] (1095–1099).** Proclaimed by
   [[urban-ii|Urban II]] at Clermont; captured Jerusalem on 15 July 1099 and
   established the [[crusader-states|crusader states]] — the one unambiguous crusading
   success.
2. **Consolidation and the military orders (1100s–1130s).** The
   [[crusader-states|Latin East]] took shape; the **Knights Templar** (recognised
   1129/1139) and **Hospitallers** fused monastic vows with knighthood, becoming great
   international orders holding castles (Crac des Chevaliers) and lands across Europe.
3. **The Second Crusade (1147–1149).** Provoked by the fall of **Edessa** to **Zengi
   of Mosul** (1144), preached by **Bernard of Clairvaux**, and joined by Louis VII of
   France and Conrad III; it failed before Damascus, while its Iberian and Baltic
   theatres were explicitly assimilated to the crusade.
4. **The road to the Third Crusade.** Mounting pressure from the Muslim
   counter-offensive culminated, just beyond this chapter's close, in
   [[saladin|Saladin's]] victory at **Hattin (1187)** and the loss of Jerusalem — the
   trigger of the Third Crusade (the full Levantine narrative is in NCMH IV Part 2,
   Mayer, "The Latin east").

## Interaction

The crusade is the supreme expression of the period's fusion of [[chivalry|knightly]]
violence with the [[gregorian-reform|reformed Church]] via the [[just-war]] tradition
and the [[peace-of-god|Peace of God]]. It reshaped relations with
[[byzantine-empire|Byzantium]] (cooperation souring into the antagonism that led to
1204), with the Islamic world, and with the [[ashkenazi-jewry|Jews of the Rhineland]]
(massacred by crusaders in 1096); it accelerated the [[commercial-revolution|Italian
maritime]] penetration of the Levant; and it gave the [[reconquista|Reconquista]] and
Baltic wars a crusading frame.

**Theological dimension (Latin-Byzantine schism):** The Crusades profoundly reshaped
Greek Orthodox religious literature and identity politics, but paradoxically did so
*indirectly*. Byzantine anti-Latin polemic in the Crusade era focused almost entirely on
theological disputes (azymes, Filioque, papal primacy) rather than on the concept of holy
war or the crusade institution itself — there is no Byzantine theological refutation of the
crusade indulgence or of monastic knighthood. The Crusades operated primarily as a
radicalizing political backdrop: the Fourth Crusade's sack of Constantinople (1204) and
the subsequent Latin occupation generated intense popular hatred of Latins and made
church union negotiations politically impossible (as Barlaam reported to Pope Benedict
XII in 1339), while the Latin Empire's insistence on Greek clergy swearing obedience to
the pope transformed the previously "academic" question of papal primacy into the
dominant theological dispute of the 1204–1261 period. The issues that dominated
Byzantine polemic — azymes (eleventh century), papal primacy (twelfth–thirteenth),
Filioque (Palaiologan) — followed the logic of internal Byzantine identity debates rather
than direct response to crusading ideology. See [[byzantine-latin-schism-polemic]]
(Kolbaba's analysis).

## End Conditions

Levantine crusading continued past this volume — through the Third and Fourth Crusades
into the thirteenth century treated by [[abulafia-ncmh-v5-2000|NCMH V]] — until the
fall of **Acre (1291)** ended the crusader states; crusading as an institution
persisted (against the Ottomans, heretics, and pagans) into the early modern era.

## Historiography

Western scholarship on the crusades is surveyed in detail in [[concepts/crusade-historiography]]. Key points for the process as a whole:

**Three-period scheme (Constable, 2001).** Giles Constable divides Western crusading historiography into three periods: (1) 1095 to c. 1600, when the crusades were part of a living struggle and accounts were inseparable from crusading propaganda; (2) 1600–1800, dominated by Enlightenment critique — Voltaire called the crusaders motivated by "the thirst for brigandage," Gibbon described their principle as "a savage fanaticism"; and (3) 1800 to the present, beginning with romantic rehabilitation and continuing through modern critical scholarship. The nineteenth century was broadly favorable; the twentieth saw a "rising tide of criticism" culminating in Runciman's verdict that the crusades were "a tragic and destructive episode . . . a long act of intolerance in the name of God."

**Traditionalists vs. pluralists.** The central modern definitional debate is between **traditionalists** (leading figure Mayer: a true crusade must be directed toward the Holy Land) and **pluralists** (Riley-Smith: a crusade is defined by papal authorization, the vow, the cross, and the indulgence, wherever directed). The pluralist position, now dominant in academic scholarship, encompasses crusades in Spain, the Baltic, against heretics, and into the early modern era.

**Non-Western perspectives** were largely absent from major Western syntheses (Grousset, Runciman, Setton's Wisconsin history). The Dumbarton Oaks volume edited by Laiou and Mottahedeh (2001) represents a systematic effort to incorporate Byzantine and Muslim viewpoints. See [[concepts/byzantine-just-war]] and [[concepts/jihad-concept-evolution]] for the ideological frameworks of those perspectives.
