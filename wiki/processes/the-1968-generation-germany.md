## Definition and Scope

"1968" in Germany designates not a single year but a generational transformation spanning roughly 1960–1980, centered on the cohort born in the 1930s–1940s who came of age politically in the 1960s. The West German "68ers" shared a global repertoire with their French, American, and other counterparts — anti-Vietnam War activism, student radicalism, sexual liberation, challenges to authority — but had a distinctive German inflection: the unresolved confrontation with the Nazi past that their parents' generation had largely evaded. The East German context was fundamentally different; this page addresses primarily the West German process.

## Causal Drivers

**Parental generation's silence.** The Mitscherlich thesis (Alexander and Margarete Mitscherlich, *Die Unfähigkeit zu trauern*, 1967 — "The Inability to Mourn") provided the generational confrontation with theoretical vocabulary. The 1950s FRG had been characterized, in this analysis, by flight into prosperity and consumer culture rather than mourning and accountability for the Nazi crimes. The Hans Globke scandal (Adenauer's state secretary had helped draft the Nuremberg Laws) and continued presence of former Nazis in public life fueled young Germans' sense of betrayal and the demand for accounting.

**Anti-imperialism and Vietnam.** The Vietnam War provided a global frame for radical politics. West Germany's position as the principal American ally in Europe made the Vietnam War an immediate political issue. The SDS (Sozialistischer Deutscher Studentenbund — not affiliated with the American SDS despite the identical acronym) led campus opposition. Demonstrations, sit-ins, and confrontations with police escalated.

**APO (Extra-Parliamentary Opposition).** When the SPD entered the Grand Coalition with the CDU/CSU in 1966, the parliamentary left was stripped of any legislative opposition. The APO (Außerparlamentarische Opposition) emerged as the organizing framework for political action outside parliament — a direct consequence of coalition politics that pushed dissent into the streets.

**Kommune 1.** The founding of Kommune 1 in West Berlin (1967), a countercultural commune that staged happenings and provocations including a satirical "attack" on Hubert Humphrey with flour, embodied the fusion of political provocation and lifestyle experimentation.

**Frankfurt School.** Herbert Marcuse's synthesis of Marx and Freud — especially *One-Dimensional Man* (1964) — provided the theoretical framework for criticizing advanced capitalist society's capacity to absorb and neutralize dissent. Theodor Adorno and Jürgen Habermas were central intellectual presences, though both were more critical of the student movement than students expected. The famous Habermas-Dutschke debate about whether radical tactics constituted "left fascism" illustrated tensions within the intellectual-activist coalition.

## Key Events

**2 June 1967:** West Berlin student Benno Ohnesorg shot and killed by police during a demonstration against the Shah of Iran's visit. Ohnesorg's death radicalized the student movement.

**11 April 1968:** Rudi Dutschke (leading SDS figure) shot by a right-wing would-be assassin. Easter riots followed across West German cities, including attacks on Springer press delivery trucks (the Springer tabloids had run vitriolic anti-Dutschke coverage).

**Late 1960s:** Sexual revolution, feminist organizing, and challenges to the Paragraph 218 (abortion ban) accelerated. The women's movement developed its own political identity, initially within and then increasingly distinct from the male-dominated student left.

## Radicalization and Terrorism

A minority of the 1968 generation radicalized into political violence. The Red Army Faction (RAF, also known as the Baader-Meinhof Group) was founded in 1970 by Andreas Baader, Gudrun Ensslin, Ulrike Meinhof, and others. The RAF conducted bombings, bank robberies, and assassinations through the 1970s and 1980s, culminating in the "German Autumn" of 1977 (kidnapping of industrialist Hanns-Martin Schleyer; Lufthansa hijacking). The RAF's terrorism was the dark endpoint of a trajectory that began in legitimate protest politics; its existence was used by conservatives to discredit the broader 1968 generation and by the generation itself as evidence of how far they had gone wrong.

## Long-Term Consequences

The 1968 generation's most durable legacy was cultural and institutional rather than revolutionary:

**Vergangenheitsbewältigung:** The 68ers forced the confrontation with the Nazi past that their parents had evaded. The Eichmann Trial (1961), Auschwitz Trial (1963–1965), and the 1968 generation's demands for parental accounting combined to make Holocaust memory a central element of German public culture — though this process took decades and was not complete until at least the 1980s. See [[vergangenheitsbewaltigung]].

**Media and institutions:** The 68ers became the generation that transformed West German universities, newspapers, and cultural institutions from the 1970s onward.

**Green Party:** The ecological activism associated with Joseph Beuys and others in the late 1970s led directly to the founding of the Green Party (Die Grünen) around 1980, which entered the Bundestag in 1983. The Greens institutionalized the anti-nuclear and environmentalist concerns that had emerged from the protest generation.

**Feminist movement:** Second-wave feminism in Germany emerged from but then against the male-dominated student left. The campaign to liberalize abortion law (Paragraph 218) and feminist cultural production became a permanent strand of West German political life.

**SPD and labor:** The longer-term electoral consequence of 1968 was the SPD's ability to attract a new generation of educated voters in the 1970s, contributing to Brandt's electoral coalition.

## East German Context

The East German version of generational conflict was suppressed and deformed by the regime. Rock and punk music in the late 1970s–1980s provided limited channels for dissent. The civic groups that launched the 1989 revolution (New Forum, Democracy Now) included members of a generation that had tried to reform GDR socialism from within — a somewhat analogous but much more constrained trajectory to the West German 68ers.

## Historiography

The 1968 generation has generated intense and politicized historiography. Conservative critiques (including those from former 68ers who later moved right, like Götz Aly) emphasize the generation's authoritarian streaks, anti-Americanism, and apologetics for Third World dictatorships. Sympathetic assessments emphasize the democratization of West German culture, the confrontation with the Nazi past, and the environmental legacy. Timothy Brown's work on the German student movement and Wolfgang Kraushaar's documentation of 1968 are key references.
