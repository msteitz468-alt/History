# The War Economy and the Expansion of State Power

The **war economy** of 1914–18 was the transformation by which belligerent states subsumed
private market capitalism into **state-directed mobilization** for [[total-war|total war]] —
a vast and abrupt expansion of government control over production, labour, and resources that
made the **First World War** a turning point in the history of the modern state. From
[[winter-cambridge-history-first-world-war-v2|Barry Supple's chapter in *The Cambridge History
of the First World War*, Vol. II]].

## The munitions shock

Pre-war planners had radically underestimated industrial war's appetite for materiel. German
armies expended more munitions in the eight days of the **Marne (1914)** than in the entire
Franco-Prussian War; French shell consumption ran ten times pre-war estimates; Britain's
projected six-month shell requirement became a *monthly* total, and the **shell shortage of
spring 1915** triggered the British state's first far-reaching intervention in the economy
(the Ministry of Munitions). The "prolonged immobilisation of mass armies" created
"insatiable demands for labour and munitions" that competitive markets could not meet — "the
market became the enemy of national survival."

## The expansion of the state

The result was an unprecedented assertion of state power over the economy. Government's share
of GDP leapt, between 1913 and 1918, from **~10% to 50–53% in France and Germany**, from 8%
to 35% in the United Kingdom, and from ~2% to ~17% in the United States. Standing armies of
under 6 million in August 1914 became ~66 million mobilized by November 1918. States took
over the **allocation of raw materials, the direction of industry, price and labour
controls, rationing, and the financing of war** through borrowing and inflation. The
**economic strength of nations** — and, decisively, the entry of the United States — rather
than battlefield genius determined the outcome: Allied combined GDP exceeded the Central
Powers' by ~55%, and the German and Austrian economies shrank under blockade while Britain's
grew.

## Aftermath and significance

Much of the wartime command apparatus was **dismantled after 1918** as governments and
business sought a return to pre-war orthodoxy — but the precedent endured. The war
demonstrated that modern economies could be **planned and mobilized by the state** on a
continental scale, a lesson revived in the **Depression** of the 1930s, in the Soviet command
economy, and above all in the still more total mobilization of the **[[second-world-war-1939|Second
World War]]**. The war's financial dislocations (debt, inflation, the eclipse of the gold
standard, the shift of financial power to the US) also seeded the **instability and depression**
of the interwar global economy.
