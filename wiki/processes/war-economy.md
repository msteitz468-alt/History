# The War Economy and the Expansion of State Power

The **war economy** of 1914–18 was the transformation by which belligerent states subsumed
private market capitalism into **state-directed mobilization** for [[total-war|total war]] —
a vast and abrupt expansion of government control over production, labour, and resources that
made the **First World War** a turning point in the history of the modern state. From
[[winter-cambridge-history-first-world-war-v2|Barry Supple's chapter in *The Cambridge History
of the First World War*, Vol. II]].

## Definition and Scope

The war economy is the wartime **subordination of the market economy to the state's war
effort**: the direction of industry, allocation of raw materials and labour, rationing, price
and wage controls, and war finance (borrowing and inflation). It is *not* a permanent command
economy — most of the apparatus was dismantled after 1918 — but a temporary, emergency
mobilization whose precedents proved durable. This page concerns the First World War; the
pattern recurred, more completely, in the [[second-world-war-1939|Second World War]].

## Causal Drivers

The driver was the **insatiable material demand of industrial mass war** colliding with the
market's inability to meet it. Pre-war planners radically underestimated munitions needs:
German armies expended more shells in the eight days of the **Marne (1914)** than in the
entire Franco-Prussian War; French consumption ran ten times pre-war estimates; Britain's
projected six-month shell requirement became a *monthly* total. The **shell shortage of
spring 1915** triggered the British state's first far-reaching economic intervention (the
Ministry of Munitions). With mass armies locked in stalemate, "the market became the enemy of
national survival," forcing the state to take over allocation.

## Major Phases

1. **Improvised response (1914–15):** initial faith in "business as usual" collapses under
   the munitions crisis.
2. **State direction (1915–17):** ministries of munitions and supply, controls over
   industry, labour, shipping, and food; war finance through debt and inflation.
3. **Total mobilization (1917–18):** rationing, conscription of labour, and near-total
   subordination of the economy — government's share of GDP reaching ~50% in France and
   Germany.

## Geographic Spread

Every major belligerent — parliamentary, autocratic, or imperial — was forced into the same
transformation, though to differing degrees. Government's share of GDP rose between 1913 and
1918 from **~10% to 50–53% in France and Germany**, from 8% to 35% in the United Kingdom, and
from ~2% to ~17% in the United States. The decisive factor was relative **economic strength**:
Allied combined GDP exceeded the Central Powers' by ~55%, the German and Austrian economies
shrank under blockade, and US entry tipped the balance — confirming that the war's outcome
turned on economic mobilization, not battlefield genius alone.

## Interaction

The war economy is the home-front realization of **[[total-war]]** and the economic
counterpart of its military ([[first-world-war-1914]]) and cultural
([[war-memory-and-mourning]]) dimensions. It interacted with **labour movements** (whose
bargaining power rose), with **[[nationalism|the nation-state]]** (whose reach expanded), and
with the **global economy** (war debt, inflation, the eclipse of the gold standard, and the
shift of financial power to the United States).

## End Conditions

With the Armistice, governments and business sought a **return to pre-war orthodoxy** and
dismantled much of the command apparatus by the early 1920s. But the precedent endured: the
war proved that modern economies could be **planned and mobilized by the state** on a
continental scale — a lesson revived in the Soviet command economy, the **Depression-era**
interventions of the 1930s, and above all the still-greater mobilization of the
[[second-world-war-1939|Second World War]]. The financial dislocations it left seeded the
instability of the interwar global economy.
