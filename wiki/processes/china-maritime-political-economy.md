## Definition and Scope

This page traces the structural dynamics of China's engagement with maritime trade from the founding of the Song Dynasty (960 CE) to the end of the nineteenth century. The framework comes from Kent Deng, ch. 23 of the Cambridge History of the Pacific Ocean, vol. 1 (2022): "China's activities at sea loomed large in two distinctive periods during the Song (960–1279) and the Ming (1368–1644), respectively, but not before and not after." The driving question is why the same culture and tradition "obviously failed to generate a linear growth in maritime activities in the manner, say, of the post-Renaissance Europeans who eventually went on to colonize the globe."

The answer Deng gives lies in China's political economy: a fundamentally land-based agrarian system structured by universal private landownership, elastic supply of mainland arable land, and a Confucian social hierarchy that ranked peasantry above merchants. Sea trade was associated with "marginal strata — fishermen and transporters — and was mainly performed in inland lakes and rivers or along coastlines until the tenth century CE."

## Major Phases

### Phase I: Song Maritime Mercantilism (960–1279)

**How It Began**

The Song emerged from fifty years of civil war holding only ~40% of the former Tang territory. The other 60% was controlled by nomads: Khitan (Liao), Tangut (Xixia), Jurchen (Jin). These groups also controlled the Silk Road and the Great Wall. Tribute demands were crushing: the 1004 Chanyuan Treaty obligated annual payments of 200,000 bolts of silk and 100,000 taels of silver to the Khitans; by 1042 (Guannan Treaty) this rose to 300,000 bolts and 200,000 taels; annual bilateral tribute payments to Tanguts added 130,000 bolts, 500,000 taels, and 200,000 catties of tea. "The Song economy produced only 200,000 taels of silver a year (as in 1078). The shortage was 400,000–500,000 taels per year for the annual ransoms. Extra silver had to come from outside the Song territory as a matter of national emergency."

Since the Silk Road was blocked by nomads, "the only option was for the Songs to take to the sea on an unprecedented scale to meet the demand from the nomads."

**Song Maritime Institutions and Technology**

The state "adopted mercantilism side by side with traditional physiocracy." Key institutional innovation: Maritime Bureaus (*shibo si*), established in Guangzhou in 971 CE and expanded along the southeast coast. Total state maritime investment: 7.75 billion bronze coins (88% from the central government). The state acted as a market entrepreneur, importing frankincense worth 1.5 billion bronze coins between 1076–1078 and re-selling domestically for profit.

Technological achievements:
- Magnetic compass became standard shipboard equipment; sea charts developed (a *Chart to Overseas Countries* was presented to Emperor Zhenzong in 1003).
- Seagoing ships of 500–1,000-ton capacity — the "Fuzhou Type" (*fuchuan*) — with ballasted keel, bilge keels, low deck length–beam ratio (2.5–4.0:1) for stability, V-shaped bottom, multiple sails, clinker-built hull.
- Fleet at peak: 3,000 ships of 500–1,000 tonnes; government edict of 1090 ordered 600 sea vessels per year from Wenzhou and Mingzhou alone.
- Forty-five new maritime destinations explored, reaching the Arabian Peninsula (Tazi) and East African coast (Zanj).
- Maritime tax revenue grew at 5.3% annually (1087–1106: from 416 to 1,110 million bronze coins). Land tax revenue declined in absolute terms. By 1077 the non-agricultural sector had grown from 35% to 70% of government revenue.
- Arab merchants settled permanently in Quanzhou; a sizeable diasporic community operated in Song seaports.

The Song also introduced porcelain as ship ballast — "heavy, waterproof, and at the same time of real market value as a commodity."

**End of Song Maritime Expansion**

"The Song maritime take-off was ruthlessly ended by the Mongol conquest in 1279." Mongol policies toward Chinese: "a combination of genocide and apartheid." Over a third of China's total population vanished by 1291; millions enslaved; Confucianism replaced; Chinese excluded from long-distance trade; coastal Maritime Bureaus closed; the second crop forbidden. Mongol naval campaigns in the Sea of Japan and Java Sea were "deadly fiascos."

### Phase II: Ming — Diplomatic Voyages, Maritime Ban, and Smuggling (1368–1644)

**Ming Restoration and Physiocracy**

The Ming expelled Mongol rule in 1368 and immediately restored physiocracy: private landownership resumed, imperial examinations restarted, Chinese language and Confucianism reinstated. Total farmland increased 3.2 times between 1381 and 1602, doubling the Song peak.

Simultaneously, the Ming issued a comprehensive maritime ban (*haijin*): "Emperor Taizu ruled that no single ship plank is permitted to enter the sea." Coastal Maritime Bureaus were closed.

**Diplomatic Gift Exchange (Tribute System)**

Foreign delegates were permitted for "diplomatic gift exchanges" (*rugong*) — the receipt of exotic goods (precious metals, gems, rare woods, live animals, slaves) in exchange for Chinese currency, silk, and imperial certificates. Seventy-six overseas locations were documented for this theatre; about half came regularly. Deng emphasizes these are "commonly translated as 'paying tribute to China', as if goods were going to China unilaterally, which was never the case during the Ming." The exchanges were "symbolic and trivial" economically; they "satisfied the ego of the Ming rulers but had very little to do with the Ming economy."

**Zheng He Voyages (1405–1433)**

Government-sponsored shipping thrived to facilitate the diplomatic exchange programme. Zheng He — Muslim eunuch-turned-admiral, born Ma Sanbao to a Yunnan Hui family of probable Arab-Mongol ancestry — led the Ming armada to the Indian Ocean seven times. Deng argues the voyages were fundamentally political, not commercial: no commercial activity is recorded in the official Ming history (*Ming shi*). Zheng's appointment was "more like a dignified exile than a new career path" — he had broken all five rules governing eunuchs, deeply threatening court politics after his participation in the 1402 coup that made Emperor Chengzhu. "So the Ming imperial voyages were led by the wrong man for the wrong reason (exile of a eunuch who broke political taboo), at the wrong time (when China rebuilt its farming economy), and to the wrong places (where no sizeable economic returns were yielded)."

Unintended consequences: 40% increase in maximum ship length; improved navigation charts; fifteen new geographic places in western Pacific and northern Indian oceans; display of China's unrivalled maritime capability.

See [[zheng-he-voyages]] for the dedicated page.

**Maritime Smuggling**

The Ming ban was ineffective. "A century after the state-sponsored extravagant tours of the Ming armada, China's maritime sector was taken over by the private sector: sea smugglers." For political correctness, smugglers were labelled "Japanese" (*woko* or "Japanese bandits"), but "most of the alleged Japanese pirates were Chinese in disguise — over 70 per cent of them." Twin Islands (*Shuangyu*) off Zhejiang boasted over 1,000 ships involving Japanese, Malays, Siamese, and Portuguese. By the 1550s, smuggler groups led by Wang Zhi and others controlled major archipelagoes. The state repeatedly issued amnesties because "the smugglers had become too powerful to suppress."

Smuggling formed "an organic part of Chinese maritime undertakings" in the context of the 1565–1815 Manila Galleon Trade and European colonial expansion. It was "the private sector that was responsible for the astronomical amount of silver to be imported to China, as well as New World crops such as potatoes, sweet potatoes, maize, tobacco, and chilli."

### Phase III: Qing — Monopoly, Smuggling, Ming Loyalism, and Opening (1644–1900)

**Qing Maritime Ban and Canton-Cohong Monopoly**

The Manchus retained Ming culture and institutions. Agriculture was protected; population reached 100 million mid-eighteenth century and ~380 million late nineteenth century. A maritime ban continued, but now politically motivated: the seventeenth-century Ming Loyalist movement (Zheng Chenggong / Koxinga, his son Zheng Jin, grandson Zheng Keshuang) controlled Taiwan 1662–1683 with 170,000 troops and 8,000 ships, running a trade network with Japan, Vietnam, Cambodia, Indonesia, and Luzon — annual profit of 2–3 million taels of silver.

Post-1683, the Qing introduced a trade monopoly at Canton through *cohong* (chartered firms). Superficially a single-port system, the "Canton-cohong were ruthlessly bypassed." Port Huangpu was sailor-unfriendly (100+ km upstream from Pearl River Estuary); Portuguese-controlled Macao and nearby ports provided alternatives. "The Canton-cohong system was merely symbolic." No opium went through Port Huangpu before 1840; the "1839 Destruction of Opium" by Commissioner Lin Zexu was conducted off Humen Beach in the open sea — demonstrating that many smuggling ports operated.

The Qing state was structurally tiny: "In 1640–1750 the ratio [of general public to salaried government officials] was about 3,800:1, and in 1850 it became 15,200:1… The Qing state controlled at most 5 per cent of the empire's total GDP." Effective enforcement of maritime bans was structurally impossible.

**Silver Flows and Smuggling**

Three phases of silver movement to China:
- Phase One (1565–1644): ~100 million pesos (2,385 tons), averaging 30.2 tons/year from Manila Galleon Trade.
- Phase Two (1645–1799): 178.3 million taels (6,686.3 tons), averaging 43.4 tons/year.
- Phase Three (1800–1890): opium imports rose sharply (from 3,562 chests in 1800–05 to 50,000 chests in 1838), eventually exceeding silver inflows; the silver trade reversed.

Despite this, "Qing China enjoyed a steady inflow of silver — a fivefold increase from 1650 to 1830 — despite large quantities of silver flowing out to pay for China's opium imports."

**Opium War and Treaty-Port Opening (1839–1900)**

"That monopoly clashed with free marketeers of the West and the tension built up to the Opium War in 1839–40 which purposely ended the Canton institution." The 1842 Nanking Treaty specified: (1) war reparation of 21 million taels; (2) opening of five ports (Guangzhou, Fuzhou, Xiamen, Ningbo, Shanghai); (3) bilaterally negotiated customs duties. Twenty-five more treaties followed over six decades. A "sixty-year golden age of maritime trade" resulted: China's foreign trade value grew from 30 million taels (1841) to 190 million taels (1881), a sixfold increase. Post-1853, the *lijin* transit surcharge applied to Chinese goods at every domestic checkpoint; foreigners were exempt after paying Maritime Customs Duty at the port — making the Nanking Treaty "the de facto guardian of free trade inside China."

"Western 'gunboat diplomacy' sped up changes, but did not create them."

## End Conditions

The cycle closed by c. 1900. The Song's state-led model "proved to be a workable model" but was destroyed by external predation (Mongols). The Ming's diplomatic-state model served political rather than economic purposes and was commercially sterile. The Qing's ban-with-monopoly was perpetually undermined by private markets too large to regulate. The post-Opium War opening was the market's victory over the state: "Clearly, the market outwitted the state. The government bans were paid no more than lip service. The Confucian state was simply too small to impose effective control of the market along the empire's vast coastal regions, let alone in the open sea."

## Historiography

Kent Deng's approach is quantitative economic history, making extensive use of Chinese dynastic records (*Song shi*, *Ming shi*, census and fiscal data) and twentieth-century Chinese economic history scholarship (Liang Fangzhong, Wu Chengming, von Glahn). The chapter challenges two Eurocentric misreadings: (1) the standard interpretation of Ming "tribute trade" as unilateral tribute flowing to China (Deng shows it was bilateral exchange), and (2) the association of Zheng He with maritime commercial ambition (Deng argues he was primarily an inconvenient political exile). Deng also challenges "gunboat diplomacy as cause" narratives: the 1839–40 Opium War merely legalized market structures already operating informally for decades.

The chapter's quantitative emphasis complements the cultural-perception critique in ch. 24 (Ronald Po, [[concepts/chinese-sea-in-literature]]), which argues that Qing maritime consciousness has been misread through the lens of nineteenth-century decline.
