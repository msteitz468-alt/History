# Virgin-Soil Epidemics and the Great Dying of the Americas

The single most consequential event of the [[columbian-exchange-1492|Columbian Exchange]] was the
**catastrophic die-off of the Indigenous population of the Americas** from Old World diseases — what
historians call the **"Great Dying."** Charles C. Mann's [[mann-1491-2005|*1491*]] makes it the engine
of his whole argument: the "empty wilderness" Europeans reported was largely the **aftermath of a
demographic catastrophe** that often ran *ahead* of the Europeans themselves. The term **"virgin-soil
epidemic"** (Alfred Crosby) names an outbreak in a population with **no prior exposure and hence no
acquired immunity**, where a disease endemic-and-survivable elsewhere becomes a mass killer.

## Why the Americas were so vulnerable

The peoples of the Americas had been **separated from Eurasia for ~15,000 years** and from its dense
agrarian populations and **domestic-animal reservoirs** — the source of most "crowd" diseases
(smallpox, measles, influenza, typhus). With few domesticated animals (only the llama, dog, turkey, and
guinea pig) and no millennia of co-evolution with these pathogens, Native Americans met them as
**entirely novel infections**, striking all ages at once (not just children), often several diseases
together, and collapsing the social capacity to nurse the sick, plant, and harvest — so famine compounded
infection. A further factor was unusually **low genetic diversity in immune-system (HLA) genes** relative
to Old World populations, which may have worsened the toll.

## The scale

Crosby emphasizes the command and psychological collapse alongside raw mortality. Motolinía: in most provinces "more than one half of the population died; in others the proportion was little less. … They died in heaps, like bedbugs." Many others "died of starvation, because as they were all taken sick at once, they could not care for each other." Cook & Borah: central Mexico ~25 million on the eve of conquest to 16.8 million a decade later. In the besieged city "the streets, squares, houses, and courts were filled with bodies, so that it was almost impossible to pass. Even Cortés was sick from the stench in his nostrils."

The death rates Mann reports are staggering and recur across the hemisphere:
- **New England, 1616–19:** an epidemic (Mann's sources suggest viral **hepatitis**, others smallpox or
  leptospirosis), carried inland by fleeing victims, killed **as much as 90%** of the coastal population.
  It emptied **Patuxet** — the Pilgrims literally built Plymouth on the deserted village — and the
  Wampanoag leader **Massasoit** allied with them from a position of catastrophic weakness; Governor
  Bradford thanked "the good hand of God" for "sweeping away" the natives "that he might make room for
  us." (Over fifty early New England colonial towns sat on disease-emptied Indian sites.)
- **The Andes:** smallpox (or similar eruptive fever) swept through the [[inca-empire|Inca empire]] *before* Pizarro,
  killing the emperor Huayna Capac ("their faces being covered with scabs") and many captains and royals, triggering the succession war between Atawallpa and Waskar (Huascar) that Pizarro's 168 men exploited at **Cajamarca (1532)**. Pedro Pizarro judged: "Had this Huayna Capac been alive when we Spaniards entered this land, it would have been impossible for us to win it, for he was much beloved by all his vassals." "Had the land not been divided between Huascar and Atahualpa, we would not have been able to enter or win the land..." (Crosby Ch. 2, citing contemporary accounts).
- **Comparative case (Siberia):** Mann cites the 1768–79 smallpox epidemics in Kamchatka — "the shoreline
  was a cemetery," three of four indigenous Siberians dead in the affected area — as a documented analogue
  for what happened, undocumented, across the Americas.
- **The Sirionó** of Bolivia lost **over 95%** to smallpox and influenza in the **1920s** — a die-off so
  recent and so total it produced the [[holmbergs-mistake|"Stone-Age" illusion]] Mann's book is named for.

## Significance

The Great Dying explains both the **ease of European conquest** (Indigenous states were already reeling)
and the **"wilderness"** the settlers found and mistook for a pristine, lightly-peopled continent — when
it was in fact the regrowth over a [[pristine-myth|managed landscape]] whose managers had died (see
[[pristine-myth]]). It is the dark corollary of the [[pre-columbian-american-population|population
debate]]: the higher the pre-contact population, the larger the catastrophe — which is precisely why that
debate is so charged. Lévi-Strauss's image, which Mann quotes: cultures are books, and "in the sixteenth
century, more books were burned than ever before or since."

## Historiography

The virgin-soil model (Crosby, *The Columbian Exchange*, 1972; Dobyns) is the framework Mann adopts, and
it is broadly accepted. Crosby (Ch. 2) provides the foundational case studies. The first well-documented pandemic began in the Greater Antilles Dec. 1518/Jan. 1519 (Santo Domingo): "It touched few Spaniards, and none of them died, but it devastated the Indians. The Spaniards reported that it killed one-third to one-half of the Indians." Las Casas claimed even higher losses. Oviedo recorded the Arawaks of Santo Domingo falling from ~1 million to fewer than 500 (children and adults) by 1548. The delay after 1492 is explained by smallpox's short duration in each patient, absence of nonhuman carriers or long-term human carriers, and the lethality of tropical sea voyages to the virus. It spread to Yucatán and central Mexico with Narváez's force in 1520 (via "a black man suffering from smallpox" who "infected the household in Cempoala... quickly infected the whole country"). In Tenochtitlán during the Noche Triste period: "When the Christians were exhausted from war, God saw fit to send the Indians smallpox, and there was a great pestilence in the city."

But it is **qualified** at the edges: **Massimo Livi Bacci** and others stress
that on Hispaniola the *first*-generation collapse owed as much to **starvation, violence, and forced
labour** as to pathogens, and that the very earliest epidemics are poorly documented (the first
well-attested Caribbean smallpox outbreak is 1518 per Crosby). Critics warn against treating disease as an
**impersonal, blameless** prime mover that obscures colonial agency. The mechanism is not in doubt; its
timing, magnitude, and weight relative to violence remain debated — see
[[pre-columbian-american-population]].

## The colonial-era waves and the demographic nadir (Burkholder & Johnson)

Burkholder and Johnson trace the **serial** character of the catastrophe across the
[[colonial-latin-america|colonial period]], stressing that it was not one die-off
but recurring waves over two centuries. Smallpox (from 1518), **measles** (1530s),
a hemorrhagic pestilence (mid-1540s), **typhus/plague** (1576), influenza, and later
**yellow fever and malaria** from Africa struck again and again. Crucially the
**timing of the nadir varied by region**: central Mexico bottomed out in the second
quarter of the seventeenth century (Borah's much-debated figures: ~25 million in
1518 — or, for cautious scholars, 10–13 million — down to ~0.75 million by 1622)
before recovery; the **Andes** and **Peru** did not reach their low point until the
**1718–20 epidemic**; the Caribbean peoples were essentially eliminated. The authors
also foreground the **non-disease multipliers** — conquest violence, the collapse of
indigenous welfare systems, forced labor ([[encomienda|the mita]]) lowering
fertility, and **flight/forced resettlement** (*congregación*/*reducción*) that
itself spread infection by concentrating populations. By the mid-seventeenth century
Spanish America's native population was perhaps under 10% — maybe under 5% — of its
1500 size. The colonial demographic record (post-1560 tax data) yields the narrower,
more reliable figures debated at [[pre-columbian-american-population]].

## Mesoamerica magnitudes and aftermath (MacLeod, CHNPA II.2)

Murdo J. MacLeod’s overview ([[cambridge-history-native-peoples-americas-v2-2|CHNPA II.2]]
Ch. 12) restates the catastrophe in regional terms for
[[colonial-mesoamerica-native-peoples|post-invasion Mesoamerica]]: from Spanish arrival native
populations declined catastrophically; on the major Caribbean islands and many humid tropical
Mesoamerican coasts native peoples **virtually disappeared**; on highlands and plateaus
populations may have fallen by **90 percent or more**. By mid-seventeenth century (earlier in
cores, much later in far peripheries and still-unconquered areas) native populations had fallen
to a remnant.

On causation, MacLeod aligns with the scholarly consensus that preinvasion American (and
specifically Mesoamerican) populations were **very large** (exact size an unresolvable
methodological side quarrel — see [[pre-columbian-american-population]]). Conquest warfare and
“culture shock” seem insufficient given period technologies and the slowness of Spanish cultural
conquest in much of Mesoamerica. With caveats, **pandemics** from Eurasian/African diseases
introduced to populations without acquired immunities are the main explanation — analogous to
Hawaii and Australia. Pre-Spanish disease history remains unclear (Gulf Coast fever research;
possible earlier Malthusian crises and nutritional failures).

**Social consequences.** Growth in non-Indian populations (Spanish immigration, African slave
imports partly as replacement labor, miscegenation) changed proportionate ethnic distribution and
forced readjustments in labor systems, conversion methods, village government, and land tenure
and use. Slow mid/late colonial demographic recovery is part of why pure [[black-legend|Black
Legend]] causation (Spanish cruelty alone) is questioned.

**Eighteenth-century return.** Great epidemics returned late in the colonial period: the
*matlazáhuatl* (probably typhus) of **1737–38** caused some native villages to disappear
completely and may have helped turn parts of the center-west definitively mestizo; the *año de
hambre* epidemic of **1786** probably killed fewer but was long remembered for accompanying
dearth. Maize and staple prices fluctuated wildly in subsistence crises; urban *alhóndigas* and
*pósitos* preferences left the countryside near starvation; the last ~20 years before
independence saw great maize-price increase especially **1808–1811**.

**Why the asymmetry ran one way.** [[diamond-guns-germs-and-steel-1997|Diamond]] supplies the ultimate cause behind virgin-soil mortality: most lethal Old World crowd diseases (smallpox, measles, influenza, tuberculosis) evolved from pathogens of domesticated herd animals, of which Eurasia had thirteen and the Americas effectively one. Dense, livestock-keeping, trade-linked Eurasian populations bred and survived these germs; isolated American populations, lacking comparable domesticates, had neither the diseases nor the acquired immunity — so the epidemiological exchange was overwhelmingly unidirectional. See [[guns-germs-and-steel]].

## Continuation of Virgin-Soil Effects (Crosby)

Even after the initial sixteenth-century waves, Old World diseases continued to inflict catastrophic losses on remaining indigenous populations in the Americas. Crosby documents the persistence of the pattern well into the nineteenth and twentieth centuries:

> “The men of the Old World continue to enjoy the benefits of biological warfare as American Indians continue to die of Old World diseases. Between 1871 and 1947, the total number of natives of Tierra del Fuego dropped from between 7,000 and 9,000 to 150, many of them victims of a malady which is even now one of the chief killers of the aborigines of the Chaco: measles.”

Most of the “really devastating killers” had crossed in the first post-Columbian century, but the process was ongoing. New arrivals such as cholera in the nineteenth century and the African anopheles mosquito (causing intensified malaria in Brazil after ~1929) added further mortality. Most New World diseases proved inexportable to the Old World (e.g., verruga Peruana, Oroya fever, Rocky Mountain spotted fever, pinta), with the major exception being venereal syphilis (Treponema pallidum), which Crosby treats as American in origin and which spread globally with profound social consequences.

The demographic vacuum created by these epidemics was filled primarily by mass migration from the Old World. “The source of the earliest mass migration of Old World peoples to the New World was not Europe... The mass of African immigrants arrived in America before the mass of Europeans.” With perhaps 8–10.5 million Africans brought by 1850 (nearly 90 percent to tropical America), and their descendants numbering at least 47 million by 1950, followed by the great nineteenth-century European torrent (45 million to the Americas by 1924), the biological replacement of populations was as consequential as the initial die-off.

`produced:` the long-term transformation of the Americas into regions whose populations were overwhelmingly of European and African ancestry. `contributed_to:` the creation of “two Europes” and “two Africas” on either side of the Atlantic, and the perception among Euro- and Afro-Americans of themselves as the “natives” of the lands whose original inhabitants had been reduced by disease.

This continuation underscores that virgin-soil vulnerability was not a one-time event but a structural feature of the post-1492 biological order, with effects lasting centuries. Updated from [[crosby-columbian-exchange-1972]] (Ch. 6).

## North America in the sixteenth century (Trigger & Swagerty, CHNPA I.1 ch. 6)

Trigger and Swagerty’s survey of [[sixteenth-century-north-american-contact]] is a primary North American synthesis for timing, regional unevenness, and mortality ranges — and for **not** collapsing the continent into a single pandemic story.

### Precontact disease pool

Disease was not unknown before contact: dysentery, influenza/pneumonia, arthritis, rickettsial/viral fevers, leishmaniasis, trypanosomiasis, endoparasites, syphilis/pinta, mild typhus; skeletal arthritis, TB, treponemal infections; maize-carbohydrate deficiencies; high infant mortality among Pueblo and Woodland groups; anemia in the Southwest. **Relative to Europe, North America lacked a heavy disease pool, especially numerous crowd infections, and was seemingly healthier.** Life expectancy was not significantly greater than Europe: Middle Mississippians ~25 mean age at death (Cahokia/St. Louis); Pecos 25–27; Texas coast ~30. Town dwellers may have lived less long than dispersed groups; TB was common among dense northeastern Iroquoians in smoky longhouses.

### Pathogen exchange and mortality ranges

After 1492 the Americas were hit by frequent virgin-soil epidemics without genetic resistance. Venereal syphilis **probably** moved New World → Europe by 1493, spreading in two decades across Europe, Africa, and Asia. Great killers introduced by Europeans, Africans, and animals included smallpox, measles, influenza, bubonic plague, diphtheria, typhus, cholera, scarlet fever, trachoma, whooping cough, chicken pox, and tropical malaria. Española’s first smallpox outbreak (1518) killed **one-third to one-half** of Arawaks and other enslaved Indians; spread through the Greater Antilles and Mesoamerican mainland; summer 1520 epidemic swept Tenochtitlan.

At least **17 major New World epidemics** are recorded for **1520–1600**. Initial virgin-soil mortality has been calculated at percentages ranging from **thirty to as high as seventy-five** during the initial outbreak; recurrence or other diseases (measles, influenza) could wipe out a group in three generations. Mortality increased from despondency and collapse of care — starvation, dehydration, suicide. Native remedies (sweatbaths and cold plunges) increased odds of dying of smallpox. Survivors were scarred; political/social/economic worlds reconfigured. Intermarriage/concubinage in small contact zones produced new peoples with some dual microbial resistance.

### Regional unevenness — preserve uncertainty

- **Southeast and Texas (documentary trauma firm).** Undisputed major ailment as early as 1526 from Ayllón, Narváez, and [[de-soto-expedition-1539]] records — clearest archaeological window for demographic collapse. At Cofitachequi, de Soto found “large uninhabited towns, choked with vegetation”; Indians confirmed plague and relocation. Marvin T. Smith: disease disrupted chiefdoms, forced town abandonment, and regrouped refugee-survivors into new confederated polities (Creeks, Cherokees, Catawbas); cultural traditions continued, but complex chiefdoms with large ceremonial centers did not return. Cabeza de Vaca reported a fatal stomach ailment reducing **Karankawa population by one-half**; pathology may be pandemic or localized (e.g. influenza traveling with him). At La Junta, townspeople described as “best formed… liveliest and most capable,” suggesting smallpox had **not** visited that Rio Grande section by 1530; western pandemic limit maybe Chihuahua–Valley of Mexico.

- **Dobyns pandemic horizon contested beyond SE/TX.** Documentary evidence for the Southeast and Texas confirms major trauma, but inferential evidence for California, the Southwest, the northern Plains, and the Northeast, as postulated by Dobyns, is **“plausible but unconfirmed.”**

- **Southwest.** Many pueblos abandoned in the sixteenth century and later; no direct documentary/osteological correlation with disease established. Traditional explanation: Athapaskan pressure/raiding. Challengers (incl. Steadman Upham from ceramic volume/variety) argue cataclysmic decline in aggregated population beginning ~A.D. 1520 — what [[coronado-expedition-1540|Coronado]] saw in 1540 may already have been a reconfigured landscape.

- **Northeast / Great Lakes.** First major recorded northeastern epidemic **1616–18**. Virginia Miller: southern pandemics did not reach Atlantic Canada; seasonal contacts did not trigger widespread infections, but 1616 had sixteenth-century Atlantic Canada antecedents; localized European disease plus dietary items (alcohol, biscuits, dried peas) reduced Micmacs by the start of the seventeenth century; 1616 struck French-trading Indians and spared Dutch-trading ones. Cartier’s 1535–36 winter near Stadacona: assumed European disease killed ~**10% of 500** inhabitants; repeated localized infusions may have role in St. Lawrence Iroquoian disappearance. Higher multiple interments among Senecas/Eries and young mortality at Seneca Cameron interpreted as late-sixteenth-century epidemics; Dobyns links frequent Seneca relocation to epidemics, but archaeology lacks expected settlement attrition. **Snow:** no Mohawk population decline before the seventeenth century. **Warrick:** Huron-Petun stable ~1400 until major decline 1630s. Trigger & Swagerty conclude there is reason to believe **no major European disease outbreaks in the Lower Great Lakes prior to historically recorded ones beginning 1633**; precontact densities lower than Dobyns; if Lakes and Middle Missouri were spared, areas farther north/west were unlikely hit; Dobyns overall estimates need reduction; lower density outside the Southeast limited spread. Northern Iroquoians may have shrunk from ~**110,000** (sixteenth–early seventeenth c.) to ~**8,000** by 1850; Snow’s preepidemic Micmac ~**12,000** vs Miller’s **50,000** — both retained as competing figures.

### “Widowed landscape” and the demographic paradox

By the founding of Jamestown 1607, Quebec 1608, and Santa Fe 1610, Natives had endured more than a century of human and nonhuman biological encounters. Francis Jennings: the continent a **“widowed landscape,”** veteran of a century of unintentional but highly lethal germ warfare. Tens of thousands of Europeans in North Atlantic whaling/fishing, plus privateers, imply several hundred thousand visitors across four generations — yet land-based expedition/colony Europeans numbered only ~**10,000 Spaniards** entered or born north of the Caribbean/Mexico in the sixteenth century; French/English efforts pale beside that. Spaniards remained vastly outnumbered by Timucuans and Pueblo Indians. Paradox: the biological balance tilted toward invaders across the seventeenth century via sixteenth-century pathogens, genetic intermixing, staple extractions, and abundant food economies.

### Mississippian political consequence (Smith + Trigger & Swagerty)

Bruce D. Smith (CHNPA I.1 ch. 5): European diseases, and to a lesser extent cultural domination, caused rapid nearly universal disappearance of chiefdom-level organizations in eastern North America within a century of contact — converting a heterogeneous landscape of rising/falling chiefdoms into a dominant trend of socio-political decline (pre-contact Vacant Quarter excepted).
