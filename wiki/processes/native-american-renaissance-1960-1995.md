# Native American Renaissance, 1960–1995

Cross-domain recovery of Native political, legal, economic, cultural, and demographic standing in the United States, Canada, and (to a lesser extent) Greenland after the [[reservation-period-1880-1960]] and the crisis of [[termination-policy]]. The label and period bounds come from Wilcomb E. Washburn’s closing chapter in [[sources/cambridge-history-native-peoples-americas-v1-2|CHNPA I.2]] (ch. 15, 1996).

## Definition and Scope

**AUTHOR POSITION (Washburn):** From a comparative and historical viewpoint, Native status improved significantly after c. 1960 in the United States, Canada, and (less fully) Greenland, so that “Native Renaissance” can legitimately name recent progress of descendants of the first Americans. He frames end-of-century perception as a glass half full/half empty: some Native and non-Native voices stress remaining deficits even as more is achieved. The chapter is explicitly evaluative—optimistic about government-to-government relations and market initiative, skeptical of radical activism and welfare-state dependency—so interpretive theses below are attributed, not asserted in wiki voice.

The process is multi-domain, not a single event: demography; tribal and pan-Indian politics; law and courts; economic policy (including gaming); art, literature, and scholarship; religious freedom; and public-health turnaround. Geographic scope is continental North America plus Greenland Home Rule. It is not limited to U.S. Red Power activism, though that is one phase.

## Causal Drivers

- **Policy reverse of termination.** U.S. government moved from 1950s [[termination-policy]] (threat to tribal governments’ existence) toward what Presidents Reagan, Bush, and Clinton termed government-to-government relations among tribal, state, and federal governments. Menominee Restoration Act (1973) is cited as signaling the official end of termination. Nixon (1970) and Reagan (1983) statements affirmed self-determination without termination.
- **Institutional inheritance of the IRA.** Representative tribal structures largely under the [[indian-reorganization-act-1934]], unevenly accepted (Navajo refused IRA authority), enabled later contracting and court development under [[tribal-self-determination]].
- **Legal and advocacy infrastructure.** Growth of tribal courts; *Williams v. Lee* (1959); Native American Rights Fund (est. 1970/71); National American Indian Court Judges Association (1968); Native American Studies programs; Vine Deloria Jr. and related intellectual activism.
- **Pan-Indian and radical pressure.** [[national-congress-of-american-indians]], [[american-indian-movement]], National Indian Youth Council (“Red Power”), Trail of Broken Treaties (1972), [[wounded-knee-occupation-1973]], Longest Walk (1978); Canadian Assembly of First Nations, Métis National Council, Inuit Committee on National Issues; Oka 1990.
- **Demographic recovery and self-identification.** Post-1950 steep census growth (partly self-identification after 1960 racial self-ID policy, ethnic pride, program benefits) underwrote political visibility.
- **Federal program surge.** War on Poverty / Great Society “Indian desks,” EDA, CETA meshed with Indian Self-Determination Act 1975; ~$3 billion annually in 1980s U.S. Indian-related appropriations (Washburn’s figure).

## Major Phases

### Demography and social indicators

1990 U.S. census: 2,015,143 American Indians and Alaskan Natives (Inuit and Aleuts), up from 1,423,043 in 1980 (~0.5% of U.S. population). Official counts include exclusive Indian identifiers and “American Indians of Multiple Ancestry”; “Americans of Indian Descent” (~5.5 million 1980, nearly 9 million 1990) are excluded. Nadir ~1890 (~228,000 in U.S.); probable pre-contact range 2–5 million. Decadal growth 1950–1990: 47%, 51%, 72%, 38%. Largest 1990 state populations: Oklahoma 252,000, California 242,000, Arizona 204,000, New Mexico 134,000; largest tribes Cherokee 308,132, Navajo 219,198, Chippewa ([[ojibwe]]) 103,826, Sioux ([[lakota]] and related) 103,255. Over half rural/nonmetropolitan; under half urban by 1980s; about one-quarter on reservations.

**Canada 1991:** slightly over 1 million of ~27 million reported Aboriginal origins (41% increase from 1986); combined single/multiple: 783,980 North American Indian, 212,650 [[metis|Métis]], 49,255 [[inuit|Inuit]]. Historical nadir by 1867: ~100,000–125,000 Indians, ~10,000 Métis/half-breeds, ~2,000 Inuit. 592 bands; 2,284 reserves/settlements totaling 12.6 million hectares (1985); average band under 700 members.

Per Washburn’s reading of census comparisons: by 1980 Indians had reached economic parity with African-Americans (employment and income), slight edge in 1990; Indian life expectancy overtook African-American between 1970 and 1980. Life expectancy U.S. Indian/Alaska Native: 51 (1940) → 61.7 (1960) → 71.1 (1980). Infant mortality Reservation States 62.7 (1954–56) → 9.7 (1986–88). Alcoholism remained the leading contemporary scourge (alcohol-related death rates 4–5× U.S. average in 1980s).

**AUTHOR POSITION (Washburn):** High intermarriage (48% of American Indian men and women married non-Indians) and OTA blood-quantum projections do not equate to disappearance of Indian identity; prior extinction predictions via disease, warfare, or assimilation failed.

### Political renaissance (U.S., Alaska, Canada, Greenland)

- **U.S. tribal government:** BIA staffing ~87% Indian after *Morton v. Mancari* (1974); Reagan-era government-to-government rhetoric; examples such as Red Lake Chippewa tribal plates (1974) and *Oklahoma Tax Commission v. Sac and Fox Nation* (1993) on taxation in Indian Country. Navajo leadership crises (MacDonald vs Zah; 1989–90 corruption convictions) illustrate internal governance stress concurrent with the renaissance.
- **Pan-Indian organizations:** NCAI as loose tribal/individual body closest to official tribal-government spokesman; AIM as radical individual organization often challenging tribal governments (notably Wounded Knee 1973). **AUTHOR POSITION (Washburn):** 1960s–70s radical challenge to tribal-government legitimacy gave way to conventional politics; AIM leaders Dennis Banks and Russell Means shifted toward influencing governments; Collier “in effect vindicated.”
- **Alaska:** [[alaska-native-claims-settlement-1971]] created Native economic corporations expected to supplant tribal/village political organizations—deliberately unlike lower-forty-eight IRA tribes; 1991 amendments and 1993 Ada Deer recognition left ultimate political status unsettled in Washburn’s judgment.
- **Canada:** 1969 Trudeau White Paper proposed ending special Indian status (analogue of U.S. termination); withdrawn 1971 after Red Paper *Citizens Plus* and protest. *Calder* (1973) and later Gitksan-Wet’suwet’en litigation shifted aboriginal-title law. James Bay and Northern Quebec Agreement (1975) as first modern Native claims settlement ($232.5 million over 21 years; exclusive ownership 5,543 km²; Cree regional-government model). Berger Mackenzie Valley Pipeline Inquiry opened fundamental status questions. Constitution Act 1982 affirmed aboriginal and treaty rights and defined Aboriginal peoples as Indians, Inuit, and Métis; Elijah Harper helped block Meech Lake 1990. Oka crisis (1990): 78-day armed Mohawk defence. Nunavut Final Land Claim (25 May 1993); Inuvialuit 1984.
- **Greenland:** Home Rule Act effective 1 May 1979; EC withdrawal 1 Jan. 1985; Inuit Circumpolar Conference founded 1977.

### Legal renaissance

Rapid post-1960 development of Indian law; ~150 tribal courts. *Williams v. Lee* (1959); *Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez* (1978) subordinated individual federal-rights claims to tribal sovereignty in enrollment disputes; *Oliphant v. Suquamish* (1978) denied tribal criminal jurisdiction over non-members; Washington fishing-rights case (1979) held treaty “in common with” language meant equal share. 1991 tribal leaders asked Bush to “restore” jurisdiction over non-Indians and non-member Indians; Congress later allowed criminal prosecution of non-member Indians but not non-Indians. Federal recognition of remnant eastern and other non-recognized tribes continued (Mohegan, Catawba, Ione Band of Miwok among recent examples Washburn cites). American Indian Religious Freedom Act 1978 (amended 1991, 1993); *Lyng* (1988) sacred-site setback.

### Economic renaissance

EDA 1966–79 spent ~$0.5 billion on reservation development with weak industrial-park occupancy (23 of 48 parks active 1979). CETA said to “mesh perfectly” with Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act (P.L. 93-638). Indian Self-Determination Act 4 Jan. 1975 allowed tribal management of IHS programs—by late 1980s more than one-third of service units tribally operated. **AUTHOR POSITION (Washburn):** 1960s–70s programs reflected optimism, militancy, and Euro-American guilt without clear purpose beyond poverty relief and political power; Canadian Indian Affairs budget ~$2.5B (1990) amid a “compelling paradox” of rising expenditures paralleling rising social-disintegration indices (illustrated by Shkilnyk’s Grassy Narrows study). NCAI president Joseph B. DeLaCruz: dependency is “our Achilles heel.” 1990s gaming under Indian Gaming Regulatory Act 1988 (Mashantucket Pequot casino ~$600M/year as spectacular case); Mississippi Choctaw corporate success as non-gaming contrast. **AUTHOR POSITION:** 1990s shift toward market and tribal/individual initiative over pure federal solutions.

### Artistic, literary, scholarly, religious

- **Art:** Indian Arts and Crafts Board (1935); Institute of American Indian Arts (Santa Fe, 1962); later artists (Red Star, Scholder, Cannon, Gorman); Canada (Morrisseau, Odjig, Bill Reid); Inuit print co-ops; Métis architect Douglas Cardinal (Canadian Museum of Civilization; planned Smithsonian NMAI). **AUTHOR POSITION:** art is where renaissance is most visible to outsiders.
- **Literature:** 1969 Momaday Pulitzer for *House Made of Dawn* as formal U.S. marker; Welch, Ortiz, Silko, Erdrich, Vizenor, et al.; Canadian counterparts.
- **Scholarship:** Native American Studies; NARF priorities; Rough Rock and Navajo Community College (1968); American Indian Chicago Conference 1960 / NIYC split; Deloria as NCAI executive director 1964 and *Custer Died for Your Sins* (1969); Princeton Convocation 1970; ~24 Indian-controlled community colleges.
- **Religion:** Collier 1934 circular; AIRFA; potlatch and Sun Dance revival; traditional Haudenosaunee renewal; Inuit evangelical Christianity; Native American Church debates.

## Geographic Spread

- **United States:** Reservations, urban centers, Alaska corporations, eastern recognition petitions.
- **Canada:** Bands/reserves, modern comprehensive claims (James Bay, Inuvialuit, Nunavut), provincial flashpoints (Oka, B.C. title).
- **Greenland:** Home Rule within the Danish realm; circumpolar Inuit networks.
- Comparative framing is deliberate in Washburn’s chapter; pace and institutional form differ (Alaska corporations vs lower-48 tribes; Canadian aboriginal title vs U.S. treaty/trust).

## End Conditions

Washburn’s chapter ends at mid-1990s with open trajectories rather than a terminal event: Alaska political form unsettled; Canadian self-government meaning under Constitution Act 1982 still contested; gaming debated; health gains coexisting with alcohol-related mortality. **AUTHOR POSITION (Washburn):** Assimilation is not extinguishing political, legal, and cultural distinctiveness of the Renaissance; multiethnic societies allow change so “Indianness” need not match first-contact culture; pan-Indian gloss can encompass historically hostile peoples because majority-culture definitions unified fractionated groups (urban migration of nearly half the Indian population accentuating this); future assessable with optimism despite intellectual “culture of despair.” Later historiography may reperiodize or reject the “Renaissance” label—record it as Washburn’s frame, not consensus fact.

## Historiography

Primary CHNPA I.2 treatment is Washburn’s personal assessment chapter, not multi-author peer narrative. Reliability notes on the source page flag evaluative glass-half-full thesis, skepticism of radical activism, and self-citation.
