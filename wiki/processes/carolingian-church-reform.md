# Carolingian Church Reform: Organisation, Law, and Liturgy

## Definition and Scope

**Carolingian church reform** (*correctio*) was the sustained effort of the Frankish rulers and
episcopate, from the councils of the 740s onward, to **organise, standardise, and "correct"** the
western church — its hierarchy, its law, and its worship — as part of the broader project of creating a
rightly ordered Christian society. In [[mckitterick-ncmh-v2-1995|NCMH II]] (Reynolds, Ch. 22) it is
examined through three linked domains: organisation, canon law, and liturgy.

## Organisation

The reform rebuilt and regularised the **diocesan and metropolitan structure** of the Frankish church:
the restoration of metropolitan (archiepiscopal) authority — Mainz restored as a metropolitanate in 748,
and a wave of new metropolitan provinces created (Embrun, Aix, Besançon, Tarentaise) in 811 — and the
strengthening of episcopal oversight over the proprietary churches (**[[eigenkirche|Eigenkirchen]]**) and
the emerging parish network. The **tithe** was made a general, legally enforceable obligation, giving the
church a regular share of agrarian production.

## Canon Law

A central instrument was the standardisation of **canon law**. Pope Hadrian I gave [[charlemagne|Charlemagne]]
an authoritative collection, the **Dionysio-Hadriana**, which became a normative reference for the Frankish
church. The period also produced the notorious **Pseudo-Isidorian (False) Decretals** — a mid-ninth-century
forgery of papal letters and conciliar canons designed to protect bishops from metropolitans and lay powers
and to exalt the authority of Rome, one of the most consequential forgeries in the history of the western
church (see [[donation-of-constantine]]).

## Liturgy

The reform pursued the **Romanisation of the liturgy**: the adoption across the Frankish church of Roman
liturgical books (the **Gregorian sacramentary**, supplemented to meet Frankish needs) and of **Roman chant**
(the ancestor of "Gregorian" chant), displacing the older Gallican rite. This drive for liturgical uniformity
was both a religious and a political act of integration.

## Significance

Carolingian church reform created much of the **institutional, legal, and liturgical infrastructure** of the
western medieval church — a standardised canon law, a Romanised liturgy, an enforceable tithe, and a
strengthened diocesan order — and embedded the ideology of *correctio* that later medieval reform movements
would inherit. It was inseparable from [[carolingian-kingship|royal government]]: the king directed the reform
of a church charged with the moral welfare of all his subjects.

## Historiography

NCMH II (Reynolds) is a specialist's survey of organisation, law, and liturgy, dense with diocesan detail. The
authenticity, dating, and influence of the Pseudo-Isidorian Decretals, and the precise mechanics of liturgical
"Romanisation," are long-standing fields of specialist debate. The reform connects to the
[[carolingian-renaissance]] (the corrected texts) and to [[religion-and-lay-society|the Christianisation of
lay life]].

## From Logan, History of the Church in the Middle Ages (2013)

Consecrated Frankish kings issued mixed secular-religious capitularies; **Admonitio generalis (789)** was Charlemagne’s first entirely religious capitulary. In 774 at the Pavia siege Hadrian gave him the **Dionysio-Hadriana**; first 60 of 82 Admonitio articles drawn from it—McKitterick: “basic outlines for the administration of the Frankish church.” Additional 22 chapters show pastoral originality: schools at every monastery/cathedral; Lord’s Prayer instruction; detailed Sunday rest; preaching syllabus (Trinity, Incarnation, judgment, charity, virtues). Mid-ninth-century **Pseudo-Isidorian Decretals**: ~100 forged early papal decretals from a Frankish workshop, vehicle for the [[donation-of-constantine]]; arose from Hincmar’s suffragans seeking direct papal connection against metropolitan interference; later used by eleventh-century reformers and Gratian (c.1140; 375 chapters from this source). **Private daily Mass** and multiplication of altars: more monks became priests wanting daily Mass—private devotion vs corporate worship “turned a corner.” Proprietary churches (*Eigenkirchen*) as origins of parishes and later lay-appointment disputes; Chrodegang of Metz c.755 rule for canons and Roman liturgy/chants at Metz; c.786 Hadrian’s deluxe sacramentary supplemented (Alcuin or another) for Frankish needs—tenth-century return to Rome as historic **Roman Rite** (little changed until 1960s). Forced Saxon conversion under Charlemagne treated as a dark counterpart to voluntary *correctio* ideals.
