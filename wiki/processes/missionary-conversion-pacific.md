## Definition and Scope

The missionary conversion of the Pacific Islands was a multi-generational process by which Protestant and Catholic mission societies — operating primarily from London, Boston, Sydney, and Paris — transformed the religious life of [[polynesia]], [[melanesia]], and [[micronesia]] between the 1790s and 1900. It was inseparable from the political, economic, and legal transformations of the same era. Conversion was never simply the outcome of missionary effort; it was shaped decisively by Islander choices, chiefly politics, inter-denominational rivalry, and the material incentives — literacy, trade access, political recognition — that missionaries carried alongside the Gospel.

The process is best understood as a negotiated encounter: "conversion was not the direct consequence of 'liberal' or 'illiberal' missionary tactics, so much as the outcome of Islanders' choices."

## Causal Drivers

- **Chiefly strategic calculation.** Missionaries first targeted chiefs for conversion, "reasoning that the people would follow them." Chiefs who welcomed missionaries hoped for "more immediate benefits than a state of grace: tools, clients and skills such as literacy among them." Rejection of indigenous gods could also undercut the ritual authority of factional rivals.
- **Literacy as power.** "In most Pacific cultures words have power: to cure, to curse, to provoke wars, to invoke the divine." Islanders were initially "more attracted to words — the power of reading and writing — than The Word as abstraction." From 1830, Māori interest shifted from Western goods to "the knowledge behind European authority."
- **Material association.** Christianity was associated with the "wealth and strength of European culture." A Samoan informant paraphrased in John Williams's 1830 journal: "Wars have ceased among them. Ships visited them without fear... and brought them an abundance of Property... It would be good to lotu too."
- **Factionalism and rivalry.** Mission affiliation became another dimension of inter-chiefly competition: "sectarianism became simply another dimension of factionalism." A chief's "pursuit of distinction, or displeasure with a missionary, could cause an entire village suddenly to switch denominations."
- **Demographic crisis.** Where epidemic disease (measles, smallpox, dysentery) decimated populations, missionaries often provided the only organised social infrastructure; conversion and survival became entangled. In Vanuatu especially, "evangelisation coincided with appalling depopulation, such that many Islanders could readily imagine the extinction of their cultures."

## Major Phases

### Phase 1 — LMS establishment in Tahiti and Tonga (1797–1830)
The London Missionary Society landed ten missionary artisans in Tonga in 1797 and focused first on Tahiti. The Tonga mission failed twice (withdrawing to Sydney in 1800) due to civil war and inability to communicate in Tongan. The decisive opening came in 1826–1827, when two Tahitian teachers were sent to Nukualofa and built a congregation before British missionaries joined.

### Phase 2 — Chiefly conversion and state religion (1820s–1850s)
The decisive pattern emerged: conversion of a paramount chief brought mass conversion. In [[tonga]], Taufa'ahau (George Tupou) combined dynastic titles and made Methodism "almost the state religion" — uniquely reversing the normal dependency: "the mission depended so massively on the King's patronage that he, rather than any missionary, was both the temporal ruler and the real head of the church." In Tahiti, mass conversion followed Pomare II's 1815 military victory at Feipi with musket-armed Christian converts. In Hawai'i, conversion of the chiefesses Ka'ahumanu and Kina'u (widows of Kamehameha I) secured the mission's position.

### Phase 3 — LMS expansion and inter-denominational rivalry (1830s–1860s)
John Williams brought the LMS to Samoa in 1830, finding a more receptive reception than in Tonga. Rivalry between LMS Congregationalists and Wesleyans (Methodists) was particularly bitter in Samoa because of a disputed 1836 "agreement" to divide spheres. "In a Samoan village in the 1830s, therefore, one might find followers of the 'lotu Tonga' (Methodist), the 'lotu Taiti' (LMS), the syncretic Sio Vili movement, charismatic cults centred on beachcombers, as well as the 'devil party'—the unconverted." Catholic missionaries entered [[polynesia]] from the early 1840s, backed by the French Navy in Tahiti and the Marquesas.

### Phase 4 — Melanesian and Micronesian missions (1848–1890s)
After several violent failures (John Williams killed at Erromango 1839; Marist bishop killed in the Solomons 1845), sustained Melanesian missions began with John Geddie's Presbyterian mission to Aneityum (1848), which became "practically a Presbyterian theocracy" within twelve years. The American Board mission to Pohnpei arrived in 1852. New Zealand Anglicans worked the northern Vanuatu islands on more culturally tolerant principles. Aneityum's success was absolute — "not one avowed pagan on Aneityum" — but the island's population was "fast dying out."

## Words and The Word — Literacy and Education

Mission societies established the first systems of public education in the Pacific. Early companies included printers: Elisha Loomis in Hawai'i, William Woon in Tonga, Samuel Ella in Samoa. "Tongans became literate so quickly, and made such demands that the Methodists brought in a press and a printer, who published 3000 copies of a school primer within ten days of his arrival." Between 1824 and 1846, the Hawai'i mission published eighty books totalling 65,444 pages.

Islanders were "typically eager for literacy, even while indifferent to church membership." In early Pohnpei, "seven schools operated on Pohnpei, enrolling from eight to thirty-four students, although there were no converts." Māori "proved equally keen to learn to read and write, expecting this technology to reveal the dynamics of European society and economy."

Mission schools produced a generation of bilingual, bicultural Islanders who became political brokers: "gifted Islanders of lesser birth used their education to achieve prominence." The Hawaiian Chiefs' Children's School (1839) educated all future monarchs. Tupou College opened in Tonga in 1866; Malua College trained LMS teachers in Samoa. Anglican training in Norfolk Island trained priests from northern Vanuatu.

## The New Laws — Missionary Influence on Legal Systems

"Missionaries were responsible for crafting the early law codes governing civil and domestic behaviour." This intersection of religion and law is treated as a major outcome of missionary conversion in [[denoon-cambridge-pacific-islanders-1997]]. See [[pacific-chiefly-state-formation]] for detailed discussion of constitutional change in Tonga, Hawai'i, Tahiti, Kosrae, and Samoa.

Key examples from this range:
- **[[tonga]]:** The Vava'u Code (1839) committed Tonga to Christianity and prohibited murder, theft, adultery, fornication, and alcohol retail. The 1862 constitution explicitly abolished "serfdom" and "vassalage," banned land sales to foreigners, and introduced individual land leasehold. The 1875 constitution (drafted by missionary Shirley Baker under the King's direction) secured international recognition from Britain, Germany, and the United States and "with few modifications, still serves the kingdom."
- **Tahiti:** Henry Nott, Pomare's teacher, was "the principal author of the 1824 constitution which established a parliament of male chiefs and elected district representatives."
- **Hawai'i:** The Kuleana Act (1850) enabled native tenants to claim fee-simple title; a simultaneous law gave foreigners the right to own land — enabling the Great Mahele's dispossession. "Cultivators who had managed to remain self-sufficient were now thrust into cash cropping, selling produce, wage labour, or prostitution."
- **Aneityum (Vanuatu):** "The church was the real centre of each village, and its Samoan or Aneityumese teacher the real political leader. Local courts comprising zealous Christians enforced Biblical injunctions, with the aid of a private police force."
- **Ambae (Vanuatu):** A surviving code of thirty-four congregation rules blended the Ten Commandments, Queensland plantation discipline, and local masculinist norms — "one constituted by adult men, heading stable and sober family households, worshipping in a congregation united under one preacher... bound together in an economy lubricated by credit and cash."

## The London Missionary Society — Specific Role

The LMS was the dominant Protestant society in central and eastern Polynesia. It pioneered the use of Islander missionary teachers: Tahitian and Samoan evangelists were deployed westward into [[melanesia]], carrying both Christianity and a form of Polynesian cultural imperialism. "British missionaries in Samoa sent Polynesians to missionise the 'savage' isles to the west." The LMS created Malua College in Samoa to train Pacific-born teachers and missionaries.

The LMS also intersected with commerce. The early LMS missionaries in Samoa "may have been the first agents in Samoa to spur production for an external market": from 1842, they collected donations of coconut oil and sold them to John Chauner Williams, son of the martyred missionary and the first commercial businessman in Samoa. LMS missionaries' sons frequently entered trade; mission networks provided cover and contacts for commercial operations. Burns Philp offered Presbyterian missionaries in Vanuatu discounts "hoping to recoup that cost through access to copra."

## Interaction with Other Processes

- **[[pacific-resource-raiding]]:** Missionaries and traders co-arrived; traders used mission networks for access to chiefs and labourers; missionaries taught that commodity production was compatible with Christian virtue.
- **[[pacific-chiefly-state-formation]]:** Mission alliances were a key tool of chiefly centralisation. Pomare II, Kamehameha I's successors, and Taufa'ahau all used mission support to consolidate political power.
- **melanesian-indentured-labour:** "Young men returning from Queensland became initiators" of Christian copra production on Aoba/Ambae — the labour trade mediated religious change.
- **Gender:** Missionaries attempted to instil Western gender norms at odds with Polynesian chiefly women's authority. In Hawai'i an 1845 law (reflecting missionary influence) made a married woman "civilly dead, with no right to dispose of property" — "profoundly at odds with the autonomy, cultural value and personal authority of Polynesian women."

## Resistance to Conversion

Resistance took many forms: ignoring missionaries while welcoming trade goods (Tannese); expelling missionaries after epidemics (Tanna expelled Paton during the 1860 measles epidemic); armed conflict (Rotuma's 1870s wars between traditionalists, Protestants, and Catholics); nativistic movements blending Christian and pagan elements (mamaia movement in Tahiti, 1826–1840s). Where Islanders "drove out missionaries, they endured naval bombardment and an invasion by sailors who destroyed crops and houses."

## End Conditions

The process did not "end" in any clean sense; by 1900 most of Polynesia and much of coastal Melanesia and Micronesia had formally converted. The process gave way to institutionalised colonial Christianity, often under territorial administration. The theocratic intensity of the early mission period moderated; mission schools were superseded or co-opted by colonial public education systems (Hawai'i's Royal School was placed under the Minister of Public Instruction in 1846). The LMS itself was wound down in the Pacific in the early twentieth century, replaced by successor national churches.

## Historiography

The core debate concerns missionary agency vs. Islander agency in conversion. [[denoon-cambridge-pacific-islanders-1997]] firmly emphasises Islander choice: "it is now impossible to know what inspired either conversion or rejection, but some circumstances can be conjectured." This represents a historiographical move away from earlier narratives that treated conversion as missionary achievement or colonial imposition. Key archival base: London Missionary Society papers (School of Oriental and African Studies; Mitchell Library, Sydney); Methodist missionary archives (Mitchell Library); Anglican archives (Lambeth Palace; University of Papua New Guinea Library). Landmark secondary works: Latukefu, *Church and State in Tonga*; Kamakau, *Ruling Chiefs of Hawaii*; Hanlon, *Upon a Stone Altar* (Pohnpei); Meleisea, *The Making of Modern Samoa*; Bonnemaison, *The Tree and the Canoe* (Vanuatu).

## The Culture Concept and Mission Transformation

Gardner (ch. 46, [[hattori-samson-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v2-2023]]) adds an intellectual history dimension to this process: how the anthropological concept of "culture" — largely generated from Pacific fieldwork — re-entered Pacific mission practice and reshaped the political meaning of conversion and decolonization. See [[concepts/culture-concept-and-missions-pacific]] for full treatment.

Before the 20th century, missionaries described Islander life using "tradition," "custom," "practice" prefaced with "primitive" or "savage." Jane Samson describes this as simultaneous "brothering" (asserting human unity to justify mission) and "othering" (emphasizing savage practices). Missionaries discovered early that conversion did not produce Europeanization: "those who learned to read Bible passages in Islander languages remained steadfastly Tahitian, Fijian, Samoan, or Tongan in their social practices."

The decisive shift came through the culture concept developed by Malinowski (*Argonauts of the Western Pacific*, 1922, from Trobriand fieldwork) and Mead (*Coming of Age in Samoa*, 1928, from Samoan fieldwork). Malinowski was explicitly anti-mission: missionaries "destroy the natives' joy in life; they destroy their psychological raison d'être." From the late 1920s, anthropology entered mission training (Radcliffe-Brown at Sydney, 1928; Maurice Leenhardt bridging French anthropology and New Caledonia mission). H. Richard Niebuhr's *Christ and Culture* (1951) gave theological grounds for accepting local cultures.

Key Pacific developments:
- **Jerusalem 1928**: Chinese and Japanese delegates demanded national churches reflecting their cultures; European missionaries rebuffed the demand but were put on the defensive.
- **Malua Conference, Sāmoa (c. 1961)**: First Pacific Island churches meeting, organized by Fijian and Tongan clergy trained in North American third-world Christian networks, declared "The Coconut curtain has lifted."
- **Pacific Theological College, Suva (1966)**: Incorporated sociology and anthropology in curriculum; produced divinity theses on indigenization of worship across all major island groups.
- **Pacific Conference of Churches**: A "staunchly political organization which supported decolonizing churches and nations."

The result was that Pacific Christianity — far from being destroyed by cultural relativism — became the ideological vehicle for decolonization: "Paciﬁc clergy and devout Christian politicians moved between pulpit and parliament" carrying theologies of enculturation (Catholic) or contextualization (Protestant). Vanuatu's constitution, PNG's constitution, and the Solomon Islands' post-independence government all bore the mark of Pacific clergy trained in this theological tradition.

**Distinctive emic–etic tension**: Pacific Islanders understood *kastom* as dynamic, politically deployable, and fragmented — not the static, bounded "culture" of the anthropological concept. The Maasina Rule movement in Malaita (late 1940s), led largely by South Seas Evangelical Mission members, codified customs "keeping those deemed worthy and rejecting those that were not" as a political act — far from the classical anthropological vision of unchanging cultural law.
