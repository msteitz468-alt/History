# Late-Medieval Religious Thought and Lay Piety

The fourteenth century saw religious life shift "to give greater weight to the voice of the laity"
and to the **moral and pastoral** rather than the purely speculative. [[jones-ncmh-v6-2000|NCMH
VI]] (Jeremy Catto, Ch. 2) traces how the scholastic achievement of the thirteenth century was
turned, in the fourteenth, toward a new theological method, a flowering of mysticism, and an
increasingly lay and affective piety.

## From Scholastic Synthesis to the *Via Moderna*

The "main determinant" of fourteenth-century religious thought was the thirteenth-century effort to
"marshal, state logically and resolve questions according to an agreed theological language." The
great syntheses — Thomas Aquinas (canonised 1323), and the Franciscan doctors **Duns Scotus**,
Peter Auriol, François de Meyronnes — were followed by a **critical re-examination of theological
language** associated with **William of Ockham** and the ***via moderna***, and by a "vigorous
return to Augustinian ideas" as moral and social questions came to dominate. These were not rigid
"schools": almost all fourteenth-century theologians were "independent thinkers... united by a
common inheritance of terms and concepts and a common analytical training."

## The Theologian in the World

Catto stresses that theology "came to focus more directly than before on the moral and pastoral
questions which confronted individuals," and that theologians now "play an active role in the
government of the Church." Their careers became practical and international — Pierre de la Palud
arguing against [[mendicant-orders|Franciscan poverty]] and promoting crusade; Richard FitzRalph
debating Greeks and Armenians and ending as archbishop of Armagh and foe of the friars; the Cretan
friar Peter Philargi rising to become Pope Alexander V. "Practical experience refocused these
theologians' interests," bringing pastoral questions to the fore — and breeding sharp controversy
(above all the renewed dispute over the **poverty of Christ** under [[papacy|John XXII]]).

## Mysticism and Lay Devotion

The century's most original religious literature was **vernacular and mystical**, drawing its
concepts from the theologians but addressing the soul directly: **Meister Eckhart's** "ground of
the soul," the "unity of essence" of **Jan Ruysbroeck**, the "naked love" of the English *Cloud of
Unknowing*, the contemplative method of **Ramon Llull**. This affective, interior piety — together
with the growth of lay literacy and devotional confraternities — gave the laity a larger and more
independent role in religious life, even as it brushed against the limits of orthodoxy (Eckhart's
propositions were censured).

## Significance

This turn — toward the pastoral, the moral, the vernacular and the lay — is the religious
counterpart of the century's wider "greater weight to the voice of the laity." Sharpened by the
trauma of [[black-death|plague]] and war into an anxious **culture of death** (the *Dance of
Death*, the cadaver tomb), and carrying the *via moderna* and the poverty controversy forward, it
is the seedbed both of late-medieval lay devotion (the *devotio moderna*) and of the reforming and
heterodox movements — Wyclif and the Lollards, and the Bohemian reform — that the volume treats in
its chapters on the [[papacy|Church]].
