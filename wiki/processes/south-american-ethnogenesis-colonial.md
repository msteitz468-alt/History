## Definition and Scope

This page documents the specifically South American colonial-era expression of [[ethnogenesis]] — the formation of new human groupings as an outcome of the colonial encounter. The volume synthesis chapter by Stuart B. Schwartz and Frank Salomon (Ch. 21 of the *Cambridge History of the Native Peoples of the Americas*, Vol. III) argues that "while colonial society was prolific of new categories of people, not all new categories of people defined themselves as peoples. And for those that did, there were various choices for group definition besides internalizing the stigma of 'mixed blood.'"

The scope is continent-wide but particularly focused on the Andes, Amazonia, and the Guiana–Orinoco zone. It covers the full colonial period and its nineteenth-century aftermath. The key analytical distinction is between *classification* (new categories imposed by colonizers) and *peoplehood* (new corporate identities actively claimed by their members as grounds for solidarity and political action). Not every colonial category produced a people; some produced only administrative fictions; others generated durable identities still operative today.

This page focuses on the South American regional expression; the general analytical concept is in [[ethnogenesis]].

## Causal Drivers

**Pre-contact instability.** Schwartz and Salomon insist that "it is a basic error to suppose prehispanic society was more stable than societies elsewhere." Migration, fission, incorporation, and imperial mitmaq colonies were already ethnogenetically active before 1492. The *Chiriguano* of eastern Bolivia were a Tupi-speaking–Arawakan fusion created before Spanish arrival. Some Panoan language fragmentation along the eastern Andes may reflect pre-Columbian political competition. "What occurred at the moment of contact was not so much a qualitative novelty as a vast upsurge in the complexity of social contacts among peoples, with drastically noncongruent schemes for describing the results."

**The casta system and blood ideology.** Iberian colonial society used the language of "blood" (*sangre*) and "purity" (*limpieza de sangre*) — already forged in the Spanish wars against Moors and Sephardim — to structure colonial inequality. "People of mixed birth formed not so much a new category as a challenge to categorization itself." The impossibility of smooth gradients between casta categories made "mixed" identity structurally anomalous, but also increasingly unavoidable as mixed people "filled social class roles vital to the colonial economy and state."

**Demographic collapse.** Old World pathogens devastated complex societies. The Omagua of Ecuador and Brazil — a dense, wealthy riverine agricultural people — broke up into separately recognized village peoples. South America's few hunter-gatherer groups, including the Sirionó, are now understood as *descended from* complex societies that "adapted to aggression by stripping down technology and organization for mobility's sake," not as survivals of primitive organization.

**Mission concentration and lingua franca promotion.** Franciscan and Jesuit missions physically relocated peoples into multi-ethnic settlements where thirty to forty "nations" might coexist speaking mutually unintelligible languages (Jesuit Joao Daniel, 1749). The colonial promotion of Quechua as the "language of Christianity" in the Andes, and of Nhengatu (Tupi-based *lingua geral*) in Brazil, produced new ethnic identities organized around colonial languages rather than pre-Columbian heritage.

**European alliance and the colonial tribe.** By recognizing leaders and supplying weapons, European colonial powers "precipitated new and violent political dynamics, which they themselves then misperceived as primordial." The most "militarized" indigenous societies of the colonial era were in many cases new formations: the [[caribs-mainland|Caribs]], the Muras, the Mundurucu, the Potiguar — each rose to regional power through alliance with one or another European state.

## Major Phases and Types of Ethnogenesis

### 1. Colonial Tribes

The "colonial tribe" — warlike, heterogeneous-origin, state-aligned — is the most visible product of contact. Typically formed at the outer edges of colonial domains where states needed militias, colonial tribes "tended to pick up elements of European culture, such as insignia based on military uniforms, as emblems of emerging solidarity." Examples:

- **[[caribs-mainland|Caribs (Kari'na)]]**: Dutch-allied trading-military polity that rose from marginal status before contact to regional hegemony over northeastern South America. Their identity was as much political as ethnic: "being caribe meant sharing the political and military orientations of Carib leaders but not necessarily their cultural proclivities as Karina."
- **Muras** (Madeira–Rio Negro, 18th c.): "composed of many different languages and people kidnapped from the villages and people who have taken refuge among them, all going by the name of Mura" (Portuguese officer, 1785). Their leader Ambrosio was a former mission Indian with a mestiza half-sister and a white captive brother-in-law.
- **Potiguar** (northeastern Brazil): Moved from Paraíba to Bahia by the Portuguese to fight the Aimore; their chief Zorobabe parlayed military service into slave trading and European-style status until he became too powerful and was exiled to Portugal.
- **Canelos Quichua** (Ecuadorian Amazon): A multiethnic conglomerate of intermarrying Quijos, Achuar, and Zaparoans who adopted Quichua as their common language.

### 2. The "Indian" as a New People

In the densely settled Andes, Spanish policy most intensively forged the pan-indigenous "Indian" category into a lived identity. The 1570s [[reduccion|reducción]] policy of Viceroy Toledo forced populations into European-style nucleated towns, reshuffling peoples and creating new communities of "outsiders" (*forasteros*). "The task of working within them (especially in the seventeenth century) would build a whole repertory of social habits around the originally vacuous word 'Indian.'"

Nativist movements were the cardinal events in making "Indian" a self-claimed category of solidarity rather than a mere colonial imposition:
- **Taki Unquy** (Peru, 1563): A nativist rejection of Christianity that gathered the sacred symbols of many formerly separate peoples on the same side of a cultural divide.
- **Santidade cults** (coastal Brazil, 1559–c. 1610): Parallel movement fusing Christian and indigenous prophetic idioms.

A striking example of colonial identity invention: the Ecuadorian Cañari, who had been enemies of the Inka and Spanish allies, came within half a century to think of themselves as "the progeny of the same Inka whom their great-grandparents had battled" — as Inka identity became a symbol of indigenous legitimacy after Inka political power ended.

### 3. Intra-Indigenous Mestizaje and Fusion

Colonial pressures produced not just "Spanish–Indian" mixing but the recombination of previously endogamous indigenous groups with each other and with Africans:

- **Andean urbanism**: Lima's El Cercado and Quito's satellite settlements housed large multi-ethnic Indian populations. Urban Indian women "dominated the marketplace," accumulated bicultural expertise, and entered marriages across pre-Columbian ethnic lines, producing a generic "Indian" urban identity.
- **The Piro (Ucayali)**: Tradition holds that "during the rubber boom enslavement forced them to wed each other and also Matsiguenka and mestizos so that they are now 'mixed people.'"
- **Arawak-speaking peoples of the Upper Rio Negro**: Devastated by epidemics, returned from headwater areas to lower waters "in the hope of rebuilding ranked phratries."

### 4. Afro-Indian Peoples

African–indigenous contact was pervasive but remains understudied. Colonial states feared it intensely: "Indian-black cooperation was the nightmare of colonial governments." Key formations:

- **Black Caribs (Garifuna)**: Escaped and castaway Africans fused with Island Carib populations on St. Vincent. After the Carib War of 1795–1797, ~2,000 were deported by the British to Roatan; they migrated to the Central American coast. "Phenotypically black but speaking the Arawak of the Island Caribs," they maintained cassava horticulture, polygyny, and couvade alongside African-derived ancestors ritual, music, and dance.
- **Ava-Canoeiro** (upper Tocantins/Araguaia, Brazil): "Somatically African-like speakers of a Tupian language," apparently the progeny of groups transplanted by bandeirante raiders in the eighteenth century but reinforced by escaped blacks.
- **Miskito** (Honduras/Nicaragua coast): Sumu Indians who integrated black castaways, eventually perceived as black themselves, and leveraged British alliance to expand political control. "In 1687 the British government in Jamaica recognized an indigenous 'king of Mosquitia' as an ally against the Spanish."
- **"Mulattos of Esmeraldas"** (Pacific coast, Ecuador): African survivors of a shipwreck joined with Indians; leaders Antonio de Illescas and Andrés Mangache "adopted their customs and dress, and married daughters of caciques." By 1600 some fifty mixed-bloods governed the Indians; the crown eventually had to negotiate rather than conquer.

### 5. Mission Peoples

Missions were generators of new hybrid identities. By concentrating thirty or forty different nations, promulgating Quechua or Nhengatu as lingua francas, and imposing Christian liturgy and governance, they created "synthetic, heterogeneous societies in which former ethnic identities were overshadowed by a new collective identity originally imposed from outside." Significantly, the colonial spread of Quechua caused the extinction of numerous Andean ethnic tongues (Cañari, Pukina, etc.), so that speaking Quechua became an index of "Indian-ness" — a colonial artifact now treated as ancient tradition.

### 6. White Captives and Renegades

"Going native" created a counter-flow: Europeans who became indigenous by captivity, marriage, or choice. The Araucanian (Reche/[[mapuche]]) case is best documented: from the 1540s, captured Spaniards were integrated into lineages, and by 1600 over sixty Spanish, mestizo, and mulatto renegades lived among the Reche. Captive women were particularly valued for labor (farming, brewing, weaving) and political capital (their children's divided loyalties were sometimes assets, as Spanish matrilateral kin were absent). One captive reported being sold seventy-three times in twelve years; Colonel Mansilla (1869) estimated 600–800 captives among 8,000–10,000 Ranqueles.

Renegade priests, outlaws, and disaffected colonists also joined indigenous communities, sometimes rising to leadership and sometimes enabling indigenous resistance. Juan Barba (c. 1600) was a renegade priest who "preached against Christianity and praising the Araucanian way of life."

## Geographic Spread

The process operated across all of South America but with important regional variation:

- **Andes**: Most intensive bureaucratic production of the "Indian" identity via reducción, mita, and tribute; also the zone of Taki Unquy and the Tupac Amaru rebellions.
- **Amazonia**: Colonial tribe formation (Caribs, Muras, Mundurucu); rubber-boom fusion; Hallelujah religion.
- **Guiana–Orinoco**: Carib–Arawak colonial antagonism; Black Carib formation; Garifuna diaspora.
- **Southern Cone (Chile, Argentina pampas)**: Mapuche/Araucanian captive-taking system; Ranqueles composite; Gauchesque renegades; longest continuous indigenous military resistance (380 years).
- **Ecuador–Colombia Pacific coast**: Esmeraldas mulatto state.

## End Conditions

The process did not end with colonial independence (1810s–1820s). Rubber boom, gold rushes, and boundary expeditions of the late nineteenth century continued to trigger new ethnogenetic moments, and some formations (Garifuna, Andean Quechua communities) remain vital today. Schwartz and Salomon's fundamental argument is that no named indigenous group in South America can be understood as a stable, primordial entity: "Most modern ethnic identities date not from remote prehispanic times but from the mid- or late colonial readjustments to the colonial situation."

## Historiography

This chapter functions as the volume's synthesis and historiographic argument. Key methodological claims:
1. Prehispanic society was no more stable than colonial society — ethnogenesis predates contact.
2. Colonial categories (Indian, mestizo, Carib, Arawak) were active constructions with real social consequences, not transparent descriptions.
3. Groups with strong corporate identity tended to define themselves in terms *other than* mixture — "mixed blood" was rarely the basis of powerful collective self-understanding.

Key secondary literature cited: Karen Vieira Powers, *Andean Journeys* (1995) on forastero migration; Mintz and Price on Afro-American cultural formation; Jonathan Hill and Norman Whitten, *History, Power, and Identity: Ethnogenesis in the Americas* (1995); George Urton on Andean mythohistory and invented tradition.

**Collection coverage**: Schwartz and Salomon's chapter is the collection's only volume-level synthesis on this theme. Andean material is well supported by the NCMH series and Thapar (South Asia comparative context). Afro-Indian formation is the least covered area; Brazilian and Guiana-specific archival research is not directly represented.
