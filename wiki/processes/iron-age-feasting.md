# Iron Age Feasting

## Definition and Scope

Communal drinking and feasting, particularly the regulated distribution of alcoholic beverages, were central to establishing and maintaining social relationships in Iron Age Europe and the British Isles. "Communal drinking and feasting, particularly the regulated distribution of alcoholic beverages, were central to establishing and maintaining social relationships in Iron Age Europe and the British Isles." (range 03, cache lines ~2174-2180 approx). The practice served elite marriage/inauguration rituals, sovereignty and patron-client relations, death and funerary rituals, and sacrifice/offering rituals. Alcohol functioned as a vehicle for maintaining elite prerogatives through feasting and distribution to warrior retinues and clients.

Beverages: local ale (fermented barley, short shelf life, no hops) and mead (elite, from wild honey, valuable sweetener); wine as luxury Mediterranean import before Roman viticulture. "Wine was a luxury import before the introduction by the Romans of viticulture..." "Mead was primarily an elite drink..." (range 03, cache lines ~2215-2235 approx). Northern "binge drinking" patterns contrasted with Mediterranean moderate daily wine consumption.

## Causal Drivers

- Pre-existing Neolithic and Bronze Age traditions of alcohol in social/ritual contexts continued and intensified.
- Elite status competition and hierarchy: redistribution, gift exchange, and display via rare imported or crafted drinking sets.
- Agricultural diversification and surplus storage enabling larger aggregations and provisioning of feasts.
- Increasing Mediterranean contact: from rare high-status imports (Hallstatt/Early La Tène) to mass-produced Italic wine amphorae (La Tène C/D, 2nd–1st c. BCE). Quantities of discarded amphorae (each ~25 liters) indicate commercial interaction and wider social use. "from the second century B.C. ... the dominant finds ... are Italic ... wine amphorae. The quantities ... suggest a level of commercial interaction not previously seen..." (range 03, cache lines ~3585-3595 approx, Wells entry).
- Horse and vehicle technology facilitating control and gatherings over larger areas; feasting equipment (cauldrons, flagons, cups, horns) in elite graves and settlements.

Classical sources (biased): Celts "exceedingly fond of wine," drank unmixed, to excess, leading to stupor or mania (Diodorus Siculus); beer drinking derided by Romans (Pliny, Ammianus). Propaganda value in conquest narratives noted.

## Major Phases and Geographic Spread

Early/Middle Iron Age (Hallstatt/early La Tène): drinking/feasting equipment (kraters, cups, cauldrons, horns, wagons) concentrated in rich elite burials (e.g., Vix krater and Attic cups; Hochdorf couch and cauldron; parallels at Grafenbühl). Small numbers of fine Mediterranean imports. Feasting as elite practice cementing hierarchies and obligations.

Later Iron Age (developed La Tène, oppida period): shift to mass wine imports at major settlements rather than (or in addition to) graves. Evidence of large-scale feasting from amphorae quantities + prolific animal bones. "In some cases, the numbers of amphorae, the manner of their discarding, or their association with prolific quantities of animal bones strongly suggest large-scale feasting..." (range 03, cache lines ~3595-3605 approx). Oppida and rural sanctuaries (viereckschanzen) as foci. Standardization of equipment alongside local production.

Regional: widespread in temperate Europe (Gaul, central Europe, Britain/Ireland); variations in beverage emphasis and scale. Not adopted uniformly (e.g., north European plain/Scandinavia limited coinage/related practices).

## Interaction with Other Processes

Closely intertwined with:
- Hierarchy and state formation: elites used feasting to rank individuals, reward clients, legitimize power; transition in some civitates from kingship to elected magistracy (vergobret) alongside continued rex references.
- Urbanization/oppida: imports concentrated at major centers of production and exchange; feasting lubricated new political and economic relationships.
- Trade and exchange: wine as prestige and then bulk commodity; reciprocal demand for local goods/raw materials.
- Ritual/religion: equipment in burials, votive deposits, sanctuaries; symbolic membership/exclusion.
- Agriculture and technology: surplus grain for ale; horses/vehicles for mobility and display; iron tools/plowshares enabling production scale.

Change from infrequent high-status items to mass goods of "distinctly less-elevated status" reflects broader socioeconomic shifts (range 03, cache lines ~3545-3575 approx).

## End Conditions

Roman conquest and provincial organization transformed or supplanted native practices: viticulture introduced locally; Roman wine consumption norms and infrastructure; decline of independent oppida and associated feasting economies in conquered regions. Traditional patterns persisted longer in unconquered or frontier zones (e.g., Britain until conquest; Insular medieval continuities noted in later sources). "The Roman conquest of France ... brought further changes." (range 03, coinage context).

## Historiography and Source Note

Classical texts require caution for bias and secondhand borrowing. Archaeological focus on mortuary contexts (highly visible) supplemented by settlement and sanctuary evidence. Debates on scale (elite only vs. broader participation), function (purely competitive vs. integrative), and exact beverages/rituals. Source (Bogucki & Crabtree 2004) synthesizes Iron Age Feasting (detailed entry) and related Wine/Feasting/Horses discussion (Peter S. Wells), grounded in material culture, classical accounts, and later Celtic/Germanic texts.
