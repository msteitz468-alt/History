## Definition and Scope

This page covers the millennium (roughly 111 BCE to 939 CE) during which the Red River plain — the core of what is now northern Vietnam — was administered as the southernmost province of successive Chinese empires (Han, the Six Dynasties, Sui, Tang), and the accompanying cultural process by which the population of that province was formed as a distinctively Sinic, yet not simply "Chinese," society. Following K. W. Taylor's *A History of the Vietnamese* (2013), this is explicitly **not** treated as a story of an already-existing "Vietnamese" people surviving occupation. Taylor writes that "This new understanding of the Vietnamese language as arising from a long history of Sinic bilingualism gives formative significance to the centuries of Chinese rule that is more plausible than the well-established cliché of 'a thousand years of Chinese domination' that imagines an already existing Vietnamese identity surviving many generations of participation in Sinic civilization while being fundamentally uninfluenced by it." The process is at once political (provincial administration under Han/Tang), demographic (northern immigration and intermarriage with the "great families"), and linguistic (bilingual contact between Annamese Middle Chinese and Proto-Viet-Muong).

Scope excludes: the later, post-independence relationship between an autonomous Dai Viet and Chinese dynasties (Song, Yuan, Ming), which is a related but distinct process of tributary diplomacy and periodic reconquest, not treated here.

## Causal Drivers

- **Imperial conquest and administrative absorption.** Qin Shi Huang's armies crossed into South China after 221 BCE; the region passed through Nan Yue (Zhao To) into the Han Empire when General Lu Bode conquered Nan Yue in 111 BCE, organizing Giao Chi, Cuu Chan, and Nhat Nam as prefectures. `caused_by: [[han-dynasty]]`.
- **Northern immigration.** Waves of officials, soldiers, merchants, and refugees (e.g., Han loyalists fleeing the Wang Mang disorders of 9–23 CE; émigrés fleeing the fall of northern China in 311 CE) settled permanently, intermarried with local lineages, and founded the "great families" that dominated provincial government for centuries.
- **Institutional Sinicization.** Han and later dynastic administrators built schools, promoted Confucian classical education, introduced patriarchal marriage and land-inheritance norms in place of local matrilineal/matrilocal practice, and (from Shi Xie's era) patronized Buddhist and Daoist establishments.
- **Bilingual language contact.** Taylor's central evidentiary claim: for over a millennium, speakers of an emergent regional Chinese dialect ("Annamese Middle Chinese," per linguist John Duong Phan) were concentrated around the administrative center near modern Hanoi, alongside a non-Chinese-speaking population of Proto-Viet-Muong (Mon-Khmer) speakers. Centuries of bilingualism produced heavy lexical borrowing in both directions.
- **Frontier security imperatives.** Chronic warfare with Lin Yi/Champa to the south and, later, Nan Zhao to the northwest forced repeated imperial military investment that entrenched provincial institutions (garrisons, peasant-soldier militias, fortified administrative centers) which subsequently outlasted the empires that built them.

## Major Phases

1. **Pre-imperial contact and conquest (3rd–1st c. BCE).** King An Duong's kingdom of Au-Lac, Zhao To's Nan Yue, and the Han conquest of 111 BCE established the earliest recorded northern political contact.
2. **Early Han provincial rule and the Trung Sisters revolt (111 BCE – 43 CE).** Coexistence of Han-appointed legates/prefects with hereditary Lac aristocracy, ending in [[trung-sisters-revolt-40-ce|the Trung Sisters' uprising]] and its suppression by General Ma Yuan, which archaeologically marks the replacement of Dong Son culture with Han-style brick-tomb burial.
3. **Great-family dominance (1st–6th c. CE).** Local rule by kin-based landed elites straddling northern-immigrant and local ancestry, epitomized by [[shi-xie|Shi Xie]]'s forty-year governorship of Giao Chi (r. c. 187–226) and the successive Du, Ly, and Trieu family regimes through the Jin, Liu Song, and Liang periods; recurrent frontier war with Lin Yi.
4. **Sui–Tang centralization and the Protectorate of An Nam (602–880s).** Sui conquest (602) and Tang reorganization imposed the "equal field" land-tax system and shifted the administrative center to the south bank of the Red River (modern Hanoi), formalized in 679 as the Protectorate of An Nam ("pacified south"). This period saw major Buddhist institutional growth and the peak of imperial integration.
5. **Crisis and the Nan Zhao War (850s–866).** Decades of factional tension between Red River plain elites (pro-Tang) and southern-province/upland leaders culminated in a decade-long war with the Nan Zhao kingdom of Yunnan, the sack of Dai La (863), and its costly reconquest by the Tang general Gao Pian (865), who then rebuilt and administered the protectorate for three years.
6. **Post-Tang autonomy and independence (880s–939).** Withdrawal of Tang garrisons (880) left local families (Khuc, then Duong Dinh Nghe) governing while nominally posing as officials of a vanished empire; Southern Han's brief conquest (930) ended with [[ngo-quyen|Ngo Quyen]]'s victory at the [[battle-of-bach-dang-938|Battle of Bach Dang (938)]], which is treated by Taylor as the formal close of the provincial era.

## Geographic Spread

Centered on the Red River plain (Giao Chi/Giao Province, later the Protectorate of An Nam, headquartered successively at Luy Lau and then Dai La, near modern Hanoi), with subordinate prefectures/provinces in the Ma and Ca River plains to the south (Cuu Chan) and, until its collapse in the 5th century, Nhat Nam extending toward Hai Van Pass. Han-style brick tombs are archaeologically attested as far south as the Gianh River (modern Quang Binh), which became the enduring frontier with Lin Yi/Champa after Nhat Nam's abandonment — a border zone that persisted in various forms into the 20th century (the 17th-parallel division). The process did not extend meaningfully into the southern coastal strip beyond Hai Van Pass, which remained under Cham polities.

## Interaction

This process is inseparable from, and is the immediate predecessor of, [[nam-tien|the southward expansion (Nam Tien) of Vietnamese speakers]] in later centuries, since the Ma/Ca River vs. Red River provincial rivalry established during the Han–Tang era (e.g., the Nan Zhao War, Duong Dinh Nghe vs. Kieu Cong Tien) recurs as a structural theme in subsequent Vietnamese history. It also underlies the historical claims later made by Ly- and Tran-era court historians (13th–15th c.), who constructed a parallel "southern" antiquity in response to Yuan and Ming imperial pressure — a nationalist retrojection that Taylor explicitly treats as a rhetorical, not empirical, continuity. The process is also foundational to the spread of [[buddhism]] and [[confucianism]] into the Red River plain (Shi Xie's patronage of Buddhist monks; Tang-era Thien/Chan lineages; equal-field-system Confucian schooling).

## End Conditions

Taylor attributes the end of the provincial relationship not primarily to local resistance but to a structural change in the character of Chinese imperial government itself: the Song dynasty (founded 960), unlike Han and Tang, was ruled by a scholar-official class suspicious of military men, with armies deliberately hobbled by divided command — "a new kind of empire led by a different kind of people... It lacked the martial prowess and strategic vision of the old Han-Tang aristocracy." Song's 980 invasion to reclaim An Nam after the assassination of Dinh Bo Linh failed disastrously against [[ngo-quyen|Ngo Quyen]]'s successors' lineage (Le Hoan), and the Song court's founding edict for that expedition, per Taylor, redefined the Vietnamese as outside the civilized world rather than as reclaimable subjects — a rhetorical shift from earlier dynasties' inclusive frontier ideology. With Song's diminished military capacity and narrower cultural universalism, "the people in the Red River plain began a new trajectory of cultural and political autonomy," ending the provincial era and opening the age of local Ly/Tran/Le dynasties documented on [[dai-viet]].

## Historiography

Taylor's account (2013) is explicitly anti-teleological: he rejects the "well-established cliché of 'a thousand years of Chinese domination'" and the Vietnamese nationalist historiographical tradition (traced to 13th–15th century court historians) that projects a continuous national identity backward into antiquity. His key evidentiary innovation is linguistic: drawing on the work of linguist John Duong Phan on "Annamese Middle Chinese," he argues the Vietnamese language and its speakers (the Kinh) emerged from the linguistic merger of Chinese-speaking settlers and Proto-Viet-Muong speakers only as the provincial relationship broke down in the 10th–11th centuries — meaning sinicization was constitutive of Vietnamese ethnogenesis, not something imposed upon a pre-existing people. Taylor cautions that surviving evidence (imperial administrative records, later Vietnamese historical compilations such as accounts recorded "six or seven hundred years later," and archaeology of tombs and citadels) is fragmentary and filtered through imperial or nationalist framing; he treats near-contemporary imperial voices (e.g., the administrator Xue Zong's dismissive characterization of the local population) as evidence of official attitude rather than objective description.
