# The Demographic Transition

## Definition and Scope

The **demographic transition** is the modern shift of human populations **from high
fertility and high mortality to low fertility and low mortality**, with a phase of
rapid growth in between as death rates fall before birth rates do. Massimo
Livi-Bacci, in [[mcneill-cwh-v7-2015|CWH VII]], makes it the organizing concept of
modern population history: over the three centuries to 2010 world population
increased **ten-fold (from ~0.7 to ~7 billion)** — more than three times the growth
of the previous seventeen centuries combined — at a pace "that has no parallel in the
history of mankind."

Livi-Bacci frames population change as the contest between **forces of constraint**
(environment, land, energy, disease, resources) and **forces of choice** (control
over marriage, reproduction, health, and migration). The transition is the story of
constraint weakening — the "secular poverty syndrome" of scarce resources and scarce
knowledge crumbling — and choice expanding, as the timing of marriage, reproduction,
and survival came progressively under individual control.

## Causal Drivers

- **Mortality decline came first.** Until c. 1850 there was little improvement in
  life expectancy anywhere, and most growth came from higher birth rates and the
  retreat of plague and famine. From the later nineteenth century, **sanitation and
  clean water** drove mortality down in a few (mostly Western) areas; in the
  twentieth century **direct medical intervention** mattered as never before.
- **Fertility decline followed**, beginning a few decades after mortality fell —
  and, Bashford stresses, driven heavily by **politics and private choice** rather
  than technology alone (see [[population-politics]]).
- Life expectancy roughly **doubled** (under ~35 years in 1700 to ~70 by 2010);
  per-capita energy and income each rose roughly ten-fold, so the *potential
  environmental impact* of humanity multiplied perhaps 200-fold (see
  [[anthropocene]]).

## Major Phases

Using Livi-Bacci's "isogrowth" framing (pre-modern populations clustered between 0
and 1% annual growth, with widely varying fertility/mortality combinations):

1. **Pre-transitional (to c. 1750):** high mortality, high fertility, growth normally
   well below 1%. Denmark c. 1800 (long life, ~4 children) and India c. 1900 (short
   life, ~7 children) had similar growth at very different points.
2. **The transition (19th–20th c.):** mortality falls, then fertility; growth surges.
   Europe and North America peaked in the nineteenth century; Latin America, Asia,
   and Africa in the twentieth.
3. **The post-war spike and deceleration:** global growth peaked c. 1970 at just over
   2% (the [[great-acceleration|Great Acceleration]]); by 2010 it had fallen to ~1.1%,
   with UN projections of ~0.34% by 2050 — slower than 1800 (see [[contemporary]]).

## Geographic Spread

Profoundly uneven — the defining feature in Livi-Bacci's account. Between 1700 and
2010 population multiplied six-fold in Europe, ten-fold in Asia and Africa, and
**eighty-fold in the Americas**. The world's geo-demography inverted: Europe's share
peaked at ~25% in 1900 and fell to ~11% by 2010; the Americas rose from <2% to ~14%;
Africa, after dipping, rebounded to ~15% and is now the fastest-growing region
(>2%/year), while much of Europe and East Asia has entered sub-replacement decline.

## Interaction

The transition is inseparable from [[industrialization]], the
[[fossil-fuel-energy-transition]], [[urbanization]], the
[[agricultural-transformation-modern|modern agricultural transformation]] (which fed
the growing numbers), [[disease-in-modern-history|the modern history of disease]]
(falling mortality), and [[population-politics]] (the contested management of
fertility). It is the demographic engine of the [[great-acceleration]] and the
[[anthropocene]].

## End Conditions

Ongoing and incomplete. Much of the rich world and East Asia has completed the
transition into ageing, sub-replacement decline; parts of Africa and South Asia are
still mid-transition. Livi-Bacci stresses that geographic *variability* is now at its
historical maximum.
