# Northwest Native History to the 1880s

Postcontact history of indigenous peoples of the Northwest Coast and adjacent interior plateaus from first documented Russian approach (1741) through the settlement onslaught of the mid–late nineteenth century. Primary synthesis: Robin Fisher, CHNPA I.2 ch. 11.

## Definition and Scope

Fisher frames Native experiences in the Northwest as both similar to and different from other indigenous peoples of North America: continental patterns of adaptation and dispossession apply, but local variations were distinctive and internal diversity was extreme. The long, narrow region’s common denominators were the Pacific Ocean west and mountains east; environments ranged from arid central Oregon through the wet Northwest Coast to cold Alaskan coasts and islands. In the eighteenth–nineteenth centuries Russia, Spain, Britain, and the United States each profoundly shaped Native histories.

**Central periodization (do not conflate):**
1. **Fur-trade era (~1740s–1840s):** Native control, reciprocity, *nondirected* cultural change.
2. **Settlement era (late 1830s–1880s):** competition, dispossession, *directed* change.

Attributing settlement-era outcomes to the fur trade is, Fisher argues, ahistorical. Natives long accustomed to limited-demand reciprocal traders were often ill-prepared for settler upheaval.

Geographic scope includes the [[pacific-northwest-coast]] culture area (Yakutat Bay south toward northern California), interior plateaus east of the coast ranges, and Alaska under Russian then U.S. rule (substantial Alaskan settlement delayed until the Klondike).

## Causal Drivers

- Precontact seasonal-round economies, hierarchical coastal societies, and long-distance trade (dentalium, obsidian, eulachon oil, slaves) that made Native peoples ready commercial partners.
- Sea-otter prices revealed at Canton after Cook (1778/1784 publication) and subsequent maritime rush; later land-based company systems ([[hudsons-bay-company]], Russian American Company, North West Company, Astor’s Pacific Fur Company).
- Potlatch prestige economics that incentivized surplus acquisition—Fisher’s contrast with ritual-alliance-only models applied to parts of the [[eastern-north-american-fur-trade]].
- Settler demand for scarce fertile land (river deltas, interior valleys) coinciding with Native population concentrations; “free land” (U.S.) / “waste land” (British colonies) discourse.
- Introduced disease (smallpox, malaria/intermittent fever, venereal disease, respiratory illness, etc.) as a real but uneven demographic force—see disease hedges below.

## Major Phases

### Precontact baseline

Over 12,000 years of seasonal-round adaptation from the Aleutians to Cape Mendocino and coast to interior plateaus. Harvest rights at valued resource sites were clearly understood; summers dispersed for hunting/gathering, winters concentrated in larger villages for ritual. Extended family was the basic social unit; coastal societies hierarchical (birth, wealth, ability as leadership attributes, yet consent-based). Greater cultural and linguistic diversity than any other region of North America—dozens of mutually exclusive languages across most major Native language families. Coast vs. interior was the fundamental cultural divide: maritime abundance and elaborate art/ceremony on the coast; smaller, more mobile, looser social organization east of the mountains (salmon plus land mammals; southern Plateau camas/wild turnip; pit houses in winter valleys).

Key peoples: [[tlingit]], [[haida]], [[kwakwaka-wakw]], Coast and Interior [[salish]], [[chinook]], Plateau Sahaptian groups including [[nez-perce]], plus Nuu-chah-nulth (whale hunters), Tsimshian, and many others.

### Early contact and maritime fur trade (1741–~1800)

- **1741:** Vitus Bering enters Gulf of Alaska; sea-otter pelts fetch high prices in northern China. Promyshlenniki push east; Alaska/Aleutian Natives first drawn into trade—Russian three-decade lead.
- **1774:** Juan Pérez meets [[haida]] off Langara Island—Spanish clothes/beads/knives for sea otter and craft goods.
- **1778:** Cook at Nootka Sound (Yuquot)—first extended middle-coast encounter; Canton prices trigger commercial boom after 1784 publication. *Sea Otter* (Hanna) 1785; by early 1790s up to ~21 vessels/summer season; initially British triangular trade, then mostly Boston Americans.
- **1792–93:** Vancouver surveys; Mackenzie “from Canada, by land” at Elcho Harbour—sea and land European connections joined for Native peoples.
- **1799:** Russian American Company; New Archangel (Sitka) in [[tlingit]] territory—by 1800 three-quarters of company sea otter from Sitka; capital 1808.

### Land-based company trade and Native control (~1805–1840s)

Simon Fraser (McLeod Lake 1805); David Thompson to Columbia mouth 1811; Lewis and Clark 1805; Astoria / Fort George. Only after NWC–[[hudsons-bay-company]] amalgamation **1821** were western fur resources systematically exploited under Simpson and John McLoughlin (Fort Vancouver). Maritime vs land-based logistics differed, but **Native role was much the same**: villages as trading nodes, ceremonial negotiation, middlemen (e.g. [[chinook]] at the Dalles; Maquinna at Yuquot; Kotlean of Sitka; Legaic’s Fort Simpson “home guard”), price inflation, quality discrimination of goods. HBC failed to impose monopoly pricing until a semblance of control in the **1840s**; competition 1790s–1840s let Indians play traders against each other. Snake Country expeditions laid SE wastes of fur against American mountain men; coast remained open to American and Russian alternatives (McLoughlin’s three price levels).

Fisher’s reciprocity thesis: mutual dependence, not unilateral Native dependence; fort “conquest” claims later used in land litigation were politics not history. Firearms did not tip the balance of power; no necessary massive warfare increase **south of Russian territory**. Cultural florescence thesis: more frequent/elaborate potlatch and totem poles (poles already present at contact—Barbeau wrong on origin) with new wealth and metal tools.

### Settlement transition (late 1830s–1880s)

Oregon Trail to Willamette; Fort Victoria 1843; **Oregon Treaty 1846** (49th parallel)—Douglas’s “monstrous treaty.” U.S.: territorial Oregon 1848, Washington 1853, Oregon statehood 1859. Canada: Vancouver Island colony 1849; Fraser gold rush 1858 → mainland BC colony; amalgamation 1866; BC province 1871. Alaska purchase 1867; substantial settlers only with Klondike.

Settler attitudes replaced fur-trader partnership imagery with hostile-savage generalizations. By 1880 ~250,000 settlers in Oregon/Washington; BC Indians still a majority in 1880 but settlers ~25,000→70,000 next decade.

**Conflict chronology (selected):** Whitman Mission attack (Cayuse, Nov 1847)—end of fur-trade mediation frontier; Rogue River / southern Oregon devastation; Washington wars after Walla Walla 1855 (Kamiakin, Leschi); [[nez-perce]] war 1877 (Wallowa dispossession, fighting retreat to Bear Paw, surrender ~50 km short of Canada); Chilcotin War 1864.

### Canada / United States comparison (Fisher’s core analytical thread)

| Dimension | South of 49th (U.S.) | North of 49th (BC/Canada) |
|---|---|---|
| Settlement timing | Earlier; government follows settlers | 10–15 year lag; early VI settlement under fur-trader governors |
| Land policy | Wholesale, after-the-fact treaties; removal/amalgamation logic; Stevens councils often 1–2 days, Chinook jargon for complex provisions, unratified while settlement encouraged | Douglas piecemeal, ahead of settlement where possible; small reserves at traditional sites; Songhee and few Island treaties, then **treaty-making discontinued for lack of funds**—most of BC never extinguished by treaty |
| Force | Volunteer warfare, governor–Army conflict, mass punitive campaigns | Top-down control; selective punishment of individuals; Royal Navy gunboat diplomacy; fewer “Indian wars” (myth of peaceful BC should not be pushed too far) |
| Post-Douglas | — | Trutch rollback after 1864: 10-acre/family minimum under Douglas becomes **maximum**; minimal rights recognition prevails after Confederation despite Ottawa’s formal Indians/land responsibility |
| Late policy | [[dawes-act-1887]] allotment—~3 million acres from NW reserves into European hands within ~30 years | 1884 potlatch ban (Indian Act)—hard to enforce; driven underground; revival after 1951 repeal shows survival |
| Demography (settlement era) | Steeper decline (WA/OR estimates 1860 ~31k+7k → 1890 ~11.2k+5.0k = 16,152) | BC 1881–1901 ~25,661→25,488—birth rate compensating despite high death rate (with caution) |

**Synthesis (R03-59):** Techniques of dispossession differed; end result the same—insufficient land for livelihood by the 1880s. Nez Perce and Kamiakin expectations that Canada would be categorically different were only partly true: “distinctions more apparent than real.”

### Resource dispossession and economic marginality

Placer mining after 1858 damaged Fraser salmon runs (hardship/starvation); fencing/grazing ended hunting and berrying; arid interior water conflicts intense; commercial canning from late 1860s—“when the tide was out the table was laid” no longer exclusive. Capable agricultural transitions blocked by land denial (e.g. two bands won 1876 Centennial wheat prizes). Wage labor (packing, railways, canning, sealing, logging, hops) was short-lived, last-hired/first-laid-off cheap labor—not equivalent to determining fur-trade role (Fisher vs Knight’s more optimistic wage-labor thesis).

### Mission and religious adaptation

Fur-trade missionaries (except Russian Orthodox among disrupted Aleuts) had limited success while Natives retained selectivity. Settlement upheaval made Christianity more attractive. Duncan’s Metlakatla as famous for “success” as Whitmans for failure—then faction and 1887 New Metlakatla (Alaska). Prophet cults (Smohalla Dreamers); Indian Shaker Church (John Slocum, Squaxin, 1881)—syncretic, more permanent.

## Disease impact (magnitude hedges preserved)

Introduced diseases included smallpox, venereal disease, malaria/intermittent fever, respiratory illness, TB, influenza, dysentery, whooping cough, measles. Smallpox probably arrived on the NW Coast in the **1770s**, with further outbreaks (central coast 1801, north mid-1830s, Washington coast 1853, BC coast **1862**)—**dispersed in time and place, not continuously pervasive**. Intermittent fever (likely malaria) from **1830** devastated lower Columbia groups including [[chinook]]; one student suggests **80–90%** drop by ~1840—became endemic/debilitating unlike pure epidemics.

Fisher’s historiographic caution: disease caused significant fluctuations and some groups considerable depopulation, but **microbes were not the sole mortality factor**—type of European presence and Native response mattered (Russian system more disruptive for Aleuts—perhaps two-thirds decline by end of 18th c.; Chinooks depleted as trade focal point). Precontact NW Coast population estimates (e.g. ~200,000) are **at best well-informed guesses**—a “numbers game” on pliable evidence. Deserted villages often reflect seasonal mobility misread as decline. Literature over-focuses death rates over birth rates/regeneration. HBC inoculation from 1830s—effect unknown. **Decline was real, but precise extent requires more caution** than worst-case rhetoric (“camps into graveyards”; claims that 1862 cut population by one-third should be treated carefully; differential severity; generational immunity; thousands vaccinated in BC by 1860s).

The March **1862** Victoria smallpox outbreak: infected northern visitors driven from town by frightened citizens, carrying disease up the coast at least to the Queen Charlottes.

## Geographic Spread

- **Coast:** Alaska panhandle ([[tlingit]]) through Haida Gwaii ([[haida]]), middle BC islands/inlets ([[kwakwaka-wakw]]), lower BC/Vancouver Island and Washington ([[salish]]), lower Columbia ([[chinook]]), Oregon coast, north California.
- **Interior:** Plateau (Sahaptian including [[nez-perce]], Interior Salish, Kootenay); Columbia basin; Fraser–Thompson–Shuswap corridors.
- **Imperial overlays:** Russian America; Spanish San Blas expeditions; British HBC/NWC; American Oregon Country / territories; post-1846 international boundary.

## End Conditions

By the 1880s (except far north still relatively isolated), most Northwest Native peoples were dispossessed of “land that was life,” numbers under pressure, cultures eroded under missionary/agent assault, protest ignored, confined to isolated reserves/reservations. Yet **neither peoples nor cultures disappeared**: late nineteenth century was **survival under pressure**—defensive conservation, major lifestyle change without assimilation, waiting for future assertion. Cultures endured and adapted.

## Historiography

Literature shows overwhelming **coastal fascination**; virtually none written by Native people at Fisher’s writing. Anthropologists (from Boas) dominated; historians latecomers. *Handbook of North American Indians* vol. 7 (Northwest Coast) confirms coast concentration and tribal approach. BC better served for general postcontact histories (Duff; Fisher *Contact and Conflict*) than Oregon/Washington/Alaska surveys; particular-group histories stronger south of the border (e.g. Josephy on Nez Perce). Canadian historians attend fur trade more than American. Few Canada–U.S. comparative studies (Fisher’s 1981 warfare comparison an exception). Cultural-florescence vs “fatal impact” (Howay) and demographic “numbers game” (Cook estimates, Boyd demography, Gibson inconsistencies) remain live debates—Fisher’s chapter is explicitly revisionist-cautious on disease magnitude and anti-teleological on fur trade → settlement.
