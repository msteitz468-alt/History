## Definition and Scope

Irish nationalism is the political movement claiming that the Irish people constitute a distinct nation entitled to self-government. As a modern political ideology it emerged in the revolutionary 1790s, achieved mass form in the second half of the nineteenth century, and produced the partitioned settlement of 1921–2 from which both [[irish-free-state]] and [[northern-ireland]] derived.

Irish nationalism is analytically distinct from [[irish-patriotism]], the earlier political culture of the Protestant settler elite that defended sectional corporate privileges. Nationalism in the modern sense rests on the principle that the inhabitants of a territory—construed as a people or nation—are the legitimate source of political authority. Irish nationalism also differs from Jacobitism, the pre-1750 Catholic loyalty to the exiled Stuarts, which was dynastic and confessional rather than national in form.

The process encompasses multiple, sometimes antagonistic, strands: constitutional nationalism (O'Connell, the Home Rule movement, Redmond), physical-force or separatist republicanism (United Irishmen, Fenians, IRB, Sinn Féin, IRA), and cultural nationalism (the Gaelic League, GAA, Irish Ireland). These strands interacted, competed, and periodically fused under crisis conditions.

## Causal Drivers

**American and French Revolutions**: The ideological framework of popular sovereignty and the nation as the unit of legitimate government entered Ireland through the United Irishmen (founded 1791). Theobald Wolfe Tone's resolutions called for "an equal representation of all the people in parliament" to replace the English-servant system of Irish governance.

**Catholic political mobilization**: The mass agitation led by Daniel O'Connell for Catholic Emancipation (achieved 1829) created the organizational and cultural infrastructure of modern Irish nationalism—the Catholic Association, the "Catholic rent," clerical participation in politics. Religion became, as Sean Connolly argues, the functional equivalent of language in European nationalism: "on the one hand as a badge of identity; on the other, as a frustrating check on the aspirations of those middle- and lower-middle-class groups... that formed the backbone of nationalist movements everywhere" (Ch. 2, [[jackson-oxford-modern-irish-history-2014]]).

**[[irish-land-question]]**: The nexus between agrarian and national grievances was especially powerful. The Land League (1879) fused the land struggle with Home Rule agitation under Parnell's leadership; as Philip Bull argued, "the issue became so important in national life that it shaped the future of Irish nationalism and the shape of the society which emerged out of the nationalist struggle, creating between the issues of land and nationalism a nexus which was so strong that the one issue became effectively a metaphor for the other" (quoted in Ch. 6, [[jackson-oxford-modern-irish-history-2014]]).

**[[great-famine-ireland-1845]]**: The catastrophe of 1845–52 was interpreted by nationalists as evidence that British rule was incompatible with Irish welfare. It accelerated emigration (see [[irish-diaspora]]) which created overseas populations with both grievances and resources to sustain nationalist politics transnationally.

**Diasporic resources**: [[irish-diaspora]] communities—especially in the United States—provided funding, organizational networks, and political pressure that sustained the nationalist movement through its most difficult phases. Fenian bombing campaigns in Britain and American Clan na Gael financing of the Land League exemplify this dynamic.

## Major Phases

### United Irishmen and Republican Origins (1791–1803)
The Society of United Irishmen, founded by Tone and others in Belfast and Dublin, initially sought constitutional reform but by 1795 committed to an independent Irish republic through French alliance. Their 1798 rebellion—brutally suppressed—established the template of armed separatism while revealing the difficulty of transcending sectarian divisions. The fusion of republican ideology with Catholic grievance in the Defender movement prefigured later nationalist syntheses.

### O'Connellite Constitutional Nationalism (1820s–1840s)
O'Connell developed mass constitutional politics—the mobilization of the Catholic electorate through the Church as organizational infrastructure, the appeal to British liberal opinion, the goal of Repeal of the [[act-of-union-1801]]. He was, as Connolly notes, "fully aware that any feasible modification of the union would involve some much more limited devolution of powers" while presenting repeal as restoration of lost status—a characteristic nationalist ambiguity between rhetoric and achievable aims. Young Ireland's failed rising of 1848 represented the physical-force alternative under Connell's movement's shadow.

### Fenianism and the New Departure (1858–1882)
The Irish Republican Brotherhood (IRB/Fenians), founded 1858, created a permanent revolutionary underground. Though their 1867 rising failed, the Fenian martyrs entered nationalist mythology, demonstrating how physical-force defeat could generate more popular support than victory. The "New Departure" of 1879–82 fused Fenianism, constitutionalism, and agrarian radicalism under the catalysis of economic crisis, producing the Land League—the most successful nationalist mass organization of the century.

### Home Rule Movement and Parnellism (1870s–1891)
The Home Rule League (1870), dominated after 1880 by Charles Stewart Parnell, achieved electoral dominance and linked land reform to constitutional self-government. Gladstone's conversion to Home Rule in 1886 transformed British politics but also precipitated the organization of Ulster Unionism (see [[ulster-unionism]]). Parnell's mastery lay in combining militant rhetoric with constitutional means—"he declined to set a boundary to the march of a nation" while never specifying whether Home Rule was final or transitional. His political destruction in 1890–1 fractured the movement for a decade.

### Cultural Nationalism and the Revival (1890s–1916)
The Gaelic Athletic Association (founded 1884) and Gaelic League (1893) created mass cultural institutions expressing Irish identity in contradistinction to British norms. As Connolly notes, this was Hobsbawm and Ranger's "invention of tradition"—the Gaelic League's idealization of the Irish-speaking western seaboard was as much an act of "imaginative appropriation" as the earlier Protestant appropriation of Gaelic antiquity. The Easter Rising of 1916—a deliberate symbolic act by a minority convinced that the Home Rule era had made comfortable ambiguity permanent—transformed the political landscape through executions that turned defeat into martyrdom.

### Revolutionary Settlement and Partition (1916–1923)
Sinn Féin's electoral triumph in 1918 destroyed the Irish Parliamentary Party but the movement's strategy remained focused on the Paris Peace Conference rather than renewed insurrection. The IRA's War of Independence (1919–21), led by a militant minority, forced British negotiation. The resulting [[act-of-union-1801|Anglo-Irish Treaty]] of 1921 created dominion status—a "half-way house"—which was then dismantled by Fianna Fáil's studied ambiguity ("a slightly constitutional party" in the words of Seán Lemass, four years before taking power in 1932). Partition separated nationalist Ireland from [[northern-ireland]] where a Catholic/nationalist minority of roughly one-third was incorporated into a Unionist-dominated state.

### Post-Independence Trajectories (1923–present)
Southern nationalism, institutionalized in the Free State and Republic, lost its primary political dynamic as formal independence was achieved. The "Northern Troubles" (1969–98) reactivated republican militancy through the Provisional IRA; the Good Friday Agreement (1998) and St Andrews Agreement (2006) represented a peace settlement that implicitly acknowledged the legitimacy of both nationalist and Unionist identities within [[northern-ireland]].

## Geographic Spread

Irish nationalism had an inherently transnational character from the outset. The [[irish-diaspora]] in the United States, Canada, Australia, and Britain constituted a parallel political arena—funding, morale, and diplomatic pressure flowed from overseas communities to the domestic movement. The "green Atlantic" (Kevin Whelan's term) of the 1790s linked United Irishmen in Ireland to American revolutionary networks. Fenianism was explicitly transnational in organization. In the twentieth century, Irish-American lobbying shaped British policy, most consequentially in the 1990s under President Clinton.

## Historiography

The historiography of Irish nationalism was shaped by the revisionist controversy of the 1980s–1990s. The IHS reform tradition (T. W. Moody, R. Dudley Edwards) sought to de-mythologize nationalist history and produce a "scientific" scholarship. Critics from Brendan Bradshaw onward argued that revisionism had become subtly anti-national and distorted by the shadow of the Northern Troubles. The peace settlement of 1998 partly released this tension, allowing a more self-confident appraisal of nationalism.

Key historiographical frameworks include: Benedict Anderson's "imagined community" model; Eric Hobsbawm's social history of nationalism (applied critically by Connolly); post-colonial theory (Seamus Deane, Declan Kiberd, David Lloyd); and revisionist political history (Roy Foster, Paul Bew).

The debate over origins—whether nationalism was essentially constructed in the revolutionary era (Anderson, Hobsbawm) or drew on deeper ethnic solidarities (Adrian Hastings, A. D. Smith)—applies acutely to Ireland, where both perspectives find evidence. Richard English's *Irish Freedom* (2006) offers the most comprehensive recent synthesis.
