## Definition and Scope

Home rule was a demand for devolved self-government for Ireland within the United Kingdom — not secession, not the restoration of a parallel Irish parliament under the Crown as O'Connellite repeal envisaged, but a subordinate Irish legislature with competence over domestic affairs while Westminster retained control over defence, foreign policy, and commerce. As Matthew Kelly writes in [[jackson-oxford-modern-irish-history-2014]], 'Convinced home rulers did not seek Ireland's secession from the union or complete separation... Home rule represented a more modest form of Irish self-government than Fenian separatism or O'Connellite repeal.' That modesty was simultaneously its greatest political asset and its most dangerous liability: orthodox home rulers genuinely believed devolution within empire was the best possible outcome for Ireland; separatists and unionists alike doubted the distinction between home rule and independence could be sustained in practice.

The movement dominated Irish politics from the early 1870s until 1918, when Sinn Féin displaced the Irish Parliamentary Party, and it was the defining question in British politics in 1886, 1893, and 1912–14.

## Causal Drivers

The immediate organizational trigger was the failure of Fenianism to achieve an Irish republic by force and the subsequent calculation by Irish Republican Brotherhood leaders that constitutional agitation might serve republican ends. Isaac Butt, a Protestant Tory who had defended Fenians in court, gave the demand its first coherent articulation in the early 1870s, framing home rule in terms designed to appeal to both Protestant opinion and British liberal sentiment — a conservative patrician politics intended to restore 'traditional social hierarchies' while pre-empting more radical agitation.

Deeper structural drivers included:

- **The Act of Union** (1801): created direct rule via Dublin Castle — a 'peculiar form of British government' that was 'emphatically a form of imperial government, maintained by a mixture of state patronage and force' — which many Irish people across the political spectrum found both ineffective and illegitimate. See [[act-of-union-1801]].
- **The Great Famine** and its political aftermath: mass death and emigration destroyed O'Connellite constitutional nationalism as an organized force, leaving the land question and the question of self-government to be reintegrated by a new generation. See [[great-famine-ireland-1845]].
- **The land question**: the alignment of agrarian agitation with parliamentary nationalism under Parnell from 1879 gave home rule a mass rural base it had lacked under Butt. See [[irish-land-war]] and [[irish-land-question]].
- **The Irish diaspora**: especially Irish-American republican organizations (Clan na Gael) provided crucial funding and ideological pressure, particularly through John Devoy's 'New Departure' telegram of October 1878, which offered republican cooperation on conditions including vigorous land agitation and an 'aggressive policy' at Westminster. See [[irish-diaspora]].
- **British Liberal politics**: Gladstone's conversion to home rule in 1885–6 gave the demand a British sponsor and made it the organizing question of late Victorian party politics. The 'deeper rhythms of liberal politics' — a belief that home rule was the only genuinely democratic alternative to permanent coercion — sustained the Liberal-Nationalist alliance through three failed bills.

## Major Phases

### 1870–1879: Butt's Moderate Phase
Isaac Butt built the Home Rule League as a parliamentary association of gentlemen MPs, relying on obstruction of parliamentary business (associated with Joseph Biggar and later Parnell) to raise the issue's profile. The movement secured 59 seats in 1874 but lacked the mass organization and financial base to compel attention at Westminster. The IRB Supreme Council agreed in the early 1870s to suspend revolutionary activity to test home rule's efficacy, then withdrew cooperation in 1877 when convinced neither condition was met.

### 1879–1891: Parnellite Dominance
[[charles-stewart-parnell]]'s assumption of effective leadership after 1879 transformed home rule into a mass movement by aligning it with the [[irish-land-war]]. Parnell built a coalition holding Fenian separatists, the Catholic Church, tenant farmers, and urban radicals — a coalition sustained by his deliberate ambiguity ('no man has the right to fix the boundary to the march of a nation'). The Irish Parliamentary Party became a disciplined bloc at Westminster capable of holding the balance of power.

Gladstone introduced the first Home Rule Bill in April 1886; it was defeated in the Commons, splitting the Liberal Party and driving Liberal Unionists (including Chamberlain) into coalition with the Conservatives. The second bill passed the Commons in 1893 but was rejected by the Lords. Parnell's exposure as an adulterer in 1890 destroyed his political authority: Gladstone and the Catholic Church both withdrew support, and Parnell died in October 1891 having led a minority of loyalists in a nine-month battle that split the nationalist movement.

### 1891–1910: Redmondite Reconstruction
John Redmond reunited parnellites and anti-parnellites in 1900, but the movement languished during a decade of Conservative government. William O'Brien's United Irish League and the Ancient Order of Hibernians provided grass-roots organization; separatists built the 1798 centenary movement, opposed the Boer War, and launched Sinn Féin (Arthur Griffith, 1905) as an alternative to parliamentary politics. Sinn Féin's 'dual monarchism' — withdrawal from Westminster and assertion of constitutional rights under the 1782 arrangement — attracted limited electoral support (a 'clutch of local government seats won in 1907–8 and lost by 1912') but articulated separatist sentiment without Fenian republicanism's organizational demands.

### 1910–1914: Third Bill and Ulster Crisis
The 1909 constitutional crisis — Lloyd George's budget, the Lords' veto, the Parliament Act of 1911 — removed the principal obstacle to home rule legislation. With the IPP holding the balance of power after two 1910 elections, Asquith committed to a third bill, introduced in April 1912. The removal of the Lords' veto made Ulster Unionist resistance the decisive obstacle: 471,414 men and women signed the Ulster Covenant in September 1912 pledging to resist home rule by force; the Ulster Volunteer Force was established in January 1913 with 85,000 men. The Irish Volunteers, founded in November 1913, were a nationalist response. The 'Curragh Mutiny' (March 1914) showed the army could not be relied upon to enforce home rule in Ulster. War broke out in August 1914 before a settlement was reached; the Home Rule Act was passed with a suspending motion, aiming to implement it after the European conflict.

### 1914–1918: Wartime Collapse
Redmond committed the Irish National Volunteers (some 180,000 strong) to the British war effort at Woodenbridge in September 1914 — a gesture that split the movement when only 10,000 separatists under Eoin MacNeill refused. The 1916 Easter Rising, conscription crisis of 1918, and the December 1918 general election destroyed the IPP: Sinn Féin won 73 seats, abstained from Westminster, and established Dáil Éireann in Dublin. See [[easter-rising-1916]] and [[irish-war-of-independence-1919]].

## Geographic Spread

Home rule mobilized all four provinces of Ireland, but its social geography was uneven. It was strongest in Catholic nationalist Ireland — Connacht, Munster, and Leinster — and weakest in Protestant Ulster, where it faced organized Unionist opposition rooted in fears of 'Rome rule', economic disadvantage, and the stripping of what unionists considered constitutional rights guaranteed by the Act of Union. The movement's British dimension was concentrated in the Liberal Party and the Irish diaspora in Britain and North America.

## Interaction

Home rule interacted in complex ways with:
- **Fenianism**: repeatedly absorbed IRB members while being shaped by republican pressure; produced the ambiguity that made the coalition possible and the ideology unstable
- **The land question**: the [[irish-land-war]] gave home rule its mass base and the social power to compel British attention, but also pushed the movement toward rhetoric and tactics that alarmed British opinion
- **Ulster Unionism**: the movement's inability to accommodate Ulster's concentrated Protestant population was its fatal structural weakness; the Ulster Crisis of 1912–14 exposed it
- **British Liberalism**: the Gladstonian alliance was both the movement's greatest political asset and a constraint — Liberal support meant calibrating Irish self-government to British liberal ideas of Ireland's 'fitness to govern'
- **The Irish diaspora**: American funding and republican pressure sustained the movement financially and ideologically; see [[irish-diaspora]]

## End Conditions

Home rule as a constitutional program effectively ended with the 1918 election. The [[irish-free-state]], established by the Anglo-Irish Treaty of 1921, was a dominion settlement, not a home rule settlement; Northern Ireland received its own form of home rule under the 1920 Government of Ireland Act. The bitter irony, as Kelly notes, was that 'home rule did not deliver Ireland over to Rome but Northern Ireland over to Protestant rule' — the very minority-protection concern unionists had raised against home rule was vindicated not in the south but in the north.

## Historiography

Home rule historiography is extensive. Foundational works include F.S.L. Lyons's account of Parnell and the Irish Parliamentary Party; A.B. Cooke and John Vincent's controversial argument (1974) that Gladstone intended home rule to fail while using it as cover for land purchase; and the 'liberal idealist' school of J.P. Parry and Eugenio Biagini, who situate Gladstone's commitment within the 'deeper rhythms of liberal politics' and an 'elective affinity' between British liberalism and Irish nationalism. Dicey's contemporary *England's Case Against Home Rule* (1886) remains the sharpest statement of Unionist constitutional theory. Recent work (McConnel, Bew, Kelly) has recovered the IPP's organizational complexity and internal tensions, including the Redmondite-Fenian nexus. The collection in [[jackson-oxford-modern-irish-history-2014]] (ch. 29, Matthew Kelly) is the basis for this page.
