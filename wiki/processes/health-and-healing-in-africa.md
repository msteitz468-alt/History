## Definition and Scope

"Health and healing" as a named sub-field in African historiography emerged only in the late 1970s, when Steven Feierman and John Janzen began steering the history of medicine in Africa away from a focus on disease epidemics toward vernacular healing practice and the politics of health. The field has since expanded to encompass: indigenous therapeutic systems (ngoma, divination, anti-sorcery movements, spirit possession); colonial medicine and its critics; psychiatric history; reproductive health; the role of nurses, midwives, and healers as historical actors; the impact of structural adjustment and AIDS on health systems; and the new science-and-technology-studies (STS) turn in medical anthropology and history.

The distinctive contribution of the field is its insistence that health and illness are irreducibly social and political, not merely biological. This page documents the historical process by which African therapeutic systems, colonial biomedicine, and postcolonial health regimes have interacted, competed, and transformed each other. It should be read alongside [[disease-in-modern-history]] (the broader global concept page).

## Causal Drivers

**Vernacular therapeutic systems** are the deep foundation. Across sub-Saharan Africa, healing was embedded in social and political life rather than constituting a separate domain. Steven Feierman's work on the Shambaa kingdom of Tanzania shows that speech about "healing and harming the land" was the primary discourse of good and bad leadership. The same language resonated in understandings of fertility, prosperity, and belonging. Victor Turner introduced the term "drums of affliction" (ngoma — literally "drum") to describe healing through dance and drumming within therapeutic associations. John Janzen demonstrated that Lemba, a healing association active across the Congo basin from at least the seventeenth century, functioned as both a polity (controlling trade routes) and a therapy management system. Patients in the lower Congo rarely made individual healthcare decisions: kin-based "therapy management groups" diagnosed causes (social/human-caused vs. natural/God-caused) and directed treatment.

**Healing and harming** are inseparable in African therapeutic cosmologies. The same nkisi medicine object (now ubiquitous in Western art galleries as "African art") could heal or harm; each nail hammered into a nkondi figure signified a diagnostic encounter. Anti-sorcery movements, prophetic healers, and spirit-possession rituals all combined therapeutic and political dimensions. Feierman's concept of "public healing" captures this: movements conventionally labelled "religious" — from Nyabingi spirit mediums in the Great Lakes to the Maji Maji rebellion in German East Africa — are more accurately therapeutic-political responses to threats to social reproduction and collective health. Colonial regimes attempted to suppress sorcery legally, driving anti-sorcery healing underground and producing the realm of "not-knowing" theorised by Murray Last.

**Colonial medicine** arrived with missionaries and conquest and was never ideologically neutral. Frantz Fanon's essay "Medicine and Colonialism" (1965) was the searing indictment. Megan Vaughan's *Curing their Ills* (1991) brought a Foucauldian discursive analysis: colonial medical texts shaped less individual African subjects than the reified "tribes" of indirect rule. Biomedical practice was inseparable from evangelical mission: surgery, tooth-pulling, soap, and injections were tools of religious conversion as well as healthcare. Wulf Sachs's *Black Hamlet* (1937) — a South African psychoanalyst placing a Rhodesian healer on his couch in Johannesburg — is a primary source of unusual intimacy, revealing both the healer's world and the contradictions of the colonial therapeutic encounter.

**Political economy and the "social costs of production"** were the axis of medical history in southern Africa. Shula Marks's work on Apartheid, disease, and health in South Africa (with Neil Andersson) pioneered a class-race-gender approach; Randall Packard's *White Plague, Black Labor* traced tuberculosis and mining labour reserves. The concept of "structural violence" (replacing "social costs of production" in more recent literature) captures the systemic damage inflicted on African health by capitalist labour systems, colonialism, and postcolonial neo-liberalism.

**Gendered health interventions** were central to colonial biopolitics. Colonial states intervened in African fertility — sometimes to increase it (where practices like genital cutting were deemed to reduce the labour supply), sometimes to restrict it (in white settler colonies fearing demographic competition). Lynn Thomas's *Politics of the Womb* documents the contradictory and politically explosive British interventions in female circumcision in Kenya. Janice Boddy's study of the colonial midwifery programme in Sudan — aimed at modifying rather than abolishing genital cutting — uses interstitial chapters of possessed women's speech to remind readers that another register of meaning was always at play.

**Structural adjustment and AIDS** (1980s–present) constitute the most recent and devastating conjuncture. Neo-liberal privatisation of health services ("cash and carry" payments) converted state-of-the-art independence-era hospitals into "near morgues" (Thierry Michel's documentary *Donka* shows this for Guinea). AIDS arrived amid this collapse. Vinh-Kim Nguyen's concept of "therapeutic citizenship" captures how AIDS treatment programmes in Abidjan created new subject positions for patients navigating between NGOs, clinical trials, and traditional healers.

## Major Phases

**Phase 1 — Pre-Colonial Healing Systems (before c. 1850).** Ngoma associations, Lemba, spirit mediums, herbalists, and anti-sorcery specialists as the primary therapeutic infrastructure. Janzen traces Lemba's origins to at least 1650 in the lower Congo; Feierman traces public healing to precolonial Shambaa politics; Neil Kodesh extends public healing frameworks to pre-colonial Buganda. Deep time for African healing history is underdeveloped relative to the colonial period.

**Phase 2 — Mission Medicine and Colonial Public Health (c. 1850–1945).** The first generation of professional African doctors trained in Western medicine from the 1860s (Adell Patton). E. E. Evans-Pritchard's *Witchcraft, Oracles and Magic among the Azande* (1937) — a foundational colonial anthropological text. Victor Turner on Ndembu ngoma (1950s). Margaret Field on anti-witchcraft practices in the Gold Coast as psychic therapy. Colonial states deploy medicine as a technology of order: sleeping sickness campaigns in German East Africa; maternity care programmes; missionary dental and surgical evangelism.

**Phase 3 — Critical History of Colonial Medicine (1970s–1990s).** Feierman and Janzen establish the "health and healing" sub-field (1979). Emphasis shifts to vernacular therapeutics, patient experience, and social critique. Political economy approach in South Africa (Marks, Packard). Megan Vaughan's *Curing their Ills* (1991) brings the linguistic turn. Shula Marks's *Not Either an Experimental Doll* (1988) — reading the letters of Xhosa schoolgirl Lily Moya, whose mental breakdown illuminates schooling, violence, colonial paternalism, and psychiatric institutionalisation.

**Phase 4 — Ethnographic History and Gender Moves (1990s–2000s).** Nancy Rose Hunt's *A Colonial Lexicon* (1999) on missionary medicine, nursing, and reproduction in the Belgian Congo introduces "middle figures" and the notion of postcolonial situations as "debris." Julie Livingston on debility, chronic illness, and aging in Botswana; Geissler and Prince on AIDS and infant illness in Kenya; Stacey Langwick on the materiality of vernacular healing and malaria in Tanzania. Psychiatry receives sustained attention: Jonathan Sadowsky on colonial and postcolonial Nigeria; Shula Marks and Megan Vaughan on southern African asylums.

**Phase 5 — STS Turn and Global Health (2000s–present).** Science-and-technology studies vocabulary (networks, experimentality, technopolitics, pharmaceuticals as global objects) reshapes the field. Gabrielle Hecht on uranium miners and technopolitics in Namibia. P. Wenzel Geissler and Catherine Molyneux, eds., *Evidence, Ethos and Experiment* (2011). Africa as site of "global health" endeavours — clinical trials, medical education, NGO humanitarian medicine — alongside widespread structural violence. Claire Wendland's concept of "clinical tourism" captures the contradictions of Western medical students seeking redemptive training in Malawi.

## Geographic Spread

- **Central Africa (Lower Congo / Belgian Congo)**: Lemba healing association; ngoma; nkisi objects; Nancy Rose Hunt's *Colonial Lexicon* centred here.
- **East Africa (Tanzania, Kenya, Uganda)**: Feierman's Shambaa kingdom work; Janzen on Great Lakes; Kodesh on Buganda public healing; Geissler and Prince in Kenya; Langwick in Tanzania.
- **Southern Africa (South Africa, Zimbabwe, Botswana, Zambia)**: The richest historiography, driven by Apartheid's extreme medicalisation of racial domination. Marks, Packard, Livingston, Karen Flint's *Healing Traditions* (tracing back to 1820s Zulu royal healing).
- **West Africa**: Relatively underrepresented because West Africa falls outside the Bantu proto-lexicon that undergirded the foundational ngoma scholarship. Murray Last on Muslim healing in Hausa northern Nigeria; Eric Silla on leprosy in Mali; Elisha Renne on polio in northern Nigeria.
- **North Africa**: Largely addressed through psychiatry (Fanon and Algeria; colonial psychiatry in French North Africa). Janice Boddy on Sudan.
- **Atlantic diaspora connection**: Historians of the African Atlantic diaspora have extended healing frameworks to enslaved Africans — tracking African surgical practices on slave ships, therapeutic traditions on American plantations (Sharla Fett), and healers who moved around the Atlantic world (James Sweet on Domingos Álvares, healer who moved from Dahomey to Brazil to Portugal).

## Interaction

- **[[colonial-rule-in-africa]]**: Colonial medicine was not an external imposition but a negotiated, contested domain; African healers, nurses, midwives, and patients shaped what biomedicine became in Africa.
- **[[women-and-gender-in-african-history]]**: Reproductive health, genital cutting, birthing practices, and nursing were central sites of colonial gender intervention and African women's agency.
- **[[youth-in-african-history]]**: Child-soldiers, AIDS orphans, and the recruitment of young men by healing-and-harming militarised movements all intersect with youth history.
- **[[african-urbanization]]**: Urban concentrations of migrant workers drove colonial sanitation campaigns and, later, the AIDS epidemic.
- **[[education-and-literacy-in-africa]]**: African nurses, doctors, and midwives emerged from mission schools; literacy enabled African healers to document their practices and negotiate with colonial institutions.
- **[[disease-in-modern-history]]**: African health history sits within the broader global history of epidemic disease, but insists on the social-political rather than purely biological framing.
- **[[islamization-of-africa]]**: Muslim healing practices — including bori spirit-possession challenged by stricter Islam — are a major thread in West African health history (Murray Last; Adeline Masquelier on Niger).

## End Conditions

Ongoing. The field has three identified turning points: (1) the 1990s Foucauldian turn toward biopower and subject formation; (2) the development of ethnographic history with attention to African textualities and materialities; (3) the arrival of STS-inspired questions about experimentality. The field's increasing presentism (focus on AIDS, neo-liberal health crises, clinical trials) is valuable but risks shallow historical time-depth. Nancy Rose Hunt calls for histories of health and healing extending into Africa's "early modern" period (sixteenth to eighteenth centuries) — still largely unwritten.

## Historiography

**Source types**: Colonial medical archives (mission records, government public health reports, asylum patient files); personal letters and diaries; ethnographic fieldwork notes (many from the colonial era and now being reinterpreted); oral histories; material culture (nkisi objects, medical instruments); visual sources (colonial photographs of hospitals, clinics).

**Key works**: Feierman and Janzen, *Social Basis of Health and Healing in Africa* (1992) — the field-defining anthology; Vaughan, *Curing their Ills* (1991); Marks, *Not Either an Experimental Doll* (1988); Hunt, *A Colonial Lexicon* (1999, Herskovits Prize 2000); Janzen, *Lemba, 1650–1930* (1982) and *Ngoma* (1992); Livingston, *Debility and Moral Imagination in Botswana* (2005); Nguyen, *The Republic of Therapy* (2010); Langwick, *Bodies, Politics, and African Healing* (2011).

**Collection coverage**: The Bibliotheca Alexandrina is weak on post-1750 African social history. This area depends heavily on specialist journal literature (Social Science and Medicine, Social History of Medicine, African Studies Review, Journal of African History) and monograph series (Ohio University Press, Indiana University Press, Duke University Press). See [[parker-reid-oxford-modern-african-history-2013]], ch. 21 (Nancy Rose Hunt) for the synthesis underlying this page.
