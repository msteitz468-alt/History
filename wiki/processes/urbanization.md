# Urbanization (The Formation of Cities)

## Definition and Scope

**Urbanization** is the long-duration process by which dispersed agricultural
populations were drawn together and reorganized into cities — "nodal points of
pilgrimages and ceremonies, exchange, storage and redistribution, and centers
for defense and warfare" ([[yoffee-cwh-v3-2015|Yoffee, CWH Vol. III]]). It is
the historical process underlying the analytical concept of [[urbanism]] and the
periodization device of the [[urban-revolution]]. It is *not* simple population
growth: urbanization restructures social life both inside and outside the city,
creating peripheries where there had been autonomous villages ([[ruralization]]).

This process succeeds and depends on [[agriculture-and-urbanism|the agricultural
foundation]] established in [[barker-cwh-v2-2015|CWH Vol. II]]: cities require a
farmed surplus and the storage and redistribution institutions to mobilize it.

## Causal Drivers

No single cause; the volume's case studies show a **polycentric** process (Henry
Wright) with recurring contributors:

- `contributed_to:` agricultural surplus and storage — frees specialists from
  subsistence (Childe's central mechanism).
- `contributed_to:` ritual and pilgrimage — cities as ceremonial centers and
  cosmograms (Wheatley; Fustel's ancestor-cult "cement"); religion may *drive*
  aggregation, not merely follow it (Göbekli Tepe, Poverty Point).
- `contributed_to:` defense and warfare — walls, refuge, and military nodality.
- `contributed_to:` exchange and long-distance trade — markets and redistribution.
- `enabled:` [[writing-and-record-keeping]] — administration that makes a large,
  heterogeneous population "legible" (James Scott) and governable.

## Major Phases

1. **First cities (~3500–3000 BCE)** — southern Mesopotamia; [[places/uruk|Uruk]]
   reaches tens of thousands by ~3200 BCE in an "explosive" trajectory. Northern
   Mesopotamian cities are equally early.
2. **City-states and peer-polities (3rd–2nd millennium BCE)** — small territorial
   [[city-state|city-states]] fighting over land, water, and trade routes;
   occasional ephemeral hegemonies collapsing back to autonomous units.
3. **Secondary and independent urbanizations** — Egypt, Indus, China, the
   Mediterranean polis, Mesoamerica ([[places/teotihuacan|Teotihuacan]]), the
   Andes ([[places/tiwanaku|Tiwanaku]]), West Africa ([[places/jenne-jeno|Jenne-jeno]]),
   and the Mississippian ([[places/cahokia|Cahokia]]) — some demic, some
   independent inventions.
4. **Imperial and founded cities (1st millennium BCE–1200 CE)** — capitals built
   as instruments of empire (Neo-Assyrian capitals, Rome, [[places/tenochtitlan|Tenochtitlan]])
   or deliberate creations ([[places/baghdad|Baghdad]] 762 CE).
5. **The medieval European urban revival (10th–13th c.)** — after the post-Roman
   contraction of the western *civitates* to shrunken episcopal/fortified cores, the
   "origins of the European town in the form known to us from the late middle ages
   lie in the tenth century" ([[reuter-ncmh-v3-1999|NCMH III]], Johanek). A renewed,
   monetised long-distance trade — driven by agrarian surplus, demographic growth,
   and new silver from the **Harz** (Goslar/Rammelsberg) — reattached crafts and
   commerce to nucleated settlements and began the **commercial revolution** that
   peaked in the thirteenth century. The Mediterranean (the Italian *civitates*;
   Córdoba and the Islamic cities as the era's true urban centres alongside
   Constantinople) led; the North Sea/Baltic emporia (Haithabu, Birka) and the
   revived Frankish, Rhenish, and Anglo-Saxon towns followed. See [[commercial-revolution]].
   By the **thirteenth century** ([[abulafia-ncmh-v5-2000|NCMH V]], Steven Epstein, Ch.
   1b) this revival reached its medieval peak: Europe remained overwhelmingly rural (only
   ~10% urban, perhaps 20% in the densest zones of Flanders and northern Italy), but rapid
   population growth — sustained by **continuous in-migration**, since unhealthy cities did
   not reproduce themselves — pushed the giants upward. **Paris** reached ~200,000;
   **Venice and Florence** ~100,000 (a transport-limited ceiling); Cologne, Milan, Bruges,
   Genoa, and London half that; while war-wrecked **Constantinople** declined to a shadow.
   Epstein emphasizes the city as a place of **wage labour and the guild** (*métier*,
   *Zunft*, *arte*), of charity dispensed largely by the Church, of professionalizing
   public service (the *podestà*, the *prévôt*), and of the rising **popolo** — but also as
   an engine of marginalization, where Jews, Muslims, lepers, and prostitutes were
   compelled to mark themselves with badges, bells, and distinctive dress and confined to
   regulated quarters. The decline of rural serfdom and the growth of cities were two faces
   of one **town–country symbiosis**.

## Geographic Spread

Treated comparatively rather than diffusionally in CWH III. The independent
hearths of urbanism broadly track (but do not coincide with) the independent
hearths of [[domestication|agriculture]]: Mesopotamia, Egypt, the Indus, China,
Mesoamerica, the Andes, and — as a distinctively non-hierarchical case — the
Middle Niger.

## Interaction

Urbanization is inseparable from **[[ruralization]]** (its mirror image: the
countryside remade as hinterland), from **state formation**, and from the
emergence of **social stratification, slavery, disease, and forced migration**
that Yoffee stresses cities produced "of unprecedented kinds and scales."

## The Fourteenth-Century Contraction (NCMH VI)

[[jones-ncmh-v6-2000|NCMH VI]] (Jean-Pierre Leguay, Ch. 5) marks the **reversal** of the
thirteenth-century urban apogee: fourteenth-century towns were "profoundly affected by the
economic contraction that followed previous expansion," aggravated by the famines of 1315–1322,
the **[[black-death|Black Death]]** and recurrent plague. The result was "a sharp drop in
population levels, barely compensated for by the influx of refugees from the countryside," with
high taxation and coinage manipulation hampering trade and sharpening social tension (the urban
revolts — the Florentine Ciompi, 1378; the Flemish risings). Yet the picture was uneven — some
towns were devastated (Coventry), others "mysteriously spared" — and new foundations continued at
the frontiers (the last Aquitaine *bastides*, the [[edward-i-plantagenet|Edwardian]] planted towns
of Wales, and the [[teutonic-knights|Teutonic]] and German-Slav colonial towns of the Baltic
east). The episode belongs to the wider [[late-medieval-crisis|late-medieval crisis]].

## End Conditions

Ongoing. Individual cities collapse and are abandoned (Cahokia, many Mesopotamian
tells), persist and are repeatedly recreated (Rome, [[places/jerusalem|Jerusalem]],
Baghdad), or are destroyed by conquest (Tenochtitlan). Urbanization as a global
process never reverses after its inception.
