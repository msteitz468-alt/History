# Trade in the Roman Empire

The system of **market exchange** that moved goods across the Roman world — from local markets to
massive long-distance flows of staples, and the luxury trade reaching China and India. Treated in
[[bowman-cah-v11-2000|CAH XI]] Ch. 24 (**William V. Harris**), who argues against the old
**minimalist/maximalist dichotomy**: Roman trade was neither a trivial by-product of an agrarian
economy nor a quasi-modern commercial system, but included both a "truly massive trade in certain
commodities" and a wide luxury trade — best compared to **1300–1800** Europe.

## The drivers: cities, armies, and transport

Two great engines pulled goods over distance:

- **The metropolitan cities** — **Rome** (near a million inhabitants), **Alexandria** and
  **Carthage** (each roughly twice the size of Europe's largest city c. 1600), plus Antioch — which
  "could satisfy few if any of their principal needs locally" and drew essential commodities from
  "enormous catchment areas."
- **The frontier armies** — some 350,000 men, regularly and decently paid, a guaranteed
  state-backed market for food, clothing, and equipment, concentrated in the north and north-west.

The **navigable Mediterranean** (and the great rivers — Guadalquivir, Ebro, Rhône, Rhine, Tiber,
Nile) made bulk transport feasible: water transport was vastly cheaper than land (Diocletian's
Edict implies a land:sea cost ratio on the order of tens to one), though Harris warns this is
**overstated** — land transport over substantial distances was common and profitable where demand
was strong (La Graufesenque pottery hauled 75+ km overland; African grain and oil carried far
inland). The governing principle was **price elasticity**: any good that could be sold at a profit
travelled, whether a precious stone or middling pottery.

## The commodities

- **Cereals** — mostly local production for local consumption, the great exception being **Rome**
  (>200,000 tonnes of wheat a year, "imported from Africa and Egypt for eight and four months"),
  shipped largely by **private merchants** even when government-distributed; the state easing the
  trade through the *praefectus annonae*, Portus, and warehouses.
- **Olive oil** — an essential (food, light, soap, cosmetics): **Baetican** (Dressel 20 amphorae,
  building Monte Testaccio), **Tripolitanian**, and rising **African** oil flowing to Rome and the
  northern armies.
- **Wine** — produced everywhere but shipped in huge quantity to the cities (Rome ~1.5 million
  hectolitres/year), from the Italian west coast, Narbonensis, Tarraconensis, and Crete; **no
  government intervention**.
- **Garum** (fish sauce), other foodstuffs, and **slaves** (Augustus' sales-tax implying ~250,000
  sales a year; Ephesus the great eastern slave market; Palmyra and Zarai as import routes).
- **Metals** (mostly imperial-owned sources, extracted and shipped by private contractors, worked
  by town craftsmen), **textiles** (wool, linen, Chinese silk), **timber**, **pottery** (the
  red-glazed **terra sigillata** travelling long distances; production migrating Italy → south Gaul
  → central Gaul/Germany; African red-slip ware), **glass**, **marble** (highly organized, imperial
  quarries — Docimium, Numidia, Teos), **papyrus** (Egypt's near-monopoly), and the **eastern
  luxuries** (spices, incense, ivory, pearls, precious stones) that Pliny claimed drained ≥100
  million sesterces a year to Arabia, India, and China.

## The personnel: merchants without a bourgeoisie

In the foreground stands the **merchant** (*negotiator*, *mercator*; *emporos*), typically dealing
in **mixed cargoes** (the obvious strategy given stale market information — surviving wrecks carry
several commodities). Harris's key social observation: most merchants — and many successful ones —
were **freedmen and slaves**, with free-born traders rarely of curial or equestrian rank. The
Roman world thus **lacked a powerful commercial bourgeoisie** of the early-modern Italian/Low
Countries type — but, Harris insists, this does **not** mean trade was unimportant: it means the
commercial enterprises were run largely by freedmen and slaves, sometimes fronting for senators
and knights with hidden commercial interests. **Fairs and periodic markets** multiplied (esp.
Flavians to Hadrian), and every city was also a local trading centre.

## Government involvement

The state intervened systematically only in the **grain supply of Rome** (and military supply),
easing it with offices, harbours, and privileges (immunity for grain- and oil-shippers by the 160s,
tax exemption for army suppliers by the Severans) — but left **wine** entirely alone and touched
oil, metals, and most commerce only lightly. Trade was overwhelmingly **private**.

## Significance

Harris reframes Roman trade as a **large and complex system** — not industrial, but far from
negligible — whose geography was set by the location of the great consuming markets (cities and
armies) and the centres of supply, mediated by the economics of transport. It is the circulatory
counterpart to the [[roman-agrarian-economy|agrarian base]]: the mechanism by which the cash crops
of the provinces reached Rome and the frontier, and the luxuries of the East reached the Roman
elite.

## The Eastern Luxury Trade and "Silk Mania" (Benjamin 2018)

[[benjamin-empires-ancient-eurasia-2018|Benjamin]] details the demand side of the
[[silk-roads|First Silk Roads Era]]. A "veritable mania for silk" gripped Julio-Claudian
Rome; the poets (Tibullus, Propertius, Ovid) tied it to sensuality, and conservatives
linked it to female promiscuity and **economic independence** — the Senate under Tiberius
even **forbade men to wear silk** (Cassius Dio), a ban Caligula flouted. **Pliny the Elder**
(65 CE) claimed India, the Seres (China), and Arabia drained Rome of **100 million sesterces
a year** — a figure the [[muziris|Muziris papyrus]] suggests was not exaggerated (one cargo
= 7% of it; Sidebotham extrapolates South Asian imports alone at ~17.64 billion sesterces).
Diocletian's price edict fixed a pound of silk at **12,000 denarii — the price of a pound of
gold wire**. Romans misunderstood silk's origin (Seneca and Virgil thought it grew on
trees) and first knew it as Coan cloth (*Coa Vestis*). Crucially, Benjamin notes the
**25% customs duty** on these imports may have helped pay the legions — making the
[[indian-ocean-trade|eastern trade]] a pillar of imperial finance, not just elite vanity.

## Cambridge Economic History of the Greco-Roman World

**Neville Morley** (Ch. 21, "The Early Roman Empire: Distribution," in
[[scheidel-cambridge-economic-history-greco-roman-world-2007|CEHGRW]]) reframes the drivers
of Roman inter-regional trade around the **scale and location of demand** rather than
innate market forces, identifying three centers: (1) **the army** — some 400,000 soldiers
absorbing 450–500 million sesterces, likely over half the imperial budget by the mid-1st
century, stationed on sparsely settled frontiers unable to feed themselves (the four Rhine
legions alone needed the tithe-equivalent output of ~40,000 square miles, but the region
held only ~20,000; in the Cananefates region, 16,000–22,000 military personnel outnumbered
~14,000 natives); (2) **the city of Rome**, which grew from ~200,000 to ~1 million from the
late Republic and demanded at least 150,000 tonnes of grain, ~75 million liters of wine, and
20–30 million liters of oil per year, drawn from Italy, Sardinia, Sicily, Africa, and Egypt
(grain), Italy, Gaul, Spain, and the Aegean (wine), and Italy, Spain, and Africa (oil); and
(3) the **urban population of the rest of the empire** (~8–9 million of 50–60 million
total). Ancient authors register this appetite vividly — Revelation 18:11–19 catalogues
Rome's imports of gold, silver, pearls, silk, wine, oil, wheat, cattle, and slaves, and
Aelius Aristides (*Or.* 26.11–13) marvels at goods flowing in from every land and sea.

**Shipwreck evidence** (Parker 1992) shows far more datable Mediterranean wrecks between
200 BCE and 200 CE than in any period before the 16th century — a dramatic rise in the
volume of goods moved, though biased toward well-surveyed coasts (southern Spain and
France) and amphora-bearing (hence archaeologically visible) cargoes; the count only
slightly exceeds the late-Republican peak, then declines rapidly after 200 CE. Amphora
evidence (Dressel 1, Dressel 2–4, African oil amphorae, Lamboglia 2) traces the wine/oil/
grain flows but cannot by itself distinguish free-market trade from elite landowners
shipping their own estate produce for domestic consumption — a live methodological dispute
(Whittaker 1985).

**Finance.** Roman commercial finance rested on *pecunia traiecticia* (maritime loans,
attested since the 4th century BCE: the lender bore shipwreck risk in exchange for higher
interest, valid only for voyages completed in the "safe" sailing season). The **Muziris
papyrus** records a maritime loan for an India cargo valued at nearly 7 million sesterces;
the Sulpicii of Puteoli (the Murecine tablets) are among the rare attested "merchant
financiers." No limited companies or bills of exchange existed as in early modern Europe,
but Morley argues Rome's commercial institutions were adequate to its actual scale of need.
Typical merchant ships ran 60–80 tonnes (per the Egyptian port register P. Bingen 77 and
state contracting norms of ≥330 tonnes total or several ≥65-tonne vessels); much larger
ships (200–400 tonnes) served the Rome-supply routes, while thousand-tonne vessels were
built only for special tasks (obelisk transport) and were uneconomical for regular trade. A
medium ship cost roughly 57,000–68,000 sesterces — below the property qualification for
municipal office — implying most shipowners were of only modest wealth and status, often
freedmen set up by patrons.

**The limits of integration.** Morley's central conclusion is that Roman economic
integration was only "limited": Rome was a **"world empire" rather than a fully integrated
"world economy"** (citing Woolf 1990/1992) — goods moved unevenly, and local shocks had
little wider effect, though shocks in Rome or on the frontiers could destabilize the whole
system; sufficient connectivity nonetheless existed to spread both disease (the Antonine
Plague) and ideas (Christianity) empire-wide — "connectivity had costs as well as
benefits." As local production caught up with demand — Gaul's wine, Britain's grain and
fine ware becoming self-sufficient by the 3rd century — inter-regional distribution volumes
declined; Rome itself stopped growing (constrained by the Tiber-port bottleneck), capping
its own demand. The state relied entirely on private contractors and shipowners for
transport — there was never a state merchant fleet — offering incentives (citizenship
privileges, exemption from provincial *munera*) to encourage annona shipping; by the 4th
century that shipping service had become a hereditary compulsory obligation (*Cod. Theod.*
13.5–7), signaling declining voluntary profitability over time (see also
[[processes/roman-imperial-taxation-and-fiscal-system]]).

**Market structure.** Elite landowners typically sold crops before harvest (per Cato and
the legal sources), transferring price and climate risk to traders in exchange for a
smaller but assured income, and preserving social distance from "sordid" trade; peasant
farmers with small surpluses bore transport costs themselves to sell at periodic local
markets (*nundinae*), which in regions like Campania formed "dendritic networks" funneling
surplus toward Rome. Roman traders also reached Arabia and India for spices, incense, and
perfumes (the *Periplus Maris Erythraei*); a Temple of Augustus at Muziris attests a
permanent Roman trading presence in southern India. Complaints that the luxury trade
"drained" imperial wealth (echoing Pliny) were likely overstated, since much of the profit
stayed with Roman merchants, perfumiers, and financiers. Trans-frontier trade to the north
brought furs, amber, leather, and slaves; Roman pottery, glass, metalwork, and coin found
beyond the frontier arrived partly through gift-exchange and raiding, not trade alone.

The Roman state's own tax-collection practice bears on the "**taxes and trade**" model
(Hopkins): whether taxes were collected mostly in cash or in kind is disputed, but by the
2nd century money taxes predominated in most regions; notably, Egypt's grain-tax take
exceeded the state's own distribution and army needs, meaning the state itself sold surplus
tax grain into local and regional markets rather than consuming all of it.

## Woolf, *Rome: An Empire's Story* (2012)

Source: [[woolf-rome-empires-story-2012]].

- **Ecological gradients.** Expansion beyond Mediterranean created N–S trade patterns: wine and especially olive oil traded north; from late second century BCE “great quantities of grain” from Tunisia, Sicily, Egypt to northern-basin cities; European provinces better for large domesticates/cavalry; more beef north of Alps, sheep/goat in arid south/east.
- **Infrastructure toolkit.** Roads, ports, taxes in kind and cash, incentives for cargoes to capitals, provincial supply of armies/courts on the move—combination of moving food, monetizing taxes, and imperial standards. Silver denarii gradually replaced other Mediterranean precious coinages in late Republic.
- **Hopkins engagement.** Woolf flags Keith Hopkins’ 1980 JRS “Taxes and Trade” as probably the most influential contribution arguing the early-imperial tax system stimulated the economy; in body Woolf cautiously includes taxation among growth stimuli (“Perhaps taxation played a part as well…”).
- **Shipwrecks and demand-led growth.** Underwater archaeology shows shipwrecks peak late Republic (greatest long-distance trade); Italian wine/ceramics spread then diminish as provincial production rises.

## From Mitchell, Later Roman Empire (2015)

### Land vs sea logistics

- Effective imperial power rested on road and sea networks; third–fourth centuries were a great age of road-building for troop shuttling between frontiers; *mansiones*/*mutationes* and relay animal requisition (*evectiones*) made long-distance official movement routine. Land transport of bulk goods was economically unfeasible for private merchants (a yoke might eat a grain load before 100 miles), so the **state alone** solved bulk overland movement by requisition/relay. `part_of:` [[roman-imperial-taxation-and-fiscal-system]]; `enabled:` [[late-roman-economy]].
- Mediterranean sea transport enabled bulk long-distance trade at a fraction of land costs (sailing season March–October); coastal mega-cities (Rome, Constantinople, Carthage, Alexandria, Antioch, Ephesus) grew beyond hinterland capacity; the state organized sea transport via contracts with large shippers, especially for the **annona** feeding Rome then Constantinople; shipwreck density and amphora studies are central archaeological evidence. In the late empire, sea routes to Rome were eclipsed by networks for Constantinople.

### Eastern frontier trade policy

Eastern frontier was porous economically despite imperial efforts to channel long-distance luxury trade: **408/9** regulation (confirmed **422**) restricted cross-border trade to Nisibis, Artaxata, and Callinicum (still in force in 562 treaty); **25%** tolls on eastern imports; Procopius shows everyday local exchange, intermarriage, and shared farming across the boundary in Chorzanene. Silk was a Sassanian-controlled bottleneck until ~551 missionaries allegedly brought sericulture knowledge to Justinian. Frontier strongholds (Dara-Anastasiupolis founded 505; Theodosiopolis; Martyropolis) served strategic control more than closed economic walls. “There is thus no contradiction between a state policy of restricted trade and free commerce throughout the border region.”
