# The New Deal

## Definition and Scope

The New Deal is the program of relief, recovery, and reform legislation and
executive action carried out by the administration of
[[roosevelt-franklin-d|Franklin D. Roosevelt]] between 1933 and roughly 1939 in
response to the [[great-depression|Great Depression]]. It is a process, not an
event: a rolling sequence of statutes, agencies, and political realignments
rather than a single designed program. It is *not* synonymous with the
Depression itself (which it addressed), with wartime mobilization (which
succeeded it), or with the broader twentieth-century growth of the American
state (which it accelerated but did not begin — Progressive-era and World War I
precedents supplied much of its toolkit and personnel).

## Causal Drivers

- **The Depression emergency** — caused_by [[great-depression]]: bank collapse
  (thousands of failures by March 1933), ~25% unemployment, farm price
  collapse, and the discrediting of the Hoover administration produced the 1932
  landslide and a Congress willing to delegate emergency power.
- **Intellectual currents** — enabled by pre-existing reform traditions:
  Progressive regulation, World War I economic mobilization (the NRA was
  consciously modeled on the War Industries Board), institutional economics,
  and the "Brains Trust" academics (Moley, Tugwell, Berle).
- **Political coalition-building** — the emerging Roosevelt coalition (urban
  labor, farmers, the South, ethnic minorities, later Black voters) both drove
  and was reinforced by New Deal distribution.

## Major Phases

1. **The Hundred Days (March–June 1933).** Emergency Banking Act, abandonment
   of the gold standard, FDIC (Glass-Steagall), AAA, TVA, NRA, CCC, and federal
   relief — an unprecedented legislative burst assembled largely by
   improvisation. John T. Flynn's hostile but well-sourced contemporary account
   ([[flynn-roosevelt-myth-1948|Flynn 1948]]) stresses the improvised character:
   his Book One is titled "Trial—and Error."
2. **The "Second New Deal" (1935–36).** Social Security Act, Wagner Act
   (NLRB), WPA under [[hopkins-harry|Harry Hopkins]], wealth-tax rhetoric — a
   shift from business-government partnership toward labor, relief, and
   regulation, sealed by the 1936 landslide.
3. **Check and stall (1937–38).** The [[court-packing-fight-1937|court-packing
   fight]], the sharp 1937–38 recession, and the failed 1938 purge of
   conservative Democrats ended the reform wave; the conservative coalition in
   Congress blocked further domestic expansion.
4. **Absorption into war preparation (1939–41).** Reform gave way to
   rearmament; full employment arrived with war mobilization, not New Deal
   spending.

## Geographic Spread

National in scope with pronounced regional concentrations: the TVA remade the
upper South; the AAA transformed (and in tenant regions, displaced) Southern
and Plains agriculture; the WPA and PWA built infrastructure in every state;
Western water and power projects (Grand Coulee, Boulder Dam completion)
permanently altered the region's economy.

## Interaction

- part_of the global 1930s turn toward state economic management —
  analogous_to (and explicitly compared at the time with) European corporatist
  and planned-economy experiments; the comparison is itself contested (see
  [[road-to-serfdom-thesis]] and [[communism-and-fascism]]).
- produced the modern American regulatory-welfare state framework (Social
  Security, deposit insurance, securities and labor regulation) and the
  long-lived Democratic electoral coalition.
- The constitutional dimension ran through the Supreme Court: invalidation of
  the NRA (1935) and first AAA (1936), then the 1937 jurisprudential shift —
  see [[court-packing-fight-1937]].

## End Conditions

Domestic reform effectively ended in 1938–39 with the conservative
congressional coalition and the pivot to war; Roosevelt himself announced in
1943 that "Dr. New Deal" had given way to "Dr. Win-the-War." Its institutions,
however, persisted and expanded, making the terminal date a matter of
convention.

## Contemporary revisionist documentation (Flynn 1940/1944/1948, attributed)

Flynn's *[[flynn-roosevelt-myth-1948|The Roosevelt Myth]]* supplies a hostile
but heavily sourced contemporary record; the following items are his, cited to
named sources, and are recorded here as attributed claims:

- **Improvisation over design.** The bank-reopening plan of March 1933 was
  devised largely by holdover Hoover Treasury officials (Mills, Ballantine) and
  bankers — Ogden Mills: "it was a bankers' plan" — and Flynn (via Moley)
  attributes the first fireside chat's text to Ballantine. Roosevelt's own
  campaign platform of 1932 (balanced budgets, states' rights, sound currency)
  is set point-by-point against the 1933–36 program as an unacknowledged
  total reversal.
- **The gold-buying episode (Oct 1933).** Prices set daily over breakfast on
  Prof. Warren's theory; Morgenthau's diary (as excerpted in *Collier's*): "If
  people knew how we fixed the price of gold they would be frightened" —
  Roosevelt once choosing a 21-cent rise because "it's a lucky number — three
  times seven."
- **NRA collapse.** The Roosevelt-appointed Darrow board condemned the NRA as
  "monopolistic, oppressive... savage, wolfish" (May 1934); Perkins records
  Roosevelt privately conceding "the whole thing has been a mess" before
  *Schechter* (1935) struck it down 9–0.
- **Relief as political machine.** A Senate committee report on the 1938
  primaries documented WPA officials in Kentucky checking the political
  standing of 17,000 relief workers and soliciting ~2% salary "contributions"
  to the Barkley campaign, with parallel findings in Pennsylvania, Tennessee,
  and Cook County — the strongest documentary basis for the revisionist
  "spend and elect" charge against the [[hopkins-harry|Hopkins]] relief
  apparatus.
- **The 1937–38 "forgotten depression."** Morgenthau's diary records
  Roosevelt in January 1938 dismissing the collapse ("the next two years don't
  count — they are already water over the dam") and planning renewed deficit
  spending; unemployment returned to ~10–11 million by 1938.

His earlier *[[flynn-as-we-go-marching-1944|As We Go Marching]]* (1944) adds
the structural documentation for the same charge, likewise attributed: the
deficit series ($3.26bn 1933-34 rising to $4.95bn 1936-37, with the claimed
"balanced" 1938 budget actually $1.45bn in deficit); the NRA as corporativism
"based, not consciously but in fact, almost wholly on the principle of the
guild or corporative system which Mussolini was in process of perfecting at
that very time," planted in the pre-existing soil of Coolidge-era trade-
association codes and already collapsing from noncompliance before *Schechter*;
the post-1938 Hansen/Keynes "Dual Consumptive Economy" doctrine of permanent
public borrowing (the 1938 Harvard-Tufts pamphlet, Hansen at the NRPB); the
NRPB's 1943 postwar-controls report and its abolition by an alarmed Congress;
and the executive-power drift — lump-sum appropriations (the RFC's $34bn
disbursed without congressional appropriation; the BEW funded at ~$1.5bn
against a $12M appropriation), O'Mahoney's count of 4,000+ executive orders,
and delegated code-making struck down 9–0 in 1935.

The earliest layer of the same record is
*[[flynn-country-squire-1940|Country Squire in the White House]]* (May 1940),
whose distinctive attributed claims are:

- **NRA genesis in organized business, not the Brain Trust.** The code idea
  descends from Chamber of Commerce planning — the Harriman Committee on
  Continuity of Business and Employment (Feb 1931, recommending production
  control, Sherman Act modification, a National Economic Council, and social
  insurance) and the Litchfield Share-the-Work committee (1932) — through the
  1932 Wagner conference draft (Flynn: "I have seen that bill, and it
  contains the germ of everything, save the licensing clause, that appeared
  in the final NRA Act"), then Johnson/Dickinson/Frank/Richberg drafting.
  Codes were written by their industries (the Steel Code by the American Iron
  & Steel Institute, which became its own code authority): "the beginning of
  the corporate state, only we called the corporatives codes." Richberg is
  quoted admitting trade associations "were asked to Washington and told to
  write their own tickets."
- **The gold-buying scheme in real time.** Warren's theory (via Morgenthau)
  plus Fisher's compensated dollar; gold from $21 to $35/oz; "within three
  months the whole scheme was recognized as a ghastly fizzle," while Treasury
  purchases raised US holdings from $8.2bn to $19bn. Footnoted first-person
  anecdote: Borah told Flynn that FDR laughed off silver policy with "I
  experimented with gold and that was a flop. Why shouldn't I experiment a
  little with silver?"
- **Spending as the "Big Gun."** $22bn of borrowed relief/recovery funds
  (1933–40) "without a single penny of taxes on anyone"; Congress's shift to
  lump-sum grants read as abdication of the appropriation power; the
  justification's evolution traced from crisis relief → "priming the pump" →
  "the deficit was not a deficit; it was an asset" → permanent replacement of
  private investment.
- **Social Security resisted, not championed.** Flynn's emphatic claim that
  FDR "held it up": refusing Wagner's 1934 bill, telling a delegation "The
  time is not ripe for old-age pensions yet," junking his own committee's
  plan, and accepting the reserve-fund design (a projected $47bn fund used to
  cover general costs) that he and Morgenthau later agreed to abandon (Flynn,
  "The Social Security Reserve Swindle," *Harper's*, Feb 1939). Similarly,
  "one reason he jumped to the support of the NRA was to defeat the first
  minimum-wage and maximum-hour bill" (the Black 30-hour bill).
- **1933 continuity with Hoover.** The bank-closing orders "were in no sense
  different from those that had been prepared by Ogden Mills"; the crisis
  changed the public mood, not the problem — "the immensity of the disaster
  had, without changing the problem, opened the road for the new President to
  do almost anything he chose to do."

Flynn's interpretive superstructure — that the planned economy "was and is
fascism by whatever name it is known" and that rearmament became the New
Deal's last spending outlet — is a position, not a finding, and is filed at
[[roosevelt-and-new-deal-revisionism]]; compare [[road-to-serfdom-thesis]]
and [[as-we-go-marching-thesis]].

## Historiography

Assessment of the New Deal is a first-order interpretive dispute — economic
effectiveness, constitutional legacy, and comparative-ideological placement are
all contested between mainstream, Old Right/revisionist, and New Left
positions. The debate is recorded at [[roosevelt-and-new-deal-revisionism]];
this page confines itself to the shared factual record, with contested
characterizations attributed. Principal sources here: Jean Edward Smith,
*[[smith-fdr-2007|FDR]]* (2007, mainstream) and John T. Flynn,
*[[flynn-roosevelt-myth-1948|The Roosevelt Myth]]* (1948, revisionist).
