# Modern Imperialism

## Definition and Scope

**Imperialism** is the extension of one polity's authority — formal or informal,
territorial or economic — over other peoples and lands. Danielle Kinsey's chapter in
[[mcneill-cwh-v7-2015|CWH VII]] insists that **empire, not the nation state, was the
dominant form of political organization** for most of world history, including the
"modern" period: the world map of 1750, 1850, and even 1950 was a map of empires.
Burbank and Cooper's line frames the chapter: "the nation-state appears as a blip on
the historical horizon... whose hold on the world's political imagination may well
prove partial or transitory." This page covers modern imperialism, including the
**"New Imperialism"** of c. 1870–1914.

## Four questions (Kinsey's frame)

1. **What are empires?** World historians divide them into **land** (Russian,
   Ottoman, Qing, Austrian, Mughal) and **overseas** (the European maritime empires
   from the late fifteenth century). Kinsey stresses the **disadvantages** of the
   binary: it dismisses land empires as "pre-modern" and "Oriental" while valorizing
   European sea empires as engines of modernity, and it obscures that **every** empire
   ran simultaneous land and sea projects (Britain governed land empires in South Asia
   and Australasia; the Ottomans controlled seaways).
2. **How did they work?** Recent scholarship sees imperial formation as **incomplete,
   heterogeneous, and contingent on local conditions** — empire as a "web," not a
   hub-and-spoke radiation of core power.
3. **How did resistance matter?** Now "one of the most pressing" questions: colonized
   peoples acted on, resisted, circumvented, and made irrelevant imperial designs, in
   ways the new cultural history traces through gender, race, sexuality, the body
   (Foucault's "bio-power"), mobility, and the colonial archive (the sati debate in
   India as a contested "technology of resistance").
4. **How was modern imperialism different?** Not in kind but in **enlarged scale,
   acceleration, and impact** — the product of multiple uneven "revolutions"
   (agriculture, [[industrialization|industry]], finance, communications, bureaucracy,
   military, belief). "Governmentality" (surveillance and regulation of everyday life)
   grew in metropole and colony alike.

## The New Imperialism (c. 1870–1914)

The most intense phase: the **scramble for Africa** and the extension of formal and
informal empire across Asia and the Pacific, enabled by the [[industrialization|second
industrial revolution]] and new "tools of empire" (Headrick) — steamships, railways,
telegraphs, quinine, repeating rifles, and machine guns. Colonial wars became
**technologically one-sided**: at Omdurman (1898) the British and their Egyptian
auxiliaries lost 40 dead to perhaps 11,000 of their opponents; the Java War, the
Herero genocide, and the Belgian Congo (c. 5 million deaths) exemplify the lopsided
violence (see [[genocide]]). Exceptions — Haiti, Ethiopia at Adwa (1896) — proved the
rule. Where outright conquest was too costly (China, Japan, the Ottomans, Siam, Latin
America), Europeans preferred short punitive expeditions and **informal empire**,
opening space for [[self-strengthening|self-strengthening]] responses.

## Imperialism and globalization

Kinsey closes on the contested relationship between imperialism and **globalization**.
One camp (Darwin, Ferguson) holds that **imperialism delivered globalization** —
British power building global integration in the nineteenth century, American power
extending it in the twentieth, with present inequalities the legacy. Post-Marxists
(Hardt & Negri) project this toward a centerless global "Empire." Kinsey urges a **less
deterministic** view: much innovation occurred *between* and *against* empires, and
colonized peoples "spawned new hybrid forms... that take on lives of their own." The
key analytical task is to study the moments when "imperial business was unfinished,
incomplete, resisted, circumvented, and made irrelevant."

## Geographic Spread

By 1914, most of Africa, South and Southeast Asia, and the Pacific were under formal
European rule; the Americas had been decolonized (politically) a century earlier but
remained economically subordinate. The United States, Japan, and later the USSR
operated their own imperial paradigms.

## End Conditions

Formal overseas empire was largely dismantled by **[[decolonization]]** (c. 1945–1975),
but Kinsey (with Burbank & Cooper) treats imperial structures as persisting into
contemporary globalization and inequality (see [[contemporary]]).

## Historiography

Kinsey's chapter is itself a survey of the **"new imperial history"**: Said's
*Orientalism* (and its critique), Chakrabarty's "provincializing Europe," the
Robinson–Gallagher "official mind," Hobson and Lenin's economic theories, the cultural
turn (Stoler, Ballantyne & Burton), and "multiple modernities." A central caution is
the **Eurocentrism** built into the very concepts of modernity and modern imperialism,
and into the colonial archives from which the discipline of history itself emerged
(see [[eurocentrism]]).
