# Disease in Modern World History

## Definition and Scope

This page covers the changing role of infectious (and later degenerative) disease in
the world since ~1750, following Mark Harrison's chapter in
[[mcneill-cwh-v7-2015|CWH VII]]. Harrison's framing resists the simple story of a
globe "unified by disease": the modern history of disease is **as much about
divergence as convergence**. The same two-and-a-half centuries that saw "an exchange
of pathogens unequaled in variety and scope" also saw "tremendous improvements in
human health" — banishing many infections from the rich world and widening the gap
between the mortality profiles of rich and poor countries. This is the disease side
of the [[demographic-transition|demographic transition]].

## Causal Drivers

- **Connection spreads pathogens.** Disease followed trade, migration, exploration,
  and war and magnified their consequences. The acceleration of transport —
  **steamships, railways, the Suez Canal (1869)** — let diseases travel faster than
  their incubation periods, repeatedly outrunning quarantine.
- **Integration also enables control.** The same connective infrastructure carried
  vaccination, germ theory, sanitary reform, and the institutions of global health.
- **Industrial urbanization** created the filthy, crowded "shock cities" in which
  cholera, typhoid, and tuberculosis flourished — and, later, the sanitation and
  clean-water systems that tamed them.

## Major Phases

1. **The retreat of plague (18th c.):** plague vanished from Western and Central
   Europe after the 1660s (last major outbreak Marseilles, 1720–21), plausibly thanks
   to **quarantine** (e.g. the Habsburg sanitary cordon) — while persisting just
   across the border in Ottoman and Russian lands.
2. **Atlantic fevers (c. 1790–1860):** **yellow fever** crossed from the Caribbean to
   Philadelphia, then to Mediterranean Europe, carried by war, steam shipping, and
   its mosquito vector — a test case for how to manage disease in an integrating
   economy.
3. **The cholera pandemics (from 1817):** erupting in Bengal and spreading worldwide
   along trade, **indentured-labor** ("coolie") migration, and the **Mecca
   pilgrimage** (35,000 pilgrims died in 1865); a disease of "the poor, the
   vanquished, and the desperate" that sharpened social tension and triggered the
   **international sanitary conferences**.
4. **The bacteriological revolution (late 19th c.):** Pasteur and Koch's **germ
   theory** shifted control from general sanitation to targeted measures (the
   conquest of yellow fever and malaria in the Panama Canal Zone, opening 1914).
5. **The epidemiological transition (20th c.):** mass vaccination, antibiotics, and
   public health drove infectious mortality down sharply in the rich world (and among
   the affluent everywhere), while **degenerative and cardiovascular** disease rose —
   capped by the [[smallpox-eradication|eradication of smallpox]] (certified 1980).
6. **The globalized present:** SARS, "swine flu," and successors are reminders, in
   Harrison's words, that "our globalized future may yet be a turbulent one."

## Geographic Spread

Pandemics of the nineteenth century mostly originated in **Asia** (cholera) or the
**Atlantic tropics** (yellow fever) and radiated outward; control technologies flowed
chiefly from the **North Atlantic** outward. The net result was **divergence**:
infectious disease retreated in rich countries and persisted in poor ones — an
inequality "still glaringly apparent."

## Interaction

Disease is the hinge between the [[columbian-exchange-1492|Columbian Exchange]]
(Old World pathogens devastating the Americas; see also [[population-politics]] on
colonial depopulation), the [[demographic-transition]] (falling mortality), the
[[atlantic-slave-trade]] and indentured-labor migrations (disease vectors), and the
rise of the global-health institutions that made the [[smallpox-eradication|smallpox
campaign]] possible.

## End Conditions

Ongoing. The epidemiological transition is far from complete and unevenly
distributed; new and re-emerging pathogens, antibiotic resistance, and the disease
consequences of climate change keep the process open (see [[contemporary]],
[[anthropocene]]).
