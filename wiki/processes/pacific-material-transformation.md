## Definition and Scope

The post-colonial remaking of the Pacific material world is the broad process by which independent and semi-sovereign Pacific Island states, beginning with Samoa's independence in 1962 and running through to the present, restructured their resource bases, economic relationships, and daily material lives. The process encompasses the redefinition of what counts as a resource (from land to ocean), the expansion of monetised exchange into subsistence communities, escalating import dependency, the integration of atolls into global communication networks, and deepening ecological stress. It is distinct from the earlier [[pacific-plantation-economy|plantation economy]] in that Island governments and communities became active, if unequal, negotiators rather than passive subjects of extraction.

As Nero notes, the transformation was simultaneous at multiple scales: global processes (climate change, commodity prices, Cold War geopolitics) restructured Island environments even as households experienced daily changes in diet, housing, and labour. The chapter frames the whole: "This chapter addresses issues which Islanders face as they interact with the environment which must sustain future generations, symbolically and materially."

## Causal Drivers

**Decolonisation and sovereignty**: Independence gave Island governments the legal standing to renegotiate resource contracts, assert EEZ rights under UNCLOS III (1982), and represent themselves in international bodies. Without sovereignty, none of the other renegotiations were possible. Britain ceded independence across its Pacific sphere between 1968 (Nauru) and 1980 (Vanuatu); Australia pressed Papua New Guinea to independence by 1975; the US arranged free association for Micronesian island groups between 1976 and 1994.

**UNCLOS III and EEZ redefinition**: The 1982 United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea allowed nations to declare 200-mile exclusive economic zones. For land-scarce Island states, this was transformative: "Resource bases change considerably once the exclusive economic zones are included." French Polynesia's EEZ (5,030,000 km²) dwarfs its land area (3,265 km²); Kiribati's EEZ (3,550,000 km²) gives a tiny land population sovereign rights over an oceanic expanse. The navigator's perspective—ocean as bridge, not barrier—was now legally codified.

**Colonial infrastructure and aid dependency**: New states inherited educational, medical, and administrative services that required ongoing metropolitan funding. "Unless resources were already depleted … the governments continued to allow mining and other projects, in return for an increasing share of the profits." Aid and resource-licensing fees were structurally intertwined, most visibly in tuna licensing where "85 per cent of these payments are directly linked to the fisheries' catch locations" yet the US insisted on calling part of the transfers "economic or technical assistance."

**Emigration and remittances**: Large fractions of Island populations relocated to metropolitan centres—"nearly 70 per cent of all Cook Islanders now live in New Zealand." Remittances from these diaspora communities provided cash income invisible to GDP statistics but critical for atoll households purchasing kerosene, school fees, and imported food.

**Global warming and environmental vulnerability**: Low-lying atolls face existential risk from sea-level rise. "Even small sea-level rises could submerge entire island chains in the Marshalls, Carolines, Kiribati, Tuvalu, the Tuamotus, Cook Islands, and much of Tonga." The South Pacific Forum (founded 1971 after the South Pacific Commission refused to debate French nuclear testing) became the vehicle for regional environmental advocacy.

## Major Phases

**1. Independence and resource renegotiation (1962–1982)**: Newly independent states renegotiated colonial extraction agreements. Papua New Guinea renegotiated the Bougainville Copper agreement on independence (1975), securing larger revenues and provincial autonomy. The UNCLOS negotiations (1970s) gave Island states their main lever for restructuring oceanic resources.

**2. EEZ assertion and fisheries politics (1982–1991)**: UNCLOS III came into force and Island states began asserting EEZ rights against distant-water fleets. The Nauru Group (1982) pooled bargaining power. Soviet entry into tuna licensing (1985–86, Kiribati and Vanuatu) embarrassed the US into signing a multilateral agreement in 1987. The Wellington Convention (1989) banned drift-net fishing in Pacific EEZs and adjacent high seas, led by Pacific nations at the UN.

**3. Resource boom and ecological crisis (1991–present)**: Logging booms struck the Solomon Islands, PNG, and Vanuatu in the 1990s. Mining disputes (Bougainville, Ok Tedi, Lihir, Grasberg/Irian Jaya) generated both national revenues and severe environmental damage. Urbanisation accelerated. The end of the Cold War weakened Island leverage over fishing fees: "the dissolution of the Soviet Union and the end of the Cold War weakened the Islands' leverage … Pacific nations now are lucky to obtain about 5 per cent of the market value of fish reported caught within their waters."

## Geographic Spread

The process is Pacific-wide but uneven by habitat type and colonial history:
- **Melanesia** (PNG, Solomons, Vanuatu, New Caledonia): large land areas, substantial mineral and timber resources, most severe extraction pressure; highest rates of ecological damage.
- **Micronesia** (FSM, Marshall Islands, Palau, Nauru, Kiribati): small, low-lying atolls; EEZ fisheries as primary resource; heavy aid dependency; Nauru's phosphate exhaustion as endpoint case.
- **Polynesia** (Samoa, Tonga, Cook Islands, Tuvalu, French Polynesia): high emigration; remittances and tourism central; copra and fisheries as main exports.

## End Conditions

The process is ongoing. Structural features — export/import imbalances, EEZ-based bargaining, aid dependency, urban drift, ecological stress — remain central to Pacific political economy as of the 1990s. Potential transformative pressures include: sea-level rise eliminating low-lying atolls; the exhaustion of non-renewable resources (phosphate at Nauru already spent); new forms of co-management blending customary and state authority (Pohnpei watershed model); and Pacific-wide regional institutions (South Pacific Forum, Forum Fisheries Agency, SPREP) developing more robust governance.

## Historiography

Nero frames the analytical tension explicitly: economists disagree whether Pacific Island economies are separable or form a single regional economy integrated into Australian/New Zealand economic space. Epeli Hau'ofa argued for "a single regional economy upon which has emerged a South Pacific society"; Te'o Fairbairn saw each economy as separately fragile. The debate turns on whether to count remittances, subsistence production, and diaspora transfers in economic assessment. Standard GDP indicators miss the "subsistence affluence" of communities like Tanna (Vanuatu), which are "both rich and poor" depending on the analytical framework applied.

A further analytical complication is semantic: Western economic categories translate poorly into Pacific vernaculars. Bare's study of Tahitian economic terms shows that concepts like capital, wages, and debt map onto fundamentally different local categories — tarahu ("to employ" connoting borrowing someone requiring reciprocal payment), aufaura'a 'avae (wages, originally tribute to chiefs). Any macro-economic analysis that ignores these mismatches is distorted.
