## Definition and Scope

The Freedom and Popular Rights Movement (jiyū minken undō) was a decade-long popular political movement in Meiji Japan that demanded constitutional government and the establishment of a national elected assembly. It was the most significant challenge to oligarchic rule in the first two decades of the Meiji era and the primary civilian vehicle through which former samurai and some commoners pressed for political inclusion.

The movement operated through petitions, political societies, lectures, newspaper articles, and ultimately political party organization. It was not a mass movement of peasants or workers — Keene notes that it "was led entirely by civilians, mainly men of middle- or lower-rank samurai status" — but it had genuine popular resonance and large public audiences. Lecturer Ueki Emori attracted crowds of 1,000 to 2,000 in Kōchi; his June 1877 lecture at a theater seating 2,000 people turned away another 2,000.

## Causal Drivers

**The defeat of armed resistance.** The decisive driver in shaping the movement's civilian character was the [[satsuma-rebellion-1877|Satsuma Rebellion]]'s defeat in 1877. [[itagaki-taisuke|Itagaki Taisuke]], who had been a supporter of Saigō Takamori, drew the explicit conclusion: "The defeat of Saigō's forces by the government army made it clear that it was useless to attempt to oppose the government by military means; therefore, the Risshi-sha's message would be spread by speeches and newspaper articles, not swords." The 1877 defeat thus channeled political opposition from military to civilian forms.

**Samurai displacement.** The abolition of samurai stipends, the 1873 conscription law, and the dismantling of feudal hierarchies left the former warrior class without social function or income. The Risshi-sha (founded 1874) was initially more a self-improvement society for displaced samurai than a political organization. The gradual radicalization of such groups reflected the deepening material grievances of the former samurai class.

**Western liberal ideas.** Books and articles introducing European political thought — constitutionalism, natural rights, parliamentary democracy — circulated widely. Ueki Emori read about "law, political economy, and natural science" in Tōkyō. French republican ideas and English liberal theory both circulated. Keene quotes Itō Hirobumi observing that "European books, with their new theories of government, had flooded into Japan, reaching the remotest hamlet."

**The emperor's own prior commitment.** The Oath in Five Articles of 1868 had promised that "measures shall be decided by open discussion." By the late 1870s, this had acquired the character of an imperial promise of parliamentary government. Advocates of popular rights could present themselves as demanding the fulfillment of the emperor's own pledge rather than as subversives.

## Major Phases

**Phase 1 (1874–1876): Organizational foundation.** Itagaki founded the Risshi-sha in 1874 and the Aikoku-sha in 1875 in Kōchi. These remained regional and relatively elite in composition. The emperor's September 1876 message to the Genrō-in commanding a constitutional draft indicated that the government itself acknowledged the need for action.

**Phase 2 (1877–1880): Post-rebellion radicalization.** The Satsuma Rebellion's defeat redirected energy from military confrontation to civilian agitation. Ueki Emori emerged as the most effective speaker and propagandist. The Aikoku-sha expanded nationally: in April 1880, at an Ōsaka convention, delegates from twenty-four prefectures passed a resolution demanding a national assembly and renamed the body the League for Establishing a National Assembly. "Kōchi was the spearhead of the Freedom and Popular Rights movement, and intellectuals from all over the country were drawn there."

**Phase 3 (1880–1881): Party formation and the constitutional crisis.** In December 1880 the League resolved to form a political party. The Jiyū-tō (Party of Liberty) was formally founded December 15, 1880. The movement's demands contributed directly to the [[political-crisis-of-1881|political crisis of 1881]] and to the emperor's rescript of October 12, 1881, promising parliament in 1890. Itagaki was elected chairman (Sōri) of the Jiyū-tō at its mass meeting in October 1881.

**Phase 4 (1882–1884): Government countermeasures and dissolution.** The government imposed increasingly stringent regulations. In March 1882 Itō Hirobumi secretly arranged for Mitsui Bank funding to send Itagaki and Gotō Shōjirō to Europe, deliberately depriving the party of its two chief leaders. In their absence, the Jiyū-tō and its rival Rikken Kaishin-tō (founded by Ōkuma in April 1882) fell into "furious exchanges of mudslinging." The Jiyū-tō was disbanded October 29, 1884; the Kaishin-tō effectively dissolved December 17, 1884.

## Geographic Spread

The movement was concentrated in Kōchi (Tosa) as its intellectual heartland, with Itagaki, Ueki, and the Risshi-sha providing its leadership and theoretical energy. It spread through the Ōsaka conventions of 1880 to all twenty-four represented prefectures. The government's concern was particularly acute because "new ideas of government had gained currency among both samurai and commoners, and in the past few years the influence had spread irresistibly to the cities and villages."

Military personnel were explicitly excluded: the Army Press Law of 1882 prohibited members of the army, navy, police, and teaching profession from attending mass meetings or joining political parties; the Imperial Rescript for Military Men (January 1882) ordered soldiers not to be "confused by public opinion and become involved in politics."

## Key Figures

- **[[itagaki-taisuke|Itagaki Taisuke]]** — founder of the Risshi-sha (1874) and Aikoku-sha (1875); chairman of the Jiyū-tō (1881–1884)
- **Ueki Emori (1857–1892)** — the movement's most effective agitator and theorist; lectured to crowds of thousands; drafted a private constitution in 1881 that included both an emperor and provisions for popular rights; one of the earliest Japanese advocates of equal rights for women
- **Gotō Shōjirō** — co-leader of the Jiyū-tō; sent with Itagaki on the Europe trip arranged by Itō

## Interaction with Other Processes

The movement is `concurrent_with` the government's own constitutional drafting process but in tension with it — the oligarchs were preparing a constitution precisely in order to contain the movement's demands within a framework they could control. It `produced` the immediate concession of the 1881 parliamentary promise and `enabled` the eventual [[meiji-constitution-1889|Meiji Constitution]], though the constitution's content (sovereignty vested in the emperor, not the people) was the opposite of what the movement's radicals sought.

The movement `contrasts_with` the [[satsuma-rebellion-1877|Satsuma Rebellion]] in its method — civilian agitation versus armed force — and in its social composition: the rebellion was a Satsuma samurai affair, while the jiyū minken movement drew from samurai throughout western Japan and increasingly from commoners.

## End Conditions

The movement's organizational forms were suppressed or manipulated into dissolution by 1884. The Jiyū-tō disbanded under combined pressure: government harassment and prosecution of members (Fukushima incident), manipulation of the leadership through the European trip, and internal factional conflict. However, the movement's fundamental demand — parliamentary government — was met institutionally, if not on the movement's terms, by the Meiji Constitution of 1889 and the first Diet session of 1890.

The liberal parties were "dead and would have to be brought back to life during the coming years" — as Keene notes at the close of chapter 35 — and indeed they were, in the 1890s and beyond.

## Historiography

Keene's treatment emphasizes the gap between the movement's rhetorical radicalism and its organizational weakness. The mass meetings, the dramatic speeches, the prison sentences, and Itagaki's famous near-martyrdom ("Itagaki may die, but freedom will never die!") all existed alongside a profound ignorance of parliamentary procedure and a susceptibility to government manipulation. Baba Tatsui, who had studied at the Middle Temple in London, "was dismayed by the party members' ignorance of even the basic rules of parliamentary discussion."

The movement's relationship to Western liberal thought was real but superficial in places — Itagaki's meeting with Herbert Spencer ended with Spencer crying "No, no, no!" and leaving the room in exasperation. The tragedy of the movement is that its most intellectually serious figure was Ueki Emori, who died at thirty-five in 1892 and who showed signs of genuine psychological instability alongside his remarkable political insights.

The movement remains significant as the first attempt at organized civilian political opposition in modern Japanese history. Its defeat in organizational terms did not erase its long-term institutional legacy.
