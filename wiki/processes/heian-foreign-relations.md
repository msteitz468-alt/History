# Heian Foreign Relations (794–1070)

Japan's relations with the other countries of East Asia during the [[heian-period|Heian
period]] were, in the *Cambridge History of Japan*'s formulation, "driven by the familiar
twin engines of fear of external power, on the one hand, and desire for material and
cultural gain on the other." The period saw the terminal reduction and eventual abandonment
of official Japanese embassies to Tang China, a decisive withdrawal from peninsular Korean
affairs, and the gradual replacement of state-managed cultural exchange with private
commercial channels. [[cambridge-history-japan-v2-heian]]

## Definition and Scope

This process covers the arc of Japanese official diplomacy from the founding of
[[heian-kyo|Heian-kyō]] (794) to approximately 1070, focusing on: (1) the relationship
with Tang and post-Tang China; (2) the Korean dimension via Silla and later Koryō;
and (3) the structural factors — geographic, fiscal, and political — that shaped
diplomatic choices.

## Causal Drivers

**Geographic insulation:** Japan's island location, "protected from the continent by
over a hundred miles of intervening sea," gave the Japanese court unusual freedom to
regulate its external relations according to internally generated needs. This is
a structural enabling factor absent from continental states.

**Fulfillment of the sinitic agenda:** By the compilation of the Taihō and Yōrō codes
at the start of the eighth century, the Japanese court had completed the main task
of assimilating Chinese governmental models. "It was no longer as necessary for
student-officials to undertake the long journey to the Chinese capital at Ch'ang-an in
search of the knowledge, books, and techniques required by the court's governmental
machinery."

**Tang dynasty decline:** From the mid-eighth century (An Lu-shan rebellion, 755) onward,
disorder in China progressively reduced the attraction of official contact. By 894, when
[[sugawara-michizane|Sugawara no Michizane]] recommended cancellation of the planned
embassy, "The Great T'ang is in a state of decline, and the emperor is not at court
[because of the rebellion] and foreign missions have ceased to come" — this was the
stated justification in his memorial.

**Rising cost and danger of embassies:** Each official embassy required several hundred
people and enormous expense. The sea crossing — increasingly via the dangerous southern
route across the East China Sea rather than the safer coastal Korean route (the latter
closed to Japanese vessels after Silla's hostility in the 660s) — was "notorious for
its great storms." By the ninth century, pirates made even the Seto Inland Sea leg
from Naniwa to Hakata unsafe.

**Private trade as an alternative:** By the ninth century, private merchants (Chinese
and others) were filling the material exchange function that official embassies had once
monopolized, at no cost to the court and without diplomatic risk.

## Major Phases

### Phase 1: Official Embassies at Long Intervals (630–838)

The sequence of official Japanese embassies to the Tang court numbered more than thirty
missions in total, including "sending-off" missions that accompanied Chinese envoys home.
Frequency declined steadily: from one every fifteen to sixteen years in the early eighth
century, to one every twenty-five years by the end of that century. Kammu sent his
one and only embassy to Tang in 804, twenty-seven years after the previous embassy of
777. The next and final Japanese embassy to Tang departed in 838.

### Phase 2: Michizane's Cancellation and Withdrawal (894)

In 894, Michizane was chosen to lead another embassy. Before departure he submitted his
celebrated memorial to Emperor Uda and the Council of State recommending cancellation,
which they accepted. The *Cambridge History* emphasizes that this "was not in intent a
particularly momentous decision" — official relations had been petering out for a century.
But "it turned out to be, in fact if not in intent, the end of the exchange of official
envoys between the two nations that had begun in 630."

Thereafter Japan's intercourse with China was "abandoned entirely to private hands, except
for a few exchanges of messages with the king of the southern Chinese coastal state of
Wu-yüeh around the middle of the tenth century and another exchange with the Sung court
in the 1070s."

### Phase 3: Private and Cultural Exchange (post-894)

The withdrawal from official diplomacy did not mean cultural or economic disengagement.
Private merchants — primarily Chinese — continued to bring continental goods and texts
to Japanese ports. Buddhist monks traveled between the countries. The flow of Chinese
cultural influence continued, now through private channels, fueling the development of
distinctively Japanese aesthetic forms even as the court drew inward.

## Korean Dimension

Japan's earliest substantial foreign relations were with the Korean peninsula. The
*Cambridge History* traces Japanese involvement from the fourth century (the contested
Mimana/Kaya territorial base) through military intervention supporting Paekche against
Silla and Koguryō, to the decisive **Battle of Hakusukinoe/Hakusuki Estuary** in 663,
when a Japanese fleet was crushed by Chinese Tang forces supporting Silla — ending
Japanese military presence in Korea for nine centuries.

After Silla's unification of the peninsula south of P'yŏngyang (with Koguryō territory
going to the Tungusic state of Parhae to the north), Japan's relations with Korea were
marked by residual hostility toward Silla, occasional alarm at rumors of Sillan or
Koguryō invasion (especially during the An Lu-shan rebellion period), and eventually
the termination of even those uneasy contacts as tenth-century upheavals swept away
the old peninsular states.

## End Conditions

The process of diplomatic withdrawal was essentially complete by 894. The subsequent
century of turmoil in China — the collapse of Tang (907) and the Five Dynasties period —
made resumed official relations structurally impossible. By the time of the Heian period's
close, Japan's engagement with the continent was entirely privatized and overwhelmingly
cultural rather than diplomatic or military.

## Historiography

The *Cambridge History* cites Charlotte von Verschuer's *Les Relations officielles du
Japon avec la Chine aux VIIIe et IXe siècles* (1985) as the main detailed study of
the eighth–ninth century diplomatic relationship. It notes disagreement between Korean
and Japanese historians about the nature and extent of early Japanese involvement in
Korean affairs, and acknowledges that its summary of the Korean dimension "owes more
to the commonly accepted Japanese interpretation."
