# Great Scandinavian Emigration

The mass transatlantic emigration of Scandinavians — overwhelmingly to the United
States — that began in earnest in the 1840s–60s and peaked later in the century.
Driven by the [[scandinavian-demographic-transition|demographic transition]]
(falling mortality, rapid population growth) pressing on limited agricultural
land, plus religious and political motives and cheap transport, it removed a large
share of the natural increase of Norway, Sweden, and Iceland in particular. The
volume treats its beginnings (chapter 38); the phenomenon is analogous to
[[irish-emigration-1800-1920|Irish emigration]] of the same era.

## Definition and Scope

CHS Vol. II (Limberg, ch. 38) treats the *beginnings* of the mass transatlantic
emigration to North America. Its scale was already disproportionate: between 1866
and 1870 Scandinavian emigration "accounted for about 10 per cent of the total
number of European migrants, but Scandinavia had only 3 per cent of the world's
population" (L34920–34922). Norway, Sweden and Denmark were "fairly early
participants" in mass emigration; Finland and Iceland followed only from the 1870s–80s.

## Causal Drivers

Three enabling conditions: rapid population growth and land shortage; new transport
infrastructure (steamboats, railways); and a receptive United States (L34859–34872).
Norway's population grew 13.4% per decade 1815–65 — "one of the fastest population
growths in Europe" (L35107–35110) — the demographic engine of departure (see
[[scandinavian-demographic-transition]]). Long coastlines and a seafaring tradition
gave easy port access and American commercial contacts (L34927–34934). Crop failures
(Sweden 1867+), religious motives, and the threatened Germanisation of Schleswig
after 1864 added powerful push factors.

## Major Phases

- **Pioneer phase (1825–1860s):** Norway led — the *Restauration* sailed from
  Stavanger in 1825 with 52 Quakers; emigration grew from hundreds in the 1830s to
  4,000 in 1849, mostly whole families from the southern and western mountain
  villages bound for Wisconsin (L35071–35131). Swedish emigration in this period was
  artisan-led and "modest," punctuated by religious colonies (Erik Jansson's Bishop
  Hill, Illinois, 1846).
- **Mass take-off (1866–70):** Crop failures pushed ~70,000 Swedes out in 1868–70
  (L35297–35298); by 1865 some 78,000 Norwegians had crossed (L35149). Denmark
  followed "a little later, a little calmer and considerably fewer" (L35311–35312),
  with Mormon emigration accounting for ~80% of Danish departures in its peak decade.

## Geographic Spread

Overwhelmingly the American Midwest — Wisconsin, Iowa, Illinois, Minnesota. Routes
ran via Göteborg/Hull/Liverpool (Swedes) and, after the 1849 repeal of the British
Navigation Acts, via Quebec on returning timber ships (44,000 Norwegians in the
decade to 1865). Internal European migration was a parallel safety valve: 25,000+
Swedes moved *into* Norway as industrial workers even as Norwegians emigrated
(L35149–35154).

## Interaction

Pull was amplified by the **Homestead Act (1862)** offering 160 free acres, by
railway land-grant marketing, by a North Atlantic shipping price war, and by
"prepaid tickets" sent home (at times over a third of sales, L35527–35528). The
"most believable medium" was letters from relatives — 100,000 a year reached
Denmark from the US by 1872 (L35444) — plus returned "yankees," emigrant handbooks,
and a booming press. States responded with emigrant-agent laws (Norway 1867,
Denmark 1868, Sweden 1869). The movement entwined with
[[scandinavian-nation-state-formation|nation-state formation]] and resembled
[[irish-emigration-1800-1920|Irish emigration]] of the same era.

## End Conditions

The chapter covers only the onset; the great waves crest later in the century.
Finland and Iceland — lacking an emigration tradition and (for Iceland) restrained
by Danish transport dependence and land/marriage law — joined only from the 1870s–80s
(L35160–35195, L35363–35381).
