# Urban Change in Merovingian Gaul

**The contraction, Christianization, and administrative reinvention of the Gallic city.** S. T. Loseby's
chapter (Oxford Handbook, Ch. 27), with Luc Bourgeois's on secondary centres (Ch. 28), traces how the
Roman *civitas* survived as the irreducible unit of Frankish government even as the physical and economic
city shrank — its forum-and-baths core giving way to walls, episcopal complexes, and suburban cult sites.

## Definition and Scope

"The *civitas*, city and territory, was the irreducible unit of Frankish administration." Of the 113
*civitates* listed in the *Notitia Galliarum* (c. 400), **108 became episcopal seats**, fusing
ecclesiastical and royal administrative geography. Royal power in each was exercised through the
**bishop–count dyad**: the *comes civitatis* as the king's fiscal and military agent, the bishop as
controller "of spiritual, social, and much economic life" (see [[merovingian-episcopate]],
[[merovingian-polity]]).

## Major Phases

- **Late-Roman restructuring.** Gallic cities peaked in the second century; through the third–fifth
  centuries they contracted dramatically, reorganized "around walls and churches" rather than civic
  cores.
- **Fiscal decay.** The land tax (*tributum/census*), still operative around 500, "decay[ed] through the
  7th century as aristocratic immunity and ecclesiastical exemptions widened" — removing a key reason to
  maintain urban administrative infrastructure (see [[merovingian-economy]]).
- **Royal abandonment of cities as seats.** Unlike their late-Roman predecessors, "Merovingian kings
  progressively shifted royal presence to rural palaces (*villae regiae*), visiting cities for
  ceremonial or judicial purposes rather than residing in them."
- **The loss of secondary centres.** Roughly **40 cities** that had held administrative rank dropped out
  of the network (Jublains, Vermand, Cimiez, Maguelone), becoming small settlements or hilltop refuges
  (see [[merovingian-rural-settlement]]).

## A Religious Construction Boom

Demographic decline did not mean inertia: by the end of the period **over 800 urban and suburban
religious establishments** — churches, monasteries, oratories — are documented across Francia, the
material correlate of [[merovingian-religious-architecture]]. The city was being remade as a Christian
rather than a classical civic space.

## End Conditions

The Merovingian city was neither the dense classical *polis* nor yet the Carolingian-era settlement; its
trajectory of contraction, episcopal dominance, and Christian rebuilding set the urban frame within which
later medieval towns would grow. The decline of urban centres also helps explain the eighth-century
withering of hospital foundation (see [[early-medieval-hospitals-and-charity]]) and the disappearance of
Roman mass-butchery (see [[farming-and-diet-in-merovingian-gaul]]).
