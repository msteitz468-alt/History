## Definition and Scope

Islam in China is the long process by which Muslim communities became established,
integrated, and periodically contested within Chinese political and cultural life,
from initial overland and maritime contact in the seventh century through the
present-day politics of Hui and Uyghur Muslim identity. It encompasses both the
Sinicized "Hui" Muslim population of China's interior provinces and the
ethnically-distinct Turkic Muslim population of the Tarim Basin/Xinjiang (see
[[uyghurs]]), which followed a substantially different historical trajectory tied to
Central Asian Islamization via the [[qarakhanid-khanate]] and [[chaghatay-khanate]]
rather than to overland Silk Road merchant settlement in China's interior.

This process is distinct from, but linked to, the general overland expansion of
Islam into Central Asia (see [[silk-roads]]) and from the maritime Islamization of
Southeast Asia and coastal China via Indian Ocean trade.

## Causal Drivers

- **Overland trade and Abbasid-era contact.** Islam traveled overland with the
  expansion of the [[abbasid-caliphate]] and its affiliated Islamized Turkic
  dynasties from the ninth century, moving from Khorasan and Transoxiana eastward
  into the Tarim Basin.
- **Mongol integration under the Yuan.** The Bukharan official Sayyid 'Ajall Shams
  al-Din (d. 1279) was appointed Mongol governor of Yunnan, enabling Chinese Muslims
  to build mosques and practice openly without any state Islamization agenda; Yuan
  patronage established medical academies in Beijing staffed by Muslim physicians and
  translated Islamic pharmaceutical texts into Chinese.
- **Astronomical and calendrical exchange.** Islamic astronomical methods and tables
  were adapted using Chinese observational data to produce court calendars; the
  Ming dynasty's official history under the Hongwu Emperor cites Islamic astronomy
  as a source of legitimacy, and Ming–Qing "Bureaus for the Administration of the
  Heavens" merged Chinese and Muslim astronomical traditions into the *Huihui li*
  (Islamic Calendar), copies of which survive in Ming and Qing official histories, a
  Korean Yi-dynasty history, and a Tibetan manuscript glossing Arabic text in
  Mongolian and Tibetan.
- **Intellectual synthesis: the Han Kitab tradition.** Chinese Muslim scholars
  translated Islamic law and ritual into Confucian idiom. Wang Daiyu (fl. 1570s–1650s)
  and Liu Zhi (c. 1670–1724) are the tradition's central figures; Liu Zhi's 1710
  *Selected Explanation of the Norms and Rites of Islam* argued that the Islamic and
  Confucian ethical orders shared the same underlying *dao* — "Once the five pillars
  are accomplished, the dao of heaven is fulfilled." Zvi Ben-Dor Benite's scholarship
  on the Han Kitab is cited by Formichi as a key framing of Chinese Islam as neither
  simple assimilation nor simple foreignness.
- **Qing-era migration and territorial consolidation.** Mass Han Chinese migration
  into Yunnan in the 18th–19th centuries created tension with established local
  Muslim communities, while Qing assertion of control over the Tarim Basin sharpened
  the conceptual and administrative distinction between "Hui" (Sinophone Muslims of
  the interior provinces) and the Turkic Muslims of the Tarim Basin — the ethnic
  boundary line underlying the modern Uyghur/Hui distinction.

## Major Phases

1. **Initial contact and foreign-community status (7th–13th c.)** — Muslim traders
   settle in Chinese port and inland cities; described in Ming-era Chinese sources
   through Marco Lipman's later phrase, Muslims remaining "familiar strangers."
2. **Yuan integration (13th–14th c.)** — Muslim technocrats, physicians, and
   astronomers hold prominent state roles under Mongol rule; Sayyid 'Ajall's
   governorship of Yunnan.
3. **Ming–Qing synthesis (16th–18th c.)** — the Han Kitab intellectual tradition
   (Wang Daiyu, Liu Zhi) articulates a Confucian-compatible Chinese Islam; continued
   calendrical/astronomical exchange via the Huihui li.
4. **Qing-era estrangement and ethnic differentiation (18th–19th c.)** — Han
   migration into Muslim-populated Yunnan and consolidation of Qing control over the
   Tarim Basin harden the Hui/Turkic-Muslim (proto-Uyghur) distinction.
5. **Contemporary halal politics and Xinjiang repression (20th–21st c.)** — a
   thriving domestic Hui-linked halal market (c. US$20 billion, dwarfed by ASEAN's
   projected $280 billion) coexists with state-directed segregation, suppression,
   and mass incarceration of Uyghur Muslims in Xinjiang, alongside a Chinese
   social-media "halalification" (*qing zhen fa hua*) backlash movement calling for
   boycotts of halal products. See [[uyghurs]] for the ethnic-group-specific
   contemporary detail.

## Geographic Spread

From coastal trade entrepôts and the Yunnan frontier in the south, to the historic
Silk Road corridor and Beijing in the north and east, to the Tarim Basin/Xinjiang in
the far west — the geographic breadth of the process corresponds to the underlying
Hui/Uyghur distinction: Hui communities are dispersed across the interior (notably
Ningxia, Gansu, Qinghai, Shaanxi provinces), while Turkic Muslim communities are
concentrated in Xinjiang.

## Interaction

This process intersects with the [[silk-roads]] (as the vector for early overland
contact and continued Central Asian exchange), the [[mongol-empire]] and its
successor [[chaghatay-khanate]] (as the channel by which Islam became dominant among
Turkic Central Asians adjoining Xinjiang), and the broader expansion of Islam into
Central Asia via the [[qarakhanid-khanate]]. It is deliberately kept distinct in this
wiki from the maritime Islamization of Southeast Asia and coastal Chinese ports,
which followed Indian Ocean trade routes rather than the overland Central Asian
corridor.

## End Conditions

The process has not ended; it continues today in an acute and contested form. Rather
than concluding, it has bifurcated into two distinct contemporary trajectories: an
economically integrated, state-tolerated Hui Muslim commercial and religious life
(centered on the domestic and export halal trade) and a severely repressed Uyghur
Muslim population in Xinjiang subject to state segregation, suppression, and
incarceration. Formichi frames the tension between China's pursuit of global halal
market share and its domestic suppression of visible Uyghur Islamic identity as the
process's central contemporary contradiction.
