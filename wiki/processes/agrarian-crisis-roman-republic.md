# The Agrarian Crisis of the Roman Republic

The slow transformation of Italian agriculture and rural society in the second
century BCE — the displacement of the small peasant farm by large, slave-worked
estates — which built toward the political explosion of 133. Treated in
[[astin-cah-v8-1989|CAH VIII]] Ch. 7 (Gabba), with the political symptoms in Ch. 6
(Astin).

## Definition and Scope

The crisis is **not** a single event but a process, uneven across regions, that
undermined the traditional foundation of Roman society — the **citizen
farmer-soldier** on a small holding supplemented by common land. Gabba is careful
to qualify the classic (Appianic) picture: the conversion of arable to pasture and
the ruin of the peasantry happened in *some* areas (notably Apulia), not uniformly;
**depopulation** from the Hannibalic War was as much a driver as displacement.

## Causal Drivers

1. **The expansion of *ager publicus*.** After the Hannibalic War Rome confiscated
   vast tracts (Beloch's estimate ~10,000 km²) from the allied communities that had
   defected — above all the entire **ager Campanus**. The Roman state lacked the
   capacity to survey or administer this land, so much of it was occupied,
   legally or illegally, by wealthy Roman and Italian *possessores* — the confusion
   the Gracchan commission would later have to untangle.
2. **The inflow of conquest wealth**, seeking the most secure and prestigious
   investment: land. The elite enlarged holdings, introduced specialised "industrial"
   crops (the **Catonian villa** of the *De agri cultura*), and extended grazing.
3. **Slave labour.** The wars of conquest poured enslaved people into Italy;
   slave-worked estates and ranches (*latifundia*) displaced free tenants and
   labourers.
4. **Depopulation and the military burden.** War losses, the flight to the cities,
   and above all the **long conscript service** of the period's continuous wars
   (Spain especially) pulled small farmers off their land for years, accelerating
   abandonment.

## Major Phases

- **Concealment (c. 200–160s).** The strains were masked: the peasantry was filling
  a post-war vacuum rather than being supplanted; conquest wealth circulated through
  building, donatives, and public works; colonization (Bononia, Aquileia, the
  southern citizen colonies) offered an outlet — until colonial foundations dried up
  in the 170s.
- **The law *de modo agrorum*** (post-Hannibalic): a cap of **500 *iugera*** on
  individual occupation of public land and limits on public grazing (100 cattle /
  500 smaller stock), with a requirement to use free labour. Almost wholly
  unenforced for lack of administrative machinery.
- **Manifestation (150s–133).** The recruiting crises, the Sicilian **slave revolt**
  (from 136), C. Laelius' abortive land proposal (140), and finally the agrarian law
  of **Tiberius Gracchus (133)** — the recovery and redistribution of *ager
  publicus* above the 500-*iugera* limit.

## Interaction

The crisis is the structural underside of [[roman-aristocratic-politics|mid-Republican
politics]] (Astin Ch. 6): for decades social-economic disparity was *not* a major
political factor; by the 130s it had become the issue that would interlock with the
erosion of the *cursus* conventions to produce the Gracchan cataclysm. It is also
entangled with the [[roman-italian-alliance|allies' question]] — much confiscated
*ager publicus* was being worked by **Italian allies** under their treaties, so the
Gracchan recovery would violate their "rights and treaties" (*iura ac foedera*).

## End Conditions

The process did not end in 133 — it drove a century of agrarian and military reform
(the Gracchi, Marius' professionalised army, the Social War, the late-Republican land
settlements). CAH VIII closes on its threshold; the Gracchi themselves belong to
CAH IX.
