## Definition and Scope

The Restoration period in Ireland (1660–88) was shaped, above all, by the need to determine what would become of the Cromwellian land settlement — the most comprehensive expropriation in early modern European history. The question of land structured political debate, drove confessional conflict, and defined the formation of the new Protestant landowning elite that would dominate Ireland through the eighteenth century. As Ted McCormick notes in [[jackson-oxford-modern-irish-history-2014]], Restoration Ireland "lacks a clear profile of its own" in Irish historiography, often viewed merely as aftermath of the Cromwellian conquest or prelude to the Jacobite crisis — yet recent scholarship demonstrates it as a period of genuine and distinct transformation.

The Restoration was not simply about land, however. It witnessed the rapid commercialisation of the Irish economy, the emergence of Dublin as a metropolitan centre with population reaching 45,000 by 1685, the consolidation of denominational Protestant communities (Anglican, Presbyterian, and dissenting), the growth of Atlantic trade, and the formation of a self-consciously "improving" provincial Protestant elite.

## Causal Drivers

**The land settlement**: The fact that the Restoration in Ireland was effected primarily by beneficiaries of Cromwellian confiscation made restoration of the pre-war status quo impossible. Charles II's "Gracious Declaration" (November 1660) attempted to satisfy incompatible claims through a classification system (various categories of "innocents," "49 officers," nominees) while confirming Cromwellian adventurers and soldiers in their holdings. The resulting Acts of Settlement (1662) and Explanation (1665) consolidated Protestant proprietorship while restoring a small minority of Catholic claimants. By c.1675, Catholics held approximately 29% of profitable land — down from 66% in 1641 and 56% in 1660.

**English court politics**: The decisive variable in Irish political change was consistently English ministerial politics rather than Irish agency. Ormond's removal in 1669 reflected Clarendon's fall in England (1667). The regium donum, concessions to Catholics in 1672, and the Popish Plot crisis of 1678–81 all originated in English political dynamics and played out in Ireland through the viceroy's patronage.

**Confessional plurality**: Unlike England, Ireland had a Protestant establishment church that represented a demographic minority, a large Gaelic Catholic majority, a substantial and growing Ulster Presbyterian community, and smaller dissenting communities (Quakers, Independents, Baptists). Managing these competing communities without the leverage of demographic Protestant dominance or consistent royal policy shaped every viceroyalty.

## Major Phases

### Land Settlement and Confessional Politics (1660–1669)

The Dublin Convention (March–May 1660), dominated by Protestant beneficiaries of the settlement, galvanized around the land question and welcomed the monarchy's restoration as their best bulwark against a "restive and resentful Catholic majority." The Act of Settlement (1662) attempted to classify claimants; the Court of Claims (January–August 1663) issued hundreds of decrees of innocence, swiftly exhausting the pool of available land for compensation and provoking Protestant fury. The Act of Explanation (1665) imposed a one-third "retrenchment" on interregnum grantees to fund "reprisals" to innocents.

Catholic political ambitions found expression in the "Loyal Formulary" or "Remonstrance" (December 1661, drafted by Confederate veteran Sir Richard Bellings): an oath of secular loyalty designed to demonstrate Catholic political reliability and potentially recover 1649 terms. Supported by Franciscan Peter Walsh, signed by nearly one hundred nobles and gentry, it was welcomed by Charles II but ultimately destroyed by Catholic clerical opposition from Rome and from archbishops Peter Talbot (Dublin) and Oliver Plunkett (Armagh), who found its implications for papal authority unacceptable.

Ormond's viceroyalty ended in 1669 with the fall of his English patron Clarendon.

### The Liberal Interlude (1669–1677)

Successive lords lieutenant (Robartes, Berkeley, Essex) operated under Charles II's more tolerant English dispensation — capped by the 1672 Declaration of Indulgence — relaxing exclusion of Catholics from towns, bar, and local office, and providing a *regium donum* to Presbyterian ministers. Essex faced the "Ranelagh undertaking," a revenue farm that privatised the exchequer (1671–82) and transferred fiscal control to Whitehall. Factional politics between Orrery and Ormond drove successive viceregal crises.

### Popish Plot and Ormond's Second Viceroyalty (1677–1685)

The Popish Plot fabricated by Titus Oates in 1678 was, as McCormick observes, "in Ireland, a non-event" — yet generated intense anxiety about Irish Catholic rebellion. Ormond's opponents raised the spectre of 1641, republishing Edmund Borlase's and Sir John Temple's histories. Oliver Plunkett was arrested and eventually hanged at Tyburn in July 1681 as the plot's Irish victim. The principal legacy was to "lash Protestant identity still more tightly to the memory and commemoration of popish rebellion."

Ormond's major initiative — a Commission of Grace for defective land titles — was abandoned with James II's accession in February 1685.

### Catholicization under James II (1685–1688)

Richard Talbot (Earl of Tyrconnell from 1685, Lord Deputy from November 1686) executed James II's programme of extending Catholic access to military and civil office at unprecedented speed. Protestant army officers were purged; Catholics appointed as judges and sheriffs; borough charters recalled and reissued with Catholic-majority compositions. Draft legislation revising the land settlement appeared. By 1688, the Catholic "new interest" — Catholics who had purchased land under the Restoration settlement — held approximately 30% of profitable land. Tyrconnell's acceleration of Catholic political power triggered the Protestant resistance that produced the [[war-of-the-three-kingdoms-ireland-1689-1691]].

## Economic and Social Development

The Irish economy underwent complex growth across the period. Following the prohibition of live cattle exports to England (1663, 1667), butter and beef emerged as major export commodities, reaching continental Europe and the Atlantic colonies. By 1683, England received only 30% of Irish exports — down from 74% in 1665. Cork's population tripled between 1659 and 1685 (reaching c.20,000), becoming Ireland's second port through Atlantic trade in colonial provisions, sugar, tobacco, and finished wool.

Dublin grew from c.30,000 in 1660 to 45,000 in 1685 and over 60,000 by the early eighteenth century — the "second city of the empire." The capital hosted a Theatre Royal in Smock Alley (built 1662), rival printers, literary circles at Dublin Castle, and in 1683 the Dublin Philosophical Society (offspring of the Royal Society), under the intellectual influence of Robert Boyle and others. William Petty's quantitative ambitions for Ireland — mapping, political arithmetic, improvement — exemplified the era's aspirational social engineering.

Rural commercialisation accelerated: relations between tenants and landlords were monetised; the proto-gentry class built country houses and consumed Continental fashions. The Ulster town of Strabane, swelled by Scottish migration, supported glovers, wigmakers, weavers, bakers, and tailors alongside a merchant class with Dublin connections.

## Plural Communities

Despite the plantation-era dispossession of Catholics, the outstanding characteristic of Restoration Ireland was "the formation and conjugation of distinct confessional and cultural communities in the midst of a commercialising provincial society" (McCormick). 

**Catholics**: Cross-cut by fading Old English/Gaelic Irish ethnic divisions and intra-confessional competition; sustained by Continental trade, military service, and an increasingly reorganised hierarchy (Oliver Plunkett, Peter Talbot as Archbishops). Gaelic Irish-language poetry (Dáibhí Ó Bruadair) lamented the dispossession of a worthy aristocracy. Old English political thought — exemplified by contrasting Geoffrey Keating's *Foras Feasa ar Éirinn* and John Lynch's *Cambrensis Eversus* (1662) — tried to articulate an Irish Catholic identity compatible with the new order.

**Quakers**: From thirty meeting places in 1660 to over fifty by 1701. Built a self-policing denominational identity through meeting records, mutual aid, and travelling ministers. Anthony Sharp (Dublin merchant-Quaker) played a major economic role. The period of consolidation enabled Quakers to survive periodic persecution through networks extending to England and America.

**Presbyterians**: Concentrated in Ulster, torn between the legacy of the Solemn League and Covenant and the need to accommodate an episcopal establishment. The regium donum (1672 onwards) provided modest state support without official recognition. Presbyterian meetings were sometimes suppressed (1682–6) but the community emerged as a de facto denominational church with purpose-built meeting houses, communal history, and fast days.

**Episcopalian Church of Ireland**: Never achieved demographic dominance; plagued by clerical pluralism and inadequate income from alienated tithe and glebes. Reform lobby emerged under William III; convocation finally summoned in 1703 became a battleground between "high" and "low Church" factions mirroring English party politics.

## Historiography

No comprehensive single-author monograph covers Restoration Ireland as a distinct period. Key interpretive surveys include David Dickson (*New Foundations: Ireland 1660–1800*, 1987), Raymond Gillespie (*Seventeenth-Century Ireland: Making Ireland Modern*, 2006), and S.J. Connolly (*Divided Kingdom: Ireland 1630–1800*, 2008). McCormick characterises the scholarship as weighted heavily toward the land settlement and politics, with economic, social, and cultural dimensions increasingly addressed through regional studies (Dickson on Cork and south Munster; Gillespie on reading and print culture; William Smyth on landscape geography) and essay collections.

A distinctively revisionist current (Connolly, Barnard) places Restoration Ireland within a comparative ancien régime framework, questioning whether Protestant dominance was uniquely oppressive by contemporary European standards. This reading has been contested by historians who emphasise the colonial character of Irish society and the experience of the Catholic majority.
