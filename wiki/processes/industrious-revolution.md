# The Industrious Revolution

## Definition and Scope

The **"industrious revolution"** names the early modern reorganization of work
and consumption by which households **worked harder and longer, and relied
increasingly on markets**, in the centuries before mechanized industry. Kaoru
Sugihara and R. Bin Wong (CWH VI) compare the concept's two originally separate
genealogies and argue that an industrious revolution occurred in **both Europe
and East Asia**, though situated in contrasting social structures.

- **The European usage (Jan de Vries).** A **demand-side** change: expanding
  consumption despite roughly flat real wages, which required people to sacrifice
  leisure and work longer to buy newly desirable commercial (and exotic overseas)
  goods — a "consumer revolution" preparing the ground for the Industrial
  Revolution.
- **The East Asian usage (Hayami Akira, for Japan).** A **supply-side** change:
  the skills and hard work cultivated in the labor-intensive agrarian household,
  which shaped a distinctive, labor-intensive path of later industrialization.

Sugihara and Wong include **China** (especially the Yangzi Delta/Jiangnan) as the
bridging case — like Europe, a vast commercial economy; like Japan, a
peasant-and-rice household economy — to show that the term applies, with
variations, across early modern world regions.

## The Seventeenth-Century Crisis Context

The process unfolded against the **general crisis** of the seventeenth century:
the **Little Ice Age** (worst c.1640s–1690s) brought harvest failures and unrest
across Eurasia. Europe met this through **inter-state competition** (war,
mercantilism, overseas extraction of silver and plantation goods); China through
an **imperial commitment to domestic order** (low transit taxes, granaries,
promotion of commerce across an area the size of Europe); Japan, unified under the
**Tokugawa** in 1600, was less affected. As conditions improved in the eighteenth
century, the industrious revolution's demand-side (Europe) and supply-side (East
Asia) features became visible.

## The Argument: A Shared Phenomenon, Divergent Paths

Sugihara and Wong refute the claim (de Vries) that East Asians worked harder only
out of **constraint** (Philip Huang's "involution" thesis of Malthusian
self-exploitation; the supposed absence of labor markets). Drawing on Pomeranz,
Li, van Zanden, and Allen, they show that **Yangzi Delta agricultural
productivity was ~90 percent of England's c.1800**, and Jiangnan female textile
incomes were comparable to British ones — so Chinese peasants were not driven by
exceptional poverty but, like Europeans, by the **desire for commercial goods**.
Indeed, there is **better direct evidence of East Asian than European** households
working more, since East Asian intensification took place visibly within rural
households (women weaving cotton, integrated silk-and-fish-pond production) rather
than in factories.

The decisive **difference** lay not in industriousness but in:

1. **Wages.** Europe's most advanced economies (England, the Netherlands) were
   **high-wage**, which gave England the incentive to develop **labor-saving
   machinery** (Allen) — the start of industrialization as cotton-textile import
   substitution. China and most of Europe were low-wage.
2. **Political economy.** European commercial capitalism combined domestic markets
   with **profits extracted overseas** (silver, plantation goods at below-wage-
   labor prices). East Asians, lacking such extraction, had to be *more*
   industrious to gain the same commercial consumption.

The upshot is **two paths to the modern economy**: a Western **capital-intensive**
path and an East Asian **labor-intensive** path — a framing central to the
[[great-divergence|Great Divergence]] debate. Sugihara extends this argument into
the industrial era in [[mcneill-cwh-v7-2015|CWH VII]]: the labor-intensive household
economy of monsoon Asia became the basis of a distinct **labor-intensive
[[industrialization]]** that, after 1950, diffused faster than the Western path and
drove the partial reversal of the divergence (see [[great-acceleration]]).

## Interaction

The industrious revolution links the [[early-modern-commerce|organization of
trade]], the [[global-silver-trade|silver-monetized]] consumer economy, and the
[[plantation-economy|plantation complex]] (whose cheap goods fed Europe's consumer
revolution). It is the labor-and-consumption dimension of the
[[great-divergence|Great Divergence]] and a corrective to Eurocentric accounts of
the origins of modern growth (see [[eurocentrism]]).
