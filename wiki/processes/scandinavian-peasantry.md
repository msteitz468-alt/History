## Definition and Scope

The social and economic situation of the Scandinavian commoners — primarily peasants, burghers, civil servants, and a growing class of smallholders/cottars — from roughly 1650 to 1750. "The great majority of the population, between 80 per cent and almost 100 per cent over the whole of Scandinavia, were linked to peasant households." (L23655–23656) This page covers tenure relations, the freeholder/tenant divide, the stavnsbånd, the rise of cottars, and the political culture of peasant society.

The period 1650–1750 saw "the culmination and end of one epoch and the beginning of a new one. The authoritarian social order which was built up during the previous centuries, with aristocratic, hierarchical and orthodox religious values, reached its culmination at the end of the seventeenth century." (L23208–23212)

## Early medieval background — Wickham, Framing (2005)

The 1650–1750 material below sits on a much longer rural baseline. [[wickham-framing-the-early-middle-ages-2005|Wickham, *Framing* (2005)]] argues that for **Denmark 400–800** (and some time after), aristocratic **landed** power over peasant neighbours had **not** been established—unlike inland England under Offa. Danish ‘chieftains’ on magnate farms (*stormandsgårder*) look like political leaders receiving restricted tribute from relatively independent village peasantries, not landlords; no archaeological signs in Wickham’s period of a shift to landed proprietorship. Peasant subjection may wait until ~tenth/eleventh centuries; local assemblies persist. Godofrid/Horic’s kingdom resembled Ine more than Offa; permanence of larger polities was blocked until after ~950, when processes paralleled Offan England (conquest, public works, tribute regularisation, aristocratic property crystallisation).

**Settlement hierarchy:** unlike early medieval western Mediterranean patterns where villages often sit near the top of local hierarchy, Danish villages are at the **base**: political elites lived in central places and isolated magnate farms, leaving villages to the peasantry—so Vorbasse-type autonomy was real but bounded by always-present wider political structures ([[settlement-archaeology]]).

**North European village belt:** Wickham proposes a common village structure from the **Rhine mouth to the Swedish border** across 400–800: concentrated settlements of modular farmsteads (long-house + smaller buildings; often ~10–20 farms; max ~200–400 people north of the imperial frontier), internal part-time crafts and hierarchies, little permanent external/internal lordship—effective foci comparable to Syrian/Egyptian villages though smaller (parallels Feddersen Wierde, Wijster, Warendorf). This early medieval peasantry-mode baseline is a structural contrast, not a continuous institutional history, with the freeholder/tenant and estate world of 1650–1750 ([[peasant-mode-of-production]], [[scandinavian-urbanisation]]).

## Causal Drivers

**Absolutism and the delegation of administration to estate-owners.** After 1660 in Denmark, the crown delegated tax collection and conscription to landowners: "Instead of building up a costly civil service system, as in Sweden, one could in fact reach more or less all the peasants by utilising the landed proprietors as an unpaid labour force." (L23820–23823) This bound Danish peasants more closely to the estate than their Norwegian or Swedish counterparts.

**The reduktion in Sweden.** Karl XI's 1680 reduktion reversed noble land alienation: "In 1680 the nobility controlled about two-thirds of the taxable value of Swedish land. In the 1720s the figure had dropped to one-third." (L23727–23729) This improved peasant fiscal conditions and created a political dividend: "the taxes on the peasantry in principle did not increase, and thereby a certain margin could be created for an increase in the peasant household's own surplus." (L23736–23737)

**Population growth and land pressure.** Rising post-1720 population without corresponding urban absorption created downward pressure: "So we see, as in Denmark, the appearance of a class of smallholders." (L20967) In Sweden, "83 per cent of population growth after 1720 took place in the agricultural sector." (L21319–21320)

**Export-led commercialisation** (timber, fish, iron) in Norway, Sweden, and Finland created ancillary incomes and differentiated peasants from cottars. In Norway, timber and fisheries functioned within a credit chain dominated by an urban patriciate: "The relationship between merchants and fishermen might well be called one of mutual dependence, but it was also one in which the balance of power was very one-sided." (L21732–21734)

**The stavnsbånd (1733).** Introduced in Denmark as an agricultural-crisis measure, this ascription law "laid down that male peasants between 14 and 36 years of age were not allowed to leave their estate." (L20562–20563) Combined with the corn monopoly that kept Danish prices high and limited Norwegian food security, the stavnsbånd became the most notorious symbol of tenant subjection; it was abolished in 1788.

## Major Phases

**c.1650–1720: Consolidation of tenant and estate structure.** In Denmark in 1700, around 60,000 peasant farms existed under some 700 manors; nearly all had become tenant farms, with freeholders reduced to c.1,700 (L20267–20271). In Sweden the crown owned roughly one-third of the land (crown land); another third was noble land (frälsejord); a third was tax land (freehold: L21100–21105). In Norway, villages were "as good as non-existent"; the cornerstone was the individual farm (L20922).

**1720–1750: Agricultural crises, government response.** A price crisis from the mid-1720s hit Danish agriculture; a devastating cattle disease struck 1745–52 (L20487–20492). Government response in Denmark mixed relief for estates with the stavnsbånd and corn monopoly. In Norway, three waves of peasant disturbances (1712/14, 1763–5, 1786–7) were responses to taxation: the ritual pattern of escalation (legal complaint → delegation to København → uprising) and containment (draconian sentences later reduced, triggering government concessions) is documented in detail (L20804–20822).

**Freehold transition underway.** In Norway freehold rose from c.20 per cent in 1661 to 33 per cent by 1721 and 57 per cent by 1801 (L20834–20838). Crown land sales in 1728 at Snåsa, Trøndelag, saw burghers buy cheap from the crown and resell to peasants "sometimes for up to three times as much as they had paid for it" — yet many peasants could afford this (L23795–23799). In Sweden, skatteköp (purchase of crown land as freehold) were regulated from 1723 and after 1750 the majority of purchasers were peasants (L23760–23762). By 1772 tax land (freehold) reached 47 per cent of Swedish land value (L23758).

**Dalarna Uprising of 1743** — the last great peasant uprising in Sweden. Triggered by conscription demands and linked to political grievances during the Age of Liberty, it was remarkable for its quasi-legal, non-violent character: "They marched on Stockholm, but in good military order." Only six leaders were executed; the other estates subsequently moderated demands; and the peasant estate was admitted to the Secret Committee (L23853–23888).

## Geographic Spread

**Denmark**: Dominated by the estate system; roughly 95 per cent of peasants were tenants in 1700. The manorial labour service (hoveri) and the stavnsbånd were the defining features. Sjælland had the harshest conditions; Funen the best; Jylland varied. By c.1810, two-thirds of Danish peasant farms had converted to freehold, but smallholders and landless labourers were "left high and dry by the countryside reforms" (L20748).

**Norway**: No comparable estate system; fewer than a handful of large manors. Social relations were structured between farmers and cottars (husmann), between farmers themselves, and between generations. Norwegian peasants had more freedom than Danish and were taxed comparatively less — partly because the crown lacked the administrative lever of the manor (L20823–20832). The state sold monastery lands from 1750 with existing users given first refusal "for a very modest sum" (L20917–20921), accelerating freehold.

**Sweden**: Three land categories (tax land, crown land, noble land) divided roughly equally in 1700 shifted over the century toward private ownership (freehold 32 per cent → 54 per cent, 1680–1825; crown land 36 per cent → 13 per cent: L21126–21128). Crofters (torpare) proliferated sharply, replacing servants as the key rural labour class: "The numbers of crofters rose sharply throughout the eighteenth century" (L21354). A social hierarchy descended from freeholders to crown tenants to crofters to backstugusittare (cottars in huts) to the emerging statare (estate labourers paid in kind) (L21394–21396).

**Finland**: Fewer noble estates than Sweden but a quasi-stavnsbånd existed (servants forbidden to leave the province until 1766: L21471–21472). A prohibition on farm division in 1739 was relaxed in 1747; smallholders grew by 16 per cent 1749–1805 (L21478). Burn-and-beat agriculture and joint family households persisted longer in eastern Finland than elsewhere.

**Iceland**: Virtually no freehold farmers or burghers; officials formed the social elite; almost all rural population was tenant (L23645–23647). The Icelandic case was structurally anomalous — a pastoral and fishing economy with no manorial administration.

## Interaction

- **Demographics**: The proletarianisation of the countryside fed into the proliferation of cottar/smallholder households (see [[scandinavian-family-demography-1650-1815]]). Norway's smallholder ratio to farmers rose from 0.18 (1723) to 0.51 (1801) (L20990–20994).
- **Absolutism and the nobility** (see [[scandinavian-absolutism]]): The introduction of absolutism in Denmark (1660) and Sweden (1680) decisively shaped tenure structures. In Denmark it delegated administration to estate owners; in Sweden it reversed noble alienation via reduktion.
- **Gender**: Women managed the domestic sphere as "an independent authority over the housework in a narrow sense: the home, food preparation, the children, the domestic animals, except the horses" (L23242–23244), but remained legally subordinate and unable to act in court without male authority. Court records, however, document actual practice diverging widely from the letter of the law (L23252–23263).
- **Urban economy**: The distinction between "centripetal" (large, labour-attracting farm) and "centrifugal" (small, labour-exporting cottar) households is a structural hinge linking rural social organisation to urban trades and the servant economy.

## End Conditions

By c.1750 the "authoritarian social order … with aristocratic, hierarchical and orthodox religious values" had reached its zenith and was beginning to erode. The period 1750–1800 (covered in ch.22 of the source) saw:
- accelerating enclosure and freehold conversion (udskiftning/udflytning in Denmark; storskifte in Sweden);
- abolition of the stavnsbånd (1788) and agricultural reforms across Scandinavia;
- gradual emergence of a bourgeois middle class replacing aristocratic cultural norms as the social ideal;
- continued rural proletarianisation, eventually driving nineteenth-century emigration from Sweden and Norway.

The peasantry remained "hedervärda" (honourable) in Swedish parliamentary language — the lowest estate but essential to all others — through the nineteenth century, when their political weight would increase substantially.

## Historiography

Harald Gustafsson's analysis in the source (ch.24) emphasises the heterogeneity of "commoners": Swedish peasant freeholders buying crown land had little in common socially or culturally with the bourgeois wholesaler entertaining King Frederik V of Denmark. Swedish historian Sten Carlsson's formulation — "All four estates were political and social realities within the frame that Absolutism admitted" — captures the persistence of estate-based thinking even as it dissolved economically. Modern research underlines that the Danish estate system created "a certain patriarchally tinged solidarity within the estate" rather than systematic exploitation, complicating simple oppression narratives (L23834–23836). Norwegian historian Øystein Rian's concept of ämbetsköpmän (office-merchants) — where the same families held offices, leased customs, operated sawmills, and ran shipping firms — illuminates the intertwining of social categories in Norway (L23635–23639).
