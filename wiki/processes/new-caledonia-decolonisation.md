# New Caledonia Decolonisation Process

## Definition and Scope

The decolonisation process in New Caledonia (Kanaky) is the long-running contest over sovereignty, political status, and Indigenous rights that has structured the archipelago's politics since French annexation in 1853. Unlike most of the Pacific region, where formal decolonisation was substantially complete by the 1980s, New Caledonia remains constitutionally part of France; the Kanak people — the archipelago's Indigenous inhabitants whose ancestors arrived approximately 3,000 years ago (c. 1000 BCE) — remain a demographic minority in their own land.

The process is structurally distinctive in three respects: (1) it operates under a formal constitutional-legal framework (the Nouméa Accord of 1998) that schedules gradual power transfers and limits referenda; (2) it involves not two but multiple competing visions of what 'decolonisation' means; and (3) it features a Kanak population that is simultaneously a demographic minority (41.2% as of 2019), a majority in two of three provinces, and the undisputed Indigenous first peoples of the archipelago.

## Causal Drivers

Colonisation began with French annexation on 24 September 1853 under Napoleon III. The colonial process involved 'massive land confiscation and displacement onto reserves, a process, in part, enabled by the ravages of introduced disease.' The Kanak population reached its nadir in the 1890s. Economic drivers included nickel extraction (still the backbone of the modern economy) and later a large French government and military presence that drew thousands of migrants from metropolitan France, other French Pacific territories, and Asia — making Kanak a minority in their own archipelago ([[hattori-samson-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v2-2023]], ch. 61, Tutugoro).

The independence movement emerged in the late 1960s when educated Kanak returned from French universities and the University of the South Pacific and began organising for restored sovereignty. The 'Pacific Way' concept championed by Fiji's Ratu Sir Kamisese Mara found resonance in New Caledonia. Violent confrontation in the 1980s ('Les Événements') led to the Matignon Accords (1988), which established a framework for provincial autonomy and land reform. A decade later, the Nouméa Accord (1998) created the current constitutional framework.

## The Nouméa Accord Framework (1998–present)

The Nouméa Accord, signed 5 May 1998, committed France to increasing the political and economic power of the Kanak over a twenty-year period, with three referenda at the end on the question of independence. The Accord's preamble acknowledged:

> 'Colonisation harmed the dignity of the Kanak people and deprived them of their identity. In this confrontation, some men and women lost their lives or their reasons for living. Much suffering resulted from it. These difficult times need to be remembered, the mistakes recognized and the Kanak people's confiscated identity restored, which equates in its mind with a recognition of its sovereignty, prior to the forging of a new sovereignty, shared in a common destiny.' (Preamble, Nouméa Accord, cited in ch. 61)

The Accord established: (1) irreversible transfers of competences from France to New Caledonia's Congress (constitutional law guarantees they cannot be retracted); (2) a Customary Senate with an advisory role on laws affecting Kanak identity; (3) a proportional representation system designed so no ethnic group gains institutional dominance; (4) three referenda on full sovereignty.

Tutugoro notes the complex political geography: Kanak are a majority in the North Province and Loyalty Islands Province, but most non-Kanak are concentrated in the South Province where the capital Nouméa is situated. Population breakdown in 2019: Kanak 41.2%, Europeans 24.1%, Wallisian/Futunan 8.3%, 'mixed' 11.3%, others 14.7% (ch. 61).

## The Three Competing Visions of Decolonisation

Tutugoro follows anthropologist Benoît Trépied in identifying three distinct conceptual tendencies within the decolonisation debate:

**1. Independence (full sovereignty)** — The FLNKS (Front de Libération Nationale Kanak et Socialiste) and allied parties envision a 'Kanaky-New Caledonia' that is sovereign, secular, and multicultural, with partnerships with France and Melanesian Spearhead Group countries. The FLNKS has worked through its 2018 brochure to articulate institutional architecture including an expanded Customary Senate that would accommodate all communities. 'There would be no antagonism, as Paul Néaoutyine likes to remind us in his speeches during political meetings, to being citizens of New Caledonia (and/or Kanaky) of Javanese, Walisian, and Vanuatuan etc. origin' (ch. 61).

The Parti Travailliste (Labour Party) under Louis Kotra Uregei took the more radical position that the Nouméa Accord itself was a mistake that made 'the colonized people a minority'; it boycotted the 2018 referendum. The Libération Kanak Socialiste preferred to wait for the third referendum to secure guarantees about customary authority legitimacy.

**2. Enhanced autonomy within France** — Non-independence parties (L'Avenir en Conﬁance, Calédonie Ensemble, Génération NC, etc.) position around maintaining the French Republic relationship, though with varying degrees of autonomy. Philippe Gomès of Calédonie Ensemble articulated a 'sovereignty-for-a-second' argument: 'Finally, give me my sovereignty for a second. There, you give it back to me... And we will thus pass from a French sovereignty that has been suffered to a French sovereignty that has been consented to. We will thus pass from a territory of the Republic to an associated state or a territory in free association with France, whatever the words' (ch. 61). Some non-independence factions explore federalism models (USA model of federated state) rather than departmentalisation.

**3. Kanak sovereignty as Indigenous right** — The CNDPA (Conseil National du Peuple Autochtone de Kanaky Nouvelle-Calédonie) advocates recognition of Kanak rights to their territory regardless of statutory evolution, in line with UN Indigenous peoples' rights frameworks. This position proposes limiting one referendum specifically to Kanak voters to demonstrate Kanak self-determination, a proposal not taken up by the High Commission due to France's republican universalism tradition (which 'has a sacrosanct abhorrence of any form of positive discrimination or displayed ethnicity,' ch. 61).

## The Flag Debate as Political Cartography

Tutugoro uses a detailed 6 March 2020 debate in New Caledonia's Congress over whether only the French tricolour (not the FLNKS flag) could be flown during the second referendum campaign as a 'measurement tool to estimate the convictions, the solidarity, and the political loyalties during national constructions' (ch. 61). The FLNKS flag — its three horizontal stripes represent sky/ocean (blue), blood of independence struggle and uterine kinship (red), and nourishing land (green); its yellow disc the rising sun; its central *flèche faîtière* (carved ancestral ridge-pole figure) crowned by a conch shell — embodies Kanak attachment to place and ancestry. The French state's attempt to restrict it to this campaign provoked all independence groups to oppose the decree and was characterised by the Union Calédonienne as the state 'unilaterally and arbitrarily modifying the organisation of the next consultation' and violating its pledged neutral role in the Accord process (ch. 61).

## Referendum Results and Trajectories

- **First referendum, 4 November 2018**: 'No' (remain French) 56.7%, 'Yes' (independence) 43.3%. High turnout. Macron declared: 'France would be less beautiful without New Caledonia.'
- **Second referendum, 4 October 2020**: 'No' 53.26%, 'Yes' 46.74%. The 'Yes' vote grew by 3.4 percentage points. Turnout 85.69%. The narrowing margin indicates movement toward independence.
- **Third referendum, 2021** (moved from 2022): held despite independence movement boycott in context of Covid mourning practices; result disputed in legitimacy.

Should three 'No' votes be recorded, the Accord provides for political partners to 'meet to consider the situation thus arising' — a deliberately vague provision. Tutugoro sketches a scenario in which the independence movement could shift strategy from 'independence vs. France' to a 'Kanak people vs. colonising state' bilateral negotiation, bypassing non-independence partners at the table — a 'significant shift' that would exclude the 1983 'victims of history' principle from Nainville-les-Roches that had previously included non-Kanak (ch. 61).

Should a 'Yes' majority be achieved, the FLNKS envisions a transition period with France accompanying handover of services, followed by a constituent assembly with options ranging from elected members to citizens drawn by lot, and determination of nationality criteria (the twenty-year residence rule could be modified) (ch. 61).

## Covid-19 as Diagnostic

Tutugoro uses the 2020 Covid-19 crisis to illuminate the political structure. When a French military aircraft carrying 180 soldiers was ordered to land, the Customary Senate and all elected independence representatives issued a coordinated press release opposing it as an infection risk — 'which has never happened before' (ch. 61). The non-independence South Province president Sonia Backès responded: 'everyone must stay in their place'. This 'war of press releases' revealed the territorial duality latent in New Caledonian politics.

Meanwhile, communities in the Grande Terre and the islands — away from Nouméa — demonstrated food self-sufficiency: 'The practice of the existing coastal reserves has enabled them to have a daily supply of fish. Food crops that have been cultivated in these islands for thousands of years through sophisticated land and water management systems have provided the population with taro, yams, bananas, kumara, and manioc' (ch. 61). Young people sent game meat and harvests to relatives in the capital.

Simultaneously, the non-independence government contracted an interest-bearing loan of 28.5 billion CFP francs (c. £217 million) repayable over twenty-five years — 'the same year as the consultation on New Caledonia's self-determination' — binding future generations to the French fiscal relationship regardless of the referendum result (ch. 61).

## Historiography

Tutugoro's chapter is based on: scientific publications; news media; personal notes and observation; interviews with political actors conducted during PhD research (provisionally titled '(Re)thinking sovereignty. Reclaiming strategies by the independence movement in New Caledonia', L'université de la Polynésie française / Université de la Nouvelle-Calédonie). The chapter is transdisciplinary, drawing on history, sociology, anthropology, and public law. Key prior work includes MacLellan (1999) on the Nouméa Accord and decolonisation; Connell (1987) on political history; Chappell (2017 French trans.) on Kanak awakening; Ramsey (2011) on cultural history. The chapter's limitation is that it was written before the controversial third referendum (December 2021), which the independence movement boycotted. Tutugoro is himself an insider-researcher; his angle is sympathetic to Kanak sovereignty claims but analytically balanced in presenting all political positions.
