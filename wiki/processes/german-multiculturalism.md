## Definition and Scope

Germany's transformation into a multicultural society is one of the most significant social processes of the postwar period. The transformation was largely unintended: the FRG initiated labor recruitment in 1955 to fuel the [[wirtschaftswunder]], explicitly treating workers as temporary "guests" (Gastarbeiter) who would return home. Max Frisch's famous line — "We sent for workers, but human beings came" — encapsulates the dissonance between policy intention and human reality. By 2005, roughly 20% of Germany's population (approximately 15 million people) had a "migration background" — a category the government found necessary to deploy because the simpler categories of "citizen" vs. "foreigner" no longer captured the complex patterns of settlement, naturalization, and identity.

The term "multicultural society" was imported to Germany from Australia by the churches in the 1970s, became a progressive mantra in the 1980s, was largely sidelined after German unification in the 1990s, and became a target of political backlash after September 11 and the European terrorist attacks of the 2000s. Chancellor Merkel declared in 2010 that "Multikulti has utterly failed."

## Historical Background: Migration in German History

Germany's multicultural present has deep historical antecedents. The Edict of Potsdam (1685) brought thousands of French Huguenots to Brandenburg-Prussia; one-third of early Berlin's population was French-speaking. Polish laborers in the Ruhr were a central element of industrialization. The Jewish minority — over half a million before the Nazi era — had complex minority rights arrangements. Turkish prisoners from the 1683 Battle of Vienna were integrated. The "guest workers" are the latest episode in an ongoing migration history, not a rupture.

## Major Phases

**Phase 1 (1955–1973): Guest Worker Recruitment.** The FRG signed bilateral labor recruitment agreements with Italy (1955), Spain (1960), Greece (1960), Turkey (1961), Portugal (1964), Tunisia (1965), Morocco (1963), and Yugoslavia (1968). The intention was rotation — workers would serve a few years and return, replaced by fresh recruits. In practice, employers preferred continuity, and workers built lives in Germany. When the oil shock led the FRG to terminate recruitment in 1973, the guest worker population did not leave.

**Phase 2 (1973–1990): Settlement.** After the recruitment stop, a series of court cases established that workers had the right to remain and to be joined by family members. The guest worker community — predominantly Turkish, Yugoslav, Greek, and Italian — settled permanently while remaining legally "denizens" rather than citizens. The official position ("Germany is not a country of immigration") contradicted demographic reality. The Kohl government in the 1980s offered financial incentives for emigration; few accepted.

**Phase 3 (1990–2000): Reunification and Refugee Crisis.** German reunification brought hundreds of thousands of ethnic German repatriates (Aussiedler) from the former Soviet Union, who could claim German citizenship under Article 116 but often spoke little German and built distinct subcultures. Simultaneously, the collapse of Yugoslavia produced massive refugee flows; Germany received over a million migrants per year through 1995 — more than any other European country. Far-right violence (Hoyerswerda 1991, Rostock 1992, Mölln 1992, Solingen 1993) accompanied the crisis. The right of asylum under Article 16 was constitutionally restricted in 1993.

**Phase 4 (2000–present): Integration and Citizenship Reform.** A new Citizenship Law (2000) took the symbolically critical step of supplementing ius sanguinis (nationality through parentage) with limited ius soli (nationality through birthplace), automatically granting dual citizenship to children born to long-term residents. An Immigration Law (2005) finally recognized Germany as a country of immigration. Government Integration Summits (from 2006) and Islam Conferences brought minority representatives into policy dialogue. Anti-discrimination legislation was enacted in 2006 under EU obligation.

## The Turkish Community

The Turkish minority is the most prominent and politicized. Three million strong, internally diverse (including Kurds, Sufis, Alevis), the Turkish community has faced persistent structural disadvantage: unemployment rates double the national average, educational attainment deficits, and long-term political exclusion as non-citizens. Integration has proceeded unevenly: 582,000 "foreign" entrepreneurs (many Turkish) created approximately 2 million jobs by the 2000s; Turkish-German literature and cinema flourished; intermarriage and cross-cultural friendships increased. But parallel societies and segregated schools remained structural concerns. The debate over Turkey's EU membership intersected awkwardly with domestic integration politics.

## Political Debates: Leitkultur vs. Verfassungspatriotismus

The central normative conflict in German multiculturalism debates runs between:

**Leitkultur** (guiding culture): advanced by CDU politicians Friedrich Merz and others, this concept holds that migrants must conform to a set of normative German cultural attributes as a precondition for inclusion. Critics saw it as a veiled assimilationism, even Germanization, that contradicted cultural pluralism.

**Verfassungspatriotismus** (constitutional patriotism): Jürgen Habermas's concept that belonging should be grounded not in ethnic or cultural identity but in commitment to a free and democratic constitutional order. This approach is compatible with cultural diversity while requiring adherence to basic liberal-democratic norms. It became the dominant left-liberal intellectual framework.

The headscarf controversy (following the Ludin case, in which an Afghan-German teacher sought the right to wear a headscarf in public school) crystallized tensions between religious freedom, state neutrality, and feminist concerns about patriarchal practices — illustrating that multiculturalism debates do not map neatly onto left-right lines.

## Intersection with European Integration

Germany's approach to multiculturalism was shaped by European processes. EU law placed constraints on German treatment of EU-national workers; the Schengen Agreement created a zone of free movement; and EU directives produced the anti-discrimination legislation Germany had long resisted. The politics of Turkey's EU candidacy intersected with the domestic situation of the Turkish minority. Germany's approach gradually converged with other European states toward a model requiring language competence and civic knowledge while allowing limited cultural accommodation.

## Historical Weight of the Holocaust

Multiculturalism in Germany is haunted by the Nazi past in distinctive ways. As Turkish-German author Zafer Şenocak observed: "One can immigrate to a country, but not to its past." The Holocaust created both a heightened moral obligation toward minorities and a specific difficulty: German history as "ethnic collective memory" did not automatically include migrant communities. The question of whether immigrants could participate in shaping the German future without sharing that particular past remained open. In 2008, the firing of Faruk Şen (a leading authority on the Turkish minority) for comparing the situation of Turks in Europe to that of Jews under the Nazis showed how live these tensions remained.

## Historiography

Key debates: (1) Was guest worker recruitment a "mistake" (Helmut Schmidt's 2004 view) or a structural consequence of capitalist labor demand? (2) Is Germany's Turkish minority integrating or forming a "parallel society"? (3) Does Leitkultur-style assimilation or Verfassungspatriotismus-style pluralism better achieve social cohesion? (4) How does Germany's specific Holocaust history shape multicultural discourse in distinctive ways?
