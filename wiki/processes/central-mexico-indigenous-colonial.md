# Central Mexico Indigenous Peoples under Colonial Rule

## Definition and Scope

Regional history of indigenous peoples of **central Mexico** — the dense agricultural plateau core of the [[basin-of-mexico|Basin of Mexico]] and adjacent valleys (Toluca, Cuernavaca/Morelos, Puebla–Tlaxcala) — from the Spanish invasion (1519–21) to Mexican independence (1821). Primary treatment follows Sarah L. Cline, “Native Peoples of Colonial Central Mexico,” *Cambridge History of the Native Peoples of the Americas* Vol. II Mesoamerica Part 2 (2000) ([[cambridge-history-native-peoples-americas-v2-2|CHNPA II.2]] Ch. 16).

The region held the **greatest numbers of both Indians and Spaniards** in colonial New Spain and therefore special importance (Cline). Geographical and cultural boundaries largely coincide: fertile plateau, mountain-bounded valleys, rainfall and groundwater supporting labor-intensive sedentary agriculture and dense population. Primary linguistic/ethnic groups: **Nahuas** (majority; Nahuatl as mother tongue and Mesoamerican lingua franca) and minority **Otomí**; Toluca also Mazahuas; Cuernavaca and Puebla densely Nahua. Otomí were generally subordinate within Nahuatl-speaking communities and left almost no colonial records in their own tongue (Cline).

This process is a regional strand of [[colonial-mesoamerica-native-peoples]] and of [[colonial-latin-america]]. Post-1821 continuities and ruptures are treated on [[central-mexico-indigenous-since-independence]].

**Emic self-identification.** Even among Nahuatl speakers, people self-identified as Culhuaque, Acolhuaque, Xochimilca, Tlahuica, and other groups tied to places and dynastic lineages — not as a single “Aztec” or “Indian” people. Spaniards labeled all New World natives “Indians,” a term meaningless or repugnant to indigenous peoples but persistent in the literature (Cline).

## Causal Drivers

- **Conquest and empire superstructure rupture.** Military defeat of the [[aztec-empire|Triple Alliance]] (1521) broke imperial tribute hegemony while leaving many component [[altepetl]] as bastions of indigenous social, economic, and political life — with Mexico City on the Tenochtitlan site as the major exception (Cline).
- **Mutual misreading of conquest.** Natives, from Mesoamerican alliance/conquest patterns, expected benefits without total destruction of existing arrangements; Spaniards, shaped by late-medieval militant Christianity and Caribbean rule, expected elite collaboration but **irrevocable** Spanish hegemony (Cline).
- **Epidemic demography.** [[virgin-soil-epidemics]] (especially the virulent late-1570s wave) devastated native populations while Spanish numbers grew by immigration and natural increase — driving land sales, household reorganization, and labor reallocation (Cline).
- **Colonial institutions grafted onto the altepetl.** [[encomienda]] (tribute/labor of a given altepetl to one Spaniard), then **corregimiento** and **repartimiento**, cabecera/sujeto remodeling, gobernador and cabildo government, and doctrina/visita parish structures all used and remade indigenous units rather than inventing rule from scratch (Cline).
- **Spiritual conquest and local Christianity.** Regular clergy (Franciscans, Dominicans, Augustinians) pursued conversion; saints, cofradías, and patron-feast life meshed better with indigenous corporate religion than Trinitarian theology or sin/salvation frameworks (Cline; see [[spiritual-conquest-and-colonial-church]]).
- **Land market and Spanish estate expansion.** Sixteenth-century real-estate market, sale of “excess” land under labor scarcity, then [[hacienda]]/[[hacienda-system]] pressure as native numbers recovered; Crown *composición* (title legalization) and reserved-land law as partial counters (Cline).
- **Alphabetic Nahuatl literacy.** Prehispanic writing tradition facilitated three centuries of native-language records by native scribes — unique vs the Andes — indexing culture contact and enabling legal defense (Cline).

## Major Phases

Cline’s colonial periodization structures the process (and tracks roughly with Nahuatl language change):

| Phase | Dates | Character (Cline) |
|---|---|---|
| **Early colonial** | Conquest–~1570s | Dense native populations; internal structures relatively intact; few Spaniards; weak but effective civil/religious institutions (suppress rebellion, collect revenue, Christianize a significant share); major epidemics as Spanish population grows |
| **Middle colonial** | ~1570s–~1720s | Stronger formal Spanish hierarchies; new native town political arrangements function; economy less based mainly on extracting tribute/labor — Spaniards acquire land, farm/ranch with privately recruited paid labor |
| **Late colonial** | ~1720s–1821 | Partial indigenous demographic recovery **increases** pressure for change: land lost by sale/usurpation channels natives into wage labor on Spanish enterprises or urban emigration |

### Conquest aftermath (to ~1550s)

Spaniards settled densely in the former imperial center; Tenochtitlan became **Mexico City**, seat of the viceroyalty of New Spain. Factors in relatively quick imperial defeat (1521) included European military technology, strategic indigenous allies (especially Tlaxcalans, also Texcocans, Xochimilcans, Huexotzincans), epidemic during the siege of Tenochtitlan, possible (and possibly exaggerated) prophetic questioning of Mexica legitimacy, and Spanish determination to fight to the death (Cline). Independent **Tlaxcala**, long under Triple Alliance attack, allied with Cortés and reaped early colonial benefits; its modern Mexican statehood is a legacy of that alliance (Cline).

From the colonial native viewpoint, the [[altepetl]] remained the highest indigenous-dominated organization. Encomienda redirected pre-existing tribute and **coatequitl** labor mechanisms so that the ultimate recipient was a Spaniard rather than the Triple Alliance; indigenous elites still mobilized levies. Early colonial altepetl could pursue localism because Spaniards preferred not to disturb structures producing taxes and labor (Cline). Native rulers sought to limit exactions but risked replacement if protests were too vigorous; Crown often ruled for natives against encomenderos from the 1530s; by ~1600 encomienda had ceased as a major institution in central Mexico (effective decline from the 1550s) (Cline). *(Institutional detail of encomienda/corregimiento belongs primarily on those concept pages; here the point is regional embedding in the altepetl.)*

### Political remodeling: cabecera, cabildo, elections

By mid-sixteenth century altepetl were constituted as units superficially like Spanish towns: main settlement as **cabecera**, outlying units as **sujetos**. Spaniards over-emphasized the central urban core and saw outlying components as subordinate where natives might see them as coequal; complex multi-tlatoani altepetl were rearranged to fit colonial structures. Town ranks (ciudad / villa / pueblo) became sources of pride or grievance; Nahuatl documents from ciudades used the Spanish term, while pueblos continued to call the polity **altepetl** (Cline).

From mid-1540s/early 1550s political towns were headed by a **gobernador** (early on often the dynastic tlatoani, but not dynastic succession — Spaniards could remove the recalcitrant). Cabildos with alcaldes, regidores, alguaciles, and escribanos (Nahuatl notaries) functioned by late 1550s/early 1560s, alongside officials with Nahuatl titles (e.g. *tlalpouhqui*, land measurer). **Outward form Spanish, workings native**: more alcaldes/regidores than the Spanish model (each component unit represented); office rotation by subunit rankings; fewer distinctions between former and current officeholders (Cline). Elections were mandated by Spanish law, but power apportionment often followed native practice — well documented for **Tlaxcala** (rotation among four parts); disputed elections studied in the **Cuernavaca** region. Even at the colonial end, town governments vigorously pursued internal disputes and defended towns against Spanish or indigenous outside encroachments (Cline).

Colonial noblemen as community protectors and Spanish intermediaries were crucial to Spanish rule, but dependency/collaboration lessened their effectiveness as brokers: compliant nobles kept or advanced position; recalcitrant ones were summarily removed (Cline).

### Religion and corporate Christianity

The “spiritual conquest” sought to transform polytheistic, animistic, fate-determined religion into Christianity. Changes that meshed with indigenous beliefs worked best: the Trinity was difficult; Christian **saints** as domain-specific intermediaries resembled native deities and fit Spanish Catholic mainstream practice if not underlying native beliefs. Friars expected to master Nahuatl; with indigenous aides they produced confessional manuals, sermons, catechisms, dictionaries, and grammars. Prehispanic conquest experience had integrated new gods into a pantheon; friars sought total replacement; outcome was something in between (Cline).

Each town and each *tlaxilacalli* received a Christian patron saint whose name entered the place name (e.g. Culhuacan → San Juan Evangelista Culhuacan); saint’s-day celebrations reinforced solidarity. Prehispanic cult sites became church/chapel locations; the sacred site at **Tepeyac** became local then regional then national pilgrimage for the Virgin of Guadalupe. Church size and elaborate furnishings signaled community unity and prominence (Cline).

Overt spectacular practices (e.g. human sacrifice) ceased almost immediately; baptism proceeded unevenly (1540s Cuernavaca censuses count many unbaptized); by end of sixteenth century probably all central Mexican Nahuas were baptized or passing as such. **Compadrazgo** was adopted enthusiastically — especially valuable under epidemic conditions. **Cofradías** established late sixteenth century, prominent seventeenth–early eighteenth: ecclesiastical insurance (burial, masses), structural stability after epidemics, reinforcement of collective identity, and a major institutional avenue of women’s leadership (Tula records: native women officers by the seventeenth century) (Cline).

Nahuas did not quickly (some argue never) transform beliefs around salvation, individual responsibility, and sin. No evidence of widespread organized resistance to evangelization in the Nahua region; communities petitioned about abusive clergy hoping for better replacements. By end of sixteenth century many friars had lost confidence in mission success (Cline). Religious structure mapped onto political: altepetl → **doctrina** seat; outlying settlements → **visitas**. Friars early abandoned training indigenous men for priesthood; the **fiscal** became the highest indigenous religious official (Cline).

### Social structure, household, gender, names

Elite/commoner division continued to the colonial end, though general native poverty somewhat collapsed the distinction; toward the end non-elite men began entering officeholder ranks, and mestizos often sat within the native elite (genealogies mattered when factions felt threatened) (Cline). Early Cuernavaca house-to-house censuses show complex multi-couple households as single economic units; the sixteenth century is the key period of residence-pattern change under epidemics and forced resettlement (**congregación**) (Cline). Friars largely suppressed elite polygyny. Kinship shifted: sibling ties important early (Motolinía on Cuernavaca inheritance); late-sixteenth-century Culhuacan testaments show weaker sibling ties under Spanish lineal inheritance patterns; Spanish kin terms entered Nahuatl (Cline).

Women owned property in their own right and bequeathed as they saw fit, often with stated exclusions; men made broader, less tendentious bequests — considerable autonomy within the Nahua family (Cline). Personal names indexed status and gender: by late sixteenth century everyone had a Christian saint given name plus second Nahuatl or Spanish name; high status used *don*/*doña* and Spanish surnames; by colonial end low-status names for both sexes were two Spanish given names (Juan Pedro, Ana María) (Cline). Under epidemic stress, women counted on their children’s godfathers but felt little obligation to their own godchildren; men took godfather roles seriously, often giving property to godchildren (Cline).

### Land tenure revolution and chinampa exception

Prehispanic categories (*tlatocatlalli*, *tecpantlalli*, *teotlalli*, *calpollalli*, *pillalli*, and further colonial Nahuatl labels such as *altepetlalli*, *tlalcohualli*, *huehuetlalli*) persisted as residual status markers even when practice changed (Cline). Dramatic sixteenth-century change: emergence of a **real estate market**; land for indigenous religious cults disappeared almost immediately; dynastic-ruler office land separated to individuals; distinctive noble *pillalli* ceased importance by the seventeenth century. Catastrophic population drop + Spanish increase: natives needed less land and sold large amounts for cash; by late sixteenth century individuals treated land as private property despite prehispanic corporate emphasis (Cline).

Persistent patterns: multiple scattered parcels of varying potential; community commons often for pasture; town government adjudicated disputes first, Spanish courts as last resort. By late seventeenth century, demographic recovery increased pressure as land had permanently passed to Spaniards; some communities bought back hacienda land but overall estate growth reduced available native land. Seventeenth-century **composición** validated titles for a Crown fee (protective yet revenue-producing); eighteenth-century courts often narrowly defined reserved lands (standard 600 varas), allowing Spanish occupation outside them — dual situation of Spanish private pressure vs Crown protection of the native agricultural base (Cline).

**Major exception — chinampas:** artificially built extensions into the southern freshwater lake system remained **almost exclusively native** well into the twentieth century because of high labor requirements; Spaniards preferred lower-labor wheat and livestock and generally did not acquire chinampa land — so natives retained rich agricultural land very near the Spanish capital (Cline; see [[chinampa-agriculture]]).

Agriculture continued largely traditional (maize primary); natives extended into animal husbandry (sheepherding most prominent ranching; enthusiastic beef eaters but not generally cattle ranchers in the central region). Highly developed Nahuatl soil vocabulary persisted (some loans into Mexican Spanish, e.g. *chinampa*); natives continued measuring land in own units (*maitl*, *yollotli*, *quahuitl*, *mecatl*, etc.) to the colonial end in some areas (Cline).

### Economy: cash, labor, crafts, markets

Prehispanic media of exchange (cacao, cotton cloaks) eased introduction of Spanish money; mixed money/cacao systems appear early (e.g. 1540s Tlaxcala market inventory). Labor path: rotary drafts via encomienda → paid **repartimiento** → informal private cash hiring as repartimiento proved inefficient for both sides. Crown conversion of tribute goods to money mid-sixteenth century either forced cash participation or reflected prior monetization (Cline).

Gendered commoner labor: men agriculture, women household textile weaving. By late sixteenth century men and women sold excess land under labor scarcity; late-seventeenth-century recovery brought more labor but less native land — general economic level declined (Cline). Prehispanic lapidaries and featherworkers became obsolete; baskets, special woven goods, pottery continued. Colonial native men became Spanish-style artisans (tailors, painters, carpenters); Spanish attempts to stop native artisan competition usually failed. Urban demand for cheap wool cloth → Spanish **obrajes** using native male convicts and wage laborers. Local/regional markets (*tianquiztli* → *tianguiz*) survived and thrived under native government regulation. Prehispanic long-distance **pochteca** disappeared as an organized high-status group soon after Conquest; colonial regional trade used pack animals, with women investing in livestock and moneylending (Cline).

### Language change and the independence documentation break

Three-stage Nahuatl change as index of culture contact (not exact correlation) (Cline / Lockhart framework as summarized by Cline):

1. **Stage 1** (contact–mid-16th c.): little Spanish contact; new phenomena named by extending Nahuatl (e.g. *mazatl* “deer” for horses); conscious Spanish loans for Christian and legal vocabulary.
2. **Stage 2** (from mid-16th c.): widespread Spanish loanwords for tools, clothing, plants, animals; horses become *cavallo* not *mazatl* — corresponds to middle colonial demographic/economic shift.
3. **Stage 3** (~18th c.): Nahuatl syntax changes; larger bilingual segment; few Spaniards bilingual — incumbent on natives to be bilingual.

Document genres: early pictorial-plus-gloss taxation/land records; earliest full-length Nahuatl docs (Cuernavaca region, 1530s–40s) house-to-house censuses; Spanish-style wills/bills of sale from second half of sixteenth century; **annals** (year-by-year altepetl events — Chimalpahin outstanding; none for late colonial); **poetry/song** (sixteenth-century written examples, none later); **primordial titles (*títulos*)** after ~1650 — establish territorial claims amid population recovery and land pressure; mix prehispanic and early colonial history through altepetl prism; intended for Spanish legal system but lacked standing to prove ownership (Cline).

**Abrupt cessation of Nahuatl documentation at independence 1821** (fewer records already in last quarter of eighteenth century). Most likely explanation: independent Mexico abolished formal racial divisions (*república de indios*) and the colonial courts’ acceptance of Nahuatl testimony and documents with Spanish translation; new system apparently did not. Suggests Nahuatl record-keeping lacked (or had lost) independent existence in native communities by colonial end. Native towns did not cease to exist but became more marginalized in the national period (Cline).

### Colonial balance sheet (Cline’s conclusion)

Spanish colonial rule was paternalistic and legally/socially unequal, yet **protected and to an extent promoted indigenous corporate life vigor** — while exploiting via encomienda, repartimiento, and the Church. Spaniards legally acquired substantial central Mexican land; the hacienda exploited individual natives. Indigenous towns retained significant though diminished land via corporate protections and pursued lawsuits with Nahuatl documents/testimony. **These protections ceased with independence.** Mid-nineteenth-century liberal reforms further assaulted collective land ownership. Colonial period = decline of populations, resources, and cultural integrity; natives at the bottom of hierarchy but **within** the system; continued existence of towns with significant native populations and distinct traits = cultural tenacity amid deleterious colonial impact (Cline).

## Geographic Spread

- **Core:** Valley/Basin of Mexico with lake system (Tenochtitlan/Mexico City island center); Nahuas and Otomís primary.
- **West:** Toluca Valley — Nahuas and Mazahuas.
- **South:** Cuernavaca/Morelos — dense Nahua agricultural populations.
- **East:** Puebla region — dense Nahua agriculture; Tlaxcala as special independent-then-allied case.
- **Northern bound near the center:** desert of nomadic/seminomadic peoples culturally related but distinct from the settled groups treated here (Cline).

## End Conditions

Ends as a **colonial** process with Mexican independence (1821): formal abolition of the *república de indios* / legal category *indio*, loss of Nahuatl’s standing in courts, and removal of corporate protections that had partially sheltered native towns — even as many colonial institutional forms (parcialidades, communal administration, principales brokerage, ceremonial life) persisted into the national period and were reworked under liberal and revolutionary states (bridge to Schryer on [[central-mexico-indigenous-since-independence]]). Indigenous presence itself did not end; the legal and documentary regime that had structured colonial corporate life did.

## Historiography

Post-WWII historiography rests on a rich Spanish **and** Nahuatl documentary base (Cline bibliographical essay). Frameworks: *Handbook of Middle American Indians* vols. 12–15; *Handbook of Latin American Studies* ethnohistory bibliography; Gerhard *Guide to the Historical Geography of New Spain*. **Two pillars:** Charles Gibson, *Aztecs Under Spanish Rule* (1964) — Spanish-language sources on political/religious town structures, social/economic organization, land tenure for the whole colonial period; James Lockhart, *The Nahuas after the Conquest* (1992) — history based exclusively on Nahuatl documentation, stressing cultural change reflected in language; builds on, modifies, and complements Gibson. Also Gruzinski *Conquest of Mexico* (1993); institution studies (Borah, Simpson, Himmerich y Valencia).

Conquest multiperspectival sources: Cortés’s *Letters*; Bernal Díaz; Sahagún Florentine Codex vol. 12 (defeated Tenochca) and 1585 Revision; Muñoz Camargo (Tlaxcalan); Durán; Lockhart *We People Here*; Codex Chimalpahin. Spiritual conquest: Ricard still important; revisions by Burkhart *The Slippery Earth*, Klor de Alva, Gruzinski *Man Gods*, Schroeder on Chimalpahin, Ruiz de Alarcón *Treatise* (1629) on surviving beliefs (Cline). Local Nahuatl corpora: Cuernavaca censuses, *Tlaxcalan Actas*, Culhuacan/Coyoacan/Chalco studies; land monographs (Prem, García Martínez, Martin); Cope on Mexico City Indians including 1692 riots; eighteenth-century casta paintings as pictorial stratification evidence (Cline).

**Collection note:** CHNPA II.2 Cline chapter is the wiki’s main colonial-central-Mexico synthesis for this process; Gibson/Lockhart monographs remain the research foundations behind it.
