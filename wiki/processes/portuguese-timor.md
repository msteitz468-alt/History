## Definition and Scope

Portuguese Timor is one of the most distinctive cases in European colonial history: a small, remote colony on the eastern half of Timor island that persisted from Portuguese first contact in the 1500s until 1975 — nearly five centuries — without ever being administered through large-scale European settlement or conventional military occupation. Ricardo Roque (in [[hattori-samson-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v2-2023]]) argues the key to understanding this durability is the role of **flags as actors** in a colonial system built on vassalage ceremony, Indigenous status hierarchies, and mutual animism rather than on disciplined armies or solid fortresses.

The chapter operates as a micro-history of a colonial object — the Portuguese national flag (*bandeira*) — to reveal larger structural features of Portuguese imperialism in Asia-Pacific and the creative agency of Timorese peoples in absorbing, repurposing, and multiplying those objects within their own political and spiritual framework.

## Causal Drivers

**Portuguese vassalage expansionism.** From the 1500s, Portugal constructed its Asian empire not primarily through settlement but through a ceremonial system of vassal–king networks. The Portuguese king styled himself "King of kings" (*rei dos reis*), and his delegates circulated material insignia of power — flags, sceptres, crowns, clothing, swords, letters patent — to Indigenous rulers who swore loyalty and tribute in exchange for Portuguese military protection and recognition of their authority. As early as 1514, King Manuel I's instructions for vassalizing the king of Marrakech specified: "as sign of this he will take our flag and with it he will make those obligations and oaths in law that customarily they do." Flags were among the "power-charged items that materialized an early modern ceremonial style of Portuguese overseas expansionism."

**The Topasse mestizo class.** Portuguese traders, soldiers, and missionaries established informal settlements in the Lesser Sunda Islands from the early 1500s, producing the so-called Topasses (also "Black Portuguese"): a mestizo ruling and warlike class based at Solor and Larantuka (eastern Flores). Embracing Christianity and Portuguese identity while adopting Indigenous traditions, the Topasses dominated early Portuguese settlements and expanded into Timor in the 1600s. They were "unwilling to accept external orders from Goa or from Lisbon over their realm" until 1701–2, when Goa appointed the first governor, António Coelho Guerreiro, to Lifau.

**Timorese lulik cosmology.** The Tetum term *lulik* conveyed a double meaning of "prohibition" and "sacredness" and applied to ancestral objects — heirlooms kept in the ritual house (*uma lulik*) of each descent group — "without which the unity and the rank of every type of Timorese collective unit … could not be maintained." Lulik objects were not static symbols but active agents: "Lulik has agency: it can be angry or hungry and even 'jump away'" (Bovensiepen). What defined something as lulik was above all "a relation of distance" — gestures of avoidance ranging from mild respect to awe (Traube). Portuguese flags, received as vassalage gifts, entered this ontological category.

**Dutch competition.** The Portuguese were never alone. Dutch expansion (Kupang taken in 1653) replicated Portuguese ceremonial strategies — "since at least the mid-1600s, in what reads perhaps as an imitation of the Portuguese ceremonial style, the Dutch started handing over flags, drums, cloths, weapons, and sceptres … to the local rulers." Dutch flags similarly "went through a process of indigenization homologous to the Portuguese flags," becoming sacred ancestral heirlooms (*pusaka*) in West Timorese princedoms.

**Timorese lineage competition.** Perhaps the most important driver in the long run: Timorese aristocratic houses competed ceaselessly for status, authority, and ceremonial centrality. Portuguese flags "became embedded in Timorese disputes between elite lineages for superiority and independent ritual-political authority." As one colonial official noted in 1897: "The régulos, datós and principais use very pompous names … and one cannot give them a better present than a national flag." Flags thus fed a centrifugal dynamic of differentiation that the Portuguese had "long lost control" of.

## Major Phases

### Phase 1: Early Modern Vassal-King Networks (1500s–1700s)

Portuguese traders and missionaries established informal contacts with Timor from the early 1500s, drawn by sandalwood. The Topasses expanded Portuguese influence militarily, including a decisive victory over the prestigious realm of Wehali in 1642. The first formally appointed governor reached Lifau only in 1701–2; the territory was effectively a Topasse domain until then. Vassalage treaties and flag distributions to *liurais* (Timorese "lords of the land") appear to pre-date formal gubernatorial administration: Governor Afonso de Castro wrote in 1867 that when the first delegate arrived, "he found the peoples already linked to Portugal through contracts, made vassals [*avassalados*] and compromised with certain obligations." Flags were also captured as war booty, and distributed by Timorese rulers themselves to sub-vassals, beginning chains of "local versions of king-vassal affiliations."

In 1769, pressed by both Dutch and Topasses, the Portuguese governor abandoned Lifau and retreated to Dili in eastern Timor. In 1851 Portugal sold Solor and Flores to the Netherlands, contracting its Asian presence to Goa, Macau, the Oecussi enclave, and East Timor.

### Phase 2: 19th-Century Ceremonial Administration (1800s–1890s)

By the late 19th century, Portuguese colonial maps depicted East Timor as "a patchwork of small reinos (kingdoms)" — forty-seven to fifty-four acknowledged kingdoms, shifting rapidly with wars and alliances. The Portuguese colonial state was structurally weak: "limited to a handful of European officers and soldiers," dependent on Timorese irregulars (*moradores*) and Indigenous warriors supplied as "customary tribute of vassalage." Governor Celestino da Silva (1849–1911) confronted this vulnerability directly: "It is hard to understand how the natives still have some sort of respect for us … because they know we have no [armed] force to compel them."

The formal vassalage ceremony as described by Celestino in 1901 involved: the *liurai* swearing obedience to the government while presenting buffaloes and other valuables; the governor reciprocating with a royal title, military rank, certificate, sceptre, and "the national flag"; a shared meal; and tribute (*serpina*) exchanged for presents of "equal value." This reciprocal structure kept Portuguese authority hostage to its vassals' recognition — a "galactic polity" (Tambiah) in which the Portuguese in Dili struggled to achieve centrality.

Celestino theorized Portuguese authority not as military force but as "prestige" — an immaterial quality "that came especially into existence through the presence and action of a certain material object: the Portuguese national flag." His famous 1894 parable: besieged Timorese warriors requested the tiny Portuguese garrison to "lower the flag, because otherwise they could not attack." The flag was not lowered; the attack did not occur. For Celestino, this proved the flag alone sustained the empire: "Portuguese ﬂags made the difference."

### Phase 3: Anti-Colonial Uprising and End of Vassalage (1890s–1913)

The 1890s saw violent punitive campaigns under Celestino da Silva to extend direct administration. In 1912–13 the Kingdom of Manufahi and its allies launched "the largest and most devastating anti-Portuguese uprising in East Timor." Portuguese military victory was followed by important changes: "the abolition of vassalage and the structure of reinos (kingdoms)." Significantly, some Portuguese observers attributed part of the 1912 Manufahi revolt to Timorese attachment to the old blue/white monarchic flag, which had been replaced by the red/green Republican flag after Portugal's 1910 revolution — a "supposed Timorese predilection for monarchic flags."

### Phase 4: Estado Novo, Japanese Occupation, and Decolonization (1933–1975)

The fascist *Estado Novo* regime (1933–74) invested in "reconstruction" of East Timor and amplified nationalist-imperialist ideologies. Stories of Timorese attachment to ancient *lulik* flags during the 1941–45 Japanese occupation were weaponized as proof of "Portuguese colonial exceptionalism" — a mild, spiritual, and benign colonialism uniquely capable of awakening "spontaneous" patriotism. Post-war propaganda in "literature, cinema, photography" presented Timorese "devotion" to the flag as evidence of lusophone affective community.

Decolonization came abruptly: after brief civil conflict in 1975, the Portuguese colonial administration abandoned East Timor, replaced by Indonesian military occupation until 1999, followed by national independence as Timor-Leste.

## The Colonial Metaphysics of Flags: Two Variants

Roque identifies two interlocking Portuguese theories of flag power in Timor:

**"Prestige."** The idea that flags carried metaphysical potency ("prestige") that caused Timorese deference independently of military force. Governor Celestino's 1897 formulation: Timorese "respect towards the Portuguese" is "with rare exceptions a kind of religion." Governor Afonso de Castro (1864): "the sovereignty of Portugal became for all people a sacred thing; respect for the Portuguese flag is a religious feeling, the love for Portugal is a cult and a necessity." This colonial animism attributed to the flag an "invisible force that seemed to emanate from certain Portuguese bodies and materials in their interactions with Timorese people."

**"Patriotism."** From the 1920s–1930s onward, the register shifted: flag reverence was reframed not as a primitive religion but as indigenous Portuguese patriotism — proof that Timorese had been spiritually absorbed into the Portuguese nation. Colonel Leite de Magalhães (1937) described his experience of the flag-raising in Timor as a "virtually religious experience of shared patriotism."

Roque argues both variants misread Timorese actions by imposing European nationalist frameworks. The Timorese were not worshipping the Portuguese nation; they were engaging with lulik objects of ancestral power.

## Reframing the "Flag Cult": Timorese Agency

The Timorese concept of **lulik** and the related concept of **rota** (foundational tokens of sociopolitical authority) provide the actual explanatory framework. Portuguese flags, received as vassalage gifts, entered Timorese *uma lulik* houses as:

- Ancestral heirlooms establishing descent-group membership and status
- Objects receiving personal names, personal origin stories, and active care
- War agents called to produce distance effects (protection or pacification) in armed conflicts
- Markers of lineage distinction that "helped to sustain the noble houses' claims for status distinction, sovereignty, and political authority"

Crucially, Timorese Islanders were "not so interested in Portuguese national flags as such; they could also safeguard as *lulik* a variety of other old flags of outsider origin." Dutch national flags appeared in lulik collections of kingdoms that had formerly paid obedience to Dutch authorities. At Maubara, the kingdom preserved Dutch flags and displayed them "proudly to the Portuguese authorities until at least 1909." The 1883 observation by Major Vaquinhas recorded ancient flags of varied designs: Crown flags from the 1640–1816 era, the United Kingdom of Portugal/Brazil/Algarves flag (1815–22), and several Portuguese naval flags not associated with the Crown — a "multitude of ﬂags with distinct patterns" exceeding any national symbolism.

Some Timorese polities even replicated flag-distribution strategies themselves — acting as givers of Portuguese-like insignia to subordinate realms, creating "alternative power sources and symbolic centres that could compete with the processes adopted by the foreigners."

## The Parable of the Failed Flag

Roque includes a crucial counter-example to Celestino's triumphant parable. In the Laleia war of 1878, Timorese warriors attacked a Portuguese party even though the victims were "covered with the flag." The king of Vemasse "had sworn on the flag" to protect the party but treacherously attacked anyway. Lieutenant Cândido "covered the two chased men with the flag, in the hope that at least on this account they would be respected." The flag failed: "even though D. João of Cairuy and the major of his kingdom, loyal to the government, were covered with the flag they were murdered." Lulik agency was contextual, relational, and reversible: "flags could quickly shift from being fabulous mediators charged with invisible potencies of unstoppable might to being useless 'intermediaries'" (Latour).

## Historiography

Roque's analysis intervenes against two dominant historiographical traditions: (1) Portuguese nationalist accounts that read Timorese flag reverence as evidence of a unique, benign, spiritually effective Portuguese colonialism; and (2) standard colonial studies that treat European flags as "merely symbolic representations of the nation." Both traditions share a Eurocentric bias that "obliterates the actual significance" of cross-cultural flag encounters.

The chapter draws on actor-network theory (Latour), Timorese ethnography (Traube, Bovensiepen, Shepherd, Hoskins), archival records from the Arquivo Histórico Ultramarino (Lisbon), and comparative analysis of Portuguese imperialism in Africa (Angola) and Asia (Sri Lanka, Goa). It develops the concept of "flag animism" — cross-cultural, mutual, and reciprocal — as an analytical alternative to national symbolism. Roque emphasizes the limits of his own archive: colonial accounts distorted Timorese actions through nationalist lenses, and "current historical evidence is still insufficient" to establish the full chronology of flag distribution in early Timor.

Collection coverage for East Timor in general is thin; this chapter and the micro-historical approach of Roque are rare exceptions in the [[hattori-samson-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v2-2023]] volume.
