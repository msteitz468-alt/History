## Definition and Scope

The **Hellenistic world** is the political and cultural order that emerged from
the breakup of [[alexander-the-great|Alexander's]] empire after his death at
Babylon in June 323 BCE, and which persisted until the absorption of its last
great kingdom — Ptolemaic Egypt — by Rome in 30 BCE. The term (coined by the
nineteenth-century historian J. G. Droysen, from Greek *hellenizein*, "to speak
or act as a Greek") names the fusion-culture in which a thin Graeco-Macedonian
ruling stratum governed vast non-Greek populations across Egypt, the Near East,
and inner Asia, spreading the Greek language, city form, and art far beyond the
Aegean homeland while itself absorbing Near Eastern monarchy, religion, and
administrative practice.

[[walbank-cah-v7-1-1984|CAH VII.1]] treats the period's first century (323–217),
which is its formative phase: the era in which the [[wars-of-the-diadochi-323|wars
of Alexander's Successors]] dissolved the single empire into a system of competing
territorial states, and in which the three great dynasties — the
[[ptolemaic-egypt|Ptolemies]] in Egypt, the [[seleucid-empire|Seleucids]] in Asia,
and the [[antigonid-dynasty|Antigonids]] in Macedonia — took the shape they would
hold until Rome. The volume closes in 217 with the Peace of Naupactus and the
[[battle-of-raphia-217|battle of Raphia]], the year [[polybius|Polybius]] singled
out as the beginning of *symploke* — the interweaving of Mediterranean affairs
that would culminate in Roman domination.

What the Hellenistic world is **not**: it is not a unified empire (Édouard Will's
chapters stress that Alexander's realm was never a state but "an artificial
aggregate of at least three states," held together only by his person); nor is it
simply "decline" from the classical polis (a Victorian framing the volume's
authors, especially J. K. Davies, explicitly reject — see [[hellenistic-polis]]).

## Causal Drivers

- **Alexander's conquest and sudden death** without a competent heir. The empire
  depended wholly on his person; his death with only a retarded half-brother
  (Philip III Arrhidaeus) and an unborn child (Alexander IV) as heirs made a
  succession crisis structurally certain. See [[wars-of-the-diadochi-323]].
- **The "spear-won" principle of kingship**: legitimacy flowing from military
  victory rather than dynastic descent, formalised when the Successors assumed the
  royal title (basileus) in 306–305. See [[hellenistic-monarchy]].
- **Greek colonisation and military settlement**: tens of thousands of Greek and
  Macedonian soldiers and settlers planted in new cities (Alexandria, Antioch,
  Seleuceia) and cleruchies across Asia and Egypt, creating the demographic base
  of a transplanted Greek civic culture.

## Major Phases

1. **The Successors, 323–276** — from the settlement at Babylon through Triparadisus
   (321), [[battle-of-ipsus-301|Ipsus]] (301), and Corupedium (281) to the
   stabilisation of the three dynasties under their second founders (Ptolemy II,
   Antiochus I, Antigonus II Gonatas). *(Chs 2, 4, 7.)*
2. **The third-century balance, 276–217** — the [[syrian-wars|Syrian Wars]] between
   Ptolemies and Seleucids, the rise of the [[achaean-league|Achaean]] and
   [[aetolian-league|Aetolian]] confederations, the new powers of Asia Minor
   ([[pergamon|Pergamum]], [[rhodes|Rhodes]]), and the Spartan social revolution of
   [[agis-iv|Agis IV]] and [[cleomenes-iii|Cleomenes III]] (crushed by Macedon at
   [[battle-of-sellasia-222|Sellasia]], 222). *(Chs 11–12.)* The phase closes at
   [[battle-of-raphia-217|Raphia]] and the [[social-war-220|Peace of Naupactus]] (217) —
   [[polybius|Polybius']] *symploke*, the threshold of the age of Rome.
3. *(Beyond this volume: the coming of Rome, 217–30, treated in CAH VII.2–VIII–IX.)*

## Geographic Spread

Greek civic and linguistic forms spread from the Aegean to the Nile, the Tigris,
the Oxus, and the Indus. The [[koine-greek|koine]] became the administrative and
commercial lingua franca of the eastern Mediterranean and Near East; Greek-style
cities appeared as far east as [[ai-khanoum|Ai Khanum]] in Bactria. But the spread
was uneven and stratified: Greek remained largely the language of the towns and the
ruling classes, while native languages (Egyptian demotic, Aramaic, Babylonian) and
priesthoods persisted underneath, a duality the source-record itself reflects — see
[[hellenistic-historiography]].

## Interaction

The Hellenistic world is the matrix within which later processes develop:
[[urbanization|Hellenistic urbanism]] (new royal capitals and grid-planned cities),
the first continuous era-dating ([[seleucid-era]]), [[ruler-cult]], the transformed
[[hellenistic-polis|city]], the [[euergetism|civic benefaction]] of the rich, and the
[[hellenistic-science|science]] and scholarship of [[alexandria|Alexandria's]] Museum and
Library. It interacts with the [[rise-of-rome]] (which will consume it) and, eastward, with
the [[mauryan-empire|Mauryan]] world (Seleucus' cession to Chandragupta).

## Plural Society, Not a Unity

J. K. Davies (CAH VII.1 ch. 8) cautions against treating the Hellenistic world as a single
"civilization." Beneath a thin cosmopolitan top layer of [[koine-greek|koine]]-speaking
courts, intellectuals, and great landowners lay **a multiplicity of societies** —
"interacting little with each other" — and it is doubtful that any unified "Hellenistic
economy" existed (most production was local subsistence). The unifying view, and the very
word "Hellenistic," is a 19th-century construct; the reality was a [[hellenistic-polis|plural,
layered society]]. See [[ptolemaic-economy]], [[seleucid-hellenization]].

## End Conditions

Within this volume the order is in vigorous, expansive equilibrium. Its eventual
end — the piecemeal Roman conquest from 200 BCE to the death of Cleopatra VII in
30 BCE — lies beyond CAH VII.1 and is the subject of later volumes. The *cultural*
Hellenistic world, by contrast, did not end with Roman conquest: it was absorbed,
so that Greek remained the language of the eastern Roman Empire and the vehicle of
early Christianity for centuries.
