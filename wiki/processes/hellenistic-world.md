## Definition and Scope

The **Hellenistic world** is the political and cultural order that emerged from
the breakup of [[alexander-the-great|Alexander's]] empire after his death at
Babylon in June 323 BCE, and which persisted until the absorption of its last
great kingdom — Ptolemaic Egypt — by Rome in 30 BCE. The term (coined by the
nineteenth-century historian J. G. Droysen, from Greek *hellenizein*, "to speak
or act as a Greek") names the fusion-culture in which a thin Graeco-Macedonian
ruling stratum governed vast non-Greek populations across Egypt, the Near East,
and inner Asia, spreading the Greek language, city form, and art far beyond the
Aegean homeland while itself absorbing Near Eastern monarchy, religion, and
administrative practice.

**Simon Price** (*Oxford History of the Classical World*, 1986) defines the period
as the ~300 years between Alexander (336–323 BCE) and Augustus (31 BCE–14 CE) —
often dismissed as an incoherent “decline of the Greek city,” but possessing internal
coherence centred on **Greek monarchies** that together controlled the area from Greece
to Afghanistan. By about **275 BCE**, after the [[wars-of-the-diadochi-323|Successors’
wars]] failed to produce a sole heir to Alexander, **three kingdoms** dominated the
eastern Mediterranean until Rome: Egypt ([[ptolemaic-egypt|Ptolemies]]), the East
([[seleucid-empire|Seleucids]]), and Macedon ([[antigonid-dynasty|Antigonids]]).

[[walbank-cah-v7-1-1984|CAH VII.1]] treats the period's first century (323–217),
which is its formative phase: the era in which the [[wars-of-the-diadochi-323|wars
of Alexander's Successors]] dissolved the single empire into a system of competing
territorial states, and in which the three great dynasties — the
[[ptolemaic-egypt|Ptolemies]] in Egypt, the [[seleucid-empire|Seleucids]] in Asia,
and the [[antigonid-dynasty|Antigonids]] in Macedonia — took the shape they would
hold until Rome. The volume closes in 217 with the Peace of Naupactus and the
[[battle-of-raphia-217|battle of Raphia]], the year [[polybius|Polybius]] singled
out as the beginning of *symploke* — the interweaving of Mediterranean affairs
that would culminate in Roman domination.

What the Hellenistic world is **not**: it is not a unified empire (Édouard Will's
chapters stress that Alexander's realm was never a state but "an artificial
aggregate of at least three states," held together only by his person); nor is it
simply "decline" from the classical polis (a Victorian framing the volume's
authors, especially J. K. Davies, explicitly reject — see [[hellenistic-polis]]).

## Causal Drivers

- **Alexander's conquest and sudden death** without a competent heir. The empire
  depended wholly on his person; his death with only a retarded half-brother
  (Philip III Arrhidaeus) and an unborn child (Alexander IV) as heirs made a
  succession crisis structurally certain. See [[wars-of-the-diadochi-323]].
- **The "spear-won" principle of kingship**: legitimacy flowing from military
  victory rather than dynastic descent, formalised when the Successors assumed the
  royal title (basileus) in 306–305. See [[hellenistic-monarchy]].
- **Greek colonisation and military settlement**: tens of thousands of Greek and
  Macedonian soldiers and settlers planted in new cities (Alexandria, Antioch,
  Seleuceia) and cleruchies across Asia and Egypt, creating the demographic base
  of a transplanted Greek civic culture.

## Major Phases

1. **The Successors, 323–276** — from the settlement at Babylon through Triparadisus
   (321), [[battle-of-ipsus-301|Ipsus]] (301), and Corupedium (281) to the
   stabilisation of the three dynasties under their second founders (Ptolemy II,
   Antiochus I, Antigonus II Gonatas). *(Chs 2, 4, 7.)*
2. **The third-century balance, 276–217** — the [[syrian-wars|Syrian Wars]] between
   Ptolemies and Seleucids, the rise of the [[achaean-league|Achaean]] and
   [[aetolian-league|Aetolian]] confederations, the new powers of Asia Minor
   ([[pergamon|Pergamum]], [[rhodes|Rhodes]]), and the Spartan social revolution of
   [[agis-iv|Agis IV]] and [[cleomenes-iii|Cleomenes III]] (crushed by Macedon at
   [[battle-of-sellasia-222|Sellasia]], 222). *(Chs 11–12.)* The phase closes at
   [[battle-of-raphia-217|Raphia]] and the [[social-war-220|Peace of Naupactus]] (217) —
   [[polybius|Polybius']] *symploke*, the threshold of the age of Rome.
3. *(Beyond this volume: the coming of Rome, 217–30, treated in CAH VII.2–VIII–IX.)*

**Attalid Pergamum (Price, OHCW):** kingdom with capital at Pergamum carved from Seleucid
western Turkey; first two Attalids (283–241) only partially independent; **Attalus I**’s
victory over Galatians (c.238) allowed assumption of kingship; power increased by Rome;
bequeathed to Rome by last king (**133**). (Place page `places/pergamon` not yet created;
actor summary lives at [[pergamon]].) **Ling (OHCW):** upper city of Pergamum (1st half
2nd c.) as the age’s finest non-orthogonal urban composition (fan-like terraces, steep
theatre, Doric stoas); Great Altar (~166–159) Gigantomachy as High Hellenistic baroque
extreme; Telephus frieze continuous narrative.

## Geographic Spread

Greek civic and linguistic forms spread from the Aegean to the Nile, the Tigris,
the Oxus, and the Indus. The [[koine-greek|koine]] became the administrative and
commercial lingua franca of the eastern Mediterranean and Near East; Greek-style
cities appeared as far east as [[ai-khanoum|Ai Khanum]] in Bactria. But the spread
was uneven and stratified: Greek remained largely the language of the towns and the
ruling classes, while native languages (Egyptian demotic, Aramaic, Babylonian) and
priesthoods persisted underneath, a duality the source-record itself reflects — see
[[hellenistic-historiography]].

### Hellenization as power (Price)

Price insists Hellenization across the Middle East was **not** an “innocent gift of
civilization” but **cultural dominance bound to royal power**. New Greek cities were
fundamental: Alexander was reported to have founded 70+ (the actual figure may be half
that); the Seleucids created 60+ settlements from western Turkey to Iran; some were old
sites renamed with Greek constitutions. In Egypt, [[alexandria|Alexandria]] replaced
Pharaonic Memphis as capital; Seleucia on the Tigris superseded Babylon ~50 km away —
clearest illustrations of Greek dominance over non-Greek populations. Greek exclusivity
could be extreme: Seleucia on the Eulaeus remained exclusively Greek (no known
Greek-named person with non-Greek father after 300 years); Egypt kept a sharp
Greek/native divide (demotic literature, separate courts). Entry to power required
adopting Greek culture or inventing ancestral Greek connections (e.g. Lampsacus–Rome
kinship via Troy/Aeneas).

### Horizons and common culture (Lane Fox)

**Robin Lane Fox** (OHCW) stresses that Greek horizons after Alexander extended to
India: Aśoka’s c.260 BCE edict listed Hellenistic kings from the Asian border through
Egypt and Macedon to Cyrene, with a Greek copy near Kandahar; Pytheas of Marseilles
sailed past Scotland noting the midnight sun. Yet “the court and the city, not Persia
or India, were the setting for Hellenistic culture and literature.” A common culture was
measured by *koine*, shared athletic games, and Hellenic relief after the Gauls were
repulsed from Greece and Delphi (279 BCE). Kings respected Athens’ legacy (theatre,
fourth-century prose, philosophers). Material culture improved (eastern scents, Fayum
land trebled under irrigation, gymnasium “work-outs”), but cultural life was restricted
to a few at the expense of others (Alexandrian taxes; gold-mines with political
prisoners by the 150s). Among Jews, voluntary Hellenizers were stopped after bitter
war; the Maccabean age (175–63 BCE) was essentially resistant; the story of
Ptolemy II’s Septuagint patronage is likely a second-century legend. Court openness
(Serapis as Greek–native fusion; Megasthenes on the Mauryas; Agatharchides on Red Sea
peoples) coexisted with city exclusivity. Intelligent observers early spotted Rome as
the lethal “cloud in the West” (Corinth sacked 146 BCE).

### Military scale and technology (Price)

Royal legitimacy rested partly on military prestige; Hellenistic armies could range
**60,000–80,000** against each other (vs ~30,000/side at Chaeronea). Total destruction
of cities and enslavement became more common. Military novelties included **500
elephants** given by an Indian king to [[seleucus-i-nicator|Seleucus I]] in 302 for
cessation of hostilities (c.400 fought in the next year’s crucial victory); Ptolemies
later used smaller African forest elephants when India supply was cut; a naval arms race
from trireme to quinquereme and prestige “sevens” through “forties”; and the torsion
catapult (probably from Philip) plus Alexander’s siege methods, which made individual
cities insecure before royal assault. Cities remained the basic focus of attachment:
the [[achaean-league|Achaean League]] rebuilt in the 280s–270s by small cities expelling
Macedonian garrisons/tyrants, under Aratus pursuing “ancestral freedom,” until
227–224 when Aratus turned to Macedon against Sparta and popular revolution.

### Philosophy after Aristotle (Barnes)

**Jonathan Barnes** (OHCW) maps a shift: philosophy became an “Art of Living,” ethics
regent; **science divorced from philosophy** and migrated to Alexandria under the
Ptolemies while Athens remained the philosophical centre; theory of knowledge and
scepticism vs dogmatism became passionate concerns; philosophy became **sectarian**
(Stoic / Epicurean / Academic labels) yet popular (Theophrastus ~2,000 students; public
honours to Zeno; an Aristotelian dialogue imprint even at a remote Afghan garrison —
Ai Khanoum). See [[stoicism]], [[epicureanism]], [[hellenistic-science]].

### Art and the eastern shift (Ling / OHCW)

The period 323–31 BCE produced major Greek masterpieces, long neglected because of
regional variation after eastern diffusion and sparse literary sources. The artistic
centre of gravity shifted east ([[pergamon|Pergamum]], Rhodes, Antioch, Alexandria;
Athens still important); patronage moved from cities/citizens to kings →
secularization, broadened subjects, art to entertain rather than elevate; Roman
conquest then moved focus west and schooled Greek artists to imperial ideology.
Sculpture trends included the Nike of Samothrace, Aphrodite types, genre “rococo”
subjects for private collectors, and multi-figure groups (Ludovisi Gaul — sympathetic
enemy physiognomy). Painting high points include Apelles’ battle panels (Alexander
mosaic at Pompeii likely a copy) and the “Unswept Floor” of Sosos at Pergamum as
“trivialization” of art.

## Interstate system and king–city relations (Billows, CHGRW)

[[sabin-van-wees-whitby-cambridge-history-greek-roman-warfare-2007|Billows (CHGRW I.10)]] frames the Hellenistic age as a **structural shift in Greek international relations**: the small-scale system of *poleis* and *ethnē* was drawn into a larger system of Hellenistic monarchies dominated by Macedonian royal families and elites. What was new was not Macedonian diplomatic technique but the existence of a small set of “**super-states**” (the Hellenistic empires) and the dual problem of inter-imperial relations and empire–city relations. Serrati (CHGRW I.14) adds that the Diadochoi were initially not tied to stable states, fighting over an infrastructure-poor Alexander empire; only over decades did regular kingdoms and somewhat less purely imperialist revenue systems emerge. Balance-of-power cooperation existed mainly to keep the throne exclusive (military path to the diadem: Agathocles 305; Attalus I 238).

Despite intermittent wars, Billows stresses a real, acknowledged **balance of power** among the three major kingdoms, expressed in dynastic intermarriage, mutual recognition of royal status, and ambassadorial exchange; limited warfare meant jockeying for power short of destroying rival dynasties (Polybius on Philip V–Antiochus III vs Ptolemy V as unfair). Empire–city relations rested on mutual dependence: kings needed cities for manpower, administration, communications, and economy; cities could rarely escape royal domination. The working solution adapted city-state benefactor culture: kings granted limited local autonomy and privileges as “benefactors”; cities voted quasi-religious and other honours; royal commands were phrased as polite requests and obeyed as gratitude (**euergetism**/honour economy — cross-link [[euergetism]], [[hellenistic-monarchy]]).

Regional contrast: Asian Greek cities (more used to royal power from Achaemenid times; many royal foundations) were often content with limited autonomy, whereas southern Greece remembered Persian-war independence, with Ptolemaic competition funding anti-Antigonid disruption and Aetolian/Achaean federal alternatives to Macedonian domination.

**Formal instruments** (ascending intrusiveness): *symbolai* (legal redress for foreigners), interstate arbitration, treaties, *isopolity* (shared citizenship potential without losing home citizenship), and rare *sympolity*/*homopoliteia* (political fusion — equal e.g. Aetolian/Achaean Leagues or Stiris–Medeon; unequal e.g. Cos absorbing Calymnos ~205). Neutral arbitration became the normal dispute-settlement mode; Rhodes a favourite arbitrator; kings often delegated (e.g. Antigonus Monophthalmus had Mytilene arbitrate Teos–Lebedos); the Samos–Priene dispute ran through Alexander, Antigonus, Lysimachus, Antiochus (probably III), Philip V, Rhodians, finally Romans. Parallel sacred instruments: panhellenic *asylia* campaigns for sanctuary inviolability after fourth-century violations (Magnesia’s Artemis Leucophryene; Smyrna’s Aphrodite Stratonicis; Teos “holy and inviolate”); expanded panhellenic festivals and sacred truces; proliferation of *proxenies*; foreign-judge panels (first known instance 311, Magnesians at Cyme at Antigonus’s suggestion).

After 197 Flamininus and Rome announced Greek freedom at the Isthmian Games 196 (free, ungarrisoned, no tribute, own laws — Polyb. 18.46.5) and withdrew forces; Greek politicians split (Philopoemen literal independence vs Aristaenus consult Rome always). After the Third Macedonian War Rome disciplined less-than-cheerful allies by deportations and Epirus devastation, reducing “autonomy” to local matters too insignificant for Rome — lip service so long as Roman interests were not impinged, then *gratia* and subservience.

## Interaction

The Hellenistic world is the matrix within which later processes develop:
[[urbanization|Hellenistic urbanism]] (new royal capitals and grid-planned cities),
the first continuous era-dating ([[seleucid-era]]), [[ruler-cult]], the transformed
[[hellenistic-polis|city]], the [[euergetism|civic benefaction]] of the rich, and the
[[hellenistic-science|science]] and scholarship of [[alexandria|Alexandria's]] Museum and
Library. It interacts with the [[rise-of-rome]] (which will consume it) and, eastward, with
the [[mauryan-empire|Mauryan]] world (Seleucus' cession to Chandragupta).

## Plural Society, Not a Unity

J. K. Davies (CAH VII.1 ch. 8) cautions against treating the Hellenistic world as a single
"civilization." Beneath a thin cosmopolitan top layer of [[koine-greek|koine]]-speaking
courts, intellectuals, and great landowners lay **a multiplicity of societies** —
"interacting little with each other" — and it is doubtful that any unified "Hellenistic
economy" existed (most production was local subsistence). The unifying view, and the very
word "Hellenistic," is a 19th-century construct; the reality was a [[hellenistic-polis|plural,
layered society]]. See [[ptolemaic-economy]], [[seleucid-hellenization]]. Price’s post-colonial
corrective to older historiography (Hellenization as royal power, not gift of civilization)
aligns with this layered picture.

## End Conditions

Within this volume the order is in vigorous, expansive equilibrium. Its eventual
end — the piecemeal Roman conquest from 200 BCE to the death of Cleopatra VII in
30 BCE — lies beyond CAH VII.1 and is the subject of later volumes. Price’s terminal
dates: Macedon conquered **168**, Attalid bequest **133**, Seleucid rump to Rome **64**,
Egypt **31**. The *cultural* Hellenistic world, by contrast, did not end with Roman
conquest: it was absorbed, so that Greek remained the language of the eastern Roman
Empire and the vehicle of early Christianity for centuries.

## Thonemann's Survey Frame (2016)

[[thonemann-hellenistic-age-2016|Peter Thonemann]] (*The Hellenistic Age*) restates the
standard bookends (**323–30 BCE**) while sharpening geography and constructivism.

**Zone.** As usually understood, a temperate strip of western Afro-Eurasia between the
**25th and 45th parallels**, Adriatic/Libya to the Himalayas — broadly the lands
Alexander ruled at death: eastern Mediterranean and Black Sea, Egypt and Levant,
Mesopotamia, Iranian plateau, lands north and south of the Hindu Kush. These core
regions were Greek-speaking or ruled by Greek-speaking dynasties from the fourth to
first centuries BCE. "Hellenistic Carthage/Arabia/India" is sometimes used for outlying
connections; "Hellenistic Ireland/China" would seem perverse.

**Unities and limits.** Seleucid, Antigonid, Ptolemaic, and Attalid monarchies really did
resemble each other more than any fifth/fourth-century kingdom; new artistic styles
(portraiture, genre realism, "Hellenistic baroque") left marks from Greece to the Ganges;
Isis and Serapis cults spread across the Greek-speaking world. Meaningful
generalizations about "the Hellenistic family/economy/aesthetic/world-view" remain hard.
Artificial unity is often imposed by excluding Sicily, Parthia, Iron Age Europe, Jewish
literature, and Roman Hellenism — "the Hellenistic world is, very largely, what we make
it" (see [[hellenistic-historiography]]).

**Narrative spine.** Philip II → Alexander (334–323) → Diadochi → three kingdoms →
Polybius's *symplokē* from the 140th Olympiad (220–216) → Roman absorption ending at
**Alexandria, 12 August 30 BCE**. Thonemann's thematic chapters supply case studies:
[[demetrius-i-poliorcetes|Demetrius]] for [[hellenistic-monarchy]];
[[eratosthenes|Eratosthenes]] and the [[museum-library-of-alexandria|Museum/Library]] for
[[hellenistic-science]]; edge "encounters" ([[ai-khanoum]], [[ashoka]],
[[eudoxus-of-cyzicus]], [[olbia]], Villa of the Papyri / [[hellenization-of-rome]]);
[[priene]] as ordinary *polis* microhistory. William Tarn's line, quoted with approval:
Greek "might take a man from Marseilles to India, from the Caspian to the Cataracts."
