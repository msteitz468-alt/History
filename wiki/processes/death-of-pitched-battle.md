# The Death of Pitched Battle

**Definition and scope.** The collapse, in the 1860s–70s, of the
[[verdict-of-battle|classic pitched battle]] as a functioning settlement
institution: the point after which even facially decisive battlefield verdicts were
no longer accepted by the defeated side, and wars could be ended only by
devastation. Framed as a process by Whitman
([[whitman-verdict-of-battle-2012|The Verdict of Battle]], 2012, ch. 6), whose two
diagnostic cases are the [[american-civil-war-1861-1865|American Civil War]] and
the Franco-Prussian War. It is *not* the disappearance of battles — the name
persisted (Verdun, Stalingrad) — but the death of battle's *verdict function*.

## Causal drivers

1. **Sovereignty shifted from princes to peoples.** A monarch could stake a
   province on a day's wager and cede it when he lost; a sovereign people cannot
   lose a battle and go home, because the stake is now the nation itself. Defeated
   populations (the Confederate South, republican France after
   [[battle-of-sedan-1870|Sedan]]) refused the verdict and fought on by siege-
   resistance and [[guerrilla-warfare|guerrilla war]].
2. **The revival of just-war moralism.** As wars became contests over causes and
   righteousness rather than legal claims ([[just-war]] displacing
   [[jus-victoriae]]), compromise verdicts became dishonorable; the enemy became a
   criminal to be crushed, not a litigant to be satisfied.
3. **The end of proprietary stakes.** Eighteenth-century wars were fought for
   divisible property ([[proprietorial-warfare]]); nationalist wars were fought
   for indivisible identity, which no verdict can apportion.
4. **Military-technical change (explicitly rejected as the driver).** Whitman
   rejects rifling (via Civil War historians Griffith, Nosworthy, and Hess — Hess:
   the war ground on because of "political determination on the part of civilians
   and government alike," not rifle range), Keegan's army-size/logistics argument
   (Sedan and Solferino disposed of entire armies in a day), and railroads as
   sufficient causes; Solferino (1859) and Königgrätz (1866) still worked as
   verdicts with modern weapons. Schmitt's chivalry-decline thesis he calls
   "little short of perverse": the "duel of honor" conception of war was a
   *nineteenth-century Romantic invention* — born with Robert Plumer Ward's 1795
   treatise, popularized by Walter Scott, Kent, and Pütter — reaching its zenith
   (Ems Dispatch honor-war of 1870, "a red rag on the Gallic bull") exactly as
   battle warfare collapsed.

**The republic mechanism.** Restrained battle warfare "had always been linked
with the success of monarchical legitimacy." The pattern is diagnostic: wars
between monarchs stayed classic for three generations after the American
Revolution first introduced guerrilla warfare (Napoleon deliberately
re-monarchized war from 1801, marrying a Habsburg; Solferino was fought with
three monarchs personally present); wars involving republics — the
Mexican-American War (1846–48, the first war between two republics), the
American Civil War, the Third Republic's *guerre à outrance* — went "dirty" and
unbounded. At Valmy (1792) Prussia stood ready to negotiate in standard
eighteenth-century fashion until news arrived the French monarchy had been
abolished — whereupon it refused to treat with a republic. Republics lacked the
professional standing army that sustained the Kriegsmanier ("nations against
armies," the levée en masse) and lacked a law of dynastic succession to frame
stakes; their wars posed foundational legitimacy questions no verdict could
settle.

## Major phases

- **1859–66, the last verdicts:** [[battle-of-solferino-1859|Solferino]] decides
  the war of 1859 in a day; Königgrätz (1866) decides the Austro-Prussian War.
- **1861–65, the American breakdown:** Gettysburg wears the trappings of decision
  but decides nothing; the war degenerates into guerrilla conflict and is ended
  only by Sherman's March and general devastation.
- **1870–71, the European breakdown:** [[battle-of-sedan-1870|Sedan]] is a
  textbook day-long verdict — Napoleon III surrenders his sword — yet the French
  people repudiate it; months of sieges and francs-tireurs follow until Moltke has
  "marched to the sea."
- **Post-1871:** the verdict function never returns; twentieth-century wars end in
  annihilation or exhaustion, and the modern humanitarian law of war (Geneva/Hague)
  replaces the [[jus-victoriae]]. Booty law dies on the same schedule: an 1871
  French high-court ruling held Lockean sanctity of private property incompatible
  with taking booty — "the 1870s" as the sharp turning point. The crisis of
  irregular-combatant status (Partisan Rangers, francs-tireurs) produced the first
  new doctrines of the modern law of war, Lieber's and Bluntschli's rules on
  guerrilla warfare and occupation.

## Interaction

Feeds directly into [[emergence-of-modern-war]], [[total-war]], and
[[peoples-war-clausewitz|Clausewitzian people's war]]; runs parallel to the
[[industrialization-of-warfare]]. Contrast [[short-war-illusion]]: 1914 planners
still expected a decisive-battle war the institution could no longer deliver.

## End conditions

The process was complete by 1871 and is irreversible on Whitman's account: no
modern polity can credibly pre-commit to accept a battlefield verdict, because
popular sovereignty and just-war morality both forbid it.

## Historiography

Whitman's legal-institutional explanation competes with technological
determinism (railroads and rifles killed decisive battle) and with David Bell's
*The First Total War* (2007), which locates the break in the French
Revolutionary/Napoleonic era rather than the 1860s. John Keegan's "era of decisive
battle drawing to a close" supplies the standard military-history framing.
Whitman's late dating rests on treating Solferino and Königgrätz as functioning
verdicts — a point critics dispute.
