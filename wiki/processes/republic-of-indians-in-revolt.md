## Definition and Scope

The "Republic of Indians in revolt" names the century-long wave of Andean indigenous resistance and insurrection running from roughly 1680 to the suppression of the last rebel nuclei around 1790. Luis Miguel Glave's analysis centres forces *within* Indian society rather than the macro-structural impositions usually invoked: as he frames it, the objective is "to approach a political economy and an ethnology of mobilization, as well as to sound out Andean thinking and daily habits of resistance leading up to the late colonial explosions" (Ch. 22, p. 503).

The process is not a single unified assault on colonial power but a series of qualitatively different struggles: small movements in the Audiencia of Quito from 1680; the highland-and-jungle rebellion of [[juan-santos-atahualpa]] in the 1740s–1756; urban riots in Cochabamba (1730), Arequipa (January 1780), and Quito (1765); and finally the massive insurrections of [[tupac-amaru-jose-gabriel]] (Tupac Amaru II) and [[tupaj-katari]] beginning in 1780. These peaks of conflict sit atop a century of daily resistance, legal manoeuvre, migration, and localised violence.

## Causal Drivers

**Transformation of the tribute system.** Between 1690 and 1730 the old community-based *tasa* (collective quota paid partly in kind) gave way to individual *padrones de tributarios*—fiscal censuses listing each household head. "The peasant faced the state without a buffer." This individualisation, combined with the extension of tribute liability to all indigenous people (*originarios* and *forasteros* alike), severed the moral-reciprocal character of the levy and made it "an economic imposition, pure and simple."

**The *reparto* or forced sale.** Under Bourbon policy, *corregidores* (district governors of natives) were required to force indigenous people to buy unwanted goods—paper, hardware, mules—at inflated prices. This made the *corregidor* not merely a despot but a "commercial monopolist with a political lock on alternative opportunities," creating endemic local resentments.

**Hacienda expansion and market penetration.** From the end of the seventeenth century the colonial market shifted from extracting surplus from ethnic economies to obligating indigenous people, as households, to consume products for which they had to pay with their labour "in spaces controlled by the new non-Indian proprietors." The agropastoral great estate (*hacienda*) swallowed communities whole; those that survived were forced to work on neighbouring estates to pay monetary tribute.

**End of the "ethnic era" and emergence of generic Indianness.** As old ethnic lordships (*señoríos*) collapsed and diverse Andean groups converged on shared conditions of subordination, a person's specific ethnic heritage "mattered less in accounting for her or his fortunes than the fact of belonging to that vast lumping category called 'Indians,' which imposed on all ethnic groups a common set of problems." This generic Indianness was the social fuel of mass mobilisation.

**The "Andean Utopia."** Messianism centred on an Inka empire reborn created "forms of messianism and idealized Indianism over and over." The invocation of an Inka past that embodied indigenous wholeness was not a backward-looking nativism but, in Glave's reading, "an attempt at modernization within the Indian 'republic' itself."

## Major Phases

**Phase 1 — Precursors in the ethnic era (c. 1620–1680).** Localised revolts in the La Paz coca valleys (1622–1626, 1664), Uru attacks on transport routes (1632), the pan-Andean conspiracy scare of 1666. These actions were "inward-looking regional" without possibilities for larger coordination; their geography etched the profile of what was to come.

**Phase 2 — North Andean wave (1690–c. 1750).** Triggered by the 1690 general numeration, epidemics, and famine. Waves of locally important violence in the Audiencia of Quito studied by Segundo Moreno. Rebels seized plazas and churches, crowned leaders as native kings, attacked *aduaneros* (tax agents). Women "frequently egged men on to increasingly daring violence and took part in violence by mutilating the bodies of lynched captives." The Quito rebellion of the *barrios* of 1765 was the culminating urban moment.

**Phase 3 — The conjuncture of 1720–1740.** A general epidemic killed over 200,000 in 1720; El Niño floods; Viceroy Castelfuerte's authoritarian reforms; increased mita quotas. The Cochabamba urban uprising of 1730 (Alejo Calatayud), the Oruro uprising of 1737 (Juan Vélez de Córdoba proclaiming Inka descent), and a wave of corregidor killings showed that the insurgency was transcending local bounds without yet achieving coordination.

**Phase 4 — Juan Santos Atahuallpa (1742–1756).** The first rebel to achieve a generalised insurgency in the highland-jungle piedmont of central Peru. Santos styled himself "Apo-Inca" (Lord Inka) and forged an alliance of highland migrants and lowland Ashaninka ('Campa') peoples. He defeated four Spanish military campaigns, held the region for over a decade, and sent a message that became legendary: "The Spaniards' time is up." His kingdom was never defeated, only isolated. He was unique as "an effective politician working at a moment when his project was not quixotic or exotic but relevant to live issues throughout the Spanish system."

**Phase 5 — The Great Rebellion (1780–1782).** The [[tupac-amaru-rebellion-1780]] had three regional hearths: (i) The **Cusco revolt** led by [[tupac-amaru-jose-gabriel]] from November 1780; he captured and executed *corregidor* Arriaga, won the battle of Sangarara, besieged Cusco, and was captured and executed in May 1781. Some 100,000 lives were lost from an affected population of 1.2 million—a mortality rate of roughly 8 percent. (ii) The **Titicaca/Puno hearth**, where Diego Cristóbal Tupac Amaru and the [[aymara-kingdoms|Aymara]] kuraka Pedro Vilca Apaza sustained the war after the rebel king's death, eventually winning a military victory at Puno before Diego Cristóbal's armistice in 1782. (iii) The **La Paz/Chayanta hearth** led by [[tupaj-katari]] and Tomás Catari (see [[siege-of-la-paz-1781]]).

**Phase 6 — Post-rebellion repression (1782–c. 1790).** Punitive measures destroyed kuraka lineages that had shown disloyalty; substitute caciques were appointed from outside indigenous communities. Inka cultural expression was systematically persecuted: performances of Inka plays banned throughout the viceroyalty; the *Royal Commentaries* of Inca Garcilaso de la Vega proscribed; Quechua prohibited in schools. The intendancy system replaced the *corregidores*, completing administrative restructuring.

## Geographic Spread

The rebellion cycle affected virtually the entire Andean viceroyalty, from the Audiencia of Quito (Ecuador) in the north to Chayanta and La Paz in what is now Bolivia. Peaks of violence were concentrated in the South Andean region — the provinces of Cusco, Tinta, Puno, and the Bolivian altiplano — but urban riots and conspiracies occurred in Lima, Cochabamba, Arequipa, and Oruro. The rebellion also drew on connections between highland and Amazonian-piedmont populations (Juan Santos's kingdom).

In Glave's mapping, local rebellions had been spreading across the entire territory since the beginning of the century and "becoming more numerous and closer together by mid-century." The Great Rebellion of 1780–1782 partially synchronised these local dynamics without subsuming them: many constituent events "turn out on analysis to have been disconnected ground-level events, bridged at the summit by ideology, by exchange of strategic messages among rebel leaders."

## Interaction

The rebellion cycle is concurrent_with and enabled_by [[bourbon-reforms]], which simultaneously provoked resistance and prevented the colonial state from conceding genuine reform. It is part_of the broader Atlantic crisis of the *ancien régime* that also produced the French Revolution of 1789: "Both countries were experiencing general systemic crises closely related to an unsuccessful transition in systems of extraction from their peasantries."

The process interacts with [[potosi-and-colonial-silver]] through the [[mita]] system, which generated sustained resentment in the southern Andes. It is analogous_to the Comunero revolt in New Granada (1781) and the North Andean movements against textile-mill labour. It produced the trans-ethnic, Inka-ideological political language that the [[tupac-amaru-rebellion-1780]] temporarily brought to civilisational scale.

The rebellion is contrasts_with Tupac Amaru II's own inclusivist political project: he sought a multiethnic urban-rural coalition under Inka rule open to mestizos and Creoles, while the Aymara radical line represented by Vilca Apaza and [[tupaj-katari]] insisted on an ethnically indigenous and messianically Andeanist stance. Glave identifies this internal tension as a key factor in the rebellion's failure to achieve lasting unity.

## End Conditions

The rebel conjuncture ended with the military defeat of each regional hearth between 1782 and 1783 and the comprehensive post-rebellion repression. But Glave stresses that repression did not change the basic situation: "conditions for explosions of local contradictions, for violence and struggle among social sectors, continued." No generalised rebellion ensued until 1814, but persistent local tension generated tumults, mutinies, and riots throughout the interval.

The deeper legacy was the permanent entrenchment of the Inkari myth ("hidden redeemer") in popular Andean consciousness, documented by ethnographers especially in the 1970s. Institutionally, the rebellion's aftermath saw the creation of a new royal Audiencia in Cusco, the disappearance of the *corregidores*, and the Intendancy system — completing the Bourbon administrative revolution that the rebellion had paradoxically accelerated. Indigenous communities "closed in on themselves," with horizontal unity "draconically repressed" and each village left to re-create indigenous identities endogenously.

## Historiography

Glave explicitly frames his contribution against histories that explain revolt primarily through external factors or "macroscopic and 'top-down' processes, which claim to explain revolt but usually explain only its context." The chapter employs Michel de Certeau's distinction between long-term colonial *strategies* and indigenous daily *tactics* of resistance (p. 160), Steve Stern's work on Peru's Indian peoples, and Alberto Flores Galindo's concept of the "Andean Utopia" (*Buscando un Inca*, 1988). Jürgen Golte's *Repartos y rebeliones* (1980) provided the most complete top-down model and has been susceptible to criticism, including from Stern and Ward Stavig. Scarlett O'Phelan's *Un siglo de rebeliones anticoloniales* (1988) offers the most comprehensive panorama of the rebellion cycle. María Eugenia del Valle de Siles remains the leading scholar on the Bolivian (Catarista) phase.
