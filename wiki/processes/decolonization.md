# Decolonization

## Definition and Scope

**Decolonization** — in Prasenjit Duara's chapter in [[mcneill-cwh-v7-2015|CWH VII]]
— is, narrowly, "the transfer of institutional and legal control by colonial
governments... to indigenously based, formally sovereign states," but more broadly a
movement "championing claims to human justice that had been denied by
[[imperialism]]." Duara focuses on twentieth-century independence from Western and
Japanese rule in **Asia and Africa** (c. 1905–1980s), and deliberately includes
states never formally colonized (China, Iran, Siam) because they shared the
anti-colonial **worldview**. He stresses the **political and ideological** dimension,
especially decolonization's entanglement with [[nationalism]] and the
[[cold-war|Cold War]].

## Causal Drivers

- **The discrediting of the "civilizing mission":** the horrors of the First World
  War shattered European claims to civilizational superiority; intellectuals East and
  West turned to indigenous traditions for alternative civilizational ideals.
- **Anti-imperial nationalism + socialism:** by the 1920s the movement combined
  advocacy of the **nation state** with an emancipatory, often **socialist** vision
  and a civilizational alternative to European Enlightenment — Gandhi's non-materialist
  Hindu culture, Sun Yat-sen's *wangdao*, Nkrumah's African communitarianism — each
  balanced against Enlightenment goals of progress.
- **The 1917 Russian Revolution and Wilson's "self-determination" (1918)** both
  energized the global movement; the Communist International turned to the colonized
  world from the 1920s.
- **Economic exhaustion of empire:** interwar and postwar sterling crises made the
  British Empire dependent on, yet unable adequately to develop, its colonies.

## Major Phases / Turning Points

- **1905 — Japan defeats Russia:** "the first military defeat of a modern European
  state by an Asian one" gave the nascent movement a *fillip* and a sense of a
  "world-redeeming project."
- **1914–1945:** anti-imperialism acquires its distinctive character; interwar
  imperial schemes of federation and "development" (a "kind of imperialism of nation
  states," e.g. Manchukuo) fail to satisfy demands for parity.
- **1955 — the Bandung Conference** of Afro-Asian solidarity: the symbolic climax,
  and the foundation of Cold War non-alignment.
- **c. 1945–1985:** independence spreads across Asia and Africa to the smaller
  Caribbean and Pacific states.

## Structural tensions (Duara's argument)

Duara reads decolonization through two unresolved tensions inherent in the
nation-state model the colonized adopted:

1. **Plural societies into disciplined nations.** Refashioning "complex and
   pluralistic historical societies into nations with a disciplined loyalty to the
   state" required remaking the hinterland in the image of urban modernizing elites —
   often a "violent transformation" of peasant and communal life. The colonial
   "dualistic society" (a modern coastal/urban sector and a vast subordinated
   hinterland — dependency theory's "articulation of modes of production") bedeviled
   the process.
2. **Universal humanism vs. competitive statehood.** These societies aspired to
   universal humanist goals even as the nation-state form had evolved "to compete for
   resources and domination" — entering what Duara calls "**cognitive globalization**,"
   adopting global conceptions of the person, the world, and the nation.

Both the Gandhian (anti-industrial, self-sufficient) and the socialist (equitable
industrialization) alternatives, Duara notes, "did not survive the twentieth
century"; today's China is "socialist in name only."

## The Cold War complication

Duara emphasizes the neglected entanglement of decolonization with the
[[cold-war|Cold War]]: socialism became central to the anti-colonial vision from the
1920s, peaking with the Vietnam War; full revolutionaries (Mao, Ho Chi Minh) built
socialist states, while others (Nehru) built mixed, state-interventionist economies.
The Cold War both empowered and constrained new states, turning many into proxy
arenas.

## Key Actors

Gandhi, Tagore, Sun Yat-sen, Nehru (the first generation); Mao, Ho Chi Minh, Nkrumah,
Frantz Fanon, Jalal Al-i Ahmad (a more radical generation, the last inspiring Iran's
1979 Islamic Revolution). The British, French, and Japanese empires as the
dismantled imperial powers.

## End Conditions / Legacy

Formal decolonization was largely complete by the 1980s, leaving the **nation state
globally triumphant** (see [[nationalism]]). Its ideological legacy persists — in the
Chinese revolution and in Middle Eastern societies where Islamism became intertwined
with anti-imperialism — even as the socialist and Gandhian alternatives faded.

**[[westad-cold-war|Westad's *The Cold War: A World History*]]** argues that decolonization and the Cold
War were deeply entangled — the newly independent world was the Cold War's principal battleground, and
each superpower offered it a rival model of modernity.
