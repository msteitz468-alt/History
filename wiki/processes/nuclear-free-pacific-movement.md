## Definition and Scope

The nuclear-free Pacific movement was the organised political response of Pacific Island peoples and states to decades of nuclear weapons testing in their region by the United States, Britain, and France. Beginning in the early 1970s as decolonisation gave more territories independent voices, the movement coalesced around the goal of "a nuclear-free and independent Pacific" and produced the South Pacific Nuclear-Free Zone Treaty of 1985 (Rarotonga Treaty). It drew on anti-colonial sentiment, environmental concern, and Islander conceptions of land and identity, distinct from the parallel arms-control discourse of Western anti-nuclear movements.

## Causal Drivers

The movement was enabled by: the post-war decolonisation wave that gave Island states independent foreign policies; the accumulating evidence of radioactive contamination and Islander displacement from [[pacific-nuclear-testing]]; the persistence of French atmospheric testing long after the US, UK, and USSR had signed the 1963 Partial Test Ban Treaty; and Islander anger at the asymmetry between their powerlessness and the nuclear powers' disregard for Islander lives. "So long as the Islands were colonies, Islanders' objections to nuclear tests could be dismissed by the authorities. But in the 1970s, as more territories gained independence and French atmospheric testing offered a rallying point for nuclear-free sentiment, popular opposition became more organised."

## Major Phases

### Emergence (1971–1975)

Heads of government at the first South Pacific Forum meeting in 1971 appealed to France to make its current test series the last in the Pacific; in 1972 they called for an end to all atmospheric testing. The **Conference for a Nuclear-Free Pacific**, held in Fiji in 1975, was the founding moment of a co-ordinated movement: delegates from across the Pacific declared that Pacific peoples were *"sick and tired of being treated like dogs"* and called for a comprehensive nuclear-free zone treaty. The 1975 South Pacific Forum accepted the idea of such a zone in principle.

### Radicalisation and Broadening (1975–1983)

The movement broadened its agenda beyond French testing to encompass opposition to US missile testing at Kwajalein, practice bombing of Kaho'olawe Island in Hawai'i, uranium mining in Australia, and Japanese dumping of radioactive waste. It supported the nuclear-free constitution of Palau, independence movements in French territories, and resistance in East Timor and Irian Jaya. Anti-nuclear activists—including Canadian David McTaggart, who was beaten by French marines boarding his *Greenpeace III* in 1973—dramatised the issue world-wide. In the early 1980s Papua New Guinea and Vanuatu endorsed a South Pacific Nuclear-Free Zone; Vanuatu declared itself nuclear-free; and Palau wrote nuclear-restrictive provisions into its constitution.

### The Rarotonga Treaty (1983–1985)

The decisive diplomatic step came from an unlikely direction: Australia, not an Island nation, proposed a South Pacific Nuclear-Free Zone Treaty at the 1983 South Pacific Forum and chaired the working group that drafted it. Australia's motivation was strategic: it feared that anti-nuclear sentiment provoked by France would be directed against the United States, "undermining its strategic position in the South Pacific." Australia therefore exercised influence over Island states to ensure the final treaty "would be moderate, directed at French bomb testing rather than United States nuclear ship visits or missile testing." Papua New Guinea, Solomon Islands, and Vanuatu wanted stronger provisions—a ban on missile testing and time limits on port visits by nuclear-armed vessels—but these were excluded. A US Congressional report later noted that "the Treaty has no adverse impact on the interests of the United States, since we are not precluded from doing anything we had been doing or were planning to do."

The Treaty of Rarotonga was concluded in 1985; 12 of the 16 Forum countries had signed by 1996. France refused to sign its protocols; so did the United States and Britain. China and the former Soviet Union signed. Islanders tended to see it as "environmental protection" rather than arms control; Western countries regarded it as an arms-control measure.

### Final Crisis (1995–1996)

The end of the Cold War and the 1992 French test moratorium appeared to close the nuclear era. Then in 1995 President Chirac announced France would conduct a final test series. The announcement "sparking outrage in South Pacific countries and protests world-wide": the South Pacific Forum's Secretary-General called it "provocative"; within weeks thousands marched in Pape'ete behind anti-nuclear banners; Greenpeace sailed the *Rainbow Warrior* into Moruroa exclusion zone; and when the first bomb exploded on 5 September 1995, Pape'ete erupted in a major riot—demonstrators stormed the airport terminal with bricks, iron bars, and a bulldozer. France completed eight tests and then joined the Treaty. "In one sense the nuclear era in the Pacific ended in 1996 . . . Almost everyone in the Pacific had long wanted the islands and the ocean to be free of all things nuclear."

### The Palau Special Case

Palau's nuclear-free constitution (1979) was the most radical expression of the movement's constitutionalisation, and it generated the most prolonged conflict. The Palauan constitution required 75 per cent approval at a referendum before nuclear-related activities could occur, effectively making Palau nuclear-free. The United States insisted that Palau's Compact of Free Association include options on 28 per cent of Palauan land for US military use—incompatible with the constitution. Between 1983 and 1993, Palauan leaders took their people through at least eight plebiscites without reaching the 75 per cent threshold. Political violence followed: "one president was gunned down in 1985, his successor killed himself in 1988, and the father of a pro-constitution activist was shot dead." A 1992 referendum finally reduced the threshold to a simple majority; Palau entered free association with the United States and became independent on 1 October 1994. The Palauan struggle illustrated that the nuclear-free movement was "as much anti-war as anti-nuclear," rooted in memories of wartime destruction and Islanders' land rights rather than abstract arms-control principles.

## Geographic Spread

The movement operated simultaneously at village, territorial, national, and regional scales. Its most organised expressions were in: the Marshall Islands (through petitions to the UN Trusteeship Council, civil suits, and compensation negotiations); French Polynesia (through territorial elections and the autonomist/independantiste parties); Palau (through constitutional politics); and the regional arena of the South Pacific Forum. New Zealand's Labour government declared New Zealand nuclear-free when it took office, banning nuclear-armed ship visits—an act that created a rift with the United States and reflected both genuine anti-nuclear sentiment and Islander influence on New Zealand domestic politics.

## End Conditions

The movement achieved its central goal in 1996 when France completed its tests and all five nuclear powers signed the Rarotonga Treaty protocols. However, the contamination of atolls, economic dependencies created by nuclear spending, and ongoing health impacts mean the movement's agenda was only partially fulfilled. Kwajalein continued to be used for US missile testing into the 1990s. The movement transformed Islander political identity, establishing "the idea that the Pacific should be nuclear-free" as a defining element of regional identity.

## Historiography

Coverage of the nuclear-free Pacific movement is strongest in sources dealing with French Polynesia and the Marshall Islands. Key texts cited in [[denoon-cambridge-pacific-islanders-1997]] include Firth, *Nuclear Playground*; Smith, *Micronesia: Decolonisation and US Military Interests*; and for French Polynesia, von Strokirch, *Tahitian Autonomy: Illusion or Reality?* The movement's history is closely intertwined with decolonisation history, making it difficult to separate nuclear politics from broader questions of sovereignty and self-determination. The tension between the movement's radical aspirations (total nuclear-free status) and the moderate Rarotonga Treaty outcome is a recurring analytical theme.
