# Colonial Latin America

The **three-century world (c. 1492–1825) that the conquest opened** — the Spanish
and Portuguese colonial societies built on the ruins of the [[aztec-empire|Aztec]]
and [[inca-empire|Inca]] empires and across the lands between. This is the
**connective tissue** of the wiki's Americas coverage: it bridges the
pre-Columbian civilizations and [[latin-america-modern|modern Latin America]],
and it is the umbrella under which the colonial institutions (encomienda, the
*mita*, the hacienda, the viceroyalty, the *sociedad de castas*) and the
silver-and-slavery economy are filed. The systematic account is Mark Burkholder
and Lyman Johnson's standard textbook [[burkholder-johnson-colonial-latin-america|*Colonial
Latin America*]].

## Definition and Scope

"Colonial Latin America" denotes the **Iberian (Spanish and Portuguese) colonial
order in the Western Hemisphere** from the Caribbean foundations of the 1490s to
the independence wars of the 1810s–20s. It is *not* a single polity but a family
of regional societies — New Spain (Mexico and Central America), Peru and the
Andes, New Granada, the Río de la Plata, and Portuguese Brazil — sharing a common
matrix: conquest, demographic catastrophe, Catholic evangelization, racially
ordered hierarchy, tribute and forced labor, and an economy organized around the
export of precious metals and plantation staples to Europe.

The volume's central insight is that this world was **not designed in Europe**.
The conquests were privately financed, locally recruited ventures led by men of
modest origin (Cortés, [[pizarro-francisco|Pizarro]]); the Crowns followed *after*
the fact, imposing institutions of government, Church, and economy on territories
already seized. Colonial Latin America is therefore best read as a **process** —
the slow elaboration of order over conquest — rather than as a plan executed.

## Causal Drivers

- **The two great conquests** — of [[spanish-conquest-of-mexico-1519|Mexico (1519–21)]]
  and [[spanish-conquest-of-peru-1532|Peru (1532–33)]] — delivered the two
  densely populated, wealthy core regions on which the whole colonial economy was
  built. Both turned on the same combination: indigenous disunity exploited by
  the invaders, plus the [[virgin-soil-epidemics|epidemic catastrophe]] that ran
  ahead of and alongside them.
- **The Iberian inheritance** — eight centuries of *Reconquest* had bred a society
  that prized military service as the road to wealth and honor, fused Church and
  Crown, and normalized the subordination and enslavement of conquered non-Christians.
  Conquistadors carried these attitudes, and the institution of the
  *encomienda*, directly to the New World.
- **The [[columbian-exchange-1492|Columbian Exchange]]** — the biological joining
  of the hemispheres, which (through disease) emptied the land of its peoples and
  (through livestock and crops) remade its economy and ecology.
- **Silver** — the discovery of [[potosi-and-colonial-silver|Potosí (1545)]] and
  Mexican deposits turned the colonies into the engine of a
  [[global-silver-trade|global bullion economy]].
- **The [[atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic slave trade]]** — which supplied the labor
  for the sugar plantations of Brazil and the Caribbean as the indigenous
  population collapsed.

## Major Phases

1. **Conquest and Caribbean foundations (1490s–1550s).** Hispaniola and the
   Antilles as the staging base; the conquests of Mexico and Peru; the wars of
   the conquistadors (Peru's civil wars, 1537–48); the "ebb tide" of conquest
   into Central America, New Granada, Chile, the Río de la Plata, and Brazil.
2. **The mature colonial order (c. 1570–1700).** The Toledan reforms in Peru, the
   silver boom, the consolidation of viceregal administration, the
   *[[casta-system|sociedad de castas]]*, the great mission Church, and the
   hacienda economy. (Cycles 2–3 of the source.)
3. **Reform and crisis (1700–1808).** The
   [[bourbon-reforms|Bourbon (and Pombaline) reforms]] — the attempt to
   recentralize, tax, and defend the empire — which strained colonial loyalties.
   (Cycle 4.)
4. **Dissolution (1808–1825).** The Napoleonic crisis of the Iberian monarchies
   triggering [[spanish-american-independence|the wars of independence]].

## Geographic Spread

From the Caribbean hearth the colonial order radiated to the two mainland cores
(central Mexico, the central Andes) and then, in the "ebb tide" of conquest, to
the peripheries — Central America and Yucatán, New Granada (the Muisca), Chile
(checked at the Mapuche frontier), the Río de la Plata and Paraguay (the Guaraní),
and Portuguese Brazil along the Atlantic coast. Settlement intensity tracked
native population density and mineral wealth: dense and rich at the cores,
thin and contested at the frontiers, where decentralized peoples (Chichimeca,
Mapuche, Maya) resisted for decades or centuries.

## Interaction

Colonial Latin America sits at the intersection of several wiki processes: the
[[columbian-exchange-1492|Columbian Exchange]] (its biological premise), the
[[global-silver-trade|global silver trade]] (its economic motor), the
[[atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic slave trade]] (its labor regime in the plantation
zones), and the early-modern expansion of the Catholic Church
([[spiritual-conquest-and-colonial-church|spiritual conquest]] as its cultural project). It is the proximate cause of
[[latin-america-modern|modern Latin America]].

## End Conditions

The colonial order did not collapse from internal exhaustion so much as from an
**external shock**: Napoleon's 1808 deposition of the Bourbon king broke the
legitimacy that bound the colonies to Spain, and the
[[bourbon-reforms|Bourbon reforms]] had already eroded Creole goodwill. The
resulting [[spanish-american-independence|independence wars (1808–25)]] ended
three centuries of Iberian rule — though much of the colonial social and economic
structure (the hacienda, the racial hierarchy, the export economy) survived into
the republics.

## Historiography

Burkholder and Johnson's textbook (1st ed. 1990; 10th ed. 2019) is the standard
English-language synthesis, integrating political, economic, social, demographic,
racial, religious, and cultural history. The field has moved decisively from the
old **institutional/"great conquistador"** narrative toward **social and ethnohistory**
— the New Philology's reading of indigenous-language sources, the quantitative
study of the demographic collapse (see [[pre-columbian-american-population]]),
the history of slavery and the *castas*, and gender history. The interpretive
charge of the whole field is framed by the [[black-legend|Black Legend]] debate
over Spanish cruelty, propaganda, and the ethics of conquest.

## CHNPA II.2 — Mesoamerica native societies under colonial rule (MacLeod)

*Edit-append from Murdo J. MacLeod, “Mesoamerica since the Spanish Invasion: An Overview,”
[[cambridge-history-native-peoples-americas-v2-2|Cambridge History of the Native Peoples of
the Americas, Vol. II Mesoamerica Part 2]] (2000). Full multi-century synthesis lives on
[[colonial-mesoamerica-native-peoples]]; this section records only MacLeod’s framing of the
**colonial** centuries within the broader Latin American colonial order.*

For most of Mesoamerica the **first two centuries after the Spanish invasion** set the scene
for related debates about change in native societies: nature, periodization, pace, and
regional/temporal variation of change, without agreement on what change or stasis means.
Recent trends attempt to see change/stasis from the native point of view (“from the inside
out”), breaking with earlier models of oppression on a mostly passive mass. Comparative
village/region approaches face baseline and equal-pressure data problems.

**Spanish paternalism and the corporate village.** Received knowledge (challenged by Part 2
chapters showing earlier state-level attacks) holds that the great assault on Native American
society — especially communal lands — was **not** primarily colonial, although intrusions were
common and often pervasive, but came during Mexico’s Liberal Reforma (focal villain the Ley
Lerdo of 1856). Colonial Spanish paternalism treated natives as a protected caste of free but
lowly Crown vassals; litigious indigenous villages and institutions (*cofradía*, *caja de
comunidad*) provided partial shelter and outside brokers/patrons. After independence, ambitious
regional elites structured government for profit and land access; cofradías, already weakened
by late [[bourbon-reforms|Bourbon reforms]], came under renewed attack.

**Institutional cluster shared with the wider colonial system.** MacLeod’s Mesoamerica deploys
the same labor, resettlement, and Church tools as the empire-wide pages above —
[[encomienda]], [[congregacion|congregación]]/[[reduccion|reducción]],
[[spiritual-conquest-and-colonial-church|missions and cofradías]], [[hacienda]],
[[casta-system|ethnic hierarchy]] — but stresses **extreme regional variation**: central Mexican
continuity under native elites; southern extractive “neglect” (Yucatan, Oaxaca, highland Maya);
northern wars of attrition and silver-mission complexes; zones of refuge on porous frontiers.
Catastrophic [[virgin-soil-epidemics|demographic collapse]] (Caribbean and tropical coasts
virtually emptied; highland losses up to ~90%) underwrote labor redesign and the
[[black-legend|Black Legend]] / White Legend quarrel over responsibility.

See [[colonial-mesoamerica-native-peoples]] for the full colonial-to-contemporary arc and
regional process links.

`[CONTRADICTION]` — **the colony and underdevelopment.** Burkholder & Johnson present
the colonial order as a **complex, negotiated, internally varied** formation and
resist reducing it to a single act of plunder. Eduardo Galeano's
[[galeano-open-veins|*Open Veins of Latin America*]] (1971) reads the *entire* three
centuries (and beyond) as one continuous **extraction** that directly *caused* Latin
American poverty — the [[dependency-theory|dependency]] thesis that "our wealth has
always generated our poverty by nourishing the prosperity of others." The two poles
frame the standing controversy over the
[[causes-of-latin-american-underdevelopment|causes of Latin American underdevelopment]]
(dependency/extraction vs. institutionalist vs. measured-historiographical accounts).
