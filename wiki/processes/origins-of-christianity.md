# The Origins and Spread of Christianity (to 70 CE)

The emergence of the Christian movement from the ferment of first-century Palestinian Judaism
and its spread across the eastern Roman empire in the **forty years between the death of Jesus
(c. 30 CE) and the destruction of the Temple (70 CE)**. Treated in [[bowman-cah-v10-1996|CAH X]]
Ch. 17 (**G. W. Clarke**).

## Within Second Temple Judaism

Clarke situates the movement firmly **inside the religious world of first-century Judaism** — a
context "which lacked sharply defined doctrines and practices," with no accepted orthodoxy, a
**range of sects** ([[second-temple-jewish-sects|Pharisees, Sadducees, Essenes]], the "Philosophy
of Judas" with its Sicarii and Zealots) and "a bewildering array of individual ascetics, prophets
and preachers." The ministry of **John the Baptist** and of **Jesus of Nazareth** (c. 30 CE) fits
"comfortably into the continuing debate within the Judaism of the day": teaching in synagogues,
attending the Temple, keeping the festivals, disputing over purity, sacrifice, the Torah, and the
Law — with **reformist tendencies** (priority of moral over ceremonial law) and an urgent
**eschatological** message of repentance before the impending Kingdom. Jesus' "increasingly
catholic" sense of "the children of Abraham" — preaching to sinners, tax-gatherers, Gentiles —
opened the lines of debate that "eventually opened the way to 'Gentile Christianity'." His
**ignominious death** (c. 30) did not end the movement: the disciples' conviction of his
**resurrection** "became the decisive confirmation of his messiahship."

## The fugitive evidence

Clarke stresses how **partial and haphazard** the evidence is. He works mainly from the seven
genuine letters of **Paul** (supplemented cautiously by **Acts**, "composed several generations
after these events … with a sharply focused historicizing agenda"), eschewing the anachronistic
self-understanding of later canonical Christianity. The result is a map full of **gaps**: of
**Egypt and Cyrene** (named at Pentecost but lacking any Pauline connexion, their nascent
communities perhaps annihilated with their parent Jewish communities in the revolt of 115–17),
of Arabia and the lands east of the Euphrates, "we cannot go any further without blind
conjecture."

## The shape of the spread

- **Palestine.** The **Jerusalem church** under James the brother of the Lord is presented (in
  Acts' staggered statistics) as the centre — but **overwhelmingly Jewish-Christian**,
  Hebrew-speaking, "zealots for the Law," Gentile converts visible only outside the city. The
  scattering after Stephen's death spread the word through Judaea and Samaria; the recorded
  communities cluster in the **hellenized seaboard cities and ports** (Caesarea, Joppa, Lydda,
  Phoenicia), inland Galilee fading "completely from our view."
- **Syria — Antioch.** Thanks to Paul's dozen years "in the regions of Syria and Cilicia,"
  **Antioch on the Orontes** appears as the ***fons et origo* of "Gentile Christianity"** — "here
  the process of Christian self-identification is declared to have its beginning."
- **Asia Minor.** Paul's mission reached the cities of **Galatia** (Iconium, Lystra, Derbe) and
  Phrygia (the Lycus valley churches: Colossae, Laodicea, Hierapolis), and made the metropolis
  **Ephesus** his headquarters along the Aegean — the "seven cities" of the **Apocalypse**
  (Ephesus, Smyrna, Pergamum, Sardis, Thyatira, Philadelphia, Laodicea) marking the region that
  was "well on the way to becoming the heartland of Hellenic Christianity."
- **Greece and the West.** The crossing to **Macedonia and Achaea** (Philippi — a Latin colony
  where Paul met "characteristically Roman" resistance and revealed his **citizenship** —
  Thessalonica, Corinth), and ultimately **Rome**.

## The method: the synagogue network

Clarke's recurring observation is that Paul's mission was **urban** and worked through the
**Jewish synagogue communities and their fringe of Gentile "godfearers"** (sympathizers) —
though the message also reached tribal/village areas (the Galatian *perichoros*). The movement
penetrated, "at least for some," into the **slave-owning levels of Hellenic society** (the
household codes of Ephesians and Colossians; the letter to Philemon). Resistance could be strong
in traditional Jewish towns (Christianity failing to establish itself even by the 4th century in
Sepphoris or Tiberias), and the following in Palestine was "neither particularly numerous nor
evenly distributed."

## Significance

By 70 CE Christianity was a scattered network of small, mostly **urban** communities across the
eastern Mediterranean, still emerging from — and in tension with — its **Jewish matrix**. The
pivotal development of the period was the **Gentile mission** (the "Apostolic Decree" freeing
Gentile converts from circumcision and full observance of the Law), which set Christianity on the
path to becoming a religion distinct from Judaism. The destruction of the Temple (70), the
chronological terminus of Clarke's study, removed the institutional centre of the parent faith
and accelerated the separation.
