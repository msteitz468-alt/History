## Definition and Scope

The Post-Wari Reorganization — archaeologists' "Late Intermediate Period" (LIP), approximately eleventh to fifteenth centuries CE — is the phase of accelerated regional diversification that followed the collapse of the [[andean-middle-horizon]]'s two pan-Andean powers, [[wari]] and [[tiwanaku]]. Lumbreras frames it precisely: "'Intermediate' indicates a period of diverse, regional developments between horizon cultures … the Late Intermediate was an era of accentuated variety, sometimes carrying forward and reworking already-ancient cultural legacies."

Where the Middle Horizon imposed a degree of interregional uniformity (orthogonal planned cities, standardised ceramics, Wari road-and-redistribution system), the LIP saw that uniformity fragment into a dozen or more distinct regional formations — kingdoms, chiefdoms, confederations, and village-level polities — organised around local ecological and economic conditions. The most powerful was the [[chimor]] kingdom of the north Peruvian coast; the most numerous were the pastoral [[aymara-kingdoms]] of the Titicaca basin; the most historically consequential was the small Cusco polity that would become the [[inca-empire]].

Chronological scope: c. CE 1000 to c. 1438 (the Inka conquest of [[chimor]]) or 1532 (Spanish arrival), depending on region.

## Causal Drivers

**Wari imperial collapse.** The city of Wari was abandoned around the tenth century CE with buildings left unfinished, indicating sudden breakdown rather than managed decline. "When Ayacucho lost its military and ideological power … other systems of regional articulation favored the growth of divergently urbanizing social forces." Post-Wari Ayacucho itself reverted to a Huarpa-like pastoral economy: "The area was left destroyed and desolate. Peasants took refuge in high areas and did not undertake new projects of agricultural development."

**Tiwanaku decline.** Tiwanaku declined contemporaneously, probably compounded by prolonged drought (raised-field agriculture was partially abandoned from the twelfth or thirteenth century CE). The [[aymara-kingdoms]] that emerged around [[lake-titicaca]] — Qulla and Lupaqa chief among them — were fundamentally pastoral adaptations to the post-Tiwanaku altiplano.

**Divergent coastal and highland trajectories.** A key structural insight of Lumbreras is that the coastal and sierra responses to Wari's fall were opposite. On the coast, where maritime and irrigation economies were independent of Wari prestige-good supply chains, cities grew rapidly once freed from Wari: "The urban system met with great success on the Pacific coast … Long-distance traffic satisfied demands for luxury items but was not vital to the survival of these peoples." In the sierra, the reverse held: "Many populations sloughed off Wari urbanism … communal forms of articulation with village-like characteristics were resumed."

**Post-Wari "epigonal" ceramics as marker.** The breakdown of Wari's polychrome pigment monopoly forced coastal and sierran peoples to revert to simpler ceramic palettes. Residual "tricolor" styles (Uhle's "epigonal") mark the transition zones of Middle Horizon 3–4 before new regional traditions fully emerged.

## Major Phases

**Phase 1 — Immediate post-Wari disorder (c. tenth–eleventh centuries CE).** Wari administrative cities abandoned or readapted to local purposes. "The eleventh through thirteenth centuries C.E. … saw the abandonment of many Wari settlements or their readaptation to local purposes." In the sierra, fortified high-altitude villages multiply; lowland settlements are abandoned. In Ayacucho (Wari's former heartland) the post-Wari population retreated to high villages of "30 to 100 circular and agglutinated houses of the Arqalla type," with purely domestic manufacturing. "As the 'Pax Wari' disappeared, sacking and invasion must have become commonplace."

**Phase 2 — Coastal urban efflorescence (c. eleventh–thirteenth centuries CE).** The northern coast produces Sican (Lambayeque), whose capital Batán Grande inherits both Moche and Wari urban traditions. South of Chan Chan, the "White-Black-Red State" (Wilson's hypothesis) controls the Chicama-Casma coast. On the central coast, the oracle-city of Pacha Kamaq retains and expands its pan-Andean religious prestige. The Chancay and Chincha valleys develop their own urban centres; Chincha merchants operate llama caravans to Titicaca and balsa-raft trade as far as Ecuador.

**Phase 3 — Chimor's peak expansion (c. thirteenth–fifteenth centuries CE).** [[chimor]] organises the "whole northern and central coast of Peru," absorbing the Lambayeque polity and extending from Tumbes north to Chillon south. [[chan-chan]] grows as its capital with nine to eleven "citadels" (large walled palaces attributed to successive rulers), a caste-stratified urban population, and the greatest hydraulic engineering of prehispanic Peru (the La Cumbre canal; the 100,000-hectare Lambayeque multi-river system). "The Chimu state achieved a spectacular expansion in comparison to other parts of the Andes during the Late Intermediate, which was for the most part an era of relatively localized developments."

**Phase 4 — Aymara señoríos consolidate (c. eleventh–fifteenth centuries CE).** Around [[lake-titicaca]], predominantly pastoral Aymara-speaking polities (Qulla, Lupaqa, and smaller groups) develop hierarchical settlement systems with elite chullpa (tower) tombs and dual (moiety) political organisation. The Lupaqa are best documented: governed by paired mallku rulers (Cari and Cusi), they maintained pastoral "archipelagos" of productive outliers at lower altitudes, a model Lumbreras traces directly to Tiwanaku's earlier complementarity system. "The Late Intermediate this area saw the rise of predominantly pastoral peoples … They created the 'lacustrine kingdoms' that both Inka and Spanish conquerors considered notably rich."

**Phase 5 — Central-sierra chiefdoms (c. 900–1460 CE).** In the "polycyclical" agricultural highlands (Mantaro, Tarma, Junín), no states comparable to coastal ones formed. The Wanka (Wanka I, c. 900–1350; Wanka II, c. 1350–1460) oscillate between open valley settlements and, in the late phase, large fortified hilltop towns (Tunanmarka, 23 ha.; Hatunmarka, 95 ha.) sheltering 8,000–16,000 people. "The overall process is reminiscent of Huarpa before the formation of the Wari state, albeit on a greater scale." No evidence of exchange or political influence beyond individual valleys until Inka conquest.

**Phase 6 — Cusco's Killke-culture pre-Inka state (c. eleventh–early fifteenth centuries CE).** The Cusco valley, freed from Wari tribute obligations after Wari's collapse, develops an agricultural intensification strategy combining irrigated maize in the Urubamba Valley with dry-land potato farming and highland livestock. The Killke culture (identified by distinctive ceramics) represents a pre-Inka local state. "Archaeologically speaking, a state already existed earlier at the local level in Cusco, where the Killke culture had developed." Killke settlements are relatively small and unfortified in the valley, contrasting with fortified hilltop sites in neighbouring Lucre. Circa 1438 (or a decade earlier), the Inka defeat the Chanka and launch the Late Horizon, but the [[andean-middle-horizon]] institutions (roads, khipu, administrative urbanism, labour organisation) form the template the [[inca-empire]] scales up.

## Geographic Spread

The process unfolds differently by zone:

- **North Peruvian coast:** [[chimor]] and [[chan-chan]] — the most powerful LIP polity, operating a strong hydraulic state from Trujillo.
- **Lambayeque coast:** Sican/Lambayeque kingdom, then absorbed by Chimor.
- **Central coast (Lima to Ica):** Pacha Kamaq oracle-city (Ichma señorío), Chancay, Chincha — regional chiefdoms with long-distance maritime and caravan trade.
- **Northern and central sierra:** Non-urban chiefdoms (Cajamarca, Huamachuco, Wanka, Tarama, Chinchaycocha) — "ethnic collectivities and not unified political structures."
- **[[lake-titicaca]] basin:** [[aymara-kingdoms]] — Qulla and Lupaqa as dominant polities; smaller pastoral groups around the lake perimeter.
- **Southern sierra (Ayacucho, Apurimac, Cusco):** Post-Wari village regression followed by Killke-culture Cusco emergence.

## Interaction with Other Processes

The Post-Wari Reorganization is the direct product of the [[andean-middle-horizon]]'s collapse, and directly produces the [[inca-empire]]. It stands as the necessary intermediate through which Middle Horizon institutional innovations (state agriculture, road networks, khipu accounting, mita labour) were preserved at regional scale long enough to be inherited and amplified by the Inka after 1438.

The process interacts with [[andean-state-formation]] as its diversifying phase: where the Middle Horizon achieved interregional integration through two dominant polities, the LIP produced the competitive ecology of kingdoms and chiefdoms from which Inka imperialism emerged. The [[inca-empire]] conquered each LIP polity in turn — [[chimor]] (c. 1470), the [[aymara-kingdoms]] (early Inka expansion), the Wanka (mid-fifteenth century CE) — absorbing their artisans, labour systems, and administrative models.

The [[vertical-archipelago]] model of production — altiplano polities maintaining low-altitude productive colonies — was a direct inheritance from Tiwanaku, practised by [[aymara-kingdoms]] throughout the LIP, and systematised empire-wide by the Inka.

## End Conditions

The Late Intermediate ends at different dates by region: for Chimor, c. 1470 with Inka conquest; for the [[aymara-kingdoms]], in the generation before 1532 as Inka domination was imposed; for the central-sierra chiefdoms (Wanka), c. 1460–1470. Inka expansion absorbed, rather than eliminated, most LIP institutions. Colonial Spanish documents of the sixteenth century preserve the clearest record of LIP social structure (señores naturales, mallkus, yana, mit'a), precisely because many institutions persisted through the Inka into the early colonial period.

The LIP also ends, in an intellectual sense, with the archaeology of Cusco immediately before Tawantinsuyu — the Killke culture and the legendary Chanka War — which "expresses the idea of Cusco as surrounded by confrontational 'barbarians' on whose defeat the future of the valley turned." That moment connects the reorganisation to the Late Horizon [[inca-empire]].

## Historiography

The Late Intermediate is the least uniformly documented of the three Andean "horizons" because its regional diversity has resisted unifying narrative. Archaeological evidence (ceramics, settlement patterns, hydraulics) is primary; documentary sources from sixteenth-century Spanish observers preserve oral traditions about Chimor's dynastic sequence (Tacaynamo → Guacricaur → Nancenpinco → Minchancaman) and the Lambayeque Naymlap legend, though these are "ideologically tinged." Moseley and Day (*Chan Chan: Andean Desert City*, 1982) and Rostworowski et al. (*The Northern Dynasties*, 1990) are foundational for Chimor. Hyslop's settlement studies are key for the Lupaqa. Lumbreras's own essay ("Los reinos post-Tiwanaku en el área altiplánica," 1974) provides a synthesis for the Titicaca kingdoms. The Wanka sequence (Wanka I–II) is elaborated by Earle, D'Altroy, and Hastorf.

Collection coverage: moderate for Chimor and [[chan-chan]] (Moseley project); weaker for the Aymara señoríos, central-sierra chiefdoms, and Cusco's pre-Inka Killke phase. The transition to the Inka remains "archaeologically … like the blink of an eye" with reasons for Cusco's rapid emergence still debated.
