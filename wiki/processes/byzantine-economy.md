# The Byzantine Economy

## Definition and Scope

The **Byzantine economy** is the economic system of the [[byzantine-empire|Eastern Roman
(Byzantine) Empire]] from the foundation of [[constantinople|Constantinople]] (330 CE) to
its fall in 1453. Following Angeliki Laiou and Cécile Morrisson
([[laiou-morrisson-byzantine-economy-2007|*The Byzantine Economy*]], 2007), it is best
understood as a **mixed economy** in which the state was, for much of the period, the
single most important economic actor — largest landlord, largest consumer, monopoly issuer
of a stable [[byzantine-monetary-system|coinage]], and guarantor (through continuously
applied Roman law) of property and contract — but never the *sole* actor. Market forces,
private landlords, artisans, money-changers and merchants always operated within, and
increasingly alongside, the state's framework.

Agriculture supplied roughly **two-thirds of GNP** throughout (as in all pre-industrial
economies); Laiou and Morrisson estimate that by the twelfth century perhaps **25% of GNP
(≈40% of monetized GNP)** came from trade and manufacture. The economy passed through
distinct phases whose variation is great enough, the authors say, to "invite the conclusion
that there are several systems succeeding each other," unified by coherence, functionality
and flexibility until its disaggregation in the mid-fourteenth century.

What it is **not**: it was not a rigidly command "port-of-trade" economy sealed off from
markets (the older orthodoxy the authors reject), nor a free market — the freedom of trade
was a *medieval* concept, expressed through privileges granted to favoured actors rather
than a general liberty.

## Causal Drivers

- **The state and its fiscal system.** A centralized state (unusual for the medieval world;
  see [[actors/byzantine-empire|Byzantine Empire]]) taxed a largely agrarian base, collected
  much of the added value of production, and paid it out as salaries to officials and
  soldiers, on defence and on public works — setting monetary circulation in motion. The
  **payment of tax in coin** (imposed on the base land tax by Constantine V in 769) drove an
  unusually high **monetization of the agrarian economy** and the early appearance of
  salaried labour.
- **Demography.** Growth tracks population: expansion from the late eighth century, marked
  acceleration in the eleventh–twelfth, a Malthusian ceiling by the late thirteenth, and
  catastrophic collapse after the Black Death (1347). Population fell from perhaps **~19–21
  million** at the sixth-century and Basil II-era peaks to a fraction of that in the
  fifteenth century.
- **Geography and the sea.** A Mediterranean core (bounded by the 3 °C January isotherm
  limiting olive cultivation) plus the mountainous Balkans and the high Anatolian plateau.
  Low-cost maritime transport and prevailing counter-clockwise winds/currents favoured the
  northern (Byzantine, later Italian) sea route.
- **Demand.** For centuries concentrated in the state and a state-derived official/military/
  ecclesiastical elite; city populations later exercised effective demand for semi-luxury
  goods (ceramics, textiles, wine). Elite demand drove secondary production and international
  exchange.
- **Institutions ("intangible resources").** Roman law protecting property/contract,
  monetary continuity, a partnership form (*chreokoinonia*) resembling the later Italian
  *commenda* but attested ~300 years earlier, comparatively high practical literacy (an
  "educated guess" of ~30% among men), and technical know-how. Laiou and Morrisson argue
  these gave Byzantium "a clear edge over the medieval West" until the late twelfth century.

## Major Phases

**1. The late-antique economy and the shift to medieval structures (6th–early 8th c.).**
The first half of the sixth century was the last flourishing of late-antique prosperity —
Constantinople the world's largest city (≥400,000; C. Zuckerman argues ~700,000), monumental
building, active long-distance trade attested by amphora and fine-ware distributions and by
shipwreck counts. From c.550 came crisis: the [[justinianic-plague-541|Plague of Justinian]]
(541/2) and its recurrences (possibly −30% population), climatic deterioration from the 530s,
two-front wars, and the seventh-century Persian and [[caliphate|Arab conquests]] that
stripped away the richest southern provinces. The result was **ruralisation**, urban decay
(*polis* to *kastron*), a tenfold-plus fall in Mediterranean shipwrecks by the eighth
century, and sharp monetary contraction. Laiou and Morrisson read this not as simple decline
but as a genuine **shift to medieval structures**. See [[late-antiquity|Late Antiquity]].

**2. Restructuring, recovery and controlled expansion (early 8th–10th c.).** From the late
eighth century, with security restored under the Isaurian and Macedonian emperors, came
demographic and agricultural recovery (pollen/¹⁴C evidence of clearance in Macedonia and
Thessaly), re-urbanisation, and **re-monetization** (coin-find indices at Athens, Corinth,
Sparta multiply). The state's role was at its heaviest: the theme system, a well-administered
land tax (1/24 of fiscal value, ~23% of cereal output), the **[[constantinople|Book of the
Eparch]]** regulating Constantinople's guilds, export bans on *kekolymena* (strategic goods),
and tenth-century **land legislation** (Romanos I's Novel of 934) trying to check the
"powerful" (*dynatoi*) from absorbing peasant land. See [[early-middle-ages|Early Middle
Ages]].

**3. The age of accelerated growth (11th–12th c.).** Sustained "Smithian" intensive growth:
population up, land cleared, mills proliferating, large estates worked by rent-paying tenant
farmers (**paroikoi**) rising alongside surviving free peasants, and provincial cities
reviving as production centres (glazed pottery at Corinth, Sardis; silk at Thebes and
Corinth). The **[[byzantine-monetary-system|Komnenian monetary reform of 1092]]** created
the gold *hyperpyron*. Crucially, **the state receded**: Alexios I's "feudal authoritarianism"
diffused power to an aristocratic elite through *pronoia* grants, tax exemptions
(*exkousseiai*) and tax-farming, while commercial privileges to **Venice (1082/1126)**, Pisa
(1111) and Genoa (1155) drew Italian merchants into a Byzantine trade that had been growing
*before* their arrival. See [[high-middle-ages|High Middle Ages]].

**4. Small-state economics (13th–15th c.).** The [[fourth-crusade-1204|Fourth Crusade's
capture of Constantinople (1204)]] shattered the unified economic space. Fragmentation into
Latin and Greek successor states multiplied courts, currencies, and transaction costs. Some
sectors still grew in the thirteenth century, but production and, decisively, **commerce and
manufacture passed to the Italians**: Byzantine silk and pottery industries could not compete
with Venice, Genoa and Lucca; the *hyperpyron* was debased away (see below) and gold coinage
abandoned after 1353. The late state, harshly fiscal yet giving away its revenue base,
"abandoned the economy"; local defence devolved to towers built by monasteries and
strongmen. Byzantine merchants (the Notaras and de Draperiis families) survived only as
junior "comprador" partners of the Italians. See [[late-middle-ages|Late Middle Ages]] and
[[latin-romania-economy|Latin Romania economy]].

## Geographic Spread

The productive core shifted over time: from a Mediterranean-wide late-antique system, to an
Anatolian-Balkan-Aegean rump after the Arab conquests, to a Balkan-Aegean heartland after
Manzikert (1071) cost the Anatolian plateau, and finally to Constantinople, Thrace,
Macedonia, the Aegean islands and the Despotate of the Morea. Constantinople was throughout
a **megalopolis** without Western parallel; [[thessalonike|Thessalonike]] was the second
city and a major fair (the panegyris of St Demetrios) and redistribution hub. Trade nodes
migrated — Trebizond and Artze (Persian/Indian-Ocean spice route) giving way to Attaleia,
Rhodes, Chios and Crete, and ultimately to Genoese **Pera/Galata** across the Golden Horn.

## Interaction

The Byzantine economy is best understood comparatively (see
[[premodern-mixed-economy|pre-modern mixed economy]] for the interpretive frame). It
**contrasts with** the [[carolingian-economy|Carolingian]] and Western medieval economies:
Byzantium never developed the [[bipartite-manor|bipartite manor]] or the *seigneurie banale*
(the strong state pre-empted lordly banal rights), kept small-scale peasant exploitation
dominant, and monetized its countryside far earlier — but the West gained decisive advantages
from the twelfth century (colonisation of resource-rich north-eastern lands, new silver
mines, the linkage of northern Europe and the Mediterranean, and the growth of manufacturing
and banking). It **contributed to** and was reshaped by the
[[medieval-europe-asia-trade|Italian-dominated Mediterranean trade system]] and the
[[silk-roads|silk routes]]. Its later trajectory is entangled with
[[latin-romania-economy|Latin Romania]].

## End Conditions

Laiou and Morrisson insist the "failure of the last hundred years cannot obscure the
achievements of the centuries that preceded it." The end was **political in the first
instance**: the extractive Komnenian regime, 1204, fragmentation, the loss of Anatolia to the
Turks, and the mid-fourteenth-century pan-European crisis left the economy peripheral to an
internationalised commercial world it could not compete in. Value-added manufacture was
"firmly lodged in Western Europe," leaving Byzantine growth "captive to the needs of others."
Where Byzantine political integration failed, Italian city-states substituted economically —
until the Ottomans re-established political integration by conquest in 1453.
