# The Nineteenth-Century West African Jihads

A wave of **Islamic revolutions (jihads)** swept the West African savanna in the nineteenth century,
overthrowing old dynasties and founding a series of large **Muslim theocratic states** — above all the
**[[sokoto-caliphate|Sokoto Caliphate]]**, the largest. Documented for the wiki through the **UNESCO
[[gha-vol6-19th-century|General History of Africa, Vol. VI]]** (chs. 21–23, Batran, Last, Ly-Tall), which
treats them as the defining **African-driven** transformation of the pre-colonial nineteenth century.

## The movement

The jihads grew from a long tradition of **Islamic scholarship and reformism** in the Sahel — energized by
the **Qadiriyya** and, later, the militant **Tijaniyya** Sufi brotherhoods — and from the grievances of
the largely Muslim, often pastoralist **Fulani (Fulbe)** clerical class against rulers they condemned as
oppressive, venal, and only nominally Muslim (practising a "mixed" Islam tolerant of pagan custom). A
charismatic scholar would declare a **jihad** against such a ruler, rally Muslim (and Fulani) support, and
build a new state governed by the **sharia** and led by a learned imam-amir.

## The major jihad states

- **The Sokoto Caliphate (1804).** **Usman dan Fodio**, a Fulani scholar, launched the first and greatest
  jihad against the **[[hausa-city-states|Hausa]]** kings of Gobir, conquering the Hausa states and founding
  the **[[sokoto-caliphate|Sokoto Caliphate]]** — the largest state in nineteenth-century Africa.
- **Massina (c. 1818).** **Seku Amadu** founded a strict Islamic state on the inner Niger delta (the Massina
  or Hamdullahi caliphate).
- **The Tukulor empire (c. 1850s–60s).** **al-Hajj Umar Tal**, a Tijaniyya leader, waged a jihad across the
  upper Senegal and Niger, conquering Massina and the Bambara states to build a vast (if short-lived)
  **Tukulor empire** — running up against the advancing French on the Senegal.

## Consequences

The jihads **Islamized and reorganized the West African savanna**, spread Arabic literacy and sharia
governance, and produced durable states. They also **vastly expanded slavery** within Africa (the jihad
states ran on enslaved labour and slave-raiding of "pagan" neighbours), and their conquests and rivalries
reshaped the region's politics on the very eve of the European **[[scramble-for-africa|Scramble]]** — into
which these states (Sokoto above all) would be absorbed.
