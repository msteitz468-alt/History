# Arab Nationalism

The political idea that **the Arabs are a single nation** — defined by a common language,
culture, and history — and should govern themselves, whether as a unified Arab state or as
independent Arabic-speaking countries. The dominant secular ideology of the twentieth-century
Arab world, from the [[middle-east-modern|Middle East]] to North Africa. From Eugene Rogan's
[[rogan-the-arabs|*The Arabs: A History*]].

## Origins: the nahda

Arab nationalism grew from the ***nahda*** — the nineteenth-century **Arabic cultural
renaissance** centered on Beirut and Cairo. Encouraged by the (often missionary-owned) printing
press, Muslim and Christian intellectuals revived classical Arabic literature, compiled
dictionaries and encyclopedias, and reconnected the Arabs of the Ottoman Empire "to the glories
of their pre-Ottoman past." The nahda "embraced all Arabic-speaking peoples without distinction
by sect or region and planted the seed of an idea … that the Arabs were a nation, defined by a
common language, culture, and history." Newspapers (twenty-five in Beirut by the late 1870s)
diffused the idea.

## From Ottomanism to nationalism

Nationalism itself was a **European import** (Enlightenment origin), spreading through the
Ottoman world in the nineteenth century. At first many Arabs **frowned on nationalism** as the
ideology of Balkan Christian secessionists (Egyptian and North African troops fought the
sultan's wars against them). The Ottoman [[tanzimat-reforms|Tanzimat]] promoted **Ottomanism**
— a shared citizenship transcending ethnic and religious divides — but it could not hold once
European colonialism removed Arab lands from the Ottoman world. Rogan stresses that **colonial
rule supplied the ingredients** for nationalism where mere resistance had not: defined
territorial frontiers, and a common foreign enemy to unify against (neither Abd al-Qadir's
Algerian war nor Urabi's Egyptian revolt was yet "nationalist" for lack of an ideology).

## Islam, reform, and the early nationalists

The relationship of Islam and modernity shaped early nationalism. **Jamal al-Din al-Afghani**
(1839–97) and **Muhammad Abduh** (1849–1905) made Islamic reform "an integral part of national
identity," debating social reform alongside the national struggle. In Egypt, the moderate
**Ahmad Lutfi al-Sayyid** and the radical, charismatic **Mustafa Kamil** (founder of *al-Liwa'*,
1900) shaped a distinct **Egyptian nationalism** against the British occupation by the early
1900s.

## The central tension: pan-Arab vs. nation-state

Rogan identifies the defining problem: the **tension between nation-state nationalism** (Egyptian,
Iraqi, Syrian) and **pan-Arab nationalism**. National movements arose within individual colonial
states against foreign rule, but "no meaningful Arab-wide nationalist movement was possible so
long as the Arab world was divided between Britain and France." By the time independence came
(1940s–50s), the [[colonial-partition-of-the-arab-world|colonial borders]] had hardened, leaving
the Arabs "a community of nations rather than a national community." Yet many believed the
smaller, colonially-drawn nationalisms were **illegitimate**, and that only **pan-Arab unity**
could restore the Arabs to greatness — the aspiration that [[gamal-abdel-nasser|Nasser]] and the
Baʿth would carry.

## Interaction and decline

Pan-Arabism peaked in the 1950s–60s (Nasserism; the short-lived United Arab Republic; the Arab
League). But repeated **defeats by Israel** — above all the [[arab-israeli-conflict|1967 war]] —
and Nasser's death (1970) discredited it (see [[arab-israeli-conflict]]). Secular Arab
nationalism then gave way, from the 1970s–80s, to the rise of **[[political-islam|Islamism]]**
as the dominant oppositional ideology — the shift Rogan and Samir Kassir's "Arab malaise" both
trace.
