## Definition and Scope

Southern Ocean seafaring and colonization covers the Indigenous and European maritime activity that brought humans to the cool, wind-dominated seas south of approximately 42° S — "the approximate limit of pre-European agriculture in New Zealand and Chile" — and the societies that adapted to those conditions. The chapter in [[jones-matsuda-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v1-2022]] (Anderson, ch. 20) defines the Southern Ocean's Pacific sector as the continuous sea between the Roaring Forties and the Screaming Sixties, its character shaped by "wet, extratropical cyclonic activity" and average wind speeds of 27 km/hr at 40°S and over 50 km/hr at 50°S. This process page focuses on South Polynesia (mainland New Zealand and its outlying island groups 500–1,300 km distant), the Subantarctic islands, Tasmania, and southern Chile, where Indigenous settlement intersected with the expansion of European commercial sealing and whaling from the 1790s onward.

The process is distinct from [[austronesian-expansion]] (the broader Pacific-wide colonization) and from European Pacific exploration. It is the *southern terminus* of Polynesian expansion and a zone where cold-climate limits reshaped the social and subsistence patterns inherited from tropical East Polynesia.

## Causal Drivers

**Wind-climate window.** Anderson's key argument is that migration to South Polynesia required a specific climate regime. Migration canoes of the thirteenth century used the **double spritsail** — "a high, quadrilateral sail with no mast but a spar fixed each side," attested in Indonesian rock art dated 1000–0 BCE and "virtually identical to the eighteenth-century Māori sailing rig." This rig was "heavily constrained in courses and passage speeds, particularly sailing from East to South Polynesia, which was directly upwind into prevailing southwesterlies." The key enabling factor: "research on the decadal movement of wind patterns has shown that westerlies were largely replaced by easterly winds during the interval 1100–1300 CE. With prevailing easterlies, migratory canoes could sail downwind to South Polynesia under the double spritsail." The window closed: "Westerly wind dominance resumed over South Polynesia in the early fourteenth century, probably preventing any further migration from East Polynesia until after the arrival of European shipping."

**Polynesian social structure.** As with the broader Austronesian expansion, Kirch's argument applies: ranking and inheritance governed by birth order gave junior siblings strong incentives to find new lands "that could be claimed, named, divided, planted, managed and inherited." South Polynesia's vast resources and uninhabited status made it attractive despite climatic challenges.

**Marine resource abundance.** The Southern Ocean's upwelling sustains "high primary productivity that sustained numerous consumers at higher trophic levels, from krill to whales." For South Polynesian settlers, marine and terrestrial faunal resources were spectacular, compensating for the failure of most tropical cultigens.

## Major Phases

### Phase 1: Settlement of South Polynesia (~1250–1300 CE)
Māori traditions describe "twelve to twenty colonizing canoes arriving in New Zealand about 750 years ago and a founding population of 300–500 people can be inferred both from the traditions and from genomic history." The date of 1250–1280 CE (Bedford, ch. 19) is based on radiocarbon chronology. Exploration was "so rapid that radiocarbon dates on the earliest sites from northern New Zealand to the Auckland Islands are virtually indistinguishable." During this same dispersal, New Zealand colonists reached the Kermadec Islands, Norfolk Island, and even the east coast of Australia, "documented by recovery of New Zealand obsidian in early archaeological sites on the Chatham, Kermadec, and Norfolk islands."

The Chatham Islands were probably colonized during this phase (Moriori language closest to Māori; artefact types matching pre-1350 CE New Zealand forms; sedimentary core evidence of cultural firing from the 13th century). The brief Auckland Islands colony on Enderby Island — a dense midden of sea lion, fur seal, albatross, penguin, and shellfish — also dates to the 13th century: "the colony was short-lived. The islands were uninhabited when discovered by Bristow in 1806."

### Phase 2: Early Faunal Exploitation and Moa Extinction (~1280–1450 CE)
First settlers exploited the "spectacular" faunal resources: "nine species of the large flightless bird the moa, which ranged in size from 28 to 250 kg, along with a whole range of other bird species. Fur seal could be found throughout both islands." The site of Wairau Bar alone holds "an estimated 33 tons of bone at the site, which represents 4,000 moa along with 2,400 moa eggs." By about 1450 CE, in Murihiku (southern New Zealand), "most of the eastern forest was gone, and all of the large flightless birds were extinct, notably moa (Dinornithiformes) represented in at least 255 sites south of Banks Peninsula. The fur seal population, perhaps originally of 3 million, had been reduced by two-thirds."

### Phase 3: Climate Deterioration and Population Restructuring (post-1400 CE)
The "Polynesian Warm Period" of early settlement gave way to cooling: "With summer temperature decline of 1.5 degrees Celsius in the South Island after 1400, the horticultural boundary retreated 150–200 kilometres northward." Only kumara (sweet potato) could support intensive production, and only in the North Island's northern districts. In Murihiku, population "peaked at no more than several thousand around 1400 and remained low or declined thereafter." A series of obligatory southward migrations followed, conducted in the waka taua — a "large, single-hulled vessel, up to 35 metres long, which was generally paddled" — capable of carrying 100 warriors.

### Phase 4: European Contact and Commercial Exploitation (1792–1850 CE)
European sealing began in 1792 at Dusky Sound, New Zealand, when the Britannia set down the first sealing gang and constructed the first ship built in the southwest Pacific. An "intense sealing boom in 1805–7 in the Antipodes Islands" produced 383,000 dried skins, "the largest haul from any Subantarctic island." By 1835, "a minimum of 800,000 fur seal skins had been taken from the New Zealand region." Shore whaling operated along Foveaux Strait and Otago coasts from 1829 to 1847; 48,000 right whales were killed between 1830 and 1849, collapsing the population.

## Geographic Spread

**Aotearoa / New Zealand:** At 236,000 km², "ten times the size of Hawai'i, and second only to New Guinea across the entire Pacific." Settled north to south ~1250–1280 CE. North Island agriculture enabled population densities of ~1 person/km² in upper districts; South Island densities as low as 0.02 people/km² in the far south.

**Chatham Islands:** 860 km east of New Zealand. No cultigens reached the Chathams; Moriori developed distinctive technology including the *waka korari* (bundle-frame boat) for passages to offshore seal and bird colonies. Population ~3,000 at European arrival. In 1835, 900 Ngati Mutunga and Ngati Tama Māori invaded; by 1862 only 101 Moriori survived.

**Auckland Islands / Subantarctic:** Mean annual temperature 8°C, 1,000 hours of sunshine per year. Brief 13th-century Polynesian colony; uninhabited at European discovery (1806).

**Tasmania:** Southern Ocean shores occupied from ~45,000 years ago; Bass Strait formed ~8500 BCE isolating the Aboriginal population. Nine tribes historically divided east–west by subsistence strategy (shellfish/marsupials vs. year-round coastal sealing). European sealing from 1798; by 1835 "the total Aboriginal population was reduced to about 400."

**Southern Chile:** The Chono and related peoples adapted sewn-plank dalca canoes to the fiord country; initial human occupation in Chiloé by 5000 BCE. The dalca spread through contact: a "product of this 'araucanization'" of Mapuche expansion south. Population collapse followed European sealers, whalers, and diseases in the 18th–19th centuries.

## Seafaring Technology Constraints

Anderson provides the fullest analysis in the source. Key points:

1. The **double spritsail** (not the later oceanic spritsail) was the rig of 13th-century migration canoes. The oceanic spritsail was a later development: "lateen traits, such as stayed masts and balance boards, were strongest in the Society Islands, historically connected to west Polynesia," and the oceanic spritsail spread to Hawai'i and the Marquesas but "not New Zealand, where the oceanic spritsail was not recorded until the 1820s."

2. Downwind passage to South Polynesia required the 1100–1300 CE easterly window. Anderson contests the "modern Polynesian voyaging movement" claim that canoes were broadly capable of windward passages, noting: "Cook's remark that Tahitian voyaging canoes 'may with ease sail 40 Leagues a day or more' is often cited as validating support, but he was actually referring to 5 knots sailing speed before the wind; it was only 1.8 knots to windward."

3. The **waka taua** war canoe (up to 35 m, paddled, carrying 100+ warriors) enabled post-1400 southward migrations.

4. The **waka korari** (Moriori bundle-frame boat) was an adaptation to the Chatham Islands' scarcity of suitable timber and exposed coasts.

## Southern Societies: Shared Adaptations

Anderson identifies a "Subantarctic model" of convergent adaptation: dispersed low-density populations relying on coastal resources, residential mobility, conservative material culture, limited food storage, and slight political stratification. He qualifies this: "empirical variation amongst the cases" is significant. Murihiku had "hundreds of items and frequent typological change" in material culture; semi-permanent villages; trade networks reaching the North Island. "Maritime capability was probably a major factor in cultural differences." Moriori discarded planked boats, tattooing, and warfare — "responses" to isolation. Chilean channel peoples had "capable longshore mobility and interactions."

## End Conditions

European commercial exploitation from the 1790s brought the process to a crisis. Indigenous societies were "overwhelmed, losing authority over their lands, resources, and people." The mode varied: "Christian proselytization was prominent in southern Chile, and strongly imposed state control in Tasmania, while in South Polynesia the relationship was largely commercial, preceding the advent of church and state in the 1840s." Mixed-race communities emerged at Bass Strait, Chiloé, and Murihiku. The process of colonial transformation rather than Indigenous colonization defines the post-1840 history of the Southern Ocean zone.

## Historiography

Anderson's chapter in [[jones-matsuda-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v1-2022]] represents the revisionist position on Polynesian sailing capabilities: the traditional "Polynesian voyaging movement" — which Anderson treats as having "adopted the late traditionalist belief that Polynesian maritime technology must have been more capable in the migration era than in the eighteenth century" — is challenged by the evidence for wind-dependent double-spritsail canoes and the climate-window hypothesis. The climate-window argument (Goodwin, Browning, and Anderson 2014) grounds the 1250–1280 CE settlement date directly in palaeoclimatic data rather than tradition. The absence of return-voyage evidence (no New Zealand obsidian in East Polynesian early sites) supports limited post-migration connectivity.

The Moriori history is a case of historiographical rehabilitation: the "anthropological myth that Moriori were a debased, non-Polynesian people" promulgated in the late 19th century has been overturned.
