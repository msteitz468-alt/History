## Definition and Scope

The Pacific fisheries and EEZ process describes the transformation of the Pacific Ocean's tuna and pelagic fish resources from an effectively open-access commons dominated by distant-water fishing fleets (Japanese, American, Soviet/Korean/Taiwanese) into a legally structured regime of exclusive economic zones in which Island states hold sovereign rights — though not, in practice, effective control — over fish within 200 nautical miles of their shores. The process begins with the UNCLOS negotiations of the 1970s and the proliferation of purse-seine technology, reaches a political climax in the mid-1980s tuna boat confrontations, and continues as a contested arena of multilateral bargaining.

Tuna fisheries are economically crucial: sea resources "provide up to 25 per cent of the gross receipts of some nations." The EEZ framework also has cultural and subsistence dimensions: "fishing for skipjack tuna has long been one of the most exhilarating sports, communal activities, and chiefly pastimes" throughout the Pacific, and reef fish (not pelagic tuna) provide the foundation for local consumption — in Tokelau "about 85 per cent of animal food consumed is local, predominantly fish and sea products (89 per cent)."

## Causal Drivers

**UNCLOS III (1982)**: The 1982 United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea allowed nations to declare 200-mile exclusive economic zones "in which each maintains sovereign rights over resources but allows free air and sea transit." For archipelagic states with tiny land areas and vast ocean territories, this was a structural revolution. The Marshall Islands (land area 176 km²) gained an EEZ of 2,131,000 km²; French Polynesia (3,265 km²) gained 5,030,000 km². The US contested the extension to highly pelagic species like tuna: "The matter of highly pelagic species, such as tuna, was contested finally only by the United States."

**Purse-seine technology expansion**: The purse-seiner enabled large-scale deep-water tuna harvesting. Beginning with eight in the Pacific in 1976, by 1982 Japan had thirty-three; "by 1984 more than 120 purse-seiners were operating in Pacific waters." The US catch in the western Pacific rose from 6 per cent of total in 1980 to 66 per cent in 1984. "Of the world's canned tuna, 60 per cent is taken in the region."

**Cold War competition**: Soviet entry into Pacific fishing licensing was geopolitically motivated. After Papua New Guinea seized the US vessel Danica (1982) and the US retaliated against the Solomons for seizing the *Jeanette Diana* (1984), "the Soviet Union entered the tuna boat wars, offering a lucrative fee to Kiribati in return for a five-year fishing licence." This agreement was followed by one with Vanuatu. The threat of Soviet influence forced the US to sign a multilateral agreement in 1987. Conversely, "the dissolution of the Soviet Union and the end of the Cold War weakened the Islands' leverage." After 1991, Pacific nations struggled to obtain even 5 per cent of the market value of fish caught in their waters, down from the 9 per cent secured in the 1987 agreement.

## Major Phases

**1. Pre-EEZ open access (to 1976)**: Distant-water fleets (Japan, US, USSR) harvested Pacific tuna under minimal constraints. In 1977, "48 per cent of all Japanese tuna was obtained within 200 miles of other nations." Island populations had no legal basis to demand licensing fees.

**2. UNCLOS negotiations and Nauru Group formation (1976–1982)**: Island states participated in UNCLOS as newly independent sovereigns. In 1982, eight contiguous states — FSM, Kiribati, Marshall Islands, Nauru, Palau, Papua New Guinea, Solomon Islands — formed the Nauru Group: "These contiguous countries cover about 14 million square kilometres, including the waters of the FSM and Papua New Guinea in which up to three-quarters of all the reported tuna are caught by purse-seiners." Through a Regional Register of Fishing Vessels and common licensing terms, the group prevented distant-water nations from playing members off against each other.

**3. Confrontations and Cold War leverage (1982–1987)**: Papua New Guinea and Solomon Islands seized US tuna vessels; US retaliated with trade sanctions. The Soviet licensing agreements with Kiribati and Vanuatu (1985–86) provided Island states with unprecedented leverage. In early 1987 the US signed a multilateral agreement with sixteen Forum Fisheries Agency members for $60 million over five years — approximately 9 per cent of the value of the catch.

**4. Post-Cold War weakening and the Wellington Convention (1989–present)**: Island leverage declined after the Soviet collapse. Licensing revenues fell back toward 5 per cent of catch value. Under-reporting by distant-water fleets remained severe: the South Pacific Commission estimated that three major fishing nations reported less than 20 per cent of their catch. In 1989, the Wellington Convention prohibited drift-net fishing within national EEZs and a defined area of the high seas — a Pacific-led initiative that preceded global discussions. A 1994 UN conference addressed regulation on the high seas to protect endangered stocks.

## Joint Ventures: The Solomon Taiyo Model

The Solomon Islands developed the region's most integrated fisheries joint venture. Since 1971, it partnered with Japanese Taiyo Fishery Company; Solomon Taiyo Ltd opened a cannery at Tulagi in 1973 and operated pole-and-line vessels. By 1987 the industry "provided about 7 per cent of all formal employment, and has consistently contributed between 30 and 40 per cent of the nation's total exports and 25 per cent of its cash national product." The venture secured approximately 9 per cent of Solomon Taiyo's gross earnings — higher than licensing fees elsewhere. Its limiting factor was human capital: "human resources rather than fish, boats, or shore bases are likely to be the limiting factor."

The contrast with licensing-only arrangements was instructive: joint ventures generated employment, local training, processing capacity, and more accurate catch data; pure licensing generated fees but no productive capacity and chronic under-reporting.

## Geographic Spread

Fisheries dynamics vary by habitat and EEZ size:
- **Central and western Pacific (FSM, Kiribati, Marshalls, PNG, Solomons)**: Core tuna grounds; Nauru Group states; highest stakes.
- **Polynesia (Tokelau, Tonga, Cook Islands, French Polynesia)**: Subsistence reef fishing dominant locally; tuna licensing secondary.
- **Micronesian atolls (Carolines, Marshalls)**: Traditional fishing (skipjack with pearl-shell lures) coexists with EEZ licensing; customary marine tenure increasingly legible.

## End Conditions

The EEZ regime is structurally stable but produces ongoing contests over enforcement, fee levels, and under-reporting. Key unresolved issues as of the mid-1990s: obtaining more than 5 per cent of catch value; monitoring and surveillance capacity; integrating traditional marine tenure with commercial management; and protecting inshore reef fisheries from urbanisation-driven degradation. Pacific nations' collective action through the Forum Fisheries Agency remains the primary mechanism for multilateral bargaining.

## Historiography

Nero draws heavily on Doulman (ed.), *Tuna Issues and Perspectives in the Pacific Islands Region*, and the South Pacific Commission's catch-monitoring studies. A recurring problem is statistical opacity: the SPC and Marine Resource Assessment Group studies (1990–91) estimated reporting rates between 15 and 21 per cent of actual catches. The distinction between licensing fees and aid payments was politically contested — the US "insist[ed] on labelling part of the transfers as economic or technical assistance—although 85 per cent of these payments are directly linked to the fisheries' catch locations" — obscuring the real economics and making debt tracking difficult.
