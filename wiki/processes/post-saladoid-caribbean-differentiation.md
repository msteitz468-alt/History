## Definition and Scope

Post-Saladoid Caribbean cultural differentiation is the long-duration process by which the relatively uniform Saladoid cultural horizon — expressed in shared ceramic iconography, village layouts, and exchange networks — dissolved after approximately AD 500–600 into a mosaic of regional pottery styles, settlement patterns, and political forms. The process was not a single event but a series of overlapping transformations unfolding differently on different islands over roughly a millennium.

The process is most visible in Puerto Rico, where it has been most systematically studied, but analogous dynamics operated throughout the Greater Antilles and northern Lesser Antilles. In the Lesser Antilles south of the Saladoid zone, populations retained earlier ceramic traditions for longer, and the post-Saladoid differentiation was less pronounced.

This page focuses primarily on Puerto Rico (Chapters 3–4 of Keegan and Hofman 2017) and should be read alongside `[[saladoid-migration-caribbean-400bce]]` (the Saladoid foundation) and `[[taino|cacicazgo (Taíno chiefdom institution)]]` (the political institutions that emerged from this process).

## Causal Drivers

**Dissolution of the "beachhead bottleneck":** The Saladoid veneer — shared iconographic vocabulary in ceramics and exchange networks — was a functional response to the risks of small, dispersed island populations that needed long-distance connections for spouse acquisition and disaster mitigation. As populations grew and communities became self-sufficient at the island scale, the material and social benefits of pan-regional integration declined. Local pottery styles that had always existed beneath the diagnostic Saladoid modes now came to the fore.

**Population growth and interior dispersal:** By approximately AD 600–900 in Puerto Rico, population pressure drove communities from coastal river margins into the interior foothills and mountains. The number of sites increased roughly five-fold in some river valleys (e.g., Loíza Valley). This dispersal changed residence and kinship patterns — from large, relatively autonomous coastal villages to smaller dispersed hamlets and "settlement clusters" organised around shared gardens and ceremonial exchange spaces. Anthropologists have long noted that changes in social organisation typically begin with changes in residence (Murdock 1949; Ensor 2013a).

**Climate change:** Paleoecological evidence indicates substantially more arid conditions between approximately AD 800 and 1000, with a pronounced drought peaking in that interval (Beets et al. 2006; Malaizé et al. 2011, 2012). Reduced agricultural productivity in coastal zones may have incentivised interior settlement, where river terrace soils offered more reliable moisture. The drier Medieval Warm Period (c. AD 950 onset) added further environmental pressure.

**The Crab/Shell dietary transition:** The shift from land-crab-dominated to marine-mollusk-dominated faunal assemblages (the "Crab/Shell dichotomy," first identified by Rainey 1940) marks the transition from Saladoid to Ostionoid contexts throughout Puerto Rico. Keegan (2006b) argues this reflects community food preferences and identity markers rather than purely ecological causes, since both resources were available simultaneously. The Archaic Age populations of Puerto Rico had relied heavily on marine mollusks; Rodríguez Ramos (2005) has demonstrated close lithic affinities between Archaic Age and Ostiones assemblages, suggesting the Ostionoid style incorporated or reflected indigenous pre-Saladoid populations and their dietary traditions.

**External influence from Hispaniola:** Ostiones-style pottery appears in Hispaniola, Jamaica, and Turks & Caicos simultaneously with its appearance in Puerto Rico (~AD 600–700), making a Puerto Rican origin unlikely. Keegan and Hofman propose that Ostionoid developed in eastern Hispaniola and spread eastward into Puerto Rico. Later, Meillacoid influences from Hispaniola (associated with intensive manioc agriculture and conuco field mounding) reached Puerto Rico before the final Chicoid/Boca Chica intrusion after AD 1200.

## Major Phases

### Phase 1: Dissolution of the Saladoid Veneer (c. AD 500–900)

The Saladoid ceramic tradition began breaking down in Puerto Rico around AD 500, though it persisted in some locations (Culebra, eastern Puerto Rico, Virgin Islands) for several additional centuries. Multiple local pottery styles coexisted:

- **La Hueca style** (c. 800 BCE–AD 400): Predating and coexisting with Saladoid; unpainted with ZIC decoration; may represent a separate early migration or Archaic Age ceramic continuation.
- **Cuevas style** (c. AD 400–1000): A transitional style found across Puerto Rico, described as "graceful" and "drab" relative to earlier Saladoid elaboration; red paint as primary decoration; classified as late Saladoid.
- **Ostiones style** (c. AD 600–1200): Thin, hard, largely undecorated vessels with red slip and simple boat-shaped forms; found island-wide after AD 900, predominant in western Puerto Rico.
- **Monserrate style** (c. AD 600–1000): Thicker, coarser ware; equivalent to pure Ostiones for eastern Puerto Rico; underrepresented in older classification schemes.

Burial practices shifted around AD 500 from central plaza interments to burials inside houses, reflecting an "emerging localization of social identity" (Morsink 2011; Torres 2012) and a transition from clan-based cemetery use to household-level lineage expression.

Key site from this phase: **Río Tanamá 2 (AR-39)**, c. AD 350–890, located 11 km inland on the Río Tanamá floodplain north of modern Arecibo. Contains a mixture of Cuevas (declining from ~40% to ~10%) and Ostiones pottery. A large borrow pit (Feature 13, 11 × 14 m) was used to obtain soil for raising structures above flood level and later filled with composted refuse — an early form of agricultural soil management. Non-local lithics (chert from Antigua, shale from Vieques) attest to continuing exchange. Radiocarbon range: cal. AD 350–880. The site was probably abandoned after major floods in the late 9th century.

### Phase 2: Interior Dispersal and Settlement Clusters (c. AD 900–1200)

By AD 900 a radical transformation in the Puerto Rican settlement landscape was underway. The number of sites increased dramatically as population expanded into the foothills and mountainous interior. New settlements were small hamlets and nuclear-family houses (c. 8 m diameter, replacing the ~20 m extended-family maloca-style structures of Saladoid times) allied in "settlement clusters" (Curet 2005; Torres 2012). Large villages continued on coastal plains but did not replicate Saladoid organisational patterns.

New pottery styles:
- **Santa Elena style** (c. AD 900–1200): Thickest pottery in Puerto Rico (8–16 mm); simple hemispherical bowls; unpolished; restricted red painting; found primarily in north-central Puerto Rico.

Stone-lined courts and plazas intensified during this phase, becoming common in both small residential settings and larger ceremonial centres. These courts served multiple functions: exchange venues, communal ritual spaces (marriage, puberty, death ceremonies), physical "roads" providing neutral passage through villages, and materialisation of moiety or sodality organisation.

Key site: **Río Tanamá 1 (AR-38)**, c. cal. AD 980–1490, located 100 m north of Río Tanamá 2. No midden deposits; 286 features including 7 houses of 5–8 m diameter; Cuevas-style pottery completely absent; a few Boca Chica sherds. Burials beneath houses (not in a central plaza). Marine fish absent; river species replaced them. Guinea pig appears for the first time. Site illustrates the transition from large communal Saladoid settlements to dispersed nuclear-family clusters.

**Tibes Ceremonial Centre**, located on the Río Portugués near Ponce: the earliest major ceremonial centre in Puerto Rico. Began as a Saladoid village with a semicircular mound arrangement around a burial plaza. After AD 700, residential activities ceased and ceremonial construction intensified, reaching its peak AD 1000–1200. The site covers 16 hectares and comprises seven parallel-sided stone-lined courts, a large quadrangular central plaza, and a unique circular "star-shaped" plaza (Plaza de Estrella). Keegan and Hofman interpret the star plaza as a formalised space for "star formation exchange" (after Rubel and Rosman 1978) — multi-community ceremonial exchange in which a host clan at the centre conducted dyadic exchanges with surrounding partner communities. The parallel courts accommodated smaller bilateral exchanges. The site was abandoned around AD 1200, coinciding with the emergence of Caguana.

### Phase 3: Emergence of Cacicazgos and Hispaniolan Influence (c. AD 1200–1500)

Final-phase pottery styles in Puerto Rico reflect Hispaniolan (Chicoid/Meillacoid) influence overlying local developments:
- **Boca Chica style** (c. AD 1200–1500): Intrusive from the southeast coast of the Dominican Republic; elaborate incised and modeled decoration with bat-face adornos; found island-wide.
- **Capá style** (c. AD 1200–1500): Local western Puerto Rico style; technologically crude but with elaborate incised designs mimicking Boca Chica.
- **Esperanza style** (c. AD 1200–1500): Eastern Puerto Rico; cruder bat-face lugs; similarities to Meillacoid.

Keegan and Hofman propose that Meillacoid represents the introduction of **intensive manioc agriculture** (permanent conuco field mounding with montones), which reorganised agricultural production and population distribution. The Spanish observed seventeen chiefs in Puerto Rico, each associated with a major river drainage, with only one village on the coast — a defensive interior orientation consistent with endemic internal warfare.

**Caguana Ceremonial Centre** (also Capá), near Utuado: Construction after AD 1200; in use until c. AD 1500. Fifteen precincts including a large rectangular court with an attached ovoid court, eight parallel-sided courts, and 22 freestanding monoliths on east and west sides of the central plaza carved with petroglyphs attributed iconographically to "Taíno" mythology. Oliver (2009) explicitly links Puerto Rico with southeastern Hispaniola at this time. Caguana appears to have been consciously modelled on Tibes's spatial layout but at a larger scale with a different artistic programme, suggesting continuity of indigenous social design under increasing Hispaniolan cultural influence.

## Geographic Spread

Post-Saladoid differentiation was most pronounced in Puerto Rico and the northern Greater Antilles (Hispaniola, Jamaica, Cuba). In the Lesser Antilles, Saladoid ceramic traditions persisted longer, and the process of differentiation followed somewhat different trajectories. Ostionoid pottery spread westward from Hispaniola to Jamaica and the southern Bahamas. Meillacoid spread rapidly across Cuba, Jamaica, and the southern Bahama archipelago. Chicoid/Boca Chica influence penetrated eastward into the Lesser Antilles (Crock 2000; Hoogland and Hofman 1999).

The spread of settlement into the Puerto Rican interior followed river drainages. All prime settlement locations were occupied by AD 1200. Specialised sites (fishing, cotton production, salt production) were established seasonally across Puerto Rico and neighbouring islands.

## End Conditions

The process did not end through internal transformation but through Spanish colonisation after 1492. At contact, Puerto Rico exhibited a mature heterarchical political system of seventeen regional chiefs with ceremonial infrastructure at Tibes, Caguana, and Jácanas, and an agricultural economy based on intensive manioc production. Depopulation through epidemic disease, enslavement, and warfare after 1492 destroyed the social systems that this millennium-long process had created. The Taíno as a distinct cultural identity effectively ceased to exist within decades of European contact, though scholarly debate continues over the degree of biological and cultural survival.

The concept of a unified "Taíno" identity for the late pre-Columbian Caribbean is itself contested (see `[[taino|cacicazgo (Taíno chiefdom institution)]]`); Keegan and Hofman (2017) and others prefer to emphasise the "mosaic" of diverse regional identities that this differentiation process produced.

## Historiography

The post-Saladoid sequence was first systematically defined by Irving Rouse (1952, 1992), who established the major pottery style sequence (Cuevas, Ostiones, etc.) and framed Caribbean culture history as a progressive evolutionary sequence from Saladoid through Ostionoid to "Taíno." Rouse's framework treated these as stages in a single cultural tradition rather than as expressions of genuine diversity.

Curet (1992, 2003, 2005) provided quantitative demographic and spatial analysis, documenting the site-number increase and its limits as a population proxy. Torres (2012) conducted the most comprehensive settlement-landscape study of the Río Portugués area, introducing "community" as the primary analytical unit and documenting the fissioning vs. dispersal distinction.

Keegan and Hofman (2017) systematically deconstruct the Rouse framework, arguing against a linear Saladoid-to-Taíno trajectory and in favour of local accommodation and multiple external influences. They resist the application of the cacicazgo concept as a universal organising device for late pre-Columbian Puerto Rico, following Torres (2013) and Pauketat (2007).

Rodríguez Ramos (2010) provided the most rigorous lithic re-analysis, demonstrating the Archaic Age roots of Ostionoid lithic traditions and thus complicating the assumption of straightforward Saladoid cultural descent.

Primary evidence: radiocarbon-dated stratigraphic sequences, ceramic compositional analysis, faunal and starch-grain analyses, stable isotope (dietary and mobility) studies, architectural analysis of post-holes and stone-lined courts, and Spanish documentary sources (used with caution as a terminus post quem for reconstruction).
