# Oaxaca Indigenous History (Colonial–Modern)

## Definition and Scope

Regional indigenous history of **Oaxaca** from the Spanish invasion to the late twentieth century, as synthesized by María de los Angeles Romero Frizzi (CHNPA II.2 Ch. 19). Present-day Oaxaca (~95,000 km²) lies where the Eastern and Southern Sierra Madre meet; only ~**9%** arable; flatlands mainly Pacific Coast (Mixtec region), Isthmus of Tehuantepec, and the central [[oaxaca-valley|valleys]]. Traditionally **sixteen** ethnolinguistic groups (Amuzgo, Chatino, Chinantec, Chocho, Chontal, Cuicatec, Huave, Ixcatec, Mazatec, Mixe, Mixtec, Nahuatl, Popoloca/Popoluca, Trique, Zapotec, Zoque); more recent counts give fourteen (Popoloca reduced to migrant families; Ixcatec nearly extinct). Linguistic categories mislead: **identity is primarily village/community-based**; families like [[zapotec|Zapotec]], [[mixtec|Mixtec]], and Mazatec encompass multiple regional languages.

1990 census: **19.3%** of Mexico’s Indian-language speakers in Oaxaca; 1993: **39.1%** of state population over five spoke an Indian language. Largest groups: Zapotec ~**342,000** speakers; Mixtec ~**239,000**. Mixtec extend into Puebla/Guerrero; few Zoque villages in Oaxaca (main concentration Chiapas). Early sixteenth-century population nearly **1.5 million**; 1980 census 3,228,895. Prolonged land use in fragile environments produced alarming erosion (esp. Mixteca).

## Causal Drivers

- **Uneven conquest responses:** alliance (Tehuantepec Zapotec lord with Cortés), decades-long mountain resistance (Mixes, sierra Zapotecs), and opportunistic use of Spaniards by valley Mixtecs and Zapotecs.
- **Indigenous mercantile boom (c. 1540–1570):** Mixtec nobles as principal merchants (cacao via Pacific ships; beasts of burden); silk, cochineal, livestock funding tribute and festivals — change driven by indigenous interest, not Spanish presence alone.
- **Epidemic collapse:** smallpox, measles, and other diseases reduced natives from ~1.5 million (1520) to ~**150,000** by 1650 — principal Spanish ally in consolidating rule ([[virgin-soil-epidemics]]).
- **Destructuring of kingdoms:** mercantile mobility and Spanish courts eroded hereditary lords; by end of sixteenth century **peasant villages** replaced regional kingdoms (Tututepec, Tehuantepec, Zaachila, etc.).
- **Communal revitalization (post-1660):** cabildo, cofradías as mantle for ancestral communal property, relative autonomy under weak haciendas (Carmagnani: reconstitution of ethnic identity).
- **Repartimiento and Bourbon extraction:** alcaldes mayores’ *repartimientos de mercancías*; late Bourbon expropriation of cofradía property for Banco de San Carlos.
- **Liberal and Porfirian property regimes:** anticorporate ideology, disentailment from 1857, 1880s–90s parcelization and sale of “remainder” forest/rotation land to bourgeoisie and foreign companies (liberal *reparto*/disentailment; [[hacienda-system]]).
- **National development bias post-1940:** controlled basic-food prices, inappropriate green-revolution packages, out-migration, coffee clientelism and violence.

## Major Phases

### 1. Contact political landscape and Spanish consolidation (1520s–1560s)

Prehispanic Oaxaca stood at the Mesoamerican heart: contact with Mayas and Nahuas; Mixtec and Zapotec writing/calendars among the oldest in Mesoamerica with some phonetic signs; just before Conquest they wrote history in ideographic books enabling cross-language communication. On the eve of Spanish arrival, Oaxaca was **not a single polity**: Mixes, Chontales and others relatively egalitarian and fragmented; Mixtecs and valley/isthmus Zapotecs highly stratified with hereditary lords (*caciques*) of near-divine status. Spatial form: central settlement + dependent villages in different ecological niches, separated by mountains but united under ruling lords; constant inter-chieftain wars produced multiethnic domains.

Indigenous reactions ranged from alliance to extreme hostility: the **Zapotec lord of Tehuantepec** sought alliance with Cortés before captains landed; mountain Mixes and Zapotecs shut themselves off and resisted contact for decades; valley Mixtecs and Zapotecs sought to use the strangers. Spanish penetration was complex — facilitated by some lords; elsewhere brutal (dogs, gold/slave hunger). Even in hospitable areas Spanish rule was not consolidated until the **1530s**; elsewhere not until ~thirty years later. City of **Antequera** (center of the valleys) founded as a tiny Spanish island (~80 vecinos) amid thousands of indigenous inhabitants. By the 1530s most Mixtec and valley kingdoms recognized Spaniards via tribute, continuing prior Mexica tribute for natives and funding Spanish presence; tribute was the main sustenance of the New Spain economy from 1530 to the early 1560s ([[encomienda]], [[spanish-conquest-of-mexico-1519]]).

### 2. Sixteenth-century mercantile boom under indigenous initiative (c. 1529–1590)

Mercantile economy quickly overlaid tribute: from 1529 Antequera residents used Indian bearers to move European goods from Veracruz and trade them to natives or send to Guatemala along prehispanic routes. **Mixtec nobles** (ancient traders) used Spanish beasts of burden and Pacific ships for cacao from Soconusco, becoming the **principal merchants of sixteenth-century Oaxaca — more important than the Spaniards themselves**. Cortés held villages in valleys and Isthmus of Tehuantepec; estate near Antequera introduced wheat, silkworms, hydraulic mills. By 1540 Mixtecs and valley Zapotecs grew wheat with plows and metal tool tips in Mixteca Alta and Valley of Etla; Mixtec kingdoms famous for silk sold in Puebla; indigenous livestock herds sometimes larger than Spanish. New activities fed luxury commerce (wine, iron, linen) as continuation of prehispanic cacao/cotton/feather trade via nobility and barrio collective church enterprises. ~**1540–1570** silk, livestock, and cochineal brought village wealth enabling easy tribute and funding nobility consumption and Catholic festivals recalling the prehispanic calendar. Far from defeated, Mixtecs and Zapotecs **prolonged past splendor** within a mercantile economy linked to New Spain and Europe.

Counter-trend: **1546** southern sierra messianic-style rebellion demanded Spanish expulsion and return to prehispanic order. By ~**1590** valley and Mixteca natives refused to obey lords (no house-building, work, or tribute). Mercantile channels of social mobility eroded hierarchical native domains; nobles identifying with Spaniards (dress, horseback) weakened traditional power. Appeals to Spanish courts for tribute reduction and arbitration of rivalries further shrank noble power to single settlements. Preconquest broad regional lords gave way by end of the sixteenth century to **peasant villages in place of indigenous kingdoms** — favoring colonial consolidation.

### 3. Demographic collapse and livestock reorientation (1520–1650)

Smallpox, measles, and other diseases reduced native Oaxaca from ~1.5 million (1520) to ~**150,000** by 1650 — worst enemy of cultural vigor. Even expanding valley lords lost tenant/serf populations. From Conquest to ~1580 Oaxaca was key to colonial/world economy via tribute and raw materials (silk, dyes) plus consumption of imports; by late sixteenth century silk declined (labor shortage + Chinese silk competition from ~1565) and dye production concentrated on best land. Response: lords and villages emphasized **small livestock** (sheep/goats) alongside corn/beans; flocks expanded into post-epidemic vacant lands — income for community life but **accelerated erosion** in fragile Mixteca Alta. Spanish economy weaker in Oaxaca than central/northern New Spain: indigenous lords still held much land; Indian herds larger than Spanish; Antequera depended on native production and Central American cacao/indigo transit trade.

### 4. Seventeenth-century crisis and the 1660 Tehuantepec rebellion

Mid-seventeenth century: labor shortage and lower product prices; Mixteca income from small livestock sales and rents to Spanish ranchers; dependence on Spanish traders via *repartimientos de mercancías* involving *alcaldes mayores* who distributed merchandise/cash and collected local products. Networks statewide; in mountains they became the principal nexus of Indian economy. Natives needed candles, mules, machetes for colonial religion and mountain transport; the problem was low prices for their products. When allotments/prices became intolerable they rebelled: **1660 Tehuantepec** Zapotec rising against the alcalde mayor who allotted more cotton than they could weave — extended to Mixe and Chontal sierras, Mixteca Baja, northern Zapotec sierra, Chinantecs. Romero Frizzi: **1660 was the last great colonial revolt in Oaxaca**, ending the vigorous sixteenth-century-style indigenous response.

### 5. Post-1660 colonial society: communal revitalization (to late eighteenth century)

Around 1660 a census showed that the native population had stopped declining and begun slow recovery. Catholic churches in nearly every village; prehispanic temples abandoned but saints combined with prehispanic concepts. Weak haciendas and retained village land enabled treating the colonial world as an external sector with relative autonomy. Radical change: decline of indigenous lords (lost land, labor, sacred power) — though mid-eighteenth-century valley lords of Etla and Cuilapan still held vast lands and tenant villages (Taylor). After **1725** no Mixteca/valley caciques as rulers; entrepreneurial commoners (*macehuales*) gained income/political influence in commercialized zones (democratization). Vacancy of high nobility filled by revitalization of **communal structures** (Carmagnani: reconstitution of ethnic identity) meshing with Spanish communal land, local government, and religious brotherhoods. Cabildo transferred from mid-sixteenth century (sometimes at native request); caciques as governors then nobles as alcaldes/regidores. ***Cofradías*** administered saint property — Spanish form as mantle for ancestral communal property; commercially oriented (Mixteca flocks, Huatulco large livestock, sierra ox/mule teams and low-interest capital); funded taxes, crop losses, church festivals; among the most monetized sectors (Spanish *pueblo de indios* form as shell for communal continuity).

Eighteenth-century population recovery renewed pressure on irrigated parcels and ancestral rivalries; land lawsuits among villages and vs caciques often more dramatic than vs Spaniards. Mixteca: **three indigenous-party suits per one against a Spaniard**; valleys and mountains/coast (minimal Spanish property) similar inter-village fighting; exceptions isthmus and La Cañada with more suits vs Spanish property. Destructured kingdoms + Spanish private-property concept: villages claimed cacique lands; multi-year/century litigation financed by renting land to Spanish ranchers (Mixteca) and valley haciendas — winners of the struggles. Spanish Mixteca livestock fattening and late-century sugarcane/syrup/liquor on rented village/cacique land; valleys: Spanish bought land in the demographic-crisis nadir; by end of eighteenth century **half** of commercial agricultural produce of the Oaxaca bishopric came from Spanish properties. Yet commerce remained the really important activity: village blankets, tallow, wool, cochineal linked to mining/urban demand of central/northern New Spain; Mixteca imports ~47% European (esp. textiles), 38% New Spain, etc. Trade thrived from cultural change (religion, tools, dress), not force alone; low raw-material prices drained local wealth. By later eighteenth century some wealthy natives saw communal structures as ballast for ambition.

### 6. Bourbon crisis, independence, and nineteenth-century pauperization

Late Bourbon reforms targeted village communal cofradía property to finance Banco de San Carlos and Crown needs; many villages hid holdings, others lost fundamental communal property — revolts intensified late century ([[bourbon-reforms]]). Independence and aftermath: anarchy, civil war, foreign invasions. Liberals opposed corporate Indian villages, communal lands, and cofradías as inhibiting initiative and growth; sought private land transfer and commercial agriculture. 1820–mid-century Oaxaca: crisis and depression; **1832** agricultural/livestock production **half** of 1780 levels — worst in Mixteca and valleys most integrated into the colonial market.

Mixteca first half of nineteenth century: private ownership rose; few haciendas prospered by buying cheap in instability and concentrated ~**1/3** of regional livestock; ranchers, mestizos, rich Indians used liberal anticorporate ideas to buy poor Mixtec land. Villages lost corporate resources and capacity to defend collective rights (political reforms); isolated indigenous villages kept more control. Mid-eighteenth-century Mixteca Alta cofradías had produced ~1/4 of market products supporting the poor majority; by ~1832 Mixtecs producing for market down **75%** without cofradía aid — majority **pauperized** while affluent/middle groups profited (Pastor). Sierra Norte: cochineal and cotton blanket trade lost markets to English cloth and aniline dyes → new land litigation and emigration. **Isthmus Zapotecs** retained communal land and coastal salt; **1825** private monopoly order on Tehuantepec salt deposits provoked multi-ethnic discontent (Zoque, Mixe, sierra Zapotec, Chontal); struggle vs salt/land privatization into later nineteenth century. Rebellions longer and multi-village vs eighteenth-century short local revolts: **Triquis and Mixtecs 1832–1857** defending cofradía/communal property; isthmus risings **1827, 1834** (salt, land, vs Oaxaca merchants); violence extended across regions for decades.

### 7. Liberal disentailment, coffee recovery, and Porfirian ruin (1857–1910)

1857 constitution embodied liberal land-distribution laws amid civil war and French intervention. Valley reaction milder where privatization tendency already existed among Indians, but remaining land after distribution could be bought by renters — aiding large valley and Mixteca hacienda owners who had rented communal land. Many communities divided cofradía lands among members to avoid loss. **1867 alone: 604 valley disentailments**; also some isthmus and coastal Mixteca settlements. Civil war/foreign intervention 1857–68 delayed privatization; by **1870** most Oaxaca land still not converted to private property.

After 1870 Liberals regained power; villages rebuilt economy. **Coffee** (promoted with tobacco, cotton as cochineal substitutes) succeeded in Chinantec sierra, southern sierra of Pochutla among Mixes, Choapan, Cuicatlán, etc.; Mixteca and valleys unsuitable. Traders and political bosses controlled networks (e.g., northern sierra requirement of 25 coffee trees/family/year); producers received ~**1/3** of coffee price — new clientelist *caciques*. Coffee intensified individualism, claims of private ownership on former communal forest, internal tensions and boundary disputes. 1870s agricultural growth largely **indigenous**: corn from ~29,000 t/year (1861–69) to ~80,000 t annual average in the 1870s; coffee 73,000 t (1861) to 228,000 t average 1874–79. Population growth >1%/year (first since late eighteenth century). 1877: **77%** of Oaxaca indigenous, high monolingualism, **90%** rural. Growth **1869–1885** due to natives, **not** foreign capital or railroad (rail to Oaxaca city 1892; Tehuantepec railroad 1907; foreign capital important only ~1900–1911). Romero Frizzi: later national/foreign capital **destructured** indigenous production and trade.

From 1885 coffee investment and 1890s laws accelerated privatization: communal land to be divided (parcels worth 200 pesos each), remainder sold to most “efficient” producers — often forest, reserves for growth, or rotation land adjudicated to state/national bourgeoisie and foreign companies (Cerro Mojarra Plantation Co., Palmer and Pinkan, Mexican Land and Coffee Co., etc.); colonial valley haciendas expanded. Uneven distribution: half of distributed land to 1/4 of peasant family heads; some parcels <1–2 ha; five largest haciendas averaged **50,000 ha** each. Most affected: Tuxtepec and La Cañada, then Mixteca and valleys, then isthmus/coast. Corn grew then stabilized then fell after 1900; villages entered the twentieth century with more people, less food, less land. Porfiriato **ruined the dual economy** (basics + cash crops for corn purchase, interregional goods, ceremonial life); capitalist replacement failed to yield desired results. 1877 Oaxaca 2nd in national corn (with Puebla, México, Michoacán), 3rd sugarcane, leader in indigo; by 1888 3rd in corn, sugar/indigo importance lost — competition from Morelos/Veracruz sugar, Chiapas plantation dyes; mountain parcel ecology suited indigenous economy better than modern plantations.

### 8. Revolution and economic coup de grâce (c. 1910–1920)

Revolution culminated long discontent (isthmus 1879, Chatino 1881). Spread through Mixteca (land still mostly native-held) and Tuxtepec/La Cañada/isthmus (foreign/national capital land). Motives complex: internal indigenous tensions, economic deterioration, state interference, nonindigenous political discontent, fiscal policy. Isthmus Zapotecs fought for local political control and lost salt deposits; Tuxtepec (largest adjudicated area, >2 million ha) revolt by small landowners mainly political; Mixteca Zapata movement diffuse (few haciendas). Neighboring villages in ancient land quarrels often took **opposite national sides** (Zapatista vs Maderista) for local reasons. **Sierra de Ixtlán Zapotec (1912–20):** regional leaders tied to Oaxaca city bourgeoisie fought Carranza centralism; mountain caudillos declared **Oaxaca sovereign** — base of support village natives fighting neighbors; some elders later claimed they never knew whether invaders were Zapata, Madero, or Carranza supporters — saved by their saints. Most violent phase to 1917, continued past 1920; **coup de grâce** to remaining indigenous economy: valley/Sierra Norte cotton textile manufacture (~10,000 indigenous weavers in 1900 vs 600 factory weavers) reduced to local consumption; coffee trade decline → precarious subsistence; Porfirian investors fled; estates/plantations/isthmus port structures ruined.

### 9. Postrevolutionary reform, Cárdenas, and late-twentieth-century organizations (1920s–1990s)

Early 1920s: governments distributed minimal land, claimed reform complete by 1928 despite landlessness; 1917–20 only ~**3,000 ha** redistributed in Oaxaca; foreign companies (United Fruit, Giorgio) reinvested in Tuxtepec. Landlords formed armed units in valleys, coast, Miahuatlán sierra, murdering agrarian reformers. By 1933: **128,000 ha** apportioned (~8,000/year) but **80%** not arable or hard to cultivate. **Cárdenas 1934–40:** land to peasants tripled to >**396,000 ha** (from 128,000 for 1915–33); irrigation, credit unions (>200), Banco de Crédito Agrícola, rural schools, socialist education. Large properties: 121 holdings >5,000 ha (1930) → 19 (1940), area 2 million → 170,000 ha. But medium (50–5,000 ha) fell 1,500→500 and small (5–50 ha) 8,400→3,700; survivors often larger; tiny plots proliferated — by end of Cárdenas ~**90%** of rural properties <5 ha constituting only **4.5%** of land area, while **0.2%** of properties (~1,000 ha medium range) held **84%** of land; only *ejidos* grew. Living conditions worsened: Oaxaca infant mortality rose 111.8→131.6 (1930–40) while national fell; death rate rose 27.6→31.2 vs national decline; emigration highest after Guanajuato (**55,000**).

Post-1940: national policy favored northern agricultural potential and industrialization via controlled basic-food prices — devastating for Oaxaca (~**85%** of land in basics in 1950). Part of corn still marketed when cash needed, at low prices; out-migration 1960s ~**290,000** (~15% of 1970 population). Trend to abandon basics for commercial crops/livestock where possible; 1985 still **68%** land in basics (cultural importance of corn + poor unirrigated soils). Coffee: advance-purchase by traders/godfather-client networks (Greenberg); INMECAFE inefficient vs cacique advance payments; Triqui continuous warfare with arms-for-coffee; Chatino daily violence. Oaxaca among top three Mexican coffee states since 1950; state GNP share fell 1.9%→1.5% in the 1960s. Development plans (SARH, INI, roads, dams, fertilizers, improved seed) assumed native “backwardness”; often inappropriate for steep multi-soil terrain; one fertilizer type recommended everywhere; improved seed risked loss of Oaxaca’s many corn varieties. Fertilizers on ~half cultivated land; middle producers with better land gained ~50% yield boost; poor peasants ended indebted and more out-migrant.

**1980–96:** Mixtec, Zapotec, Mixe, Chinantec and other leaders formed organizations (**COCEI, MULT, SER, MICHIZA**, etc.) for language/culture, training, forest management, coffee commercialization. **1994:** bill for constitutional recognition of traditional election of authorities in Oaxaca legislature. Closing tension: world-market liberalization vs indigenous rights claims; possible pluricultural state vs modernization tensions within communities ([[indigenismo]]).

## End Conditions

Ongoing: late-twentieth-century organizational boom and 1994 traditional-authority bill mark a shift from purely defensive land/cofradía politics toward pluricultural constitutional claims, under continuing tension with market liberalization and internal community modernization conflicts. Romero Frizzi closes on that unresolved tension rather than a terminal date.

## Historiography

Romero Frizzi notes no rich colonial document collections comparable to central Mexico; the only major chronicler is Fray Francisco de Burgoa (mid-seventeenth century). Key secondary anchors she flags: colonial — Taylor *Landlord and Peasant*; Chance on Antequera and northern sierra; Carmagnani on ethnic reconstitution; Spores on Mixtecs; Romero Frizzi Mixteca Alta economy; Pastor *Campesinos y reformas*; Hamnett southern Mexico trade; Zeitlin/Tutino on Tehuantepec; 1660 rebellion (Rojas, Díaz Polanco). Nineteenth — Berry *Reform in Oaxaca*; Pastor; Chance & Taylor on cofradías/cargos. Revolution — Ruiz Cervantes; Garner on sovereignty. Twentieth — Greenberg *Blood Ties* (Chatino); Bartolomé & Barabas ethnic dynamics; Boege Mazatecs. Primary: Córdova Zapotec language; *relaciones geográficas*; colonial codices underused for cultural change. Prehispanic baseline cross-links to Marcus & Flannery (CHNPA II.1 Ch. 8).
