# Postglacial Environment of the Near East

The physical-geographic stage on which Near Eastern agriculture and the first
cities arose: the terrain, soils, vegetation and climate of South-West Asia,
Egypt and South-East Europe as they stood after the last glaciation and before
human landscapes overwrote them. Reconstructed systematically in K. W. Butzer's
chapter in [[edwards-cah-v1p1-1970|CAH Vol. I, Part 1]] (Ch. II), this is the
environmental backdrop against which [[early-agriculture-southwest-asia]],
[[sedentism]] and [[urbanization]] must be read.

## Definition and Scope

This page covers the *natural* (pre-agricultural) environment and its
postglacial changes — **not** the later anthropogenic landscape. Butzer's
central caution frames the whole topic: the Near East "presented a somewhat
different aspect in pre-Neolithic times," but the difference is overwhelmingly in
**vegetation and soils** (degraded by millennia of farming, fuel-gathering,
goat-browsing and overgrazing), not in topography, which "has not changed
perceptibly." Present landscapes are therefore a poor guide to prehistoric ones,
and "past and present population densities in any given region need by no means
correspond."

## The Threefold Terrain Framework

Butzer divides the region into three latitudinal belts that structure all
settlement:

1. **Northern Plains and Tablelands** (European Russia): tectonically stable,
   horizontally bedded; mantled in wind-blown **loess** yielding deep, fertile
   **chernozem** ("black earth") soils whose good drainage favours open vegetation
   — significant for early farmers along the Danube and Hungarian plains.
2. **The Mountain Belt** (Balkans, Anatolia, Caucasus, Iran): alpine folding and
   faulting; a mosaic of chains and intermontane basins. Agriculture confined to
   basins, herding on slopes; a patchwork of ecological niches and a possible
   (but not certain) impediment to exchange — natural gateways (Cilician Gates,
   Bosporus/Dardanelles, Diyala–Zagros passes) matter accordingly.
3. **Southern Hills and Plains** (North Africa, Arabia, the Fertile Crescent):
   sedimentary cover on an ancient crystalline shield; settlement governed by
   **water distribution** rather than terrain.

## Soils

- **Loess/chernozem** of the northern steppe — the most fertile and most stable.
- **Terra rossa** of the Mediterranean littoral and the hilly flanks of the
  Fertile Crescent — rich red loams, easily eroded, largely stripped away by
  deforestation, Byzantine-era agricultural decline and overgrazing; it "cannot
  be replaced" under present climate (denuded limestone of the Peloponnese, Syria).
- **Alluvium** of the great valleys (Nile, Tigris–Euphrates): highly fertile
  where fine-grained and repeatedly replenished, but prone to **salinization**
  where bedrock salt and evaporation combine — flagged here as the long-run
  hazard of irrigation in the low Mesopotamian plain.

## Vegetation and the Climatic Belts

Butzer's reconstructed natural vegetation for c. 8000–5000 BCE runs from
cool-temperate coniferous forest (Armenian–Kurdish highlands, Caucasus), through
warm-temperate hardwood forest, to the **Mediterranean evergreen-oak woodland**
(Aegean to Levant) — the last now largely reduced by human agency to *maquis* and,
at the climax of impoverishment, thorny *garigue*. Inland lie the natural
**grasslands** (inner Fertile Crescent, Iran, Turkestan), still open to dry
farming, grading into **semi-desert** (nomadic pastoralism only) and true
**desert**.

## The Arid Limit (Anti-Determinist Caveat)

A recurring analytical line is the **"arid limit"**: below ~4 in. annual rainfall,
human existence outside oases and *galeria* woodland was "distinctly marginal."
Crucially, Butzer states it is "impossible to speak of 'climatic controls' in a
deterministic sense" — climate sets broad limits that technology (irrigation,
clothing, heating) can offset. This 1970 formulation is an early, explicit refusal
of [[societal-collapse-and-environmental-determinism|environmental determinism]],
predating and contrasting with later Diamond-style arguments.

## Postglacial Climate Change: Pluvials and the Neolithic Sub-pluvial

- During the **Würm/Last Glacial** (main and late stages, ~26,000 BCE onward),
  mountain glaciers and moister **"pluvial"** conditions prevailed in lower
  latitudes; vegetation belts were displaced southward.
- In Egypt, Butzer identifies a **"Neolithic Sub-pluvial" (c. 5500–2350 BCE)**, a
  moister phase during which parts of the now-hyperarid desert carried vegetation
  and seasonal streams — a window relevant to early Nile-margin settlement before
  the desiccation that concentrated population on the floodplain.

## The Great Alluvial Lowlands

- **Mesopotamia:** the interior "delta" of the Tigris and Euphrates is a young,
  aggrading plain reaching toward the **Persian Gulf** (whose head stood further
  inland in antiquity along the Shatt al-Arab). Flooding was less regular and more
  destructive than the Nile's, and the delta margin — not the lower floodplain —
  was the early centre of agricultural settlement, the seedbed of the
  [[chalcolithic|Ubaid period]].
- **Egypt:** the Nile floodplain offered uniquely regular, predictable inundation;
  the contrast with Mesopotamia's erratic rivers is a structural difference Butzer
  draws out and a theme the wiki carries into the divergent characters of the two
  earliest [[urbanization|urban civilizations]].

## Interaction

This environmental frame is the precondition for, not the cause of,
[[early-agriculture-southwest-asia|Southwest Asian agriculture]]
(cf. [[why-did-agriculture-begin]]), [[domestication]], and the
[[urban-revolution|emergence of cities]] documented from the Ubaid period onward.
Read against Holocene-scale climate and the [[secondary-products-revolution]].

## End Conditions

The "natural" environment ends, in Butzer's framing, precisely where the wiki's
agricultural and urban narratives begin: human modification of soils and
vegetation from the Neolithic onward transforms the landscape so thoroughly that
the prehistoric baseline survives only as relict patches and as the work of
reconstruction.
