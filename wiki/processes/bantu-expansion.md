## Definition and Scope

The **Bantu expansion** is the spread, over several millennia, of
**Bantu-speaking farmer-herders** from a homeland around **west Cameroon /
southeastern Nigeria** across roughly the southern third of Africa — carrying
agriculture, and later iron-working, into central, eastern, and southern Africa
and largely supplanting or absorbing resident hunter-gatherer and (in the south)
Khoe-San communities. It is the African instance of an [[agricultural-origins|agriculture]]-driven
language dispersal, discussed in CWH Vol. II (Pala et al. Ch. 2; Ehret Ch. 3;
Lane Ch. 18) and a counterpart to the [[austronesian-dispersal]] in the Pacific.

## The two streams

Bantu-speakers spread in two broad streams from the Cameroon/Nigeria core:
- a **western stream** through the Congo forest zone, assimilating Central African
  forest foragers (the "Batwa"/"Pygmy" communities);
- an **eastern stream** that, in East Africa, **encountered settled populations
  already growing cereals** — and borrowed heavily from them. Sudanic and Sahelian
  **loanwords** for livestock and cereals in Eastern Bantu languages directly
  attest the influence of Nilo-Saharan/Sahelian speakers on the Eastern Bantu
  ("Mashariki") during the last millennium BCE.

Domesticates reached the **Southern Cape ~2,000 years ago**.

## The central debate: migration vs. assimilation

A major question (the African parallel to [[demic-vs-cultural-diffusion]]): was
farming taken south by Bantu-speaking farmers in a **relatively straightforward
migration**, or by more **complex scenarios of assimilation and exchange**?

[[archaeogenetics|Genetic evidence]] points to **heavy assimilation**, not simple
replacement:
- southern-African Bantu-speakers show a **maternal founder effect ~2,000 years
  ago** but carry mtDNA/Y lineages from west Central Africa, East Africa, *and*
  southern Africa, with **Khoe-San ancestry increasing southward**;
- southwest Bantu-speakers carry many **Central African forest-forager lineages**
  (assimilated in the forest zone) — counter-intuitively *more diverse* in
  Bantu-speakers than in the surviving foragers themselves, implying large-scale
  assimilation plus drift-driven loss among foragers.

So the expansion "hugely reshaped the genetic landscape of Africa," but through
entanglement with indigenous peoples rather than wholesale displacement.

## The collapse of the old model (CWH IV)

[[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|CWH IV]] (Burstein, Ch 23) records how the classic mid-twentieth-century
picture **collapsed in the 1990s**. The old view held the Bantu expansion to be a relatively
**rapid migration of iron-using farmers** out of the Nigeria–Cameroon borderland, carrying a
ready-made package of mixed agriculture, pottery, and **iron metallurgy** that gave them
decisive advantage over foragers. Intensified linguistics, archaeology, and radiocarbon dating
overturned this on several fronts: the break-up of Proto-Bantu may have begun as early as the
**third millennium BCE** (far too early for iron to have been the cause); Proto-Bantu were
**forest root-crop cultivators** who only acquired **grain crops** later, from **Cushitic
speakers** met during the expansion; and ironworking in the **Great Lakes region** dates to the
**early first millennium BCE — earlier than in North Africa or the Nile Valley**, strongly
implying **independent sub-Saharan invention** of iron rather than diffusion from the
Mediterranean (a key point Burstein shares with the [[nok-culture|Nok]] evidence). The expansion
is now seen as a **gradual, centuries-long process** that grew "as much by absorbing foraging
and farming peoples as by migration" — converging with the genetics-led assimilation account
above.

## End Conditions

By ~500 CE farming, herding, and (often) iron-working communities coexisted with
foragers and pastoralists across central and southern Africa; Bantu languages
came to cover about a third of the continent. Detailed reconstruction of the
processes "still lies ahead" (Pala et al.).

## Historiography note

The simple "Bantu migration carried farming + iron south in one wave" model is now
regarded as too crude; genetics and linguistics favour a protracted,
assimilation-heavy process. The expansion is a key test case for the
farming/language-dispersal hypothesis (see
[[historical-linguistics-and-agriculture]]).
