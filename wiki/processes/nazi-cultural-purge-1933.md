# Nazi Purge of German Cultural Life (1933)

The systematic removal of Jewish, left-wing, and "modernist" figures from
German orchestras, opera, theatre, cinema, radio, the press, literature, the
visual arts, and the universities in the first year of Nazi rule — framed as
combating "cultural Bolshevism." Source: Evans
([[evans-coming-of-third-reich-2003]], Part 6). Distinct from, and broader
than, [[nazification-of-german-education-1933-1939]] (schooling) and
[[persecution-of-german-jews-1933-1939]] (many purged figures were non-Jewish
leftists or modernists).

## Definition and Scope

Hitler had written that "artistic Bolshevism is the only possible cultural
form and spiritual expression of Bolshevism as a whole"; the attribution of
modernism to "Jewish subversion" was, Evans stresses, factually baseless —
"Modernist German culture was not sustained by the Jews, many of whom in
practice were as culturally conservative as other middle-class Germans."

## Causal Drivers

Nazi ideology (Hitler the failed artist's "morbid excrescences of insane and
degenerate men" verdict on modernism; Rosenberg's traditionalist aesthetics);
pre-existing conservative distaste for Weimar modernism reaching well beyond
the NSDAP; the new Reich Ministry for Popular Enlightenment and Propaganda
(decree of 13 March 1933; ~350 officials averaging barely thirty years old) —
Evans calls it Hitler's "most original institutional creation." Goebbels's
stated aim (15 March 1933): not merely to neutralize the majority who had not
voted Nazi but to "work on people until they have become addicted to us." The
recurring mechanism was the "dialectic that drove the seizure of power onward
from both above and below": brownshirt disruption gave commissioners the
public-order pretext for bans, while professional bodies "co-ordinated"
themselves.

## Major Phases

1. **Music** (Feb–June): concerts by Fritz Busch, Bruno Walter, and Otto
   Klemperer disrupted or banned; the 7 April civil-service law extended to
   conductors, singers, and academy professors (Schoenberg and Schreker
   dismissed from the Prussian Academy); jazz condemned as racially
   "inferior" but persisting clandestinely. Furtwängler's open letter of 11
   April protesting the dismissal of Jewish musicians was answered not with
   confrontation but financial strangulation, until he appealed to Hitler for
   a Reich takeover of the Philharmonic (26 October) — capture completed (see
   [[coercion-and-consent-nazi-germany]]).
2. **Film**: Hugenberg's UFA began "voluntarily" dismissing Jewish staff in
   March; Reich Film Chamber 14 July. Lang, Wilder, Pabst, and Ophüls
   emigrated — though of the 75 top-billed stars of 1932, only 13 left.
3. **Radio**: 270 sackings (13% of staff) in six months; centralized under
   Goebbels by decree of 30 June 1933.
4. **Press**: KPD/SPD papers closed; the journalists' association
   self-coordinated 30 April (Otto Dietrich), the publishers' 28 June (Max
   Amann); open press conferences became secret briefings. Persecuted
   journalists: Carl von Ossietzky (tortured at Sonnenburg, forced to dig a
   mock grave), Erich Mühsam (killed at Oranienburg), Ernst Toller (exile;
   suicide 1939).
5. **Literature**: Brecht, Remarque, both Manns, Döblin, Feuchtwanger, Zweig
   emigrated; denaturalization of émigrés from 23 August 1933. Evans: "By the
   end of 1933 there was scarcely a writer of any talent or reputation left in
   Germany." Approximately 2,000 people active in the arts emigrated.
6. **Visual art and architecture**: the Prussian Academy purged (Liebermann
   forced out at 86; Kollwitz expelled; Nolde expelled despite party
   membership since 1920); 27 museum curators sacked; "Chamber of Art
   Horrors" exhibitions of "Cultural Bolshevism"; Mies van der Rohe expelled;
   the Berlin Bauhaus raided and closed in April 1933.
7. **Universities**: via the 7 April law, 1,145 of 7,758 established
   university teachers (15%) dismissed by the 1933–34 academic year — nearly a
   third in Berlin and Frankfurt; about a third dismissed as Jewish; twenty
   past or future Nobel laureates dismissed or driven out (Einstein, Hertz,
   Schrödinger, Born, Haber, Krebs). Evans cites the citation study (émigré
   biologists averaged 130 postwar citations against 42 for those who stayed):
   "those who went were generally better than those who stayed." When Carl
   Bosch warned Hitler of the damage to German science, Hitler replied that
   Germany "could get on for another hundred years without any physics or
   chemistry at all."
8. **Culmination**: the [[book-burnings-1933|book burnings]] of 10 May 1933.

**The Heidegger case.** Martin Heidegger — elected Rector of Freiburg on 21
April 1933 by a vote from which the university's twelve already-suspended
Jewish professors were barred — declared academic freedom "not genuine,"
ended his inaugural with the Horst Wessel Song, joined the party on 1 May,
introduced the Führer principle into university governance, denounced the
chemist Staudinger to the political police, and enforced the dismissal of
Jewish colleagues including his mentor Husserl (though Evans specifically
flags the library-ban story as unfounded). "The Führer himself and he alone is
the German reality, present and future, and its law." Husserl, 4 May 1933:
"The future alone will judge which was the true Germany in 1933." Heidegger
resigned the rectorate in April 1934 amid infighting but never apologized.

## Geographic Spread

Nationwide, centrally coordinated (students' union; Propaganda Ministry) with
regional variation — Thuringia had purged modernist art from Weimar's museum
even before 1933.

## End Conditions

The visible purge phase was essentially complete by December 1933; racial
exclusion and cultural policing then institutionalized in the Reich culture
chambers (see [[evans-third-reich-in-power-2005]] for 1933–39).
