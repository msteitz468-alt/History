# Arctic Native History from Norse Contact to Modern Times

Long-duration process spanning Norse–[[thule-culture|Thule]] encounter in Greenland and Labrador through commercial whaling, mission-trade pacification, contact-traditional fox-trade stabilization, postwar centralization, and late-twentieth-century land claims and self-government. Primary synthesis: David Damas, CHNPA I.2 ch. 14 (“The Arctic from Norse Contact to Modern Times”), with modern political notes from Washburn ch. 15.

## Definition and Scope

This process covers Arctic North America and Greenland from roughly the Norse arrival in southwest Greenland near the end of the tenth century through the political settlements of the 1970s–1990s. It is **not** a precontact archaeological reconstruction of the full Palaeo-Eskimo sequence (that belongs with [[dorset-culture]], [[thule-culture]], and related pages). It **is** the post–Norse-contact history of peoples properly designated [[inuit]] (and, carefully, related Eskimo-Aleut groups such as [[yupik|Yuit/Yupik]] speakers where the source names them), including colonial Greenland and Alaska.

Core arc (Damas): Dorset remains at Norse arrival → Thule expansion and Inuksuk-phase co-occupation → multi-causal Norse colony disappearance → intermittent violent/trade contact (Frobisher–Davis; seventeenth-century kidnappings) → eighteenth-century mission-trade pacification in Greenland/Labrador → nineteenth-century exploration ethnography and divergent eastern vs western commercial whaling → early twentieth-century fox-trade “contact-traditional” horizon under HBC/RCMP/missions → WWII ending isolation, postwar centralization and welfare state → 1960–90 political organizations culminating in Greenland Home Rule, [[alaska-native-claims-settlement-1971]] (+1988 amendments), James Bay and Northern Quebec Agreement, Western Arctic (Inuvialuit) Claim, [[nunavut]] Agreement, and ICC pan-Inuit politics under continuing economic dependency.

## Causal Drivers

- **Climate and ice ecology:** Christian Vibe’s periodic sea-ice stages (pre-1150 cool stagnation linked to Thule in north Greenland; 1150–1340 warmer Baffin Bay whaling; 1340–1450 cold Inuksuk southward ice-hunting expansion; 1450–1560 warmer stages and possible seal absence at Eastern Settlement) structure Norse–Thule settlement dynamics; Damas finds aspects persuasive but notes paucity of accurate dating for southernmost and east-coast Greenland.
- **Thule technological package and radiation:** Maritime hunting, dog sleds, and related toolkit explain biological, linguistic, and cultural similarities of Eskimo peoples from Bering Strait to Greenland (McGhee, endorsed by Arctic prehistorians per Damas).
- **European demand:** Iron and wood for Inuit; hides, oils, falcons, walrus ivory (Norse); baleen, whale products, white fox, and later oil (commercial eras).
- **Mission and trade monopolies:** Hans Egede (1721), Moravians (Greenland and Labrador), Royal Greenland Trade Department monopoly, HBC in Canadian Arctic—pacification, conversion, and goods flow without full agricultural dispossession.
- **State incorporation:** Danish colonial administration and G-50/G-60 modernization; Canadian RCMP, NWT Council, family allowances, DEW line; U.S. Alaska wartime boom, Anti-Discrimination Act 1945, statehood 1958, ANCSA 1971.
- **Native political organization:** AFN, ITC/Inuit Tapirisat, COPE, LIA, Makivik, regional parties in Greenland (Siumut, Atassut, Inuit Ataqatigiit), ICC from 1977.

## Major Phases

### 1. Norse, Dorset, and Thule (c. 985–c. 1500)

Norse settlers occupied two southwest-Greenland colonies—the Eastern Settlement (peak ~4,000–5,000) and Western Settlement (peak ~1,000–1,600)—for up to five centuries, combining animal husbandry (sheep and goats chief; also cattle, pigs, horses) with sea-mammal and some terrestrial hunting; grain failed; farms deep in fjords. When Erik the Red landed near the end of the tenth century, remains of prior inhabitants (dwellings, skin boats, stone implements) belonged to [[dorset-culture|Dorset]]; Late Dorset probably occupied west Greenland north of Holsteinsborg c. A.D. 700–900, preceding Norse by about a century; meetings during Norse times judged unlikely.

First Norse–[[thule-culture|Thule]] contacts are usually assigned to the early thirteenth century around Disko Bay. Sagas stress bloody combats; archaeology indicates peaceful contacts also. Norse materials in Thule sites define the **Inuksuk (Inugsuk) phase**—not necessarily exchange or pillage (materials may have been collected from abandoned farms). Theories of Norse disappearance (epidemics, lower fertility, pirates, Inuit extermination, emigration, absorption) remain multi-causal and unsolved (Kleivan); McGovern suggests Western Settlement may have been overrun while Eastern Settlement was too populous, citing resource competition and Norse failure to adopt ice hunting (especially ringed seal) under cooling climate.

Norse remains at L’Anse aux Meadows confirm Newfoundland; saga geography (Markland, Helluland, Vinland); first “skraeling” encounter likely Hamilton Inlet with Point Revenge Indian culture rather than Dorset. Effects of Norse contacts on Thule were probably minor; no evidence of epidemic spread to Thule. McGhee argues contacts were more frequent and widespread than historical accounts record.

### 2. Rediscovery, warfare, trade, and kidnapping (15th–17th centuries)

Fifteenth century severed Greenland–Scandinavia ties and ended Norse colonies. Cabot (1497) and João Fernandes (1502) left no recorded Inuit contact. Frobisher (1576–78) and Davis (1585–87) set a pattern of mixed warfare and trade; Inuit sought metal by any means; English kidnap readiness undermined peace. Seventeenth century: continued hostility interspersed with trade; between 1605 and 1660 Danish-Norwegian and Dutch ships kidnapped about thirty Eskimos for exhibition. Late-century Greenland contacts became more peaceful and trusting.

### 3. Eighteenth-century pacification (missions and monopolies)

**Greenland:** 1721 landmark—Hans Egede’s Norwegian Lutheran mission, first European settlement since Norse. Royal Greenland Trade monopoly after 1774; coast closed to foreign ships 1776; deliberate policy kept hunting principal occupation and discouraged population aggregation. Moravians from 1733; interbreeding created intermediate West Greenland population; Instruction of 19 April 1782 established two inspectorates.

**Labrador:** Moravians founded Nain (1771), Okak (1776), Hopedale (1782)—proselytizing plus trade; virtual abandonment of much northern coast as Inuit concentrated near mission centers.

**Central/western coasts:** Hearne at Coppermine mouth July 1771 (Athapaskan companions attacked Inuit below Bloody Falls); Mackenzie 1789 to Arctic Ocean without direct Inuit contact. By 1800, pacification achieved in Greenland and Labrador; vast central regions still almost unaffected.

### 4. Nineteenth-century exploration and commercial whaling

Explorers (Parry and Lyon among Iglulik; Ross among Netsiliks; Franklin parties; Beechey at Point Barrow) produced classic ethnography. Eastern Arctic commercial whaling (English, then Scottish shore stations; later Roes Welcome Sound American overwintering) brought firearms, whaleboats, genetic interchange and disease, but no clear large-scale depopulation in east-central Arctic. **Western Arctic whaling** (Herschel Island 1889–1906) was catastrophic for Mackenzie Eskimos: mid-century estimates 2,000–2,500 → only 200–300 by end of whaling. Damas warns against overgeneralizing “domination” narratives from the western case to the east. Caribou Eskimo inland adaptation west of Hudson Bay emerged as coastal habitation was largely deserted.

### 5. Contact-traditional horizon (c. 1900–1940)

White-fox trade became chief Central Arctic outside-economy link; HBC posts 1911–23 reached almost all Arctic Canada Inuit groups; Depression nearly monopolized trade under HBC. Rifles, nets, wooden boats, steel traps altered seasonal rounds and settlement (small hunting-trapping sites replaced large winter sea-ice aggregations). NWMP/RCMP, NWT Council (from 1921), Anglican and Oblate missions (syllabary from E.J. Peck 1894). Greenland: Act of Administration of 18 April 1925 (districts, compulsory education 7–14, municipal councils of Greenlanders). Labrador: Moravian trade gave way to HBC; cod then fox under Depression strain. Population uneven: Greenland nearly +50% 1900–40; Labrador slow; Mackenzie original Inuit demise not offset by Alaskan influx.

### 6. WWII and postwar centralization

**Greenland:** Kauffman Treaty U.S. bases; cryolite; commodity flood; Helge Kleivan’s 1945 underdevelopment diagnosis despite benevolent Danish rule; G-50 plan (commercial fishing, town relocation, open trade) without training Greenlanders for skilled jobs → Danish influx and inequality; Constitution 1953 abolished colonial status. **Labrador:** confederation 1949; 1950s government-directed coastal centralization. **Canadian Arctic:** family allowance (full by 1947) outstripped fur income; DEW line jobs; mid-1950s large-scale centralization; Cape Dorset soapstone art from 1949. **Alaska:** wartime boom; Anti-Discrimination Act 1945; housing programs; education lag into 1960.

### 7. Political awakening and settlements (1960–1995)

- **Greenland Home Rule** (law 29 Nov. 1978; effective 1 May 1979); EC withdrawal 1 Jan. 1985 (52–46%); ICC identity over Europe (Washburn).
- **James Bay and Northern Quebec Agreement 1975** (first modern Canadian Native claims settlement per Washburn; Inuit + Cree; Makivik; dissident Inuit Tungavingat Nunami).
- **COPE / Western Arctic (Inuvialuit) Claim** finalized 5 June 1984.
- **Labrador Inuit Association** 1973; land-use study accepted Ottawa 1978; self-government lagged into 1990s.
- **[[nunavut]]:** ITC proposal; land claim spring 1990; final ratification of Nunavut Agreement **25 May 1993**; territory by 1999 (Washburn: 17,500 Inuit, 80% of designated area, ownership of 350,000 km²).
- **[[alaska-native-claims-settlement-1971]]:** AFN 1966 → ANCSA corporations; 20-year termination-like provisions; Reagan amendments Feb. 1988; status still unsettled into 1990s (Washburn: Ada Deer 1993 tribal recognition; ultimate status open).
- **Inuit Circumpolar Conference** founded 1977; UN NGO 1983; environmentalism defending hunting rights vs Greenpeace-style bans; assertive pan-Inuit image under continuing dependency.

## Geographic Spread

Greenland (west coast primary; east and Polar Eskimo later); Labrador and northern Newfoundland contacts; Canadian High and Central Arctic (Baffin, Hudson Bay shores, Boothia, King William Island, Mackenzie delta); northern and northwestern Alaska and Bering Sea (Inuit and [[yupik|Yuit]] speakers). Expansion and abandonment of High Arctic islands after Thule peak; postwar nucleation into towns (Godthab/Nuuk region, Frobisher Bay/Iqaluit, Inuvik, Barrow, Kotzebue, etc.).

## End Conditions

The process is **ongoing**. Political milestones of the 1970s–90s (Home Rule, ANCSA and amendments, James Bay, Inuvialuit claim, Nunavut Agreement) transformed legal status and local authority without ending economic dependency on state transfers (Damas: NWT-scale subsidization ratios; mid-1980s Greenland “artificial respiration from Denmark”). Cultural and political assertiveness replaced older images of passive hunter-trappers; resource rights, wildlife quotas, and circumpolar diplomacy remain live arenas. Climate change and resource extraction (beyond Damas’s close) continue the arc.

## Historiography

Damas synthesizes Norse–Inuit archaeology (Gad; Kleivan; McGhee; Mathiassen/Holtved Inuksuk), voyage literature (Frobisher/Stefansson; Davis; Baffin), explorers (Parry, Lyon, Ross, Franklin, Beechey, Kashevarov), mission and colonial series (Moravian reports; Helge Kleivan on Labrador; Gad’s *History of Greenland*), whaling studies (W.G. Ross; Bockstoce/Stevenson), classic ethnography (Boas, Murdoch, Rasmussen Fifth Thule, Jenness, Birket-Smith, Spencer), administration comparisons (Jenness *Eskimo Administration* 5 vols), and land-claims-era work (Burch; Duffy *Road to Nunavut*). Handbook of North American Indians vol. 5 *Arctic* is the standing synthesis pointer. Interpretive open questions: multi-causal Norse demise; Vibe climate dating gaps; east–west asymmetry of whaling impacts; ICC environmental philosophy vs Western animal-rights frames. Terminology: prefer **Inuit** for Arctic North American self-designation; do not replace “Eskimo” wholesale for all Eskimo-Aleut or prehistoric populations; [[yupik|Yuit]] and Aleut lack *Inuit* in their vocabularies (Damas bibliographic essay).
