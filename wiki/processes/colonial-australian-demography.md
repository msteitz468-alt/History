# Colonial Australian Demography

## Definition and Scope

The demographic history of colonial Australia (1788–1901) consists of two radically divergent trajectories: the catastrophic decline of the [[aboriginal-australians|Aboriginal population]] and the explosive growth of the settler population through convict transportation, assisted immigration, free migration, and high natural fertility. By 1901 the settler colony had become "one of the most urbanised societies in the world," with 3.8 million people — including 2.9 million native-born — concentrated in coastal capital cities, while fewer than 100,000 Aboriginal people survived.

The chapter (McCalman and Kippen) emphasises that "the history of colonial Australia's population is one of stark contrasts: a largely opaque record of the decline of the Aboriginal people, and a conscientiously documented populating of the continent by the forced and voluntary immigration of external settlers."

## Causal Drivers

**Aboriginal population collapse.** The estimated pre-contact Aboriginal population of c. 1 million was devastated by:
- *Smallpox epidemics*: erupted at Port Jackson in April 1789, killing entire communities. Smallpox travelled inland twice more (late 1820s, early 1860s), each time "'clearing' the country for the advance of pastorialist pioneers." In Victoria, the Aboriginal population fell from an estimated 60,000 in 1788 to around 650 in 1901 — smallpox having already "cut the population in half twice before European permanent settlement in 1834."
- *Slow-burning infections*: tuberculosis, sexually transmitted infections, and typhoid ("first known as 'colonial fever'") killed across all age groups and prevented women from reproducing. "Tuberculosis remained a disproportionate killer of Aboriginal Australians until the last quarter of the twentieth century."
- *Ecological displacement*: by 1850 "almost 17 million sheep and cattle" grazed "the best land," destroying Aboriginal food systems (see [[environmental-transformation-colonial-australia]]).
- *Institutional confinement*: when brought under Protection Board reserves from the 1860s, Aboriginal people were forced into settled existence, increasing exposure to respiratory and diarrhoeal diseases. Reserve babies "typically died of respiratory diseases before late weaning."

**Convict transportation (1788–1868).** 165,000 convicts were transported, providing unique records (height, birthplace, skills, literacy, religion). However, their demographic impact was "rather less" than their social impact: most male convicts in Van Diemen's Land "failed to find a wife in the colonies and left no discoverable descendants." Only 16 per cent of those transported before 1821 became "something more than labourers."

**Sex ratio imbalance.** Convict transportation produced a severe male surplus: in Van Diemen's Land six men for every woman in the early 1820s, and still more than twice as many men as women by the early 1850s. This shaped unusual marriage patterns: female marriage rates were very high (almost two-thirds of women aged 20–24 were ever married in late-1850s Victoria) while many men were excluded from the marriage market.

**Assisted immigration.** The settler population grew slowly until the 1830s. The 1788–1850 period saw planned colonisation: South Australia on the Wakefield model, Van Diemen's Land as a penal colony. "The great European peopling of Australia thus occurred between 1850 and 1890," distinctive for its "high level of government-assisted immigration, paid for by the sale of land seized by the crown." Between 1861 and 1900, 1.2 million people emigrated from Britain, a third to Queensland, almost half of them government-assisted.

**Gold rushes.** The 1850s gold rush "accelerated settlement by free immigration, extinguishing the convict stigma." In the gold decade 1851–61 the Australian population nearly trebled, from 405,000 to 1,145,000. Victoria's self-funded founding population in the 1850s "brought a wave of modernity to an otherwise traditional, pre-industrial outpost."

**High natural fertility.** Founding-generation couples practised natural fertility; completed families of more than ten or twelve children were common. In the mid-nineteenth century Australian women averaged six children each. Continued geographical endogamy "rapidly produced dense kinship networks" within two generations.

## Major Phases

**Phase 1 — Convict colony (1788–c.1838).** Population growth slow; stigma and distance deters free settlers. By 1838 colonised Australia was still an "archipelago" of Europeans "pullulating timidly on the edge of alien shores." From 1830, free immigrants and native-born overtook the bond population in New South Wales.

**Phase 2 — Pastoral expansion and planned colonisation (1830s–1850).** New colonies established in South Australia and Victoria; second smallpox epidemic swept Murray-Darling basin in the 1830s; convict transportation to Van Diemen's Land increased. Planned colonisation imported couples and families — "young couples and established families who could become self-sufficient pioneers." Total population c. 400,000 in 1851.

**Phase 3 — Gold-rush transformation (1851–1865).** Population trebled; Victoria overtook New South Wales and maintained ascendancy until the 1890s. 584,000 people arrived in Victoria 1851–60, 85 per cent paying their own way. High internal mobility: "just under half that number was to move on, looking for a new El Dorado."

**Phase 4 — Assisted immigration peak and fertility transition (1865–1890).** Queensland received the largest share of assisted immigrants (107,000 in the 1880s alone), producing "a pioneering population far less endowed with both educational and financial capital." Birth rates fell steadily: from an average of 7 births per married woman (cohort born 1837–46) to 5.3 births (cohort born 1862–66). Fertility limitation relied primarily on abstinence, withdrawal, and from the 1890s abortion.

**Phase 5 — Depression, drought, and demographic transition (1890–1901).** The 1890s depression combined with three El Niño drought periods (1895–1903) produced "the most severe drought known to Europeans in Australia." Population growth slowed; by 1901 the colony "had come to depend on natural increase rather than migration." Life expectancy at birth had risen from 45 to over 50 years; more than half the population could expect to survive to their 60th birthday.

## Geographic Spread

Demographic distribution in 1851: New South Wales most populous (178,688), followed by Van Diemen's Land (70,130), Victoria (77,345), South Australia (63,700), Western Australia (11,743), and Queensland (8,575). By 1901 New South Wales and Victoria each housed more than 1 million people. South Australia was the most urbanised state (45 per cent in the metropolis); Western Australia and Tasmania least (20 per cent each). One-quarter of all Australians lived in Melbourne or Sydney in 1901.

## Health and Disease

**European settler health.** Despite difficult conditions, infant mortality was relatively low by European standards: just over 10 per cent of babies died before their first birthday in Tasmania and South Australia, versus typical European rates of 15 per cent and above. Life expectancy was around five years higher than in England and Wales. Colonial Australia was "a more healthful place to live than Britain, for settlers not Aboriginal Australians."

**Disease burden.** Typhoid ("colonial fever") was ubiquitous wherever soil and water were contaminated. Measles, whooping cough, scarlet fever, and new influenza strains caused severe mortality crises among the immunologically naive colonial-born: in Tasmania in the early 1850s, successive waves killed one in fifteen children. Group A streptococcus (scarlet fever, rheumatic fever, puerperal fever) created chains of poverty and infection lasting into adult life. Tuberculosis was near-universal — "a majority of Australians were infected by the time they reached 30 years of age" — but declined over the century for settlers while remaining "a disproportionate killer of Aboriginal Australians."

**Sanitation reform.** Urban sanitation was catastrophically inadequate through the 1880s: wells contaminated by cesspits, the Yarra a "stinking receptacle for all manner of solid and liquid waste." The 1890s colonial inquiries revealed "a range of sanitary abominations." Melbourne's typhoid peak in the late 1880s drove sewering despite financial stringency. Clean drinking water was "the single most important factor in the health transition in comparable New World urban aggregates."

**Epidemiological transition.** F.B. Smith identified the decline in tuberculosis and infant mortality as the distinctive changes of the late colonial/early Federation era. The transition combined: compulsory schooling from the 1870s, better urban food distribution (railways, Chinese market gardens), improved water reticulation, antiseptic midwifery (from 1887), and the Infant Life Protection Act of 1893. The real fall in infant mortality did not begin until the turn of the century.

**Maternal and reproductive health.** High infant mortality pushed women toward shorter birth intervals. From the 1870s women increasingly stopped childbearing after the third or fourth child. By the 1890s post-abortal sepsis "began appearing in increasing numbers in hospitals" as women employed self-administered abortion to limit families. In 1903 a New South Wales Royal Commission blamed fertility decline on couples' "selfishness."

## End Conditions

By 1901 the demographic transition — to lower fertility and lower mortality — was "well advanced." The settler population had shifted to dependence on natural increase. The Aboriginal population had suffered catastrophic declines in fertile, well-watered regions; in Tasmania "official Aborigines" were supposedly extinct and in Victoria reduced to 1 per cent of their original strength — though "unofficial Aborigines" (descendants of mixed relationships) were beginning to rebuild communities. The Federation era inherited both a powerful demographic momentum in the settler population and an unresolved humanitarian catastrophe in the Aboriginal population.

## Historiography

The chapter (McCalman and Kippen) notes Victoria as the best-documented colony for demographic history, owing to the statistician William Henry Archer (disciple of William Farr) and his successor H.H. Hayter, plus Timothy Coghlan of New South Wales. Queensland, South Australia, and Western Australia "are sorely in need of demographic historical research." Aboriginal population history is doubly thin: colonial authorities documented the European population "exhaustively" while Aboriginal statistics were "opaque." Estimates of the pre-contact Aboriginal population have risen as archaeological and anthropological research has revealed the complexity of Aboriginal land management and food systems.

The Noel Butlin debate on smallpox introduction (British responsibility vs. prior Indonesian/Macassan transmission via trepang fishermen) remains partially open; subsequent work (Judy Campbell, *Invisible Invaders*, 2002) has developed more complex models for disease spread through Aboriginal trade networks.
