# Wartime Disillusionment and the Birth of Dada and Surrealism

The **intellectual and artistic reckoning** with the First World War: the collapse of the
prewar generation's faith in heroism, reason, and progress under the weight of four years of
industrialized slaughter, and the avant-garde movements — **Dada** and **Surrealism** — that
crystallized out of that collapse. Distinct from [[war-memory-and-mourning]] (the ritual and
commemorative response to mass bereavement), this process concerns the war's effect on
**ideas, literature, and art**. Source: [[paxton-europe-twentieth-century-2011]], ch. 4.

## Definition and Scope

This is the process by which the optimistic, rationalist self-image of prewar European
"advanced" civilization was discredited by its own capacity for mechanized mass death, and by
which that discrediting was worked out in literature (the war-poetry canon of repudiation) and
in the visual and literary avant-garde (Dada, then Surrealism). It is not identical to
[[total-war]] (the structural/military-economic transformation) or to [[war-memory-and-mourning]]
(commemoration of the dead); it concerns the war's effect on the intellectual and artistic
self-understanding of European elites.

## Causal Drivers

The driver was the **gap between 1914 expectation and 1914–18 reality**. Poets and writers
who went to war in August 1914 largely shared the era's belief that war "sharpened" a man and
released a virile, heroic self from bourgeois "brittle things" — sentiments voiced by Rupert
Brooke ("Now, God be thanked Who has matched us with His hour . . .") and Charles Péguy
("Blessed are they who died in great battles"). Trench warfare — mechanized, impersonal,
prolonged — falsified this expectation. Wilfred Owen, who survived to 1918, moved from
"pallid romantic verses" to the "old lie" denunciation of *Dulce et Decorum Est*
(1917) and a rewritten Parable of Abraham in which "the old man would not [heed the angel], and
slew his son / And half the seed of Europe, one by one." Erich Maria Remarque's *All Quiet on
the Western Front* (1929) made the patriotic older generation — not the enemy — the villain:
"The first bombardment showed us our mistake, and under it the world as they had taught it to
us broke in pieces." Lytton Strachey's *Eminent Victorians* (1918) turned the same repudiation
on Victorian biography itself.

## Major Phases

1. **1914–15 — heroic idiom.** Poets and volunteers write within an unbroken prewar rhetoric of
   heroism, national purification, and release from bourgeois constraint.
2. **1916–18 — repudiation.** Sustained trench experience (Verdun, the Somme) produces a
   literature of anger directed at the "fathers" who sent the young to war, not primarily at the
   enemy. Owen, Siegfried Sassoon, and Remarque exemplify the turn.
3. **1916 — Dada.** Founded in neutral Zürich in 1916 by the Romanian poet Tristan Tzara,
   spreading by war's end to Paris, Berlin, and New York. Dada elevated mockery of "stuffy
   bourgeois culture" to an art form — simultaneous poetry readings, a moustached Mona Lisa,
   Marcel Duchamp's urinal-as-"Fountain" (New York, 1917) — announcing the "necessary
   destruction" of the old world: "After the carnage, we keep only the hope of a purified
   humanity."
4. **1918–20 — Surrealism emerges from Dada.** André Breton, a medical orderly in a French
   psychiatric hospital, found in shell-shock patients evidence of the unconscious's power; a
   reader of Freud, he sought a literature liberated from "any control exercised by reason" and
   began "automatic writing" (*Les Champs Magnétiques*, 1920), rejecting "the reign of logic."
5. **Postwar — political radicalization.** Disillusioned avant-gardists moved toward
   anti-bourgeois politics of both extremes. Filippo Marinetti (Italian Futurism) aligned with
   Mussolini; Breton and Louis Aragon looked to the Bolshevik Revolution as the path to
   creative liberation.
   T. S. Eliot's "The Hollow Men" (1925) — "Not with a bang but a whimper" — became the
   canonical statement of postwar spiritual emptiness, though its private origins were
   personal rather than directly wartime.

## Geographic Spread

Centered in the major western-front belligerents (Britain, France, Germany) and radiating
through neutral hubs (Zürich) to New York; the postwar avant-garde network was pan-European
and transatlantic by the mid-1920s.

## Interaction

This process runs parallel to [[total-war]] and [[war-economy]] (the structural transformation
of state and economy) and to [[war-memory-and-mourning]] (the commemorative response of
ordinary bereaved families) but occupies a distinct register — elite intellectual and artistic
reckoning rather than mass ritual or state mobilization. It also intersects
[[political-modernism]] and [[modernism]]: the war did not create new artistic forms so much as
it made the prewar avant-garde's "criticisms, experiments, and mockeries" suddenly "much more
appropriate and even acceptable to ordinary people," increasing the currency of violence and
anger in the arts.

## End Conditions

The process does not end cleanly; it feeds directly into interwar modernism and the
politicization of the avant-garde in the 1920s–30s (Surrealism's flirtation with communism,
Futurism's absorption into Italian Fascism), and into the broader crisis of liberal-rationalist
confidence that the [[world-wars-era|World Wars Era]] as a whole represents.
