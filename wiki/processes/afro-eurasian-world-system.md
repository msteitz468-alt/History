## Definition and Scope

The **Afro-Eurasian world system** is the name given to the increasingly
interlinked network of long-distance exchange that, over the [[middle-millennium|
Middle Millennium]], bound the densely settled civilizations of the eastern
hemisphere — from the Atlantic coasts of Europe and North Africa to China and
Japan — into a single, if loose and uneven, economic system. It is the master
process around which [[kedar-cwh-v5-2015|CWH V]] is organized: the editors name the
**development of trade networks** as one of the three "regular trans-civilizational
interactions" (alongside [[state-formation|conquest-driven state formation]] and
[[middle-millennium|religious ecumenes]]) that integrated large-area units in this era.

The canonical reconstruction is **Janet Abu-Lughod's** *Before European Hegemony*
(1989): by c. 1250–1350 the Old World's regional circuits had fused into a single
system of roughly **eight interlocking circuits** — China to the Strait of Malacca;
India to Malacca; China to the Black Sea; Arabia to East Africa and West India; the
eastern Mediterranean to the Persian Gulf; Egypt to the Indian Ocean; the
trans-Mediterranean; and the West European subsystem — to which CWH V adds the
**trans-Saharan** circuit linking sub-Saharan Africa to the Maghreb. The system had
no single hegemon: it was polycentric, with several co-existing "engines."

**What it is not.** It is not a unified market or a single political order, and it
is not "globalization" in the modern sense (the Americas and Oceania lay outside
it). The editors are careful that, despite all these connections, in 1500
"economic exchange circuits played second fiddle to war and conquest" in connecting
populations — the system was real but thinner and more fragile than its modern
successor.

## Causal Drivers

- **The [[silk-roads|Silk Roads]] and Indian Ocean routes**, inherited and
  intensified from the classical era, carrying silk, ceramics, spices, slaves, and
  bullion.
- **Smith's three zone-types** (Ch 9): *engines* of production, consumption, and
  exchange (China and the Islamic heartland), *passageways* (Central Asia, the
  Southeast Asian sea lanes), and *cul-de-sacs* (western Europe, West Africa) that
  drew in goods but produced less for export.
- **The European [[commercial-revolution|commercial revolution]]** (Balard, Ch 10):
  credit instruments, bills of exchange, revived gold coinage, maritime republics,
  and the Champagne and Hanseatic networks — a "first globalization" tying the
  Mediterranean to the Baltic.
- **Islam as a commercial ecumene**, providing a shared legal-commercial framework,
  language, and protected-minority (Jewish, Christian) trading diasporas — e.g. the
  Radhanite merchants and the [[cairo-geniza|Geniza]] traders.
- **The Pax Mongolica** (mid-13th–14th c.), which lowered transaction costs across
  the entire breadth of the system and let a single silver stratum circulate from
  England to China.

## Major Phases

1. **Deep roots (500–1000).** Reconstitution of Eurasian exchange around the
   Abbasid, Tang, and Byzantine "engines" — the [[silk-roads|second Silk Roads era]]
   of [[early-middle-ages|the Early Middle Ages]].
2. **Thickening (1000–1250).** The commercial revolution in Europe, the Song
   economic boom, the Indian Ocean trade surge, and the trans-Saharan gold trade
   knit the circuits ever closer ([[high-middle-ages|High Middle Ages]]).
3. **Coalescence under the Pax Mongolica (1250–1350).** The circuits fuse into
   Abu-Lughod's single system at maximum extent — the period's high-water mark of
   integration.
4. **Shock and reconfiguration (1347–1500).** The **[[black-death|Black Death]]**
   travels the very webs that defined the system, and the Mongol order fragments;
   the system contracts and then reconstitutes around new gunpowder empires
   (Ottoman, Ming, Timurid) on the eve of European oceanic expansion.

## The Two Engines (Smith, Ch 9)

Richard Smith's chapter anatomizes the system's two great commercial **engines**,
which together "provided a good balance during the first half of the Middle
Millennium":

- **China.** A rich agricultural base, an innovative industrial sector, a vast
  internal market, and an infrastructure of canals and roads whose "commercial
  energy rippled through much of the rest of Eurasia." The Tang–Song population
  doubling and the **southward demographic shift** turned China's external trade from
  the overland [[silk-roads|Silk Roads]] toward **maritime** outlets into the Indian
  Ocean (Hangzhou the largest city on earth; Quanzhou and Guangzhou the busiest
  ports). The state regulated and taxed trade — and under the [[ming-dynasty|Ming]]
  channeled it through the **tribute system**, in which "gifts" exceeding the tribute
  confirmed China's centrality — even as [[confucianism|Confucian]] ideology
  disparaged merchants as parasites who held no political power.
- **The Islamic heartland.** A contiguous land–sea **passageway** from Persia across
  Iraq, Syria, and Egypt to al-Andalus — "custom-made for middlemen" at the
  intersection of the Eurasian and African landmasses, flanked by the Indian Ocean
  and the Mediterranean. Less populous and less agriculturally rich than China but
  far more commercially diversified and esteemed: the Prophet had been a merchant,
  Islam supplied a commercial law, a common language, and an international currency
  (the gold dinar), and [[baghdad|Baghdad]] became "for a while, the commercial
  center of Eurasia." Its chronic deficits — wood, minerals, and **manpower** — it
  met by importing [[slavery|slaves]] in a continuous flow.

Around these engines lay Smith's **passageways** (Central Asia, the Southeast Asian
sea lanes) and **cul-de-sacs** (western Europe, West Africa) — the latter capable,
as with [[commercial-revolution|Europe]], of growing into engines themselves.

## Geographic Spread

Anchored on the two great "engines" (China, the Islamic heartland), the system
radiated through Central Asian and maritime Southeast Asian passageways to the
European and West African peripheries, and down the East African coast to the
[[swahili-coast|Swahili]] cities and beyond. Its westernmost extension reached the
North Sea and Baltic; its African extension crossed the Sahara to the
[[western-sudan-states|Sahelian states]] and reached, via the Indian Ocean, the
Zanj coast.

## Interaction

The world system is the connective tissue linking nearly every other CWH V process
and actor: it carried the [[mongol-empire|Mongol]]-sponsored exchange of goods and
ideas, the transmission of [[science-transmission|science and technology]], the
spread of the [[middle-millennium|world religions]], and — fatefully — the
**[[black-death|Black Death]]**. It is the empirical content of the volume's
"expanding webs of exchange and conflict," and the direct ancestor of the
**proto-globalization** that Olstein (Ch 25) sees as the period's net outcome.

## End Conditions

The thirteenth/fourteenth-century system did not end so much as transform: plague
and Mongol fragmentation broke its specific configuration, but the underlying webs
persisted and were reorganized under the Ottoman, Timurid, and Ming states. By
1500, European Atlantic navigation began to extend the system across the oceans for
the first time, dissolving the Afro-Eurasian world system into the genuinely global
one of the [[early-modern|Early Modern]] era — the subject of [[bentley-cwh-v6|CWH VI]].
