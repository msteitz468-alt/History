## Definition and Scope

The "age of revolutions" — conventionally defined by the American and French Revolutions and the Atlantic revolutionary wave — was simultaneously a Pacific phenomenon of conflict, contestation, and radical reorganization of place and people. Sivasundaram argues that "it is necessary to recontextualize the 'age of revolutions', so often cast as a European phase of transition to modernity, as a Pacific concept of conflict and contestation" (ch. 44, [[hattori-samson-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v2-2023]]). The process unfolded in the decades straddling the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries and encompassed politics, culture, knowledge-making, environmental exploitation, and imperial violence.

The core transformation was the remaking of Epeli Hau'ofa's "Sea of Islands" — a space of dense Indigenous habitation, memory, and connection — into "islands in a far sea": discrete nodes ripe for colonization and extraction, hemmed in "by modern formations of empire, knowledge, and culture."

## Causal Drivers

**Anglo-French imperial rivalry.** Three French expeditions (La Pérouse 1785–8, d'Entrecasteaux 1791–4, Baudin 1800–3) each reflected different phases of France's revolutionary trajectory. Britain raised its flag on Tasmania directly after rumours that France intended to annex New South Wales. The fate of La Pérouse became an arena where national honour, humanitarian sentiment, and imperial surveillance were entangled.

**Atlantic revolutionary politics carried into Pacific space.** The crew of the St Patrick (1825) exemplified this: British sailors who had fought in Chile's war of independence under Thomas Cochrane, Polynesian labour recruited in earlier Pacific voyages, a Chilean-flagged ship commanded by an Irishman flying an Irish harp, and a Bengali steward. Dillon himself "was obsessed with aristocratic status and pedigree," yet was shaped by Napoleonic-era mobility. "The American Revolution released American traders from the East India Company's monopoly, making them flock to the Pacific in the age of revolutions."

**Indigenous strategic agency.** Māori rangatira (chiefs) such as Hongi Hika travelled to London to obtain muskets; the Māori visitors Brian Boroimbe and Morgan McMurroch journeyed to Calcutta — "to try their fortunes in obtaining Muskets and Gunpowder from the Merchants of Calcutta." They appropriated new monarchical vocabularies while pursuing pre-existing political objectives. Brian observed acutely to Dillon at Budge Inn: "you will come and take my country too, I have no doubt, as you have taken this."

**Extractive commercial economy.** The Bass Strait sealing economy from 1810 created polyglot communities ("English, Irish, American, Portuguese, 'lascar', New Zealand, Tahitian, and Australian Aboriginal members") operating in the interstices of imperial order.

## Major Phases

### Phase 1: Elite Exploration and Imperial Rivalry (1785–1800)
French and British scientific voyages competed to map, claim, and know the Pacific. La Pérouse was positioned as "France's response to Cook." D'Entrecasteaux's search expedition was wrecked by European revolutionary politics from within: "Royalist and republican commitments divided the crew"; the expedition's papers were seized at St Helena. Baudin's expedition (1800–3) operated under Napoleonic instruction.

### Phase 2: Commercial Penetration and Multicultural Crew Networks (1800–1830)
Private traders, sealers, and whalers proliferated. The seas "were becoming populated in new ways as Europeans were arriving in greater numbers." Calcutta became a hub connecting the Pacific to India: the sword guard from Vanikoro was examined by the Royal Asiatic Society, Māori visitors were received by the Acting British Governor-General, and Indian newspapers reported on Polynesian travellers. This phase shows "how the Pacific was being located within global circuits of migration, exchange, and knowledge."

### Phase 3: Colonial Consolidation and Aboriginal War in Tasmania (1817–1838)
Settlement of Van Diemen's Land intensified as retired Napoleonic War officers and gentry arrived. The colonial population increased sixfold from 1817 to 1824, transforming the island "from 'a creole society based on small-scale agriculture, whaling and sealing to a largely pastoral economy based on the production of fine wool.'" Aboriginal resistance escalated. Martial law (1828–31), the "Black Line" of 1830 (a human chain of troops, convicts, and settlers modelled partly on "imperial hunts" and Peninsular War tactics), and eventual transportation of Aboriginal Tasmanians to the Bass Strait reserve under George Augustus Robinson produced their near-annihilation in settler demographic terms.

## Geographic Spread

The process connected nodes in a vast network: Van Diemen's Land / Tasmania; the Bass Strait sealing islands; Sydney (Port Jackson); Tikopia and Vanikoro (Southwest Pacific); Tahiti; Bay of Islands (New Zealand/Aotearoa); Valparaiso and the Pacific coast of South America; Calcutta; and London. Dillon's St Patrick traced a single itinerary from Valparaiso via Tikopia, New Zealand, Tahiti, and the East Indies to Calcutta, illustrating how "Calcutta was becoming connected with the Pacific."

## End Conditions

The process transitions into formal colonial rule from the mid-nineteenth century, when most Pacific island groups were annexed by European powers or the United States. The transformation from a "Sea of Islands" to "islands in a far sea" was not complete but was structurally established. Islander agency continued within colonial frameworks — new monarchical institutions, Christianity, literacy — but the strategic openness of the revolutionary age narrowed.

## Historiography

Sivasundaram situates this chapter within the broader project of decentring European history: "Bringing it to the centre of this period is to argue that places far from Europe could inflect and change the politics of this moment. It also challenges one of the most Eurocentric terms of periodization in historiography." His methodological approach combines microhistory (the Dillon voyage as a case study traversing multiple scales) with structural argument about global transformation. The chapter draws on Hau'ofa's "Sea of Islands" framing (1994) as a counterpoint to the colonial imagination that reduced the Pacific to isolated specks.
