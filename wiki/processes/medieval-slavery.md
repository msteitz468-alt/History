# Medieval Slavery (c. 500–1420)

## Definition and Scope

The history of slavery across the **"medieval millennium"** — the long span between the fall of
Rome and the rise of the Atlantic [[plantation-economy|plantation complex]] that the
[[cambridge-world-history-slavery-v2|Cambridge World History of Slavery, Vol. 2]] sets out to
recover from neglect. It is **not** a single system but a global patchwork: the post-Roman
Mediterranean, the Islamic world from the Atlantic to Central Asia, Byzantium, Carolingian and
later Latin Europe, Scandinavia and the British Isles, China and Korea, South India, sub-Saharan
Africa, and precontact America all practised forms of slavery and slaving.

The volume's editors deliberately set aside the rigid [[slave-society]] binary in favour of a
**continuum of dependency and coerced labour**. Slavery in this period rarely organised central
production (the great exception being some Islamic plantation and mining regimes); far more often
it was **domestic, sexual, military, and administrative**. The decisive medieval fact is that
**slaving became bound up with religion**: Abrahamic states (Christian and Muslim alike) treated
the enslavement of co-religionists as illegitimate and the enslavement of outsiders — pagans,
"infidels," the peoples of the frontier — as licit, so the trade ran along confessional fault
lines.

## Causal Drivers

- **Religious frontier-making.** As Christianity and Islam expanded, the licit supply of slaves
  was pushed to the **pagan peripheries**: the Slavic and Baltic lands, the Eurasian steppe,
  sub-Saharan Africa, the Caucasus. The very word **"slave"** derives from *Slav* (Latin
  *sclavus*, Arabic *saqaliba*) — a measure of how completely the early-medieval European trade
  ran on captives from the still-pagan Slavic east.
- **Long-distance trade networks.** The [[trans-saharan-trade|trans-Saharan]] routes (long
  predating Islam), the western **Indian Ocean** world, and the **Mediterranean–Black Sea**
  circuit moved enslaved people across the barriers — desert, sea, steppe — that made them
  controllable. In the later medieval centuries **Genoese and Venetian** merchants ran the
  western end of a transcontinental system whose eastern counterparts operated in the steppe.
- **Imperial demand for elite slaves.** States deliberately bred a dependence on the "socially
  dead": deracinated slaves with no kin-ties made trustworthy soldiers and officials. This drove
  a specialised long-distance trade in young men and women for **military slavery** (see below).
- **The Mongol eruption.** The [[mongol-empire|Mongol]] conquests generated forced migration and
  enslavement on a vast scale and fed captives into the Black Sea and Central Asian markets.

## Major Phases

1. **Post-Roman transition (c. 500–800).** Mediterranean chattel slavery persists but contracts;
   in the Carolingian world and Byzantium slaves remain significant; the saqaliba trade carries
   Slavic captives south into al-Andalus and the Islamic Mediterranean.
2. **The Islamic high tide (c. 750–1250).** The Abbasid world is the great slaving hub —
   concubinage, domestic service, the Zanj agricultural slavery of southern Iraq (and its great
   revolt, 869–883), and the rise of **military slavery**.
3. **Military-slave states (c. 833–1517).** Turkic slave soldiers become the backbone, then the
   masters, of Islamic states: the Abbasid *ghilman*, the **Delhi Sultanate's** slave armies,
   and above all the **[[mamluk-sultanate|Mamluk Sultanate]]** of Egypt (1250–1517), a regime
   literally ruled by manumitted military slaves and replenished from the Black Sea.
4. **The decline of chattel slavery in NW Europe (c. 1100–1400).** In Latin Christendom north of
   the Alps, chattel slavery gives way to **[[feudalism|serfdom]]** — the unfree become bound to
   the land rather than saleable as persons — even as slaving intensifies in the Mediterranean
   and on the Atlantic islands on the eve of the Atlantic trade.

## Military Slavery — the distinctive medieval institution

The medieval period's signature contribution was **institutionalised military slavery**:
recruiting elite soldiers and administrators precisely because, as natally alienated outsiders,
they had no rival kin-network and so were bound to the ruler alone. Its forms ran from the
Abbasid *ghilman* through the **mamluk** system to the Ottoman **devshirme** (the levy of
Christian boys for the Janissary corps and the palace bureaucracy, straddling this volume and the
early-modern Ottoman state). This inverts the New World image of the slave as a degraded field
hand: here the slave could be a general or a vizier. See [[slavery]] on the paradox of the
"socially dead" as trusted instruments of power.

## Geographic Spread

The volume's reach is global: **western Indian Ocean** (East African, Ethiopian, and South Asian
captives moving to Arabia, the Gulf, and India); **China and Korea** (penal and domestic slavery
within societies-with-slaves); **South India** (graded agrarian bondage, *pannaiyal*);
**sub-Saharan Africa** (archaeological evidence for slaving well before the Atlantic trade);
**Scandinavia and the British Isles** (the Viking thrall trade); **Iberia** (the
Christian–Muslim frontier as a slaving zone); and **precontact America** (captive-taking among
Mesoamerican and other societies).

## Interaction

Medieval slavery is entangled with [[trans-saharan-trade]], [[indian-ocean-trade]], the
[[mongol-empire]], the rise of [[feudalism|serfdom]], and the religious geography of
[[spread-of-islam|Islam]] and Latin Christendom. Crucially, it forms the **bridge** between
[[slavery|ancient slavery]] and the [[atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic system]]: the legal
categories, the Mediterranean sugar-and-slave plantations of Cyprus, Crete, and the Atlantic
islands, and the Iberian slaving experience were all inherited directly by the New World.

## End Conditions

The period does not end slavery — it transforms its geography. In north-west Europe chattel
slavery had largely dissolved into serfdom by 1400; in the Mediterranean and Atlantic islands,
sugar plantations worked by enslaved Africans were already prefiguring the
[[atlantic-slave-trade|transatlantic complex]] that Vol. 3 of the series takes up after 1420.

## Historiography

The medieval millennium has been the **least-studied** span in the history of slavery, partly
because the [[slave-society]] model (built on Greece, Rome, and the Atlantic) marginalised
periods where slavery was real but not economically central. Recent scholarship (Vlassopoulos;
Joseph Miller's *The Problem of Slavery as History*; the "slaving zones" framework of Fynn-Paul)
reframes it through a **global, continuum-based** approach that treats slaving as a process
rather than slavery as a status, and recovers the agency and experience of the enslaved against
sources written almost entirely by slaveholders and traders.
