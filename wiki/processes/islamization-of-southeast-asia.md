# The Islamization of Southeast Asia

Anthony Reid ([[reid-southeast-asia-age-of-commerce-v2-1993|Vol. 2]], Ch. III) argues that the
[[southeast-asian-age-of-commerce|age of commerce]] drove a **religious revolution**: **more than half**
the population of Southeast Asia adopted one of the scriptural "**religions of the book**" — Islam in the
archipelago, Christianity in the Philippines and pockets of the east and Vietnam — while the mainland
deepened its commitment to a reformed **Theravada Buddhism** and Vietnam to **Confucianism**. Reid treats
these as a **connected phenomenon**, "not seriously examined as a whole": all were **scriptural
orthodoxies validated by an authority outside the local society**, all stressed **personal morality**,
and all claimed **universal validity** — displacing the diverse, oral, pragmatic local
[[animism|animism]] Reid calls "Southeast Asian religion." This page covers the Islamization of the
archipelago (the wiki's first dedicated treatment) within that wider frame.

## From quarantine to conversion

Indian [[hinduism|Hindu]] and [[buddhism|Buddhist]] ideas had spread for over a millennium (see
[[indianization-of-southeast-asia]]) without demanding exclusivity — they "added rituals and deities"
without requiring the renunciation of anything. Muslim, Christian, Jewish, and Chinese-religious traders
were at first in a **"quarantine" stage** (a term from African historiography): tolerated commercial
minorities, neither converting nor converted. Islam built substantial port communities in north Sumatra,
east Java, Champa, and east Malaya in the late thirteenth–fourteenth centuries; the first Muslim state,
**Pasai**, dates from c. **1297**. The decisive shift came **1400–1650**, when [[melaka|Melaka]] became
both Muslim and the greatest port of the region (early fifteenth century) and seeded Islam along the
spice route to Java and Maluku and the trade route to Brunei and Manila.

## The high tide: the silver boom of 1570–1630

The most intense phase of **both** Islamization and Christianization coincided with the commercial high
tide — the **silver boom of 1570–1630**. Key turning points: the Portuguese onslaught on Melaka (1511)
made no Christian converts but **consolidated militant Muslim dynasties** ([[aceh-sultanate|Aceh]] and
Banten, founded as anti-Portuguese centres in the 1520s; [[majapahit|Hindu Majapahit]] fell to Muslim
arms c. 1527); the Spanish capture of Manila (1571) was followed by the conversion of virtually all
lowland Luzon by 1620 and the Visayas by 1650 — "unprecedented in Christian missionary history." Islam
was adopted by the rulers of **Mataram**, **South Sulawesi (Makassar, 1603–12)**, Buton, Lombok,
Sumbawa, Magindanao, and southern Borneo, while established sultanates (Aceh, Johor, Patani, Banten,
Ternate) extended Islamic conformity into their hinterlands. **Christian–Muslim rivalry**, sharpened by
direct contact with **Mecca and the Ottoman caliphate**, redefined states as members of an international
brotherhood and pressured all who traded with either side to declare a stance.

## Two missionary strategies

Reid contrasts the methods of the two faiths:

- **Islam** spread through merchants and Sufi teachers who **married locally** and were often local
  themselves — culturally accommodating of the existing spiritual landscape. But its outward markers were
  demanding: the **renunciation of pork** (a central food and feast element — domestic pigs were often
  slaughtered in one final feast to mark conversion) and **circumcision**. Islamization was so total a
  change of food, dress, and custom that it was frequently experienced as a **change of ethnic status** —
  in the Malay world, "becoming Malay" (*masuk Melayu*) and "becoming Muslim" fused.
- **Christianity** relied on a **celibate, disciplined European priesthood** defining the community
  through baptism and catechism. Because Iberian priests could not marry locally and made little use of
  Asian clergy before 1700, Christian boundaries were drawn in **Iberian terms**, and communities lacking
  a resident priest were simply lost. Catholic proselytists took a **tougher stance** on destroying the
  pagan past — Magellan made the Cebu elite burn their idols; the Jesuits in Maluku sought out and
  destroyed "diabolical cults," a high-risk policy that bred fear.

Both required, at minimum, the **selective renunciation** of the old religion — the line between true
*conversion* and mere passive *adhesion* (following one's ruler in nominal membership). Both also
produced a **literary flowering**: a Malay-language Islamic scholarship (Hamzah Fansuri, Syamsuddin,
ar-Raniri, Abdurrauf of Singkil) strikingly contemporary with Christian devotional literature in Malay,
Tagalog, and Vietnamese.

## The mainland: Theravada reform and Confucianism

On the mainland, **Theravada Buddhism** had been accepted by the major states before 1400, so the change
was less dramatic, but between 1400 and 1650 the **sangha** (monastic order) was reformed into a unified
body under royal control and made a popular spiritual force outweighing the local spirit cults. In
**Vietnam**, the Le emperor Le Thanh Tong (r. 1460–97) established **Confucianism** as state ideology via
the examination system — though, as the Jesuits found, less securely rooted than in China, which is why
Alexandre de Rhodes converted Vietnamese (190,000 baptisms c. 1590–1645) more easily than his colleagues
converted the Chinese.

## Significance and links

The religious revolution gave maritime Southeast Asia its enduring religious map — a **Muslim archipelago**
and a **Buddhist/Confucian mainland** — and bound the region into the global communities of Islam and
Christendom. It is inseparable from the [[southeast-asian-age-of-commerce|commercial boom]]: the same
trade routes, cosmopolitan cities, and universalising connections carried the scriptural faiths. It runs
parallel to the [[islamization-of-africa|Islamization of Africa]] (Reid borrows the African "quarantine"
model) and completes the religious story the [[indianization-of-southeast-asia|Indianization]] page began.

## Historiography

Against **van Leur**, who saw early Islamization as mere "adhesion" adding the *shahada* to old practice,
Reid stresses that as prophetic religions Islam and Christianity *did* demand boundary-crossing
renunciation. The deeper debate concerns **why** conversion happened when it did: explanations range from
the appeal of scriptural **universalism** to a cosmopolitan commercial society, to the **political/communal
identity** it conferred in an age of Christian–Muslim confrontation, to ruler-led adoption for trade
advantage. The relative weight of merchant diasporas, Sufi orders, and royal initiative in the
archipelago's conversion remains contested, and the sources are far richer for Christianization (Spanish
records) than for the more profound move to Islam.
