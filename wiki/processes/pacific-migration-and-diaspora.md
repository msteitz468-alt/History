## Definition and Scope

Pacific migration and diaspora encompasses the accelerating movement of Pacific Islanders — voluntary migrants, assisted migrants, exiles, and their descendants — between Pacific Islands, within Pacific Rim cities, and beyond. As [[denoon-cambridge-pacific-islanders-1997]] frames the phenomenon: "Including indigenous peoples, over a million Islanders live and work in New Zealand, the United States and Australia." Auckland is described as "the largest Polynesian city, home to over 103,000 Islanders and over 104,000 Maori, together comprising more than 22 per cent of Auckland's population."

The process is both very old — "the histories of Islanders are histories of movement — into the Pacific to settle; sailing between islands to maintain links; marrying, trading and warring" — and importantly new in its scale, legal structure, and consequences for home communities.

## Major Migration Streams and Nodes

**New Zealand:** Largest host population proportionally. In 1991 the Islander population was 167,073, with 60.2 per cent growth in the decade. "Islanders form the fastest-growing component of New Zealand's population, due to their fertility rate and numbers of immigrants (50.8 per cent of the total). Samoans form the majority of this community, at 85,743." Niue, Tokelau, and Cook Islands maintain especially high proportions abroad: 85, 70, and 69 per cent of their total populations reside in New Zealand.

**United States:** Second most important destination. The 1990 census counted 154,010 Pacific Islanders plus 211,014 Hawaiians. Samoans are the largest group (62,964). Compacts of Free Association (FSM, Marshall Islands, post-1994 Palau) created free entry; Guamanians (since 1950) and American Samoans (since 1951) had earlier access. Guam has served as an intermediate node for movement into the continental US.

**Australia:** Approximately 65,000 with at least one Islander ancestor (1986 data). New Zealand Maori (26,000) were 40 per cent of Islanders in Australia, largely invisible in statistics because they entered through the Trans-Tasman Agreement rather than Pacific Islander immigration channels.

**Step-migration and network nodes:** The source describes well-worn routes: "from Lamotrek to Ulithi, Yap and Guam ... expanded today to include Honolulu and Los Angeles. A well-worn north-eastern route runs from Apia and Tongatapu to Pago Pago and on to Honolulu, San Diego and Salt Lake City: a southern route runs to Apia, Auckland and Sydney." American Samoa functions as a stepping-stone to the US for Western Samoans and Tongans.

## Exiles and Assisted Migrants

The source distinguishes voluntary migrants from "relocated communities" — those moved at the instigation of colonial administrations. Key cases:
- **Bikinians:** Evacuated from Bikini Atoll for nuclear testing; resettled to Rongerik, Kwajalein, Kili; later Majuro. "Bikinians wanted the United States administration to take responsibility for them, thereby establishing dependence upon a wealthy, powerful, but largely absent foreign government."
- **Banabans:** Resettled from Banaba to Rabi Island, Fiji (1945), having been relocated to Tarawa, Nauru, and Kosrae during Japanese occupation. They "strategically moved closer to the British High Commissioner ... so that they could state their grievances to him, and claim proper compensation for phosphate mining." By 1985 the Banaban community on Rabi numbered 4,064 (up from 1,003 in 1945).
- **I-Kiribati and Phoenix Islands settlers:** Colonial resettlement experiments in the Phoenix and Line Islands, complicated by drought, land allocation failures, and ultimately the need for further resettlement to the western Solomons.
- **Carolinians relocated to Saipan** by Germans after typhoon devastation, in process providing colonial labour.

## The MIRAB Debate

The MIRAB model (Migration, Remittances, Aid, Bureaucracy), coined to describe Kiribati, Tuvalu, Cook Islands, Niue, and Tokelau, characterises economies where "a sustainable economy is unachievable — a mirage — and is replaced by development through international largesse." Variants include MURAB (adding urban dimension) and MIRAGE.

Hau'ofa rejects the dependency framing: "To label this 'dependency' is belittling, and masks relationships of dominance." The model ignores "the dependency of the host community on inexpensive imported labourers, for whom it need not pay the costs of schooling and retirement."

However, the economic stress of remittance-based living is real: in New Zealand in 1991 "80 per cent of the employed Islanders earned less than $20,000 per annum, compared to 64 per cent for the general population. Islanders were twice as likely to be unemployed."

## Health

Migration carries documented health costs. The Tokelau Islands Migrant Study (1968–1986) found that "the incidence and prevalence of a number of chronic diseases — such as diabetes, gout, chronic lung disease, asthma, hypertension, and possibly coronary heart disease — were higher among the migrants than among the non-migrants." Obesity, dietary change, and stress were factors. Yet "86 per cent [of migrants] reported that their life in New Zealand was 'better' than it had been in Tokelau," citing "improved living standards, more independence, and better educational and cultural opportunities."

## Demographic Stress on Home Communities

Heavy emigration produces demographic skews: the source notes that "the 15-29 age group moves first, seeking higher education and employment." On Niue, 85 per cent of the population (14,500) live in New Zealand; fewer than 2,500 remain on the island, with over 8 per cent in the "extreme elderly" category. "Who is the constituency of the Niuean government: only those residing on the island, or the entire population — and are the interests of the two populations compatible?"

## Gender Patterns

Migration patterns are gendered. Palauan women migrating to Guam "outnumber men and have a higher school completion rate than Palauan men." I-Kiribati women moving to the Marshalls appear to use female out-marriage as a migration strategy. Senior Marshallese women move to California to run households for younger immigrants. Chuukese migration (post-Compact) was initially predominantly male — consistent with Carolinian atolls where "men were expected to travel and often married outside the home atoll, and women ideally stayed home to protect continued matrilineal land rights" — but over two decades "expectations and migration patterns have been transformed."

## Identity and Citizenship Questions

Migration raises fundamental questions about who counts as "Pacific Islander" and what rights attach to that identity. "Racial purity may be a more Western than Pacific manner of conceptualising membership." Fiji's successive citizenship definitions changed the status of inter-island Pacific Islander families. Palau's national constitution restricts citizenship to those tracing Palauan heritage through at least one parent — adopted to "forestall the processes whereby the Chamorros of Guam have become a minority on their own island." Until 1990 "all Pacific Islanders were amalgamated with 'Asians' in a composite category of Asia-Pacific Islander" in the US census.

## Historiography

[[denoon-cambridge-pacific-islanders-1997]] synthesises a large body of migration research (Connell, McCall, Bathgate, Lieber, Marcus) and casts it within the sea-of-islands paradigm. The source is critical of both dependency-theory frameworks (which are seen as belittling Islander agency) and naive sovereignty rhetoric (which obscures real economic constraints). The collection is strongest on Polynesian and Micronesian migration; Melanesian internal migration and urbanisation receive briefer treatment.

## Fischer (2002): "contemporary voyaging" and the MIRAB Pacific

[[fischer-history-pacific-islands-2002|Fischer (2002)]] frames late-twentieth-century out-migration as "contemporary voyaging" reconnecting peoples "separated by nineteenth-century colonialism" (L9103–9105). **Auckland is "now the Polynesian capital of the world"** (L9121–9126); large shares of small-island nations live abroad — Cook Islanders 69%, Tokelauans 70%, Niueans 85% (L9128–9133) — and Samoa "remains the hub of Polynesian migrations as in prehistory," with nearly half of both Samoas' populations now overseas (L9161–9172). The 1980–90 US Islander growth rate (41.5%) dwarfed the national 9.8% (L9134–9141). Fischer distinguishes Melanesia's largely *internal* urbanisation from Polynesian/Micronesian *external* migration (L9197–9209), and flags structural strains: nearly a third of Tuvaluan and Kiribati men work abroad as seamen (L9219–9224), Niue faces possible abandonment like a Mystery Island (~1,480 residents) (L9229–9238), and Tongan remittances exceeded exports and tourism combined (L9553–9557). "More than one in nine Pacific Islanders now lives abroad" (L9312–9314).
