## Definition and Scope

"Trading nature" designates the transportation and introduction of biota — plants, animals, fungi, pathogens, and vermin — that accompanied human settlement, migration, and colonization of the island Pacific and continental coastlines across the longue durée of human habitation. "The 'trading' of nature transpired over the longue durée of human habitation, making possible the migration of Indigenous groups since ancient times and sharply escalating with the appearance of Europeans prior to 1800" (Igler, ch. 47, [[hattori-samson-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v2-2023]]).

The process spans from initial Polynesian settlement of Remote Oceania through the European imperial period. J.R. McNeill's framework ("Of Rats and Men," 1994) provides the organizing temporal structure: the "ages" of the outrigger, the sailing ship, and the steamship, observing that the island Pacific "exhibits eras of calm interrupted by spurts of torrential change" aligned to new forms of transport. Scholars have used multiple labels: "ecological revolutions," "the Columbian/Magellan Exchange," "green imperialism," "tending the wild."

## Causal Drivers

**Indigenous "tending the wild."** Native peoples throughout the Pacific transformed nature into food and tools through intentional management: the Chumash of the California Channel Islands practiced controlled communal burns (2.4–5.25 million hectares annually) to "tend the wild"; Polynesian settlers cleared forests, introduced fire, and managed landscapes as "landscape enhancement" to support human populations. "'Sustaining human life in Remote Oceania,' Matthew Spriggs writes of the earliest settlers, 'may well have been impossible' without the radical disturbance caused by introduced species, extensive clearing of forests, and intensive agricultural systems."

**Portmanteau biota in Polynesian voyaging.** Each wave of Pacific settlement carried a standard suite of companion species: dogs, chickens, pigs, and rats (fauna), plus breadfruit, bananas, taro, yams, and pandanus (flora). "Canoe plants" — a term denoting the widespread transfer of biota by Islanders — were intentionally loaded for future sustenance. The Polynesian rat (*Rattus exulans*), carried intentionally or not, was a critical co-traveller.

**European "improvement" ideology.** "Natural exploitation — and in particular the period's ideology of 'improvement' — drove Tasmania's colonization." George Forster articulated the logic in 1777: "I cannot help thinking that our late voyage would reflect immortal honour to our employers, if it had no other merit than stocking Taheitee with goats, the Friendly isles and New Hebrides with dogs, and New Zealand and New Caledonia with hogs." European voyagers introduced species without Islander consent — at Huahine, Cook ordered sailors to plant gardens and land goats, chickens, rabbits, a horse, a mare, and sheep, all "under the gaze of armed marines."

## Major Phases

### Phase 1: The Outrigger Age — Indigenous Settlement of Oceania (c. 4,000 BP–1500 CE)
Ancient Lapita cultures spread eastward from Near Oceania into Tonga and Sāmoa c. 2,000 years ago. Through successive chains of migration, settlers brought fauna and flora to each new island. Every settlement involved trade-offs: "human colonization across forty-one Pacific islands caused the global extinction of nearly 1,000 species of birds" (Lewis). The Stumbling Moa-nalo (*Ptaiochen pau*) in Hawai'i — large, flightless, and edible — disappeared rapidly after human arrival. The Polynesian rat, wherever it was introduced, expanded rapidly and caused substantial deforestation and lowland bird extinction. On Rapa Nui (Easter Island, settled c. 1200 CE), rat-induced deforestation was "complete, or nearly complete" by the late 18th century; Diamond's "Easter Island collapse" narrative is rebutted by Hunt and Lipo, who show the human population had not collapsed before European contact — it was introduced disease that initiated steep decline.

### Phase 2: The Sailing Ship Age — European Commercial and Scientific Voyaging (1767–c. 1870)
Cook, Wallis, and Bougainville each arrived at Tahiti within three years (1767–9). Tahiti received "cattle, donkeys, goats, guinea pigs, horses, rabbits, sheep, new varieties of dogs and pigs, and a pregnant cat gifted to 'Queen' Purea by Samuel Wallis in 1767" plus dozens of bird species and plant varieties. Some invasive species had "evident impact"; others were "relatively harmless additions." "Tahitians . . . maintained 'much more of a controlling hand [over the natural production] than the voyagers found comfortable'" (Newell), but could not control viral and bacterial agents.

**Breadfruit transplantation** was the era's emblematic imperial ecological project. Joseph Banks, Cook's naturalist and later director of English government-sponsored science, organised Captain Bligh's mission to bring Tahitian breadfruit (*Artocarpus altilis*) to Britain's Caribbean slave islands. The *Bounty* mutiny (1789) delayed but did not prevent success: Bligh's *Providence* arrived in Matavai Bay in April 1792 and eventually delivered 780 large pots and 3,012 small pots of breadfruit to St Vincent and Jamaica. The enslaved Africans intended as beneficiaries "cared little for the new addition to their diet." Bligh's tombstone memorialized him as "the celebrated navigator who first transplanted the Bread fruit tree from Otaheite to the West Indies" — no mention of the mutiny.

George Vancouver introduced cattle to Hawai'i in 1793 (one bull, one cow, both barely alive). By 1816 when Ludwig Choris painted Honolulu, cattle and horses were conspicuous in the agricultural landscape. John Ryan Fischer's "cattle colonialism" thesis shows these animals "severely disrupted the subsistence patterns of Indigenous populations in both Hawai'i and California, and pastoral culture contributed to new systems of land tenure introduced in the 1850s."

**Diseased goods.** Captain Cook recognized the risk from sexually transmitted disease in his crew but acknowledged "the very thing happened that I had above all others wished to prevent." On return to Hawai'i the following year, "the Islanders confronted Cook with corporeal evidence of the new disease among them." Disease was part of the traded biota.

**Islander resistance** to ecological imposition: "The arrival of a foreign vessel could inspire well-warranted alarm from a local group, who sometimes 'warned off' vessels from approaching shore" from Cook's era through the 1840s US Exploring Expedition. Islander refusals were assertions of sovereignty over land and rejection of introduced biota.

### Phase 3: The Steamship Age — Australia and the Continental Pacific Rim (1850–1900)
Australia from the 1850s gold rush onwards experienced a cascade of introductions: red fox, feral cat, feral pig and donkey, water buffalo, camel, European rabbit. The rabbit multiplied into the tens of millions and overran coastal and interior regions. Prickly pear from South America — introduced for fencing and cochineal dye — covered 23 million hectares of Western Australia by 1920. Control came only via biological control (the American moth *Cactorblastis cactorum* from the 1920s). Australian gold-rush migrants carried Eucalyptus seeds to California; by the end of the 1850s the largest San Francisco nursery offered the saplings for sale — "botanical xenophobia" later contested their status as "native."

The Russian empire's assault on the North Pacific — documented in Ryan Tucker Jones's *Empire of Extinction* — drove the Steller's sea cow (*Rhytina borealis*) to extinction "on an unrecorded day in the 1760s."

## End Conditions

The process does not end before 1900 — it accelerates into the steamship age and beyond. By 1900, the island Pacific had been structurally transformed: endemic bird species largely gone, introduced mammals established, agricultural monocultures displacing forest, disease-weakened populations under colonial rule. The "high rate of species extinction in the Pacific represents a logical, if unforeseen, culmination of trading nature over the past millennia."
