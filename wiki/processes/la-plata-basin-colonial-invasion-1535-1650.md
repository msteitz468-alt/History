## Definition and Scope

The La Plata Basin colonial invasion (1535–1650) was the process by which Spanish (and, on the Brazilian frontier, Portuguese) power penetrated the vast river system draining present-day Paraguay, Argentina (Tucumán, Cuyo, Córdoba), and the Río de la Plata delta. Unlike the conquests of central Mexico or the Andes, the invasion did not involve a "state takeover of an existing state" but rather "a long and arduous campaign to 'pacify the territory' and build a labor-exploiting system amid native societies of drastically unfamiliar constitution" (Garavaglia, Ch. 14). The indigenous societies of the region — Guaraní, Diaguita, Comechingones, Sanavirones, Huarpes, and numerous Chaco groups — lacked the tributary or semi-tributary surplus systems that Europeans treated as the essence of politics, which gave the region its colonial label of "marginal." Three sub-regions carry distinct trajectories: Paraguay and the Guaraní heartland; Tucumán and the Andean margins (northwestern Argentina); and Cuyo (present-day Mendoza, San Juan, San Luis).

## Causal Drivers

- **Guaraní agricultural surplus.** Two annual corn harvests and a developed slash-and-burn system made the Guaraní zone uniquely attractive. "Spaniards preferred to settle near Guaraní territory to take advantage of this abundance."
- **Silver-mine demand at Potosí.** The amalgam-process boom after c. 1570 expanded Potosí's appetite for food, mules, textiles, and labor. The entire Tucumán corridor — Santiago del Estero, Córdoba, Salta, Jujuy — was organized around supplying this demand. See [[mita]] and the broader [[andes]] supply network.
- **Overflow of Peruvian conquistadores.** The closure of Peru's undistributed encomiendas after the 1530s–1540s generated restless soldiers whom colonial authorities pushed into the La Plata hinterlands; the conquest chronology thus lags 15–20 years behind Peru.
- **Bandeirante slave raiding.** Portuguese mixed-race expeditions out of São Paulo destroyed dozens of Spanish and Jesuit settlements across Guayrá and Tape (active from c. 1600 to 1676), fundamentally reshaping indigenous distribution and Spanish defensive strategy.
- **Indigenous labor scarcity.** Because native societies lacked internal tribute hierarchies, Spaniards could not simply commandeer existing surplus flows; they had to construct new coercive institutions from scratch, producing a heavily personal-service encomienda distinct from the Mexican or Andean variants.

## Major Phases

**Phase 1 — First contacts and founding of Asunción (1516–1537).** Juan Díaz de Solís reached the Río de la Plata in 1516 and was killed by natives. Sebastián Cabot established Fort Sancti Spiritus (1527), destroyed two years later. Pedro de Mendoza's large expedition (c. 1,200 men, 1535) attempted Buenos Aires; a native siege forced survivors upriver, leading to the founding of [[asuncion]] in 1537 next to the Cario-Guaraní hamlet of Lambaré.

**Phase 2 — Alliance, cuñadazgo, and first exploitation (1537–1555).** The Carios of Lambaré, defeating their Chaqueno enemies with Spanish help, handed over women as alliance tokens. Spaniards accumulated women as labor units, triggering the [[paraguay-mestizaje]] process. The Aracaré episode (1542) exposed the fundamental asymmetry: when the Guaraní chief refused to serve a Spanish expedition, Governor Cabeza de Vaca had him hanged; the ensuing uprisings showed that Spaniards "were no longer acting like allies but like 'masters' of the Guaraní." By 1547 most Guaraní groups were in open rebellion.

**Phase 3 — Encomienda institutionalization (1555–1597).** Governor Martínez de Irala granted the first 320 encomiendas in 1555, affecting 20,000–27,000 Indians. Two labour categories emerged: *mitayos* (rotating village service) and *originarios/yanaconas* (permanent household or farm bondage). The boundary between these blurred immediately, as "yanaconization" of mitayos began almost at once. Franciscan reducciones around Asunción (from 1580) consolidated colonial control while partially blunting encomiendero violence. See [[reduccion]].

**Phase 4 — Yerba mate expansion, bandeirante raids, and Jesuit reducciones (1597–1650).** Yerba mate exports to Andean markets intensified labour demands through *beneficios yerbateros* (forced harvest drafts) and *mandamientos* (state labour assignments). Simultaneously, Portuguese bandeirante raids destroyed Ciudad Real del Guayrá, Villa Rica, and dozens of Jesuit missions, forcing a massive westward displacement of Guaraní populations. The Jesuit reductions, begun from 1610 under Governor Hernandarias, carved out a defensive zone between Spanish encomiendero demands and Portuguese slave raiders. Francisco de Alfaro's inspection (1611) attempted to abolish personal service but was effectively nullified within two years.

**Phase 5 — Tucumán and Cuyo conquest (1553–c. 1660).** Santiago del Estero was founded in 1553 as the "mother city" of Tucumán. An encomienda regime of personal service (mita and yanaconazgo) linked Diaguita and other northwest Argentine communities to Potosí markets via textile tribute and mule-team transport. The Calchaquí valleys resisted continuously: the first great uprising (1560–1563) under kuraka Juan Calchaquí deterred settlement for two decades; the "great rebellion" of 1630–1643 cleared Spaniards from the valleys; only the final suppression (1656–1664) definitively broke resistance, at the cost of forcibly displacing and dispersing the Quilmes and other peoples.

## Geographic Spread

The process extended along the Paraná-Paraguay river system from the Río de la Plata delta northward into Guayrá (now southern Brazil) and westward toward the Andean foothills, encompassing:

- **Paraguay lowlands**: the Guaraní heartland, most intensely transformed
- **Guayrá and Tape**: destroyed by bandeirantes by 1632–1676
- **Tucumán valleys**: Calchaquí, Santa María, Humahuaca corridor; linked via mule road to Potosí
- **Santiago del Estero plains**: multiethnic, textile-producing encomienda communities
- **Córdoba ranges**: dispersed Comechingón and Sanavirón communities rapidly absorbed into ranch and farm labor
- **Cuyo (Mendoza, San Juan, San Luis)**: Huarpe tributary labor shipped across the Andes to Chilean mines and farms until the 1640s–1660s

## End Conditions

The process as a discrete "invasion" phase closed around 1650–1660. By then:

- The Jesuit reduction system had stabilized the Guaraní population within an autonomous missionary zone, effectively ending open encomiendero access to those communities.
- The Calchaquí valleys were depopulated and their peoples dispersed as forced yanaconas.
- The demographic catastrophe — from roughly 500,000 Guaraní at contact to fewer than 30,000 under Spanish control by 1651–1652 — had transformed the social landscape.
- The Cuyo "war frontier" opened in 1660 as Puelche and Pehuenche groups, now horse-equipped and aware of Spanish vulnerability, shifted from exchange to warfare.
- Personal-service encomienda survived until the end of the colonial era, but the dramatic land-grabs and population reconfigurations of the first century were largely complete.

The long-term cultural legacy included Paraguayan bilingualism (Guaraní and Spanish), persistent Quechua loan-words in northwestern Argentine vernacular, and the survival of indigenous subsistence strategies — "the indigenous struggle for survival had been successful enough to form a strategic factor in the makeup of the country through the Independence Era" (Garavaglia).
