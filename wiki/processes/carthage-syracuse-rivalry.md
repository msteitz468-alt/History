# The Carthage–Syracuse Rivalry (the Sicilian Wars)

## Definition and Scope

For roughly two and a half centuries before [[roman-republic|Rome]] intervened, the
defining external relationship of [[carthage|Carthage]] was its struggle with Greek
[[syracuse|Syracuse]] for supremacy in [[sicily|Sicily]]. Richard Miles
([[miles-carthage-must-be-destroyed-2010]]) frames this not as a clean "clash of
civilisations" but as a self-perpetuating cycle he calls the **"economy of war"**: a
recurring conflict whose enslavements, city-captures, and reparations funded the next
round, with the smaller Punic, Greek, and indigenous Sicilian cities as the chief
victims. What mattered to Carthage was not Sicilian *land* (western Sicily saw minimal
agricultural import from it) but its **ports** — Panormus and Lilybaeum — without which
"Carthage would have faced the risk of economic disaster."

This page covers the long dynamic; for the war that ended it and brought in Rome, see the
[[first-punic-war-264-241bce|First Punic War]].

## Major Phases

| Date | Episode | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| 483–480 BCE | Terillus of Himera appeals to the Magonid **Hamilcar**; **[[battle-of-himera-480bce|Battle of Himera]]** | Magonid disaster — a *private* clan enterprise, not a state war; Hamilcar killed; Gelon's terms mild (2,000 talents, two temples) |
| 410–405 BCE | **Hannibal** (grandson of the Hamilcar of Himera) sacks Selinus and razes Himera (409, slaughtering 3,000 at the spot his grandfather fell) | Peace of 405 (Himilco) recognises **Carthaginian authority over west/central Sicily** plus tribute — its first formal Sicilian hegemony |
| 397 BCE | **[[dionysius-i|Dionysius I]]** of Syracuse poses as Greek liberator, massacres Punic populations, destroys **Motya** (never rebuilt) | Carthage founds **Lilybaeum** as Motya's maximum-security successor |
| 392–338 BCE | Repeated treaties (392/3, 373, 338) freezing a Carthaginian zone in western Sicily | Carthage reacts only defensively (Magonids, then Hanno) and "could never provide any lasting security" |
| 340 BCE | The Corinthian **Timoleon** destroys a Carthaginian army at the **Crimisus**, with unusually heavy citizen losses | Loss of the elite **Sacred Band**; citizen regiments thereafter mobilised "only in the gravest crises" — entrenching the mercenary system |
| 311–306 BCE | **[[agathocles-of-syracuse|Agathocles]]** carries the war to Africa (310) | Carthage survives but is brought "to the brink of financial ruin"; Africa exposed as a viable target |
| 265 BCE | The **Mamertine** crisis at Messana | Both Carthage and Rome are drawn in — the rivalry mutates into the [[first-punic-war-264-241bce|First Punic War]] |

## How the Rivalry Shaped Carthage

- **The military system.** The Sicilian wars entrenched Carthage's reliance on
  **mercenaries** (Libyans, Iberians, Campanians, Gauls, Balearic slingers). The first
  Carthaginian **coinage** was struck specifically to pay them, bearing the legend
  *Qrthdst/mḥnt* ("Carthage / the camp"). The Sicilian army became "a quasi-independent
  institution with its own coinage and administrative structure."
- **The constitution.** The shock of Himera prompted early-5th-c. reforms creating the
  lasting institutions — the **Tribunal of One Hundred and Four**, the **suffeture**, and
  the **Popular Assembly** — though this was elite reorganisation, not democratisation
  (wealth remained the qualification; [[aristotle|Aristotle]] ranked the constitution with
  Sparta's and Crete's). Generals, elected by the whole citizenry, were distrusted,
  audited rigorously, and uniquely **crucified for failure** — a "political decapitation"
  that, as Diodorus observed, drove commanders either to desert or to "attempt to become
  tyrants."
- **Religion.** The conflict carried [[carthaginian-religion|religious]] freight: the cult
  of Demeter and Core entered Carthage officially in 396 BCE after plague struck Himilco's
  army; the sanctuary of Astarte at **Eryx**, linked to [[heracles-melqart-syncretism|Melqart]],
  became a recurrent symbolic target.

## Interaction and Historiography

The rivalry is the seedbed of the [[punic-faith|anti-Carthaginian stereotype]]: Syracusan
propaganda (the Deinomenids, then **Timaeus**) retrospectively recast Himera as a "western
front" of the Greek–Persian war — a Salamis in the west — masking the reality of a failed
clan intervention and Gelon's own refusal to help mainland Greece. The chief surviving
source, **Diodorus**, draws heavily on the hostile Timaeus, so the "inter-ethnic total
war" narrative must be read against the grain of continuing Greek–Punic acculturation
visible in the material record.

## End Conditions

The rivalry did not so much end as **escalate**: the entry of Rome at Messana in 265 BCE
absorbed the centuries-old Sicilian contest into a far larger Roman–Carthaginian struggle.
Syracuse under [[hiero-ii-of-syracuse|Hiero II]] allied with Carthage, then defected to
Rome (263).
