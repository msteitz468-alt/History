# Australian Frontier Conflict

The **frontier wars** were the protracted, dispersed violence between
[[aboriginal-australians|Aboriginal peoples]] and settlers that accompanied the British
occupation of [[australia|Australia]] from 1788 into the twentieth century. As pastoral
[[australian-pastoral-expansion|expansion]] pushed onto Aboriginal country, conflict over
land, water, and game produced raids, reprisals, massacres, and disease-driven
depopulation — the violent counterpart to [[terra-nullius]] and
[[settler-colonialism|settler colonialism]]. It was not a single war but an episodic,
continent-wide series of conflicts, pacification campaigns, massacres, and reprisals from
the first British settlement at Port Jackson (1788) through the last documented large-scale
massacres of the 1920s–1930s.

## Definition and Scope

Per [[bashford-macintyre-cha-v1-2013|CHA Vol. 1]] (Ch. 14, Banivanua Mar and Edmonds,
'Indigenous and settler relations'), frontier conflict was neither simply violence nor
conciliation but a dynamic in which "contact, conciliation and conflict would always be
closely intertwined" (p. 342). The chapter treats it as central rather than peripheral to
colonial history.

## Causal Drivers

- [[australian-pastoral-expansion]] — the wool economy required Aboriginal lands;
  competition over water, food, and territory was the direct driver of most violence.
- [[settler-colonialism]] — the structural project of replacing Indigenous with settler
  occupation; "supercessionism" — that settlers "should rightly replace Indigenous people
  — was promoted, based on claims of British moral and racial superiority, and Lockean
  principles of civilisation, property and the imperative to cultivate land" (CHA I, p. 512).
- Introduced disease — preceding and accompanying the physical frontier, devastating
  populations before settlers arrived in numbers (the 1789 smallpox epidemic killed perhaps
  up to 80 per cent of the population in the Sydney region, spreading inland along Indigenous
  trading networks; CHA I, p. 343).
- [[terra-nullius]] — the legal doctrine that denied Aboriginal land rights, removing legal
  obstacles to pastoral occupation.

## Major Phases

### First contacts and the urban frontier (1788–c. 1816)

Phillip's Instructions directed him to "endeavour by every possible means to open an
Intercourse with the Natives and to conciliate their affections" yet to "punish" those who
would "wantonly destroy them" (HRA I.1, pp. 13–14; CHA I, p. 342). Settlers on the
Cumberland Plain occupied rich Darug land and "prevented Darug people from harvesting native
yams… Some also kidnapped Darug children to work as forced labourers" (Goodall, CHA I,
p. 344). Resistance led by the warrior **Pemulwuy** was severe enough that in May 1795
officials feared the Hawkesbury settlement would be abandoned; Lieutenant-Governor Paterson
ordered troops to shoot Darug on sight and gibbet the bodies. In April 1816 Governor
Macquarie sent three military detachments into Dharawal lands with orders to hang the
"guilty" in trees "to strike the great terror into the survivors"; at least sixteen were
killed (**Appin massacre**), followed by a Proclamation proscribing Aboriginal entry into
towns and a pass system.

### Van Diemen's Land: the Black War (1824–1834)

On contact in 1803 there were **6,000–8,000** Aboriginal people in
[[van-diemens-land|Van Diemen's Land]]; by 1838 only around **60 survived**, sequestered on
Flinders Island (Ryan, CHA I, p. 347). Lieutenant-Governor George Arthur declared martial
law in November 1828 and used military "roving parties". In October 1830 he extended martial
law island-wide and launched the **Black Line** — c. **2,200 men** (mostly armed civilians
and convicts; only 550 troops) intended to sweep Aboriginal people onto the Forestier and
Tasman Peninsulas; it captured few. Conciliation passed to George Augustus Robinson's
"friendly mission"; Aboriginal leader **Mannalargenna** of the Oyster Bay people negotiated
a verbal covenant of future welfare, and Reynolds credits such leaders with ending the war.
Arthur later lamented that "a treaty was not entered into" and that the devastation "must
ever remain a stain upon the colonisation of Van Diemen's Land" (Arthur to Hay, Sept. 1832;
CHA I, p. 420).

### Mainland expansion (1824–1838)

Governor Brisbane proclaimed martial law on the Bathurst Plains (1824) after Wiradjuri
resistance and established a mounted police force. Julie Evans argues martial law "served to
formalise the frontier as a legal space of violence" (CHA I, p. 345). In 1834 WA Governor
James Stirling led a force south of Fremantle into Pinjarup country — the **Battle of
Pinjarra / Pinjarra Massacre** (one settler and 20–25 Aboriginal people killed). 1838
produced the **Waterloo Creek** massacre of Kamilaroi people (c. 40–50 killed, Major James
Nunn) and the **Myall Creek massacre** (at least **22** men, women, and children killed and
burned). Governor Gipps secured retrials and seven settlers were hanged in December 1838 —
"one of the rare instances when white men were tried, convicted and executed for the mass
killing of Aboriginal people" (CHA I, p. 488), which "enraged many settlers" (p. 489).

### The pastoral interior, north, and west (1840s–1920s)

Overlanders occupied central and northern Australia with cattle only from the 1880s.
"Regions such as the Kimberley in Western Australia experienced until at least the 1920s the
kind of frontier violence that had occurred earlier in the south" (CHA I, p. 359). The
**Forrest River massacre** (1926) and the **Coniston Station massacre** (1928) are among the
highest-profile twentieth-century incidents.

## Geographic Spread

The frontier moved with pastoral occupation: from the Cumberland Plain and Hawkesbury
(1790s), to Tasmania (1820s–30s), the inland NSW plains and Western Australia (1830s), then
Queensland, central Australia, and the Kimberley/Northern Territory (1860s–1920s).

## Interaction

Frontier conflict was inseparable from [[australian-pastoral-expansion]] and the
[[colonial-australian-economy|colonial economy]] it funded, and from the legal architecture
of [[terra-nullius]] and [[colonial-australian-law]]. Violence shaded into
"administered violence" through protection legislation.

## End Conditions

Direct frontier violence gave way to the **mission/reserve and "protection"** system —
"administered violence" through legislation (CHA I, p. 364). Victoria's 1869 Act created the
Board for the Protection of Aborigines with power over residence, employment, earnings, and
the custody of children; the "Half Caste Act" (1886) introduced blood-quantum exclusions;
Queensland's 1897 *Aboriginals Protection and Restriction of the Sale of Opium Act* gave
sweeping removal powers. By Federation all colonies had comparable laws — "Aboriginality
would be defined and managed in 67 different ways in over 700 separate pieces of legislation"
(McCorquodale, CHA I, p. 363).

## Aboriginal Agency

Aboriginal peoples were not passive victims. Resistance and negotiation were widespread —
the Darug war of occupation, Wiradjuri resistance, Pemulwuy's campaign, Mannalargenna's
covenant, the Coranderrk rebellion, and pan-colonial land-rights petitioning from the 1840s
to the 1890s. Indigenous people "directly requested grants of land, recruited white
supporters… or reoccupied and squatted on land in their country" — a "nineteenth-century
land rights campaign, spanning Victoria and New South Wales from the 1840s" (Goodall, CHA I,
p. 357).

## Historiography

The scale and characterisation of frontier violence is among the most contested questions in
Australian historiography — the **"history wars"** between scholars emphasising systematic,
policy-driven killing and dispossession (Reynolds, Ryan, Attwood, Goodall, Banivanua Mar and
Edmonds) and those minimising it (Windschuttle). See [[australian-history-wars]] for the full
debate. A signal methodological point: the chapter insists resistance and accommodation be
read alongside dispossession, since Aboriginal people "maintained degrees of autonomy in the
aftermath of occupation" (CHA I, p. 366).
