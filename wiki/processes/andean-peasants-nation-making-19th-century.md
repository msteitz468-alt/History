## Definition and Scope

Brooke Larson's chapter (Ch. 23 of [[cambridge-history-native-peoples-americas-v3-2]]) documents the century-long confrontation between emergent Andean republics and their indigenous peasant majorities. Between roughly 1820 and 1910, "fledgling Andean republics emerged from the chaos of the independence era" and began dismantling colonial administrative structures while simultaneously deploying new forms of extraction and cultural repression against indigenous communities. The four republics examined — Colombia, Ecuador, Peru, and Bolivia — all grappled with a defining contradiction: "to bring Indians into the economy as subsistence laborers, but to lock them out of the nation as citizens."

Larson situates this process against earlier historiography that portrayed indigenous people as "isolated, atomised, passive, parochial, and crushed under the forces of modernization." Her counter-argument foregrounds indigenous agency: "resilient popular cultures, memories, and discourses" and "the more hidden counterforces of Andean peasant politics, militarised Indian movements, and cultural forms of resistance that went into the unmaking, and reordering, of postcolonial political formations."

## Causal Drivers

**Independence and the collapse of the dual-republic framework.** The end of colonial rule destroyed the tributary pact — the implicit bargain between the crown and indigenous communities whereby tribute payment bought corporate rights and a degree of legal protection. The new republics inherited a contradiction between Enlightenment principles of individual equality and the social reality of ethnically stratified societies. Liberal constitutions proclaimed citizenship; practice reinstated tribute and servitude.

**The tribute abolition/restoration cycle.** Peru abolished Indian tribute in 1854, Ecuador in 1857, Bolivia in 1874. But abolition was not accompanied by any positive institutional framework. Peru's President Castilla discovered that guano revenues could not fund provincial bureaucracies that had depended on tribute; he tried (and failed) to restore it within two years of abolition. A later regime restored it temporarily in 1866, provoking the altiplano rebellion of Huancané. It reappeared as a "war tax" in 1879. See [[contribucion-indigena]].

**Export-driven capitalism.** Coastal Peru's guano boom of the 1840s plugged the west coast into Atlantic markets. By the 1860s–1870s, wool, nitrates, quinine, and coffee were drawing capital into highland regions. The southern Peruvian wool trade created a "commercial revolution" connecting Arequipa merchant houses to Aymara- and Quechua-speaking herding communities of the altiplano; the railroad reaching Puno in 1874 displaced indigenous pack traders and muleteer networks that had sustained interregional commerce for centuries.

**Scientific racism and the "Indian problem."** Creole elites across all four republics constructed racial-evolutionary discourses defining Indianness as the obstacle to modernity. Colombia's liberals in the 1850s produced "pseudo-scientific regional-racial typologies" associating whiteness with progress and Indianness with indolence and anti-market character. These civilising discourses legitimated land divestiture, forced labour, linguistic suppression, and the banning of indigenous religious festivals.

**War and militarisation.** The War of the Pacific (Chile vs. Peru and Bolivia, 1879–1883) was the "critical breakpoint" of the century. Indian communities "virtually bankrolled the war effort" through war taxes, supplies, and conscription. Wartime mobilisation created Indian guerrilla forces and autonomist microrepublics that could not be easily demobilised. Militarised peasant movements in the Central Sierra (Mantaro Valley) became the basis for later claims of citizenship and land rights that the postwar oligarchy violently repressed.

## Major Phases

**Phase 1 — Liberal retreat and *pax republicana* (c. 1820–1850).** The immediate postindependence decades saw liberal land reforms languish, tribute restored, and state power devolve to provincial elites and local authorities. This transitory reprieve "opened new spaces for Andean peoples to negotiate political pacts, recover lost lands, restore ethnic authorities, rearrange translocal networks of patronage, kinship, barter, and trade." In Bolivia, Andean communities continued to manipulate colonial-customary terms of the "tributary pact" — paying tribute in exchange for legal recognition of communal landholding.

**Phase 2 — Liberal offensive (c. 1850–1879).** Export revenues and liberal ideology emboldened Creole elites to attack corporate community structures. Peru's Civil Code of 1852 declared all lands alienable; the abolition of tribute (1854) removed legal recognition of communal rights. In Colombia the Liberals in 1849–1880 legalised the immediate sale of *resguardo* (reservation) lands, abolished hereditary chiefdoms, and attempted cultural homogenisation through "whitening." Ecuador delivered the civilising mission to the Church, which coerced Indian labour into road-building while effacing indigenous people from census records. These reforms "provoked powerful backlash effects of ethnic and communal action and consciousness."

**Phase 3 — War, rebellion, and retrenchment (1879–c. 1900).** The War of the Pacific shattered the liberal project in Peru and Bolivia. Indian communities were recruited, taxed, and mobilised; where they expected citizenship as reward, they faced "campaigns of military pacification." Peru's civilising project "rapidly collapsed into campaigns of military pacification of the Indian patriot-soldiers, rebels, and guerrillas, who would not lay down their arms or their ideals after war and betrayal." Bolivia's collision course with Aymara and Quechua communities over land privatisation produced the Federal War/Zárate Willka uprising of 1899, in which militarised Aymara forces aligned with Liberal federalists only to be massacred once the war was won.

**Phase 4 — Oligarchic consolidation and early indigenismo (c. 1900–1910).** Postwar landed oligarchies consolidated control over the altiplano wool trade and highland estates. A "pact of discrimination" against the Indian majority emerged as elite consensus. But the defeat and betrayal of indigenous wartime allies prepared the groundwork for early twentieth-century cycles of Indian insurgency and the rise of indigenista intellectual and political movements.

## Geographic Spread

**Colombia** — Indian communities a small minority; northern highlands experienced communal erosion and peasantisation; southern Paez and Pasto territories became sites of militant ethnic resistance, land recuperation campaigns, and Paez caudillismo drawing on nine successive civil wars. The quinine boom (c. 1850–1900) devastated forests and communities of the Cauca department.

**Ecuador** — Indian peasantries constituted approximately half the republic's population but mostly outside freeholding communities. Conservative Church hegemony coerced labour while effacing Indians from census records. The Otavalans developed an exceptional alternative mercantile culture combining craft specialisation, selective cultural innovation, and ethnic solidarity — "an alternative mercantile culture, which reinforced the ethnic boundaries and identities of the whole group."

**Peru** — Indians composed 55–62 percent of the population throughout most of the century (Kubler; Gootenberg). The southern highlands underwent a "commercial revolution" centred on wool, with the railroad displacing traditional market circuits. The Central Highlands bore the brunt of the Pacific War. Northern coastal elites imported Chinese indentured workers rather than force highland Indians to migrate, revealing the structural limits of liberal labour-market ideology.

**Bolivia** — Sustained the [[contribucion-indigena|*contribución indígena*]] (colonial tribute under a new name) longer than the other republics, giving Aymara- and Quechua-speaking communities in the northern altiplano and northern Potosí leverage to negotiate on colonial-customary terms. Land privatisation laws of the 1870s–1880s put the Bolivian state on a direct collision course with ayllu communities, producing violent confrontations into the early twentieth century.

## Interaction

This process is preceded_by [[republic-of-indians-in-revolt]], whose suppression both destroyed indigenous political leadership and implanted the Inkari messianic legacy that continued to animate Andean mobilisations throughout the nineteenth century. It is concurrent_with global processes of capitalist expansion and the spread of liberal political economy, which arrived unevenly across Andean highland regions. It produced the conditions (dispossession, militarisation, betrayal) that generated early twentieth-century indigenismo and the Bolivian and Peruvian agrarian reform movements.

The process is contrasts_with the coastal and export-sector histories of the same republics: while seaboard oligarchies embraced European modernity and global markets, highland indigenous communities maintained "strong traditions of Andean agency, land-based ayllus, submerged and occasionally insurgent ethnic politics, and resilient popular cultures."

The Otavalan case is analogous_to other ethnic entrepreneurial adaptations (such as Bolivian chola chicha manufacturers) in which communities selectively incorporated market tools within their own cultural matrix to preserve subsistence security and ethnic autonomy.

## End Conditions

The process does not resolve within the period covered but rather generates its successors: early twentieth-century cycles of Indian insurgency across southern Peru and Bolivia, the rise of indigenista intellectual movements, and eventually the mid-twentieth-century agrarian reforms in Bolivia (1953) and Peru (1969). As Larson notes, "the so-called Indian problem as posed remains unresolved to this day." The nineteenth century's specific contribution was to transform the colonial dual-republic framework — with its internal contradictions between protection and exploitation — into a republican framework of formal individual equality combined with systematic racial-capitalist dispossession.

## Historiography

Larson draws on a wide scholarly literature including George Kubler's demographic studies of Peru (*The Indian Caste of Peru*, 1952); Joanne Rappaport's work on Paez politics and memory (*The Politics of Memory*, 1990); Frank Safford on Colombian racial ideology; Nils Jacobsen on the Peruvian altiplano wool trade (*Mirages of Transition*, 1993); Paul Gootenberg on Peruvian fiscal and demographic history; and Brooke Larson's own earlier work on Cochabamba. The chapter identifies as a major historiographic gap the virtual invisibility of rural Andean peoples in nineteenth-century sources and scholarship — a product partly of thin documentation and partly of implicit assumptions about passivity that more recent research has challenged.
