# Northern Interior (Subarctic) Postcontact History

Arthur J. Ray’s CHNPA I.2 ch. 13 synthesis: boreal-forest postcontact history as **fur-trade continuity**, not settler-agricultural removal. Subarctic Natives kept the fur trade central from first European encounter; Europeans generally did not push them off the land because extensive agriculture is not possible; languages flourished; after traders, missionaries and government agents mattered most—yet even these had little impact before the early twentieth century. Today’s societies strongly reflect aboriginal roots, two to four centuries of fur-trading traditions, missionary work, and recent government programs.

> "The postcontact experience of the Native peoples of the boreal forest zone has been fundamentally different from that of aboriginal groups living in all other areas of North America. The fur trade has remained important for Subarctic Natives ever since they first encountered Europeans. In most places Europeans have not pushed them off the land because extensive agriculture is not possible. Native languages continue to flourish. … This means the continuity with the recent past is very powerful in the Northern Interior." — Ray

## Definition and Scope

**What it is.** The long postcontact process (c. 1600–present) among hunter-fisher-gatherers of the North American boreal forest (Subarctic): from near Labrador west-northwest ~5,500 km into central Alaska. Not unbroken forest—eastern two-thirds is rocky Canadian Shield; productive forests concentrate in sheltered lowlands beside lakes/rivers where woodland caribou, moose, furbearers, waterfowl, and staple fish (whitefish, sturgeon, lake trout) concentrated. Mackenzie drainage: poorly drained heavy forest, beaver/muskrat abundance; Peace–Athabasca once rich in moose and bison.

**What it is not.** Settler-agricultural frontier dispossession of the kind that reorganized the eastern woodlands, Plains, or Southwest. The master process remains commercial fur-trade articulation of Native economies—later layered with missions, numbered treaties, game laws, welfare, schooling, and mega-projects—while a residual non-agricultural land base and language continuity persist.

**Contact-era linguistic map.** Athapaskan ([[dene]]) speakers northwest of the Churchill River; Algonquian ([[cree]], [[ojibwe]]) in the Churchill Valley and woodlands eastward except Labrador coast ([[inuit]]). Both families lived mainly lakeside/riverside with regional subsistence variation (caribou + fish lower Mackenzie; moose on Yukon headwaters/Liard; bison/moose Peace–Athabasca; Algonquians favoring moose then caribou). Social organization: small winter hunting groups (few related families), local bands coalescing at fishing sites, no hereditary chiefs—consensus leadership by skilled/generous hunters; Chipewyan barren-ground caribou zones supported much larger winter groups (400–1,000) using chute-and-pound drives.

**Contact locations disputed.** Archaeology, documents, and oral evidence conflict; contact itself rearranged populations (especially central/western Subarctic) before first recorded European observers—debates include northern Ojibwas, Woodland Assiniboines, Western Woods Crees.

## Causal Drivers

- **European fashion demand:** Felt-hat boom (esp. after 1550) made beaver underwool the staple; NW Europe depleted by mid-16th c.; Siberia and northern North America remaining preserves. Coat beaver (worn cloaks, guard hairs gone) paid premium in 16th–17th c.
- **Middleman succession:** Indians monopolized inland traffic; every time Europeans bypassed one middleman group, another emerged. Trading frontier expanded with Native entrepreneurs at the cutting edge, often with conflict and migration—so explorers rarely saw a pristine aboriginal world. Spatial evolution 1581–1630s models a process continuing ~200 years to the lower Mackenzie and Fraser headwaters (`part_of: [[eastern-north-american-fur-trade]]`).
- **Competitive leverage:** Anglo-French then HBC–NWC rivalry gave Indian consumers price/quality leverage (overplus, quality-gated muskets/kettles/cloth, gift ceremonialism, credit as trader obligation). Monopoly 1821–70 reversed the balance.
- **Ecology and tenure:** Growing reliance on sedentary furbearers (beaver) plus debt individualization catalyzed family hunting territories (Speck’s system now generally treated as fur-trade product, not pure aboriginal tenure—Bailey; Bishop & Morantz *Who Owns the Beaver?*).
- **State and capital after 1870:** Rupert’s Land transfer, numbered treaties, industrial transport, ranch-fur competition, registered traplines, family allowance/schooling, hydro/pipeline mega-projects.

## Major Phases

### 1. Expanding fur trade (c. 1534–1763)

First recorded European contact: Cartier, Labrador coast 1534; Basque whaling; Tadoussac (Saguenay outlet, Montagnais country) principal French Basque port. Montagnais became first major middlemen (Tadoussac, Lac St-Jean); Champlain’s inland advance and military aid undercut them; by mid-1630s Hurons largely replaced Algonquins/Montagnais as leading French middlemen.

European goods: initially prestige/supernatural, then utilitarian/military; metal kettles transformed migratory domestic life. Epidemics 1634–40 (measles, influenza, smallpox to James Bay, mortality often >50%)—Ray rejects Calvin Martin’s “war on animals” thesis: little evidence religions shattered into wanton overkill; Natives blamed witchcraft by neighbors or French, not animals (`concurrent_with: [[virgin-soil-epidemics]]`).

Mid-17th c. [[haudenosaunee-confederacy|Iroquois]] wars (Dutch arms advantage) dispersed Algonquins, Hurons, Petuns, Nipissings; Ojibwas/Ottawas fled west along Superior; French inland trade in tatters until post-1666 peace and coureurs de bois expansion. By 1660 Cree bands from Lake of the Woods, English River/Lac Seul, and western James Bay participated via middlemen.

**HBC 1670:** Nonsuch/Groseilliers 1668–69; charter 2 May 1670—Rupert’s Land title and monopoly on drainage into Hudson Strait. Trade protocol: gift exchange + barter; “trading captains”; Made Beaver (MB) unit of account lasting in many northern areas past WWI. Bay posts (Rupert, Moose, Albany, York 1682, Severn) armed local Crees—Nixon reported Bay cordon denying distant Indians Fort Albany access except Assiniboine allies. York Factory via Hayes–Echimamish became chief western Canadian fur warehouse before 1867. 1694–1720 Cree wars on Chipewyans (Churchill) and Beavers (toward Athabasca); Fort Churchill 1717 armed Chipewyan pushback. By ~1715 Assiniboine–Cree middlemen to Athabasca; Blackfeet rarely at York after 1720s.

HBC “slept by the frozen sea” while French seized Bay posts (1686–1714), then after Utrecht reopened Postes du Nord and La Vérendrye to lower Saskatchewan—wise cost control leaving inland trade to Indian partners; Indians still needed bulky English goods. By 1763 European goods well integrated S/E of Churchill; competitive market kept Natives strong; little widespread depletion; Homeguard near posts largely mixed descent (French-Native “bois brulés”/Métis; English/Scottish-Native “mixed-bloods”/country born)—see **Métis formation (folded)** below.

### 2. Destructive competition (1763–1821)

Montreal trade passed to Scottish/English/colonial entrepreneurs using French Canadian, mixed-blood, and Indian labor. Pontiac briefly hampered NW trade. Nor’Westers reopened Saskatchewan 1765–74; **Cumberland House 1774**—HBC’s first inland post, end of frozen-sea policy. NWC into Athabasca–Mackenzie (Peter Pond 1778) and across Rockies after Mackenzie 1793. Two camps built >600 posts; by 1821 most Indians S/E of Peace River lived near a post → Indian middlemen obsolete there; labor <300 (1774) to >1,800 (1820).

**Provisioning and manufacture:** Homeguards supplied meat/fish; Rainy–Winnipeg Ojibwas (immigrated after ~1730) provided whitefish, sturgeon, wild rice, corn; Peace River Beavers and Crees provisioned Athabasca brigades (NWC force 1802–15); both companies depended on Ojibwa canoe builders for canots du nord. NWC labor ~¾ French Canadian including many French-Indian descent plus Montreal-area Iroquois trappers.

**Crisis to 1821:** Chipewyans expanded into full boreal forest after Cree smallpox 1781–2—by 1821 held most country N of Churchill and E of Athabasca (Caribou Eaters; Athabascas; upper Churchill). Overtrapping, tularemia, game depletion; by 1821 English River–Rainy River bands lacked caribou skins for moccasins. Alcohol far more available S/E of Mackenzie; NWC debt abuses (seizing wives). Net: higher prices/cheaper goods vs lost independence and social disruption—“Most Indians traded a high degree of economic independence for interdependence with Euro-Canadians.”

### 3. Monopoly / shifting power (1821–1870)

HBC–NWC amalgamation 1821 (mutual ruin; British alarm at lawlessness/alcohol): 20-year monopoly on Rupert’s Land and Northwest. George Simpson: “fur desert” vs American advance; beaver conservation (closed posts, seasons, 1822 steel-trap ban except US frontier, 1826 quotas)—initially failed (officer profit share; Native circumvention; spiritual game-keeper worldview); tighter 1839 quotas → rebound by 1840s. Monopoly encouraged family hunting territory trends where ecology allowed.

**Austerity and racial hierarchy:** gift ceremonialism cut; alcohol banned most areas; tea/bannock rose as staples; ≥250 laid off, targeting large Native families; rigid race/class system blocked mixed-ancestry officer rise; after 1821 officers not to marry Indian women. Company still depended on cheap Indian bush labor and sought to keep people in the woodlands. Interior Alaska entered face-to-face trade late: Russian Nulato 1839; HBC Fort Yukon 1847.

### 4. End of the old order (1867–1921)

1870: HBC relinquished monopoly and Rupert’s Land title ($1.5 million + land grants); Canada assumed sovereignty and legal welfare responsibility; BNA Act reserved Indians/Indian lands to federal jurisdiction. Robinson precedents (1850) → eleven treaties/adhesions 1871–1929 covering wooded territory James Bay to lower Mackenzie (annuities, reserves, hunt/fish on undeveloped crown lands). Treaty Three (1873): Rainy River/Lake of the Woods Ojibwas (diversified after Dawson Road) vs Osnaburgh fur-oriented bands. Annuities/ammo/twine initially subsidized fur participation; inflation later eroded value. Treaty hunting clauses reserved government regulation—later used “in name of conservation”; only *Sparrow* (1990 SCC) strongly reasserted aboriginal rights to land/living resources unless clear extinguishing legislation (`concurrent_with: [[treaty-sovereignty-native-north-america]]`).

Industrial competition after CPR 1885 and telegraph: cash buyers, Revillon Frères (James Bay from 1899), Northern Trading Company, Lamson & Hubbard; 1914 HBC “black year” (London market closed, no credit)—never fully regained traditional confidence. Positives: higher returns, cash, more goods. Negatives: European “highliner” trappers dynamiting dams and trespassing—“scourge of the industry” by WWI’s end.

### 5. Marginalization (1921–1945)

Boom-bust eliminated major rivals; HBC absorbed Revillon but kept it as “sane competitor” until 1936; still only ~¼ Canadian wild fur by WWII eve (aviation + individual buyers). Ranch silver fox/mink rose—Natives did not benefit. Utility furs (muskrat, hare, squirrel) cyclic. Government conservation: multi-year beaver closures; late 1930s–40s preserves (nearly all James Bay + large Saskatchewan River tracts); **registered traplines** (BC 1925 → Yukon 1950) intended to protect Natives but often curtailed crown-land hunting rights—did help beaver recover. Bush aviation and rail to Churchill (1929)/James Bay (1932) displaced Native transport labor; Moose Factory’s Moose Works closure devastated a century-old Native workforce hub.

Government reluctant on welfare since 1870 (fear of permanent wards); missionaries and HBC filled gaps; after Confederation company pressed reimbursement—state assumed social overhead formerly company. Indian Act agents increasingly dictated political constitution and regulated life. Missionary acceleration: James Evans Cree syllabics Norway House 1840 (later Athapaskan/Inuktitut); Protestant–Catholic rivalry across Subarctic schools.

### 6. Modern era (post-1945)

Fur price crash vs soaring manufactures made trapping marginal. 1945 family allowance extended to Natives—then used to force school attendance (withhold for truants); household replaced winter hunting group as primary economic unit; school curriculum failed bush skills.

**James Bay (1971–75):** Quebec unilaterally announced $5.6 billion SE James Bay hydro (30 Apr 1971) flooding Cree fishing/trapping of Rupert House, Nemiscau, Waswanipi, Mistassini. Crees + Indians of Quebec Association; Malouf injunction forced talks. **1975 James Bay and Northern Quebec Agreement:** Grand Council of the Crees (Aug 1974) + Inuit (later Naskapi modification)—compensation (Ray: >$90 million; Washburn renaissance chapter cites $232.5 million over 21 years and exclusive ownership 5,543 km²), land categories, hunting priority, income security for hunters/trappers, Cree Regional Authority. By early 1980s more full-time Cree hunters than pre-agreement eve; Crees later blocked Great Whale and Nottaway–Broadback–Rupert; by 1995 more united and internationally assertive (`concurrent_with: [[native-american-renaissance-1960-1995]]`).

**Mackenzie Valley / Berger (1974–77):** Multinational $7 billion gas pipeline proposal → Berger Inquiry community hearings; Dene, Métis, Inuit opposed until claims settled. 1977: no Yukon pipeline; Mackenzie delay ≥10 years. 1973 Dene caveat; courts doubted Treaties 8/11 extinguished title. **1975 Dene Declaration**—equitable claims + self-government within Canada. s.35 Constitution Act 1982 entrenched existing aboriginal/treaty rights. 1988 Dene–Métis AIP; 1990 assembly rejected but allowed regional deals; Yukon umbrella AIP; 1982 NWT division plebiscite (western Dene/Métis/Euro-Canadian vs eastern Inuit Nunavut path).

**Interior Alaska Athapaskans:** US takeover 1867, steamboats 1869, rifles, fish wheels (~1900), settlement nucleation, ANCSA 1971 cash + share of 15.6 million acres for Athapaskan groups via regional/village corporations (`concurrent_with: [[alaska-native-claims-settlement-1971]]`).

## Geographic Spread

Labrador / Gulf of St. Lawrence → James Bay / Hudson Bay coasts → Shield interior (Churchill, English River, Rainy–Winnipeg) → Saskatchewan–Athabasca–Mackenzie → Yukon Valley and interior Alaska. Extension is the spatial growth of [[eastern-north-american-fur-trade]] until it reached the lower Mackenzie and Pacific-slope headwaters.

### HBC as Subarctic institutional through-line (folded—do not duplicate full actor page)

Charter 1670 → Bay-side MB protocol → frozen-sea inland policy vs French → forced inland 1774 → ruinous NWC rivalry to 1821 → Simpson monopoly conservation/austerity/racialized hierarchy → 1870 title surrender → industrial-age competition (Revillon, cash buyers, aviation) → dual trader/welfare role later state-reimbursed → ~¼ Canadian wild fur by WWII. Company archives (Provincial Archives of Manitoba) are Ray’s primary documentary spine.

### Métis / mixed-blood formation in Subarctic post orbits (folded)

Structural product of fur-trade marriages (French coureurs; HBC ban ignored; Van Kirk *Many Tender Ties*; Brown *Strangers in Blood*). By 1763 Homeguards largely mixed. 1774–1821: mixed-blood labor in NWC voyageurs and HBC seasonal lowest rung. After 1821: permanent trades but blocked from officer class; Victorian/missionary morality degraded Native and mixed-blood wives’ status. Modern Mackenzie politics: joint Dene–Métis AIP 1988 / rejection 1990; western NWT polity includes Dene, Métis, Euro-Canadians. Red River / 1869–1885 Plains Métis narrative primarily under [[metis]] and [[great-plains-native-history-1650-1885]].

## End Conditions

**Not ended.** Fur trade no longer sole master economy, but Ray’s continuity thesis holds: layered aboriginal + fur-trade + missionary + state influences persist; residual land base and languages remain; modern Cree (James Bay) and Dene–Métis (Mackenzie) politics reassert control over development without erasing the long fur-trade-shaped relationship to land and animals. Transformation, not termination—industrial capital, conservation bureaucracy, and mega-project bargaining replace competitive middleman leverage as the main constraints.

## Historiography

- **Primary spine:** HBC archives (Manitoba); HBRS published volumes; E.E. Rich on mercantile HBC to 1867; Ray *Canadian Fur Trade in the Industrial Age* and *Indians in the Fur Trade*; Ray & Freeman *“Give Us Good Measure”* (pre-1763 economics).
- **Classics:** Harold Innis *Fur Trade in Canada*.
- **Land tenure debate:** Speck (aboriginal family territories) vs A.G. Bailey (fur-trade product); Bishop & Morantz (eds.) *Who Owns the Beaver?* (1986)—current consensus leans fur-trade origin for exclusive family tracts while sharing within winter groups.
- **Overkill thesis:** Calvin Martin *Keepers of the Game* vs Krech (ed.) *Indians, Animals, and the Fur Trade*—Ray sides against Martin.
- **Regional monographs:** Francis & Morantz *Partners in Furs* (E James Bay); Bishop *Northern Ojibwa and the Fur Trade*; Yerbury and Abel (*Drum Songs*) on Athapaskans/Dene; Van Kirk and Jennifer Brown on women and company families; Grant on missions.
- **Modern resource politics:** Richardson and Salisbury on James Bay; Berger report and O’Malley on pipeline inquiry; Watkins *Dene Nation*; Asch on aboriginal rights and NWT constitutions.
- **Contact-location uncertainty** and pre-observer disruption remain standing methodological limits (`date_precision` for many contact maps = disputed/approximate).
