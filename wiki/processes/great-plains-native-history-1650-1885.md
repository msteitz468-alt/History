# Great Plains Native History, c. 1650–1885

Horse-era Plains Native history from the arrival of the horse through the end of free plains life on both sides of the 49th parallel. Principal synthesis: Loretta Fowler, CHNPA I.2 ch. 9.

## Definition and Scope

Mid-seventeenth through late nineteenth centuries brought huge change: Native peoples seized European/settler opportunities, adapted technologies, transformed institutions, and created multiethnic western society. Some expanded and dominated neighbors; others resisted dispossession. Eventually U.S. and Canadian expansion forced peoples onto small reserves where struggle for valued things and self-directed change continued.

The Plains extended from Missouri/Lower Mississippi bottoms to Rocky foothills with **two adaptations**:

1. **Riverine horticultural villages** on Missouri tributaries and Lower Mississippi rivers (Caddoans—Caddos, Wichitas, Pawnees, Arikaras—from SE ~A.D. 900; Mandans and Hidatsas on upper Missouri ~A.D. 1100; later Ohio-Valley Siouans—Missouris, Otos, Iowas, Omahas, Poncas, Kansas, Osages—east of the Caddoans). Villages often permanent/fortified; summer communal bison hunts; crops of corn, beans, squash, melons, sunflowers.
2. **Nomadic hunting bands** in uplands west/southwest/northwest of the Missouri—more egalitarian than villagers’ hereditary stratification; winter bands aggregated for communal hunts and ceremonies spring–fall.

Late-eighteenth-century nomads included Algonquian [[actors/blackfoot-confederacy|Blackfeet]] (Siksika, Blood, Piegan), Plains Crees, Gros Ventres, Arapahos, [[actors/cheyenne|Cheyennes]]; Siouan Assiniboines/Stoneys, Teton [[actors/lakota|Sioux]] (Lakota), Yanktons/Yanktonais, some Santee, [[actors/crow-apsalooke|Crows]] (split from Hidatsas); Athapaskan Sarsis and Kiowa-Apaches; Kiowa; [[actors/comanches|Comanches]] (Uto-Aztecan) on southern Plains into the Southwest.

## Causal Drivers

- **Horse complex** — Spanish–Eastern Pueblo trade early seventeenth century; Apaches traded captives/raided for stock 1660s–70s; by 1680s–90s Kiowas/Kiowa-Apaches traded horses to Caddoans south of Platte; early eighteenth-century Comanches raided Spanish/Apache settlements and traded at [[places/black-hills|Black Hills]] fairs; Blackfeet/Gros Ventres equestrian by 1740s; Crows via Shoshones/Flatheads ~1740s; Tetons traded horses to eastern Sioux at James River fair 1750s; by early nineteenth century all Plains groups had horses. Horses made meat more reliable, freed young men for raids, and let wealthy men loan horses for political loyalty—wealth/status stratification and polygyny correlation among Blackfeet and Kiowas.
- **Gun trade** — Flintlock musket became a war necessity (bow superior for hunt); French guns gave Pawnees, Wichitas, Missouris, Otos, Osages, Kansas an edge against Spain’s Apache allies. Seventeenth–early-nineteenth-century trade neither side wholly dominated, though exchange disproportionately benefited Europeans/Americans.
- **Slave, horse, and captive markets** reorganized economics, politics, and religion; expanders included Comanches and Osages (south), Assiniboines and Plains Crees (northwest), Teton Sioux (central/northern).
- **Disease** — [[processes/virgin-soil-epidemics]]: 1780s smallpox killed ~one-third of Plains Natives; 1837 killed half—Mandans from ~9,000 (1750) to just under 150; Wichitas lost almost 90 percent late seventeenth–late nineteenth century. Social effects: captive adoption, relaxed exogamy (Cheyennes after 1849 cholera), combined polities (Mandan–Hidatsa–Arikara sections).
- **Robe trade** (after American Fur Company–Blackfeet accommodation 1831; steamboats) brought nomad prosperity and more polygyny as hunters needed more women to tan hides; depots Fort Union, Fort Pierre, Fort Laramie, Fort Mackenzie, Fort Cass, Fort Clark. Profit margins 200–2,000 percent (Denig).
- **Settler expansion and game destruction** — U.S./Canadian westward expansion; commercial undressed-hide market with repeating rifles nearly extinguished buffalo by the 1880s; contributed_to [[events/red-river-war-1874]] and southern confinement.

## Major Phases

### 1. Pre-horse trade and dual adaptations (to late 17th c.)

Plains peoples linked via trade centers/fairs to Pacific Coast, Southwest, and east of Mississippi; Pueblo centers (Pecos, Piro, Taos) exchanged corn/cloth/turquoise for hides/meat. Calumet Dance diffused from Caddoans; Sun Dance arbor/altar linked to Pawnee/Arikara rites and self-torture to Mandan Okipa. Mid/late sixteenth-century Spanish explorers met pedestrian hunters (probably Apaches) using dogs.

### 2. Horse-and-gun reorganization and indigenous expansion (c. 1680–1830s)

- **Comanche** preeminence in Southwest raiding and southern uplands below the Platte until the 1830s; by 1750 horse trading probably outweighed bison hunting ([[processes/comanche-empire]]).
- **Osage** hegemony between Missouri and Red Rivers by ~1750, forcing Caddos, Pawnees, Wichitas; analogous_to Comanche expansion east of Comanche sphere.
- **Assiniboine–Cree** middleman expansion; Blackfeet–Gros Ventre–Sarsi drive of Shoshones south from 1740s; 1781 smallpox weakened Shoshones—by 1790s pushed west of Rockies ([[actors/blackfoot-confederacy]]).
- **Teton Sioux** equestrian “pirates of the Missouri” by 1760s; Lewis and Clark (1804) recognized Sioux as dominant power on the Missouri after smallpox devastated villagers ([[actors/lakota]]). Western expansion drove Kiowas south of Black Hills and Crows west to Powder/Yellowstone by 1825; Cheyenne–Arapaho alliance with Tetons dominated central uplands until 1870s. Nineteenth-century Teton warfare aimed not merely at horses/revenge but to exterminate rivals (Fowler).
- **Métis** ethnogenesis after 1821 HBC–NWC merger: Red River population 500 (1821) → 12,000 (1870) ([[actors/metis]]).

### 3. Villager subordination and Indian Territory pressure (c. 1825–1876)

Eastern removals pushed thousands into Osage/Kansa country; 1825 Congress created [[places/indian-territory|Indian Territory]]; 1854 Kansas-Nebraska Act excised land for territories. Pawnees: ~20,000 mid-eighteenth century → ~6,200 (1850) → 2,400 (1869); full relocation to IT by 1876 under Teton pressure (~8:1 outnumbered), game collapse, and failed protection. Osages, Kansas, Iowas, Oto-Missouris, Poncas (forced march 1877) similarly removed. After 1837 smallpox, Mandans/Hidatsas founded Like-a-Fishhook (1845); Arikaras joined 1862 under Teton pressure; 1880 Fort Berthold halved for Northern Pacific. Warfare elevated war chiefs over hereditary civil authority—villagers especially vulnerable when settlers crossed the Missouri in the 1840s.

### 4. Nomad treaties, wars, and U.S. military conquest (c. 1851–1878)

Annexations and California gold drove trails through hunting grounds. 1851 Fort Laramie; 1853 Fort Atkinson; 1855 Judith River—defined “tribal” territories unrealistically; “touching the pen” later enabled fraud. Denver gold (1858) brought ~40,000 trespassers onto Arapaho/Cheyenne lands; **Sand Creek** (Nov 1864)—Colorado militia massacred peaceful Cheyennes and Arapahos ordered by Army to camp there; aftermath major multi-tribal offensive into 1865 ([[processes/southern-plains-indian-wars]]). Dakota War 1862 (Santee/Dakota): over 500 settlers killed; 39 executed; all Santees expelled. Indian scouts after mid-1860s (Pawnee/Arikara/Hidatsa vs Sioux; Crows; Osages vs Cheyennes; IT villagers vs Comanches). 1867 southern reservation agreements ([[events/medicine-lodge-treaty-1867]]); by 1875 hunger drove ~2,500 Comanches and Kiowas to reservation between Canadian and Red Rivers ([[events/red-river-war-1874]]). Powder River war → 1868 Great Sioux Reservation; Black Hills gold 1874 → [[events/battle-of-little-bighorn-1876]] → winter campaigns 1876–77 → fraudulent 1876 Black Hills cession. By **1878 all American Plains Natives forced onto reservations**.

### 5. Canadian Plains: numbered treaties and 1885 (c. 1869–1885)

Canada negotiated seven numbered treaties 1871–77 (six involving Plains Indians)—Native parties often instigated agricultural-aid demands; Canadian motives included cheap settlement/railroad and avoiding U.S.-style wars amid disappearing buffalo. Métis Red River resistance 1869 (Louis Riel); Northwest Rebellion 1885 crushed by 8,000 troops; Cree leaders arrested to break treaty-pressure movement. Endpoint: expropriation of resources for federal governments and corporations while excluding Natives from regional development—yet on reservations peoples struggled with remarkable success to retain group identity and pursue political independence and economic self-sufficiency against odds (Fowler).

## Geographic Spread

U.S. and Canadian Great Plains from the lower Missouri and Red River south through Texas borderlands to the Saskatchewan and Alberta foothills; Black Hills, Powder River, Canadian–Red River corridor, and Red River of the North as strategic nodes.

## End Conditions

Military-ecological confinement of free equestrian life: U.S. by ~1878; Canadian free plains war complex crushed 1885. Buffalo nearly extinct by 1880s. Process end is **not** cultural end—reservation-era identity politics continue.
