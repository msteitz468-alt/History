# The Byzantine Monetary System

## Definition and Scope

For more than a millennium Byzantium maintained a **durable and flexible monetary system**,
guaranteed by the state, that reduced transaction costs across a large domestic market and
gave the [[byzantine-economy|Byzantine economy]] one of its principal comparative
advantages. Its anchor was the **gold *solidus* / *nomisma***, kept close to full purity
from Constantine I into the eleventh century — the **"dollar of the Middle Ages"** (R. S.
Lopez's phrase), an international reserve and trade currency in the eastern Mediterranean
alongside the Fatimid dinar. Because tax was payable in coin, the system also drove an
unusually **high monetization** of the countryside, and with it the early appearance of
salaried labour. This page follows Laiou and Morrisson,
[[laiou-morrisson-byzantine-economy-2007|*The Byzantine Economy*]] (2007), and Cécile
Morrisson's numismatic work.

## Major Phases

### Late antiquity: the solidus system
The gold *solidus* circulated with its 1/2 and 1/3 fractions, down through the copper
*follis* to the tiny *nummus* (worth a loaf of bread). Coins could move in sealed bags of 72
to 7,200 pieces (1–100 lb of gold). More than a million solidi were reportedly spent on
Hagia Sophia; the banker Julianus Argentarius spent 26,000 on San Vitale at Ravenna. Coin
finds proportional to population attest deep monetization even in the countryside.

### Seventh–eighth-century contraction and simplification
The seventh-century crisis shrank the fractional gold, cut copper weights, and — with the
loss of Balkan and eastern-Anatolian mines and a gold/silver-ratio gap with the Caliphate —
drove sharp monetary contraction. Gold-coin hoards fell from ~95 (6th–7th c.) to 11
(700–850). The Isaurians created the silver **miliaresion** (competing with the Arab dirhem),
and the system simplified to roughly one denomination per metal: gold *nomisma* (avg. 97%
fine), silver *miliaresion* (1/12 *nomisma*), copper *follis* (1/24 *miliaresion*). Minting
centralized on Constantinople as the western mints (Rome to 776, Ravenna to 751, Syracuse to
879) went autonomous and debased — Sicilian gold fineness fell from 97% to as low as 10%
under Basil I.

### Re-monetization and "creeping devaluation" (9th–10th c.)
From the 830s coin-find indices at Athens, Corinth and Sparta multiply severalfold; die-count
analysis shows rising issue volume from the 950s. Gold purity drifted gently down — 97% (9th
c.) → 92.1% (977–1001) — a "creeping devaluation" achieved by not refining newly-mined gold,
expanding the money supply ~0.2%/year. **Nikephoros II Phokas** (963–9) struck a light
*tetarteron* (22 vs 24 carats) and tried to force its use while collecting tax in the heavier
coin; merchants passed the cost on in prices — an early demonstration that the state could
not fully command the market.

### Eleventh-century debasement and the Komnenian reform (1092)
Debasement accelerated in three phases: a "creeping" phase (950s–1030s, ~−0.04%/yr, fineness
~94–90%); a steeper phase under Constantine IX to Romanos IV (1041–71, ~−0.4%/yr, down to
~70%); and a collapse after 1068 under Michael VII "**Parapinakes**" (a *nomisma* now bought
only ¾ of a *modios* of wheat) — gold content falling to ~10%, "gold" coins carrying up to
18% copper and 71% silver. Increasing coin diameter to preserve weight produced the
characteristic broad, thin, concave **scyphate** ("cup-shaped") fabric.
**[[alexios-i-komnenos|Alexios I Komnenos]]** reformed the coinage in **1092** (first
elucidated by M. Hendy, 1969), creating the fire-refined gold **hyperpyron** (~20.5 carats),
an electrum *trachy aspron* (1/3), a billon *aspron trachy* ("stamenon"), and copper
*tetartera*. The *hyperpyron* stayed broadly stable through the twelfth century (drifting to
~82% fine by 1204), though the subsidiary denominations debased and their volatility
confused crossing Crusader armies.

Morrisson argues the earlier (pre-1070) debasement did *not* clearly harm the economy: the
extra coin struck was roughly matched by growth in monetized transactions, with some price
stability — a partly rational response to economic growth, not pure fiscal desperation.

### Debasement to collapse and the silver standard (13th–15th c.)
Per George Pachymeres, the *hyperpyron* fell from ~16/24 carats under John III Vatatzes to
15/24 (Michael VIII, funding payments to the Italians), 14/24 (Andronikos II), to ~12/24 by
c.1308. Gold-coin output dwindled through the 1320s; **after 1353 gold coinage ceased**,
ending the thousand-year *solidus*/*nomisma* (John V struck one known ceremonial gold florin,
1354). In **1367** a silver system replaced it: the *hyperpyron* became a money of account,
payable in large silver **stavrata** (modelled on the Angevin *gigliato*) — a "double
reversal," since the West had returned to gold from the mid-thirteenth century. The
[[latin-empire-of-constantinople|Latin Empire]]'s coinage had been purely imitative (billon
*stamena* with 0.15% silver). The **Venetian ducat** (1282) displaced the *hyperpyron* in
long-distance trade from the 1350s; by the late fourteenth century coins of actual Byzantine
issue circulated only in Constantinople and its hinterland, amid a "competitive debasement"
of imitation silver ducats from which Byzantium, excluded from seigniorage, gained nothing.
George Gemistos Plethon (1420) complained of being forced to use foreign copper "for which
others reap the profit, whereas we retain only the ridicule."

## Denomination Tables (as given by Laiou & Morrisson)

**Komnenian reformed system (1092–1204)** — 1 gold *hyperpyron* = 3 electrum *trachy aspron*
= 48 billon *aspron trachy* (stamenon) = higher multiples of copper *tetartera*.

**Late system, three stages (Table 5.1):**
- *Post-Komnenian* (1204–1304): gold *hyperpyron* (~4.3 g, 75→50% Au) = 12 silver *trachy
  aspron* = 288 *aspron trachy* (stamenon) = 576 *tetartera*.
- *Westernized Palaiologan* (1304–67): *hyperpyron* = 12 silver *basilikon* = 24 half-
  *basilikon* = 96 billon *tornese/politikon* = 384 *stamena trachia*.
- *Silver hyperpyron / stavraton* (1367–1453): *hyperpyron* (account) = 2 silver *stavraton*
  (~8 g, 95% Ag) = 4 half-*stavraton* = 16 *doukatopoulon* = 192 *tornese*.

## Interaction

The system was the monetary face of the [[byzantine-economy|Byzantine economy]] and of the
[[actors/byzantine-empire|state's]] fiscal apparatus. Its late collapse both reflected and
accelerated Byzantium's subordination within the
[[medieval-europe-asia-trade|Italian-dominated Mediterranean trade system]]. Compare the
antecedent [[late-roman-coinage|late Roman coinage]] and the broader story of
[[coinage|coinage]]; contrast the [[carolingian-coinage-reform|Carolingian]] silver-penny
regime of the medieval West.
