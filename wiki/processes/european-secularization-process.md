## Definition and Scope

The sharp, sustained decline in Christian religious observance, belief, and
institutional authority across Western Europe from roughly 1965 to the end of the
twentieth century. It is not identical to the separation of church and state (a
legal/constitutional process that is one contributing factor among several) nor to
irreligion as such — most Western Europeans continued to self-identify as Christian
even as regular observance collapsed. By the end of the century the region could be
described as **post-Christian**: Christianity continued to shape holidays, identity,
and life-cycle rites (marriage, baptism, burial) in a diffuse way, but organized
religion had become the private faith of a minority rather than the cornerstone of
public life.

## Causal Drivers

- **The cultural revolution of the 1960s** — the same shift toward individual
  freedom, self-expression, and choice over constituted authority that drove
  [[second-wave-feminism-in-europe]] and the sexual revolution undermined religious
  authority on marriage, sexuality, and family. This is identified by Paxton and
  Hessler as the primary driver, in the cultural rather than institutional sphere.
- **Disestablishment of state churches (1970s–1990s)** — new constitutions
  progressively eliminated language identifying a nation's confessional orientation
  (Greece as Orthodox, Ireland/Italy/Spain/Portugal as Catholic, Iceland as
  Lutheran); governments relinquished roles in appointing bishops. Notably,
  disestablishment correlates with religious *identification* (church-tax rolls) more
  than with religious *practice* — comparison of Denmark (established church, 88%
  nominal affiliation) and the Netherlands (disestablished, 46% nominal affiliation)
  in the 1999 European Values Study shows the Dutch were consistently more devout by
  practice (church attendance, prayer, volunteering) despite lower nominal
  affiliation.
- **Vatican II's ambiguous effect** — see [[vatican-ii]]. Reform modernized Catholic
  worship and doctrine but did not halt, and by some readings hastened, departure
  from strict observance (e.g., reaction to *Humanae Vitae*, 1968, encouraged
  selective adherence even among committed Catholics).
- **Loss of political leverage tied to authoritarian regimes** — in Spain and
  Portugal, mass attendance fell sharply only after the Franco and Salazar
  dictatorships ended, once church attendance ceased to be a prerequisite for career
  advancement (Spanish weekly mass attendance: 64% in 1970, 31% by 1983).

## Major Phases

1. **Postwar religious revival (1945–early 1960s)** — moral revulsion against Nazism
   strengthened churches; Protestant theologians (Karl Barth, Dietrich Bonhoeffer)
   and popularizers (Bishop John Robinson's *Honest to God*, 1963) sought renewal;
   Pope John XXIII convened Vatican II (1962–1965).
2. **Institutional modernization (1947–1970s)** — women's ordination beginning with
   a 1947 Danish Lutheran parish (state-driven in Scandinavia); Vatican II's
   vernacular liturgy, ecumenism, and religious-freedom doctrine.
3. **Sharp decline (post-1965)** — church attendance, confirmations, and ministry
   entrants fall steeply; civil ceremonies partially displace religious rites; only
   17% of Europeans in the 1980s–90s considered passing religious faith to children
   important, versus 40%+ who called religion personally important — evidence of the
   privatization of belief.
4. **Partial stabilization under John Paul II (1978–2005)** — a charismatic,
   theologically conservative papacy slowed Catholic departure without reversing the
   underlying trend.

## Geographic Spread

Most advanced in Protestant northern Europe and militantly secular France;
significantly slower in Catholic Ireland (Europe's most religious country by end of
century) and Orthodox Greece (98% Orthodox self-identification in the 1950s).
Southern/Mediterranean Catholic countries show a distinctive pattern: political
liberalization (end of authoritarian rule), not cultural change alone, triggered
the steepest observance declines.

## Interaction

Runs parallel to and is often analytically linked with [[second-wave-feminism-in-europe]]
and the broader sexual revolution — both are treated by Paxton and Hessler as facets
of the same post-1960s cultural shift privileging individual autonomy over inherited
authority. Also interacts with [[postwar-immigration-to-western-europe]]: the growth
of Islam as Western Europe's second-largest religion occurred simultaneously with
Christian decline, producing distinctive tensions (e.g., the French headscarf
affair) between a secularizing majority culture and religiously assertive minority
communities.

## End Conditions

Ongoing as of the source's 2011 publication date; no reversal is evident. Some
scholars (cited in Paxton/Hessler) challenge the classic secularization model
(which linked religious decline mechanically to industrialization and
modernization) on the grounds that it cannot explain American religiosity despite
comparable modernization, and note that eclectic spirituality and occult interest
in the 1980s–1990s complicate any simple narrative of triumphant scientific
rationality replacing religion.

## Historiography

Paxton and Hessler's ch. 20 synthesis draws on the European Values Study and
Eurostat data alongside Hugh McLeod and Werner Ustorf's edited volume *The Decline
of Christendom in Western Europe* (2003) and Philip Jenkins's *God's Continent*
(2007). Owen Chadwick's *The Christian Church in the Cold War* (1992) is cited as an
authoritative short survey. Collection coverage of post-1970 European religious
history is otherwise essentially absent from this wiki — a genuine gap consistent
with the collection's general weakness on the post-1970 period.
