# Japanese Environmental History (c. 1580–1900)

The history of [[japan|Japan's]] **relationship with its natural environment** across the
[[edo-period|early-modern]] and nineteenth-century periods — the ecological underside of the
[[tokugawa-economy|Tokugawa economy]], read in a **Pacific context**. From Brett L. Walker's
chapter in the [[howell-early-modern-japan-asia-world|New Cambridge History of Japan Vol. II]]
(ch. 9).

## Definition and Scope

Walker situates early-modern Japan within the **Pacific environment** — its tectonics and
seismicity, its mountainous, ore-rich spine and fertile alluvial plains, its temperate climate,
and its **thriving coastal waters**. Nature, he argues, "permeated Japanese political life":
the most legitimate governments were those that **conformed to the natural rhythms** of that
environment (if only by analogy). This makes environmental history not a marginal topic but a
frame for understanding Tokugawa political legitimacy, economy, and culture.

## Causal Drivers and Major Themes

- **The marine–agricultural metabolism.** Walker stresses a striking **transfer of energy from
  the Pacific to Japan's fields**: rising agricultural productivity (especially commercial
  cash-cropping) depended on **marine fertilizers** — herring mulch, whale oil, and (with human
  biowaste) the recycling of urban nightsoil. Pacific currents carried nutrients up the food
  chain to Japan's urban consumer markets, tying the [[tokugawa-economy|commercial economy]] to
  the ocean's ecology.
- **Forests and resource management.** Population and urban growth and the demand for
  construction timber drove **deforestation** in the seventeenth century, met by increasingly
  sophisticated forest management and reforestation — an early case of conservation under
  resource pressure.
- **Famine.** The other edge of Japan's weather: ample precipitation enabled cash-crop farming,
  but the eighteenth-century climatic vagaries brought devastating **famines** (the **Kan'ei**
  famine of 1638–43; the **Tenmei** famine of 1782–88, one of the worst in Japanese history,
  which fueled mass uprisings and political blame against the **Tanuma** regime). Famine ties
  environmental history to [[tokugawa-economy|economic]] and political crisis.

## The Meiji rupture: from conformity to control

Walker's central argument is a **shift in the human–nature relationship** with
[[meiji-modernization|Meiji modernization]]: planners "replaced conformity with nature … with
**control over nature**." His emblem is the **Ueno Zoological Garden**, where nature was
mastered "by spectacle" and Japan's place "at the top of the natural heap" enacted as a public
performance of empire. The **annihilation of the Japanese and Hokkaido wolves** within decades
of 1868 — sweeping aside centuries of wolf worship rooted in Buddhist, Confucian, and Shintō
belief — exemplifies how "East Asian ideas about nature crumbled quickly under the … material
requirements of [[industrialization|industrialization]]."

## Interaction and End Conditions

Japanese environmental history connects the [[tokugawa-economy|Tokugawa economy]] (its marine
and forest resource base), [[meiji-modernization|Meiji modernization]] (the turn to mastery),
and [[industrialization|industrialization]]. Walker closes on a present-day note: Japan's
industrial path "planted the seeds" of the climate disruption, extinctions, and sea-level rise
of the [[anthropocene|high-modernist age]] — a lesson, he writes (recalling the 2011 Tōhoku
tsunami's "obliterated seawalls"), that "nature is not controllable."
