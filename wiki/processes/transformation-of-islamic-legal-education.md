## Definition and Scope

This process describes the structural replacement of [[madrasa]]-based Islamic legal training with state-controlled modern law faculties and the consequent transformation — and ultimate marginalization — of the [[ulama]] as the authoritative bearers of juristic knowledge. It is a critical component of what Hallaq calls the [[destruction-of-the-sharia-thesis|structural destruction of the Sharīa]].

## Causal Drivers

The process was enabled by and ran parallel to [[waqf]] confiscation (the financial base of independent madrasa education), [[codification-of-islamic-law]] (which made traditional jurisprudence formally redundant in state courts), and the replacement of the [[qadi-judiciary]] with salaried civil judges in modern hierarchical court systems.

## Major Phases

### Ottoman and Egyptian Precedents (1826–1920)
The critical inaugural moment was 1826, when the Ottoman state placed waqf under the Imperial Ministry of Endowments. This severed the financial independence of traditional legal education. In the same year, the first modern law school was established in Istanbul (1876). Madrasa training did not immediately disappear but was progressively marginalized as state courts began operating according to codified law that required modern legal training, not classical [[usul-al-fiqh]] methodology.

Muḥammad ʿAlī's Egypt replicated this trajectory: madrasa endowments were seized, and French-trained lawyers became the new legal elite. By the 1920s, Egyptian legal professionals considered the fiqh "culturally remote, juristically complex and a judicially obscure system of rules."

### Nationalist Reconfiguration (1920–1970)
Post-independence states continued and deepened the colonial pattern:

- **Turkey (1926)**: Madrasa system abolished; all legal education moved to secular faculties.
- **Iran (1927–31)**: Pahlavi Shah confiscated ulama waqfs, placed their administration under the Ministry of Education; any retained ulama personnel were henceforth paid civil servants, "depriving them of their traditional independence."
- **Egypt (1961)**: Nasser's "reform" of al-Azhar introduced scientific subjects (engineering, medicine) into the curriculum, nationalizing its administration and making the appointment of the Shaykh al-Azhar a presidential prerogative. Al-Azhar grew in enrollment (from 65,000 students in 1963 to nearly 1 million in 1993) but its legal function was extinguished.

### Fragmentation of Authority (1970–present)
By the 1970s, the educational transformation had produced a fragmented landscape:

- The surviving **ulama** function now "nearly exclusively [as] educational (i.e., not legal in the sense that obtained before the nineteenth century)." They "remain largely dissociated from other technical professions" and, paradoxically, may drift toward Islamism when exposed to modern scientific education ("techno-Azharites").
- **Islamists**, the new mass religious-political force, are "trained in a wide variety of modern technical disciplines, ranging from engineering and medicine to accounting, business and teaching in 'secular' schools." They lack classical training: "having shed the mantle of traditional juristic and hermeneutical authority, the Islamists do not feel bound by the cultural and epistemic systems developed throughout Islamic intellectual and legal history."
- The proliferation of **internet fatwas** is the direct structural consequence: "The recent proliferation of fatwās on the Internet, in print media and in video-recordings attests to a multifarious production of 'religious knowledge' that has consistently lacked any axis of authority."

## Structural Consequences

The educational transformation is one of the clearest demonstrations of the [[destruction-of-the-sharia-thesis]]: it destroyed the *reproductive mechanism* of Sharīa knowledge, not just the institutional structures. The Arabicate hermeneutical tradition transmitted through living scholars, the apprenticeship model of juristic formation, the integration of legal with theological, logical, and linguistic disciplines — all of these were terminated by the substitution of university-style law faculties for the madrasa tradition.

The Egyptian SCC's 1993 definition of Sharīa (broad principles applicable by any judge with "basic knowledge of Islamic fiqh" but "sufficiently trained in modern law") makes the epistemological rupture explicit: the classical hermeneutic tradition — its prerequisites, disciplines, and training methods — was declared unnecessary. As Hallaq notes, such judges are typically characterized by "well-nigh ignorance of fiqh, of Quranic exegesis, of ḥadīth … and even of basic skills in classical legal Arabic."
