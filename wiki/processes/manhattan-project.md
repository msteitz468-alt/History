# Manhattan Project

The United States' wartime program (with British and Canadian
participation) to develop nuclear weapons, run under military direction by
General Leslie Groves with [[oppenheimer-j-robert|J. Robert Oppenheimer]] as
scientific director of the Los Alamos Laboratory, culminating in the first
nuclear detonation and the atomic bombings that ended the Second World War
in the Pacific.

## Definition and Scope

The project encompassed a vast, compartmentalized industrial and scientific
effort: uranium enrichment facilities at Oak Ridge, Tennessee; plutonium
production reactors at Hanford, Washington; and weapons design and assembly
at Los Alamos, New Mexico, employing well over 100,000 people at its peak
while maintaining strict secrecy even from most participants about the
project's overall purpose.

## Causal Drivers

The program was launched partly from fear — voiced in the 1939
Einstein-Szilárd letter to Roosevelt — that Nazi Germany might develop
nuclear weapons first, and was sustained by essentially unlimited wartime
funding and priority access to scarce materials and scientific talent,
including many émigré physicists who had fled fascist Europe.

## Major Phases

Early theoretical and organizational work (1939–1942) gave way to
full industrial mobilization after 1942, culminating in the successful
Trinity test in the New Mexico desert on 16 July 1945, followed weeks later
by the operational bombings of Hiroshima (6 August) and Nagasaki (9 August
1945).

## Geographic Spread

Though centered in the United States, the project drew on British and
Canadian scientific contributions (formalized in the 1943 Quebec Agreement)
and depended on uranium ore supplies sourced internationally, including from
the Belgian Congo.

## Interaction

The project's success directly launched the nuclear arms race that defined
Cold War strategic competition between the United States and Soviet Union,
whose own weapons program (aided by espionage against Manhattan Project
secrets) achieved its first test by 1949.

## End Conditions

The project formally concluded with the transfer of nuclear weapons
development to civilian control under the Atomic Energy Commission (1946),
though its wartime organizational model and personnel continued directly
into postwar nuclear weapons programs.
