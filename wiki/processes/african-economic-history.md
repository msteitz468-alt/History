## Definition and Scope

African economic history as a scholarly field traces the long-run performance of African economies from the pre-colonial era through to the contemporary period. As Morten Jerven argues in [[parker-reid-oxford-modern-african-history-2013]], the field has been characterized by two successive orientations: an earlier generation of historians focused on demonstrating the rationality, agency, and market participation of African actors; a more recent wave, dominated by economists, has focused on explaining persistent divergence and "chronic failure of growth" relative to other world regions. Jerven argues that this framing has produced a distorted picture: African economic history is in fact "characterized by dynamism — as opposed to persistence, and diversity in outcomes across time and space — in contrast to the average stagnation that has prompted the economic literature."

The field covers aggregate economic growth, the slave trades and their economic consequences, cash-crop revolutions, colonial political economy, postcolonial development strategies, structural adjustment, and the contemporary growth upturn. It is methodologically contested, with historians emphasizing archival source criticism and institutional change over time, while economists favour large cross-country datasets and econometric techniques. Data quality is a fundamental constraint: reliable GDP per capita estimates for most African economies only exist from c.1950, and pre-1960 figures are largely projections.

## Causal Drivers

**The slave trades**: The Atlantic and Indian Ocean slave trades removed millions of productive people from African economies. Slave-trading states such as Asante, Dahomey, and Oyo captured economic rents through participation, but the longer-run demographic and institutional costs were substantial. Labour scarcity was the continent's defining economic constraint for most of its history.

**Transition to legitimate commerce**: After Britain banned the slave trade in 1807, a transition to "legitimate commerce" — export of primary agricultural and mineral products — occurred unevenly across regions. The "crisis of adaptation" thesis, which held that the abolition of slave exports fundamentally disrupted African political economies, is now regarded as overstated. In West Africa, peasant farmers drove a cash-crop revolution in groundnuts, palm oil, cocoa, and rubber with surprising speed. The Sokoto Caliphate redirected slave labour toward domestic production rather than export, minimizing disruption.

**Colonial political economy**: Colonial regimes shaped African economies through two main mechanisms: (1) coercion — taxes, land alienation, and forced labour that compelled engagement with colonial markets; and (2) selective development — investment in infrastructure (railways, ports) and marketing institutions that served export extraction. In settler colonies (South Africa, Kenya, Rhodesias, Algeria), land alienation and crop restrictions artificially suppressed African peasant competition and depressed real wages. In peasant colonies (Gold Coast, much of French West Africa), the colonial state had less leverage, and African smallholders drove export growth largely despite, not because of, state intervention. Cocoa production on the Gold Coast from the 1890s was "fundamentally an entrepreneurial response by African peasants."

**Marketing boards**: Late colonial marketing boards, originally designed to stabilize commodity prices, became the primary mechanism through which the postcolonial state extracted revenue from peasant agriculture. After independence, states used marketing board surpluses to finance import-substitution industrialization and expanding state payrolls. In many cases — Ghana in the 1960s–70s being paradigmatic — boards set producer prices at a fraction of world market prices, generating predatory rent-seeking and disincentivizing production.

**Structural adjustment**: The economic crises of the late 1970s–80s forced most African states to accept IMF- and World Bank-sponsored structural adjustment programs, which curtailed state intervention in markets, devalued currencies, and reduced subsidies. The impact was contractionary in the short run; the long-run effects remain debated.

**External demand cycles**: African economic performance has been tightly linked to commodity export demand. Periods of sustained growth coincide with strong external demand for minerals and agricultural products; stagnation follows demand collapses.

## Major Phases

**Pre-colonial growth (to c.1880)**: Extensive growth predominated — more people using more land. Internal markets for agricultural goods, handicrafts, textiles, metals, and currencies were widespread. Trans-Saharan trade linked North and sub-Saharan Africa; Indian Ocean trade connected eastern and southern Africa to Asian markets. New cultigens from Asia (banana) and the Americas (cassava, maize) had substantial productivity effects. The slave trades both concentrated wealth in certain states and imposed long-run demographic costs.

**Cash-crop revolution (c.1880–1930)**: The commercial transition to legitimate exports accelerated under colonial rule. Cocoa, groundnuts, palm oil, coffee, tobacco, cotton, and rubber all expanded dramatically. This was largely peasant-led and family-labour-based. Per capita incomes rose in both Kenya and Ghana during this period; real wages also increased in British Africa. Colonial states captured rents through taxation rather than directing production.

**Late colonial industrialization and marketing boards (1930–1960)**: The Great Depression and Second World War forced partial import-substitution industrialization. Marketing boards provided colonial governments their first systematic revenue base. By independence, African economies showed wide variation: Sierra Leone and Zambia were mineral-rich; the Belgian Congo had a broader industrial base; the Gold Coast was specialized in cocoa; Sahelian territories had few resources of either kind.

**Postcolonial growth and crisis (1960–1990)**: By 1967–8, half of African economies were experiencing sustained rapid growth. From the early 1970s, oil shocks and commodity price collapses reversed these trends. The 1980s saw generalized economic failure across sub-Saharan Africa — "sustained growth failure was the rule rather than the exception." Only Botswana and Mauritius maintained robust growth throughout this period.

**Recovery and contemporary growth (1990–present)**: Since the mid-1990s, growth has returned to most African economies. This upturn is more dependent on mineral and commodity exports — and thus on external demand — than earlier growth episodes, raising questions about sustainability and structural diversification.

## Geographic Spread

The process operated at continental scale but with significant regional variation. West African forest zones led the cash-crop revolution; settler colonies in eastern and southern Africa had more coercive colonial economies and slower distributional gains. Sahelian and equatorial Central African territories were economically marginal under colonial rule. South Africa followed a distinctive trajectory — highly industrialized by African standards but organized around racial exclusion of black workers from skill-intensive employment and land ownership.

## End Conditions

The process is ongoing. The field itself has been reinvigorated since c.2000 by economists working on institutional economics and the "new African economic history." The central challenge identified by Jerven is to explain growth and its political distribution, not — as in the pessimistic paradigm of the 1980s–90s — to explain underdevelopment as a fixed condition.

## Historiography

**Source quality**: Pre-1950 economic data for Africa are extremely limited. GDP per capita estimates are largely projections and assumptions extrapolated from trade statistics and population figures. Export data are more reliable than production data. The Maddison dataset provides single-year Africa-wide estimates before 1950 that obscure enormous regional variation. Quantitative approaches systematically understate pre-colonial and early colonial economic change.

**Scholarly debates**: (1) The "crisis of adaptation" thesis (Hopkins 1973; contested by subsequent scholarship) — whether abolition of the slave trade caused structural economic crisis for African polities. The consensus is that it was overstated. (2) The "reversal of fortune" thesis (Acemoglu, Johnson, Robinson 2001–2) — that colonial institutional choices determine current income levels. Challenged by African historians as a "compression of history" that ignores variation over time (Austin). (3) Whether colonial rule was economically harmful or beneficial. The evidence suggests mixed effects: peasant colonies benefited from market access; settler colonies suppressed African living standards; extractive regimes in equatorial Central Africa were purely destructive. (4) The causes of postcolonial stagnation: predatory state extraction vs. external shocks vs. institutional pathologies vs. adverse terms of trade.

**Methodological approaches**: Economic history (archival + quantitative), development economics (cross-country datasets, instrumental variables), political economy, social history of labour. A tension between economists seeking "portable" causal claims and historians emphasizing context, contingency, and institutional change over time remains unresolved.

**Recent revisionism**: The turn since 2000 toward explaining growth episodes — rather than growth failure — represents the major reorientation. Jerven's work on data quality challenges the empirical foundations of many cross-country growth studies applied to Africa. The renewed attention to precolonial economies and internal markets corrects an earlier over-emphasis on external linkages.

**Collection coverage**: The Bibliotheca Alexandrina is weak on post-1750 Africa generally. The Parker–Reid Oxford Handbook provides this entry's primary sourcing. For depth, specialist monographs in the Brill and Routledge series, and A. G. Hopkins's An Economic History of West Africa (1973), would need separate ingestion.
