# Dissolution of the Soviet Union
## Definition and Scope
The **Dissolution of the Soviet Union** was the political process that led to the fragmentation of the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics (USSR) into fifteen independent sovereign states. Spanning from the late 1980s to December 25, 1991, the collapse was driven by the rise of nationalisms, economic disintegration, and the failure of Mikhail Gorbachev's reform policies. The process concluded with the Belovezha Accords, the establishment of the Commonwealth of Independent States (CIS), and Gorbachev's resignation, marking the end of the Cold War and the global bipolar international system.

## Causal Drivers
- **Unchained Nationalisms:** The policy of **[[glasnost|Glasnost]]** allowed non-Russian republics to openly express long-suppressed historical grievances against Moscow.
- **Economic Collapse:** The failures of **[[perestroika|Perestroika]]** produced severe shortages of food and consumer goods, inflation, and a breakdown of trade networks, eroding the population's faith in the Soviet system.
- **Rivalry between Gorbachev and Yeltsin:** Boris Yeltsin, as President of the Russian SFSR, championed Russian sovereignty and cooperated with non-Russian republics to dismantle the central Soviet institutions in his struggle for power against Gorbachev.
- **Loss of Eastern Europe (1989):** Gorbachev's decision not to intervene in the peaceful revolutions that overthrew communist regimes in Eastern Europe severely weakened the credibility and authority of the Soviet center.

## Major Phases
- **The Baltic Vanguard (1988–1990):** Estonia, Latvia, and Lithuania led the way by organizing "People's Fronts," declaring the right to reject Soviet laws (Estonia, November 1988), and asserting that their 1940 incorporation into the USSR was null and void. On March 11, 1990, Lithuania declared full independence (Line 7057).
- **The War of Laws (1990):** Following elections, many republics, including the giant Russian SFSR under Boris Yeltsin (June 1990) and Ukraine (July 1990), issued declarations of state sovereignty, asserting that their local laws took precedence over Soviet laws.
- **Transcaucasian Conflicts (1988–1991):** Nationalisms took a violent turn in Transcaucasia, leading to ethnic clashes between Armenia and Azerbaijan over Nagorny Karabakh, and military crackdowns by Soviet forces (such as the Tbilisi massacre in April 1989 and the Baku intervention in January 1990).
- **The August Coup and Final Collapse (1991):** In August 1991, conservative communist hardliners staged a coup to block Gorbachev's new Union Treaty. The coup collapsed within three days due to popular resistance led by Yeltsin. The collapse of the coup discredited the Party and the police, prompting all remaining republics to declare independence.
- **Formal Dissolution (December 1991):** On December 8, 1991, the leaders of Russia (Yeltsin), Ukraine (Kravchuk), and Belarus (Shushkevich) signed the Belovezha Accords, declaring the USSR dissolved. On December 21, the Alma-Ata Protocol expanded this to eleven republics, forming the Commonwealth of Independent States. Gorbachev resigned on December 25, 1991, and the Soviet flag was lowered from the Kremlin.

## Geographic Spread
The collapse affected all fifteen union republics of the USSR, spanning Eastern Europe (Russia, Ukraine, Belarus, Moldova), the Baltic region (Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania), Transcaucasia (Georgia, Armenia, Azerbaijan), and Central Asia (Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan, Turkmenistan, Uzbekistan).

## Interaction
- **With the Russian Republic:** Yeltsin's Russian SFSR refused to support the central Soviet government, utilizing Russian nationalism and institutions to accelerate the dissolution of the center (Line 7087).
- **With the Economy:** The dissolution led to a sudden rupture of integrated trade and industrial links across the former republics, causing severe economic depression and inflation in all post-Soviet states.

## End Conditions
The process ended with the formal de jure dissolution of the Soviet Union on December 26, 1991. The Russian Federation, led by Boris Yeltsin, assumed the USSR's seat at the United Nations Security Council and inherited its nuclear arsenal, embarking on the difficult construction of a new state.
