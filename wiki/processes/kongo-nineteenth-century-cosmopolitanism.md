## Definition and Scope

Vos (ch. 10 of Bostoen & Brinkman eds., *The Kongo Kingdom*, Cambridge 2018) analyses the nineteenth-century history of the [[kingdom-of-kongo|Kingdom of Kongo]] through the frameworks of **cosmopolitanism** and **extraversion** — the latter concept drawn from Jean-François Bayart's political sociology of the African state. The central argument is that Kongo elites actively engaged with the Atlantic world across the nineteenth century as a strategy for accumulating wealth and power at home, using three main "extraversive" practices: adoption of the Order of Christ as a politico-religious institution, control of imported textiles as currency for patronage, and alliance with the Portuguese imperial state. Each of these practices had deep pre-colonial roots but was transformed — ultimately into dependency — by the colonial moment of the 1880s–1900s.

The process is significant because it complicates teleological narratives of African passivity. Kongo elites were not passive victims of European expansion; they were active agents who sought out external partnerships. The paradox, as Bayart argued, is that this very agency helped constitute the conditions of their later subordination.

---

## Definition of Extraversion

Vos builds on Bayart's concept of **extraversion**: African governing elites who, finding their domestic accumulation of wealth and power constrained, mobilised "resources derived from their (possibly unequal) relationship with the external environment." In the post-colonial era these resources stem from diplomatic connections, foreign military supplies, and western education; in nineteenth-century Kongo they included Catholic priestly legitimacy, imported prestige goods (especially textiles), and Portuguese military backing. Extraversion should be distinguished from cosmopolitanism proper (participation in diverse global networks) and from mere "discovery" of the West (Northrup 2014); extraversion captures the strategic, rent-seeking dimension of elite engagement with outsiders.

## Phase 1: The Order of Christ as Religious-Political Extraversion (16th–19th c.)

Kongo's conversion to Christianity in the late fifteenth century (under Nzinga a Nkuwu, baptized João I, 1491, and developed by [[afonso-i-kongo|Afonso I]]) was "the kingdom's quintessential form of extraversion": the ruling class transformed an imported religion into a royal political cult that centralised power. The **Order of Christ** — the Portuguese crusader order — was domesticated by the Kongo nobility as a title association. Kongo rulers "routinely knighted their own nobles" (Fromont 2014) without Portuguese authorization. The cross became a symbol of authority used to protect against malevolent forces and establish tax-collection stations. Recent archaeological research in Ngongo Mbata confirms stone churches that, at the kingdom's seventeenth-century peak, linked important marketplaces within the orbit of Mbanza Kongo.

The Order outlived its usefulness in Portugal itself by the mid-seventeenth century but persisted in Kongo precisely because it maintained its politico-religious character. Investiture required an ordained priest, making the order dependent on periodic foreign clerical input even while the Kongo controlled the institution.

By the nineteenth century, Mbanza Kongo's political importance had declined relative to coastal broker-chiefs (see [[kongo-decentralization|Kongo Decentralization]]) who had used [[atlantic-slave-trade|the slave trade's]] decentralisation to accumulate material wealth. The kings at Mbanza Kongo functioned primarily as a religious centre, and access to senior titles still ran through the Order of Christ.

**Colonial transformation of the Order.** Three documented investiture ceremonies show the oath's changing content:
- *1856 (Sarmento)*: Knight swears to defend the Catholic faith, "respect only one God," support priests, and "persecute all idols and witchcraft."
- *1881 (Barroso)*: Emphasis shifts to obedience to "the king of Kongo and the king of Portugal."
- *c. 1902–1913 (Ladeiras)*: Oath demands respect for "the laws of the government."

Vos notes that the apparent change may reflect the perceptions of colonial-era witnesses rather than substantive ritual change: for Kongo, eradicating witchcraft from politics had always been part of effective governance; devotion to the Catholic faith was a declaration of political loyalty. But the functional outcome was that the Order became a mechanism through which the colonial state socialised the Kongo ruling class.

By 1900 the Order had "outlived its usefulness" in Kongo. A new fashion for colonial military titles emerged: Álvaro XIV (r. 1891–6) obtained the honorary rank of colonel; Henrique III (r. 1896–1901) a similar title; Pedro VI (r. 1901–10) was made major (1903) and then lieutenant colonel (1905). Pedro VI explicitly sought a military rank "to earn the respect of my subjects." The colonial government distributed these titles partly to socialise the African ruling class through its army (Ranger 1983), but the initiative often came from the recipients themselves.

## Phase 2: Textiles as Patronage Currency (17th–early 20th c.)

Along the entire African Atlantic coast, exotic textiles occupied a central place among barter goods on slaving vessels. In Kongo they served as the main currency for political patronage: imported in large bundles, textiles were traded inland for slaves, ivory, and later rubber, while brokers retained items for personal distribution to clients. Vos documents this through:

- *1794 cargo manifests*: The slaver *Enterprize* carried cotton bafts, romals, chelloes, calicos, bejutapauts, byrampauts, neganepauts, nicanees, photaes, Indian "long cloths," Guinea stuffs from Manchester, and worsted caps — a combination of Asian and European manufactures.
- *Tuckey 1816 at Boma*: A single slave was priced at two muskets, two casks of gunpowder, two jars of brandy, five knives, strings of beads, and an array of exotic textiles.
- *Tuckey at Malembo*: The hybrid dress of Kongo broker elites — a "singular medley of European and native costume" — used imported garments to signal cosmopolitan status to foreign trading partners while retaining local markers of aristocratic identity (animal-skin aprons worn as "a mark of gentility").

The most powerful coastal brokers could outstrip Mbanza Kongo's resources. In 1886, a Portuguese visitor to Vumpa found Dom Paulo, the chief broker, with "dozens of women and many slaves," a house filled with firearms, liquor, textiles, and European goods; he provided credit to other merchants and was "highly respected among all neighbouring populations and even by the European traders who live on his land."

Dom Pedro V (r. 1860–91), recognising this, used the abolitionist campaign and the rise of "legitimate commerce" to his advantage: inviting European commercial houses (the French House of Daumas, the Portuguese João Luís da Rosa, and a Dutch firm from Rotterdam) to open stores for rubber purchase at Mbanza Kongo in the early 1880s. Pedro collected tribute — cloth, guns — for land concessions, and distributed these to his client-chief network.

**Democratisation.** By the end of the nineteenth century, colonial-economy wages allowed young men employed as carriers for rubber factories to acquire imported textiles directly. A decade after Bentley arrived in 1879 to find only a handful of men in European dress, "cloth was to be earned by anyone who went to fetch our stores." The Portuguese administrator Leal (1915) lamented that the "proud chiefs of old times" now wore "imported jackets, chintz, and felt hats." But Vos reads this as democratisation of conspicuous consumption: the old slave-trading oligarchy's monopoly on prestige goods was broken.

## Phase 3: Alliance with Portugal and the Colonial Transition (mid-19th c.–1913)

The longer story of Kongo–Portugal relations in the nineteenth century is marked by a "reversal of status" (Newbury 2003): European powers pressed their former African patrons into subordinate positions. For Kongo:

- *1860*: Pedro V came to power with Portuguese military support — having ousted his rival Álvaro Ndongo from Mbanza Kongo with Luanda's help. This effectively made the royal court dependent on outside support.
- *1870*: Portugal withdrew its colonial army from Mbanza Kongo.
- *1880–85*: In the context of the European race for the Congo, Portugal sent Padre António Barroso on a "patriotic and religious" mission. Pedro V's claims to sovereignty over the Congo's south bank were a Portuguese trump card at the Berlin Conference (1884–5). The International Congo Association took the king's protest against their treaty with a lower-ranked Kongo chief "more seriously than is often assumed in the literature on the European 'scramble'" (Wauters 1884).
- *1888*: Portuguese colonial troops reoccupied the Kongo capital. The colonial government took away the king's authority to collect tribute and rents from trade caravans and factories. Pedro V became dependent on a government stipend and mission-distributed presents to maintain his client networks — "master or dupe" of the Portuguese alliance, simultaneously both.

**Key individual: Dom Álvaro de Agua Rosada (alias "Tangi")**
Born c. 1870, one of Pedro V's younger sons. Educated by Padre Barroso, taken to Lisbon in 1888 to improve his Portuguese. Appointed lieutenant of the colonial army's auxiliary troops (*segunda linha*). He facilitated Portuguese trade-factory establishment in eastern Kongo, recruited carriers, and offered local chiefs protection against the nearby Congo Free State — earning the sobriquet *mundele andombe* ("a black white man"). He married, accrued followers, and built a settlement along the rubber highway. Two of his sons later became successful businessmen in Congo-Kinshasa. Tangi represents the "cog" in the colonial system who could use a subordinate position to build his own clientele — though the chapter also notes that abuses by such intermediaries lay at the root of the 1913 Kongo revolt.

## Conclusion: From Cosmopolitanism to Dependency

Vos's analytical conclusion is measured: "gross asymmetries in the relations of power between Africa and the West emerged only in the second half of the nineteenth century, allowing European empires to reverse existing patron–client relationships." Before that reversal, European traders and missionaries were, in Northrup's phrase, the "subordinate party" on the African coast, dependent on African patrons. The debate over Bayart's stronger claim — that African elites were "active agents in the mise en dépendance of their societies" — remains open: Kongo history broadly supports the claim but also shows that the external relations pursued by Kongo elites "varied in nature and did not always entail dependency."

The broader population's eventual participation in the colonial economy as workers and cash-crop producers extended and popularised forms of extraversion that had begun as elite strategies. The "modern life-styles" pioneered by colonial-era Africans "borrowed as much from their ancestors as they did from Europeans."

## Historiography

**Sources.** Vos draws extensively on the Arquivo Nacional de Angola (ANA), the Arquivo Histórico Ultramarino (AHU), published missionary memoirs, and the Brásio *Monumenta Missionaria Africana* collection. British Baptist Missionary Society sources (especially Bentley 1900) and published traveller accounts (Tuckey 1818, de Sarmento 1880) supplement the Portuguese archive. The chapter explicitly acknowledges that Kongo's relations with non-Portuguese powers before the reign of Pedro V "remain insufficiently studied."

**Theoretical debates.** The chapter engages directly with Bayart (1993, 2000) on extraversion; Northrup (2014) and Getz (2012) on "discovery" and cosmopolitanism; Newbury (2003) on patron–client reversal; and Ranger (1983) on the colonial socialisation of African elites through military institutions. It steers between the "passive victim" narrative and the extreme agency-centred account, proposing that Kongo elites were rational strategic actors operating within a structural transformation that ultimately overwhelmed their agency.

**Periodisation.** The chapter's "nineteenth century" encompasses roughly 1800–1915 and self-consciously crosses the pre-colonial/colonial divide, arguing that continuities across that divide are more analytically productive than the standard periodisation.

**Collection coverage.** Covered by [[bostoen-brinkman-kongo-kingdom-2018]]. The post-1750 world is a known gap in the collection; this source partially fills the Kongo dimension of that gap.
