# Late Medieval Scandinavian Agrarian Crisis

The long contraction of rural settlement and seigneurial income across Scandinavia
between the [[black-death-scandinavia-1349|Black Death]] and c. 1520, the dominant
economic process of the [[late-middle-ages|late Middle Ages]] in the region. Per
[[helle-cambridge-history-scandinavia-v1-2003|the Cambridge History]].

## Major Phases and Regional Pattern

- **[[norway|Norway]]** — the most catastrophic: by c. 1520 c. 56% of named farms and
  c. 61–62% of holdings deserted; land rents fell to **20–25%** of pre-plague levels
  and corn-tithe revenue to about one-fifth. Worst in marginal districts (80%+
  abandonment); fertile coastal areas near Stavanger under 30%, cushioned by fishing.
- **[[denmark|Denmark]]** — abandonment peaked 1375–1410; Sjælland land rents fell
  c. 57%. The old *bryde* leasehold estates were broken up and replaced by unusually
  **large tenant farms** (averaging c. 15 ha — exceptional in Europe). By 1600, 143
  medieval churches had been abandoned (85% in Jutland).
- **[[sweden|Sweden]]** — severe in Småland and Bohuslän (30–40%), minimal in fertile
  Skåne.
- **Counter-trends** — [[finland|Finland]] and northern Sweden escaped the plague and
  kept expanding; northern Norway (Troms, Finnmark) grew on fishing.

## Economic Restructuring

A general shift **from cereal cultivation to animal husbandry**: Danish farmers
exported beef cattle to the Netherlands and north Germany; Norwegian **lumber** export
expanded with the water-powered gate saw (from the late 14th century); Swedish **iron**
exports grew from c. 300 tons (c. 1370) to c. 1,100 tons (early 1490s), employing
c. 3% of the population. Surviving tenants and labourers generally **benefited** (lower
rents, vacant land, higher wages) — most of all in Norway.

## Interaction

The crisis interlocks with the [[black-death-scandinavia-1349|Black Death]], the
[[scandinavian-noble-decline|decline of the nobility]], and the political weakness of
Norway within the [[kalmar-union-1397|Kalmar Union]].

## Historiography

The Scandinavian Research Project on Deserted Farms and Villages (1960s–70s) underlies
all settlement claims. Norway's higher abandonment figures partly reflect its
single-farm settlement pattern (easier to detect desertion than in villages). See
[[helle-cambridge-history-scandinavia-v1-2003]].
