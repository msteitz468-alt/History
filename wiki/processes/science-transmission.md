## Definition and Scope

The **transmission of science and philosophy** is the trans-regional movement and
transformation of theoretical knowledge — astronomy, mathematics, medicine, optics,
logic, and natural philosophy — across the [[middle-millennium|Middle Millennium]],
as analyzed by Charles Burnett in [[kedar-cwh-v5-2015|CWH V]] (Ch 13). It is the
"theoretical" counterpart to the "practical" knowledge of
[[technology-and-innovation|technology]]: where artisanal technique spread largely
through practice, learned science moved through **texts, translators, and mobile
scholars**, concentrated at particular courts and cities that became "hubs of
learning and intellectual exchange." Burnett's central finding: as ideas moved they
were "transformed and appropriated to new situations and cultures," and their
transmission frequently **transcended religious and other boundaries**.

## The Politics of Origins

A recurring theme — in the period's own sources and in modern scholarship — is the
**contested question of where knowledge originates**, which Burnett shows is rarely
neutral. One ancient and medieval *leitmotif* held that all knowledge "originated in
the East and flowed West" (echoed in Walter Raleigh's 1614 *History of the World*).
Modern correctives — Joseph Needham's *Science and Civilisation in China*, Rashed's
*Encyclopedia of the History of Arabic Science*, Selin on non-Western science, and
Edmund Burke III's "Islam at the center" — react against a Eurocentric history of
science. Period actors legitimated knowledge through **genealogies of authority**:
the Chinese attributing the sciences to the Yellow Emperor, the Greeks to Hermes
(gathered in Isidore's *Etymologiae*), and Islamic science adapting the *isnad*
(chain of transmission) from hadith to scholarship. The question of cultural origins
links this process directly to [[eurocentrism]].

## The Translation Movements

Two great translation enterprises anchor the process:

- **The Greco-Arabic translation movement (Baghdad, 8th–9th c.).** Under the
  [[abbasid-caliphate|Abbasids]], Greek (and some Persian and Sanskrit) works in
  philosophy, medicine, astronomy, and mathematics were rendered into Arabic — the
  intellectual foundation of the Islamic "golden age," carried on at courts and
  institutions associated with [[baghdad|Baghdad]] (the "House of Wisdom"
  tradition). Indian numerals and the place-value system entered the Islamic world
  here.
- **The Arabic-Latin translation movement (Iberia and Sicily, 12th–13th c.).** In
  **Toledo** (after the Christian reconquest) and Norman Sicily, the accumulated
  Greco-Arabic corpus — Aristotle with his Arabic commentators (Ibn Rushd/Averroes),
  al-Khwarizmi, Ibn Sina/Avicenna, Ptolemy's *Almagest* (via Arabic), and original
  Arabic science — was translated into Latin, often by **cross-confessional teams**
  (Jewish, Christian, and Muslim scholars; figures like Petrus Alfonsi, an
  Andalusian Jewish convert). This corpus stocked the new
  [[medieval-university|universities]] and reshaped Latin learning.

## Centers, Carriers, and Transformation

Knowledge concentrated at **hubs** — Baghdad, Toledo, later centers in Mongol Iran
(Maragha observatory) and Timurid Samarkand (Ulugh Beg) — where "books and mobile
practitioners brought new ideas," though these were also "guarded lest they fall
into the wrong hands." Carriers included translators, physicians, astronomers,
pilgrims, and diplomats. Crucially, ideas were **not transmitted unchanged**: they
were appropriated, corrected, and recombined as they crossed linguistic and
confessional frontiers — Indian numerals becoming "Arabic" then "Western," Greek
astronomy refined in Arabic and returned to Latin Europe transformed.

## The Americas Apart

Burnett notes that the western hemisphere developed mathematical and cosmological
knowledge **independently** of Eurasia: the Maya invented a syllabic
[[mesoamerican-calendar|writing and numerical system]] with a true zero, and Aztec
and Maya cosmology built a *layered* rather than concentric universe, giving special
prominence to Venus. These were genuine parallel discoveries, outside the
trans-Eurasian transmission web until the fifteenth-century voyages.

## Interaction

Science transmission moved along the same [[afro-eurasian-world-system|world-system]]
channels as goods and religion — the [[silk-roads|Silk Roads]], the
[[indian-ocean-trade|Indian Ocean]], and the Mediterranean — and depended on the
same court patronage that produced [[courtly-culture|courtly culture]] and on the
institutions of [[educational-institutions|higher learning]]. It is a prime instance
of the volume's thesis that connection across distance was a primary engine of
history.
