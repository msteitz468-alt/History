## Definition and Scope

Heywood (ch. 11 of Bostoen & Brinkman eds., *The Kongo Kingdom*, Cambridge 2018) examines how enslaved and free Kongo people in Brazil — arriving from the 1580s through the 1850s — maintained, adapted, and transmitted a distinctively Kongo cultural identity. The central argument is that this identity was not constructed in Brazil from scratch, nor was it a simple "survival" of unchanged African culture: it was a pre-existing identity, already well-developed in the [[kingdom-of-kongo|Kingdom of Kongo]] itself, that diaspora communities creatively adapted to Brazilian conditions. The primary carriers of Kongo identity were **royalty** (the Christian king of Kongo as a cultural-political symbol) and **Catholic Christianity** as practised in the Kongo idiom — two markers that Kongo people shared across class lines and that distinguished them from other African "nations" in Brazil.

---

## The Intellectual Debate

Heywood situates the chapter within a long-running controversy over African cultural survival in the Americas:

- **Herskovits (1941)**: Found African survivals "just about everywhere" in the Americas.
- **Frazier (1957)**: Argued that plantation slavery stripped enslaved Africans of "almost completely" their African heritage.
- **Mintz & Price (1976)**: Proposed a creole synthesis beginning during the Middle Passage, cautioning against attributing diasporic cultures to any single African origin.
- **Recent scholarship (Fromont 2013, 2014; de Mello e Souza 2002)**: Refocused on the specific historical role of Kongo royalty and Christianity in Brazil.

Heywood's position is that the Kongo case provides especially clear evidence for pre-existing African identity informing diaspora cultural formation. The *Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade Database* and the *African Origins Project* now permit linkage of specific enslaved individuals to their regions of origin; Henry Lovejoy's work (2010, 2012) demonstrates what is possible when databases are fully exploited. For Kongo people the task is complicated because they overlapped with Mbundu captives and were often recorded collectively as "Angolas" in Brazilian records.

## The African Background: Kongo Identity Before Diaspora

Heywood's methodological premise is that "identities are situational" and that examining the Central African background is the "crucial first step in understanding identity formation in the Americas." Three aspects of Kongo culture, well-documented in the seventeenth-century sources, provided the building blocks:

**1. Royal identity and pride in the kingdom.** Seventeenth-century sources reveal a strong collective sense of Kongo distinctiveness. Giovanni Antonio Cavazzi (1650s) noted that Kongo people always praised their country as the most beautiful with the best food, that "God devoted himself to constructing Kongo," and that even people of humble peasant birth insisted on being addressed as "Dom or Dona." The Dutch officials who hosted King Garcia II's ambassadors in the Netherlands marvelled at their deportment, education (the ambassadors spoke Latin learned in Kongo's schools), and the elaborate royal *sangamento* (military-ceremonial dance). The Portuguese soldier Cadornega believed the Kongo remained "arrogant" even after the humiliation of the Battle of Mbwila (1665), when king António I was beheaded and his head displayed in Luanda.

**2. Catholic Christianity as a Kongo-specific marker.** The Kongo had the longest and most sustained exposure to Christianity of any African group involved in the slave trade, beginning with the conversion of Nzinga a Nkuwu (João I) in 1491 and institutionalised by [[afonso-i-kongo|Afonso I]]. By the early seventeenth century Mbanza Kongo was an Episcopal See with a cathedral and twelve churches. The kingdom maintained Catholic lay teachers (*mestres de escola*) who spread Christian practice even to remote districts. As late as the 1790s, the Capuchin da Dicomano found that Kongo Christianity was so interwoven with local culture that interpreters "do not dare to translate what the father says against the customs, for it is *muccano/nkanu*!" — a judicial concept meaning the accusation itself would be improper. Kongo's indigenous *mestres* had succeeded in institutionalising Kongo Christianity so thoroughly that European missionaries found themselves outsiders to it.

**3. Performance culture linked to royalty and religion.** Military dances (*sangamento*), coronation rituals, public processions, penitence ceremonies with flagellation, feast-day celebrations, and royal receptions for diplomatic delegations were all documented in the early seventeenth century. These elaborate performances — some involving hundreds of armed men, mixed-gender participation, and court ceremony — constituted a repertoire of culturally shared practice that enslaved Kongo people carried with them.

## Mechanisms of Transmission: Who Came to Brazil

Heywood estimates from Dutch records that slaves from the Kongo/Loango/Soyo region made up about 23% of arrivals in Recife between 1638 and 1645. The total flow was continuous from the 1580s to the early 1850s. Several documented transmission mechanisms stand out:

**Enslaved Christians.** A significant proportion of Kongo captives exported to Brazil had been baptized before departure. In 1642 the Dutch agent Pieter Moortamer expedited export of 78 Manicongo slaves from Luanda noting that "most of them speak Portuguese and are a little instructed." Capuchin missionaries sold Kongo Christians to slave traders without compunction. In 1651 King Garcia II sent over 200 Kongo Christians from Ulolo (found guilty of murdering a missionary) to Brazil; the Battle of Mbwila (1665–6) produced another large wave including "two illegitimate children of King António I and his relatives," who arrived as *degredados* (condemned freemen) rather than slaves, reflecting their noble status.

**Kongo nobles falsely enslaved.** Two letters dated 5 July 1734 — preserved in the Arquivo Histórico Ultramarino — from enslaved Kongo noblemen Dom Deonizio (nephew of king Manuel II, r. 1718–43) and Dom Jorge Manuel to the king of Portugal document Kongo nobles working on Brazilian plantations, appealing for royal intervention to free them from unjust enslavement. The letters demonstrate that the correspondents maintained their Kongo noble identity, understood themselves as wrongly enslaved freemen, and appealed to the political relationship between the king of Portugal and the king of Kongo for redress.

**Diplomatic presence in Recife.** In December 1624 – March 1643, during the Dutch occupation of Brazil, three ambassadors from the court of the Count of Soyo (Miguel de Castro, Bastião of Soyo, António Fernandez) arrived in Recife to seek Dutch military support. Garcia II's own ambassadors followed. Their elaborate public performances of Kongo royal culture — the *sangamento*, courtly dress, Latin speeches — before both Dutch officials and the enslaved Kongo community in Recife provided a powerful reinforcement of cultural memory.

## The Congadas and Autos de Congo

The principal institutional carrier of Kongo identity in Brazil was the *congada* / *auto de congo* — a theatrical festival tradition centred on the election and coronation of a "King of Congo." By the early twentieth century, these celebrations consisted of elaborate folk dramas performed on saints' days (particularly Saint Anthony and Saint Benedict) by members of *irmandades* (Catholic brotherhoods) in communities from the Amazon to Porto Alegre. The performances featured:
- Songs in African languages and Portuguese suffused with Kikongo/Kimbundu terms: *Ganga* (from *nganga*, "priest"), *Zumbi* (from *nzumbi*, "spirit"), address to "our Muxikongo mother" (*Muxikongo* = inhabitants of the Mbanza Kongo heartland).
- Dramatic speeches by the King of Kongo and his queen (eventually identified with Queen Njinga of Ndongo and Matamba).
- The symbolic election and coronation of the king as a central act.

**Origins and dating.** Folklorists Gustava Barroso and Luís da Câmara Cascudo, working in Northeast Brazil in the late 1800s and early 1900s, conjectured that these performances derived from historical events in Angola and Kongo during the Dutch occupation (1641–8) and began circulating as folk tradition from the early eighteenth century. The earliest documentary record of the celebrations is from Recife: the Câmara of Recife recorded the crowning of Antônio Carvalho and Ângela Ribeiro as *Reis Congos* in 1674. In 1711, the Brotherhood of the Rosary of Blacks of Olinda recorded the election of a "King of Congo" with the Bishop's official recognition. The 1782 constitution of the Brotherhood of Nossa Senhora do Rosário dos Homens Pretos of Recife required members to elect "a King of Congo and a queen" annually.

**Heywood's interpretation.** The *congadas* were not, as Brazilian observer Henry Koster (1816) and later scholars Suzel Ana Reily (2001) have argued, either copies of European customs or politically motivated modern inventions. They were the product of enslaved Central Africans and their descendants creatively adapting real African historical memories — the Christian king of Kongo, royal ceremony, the *sangamento*, diplomatic ritual — to Brazilian conditions. The election of a king of Kongo "symbolically restored the king in his splendour and power among his subjects in Brazil," creating what Heywood, following Stuart Hall, calls an "empowerment drama" that asserted a counter-hegemonic idiom of African sovereignty against the reality of servile status.

The Kikongo language was itself a vehicle of identity: enslaved Kongo people used their language in the *congadas*, particularly in the Kisikongo variety of the Mbanza Kongo heartland. This "creatively manipulated" use of language in performance "symbolically reaffirmed their links to the kingdom."

## Christianity as Identity Marker in Brazil

Kongo people's Christian identity stood out in Brazil because no other African "nation" came from a country that Portuguese slave-owners and government officials recognised as Christian with kings whose status Europeans acknowledged. This created asymmetries: a Kongo slave in Itaubira, Minas Gerais (Pedro "of the Congo nation," 1768) led informal mass for a mixed group of slaves and was brought before the Inquisition — demonstrating both the continuation of Christian practice and its contestation. Kongo Christian practice in Brazil synthesised Kongo and European elements, parallel to what had happened in Africa: "even the interpreters do not dare to translate what the father says against the customs."

The specifically Kongo variant of Christianity — centred on the Christian king, the *sangamento* as a religious-military ceremony, public processions, Catholic brotherhoods, and lay *mestres* — was what the *congadas* transmitted across generations. Heywood argues this Christianity "that enslaved Kongo people in Brazil espoused and identified with was connected directly with the Christianity that developed under Kongo cultural traditions."

## Historiography

**Source base.** Heywood draws on Brazilian curia archives (Arquebispo da Bahia), the Dutch West India Company records (Nationaal Archief Nederland), the Arquivo Histórico Ultramarino, the Arquivo Nacional Torre de Tombo Inquisition records, the Brásio *Monumenta Missionaria Africana*, and the *Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade Database*. Published Brazilian and Portuguese chronicles (Cadornega, Carli, Cavazzi) provide cultural descriptions from the seventeenth century. The 1734 noblemen's letters (AHU Caixa 30) are a particularly striking primary source reproduced in full.

**Gaps and limits.** Enslaved Kongo people "rarely appear as a separate category of slaves in the earliest Brazilian records" — the overlap with Mbundu captives under the blanket "Angola" category obscures quantification. Cuba is noted as an almost completely unstudied case for Central African identity formation.

**Debates engaged.** The chapter directly refutes Suzel Ana Reily's dismissal of Afro-Brazilian African-identity claims as "politically motivated forums for collectively negotiating the past." Heywood instead argues the traditions are historically grounded in specific Kongo events and practices. It engages Fromont's (2013, 2014) visual-culture analysis as complementary; de Mello e Souza's work (2002) on the King of Kongo celebration as a reference point for understanding Afro-Brazilian distinctiveness.

**The creolisation debate.** Heywood's position implicitly differs from Mintz & Price's creolisation model: while she does not deny transformation, she insists that the specific content of Kongo identity in Brazil — royalty, Christianity, *sangamento*, Kikongo language elements — can be traced to specific African antecedents and that newly arriving Africans continuously refreshed diaspora cultural memory well into the eighteenth century.

**Collection coverage.** This page draws on [[bostoen-brinkman-kongo-kingdom-2018]]. The collection is generally weak on the Atlantic diaspora and post-1750 Americas; this chapter partially addresses that gap for the Kongo case specifically.
