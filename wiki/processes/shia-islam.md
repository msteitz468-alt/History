## Definition and Scope

Shi'a Islam (*Shi'at 'Ali*, 'the party of 'Ali') emerged from the conviction that the rightful leadership of the Muslim community — the imamate — belonged exclusively to the family of 'Ali b. Abi Talib, the Prophet's cousin and son-in-law, and through him to Muhammad's house of Hashim. This page covers the formative process: from the original partisan movement supporting 'Ali's claim (656 CE) through the crystallization, after the [[abbasid-revolution-750|Abbasid Revolution]] (750 CE), of Shi'ism as a distinct and self-conscious community in permanent opposition to Sunni majoritarianism.

It is distinct from — though historically entangled with — the later fully developed theological systems of Twelver Shi'ism and Isma'ilism, which are products of the ninth century and after.

## Causal Drivers

### The Succession Crisis

The immediate occasion was the death of Muhammad in 632 CE without an unambiguous designated successor. 'Ali's partisans held that Muhammad had implicitly (and at Ghadir Khumm, explicitly) designated 'Ali; the community majority followed Abu Bakr, then 'Umar, then 'Uthman. When 'Ali finally became caliph (656), the [[first-fitna-656|First Fitna]] erupted almost immediately. 'Ali was assassinated in 661 and his son Hasan renounced the caliphate to Mu'awiyah.

### Karbala' and the Emotional Foundation

The decisive event generating Shi'i identity as distinct from mere political preference was the killing of 'Ali's second son Husayn at Karbala' (680 CE) during the [[second-fitna-680|Second Fitna]]. Husayn's refusal to submit to Yazid I and his death with a small band of followers produced a narrative of martyrdom, righteous suffering, and divine injustice that became the emotional core of Shi'i piety. Hodgson notes that Karbala' became a permanent reference point for the Abbasid propaganda against the Umayyads: Abu Muslim raised his black banners in Khurasan partly as a symbol of mourning for Husayn and subsequent 'Alid martyrs ([[hodgson-venture-of-islam-v1-1974]]).

### Mawali Discontent

A structural driver of Shi'i growth was the discontent of the *Mawali* — non-Arab converts to Islam, primarily Iranian, who were affiliated to Arab tribes as clients but denied full social and economic equality with Arab-born Muslims. The [[umayyad-caliphate|Umayyad Caliphate]]'s persistent privileging of Arab descent over Islamic confession alienated this population. The 'Alid cause, with its egalitarian overtones and its critique of Umayyad legitimacy, attracted Mawali support intensely — particularly in Khurasan, where the eventual Abbasid rising began.

## Major Phases

### Phase 1 — The Partisan Period (656–680)

Shi'ism in this period was primarily a political faction supporting 'Ali and his claims against rival caliphs. The Kufan garrison town was the center of 'Alid partisanship. After 'Ali's assassination, leadership of the cause passed uncertainly between Hasan (who abdicated) and various other candidates.

### Phase 2 — Consolidation through Martyrdom (680–c. 685)

Husayn's death at Karbala' transformed what had been a political faction into something approaching a devotional movement. The uprising of Mukhtar al-Thaqafi at Kufah (685–687 CE) in support of Ibn al-Hanafiyyah (Muhammad b. al-Hanafiyyah, 'Ali's son by a different mother) introduced the first explicitly messianic element: Mukhtar proclaimed Ibn al-Hanafiyyah the *Mahdi*, the 'Guided One', who would fill the world with justice as it was filled with injustice. Some followers believed he had not died but was in *ghayba* (occultation), waiting to return.

### Phase 3 — The Imam Lines and Ghulat Speculation (c. 685–750)

This period saw the multiplication of 'Alid imam lines and the emergence of the *Ghulat* ('exaggerators') — radical Shi'i thinkers who developed speculative theology about the divine nature of the imam, the transmigration of souls, and the spiritual dimensions of the cause. Hodgson describes them as "try[ing] to work out, above all, the religious implications of a historical situation in which truth and justice seemed to be represented by a defeated minority, the Shi'ah, overridden by an arrogant majority in the name of Islam itself."

Key Ghulat doctrines:
- The imam receives continuous divine guidance analogous to prophetic revelation
- Messianic return (*raj'a*) of deceased imams
- Present spiritual resurrection for the faithful, not merely future bodily resurrection
- The imam possesses *'ilm* (knowledge of God's will) inaccessible to ordinary believers

The imam Ja'far al-Sadiq (d. 765), sixth imam of the line through Husayn, became the most important intellectual figure of this phase. He is credited by Hodgson with disciplining and moderating Ghulat excesses, establishing the basis for both Twelver and Isma'ili traditions, while attracting major theological thinkers around him. His moderation may have been partly a response to the anti-Shi'i repression under the Abbasid caliph al-Mansur.

The chart of 'Alid candidates reproduced in Hodgson shows the proliferation of competing claimants: Ibn al-Hanafiyyah's line claimed by at least three men; the Husayni line through 'Ali Zayn al-'Abidin, Muhammad al-Baqir, and Ja'far al-Sadiq; and various collateral claims.

### Phase 4 — The 'Abbasi Betrayal and Sectarian Crystallization (750–c. 874)

The [[abbasid-revolution-750|Abbasid Revolution]] was launched on largely Shi'i grounds — avenging the house of Muhammad, especially Husayn at Karbala', and promising genuine Islamic governance. Once victorious, the Abbasids systematically eliminated their Shi'i allies:

- Abu Salamah, head of the Shi'i coalition in Iraq, hoped to install an 'Alid caliph; he was murdered when no 'Alid proved ready to act.
- Abu Muslim, the military architect of the revolution, was lured to his death by al-Mansur (754 CE).
- Muhammad 'al-Nafs al-Zakiyyah' ('the pure soul'), who claimed the title *Mahdi* and rose against the Abbasids in the Hijaz, was crushed; even Basra's sympathizers were bloodily suppressed.

This betrayal produced a definitive polarization. As Hodgson writes: "The two sides in this schism have subsequently been labelled, conventionally, 'Sunnis' and 'Shi'is'. Each side adopted a historical position in justification of its present attitude." The Shi'a crystallized their rejection of Abu Bakr, 'Umar, and 'Uthman as usurpers; the Sunnis (Arabic: 'Men of the Sunnah and the Jama'ah') responded by accepting the religious authority of all Muhammad's companions without distinction.

## Geographic Spread

The center of early Shi'ism was **Kufah** in Iraq, which had been 'Ali's capital and remained the most reliably 'Alid-sympathetic garrison town. **Basra** had more mixed allegiances. The Hijaz (Medina, Mecca) housed pious 'Alid-leaning scholars without the insurrectionary character of Kufah. **Khurasan** in northeastern Iran became crucial in the Abbasid period: its Mawali population and distance from Umayyad power made it the most fertile ground for Abbasid-cum-Shi'i propaganda.

After 750, significant 'Alid political activity shifted to the Hijaz and to peripheral regions of the Abbasid empire. In Morocco, the 'Alid refugee Idris b. 'Abd Allah (who had participated in a Shi'i rising at Mecca in 786) founded the Idrisid dynasty, establishing Fez as a cultural center — the earliest durable 'Alid political state in the Maghrib.

## Imamate Doctrine — The Core Theological Contribution

The defining theological innovation of Shi'ism was the doctrine of *nass* (designation): the idea that the imam was appointed by his predecessor through explicit divine guidance, not elected by the community. This gave the imam a unique *'ilm* (knowledge) of God's will that no community majority vote could replicate or override. Hodgson's genealogical chart marks with an asterisk those candidates "whose position received, in their lifetime, at least some of its force from the doctrine of *nass*."

The *nass* doctrine distinguished the organized Shi'a (already prominent under Hisham's caliphate, centered on the Abu Hashim — then 'Abbasid — network) from more moderate Shi'is like Zayd b. 'Ali, who led an insurrection in Kufah in 740 CE. Zayd rejected the *nass* doctrine and the damnation of early caliphs; his more moderate Zaydi tradition survived in Yemen.

## Interaction with Other Processes

- `concurrent_with: [[piety-minded-opposition]]` — Shi'i scholars were part of the broader piety-minded opposition but with distinctive imamate commitments
- `contrasts_with: [[kharijites]]` — both opposed the Umayyads but on incompatible theological bases; Kharijites rejected hereditary leadership entirely
- `enabled: [[abbasid-revolution-750]]` — Shi'i networks, emotional resonance, and organizational infrastructure made the revolution possible, even though the Abbasids subsequently betrayed the Shi'a
- `produced: [[imamate]]` — the full theology of the imamate emerged from Shi'i necessity to justify a divinely-guided minority against a majority

## End Conditions / Transformation

As a unified political-religious movement aspiring to restore 'Alid rule, early Shi'ism effectively ended with the failure of post-Abbasid revolts (750s–760s). What survived was a doctrinal and devotional tradition — increasingly quietist in the face of Abbasid power — that continued to develop the imamate theology. The period closes with Ja'far al-Sadiq's generation laying the theological groundwork for the eventual Twelver and Isma'ili branches.

## Historiography

Hodgson's foundational article 'How Did the Early Shi'a Become Sectarian?' (*Journal of the American Oriental Society*, 75, 1955) is explicitly cited in the source text as the key analytical treatment. He acknowledges that the early dating of certain ideas in that article "rests on the overall weight of very scanty evidence rather than on any sure ground point by point," and that the disciplining of Ghulat notions by Ja'far al-Sadiq probably came primarily in response to Abbasid repression rather than internal theological development alone.

Watt's suggestion (*Islamic Philosophy and Theology*) that Shi'ism traces to 'south' Arab tribes with Yemeni kingship traditions while Kharijism represents 'north' Arab tribal culture is rejected by Hodgson, who argues that present charismatic leadership proved more crucial for Kharijites than hereditary charisma was for Shi'is.

Primary sources for early Shi'ism are almost entirely mediated through later compilations; the Ghulat writings survive only in fragments quoted by hostile sources. This makes confident reconstruction of Ghulat theology particularly hazardous.
