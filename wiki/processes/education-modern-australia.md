## Definition and Scope

The transformation of Australian education across the twentieth century constitutes a process of progressive expansion from compulsory elementary schooling (colonial-era) through universal secondary (post-WWII) to mass higher education (post-1987). The process is marked by four structural tensions: State versus Commonwealth authority; public versus non-government schooling; meritocracy versus persistent class/race stratification; and education as public good versus marketised private benefit. Source: [[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]] (chapter by Alison Mackinnon and Helen Proctor).

---

## Causal Drivers

**State sovereignty** was the constitutional baseline: "schooling remained a State responsibility at Federation." Each State ran its own centralised education department, defended by Australian educationalists such as Frank Tate in the 1930s as "a practical solution to the problem of providing equitable access to education across a sparsely populated landmass."

**Demographic pressure** — the post-WWII baby boom, large-scale immigration, and (from the 1980s) international student demand — repeatedly overwhelmed existing infrastructure and forced federal intervention.

**Commonwealth fiscal power**, transferred definitively in the 1942 Uniform Tax case, gave Canberra leverage it deployed incrementally: post-war reconstruction training, Mills Committee grants (1951), Murray Report (1957), Schools Commission (1973), Dawkins reforms (1987–88).

**Economic ideology** shifted the model: Keynesian growth doctrine treated education as "an engine of economic growth and agent of social welfare"; post-1975 neoliberal reform demanded institutional efficiency, market competition, and private contribution to costs.

## Major Phases

### Phase 1: Consolidation and the New Education movement (1901–1939)
By 1900 all Australian colonies had compulsory elementary schooling; Queensland was last to legislate for compulsion (1900), New South Wales and Tasmania last to abolish school fees (1906 and 1908). Nearly 30 per cent of public school children in New South Wales were in rural primary schools around World War 2; up to three-quarters in other States. Reform came from an early-century generation of Directors of Public Instruction — Cyril Jackson (WA), Frank Tate (Vic), Peter Board (NSW) — who rewrote syllabuses to include nature knowledge, physical education and music, and expanded post-primary schooling. The international *New Education* movement, which "aimed to broaden and humanise schools by focusing on the individual child," was promoted in Australia by the 1937 New Education Fellowship Conference, essentially single-handedly revived by the ACER (Australian Council for Educational Research, established 1930 with a Carnegie Corporation grant).

Catholic schools educated "one in four or five Australian children during the twentieth century," staffed almost entirely by nuns, brothers and priests until the 1960s, who "subsist[ed] on much less than a lay teacher's wage." Independent ('corporate') schools charged high fees and followed traditions of British public schools: "religion, discipline, culture, athletics and service."

Intelligence testing, imported from France, UK and the United States from the 1930s, gave policy-makers an empirical framework for sorting children — entrenching meritocracy as the organising principle of secondary selection.

**Exclusion of Indigenous children** was systematic: in New South Wales, a 1902 policy of 'exclusion on demand' — Aboriginal children could be removed from schools if white parents objected — remained in effect with variations across States through the assimilationist period into the 1960s. Indigenous parents objected formally; correspondence files contain letters such as one from World War 1: "I am a taxpayer and an elector, so therefore I am assisting to carry the burden of education of the children of NSW."

### Phase 2: Commonwealth Entry and Post-War Expansion (1940–1972)
Federal government involvement began with wartime free places and the Commonwealth Reconstruction Training Scheme (CRTS) for returned service personnel — men and women — who "doubled undergraduate numbers." Four state universities at Federation; University of Queensland (1909) and University of Western Australia (1911) followed. Until World War 2 universities enrolled only ~14,000 students (0.2 per cent of population). The first Australian PhD was not awarded until 1948.

The Murray Report (1957), commissioned by Menzies, created the Australian Universities Commission and "revolutionised Australian universities": enrolments rose from 14,000 to 69,000 by 1963. The 1964 Martin Report established a binary system — universities for research and theory, cheaper Colleges of Advanced Education (CAEs) for applied/vocational training — and by 1974 there were 19 universities.

By 1971 technical education enrolled close to 400,000 students — the largest post-secondary sector — compared with 123,000 in universities and 44,000 in CAEs. The Kangan Report (1974) created Technical and Further Education (TAFE) as a distinct Commonwealth-funded sector.

### Phase 3: Whitlam Reforms and the End of the Long Boom (1972–1987)
The Whitlam government abolished university fees in 1974 and established the Schools Commission, which "allocate[d] per capita grants to all schools, including non-government schools, on the basis of need" — a historic reversal of Labor policy on state aid. Public expenditure on education peaked at 6.1 per cent of GDP in 1977–78, a level never since regained. Women, who made up less than a fifth of undergraduates in 1949, outnumbered men by 1987.

The Whitlam government created the National Aboriginal Consultative Group (1972), leading in 1977 to the National Aboriginal Education Committee. The Hawke government launched the first National Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Education Policy in 1989, described as "a milestone in Australian history, uniting all states and territories with the Commonwealth in a commitment to pursuing national goals for Indigenous education" — though the subsequent Liberal–National government dismantled it.

### Phase 4: Marketisation and the Unified National System (1987–present)
In 1987 Minister John Dawkins abolished the binary divide and created a "unified national system" of 36 universities merged from 70 institutions. A Higher Education Contribution Scheme (HECS) introduced deferred fees — "widely imitated elsewhere." The Australian Research Council (ARC) linked research funding to commercial and national strategic objectives.

Higher education enrolments rose from 394,000 in 1987 to 631,000 in 1996. International student fee income reached $14.2 billion by 2007–08 but caused student–teacher ratios to deteriorate from 14:1 (1996) to 20:1 (2006). Australia became "the only OECD country where the public contribution to higher education remained at the same level in 2005 as in 1995."

In schools, the proportion attending government secondary schools fell from 75.9 per cent (1975) to 61.8 per cent (2005), with dramatic growth in low-to-moderate-fee independent schools including Islamic, evangelical Christian and Steiner schools. PISA results from 2000 classified Australia as "high quality, low equity." The Rudd government's ACARA (2008) introduced a national curriculum authority and the MySchool website of comparative school data.

## Geographic Spread

Centralisation was always metropolitan-oriented; rural and regional students faced systematic disadvantage. Rural primary schools remained prominent as late as the 1940s (30 per cent of NSW public school children). By the twenty-first century remote, rural and regional students remain underrepresented in higher education, "and here, of course, many Indigenous students can be found."

## End Conditions

The process is ongoing. At century's end the dominant framework is an "emphasis on vocationalism" driven by "skill shortages" and global competition for students. The tension between education as "a universal right and a public good" (UNESCO) and education as private investment remains "endemic in academic debate." The goal of broadening access for disadvantaged groups — class, Indigenous, rural — remains unrealised: "the dominance of higher socioeconomic groups remains intact."

## Historiography

The chapter by Mackinnon and Proctor in [[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]] grounds analysis in Simon Marginson's *Educating Australia* (1997) and statistical work by Anderson and Vervoorn. It stresses that adult and vocational education are systematically undervalued in conventional educational historiography — "a Cinderella-like existence in the back kitchen of education." The historiography of Indigenous schooling is frank about its incompleteness: "We have nothing like a full picture of the history of Indigenous engagement with formal schooling." The chapter does not cover pre-Federation schooling, which belongs to the colonial-era framework; see [[environmental-transformation-colonial-australia]] for context on colonial institution-building.
