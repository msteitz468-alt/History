## Definition and Scope

Contemporary Pacific resource extraction describes the post-1967 cycle of large-scale mining (copper, gold, nickel, phosphate) and industrial logging in Melanesia and Indonesia's Irian Jaya, conducted by transnational corporations in partnership with — and often in tension with — post-colonial governments, provincial governments, and customary landowners. The process is distinct from the earlier colonial extraction cycle ([[pacific-mining-colonies]]) in that independent or semi-sovereign governments possessed legal and political capacity to renegotiate terms, demand royalties, and (eventually) litigate environmental claims. Yet structural asymmetries — capital requirements, technical expertise, and fiscal desperation — constrained governments severely.

The political paradox Nero identifies is precise: "for a weak government, minerals proved a very mixed blessing." Mining revenues funded essential services; mining politics fragmented state authority; mining pollution destroyed landowner livelihoods; and the fiscal windfall was regularly dissipated by renegotiation, corruption, and landowner compensation demands.

## Causal Drivers

**Commodity demand and technology**: Large transnational mining syndicates — CRA/Conzinc Riotinto, Freeport, Kennecott, BHP — developed technology capable of exploiting "colossal deposits of low-grade copper, capped by gold and laced with silver, requiring immense capital and skilled engineering." Remote, geologically inhospitable terrain (Bougainville, Ok Tedi's Mount Fubilan, Irian Jaya's Ertsberg/Grasberg) was accessible only at industrial scale.

**Weak state fiscal capacity**: Post-colonial governments in PNG, the Solomons, and Vanuatu lacked tax bases and depended on aid. Mining companies exploited this desperation. As Nero notes of the Somare government: "By choosing to improve and extend services, the new government was bound to seek an accommodation with foreign governments on long-term aid and with foreign companies on major resource investments."

**Customary land tenure**: Landowners held moral (and, in PNG, increasing legal) leverage over surface and sub-surface resources even when statutory mineral rights were vested in the state. Landowners "rather than well-paid mining workers or governments, have prevented some projects from beginning, just as they have extracted compensation and services from companies and governments, and in extreme cases brought operations to a standstill."

**Logging commodity boom**: Timber prices doubled in 1993, triggering a Solomon Islands logging surge where exports jumped to SI$230 million, "suddenly accounted for 55 per cent of all exports, overshadowing fisheries and contributing 25 per cent of government revenues." Similar boom conditions hit PNG and Vanuatu.

## Major Cases

### Bougainville Copper (1967–1989)
Conzinc Riotinto's Bougainville Copper Pty Ltd came into production in 1972. Profits in 1973 were AU$38.3 million (earnings AU$98.1 million), with the PNG government earning AU$1.1 million in royalties plus AU$10.4 million in dividends. Michael Somare's government renegotiated the agreement at independence, increasing the national share and creating provincial autonomy for Bougainville. But the "more explosive issue was the distribution of revenues between national and provincial governments and landowners." Nasioi landowners bore "the environmental costs, including over 100,000 tonnes of tailings each day." After negotiations collapsed in 1989, the [[bougainville-civil-war-1988|Bougainville Republican Army]] forced closure of the mine and launched insurrection.

### Freeport/Ertsberg and Grasberg, Irian Jaya (1967–present)
The Indonesian government authorised the New Orleans-based Freeport company in 1967. The Minister for Mines "accepted the draft written by the company as [the] basis for negotiations." Indonesian constitutional law ("minerals as state property") and the Agrarian Law (recognising only land under cultivation) stripped the Damal and Amungme peoples of compensation rights. The Ertsberg mill had a daily capacity of 115,000 tonnes and deposited tailings directly into rivers. By the late 1980s, Ertsberg exhausted, Freeport signed an agreement for the nearby Grasberg deposit — reserves of gold, silver, and copper "valued at $50 billion." In 1977, OPM (Free Papua Movement) rebellion prompted army strafing of villages. In 1996, Amungme leader Tom Beanal filed a US$6 billion class action in New Orleans "alleging environmental and cultural destruction" — illustrating the globalisation of litigation against transnational extractors.

### Ok Tedi (1982–present)
In PNG's remote Star Mountains (colonial administration arrived only in the 1950s), a consortium including the PNG government (20 per cent) opened Ok Tedi Mining in 1982, explicitly including "social as well as environmental issues" in its agenda. The tailings dam collapsed; tailings entered the Fly River, "which discharges into Torres Strait—with international ramifications." Local royalty shares increased from 5 to 20 per cent in 1989. When Fly River landowners, represented by Australian lawyers, successfully sued Broken Hill Proprietary Ltd in Melbourne courts, it marked the first time a transnational mining suit against a Pacific project succeeded in the company's home jurisdiction.

### Mount Kare Gold Rush (1987–1990)
CRA discovered alluvial gold north of Tari (Southern Highlands) in 1987, triggering a "classic rush." Large-scale clan battles erupted. "By 1990 more than 100 million Kina ($100 million) worth of gold had been removed." The government could neither prevent the rush nor capture its profits. A survey found: "Its bywords, among the people of Tari at least, were instant riches, sickness, immorality and waste." The episode illustrated how small-scale alluvial gold escapes state and corporate frameworks entirely, producing short-lived enrichment and lasting ecological and social damage.

### Solomon Islands Logging Boom (1991–present)
In 1991 the Solomons exported SI$54 million in logs; by 1993, SI$230 million. "At the present rate of extraction the forests could be depleted by the year 2000, with little reforestation achieved." Kwara'ae intellectual David Gegeo documented the social effects: "the last 20 years or so have seen a monumental escalation in the number of disputes and in their degree of intensity. Rightful ownership has become the dominant theme." Land-tenure disputes between clans, fuelled by the cash value of timber, undermined extended-family mediation mechanisms.

## Geographic Spread

Almost exclusively **Melanesia** and **Indonesia's Irian Jaya**:
- **PNG**: Bougainville, Ok Tedi, Porgera, Lihir, Mount Kare, oil prospecting
- **Irian Jaya (Indonesia)**: Freeport Ertsberg/Grasberg
- **Solomon Islands**: Logging (Malaita, Guadalcanal); Gold Ridge mine (later period)
- **Vanuatu**: Logging boom 1990s
- **New Caledonia**: Nickel (French-controlled; Kanak dispossession — see [[new-caledonia]])

Polynesia and Micronesia are largely absent from this process (except for exhausted phosphate islands: Nauru, Banaba/Ocean Island, Angaur in Palau).

## End Conditions

Structurally, the process continues. Mining is capital-intensive and can only be conducted by large syndicates, which structurally advantages corporations over governments and communities. Landowner power has grown and will continue to grow in PNG; Indonesia maintains coercive capacity to suppress it in Irian Jaya. Environmental litigation has globalised: Ok Tedi (Melbourne), Freeport/Amungme (New Orleans). Logging rates remain above sustainable levels in the Solomons and PNG. The fundamental tension — between landowners' perpetual attachment to land and companies'/governments' transient interests — is structural and unresolved.

## Historiography

Nero's analysis emphasises political economy over pure environmental history: resource extraction generated as many political crises as fiscal solutions. The Bougainville case shows that revenue sharing without genuine community consent produces insurgency. The Irian Jaya case shows that without democratic constraints, state-corporate alliances can suppress landowner claims indefinitely — but at long-term costs to provincial stability and national legitimacy.

Howard, *Mining, Politics, and Development in the South Pacific*, provides regional framework. Case-specific studies: Jackson, *Ok Tedi: Pot of Gold?*; Hyndman, "A Sacred Mountain of Gold"; Denoon et al. (eds), *Mining and Mineral Resource Policy Issues in Asia-Pacific*. Logging: Park, *Tropical Rainforests*; Bennett, "Forestry, Public Land, and the Colonial Legacy in Solomon Islands."
