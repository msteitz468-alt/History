## Definition and Scope

This process page covers the long arc of Germany's national economy as a vehicle of militarized national strategy from 1914 to 1945: the improvised war economy of WWI, the destabilization and stabilization of the Weimar Republic's economic order, and the Nazi rearmament economy that culminated in the Second World War. The demarcation dates (Tooze, ch. 18 of [[smith-oxford-modern-german-history-2011]]) span "the rise and fall of the idea of the German national economy not merely as a vehicle for peaceful social evolution, but as a means by which to violently redirect the course of history."

This is distinct from the narrower [[war-economy]] concept page and from the [[total-war-germany-1914-1918]] process, which focuses on the First World War's mobilization specifically.

## Causal Drivers

**Constitutional fragmentation**: Bismarck's constitution left industrial policy to member states, not the Reich. No mechanism existed for centralized national economic command. The World War I war economy was therefore improvised — first by private industrialists (Walther Rathenau, AEG), then by contested military-bureaucratic structures.

**Global resource asymmetry**: Germany's fundamental strategic problem across both world wars was confronting empires (British, French, Russian, American) with vastly greater global resource access. Three-quarters of the shells fired in the Somme barrage came from North America. Germany's 1939 rearmament effort could not overcome the material arithmetic: "German armaments production relative to that of its enemies mirrored exactly the pre-war ratio of the output of steel and GDP" ([[smith-oxford-modern-german-history-2011]], ch. 18).

**American capital as structural lever (1919–1931)**: Weimar's stabilization (Dawes Plan, 1924) rested on ~$3 billion in American private investment. Gustav Stresemann's foreign strategy used German indebtedness to Wall Street as leverage against French and British reparations demands. When American capital dried up after 1929, this architecture collapsed.

**The Great Depression as enabling condition for Nazism**: The Depression destroyed the international economic framework in which German big business had prospered (multilateral trade, Wall Street partnerships, steel cartels). It also enfeebled trade unions and collapsed the center of German politics, creating the vacuum in which Hitler's NSDAP could position itself as the nationalist alternative.

## Major Phases

### Phase 1: WWI War Economy (1914–1918)

The **Hindenburg Program** (1916–1917), aiming to double armaments output within a year, was the most ambitious wartime production effort. Backed by the Auxiliary Service Law (Hilfsdienstgesetz) conscripting all males 17–60. But it was "a self-serving vehicle for the interests of German heavy industry, which after superficial initial successes unhinged the precarious balance of the German war economy." Agriculture failed even more completely than industry. Combined with the Supreme Command's refusal to force conservatives to democratize, chronic food shortages prevented the regime from rallying the home front. By 1918, survival rested on one last military gamble — which failed ([[smith-oxford-modern-german-history-2011]], ch. 18).

Walther Rathenau's War Raw Materials Office inspired Lenin's conception of socialist planning, while Bucharin described the German war economy as a "new leviathan" surpassing Hobbes's fantasy. German practitioners were more critical, comparing their improvised system unfavorably with Lloyd George's centralized British model.

### Phase 2: Weimar Stabilization and Fragility (1919–1929)

The Zentrale Arbeitsgemeinschaft (ZAG, October 1918) bonded employer organizations and trade unions to preserve the German economy through revolution and defeat. The new Weimar Constitution (1919) made satisfaction of basic material needs a constitutional obligation.

The 132 billion Reichsmark reparations bill (London ultimatum, May 1921) — 180% of GDP — forced Germany to define, measure, and govern its "national economy" for the first time. Economic statistics moved to the center of cabinet politics.

The Dawes Plan (1924) stabilized the currency and opened Wall Street funding. Germany built an ambitious welfare state: unemployment insurance passed in 1927 by a Reichstag majority of 355 to 47. IG Farben, Siemens, and AEG negotiated global market-sharing agreements with American counterparts; GM bought Opel; Ford established Cologne operations; German heavy industry joined a European steel cartel.

**Structural weakness**: The balance of payments showed persistent large trade deficits and dangerous short-term borrowing (Table 18.1 in [[smith-oxford-modern-german-history-2011]]). High relative wages and welfare costs constrained exports. German business lobbied against welfare spending while benefiting from the Republic's internationalist foreign policy — a structural contradiction.

### Phase 3: Depression and Collapse (1929–1933)

The cyclical downturn began in Germany in 1928. Under Chancellor Brüning (1930–1932), austerity deflation succeeded in eliminating the trade deficit — the deficit of -2.5 billion Reichsmarks in 1927 became a surplus of 3.2 billion by 1931 — but at the cost of 6 million unemployed. Brüning's strategy was to use German indebtedness to force the US to broker reparations relief; it partially succeeded (Hoover Moratorium, July 1931) but too late to prevent political catastrophe.

The June 1931 banking panic (DANAT bank collapse) forced a bank holiday and suspension of gold convertibility, effectively removing Germany from the gold standard. Exchange controls were imposed. The Reichsmark remained overvalued (Germany could not follow Britain's September 1931 sterling devaluation without losing US goodwill). Wage and price cuts under emergency decrees deepened the depression.

The political consequence: the unemployed flocked to the Communist KPD; the "rest of the population" found the NSDAP as the nationalist alternative. "What united the extremes was their hostility to the Republic and their rejection of the bankrupt system of international economics on which Weimar's stabilization had so fundamentally relied" ([[smith-oxford-modern-german-history-2011]], ch. 18).

### Phase 4: Nazi Rearmament Economy (1933–1939)

[[Adolf-hitler]]'s regime accomplished "the single most dramatic transfer of resources ever realized by a capitalist state in peacetime." The civilian/military phasing (civilian recovery 1933–1936, then Four Year Plan rearmament) is a myth: "the money spent in the first six months of 1933 came largely from schemes devised in the autumn of 1932 that were always intended as cover for clandestine rearmament." Military spending dominated by 1934; in December 1933, only ten months after Hitler took power, new funds to civilian job creation were halted.

Key structural features:
- **Foreign exchange crisis**: Germany's balance of payments was in constant danger. Hjalmar Schacht's "New Plan" (1934) imposed comprehensive exchange rationing; business associations were reorganized and subordinated to state direction.
- **Goering's Four Year Plan** (1936): Investment in synthetic rubber, oil, textiles, and iron from domestic ore — autarky-oriented production reducing import dependence.
- **Blurring of state and corporation**: "Extending the state's influence into the economic sphere everywhere had the effect of blurring the demarcation between state and civil society." IG Farben executives occupied key roles in Goering's organization while their corporate priorities merged with regime priorities.
- **Labor mobilization**: Destruction of free trade unions (spring 1933) and creation of the German Labor Front (DAF). Full employment achieved by mid-1930s. Farm labor under state discipline from the 1934 harvest; hundreds of thousands of workers tied to workplaces by military discipline by 1938.
- **Macro-economic management**: The Third Reich developed its own organic macro-economic vision — a precursor to Keynesian demand management but oriented toward military production rather than civilian welfare.
- **Consumer trade-offs**: Remilitarization was "a spectacular act of collective mass consumption" in the sense that it commanded genuine popular enthusiasm. But the hidden cost was severe: 65 billion Reichsmarks spent on rearmament (Hitler's own boast) was approximately the cost of providing quality housing with plumbing and electricity to all Germans.

### Phase 5: War Economy Mark II (1939–1945)

By 1939, the Third Reich's economic destabilization was advanced: foreign exchange crisis, inflationary pressure, foreign creditors suspended since 1933. The decision for war in 1939 reflected Hitler's conviction (confirmed by military-economic data) that delay would allow the Allied powers to overtake Germany's temporary armaments advantage.

The **Blitzkrieg economy myth**: Germany's 1940 victory over France was achieved despite, not because of, economic preparation — the Wehrmacht was materially inferior in 1940. It was only after the lightning victories that a new economic vision of rapid mobile warfare gained traction.

**1941 as apex**: The only moment at which all competing Nazi agencies formulated something resembling a coherent overall strategic-economic plan. This moment simultaneously produced: the decision to starve Soviet urban populations (Hunger Plan), Reinhard Heydrich's orders for a Europe-wide "solution to the Jewish Question," and the expansion of Auschwitz as a labor facility for the Generalplan Ost settlement vision. [[the-holocaust]] was thus entangled with economic-military planning from its inception.

**Albert Speer's "Armaments Miracle"** (1942–1944): Speer used occupied Europe's forced labor and food extraction to sustain armaments output under Allied bombing. Unlike the Hindenburg Program of 1917, the home front held together to the bitter end through coercion, SS surveillance, and production as quasi-ideological purpose. "The triumph of production, as such, came to provide a raison d'être for the dogged continuation of a war, a token that all was not yet lost." Peak fighter aircraft output in summer 1944 used "the emaciated remnants of the Jewish population of Hungary, supplied to Speer directly from the Auschwitz railway yard."

**Final arithmetic**: Allied armaments production outpaced Germany's by ratios of 2.7:1 overall (rifles) to 15.6:1 (machine pistols) across major categories. The ratio mirrored the pre-war steel and GDP gap exactly.

## Geographic Spread

The war economy extended across the Reich domestically and, from 1939, across occupied Europe. The Reichsbahn (national railway) was mortgaged to reparations creditors in the 1920s. The Ruhr was the industrial heart; its French occupation in 1923 was the proximate cause of hyperinflation. From 1941, the occupied Soviet territories were planned as a food and raw materials colony; occupied Western Europe provided food transfers, forced laborers, and financial extractions.

## Historiography

Adam Tooze (ch. 18, [[smith-oxford-modern-german-history-2011]]), author of the foundational *Wages of Destruction: The Making and Breaking of the Nazi Economy* (2006), provides the framework.

**Key debates**:
- **Civilian vs. military phase in Nazi economy**: The older consensus (civilian recovery 1933–1936, then militarization) is rejected by Tooze as apologetic. Rearmament was the goal from the outset.
- **State vs. business control**: Rather than business "capturing" the Nazi state (as the US Justice Department argued at Nuremberg against IG Farben), the most accurate characterization is "a productive and mutually profitable partnership" in which corporate priorities blurred with regime priorities.
- **Organizational coherence**: The Nazi war economy was not the monolithic "leviathan" of contemporaries' imagination. It was riven by factional infighting (Schacht vs. Goering; Four Year Plan vs. Wehrmacht vs. SS). But this is not exceptional compared with Roosevelt's New Deal "alphabet soup" of agencies.
- **Modernization vs. catastrophe**: The long-run growth trajectory suggests Germany would have achieved the same per-capita GDP outcomes through peaceful development. The wars and Nazi regime were not a modernizing short-cut but a violent detour that cost tens of millions of lives and required international architecture to contain German economic power afterward.

## The WWII war economy — Evans, *The Third Reich at War* (2008)

**Plunder as fiscal base.** The 1940 conquests gave Germany a sphere of 290 million people with a prewar GDP larger than the USA's. "Occupation costs" were disguised reparations — 25 billion RM from France alone by end-1943 (~20 million RM/day); a rigged clearing system (Reichsmark overvalued >60% in France) left unpaid German debts of 8.5 billion RM to France, ~6 billion to the Netherlands, 5 billion to Belgium/Luxembourg. Over 30% of occupied Western Europe's net wartime production was extracted (France: ~40% of resources, occupation payments ~60% of French public spending by 1943); soldiers' parcel post home from France ran to 3 million packages a month. Evans estimates ~25% of Reich revenues ultimately came from conquest.

**Home-front finance and rations.** The war was financed roughly half by (sharply progressive) taxation and half by occupied-territory exactions plus the silent redirection of savings-bank deposits into arms (8 billion RM in 1940, 12.8 billion in 1941) — deliberately avoiding 1914-style public war loans. Civilian rations fell from 2,570 kcal/day (October 1939) through the April 1942 cuts to collapse by April 1945; soldiers ate several times better by policy. Black-market barter was normalized by 1944. The spring 1943 joke: "In India, one person starves for everybody; in Germany everybody starves for one person."

**The Speer question.** See [[speer-albert]] for the rationalization "miracle" and its limits; [[forced-labor-nazi-germany]] for the labor substitute for unmobilized German women; and [[total-war]] for the 1943 mobilization theatre. By 1944 Germany devoted 75% of GDP to war and still faced an Allied:Axis GDP ratio above 3:1. Source: [[evans-third-reich-at-war-2008]]
