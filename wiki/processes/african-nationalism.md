# African Nationalism

**African nationalism** — the movement for African self-rule against colonial domination — took its modern
form in the colonial decades, above all between the world wars. Documented for the wiki through the **UNESCO
[[gha-vol7-colonial-1880-1935|General History of Africa, Vol. VII]]** (chs. 19–24, "African politics and
nationalism, 1919–35"), it is the bridge between primary
[[african-resistance-to-colonial-rule|resistance]] and full **[[decolonization|decolonization]]**.

## From resistance to nationalism

As armed primary resistance was crushed, opposition shifted into **new political forms**. GHA VII traces
the rise, especially after the **First World War**, of:

- a **Western-educated elite** (lawyers, journalists, teachers, clergy, ex-servicemen) who turned the
  colonizers' own language of rights, self-determination, and Christianity against colonial rule;
- **proto-nationalist associations**, congresses, and a vigorous African **press**, demanding reform,
  representation, and an end to discrimination (e.g. the National Congress of British West Africa, 1920);
- **labour and economic protest** (strikes, cocoa hold-ups, tax revolts) and religious movements
  (independent churches, the **Kimbanguist** and **Watchtower** movements) that fused spiritual and
  anti-colonial energies.

The First World War was a catalyst: it **discredited European claims** to civilizational superiority,
mobilized hundreds of thousands of African soldiers and labourers, and exposed them to wider worlds and
**Wilsonian self-determination** rhetoric.

## Pan-African and diaspora currents

African nationalism drew on **[[pan-africanism|Pan-Africanism]]** and the ideas of the **African diaspora**
— W. E. B. Du Bois's Pan-African Congresses, Marcus Garvey's "Africa for the Africans," the **Négritude**
of Senghor and Césaire — linking the colonized continent to the struggles of African-descended peoples in
the Americas. The **Italian invasion of Ethiopia (1935)** — the assault on Africa's great symbol of
independence — was a galvanizing shock that radicalized Pan-African and nationalist sentiment worldwide.

## Significance

The 1919–35 period was **formative rather than triumphant**: it built the leaders, organizations, ideas,
and grievances on which the post-1945 mass nationalism and **[[decolonization|decolonization]]** would draw.
GHA VII's framing — colonialism generating the forces that destroyed it — runs directly into Vol. VIII.

## The Second World War as catalyst (GHA VIII)

The **UNESCO [[gha-vol8-since-1935|General History of Africa, Vol. VIII]]** (Section I, chs. 2–4) opens with
the **Second World War as the great accelerator** of African nationalism. Africa was a major theatre and
arsenal: the **Italian conquest of Ethiopia (1935)** and the North African campaigns made it a battleground,
and the colonies supplied **soldiers (hundreds of thousands of African troops), labour, and raw materials**.
The war **discredited European prestige** (the fall of France; Japan's defeats of Western powers in Asia),
spread **Atlantic Charter** rhetoric of self-determination, and returned **ex-servicemen** with new
horizons and grievances — so that the **5th Pan-African Congress (1945)** could turn squarely toward
independence. After 1945, nationalism became a **mass movement**.
