# African Resistance to Colonial Conquest

Africans did **not** passively accept the European conquest of the **[[scramble-for-africa|Scramble]]**:
across the continent they **resisted, negotiated, and adapted** — a point that is the organizing argument
of the **UNESCO [[gha-vol7-colonial-1880-1935|General History of Africa, Vol. VII]]** (chs. 2–8), which
replaces the old colonial story of "pacification" with one of **African initiatives and reactions**.

## "Initiatives and reactions," not "pacification"

Editor **A. Adu Boahen** insists that African societies faced conquest with a **range of strategies** —
armed confrontation, diplomacy and alliance, strategic submission, and religious or economic resistance —
chosen rationally according to circumstance. What looks in colonial sources like "pacification" was in fact
the **violent suppression of widespread, determined African resistance**, much of it sustained for years
against overwhelming firepower.

## The great resistances

- **Samori Touré** built a large Mande empire in the western Sudan and fought the **French for nearly two
  decades** (until his capture in 1898) — the most formidable West African resistance.
- **Ethiopia** under **Menelik II** alone *defeated* the Scramble outright, destroying an Italian army at
  **Adwa (1896)** and preserving its independence (see [[solomonic-ethiopia]], [[imperialism]]).
- The **Asante** fought a series of Anglo-Asante wars; the **Zulu** crushed a British column at **Isandlwana
  (1879)** before defeat; the **Ndebele** and Shona rose in the **Chimurenga (1896–97)**.
- The **Maji Maji rebellion (1905–07)** in German East Africa — a vast, religiously-inspired rising whose
  fighters believed sacred water (*maji*) would turn bullets to water — was crushed with a scorched-earth
  famine costing perhaps 250,000–300,000 lives.
- The **Herero and Nama (1904–08)** in German South-West Africa were met with an explicit extermination
  order — the **Herero genocide**, the first genocide of the twentieth century (see [[genocide]]).
- In the north, the heirs of **Abd al-Qadir**, the **Sanusiyya** in Libya, and the Sudanese **Mahdiyya**
  (until Omdurman, 1898) waged sustained Islamic resistance.

## Significance and afterlife

Almost all **primary (armed) resistance failed** against the industrial firepower of the conquerors (the
Maxim gun; see [[imperialism]] on the technological asymmetry). But GHA VII argues it was **not futile**: it
asserted African agency, imposed real costs and delays on the conquerors, and seeded the **memory and
legitimacy** on which twentieth-century **[[african-nationalism|nationalism]]** and
[[decolonization|decolonization]] would later build — primary resistance giving way to the religious,
economic, and political resistance of the colonial decades.
