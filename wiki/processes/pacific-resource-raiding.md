## Definition and Scope

"Resource raiding" — the term used by [[denoon-cambridge-pacific-islanders-1997]] — describes the first wave of systematic European economic engagement with [[oceania]]: the extraction of natural commodities (whales, bêche-de-mer/sea slugs, sandalwood, sealskins, timber, guano, pearl-shell) for Asian and Atlantic markets. It was distinguished from later plantation or mining regimes by its moving-frontier character: vessels prospected from island group to island group, depleting stocks and shifting south and east. Unlike the later plantation economy, resource raiding did not immediately require control over land; it required Islander labour and chiefly co-operation, which gave chiefs significant but temporary leverage.

The process began in the central and northern Pacific in the 1790s, ran its peak from roughly 1800 to the 1860s, and wound down as natural stocks were depleted and conditions began to favour settled plantation agriculture with reliable labour supplies.

## Causal Drivers

- **Asian demand.** Bêche-de-mer (trepang/sea slugs), sandalwood, pearl-shell, and turtle-shell were carried primarily into Chinese and South-East Asian markets. The text notes that "for three centuries after Magellan, most European trade in the Islands responded to Asian demand." The logic of tobacco addiction was captured by Shineberg: teaching Islanders to smoke tobacco so that Chinese could burn incense, so that Australians could drink Chinese tea.
- **Contraction of East Asian maritime trade.** The collapse of the Ming dynasty, Qing continental reorientation, and Tokugawa restrictions on overseas trade contracted pre-existing trade networks, leaving a gap that European vessels filled by 1790s.
- **Trans-Pacific fur trade.** From the 1790s, American and British merchants developed a fur trade from the North American coast to China, requiring resupply stops in Honolulu, Papeete, and Koror — creating the region's first real port towns and inserting Islanders into Atlantic as well as Asian circuits.
- **Settlement of eastern Australia (1788).** The establishment of New South Wales created a regional shipping base that made it possible "to link the Islands into Atlantic markets" for the first time.
- **New England whaling.** Whalers dominated Pacific waters from the 1800s; by the 1830s Hawaiian sailors were so reliably available that "whalers often embarked short-handed from New England, recruiting their full complement at Honolulu. Soon there were perhaps 3000 Hawaiians on whalers."

## Major Phases

### Phase 1 — Asian-circuit trade, pre-1790
Sporadic: western New Guinea supplying slaves, birds of paradise, and sago to the sultanate of Tidore; Chinese and South-East Asian vessels seeking sandalwood and bêche-de-mer in the western Carolines. No systematic European presence.

### Phase 2 — Northern/central Pacific resupply economy, 1790s–1820s
Fur-trade vessels and early whalers treating Honolulu, Papeete, and Koror as resupply depots. Kamehameha I granted himself a sandalwood monopoly in Hawai'i by ~1790 and bought ships to trade directly with China.

### Phase 3 — Bêche-de-mer and sandalwood boom, 1820s–1850s
By 1830 bêche-de-mer was Fiji's leading export; stocks were soon depleted, forcing vessels to spend weeks at anchor and employ scores of Europeans, Fijians, and other Islanders. Sandalwood ran a parallel trajectory: high profits in the first decade, then over-supply and price collapse. "Most traders in these islands were based in Sydney, and at first they could buy sandalwood for trivial amounts of iron, beads and glass, but Islanders soon acquired better knowledge of comparative values."

In [[new-zealand]], bay whaling and sealing attracted Australian adventurers from the 1800s onward; Maori farmers swiftly adopted potatoes and wheat and by the 1850s owned and operated most coastal shipping on the North Island.

### Phase 4 — Melanesian frontier and guano, 1840s–1870s
Sandalwood shifted to Melanesia (New Caledonia, New Hebrides/Vanuatu) in the 1840s. Micronesian nitrates followed. Torres Strait attracted pearling vessels from Sydney, Japan, and further afield by the 1870s.

### Phase 5 — Transition to plantation and company agriculture, 1860s onward
When natural stocks were exhausted and shipping costs fell, the "moving frontier" logic gave way to settled plantations. Copra (dried coconut) emerged as the dominant crop precisely because it was "the least labour-intensive of all the tropical products." The transition required control over land, which set the conditions for the New Zealand Wars and dispossession elsewhere.

## Geographic Spread

- **Polynesia:** Hawai'i (sandalwood, whaling); [[new-zealand]] (sealing, bay whaling, timber, flax, wheat); Tahiti (pork, sandalwood)
- **Melanesia:** Fiji (bêche-de-mer, sandalwood, beachcomber networks); New Caledonia and New Hebrides/Vanuatu (sandalwood from 1840s); Solomons (labour later drawn for Queensland)
- **Micronesia:** Carolines (bêche-de-mer, Yapese aragonite disc trade); Torres Strait (pearl, trochus, turtle-shell, bêche-de-mer)
- **New Zealand / Australia coasts:** Sealskins, New Zealand timber, guano

## Interaction with Other Processes

- **[[pacific-chiefly-state-formation]]:** Chiefly access to trade goods (especially whale's teeth in Fiji, muskets everywhere) fuelled political centralisation. The Vunivalu of Bau "received huge quantities of whale's teeth, which consolidated his regional influence."
- **[[missionary-conversion-pacific]]:** Missionaries and traders often co-arrived; missionaries taught Islander producers that commodity exchange was compatible with Christianity, inadvertently deepening market integration.
- **Melanesian labour trade:** The moving frontier conditioned Islanders to wage labour; when natural resources were exhausted, many recruits moved voluntarily (or under coercion) to Queensland sugar plantations and other plantation zones.
- **[[new-zealand-wars-1860]]:** Maori commercial success and independent shipping (fifty-three vessels of 14+ tons registered in Auckland alone by 1858) fuelled settler competition for land, directly precipitating war.

## End Conditions

Resource raiding ended when:
1. Natural stocks (sandalwood, bêche-de-mer beds, seal populations) were depleted.
2. Bulk shipping enabled lower-value bulk commodities (copra, sugar) to be exported profitably.
3. Colonial annexation (Fiji 1874, New Caledonia 1853, etc.) replaced the informal beach economy with administered plantation or peasant-production regimes.
4. The "moving frontier" reached the last accessible zones (Torres Strait, Melanesian coasts) by the 1870s–1880s.

A residual pearl and bêche-de-mer industry survived in Torres Strait under Queensland regulation into the twentieth century, but it was absorbed into a colonial administrative framework. Only in New Zealand did Islanders master the new technology: "only in New Zealand did Islanders master the new technology, build their own vessels, and (briefly) make profits at sea."

## Historiography

[[denoon-cambridge-pacific-islanders-1997]] situates resource raiding against the backdrop of development theory (Locke, Smith, Rostow), arguing that Islanders were neither passive victims nor orderly beneficiaries but active agents who "deployed whatever political and cultural weapons came to hand." The key secondary sources are Shineberg, *They Came for Sandalwood* (the standard account of the sandalwood trade) and Salisbury, *From Stone to Steel* (on the impact of metal tools on Island economies). Chappell's archival work reconstructs the lives of Islanders on whaling vessels. Debate continues over the degree of Islander agency versus structural constraint — a tension also present in the parallel debate on the Queensland labour trade (Munro, 'Revisionism and its Enemies' vs. Brass, 'The Return of "Merrie Melanesia"').

## Collection Coverage Note

The Cambridge Pacific Islanders volume provides solid synthesis coverage of the whaling, sandalwood, and bêche-de-mer trades for Polynesia and Fiji. Melanesian coverage is thinner. The Torres Strait pearl economy is treated briefly. No dedicated monograph on the bêche-de-mer trade is yet ingested.
