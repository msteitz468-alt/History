# Self-Strengthening (Responses to Western Power)

## Definition and Scope

"**Self-strengthening**" — originally the name (*ziqiang*) of the Qing reform program
of the 1860s–90s — is used by R. Bin Wong in [[mcneill-cwh-v7-2015|CWH VII]] as a
general category for the **spectrum of political responses** by which states outside
the North Atlantic core confronted the wealth and power of industrializing Europe and
the United States. The common project, in Wong's formulation, was to "integrate
emulation of Western practices producing power and wealth with domestic political
priorities and economic institutions" — to borrow selectively enough to survive
without surrendering sovereignty or social order.

## Causal Drivers

The [[long-19th-century|nineteenth century]] was "Europe's century": British naval
and economic dominance, the [[industrialization|second industrial revolution]], and
the [[imperialism|scramble for colonies]] confronted every other polity with a
**contradictory compound of pressure and promise**. Late-century globalization
produced **convergence toward industrial modernity for only a few** (Western Europe
and some white settler colonies) and, for most others, **reinforced the gap** between
rich and poor, powerful and weak. Wong frames the question as a balance — was
divergence due to the **failures** of non-Western leaders to adopt "best practices,"
or to the **success** of Western powers in subordinating others' economies
(Wallerstein's world-system)? — and declines to resolve it.

## The Spectrum of Responses

Wong's chapter is explicitly **global and comparative**, ranging across Eurasia:

- **The Ottoman Empire:** the **Tanzimat** ("Reorganization," 1839–1876) — military,
  administrative, fiscal, legal, and economic reform; arsenals, factories, railways
  (from 1866). Constrained by territorial losses, weak economic elites, and a
  religious counter-vision of reform distinct from the European constitutional model.
- **Russia:** emulation from Peter the Great; the **emancipation of the serfs (1861)**
  and zemstvo reforms (1864); state-led industrialization under **Sergei Witte** (from
  1892) drawing French, German, and British capital and technology transfers.
- **The Habsburg Empire:** modest, uneven industrialization (the Czech lands) within a
  multinational dynastic structure ill-fitted to nationalist demands.
- **The United States:** the most successful at "becoming strong and rich" — abundant
  land and resources, a small population, British-derived institutions, and (like
  Britain) a **high-wage economy** that favored capital-intensive industrialization
  (see [[great-divergence]], [[united-states]]).
- **East Asia (the paradigm cases):** **Meiji Japan** (from 1868) and the **Qing
  self-strengthening movement** — the states that "withstood the pressures" of
  colonial expansion by partial accommodation. Wong stresses that in East Asia this
  "worked well enough" to keep various indigenous ideas and institutions of
  **continuing global relevance down to today**.

## Geographic Spread

The "in-between" zone — neither fully colonized like much of Africa and South/Southeast
Asia, nor at the industrial core — comprised the Ottoman, Russian, and Habsburg empires
and the formally independent East Asian states (China, Japan, Korea) and Siam.

## Interaction

Self-strengthening is the obverse of [[imperialism]] (it is what informal empire left
room for) and a partial cause of the later, faster diffusion of [[industrialization]]
to East Asia and thus of the **partial reversal of the [[great-divergence|Great
Divergence]]**. It overlaps [[nationalism]] (reform in the name of the people/nation)
and prefigures twentieth-century [[decolonization]] and developmentalism.

## The Chinese case (Spence)

Jonathan Spence's [[spence-search-for-modern-china|*Search for Modern China*]] gives the Chinese
self-strengthening movement its narrative: the **Tongzhi Restoration** reformers — the very
provincial statesmen (Zeng Guofan, **Li Hongzhang**, Zuo Zongtang) who had crushed the
**[[taiping-rebellion|Taiping]]** — who from the 1860s built arsenals, shipyards, and a modern
navy and adopted Western technology while preserving the Confucian order ("Chinese learning for
the essence, Western learning for use"). Its inadequacy was exposed by humiliating defeat in the
**[[first-sino-japanese-war-1894-1895|Sino-Japanese War (1895)]]** — Japan's
[[meiji-modernization|fuller modernization]] outpacing China's partial reform — which in turn
spurred the more radical **1898 reform movement**, the Boxer catastrophe, and ultimately the
[[fall-of-the-qing-1911|1911 Revolution]].

## End Conditions

The classic self-strengthening era closes with the First World War and the collapse of
the multinational empires. Wong's larger point is that emulation-with-adaptation, far
from a failed imitation of the West, seeded durable East Asian models — most visibly
in the [[industrious-revolution|labor-intensive]] developmental path that flowered
after 1950 (see [[contemporary]]).
