## Definition and Scope

The relationship between **food production and the rise of cities** — long
treated as the foundational link in the "traditional conceptualization of the
city" (Gallagher & McIntosh, CWH Vol. II, Ch. 7; see also CWH Vol. III on early
cities). Successful agriculture allowed villages and towns to emerge; large
settlements in turn became **catalysts for agricultural innovation**. This page
covers that two-way relationship and, crucially, the volume's challenge to the
standard top-down model of it.

## Beyond the top-down narrative

The "standard narrative" holds that agriculture was a simplistic, irreversible
driver pushing societies toward civilization, elite-controlled surplus, and the
elaboration of political/social systems — that urban zones were "highly
centralized systems abstracted from [a] hinterland . . . under despotic control."
Gallagher & McIntosh argue this was **not the case everywhere**. The relationship
between centres and peripheries, urban spaces and hinterlands, took many forms;
complex decision-making and control were often **negotiated** among many points
of concentration rather than dictated by urban elites.

## Heterarchy vs. hierarchy

The key analytical concept is **[[heterarchy]]** (Carole Crumley): a system whose
elements are ranked in *multiple ways* or are *unranked*, as opposed to a single
vertical **hierarchy**. Heterarchical urbanism distributes power among
horizontally related, self-identified **corporate groups** (groups holding real
or symbolic property in common) rather than concentrating it in a despotic elite.
This debate is now developed in its own page —
[[hierarchy-vs-heterarchy-early-cities]] — and elaborated across
[[yoffee-cwh-v3-2015|CWH Vol. III]] Part IV.

## Case study: Jenne-jeno (middle Niger, Mali)

The volume's exemplar is **Jenne-jeno**, a tell rising 7 m over the middle Niger
floodplain, founded in the 3rd century BCE and, with ~70 contemporaneous tells
within 4 km, abandoned around 1400 CE. It deliberately *breaks* the standard
"archaic urbanism" model:
- It shows nucleation, population growth, scale, and internal differentiation —
  but **lacks** subsistence intensification, clear vertical stratification, and
  public monuments ("cities without citadels," S. & R. McIntosh).
- Its many satellite mounds housed **spatially discrete specialist groups** —
  distinct fishing styles, aquatic-mammal hunting, weaving, and especially
  **iron-smelting** (a dangerous, occult-associated craft that migrated out to
  satellites over time).
- The urban complex was built on **reciprocal relationships among specialized
  corporate groups**, *not* on a single elite extracting agricultural surplus.
  It served a region far larger than its own farming hinterland.

Jenne-jeno is the volume's proof that urbanism could arise **without** despotic
agricultural surplus-extraction — a heterarchical alternative drawn, pointedly,
from sub-Saharan Africa.
