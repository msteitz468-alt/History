# Trans-Saharan Trade

## Definition and Scope

This page tracks the rise of regular commerce across the **Sahara** — "the world's largest
desert" — connecting Mediterranean North Africa (the **Maghrib**) with the West and Central
African savannah (the **Bilad es-Sudan**), as set out in [[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|CWH IV]]
(Austen, Ch 24; Burstein, Ch 23). Its establishment is the volume's emblem of the "birth of
**Afro-Eurasia**": the moment tropical Africa entered "the mainstream of world history."

## The Two Barriers and the Camel

For most of antiquity the Sahara was a barrier as formidable as the South Atlantic. The
**Holocene wet phase** (c. 9000–3000 BCE) had supported a populous, fishing-and-herding Sahara;
its **desiccation** (complete by ~300 CE) pushed populations south and severed easy contact for
over two millennia. Crossing the desert required (1) **goods valuable enough** to justify the
cost and risk, and (2) a viable **transport animal**. The decisive technology was the
**domesticated camel**, adopted in North Africa around the turn of the Christian era — yet a
long gap separates the camel's arrival from sustained trade, a gap CWH IV attributes not to
technology but to **politics and culture**.

## The Commodities

The trade's logic was complementary scarcity: the Sudan needed concentrated, transportable
**salt** (and copper) that the desert supplied in mined blocks; the Mediterranean wanted Sudanic
**gold** (from Bambuk, Buré, and Guinea) and **slaves**. Manufactured **textiles, glass,
ceramics, and metalware** flowed south. Because salt, gold, and slaves leave few archaeological
traces, the trade's **early phases are hard to date** — the crux of a long scholarly dispute
(see [[pre-islamic-trans-saharan-trade]]).

## Precursors and Establishment

The **[[garamantes|Garamantes]]** of the Fezzan ran the strongest pre-Islamic cross-desert
networks (slaves and luxuries) in the early centuries CE. But **regular, large-scale**
trans-Saharan trade is securely attested only from the **eighth–ninth centuries CE**: gold-coin
moulds at **Tadmekka**, and the rise of desert-edge entrepôts. CWH IV (Austen) stresses the
**paradoxical role of dissident Islam**: the **Kharijite** Berber states — the Ibadi
**Rustamids** of Tahert and the Sufri **Sijilmasa** — pioneered the trade, while the Berber
**Mazata** revived the old Garamantian routes from **Zawila** to **Kanem**. Trade long carried
a "**reprehensible**" reputation among orthodox (Maliki Sunni) scholars; only with the spread
of orthodox Islam across both shores of the desert — and the conversion of Sudanic rulers like
those of **[[ghana-empire|Ghana]]** and **Kanem** — did the two sides fuse into a "common
culture," a process not complete until after 1000 CE.

## Interaction and Significance

Trans-Saharan trade is the West African counterpart to the [[silk-roads|Silk Roads]] and the
[[indian-ocean-trade|Indian Ocean]] networks — and, like them, a carrier of **religion as much
as goods**. Islam spread south **by commerce and the conversion of rulers, not conquest**,
producing the accommodating Sahelian Islam later observed by Ibn Battuta. Together with the Red
Sea route that raised **[[aksum|Aksum]]**, it reintegrated Africa into a civilization
"extending from Afghanistan to the Atlantic," completing the **reconnection of sub-Saharan
Africa to Eurasia**.

## The Western Sudanic States (CWH V, Conrad)

[[kedar-cwh-v5-2015|CWH V]] (David C. Conrad, Ch 22) extends the story to 1500, tracing
the three great empires that "drew significant resources from long-distance and
trans-Saharan trade in gold, copper, iron tools and weapons, slaves, salt, and other
goods": [[ghana-empire|Ghana/Wagadu]], [[mali-empire|Mali]] (apogee under
[[mansa-musa|Mansa Musa]], whose 1324 hajj advertised Sahelian gold across the Old
World), and [[songhay-empire|Gao/Songhay]]. Conrad stresses that these states
**gained political dominance by controlling trading centers and the sources of
artisanal, agricultural, and mineral goods**, and that their history must be
reconstructed by combining the Arabic record with **archaeology, epigraphy,
linguistics, and oral tradition** (the Mande Sunjata epic). The gold flowing north
across the Sahara was a principal bullion source for the
[[afro-eurasian-world-system|Afro-Eurasian economy]] and the European
[[commercial-revolution|commercial revolution]] — until the Atlantic reorientation of
trade and the gunpowder Moroccan conquest of Songhay (1591) ended the system's
medieval form.
