# The Mycenaean Palace Economy

## Definition and Scope

The **Mycenaean palace economy** is the administered economic system documented by the
**Linear B** tablets (deciphered by Michael Ventris, 1952) at Knossos, Pylos, Thebes,
Mycenae, Tiryns, and Midea, running from roughly 1400 to the destructions of c. 1200 BCE
([[bronze-age-collapse-1200bce]]). John Bennet's chapter in the *Cambridge Economic
History of the Greco-Roman World* ([[sources/scheidel-cambridge-economic-history-greco-roman-world-2007]])
is the source for this page. It is **not** a comprehensive command economy: the palaces
administered a *selective* share of production — Whitelaw's calculation that the Pylos
palace's own ceramic consumption implies a palatial share of regional pottery output of at
most ~2% is the clearest evidence that most economic activity occurred entirely outside
palatial record-keeping.

## Causal Drivers

- **Linear B as an exclusively administrative script.** Used from c. 1400 BCE only for
  economic/administrative accounting — never legal, literary, or narrative — meaning the
  surviving record is intrinsically a record of palatial extraction and allocation, not of
  the wider economy.
- **The talasia (ta-ra-si-ja) system.** A weighed raw-material allocation (wool, bronze)
  issued to workers/officials who returned a finished product of equal weight — documented
  in exhaustive detail for the Knossos wool/textile industry (~600 flock-census tablets
  recording ~66,000 sheep, implying a total flock up to 100,000) and applied identically to
  Pylian bronze-working.
- **Land tenure structuring elite power.** The **wanax** ("lord," later Greek anax) held
  the largest temenos estate; the **lawagetas** ("leader of the host") held one-third that
  size (Pylos Er 312). Below them, officials — **hekwetai** ("followers"), **gwasileus**
  (later basileus, local craft supervisors), **ko-re-te** district officials — administered
  communities (**damo**) that could hold or lease (**o-na-to**) land.

## Major Phases

1. **Emergence (c. 1900–1450 BCE).** Palatial societies appear first on Minoan Crete
   (first palaces c. 1900 BCE), then on the Mycenaean mainland (palaces from the
   late 15th–early 14th century BCE).
2. **Mainland takeover of Knossos (~1450–1400 BCE).** Mycenaean administrators adopt and
   adapt Linear B for Crete; two major Knossos tablet horizons (late 15th c., mid-14th c.).
3. **Mature palatial administration (14th–13th c. BCE).** The system reaches its fullest
   documented form at Pylos (~1,100 tablets, 13th century) — wool/textile talasia,
   Ma-series polity-wide commodity assessments, feasting for 1,000+ people, corvée-built
   public works (Kopaïs drainage and Gla fortifications, the Mycenae road network, the
   Tiryns dam, a probable Pylos harbor basin).
4. **Continuity after Knossos's fall.** Chania on Crete yields three Linear B tablets from
   the mid-13th century, showing the administrative system persisted locally after
   Knossos's own destruction.
5. **Collapse (c. 1250–1150 BCE).** Destructions/abandonments across Mycenae, Tiryns,
   Midea, Pylos, and Thebes concentrate in this window; no archaeological evidence supports
   a new-population invasion for this exact period.

## Geographic Spread

Centered on the Aegean palatial sites — Knossos and Chania on Crete; Pylos, Mycenae,
Tiryns, Thebes, and Midea on the mainland — but linked into the wider eastern
Mediterranean exchange system, as shown by three Late Bronze Age shipwrecks: **Uluburun**
(before 1300 BCE, likely elite/state-sponsored, carrying 10 tons of copper, ~1 ton of tin,
175 blue glass ingots, and a broken gold scarab of Nefertiti), **Cape Gelidonya** (c. 1200
BCE, likely independent scrap-metal trading), and **Point Iria** (c. 1200 BCE, likely
intra-Aegean).

## Interaction

- **Finley/Killen "redistribution" vs. "mobilization" debate.** Finley (1957), drawing on
  Polanyi, characterized the palace economy as "a massive redistributive operation."
  Killen's refinement — the term "mobilization" (per Earle), "the recruitment of goods and
  services for the benefit of a group not coterminous with the contributing members" —
  better captures the palace's selective, non-comprehensive extraction, which Bennet
  concludes is best termed a **"command economy"** geared to palatial activities rather
  than the whole regional economy.
- **Exotic-materials control.** Ivory, blue glass (kuwanos, imported as raw bun ingots from
  Mesopotamia via routes attested at Uluburun), and gold were worked under tighter,
  centralized control than staples — a distinction from the looser control over grain and
  ceramic production.
- Feeds directly into [[periods/late-bronze-age]] and the standing
  [[bronze-age-collapse-1200bce|Bronze Age Collapse]] controversy; the aftermath is treated
  in [[processes/early-iron-age-greek-economy]].

## End Conditions

The system ended with the wave of Aegean palatial destructions concentrated c. 1250–1150
BCE. Causes remain debated and multi-causal — local political collapse, the breakdown of
long-distance exchange monopolies (tied to the Hittite collapse, the destruction of Ugarit
c. 1182 BCE, and Egyptian retraction from Syria-Palestine), earthquakes, and/or
agricultural shortfalls — and are not resolvable given the period's poor chronological
resolution. With the palaces gone, the Linear B script itself disappears, and the
successor economy is the diminished, non-literate oikos economy of the Early Iron Age
(see [[processes/early-iron-age-greek-economy]]).
