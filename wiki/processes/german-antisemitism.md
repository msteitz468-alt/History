## Definition and Scope

This process page covers German antisemitism as a continuous ideological and political dynamic from 1870 to 1945, distinguishing its multiple forms and tracing the structural conditions under which it escalated from social prejudice and legal discrimination to genocidal mass murder. The process is explicitly distinguished from [[the-holocaust]] (which covers the event of mass murder as such) and from [[german-antisemitism]] as an abstract idea: this page tracks the socio-political process through which anti-Jewish ideology became state policy and ultimately genocide.

Three forms of antisemitism require analytical distinction throughout:
1. **Religious anti-Judaism**: the traditional Christian charge of deicide, which mandated conversion, segregation, or expulsion but not necessarily physical extermination
2. **Social antisemitism**: cultural, economic, and social exclusion of Jews from bourgeois or nationalist institutions, without an explicitly racial or biological framework
3. **Racial antisemitism**: the pseudo-scientific claim that Jews constitute a biologically fixed racial category, unassimilable through conversion or acculturation, whose influence on "Aryan" society is by definition pathological and must be eliminated

These three forms were not always clearly distinguished in practice, and their interaction — and the conditions under which the third form became dominant — is a central question of the historiography.

Sources: [[smith-oxford-modern-german-history-2011]], Chapters 20, 22, 24.

## Major Phases

### Phase 1: Liberal nationalism and the Antisemitismusstreit (1870–1890)

The founding of the Second Empire coincided with the legal emancipation of German Jews (1871), granting full civic equality after decades of partial and contested emancipation in individual states. The liberal assumption was that equality would produce assimilation, and that assimilation would dissolve the "Jewish question."

The **Anti-Semitism Dispute (*Antisemitismusstreit*, 1879–1881)** began with historian Heinrich von Treitschke's essay "Our Views" (*Unsere Aussichten*, 1879), which characterizes Jews as "German-speaking Orientals" and demands their assimilation into the Protestant bourgeoisie. Treitschke's targets are the Ostjuden — Eastern European Jewish immigrants — whom he describes as "ambitious trouser-selling youths … forcing their way across our eastern border from the inexhaustible Polish cradle." The dispute reveals that even liberal nationalism set conditional terms for Jewish belonging: Jews could be Germans only by becoming Protestant-bourgeois in all relevant cultural respects.

This episode belongs to what Rebekka Habermas calls the "reconceptualization of the secular and the religious": Orthodox Jews, like Catholics, were coded as belonging to a "religious" sphere with negative connotations of irrationalism and foreign loyalty. Assimilated, liberal Jews were allowed into the Protestant-coded "secular" realm. The social pressure to perform cultural invisibility is thus built into the logic of Wilhelmine liberalism, not only into explicit antisemitic agitation.

The **Conservative Party's Tivoli Program (1892)** proclaimed intention to "combat the widely obtruding and decomposing Jewish influence" and demanded "a Christian authority for the Christian people." This represents an antisemitism of cultural-religious victimhood — Christians defending themselves against Jewish economic and cultural power — rather than biological racism. Pieter Judson (Chapter 22) notes this form "had little to do with ideas about the German nation as such."

### Phase 2: Racial-nationalist crystallization (1890–1914)

The Pan German League, Eastern Marches Society, and Navy League popularized ethnic definitions of German nationhood after 1890. Bismarck's departure removed the constraint his personal prestige had imposed on the most radical nationalists, who now openly challenged the government's alleged "half measures."

Racial antisemitism emerged within this context as a coherent worldview for the Pan German fringe. The colonies provided a practical arena for working out racial categories: disputes over interracial marriage in Southwest Africa, and the legal status of children of "mixed-race" unions, introduced race as a legal-administrative concept. Massimo Ferrari Zumbini argues that migration flows in the 1890s produced "a merging of anti-Slavism and anti-Semitism that was understood in expressly racial terms."

The Zionist movement emerged partly as a response to the demonstrated impossibility of full assimilation. Theodor Herzl's *Der Judenstaat* (1896) accepted the antisemitic premise that Jews could not become Europeans and argued for the logical consequence: a Jewish national home.

### Phase 3: World War I, the Stab-in-the-Back Legend, and Radicalization (1914–1933)

The war produced new forms of antisemitism:

**The *Judenzählung* (Jewish census, 1916)**: The Prussian military conducted a census of Jewish soldiers, ostensibly to refute antisemitic claims that Jews avoided military service. The results (showing Jews served proportionally) were suppressed; the census itself became evidence for antisemites of Jewish cowardice.

**The Stab-in-the-Back (*Dolchstoßlegende*)**: Following defeat, right-wing nationalists attributed Germany's collapse not to military failure but to domestic subversion by socialists and Jews. "Many German and Russian nationalists openly equated Bolshevism with Jews, arguing that a rising tide of foreign Jewish influence in German society had produced the German Revolution in 1918–1919" (Chapter 22). This constituted a medicalized, racialized construction of national difference: Jewish influence as disease, infection, poison.

**Freikorps violence (1919–1923)**: Bands of nationalist veterans in the Baltic region sought to hold "Slavic or Jewish Bolshevism" at bay. The equation of Jew and Bolshevik became operational in frontier violence.

**Weimar antisemitism**: Jews were systematically associated in antisemitic media with the downsides of modernity — "capitalist economy gone awry, blatant materialism, even socialism, rapid change, or the new cigarette-smoking femininity with its bobbed hair and wild dance parties." Anti-Semitic riots increased even in areas of long-established Jewish-Christian coexistence. The Eastern European Jewish immigrants who arrived during and after the war became particular targets.

### Phase 4: National Socialist antisemitism as state policy (1933–1945)

Hagen's Chapter 24 provides the most detailed analysis of how Nazi antisemitism functioned:

**1933–1935**: The regime purged Jews from public employment, academia, medicine, law, and entertainment. These purges rewarded Nazi followers with vacated positions. Grassroots anti-Jewish violence (SA and Hitler Youth) was tolerated strategically but often suppressed when it interfered with higher political priorities (economic stabilization, foreign relations).

**The Nuremberg Laws (1935)**: Stripped Jews of citizenship. Hitler declared they would establish "tolerable relations with the Jewish people," enabling Jews to "pursue their own *völkisch* life" — hypocritically presenting segregation as a stabilizing settlement. The short-term aim was to "normalize" and "legalize" segregated Jewish life and deflect foreign criticism while pursuing rearmament.

**Aryanization (1936–1938)**: Expropriation of Jewish businesses to offset the colossal costs of rearmament. The "wild aryanization" in Austria after the *Anschluss* (March 1938) accelerated antisemitic radicalism within the party.

**Kristallnacht (November 9–10, 1938)**: See [[kristallnacht-1938]] for detailed treatment. The pogrom "inaugurated a government-managed terminal confiscation of Jewish assets in Germany and a massive wave of Jewish emigration."

**1939–1941**: Anti-Jewish policy under the war focused on expelling Jews from incorporated Polish territories and ghettoizing Polish Jews. The Madagascar Plan (proposing deportation to the French colonial island) was abandoned when Britain refused to capitulate.

**The transition to mass murder (1941–1942)**: The invasion of the Soviet Union (June 1941) transformed the scale of anti-Jewish violence. SS Einsatzgruppen shot a half-million Jews by the end of 1941, including the entire Jewish population of Lithuania-Baltic. On December 12, 1941, following the US declaration of war, Hitler announced that "the world war is here, the extermination of the Jews must be its necessary consequence." The Wannsee Conference (January 1942) coordinated government agencies for deportation and murder.

## Intentionalism vs. Functionalism

The central historiographical dispute over the Holocaust is directly relevant here:

**Intentionalist** accounts (Friedländer, Kershaw) emphasize the centrality of Hitler's personal antisemitic ideology — his "redemptive antisemitism" — as the driving force. The Holocaust represents the triumph of Hitler's will, breaking through circumstances that had temporarily constrained it.

**Functionalist/structuralist** accounts (Hans Mommsen, Martin Broszat) emphasize "cumulative radicalization" driven by bureaucratic competition among agencies, each attempting to "work toward the Führer" by maximizing the harshness of anti-Jewish measures, without a single decision or blueprint.

The current consensus (Browning, Longerich) holds that the transition to mass murder was a **process** of escalating decisions responding to wartime circumstances, particularly: (1) the capture of millions of east European Jews; (2) failed deportation plans; (3) food-supply pressures requiring the "elimination" of "useless eaters"; (4) the American entry into the war.

Hagen's "three horsemen" framework adds a third term: Volksgemeinschaft. The domestic social promise of the Nazi racial state **required** east European empire for its material fulfillment; empire generated the conditions for mass murder; anti-Jewish ideology prescribed it. Neither antisemitism alone nor empire alone explains the Holocaust — only their convergence does.

## Historiography

The German-language historiography of antisemitism is among the richest in modern historical scholarship:
- Shulamit Volkov, *Germans, Jews, and Anti-Semites* (2006): antisemitism as a "cultural code"
- Massimo Ferrari Zumbini, *Die Wurzeln des Bösen* (2003): long-term roots of political antisemitism from Bismarck to Hitler
- Saul Friedländer, *Nazi Germany and the Jews* (2 vols, 1997–2007): the definitive synthesis emphasizing "redemptive antisemitism"
- Peter Longerich, *Holocaust* (2010): comprehensive account of Nazi persecution and murder
- Raul Hilberg, *The Destruction of the European Jews* (3rd ed., 2003): unrivaled empirical depth on bureaucratic process

**Collection coverage**: The Oxford Handbook chapter (Hagen) is the primary collection source. The broader secondary literature cited above is not directly available in the Bibliotheca Alexandrina.
