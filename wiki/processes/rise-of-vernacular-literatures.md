# The Rise of the European Vernaculars

## Definition and Scope

This is the long process by which the spoken languages of Europe — Romance, Germanic,
Celtic, and Slavonic — became **written and literary languages**, rising toward their
thirteenth-century maturity in competition with Latin as the medium of religion,
learning, literature, and administration. [[abulafia-ncmh-v5-2000|NCMH V]] (Colin
Smith, Ch. 4) stresses that this was "far from a uniform or steady process": it ran on
different timetables in each region, and its history is one of **productive symbiosis
with Latin**, not simple replacement.

## Causal Drivers

- **The divergence of Romance from Latin.** Following Roger Wright's influential (and
  still contested) thesis, the awareness that spoken Romance had diverged from written
  Latin came *late*. Until c. 800 a single Latin orthography served all users: a text
  in standard spelling could be read aloud with whatever regional adjustments made it
  intelligible — exactly as written English today is read with many pronunciations.
- **Carolingian liturgical reform.** The decisive break was unintended.
  [[carolingian-renaissance|Charlemagne's]] adviser **Alcuin** of York insisted each
  Latin letter be given a fixed "classical" phonetic value in chant and recitation. The
  effect was to *create* "medieval Latin" as a language firmly marked off from the
  spoken vernaculars — and, once the reformed liturgy became incomprehensible to the
  unlettered, to force the stipulation (Council of Tours, 813) that **sermons be
  preached in the vernacular**, Romance or Frankish. The reform reached Spain beyond
  Catalonia only with the replacement of the Mozarabic liturgy (Council of Burgos,
  1080), which is why would-be-Latin documents enclosing Romance vocabulary persisted
  there until c. 1100.
- **Alphabets for the unwritten tongues.** Pagan peoples first felt the need to write
  with brief inscriptions (Irish **ogam**, Germanic **runic** alphabets), later adapting
  the Latin alphabet for long texts: Old Irish (c. 600), Old High German (later 8th c.),
  Old English (West Saxon, under [[alfred-the-great|Alfred]]), Icelandic (after 1000);
  the Slavic languages received the **Glagolitic/Cyrillic** alphabet from
  [[cyrillo-methodian-mission|Cyril and Methodius]] (mid-9th c.) on a Greek base.

## Major Phases

- **Early written vernaculars (9th–11th c.)** — the *Oaths of Strasbourg* (842), early
  Provençal (the *Boecis*), the Old French of the emergent *chanson de geste*.
- **Courtly explosion (12th c.)** — the Provençal lyric of *fin'amors* spread its forms
  across Europe (northern France, Galicia-Portugal, the *Minnesänger* of southern
  Germany, later Sicily under [[frederick-ii-hohenstaufen|Frederick II]]); the *chansons
  de geste* (the "Oxford" *Chanson de Roland*, c. 1100, associated with the spirit of the
  [[crusades|First Crusade]]) and the romances of Chrétien de Troyes.
- **Thirteenth-century maturity and standardization** — francien (the dialect of Paris
  and the court) triumphed over Picard, Norman, and Champenois; the scribes of
  **[[alfonso-x-castile|Alfonso X]]** (r. 1252–84) standardized Castilian (Toledo norms)
  and made the vernacular royal policy for all writing but international diplomacy; the
  conquest of Valencia (1238) and Sicily and the prosperity of Barcelona fostered
  Catalan; and Tuscan rose on the "expressive and intellectual power" of Dante (whose
  *De vulgari eloquentia*, c. 1305, is the most penetrating medieval reflection on these
  questions), Petrarch, and Boccaccio.

## Geographic Spread and Interaction

Literary forms migrated with surprising ease across linguistic borders, because a
language was not yet firmly tied to a political frontier or to national identity (a tie
that "too would change by 1300"). The Arthurian matter passed from Geoffrey of
Monmouth's Latin *Historia* through Wace's French *Brut* (1155) to Layamon's English; the
*Roland* was adapted into Navarrese, German, Norse, Welsh, and Middle English. In Spain,
**Arabic** functioned as a "classical" language alongside Latin: translation moved from
Arabic-into-Latin under twelfth-century Toledo patronage to Arabic-into-Castilian under
Alfonso X. The relationship to Latin is best seen as **symbiosis** — the very
internationalism of Latin fostered a parallel internationalism among the vernaculars.

## End Conditions

The process did not end so much as consolidate: by c. 1300 the major vernaculars
possessed mature literatures and increasingly served as **chancery and law-code
languages** (rex Francie not rex Francorum — see [[abulafia-ncmh-v5-2000|the volume's
introduction]] on the territorialization of kingship), beginning the slow association of
language with **nationhood** that matures in the later Middle Ages.

## The Fourteenth-Century Flowering (NCMH VI)

[[jones-ncmh-v6-2000|NCMH VI]] (Nick Havely, Ch. 10) treats the fourteenth century as the moment the
vernacular achieved full literary **authority and self-consciousness**. **Dante's** *De Vulgari
Eloquentia* (early in the century) theorised the "illustrious" vernacular and named its three high
subjects — "prowess in arms, the flames of love, and the direction of the will" — and the *Commedia*
demonstrated it; the "**three crowns of Florence**" (Dante, **Petrarch**, **Boccaccio**) wrote in
both Latin and Italian, negotiating a shifting *rapprochement* (Auerbach) between the learned and
the mother tongue (Boccaccio's *Teseida* claiming to be the first to "make the Muses sing of... war
in the Italian mother tongue"). The same flowering produced **Chaucer** and Langland in English,
and a rich French courtly literature (Machaut, Froissart) — the vernacular now the vehicle of major
art, increasingly tied to the court and to emerging national identity. It is the literary
counterpart of [[international-gothic|International Gothic]] and of the century's "greater weight to
the voice of the laity."
