# West and Northwest Mexico Prehistory

## Definition and Scope

Long-term cultural development in **Western and Northwestern Mexico** as reconstructed
by Shirley S. Gorenstein (CHNPA II.1 Ch. 7) — the empirical foundation for
[[greater-mesoamerica|Greater Mesoamerica]]. Until the late twentieth century the region
was often treated as a Mesoamerican “backwater” under a Central Mexican
(Aztec-centric) model; when complex culture was found, it was still sometimes declared
“not Mesoamerican” because it did not match the Central Mexican civilizational form.
Gorenstein’s premise is the reverse: West/NW Mexico was an **integral** civilization
zone — large dense settlements with public functions, stratified specialized populations,
and material culture reflecting those conditions — comparable in social structure and
cultural content, not only by contiguity.

**Geographic bounds (Gorenstein).** Western Mexico is roughly coastal west to ~101° W,
including (whole or part) Sinaloa, Durango, Nayarit, Zacatecas, Jalisco, Guanajuato,
Michoacán, and Guerrero. Northwestern Mexico includes parts of Jalisco, Durango,
Zacatecas, Chihuahua, and Sonora. Combining tectonic/natural regions with archaeological
data yields **six archaeological regions**: (1) Western Mesa Central (Nayarit, Jalisco);
(2) Central Mesa Central (Guanajuato, Michoacán); (3) Balsas–Tepalcatepec Basin
(Michoacán, Guerrero); (4) Coastal West Mexico (Guerrero–Sinaloa); (5) Sonora and
northern Sinaloa; (6) Sierra Madre Occidental and Western Mesa del Norte (eastern Sonora,
Chihuahua, Zacatecas, Durango). There is no scholarly consensus on finer internal
regions; modern-state units still dominate the literature.

## Causal Drivers

- **Autochthonous sequences** across shaft-tomb/Teuchitlán, Chupícuaro, Michoacán
  ceremonial centers, coastal complexes, and northern village traditions.
- **Selective interaction** with Central Mexican civilizations ([[teotihuacan]], later
  Tula and the [[aztec-empire|Aztec]] world) without reducing West Mexico to a
  periphery.
- **Long-distance exchange** — inland turquoise/mining corridor and coastal
  metallurgy/cotton/parrot corridor binding the U.S. Southwest, northern Mexico, and
  the Mesoamerican heartland.
- **West Mexican metallurgy** (from ~A.D. 800), with technical affinities to Ecuador,
  Colombia, and lower Central America — a distinctive high-culture contribution
  (*contributed_to:* [[mesoamerica-andes-pacific-contacts]]).
- **Late Postclassic administrative state formation** in the Lake Pátzcuaro basin
  ([[purepecha|Purépecha]] / Tarascan).

## Major Phases

### Preclassic–Protoclassic: shaft tombs, Teuchitlán, Chupícuaro

The **West Mexican tomb complex** (Magdalena Lake Basin and related) is
radiocarbon-dated from the **2nd century B.C. to the 5th century A.D.** Tombs encode
supernatural ideas distinct from other Greater Mesoamerican regions of the same or
earlier times; cultural precedents for tomb building have been widely linked to South
America — a hypothesis strengthened, for many archaeologists, by later West Mexican
metallurgy with South American technical affinities (though metallurgy appears centuries
later).

**Phil Weigand** documented 112 tomb-complex-period sites in the Jalisco highland lake
system (Magdalena Lake Basin: lacustrine resources, intensive agriculture, nearby
obsidian and copper). Site hierarchy grades from hunting stations to **Ahualulco**
(Type 1): six mound groups and hierarchical tombs implying hierarchical lineages.
**Teuchitlán tradition** phases: San Felipe (~1000–300 B.C.); El Arenal (~350/300 B.C.–
A.D. 150/200, tomb-complex peak); Ahualulco (A.D. 200–400); Teuchitlán I (400–700);
Teuchitlán II (700–1000). Weigand argues **statelike organization** in the Ahualulco
phase, Central Mexican connection in the Teuchitlán phase, decline of the tomb complex
as ballcourts amplify, then Huistla (~800–1200) and Etzatlán (~1200–Conquest).

Contemporary non-tomb cultures include Betty Bell’s **El Cerro Encantado** (NE Jalisco,
~A.D. 100) with hollow redware figurines linked to **Chupícuaro**, and Isabel Kelly’s
**Tuxcacuesco** complex (southern Jalisco), partly contemporary with the later tomb
complex and connected to Colima’s Ortices phase.

**Chupícuaro** (Lerma River Valley, Guanajuato; excavated 1949 before Solís dam
flooding) provides the richest Late Preclassic information for the Mesa Central: ~400
graves; black-brown and elaborate geometric polychrome wares; hollow/solid figurines.
Braniff & Florance characterize a small-village pattern ~100 B.C.–A.D. 100, then spread
into the larger Mesa Central (Basin of Mexico, Morelos), west of the Lerma (Lake
Cuitzeo to Zacapu), and possibly the Mesa del Norte. El Opeño tombs date ~1200 B.C.,
but regional clarity rises with Chupícuaro.

### Classic–Epiclassic: Michoacán centers, Chalchihuites, La Quemada

Helen Perlstein Pollard’s synthesis: between **A.D. 400–900** ceremonial centers
appeared near Jiquilpan (El Otero), Cuitzeo (Tres Cerritos), Lake Pátzcuaro
(Tingambato), possibly Queréndaro/Zinapécuaro — monumental architecture and elaborate
tombs. Three open explanations remain: indigenous development from local/northern/
western roots; indigenous development strongly shaped by a [[teotihuacan|Teotihuacan]]
economic network; or Epiclassic Teotihuacan elite migration. Early–Middle Postclassic
settlements (≥5,000 people), monumental architecture, polychrome ceramics, and metal
artifacts indicate occupational and social stratification.

The **[[chalchihuites|Chalchihuites]]** tradition (Zacatecas/Durango; Classic into
Postclassic, not to Contact) links egalitarian agricultural villages to mining-driven
stratification at **Alta Vista** (blue-green stones, cinnabar, rhyolite, chert, ocher;
km-scale tunnels). **La Quemada** (Malpaso–Juchipila, south-central Zacatecas) is an
extravagantly fortified ceremonial center; Trombold places major occupation ~A.D.
600–800 within Chalchihuites tradition. Ben Nelson and Peter Jiménez Betts read its
florescence as an **Epiclassic West Mexican** phenomenon, not a Central Mexican state
satellite. Abandonment of La Quemada vicinity and the Suchil branch ~A.D. 850–900
coincides with environmental/productivity crisis.

### Coastal West Mexico and the Balsas

Coastal West Mexico (Guerrero–Sinaloa plains) supported dense populations via high
nonagricultural food production and was a possible locus of waterborne contacts to
Central/South America and overland trade toward the Greater Southwest. Guerrero
littoral survey may begin in the 3rd millennium B.C.; Matanchen complex (Mountjoy)
~2000 B.C.; San Blas ~700–400 B.C. multi-niche maritime/forest exploitation.
**Amapa** (Nayarit; Meighan) exemplifies Classic–Postclassic coastal complexity: Cerritos
phase (A.D. 900–1050/1100) brings mounds, plazas, masonry, moldmade figurines, copper
bells and needles — a key Greater Mesoamerican node for Southwest land and
Central/South American sea communication.

The **Balsas–Tepalcatepec Basin** (>110,000 km²) shows prehispanic irrigation and gold
placers; Kelly’s Apatzingán ceramic sequence runs Preclassic–Conquest; Paradis at Amuco
(Middle Balsas) finds two Olmec figurine fragments in Early Sésame (~from 1600 B.C.),
raising questions about West Mexico–Gulf [[olmec|Olmec]] relations and a 2nd-millennium
exchange network (obsidian, Pacific shell, perhaps jade) involving Olmec then
Teotihuacan. The Mezcala subregion is famous for austere geometric lithics.

### Northwest: Rio Sonora, Trincheras, Casas Grandes

In extratropical drylands, the **Rio Sonora** tradition develops houses-in-pits to
surface houses, walled plazas, and ballcourts (Late phase 1200–Contact). Controversy
persists: indigenous 3–4 tier settlement hierarchies (Doolittle) vs “statelets” as
products of the [[casas-grandes-paquime|Casas Grandes]] economic system (Pailes).
**Trincheras** (NW Sonora) crystallizes Late Classic/Postclassic with cerros de
trincheras and distinctive polychrome; little interchange with Rio Sonora.

**[[casas-grandes-paquime|Paquimé]]** (Di Peso: Viejo then Medio periods) becomes the
great multi-storied production and administrative center of a hinterland of hundreds or
thousands of settlements over perhaps ~85,000 square miles, then burns, is destroyed,
and is widely abandoned.

### Late Postclassic: Tarascan/Purépecha empire

Culmination of West/NW political development was the **[[purepecha|Tarascan
(Purépecha) state]]**, formed differently from Central Mexican polities of Teotihuacan,
Tula, and Aztec Tenochtitlan: **absorption** of defeated peers and exclusive power at
[[tzintzuntzan|Tzintzuntzan]], not Aztec-style **coordination**. Empire ~75,000 km² by
Conquest; metallurgical elite monopoly; takeover of long-distance routes cutting the
eastern/Central Mexican branch.

## Geographic Spread

Michoacán, Jalisco, Colima, Nayarit, Guanajuato, Guerrero, Zacatecas, Durango, Sinaloa,
Sonora, Chihuahua, and adjacent zones — from Pacific littoral through highland lakes
and the Mesa Central to the Sierra Madre Occidental and Chihuahuan desert margins.

## Interaction

### Inland and coastal long-distance routes (Classic–Late Postclassic)

Kelley/Weigand and related work identify two major routes Classic–Middle Postclassic:

1. **Inland:** La Quemada → [[chalchihuites|Chalchihuites]] → Schroeder → Zape →
   [[casas-grandes-paquime|Casas Grandes]] → U.S. Southwest.
2. **Coastal:** Central Mexico via Lerma–Chapala → Ixtlán → Amapa → Chametla–Culiacán–
   Guasave → north or east across the Sierra Madre to Casas Grandes.

Weigand surveyed the Colorado–Suchil drainage: 8 mining areas, ~750 mines over ~400
years (chrysocolla/malachite blue-greens, cinnabar, etc.); Central Mexico was a major
consumer; Alta Vista was a local extractive economy **and** port of entry/processing for
chemical turquoise from the Southwest/Coahuila; La Quemada directed much turquoise flow
into Central Mexico. Early Postclassic coastal routes also spread copper metallurgy
knowledge to the Southwest and West Mexico; cotton, textiles, and parrots moved
northward; metals/cotton branched toward the Maya. Late Postclassic: Tarascans took over
routes, cut the eastern/Central Mexican branch, and built their own Southwest connection;
Sonoran statelets traded parrots, macaws, feathers, coral, shell, and metal north and
turquoise, hides, jasper, cotton, salt, and slaves south (Riley). Spaniards later
resurrected many old routes for mining transport.

Trade-structure debates: Central Mexican-dominated networks vs Weigand/Braudel-style
**trade structure** transcending any one political network. Before ~A.D. 1000, a Loma San
Gabriel–Mogollon configuration blurred Southwest vs northern Mexico distinctions
(Foster).

### Metallurgy

Metallurgy first appears in Greater Mesoamerica in West Mexico (Jalisco, Michoacán,
Nayarit) by ~**A.D. 800** (possibly earlier), already technically well developed.
Hosler: designs, techniques, and often materials derived from Ecuador, Colombia, and
lower Central America; A.D. 1200/1300–Contact changes from indigenous development plus
continuing southern contacts and new links to southern Peru/Bolivian highlands. Two
technological periods: (1) ~800–1200/1300 copper (possibly low As-Cu), lost-wax and
open-mold + cold work/annealing; (2) 1200/1300–1520 bronzes and alloys enabling wirework
bells, loop-eye needles, ax-monies — ax-money similarities to the Andes confirm
maritime trade. Changes concentrated in the Tarascan region and coastal Guerrero; color
(gold/silver = sun/moon) prioritized for elite status (Hosler); Pollard: nonutilitarian
metal almost exclusively royal/high-status/state property — mining both product and
support of the unusual centralized state form.

## End Conditions

Spanish conquest (1520s–30s) reorders political structures; indigenous peoples and
languages (including Purépecha) persist. Colonial mining and road systems overlay many
pre-Hispanic corridors.

## Historiography

Gorenstein’s chapter synthesizes Weigand (Teuchitlán, mining), Pollard (Michoacán/
Tarascan), Kelley, Di Peso (Casas Grandes), Hosler (metallurgy), Trombold/Nelson
(La Quemada), Doolittle/Pailes/Riley (Sonora), and earlier Kelly, Braniff, Mountjoy,
Meighan work. Distinctive West Mexican contributions under the Greater Mesoamerica label:
**long-distance trade role**, **metallurgy**, and a **particular form of the
administrative state**. Parallel Oaxaca evidence (Marcus & Flannery) for early writing,
synoikism, and multipolar Postclassic polities enlarges political typology beyond the
Aztec template (see R06 synthesis: absorption/centralization vs coordination;
expansionist Monte Albán vs Mixtec cacicazgos).
