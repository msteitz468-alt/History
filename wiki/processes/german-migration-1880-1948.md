## Definition and Scope

German migration history between 1880 and 1948 encompasses five largely distinct but structurally interconnected streams of population movement:

1. **Emigration**: German citizens leaving for the Americas, colonial Africa, and elsewhere
2. **Labor immigration**: Seasonal and permanent labor migrants, primarily from Eastern Europe (Poles, Italians, Austro-Hungarians) entering the German industrial and agricultural economy
3. **Transit migration**: Eastern Europeans (especially Jews) crossing Germany en route to North and South America
4. **Political and racial emigration**: Antifascists and Jews fleeing the Nazi regime from 1933
5. **Mass displacement**: World War II ethnic resettlements, POW labor, forced labor of occupied populations, and the postwar expulsion of ethnic Germans

These streams demonstrate that "German history was embedded in transnational processes" and that "migration was one of the forms through which large segments of the population experienced global entanglement first hand" (Conrad and Ther, Chapter 25).

Source: [[smith-oxford-modern-german-history-2011]], Chapter 25 (Sebastian Conrad and Philipp Ther, "On the Move: Mobility, Migration, and Nation, 1880–1948").

## Causal Drivers

**Industrialization and internal migration**: Population growth, urbanization, and economic opportunity in industrial centers drove massive rural-to-urban migration that was typically the first stage of a longer journey. Germany's internal migration rates were comparable to those of the most rapidly industrializing European societies.

**Overseas emigration pressure**: The third wave of German trans-Atlantic migration (1880–1883) sent more than two million people overseas. Six million Germans emigrated between 1820 and 1913, over 90% settling in the United States. The primary motive was economic; middle-class emigrants (those with means to travel) predominated, not the poorest.

**Nationalist diaspora ideology**: The shift in terminology from *Auswanderer* (emigrant) to *Auslandsdeutsche* (diaspora German) reflects a nationalist reconceptualization: emigrants should remain German, not dissolve into the "melting pot." The German Colonial Society, General German School Association, and similar organizations worked to redirect emigration toward colonial Africa (where Germans would remain culturally distinct) and to maintain connections with diaspora communities in the Americas. The 1913 citizenship law, by making citizenship permanent and transmissible to descendants regardless of residence, institutionalized this ethnic conception of German nationhood — with consequences that lasted into the 1990s, when descendants of eighteenth-century emigrants from Russia (*Aussiedler*) "returned" to Germany.

**Labor demand**: From the mid-1890s, Germany became a net labor importer, second only to the United States. An estimated 500,000 foreigners worked in German agriculture by 1914; almost 700,000 worked in German industry. The *Karenzzeit* (restricted period) — obliging seasonal workers to return home each winter to prevent permanent settlement — was a distinctively German innovation, introduced specifically to manage the "Polonization" fear raised by Polish agricultural workers.

**Anti-Semitic discourse about transit migration**: More than five million people crossed Germany in transit to American ports (Hamburg, Bremerhaven) between 1880 and 1914, disproportionately Eastern European Jews. The "popular imagination" linked these transit migrants with "radicalism, poverty, trafficking in women from Eastern Europe, unhygienic conditions, and the importing of infectious diseases" (including the cholera epidemic of the early 1890s). In 1885, 32,000 Poles and Jews without Prussian citizenship were jointly expelled — showing how antisemitic and anti-Slavic fears merged in migration policy.

**World War I**: The war closed borders and converted voluntary labor migrants into coerced workers. About 300,000 Polish-Russian workers were trapped in Germany as civilian prisoners of war when borders closed in August 1914; 600,000 more were forcibly recruited from occupied Russian territories. One million POWs (mostly Russian and Serbian) were put to agricultural labor. About 130,000 Belgian workers were coerced, prompting international protests. Conrad and Ther argue this forced labor system "inaugurated a slide into the altogether different universe of inhumane treatment and exploitation of prisoners of war and civilians that characterized World War II."

## Major Phases

### Phase 1: Emigration, Colonial Ideology, and Labor Import (1880–1914)

The period opened with the final great wave of German emigration (1880–1883) and closed with the transformation of Germany into a labor importer. The fundamental tension was between nationalist concern about the "loss of national energies" through emigration and the economic necessity of imported labor.

Colonial settlement ideology attempted to resolve this tension by redirecting emigration to Africa, where Germans could remain German. The project largely failed: fewer than 20,000 Germans lived in all overseas possessions by 1914. The Prussian Colonization Commission's settlement of German farmers in Polish-speaking East Prussia was a domestic counterpart — an internal colonial project — that likewise encountered practical limits and local indifference or resistance.

Racial categories entered migration discourse in two registers: the racialization of overseas colonial practice (bans on interracial marriage in Southwest Africa; campaigns to bring German women to the colonies as "bearers of German culture") and the domestic equation of Polish and Jewish immigrants as threats to German racial-cultural purity.

### Phase 2: World War I and its aftermath (1914–1923)

The war militarized and coerced migration patterns, converting the pre-war system of voluntary seasonal labor into a system of forced labor. Post-war settlement produced large population movements: about 250,000 Polish-speaking Germans exercised the Versailles option to move to Poland; one million Germans from ceded territories (Alsace-Lorraine, Polish Corridor, etc.) moved to the Weimar Republic; Russian political refugees, Eastern European Jews, and former POWs refusing Soviet repatriation all arrived in Germany.

The fall of the three European empires "led to massive population movements that also deeply affected the Weimar Republic." This was "especially true for the influx of Eastern European Jews that in some ways changed the character and focus of antisemitism" — providing visible targets for antisemitic rhetoric about Bolshevism, cultural foreignness, and racial contamination.

### Phase 3: National Socialism and political/racial emigration (1933–1939)

The Nazi seizure of power generated two distinct refugee streams:

**Political emigrants**: About 16,000–19,000 antifascists fled in the first two years; by 1939 approximately 30,000 political refugees had left. Initially they went to neighboring countries (France, Belgium, Netherlands, Czechoslovakia, Austria). The consolidation of Nazism domestically and its international accommodation (Polish-German Non-Aggression Pact 1934; Munich Agreement 1938) progressively reduced the security of exile hosts. The refugees caught in France when it fell in 1940 faced detention or delivery to German security services; "only some managed to cross the Atlantic."

**Jewish emigrants**: Over 250,000 German Jews fled between 1933 and the outbreak of war — approximately half the 1933 Jewish population. They were "mostly unwanted" by receiving states. The United States immigration quota system severely limited entry; Palestine absorbed significant numbers but with British restrictions; Latin American countries (Argentina, Brazil) took smaller numbers. Conrad and Ther note that unlike political émigrés, Jewish refugees increasingly recognized their exile as permanent, not temporary.

The 1938 Evian Conference, convened to address the refugee crisis, produced no concrete commitments from any major receiving state — demonstrating the international abandonment of Germany's Jews that the Nazis interpreted as tacit permission to proceed.

### Phase 4: World War II mass displacement (1939–1948)

The war produced the largest forced population movements in European history:

**Nazi resettlement policy**: The SS's Race and Settlement Main Office oversaw the resettlement of ethnic Germans (*Volksdeutsche*) from Soviet-annexed territories (Bukovina, Volhynia, Baltic states) into incorporated Polish territories from which Polish and Jewish populations had been forcibly removed. Himmler believed this could be accomplished rapidly; in practice, most settlers spent the war in camps, their citizenship status withheld pending "politically loyal behavior." Young German activists sent as *Volkstumsarbeiter* ("cultural workers") to the annexed eastern territories frequently encountered bilingual, Catholic, Nazi-indifferent settlers who "shared more with local Polish speakers than they did with the middle-class nationalist activists from Germany."

**Forced labor**: The occupied territories across Europe provided forced labor for the German war economy. Eastern European workers were subjected to explicitly racist conditions; Western European workers (including French) faced somewhat less brutal but still coercive conditions. The scale dwarfed World War I forced labor.

**Expulsion of ethnic Germans (1945–1948)**: The collapse of the Third Reich triggered the largest single displacement: approximately 12 million ethnic Germans were expelled or fled from Central and Eastern Europe (Poland, Czechoslovakia, Hungary, Yugoslavia, Romania). This was the direct consequence of Nazi eastward expansion and ethnic resettlement policies, now reversed by the victorious Allies and the successor states. The expelled populations (the *Heimatvertriebenen* — "expellees from the homeland") became a significant political constituency in postwar West Germany.

## National-Transnational Tension

A structural tension runs throughout: "transnational processes of labor and ethnic migration were negotiated in national terms." Migration permanently undermined the national boundaries it was supposed to reinforce; simultaneously, migration produced nationalist backlash that sought ever-more restrictive control. The history of German citizenship law (1870, 1913, post-1945) tracks successive attempts to resolve this tension through legal definition — always with racial implications.

The *Lebensraum* concept, first coined as a scholarly term by geographer Friedrich Ratzel without political aspirations, emerged as "one of the answers to overcome various dangers to the nation, such as immigration from the East, the narrowing of Germany's sphere of economic interests, the closing of the colonial frontier, and the rivalry of the world powers" — showing how migration anxiety fed directly into Nazi expansionist ideology.

## Historiography

Sebastian Conrad (Freie Universität Berlin) and Philipp Ther (Universität Wien) situate German migration history within the transnational turn in historical scholarship. Their framework emphasizes that German history cannot be understood within national borders alone: migration embedded German society in global circuits of capital, labor, disease, and ideology. The connection between colonial practice (racial segregation in Africa) and metropolitan policy (antisemitism, "Polonization" fears) is a central argument — the colonial arena was a laboratory for racial population management later applied in Europe. This argument connects to a broader debate about the colonial roots of Nazi genocide (Benjamin Madley, "From Africa to Auschwitz," 2005; Jürgen Zimmerer's work on the Herero genocide as precursor).

**Collection coverage**: The chapter by Conrad and Ther is the primary collection source. The broader comparative literature on European migration, expulsion, and ethnic cleansing (Naimark, Liebermann, Ther's own monographic work) is not directly available.
