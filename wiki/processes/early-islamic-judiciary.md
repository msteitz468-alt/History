## Definition and Scope

The rise and professionalization of the early Islamic judiciary designates the long process — spanning roughly the first through third Islamic centuries (640–900 CE) — by which the office of the Islamic judge (*qāḍī*) evolved from a multi-purpose administrative role embedded in the garrison-town tribal order into a specialized, salaried, hierarchically organized legal institution operating under caliphal delegation and governed by the norms of *sharīʿa*. The process was neither linear nor uniform; regional variation, political disruption, and the contested nature of legal authority all shaped its trajectory.

This process must be distinguished from the parallel development of Islamic substantive law (*fiqh*), which was largely the work of private legal specialists rather than judges. The judiciary's professionalization was primarily an institutional and administrative development; the elaboration of doctrine was a scholarly one, though the two interacted continuously.

## Causal Drivers

**1. Expansion and demographic complexity.** The Arab conquests brought Muslim populations into contact with Christians, Jews, and other communities in towns previously beyond the garrison system. "After the third quarter of the first century H, judges began to be appointed to such towns as Alexandria in Egypt and Ḥimṣ in Syria, and to large cities in the former Sasanid world, primarily Khurasan." Mixed communities generated a volume and complexity of litigation that could not be managed by part-time arbitrators.

**2. Umayyad centralization.** The Umayyad dynasty used judicial appointments as an instrument of central control. The caliph Sulaymān b. ʿAbd al-Malik (r. 96–99/714–717) "seems to have been the first caliph to appoint judges directly from Damascus, thereby initiating the policy of removing from local governors the authority to make such appointments." This shift established the principle that judicial investiture was a caliphal prerogative, which the Abbasids institutionalized more thoroughly.

**3. Growth of the legal scholar class.** By the early second/eighth century, a distinct group of private religious scholars (*fuqahāʾ*) had emerged whose legal knowledge was recognized as superior to that of most judges. The doctrine that "a judge must consult the legal specialists, the *fuqahāʾ*, especially if he is not one of them" was already circulating by ca. 715 CE. This doctrine created pressure on the judiciary to engage with, and ultimately to be drawn from, the scholarly class.

**4. Abbasid institutional ambition.** The Abbasid revolution (750 CE) accelerated judicial centralization by tying the legitimacy of the caliphate to the administration of divine law. The appointment of Abū Yūsuf (d. 182/798) as the first *qāḍī al-quḍāt* (chief justice) by Hārūn al-Rashīd shortly after 170/786 was the institutional culmination of this trajectory.

## Major Phases

### Phase 1: The Proto-Qāḍī (ca. 20–80 H / 640–700 CE)

The earliest judges were appointed from garrison-town notables and combined judicial, administrative, policing, and religious functions. Legal knowledge was not a requirement. Sources for resolving disputes were tribal custom, caliphal ordinance, and Companion precedent (*sunan*). Court procedure was embryonic: written records were exceptional, witnesses were not systematically examined, and no formal court apparatus existed. See [[proto-qadi]].

### Phase 2: Functional Differentiation (ca. 80–120 H / 700–740 CE)

The ninth decade of the Hijra marks the beginning of the judge's office "increasingly limited to conflict resolution and legal administration." Caliphal appointment from the center began to replace gubernatorial discretion. Court procedures crystallized: written records became more regular (though still incomplete), inter-judge correspondence (*kitāb al-qāḍī ilā al-qāḍī*) was established by 100/718, and the *jilwāz* (court sheriff) and court scribe (*kātib*) emerged as permanent functionaries. The legal specialists also emerged in this phase as an independent epistemic force. See [[legal-specialists-formative]].

### Phase 3: Institutionalization (ca. 120–200 H / 740–815 CE)

This is the phase Hallaq terms "the judiciary coming of age." The key developments were:

- **Delegation and judicial hierarchy**: Abbasid caliphs appointed provincial judges directly; the chief justice (*qāḍī al-quḍāt*) was created to coordinate provincial appointments. Deputy judges (*khalīfas* or *nāʾibs*) received delegated jurisdiction, sometimes limited by subject matter (criminal cases, estate settlement) or by territory. "The judicial powers delegated at this level were frequently limited in jurisdiction."
- **Court apparatus completion**: By ca. 800–815 CE, "the structure and make-up of the court had taken final shape." Witness examiners (*aṣḥāb al-masāʾil*), the court chamberlain, trustees (*umanāʾ al-ḥukm*), estate appraisers (*qaṣṣām*), and public heralds (*munādīs*) all became permanent features.
- **The judge's register (dīwān)**: Systematic record-keeping developed across the second/eighth century. The Basran judge Sawwār b. ʿAbd Allāh (appointed 140s/760s) initiated "a fairly elaborate keeping of records"; al-Mufaḍḍal b. Faḍāla of Fustat (ca. 168/784) expanded the dīwān to include records of inheritance, bequests, and debts. By century's end the dīwān contained court minutes (*maḥāḍir*), certified verdicts (*sijillāt*), lists of court witnesses, waqf registers, and inter-judge correspondence.
- **Salaries and professionalization**: Judges shifted from military stipends (*ʿaṭāʾ*) to judicial salaries (*ujriya*). Egyptian qāḍīs earned ca. 30 dinars/month by 140/757; the salary of Fustat's judge reached 168 dinars/month by 198/813, described as unprecedented and thereafter normative. Rising salaries encouraged exclusivity: judges abandoned secondary occupations (halter-making, book-copying, selling yogurt) for full-time service.

### Phase 4: Consolidation and Stress (ca. 200–300 H / 815–912 CE)

By the early third/ninth century the judicial functions were "defined once and for all": adjudicating disputes, supervising court staff, managing waqf, guardianship of orphans and absentees, and occasional liturgical duties (leading Friday prayer, announcing Ramadan). Story-telling, policing, and tax collection were as a rule removed. The judicial office became coveted enough to generate competition and corruption among the learned hierarchy.

Simultaneously, caliphal authority over judicial appointments began to erode as military commanders and provincial dynasties assumed de facto control. Appointments remained "nominally caliphal" even when the caliph had no real role — the principle of delegation persisted as a legal fiction.

The **mazālim tribunals** emerged in this phase as a supplementary institution: boards of grievances convened by governors and viziers "theoretically for the purpose of correcting wrongs committed by state officials." They applied wider procedural latitude than Shariʿa courts and imposed penalties exceeding prescribed Shariʿa limits. Evidence from Egypt shows they functioned primarily as substitutes when no Shariʿa judge could be found, not as encroachments on the qāḍī's jurisdiction.

## Judicial Independence

A distinctive structural feature that emerged early and persisted: judicial independence from the appointing power in matters of adjudication. "As a rule, no authority could redirect cases or interfere in the process of adjudication." The independence of the judge was the consequence of the delegation theory: once invested, the qāḍī's court was sacrosanct until his dismissal. The principle was vivid in the case of the Egyptian judge Khayr b. Nuqaym (ca. 135/752), who resigned when the governor released a soldier whose case was mid-proceedings, and conditioned his return on the soldier's re-arrest.

There was no formal appeal system. Islamic law instead permitted *successor judicial review*: a newly appointed judge could review and reverse his predecessor's decisions, but only after assuming office, and only within the scope of his own tenure.

## Geographic Spread

The judiciary spread unevenly. Egypt and Iraq (Kufa, Basra, Baghdad) are the best-documented centers from the sources Hallaq uses. Khurasan was incorporated early as a major eastern appointment. Syria and Yemen appear as secondary centers. Andalusia developed a distinctive variant in which consultation of legal specialists (*mushāwars*) became obligatory and a judge's decision was invalid without their prior approval — a stronger institutionalization of juristic authority over the bench than obtained in the east.

## End Conditions

The process did not have a clean termination. By the third/ninth century, the judiciary was professionally formed and its basic structures fixed; subsequent centuries brought refinement rather than transformation. The disintegration of Abbasid central authority from the late third/ninth century onward altered the political context without dismantling the judicial institution — local dynasties inherited and perpetuated the qāḍī system as a marker of Islamic governance.

## Historiography

The primary sources for this process are biographical-prosopographical: al-Kindī's *Akhbār Quḍāt Miṣr* (judges of Egypt) and Wakīʿ's *Akhbār al-Quḍāt* (judges of Iraq). Both are ninth/tenth-century compilations drawing on earlier materials; they preserve granular anecdote but may anachronistically project later norms onto earlier practice. Hallaq reads them critically and cross-checks accounts where possible.

The major scholarly debate concerns the relative weight of caliphal versus scholarly authority in shaping the early judiciary. Hallaq argues that the legal specialists progressively eclipsed caliphal law as the source of normative doctrine, even as the caliphate retained formal investiture authority. This challenges older narratives (e.g., Schacht) that center caliphal legislation in the formation of Islamic law.
