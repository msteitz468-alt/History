## Definition and Scope

Romanization refers to the long, uneven process by which conquered peoples across the Roman Republic and Empire adopted elements of Roman law, language, civic institutions, material culture, and identity, distinct from the earlier, more geographically specific [[processes/romanization-of-italy|Romanization of Italy]] itself (the 1st-century-BCE process that unified the Italian peninsula's diverse peoples under Roman citizenship). The broader imperial process operated across widely different provincial contexts — Gaul, Iberia, Britain, North Africa, and the Greek-speaking east — with markedly different depth, pace, and outcomes.

## Causal Drivers

Provincial elites were drawn toward Roman models by tangible incentives: citizenship grants, access to Roman legal protections, career paths in the imperial administration and army, and the prestige value of Roman-style urban amenities (fora, baths, amphitheaters). Veteran colonization, garrison towns, and the spread of Roman law and coinage reinforced the process from below, while emperors from Claudius (extending citizenship and senatorial rank to provincials) to Caracalla (the near-universal citizenship grant of 212 CE, the Constitutio Antoniniana) extended it from above.

## Major Phases

Romanization proceeded earliest and most thoroughly in the western provinces (Gaul, Spain, later Britain), where it substantially displaced pre-Roman languages and political structures over several centuries; in the Greek-speaking eastern provinces, by contrast, Hellenistic urban culture was already so developed that "Romanization" took the form of administrative and legal integration layered atop persistent Greek cultural dominance, producing a durably bilingual Greco-Roman elite culture rather than linguistic Latinization.

## Geographic Spread

The process's reach tracked the empire's expansion and consolidation, from the earliest provincialization of Sicily and Spain through the conquest of Gaul, Britain, and the Danubian and North African provinces, with intensity generally highest near military garrisons, veteran colonies, and major administrative centers, and weakest in remote rural hinterlands.

## Interaction

Romanization operated alongside, and was distinct from, contemporaneous processes such as [[processes/romanization-of-italy]] (its Italian-peninsula-specific predecessor and template) and later [[processes/christianization-of-the-roman-empire|Christianization]], which eventually provided a new, competing framework of imperial cultural unity that partly succeeded, and partly supplanted, the Roman civic-cultural synthesis Romanization had built.

## End Conditions

The process did not have a clean endpoint; it decelerated and reversed unevenly with the political fragmentation of the Western Empire in the 5th century, as post-Roman successor kingdoms retained substantial Romanized legal, linguistic, and religious inheritances (especially in Gaul, Spain, and Italy) even as centralized Roman political authority collapsed, while in the East, Roman (increasingly Greek-inflected) institutions persisted for another millennium under the Byzantine Empire.
