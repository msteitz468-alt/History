# Medieval Warfare (c. 600–1500)

## Definition and Scope

The practice and organisation of war across the global "medieval world" of roughly 600–1500 CE —
not a single system but a set of regional military cultures in constant interaction, as set out
in the [[cambridge-history-of-war-v2|Cambridge History of War, Vol. 2]]. The volume consciously
breaks with the Europe-centred tradition that "began with Sir Charles Oman" in the 1880s, placing
Latin Christendom alongside the Islamic world, Byzantium, the Eurasian steppe, China, Japan,
India, Southeast Asia, and the pre-Columbian Americas. This page treats medieval war as a
long-duration **process** — how armies were raised, equipped, paid, and used — complementing the
[[military-history-middle-millennium]] concept page (Clifford Rogers' analytical frame of the
steppe-vs-sedentary leitmotif).

## Causal Drivers

- **State capacity.** The size, cohesion, and reach of armies tracked the fiscal and
  administrative capacity of [[state-formation|states]] — from the contracting post-Roman
  kingdoms, through the highly organised Tang and Byzantine military states, to the
  reviving tax-and-contract armies of late-medieval Europe.
- **The steppe.** The recurring confrontation between **[[pastoral-nomadism|mounted nomads]]** and
  settled agro-urban civilisations is the volume's organising thread: the mobility, archery, and
  endurance of steppe cavalry (Avars, Hungarians, Turks, and above all the
  [[mongol-empire|Mongols]]) repeatedly overturned the military balance of Eurasia.
- **Warrior elites and military slavery.** War was the business of specialised elites — the
  Western knight, the Byzantine and Chinese officer corps, the Japanese warrior — but also,
  distinctively, of **military slaves**: the Islamic world's *ghilman* and **mamluk** systems
  (the volume opens with "the early Islamic empire and the introduction of military slavery";
  see [[medieval-slavery]]).
- **Religion.** [[crusades|Crusade]] and *jihad* mobilised manpower and reframed war as holy
  obligation across the Mediterranean and Near East.

## Major Phases

1. **Foundations (c. 600–1000).** The post-Roman West fragments into kingdom-scale warfare; the
   Islamic conquests and the rise of military slavery; Byzantium's resilient military system; the
   Scandinavian (Viking) raiding world; and the steppe pressure of Avars, Hungarians, and Turks.
   In East Asia, Tang China fields large, bureaucratically organised armies.
2. **Interactions (c. 1000–1300).** The age of the **castle**, the mounted knight, and the
   [[crusades|Crusades]] in the Latin West; the explosive [[mongol-empire|Mongol]] conquests
   that linked Eurasia by conquest; continued Chinese military development under the Song.
3. **Nations and formations (c. 1300–1500).** The **infantry revival** (English longbow, Swiss
   pike, urban militias), the growth of **contract and standing armies**, gunpowder artillery
   battering down the castle, and the consolidation of the [[ottoman-empire|Ottoman]] military
   state — the immediate prelude to the early-modern [[military-revolution]].

## Operational and Tactical Layer

*(Added in the 2026-06-24 warfare-hub pass. Grounded in CHW Vol. 2, chs. 1, 4, 6, 10, 11, 13,
22–23. This section feeds the [[hubs/warfare/warfare-hub|Warfare & Strategy Hub]] and the
medieval war hubs.)*

**Battle was the exception, not the rule.** Across most medieval military cultures the strategic
norm was the *Vegetian* one — control fortresses, live off and devastate the enemy's land, and
avoid pitched battle except on very favourable terms. Morillo notes the sedentary pattern
("avoiding battle except in very favorable circumstances") parallels both Vegetius and Sunzi, and
Rogers' work on the "Vegetian strategy" of battle-avoidance underlies it. The English
*chevauchée* of the Hundred Years' War — a mounted devastation raid designed to ruin the enemy's
fisc and provoke or avoid battle at choice — is the type-case. Siege, not field battle, decided
most wars: as France observes of the Crusades, "siege posed the greatest challenge to the army
because it was forced to stay in one place."

**Recruitment systems were the deep variable.** The volume's comparative point is that *who could
be made to fight, and how they were paid*, shaped everything downstream:

- **Feudal levy → paid contract.** In Latin Europe the tenurial summons gave way to **pay** and
  the **indenture** (contract): by 1346 Edward III's men served for pay "for as long as the king
  required," and captains contracted to supply set numbers of men-at-arms and archers. This
  produced the late-medieval **mixed retinue** (men-at-arms and mounted archers together) and the
  administrative records that make the period quantifiable.
- **Military slavery.** The Islamic solution to unreliable tribal armies was the purchase and
  training of **ghilman / mamluks** — mostly Turkic steppe horse-archers — as salaried,
  manumitted professional soldiers loyal to the ruler. At Samarra the Turkish regiments became so
  central they "made and unmade the caliphs"; the system's logic (innate steppe military quality +
  engineered loyalty) outlived the Abbasids and produced the Mamluk sultanate that stopped the
  Mongols at [[hubs/warfare/high-middle-ages/mongol-conquests/battle-of-ain-jalut-1260|Ain Jalut]].
- **The thematic militia + professional core.** Byzantium met the Islamic onslaught by turning its
  field armies into **provincial militias** (*themata*) financed by the districts that raised them,
  retaining a professional core and, from Constantine V, the standing **tagmata** guard — the only
  heavy cavalry of the dark-age centuries. Doctrine became defensive and attritional: lookouts,
  evacuation to mountain fortresses, scorched earth, and harrying invaders rather than risking
  battle. Byzantine military **manuals** (the *Strategikon*, *Taktika*, *Praecepta Militaria*) are
  distinctive in offering "specific advice on how to fight specific foes on specific terrain."
- **The whole society as army (steppe).** Among the Turks and Mongols the pastoral way of life was
  itself lifelong military training; the great **hunt** (*nerge*) doubled as a manoeuvre exercise
  in encirclement, and the **decimal organisation** (tens / hundreds / thousands / tümens) was
  simultaneously a tool of battlefield command and of political control — deliberately breaking up
  conquered tribes so that, once "included in the military system… their chances of resistance were
  significantly reduced."

**The arms balance — missile, shock, and mobility.** The medieval rock-paper-scissors:

- **Steppe horse-archery** combined the **composite bow** (Mongol draw weight lethal "both at
  close and long range," ~60 arrows carried) with **remounts** — three to five horses per warrior —
  giving unmatched operational mobility (armies that "could move almost without stopping") and the
  signature **feigned retreat** into ambush (the Rus' pursued the Mongols "for eight days" before
  destruction at the Kalka, 1223). Its limit was **forage**: Mongol horses "lacked food during the
  Hülegüid invasions of Syria," a structural reason the Ilkhans could never hold the Levant.
- **Western heavy cavalry** — the charge of the mailed, later plate-armoured knight — was
  battle-winning shock but slow, forage-hungry, and brittle against prepared infantry.
- **The infantry/missile revolution.** From c. 1300 massed infantry began to decide battles:
  Flemish militia at Courtrai (1302), Scots pike at Bannockburn (1314), and above all **English
  longbowmen** — drawn "to the cheek instead of the chest," the product of "a lifetime of training"
  — at Dupplin Moor (1332), Halidon Hill (1333), and then Crécy, Poitiers, and Agincourt. The
  **crossbow** (powerful, low-skill, requiring the **pavise** shield to reload) armed the Genoese
  mercenaries who fought *for* France. Curry, following Rogers, frames this as an "infantry
  revolution," soon joined by an "artillery revolution" as gunpowder guns "out-paced
  fortifications" and forced the redesign of defences.

**Siege and engineering decided campaigns.** The counterweight **trebuchet** (a post-Roman
re-introduction) and mining brought down walls; the Mongols' edge was importing **Chinese and
Muslim engineers** to build stone-throwers on site, then driving prisoners forward "as a living
shield." Crusader siegecraft depended utterly on **sea power** — Genoese and English fleets carried
the timber and food without which Antioch and Jerusalem could not have fallen.

**Command, intelligence, and deception.** The Mongols industrialised what the steppe had always
done: systematic **intelligence** (merchants, defectors, guides recruited before every campaign),
**deception** (dummies on spare horses, civilians paraded as troops), and **multi-column
operations** across strategic distances so that "the enemy lost the opportunity to coordinate the
actions of its units." **Terror** was a deliberate operational instrument — the massacre of towns
that resisted, advertised to induce the next town's surrender.

## Geographic Spread

The volume's reach is its point: medieval military history is **global**. Alongside Latin Europe
it treats the **Islamic world** (military slavery, the Mamluk defeat of the Mongols at Ayn Jalut
1260), **Byzantium and the Balkans**, the **Eurasian steppe**, **Tang–Song–Yuan China**,
**Japan** (the evolution of the warrior), **India** (the Delhi Sultanate's military and the
import of Central Asian cavalry), **Southeast Asia** (the Khmer and mainland states), and the
**pre-Columbian Americas** (Maya and Aztec warfare, with their distinctive emphasis on
captive-taking). A concluding cross-cultural part compares justifications and customs of war and
surveys land and maritime technologies.

## Interaction

Medieval warfare is entangled with [[state-formation]], [[feudalism]], [[medieval-slavery]]
(military slavery), the [[crusades]], the [[mongol-empire]], and [[pastoral-nomadism]]. Its late
phase produces the conditions for the [[military-revolution]] — gunpowder, infantry, and the
fiscal-military state — that defines early-modern war.

## End Conditions

There is no single end point: the diffusion of **gunpowder** (cannon against fortifications from
the fourteenth century, then handheld firearms), the **infantry revolution**, and the rise of
larger, tax-funded standing armies gradually transform medieval into early-modern warfare across
the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries — earlier and more decisively in some regions than others.

## Historiography

Anglophone study of medieval war "began with Oman" and long focused narrowly on Latin-European
battles and the supposed dominance of heavy cavalry (the "age of cavalry"). Recent scholarship —
crystallised in this volume — is **global and comparative**, sceptical of the cavalry-dominance
thesis (Morillo, Rogers on the "Vegetian" strategy of avoiding battle), attentive to logistics,
fortification, and recruitment systems, and integrates the steppe, the Islamic world, and Asia
as full participants rather than backdrops. A standing caution: CHW Vol. 2 is a **survey**, strong
on the operational and institutional picture but thin on single-battle tactical reconstruction —
the warfare-hub battle pages it now feeds flag where that depth depends on dedicated studies
(Ayton & Preston on Crécy, Curry on Agincourt, France's *Victory in the East* on the First
Crusade). See [[military-history-middle-millennium]] for the analytical frame and the
[[military-revolution]] debate for the transition out of the period.
