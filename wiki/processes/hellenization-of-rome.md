# The Hellenization of Roman Culture

The absorption of Greek language, literature, religion, art, philosophy, and material
life into Roman society in the third and second centuries BCE — and the ambivalent
Roman response to it. Treated in [[astin-cah-v8-1989|CAH VIII]] Chs 12 (Rawson, the
cultural dimension) and 13 (Morel, the archaeological/material dimension).

## Definition and scope

Rawson insists the term "Hellenization" is "too undefined": it must be broken into
**many elements and sources**, distinguishing what Romans could readily admire or
absorb from what they only slowly appreciated or actively rejected. Crucially, Rome
was **the conquering, not the conquered, partner** — so "the conqueror was never
taken wholly captive" (against Horace's tag); the vitality of Roman tradition was
greater than that of almost any culture Greece touched. Much early Greek influence
arrived **indirectly**, filtered through Magna Graecia, Sicily, and Etruria, before
direct contact with "real Greece" (*germana Graecia*) from 200.

## Causal Drivers

1. **Conquest and contact.** The [[second-macedonian-war-200-196bce|Second Macedonian
   War]] (200) and the [[roman-syrian-war-192-188bce|war with Antiochus]] brought
   armies into the Greek world and initiated "unprecedently rapid social and cultural
   change."
2. **Booty, slaves, and wealth.** Greek **art** flowed to Rome as plunder (Marcellus
   from Syracuse, 211; Fabius' Hercules from Tarentum, 209), and Greek-speaking
   **slaves** — including educated ones — poured into Italy, staffing elite households
   and (in the countryside) the [[agrarian-crisis-roman-republic|slave-worked villa]].
3. **Embassies and hostages.** A "perpetual queue" of Greek envoys treated the Senate
   to displays of Greek oratory; royal hostages (Philip's son Demetrius, the Seleucid
   Demetrius) mixed with young Roman nobles.

## Major Phases

- **The literary awakening (from 240).** Latin literature began as **adaptation from
  Greek**: **Livius Andronicus** (a freedman, his Latin *Odyssey* in Saturnians, the
  first Latin play 240), **Naevius** (Campanian; comedies, tragedies, and the historical
  epic *Bellum Punicum*), **[[cato-the-elder|Cato's]]** friend **Ennius** of Rudiae
  ("three hearts" — Oscan, Greek, Latin — the *Annals* in Greek hexameters), and above
  all **Plautus**, whose comedies (the *palliata*, in Greek dress) are a prime gauge of
  how far, and how shallowly, Greek culture had reached a mixed Roman audience.
- **Religion.** Under the stress of the Hannibalic War the Senate imported Greek rites
  on the advice of the **Sibylline books** — the *lectisternium* (217), games to Apollo
  (212), the cult of **Venus Erycina**, and the **Great Mother (Magna Mater)** from
  Pergamene Phrygia (205) — but each was **firmly adapted** and de-orgiasticised; "the
  traditional structure of Roman religion was never, in our period, broken down." The
  **Bacchanalian affair** (186) was a policing of organisation, not a war on Greek
  religion as such.
- **The material transformation (Morel, Ch. 13).** Archaeology shows Italy 300–133
  reshaped by conquest wealth: new specialised **production** (wine, oil) and
  long-distance **trade** (black-glaze pottery, amphorae), Greek-influenced
  **architecture and art** (the Hellenistic-style villa, temple-building, the Basilica
  Porcia — a Greek form under Cato's own name), and the urbanisation of Rome into
  "something more like a Hellenistic city."

## Interaction: reaction and acceptance

Rawson cautions against the old picture of a simple **philhellene vs. hellenophobe**
party conflict; mostly Greek ways "seeped in" without a conscious issue being made.
But [[cato-the-elder|Cato]] — the one figure whose mind we can read — shows **real
conviction** about the dangers of Greek influence (the warning to his son that Greek
literature would "prove the city's ruin"; his hostility to Greek doctors) *while
himself* learning Greek, studying with Ennius, and building in Greek forms. The
tension is structural: the competitive oligarchy found Greek culture a **profitable new
medium** for advertising achievement (poetry, monuments, the blurring of human and
divine), so that even reluctant nobles adopted it "through fear of being left behind."
This connects to [[roman-aristocratic-politics|the politics of *mores*]] — sumptuary
law, censorial *nota*, the prosecutions — as the elite's anxious self-regulation in the
face of its own transformation.

## End Conditions

The process did not end in 133 — it accelerated into the age of the **Scipionic circle**
([[scipio-aemilianus]], [[polybius]], the philosopher Panaetius), the visit of the
Athenian philosophers (Carneades, 155), and the full literary culture of the late
Republic. CAH VIII captures its formative, ambivalent first phase. The Hellenization
of Roman culture is the cultural counterpart of the political *symploke* — the fusion
of the Greek and Roman worlds that the volume narrates.

## OHCW (Boardman/Griffin/Murray 1986) — Crawford, Rawson, Brown

Crawford (Ch. 16) dates the **rapid Hellenization of Rome** to the late fourth–early
third centuries: Via Appia Rome–Capua (312, Ap. Claudius Caecus as censor) and first
Roman silver coinage on Greek models; victory ideology after Alexander; Hercules cults;
Genucilia and black-slip pottery; five-class census structure.

Rawson (Ch. 17) stresses elite Hellenization as precondition for Greco-Roman elite
co-operation: Roman superiority claimed in war/statecraft, Greeks typed as
effeminate/loquacious; mid-third century new epoch (plays on Greek model; bilingual
schooling); Syracuse sack 212 as Polybius’ start of taste for Greek art; stages of
luxury/moral decline dated variously (Asia 190 booty; Pydna; Sulla’s Asian campaigns
as Sallust’s turning-point); formal *paideia* challenged traditional apprenticeship;
Carneades’ embassy 155; first century Latin refined and colonized prose genres.

P. G. McC. Brown (Ch. 18) anchors the literary opening: **Livius Andronicus**’ play
at Rome in 240 BCE as conventional start; **Plautus** (~20 comedies c.205–184) as
earliest complete Latin works — creative adaptations of New Comedy (Bacchides papyrus
shows cuts, reordering, added jokes, *cantica*, Roman institutions mixed into Greek
settings); **Terence** (160s) more Menandrian, new elliptical style, school-text
afterlife; **Ennius** (239–169) *Annals* introduce Greek hexameter into Latin epic and
shape Lucretius and Virgil. Across Crawford–Rawson–Brown, OHCW pairs mid-Republican
**arms** with deepening Greek **cultural** conquest of Rome.

Source: [[boardman-oxford-history-classical-world-1986]] (Chs. 16–18).

## Thonemann: Villa of the Papyri as Limit-Case (2016)

[[thonemann-hellenistic-age-2016|Thonemann]] (ch. 5) treats the **Villa of the Papyri** at
Herculaneum as the western "encounter" of Hellenistic civilization — and as evidence that
Roman Hellenism remained **strange** from a Greek East perspective.

- Hellenization of non-Greek Italy accelerated after Roman eastern conquests from the
  **220s BCE**; second-century Italy flooded with Greek war-booty. First marble building
  in Rome — temple of Jupiter Stator, Campus Martius — by Q. Caecilius Metellus after
  defeating Andriskos **148 BCE**, designed by a Greek architect. For aristocrats, Greek
  material culture signalled membership of the "conquering class."
- Villa built shortly after **50 BCE**; ~250 × 80 m (20,000 m²); atrium house plus long
  peristyle garden (evoking Greek gymnasium courtyards — Cicero named villa spaces
  "gymnasium," "palaestra," "Academy"). Buried by Vesuvius AD 79; excavated 1750s–60s:
  ~85 sculptures; **>1,000** carbonized papyrus rolls, almost all Greek Epicurean texts
  dominated by **Philodemus of Gadara** (c. 110–40 BCE), likely under patronage of
  L. Calpurnius Piso Caesoninus (Caesar's father-in-law).
- **Contrast with the Greek East**: private intellectual patronage of the Piso–Philodemus
  type was **unknown** in the Hellenistic Greek world; no private houses (and few royal
  palaces) match the Villa's scale; private Greek art collections in a mish-mash of styles
  have no real Hellenistic parallel. To a second/first-century Greek, Romans remained
  "quite as strange and unfamiliar as any Bactrian or Scythian nomad."
