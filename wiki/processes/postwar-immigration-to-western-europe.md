## Definition and Scope

The sustained inflow of non-European (and, in the earliest postwar years,
intra-European) migrant labor into Western Europe from 1945 onward, and its
transformation over four decades from a transient "guest worker" phenomenon into
permanently settled ethnic minority populations. For the first time in modern
history, the number of people arriving in Europe from elsewhere exceeded the
number leaving. This process is distinct from earlier interwar European labor
migration (France's absorption of Poles, Portuguese, and Italians) and from the
much older history of European colonial emigration outward; its distinguishing
feature is the reversal of direction and the racial/religious diversity of the new
migrant streams.

## Causal Drivers

- **Postwar economic expansion and labor shortages** — the "economic miracle" of
  the 1950s–1960s created demand for unskilled labor in construction, factory work,
  and undesirable shifts that European nationals declined.
- **Decolonization** — conferred rights of entry on former colonial subjects. The
  transformation of the British Empire into the Commonwealth gave Jamaicans,
  other Afro-Caribbeans, Indians, and Pakistanis an unlimited right to migrate to
  Britain; Algerian independence (1962) triggered a surge of North African
  immigration to France, alongside West Africans, Vietnamese, and citizens of
  France's overseas départements (Guadeloupe, Martinique, French Guiana, Réunion).
- **Government-to-government recruitment contracts** — lacking colonial ties, West
  Germany signed formal recruitment agreements bringing in *Gastarbeiter* ("guest
  workers") from southern Europe and, from the mid-1960s, growing numbers of Turks.
- **Chain migration** — once individual workers established a foothold at a plant
  or in a town, word of mouth brought friends, neighbors, and family members from
  the same home region to the same employer, concentrating specific ethnic groups
  in specific towns and factories (French North Africans in street cleaning and
  janitorial work; Jamaican women as British nurses; Turks in German mining).

## Major Phases

1. **Initial European labor migration (immediately postwar)** — 10 million ethnic
   Germans expelled from Eastern Europe into West Germany; Britain's European
   Volunteer Worker Service recruited Italians and displaced Poles.
2. **Non-European expansion (1950s–early 1970s)** — decolonization and economic
   expansion combine to bring in Commonwealth Afro-Caribbean and South Asian
   migrants to Britain, North African and West African migrants to France, and
   Turkish/southern European guest workers to West Germany.
3. **Recruitment freeze and unintended settlement (1973–1974 onward)** — the
   economic downturn led nearly every Western European country to halt foreign
   labor recruitment, but laws allowed family reunification and maintained refugee
   provisions. Paradoxically, this **transformed** rather than stabilized the
   foreign-born population: a transient, heavily male guest-worker population became
   a settled minority of immigrant families, since return became less attractive
   than the alternative of bringing dependents to Europe.
4. **Impoverishment and underclass formation (1970s–1990s)** — recession-era
   unemployment fell disproportionately on immigrant manufacturing workers;
   residential segregation (reinforced by discrimination, e.g., British landlords'
   "no coloured" rental practices before mid-1960s legal reform) compounded into
   educational segregation and blocked social mobility, producing what sociologists
   termed a permanent "ethnic underclass" within a broader "two thirds society."
5. **Politicization and identity conflict (1980s–2000s)** — race riots (Notting
   Hill 1958; Brixton and other British cities 1979–2001; French suburbs 2005);
   rise of Far Right anti-immigrant parties (Front National, Vlaams Blok, Austria's
   Freedom Party, Republikaner); collective-rights controversies centered on
   Europe's growing Muslim population (the French headscarf affair, 1989–2004; the
   Salman Rushdie/*Satanic Verses* fatwa controversy, 1989; post-9/11 tightened
   policing following the 2004 Madrid and 2005 London bombings).

## Geographic Spread

Britain (Commonwealth Afro-Caribbean and South Asian immigration), France
(Algerian/North African, West African, Vietnamese, and overseas-département
immigration), and West Germany (Turkish and southern European guest workers) were
the three principal destinations, each shaped by distinct colonial or
labor-recruitment relationships. By the late twentieth century prosperity —
and immigration — spread to other parts of the continent, including previously
emigrant-sending southern Europe.

## Interaction

Directly produced Islam's emergence as Western Europe's second-largest religion —
by the early twenty-first century France counted roughly 4 million Muslims,
Germany 2 million, Britain over 1 million — creating friction with
[[european-secularization-process]] and generating distinctive controversies (mosque
construction opposition, halal slaughter bans in Sweden and Switzerland, the
headscarf affair) absent from the Christian secularization narrative proper.
Interacts with mainstream party politics across the spectrum: even establishment
conservative politicians (Margaret Thatcher's 1978 "swamped by a different
culture" remark; Jacques Chirac's 1991 "noise and smell" speech; Helmut Kohl's 1993
integration remarks) periodically mobilized anti-immigrant sentiment.

## End Conditions

Ongoing and, per Paxton and Hessler, irreversible as of the source's 2011
publication. No terminal event closes this process within the source's coverage;
it instead evolves in place from labor migration through settlement, poverty,
politicization, and (partial, uneven) legal accommodation — automatic citizenship
for children born in Germany (1990s reform), extension of local voting rights to
resident noncitizens in Sweden, Norway, and the Netherlands — alongside continued
exclusionary pressures (rejection of dual citizenship in most countries; Far Right
electoral persistence at 10–20% in several states).

## Historiography

Panikos Panayi's prolific output, especially *An Ethnic History of Europe since
1945* (2000), and Alec Hargreaves's *Immigration, "Race," and Ethnicity in
Contemporary France* (1995) anchor the national case studies synthesized in Paxton
and Hessler's ch. 20. Rogers Brubaker's *Citizenship and Nationhood in France and
Germany* (1992) is the standard comparative treatment of the citizenship-law
divergence between the two countries. This is a substantive gap-filling addition:
the wiki's collection coverage of the post-1970 period generally, and of
Western European immigration and race relations specifically, was previously
absent.
