## Definition and Scope

The peaceful transformation of Spain from Francisco Franco's authoritarian dictatorship into a parliamentary democracy, running from Franco's death in November 1975 through the consolidated 1982 election of Felipe González's Socialist Workers' Party. Distinct from — and, per Paxton and Hessler, markedly more tranquil than — the contemporaneous [[carnation-revolution-1974|Portuguese transition]], which involved a military coup, mass social revolution, and near civil war. The Spanish process instead proceeded through managed constitutional reform under a restored monarchy.

## Causal Drivers

`caused_by` [[death-of-franco-1975|Franco's death]] (November 1975, age 82) and the succession of Prince Juan Carlos, grandson of the last reigning Bourbon king Alfonso XIII, which Franco himself had arranged as his chosen successor but which Juan Carlos used to steer Spain toward democracy rather than continuity. `contributed_to` by: the memory of the 1936–1939 Civil War, which gave both Left and Right strong incentives toward reconciliation rather than confrontation; the waning international appeal of communism, which meant Santiago Carrillo's legalized Communist Party (1977) posed less of a polarizing threat than it would have a generation earlier; and the strong pull of European Community membership, which rewarded market-economy and open-political-system reforms even during the 1970s recession.

## Major Phases

**1975–1977:** Adolfo Suárez's Democratic Center government prepares the ground; the first free elections in forty-one years (June 1977) give Suárez a plurality. **1978:** A new constitution — disestablishing the Catholic Church, restoring divorce, legalizing strikes — passes a December referendum by a slender majority (32% abstention), over the opposition of several generals who resigned when the Communist Party was legalized. Catalonia regains regional autonomy. **February 1981:** A group of Civil Guard officers seizes the Spanish parliament and holds deputies hostage, expecting broad military backing for a coup; King Juan Carlos personally extracts loyalty pledges from all but one regional army commander and the coup collapses within eighteen hours — the decisive test of the new order's stability. **1982:** Felipe González's Socialists win power in a quiet, orderly transfer, confirming democratic consolidation; further coup plotting (also 1982) is discredited and Spain joins NATO the same year (confirmed by 1986 referendum, with nuclear weapons excluded at Spain's request).

## Geographic Spread

National in scope; regional autonomy movements (Catalonia, later the Basque Country) are managed within the constitutional settlement rather than resolved by it — ETA's separate, violent insurgency (see [[left-wing-terrorism-western-europe-1970s]]) continues throughout and beyond this process.

## Interaction

`analogous_to` the Carnation Revolution in Portugal in starting conditions (a long-lived Iberian authoritarian dictatorship reaching its end in the mid-1970s) but `contrasts_with` it sharply in mechanism and outcome — managed royal succession and constitutional reform versus military coup and social revolution. `enabled` Spain's 1986 accession to the European Community, cementing the country's return to the Western European mainstream after decades as one of Europe's "pariahs" (alongside Portugal) under authoritarian rule.

## End Conditions

Substantially complete by the 1982 election, which transferred power to the Socialists through ordinary constitutional process and confirmed that the failed February 1981 coup attempt had not represented a durable threat. González would govern until 1996, but by 1982 Paxton and Hessler judge that "Spanish democracy seemed to have taken firm root."

## Historiography

Paxton and Hessler's account draws on Paul Preston, *The Triumph of Democracy in Spain* (1987); Raymond Carr, *Spain: Dictatorship to Democracy* (2nd ed., 1991); and Kenneth Maxwell and Steven Spiegel, *The New Spain: From Isolation to Influence* (1994). The comparison with Portugal is explicit in the source and worth a dedicated comparative page in `wiki/comparisons/` (not yet created): "Precedent suggested that dying dictatorships usually end in revolutionary turmoil... In both countries, however, political moderation finally prevailed (in Portugal only after an authentic social revolution)."
