# The Price Revolution

The **Price Revolution** was the sustained, century-long **rise in prices** that swept
sixteenth- and early-seventeenth-century Europe — a roughly three- to sixfold increase in the
general price level, modest by modern standards but unprecedented and disorienting for a
society used to stable prices. It is a foundational episode in economic history and the
economic backdrop to the era's social and political upheavals. From
[[new-cambridge-modern-history-v3|*The New Cambridge Modern History*, Vol. III]] (F. C.
Spooner).

## Definition and Scope

The term denotes the **secular inflation of c. 1500–1650**, most studied for Spain, England,
and France. It is "price revolution" only relative to the near-static prices of the Middle
Ages; the annual rate was low (around 1–1.5%) but its cumulative effect over a century
transformed wages, rents, and state finances. The page concerns the causes, course, and
consequences of this inflation, not the wider commercial expansion of which it was part.

## Causal Drivers

Spooner and NCMH III stress that the inflation was **multi-causal**, and reject the old view
that it was "solely the product of the sudden influx of silver from America":

- **American silver and gold** — the bullion pouring in from Potosí and Mexico (the
  [[global-silver-trade]]) "did quicken and steepen the price rise," especially from the
  1550s–60s.
- **Population growth** — Europe's demographic recovery and expansion raised demand for food
  and land ahead of supply (a Malthusian pressure many historians now weight heavily).
- **Monetary debasement** — repeated reductions in coin silver content by hard-pressed states.
- Velocity and credit changes accompanying commercial growth.

The episode prompted the first systematic **monetary theory**: the **quantity theory of
money** (Martín de Azpilcueta and the School of Salamanca; later Jean Bodin), linking the
money supply to the price level.

## Major Phases

1. **Gentle rise (c. 1500–50):** prices creep up amid demographic recovery, before the great
   silver flood.
2. **Acceleration (c. 1550–1600):** the peak inflow of American silver steepens the rise
   across western Europe.
3. **Plateau and crisis (c. 1600–50):** prices level off as population growth stalls and the
   silver inflow slows, merging into the [[seventeenth-century-general-crisis|17th-century
   crisis]].

## Geographic Spread

The inflation radiated **outward from Spain** (the point of entry for American silver) across
France, the Netherlands, England, the Empire, and Italy, and via trade into the Baltic, the
Ottoman lands, and ultimately Asia (where much of the silver ended up). Its timing and
severity varied: Spain inflated earliest and hardest; England's rise was steep in the later
16th century.

## Interaction

The Price Revolution was bound up with the **[[global-silver-trade]]** (its monetary motor),
with the **[[habsburg-empire|Habsburg]]** and other states (whose fixed revenues it eroded,
intensifying the fiscal-military squeeze), with **social change** (eroding real wages, favouring
landlords/commercial farmers over wage-earners and fixed-rent recipients), and with the
intellectual birth of **economic theory**.

## End Conditions

The inflation **slackened by the mid-seventeenth century** as the silver influx fell, European
population growth halted, and the [[seventeenth-century-general-crisis|general crisis]] brought
stagnation. Prices then entered a long period of relative stability until the eighteenth
century.

## Significance

The Price Revolution depressed living standards for wage-earners while enriching those who
controlled land and trade, contributing to the social tensions and state fiscal crises of the
later sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. As the first inflation to be theorized, it is a
landmark in the history of economic thought, and a classic case of the early-modern world
economy's integration through the [[global-silver-trade|silver]] that linked the Americas,
Europe, and Asia.
