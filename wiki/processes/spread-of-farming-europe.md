# Spread of Farming in Europe

The spread of farming into Europe from its Near Eastern and Balkan hearths was a long-term process of colonization, adoption, and adaptation unfolding over roughly three millennia (seventh to fourth millennia BCE). It transformed forager landscapes into agricultural ones, established the economic base for later European prehistory, and set in motion demographic, social, and environmental changes whose effects persisted into the Bronze Age.

## Definition and Scope

This process encompasses:
- The initial appearance of domesticated cereals (emmer, einkorn, bread wheat, barley) and livestock (sheep, goats, cattle, pig) in Greece and the southern Balkans ~7000 BCE.
- Northward and westward expansion via the Balkans and central Mediterranean (Cardial/Impressed Ware traditions) and the loess corridor of temperate Europe (Linearbandkeramik/LBK).
- Consolidation, infilling, and secondary colonization of less optimal soils and regions (Alpine foreland, western Mediterranean, Iberia, British Isles, Scandinavia) through the fifth and fourth millennia.
- The development of regionally adapted mixed farming systems integrating cultivation and animal husbandry.

It is distinct from the origins of agriculture in Southwest Asia and from later intensification (plough, secondary products, metallurgy) treated in subsequent processes.

## Causal Drivers (Grounded in Champion et al. 1984)

**Direct colonization** was a major mechanism, especially along the southeast–northwest axis:

"the apparent role played by direct colonization in many areas (Fig.5.7) notably on the south-east/north-west axis as well as in the central Mediterranean and to the immediate east of the Carpathians. Colonization is difficult to prove directly, though in Greece and the southern Balkans, for example, similarities in painted pottery, clay figurines and stamp-seals and other material equipment to that in the adjacent Near East are cited as supporting evidence for the hypothesis."

"Even with colonization there may regularly have been an indigenous contribution of population and skills; cattle for example seem to have been domesticated (at least on morphological criteria) earlier in Greece... than in the Near East..."

**Environmental adaptation**: Prime locations were selected first — loess soils in temperate Europe ("well drained and easily tillable"), light fertile valley soils for Balkan tells (Karanovo etc.), specific soils on the Tavoliere plain of Apulia. Later infilling and secondary colonization moved onto interfluves, poorer soils, and uplands.

**Social and economic factors**: "It was suggested in Chapter 4 that one motive for a shift to agricultural production in the first place may have come from social conditions of emulation or status-seeking through the provision of food." Cereal products could be stored; fields and pasture more easily held as personal property; production could be intensified "and used to buttress social differentiation."

The economy was a "high-yielding, diverse and above all secure blend of resources" with cereals dominant but animals essential for manure, traction, mobility, and risk-spreading.

## Major Phases

1. **Primary agricultural settlement** (7th–6th millennia BCE): Balkan tells (Karanovo, Azmak, Chevdar, Kazanluk) on fertile valleys; early Greek sites (Argissa, Sesklo, Nea Nikomedeia, Knossos); initial LBK on loess from the Carpathian fringe westward.

2. **Consolidation and expansion** (6th–5th millennia): Full LBK across central Europe; Cardial/Impressed Ware in western Mediterranean and Iberia; Alpine foreland and north-west European uptake (fifth millennium).

3. **Secondary colonization and intensification** (5th–4th millennia): Movement off prime soils; plough agriculture evidenced by ard-marks under barrows (southern England to Poland, early 4th millennium); permanent fields; forest clearance on large scales; settlement into British Isles, Scandinavia, more marginal zones. Population growth and "gradual intensification of the agricultural economy."

## Geographic Spread

The process was rapid once underway in favourable zones but uneven. Primary settlement favoured high-quality soils near water; later spread filled gaps and entered new ecological zones. The "south-east/north-west axis" carried the LBK and related traditions; Mediterranean routes carried Cardial pottery and mixed farming. Indigenous forager contributions and local domestications (e.g., cattle) varied by region. By ~4000 BCE farming economies were established across most of Europe except the far north and some upland/marginal areas.

## Interaction with Foragers and Other Processes

The frontier was not always sharp. Maps in the source show Mesolithic sites alongside early Neolithic ones. Forager–farmer interactions included possible population admixture, exchange, and the persistence of foraging in marginal zones. The shift "was not in the long term stable"; environmental disequilibria (soil erosion, heath formation, peat growth from clearance and grazing) and social stresses began to appear by ~3000 BCE, setting conditions for the next phase of expansion and hierarchy.

## End Conditions / Transformation

By the end of the fourth millennium the initial farming adaptations had produced permanent settlement, substantial population growth, wide exchange networks (Spondylus, obsidian, flint/stone axes, early copper), and the first clear signs of differentiation in some regions (fortified sites, rich cemeteries such as Varna, large Irish passage graves, Dhimini/Sesklo acropoleis). These laid the foundations for Bronze Age developments without being identical to them. The "replacement of the first agricultural adaptations in Europe was initiated soon after 7000 and completed everywhere by about 4000 bc but the second set of adaptations were not in the long term stable."

## Additional dates (Fagan & Durrani 2020)

Fagan & Durrani (2020) corroborate the Champion et al. picture with further
detail: the **Bandkeramik (Linear Pottery) culture** appeared on the Middle
Danube ~5300 BCE and spread to Holland and Ukraine, with villages of 40–60
people holding territories of roughly 202 ha; a **Black Sea flood event** (~5500
BCE, the Euxine Lake breach) may have driven inland migration up the Danube. The
**plow** reached Europe by ~2800 BCE, coinciding with a shift from communal to
individual burial and emergent hereditary chieftainship, visible in the
monument sequence at **Avebury** (~2550 BCE) and **Silbury Hill** (~2200 BCE).
(Fagan & Durrani 2020)

## Historiography Note (from source)

The authors emphasize that "economy and society are indivisible" and reject models that divorce subsistence from social organization or treat diffusion as the sole explanation. They stress local adaptation, variation in pace and character, and the need for detailed local subsistence studies over averaged rates of spread.
