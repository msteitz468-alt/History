## Definition and Scope

The Roman conquest of Italy denotes the roughly century-and-a-half process by which the city-state of Rome subdued and incorporated the diverse peoples of the Italian peninsula — Etruscans, Latins, Samnites, Greek colonial cities of the south, and others — into a hegemonic system under Roman leadership, culminating in effective Roman control of peninsular Italy by 264 BCE, on the eve of the First Punic War. It is distinct from the later cultural process of the [[processes/romanization-of-italy|Romanization of Italy]], which describes the subsequent cultural and linguistic integration of the peninsula rather than its military conquest.

## Causal Drivers

The conquest was enabled by Rome's centuriate military system and the flexible allied-and-citizen structure Rome extended to defeated peoples (full citizenship, citizenship without the vote, or formal alliance with manpower obligations), which converted former enemies into a growing reservoir of soldiers rather than merely subjugated tributaries. The trauma of the [[events/gallic-sack-of-rome-390bce|Gallic Sack of Rome]] (390 BCE) is often cited as a spur to subsequent Roman military and diplomatic reorganization and a more assertive expansionist posture in the fourth century.

## Major Phases

Key phases included the capture of Veii (396 BCE) and subjugation of southern Etruria; the Latin War (340–338 BCE) and reorganization of the Latin League into direct Roman alliance structures; the protracted Samnite Wars (343–290 BCE), Rome's most difficult peninsular conflicts; and the Pyrrhic War (280–275 BCE) against the Greek cities of southern Italy and their Epirote ally King Pyrrhus, whose costly defeat of Rome in early battles but ultimate failure gave the language "Pyrrhic victory" and left Rome dominant over the whole peninsula by 264 BCE.

## Geographic Spread

Conquest proceeded outward from Latium in concentric phases — first central Italy (Etruria, the Latin cities, the Volscians and Aequians), then the mountainous Samnite interior, and finally the Greek and Oscan-speaking south — with the far north (Cisalpine Gaul) and the Alps remaining outside full Roman control until later conquests in the following two centuries.

## Interaction

The conquest of Italy created the manpower base and logistical platform that enabled Rome's subsequent transformation into a Mediterranean-wide power, feeding directly into the [[processes/romanization-of-italy|Romanization of Italy]] and the wars against Carthage.

## End Conditions

The process is conventionally treated as complete with Roman hegemony over the peninsula (excluding Cisalpine Gaul) established by 264 BCE, after which Roman warfare turned outward — to Sicily and the wider Mediterranean — rather than continuing to consolidate peninsular Italy itself.
