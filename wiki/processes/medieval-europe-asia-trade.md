# Medieval Europe–Asia Trade under the Mongols

The penetration of **Latin (chiefly Italian) merchants into Mongol Asia**, c. 1260–1350 — the
commercial strand of Mongol–Western contact, made possible by the Pax
Mongolica and the [[silk-roads|trans-Asian routes]]. From [[jackson-mongols-and-the-west|Jackson's]]
"Traders and Adventurers."

## Definition and Scope

The process is the **direct commercial penetration of Mongol Eurasia by European merchants**
(above all Genoese and Venetian), from the Black Sea gateways to the markets of Persia,
Central Asia, and China. It encompasses both the Mongols' own promotion of trade and the Latin
mercantile networks that exploited it; it is the economic counterpart of the era's
[[medieval-latin-missions-to-asia|missions]] and [[mongol-latin-crusade-alliance|diplomacy]].

## Causal Drivers

The conquests created a **wealthy Mongol elite** eager for luxuries unavailable in the steppe
(above all **gold brocade**, the *nasij* known as *panna tartarica*), and the Mongols
**actively promoted trade** — issuing **paiza** safe-conduct tablets, granting use of the
*yam* relays, and financing merchant partnerships (***ortogh***). The pax mongolica made
long-distance trade secure and calculable.

## Major Phases

1. **Opening (c. 1260–80):** Italian expansion into the Black Sea after the Fourth Crusade;
   the **Polo family's** first journeys.
2. **Heyday (c. 1280–1340):** Caffa and Tana as gateways; Marco Polo's book; merchants
   reaching Tabriz, Central Asia, and China; **Pegolotti's** handbook (c. 1340) calling the
   road to Cathay "perfectly safe."
3. **Collapse (c. 1340–50):** war among the khanates, plague, and the closing of the routes.

## Geographic Spread

The trade ran from the **Genoese (Caffa) and Venetian (Tana) colonies** of the Black Sea —
the gateways to the [[golden-horde|Golden Horde]] — east to Tabriz, across Central Asia, and to
**China**, with goods (silk, spices) and **Kipchak slaves** (sold on to the
[[mamluk-sultanate|Mamluks]]) moving along it.

## Interaction

It ran on the **pax mongolica** and the **[[silk-roads]]**, formed one of the three
strands of Mongol–Latin contact (with [[medieval-latin-missions-to-asia|missions]] and
[[mongol-latin-crusade-alliance|crusade diplomacy]]), enriched the Italian maritime cities,
and produced the travel literature (Marco Polo, Pegolotti) that shaped Europe's
[[medieval-european-images-of-the-mongols|image of Asia]].

## End Conditions

The system was as fragile as the missions: the **fragmentation and wars** of the khanates, the
**[[black-death|Black Death]]** (which entered Europe through Caffa in 1346), the **fall of the
Yuan (1368)**, and the closing of the overland routes ended direct Latin trade with the Far
East. European commerce fell back on the Mediterranean and Black Sea entrepôts — and the
remembered "open road to Cathay" became a spur to the later search for a **sea route to the
Indies** ("would Columbus have sailed?").
