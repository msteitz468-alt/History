# Medieval Europe–Asia Trade under the Mongols

The penetration of **Latin (chiefly Italian) merchants into Mongol Asia**, c. 1260–1350 —
the commercial strand of Mongol–Western contact, made possible by the
[[pax-mongolica|Pax Mongolica]] and the [[silk-roads|trans-Asian routes]]. From the Black
Sea colonies of **Genoa and Venice**, Latin traders reached the markets of Persia, Central
Asia, and even China, producing both real fortunes and the most famous travel book of the
Middle Ages. Treated in [[jackson-mongols-and-the-west|Jackson's]] "Traders and
Adventurers."

## The Mongol commercial system

The conquests created a newly **wealthy Mongol elite** willing to pay inflated prices for
luxuries unavailable in the steppe — above all **gold brocades**, the *nasij* known in
Europe as ***panna tartarica*** ("Tartar cloth"), used for ceremonial robes, gifts, and
tent-linings as the chief index of status. The Mongols did not merely receive trade
passively (Allsen) but **actively promoted** it: rulers issued the **paiza** tablets of
authority granting merchants safe-conduct and use of the *yam* relays, and advanced capital
to merchant partnerships — the ***ortogh*** (from Turkish *ortaq*, "partner") — who imported
the goods the court wanted and, in Yuan China, even acted as tax-farmers. Ghazan in Persia
and Qaidu and the Chaghadayids in Central Asia reformed currencies and founded towns
(Andijan) to foster commerce.

## Latin merchants in the Mongol world

Italian merchants, long present in Constantinople and the Crusader ports, expanded into the
**Black Sea** after the Fourth Crusade. With the Mongols' arrival the northern shore became
a commercial frontier: **Soldaia** (where Marco Polo's uncle owned a house), and then the
great Genoese colony of **Caffa** and the Venetian **Tana** at the mouth of the Don, became
the gateways to the [[golden-horde|Golden Horde]] and the routes east. Through them flowed
silk, spices, and the **Kipchak slaves** sold on to the [[mamluk-sultanate|Mamluks]]; from
them merchants pushed to Tabriz, Central Asia, and China. The **Polo family** — Niccolò,
Maffeo, and **Marco** — made their celebrated journey to the court of **Khubilai**, and
Marco's book became Europe's defining (if doubted) account of the East. The merchant
handbook of **Francesco Pegolotti** (*La pratica della mercatura*, c. 1340) could even
describe the road to **Cathay** as "perfectly safe, whether by day or by night" — the
high-water mark of European commercial confidence in Mongol Asia.

## Collapse

The system was as fragile as the [[medieval-latin-missions-to-asia|missions]] it paralleled.
The fragmentation and wars of the khanates, the **[[black-death|Black Death]]** (which
entered Europe precisely through Caffa in 1346), the **fall of the Yuan (1368)**, and the
closing of the overland routes ended the direct Latin trade with the Far East; Pegolotti's
"safe road" was already nostalgic when he wrote it. European commerce fell back on the
Mediterranean and Black Sea entrepôts, and the memory of the open road to Cathay became one
of the spurs to the later search for a **sea route to the Indies**.

## Significance

This trade was the economic face of the [[chinggis-exchange|Mongol-era opening]] of Eurasia:
it enriched the Italian maritime cities, carried Asian goods and knowledge west, produced
the travel literature (Marco Polo, Pegolotti) that shaped Europe's image of Asia — and, in
its collapse and remembered promise, helped motivate the oceanic voyages of the 15th
century ("without the Mongol Empire would Columbus have sailed?").
