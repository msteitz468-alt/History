# Eastern Seed-Crop Complex

## Definition and Scope

The **Eastern Seed-Crop Complex** (Eastern Agricultural Complex) is the independent Eastern Woodlands domestication of oily and starchy seed plants **before** full maize dependence — a genuine New World hearth contrasting with the Southwest’s Mexican-origin crop package. *part_of:* [[early-agriculture-americas]] and [[north-american-agricultural-transition]]; *contrasts_with:* [[southwest-agricultural-development]]; *enabled:* [[hopewell-culture]] dietary base; *preceded_by* foraging.

Cordell & Smith (CHNPA I.1 ch. 4): within the deciduous forest region of eastern North America the transition from exclusive wild-species reliance to crop-centered farming was neither abrupt nor uniform; unlike the Southwest, it was not initially based on tropical cultigens from Mesoamerica.

## Causal Drivers

- Documented seed-morphology changes marking domestication
- Intensive management of floodplain and riverine habitats
- Seasonal scheduling of fishing camps and garden plots (later pre-adaptation for maize)
- Bow and arrow, thermally efficient pottery, and settlement filling in Late Woodland

## Major Phases

**Domestication by 2000–1500 B.C.** Based on seed-morphology changes, at least four indigenous North American seed plants were domesticated by 2000–1500 B.C.: lamb’s quarter/chenopodium, marsh elder (*Iva*), squash, and sunflower. A total of **seven local crop plants** were cultivated prior to maize introduction ~A.D. 200. Early *Cucurbita* rind fragments (5000–3000 B.C.) are now more likely wild eastern gourdlike squash (*cf. C. texana*) domesticated locally, not Mexican domesticated squash introducing agriculture.

**Dietary primacy ~200 B.C.–start of Christian era.** Despite early domestication, indigenous crops became major dietary items only between ~200 B.C. and the beginning of the Christian era (or somewhat earlier) over a broad **interior riverine East** — marking the initial strong horticultural component. Debate continues on the pre-A.D. 800–1100 economic role (affluent foragers vs gardeners vs strong food producers). Farming economies were largely restricted to that interior riverine area until ~A.D. 800–900 (squash only frequent outside).

**Hopewellian association (~200 B.C.–A.D. 400).** With the premaize seed-crop rise, the interior riverine East developed a network of Hopewellian societies. See [[hopewell-culture]].

**Late Woodland (A.D. 400–800).** Links Hopewellian societies to the A.D. 800–1100 maize-centered emergence. Archaeologically less visible due to reduced corporate ceremonial evidence and bland domestic uniformity. By ~A.D. 400 high corporate integration largely disappeared — smaller simplified mortuary programs, almost no large earthwork labor, reduced long-distance trade. Hopewellian mortuary-centered societies were replaced by more unbounded kinship networks among dispersed households; material culture largely undecorated over broad areas.

Late Woodland domestic reorganization included: bow and arrow introduction (easier large-game hunting); thinner-walled, better-tempered, thermally efficient globular cooking vessels with growing seed-crop reliance; reduced resource territories and longer occupation of locales in some rich areas; more settlements suggesting population growth/landscape filling; typical clusters of ≤4 households with little internal organization; some larger organized farming villages by A.D. 600 (e.g. ditched bluff-edge Waterplant site, central Ohio; middle Ohio Valley ≥10 households).

**Maize transition A.D. 800–1100.** Maize introduced ~A.D. 200 (most likely from the Southwest) remained relatively minor for 6–7 centuries (stable carbon isotopes); A.D. 800–1100 dramatic shift to maize-centered field agriculture across much of the East. Where premaize food production already existed (e.g. Middle Ohio), maize replaced indigenous starchy staples (maygrass, chenopodium), which disappeared after A.D. 1000; farther north, spring–summer macroband fishing camps near good agricultural land provided seasonal scheduling preadaptation. See [[maize-domestication]].

## Geographic Spread

Interior riverine Eastern Woodlands core; limited farming economies outside until after ~A.D. 800–900. Northern flint / eastern eight-row maize then opened Northeast and Great Lakes. Plains Woodland (~100 B.C.–A.D. 900) shows interaction with Hopewellian East (pottery, beads, mound burial, copper, obsidian) but **no direct evidence** Plains Woodland groups relied on agriculture despite grinding stones/stone hoes.

## Interaction

- *enabled:* [[hopewell-culture]] premaize farming base
- *followed_by:* eastern maize dietary dominance and light bridge to [[mississippian-culture]] (Cordell & Smith defer ranked agricultural chiefdoms to ch. 5)
- *contrasts_with:* SW Initiation-period utilitarian stylistic homogeneity (not shared ceremonial iconography)

## End Conditions

Transformed rather than ended: after A.D. 1000 many indigenous starchy staples disappear where maize takes the carbohydrate role; sunflower, squash, and beans continue variably. The complex’s historical significance is as the independent Eastern path that preceded and then was partly superseded by maize-centered systems ([[oneota]], [[fort-ancient-culture]], [[plains-village-tradition]], northern Iroquoian trajectories).
