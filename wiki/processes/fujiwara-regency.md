## Definition and Scope

The Fujiwara Regency designates the process by which the Northern House of the [[fujiwara-clan|Fujiwara clan]] achieved and maintained near-total dominance of the Heian court from the appointment of Yoshifusa as regent in 857 to the accession of Go-Sanjō in 1068 — the first emperor in 170 years whose mother was not a Fujiwara. The mature phase, often called the *sekkan jidai*, ran from 967, when an unbroken succession of Fujiwara regents began, to 1068. At its apex under [[fujiwara-michinaga|Fujiwara no Michinaga]] (995–1028), twenty-two of twenty-five senior ministers were Fujiwara, the rest being Minamoto closely related to Michinaga by marriage.

The regency was "in many aspects simply a prolonged and institutionalized phase in the cyclically shifting balance of power between the imperial line and the noble clans that had for shorter periods and in less formal ways been characteristic of Japanese government since at least the seventh century" (CHJ Vol. 2, p. 74). It was distinguished from earlier noble dominance by its duration, its inheritance in a single family line, the greater absoluteness of its domination, and its coincidence with period of shrinking central government authority that made personal relationships and private purposes salient features of governance.

## Major Phases

### Establishment: Yoshifusa and the First Regency (857–891)

Fujiwara no Yoshifusa (804–872) became the first regent appointed from outside the imperial clan when he was recognized as guardian of his maternal grandson, Emperor Seiwa, in 858. The formal office was confirmed by imperial rescript in 866. At the time of his death in 872, Yoshifusa had transformed the Fujiwara leadership from the most powerful clan at court into something institutionally different: the holder of an ad hoc regental office that stood outside the statutory framework but above its regular organs.

Yoshifusa's heir Mototsune (836–891) consolidated the position, serving as regent through four reigns and receiving in 887 the *kampaku* title for his role as regent to an adult emperor — the first formal use of that title in its classic sense. The Akō Controversy of 887 and related episodes of the period reflect the ongoing problem of how to define the Fujiwara leader's position vis-à-vis the statutory government.

### Interlude: Rule Without Regent (891–967)

After Mototsune's death in 891, Emperor Uda refused to appoint a successor regent, instead relying on the scholar-official Sugawara no Michizane and Mototsune's son Tokihira. This "anti-Fujiwara" interlude lasted only until Michizane's exile in 901. Even during this period the Fujiwara held high office; what lapsed was not their power but the formal regental title. Tadahira (880–949) resumed the regency and in his time the formal distinction between *sesshō* (regent for a minor emperor) and *kampaku* (regent for an adult) was clearly articulated for the first time (see [[sekkan-system]]).

### The Full Regency Period (967–1028)

In 967 Fujiwara no Saneyori was appointed *kampaku*, beginning what the CHJ describes as "the full regency period," in which heads of the Northern House served as regents almost without interruption. This phase saw:

- **Kanemichi vs. Kaneie** (970s): A succession dispute between brothers, resolved in Kanemichi's favor by an alleged deathbed letter from their sister Empress Anshi designating seniority order.
- **Kaneie** (986–990): High-handed regency; his daughter Senshi was the mother of Ichijō, making him regent to his grandson.
- **Michikane** (995): Brief regency, lasting only days before his death.
- **Michinaga** (995–1028): The apex of Fujiwara power. See [[fujiwara-michinaga]].

### The Regency Under Yorimichi and Decline (1028–1068)

Michinaga's son Yorimichi (992–1074) inherited the regency but lacked his father's political skill and, crucially, failed to marry daughters into the imperial succession. When Emperor Go-Reizei abdicated in 1045, the reigning emperor was able to install as crown prince a son born of an imperial princess rather than a Fujiwara consort. The accession of Go-Sanjō in 1068, the first emperor without Fujiwara maternal ties in 170 years, ended the regency's structural foundation. Yorimichi's brother Norimichi succeeded as regent in 1068 but the political center of gravity had shifted irreversibly to the imperial line.

Yorimichi's regency was not without consequence. In the 1040s he presided over an important fiscal reform: the introduction of fixed, uniform tax rates (not subject to change by provincial administrators) and the formal recognition of most existing shoen. This reform is thought to have stabilized court revenues for the final century of court autonomous rule.

## Geographic Spread

The regency was a specifically capital phenomenon — a mechanism of Kyoto court politics. Its effects, however, spread throughout the country through:

- **Provincial governorships**: The regent's controlling influence over appointments made governors politically dependent on him and generated a stream of gifts and commendations.
- **Shoen accumulation**: Provincial tax managers commended holdings to the regent as the most powerful patron; by Michinaga's time the regental shoen holdings were described (hyperbolically) as encompassing all land in the country.
- **Household administration**: The regent's household administrative office (*mandokoro*) grew from a handful of titled officials to over twenty court-ranked officials by Yorimichi's time, managing a "large private empire of mansions, palaces, and land" (CHJ Vol. 2, p. 79).

## Interaction with Other Processes

- **preceded_by**: [[fujiwara-clan]] ascendancy under Nakatomi/Fujiwara from the Taika Reform era
- **concurrent_with**: [[heian-court-culture|Heian court culture]] — the regency's concentration of wealth and leisure at court was a material precondition for the literary and artistic flourishing of the period
- **produced**: [[sekkan-system]] as institutional form
- **contrasts_with**: [[insei]] (cloister government) — the retired-emperor system that displaced regency as the dominant power mechanism after 1068
- **enabled**: Growth of shoen system and provincial gentry power through withdrawal of central government from direct administration

## End Conditions

The regency ended through demographic failure of its marriage-politics mechanism: Yorimichi's inability to produce Fujiwara-born imperial heirs meant no Fujiwara grandson occupied the throne after Go-Reizei's abdication in 1045. Without the blood tie of maternal grandfather to minor emperor, the structural rationale for a Fujiwara regent dissolved. Emperor Go-Sanjō and the subsequent retired emperors then systematically rebuilt direct imperial power through the [[insei]] apparatus.

## Historiography

The long-dominant view of the regency as a usurpation of imperial power by self-interested nobles has been substantially revised in modern Japanese historiography. The CHJ reflects the current scholarly consensus: the regency was not a genuine seizure of new power but "the confirmation of existing power through the creation of a new office" (CHJ Vol. 2, p. 77). The emperor was not displaced from constitutional centrality; the regency's "essential legitimacy... continued to be derived from the person of the emperor and from the by then hallowed, if much truncated and changed, framework of the statutory regime" (CHJ Vol. 2, p. 74–75).

The most significant revisionist argument in the CHJ is that "the terms 'regency government' and 'regency period' are misnomers": what distinguished this phase of Heian history was not the regency itself but the change in provincial administration and tax policy at the beginning of the tenth century. The regency was "simply an adjustment in, or relatively limited transformation of, the central government mechanism centering on the Council of State" (CHJ Vol. 2, p. 75).
