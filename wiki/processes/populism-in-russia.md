# Populism in Russia (Narodnichestvo)

**Populism in Russia** (*narodnichestvo*) was the dominant radical movement and ideology of the Russian intelligentsia in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. Staking the future of Russia on the peasantry and the traditional peasant commune (*obshchina*), populists sought to bypass the capitalist stage of development and transition directly to agrarian socialism. The movement evolved from peaceful propaganda to revolutionary terrorism and eventually political party organization in the Socialist Revolutionary Party.

## Definition and Scope

The process refers to the development of the populist movement and its associated theories between 1860 and 1918. Unlike Western populist movements, Russian populism was initiated by the educated elite (the *intelligentsia*) on behalf of the peasants, rather than by the peasants themselves. Its key tenets included:
- **Agrarian Socialism:** The belief that the collective **peasant commune** (*mir* or *obshchina*) was a morally superior, proto-socialist institution that would allow Russia to avoid the evils of Western industrial capitalism.
- **The Intelligentsia's Debt:** The moral conviction that the educated classes owed a debt to the working masses, whose exploitation had funded the elite's culture and education.
- **Subjective Sociology:** A methodological approach (developed by Mikhailovsky) that rejected objective historical laws in favor of moral values and the agency of "critically thinking individuals" in shaping history.

## Causal Drivers

- **Disappointment with the 1861 Emancipation:** Radicals believed the emancipation settlement was a betrayal of the peasantry, as it deprived them of land and burdened them with redemption payments, leaving them destitute.
- **Theoretical Founders:** **Alexander Herzen** and **Mikhail Bakunin** preached from exile that the peasant commune was the seed of Russia's future socialist transformation. Bakunin's anarchism also provided a call for violent, spontaneous rebellion.
- **The Russian Autocracy:** The lack of legal political channels, representation, or freedom of expression under the autocracy left reform-minded intellectuals with no outlet, pushing them toward underground organization and radicalism.

## Major Phases

### 1. Intellectual Origins and Nihilism (1860s)
The movement was prepared by the writings of **Nicholas Chernyshevsky** (whose novel *What Is To Be Done?* served as a handbook for radical lifestyle and cooperation) and the critic Nicholas Dobroliubov. Initially, this took the form of **nihilism** (spokeman: Dmitrii Pisarev), which focused on individual liberation and the rejection of traditional values, before evolving into social protest.

### 2. "Going to the People" (1874)
In the summer of 1874, in a spontaneous movement of romantic idealism, thousands of educated young men and women went to the countryside (*khozhdeniye v narod*) to live among the peasants, teach them, and spread socialist ideas. The campaign was a disaster: the peasants, suspicious of the outsiders and loyal to the tsar, rejected their ideas and frequently turned them over to the police, leading to mass arrests.

### 3. The Turn to Terrorism (1876–1881)
Following the failure of peaceful propaganda, the movement reorganized into the conspiratorial group **"Land and Liberty"** (*Zemlya i Volya*). In 1879, the group split over tactics:
- **"Black Partition"** (*Chernyi Peredel*): Led by George Plekhanov, they advocated continued peaceful propaganda among peasants (Plekhanov later converted to Marxism).
- **"The Will of the People"** (*Narodnaya Volya*): A disciplined terrorist organization that focused on assassinating government officials and the tsar to trigger a popular revolution. On March 13, 1881, they successfully assassinated Emperor **Alexander II**, but this led only to severe government reaction and the suppression of the group.

### 4. Theoretical Consolidation (1880s–1890s)
Populism was kept alive by theoreticians like Peter Lavrov (who emphasized the role of "critically thinking individuals") and Nicholas Mikhailovsky. They defended the commune against the rising Russian Marxist school, which argued that capitalism was inevitable in Russia.

### 5. The Socialist Revolutionary Party (1901–1918)
In the early twentieth century, populism reborn as the **Socialist Revolutionary (SR) Party** under the leadership of **Victor Chernov**. The SRs combined populist agrarian socialism (calling for the "socialization of land") with Marxist organizational tactics, maintaining a Combat Organization that executed political assassinations. The SRs gained a massive following among the peasantry.

## Interaction

- **Conflict with Marxism:** The populists engaged in a bitter ideological debate with the Social Democrats (Marxists). While Marxists focused on the industrial proletariat and viewed capitalism as a necessary historical stage, populists focused on the peasantry and argued that Russia could bypass capitalism.
- **Peasant Commune:** The populist defense of the commune was challenged by Witte's industrialization and Stolypin's land reforms, which actively sought to dismantle the commune and establish private property.

## End Conditions

The populist process ended during the **1917 Revolutions**. Although the SRs won a massive victory in the democratic elections to the Constituent Assembly in late 1917 (reflecting the peasant majority), the assembly was dissolved by the Bolsheviks in January 1918. During the subsequent Civil War, the SRs were suppressed, and the Bolsheviks' command economy and later collectivization dismantled the peasant commune, ending populism as a political force.
