# Cross-Channel Exchange between Britain and Gaul

**From a single tax-driven Roman economic zone, through collapse, to a new and broad-based North Sea
world.** Robin Fleming's chapter (Oxford Handbook, Ch. 17) traces the movement of people and things
across the Channel from the fourth to the seventh century, arguing for a **genuine hiatus** between the
late-Roman and early-medieval exchange systems and against the older picture of Merovingian commercial
hegemony over Britain.

## Definition and Scope

The process spans two distinct economies separated by a fifth-century break, and the chapter is as
much about what did *not* persist as what did.

## The Late-Roman Fiscal Economy (4th century)

Britain, northern Gaul, and the Rhineland "formed a single, integrated economic region." Cross-Channel
movement of pottery and coin was driven by the Roman state — the *annona* (tax-in-kind) and
*coemptiones* (bulk purchases), "Wickham's 'bulk product hegemonies'" — not private commerce. Pottery
maps the tax-flow: Argonne, Mayen, and Céramique à l'éponge wares reach Britain; British BB1 and New
Forest wares reach Brittany and Normandy, moving as "subsidiary cargo" on annona shipments through the
Richborough–Boulogne crossing. Mobile state agents are tracked by crossbow brooches and belt sets,
some isotopically born outside Britain — "people who were part of a broader, late-Roman world," not
"barbarian invaders."

## Collapse and Hiatus (c. 400–500)

The Roman economy collapsed in Britain c. 400–425: coin supply ceased ("less than three dozen"
fifth-century coins, "most of them pierced and worn as jewelry"), villas and cereal-export systems
ended. Fleming rejects the older "early Frankish settlement" reading of fifth-century English belt-set
and weapon burials (Hawkes, Evison): "it is difficult to identify self-consciously 'Frankish' burials
in Gaul until the very end of the fifth century." Almost all Frankish-style objects in Britain — and
"Anglo-Saxon" pots and brooches in Gaul — date to the sixth century and later, "argu[ing] for a genuine
hiatus in the persistence of late-Roman networks across the channel."

## The Sixth-Century North Sea World

The new exchange was **broad, non-elite, and polycentric** — a key [REVISION] of the Merovingian-
hegemony model. Frankish goods in sixth-century Kent appear "not limited to the fanciest 1 percent…
but… in graves of quite middling people — perhaps the best-off 40 percent," implying many boat-owning
households rather than royal-monopoly trade. Jean Soulat catalogued **800-plus** "Merovingian" finds in
England (450–650), but Fleming reads the scale as evidence of breadth, not control. East and west
differed sharply: in the **west**, Frankish (and earlier Byzantine) merchants traded E ware and glass
"only with elite high-status sites"; in the **east**, exchange was broad and involved Frisians,
Scandinavians, and part-time mariners. "Circular migrators" — recent migrants still journeying between
old Continental and new British homes — were likely "central in early maritime exchanges."

## Interaction and End Conditions

The process feeds directly into the bottom-up reading of the Merovingian economy (see
[[merovingian-economy]] and [[merovingian-emporia]]): broad rural access to imports across the North
Sea parallels Theuws's argument for inland northern Gaul. By the later seventh century the river-and-
coast networks were being formalized into the emporia (Quentovic, Dorestad) that the Carolingians would
tax.
