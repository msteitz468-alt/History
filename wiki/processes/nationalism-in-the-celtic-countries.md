# Nationalism in the Celtic Countries

The nineteenth- and twentieth-century emergence of distinct nationalist and
cultural-revival movements among the Celtic-language peoples of the
British Isles and Brittany — the Welsh, Scots (Gaelic and Lowland),
Irish, Cornish, Manx, and Bretons — asserting linguistic, cultural, and, in
varying degrees, political autonomy from the English- and French-dominated
states in which they were incorporated.

## Definition and Scope

The process spans a spectrum from cultural revivalism (language
preservation, folklore collection, literary and musical movements such as
the [[celtic-revival-ireland|Celtic Revival]]) to explicit political
nationalism seeking autonomy or independence, exemplified most fully by
Irish nationalism's culmination in independence (1922) and, more recently,
by devolved government in Wales and Scotland and ongoing regional-language
movements in Brittany and Cornwall.

## Causal Drivers

Romantic-era intellectual interest across Europe in vernacular language,
folk tradition, and pre-modern national origins provided much of the
cultural vocabulary for these movements, which drew on real historical
figures and narratives — including medieval resistance leaders such as
[[owain-glyndwr|Owain Glyndŵr]] in Wales — as symbols of a distinct national
past predating incorporation into the English or French state.

## Major Phases

Nineteenth-century cultural and linguistic revival movements (language
societies, folklore collection, literary revivals) preceded more explicitly
political nationalist organizing in the late nineteenth and early twentieth
centuries, which in Ireland culminated in armed revolution and independence
(1916–1922), while Welsh, Scottish, Cornish, and Breton movements more often
pursued cultural preservation and, later, devolved political autonomy within
existing states.

## Geographic Spread

The process unfolded largely independently but with mutual awareness across
Ireland, Wales, Scotland, Cornwall, the Isle of Man, and Brittany, each
adapting shared Romantic-nationalist and Celticist ideas to distinct local
political circumstances and degrees of state centralization.

## Interaction

Celtic nationalism intersects closely with the broader European wave of
nineteenth-century linguistic and ethnic nationalism, while its outcomes
diverged sharply by context: Irish nationalism achieved full state
independence, while Welsh, Scottish, Cornish, and Breton movements have
generally pursued cultural recognition and devolved autonomy within larger
states rather than full separation.

## End Conditions

The process remains ongoing: devolution in Scotland and Wales (from 1999),
continued Scottish independence debate, and active language-revival and
regional-autonomy movements in Cornwall and Brittany show no clear
end point, in contrast to the Irish case's definitive resolution in
independence.
