# Nanban Trade (南蛮貿易, "Southern Barbarian Trade")

The **nanban** ("southern barbarian") trade was the Japanese term for commerce with the Portuguese
and Spanish — European traders who arrived from the south (Southeast Asia and Macao) in the mid-
sixteenth century. It encompassed not only goods but also the Catholic missionary enterprise that
accompanied Iberian commercial expansion, and its entanglement of commerce with religion was the
central problem the [[tokugawa-shogunate|Tokugawa bakufu]] inherited and sought to resolve.
Source: [[sadler-shogun-tokugawa-ieyasu-1937]].

---

## Definition and Scope

"Nanban" (literally "southern barbarians") was the Japanese rendering of a classical Chinese term
for peoples to the south. Applied initially to the Portuguese who arrived at Tanegashima in 1543,
it came to designate Iberian (Portuguese and Spanish) traders and missionaries generally. The trade
covered Chinese silk, Indian textiles, European weapons and curiosities, and Japanese silver and
copper exported in return. Jesuit missionaries arrived with the Portuguese (Francis Xavier, 1549);
Franciscans arrived with the Spanish after their establishment at Manila (1571). The two orders
were rivals, and their mutual denunciations to Japanese authorities significantly shaped the
Tokugawa anti-Christian policy.

The nanban trade is distinct from — and partially prior to — the [[red-seal-ships|red-seal ship
system]], which was Tokugawa Japan's own state-licensed commercial apparatus aimed at Southeast
Asian entrepôts. The two systems overlapped in the early Tokugawa decades before the nanban trade
was terminated.

---

## Causal Drivers

**Portuguese Estado da India** required profits from the Japan-China silk trade to sustain its
Indian Ocean empire. Macao (established 1557) served as the Sino-Japanese entrepôt; the annual
"black ship" from Macao to Nagasaki was the commercial centerpiece of the Portuguese-Japan
relationship.

**Japanese silver supply** — Japan was one of the world's largest silver producers in the sixteenth
century — made it a vital node in global silver circulation, connecting to Chinese demand via
Portuguese intermediaries.

**Missionary enterprise** was inseparable from the Portuguese commercial presence in formal terms
(the *padroado* system obligated Portugal to support Catholic missions) but increasingly
incompatible with Japanese political stability as mission success created a class of Japanese
Christians (some 300,000 by its peak) with potential loyalties to a foreign spiritual authority.

---

## Major Phases

**Early missionary tolerance (1543–1587).** Oda Nobunaga actively patronized Jesuit missionaries
as a counterweight to Buddhist institutional power, allowing rapid conversion growth especially in
Kyushu. The southern daimyo — Arima, Ōmura, Ōtomo — converted, partly for commercial access to
the Portuguese ships they controlled.

**Hideyoshi's anti-Christian edicts (1587, 1597).** Toyotomi Hideyoshi issued a decree expelling
Jesuit missionaries in 1587 (largely unenforced) and in 1597 executed twenty-six Christians
(the Martyrs of Japan) in Nagasaki. Commercial trade with Portugal was allowed to continue despite
these suppressions.

**Early Tokugawa period (1600–1612).** Ieyasu initially tolerated Christianity, hoping to attract
European trade. His letter to the Viceroy of New Spain (1612) captures the distinction he tried to
maintain: "the mutually profitable trade that will follow the arrival of merchant vessels is in the
highest degree desirable" while "the religion that your honored country uses has tendencies of quite
a different kind." The Okamoto Daihachi forgery scandal (a Christian secretary of Honda forged
documents for Arima, a Christian daimyo) and the Okubo Nagayasu mining fraud (alleged conspiracy
to bring in foreign soldiers) hardened Ieyasu's view that institutional Christianity was
incompatible with the bakufu's order.

**Protestant competition and the Madre de Dios incident (1609).** The arrival of Dutch and
English traders — Protestant, anti-Catholic, and commercially credible witnesses against Iberian
missionary aims — gave Ieyasu an alternative. [[adams-william|William Adams]] explained to Ieyasu
that Catholic missionaries had been expelled from England, Holland, and Scandinavia. The 1609
destruction of the Portuguese galleon *Madre de Dios* at Nagasaki — sanctioned by Ieyasu after a
cargo dispute between Arima Harunobu's men and Portuguese sailors at Macao — signaled that the
bakufu's protection of Iberian merchants was contingent and revocable.

**Suppression (1612–1639).** In 1612 Ieyasu issued an edict against Christianity for officers
and samurai. In 1614 all Jesuits were ordered to leave Japan; sixty-three Jesuits and prominent
Japanese Christians were shipped to Manila. The Franciscan Sotelo's involvement with
[[date-masamune|Date Masamune's]] embassy to Europe (1613) and the subsequent confirmation of
Japanese Christians fighting under Spanish insignia at the Osaka siege (1615) accelerated the
crackdown. The [[christianity-in-japan|persecution]] culminated in the [[sakoku]] edicts of
1633–1639 and the expulsion of the Portuguese in 1639.

---

## Geographic Spread

Nanban trade was concentrated at Hirado (the first Portuguese port of call), Nagasaki (which
became the designated Portuguese trading port under Ōmura Sumitada, 1571), and Hakata. Missionaries
spread inland, with concentrations in Kyushu and Kyoto. The trade reached its maximum geographic
penetration in the 1580s when Jesuit networks extended into Kyoto and the Kinai region.

---

## Interaction with Other Processes

The nanban trade was the commercial-religious system that made the [[red-seal-ships|red-seal ship]]
system necessary. By providing an alternative (Japanese-controlled, non-missionary) framework for
overseas commerce, Ieyasu could argue that expelling missionaries did not mean abandoning foreign
trade. The Dutch factory at Hirado (established 1609) and the English factory (1613–1623)
overlapped the last years of the nanban system, demonstrating that trade without missionaries was
possible.

## End Conditions

The nanban trade ended with the expulsion of the Portuguese in 1639 following the Shimabara
Rebellion (1637–1638), in which Japanese Christians and displaced peasants rose in revolt. The
bakufu concluded that any contact with Catholic countries was irreconcilable with domestic order.
The Spanish had already been effectively excluded in the 1620s. The Dutch alone were retained,
confined to the artificial island of Deshima at Nagasaki.

---

## Historiography

The nanban trade is among the best-documented episodes in early modern Japanese history, supported
by Jesuit annual letters and reports (*cartas*), Portuguese Estado da India records, Japanese
domain chronicles, and the physical evidence of nanban art (Japanese paintings of Portuguese
merchants and their ships form a recognized artistic genre). The standard interpretive framework —
that Iberian missionary activity fatally entangled commerce with religion and forced the bakufu to
choose — is well established. More recent scholarship has emphasized the commercial logic of
Japanese silver exports and has complicated the simple missionary-threat narrative by examining how
Japanese Christians actively shaped their own conversions and persecutions.
