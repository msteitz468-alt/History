## Definition and Scope

The apartment compound system was the dominant form of residential organization at [[teotihuacan]] during most of the city's urban history. A walled compound enclosed multiple apartments sharing internal open courts, housing what appears to have been a corporate residential group — probably a kin-based or occupationally related unit — of roughly 60–100 people. The TMP (Teotihuacan Mapping Project) identified approximately 2,300 such compounds across the city. Most of the city's population of 70,000–100,000 lived in these structures; insubstantial structures housed perhaps 15 percent of people outside the compound system (Robertson 2008, cited in Cowgill 2015, Ch. 10).

## Emergence: Urban Renewal in the Tlamimilolpa Phase

Multi-apartment residential compounds began appearing in the preceding period but underwent massive construction and renovation during the Tlamimilolpa phase (roughly 200–275 CE): "far more effort than before went into continued construction and renovation of the multi-apartment residential compounds" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 7). This represents a deliberate, probably state-directed reorganization of residential space — a kind of urban renewal — in which the city's population was rehoused in standardized walled compounds replacing earlier, more varied structures.

The scale of this investment in residential construction is significant: Tatsuya Murakami (2010) documents changing scales of civic-ceremonial and residential building over time, and the shift toward residential investment suggests a changed relationship between state and population — possibly a "corporate" political turn after the desecration of the Feathered Serpent Pyramid around 300–350 CE.

## Compound Structure

Each compound was enclosed by exterior walls and contained multiple apartments arranged around internal open courts. The freestanding walls enclosed both living and working spaces. Doorways were probably closed with textile hangings rather than solid doors, as no hinged door hardware has been identified; stone loops in doorjambs served as attachment points. Compounds lacked locks, with surveillance and possibly supernatural sanctions deterring intrusion.

The civic-ceremonial core of Teotihuacan was itself enclosed by freestanding walls impeding access from the rest of the city, suggesting that the compound enclosure system replicated at a micro-scale the boundaries of larger urban districts.

## Craft Specialization by Compound

A defining feature of the apartment compound system was its integration with specialized craft production. Different compounds concentrated on different crafts:

- **San Martín Orange Ware** was concentrated in the Tlajinga district (compounds 33:S3W1 and surrounding area), where potters worked at a household or local level independent of the state.
- **Obsidian blade production** was concentrated in "precinct" workshops near the [[moon-pyramid]] and in residential compounds throughout the city.
- **Lapidary work** in greenstone and shell was concentrated at Tecopac (N3E5) and at La Ventilla.
- **Figurines and censer ornaments** were produced at Cosotlán 23 (N5W3).
- **Ceramic outcurving bowls** were produced at San José 520 (S2E2 periphery).

Spence (1987) estimated large numbers of individuals engaged in obsidian crafting in each compound, but Cowgill argues that probably only one or two workers at any one time could have produced the observed amounts of debris, suggesting smaller-scale production within a larger residential context.

## Ethnic Enclaves

Several compounds or compound clusters housed populations with distinct ethnic or regional affiliations, evidenced by non-local ceramics, foreign burial practices, and architectural differences:

- The **Oaxacan (Zapotec) Enclave** is the best-documented, with Monte Albán-style urn burials and other Zapotec material culture markers.
- A **Merchants' Enclave** (probably Lowland Maya) is identified by concentrations of Maya-style ceramics.
- Compound 19:N1W5, about 150 m east of the Oaxacan Enclave, had northern residents with Michoacán ties and southern residents with Oaxacan connections, demonstrating micro-scale ethnic differentiation within a single compound (Gómez 2002; Gómez and Gazzola 2007).

No walls specifically enclosing ethnic enclaves have been detected, suggesting that ethnic identity was maintained through material and social practice rather than physical boundary enforcement.

## Corporate Group Identity

The compound appears to have functioned as the basic social unit of Teotihuacan urban life. Evidence for compound-level corporate identity includes:

**Burial practices**: Martha Sempowski's studies show compound-specific patterns in grave offerings that differed significantly across compounds and changed over time within compounds — a pattern better explained by compound-level corporate practices than by individual or city-wide norms (Sempowski and Spence 1994; Clayton 2009, 2011).

**Mural iconography**: Elaborately costumed processions in compound murals share a generic resemblance city-wide but differ in specific regalia in every compound. "It may be that many depict rituals whose right (and duty) to perform was 'owned' by occupants of the compound. Perhaps occupants of a compound had a monopoly on certain sacred knowledge" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 8). If two compounds ever share specific iconography, it would suggest cross-cutting sodalities.

**Ritual objects**: The Old God (Huehueteotl) brazier, found across the full socioeconomic range of compounds, appears to have been used in compound-wide domestic observances — a standardized form of corporate religious practice.

## Variation in Compound Status and Wealth

Compounds varied considerably in quality and wealth. High-status compounds such as the Viking Group (N3E1), part of the Avenue of the Dead Complex, contained remarkable deposits of raw materials (35 kg of mica under one floor). At the lower end, the potters of Tlajinga 33 lived in a compound with no wall paintings beyond red borders and with evidence of child mortality and health stress.

Ian Robertson's neighborhood analyses found markers of wealth inequality increasing across time from the Miccaotli through Xolalpan phases, with divergence accelerating in the terminal period. Sempowski's data suggest that late in the city's history "these compounds increasingly differed from one another in the wealth deposited in burials. In some, although not all, grave offerings were markedly impoverished relative to those of the Xolalpan phase" (Ch. 9). This growing differentiation may have contributed to the social tensions preceding collapse.

## Scale variation and social unit (Cowgill CHNPA 2000)

Cowgill stresses that the distinctive apartment compound became very widespread and
eventually housed nearly the entire city. Size was highly variable: roughly one-quarter of
residential space under 2,000 m², one-quarter over 4,500 m², extremes below 1,200 and above
6,400 m². Most compounds housed several extended families; many probably held sixty or more
individuals. Occupants of a compound probably formed a social unit **above the extended
family/household level but below the barrio level** — a distinctive factor in Teotihuacan
social constitution (Millon). Concrete-surfaced compounds begin by ~A.D. 200 and become
typical by ~A.D. 300; they are absent from the Patlachique–Tzacualli city of adobe/perishable
residences.

## End Conditions

The apartment compound system collapsed with the Teotihuacan state around 550–650 CE (date
range disputed; see [[teotihuacan-collapse-550ce]]). Signs of rapid abandonment in La Ventilla — artisans' tools and unfinished objects left in place — suggest the process was abrupt rather than gradual. San Martín Orange Ware ceased production: market conditions no longer supported its degree of specialization. Candeleros and composite censers disappeared. Post-destruction material culture shows the **apartment-compound residential type itself disappears** along with talud-tablero temple style (Cowgill 2000). The Coyotlatelco occupants who reused the city's residential areas did not reproduce the apartment compound system in its Teotihuacan form.

## Evans & Webster 2001 (Abrams; Feinman; Gonlin & Evans)

[[evans-webster-archaeology-ancient-mexico-central-america-2001|Evans & Webster (eds.) 2001]]
adds quantitative dwelling metrics and craft-locus precision (**EW2001**).

**Elliot M. Abrams (Architecture: Vernacular-Mundane):** At Teotihuacan (~A.D. 600) the
commoner dwelling was the apartment compound: rectilinear buildings averaging **~50–60 m per
side**, housing **30–100 individuals** (up to ~20 patrilineally related families); built on
prepared ground (not elevated platform); exterior walls cut/uncut stone + adobe upper;
beam-and-mortar roofs with open courtyards; **~2,000 compounds** closely spaced by alleys;
elite “palaces” similar form but higher quality; compounds grew incrementally over lineage
occupation.

**Feinman / Gonlin & Evans:** Most firmly provenienced specialized craft production took place
**within apartment compounds** (multi-family residential complexes) rather than non-domestic
workshops or factories; compounds housed **cognatic kin corporate groups** for craft
production, group ritual, and external ties, with individual family suites for reproduction,
consumption, and household ritual. Oztoyahualco soil chemistry defined food prep/consumption
and patio ritual areas suggesting three families (Widmer). Ethnic enclaves (Tlailotlacan;
Merchants’ barrio) sit within this compound fabric (Chance & Stark). Aligns with Cowgill CHNPA
scale-variation notes above; Abrams’s 30–100 range and ~2,000 compounds match Millon/TMP
orders of magnitude.
