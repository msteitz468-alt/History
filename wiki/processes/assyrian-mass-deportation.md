# Assyrian Mass Deportation

**Systematic mass deportation** — the uprooting of whole populations and their
forced resettlement in distant provinces — was the **signature instrument of
[[neo-assyrian-empire|Neo-Assyrian]] imperial control**, alongside
[[assyrian-civilization#Warfare and calculated frightfulness|"calculated
frightfulness."]] More than any other policy it reshaped the ethnic and linguistic
map of the ancient Near East. [[boardman-cah-v3p2-1991|CAH Vol. III, Part 2]]
(Chs. 21–22 & 26, Brinkman & Grayson) treats it.

## Definition and Scope

Earlier Assyrian kings (from **Tiglath-pileser I**, c. 1100) had moved conquered
people, but **mainly one-way — *to* Assyria — as labour**. The decisive innovation
was **[[tiglath-pileser-iii|Tiglath-pileser III's]] (744–727)** practice of
**two-way exchange**: systematically swapping whole population groups between
regions to sever rebels from their homeland and replace them with strangers who had
no local roots to rebel for. It became standard Sargonid policy. The scale was
enormous — by official (if tendentious) Assyrian count, **almost half a million
people were deported from Babylonia alone between 745 and 702**, over half of them
[[chaldeans|Chaldaeans]]; the [[nineveh|Neo-Assyrian capitals]] grew into
**cosmopolitan, multiethnic cities** (Egyptians, Medes, Luwians, Arameans attested
as house-owners) chiefly through deportation.

## Causal Drivers

Grayson identifies the twin motives:
- **Labour supply** — to work great building projects (new palaces and capitals)
  and to bring uncultivated land into production, feeding the swelling non-farming
  population of bureaucrats and soldiers (a need already driving Tiglath-pileser I's
  transfers).
- **Pacification** — to "remove particularly rebellious groups from their home
  territory, thereby depriving them of their effectiveness," and as "an admonitory
  example to other potential rebels." Like terror, it was applied **selectively**.

## Major Episodes

- The **[[fall-of-samaria-722bce|deportation of Samaria]] (722)** — the elite of the
  [[kingdom-of-israel|northern kingdom of Israel]] removed to Mesopotamia and Media,
  the legendary **"ten lost tribes,"** and foreigners settled in their place (the
  tradition behind the Samaritans). The paradigmatic case.
- The repeated **deportations from [[chaldeans|Chaldaean]] Babylonia** under
  [[sargon-ii|Sargon II]] and [[sennacherib|Sennacherib]], which eclipsed the
  Bit-Yakin tribe.
- Resettlements across Syria, Anatolia, and the Zagros that accompanied nearly every
  major conquest.

## Interaction

Deportation worked together with **[[assyrian-civilization|provincial
administration]]** and **terror** as the three pillars of Assyrian control. But it
carried a structural cost that Oates makes central to the
**[[fall-of-nineveh-612bce|fall of Assyria]]**: it "left a growing legacy of
subject peoples, both in metropolitan Assyria and in its garrisons abroad, whose
loyalty in times of weakness the state could not ensure." It also accelerated the
**Aramaization** of Mesopotamia, as deported and resettled Aramaeans spread their
language until it replaced Akkadian as the Near Eastern *lingua franca*.

## End Conditions

The policy ended only with the empire that practised it (609). But its **method was
directly inherited** by the [[neo-babylonian-empire|Neo-Babylonians]]: the
**Babylonian Exile** of Judah ([[fall-of-jerusalem-587bce|587]]) was Assyrian
deportation practice applied by [[nebuchadnezzar-ii|Nebuchadnezzar II]] — with the
difference that the exiles' descendants returned, where Israel's had not.
