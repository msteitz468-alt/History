## Definition and Scope

**Agricultural origins** is the global, multi-millennial process by which human
societies shifted from foraging (hunting, fishing, gathering) to food production
(cultivating domesticated plants and herding domesticated animals). It is the
defining process of the [[neolithic]] and "arguably the most important event in
world history" (Barker & Goucher) — the foundation for population growth,
sedentism, urbanism, states, and ultimately human ecological dominance.

This page covers the *transition as a process*. For the coevolutionary
biological mechanism see [[domestication]]; for the Childean framing see
[[neolithic-revolution]]; for the central explanatory dispute see
[[why-did-agriculture-begin]].

**What it is not.** It is *not* a single event, a single invention, or a
one-way ladder of progress. The dominant 2015 synthesis (this volume) replaces
the old linear model with a picture of **many varied, partial, and sometimes
reversible pathways**.

## Causal Drivers

No single cause operated everywhere; the volume catalogues a spectrum:
- **Climate.** The Pleistocene→Holocene transition (~11,700 BP) and the
  [[younger-dryas]] cold snap stressed forager economies dependent on wild
  cereals, especially in Southwest Asia — the classic "push." But climate "cannot
  have been a simple forcing agent" (Dillehay on Peru): in the Nanchoc valley,
  intensification of cultigens occurred through *human decision-making, risk
  management, and communal resource use*, not climatic compulsion alone.
- **Population dynamics.** Population pressure as a push factor (Boserup-style);
  also farming-driven population growth as a *consequence* that then fed forward.
- **Social and ideological "pull."** More recent thinking favours *internal*
  drivers — social competition, feasting, the prestige and ritual value of new
  foods (cereals for beer as much as bread), and shifts in belief systems.
- **Pre-existing intensification.** "Pre-domestication cultivation" — managing
  still-wild plants — was already underway among late-glacial foragers (Natufian
  SW Asia; early Holocene China), so the "transition" often had deep roots.

## Major Phases / Patterns

- **Pre-domestication cultivation** (late Pleistocene–early Holocene): tending
  and harvesting wild stands; sedentary or semi-sedentary foragers (Ohalo II,
  ~19,000 BP; Natufian villages).
- **Morphological domestication**: selection (conscious or not) for traits —
  non-shattering rachis, larger seeds, loss of natural dispersal — that mark
  plants/animals as domesticated. Detectable archaeobotanically/zoologically.
- **Commitment to farming**: domesticates become dietary *staples* and reorganize
  settlement and labour. Often centuries after first domestication (e.g. rice
  only displaced acorns/water-chestnuts as a Yangtze staple by ~3000 BCE).
- **Diffusion / "food globalization"**: domesticates and techniques spread far
  from their hearths, sometimes faster than the people (banana phytoliths reach
  Sri Lanka and West Africa "at bafflingly early dates"; millet crosses the
  steppe from China to Europe). Spread occurred by **both** farmer migration
  (demic) **and** adoption by indigenous foragers (cultural), in region-specific
  mixes.

## Geographic Spread

Independent primary hearths (each detailed in later cycles): **Southwest Asia**,
**China** (north millet / Yangtze rice), **South Asia**, **sub-Saharan Africa**
(Sahel), **Mesoamerica**, **the Andes**, **eastern North America**, and **New
Guinea**. From these, agriculture radiated into neighbouring regions over
thousands of years, reaching temperate Europe (~6000 BCE), Japan (~900 BCE,
Yayoi), island Southeast Asia and the Pacific (Austronesian dispersal), and
southern Africa (~2,000 years ago). Sequence varied: in most regions plant
cultivation preceded herding, but **in Africa animal domestication and
pastoralism usually came first**.

## Interaction

- Drives and is driven by [[sedentism]] and demographic growth.
- Generates [[domestication]] (its biological core) and, downstream, the
  [[secondary-products-revolution]] *(Cycle 2)*.
- Sets up [[agriculture-and-urbanism|urbanism]] and emergent social inequality.
- Closely entangled with [[pastoralism]] *(Cycle 2)* and with language spread
  (see [[archaeogenetics]] and agricultural linguistics, Cycle 2).
- Continues the [[paleolithic-global-dispersal]] of *Homo sapiens* into a new,
  anthropogenic-landscape phase.

## End Conditions

The process "ends" only in the sense that, region by region, food production
became the dominant subsistence base and the porous forager–farmer frontier
closed. Foraging persisted (and persists) at the margins. Globally the process
transitions seamlessly into the intensification, metallurgy, and urbanism of the
[[chalcolithic]] and [[early-bronze-age]].

## Historiography note

The reframing of agricultural origins from a single "Neolithic Revolution" to a
plural, contingent, non-linear process is the central historiographical
achievement of this volume — see [[neolithic-revolution]] for the conceptual
history and [[why-did-agriculture-begin]] for the live debate.

[[diamond-guns-germs-and-steel-1997|Diamond]] turns this process into the *ultimate*
cause of the modern world's inequalities: because only a few regions held rich suites
of domesticable plants and large mammals (the [[continental-axes|"Ancient Fourteen"]],
thirteen of them Eurasian), food production arose earliest and most productively in
Eurasia, and its package then spread fastest along the continent's east–west axis —
yielding the surpluses, dense populations, germs, writing, and states that decided later
collisions such as [[collision-at-cajamarca-1532|Cajamarca]]. See [[guns-germs-and-steel]],
[[continental-axes]], and [[geographic-determinism]].
