# The Iron Age Economy of the Western Mediterranean

## Definition and Scope

Michael Dietler's chapter in the *Cambridge Economic History of the Greco-Roman World*
([[sources/scheidel-cambridge-economic-history-greco-roman-world-2007]]) covers the
western Mediterranean coastal zone — from southern Spain through southeastern France
(North Africa excluded) — from the 8th century BCE to the Roman conquest (~2nd century
BCE). Three indigenous linguistic groups occupied the zone: **Iberian** (non-Indo-European,
script derived from the Phoenician alphabet, still largely undeciphered), **Celtic**
(Indo-European), and **Ligurian** (uncertain affiliation, known only via toponyms).
Dietler explicitly flags the textual record as strongly "Hellenocentric" — nearly all
surviving textual evidence is Greek — and argues Greek economic activity here must be
read within a broader social landscape where Greeks were often marginal: "as much
transformed as they were agents of transformation."

## Causal Drivers

- **Phoenician colonization for metal resources.** Beginning c. 800 BCE (possibly as
  early as 900 BCE, per secure radiocarbon dates at Morro de Mezquitilla), Phoenician
  settlements were sited at river mouths near silver, gold, copper, tin, lead, and iron —
  Cadiz/Gades, Toscanos, Morro de Mezquitilla. The late 7th century BCE marked the apex of
  Phoenician trade expansion; many settlements declined or were "indigenized" by the early
  6th century BCE, linked to the fall of Tyre to the Assyrians.
- **Etruscan trade networks (not colonization).** Etruscan traders — never colonists —
  were the first alien economic agents in Mediterranean France, identified via wine
  amphoras and bucchero nero pottery from the late 7th century BCE, concentrated within
  ~30 km of the coast, densest in the lower Rhône basin.
- **Phocaean Greek colonial foundation.** Massalia (Marseille), founded c. 600 BCE by
  Phocaean Greeks, was the first permanent colonial settlement in southern France and the
  entire western Mediterranean coast up to the Spanish Phoenician sites.

## Major Phases

1. **Phoenician expansion, c. 800–600 BCE.** Small (mostly <1–4 ha) coastal settlements
   dedicated to metal extraction/processing; Sa Caleta (Ibiza, ~4 ha) and Toscanos (near
   Malaga, founded ~740–730 BCE, growing to 12–15 ha, population 1,000–1,500, with a large
   two-story warehouse c. 700 BCE) are the best-documented sites.
2. **Carthaginian takeover, 6th–5th c. BCE.** Carthage, itself a former Phoenician colony,
   took over southern Spain economically and politically, founding or absorbing Phoenician
   sites; Barcid expansion (late 3rd c. BCE) founded Cartago Nova (229 BCE) and extended
   control into interior southern Spain, before annexation by Rome after the Second Punic
   War.
3. **Massaliote growth, c. 600–300 BCE.** Massalia grew from ~12 ha (first generation) to
   ~30 ha (mid-6th c.) to ~40 ha (late 6th c., ramparted) to a maximum ~50 ha (late 4th–2nd
   c. BCE), with an estimated population of 15,000–20,000 at its height — far larger than
   any other western Mediterranean settlement, colonial or indigenous, until Roman times.
   Its small agricultural hinterland (chora), confined to roughly a 10 km radius until the
   late 3rd century BCE, meant it depended on trade — especially with Languedoc and
   Catalonia — for grain; Strabo (4.1.5) describes Massaliote land as suited to vines and
   olives but "too poor for grain."
4. **Secondary colonization and indigenous adaptation.** Massaliote secondary colonies
   (Agathe/Agde, Olbia, Antipolis, Nikaia, Tauroeis) and Greek Spanish sites (Emporion,
   Rhode, Mainake, Hemeroskopeion, Alonis) remained small trading posts without political
   hegemony over their hinterlands; indigenous Iberian wine production is attested as
   early as the 7th century BCE (Alt de Benimaquia, Denia), while French wine production
   remained essentially limited to Massalia until after the Roman conquest.

## Geographic Spread

Southern Spain and Ibiza (Phoenician then Carthaginian), the Catalan coast and Languedoc
(Massaliote/Greek and indigenous Iberian), and the lower Rhône basin (Etruscan trade
penetration). Grain-storage evidence — dense concentrations of underground pit-granaries,
including over 30 "fields of silos" within 15 km of Mas Castellar at Pontós near
Emporion — indicates significant indigenous surplus production for export to the Greek
colonies, especially in the 5th–3rd centuries BCE.

## Interaction

- **Diet and consumption goods.** Cereal staples and ovicaprid/cattle/pig meat were
  broadly similar across colonists and indigenous societies, but wine and olive oil as
  *processed* foodstuffs were introduced by Greeks, Etruscans, and Phoenicians; indigenous
  societies quickly adopted wine into existing feasting culture while showing long
  resistance to olive oil (especially in France).
- **Coinage.** An eastern Mediterranean (Ionian Greek/Lydian) innovation of the late 7th
  century BCE; Massalia was the first western Mediterranean minting authority (last
  quarter of the 6th century BCE), followed by Emporion and Rhode. Phoenician cities and
  Carthage did not adopt coinage until after the mid-5th century BCE, and Punic Spanish
  colonies not until the late 3rd century BCE — coinage functioned mainly for state
  payments and symbolic authority rather than everyday trade until the 1st century BCE;
  most Iron Age exchange in the region was conducted by barter.
- **Mixed, non-monopolistic trade networks.** Shipwreck evidence (nearly 70 wrecks) shows
  small, heterogeneous cargoes (e.g. the El Sec wreck off Majorca, 4th c. BCE, carrying 29
  amphora types from Corinth, Greco-Italic, Samos, Chios, Punic, and Ibiza sources) —
  evidence against any single-polity trade monopoly, including the "Emporitan monopoly"
  over Iberian trade that Dietler calls implausible/anachronistic. The Pech Maho lead
  tablet (c. 475–450 BCE) records a Greek-language ship-purchase contract at Emporion
  witnessed by people with Iberian names, with an Etruscan-language commercial record on
  its reverse — direct evidence of ethnically mixed maritime trading networks.
- **Historiographical critique.** Dietler explicitly rejects the older "Hellenization"
  framework — which treated native adoption of Greek goods/culture as natural and
  inevitable, rooted in post-Renaissance European Hellenophilia — and mechanistic
  world-systems models, arguing instead for indigenous consumption as an active,
  selective, socially situated process. This is a live historiographical debate rather
  than settled fact.

## End Conditions

The distinctively mixed, multi-agent Iron Age trading system of the western Mediterranean
ended with the region's absorption into Roman control following the Second Punic War
(southern Spain) and the later Roman conquest of Gaul, which subordinated the surviving
Massaliote and indigenous networks to the Roman imperial economy.
