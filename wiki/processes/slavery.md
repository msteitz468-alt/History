# Slavery (Ancient and Premodern)

## Definition and Scope

**Slavery** was a near-universal institution across the complex societies of 1200
BCE–900 CE — "it is hard to find any ancient civilizations in which some slavery did
not exist" ([[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|Hunt, CWH IV, Ch 4]]). This page treats slavery
as a long-duration institution and its relationship to the volume's master themes of
[[state-formation|states]], [[empire|empires]], and [[trade-network-formation|networks]].

The traditional definition — slaves as **people treated as property** — is useful
but imperfect (property rights over persons exist in non-slave relations, e.g.
bride-price; and slaves are never treated *consistently* as property, since law
holds them responsible for crimes). Hunt adopts **Orlando Patterson's** more
precise definition: slavery is *"the permanent, violent domination of natally
alienated and generally dishonored persons."* The decisive criterion is **natal
alienation** / "social death": a slave has none of the birth-rights — family, clan,
village, polity — that would otherwise limit a master's control. This is what
distinguishes a slave from a serf (tied to land, oppressed, but possessing
acknowledged family and not saleable apart from the land).

## "Slave Societies" vs. "Societies with Slaves"

A key analytical distinction:
- **Societies with slaves** — slavery exists but is one subordinate status among
  many; this describes *almost all* ancient complex societies (New Kingdom Egypt,
  Neo-Assyria, Neo-Babylonia, Han China, [[silla|Silla]] Korea, Visigothic Spain,
  Abbasid Iraq, India, Judaea).
- **Slave societies** — slaves form a large share of the population (sometimes
  >30%) and dominate the central productive sector (agriculture). Of the canonical
  five in world history, **two fall in this period: classical Athens (c. 500–300
  BCE) and Roman Italy/Sicily (c. 200 BCE–200 CE)**. Roman slavery, at a
  conservative ~6 million slaves, may have rivaled 19th-century New World slavery in
  scale.

A rule of thumb (Hunt): the *extent* of slavery mattered more to a society's overall
social structure and culture than to the *individual* slave's experience — a mine
slave's or house-slave's life was similar in Athens, Carthage, Han Chang'an, or
Rome. But slave societies bred distinctive features: extreme social distance and
terror-based control (the Roman *Senatus Consultum Silanianum* executed all of a
household's slaves if one killed the master), and an ideology in which the
slave/free dichotomy became the pivot of the whole worldview (most starkly in
democratic Athens, where citizen equality was stressed precisely as the free
inverted the enslaved).

## The Uses of Slaves

Ancient slavery was a flexible labor system spanning every kind of work:
- **Domestic and display** — ubiquitous even in low-slavery societies; cooking,
  cleaning, wet-nursing, accounts; large retinues as conspicuous status display.
- **Sexual** — a ubiquitous subset; concubines, prostitutes, the routine vulnerability
  of all slave women to coerced sex. Some legal systems (early and medieval Islamic
  law) required the freeing of a slave woman who bore her acknowledged master's child.
- **Craft and mining** — in developed urban economies (Neo-Babylonia, Athens, Rome);
  the Athenian silver mines at Laurion employed 10,000+ slaves.
- **Agricultural** — the mark of a true slave society (Roman *villa* estates run by a
  slave *vilicus*).
- **Military and administrative** — paradoxical but well-attested uses of the
  "socially dead": deracinated slaves with no elite kin-ties made trustworthy
  instruments of rulers fearful of their own nobles — Abbasid Turkic slave soldiers
  (the antecedents of the *mamluk* system), eunuch administrators (Rome, Byzantium,
  China), the slave-secretaries of democratic Athens, and the *familia caesaris* of
  the early Roman Empire.

## Causal Drivers: Cities, Trade, Empires, and States

Hunt's central argument inverts the New World image of slavery as primitive: ancient
slavery grew from **economic and social dynamism**, not backwardness.
- **[[urbanization|Cities]]** concentrated the markets and craft demand that created
  niches for slave labor; the countryside, organized by older relations of
  dependence, used chattel slaves far less.
- **[[trade-network-formation|Trade]]** both supplied slaves and made them
  controllable — the key was **displacement**. Captives transported and dispersed far
  from home, among unfamiliar peoples, became governable; hence the advice in Greek
  and Roman texts not to buy too many slaves from one place. Notorious slave routes
  (Mediterranean, Indian Ocean, trans-Saharan — the last long predating Islam) all
  exploited the way **water and desert separate**: barriers a fugitive lacked the
  resources to cross.
- **[[empire|Empires]]** supplied slaves through conquest (a single campaign in
  western Greece reportedly yielded 150,000) and — as the Roman case shows — through
  the *economic disruption* of conquest, which urbanized Italy, grew markets, and
  emptied the countryside of peasant-soldiers, making slave estates attractive. Rome
  became a slave society as much from opportunism and disruption as from raw capture.
- **Strong-state/weak-periphery interaction** generated slaves at the edges: cheap
  Athenian slaves flowed from raids and sales among the less-organized inland peoples
  ringing the Greek coastal cities; conversely, nomadic Saracens raided their larger
  sedentary neighbors.

## States and Slaves

Slavery and the [[state-formation|state]] were symbiotic. Large concentrations of
slaves *required* the state's monopoly of violence (the earliest law code, Sumer
c. 2100 BCE, already legislates fugitive slaves). The state was also a major
**enslaver** through its two core powers — **war** (captives auctioned or set to
state labor) and **punishment** (Roman *damnatio ad metallum*; Han penal slavery,
so pervasive that the common Chinese word for slave derived from "child," and
private slaves were marked like convicts). Control of slaves was a fiscal and
political resource: when the usurper [[wang-mang|Wang Mang]] tried to ban slavery
(7 CE) his aristocracy forced him to retreat — revealing both elite dependence on
slave estates and the emperor's interest in curbing a rival power base.

## Enslavement and Manumission

The principal sources of slaves were **foreign capture** (war and raiding),
**birth** into slavery, and — more rarely — enslavement within one's own society
(penal slavery; sale of children under extreme poverty; debt). Racialized
justification (as in the later Atlantic system) was an extreme form of the common
ancient assumption that slaves were *outsiders* without rights — "southern
barbarians" in China, "barbarians" generally in Greece. **Manumission** was never
at the slave's discretion, but ex-slave classes (Roman *liberti*, manumitted
Abbasid soldiers) could be socially significant.

## Interaction and End Conditions

Slavery is entangled with [[empire]] (conquest as supply), [[trade-network-formation]]
(transport as control), [[urbanization]] (markets as demand), and [[state-formation]]
(coercion as precondition). The institution did not end within the period — it
persisted, transformed, into the medieval Mediterranean and Islamic worlds and
beyond. Its scale and centrality fluctuated with the rise and fall of the slave
societies (Athens, Rome) whose collapse diminished, without abolishing, chattel
agriculture.

## Historiography

Hunt foregrounds the **evidentiary asymmetry**: nearly all sources were produced by
elite male slaveholders, so the slave's viewpoint is almost absent and reliable
numbers are scarce; and we know far more about importing states than the raided
peripheries that supplied them. The old **Marxist "slave mode of production"**
reading of Egypt, Babylonia, and China is now rejected — dependent peasants, not
chattel slaves, dominated those countrysides. The comparative, global, thematically
organized approach (drawing on Patterson's *Slavery and Social Death* and the
*Cambridge World History of Slavery*) is the current standard, deliberately setting
the canonical Greco-Roman cases against African, Chinese, Near Eastern, and Islamic
systems.
