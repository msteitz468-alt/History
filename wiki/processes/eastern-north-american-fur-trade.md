# Eastern North American Fur Trade

## Definition and Scope

The commercial and political system linking Eastern Woodlands and Great Lakes Native hunters and middlemen to European markets for beaver, fancy furs, hides, and (in the Southeast) deerskins and Indian slaves—a central driver of seventeenth–eighteenth-century alliance, war, and material culture change. Primary CHNPA anchors: Trigger & Swagerty (sixteenth-century takeoff) and Salisbury ch. 7 (1600–1783 interior system).

## Causal Drivers

- European hat/felt markets (beaver felt boom last two decades of the 16th c.); earlier demand for fancy furs and wild hides (deer, moose, seal) for status and leather
- Native control of hunting grounds, northern thick-pelt sources, and trade routes (Montagnais at Tadoussac; later Huron–Ottawa northern networks)
- Competition among French, Dutch, English (and residual Spanish southeastern spheres)
- Wampum as commercialized “words” of peace/condolence linking southern New England producers to [[haudenosaunee-confederacy]] distribution (Salisbury)

## Major Phases

### Sixteenth-century origins (Trigger & Swagerty)

- **Fisheries sideline → professional trade:** Dry fishery and Basque whaling brought thousands of Europeans onto NE shores (perhaps ~20,000 by 1580) in contact with Beothuks, Montagnais (Innu), [[micmac-mikmaq]]. Skin trade almost from the start, often clandestine to avoid taxes.
- **Volume debate:** Beaver trade important mainly last two decades of the century; fancy furs/hides earlier and considerable. Beaver hunting calorically efficient (~7:1) vs fancy furs; “coat beaver” (15–18 months body-wear) prized for felt.
- **Routine trade by late 1530s** along southern Labrador; Verrazzano 1524 found Maine Indians already with ship-trade protocol; Micmacs at Chaleur Bay held skins on sticks; Cartier-era exchanges.
- **Tadoussac focus 1580s:** Professional Breton/St. Malo fleets; fourteen-fold profits reported; by century’s end Tadoussac replaced Strait of Belle Isle as major source of European goods north/west of the St. Lawrence; Montagnais redistributed furs from James Bay to Lake Huron. Mid-Atlantic remained sparse (Virginia iron axe still a rich present in 1607; goods flowed via St. Lawrence–Great Lakes rather than direct coastal Virginia trade).

### 1600–1660 — Permanent posts and arms race (Salisbury)

- **Quebec 1608; Huron linchpin:** After Champlain’s 1609–10 Mohawk routs and 1615 Huron alliance, [[huron-wendat]] supplied beaver from Nipissings, Neutrals, Petuns, Ottawas for metal, glass, cloth, and protection from Iroquois aggression.
- **Dutch–Mohawk axis:** Fort Orange (1624); by 1630 [[mohawk]] defeated Mahicans for free Dutch access including guns for other Iroquois—unrivaled manufactures/wampum stimulated trapping until Iroquois territories depleted.
- **Structural contrast feeding [[beaver-wars-17th-century]]:** Hurons had Indian allies with northern pelts; Iroquois had European allies who would provide guns. Overhunting sped interior expansion; midcentury upheavals as severe as coastal.
- **Huron epidemics 1630s** cut population of 21,000 in half; Jesuit condition of alliance; Christian trader privileges sowed faction—survivors more trade-dependent even as numbers fell.
- **Wampum circuit:** Narragansett and Pequot production; Dutch commercialization; Mohawk distribution across Five Nations for Beaver Wars.

### 1660–1701 — French western alliance; Grand Settlement

Upper Lakes refugee villages (Chequamegon, Sault, Green Bay, Michilimackinac, Kaskaskia) mixed Central Algonquian and northern Iroquoian peoples with French posts; intermarriage, Feast of the Dead, Midewiwin, calumet rituals facilitated multi-ethnic trade. French Fort Frontenac (1673), Niagara (1676); Iroquois losses 1689–98 catastrophic; by late 1690s French allies sold pelts directly to Albany, bypassing Iroquois middlemen. **Grand Settlement 1701:** French opened Detroit-area hunting/trade for Iroquois neutrality.

### Eighteenth century — Play-off trade and English commercial edge

Fort Pontchartrain (Detroit) and Fort Oswego (1727) undermined Iroquois Great Lakes middleman role. English goods quality/quantity/price advantages drew Huron-Petuns, Miamis, Mississaugas to Albany; Fox Wars disrupted French western alliance. Southeast parallel: deerskin-and-slave complex (Westos, then Creek/Yamasee) rather than beaver alone. Ohio multi-ethnic “republican” trade rebuffed exclusive French, English, or Six Nations control until [[seven-years-war-1756]] and British postwar monopoly.

### Alcohol and dependency

Unregulated English private trade (especially Maine [[abenaki]]) dispensed alcohol with violence; Salisbury’s epilogue lists alcohol among major non-epidemic mortality and fertility-decline causes alongside warfare and land-loss dietary change.

## Geographic Spread

Northeast Maritimes and St. Lawrence → Great Lakes and Ohio Valley; Southeast deerskin/slave circuits from Virginia/Carolina into the interior; later links to Northern Interior and Hudson’s Bay competition (Part 2).

### Extension into the Northern Interior (Ray, CHNPA I.2)

Arthur Ray frames Northern Interior postcontact history as **spatial/temporal extension** of this process: Gulf/St. Lawrence origins → Ottawa–Huron networks → Iroquois-war reconfiguration → [[hudsons-bay-company]] Bay entry 1668–70 dual system with French interior → Assiniboine–[[cree]] westward middleman advance to Athabasca → Nor’Wester/HBC saturation to Mackenzie and Pacific slope headwaters → industrial transport reopening competition → twentieth-century ranch/utility-fur transformation and state conservation. Same dynamics recur: middleman succession, competitive Indian consumer leverage, credit/gift diplomacy, resource-tenure transformation—until monopoly or industrial capital restructures them. Continuity of fur trade (not agricultural dispossession) as master process is what makes Subarctic history exceptional within the continental arc (see [[northern-interior-subarctic-postcontact]]).

### Northwest Coast contrast (Fisher)

Some scholars (e.g. Rotstein-type arguments) hold that Native trade in parts of North America was not profit-motivated but served **ritual alliance-making**, so price was not market-determined. Fisher: Northwest Coast evidence “**overwhelmingly suggests**” fur prices set by **supply, demand, and competition**. Potlatch prestige through giving wealth created incentive to acquire surplus—**profit motive not confined to Europeans**. Meares: Natives “possessed all the cunning necessary to gains of mercantile life.” Coastal HBC posts through the 1840s failed monopoly control; Indians played competing traders against each other. This is a **regional contrast claim**, not a denial that alliance/gift dimensions structured eastern and Subarctic exchange (Ray’s gift + barter MB protocol remains standard for the Bay).

## End Conditions

Regional beaver decline as territories overhunted; settlement agriculture and land cessions shrink Native hunting bases in the agricultural East; trade shifts north and west. After 1763 British monopoly and Amherst’s gift/ammunition cuts transform political economy of exchange into grievance (see [[pontiacs-war-1763]]). In the Subarctic, the process does **not** end with settlement agriculture: 1821 HBC monopoly, 1870 Rupert’s Land transfer, industrial-age competition, and twentieth-century state conservation/welfare reconfigure rather than abolish fur articulation of Native economies (Ray). On the Northwest Coast, Fisher periodizes a sharper **fur trade → settlement** break from the late 1830s–1840s, after which Native determining economic role collapses into wage-labor marginality.
