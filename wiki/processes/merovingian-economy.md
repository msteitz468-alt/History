# The Merovingian Economy

**A bottom-up "ritual economy" driven by rural demand, not elite extraction — the strongest revisionist
case in the Handbook's economic section.** Frans Theuws's chapter (Oxford Handbook, Ch. 38), with Ralph
Patrello's on belt buckles and social bonds (Ch. 39), argues that economic development in post-Roman
northern Gaul was powered by the aggregate demand of the rural population — stimulated by the furnished
burial rite — rather than by the aristocratic demand of the Wickham/Devroey model.

## Definition and Scope

Theuws's claim is that the early medieval exchange system was **"eclectic"** — "not dominated by any single
type (gift, commodity, market) but composed of a series of articulating forms of exchanges" — in which
agents were not role-fixed: "farmers might have acted as long-distance traders, or they could become
warriors and travel large distances," and objects changed character as they moved (a workshop commodity
becoming a gift between women, then a grave good).

## The "Ceremonial Fund" Mechanism

Drawing on Eric Wolf's peasant economics, Theuws argues that beyond subsistence and a "replacement fund,"
rural households produced a **"ceremonial fund"** for social and ritual obligations. In fifth–sixth-century
northern Gaul, new life-cycle rituals — above all the **furnished burial rite** — dramatically expanded
this fund, generating sustained demand for iron, glass, garnet jewellery, and ceramics. Because these goods
were buried and "permanently removed from circulation… the demand for new products was renewed constantly,"
and rural dwellers, lacking heavy rents or taxes, had the disposable resources to meet it. Theuws labels the
system a **"ritual economy"** in which economy and ritual were structurally intertwined.

## Weak Elites, Dense Trade

The model rests on the weakness of elite control in northern Gaul: the Roman tax system had ceased there by
the fifth century; monasteries were scarce before the mid-seventh; and there is an "almost complete absence
of elite residential sites in the archaeological record" — no Merovingian villas (see
[[end-of-the-roman-villa]], [[merovingian-rural-settlement]]). Rich graves (Krefeld-Gellep, Morken) "need
not represent an omnipresent hereditary aristocracy." Yet trade was intense: Theuws cites Wickham's own
admission that northern Gaul's exchange was "second only to the intensity of exchange in the Nile Valley" —
they disagree only about its *source* (aristocratic vs. rural demand). Theuws also rejects the Loveluck–Tys
coastal/inland dichotomy as an artefact of differential preservation, since inland cemeteries yield foreign
goods comparable to the coast (see [[merovingian-emporia]], [[cross-channel-exchange-britain-and-gaul]]).

## Belt Buckles as Social Bonds (Patrello)

Patrello's study of 19 southwestern-Gallic cemeteries finds that **~25% of plate-buckle graves contained
incomplete belt sets**, often deliberately fragmented — fragments deposited in graves while remaining pieces
"circulated among the living." Belt exchange formalized political allegiance (Gregory and
[[venantius-fortunatus|Venantius Fortunatus]] both document it); the *cingulum* became metonymic for office
itself (Clovis bribing Ragnachar's retainers with "gilt belt buckles"; loss of a belt meaning loss of
status). Read through Chapman's fragmentation studies and Weiner's "inalienable objects," the buckle
fragment was a *pars pro toto* keepsake of a bond — evidence that objects circulated "independently of
people whose identity they ostensibly expressed," undercutting ethnicity-based readings (see
[[ethnogenesis]], [[merovingian-archaeology]]).

## End Conditions: Inclusive to Extractive

Across the eighth–ninth centuries the economy was transformed "from bottom-up inclusive to top-down
extractive" (Acemoglu–Robinson terms): the Church absorbed the ceremonial fund by taking control of
life-cycle rituals (baptism, marriage, burial), and Carolingian aristocrats then "skim[med] off the wealth
of the countryside by reorganizing landed property in bipartite estates," taxing the emporia that "had grown
outside their control," and monopolizing coin production (see [[merovingian-coinage]]).

## Wickham, Framing (2005) — aristocratic-demand frame

[[wickham-framing-the-early-middle-ages-2005|Wickham, *Framing* (2005)]] supplies the comparative
aristocratic-demand account that Theuws (above) revises. Both agree that northern Gaul’s exchange
intensity was exceptional (Wickham: second only to the Nile valley among his regions); they disagree on
whether the motor was elite rent and wide landowning or aggregate rural ritual demand. Wickham’s
regional claims for Merovingian Francia:

### Fiscal margin by the 570s

Gregory of Tours shows land tax (*tributum*/*census*) in kind, exacted by counts or central officials
(e.g. *referendarius* Marcus), based on simplified written assessments with Roman roots—most explicit
for Tours, Poitiers, Limoges, Clermont (ex-Visigothic). Assessments were seldom revised; Poitiers 589
required royal *discriptores*. Chilperic’s 579 punitive tax—the only detailed rate figure—was 1 amphora
of wine per *aripennis*; Lot’s calculation of ~10% of the crop is **far below Roman levels**. Tax had
become relatively **marginal by the 570s**, enabling decades between ad hoc reassessments (see
[[tax-based-vs-land-based-states]]).

### Tenant-run estates, not slave plantations

Refining Tits-Dieuaide (1985), Wickham finds even less evidence than she allowed for anything but free
or unfree **tenants**: *villa cum mancipiis* formulas and Bertram of Le Mans’s will (616) describe
unfree families on *colonicae*/*condomae*, not plantation gangs. Assuming Roman slave-mode survival in
Gaul is a “mirror game,” since plantations are not documented there before the invasions either.
**Vineyards** are the major exception: from Aredius & Pelagia (572) through Remigius, Erminethrudis, and
seventh–eighth-century charters, vineyards are the only land type regularly worked by slave specialists
as mini-demesnes. Wine remained commercial on the northern production margin (Paris–Moselle–Le Mans
access to northern luxury demand)—the main Frankish parallel to sixth-century Oxyrhynchite *autourgiai*,
but small and marginal relative to later bipartite demesnes ([[bipartite-manor]]).

### North outside the Mediterranean system; incomplete fifth-century crisis

From the third century northern Gaul/Germania/Britain lay outside Mediterranean bulk exchange (animal
fats not Spanish oil; Gaulish sigillata). The northern crisis came early (fifth century) but was
**incomplete for exchange**; by the eighth century northern wealth indicators rose while Mediterranean
economies localised. Late Roman **Argonne** fine ware (c.400-km radius, military-buttressed) contracted
in the fifth century but continued to the late sixth; **Mayen** coarse ware (Eifel) held the middle/lower
Rhine ~300 km into the eleventh century—with Aswan, among the longest-lived post-Roman kiln complexes—
and produced the full Frankish range. Fifth-century localisation never threatened professional ceramic
production: northern demand never collapsed in the way it did in parts of Britain or involuted Spanish
coasts ([[end-of-the-roman-villa]], [[aristocratic-demand]]).

### Hierarchical markets and bulk river trade

Merovingian Francia had hierarchical markets (St-Denis fair by the 630s; urban markets; *nundinae*),
[[konigsnahe|Königsnahe]] merchants (Priscus; Verdun’s 7,000-solidi royal loan), and bulk trade in wine,
salt, livestock, cloth, and slaves along the Loire, Seine, Meuse, Moselle, and Rhine—more elaborated by
700 than Mediterranean systems outside the south-east. Marseille prospered as a gateway for luxuries, but
those imports were **marginal to the economy as a whole**—Pirennean texts need archaeological perspective
([[pirenne-thesis]]).

### Southern Gaul ceramics: localisation with residual networks

Southern Gaul imported ARS/African oil (and eastern goods at Marseille) into the seventh century and
produced DSP fine wares in Provençal, Languedocian, and Aquitainian networks; sixth-century fine wares
became less available and less fine (weakening demand, paralleling inland Spain a century earlier). On
the Mediterranean coast this was partly offset by Rhône-valley *céramiques à pâte kaolinitique* common
wares (Béziers–Marseille into the ninth century) and Poitevin **‘E ware’** coarse ware exported to
Britain and Ireland ([[cross-channel-exchange-britain-and-gaul]]). Despite rich, wide-owning aristocracies
(especially Aquitaine), ceramic localisation from the sixth century partially diverges from documentary
wealth—though unambitious common/coarse types still show complex demand, as in northern Gaul where
large-scale exchange is best documented archaeologically in coarse-ware systems correlating with a rich
and wide-owning aristocracy.
