# The Merovingian Economy

**A bottom-up "ritual economy" driven by rural demand, not elite extraction — the strongest revisionist
case in the Handbook's economic section.** Frans Theuws's chapter (Oxford Handbook, Ch. 38), with Ralph
Patrello's on belt buckles and social bonds (Ch. 39), argues that economic development in post-Roman
northern Gaul was powered by the aggregate demand of the rural population — stimulated by the furnished
burial rite — rather than by the aristocratic demand of the Wickham/Devroey model.

## Definition and Scope

Theuws's claim is that the early medieval exchange system was **"eclectic"** — "not dominated by any single
type (gift, commodity, market) but composed of a series of articulating forms of exchanges" — in which
agents were not role-fixed: "farmers might have acted as long-distance traders, or they could become
warriors and travel large distances," and objects changed character as they moved (a workshop commodity
becoming a gift between women, then a grave good).

## The "Ceremonial Fund" Mechanism

Drawing on Eric Wolf's peasant economics, Theuws argues that beyond subsistence and a "replacement fund,"
rural households produced a **"ceremonial fund"** for social and ritual obligations. In fifth–sixth-century
northern Gaul, new life-cycle rituals — above all the **furnished burial rite** — dramatically expanded
this fund, generating sustained demand for iron, glass, garnet jewellery, and ceramics. Because these goods
were buried and "permanently removed from circulation… the demand for new products was renewed constantly,"
and rural dwellers, lacking heavy rents or taxes, had the disposable resources to meet it. Theuws labels the
system a **"ritual economy"** in which economy and ritual were structurally intertwined.

## Weak Elites, Dense Trade

The model rests on the weakness of elite control in northern Gaul: the Roman tax system had ceased there by
the fifth century; monasteries were scarce before the mid-seventh; and there is an "almost complete absence
of elite residential sites in the archaeological record" — no Merovingian villas (see
[[end-of-the-roman-villa]], [[merovingian-rural-settlement]]). Rich graves (Krefeld-Gellep, Morken) "need
not represent an omnipresent hereditary aristocracy." Yet trade was intense: Theuws cites Wickham's own
admission that northern Gaul's exchange was "second only to the intensity of exchange in the Nile Valley" —
they disagree only about its *source* (aristocratic vs. rural demand). Theuws also rejects the Loveluck–Tys
coastal/inland dichotomy as an artefact of differential preservation, since inland cemeteries yield foreign
goods comparable to the coast (see [[merovingian-emporia]], [[cross-channel-exchange-britain-and-gaul]]).

## Belt Buckles as Social Bonds (Patrello)

Patrello's study of 19 southwestern-Gallic cemeteries finds that **~25% of plate-buckle graves contained
incomplete belt sets**, often deliberately fragmented — fragments deposited in graves while remaining pieces
"circulated among the living." Belt exchange formalized political allegiance (Gregory and
[[venantius-fortunatus|Venantius Fortunatus]] both document it); the *cingulum* became metonymic for office
itself (Clovis bribing Ragnachar's retainers with "gilt belt buckles"; loss of a belt meaning loss of
status). Read through Chapman's fragmentation studies and Weiner's "inalienable objects," the buckle
fragment was a *pars pro toto* keepsake of a bond — evidence that objects circulated "independently of
people whose identity they ostensibly expressed," undercutting ethnicity-based readings (see
[[ethnogenesis]], [[merovingian-archaeology]]).

## End Conditions: Inclusive to Extractive

Across the eighth–ninth centuries the economy was transformed "from bottom-up inclusive to top-down
extractive" (Acemoglu–Robinson terms): the Church absorbed the ceremonial fund by taking control of
life-cycle rituals (baptism, marriage, burial), and Carolingian aristocrats then "skim[med] off the wealth
of the countryside by reorganizing landed property in bipartite estates," taxing the emporia that "had grown
outside their control," and monopolizing coin production (see [[merovingian-coinage]]).
