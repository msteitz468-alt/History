# Rise of Rome (Polybian Theme)

The process by which Rome, already master of Italy, extended its power
over the entire Mediterranean world within roughly a century — the
subject the Greek historian [[polybius|Polybius]] set out explicitly to
explain: "by what means and under what system of polity" Rome achieved
dominion over "almost the whole inhabited world" in under fifty-three
years.

## Definition and Scope

This process picks up where [[roman-expansion-italy|Rome's unification of
the Italian peninsula]] left off (by 264 BCE) and traces Rome's subsequent
expansion through the [[first-punic-war-264-241bce|First]],
[[second-punic-war-218-201bce|Second]], and
[[third-punic-war-149-146bce|Third Punic Wars]] against Carthage and the
[[first-macedonian-war-214-205bce|First]],
[[second-macedonian-war-200-196bce|Second]], and
[[third-macedonian-war-171-168bce|Third Macedonian Wars]] against the
Hellenistic kingdoms — the sequence of conflicts Polybius treated as a
single interconnected phenomenon rather than isolated wars.

## Causal Drivers

Rome's alliance system in Italy provided an exceptional depth of military
manpower unmatched by any Mediterranean rival; repeated victories over
Carthage removed its principal naval and commercial competitor, while
victories over Macedon and the Seleucid Empire exploited the chronic
disunity among the Hellenistic Successor kingdoms.

## Major Phases

The Punic Wars (264–146 BCE) eliminated Carthage as a great power; the
Macedonian and Syrian Wars (214–188 BCE, continuing through 168 BCE)
established Roman hegemony over the Greek world without immediate formal
annexation; direct provincial annexation followed more gradually in the
following century.

## Geographic Spread

The process extended Roman power from Italy across Sicily, Spain, North
Africa, and the entire eastern Mediterranean littoral within a century.

## Interaction

Polybius framed this expansion as historically unprecedented in speed and
scale, distinguishing it from earlier, more gradual imperial formations; the
process directly set the stage for the political strains (provincial
governance, the wealth of conquest, and army loyalty) that later
contributed to the collapse of the Roman Republic.

## End Conditions

Polybius's own account concludes around 167–146 BCE, by which point Roman
supremacy over the Mediterranean was effectively unchallenged, even where
formal annexation of some eastern territories was not completed until later
generations.
