# Formation of the Doctrinal Legal Schools (Madhhab Formation)

Part of [[formative-period-islamic-law]]. Source: [[hallaq-origins-evolution-islamic-law-2005]], chapters 7–8 and Conclusion.

## Definition and Scope

The madhhab-formation process describes the centuries-long transformation by which loose scholarly circles coalesced into personal schools and finally into the four enduring doctrinal legal schools (madhhabs) of Sunni Islam: Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi'i, and Hanbali. It is not the same process as the formation of Islamic law itself — the [[formative-period-islamic-law]] produced the substantive law; the madhhab-formation produced the institutional and doctrinal vehicle through which that law would be transmitted, elaborated, and applied for the next millennium.

Hallaq distinguishes four historical meanings of the term *madhhab*: (1) a jurist's individual opinion; (2) a principle underlying a cluster of derivative cases; (3) the most authoritative opinion within a school's collective corpus (*al-madhhab* with the definite article); and (4) a community of jurists bound by loyalty to a cumulative collective doctrine attributed to an eponym. The madhhab-formation process is the history of how meaning (1) became meaning (4).

## Causal Drivers

**Epistemic authority of private jurists.** From the very start, legal authority in Islam resided in the persons of learned individuals, not in the state. Because the caliphate never appropriated law-making, legal governance had to be anchored elsewhere. Scholarly circles were the embryo of this alternative anchor. See [[law-and-politics-formative]] and [[epistemic-authority-islamic-law]].

**The need for a unifying axis of authority.** The multiplicity of personal schools — dozens of jurists, each with followers, in every major city — produced doctrinal fragmentation. Doctrinal schools solved this by constructing a single founding figure (the *imam*, the absolute *mujtahid*) around whose methodology an entire cumulative tradition could be organized. This is the process Hallaq calls "authority construction."

**The Great Synthesis.** No personal school could survive unless it achieved a workable synthesis between rationalist (*ahl al-ra'y*) and traditionalist (*ahl al-hadith*) approaches to deriving law. Schools that failed this synthesis (notably the [[zahirism|Zahirites]]) could not attract sufficient juristic talent or popular acceptance. See [[great-synthesis-islamic-law]].

**Political patronage.** Ruling dynasties needed the jurists to legitimate their rule; jurists needed financial patronage. Schools that secured political backing flourished; those that did not withered. The Hanafis were backed by the [[abbasid-caliphate]] in Iraq and eastern provinces. The Malikis were adopted by the Spanish Umayyads in Andalusia ca. 200/815. The Shafi'is gained strength under the Ayyubids (6th/12th century). The Hanbalis survived in Baghdad largely without formal support but through popular mobilization.

**Theological alignment.** Schools that allied with mainstream or non-sectarian theological movements survived; those that allied with sectarian movements (such as the Mu'tazilites) were damaged when those movements lost political favor. The Hanafis ultimately aligned with the Maturidites; the Shafi'is with the Ash'arites. The [[mihna-833]] accelerated traditionalism and dealt a lasting blow to rationalist-political coalitions.

## Major Phases

### Phase 1: Scholarly Circles (ca. 690–720 CE)
Pious scholars debating Quranic interpretation and emerging legal issues in the mosque-based *halqa*. No methodology, no doctrinal boundaries, no loyalty to a single teacher. Legal authority and political authority were not yet separated.

### Phase 2: Personal Schools (ca. 720–865 CE)
Prominent teachers — Abu Hanifa (d. 150/767), Malik (d. 179/795), Awza'i (d. 157/773), Thawri (d. 161/777), Shafi'i (d. 204/820), Ibn Hanbal (d. 241/855), and many others — attracted followings. Students and judges adopted a leading jurist's doctrine, but without exclusive loyalty; combining doctrines from two or more teachers was common and accepted. These are personal schools: the doctrine is that of a single living (or recently dead) jurist, non-cumulative and non-collective.

### Phase 3: Authority Construction and Doctrinal Consolidation (ca. 865–950 CE)
The pivot from personal to doctrinal school. This involved two linked processes:

**Dissociation upward:** The eponym's doctrine was detached from the doctrines of his teachers, making him appear to have derived law directly from the revealed texts by his own methodology alone. Example from Hallaq: Malik's *Muwatta'* records an opinion transmitted anonymously ("Malik heard that..."); within two generations, Sahnun's *Mudawwana* presents the identical ruling as Malik's own direct opinion.

**Attribution downward (takhrij):** Later jurists' doctrinal constructions were attributed back to the founding eponym. The Hanbalite case is extreme: Ibn Hanbal was primarily a hadith scholar with minimal juristic output; it was Abu Bakr al-Khallal (d. 311/923) who gathered his scattered opinions and built them into a coherent corpus. "To say that Khallal and his associates were the real founders of the Hanbali school is merely to state the obvious" (Hallaq).

Both processes together constituted the construction of the absolute *mujtahid* — the idealized all-knowing jurist-founder who derived the school's methodology directly from revelation. This was not historical falsification in any simple sense; it was the discursive precondition for the doctrinal school's authority.

### Phase 4: Diffusion and Territorial Settlement (ca. 950–1100 CE)
The four surviving doctrinal schools spread into distinct geographic domains through three mechanisms: judicial appointment, teaching circles, and legal debate. By the end of the 4th/10th century, the Hanafis dominated Iraq and the eastern provinces; the Malikis, Egypt, the Maghrib, and Andalusia; the Shafi'is, parts of Egypt, Syria, and the Iranian world; the Hanbalis, Baghdad.

## Surviving and Defunct Schools

Of the dozens of personal schools that emerged during the 2nd–3rd/8th–9th centuries, only four survived to become doctrinal schools. The others failed for one or more of four identifiable reasons:
1. **Lack of political support** (e.g., the Awza'ites in Syria, displaced by Shafi'ism when the Tulunids arrived in 284/897).
2. **Failure to achieve the [[great-synthesis-islamic-law]]** — the critical balance between rationalism and traditionalism (e.g., [[zahirism]] under Dawud al-Zahiri, which insisted on literalism and rejected analogy entirely).
3. **Alliance with a sectarian theological movement** that later lost power (e.g., Jarirish under al-Tabari, whose personal attacks on Ibn Hanbal alienated Baghdad's Hanbali population).
4. **Absence of a distinct juristic identity** sufficient to compete with established schools (e.g., Awza'ism, too close to Medinese doctrine to carve its own niche).

"Four Muhammads" — Marwazi, Tabari, Ibn Khuzayma, Ibn al-Mundhir — each had personal followings that were eventually absorbed by the Shafi'i doctrinal school, illustrating how the absorption of defunct schools also served authority construction.

## End Conditions

The madhhab-formation process was substantially complete by the middle of the 4th/10th century, when the four doctrinal schools had constructed their founding eponyms, established their methodological canons, and achieved sufficient geographic diffusion to survive. Later juristic activity — commentary, *takhrij*, *fatwa* compilation — operated within the established boundaries of the schools rather than transforming their structure. Hallaq identifies the completion of legal theory ([[usul-al-fiqh]]) as the final prerequisite, achieved approximately contemporaneously with the crystallization of the doctrinal schools.

## Historiography

Hallaq explicitly argues against Christopher Melchert's thesis (Melchert, *The Formation of the Sunni Schools of Law*, Leiden: Brill, 1997) that education and pedagogical transmission were the primary causes of madhhab formation. Hallaq's counter: pedagogy was a vehicle, not a cause. There had to be a doctrinal school for it to be taught. The real drivers were doctrine-building, authority construction, and the Great Synthesis. Similarly, Hallaq rejects the notion that madrasas or commentaries caused the schools; the schools had to exist first for madrasas to teach them.

The modern scholarly tendency — which Hallaq calls "thoroughly flawed" — to treat differences between the schools as minor or merely pertaining to details is refuted with concrete examples: the Hanafi vs. Hanbali definition of usurpation (*ghasb*) produced differences in damage awards that in at least one documented 21st-century commercial dispute (Delaware Superior Court) produced outcomes differing by over $300 million.
