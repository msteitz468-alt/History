# The Spiritual Conquest and the Colonial Church

The Catholic Church was, with the [[colonial-spanish-american-administration|royal
bureaucracy]], one of the two great institutional buttresses of Iberian rule in the
Americas. The **conversion of the Indians** was the *theoretical justification* of
the whole colonial enterprise, and the Church came to touch every part of colonial
life — spiritual, educational, charitable, economic, and political. This page
follows Burkholder and Johnson's [[burkholder-johnson-colonial-latin-america|treatment]];
it is the colonial-American chapter of the [[catholic-reformation|Catholic
Reformation]].

## Royal patronage

Uniquely, the colonial Church operated under **greater royal control than the Church
in Spain itself**. The *patronato real* (Spanish) and *padroado* (Portuguese) —
granted by a series of papal bulls — gave the Crowns control of tithe income,
appointment of clergy, founding of churches, and licensing of missionaries; nothing
papal reached the Indies without the Council of the Indies certifying it did not
infringe royal patronage. Church and Crown were fused.

## The spiritual conquest

Systematic evangelization began with the **mendicant orders** ("regulars"): the
**twelve Franciscans of 1524** in New Spain (some Franciscans bearing a
**millenarian** hope that conversion would hasten Christ's return), followed by
Dominicans and Augustinians, and later the **Society of Jesus** (Brazil 1549, Peru
1568, New Spain 1572). Conquest itself aided them: military defeat discredited the
Aztec and Inca gods. The friars **learned native languages** (Nahuatl, Quechua,
Aymara, Tupi) — until 1580 more titles were printed in native languages in Mexico
than in Castilian or Latin — even teaching Nahuatl as a *lingua franca* to keep
Indians apart from corrupting Europeans.

## Missions, reductions, and conflict with settlers

To gather dispersed and dwindling populations under supervision, the orders founded
mission towns — **congregación** (New Spain), **reducción** (Peru, where Toledo
resettled up to 1.5 million people), and **aldeias** (Jesuit Brazil). The famous
**Guaraní missions** of Paraguay peaked at 30 villages and 140,000 Indians (1732).
Because the colonial economy hungered for Indian labor, the missions — especially
Jesuit ones defending their charges from slavers (the São Paulo **bandeirantes**) —
clashed repeatedly with settlers, a tension that helped doom the Society (expelled
from Portuguese lands 1759, Spanish 1767).

## Syncretism and incomplete conversion

Conversion was broad but shallow. A chronic **shortage of clergy** let Indians blend
Catholicism with traditional belief in endlessly varied ways — provoking
never-fully-successful campaigns to "extirpate idolatry" (and, in the 1560s Andes,
the millenarian anti-Christian **Taki Onqoy** movement). The emblem of the fusion is
**Our Lady of Guadalupe**, venerated on the hill of the native goddess **Tonantzin**
north of Mexico City and made patroness after the 1736–37 epidemic. The **cofradías**
(lay confraternities) became central institutions of indigenous community life. An
attempt to train a **native clergy** (the College of Santiago Tlatelolco, 1536)
failed against anti-Indian prejudice, and Indians were formally barred from
ordination 1555–91.

## The Inquisition

The Crown established **tribunals of the Inquisition** at Mexico City and Lima
(1569) and Cartagena (1610) — founded chiefly against Protestant "interlopers,"
though they also prosecuted *conversos* (the Lima auto de fé of 1639 burned eleven
accused crypto-Jews). **Indians were exempt** (deemed permanent minors), left to
episcopal authority. The Holy Office ordered death in fewer than 1% of cases — its
staples were bigamy, blasphemy, and censorship — and waned after the
mid-seventeenth century. Brazil had no separate tribunal; suspects were shipped to
Portugal.

## An economic institution and a creolized Church

By 1600 the Church began an ascent to become the colonies' **wealthiest
institution**: tithes, fees, gifts, and *pious works* (chantries, dowries) made it
the chief **mortgage lender** (~5–6%, structured to avoid the sin of usury) and a
great urban and rural landlord; the regular orders, above all the Jesuits, were
major producers of sugar, wine, and textiles. **Nunneries** housed and educated
elite women (over 20% of Lima's women at their height) and mirrored the social
hierarchy (black-veil *criollas* with dowries; white-veil and servant women below).
The Church also dominated **education** (the universities of Lima and Mexico,
1551). Over the seventeenth century the clergy **creolized** — American-born priests
came to predominate, and nearly every elite family had relatives in the Church —
binding it firmly to American soil.

## Interaction and End Conditions

The Church was inseparable from [[colonial-spanish-american-administration|royal
administration]] (the *patronato*) and from the [[encomienda|labor regime]] (mission
labor, the settler–missionary conflict). The **Jesuit expulsion** (1759–67), a
[[bourbon-reforms|Bourbon]]/Pombaline assault on the Church's autonomy and wealth,
marks the great turning point; the institution nonetheless remained strong to the
end of the colonial era.
