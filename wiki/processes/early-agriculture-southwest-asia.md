## Definition and Scope

The origin of agriculture in **Southwest Asia** (the "Near East" — Levant,
Anatolia, the Zagros/Mesopotamian flanks) — the **first and most intensively
studied** of the world's farming hearths, and the model on which the whole
concept of the [[neolithic-revolution]] was built. Domesticates here were the
"founder crops" (emmer and einkorn wheat, barley, legumes) plus sheep, goats,
pigs, and cattle. Covered in CWH Vol. II Ch. 8 (Alan Simmons) with case study
'Ain Ghazal in Ch. 9 (Gary Rollefson).

This region "has probably been the greatest focus of scholarly attention" in
agricultural-origins research, partly because of arid-climate preservation and
partly because Western archaeology's roots lay here (T. E. Lawrence, Woolley,
Garstang, Kenyon at Jericho; Braidwood at Jarmo; Mellaart at Çatalhöyük).

## Chronology (calibrated BP / BCE)

| Phase | Calibrated dates | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Natufian** | ~13,000–10,000 BCE | semi-sedentary foragers, wild-grain harvesting; pre-Neolithic threshold |
| **PPNA** (Pre-Pottery Neolithic A) | ~9700–8500 BCE | cultivation of (still largely wild) cereals; round houses; Jericho tower; Göbekli Tepe |
| **PPNB** | ~8500–6700 BCE | morphological domestication; rectangular houses; the "florescence"; skull-plastering |
| **PPNC** | ~6700–6250 BCE | defined at 'Ain Ghazal; herding intensifies |
| **PN** (Pottery Neolithic) | ~7000–4900 BCE | pottery; regional diversification |

(Note the Natufian/PPNA terminology comes largely from Kenyon's Jericho work; "PPNC" was defined at the Jordanian megasite 'Ain Ghazal.)

## Causal Drivers and the theory history

SW Asia is where the great explanatory models were proposed and tested:
- **Childe's "oasis propinquity" theory** — early-Holocene aridity drove
  people/plants/animals together at oases, prompting domestication. *Refuted:*
  there was no catastrophic early-Neolithic drying (it got *wetter*), and most
  early sites are not in oases/river valleys (Jericho is an exception).
- **Braidwood's "hilly flanks" model** — domestication in the Zagros foothills'
  "nuclear zone," via interdisciplinary fieldwork. *Weakened:* the earliest sites
  are not actually in the foothills.
- The early Neolithic in fact enjoyed **optimal, often better-than-today**
  conditions; the [[younger-dryas]] stressed *late Natufian* foragers, and a
  sharp ~6200 BCE (8.2 ka) arid event roughly coincides with the end of the PPN.

The volume's framing: no single climatic trigger — see
[[why-did-agriculture-begin]]. Striking evidence (ritualized contexts for the
earliest cereals, sickle blades, grindstones; Göbekli Tepe's monumental ritual
*preceding* full farming; the suitability of cereals for **alcohol/feasting**)
supports social and ideological "pull" alongside environmental factors.

## The package decouples

Simmons stresses that the "Neolithic package" is not a unit: there were
**semi-sedentary villages without agriculture** and **agriculture without
villages**; some "marginal" Neolithic peoples remained mobile
hunter-gatherers who adopted Neolithic material culture. Natufian and PPNA
"hunter-gatherers" practised cultivation and possibly animal management long
before plants/animals were morphologically domesticated — reinforcing the
"pre-domestication cultivation" theme (see [[domestication]]).

## Geographic Spread

The wheat/barley/sheep/goat system was not confined to the "hilly flanks": very
similar conditions extended east across the Iranian plateau to Afghanistan,
Pakistan, and NW India, much of it archaeologically unexplored — so the Fertile
Crescent may not have been an *exclusive* domestication centre (some genetic
studies of the plants/animals already imply this). From SW Asia the package
spread into Europe (see [[early-agriculture-europe]] *(Cycle 6)*) and toward
South Asia (see [[early-agriculture-south-asia]]) — by demic and cultural
diffusion in region-specific mixes ([[demic-vs-cultural-diffusion]]).

## Case study: 'Ain Ghazal (Jordan)

'Ain Ghazal, on the Zarqa River near Amman, was **founded ~8500 BCE** (MPPNB) —
about a thousand years *after* agriculture began — and occupied continuously for
~2,500 years across four phases (MPPNB → LPPNB → PPNC → Yarmoukian PN), growing
from a ~2 ha village into a ~15 ha **"megasite."** It is famous for:
- some of the world's **earliest large-scale statuary** — two-headed plaster
  busts and free-standing lime-plaster human figures (caches from 1983–85);
- **skull removal and plastering** (ancestor ritual): subfloor burials whose
  skulls were detached, plaster-modelled, and curated;
- a sequence of **temples and shrines** in the LPPNB/PPNC.

Rollefson reads 'Ain Ghazal as a case of **self-induced environmental change**:
the inhabitants' own "unwitting actions" (deforestation for lime-plaster and
fuel, overgrazing) degraded the local landscape — evidence of the volume's
**"mutual domestication"** theme, in which people and landscape reshaped each
other and helped drive the megasite's eventual decline.

## Site detail (Fagan & Durrani 2020)

Fagan & Durrani (2020) add further chronological texture across the region:

- **Karacadağ Mountains** (SE Turkey): DNA evidence points here as the likely
  origin of the earliest domesticated cereal, einkorn wheat; rachis-toughening
  could occur within 20–30 generations of selection (see [[domestication]]).
- **Jordan Valley figs**, cultivated by ~9400 BCE — possibly the earliest
  domesticated plant identified in the region.
- **Abu Hureyra** (Syria): founded ~11,500 BCE as a foraging village; underwent
  an abrupt shift ~9000 BCE to herding goats/sheep and growing einkorn and pulses
  following the Younger Dryas-driven collapse of nut-forest resources; grew to
  12 ha before abandonment ~6000 BCE. Skeletal evidence (Theya Molleson) shows
  repetitive-task wear consistent with grain-grinding posture, disproportionately
  affecting women — early evidence for a sexual division of agricultural labor.
- **Çayönü Tepesi** (SE Turkey), occupied ~8600–7000 BCE: a communal "House of
  the Dead" containing 40+ human skulls and remains of ~400 individuals overall,
  with possible evidence of sacrifice.
- **Göbekli Tepe**: monumental T-shaped megalithic pillars (up to 2.4 m and 7
  tons) from ~9600 BCE, forming a pre-farming ritual complex.
- **Nevalı Çori**: contemporary cult buildings with carved anthropomorphic and
  bird figures.
- **Ganj Dareh** (Zagros, Iran): a seasonal camp from ~10,500 BCE that became a
  farming village (goat/sheep herding, cereal horticulture) roughly 1,500 years
  later.
- **Jarmo** (northern Iraq): by ~6000 BCE, 80%+ of the diet came from farming and
  herding.
- **Ali Kosh** (Khuzistan lowlands, Iran): settled from ~9000 BCE, documenting
  over 2,000 years of farming/herding, transhumance, and the development of
  irrigation.

On the ritual-vs-drought debate, Fagan & Durrani present **Steven Mithen's
"ritual-first" hypothesis** — that the elaborate ritual/communal construction at
Göbekli Tepe, Çayönü, and Nevalı Çori *predates* clear farming evidence and may
have driven, rather than followed, cereal cultivation, as large ritual
gatherings needing food surplus incidentally propagated cereal stands near the
sites — as a live rival to drought-driven models, explicitly logging it as an
"unresolved debate" rather than adjudicating it. (Fagan & Durrani 2020)

## End Conditions

Transitions, region by region, into the Chalcolithic and the urban world of
Mesopotamia and the Levant — the subject of later Processing-List volumes (CAH,
CWH Vol. III). The PN megasites fragment; settlement disperses.

## Historiography note

Best-documented hearth in the collection and in scholarship generally; the
volume's main caution is that the **Jericho "type site" was atypical**, and that
balanced understanding required the later discovery of more modest communities
across Jordan, Turkey, and Cyprus. Politics has long restricted fieldwork in
Iran and Iraq, biasing the record toward the Levant.
