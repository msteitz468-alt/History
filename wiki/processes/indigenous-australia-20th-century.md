## Definition and Scope

The political, social, and demographic history of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples from Federation (1901) to the present — spanning the eras of formal "protection" (enforced segregation), biological absorption schemes, post-war assimilation policy, the campaigns for citizenship and civil rights, the 1967 referendum, self-determination, land rights and native title (Mabo), the stolen generations reckoning, and the contemporary "Closing the Gap" framework.

Source: [[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]], chapter by Anna Haebich and Steve Kinnane.

---

## Causal Drivers

At Federation, an estimated 93,000 Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander survivors constituted "a fraction of the pre-1788 population calculated at between 300,000 and over 1 million." The Constitution excluded "Aboriginal natives" from electoral calculations and made race "a 'constituent element' of the nation's founding Constitution." State governments implemented protectionist legislation — Queensland (1897), Western Australia (1905), NSW (1909), South Australia (1911), Northern Territory (1911) — enabling "governments to control virtually every aspect of Indigenous people's lives."

Three structural conditions shaped the century:
1. **Racial ideology** — Social Darwinist extinction theory and later eugenics supplied the rationale for protection, child removal, and biological absorption.
2. **Land dispossession** — Clearing Indigenous people from "lands desired for white settlement, while making them available as cheap labour for local employers."
3. **Jurisdictional fragmentation** — Indigenous affairs remained a State responsibility until the 1967 referendum; this fragmented accountability and enabled the most repressive State regimes.

## Major Phases

### Phase 1: Protection and Segregation (1901–c.1940)

Protectionist legislation created "reserves, missions and residential institutions" as "the typical instruments of such control." The laws could "take Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children from parents and communities" — the origins of the Stolen Generations. In Western Australia, the 1905 Aborigines Act followed the Roth Royal Commission, which exposed an exploitative system where police arrested Aboriginal men for rations and "kept the women for themselves." Surveillance cards tracked individuals by descent — "half-caste," "quarter-caste" — with women of mixed descent remaining subject to the 1905 Act regardless of age, "affecting their choice of sexual and marriage partners and their rights over their children."

A policy of **biological absorption** was formalised: the 1937 Conference of Commonwealth and State Aboriginal Authorities resolved that "the destiny of the natives of aboriginal origin, but not the full-blood lies in their ultimate absorption by the people of the Commonwealth." The WA 1936 Native Administration Act expanded child removal and "enabled the State to arrange marriages to ensure progressively lighter 'castes.'"

#### Indigenous Political Resistance

Against these controls, Indigenous organisations formed: the Australian Aboriginal Progressive Association (AAPA, NSW, 1924); the Australian Aborigines' League (AAL, Victoria, 1933); the Aborigines' Progressive Association (APA, NSW, 1937); the Native Union (WA, 1926); the Euralian Association (WA, 1932). These were "grassroots political organisations formed by respected community leaders with the core aim of securing equal rights."

The **National Day of Mourning (26 January 1938)** — held concurrently with 150th anniversary celebrations — brought Koori leaders including William Cooper, William Ferguson, Jack Patten, Fred Maynard and Pearl Gibbs together in Sydney. Their proclamation: "HEREBY MAKE PROTEST against the callous treatment of our people by whitemen during the past 150 years, AND WE APPEAL to the Australian Nation of today to make new laws for the education and care of Aborigines…FULL CITIZEN STATUS and EQUALITY WITHIN THE COMMUNITY." This event "marked the beginning of a national Indigenous consciousness."

### Phase 2: Assimilation (c.1940–1967)

Post-war, the government adopted assimilation as its organising policy — driven partly by new international human rights standards (the 1948 UN Declaration) and the "humiliating charges of racism and even genocide" Australia faced internationally. Minister for Territories Paul Hasluck (1951–63) sought to transform the Northern Territory into an assimilation showpiece. The 1961 Commonwealth definition of assimilation expected that Aboriginal people "eventually attain the same manner of living as other Australians…live as members of a single Australian community enjoying the same rights and privileges, accepting the same responsibilities, observing the same customs."

Most Indigenous people rejected the demand to abandon cultural identity while strategically "harnessing the rhetoric of assimilation in order to achieve Indigenous aspirations for improved health, education and employment."

Key events:
- **1946 Pilbara pastoral strike** — Aboriginal stockmen campaigned for equal wages and conditions.
- **Federal Council for the Advancement of Aborigines and Torres Strait Islanders (FCAATSI, 1964)** — principal advocacy coalition, combining unions, churches, and growing Indigenous representation.
- **1965 Freedom Ride** — led by Charles Perkins from the University of Sydney, exposing segregation in country towns.
- **1966 Gurindji Walk-off from Wave Hill station** — Vincent Lingiari led 200 Aboriginal stockmen and families off the Vesteys pastoral lease, camping at Wattie Creek and petitioning for "return of traditional lands."

### Phase 3: The 1967 Referendum and Its Aftermath

See [[1967-referendum]]. The referendum secured 90.7 per cent support; it removed the constitutional exclusion of Aboriginal people from electoral reckoning and gave the Commonwealth power to make laws for Indigenous people. But "younger, urban Indigenous people held that 'equality with whites was no longer an aim, but a stepping stone.'" A new generation influenced by African American Black Power tactics challenged FCAATSI to throw off non-Indigenous tutelage.

The **1971 Blackburn judgment** (Gove Peninsula/Nabalco) found that while the Yolngu had "established systems of law and a spiritual connection with the land, this did not constitute collective rights in, or ownership of the land, in common law" — upholding terra nullius and catalysing the push for land rights.

The **Aboriginal Tent Embassy** was erected on the lawns of Parliament House, Canberra, on 27 January 1972, in response to Prime Minister McMahon's rejection of land rights. It "symbolised that Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people remained 'stateless' within the Australian nation while their land rights were denied."

### Phase 4: Self-Determination and Land Rights (1972–1992)

The Whitlam Labor government (1972–75) announced a policy to "restore to the Aboriginal people of Australia their lost power of self-determination." Expenditure on Indigenous services rose from $23 million (1971–72) to $141 million (1975–76). The **Aboriginal Land Rights (Northern Territory) Act 1976**, enacted by the Fraser government on revised Whitlam-era recommendations, established a mechanism for transfer of Aboriginal reserves and crown lands to land councils with mining royalty rights — but was confined to the Northern Territory. The Fraser government's subsequent shift to "self-management" substituted government-defined frameworks for genuine autonomy. The Community Development Employment Program (CDEP, 1977) pooled unemployment benefits into community wage funds, enabling locally controlled employment but criticised by some in government as reinforcing "welfare dependency."

By 1979, the National Aboriginal Conference called for a treaty ("Makarrata"), adopting the Yolngu concept meaning "coming together after dispute." The Fraser government refused, citing sovereignty concerns. The Hawke Labor government (1983–) proposed national land rights legislation but "gave way in the face of a campaign conducted publicly by the mining industry." ATSIC (Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Commission, 1989) was legislated as an elected Indigenous body controlling certain program funding, but remained "subject to the Minister" and did not control all Commonwealth programs.

### Phase 5: Mabo, Native Title, and Reconciliation (1992–2008)

See [[mabo-decision-1992]]. In 1992, the High Court found in favour of Eddie Mabo and the Meriam people, ruling that "the Meriam people were entitled as against the whole world to the possession, occupation, use and enjoyment" of their island. Justice Brennan stated: "The common law of this country would perpetuate an injustice if it were to continue to embrace the enlarged notion of terra nullius." The **Native Title Act 1993** codified the decision. The Keating government also undertook a "social justice package" and Paul Keating's December 1992 Redfern speech acknowledged: "We took the traditional lands and smashed the traditional way of life. We brought the disasters. The alcohol. We committed the murders. We took the children from their mothers."

The 1996 **Wik decision** found that the granting of a pastoral lease did not fully extinguish native title — "it was possible for native title and pastoral rights to coexist." The Howard government subsequently amended the Native Title Act to protect pastoralists, refused to apologise to the Stolen Generations, and in 2004 abolished ATSIC with bipartisan support. The 2007 **Northern Territory Intervention**, responding to allegations of child abuse without consultation with community leaders, banned alcohol, quarantined welfare payments, abolished CDEP, and mobilised the military — requiring exemption from the Racial Discrimination Act. Mick Dodson described it as "storm-trooper tent diplomacy."

On 13 February 2008, Prime Minister Kevin Rudd delivered a formal apology to the Stolen Generations: "For the pain, suffering and hurt of these Stolen Generations, their descendants and for their families left behind, we say sorry."

### Phase 6: Closing the Gap (2008–present)

The "Closing the Gap" policy framework, developed in 2006 by Indigenous and non-Indigenous organisations and adopted by the Council of Australian Governments in 2007, aims to improve Indigenous health, education, employment, and housing to non-Indigenous levels. The 2011 census recorded 548,000 people who identified as Indigenous — more than double the 265,000 in 1991 — constituting 2.5 per cent of the total population, with a median age of 21 years against 37 for non-Indigenous Australians. Life expectancy of Indigenous men remained 11.5 years below non-Indigenous men; women 9.7 years below. Between 1994 and 2008 the proportion of Indigenous men employed in urban and regional centres rose from 36 per cent to over 62 per cent, but "employment prospects in remote areas remained low."

## Geographic Spread

Different State regimes created distinct regional experiences. The Northern Territory — where Aboriginal people constituted 80 per cent of the population in the early twentieth century — was the site of the most intense assimilation experiments, the Gurindji Walk-off, and the 2007 Intervention. Queensland under Bjelke-Petersen maintained segregated settlements into the late 1970s. Western Australia pursued biological absorption most aggressively. Torres Strait Islanders, through the Mabo case, secured the landmark native title recognition. Urban Indigenous communities (a third live in State capitals) experienced better outcomes on most wellbeing indicators than remote communities.

## End Conditions

The process is ongoing. Haebich and Kinnane conclude that the century's story is "one of resurgence of cultural and political governance, collective cultural identity and engagement with the nation-state." Indigenous Australia "exists today as a diverse and complex network of people engaged with the modern state, yet founded in shared cultural and historical experience." Persistent structural disadvantage and the unfinished work of treaty and sovereignty recognition remain central challenges.

## Historiography

The chapter draws on major works: Anna Haebich's *Broken Circles* (2000) and *Spinning the Dream* (2008); John Chesterman and Brian Galligan's *Citizens Without Rights* (1997); Bain Attwood and Andrew Markus's *The Struggle for Aboriginal Rights* (1999); Russell McGregor's *Indifferent Inclusion* (2011); the HREOC *Bringing Them Home* report (1997); and Rosalind Kidd's *Hard Labour, Stolen Wages* (2007). A key historiographical debate concerns the tension between citizenship (universal rights) and self-determination (collective Indigenous rights): for most of the century, Australian policy and most non-Indigenous advocacy framed the "Aboriginal problem" in terms of equal citizenship, while Indigenous leaders increasingly asserted the prior and distinct basis of their rights in sovereignty, land, and cultural governance.
