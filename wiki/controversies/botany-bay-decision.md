# The Botany Bay Decision — Penal Dumping Ground vs. Imperial Strategy

One of the "most enduring of all Australian historiographical debates" (CHA vol. 1, ch. 3,
Christopher and Maxwell-Stewart): why did the Pitt administration choose [[botany-bay|Botany
Bay]] as the destination for British convicts in August 1786, and what was the intended
purpose of the resulting colony?

## Background

The American Revolution terminated the Atlantic market for British convict labour
(1776–83). The hulks used as a stopgap became dangerously overcrowded after 1783.
Alternative sites — west Africa (Senegambia, McCarthy Island), Das Voltas Bay (Namibia),
India, Madagascar — were all investigated and rejected on grounds of disease, expense,
EIC monopoly, or inadequate surveying. The *Nautilus* returned in July 1786 declaring
Das Voltas Bay "doomed to everlasting sterility." Just two weeks later, Botany Bay was
announced.

## Position 1: Penal-Management Crisis (Majority Historiographical View)

**Core argument:** Botany Bay was selected primarily because in August 1786 there was
simply nowhere else to send convicted felons. It was the least-bad surviving option, not
a considered strategic choice.

**Principal scholars:** **Mollie Gillen**, "The Botany Bay Decision, 1786: Convicts not
Empire," *English Historical Review* 97 (1982) — the clearest statement of this position.
Also **David MacKay**, "Direction and Purpose in British Imperial Policy, 1783–1801,"
*Historical Journal* 17 (1974), who argued it "requires some gifts of imagination to
embody the foundation of the New South Wales colony into the substance of some wider
imperial purpose."

**Evidence cited:**
- The decision followed immediately from the failure of *all* alternative sites; strategic
  Botany Bay had been known since 1779 but not acted on until no other option remained.
- The government "continued to fill the settlement with subsequent drafts of convicts"
  rather than building it out as an imperial base — inconsistent with a primarily strategic
  purpose.
- Gillen argues the proposal to permit transports to return laden with tea from Canton
  "appears to have first been proposed three weeks after the scheme was initially
  announced" — suggesting it was a cost-saving afterthought, not a founding rationale.
- The naval stores argument (flax and timber from Norfolk Island) was widely seen even at
  the time as a "belated attempt by supporters of the proposal to put a favourable gloss
  on the scheme."
- The Beauchamp Committee (1785) had explicitly condemned the idea of a self-governing
  "entire colony of male and female convicts" — the government's own committee discredited
  the utopian reading, leaving penal management as the operative goal.

## Position 2: Imperial Strategic Design (Minority but Sustained View)

**Core argument:** The decision was shaped by wider imperial considerations — Pacific
trade security, naval stores, the China route — that converged with the penal need.
Botany Bay served strategic ends and was not a mere dumping ground.

**Principal scholars:** First articulated by **E.C.K. Gonner**, "The Settlement of
Australia," *English Historical Review* 3 (1888). Expanded by **H.T. Fry** (on the
Cathay/China trade route), **K.M. Dallas** (*Trading Posts or Penal Colonies*, 1969),
**Geoffrey Blainey** (*The Tyranny of Distance*, 1966/1983), and above all **Alan Frost**
in three major works: *Convicts and Empire: A Naval Question* (1980); *Botany Bay
Mirages* (1994); *The Global Reach of Empire* (2003).

**Evidence cited:**
- Just seven days separated the annexation of Penang (securing the Malacca Strait route)
  and the announcement of the Botany Bay decision — suggesting coordinated Pacific strategy
  in the wake of the Fourth Anglo-Dutch War (1780–84).
- Alexander Dalrymple's 1782 "Memoir concerning the Passages to and from China" flagged
  the utility of a southern passage around Australia to maintain the China trade in
  wartime — exactly the route the First Fleet used.
- The government signed an agreement with the East India Company in December 1786
  permitting transport contractors to proceed from Botany Bay to Canton for tea, and
  promoted this as a "considerable saving also to the Company."
- The settlement plan explicitly included simultaneous colonisation of **Norfolk Island**
  (Cook's identified source of naval stores) — suggesting the scheme was designed to
  address Britain's 61 per cent decline in navy-yard timber stocks between 1775 and 1790.
- The colony did achieve at least one imperial objective quickly: by the 1790s it provided
  a harbour where ships could be careened and resupplied, "thus fulfilling at least one
  newspaper prediction."

## Complicating Factors Identified in the Sources

**Alan Atkinson** (*The Europeans in Australia*, vol. 1, 1998) offers a variant:
Botany Bay was selected partly as a liberalising alternative to the hulks — a "ragged but
real commonwealth" where convicts could be exiled as peasants rather than enslaved
behind bars. This reading is largely rejected by the Beauchamp Committee evidence, but
Atkinson argues the sticking point that prevented the Committee naming a site earlier
was East India Company objections, with New South Wales as the committee's preferred
option all along.

**Christopher and Maxwell-Stewart** (CHA vol. 1, ch. 3) add a structural-economic
argument that cuts across both camps: the real driver was the **racialisation of
unfreedom**. British and Irish convicts were white; the Caribbean was amply supplied with
enslaved Africans; and India had no need for European convict labour. The result was that
"New Holland was colonised by convict labour because the vast majority of those felons
were white" — a market-driven outcome that made strategic or punitive rationales somewhat
secondary to the structural impossibility of selling white convict bodies anywhere else.
This does not fully support either main position but reframes the question.

## Resolution Status

Open. The debate has shifted rather than resolved: most historians now accept that both
penal and strategic considerations were present, but disagree about which was the
"preliminary impulse." Frost's strategic interpretation remains influential but has not
displaced the penal-management consensus. Christopher and Maxwell-Stewart's racial-labour
framing offers a synthesis that reframes rather than adjudicates the original dispute.
