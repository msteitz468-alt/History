# Hierarchy vs Heterarchy in Early Cities

## The dispute

How was power organized in the earliest cities — concentrated in a despotic ruler
and palace, or **distributed** among councils, corporate groups, oligarchies, and
middle elites? This is the central interpretive question of Part IV of
[[yoffee-cwh-v3-2015|CWH Vol. III]], "The Distribution of Power." It is an
**interpretive** dispute about social structure, bearing directly on
[[what-defines-a-city|what counts as a city]] and on whether the
[[urban-revolution]] necessarily produced kingship.

## Positions

**1. Centralized hierarchy / the "archaic state."**
The older orthodoxy (Childe; settlement-hierarchy state theory; the "oriental
city"): early cities were apex nodes of vertical hierarchies, ruled by kings,
with palaces owning land and controlling economy and ideology. *Evidence:*
[[uruk|Uruk]]'s dominant ruler iconography; Mesopotamian and Egyptian kingship;
four-tier settlement hierarchies read as states (Wright & Johnson).

**2. Heterarchy / distributed power.**
Power was often **ranked in multiple ways or unranked** ([[heterarchy]]; Crumley;
S. & R. McIntosh). *Evidence:* [[jenne-jeno|Jenne-jeno]] — a large, dense city of
reciprocal corporate groups with no citadel or king; [[teotihuacan|Teotihuacan]] —
no depicted rulers, no royal tomb, plausibly collective/council rule; Greek
**oligarchies and assemblies**; the muted hierarchy of the [[mohenjo-daro|Indus]]
cities; councils of elders/young men even at Uruk. The locus classicus is
Thorkild Jacobsen's **"primitive democracy"** thesis: that early Sumerian kingship
was *evolutionary*, not primitive, and that authority was at least sometimes
checked by an **assembly of elders and free men**. Its prime text is the Sumerian
epic of **Gilgamesh and Agga** ([[uruk|Uruk]] vs. Kish), in which Gilgamesh, having
been refused by the senate of elders, wins the backing of the assembly of fighting
men to resist — read as a memory of a deliberative, two-chamber polity behind the
later façade of one-man rule. In the Standard Version (ll. 771–4000) Gilgamesh "convened [the assembly,] the crowd gathered round" (ll. 2038); he addresses "the elders of Uruk-the-City" (ll. 2052) and "the young men of Uruk" (ll. 2076), who offer counsel against the Humbaba expedition (ll. 2130–2152); the "senior advisers" and regents play advisory roles (ll. 2455+). CAH ([[edwards-cah-v1p2-1971]], Ch. XIII, Gadd)
records the debate while cautioning it rests largely on inference from myth and
from the land-tenure behind [[urukagina|Urukagina's reforms]]. The Standard Version assemblies **concurrent_with** (and support) the heterarchy evidence from the Sumerian poem.

**3. Mixed / case-specific.**
Most cities combined both; the analytical task is to specify the *balance* in each
case, not to award a single label (Yoffee). The "orientalist" caricature of
pre-Greek cities as uniform totalitarian monarchies (flagged by Morris & Knodell)
is rejected, but so is a blanket denial of early-urban hierarchy.

## State of play

**Partially resolved.** There is broad consensus that the uniform-despotism model
is wrong and that heterarchical and collective forms were real and widespread.
What remains disputed is *how to read negative evidence*: does the absence of
palaces and royal monuments (Jenne-jeno, Indus, Teotihuacan) demonstrate
heterarchy, or merely an undetected or differently-expressed hierarchy? That
question is open.

## Why it matters

It determines whether the city and the state are the same thing, whether
[[urbanization]] entails kingship, and how far the Mesopotamian template should
govern the comparative study of cities.
