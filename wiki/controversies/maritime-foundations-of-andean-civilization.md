## Background

In the Late Archaic period (~5,000–3,000 BP), the coastal valleys of Peru witnessed some of the most dramatic cultural developments in the pre-Columbian Americas: large, permanent settlements with monumental U-shaped platform mounds at sites such as Aspero, El Paraiso, La Galgada, and Huaca Prieta — all constructed before maize agriculture became significant. The central question this raised is: **what economic base supported these early complex cultures if not intensive agriculture?**

Michael Moseley's answer, advanced most fully in *The Maritime Foundations of Andean Civilization* (Menlo Park, 1975), was that the extraordinarily productive cold-water fisheries of the Humboldt Current — anchovies and other small schooling fish, sea mammals, and shellfish — supplied the protein and caloric surplus that allowed sedentary, nucleated communities to form before agriculture.

## Position 1: The Maritime Thesis (Moseley)

Moseley and collaborators argued that the rich seafood of the cold Peruvian coastal waters, not domesticated plants and animals, was the foundation of pristine civilisation in the Andes. He noted that the largest Late Archaic coastal sites show:

- Abundant remains of small fish (anchovies dominated ~90% of animal remains at El Paraiso), sea mammals, and shellfish as the primary protein source
- No evidence of intensive maize agriculture, or even significant food-crop cultivation, in preceramic layers
- Cotton and gourd cultivation as the main cropping activity — industrial rather than food crops
- Permanent, densely nucleated settlements along river valleys near the coast, supported by marine resources year-round

Lynch (Ch. 3, [[cambridge-history-native-peoples-americas-v3-1]]) summarises this position: "Moseley and his collaborators and students have been the most effective advocates of the view that early civilization in Peru, as a pristine case, was founded not upon domesticated plants and animals but on the rich seafood of the cold coastal waters."

The thesis implied a reversal of the standard evolutionary sequence (agriculture → sedentism → complexity): here, maritime foraging achieved the sedentism that allowed complexity to emerge, with agriculture entering later as a supplement or intensification strategy.

## Position 2: The Agricultural and Multi-Resource Critique

Several scholars challenged the maritime thesis:

**Alan Osborn** ("Strandloopers, mermaids, and other fairy tales," 1977) argued that coastal resources, while productive, were inherently unstable. The Pacific coast is periodically devastated by El Niño events, which collapse fisheries while simultaneously bringing rain to the desert coast — redistributing productivity from sea to land. A civilisation wholly dependent on marine resources would be periodically catastrophic. Osborn contended that stable civilisations required a diversified resource base.

**Highland perspective (Burger):** Richard Burger drew attention to the evidence from highland sites (Kotosh, La Galgada, Huaricoto, Huacaloma) that monumental architecture and complex culture appeared in the interior valleys as early as on the coast: "The results of excavations at La Galgada, Huaricoto, and Huacaloma... should give pause for thought to those who associate the appearance of early monumental architecture and, by extension, early complex societies, with the particular environment and resources of the Peruvian coast."

**James B. Richardson III** and others refined Moseley's model, allowing a greater role for highland agriculture and inter-zonal exchange, while still crediting marine resources with the enabling role in early sedentism.

## Position 3: Roosevelt's Synthesis

Anna C. Roosevelt (Ch. 4, [[cambridge-history-native-peoples-americas-v3-1]]) accepts that marine resources were critical enabling conditions but embeds the maritime thesis in a broader comparative framework:

1. "Marine faunas may have supported some of the earliest sedentary, nucleated communities in the Americas before the development of agriculture, according to Michael Moseley. However, their productivity is subject to periodic catastrophic drops, when weather patterns of the 'El Niño' phenomenon allow warm, nutrient-poor equatorial waters to displace the cool southern currents."
2. At El Paraiso, Jeffrey Quilter's re-excavation showed that "the evidence does not indicate political hierarchy and centralization at the site at this time" and that "the site exhibits varying aspects of elaboration of some aspects of culture and not others... it has not yielded evidence of complex political organization." This undermined the claim that early coastal sites were chiefdoms organised to pool resources from a wide region.
3. Roosevelt argues that the trajectory was: early sedentism based on aquatic resources → cultivation of industrial plants (cotton, gourd) → late Archaic ceremonial elaboration → gradual intensification of food crops → Late Horizon agricultural states. Maritime resources were critical at the start but were not the sole or continuing driver.
4. Crucially, she notes that in [[amazonia]], the earliest pottery (Taperinha ~7,000 BP) also appeared alongside specialised aquatic foraging — not agriculture — "the sedentism achieved by aquatic foraging, in turn, may have stimulated the initiation of plant cultivation." This comparative pattern suggests that rich aquatic resources, not agriculture per se, are the key enabling variable for early sedentism.

## Key Empirical Points of Contention

| Issue | Moseley/maritime | Critics |
|---|---|---|
| Primary food at coastal Archaic sites | Fish/shellfish dominant (anchovies ~90% at El Paraiso) | Agreed, but El Paraiso also had 31 plant species; inter-zonal exchange significant |
| Role of maize | Absent pre-3,000 BP; irrelevant to complexity origin | Some claims of earlier maize remain under debate |
| Stability of marine resources | El Niño periodically devastating but manageable | Instability was a structural weakness; diversification essential |
| Social organization of early coastal sites | Hierarchical chiefdoms managing regional resources | Quilter and others: evidence is for egalitarian or weakly ranked communities |
| Highland sites | Coastal primacy | Highland complex sites equally early (Kotosh tradition) |

## Resolution Status

Partially resolved. The maritime thesis has been refined rather than refuted. Most specialists now accept:

- Marine protein was a major enabling resource for early sedentism and complexity on the Peruvian coast
- The "cotton preceramic" pattern — cultivating industrial rather than food crops, while subsisting on fish — is empirically documented
- Neither maritime nor agricultural resources alone explain the trajectory; the convergence of coast, highland, and eventually lowland resources was necessary for the civilisational apogee of later Andean culture
- The maritime thesis as originally stated (marine resources as *the* foundation) is too strong; the interaction of maritime foraging, incipient horticulture, and inter-zonal exchange is the more accurate picture

The debate is consequential for understanding whether environmental circumscription (Carneiro) or resource abundance (maritime thesis) better explains early complexity in the Andes — a dispute connected to broader theories of state formation. See [[andean-state-formation]].
