## The Dispute

Where and when did the speakers of **Proto-Indo-European (PIE)** — ancestor of a
family stretching from Sanskrit and Persian to Greek, Latin, Celtic, Germanic and
the Anatolian languages — originate, and by what mechanism did the family spread
across Eurasia? This is one of the **standing controversies** mandated by
CLAUDE.md and one of the oldest live debates in prehistory, bearing directly on
[[migration-in-human-history|prehistoric migration]], the
[[demic-vs-cultural-diffusion|demic-vs-cultural diffusion]] question, and the
peopling of Bronze Age Europe and South Asia. It is the Indo-European counterpart
to the wiki's [[afroasiatic-homeland]] and [[african-language-family-origins]]
debates.

## Position 1 — Steppe / Kurgan Hypothesis

Associated with **Marija Gimbutas** (1950s–70s) and, in its modern form, **David
Anthony** (*The Horse, the Wheel, and Language*, 2007):

- The PIE homeland was the **Pontic-Caspian steppe** (~4500–3000 BCE); the
  archaeological correlate is the Yamnaya and antecedent Kurgan cultures.
- Dispersal was driven by **mobile pastoralism**, the domesticated **horse**, and
  **wheeled vehicles**, enabling rapid, partly migratory expansion across Europe
  and into Central and South Asia. Links to [[pastoral-nomadism]] and the
  [[eurasian-steppe]].
- Now strongly supported by **ancient DNA** (Haak, Reich, Allentoft 2015): a
  massive Yamnaya-derived genetic influx into Central Europe ~3000–2500 BCE
  (Corded Ware) and a steppe component in South Asia. This evidence, post-dating
  the other positions, has moved much of the field toward the steppe model.

## Position 2 — Anatolian / Farming-Dispersal Hypothesis

Associated with **Colin Renfrew** (*Archaeology and Language*, 1987):

- PIE spread out of **Anatolia** with the [[early-agriculture-southwest-asia|Neolithic
  agricultural expansion]] from ~7000 BCE, carried by farmers via **demic
  diffusion** rather than by mounted conquerors.
- Attractive because it ties language spread to a demonstrable demographic
  process (farming) rather than to elusive invasions; criticized for being too
  early to fit the linguistic reconstruction (PIE vocabulary for wheels and wool
  implies a 4th-millennium, not 7th-millennium, community).
- Some Bayesian phylogenetic studies (Gray & Atkinson 2003; Bouckaert 2012) have
  supported an Anatolian-age origin, keeping the position alive against the aDNA
  tide; hybrid models now place an ultimate deep origin south of the Caucasus with
  a steppe staging area.

## Position 3 — The Indo-Hittite Model (the CAH's 1970 framing)

[[edwards-cah-v1p1-1970|CAH Vol. I, Part 1]] (Ch. IV, Albright & Lambdin) records
an important earlier state of the debate:

- It works within **E. H. Sturtevant's "Indo-Hittite"** framework: the **Anatolian
  group** (recovered after the decipherment of cuneiform Hittite in 1915) is so
  divergent — e.g. it lacks verbal *Ablaut* — that it is best treated as a
  **coordinate sister branch** that separated very early, implying "a long period
  of slow Indo-European development."
- Albright **rejects the equestrian-nomad picture**: it is "erroneous" to say that
  "during the third millennium the Indo-Europeans were nomad horsemen," a claim he
  attributes to "false a priori hypotheses and misdated stratigraphic finds,"
  noting that large-scale horseback riding and spoked-wheel chariots came only late
  in the 2nd millennium. This is a direct early challenge to the mechanism at the
  heart of Position 1.
- He also doubts that the Indo-Hittites **migrated into** Anatolia at the start of
  the 2nd millennium, arguing Hittite was already sharply distinct much earlier —
  leaning, in effect, toward a long Anatolian presence.
- Tellingly, on the central question the chapter punts: "There has been much
  debate as to the original home of the Indo-Europeans, but this matter does not
  concern us here."

## The Anatolian Branch on the Ground (CAH XVIII)

A separate strand of the same problem is the **archaeological** arrival of the
Anatolian languages, addressed by **James Mellaart** in
[[edwards-cah-v1p2-1971|CAH Vol. I, Part 2]] (Ch. XVIII):

- Mellaart read the **end-EB2 catastrophe** in Anatolia (c. 2300 BCE) — a collapse
  from ~450 settlements to ~125, with burn-layers at [[troy|Troy II]],
  Poliochni, Beycesultan and elsewhere — as the trace of an **invasion of
  Indo-European speakers** he identified with the **Luwians**, coming across the
  Dardanelles from the Balkans/Pontic region.
- His cited evidence: a hieroglyphic-Luwian-looking **seal from Beycesultan** and
  the obelisk at [[byblos]] inscribed for "**Kukun son of the Lukka-man**" (Lukka =
  Luwians). See [[early-bronze-age-anatolia]] for the full horizon.
- This is a classic **migrationist** argument and cuts across the homeland
  positions above: a destruction-by-invasion at the *end* of EB2 fits neither a
  slow 7th-millennium farming diffusion (Position 2) nor sits comfortably with the
  very early Indo-Hittite split (Position 3), and modern scholars caution that
  burnt layers need not mean ethnic newcomers. It nonetheless remains one of the
  oldest concrete attempts to date the **Anatolian branch's** appearance from
  material evidence rather than from linguistic reconstruction alone.

## Where Things Stand

**Partially resolved, tilting to the steppe.** Ancient DNA since 2015 has given the
Steppe/Kurgan model decisive new support for the *European* and *South Asian*
expansions, and the field's centre of gravity has shifted accordingly. But the
*ultimate* origin and the place of Anatolian remain open: the early, deep split of
Anatolian that the 1970 Indo-Hittite framing captured has resurfaced in modern
"Indo-Anatolian" phylogenies that posit a homeland south of or around the Caucasus
with a subsequent steppe phase. The CAH chapter is valuable here less as authority
than as evidence that the mechanism debate (mounted pastoralists vs. slow
diffusion) and the Anatolian-branch puzzle are a century old.
