# How to Read Sima Qian's Xiongnu Account: Empirical Record vs. Ideological Construction

A genuine scholarly controversy about how to read and use [[shiji-hsiungnu-monograph|chapter 110 of the *Shih chi*]] (the Xiongnu monograph) as historical evidence. The dispute has both methodological and historiographical dimensions: it concerns what [[sima-qian|Sima Qian]] was doing, how reliable his account is, what ideological framework distorts or shapes the information he transmitted, and how later scholarship has been trapped (or not) in his binary framework.

## The Core Questions

1. Was Sima Qian primarily an empirical collector of data, or a creative ideologist who imposed a normative framework on the north?
2. Is the ethnogenealogy at the opening of chapter 110 (Xiongnu as descendants of a Xia dynasty ruler) Sima Qian's own invention, or does it reflect widespread Han perception?
3. How much does the normative (cosmological/ideological) strand distort the empirical content, and can the two be reliably separated?
4. To what extent has later scholarship been trapped in Sima Qian's binary north-south (nomadic *yin* vs. agrarian *yang*) framework?

## Position 1: Sima Qian as Compilateur Within the Confucian Tradition

**Proponents**: Édouard Chavannes (*Les mémoires historiques de Se-ma Ts'ien*, 1895–1905); Burton Watson (*Ssu-ma Ch'ien, Grand Historian of China*, 1958).

**Argument**: Sima Qian's main achievement was the organization of already existing materials within a historical context. He was a *compilateur* rather than an original thinker, working within the Confucian historiographical tradition that extended from the Spring and Autumn Annals and the Tso-chuan. The *Shih chi* represents a more advanced evolutionary stage of that tradition, with Sima Qian as its "continuator" and "innovator" within it. Under this view, the account of the Xiongnu should be read within the Confucian framework, its ideological elements acknowledged but not systematically extracted from the empirical content.

Piet Van der Loon's related view proposed a three-stage evolutionary model: "ritual" historiography (before Confucius), "moralizing" historiography (with and after Confucius), and "systematic" history (with Sima Qian), the last appearing when China became a centralized power capable of "transmitting the experience of statecraft." Hulsewé argued that Sima Qian's reorganization of historical materials produced forms that "remained standards for official history writing for the following two thousand years."

**Best arguments**: Sima Qian drew explicitly and frequently on earlier records; he credited written sources (memorials of Chao Cuo, the *Ch'un-ch'iu*) throughout the *Shih chi*; his declared aims (in the letter to Ren An) place him in the tradition of investigating "heavenly and human" phenomena as a unified field. The Confucian historiographical tradition he inherited was real and shaped his work at every level.

**Limitations noted by critics**: This reading underestimates the degree to which Sima Qian broke with earlier historiographical practice in his empirical treatment of Inner Asia. No precedent existed for the ethnographic depth of chapter 110; earlier accounts of northern peoples were entirely mythological in character (*Shan-hai ching*, *Huai-nan Tzu*). The "continuator" reading cannot adequately explain what was new about chapter 110.

## Position 2: The Two-Strand Model (Di Cosmo 2002)

**Proponent**: Nicola Di Cosmo (*Ancient China and Its Enemies*, Cambridge University Press, 2002).

**Argument**: Chapter 110 contains two analytically distinct orientations that must be separated:

**(1) The empirical-descriptive strand**: A genuine methodological innovation derived from Sima Qian's dual role as astronomer-historian. His position as Grand Astrologer (T'ai-shih-ling) trained him in empirical observation, systematic recording, and pattern recognition — a method he applied to historical and geographical phenomena as well as celestial ones. The result was "a paradigm shift from the moralistic or chronachistic accounts of the past to the historically and ethnographically rich report that Ssu-ma Ch'ien was able to produce." The ethnographic content of chapter 110 (pastoral economy, burial customs, military training, ritual, vocabulary) constitutes primary evidence partially verifiable against the archaeological record.

**(2) The normative-cosmological strand**: A systematic effort to insert the Xiongnu into Chinese cosmological and historical thought — to make the north "explainable" by fitting it into the correlative framework of yin-yang correspondences and constructing a genealogy linking the Xiongnu to the Xia dynasty. This strand is not evidence about the Xiongnu but about Han intellectual categories.

Di Cosmo's practical prescription: scholars must determine, passage by passage, which strand they are reading before assessing its evidential value. The ethnographic content (strand 1) can be cross-checked against archaeology; the ethnogenealogy and astrological prognostications (strand 2) should be treated as evidence for Han cosmological thought, not Xiongnu history.

**Best arguments**: Supported by the intellectual history of the early Han (the flourishing of correlative cosmology and yin-yang thought under Wudi); consistent with Sima Qian's own methodological statements; explains why the *Shih chi* chapter 110 is simultaneously the best and most problematic source for Xiongnu history. The archaeological record partially confirms and partially contradicts specific details, which is exactly what we would expect if some passages are grounded in observation and others in cosmological convention.

**Limitations / open questions**: The boundary between the two strands is not always clear, and the two may be more deeply intertwined than Di Cosmo's model allows. Even the empirical observations were filtered through interpretive categories — the question of whether a "pure" empirical strand can ever be recovered remains methodologically contested.

## Position 3: Sima Qian as Implicit Pacifist vs. Ban Gu as Militarist

**Proponents**: Wang Gungwu; Chusei Suzuki; other scholars of Han foreign policy.

**Argument**: A subsidiary controversy concerns the contrast between Sima Qian's Xiongnu account and Ban Gu's version in the *Han shu* (chapter 94). Ban Gu's text is largely a verbatim copy of *Shih chi* 110 in its first portion, but Ban Gu (a) omits Sima Qian's name entirely, (b) adds characterizations absent from Sima Qian ("the Hsiung-nu had human faces but hearts of beasts"), and (c) explicitly supports a more militant forward policy toward the Xiongnu. Di Cosmo interprets this divergence as evidence of Sima Qian's implicit pacifism and comparative sympathy for understanding the Xiongnu on their own terms — sympathy constrained by political self-censorship (Sima Qian admitted in the chapter 110 colophon that he was "not free to speak openly").

Sima Qian's descriptions of Xiongnu simplicity and egalitarianism function, in Di Cosmo's reading, as "a veiled criticism of the cumbersome legal system put into place by Ch'in Shih Huang-ti and his legalist advisers" — and, by extension, of Wudi's own increasingly harsh rule. The relative objectivity of the descriptive passages, compared to the loaded rhetoric of contemporaries, reflects not political neutrality but a different critical strategy. "It is in the silent contrast between the description of the 'different' and the consciousness of one's own cultural dimension that the historical narrative finds its true and most powerful message."

**Best arguments**: The textual contrast between Sima Qian and Ban Gu is clear and well-documented. The *Shih chi*'s broader critical stance toward Wudi is established by multiple chapters. Sima Qian's personal history (castration for defending Li Ling against the emperor) provides strong biographical motivation for critical distance from official foreign policy.

**Limitations**: The inference from "objective description" to "pacifist sympathy" requires care; Sima Qian's approach may reflect methodological commitments (the astronomer's commitment to empirical recording) as much as political position. The self-censorship itself makes the argument hard to test — we cannot know what Sima Qian would have said freely.

## Position 4: Ethnogenealogy as Invention vs. Widespread Perception

**The question**: Was the genealogy linking Xiongnu to Xia dynasty descendants Sima Qian's personal creation, or did it reflect existing Han perceptions?

**Evidence for "widespread perception"**: Di Cosmo cites a memorial by Chufu Yan, roughly contemporary with Sima Qian, which asserts "ever since the times of Emperor Shun and the rulers of the Hsia, Shang, and Chou dynasties, no attempt has ever been made to order or control them" — suggesting the idea of a continuous northern enemy since legendary antiquity was current among Han intellectuals before Sima Qian formalized it in writing. "It is more likely that Ssu-ma Ch'ien set into writing and detailed historically a perception widespread among his Han contemporaries."

**Evidence for "Sima Qian's invention"**: The specific genealogical linkage (Xiongnu as descendants of Xia ruler Shun-wei) has no parallel in other surviving sources; phonetic connections between Xiongnu ethnonyms (Xiongnu, Hun-yu) and earlier northern peoples' names are tenuous for most of the links; and Sima Qian himself admitted he lacked documentation for the Xiongnu royal house's actual genealogical history. The specific historiographical form — a numbered series of passages tracing a pendular history of north-south confrontation from mythical times to the present — appears to be Sima Qian's literary construction.

O. Maenchen-Helfen ("Archaistic Names of the Hsiung-nu," *Central Asiatic Journal* 6, 1961) notes that some phonetic connections were "archaistic" — retrospective identifications applied by later Chinese scholarship to earlier textual ethnonyms, not genuine etymological relationships.

**Resolution status**: Open. Most scholars accept that Sima Qian synthesized existing perceptions into a formal written genealogy, but debate continues on the degree of his creative intervention. Di Cosmo's position is that the perception was widespread but the formal historical construction — the numbered genealogical chain with its literary apparatus — was Sima Qian's own.

## The Methodological Stakes

The controversy has direct implications for how scholars use the *Shih chi* as a primary source:

- **For Xiongnu history**: If the two strands can be separated, the empirical content of chapter 110 can be used (cautiously, with archaeological cross-checking) as primary evidence for Xiongnu society and economy. If the strands cannot be reliably separated, the entire text must be treated with greater skepticism.

- **For Han intellectual history**: Regardless of one's position on strand separability, the normative-cosmological passages (the ethnogenealogy, the astrological chapter 27) are straightforwardly primary evidence for early Han cosmological thought and the formation of [[sinocentrism|sinocentric]] historical frameworks.

- **For later historiography**: Di Cosmo's argument that "virtually all we know about the rise of the Hsiung-nu and the transformation of the frontier in the Ch'in-Han transition derives, in a historical sense, from the Shih chi" implies that the China-vs.-north binary embedded in chapter 110 has structured the field's basic questions. Scholars who have not recognized this are, Di Cosmo argues, "trapped" in Sima Qian's framework even when they think they are studying the Xiongnu independently.
