# The Emergence of Early Israel — Origins Models and the Archaeological Record

The origins of ancient Israel — who constituted early Israel, where they came from, and
how they established themselves in the Canaanite highlands — is one of the longest-running
and most methodologically contested problems in Levantine archaeology and biblical
studies. It intersects the reliability of the Hebrew Bible as a historical source, the
interpretation of Egyptian and extrabiblical textual evidence, and competing theoretical
frameworks for reading material culture and ethnicity in the archaeological record.

Per the Contradiction Protocol this page **records** the positions; it does not adjudicate.

The primary source for this page is [[sources/dever-beyond-the-texts-2017|Dever (2017)]],
Chapter 3, "The Emergence of Israel in the Light of History," which takes the
**archaeological record as the primary source** and evaluates each model against it.

---

## The Merneptah Stele — The First Textual Reference to Israel (c. 1207 BCE)

The stele of Pharaoh Merneptah (also Merenptah; r. 1213–1203 BCE), found at Thebes and
dated securely to c. 1208 BCE, is the earliest extrabiblical reference to Israel. In a
victory poem celebrating defeat of Libyan invaders, the text reads in part:

> "Israel is laid waste; his seed is not; Hurru is become a widow for Egypt! All lands
> together, they are pacified." (Merneptah Victory Stela, as quoted in Dever 2017, 192)

Three entities — Ashkelon, Gezer, and Yenoam — carry the Egyptian determinative sign for
"city-state." Israel, by contrast, carries the sign for "people" (a plural gentilic),
indicating a socioeconomic group not yet centrally organized as a state. Dever draws four
minimum inferences from the stele:

1. By 1210 at the latest, an entity calling itself "Israel" existed in Canaan, known to
   Egyptian intelligence by that name.
2. It was established enough to be perceived as a possible challenge to Egyptian hegemony.
3. It was not an organized state but a loosely affiliated people — an ethnic group.
4. It was located not in the Egyptian-controlled lowlands but in the more remote central
   hill country.

**Scholarly dispute over the stele's significance.** Biblical minimalists including
Thomas L. Thompson disputed that Merneptah's "Israel" referred to the historical Israel
of later tradition, calling it merely "the earliest known usage of a name" that does not
necessarily identify the people known from Assyrian-period texts. Keith Whitelam similarly
held that the inscription offers "very little unambiguous evidence about the nature and
location of ancient Israel." Dever regards these positions as analytically nihilistic —
if the name is taken as a coincidence, the alternative requires explaining how an Egyptian
scribe invented it independently.

## The Four Classic Models

### 1. Conquest Model (W. F. Albright, G. E. Wright)

**Position.** Drawing on the biblical books of Joshua and Judges, Albright and Wright in
the mid-twentieth century argued that incoming Israelite tribes — recently arrived from
outside Canaan following an Exodus from Egypt — conquered and destroyed the Canaanite
city-states around 1200 BCE. Archaeological destruction layers at major sites were
interpreted as evidence of Israelite military operations.

**Evidence invoked.** Destructions at Hazor (Stratum XIII), Lachish (Stratum VI), Bethel,
and a handful of other sites; the overall collapse of Late Bronze Age Canaanite urban
civilization in the late thirteenth/early twelfth century.

**Weaknesses as Dever presents them.** The book of Joshua lists approximately thirty-seven
sites "taken" in Canaan west of the Jordan. The archaeological record decisively
contradicts this picture:

- Jericho shows no thirteenth- or twelfth-century occupation.
- Ai was abandoned from c. 2300 to 1200 BCE and was literally a ruin heap (*tel*) before
  any Israelite settlers arrived.
- Arad shows no Late Bronze occupation whatsoever.
- Gezer, Shechem, Megiddo, and several others show no Israelite-caused destruction; they
  continued as Canaanite or Philistine sites.
- Only Hazor, and possibly Bethel, show destructions broadly compatible with the biblical
  account — and the agents of those destructions remain unknown.

Dever's conclusion: "There was no statistically significant destruction of Canaan or mass
slaughter of its inhabitants at the end of the Late Bronze Age.… That disposes of the
first model to be discussed, the traditional conquest model." The book of Joshua is
characterized as "nearly all fictitious, of little or no value to the historian … largely
a legend celebrating the supposed exploits of a local folk hero."

### 2. Peaceful Infiltration / Sedentarized Pastoralists (A. Alt, M. Noth, I. Finkelstein, A. Rainey)

**Position.** Alt and Noth pioneered a "territorial history" approach in the 1920s–1940s,
arguing that the first Israelite settlers were pastoral nomads from Transjordan who
initially crossed the Jordan seasonally in search of water and pastures, then gradually
sedentarized in the highland frontier. Weippert carried this forward in the 1970s.
Israel Finkelstein substantially revived this approach in a series of publications from
1991 onward, arguing that the Iron I highland villages represent the **resedentarization
of local pastoral nomads** displaced when Middle Bronze Age city-states collapsed
c. 1550–1450 BCE. Anson Rainey extended the argument by identifying the ancestors of
Israel with the **Shasu** — Transjordanian pastoral nomads attested in Egyptian sources.

**Evidence invoked.** Cyclical oscillation between nomadism and sedentism in the
archaeological record of the southern Levant; survey evidence that the earliest Iron I
sites in Samaria/Ephraim appear on the eastern (steppe-side) margins before spreading
west; the oval or circular plan of several village sites (Finkelstein argued this
mirrored a "tent-circle" precedent); Tell el-ʿUmeiri in Transjordan shows pillar-courtyard
houses (Buildings A and B) with pottery paralleling Cisjordanian Iron I assemblages,
leading Larry Herr to propose a "Reubenite hypothesis" linking early Israel to a
Transjordanian prototype.

**Weaknesses as Dever presents them.**

- *Demographic impossibility.* Finkelstein acknowledges nomadic elements typically
  constitute only 10–15 percent of a dimorphic society's population. His own Late Bronze
  Age population estimate for the central hill country (~10,000–12,000) yields a nomadic
  reservoir of ~1,200 people — wholly insufficient to explain the documented growth from
  ~20,000 (twelfth century) to ~40,000 (eleventh century) highland settlers. As Dever
  writes: "There must have been a substantial in-migration from somewhere else in Canaan,
  even if the entire nomadic population had become sedentarized."
- *No ethnographic support.* Michael Saidel's detailed ethnographic study of Bedouin
  tent-camp layouts demolished Finkelstein's (and Fritz's) argument that the four-room
  pillar-courtyard house derived from a tent prototype: "the parallels drawn … between
  the four-room house of Iron Age I Palestine and Transjordan and the Bedouin tent are
  baseless." More broadly, pastoralists throughout the ancient and modern Middle East
  have consistently resisted sedentarization rather than sought it; they settle mainly
  under state coercion, market surplus pressure, or extreme stress — none of which is
  attested on the Late Bronze/Iron I horizon.
- *No external coercive power.* The Egyptian authorities who had dealt with the Shasu for
  centuries never succeeded in settling them except in small numbers. On the Late
  Bronze–Iron I horizon the urban authorities and market towns Finkelstein's theory
  requires were conspicuously absent.
- *Agricultural competence.* At ʿIzbet Ṣarṭah — Finkelstein's own excavation —
  paleobotanist Baruch Rosen demonstrated from Stratum III that the settlers **from the
  beginning** produced a substantial agricultural surplus and relied on cattle at 43
  percent of animal bones. Rosen's verdict: "the economic system of ʿIzbet Sartah is
  typical of a sedentary settlement based on agriculture and animal breeding." These were
  experienced farmers, not recently settled nomads.
- *The chronological argument against the Reubenite hypothesis.* For Tell el-ʿUmeiri
  (Stratum 12) to serve as a "prototype" for the Cisjordanian hill-country villages,
  it would need to precede them; but the Merneptah stele proves Israel was already in
  place and known to Egyptian intelligence by 1208 BCE, before any reliable Transjordanian
  predecessor can be dated. Dever: the Transjordanian evidence "cannot be regarded as the
  'prototype' of the central hill-country repertoire."
- *Climate argument fails.* A posited drought c. 1400–1200 BCE, far from driving nomads
  to farm, would have made farming even less attractive than herding.

### 3. Peasant Revolt (G. Mendenhall, N. Gottwald)

**Position.** George Mendenhall argued in 1962 (*The Tenth Generation*, 1973) that "there
was no real conquest of Palestine in the sense that has usually been understood; what
happened instead may be termed, from the point of view of the secular historian interested
only in socio-political processes, a **peasant's revolt against the network of interlocking
Canaanite city-states**." Norman Gottwald built this into a magisterial Marxist
sociological synthesis in *The Tribes of Yahweh: A Sociology of the Religion of Liberated
Israel, 1250–1050 BCE* (1979), arguing that the new theological ideal of Yahwism drove
the consolidation of a revolutionary, egalitarian tribal society.

**Evidence invoked.** The Amarna letters' documentation of class tensions, the Habiru
(ʿApiru) as a structural underclass in Late Bronze Age Canaan, and the apparent
discontinuity between Canaanite urbanism and Iron I communitarian village society;
biblical tribal terminology and early poetry (the Song of Deborah, Judg 5) as
contemporary witness to a premonarchic confederation.

**Weaknesses as Dever presents them.**

- No archaeological evidence for sedentarized tribes, neither then nor now.
- The Marxist notion of class warfare is a modern construct; the biblical sociology
  reflects the later writers' world, not that of early Israel.
- The term "tribe" is analytically too vague and has been largely abandoned by
  anthropologists as a comparative category.
- There is no evidence that Yahwistic monotheism existed until late in the monarchy;
  the "liberated Israel" concept projects later theology backward.
- The notion of a revolutionary Yahwistic fervor as the prime mover in cultural change
  is unverifiable archaeologically.

Dever does acknowledge what "was prescient" in Mendenhall and Gottwald: "a sense that
a socioeconomic revolution was taking place and the recognition that the prime movers
were part of the indigenous population of Canaan." Only Gottwald's stress on indigenous
origins remains relevant for archaeological discussions today.

### 4. Indigenous Sedentarization / Agrarian Reform (Dever, Stager, Killebrew, Faust — now consensus)

**Position.** Now the dominant model in the field. Early Israel consisted primarily of
**indigenous peoples of Canaan** — not external invaders, not predominantly Transjordanian
pastoral nomads — who withdrew from the collapsing Late Bronze Age lowland city-state
system to colonize the highland frontier. Dever calls this the "agrarian reform model":
the settlers were a "motley crew" including urban dropouts, Habiru/social bandits,
displaced villagers, refugees from Philistine disruption of the coastal zone, small
numbers of pastoral nomads, and possibly a tiny "exodus group." They were held together
not by biological ethnicity but by an ideology — a communitarian, anti-statist social
vision oriented toward land reform.

Dever's formulation: "It is these local land-hungry peasants and outcasts — not imaginary
pastoralists — who formed the reservoir from which the hill-country colonists were
derived."

**Evidence invoked.**

*The Highland Village Explosion.* Survey data (primarily Finkelstein 1988) document a
nearly sevenfold increase in known sites from Late Bronze to Iron I in the central hills
(Manasseh/Ephraim: from ~30 Late Bronze sites to 218 Iron I sites). The total Iron I
site count across the region grew from ~58 in the Late Bronze Age to ~331–366 by Iron I.
Dever and Stager agree this represents a genuine population influx:

> "This extraordinary increase in population in Iron I cannot be explained only by natural
> population growth of the few Late Bronze Age city-states in the region: there must have
> been a major influx of people into the highlands in the twelfth and eleventh centuries
> BCE.… That many of these villages belonged to premonarchic Israel … is beyond doubt."
> (Stager, as quoted in Dever 2017, 154)

Sites are small (1–3 acres), unfortified, established de novo on virgin soil or long-
abandoned Bronze Age sites, situated on defensible hilltops near springs with extensive
terrace systems — characteristics of experienced subsistence farmers, not nomadic
newcomers.

*Material Continuities with Canaan.* Pottery forms show clear continuity with Late Bronze
Age Canaanite repertoires (figs. 3.15–3.16 in Dever). Wheel-made to handmade production
shifts reflect a move from urban industrial production to cottage industry; the basic
forms (collar-rim store jars, flanged-rim cooking pots) are the same. This continuity
is decisive: "the Iron I Israelite pottery is strongly in the Late Bronze tradition." These
settlers knew Canaanite material culture from the inside.

*Agrarian Frontier.* Lenski and Lenski's sociology of frontiers (1978) explains both the
demography and the ideology: "Those who respond to the challenge of the frontier … are
primarily men with little to lose, with little at stake in the established order."

## The Ethnicity Markers Debate

Whether specific material culture traits can identify an "Israelite" assemblage — and
thus anchor the ethnogenesis of Israel to a traceable archaeological moment — has been
vigorously contested.

### Collared-Rim Store Jars

Large, distinctive storage jars with a reinforcing band (collar) around the neck are
common at well-defined early Israelite hill-country sites. For some decades they were
treated as a diagnostic Israelite ethnic marker. Dever now qualifies this: "these store
jars are attested in Transjordan, and there they can extend into Iron II. Nevertheless,
they are common at well-defined early Israelite sites, whereas they are rare at Iron I
Canaanite sites." Useful but not exclusive; Galilean sites have variant pithoi with
Phoenician affinities.

### The Four-Room (Pillar-Courtyard) House

The pillar-courtyard house — a U-shaped structure with two or three rooms or banks of
rooms surrounding a central courtyard, side rooms set off by monolithic or stacked-stone
pillars — is ubiquitous at Iron I highland village sites. Dever regards continuity of this
house form into Iron II and through the monarchy as "decisive" for identifying a lasting
Israelite cultural tradition. Stager's reading of the form is the best accepted:

> "The pillared house takes its form not from some desert nostalgia monumentalized in stone
> and mudbrick, but from a living tradition. It was first and foremost a successful
> adaptation to farm life: the ground floor had space allocated for food processing, small
> craft production, stabling and storage; the second floor was suitable for dining,
> sleeping, and other activities." (Stager, as quoted in Dever 2017, 200)

Finkelstein's argument that the form derives from a bedouin tent prototype has been
systematically refuted on ethnographic grounds (Saidel). Most specialists accept the
house as a functional agrarian adaptation rooted in local Canaanite building traditions.

### Pig Bone Absence

The striking near-absence of pig bones at Iron I highland Israelite sites — in contrast
to their abundance at Philistine sites (Ekron: 18%; Ashkelon: 2.3%; Ashdod: 10.5%) —
was long treated as a reliable ethnic marker reflecting early food taboos. Dever's
updated assessment is more cautious: "More recent data … require a reassessment of the
whole problem. It now appears that pig bones are also rare in Iron I in (1) many lowland,
continuing Canaanite sites and (2) nonurban Philistine sites. It also seems that by Iron
II, pigs were consumed at Israelite sites in the north, compared to the south." This
suggests the biblical ban on pork may be a relatively late Deuteronomistic codification
(eighth–seventh century), not an early ethnic boundary. The marker is not as diagnostic
as once thought; the Philistine vs. Israelite distinction persists but cannot be
mechanically applied.

### Theoretical Debate on Ethnicity

The broader question of whether material culture can reflect ethnicity at all has been
contested:
- **Minimalist / postmodern position** (Finkelstein after c. 1993; Thompson, Whitelam,
  Lemche): ethnic boundaries are so fluid as to be unrecognizable in the archaeological
  record; "Israelite" is a textual construct; material culture cannot prove identity.
  Thompson: "The concept of [Israelite] ethnicity, however, is a fiction, created by
  writers."
- **Positivist position** (Dever, Stager, Faust, Mazar, Bloch-Smith): Fredrik Barth's
  1969 reformulation of ethnicity emphasizes "self-ascription and ascription by others"
  as the defining criterion; this does leave material correlates. Dever: "The widespread
  notion that material culture in itself cannot reflect ethnicity is absurd, a product
  of postmodernist dogma." The combination of settlement type, house form, ceramic
  tradition, food practices, and the Iron I–II continuity constitutes a recognizable
  cultural trajectory.
- Stager's mediating caution: "Without clear indications from texts, I seriously doubt
  whether any archaeologist can determine the ethnic identification of Iron I villagers
  through material culture remains alone."

Faust's *Israel's Ethnogenesis* (2006) provides the most sustained treatment, arguing
that ethnicity crystallizes in interaction with "the other" — in this case, the
[[philistines|Philistines]] on the coastal plain — and that the material record does
reflect this boundary-marking dynamic.

## Excursus: Rainey's Transjordanian Shasu Model

Anson Rainey argued that early Israel's ancestors were among the Shasu — Egyptian-
attested pastoral nomads dispersed across southern Transjordan and the margins of "the
land of Amurru." Rainey accepted Herr's Reubenite hypothesis and identified the Shasu
with early Israelites settling in Transjordan before crossing the Jordan.

Dever's critique: Rainey "produces absolutely no motivation for their moving en masse
into the central hills or for their becoming rapidly sedentarized in the late
thirteenth–early twelfth century. There were no urban authorities to coerce them, no
market towns for their products, no natural conditions such as systemic drought that we
know of that would have compelled a major change in lifestyle." Even if some Shasu
settled west of the Jordan, their numbers would have been far too small to account for
the documented population explosion. "Both Rainey's and Finkelstein's nomadic
sedentarization theories fail for the same reason: there is no evidence whatsoever for
them, archaeological or textual."

## Excursus: Finkelstein's "Sedentarized Pastoralists" Model in Detail

Finkelstein's synthesis (*The Archaeology of the Israelite Settlement*, 1988; revised
in articles 1991–) argued that long-term cyclical "oscillation" between nomadism and
sedentism explains the Iron I village explosion: displaced pastoral nomads from the
Middle Bronze Age collapse c. 1550–1450 had been invisible in the steppe through the
Late Bronze Age; the Late Bronze collapse triggered their re-sedentarization.

Dever systematically rebuts each point: (1) the oscillation model misreads the earlier
periods — Early Bronze II–III was expanding urban, not nomadic; (2) the demographic
numbers simply do not work; (3) the west-to-east origin for the earliest sites is
unsubstantiated in Finkelstein's own maps; (4) the pottery shows continuity with Late
Bronze Canaanite, not nomadic, assemblages; (5) "innovations" like cisterns and silos
are not new but their intensive and systematic combination in an agrarian system is;
(6) the ethnic-identification agnosticism ignores the unbroken Iron I–II cultural
continuity and the contemporary Merneptah textual evidence.

## Resolution Status

The field has reached a **partial consensus**:

- The conquest model is abandoned — archaeology has effectively disproved a sudden,
  pan-military Israelite conquest of Canaan.
- The peaceful infiltration and peasant revolt models have been largely superseded,
  though each contributed insights (indigenous origins; social rupture).
- The **indigenous origins model** in some form now commands universal assent among
  archaeologists and most biblical scholars.
- Debate continues on: (a) the precise ethnic mix (proportions of lowland refugees,
  pastoral nomads, Transjordanian elements, possible exodus group); (b) the reliability
  of material-culture ethnic markers; (c) the methodological question of whether
  archaeology alone can identify "Israel" without textual support; (d) the significance
  of the Merneptah stele for pre-state Israel's extent and organization.

The controversy interfaces directly with [[the-exodus|The Exodus — Historicity and Date]],
[[ethnogenesis]], and the broader [[bronze-age-collapse-1200bce|Bronze Age Collapse]].

## Noll (2013): Israel as a subset of Canaan

[[sources/noll-canaan-israel-antiquity-2013|K. L. Noll (2013)]] frames the emergence not as
Israel arriving in Canaan but as Israel **being** Canaan: "Canaanite" was a geographic, not
ethnic, label, and "almost all researchers today speak of a single, relatively homogenous
'[[canaan|Canaanite cultural sphere]]', of which Israel was a part." Key points he adds:

- On the **[[merneptah|Merneptah stela]]** (c. 1207 BCE), Israel carries the hieroglyphic
  determinative for a *people* (not a city), placed near Ashkelon, Gezer, and Yanoam. Its
  location is genuinely uncertain: the highland-survey data suggest very few hill-country
  inhabitants, and the disputed relief at Karnak shows the defeated enemy in typical Canaanite
  dress driving a **chariot** — a lowland technology — so Merneptah's Israel may have been a
  lowland group. The "annihilation" boast is formulaic and need not sever this Israel from
  biblical Israel.
- The **12 tribes are geography, not genealogy.** Israel's kinship story is an *eponymic
  legend* — invented genealogy expressing social and political alliance, freely revised as
  relationships changed (the shifting status of Dan, Gad, and the slave-girls' sons). The
  number 12 is artificial.
- The Bible offers **no Bronze Age memory**: its authors had forgotten the Egyptian empire
  ever ruled Canaan, and the patriarchal and conquest narratives are riddled with Iron Age
  anachronisms (Philistines and Beer-sheba in Genesis; uninhabited or unfortified Jericho,
  Ai, Arad, Heshbon, Hebron). So the emergence must be reconstructed from the dirt, not the
  text. This aligns Noll with the indigenous-origins consensus while pushing the biblical
  account further toward late literary artifact than [[sources/dever-beyond-the-texts-2017|Dever]].
