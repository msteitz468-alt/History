# Did the Ottomans Drive European Expansion? (Mikhail's Thesis)

An interpretive dispute over **how central the Ottoman Empire and Islam were to the making of
the early-modern "Western" world** — and specifically whether **fear of the Ottomans drove
European overseas expansion**. Raised provocatively by Alan Mikhail's
[[mikhail-gods-shadow|*God's Shadow*]] (2020), a biography of [[selim-i|Selim I]] that doubles
as a "revisionist account … of the role of Islam and the Ottoman Empire in defining the shape
of the Old and New Worlds."

## Position 1: Mikhail — the Ottomans made the modern world

Mikhail argues that the Ottoman Empire was "the most powerful state on earth" around 1500 and
"the very reason Europeans went to America":

- **The Ottoman monopoly of the eastern trade routes** and their military dominance of the
  Mediterranean "pushed Spain and Portugal out of the Mediterranean, forcing merchants and
  sailors … to become global explorers … all to avoid the Ottomans" — the
  [[columbian-exchange-1492|voyages of discovery]] as a flight from, and flanking of, Ottoman
  power.
- **Columbus as a crusader.** Drawing on the [[reconquista|Reconquista]], Mikhail casts
  Columbus as a "Matamoros" (Moor-slayer) who sailed "imbued with a zeal for waging
  Christianity's war against … Islam"; Iberians carried the **crusading mentality of the
  Reconquista to the New World**, recasting Native Americans (and later the justification of
  African slavery) through the lens of holy war against Islam.
- **The Ottomans catalyzed the Reformation.** The fear and "existential introspection" provoked
  by Ottoman victories (especially [[selim-i|Selim's]] conquests of 1514–17) created "fertile
  ground for challenges to the … religious and political order" — Luther repeatedly invoking
  the "Turk" against the pope.
- **[[selim-i|Selim I]] as a pivotal world-historical figure** — the sultan who made the empire
  majority-Muslim, united sultanate and caliphate, and "made our modern world."
- Against the "[[ottoman-decline-thesis|rise of the West]]" narrative: in 1500 "there was no
  such thing as … 'the West'"; the Ottomans remained central to world affairs until WWI, and
  reading their history through the nineteenth-century "Sick Man" lens is "historically
  indefensible."

## Position 2: The conventional / skeptical view

Mikhail himself calls his account "innovative, even revolutionary," and many specialists regard
its boldest claims as **overstated**:

- **Multiple causes of expansion.** The standard account attributes the Iberian voyages to a
  convergence of motives — the **spice and gold trade** (bypassing Venetian *and* Muslim
  middlemen, but not solely the Ottomans), Portuguese maritime-technical advances down the
  African coast (well underway before Selim), crusading-missionary zeal, and Genoese-Iberian
  commercial drive. Critics argue Mikhail **inflates a real factor (anti-Islamic crusading
  ideology) into the master cause**, and that the Mamluks and Venetians, not specifically the
  Ottomans, controlled the relevant Levant routes in Columbus's day.
- **Causation vs. context.** That Europeans feared and reacted to the Ottomans is uncontested;
  that the Ottomans *caused* the discoveries, the Reformation, and the shape of the modern
  world is a much stronger, contested claim — closer to suggestive reframing than demonstrated
  causation. The "Taino–Muslims" and Columbus-as-jihad-warrior chapters in particular have been
  judged by reviewers as **straining the evidence**.
- **Genre.** *God's Shadow* is a popular, argumentative work; its corrective intent (against
  Islamophobia and the "rise of the West") is widely welcomed, but its sweeping global claims
  are read by academics as **provocation more than consensus**.

## State of the debate

The debate is **open**. There is broad agreement that the Ottomans were a great power long
underweighted in "rise of the West" narratives, and that anti-Islamic crusading ideology shaped
Iberian expansion — points Mikhail shares with the rejection of the
[[ottoman-decline-thesis|"decline"]] paradigm. What remains contested is the **strength of the
causal claims**: whether the Ottomans were *the* reason for European expansion and *the* makers
of the modern world, or one powerful factor among several. The dispute is as much about
**historical method and emphasis** (provocative reframing vs. cautious multicausal explanation)
as about facts.
