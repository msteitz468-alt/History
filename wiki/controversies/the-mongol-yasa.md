# Did the "Great Yasa" of Chingiz Khan Exist?

A scholarly dispute over whether [[genghis-khan|Chingiz Khan]] promulgated a single,
written, permanently binding **law code** — the **"Great Yasa"** (*yasa* / *jasagh*,
"order, decree, law") — that served as a foundational institution of the
[[mongol-empire|Mongol Empire]]. The debate is fundamentally about **source reliability**:
no copy of the Yasa survives, and the question is what, if anything, the fragmentary and
late references to it actually attest. [[morgan-the-mongols|David Morgan's]] influential
skeptical case has reshaped the field.

## The traditional view

The long-received account holds that Chingiz Khan laid down a code of laws, probably at
the *quriltai* of **1206** (perhaps supplemented later); that this **Great Yasa was
written down** and kept in the treasuries of the Mongol princes for consultation; that it
was regarded as permanently and universally binding; and that, though no complete copy
survives, it can be substantially **reconstructed** by collecting the "fragments" quoted
in later sources. On this view the Yasa is one of the essential institutional foundations
of the empire — the Mongols' equivalent of a constitution. (The fullest modern defense of
a "positive" view is Paul Ratchnevsky's.)

## Morgan's skeptical view

[[morgan-the-mongols|Morgan]] argues there is "little convincing evidence that a written
legal code ever did exist." His case:

- **The two prime sources for the 1206 institution do not support it.** The *Secret
  History's* account of 1206 does **not use the word *yasa*** for a code; it grants the
  judge **Shigi-Qutuqu** the right to decide cases of robbery and fraud and to record his
  decisions in a "blue book" (*koko debter*) — i.e. *ad hoc* **case law**, not a general
  code. Rashid al-Din's account of 1206 likewise contains **no reference to a Yasa**;
  earlier scholars (Riasanovsky) misdated and mistranslated his material.
- **The "fragments" collapse to one source.** David Ayalon showed that essentially all
  the supposed fragments of the Yasa derive from **Juwayni** — and Juwayni's chapter is
  about **military training, communications, and taxation**, Chingiz's administrative
  decrees, not a legal code.
- **The taboo theory is implausible.** To save the code one must posit (Ayalon) that its
  text was secret/taboo — i.e. that the Mongols enforced a code whose contents were
  unknowable to those bound by it.

What probably existed, Morgan suggests, was (1) a body of **unwritten Mongol customary
law** (which Chaghatai was famed for upholding), evolving before and after Chingiz; (2)
**case law** of the sort Shigi-Qutuqu recorded; and (3) **administrative decrees**
preserved for consultation. These were **later misremembered as a single "Great Yasa,"**
a belief that hardened over the following centuries (some Chinese sources even ascribe a
yasa to Ögedei rather than Chingiz).

## The intermediate position

Many scholars now accept much of Morgan's deflation while holding that "the Yasa" usefully
names the **real, if uncodified, body of Chingizid law and custom** that carried genuine
authority across the khanates — so that the dispute is partly about the word "code." The
consensus has shifted decisively **away from** the reconstructable-written-text view and
toward an evolving-custom-plus-decrees model, even where scholars resist Morgan's strongest
formulations.

## Significance

The question matters beyond philology: a written Yasa would make the Mongols precocious
**state-builders with a constitution**; its absence fits the picture of an empire run by
custom, charisma, and pragmatic decree — bearing on how "institutionalized" the Mongol
state really was (a theme also of [[mongol-empire|the empire's]] historiography and the
[[kautilyan-statecraft|comparison]] with codified statecraft traditions).
