## The Dispute

In 1986, philosopher Jürgen Habermas published a broadside in the weekly *Die Zeit*
accusing historians Andreas Hillgruber and Ernst Nolte of "apologetic tendencies"
in recent work on the Nazi period, and charged that a broader neoconservative
current within the West German historical profession sought to replace two decades
of critical history with a new historical nationalism. The controversy engulfed
nearly every prominent German historian within seven months, playing out in
specialist journals, conferences, and the mainstream press.

**Nolte's position**: In works including *Germany and the Cold War*, Nolte drew
sweeping comparisons that tended to relativize the Holocaust — suggesting, for
example, that American conduct in Vietnam was an "essentially crueller version of
Auschwitz." During the controversy itself he went further, proposing without
documentary support that Hitler's genocide of the Jews was an "'Asiatic' action"
provoked by Nazi awareness of Stalin's "Asiatic methods," and that "with the sole
exception of the technical process of the gassing," nothing about the Holocaust was
"original." Nolte's specific claims were, per Paxton and Hessler, "effectively
refuted during the ensuing debate."

**Hillgruber's position**: A distinguished historian known for path-breaking work
on Hitler's strategic thinking, Hillgruber was — in the assessment cited by Paxton
and Hessler (citing Gordon Craig's contemporary review) — "quite unfairly targeted"
by Habermas's original article, with one American commentator suggesting Habermas
may not have engaged seriously with the actual content of Hillgruber's book before
attacking it.

**Habermas's position**: Rooted in his broader philosophical commitment (developed
in *The Theory of Communicative Action*, 1981) to reason as fundamentally social and
communicative, Habermas viewed the attempt to historically relativize or
contextualize the Holocaust as an assault on the postwar West German commitment to
critical self-examination and democratic, Enlightenment values, replacing it with a
chauvinistic nationalism that elevated pride above communicative rationality.

## Unresolved Core Problem

Beyond the specific claims at issue, the debate exposed a genuine, unresolved
tension articulated by Christian Meier, then president of the German Historical
Association: as the generation with direct responsibility for Nazi crimes receded,
an increasing number of Germans had "nothing to do with the atrocities of that
time" and questioned why they should "belong to a stigmatized people" — even as the
historical understanding of the crimes' scale and character continued to deepen.
How to integrate the Holocaust into a German national history that also included
the successful postwar West German democracy remained, in this source's assessment,
a live problem rather than one settled by the Historikerstreit's outcome.

## Resolution Status

Classified here as **partially-resolved**: Nolte's specific comparative claims were
substantively discredited within the historical profession, and the episode
reinforced (rather than displaced) the dominant critical-historical treatment of
Nazism in German academic life through the following decades. However, the
underlying question of how a nation integrates a genocide into a continuous
national historical identity — the deeper issue Habermas raised — was not resolved
by the controversy and recurred in subsequent German debates (e.g., the 1990s
controversy over novelist Christa Wolf and East German intellectuals' collaboration
with the GDR).

## Historiography

Charles S. Maier's *The Unmasterable Past: History, Holocaust, and German National
Identity* (1988) and Peter Baldwin's edited volume *Reworking the Past: Hitler, the
Holocaust, and the Historians' Debate* (1990) are the standard English-language
treatments. Gordon Craig's contemporary "The War of the German Historians" (*New
York Review of Books*, Jan. 15, 1987) is the source Paxton and Hessler draw on most
directly for the assessment of Hillgruber's unfair treatment. This controversy sits
within the longer arc of West German *Vergangenheitsbewältigung* ("coping with the
past"), which also included the 1958 Lithuanian-Jews SS trial, the 1961 Eichmann
trial, and the 1963–1965 Auschwitz trials — earlier catalysts for public reckoning
with Nazi crimes that preceded and set the stage for the 1986 controversy.
