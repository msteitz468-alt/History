# Was the Roman Near East Graeco-Roman or Fundamentally Native?

A genuine interpretive dispute over how to read the culture of the Roman Near East (roughly
Roman Syria, Palestine, Arabia and Mesopotamia, 1st c. BCE – 7th c. CE): as a broadly
Graeco-Roman world with a native substrate, or as an enduringly Semitic/Arab/Iranian world
wearing a Graeco-Roman veneer. The dispute is one of emphasis and direction of influence more
than of the underlying facts, which both sides largely share.

## Position A — The Graeco-Roman / Hellenization reading

Associated most prominently with **Fergus Millar**, *The Roman Near East, 31 BC–AD 337* (1993),
and the broader tradition of classical scholarship on the region.

**Best statement of the position:** the documentary record of the Roman Near East — Greek and
Latin inscriptions, civic institutions (the *boulē*, colonial charters), the language of the
elite — shows a region substantially integrated into Graeco-Roman civic and cultural forms.
Where native evidence is thin or ambiguous, the responsible historian declines to reconstruct a
robust "native culture" that the sources do not attest; the claim that there was a unified
"Syrian" culture spanning the Syriac-literate zone and Roman Syria "goes beyond our evidence."
Persian and other non-Roman sources warrant the same source-critical caution as any other.

## Position B — Ball's orientalization / native-continuity reading

Stated in Warwick Ball, *[[ball-rome-in-the-east-2000|Rome in the East]]* (2000); see
[[orientalization-of-rome]].

**Best statement of the position:** classical scholarship is "necessarily Eurocentric" and
perpetuates a Gibbon-era bias in new words. It over-reads a proportionally tiny corpus of Greek
inscriptions (a lingua franca, like English in modern India) into evidence of Greek "character,"
while discounting the far larger native epigraphic record (some 18,000 Safaitic inscriptions
alone) and, above all, the **material remains** — which the region possesses in greater
abundance than any province save North Africa and which show native continuity across the Roman
conquest. Source-criticism is applied asymmetrically (Josephus and Persian sources flagged as
"one-sided"; equally partial Greek and Latin sources are not). On this reading the Roman
presence is a veneer, and the deep current runs east-to-west.

## Shared factual ground, framed per side

Both sides accept: the prevalence of Greek as an administrative language; the existence of
native cults, dynasties and languages; the persistence of Semitic place-names that resurface
after the Muslim conquest; the monumental architecture of the client kingdoms; and the eastern
origin of the Severan emperors and of Christianity. The disagreement is over what this
**adds up to** — whether a Greek inscription evidences a Hellenised city (A) or merely a scribe
(B); whether native names in a minority evidence a Semitic majority (B) or cannot bear that
weight (A); whether Rome "founded" its eastern cities or merely refounded older ones (a factual
continuity both grant, but weight oppositely).

Related sub-disputes Ball raises, each contestable on its own terms: the reality of an organised
overland **"Silk Road"** (Ball: a modern myth — see [[roman-eastern-trade]]); the "Roman"
character of **[[gandharan-art|Gandharan art]]**; whether **[[philip-the-arab|Philip the Arab]]**
rather than Constantine was the first Christian emperor; and the derivation of much "imperial
Roman" architecture (colonnaded streets, the tetrapylon, the temenos-temple) from eastern forms.

## Resolution status

**Open.** Ball's book is a deliberately revisionist synthesis by an archaeologist writing
against the classical mainstream; it has been read as a valuable corrective on the weight of
material evidence and as overstated in its inversion. The debate turns on methodology — the
relative evidential weight of texts versus material remains — as much as on any single fact.

**Curator's note (2026-07-08):** recorded per the wiki's neutral-controversy convention; the
positions are stated in their strongest form without adjudication. Ball's descriptive
archaeology is broadly reliable; his largest interpretive inversions (Silk Road, Philip's
Christianity, the near-total superficiality of Romanisation) are where he is most exposed and
should be cited as *his* argument, not as settled fact.
