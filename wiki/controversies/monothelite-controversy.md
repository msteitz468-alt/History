# Monothelite Controversy

The **Monothelite Controversy** was a major theological and political dispute in the seventh-century Byzantine Empire. It centered on the question of whether Jesus Christ had one will (*Monothelitism*) or two distinct wills (*Dyothelitism*) corresponding to his divine and human natures. Promoted by Emperor [[heraclius|Heraclius]] and Patriarch Sergius I of Constantinople as a political compromise to reconcile Monophysites with Chalcedonians, Monothelitism failed to achieve religious unity and instead caused a major schism between Constantinople, Rome, and the Western Church, culminating in the Sixth Ecumenical Council (680–681).

## Background and Context

Following the Council of Chalcedon (451), the Eastern Empire was divided between Chalcedonian orthodox Christians (who held that Christ possessed two natures, divine and human) and Monophysites (prevalent in Egypt and Syria, who held that Christ possessed a single nature). This division threatened imperial security, as Monophysites felt alienated from the central government in Constantinople.

During the final Persian War, Emperor Heraclius sought to restore religious unity. Under his guidance, Patriarch Sergius developed a compromise formula: **Monoenergism** (asserting that Christ had two natures but a single divine-human activity). When this was rejected, Sergius proposed **Monothelitism** (asserting that Christ had two natures but a single divine-human will). In 638, Heraclius issued the *Ekthesis*, an imperial edict officially promulgating Monothelitism as the doctrine of the empire.

## The Theological Dispute

### Position A: Monothelitism (One Will)
Monothelitism argued that since Christ was a single person, he must possess a single, integrated will. A separate human will in Christ, they argued, could potentially oppose his divine will, introducing conflict into the person of the Savior.
- **Key Proponents**: Patriarch Sergius I of Constantinople, Emperor Heraclius, Patriarch Cyrus of Alexandria, Pope Honorius I (who tentatively supported the formula in letters, leading to his posthumous condemnation).
- **Arguments**: Asserting a single will preserved the unity of Christ's person and provided a bridge to win back Monophysites in Egypt and Syria, who rejected any terminology that separated Christ's divinity from his humanity.

### Position B: Dyothelitism (Two Wills)
Dyothelitism argued that since a will is a property of a *nature*, not a *person*, Christ must have two wills—one divine and one human—corresponding to his two complete natures. To deny Christ a human will was to deny his full, authentic human nature.
- **Key Proponents**: **Maximus the Confessor**, Pope Martin I, Sophronius of Jerusalem.
- **Arguments**: If Christ lacked a human will, his human obedience to the Father was an illusion, and human freewill was not redeemed. Christ's human will was distinct but always in perfect, free submission to his divine will.

## Resolution and Political Consequences

The controversy had severe political ramifications. While the *Ekthesis* failed to win back the Monophysites before they were conquered by the Arabs (see [[battle-of-yarmuk-636|Yarmouk]]), it directly alienated the Western Church. In 649, Pope Martin I and Maximus the Confessor convened the **Lateran Council** in Rome, which formally condemned Monothelitism and the imperial edicts of Heraclius's successor, Constans II (who had issued the *Typos* in 648, banning all discussion of the wills of Christ).

In response, Constans II had Pope Martin I and Maximus the Confessor arrested, brought to Constantinople, tried for treason, and exiled. Maximus was mutilated (his tongue cut out and his right hand amputated) to prevent him from speaking or writing.

The controversy was finally resolved at the **Sixth Ecumenical Council** (Constantinople III, 680–681), convoked by Emperor Constantine IV. The council rejected Monothelitism, condemned its major advocates (including the deceased Pope Honorius I), and declared:
> *"We likewise preach two natural wills in him [Christ], and two natural operations, without division, without change, without separation, without confusion."*

This resolution restored communion between Rome and Constantinople but marked the failure of the imperial policy to unify the Monophysite East, which was by then permanently under Muslim rule.
