# The Great Divergence — When and Why Did Europe Pull Ahead?

A **standing controversy** of early modern world history, and the unifying
question of much of [[bentley-cwh-v6-2015|CWH VI]]. The term, from Kenneth
Pomeranz's *The Great Divergence* (2000), names the dispute over **when** the most
advanced regions of Western Europe pulled decisively ahead of the most advanced
regions of Asia in productivity and living standards, and **why**. The stakes are
large: the answer shapes whether modern economic growth is read as the fruit of
unique European virtues or of contingent conjuncture and global extraction — and
thus bears directly on [[eurocentrism|Eurocentrism]] as a problem in world
history.

## The Dispute

The debate is both **chronological** (did Europe diverge gradually from the late
Middle Ages, or suddenly around 1800?) and **causal** (institutions? science?
wages? geography? coal? colonies and slavery?). It turns heavily on
**comparison**: matching like with like (e.g. the Yangzi Delta with England,
rather than "China" with "Europe") to avoid the old Marx/Weber framing of
extractive Asian despotisms versus commercial Europe.

## Positions

**1. Late and contingent (Pomeranz and the "California School").** Until c.1800
the most advanced regions of China resembled England in land and product markets,
agricultural productivity, wages, and living standards (the
[[industrious-revolution|industrious revolution]] occurred in both). Divergence
came late and rested on two contingencies England happened to enjoy: accessible
**coal**, and the **New World** — whose plantation resources and slave labor
(see [[plantation-economy]], [[atlantic-slave-trade]]) relieved the ecological
constraints (the "land constraint") that East Asian cores could not escape. CWH VI
broadly adopts this stance: Trivellato (trade organization), Black (warfare; see
[[military-revolution-thesis]]), and Sugihara & Wong (industrious revolutions) all
locate divergence late and emphasize Asian dynamism into the eighteenth century.

**2. Early and institutional/structural (Landes, North; partly Allen).** Europe's
advantage was deep and endogenous — superior property rights, legal and political
institutions, the Scientific Revolution, a culture of innovation, or (Robert
Allen) a distinctive **high-wage economy** that uniquely incentivized
labor-saving machinery. On this view, divergence was long in the making and Asia
lacked key preconditions.

**3. Composite / multi-causal.** Many recent scholars combine elements: real but
modest early advantages, amplified after 1800 by coal, colonies, high wages, and
the political economy of European inter-state competition. Sugihara's framing of
**two paths** — a Western **capital-intensive** and an East Asian
**labor-intensive** path to industrialization — reframes the question from "who
won" to "how each path formed."

## The long-run sequel (CWH VII)

[[mcneill-cwh-v7-2015|CWH VII]] carries the question past the moment of divergence
into the [[long-19th-century|nineteenth]] and [[contemporary|twentieth]] centuries.
Sugihara's chapter on global [[industrialization]] reframes the divergence as the
**formation of two paths** — a Western **capital- and resource-intensive** route
(built on "coal and North America") and an East Asian **labor-intensive,
resource-saving** route grounded in intensive rice farming and household
proto-industry — that then *interwove*. On this reading the divergence is not a
permanent European victory but a phase: industrialization's rapid post-1950
diffusion, above all the East Asian ascent (China surpassing the United States as
the world's largest energy user in 2010; see [[great-acceleration]]), represents a
**partial reversal** that restores Asia toward the ~50% share of world population
and output it held before 1800. CWH VII thus reinforces the late-and-contingent
position while shifting the analytical question from "why did Europe win?" to "how
did each regional path form, and how is the gap now narrowing?"

## Status

**Open.** There is growing (not universal) agreement that the *gap* was small
before c.1750–1800 and widened sharply thereafter, but no consensus on the causal
weight of coal, colonies/slavery, institutions, wages, and science. Quantitative
comparison (GDP, wages, productivity, life expectancy) has narrowed some claims
without settling the question.
