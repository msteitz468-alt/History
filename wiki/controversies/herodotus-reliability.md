# The Reliability of Herodotus as a Historical Source

Whether — and how far — **Herodotus of Halicarnassus** (c. 484–425 BCE) can be trusted is the
foundational source-reliability dispute of ancient Greek history, because for vast stretches
of the Archaic and early Classical world (the [[achaemenid-empire|Achaemenid empire]], the
[[ionian-revolt-499bce|Ionian Revolt]], [[battle-of-marathon-490bce|Marathon]], the Scythians,
Egypt) he is the **only continuous narrative source**. This page is one of the standing
controversies of the wiki; [[boardman-cah-v4-1988|CAH Vol. IV]] (Chs. 8 Murray, 9 Hammond)
treats his method at length.

## Positions

### 1. The trustworthy enquirer
Hammond (CAH IV Ch. 9) defends Herodotus as **contemporary history "in the fullest sense"**:
his account derived from eyewitnesses and was recited back to audiences "which contained
veterans of those wars" and could correct him. Moments of action against Persia "were brief
in time and exciting in character, and must have stayed vivid in men's minds"; so "the facts
related by Herodotus are very likely to be correct," and **his sequence of events** (Eretria
falling days before Marathon; the shield-signal and the race to Athens) "is taken to be
almost certainly correct." On this view, modern attempts to **rearrange** his chronology
(Grote onward) are the error — Herodotus *and* Thucydides agreed, and "it is rash to dismiss
both as mistaken."

### 2. The unreliable partisan ("Father of Lies")
Already in antiquity **Plutarch** wrote *On the Malice of Herodotus*, accusing him of being
"a friend of the barbarian," and of unfairly blackening states such as **Corinth** while
flattering **Athens** (which rewarded his recitation, tradition says, with ten talents).
Modern critics add his weakness for the **marvellous and miraculous** (Hippias' tooth,
underwater swimmers, dream after dream), his impossible **numbers** (90,000 in the Persian
fleet; 6,000 Argive dead at [[battle-of-sepea-494bce|Sepea]]), and an alleged **bias against
the Ionians**.

### 3. The oral-tradition / source-critical reading
Murray (CAH IV Ch. 8) reframes the question. Herodotus' history "rests essentially on **oral
traditions and must be analysed appropriately**," not with methods made for documents. The
*kinds* of tradition differ: the **mainland** *logioi andres* were aristocratic, politically
biased but rational; **Delphi's** were priestly and moralizing; the **East Greek** traditions
were non-aristocratic, structured as **folk-tales** with moral patterns — which is why his
Ionian narrative is "less reliable... more difficult to handle." Crucially, the demoralized
self-image of the defeated Ionians ("ashamed of being Ionian," the myth of Ionian softness)
is **a distortion Herodotus inherited, not one he created** — "he is himself a witness to the
demoralization of his own society." Herodotus is thus the last and greatest **logopoios**
(maker of tales) who is also a **logographos** (writer) — fixing, and so beginning to destroy,
the oral art on which he drew. The **Histiaeus** material is the clearest case: a separate,
largely fabulous *biographical logos* (the Trickster type, like Odysseus) not to be confused
with the real history of the revolt.

## Lines of test

- **Cross-checks confirm the framework.** The **Persepolis Fortification Tablets** confirm
  Herodotus' knowledge of Persian officials' names and marriages (e.g. **Datis/Datiya**,
  Megabates); archaeology confirms destruction levels (the burning of [[sardis|Sardis]], the
  siege-mound at Paphus, the abandonment of Miletus's harbour) and the **Marathon arrowheads**
  in the Soros fill.
- **But invented episodes recur** — the Lindian Temple-Chronicle's miraculous siege by Datis,
  Alexander I of Macedon's self-serving tale of murdered Persian envoys (which Hammond calls
  "certainly fictitious"), the tattooed-slave message that "began" the revolt.
- **No better source existed.** Murray concludes that no ancient literary or material evidence
  "either suggests there was a better account of the Ionian Revolt than that of Herodotus, or
  offers a basis on which to construct one" — the idiosyncrasies of his narrative are
  explicable *only* if he did **not** use earlier written histories.

## Resolution status

**Open.** The modern consensus is neither the naïve "Father of History" nor the dismissive
"Father of Lies" but the **source-critical** position: Herodotus is indispensable and broadly
reliable on **events and sequence**, far less so on **numbers, motive, and the structural and
moral patterning** he imposed — and his reliability varies systematically with the *type of
tradition* behind each passage. The debate (Detlev Fehling's invented "source-citations";
François Hartog's "imaginary Scythians"; the defence of his autopsy) remains live.
