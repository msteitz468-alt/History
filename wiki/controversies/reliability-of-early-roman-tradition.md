# Reliability of the Early Roman Annalistic Tradition

## The Dispute

How much of the Roman annalistic tradition about the regal period (753–509 BCE)
and the early Republic (509–c. 350 BCE) reflects genuine historical memory, and
how much is later invention?

The question divides over methodology, source evaluation, and historical
epistemology. The stakes are high: if the annalistic tradition is largely
fabricated, the early Republic (including the Struggle of the Orders, the Gallic
Sack, the Samnite Wars) must be reconstructed almost entirely from institutional
survivals and archaeology. If it is partially reliable, the narrative has real
content.

## The Skeptical Position (Ogilvie, Drummond)

**Evidence and argument.** [[early-roman-source-criticism|CAH VII.2 Ch. 1]]
(Ogilvie & Drummond) makes the case that:

- All surviving accounts of the regal period and early Republic were written by
  authors of the first century BCE and later (the "big annalists": Antias,
  Quadrigarius, Macer) drawing on scarce and unreliable earlier sources.
- The authentic documentary base (consular list, pontifical records, a handful of
  inscriptions) could not have supported the detailed narrative that survives.
- The techniques of fabrication are demonstrable: retrojection of later experience,
  family aggrandizement (the Valerii, the Fabii), Greek parallels, repetition of
  episodes, literary elaboration.
- Even Q. Claudius Quadrigarius — himself an annalist — began his history at
  390 BCE, evidently regarding earlier material as largely legendary.

**Scope of skepticism.** The skeptics do not claim everything is false, but insist
that the **burden of proof** lies with those who accept a specific datum, not with
those who doubt it. Without a demonstrable mechanism of preservation, a tradition
should not be accepted as authentic.

## The Moderate Position (Cornell, Momigliano partially)

**Evidence and argument.** Cornell (Chs. 6–8 of the same volume) argues that:

- The structural framework of the tradition — the sequence of constitutional
  development, the general pattern of expansion, the nature of the institutions —
  is coherent and broadly confirmed by institutional survivals, comparative
  material, and archaeology where it can be tested.
- The consular fasti, though not without problems (gaps in the early decades, the
  "Dictator Years," the interkalary years controversy), derive from a common
  source of probable early authenticity.
- Oral tradition, clan memories, and priestly records, though later elaborated,
  provided a genuine core of information that the annalists expanded rather than
  invented from nothing.

**Scope.** The moderate position accepts that most specific narrative detail is
unverifiable and often fictional, but insists that the broad outline of
constitutional and military history from c. 400 BCE is usable, and that even
earlier periods preserve genuine institutional memory.

## The Conservative Position (Mommsen tradition)

Theodor Mommsen and his successors argued for authentic continuity from the
beginning of the Republic: the consular fasti reflect genuine records, the
pontifical tablets preserved real information, and the discrepancies between
sources reflect variant traditions rather than universal fabrication. This
position is now largely abandoned in its strong form but informs modern defenders
of fasti authenticity.

## The Central Evidential Disputes

1. **The Gallic Sack of 390 BCE** (or 387 by Greek reckoning): Did it destroy the
   early records? Livy says yes (*vi.1.2*); Ogilvie & Drummond note the sack is
   itself of uncertain magnitude and the archaeological evidence shows *no trace*
   of a conflagration — casting doubt on both the scale of destruction and its
   role as an explanation of lost documents.

2. **The consular fasti before c. 450 BCE**: The alternation of consuls with
   "consular tribunes" in the fasti (445–367 BCE) and the "Dictator Years" represent
   known problem zones. Whether the fasti for the very early Republic are genuine
   or reconstructed is unresolved.

3. **The Struggle of the Orders narrative**: Is the detailed account of plebeian
   agitation, secessiones, and tribunician prosecutions in the fifth century
   genuine institutional history or a Gracchan-era retrojection? The skeptics
   incline heavily toward the latter.

4. **Porsenna's capture of Rome** (c. 508 BCE): The main tradition (heroic
   resistance) vs. the counter-tradition (Tacitus, Pliny) that Porsenna actually
   took Rome is the clearest example of the annalistic tradition actively suppressing
   embarrassing facts.

## Drummond's Chapter 5 Analysis: The Consular Fasti

CAH VII.2 Ch. 5 (Drummond) offers the volume's most detailed analysis of the fasti
as a historical source. Key arguments:

**Why the fasti are probably authentic from c. 509 BCE.** The consular list has
three independent lines of evidence for early authenticity:
1. The names in the early fasti match the family patterns known from independent
   sources — the great patrician houses (Valerii, Fabii, Cornelii, Quinctii) dominate
   in proportions that reflect their historical power, with no suspicious under- or
   over-representation of families whose later prominence might explain retrojection.
2. The "archaic" character of some names — extinct *gentes* or rare praenomina that
   had no motive for a later forger to invent — suggests a genuine record rather than
   wholesale construction.
3. The systematic alternation of the fasti with the consular tribunate years (444–367)
   and the internal consistency of the "Dictator Years" problem (isolated years where
   the fasti record dictators but no consuls — probably an intercalation problem, not
   an invention) argue for a pre-existing record that later editors struggled to
   reconcile, not a single constructed narrative.

**The "plebeian names in the early fasti" problem.** A handful of names in the
earliest fasti years have cognates among later plebeian families (e.g., Brutus,
Lucretius). The "patrician coup" hypothesis treats these as real plebeians later
excluded. Drummond argues: (a) patrician families of the same name, later extinct,
are the most parsimonious explanation; (b) the naming convention does not allow
confident identification of patrician vs. plebeian status from a name alone in this
period; (c) the alternative requires positing a dramatic reversal in the fifth
century for which the sources provide no evidence. The fasti are therefore broadly
usable as a skeleton from 509 BCE, though specific names may be corrupt or doubtful.

**Fifth-century narrative is largely unusable.** Even while defending the
fasti's framework, Drummond is unsparing about the narrative detail: the specific
campaigns, senatorial debates, and tribunician agitations recorded in Livy and
Dionysius for 509–400 BCE are mostly fictitious. The annalistic tradition invented
episodes by the technique of "analogical elaboration" — giving each institution its
standard later functions in contexts where it could not yet have had them, retrojecting
Gracchan constitutional vocabulary, and duplicating real later events as fictitious
earlier precedents (the Valerian-Horatian laws of 449 duplicate the Publilian law
of 339). Concretely: the probability that any specific debate, prosecution, or
diplomatic episode recorded for c. 450 BCE is authentic is low.

## Cornell's Chapter 7 Analysis: Fourth-Century Chronology

CAH VII.2 Ch. 7 Appendix (Cornell) contains a detailed analysis of the chronological
discrepancies within the fourth-century Roman tradition — a set of problems that bear
directly on source reliability.

**The Varronian vs. Polybian dating.** The Roman tradition's "Varronian" chronology
(the standard dating system derived from Varro's reconstruction, placing the foundation
of Rome in 753 BCE) places the **Gallic Sack in 390 BCE**. Polybius (I.6.2), drawing
on a Greek synchronism (probably Philistus or Timaeus), places the sack in **387/386 BCE**
— synchronized with the Peace of Antalcidas and the siege of Rhegium by Dionysius I
of Syracuse. The discrepancy is exactly four years.

**The "Dictator Years" (Varronian 333, 324, 309, 301 BCE).** The *Fasti Capitolini*
record four years in the fourth century in which a dictator and *magister equitum*
held office instead of consuls — "Dictator Years." Cornell demonstrates these are
**late fabrications**: they appear in no source other than the *Fasti Capitolini*,
and the constitutional anomaly they represent (a dictator naming the year in place
of consuls) is impossible to reconcile with all other evidence for the dictatorship.
Their function was to **lengthen the Varronian chronology by four years** to reconcile
the internal arithmetic of the fasti with evidence that the list was too short after
the Gallic Sack.

**The "five-year anarchy" (Varronian 375–371 BCE).** The *Fasti Capitolini* also
record five consecutive years of "anarchy" during the Licinian-Sextian rogations in
which no curule magistrates were elected. Diodorus records only one such year. Cornell:
the five-year period is a similar device — an extension of the chronology to make
the arithmetic work. A real constitutional standoff occurred; five years of total
magisterial vacancy did not.

**The consequence.** The Varronian system adopted both the four Dictator Years and
the extended anarchy, placing the Gallic Sack four years earlier than the Greek
evidence supports. All **absolute dates in the Varronian framework from 390 to c.
301 BCE should be understood as approximately four years too early**. The relative
sequence within the fasti (which consul held office in which relative year) remains
reliable; it is only the absolute location in Julian years that is distorted.

**Implications for the reliability debate.** The *Fasti Capitolini* — the inscribed
consular list set up in the Forum under Augustus — contain demonstrable late
fabrications in the fourth century. This does not invalidate the fasti as a whole
(Drummond's defense of their overall authenticity from c. 509 still holds), but it
requires that each element be evaluated independently. Late-Republican chronographers
had access to conflicting sources differing by a few years and resolved the conflicts
by inserting years rather than critically adjudicating them. The result is a
chronological framework that is trustworthy in its relative sequence but not in its
absolute dates for the period 390–301 BCE.

## Resolution Status

**Open.** The CAH VII.2 editors explicitly adopted a pluralist methodology
precisely because the dispute could not be resolved — they gave separate chapters
to scholars (Torelli, Momigliano, Drummond, Cornell) whose methodological
commitments differed. The trend of scholarship since the 1970s has moved toward
greater skepticism about narrative content and greater reliance on archaeology and
institutional analysis, while accepting that the consular fasti provide a usable
chronological skeleton from the mid-fifth century onward.

## Woolf, *Rome: An Empire's Story* (2012)

[[woolf-rome-empires-story-2012|Greg Woolf]] reinforces the skeptical pole with a firm archaeology-first rule. Surviving accounts of the regal period have a “mythic quality”; none was written less than three centuries after the supposed foundation of the Republic—“Our only real control on these myths is archaeological.” Almost no Greek writer mentions Rome before 300 BCE, and no native historian before 200 BCE, by which time Rome already dominated Italy; ancient narratives are “transparently written in the knowledge of (and often to explain) Rome’s imperial destiny,” so “a reliable account of early Rome must begin from archaeology.” Later dating often depends on synchronism with Greek history (tyrant expulsions; Veii ~ Athens–Sparta; Gallic sack ~ northern temple-pillagers); stories of self-sacrifice and social solidarity comfort a late Republic torn by civil conflict—“hardly reliable history.” Position only among Ogilvie/Drummond/Cornell poles; does not adjudicate fasti authenticity.
