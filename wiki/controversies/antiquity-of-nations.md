# The Antiquity of Nations — Are Nations Modern or Ancient?

How old are nations and nationalism? Since the field's "spirited revival" in the 1980s a
"great fault line" has run through it, in Azar Gat's phrase. On one side, **modernists** hold
the nation a creation of modernity — born of the French and Industrial revolutions (or, at the
earliest, the early-modern period). On the other, **traditionalists** ("primordialists" or
"perennialists") hold that nationhood "existed before modernity… perhaps as far back as
antiquity, and not only in Europe but throughout the world." The dispute is one of
**periodization** (when did the nation begin?) but is entangled with **definition** (what
counts as a nation?) and **causation** (what makes ethnicity and nationalism so potent?).

This page records the positions. It does not adjudicate. The wiki's fullest statement of the
deep-roots case is [[sources/gat-nations-2013|Gat & Yakobson, *Nations* (2013)]]; the
[[nationalism]] page (after Roshwald) takes a middle position.

## Position 1 — Modernism: the nation is modern

The dominant late-20th-century school. The nation is "a product of processes of social
integration and political mobilization" that could only occur "with the advent of print
technology, wide-scale capitalist economies and, later, industrialization, urbanization, mass
education, and mass political participation… with active solicitation by the state."

- **Ernest Gellner** — nationalism is a functional requirement of industrial society, which
  needs a mobile, literate, culturally standardized workforce; "it is nationalism which
  engenders nations, and not the other way round." Nations are a sociological necessity of
  modernity, not an awakening of something old.
- **Benedict Anderson** — the nation is an **"imagined community,"** made thinkable by
  **print-capitalism**, which fixed vernaculars and let dispersed strangers picture themselves
  as one people; the decline of sacred script-languages and dynastic realms cleared its way.
- **Eric Hobsbawm** — nations and their traditions are largely **"invented,"** instruments of
  19th- and 20th-century state and elite politics; earlier "proto-national" bonds (religion,
  language) were *mobilized* but did not amount to nations.
- **Hans Kohn** (the school's "seminal" precursor) — nationalism an "artificial historical
  construct, built in the nineteenth century on older and more natural feelings of love for
  one's place, language, and customs."
- **John Breuilly** — nationalism is best understood as a form of modern **politics**, a way of
  contesting and capturing the modern state.

**Best argument:** the *scale, intensity, popular reach, and global diffusion* of nationalism
are genuinely new; mass national consciousness presupposes mass communication, literacy, and
political participation that did not exist before the 19th century.

## Position 2 — Ethnosymbolism: modern nations, premodern roots

A self-consciously *middle* position, associated above all with **Anthony D. Smith** (with
John Armstrong). Nations as fully developed political-cultural communities are largely modern,
**but** they are typically built upon and around **pre-existing ethnic cores** — *ethnies*
with their own myths, memories, symbols, and traditions of shared ancestry. Modern nationalists
do not invent from nothing; they rediscover, select, and reinterpret a real ethno-symbolic
inheritance. **Adrian Hastings** pushed the roots earlier still, arguing (from the English
case and the role of vernacular Bibles) that nations existed in the Middle Ages.

**Best argument:** it explains both the *novelty* of mass nationalism and the *depth* of
feeling and the specific ethnic content that bare modernization theory cannot supply — why
nationalism so often takes the form of the break-up of multiethnic empires rather than the
creation of new "all-imperial" nations.

## Position 3 — The deep-roots / perennialist position (Gat & Yakobson)

[[sources/gat-nations-2013|Gat]] stands "closer to" the traditionalists but argues the debate
must be **"transcended."** His moves:

- **Accept the modernists' own definition.** If one takes Gellner's definition of the nation as
  "a rough congruence between culture or ethnicity and state," then "nations are not confined to
  modern times." Karl Deutsch ("the coming together of a state and a people") implied the same.
- **Nationalism is a form of [[ethnicity|political ethnicity]].** "Ethnicity has always been
  highly political, ever since the emergence of the state and even before"; "ethnicity made the
  state and the state made ethnicity, in a reciprocal and dialectical process." See [[nation]].
- **[[national-state|National states]] existed in premodern times and worldwide.** Where a state
  was roughly congruent with one *ethnos* / *Staatsvolk*, you have a national state — and these
  "dynastic" or "territorial" states "in fact tended to be national monarchies." See
  [[premodern-european-nationhood]] for the European case (England as paradigm).
- **Empires were not ethnically blind.** "Nearly all of them were grounded in and relied upon a
  dominant ethnic nucleus."
- **The civic/ethnic and modern/premodern dichotomies are "false dichotomies."** See
  [[civic-and-ethnic-nationalism]].
- **What modernity did** was *release, transform, and enhance* an old phenomenon through mass
  empowerment — not create it. Nationalism is "the elephant in the room" that liberalism and
  Marxism failed to see.

**Best argument:** premodern cases of state–ethnos congruence and durable national feeling
(Pharaonic Egypt, the Jews, Armenians, the Greek *poleis*' shared Hellenism, Tang/Song China,
Japan, medieval England and France) are too numerous and too politically consequential to
dismiss as mere "proto-nationalism."

## Position 4 — Strong primordialism (largely superseded)

The view that nations and ethnic groups are **natural, given, quasi-eternal** units of human
belonging. In its strong form (everyday nationalist self-understanding; some mid-century
scholarship) it is rejected across the academy — and Gat too rejects it: nations "form and
disappear, and are therefore not 'primordial' in this sense," and the phenomenon "has evolved
in history," so even "perennial" is "insufficiently reflective of historical change." It
survives mainly as the *emic* self-image nationalists hold of their own nations, and as the
foil against which the other positions define themselves.

## Where the lines actually fall

Gat's own claim is that the gulf is **narrower than the rhetoric** suggests: leading modernists
concede a "proto-national base" (Hobsbawm) and pre-modern national sentiment "here and there"
(Kohn, Gellner). The live questions are therefore less "modern or ancient?" than:
1. **Definition** — how much state–culture congruence, and how much popular reach, must a
   community have to count as a "nation" rather than an "ethnie" or "proto-nation"?
2. **Continuity vs. construction** — are modern nations the *descendants* of premodern ethnic
   communities, or fresh *constructions* that raid the past for usable symbols?
3. **Causation** — the question Gat says both camps neglect: *why* is political ethnicity such a
   "potent, indeed explosive" force at all?

## Resolution status

**Open.** No consensus exists; the modernist paradigm remains dominant in sociology and the
"invention" literature, while ethnosymbolism and the deep-roots position command strong support
among historians of the premodern world. The debate is partly **terminological** (rival
definitions of "nation") and partly **substantive** (the weight of the premodern evidence).
