## Overview

The Clovis-first controversy is the central debate in the archaeology of the first Americans. It concerns when humans first arrived in the Americas, by what route, and what cultural tradition they represented. The debate is partially resolved — Clovis-first is no longer tenable — but many specific questions remain open.

## The Clovis-First Position

From the 1930s through the 1980s, the dominant hypothesis was that [[clovis-culture]] hunters were the founding population of the Americas. On this model:

- Humans crossed Beringia from Siberia and entered North America through an **ice-free corridor** that opened between the Laurentide and Cordilleran ice sheets as they retreated after the Last Glacial Maximum.
- The corridor became viable approximately 14,500–13,000 years ago.
- Clovis people — identified by a distinctive fluted projectile point — were the first to move southward from Beringia in significant numbers, spreading rapidly across the unglaciated Americas in a few centuries (~11,050–10,650 BCE, i.e., ~12,900–12,600 BP).
- Clovis may have directly caused the [[megafaunal-extinction-americas-12000bp|megafaunal extinctions]] through skilled hunting of naïve prey (the "overkill" hypothesis).

Proponents relied on the clear association of Clovis points with extinct megafauna at multiple kill sites, the apparent absence of older dated assemblages, and the logic of ice-sheet chronology. The Clovis-first model was the professional consensus for roughly fifty years.

## Pre-Clovis Evidence

Beginning in the 1970s, a series of sites produced dates or evidence that challenged the Clovis-first model. Snow, Gonlin, and Siegel (2020) assess the major candidates:

**Sites with broadly accepted pre-Clovis evidence:**
- **Monte Verde, Chile** (~10,500 BCE / ~12,500 BP): the single most decisive site for overturning Clovis-first. Located more than 15,000 km south of Alaska, it dates to centuries before the ice-free corridor was open or viable, forcing consideration of either an earlier coastal entry or a much earlier interior passage. The site's careful excavation and multiple convergent lines of evidence led to wide acceptance.
- **Paisley Caves, Oregon**: human ancient DNA (aDNA) extracted from coprolites dated to ~14,300 BP — among the earliest direct evidence of human presence in the Americas. Five coprolites predate 11,050 BCE (the Clovis threshold). The ice-free corridor was not viable this early, implying either a coastal route or earlier interior entry. Western Stemmed Tradition points found at the same site, coeval with Clovis points, demonstrate at least two technologically divergent traditions during the Clovis era.
- **Meadowcroft Rockshelter, Pennsylvania**: a deeply stratified site with the longest occupational sequence in the New World. Six radiocarbon dates associated with the deepest cultural materials (the "Miller complex") average ~12,605–12,005 BCE — well before Clovis. The site produced small prismatic blades and a lanceolate (Miller Lanceolate) point distinct from Clovis technology.
- **Manis Mastodon site, Washington**: a mastodon skeleton ~13,800 years old with an embedded bone projectile point made from mastodon bone — implying hunters who had already encountered and killed mastodons earlier.
- **Gault site, Texas**: contains a "Gault Assemblage" (GA) below clearly Clovis strata, dating to ~15,000–14,000 BCE. The GA includes ~150,000 lithic specimens, including blades and bifaces, with technological differences from Clovis suggesting no direct historical connection. An artificial stone pavement with blade tools dates to ~14,000 BCE.
- **Page-Ladson and Little Salt Spring, Florida**: generally accepted pre-Clovis evidence in the Southeast.
- **Rimrock Draw Rock Shelter, Oregon**: artifacts below a volcanic ash layer dating to 15,400 BP.
- **Daisy Cave, California** (San Miguel Island): occupation from ~9,550–6,550 BCE, demonstrating seaworthy boats capable of crossing at least 19 km of open ocean. Fishhooks from Baja California predate 10,000 BCE, providing additional evidence for early coastal migration.

**The Bluefish Caves question**: Bones bearing tool marks from Canada's Yukon Territory suggest human presence ~24,000 years ago — consistent with genetic and linguistic evidence for long Beringian occupation but earlier than most archaeologists are prepared to accept on current evidence.

**The "pioneer" phase model**: Many pre-Clovis sites share a curious pattern — convincing Paleoindian strata overlie undated earlier strata with a few blades. Some researchers interpret this as a **"pioneer" phase** of colonization (low archaeological visibility, small populations) preceding the denser "residential" phase represented by Clovis.

## Positions and Scholarly Alignments

**Position 1 — Modified Clovis-first (now a minority position):** Clovis represents the first significant colonization; pre-Clovis evidence is either contaminated, misinterpreted, or represents ephemeral earlier explorations that left no enduring lineage.

**Position 2 — Pre-Clovis via coastal route (dominant current view):** Humans arrived before ~13,000 BP via the Pacific coast, exploiting maritime resources. The Cordilleran ice sheet had pockets of unglaciated coastal refugia (northern Queen Charlotte Islands, Prince of Wales Island, Gulf of Alaska coast deglaciated by ~12,000 BCE). This pre-Clovis coastal population likely gave rise to the Western Stemmed Tradition seen on the Pacific coast, while interior Paleoindians descending from Northern Paleoindians in Alaska developed Clovis technology. The Clovis tradition appears to derive from Northern Paleoindian complexes in Beringia (Smith et al. 2014).

**Position 3 — Solutrean hypothesis (largely rejected):** A minority view (Stanford & Bradley) holds that Clovis overshot flaking technique resembles Upper Paleolithic Solutrean technology from Iberia, suggesting an Atlantic crossing. Archaeological, genetic, linguistic, and dental evidence overwhelmingly refutes this; the resemblance is almost certainly convergent. The book explicitly states there is "little or no support" for this alternative.

**Position 4 — Very early entry (~130,000 BP):** A 2017 claim (Holen et al.) for a human presence in southern California 130,000 years ago has not received general acceptance.

## Key Unresolved Questions

1. What precise routes did pre-Clovis people follow — coastal only, interior only, or both?
2. Was there a single colonization event or multiple waves?
3. Is the population that arrived pre-Clovis ancestral to Clovis people, or a separate lineage?
4. How early were humans actually in Beringia? Was occupation continuous or interrupted by the Last Glacial Maximum?
5. The ice-free corridor may have been viable only from ~12,600 years ago (10,650 BCE), by which time Clovis had already spread widely — what does this imply for the corridor's role?
6. The relationship between Northern Paleoindians of Alaska and interior Paleoindians south of the ice sheets remains tenuous and contested.

## Dating Methods and Confidence

Radiocarbon dating (calibrated) is the primary method. Clovis components most reliably date to ~11,050–10,650 BCE (after calibration). Pre-Clovis claims often rely on stratigraphic association rather than direct dating of cultural materials, or on OSL (optically stimulated luminescence), which is less precise for this period. Only a few pre-Clovis sites meet the most stringent requirements for demonstrating antiquity.

The Younger Dryas cold spell (~10,950–9,750 BCE) complicates interpretation — it coincided with the Clovis to Folsom transition, and its effect on human populations is not yet fully understood.

## Historiography

The debate ran from the 1970s through the 1990s with the Clovis-first establishment resisting pre-Clovis claims. The 1997 "validation" of Monte Verde by a team of skeptical archaeologists who visited the site is widely cited as the paradigm shift. DNA analysis (beginning ~2010s) and ancient DNA from coprolites (Paisley Caves) added molecular evidence to the picture. The field is now in a phase of documenting and refining the pre-Clovis record rather than proving its existence.
