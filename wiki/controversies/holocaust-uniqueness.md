# Was the 'Final Solution' Unique?

Is [[the-holocaust|the Holocaust]] historically unique, or is it best
understood comparatively alongside other genocides? The question is
simultaneously empirical, methodological, and moral-political, and it
structured German public debate from the Historikerstreit (1986–7)
onward.

## Positions

### Categorical uniqueness / incomparability
Some commentators hold that the Holocaust is illegitimate to compare
with anything else. Evans (2015) objects on method: "unless we compare
it to other events, we cannot establish its uniqueness," and if it is
utterly unique "it cannot be repeated in any form," rendering "never
again" meaningless — categorical uniqueness, he argues, belongs "in the
realms of theology," not history. He characterizes the standard
collection on the debate (Alan S. Rosenbaum, ed., *Is the Holocaust
Unique?*, 3rd ed. 2009) as marred by "excessive moralising and, in
places, mystification (including the argument that it is sacrilegious
or immoral to engage in comparison at all)."

### Comparability with a distinctive core (Evans's own position, attributed)
Evans works through the major comparators and concludes the 'Final
Solution' was "one genocide among many" but with features distinguishing
it from all the rest — "bounded neither by space nor by time":

- **Soviet occupation of eastern Poland (1939–41)**: up to 1.5 million
  of the Polish elite deported, a third dying en route; 20,000 officers
  shot. But victims were attacked "not on racial but on class grounds";
  deportation went *into* the Soviet interior aiming at political
  neutralization, not elimination of a people.
- **Holodomor**: man-made ("neither accidental nor natural" — grain
  seizure, refusal of aid, movement bans), ~3 million dead the most
  plausible figure (Conquest 5m; Wheatcroft ~3m; archival 1.8m + ~1m
  typhus; the 7–10 million claims of Ukrainian memory politics are
  "clearly intended to rank the Holodomor above the Holocaust"). But
  it served collectivization and broke peasant resistance; it "did not
  target Ukrainians because they were Ukrainians, nor did it attempt to
  kill them all without exception."
- **Armenian genocide (1915)**: closest structural parallel (ethnically
  homogeneous state project, revolutionary regime, minority branded
  agent of a foreign power, wartime context; Weimar's extreme right
  called Armenians "the Jews of Turkey" and viewed the killing
  approvingly). But Armenians were geographically concentrated, killed
  chiefly by deportation, not accused of a *world* conspiracy, and not
  hunted outside Turkey.
- **Rwanda (1994)**: at least 800,000 Tutsi killed, ~70% of the group,
  with globally exterminatory rhetoric — "yet for all its genocidal
  ideology and ambition, this, too, was a regionally limited action."
- **Herero/Nama (1904–7)**: genocidal, but the victims were never seen
  as a global threat; "if there was a parallel with Nazi racial policy,
  it was a parallel with Nazi policy towards the Slavs, not Nazi policy
  towards the Jews" (see [[colonialism-nazism-continuity]]).

What made the Nazi genocide of the Jews stand out, on this account:
Jews were constructed as the *Weltfeind*, a global enemy — Himmler
personally sought Finland's tiny Jewish community; the Wannsee minutes
listed Ireland and Iceland — so the program was "geographically and
temporally unlimited"; it was embedded in a still larger scheme of
racial reordering (Generalplan Ost's 30–45 million projected Slav
deaths); it was uniquely obsessive, sadistic and humiliation-driven
(rooted in the stab-in-the-back myth); and no other genocide used
poison gas in purpose-built facilities (~3m gassed, 1.3m shot, 700,000
in gas vans, up to 1m by starvation/disease/maltreatment of the ~6m
total). Evans cautions against reducing uniqueness to gassing or to
"industrial" impersonality — the killing was face-to-face violent and
the machinery makeshift.

### Economic-rationality reduction (rejected by Evans, attributed)
Götz Aly's argument (*Hitler's Beneficiaries*) that extermination was
driven by war-economic considerations is, for Evans, "unconvincing in
[its] vast overestimation of the resources acquired" — eastern Jews
were overwhelmingly poor; ideology, not economics, explains why Jews
ranked below every other group in Nazi rationing hierarchies.

### The double-genocide / "Bloodlands" framing
Timothy Snyder's *Bloodlands* (2010) treats Nazi and Stalinist killing
(~14 million dead in Poland, Belarus, Ukraine, the Baltics, western
Russia) within one frame. Evans's review (ch. 25) grants the scale of
Stalinist murder but argues the book (a) sidelines millions of Russian
victims outside the "Bloodlands" construct, (b) contains errors on the
German side (e.g. the Reinhard camps closed in 1943 because their task
was complete, not in 1944 before the Red Army), (c) revives Gerlach's
little-accepted 12 December 1941 "decision" thesis where most
historians now see a cumulative process driven by propaganda from above
and implemented haphazardly toward total annihilation, and (d) "forms
part of a post-Cold War narrative that homogenises the history of mass
murder by equating Hitler's policies with those of Stalin." Recorded
here as Evans's attributed critique; Snyder's framing has wide
scholarly and public support.

## Resolution status

Open. The comparative-genocide framework (Bloxham, Moses, Stone, Weitz)
is now the scholarly mainstream, but the weighting of uniqueness claims
and the legitimacy of Nazi–Soviet equivalence remain actively disputed
(with live memory-politics stakes: Holodomor recognition campaigns,
the Canadian Museum of Human Rights controversy).
