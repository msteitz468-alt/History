# Continuity vs. Discontinuity in the Vietnamese Past

Whether "Vietnam" names a continuous national community reaching back four millennia, or a
convenient label for a discontinuous series of experiments on the southern edge of the Sinic
world, is the central interpretive dispute in the historiography of premodern Vietnam. It bears
directly on how independence (939 CE), the dynastic sequence, the north–south divisions, and the
southward expansion ([[nam-tien]]) are narrated.

## Positions

### Nationalist continuity
The dominant frame in modern Vietnamese scholarship and state historiography: the Vietnamese nation
is continuous from the **Phung Nguyen** and **Dong Son** cultures (2nd–1st millennia BCE) through the
[[trung-sisters|Trung sisters']] revolt, the millennium of Chinese rule, the independent dynasties,
and the twentieth-century revolution — a single people repeatedly resisting northern (Chinese) and
Western domination. On this view the **thousand years of Chinese provincial rule** is an occupation
endured by an already-formed nation, and reunification (whether by [[le-thanh-tong|Le Thanh Tong]],
[[nguyen-hue|the Tay Son]], Gia Long, or Hanoi in 1975) recovers a natural unity. This reading has
deep roots: Taylor notes that Tran- and Le-era court historians already constructed a "southern"
imperial history parallel to the northern one.

### Taylor's discontinuity
[[taylor-history-of-the-vietnamese-2013|K. W. Taylor]] argues the Vietnamese past "does not display
an internal logic of development leading to the present" but "reveals a series of experiments...
None has been a final solution." Projecting national identity onto Phung Nguyen is "an exuberant use
of evidence"; the search for ancient origins is "a means of self-affirmation, not a scholarly
endeavor." Vietnamese history is unintelligible apart from Chinese history — it is a **Sinic-world**
experiment comparable to Korea and Japan, not primarily a Southeast Asian one. Taylor's own trajectory
is notable: his *Birth of Vietnam* (1983) was closer to the resistance-and-continuity model; by 2013
he had moved decisively toward discontinuity, treating each dynasty and region as its own world.

### Regionalist heterogeneity
A related but distinct emphasis (shared by Taylor and others, e.g. Li Tana on the Nguyen south, and
compatible with [[goscha-vietnam-a-new-history-2016|Goscha]]): the decisive fact is not continuity or
its absence but the persistent **plurality of Vietnamese lands** — the Red River north, the Ma/Ca
frontier, the central coast, and the trans-Hai Van south developed different economies, polities, and
even relationships to Champa and Cambodia. The Mac–Le, [[trinh-nguyen-war-1627-1672|Trinh–Nguyen]], and Tay Son
divisions are then not aberrations from unity but the normal condition, and "unification" is an
achievement (and imposition) rather than a recovery.

## Shared Factual Ground, Framed Per Side

All sides accept the same skeleton: Dong Son bronze culture; Han conquest (mid-1st c. CE) and the
disappearance of Dong Son; roughly a millennium of provincial administration; independence in 939;
the Ly–Tran–Le dynastic sequence; the 15th-century Ming occupation and Le restoration; the southward
expansion at Champa's and Cambodia's expense; the 16th–18th-century divisions; and nineteenth-century
Nguyen unification followed by French conquest. What differs is the **connective tissue**: whether these
are episodes in one national life (continuity), successive unrelated experiments (Taylor), or a standing
regional plurality periodically forced into unity (regionalist).
