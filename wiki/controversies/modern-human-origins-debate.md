## The Dispute

How, where, and when did anatomically and behaviorally modern *Homo sapiens*
originate, and what became of the other hominids it encountered as it spread? Two
classic models framed the debate through the 1980s–2000s; ancient DNA has since
forced a partial synthesis.

## Position 1 — Recent African Origin / Replacement ("Out-of-Africa")

*Homo sapiens* arose as a distinct species in Africa ~200–150 kya, then spread
across Eurasia after ~60 kya ([[out-of-africa-2-60kbp]]), **replacing** other
hominids (the [[neanderthals]], [[homo-erectus]]) with little or no interbreeding.

Evidence marshalled by [[tattersall-world-from-beginnings-2008|Tattersall (2008)]]
and others: mitochondrial DNA variation highest in Africa and consistent with a
single recent African source ("African Eve," ~290–140 kya); a recent population
bottleneck (human mtDNA diversity less than within a single chimp population);
Neanderthal mtDNA falling well outside the modern range and no closer to modern
Europeans than to anyone else; and the species-distinct anatomy of Neanderthals.
Tattersall takes the strong form: the Neanderthals were a separate species
(*Homo neanderthalensis*) and were essentially replaced, with at most negligible
"Pleistocene hanky-panky."

## Position 2 — Multiregional Continuity

Modern humans evolved *in place* across the Old World from local *Homo erectus*
populations (e.g. Australian Aboriginals from Javan "Java Man," Chinese from "Peking
Man"), kept as one species by continual gene flow between regions. Proponents
(Wolpoff, Thorne, and others) emphasize apparent regional skeletal continuity.

Tattersall's critique: the model faces the logical problem that two variants of one
species cannot be independently derived from a single antecedent species, forcing
its defenders to fold all post-*habilis* hominids into *Homo sapiens* — which, he
argues, "would make a mockery of any attempt to sort out hominid evolutionary
history on the basis of morphology." He treats it as a fallback from the discredited
"single-species hypothesis."

## Position 3 — Assimilation / "Leaky Replacement" (post-2010)

After this book was written, sequencing of the **Neanderthal (2010) and Denisovan
nuclear genomes** showed that non-African modern humans carry ~1–2% Neanderthal
ancestry, and some populations carry Denisovan ancestry — i.e. **real but limited
interbreeding** accompanied an out-of-Africa expansion that was still predominantly
a replacement. This largely vindicates the African-origin framework while refuting
the strict no-admixture stance, and it falsifies classic multiregionalism's claim
of deep regional continuity.

## Status

**Partially resolved.** The recent-African-origin core is now consensus; strict
zero-admixture replacement (the form defended in this source) is rejected; classic
multiregionalism is rejected, though its emphasis on gene flow is partly absorbed
into the assimilation model. Live questions remain: the exact African geography and
tempo of *sapiens* origins (single region vs. pan-African "structured"
metapopulation), the number and timing of dispersals, and how much archaic
admixture shaped modern phenotypes.
