# Is History a Science?

Whether history can frame general laws, achieve completion, or predict — or whether it
is an art, or a discipline *sui generis*. Evans (1997, chs. 1–2) notes the debate is
partly an artifact of English usage: German *Wissenschaft* covers any organized
discipline, so *Geschichtswissenschaft* raises no eyebrows and the fight "never
arisen" there in the same terms.

## Position: History Is a Science

- **J. B. Bury** (Regius inaugural, Cambridge, 1903): "History is a science, no less
  and no more... History is not a branch of literature" — Mommsen the epigrapher, not
  Mommsen the Nobel-winning stylist, as the model. Rooted in the Rankean inductive
  ideal (see [[rankean-historicism]]); Langlois and Seignobos predicted critical
  scholarship would one day be "finished."
- **Covering-law and predictive ambitions**: Carr argued history generalizes like
  science and can inform prediction under specified conditions (Versailles delegates
  studying Vienna 1815). **Toynbee's** *A Study of History* posited laws of
  civilizational rise and collapse. **Paul Kennedy's** *Rise and Fall of the Great
  Powers* (1987) turned an "overstretch" pattern into prophecy.
- **Quantification**: Annales — Le Roy Ladurie: "History that is not quantifiable
  cannot claim to be scientific"; US cliometrics — Fogel's explicit "scientific vs.
  traditional" dichotomy (mathematical models, statistical testing, team production).
  Real wins: Laslett's demography demolishing the extended-family myth of
  pre-industrial England.

## Position: History Is Not (Strong-Sense) Science

- **Trevelyan** ("Clio: A Muse," answering Bury): scientific history rests on "a
  misapplication of the analogy of physical science"; history yields no predictive
  laws; the campaign threatened to banish "literature, emotion and speculative
  thought." (His stance was also explicitly nationalist — German method as "a strait
  waistcoat to English limbs.")
- **Geyl vs. Toynbee**: Toynbee "simply selected the evidence he wanted" (e.g., the
  New England "challenge and response" case ignoring Spanish decline and British sea
  power); "a prophet, not a historian." **Froude's** child's-box-of-letters image:
  "we can spell any word we please."
- **Predictive failures**: Kennedy missed the Soviet collapse he was writing beside;
  "lessons of appeasement" led Eden into Suez 1956; Bolsheviks' fear of "the Russian
  Bonaparte" (Trotsky) shows law-avoidance shaping events; virtually no historian
  predicted 1989–91. Evans: "It is always a mistake for a historian to try to predict
  the future."
- **Cliometric overreach**: Fogel and Engerman's *Time on the Cross* (1974) — slavery
  as efficient, slave living standards comparable to free workers — dismantled by
  critics as riddled with statistical and citation errors all tending one way; "the
  two authors' entire argument simply fell apart" (Evans's summary).
- **Kuhn** (*Structure of Scientific Revolutions*, 1962) undercut the Popperian image
  of science itself; imported into history, paradigm-talk proved corrosively
  relativist, though Kuhn denied applicability outside natural science.

## Position: Sui Generis / Craft

Elton: history is neither art nor science but "a study different from any other and
governed by rules peculiar to itself" — while accepting the post-Newtonian
observer-effect as "not a bad analogy for good history." Mommsen (1874): the true
historian is "not trained but born." Marc Bloch: history as craft, learned on the job.
**Evans's own position**: science only in the weak *Wissenschaft* sense (organized,
methodical, peer-reviewed knowledge); "To search for a truly 'scientific' history is
to pursue a mirage"; history "is, or can be, an art" and is a craft — with "a healthy
degree of scepticism" owed to every generation's newly proclaimed one-true-scientific
method.

## Resolution Status

Largely resolved by exhaustion of the strong program: covering-law and completion
ambitions are dead, quantification survives as one sub-specialism among many, and
most practitioners hold some version of the weak-science/craft view. The recurring
pattern (per Evans): each insurgent "scientific" method claims to obsolete all prior
history, then settles into a sub-specialism.
