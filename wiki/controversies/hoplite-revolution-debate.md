# The Hoplite Revolution Debate

Whether the spread of hoplite panoply and close-order fighting caused a social-political “revolution” in the Archaic polis. Principal Hall treatment: [[sources/hall-history-archaic-greek-world-2014|Hall (2014)]] Ch.7.

## Positions

### Classical revolution thesis
Phalanx and heavy shield postdate Homeric duels; Corinthian vase-painting shows full panoply/phalanx tactics by ~675–650, coinciding with earliest tyrannies; Cypselus/Theagenes supposedly backed by a “hoplite class”; Pheidon (if mid-seventh) beats Spartans at Hysiae with new tactics (shield invented at Argos); middling farmer-warriors demand political parity — egalitarian polis ideal. Largely indebted to Aristotle *Pol.* 4.10.10 (cavalry aristocracies → broader citizen body as heavy infantry grows), itself an evolutionist theory without genuine early documentation, modeled on fifth-century naval-power explanations (Old Oligarch).

### Gradualist equipment / tactics-first alternatives
(1) Archaeology/art history: panoply items adopted gradually, largely predating phalanx tactics (Argos warrior grave late 8th c. bronze breastplate; Corinthian helmet from ~700; greaves late 8th on Crete). (2) Literary view: equipment responds to tactics, so close-formation fighting may predate the hoplite shield ca. 700; *Iliad* “zoom” on heroes masks decisive mass fighting (“fence of spears,” Myrmidones wall, clash of shields). Emergence of hoplite warfare accompanies the rise of the polis.

### Gradualist / Hall (political consequences underdetermined)
Little independent evidence for early monarchies; specialized cavalry is late Archaic/Classical outside Thessaly — Archaic mounted figures are often wealthy infantrymen riding to battle. No source says tyrants seized power with hoplite-class support; Aristotle (*Pol.* 5.8.6) distinguishes a king’s bodyguard of citizens from a tyrant’s foreign guard. Homeric close-packed passages may be “mass fighting” not Classical “massed” phalanx. Poems may take recognizable form in the seventh not eighth century; nascent egalitarianism fits poorly with Hall’s eighth-century picture of leaders and followers ([[concepts/basileus-and-big-man]]).

## Hall on “teamwork” and Tyrtaeus

Lingering belief that phalanx “teamwork” equals middling egalitarianism lacks support even classically. Xenophon/Arrian/Plutarch place best troops (*aristoi*) in front (and rear), worst in middle — front is the cutting edge. Outside Sparta hoplites self-equip; Classical shield+spear ~25–30 drachmas, breastplate far more expensive — not a broad middling franchise by equipment alone.

Archaic focus is on *promakhoi* (front rank). Tyrtaeus frs. 10–11 mention spears, swords, helmets, hollow shields; “stand fast with one another”; shield-pushing; light-armed slingers/javelin-men crouch near full-armored (not separate Classical units). Front-rankers called *agathoi/esthloi*; shame language is status-coded. “An attentive reading of Tyrtaeus… reveals not so much an egalitarian ethos as an expression of class chauvinism. This conclusion stands in stark variance to the standard picture of Sparta as the model hoplite state.”

## Van Wees on shield-locking and phalanx density

A separate strand of revisionism, argued by Hans van Wees in
[[sources/osborne-classical-greece-2000|Osborne (ed.), *Classical Greece 500-323 BC*
(2000)]] Ch. 4, targets not the origin of the phalanx but the traditional reading of how
densely it fought. The conventional interpretation of Thucydides 5.71.1 — that each
hoplite's shield protected the exposed (unshielded) side of the man to his left,
requiring a tightly locked, low-mobility line — is, on van Wees's argument, likely
overstated. He notes that a hoplite needed a **sideways-on stance** to wield spear or
sword with any force, which would have positioned him centered behind his own shield
rather than extending it far to help his neighbour, and cites Polybius's estimate (18.30.6-11)
that a soldier fighting with a cutting or thrusting weapon and shield needed **at least
six feet of space in every direction** — considerably looser than the packed-shoulder
image often drawn from Thucydides. This bears on the wider revolution debate insofar as
a looser, more individually-fought phalanx sits less comfortably with readings (classical
or Hall's gradualist alternative alike) that lean on close-order "teamwork" as evidence
for a particular social or tactical transition. Not adjudicated here.

## CHGRW positions (Wheeler, Krentz, Hunt, van Wees, Whitby)

[[sabin-van-wees-whitby-cambridge-history-greek-roman-warfare-2007|CHGRW]] Part I does not
adjudicate the revolution debate but multiplies the poles and warns against Roberts-style
“military revolutions” transferred to antiquity.

**Continuity over revolutions (Wheeler I.7).** The traditional two-revolution narrative
(seventh-century phalanx/*polis*; early-fifth-century strategic awakening after Persia and
the Peloponnesian War) invites revision; recounting archaic and classical combat from
**development and continuities** may be more enlightening. Neither the double-grip *aspis*
(widespread by c.650) nor other armour produced a sudden tactical revolution — equipment
shows desire for a larger sturdier shield, not a pre-existing mass formation needing a
better shield. Scholarly poles include: some find a phalanx already in Homer and deny a
hoplite-armour revolution; others challenge the classical closed phalanx and treat
*othismos* as myth. Growing support for a seventh- or even sixth-century *Iliad* text
undercuts Homer as earliest military witness.

**Dating the closed phalanx (van Wees I.9).** Majority dates the closed hoplite phalanx to
seventh-century early state-formation. Alternative (van Wees): private retinues integral
until the late sixth century when state-formation produced centralized forces — and the
classical closed style may not predate that. Homeric war bands of chieftain + retainers in
open mixed formation show social structure dictating organization and battle form;
archaic formalization of units more likely from state-formation than battle-type driving
society.

**Agonal model (Krentz I.6; Wheeler).** Krentz dismantles the Mardonius/Herodotus agonal
model (annual farmer contests, ritualized limited wars for status): Mardonius was “mostly
wrong.” Krentz (2002) argues agonal conventions developed **only after the Persian Wars**;
Hanson argues for historicity of battle conventions c.700–450. Hunt’s baseline (“hoplites
as dinosaurs” — primacy early seventh to late fifth, then decline) is challenged on two
fronts: archaic multi-weapon coordination already mixed light troops among hoplites; early
Peloponnesian War already shows unescorted hoplites defeated in rough terrain; and
fourth-century “decline” is overstated — the hoplite phalanx remained best for plains
battles and Near Eastern employers.

**Othismos (Wheeler).** Metaphorical / open-formation school (Fraser; Cawkwell, Krentz,
Goldsworthy, van Wees) vs traditional literal push (Woodhouse, Grundy, Cartledge,
Holladay, Anderson, Pritchett, Hanson, Lazenby et al.). Wheeler: many references
metaphorical, but some shoving literal; defining battle as shoving match *or* denying
*othismos* both go too far. Whitby (I.3): ancient writers presuppose audience knowledge of
normal battle — Herodotus describes the odd Battle of the Champions but not the full
encounter; Xenophon says Coronea was “unlike any other” without defining the norm —
hence modern disagreement. The **Chigi vase** is prominent in dating equipment/tactics but
“does not add to our knowledge of the nature of hoplite warfare” with ordered ranks and
overlapping shields — art generalizes rather than specifies combat mechanics.

**“Hoplites as middle class” (van Wees).** Almost entirely modern invention; Aristotle’s
single “middle group” link is vague and elsewhere identifies hoplites with the rich, not
the *mesoi*. See social composition on [[hoplite-warfare]].

## Shared factual ground

Heavy infantry equipment and closer formations become more common in the seventh–sixth centuries; vase-painting and graves document panoply pieces; Archaic poetry valorizes standing in ranks; tyranny and broader citizenship appear in the same centuries. Causation from military form → political revolution remains disputed. CHGRW adds: panoply elements from mid-eighth century; depth likely sixth century; ritualized agonal rules largely post-Persian Wars on the revisionist reading; social composition of classical hoplite armies always mixed leisured and working / non-citizen.
