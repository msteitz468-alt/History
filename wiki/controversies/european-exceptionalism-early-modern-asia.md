# European Exceptionalism in Early Modern Asia

Did the Dutch and English East India Companies **transform** early modern Asia — or
were they relatively minor players who survived by adapting to powerful, dynamic
Asian economies and states? This is the organising debate of
[[clulow-mostert-dutch-english-east-india-companies-2018|Clulow & Mostert (2018)]],
whose epilogue (Tonio Andrade) traces it as the through-line of
[[dutch-east-india-company|VOC]] historiography from Marx to the present. The volume
sides decisively with the revisionists, framing the companies as "powerful on the
waves but weak on land" — and, in Peter Good's chapter on
[[nader-shah|Nader Shah]], sometimes weak even on the waves.

## Position 1 — The Companies as Transformative

- **Marx** treated the VOC as a more primitive forerunner of nineteenth-century
  English rule: "parasites" who planted European control atop Asian "despotic"
  structures, gaining a monopoly over *carrying* without restructuring Asian
  economies — "a system of plunder."
- **Weber** saw the VOC as a preliminary stage of the modern stock company
  (transferable shares, bookkeeping) but ultimately "colonial capitalism," with
  Asian "patrimonial" government lacking the *calculability* capitalism required.
- **Niels Steensgaard** (*Carracks, Caravans and Companies*, 1973) argued the VOC
  *did* revolutionise Asian trade — not by importing capitalism but by **rationalising
  violence** to impose monopoly and thereby create market predictability,
  distinguishing the profit-rational Dutch from the crusade-driven Portuguese.

## Position 2 — Asian Resilience

- **J. C. van Leur** attacked writing history "from the deck of the ship… the high
  gallery of the trading-house," argued Asian networks were far more resilient than
  assumed, and located the real Western "disjuncture" in the **nineteenth century**,
  not the seventeenth.
- **M. A. P. Meilink-Roelofsz** (1962) corrected van Leur's "peddler trade" picture,
  documenting formal, bulk-goods Asian trade structures "from the Arabian Sea to the
  China Seas" — while granting the VOC "seriously disturbed or even utterly destroyed
  various aspects of the native economy." Her view "has tended to prevail."
- **Sanjay Subrahmanyam** attributed the seventeenth-century transformation chiefly to
  **Asian state expansion** (Ottomans, Safavids, Mughals); Dutch growth was "part of a
  general expansion of Asian maritime trade."
- **Leonard Blussé** (the volume's dedicatee) used Asian-language sources to show the
  **Chinese** dominated East and Southeast Asian trade through the long seventeenth
  century, and built the TANAP program (2000–2007) to fuse European and Asian
  historiography. **John E. Wills Jr.** coined **"interactive dominance"** — European
  power as emergent, gradual, and dependent on the active participation of Asian
  officials, merchants, and brokers.

## Position 3 — Global Early Modernisation

Andrade's synthesis: widespread **mutual adoption** undercuts any unique European
edge. Japanese troops used the musketry countermarch "long before Europeans were";
Makassarese drew military manuals from European *and* Islamic sources; East Asian
mariners sailed "Chinese junks with European rigging." Victor Lieberman's *Strange
Parallels* shows "much of Eurasia was undergoing remarkably similar trends." The
final verdict: the VOC "was probably not the catalyst of capitalism that it was once
considered to be, but it helped connect human societies in denser and stronger webs
than ever before."

## Status

**Partially resolved** — the field has converged strongly on Asian resilience and
parallel development, away from the older transformative/exceptionalist account,
while continuing to debate the *degree* of the companies' destructive impact
(Meilink-Roelofsz vs. a pure van Leur position) and how to model the networks without
losing sight of "war, weather, and individual personalities."
