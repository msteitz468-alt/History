# The Peopling and "Race" of Ancient Egypt

Who were the ancient Egyptians, racially and biologically — and how African were they? The question has
been politically charged since the nineteenth century, when European scholarship habitually detached Egypt
from Africa. It became the signature controversy of the **UNESCO [[gha-vol2-ancient-civilizations|General
History of Africa, Vol. II]]**, whose opening chapter by **Cheikh Anta Diop** argues that the ancient
Egyptians were a **Black African people**. The wiki records the positions without adjudicating.

## Why it is contested

The dispute mixes **evidence** (skeletal remains, ancient depictions and texts, language, genetics) with
**framing**: nineteenth- and twentieth-century scholarship applied modern racial categories ("white,"
"negro," "Mediterranean," "Hamitic") that most specialists now regard as **anachronistic and ill-defined**.
It also carries a heavy **political charge** — the "bleaching" of Egypt served colonial-era assumptions, and
its reclamation became central to Pan-Africanist and Afrocentric thought.

## Positions

- **The Black-African thesis (Diop, Obenga).** In GHA II, **Cheikh Anta Diop** argues the Egyptians were
  **negro/Black** from the Predynastic period on. He marshals craniometry (re-reading the same skull series
  others called "Mediterranean" as substantially negroid), melanin analysis, **classical testimony**
  (Herodotus describing Egyptians as "black-skinned and woolly-haired"), Egyptian self-representation, and
  **linguistic** comparison (parallels he draws between ancient Egyptian and **Wolof** and other African
  languages). His central polemic is that European scholarship **"bleached"** Egypt by redefining its terms
  whenever the evidence pointed African. *Held by:* Diop, Théophile Obenga, the Afrocentric school.
- **The older "Mediterranean/Hamitic" view.** The displaced orthodoxy held the Egyptians to be a
  "Mediterranean," "Hamitic," or "brown" people distinct from sub-Saharan Africans — at its worst tied to
  the racist **Hamitic hypothesis** (civilization brought by lighter-skinned northerners). Now discredited
  in that form (see [[bantu-expansion]] on the Hamitic myth).
- **The modern consensus: indigenous Northeast Africans.** Most current specialists hold the Egyptians were
  an **indigenous Nile-valley population**, biologically continuous with Northeast Africa, **variable** and
  clinal (more sub-Saharan affinity in the south/Upper Egypt and Nubia, more Levantine in the north), and
  **not usefully sorted into modern "races"** at all. Egypt is treated as an **African civilization in its
  African context** — which was GHA II's broader and now widely-accepted point, even where Diop's strongest
  specifics are doubted.
- **"The question is anachronistic."** A meta-position: the ancient Egyptians did not classify themselves by
  modern skin-colour "race," and forcing them into "Black" or "white" imports a category that did not exist;
  the productive questions are about **biological affinity, language, and culture**, not "race."

## The 1974 UNESCO symposium

GHA II prints, as an **annex to Diop's chapter**, the report of the 1974 Cairo symposium "**The Peopling of
Ancient Egypt and the Deciphering of the Meroitic Script**," where Diop and Obenga presented their case to
an international panel of Egyptologists. The symposium famously reached **no consensus**: the official record
notes that the expected unanimity did not emerge, that Diop and Obenga's arguments were not refuted but not
generally accepted either, and that participants agreed mainly that the problem needed further
interdisciplinary work — an outcome the wiki takes as emblematic of the dispute's **unresolved** core.

## Resolution status

**Partially resolved.** The **broad** claim — that Egypt was an African civilization, deeply connected to
Nubia and the wider continent, and that the old "white/Hamitic Egypt" was an ideological construct — is now
mainstream. The **specific** claim that the Egyptians were "Black" in the modern sense remains contested,
and the **framing** itself (whether "race" is the right question) is increasingly rejected. Diop's linguistic
and craniometric methods are regarded as overreaching by most specialists, even by those sympathetic to his
anti-colonial purpose.
