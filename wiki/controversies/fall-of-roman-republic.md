# Causes of the Fall of the Roman Republic

Why did the political order known as the **Roman Republic** collapse between the
Gracchi (133) and the Second Triumvirate (43)? The synthesizing question of
[[crook-cah-v9-1992|CAH IX]], framed by its editors (Crook, Lintott, Rawson) in the
**Epilogue** as: **"Did it decline, or was it assassinated?"** — did the Republic
contain "the dialectic of its own collapse," or could it, but for the ambitions of
Pompey, Caesar, Antony, and Octavian, have survived?

## Positions

### 1. Moral decline (the ancient Roman view)
The standard ancient explanation (Sallust, Polybius' *metus hostilis*, Florus): the
destruction of Carthage removed the fear that restrained Rome, and **wealth, greed,
and luxury** corrupted the body politic. **Rejected** by the CAH editors: moral
change occurs in every age and is "not necessarily a symptom of morbidity"; wealth
often accompanies advance, not decline.

### 2. The fall of a corrupt oligarchy
The view that what fell was merely "a corrupt and unlamented oligarchy" playing a
private game. **Also rejected**, in light of recent work (Nicolet, Millar, Lintott,
Brunt) stressing the **genuinely democratic / "mixed-constitution" aspects** of the
Republic — Polybius "drew a true picture after all," and the governing class depended
each generation on the electorate. This forces the question onto **structural** rather
than merely personal ground.

### 3. Structural faults under the strain of empire (the editors' synthesis)
The CAH editors locate the causes in the **institutions of a city-state straining to
run an empire**:

- **The manpower–land nexus.** Conquest brought wealth and satisfied the aristocracy's
  supreme value, ***gloria*** — but eroded the peasant reservoir of legionaries, as
  the rich sought land for the spoils of empire and the peasantry fought to keep their
  holdings. Hence **agrarian laws were the recurring focus of conflict**; and when
  spare land ran out, "Paul could only be paid by robbing Peter," and armies began to
  **bargain for land themselves**.
- **The over-mighty commands.** Empire tempted the *principes viri* with **extended
  commands over vast regions**, breeding independent initiative, impatience with the
  Senate, and the habit of "short cuts" at home — and ambitions "lured by the ghost of
  **Alexander**" (Pompey, Caesar). De-politicizing the army was the unsolved problem.
- **The widening of the *populus*.** The [[social-war-91-88bce|Social War]]'s
  **enfranchisement of Italy** made the *populus Romanus* far larger than the
  [[plebs-urbana|city plebs]] that had spoken for it — an "important indirect factor."
- **The politicization of the urban plebs**, the collapse into **violence and bribery**
  (the anarchy of the 50s, when elections could not be held), in a society whose law
  and politics had only a "flimsy" barrier against *vis*, and where conflict could only
  take the form of *stasis*.
- **Debt** — the cycle of borrowing on land to fund a prestige lifestyle and political
  manipulation — destabilizing all orders.
- **The conceptual failure**: the Romans (like the ancients generally) **could conceive
  political improvement only as "putting the clock back"** to a supposed better past —
  so the Republic "did not try radically enough" to adapt institutions to rapid change.

### 4. "Assassination" by the dynasts
That but for the ambitions of **Pompey, Caesar, Antony, and Octavian** the Republic
might have survived. The editors **partly resist** this: the *principes* "manipulated
violence and corruption" but did not necessarily *want* to be dictators; serious men
were genuinely grappling with real problems (*repetundae*, land, the franchise, the
commands), and the conflict shows public life "**healthy, not ailing**." Yet the
army's loyalty to its general (the lesson of [[sulla]]) made the dynasts' route
possible.

### 5. Inevitable sea-change
The editors' ultimate verdict: **"some sea-change... was as inevitable as anything in
history can be."** In the wider world Rome now ruled, "maintenance of the old
Republican polity would have been a nonsense"; the venerable institutions would be
"used to clothe the new order." But *whether that particular new order* (the Augustan
Principate) was equally inevitable is "altogether too hard a question."

## Not decline but change

The volume's emphatic theme: the last century was **not an age of decline but of rapid
advance** — in [[roman-economy-late-republic|economy]], [[late-republican-intellectual-culture|culture
and letters]] (Cicero, Catullus, Lucretius, Sallust; the "Augustan" writers were
mostly formed before Augustus), [[roman-public-law|criminal]] and
[[roman-private-law|civil law]], architecture, and [[roman-republican-religion|religion]]
(the "decline of religion" stereotype is "most emphatically repudiated"). The change
itself, however — the rapid assault on *mos maiorum* sweeping away "landmarks and
handholds" — was a **factor making for instability**. Augustus, it is suggested, did
not culminate the "Roman Revolution" but **applied the brake** to it.

## What was gained and lost

**Lost**: the *libertas* and equality of the Republican order — "such as they were,"
and only for free-born citizens — and the rule of law (which returned under Augustus,
but only "because... the ruler determined it should"). **Gained**: the **basis for an
imperial world-order** — the enfranchised Italians and provincials as an enlarged
governing class, the **professional army**, the command-and-tenure **paradigm** for the
emperors, and the first steps toward **municipal integration**. The deepest long-term
change was "**the reduction of the independent points of decision-making to,
ultimately, just one**" — Tacitus' bitter irony: "What need for endless harangues...
now that policy is settled by one supremely wise man, and one alone?"

## Resolution status

**Open.** The CAH editors offer a structural synthesis but explicitly leave the
counterfactual ("could it have survived?") and the inevitability of the Principate
unresolved. The debate continues to run between "decline" (structural) and
"assassination" (contingent) readings.
