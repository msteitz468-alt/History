# Syncretism vs. Authenticity: Is Asian Islam a "Thin Veneer"?

A long-running interpretive dispute in Islamic Studies and Asian Studies over how
to characterize Islam as practiced across Asia — especially Southeast Asia — relative
to an assumed Arabo-Persian "norm." The dispute is not about facts (all sides agree
Asian Muslim practice incorporates pre-Islamic and local elements) but about how to
*read* that incorporation: as evidence of shallow or incomplete Islamization, or as a
fully Islamic tradition constituted through local terms.

## Position A — The "Thin Veneer" / Syncretism Thesis

Asian Islam is a syncretic overlay on a durable Hindu–Buddhist (or animist,
Confucian) substratum — "authentic" Islam remaining lodged in the Arab Middle East,
from which Asia received a diluted, hybridized form.

- **J. C. van Leur** coined the enduring image of Islam in Java as a "thin, easily
  flaking glaze" over an ever-strong Hindu–Buddhist substratum (Indology thesis,
  1930s–40s).
- **Clifford Geertz**, *The Religion of Java* (1960) — "the first significant
  anthropological study focusing explicitly on Islam" — concluded "it is very hard
  for a Javanese to be a 'real Moslem,'" describing Indonesian Islam as having
  "vegetated" once "cut off from its centers of orthodoxy at Mecca and Cairo."
- Roots in 1860s Dutch Protestant missionary reports and in colonial Indology, which
  treated Java as essentially "Hindu-Buddhist"; reinforced by anthropology's
  attraction to Robert Redfield's "Little Tradition" paradigm, which cast Asian Islam
  as a heterodox local variant.
- Institutionally entrenched: US Cold-War area-studies funding subsumed "Islamic
  Studies" under "Near and Middle Eastern Studies," privileging Arabic textual
  "heartlands" (Syria, Egypt) and marginalizing Islam as a non-Arab tradition.

## Position B — The "Authenticity" / Contextual-Orthodoxy Thesis

Asian Islam is not derivative or incomplete; local forms represent ongoing
renegotiation producing multiple instances of "contextual orthodoxy," and Asia's
Muslims have actively shaped Islamic orthodoxy and orthopraxy at the supposed
"center" as much as the "periphery."

- Since the 1990s, scholars have argued for approaching Islam in South and Southeast
  Asia as "authentic," and noted the parallel continuities between pre-Islamic and
  Islamic orders in West Asia itself.
- **Marshall Hodgson**, *The Venture of Islam* (1974), advanced the concept of
  "Islamicate" culture to capture Islam's broad worldly impact and to bring "the
  peripheries" into Islamic Studies.
- **Bruce Lawrence** and **Richard Eaton** (South Asianists) argue Islamic Studies
  fails its subject unless it foregrounds the worldview of Asia's Muslim millions, and
  propose reading Islamic history as a window onto global history.
- **Richard W. Bulliet**, *Islam: The View from the Edge* (1994), relocated agency to
  the "edge" — "wherever people make the decision to cross a social boundary and join
  the Muslim community."
- **Chiara Formichi**, *Islam and Asia* (2020), extends Bulliet's "edge" from
  eleventh-century Iran to Central Asia, South Asia, China, and Southeast Asia, and
  rejects the "syncretic/foreign" master narrative in favor of Asia's history as
  "intrinsically interconnected with the history of Islam, and vice versa."

## Shared Factual Ground, Framed per Side

Both sides accept that early Javanese and Acehnese mosques (mid-16th c.) echo
pre-Islamic (Balinese Hindu) temple architecture; that Islamic and local practices
blended (e.g. Sultan Agung's Javanese calendar, `'adat` law); and that most Asian
Muslims share a strong sense of belonging to the `umma`. Position A reads the
architectural and ritual hybridity as a "less authentic" form; Position B reads the
same evidence as "contextual orthodoxy" — reconciliation of local and Islamic
devotional forms without loss of Islamic commitment.

## Resolution Status

**Open.** The revisionist (Position B) case has gained ground in specialist
scholarship since the 1990s, but the "thin veneer" framing persists in general
narratives, museum practice (Islamic-art galleries that exclude China and Southeast
Asia), and the institutional structure of area studies. Formichi argues the
reconceptualizations "have remained marginal and disengaged from the study of Islam
in Asia."
