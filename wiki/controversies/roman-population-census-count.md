# Roman Population and the Augustan Census: High Count vs. Low Count

A long-running dispute over how to read the Roman census figures reported by Augustus in his
*Res Gestae* — and, through them, over the total population of Roman Italy and the empire at the
turn of the millennium. Surveyed in Walter Scheidel's demography chapter (Ch. 3) of
[[scheidel-cambridge-economic-history-greco-roman-world-2007|The Cambridge Economic History of the
Greco-Roman World]] (2007); see [[ancient-demography]] for the surrounding demographic framework.

## The Evidence

Augustus records a census figure of 4,063,000 *cives* (citizens) for 28 BCE, sharply higher than
the reported 910,000 citizens of the census of 70/69 BCE. What the census actually counted — and
therefore what population figure the 4,063,000 implies — is the crux of the dispute.

## Position: The High Count (Elio Lo Cascio)

Lo Cascio reads the Augustan censuses as continuing the Republican practice of counting only adult
male citizens. On this reading, the 28 BCE figure of roughly 4 million adult male citizens implies
a total Italian citizen population, including women and children, in excess of 10 million, and a
gross population of Roman Italy (citizens plus slaves and non-citizens) on the order of 20 million.
Proponents of the high count argue it better matches literary impressions of a densely populated,
heavily urbanized Italy and avoids assuming an implausibly sharp census-methodology change under
Augustus.

## Position: The Low Count (Karl Julius Beloch; developed by Peter Brunt, Neville Morley, Walter
Scheidel)

Beloch's original conjecture, developed and defended by Brunt, Morley, and Scheidel, holds that the
Augustan census changed methodology to count *all* citizens — men, women, and children — not adult
males alone. On this reading, the 28 BCE figure yields a citizen population of roughly 4 million for
Italy, an order of magnitude lower than the high-count implication. Proponents argue the low count
is far more consistent with comparative population densities attested elsewhere in the ancient
Mediterranean, and with the scale of the well-documented central Italian slave-based villa economy,
which the high count's implied free population would leave difficult to fit alongside a large
enslaved workforce on the same land base. Scheidel and Morley have also pointed to internal
inconsistencies in the ancient sources' reported growth rates between censuses as further evidence
against a constant adult-male-only counting methodology across the transition.

## Shared Factual Ground, Framed per Side

Both positions agree on: the raw figures reported in the *Res Gestae* and by Livy's periochae; that
*some* change occurred in Roman census procedure between the late Republic and the Augustan
principate; and that the resolution of this dispute materially changes estimates of Italian
population density, urbanization rate, and the proportion of the population enslaved. They diverge
entirely on what the reported numbers were actually counting.

## Resolution Status

The dispute remains genuinely open. Scheidel notes that "most scholars" currently favor some
version of the low count, but treats the matter as unresolved rather than settled, given the
fragmentary and rhetorically shaped nature of the ancient source material (the *Res Gestae* itself
is a monument text, not a technical demographic report).
