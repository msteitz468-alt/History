# Was There Regular Trans-Saharan Trade Before Islam?

## The Dispute

Did **regular, large-scale trade cross the Sahara before the Islamic camel caravans** of the
eighth–ninth centuries CE — or did sustained trans-desert commerce only begin with Islam? In
[[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|CWH IV]] (Austen, Ch 24; Burstein, Ch 23) this is a "long-standing
historical debate," turning on how to read fragmentary and largely **archaeological** evidence
for the [[trans-saharan-trade|trans-Saharan trade]].

## Positions

**1. Minimalist — no regular trade before c. 700 CE.**
The trade's staple goods — **gold, slaves, salt** — leave almost no archaeological trace, and
**direct written accounts** of caravan commerce appear only in medieval Arabic sources.
Excavation in the Western Sahel shows "a total lack of archaeological evidence... attesting to
Roman or other pre-Arab Mediterranean presence." Analyses of Byzantine and early Islamic North
African **coinage** reveal no trans-Saharan gold before the **mid-eighth century**; the
earliest secure proof is the ninth-century gold-coin moulds at **Tadmekka**. On this view,
sustained trade awaited the camel caravan, the Berber Kharijite states, and the unifying spread
of Islam. *Strength:* rests on the absence of hard evidence. *Weakness:* absence of evidence for
trace-less commodities is weak proof of absence, and little archaeology has been done in the
Sudan.

**2. Revisionist — early cross-desert networks via the Garamantes.**
Circumstantial evidence points to **smaller-scale trade in the early centuries CE**: Saharan
**rock art** of horses and chariots aligned north–south; **camels** in North Africa by the
first century BCE; the unexpected **gold minting at Carthage** in the fourth century CE; and,
above all, the wealth of the **[[garamantes|Garamantes]]**, who imported lavish Roman goods that
their known exports cannot explain — likely paid for in **slaves** raided from the south (Andrew
Wilson). Herodotus' fifth-century-BCE description of a desert route, and finds of Mediterranean
goods at **Kissi (Burkina Faso)**, are read as traces of this earlier, thinner network.
*Strength:* fits the Garamantian evidence and the logic of their economy. *Weakness:* still
lacks direct proof of goods actually reaching the Sudan; the gold routes lay far from the
Garamantes.

## CWH IV's Framing

The two authors lean **cautiously revisionist** — Burstein and Austen both hold that
**some** cross-desert exchange existed in antiquity (centered on the Garamantes and on salt and
slaves rather than gold), while agreeing that **regular, large-scale, gold-based** trade
uniting the two shores is securely attested only from the eighth–ninth centuries CE. Per the
Contradiction Protocol both positions are recorded without adjudication.

## Resolution Status

**Open.** Recent scholarship (Mattingly's Garamantes excavations, the Kissi finds) has
"pushed in a more affirmative direction," but the **scale, regularity, and dating** of any
pre-Islamic trans-Saharan trade remain genuinely disputed, pending much more archaeology in
the Sudan and the desert.
