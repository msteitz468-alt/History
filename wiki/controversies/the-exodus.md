# The Exodus — Historicity and Date

The Exodus — the tradition of Israelite enslavement in Egypt, deliverance under
Moses, and entry into Canaan — is one of the foundational narratives of the
Hebrew Bible and one of the most disputed problems where biblical tradition meets
the archaeological and Egyptological record. CAH II.2 (chapter XXVI, on Palestine
in the period of the migrations) engages it through the source-critical and
chronological debates current in mid-20th-century scholarship.

Per the Contradiction Protocol this page **records** the positions; it does not
adjudicate.

## The Source Problem

CAH II.2 treats the Pentateuchal narrative through the framework of **JEDP source
criticism** (the documentary hypothesis associated with Eissfeldt and the
Wellhausen tradition): the text is a composite woven from sources (Yahwist J,
Elohist E, Deuteronomic D, Priestly P) written centuries after the events they
purport to describe. On this view the patriarchal and Exodus narratives are
shaped by the theological and political concerns of the monarchic and later
periods and cannot be read as direct reportage. The volume discusses the
patriarchs partly as **personifications** of tribal and ethnic relationships
rather than as straightforward biographies. This source-critical caution frames
every position below.

## The Positions

### 1. The "Early Date" (c. 1450 BCE)

Built on the internal biblical chronology: 1 Kings 6:1 places the Exodus 480 years
before Solomon's fourth year, yielding a mid-15th-century date. This puts the
Exodus in the reign of the early 18th-Dynasty pharaohs and the conquest of Canaan
in the [[amarna-letters|Amarna age]] — and proponents have linked the
**[[habiru|ʿApiru / Habiru]]** who trouble the Canaanite city-states in the Amarna
letters to the invading Hebrews. *Difficulties:* the 480 years is suspiciously
schematic (12 × 40); the Amarna ʿApiru are a social category, not an ethnos (see
[[habiru|Habiru]]); and the archaeology of the Canaanite cities does not fit a
15th-century conquest.

### 2. The "Late Date" (c. 1290–1260 BCE)

The majority position in the volume and in much 20th-century scholarship. It
identifies the pharaoh of the oppression with [[sethos-i|Sethos I]] and/or the
pharaoh of the Exodus with [[ramesses-ii|Ramesses II]], anchored on Exodus 1:11,
where the Israelites build the store-cities of **Pithom and Raamses** — the latter
being Pi-Ramesse, Ramesses II's eastern Delta capital. The [[merneptah|Merneptah]]
"Israel" stele (c. 1208 BCE), which names "Israel" as a people already in Canaan,
sets a *terminus ante quem*: Israel must be in the land by the late 13th century,
which a c. 1290–1260 Exodus and a generation of wandering can accommodate.

### 3. Largely Non-Historical / Mythologised Tradition

A position the volume gestures toward through its source criticism and that has
become dominant in later scholarship: the Exodus as we have it is a foundational
**origin myth** assembled long after any events it might dimly reflect, with
little or no recoverable historical core. On this view the emergence of Israel in
the central Canaanite highlands (c. 1200 BCE) was substantially an *indigenous*
process — pastoralists and displaced Canaanites coalescing in the
[[bronze-age-collapse-1200bce|collapse]] aftermath — rather than an external
invasion or migration from Egypt, with the Exodus tradition perhaps preserving the
memory of a smaller group's experience generalised to the whole nation.

## Cross-Cutting Threads

- **Hyksos memory.** The volume notes the possibility that the Exodus tradition
  preserves a folk-memory of the expulsion of the [[hyksos|Hyksos]] (the Semitic
  rulers expelled from the Delta c. 1550 BCE), telescoped and retold.
- **The ʿApiru/Hebrew link.** The phonetic resemblance of *ʿApiru/Habiru* to
  *ʿibri* (Hebrew) has tempted scholars since the Amarna letters were read, but the
  Habiru are a transregional social class, not the biblical nation; see
  [[habiru|Habiru]].
- **Archaeology of the conquest.** The destruction layers at Canaanite sites
  (Jericho, Ai, Hazor) do not align cleanly with any single conquest date — a
  major pressure on all the "invasion" models and a support for the indigenous-
  emergence view.

## Historiography and Status

CAH II.2 reflects mid-century critical orthodoxy: documentary-hypothesis source
criticism plus a preference for the late (Ramesside) date. Since 1975 the centre
of gravity has moved further toward position 3, driven by survey archaeology in
the highlands (Israel Finkelstein and others) showing a gradual indigenous
settlement, and by the broader collapse-era framework that makes early Israel one
of the small peoples emerging from the wreck of the Bronze Age Levant. The early
date retains adherents mainly in confessional scholarship. The debate is **open**;
its biblical-source dimension is inseparable from the wider question of how to use
the Hebrew Bible as a historical source (see [[hebrew-kingdom|Hebrew Kingdom]]).
