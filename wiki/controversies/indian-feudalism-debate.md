# The Indian Feudalism Debate

## The Dispute

How should the **early medieval period** of South Asia (c. 300–900 CE) be characterized? In
[[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|CWH IV]] (Kaul, Ch 18) this is the central historiographical question
of the post-Gupta centuries, and a genuine interpretive conflict between serious scholars
over whether the era was one of **decline and feudal fragmentation** or of **integration and
new state formation**. The crux is how to read the spread of royal **land grants** to
brahmanas, temples, and officials, with their fiscal immunities.

## Positions

**1. Indian feudalism (R. S. Sharma and followers).**
The early medieval period saw a **crisis**: the decline of long-distance trade, the **end of
the money economy**, the decay and desertion of towns, and an **agrarian shift / ruralization**
driven by tax-free land grants — together producing a **decentralized, feudal** order
"patterned closely on classical European developments." *Strength:* land grants and the
growth of an intermediary landed class are well attested. *Weakness:* later work disputes the
claimed collapse of trade, money, and urbanism.

**2. Integrative state formation / "Third Urbanization" (B. D. Chattopadhyaya, Herman Kulke,
and others).**
The image of a "drastic and pervasive crisis" is **erroneous**. Trade and money did **not**
vanish; while some cities (Taxila, Varanasi, Pataliputra) declined, others flourished and
**new urban centres arose** by the 8th–9th c. (a "Third Urbanization"). Land grants played an
**acculturating, integrative** role — extending cultivation, drawing new regions and groups
into caste society and the brahmanical fold, and letting kings build **sovereignty over an
expanding realm** through donation. State formation was now **diffused and subregionally
rooted** rather than centered on the Gangetic valley. *Strength:* better fits the evidence of
continued commerce and new cities. *Weakness:* "integration" can understate real
dislocations.

## CWH IV's Position

The chapter sides substantially with the **revisionist** view: it states that "later
scholarship has shown the Indian feudalism theory to be **deeply flawed**" and that the
image of pervasive crisis is mistaken, while endorsing the **integrative state formation** and
**Third Urbanization** perspectives. Per the Contradiction Protocol, both positions are
nonetheless recorded here with their best arguments.

## The Comparative-Category Objection (CWH V, Reynolds)

[[kedar-cwh-v5-2015|CWH V]] (Susan Reynolds, Ch 4) adds a third, more radical line of
attack that undercuts the debate's very terms. Reynolds — the leading critic of
"feudalism" as a construct — argues that **"feudalism" is itself a flawed
eighteenth/nineteenth-century European category** that fuses two distinct ideas
(elite vassalage-and-fiefholding; Marxist landlord–peasant relations), fits the
European evidence poorly, and fits **other societies worse still** when they are
"squashed into it" by words like *fief*, *vassal*, and *enfeoffment*. On this view
the "Indian feudalism" framing imports not just a contested empirical model but a
misleading vocabulary; Reynolds urges deliberately **generic, untechnical language**
and warns against the teleology of reading South Asian social structure as a
variation on a European template. See [[social-hierarchy-and-solidarity]] and
[[eurocentrism]]. This strengthens the revisionist case while questioning whether
"feudalism" — Indian or European — is a usable comparative category at all.

## Resolution Status

**Partially resolved.** The strong "feudalism = European-style decline" thesis has lost
ground; the integrative/Third-Urbanization model is now dominant in the scholarship CWH IV
reflects, and Reynolds's critique (CWH V) presses further by challenging the category
"feudalism" itself. But the broader question — how to weigh land grants, ruralization, and
continued commerce — remains an active field (the "Feudalism Debate" literature).
