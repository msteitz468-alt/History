# Historicity of the Lelantine War

Whether the **Lelantine War** was a major early Archaic multi-state conflict, a limited local war later magnified, or largely a modern reconstruction. [[sources/hall-history-archaic-greek-world-2014|Hall (2014)]] Ch.1 is the wiki’s principal method-first treatment.

## Positions

### Maximalist multi-polis war
Thucydides treats the war as widely known and involving many allies; modern narratives assemble Samos, Miletus, Thessaly, Corinth, Megara, etc., into a “first Panhellenic war.”

### Local war, limited allies
Accepts fighting over the Lelantine plain but rejects the grand alliance roster as non-contemporary and inconsistent.

### Composite / methodologically insecure (Hall)
Hall argues the impressive composite is “probably little more than a modern historian’s fantasy, cobbled together from isolated pieces of information that, both singly and in combination, command little confidence.” Authors span some nine centuries from Hesiod, Archilochus, and Theognis to Aelian.

## Hall’s source tests (detail)

**Late elaboration of earlier authors.** Late testimony weighs less if derived from sources we already possess. Plutarch’s poetic contests at Amphidamas’ funeral “stand a good chance of representing an elaboration on the testimony of Hesiod, who never actually mentions the Lelantine War.”

**Thucydides requires tradition, not authenticity.** Hall reads Thucydides’ Lelantine War as rhetorical parallel to the Trojan War (earlier intra-Greek war lesser than the Peloponnesian War just as Troy is lesser than the Persian War). Readers had to know a multi-ally story, but “the existence of a tradition that predates Thucydides does not guarantee its authenticity,” and no earlier literary source implies a broader conflict.

**Anachronistic alliance stack.** Modern rosters mix hostilities that cannot be contemporary (e.g. Corinthian expulsion of Eretrians from Corcyra traditionally 733 vs Argos–Aegina alliance ca. 500). The Eretrian settlement on Corcyra is mentioned only by Plutarch and has “received absolutely no corroboration from archaeological investigations on the island.”

**Monuments create memory.** Plutarch’s Thessalian intervention may derive from a monument-story at Chalcis (the column of Kleomakhos) rather than the same tradition Thucydides knew: “Monuments may create, as much as perpetuate, social memory.”

**Herodotus’ Eretria–Miletus alliance.** Herodotus (5.99) cites an earlier Eretria–Miletus vs Chalcis–Samos alliance to explain Eretrian aid to the Ionians in 499; wording leaves little room for additional combatants, and “neither can we exclude the possibility that the earlier, undated alliance was invented to justify Eretrian intervention at the beginning of the fifth century.”

**Cavalry inference from class names.** Hall doubts that Aristotle’s cavalry war between Eretria and Chalcis comes from Archilochus (who implies swords/infantry); more likely Aristotle inferred cavalry combat from elite names Hippeis (Eretria) and Hippobotai (Chalcis).

**Circular dating of Hesiod.** Hesiod’s conventional date ca. 700 partly depends on assuming he was contemporary with the Lelantine War—circular, since only Plutarch, not Hesiod, associates Amphidamas with that war: “Such circular reasoning cannot command much faith.”

**Poetic traditions are cumulative.** Archilochus is conventionally mid-seventh century, but literary scholars doubt autobiographical reading of Archaic lyric; the Theognidea “is more of a compendium of Archaic Greek poetry than the work of a single author.”

**Contextual fit of Kleomakhos burial.** Even assuming a real war ca. 700, burial of distinguished warriors in the agora and tall-column grave markers fit poorly for the early seventh century (columns unlikely before the sixth at earliest). West Gate “heroizing” burials exist but are not necessarily urban-agora practice.

**Chronological scatter.** Evidence points scatter from West Gate ca. 720 and Lefkandi house abandonment, through Archilochus mid-seventh century, to Theognidea’s Cypselid descendants later still. A continuous war of a century and a half would be remarkable yet uncommented. Hall’s conclusion: “In short, we do not know when – or even whether – the Lelantine War occurred.”

**Positivist fallacy.** Matching archaeology to textual correlates risks equating the archaeologically visible with the historically significant (Snodgrass via Hall).

## Shared factual ground

Chalcis and Eretria were major late Geometric centres; the plain between them is agriculturally valuable; later authors and some archaeological complexes (Eretria West Gate) have been linked to conflict memory. Outcome is unrecorded; Lefkandi “destruction” ca. 700 is overstated as war evidence.
