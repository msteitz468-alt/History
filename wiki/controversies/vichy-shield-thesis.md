# Was Vichy a Shield?

Did [[vichy-france]] protect the French from a worse fate — the "lesser evil"
— or did its collaboration cost France more than direct occupation would
have? This was the central question of postwar French memory, argued first in
a courtroom and then in the historiography.

## Position 1 — The shield thesis

Its founding text is Pétain's own Sigmaringen declaration (August 1944,
drafted by Henri Massis): "If I could not be your sword, I tried to be your
shield," elaborated at his 1945 trial ("Every day, a dagger at my throat, I
struggled against the enemy's demands… While General de Gaulle carried on the
struggle outside our frontiers, I prepared the way for Liberation by
preserving France, suffering but alive"). Laval claimed to have acted as a
"screen"; Xavier Vallat argued at his trial that 95% of French-national Jews
survived against 8% across nine fully-occupied countries, so "it was better
that the French Government got into it." Robert Aron's *Histoire de Vichy*
(1954), working largely from trial records, gave the thesis scholarly form:
material life was easier in France than elsewhere under occupation; the
reproaches against Vichy "are moral rather than material." A related motif is
the "double game" — Pétain secretly with the Allies — and the sword-and-shield
complementarity of Pétain and de Gaulle.

## Position 2 — Paxton's comparative demolition (1972)

[[paxton-vichy-france-1972]], written from the captured German archives,
attacked the thesis on both its legs.

**Collaboration was sought, not endured.** The German records show Vichy as
suitor: Montoire, the Paris Protocols, Darlan's note verbale of 14 July 1941
were French initiatives rebuffed or ignored by a Germany interested only in
exploitation ("There is no doubt… who was the suitor and who was the wooed";
Pétain in the German archives is "Marshal Bitte," not "Marshal Nein"). Hitler
himself investigated and dismissed the "double game" rumor in 1940.

**The shield protected nothing measurable.** The valid comparison is with
fully-occupied Western countries, not Poland (whose fate was fixed by Nazi
racial policy). On that comparison: French caloric intake was the lowest in
Western Europe except Italy; the franc fell faster than any Western currency
but the lira; occupation costs took 58% of state income; no territorial
concessions were ever won. Forced labor took ~3.3% of the French population
against Belgium's 3.4% and Holland's 3.0% — refuting Laval's claimed 80%-vs-16%
contrast — and France was in 1943 the largest single supplier of foreign male
labor to Germany in all occupied Europe. On the Jews, Paxton reframes the
question: not whether France's 26% Jewish population loss beat Holland's 86%,
but "whether more Jews were deported from France because of Vichy
preparations and assistance than would have been the case if the Germans had
had to do it all alone. Vichy bears a heavy burden of responsibility, seen in
these terms" (see [[vichy-anti-jewish-laws-1940]] and [[the-holocaust]]).
Structurally, Vichy could never bargain because it could not credibly
threaten to defect: its leaders would have lost the National Revolution.
Verdict: "Judged by its fruits, Vichy negotiation was barren… A gauleiter
would have made many Frenchmen suffer; in the end he might have gotten less."

## Resolution status

**Partially resolved toward Position 2.** Paxton's findings — collaboration
as French initiative, the National Revolution as indigenous, the failure of
the shield on comparative measures — became the mainstream of French and
Anglophone scholarship after the book's stormy 1973 French reception, and no
substantial scholarly rehabilitation of the shield thesis has followed. What
remains genuinely open is second-order: how much protective effect Vichy's
residual administration had at the margin (the survival rate of *French*
citizen Jews relative to foreign Jews in France remains the shield argument's
strongest surviving datum, though Paxton attributes it to German priorities
and French geography more than Vichy protection), and the moral weighting of
complicity versus constraint. Paxton's own caution about his sources — the
book sees Vichy through German archives, French ones being closed — is part
of the record.
