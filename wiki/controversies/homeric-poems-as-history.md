# The Homeric Poems as History (the Homeric Question)

How much real history — and of *which* age — do the *Iliad* and *Odyssey* preserve?
This is the "Homeric Question" in its evidentiary form: not who Homer was (see
[[homer|Homer]]) but **whether and how far the poems can be used as a source** for the
Late Bronze Age they purport to describe. [[edwards-cah-v2p2-1975|CAH Vol. II.2]]
(Ch. XXXIX(b), G. S. Kirk) is itself a sustained intervention in the dispute. It is a
genuine **source-reliability** controversy, parallel to the standing wiki
controversies on the [[reliability-of-herodotus|reliability of Herodotus]] and the
[[the-exodus|historicity of the Exodus]].

## The core problem

The poems were composed (in monumental form) in **eighth-century Ionia**, roughly
**half a millennium after** the [[trojan-war-1250bce|Trojan War]] they describe, and
were transmitted across that gap by an **oral, illiterate** tradition (writing having
vanished with [[aegean-scripts|Linear B]]). They therefore "may reflect events,
objects, customs, beliefs and techniques from any or every period within this

## Positions

**1. A continuous Bronze Age poetic tradition (the optimistic case).** On this view,
substantial Mycenaean-age poetry survived more or less continuously into Homer,
carrying accurate Bronze Age detail. Its **best evidence**:
- the **Catalogue of Ships** (*Iliad* II), a political geography of Mycenaean Greece
  naming ~170 places, half identified as Achaean sites, many (Eutresis, Dorium,
  Pylos) abandoned at the Dorian migrations and unlocatable to later Greeks — "a solid
  core of information" that "cannot be just an archaizing construction";
- datable **Bronze Age realia** described accurately: the **boar's-tusk helmet**
  (*Il.* X), the **body-shield** of Ajax, the **silver-studded sword**, metal
  **greaves**, Nestor's cup;
- specific name-epithets ("lord of men Agamemnon," using the Mycenaean term *wanax*).

**2. Discontinuity — chiefly Dark Age and Ionian (Kirk's own argument).** Kirk
emphasizes the limits of the optimistic case and a "strong discontinuity" in detail:
- **Linguistic proof of lateness:** pervasive **vowel-contraction** and **ignored
  digamma** in basic formulae are post-Mycenaean (probably tenth-century), showing
  "much of the formular system... was developed at least two or three hundred years
  after the fall of Troy";
- **Misunderstood Bronze Age institutions:** Homeric **chariot warfare** (heroes
  ride to the fight and dismount to spear-duel — an "equine taxi-service") *misuses*
  the real Bronze Age chariot (a massed mobile platform), proving no detailed poetic
  tradition of Achaean warfare survived;
- **The palace economy is absent:** the Linear B tablets reveal a minutely
  specialized, scribal, redistributive bureaucracy that the poems — even in their
  palace scenes (Pylos, Sparta, Scherie) — know nothing of; *basileus* means "king"
  in Homer but "mayor/overseer" in the tablets;
- Conclusion: most "Bronze Age" memory descended **not** as continuous poetry but as
  ordinary prose reminiscence for two or three generations, then was crystallized in
  Dark Age verse. The Catalogue is the one probable exception.

**3. The Analyst position (superseded).** Nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century
"Analysts" dissected the poems into an original core plus the work of later redactors
and editors. Kirk holds this "largely obsolete": the comparative study of oral poetry
shows an organic, continuous process of elaboration by generations of singers, not
editorial strata — "one main contributor... and scores or even hundreds of preceding
subsidiary singers."

**4. Oral-traditional unitarianism (Kirk's framework).** Following Milman Parry and
A. B. Lord's analysis of the **formulaic system** and the living **Yugoslav
*guslari***, the poems are seen as the product of a long oral tradition culminating in
a single **monumental composer** for each epic. This frame dissolves the old
unity/multiplicity debate but raises new ones (dictation to a scribe? one poet for
both poems? how fixed was the early text?).

## What is (relatively) agreed

- The poems are **traditional and oral** in technique, with a real eighth-century
  **monumental composer** working in Ionia.
- The **broad outline** of the subject — a coalition Achaean attack on Troy, Agamemnon
  of Mycenae as over-king, the fall of the city, troubled homecomings — belongs to the
  **Bronze Age**; the **similes and everyday detail** belong mainly to the **Ionian**
  present.
- The poems are therefore **unreliable for precise Bronze Age fact** but invaluable as
  a picture of a remembered Heroic Age and as the chief evidence that an oral culture
  persisted through the [[greek-dark-age|Dark Age]].

## Resolution status

**Open.** CAH II.2 (Kirk) argues the cautious, discontinuity-leaning position;
the decipherment of Linear B (1952) had shortly before encouraged optimism about
Bronze Age survivals, and the Hittite [[ahhiyawa-question|Aḫḫiyawa]] texts pointed the
same way. Later excavation (Korfmann at Troy) and continued oral-theory work keep the
debate alive. This page records the positions; per CLAUDE.md it does not adjudicate.
