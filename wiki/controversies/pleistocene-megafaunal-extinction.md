## The Dispute

In the Late Pleistocene, large-bodied animals went extinct across multiple
continents — over 30 genera in North America, the giant marsupials, birds, and
reptiles of Sahul, and large fauna in South America and northern Eurasia. The
extinctions broadly **follow the arrival of *Homo sapiens*** in each landmass
(Australia ~46,000–50,000 BP; the Americas ~13,000–12,000 BP), but they also
coincide with major **climatic transitions**. Did humans cause them, did climate
cause them, or both? This is one of the central causation debates in Paleolithic
prehistory, and it recurs independently on each continent — which is precisely
what makes it powerful evidence and hard to resolve.

## Positions

### Position 1: Overkill (human hunting primary)
- Megafauna that had never encountered human hunters were insufficiently wary;
  even low-intensity hunting of slow-reproducing large animals can drive
  extinction ("blitzkrieg" in its strong form)
- **Americas**: Clovis kill sites (Colby, Dent, Naco, Lehner, Murray Springs)
  give the clearest global evidence of human predation on proboscideans;
  experiments show Clovis points penetrate elephant hide. Extinctions track the
  spread of hunters.
- **Australia**: the **Lynch's Crater *Sporormiella* sequence** — dung-fungus
  spores (a proxy for large-herbivore biomass) collapse ~41,000 BP, *before*
  the rise in charcoal and fire-tolerant vegetation. This ordering implies
  humans removed the megafauna first and the vegetation change was a
  *consequence*, not the cause.

### Position 2: Climate (environmental change primary)
- Extinctions coincide with abrupt climate events: the **Younger Dryas**
  (~12,900–11,700 BP) in the Americas; the "great drying" and aridification in
  Sahul. Shifting vegetation and water stress could collapse megafauna
  populations independent of people.
- **Americas counter-evidence** (Grayson & Meltzer): only ~14 of 37 extinct
  genera have *any* kill or butchery association; mammoth remains may be
  over-represented simply because large bones are easier to find. If Clovis
  rarely targeted megafauna, and there is no evidence of large-scale habitat
  modification, the human role becomes tenuous.
- **Australia**: the near-total **absence of kill sites** (the one contender,
  Cuddie Springs, is stratigraphically disputed) weakens a direct-hunting story.

### Position 3: Synergy (both, interacting)
- Human arrival and climate stress coincided closely on several continents;
  each may have been survivable alone but not together. Hunting pressure on
  populations already stressed by environmental change, or human-set fire
  altering vegetation, could tip declining species over the edge.
- This is increasingly the default position, though it is criticized as
  difficult to falsify.

## Why It Stays Unresolved

- **Dating resolution**: the coincidence of "human arrival" and "climate change"
  is often within the error bars of the dating methods, so temporal ordering
  (cause vs. effect) is hard to establish.
- **Taphonomic bias**: the archaeological and paleontological records over-
  represent large, durable bones and conspicuous kill sites, inflating the
  apparent role of big-game hunting.
- **Continental contrast**: the Americas have kill sites but a short human
  prelude; Australia has a long human prelude but no kill sites. No single
  model fits both cleanly, which is itself an argument that local conditions
  mattered.

## Resolution Status

**Open.** Few researchers now defend pure single-cause models; the debate has
shifted toward *weighting* human vs. climatic contributions and toward
continent-specific reconstructions. The *Sporormiella* proxy strengthened the
human case in Australia; the Grayson–Meltzer critique strengthened the climate
case in the Americas. The two regional records pull in opposite directions.
