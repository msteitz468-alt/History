# The Morality of Strategic Bombing

The dispute over whether the Anglo-American area bombing of German cities (see [[strategic-bombing-campaign]]) was militarily necessary, morally justified, both, or neither. Genuinely open: as Evans writes, "Arguments will no doubt continue to rage over this difficult question."

## Positions

- **Military necessity / war-shortening.** Bombing diverted the Luftwaffe and hundreds of thousands of men and 88mm guns to home defense, crippled fuel and transport in 1944–45, made Overlord possible, and shortened the war, saving Allied (and arguably German) lives. Held broadly by official histories and defenders of Harris; the economic-effects case strengthened by Tooze and by USSBS data on 1944 production losses.
- **Disproportionate / criminal.** Undermining civilian morale and revenge were explicit aims ("customarily . . . regarded as a war crime" — Evans's phrasing of the customary-law standard); 400,000–500,000 civilians died; the final year's bombing (1.18 of 1.42 million tons) was "too indiscriminate to be justifiable" once the war was decided. Associated with Grayling (*Among the Dead Cities*, 2006), earlier with the Dresden debate; in Germany with Friedrich's *Der Brand* (2002), which in turn triggered a controversy about victim-discourse relativization.
- **Phase-distinguishing positions.** Many scholars (Overy, Evans himself) accept the campaign's strategic contribution while condemning its late-war continuation and the morale-bombing rationale — Evans records both that it shortened the war and that the German dead "were not mere 'collateral damage.'"
