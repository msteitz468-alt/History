# American Influence on Nazi Race Law (Nuremberg Laws, 1935)

**Core dispute:** To what extent, if any, did Nazi lawyers and policymakers draw direct inspiration, precedent, or models from United States race law (federal immigration/naturalization statutes, state second-class citizenship regimes, and state anti-miscegenation / "race defilement" laws) when drafting the Reich Citizenship Law and the Law for the Protection of German Blood and German Honour (the "Nuremberg Laws" promulgated 15 September 1935)?

This is a dispute over interpretive weight and causal mechanics in a specific domain of legal technology, not a claim that the United States caused Nazism, the Holocaust, or even the general direction of Nazi antisemitism.

## Positions

**Position A: Sustained, significant, and policy-relevant interest; direct influence on key provisions (Whitman 2017 and supporting evidence).**

- The United States in the 1920s–early 1930s was the "classic example" and innovative global leader in explicit racist legislation in the eyes of Nazi observers (not limited to the South).
- Primary evidence (grounded in transcript and documents): (1) detailed Ministry of Justice memo on American race law presented at the June 5, 1934 planning meeting chaired by Franz Gürtner; participants "returned repeatedly" to the models; (2) verbatim transcript shows radicals (esp. Roland Freisler) as the most ardent champions — Freisler: "This jurisprudence would suit us perfectly, with a single exception... Over there they have in mind, practically speaking, only coloreds and half-coloreds... but the Jews... are not reckoned among the coloreds." Freisler advocated proceeding with American-style "primitivity" ("A state even simply says: 'colored people.' Such a procedure would be crude [roh], but it would suffice."); (3) the 1933 Prussian Memorandum invoked Jim Crow ("the southern states of North America maintain the most stringent separation...") while proposing its own "limited" version and new crimes of "Race Treason"; (4) *Mein Kampf* and Nazi legal literature (Krieger, Kier in the *National Socialist Handbook*, Koellreutter, Leers, Sahm) repeatedly praised US immigration quotas, second-class citizenship devices, and anti-miscegenation statutes as achieving the "fundamental recognition" of race order.
- Specific borrowings argued: American anti-miscegenation statutes (criminal penalties, void marriages, classification rules) supplied the leading example for criminalizing "Rassenschande" in the Blood Law; US second-class citizenship (disfranchisement, "non-citizen nationals," covert "Umwege" around equality rhetoric) informed the Reich Citizenship Law's *Staatsbürger* / nationals distinction. Heinrich Krieger (Arkansas Law exchange student) was the single most important vector.
- Nuance in the position: Nazis sometimes rejected US approaches as *too* harsh (one-drop rule; "unforgiving hardness"); Jews classified Caucasian; US relied on subterfuge while Nazis wanted open statutory racism; moderates (Gürtner, Lösener) resisted on grounds of civil-law "legal science"; influence was conceptual/inspirational and selective, never wholesale copy. Radicals pushed hardest; the meeting records real debate.
- This position treats the evidence as showing genuine policy-making engagement, not mere taunting or deflection. "Only a naive... understanding of law... would dismiss the American example as insignificant." (Whitman)

**Position B: References existed but were insignificant, propagandistic, or not a meaningful source of influence (prior scholarly consensus: Rethmeier 1995, Bernstein, Hanke, Guettel 2012, and others).**

- Nazi citations of US (especially Southern) race law were few, fleeting, and primarily rhetorical — deployed to claim spurious parallels or to deflect foreign criticism after 1933, not to borrow operational models.
- Key counter-evidence cited: American law classified Jews as Caucasian (a "gross error" from the Nazi racial viewpoint); the US maintained a formal constitutional commitment to equality (Reconstruction Amendments) that Nazis contemptuously rejected; segregation (Jim Crow proper) was not central to the Nuremberg Laws' text or purpose (which focused on citizenship stripping and sexual/reproductive boundaries rather than public accommodations or education).
- Broader claim: the Nazis had abundant indigenous German sources (imperial precedents, völkisch ideology, post-1918 radicalization) and did not "need" American lessons in racism. Any apparent parallels are superficial or coincidental. The detailed transcript discussion is acknowledged but interpreted as confirming that US material was ultimately unusable for Jews.
- Some versions of this position acknowledge Nazi interest in US eugenics (Kühl) or westward expansion analogies for *Lebensraum*, but sharply distinguish those from the drafting of the 1935 race laws themselves.

**Shared factual ground (framed per side).**

Both sides agree that Nazi legal publications and internal documents from the early 1930s contain multiple, non-trivial references to US race statutes and jurisprudence (Prussian Memorandum, June 5 1934 transcript with prepared Ministry memo, *Mein Kampf*, Krieger/Kier/others). The transcript records detailed discussion of US immigration, second-class citizenship, and anti-miscegenation rules, with Freisler explicitly championing the approach. Both agree that the Nuremberg Laws did not replicate any single American statute verbatim and that Nazis sometimes viewed US measures as too harsh or constrained by liberalism. The disagreement is over the *weight* and *direction of influence* in the actual drafting process (June 1934 meeting, Prussian Memorandum → final 1935 text) and whether the references were operational/policy-shaping or merely instrumental/propagandistic.

## Historiography and Resolution Status

Pre-Whitman scholarly consensus (especially German and some US legal historians) leaned strongly toward Position B: references were real but unimportant for understanding the content or causation of the Nuremberg Laws.

Whitman (2017) assembles the primary transcript and memorandum evidence in detail and argues for Position A with careful qualifications. The book has reopened the question in legal-historical circles.

As of 2026, the dispute remains **open** in the broader historiographical discourse (partially resolved in specialized legal-comparative scholarship toward greater weight on documented engagement). Whitman's assembly of the June 5, 1934 transcript and Prussian Memorandum evidence has forced re-examination of the prior consensus; the "insignificance" position must now account for the primary record of policy discussion rather than dismiss references as mere rhetoric. No new full consensus has crystallized. The wiki preserves both positions with attribution and primary quotes.

The wiki records both positions in their strongest forms with attribution. The curator's working assessment (high confidence in the *fact* of sustained Nazi study and selective borrowing for legal technique; low confidence in any stronger causal claim that US models "produced" the laws or the subsequent radicalization) is noted separately as a dated curator note rather than absorbed into wiki voice.
