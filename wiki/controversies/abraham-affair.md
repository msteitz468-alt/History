# The Abraham Affair

The 1980s dispute over David Abraham's *The Collapse of the Weimar Republic*
(Princeton UP, 1981) — the sharpest modern test case of whether historical
interpretations can be falsified by evidence, and of how the profession polices
evidentiary standards. Substantively it concerns the old "agency" question: did German
big business put Hitler in power? (See [[appointment-of-hitler-30-january-1933]],
[[weimar-republic]], [[causes-of-the-second-world-war]].) Source: Evans, *In Defence
of History* (1997), ch. 4, who was himself an early favorable reviewer.

## The Course of the Dispute

Abraham's structural-Marxist account (rival agrarian/industrial power blocs, schema
from Nicos Poulantzas) was initially well reviewed. **Henry Ashby Turner** (Yale,
avowedly anti-Marxist, then completing *Big Business and the Rise of Hitler*) publicly
accused Abraham of "deliberately inventing and falsifying archival material."
**Gerald Feldman** — who had originally recommended publication despite flagged
errors — learned that graduate student **Ulrich Nocken** had found "hundreds of
egregious mistakes": paraphrases printed as quotations, misattributed documents,
mistranslations. The decisive example: Abraham quoted an industrialist's letter as
calling it desirable "to crystallize the bourgeois right and the Nazi Party into
one" — the original said **not** desirable. Abraham admitted his research notes had
conflated quotation and paraphrase. Feldman's campaign of circulars and calls to
hiring departments ("unfit to be a member of the scholarly community") ended
Abraham's career; he retrained as a lawyer and re-entered academia in law.

## Positions

- **Turner/Feldman**: the errors were falsification-in-effect; Feldman: their general
  tendency was "to exaggerate the evil role played by industry, to overdramatize its
  power and self-assurance, and to make arguments far beyond what the evidence would
  allow."
- **Abraham and defenders** (Lawrence Stone, Peter Novick): errors were careless, not
  fraudulent ("I don't believe any scholar in the Western world has impeccable
  footnotes" — Stone); the prosecution was a political proxy war of "fact fetishists"
  against Marxist historical imagination, and the punishment wildly disproportionate.
- **Evans's assessment**: not deliberate falsification but Abraham "subconsciously
  moulded the evidence... to fit the interpretation he had worked out beforehand";
  crucially, his "corrected" second edition fixed citations while altering "not one
  single aspect of his interpretation." Yet the affair is *not* reducible to
  Marxist-vs-anti-Marxist evidentiary incommensurability (Hayden White's framing):
  respected Marxists like Tim Mason publicly endorsed the accuracy standard. The
  professional death penalty, however, was wrong — Evans's pointed parallel:
  **Lawrence Stone himself**, at a similar age in 1951, had his gentry-decline article
  demolished by Trevor-Roper and Cooper ("one of the most vitriolic attacks ever made
  by one historian on another"), admitted "very serious mistakes," moved to Princeton,
  and became one of the most influential historians of his generation. "To deny
  Abraham the same chance of making amends... was surely wrong."

## Significance

Cited on both sides of [[postmodernism-and-historical-knowledge]]: as proof that
evidentiary standards are politically weaponized, and as proof that "interpretations
really can be tested and confirmed or falsified by an appeal to the evidence"
(Evans, pairing it with the 1950s "storm over the gentry"). Also a standing
methodological caution — J. H. Hexter's rule contra Christopher Hill's
thesis-first evidence-gathering: the historian "needs to look for vulnerabilities in
that thesis and to contrive means of testing them."
