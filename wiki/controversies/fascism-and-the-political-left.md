# Was Fascism a Movement of the Left?

This page records a dispute that is **institutionally asymmetric**: one position
holds the near-unanimous support of academic fascism studies; the other is
carried mainly by classical-liberal economists, political writers, and popular
works, and has been enormously influential in twenty-first-century American
public discourse. Per the Contradiction Protocol, both positions are recorded
with their best arguments; the wiki does not adjudicate between them.

## Position 1 — Academic fascism studies: fascism as revolutionary right

The academic field (Robert Paxton, *The Anatomy of Fascism*; Roger Griffin,
*The Nature of Fascism*; Stanley Payne, *A History of Fascism*; Richard Evans,
*The Coming of the Third Reich*; Zeev Sternhell on the intellectual origins)
treats fascism as a **revolutionary nationalism of the right**: anti-liberal,
anti-conservative in style but allying with conservative elites in practice,
and violently anti-Marxist. Key evidence:

- Fascists and Nazis rose to power by destroying the parties and unions of the
  left; communists and social democrats filled the first concentration camps
  after the [[nazi-seizure-of-power-1933]].
- The Nazi "socialist" label (NSDAP) was rejected in substance: the party's
  left-Strasserite wing was purged (Night of the Long Knives, 1934), private
  property and big industry were retained under state direction, and
  independent labor organization was abolished.
- Fascism's defining commitments — hierarchy, ultranationalism, palingenetic
  rebirth myth, cult of violence, anti-egalitarianism — sit opposite the
  left's egalitarian universalism, however statist particular left regimes
  have been. The comparative page [[communism-and-fascism]] records both the
  structural similarities of the totalitarian regimes and the doctrinal
  opposition.
- Mussolini's personal origin as a socialist journalist is undisputed but
  marks a **break**, not a continuity: his 1914–15 interventionism expelled
  him from the Socialist Party, and Italian Fascism was built in armed combat
  against the socialist movement (the *biennio rosso* squadrism of 1920–22).

*Paxton in his own words (now ingested — [[paxton-vichy-france-1972]]):* his
1972 ideal-type, the seed of *The Anatomy of Fascism*, defines fascism as "a
mass antiliberal, anticommunist movement, radical in its willingness to
employ force and in its contempt for the upper-class values of the time,
sharply distinct not only from its enemies on the left but also from its
rivals on the right, traditional conservatives" — noting that "no undiluted
fascist regime has wielded power": every fascist movement reached power in
coalition with conservatives. On that spectrum he places Vichy's
[[national-revolution-vichy|National Revolution]] "nearer the conservative
than the fascist end" — while conceding a broader sense in which "hard
measures by a frightened middle class — that, indeed, is one good general
definition of fascism. In that broader sense, Vichy was fascist."

*Paxton's mature statement (now ingested — [[paxton-anatomy-of-fascism-2004]]):*
*The Anatomy of Fascism* (2004) is Position 1's fullest single text, and it
gives both sides material. For Position 1: fascism's founding act was a
declaration of "war against socialism"; its rooting everywhere ran through
armed destruction of the organized Left (the Po Valley squadrism
statistics; "Fascist violence was neither random nor indiscriminate…
only the fascists were tough enough to save the nation"); its route to
power "has always passed through cooperation with conservative elites";
and in power it "left the distribution of property and the economic and
social hierarchy largely intact" — "revolutionary in its radically new
conceptions of citizenship… counterrevolutionary, however, with respect to
such traditional projects of the Left as individual liberties, human
rights, due process, and international peace." What fascism offered
conservatives was "a magic formula for weaning workers away from Marxism."
But Paxton also documents, without embarrassment, the facts Position 2
builds on: the 1919 program was "a kind of 'national socialism'" (women's
suffrage, capital levy, seizure of Church property); the founding
constituencies included revolutionary syndicalists, and "Mussolini… was,
in turn, Sorel's most successful disciple"; the Cercle Proudhon (1911) was
"the first concrete example of 'national socialism' in practice"; fascists
insisted they were "neither Right nor Left" with an absolute contempt for
the center; and the 19th-century Left was "a capacious family" including
nationalists and anti-Semites, whose post-1880s Marxist discipline expelled
"the old working-class xenophobia it had once tolerated" — after which
"those young people and intellectuals who were heated by insurrectionary
fevers but still clung to the Nation found a new home in fascism." His
verdict on the program drift: "we must not let its conspicuous presence in
early texts confuse us about what Fascism later became in action." See
[[generic-fascism]] for the full definitional apparatus.

*Payne in his own words (now ingested — [[payne-history-of-fascism-1995]]):*
the field's standard narrative historian gives both sides their best
documented material. For Position 2's genealogy, Payne is emphatic that
Italian Fascism's founding cadre came from the revolutionary left: "The
nucleus that eventually founded Fascism in Italy did not... stem either from
the cultural elite or from the right-wing nationalists, but from the
transformation of part of the revolutionary left," the revolutionary
syndicalists — who justified "proletarian nationalism" partly by citing Marx
and Engels' own endorsements of imperialism. The very word *fascio* "had
been standard practice among various sectors of Italian radicalism since the
1870s"; the 1919 San Sepolcro program was "basically leftist"; through 1920
Mussolini pursued a "national socialism"/"labor party" strategy; Lenin
praised Mussolini's 1912 party takeover, and Gregor (quoted by Payne) finds
Leninism and early Mussolinianism structurally parallel (vanguard elites
bringing consciousness to the masses "from without"). Payne even documents
the Salò Republic's terminal "return to origins": the February 1944
socialization decree was "Mussolini's revenge against the bourgeoisie."
Payne also states fascism "was of neither the right nor the left" in its
self-conception, as ideological "latecomers" hostile to all existing
currents. But for Position 1 he is equally firm: the fascists' *actual*
allies were "on the radical authoritarian right"; the agent theory of
capitalism is "definitively" refuted, yet so is the notion of fascism as
socialism in power — squadrismo was built on killing Socialists (Salvemini's
~900 dead by 1922), and Comintern strategy itself "was aimed not at
preventing a Nazi triumph but at weakening the Socialists." His three-sector
typology ([[payne-typology-of-authoritarian-nationalism]]) is the field's
standard rebuttal to any one-axis left/right placement.

*Sternhell in his own words (now ingested —
[[sternhell-birth-fascist-ideology-1994]]):* the scholar both positions cite
is now on the shelf, and he resists conscription by either. His thesis
([[sternhell-thesis]]): fascist ideology was "a synthesis of organic
nationalism with the antimaterialist revision of Marxism" — emphatically "a
revision of Marxism and not a variety of Marxism or a consequence of
Marxism" — complete before 1914 and born on the revolutionary Left as much
as on the nationalist Right. For Position 2's genealogy he supplies the
richest documentation in the literature: the Sorelian pipeline
([[sorel-georges]]), the [[cercle-proudhon]] where syndicalists and
Maurrassians jointly resolved "to destroy democratic institutions,"
[[mussolini-benito|Mussolini]]'s syndicalist formation ("my Marxism was...
a Marxism revised by Sorel"), the *Utopia* circle that forked into future
Communists and future Fascists, and the June 1919 Fascist program copied
"almost [as] an exact replica" from [[de-ambris-alceste|De Ambris]]'s
national-syndicalist expropriation plan (see [[national-syndicalism]]).
But his framework equally denies Position 2's conclusion: the synthesis is
"neither left nor right" — its second pillar is integral nationalism, its
essence the rejection of the Enlightenment, and it kept the market economy
and private property ("a revolutionary capitalism"), which cuts against the
"fascism = collectivism" equation; and his fascism/Nazism firewall (racism
"was not a necessary condition for the existence of fascism") complicates
any argument that runs through Nazi "socialism." Complications he documents
for a simple pipeline story: the "economists" of the movement (Arturo
Labriola, Leone, De Ambris) and Sorel's chief disciple Berth never became
Fascists, while Valois ran the circuit both ways and died in Bergen-Belsen
as a resister.

## Position 2 — The Hayek–Goldberg–D'Souza line: fascism as left-wing collectivism

An argument with serious intellectual roots (F. A. Hayek, *The Road to
Serfdom*, 1944; John T. Flynn, *As We Go Marching*, 1944; elements in Zeev
Sternhell's and Renzo De Felice's scholarship on fascism's socialist origins)
carried into mass-market form by Jonah Goldberg, *Liberal Fascism* (2008), and
extended by Dinesh D'Souza, *The Big Lie* (2017) — see [[dsouza-big-lie-2017]].
Its strongest claims:

- **The American right is not the European right.** The left–right axis was
  coined for the 1789 French Assembly, where "right" meant throne-and-altar
  reaction. The American right descends from Lockean classical liberalism —
  limited government, individual rights, markets — and shares nothing
  structurally with monarchism or with fascist statism. On this point the
  position converges with mainstream American political science (Louis Hartz,
  *The Liberal Tradition in America*, 1955: America has no genuine reactionary
  tradition for fascism to grow from). If "right" is defined as the party of
  state power against the individual, fascism cannot coherently be assigned to
  the American right.
- Fascism and Nazism were statist, collectivist, and anti-capitalist in
  program and rhetoric; on a state-versus-individual axis they sit with the
  collectivist left, not with classical liberalism. Mussolini's Marxist
  formation, the syndicalist and Sorelian intellectual pipeline, and the
  NSDAP's name and 25-point program are read as continuity rather than break.
- American Progressivism shared fascism's enthusiasms (eugenics, state
  planning, Wilson-era censorship and war corporatism); the New Deal borrowed
  corporatist forms admired by contemporaries in Rome.
- D'Souza extends this into a genealogical claim: the Democratic Party, as
  the historical party of slavery, the Ku Klux Klan, and Jim Crow — and given
  Nazi jurists' documented study of U.S. race law — is fascism's real American
  heir, and the left's anti-fascist rhetoric is projective "transference"
  (see [[big-lie-propaganda]] for the propaganda concept the title borrows).

*Hayek in his own words (now ingested — [[hayek-road-to-serfdom-1944]]):*
the position's founding text is more layered than its popularizations. Hayek's
formal claim is structural: communism, fascism, and comprehensive socialism
are all species of collectivism — "they all differ from liberalism and
individualism in wanting to organize the whole of society… they are
totalitarian in the true sense of this new word" — plus a German-specific
genealogy (ch. 12, from Sombart and Plenge through Spengler and Moeller van
den Bruck: "the socialists of the Left approached more and more to those of
the Right"), argued largely through ex-left witnesses (Eastman, Chamberlin,
Voigt, Heimann, de Man). But he hedges in ways his heirs often drop: he
denies inevitability ("Nor am I arguing that these developments are
inevitable"), denies bad faith ("I have never accused the socialist parties
of deliberately aiming at a totalitarian regime"), accepts extensive state
intervention (ch. 3) and a welfare floor (ch. 9) — and his 1956 foreword
explicitly refuses the conservative label in terms Position 1 can also
quote: conservatism "in its paternalistic, nationalistic, and power-adoring
tendencies… is often closer to socialism than true liberalism." See
[[road-to-serfdom-thesis]] for the full argument, its critiques, and the
"inevitability thesis" misreading both sides have traded on.

*Flynn in his own words (now ingested — [[flynn-as-we-go-marching-1944]]):*
the 1944 companion text cuts across both positions rather than sitting neatly
in Position 2. Flynn supplies its strongest documentary material: the
syndicalist "incredible synthesis" (Sorelian unions and cartelizing
businessmen converging from Left and Right on producer-group planning outside
the parliamentary state); the founding Fasci's left-wing 11-point program of
1919 and the NSDAP's "predominantly socialist" economic points, both abandoned
in power; his flat "Fascism was a leftist job"; and the socialist
self-identification of early NSDAP figures (Flynn: "Strasser, Roehm, and
Streicher were socialists"). Yet his settled definition makes fascism "a
defense of capitalist society, an attempt to make it function" — definitionally
pro-capitalist against communism — and Radosh's 1973 preface notes the
argument's "striking resemblance" to Marxist analyses of fascism; Socialist
leader Norman Thomas praised and quoted Flynn's definition in 1943. Flynn is
thus cited by Position 2 for the genealogy and by left anti-imperialists for
the structural analysis — a double reception recorded at
[[as-we-go-marching-thesis]].

*A test case both sides can draw on — the Left at Vichy:* Paxton's own
research documents ex-left pathways into [[vichy-france]]: ex-SFIO
Neosocialists (Déat), ex-Communists turned fascist (Doriot, Marion), the
syndicalist theorist Lagardelle (who introduced Sorel to Mussolini), and the
anti-communist CGT "Syndicats" minority (Belin, minister of labor). His own
gloss is that these were driven by pacifism, anti-communism, and planism
rather than ideological conversion — "the collaborators were a selection of
Third Republic notables from one end of the political spectrum to the other,"
with "antibolshevism… the nearest thing to a Vichy common denominator."
Position 2 reads the same roster as further evidence of the
collectivist-left-to-fascism pipeline.

*Griffin's placement (now ingested — [[griffin-nature-of-fascism-1991]]):*
the "new consensus" definer splits the difference terminologically: he
entertains the fascists' own "beyond left and right" self-understanding but
settles, "for practical taxonomic purposes," on fascism as "a sub-category
of the ultra-right but with special qualities of its own" — a "palingenetic
ultra-right." Two of his findings feed the debate: the rebirth myth at
fascism's core is not right-wing property (Sewell and Hunt document the same
palingenetic experience driving the French Revolution — the National
Assembly "participating in what seemed to them to be the regeneration of the
world"), and his case studies include textbook left-to-fascist migrations
(Doriot the ex-Communist, Déat's "neo-socialism," the ex-syndicalists of
1914). But his definitional verdict is firmly against the
fascism-as-collectivist-left equation: fascism is "radically
anti-conservative" yet ultra-right, and what distinguishes it is the mythic
core, not state economics. See [[palingenetic-ultranationalism]].

## The shared factual ground and how each side frames it

Several facts are common ground and disputed only in framing. The Progressive
movement embraced eugenics; the Democratic Party was the party of slavery and
segregation from the Jacksonian era to the mid-twentieth century (see
[[american-civil-war-1861-1865]]); Nazi lawyers drafting the Nuremberg Laws
studied U.S. race statutes (documented by James Q. Whitman, *Hitler's American
Model*, 2017); Mussolini was a prominent Marxist before 1914; and the fascist
and Nazi programs contained extensive anti-capitalist planks. To this list
Paxton (2004) adds a fact each side frames its own way: "It may be that the
earliest phenomenon that can be functionally related to fascism is American:
the Ku Klux Klan" — the first Klan as "arguably a remarkable preview of the
way fascist movements were to function in interwar Europe." Position 1 reads
this as fitting Paxton's pattern that "the most precocious democracies — the
United States and France — should have generated precocious backlashes
against democracy"; Position 2 reads it as the academy's own leading scholar
locating proto-fascism in the historical Democratic Party's militia.

- **Position 1 reads these** as facts about American racism, about the
  Progressive era, and about fascism's opportunism — arguing that ideology is
  not fixed by party label across a century of realignment, that borrowing
  race statutes does not make the lender the borrower's ideological parent,
  and that defining "left" as "statism" erases the anti-egalitarian core
  distinguishing fascism from socialism.
- **Position 2 reads these** as a suppressed genealogy — arguing that the
  post-war academy re-coded fascism as "right-wing" precisely to bury these
  continuities, and that Position 1's reliance on realignment and on an
  egalitarian definition of "left" assumes what it needs to prove.

## Resolution status

**Open as a live interpretive dispute; closed within the academy.** No
significant academic historian of fascism endorses the left-fascism genealogy
(Griffin, Paxton, and Evans have each publicly rejected the Goldberg/D'Souza
framing), and Position 2 answers that this unanimity reflects the very
ideological gatekeeping it alleges. The dispute is a permanent feature of
American political discourse and is recorded here without adjudication.

as among the strongest analyses of the subject, weighting Position 2's

## Griffin (2007) Addendum: Modernism Across the Spectrum

*Modernism and Fascism* complicates both camps' narratives. Against the left-consensus habit of treating modernism as naturally progressive, Griffin documents modernism's collusion with ultranationalism (the "usual suspects": Yeats, Pound, Wyndham Lewis, Jünger, Schmitt, Benn, Marinetti, Céline, Drieu la Rochelle; Heidegger "an academic industry in its own right") and criticizes scholarship (V&A 2006 catalogue, Lodder) for passing over Le Corbusier's French-fascist association while assuming modernism's left affinity. Against right-polemic genealogies, he equally rejects direct-lineage causation in *both* directions: Virilio's Marinetti-to-Auschwitz line and Lukács's Nietzsche/Expressionism-to-fascism line are twin reductionisms — fascism's links to cultural modernism are "multi-causal and 'rhizomic' rather than 'arborial'," and Blok/Mayakovsky/Tatlin are no more responsible for Stalin's purges than Marinetti for Fascism. Bolshevism and fascism are congeneric as [[political-modernism]] (Bauman: Hitler and Stalin "legitimate offspring of the modern spirit") while remaining differentiated by fascism's organic nationalism and its perpetual liminality. (Source: [[griffin-modernism-and-fascism-2007]].)

## Payne (1980): border-crossing cases

*Fascism: Comparison and Definition* ([[payne-fascism-comparison-definition-1980]]) supplies data points on the fascism/left boundary. Payne endorses Sternhell's priority claim: "nearly all the ideas found in fascism and nazism first appeared in France," where the revolutionary left first fused nationalism with collectivist aspirations. Case studies: Italian Fascism "was created by the nationalization of certain sectors of the revolutionary left" (the national syndicalists); Déat's neosocialism broke from the SFIO carrying ~a fifth of the members and became fascist only under occupation (his RNP "the extreme left of wartime French fascism"); Doriot came from Communism (1935 break over Stalinism), his PPF by far the largest French fascist group; Valois's Le Faisceau formula was "nationalism plus socialism equals fascism"; Mosley came out of Labour. The Portuguese National Syndicalists' 1935 coup attempt was made in alliance with anarchosyndicalists — "proving that the allies of fascism might be found on the extreme left as well as the extreme right." The most striking reverse crossing: Chile's Nacis (MNS) under González von Mareés, after the government massacred over 50 captured members, swung behind Aguirre Cerda's Popular Front candidacy (providing his winning margin), dropped antisemitism, gained a Jewish leader by 1938, and reconstituted as the Popular Socialist Vanguard by 1941; Bolivia's "military socialism" (Toro, 1936) fed the populist-left MNR. Payne also notes Nazi radicals (Strasser, Goebbels, Himmler) rejected Mussolini's regime as "too conservative or allegedly capitalistic" — the same critique made by Falangist and French leftist-fascists. Conversely he traces the polemical inflation of "fascism" to the Comintern, which stretched the label even before Mussolini took power, so that "antifascism" was "taken up in some countries before any fascism existed to combat."
