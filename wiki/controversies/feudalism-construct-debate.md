# Is "Feudalism" a Valid Category? (The Brown–Reynolds Critique)

## The Dispute

Whether **"feudalism"** (and the cognate "feudal society," "feudal system") names a
real structure of medieval European society or is a misleading abstraction imposed
by post-medieval scholarship. The question is methodological as much as factual: it
concerns what kind of model historians may legitimately build out of the medieval
evidence. See the concept page [[feudalism]].

## Positions

**1. Feudalism as a descriptive model.** The classic syntheses — F. L. Ganshof's
narrow legal *Feudalism* (the law of fiefs and vassalage) and Marc Bloch's broad
*La société féodale* (an entire social type marked by personal dependence,
fragmented authority, and a warrior aristocracy) — treat feudalism as a genuine,
analytically useful description of medieval (especially eleventh–twelfth century)
society. This usage underlies most twentieth-century textbooks and the Marxist
"feudal mode of production."

**2. Feudalism as an incoherent construct.** Elizabeth A. R. Brown ("The Tyranny of
a Construct," 1974) and, at length, **Susan Reynolds** (*Fiefs and Vassals*, 1994;
summarised in [[luscombe-ncmh-v4-2004|NCMH IV]] Ch. 4) argue that:
- the term was invented by sixteenth-century jurists generalising the *Libri
  Feudorum* and was never used by medieval people;
- the supposed uniform fief–vassal nexus is largely read back into varied evidence;
- the model wrongly denies the eleventh and twelfth centuries any sense of **public
  authority, law, and community**, reducing all power to private lordship.
Reynolds concludes the construct obscures more than it reveals and is best dropped.

**3. The middle position.** Many medievalists now accept the critique of "feudalism"
as a *system* while continuing to study fiefs, homage, vassalage, knightly service,
and seigneurial lordship as real (if regionally diverse and chronologically uneven)
phenomena — describing the relations without the totalising label.

## State of the Debate

**Open, with the critique ascendant.** Since the 1990s the burden of proof has
shifted: specialist literature increasingly avoids "feudalism" as an analytic term
or uses it under explicit protest, while conceding that Reynolds may overstate the
uniformity of what she demolishes. The dispute connects directly to the
[[feudal-revolution-debate|"feudal revolution"/*mutation de l'an mil*]] controversy —
if "feudalism" is not a coherent system, the idea of a sudden "feudal
revolution" around 1000 also loses its object. Both are facets of a single larger
question: how much real structural change separated the Carolingian order from the
High Medieval one, and how much is an artefact of historians' models and the
surviving sources.
