# Causes of the First World War

Why Europe went to war in **1914** is among the most debated causal questions in all of history — argued
continuously for over a century without consensus. The wiki records the positions without adjudicating,
following the framework of **[[joll-origins-first-world-war|James Joll's *The Origins of the First World
War*]]**, the standard survey.

## Why it is contested

The war's outbreak sits at the intersection of an **immediate crisis** (the five weeks from Sarajevo to
general mobilization) and **deep structural conditions** accumulated over decades. Every explanation must
decide how much weight to give to contingency versus structure, to individuals versus systems, and to one
power's responsibility versus a shared European failure. The **war-guilt clause** of the Versailles Treaty
(Article 231) gave the question immediate political stakes that have never fully dissipated.

## Positions

- **German responsibility (the Fischer thesis).** Fritz Fischer (*Germany's Aims in the First World War*,
  1961) argued from German archives that Germany **deliberately sought** a war of expansion, driven by
  annexationist war aims and by elites using external aggression to manage internal crisis. This broke the
  post-1919 move toward "shared responsibility" and reopened the whole debate. *Held by:* Fischer, the
  Fischer school (Geiss, Wehler).
- **Systemic / alliance failure.** The rigid **Triple Alliance / Triple Entente** blocs turned a Balkan
  quarrel into a continental war; no single power is uniquely guilty — the **states-system** failed.
- **Militarism and mobilization timetables.** The **arms race**, the "cult of the offensive," and railway
  **mobilization schedules** (the Schlieffen Plan) compressed decision time and made escalation
  self-driving once mobilization began (an argument associated with A. J. P. Taylor's "war by timetable").
- **Primacy of domestic politics.** Following Eckart Kehr, the war is explained by **internal** social and
  political tensions — elites in Germany, Austria-Hungary and Russia choosing war to contain democratic
  and socialist pressures.
- **Imperial and economic rivalry.** Colonial competition and German *Weltpolitik* as background causes
  (overlapping the Marxist reading of [[hobsbawm-age-of-empire|Hobsbawm]] and Lenin).
- **Contingency / "sleepwalkers".** Christopher Clark (*The Sleepwalkers*, 2012) and others stress
  **shared, distributed responsibility** and the role of miscalculation and contingency — leaders who
  "sleepwalked" into a catastrophe none fully intended.

**Strachan's synthesis (from *To Arms*, Ch. 1 origins material).** Emphasizes that long-range factors (Weltpolitik, arms races, domestic structures) created fatalism and militarized diplomacy but did not dictate a master plan. The "blank cheque" was issued with little discussion of implications; Moltke pushed preventive war from a pessimistic, fatalistic stance ("Slav vs Teuton") rather than Fischer-style calculated domestic diversion. "What remains striking... is that the decision for peace or war was made conditional not on the objectives of policy but on the state of military readiness." July decisions were incremental, often improvised (e.g. Russia's partial mobilization proved technically unworkable), and shaped by prior crises (Bosnia 1908 humiliated Russia; Agadir hardened Entente). Individuals (Conrad's va banque fatalism, Pasic's domestic constraints, Bethmann's fatalism after wife's death) and accidents interacted with structures. No power pursued consistent grand design; diplomacy (limit/localize) clashed with war plans (Germany's two-front priority). Economic interdependence and genuine desire for peace among businessmen existed alongside nationalist rhetoric. Positions are complementary layers, not mutually exclusive.

## Resolution status

**Open.** Joll's own conclusion — that the war's origins lie in the **interaction** of the July Crisis
with the alliance system, militarism, domestic politics, imperialism and the ideological "mood of 1914",
with no single sufficient cause — remains the mainstream scholarly stance, even as the Fischer and
"sleepwalkers" poles continue to define the debate's extremes.
