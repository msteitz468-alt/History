# The Nature of Carolingian Government

## The Dispute

How "real" was Carolingian government? Did [[charlemagne]] and his successors build a genuine
administrative state — extending royal oversight through institutions such as the
**[[missi-dominici]]**, the **scabini**, and the **[[carolingian-capitularies|capitularies]]**, and
partly reconstituting the **public authority** of the Roman world — or was the [[carolingian-empire]]
a fundamentally personal, conquest-driven hegemony whose apparent institutions existed mostly on
parchment? NCMH II (Fouracre, Ch. 3) states flatly that "opinion on this important question is sharply
divided."

## Position 1 — The Optimistic View

The growing volume of capitularies from Pippin III onward is read as evidence of **government by the
written word**. Extrapolating from them yields an impressive list of institutions and intentions which,
treated as innovations, imply a thorough reform of Frankish rule: the *missi dominici* as travelling
inspectors of local government; the *scabini* as legal experts professionalising the courts; the oaths
of fidelity binding officers to their public duty; and the religious mandate of an anointed, quasi-Roman
emperorship. On this view the Carolingian state, though unable to revive Roman direct taxation,
**compensated with ties of loyalty and religious obligation**, persuading its officers to perform public
duties — a partial reconstitution of Roman public order. (Boussard and others.)

## Position 2 — The Pessimistic View (Reuter)

**Timothy Reuter** and others question how far the good intentions of the capitularies were ever put into
practice. There are **no case records** showing the *missi* actually reported on local government; surviving
court records reveal a judicial process that stayed "basically pragmatic... scabini or no scabini." On this
account **no substantial new structure of government evolved**; what sustained Carolingian growth was the
**plunder and tribute** that flowed from military success. When expansion stopped, the lack of structure was
exposed, and the magnates who had built the empire by fighting together profitably began to dismantle it by
fighting each other — the dynamic visible in the civil wars of 840–843 ([[battle-of-fontenoy-841]],
[[treaty-of-verdun-843]]). The strength of this view is that it rests on evidence of **performance rather
than intention**.

## Position 3 — The Equivocal / Middle View

Fouracre's own resolution treats the sources as more complex than either camp allows. Capitularies are a
**heterogeneous genre** — some idealistic, some real orders, some responses to local requests — and cannot be
read uniformly. What they reflect is the **revival of intellectual and religious activity in conjunction with
the growth of royal power**: "revival, reform and expansion went hand in hand." The reform programme was real
in aspiration and uneven in execution; even where administration fell short, the ideology of just Christian
rule "strengthened the hands of the rulers at every turn." Against the pure plunder thesis, Fouracre notes
that the profitability of early medieval war is incalculable, that much fighting was against the poor,
pre-monetary Saxons (a long-term investment, not a payday), and that large multi-ethnic campaigns required
exactly the kind of logistical organisation the capitularies prescribe.

## Stakes

The debate bears directly on a larger question that runs through [[mckitterick-ncmh-v2-1995|NCMH II]]: was
the Carolingian state **objectively different** from its Merovingian predecessor, or did custom and
continuity outweigh innovation? Fouracre's verdict leans toward continuity — "in 814 as in 714, power on the
ground lay in the hands of counts and bishops" — even as the ideological and cultural superstructure was
transformed.

## Resolution Status

**Open.** The positions turn on what weight to give prescriptive sources (capitularies) versus the near-total
absence of records of implementation, and the question is unlikely to be settled given the evidence. It
connects to broader controversies over [[fall-of-rome-causes|Roman continuity]] and the
[[post-roman-transformation-of-the-west|transformation of the Roman world]].
