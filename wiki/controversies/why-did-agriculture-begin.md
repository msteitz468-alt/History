## The Question

Why did people take up farming at all? The puzzle is sharpened by two findings
that make agriculture look like a *bad* deal for the first adopters:
1. **Foraging could be "affluent."** Ethnography of surviving foragers — the
   classic case being the !Kung San of the Kalahari (Lee, 1960s–70s) — showed
   secure, varied diets obtained with *less* daily labour than neighbouring
   farmers, with ample time for socializing. Foragers were not living "hand to
   mouth."
2. **Early farming damaged health.** Bioarchaeology shows the transition brought
   nutrient deficiencies, infectious disease, dental pathology, and heavy
   repetitive labour (see [[neolithic]] and the bioarchaeology chapter, Cycle 2).

So the old assumption that the advantages of farming were "obvious" to any
forager given the chance collapses. Why, then, did it happen — independently —
in so many parts of the world? This is one of the central open problems of
world prehistory.

## Positions

**1. Environmental / climate "push."** The end-Pleistocene climate shift and the
[[younger-dryas]] cold reversal stressed forager economies dependent on wild
cereals, pushing them toward cultivation. Descends from Childe's "oasis
hypothesis" (though Childe wrongly thought the early Holocene grew *arid*; in
SW Asia it grew wetter). Still the dominant framing for Southwest Asia. *Best
argument:* the timing coincidence of the Younger Dryas and the earliest
cultivation in the Levant. *Weakness:* climate "cannot have been a simple forcing
agent" — agriculture also arose in regions and moments without environmental
crisis (Dillehay, Nanchoc valley, Peru).

**2. Population pressure "push."** Rising population (locally or regionally)
forced intensification of food production beyond what foraging could supply
(Boserup, Cohen). *Weakness:* population growth is often better read as a
*consequence* of farming than its cause; hard to demonstrate pressure
archaeologically prior to the transition.

**3. Social-competition / feasting "pull."** Internal social dynamics — the
pursuit of prestige through feasting, surplus accumulation, and competitive
generosity — drew societies into producing storable surpluses. Cereals were
well suited to brewing, and beer's importance in ritual feasting may have been
valued above bread (the volume notes ritualized contexts for the earliest
cereals, sickle blades, and grindstones in SW Asia). *Strength:* explains why
people would adopt costly cultigens for reasons other than calories. *Weakness:*
harder to evidence than climate; risks projecting modern "Big Man" ethnography.

**4. Ideological / ritual "pull."** Shifts in belief and worldview — the "magic
and mystery" of new foods, changed relationships to land and ancestors —
motivated cultivation. Linked to Göbekli Tepe-style monumental ritual preceding
full agriculture. *Weakness:* difficult to access archaeologically outside
exceptional sites.

**5. No single cause — region-specific multicausality.** Barker & Goucher's own
position: "the global pathways to food production were many and varied." The
drivers differ by region and even by site; the search for a universal cause is
itself the error. This is the volume's framing and the current consensus
*meta*-position.

## Resolution Status

**Open.** There is broad agreement that (a) no single universal cause works,
(b) the old "agriculture was obviously better" assumption is dead, and (c) the
shift has both external (climate, population) and internal (social, ideological)
components in region-specific mixes. But *which* drivers dominated *where*
remains actively debated, and the relative weight of "push" vs. "pull" is
unresolved.
