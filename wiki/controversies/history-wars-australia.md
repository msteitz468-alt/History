# History Wars — Australia's History Anxiety and the Debate over Frontier Violence

## Background and Origin

Australia's "history anxiety" is a recurring cultural condition rooted in the peculiar circumstances of settler colonialism. As Mark McKenna argues in [[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]], the condition has structural causes: British settlers arrived in a land they described as "a verifiable blank" (explorer James Calder on Tasmania, 1840s), erasing Aboriginal history even as they depended on the very land from which that history had been violently cleared.

The founding paradox was visible at Federation in 1901. The new [[commonwealth-of-australia]] simultaneously celebrated "evolution not revolution" and constructed itself on "the total denial of Indigenous history." The *Sydney Morning Herald* on 1 January 1901 described the Aboriginal person as "a happy animal" requiring management; a school text of 1917 (Walter Murdoch) stated frankly: "when people talk about 'the history of Australia' they mean the history of the white people who have lived in Australia...[the Indigenous people] have no history, as we use the word."

The Anzac legend — with Gallipoli in 1915 as the "birth" of the nation — was itself partly an anxious response to this vacuum: "Australian 'history' and the Australian nation were born in the same place and time, not on Australian soil but at the Gallipoli peninsula in Turkey." This founding myth bypassed entirely the question of what had happened on Australian soil.

## The Seeds of Controversy (1930s–1960s)

On 26 January 1938 the sesquicentennial of British settlement was commemorated with a state-organised parade; simultaneously, 100 Aboriginal protesters led by William Cooper, William Ferguson, and Jack Patten declared the day "a Day of Mourning," resolving: "We, as aborigines, have no reason to rejoice on Australia's 150th birthday...We have had 150 years of the white man looking after us and the result is our people are being exterminated...We ask for ordinary citizen rights and full equality with other Australians." McKenna identifies this juxtaposition — "thin theatre of a state-organised event to commemorate the history of British civilisation and a minority Aboriginal protest determined to expose the same history as a cause for mourning" — as containing "the seeds of Australia's future history anxiety." (McKenna, in [[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]])

Manning Clark (1915–1991) was a pivotal figure: embarking in the late 1940s on "the uncharted sea of Australian history," his six-volume *A History of Australia* (1962–87) emerged "both as a leading academic historian and public intellectual" and proved "the most adept at forging the connection between national identity and history."

From the 1960s, new social history — labour history, women's history, and Indigenous history — shattered the Whig consensus of British progress. "The Australian nation and its British colonial past were exposed as racist, sexist and exploitative." Some historians on the left declared their work "necessarily biased" (Humphrey McQueen) or "politically utilitarian" (Henry Reynolds).

## The History Wars: Structure of the Dispute

By the mid-1990s the debate had acquired its most intense and acrimonious form. McKenna identifies the following core questions at stake:

1. Was there a frontier war?
2. How many Indigenous people died on the frontier?
3. Were Aboriginal people massacred, or were most deaths from disease?
4. How many Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children were removed from their parents by state authorities?
5. Did removed children constitute the "Stolen Generations"?
6. Should the Australian government offer a formal apology?

### The Critical/Revisionist Position
Historians including Henry Reynolds (*The Other Side of the Frontier*, 1981; *This Whispering in Our Hearts*, 1998), Bain Attwood, and others documented frontier violence, dispossession, and child removal policies with archival evidence. Reynolds argued his work was "politically utilitarian" — that is, aimed at exposing and remedying historical injustice. The 1997 *Bringing Them Home* report on the Stolen Generations brought the removal of Aboriginal children into mainstream public discourse and created "National Sorry Day."

### The Conservative/Nationalist Position
Geoffrey Blainey coined the phrase "black-armband history" for what he saw as an unbalanced focus on Australia's failures. John Howard (Opposition Leader 1988, PM 1996–2007) consistently condemned histories that had "denied Australians the possibility of celebrating their past achievements." Howard championed a reinvigorated Anzac mythology as a national founding story "not haunted by the troubled history of dispossession." He refused to apologise for the Stolen Generations throughout his government, arguing that present generations should not be held responsible for past policies.

Keith Windschuttle's *The Fabrication of Aboriginal History* (2002) disputed specific casualty figures and methodological claims made by frontier-violence historians, triggering intense scholarly debate about historical method and evidence.

The History Wars were "fuelled by the vigorous support of Rupert Murdoch's News Corporation" and "marked by intense and often acrimonious public debate regarding the history of Indigenous–settler relations." (McKenna, in [[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]])

### The Reconciliationist Position
Paul Keating's Redfern speech (1993) represented a confessional turn: Keating spoke "in a confessional tone of the nation's responsibility for murder, dispossession and the removal of Indigenous children." Kevin Rudd's formal apology on behalf of the nation (February 2008) was described by the *Sydney Morning Herald* as "a nationwide emotional release...a gesture of atonement for the full disastrous history of indigenous relations since 1788." McKenna reads the apology as "as much a cry for absolution — for an end to the history anxiety — as it was an acknowledgement of wrongdoing."

## The Mabo Decision (1992)

The High Court's *Mabo* decision recognised "native title" as having existed within common law, exposing the fiction of *terra nullius*. It made impossible any national founding narrative that rested comfortably on settlement without acknowledging prior Indigenous ownership. The nation appeared, in McKenna's phrase, to be "without ethical roots." The *Mabo* decision sharpened the political stakes of the History Wars by making Indigenous land rights a legal reality, not merely a historical argument.

## The Anzac Counter-Narrative

The History Wars ran concurrently with a dramatic revival of Anzac commemoration from the early 1990s. McKenna argues these were related phenomena: "the Anzac legend — now refashioned in the image of conservative nationalism as the embodiment of Australian 'values'...provided a solution to decades of divisive debate over the history of Indigenous–settler relations." Anzac Day became "Australia's de facto national day, the nation's exalted moment of creation" — a foundational narrative "not haunted by the troubled history of dispossession." Prime ministers journeyed to Gallipoli for "fully choreographed spectacles that resembled concert entertainment, all beamed to nationwide television audiences." The relationship between the Anzac revival and deflection of settler-colonial reckoning is itself a historiographical controversy.

## Resolution Status

The formal apology (2008) marked a partial resolution — the question of whether a government should apologise was settled in the affirmative — but structural questions remain open:
- Debates over casualty figures and the extent of frontier violence continue in academic literature
- Land rights, the Stolen Generations, and appropriate redress remain live political issues
- The tension between celebratory and critical national histories has not been resolved
- The appropriateness of the Anzac myth as national foundation remains contested

The controversy is partially resolved (apology delivered; terra nullius overturned) but fundamentally open (structural injustice unresolved; historiographical debates ongoing).
