# The reliability of the ancient sources on the Druids

## The Dispute

How much can actually be known about the historical Iron Age Druids? The surviving Greek and Roman references to them are remarkably few — all of them, Ronald Hutton notes in *[[sources/hutton-blood-and-mistletoe-2009|Blood and Mistletoe]]* (2009), would fit into about a dozen pages of fairly large print — and they are mutually contradictory, portraying Druids variously as bloodthirsty barbarians performing human sacrifice, as learned philosophers, theologians, and natural scientists, or as a mixture of both. Only two witnesses ([[actors/julius-caesar|Julius Caesar]] and Cicero) could conceivably have seen Druids themselves, and both were motivated authors writing to advance specific rhetorical and political purposes; Caesar's account also contains an apparent logical defect.

## Source-by-Source Assessment (Hutton ch. 1)

### Julius Caesar (*De Bello Gallico*, 50s BCE)

Caesar's account is the fullest and the only one left by an author who might have had first-hand experience of Druids. He portrayed them as a learned priestly caste with judicial, educational, and religious roles, exempt from military service and taxation, studying for up to twenty years, meeting annually "at a holy place in the territory of the Carnutes," and teaching above all that "souls do not perish, but after death pass from one to another." He also made them "administrators" of mass human sacrifice, including burning people alive in giant wicker figures. The logical defect noticed by scholars in 1891 and never satisfactorily resolved: given the importance Caesar attributed to Druids and their apparent centrality in Gallic society, they are completely invisible in his narrative of the actual conquest of Gaul — they appear only in the self-contained ethnographic survey section, not in the action. A continuation of his history by Aulus Hirtius also makes no mention of Druids. Three explanations have been offered — that the narrative of military operations did not require such comment; that Caesar was quoting an earlier writer describing a system that had ceased to exist; that he misrepresented or invented the Druids "to suit his own purposes" — and none is fully satisfactory. Hutton notes Caesar's colleague Gaius Asinius Pollio charged that Caesar "was too quick to believe others' accounts … and give a false account of his own actions, either on purpose or through forgetfulness." Caesar's Druids were, conveniently, learned enough to make Gaul worth including in the empire, organized enough to pose a serious political threat, and barbaric enough to make conquest a civilizing mission. Stuart Piggott, Daphne Nash, and Sean Dunham have each suggested that Caesar projected the Roman system of priesthood onto an alien people, with his Druids exhibiting the religious functions of Roman senators ([[sources/hutton-blood-and-mistletoe-2009|Hutton, Blood and Mistletoe (2009)]]).

### Cicero (*De Divinatione*, c. 44 BCE)

Cicero reported meeting a Gallic Druid, Divitiacus of the Aedui, who claimed knowledge of the natural world and made predictions. Divitiacus is also known from Caesar's own narrative, where he appears as a leading political figure and military ally of Rome — but Caesar never calls him a Druid. Sean Dunham has used this discrepancy to argue that *druides* was simply the Latinization of the native term for the religious function exercised by tribal aristocrats generally, not a distinct caste. Cicero simultaneously defended Gallic human sacrifice as proof of their barbarism (and thus unreliability as court witnesses) while presenting Divitiacus as a figure of apparent wisdom — the same ambivalence found throughout the classical tradition.

### Diodorus Siculus, Strabo, and Pomponius Mela (1st c. BCE – 1st c. CE)

These three authors have since the 1950s been assumed to draw on a single lost source — the philosopher Posidonius of Syria, who visited south-eastern Gaul in the early 1st c. BCE. If so, then virtually all pre-conquest testimony about Druids rests on "the indirect testimony of one traveller." But Hutton argues this "Posidonian tradition" is itself insecure: the term's clearly attributed extracts from Posidonius never mention Druids; the accounts in Diodorus and Strabo show significant discrepancies; and Caesar's account reads so differently that recent scholarship tends to conclude it was not derived from Posidonius at all. Strabo distanced himself from his Druid material by using indirect constructions ("it is said," "they say") and admitted he was describing Gallic customs "as we understand that they existed in former times," not in his own day. He had "a patent motive for exaggerating the poverty of British culture" — he follows his account with an argument that Britain was not worth the cost of Roman conquest. Pomponius Mela may simply be "a rehash of Caesar with a few imaginative flourishes."

### Pliny the Elder (*Naturalis Historia*, 70s CE)

Pliny is the source of the famous account of Druids cutting mistletoe from oak trees with a golden sickle, of their reverence for oak groves, and of his suggestion that the very word "Druid" derived from the Greek word for "oak" (now largely abandoned). He is also the source of the claim that Claudius "very thoroughly suppressed" Druidry in Gaul. None of his references to Druids cite the books from which he took them — suggesting they came to him by word of mouth rather than documented sources. Pliny had visited the extreme south of Gaul and served in Germany; he had ample opportunity to collect gossip about what Druids had supposedly done. His reliability as a general authority is severely limited: he populated the central Sahara with headless people, one-legged tribes who used their enormous foot as a sunshade, and dog-headed men. Mary Beagon has described him as "regarded as a mine of misinformation, if not actual fantasy." His treatment of Druids was additionally coloured by his assimilation of them to "magicians" — a category he was determined to condemn as a degeneration of both religion and science. By his time, Druids had shed the judicial and philosophical functions mentioned by earlier writers and appeared as folk magicians and healers, but whether this reflects a real decline caused by Romanization or simply reflects Pliny's interests, cannot be determined ([[sources/hutton-blood-and-mistletoe-2009|Hutton, Blood and Mistletoe (2009)]]).

### Tacitus (*Annals* and *Agricola*, early 2nd c. CE)

Tacitus provided the most dramatic ancient image of Druids — the scene at the Menai Strait when Suetonius Paulinus attacked Anglesey (Mona), where "all around were druidae, raising their hands to the sky and shouting dreadful curses." He also stated they used prophecy to encourage revolt in 69 CE. E. W. Black has stated flatly that "Tacitus cannot be trusted as an authority on first-century Britain," writing some fifty years after events and reshaping earlier material to rhetorical purpose. David Braund has reinforced this: "it would be facile in the extreme to treat artful Tacitus as no more than a reporter of events." Notably, Tacitus never stated explicitly that Anglesey was an especially important place for Druids — he chose it as a target because it was densely populated and harboured refugees. When he reported the island's second capture eighteen years later, he made no mention of Druids at all ([[sources/hutton-blood-and-mistletoe-2009|Hutton, Blood and Mistletoe (2009)]]).

### The Admiring Greek Tradition

A separate cluster of Greek authors — Dion Chrysostom, Clement of Alexandria, Hippolytus, Diogenes Laertius, Ammianus Marcellinus — presented Druids as wise philosophers linked to Pythagorean teaching. Hutton notes that this admiring tradition is actually older and more sustained than the hostile Roman one: it spans six hundred years, while the denunciations are concentrated in the two centuries of Roman conquest. Its limitation is that these authors were often very far from Gaul in time and geography, and their portrait of the Druids as Pythagorean philosophers may itself be a projection of Greek intellectual categories. Furthermore, the doctrine of transmigration of souls attributed to Druids by Caesar and Diodorus differs significantly from what Pomponius, Lucan, and Valerius Maximus describe — the Gallic belief in reincarnation in a parallel world in familiar bodies, which is "a completely different concept from the Pythagorean one of rebirth of the soul in a different body in the same world," and which is also supported by archaeological evidence of goods buried for use in an afterlife.

### The Human Sacrifice Question

Hutton deals at length with the classical charges of human sacrifice, concluding that "the Greek and Roman sources for Druidry are not, as we have received them, of sufficiently good quality to make a clear and final decision on whether the sacrifice of people was indeed a part of their belief system." He notes that for Graeco-Roman authors, the charge of human sacrifice was "part of a complex and wide-ranging Graeco-Roman discourse about civilisation and religion" (J. Rives) — used against foreign peoples, internal minorities, and religious dissenters alike. The same charge was levelled against Christians by pagans, against Jews by Christians, and against heretics by the Church. The Gauls and Druids, lacking their own written testimony, suffered the same handicap as heretics in this discourse.

## Positions

- **Minimalist (Hutton's emphasis).** Because the texts are sparse, contradictory, rhetorically motivated, and largely derivative (most descend from the lost work of Posidonius), there exists no single authentic and authoritative portrait of the Druids. Almost nothing about them can be established beyond dispute, and archaeology has so far produced no artefact whose connection to Druids commands general agreement. This very indeterminacy is what made Druids endlessly available for later reinvention (see [[processes/druid-revival|The Druid Revival]]).
- **Critical-realist.** The classical ethnography, read carefully against its biases, preserves a genuine core: a recognizable learned priestly class with judicial, religious, and educational roles across Gaul and Britain. (This is closer to the synthesis in [[concepts/druids|Druids and Celtic Religion]].)
- **Archaeological.** Material evidence — sanctuaries, votive and weapon deposits, ritual shafts, sacrificial remains — can independently illuminate Iron Age religion, though attributing any of it specifically to "Druids" remains contested.

## Significance

The unresolvable character of the source problem is, for Hutton, the engine of the whole British reception history: with no fixed ancient portrait to constrain them, later Britons could choose whichever image of the Druid suited their cultural, religious, and political purposes.
