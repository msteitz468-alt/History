# Causes of the Bronze Age Collapse

> **Standing controversy** (CLAUDE.md). Companion to the event page
> [[events/bronze-age-collapse-1200bce|Bronze Age Collapse]].

## The Dispute

Why did the interconnected palace-based civilizations of the Late Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean and Near East — Mycenaean Greece, the Hittite Empire, Ugarit, Cypriot polities, and (in weakened form) New Kingdom Egypt — collapse or fundamentally transform within roughly a single generation around 1200–1150 BCE? This is among the most durable causal debates in ancient history, complicated by fragmentary and unevenly distributed evidence across the affected regions.

## Positions

**Sea Peoples invasion (traditional narrative).** Egyptian sources, above all Ramesses III's Medinet Habu inscriptions, describe waves of seaborne and land raiders ("Sea Peoples") sweeping through Hatti, Cyprus, and the Levant before being repelled from Egypt itself. Older scholarship treated this invasion as the primary cause of collapse. Robert Drews's influential critique (*The End of the Bronze Age*, 1993) argued the Sea Peoples were more a symptom and opportunistic beneficiary of prior military and systemic weakness than an independent prime mover, pointing to a broader "catastrophe" in infantry tactics (the spread of javelins and cut-and-thrust swords undermining chariot-based elite warfare) as a more fundamental military cause.

**Systems collapse (multi-causal).** Eric Cline's synthesis (*1177 B.C.: The Year Civilization Collapsed*, 2014) argues no single cause suffices; instead, a "perfect storm" of simultaneous stresses — climate change, earthquakes, internal rebellion, and disrupted international trade — overwhelmed a set of Late Bronze Age palace economies that had become tightly interdependent and therefore fragile, such that no single shock would have been catastrophic alone.

**Climate and drought.** Paleoclimatic studies (Kaniewski et al.; Drake) present evidence of a significant, multi-decade drying trend across the eastern Mediterranean beginning in the late 13th century BCE, which would have strained the agricultural surplus that centralized palace economies depended on to feed non-agricultural populations, craft specialists, and military forces.

**Earthquake storm.** Amos Nur and Eric Cline have proposed a cluster of major earthquakes along the region's active fault lines within a few decades around 1200 BCE, citing archaeological destruction layers at multiple sites, which could have compounded other stresses even without being independently decisive.

**Internal social and political breakdown.** Some destruction layers (notably at Ugarit, whose final royal correspondence describes desperate appeals for grain and military help against attackers) suggest internal unrest, peasant revolt, or elite conflict compounding external pressures rather than a purely external invasion narrative.

**Trade-network and palace-economy fragility.** The Late Bronze Age's highly centralized, redistributive palace economies depended on long-distance exchange of essential materials (above all tin and copper for bronze); disruption of these networks — whether by piracy, political collapse of a key node, or simple over-extension — could have triggered cascading economic failure independent of any single military or environmental shock.

## Shared Factual Ground

All positions agree on the basic chronology (destruction layers across many sites cluster within roughly 1200–1150 BCE), on the reality of significant population decline and the disappearance of palace-based writing systems (Linear B) in Greece, and on the disproportionate severity of the collapse for centralized palace economies compared to smaller-scale or non-palatial settlements, which in several regions show greater continuity.

## Resolution Status

Open. The current scholarly consensus, following Cline, leans toward multi-causal "systems collapse" explanations over any single decisive cause, but the relative weighting of climate, invasion, earthquake, and internal factors — and how uniformly a single explanation applies across such geographically diverse evidence — remains actively debated.
