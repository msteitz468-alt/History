# Was There a "Military Revolution" in Early Modern Warfare?

A long-running interpretive dispute over whether early modern warfare underwent
a "revolution" (see [[military-revolution]]) and, if so, whether it explains the
growth of the European state and the global "rise of the West."

## The Dispute

The question is partly factual (did the pace and character of change amount to a
revolution?) and partly causal (did military change drive state formation and
European expansion, or merely accompany them?). Because the thesis has been used
to underwrite a Eurocentric account of world history (see [[eurocentrism]]), it
carries weight beyond military history.

## Positions

**1. The classic thesis (Michael Roberts, Geoffrey Parker).** A genuine
revolution occurred — gunpowder weapons, drill and volley fire, the
*trace italienne*, and ballooning army sizes — concentrated in 1560–1660 and
diffusing from a European core. It forced the growth of fiscal-bureaucratic
states and gave Europeans the edge that made overseas empire possible. Parker
globalized the argument and tied it explicitly to "the rise of the West."

**2. The skeptical case (Jeremy Black, CWH VI).** Change was real but
*incremental*, not revolutionary, with extensive medieval precedents.
Technology was an agent marshalled by technique and culture, not an autonomous
cause. Western land superiority over the Asian empires came late (India after
c.1750–1800; China after c.1830–1860) and was contingent on non-military factors
— local alliances, opponents' disunity, finance, leadership. The fixation on
set-piece battles neglects the ubiquitous "little war" of raids and irregulars.
If "revolution" means abrupt fundamental change, none occurred.

**3. The "gunpowder empires" / parallel-transformation view.** Significant
military transformation occurred *outside* Western Europe — most clearly in the
Ottoman Empire (army and navy remade between 1450 and 1600; superior musket
barrels; a cohesive artillery corps), and among the Safavids, Mughals, and
Manchu/Qing. This undercuts the diffusion-from-Europe model and reframes the
period as one of multiple, locally rooted military developments.

## Status

**Open.** There is broad agreement that war, finance, and state capacity were
linked in this period, but no consensus on whether the changes were
"revolutionary," whether a single European model diffused outward, or how much
military change explains European expansion. CWH VI (Black) represents the
revisionist, late-and-contingent-divergence position; it does not adjudicate the
question so much as dissolve the strong form of the thesis.
