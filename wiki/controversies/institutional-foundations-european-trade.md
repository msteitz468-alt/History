# What Made European Long-Distance Trade Possible?

A live debate within [[new-institutional-economics|institutional economic history]]
over the causal foundations of the institutions that allowed long-distance trade
to grow in medieval and early-modern Europe despite the continent's legal and
political fragmentation. The dispute is about **causation**, not facts: all
parties agree the institutions (secure property, enforceable cross-border
contracts, protection against violence) emerged; they disagree on *what produced
them*.

## Position 1 — Strong States / Limited Government

**Held by:** Douglass North, John Wallis & Barry Weingast; Daron Acemoglu, Simon
Johnson & James Robinson; Eric Jones; (on credit access) David Stasavage.

**Claim:** Institutional improvement required the formation of stronger states
with the military means to protect trade and the legal power to adjudicate
disputes — *and* effective constraints on executive power (limited government,
e.g. England after the Glorious Revolution of 1688–89) so rulers could credibly
commit not to prey on merchants. England is the paradigm case.

**Best arguments:** royal regulation of credit, courts, and chartered companies in
England has a long pedigree; constitutional change preceded Britain's commercial
and industrial expansion; secure property rights plausibly require a sovereign
strong enough to enforce them.

## Position 2 — Private-Order Solutions

**Held by:** Avner Greif (with Paul Milgrom and Weingast); much of the trade-
network literature.

**Claim:** Trade grew in the *absence* of strong states because merchants built
private institutions — reputation and repeat-transaction networks, merchant
guilds capable of organizing boycotts, the community responsibility system, and a
*lex mercatoria*. These reduced reliance on rulers to enforce contracts.

**Best arguments:** Maghribi traders, Sephardic Jews, and Armenians built vast
networks with no home-government support; medieval guilds demonstrably
adjudicated members' disputes and coerced rulers via collective departure.

**Internal critique (Sheilagh Ogilvie):** merchant guilds and chartered companies
often used corporate power for **regulatory capture and rent-seeking**, excluding
competitors and strangling growth — so private order is not automatically benign.

## Position 3 — Urban Competition

**Held by:** Oscar Gelderblom, [[gelderblom-cities-of-commerce-2013|*Cities of
Commerce*]] (2013).

**Claim:** Neither strong states nor private order is necessary. Europe's
fragmentation itself produced the solution through **competition between
autonomous cities** that continuously adapted their institutions to attract
footloose merchants (see [[urban-competition-commercial-institutions]]). Cities
created [[open-access-institutions|inclusive, open-access institutions]] under
*economic* constraint (merchants could relocate), not the *political* constraint
of limited government. Private-order solutions mattered but worked only because
urban governments embedded and supported them; central royal courts were too slow
and too Roman-law-bound to serve merchants.

**Best arguments:** the [[bruges|Bruges]]–[[antwerp|Antwerp]]–[[amsterdam|Amsterdam]]
sequence shows inclusive institutions arising where rulers were weak and where
merchants were politically excluded (Amsterdam's magistrates actively excluded
most traders), so political franchise cannot be the driver; absolutist rulers
(Burgundy, Habsburg, the Ottomans) granted commercial cities institutional
flexibility, severing the "limited government" requirement.

**Limits:** built on three success cases; Gelderblom concedes London's early
monopoly position differed, and the thesis is strongest where multiple gateways
shared a hinterland.

## Resolution Status

**Open.** The positions are not wholly exclusive — North himself placed the Low
Countries *between* the Italian city-states and England, and Gelderblom frames his
account as compatible with theories stressing institutional adaptiveness while
rejecting the *limited-government* requirement. Per CLAUDE.md, positions are
recorded with their best arguments and proponents rather than adjudicated here.
