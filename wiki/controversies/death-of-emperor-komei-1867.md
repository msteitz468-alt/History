# Cause of Death of Emperor Kōmei (1867)

Emperor Kōmei — the predecessor of [[emperor-meiji]] and a staunch conservative opponent of foreign treaties — died on January 30, 1867, at the age of thirty-six, following a sudden illness officially attributed to smallpox. His unexpected death, coming at a moment when his intransigence was the principal obstacle to the movement that became the [[meiji-restoration-1868|Meiji Restoration]], has generated a persistent historiographical controversy: did he die of disease, or was he assassinated?

## The Source Material

The course of Kōmei's illness is documented in letters and diaries of contemporaries including Nakayama Tadayasu (the emperor's grandfather) and Nakayama Yoshiko (his mother). These record: onset of fever on January 16; spots on January 20 (diagnosed as smallpox, signed by fifteen physicians); apparent improvement around January 24; then violent relapse on January 30 with "purple spots" on the face and blood from "nine apertures"; death in agony shortly after.

The official diagnosis was hemorrhaging pustular ("black") smallpox, a virulent and nearly always fatal variant. A "curious blank" in the official illness report beginning January 31 has been cited by some scholars as evidence of deliberate erasure ([[keene-emperor-of-japan-meiji-2002]]).

## Position 1: Death by Smallpox (Haraguchi Kiyoshi)

Haraguchi Kiyoshi, the leading proponent of the illness theory, argues:
- The course of Kōmei's illness matches known smallpox progression (specifically the hemorrhagic/black variety, which carried nearly 100% mortality)
- The symptoms of black smallpox closely resemble arsenic poisoning — which is why confusion arose — but are clinically distinguishable
- Haraguchi compared Kōmei's documented symptoms against records of the 1946 Nagoya smallpox epidemic (18,000 cases) and found the match consistent with illness
- Iwakura Tomomi's first reaction on learning of Kōmei's death was reportedly "I am finished!" and a consideration of withdrawing from public life entirely — not the response of a man who had arranged the death
- The fact that Kōmei contracted smallpox was not strange: the disease was circulating at court

## Position 2: Assassination by Poisoning (Nezu Masashi and others)

The assassination theory rests on several circumstantial arguments:
- The "too great a piece of luck" argument: Kōmei was the central political obstacle to the Restoration. His death at exactly this moment, leaving a malleable fifteen-year-old successor, was almost uniquely convenient for the anti-shogunate faction. Sir Ernest Satow wrote: "it is impossible to deny that his disappearance from the political scene, leaving as his successor a boy of fifteen or sixteen years of age, was most opportune" ([[keene-emperor-of-japan-meiji-2002]])
- The source of the contagion was Fujimaru, a page who had recovered from smallpox weeks earlier and was "presumably no longer infectious." That Kōmei was the only court member to contract it from this source is suspicious
- The symptoms of arsenic poisoning closely match those of hemorrhagic smallpox — both can produce purple discoloration, multiple organ failure, and rapid deterioration after apparent recovery
- The blank in the official record from January 31 is unexplained
- Rumors of poisoning circulated almost immediately and were recorded by Satow in 1867

Nezu Masashi identifies the poisoner as members of the court who had accepted bribes from the shogunate during the Kazunomiya marriage arrangements — specifically Chancellor Kujō Hisatada, Minister Koga Tatemichi, Iwakura Tomomi, and Chigusa Arifumi — and suggests a court lady administered the poison. The most specific theory is that Iwakura arranged for poison to be placed on two new writing brushes given to the emperor (who habitually licked his brush when composing), but Keene notes this "conflicts with other evidence concerning the progress of the emperor's illness."

## Assessment

Keene's account in [[keene-emperor-of-japan-meiji-2002]] treats the controversy with appropriate agnosticism: "It is unlikely we shall know the cause of Kōmei's death unless permission is granted to examine his remains for possible traces of arsenic." The imperial remains have not been examined.

The strongest argument against assassination is Haraguchi's clinical analysis of smallpox progression. The strongest argument for it is the extreme political convenience of the death combined with the suspicious blank in the official record. Neither the illness nor the assassination hypothesis can be definitively proven from available sources.

The controversy illustrates a structural problem in Meiji-period historiography: the official *Meiji Tennō ki* (Record of Emperor Meiji) was compiled decades after events and reflects political choices about what to include; independent sources (diaries, foreign diplomatic accounts) provide crucial correctives but are incomplete.
