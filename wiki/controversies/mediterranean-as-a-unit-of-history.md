# The Mediterranean as a Unit of History

A live historiographical debate — foregrounded by Charles Freeman as the organizing question of his own book (*Egypt, Greece, and Rome: Civilizations of the **Ancient Mediterranean***) — over whether, and in what sense, "the Mediterranean" is a coherent object of historical study, and what makes its history hang together. It is a dispute about *method and framing*, not about facts.

## Position 1 — Braudelian Environmental *Longue Durée*

**Fernand Braudel** (*The Mediterranean and the Mediterranean World in the Age of Philip II*, 1949) treated the sea and its geography as the deep, slow-moving determinant of human history. On this view a shared physical environment — climate, mountains, sea-lanes, the rhythm of agriculture — imposes long-term structures beneath the froth of political events. History is written from the geography up, over centuries, with individual events relegated to the surface.

**Best arguments:** captures genuine ecological and structural continuities; explains why disparate Mediterranean societies share economic and social rhythms.
**Critiques:** verges on environmental determinism; underweights human agency and rapid change.

## Position 2 — Connectivity and Microregions

**Peregrine Horden and Nicholas Purcell** (*The Corrupting Sea*, 2000) reframe Mediterranean unity not as environmental uniformity but as **connectivity**: a fragmented mosaic of countless ecological *microregions*, each risk-prone and specialized, knit together by dense, opportunistic exchange and mobility. The sea unifies by *linking difference*, not by imposing sameness.

**Best arguments:** accounts for the region's extreme local diversity and its intense interconnection at once; grounds "the Mediterranean" in interaction rather than climate.
**Critiques:** may underweight urbanization and the state; Barry Cunliffe's work on the Atlantic seaboard suggests "connectivity" is not uniquely Mediterranean, questioning whether it defines the region at all.

## Position 3 — Events, Individuals, and Agency

**David Abulafia** (*The Great Sea*, 2010) counters both structural models by writing the Mediterranean's history through **events, movements of people, and human agency** — traders, conquerors, migrants, cities — rather than deep environmental structure. The sea's history is made by what people did on and across it.

**Best arguments:** restores contingency and human decision; readable narrative history of the sea as a human arena.
**Critiques:** risks losing the structural insights that made "Mediterranean history" a distinctive enterprise in the first place.

## Shared Factual Ground, Framed per Side

All parties agree the ancient and medieval Mediterranean was densely interconnected by seaborne exchange and that its societies shared much. They differ on the *engine*: deep environment (Braudel), the logic of connectivity among microregions (Horden & Purcell), or human agency and events (Abulafia). Cunliffe's Atlantic comparison sits athwart the debate, questioning Mediterranean exceptionalism entirely.

## Curator's Note
