# The "Feudal Revolution" / Mutation de l'an Mil

## The Dispute

Did the decades around the year 1000 witness a sudden, structural transformation of European society — a **"feudal revolution"** (French *mutation féodale* or *mutation de l'an mil*) — in which the "public" order inherited from the [[carolingian-empire|Carolingian]] state collapsed and was replaced by privatised, castle-based seigneurial lordship? Or is that dramatic picture an **optical illusion** produced by a change in the *documentation* — the appearance, around the millennium, of a more garrulous, narrative style of charter that recorded for the first time things that had in fact existed, unrecorded, for generations?

This is the central interpretive question of the long tenth century, and [[reuter-ncmh-v3-1999|NCMH III]] (Reuter, Introduction) organises much of the volume around it while explicitly declining to resolve it: "any attempt to offer a synthesis at this stage would be futile."

## The Mutationist Model

The "totalising interpretation" (Reuter's phrase) links a cluster of changes into a single, rapid transformation centred on c. 980–1060:

1. a shift from **slavery** to a **serfdom** that absorbed much of the formerly free peasantry (a thesis Marxian in origin);
2. the **freezing of settlement patterns**, previously fluid, into fixed villages;
3. the spread of the small, **private, residential fortification** (the castle) in place of the older communal refuge;
4. a shift in **family consciousness** from broad bilateral kindreds to agnatic **lineages** named after their castles;
5. a new **banal lordship** (*seigneurie banale*) exercised without legitimating public office;
6. the **hollowing-out of public order** — royal authority first, then comital authority — into "ties between man and man," i.e. privatised, personalised power; and
7. the consolidation of lords and their warriors into a single **dominant warrior class** with its own ideology and initiation rites (knighthood).

The model was built from the great French and Catalan regional studies — **Georges Duby** on the Mâconnais, **Pierre Bonnassie** on Catalonia, **Pierre Toubert** on Latium (*incastellamento*), and the strong statements of **Jean-Pierre Poly and Éric Bournazel** (*La mutation féodale*) and **Guy Bois** (*La mutation de l'an mil*). **Thomas Bisson's** 1994 *Past & Present* article ("The 'Feudal Revolution'") restated it forcefully as a crisis of violence around 1000.

## The Challenges

- **The documentary critique.** **Dominique Barthélemy** argues the "mutation" is largely a *mutation documentaire*: charters became more loquacious, so historians mistook newly-recorded phenomena for new phenomena, contrasting them with an idealised Carolingian past that may never have existed. Bisson's article drew published rebuttals from Barthélemy, **Stephen White**, **Timothy Reuter**, and **Chris Wickham**.
- **The anti-"feudalism" critique.** **Susan Reynolds** (*Fiefs and Vassals*, 1994) contends that "feudalism" as a coherent system of fiefs held by vassals on defined terms was a construction of twelfth-century lawyers; for the tenth century there was no necessary link between vassalage and benefice, and the juridified system simply did not yet exist.
- **The slavery–serfdom critique.** The extent of early medieval slavery and the sense in which it was "replaced" by serfdom is, in Reuter's words, "highly contentious" (Verhulst, Barthélemy).
- **The regionalist critique.** The model was derived from west Francia (including Catalonia) and northern Italy and, Reuter stresses, **does not work** for important regions: **Germany** (where the decisive break is placed in the later eleventh century — reform, Investiture Contest), **England** (where the model has been explicitly rejected — James Campbell), **León**, and **southern Italy**.

## Significance

The debate is not merely technical: it determines whether one reads the long tenth century as the **end of antiquity's long shadow** (the final destruction of the Roman/Carolingian "public" state) and the **birth of the High Medieval order**, or as a period of largely continuous, undramatic evolution whose apparent suddenness is a trick of the sources. It bears directly on the periodisation boundary between the [[early-middle-ages|Early]] and [[high-middle-ages|High Middle Ages]].
