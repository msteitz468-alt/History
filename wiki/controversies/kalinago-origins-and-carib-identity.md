One of the most persistent debates in Caribbean archaeology concerns the origins of the people Europeans called "Carib" or "Island Carib" — known to their modern descendants on Dominica as Kalinago — and whether the label "Carib" itself reflects an indigenous reality or a colonial projection.

## Positions

### Position A: Invasion Model (Traditional)

**Proponents:** Louis Allaire (1987, 1996), Philip Boucher (1992), Irving Rouse (1948, 1992)

The traditional account, dominant through most of the 20th century and traceable to Columbus's own diario, holds that a group of Caribs from northeastern South America invaded the Lesser Antilles shortly before European contact, displacing or intermarrying with the existing Arawak (Taíno-related) populations. According to this model:

1. The Kalinago were intrusive migrants from coastal South America or the Guianas who entered the Antilles in a late, historically visible wave.
2. Their language retains a distinctive "men's language" (Carib) overlaid on an "Arawak" substrate, which is taken as evidence that the invaders killed or drove off Arawak men and adopted Arawak women.
3. The hostile encounters Columbus and subsequent Spanish encountered in the Lesser Antilles — and the fear expressed by Greater Antillean peoples toward "Caribes" — reflects actual Carib raiding of Taíno communities for captives.
4. The Cayo archaeological complex represents the material trace of this South American intrusion.

**Best arguments for this position:**
- Linguistic evidence: the dual-language structure (Carib "men's language" and Arawak "women's language") documented by early French missionaries is unusual and requires explanation.
- Ceramic continuity: Cayo pottery has clear affinities to the mainland Koriabo complex of coastal Guyana, suggesting mainland South American connections.
- Historical chronicle accounts (Breton 1665/1666, Du Tertre 1667–1671, Rochefort 1665) consistently describe the Kalinago as immigrants from the mainland who conquered earlier Arawak-speaking peoples.
- The Carib assertively identified their own South American origins in accounts recorded by European chroniclers.

### Position B: In-Place Development with Mainland Exchange (Keegan/Hofman Model)

**Proponents:** William Keegan, Corinne Hofman, Dave D. Davis and R. Christopher Goodwin (1990), Jalil Sued-Badillo (1978)

The revisionist position, developed primarily from the 1990s onward, argues that the Kalinago were not recent South American invaders but rather the descendants of indigenous Lesser Antillean communities whose pre-existing exchange networks with mainland South America intensified in the late pre-contact and early colonial periods. According to this model:

1. The "Carib" identity as projected onto Lesser Antillean peoples is primarily a European colonial construct, originating with Columbus's confused use of "Caniba" (the Grand Khan's people), "Caribes" (mythical beings in indigenous cosmology), and "Cannibales" (a general term for hostile natives).
2. Columbus had "absolutely nothing to do with the indigenous Carib" in his first voyage — the passages in the diario mentioning Caribes are abstractions by Las Casas reflecting later colonial projections.
3. The Cayo ceramic complex reflects intensified exchange with mainland South America rather than population replacement — ceramic continuity of Suazoid alongside Cayo suggests the local population adopted new pottery traditions rather than being replaced.
4. The archaeological record shows no sharp discontinuity consistent with invasion; the development of Kalinago culture is better explained by local processes of cultural change augmented by mainland interaction.
5. The dual-language structure can be explained by long-distance exchange and trade partner networks rather than conquest and population replacement.

**Best arguments for this position:**
- The Argyle site (St. Vincent) — the first fully excavated early colonial Kalinago settlement — shows Cayo pottery in direct association with early European trade goods (16th–early 17th century), confirming the archaeological identification, but the ceramic assemblage is a "mosaic-like cultural aggregate" (Boomert) mixing Koriabo, Meillacoid, and Chicoid affiliations that is more consistent with exchange than replacement.
- Davis and Goodwin (1990) pointed out that no confirmed Carib sites existed prior to the 1990s — their archaeological "invisibility" is better explained if they were not intrusive but developed from existing populations.
- Columbus explicitly states that Lucayan peoples initially identified *him* as a Caribe — which the revisionist model interprets as indicating the Carib were mythological beings in indigenous cosmology, not known real peoples.
- The diario's "Carib" passages contain anachronisms and are identified by Keegan and Henige as Las Casas interpolations, not Columbus's firsthand observations.
- Continuity of Suazoid pottery alongside Cayo in the Windward Islands suggests population continuity rather than replacement.
- The "cannibal" attribution served Spanish colonial interests by justifying enslavement of "Carib" peoples under papal bull — a politically motivated legal category that had nothing to do with actual practice.

## Evidence

**Primary source analysis:** Columbus's diario is the founding document for the Carib controversy. The word "Carib" (in various spellings: Caribes, Caniba, Canima, Cannibales) appears ten times in four distinct contexts, all analyzed by Keegan (1996a, 2007) as internally inconsistent and reflecting Columbus's projection of his preconception of the Grand Khan's subjects onto ambiguous indigenous communications. Columbus did not understand the languages of the peoples he encountered and repeatedly admitted this (Dunn and Kelley 1989:183, 217). The passages describing Caribes as mythological beings ("one-eyed men, and others with snouts of dogs, who ate men") — with Las Casas's marginal note "all this must be said of the Caribes" — are consistent with the revisionist reading that Caribes were supernatural beings in indigenous cosmology, not a neighboring ethnic group.

**Archaeological evidence:** The Argyle site (see [[lesser-antilles]]) demonstrates Kalinago occupation in the 16th–early 17th century through direct association of Cayo pottery with datable European materials. Leiden University surveys from 2009–2016 identified over twenty Cayo sites across Grenada, the Grenadines, St. Vincent, Dominica, and traces north to Guadeloupe. Site locations — ridge tops, windward coasts, defensive positions — match chronicle descriptions of Kalinago settlement patterns. Ceramic analysis (Boomert 1986, 2004, 2009, 2011) establishes the Koriabo affiliation of Cayo pottery, which the invasion model takes as direct evidence of South American origin and the in-place model takes as evidence of long-distance exchange networks.

**Linguistic evidence:** The dual-language structure of Island Carib — a "men's language" with Carib vocabulary and a "women's language" with Arawak vocabulary — is the central linguistic argument for the invasion model. Revisionist scholars note that similar trade-language phenomena arise from exchange networks without requiring conquest; the persistence of Arawak in women's speech could reflect marriage exchange and exogamy rather than the enslavement of Arawak women by Carib invaders.

**Demographic evidence:** The absence of Archaic Age sites south of Guadeloupe and the complex settlement sequence of the southern Lesser Antilles (Saladoid → Troumassoid/Suazoid → Cayo) shows long occupation histories inconsistent with a late, clean-sweep invasion. The continuity of Suazoid alongside Cayo in the Windward Islands (noted by Boomert) further complicates the replacement model.

## Resolution Status

**Partially resolved** toward the in-place development model, but not resolved by consensus.

The field has shifted substantially since the 1990s. The excavation of confirmed Kalinago (Cayo) sites, beginning with the Argyle site, has established that the archaeological Kalinago can be identified and that their material culture is a "mosaic-like cultural aggregate" of South American, Greater Antillean, and European influences — consistent with long-distance exchange networks rather than a simple invasion. The critique of Columbus's diario as a reliable source for Carib identity has been widely accepted among Caribbean archaeologists.

However, significant questions remain open: the precise mechanisms by which Cayo pottery and mainland Koriabo influences arrived in the Windward Islands; the relationship of Cayo to Suazoid in the sequence; the linguistic evidence for the dual-language structure; and the degree to which European colonial categories (especially the legal "Carib" category permitting enslavement) distort what the chronicles actually preserve about pre-contact conditions.

The traditional invasion model retains supporters (particularly among historians drawing on linguistic evidence) while the archaeological consensus has moved toward acknowledging the colonial construction of the Carib/Arawak dichotomy as the decisive interpretive issue.
