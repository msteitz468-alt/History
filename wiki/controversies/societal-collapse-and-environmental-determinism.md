## The Dispute

Did pre-modern societies "collapse" because they degraded or outgrew their natural
environments? The question — one of the **standing controversies** flagged in the
wiki's design (geographic/environmental determinism à la Diamond and its critics) —
is sharpened by [[kedar-cwh-v5-2015|CWH V]]'s environmental chapter (Radkau, Ch 2),
which uses several Middle-Millennium cases as test beds: the **Classic
[[tikal|Maya]] collapse** (8th–9th c.), the abandonment of **[[angkor|Angkor]]**
(c. 15th c.), the decline of **Great Zimbabwe**, and the end of the **Norse
Greenland** settlements. It belongs to the same family as the wiki's
[[pleistocene-megafaunal-extinction]] debate and is downstream of the wider
question of [[divergence-convergence|why civilizations diverge]].

## Position 1 — Environmental / Ecological Collapse

Associated above all with **Jared Diamond** (*Collapse*, 2005; *Guns, Germs and
Steel*, 1997), and earlier with the "Classic Maya collapse" ecological model
(Culbert and others) and Marvin Harris's cultural materialism:

- Societies that overshoot their resource base — through deforestation, soil
  erosion, salinization, or overpopulation — trigger self-reinforcing ecological
  crises that bring down their political and demographic order.
- The Maya are the paradigm case: a "vicious cycle of overpopulation, deforestation,
  soil erosion, and overuse of fragile tropical soils," with the collapse occurring
  "fairly abruptly... without any destructive conquerors." Great Zimbabwe shows soil
  erosion from smelting-driven deforestation; Angkor's fragile clay-soil irrigation
  system silted and failed; Norse Greenland starved as the Little Ice Age advanced.
- Radkau concedes the ecological explanation for the Maya "has remained quite
  persuasive to this day," and that medieval farming systems were genuinely fragile,
  dependent on "ecological reserves."

## Position 2 — Resilience, Transformation, and Contingency

Associated with **Patricia McAnany and Norman Yoffee** (*Questioning Collapse*,
2010), Radkau's own emphasis, and case revisionists (Berglund on Norse Greenland;
James C. Scott on upland Southeast Asia):

- "Collapse" overstates discontinuity. The Maya **people did not disappear**; the
  Norse Greenland decline was less catastrophic than Diamond claims and partly a
  rational switch to marine subsistence on the Inuit model; Angkor's hinterland
  population may have "led a more pleasant life" after the centralized apparatus
  failed (Scott: *The Art of Not Being Governed*).
- The undoing was often **political** (failure of the regimes that maintained
  irrigation works or terraces) rather than strictly ecological — and the ruins
  overgrown by jungle suggest cities undone by an "overpowering" nature reclaiming
  them, not a destroyed one.
- Methodological objection: Diamond-style "big surveys" (also Flannery, Harris)
  take "bold flights through world history without agonizing much about source
  criticism," leaping from prehistory to modernity and treating environmental
  overshoot as inherent to human nature. Horden & Purcell's *Corrupting Sea*
  similarly attacks Mediterranean "ecological determinism" by insisting history
  unfolded in heterogeneous "small worlds."

## Antecedents — Anti-Determinism in the CAH (1970)

The refusal of climatic determinism long predates *Questioning Collapse*. In
[[edwards-cah-v1p1-1970|CAH Vol. I, Part 1]] (Ch. II), K. W. Butzer — surveying
the [[postglacial-environment-near-east|natural environment of the Near East]]
before agriculture — states flatly that it is "impossible to speak of 'climatic
controls' in a deterministic sense." Climate sets *broad limits* (his "arid
limit" of ~4 in. annual rainfall) that human technology — irrigation, clothing,
heating — can offset. This is essentially the resilience side's position stated a
generation before Diamond, and from within the most authoritative reference work
of its day: evidence that the dispute is less a recent reaction to Diamond than a
recurring structural fault-line in the discipline.

## A Bronze Age Test Case — the Fall of the Old Kingdom

The dispute runs deep into antiquity. The
[[fall-of-old-kingdom-2160bce|collapse of the Egyptian Old Kingdom]] (~2160 BCE)
is argued along exactly the same fault-line. **Barbara Bell** (1971) attributed it
to a run of catastrophically **low Nile floods** — a regional drought — citing
famine imagery (the emaciated figures on Unas's causeway, the legendary "seven
years' famine of Djoser," First Intermediate nomarchs boasting of feeding their
people). **W. Stevenson Smith**, in the same year's [[edwards-cah-v1p2-1971|CAH
Vol. I, Part 2]], gives a purely **structural** account: hereditary nomarchs,
fragmentation of crown lands, and tax-exempt funerary endowments that "exhausted
the king's resources." The same case is now linked to the **4.2-kiloyear event**
in palaeoclimate, keeping the climatic reading alive — a textbook instance of the
recurring environmental-vs-political standoff.

## A Second Bronze Age Test Case — the Fall of Akkad

The same standoff governs the collapse of the [[akkadian-empire|Akkadian Empire]],
the world's first empire (~2154 BCE). [[edwards-cah-v1p2-1971|CAH Vol. I, Part 2]]
(Gadd, Ch. XIX) gives a strictly **political-military** account: imperial
overextension, the unreconciled Sumerian cities revolting at every accession, and
the convergent [[gutian-invasion|Gutian]] and Amorite invasions. Sumerian tradition
(*The Curse of Agade*) gave a **moral-providential** one — divine punishment of
[[naram-sin|Naram-Sin's]] impiety. Modern palaeoclimate work (**Harvey Weiss**,
from the abrupt abandonment of **Tell Leilan** in the Khabur) added a **climatic**
one: the **4.2-kiloyear aridification event** as the trigger of collapse and
nomadic influx — the very same horizon invoked for the
[[fall-of-old-kingdom-2160bce|Egyptian Old Kingdom]] and the
[[early-bronze-age-levant|Levantine EB cities]]. That a *single climatic event* is
now proposed to explain a near-simultaneous collapse across Egypt, the Levant, and
Mesopotamia is the strongest modern form of the determinist case — and is contested
on exactly the resilience/contingency grounds above (regional variation, political
agency, the patchiness of the synchronism). CAH (1971) predates the thesis entirely.

## A Third Position — Collapse as Rational Economizing (Tainter)

[[fagan-world-prehistory-2020|Fagan & Durrani (2020)]] introduce a further,
partly orthogonal account: **Joseph Tainter's** collapse-as-rational-process
thesis. On this view, growing complexity is initially a successful, low-cost
strategy for solving problems — agricultural intensification, bureaucracy, and
trade networks that fund elite-driven monumental works — but the **marginal
returns on further complexity decline over time**, leaving no reserve capacity to
absorb droughts, famines, or floods. Collapse follows once a society's
constituents perceive that continued centralization no longer pays off:
"collapse is not a catastrophe but a rational process." Tainter adds a
structural condition for *complete* collapse: it requires a power vacuum. In
"peer-polity interaction" systems — Fagan & Durrani cite Sumerian cities,
Minoan/Mycenaean palace-kingdoms, and Maya city-states — the collapse of one
polity alone simply invites domination by a rival, so systemic collapse occurs
only when the whole interacting cluster fails simultaneously. **Teotihuacan** is
their illustrative case: peak population above 125,000 (over 85% of the Valley
of Mexico's population) by AD 600, collapsing in the 8th century AD with
population falling to a quarter of its peak within 50 years. Tainter's thesis sits
alongside, rather than simply restating, Positions 1 and 2 above: it treats
collapse as an economizing decision rather than either an ecological overshoot
or a political failure narrowly construed, though it shares Position 2's
resistance to reading collapse as pure environmental catastrophe.

## Where Things Stand

**Open.** Radkau's own resolution is a middle path: the Middle Millennium shows
"not only indications of degradation, but also signs of a symbiosis of humans and
nature and of a co-evolution of culture and nature." The cases remain individually
debated — the ecological reading of the Maya is resilient; Angkor and Norse
Greenland have moved toward contingency-and-resilience readings; Great Zimbabwe
remains thin. The broader methodological dispute over **environmental determinism**
as a mode of world-historical explanation is unresolved and recurs across the wiki
(see geographic determinism when created, and [[pleistocene-megafaunal-extinction]]).
