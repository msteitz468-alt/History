# The Causes of the Second Punic War

A genuine and long-standing interpretive dispute, already alive in antiquity and
sharpened by modern scholarship. Treated across [[astin-cah-v8-1989|CAH VIII]] Chs
2–3 (Scullard, Briscoe). The war's *beginning* (the
[[siege-of-saguntum-219bce|fall of Saguntum]]) is clear; its *causes* are not.

## The ancient debate

- **Polybius** distinguished beginnings (*archai*) from causes (*aitiai*) and
  located three real causes: (1) the **wrath of [[hamilcar-barca|Hamilcar]]** and
  the Barcids at the outcome of the First War; (2) Rome's **seizure of Sardinia**
  (238) during Carthage's Mercenary War, which Polybius judged unjust; and (3) the
  **success of the Carthaginians in Spain**, which gave them the confidence and base
  to act. He treated Saguntum and the Ebro crossing as *archai*, not *aitiai*.
- **Fabius Pictor** (the anti-Barcid Roman tradition) blamed instead the personal
  **ambition of Hasdrubal and Hannibal**, governing Spain independently of Carthage
  — a view Polybius explicitly rejected, and which served the self-justification of
  Carthaginians who, after the defeat, wished to blame the Barcids.

## The Saguntum / Ebro legal tangle

The factual core is a collision of two agreements:
- the **Ebro treaty** (226) between Rome and Hasdrubal, setting the Ebro as the
  northern limit of Carthaginian arms — which Carthage read as implicitly freeing
  it to act *south* of the river; and
- Rome's separate tie with **[[siege-of-saguntum-219bce|Saguntum]]**, a city
  *south* of the Ebro, formed "several years before."

Disputed points (Scullard, Ch. 2): the **date** of the Saguntine tie (before or
after 226?); its **legal nature** (a formal *foedus* binding Rome, or a looser
*deditio in fidem* leaving Rome morally but not legally obliged — which better
explains Rome's long inaction during the siege?); whether the Ebro agreement bound
the **Carthaginian state** or was Hasdrubal's unilateral pledge (Bickerman's
*berit* hypothesis); and the geography (later writers, even Polybius once, wrongly
placed Saguntum *north* of the Ebro to brand Hannibal a treaty-breaker).

## The modern positions

- **"Wrath of the Barcids" accepted vs. rejected.** Scullard separates the certain
  fact (Hamilcar built a Spanish empire) from the disputed claim (he planned it
  from the start as a base for a war of revenge), reading the Spanish venture as
  primarily **compensatory** for lost Sicily and Sardinia. Errington (1970) rejects
  "the wrath of the Barcids" as a later oral tradition circulating about Polybius'
  time.
- **Roman responsibility.** Was Rome's pressure (the Sardinian seizure, the
  Saguntine tie used to hem in Punic Spain) a provocation, or was Carthage the
  aggressor at Saguntum?
- **Defensive vs. aggressive imperialism.** The deeper frame: Briscoe inclines to
  see Rome's wars of this era as driven less by **acquisitive imperialism** than by
  insecurity (a reading he extends to the 200 war on Macedon); **W. V. Harris**
  (whose chapter on western expansion is in the same volume) argues for a more
  systemic Roman appetite for war and conquest. The two positions frame the whole
  interpretation of mid-Republican expansion.

- **Hoyos: Rome's démarche as the proximate trigger.** Dexter Hoyos
  ([[hoyos-hannibals-dynasty-2003|*Hannibal's Dynasty*, 2003]]) rejects any genuine Barcid
  war-plan under [[hamilcar-barca|Hamilcar]] or [[hasdrubal-the-fair|Hasdrubal the Fair]] —
  the Spanish programme, he argues, was pragmatic rebuilding, undertaken without the
  alliance- or fleet-building a war of revenge would require. He reads the **238/237
  Sardinia seizure** as an opportunistic Roman power-play (not a treaty entitlement), and
  locates the proximate trigger in Rome's own conduct in 220–219: entrenching the anti-Punic
  faction at [[siege-of-saguntum-219bce|Saguntum]] by executing its opponents, and delivering
  an uncompromising démarche to [[hannibal|Hannibal]] at New Carthage, which Hannibal read as
  intent to destabilise Punic Spain. On this view the initiative for renewed war came from
  Rome, though Hoyos agrees the underlying great-power collision was probably unavoidable.

- **Mutual-willingness framing.** Charles Freeman ([[freeman-egypt-greece-rome-2014]])
  offers a middle-ground reading distinct from both the pro-Rome and pro-Barcid
  positions above: neither Rome nor Carthage, in his view, "appears to have had any
  inhibitions about going to war again" in 219–218 — a mutual-willingness account that
  resists placing sole blame on either Hannibal's siege of Saguntum or Roman provocation.

## Resolution status

**Open.** The legal and chronological puzzles of Saguntum and the Ebro cannot be
fully resolved from the propaganda-laden sources; the "defensive vs. aggressive
imperialism" debate is a structural disagreement about the nature of Roman power,
not a factual question to be settled.
