# The Aḫḫiyawa Question

Is **Aḫḫiyawa** — a western power named repeatedly in 14th–13th-century
[[hittites|Hittite]] records — the same as the **Mycenaean Greeks** (the *Achaioi*
of Homer)? The question matters enormously: if yes, it is the **only contemporary
documentary attestation of Mycenaean Greece in the political history of the Late
Bronze Near East**, naming the [[mycenaean-civilization|Mycenaeans]] as a player in
the great-powers game. [[edwards-cah-v2p2-1975|CAH Vol. II, Part 2]] is unusual in
**disagreeing with itself**, which is why this is filed as a live controversy.

## The evidence

Aḫḫiyawa appears in Hittite texts from [[mursili-ii|Muršili II]] onward: its king is
addressed (at times) as a "Great King," an equal; it is a **sea-power** linked to
the coastal city **Millawanda/Millawata** (widely identified as Miletus); its gods
are invoked when the Hittite king is ill; a member of its royal house learns
chariot-driving in Hatti; and the **Tawagalawa letter** is a Hittite protest to the
Aḫḫiyawan king over one **Piyamaradu**, who used Millawanda as a base against Hittite
Lukka (Lycia). The name *Aḫḫiyawa* resembles *Achaiwia* / *Achaioi*.

## The positions

**1. Aḫḫiyawa = the Mycenaean Greeks (mainland).** Held in this volume by
**Stubbings** (Ch. XXII(a)) and the Aegean tradition (Page): "it is now generally
accepted that the name of Aḫḫiyawa... refers to the land of Mycenaean Greeks." The
match in name, the sea-power profile, the Syrian trade, and the Millawanda/Miletus
link (where Mycenaean pottery and a Mycenaean-style wall are found) all point to
Greeks. The strongest single argument is that it makes the Hittite, Egyptian and
archaeological pictures cohere.

**2. Aḫḫiyawa = an Anatolian power, not Mycenae.** Held in this same volume by
**Goetze** (Ch. XXI(a)): "the similarity in name with that of the Achaeans is not
sufficient reason to seek its capital in Mycenae... the texts furnish no valid
argument for looking outside of Asia Minor," with a north-western Anatolian location
preferred. This is the older, cautious philological position.

**3. Aḫḫiyawa = a Mycenaean offshoot, not the mainland.** A middle view (noted by
Stubbings): even if Aḫḫiyawa is "Greek," the Hittites may have meant not the
Peloponnesian heartland but a **dependent Mycenaean kingdom** — **Rhodes** is a
plausible candidate (thoroughly Mycenaean by LH IIIa, a wide-ranging sea-trader), or
a coastal Anatolian principality under Mycenaean rulers.

## Status

**Partially resolved.** The mainstream of scholarship since this volume has settled
firmly on **Aḫḫiyawa = the Mycenaean Greek world** (position 1/3), so Goetze's
strict Anatolian view (position 2) is now a minority. What remains genuinely open is
**which** Mycenaean entity is meant — the mainland kingdom(s), a single hegemon, or a
western Anatolian/Rhodian outpost — and how much real authority the Aḫḫiyawan king
held over Millawanda and figures like Piyamaradu. The volume's internal split
(Goetze vs. Stubbings) preserves the moment before the consensus hardened.

## Significance

On the answer hangs whether the **Mycenaeans** can be written into documented
international history (the Hittite archives) rather than known only archaeologically
and through later Greek legend — and, by extension, whether the Hittite west
(Wiluša/[[troy|Troy]], Lukka, Millawanda) is the historical backdrop to the
**Trojan War** tradition.
