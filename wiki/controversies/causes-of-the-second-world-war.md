# Causes of the Second World War

What caused the **Second World War** is less evenly balanced than the [[causes-of-the-first-world-war|1914
debate]] — a strong mainstream view assigns primary responsibility to **Nazi Germany's expansionist,
ideological aims** — but it remains genuinely contested at the edges, above all because of **A. J. P.
Taylor's** revisionist intervention. The wiki records the positions following
[[taylor-origins-second-world-war|Taylor's *Origins of the Second World War*]] and its critics.

## Positions

- **Intentionalist (the mainstream).** The war was the intended outcome of **Hitler's programme** — the
  *Mein Kampf* / *Lebensraum* design for a racial war of conquest and annihilation in the East. Nazi
  ideology, not accident, is the proximate cause; appeasement merely delayed an inevitable reckoning.
  *Held by:* Trevor-Roper, Bullock, the bulk of the field.
- **Taylor's revisionism.** [[taylor-origins-second-world-war|A. J. P. Taylor]] argued that **Hitler was
  an opportunist improviser**, not the executor of a blueprint; that the war grew from the **unstable 1919
  settlement** and ordinary great-power diplomacy; and that 1939 owed much to **miscalculation**. Widely
  attacked for normalizing Hitler and slighting Nazi ideology, but field-changing in forcing attention to
  structure and the diplomatic record.
- **The legacy of 1919.** A structural view (overlapping both poles): the **Versailles settlement** — too
  harsh to reconcile Germany, too soft to keep it down — and the German grievance it bred were the
  enabling condition; the Second World War as the unfinished business of the [[first-world-war-1914|First]].
- **Failure of collective security / appeasement.** The collapse of the **League of Nations** and the
  Anglo-French policy of **appeasement** (Munich, 1938) emboldened Hitler; the debate is whether
  appeasement was culpable weakness or rational *Realpolitik* given the constraints.
- **Economic and Depression causation.** The **Great Depression**, autarky, and the collapse of the
  liberal world economy radicalized politics and made expansion (German, Japanese, Italian) seem a
  solution to economic crisis.
- **The Asia-Pacific origins.** A separate strand stresses that the war began in **Asia** (Japan's
  invasion of China, 1937, or Manchuria, 1931) and that Japanese imperial expansion had its own logic,
  not reducible to the European story.

- **Payne (1995) — twofold causation.** [[payne-history-of-fascism-1995|Payne]]
  splits the difference with a two-factor account: "the mutual competition of
  power politics and imperial interests, compounded by the effects of the new
  revolutionary ideologies in Germany, Italy, Japan, and the Soviet Union" —
  yet insists the war "would not have occurred" without Hitler, Mussolini,
  and the Japanese military personally. He adds a Taylor-adjacent nuance the
  mainstream partly accepts: "Hitler had no intention of starting a world war
  in 1939"; he sought to isolate and destroy enemies one at a time, and "the
  decision for a broader European war on September 3, 1939, was thus made in
  London and Paris," partly for the balance of power and partly from "a kind
  of moral conviction that Hitlerism was so evil and dangerous that it must
  be stopped." The scale of Hitler's goals (Lebensraum, racial war) remains
  for Payne the "revolutionary or ultra-'fascist'" cause distinguishing 1939
  from ordinary German expansionism; the December 1941 declaration of war on
  the US was "the most serious of Hitler's blunders." His closing thesis:
  the war was fascism's chosen test and therefore its self-destruction —
  fascism was "destroyed from the outside by military defeat," discredited
  "to a degree unprecedented among major modern political movements."

- **Evans (2005), *The Third Reich in Power*** (ingested 2026-07-02) — a firm,
  evidence-heavy intentionalist position on the German side: "War had been
  the objective of the Third Reich and its leaders from the moment they came
  to power in 1933," and the peacetime Reich was a society mobilized for it.
  Evans explicitly rejects the pure structuralist/functionalist model of
  "ceaseless radicalization driven by competition between satraps": "the
  Third Reich was driven in the first place from above, by Hitler and his key
  henchmen... he was in the driving-seat" — while conceding real moments of
  following others (Göring on Austria) and yielding (Munich). Documented
  waypoints: Hitler's 8 February 1933 statement that all job creation be
  judged by rearmament; the 28 February 1934 "living-space within ~8 years"
  address; the Four-Year Plan memorandum (1936); the Hossbach conference
  (1937); the "1943–45 at the latest" target. He equally documents that
  **German popular opinion was consistently war-anxious** (Sopade/SD reports,
  the silent Berlin parade of 27 September 1938) — the drive came from the
  top, not popular demand; Britain's September 1939 declaration was Hitler's
  "first serious miscalculation," and the Polish war "was from the outset a
  war of racial conquest, subjugation and extermination."

### Steiner's *Triumph of the Dark* (2011), via Evans (2015)

Zara Steiner's Oxford History of Modern Europe volume — Evans predicts it
"will remain the standard account" (his most laudatory verdict in the
collection). Her position: "It is with Hitler and Hitler's intentions that
any student of European international history must start" — expansionist
aims fixed before 1933, not products of the international system; "Whether
Germany was led by Bismarck, Wilhelm II, or Hitler made a vital difference
to its policies." Polycracy existed (Ribbentrop's Anglophobia, Göring's
maneuvers) but Hitler determined policy. Supporting judgments (Steiner's,
relayed by Evans): [[chamberlain-neville|Chamberlain]] "limited insight
and imagination, repeatedly prone to wishful thinking," with
"hubristic... self-confidence"; Eden "wobbly and unclear"; Halifax "out of
his depth"; Daladier by contrast clear-eyed ("I am not proud"). On the
"Munich bought time" defense: Germany, not the West, gained most from the
year's peace (the Wehrmacht was so unprepared in September 1938 that
generals contemplated arresting Hitler), though Steiner ultimately judges
the firmer-stand counterfactual "implausible" absent Anglo-French staff
coordination. League collapse ran through the 1933 German walkout and the
Ethiopia/Hoare–Laval debacle, which convinced Hitler the West would not
resist (→ Rhineland 1936). Stalin's pact calculation — let "the
capitalist powers fight it out" — rested on illusions that "would cost
his country dear in 1941"; an Anglo-French-Soviet alliance would have made
attacking Poland "an act of desperation," but talks foundered on
Chamberlain's anti-Communism and East European fears. Hitler did not want
"this particular war" (a 1939 two-front war with the West) and hoped for
Anglo-French inaction to the end; Steiner stresses — Evans concurring —
that the war was from the outset "racially motivated," which international
opinion never fully registered. Source:
[[evans-third-reich-history-memory-2015]].

## Evans's bounded counterfactual (2003, attributed)

The concluding synthesis of [[evans-coming-of-third-reich-2003]] ("A
Revolution of Destruction?") bears directly on the causal-weight question. By
the second half of 1932, Evans argues, "a military regime of some description
was the only viable alternative to a Nazi dictatorship" — parliamentary
legitimacy having collapsed under Brüning and been "massively and
deliberately accelerated by Papen." His counterfactual Schleicher-type
regime "would most probably have crushed many civil freedoms... launched a
drive for rearmament, repudiated the Treaty of Versailles, annexed Austria
and invaded Poland," and "would almost certainly have imposed severe
restrictions on the Jews" — **but**: "it is unlikely on balance that a
military dictatorship in Germany would have launched the kind of genocidal
programme that found its culmination in the gas chambers of Auschwitz and
Treblinka." The counterfactual severs structural authoritarian drift (war
over Poland plausibly overdetermined) from the specifically genocidal outcome
(Hitler-contingent). He pairs this with a comparative-European point: Weimar's
collapse "would not have appeared particularly unusual to a dispassionate
observer" amid the era's dictatorships — what made Germany different was
scale: "Europe's most powerful, most advanced and most populous country."
And with an ideological-overlap thesis: "only by striking a chord with
pre-existing, often deep-seated social and political values could the Nazis
rise so rapidly" — against both "political suicide" and structural-determinist
readings, with contingency preserved throughout ("chance and contingency were
to play their part here, too").

## Resolution status

**Partially resolved.** Unlike the 1914 debate, there is a clear mainstream: **German (and Japanese)
expansionist aggression** bears primary responsibility, and Taylor's strongest claims are largely
rejected. But the **weight of structure vs. intention**, the **judgement of appeasement**, and the
**relation of the European and Asian wars** remain live questions.
