# The Nature of Roman Imperialism

The central interpretive dispute of mid-Republican history: **why did Rome expand
so relentlessly** across the Mediterranean between the Punic Wars and 133? The
debate runs through every chapter of [[astin-cah-v8-1989|CAH VIII]] and pits the
volume's own contributors against one another.

## The two poles

### "Defensive imperialism"

The older orthodoxy (Maurice **Holleaux**, developed by Ernst **Badian**, and
represented in CAH VIII by **Errington** and **Briscoe**): Rome did not pursue a
premeditated programme of conquest. It was **reactive** — responding to appeals
from weaker states (the Mamertines, Saguntum, Greek *amici*), to perceived threats,
and above all to **fear** of strong neighbours. Eastern expansion in particular
(the Illyrian and Macedonian wars) is read as a series of limited, defensive
interventions with no territorial ambition; Briscoe extends this to the 200 war on
Macedon, driven by the trauma of Hannibal in Italy.

### "Aggressive / systemic imperialism"

The revisionist case, made forcefully by **W. V. Harris** (*War and Imperialism in
Republican Rome*, 1979, and his CAH VIII Ch. 5): Roman society was **structurally
geared to annual warfare**. War brought the aristocracy *gloria* and the *triumph*,
brought booty and land (*ager publicus*) to commanders and citizens alike, and was
sustained by a militarised value-system and the *fetial* machinery for manufacturing
*iusta causa*. On this reading the "appeals" and "fears" are largely pretexts or
rationalisations; the **regularity** of Roman war-making is the fact to be explained.
Harris applies this to Cisalpine Gaul, Spain (the pretextual *casus belli*, the
massacres, the relentless advance), and above all the **[[third-punic-war-149-146bce|
Third Punic War]]**, which he argues cannot be explained by genuine fear of a
disarmed, fleetless Carthage.

## Key battlegrounds within the volume

- **The Illyrian Wars (229/219):** reactive policing of piracy (Errington) vs.
  opportunistic weakening of neighbours.
- **The causes of the Second Punic War:** the [[causes-of-second-punic-war|Saguntum/
  Ebro debate]] — Briscoe's defensive reading vs. Harris's systemic one.
- **The Third Punic War:** the sharpest case. Harris dismisses both the
  "fear of Masinissa" and "fear of a resurgent Carthage" theories; the war is
  driven by the habit of profitable, glorious war.
- **Polybius' own view:** in his chapter on Greek reactions to the war (XXXVI.9),
  Polybius sets out opposing arguments. Whether *he* held the war defensive or an
  expression of a "more extreme love of power" infecting the Senate since **Pydna**
  is itself disputed — Harris reads the rhetorical structure (B caps A) as favouring
  the love-of-power view; Walbank is more cautious.

## Harris 2016 restatement (*Roman Power*)

[[harris-roman-power-2016|Harris, *Roman Power* (2016)]] restates and extends the 1979
aggressive-imperialism case inside a full-arc power framework ([[roman-power]]):

- **Definition.** Imperialism is “the activity by which a state or its surrogates
  impose its power, which it subsequently exercises and maintains, far beyond its
  previous boundaries, as part of a long-lasting policy of expansion” — “undeniably
  what Rome did, over several centuries.” Reluctance to call republican expansion
  imperialism is “bizarre evasion” driven by modern power-worship.
- **Formal and informal empire.** Equating the empire with provincial frontiers is
  a mistake: provinces + kings/vassals + free cities (Strabo). Macedonia’s late
  annexation (148) is not a puzzle if subjects and subordinate “friends” are lumped
  as realistic dominion.
- **“No plan” is a red herring.** No large empire was built by a blueprint; mid-
  republican Romans prayed for a growing empire; the Senate decided major wars in
  advance (Perseus 172; Carthage 149) and kept decisions secret.
- **Defensive imperialism as myth.** Nineteenth-/early-twentieth-century construct
  from Roman self-justifications; genuine defensive cases exist (Hannibal,
  Mithridates, Gauls 225) but the pattern is aggressive. After Zama no Mediterranean
  power could withstand prolonged Roman onslaught.
- **Elite and popular causes.** Senatorial *certamen gloriae* (gloria, laus, triumph)
  was functional for power and wealth; ordinary citizens expected material and
  psychological gains; assemblies almost never balked at foreign war before 151.
- **Economic motives integral.** Wars as “well-organized system of pillage” continuing
  after battle (enslavement, land, provincial revenues); ending of citizen *tributum*
  in 167 is part of the payoff structure.
- **Against “realist” parity (Eckstein).** Claim that all Mediterranean states were
  equally ruthless is false; centuries of nearly unremitting Roman warfare lack
  parallel; high military participation ratio (Andreski’s MPR) linked to large-scale
  enslavement of POWs.

Organizational techniques of **enduring control** (colonies, roads, citizenship,
elite co-optation) matter as much as battlefield wins; territory under Roman control
expanded ~3,000% between c.400 and 280 BC. Cross-link: expansion arc runs continuously
to Tiberius’s brake in **AD 16** ([[augustan-imperial-expansion]], [[tiberius]]).

## Resolution status

**Open.** Harris's 1979 book reframed the field and most scholars now grant that
the older "defensive imperialism" understated Roman bellicosity; but the precise
weight of fear, opportunism, economic motive, and aristocratic competition remains
contested, and varies case by case. The 2016 restatement keeps the aggressive/
systemic core while embedding it in external–internal power linkage and formal/
informal empire; realist multipolar readings remain a live counter-position. This is
the etic frame for the whole of Roman expansion in this period.

## CHGRW (Serrati, Sidebottom, Lendon, Goldsworthy, Adams)

[[sabin-van-wees-whitby-cambridge-history-greek-roman-warfare-2007|CHGRW]] largely **endorses
the post-Harris aggressive/systemic consensus** while refining mechanisms and adding
Principate-era IR ideology.

**Historiography and nuance (Serrati, I.14).** Historiography of Roman imperialism tracks modern
empire discourse: Mommsen’s positive justification; early-20th-c. “defensive imperialism”; Harris
(1979) and post-colonial critique — Romans as aggressors for territory, gain, and political
advancement. Contemporary scholarship is more nuanced (North 1981: conscious decisions vs
structural factors; some defensive wars possible) but “it is now generally accepted that the
Romans were an aggressive imperialist power, even though not all of their wars fell into this
pattern and not all of their aggression stemmed from conscious political choice.” Two broad
control models: **West** — direct conquest and provincialization; **East** — conquer without
permanent occupation, foster disunity, extract taxes/plunder while claiming Greek “freedom.”
Different policy ≠ less acquisitiveness. Against purely ad hoc readings (e.g. Gruen), Serrati
sees the volume of eastern exploitation as deliberate maximum-profit/minimum-legion strategy.
Hornblower (I.2) endorses recent insistence that Roman militarism was more marked than Greek
(Harris 1979), while noting built-in limitations until force over “legitimate” authority became
certain late (Senate’s refusal of Pompey’s eastern arrangements end of 60s).

**Amicitia, fetials, status warriors (Billows/Sidebottom).** Greek *philia* ≠ Roman *amicitia*:
transferred internationally, Romans always cast themselves as superiors owed deference — making
*amicitia* “an extraordinarily hypocritical… tool of aggression under the guise of *bellum
iustum*” (Billows via Gruen). Sidebottom: do not elevate post-Renaissance Western diplomatic
norms to universal practices; *fetiales* rituals framed war as putting issues before the gods —
victory proved a “just war.” Rejects Badian-style reluctant-annexation reading of Republican
gifts/wills of kingdoms: Senate reluctance was about not letting *individuals* harvest prestige
from new provinces (Harris/North), not principled anti-annexation. Cicero’s *fides* + *salus*
justification does not equal reluctant imperialism: Romans as “status warriors” could treat mere
barbarian existence/attitude as threat. Stoic *philanthropia* could justify *any* imperial war.
Goldsworthy: imperialism’s focus was ethnic/political (peoples, kings), not territorial/geographic
— “no evidence for the Romans ever fighting a war simply to control territory” (Isaac). On
Parthia: Isaac sees Rome usually aggressive; Goldsworthy adds Romans may still have *feared*
them (Carthage 149 analogy) while aiming at ultimate victory and using war-threat for lesser ends.

**Virtus as structural cause (Lendon, I.15).** Cult of *virtus* as structural driver of
mid-Republican war (Harris): most admired quality best displayed in war; rich needed war for
politics, poor for loot. “Origins” of each war over-invested if seeking modern-state-scale *casus
belli* — extreme sensitivity to honour made almost any slight a war ground; problem was selecting
which wars to fight each year.

**Italian demography and the “crisis of the peasantry” (Adams, II.6).** Traditional view — long
campaigns emptied Italian small farms into latifundia, causing Gracchan crisis and recruit shortage
— is doubted: archaeological field survey shows late-second/early-first-century Italy densely
settled; no archaeological evidence for latifundia growth at small farms’ expense; 133 BC crisis
concerned *ager publicus* allocation, not small-farm plight; army service remained crucial income
for archaeologically almost-invisible smallholders. Cross-links the imperialism debate to the
[[marian-army-reforms|recruitment/reform]] problem.

## Woolf, *Rome: An Empire's Story* (2012)

[[woolf-rome-empires-story-2012|Greg Woolf]] situates mid-Republican expansion between defensive and pure-Harris poles. For early Italy he stresses **institutional comparative advantage**, not unique militarism or technology: “Rome’s comparative advantage over its rivals must have been institutional… Nor is it really plausible to argue that Romans were more militaristic than their opponents.” Booty and prestige alone do not explain hegemony; “It was the structure of alliances built up from the fourth century that locked Rome into expansion,” pushing potential enemies farther out as allies were absorbed (Near Eastern / Aztec / Inka parallels). Late third–early second-century wars also faced **genuine major threats** (Carthage close at hand; eastern anarchy) that required the expansionist dynamic to be “stepped up, not wound down”—yet choice remained real (e.g. the assembly first refused then accepted war with Philip V in 200 BCE).

Voices of restraint matter on Woolf’s map: marked resistance to annexing territory east of the Adriatic; the Pergamum bequest of 133 accepted only when Tiberius Gracchus tied it to Italian land distributions; destruction of Carthage and Corinth (146) as synchronised obliteration signalling hegemony “unlike any other.” Eastern interventions were “inconsistent and unpredictable.” By the later Republic the system could not operate “on any scale larger than could be handled by one man with a moderate-sized army and an oversized ego,” with tax-farming and corruption built in; Crassus’ Parthian war is “as unnecessary as it was disastrous.” Cicero’s moral *patrocinium* diagnosis is rejected as structurally blind. After Actium the frame shifts: conquest-state → tributary/security empire (“After Actium security always trumped glory”); political economy as redistributive frontier spending; ancient vs modern empires incomparable in scale. Method map: Harris 1979, Gruen, Eckstein (*Mediterranean Anarchy*). Positions only—not adjudicated against Harris/Badian/Eckstein.
