# The Causes of Latin American Underdevelopment

Why is Latin America poorer than North America and Western Europe — and why has the gap
persisted? This is one of the great causal debates in modern history, and the wiki's two
[[colonial-latin-america|colonial]] sources stake out opposite poles of it:
[[galeano-open-veins|Galeano's *Open Veins*]] (the dependency indictment) versus
[[burkholder-johnson-colonial-latin-america|Burkholder & Johnson]]'s measured synthesis.

`[CONTRADICTION]` — flagged on [[colonial-latin-america]]: Galeano reads the whole colonial
era as a single act of extraction that *caused* underdevelopment; Burkholder & Johnson present
a complex, negotiated colonial order and resist reducing it to pillage.

## The positions

**1. Dependency / extraction (Galeano; Frank; Williams; Stein & Stein).** Latin America was
made poor by the same process that enriched the core. [[global-silver-trade|Silver]] funded
European (not Spanish) capitalism; [[plantation-economy|monoculture latifundia]] drained soil
and society; nineteenth-century free trade and twentieth-century US imperialism (the IMF,
multinationals) continued the drain. Underdevelopment is *relational and produced* — see
[[dependency-theory]]. **Strength:** takes the real violence and wealth-transfer seriously.
**Weakness:** mono-causal, externalist, empirically loose; cannot explain East Asian success
within the same world economy.

**2. Institutionalist (North; Engerman & Sokoloff; Acemoglu, Johnson & Robinson).** What
mattered was not extraction *per se* but the **institutions** colonialism created. Where
endowments favored plantations and mines worked by coerced labor and dense native populations
(the [[encomienda]], the [[mita-and-andean-reciprocity|mita]], slavery), Iberians built
**extractive institutions** — concentrated land, power, and human capital — that persisted and
throttled later growth; British North America's smallholder, free-labor settlement bred
**inclusive** institutions. **Strength:** explains the *reversal of fortune* (the richest
colonial cores became the poorest modern regions) and the divergence from North America.
**Weakness:** debated measurement; risks institutional determinism.

**3. Measured colonial historiography (Burkholder & Johnson).** Resists a single story: the
colonial economy was internally varied, locally negotiated, and not simply a metropolitan
machine; much of modern poverty is also rooted in **post-independence** instability, civil war,
and policy. **Strength:** empirical nuance. **Weakness:** offers diagnosis-by-complexity rather
than a crisp causal claim.

**4. Culturalist / policy (older "Black Legend"-adjacent and liberal accounts).** Locates
causes in the **Iberian inheritance** (rentier values, weak property rights, Counter-Reformation
absolutism), nineteenth-century *caudillo* instability, and twentieth-century policy errors
(over-protected import substitution). Connected to the [[black-legend]] debate over Spanish
"backwardness." **Weakness:** can shade into ethnic essentialism; underrates external forces.

## Why it matters

The stakes are both historical and political: whether underdevelopment is best remedied by
**delinking/redistribution** (the dependency prescription), by **institutional reform**, or by
**market integration** turns on the diagnosis. The debate also reframes the conquest and
colony: was Potosí's silver a curse that drained the Andes, or did the deeper damage lie in the
*institutions* the silver economy entrenched?

## Resolution status

**Open.** Mainstream economic history has largely moved from strict dependency theory toward
**institutionalist** explanations, but no consensus reconciles institutions, external
extraction, geography/endowments, and post-independence agency. The ECLA terms-of-trade insight
and the moral force of the extraction account remain live; the externalist mono-causality of
strict *dependencia* is widely rejected.
