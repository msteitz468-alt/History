# The Mound Builder Myth

## The Dispute

From the late eighteenth through most of the nineteenth century, the thousands of earthen mounds and enclosures of the Ohio and Mississippi valleys were widely attributed to a vanished **"Mound Builder" race** — a people supposedly more civilized than, and ethnically distinct from, the Native Americans encountered by Europeans. On this view the mounds testified to a lost golden age that had been overrun and obliterated by "savage" Indians, who were therefore cast as intruders rather than heirs. The rival position, which eventually prevailed, held that the earthworks were the work of the **ancestors of the living Native nations**, in unbroken cultural continuity.

The dispute is classified here as primarily a **source-reliability / interpretation** conflict: the physical evidence (the mounds) was never in doubt; what was disputed was who made it, and that dispute was driven less by the evidence than by the ideological needs of a settler society.

## Why the Myth Was Attractive

The "vanished race" theory did political and moral work for nineteenth-century America. If a superior people had built the mounds and been destroyed by the ancestors of contemporary Indians, then:

- Native dispossession could be reframed as a kind of cosmic justice or mere repetition of an earlier conquest;
- the continent could be endowed with a romantic, ruins-strewn antiquity to rival Egypt and Greece; and
- living Native peoples could be denied any claim to the monumental past of their own land.

This is the framing captured in the very subtitle "Vanished Races." See [[allen-prehistoric-world-1885|Allen's *The Prehistoric World; or, Vanished Races* (1885)]], which states the reflex plainly: "The Indians of the prairies seem to be intruders on a fairer civilization... In the presence of their imposing ruins, we can not think that nomadic savages built them."

## Position 1 — A vanished, separate Mound Builder race

**Best arguments (as advanced at the time):** the scale and geometric regularity of works such as the Newark and Marietta enclosures and the great platform mound at [[cahokia|Cahokia]] seemed beyond the capacity of mobile foragers; living tribes disclaimed knowledge of the builders; and the apparent absence of mound-building among historic Indians implied a cultural break. Squier & Davis's monumental *Ancient Monuments of the Mississippi Valley* (1848), the first Smithsonian publication, lent the idea scientific prestige even as its authors were cautious.

**Holders:** popular and much antiquarian opinion through the 1880s; promoters of Old World, Toltec, or lost-continent origins.

## Position 2 — Ancestral Native Americans built the mounds

**Best arguments:** documented historic mound use and bone-burial customs (e.g. the Iroquois practice of gathering the dead into a mound every eight to ten years); the Natchez of the lower Mississippi, observed by the French still using temple mounds into the eighteenth century; stylistic and material continuities between mound contexts and historic Native material culture; and the absence of any positive evidence for an exterminated alien population.

**Holders:** Thomas Jefferson, whose careful stratigraphic excavation of a Virginia barrow (1780s) is often called the first scientific archaeology in America, concluded the mounds were Indian. The decisive case was made by **Cyrus Thomas** in his 1894 report for the Smithsonian's Bureau of Ethnology, which after systematic survey concluded flatly that the mounds were built by the ancestors of the historic Indians, ending the controversy in professional scholarship.

## Resolution

**Resolved by consensus.** Modern archaeology treats the earthworks as the products of indigenous North American societies across millennia — the Archaic (Watson Brake, Poverty Point), [[north-american-mound-building|Woodland Hopewell, and Mississippian]] traditions culminating at Cahokia — with no vanished alien race involved. The "Mound Builder" race is now studied not as prehistory but as a chapter in the history of American racial ideology and the history of archaeology.

## Allen (1885) as a Transitional Witness

[[allen-prehistoric-world-1885|Allen's 1885 synthesis]] is valuable precisely because it catches the controversy mid-collapse, nine years before Thomas. Allen preserves the romantic "vanished people" rhetoric of his subtitle, yet his chapter X is openly titled "Hard to distinguish them from the Indians," and he concedes that "some comparatively recent Indian tribes formed and used mound structures," cites the Iroquois bone-mound custom, and entertains Natchez and Pueblo continuity. The book thus documents the moment when even popular synthesizers, drawing on Putnam-era professional opinion, were already retreating from the strong "separate race" thesis toward the continuity position that Cyrus Thomas would soon make orthodox.
