# What Caused the End-Cretaceous (K–Pg) Mass Extinction?

The end-Cretaceous extinction (~66 million years ago, the K–Pg boundary; older
literature K–T) ended the reign of the non-avian dinosaurs and eliminated
pterosaurs, many marine reptiles, the ammonites, and much marine plankton. It is
the **best-studied of the five** [[concepts/mass-extinction|mass extinctions]],
recorded at scores of sites worldwide, yet the *cause* remains debated — a model
case of the difficulty of inferring cause and effect from the fragmentary fossil
and rock record.

## The Puzzle to Be Explained

Any adequate cause must account for four features: the event was **global**,
**rapid**, **biologically broad**, and **environmentally extensive**. But it must
also explain a fifth, awkward feature — **differential survival**: birds,
mammals, many insects, lizards, turtles, fish, corals, and diatoms passed
through and often flourished, while dinosaurs and ammonites did not, despite
sharing environments. This selectivity is the strongest argument against any
single catastrophic mechanism.

## Position 1 — Asteroid Impact (the Alvarez hypothesis)

In 1980 Luis and Walter Alvarez and colleagues found a sharp **iridium anomaly**
(iridium is rare on Earth, abundant in meteorites) in boundary rocks at Gubbio,
Italy, together with **shocked quartz** and glassy **spherules/tektites**. They
argued a ~10 km impact threw up a dust cloud that blocked sunlight, halted
photosynthesis on land and in the oceans, and collapsed food chains. The later
identification of the **Chicxulub crater** (Yucatán, Mexico) of matching age, and
the worldwide distribution of the boundary markers, made this the leading
explanation. Sharp coincident changes in marine faunas and in land-plant pollen
(a fern spike replacing angiosperms) support an abrupt event.

**Best argument:** the physical impact signature is global and unambiguous; the
coincidence of a crater of the right size and age with the extinction "cannot be
mere coincidence."

## Position 2 — Deccan Traps Volcanism

The boundary also coincides with the eruption of the **Deccan Traps**, flood
basalts covering ~250,000 square miles of western India, a mile or more thick.
The release of sulfur dioxide and CO₂ could have driven severe climate change,
acid rain, and ocean anoxia. Many paleontologists, skeptical of the impact model,
note that the precise timing of extinction **differs between groups** and that
some groups were little affected — patterns easier to reconcile with prolonged
environmental stress than with an instantaneous catastrophe.

**Best argument:** volcanism operates over the right (gradual-but-rapid)
timescale to produce staggered, differential effects, and its outgassing may
dwarf the impact's; its timing tracks the recovery of marine plankton.

## Position 3 — Combined / Multi-Causal

Because both impact and volcanism have strong, independent evidence, many
researchers hold they **reinforced one another** — a one-two blow injecting
aerosols into the atmosphere to produce a "nuclear winter"–like effect — possibly
compounded by contemporaneous sea-level change and continental reconfiguration.
On this view there may be **no single cause**, and the different extinction
events of Earth's history need not share one mechanism.

## State of the Debate

Rhodes credits the Alvarez team with **reviving serious, quantitative debate** by
giving a once-speculative subject a hard empirical basis. The impact is now
broadly (not universally) regarded as the principal trigger, with Deccan volcanism
a contributing or aggravating factor — hence *partially resolved*. Genuine
ambiguity persists over the exact timing of each group's extinction and over
whether an impact alone can produce such strongly differential survival. The
dispute exemplifies a recurring methodological problem for [[concepts/deep-time|
deep-time]] history: limited time resolution in old rocks makes "sudden" and
"gradual" hard to distinguish.
