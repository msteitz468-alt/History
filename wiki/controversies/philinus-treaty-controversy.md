# The Philinus Treaty — Did Rome and Carthage Agree to Spheres of Influence?

## Statement of the Controversy

**Polybius** (III.26.2–5), drawing on the Sicilian historian **Philinus of Agrigentum**
(his principal source for the pro-Carthaginian perspective in the First Punic War),
states that Philinus claimed a treaty existed between Rome and Carthage under which:
- Rome agreed not to interfere in Sicily
- Carthage agreed not to interfere in Italy

If this treaty existed and was valid in 264 BCE, Rome violated it by crossing the
Straits to help the Mamertines — making Rome the aggressor and treaty-breaker.

Polybius **vehemently denied** the treaty's existence: "there is, as a fact, no such
document at all, nor ever was there" — basing this on his personal search (or that of
his friends) in the **treasury of the aediles** (the Roman document archive), where
the three known Romano-Punic treaties were kept on bronze.

## Positions and Evidence

### Against the Treaty (Polybius's Position)

- No bronze copy found in the Roman treasury of the aediles — the same archive that
  preserved the other three Polybian treaties
- The existence of the treaty, if true, would have branded Rome as a treaty-breaker
  in 264 — making this information politically convenient for Carthage and for
  anti-Roman writers
- **Cato**, Rome's greatest denouncer of Carthage (*foedifragi*, "treaty-breakers"),
  never cited a Roman violation of this treaty — something he surely would have done
  if it existed
- The treaty's clause (Roman non-interference in Sicily) clashes with another clause
  in the 279/8 treaty that *permits* both sides to send troops to Italy or Sicily

### For the Treaty

**Servius** (*Commentary on Vergil*, Scholium on Aeneid IV.628) states that a treaty
provided "that the Romans should not approach the shores of the Carthaginians nor the
Carthaginians those of the Romans" — the two *litora* (shores) clearly corresponding
to Italy and Sicily.

**Livy** (Epitomator, Book XIII) describes the 279/8 treaty as the fourth renewal
("quarto... renovatum") of an original treaty. If there were five treaties total
(original + four renewals), one is unaccounted for in Polybius — consistent with a
306 BCE "Philinus" treaty being Livy's unknown extra.

**Livy XXI.10.8** puts the following words into the mouth of **Hanno** in the Saguntum
debate: "we could not keep our hands off Tarentum, that is from Italy, as by treaty
bound" — explicitly citing a treaty obligation to refrain from Italy.

**The Punic fleet at Tarentum (272 BCE)**: Livy (*Epitome* XIV) states that this action
was "a breach of treaty" (*quo facto ab his foedus violatum est*) — if the fleet's
appearance at Tarentum was a treaty violation, there must have been a treaty forbidding
Carthage to intervene in Italy.

**The 279/8 treaty clause** itself: Polybius (III.25.3–6) records a mutual-aid clause
in the 279/8 treaty allowing both parties to send troops to Italy or Sicily in the
other's territory against Pyrrhus. This permissive clause **implies a prior prohibition**
— you do not need to create an exception to a rule that doesn't exist. Scullard finds
this the strongest indirect evidence for some form of the "spheres" arrangement.

### State of the Evidence

Polybius's failure to find the document is not conclusive proof it never existed:
- Cicero (*De Legibus* III.46) complains bitterly about Roman carelessness with
  documents — "we have no guardianship of the laws... we get them from the state
  copyists but have no official records"
- The three known treaties were on bronze; other treaties may have existed only as
  original written documents on skin or papyrus — more easily lost, mislaid, or
  suppressed
- One Lex Acilia had been turned face-to-wall and used as backing for the lex agraria
  (Mattingly): bronze was reusable
- If the treaty branded Rome as an aggressor in 264, there was motive for deliberate
  suppression (Suet. *Iul.* 28.3 records Pompey breaking into the *Aerarium* and
  altering a law)

The equally plausible counter-argument: if Philinus invented the treaty (or it was
Carthaginian propaganda), one would expect Cato — the most zealous denouncer of
Carthaginian bad faith — to have countered the charge. His silence on Rome's alleged
violation is striking.

## Dating of the Alleged Treaty

Most scholars who accept the treaty's existence place it in **306 BCE** (the year of
the documented Livian treaty renewal), when:
- Rome was near victory in the Samnite Wars and could plausibly claim to speak for
  all Italy
- Carthage was embroiled with Agathocles and wanted Roman neutrality
- Both powers were growing in confidence and strategic self-awareness

Some prefer to see the "spheres" understanding as a vague clause of the 279/8 treaty
(made more explicit by post-war Carthaginian propaganda), which then became the
"Philinus treaty" story. This view is associated with De Sanctis and Walbank.

## Resolution Status

**Open.** Scullard (CAH VII.2) concludes that "some such reciprocal self-denying
agreement seems probable" but that its exact terms, date, and formal status "must
remain uncertain." The combination of Servius's testimony, Livy's Hanno speech, the
272 BCE foedus-violation notice, and the 279/8 permissive clause creates a cumulative
case that *some* mutual territorial understanding existed. Whether it was a formal
treaty (as Philinus claimed), a looser understanding, or merely retroactive
propaganda remains unresolved.

The controversy touches directly on whether Rome committed an act of aggression in
264 BCE — a question with significant historiographical implications for how we assess
Rome's rise to Mediterranean dominance.

## Scholars Involved

- **Polybius** (2nd cent. BCE): denies treaty; primary ancient source for both sides
- **Philinus of Agrigentum** (3rd cent. BCE): asserted treaty; his work does not survive
- **Mommsen** (19th cent.): foundational discussion; many combinations of treaty dates
- **Taubler** (1913): placed first two Polybian treaties in 348 and 306
- **Heuss** (1949): Carthage had no hostile intent toward Italy; treaty unnecessary
- **Meister** (1970, 1975): argued for the treaty in 306
- **Mitchell** (1971): independent argument for the treaty
- **Walbank** (1957–79): detailed Polybius commentary; cautious on the treaty's existence
- **Badian** (1980): renewed attack on the treaty's existence
- **Scullard** (CAH VII.2, 1989): accepts probability of some form of the arrangement
