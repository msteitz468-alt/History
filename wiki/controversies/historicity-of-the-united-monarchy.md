# Historicity and Scale of the Israelite United Monarchy

Both the **historicity** and the **scale** of the tenth-century BCE polity associated with [[david|David]] and [[solomon|Solomon]] are at issue. The dispute centers on whether archaeology confirms, undermines, or is simply neutral toward the biblical picture of a unified [[hebrew-kingdom|Hebrew monarchy]] governing Judah and Israel from [[jerusalem|Jerusalem]] in the tenth century BCE. Three substantive positions can be distinguished.

## Background

The controversy became acute in the 1990s when two independent challenges converged. First, a group of "biblical revisionists" (the "Copenhagen School") denied any historical value to the monarchic narratives and depicted the united monarchy as a Hellenistic or Persian literary invention. Second, and more consequentially for archaeology, Israel Finkelstein introduced the "Low Chronology," which proposed down-dating the Iron I–IIA transition by roughly eighty to one hundred years, effectively stripping the tenth century of all monumental archaeological correlates. These two challenges intersected with longer-standing debates over the six-chambered gates at Gezer, Hazor, and Megiddo; the size and character of tenth-century [[jerusalem|Jerusalem]]; and the meaning of extrabiblical inscriptions (the Tel Dan stele, the Mesha inscription). See [[sources/dever-beyond-the-texts-2017|Dever (2017)]], Chapter 4.

## Position 1 — Minimalist / Revisionist

**Principal scholars:** Philip R. Davies, Thomas L. Thompson, Niels Peter Lemche (the "Copenhagen School"); also Mario Liverani (*Israel's History and the History of Israel*, 2007) for a sophisticated version.

**Core claim:** The Hebrew Bible's narratives about Saul, David, and Solomon are literary constructs of the Persian or Hellenistic period with little or no historical residue. The polity described in 1–2 Samuel and 1 Kings is "an imaginary world of long ago that never existed as such" (Thompson, quoted in Dever). By the tenth century, Judah was a sparsely settled tribal region incapable of supporting any monarchy. Lemche characterizes the supposed transition from the Amarna-age city-state system to an Israelite state as merely a shift from one "patronage society" to another — neither a definition of a state nor an explanation of change.

**Key arguments:**
- Absence of monumental architecture in tenth-century Jerusalem that could be independently dated.
- Dependence on biblical texts composed centuries after the events and shaped by theological agendas.
- Thompson's population estimate: "only a few dozen villagers lived as farmers in all the Judean highlands" in the tenth century, making any kingdom impossible.
- Liverani and others argue that "the Israel of the texts" is a Hellenistic literary creation; his chapters on the monarchic period begin with "The Invention of…"

**Assessment in the literature:** Dever argues that this position "exhibits neither patience nor competence" regarding the archaeological data and that its population claims are flatly contradicted by survey results (Ofer's survey maps at least 270 tenth-century sites in Judah alone, with a built-up area of ~5,000 acres and a population of up to 50,000). Minimalists tend to treat the absence of excavations on the Temple Mount as proving the absence of Solomonic construction rather than as a logistical constraint.

## Position 2 — Low Chronology

**Principal scholars:** Israel Finkelstein; associates and students include Liliane Nadelman Franklin (ashlar masonry argument), David Ussishkin (Jezreel link), and Ze'ev Herzog and Lily Singer-Avitz (southern stratigraphy). Some C-14 dating work by Finkelstein and Piasetzky.

**Core claim:** The conventional Iron I–IIA chronological horizon should be shifted down by roughly eighty to one hundred years. The monumental strata at Megiddo (VA/IVB), Hazor (X), and Gezer (VIII) — including the famous six-chambered gates — belong not to the tenth century (Solomon's era) but to the ninth century, the age of the Omride dynasty (**Ahab**). If correct, there is no "age of Solomon" in the archaeological record: the tenth century leaves only a modest polity at best, and a full northern Israelite state emerges only in the ninth century. In the south, Finkelstein placed the emergence of a Judahite state as late as the eighth century (gradually retreating to the ninth century in later publications).

**Key arguments (Finkelstein's sequential case):**
1. **Lachish VI argument (initial, now widely rejected):** Absence of Late Mycenaean IIIC:1 monochrome pottery from Lachish VI implies Philistine wares arrived later. Critics note this is an argument from silence — Lachish is simply too far inland for Philistine monochrome ware regardless of date.
2. **Jezreel connection:** Ussishkin's excavations at Jezreel showed Iron Age pottery similar to Megiddo VA/IVB. Since Jezreel is biblically identified as Ahab's winter retreat (ninth century), Megiddo VA/IVB should be ninth century too. Critics counter that the pottery of the mid-to-late tenth and early-to-mid ninth century is so similar at well-stratified sites that fine-grained distinctions cannot convincingly be made, and this similarity does not prove chronological identity.
3. **Ashlar masonry argument:** Norma Franklin argued that the Egyptian "short cubit" ashlar masonry at Megiddo VA/IVB matches Building Period I at Samaria (Ahab, ninth century). Critics note that ashlar masonry of this kind occurs both earlier and later and is not a reliable independent date.
4. **C-14 dating:** Finkelstein claimed radiocarbon dates supported his low chronology at Megiddo; Amihai Mazar's C-14 program at nearby Tel Reḥov produced dates supporting the conventional (or "modified conventional") chronology. Ongoing joint work at the Weizmann Institute has narrowed the differences to forty to fifty years. As Dever summarizes: "All authorities agree that future C-14 dates are not likely to resolve the issue completely, due to factors of uncertainty built into the method itself."
5. **Jerusalem and the stepped stone structure:** Finkelstein, Ussishkin, and Herzog argued that the massive "stepped stone structure" on the eastern Ophel — key evidence for a substantial tenth-century Jerusalem — dates to the late Iron II or even Persian period. Jane Cahill's detailed ceramic analysis (the only published pottery study) dates the sealed fills below the stone mantle to the early Iron I (twelfth century BCE) at latest, with ceramic parallels to tenth-century levels at other sites. Finkelstein's own criterion — "the key site for identifying the Shoshenq horizon … is Tell ʿArād" — actually supports the conventional chronology, since Arad XII is dated to circa 920 by virtually all authorities including Finkelstein himself.

**Finkelstein on the Shoshenq list:** Finkelstein eventually accepted the Shoshenq campaign (c. 920 BCE) as a fixed point but proposed that it destroyed a "Saulide confederacy" centered at Gibeon rather than a Davidic/Solomonic state centered at Jerusalem. Archaeological evidence for any significant tenth-century occupation at Gibeon is absent.

**Finkelstein's own internal inconsistency (noted by Dever):** In 1995 Finkelstein declared that "the genuine exceptional event in the highlands of the southern Levant in the late-second to early-first millennium BCE was not the 'Israelite Settlement,' but the emergence of the United Monarchy — the unification of the entire region and most of the lowlands under one rule." He never reconciled this statement with his later denial of any such entity.

**Raz Kletter's critique (2004):** "The LC [low chronology] is not based on sound methodology, but largely on negative evidence and on an outdated model of social evolution.… Much like a book of fiction, a theory presented to the academic world must be explored and evaluated by scholars other than those who wrote it. If it has to be constantly nourished and kept alive by frequent changes and revisions from its author, it does not deserve a place of its own."

## Position 3 — Centrist / Conventional

**Principal scholars:** William G. Dever, Lawrence Stager, Amihai Mazar, Avraham Faust, Nadav Na'aman (non-maximalist but anti-minimalist); Jane Cahill (Jerusalem stratigraphy); Anson Rainey and Kenneth Kitchen (Shoshenq chronology). Also represented by the "modified conventional chronology" of A. Mazar (Iron IIA beginning c. 980 BCE rather than c. 1000).

**Core claim:** A real, historically recognizable tenth-century polity — not the grandiose biblical empire, but an "early inchoate state" — existed in Judah by the early tenth century and likely extended into northern Israel as well. The conventional chronology (Iron IIA = c. 1000–925 BCE) is supported by stratigraphy, ceramic typology, and the Shoshenq fixed point, and the Low Chronology "has no basis whatsoever in fact." The scale was modest: Jerusalem was not a great imperial capital but a city of 5–25 acres with a population of perhaps 1,000–3,000, functioning as "the symbolic center, the capital of the cosmion" (Stager), or like a medieval *Burg* that was nonetheless "a power base of a political entity" (A. Mazar).

**Key archaeological evidence:**

### The Six-Chambered Gates (Hazor X, Megiddo VA/IVB, Gezer VIII)
Three sites show nearly identical four-entryway gates and casemate city walls in what the conventional chronology assigns to the tenth century. Yadin famously related all three to Solomon's centralized administration in Jerusalem (1 Kgs 9:15: "the levy which Solomon raised to build … Jerusalem and Hazor and Megiddo and Gezer"). Dever, who excavated the Gezer gate: "These monumental constructions were dated by the excavators to the mid-tenth century on the basis of the fundamental tools of archaeology: meticulous stratigraphy and comparative ceramic typology — not, as charged, on a literalistic reading of 1 Kgs 9:15–17." The pottery on the floor of Palace 10,000 adjoining the Gezer gate is red-washed, unslipped ware dating to "a short phase after the floruit of degenerate Philistine red-washed and painted wares but before red-slipped and hand-burnished wares come to predominate." The Shoshenq list plausibly includes Gezer (cartouche 12), providing a destruction date of c. 920. The Low Chronology "simply has no candidate for any ninth-century destruction" at Gezer.

### Khirbet Qeiyafa
A 6-acre fortified site in the Shephelah (Sorek Valley), excavated from 2008 by Garfinkel. A one-period barracks town (Stratum IV) with two three-entryway gates, casemate walls, courtyard houses integrated into the wall (a Judahite planning type), a tripartite storage building, and a cult room. The pottery is "clearly early, by consensus late eleventh- or early tenth-century at the latest" — even Low Chronology advocates accept this. C-14 dates cluster c. 1012–967 (68.2 percent confidence). Five-line and other ostraca in Proto-Canaanite script contain words for *mlk* (king) and *špṭ* (judge). Rollston (leading epigrapher): "This inscription constitutes further evidence for the presence of trained scribal professionals in the southern Levant during the late eleventh and early tenth centuries BCE. Those who wish to argue that there were no trained scribal professionals in ancient Israel and Judah during the tenth and ninth centuries continue to find themselves defending a position that is flying in the face of the epigraphic evidence." Dever: "Khirbet Qeiyafa alone, with its date more than a century earlier than the low chronology and the Iron I/IIA transition, settles the question of when a Judahite monarchy emerged, however limited."

### The Tel Dan Stele
An Aramaic inscription found in 1993–1995 in the gate complex at Tel Dan, datable paleographically to c. 841 BCE (the reign of Hazael of Damascus). It refers explicitly to *bêt-dāwîd* ("house/dynasty of David") and to a state of "Israel," naming two biblical kings — Jehoram of Israel and Amaziah of Judah. The name David may also appear on the ninth-century Moabite Mesha inscription. Dever: "An unforced reading can only conclude that the inscription … means just what it says. There was a historical David, and he was the founder of a dynasty well known to his neighbors." The stele is significant because it predates the biblical texts and is entirely independent of Israelite/Judahite literary tradition. Minimalists have claimed the stele is a modern forgery; this view is rejected across the field.

### The Shoshenq Campaign (c. 920 BCE)
Pharaoh Shoshenq I's topographic list at Karnak names over one hundred Levantine sites destroyed c. 920 BCE. Virtually every major excavated tenth-century Israelite site (Megiddo VA/IVB, Hazor X, Beth-Shean V lower, Gezer VIII, Tell el-Farʿah VIIb, Tel Reḥov V, Arad XII, multiple Negev forts, etc.) shows a destruction attributable to this horizon. Dever: "Several facts are established by the Shoshenq data. First, the pharaoh's extensive campaign in the southern Levant — to more than one hundred places, none in Judah — makes no sense if it had been directed merely at the Iron II highland villages, hardly a threat or of interest to Egypt. The objective must have been a well-established polity in southern Canaan." Furthermore, the anchor provided by Shoshenq's death (c. 924–918 BCE on alternative astronomically fixed chronologies) allows Solomon's death to be placed roughly c. 925–923 BCE on a historical synchronism. Working backward from Solomon to David, the Davidic dynasty must have existed by the mid-tenth century.

### Tel Reḥov C-14 Dates
Amihai Mazar's C-14 dating program at Tel Reḥov (near Beth-Shean) produced a series of results supporting the conventional or "modified conventional" chronology. Stratum V ends c. 920 (consistent with Shoshenq); Stratum IV ends c. 840 (consistent with Aramean incursions). These directly contradict Finkelstein and Piasetzky's C-14 results from Megiddo. Finkelstein himself later "actually moves up to Mazar's dates for the end of Reḥov V at circa 920 and Stratum IV at circa 840" (Dever, note 7, citing Finkelstein and Piasetzky 2011).

### Tenth-Century Jerusalem
Dever summarizes the centrist position: "(1) Some scholars have asserted that Jerusalem in the tenth century was simply 'too small' to have served as the capital of any Judahite kingdom or state.… [But] tenth-century Jerusalem was probably at least 10 acres in size, with an estimated population of some 1,000, or even up to 25 acres and a population of as many as 2,000–3,000. And in the surrounding countryside of Judah, surveys by Ofer have mapped at least 270 tenth-century sites, with a built-up area of some 5,000 acres and a population of up to 50,000." Na'aman (himself no maximalist): "Thanks to extensive archaeological research we can establish that over the course of the tenth–ninth centuries BCE, Judah developed from a rural, sparsely inhabited hill country polity to a state with a set of borders, fortified cities and a medium-sized centre." Cahill: "The archaeological evidence demonstrates that during the time of Israel's divided monarchy, Jerusalem was fortified, served by two water-supply systems, and populated by a socially stratified society that constructed at least two new residential quarters."

### The Negev Forts Network
More than fifty small, one-period forts scattered across the central Negev highlands, dated to the tenth century on pottery grounds and destroyed in the Shoshenq raid (c. 920) — a point accepted even by Finkelstein. The network "cannot possibly have been built and manned by highland villages such as those of the Iron I period. They are evidence of a centralized Judahite government in the tenth century" (Dever).

## Key Distinctions Cutting Across the Debate

**What is NOT disputed:**
- The Low Chronology and the Minimalist positions are distinct: the Low Chronology at least concedes a historical United Monarchy of some kind (Finkelstein posited a "Saulide confederacy" at Gibeon); only the Minimalists deny it entirely.
- No serious scholar today defends a maximalist reading of the biblical united monarchy as a vast empire stretching to the Euphrates. The debate is between those who see a modest real tenth-century state and those who deny any such state before the ninth or even the eighth century.
- David's historical existence is now supported by the Tel Dan stele; his "empire" as described in 2 Samuel is not supported archaeologically.
- The Temple Mount has never been excavated; the absence of archaeological evidence for Solomon's Temple is an access problem, not evidence of absence.

**The three-level chronological argument (Dever's synthesis):**
The entire Low Chronology depends on a cascade of stratigraphic re-assignments. If any one major site is correctly dated by the conventional chronology, the cascade breaks down. The Khirbet Qeiyafa C-14 dates (1012–967), accepted even by Low Chronology advocates, place a government-backed Judahite fortification in the late eleventh–early tenth century — precisely where the conventional chronology requires it and more than a century earlier than the Low Chronology can accommodate.

## Noll (2013): the moderate-skeptic reading

[[sources/noll-canaan-israel-antiquity-2013|K. L. Noll's textbook (2013)]] sits between the
minimalist and centrist poles and declines to defend a "United Monarchy" as usually defined.
His position:

- He frames the contest as **High Chronology** (a real Davidic–Solomonic state controlling
  Megiddo and Hazor, resting on biblical texts such as 1 Kgs 9.15) versus **Low Chronology**
  (Saul, David, and Solomon as minor kings of the Cisjordan Highlands who never held a major
  city — a thesis independent of any biblical text). Noll favours the **Low or Middle
  Chronology** and structures the book on Herzog's Middle Chronology.
- **Radiocarbon trends Low.** When sufficient, securely stratified samples are tested the C-14
  dates fall where both camps expected, but the general trend "favour[s] the Low Chronology";
  the Mazar/Herzog **Middle Chronology** is "mainly the result of dates obtained by the
  radiocarbon method, which fit the low chronology better" — "a generous concession towards
  the low chronology."
- **The biblical splendour is displaced memory.** The Cisjordan Highlands "could not produce
  this many men of military age in a single generation"; David's conquest catalogue (2 Samuel
  8) reads as later Iron II material retrojected (his Edomite victory in 2 Sam. 8.13 duplicates
  an eighth-century king's in 2 Kgs 14.7; "Hadadezer" echoes the ninth-century Hazael of
  Damascus), and Solomon's splendour "sounds suspiciously like the rise of the
  [[omride-dynasty|Omrides]] of Samaria some forty to fifty years later."
- Noll's verdict is agnostic, not nihilist: it is "possible, but by no means demonstrated,
  that some of the tales in 1–2 Samuel and 1 Kings 1–11 derive from faint memory of real
  tenth-century events." Where [[sources/dever-beyond-the-texts-2017|Dever]] uses the same C-14
  and gate evidence to defend a real (if modest) tenth-century polity, Noll doubts the "United
  Monarchy" label survives at all — the two skeptics' point of divergence.
