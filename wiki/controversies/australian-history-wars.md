# Australian History Wars: Scale and Character of Frontier Violence

*See also [[australian-frontier-conflict]], [[aboriginal-australians]], [[settler-colonialism]], [[terra-nullius]], [[bashford-macintyre-cha-v1-2013]] (Ch. 14, Banivanua Mar and Edmonds).*

## The Dispute

The **Australian History Wars** is a term for a prolonged public and scholarly controversy
over the scale, character, and moral weight of frontier violence between European settlers
and Aboriginal Australians during the colonial period (c. 1788–c. 1930). The controversy
intensified sharply in the early 2000s following Keith Windschuttle's claims that mainstream
historians had exaggerated or fabricated evidence for massacres in Tasmania.

The dispute spans:
- **Quantitative scale**: how many Aboriginal people were killed by settler violence?
- **Intentionality**: were killings systematic (constituting genocide) or incidental?
- **Source reliability**: are historians' accounts of massacres based on credible evidence?
- **Moral characterization**: should colonial history be framed as invasion and dispossession, or as settlement with regrettable incidental violence?

## Positions

### Position 1: High-Scale, Systematic Violence (Reynolds, Ryan, Attwood and others)

**Principal scholars**: Henry Reynolds (*Frontier: Aborigines, Settlers and Land*, 1987;
*Fate of a Free People*, 2004), Lyndall Ryan (*Tasmanian Aborigines: A History Since 1803*,
2012; 'Waterloo Creek, Northern New South Wales, 1838'; 'Settler Massacres on the Port
Phillip Frontier, 1836–1851'), Bain Attwood (*Rights for Aborigines*, 2003; co-editor with
S.G. Foster, *Frontier Conflict: The Australian Experience*, Canberra: National Museum of
Australia, 2003), Robert Manne (ed., *Whitewash: On Keith Windschuttle's Fabrication of
Aboriginal History*, Black Inc., 2003), Stuart Macintyre and Anna Clark (*The History Wars*,
MUP, 2003).

**Key arguments**:
- Frontier violence was endemic, operating across the continent from the 1790s through at
  least the 1920s (Forrest River massacre 1926; Coniston Station massacre 1928 are cited
  as twentieth-century examples, Frost, CHA I, p. 359 n.71).
- Van Diemen's Land: on initial contact in 1803 there were "6–8,000 Aboriginal people
  living in Van Diemen's Land, but by 1838 only around 60 survived" — a demographic
  catastrophe requiring explanation (Ryan, quoted in CHA I, p. 347). Reynolds credits
  Aboriginal leaders (e.g. Mannalargenna) with negotiating the end of the Black War.
- Mainland examples documented in CHA I: Hawkesbury/Darug war of occupation (to early 1800s);
  Appin massacre (1816, at least 16 Dharawal killed); Bathurst Plains martial law (1824);
  Waterloo Creek (c. 40–50 Kamilaroi killed, 1838); Myall Creek (at least 22 killed, 1838);
  Pinjarra (20–25 killed, 1834).
- Protection legislation from 1869 onward constituted "administered violence" by other means:
  forced removal, segregation, and racial caste legislation (historian Thom Blake: "if the
  sight of a trooper or policeman with a rifle evoked terror in the nineteenth century, in
  the twentieth century it was a policeman with a removal order", quoted in CHA I, p. 364).
- The scale of the Tasmanian catastrophe and similar patterns elsewhere are cited as evidence
  of at minimum structural genocide.

### Position 2: Low-Scale Revisionism (Windschuttle)

**Principal scholar**: Keith Windschuttle (*The Fabrication of Aboriginal History*, Vol. 1:
*Van Diemen's Land 1803–1847*, Macleay Press, 2002).

**Key arguments**:
- Mainstream historians (particularly Ryan) fabricated or grossly inflated casualty figures;
  the footnotes do not support the claims made.
- The Tasmanian Aboriginal population decline was primarily due to disease and demographic
  factors, not deliberate killing.
- The category "massacre" is applied loosely to events that were small-scale, reactive
  skirmishes, not systematic extermination.
- Frontier violence, where it occurred, was the result of individual criminal behaviour
  rather than colonial policy, and was prosecuted as such (the Myall Creek convictions
  being evidence of the rule of law in operation).
- The "History Wars" framing reflects a political agenda among academics to delegitimize
  the settler nation rather than dispassionate historical inquiry.

*Note: Windschuttle is not cited directly in CHA I Chapter 14, but his argument is the
target of the counter-literature cited there; Banivanua Mar and Edmonds cite Manne's
Whitewash, Macintyre and Clark's History Wars, and Attwood and Foster's Frontier Conflict
(all 2003) as the key responses (CHA I, p. 344 n.4).*

### Position 3: Contextual Differentiation (Attwood/Foster and others)

**Principal works**: Bain Attwood and S.G. Foster (eds), *Frontier Conflict: The Australian
Experience* (National Museum of Australia, 2003); Heather Goodall, *Invasion to Embassy:
Land in Aboriginal Politics in New South Wales, 1770–1992* (Sydney UP, 2008).

**Key arguments**:
- Neither Windschuttle's minimization nor a simple "genocide" framing captures the full
  complexity: violence varied enormously by region, period, industry, and colonial
  administration.
- Tasmania was an extreme case (near-total demographic collapse within 35 years); other
  regions show different patterns.
- The debate should distinguish between: direct killing in frontier conflict; demographic
  collapse from disease (which preceded the physical frontier); deprivation of food and
  water; and structural violence through legislation and reserve systems.
- Aboriginal **agency** deserves central attention: resistance, negotiation, petitioning
  for land, and adaptation were continuous throughout the century, not simply victimhood.

## Key Evidence in Dispute

- **Van Diemen's Land population collapse**: the transition from 6–8,000 in 1803 to ~60
  in 1838 is accepted as fact; what is disputed is the proportional contribution of direct
  killing vs. disease vs. displacement.
- **The Myall Creek convictions (1838)**: seven settlers hanged for the killing of at least
  22 Aboriginal people — documented in trial records; this is not in dispute. What is
  disputed is whether it represents the norm or an exceptional case of legal accountability.
- **Lyndall Ryan's footnotes**: Windschuttle's core evidential challenge targeted specific
  citations in Ryan's *Tasmanian Aborigines*; Ryan revised the 2012 edition in response.
- **The 1789 smallpox epidemic**: some scholars (Craig Mear, Christopher Warren) dispute
  whether the epidemic was introduced deliberately by the First Fleet or arrived through
  other vectors. See the citations in CHA I, p. 343 n.3 (Campbell, Butlin, Mear, Warren).

## Resolution Status

*Partially resolved.* The scholarly consensus, represented in a synthesis like CHA I
(2013), takes a high-scale position on frontier violence as policy and practice, treats
Windschuttle's specific factual challenges as having been substantially rebutted by
Manne et al. (2003) and Ryan's revised work, and integrates Aboriginal agency alongside
victimhood. However, the **moral and political** dimensions of the dispute — whether
"genocide" is the correct legal/historical category, whether it delegitimizes the modern
Australian state — remain open and contested in public discourse. The *quantitative*
question of total deaths remains genuinely uncertain; research by Ryan's Colonial
Frontier Massacres project (post-2013) has continued to raise estimated totals.

The dispute is also a controversy about **historiographical practice**: whether oral
tradition, indirect evidence, and demographic inference constitute valid historical
evidence for frontier deaths when documentary records are sparse or deliberately suppressed.
