# Origins of West African States — Indigenous Development vs. the Hamitic Hypothesis

A foundational interpretive dispute about **how the states and kingships of West
Africa came into being**: were their political institutions — above all **"divine
kingship"** — borrowed from outside (the Near East, "Hamitic" pastoralists from the
north-east), or did they develop **indigenously** out of local stateless societies?
Documented for the wiki through **Robin Horton's** chapter in
[[ajayi-crowder-history-west-africa-v1-1971|*History of West Africa* Vol. I]] (1971),
which mounts a systematic rebuttal of the diffusionist case.

## The two positions

### Position A — The Hamitic Hypothesis (diffusionist; now rejected)
The older colonial-era view held that achievements in sub-Saharan Africa — states,
kingship, ironworking, monumental building — were brought by lighter-skinned
"Hamitic" invaders or by diffusion from the ancient Near East, because Black Africans
were supposed incapable of originating them. As applied to states, it asserted that
(1) a uniform institution of "divine kingship" is found across West African states;
(2) it has strong affinities with the kingships of the ancient Near East; (3) there
is nothing corresponding to it in the indigenous stateless societies; and (4) West
African dynastic myths point to an **external origin** for the ruling line.

### Position B — Indigenous development (the Ibadan-school consensus)
Horton and the nationalist-era historians of the **Ibadan school** answer each claim:
- **No uniform "divine kingship":** the term is "little more than an umbrella" for a
  wide range of man–god relations, from [[asante-empire|Akan]] and
  [[dahomey|Dahomean]] kings merely *close to* ancestral spirits, to the
  [[oyo-empire|Alafin of Oyo]] partly *imbued* with them. West African kingship
  "seldom approaches the fully divinised pattern typical of Egypt."
- **Continuity with stateless societies:** the religious cast of authority in
  [[stateless-societies|stateless societies]] already backs leaders with spiritual
  force; Horton's New Calabar fieldwork shows a dynasty moving from human-backed to
  "more spirit than man" kingship **within four generations and without external
  influence** — a political device to compensate for a weak personal following.
- **Myths of alien origin are suspect:** many (Igala, [[mossi-states|Mossi]]) lack
  any corroboration and look like **legitimation devices** — claims to outsider or
  divine status by which rulers consolidate power in divided societies, "drawing on
  an ancient and thoroughly indigenous heritage."
- **Trade, not conquest, was the engine:** the great states tally with the
  **junctions and termini of trade routes**, developing from the Sahel down to the
  forest and finally the coast as the trade routes did — see [[state-formation]] and
  [[trans-saharan-trade]].

## Resolution status

**Resolved by consensus.** The Hamitic Hypothesis is now universally rejected as a
racist artefact of colonial historiography; the indigenous-development model is the
scholarly standard, underpinning the [[griot-oral-tradition|recovery]] of African
history and the UNESCO *General History of Africa* project. The controversy is
retained here because it shaped the field: the autonomy of African political history
had to be *argued for*, against a diffusionist default, and the structure of that
argument (continuity, legitimation, commerce) still organises how state formation in
the region is explained.
