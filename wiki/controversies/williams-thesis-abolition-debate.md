# Why did slavery end? The Williams thesis vs. Drescher's econocide

One of the great debates of modern economic and social history: **why was Atlantic slavery
abolished** — by the very powers, above all **Britain**, that had profited most from it, and at a
moment when the system was still highly productive? The dispute turns on the relative weight of
**economic interest** versus **ideology, politics, and moral mobilisation**. It is the central
historiographical question behind the wiki's [[abolition-of-slavery]] page.

## Position 1 — The Williams thesis (economic decline)

**Eric Williams**, *Capitalism and Slavery* (1944): slavery and the slave trade were abolished
because they had **ceased to pay**. In his account the profits of the [[atlantic-slave-trade|slave
trade]] and the [[plantation-economy|West Indian plantations]] had **financed the British
[[industrial-revolution|Industrial Revolution]]**; but by the late eighteenth century the mature
industrial economy no longer needed the protected sugar colonies, which had entered decline.
Abolition (1807) and emancipation (1833) thus **ratified an economic shift** — capitalism
discarding an institution it had outgrown — rather than expressing disinterested humanitarianism,
which Williams treated with sharp scepticism. The thesis was foundational for later
[[dependency-theory|dependency]] and Black-radical historiography.

## Position 2 — Drescher's econocide (abolition against interest)

**Seymour Drescher**, *Econocide* (1977) and *[[drescher-abolition|Abolition]]* (2009): the
**decline thesis is empirically wrong**. On the quantitative evidence, the British slave system was
**expanding and profitable** at the moment of abolition — its share of British overseas trade and
its output **rising**, not falling. Abolition therefore **destroyed thriving economic value**
("econocide"), and cannot be explained as the shedding of a spent sector. For Drescher the decisive
cause was **political and ideological**: an unprecedented **popular mobilisation** (mass petitions,
sugar boycotts, public opinion — the first sustained humanitarian movement around distant
suffering), driven by evangelical and Enlightenment conviction and reinforced by **enslaved
resistance** (above all the [[haitian-revolution-1791|Haitian Revolution]]).

## Position 3 — Syntheses

Most current scholarship accepts Drescher's **refutation of strict economic determinism** while
resisting a purely idealist account. Syntheses (Davis, and the framing of the
[[cambridge-world-history-slavery-v4|Cambridge World History of Slavery]]) hold that **capitalism,
ideology, and resistance were entangled**: free-labour ideology and the moral revolution were
*themselves* products of the same commercial society that slavery helped build, and abolition
served British **hegemonic** and strategic interests even as it sacrificed planter wealth. The
"why" is thus over-determined rather than reducible to a single cause.

## Resolution status

**Partially resolved.** Drescher's empirical case against the Williams "decline" mechanism is
widely accepted — few now argue the British slave colonies were collapsing when abolished. But the
larger question of how to weight economics, ideology, politics, and enslaved agency remains open
and actively debated, and Williams's insistence on the slavery–capitalism link has been revived by
the recent "**new history of capitalism**."
