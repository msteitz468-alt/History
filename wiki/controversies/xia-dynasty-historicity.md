# The Historicity of the Xia Dynasty and the Erlitou Identification

Did the **[[xia-dynasty|Xia dynasty]]** — the "first dynasty" of later Chinese texts — exist
as a historical state, and does it correspond to the archaeological
**[[erlitou-culture|Erlitou culture]]**? This is the central interpretive dispute of early
Chinese archaeology, and it turns on the deeper question of **how far textual records may be
matched to material remains** (a source-reliability conflict). [[liu-chinese-neolithic-2004|Li
Liu (2004)]] frames three scholarly positions plus her own.

## Positions

**1. Civilisation/state in the Longshan or earlier (radical Chinese position).** Some Chinese
archaeologists and historians cite written inscriptions, bronze, city walls, institutionalised
ritual, human sacrifice, and mortuary stratification in the [[longshan-culture|Longshan]] or
before, and link the legendary **Five Emperors** to late Neolithic cultures. *Held by:* Li
Xueqin (1997b), Cao (2001), Xu Shunzhan (1999).

**2. Xia = Erlitou (the standard Chinese consensus).** The Xia (~2100–1600 BC) is the first
state, materially represented by the Erlitou culture (~1900–1500 BC), with the
[[erlitou|Erlitou site]] as the **late Xia capital**. *Held by:* most Chinese archaeologists —
Chang (1999), Childs-Johnson, Du (1991), Gao Wei et al. (1998), Li Xueqin (1997a), Song
(1991), Wang Lixin (1998), Zhao Zhiquan (1987), Zou (1980) — and some Western Sinologists.

**3. Erlitou ≠ Xia, and the Xia may not have existed (Western skeptical view).** Many Western
Sinologists question linking a textually-attested dynasty to a pottery horizon; some doubt the
early Xia was a state, or existed at all. *Held by:* Allan (1984, 1991 — doubts the Xia
existed), Bagley (1999), Keightley (1983), Linduff (1998), Railey (1999), Thorp (1991).

**4. Liu's own position.** Erlitou is the **first state-level society in China regardless of
its relationship to the Xia**. Measuring statehood by **[[settlement-archaeology|settlement
hierarchy and mortuary data]]** rather than an artifact checklist, Liu finds the earliest
unmistakable state level only in **Erlitou Phase II (~1800 BC)** — which does *not* match the
traditional ~2100 BC Xia founding. So the early "Xia" (late Longshan / Erlitou Phase I) may
not have been a state; the durable state appears, archaeologically, as Erlitou.

## Why It Persists

The dispute reflects a methodological divide: **Chinese scholars** tend to accept ancient
texts as reliable and to match finds to recorded events ("departure from the era of doubting
history," Li Xueqin), while **Western Sinologists** are more critical of textual records and
of the matching method itself. Liu adds that the **nationalist and historiographic stakes** of
modern Chinese archaeology make the search for the Xia "socially and intellectually
meaningful," sharpening investment in the identification (see [[nationalism-and-archaeology]];
the state-funded **Xia-Shang-Zhou Chronology Project**, 1996–99, drew criticism on exactly
these grounds).

## Resolution Status

**Open.** No inscriptional evidence ties Erlitou to a polity named "Xia." Liu's settlement-and-
mortuary case for Erlitou **statehood** is widely accepted; the **Xia identification** remains
unresolved and is unlikely to be settled without contemporary writing.
