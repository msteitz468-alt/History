# Great Man vs. Structural/Systemic Causation in History

A long-standing interpretive dispute about the relative weight of individual agency versus large-scale impersonal forces in driving historical change. Directly relevant to how the wiki treats actors, events, and processes.

## Positions and Exemplars
- **Great Man emphasis**: Thomas Carlyle ("history is the biography of great men"); some military history (decisive battles, commanders); many traditional political biographies.
- **Structural emphasis**: Fernand Braudel (longue durée), Marxist modes of production, demographic and environmental historians (McNeill, Diamond in some readings), institutional economists.
- **Synthetic**: Most working historians combine both — e.g., "structures made X possible/likely; individual Y seized or squandered the opening."

The wiki's link taxonomy (caused_by vs contributed_to vs preceded_by) and emphasis on both actor pages and processes are attempts to hold both dimensions without collapsing one into the other.

## The Debate in Historiography (Evans 1997, ch. 6)

- **Elite-history maximalism**: Sir Lewis Namier held there is "no free will in the thinking and actions of the masses, any more than in the revolutions of planets... and in the plunging of hordes of lemmings into the sea" — history is the study of "people who mattered" (Brooke). John Vincent: "History is about winners, not losers... History is deeply male... about the rich and famous, not the poor." E. H. Carr, from the opposite politics, agreed in effect: the masses enter history only when organized into movements contributing to progress. E. P. Thompson's counter-manifesto — rescuing the poor "from the enormous condescension of posterity" — and the post-1960s explosion of social history buried the maximalist position.
- **Carr's structuralism and its limit case**: Carr treated great men as embodiments of wider forces ("Few great men have been so conspicuously as Stalin the product of the time and place in which they lived," following Trotsky on Stalin as creature of the 1920s bureaucracy) — yet his own *History of Soviet Russia* included a "Personalities" chapter conceding that "The events of history are set in motion by the wills of individuals," within conditions that "impose still narrower limits on what they can effectively will." Late in life he allowed that Lenin, surviving, would have industrialized "more humanely" than Stalin — an individual-level difference his framework struggled to accommodate. Evans notes Hitler as the parallel test: a fringe figure absent the Depression, but once in power exhibiting personality traits irreducible to "vast impersonal forces."
- **Evans's synthesis**: nobody "has managed to better Marx's dictum that people make their own history, but they do not do it under circumstances of their own choosing." Reducing a person to "a statistic, a social type, or the mouthpiece of a collective discourse... does violence to the complexity of human nature"; calling Stalin simply "a monster and a freak" explains nothing and "hugely overestimates the freedom of manoeuvre that people, even dictators, have." His Hitler-dies-in-1928 counterfactual (see [[causation-in-history]]) illustrates the interactionist result: war likely anyway, the systematic Final Solution far less certain.
- **Postmodernism's ironic contribution**: despite dethroning the author, [[postmodernism-and-history|postmodernist history]] restored the individual — the marginal and obscure rather than the great (Ginzburg's Menocchio; see [[microhistory]]) — correcting social-science history's "virtual elimination of the individual human being in favour of anonymous groups and trends."
