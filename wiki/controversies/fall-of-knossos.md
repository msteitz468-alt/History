# The Fall of Knossos — Date and Agency

A cluster of linked disputes over the end of [[minoan-civilization|Minoan]]
[[knossos|Knossos]]: **when** the final palace was destroyed, **who** destroyed it,
and **how old** the [[aegean-scripts|Linear B]] tablets baked in that destruction
really are. Because the Knossos tablets are the **earliest Greek documents**, the
question carries the whole chronology of the Mycenaean takeover of Crete. Raised in
[[edwards-cah-v2p1-1973|CAH Vol. II, Part 1]] (Chs. XII–XIII).

## Background

Evans dated the **final destruction of the Knossos palace to ~1400 BCE** (LM II /
LM IIIA), after which it was never rebuilt; the Linear B tablets were baked in that
fire. Ventris's 1952 decipherment then showed those tablets are in **Greek** —
proving **Mycenaean Greeks ruled Knossos** by ~1400, and demolishing Evans's own
theory that Minoans had conquered Mycenae. The CAH takes the destruction as
falling **"some time after 1400 BCE."**

## The positions

**1. Evans / the orthodox date (~1400, single destruction).** The palace fell once,
about 1400 BCE; the Linear B tablets belong to that level. CAH (Matz, Chadwick)
defends this against down-dating, citing connexions between the tablets, the seal
impressions, and the Palace Style, and the stratigraphy of the destruction layer.

**2. Palmer's down-dating of the tablets.** **L. R. Palmer** argued the Knossos
Linear B tablets are **much later** — close in date and appearance to the thirteenth-
century mainland tablets (Pylos) — and may come from a **"reoccupation phase"** after
the palace's destruction, implying the excavators (Evans, Mackenzie) misrecorded the
stratigraphy. CAH reports that this conjecture and "the far-reaching historical
conclusions to be drawn from it have not stood up to further archaeological
investigation," while conceding a few genuinely later (LM IIIA) tablets exist.

**3. Who destroyed it — three readings of the agency:**
   - **mainland Mycenaean conquest** — the Greeks of the mainland, who profited from
     Crete's eclipse to expand across the east Mediterranean (the favoured reading,
     *if* Knossos was a rival rather than already a subject state);
   - **a native Minoan revolt** against the Mycenaean overlords (but then the failure
     of Crete to revive afterwards is hard to explain);
   - **a natural cataclysm** (earthquake) — the destroyers may simply have been the
     same seismic forces that repeatedly wrecked the palaces.

## Why it matters

The dispute sets the **clock for the Mycenaean takeover of the Aegean** and tests a
methodological nerve: how far a decipherment (Linear B = Greek) can override an
excavator's stratigraphy, and how far it can override it the *other* way (Palmer's
re-dating). It is entangled with the related question of whether the Greeks took
Crete by **conquest or peaceful succession** (Matz leans, strikingly, to a "passive
renunciation of power" in keeping with Minoan character).

## Resolution status

**Open.** The CAH contributors uphold the ~1400 date and reject Palmer's down-dating,
but acknowledge the presence of some later tablets and decline to assert the *cause*
of the final ~1380 catastrophe. The agency of the destruction remains genuinely
unresolved.
