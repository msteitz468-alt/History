# Why No Socialism in America? (The Sombart Question)

Named for Werner Sombart's *Why Is There No Socialism in the United
States?* (1906). Every industrial society in Europe produced a mass
socialist or labor party; the United States, the most industrial of all,
did not. The explanations divide sharply, and the dispute is genuinely
open.

## Positions

**Sombart (1906) — material prosperity.** "All socialist utopias came to
grief on roast beef and apple pie": high wages, meat-eating workers, open
land, and social mobility dissolved class solidarity. Sombart himself
hedged that the material conditions were changing and socialism might yet
come — it did not.

**Hartz (1955) — the ideological/fragment explanation.** In
[[hartz-liberal-tradition-1955]], socialism is not the product of economic
conditions but of the feudal ethos and the class categories it bequeaths:
"The hidden origin of socialist thought everywhere in the West is to be
found in the feudal ethos." No feudalism → no class consciousness → no
socialism; the American worker was an incipient entrepreneur inside the
[[lockean-consensus]], and there was no bridge from American Progressivism
to Marx as there was from European Liberal Reform to social democracy.
Hartz's negative proof: the [[great-depression]] supplied every
"objective" Marxian precondition, and what emerged was the New Deal —
"a movement within the framework of the liberal faith." Socialist
strength appeared only where a genuine European working class had
immigrated (German Wisconsin; Debs's and De Leon's milieux).

Hartz's comparative extension in
[[hartz-founding-new-societies-1964]] (the [[fragment-thesis]]) supplies
the thesis's natural-experiment evidence: fragments of the same
industrializing civilization *did* produce mass labor parties precisely
where the founding migration carried a radical ethos (Australia's Labour
Party mastering its scene "with an ease unheard of in the annals of
European socialism") and failed to wherever the bourgeois ethos arrived
first (US, English Canada, Dutch South Africa) — economic development
being similar, the ideological cargo of the founders is what varies with
the outcome. Gad Horowitz's "Tory touch" amendment used the same
framework to explain why Canada, unlike the US, produced the CCF/NDP.

**Political-institutional explanations.** First-past-the-post and the
presidential system punish third parties (Duverger logic); federalism
fragments labor politics; and state and employer repression (injunctions,
Pinkertons, the 1919–20 Palmer raids, criminal syndicalism laws) was
unusually violent — socialism was not stillborn but killed. Hartz's
counter is that the hysteria itself is evidence of the consensus: fewer
radicals than anywhere in the West, yet vaster panic.

**Ethnic and racial fragmentation.** A working class divided by
successive immigrant waves, language, religion, and above all the color
line could not form the solidarity European labor achieved (later
scholarship: Katznelson, Lipset & Marks, *It Didn't Happen Here*, 2000,
which weighs all factors and leans institutional-plus-ideological).

**Revisionist minimization of the premise.** Historians of American
radicalism (Montgomery, Gutman school) argue the question is loaded:
American workers built massive, militant movements (Knights of Labor,
IWW, Debsian socialism polling 6% in 1912, municipal socialism) whose
defeat needs explaining politically, not treated as a metaphysical
absence.

## Resolution status

Open. Lipset & Marks's synthesis is the closest thing to a modern
consensus — no single factor suffices; the ideological (Hartz), the
institutional, and the ethnic-fragmentation accounts each carry weight —
but the rank-ordering of causes remains contested, and the revisionist
challenge to the question's framing persists.
