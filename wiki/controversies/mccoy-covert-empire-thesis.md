## Overview

Alfred W. McCoy's *[[mccoy-cold-war-five-continents-2024|Cold War on Five Continents]]* (2024) advances a cluster of interlocking interpretive claims about the nature and causal structure of the [[cold-war|Cold War]]. This page records those claims as positions in a live historiographical dispute, per this wiki's practice of not adjudicating contested sources in wiki voice. The underlying factual record McCoy assembles — operational details of [[central-intelligence-agency|CIA]] and allied covert operations, declassified quotations, casualty figures — is treated as attributed fact on the relevant actor/event/process pages; the interpretive superstructure below is not.

## Position A: The Covert-Empire Thesis

**Held by:** Alfred McCoy, and to varying degrees by historians in the "new intelligence history" tradition (e.g., Frances Saunders on CIA cultural operations).

**Strongest form:** The Cold War is best understood not primarily as a Moscow–Washington diplomatic and nuclear rivalry but as a global contest fought substantially through [[covert-action|covert action]] — coups, propaganda, paramilitary proxy forces, cultural infiltration — making intelligence agencies, not foreign ministries or militaries, the primary institutional engines of the conflict. McCoy explicitly adopts a "bottom-up, outside-in" method, prioritizing peripheral and local conflicts over top-down diplomatic history, and asserts the Cold War was "nothing less than the longest, largest, and most consequential conflict in the history of humankind."

## Position B: The Diplomatic-History / Great-Power-Rivalry Consensus

**Held by:** the mainstream tradition of Cold War historiography McCoy explicitly positions himself against — diplomatic historians emphasizing superpower deterrence, détente, arms control, and state-to-state negotiation (e.g., the "orthodox" and post-revisionist schools centered on nuclear stability and top-level diplomacy).

**Strongest form:** While covert action was a real and consequential instrument, the Cold War's decisive dynamics ran through direct superpower diplomacy, the nuclear balance, and alliance politics; treating field-level covert operations as the central axis risks overstating the causal weight of episodic, often poorly-controlled operations relative to structural forces (economic systems, ideology, deterrence) that diplomatic historians center.

## Position C: The "Men on the Spot" Thesis

**Held by:** McCoy, drawing an explicit analogy to 19th-century British imperial agents (Cecil Rhodes, Gordon of Khartoum, T.E. Lawrence).

**Strongest form:** Individual field operatives — Kermit Roosevelt, Norman Darbyshire, Larry Devlin, [[edward-lansdale|Edward Lansdale]], Lucien Conein — exploited "voids" or ruptures created by decolonization to exercise personal, historically consequential agency largely independent of central government control, forming a recognizable historical type comparable across roughly thirty identified operatives (18 American, 4 French, 4 British, 4 other).

## Position D: The Structural-Causation Counter-View

**Strongest form (inferred from the diplomatic-history tradition and standard causal-analysis practice this wiki applies elsewhere):** Individual operatives operated within, and were empowered or constrained by, institutional mandates, budgets, and geopolitical conditions set by Washington and Moscow; treating operator biography as a primary causal category risks a "great man" fallacy that understates bureaucratic, economic, and structural determinants of outcomes.

## Position E: CIA Coups as Primary Cause of Later Civil Wars/Kleptocracies

**Held by:** McCoy, applied specifically to Guatemala (post-1954 decades of civil war), Congo/Zaire (Mobutu's kleptocracy and the Second Congo War, ~5 million dead by McCoy's figure), Chile (Pinochet-era repression), and Iran (post-1953 authoritarian consolidation and its long arc toward 1979).

**Strongest form:** CIA-organized regime change in each case set in motion decades-long, foreseeable trajectories of civil violence, repression, or state predation for which the coups bear primary causal responsibility, not merely a contributing or precipitating role.

## Position F: Coups as Contributing, Not Primary, Cause

**Strongest form:** Coups often triggered or accelerated conflicts, but subsequent decades of violence and misrule also depended on domestic political structures, ethnic and regional cleavages, economic conditions, and successor leaders' choices; collapsing these into a single "the coup caused it" causal chain risks a `caused_by` claim where `contributed_to` or `enabled` is more defensible under this wiki's link taxonomy.

## Position G: Mackinder Geopolitical Determinism

**Held by:** McCoy, who reads Halford Mackinder's 1904 "Heartland"/"World Island" thesis as the deep structural logic beneath Cold War conflict locations, with decolonization and covert action as the mechanisms of a superpower contest for the Eurasian "marginal crescent."

## Position H: Multi-Causal Cold War Geography

**Strongest form:** Conflict locations were shaped by a more contingent mix of decolonization timing, resource endowments (oil, copper, diamonds), local political alignments, and opportunistic superpower response, rather than by a single overarching geopolitical logic derived from a century-old theoretical framework; many Cold War historians would not treat Mackinder as load-bearing at all.

## Position I: NATO Expansion as Catalyst for Russian Revanchism (post-1991 coda)

**Held by:** McCoy, in his framing of the post-2014/2022 Russia–NATO confrontation as a continuation of Cold War covert-empire dynamics, citing Putin's own 2021 statement calling the Soviet collapse "the greatest geopolitical catastrophe of the 20th century."

## Position J: Russian Agency / Regime-Choice View

**Strongest form (standard alternative in NATO-expansion historiography):** Post-Soviet Russian foreign policy, including the 2022 invasion of Ukraine, reflects choices by the Putin regime rather than being substantially caused by NATO's post-1991 enlargement; NATO expansion is, on this view, a contributing context at most, not a primary cause.

## Shared Factual Ground, Framed Per Side

Both sides of each dispute above generally accept: the documented existence and scale of the named CIA and allied covert operations (personnel, budgets, dates, declassified quotations); the casualty figures McCoy cites, sourced to CIA officers, congressional testimony, truth commissions, and archival document caches (e.g., the 1992 Paraguayan "Archive of Terror" on Operation Condor); and the basic sequence of events in each case (coup dates, subsequent regimes, subsequent conflicts). What is contested is the *weighting* of covert action against other causal factors, the *causal directness* asserted between coups and later outcomes, and the *generalizability* of the "man on the spot" and Mackinder frameworks as explanatory devices for the Cold War as a whole.
